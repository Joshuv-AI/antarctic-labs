Local Existence and Finite-Time Singularity Formation in the Vlasov-Poisson-Isotropic Landau System
Jin Woo Jang †, Junsung Kim ‡

Abstract
. The isotropic Landau (Coulomb) operator was introduced in kinetic theory by Krieger and Strain [ 30 ] . In this work, we study the spatially inhomogeneous Vlasov–Poisson–isotropic Landau system. We first establish a local–in–time existence theory for the Cauchy problem: for initial data satisfying a suitable smallness condition in an appropriate norm, there exists a non–negative solution on a time interval [ 0 , T ] [0,T] , where the lifespan T T depends on the size of the initial data. Beyond the local theory, we investigate a mechanism that may lead to the breakdown of global existence. We show that finite–time singularity formation can occur in the gravitationally attractive case, provided that the weak solution satisfies certain a priori regularity and decay assumptions, the initial gravitational field energy exceeds the kinetic energy, and the resulting energy gap dominates the diffusive effect of the collision operator. As a consequence, if the solution is further assumed to belong to a suitable measure space up to the maximal existence time, it collapses to a single point in physical space at that time. The proof of finite–time singularity formation is based on deriving an upper bound for the second spatial moment, which becomes negative in finite time.

1.
Introduction

In this article, we study the Vlasov–Poisson–isotropic Landau (VPiL) system in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+E_{f}\cdot\nabla_{v}f=Q_{\mathrm{iso}}(f,f),\qquad E_{f}=(\mp)\,\frac{x}{4\pi|x|^{3}}\star_{x}\rho_{f}.

Here,
(-)
and
(+)
correspond to the gravitational and plasma cases, respectively.
The symbol
\star_{x}
denotes convolution over
\mathbb{R}^{3}
in the
x
-variable, and the mass/charge density is defined by

\rho_{f}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f\,dv,

where the particle mass and charge have been normalized so that
\textup{m}=\textup{e}=1
.
The collision operator
Q_{\mathrm{iso}}
is an isotropic Landau operator associated with a Coulombic cross section and is given by

(1.2)

\displaystyle Q_{\mathrm{iso}}(f,f)=Q_{\mathrm{KS}}(f,f)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\,\Delta_{v}f+f^{2}.

Here, the nonlinear diffusion coefficient term

(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f(v)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{f(u)}{|u-v|}\,du

corresponds to the Newtonian potential of
f
, which may be understood as the (weak) solution to the Poisson equation.
This definition is natural whenever
f
is locally Hölder continuous in the classical sense or is
L_{v}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
in the sense of distribution.
The subscript “KS” refers to the formulation introduced by Krieger and Strain
[
30
]
.

1.1.
Background on Landau-type Collisional Kinetic Equations

The mathematical theory of kinetic equations has seen extensive development across both collisional and collisionless regimes. In the collisional regime, the Boltzmann equation plays a central role: it models dilute gases composed of neutral atoms or molecules undergoing short–range binary interactions and governs the evolution of the velocity distribution function through a first–order integro–differential operator. The Landau equation–often regarded as the Boltzmann equation for Coulombic interactions–arises as the grazing–collision limit of the Boltzmann equation and is used widely to model plasmas where particles interact through long–range repulsive Coulomb potentials. These two equations are closely related: repulsive long–range interactions in plasmas generate scattering effects analogous to grazing collisions in rarefied gases, and indeed the rigorous grazing limit of the Boltzmann equation leads to the Landau equation
[
14
]
.

It is well known that Maxwellian distributions of the form
Ce^{-c|v|^{2}}
are stationary solutions of the Landau equation. Supported by experimental observations
[
40
,
34
]
as well as classical kinetic theory
[
25
,
26
,
39
,
44
]
, Maxwellians are understood as thermal equilibrium states of dilute plasmas. Consequently, early mathematical investigations primarily focused on small perturbations around Maxwellians as the first step toward understanding nonequilibrium statistical phenomena. The global well–posedness of such perturbations was first established by Guo
[
25
]
, who later proved their exponential convergence to equilibrium in
[
26
]
. Subsequent works by Strain
[
39
]
, Wang
[
44
]
and others have extended this perturbative framework and broadened the class of admissible solutions.

For the spatially homogeneous Landau equation, large–data global well–posedness is known for a wide range of interaction potentials
[
24
,
1
,
42
,
12
,
13
,
43
]
. In sharp contrast, for the inhomogeneous Landau equation, the well–posedness theory for evolutionary solutions remains largely open. Within the vacuum perturbative framework, progress toward local well–posedness has been made along several directions. The existence of local weak solutions was established in
[
28
,
10
,
27
,
37
]
. To further obtain properties such as regularity and uniqueness, these works require additional mesoscopic assumptions on the hydrodynamic fields–typically a uniform positive lower bound on the local charge density, together with uniform upper bounds on the charge, energy and entropy. Under such assumptions, one can derive
L^{\infty}
a priori bounds for solutions, depending on the potential exponent
[
8
,
38
,
36
]
. For very soft potentials, further moment bounds on appropriate velocity moments are also needed. These estimates allow the use of Harnack-type inequalities, leading to global Hölder continuity of weak solutions under quantitative control of the hydrodynamic quantities (see Theorem 1.2 in
[
8
]
).

Another line of research exploits Gaussian decay in the velocity variable
[
38
]
to compensate for the polynomial growth of the derivatives of the Landau coefficients. Henderson et al.
[
28
]
demonstrated that combining Hölder continuity with Schauder estimates yields local Hölder bounds on derivatives, which subsequently imply global smoothness of weak solutions. As a consequence, any finite–time blow–up of
f
or its derivatives must be accompanied by the loss of at least one uniform boundedness condition on the hydrodynamic quantities. An alternative method was proposed by Sanchit and Henderson et al.
[
27
,
10
]
, who proved local–in–time existence by assuming weighted regularity of the initial data, without requiring a priori boundedness of hydrodynamic fields.

The perturbative global theory near vacuum has also seen progress across different interaction regimes. In the moderately soft potential case, Luk
[
33
]
obtained global existence and stability by combining dispersive decay from the transport operator with weighted energy estimates, while exploiting a favorable null structure in the collision operator. Sanchit
[
9
]
later extended this strategy to the hard potential regime, where enhanced coercivity allows a technically simpler yet conceptually parallel argument. In the very soft potential regime and the Coulombic case, the global existence problem remains unresolved. Nevertheless, Bedrossian et al.
[
3
]
ruled out the presence of self–similar blow–up solutions, thereby eliminating one natural mechanism for singularity formation.

From a modeling perspective, plasmas also generate long–range self–consistent electromagnetic fields. An alternative physically comprehensive formulation is therefore the Vlasov–Poisson–Landau (VPL) system, in which collisional effects are coupled with transport induced by the self–consistent electric field. Existing mathematical studies of the VPL system have focused largely on perturbations near Maxwellians; see
[
45
,
26
,
39
,
44
]
. In contrast, near-vacuum regimes in the whole space remain entirely open for the VPL system. The well-posedness issues differ fundamentally from those of the inhomogeneous Landau equation on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
: in particular, the definition of the electric field requires spatial integrability that cannot be derived from the conditional regularity framework for the Landau equation, as that theory crucially assumes a uniform positive lower bound on the charge density.

1.2.
The Vlasov–Poisson–Isotropic Landau System and Main Results

In this paper, we focus on the near–vacuum regime of the Vlasov–Poisson–isotropic Landau (VPiL) system for the Coulomb potential:

\begin{gathered}\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+E_{f}\cdot\nabla_{v}f=(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\Delta_{v}f+f^{2},\\
\rho_{f}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f\,dv,\quad E_{f}=\mp\frac{x}{4\pi|x|^{3}}\star_{x}\rho_{f},\end{gathered}

for
(t,x,v)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
A distinctive feature of this model is that the total energy is not conserved and may even increase, in sharp contrast to the classical Vlasov-Poisson-Landau system. More precisely, observe by Lemma
5.7
in our case or
[
21
]
in the homogeneous case that if
f\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|v|^{2}f\,dvdx\pm\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|E_{f}|^{2}\,dx\right)=2\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}ff\,dvdx\geq 0.

The signs
(+)
and
(-)
on the left-hand side correspond to the plasma and gravitational cases, respectively. This lack of a dissipative energy law precludes the use of standard global energy methods. Then, this property suggests that the dynamics may amplify concentration rather than promote smoothing, leaving open the scenario of finite–time singularities. As another feature of this model, since in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
the electric field must remain well–defined, one cannot assume an
a priori
lower bound on the charge/mass density that is uniform in
x
. This prevents a direct application of existing conditional regularity theories, such as global
L^{\infty}
bounds
[
8
]
,
C^{\infty}
smoothing
[
28
]
, or Gaussian bounds
[
38
]
.

To the best of our knowledge, there has been no mathematical theory developed for the VPiL system, leaving a gap in the mathematical theory of collisional plasmas. In this work, we establish the local existence of a slowly decaying weak solution to the Cauchy problem (
1.1
). We further show that any non–negative weak solution to (
1.1
) in the gravitational case, satisfying certain physically relevant conditions, has a finite maximal existence time. In particular, any such non–negative global–in–time solution (if it exists) necessarily loses regularity after some finite time.

Our main theorems can be (informally) summarized as follows:

•

(
Local existence
) In both gravitational and plasma cases, if the initial data exhibits sufficient polynomial decay together with its derivative, and is small in an appropriate weighted
C^{2}
norm, then there exists a polynomially decaying solution to (
1.1
) on the interval
[0,T]
, where
T
depends on the size of
f^{\textup{in}}
in a suitable weighted
C^{2}
norm.

•

(
Finite-time singularity
) We consider the purely gravitationally attractive case. If the gravitational field energy exceeds the kinetic energy and the resulting energy gap is sufficiently large compared to the diffusive effect induced by the collision operator, then the solution to the collisional system develops a finite-time singularity. Moreover, if we further assume that a weak solution exists up to the maximal existence time in an appropriate measure space, then the solution collapses to a single point in space at that time.

1.3.
Relationship between Landau and Isotropic Landau Operator and these Basic Properties

The classical Landau collisional operator
Q(\cdot,\cdot)
is given by

(1.3)

\displaystyle Q(f,f)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}\partial_{v_{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Pi_{ij}(v-v_{\star})\left((\partial_{v_{j}}f)(v)f(v_{\star})-(\partial_{v_{j}}f)(v_{\star})f(v)\right)\,dv_{\star},

where the kernel matrix
(\Pi_{ij}(v))_{1\leq i,j\leq 3}
in (
1.3
) is given by

(1.4)

\displaystyle\Pi(v)=\frac{1}{8\pi}\left(\mathbb{I}_{3}-\frac{v\otimes v}{|v|^{2}}\right)|v|^{\gamma+2},\quad\gamma\in[-3,1].

Here, the interaction exponent
\gamma
characterizes different physical regimes:

\gamma\in(0,1]
corresponds to hard potentials,

\gamma\in(-2,0)
to moderately soft potentials,
and
\gamma\in(-3,-2]
to very soft potentials.
In particular,
\gamma=0
is known as the Maxwellian molecule case,
while
\gamma=-3
represents the Coulomb potential,
which is regarded as the most physically relevant regime for plasma dynamics. One can simplify the Landau operator (
1.3
) as

\displaystyle Q(f,f)=a_{ij}(f)\partial_{v_{i}v_{j}}f-c(f)f,

where for some
a_{\gamma},c_{\gamma}>0
,

(1.5)

\displaystyle a_{ij}(f)=a_{\gamma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|w|^{\gamma+2}\left(\delta_{ij}-\frac{w_{i}w_{j}}{|w|^{2}}\right)f(v-w)\,dw,

and

\displaystyle c(f)=\begin{cases}c_{\gamma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|w|^{\gamma}f(v-w)\,dw,\quad&\gamma\in(-3,1],\\
f,&\gamma=-3.\end{cases}

Notice that since
f
formally satisfies

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\varphi Q(f,f)\,dvdx=0,\qquad(\varphi=1,v_{i},|v|^{2}),

we have for
t>0

(1.6)

\displaystyle\textup{M}(t)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}f(t,x,v)\,dvdx=\textup{M}(0),

(1.7)

\displaystyle\textup{P}(t)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}vf(t,x,v)\,dvdx=\textup{P}(0),

(1.8)

\displaystyle\textup{KE}(t)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}f(t,x,v)\,dvdx=\textup{KE}(0).

In this paper, our main focus is the collisional Vlasov–Poisson system
(
1.1
), where the collision operator is given by the isotropic variant
(
1.2
) of the Coulombic Landau operator. This operator can be viewed
as a simplified model obtained by replacing the anisotropic matrix appearing in
(
1.4
) with its velocity-averaged (radially symmetric) counterpart, in
particular neglecting the
v\otimes v
contribution to the diffusion
structure. Although such an approximation removes the physically correct
directional degeneracy of the Coulomb kernel, it retains the key feature that
the potential term in velocity is governed by the inverse Laplacian

(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}
.

We also have the formal properties of the physical quantities for VPiL.

Remark 1.1

(Formal conservation and monotonicity properties for VPiL)
.

Notice that the operator
Q_{\textup{iso}}
exhibits several properties with respect to physical quantities. More precisely, we observe that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}Q_{\textup{iso}}(f,f)\,dv=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left((-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\Delta_{v}f+f^{2}\right)\,dv=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left(-f^{2}+f^{2}\right)\,dv=0.

This identity yields the following continuity equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla_{x}\cdot j=0,

where the mass/charge density
\rho(t,x)
and current density
j(t,x)
are defined in terms of
f
by

\displaystyle\rho(t,x)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(t,x,v)\,dv,\quad j(t,x)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}vf(t,x,v)\,dv,

where we work in normalized units in which the particle charge and mass satisfy
\textup{e}=\textup{m}=1
.

In addition, the total mass/charge is formally conserved:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\textup{M}(t)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\frac{d}{dt}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}f(t,x,v)\,dvdx=0.

On the other hand, we note that the total energy increases in time. This was first observed in the spatially homogeneous case by
[
22
]
. In the spatially inhomogeneous case, we observe that

(1.9)

\begin{gathered}\frac{d}{dt}\textup{E}_{\textup{tot}}(t)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|v|^{2}f(t,x,v)\,dvdx\pm\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla(K\star\rho)(t,x)|^{2}\,dx\right)\\
\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\textup{KE}(t)\pm\mathscr{E}_{E}(t)\right)=2\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}ff\,dvdx\geq 0,\end{gathered}

where
K(x)=\pm\frac{1}{4\pi|x|}
. Unlike the classical Vlasov–Poisson–Landau system, in which the total energy is conserved, the present model exhibits an increasing energy.

Lastly, for
f\geq 0
, the entropy dissipation relation formally holds as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\textup{H}(t)

\displaystyle\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\frac{d}{dt}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}f(t)\log f(t)\,dxdv

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\{-v\cdot\nabla_{x}f-E_{f}\cdot\nabla_{v}f+Q_{\textup{iso}}(f,f)\}\log f\,dvdx

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}Q_{\textup{iso}}(f,f)\log f\,dvdx=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\{(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\Delta_{v}f+f^{2}\}\log f\,dvdx

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}f\left\{2(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}(\nabla_{v}f)\cdot\frac{\nabla_{v}f}{f}+(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\left(\frac{\Delta_{v}f}{f}-\frac{|\nabla_{v}f|^{2}}{f^{2}}\right)\right\}\,dvdx

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}(\nabla_{v}f)\cdot\nabla_{v}f-\frac{(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f|\nabla_{v}f|^{2}}{f}\,dvdx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{8\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{9}}\frac{f(u)f(v)}{|u-v|}|\nabla_{u}\log f(u)-\nabla_{v}\log f(v)|^{2}\,dudvdx\leq 0,

where
(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f(v)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{f(u)}{|u-v|}\,du
.

1.4.
Singularity Formation in the Attractive Case

Before turning to the nonlinear dynamics of the Vlasov–Poisson–isotropic Landau
model, it is useful to recall a classical diagnostic tool in kinetic theory for detecting
the breakdown of solutions: the
mixed-moment argument
. This method tracks
the evolution of low-order spatial moments and attempts to force the second spatial
moment to become negative in finite time, thereby producing a contradiction. The
mechanism is closely tied to the presence of the nonlinear (gravitational) Poisson
term, which plays an essential role.

For instance, consider the spatially inhomogeneous classical Landau equation
without a self-consistent field. In this case, the mixed-moment argument alone never
produces finite-time breakdown. Indeed, if

f(t,x,v)\geq 0
is a regular solution, the second spatial moment

\textup{I}(t)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|x|^{2}f(t,x,v)\,dvdx

is an upward-opening quadratic polynomial in
t
and therefore remains nonnegative
for all time. To see this, recall the total energy conservation law (
1.8
). By direct
differentiation of
\textup{I}(t)
, we find

\displaystyle\textup{I}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(x\cdot v)\,f(t,x,v)\,dv\,dx,

\displaystyle\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{KE}(0)>0,

\displaystyle\textup{I}^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=0,

where
\mathrm{KE}(0)
denotes the initial kinetic energy as defined in (
1.9
), which is conserved in
time. Hence
\textup{I}(t)
is an upward-opening quadratic polynomial in
t
.

For a quadratic polynomial to become negative at some finite time, its minimum
value must be negative. The minimum of
I
occurs at

t_{*}=-\frac{\textup{I}^{\prime}(0)}{\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(0)},

and therefore

\textup{I}(t_{*})=\textup{I}(0)-\frac{\textup{I}^{\prime}(0)^{2}}{2\,\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(0)}.

Thus, a necessary condition for
\textup{I}(t)
to attain negative values is

\textup{I}^{\prime}(0)^{2}-2\,\textup{I}(0)\,\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(0)>0.

However, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality we have

\textup{I}^{\prime}(0)^{2}\leq 2\,\textup{I}(0)\,\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(0),

so the above condition can never be satisfied. Consequently,
\textup{I}(t)

remains nonnegative for all
t
.

This shows that the mixed-moment argument cannot yield any mechanism
for finite-time singularity formation in the classical Landau equation without an attractive self-consistent field.

The situation changes markedly for the classical Vlasov–Poisson–Landau system with
attractive self-interaction. If the initial field energy exceeds twice the kinetic energy,

\mathscr{E}_{E}(0)>2\mathrm{KE}(0)
, then the conserved quantity
\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(t)
becomes
proportional to
(2\mathrm{KE}(0)-\mathscr{E}_{E}(0))<0
, and the quadratic polynomial

\textup{I}(t)
opens downward. In this regime, the mixed-moment argument does permit
finite-time singularity formation.

In contrast, the isotropic Landau equation with a gravitational self-consistent field does not conserve energy (see
(
1.9
)), and the Taylor expansion of
\textup{I}(t)
consequently contains a nontrivial cubic term. The coefficient of this term is positive, reflecting the strictly increasing energy. This makes the analysis more delicate and requires a refined mixed-moment argument. In particular, we assume that the absolute value of the negative quadratic coefficient
|-(\mathscr{E}_{E}(0)-\textup{KE}(0))|
is sufficiently large–equivalently, that
\mathscr{E}_{E}(0)\gg 1
–relative to the cubic coefficient. Under this assumption, the cubic upper bound for
\textup{I}(t)
can still be forced to become negative within a finite time interval. This refined argument will be carried out for the Vlasov–Poisson–isotropic Landau model in Section
5
.

2.
Main Theorems and Strategies

Now, we are in position to state the main theorems precisely. We begin by introducing the necessary function spaces. As in
[
33
,
9
]
, the solution space
X_{T}^{m}
is defined using
\langle x-vt\rangle
weight.

Definition 2.1
.

For each
T>0
, we denote the local phase space as

\displaystyle\mathscr{Q}_{T}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{6}.

For
m\in\mathbb{R}
,
1<p_{0}<\frac{3}{2}
,
3<q_{0}<\frac{11}{3}
and
w=\langle x\rangle\langle v\rangle^{8}
, the solution space
X_{T}^{m}
is defined by

\displaystyle X_{T}^{m}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\left\{f\in W_{p_{0}}^{1,2}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})\cup W_{q_{0},w}^{1,2}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})\ \bigg|\ \sup_{(t,x,v)\in\overline{\mathscr{Q}_{T}}}\langle x-vt\rangle^{m}\langle v\rangle^{m}|f(t,x,v)|<\infty\right\},

endowed with a norm

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{X_{T}^{m}}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\lVert\langle x-vt\rangle^{m}\langle v\rangle^{m}f(t,x,v)\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\overline{\mathscr{Q}_{T}})}.

Here,
W_{p_{0}}^{1,2}
and
W_{q_{0},w}^{1,2}
denote the (weighted) parabolic Sobolev spaces as defined in (
2.11
). The Sobolev conditions in the definition of
X_{T}^{m}
are imposed only to ensure sufficient regularity, while the norm
\|\cdot\|_{X_{T}^{m}}
measures the weighted
L^{\infty}
size of the solution.

To introduce the space for initial datum, for
m\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the weighted
L^{\infty}
space by

\displaystyle L^{\infty}_{m,2m}(\mathbb{R}^{6})\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\biggl\{f:\mathbb{R}^{6}\to\mathbb{R}\ \bigg|\ \sup_{(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{6}}\langle x\rangle^{m}\langle v\rangle^{2m}|f(x,v)|<\infty\biggr\},

where
\langle z\rangle\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}(1+|z|^{2})^{1/2}
for
z\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The associated norm is given by

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{m,2m}(\mathbb{R}^{6})}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\sup_{(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{6}}\langle x\rangle^{m}\langle v\rangle^{2m}|f(x,v)|.

In accordance with this space, we define the initial space, denoted by
\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
as the set of functions
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
such that

\displaystyle f,v\cdot\nabla_{x}f,\nabla_{v}f,\Delta_{x}f,\Delta_{v}f\in L^{\infty}_{m,2m}(\mathbb{R}^{6}),

and

\displaystyle\langle x\rangle^{m}\langle v\rangle^{2m}\big(|f|+|v\cdot\nabla_{x}f|+|\nabla_{v}f|+|\Delta_{x}f|+|\Delta_{v}f|\big)\to 0\quad\text{ as }|x|,|v|\to\infty,

endowed with a norm

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}(\mathbb{R}^{6})}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\|(|f|+|v\cdot\nabla_{x}f|+|\nabla_{v}f|+|\Delta_{x}f|+|\Delta_{v}f|)\|_{L^{\infty}_{m,2m}(\mathbb{R}^{6})}.

In fact, on any finite time interval [0,T], the quantity
\langle x-vt\rangle^{m}\langle v\rangle^{m}
can be bounded in terms of
\langle x\rangle^{m}\langle v\rangle^{2m}
(see Lemma
4.1
), which motivates the above choice of weights for the initial data.

Using this functional setting, we make precise the definition of a weak solution. Let
m>3
, and suppose that
f:\overline{\mathscr{Q}_{T}}\to\mathbb{R}
is a classical solution to the initial-value problem associated with (
1.1
), with initial data
f^{\textup{in}}
. Then, since the solution is locally integrable, for each
T>0
and for any test function
\varphi\in C_{c}^{2}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
, the left-hand side of (
1.1
) can be written by

\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+E_{f}\cdot\nabla_{v}f)\varphi\,dxdvds\\
=-\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}f^{\textup{in}}\varphi(0)\,dxdv-\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(\partial_{t}\varphi+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi+E_{f}\cdot\nabla_{v}\varphi)\,dxdvds.

Using integration by parts and the self-adjointness of
(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}
, the right-hand side of (
1.1
) can be written as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}f\Delta_{v}[(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\varphi]+f^{2}\varphi\,dxdvds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}f[2\nabla_{v}\{(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\}\cdot\nabla_{v}\varphi+(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\cdot\Delta_{v}\varphi]\,dxdvds.

Here, we rely on the sufficient regularity of
f
, specifically its local Hölder continuity in
v
-variable, to justify
\Delta_{v}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f=-f
. Moreover, assuming that
f
is sufficiently integrable with appropriate polynomial decay at infinity in the
v
-variable, we obtain the field representation:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\nabla_{v}\{(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\}(v)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{u-v}{|u-v|^{3}}f(u)\,du.

Combining the results above, we define the integral solution to (

2.1.
Main Strategies

2.1.1.
Construction of the Approximate Sequence

Our solution construction is based on the method of continuity applied to a suitably designed approximation scheme (
3.4
). To ensure uniform ellipticity and coefficient regularity necessary for global parabolic estimates, we introduce artificial diffusion and cutoff modifications in both the spatial and velocity variables, together with a time–mollified characteristic function. This enables us to employ Schauder- and
L^{p}
-type estimates (see Theorems
B.2
,
B.3
, and
B.4
). The corresponding functional framework is the intersection space
Y_{T,\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w}
(defined in Section
•
‣
2.3
), which simultaneously provides boundedness, Hölder regularity, and integrability required for the comparison arguments developed later. Detailed steps are given in Section
3
.

To control the iterative sequence, we utilize the nonlinear estimate established in Section
4
. In particular, the classical maximum principle for parabolic equations yields polynomial decay of the solution in both
x
and
v
. Such decay is crucial to ensure that nonlinear quantities, including
E_{f}
and
(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f
, are well-defined within the weak formulation. For example, if
G
satisfies sufficient polynomial decay, then
E_{G}
and
(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}G
remain well-defined and
G(t,\cdot,\cdot)\in L^{1}_{x,v}
provided

G\lesssim\langle x\rangle^{-3-\varepsilon}\langle v\rangle^{-3-\varepsilon}.

A challenge arises because the standard weight
\langle x\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m}
is not invariant under the transport operator
v\cdot\nabla_{x}
. To overcome this, we employ the transport-invariant weight
\langle x-vt\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m}
. Although this introduces additional terms when differentiating with respect to
v
, the resulting coefficients grow at most polynomially in
T
, which remains manageable within our analysis.

2.1.2.
To Study the Finite-Time Singularity Formation

We focus only on the gravitational case, in which the attractive interaction may lead the particles to collapse onto a set of measure zero. For the singularity analysis, we employ a mixed-moment method inspired by

[
29
,
5
,
11
]
. The central object is the
second spatial moment

\textup{I}(t)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|x|^{2}f(t,x,v)\,dvdx,

whose evolution is governed by the fundamental identity

\textup{I}^{\prime}(t)=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(x\cdot v)f(t)\,dvdx.

Using the equation and successive differentiations of
\textup{I}(t)
, we establish the
following exact formulas:

\displaystyle\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{KE}(t)-\mathscr{E}_{E}(t),

\displaystyle\textup{I}^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}(2\mathrm{KE}(t)-\mathscr{E}_{E}(t))=4\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\,f\,dvdx,

where
\mathrm{KE}(t)
and
\mathscr{E}_{E}(t)
, defined in (
2.5
) and (
2.6
), denote the total kinetic energy and gravitational field energy, respectively. Since the right-hand side
is non-negative, we obtain
\textup{I}^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)\geq 0
, ensuring that
\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(t)
is
non-decreasing.

To extract a quantitative differential inequality, we estimate the Newtonian
potential term via weighted polynomial decay of
f
, propagated from the local
existence theory. In particular, the bootstrap assumptions imply

\|(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}\lesssim\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}},

from which we derive

\textup{I}^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)\leq C\|f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}\|f^{\textup{in}}\|_{L^{1}_{x,v}}\lesssim_{m}\frac{\|f^{\textup{in}}\|_{L^{1}_{x,v}}}{\|f^{\mathrm{in}}\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}}},

where we have used the uniform bounds on the weighted
C^{2}
norms of order
m
obtained from the
nonlinear estimates in Section
4
.

Combining the above estimates, we show that
\textup{I}(t)
satisfies

\textup{I}(t)\leq\textup{I}(0)+\textup{I}^{\prime}(0)t+(2\mathrm{KE}(0)-\mathscr{E}_{E}(0))t^{2}+C\frac{\|f^{\textup{in}}\|_{L^{1}_{x,v}}}{\|f^{\mathrm{in}}\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}}}t^{3}.

Thus,
\textup{I}(t)
is bounded above by a cubic polynomial whose coefficients depend
only on the initial data.

The hypotheses in Theorem
2.7
impose a negative initial mixed moment

\textup{I}^{\prime}(0)<0
, large initial gravitational field energy relative to the kinetic energy
\mathscr{E}_{E}(0)>2\textup{KE}(0)
; more precisely, we assume the condition

(\mathscr{E}_{E}(0)-2\mathrm{KE}(0))\,\|f^{\mathrm{in}}\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}}\gg\|f^{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{1}_{x,v}},

which ensures that the negative quadratic term dominates the small cubic term. As a consequence, the cubic polynomial attains a negative local minimum
at a finite time
t=t_{\ast}>0
. Since the non-negativity of
f
enforces

|x|^{2}f\geq 0
almost everywhere, the spatial moment
\textup{I}(t)
cannot become
negative without violating positivity of the solution. Therefore, the solution
must lose at least one of the regularity hypotheses before
t_{\ast}
, proving that the maximal
lifespan is finite.

2.2.2.
On the Weighted Elliptic Regularity Theory.

As we can see in Lemma

, the fact that the sequence element
\{f^{n}\}
in the iterative scheme belongs to a weighted

[

, where the authors proved the global a priori estimate for elliptic and parabolic equations in weighted mixed-norm Sobolev spaces.
Beyond the specific nonlinear framework considered here, substantial progress has also been made on linear degenerate elliptic equations in the setting of weighted regularity theory. Although not directly utilized in our iteration, such results characterize regularizing mechanisms inherent in kinetic models. In particular, the introduction of artificial diffusion in our linearized approximation is consistent with these analytical strategies, as it facilitates the construction of approximate solutions in weighted Sobolev spaces. We refer to

2.2.3.
On Singularity Formation in Various Kinetic Equations

In parallel with advances on local and global existence, several kinetic models are known to develop singularities in finite time under appropriate physical conditions. For the Vlasov–Poisson system, Horst
[
29
]
established in 1982 that any classical solution in the attractive (gravitational) case with spatial dimension

d\geq 4
must blow up in finite time whenever the initial total energy is negative. For the Vlasov–Manev equation, a kinetic model arising in stellar dynamics, Bobylev et al.
[
5
]
proved in 1997 the nonexistence of global classical solutions by employing a moment-based argument. More recently, Choi and Jeong
[
11
]
showed that solutions to the Vlasov–Riesz equation have finite lifespan when the initial kinetic energy is sufficiently small relative to the initial interaction energy. They further demonstrated finite-time singularity formation for the Vlasov–Riesz–Fokker–Planck equation, provided the initial data are such that the second mixed moment is sufficiently negative in a quantitative sense, as measured in terms of
\textup{I}(0)
and the initial total energy.

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\ \lVert f\rVert_{L_{p,q,w}(\Omega_{T})}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{T}w_{1}(t)\left(\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|f(t,x,v)|^{p}w_{2}(x,v)\,dxdv\right)^{\frac{q}{p}}\,dt\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f,\,\,\nabla_{x,v}f,\,\,\nabla_{x,v}^{2}f\in L^{1}_{\textup{loc}}(\Omega_{T}),\quad\textup{ and }\quad\|f\|_{W_{p,q,w}^{1,2}(\Omega_{T})}<\infty,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\lVert f\rVert_{W_{p,q,w}^{1,2}(\Omega_{T})}

\displaystyle\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\lVert f\rVert_{L_{p,q,w}(\Omega_{T})}+\lVert\partial_{t}f\rVert_{L_{p,q,w}(\Omega_{T})}+\lVert\nabla_{x,v}f\rVert_{L_{p,q,w}(\Omega_{T})}+\lVert\nabla_{x,v}^{2}f\rVert_{L_{p,q,w}(\Omega_{T})}.

Finally, we define the non-weighted Sobolev space by

\displaystyle W_{p,q}^{1,2}(\Omega_{T})\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}W_{p,q,1}^{1,2}(\Omega_{T}),\ \ W_{p}^{1,2}(\Omega_{T})\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}W_{p,p,1}^{1,2}(\Omega_{T}),

where the weight is identically one, and the mixed norm reduces to the standard

-based parabolic Sobolev norm.

\displaystyle L^{\infty}_{m,2m}(\mathbb{R}^{6})\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\biggl\{f:\mathbb{R}^{6}\to\mathbb{R}\bigg|\ \sup_{(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{6}}\langle x\rangle^{m}\langle v\rangle^{2m}|f(x,v)|<\infty\biggr\},

2.4.
Outline of the Paper

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
3
, after establishing the solvability of the heat equation in the intersection of Hölder and

to the linearized problem (
3.15

B.3

3.
Solution to a Linear Problem

In this section, we construct a family of linearly approximate solutions to the initial-value problem for (
1.1
). For each small
\varepsilon>0
and, given
g\in Y_{T,\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w}
with
g\geq 0
, in
\Omega_{T}
, we define a related linear operator
\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}:Y_{T,\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w}\rightarrow Y^{\prime}_{T,\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w}
given by

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\partial_{t}f-\varepsilon\Delta_{x,v}f+\chi(\varepsilon v)v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+J^{\varepsilon}(t)\chi(\varepsilon x)E_{g}\cdot\nabla_{v}f-(-\Delta_{v})_{\varepsilon}^{-1}g\Delta_{v}f-gf,

where we define the localized diffusion coefficient

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}_{\varepsilon}g(v)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\chi(\varepsilon u)g(u)}{|u-v|}\,du,

where the function
\chi\in C_{c}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
is a smooth cut-off function that satisfies

(3.2)

\displaystyle 0\leq\chi\leq 1,\quad\chi\equiv 1\,\,\textup{ in }\,\,B_{\frac{1}{2}}(0),\quad\textup{ and }\quad\textup{supp }\chi=B_{1}(0),

and
J^{\varepsilon}
is a time-regularized characteristic function defined via the following mollification:

(3.3)

\displaystyle J^{\varepsilon}(t)

\displaystyle\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\left(1_{\left[-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},T-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right]}\star\rho_{\varepsilon}\right)(t),

with a mollifier
\rho_{\varepsilon}
supported on
\left[-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right]
.

Using this operator, we define the following linear approximate equation for (
1.1
) for each given non-negative
g\in Y_{T,\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w}
as

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f)=\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon})I^{\varepsilon},

where

\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon})\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}(-\varepsilon\Delta_{x,v}f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}+\chi(\varepsilon v)v\cdot\nabla_{x}f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}+J^{\varepsilon}(t)\chi(\varepsilon x)E_{g}\cdot\nabla_{v}f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}-(-\Delta_{v})_{\varepsilon}^{-1}g\Delta_{v}f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}-gf^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}),

(3.5)

\displaystyle\{f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}\geq 0\}_{\varepsilon>0}\subset C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{6})\textup{ s.t. }f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}\to f^{\textup{in}}\textup{ in }\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}(\mathbb{R}^{6}),

where the approximates are just obtained from multiplying
f^{\textup{in}}
by a
6
-dimensional smooth cut-off. Here, another time-regularized characteristic function near
t=0
,
I^{\varepsilon}
is defined by

(3.6)

\displaystyle I^{\varepsilon}(t)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}(1_{\left[-\frac{\varepsilon^{\kappa}}{2},\frac{3\varepsilon^{\kappa}}{2}\right]}\star\rho_{\varepsilon^{\kappa}})(t),

where
0<\kappa\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\min\{\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\}
with

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}/p_{0}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}

\displaystyle\textup{ the maximum of the polynomial growth order in }\lambda^{-1}\textup{ and }(\Lambda/\lambda)

\displaystyle\textup{ arising from the global estimate }W_{p_{0}}^{1,2}(\Omega_{T})\to L_{p_{0}}(\Omega_{T}),

\displaystyle\kappa_{2}/q_{0}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}

\displaystyle\textup{ the maximum of the polynomial growth order in }\lambda^{-1}\textup{ and }(\Lambda/\lambda)

\displaystyle\textup{ arising from the global estimate }W_{q_{0},w}^{1,2}(\Omega_{T})\to L_{q_{0},w}(\Omega_{T}).

Based on linear analysis, we will introduce a sequence of iterated solutions
\{f^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
at the end of the section. Let
\varepsilon>0
and
T>0
be given. Let a non-negative function
g\in Y_{T,\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w}
, satisfying the following pointwise bound, be given:

(3.7)

\displaystyle 0\leq g\leq C_{g}\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon^{\prime}v)vt\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m},

for some constants
0<C_{g}
,
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq 1
and exponent
m>3
. This decay estimate will be further established in Section
4
. We first establish boundedness and continuity of the coefficients in (
3.4
) for each given
g
.

The following lemma establishes
L^{\infty}
-boundedness of the coefficients.

Lemma 3.1

(
L^{\infty}
boundedness of coefficients)
.

\chi(\varepsilon v)v
,
E_{g}
and
(-\Delta_{v})_{\varepsilon}^{-1}g
are essentially bounded with their respective domains.

Proof.

The function
\chi(\varepsilon v)v
is bounded by the definition of
\chi
as given in (
3.2
).

By the upper bound (
3.7
) of
g
, the electric field term
E_{g}(t,x)
satisfies the estimate

\displaystyle|E_{g}(t,x)|\leq C_{g}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{dv}{\langle v\rangle^{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{dy}{|y|^{2}\langle x-y-\chi(\varepsilon^{\prime}v)vt\rangle^{m}}<\infty,

where we used

(3.8)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\langle z\rangle^{-m}}{|x-z|^{k}}dz\lesssim_{m,k}\frac{1}{\langle x\rangle^{k}},\text{ for }m>3\text{ and }0<k<3.

Finally, for the potential term
(-\Delta_{v})_{\varepsilon}^{-1}g
, we use the upper bound (
3.7
) of
g
and (
3.8
) again to obtain

\displaystyle|(-\Delta_{v})_{\varepsilon}^{-1}g(t,x,v)|\leq\frac{C_{g}}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{du}{|u-v|\langle u\rangle^{m}}<\infty.

∎

The following two lemmas prove the Hölder continuity of the coefficients in (
3.4
).

Lemma 3.2

(Hölder continuity of
\chi(\varepsilon v)v
and
(-\Delta_{v})_{\varepsilon}^{-1}g
)
.

Let
1\leq p_{0}<\infty
and let
0<\alpha<1
. Suppose that
g\in(W_{p_{0}}^{1,2}\cap C_{\textup{para}}^{2,\alpha})(\mathscr{Q}_{T})
and satisfies (
3.7
). Then the coefficients
\chi(\varepsilon v)v
and
(-\Delta_{v})_{\varepsilon}^{-1}g
are Hölder continuous in
(x,v)
with exponent
\alpha
and in
t
with exponent
\alpha/2
. In particular, both functions are parabolically Hölder continuous.

Proof.

Since
\chi(\varepsilon v)v
is compactly supported in
v
, it suffices to show that it has a bounded derivative. Indeed, we observe that

\displaystyle\nabla_{v}(\chi(\varepsilon v)v)=\varepsilon(\nabla_{v}\chi)(\varepsilon v)v+\chi(\varepsilon v)\cdot\textup{Id}.

This yields the uniform bound:

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla_{v}(\chi(\varepsilon v)v)\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{v}}\leq\varepsilon\lVert\nabla\chi\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lVert v\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\textup{supp}(\chi(\varepsilon v)))}+3\lVert\chi\rVert_{L^{\infty}}<\infty.

Thus,
\chi(\varepsilon v)v
is Lipschitz (and therefore Hölder) continuous in
v
, and trivially in
x
and
t
since it depends only on
v
.

We now turn to the Hölder continuity of
(-\Delta_{v})_{\varepsilon}^{-1}g
. First, fix
(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
and consider
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,T]
. Since
g\in(W_{p_{0}}^{1,2}\cap C_{\textup{para}}^{2,\alpha})(\mathscr{Q}_{T})
, using Lemma
A.1
and the representation formula for the Newtonian potential, we have

\displaystyle|(-\Delta_{v})_{\varepsilon}^{-1}g(t_{1},x,v)-(-\Delta_{v})_{\varepsilon}^{-1}g(t_{2},x,v)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{-3}\lVert g(t_{1},x,\cdot)-g(t_{2},x,\cdot)\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{v}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{-3}[g]_{C_{\textup{para}}^{\alpha}}|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{\alpha/2}.

Similarly, for fixed
(t,v)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we have

\displaystyle|(-\Delta_{v})_{\varepsilon}^{-1}g(t,x_{1},v)-(-\Delta_{v})_{\varepsilon}^{-1}g(t,x_{2},v)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{-3}\lVert g(t,x_{1},\cdot)-g(t,x_{2},\cdot)\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{v}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{-3}[g]_{C_{\textup{para}}^{\alpha}}|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}.

Finally, for fixed
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we estimate

\displaystyle|(-\Delta_{v})_{\varepsilon}^{-1}g(t,x,v_{1})-(-\Delta_{v})_{\varepsilon}^{-1}g(t,x,v_{2})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{-3}\lVert g(t,x,\cdot+v_{1})-g(t,x,\cdot+v_{2})\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{v}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{-3}[g]_{C_{\textup{para}}^{\alpha}}|v_{1}-v_{2}|^{\alpha}.

Then, recall that for any
\delta>0
, the parabolic Hölder seminorm satisfies the interpolation-type estimate

\displaystyle[g]_{C_{\textup{para}}^{\alpha}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})}\leq\delta[g]_{C_{\textup{para}}^{2,\alpha}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})}+C\delta^{-\alpha/2}\lVert g\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})}<\infty,

where the constant
C
depends only on the spatial dimension. See Chapter 8 of
[
31
]
for details.
∎

Lemma 3.3

(Hölder continuity of
E_{g}
)
.

Let

(3.9)

\begin{gathered}1<p_{0}<\frac{3}{2},\quad 3<q_{0}<\frac{11}{3},\quad 0<\alpha<1,\text{ and }w=w(x,v)=\langle x\rangle\langle v\rangle^{8}.\end{gathered}

Suppose that
g\in Y_{T,\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w}
and satisfies (
3.7
). Then the coefficient
J^{\varepsilon}(t)\chi(\varepsilon x)E_{g}(t,x)
is Hölder continuous in
x
with exponent
\alpha
and in
t
with exponent
\alpha/2
. In particular, it is parabolically Hölder continuous.

Proof.

Step 1: Hölder continuity in space.

Since
J^{\varepsilon}(t)\chi(\varepsilon x)
is smooth with compact support, it suffices to establish the local Hölder continuity of
E_{g}(t,x)
in
x
. We begin with showing the Lipschitz continuity of
E_{g}(t,x)
uniformly in
t\in[0,T]
.

Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, fix
t\in[0,T]
, and define
x_{0}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}x_{1}+\tau(x_{2}-x_{1})
for
\tau\in[0,1]
. Then by the fundamental theorem of calculus, we can write the difference as

\displaystyle|E_{g}

\displaystyle(t,x_{1})-E_{g}(t,x_{2})|=\left|\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\nabla_{x}g)(t,x_{0}-y,v)\cdot(x_{2}-x_{1})\nabla_{y}\left(\frac{1}{|y|}\right)\,dydvd\tau\right|.

We perform integration by parts with respect to
y
, using the identity:

\int_{|y|>\varepsilon}[(\nabla_{x}g)(t,x_{0}-y,v)\cdot(x_{2}-x_{1})]\nabla_{y}\left(\frac{1}{|y|}\right)\,dy\\
=-\int_{|y|=\varepsilon}g(t,x_{0}-y,v)\left(-\frac{y}{|y|^{3}}\right)(x_{2}-x_{1})\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}dS_{y}+\int_{|y|>\varepsilon}g(t,x_{0}-y,v)(x_{2}-x_{1})\Delta_{y}\left(\frac{1}{|y|}\right)\,dy.

Noting that
\Delta_{y}\left(\frac{1}{|y|}\right)=0
for
y\neq 0
, the second integral vanishes. The first boundary integral term becomes:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}g(t,x_{0}-\varepsilon\omega,v)\omega[(x_{2}-x_{1})\cdot\omega]d\omega\to\frac{4\pi}{3}(x_{2}-x_{1})g(t,x_{0},v)\quad\textup{ as }\varepsilon\to 0^{+}

a.e. in
\mathscr{Q}_{T}
. Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle|E_{g}(t,x_{1})-E_{g}(t,x_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{3}|x_{2}-x_{1}|\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}g(t,x_{1}+\tau(x_{2}-x_{1}),v)\,dvd\tau.

Since
g(t,x,v)\leq C_{g}\langle v\rangle^{-m}
for some
m>3
by (
3.7
), we have

\displaystyle|E_{g}(t,x_{1})-E_{g}(t,x_{2})|\leq C(m,g)|x_{1}-x_{2}|.

Hence,
E_{g}
is Lipschitz continuous with respect to
x
, uniformly in
t
.

Step 2: Hölder continuity in time.

Let
t_{1}<t_{2}\in[0,T]
and fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Then

\displaystyle|E_{g}(t_{2},x)-E_{g}(t_{1},x)|\leq\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\partial_{t}g(\tau,y,v)|\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2}}\,dvdyd\tau.

To estimate this, apply Hölder’s inequality in
(y,v)
with exponents
q_{0}>3
,
q_{0}^{\prime}
such that
1/q_{0}+1/q_{0}^{\prime}=1
. Then

|E_{g}(t_{2},x)-E_{g}(t_{1},x)|\\
\leq\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\partial_{t}g(\tau,y,v)|^{q_{0}}\langle y\rangle\langle v\rangle^{8}dvdy\right)^{\frac{1}{q_{0}}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2q_{0}^{\prime}}\langle y\rangle^{\frac{q_{0}^{\prime}}{q_{0}}}}\frac{1}{\langle v\rangle^{\frac{8q_{0}^{\prime}}{q_{0}}}}\,dvdy\right)^{\frac{1}{q_{0}^{\prime}}}\,d\tau\\
=:\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left(\textup{I}\right)^{\frac{1}{q_{0}}}\left(\textup{II}\right)^{\frac{1}{q^{\prime}_{0}}}d\tau,

for appropriate weights
\langle x\rangle\langle v\rangle^{8}
ensuring the integrability of the second factor (e.g., using Muckenhoupt weight theory). Under the conditions:

(for local integrability of
II
near
y=x
)

\displaystyle 2q_{0}^{\prime}<3,

(for integrability of
II
at infinity)

\displaystyle\left(2+\frac{1}{q_{0}}\right)q^{\prime}_{0}>3,\,\frac{8}{q_{0}}q^{\prime}_{0}>3,

(Muckenhoupt
A_{q_{0}}
weight condition for
\langle y\rangle\langle v\rangle^{8}
)

\displaystyle 1+8<6(q_{0}-1),

the second integral is uniformly bounded in
x
. If
q_{0}\in(3,11/3)
, all the three conditions above are satisfied. Thus, with
w=\langle y\rangle\langle v\rangle^{8},
we have

\displaystyle|E_{g}(t_{2},x)-E_{g}(t_{1},x)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\lVert\partial_{\tau}g(\tau)\rVert_{L^{q_{0}}_{w}}\,d\tau\lesssim|t_{2}-t_{1}|^{1-1/q_{0}}\lVert\partial_{t}g\rVert_{L^{q_{0}}_{w}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})}.

Then we conclude

|J^{\varepsilon}(t_{2})E_{g}(t_{2},x)-J^{\varepsilon}(t_{1})E_{g}(t_{1},x)|\\
\leq|J^{\varepsilon}(t_{2})||E_{g}(t_{2},x)-E_{g}(t_{1},x)|+|J^{\varepsilon}(t_{2})-J^{\varepsilon}(t_{1})||E_{g}(t_{1},x)|\\
\lesssim_{g,\varepsilon}|t_{2}-t_{1}|^{\alpha/2}(T+1)^{1-1/q_{0}-\alpha/2}+|t_{2}-t_{1}|^{\alpha/2}\lesssim|t_{2}-t_{1}|^{\alpha/2}.

Here, we used the fact that
1-1/q_{0}-\alpha/2>0
for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
, which follows from the assumption
q_{0}\in(3,11/3)
in (
3.9
).

Step 3: Conclusion.

Combining both parts, we have

•

J^{\varepsilon}(t)\chi(\varepsilon x)E_{g}(t,x)
is Hölder continuous in
x
with exponent
\alpha
,

•

and is Hölder continuous in
t
with exponent
\alpha/2
.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle|J^{\varepsilon}(t_{1})\chi(\varepsilon x_{1})E_{g}(t_{1},x_{1})-J^{\varepsilon}(t_{2})\chi(\varepsilon x_{2})E_{g}(t_{2},x_{2})|

\displaystyle\lesssim|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}+|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{\alpha/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim(|x_{1}-x_{2}|+|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{1/2})^{\alpha}

for all
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,T]
and
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
; that is,
J^{\varepsilon}(t)\chi(\varepsilon x)E_{g}(t,x)
is parabolically Hölder continuous.
∎

Now we have sufficient regularity of the coefficients of the linear operator
\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}
to solve the corresponding equation between the spaces
Y_{T,\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w}
and
Y^{\prime}_{T,\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w}
, as stated in Lemma
3.5
.
Before proceeding further, we first recall the solvability of the heat equation between
Y_{T,\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w}
and
Y^{\prime}_{T,\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w}
.

Lemma 3.4

(Solvability of the heat operator)
.

Let
\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w
be defined as (
3.9
). For each
\lambda>0
, the heat operator
(\partial_{t}-\Delta_{x,v}+\lambda):Y_{T,\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w}\to Y^{\prime}_{T,\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w}
is uniquely solvable.

Proof.

Given
g\in Y^{\prime}_{T,\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w}
, define

u=G_{\lambda}\star_{(t,x,v)}g,
where

\displaystyle G_{\lambda}(t,x,v)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\frac{1_{t>0}}{(4\pi t)^{3}}\exp\left(-\frac{|x|^{2}+|v|^{2}}{4t}-\lambda t\right),

is the fundamental solution to the heat equation

\partial_{t}u-\Delta_{x,v}u+\lambda u=0,
and
\star_{(t,x,v)}
denotes convolution over
\mathbb{R}^{7}
in the
(t,x,v)
variables. By Lemma 5.1.1(i) in
[
32
]
and its proof, the function
u
satisfies:

(i)

u\in W_{p_{0}}^{1,2}(\Omega_{T})
,

(ii)

\partial_{t}u-\Delta_{x,v}u+\lambda u=g
a.e. in
\Omega_{T}
.

Moreover, by Theorem 2.2(B) in
[
35
]
, we also have

u\in W_{q_{0},w}^{1,2}(\Omega_{T}),
where the weight class used in
[
35
]
is a parabolically modified Muckenhoupt class (based on the distance
d((t,x),(s,y))=\max\{|t-s|^{1/2},|x-y|\}
). Since our weight
w(x,v)=\langle x\rangle\langle v\rangle^{8}
is independent of time and it is in the Muckenhoupt class using the uniform distance
d((t,x),(s,y))=\max\{|t-s|,|x-y|\}
, it also belongs to this modified Muckenhoupt class.

From the precious steps, we know all the integrability of the function
u
and its weak derivatives. To prove that
u\in C^{2,\alpha}_{\textup{para}}(\Omega_{T})
, which is the final ingredient needed to establish the surjectivity of the operator
\partial_{t}-\Delta_{x,v}+\lambda
, it suffices to show
u
has parabolic Hölder regularity. Injectivity follows from standard
a priori
estimates, such as those in Theorems
B.2
-
B.4
.

To establish Hölder continuity, we first estimate the parabolic Hölder seminorm of
u
. Fix a spatial unit vector
e_{1}
. Then for small
h\in\mathbb{R}
, using the Hölder continuity of
g
, we have

|u(t,x+he_{1},v)-u(t,x,v)|\\
\leq\int_{-\infty}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}G_{\lambda}(s,y,u)|g(t-s,x-y+he_{1},u-v)-g(t-s,x-y,u-v)|\,dudyds\\
\leq[g]_{C_{\textup{para}}^{\alpha}(\Omega_{T})}|h|^{\alpha}\int_{-\infty}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}G_{\lambda}(s,y,u)\,dudyds\leq\frac{[g]_{C_{\textup{para}}^{\alpha}(\Omega_{T})}}{\lambda}|h|^{\alpha}.

Similar estimates in time and space directions show that
u\in C^{\alpha}_{\textup{para}}(\Omega_{T})
. To gain the regularity
C^{2,\alpha}_{\textup{para}}
, define mollified functions
u_{n}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\xi_{n}\star_{(t,x,v)}u
, where

\displaystyle\xi_{n}(t,x,v)=n^{7}\xi(nt,nx,nv),

where
\xi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{7})
,
\textup{supp }\xi=\{(t,x,v):|t|^{2}+|x|^{2}+|v|^{2}\leq 1\}=B_{1}
,
\xi\equiv 1
on
B_{1/2}
, and
0\leq\xi\leq 1
. Then, since
u_{n}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})
, the interior Schauder estimate (Theorem 8.11.1 in
[
31
]
) implies that for each
R>0
, we have

\displaystyle\|u_{n}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}_{\textup{para}}(Q_{R})}\leq N\left(\|g_{n}\|_{C^{\alpha}_{\textup{para}}(\Omega_{T})}+\|u_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{2R})}\right),

where
Q_{R}=(-R^{2},0)\times\{(x,v):|x|^{2}+|v|^{2}\leq R^{2}\}
, and
N
depends only on
R,\alpha
, and the ellipticity constant (equal to 1 in our case). Since
g\in C^{\alpha}_{\textup{para}}(\Omega_{T})
, the terms on the right-hand side are uniformly bounded in
n
. Moreover, by Young’s inequality for convolutions and the uniform continuity of
u
, the
L^{\infty}
norm of
u_{n}
in
Q_{2R}
is also uniformly bounded. Thus,
\{u_{n}\}
is uniformly bounded in
C^{2,\alpha}_{\textup{para}}(Q_{R})
. By the Arzela-Ascoli theorem, up to a subsequence,
u_{n}\to\bar{u}\in C^{2}_{1}(Q_{2R})
, where
C^{2}_{1}
denotes functions with classical derivatives of order
\geq 2
in
(x,v)
and
\geq 1
in
t
.

Meanwhile, we also have
u_{n}\to u
in
C^{\alpha}_{\textup{para}}(\Omega_{T})
, so

\displaystyle\|u-\bar{u}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{2R})}\leq\|u-u_{n}\|_{C^{\alpha}_{\textup{para}}(\Omega_{T})}+\|u_{n}-\bar{u}\|_{C^{2}_{1}(Q_{2R})}\to 0\quad\textup{ as }\,\,n\to\infty.

Hence
u=\bar{u}\in C^{2,\alpha}_{\textup{para}}(Q_{2R})
, and this holds for all
R>0
with arbitrary center of the cylinder, implying

\displaystyle u\in C^{2,\alpha}_{\textup{para, loc}}(\Omega_{T}).

To obtain global regularity, note that
\|u\|_{C^{2,\alpha}_{\textup{para}}(Q_{R}(z_{0})\cap\Omega_{T})}<\infty
for all
R>0
and
z_{0}\in\Omega_{T}
. Also, by Theorem 8.7.3 in
[
31
]
, since
g\in C^{\alpha}_{\textup{para}}(\Omega_{T})
, there exists a unique global solution
u_{0}\in C^{2,\alpha}_{\textup{para}}(\Omega_{T})
to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{0}-\Delta_{x,v}u_{0}+\lambda u_{0}=g,\quad\textup{ in }\,\,\Omega_{T}.

By uniqueness,
u=u_{0}
in every compact subset of
\Omega_{T}
, and thus

\displaystyle u\in C^{2,\alpha}_{\textup{para}}(\Omega_{T}).

This completes the proof.
∎

We are now in a position to address the solvability of the linear approximate equation (
3.4
).

Lemma 3.5

(Solution to a linear problem)
.

Let
\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w
be defined as (
3.9
). Let
\varepsilon>0
and let
g\in Y_{T,\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w}
with
g\geq 0
and let
f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
be the approximated initial data defined in (
3.5
). Then for each
T>0
, there exists a unique function
f\in Y_{T,\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w}
that solves

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f)=\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon})I^{\varepsilon}\quad\textup{ in }\,\,\mathscr{Q}_{T},\textup{ and }f(t,\cdot,\cdot)=f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,\cdot)\quad\textup{ in }\,\,t\in[0,\varepsilon),

where
\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}
is defined as in (
3.1
).

Proof.

We first work in the extended domain
\Omega_{T}=(-\infty,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{6}
. From Lemmas
3.1
,
3.2
, and
3.3
, we obtain global Schauder-type
a priori estimate
for the terminal-value problem associated with
\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}
. In particular, by Theorem
B.2
, for each
\lambda>0
, there exists a constant
C_{S}>0
, depending only on
\varepsilon,T,\lambda,g,C_{g},\alpha
such that

(3.11)

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{C_{\textup{para}}^{2,\alpha}(\Omega_{T})}\leq C_{S}\lVert\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f)+\lambda f\rVert_{C_{\textup{para}}^{\alpha}(\Omega_{T})},\quad\forall f\in C_{\textup{para}}^{2,\alpha}(\Omega_{T}).

Similarly, by Theorem
B.3
and Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
, we have
L^{p_{0}}
-type estimates; i.e., there exists
\lambda_{0}\geq 1
, such that for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
there exists
C_{p_{0}}>0
depending only on
\varepsilon,p_{0},T,g,C_{g},\lambda
with

(3.12)

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{W_{p_{0}}^{1,2}(\Omega_{T})}\leq C_{p_{0}}\lVert\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f)+\lambda f\rVert_{L_{p_{0}}(\Omega_{T})},\quad\forall f\in W_{p_{0}}^{1,2}(\Omega_{T}).

Moreover, for weighted mixed Sobolev spaces, by
[
15
]
or Theorem
B.4
, there exists
\lambda\geq 1
such that for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
, there exists
C_{w}>0
depending only on
\varepsilon,q_{0},T,g,C_{g},w=\langle x\rangle\langle v\rangle^{8}
and
\lambda
such that

(3.13)

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{W_{q_{0},w}^{1,2}(\Omega_{T})}\leq C_{w}\lVert\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f)+\lambda f\rVert_{L_{q_{0},w}(\Omega_{T})},\quad\forall f\in W_{q_{0},w}^{1,2}(\Omega_{T}).

Then we are now ready to proceed with the method of continuity (Theorem
B.1
). Define the operators
\mathscr{L}_{1}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}+\lambda
and
\mathscr{L}_{0}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\partial_{t}-\Delta_{x,v}+\lambda
. Then, we obtain the analogous inequalities to
B.2
-
B.4
in terms of the operator
\mathscr{L}_{0}
as well. Therefore, by interpolating these endpoint inequalities, we obtain that
there is some constant
C>0
independent of
\beta\in[0,1]
such that

\|f\|_{(C_{\textup{para}}^{2,\alpha}\cap W_{p_{0}}^{1,2}\cap W_{q_{0},w}^{1,2})(\Omega_{T})}\leq C\|\mathscr{L}_{\beta}f\|_{(C^{\alpha}_{\textup{para}}\cap L_{p_{0}}\cap L_{q_{0},w})(\Omega_{T})},

where
\mathscr{L}_{\beta}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}(1-\beta)\mathscr{L}_{0}+\beta\mathscr{L}_{1}
for each
\beta\in[0,1].

Then by the method of continuity (Theorem
B.1
) and Lemma
3.4
, the operator
\mathscr{L}_{1}=\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}+\lambda:(C_{\textup{para}}^{2,\alpha}\cap W_{p_{0}}^{1,2}\cap W_{q_{0},w}^{1,2})(\Omega_{T})\to(C^{\alpha}_{\textup{para}}\cap L_{p_{0}}\cap L_{q_{0},w})(\Omega_{T})
is onto and hence is invertible.

Now, define the time-regularized source term:

\displaystyle h=(\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}+\lambda)(e^{-\lambda t}f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon})I^{\varepsilon}\in(C^{\alpha}_{\textup{para}}\cap L_{p_{0}}\cap L_{q_{0},w})(\Omega_{T}),

which is well-defined since
f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
. Then by the above solvability result, there exists a unique solution
f^{\prime}\in(C_{\textup{para}}^{2,\alpha}\cap W^{1,2}_{p_{0}}\cap W_{q_{0},w}^{1,2})(\Omega_{T})
to the equation:

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}f^{\prime}+\lambda f^{\prime}=h\quad\textup{ in }\,\,\Omega_{T}.

Note that

•

I^{\varepsilon}(t)=1
on
[0,\varepsilon]
,

•

I^{\varepsilon}(t)=0
on
(2\varepsilon,T]
,

so in particular,

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}f^{\prime}+\lambda f^{\prime}=0\quad

\displaystyle\textup{ in }\left(2\varepsilon,T\right)\times\mathbb{R}^{6},\textup{ and }f^{\prime}=e^{-\lambda t}f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}\quad\textup{ in }\left[0,\varepsilon\right]\times\mathbb{R}^{6}.

Define
f(t,x,v)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}e^{\lambda t}f^{\prime}(t,x,v)
. Then, by linearity of the operator
\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}+\lambda
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f)=e^{\lambda t}(\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}f^{\prime}+\lambda f^{\prime})=\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon})I^{\varepsilon},\textup{ and }

(3.14)

f(t,x,v)=f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}(x,v)\quad\textup{ for }\,\,t\in[0,\varepsilon).

This completes the construction.
∎

By applying Lemma
3.5
inductively under the assumption (
3.7
), we construct a sequence
\{f^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}

\subset Y_{T,\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w}
satisfying the following equation for each
n\geq 1
:

(3.15)

\partial_{t}f^{n}+\chi\left(\frac{v}{n}\right)v\cdot\nabla_{x}f^{n}+J^{\frac{1}{n}}(t)\chi\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)E_{f^{n-1}}\cdot\nabla_{v}f^{n}-(-\Delta_{v})_{n^{-1}}^{-1}f^{n-1}\Delta_{v}f^{n}-f^{n-1}f^{n}\\
=\frac{1}{n}\Delta_{x,v}f^{n}+\mathscr{L}_{\frac{1}{n},f^{n-1}}(f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}})I^{\frac{1}{n}}(t)\quad\textup{ in }\,\,\overline{\mathscr{Q}_{T}},

with
f^{0}\equiv 0
. We will explicitly use this sequence in the discussion following Lemma
4.2
.

4.
Upper Bounds and Passing to the Limit

In this section, we establish the polynomial decay of the sequence
\{f^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
constructed in the previous section.
We first introduce a lemma on the comparison of weights, which will be used to show that each iterated element
f^{n}
exhibits the desired decay in
X_{T}^{m}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\varepsilon>0,T>0
, and
m>0
. Then for all
(t,x,v)\in\mathscr{Q}_{T}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle x\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m}\leq(2\langle T\rangle)^{m}\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m},

where
\chi(\cdot)
is the cut-off function defined as in (
3.2
).

Proof.

Fix
t\in[0,T]
. Then by the Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle 1+|x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 1+2|x|^{2}+2t^{2}|v|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+2|x|^{2})(1+2t^{2}|v|^{2}).

Thus,

\displaystyle\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{2}\leq 4(1+T^{2})\langle x\rangle^{2}\langle v\rangle^{2}.

Hence, in
0\leq t\leq T
, we have

\displaystyle\langle x\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m}\leq(2\langle T\rangle)^{m}\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}.

∎

We now introduce a crucial lemma concerning the comparison principle.
For each fixed
g
, we compare the solution
f
of the
\varepsilon
–approximated linear problem (
3.10
) with an auxiliary function
\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}
depending on
\varepsilon>0
.
This comparison directly yields the same upper and lower bounds for each iterated element
f^{n}
solving (
3.15
).

Lemma 4.2

(Function-wise upper and lower bound)
.

Let
g\in Y_{T,\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w}
satisfy
g\geq 0
and

\displaystyle g(t,x,v)\leq C_{g}\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon^{\prime}v)vt\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m},

for some
C_{g}\geq 0
and
0<\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq 1
. Let
f\in Y_{T,\alpha,p_{0},q_{0},w}
be a solution to the linear problem (
3.10
) for some
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}
with the approximated initial data
f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
(see the definition (
3.5
)) satisfying
f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
. Then, there exists a constant
M=M(m)>0
such that

\displaystyle f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}I^{\varepsilon}\leq f(t,x,v)\leq e^{M(T+1)^{3}(C_{g}+1)}\lVert f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}}(2\langle T\rangle)^{m}\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m},

for
(t,x,v)\in\overline{\mathscr{Q}_{T}}
where
I^{\varepsilon}
is defined as in (
3.3
).

Proof.

Upper bound on
t\in[0,\varepsilon]
.
In this interval, we have
f(t)=f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}
by (
3.14
). Then by Lemma
4.1
, we have

\displaystyle|f(t,x,v)|\leq\langle x\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-2m}|\langle x\rangle^{m}\langle v\rangle^{2m}f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}|\leq\lVert f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}}(2\langle T\rangle)^{m}\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m}.

This proves the desired upper bound with
M=0
.

Upper bound on
t\in[\varepsilon,T]
.
We define the auxiliary function

\displaystyle\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)=e^{C^{\prime}(t-\varepsilon)}(2\langle T\rangle)^{m}\lVert f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}(\mathbb{R}^{6})}\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m},

with a sufficiently large
C^{\prime}>0
which will be determined as in (
4.15
). Note that by Lemma
4.1
again, we have
\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon,x,v)\geq f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}(x,v)=f(\varepsilon,x,v)
. Therefore, by the comparison principle, to establish
f\leq\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}
, it suffices to verify the following:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(\overline{f}_{\varepsilon})\geq\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f)=\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon})I^{\varepsilon}\quad\textup{ in }\,\,(\varepsilon,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{6},

where
\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}
denotes the linear differential operator considered in (
3.4
).

To this end, we proceed to compute and estimate each term in
\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(\overline{f}_{\varepsilon})
.

(I) Transport term:
Note that

(4.2)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\chi(\varepsilon v)v\cdot\nabla_{x})\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}=C^{\prime}\overline{f}_{\varepsilon},

since
(\partial_{t}+\chi(\varepsilon v)v\cdot\nabla_{x})\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}\equiv 0
.

(II) Acceleration term:
To estimate the acceleration term
J^{\varepsilon}(t)\chi(\varepsilon x)E_{g}\cdot\nabla_{v}\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}
, we begin with computing the velocity gradient

\displaystyle\nabla_{v}\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}.

Using the chain rule, this term can be written as

\nabla_{v}\langle\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x\rangle^{-m}=-\frac{m}{2}\langle\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x\rangle^{-m-2}\nabla_{v}|\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x|^{2}\\
=-mt\langle\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x\rangle^{-m-2}\left[\chi(\varepsilon v)(\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x)+\varepsilon(\nabla_{v}\chi)(\varepsilon v)\left\{v\cdot(\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x)\right\}\right].

Now apply the bounds,
0\leq\chi\leq 1
,
|\nabla_{v}\chi|<\infty
and
|v|\leq\langle v\rangle
, we obtain the estimate:

(4.3)

\displaystyle|\nabla_{v}\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}|\leq C(m,\chi)T\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}.

To estimate the electric field
E_{g}
, we use the bound:

\displaystyle|E_{g}(t,x)|\leq C_{g}\sup_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{dv}{\langle v\rangle^{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{dy}{|x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-y|^{2}\langle y\rangle^{m}}.

By using (
3.8
), we conclude

(4.4)

\displaystyle\lVert E_{g}\rVert_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim_{m}C_{g}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle|J^{\varepsilon}(t)\chi(\varepsilon x)E_{g}\cdot\nabla_{v}\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}|

\displaystyle\leq\lVert E_{g}\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}|\nabla_{v}\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}|\leq C(m,\chi)\lVert E_{g}\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}(T+1)\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}

(4.5)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}(m,\chi)(T+1)C_{g}\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}.

Here, the constant
C_{1}
may be redefined, if necessary, to absorb numerical factors.

(III) Nonlocal collision term:
To estimate the term
(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}g\Delta_{v}\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}
, we first compute the velocity Laplacian of the weight function:

\displaystyle\Delta_{v}\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}.

By direct computation, this can be written as:

\Delta_{v}\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}=-mt\left[\nabla_{v}\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m-2}\right]\cdot[\chi(\varepsilon v)(\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x)\\
+\varepsilon(\nabla_{v}\chi)(\varepsilon v)\left\{v\cdot(\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x)\right\}]\\
-mt\langle\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x\rangle^{-m-2}\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[\chi(\varepsilon v)(\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x)+\varepsilon(\nabla_{v}\chi)(\varepsilon v)\left\{v\cdot(\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x)\right\}\right]\\
=:\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}.

We first estimate
\Delta_{1}
. Noting that

\nabla_{v}\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m-2}=-(m+2)t\langle\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x\rangle^{-m-4}[\chi(\varepsilon v)(\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x)\\
+\varepsilon(\nabla_{v}\chi)(\varepsilon v)\left\{v\cdot(\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x)\right\}],

we deduce the bound

(4.6)

\displaystyle|\nabla_{v}\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m-2}|\leq(1+\|\nabla_{v}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}})(m+2)T\langle\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x\rangle^{-m-3}.

Next, we simplify and estimate
\Delta_{2}
. For the first divergence component:

\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[\chi(\varepsilon v)(\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x)\right]=\nabla_{v}\left[\chi(\varepsilon v)\right]\cdot(\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x)+\chi(\varepsilon v)\nabla_{v}\cdot(\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x)\\
=\varepsilon(\nabla_{v}\chi)(\varepsilon v)\cdot(\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x)+\chi(\varepsilon v)\left[3t\chi(\varepsilon v)+v\cdot\varepsilon(\nabla_{v}\chi)(\varepsilon v)t\right],

yielding the bound

(4.7)

\displaystyle|\nabla_{v}\cdot[\chi(\varepsilon v)(\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x)]|\leq\|\nabla_{v}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}}|\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x|+(3+\|\nabla_{v}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}})T.

For the second divergence component, we compute:

\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[\varepsilon(\nabla_{v}\chi)(\varepsilon v)\left\{v\cdot(\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x)\right\}\right]\\
=\varepsilon^{2}(\Delta_{v}\chi)(\varepsilon v)\left\{v\cdot(\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x)\right\}+\varepsilon(\nabla_{v}\chi)(\varepsilon v)\cdot\nabla_{v}\left\{v\cdot(\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x)\right\}\\
=\varepsilon^{2}(\Delta_{v}\chi)(\varepsilon v)\left\{v\cdot(\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x)\right\}\\
+\varepsilon(\nabla_{v}\chi)(\varepsilon v)\cdot\left[3(\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x)+tv(\varepsilon(\nabla_{v}\chi)(\varepsilon v)\cdot v)+3tv\chi(\varepsilon v)\right].

Then using
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
, we obtain

(4.8)

|\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[\varepsilon(\nabla_{v}\chi)(\varepsilon v)\left\{v\cdot(\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x)\right\}\right]|\\
\leq\|\Delta_{v}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}}|\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x|+3\|\nabla_{v}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}}|\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x|+\|\nabla_{v}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}T+3\|\nabla_{v}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}}T\\
=(\|\Delta_{v}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}}+3\|\nabla_{v}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}})|\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x|+(\|\nabla_{v}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+3\|\nabla_{v}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}})T.

Combining these estimates (
4.6
), (
4.7
) and (
4.8
), we estimate the full Laplacian:

(4.9)

|\Delta_{v}\langle\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x\rangle^{-m}|\leq|\Delta_{1}|+|\Delta_{2}|\\
\leq mT(1+\|\nabla_{v}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}})^{2}(m+2)T\langle\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x\rangle^{-m-3}|\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x|\\
+mT\langle\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x\rangle^{-m-2}(\|\nabla_{v}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}}|\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x|+(3+\|\nabla_{v}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}})T\\
+(\|\Delta_{v}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}}+3\|\nabla_{v}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}})|\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x|+(\|\nabla_{v}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+3\|\nabla_{v}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}})T)\\
\leq C(m,\chi)(T+1)^{2}\langle\chi(\varepsilon v)vt-x\rangle^{-m}.

To complete the estimate, we compute the Laplacian of the full weight function:

\Delta_{v}\left(\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m}\right)=\Delta_{v}\left(\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}\right)\langle v\rangle^{-m}\\
+2\nabla_{v}\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}\cdot\nabla_{v}\langle v\rangle^{-m}+\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}\Delta_{v}\left(\langle v\rangle^{-m}\right).

Applying the previous bounds, (
4.3
) and (
4.9
), we obtain

(4.10)

\displaystyle\Delta_{v}\left(\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m}\right)\leq C(m,\chi)(T+1)^{2}\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m}.

Altogether, we obtain the following upper bound for the localized diffusion term:

\displaystyle|(-\Delta_{v})_{\varepsilon}^{-1}g\Delta_{v}\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}|\leq C_{2}(m,\chi)(T+1)^{2}\lVert(-\Delta_{v})_{\varepsilon}^{-1}g\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t,x,v}}\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}.

Moreover, since

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{v})_{\varepsilon}^{-1}g\leq C_{g}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{du}{|u-v|\langle u\rangle^{m}}\leq C_{2}(m)C_{g},

we conclude that

(4.11)

\displaystyle|(-\Delta_{v})_{\varepsilon}^{-1}g\Delta_{v}\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}|\leq C_{2}(m,\chi)(T+1)^{2}C_{g}\overline{f}_{\varepsilon},

where
C_{2}(m,\chi)
may be redefined to absorb all multiplicative factors, if necessary.

(IV) Additional collision term:
The remaining contribution from the collision operator is bounded from above in a straightforward manner. Specifically, we have

(4.12)

\displaystyle g\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}\leq C_{g}\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}.

(V) Artificial diffusion term:
To estimate the diffusion term
\Delta_{x,v}\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}
, we compute the spacial Laplacian of the weight function
\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}
. A direct calculation yields that

\Delta_{x}\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}=\nabla_{x}\cdot[-mx\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-(m+2)}]\\
=-3m\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-(m+2)}+m(m+2)\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-(m+4)}(x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt),

which implies the bound:

\displaystyle\Delta_{x}\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}\leq m(m+5)\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}.

Combining this with (
4.10
), we obtain

(4.13)

\displaystyle\Delta_{x,v}\overline{f}_{\varepsilon}\leq C_{2}(m,\chi)(T+1)^{2}\overline{f}_{\varepsilon},

where the constant
C_{2}
may be redefined to absorb numerical factors, if necessary.

(VI) Artifical inhomogeneous term associated with initial data:
Since
f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}\in\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}
, Lemma
4.1
yields the following estimate:

(4.14)

|\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon})|\leq\left(\lVert v\cdot\nabla_{x}f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{m,2m}}+(C_{g}+1)(C_{1}(m)\lVert\nabla_{v}f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{m,2m}}\right.\\
\left.+C_{2}(m)\lVert\Delta_{v}f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{m,2m}}+\lVert f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{m,2m}})\right)(\langle x\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m})\langle v\rangle^{-m}\\
\leq C(m,\chi)\lVert f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}}\left(C_{g}+1\right)(2\langle T\rangle)^{m}\langle x-\chi(\varepsilon v)vt\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m},

for some
C(m,\chi)>0.

Combining (
4.2
), (
4.5
), (
4.11
), (
4.12
), (
4.13
) and (
4.14
), we assign a sufficiently large constant

(4.15)

\displaystyle C^{\prime}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}M(m,\chi)(T+1)^{2}(C_{g}+1),

such that the
transport term
in
(I)
absorbs all the rest of the terms from
(II)
to
(VI)
on the left-hand side of the desired inequality (
4.1
). This allows us to conclude that

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(\overline{f}_{\varepsilon})\geq\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon})I^{\varepsilon}\quad\textup{ in }\,\,\mathscr{Q}_{T}.

By merging the upper bounds over two distinct time intervals, the desired estimate is achieved.

Lower bound in
t\in[0,\varepsilon]
.
Since
f=f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}
in this time interval, the desired lower bound
f\geq f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}I^{\varepsilon}
trivially holds.

Lower bound in
t\in[\varepsilon,T]
.
By comparison principle, it suffices to show

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}I^{\varepsilon})\leq\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f)=\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon})I^{\varepsilon}\quad\textup{ in }\,\,[\varepsilon,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{6}.

This inequality holds, since

\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}I^{\varepsilon})=\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon,g}(f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon})I^{\varepsilon}+f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}I^{\varepsilon},

and, by definition of
I^{\varepsilon}(t)
in (
3.3
), we have
\partial_{t}I^{\varepsilon}\leq 0
for each
t\in[\varepsilon,T]
and
\varepsilon>0
. Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle f^{\textup{in}}_{\varepsilon}I^{\varepsilon}\leq f\quad\textup{in}\,\,t\geq\varepsilon.

By combining the lower bounds on the two distinct time intervals, we obtain the desired estimate.

In the above proof, the constant
M
was written as if it depends on
\chi
. More precisely, increasing
\|\chi\|_{C^{2}}
increases the corresponding value
M(\chi)
. However, this dependence on
\chi
can in fact be removed. We may define

M(m)\coloneqq\inf\{\,M(m,\chi):\chi\in C_{c}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\textup{ and satisfies }\eqref{def cutoff}\,\},

which yields a choice of
M
independent of
\chi
. The infimum
M(m)>0
exists for each fixed
m
. This completes the proof.
∎

Since our initial sequence element
f^{0}\equiv 0
for the approximated system (

) is non-negative and bounded from above by a constant multiple of
\langle x-\chi(v)vt\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m}
, we can apply Lemma
4.2
inductively on each element of the iterated solutions
\{f^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
. Then we first obtain that
f^{1}
satisfies

(4.16)

\displaystyle f^{\textup{in}}_{1}I^{1}\leq f^{1}\leq C_{0}\left\langle x-\chi\left(v\right)vt\right\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m},

for some
C_{0}>0
. Then by choosing
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\frac{1}{n-1}
and
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{n}
on each step for
n\geq 2
, we have

(4.17)

\displaystyle f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}}I^{\frac{1}{n}}\leq f^{n}\leq C_{n-1}\left\langle x-\chi\left(\frac{v}{n}\right)vt\right\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m},

where
C_{n-1}
depends only on
f^{n-1},T,f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}}
and
m
. In Lemma
4.3
, we will choose
f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}}\in\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}
sufficiently small so that the sequence
\{C_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}
is uniformly bounded.

Lemma 4.3

(Uniform upper bound)
.

Fix
m>3
. Let
M=M(m)
be as introduced in Lemma
4.2
. Assume that the initial datum
f^{\textup{in}}\in\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
satisfies
f^{\textup{in}}\geq 0
and

(4.18)

\displaystyle\|f^{\textup{in}}\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}}\leq\frac{1}{e^{-e}M(2\langle T\rangle)^{m}(T+1)^{3}e^{M(T+1)^{3}}}.

Then each sequence element
f^{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}}I^{\frac{1}{n}}\leq f^{n}\leq C\left\langle x-\chi\left(\frac{v}{n}\right)vt\right\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m}\quad\textup{ in }\,\,\mathscr{Q}_{T},

for some
C=C(f^{\textup{in}},m)
given by

C=\frac{c_{0}}{M^{2}\,\|f^{\textup{in}}\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}}},

where
c_{0}>0
is an absolute constant. In particular, this lemma specifies the time interval
[0,T]
associated with the local existence result stated in Theorem
2.3
.

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle\|f^{\textup{in}}\|\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\|f^{\textup{in}}\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}}(2\langle T\rangle)^{m},

and for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define

\displaystyle\|f\|_{n}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\sup_{(t,x,v)\in\overline{\mathscr{Q}_{T}}}\langle x-\chi(v/n)vt\rangle^{m}\langle v\rangle^{m}|f(t,x,v)|.

Before proceeding, recall the definition of the approximated initial data
f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}}
in (
3.5
), which is obtained by cutting off
f^{\textup{in}}
. Consequently,
\|f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}}\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}}\uparrow\|f^{\textup{in}}\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}}
as
n\to\infty
. For
n=0
, we put
f^{0}\equiv 0
. Also, introduce the function

\displaystyle\Phi(s)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}e^{M(T+1)^{3}(s+1)}\lVert f^{\textup{in}}\rVert.

We begin with the base case. We first observe that

\displaystyle\lVert f^{1}\rVert_{1}\leq\Phi(\lVert f^{0}\rVert_{0})

holds by Lemma
4.2
with
g=f^{0}=0
,
C_{g}=0
,
f=f^{1}
,
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon=1
. Recall that
\lVert f^{0}\rVert_{0}=0
. Next, by applying Lemma
4.2
for
f^{2}
with
g=f^{1}
,
C_{g}=\|f^{1}\|_{1}
,
f=f^{2}
,
\varepsilon^{\prime}=1,
and
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\lVert f^{2}\rVert_{2}\leq\exp\left[M(T+1)^{3}(\lVert f^{1}\rVert_{1}+1)\right]\lVert f^{\textup{in}}_{\frac{1}{2}}\rVert\leq\Phi(\lVert f^{1}\rVert_{1})\leq(\Phi\circ\Phi)(0),

since
\Phi
is increasing. By induction, defining
\Phi^{k}
as the
k
-fold composition of
\Phi
, it follows that

\displaystyle\lVert f^{k}\rVert_{k}\leq\Phi^{k}(0)\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

To conclude the existence of a uniform-in-
n
constant
C>0
in Lemma
4.3
, it suffices to show the sequence
\{\Phi^{k}(0)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
remains bounded – i.e., that it admits a finite supremum – and does not converge to zero. According to Lemma
A.2
, this can be guaranteed by choosing
T>0
sufficiently small depending on
\lVert f^{\textup{in}}\rVert_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}}
so that the algebraic equation

\displaystyle\lVert f^{\textup{in}}\rVert e^{M(T+1)^{3}(s+1)}=s,

admits a real root. More precisely, a hypothesis of Lemma
4.3
implies

\displaystyle\|f^{\mathrm{in}}\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}}\,(2\langle T\rangle)^{m}M(T+1)^{3}e^{M(T+1)^{3}}\leq\frac{1}{e},

which guarantees the existence of such a root (see Lemma
A.2
(i), with
c=\|f^{\textup{in}}\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}}
and
A=(2\langle T\rangle)^{m}M(T+1)^{3}e^{M(T+1)^{3}}
in the notation of Lemma
A.2
).

Finally, we find a uniform upper bound of the weighted
L^{\infty}
norm of
\{f^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
, that is,
\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\Phi^{k}(0)
.
By Lemma
A.2
(ii), we have

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\langle x-\chi(v/n)vt\rangle^{m}\langle v\rangle^{m}f^{n}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{6})}\leq\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Phi^{n}(0)\leq s_{\mathrm{large}},

where
s_{\mathrm{large}}>0
(see Lemma
A.2
(iii)) denotes the larger real root of the algebraic
equation

\Phi(s)=s,

whenever the two real roots exist, and denotes the unique real root in the case that the equation admits only one real solution.
Using the asymptotic behavior of the Lambert
W
-function, the larger real
root of the equation
ae^{bs}=s
with

a\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\|f^{\mathrm{in}}\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}}\,e^{M(T+1)^{3}},\qquad b\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}M(T+1)^{3}

satisfies the upper bound

s_{\mathrm{large}}\leq\frac{c_{0}}{ab^{2}},

for some absolute constant
c_{0}>0
.
Therefore, by choosing
C\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}c_{0}M^{-2}\|f^{\textup{in}}\|^{-1}_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}}
and recalling that
a,b>0
, we obtain
the desired local-in-time
L^{\infty}
bound.
∎

.

(4.19)

\displaystyle\psi(t)=(2\langle t\rangle)^{m}(t+1)^{3}e^{-M(t+1)^{3}},\qquad t\geq 0

for fixed

, where

.

We are now ready to prove our main local existence result, Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

The proof of Theorem
2.3
consists of two steps.

Step 1: Passing to the limit.
By Lemma
4.3
, the sequence
\{f^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})
. Hence, by the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, there exists a function
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})
such that
f^{n}
converges to
f
in the weak*-topology of
L^{\infty}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})
; i.e., in
\sigma(L^{\infty}(\mathscr{Q}_{T}),L^{1}(\mathscr{Q}_{T}))
. In addition to this boundedness, we establish the non-negativity and the polynomial decay of
f
in
x,v
of order
(m,m)
. Let
\varphi\in C_{c}^{2}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})
be a non-negative test function,
\varphi\geq 0
. Then, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathscr{Q}_{T}}f\varphi=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathscr{Q}_{T}}f^{n}\varphi\leq C\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathscr{Q}_{T}}\left\langle x-\chi\left(\frac{v}{n}\right)vt\right\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m}\varphi,

where
C>0
is the constant from Lemma
4.3
. Note that

\displaystyle\left\langle x-\chi\left(\frac{v}{n}\right)vt\right\rangle^{-m}\to\langle x-vt\rangle^{-m},\quad\textup{uniformly on each compact subset of $\mathscr{Q}_{T}$.}

Hence, by dominated convergence on the compact support of
\varphi
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathscr{Q}_{T}}f\varphi\leq C\int_{\mathscr{Q}_{T}}\langle x-vt\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m}\varphi.

Since this inequality holds for every non-negative
\varphi
, we apply elementary pointwise bounds for weak formulation
[
7
, Exercise 4.26]
to conclude

\displaystyle f\leq C\langle x-vt\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m}\quad\textup{almost everywhere in }\,\,\mathscr{Q}_{T}.

To show non-negativity of
f
, we observe that for any
\varphi\in C_{c}^{2}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})
with
\varphi\geq 0
:

\displaystyle 0\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathscr{Q}_{T}}f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}}I^{\frac{1}{n}}\varphi\leq\int_{\mathscr{Q}_{T}}f\varphi.

Hence,
f\geq 0
almost everywhere in
\mathscr{Q}_{T}
. Combining the upper and lower bounds, we obtain

0\leq f\leq C\langle x-vt\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m}.

Next, we verify that the limit
f
belongs to either
W_{p_{0}}^{1,2}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})
or
W_{q_{0},w}^{1,2}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})
. Recall the global a priori estimates, (
3.12
) and (
3.13
), and note that
f^{n}
solves

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{\frac{1}{n},f^{n-1}}(f^{n})=\mathscr{L}_{\frac{1}{n},f^{n-1}}(f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}})I^{1/n}\quad\textup{ in }\mathscr{Q}_{T}.

Thus, we have

(4.20)

\displaystyle\|f^{n}\|_{W_{p_{0}}^{1,2}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\mathscr{L}_{1/n,f^{n-1}}(f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}})I^{1/n}\|_{L_{p_{0}}(\Omega_{T})},

(4.21)

\displaystyle\|f^{n}\|_{W_{q_{0},w}^{1,2}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\mathscr{L}_{1/n,f^{n-1}}(f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}})I^{1/n}\|_{L_{q_{0},w}(\Omega_{T})}.

From the general parabolic regularity theory, the implied constants in these estimates grow polynomially in
(\Lambda/\lambda)
and
1/\lambda
, where

\displaystyle 0<\lambda\leq(\textup{leading coefficient matrix})\leq\Lambda.

For the present sequence of problems, Lemma
4.3
gives

\displaystyle\lambda^{-1}=n\quad\textup{and}\quad(\Lambda/\lambda)=1+n\|(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f^{n-1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})}\lesssim n+1.

Define the growth exponents

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}/p_{0}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}

\displaystyle\textup{ the maximum of the polynomial growth order in }\lambda^{-1}\textup{ and }(\Lambda/\lambda)

\displaystyle\textup{ arising from the global estimate }W_{p_{0}}^{1,2}(\Omega_{T})\to L_{p_{0}}(\Omega_{T}),

\displaystyle\kappa_{2}/q_{0}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}

\displaystyle\textup{ the maximum of the polynomial growth order in }\lambda^{-1}\textup{ and }(\Lambda/\lambda)

\displaystyle\textup{ arising from the global estimate }W_{q_{0},w}^{1,2}(\Omega_{T})\to L_{q_{0},w}(\Omega_{T}),

and set
0<\kappa\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\min\{\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\}.
Assume first that
\kappa=\kappa_{1}
, and recall the definition of the linear problem (
3.4
), in particular that the time-regularized characteristic function
I^{\varepsilon}
converges at rate
\varepsilon^{\kappa}
. Applying (
4.20
) to
f^{n}
gives

\displaystyle\|f^{n}\|_{W_{p_{0}}^{1,2}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})}

\displaystyle\leq\|f^{n}\|_{W_{p_{0}}^{1,2}(\Omega_{T})}

\displaystyle\lesssim(n+1)^{\kappa/p_{0}}\|\mathscr{L}_{1/n,f^{n-1}}(f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}})I^{1/(n+1)^{\kappa}}\|_{L_{p_{0}}(\Omega_{T})}

\displaystyle\lesssim(n+1)^{\kappa/p_{0}}\left(\int_{-1/(n+1)^{\kappa}}^{2/(n+1)^{\kappa}}\|\mathscr{L}_{1/n,f^{n-1}}(f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}})\|^{p_{0}}_{L_{p_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{6})}\,dt\right)^{1/p_{0}}

(4.22)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|\mathscr{L}_{1/n,f^{n-1}}(f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}})\|_{L_{p_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{6})}.

The implicit constants here are independent of
n
. The final term in (
4.22
) is finite because

\|(E_{f^{n-1}},(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f^{n-1})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})}

is uniformly bounded in
n
by Lemma
4.3
and
\|f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}}\|_{L_{p_{0}}}\lesssim_{m}\|f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}}\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}}\leq\|f^{\textup{in}}\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}}
with same estimate for its derivatives. Consequently,
\{f^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is bounded in
W_{p_{0}}^{1,2}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})
. By reflexivity, there exists a weakly convergent subsequence whose limit
g
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{\mathscr{Q}_{T}}g\varphi=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathscr{Q}_{T}}f^{n}\varphi=\int_{\mathscr{Q}_{T}}f\varphi,\quad\forall\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})\subset L_{p_{0}}(\mathscr{Q}_{T}),

so that
g=f
almost everywhere in
\mathscr{Q}_{T}
.

The case
\kappa=\kappa_{2}
is treated analogously, but when we obtain the finiteness of the norm of
\mathscr{L}_{n^{-1},f^{n-1}}(f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}})
in
L_{q_{0},w}
, notice that
L^{\infty}_{m,2m}(\mathbb{R}^{6})\hookrightarrow L_{q_{0},w}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
holds for
m>3
,
3<q_{0}<\frac{11}{3}
and
w=\langle x\rangle\langle v\rangle^{8}
. Hence,
\{f^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is bounded in
W_{q_{0},w}^{1,2}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})
and its weak limit belongs to this weighted Sobolev space.

Combining these two inclusions, we conclude that the limit function
f
belongs to the space
X_{T}^{m}
.

Step 2: Verification of the weak formulation in the limit.
We now show that the limit function
f
, obtained in the previous subsection above, satisfies the weak formulation defined in Definition
2.2
, with dependence on the initial data
f^{\textup{in}}
. Let
\varphi\in C_{c}^{2}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{6})
be a smooth, compactly supported test function. Multiplying the approximate equation (
3.15
) by
\varphi
and integrating over
\mathscr{Q}_{T}
, we obtain:

\int_{\mathscr{Q}_{T}}\varphi\left[\partial_{t}f^{n}+\chi\left(\frac{v}{n}\right)v\cdot\nabla_{x}f^{n}+J^{\frac{1}{n}}(t)\chi(x/n)E_{f^{n-1}}\cdot\nabla_{v}f^{n}\right]\,dxdvdt\\
=\int_{\mathscr{Q}_{T}}\varphi\left[(-\Delta_{v})_{n^{-1}}^{-1}f^{n-1}\Delta_{v}f^{n}+f^{n-1}f^{n}+\frac{1}{n}\Delta_{x,v}f^{n}+\mathscr{L}_{\frac{1}{n},f^{n-1}}(f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}})I^{\frac{1}{n}}\right]\,dxdvdt.

To verify convergence to the weak formulation, we analyze the left-hand and right-hand sides by separating them into linear and nonlinear components.

•

Linear terms:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f^{n},\quad\chi\left(\frac{v}{n}\right)v\cdot\nabla_{x}f^{n},\quad(1/n)\Delta_{x,v}f^{n},\quad\textup{and}\quad\mathscr{L}_{n}(f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}})I^{\frac{1}{n}}.

•

Nonlinear terms:

\displaystyle\chi(x/n)E_{f^{n-1}}\cdot\nabla_{v}f^{n},\quad(-\Delta_{v})_{n^{-1}}^{-1}f^{n-1}\Delta_{v}f^{n},\quad\textup{and}\quad f^{n-1}f^{n}.

We now verify that the linear terms in the approximate equations converges to their counterparts in the weak formulation as
n\to\infty
, using the test function
\varphi\in C_{c}^{2}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{6})\subset L^{1}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})
.

(L1) Time derivative term:
We first examine the time derivative term:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi\partial_{t}f^{n}\,dxdvd\tau=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left[-\iint\varphi(0)f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}}\,dxdv-\int_{0}^{T}\iint\partial_{t}\varphi f^{n}\,dxdvd\tau\right]\\
=-\iint\varphi(0)f^{\textup{in}}\,dxdv-\int_{0}^{T}\iint\partial_{t}\varphi f\,dxdvd\tau.

Here, in the first equality, we used
f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}}\to f^{\textup{in}}
as
n\to\infty
in
L^{\infty}_{x,v}
and in the second equality, we used the definition of the weak*-convergence
f^{n}\rightharpoonup f
in
L^{\infty}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})
.

(L2) Advection term:
Next, consider the transport term:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi\chi(v/n)v\cdot\nabla_{x}f^{n}\,dxdvd\tau=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\iint f^{n}\chi(v/n)v\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi\,dxdvd\tau.

Since
\chi(v/n)v\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi\equiv v\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi
for large
n
, we have:

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\iint fv\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi\,dxdvd\tau.

(L3) Diffusion term:
The artificial term vanishes in the limit:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{n}\Delta_{x,v}f^{n}\varphi\,dxdvd\tau

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\iint\frac{1}{n}f^{n}\Delta_{x,v}\varphi\,dxdvd\tau

\displaystyle=0,

since
\Delta_{x,v}\varphi\in C_{c}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})
and
\{f^{n}\}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}(\mathscr{Q}_{T})
.

(L4) Inhomogeneous source term:
Finally, we analyze the inhomogeneous source term:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\varphi\mathscr{L}_{\frac{1}{n},f^{n-1}}(f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}})I^{n}\,dxdvd\tau\to 0\quad\textup{ as }\,\,n\to\infty,

because the integrand converges to zero pointwisely and is uniformly bounded by an integrable function due to the decay of
\mathscr{L}_{\frac{1}{n},f^{n-1}}(f^{\textup{in}}_{n^{-1}})
. Hence, the dominated convergence theorem applies.

Thus, all linear terms in the approximate equation converge to their respective terms in the weak formulation satisfied by
f
.

Before passing to the limit in the nonlinear terms, we obtain the following convergence from the dominated convergence theorem:

(4.23)

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{v})_{n^{-1}}^{-1}f^{n}\to(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f,\quad

\displaystyle\textup{in }\mathscr{Q}_{T},

(4.24)

\displaystyle\nabla_{v}(-\Delta_{v})_{n^{-1}}^{-1}f^{n}\to\nabla_{v}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f,\quad

\displaystyle\textup{in }\mathscr{Q}_{T},

(4.25)

\displaystyle E_{f^{n}}\to E_{f},\quad

\displaystyle\textup{in }\mathscr{Q}_{T}.

To prove these convergences, we use (i)
f^{n}\to f
a.e. in
\mathscr{Q}_{T}
up to a subsequence, and (ii)
|f^{n}(t,x,v)|\leq C\langle x\rangle^{-m}\langle v\rangle^{-m}
. Since (ii) is established in Step 1, it remains only to prove (i). The latter follows from the Rellich-Kondrachov theorem together with local compactness on bounded cylinders and a diagonal argument.

Now, we inspect the nonlinearity term by term:

(N1) Collision terms:
We analyze the term

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{v})_{n^{-1}}^{-1}f^{n-1}\Delta_{v}f^{n}+f^{n}f^{n-1}.

Testing against
\varphi\in C_{c}^{2}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{6})
, we compute:

\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\varphi[(-\Delta_{v})_{n^{-1}}^{-1}f^{n-1}\Delta_{v}f^{n}+f^{n-1}f^{n}]\,dxdvd\tau\\
=\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}f^{n}\Delta_{v}[\varphi(-\Delta_{v})_{n^{-1}}^{-1}f^{n-1}]+\varphi f^{n-1}f^{n}\,dxdvd\tau\\
=\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}f^{n}\Delta_{v}\varphi(-\Delta_{v})_{n^{-1}}^{-1}f^{n-1}\,dxdd\tau+\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}2f^{n}\nabla_{v}\varphi\cdot\nabla_{v}(-\Delta_{v})_{n^{-1}}^{-1}f^{n-1}\,dxdvd\tau\\
+\int_{0}^{T}\varphi f^{n-1}f^{n}(1-\chi(v/n))\,dxdvd\tau=:I+II+III.

For the term
I
, we further decompose it as

I-\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}f\Delta_{v}\varphi(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\,dxdvd\tau\\
=\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(f^{n}-f)\Delta_{v}\varphi(-\Delta_{v})_{n^{-1}}^{-1}f^{n-1}\,dxdvd\tau\\
+\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}f\Delta_{v}\varphi(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}(\chi(v/n)f^{n-1}-f)\,dxdvd\tau=:I_{1}+I_{2}.

For
I_{1}
, since
(-\Delta_{v})_{n^{-1}}^{-1}f^{n-1}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}
uniformly in
n\in\mathbb{N}
due to the estimate

\displaystyle|(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f^{n}(t,x,v)|\leq\frac{C}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{du}{|u-v|\langle u\rangle^{m}}<\infty,

(by decay properties from Lemma
4.3
), and since
f^{n}\to f
a.e. with uniform integrable bound, the dominated convergence theorem provides
I_{1}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. For
I_{2}
, by the convergence (
4.23
) and the compact support of
\Delta_{v}\varphi
, the dominated convergence again applies, giving
I_{2}\to 0
. Therefore,

(4.26)

\displaystyle I\to\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}f(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\Delta_{v}\varphi\,dxdvd\tau,\quad n\to\infty.

For the term
II
, we again decompose the term as

\frac{1}{2}\left(II-2\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}f\nabla_{v}\varphi\cdot\nabla_{v}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\,dxdvd\tau\right)\\
=\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(f^{n}-f)\nabla_{v}\varphi\cdot\nabla_{v}(-\Delta_{v})_{n^{-1}}^{-1}f^{n-1}+f\nabla_{v}\varphi\cdot\nabla_{v}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}[\chi(v/n)f^{n-1}-f]\,dxdvd\tau\\
=:II_{1}+II_{2}.

For the difference
II_{1}
, using again that
\nabla_{v}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f^{n-1}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}
uniformly in
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
f^{n}\to f
a.e., we conclude
II_{1}\to 0
via dominated convergence. Next, for
II_{2}
, the convergence (
4.24
) and the boundedness of
f
and
\nabla_{v}\varphi
on the compact support again ensures
II_{2}\to 0
via dominated convergence. Hence,

(4.27)

\displaystyle II\to 2\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}f\nabla_{v}\varphi\cdot\nabla_{v}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\,dxdvd\tau.

For the term
III
, since
\varphi(1-\chi(v/n))=0
for sufficiently large
n
, we conclude that by the dominated convergence again,
III\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Combining (
4.26
), (
4.27
) and the fact that
III\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, we find that the nonlinear collision term converges to its weak limit:

\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\varphi\left[(-\Delta_{v})_{n^{-1}}^{-1}f^{n-1}\cdot\Delta_{v}f^{n}+f^{n-1}f^{n}\right]\,dxdvd\tau\\
\to\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}f\cdot(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\cdot\Delta_{v}\varphi+2f\nabla_{v}\varphi\cdot\nabla_{v}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\,dxdvd\tau.

Thus it verifies convergence of the nonlinear terms in the weak formulation.

(N2) Acceleration term:
We now analyze the nonlinear acceleration term

\displaystyle\chi(x/n)E_{f^{n-1}}\cdot\nabla_{v}f^{n}.

Testing this term against
\varphi\in C_{c}^{2}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{6})
, we consider the limit

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\chi(x/n)E_{f^{n-1}}\cdot\nabla_{v}f^{n}\varphi\,dxdvd\tau-\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}E_{f}\cdot\nabla_{v}f\varphi\,dxdvd\tau.

Integrating by parts in the velocity variable, and using that for sufficiently large
n
,
\chi(x/n)\varphi\equiv\varphi
, we write:

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(f^{n}E_{f^{n-1}}-fE_{f})\cdot\nabla_{v}\varphi\,dxdvd\tau.

Splitting the integrand:

=-\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(f^{n}-f)E_{f^{n-1}}\cdot\nabla_{v}\varphi+f(E_{f^{n-1}}-E_{f})\cdot\nabla_{v}\varphi\,dxdvd\tau=:\textit{III}_{1}+\textit{III}_{2}.

For
\textit{III}_{1}
, since
E_{f^{n-1}}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}
uniformly in
n
by (
4.4
), we have
\textit{III}_{1}\to 0
by dominated convergence. For
\textit{III}_{2}
, we use the convergence:

\displaystyle E_{f^{n-1}}\to E_{f}\quad\textup{ a.e. in }\,\mathscr{Q}_{T},

as established in (
4.25
). Moreover, since
f
is fixed and bounded (with decay), and
\nabla_{v}\varphi
is compactly supported, we again apply the dominated convergence theorem to obtain
\textit{III}_{2}\to 0
.

Combining the two limits, we conclude:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\chi(x/n)E_{f}^{n-1}\cdot\nabla_{v}f^{n}\varphi\,dxdvd\tau\to\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}fE_{f}\cdot\nabla_{v}\varphi\,dxdvd\tau.

Therefore, we have shown that the limit
f
is a weak solution to the initial value problem of (
1.1
) for
f^{\textup{in}}\in\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
with
f^{\textup{in}}\geq 0
.∎

Remark 4.5
.

The solution
f
constructed above is never identically equal to zero for any
t>0
, unless the initial data satisfies
f^{\textup{in}}\equiv 0
. This follows from the structure of the weak formulation in Definition
2.2
.

Remark 4.6
.

Although
f^{\textup{in}}\in C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
, the same argument as in (
4.22
) does not yield the
C^{2,\alpha}_{\textup{para}}
regularity of
f
, since

\|I^{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{\alpha/2}_{t}}\to\infty\quad\textup{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

5.
Singularity Formation

In this section, we prove Theorem
2.7
and Corollary
2.9
. Throughout, we assume the hypotheses of Theorem
2.7
and let
f
denote the corresponding solution to (
1.1
). We also recall from Theorem
2.7
that gravitational interaction is given by the Coulomb-type potential
K(x)=-\frac{1}{4\pi|x|}
.

In Lemma
5.1
, we establish the
L^{\infty}
–boundedness of the coefficient appearing in the potential, as well as the boundedness of its derivatives, namely the gravitational field
E_{f}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For each
t\in[0,T]
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}K\star_{x}\rho,\quad K\star_{x}\rho\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}),

where
\star_{x}
denotes the convolution in the spatial variable.

Proof.

This follows from Lemma
A.1
and the integrability of the local mass
\rho\in L^{\infty}([0,T];(L^{1}\cap L^{\infty})(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
, which is ensured by the decay condition imposed on
f\in X_{T}^{m}
.
∎

Before proceeding, we establish rigorously the differentiability of the relevant real-valued functionals associated with
f
.

Lemma 5.2
.

We have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
|x|^{2}\\
|v|^{2}\\
x\cdot v\end{pmatrix}f(t,x,v)\,dxdv

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
|x|^{2}\\
|v|^{2}\\
x\cdot v\end{pmatrix}\partial_{t}f(t,x,v)\,dxdv,\quad\textup{for a.e. }t\in[0,T],

and for each fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
we have for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{t}f(t,x,v)\,dv,

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\cdot j(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}v\cdot\nabla_{x}f(t,x,v)\,dv.

Proof.

Let
w(x,v)\in\{1,|x|^{2},|v|^{2},x\cdot v\}
and let
\bar{f}\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}wf
. Since
f\in X_{T}^{m}\cap A_{2}
, we have
|\partial_{t}\bar{f}|\leq wg_{1}
for all
(t,x,v)
, where
wg_{1}\in L^{\infty}_{x,v}
with
g_{1}
bounded. Then for
(x,v)
, we have that
\partial_{t}\bar{f}(\cdot,x,v)
is bounded in
[0,T]
, hence for the mapping
t\mapsto\bar{f}(t,x,v)
, the fundamental theorem of calculus holds

\displaystyle\bar{f}(t+h,x,v)-\bar{f}(t,x,v)=\int_{t}^{t+h}\partial_{\tau}\bar{f}(\tau,x,v)\,d\tau\quad\textup{for }t,t+h\in[0,T].

By integrating the above identity, we obtain for
t,t+h\in[0,T]
,

(5.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\bar{f}(t+h,x,v)-\bar{f}(t,x,v)\,dxdv=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\frac{1}{h}\left(\int_{t}^{t+h}\partial_{\tau}\bar{f}(\tau,x,v)\,d\tau\right)\,dxdv.

Indeed, by the Lebesgue differentiation theorem and dominated convergence theorem, the right-hand side converges to
\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\partial_{t}\bar{f}(t,x,v)\,dxdv
as
h\to 0
for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
. The integrability of
\partial_{t}\bar{f}
is guaranteed by
|\partial_{t}\bar{f}|\leq|w|g_{1}\in L^{1}_{x,v}
. Therefore, this establishes the first claim.

The remaining assertions follow by the same argument, using the corresponding domination assumptions (e.g.
g_{1}\in L^{\infty}_{x}L^{1}_{v}
and
g_{2}\in(\dot{L}^{\infty}_{1}\cap\dot{L}^{1}_{1})_{v}
) for the relevant weighted quantities. This completes the proof.
∎

In Lemma
5.3
and
5.4
, we show two fundamental conservation laws.

Lemma 5.3

(Mass conservation)
.

It holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\lVert\rho(t)\rVert_{L^{1}_{x}}=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}f^{\textup{in}}(x,v)\,dvdx<\infty.

Proof.

In view of (
5.1
) with
w=1
, it suffices to show that
\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\partial_{t}f(t,x,v)\,dxdv=0
for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
, which implies the conservation of the total mass.

Since
f
satisfies the equation (
1.1
) for a.e. in
\mathscr{Q}_{T}
, we may substitute the equation into the integrand
\partial_{t}f
to obtain for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\partial_{t}f(t)\,dvdx=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}-v\cdot\nabla_{x}f-E_{f}\cdot\nabla_{v}f+(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\Delta_{v}f+f^{2}\,dvdx.

By the assumptions
f\in X_{T}^{m}
, together with (
2.7
) and (
2.9
), each term on the right-hand side of (
5.2
) belongs to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
. Hence, all the integrals are well-defined, and linearity of integration applies.

We now evaluate each term. The first two terms vanish by the divergence theorem for Sobolev functions in
x
and
v
, respectively, since the associated fluxes are integrable and decay at infinity. The third term vanishes by integration by parts for Sobolev functions in
v
, noting that
(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\Delta_{v}f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
. This integrability follows from the assumption
f\in X_{T}^{m}
together with (
2.7
) and (
2.9
). Finally, the remaining term
f^{2}
is integrable and cancels with the corresponding contribution from the collision operator.

Consequently, the right-hand side of (
5.2
) vanishes, and therefore
\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\partial_{t}f(t,x,v)\,dxdv=0
for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.4

(Continuity equation)
.

It holds that, for each fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho(t,x)+\nabla\cdot j(t,x)=0,\quad\textup{ for a.e. }t\in[0,T].

Proof.

Throughout this proof, fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. By Lemma
5.2
, integrating equation (
1.1
) with respect to
v
yields

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla_{x}\cdot j=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}-E_{f}\cdot\nabla_{v}f+(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\Delta_{v}f+f^{2}\,dv,\quad\text{ for a.e. }t\in[0,T].

By the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
5.3
, each term on the right-hand side is integrable in
v
and vanishes by applying the divergence theorem to the first term and integration by parts to the second term. Consequently, the right-hand side is identically zero, which proves (
5.3
).
∎

Now, recall from either Definition
2.5
or Section
2.3
that the functional
\textup{I}(t)
is defined as

\displaystyle\textup{I}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|x|^{2}f(t,x,v)\,dxdv.

In Lemma
5.5
, we derive explicit formulas for
\textup{I}^{\prime}(t)
and
\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(t)
, respectively.

Lemma 5.5
.

The first and second derivatives of
\textup{I}(t)
are given by

\displaystyle\textup{I}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(x\cdot v)f(t,x,v)\,dxdv,\quad\textup{for a.e. }t\in[0,T],

\displaystyle\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|v|^{2}f\,dxdv+\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(K\star_{x}\rho)f\,dxdv\quad\textup{for a.e. }t\in[0,T].

Proof.

By Lemma
5.2
, the function
\textup{I}(t)
is differentiable for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
, and its first derivative is given by
\textup{I}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|x|^{2}\partial_{t}f(t,x,v)\,dxdv
for a.e.
t
. Substituting equation (
1.1
) into this expression
\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|x|^{2}\partial_{t}f\,dxdv
and applying the divergence theorem together with integration by parts, under the condition
m>6
and the admissible assumptions (
2.7
) and (
2.9
), we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textup{I}^{\prime}(t)&=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|x|^{2}\partial_{t}f(t,x,v)\,dvdx\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|x|^{2}\left[-v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+(\nabla_{x}K\star_{x}\rho)\cdot\nabla_{v}f+(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\Delta_{v}f+f^{2}\right]\,dvdx\\
&=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(x\cdot v)f(t,x,v)\,dvdx.\end{split}

We now turn to the second derivative
\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(t)
. By Lemma
5.2
again,
\textup{I}^{\prime}(t)
is differentiable for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
and its derivative is given by
\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(x\cdot v)\partial_{t}f\,dxdv
. Substituting equation (
1.1
) into this expression and applying integration by parts and divergence theorem under the admissible assumptions (
2.7
) and (
2.9
), we arrive at a representation of
\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(t)
:

\displaystyle\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(x\cdot v)\left[-v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+(\nabla_{x}K\star_{x}\rho)\cdot\nabla_{v}f+(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\Delta_{v}f+f^{2}\right]\,dvdx

(5.4)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|v|^{2}f-x\cdot(\nabla_{x}K\star_{x}\rho)f+2(x\cdot\nabla_{v}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f)f\,dvdx.

To simplify (
5.4
), we first establish the following identity

(5.5)

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(x\cdot\nabla_{v}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f)f\,dxdv=0.

Indeed, the integrand
(x\cdot\nabla_{v}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f)f
is integrable since
\nabla_{v}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\in L^{\infty}_{t,x,v}
and
xf(t)\in L^{1}_{x,v}
. Exchanging the velocity variables in the double integral shows that the expression is antisymmetric, and hence equal to its own negative, which implies (
5.5
).

Next, we evaluate the force term. Using the identity
\nabla_{x}K(x)=+\frac{x}{4\pi|x|^{3}}
, we rewrite the integral involving
x\cdot(\nabla_{x}K\star\rho)f
as a double integral in the spatial variables. By symmetry and an application of Fubini’s theorem, the mixed term cancels, leaving the expression:

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}x\cdot(\nabla_{x}K\star_{x}\rho)f\,dvdx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\frac{\rho(y)}{|x-y|}\,dy\rho(x)\,dx

(5.6)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(K\star_{x}\rho)\rho\,dx.

Substituting the expression (
5.5
) and (
5.6
) into (
5.4
), we obtain the desired formula for
\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}
.
∎

In Lemma
5.7
, we derive an explicit expression for
\textup{I}^{\prime\prime\prime}
. Lemma
5.6
is then devoted to the evaluation of one of the terms arising in
\textup{I}^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)
.

Lemma 5.6
.

We have for a.e.
t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho(t,x)(K\star\rho)(t,x)\,dx=2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\nabla K\star\rho)(t,x)\cdot j(t,x)\,dx.

Proof.

Firstly, we show

(5.7)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho(K\star\rho)(t,x)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{t}\rho(K\star\rho)(t,x)+\rho(K\star\partial_{t}\rho)(t,x)\,dx\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in[0,T].

To this end, recall from Lemma
5.2
that

\displaystyle\rho(t,y)-\rho(s,y)=\int_{s}^{t}\partial_{t}\rho(\tau,y)\,d\tau\quad\textup{ for }t,s\in[0,T],\textup{ for }y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

Multiplying this identity by
K(x-y)
and integrating with respect to
y
, we obtain

(5.8)

\displaystyle(K\star\rho)(t,x)-(K\star\rho)(s,x)=\int_{s}^{t}(K\star\partial_{t}\rho)(\tau,x)\,d\tau\quad\textup{ for }t,s,\textup{ for }x.

Consequently, by the Lebesgue differentiation theorem,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\left[(K\star\rho)(t+h,x)-(K\star\rho)(t,x)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}(K\star\partial_{t}\rho)(\tau,x)\,d\tau

(5.9)

\displaystyle\to K\star\partial_{t}\rho(t,x),

as
h\to 0
for a.e.
t
for each fixed
x
. We now compute

\frac{1}{h}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho(t+h)(K\star\rho)(t+h)-\rho(t)(K\star\rho)(t)\,dx\\
=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho(t+h)\left[\frac{(K\star\rho)(t+h)-(K\star\rho)(t)}{h}\right]\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left[\frac{\rho(t+h)-\rho(t)}{h}\right](K\star\rho)(t)\,dx\\
\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}D_{1}+D_{2}.

By (
5.8
), (
5.9
) and the dominated convergence theorem, we have for a.e.
t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle D_{1}\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho(t)(K\star\partial_{t}\rho)(t)\,dx,\quad D_{2}\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{t}\rho(t)(K\star\rho)(t)\,dx.

Here the convergence of
D_{1}
is justified by the domination
\|\rho(x)\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}}\|K\star\|g_{1}(x)\|_{L^{1}_{v}}\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\in L^{1}_{x}
, while the convergence of
D_{2}
follows from the domination
\|g(x)\|_{L^{1}_{v}}\|K\star\rho\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}\in L^{1}_{x}
for
D_{2}\to\int\partial_{t}\rho(K\star\rho)\,dx
. This proves (

).

Next, we simplify the right-hand side of (
5.7
) using the continuity equation (
5.3
). By Fubini’s theorem and the symmetry of the kernel, the two terms on the right-hand side coincide, so it suffices to consider
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{t}\rho(K\star\rho)\,dx
. Invoking (

), we obtain for a.e.

\displaystyle(\textup{RHS of }\eqref{field energy diff})

\displaystyle=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla\cdot j(t,x)(K\star\rho)(t,x)\,dx

\displaystyle=2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}j(t,x)\cdot(\nabla K\star\rho)(t,x)\,dx,

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

The third derivative
\textup{I}^{\prime\prime\prime}
of
\textup{I}(t)
exists for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
and admits the representation

\textup{I}^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}v\cdot(\nabla_{x}K\star_{x}\rho)f\,dvdx+4\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\,f\,dvdx,

for a.e.
t
.
In particular, by invoking Lemma

, we obtain for a.e.

(5.10)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho K\star_{x}\rho\,dx\right)

\displaystyle=4\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}ff\,dvdx.

Proof.

In view of Lemma
5.6
, it remains to compute the time derivative of the kinetic energy
\textup{KE}(t)
. By Lemma
5.2
and equation (

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|v|^{2}f(t,x,v)\,dxdv\right)=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|v|^{2}\partial_{t}f\,dxdv\\
=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|v|^{2}[-v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+(\nabla_{x}K\star_{x}\rho)\cdot\nabla_{v}f+(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\Delta_{v}f+f^{2}]\,dvdx\\
=-2\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}v\cdot(\nabla_{x}K\star_{x}\rho)f\,dvdx+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|v|^{2}[(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\Delta_{v}f+f^{2}]\,dvdx.

Consequently,

(5.11)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho K\star_{x}\rho\,dx\right)=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|v|^{2}[(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\Delta_{v}f+f^{2}]\,dvdx.

We now evaluate the right-hand side of (

). A direct computation yields

\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}|v|^{2}[(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\Delta_{v}f+f^{2}]\,dvdx=6\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\,f\,dvdx+4\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}v\cdot\nabla_{v}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\,f\,dvdx,

The remaining term can be further simplified as follows:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}v\cdot\nabla_{v}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\,f\,dv=\frac{1}{4\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\frac{v\cdot(u-v)}{|u-v|^{3}}f(u)f(v)\,dudv\\
=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left[-\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\frac{f(u)f(v)}{|u-v|}\,dudv+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\frac{u\cdot(u-v)}{|u-v|^{3}}f(u)f(v)\,dudv\right]\\
=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left[-\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\frac{f(u)f(v)}{|u-v|}\,dudv-\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}u\cdot\nabla_{u}\left(\frac{1}{|u-v|}\right)f(u)f(v)\,dudv\right]\\
=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\,f\,dv-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}u\cdot\nabla_{u}(-\Delta_{u})^{-1}f(u)f(u)\,du=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\,f\,dv.

Putting all the terms together yields the desired formula.
∎

We now have all the necessary identities at hand and are ready to establish a

Remark 5.9
.

By Lemma

and

, there exists a constant

,

\|f^{\textup{in}}\|_{L^{1}_{x,v}}
and

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho K\star_{x}\rho\,dx\right)

\displaystyle=4\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f\,f\,dvdx

Proof of Theorem
2.7
.

We aim to show that the spatial moment
\textup{I}(t)
becomes negative in finite time under the assumptions of Theorem
2.7
.

By Lemma
5.5
, and Remark
5.9
, together with Lemma
5.7
, we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(t)\leq C_{1}t+kC_{1}+\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(0),\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in[0,T].

Here, by arguments analogous to those in Remark
5.9
and Lemma
5.8
, and recalling the constants
C_{*},C_{**}
introduced in Remark
5.9
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\rho|K\star_{x}\rho|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C_{*}\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3})}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{6})}

\displaystyle\leq C_{*}C_{**}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{-m}\,dv\|f\|_{X_{T}^{m}}\|f^{\textup{in}}\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{*}C_{**}\left(\frac{4\pi}{3}+\frac{4\pi}{m-3}\right)\frac{\|f^{\textup{in}}\|_{L^{1}}}{\|f^{\textup{in}}\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}}}

\displaystyle=:C_{2}(\|f^{\textup{in}}\|_{L^{1}},\|f^{\textup{in}}\|_{\mathscr{C}^{2}_{m,0}})=kC_{1},

for some
k(m)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\frac{m\pi}{3(m-3)}>0
, provided

. Integrating the inequality for
\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(t)
twice in
t
yields

\displaystyle\textup{I}(t)\leq\frac{C_{1}}{6}t^{3}+\left(kC_{1}+\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(0)\right)\frac{t^{2}}{2}+\textup{I}^{\prime}(0)t+\textup{I}(0)=:g(t),\quad\text{ for all }t\in[0,T].

We now show that the cubic polynomial
g
attains a negative local minimum. Since
g^{\prime}
is a quadratic polynomial, it has two distinct real roots if and only if its
discriminant is positive. A direct computation gives

\Delta\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\bigl(kC_{1}+\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(0)\bigr)^{2}-2C_{1}\textup{I}^{\prime}(0).

Because
\textup{I}^{\prime}(0)<0
by assumption, the second term on the right-hand side is strictly
positive, and hence
\Delta>0
.
Therefore,
g^{\prime}(t)
has two distinct real roots. Moreover, since
\textup{I}^{\prime}(0)<0
again, the larger
root
t_{2}
satisfies
t_{2}>0
. As
g
is a cubic polynomial, the identity

g^{\prime}(t_{2})=\frac{C_{1}}{2}t_{2}^{2}+(kC_{1}+\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(0))t_{2}+\textup{I}^{\prime}(0)=0

allows us to
eliminate the quadratic term in
g(t_{2})
, thereby rewriting
g(t_{2})
as a linear function of
t_{2}
:

\displaystyle g(t_{2})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2}{3}\textup{I}^{\prime}(0)-\frac{(kC_{1}+\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(0))^{2}}{3C_{1}}\right)t_{2}+\left(\textup{I}(0)-\frac{(kC_{1}+\textup{I}^{\prime\prime}(0))\textup{I}^{\prime}(0)}{3C_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle=:M_{2}+M_{3}.

By the positivity of the discriminant and the assumption
\textup{I}^{\prime}(0)<0
, we have

M_{2}<0
. Thus, to ensure that
g(t_{2})<0
, it suffices to verify that

M_{3}\leq 0
. A direct computation shows that this condition is equivalent to the hypothesis (
2.10
). Consequently,

g(t_{2})<0\qquad\text{for some }t_{2}>0,

and hence
g
attains a negative local minimum in finite time.

Finally, since
\textup{I}(t_{2})<0
, the non-negative solution cannot be extended beyond
t_{2}>0
. This completes the proof of Theorem
2.7
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
2.9
.

In Corollary
2.9
, recall that we assumed the existence of an
L^{1}_{x,v}
-valued measure
g_{t}:[0,t_{\max}]\to\mathscr{M}^{1}_{2}
, which represents a weak solution
f\in X_{T}^{m}\cap A_{2}
on the time interval
[0,t_{\max})
. By mass conservation (Lemma

), we have

g_{t}(\mathbb{R}^{6})=M>0

for all
t\in[0,t_{\max}].
Also, note that for all
t<t_{\max}
,

Moreover, by the singularity mechanism established in Theorem
2.7
together with continuity of the spatial moment
\textup{I}(t)
up to

established in Lemma

, we obtain

\lim_{t\to t_{\max}^{-}}\textup{I}(t)=0.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Then for every

,

\textup{I}(t)\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\,g_{t}\big(\{|x|\geq\varepsilon\}\big),

and therefore
g_{t}\big(\{|x|\geq\varepsilon\}\big)\to 0
as

.
Consequently,

g_{t_{\max}}\big(\{|x|\geq\varepsilon\}\big)=0,\qquad\forall\,\varepsilon>0.

Since

which is a set of Lebesgue measure zero in

.

In particular, the measure

is singular with respect to Lebesgue measure and therefore cannot be represented by an

-density. This shows that, at the maximal non-negativity time

, the mass collapses onto a set of measure zero in phase space, and no continuation of the weak solution within our

-framework is possible.
∎

Appendix A
Preliminary Lemmas

In this section, we collect two lemmas that are used in the main sections.

We first establish an embedding property of the singular integrals, which will be used to estimate
(-\Delta_{v})^{-1}f
and
E_{f}
in
C^{\alpha}_{\textup{para}}
.

Lemma A.1

(Boundedness of the singular integrals)
.

Let
1<p<\infty
and let
f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Suppose
0<\beta<3
. Then, if
p<\frac{3}{3-\beta}
, there exists a constant
C>0
, depending only on
\beta,p
such that

(A.1)

\displaystyle\lVert f\star|\cdot|^{-\beta}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq C\big(\|f\|_{L^{p}}+\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\big).

Proof.

Without loss of generality, suppose
f\geq 0
. We estimate the integral
f\star|\cdot|^{-\beta}
into two regions:

Local region
|x-y|<1
: Using the assumption
f\in L^{\infty}
and applying Hölder’s inequality with exponent 1, we have

\displaystyle\int_{|x-y|<1}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|^{\beta}}\,dy\leq\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\left(\int_{|x-y|<1}\frac{dy}{|x-y|^{\beta}}\right).

We perform a change of variable
z=x-y
, yielding:

(A.2)

\displaystyle C(\beta)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\int_{|x-y|<1}\frac{dy}{|x-y|^{\beta}}=\int_{|z|<1}\frac{dz}{|z|^{\beta}}<\infty,

since
\beta<3
. Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{|x-y|<1}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|^{\beta}}\,dy\leq C(\beta)\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}.

Far region
|x-y|\geq 1
: In this region, we have

\displaystyle\int_{|x-y|\geq 1}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|^{\beta}}\,dy\leq\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\left(\int_{|x-y|\geq 1}\frac{dy}{|x-y|^{\beta p^{\prime}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}.

As before, we define

\displaystyle C(\beta,p)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}\left(\int_{|z|\geq 1}\frac{dz}{|z|^{\beta p^{\prime}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}=\left(4\pi\int_{1}^{\infty}r^{2-\beta p^{\prime}}\,dr\right)^{1-\frac{1}{p}}<\infty,

so that

\displaystyle\int_{|x-y|\geq 1}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|^{\beta}}\,dy\leq C(\beta,p)\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}.

Combining the estimates in all cases, we have the desired bound with the implied constant depending only on
\beta
and
p
and complete the proof.
∎

Next, we present a result that is used to establish the uniform boundedness of
\{C_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
appearing in (
4.17
).

Lemma A.2

(Boundedness of the compositions of exponential functions)
.

Define

\displaystyle\Phi(s)\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}ce^{A(s+1)},\quad\textup{ for }s>0,

for constants
c,A>0
, and denote the compositions of
\Phi
by
\Phi^{k}=\underbrace{\Phi\circ\Phi\circ\cdots\circ\Phi}_{k\textup{ times}}
. Then the following statements hold:

(i)

Existence of fixed points: The equation
\Phi(t)=t
has two positive roots if and only if

(A.3)

\displaystyle 0<c<\frac{\exp\{-A\}}{eA}.

(ii)

Boundedness of iterates: Under the conditions in (i), suppose that equation
\Phi(t)=t
has two positive roots, say
0<N_{1}<N_{2}
. Then, the sequence
\{\Phi^{k}(s)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly bounded if and only if
s\leq N_{2}
.

(iii)

Positivity of iterates: Again, under the conditions in (i), we have that for all
s\in(0,N_{2})
,
\textup{inf}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\Phi^{k}(s)>0
.

Proof.

(i) Notice that
\Phi(s)=s
for some positive number
s>0
if and only if
te^{-t}=cAe^{A}
for
t\overset{\mbox{\tiny{def}}}{=}As
.
But since
te^{-t}\leq\frac{1}{e}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the first statement is equivalent to (
A.3
).

(ii) and (iii) Suppose
\Phi(s)<s
for some
s>0
by letting
c
be sufficiently small. Then, the graph of
y=\Phi(s)
intersects the identity line
y=s
at exactly two points, yielding two fixed points

\displaystyle 0<N_{1}<N_{2}<\infty,\quad\textup{such that}\quad\Phi(N_{1})=N_{1},\quad\Phi(N_{2})=N_{2}.

We analyze three cases based on the initial value
s\in(0,\infty)
.

Case 1.

0<s\leq N_{1}
: Since
\Phi(s)>s
for
0\leq s<N_{1}
, and
\Phi
is strictly increasing, the sequence
\{\Phi^{k}(s)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is strictly increasing. Moreover, for all such
s
, since
\Phi(s)<N_{1}
, the sequence is bounded above by
N_{1}
. We have proved (ii) and (iii) in this case.

Case 2.

N_{1}<s<N_{2}
: In this interval,
\Phi(s)<s
, so the sequence
\{\Phi^{k}(s)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is strictly decreasing. Again, because
\Phi(s)>N_{1}
for
s\in(N_{1},N_{2})
, the sequence is bounded below by
N_{1}
. We have proved (ii) and (iii) in this interval.

Case 3.

s>N_{2}
: Here
\Phi(s)>s
, and since
\Phi
is strictly convex on
(0,\infty)
(i.e.,
\Phi^{\prime\prime}>0
), and
\Phi^{\prime}(N_{2})>1
, the sequence
\{\Phi^{k}(s)\}
grows faster with each iteration. More precisely, for each
s>N_{2}
, applying the mean value theorem yields

\displaystyle\Phi^{2}(s)-\Phi(s)=\Phi^{\prime}(s^{\prime})(\Phi(s)-s)>\Phi(s)-s,

for some
s^{\prime}\in(s,\Phi(s))
, using
\Phi^{\prime}(s^{\prime})>1
. Inductively, the sequence
\{\Phi^{k}(s)\}
satisfies

\displaystyle\Phi^{k+1}(s)-\Phi^{k}(s)>\Phi^{k}(s)-\Phi^{k-1}(s),

and diverges to
\infty
. This shows
\{\Phi^{k}(s)\}
is unbounded for
s>N_{2}
.

Consequently, for any case, (ii) and (iii) hold, completing the proof.
∎
