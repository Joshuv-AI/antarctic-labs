Implementing measurement-based care in the analgesic management of cancer pain patients receiving intrathecal drug infusion
Authors: Mingling Yi, Panchuan Huang, Fangli Cao, Wuchao Wang, Xiaoqiu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To evaluate the efficacy and safety of a structured measurement-based care (MBC) strategy with a “Monitor-Assess-Adjust” closed-loop protocol for managing moderate-to-severe cancer pain in patients receiving intrathecal morphine patient-controlled analgesia (IT-PCA). A retrospective observational study was conducted at two pain centers, enrolling 15 patients with cancer pain (Visual Analog Scale [VAS] ≥ 7). All patients initially received intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (IV-PCA) for dose titration, followed by IT-PCA implantation. A structured MBC protocol was implemented, including three core components: (1) Multidimensional assessment: regular evaluations using the VAS, Brief Pain Inventory (BPI), and leeds assessment of neuropathic symptoms and signs (LANSS) scale; (2) Dynamic individualized titration: a standardized dosing algorithm based on pain reassessment every 15 min during IT-PCA initiation, with adjustments as follows: 50–100% dose increase for unchanged/elevated VAS, 50% increase for VAS 4–6, and dose maintenance for VAS 1–3 (target: VAS < 4); (3) Systematic follow-up: assessments at baseline, post-IV-PCA, post-IT-PCA, discharge, and 1, 3, 6 months after discharge. After IT-PCA under the MBC protocol, the mean VAS score decreased significantly from 8.28 ± 0.76 at baseline to 2.14 ± 0.90, representing a 76.4% pain reduction (P < 0.05), and this effect was sustained throughout follow-up. Pain relief and quality of life (BPI scores) were significantly improved compared with baseline and post-IV-PCA stages (P < 0.01). Notably, the incidence of opioid-related adverse reactions was extremely low: only 1 patient (6.7%) experienced mild, manageable constipation, with no cases of nausea, vomiting, or respiratory depression. Implementing IT-PCA within an MBC framework enables effective, individualized analgesia, sustains long-term pain relief, and optimizes the therapeutic benefit-risk profile by minimizing adverse effects, thus improving the quality of life in cancer pain patients. This study provides evidence for a data-driven, precision medicine approach to intrathecal analgesia management.

Introduction

Cancer pain is one of the most common and distressing clinical syndromes, which significantly impairs cancer patients’ daily activities and quality of life1. More than half of all cancer patients experience pain, and the incidence is even higher in those with metastatic or terminal disease, with 30.6.0% reporting moderate to severe pain2. Despite adherence to the World Health Organization (WHO) Three-Step Analgesic Ladder and its core principles, 15% of cancer patients still suffer from refractory cancer pain. In the advanced stages of the disease, conventional analgesic treatments are often ineffective, and the associated adverse reactions are intolerable for patients3. Recent advances in cancer pain management have emerged not only in analgesic technologies but also in innovative management concepts. Thus, it is urgent to develop a comprehensive pain management strategy to achieve more effective pain control4,5.

Interventional pain procedures can provide adequate analgesia in patients for whom systemic opioids are ineffective or associated with severe adverse effects6. Intrathecal analgesia delivers analgesic agents (e.g., morphine) directly into the intrathecal space via a soft catheter, thereby blocking pain signal transmission to the central nervous system7. This approach requires a significantly lower drug dose, leading to a marked reduction in opioid-related adverse reactions, and it has now been widely popularized and recognized worldwide8,9. Intrathecal opioid administration is often necessary for cancer patients with intractable pain, and intrathecal morphine patient-controlled analgesia (IT-PCA) has been proven effective for the treatment of refractory cancer pain. The advantages of intrathecal drug delivery include rapid onset and constant-rate drug administration, with a theoretical dose as low as 1/300 of the equivalent oral dose10. Therefore, intrathecal opioid delivery can effectively alleviate cancer pain while minimizing toxic effects.

The primary goal of pain management is to reduce pain to a level that allows patients to maintain an acceptable quality of life. A key challenge in clinical practice is the rational application of cancer pain management strategies11. Several factors hinder adequate pain relief in clinical settings, including the lack of a consensus on the selection of treatment strategies and the absence of comprehensive, timely pain assessment, which often results in inadequate analgesic dosing9. Thus, developing a standardized therapeutic strategy for cancer pain is of great clinical significance. As mentioned, remarkable progress has been made in both analgesic technologies and management concepts for cancer pain. Evidence indicates that Measurement-Based Care (MBC), which quantifies symptom severity and guides clinical treatment decisions, can improve the efficacy of conventional care for chronic conditions12. The MBC strategy involves systematic assessment of symptoms, adverse effects, disease severity, patient compliance, and treatment tolerance, with subsequent adjustments of medication doses and treatment regimens based on these assessments13. Through continuous monitoring of patients’ conditions, clinicians can promptly modify treatment plans to maximize therapeutic benefits14. MBC has been successfully applied in the management of various chronic diseases, such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and depression15. In recent years, it has also been adopted to improve clinical outcomes and prognosis in patients with chronic pain16,17. However, the application of MBC in the analgesic treatment and management of cancer pain remains largely unexplored.

This study aimed to assess the efficacy and safety of a structured MBC approach incorporating a “Monitor-Assess-Adjust” closed-loop protocol in patients with moderate-to-severe cancer pain treated with intrathecal morphine patient-controlled analgesia (IT-PCA).

Methods

Patients

This study was approved by the Institutional Medical Ethics Committee of the participating centers, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to enrollment. Patients were recruited from two specialized pain management centers, with a total of 15 patients with moderate-to-severe cancer-related pain who underwent intrathecal morphine pump implantation included in the analysis. All patients were admitted for comprehensive clinical evaluation to assess analgesic efficacy and monitor potential drug-related adverse events. Pain intensity was assessed using the self-reported VAS and BPI. The inclusion criteria were: (1) pathologically confirmed malignant tumor; (2) baseline VAS score ≥ 7 for cancer pain; (3) mean pain duration ≥ 6 months; (4) estimated survival time > 3 months; (5) ability to complete self-assessments of pain and quality of life.

Intravenous PCA with morphine

Prior to IT-PCA implantation, all patients received intravenous morphine PCA (IV-PCA) in accordance with the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines for rapid opioid titration, to evaluate individual responses to morphine. The minimum effective IV-PCA dose was determined for each patient based on their clinical response and was maintained for 3 consecutive days, after which the efficacy of morphine treatment was evaluated. For opioid-naïve patients, oral immediate-release morphine (10 mg) was administered, with pain assessment performed 60 min after dosing. For patients with opioid tolerance, the total 24-hour oral morphine dose was calculated using standard opioid conversion ratios: 20 mg oral morphine = 10 mg oral oxycodone; 4.2 mg transdermal fentanyl (72-hour formulation) = 60 mg oral morphine per day. The initial IV-PCA dose was set at 1/10 of the 24-hour oral morphine equivalent dose, delivered via a PCA pump (Shanghai Graseby Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Intrathecal PCA with morphine

All patients underwent minimally invasive implantation of a semi-implantable intrathecal drug infusion system during a single hospital admission, with all surgical procedures performed under local anesthesia. Intrathecal access was achieved via spinal puncture at the L2–L4 intervertebral space in 14 patients; for 1 patient with intraspinal metastasis secondary to hepatocellular carcinoma, puncture was performed at the T7–T8 intervertebral space. After confirming intrathecal positioning via free cerebrospinal fluid flow, an intrathecal infusion catheter (Omnitest Medical, Germany) was advanced into the intrathecal space. After successful subarachnoid puncture, a permanent intrathecal catheter was inserted. The distal end of the catheter was connected and fixed to the infusion port (port) implanted under the abdominal wall skin. After the operation, the infusion port was implanted percutaneously through a sterile butterfly needle (Huber needle), and the external end was connected to the patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) pump for morphine, implementing continuous background infusion combined with patient-controlled single-dose additional analgesia. Intrathecal-PCA was achieved.

Pain assessment

Pain assessment was conducted using three well-validated tools: VAS, BPI, and Leeds Assessment of Neuropathic Symptoms and Signs (LANSS) scale. For VAS scoring, pain intensity was categorized as follows: 1–3, mild pain; 4–6, moderate pain; 7–10, severe pain. The BPI is a multidimensional pain assessment instrument administered before and after analgesic intervention, including 7 items evaluating the impact of pain on daily function, mood, mobility, work activity, interpersonal relationships, sleep, and quality of life; each item is scored from 0 to 10 (0 = no impact, 10 = severe impact). The LANSS scale was administered at baseline to screen for neuropathic pain, as it is a well-validated and reliable tool for this purpose.

The degree of pain relief was calculated using the formula: (Baseline VAS score − Post-treatment VAS score)/Baseline VAS score × 100%. Pain relief was categorized based on the percentage reduction in pain intensity: <24% = no relief, 25–49% = mild relief, 50–74% = moderate relief, 75–99% = substantial relief, 100% = complete relief. The clinical target for pain management was a 30–50% reduction in pain intensity, with a 30% reduction defined as the threshold for clinically meaningful pain improvement.

Measurement-based care strategy

A structured MBC framework, defined as a “Monitor-Assess-Adjust” closed-loop management system18, was used to guide all treatment decisions, enabling dynamic, individualized therapeutic adjustments based on each patient’s real-time clinical response.

Multi-dimensional monitoring & assessment

Quantitative and qualitative assessments were performed at predefined time points (baseline, post-IV-PCA, post-IT-PCA, discharge, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months after discharge) using the following tools:

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 13.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare outcomes across different time points, followed by the least significant difference (LSD) post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Among the patients, 9 (60%) presented with chest and back pain; over 40% had persistent pain, and 26.7% had persistent pain accompanied by paroxysmal exacerbation. After discharge, all patients continued to receive standardized medical management and completed follow-up as scheduled: 11 patients completed the 3-month follow-up, and 7 of these completed the 6-month follow-up. Pain assessment

Compared with the baseline VAS score at the initiation of IV-PCA, the maximum, minimum, and mean VAS scores were significantly lower at post-IV-PCA, post-IT-PCA, discharge, and the 1-month follow-up (P < 0.05, Fig. 1A). After IV-PCA, the mean VAS score was 3.85 ± 0.70, which was 46.5% of the baseline score (8.28 ± 0.76), demonstrating that rapid intravenous morphine titration achieved good pain relief in all patients. The mean VAS score was further reduced to 2.14 ± 0.90 after IT-PCA, representing a 76.4% pain reduction from baseline (Fig. 1B). The maximum, minimum, and mean VAS scores after IT-PCA were significantly lower than those after IV-PCA (P < 0.05, Fig. 1), and no significant differences were observed in these scores between post-IT-PCA, discharge, and the 1-month follow-up (P > 0.05, Fig. 1).

Quality of life

Both IV-PCA and IT-PCA with morphine significantly improved anxiety-related symptoms (e.g., irritability, insomnia, emotional despair) in patients. BPI scores evaluating the impact of pain on daily life, mood, sleep, and interest in life were significantly lower after IV-PCA and IT-PCA compared with baseline (P < 0.01, Fig. 2), suggesting that pain relief was associated with improved quality of life. No significant differences were observed in BPI scores for walking ability, daily work, and interpersonal relationships across different time points (P > 0.05, Fig. 2). Two patients experienced severe functional dependence in activities of daily living. One patient developed paraplegia secondary to intraspinal metastatic tumor, and another exhibited 20% muscle strength impairment resulting from combined metastatic involvement of the brain and spine.

Follow-up pain assessment

All patients achieved satisfactory analgesic effects after IT-PCA, and this pain reduction was sustained throughout the follow-up period. Specifically, VAS scores remained between 1 and 3 at post-IV-PCA, post-IT-PCA, 7-day follow-up, 1-month follow-up, and 3-month follow-up, and all were significantly lower than the baseline score (P < 0.01, Fig. 3). No significant differences were observed in VAS scores between post-IV-PCA and post-IT-PCA, or among the 7-day, 1-month, and 3-month follow-up time points (P > 0.05).

Morphine dosage

Fourteen patients received a titrated 24-hour intravenous morphine dose of 32–310 mg before intrathecal infusion system implantation. The opioid conversion ratio for morphine was 1.0 mg intrathecal = 10 mg epidural = 100 mg intravenous. The initial IT-PCA background dose was set at 1% of the effective 24-hour IV-PCA morphine dose, resulting in an initial background dose of 0.5–3 mg/d. The PCA bolus dose was set equal to the background dose, with a lockout time of 30 min to 1 h. Titration was considered satisfactory when the patient achieved > 50% pain relief with no intolerable adverse reactions. Dynamic pain assessment was performed until 7 days before discharge, with close monitoring of adverse effects during this period; the results confirmed that an effective and safe titrated dose was maintained in all patients. The mean daily intrathecal morphine dose at the 3-month follow-up was 3.25 mg/d (range: 1.0–5.5 mg/d).

A notable exception was Patient 7, who was prescribed 640 mg of oral morphine daily in the outpatient setting and required a 24-hour intravenous morphine dose of 310 mg after IV-PCA initiation. This patient had a LANSS score > 12, confirming a neuropathic pain component, and was therefore prescribed oral pregabalin (75 mg twice daily)19 and oral amitriptyline hydrochloride (12.5 mg at bedtime)20 as adjuvant analgesics. To achieve adequate pain relief, the intrathecal morphine dose was titrated to a mean of 56 mg/d (range: 48–64 mg/d) (Fig. 4).

Adverse reactions

Throughout the study period, only 1 patient experienced constipation (difficult defecation and dry stools), which was relieved with symptomatic treatment (warm water with compound polyethylene glycol)21. One patient developed nonspecific weakness on the 3rd postoperative day, which resolved spontaneously without intervention. No cases of nausea, vomiting, or respiratory depression were observed in any patient. No infection, equipment-related complications, or catheter-related complications occurred, even with minor increases in the intrathecal morphine dose during long-term follow-up.

Discussion

Cancer pain management is an essential component of comprehensive cancer treatment. Accurate and comprehensive pain assessment, combined with individualized pain management strategies, is critical to improving the quality of cancer pain management22–25. In the present study, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of IT-PCA compared with IV-PCA for moderate-to-severe cancer pain under a structured MBC framework. The results demonstrated that intrathecal morphine analgesia guided by the MBC protocol achieved satisfactory pain relief with mild, manageable adverse effects and significantly improved patients’ quality of life. Our findings suggest that MBC-based intrathecal morphine administration may serve as a valuable therapeutic strategy for refractory cancer pain.

Measurement-Based Care (MBC) is a systematic, continuous assessment approach that monitors treatment progress and guides evidence-based clinical decision-making26. The MBC strategy encourages clinicians to conduct active, quantitative pain assessment, monitor changes in pain intensity over time, and thus optimize the quality of pain management16. In this study, we conducted comprehensive evaluations at critical time points (admission, IV-PCA titration, IT-PCA titration, discharge, follow-up), recording VAS scores, systemic and intrathecal opioid doses (basal and bolus doses for breakthrough pain), and adverse events. The selection of validated assessment scales is critical for guiding treatment adjustments: the LANSS scale was used to identify the pain mechanism (nociceptive vs. neuropathic), and the BPI scale for a comprehensive evaluation of pain-related impacts on quality of life. The MBC strategy centers on the pain occurrence mechanism, combines multiple dimensions of assessment including pain intensity, social factors, psychology, and quality of life, employs combined medication, enhances synergy, and emphasizes comprehensive management.

Intrathecal opioid administration is a well-established intervention for controlling intractable cancer pain7–9. Opioids exert their analgesic effect by binding to opioid receptors in the spinal dorsal horn, thereby blocking the transmission of nociceptive signals to the central nervous system27,28. The intrathecal drug delivery system has been widely used for cancer pain management due to several advantages, including a low risk of intrathecal infection29.

In this study, we applied IT-PCA with morphine to patients with advanced cancer and a short expected survival time; compared with systemic opioid administration, intrathecal delivery achieved superior analgesic efficacy with a much lower drug dose and fewer adverse effects. The maximum 520% increase in opioid dose after hospital admission indicated that analgesic dosing was insufficient for these patients in the outpatient setting, a common clinical problem in advanced cancer pain management. One patient (patient 7) who had a LANSS score > 12 (neuropathic pain), required a high 24-hour intravenous morphine dose of 310 mg, which is consistent with previous evidence that neuropathic pain is associated with opioid resistance and requires higher opioid doses30. Notably, no serious postoperative adverse events (e.g., nausea, vomiting, respiratory depression) were observed in any patient, and only one patient had mild constipation that responded to conservative treatment. This low incidence of adverse reactions is attributable to the low intrathecal opioid dose required for analgesia, which minimizes systemic drug exposure and related toxic effects.

To balance effective pain control and minimal adverse effects, we used the minimum effective morphine dose for each patient in this study. The initial intrathecal morphine dose was set at 1% of the effective 24-hour IV-PCA dose, in accordance with previous studies31,32. Fourteen of the 15 patients had a 24-hour IV-PCA morphine dose of 80–120 mg, resulting in an initial IT-PCA dose of 0.5–1.0 mg/d; the dose was adjusted to 1.0–5.5 mg/d at the 1- and 3-month follow-ups, with concomitant improvements in quality of life. In contrast, Patient 7 (LANSS score > 12) required a much higher intrathecal morphine dose (48–64 mg/d), which further supports the notion that neuropathic pain is associated with increased opioid consumption30. Collectively, these findings confirm the feasibility of intrathecal morphine administration for the treatment of moderate-to-severe cancer pain, with individualized dosing based on pain mechanism and clinical response.

The timing of pain reassessment is critical for the effective evaluation of treatment response in MBC. Intrathecal opioids have an onset of analgesia of 15–45 min and a duration of action of up to 30 h; thus, we performed pain assessment every 15 min during IT-PCA titration, with dose adjustments until the VAS score was consistently < 4. The BPI scale, a validated tool for cancer pain assessment33, was used to evaluate patients’ quality of life. Our results showed that pain intensity was significantly lower after intrathecal morphine administration compared with baseline and post-IV-PCA, with a concomitant significant improvement in quality of life; this pain relief and quality of life improvement were sustained at the 1-, 3-, and 6-month follow-ups. These findings confirm that the MBC protocol is an effective approach for the management of opioids and pain in cancer patients.

The core value of the “Monitor-Assess-Adjust” closed-loop MBC framework is its ability to standardize the entire process of cancer pain management: it ensures that treatment adjustments are evidence-based and timely, and avoids the blindness and subjectivity of traditional empirical treatment34. This closed-loop management model enables dynamic, individualized opioid dose adjustment based on real-time pain assessment data, maximizing the balance between analgesic efficacy and drug safety—an essential guarantee for improving therapeutic outcomes and quality of life in cancer pain patients18.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, it is a retrospective observational study with a relatively small sample size (n = 15), which may reduce statistical power and increase the risk of potential selection bias, thus restricting the generalizability of the conclusions. Second, the study should be considered exploratory in nature rather than confirmatory, aiming to generate preliminary evidence and hypotheses for clinical practice instead of drawing definitive conclusions. Third, the follow-up period was relatively short, with only 7 patients completing the 6-month follow-up. Fourth, the study was conducted at two specialized pain management centers, and the results may not be fully representative of broader populations. Therefore, future research is warranted to design large-sample, multicenter, prospective cohort studies to further validate and confirm the efficacy, safety, and long-term outcomes of the mechanism-based classification (MBC) strategy in intrathecal analgesia for patients with refractory cancer pain.Despite these limitations, this study demonstrates that integrating a structured MBC strategy with IT-PCA significantly optimizes the clinical benefit-risk ratio for cancer pain management. We observed a high rate of sustained pain relief (76.4% reduction from baseline) coupled with a markedly low incidence of typical opioid-induced adverse effects (e.g., no nausea, vomiting, or respiratory depression). This favorable profile is likely attributable to the precise, data-driven dosing enabled by the MBC protocol, which minimizes systemic drug exposure compared with conventional oral or intravenous opioid administration. The closed-loop “Monitor-Assess-Adjust” model ensures that therapy is continuously aligned with individual patient needs, embodying the principles of personalized and precision pain medicine.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that intrathecal morphine PCA guided by a MBC strategy provides a safe, effective, and sustainable approach for managing refractory moderate-to-severe cancer pain. The MBC framework, with its emphasis on systematic multidimensional assessment and algorithm-driven dose titration, enables dynamic, individualized treatment that maximizes analgesic efficacy while minimizing opioid-related adverse events. This approach offers a robust, evidence-based model for enhancing the precision and clinical outcomes of advanced cancer pain management, and it provides a basis for the clinical application of data-driven, precision medicine in pain management.
