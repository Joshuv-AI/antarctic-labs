Association-gated multiphoton activation of inert haloarenes via sequential electron donor–acceptor assembly
Authors: Kakeru Matsukuma, Masanori Tayu, Kento Muto, Ryota Itai, Masahiro Noji, Satoshi Hayashi, Sayaka Ohrui, Nozomi Saito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe functionalization of recalcitrant chemical bonds that require extreme reduction potentials is often thwarted by the absence of a defined reaction field to control potent intermediates. Conventional multiphoton photocatalysis generates super-reductants, whose immense energy dissipates through chaotic, diffusion-controlled pathways. Herein, an “association-gated” strategy is introduced that constructs sequential electron donor–acceptor complexes as programmable reaction microenvironments. This system initially captures a single photon through its sulfide/naphthalene monoimide complex. The resulting naphthalene monoimide radical anion serves as a scaffold to pre-organize the haloarene substrate into a second, distinct electron donor–acceptor complex, creating a reaction pocket for the final, energy-storing excitation. Such supramolecular confinement generates an extreme reducing potential at the carbon–halogen bond, suppressing both diffusive side-reactions and parasitic hydrogen atom transfer from amine donors. This unlocks a broad scope of transformations under air-tolerant conditions, providing a versatile tool for late-stage diversification.Subject terms: Photocatalysis, Photocatalysis, Synthetic chemistry methodology, Catalytic mechanisms

Introduction

The activation of inert chemical bonds via visible-light photocatalysis is fundamentally constrained by the energy of a single photon. With visible light (400–800 nm) providing a thermodynamic ceiling of ~40–70 kcal/mol, redox processes requiring potentials more negative than −2.0 V vs the saturated calomel electrode (SCE) remain largely inaccessible to conventional single-excitation cycles1,2. To breach this barrier, multiphoton strategies have been increasingly adopted, which accumulate the energy of two or more photons to drive highly endergonic transformations (Fig. 1a). Prominent strategies such as consecutive photoinduced electron transfer (ConPET)2–8, electrophotocatalysis (EPC)1,7,9–12, and sensitization-initiated electron transfer (SenI-ET)13–15 operate on this principle, generating “electron-primed” super-reductants by coupling another energetic input—either a second photon or an electrochemical potential—to a pre-reduced intermediate. While these multiphoton regimes successfully access extreme redox potentials, they face a fundamental stability–reactivity dilemma. Ideally, the super-reductant must be sufficiently long-lived to encounter the substrate; however, such persistence enables the unconfined species to diffuse into the bulk and engage in indiscriminate redox events with solvents, additives, or non-target functional groups2–15. Consequently, the central challenge in high-energy photocatalysis is not merely how to accumulate energy, but how to confine and direct that energy to prevent chaotic background reactivity. To address this, this study proposes an “association-gated multiphoton catalysis” framework, wherein the accumulation of photonic energy is orchestrated by the sequential formation of electron donor–acceptor (EDA) complexes (Fig. 1b)16–23. In this design, the radical anion generated via the photoexcitation of an initial donor–acceptor EDA complex does not simply diffuse into the bulk; instead, it serves as a structural component to assemble a second EDA complex with the recalcitrant substrate. Upon excitation, the second EDA complex generates the substrate radical anion, which undergoes mesolytic cleavage to release a leaving group and a reactive radical that is subsequently captured by a trapping agent. By ensuring that the super-reducing intermediate immediately participates in specific EDA formation, this mechanism effectively channels redox equivalents into targeted bond activation before they can dissipate through indiscriminate diffusion-controlled pathways.

Electron-rich haloarenes represent the ideal testing ground for this association-gated concept. Although these molecules are privileged motifs in pharmaceutical and materials chemistry24–27, their reduction potentials often approach or exceed −3.0 V vs SCE, rendering them a benchmark for super-reductant systems1,28,29. Conventional multiphoton methods can technically reduce these bonds; however, the lack of spatial control often induces undesired reactions. Furthermore, the amine radical cation generated from the use of sacrificial amines to prime the catalyst promotes simple hydrodehalogenation rather than programmable functionalization. Therefore, demonstrating the programmable functionalization of these recalcitrant substrates would serve as a definitive proof-of-principle for the superior control offered by sequential EDA assembly.

Here, we show the realization of this strategy using a sulfide/naphthalene monoimide (NMI) hybrid platform (Fig. 1c). In contrast to amine-based systems, sulfides are employed as catalytic donors30 that function exclusively as redox modulators. Unlike sacrificial amines, which irreversibly fragment to supply hydrogen atoms from their oxidized radical cations, the selected sulfide mediators form reversible radical cations that stabilize the photoactive assembly without engaging in parasitic hydrodehalogenation pathways31–34. Mechanistically, the neutral sulfide forms an initial EDA complex with NMI to harvest the first photon; the resulting reduced NMI mediator then associates with the haloarene to form a second, electronically distinct EDA complex. Excitation of this transient assembly allows access to the extreme potentials required for carbon–halogen bond cleavage. By confining the entire energy-accumulation sequence within these defined assemblies, this system not only allows the precise editing of highly recalcitrant bonds, but also establishes a generic framework for decoupling thermodynamic potency from kinetic instability. By harnessing supramolecular confinement, the means by which fleeting physical transients can be transformed into disciplined synthetic reagents are determined from the viewpoint of selective molecular construction.

Results

Prior to the development of association-gated multiphoton catalysis, the interactions between Ph2S and NMI were investigated. The addition of a mixture of NMI and Ph2S (1:10) to MeCN resulted in a red-shift transition in the ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption band compared with the bands recorded for the individual components (Fig. 2a and supplementary Fig. S2a). The absorbance profile recorded for the Ph2S–NMI EDA complex indicates that the complex can be activated by photoirradiation at 390 nm (Kessil PR160L, supplementary Fig. S1). Furthermore, for a similar mixture of NMI and Ph2S (1:10) in CD3CN (green line), the proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectrum showed a higher field shift for the aromatic proton signal of NMI compared with that recorded for NMI alone (orange line) (Fig. 2b and supplementary Fig. S3a). These observations indicate that Ph2S and NMI form an EDA complex. Job’s plot analysis using 1H NMR spectroscopy revealed that NMI and Ph2S form a 1:1 EDA complex (Fig. 2c and supplementary Fig. S3b). To further quantify this interaction, UV–vis titration experiments were conducted by varying the concentration of Ph2S while maintaining the concentration of NMI at 0.01 M. The absorbance at 400 nm was analyzed, where Ph2S does not exhibit measurable absorption under the present conditions (supplementary Fig. S2b). As shown in Fig. 2d and supplementary Fig. S2c, the absorbance increased with increasing Ph2S concentration and was fitted to a 1:1 binding model using nonlinear least-squares analysis, affording an apparent association constant of K ≈ 30 M−1 (supplementary Fig. S2d). This result is consistent with weak ground-state association between NMI and Ph2S35. Subsequently, the fluorescence intensity of NMI (0.01 M in MeCN) at 441 nm was measured using 365 nm light excitation. As shown in Fig. 2e and supplementary Fig. S4a, upon gradually increasing the concentration of Ph2S, the fluorescence intensity of NMI decreased in proportion to the amount of Ph2S added. Additionally, the fluorescence lifetime measurement revealed that the lifetime of NMI decreased progressively with increasing concentrations of Ph₂S (Fig. 2f and supplementary Fig. S4b). Based on these experiments, NMI and Ph2S interactions were investigated. As shown in Fig. 2g and supplementary Fig. S4c, the Stern–Volmer plots obtained from emission intensity (I0/I) and fluorescence lifetime (τ0/τ) showed good linearity over the examined concentration range, with closely matching slopes (KSV ≈ 14–15 M−1). Because purely dynamic quenching predicts identical Stern–Volmer behavior for intensity and lifetime, these results indicate that quenching of the excited state of NMI by Ph2S is predominantly governed by a dynamic process under the present conditions36,37. This behavior does not exclude the presence of ground-state association between NMI and Ph2S. In fluorescence quenching systems, weak pre-association can coexist with dynamically dominated excited-state quenching when the equilibrium constant for complex formation is modest and collisional pathways remain competitive (supplementary Fig. S4d)38,39. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were subsequently performed for the molecular orbitals to reveal the structural details of the EDA complex (see Source Data). The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) was mainly distributed over the Ph2S moiety, whereas the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) was localized on the naphthalene core of NMI. Notably, the HOMO of Ph2S and the LUMO of the naphthalene core exhibited significant orbital overlap. The distance between the closest carbon atom of Ph2S and the naphthalene ring was determined to be 3.55 Å, which is within the typical range for EDA complexes (Fig. 2h and supplementary Fig. S18)16,40. Furthermore, non-covalent interaction (NCI) analysis revealed van der Waals interactions between the Ph2S moiety and the naphthalene core of NMI (Fig. 2i and supplementary Fig. S19). Based on this strong evidence for the formation of an EDA complex between NMI and Ph2S, the possibility of photoinduced charge transfer was investigated from a chemical perspective (Fig. 2j and supplementary Fig. S5). Specifically, brominated NMI was irradiated using a 390 nm light-emitting diode (LED) for 24 h in the presence of both Ph2S (100 mol%) and 1,4-cyclohexadiene (1,4-CHD, 3 equiv). The results showed complete debromination to the corresponding NMI. Since the radical anions of haloarenes are known to readily undergo fragmentation to generate the corresponding aryl radicals, the obtained results suggest that the Ph2S–NMI EDA complex undergoes photoinduced charge transfer, leading to the formation of NMI radical anion species.

This series of mechanistic investigations provided strong evidence for the formation and charge-transfer activity of an EDA complex between NMI and Ph2S. Subsequent efforts focused on optimizing the association-gated multiphoton catalysis using the intramolecular hydrocyclization of chloroarene 1a, which corresponds to product 2a and exhibits a reduction wave outside the solvent window (Ered < −3.5 V vs Fc/Fc+ in MeCN), as a benchmark reaction (Table 1 and supplementary Tables S1–S3)41. Under LED irradiation (390 nm) at room temperature, 1a (0.05 M in MeCN) reacted with 1,4-CHD (3 equiv), NMI (10 mol%), Ph2S (10 mol%), and Cs2CO3 (1.5 equiv) to afford the desired benzodihydrofuran 2a in a quantitative yield (entry 1). Substituting NMI with other imide-based acceptors, such as N-phenylphthalimide (PhthNPh) and perylene diimide (PDI), afforded 2a in yields of 75% and 72%, respectively (entries 2 and 3). In contrast, the use of typical ConPET catalysts, including 1,2,3,5-tetrakis(carbazole-9-yl)-4,6-dicyanobenzene (4CzIPN), 2,4,5,6-tetrakis(diphenylamino)isophthalonitrile (4DPAIPN), and 9,10-dicyanoanthracene (DCA) resulted in lower yields (entries 4–6). The evaluation of alternative catalytic donors revealed that diisopropyl sulfide (iPr2S) and dibenzothiophene (DBT) afforded 2a in yields of 94% and 74%, respectively (entries 7 and 8), while triphenylamine (Ph3N) led to a decreased yield of 59% (entry 9). Crucially, in the absence of NMI, Ph2S, Cs2CO3, or light irradiation, a dramatic drop in yield was observed (entries 10–13), thereby highlighting the essential roles of these individual components. Notably, the reaction proceeded in the presence of air and water, highlighting the robustness and operational simplicity of association-gated multiphoton catalysis (entries 14 and 15).

Subsequently, the substrate scope for the intramolecular hydrocyclization was explored under the optimized reaction conditions (Fig. 3). Bromoarene bearing both cyano and methoxy substituents on the aromatic ring afforded benzodihydrofuran 2b in quantitative yield. When 1-(but-3-en-1-yloxy)-2-chlorobenzene was used as the substrate, chromane 2c was obtained in a quantitative yield. Furthermore, haloarenes containing nitrogen linkers furnished valuable nitrogen-containing heterocycles; dihydroindole 2d and benzimidazole 2e were obtained in 49% and 78% yields, respectively. The lower yield of 2d may be attributed to its behavior as an electrophilic arene under the reaction conditions, which may have led to competing side reactions.

To elucidate the reaction mechanism, a series of mechanistic experiments were conducted. As shown in Fig. 4a and supplementary Fig. S7, hydrocyclization proceeds only under continuous LED light irradiation, implying that a long-term radical chain mechanism is unlikely to be operative, although the possibility of a short-term radical chain process cannot be entirely excluded. To evaluate the involvement of the two-photon process, the dependence of the reaction rate on the light intensity was examined. The conversion of 1a exhibited an approximately quadratic dependence on the irradiation intensity, consistent with a two-photon process (Fig. 4b and supplementary Fig. S8)42. Collectively, these findings and the low quantum yield (supplementary Fig. S6) strongly suggest the presence of a two-photon mechanism based on sustained photoirradiation.

The interaction between 1a and the NMI radical anion was subsequently investigated. The addition of a mixture of the electrochemically generated NMI radical anion and 1a (1:1) to MeCN resulted in a red-shift transition in the UV–vis absorption band compared with the bands recorded for the individual components (Fig. 4c and supplementary Fig. S12). By contrast, when chlorocyclohexane was mixed with the electrochemically generated NMI radical anion under otherwise identical conditions, the resulting UV–vis spectrum was essentially identical to that of the NMI radical anion alone, and no appreciable spectral deviation was observed. These results suggest that NMI radical anions and haloarenes form an EDA complex1,12.

The intramolecular cyclization of 1a was then examined using an electrochemically prepared NMI radical anion to verify the involvement of a second photon. The cyclization proceeded only under photoirradiation, thereby supporting the necessity of a second-photon process (Fig. 4d and supplementary Fig. S9).

As shown in Fig. 4e, supplementary Fig. S13 and S14, the absorbance profile of the NMI˙−–1a EDA complex indicates that the complex can be activated by photoirradiation at 390 nm, consistent with the Ph2S–NMI EDA complex; accordingly, a single 390 nm light source is sufficient to activate both EDA complexes.

It has been suggested that hydrogenated Meisenheimer-type NMI species can function as hyper-reductants under certain conditions43. In the present system, however, such Meisenheimer complexes are not sufficiently reducing to account for the dechlorination of 1a as freely diffusing “super-reductants,” and therefore cannot constitute the primary operative reductant in solution. Nevertheless, a mechanistic scenario in which a Meisenheimer-type reduced NMI species forms a donor–acceptor assembly with 1a and undergoes association-gated photoexcitation to deliver reduction cannot be categorically excluded. Indeed, control experiments show that independently prepared NMIH2 can, in principle, promote the dechlorination of 1a (supplementary Fig. S10), indicating that Meisenheimer-type species could, under certain circumstances, participate in the overall redox manifold.

Importantly, however, the UV–vis spectrum recorded under the current electrochemical priming conditions differs markedly from that of isolated NMIH243, indicating that the dominant reduced NMI species present under the reaction conditions is distinct from the hydrogenated Meisenheimer complex. Taken together, the above observations support a mechanistic picture in which excitation of the NMI˙−-haloarene EDA complex constitutes the major productive pathway, while the Meisenheimer-type pathway, if it operates at all, only imparts a minor, secondary contribution (supplementary Fig. S11).

Subsequently, quantum chemical calculations were used to analyze the details of charge transfer within the EDA complex formed between NMI˙− and chloroarene (see Source Data). DFT calculations performed for the NMI˙−–o-chloroanisole EDA complex 3b′ show substantial overlap between the singly occupied molecular orbital localized on the NMI fragment and the LUMO centered on the chloroarene, indicating a compact donor–acceptor assembly suited for charge transfer (Fig. 4f and supplementary Fig. S20). Time-dependent (TD)-DFT analysis reveals low-lying charge-transfer excited states in the 390–410 nm region with appreciable oscillator strengths, consistent with the experimentally observed charge-transfer band at the irradiation wavelength. Natural transition orbital (NTO) analysis further shows that these states are dominated by electron transfer from NMI-centered occupied orbitals into virtual orbitals delocalized over the chloroarene framework, supporting assignment of the long-wavelength band as an intracomplex NMI˙− to haloarene charge-transfer excitation (Fig. 4g and supplementary Figs. S21, 22). To prove the role of association in governing reactivity, a competitive experiment was conducted using chlorobenzene (Ered ≈ −2.6 V)44 and chlorocyclohexane (Ered ≈ −2.7 V)44 under phosphonylation conditions. Notably, only chlorobenzene furnished the corresponding phosphonylated product 11e, whereas the product 11e′ derived from chlorocyclohexane was not detected. This outcome indicates that substrate activation is not dictated solely by reduction potential, but is strongly influenced by the substrate’s ability to engage in donor–acceptor interactions, consistent with an association-gated mechanism (Fig. 4h and supplementary Fig. S15).

Based on the above mechanistic studies, a plausible reaction mechanism was proposed, as shown in Fig. 5. Initially, EDA complex 3a is formed between Ph2S (4) and NMI. Upon subsequent LED light irradiation, complex 3a undergoes photoinduced SET, generating the NMI radical anion and Ph2S radical cation 5. Subsequently, the NMI radical anion and 1a form EDA complex 3b, which promotes the single-electron reduction of chloroarene 1a by a second photoinduced SET, resulting in the formation of aryl radical 6. This radical undergoes intramolecular cyclization to form alkyl radical intermediate 7, which then furnishes benzodihydrofuran 2a via hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) from 1,4-CHD (8), generating radical 9. Finally, an SET event between 9 and radical cation 5 regenerates 4, thereby closing the catalytic cycle. While direct spectroscopic observation of the second donor–acceptor assembly remains challenging, the mechanistic interpretation is based on the overall consistency of spectroscopic, kinetic, and reactivity data.

After the successful establishment of association-gated multiphoton catalysis for the activation of haloarenes, a general dehalogenative functionalization platform was developed by coupling haloarenes with a diverse set of radical trapping agents (Fig. 6). Notably, it was found that in the reductive hydrogenation process45 performed using 1,4-CHD as the hydrogen donor, various haloarenes bearing ester, biphenyl, and alcohol moieties were efficiently converted to the corresponding arenes (10a–10c) in good-to-excellent yields (Fig. 6a and supplementary Table S4). Moreover, dimethyl tetrachloroterephthalate, a harmful herbicidal compound of environmental concern46, was completely dechlorinated to afford 10d in an 82% yield, underscoring the potential of this strategy to contribute to sustainable chemical practices through the catalytic detoxification of persistent polyhalogenated pollutants47. Subsequently, an Arbuzov-type phosphonylation protocol48 was investigated (Fig. 6b and supplementary Table S5). It was found that under association-gated multiphoton catalysis conditions, triethyl phosphite served as an effective aryl radical trap, enabling the formation of aryl phosphonates 11a–11f from a range of haloarenes. This reaction exhibited a high functional group tolerance, accommodating electron-deficient substrates bearing cyano (11b) and ester (11c) groups, as well as electron-rich haloarenes (11d, 11e) and heteroaromatic compounds (11f), which are generally challenging in the context of reductive processes49. Encouraged by these successful hydrogenation and phosphonylation reactions, the versatility of this association-gated multiphoton catalysis was further explored. Specifically, the use of tert-butyl isocyanide enabled the direct transformation of bromoarenes50 into their corresponding aryl nitriles 12a and 12b (Fig. 6c and supplementary Table S6). Moreover, the use of N-methylpyrrole as a trapping agent4 led to the formation of biaryl products 13a–13d via radical–aryl coupling (Fig. 6d and supplementary Table S7). The coupling to generate 13d proceeded in moderate yield, and 4-bromoanisole was recovered in 69%; given the excellent mass balance, extending the reaction time is expected to improve the yield. Additionally, when bis(neopentylglycolato)diboron (B2nep2) or bis(pinacolato)diboron (B2pin2) were used as trapping agents9,51, synthetically useful arylboronic acid ester derivatives 14a–14g were obtained in excellent yields (Fig. 6e and supplementary Table S8). Furthermore, the combination of alkenes with 1,4-CHD52 afforded alkylated products 15a and 15b in yields of 69% and 37%, respectively (Fig. 6f and supplementary Table S9). Notably, in the trapping of 4-cyanobromobenzene with N-methylpyrrole (Fig. 6d) and of methyl 4-chlorobenzoate with B2pin2 (Fig. 6e), our association-gated conditions furnished exclusively the trapped products 13b and 14g, respectively. By contrast, conventional conPET conditions produced mixtures of the desired products 13b and 14g and the corresponding dehalogenated products, benzonitrile and methyl benzoate, respectively (supplementary Figs. S16, 17)4,53. These comparisons highlight the superior chemoselectivity of the association-gated system, which favors productive radical coupling over competing hydrogen atom incorporation. In the reaction leading to 15b, the hydrodehalogenated product, methyl benzoate, was observed as a byproduct, whereas no hydrodehalogenated product was detected in the reaction affording 13c, indicating that the reactivity of the coupling partner also influences the extent of competing hydrogenation.

A gram-scale borylation protocol was also performed to demonstrate the practicality of this association-gated multiphoton catalysis strategy. As shown in Fig. 6g, the borylation of methyl 4-bromobenzoate (7.0 mmol, 1.5 g) with B2pin2 proceeded smoothly to afford the borylated product 14g in a 73% yield. Notably, 14g serves as a versatile intermediate for the synthesis of various high-value pharmaceuticals and bioactive compounds54. For instance, a Suzuki–Miyaura coupling of 14g, followed by hydrolysis, delivered the highly selective human RARβ2 receptor agonist AC-55649 (16)55,56. Furthermore, after obtaining 17 via the amidation of 14g, a second Suzuki–Miyaura coupling reaction was performed to obtain the hH-PGDS inhibitor 1857. These transformations highlight the synthetic utility of association-gated multiphoton catalysis for accessing complex and biologically relevant molecules from readily available starting materials. Notably, the association-gated multiphoton catalysis strategy developed herein enabled a series of late-stage functionalization reactions of loratadine, a widely used antihistamine, thereby demonstrating its utility in medicinal chemistry (Fig. 6h). Specifically, in the presence of appropriate radical-trapping reagents, phosphonylation (11g), cyanation (12c), arylation (13e), and borylation (14h) reactions proceeded efficiently to furnish structurally diversified loratadine analogs. These modifications not only showcase the functional group tolerance and versatility of the developed method but also provide rapid access to analogs that may exhibit altered pharmacological properties, metabolic stabilities, or target selectivities. Collectively, these results underscore the value of association-gated multiphoton catalysis as a powerful platform for the late-stage diversification of complex bioactive molecules, offering new avenues for lead optimization and structure–activity relationship studies in drug discovery58,59.

Discussion

The study establishes an association-gated multiphoton strategy that addresses a central limitation of electron-primed photoredox catalysis, namely the difficulty of combining extremely negative effective redox potentials with high chemoselectivity under operationally simple conditions. A sequential EDA network was designed in which a sulfide–NMI complex and an NMI radical anion–haloarene complex harvest two photons in a defined sequence, such that the product of the first charge-transfer event becomes a structural component of the second EDA complex. Mechanistic experiments and computations collectively support this architecture. Specifically, UV–vis, fluorescence, NMR, and NCI analyses confirm EDA formation, while TD-DFT and NTO analyses reveal excited states with strong charge-transfer character within the irradiation window. Furthermore, intensity-dependence and ON/OFF experiments indicate a two-photon process, and the electrochemical generation of an NMI radical anion followed by photochemical cyclization demonstrates the requirement for a second photon. The short-lived NMI radical anion is thereby rendered catalytically competent through association-gated excitation of the NMI˙−–haloarene EDA complex. While this activation mode may conceptually intersect with Meisenheimer manifolds, a decisive feature of the current design is the replacement of sacrificial amines with a sulfide donor. By circumventing the facile hydrogen-atom transfer associated with amine radical cations, this platform suppresses trivial hydrodehalogenation, thereby enabling the diverse array of programmable functionalization reactions demonstrated herein.

The resulting platform converts a wide range of haloarenes into a common aryl-radical reservoir under UV irradiation in the presence of a catalytic amount of sulfide. Intramolecular reductive hydrocyclization furnishes oxygen- and nitrogen-containing heterocycles, while coupling with diverse radical trapping agents enables hydrogenation, phosphonylation, cyanation, arylation, borylation, and alkylation with broad functional-group tolerance. The operational robustness of the system is demonstrated by air- and water-tolerant conditions, a gram-scale borylation that feeds directly into the synthesis of bioactive targets, the catalytic detoxification of a polyhalogenated herbicide, and the late-stage diversification of loratadine.

Association-gated multiphoton catalysis mediated by sequential EDA complexes therefore represents a general design principle for channeling multiple photon excitations through controlled molecular association. The extension of this framework to other donor–mediator pairs and bond activations that demand extreme yet chemoselective reducing power should provide a foundation for next-generation electron-primed catalytic systems.

Methods

General procedure for the hydrocyclization of alkenes via association-gated multiphoton catalysis

To a flame-dried glass vial equipped with a stir bar, haloarene 1 (0.2 mmol, 1.0 equiv), NMI (0.02 mmol, 10 mol%), 1,4-cyclohexadiene (0.6 mmol, 3.0 equiv), and Cs2CO3 (0.3 mmol, 1.5 equiv) were added with anhydrous MeCN ([1] = 0.05 M). Subsequently, Ph2S (0.02 mmol, 10 mol%) was added and the mixture was reacted under 390 nm LED illumination for 60 h while maintaining ambient temperature using a cooling fan. After this time, the solvent was removed in vacuo and directly purified by preparative thin-layer chromatography (TLC) or flash column chromatography on silica gel to yield the corresponding hydrocyclization product 2.

To a flame-dried glass vial equipped with a stir bar, haloarene (0.2 mmol, 1.0 equiv), 1,4-cyclohexadiene (0.6 mmol, 3.0 equiv), CsOAc (0.3 mmol, 1.5 equiv), and NMI (0.02 mmol, 10 mol%) were added with anhydrous MeCN ([haloarene] = 0.05 M). Subsequently, Ph2S (0.02 mmol, 10 mol%) was added and the mixture was reacted under 390 nm LED illumination for 60 h while maintaining ambient temperature using a cooling fan. After this time, the solvent was removed in vacuo and directly purified by preparative TLC to yield arene 10.
