Research Priority Setting Is Not Research: A Call for Ethical Clarity in Australia
Authors: Bec Jenkinson, Gordon McGurk, Jonathan Quicke, Janelle Bowden, Nadine E Foster
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn Australia, research priority setting (RPS) may be misclassified as research requiring ethics approval, despite its purpose as a collaborative, pre‐research involvement activity. Treating RPS as research creates delays, diverts resources and undermines partnership principles central to inclusive, community‐centred priority setting. Recent revisions to the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research clarify that pre‐research consultation does not require ethics review, creating an opportunity for consistent national practice. We call for institutions to recognise RPS as involvement and provide proportionate, non‐ethics oversight to accelerate RPS and deliver more responsive research agendas that reduce waste.Keywords: ethics, research, research priority setting

Introduction

Australia's National Health and Medical Research Strategy 2026–2036 [1] (‘the Strategy’) foregrounds systematic, transparent and coordinated research priority setting (RPS) as integral to efficient, impactful and equitable research. We define RPS as a process for deciding which areas of research effort offer wide benefits to society and should be pursued, given the context of finite resources [2]. There are several established approaches to RPS used globally (see Table 1) [3].

In Australia, RPS is often treated as research, requiring ethical review and approval. This article argues that this is a misclassification, and that RPS is more appropriately considered involvement, a pre‐research activity. The consequences of this misclassification include project delays, resource diversion and erosion of key RPS principles. Instead, institutions should clarify and consistently apply the boundary between research and involvement, enabling the Strategy's aspiration for coherent, community‐centred, inclusive national RPS.

Involvement includes a range of activities that differ from research in purpose, but not always in methods [4, 5, 6, 7]. In Australia, Consumer and Community Involvement refers to collaborative partnerships between health consumers, community members and researchers across the research process [8]. Clinicians, as key end‐users of health and medical research, also frequently contribute to involvement activities. Conversely, Australia's National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research (‘the National Statement’) defines research as activity intended to generate new knowledge through systematic investigation [9].

Internationally there is clear guidance that RPS is not research [3, 10]. In Australia, the Strategy calls for RPS that is inclusive, equitable and aligned to community needs [1]. This positions RPS as a participatory decision‐making process that supports transparent and inclusive decisions about what research should be done. This ensures that future research, funding decisions and study designs reflect priority needs. Although RPS may draw on methods such as surveys and workshops (see Table 1), these are used to elicit perspectives and support deliberation, rather than to test hypotheses, evaluate interventions or generate analytical datasets. Accordingly, RPS yields priorities and guidance, not evidence claims or findings about participants.

The Consequences of Misclassifying RPS Activities

A key consequence of misclassifying RPS as research is the perceived need for ethical approval. RPS teams in Australia anecdotally report various approaches to, and divergent advice from, ethics review. Exemptions from ethics review appear to be rarely sought. Most commonly RPS teams are advised to use lower risk pathways [9] to secure ethics approval for the collection of public input. Some RPS teams seek ethics approval for all aspects of RPS activities, including consumer and clinician steering group membership. Seeking ethics approval for activities that are not research has several important consequences.

Firstly, it overburdens Human Research Ethics Committees (HRECs) and lower risk review entities, creating bottlenecks that delay both RPS activities (which should not need approval) and research projects (that genuinely depend on it). Activities that could begin much sooner, under light‐touch institutional authorisation processes, may instead be delayed while applications are reviewed, queried, refined and finally approved.

Secondly, because RPS processes evolve, protocols approved following ethics review usually require multiple amendments. For example, the James Lind Alliance (JLA) method [10], which is profiled in the Strategy [1] and recognised by the Australian Clinical Trials Alliance [11] and World Health Organization [3] as a comprehensive approach [1], uses sequential surveys to elicit and shortlist community priorities. The content of subsequent surveys cannot be known at the outset, so instruments cannot be submitted in advance, triggering repeated ethics amendments. In fast‐moving clinical or policy contexts, such delays can mean that priorities are identified only after funding windows have closed or clinical need has shifted, reducing the relevance and impact of subsequent research.

Thirdly, securing ethics approval for RPS activities diverts scarce resources from more impactful work. Both ethics review bodies and RPS teams, including administrative officers, expend resources preparing and reviewing documents, processing amendments and maintaining systems intended for research oversight. Efforts that could support inclusive engagement or outreach to under‐represented communities are diverted to administrative or compliance tasks, without enhancing the safety or integrity of RPS.

Fourthly, complying with ethics review requirements may unintentionally undermine the partnership principles integral in many RPS approaches. For example, in the JLA method, decisions are made deliberatively by a steering group of consumers, clinicians and researchers. This includes co‐writing summary questions that reflect public contributions to the initial survey, for use in a shortlisting survey. However, an ethics review entity may request changes to summary questions without recognising the deliberative work underpinning them, potentially conflicting with National Statement requirements [9].

Finally, misclassifying RPS activities as research reframes consumer and clinician partners as research participants, creating and sustaining power imbalances that undermine collaboration. Where research participants read information sheets and sign consent forms, partners agree on project scope and make mutual commitments to collaborate. Researcher–participant interactions are predetermined by an approved study protocol, while collaborative partnerships are dynamic, interest‐driven and ongoing. Misclassifying RPS as research can hinder these developing relationships, and individuals may opt out if they feel they are being studied, rather than entering an authentic partnership.

Reasons for Misclassification of RPS Activities

The current practice of RPS teams seeking ethics review may reflect a culture of caution among institutions and ethics review bodies. Ethics coordinators or committees may advise seeking approval as a precautionary measure and RPS teams may value the reassurance of ethics review, especially if they perceive it to be a requirement for publication in academic journals or their planned contributors are at increased risk of harm under the National Statement [9]. In practice, journals typically accept a governance statement explaining that an ethics review was unnecessary because the activity was not research, or outlining the nature of the activity, the safeguards in place and the absence of human research data collection [4, 5]. This culture of caution perpetuates a cycle of misclassification, as RPS teams and ethical review entities follow earlier examples.

An Opportunity for Clarity

Recent revisions to the National Statement clarify the boundary between research and involvement: ‘Consultation and engagement with potential participants, co‐researchers and community representatives that precedes the development, design or conduct of the research is not itself human research and typically does not require ethics review’ [9]. Recognising and implementing this clarification would align Australia with jurisdictions where RPS is understood to be pre‐research engagement [3, 10].

RPS projects undeniably have ethical dimensions and warrant proportionate oversight to ensure safety, fairness and transparency. However, that oversight lies outside the jurisdiction of ethics review bodies as human research ethics principles, by definition, apply to research [4, 12]. Other safeguards may be more useful, appropriate and specific to RPS [5, 12, 13]. Organisations such as the JLA [10] and WHO [3] provide detailed guidance outlining ethical foundations, such as partnership, transparency, accountability and inclusivity. These principles are enacted before, during and after RPS, through risk assessments and safeguards such as clear role descriptions, transparent and safe facilitation and processes, inclusive recruitment, attention to privacy, management of conflicts of interest and respectful communication [5, 12, 13]. Such safeguards also take account of the needs of contributors who may be at increased risk of harm [9], such as children and young people, people living with illness or disability, or Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples and communities.

As with other non‐research activities, such as quality assurance, quality improvement or service evaluation, institutions need a coherent approach to the oversight of RPS. At a minimum, such oversight must recognise RPS as involvement, not research. Within this framing, institutional representatives with expertise in ethical decision‐making [9] can still support ethical practice, uphold the boundary between research and involvement, signpost practical involvement resources and champion inclusion [14]. Researchers planning RPS activities can avoid misclassification through careful justification and framing of their project. Nomenclature such as ‘contributor’, ‘steering group member’ and ‘workshop attendee’ distinguishes partners from ‘research participants’. Better use of institutional resources would streamline RPS activities, prevent unnecessary ethics reviews and conserve ethical review capacity for research projects.

RPS has significant potential to improve the relevance, efficiency, timeliness and impact of health and medical research in Australia, reducing research waste. Achieving these benefits depends on applying appropriate levels of oversight to support efficiency, accessibility and partnership principles. Research institutions need to act decisively: establish simple acknowledgement pathways for involvement, provide practical guidance and tools for safe and inclusive facilitation, and reserve ethics review for research. Doing so will strengthen partnerships with consumers and clinicians, allow priority setting to occur while clinical, policy or funding opportunities are live, and deliver more responsive research agendas that reduce waste.
