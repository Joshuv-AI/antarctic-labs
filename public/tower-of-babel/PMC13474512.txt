Geospatial genetic evolution and phenotypic plasticity in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Ruoxi Hong, Pin Wu, Yuyue Qiu, Shichen Dong, Yuhan Huang, Kaping Lee, Huifang Wang, Mei Li, Cuijuan Zhang, Feng Ye, Shanlan Mo, Siming Xue, Wei Wu, Zheng Hu, Weimin Zhang, Qimin Zhan, Guibo Li, Shusen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe interplay between genotype and phenotype in shaping spatial heterogeneity and metastatic evolution in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) remains poorly understood.MethodsHere, we developed a pathology-guided geospatial genomic and transcriptomic sequencing workflow (pGeo-G&T-seq) and applied it to TNBC specimens from primary tumors and matched metastatic sites. Using laser capture microdissection (LCM) we isolated mRNA and gDNA for whole-exome sequencing, whole-genome sequencing and whole-transcriptome sequencing to analyze spatial genomic heterogeneity and transcriptomic plasticity. Findings were further evaluated using independent bulk transcriptomic, single-cell immunotherapy, and spatial transcriptomic TNBC datasets.ResultsThe analysis unveiled spatial intratumoral heterogeneity at genomic and transcriptomic levels characterized by distinct spatial patterns of “decay” or “uniformity”, demonstrating regional clonal expansion and genetic mosaicism, including heterogeneity in driver alterations and molecular subtypes. Notably, we identified a previously uncharacterized oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)-associated transcriptional state associated with poor prognosis and features of limited immunotherapy responsiveness in single-cell cohorts. Intriguingly, genetic ancestry was associated with relatively stable transcriptional programs in a subset of cases. Furthermore, phylogenetically closely related clones from primary tumors and lymph node metastases shared convergent biological processes, suggesting that phenotypic traits may be partially retained during metastatic colonization.ConclusionsThese findings support a model in which genetic evolution and phenotypic retention contribute to the spatial heterogeneity and metastatic progression of TNBC. More broadly, our pathology-guided spatial multi-omics framework enables the dissection of genotype–phenotype coupling in intact tissue, while the identification of an OXPHOS-associated transcriptional program may inform biologically guided patient stratification and generate hypotheses for future therapeutic studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13073-026-01684-0.

Background

Breast cancer is the second most prevalent malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality in women [1]. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), accounting for 15–20% of cases [2], lacks expression of estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and human epidermal growth factor receptor-2 (HER2) [3], and is associated with aggressive clinical behavior, early recurrence, and limited therapeutic options [4]. Gene expression profiling has stratified TNBC into multiple molecular subtypes [5–14], some of which show differential responses to targeted therapies in clinical trials [12]. However, these classifications are largely derived from bulk multi-omics data and therefore fail to capture the extensive intratumoral heterogeneity and phenotypic plasticity that characterize TNBC tumors and influence therapeutic outcomes.

Tumor heterogeneity is driven by clonal evolution, yet current approaches often rely on spatially discrete sampling or single-modality profiling, limiting insight into how genetic and transcriptional programs evolve within continuous tissue space. Although recent methods such as base-specific in situ sequencing (BaSISS) have begun to map spatial clonal evolution by integrating pathology, laser capture microdissection (LCM), and sequencing [15], their dependence on separate tissue sections and targeted mutation panels constrains comprehensive, genome-wide and transcriptome-wide characterization of spatial evolution.

A further unresolved question is the extent to which genotype constrains or permits phenotypic plasticity during tumor progression and metastasis. Studies in colorectal cancer suggest that genotype may exert limited control over transcriptomic phenotypes [16], while work in TNBC has shown that resistant genotypes can pre-exist treatment and be selectively enriched, with transcriptional changes emerging later [17]. However, the lack of parallel genomic and transcriptomic profiling from the same spatially defined tumor regions has hindered systematic dissection of genotype–phenotype relationships within intact tissue architecture. Moreover, whether tumor phenotypes are retained or reshaped during metastatic dissemination remains incompletely understood.

To address these challenges, we developed a pathology-guided geospatial genomic and transcriptomic parallel sequencing workflow (pGeo-G&T-seq) and applied it to paired primary tumors, axillary lymph node metastases, and distant lymph node metastases from patients with TNBC. By integrating spatially resolved genomic and transcriptomic data and further evaluating our findings in independent TNBC bulk, single-cell, and spatial transcriptomic sequencing cohorts, we delineate coordinated patterns of genomic and transcriptomic intratumoral heterogeneity, reconstruct spatial clonal evolution during metastasis, and identify an OXPHOS-enriched tumor cell program associated with poor prognosis and reduced treatment-associated immune expansion in single-cell immunotherapy cohorts. Together, our study provides a spatial multi-omics framework for understanding genetic control, phenotypic plasticity, and clonal continuity in TNBC progression.

Methods

Patient cohorts, sample collection, and analytic overview

This study comprised a discovery cohort and multiple external validation cohorts.

The discovery cohort included fresh tumor biopsy specimens and matched peripheral blood samples collected from ten treatment-naïve patients treated at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC), including eight patients with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and two patients with luminal breast cancer. Tumor biopsies were obtained from primary breast tumors, paired axillary lymph node metastases, and distant lymph node metastases (Additional file 1: Table S1). Fresh tumor biopsies were immediately placed on ice in a 10 cm dish and embedded in OCT within 30 min to preserve tissue integrity. Using the pGeo-G&T-seq workflow in combination with laser capture microdissection (LCM), we profiled 375 cell clusters with estimated tumor purity > 40%, derived from ten primary tumors, nine axillary lymph node metastases, and three distant lymph node metastases. After quality control, 236 cell clusters were retained for whole-transcriptome sequencing (WTS) analyses, 230 for whole-exome sequencing (WES) analyses, and 293 for whole-genome sequencing (WGS) analyses. The discovery cohort was used for spatial genomic and transcriptomic profiling, intratumoral heterogeneity analyses, phylogenetic reconstruction, metastatic evolution analyses, and genotype–phenotype coupling analyses.

For external evaluation of TNBC subtypes and their clinical relevance, we analyzed several public bulk transcriptomic cohorts. These included 277 TNBC samples (Breast Invasive Ductal Carcinoma) from the METABRIC cohort [18], 346 TNBC samples from the iSPY-2 cohort [19], 482 TNBC samples from the BrighTNess cohort [20], and 874 TNBC samples from the SCAN-B cohort [21]. These datasets were used for subtype evaluation, prognostic analyses, and treatment-response analyses, as appropriate.

To assess tumor cell state heterogeneity and therapeutic relevance at single-cell resolution, we analyzed two published TNBC single-cell cohorts. The scTNBC cohort included 12 patients with paired pre-treatment and on-treatment biopsies obtained during anti-PD-1 therapy and was used to evaluate tumor cell state composition and its association with treatment-related T-cell clonotype expansion [22]. The scPCPRT cohort consists of 34 treatment-naïve patients who received neoadjuvant pembrolizumab monotherapy, followed by pembrolizumab combined with hypofractionated radiotherapy [23]. Serial ultrasound-guided biopsies were analyzed at three defined timepoints: prior to pembrolizumab cycle 1 (baseline group), 3 weeks after pembrolizumab cycle 1 (on-treatment monotherapy, PD1 group), and 3 weeks after pembrolizumab cycle 2 combined with focal RT (on-treatment combination therapy, RTPD1 group). Following these treatments, patients received standard-of-care chemotherapy and surgery, where clinical response was determined by pathological complete response (pCR), including 23 responders and 11 non-responders. This precise temporal sampling allows for a longitudinal evaluation of how cellular states evolve from treatment-naïve baseline through various stages of neoadjuvant immunotherapy.

For spatial validation, we analyzed a published 10 × Visium spatial transcriptomics dataset comprising 10 TNBC samples [24]. This dataset was used to characterize the spatial distribution of tumor cell states and to assess neighborhood enrichment relationships between state-enriched tumor regions and surrounding cell populations.

Public data sources, accession numbers, and the corresponding references for all external cohorts are provided in the Data availability section and in the relevant Methods subsections below.

Spatial sampling, ROI annotation, laser capture microdissection, and spatial registration

Fresh-frozen tissue samples from primary tumors and matched lymph node metastases were processed for laser capture microdissection (LCM) as previously described [25]. Briefly, OCT-embedded tissues were serially sectioned at 10 μm thickness. Cryosections were mounted onto membrane slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific, LCM0522) and stored at −80°C until use.

To guide spatial sampling, an adjacent section was stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and reviewed by a qualified pathologist. Tumor regions were annotated on the basis of standard histomorphological criteria, including malignant epithelial/tumor nests, stromal regions, immune-rich areas, and transitional zones. Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined as contiguous and morphologically coherent areas with minimal necrosis or artefact and relatively homogeneous cellular composition at the microdissection scale. For each candidate ROI, the relative proportions of tumor cells, immune cells, and fibroblasts were estimated by pathological review. When feasible, ROIs were preferentially selected along the morphologically defined tumor boundary to capture biology associated with the tumor-non-tumor interface. Boundary-adjacent regions were annotated on the H&E reference section and matched to the corresponding locations on the LCM slide while preserving tissue integrity and ensuring sufficient cellular material for downstream analyses.

Because H&E staining is not compatible with LCM and Histogene staining provides reduced morphological resolution, the adjacent H&E-stained section was used as the histological reference for ROI localization. Before microdissection, LCM slides underwent rapid staining to facilitate microscopic visualization and confirm the identity and position of each target ROI. ROIs were then isolated using a Leica LMD7000 system. Images acquired before and after capture, together with the excised ROI contours, were re-evaluated by the pathologist to confirm cellular composition and minimize cross-compartment contamination. Each captured cluster was estimated to contain approximately 500–1000 cells, and 375 clusters with an estimated tumor purity > 40% were retained for downstream analyses.

For spatial registration, images acquired before and after LCM were aligned and merged to generate a contour map of each captured ROI on the tissue section. Contour maps were exported in PDF format and imported into AutoCAD to extract centroid coordinates for each cluster. The Euclidean distance between the centroid coordinates of any two clusters on the same section was calculated and defined as the spatial distance.

Each spatially registered cluster corresponded to the same microdissected material subjected to pGeo-G&T-seq, generating paired DNA-derived genomic and RNA-derived transcriptomic profiles. Spatial coordinates were linked to molecular data using unique cluster identifiers, enabling integrated analyses of genomic alterations and gene-expression programs in their spatial tissue context, including comparisons between boundary-adjacent and more interior tumor regions.

mRNA and gDNA extraction from microdissected cell clusters

The extracted cell cluster tissues were incubated with 9 μL RLT Plus buffer (QIAGEN 1053393) for 15 min in room temperature to lyse the cell clusters and immediately frozen on dry ice, and subsequently stored frozen at −80 °C. mRNA and gDNA were separated, and then mRNA was reverse transcribed, amplified, purified to obtain cDNA, while gDNA was amplified and purified, as described in previous study [26]. M-280 Streptavidin Dynabeads® (Invitrogen, 11205D) 10 μL for each sample were washed according to the manufacturer's recommendations, mixed with biotinylated Oligo-dT30VN primer (100 μM) at a 1:1 ratio, and incubated at room temperature for 20 min. Oligo-dT30VN-labeled beads were then washed and resuspended in bead resuspension buffer (9.5 μL NF-H2O, 0.5 μL RNase inhibitor). For mRNA and gDNA separation, 10 μL Oligo-dT30VN-labeled beads were added to the PCR tube containing the cell clusters and 1 μL spike-in RNA (1:250,000) was added and incubated at room temperature for 20 min. The sample was then placed on a magnetic stand for 2 min to separate the beads. Transfer the supernatant containing gDNA to a new tube, wash the beads twice with 10 μL G&T-seq wash buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.3, 75 mM KCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 10 mM DTT, 0.5% Tween-20, 0.2 × RNase inhibitor), and transfer the supernatant containing gDNA to the same tube from the previous step and store on ice until the next purification and amplification. Add RT (reverse transcription) reaction reagent to the beads with mRNA for subsequent RT.

Purification and amplification of gDNA

AmPure XP beads (Agencourt, catalog number A63881) were added to the supernatant containing gDNA at a 1:1 ratio of gDNA to beads and incubated at room temperature for 8 min. The supernatant was discarded, the beads were washed twice with 200 μL 80% ethanol, and then 4.3 μL NF-H2O was added. The gDNA was amplified using the MGIEasy Single Cell Whole Genome Amplification Kit (MGI, 1,000,007,744). 3 μL Denature Lysis Buffer was added to the beads and incubated at 65 °C for 10 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 3 μL Neutralization Buffer to each sample and incubated at room temperature for 3 min. 40 μL Master Mix (39 μL Amplification Reaction Buffer, 1 μL Amplification Enzyme) was added to the DNA samples for amplification. Place the tube in a PCR machine and perform DNA amplification with the following program settings: 65 °C for 8 h, 65 °C for 5 min, and a final step down to 4 °C.

Reverse transcription, amplification, purification of cDNA

For reverse transcription, 10 μL RT Mastermix (2 μL 5 × SuperScript II First-Strand Buffer, 2 μL 5 M Betaine, 0.9 μL 100 mM MgCl2, 1 μL 10 mM dNTPs, 0.25 μL 100 mM DTT, 0.1 μL 100 μM TSO, 0.25 μL 40 U/μL RNase inhibitor, 0.5 μL 200 U/μL SSII, 3 μL NF-water) was added to the tube containing mRNA beads. The samples were placed in a Veriti 96-well PCR instrument (Applied Biosystems, 4,375,786) for RT using the following program settings: 42 °C for 60 min, 50 °C for 30 min, 60 °C for 10 min, and finally reduced to 4 °C. Then 15 μL PCR Mastermix (12.5 μL 2 × KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix, 0.25 μL 10 μM IS PCR Primer, 2.25 μL NF-H2O) was added to each RT reaction tube. The tubes were placed in a PCR machine and cDNA was amplified using the following program settings: 98 °C for 3 min, 17 cycles of 98 °C for 20 s, 67 °C for 15 s, 72 °C for 6 min; then 72 °C for 5 min, and a final step at 4 °C. The amplified cDNA was stored at − 20 °C until purification. To purify the amplified cDNA, room temperature AMPure XP beads were added to the tube containing the cDNA at a ratio of 0.8:1, mixed, and incubated at room temperature for 8 min. The supernatant was removed and the beads were washed twice with 200 μL of 80% ethanol and air dried. The beads were then resuspended in 21 μL of NF-H2O.

WES, WGS and WTS library preparation and sequencing

Genomic DNA was fragmented using a Covaris LE220 ultrasonicator (Covaris, Woburn, MA, USA). The WES libraries were constructed using the MGIEasy Exome Universal Library Prep Set V1.0 (MGI, 1,000,009,657) and MGIEasy Exome Capture V4 Probe Set (MGI, 940–000186-00). The WGS libraries were constructed using the MGIEasy Universal DNA Library Preparation Kit (MGI, 1,000,017,571). After quality and quantity assessment of the libraries, PE100 sequencing (100-bp paired-end sequencing) was performed on the Dipseq-T1 platform for WES and WGS sequencing.

The WTS libraries were constructed using Tn5 cDNA library, same as previous study [27]. 1.0 ng of cDNA from each sample was mixed with the fragmentation mix containing the BGI enzyme Tn5 transposase (BGI, product number BGE005) with an embedded adapter in a final volume of 20 μL and heated to 55 °C for 7 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 5 μL of 0.1% SDS to each sample. 25 μL of PCR reaction mixture (10 μL 5 × KAPA Fidelity buffer, 1.5 μL 10 mM of each dNTP, 1 μL 10 mM PhoAd153 F primer,1 μL 10 mM Ad153 R primer, 1 μL 0.5 μM Ad153-F tag, 1 μL 0.5 μM Ad153-R tag, 1 μL KAPA HiFi DNA polymerase) was added to each fragmented cDNA sample. The samples were transferred to a thermal cycler for amplification using the following program settings: 72 °C for 5 min, 95 °C for 3 min, 15 cycles of 98 °C for 20 s, 60 °C for 15 s, 72 °C for 25 s; then 72 °C for 5 min and a final step at 4 °C. After amplification, fragments of 300 bp ± 100 bp size were selected using 0.6 × and 0.2 × AMPure XP beads. They were then pooled for a total of 400 ng cDNA. The pooled cDNA was circularized by adding 5 μL 20 μM Splint oligo and NF-H2O in a final volume of 70 μL and the samples were heated to 95 °C for 3 min. Ligation buffer (12 μL 10 × TA buffer, 1.2 μL 100 mM ATP, 0.42 μL T4 DNA ligase (600 U/μL), 36.38 μL NF-H2O) was added to each reaction to a final volume of 120 μL. The samples were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h and a final step at 4 °C.

EXO digestion was then performed by adding 0.4 μL 10 × TA buffer, 1.95 μL EXO I (20 U/μL), 1.65 μL EXO III (100 U/μL) and 1 μL NF-H2O to each pooled sample to a final volume of 128 μL. The samples were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The reaction products were then purified by adding 320 μL AMPure XP beads to obtain cDNA libraries. Rolling circle amplification (RCA) was performed to generate DNA nanoballs (DNBs), which were then loaded onto the BGISEQ-500 sequencing platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China). After quality and quantity control, WTS-seq regions were sequenced on BGISEQ-500 sequencing (100-bp paired-end sequencing) platform.

SNV and indels calling from WES sequencing

Paired-end reads (2 × 150 bp) reads were quality control using SOAPnuke [28] (v2.0.7), and FASTQ data corresponding to each cell cluster was aligned to GRCh38 using BWA [29] (BWA, v0.7.17-r1188). the result is piped directly to samtools [30] (v1.5) to coordinate for sort processing and output in BAM format. PCR duplicates were flagged and removed using Sentieon (v1.4.1.3) driver function. Total 230 tumor cell clusters with tumor purity > 40%, target coverage 10 × > 0.75 and target average depth > 100 were used for further analysis.

Single Nucleotide Variants (SNVs) and small-scale insertion/deletions (Indels) calling was performed based on paired peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) and tumor cell clusters. Given that single cell amplification (MDA) was used before sequencing, two strategies were used to detect mutations to ensure the accuracy of mutation detection: WES strategy and Single cells strategy. For WES strategy, SNVs and indels were called using MuSE [31] (v1.0rc), Mutect2 [32](v3.0.1), Svaba [33] (v0.2.1), Strelka2 [34] (v2.9.9), SomaticSniper [35], Lancet [36] (v1.0.7), Svaba (v0.2.1), GATK [32] (v4.0.10.1) (HaplotypeCaller module) and annotated using ANNOVAR [37]. The SNVs and indels detected by at least two methods were defined as the initial mutation set. The filtering criteria for mutations are as follows: (1) detected by Mutect2, (2) ALT read count > = 2, (3) observed in at least 3 cell clusters from the same patient, (4) indels with length < = 4 will be filtered out (Result1). For Single cells strategy, 3 tools: SciPhi [38] (v0.1.4, Result2), ProSolo [39] (v0.6.1, Result3) and SCcaller [40] (v2.0.0, Result4) were used with default parameters. Only mutations detected in at least two Results were used for further analysis. For each cell clusters, tumor mutation burden (TMB) was calculated as the number of base substitution and indel mutations per megabase of genome examined.

Mutation signature analysis

All mutation data in primary cell clusters (n = 95, only cell clusters with at least 30 SNVs were included in this analysis) were converted into a matrix that was made up of 96 features comprising mutation counts for each mutation type. The package “DeconstructSigs” [41] (v1.8.0) was used to identify mutational signatures that presented in the cell clusters using linear decomposition method and default parameters (which filtered out fitted signature weights below 6% in each sample). All single base-substitution signatures (SBSs) in the COSMIC (v2) catalog were used for signature inference. Only signatures with a proportion of primary tumors more than 40% were shown.

WGS data processing and somatic copy number alterations calling

Paired-end reads (2 × 150 bp) in FastQ format corresponding to each cell cluster was aligned to the reference human genome (GRCh38), using BWA-MEM algorithms in Sentieon. PCR duplicates were mark and removed using Dedup method and genome coverage were calculated using WgsMetricsAlgo algorithm in Sentieon DRIVER. Total 293 cell clusters with tumor purity > 40% and MEAN COVERAGE > 2 were used for further analysis.

In dividing the genome into bins for copy number estimation, we utilize a method that partitions the genome into bins of 50 kb size based on the unique mappability of sequences across the human genome, with each bin containing the same number of mappable positions, following Baslan et al. [27]. Bin counts were normalized for GC content with lowess regression and bin-wise ratios were calculated by computing the ratio of bin counts to the sample mean bin count. Segmentation was performed with circular binary segmentation from R Bioconductor DNACopy package (alpha = 0.05, nperm = 1000, undo.SD = 1.0, min.width = 5). Then processed segmented copy number data were used as input for GISTIC [42] (v2.0) to identify significantly amplified or deleted regions. GISTIC was run with a 0.99 confidence level and other default parameters. Aberrant focal regions with FDR Q-values %0.25 were considered significant. The significant focal deletion with the “Actual Copy Change Given” ≤ −1 and focal amplification with the “Actual Copy Change Given” > = 1 were defined as present in cell clusters (all_lesions.conf_99.txt file).

Weighted genome instability index (wGII)

To assess chromosomal instability, the weighted genome instability index (wGII) was calculated based on segmentation files. For each chromosome, GII was calculated as the fraction of the genome with aberrant copy numbers. The wGII was calculated as the mean fraction across all 22 chromosomes, which can eliminate the influence of different chromosome lengths.

RNA-seq data processing

Raw sequencing reads were first aligned to ribosomal RNA sequences downloaded from NCBI Reference Sequence Database using SOAP aligner (soap2, V2.21t) to remove reads containing rRNA. The unmapped reads were further cleaned by removing containing adaptor reads, low-quality base (> 50% based with quality < 5), low-quality reads (with more than 10% unknown bases) and trimming 15 bp of read’s head and then aligned to human genome assembly GRCh38. Gene expression TPM was calculated using bowtie2 plus RSEM [43] (v1.2.3) with default parameters.

t-SNE dimensionality reduction

Gene expression matrices were converted to a Seurat object using the Seurat R package (version 4.1.0). From this, all cell clusters were removed that had below 500 expressed genes and over 20% gene expression derived from mitochondrial genome, meanwhile, genes expressed in at least 3 cell clusters are retained. From the remaining 559 cells clusters, 25,773 gene expression matrices were normalized using linear regression as implemented in Seurat’s NormalizeData function. To reduce dimensionality, the variably expressed genes were summarized by principal component analysis, and then the t-SNE dimensionality reduction (RunTSNE function) was used to further summarize the principal components. They were estimated by an Elbow plot, in combination with exploration of the top genes from each principal component. The graph-based clustering approach implemented in the FindClusters function of the Seurat package was used for data clustering, with a K parameter of 30 and default parameters used otherwise. Finally, Seurat’s default function FeaturePlot is used to display the clustering results [26].

Only cell clusters with tumor purity > = 75% and expressed gene > 5000 were used for further analysis (n = 236 cell clusters). DESeq2 [44] (v1.42.1) was used for differential expressed genes (DEG) identification. Genes with |log2 FC|> 1.5 and adjusted p < 0.01 identified as DEGs.

HLA genotyping, LOH inference, and neoepitope prediction

HLA-related analyses, including HLA genotyping, loss-of-heterozygosity inference, and neoepitope prediction, were performed as an integrated analytical workflow. HLA genotyping was performed using the Polysolver algorithm (v4) [45], which enables high-resolution HLA allele prediction from whole-exome sequencing data, even at relatively low sequencing coverage. The BAM files of PBMC samples and tumor cell clusters were used as input data, and Polysolver was employed to identify the two alleles of the HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C genes. An HLA locus was considered to exhibit LOH if the matched PBMC sample (serving as the germline control) showed heterozygosity at that locus, while the corresponding tumor cell cluster exhibited loss of one allele, resulting in apparent homozygosity.

HLA mutations were identified by comparing tumor BAM files with the matched PBMC BAM files. Neoepitopes were predicted for each cluster by enumerating all mutant peptides ranging from 8 to 14 amino acids in length that arose from mutations in expressed genes, and then determining whether their predicted binding affinity to the patient’s germline HLA alleles was < 500 nM.. Neoantigen prediction was performed using four tools, MHCflurry [46] (v2.0.2), MixMHCpred [47] (v2.1), NetMHC [48](v4.0), NetMHCpan [49] (v4.1). Only neoantigens identified by all four tools were retained in the final neoantigens list. For each cell clusters, tumor neoantigen burden (TNB) was calculated as the number of neoantigens per megabase of genome examined.

Intrinsic molecular subtypes of TNBC

Based on the Intra-tumoral/inter-tumor gene expression heterogeneity of TNBC clusters (tumor purity > = 75%, gene count > = 5000, the number of TNBC cell clusters: 175), NMF clustering analysis was performed to identify distinct modules (SD top 2000). The optimal number of clusters (k = 4) and their stability were determined by the consensus clustering algorithm. The R package “NMF” was used to perform the consensus clustering. Based on the gene expression characteristics of each module, GO, KEGG metabolism, and HALLMARK gene enrichment analysis (clusterProfiler [50], v3.18.1) was performed to define the pathway characteristics of different modules.

Quantification of spatial decay and uniformity patterns

To characterize spatial intratumoral heterogeneity, we evaluated the dependency of molecular similarity on physical spatial distance. For each patient, we calculated the Euclidean distance between the centroids of all pairs of tumor cell clusters. Simultaneously, we computed pairwise molecular similarities across four dimensions: (i) SNV similarity: Calculated as the Jaccard similarity coefficient based on the binary mutation matrix (0/1) of shared somatic variants. (ii) WTS similarity: Calculated as the Spearman correlation coefficient of the expression profiles of the top 2,000 most variable genes. (ii) CNV similarity: Defined as the proportion of genomic segments exhibiting identical copy number states (gain, loss, or neutral) derived from Circular Binary Segmentation (CBS). (iv) Microenvironment similarity: Calculated as the Spearman correlation coefficient of immune and stromal cell abundance estimated by xCell.

To quantify the relationship between spatial distance and molecular similarity, we performed Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. To rule out the confounding effect of tumor purity, we further calculated partial Spearman’s correlation coefficients, adjusting for both the mean and the absolute difference in tumor purity between cluster pairs. Statistical Thresholds: (1) spatial decay pattern: Defined by a statistically significant negative partial correlation (Partial Spearman’s p < 0.05), indicating that molecular similarity significantly diminishes with increasing spatial distance. (2) Spatial Uniformity Pattern: Defined by the lack of a significant negative correlation (p ≥ 0.05), indicating that molecular similarity is spatially independent or homogeneous across the sampled region.

Definition of bulk TNBC subtypes and corresponding tumor states in public datasets

Here, we use “subtype” to refer to bulk or laser-captured tumor cell clusters TNBC molecular classifications derived from transcriptomic profiling, consistent with previous TNBC subtype frameworks. When the same transcriptional programs are evaluated at the level of individual tumor cells and spatial spots, we refer to them as tumor cell “states”. The gsva function of R-package GSVA [51] (v1.38.2) was used to calculate the gsva score for 277 TNBC samples (Breast Invasive Ductal Carcinoma), with ER−, PR− and HER2−, in METABRIC cohort using the top 100 feature genes of four subtypes (IM regulatory, LAR, OXPHOS, IM/MES). Then, the pheatmap function from the pheatmap R package (v1.0.12) was employed for sample clustering based on the GSVA score matrix, and the resulting clusters were categorized into single subtypes (genes highly expressed exclusively in one subtype) and mixed subtypes (genes highly expressed in two or more subtypes). Notably, only samples with high or moderate cellularity were included in the subsequent clinical prognostic association analysis of tumor subtypes within the METABRIC TNBC cohort. The same approach was used for TNBC samples from the Bulk RNA-seq dataset (iSPY-2 TNBC cohort (n = 346) [19], BrighTNess TNBC cohort (n = 482) [20], SCAN-B cohort (n = 874) [21]), and tumor cells from the two TNBC single cell cohorts [22, 23].

For the two independent single-cell TNBC validation cohorts, normalized gene expression matrices and curated cell-type annotations were directly used as provided by the respective published studies: (1) The scTNBC cohort includes 12 TNBC patients with paired pre- and on-treatment biopsies receiving anti-PD-1 therapy, yielding a total of 15,268 quality-controlled tumor cells. (2) The scPCPRT cohort includes 34 treatment-naive TNBC patients receiving neoadjuvant pembrolizumab monotherapy followed by pembrolizumab plus hypofractionated radiotherapy, yielding 50,183 high-quality tumor cells. The same GSVA-based signature-scoring framewok was applied to tumor cells from both single-cell cohorts to identify corresponding tumor cell states and ensure consistency across datasets.

TNBC spatial transcriptomics dataset

To explore the spatial distribution of tumor cell states, the following analytical procedures were performed: (1) Multi-sample integration: Each sample was normalized using the default parameters of the SCTransform function from the Seurat R package. Subsequently, all samples were merged, and batch effect correction was conducted using the Harmony R package. (2) Spot cell type annotation: The TNBC single-cell RNA sequencing dataset published by Bassez A et al. [22] was used as a reference to perform RNA composition analysis by deconvolution (RCTD, v4.1.1) for each spatial transcriptomic (ST) spot. The spot type was defined based on the maximum deconvolution score. The annotated cell types included: ASDC, B_cell, Mac_C1_CD14, Mac_C2_CD16, Mac_C3_CCR2, Mac_C4_CCL2, Mac_C5_CCL18, Mac_C6_MMP9, Mac_C7_CX3CR1, Mac_C8_MT1G, Mac_C9_SLC2A1, Mac_C10_LYVE1, Cancer_cell, CD4_EM, CD4_EX, CD4_EX_Proliferating, CD4_N, CD4_REG, CD4_REG_Proliferating, CD8_EM, CD8_EMRA, CD8_EX, CD8_EX_Proliferating, CD8_N, CD8_RM, cDC1, cDC2, Endothelial_cell, Fibroblast, Tγδ, LanghDC, Mast_cell, mregDC_migDC, Myeloid_cell, NK_CYTO, NK_REST, pDC, quiescent_migDC, T_cell, Vγ9/Vδ2 Tγδ. (3) Tumor spot state definition: The AddModuleScore function was utilized to calculate the enrichment scores of the four subtype-specific gene sets for all tumor spots. The definition criteria were as follows: spots were classified as pure state-enriched spots if the highest score was at least one-quarter higher than the second-highest score; otherwise, the spots were defined as mixed subtypes. (4) Cell neighborhood analysis: Squidpy (v1.7.0) was employed to identify clusters adjacent to each other in the tissue by calculating neighborhood enrichment. If spots of one cluster were frequently adjacent to those of another cluster, they would exhibit a high spatial proximity enrichment score, indicating significant enrichment of spatial adjacency.

Archetype analysis

ParetoTI [52] package (v0.1.13) allows describing a continuum of cell states by identifying representative cells with most distinctive expression (archetypes). The cell clusters which tumor purity > = 30% and more than 5,000 expressed genes were used (count: 409), meanwhile, genes expressed in at least 5% of the samples were also selected as inputs. The cell clusters and genes satisfying the above two conditions were selected to construct the analysis model.

Combined with a variety of statistical indicators (Variance explained, Variance explained on top of k-1 model, Mean variance in position of vertices, T-ratio of volume of polytope by volume of convex hull), 4 archetypes were identified to construct polyhedral, analyze the gradient distribution of tumor cell clusters in the task model space, study the heterogeneity of tumors, and predict the state of tumor cells (clusters) in multiple tasks.

VIPER analysis

The regulator activity was inferred via VIPER algorithm [53] using transcriptome data from 175 TNBC tumor cell clusters with tumor purity > = 75% and gene count > = 5000, and 8 normal epithelial cell clusters with purity > 95% as a control group. The regulon networks of breast cancer were obtained from ARACNe package [54] and regulators activities of each cell cluster were calculated using VIPER [53] tool which inferred how each regulator regulates its target genes based the regulon network. The positive value indicates that the regulator positively regulates its target genes to be overexpressed, and the negative value indicates negative regulatory effect.

Quantification of metastatic ability

The metastatic potential of cell clusters was quantified using enrichment scores of the invasiveness gene sets (EMT, Metastasis, Invasion) downloaded from the Cancer Single-Cell State Atlas (CancerSEA) database. The gsva function of R-package GSVA (v1.38.2) was used to calculate the gsva score of three invasiveness gene sets for 236 cell clusters. Then, pheatmap function in pheatmap R package (v1.0.12) was applied to the gsva score matrix to cluster the cell clusters into three groups, namely high invasiveness (n = 83), moderate invasiveness (n = 50), and low invasiveness (n = 103).

Phylogenetic tree reconstruction

PHYLIP was run via the online version (http://www.trex.uqam.ca/index.php?action=phylip) and the maximum parsimony method (Pars) was applied to reconstruct the phylogenetic tree of each patient using somatic mutations (SNVs and Indels) and genome-wide copy number profiles (50 k bin). For each patient, a mutation matrix was generated with columns labeled variations (SNVs, Indels and bins) and rows labeled tumor cell clusters, and its elements can only be 0, 1, 2, 3. For SNVs and Indels, two states, 0 (without mutation) and 1 (with mutation) were defined. For genome-wide copy number profiles, the processed segmented copy number data were output by DNACopy package. The copy number values were equal to 2^seg.mean × 2. Candidate gain and loss regions were identified using the following cutoffs: loss region with copy number < 1.5 and gain region with copy number > = 2.5. So, three copy number states per bin, 1 (copy number loss), 2 (no aberration), and 3 (copy number gain), were defined. Normal samples were defined by default as those without genetic mutations. PHYLIP (Pars) was then applied to each patient's mutation matrix to construct phylogenetic tree. The tree structure information was saved in Newick format, and then FigTree (v1.4.4) was used to visualize the tree structure with normal sample as root. Case P10 was removed due to only lymph node metastases were sampled.

Genetic timing of cell clusters

The branch lengths between two nodes in the evolutionary tree were quantified based on the minimum number of substitutions. The genetic time of a cell cluster was defined as the length between the adjacent internal node (non-leaf node) of the cell cluster and the root node output by FigTree. The smaller the genetic time of the cell cluster, the earlier it branches in the tumor evolution process.

To compare the genetic time between patients, for each case, the scaled genetic time of the cell cluster was calculated as the genetic time divided by the maximum genetic time for that case, ranging from 0 to 1. The absolute value of the difference in scaled genetic time between evolutionarily close paired primary-metastasis clusters (P-M pair) in each case was defined as Tpm. The genetic time differences within primary tumor clusters (P clusters from P-M pairs versus other primary tumor clusters, P-P pairs) and within metastatic clusters (M clusters from P-M pairs versus other metastatic clusters, M-M pairs) were defined as Tpp and Tmm, respectively. For each case, the Tpp group was further divided into two subgroups based on the scaled genetic time cutoff value (Tpm, defined as the maximum value of all Tpms), called Tpp_1 (Tpp < Tpm) and Tpp_2(Tpp > Tpm). The same approach was applied to the Tmm group. In the analysis of Fig. 6H, one outlier value Tpm was removed, which had no effect on the scaled genetic time cutoff value Tpm. The Hallmark pathway similarity between two cell clusters was quantified using the Spearman correlation coefficient based on the Hallmark pathway (n = 47) enrichment scores.

Identification of subclonal mutations

PyClone [55] (v0.13.1) was applied to infer the clonality of mutations. The mutation input file was prepared for each cell cluster by obtaining the reference and variant read counts for each SNV/Indel, with major (set to total copy number) and minor copy numbers (set to 0). SNVs/Indels without copy number information or with major copy number equal to zero were removed, and the normal copy number was set to 2 for autosomes. The mutation input files for all cell clusters of a patient, along with tumor purity were used as inputs for PyClone using the total copy number option. ‘‘Cellular prevalence’’ outputted by PyClone fits the definition of CCF. Mutations with CCF < 0.1 were discared for further analysis.

To determine the optimal CCF cutoff for clonal and subclonal status, we first calculated merged CCF for each tumor, as previously described [56], and applied Gaussian mixture model on the density distribution for CCF in all tumors by using mclust (v6.1) package in R and identified 6 clusters. To aggregate these 6 clusters into two cluster, hierarchical clustering was then performed on the centers of 6 clusters, resulting in two major clusters: 4 original clusters were merged into one major cluster, defined as subclonal status, and the other original 2 clusters was defined as clonal status with CCF > 0.58.

Assignment of selected/not selected/maintained alterations

Mutations with CCF > 0.1 were categorized into three groups based on evidence of selection in the metastases, same as previous published method [57]. “unselected”, defined as present in the primary and absent from metastasis. “maintained”, defined as the most recent common ancestor clones, subclonal/clonal in both primary and metastasis. “selected”, defined as subclonal in the primary and clonal in metastasis; or absent in the primary and present in metastasis.

Enrichment of events in metastases

Odds ratios (OR) were calculated to characterize the enrichment of events in metastasis, same as previous published method [58] were used in this analysis. Specifically, for each combination of event i and tumor site j, a 2 by 2 contingency table was constructed for paired primary and metastatic tumor, which contained the number of cell clusters with event i in primary tumor, the number of cell clusters without event i in primary tumor, the number of cell clusters with event i in metastatic tumor, the number of cell clusters without event i in metastatic tumor. Then Fisher’s exact test was applied on this contingency table, thus OR and corresponding p-value could be obtained. P-values were adjusted using the BH method implemented in the R function p.adjust for each patient. Only events in “selected group” with ORs < 0.5 and p-values < 0.1 were defined as metastatic driver event.

Cell type deconvolution prediction

ESTIMATE [59] (R package, v1.0.13), xCELL [60] (https://xcell.ucsf.edu/) and MCPcounter [61] (v1.2.0) were used to estimate immune and stromal cell populations.

Tumor Immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) analysis

The transcriptome profiles of the cell clusters were uploaded to the TIDE [62] website (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu), and then downloaded the TIDE scores, T-cell dysfunction score and exclusion score for all cell clusters.

Gene and pathway enrichment clustering

Hallmark cancer pathways were downloaded from MSigDB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/human/genesets.jsp?collection=H). Three unrelated pathways MYOGENESIS, SPERMATOGENSIS and PANCREAS_BETA_CELLS were removed, and the pathway COMPLEMENT was renamed with COMPLEMENT_INNATE_IMMUNE_SYSTEM. WNT_SIGNALING pathway was downloaded and added (http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/geneset_page.jsp?geneSetName=WNT_SIGNALING). Finally, total 47 pathways were used in this analysis. After filtering samples with tumor purity > = 75% and gene expression number > 5000, there are 166 cell clusters across 14 tumors with more than 5 cell clusters in 7 TNBC patients, and protein-coding genes (n = 25,799) was used as input for single gene set enrichment (ssgsea) analysis using the GSVA [63] R package (v1.38.2). The s.d. and mean of enrichment were then recorded for per-tumor. Jointly clustering the s.d. and mean of pathway enrichment by using pheatmap, and grouping the distinguishing different features, the pathways can be divided into four group. Pagel’s λ was calculated for four group, it returns the Lambda and p values. Gene (n = 12,037 genes with TPM > = 20 in more than 5% cell clusters) enrichment clustering was the same as pathway analysis.

Transcript phylogenetic signal analysis

The cell clusters with unmatched DNA-RNA data were excluded in this analysis. In the tree structure, the cell clusters without matched DNA-RNA data were removed using drop.tip from ape R package-ape (v5.6.1). Total 75 cell clusters from 6 TNBC patients (range 7–20 cell clusters per patient) with matched DNA-RNA data were used in this analysis. For gene level analysis, ribosomal genes were removed and only high expressed genes with TPM > = 20 in more than 5% cell clusters were included. For pathway level analysis, 47 pathways were included. In the genic phylogenetic signal analysis, Pagel’s λ was calculated for four groups (n = 47 pathways) using “phylosig” in package phytools (v1.2.0). This returns the maximum-likelihood Pagel’s λ estimate and a p value for the likelihood ratio test with the null hypothesis of λ = 0. This analysis was performed for all trees, with median p < 0.05 indicating evidence of phylogenetic signal for that gene, same as previous published method [16].

Gene set enrichment analysis

GSEA (v1.64.0) and clusterProfiler (v4.10.1) were used for Gene Set Enrichment Analysis. Hallmark gene sets used in this article were downloaded from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) org:Profiler database. GSEA results were visualized by enrichplot package (v1.22.0).

Survival analysis

Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method, with p value determined by a log-rank test. Kaplan–Meier figures were drawn by R package survminer (v0.4.9) and survival (v3.6.4). Univariate analyses were performed using Cox models in R package survival (v3.7.0).

Using this approach, we profiled 375 tumor cell clusters (median = 16 cell clusters per tumor) with an estimated tumor purity > 40% from ten primary tumors (BC), nine axillary lymph node metastases (metastasis-A), and three distant lymph node metastases (metastasis-D) collected from treatment-naive breast cancer patients (Fig. 1B). After quality control, 236 clusters were retained for whole-transcriptome sequencing, 230 for whole-exome sequencing, and 293 for whole-genome sequencing (Fig. 1C and Additional file 2: Fig. S1B, C), providing matched spatial multi-omics data for downstream analyses. Spatial coordinates of each cell cluster were documented (Fig. 1D). Detailed clinicopathological characteristics of the patients are available in Additional file 1: Table S1.

This dataset enabled us to quantify spatial intratumoral heterogeneity, reconstruct phylogenetic relationships between primary and metastatic lesions, and examine the coupling between genomic evolution and phenotypic diversification. We therefore next investigated how spatially resolved genomic and transcriptomic programs shape metastatic progression and clinically relevant subtype states in TNBC.

Genomic characteristics of tumor cell clusters

To validate the accuracy of the pGeo-G&T-seq workflow, we first detected somatic variants in each cell cluster. A total of 4,968 somatic mutations were identified across 230 cell clusters with WES data, including 1,819 non-silent mutations and 3,149 silent mutations. The median number of non-silent mutations per cell cluster was 33 in primary tumors and 29 in metastatic lesions. At the tumor cluster level, higher mutation burdens were observed, with medians of 98 and 91.5 non-silent mutations in primary tumors and metastases, respectively, exceeding values reported in previous single-region sequencing studies [12, 64, 65].

Consistent with previous studies, the most frequently mutated cancer-related gene in the cohort was TP53 (coding mutations in 76% of tumors), followed by HLA-B (24%), PIK3CA (19%), and APOB (19%) [66–69] (Additional file 2: Fig. S1D). Notably, the relatively high frequency of HLA-B point mutations likely reflects the cluster-level resolution of our analysis. Matched germline and intratumoral profiling revealed pronounced spatial and clonal heterogeneity of both HLA-B point mutations and loss of heterozygosity between primary tumors and matched lymph node metastases (Additional file 1: Table S2). Mutational signature analysis identified enrichment of SBS1 and 5 (clock-like signatures) and SBS13 (APOBEC-related signature), in line with previous studies [12, 70, 71] (Additional file 2: Fig. S1E). Recurrent somatic copy number alterations (SCNAs) were further investigated, showing comparable alteration frequencies of TNBC driver genes in primary tumor between this cohort and the FUSCC cohort [12] (Additional file 2: Fig. S1F). Collectively, the spectrum of recurrent genomic alterations observed in this study closely mirrors prior findings, underscoring the robustness and high quality of the dataset.

Spatial intra-tumoral heterogeneity in TNBC

TNBC is characterized by significant intra-tumoral heterogeneity, which contributes to poor prognosis and variable therapeutic responses [72–74]. However, the spatial organization of heterogeneous genotypes and phenotypes within tumors remains largely unexplored. Leveraging the pGeo-G&T-seq dataset, we systematically characterized the spatial intra-tumoral heterogeneity of genetic variations and transcriptomic phenotypes in TNBC. Across tumor cell clusters, we observed substantial variability in diverse driver events, tumor mutation burden (TMB), tumor neoantigen burden (TNB), and weighted genomic instability index (wGII) across cell clusters. Most driver alterations were restricted to subsets of tumor cell clusters, with notable exceptions including TP53 mutations (detected in 36–100% of clusters) and MYC amplification (12.5–100%) (Fig. 2A). Five patients (one luminal B and four TNBCs) with sufficient paired primary and metastatic cell clusters and preserved spatial coordinates were selected for further topographical analyses. Driver events were traced back to the spatial locations of cell clusters to delineate their distribution patterns. Distinct driver alterations, such as IRS2 and CCNE1 amplification in case P05, exhibited pronounced spatial mosaicism while maintaining regional spatial continuity within and across tumor sections (Fig. 2B). To explore transcriptomic heterogeneity, non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) classified 175 TNBC cell clusters with WTS data into four subgroups based on the top 2000 highly variable genes (Additional file 2: Fig. S2A). These subgroups were defined as an immunomodulatory (IM) subtype, characterized by inflammatory response and leukocyte chemotaxis; a luminal androgen receptor (LAR) subtype, enriched for steroid hormone biosynthesis and androgen response; an immunomodulatory and mesenchymal-like (IM/MES) subtype, featuring epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathways together with immune-related signatures; and a putative OXPHOS subtype, marked by oxidative phosphorylation and metabolic processes (Additional file 2: Fig. S2B–D). The IM, LAR, and IM/MES subtypes correspond to previously reported TNBC molecular subtypes, e.g., immunomodulatory, luminal androgen receptor, and mesenchymal-like programs described in prior transcriptome-based classifications [5, 8, 12], whereas the OXPHOS subtype was not explicitly represented in prior classifications and was primarily observed in case P03 in our discovery cohort. Subtype compositions varied widely among tumors, with one to three subtypes coexisting within individual tumor (Fig. 2A and Additional file 2: Fig. S2E). Although transcriptional subtypes were detected in both primary and metastatic lesions, no significant enrichment was observed in metastatic tumors, underscoring the extensive transcriptomic heterogeneity of TNBC (Additional file 2: Fig. S2F). Spatial mapping further revealed intratumoral subtype mosaicism (Additional file 2: Fig. S2G-J), which was most prominent in case P03 that two adjacent slices of the primary tumor (separated by ~ 40 µm) exhibited entirely different subtypes (Additional file 2: Fig. S2H), highlighting the spatially phenotypic complexity of TNBC.

To examine whether transcriptionally defined tumor subgroups correspond to distinct pathological and spatial contexts, we integrated histopathological assessment, microenvironmental deconvolution, and genomic profiling at the level of laser-captured tumor cell clusters. Pathological review of LCM sections and adjacent reference sections confirmed consistently high tumor purity (> 75%) across all four subtypes (OXPHOS, IM_regulatory, IM/MES, and LAR), minimizing confounding from variable tumor content (Additional file 2: Fig. S2K), and OXPHOS-associated clusters were not located adjacent to necrotic regions. Microenvironmental deconvolution using MCPcounter revealed coherent subtype-specific patterns, with OXPHOS-associated clusters displayed an immune-cold phenotype, IM_regulatory clusters exhibited immune-hot characteristics, IM/MES clusters showed intermediate immune infiltration with increased B cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells, and LAR clusters enriched for stromal components (Additional file 2: Fig. S2L). Analysis of genetic alterations further revealed subtype-associated distributions of mutations and copy-number changes, including higher frequencies of TP53 and RB1 alterations in IM_regulatory clusters, enrichment of canonical PIK3CA and AKT1 mutations in LAR, and more frequent DNAH family gene mutations in OXPHOS and IM/MES (Additional file 2: Fig. S2M-N). Copy-number analysis revealed shared gains and losses across OXPHOS, IM_regulatory, and IM/MES clusters, while LAR clusters exhibited additional subtype-specific copy-number changes (Additional file 2: Fig. S2O-R). Additionally, copy number gains of nine driver genes (KEL, GATA3, DHX9, BTG2, ELF3, PTPRC, SPTA1, RIT1, and ZBTB7) and copy number losses of twelve driver genes (NTRK3, RNF111, TCF12, KLF5, PIK3R1, IL6ST, MAP3K1, APC, MSH3, B2M, FAT1, and IRF2) were significantly enriched in the OXPHOS-associated clusters in our cohort (Additional file 2: Fig. S2S, Additional file 1: Table S3). Together, these results suggest that TNBC transcriptional programs are associated with distinct spatial, microenvironmental, and genomic architectures.

Additionally, tumor cell clusters within the same tumor exhibited varying metastatic potential, characterized by signature scores for invasiveness, EMT, and metastasis (Fig. 2A and Additional file 2: Fig. S2T). Notably, clusters with higher metastatic capacity also showed elevated stemness scores (Additional file 2: Fig. S2U). Intriguingly, tumor cell clusters derived from primary tumors, axillary lymph nodes, and distant lymph nodes demonstrated distinct metastatic potentials. Specifically, clusters from distant lymph nodes exhibited higher metastatic capacity than those from axillary lymph nodes (Fig. 2C), suggesting that dissemination to proximal lymph nodes may not require highly aggressive metastatic traits, whereas colonization of distant lymph nodes is associated with enhanced metastatic capacity.

Spatial intratumoral heterogeneity was evaluated by quantifying genetic and phenotypic similarities relative to spatial distance between tumor cell clusters. In several cases (e.g., P01, P03, and P05), both genetic variations (e.g., point mutations and genomic instability) and phenotypic features (e.g., gene expression patterns and microenvironment characteristics) exhibited a "spatial decay" pattern, with decreasing similarity as spatial distance increased. In contrast, other cases showed "spatial uniformity" pattern, where similarity remained weakly affected or largely independent of spatial distance (Fig. 2D and Additional file 2: Fig. S2V). Metastatic lesions demonstrated greater spatial uniformity compared to their paired primary tumors, as observed in P03 and P05, indicating increased homogeneity following metastatic dissemination (Additional file 2: Fig. S2V). Notably, in case P09, distant lymph node metastases were more homogeneous than both primary tumors and proximal lymph node metastases, suggesting that tumor clones of distant metastases may be subject to greater selection pressure (Additional file 2: Fig. S2W).

In summary, TNBC displays extensive spatial heterogeneity at both genomic and phenotypic levels, characterized by regional clonal expansion and mosaicism.

OXPHOS-enriched subtype is associated with poor prognosis

Oxidative phosphorylation is an active metabolic pathway in many cancers, and elevated OXPHOS activity has been associated with poor treatment outcomes in TNBC patients receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy [75]. We observed that the primary tumor of case P03 comprised both OXPHOS-enriched and IM regulatory subtypes, whereas only the OXPHOS subtype was detected in the paired axillary lymph node metastasis (Fig. 3A). Archetypal analysis [76] further supported this observation, revealing distinct expression patterns within the tumor (Additional file 2: Fig. S3A). Notably, the OXPHOS-enriched subtype, which was initially subclonal in the primary tumor, exhibited clonal expansion in the metastases, consistent with the possibility that OXPHOS-associated clusters were represented among metastasis-seeding populations. Interestingly, both primary and axillary lymph node metastasis cell clusters with the OXPHOS subtype exhibited significantly higher EMT, invasion, and metastasis signatures compared to the IM subtype (Additional file 2: Fig. S3B,C). To evaluate the clinical relevance of the OXPHOS-enriched transcriptional pattern, we analyzed the METABRIC TNBC cohort (Additional file 1: Table S4). This analysis identified the presence of both pure OXPHOS subtype and mixed subtypes within the cohort (Additional file 2: Fig. S3D), with patients classified as OXPHOS subtype showing significantly worse prognosis (Fig. 3B and Additional file 2: Fig. S3E). In SCAN-B cohort [21], higher OXPHOS GSVA scores were consistently associated with poorer clinical outcomes, while IM and IM/MES subtypes were associated with more favorable prognosis (Additional file 2: Fig. S3F-I, Additional file 1: Table S5). Master regulators, including transcription factors (TFs), have been implicated in tumor heterogeneity and functional specialization through distinct transcriptional programs [77]. Accordingly, we used the VIPER [53] (Virtual Inference of Protein-activity by Enriched Regulon analysis) algorithm to infer aberrant protein activity patterns of the OXPHOS-enriched subtype. The analysis revealed 21 aberrant core regulators significantly up-regulated in the OXPHOS-enriched subtype, including the master TF SOX4 [78, 79], mTORC1 activator RHEB [80] and oncoprotein STMN1 [81] involved in TNBC, and 34 down-regulated, including the TFs involved in mammary gland development (GLI2) [82, 83] and tumor suppressor SOX17 [78] (Additional file 2: Fig. S3L, Additional file 1: Table S6). The OXPHOS-enriched tumor cell state is associated with reduced treatment-related immune expansion

To investigate the potential clinical relevance of the OXPHOS-associated transcriptional program in the context of immunotherapy, we analyzed two public single-cell TNBC datasets: the scTNBC cohort (12 patients with paired pre- and on-treatment biopsies receiving anti-PD-1 therapy [22]) (Additional file 1: Table S7), and the scPCPRT cohort [23] (34 treatment-naive patients receiving neoadjuvant pembrolizumab monotherapy followed by pembrolizumab plus hypofractionated radiotherapy) (Additional file 1: Table S8). In both cohorts, the majority of tumor cells were enriched for the IM-regulatory signature; however, a small fraction of cells showed enrichment of OXPHOS-associated state signature genes, further indicating the presence of an OXPHOS-associated tumor cell state (Additional file 2: Fig. S4A,B). Although a predominant state was observed in most samples, substantial intra-sample subtype heterogeneity was consistently detected (Fig. 3D). Based on T-cell clonotype expansion during treatment, patients in scTNBC cohort were divided into two groups: group E (with clonotype expansion) and group NE (with limited or no expansion). Intriguingly, OXPHOS-associated tumor cells were enriched in the NE group (Fig. 3E and Additional file 2: Fig. S4C), whereas tumor cells enriched for the other three state signatures were more frequent in the group E (Additional file 2: Fig. S4D). A similar association was observed in the PCPRT scRNA-seq cohort (baseline time point, PD-1 monotherapy time point, RTPD1 combination therapy time point; 23 responders, 11 non-responders). GSVA analysis of tumor cells showed baseline OXPHOS enrichment was significantly higher in non-responders (NR) (Fig. 3F), while other subtypes were enriched in responders (R) (Additional file 2: Fig. S4E). Tumor cells were categorized as pure/mixed subtypes, with IM being the most prevalent (Additional file 2: Fig. S4F). Baseline samples (≥ 100 tumor cells) were divided into OXPHOS-high (≥ 50% OXPHOS cells) and OXPHOS-low (≤ 50% OXPHOS cells) groups; Fisher’s test supported an association between OXPHOS-high status and non-responsiveness (p = 0.04, Fig. 3G), and a similar association was observed in the post-PD-1 cohort (Additional file 2: Fig. S4G).

By contrast, analyses of the iSPY-2 (immunotherapy, Additional file 1: Table S9) and BrighTNess bulk cohorts (neoadjuvant chemotherapy, Additional file 1: Table S10) did not provide statistically significant support for a predictive association between OXPHOS activity and treatment response (Additional file 2: Fig. S4H,I). Only LAR subtype scores were elevated in non-responders in one BrighTNess arm, consistent with prior reports of LAR-like TNBC chemoresistance (Additional file 2: Fig. S4I). Collectively, these analyses indicate pervasive cellular-level state admixture and suggest that enrichment of OXPHOS-associated tumor cells is correlated to limited immunotherapy responsiveness in single-cell TNBC cohorts.

Notably, the OXPHOS-associated tumor cells demonstrated the most prominent enrichment in hallmark pathways related to cell proliferation, such as E2F targets and G2M checkpoints, across all groups (Additional file 2: Fig. S4J,K). Conversely, immune-related signaling, including Interferon-alpha and Interferon-gamma responses, was downregulated in OXPHOS-associated tumor cells relative to other subtype program-enriched tumor cells (Additional file 2: Fig. S4J,K). Additionally, genes involved in antigen processing and presentation showed consistently lower expression in the OXPHOS-associated tumor cells than in other tumor cell states (Additional file 2: Fig. S4L). These consistent patterns indicate OXPHOS-associated tumor cells represent a biologically distinct state characterized by increased proliferative programs and reduced antigen-presentation/immune-response signatures.

We next investigated the cellular network dynamics between different therapeutic response groups, both pre- and on-treatment in scTNBC cohort. Cell–cell correlations were calculated between subpopulations in groups E and NE to identify potential cellular modules. Group E exhibited tightly connected cellular modules in both pre- and on-treatment samples, whereas group NE displayed weaker and less organised associations, suggesting a more organized cellular structure in group E (Additional file 2: Fig. S5A-D). Interestingly, OXPHOS-associated tumor cells formed strong associations with other tumor cell states and cDC2s (Additional file 2: Fig. S5A-D). These correlations were more pronounced in pre-treatment samples from group E compared to group NE and were maintained during treatment in group E, but were lost in group NE (Additional file 2: Fig. S5A,B). A similar pattern was observed in the PCPRT single-cell cohort (Additional file 2: Fig. S5E,F).

We further assessed the spatial organization of these tumor cell states using an independent 10 × Visium TNBC spatial transcriptomics dataset (Additional file 1: Table S11) [24]. Cross-sample integration, batch effect elimination, and cell type annotation were performed on the 10 TNBC samples (Additional file 2: Fig. S6A-D). Consistently, OXPHOS-enriched tumor regions were less prevalent than other subtype regions across samples (Additional file 2: Fig. S6E-G). Neighbourhood enrichment scores were calculated to quantify spatial co-enrichment of distinct cell types and characterize cellular neighborhoods. OXPHOS-associated tumor cells showed spatial colocalization with IM/MES-enriched tumor regions across multiple samples (Fig. 3H), with the most prominent co-enrichment observed in samples sEA6 and SAA6 (Fig. 3I-L).

Collectively, these findings suggest that OXPHOS-associated tumor cells may be clinically relevant and associated with reduced treatment-related immune expansion. These observations raise the possibility that rare, spatially distributed tumor cell states, rather than only the predominant bulk subtype, may be relevant to treatment-associated tumor–immune dynamics.

Spatiotemporal evolution trajectory in TNBC

The spatial distribution of genotypes and phenotypes across TNBC cell clusters suggests tumor clonal expansion. To further explore the spatial growth patterns of tumor subclones and their metastatic routes, we reconstructed the spatial evolution trajectory using single nucleotide variants (SNVs), genome-wide copy number profiles [27, 84], and the geospatial location of cell clusters. Branch nodes were used to quantify the genetic timeline of cell clusters (Fig. 4A, B and Additional file 2: Fig. S7, Methods). In case P05, the evolutionary trajectory of cell clusters within primary tumor exhibited spatial continuity at the two-dimensional level. The metastatic tumor in case P05 was inferred to have been seeded by two distinct clonal waves: one originating from the tumor biopsy margin (metastasis-A, cell cluster 4, seeded by primary clusters 2, 3, 44, and 47, which were genetically early), and another originating from the tumor biopsy center (metastasis-A, cell clusters 1, 2, 3, and 6, seeded by primary cluster 8, which was genetically late) with the latter clone undergoing clonal expansion within the metastatic lesion (Fig. 4A). A similar pattern was observed in case P03. Across nine cases with paired primary and metastatic cell clusters, evolutionary trajectory analysis revealed that 10 of 11 metastatic lesions were polyclonally seeded, with metastatic cell cluster branches distributed across multiple phylogenetic lineages [56, 85, 86]. In three cases (P03, P05, and P09), clonal expansion was restricted to cell clusters of genetically late origin (Fig. 4A, B and Additional file 2: Fig. S7C). For instance, in P03, the metastatic lesion comprised two genetically distinct clones: one derived from an early genetic seeding event (cell cluster 35) and another from a late genetic seeding event (Fig. 4B). Notably, genetically late-derived clusters underwent clonal expansion within the metastasis and acquired additional variations, such as mutations in PRSS48 and POLDIP2. To further characterize the metastatic cell clusters seeded by genetically early versus late origins, we analyzed their genomic and transcriptomic features. While metastatic potential did not differ between these groups, early-origin clusters exhibited lower mutation burdens, reduced cell-cycle activity, higher hypoxia scores, and pronounced immune dysfunction (Fig. 4C).

In summary, spatially contiguous growth represents a dominant feature of TNBC, consistent with previous observation [87]. In our cohort, most lymph node metastases arise through polyclonal seeding, with cell clusters of genetically late origin undergoing clonal expansion, whereas early-origin clusters remain quiescent, characterized by elevated hypoxia scores and reduced proliferative activity (Fig. 4D).

Somatic copy number alterations are associated with tumor progression

Tumors accumulate increasing mutational burdens during evolution [88]. In TNBC, genomic instability, as measured by the weighted genomic instability index (wGII), significantly increases during tumor evolution, surpassing changes in tumor mutational burden (TMB) or tumor neoantigen burden (TNB) (Additional file 2: Fig. S8A). To identify focal-level genomic alterations contributing to tumor evolution, we assessed the correlation between somatic copy number alterations (SCNAs) and the genetic time of the cell clusters (Methods, Additional file 1: Table S12). We identified 40 focal-level SCNAs whose copy numbers were progressively gained or lost during tumor evolution in at least three TNBC patients, after adjusting for tumor purity (Fig. 4E). For instance, copy number gain of the 8q24.21, which harbors the MYC gene, showed a significant positive correlation with tumor spatial evolution (Additional file 2: Fig. S8B, C).

Among these SCNAs, 82.5% (33 of 40 focal-level SCNAs containing coding genes) were associated with corresponding changes in gene expression levels (Fig. 4F). The deletion of 6q23.1 had the most pronounced effect, with gene expression levels significantly reduced in the deletion group compared to the wild-type group (median 0.29 in the WT group vs. −0.31 in the Del group, p = 3.1e-05). Genes in this region were also downregulated in metastatic tumors compared to primary tumors (median −0.29 in the metastases vs. 0.19 in primary tumors, p = 6.3e-05) (Fig. 4G). Lower expression of genes within the 6q23.1 region was associated with significantly shorter relapse-free survival (Fig. 4H, Additional file 2: Fig. S8D) and worse overall survival (Additional file 2: Fig. S8E,F) in the METABRIC TNBC cohort.

To explore biological programs associated with 6q23.1 deletion contributes to tumor progression and metastasis, we stratified tumor cell clusters based on the presence of this deletion. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that cell clusters with 6q23.1 deletion were enriched in epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathway (Fig. 4I and Additional file 2: Fig. S8G), which was supported by an EMT score system (Additional file 2: Fig. S8H). However, the expression of E2F targets pathway was downregulated in these clusters (Additional file 2: Fig. S8G). This apparent trade-off between enhanced invasive potential and reduced proliferative capacity suggests that 6q23.1 deletion maybe associated with transcriptional programs related to metastatic dissemination.

Another notable focal SCNA, deletion of 15q14, was associated with reduced gene expression and poorer clinical outcomes in the METABRIC TNBC cohort (Additional file 2: Fig. S8I-M). However, no significant difference in expression levels was observed between primary tumors (median −0.07) and metastatic tumors (−0.11). GSEA indicated that clusters with 15q14 deletion exhibited activation of carcinogenic pathways, including E2F targets, G2M checkpoint, MYC, and oxidative phosphorylation (Additional file 2: Fig. S8N), suggesting the deletion of 15q14 may be associated with tumor growth-related transcriptional programs.

Genetic evolution partly constrains transcriptional heterogeneity in TNBC

The extent to which transcriptional heterogeneity reflects underlying genetic lineage versus phenotypic plasticity remains incompletely understood in TNBC. To address this question, we first characterized expression heterogeneity across and within tumors using RNA-seq data from 166 cell clusters across 14 tumors with more than 5 cell clusters from 7 TNBC patients (mean: 12 clusters per tumor; range: 5–25), in which we had obtained high-quality RNA-seq data. After excluding lowly expressed genes, 12,037 genes were grouped into four expression heterogeneity groups based on their expression variability within and between TNBC tumors (729 genes in group1, 2,091 genes in group2, 5,278 genes in group3, 3,939 genes in group4) (Additional file 2: Fig. S10A, B). This analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity at the gene-expression level, with some gene groups showing relatively stable high expression, whereas others displayed marked inter- and intra-tumor variability.

To determine whether, and to what extent, the observed heterogeneity is structured by genetic lineage during tumor evolution, we examined gene-level phylogenetic signals across 75 cell clusters from 6 TNBC patients with matched DNA-RNA data (range, 7–20 cell clusters per patient), tracing their variation along tumor evolutionary history. Across individual patients, 1,059–8,065 genes mirrored genetic evolutionary patterns, exhibiting significant phylogenetic signals (Pagel’s λ statistic, p < 0.05) in TNBC patients (Additional file 2: Fig. S10G), indicating that the gene expression similarity between cell clusters was strongly correlated with underlying genetic evolution, in contrast to colorectal cancer, in which only a limited number of genes exhibit such phylogenetic signals [16]. Gene set enrichment analysis further revealed that genes with phylogenetic signals were significantly enriched in Hallmark pathways, including interferon gamma response signatures, mTORC1 signaling, MYC targets and oxidative phosphorylation pathways, highlighting the important role of immune and cell growth signatures along tumor genetic evolution (Additional file 2: Fig. S10H).

Phylogenetic signal analysis identified 21 pathways recurrently enriched across at least three patients, of which 38% were linked to immune signaling and 29% to oncogenic pathways (Fig. 6C and Additional file 2: Fig. S10I). The number of pathways exhibiting phylogenetic signal was independent of both the number of cell clusters and tumor purity (Additional file 2: Fig. S10J). Along evolutionary trajectories of tumors, oncogenic signaling pathways were upregulated, while immune-related signaling pathways were downregulated (Additional file 2: Fig. S10K). For instance, in patient P03, oncogenic pathways predominated in metastatic clusters compared to primary tumors. In contrast, patient P04 displayed immune-related pathways with reduced expression in metastases (Additional file 2: Fig. S10L). Together, these findings indicate that part of the observed transcriptional heterogeneity is not random but reflects genetically structured tumor progression.

To further quantify genotype–phenotype coupling, we compared the concordance between mutation-based phylogenetic trees and expression-based dendrograms across patients. Six TNBC patients were stratified into 2 groups based on the correlation between the somatic alterations-based phylogenetic trees and the expression-based dendrograms (Fig. 6D and Additional file 2: Fig. S11A). The low_corr group (P03, P04, P05, P09) exhibited weak alignment between genetic and transcriptional profiles, while the high_corr group demonstrated strong concordance, supported by gene-level analyses (Additional file 2: Fig. S11B). GSEA revealed enrichment of E2F targets, G2M checkpoint and MYC targets signaling in the high_corr group, while the low_corr group was enriched for EMT and immune-related signaling (Fig. 6E). The low_corr group also exhibited increased invasive potential (Additional file 2: Fig. S11C), elevated expression of cytokines and chemokines (Additional file 2: Fig. S11D), higher ImmuneScore, StromalScore, CytotoxicScore (Additional file 2: Fig. S11E, F), and greater immune infiltration (Additional file 2: Fig. S11G), suggesting that transcriptional variation in these tumors may be more strongly influenced by microenvironmental context than by genetic ancestry. Notably, higher expression of low_corr signature genes was associated with poorer clinical outcomes in the METABRIC TNBC cohort (Fig. 6F, G and Additional file 2: Fig. S11H-J, Additional file 1: Table S15).

Finally, we sought to determine whether phenotypic traits remain stable during lymph node metastasis. To this end, we defined a genetic time cutoff (Tpm) based on the scaled genetic time of evolutionarily proximate paired primary-metastasis clusters (P-M pairs, Methods). We analyzed the hallmark pathway similarity between paired P-M clusters, as well as the similarity within primary tumor clusters (P clusters from P-M pairs versus other primary tumor clusters, P-P pairs) and within metastatic clusters (M clusters from P-M pairs versus other metastatic clusters, M-M pairs), both within and beyond the Tpm (Fig. 6H, Methods). Within the Tpm, hallmark pathways similarity between paired P-M clusters was comparable to that observed within primary and metastatic tumor clusters, with no statistically significant differences (p = 0.2 and p = 0.62, respectively). However, beyond the Tpm, hallmark pathway similarity within primary tumor clusters was significantly reduced (p = 0.031, Fig. 6I). A similar trend was observed within metastatic clusters, although it did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.41, Fig. 6H). For instance, in patient P03, the primary tumor cell cluster, BC_9, which is likely the genetic origin of the lymph node metastasis, exhibits a phenotype similar to that of the metastasis cell cluster. After the metastasis colonizes, subsequent clonal evolution leads to the gradual loss of certain phenotypes (Fig. 6I). These findings suggest that during lymph node metastasis, metastatic cells initially retain transcriptional programmes similar to those of their genetically related primary tumor counterparts, followed by gradual diversification as metastatic colonisation proceeds.

In summary, our findings provide evidence that expression patterns mirror genetic history in TNBC progression. While heritable genetic alterations shape core transcriptional programs in some patients, others with weaker alignment between genetic changes and gene expression exhibit greater immune infiltration, enhanced invasive potential, and poorer clinical outcomes. Furthermore, our analyses reveal that during lymph node metastasis, metastatic clones preserve key pathways from their primary tumor of origin, with diversification occurring during clonal colonization and expansion. These findings highlight the complex dynamics of tumor evolution and underscore the importance of integrating genetic and transcriptional data to identify actionable therapeutic targets.

Discussion

The mechanisms by which genetic variations and expression changes influence tumor clonal evolution, and how these processes contribute to tumor pathological and molecular heterogeneity, remain incompletely understood. In this study, we developed pGeo-G&T-seq, a pathology-guided spatial multi-omics framework that enables joint genomic and transcriptomic profiling of spatially defined tumor cell clusters within intact tissue architecture. Applying this approach to paired primary and metastatic breast cancer specimens allowed us to resolve genomic heterogeneity, transcriptional plasticity, and clonal relationships in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) at spatially localized resolution. Collectively, our findings suggest that TNBC progression involves spatially organized molecular heterogeneity, that genotype and phenotype remain coupled in a subset of tumors, and that phenotypic programs can be partially retained during metastatic dissemination.

From a technical perspective, previous single-cell genomic studies have provided important insight into clonal evolution in TNBC, but often without spatial resolution. Compared with BaSISS, GeoMx and Visium, pGeo-G&T-seq uniquely combines pathology-guided microdissection with parallel genome-wide DNA and RNA profiling from the same spatially defined tissue fragment, enabling direct integration of clonal genotypes with transcriptional states. This provides spatial precision and analytical depth that are complementary to, rather than overlapping with, existing spatial multi-omic platforms. A practical limitation is that a subset of microdissected regions yielded transcriptomes without matched DNA profiles because of differential DNA/RNA recovery and library quality. Accordingly, all integrative analyses were restricted to clusters with paired DNA/RNA data. Additionally, although pGeo-G&T-seq does not provide single-cell resolution, it enables examination of spatial dynamics of tumor evolution. Despite these limitations, the approach was sufficient to reveal distinct spatial organizations of tumor heterogeneity, including both decay-like and spatially uniform molecular patterns across cases. These differences appear to be influenced by the tumor microenvironment, as evidenced by our findings in involved-lymph nodes, where the relatively uniform microenvironment was associated with spatial uniformity in molecular features. This suggests that microenvironmental constraints may contribute to spatial patterns of tumor heterogeneity. Furthermore, our approach allowed us to observe co-localization of driver genes and asynchronous mutations within anatomically proximate regions, highlighting that histologically similar tumor areas can harbour distinct genetic alterations and transcriptional states, thereby underscoring the complex interplay between genetic and microenvironmental factors.

Our findings also confirm the presence of molecular subtype mosaicism in TNBC and identify a previously underappreciated OXPHOS-associated tumor cell state, supported across our discovery cohort and independent bulk, single-cell, and spatial transcriptomic datasets. This state was associated with poor prognosis and, in single-cell immunotherapy cohorts, associated with features of reduced treatment-associated immune expansion. These observations are consistent with prior studies showing that residual TNBC cells surviving chemotherapy can upregulate mitochondrial OXPHOS, thereby gaining a survival advantage, whereas pharmacologic inhibition of this adaptive mechanism delays tumor regrowth [91]. In parallel, longitudinal studies in the neoadjuvant setting support the emergence of chemotherapy-induced, reversible drug-tolerant states accompanied by adaptive reprogramming [92]. In addition, single-cell profiling of breast cancer metastasis has shown that micrometastatic cells upregulate OXPHOS during metastatic seeding [93]. Together, these findings suggest that OXPHOS-related biology marks an aggressive metabolic program associated with tumor progression and adverse clinical outcome in TNBC. However, although some single-cell analyses suggested a link with limited immunotherapy responsiveness, bulk immunotherapy cohorts did not show statistically supported associations with treatment response. This discrepancy indicates that bulk transcriptomes may not adequately capture rare but clinically relevant tumor cell states, highlighting the value of integrative stratification frameworks incorporating single-cell and spatial information.

The spatial evolutionary trajectory was reconstructed using both somatic point mutations, genome-wide copy number profiles [27, 84] and the geographic spatial location of cell clusters to further characterize the spatial growth pattern and the metastatic route in TNBC. Spatially contiguous growth emerged as a dominant feature of TNBC. The origin of lymph node metastases is predominantly polyclonal, in which cell clusters of genetic early-origin are in a quiescent state with low proliferative capacity and high hypoxia, which may warrant further investigation in the context of tumor recurrence and drug resistance. Genomic instability increased more pronouncedly compared with TMB and TNB during tumor evolution, indicating that focal-level SCNAs might contribute to TNBC evolution and metastatic progression.

By leveraging the framework proposed by Jacob Househam et al. [16], we examined the genetic determinants of gene expression and found that, in TNBC, many transcriptional programs are closely aligned with genetic ancestry. This pattern contrasts with colorectal cancer, in which gene expression was reported to be less tightly constrained by genotype [16]. The discrepancy may be due to different methods used for phylogenetic tree construction. Previous studies have showed that TNBCs characterized by extensive SCNAs but relatively limited SNVs [73, 94–96], and work from Navin and others has highlighted the importance of copy number evolution in TNBC progression [17, 84, 97, 98]. Accordingly, we employed both SNV and genome-wide copy number profiles data to construct the phylogenetic tree. Our analyses suggest that copy number alterations likely contribute to the robust phylogenetic signal observed in TNBC, reflecting the tumor's complex genetic evolution. In contrast, the CRC phylogenetic tree was constructed based on SNV data, which may explain why Jacob Househam et al. found less correlation between genetic ancestry and gene expression in CRC [16]. Consistent with Jacob Househam et al. [16], our findings suggest that the strong correlation between genetic ancestry and gene expression in tumors may indicate an "equilibrium " state of evolution, potentially associated with altered immune surveillance. High proliferative demand may be a key feature of these tumors, as both their study and ours have observed that the MYC pathway, essential for tumor growth, shows a strong phylogenetic signal. In contrast, tumors exhibiting weaker coupling between genetic ancestry and gene expression showed significantly higher immune infiltration, enhanced invasiveness, and poorer clinical outcomes, suggesting that these tumors may have adapted to evade immune surveillance and foster metastasis, despite being less driven by genetic mutations. Together, these observations suggest distinct therapeutic strategies for TNBC, where tumors with strong genotype–phenotype coupling may benefit from targeting genetic pathways, while tumors with weaker coupling may require therapies focused on microenvironmental interactions and transcriptional plasticity.

Notably, our study design enabled assessment of phenotypic continuity across metastatic dissemination by integrating DNA-based phylogenetic relationships with transcriptomic profiles. Evolutionarily close paired primary-metastasis (P-M) clusters shared similar core hallmark pathway activities, whereas phenotypic divergence increased with evolutionary distance. These findings suggest that phenotypic similarity is constrained by clonal relatedness and support partial retention of phenotypic programs during early metastatic spread, followed by later diversification. This interpretation aligns with a strong founder effect reported by Kuett et al., who showed that tumor cell phenotypes are largely shared between primary and metastatic sites despite shifts in relative abundance [99]. However, our findings do not exclude the contribution of convergent adaptation or microenvironment-driven constraints. Together, these results support a model in which clonal continuity may constrains phenotypic space during dissemination, while environmental adaptation further modulates tumor phenotypes at metastatic sites.

Conclusions

In summary, our study establishes pGeo-G&T-seq as a robust platform for investigating the spatial and molecular complexity of TNBC. By integrating genome, transcriptome, and spatial pathology data, we demonstrate distinct spatial patterns of heterogeneity, identify an OXPHOS-associated transcriptional program with clinical relevance, and unveil the role of genetic context in shaping transcriptional stability and divergence during TNBC metastatic evolution. The insights gained from pGeo-G&T-seq provide a framework for future studies that consider both dominant molecular subtypes and rare, spatially distributed tumor cell states. Beyond TNBC, this approach holds potential for advancing our understanding of spatial genotype–phenotype dynamics in other cancers, supporting future efforts to develop spatially informed approaches to tumor stratification.
