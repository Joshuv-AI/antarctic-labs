1.0 Summary
 Land surface types that are common in the Southeast United States (e.g., forests, agriculture, urban) can
 play key roles in the regulation of surface-atmosphere energy exchange and boundary-layer evolution in
 the region (Hinkle et al. 2024). Heterogeneity in these land-surface types and associated emissivities can
 drive strong transitions in land surface temperature (LST), which is an important component of the
 surface energy balance. Within the forested canopy surrounding the U.S. Department of Energy
 Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) User Facility’s field deployment at the Bankhead National
 Forest (BNF) in Alabama main site (M1) (Kuang et al. 2026), the different components of the vegetation
 and ground surface can create strong gradients in surface temperature that can influence the canopy
 thermal environment and vegetative function (e.g., stress, transpiration), both of which can have
 important, yet poorly characterized, controls on lower atmospheric processes (e.g., thermal turbulence,
 secondary circulations). To address this knowledge gap, we proposed to characterize gradients in LST at
 the BNF main site, across heterogeneous land-surface types, including:
  • the canopy vegetation (e.g., leaves, stems, branches) surrounding the M1 instrument field
  • the under-canopy ground surface (e.g., soil, litter layer) surrounding the M1 instrument field
  • the cleared area (e.g., soil, gravel) within the M1 instrument field
  • the canopy vegetation in the area between the S10 main tower and M1 instrument field.

 To obtain these measurements across these spatial scales, we deployed a combined infrared and visual
 camera system on the ARM tethered balloon system (TBS) (Dexheimer et al. 2024) at the M1 site,
 providing observational coverage of the land surface from different altitudes within and immediately
 surrounding the M1 instrument field, and extending outwards to the S10 main tower (Figure 1).

These measurements occurred during the BNF-4 intensive operational period (IOP) in August 2025 using
a baseline midwave infrared (MWIR) camera system in two configurations: (1) Mirage MWIR 640P +
SONY R10C camera (Figure 2a), or (2) Mirage MWIR 640P + GoPro camera (Figure 2b), previously
deployed by the TBS team (Amerson et al. 2023, 2025).

Figure 2. a.) The Mirage MWIR 640P (silver housing) and SONY R10C (black housing) cameras. b.)
 The Mirage MWIR 640P (silver housing) and GoPro (black housing) cameras.

 The camera system was deployed on the TBS during select periods to capture diurnal variability in
 temperature (e.g., morning, afternoon, evening transitions) and variability in cloud-cover (e.g., clear sky,
 broken clouds, overcast). An example of a sampling period with broken clouds and the resulting shading
 heterogeneity on the land-surface (e.g., forest canopy, clearing) is shown in Figure 3.

 Figure 3. The TBS is profiling above the M1 instrument field, where (left) the GoPro camera is looking
 down at the cloud-shadowed surface, and (right) the surface all-sky imager (ASI) is looking
 up and capturing the TBS (red arrow) and the cloud-blocked sun in its field of view.

 TBS loiters were performed at specific altitudes to capture fields of nview that included the cleared M1
 instrument field, forested areas immediately surrounding the M1 instrument field, and areas extending
 outwards to the S10 main tower. At each TBS loiter altitude, the Mirage 640P provided thermal images of
 surface temperature and the SONY R10C (or GoPro) camera provided high-quality visual (RGB) images
 to assist in identification of surface physical features associated with the surface thermal measurements.

 Several operational issues were also encountered and remedied during the deployment of the camera
 system associated with: (1) finding an appropriate reference “surface” for thermal calibration of the
 MWIR camera, (2) mitigating the twisting and tangling of the camera system cables during gimbal
 panning, and (3) establishing robust procedures for finding “reference points” (e.g., the S10 Main Tower,
 the Guest Instrument Facility), for consistent “downward pans” and “upward pans” between the horizon
 and the M1 instrument field.

2.2 Further Research and Data Processing Opportunities
 • Compare MWIR-measured surface temperatures with baseline surface infrared thermometers, S10
 main tower distributed temperature sensing (DTS) systems, and S10 main tower meteorology
 systems.
 • Compile statistics/histograms of thermal variability per land-surface area (e.g., ± 3 ℃ per 1 km2) and
 classify them by atmospheric conditions (e.g., air temperature, humidity, cloud cover). Sky cloud
 cover conditions can be obtained from the all-sky imager (ASI), which can also yield time-lapsed
 comparisons between synchronized TBS-thermal images and surface ASI images (similar to
 Figure 3).
 • Assess the impacts of cloud properties (e.g., fraction and altitude), time of day, temperature, and
 relative humidity on differences in temperature profiles between the S10 and M1 measurement sites.
 • Evaluate infrared imagery collected from the TBS to learn the relationships between land-cover type
 and: (1) vertical temperature profiles between the S10 and M1 sites, and (2) surface temperature
 gradients between the S10 and M1 sites.
