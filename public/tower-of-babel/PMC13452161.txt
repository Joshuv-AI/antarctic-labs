Metagenomic analysis of commensal small mammal samples from an international cargo shipping area, Bangkok Port, reveals potential zoonotic pathogens and implications for One Health surveillance
Authors: Dararat Sirimongkol, Peerawat Wongluechai, Tatiyanuch Chamsai, Thekhawet Weluwanarak, Narinthip Chaipromkhieo, Nareerat Sangkachai, Kanittha Tonchiangsai, Choenkwan Pabutta, Pornchai Kerdsiri, Ladawan Sariya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Commensal small mammals, such as rats and shrews, are recognized reservoirs for numerous zoonotic pathogens; however, their role in pathogen circulation at transport hubs remains underexplored. This study employs shotgun metagenomic sequencing to characterize microbial communities and assess zoonotic potential in tissue samples from commensal small mammals captured at Bangkok Port, an international cargo shipping hub in Thailand between June and August 2025. Following host read depletion, taxonomic profiling was performed to identify bacterial taxa of public health relevance, including sequences assigned to Bordetella spp., Yersinia pestis, Bartonella elizabethae, and Acinetobacter baumannii. The virulence factor and antibiotic resistance gene profiles revealed that some of these pathogens have pathogenic potential and are related to drug-resistant bacteria. In addition, Y. pestis was identified as a shared taxon among rats, shrews, and their associated fleas. The findings support the ecological roles of mammalian hosts and their ectoparasites as reservoirs for pathogens of public health concern. These results emphasize the need to strengthen surveillance programs for commensal small mammals to monitor and mitigate the spread of transboundary pathogens at international maritime gateways.

Introduction

International cargo ports serve as critical nodes in global maritime trade by facilitating the large-scale transboundary movement of goods, raw materials, and industrial products. Bangkok Port (Khlong Toei Port), located along the Chao Phraya River in central Bangkok, functions as one of Thailand's principal commercial gateways and is recognized as one of the world's busiest river-based container terminals. Its strategic location enables efficient connectivity to land-based logistics systems, including trucking and rail networks, thereby supporting rapid domestic cargo distribution and extensive international trade. The port handles a broad array of commodities, including consumer goods and industrial machinery, and operates across densely utilized warehouses, storage yards, and docking areas (Port Authority of Thailand, https://bkp.port.co.th/cs/internet/bkp/index.html, accessed December 7, 2025). These high-traffic operations create complex ecological conditions that may facilitate the introduction, persistence, and dissemination of zoonotic and other transboundary pathogens at the human–animal–environment interface. In response to these potential health risks, the International Disease Control, Ports and Quarantine Unit at Bangkok Port has conducted ongoing surveillance, including routine commensal small mammal trapping. In 2024, 313 traps were deployed, resulting in the capture of 65 rats and shrews (trap success: 21%). In 2025, 200 traps yielded 54 captures (trap success: 27%). These findings indicate a persistent and stable presence of commensal small mammals within the port environment. As global trade volume continues to increase, the likelihood that infected commensal small mammals, or the pathogens they harbor, may be introduced or disseminated across national borders through cargo movement also increases. This risk underscores the importance of robust national and international regulatory frameworks, including the Animal Epidemics Act B.E. 2558 (2015), which mandates disease surveillance at international points of entry to support the prevention and control of transboundary animal and zoonotic diseases [1].

Commensal small mammals, particularly rats and shrews, serve as reservoirs for diverse zoonotic pathogens, including Leptospira sp., Yersinia pestis, Hantaviruses, Rabies virus, and Rickettsia [2], [3], [4], [5]. Traditional pathogen surveillance approaches, such as culture-based diagnostics and targeted polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays, often lack the requisite sensitivity or breadth to detect unexpected, diverse, or emerging microorganisms. In contrast, shotgun metagenomic sequencing offers a robust framework for characterizing the total microbial landscape, including zoonotic pathogens and antimicrobial resistance determinants [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]. This approach is particularly valuable in complex environments, such as cargo ports, where routine operations can facilitate the mixing and dissemination of microbial communities across wildlife, humans, and globally transported goods. In this study, shotgun metagenomic sequencing was applied to characterize the microbial composition of tissue samples collected from rats and shrews at Bangkok Port to comprehensively profile microbial diversity and identify potential zoonotic pathogens and antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs). It was hypothesized that commensal small mammals residing in the Bangkok Port cargo shipping area act as reservoirs for a wide range of microorganisms, including potential zoonotic pathogens and ARGs, and that shotgun metagenomic sequencing can offer a comprehensive method for their detection to support One Health surveillance. Strengthening surveillance of rodent populations in international port environments provides essential data for biosecurity frameworks, enabling the early detection of infectious diseases and mitigating the transboundary dissemination of pathogens at strategic points of entry.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and nucleic acid extraction

Live trapping of rats and shrews was conducted as part of an observational cross-sectional study (June–August 2025) across eight warehouse areas within the international cargo vessel berth of Bangkok Port, Khlong Toei District, Bangkok, Thailand (Fig. 1). All animal trapping procedures and biosafety protocols were reviewed and approved by the Committee for Animal Care and Use (protocol no. MUVS-2025-04-22) and were conducted in accordance with the Ethical Principles and Guidelines for the Use of Animals of the National Research Council of Thailand. Animals were euthanized by an overdose of inhaled isoflurane following capture, and ectoparasites were collected by inspecting the containers for detached specimens and by brushing, combing, and gently tapping each animal over a water-filled white tray. Fleas were collected using fine-tipped forceps, following the methods outlined by Changbunjong et al. (2010) [11] and Chotelersak et al. (2015) [12].Fig. 1Location of live trapping within the international cargo vessel berth of Bangkok Port. Yellow star symbols indicate the eight warehouse areas where live trapping was conducted.Fig. 1

Location of live trapping within the international cargo vessel berth of Bangkok Port. Yellow star symbols indicate the eight warehouse areas where live trapping was conducted.

A total of 54 small mammals underwent flea collection, after which they were subsequently identified to species based on external morphological characteristics according to the taxonomic keys published by Francis in 2008 [13]. Forty-eight individuals were identified to the species level and selected for metagenomic sequencing. Ectoparasites were preserved in 95% ethanol and identified under a stereomicroscope, without clearing or permanent slide mounting, using the identification keys of Lewis (1993) [14]. The specimens were identified as the oriental rat flea, Xenopsylla cheopis, and preserved in 95% ethanol before being pooled for nucleic acid extraction using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit, as per the manufacturer's instructions from QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany.

Following euthanasia, tissue samples from commensal small mammals, including brain, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and urinary bladder, were aseptically collected. Genomic DNA and total RNA were extracted from each tissue sample using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit and the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), respectively, following the manufacturer's guidelines. Total RNA was subsequently converted to complementary DNA (cDNA) using random hexamer primers and the SuperScript™ III First-Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen, CA, USA). All extracted nucleic acid and cDNA samples were stored at −80 °C until further processing.

Metagenome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

DNA and cDNA extracted from tissue samples of 48 individual small mammals, representing four species, were pooled at the species level into five samples: R. rattus (n = 37, subdivided into two pools, R. rattus_1 [n = 20] and R. rattus_2 [n = 17]), R. exulans (n = 3), R. norvegicus (n = 2), and Suncus murinus (n = 6). The DNA extracted from the host-associated fleas (n = 9) was pooled into a single sample and grouped as vector group for metagenomic analysis. All samples were submitted to Vishuo Biomedical (Thailand) Ltd. for metagenome sequencing. DNA libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform using 150 bp paired-end reads, generating approximately 20 Gb of data per library. Raw image data were processed using Bcl2fastq (v2.17.1.14) for base calling and initial quality assessment [15]. Adapter trimming and quality filtering were performed using Cutadapt (v1.9.1) to obtain high-quality reads. Host-derived sequences were removed using BWA (v0.7.12) by mapping reads against reference genomes of closely related host species for rat and shrew samples (GCF_036323735.1, GCF_024139225.1, and GCF_027595985.1) and against the X. cheopis reference genome (GCA_049309425.1) for flea samples [16]. Subsequently, quality-filtered, host-depleted reads were assembled de novo using MEGAHIT (v1.2.9) [17]. The protein-coding genes were predicted from the assembled contigs using Prodigal (v3.02) [18]. Redundant gene sequences were removed using MMseqs2 with unigenes clustered at 95% sequence identity and 95% coverage [19]. Finally, pre-processed reads were aligned to the nonredundant unigene set using SOAPaligner (v2.21) to generate gene abundance profiles for downstream analyses, such as microbial species annotation, gene functional annotation, and alpha diversity analysis [20].

Based on the predicted protein sequences of the coding genes, sequence similarity searches were performed using the BLASTp algorithm implemented in the DIAMOND software package (version v0.8.15.77) against multiple reference databases, including the NCBI nonredundant protein (NR), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database [21]. To characterize the microbial composition of the samples, the unigene sequences were queried against the NR database using DIAMOND. Subsequently, species annotations were derived from the corresponding taxonomic annotation information in the NR database. Alpha (α) diversity analyses were conducted at the species level using multiple statistical indices to estimate the species abundance (ACE and Chao1) and diversity (Shannon and Simpson).

Results

Metagenomic analysis and sequence assembly

Six metagenomic libraries were constructed based on species grouping, comprising four libraries from pooled rat samples, one library from pooled shrew samples, and one library from pooled flea samples. Initial paired-end sequencing yielded between 1.3 × 108 and 1.7 × 108 reads per sample. Following quality control and the removal of host-derived reads, between 8.2 × 105 and 3.5 × 106 reads per sample were retained for assembly, resulting in 4.5 × 103 to 3.4 × 104 scaffolds per pooled sample (Table S1). After gene prediction and redundancy reduction, a representative set of unigenes was obtained from each sample to facilitate downstream metagenomic profiling.

Microbial community composition

Overall, the microbial composition across pooled rat, shrew, and flea samples was predominantly bacterial, with lower proportions of viruses, eukaryotes, and archaea (Fig. 2). For bacterial composition, focusing on taxa of known or potential relevance to human health, the most abundant microbial genera detected in rat and shrew samples were Bordetella (B. pertussis), Chlamydia (C. trachomatis), Bacillus (B. cereus), Streptococcus (S. pyogenes), and Yersinia (Y. pestis). In contrast, the flea sample predominantly harbored Bartonella (B. elizabethae, B. kosoyi, B. queenslandensis) and Acinetobacter (A. baumannii) (Fig. 3A and B). Venn diagram analysis revealed overlapping microbial communities among rats, shrews, and fleas at the genus level, including Klebsiella, Salmonella, unclassified Candidatus Omnitrophota, Yersinia, Rhodococcus, Staphylococcus, Enterobacter, Escherichia, unclassified Gammaproteobacteria, Clostridioides, Streptococcus, and unclassified Alphaproteobacteria. At the species level, the shared taxa included K. pneumoniae, Y. pestis, Gammaproteobacteria bacterium, E. coli, S. aureus, S. enterica, C. difficile, unclassified Rhodococcus, and Candidatus Omnitrophota bacterium (Fig. 3C).Fig. 2Microbial composition of each pooled sample group.Fig. 2Fig. 3Analysis of the microbial communities in each sample group. The viral fraction was markedly higher in shrews (37.64%) than in rat (0.2–0.8%) and flea samples (0.05%) (Fig. 2). The viruses detected in shrew and rat samples were predominantly retroviruses (Gammaretrovirus and Betaretrovirus) and bacteriophages. In contrast, the flea samples were characterized mainly by insect-infecting viruses (Abisko virus and Bracoviriform facetosae) and phages (Caudoviricetes).

Alpha diversity index analysis

Rat and shrew samples were categorized as host samples, whereas the flea sample was grouped as a vector sample to descriptively evaluate microbial diversity using alpha diversity index analyses. The estimated microbial richness (ACE and Chao1) in host samples exhibited two distinct ranges. In S. murinus and the two R. rattus samples lower richness values were noted, (ACE: 37.10–53.68; Chao1: 34.67–53.50), whereas higher richness values were observed in R. norvegicus and R. exulans (ACE: 73.54–77.20; Chao1: 73.00–75.46) (Table 1). The Shannon diversity index of the host samples spanned from 2.408 to 3.445, whereas Simpson's diversity index ranged from 0.680 to 0.856. In contrast, the X. cheopis vector sample showed significantly higher estimated microbial richness (ACE = 874.10; Chao1 = 875.03) than the host samples and had a Shannon diversity index of 3.731, whereas its Simpson diversity index (0.793) fell within the range observed for the host samples. Good's coverage values exceeded 0.99 for all samples, confirming that the sequencing depth was sufficient to capture most of the microbial diversity present in each metagenomic library. Functional profiling of microbial communities based on KEGG pathways

KEGG functional analysis revealed host-specific patterns associated with potential pathogen transmission. The S. murinus samples exhibited enrichment of pathways related to human infectious diseases caused by parasitic, viral, and bacterial pathogens, indicating a potential role in zoonotic disease transmission. Similarly, R. norvegicus exhibited an increased abundance of pathways associated with infectious bacterial diseases and antimicrobial resistance. In contrast, R. rattus and R. exulans exhibited limited representation of infectious disease-related pathways, indicating a comparatively lower potential involvement in pathogen transmission. Antibiotic resistance gene profiles

Shotgun metagenomic analysis revealed a higher diversity of ARGs in shrew and flea samples than in rat samples (Fig. 5). Among the ARGs detected in the shrew samples, only the elongation factor Tu (EF-Tu) gene associated with resistance to elfamycin antibiotics exhibited high sequence similarity (82% identity) (Table S2). In contrast, flea samples harbored acquired resistance genes, tetracycline resistance genes (tet(32), tet(O/32/O), tet(O/W/O)), and multidrug efflux pumps (adeJ), with sequence identities ranging from 83% to 100%. Several housekeeping genes associated with antibiotic resistance through point mutations were identified, including EF-Tu variants, fusA, gyrA, gyrB, parC, rpoB, rpoC, rpsJ, and rsmA, with sequence identities ranging from 81% to 95%. In rat samples, β-lactamase genes belonging to the TEM family (TEM-116, TEM-162, and TEM-229), the multidrug efflux pump gene mdtF, and the mutation-associated resistance gene rpoB were detected with high confidence, exhibiting identity values ranging from 80% to 100% (Table S2).Fig. 5Visualization of antimicrobial resistance gene profiles based on relative abundance in each sample.Fig. 5

Virulence factor gene profiles

Virulence factor database analysis identified virulence factor (VF)-related genes exclusively in R. norvegicus and X. cheopis samples. For VF detection, only gene matches with sequence identity greater than 95% were considered. In the R. norvegicus sample, a limited number of VF genes were identified, including fimG and fimC, which encode fimbrial-like adhesin proteins involved in bacterial adhesion, and ibeB, which is associated with host cell invasion (Table S3). In addition, cyaA, which encodes a bifunctional hemolysin–adenylate cyclase toxin and is a key VF involved in host immune modulation in Bordetella species, was detected in the R. norvegicus sample. This result is consistent with the high abundance of Bordetella identified in the microbial taxonomic analysis (Fig. 3A). In contrast, the X. cheopis sample contained several VF categories, including adhesion (omp89), biofilm formation (bap, pgaC), immune evasion mechanisms (lpxL), host cell invasion (ialB), iron acquisition (bauD, basF, BJAB0715_01029, hbpC), type IV secretion system (virB, trw), toxin (plcD), and virulence-associated function (ABBFA_003461, ABZJ_00090, ABZJ_00094, vapA3, vapA5). These VF genes were primarily associated with Bartonella and Acinetobacter (Table S3 and Fig. 3A).

Discussion

This study used shotgun metagenomic sequencing to characterize microbial communities and functional profiles in commensal small mammal and flea samples collected at Bangkok Port, Thailand, an international cargo port. Potential human pathogens, including C. trachomatis, B. pertussis, and Y. pestis, were found in rat and shrew samples, whereas Bartonella (B. elizabethae, B. kosoyi, and B. queenslandensis) and A. baumannii were identified in flea samples. However, C. trachomatis is a strictly human pathogen that is closely related to C. muridarum [22]. C. muridarum is a natural rodent pathogen; thus, the detection of C. trachomatis in rat samples may reflect taxonomic assignment limitations rather than true host adaptation [23]. Similarly, B. pertussis infects only humans, has no known animal reservoir, and does not persist in the natural environment [24]. The sequences detected may represent closely related rodent-associated B. pertussis species. For instance, B. bronchiseptica can colonize a broad range of mammalian hosts, including rodents, and cause respiratory infections in humans, particularly in immunocompromised individuals [25]. The detection of Bordetella and the virulence gene cyaA in this study indicates the presence of bacteria with potential pathogenicity in commensal small mammals. However, the presence of B. bronchiseptica in the samples should be further confirmed using PCR with specific primers.

In the flea sample, zoonotic potential, including Bartonella (B. elizabethae, B. kosoyi, B. queenslandensis) and A. baumannii, was detected. B. elizabethae has been detected in rats and dogs, and it can cause endocarditis or myocarditis in humans [26]. Detection of VF-related genes in the sample, including type IV secretion system components and adhesion mechanisms, indicated the pathogenic potential of Bartonella. In contrast, B. kosoyi and B. queenslandensis exhibit close phylogenetic relationships to B. elizabethae, suggesting a potential risk of zoonotic spillover, although confirmed human infection data remain limited [27], [28]. Additionally, A. baumannii was identified in the flea sample and is recognized as a major cause of nosocomial infections, with high mortality rates among critically ill patients and immunocompromised individuals [29], [30]. Several VFs associated with A. baumannii, including genes related to adhesion, biofilm formation, iron acquisition, lipopolysaccharide synthesis, and immune evasion, were identified. The ARG profile revealed important resistance determinants in A. baumannii, particularly those associated with antibiotic efflux, target alteration, and target protection. These findings underscore the potential role of fleas as reservoirs of pathogenic and drug-resistant bacteria, which may pose zoonotic and public health risks.

Furthermore, Y. pestis DNA was detected in rat, shrew, and flea samples, indicating ecological connectivity among hosts and vectors and highlighting the importance of considering both groups in zoonotic surveillance. Y. pestis is a well-established rodent-associated pathogen and causative agent of plague. This pathogen is typically transmitted to humans through the bite of infected fleas [31], [32]. However, no associated VFs were identified in any of the samples. The absence of virulence genes may reflect low bacterial abundance in the samples. Consequently, the pathogenic potential of the detected Y. pestis should be confirmed by the targeted detection of key virulence-associated plasmids (pMT1 and pPCP1) using species-specific PCR.

Limitations

This study provides a preliminary characterization of bacterial communities in rats, shrews, and fleas. The detection of several shared bacterial taxa between small mammal host samples and flea sample indicates potential ecological links between reservoir hosts and vectors. However, the limited sample size constrains robust statistical inference. Future research should involve larger sample sizes to understand the epidemiological significance of these results.

Conclusions

Metagenomic sequencing of small mammals and their associated fleas found at a cargo port in Thailand uncovered diverse microbial communities, including sequences linked to zoonotic pathogens, virulence factors, and antimicrobial resistance. The detection of antimicrobial resistance-associated genes in both small mammals and fleas, along with the identification of Y. pestis, sequences, highlights the usefulness of shotgun metagenomics for the simultaneous characterization of a broad spectrum of microorganisms and genetic determinants that impact public and animal health simultaneously. Furthermore, the findings suggest that mammalian hosts and their ectoparasites offer complementary information on pathogen diversity, thereby supporting integrated surveillance strategies that incorporate hosts and vectors at points of entry. This study provides baseline data on microbial communities linked to commensal small mammals and fleas at an international cargo port in Thailand, and highlights the utility of metagenomic sequencing as a tool for comprehensive pathogen surveillance within a One Health framework. Although metagenomic findings require confirmation by targeted diagnostic methods, this approach enables broad, untargeted screening to inform risk assessment and guide surveillance priorities. Future studies incorporating longitudinal sampling, environmental monitoring, and confirmatory molecular approaches will enhance our understanding of temporal changes in microbial communities and boost evidence-based biosecurity and public health surveillance at international ports.
