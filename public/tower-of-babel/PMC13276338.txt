The damaged connectome: Mechanisms and consequences of brain network reorganization following neurotrauma
Authors: Moustafa A Mansour, M Wahid, Mohamed AS El Molla, Mohamed H Helmy, Taha Mohammed Adel, Hamdi Nabawi Mostafa, Mohamed Said Elsanafiry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraumatic brain injury (TBI) represents a major cause of chronic disability worldwide, with outcomes that are notoriously heterogeneous and difficult to predict. This heterogeneity stems from the fact that TBI is not merely a focal injury but a whole-brain network disorder. The brain's structural and functional organization—the connectome—is profoundly disrupted by traumatic forces. However, the post-injury period is not static; it is characterized by a dynamic, multi-stage process of reorganization that unfolds from hours to years. This review synthesizes current evidence on how neurotrauma dismantles brain networks and how the brain subsequently attempts to repair and reconfigure itself. We explore the cellular and systems-level mechanisms of neuroplasticity, the advanced neuroimaging techniques used to measure network changes, and the critical distinction between adaptive and maladaptive reorganization. Finally, we discuss the therapeutic implications of this network-oriented perspective, highlighting neuromodulation, cognitive rehabilitation, and pharmacological interventions as tools to guide recovery, and conclude by outlining key future directions for the field.

Introduction: The brain as a complex network

Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI) is a critical global public health issue, contributing to significant mortality and long-term cognitive, emotional, and physical disability (Maas et al., 2017). For decades, the focus of TBI research and clinical management was on focal lesions—contusions, hematomas, and their mass effect. While crucial, this view fails to fully explain the pervasive and often persistent deficits observed in patients, particularly in those with diffuse injuries. The advent of network neuroscience has provided a transformative paradigm: the brain as an intricate connectome, a complex system of interconnected neural elements (Sporns, 2013).

In this framework, the brain is composed of nodes (distinct brain regions) and edges (the structural or functional connections between them). This architecture can be quantified using graph theory, which has revealed that the healthy brain operates as a "small-world" network—a configuration that optimizes the balance between segregated, specialized processing (high clustering) and integrated, global communication (short path length) (Fig. 1) (Bullmore and Sporns, 2009). Critical to this efficiency are highly connected hubs, such as those within the Default Mode Network (DMN), Salience Network (SN), and Executive Control Network (ECN), which facilitate the routing of information and are metabolically expensive and vulnerable to injury (Crossley et al., 2014).

Small-World Connectome Model of the Human Brain.

This illustration depicts the human brain's connectome, which is structured as a small-world network. Functional modules (green area), composed of neurons (black dots), are densely interconnected internally by short-range links (black lines). These modules are then linked to one another via long-range pathways (navy blue lines) that traverse the brain's white matter (blue area). This architecture supports specialized, parallel processing within modules while also enabling the integration of information across modules to produce complex responses. The 'wiring cost'—the energy needed to maintain connections—is low for short links but high for long ones, which are crucial for overall network efficiency.

Neurotrauma, by its very nature, is a direct assault on this connectome. The primary injury—characterized by diffuse axonal injury (DAI), focal lesions, and metabolic crises—severs structural edges and disrupts the functional synchrony that underlies cognition and behavior. The ensuing neurological and functional outcomes are therefore not solely a product of tissue loss but of network disintegration. However, the brain is not passive. A period of dynamic reorganization follows, encompassing a spectrum of plastic changes from synaptic sprouting to large-scale network shifts.

This review will argue that the functional recovery after TBI is a direct reflection of the efficacy and direction—adaptive or maladaptive—of this protracted reorganization process. We will synthesize evidence from molecular biology, systems neuroscience, and clinical neurology to build a unified model of post-traumatic network plasticity.

Literature search strategy

This is a narrative review, in which we conducted a literature search of PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science from January 2000 to December 2025. Search terms included combinations of: "traumatic brain injury," "TBI," "concussion," "connectome," "network reorganization," "diffusion tensor imaging," "resting-state fMRI," "graph theory," "neuroplasticity," "hyperconnectivity," "neuromodulation," and "cognitive rehabilitation." We prioritized peer-reviewed original research articles, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses in English. Studies were included if they addressed structural or functional brain network changes following TBI in humans or animal models. We excluded case reports, editorials, and studies without a clear connectome or network analysis component. Given the narrative scope, we also drew on select studies from stroke, aging, and other neurological conditions to generate hypotheses, but these are explicitly labeled as such.

The immediate impact: how neurotrauma disrupts brain networks

The initial traumatic event triggers a cascade of pathophysiological processes that directly compromise network integrity. These disruptions can be conceptualized as affecting both the structural scaffolding (the edges) and the dynamic communication (the functional connections) of the brain's networks.

Structural disconnection: the severing of edges

The hallmark of TBI, particularly in its moderate to severe forms, is DAI. DAI results from shearing and tensile forces during acceleration-deceleration, leading to mechanical disruption of axons and subsequent Wallerian degeneration (Johnson et al., 2013). From a network perspective, DAI is the primary mechanism for the widespread weakening or loss of structural edges. Tractography studies using Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) consistently show reduced fractional anisotropy (FA) and increased mean diffusivity (MD) in major white matter tracts like the corpus callosum, cingulum, and superior longitudinal fasciculus, correlating with the severity of injury and cognitive impairment (Fig. 2) (Sharp et al., 2014). This diffuse disconnection undermines the brain's small-world efficiency, increasing the physical path length for information transfer between distant nodes.

Alongside DAI, focal injuries such as contusions and hematomas act by directly destroying critical network nodes. When these nodes are hubs, the impact is disproportionately large. Computational models demonstrate that targeted attacks on high-degree hubs lead to a more rapid and catastrophic breakdown of global network efficiency compared to random node failure (Sharp et al., 2011; Achard et al., 2006). For instance, focal injuries to hub regions like the precuneus (a key DMN node) or the anterior insula (a SN hub) are often associated with severe and persistent deficits in consciousness and cognition.

Acute functional network dysregulation

Concurrent with structural damage, there is an immediate dysregulation of functional networks, typically measured by resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI). The most consistently reported acute change is a profound suppression of functional connectivity (FC), especially within the DMN (Sharp et al., 2011; Achard et al., 2006). The DMN, active during rest and self-referential thought, exhibits high metabolic activity and is exquisitely vulnerable to perturbations. Its acute disruption is thought to be a neural correlate of altered states of consciousness, such as coma.

This initial hypoconnectivity is likely driven by multiple factors: the physical disconnection of axons, a massive release of excitotoxins (e.g., glutamate) that disrupts normal synaptic signaling, and a subsequent metabolic crisis of "spreading depression" that further impairs neuronal function (Giza and Hovda, 2014). The brain's functional networks, in essence, become fragmented and isolated, losing their normal anti-correlated relationships and dynamic flexibility. This acute phase of network failure sets the stage for the subsequent, prolonged period of reorganization, which can follow divergent paths towards recovery or chronic dysfunction.

Heterogeneity of TBI and network outcomes

TBI is not a single entity. Network disruption and reorganization differ substantially across injury severity, time since injury, and patient age (Maas et al., 2017; Crossley et al., 2014). Briefly, mild TBI (concussion) often shows transient hyperconnectivity that normalizes within weeks to months (Sharp et al., 2011; Achard et al., 2006), whereas moderate-severe TBI frequently demonstrates persistent hypoconnectivity and structural disconnection (Johnson et al., 2013; Sharp et al., 2014). In the acute phase (<72 h), functional networks are typically fragmented and hypoconnected (Sharp et al., 2011; Giza and Hovda, 2014). The subacute phase (weeks to months) is characterized by variable hyperconnectivity (Sharp et al., 2011; Achard et al., 2006). In the chronic phase (>6 months), some patients show adaptive small-world reorganization, while others develop maladaptive patterns such as epileptogenic circuits (Bullmore and Sporns, 2009; Sharp et al., 2014). Pediatric patients may show greater plasticity but also longer vulnerability to disruption, while older adults often have reduced reserve and worse outcomes (Maas et al., 2017; Crossley et al., 2014). Throughout this review, we specify severity, timing, and population where the literature permits.

The dynamic response: mechanisms of reorganization

Following the initial period of disruption, the brain enters a prolonged phase of dynamic reorganization (Fig. 3). This process is not monolithic but involves a cascade of plastic changes operating across multiple spatiotemporal scales, from the molecular synthesis of new synapses to the large-scale re-routing of entire functional networks.

Cellular and molecular plasticity: Rebuilding the circuitry

At the microscopic level, the brain attempts to repair and rewire itself through a series of well-orchestrated, though often imperfect, mechanisms (Fig. 3).

1.Axonal Sprouting and Synaptogenesis: In response to DAI, surviving axons undergo reactive synaptogenesis, sprouting new terminals to form connections with denervated postsynaptic sites. This process is governed by activity-dependent molecular cues such as Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) and growth-associated proteins (e.g., GAP-43) (Carmichael et al., 2005). While this can restore some connectivity, the new connections may be inefficient or inappropriate, potentially leading to maladaptive outcomes like hyperexcitability.

2.Synaptic Scaling and Homeostatic Plasticity: To maintain network stability amidst widespread changes in input, neurons engage in homeostatic plasticity. This involves scaling the strength of all synapses on a neuron up or down to stabilize overall firing rates (Turrigiano, 2012). After TBI, a disruption in this homeostatic balance is thought to contribute to network hyper- or hypo-excitability.

3.The Dual Role of Neuroinflammation: Microglial and astrocytic activation is a persistent feature of TBI. Initially, pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α) can exacerbate injury and inhibit plasticity. However, in later phases, a shift towards an anti-inflammatory phenotype (e.g., releasing IL-10, TGF-β) can aid in debris clearance and support synaptic repair (Jassam et al., 2017). Systems-level reorganization: rewiring the network

The cellular changes manifest at the macro-scale as measurable alterations in functional network architecture, often following a non-linear temporal pattern.

•The Hyperconnectivity Phase: Hyperconnectivity in the subacute period represents a brain’s compensatory mechanism to overcome neural inefficiency by recruiting additional cognitive resources. Multiple studies provide strong evidence for this phenomenon (Hillary and Grafman, 2017; Wong et al., 2023). Specifically, patients with good cognitive recovery often demonstrate increased functional connectivity between the Default Mode Network (DMN) and Frontoparietal Network (FPN), suggesting enhanced top-down cognitive control (Vicentini et al., 2021; van der Horn et al., 2024). The evidence suggests this is a widespread neurological response, with Hillary et al. reviewing 126 studies and concluding that hyperconnectivity is a common adaptive network response to neurological disruption (Hillary et al., 2015). While potentially beneficial in the short term, Hillary et al. caution that chronic hyperconnectivity might eventually lead to metabolic stress on neural network hubs (Hillary and Grafman, 2017).

•Compensatory Recruitment and Diaschisis: Diaschisis is now understood as a network-level phenomenon of remote brain dysfunction following localized injury, with recovery involving complex neural reorganization. Recent research provides strong evidence for this perspective (Carrera and Tononi, 2014; Fornito et al., 2015). The concept goes beyond simple local injury effects, revealing that brain lesions can cause widespread functional connectivity changes (Wawrzyniak et al., 2022). Specifically, patients may recruit contralateral homologous regions during tasks, which can represent either adaptive compensation or a less efficient processing strategy (Stockert et al., 2020). The resolution of diaschisis appears to be a dynamic process, with studies showing that functionally connected regions can experience temporary dysfunction before gradually recovering (Wawrzyniak et al., 2022). This network perspective provides a more nuanced understanding of brain injury and recovery, highlighting the brain’s remarkable adaptive capabilities.

•Temporal Evolution of Network Topology: Longitudinal graph theory studies suggest brain networks undergo a complex reorganization process after traumatic injury, initially increasing connectivity indiscriminately before potentially re-establishing a more efficient small-world architecture during recovery. Evidence from multiple studies supports this trajectory (Nakamura et al., 2009; Moreira da Silva et al., 2020). Specifically, Nakamura et al. found increased “small-worldness” from 3 to 6 months post-injury, with reduced connectivity strength. Moreira da Silva et al. identified 16 brain network metrics discriminating post-injury phases, with 11 metrics explaining 90% of cognitive recovery variability (Moreira da Silva et al., 2020). The research suggests that failure to re-establish an optimal network balance may contribute to chronic cognitive impairment, though more longitudinal research is needed to definitively confirm this mechanism (Pandit et al., 2013).

Measuring reorganization: The role of neuroimaging

The ability to visualize and quantify the connectome in living humans has been the driving force behind the network theory of TBI. Multimodal neuroimaging provides the essential tools to measure both the structural scaffold and its dynamic functional outcomes.

•While powerful, these techniques have significant limitations in TBI. First, motion artifact is a major confound in rs-fMRI, as TBI patients (particularly those with agitation or cognitive deficits) may move more than controls, artificially reducing functional connectivity. Second, focal lesions complicate node definition: standard atlases may label damaged tissue as a functional node, biasing graph metrics. Third, DTI tractography struggles with crossing fibers, which is particularly problematic in injured white matter where edema, Wallerian degeneration, and axonal sprouting create complex microstructural environments. Fourth, graph theory metrics (e.g., global efficiency, modularity) can be analytically unstable, with small changes in node definition or thresholding producing large changes in outcomes. Finally, the correspondence between structural and functional connectivity is weak in injured brains, meaning that DTI and rs-fMRI often provide non-overlapping (rather than convergent) information.

The double-edged sword: maladaptive vs. adaptive reorganization

Not all plastic change is beneficial. The same mechanisms that underpin recovery can also lead to persistent symptoms, making the functional outcome a balance between adaptive and maladaptive reorganization (Fig. 4).

•Adaptive Reorganization: This generally refers to plastic changes that appear to successfully restore or compensate for lost function, although the same changes may be maladaptive in other contexts.

Vermeij et al. found that in older adults, additional bilateral prefrontal activation during a working memory task did not result in better cognitive performance (Vermeij et al., 2014). Similarly, Colcombe et al. demonstrated that better-performing older adults actually showed less bilateral activity, contrary to the assumption that more neural recruitment is always beneficial (Colcombe et al., 2005). Anderson et al. further clarified that while some brain over recruitment can be compensatory, not all increased activation represents an effective cognitive strategy (Anderson, 2019). This suggests that neural compensation is complex, context-dependent, and can sometimes indicate neural inefficiency rather than adaptive processing.

Modulating reorganization: Therapeutic implications

The recognition that recovery after TBI is an active process of network reorganization opens a new frontier for therapeutic interventions. The goal is no longer simply to protect the brain from damage, but to actively guide and enhance adaptive plasticity while suppressing maladaptive changes. This paradigm shift moves us towards a future of "network-guided rehabilitation."

While the interventions discussed below are promising and emerging research avenues, they are not established clinical protocols for routine TBI care. Current evidence is limited by small trial sizes, heterogeneous stimulation targets, sparse mechanistic imaging endpoints, and a lack of validated connectome-guided protocols. Therefore, we present this section to illustrate future directions, not as a clinical roadmap.

Neuromodulation: Resetting network dynamics

Non-invasive brain stimulation techniques can modulate cortical excitability and are being investigated as tools to directly influence network connectivity (Fig. 5).

•Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS): Repetitive Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (rTMS) can modulate brain network connectivity through frequency-specific stimulation, with distinct effects on neural excitability and functional connections. High-frequency rTMS (>5 Hz) increases cortical excitability and can enhance cognitive processing (Chen and Seitz, 2001). For instance, stimulating the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex can improve cognitive control (Guse et al., 2010). Conversely, low-frequency rTMS decreases cortical excitability, potentially reducing network hyperconnectivity (Eldaief et al., 2011). Multiple studies demonstrate nuanced network effects: Zhang et al. found that high-frequency rTMS leads to changes across multiple networks, while low-frequency stimulation produces more targeted alterations (Zhang et al., 2020). However, Beynel et al. cautions that the simple high-frequency facilitation/low-frequency inhibition model may not fully explain complex network dynamics (Beynel et al., 2020).

Multiple studies provide evidence for this effect: Fregni et al. demonstrated that anodal stimulation of the left prefrontal cortex increases working memory task accuracy (Fregni et al., 2005). Jacky Au et al. found that tDCS enhanced training performance with effects preserved months after training (Au et al., 2016). Zaehle et al. showed tDCS modulates working memory performance by altering neural oscillatory activity (Zaehle et al., 2011). However, the evidence is not uniformly conclusive. Nilsson et al. questioned the general usefulness of current tDCS protocols, finding minimal cognitive enhancement in older adults (Nilsson et al., 2017). The most promising results appear in younger populations and with specific stimulation parameters.

Specifically, research has shown that participants can learn to modulate the Default Mode Network (DMN) through real-time fMRI neurofeedback (Pamplona et al., 2020). Cognitive rehabilitation as network training

Cognitive rehabilitation can indeed be effectively conceptualized as a structured method to guide experience-dependent neural plasticity through targeted network-specific exercises.

The evidence supports this framework with several key insights: Kleim et al. demonstrates that neural plasticity is fundamental to learning and relearning in damaged brains, while Crosson et al. confirms that neural plasticity has provided “new models and tools for neurocognitive rehabilitation (Kleim and Jones, 2008; Crosson et al., 2017).” Cicerone et al. further substantiates the approach, noting that cognitive rehabilitation relies on “mechanisms of neuroplasticity” dependent on active attention, learning, feedback, and motivation (Cicerone, 2012). The proposed network-specific training strategies align with emerging understanding that rehabilitation can dynamically adjust task-related brain activity. While promising, more research is needed to definitively map precise intervention-network relationships and generalizability across different cognitive domains.

The principle of “neurons that fire together, wire together” is a fundamental mechanism of neural rehabilitation that enables targeted neural network reconfiguration through repeated activation of desired pathways. Researchers have found strong theoretical and preliminary experimental support for this mechanism. Jo et al. directly hypothesized that strengthening corticospinal-motoneuronal synapses through Hebbian plasticity can promote functional recovery (Jo et al., 2023). Takeuchi et al. further elaborated that synchronizing rehabilitation with voluntary neural activity can reinforce neural connections between impaired muscles and residual motor areas (Takeuchi and Izumi, 2015). Nudo et al. critically emphasized that behavioral experience is the most potent modulator of brain plasticity, with the injured brain being particularly malleable (Nudo, 2013). The brain can be reshaped after injury in either adaptive or maladaptive ways, depending on the quality and quantity of motor experience.

Pharmacological interventions: enhancing the plasticity substrate

Certain medications may act, in part, by creating a more permissive environment for adaptive reorganization.

•Amantadine: Amantadine shows promising potential for cognitive recovery through its unique mechanism of NMDA receptor antagonism and dopamine modulation, with moderate clinical evidence supporting its use in TBI patients. Clinical studies demonstrate significant cognitive improvements in TBI patients. One randomized trial showed improvements in Mini-Mental Status scores by 14.3 points and Disability Rating Scale scores by 9.8 points when using amantadine (Meythaler et al., 2002). Another systematic review found the drug well-tolerated, with potential to hasten intermediate-term cognitive recovery (Loggini et al., 2020). The drug’s mechanism involves multiple neurological pathways: reducing glutamate-mediated excitotoxicity, increasing dopamine release, and potentially protecting dopamine neurons (Nakano et al., 2019; Ossola et al., 2011). However, Khormali et al. cautions that more research is needed to fully understand its efficacy and optimal application (Khormali et al., 2022).

However, the translation of neurogenic changes to functional recovery remains inconsistent - Wang et al. specifically noted that despite enhanced neuroplasticity, no significant functional improvements were observed (Levy et al., 2019). The most promising findings suggest early post-injury administration may optimize the brain’s reorganization potential.

•Future agents: Research is actively focused on developing drugs that directly target neuroplasticity mechanisms, with particular emphasis on inhibiting axonal growth inhibitors like Nogo-A and exploring combination therapeutic approaches. Multiple studies demonstrate that blocking Nogo-A can enhance axonal regeneration and functional recovery (Pernet and Schwab, 2012; Liebscher et al., 2005). Researchers have identified several promising strategies, including function-blocking antibodies, receptor antagonists, and signal transduction inhibition (Kastin and Pan, 2005). Current experimental approaches show significant potential in animal models, with function-blocking anti-Nogo-A antibodies already being tested in clinical trials for spinal cord injury (Liebscher et al., 2005).

Future directions and conclusions

The network perspective on neurotrauma has fundamentally altered our understanding of brain injury and recovery. As the field matures, several exciting frontiers are emerging.

Future directions

•Personalized Network Medicine: Future treatments will be tailored to an individual's unique pattern of network disruption, as revealed by their baseline connectome. A patient with DMN hyperconnectivity might receive inhibitory rTMS to the PCC, while one with executive network hypoconnectivity might receive excitatory stimulation to the DLPFC.

Limitations of this review

This article is a narrative review designed to synthesize broad concepts in post-traumatic network reorganization rather than a systematic quantitative summary. Accordingly, we did not adhere to PRISMA guidelines, which are better suited to meta-analyses of intervention effects. Our literature selection prioritized studies that illustrate key mechanistic principles and replicated findings, with the goal of building a conceptual framework rather than exhaustive enumeration.

We acknowledge that the inherent heterogeneity of TBI—across severity, injury mechanism, time since injury, and patient age—makes direct synthesis challenging. Wherever possible, we have stratified findings by these variables. However, readers should be aware that the literature does not always permit clean separation, and some degree of generalization remains necessary to identify common principles.

Finally, to generate hypotheses where direct TBI evidence is sparse, we have occasionally drawn on findings from related conditions such as stroke, aging, and chronic pain. These instances are explicitly labeled as hypothesis-generating or analogous, and we encourage readers to interpret them cautiously. Our goal is to be transparent about where evidence is strong versus where it remains inferential.

Conclusion

Traumatic brain injury is a catastrophic event that dismantles the intricate architecture of the human connectome. The journey from injury to outcome is not a passive process of healing, but an active, dynamic, and prolonged struggle of the brain to reorganize itself. This process, unfolding from the molecular to the systems level, is a double-edged sword, capable of producing both adaptive recovery and maladaptive pathology. The evidence strongly suggests that functional outcome is closely associated with the efficacy and direction of this network reorganization, although causality remains difficult to establish definitively.

The great promise of this connectome-based approach is that it provides a mechanistic framework to explain the heterogeneity of TBI outcomes and a roadmap for developing next-generation, targeted therapies. By moving beyond a purely lesion-centric view and embracing the brain as a complex, adaptive network, we open the door to interventions designed not just to protect the brain from damage, but to actively guide it toward a state of health and restored function. The future of neurotrauma care lies in learning to speak the language of the brain's networks and partnering with its innate, if sometimes misguided, capacity for change.
