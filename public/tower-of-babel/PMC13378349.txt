The inconvenient truth about direct air capture: Realigning climate strategy with physical reality
Authors: Charles Q. Jia, Donald W. Kirk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDirect air capture (DAC) is promoted as an essential climate solution, yet thermodynamic and energy constraints make deployment at climate-relevant scales deeply problematic. Current DAC systems require 1,500–3,000 kWh per tonne of CO2 captured and stored—one to two orders of magnitude higher than point-source capture and far beyond what global clean-energy availability can support. Meeting even the lower bound of the IPCC’s mid-century carbon-removal targets via DAC alone would demand more than half of today’s global electricity, diverting clean energy away from direct decarbonization. Overreliance on DAC thus risks institutionalizing energy inefficiency and delaying essential emissions cuts. Historical precedents, from acid rain mitigation to ozone recovery, demonstrate that pollution is best addressed at its source. We propose a strategic realignment that prioritizes emissions prevention, strengthens natural carbon uptake through photosynthesis, and deploys proven, energy-positive, negative-emission pathways, such as biochar, that leverage rather than oppose fundamental thermodynamic constraints.

Main text

In 2025, the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) named direct air capture (DAC) one of its “Top 10 Emerging Technologies in Chemistry,” reflecting significant optimism in DAC as a climate solution.1 While advancements in sorbents and separation processes have improved capture efficiency, this optimism often overlooks the profound thermodynamic hurdles. CO2 is an exceptionally stable molecule and exists in a highly dilute concentration of ∼0.04% in the atmosphere, making its capture inherently energy-intensive due to the entropy-of-mixing penalty.2,3,4,5

Current DAC plants, validated at commercial facilities, require extraordinary energy inputs—ranging from 1,500 to 3,000 kWh per ton of CO2—far exceeding theoretical minima.6,7,8,9 This is significantly less efficient than capturing CO2 at its source, such as at coal gasification plants (6.3–25.2 kWh/ton) or direct coal combustion facilities (31.5–44.1 kWh/ton).10 This energy disparity is not a simple engineering challenge to be optimized away but a manifestation of fundamental physical limits. It is, therefore, inaccurate to frame DAC’s energy burden as merely an engineering hurdle. It is a structural consequence of the low atmospheric CO2 concentration and thus, a predictable constraint on any technology attempting to extract it from ambient air. Recent advances in metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), covalent organic frameworks (COFs), and other novel sorbents have improved CO2 capacities under DAC-relevant conditions,11 yet their carbon footprint during synthesis, regeneration energy penalties, and long-term stability under ambient air cycling remain substantial barriers. While advancing material science, these innovations do not alter the fundamental entropy-of-mixing constraint.

The mismatch between DAC’s thermodynamic demands and climate-relevant scales becomes clear when applying operational datasets from full-scale plants. Even if one used the 36,000 tonne per year capacity of Climeworks’ mammoth facility, reaching the lower bound of the IPCC’s 7 gigaton (Gt) per year removal requirement12 would require approximately 194,000 such plants. The energy implications are decisive; at demonstrated values of around 2,500 kWh per tonne of CO2 captured and stored,7,13 removing 7 Gt annually would require about 17,500 TWh yr−1, over half of the world’s total electricity generation in 2024, ∼30,000 TWh,14 making it an impractical solution for climate mitigation and reinforcing the need to focus on more feasible approaches. Even removing 3 Gt yr−1 from aviation, shipping, and cement—often cited as “hard-to-abate” justifications15—would require 7,500 TWh, or one-quarter of total global electricity. Diverting renewable energy to DAC in a world still heavily reliant on fossil fuels actively reduces the potential for overall climate benefits. DAC cannot play a role beyond a niche without creating a clean-energy deficit that undermines overall mitigation. While our analysis concludes that DAC cannot be a climate-relevant solution at the Gt scale, it is possible to deploy DAC at the Mt scale in specific contexts, such as pairing with ultra-low-cost stranded renewable energy or long-duration storage, provided such deployment does not divert clean energy from direct fossil-fuel displacement. However, the arithmetic of global clean-energy supply makes even this limited role contingent on deep prior decarbonization.

History confirms that pollution problems are most successfully addressed by tackling emissions upstream, before atmospheric dilution exacerbates removal difficulty. Three major environmental successes illustrate this principle: tackling acid rain, SO2 scrubbers, fuel switching, and cap-and-trade reduced the United State’s SO2 emissions by 93% between 1990 and 2019.16 Addressing ozone depletion, the Montreal Protocol phased out >99% of chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), enabling observable recovery of stratospheric ozone.17 Controlling persistent organic pollutants (POPs) through regulatory bans rather than downstream remediation delivered ecosystem recovery. Inspired by Rachel Carson’s book “Silent Spring,” the ban on dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT) was a key factor in the remarkable recovery of the bald eagle, from 417 nesting pairs in 1963 to over 71,400 in 2021.18,19 These successes were not achieved by cleaning up the pollution after the fact but by preventing it at its origin. In all cases, upstream control proved more efficient and effective than capturing dilute pollutants after dispersion. DAC inverts this logic: it attempts the thermodynamically hardest task (removing a dilute pollutant) instead of the easiest (preventing it from entering the atmosphere). As early as 2010, Tsouris et al. argued that the sheer scale of investment required for atmospheric CO2 capture would be better directed toward preventing emissions at concentrated sources and expanding carbon-free energy generation. Their conclusion—that industrial separation of a dilute gas from an effectively infinite system cannot be achieved within realistic technical and economic constraints—has only become more salient with subsequent operational data from pilot and commercial DAC facilities.20

Instead of investing massively in the energetically expensive DAC, perhaps, we should be more humble and turn to “nature’s DAC”—photosynthesis. Terrestrial plants remove a massive amount of CO2 from the atmosphere, already. Recent estimates suggest that global terrestrial photosynthesis captures approximately 575 Gt of CO2 per year, a figure about 31% higher than previously thought21—orders of magnitude greater than any engineered removal pathway. Natural process is not simply an appeal to “nature-based solutions” but the recognition that photosynthesis overcomes the entropy-of-mixing barrier by harvesting solar energy and chemically reducing CO2 directly into biomass. We can work with and enhance this natural cycle through methods like biochar. Biochar is a stable, carbon-rich material produced from biomass through pyrolysis (heating in the absence of oxygen). It effectively sequesters carbon for hundreds to thousands of years when added to soil. The potential for biochar is significant, with some estimates suggesting it could sequester up to 2 Gt CO2 per year by 2050.22,23 Unlike the energy-intensive DAC process, biochar production can be energy-positive, with byproducts like syngas and bio-oil that can be used as renewable energy sources. Furthermore, biochar offers numerous co-benefits,24 including improved soil fertility, water retention, nutrient efficiency, and reduced N2O and CH4 emissions. Biochar and natural photosynthesis are not without constraints. Scaling biochar to multi-Gt levels requires careful management of biomass feedstock availability, land-use competition with food production, and standardized protocols for carbon accounting. These challenges are real and must be addressed through integrated land-energy-carbon systems analysis, but they do not negate the thermodynamic advantage over DAC.

DAC offers a remarkable technological capability but not a climate-relevant solution at scale. Physics, not pessimism, sets these limits. A climate strategy grounded in thermodynamic realism prioritizes direct emissions reductions, embraces natural carbon uptake, and scales energy-positive, land-compatible carbon sequestration methods such as biochar. Future work should focus on optimizing these strategies within integrated land-energy-carbon systems, rather than assuming that atmospheric CO2 can be reversibly and affordably reduced at multi-Gt levels with DAC. Aligning mitigation pathways with physical limits is not an argument against innovation—but a prerequisite for successful climate action.
