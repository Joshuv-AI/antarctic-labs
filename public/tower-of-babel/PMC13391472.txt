Wild boar (Sus scrofa) meat quality research at the crossroads of genetics and nutrition: a global bibliometric mapping
Authors: Le Pham Tan Quoc, Pham My Hao, Pham Thi Quyen, Nguyen Huynh Dinh Thuan, Lien Le Phuong Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study provides a comprehensive bibliometric analysis of global research on wild boar (Sus scrofa) meat quality and related attributes, including carcass traits, fatty acid composition, and nutritional characteristics. A total of 423 articles indexed in the Web of Science Core Collection were retrieved and analyzed using RStudio with the Bibliometrix package, complemented by VOSviewer for network visualization. The results indicate a marked increase in scientific production, reflecting growing interest in both genetic and quality-related aspects of wild boar meat. Leading journals such as Journal of Animal Science and Meat Science dominate the field in terms of productivity and citation impact, highlighting the strong linkage between animal genetics and meat science. Author and country collaboration networks reveal the presence of core research groups, primarily concentrated in Europe, with increasing international cooperation. Keyword co-occurrence and thematic evolution analyses identify three principal research directions: (i) genetic improvement and production traits, (ii) molecular mechanisms and genome-wide association studies, and (iii) meat quality and lipid composition. Overlay and thematic evolution analyses reveal a transition from traditional studies on carcass and growth traits toward genomic approaches and nutritional evaluation in recent years. Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) further confirms the coexistence of production genetics, molecular biology, and meat quality as the field’s main conceptual pillars. This study covers the gap of literature by summarizing research output on this individual topic. Overall, the presented work clarifies the intellectual structure, thematic development, and emerging trends in wild boar meat research, providing a valuable reference framework for future investigations integrating genomics, meat science, and nutritional evaluation.Keywords: Bibliometric, Carcass traits, Meat quality, Science mapping, Sus scrofa, Wild boar

Introduction

In recent years, global meat consumption has continued to increase alongside population growth, rising income levels, and shifts in dietary patterns (González et al., 2020). In addition to conventional livestock and poultry meat, protein sources derived from wild animals—particularly wild boar (Sus scrofa)—have attracted growing interest from both consumers and researchers (Hoffman & Cawthorn, 2012; Lestingi, 2023). Wild boar is often perceived as a natural food source with minimal exposure to intensive farming systems and is therefore associated with “clean” and “natural” meat, as well as potentially higher nutritional value compared to commercially farmed pigs (Stillfried et al., 2017).

Sus scrofa is widely distributed across Europe, Asia, and several other regions of the world, demonstrating high ecological adaptability (Markov et al., 2022). The increasing population density of wild boar in many countries not only poses challenges for wildlife management but also creates opportunities for meat production through hunting or semi-wild farming systems (Castagna et al., 2024). In this context, evaluating meat quality, carcass traits, fatty acid profile, and nutritional composition of wild boar has become an important research topic to determine its commercial potential and associated health benefits.

Wild boar meat quality is assessed using various indicators, including pH, color, tenderness, water-holding capacity, fat-to-lean ratio, and sensory characteristics. Compared to intensively farmed pigs, wild boar meat generally exhibits lower fat content, firmer muscle structure, and higher protein levels (Borilova et al., 2016; Jukna & Valaitienė, 2012). Moreover, the fatty acid composition of wild boar meat has been reported to contain higher proportions of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), particularly omega-3 and omega-6, depending on natural feeding conditions and habitat (Strazdina et al., 2013). These attributes have increased interest in the nutritional value and potential health benefits of wild boar meat within modern diets.

Carcass traits such as carcass weight, proportion of commercial cuts, backfat thickness, and loin eye area are also critical indicators reflecting growth performance and processing potential (Müller et al., 2000). Factors including sex, age, hunting season, geographic location, and ecological conditions may significantly influence these parameters (Ludwiczak et al., 2020). Therefore, synthesizing and analyzing existing research is necessary to better understand variations and trends within this field.

Although numerous individual studies have examined meat quality and nutritional composition of Sus scrofa, these works are dispersed across different countries and journals, employing heterogeneous methodologies and evaluation criteria. To date, systematic studies aimed at synthesizing and assessing the intellectual structure, research trends, collaboration networks, and prominent themes in wild boar meat quality research remain limited. In the context of rapidly expanding scientific output, the application of bibliometric analysis to quantitatively and visually map the global research landscape has become increasingly important.

Bibliometric analysis is a quantitative scientific approach that enables the evaluation of publication growth, identification of influential authors and countries, analysis of collaboration networks, and exploration of core research themes and emerging trends through keyword and co-occurrence analyses (Kumar et al., 2023). This approach not only helps identify research gaps but also provides strategic direction for future studies in meat science and wildlife-derived food technologies.

In addition, existing research remains fragmented across multiple disciplines, including animal genetics, meat science, and wildlife management, with limited integration across these domains. Wild boar represents a unique model due to its dual role as both a wild species and a genetic resource closely related to domestic pigs (Chen et al., 2007), making it highly relevant for studies on genetic diversity, meat quality, and alternative protein systems. Therefore, a dedicated bibliometric analysis is necessary to capture the specific intellectual structure and research evolution of this field.

The novelty of this study lies in its integrated analysis of wild boar meat research at the intersection of genetics, carcass traits, and nutritional composition. By combining performance analysis with advanced science mapping techniques, this study not only evaluates publication trends but also reveals the conceptual structure, thematic evolution, and collaborative patterns within the field. Furthermore, this work contributes to broader areas such as meat science and sustainable protein research by highlighting the role of wild boar as a potential alternative meat source and a model for comparative studies between wild and domesticated species.

Based on these considerations, this work aims to comprehensively analyze the global research landscape related to wild boar (Sus scrofa) in connection with meat quality, carcass characteristics, fatty acid composition, and nutritional value. Specifically, the objectives are to: (i) evaluate publication growth and temporal trends; (ii) identify influential authors, countries, and journals; (iii) analyze scientific collaboration networks; and (iv) determine major research themes and emerging trends in the field. The findings are expected to provide a systematic overview of the current intellectual structure and contribute to guiding future research and sustainable utilization of wild boar meat resources.

Data source and search strategy

The bibliographic data were retrieved from the Web of Science Core Collection (WoS) database, which is widely recognized for its high-quality indexing standards and comprehensive citation information. WoS was selected to ensure consistency and reliability of metadata and citation records. In addition, WoS is frequently used in bibliometric studies due to its rigorous journal selection criteria, standardized indexing system, and strong coverage of high-impact journals, making it particularly suitable for citation-based analyses and comparative evaluations across studies. However, it should be noted that the use of a single database may limit the overall coverage of the literature.

The literature search was conducted on February 9, 2026 (09/02/2026).

The following search query was applied in the Topic (TS) field, which includes title, abstract, and author keywords:

(“wild boar” OR “Sus scrofa”) AND (“meat quality” OR “carcass traits” OR “fatty acid” OR “nutritional composition”). This query ensures a focused retrieval of relevant studies, although some publications using alternative terminology may not be captured.

The initial search yielded 447 records. To ensure data consistency and focus on original research contributions, the document type was refined to “Article” only, excluding reviews, conference proceedings, editorial materials, book chapters, and other document types. While this improves data homogeneity, it may exclude certain types of publications, such as review articles. After this filtering process, a total of 423 articles were retained for bibliometric analysis.

All records were exported in “Full Record and Cited References” format and saved as plain text files compatible with bibliometric analysis tools in R.

Data analysis

The bibliometric analysis was performed using RStudio with the Bibliometrix package and its Biblioshiny interface, complemented by VOSviewer for network visualization (Aria & Cuccurullo, 2017; van Eck & Waltman, 2017). Both performance analysis and science mapping techniques were applied. Descriptive indicators were used to evaluate annual scientific production, leading authors, countries, institutions, journals, and citation metrics (total citations and h-index). Co-authorship analysis examined collaboration networks at the author and country levels. Keyword co-occurrence analysis based on Author Keywords and Keywords Plus was conducted to identify major research themes, while Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) was used to explore the conceptual structure of the field (Yuan et al., 2022; Isfandyari-Moghaddam et al., 2023; Atkinson, 2024). Thematic mapping and thematic evolution analysis were further applied to detect core themes and emerging research trends in studies related to wild boar meat quality and associated attributes.

Methodological considerations

Although WoS ensures high-quality and standardized bibliographic records, limiting the data source to a single database may exclude relevant studies indexed elsewhere. Furthermore, restricting the document type to “Article” enhances data homogeneity but may omit influential review articles.

Despite these limitations, the combination of WoS data and Bibliometrix-based analysis provides a robust framework for systematically mapping the scientific landscape of research on wild boar meat quality and related attributes.

Scientific production, productive authors, and geographical analysis

Analysis of publication output by year indicates that research on wild boar meat quality (Sus scrofa) and related traits (carcass traits, fatty acids, nutritional composition) has a relatively early origin but has developed unevenly over time (Fig. 1). The first publication was recorded in 1959; however, throughout the 1960s to the late 1980s, the number of publications remained very limited and sporadic. This suggests that in the early period, studies on wild boar meat were mainly descriptive or appeared as supplementary components within broader wildlife and ecological research rather than as an independent field within food science or meat science (Takahashi & Tisdell, 1989).

Annual scientific production of wild boar meat quality research

From the mid-1990s onward, the number of publications began to increase gradually, reflecting growing interest in alternative protein sources and wild game meat in the context of global food diversification (Wolkers et al., 1994). Notably, the early 2000s showed a clear rise, with 2003 recording 18 publications, marking a transition from an exploratory phase to a more systematic development stage. This increase may be associated with advances in modern biochemical analytical methods, particularly techniques for determining fatty acid composition and evaluating meat quality (Marchiori & Felício, 2003).

In addition, this growth may also be driven by increasing consumer interest in game meat as a natural and organic protein source, along with rising concerns regarding sustainability and the environmental impacts of conventional livestock production. The recognition of wild boar as a potential alternative protein source has stimulated research attention. Furthermore, advances in omics technologies (e.g., genomics, proteomics, and metabolomics) have enabled more in-depth investigations into meat quality and biological mechanisms, thereby accelerating the development of this research field.

During the period 2006–2014, publication output remained high and relatively stable, with several years exceeding 20 publications (2009, 2010, 2012). This period can be considered a phase of strong growth, as studies increasingly focused on nutritional value, fatty acid profiles, and the potential health benefits of wild boar meat compared with commercial pork (Soriano et al., 2006; Sales & Kotrba, 2013). At the same time, the expansion of wild boar populations in Europe and issues related to wildlife management also contributed to increased research on the utilization of wild boar meat as a sustainable food resource (Gunduz et al., 2007). This trend is also closely associated with wildlife management policies in Europe, where increasing wild boar populations have required population control strategies, thereby promoting research on the utilization of wild boar meat.

From 2015 to 2024, publication numbers fluctuated but remained relatively high, indicating that the field has entered a mature and stable stage. Research topics during this period have expanded toward sensory quality evaluation, food safety, and the effects of hunting season, geographic region, and semi-wild production systems on meat quality (Altissimi et al., 2023; Lénárt et al. 2023).

Overall, the publication trend shows a clear shift from an initial scattered stage to a phase of strong growth, followed by stabilization at a relatively high level. This reflects the formation and development of a specialized research field within meat science and nutrition, while also highlighting the potential for further expansion in response to increasing interest in sustainable and nutritionally valuable food sources (Sampels et al., 2023).

The predominance of single-publication authors reflects the participation of occasional or interdisciplinary contributors, while the limited number of highly productive scholars likely represents established research groups that serve as intellectual anchors, guiding research directions and consolidating knowledge development within the field.

Figure 8 presents the keyword co-occurrence network, reflecting the thematic structure and relationships among research directions in wild boar meat quality studies. The keywords are organized into distinct clusters, indicating that the field has evolved based on multiple scientific pillars that are closely interconnected.

First, a major cluster revolves around genetics- and breeding-related terms, such as “gene”, “QTL”, “genetic variation”, “marker”, “growth”, and “carcass traits” (Szemethy et al., 2021). The strong interconnections among these terms suggest that studies not only describe phenotypic characteristics but also seek to explain the genetic mechanisms underlying production traits in Sus scrofa, emphasizing the fundamental role of genetic research in understanding differences between wild boars, domestic pigs, and hybrid populations (Goedbloed et al., 2013).

Another thematic cluster focuses on terms such as “meat quality”, “pH”, “color”, “water holding capacity”, “tenderness”, and “intramuscular fat”. This cluster represents studies evaluating physicochemical and sensory properties of meat. The connections between this cluster and the previous one indicate that multiple research themes are linked within the network (Poeta et al., 2007; Szemethy et al., 2021).

Additionally, the presence of terms such as “fatty acids”, “lipid profile”, and “nutritional composition” highlights a research direction centered on the chemical composition and nutritional value of wild boar meat. These keywords frequently co-occur with “meat quality”, indicating that compositional analysis is an important component of the field (Liu et al., 2021).

Overall, Fig. 8 shows a highly interconnected keyword network, with several central terms—such as “wild boar”, “Sus scrofa”, “meat quality”, and “carcass traits”—serving as linking nodes between research clusters. These results highlight a structured and interconnected research landscape composed of multiple thematic domains.

In the overlay visualization (Fig. 8b), the map highlights the temporal evolution of research focus within the field. Terms related to genetics and genome mapping appear more prominently in earlier periods, reflecting the foundational stage of scientific development (Frantz et al., 2012). In contrast, terms such as “fatty acids”, “genome scan analysis”, and “meat quality traits” have become increasingly prominent in recent years, indicating a shift from fundamental research toward applied and food-oriented studies (Bella et al., 2024).

This shift reflects a change in research emphasis over time, from foundational biological studies to more application-oriented topics (Macháčková et al., 2021). Therefore, Fig. 9 directly contributes to addressing RQ6 by identifying emerging themes and evolving research trends in wild boar (Sus scrofa) meat quality studies.

Figure 10 illustrates the thematic evolution of wild boar and pig-related research across three periods (1959–2010, 2011–2018, and 2019–2026). In the earliest period (1959–2010), the dominant themes included “intramuscular fat”, “pig”, and “meat quality”, reflecting foundational studies on carcass traits and muscle fat deposition in domestic pigs. The presence of “fatty acids” indicates early biochemical characterization, but the overall focus remained centered on production and quality traits rather than nutritional evaluation.

During 2011–2018, the thematic structure diversified. Core topics such as “meat quality” and “growth” persisted, while new themes emerged, including “identification”, “performance”, “skeletal muscle”, and notably “wild boar”. This shift suggests an expansion from traditional pig production studies toward species identification, muscle biology, and performance evaluation, alongside increasing attention to wild boar populations.

In the most recent period (2019–2026), the thematic map shows consolidation around “pig” and “wild boar” as central nodes, accompanied by more specific genetically related terms, such as “candidate genes”, “Sus scrofa domestica”, and “region”. The emergence of these terms indicates a stronger emphasis on genomic characterization and differentiation between wild and domestic populations. Meanwhile, “meat quality” remains present but appears more integrated within genetic and species-specific contexts rather than as an isolated theme.

Overall, Figs. 8, 9 and 10 indicate that research on wild boar meat quality possesses a multi-pillar conceptual structure, closely integrating genetics, carcass traits, and nutritional composition. The development of the field shows a transition from studies focused primarily on genetic mechanisms toward evaluations of meat quality and nutritional value, reflecting both the maturation of the discipline and the expansion of its application scope. These findings provide a foundation for deeper analyses of knowledge structure and thematic evolution in the following sections.

Intellectual landscape

Thematic evolution

To clarify shifts in research focus over time, a thematic evolution analysis was conducted to identify how specific terms and research directions have been maintained, expanded, or replaced in recent periods. Figure 11 shows substantial changes in the thematic structure of research on wild boar (Sus scrofa), illustrating the temporal occurrence of key terms and their prominence across different time intervals.

Thematic evolution of the observed research field during the period of 2009–2026. Dot size represents the highest frequency of keywords in published articles at a given periods

In the earlier period (approximately 2009–2014), terms such as “muscle”, “traits”, “lipid metabolism”, “fatty-acid composition”, and “quality” were particularly prominent. This indicates that early research primarily focused on fundamental biological characteristics of muscle tissues and lipid composition, as well as the evaluation of meat quality traits from physiological and biochemical perspectives (Kosovac et al., 2009). The appearance of the term “large white” further suggests that comparative studies between wild boar and commercial domestic pig breeds were common during this stage.

During the intermediate period (2015–2019), terms including “gene”, “expression”, “genome-wide association study (GWAS)”, “genome-wide association”, and “growth-performance” became more prominent. This pattern shows that an increased research focus on molecular-level analysis and genome-based approaches (Wang et al., 2018).

In the most recent period (2020–2026), terms such as “myoglobin”, “association”, “GWAS”, “game meat”, and “red deer” appear more frequently and extend into the latest years. The concurrent emergence of terms in 2022, such as “chemical-composition” and “profiles”, indicates an increasing integration of molecular approaches with chemical composition assessments. These results suggest a diversification of research topics, combining analytical approaches with broader applications in food science. The presence of terms such as “red deer” and “fallow deer” also indicates an increasing number of interspecies comparative studies in the evaluation of game meat quality (Klupsaite et al., 2020).

Importantly, terms related to fatty acid composition and lipid metabolism emerged early and persisted across later periods, indicating that these topics represent stable and continuously evolving research themes. In contrast, terms associated with genomic analysis and GWAS clearly reflect the modernization of research methodologies driven by advances in biotechnology.

Overall, Fig. 11 demonstrates that the field has evolved from fundamental physiological and biochemical investigations toward molecular genetics and, more recently, toward broader evaluations of game meat value within a wider research context. This thematic transition helps address RQ6 regarding recent research trends and highlights the growing integration of genetics, muscle biology, and food science within this domain.

Conceptual structure

Factor analysis using Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) identified three major conceptual clusters representing the intellectual structure of research on wild boar and meat-related traits (Fig. 12). The first two MCA dimensions, Dim 1 (63.74%) and Dim 2 (18.14%), together explain 81.88% of the total dataset variance, indicating that the two-dimensional structure adequately captures the conceptual relationships among keywords. Dim 1 can be interpreted as the primary axis separating studies focused on improving production traits from those emphasizing meat quality and composition. Meanwhile, Dim 2 reflects the distinction between research centered on molecular genetic mechanisms and studies oriented toward applied meat product evaluation. The proportion of variance explained by the first two dimensions suggests that the intellectual structure of the field is relatively well defined, with thematic clusters clearly separated yet still interconnected.

Factorial approach using Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) elucidates conceptual structure map (a) and topic dendrogram (b). Colors represent the three major trends of published articles

Cluster 1 includes keywords associated with genetic improvement and production traits, such as “growth”, “quantitative trait loci”, “meat quality traits”, “backfat thickness”, etc. This cluster represents studies targeting economically important livestock traits, particularly growth performance and carcass characteristics, and reflects the foundational role of breeding and productivity improvement research.

Cluster 2 comprises terms related to molecular genetics and gene regulation mechanisms, including “gene”, “loci”, “linkage”, “expression”, “carcass traits”, etc. This cluster represents research focusing on the genetic mechanisms underlying phenotypic variation, particularly studies applying genomic tools and linkage analyses to identify candidate genes associated with meat-related traits (Pai et al., 2015).

Cluster 3 groups keywords related to meat quality and lipid characteristics, such as “muscle”, “fatty acids”, “pork”, “quality”, etc. This cluster reflects research addressing meat composition, physicochemical properties, and nutritional value, highlighting the growing interest in the quality of meat products (Leskanich et al., 1997).

Overall, the MCA results indicate that research on wild boar meat quality is structured around three main orientations: (i) genetics and improvement of production traits; (ii) molecular genetic mechanisms; and (iii) meat quality and nutritional composition. The relative separation among clusters reflects the degree of specialization within each research direction, while their spatial proximity suggests an increasing integration between genetics and meat science in recent years.

Conclusion

This bibliometric study offers a systematic overview of the scientific landscape concerning wild boar (Sus scrofa) meat quality research. The results showed that the field has evolved from foundational studies on carcass traits and lipid metabolism to more advanced genomic and association-based approaches, while increasingly incorporating nutritional and quality-oriented perspectives. The intellectual structure of the field is organized around three major research axes: production genetics, molecular mechanisms underlying phenotypic variation, and meat quality with lipid composition analysis. Thematic evolution analysis indicates a clear shift toward integrative and application-oriented research, particularly in the context of genome-wide association studies and the evaluation of wild game meat as a potential sustainable protein source. Despite the growing body of literature, collaboration networks remain relatively concentrated within specific research clusters, suggesting opportunities for broader interdisciplinary and international cooperation. Future research should emphasize the integration of multi-omics approaches with meat quality assessment, comparative analyses across wild and domestic populations, and deeper exploration of nutritional implications for human health. By mapping the development, structure, and emerging directions of the field, this study contributes to a clearer understanding of how wild boar meat research has progressed and where it is likely to advance in the coming years.
