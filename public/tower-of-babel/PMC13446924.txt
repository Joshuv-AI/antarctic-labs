Risk Factors for Vertical Transmission of Human Immunodeficiency Virus in Angola: A Case-Control Study
Authors: Paulo Ney Solari, Pedro Aguiar, Gonçalo Figueiredo Augusto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVertical transmission of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) remains a public health challenge in Angola despite the expansion of prevention of mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) programmes. This study aimed to identify factors associated with vertical HIV transmission in a referral maternity hospital in Luanda. An observational case-control study was conducted using retrospective clinical and laboratory records from 2019 to 2024. A total of 204 mother-infant pairs were included, comprising 68 cases (HIV-infected infants) and 136 controls (HIV-exposed but uninfected infants), frequency-matched at a 1:2 ratio by infant year of birth and maternal age group. Infant HIV status was determined up to 18 months of age, or after breastfeeding cessation when applicable, according to the national diagnostic algorithm. Multivariable unconditional binary logistic regression was used, with matching variables retained in the model. Several factors were independently associated with lower odds of vertical transmission, including suppressed maternal viral load (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 0.07; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.03–0.20), adequate paediatric follow-up (aOR = 0.08; 95% CI: 0.02–0.27), caesarean delivery (aOR = 0.13; 95% CI: 0.04–0.37), maternal age over 25 years, formal education, adequate initiation of antiretroviral therapy during pregnancy, and attendance at four or more antenatal consultations. These findings suggest that gaps in the PMTCT cascade may be associated with residual vertical HIV transmission in this referral maternity setting. Given the observational design, these associations should not be interpreted as causal. Strengthening virological monitoring, treatment adherence support, and retention of mother-infant pairs in care remains an important programmatic consideration for PMTCT services.

Introduction

According to the UNAIDS Terminology Guidelines (2024), vertical transmission refers to the passage of the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) from an HIV-positive mother to her child during pregnancy, childbirth, or breastfeeding. This is the main route of infection in children and remains a key challenge for prevention programmes, particularly in countries and regions with limited resources [1].

To accelerate the global response, UNAIDS launched the Global Plan to End New HIV Infections in Children and Keep Mothers Alive in 2011, in line with the Millennium Development Goals. This plan setsets ambitious targets for reducing paediatric infections and increasing maternal survival, becoming a strategic benchmark for countries with the highest burden of disease, including Angola [2].

It is estimated that every year, around 1.3 million women of reproductive age living with HIV become pregnant worldwide. Without preventive measures, the vertical transmission rate of the virus is between 15% and 45%. Given this reality, early detection of infection must be quickly followed by the initiation of lifelong antiretroviral treatment, combined with regular clinical follow-up, maintenance of viral suppression and access to services that also include care for sexual partners [3].

Despite advances in the global response to HIV, antiretroviral treatment coverage among children remains well below that of adults. In 2022, only 56% of children living with the virus in sub-Saharan Africa were receiving treatment, while globally 83% of adults already had access to therapy. This difference translated into approximately 70,000 preventable child deaths from AIDS-related illnesses, reinforcing the urgent need to invest in early diagnosis and immediate linkage to treatment in this particularly vulnerable population [4].

In 2024, approximately 120,000 children contracted HIV globally, with a confidence interval (CI) between 82,000 and 170,000, corresponding to a 62% reduction compared to 2010, when an estimated 310,000 new infections were reported. These data reflect substantial advances in access to prevention of vertical transmission [5].

According to UNICEF [6], in 2024, approximately 120,000 new infections occurred in children under 5 years of age, corroborating the 62% reduction since 2010, which reinforces the effectiveness of vertical transmission elimination programmes. It is estimated that in 2024, approximately 1.38 million children aged 0–14 years were living with HIV worldwide, of whom approximately 86% were in sub-Saharan Africa. This region, particularly Southern Africa, remains the epicentre of the paediatric epidemic, reflecting persistent inequalities in access to prevention of vertical transmission, early diagnosis and antiretroviral treatment. Although there has been a 62% reduction in new paediatric infections since 2010, it is estimated that every day 712 children continue to be infected and 250 die from AIDS-related causes, deaths that could be largely prevented with adequate access to care and treatment [7].

Among the main factors associated with vertical transmission of HIV identified in East Africa are late maternal diagnosis, inadequate adherence to antiretroviral treatment, lack of therapeutic coverage at the time of delivery, and lack of prophylaxis or clinical follow-up of newborns [8]. These findings point to the urgent need for interventions tailored to the local context, focusing on early linkage of pregnant women to health care, continuous monitoring of treatment adherence, and strengthening of maternal and child health services as strategic pillars for achieving the elimination of vertical transmission.

Despite advances in preventing vertical transmission of HIV, countries with generalised epidemics continue to face major challenges. Botswana stood out as the first in Africa to achieve World Health Organisation (WHO) silver certification, with 100% antiretroviral therapy (ART) coverage among pregnant women and a reduction in transmission to 1.6% in 2020. Malawi and Mozambique have also significantly expanded access to ART, with promising results. However, in high-prevalence settings, barriers remain that require integrated approaches based on gender equity, human rights, and community involvement. The WHO tier classification recognises these advances by requiring broader criteria that go beyond the infant infection rate [9].

However, national scientific output on factors associated with vertical transmission of HIV in Angola remains very limited. Most of the available information is limited to aggregate statistics on service coverage, without more in-depth analyses that would allow for the identification of risk determinants adjusted to the clinical and epidemiological reality of the country.

This lack of evidence hinders the design of interventions based on robust data and compromises the continuous monitoring of progress towards the elimination of vertical transmission. In this context, the present study seeks to contribute to filling this gap by identifying factors associated with vertical transmission of HIV in a referral maternity hospital in Angola.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

A case-control observational study was conducted to identify factors associated with vertical transmission of HIV, defined as HIV infection occurring from pregnancy through the end of breastfeeding. The primary outcome was confirmed infant HIV infection. Infant HIV status was determined up to 18 months of age, or after breastfeeding cessation when breastfeeding continued beyond the routine testing schedule, in accordance with the national diagnostic algorithm, using virological and/or serological testing. This outcome was conceptualised as reflecting the cumulative risk of intrauterine, intrapartum and early postnatal transmission. Exposures included maternal, obstetric and neonatal characteristics during pregnancy and delivery, as well as early postnatal interventions (such as infant feeding practices and paediatric follow-up) that could influence this cumulative risk.

Study Location and Period

The study took place at the Lucrécia Paim Maternity Hospital in Luanda, Angola, one of the main referral centres for obstetric and neonatal care in the country. Data collection covered a 5-year period, from January 2019 to December 2024, based on clinical and laboratory records available at the institution.

Study Population

Mother-infant pairs were excluded if essential clinical records were missing, particularly infant HIV test results or key information on antenatal care, delivery, and neonatal follow-up, or if follow-up was insufficient to determine infant HIV status up to 18 months of age or after breastfeeding cessation, when applicable. Infants with indeterminate test results without definitive laboratory confirmation were also excluded.

In practice, no mother-infant pairs were excluded due to missing essential records or insufficient follow-up; exclusions were limited to indeterminate infant HIV results without definitive laboratory confirmation (n = 51). A total of 255 mother-infant pairs were initially identified. Fifty-one pairs, corresponding to 20.0% of the initially identified records, were excluded because infant HIV status could not be definitively classified. The final analytic sample included 204 mother-infant pairs, comprising 68 cases and 136 controls.

Because the excluded pairs had indeterminate infant HIV status, they could not be validly classified as either cases or controls. Their exclusion may have introduced selection bias if indeterminate status was related to poorer follow-up, incomplete laboratory documentation, delayed testing or other characteristics associated with the PMTCT cascade. The likely direction of this bias is uncertain. If indeterminate pairs included infants at higher risk of infection because of weaker follow-up or poorer documentation, their exclusion may have led to underestimation of the burden of vertical transmission and attenuation of associations related to inadequate follow-up. Conversely, if most indeterminate infants were uninfected but incompletely documented, exclusion may have had a smaller effect on the estimated associations. This issue is addressed as a limitation.

Sampling

The study size was determined by the number of eligible mother-infant pairs available during the study period. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, as the study was based on retrospective clinical and laboratory records from a defined institutional cohort. All eligible cases with confirmed infant HIV infection were included, and two controls per case were selected, resulting in a 1:2 case-control ratio.

Eligible cases were identified among HIV-exposed infants diagnosed during the study period with confirmed HIV infection, according to the national diagnostic algorithm. Controls were selected from the same healthcare setting and consisted of HIV-exposed but uninfected infants, ensuring comparability of the underlying source population.

Controls were frequency-matched to cases by infant year of birth and maternal age group to minimise variability related to temporal changes in clinical protocols, service delivery, prevention strategies, and maternal age distribution. Matching was performed at the group level rather than at the individual level; therefore, no individual case-control pairs or matched sets were created. The matching variables were included as covariates in the multivariable logistic regression model.

Control Selection

For each case, two controls were selected from the population of HIV-positive mothers who delivered at Lucrécia Paim Maternity Hospital during the same study period. Controls were identified through delivery and paediatric follow-up registries and were required to be HIV-exposed but consistently HIV-negative throughout follow-up up to 18 months of age or until definitive HIV status was established, with complete clinical documentation.

All potentially eligible controls were first identified from the same institutional delivery and paediatric follow-up registries used to identify cases. Within this pool of eligible HIV-exposed but uninfected mother-infant pairs, controls were selected consecutively until the predefined 1:2 case-control ratio was reached, while preserving the frequency-matching structure by infant year of birth and maternal age group. No randomisation was applied, as the study was based on retrospective routine clinical records. This approach was used to ensure that cases and controls originated from the same underlying source population and were managed within the same healthcare setting.

Data Source and Collection Procedure

The data used in this study were extracted from maternal, obstetric, and neonatal clinical records, as well as laboratory records available at the hospital, covering all information relevant to the epidemiological, clinical, and therapeutic characterisation of the mothers and newborns included.

Bias and Quality Assurance

To minimise selection bias, cases and controls were drawn from the same base population (HIV-positive women who gave birth at the Lucrécia Paim Maternity Hospital during the study period), and controls were selected from the same records and follow-up pathways as cases. To reduce information bias, data extraction followed a standardised form based on predefined operational definitions. Key variables (HIV test results in infants, maternal viral load, timing of ART initiation, and mode of delivery) were verified against laboratory and obstetric records when available. The anonymised dataset was reviewed for internal consistency and implausible values prior to analysis.

Study Variables

Sociodemographic variables included maternal age (years), categorised as ≤25 years or >25 years; marital status, classified as with or without a partner; maternal education, categorised as with or without formal education, considering “educated” as having completed at least primary education; and maternal occupation, classified as working or non-working, with students, housewives and unemployed mothers included in the “non-working” category.

Infant year of birth was collected from maternity records and grouped into two birth periods: 2019–2021 and 2022–2024. This grouping was used to account for temporal variation in PMTCT implementation, clinical protocols, service delivery, and record quality during the study period, while avoiding overparameterisation of the multivariable model.

Obstetric variables included type of delivery (vaginal or caesarean); initiation of ART during pregnancy, classified according to national ART/PMTCT guidance as adequate when initiated by the 14th week of gestation and inadequate when initiated after that period or not initiated [10]; and maternal adherence to ART, classified as good when ≥95% of prescribed doses were taken according to clinical records, and poor when <95%, including treatment interruptions, dropouts, or repeated missed appointments.

Clinical and neonatal variables included the number of antenatal appointments, categorised as <4 or ≥4 according to WHO recommendations; the timing of maternal HIV diagnosis, classified as early when HIV infection was known before pregnancy or diagnosed during antenatal care before labour, and late when diagnosis occurred at labour/delivery, postpartum or during breastfeeding; maternal viral load, classified as suppressed or unsuppressed according to national thresholds; CD4 cell count, categorised as low or adequate based on national guidelines [10]; and the presence of associated maternal co-infections (yes/no).

Neonatal variables included the type of infant feeding during the first 6 months of life, categorised as exclusive or non-exclusive, according to national PMTCT guidelines [10]; birth weight, classified as ≥2,500 g (adequate) or <2,500 g (low birth weight); antiretroviral prophylaxis in the newborn (yes/no); and adequate paediatric follow-up, defined according to the national HIV testing and PMTCT follow-up algorithm as documented attendance of the HIV-exposed infant at paediatric follow-up services until final HIV status classification, including completion of recommended HIV diagnostic testing according to age and breastfeeding status [10].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 20.0 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive and bivariate analyses were performed to compare cases and controls. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson's chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

The reference categories in the bivariable and multivariable analyses were defined as the absence of exposure or intervention, or as the clinically highest-risk condition, where applicable. This approach allowed odds ratios (ORs) below one to be interpreted as associations with lower odds of transmission and ratiosratioORs above one as associations with higher odds of transmission, facilitating the clinical interpretation of associations between maternal, neonatal, and healthcare-related variables and mother-to-child transmission of HIV.

Variables were considered for inclusion in the multivariable analysis based on two criteria: (i) a p value of ≤0.20 in the bivariable analysis and (ii) recognised clinical relevance according to the scientific literature, even if not statistically significant in the bivariable analysis. Multivariable associations were estimated using unconditional binary logistic regression, and results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% CIs and corresponding p values. Given the frequency-matched case-control design, conditional logistic regression was not applied because matching was performed at the group level rather than through individual case-control pairs or matched sets. The matching variables, namely maternal age group and infant year of birth, were included as covariates in the multivariable model. For model stability, year of birth was entered as birth period, grouped as 2019–2021 and 2022–2024. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

An initial multivariable model including all selected variables was adjusted. Model optimisation was subsequently performed using a stepwise selection procedure (Wald), applied as an exploratory tool to identify variables with limited contribution to the model. Variables identified as non-contributory due to high p values, collinearity, or mediation by upstream determinants were evaluated in light of clinical plausibility and epidemiological relevance.

Based on this combined statistical and clinical assessment, a final multivariable model was constructed manually using the enter method, retaining variables independently associated with the outcome or considered essential confounders. The matching variables were forced into the final model and retained regardless of statistical significance. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and overall model performance was summarised using Nagelkerke’s pseudo-R2. No subgroup analyses, interaction tests, or sensitivity analyses were performed.

Missing Data

There were no missing data in the final analytic dataset (n = 204); therefore, no imputation was performed.

Patient and Public Involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research, as the study was based solely on retrospective clinical records.

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Data were obtained retrospectively from routine clinical records. Prior to analysis, all databases were fully anonymised, and no personal identifiers were accessed or retained. Given the retrospective design of the study, the exclusive use of secondary data, and the minimal risk to participants, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived. Institutional authorisation for data collection and ethical approval were obtained, with identifying details retained for review.

Marital status was also associated with transmission, as having a partner was associated with reduced odds of HIV transmission compared with having no partner (OR = 0.53; 95% CI: 0.28–0.98; p = 0.041). Similarly, maternal education was associated with lower odds of transmission, with women with formal education presenting lower odds of transmission compared with those without education (OR = 0.39; 95% CI: 0.21–0.73; p = 0.003). Maternal occupation was not significantly associated with the outcome (p = 0.165).

Regarding obstetric and treatment-related variables, caesarean delivery was associated with lower odds of vertical transmission compared with vaginal delivery (OR = 0.33; 95% CI: 0.17–0.63; p < 0.001). Adequate initiation of ART during pregnancy was strongly associated with reduced odds of transmission (OR = 0.33; 95% CI: 0.18–0.61; p < 0.001), as was good maternal adherence to ART (OR = 0.36; 95% CI: 0.20–0.65; p < 0.001).

Clinical maternal factors showed particularly strong associations. The presence of associated maternal coinfections was linked to markedly increased odds of mother-to-child transmission (OR = 3.58; 95% CI: 1.79–7.15; p < 0.001). In contrast, viral load suppression was strongly associated with lower odds of transmission, with substantially lower odds of transmission among women with suppressed viral load compared with those not suppressed (OR = 0.09; 95% CI: 0.04–0.18; p < 0.001). Likewise, an adequate CD4 cell count was associated with reduced odds of transmission (OR = 0.21; 95% CI: 0.11–0.40; p < 0.001).

In contrast, several neonatal and peripartum interventions were associated with lower odds of transmission. Neonatal antiretroviral prophylaxis was associated with significantly lower odds of HIV transmission compared with no prophylaxis (OR = 0.27; 95% CI: 0.11–0.67; p = 0.003). Similarly, administration of intrapartum ART was strongly associated with reduced odds of transmission (OR = 0.11; 95% CI: 0.05–0.25; p < 0.001). Antenatal care was also significantly associated with the outcome. Mothers who attended four or more antenatal consultations had substantially lower odds of transmitting HIV to their infants compared with those with fewer than four visits (OR = 0.27; 95% CI: 0.15–0.51; p < 0.001).

Regarding the timing of diagnosis, late maternal HIV diagnosis was associated with increased odds of mother-to-child transmission compared with early diagnosis (OR = 1.81; 95% CI: 1.00–3.26; p = 0.048), highlighting the importance of timely HIV testing during pregnancy. Infant feeding practices were also associated with transmission. Exclusive breastfeeding was associated with lower odds of transmission compared with non-exclusive feeding practices (OR = 0.34; 95% CI: 0.18–0.65; p < 0.001). In addition, adequate paediatric follow-up was strongly associated with lower odds of transmission, with markedly reduced odds of HIV transmission among infants who attended scheduled paediatric consultations compared with those without follow-up (OR = 0.15; 95% CI: 0.06–0.33; p < 0.001).

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis (Table 3), birth period was included as a matching variable and was not independently associated with mother-to-child transmission after adjustment (aOR = 1.22; 95% CI: 0.51–2.91; p = 0.661). Several factors remained independently associated with mother-to-child transmission of HIV after adjustment for birth period, maternal age group and other covariates. Maternal age above 25 years was associated with significantly lower odds of vertical transmission compared with younger maternal age (aOR = 0.17; 95% CI: 0.07–0.44; p < 0.001).

Maternal education also remained independently associated with the outcome, as women with formal education had lower odds of transmitting HIV to their infants compared with those without formal education (aOR = 0.28; 95% CI: 0.11–0.73; p = 0.010). With regard to maternal HIV care, adequate initiation of ART during pregnancy was independently associated with lower odds of transmission (aOR = 0.29; 95% CI: 0.12–0.70; p = 0.006). Antenatal care was also associated with lower odds of transmission, with attendance at four or more antenatal visits associated with reduced odds of transmission (aOR = 0.27; 95% CI: 0.11–0.66; p = 0.004).

Postnatal care factors demonstrated particularly strong associations. Adequate paediatric follow-up was associated with a marked reduction in the odds of mother-to-child transmission (aOR = 0.08; 95% CI: 0.02–0.27; p < 0.001).

Obstetric and virological factors remained significant after adjustment. Caesarean section was associated with lower odds of transmission compared with vaginal delivery (aOR = 0.13; 95% CI: 0.04–0.37; p < 0.001). Furthermore, suppression of maternal viral load showed the strongest independent association with the outcome, with substantially lower odds of transmission among women with suppressed viral load compared with those without suppression (aOR = 0.07; 95% CI: 0.03–0.20; p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this case-control study conducted at a referral unit in Angola, we identified several factors independently associated with lower odds of vertical HIV transmission, including suppression of maternal viral load (aOR = 0.07; 95% CI: 0.03–0.20), adequate paediatric follow-up (aOR = 0.08; 95% CI: 0.02–0.27), caesarean delivery (aOR = 0.13; 95% CI: 0.04–0.37), maternal age >25 years (aOR = 0.17; 95% CI: 0.07–0.44), formal schooling (aOR = 0.28; 95% CI: 0.11–0.73), adequate initiation of ART during pregnancy (aOR = 0.29; 95% CI: 0.12–0.70) and ≥4 antenatal consultations (aOR = 0.27; 95% CI: 0.11–0.66). These findings suggest that vertical transmission in this setting may be closely related to the continuity and performance of the PMTCT cascade, including early diagnosis, timely ART initiation, virological monitoring, retention in care and mother-infant follow-up. However, given the observational case-control design, these associations should not be interpreted as evidence of causality.

The factor most strongly associated with lower odds of vertical transmission was suppression of maternal viral load. This result is biologically plausible and is consistent with contemporary evidence that the risk of perinatal and postnatal transmission increases gradually with higher viral load levels, even in settings with high ART coverage [11]. In addition, recent studies show that virological failure during pregnancy/delivery is strongly associated with vertical transmission, underscoring the importance of virological monitoring and rapid intervention when signs of treatment failure are detected [12].

From a programmatic perspective, these findings are consistent with the view that ART availability alone may not fully address residual vertical transmission if barriers to adherence, timely viral load monitoring and linkage to appropriate clinical responses are not adequately addressed. Strategies such as adherence support, timely review of viral load results and risk stratification of mother-infant pairs may help identify dyads requiring closer follow-up, although the present study cannot determine the causal impact of such interventions [13].

Appropriate initiation of ART during pregnancy and ≥4 antenatal consultations remained associated with lower odds of vertical transmission after adjustment. These two indicators tend to capture both “time” (the earlier, the greater the likelihood of suppression before delivery) and “quality of care” (more opportunities to reinforce adherence, manage comorbidities, and plan for delivery). Recent evidence shows that indicators of prenatal quality are associated with a lower risk of adverse outcomes and vertical transmission of infections (including HIV) [14].

From a health policy perspective, the observed associations are compatible with the importance of strengthening early antenatal care uptake, reducing interruptions in care, and improving integration between prenatal care, ART services, laboratory monitoring and counselling. These implications should be interpreted as programmatic considerations informed by the observed associations, rather than as causal effects directly demonstrated by this study.

Adequate paediatric follow-up was strongly associated with lower odds of vertical transmission. This finding is consistent with the literature describing retention throughout the PMTCT cascade as a determinant of residual risk of infant infection: when there is loss to follow-up, there is an increase in failures in prophylaxis, early diagnosis (EID), infant feeding management, and timely identification of clinical events [15, 16].

Programmatically, this points to mother-baby retention interventions (e.g., active reminders, community linkage, joint appointment scheduling, “focal points” for high-risk dyads, and integration of EID with vaccination/growth consultation). Given that transmission can occur beyond delivery (particularly in contexts of breastfeeding, therapeutic interruptions, or virological failure), the observed association with paediatric follow-up may reflect the ability of continuous care to identify and address risk trajectories early.

Caesarean delivery was associated with lower odds of vertical transmission after adjustment. Although caesarean delivery may reduce intrapartum transmission in selected circumstances, particularly when maternal viral suppression is not achieved or virological status is unknown [17], this association should be interpreted with caution. In this retrospective observational study, mode of delivery may also reflect access to better obstetric care, timely clinical decision-making, referral pathways, operating theatre availability and closer coordination between obstetric and HIV services. Therefore, the observed association may partly represent the broader quality and organisation of care rather than an independent causal effect of caesarean delivery itself. The available data do not allow us to fully disentangle these mechanisms.

Maternal age of >25 years and formal education were associated with lower odds of vertical transmission. These factors may translate into greater health literacy, better ability to navigate the system, greater autonomy to maintain appointments and medication, and a lower probability of late diagnosis. Evidence suggests that adolescents and young women living with HIV face increased vulnerabilities (school disruption, stigma, economic dependence, unstable relationships, violence, barriers to accessing friendly services), with repercussions on retention and maternal and child outcomes [18].

These findings may indicate the importance of paying particular attention to younger women and those with low educational attainment in PMTCT services. Potential programmatic responses could include simplified and repeated counselling, adherence support focused on practical barriers, stigma reduction within health facilities and care models that minimise indirect costs, such as time and transport. However, these implications should be interpreted cautiously, as the study design does not establish causal effects.

Nevertheless, the clinical relevance of these variables remains. For example, sexually transmitted co-infections during pregnancy have been associated with an increased risk of vertical HIV transmission in recent population studies, reinforcing the importance of integrated screening and treatment [19]. Similarly, neonatal prophylaxis and intrapartum interventions remain essential components of the PMTCT package, particularly when virological suppression is not guaranteed.

This study has some limitations. First, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, as the study was based on the number of eligible mother-infant pairs available in retrospective clinical and laboratory records from a single referral maternity hospital. Although all eligible confirmed cases were included and a 1:2 case-control ratio was used to increase statistical efficiency, the number of cases remained limited. With 68 cases and several covariates included in the multivariable model, the analysis was close to the commonly used rule of thumb of approximately 10 events per variable. Therefore, some adjusted estimates may be imprecise and should be interpreted with caution.

Second, as this study was conducted in a single referral maternity hospital in Luanda, the findings may not be representative of all healthcare settings in Angola, particularly primary-level facilities, rural areas or provinces with different access to PMTCT services, laboratory monitoring and obstetric care. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as reflecting the experience of a referral maternity setting rather than national estimates.

Third, control selection was based on consecutive sampling from the pool of eligible HIV-exposed but uninfected mother-infant pairs identified in the same institutional records as cases. Although controls were drawn from the same source population and healthcare setting as cases, no randomisation was applied. Therefore, the possibility of residual selection bias cannot be fully excluded, particularly if the availability or completeness of records differed systematically between included and non-included mother-infant pairs.

Fourth, 51 mother-infant pairs, representing 20.0% of initially identified records, were excluded because infant HIV status remained indeterminate without definitive laboratory confirmation. The direction of bias introduced by this exclusion is uncertain. If indeterminate status was more common among infants with poorer follow-up, delayed testing or weaker documentation, exclusion may have underestimated the burden of vertical transmission and attenuated associations related to inadequate follow-up. However, because these pairs could not be validly classified as either cases or controls, their exclusion was necessary to preserve outcome validity.

Fifth, the study relied on retrospective clinical and laboratory records. In this context, the quality and completeness of viral load documentation may have varied across patients and over time. Because data collection covered a 5-year period, changes in clinical practice, laboratory access, documentation procedures or national thresholds may have affected how viral load suppression was recorded or classified. Although maternal viral load was categorised according to the information available in clinical and laboratory records, inconsistent documentation or classification of “suppressed” and “unsuppressed” viral load over time may have introduced exposure misclassification.

Additional limitations include possible misclassification of other key exposures and covariates, such as timing of ART initiation, adherence, infant feeding practices and paediatric follow-up, which were dependent on the accuracy of routine clinical records. Residual confounding may also be present, particularly because some variables, such as mode of delivery, may reflect access to care and risk-based clinical decision-making rather than isolated causal mechanisms. Finally, factors such as incident maternal infection during pregnancy or breastfeeding, stigma and social determinants are not always well captured in clinical databases but may strongly influence adherence and retention, which are critical dimensions for achieving elimination of vertical transmission [9, 20].

Conclusion

In this case-control study conducted at a referral maternity hospital in Luanda, several maternal, obstetric, and paediatric care-related factors were associated with lower odds of vertical HIV transmission. Maternal viral load suppression showed the strongest association, followed by adequate paediatric follow-up, caesarean delivery, maternal age above 25 years, formal education, adequate initiation of ART during pregnancy and attendance at four or more antenatal consultations. These findings suggest that gaps in the PMTCT cascade may be associated with residual vertical transmission in this setting. However, given the observational design and the use of retrospective clinical records, the associations observed should not be interpreted as causal. In particular, the association with caesarean delivery may partly reflect better access to obstetric care and risk-based clinical decision-making rather than a direct independent effect of delivery mode. Strengthening virological monitoring, treatment adherence support and retention of mother-infant pairs in care remains an important programmatic consideration for PMTCT services, but further prospective and multicentre studies are needed to confirm these findings and better clarify causal pathways.
