Occupational Pelvic and Chest-Level Radiation Exposure Among Interventional Echocardiographers During Percutaneous Structural Heart Interventions
Authors: Priscilla Wessly, Jeremiah Haines, Emily Jimenez, Lauren Howard, Brianna Madalinski, Renuka Jain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRapid expansion of transcatheter structural heart interventions has increased occupational radiation exposure for interventional echocardiographers (IEs).ObjectivesThe authors aimed to quantify real-time underlead pelvic and chest-level radiation exposure to IEs during left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO), transcatheter mitral valve intervention (TMVI), and transcatheter tricuspid valve intervention (TTVI); estimate implications for fetal safety; and identify independent predictors of higher chest dose.MethodsThis prospective, single-center study quantified radiation exposure during 118 LAAO, 59 TMVI, and 53 TTVI procedures from February 2025 to February 2026. Multivariable linear regression on log-transformed chest-level dose identified independent predictors. Comparisons used Kruskal-Wallis, Mann-Whitney U, and Spearman tests.ResultsPelvic dose was 0 μSv in 98.7% of cases; estimated dose for full pregnancy was ∼2.3 μSv, well below the recommended limit. Median raw chest-level exposure was 9.0 μSv (IQR: 3.2-21.0) for LAAO, 18.0 μSv (7.5-43.5) for TMVI, and 36.0 μSv (16-116) for TTVI (P < 0.001). IEs received 10- to 50-fold higher doses than sonographers (P < 0.001). Independent predictors of higher chest-level dose were higher log(dose-area product) (1.57; 95% CI: 1.29-1.91), head-of-bed positioning (2.17; 95% CI: 1.06-4.43), TMVI (2.27; 95% CI: 1.44-3.58), and TTVI (2.70; 95% CI: 1.35-5.40) vs LAAO, and half-shield-only use (3.78; 95% CI: 1.51-9.44) (all P ≤ 0.035).ConclusionsThe near absence of detectable under-lead pelvic radiation with standard lead apparel and shielding provides strong reassurance regarding reproductive safety. IEs sustain higher chest-level radiation exposure during TMVI and TTVI than LAAO. Head-of-bed positioning and half-shield-only use are key modifiable drivers, underscoring the need for standardized full-shielding protocols and positional awareness.

The volume of transcatheter structural heart interventions (SHIs) is expanding rapidly, driven by device advancements, advanced imaging, and the rising prevalence of valvular heart disease in an aging population. This growth has underscored the key role of the interventional echocardiographer (IE) in providing real-time imaging guidance during these increasingly complex procedures.

IEs work in close proximity to the fluoroscopy C-arm, placing them in the path of scatter radiation, the dominant source of occupational exposure in the cardiac catheterization laboratory. Prior studies have consistently demonstrated that IEs receive substantially higher radiation doses than interventional cardiologists, largely due to this proximity.1 Monte Carlo simulations have further highlighted elevated absorbed doses to the waist and lower body from table-edge scatter, with rates varying markedly by C-arm projection.2

Cumulative occupational radiation exposure carries well-documented risks, including posterior subcapsular cataracts, malignancies (particularly of the brain, neck, and breast), reproductive complications, genetic effects, and accelerated atherosclerosis.1,3,4 Of particular concern is the uncertainty regarding fetal and reproductive impacts for pregnant or potentially pregnant IEs, given high pelvic scatter radiation. This uncertainty affects many IEs planning to start a family or during pregnancy, often influencing career and family decisions.5

With the rapid growth in SHI volumes and increasing procedural complexity, especially tricuspid interventions that often require steeper C-arm angulation and longer fluoroscopy times, precise quantification of occupational radiation risk to IEs is critical. Prior reports have largely focused on left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) and transcatheter mitral valve intervention (TMVI) procedures. Data on radiation exposure to IEs during transcatheter tricuspid valve interventions (TTVIs) and quantification of pelvic exposure relevant to reproductive safety are lacking.1

This prospective study quantified real-time occupational radiation exposure among IEs and sonographers during percutaneous SHIs at a high-volume U.S. center. We aimed to: 1) quantify under-lead pelvic radiation doses to IEs and estimate cumulative fetal radiation dose during pregnancy; 2) characterize chest-level radiation doses across LAAO, TMVI, and TTVI; 3) identify procedural, positional, and shielding determinants of higher exposure; and 4) directly compare radiation exposure between IEs and sonographers.

Methods

Study design and oversight

This prospective observational study quantified real-time occupational radiation exposure to IEs during percutaneous SHIs. The Wake Forest School of Medicine Institutional Review Board approved the protocol. A waiver of signed consent and health insurance portability and accountability act authorization was granted. Although signed consent was not required, an information sheet was provided to all participating IEs and sonographers, who then provided verbal consent. Consecutive procedures were enrolled at a single high-volume U.S. center from February 2025 through February 2026.

Study population and procedures

All SHIs requiring transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) guidance were eligible. Procedures included LAAO (plug-like or disc-and-lobe devices), TMVI (mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair, transcatheter mitral valve replacement (TMVR), and valve-in-valve TMVR), and TTVI (edge-to-edge repair and transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement). Participation was voluntary. All procedures were performed in a hybrid catheterization laboratory equipped with single-plane fluoroscopy. A GE Allia IGS 5 system (GE HealthCare, Chicago, Illinois) was used for the TMVI and TTVI procedures, and a GE Innova IGS 530 system was used for the LAAO and transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) procedures. IEs wore standard 0.5 mm lead apparel (double-sided skirt and apron in most cases; one IE used a single-sided frontal apron) plus a thyroid collar. A mobile lead-acrylic shield (0.5 mm lead equivalent full shield, half shield, height-adjustable full shield or a combination of shields) was positioned between the patient and the IE in every case. Interventional cardiologists used only ceiling-suspended 0.5 mm lead-equivalent MAVIG (Munich, Germany) shields.

Sonographer radiation exposure was measured during the same TEE-guided procedures and during TAVR cases that involved transthoracic echocardiography.

Radiation dosimetry

Real-time personal radiation exposure was measured using the RaySafe i3 system (Unfors RaySafe AB, Billdal, Sweden), which reports personal dose equivalent Hp(10) in microsieverts (μSv). Data were retrieved after the procedure via RaySafe Dose Viewer software.

Chest-level exposure was recorded with a dosimeter worn on the upper arm closest to the C-arm to capture upper-body scatter during TEE probe manipulation. This placement measures local personal dose equivalent (Hp10) at the upper arm. Pelvic exposure (under lead) was measured with a separate dosimeter placed under the lead apron in the suprapubic region. Fluoroscopy time, air kerma, and dose-area product (DAP) were automatically captured by the fluoroscopy system.

Statistical analysis

Baseline patient, procedural, and exposure characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR), depending on distribution (assessed via Shapiro-Wilk test and histograms). Categorical variables are reported as count (percentage).

Comparisons across procedure types (LAAO, TMVI, TTVI) used Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables. Because under-lead pelvic dose was 0 μSv in 98.7% of procedures, it was summarized descriptively without multivariable modeling. A conservative estimate of cumulative fetal exposure was calculated from the observed nonzero rate and institutional monthly procedural volume.

Univariable associations with raw IE chest-level radiation dose were assessed using Spearman rank correlation for continuous predictors (reported as ρ with overall median and IQR) and Mann-Whitney U tests for binary or grouped predictors (reported as median [IQR] in exposed vs reference groups). All P values were 2-sided, with missing values excluded pairwise.

Because chest-level radiation dose was markedly right-skewed with a high proportion of low values, univariable comparisons were performed using nonparametric tests (Kruskal-Wallis, Mann-Whitney U, and Spearman rank correlation) that directly report medians and ranks. Multivariable linear regression was performed on the natural log-transformed chest-level radiation dose (ln[chest-level dose + 0.01 μSv]).6 To address right-skewness and zero/low values, a small constant (+0.01) was added to both chest radiation dose (μSv) and DAP (mGy·cm2) before natural log transformation of each. This constant was chosen because it was substantially smaller than the dosimeter’s measurement precision and well below the 25th percentile of observed chest-level doses. Covariates were selected a priori and forced into the model: log(DAP) per unit increase (to adjust for differences in total radiation output), IE position at the head of bed, half shield (vs adjustable full/combination shielding only), ICE use, steep RAO, and procedure type (TMVI and TTVI vs LAAO as reference). Exponentiated coefficients are reported as adjusted fold changes in geometric mean chest dose with 95% CI. Models were fit on complete cases (n = 230). Model fit was assessed with R2, adjusted R2, and variance inflation factors. A sensitivity analysis was performed entering only variables with univariable P ≤ 0.20. Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted using alternative constants for the log transformation (+0.001 μSv and +0.1 μSv). Residual plots (residuals vs fitted values, normal Q-Q plot, and histogram) were inspected to evaluate model assumptions.

Direct comparisons between IEs and sonographers performing the same procedures were performed using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Sonographer chest radiation exposure was compared across procedure groups (LAAO, TMVI, TTVI, and TAVR) using Kruskal-Wallis tests. DAP-normalized and raw chest-level doses were compared across C-arm angulation categories using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Monthly trends in median normalized exposure by procedure type were assessed with Spearman rank correlation. Spearman rank correlation was used to assess the relationship between fluoroscopy time and IE chest-level radiation dose, overall and by procedure type.

Analyses were performed using Python 3.13 (pandas, statsmodels, SciPy, seaborn, matplotlib). Two-sided P ≤ 0.05 was considered significant; no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied given the exploratory design.

Results

Patient and procedural characteristics

A total of 230 procedures (118 LAAO, 59 TMVI, and 53 TTVI) were included. Patient age (mean age 77.9 ± 8.3 years, P = 0.132) and sex (51.3% female, P = 0.096) were comparable across groups. Median body mass index was significantly higher in LAAO (P < 0.001). TTVI showed the longest fluoroscopy time and highest DAP (P < 0.001 and P = 0.036, respectively), while ICE use was almost exclusive to TTVI (41.5%; P < 0.001). Pelvic and fetal dose estimates

Under-lead pelvic dose was 0 μSv in 227 of 230 procedures (98.7%). Detectable pelvic dose occurred in only 3 procedures (1.3%), each measuring 1 μSv. Given the highly zero-inflated distribution and very low event rate, no modeling was performed. A conservative estimate of cumulative fetal exposure, based on the observed nonzero rate (1.3%) and assuming continued use of standard lead apparel, full shielding, and side-of-bed positioning, yielded ∼2.3 μSv over a 9-month period. Univariable predictors of IE chest-level radiation dose

Higher raw chest-level dose was associated with higher DAP (ρ = 0.348, P < 0.001), IE position at the head of the bed (P = 0.003), ICE use (P < 0.001), steep RAO (P < 0.001), and half-shield-only use (P = 0.006). Doses were significantly higher in TMVI and TTVI than in LAAO (P = 0.001 and P < 0.001, respectively). Body mass index and use of a disposable radiation-absorbent drape were not associated (P = 0.437; P = 0.854, respectively) (Supplemental Table 1). Multivariable predictors of IE chest-level radiation dose

In the multivariable model (n = 230 complete cases, R2 = 0.297, adjusted R2 = 0.275), variance inflation factors for all covariates ranged from 1.07 to 3.18, indicating no concerning multicollinearity. Higher log(DAP) per unit was associated with a 1.57-fold higher dose (95% CI: 1.29-1.91; P < 0.001). IE positioning at the head of the bed (adjusted fold change 2.17; 95% CI: 1.06-4.43; P = 0.035, n = 17) was independently associated with significantly higher doses. Compared with LAAO, both TMVI (adjusted fold change 2.27; 95% CI: 1.44-3.58; P < 0.001) and TTVI (adjusted fold change 2.70; 95% CI: 1.35-5.40; P = 0.005) also remained independent predictors of increased chest-level radiation exposure. Use of a half-shield only (vs adjustable full/combination shielding) was associated with a substantially higher chest-level radiation dose (adjusted fold change 3.78; 95% CI: 1.51-9.44; P = 0.005). Neither ICE use (adjusted fold change 1.36; 95% CI: 0.66-2.80; P = 0.403) nor steep RAO (adjusted fold change 1.36; 95% CI: 0.78-2.38; P = 0.276) showed independent associations after adjustment (Figure 5). The adjusted fold changes represent effects on the geometric mean chest-level dose. Results of the multivariable model were consistent in direction and magnitude with the univariable nonparametric findings. A sensitivity analysis using only variables with univariable P ≤ 0.20 yielded identical results (Supplemental Table 2). Discussion

This prospective observational study of 230 consecutive SHIs provides the largest and most contemporary assessment of occupational radiation exposure to IEs to date, highlighting implications for fetal radiation safety. The principal finding is the near absence of detectable under-lead pelvic radiation. Pelvic dose was undetectable (0 μSv) in 98.7% of 230 procedures, resulting in an estimated fetal dose of only 2.3 μSv over an entire 9-month pregnancy, well below the International Commission on Radiological Protection-recommended occupational limit of 1 mSv (1,000 μSv). Chest-level exposure remained procedure-dependent and higher than in sonographers: the median raw IE chest radiation dose increased significantly from LAAO (9.0 μSv) to TMVI (18.0 μSv) to TTVI (36.0 μSv). Independent predictors of higher chest-level dose included log(DAP), head-of-bed positioning, half-shield-only configuration, and TMVI or TTVI procedure type. This is the first study to include TTVI, report both raw and DAP-normalized exposure with full multivariable modeling, and provide robust reassurance on fetal radiation safety in a contemporary U.S. cohort, addressing critical gaps in radiation data for complex tricuspid valve interventions and fetal radiation safety.

Baseline characteristics and procedure-specific exposure

The cohort reflected a typical SHI population at a high-volume center: older age (mean 77.9 years), a balanced sex distribution (51.3% female), and a high body mass index (median 27.9 kg/m2). Body mass index was higher in LAAO cases (P < 0.001) but was not associated with higher radiation doses in univariable analysis (P = 0.437), suggesting procedural factors override the potential contribution of body mass index to increased scatter radiation. Fluoroscopy time and DAP were significantly higher in TTVI than in LAAO (P < 0.001 and P = 0.036), consistent with greater procedural complexity, steeper RAO angulation, and ICE use (P < 0.001).

Pelvic radiation and fetal implications

A major contribution of the present work is the first prospective quantification of under-lead pelvic exposure in IEs, a critical gap previously identified in the literature.1,5 Importantly, no detectable pelvic dose occurred in any case when full shielding was used in combination with side-of-bed positioning—the configuration employed in the large majority of procedures. Detectable pelvic dose (1 μSv) was limited to 3 procedures (1.3%): 2 with half-shield-only use at the side-of-bed position and 1 with combination shielding at the head of the bed during a prolonged TMVR procedure (fluoroscopy time 40.6 min). The estimated cumulative fetal dose of ∼2.3 μSv assumes continued adherence to standard lead apparel, full shielding, and side-of-bed positioning.

Monte Carlo simulations have previously demonstrated high radiation exposure to the waist and lower body of IEs from table-edge scatter, particularly during steep RAO projections.2 Studies have emphasized the absence of such data and its implications for reproductive safety, workforce equity, and retention of female IEs.1,5 Wessly et al documented widespread reproductive health concerns among IEs, reported by 71% overall (66% male, 80% female). Among female IEs, 56% reported that their role affected preconception health and pregnancy planning, with inconsistent use of fetal monitoring or lower-body protection use during pregnancy.5

Our study provides reassurance that adherence to standard lead apparel in addition to appropriate shielding effectively attenuates pelvic scatter in contemporary SHIs. These data fill a critical gap left by prior studies and provide reassurance to IEs irrespective of sex.

Chest-Level radiation exposure and predictors

Our results (Table 2, Figure 2, Central Illustration) extend the emerging literature on radiation exposure for IEs. Prior studies have consistently shown that IEs frequently receive radiation doses exceeding those of interventional cardiologists, with exposure varying by C-arm angulation and echocardiography modality.6, 7, 8, 9 This study is, to our knowledge, the first to quantify chest-level radiation exposure using dosimetry placed on the upper arm closest to the C-arm, specifically to capture scatter during TEE probe manipulation. However, this anatomic site does not represent whole-body effective dose or organ-specific doses (eg, ocular lens, thyroid, or hands) and therefore should not be directly compared with regulatory whole-body effective dose limits. Corrigan et al reported markedly higher hand/finger doses in IEs (mean 416 μSv overall; 676 μSv during TEE-guided procedures) using a dosimeter on the ring finger closest to the C-arm.10 Although our median LAAO chest-level dose of 9.0 μSv is numerically similar to that reported by McNamara et al, that study measured head-level radiation exposure with dosimeters placed on the glasses or thyroid collar. The similarity is therefore likely coincidental given the different anatomic sites.8 Importantly, inclusion of more complex valve interventions revealed a clear escalation: TMVI and TTVI produced approximately 4-fold higher raw chest-level doses than LAAO and remained independently associated with 2.27-fold and 2.70-fold higher exposure after multivariable adjustment, respectively. This gradient is likely driven by higher DAP than in LAAO, reflecting the greater procedural complexity and heavy dependence on steep RAO projections. Although DAP-normalized doses help isolate procedural geometry effects, raw chest doses remain clinically relevant. Median raw chest-level doses during TMVI (18.0 μSv) and TTVI (36.0 μSv) exceeded the ∼11 μSv mean chest dose associated with transient increases in DNA damage markers (phosphorylated ataxia telangiectasia mutated [pATM] and phosphorylated histone H2AX [γ-H2AX]) in prior fluoroscopy studies.11 This highlights the value of reporting raw doses in addition to DAP-normalized values. However, the clinical significance of these transient changes and the long-term cumulative effects of repeated occupational exposure remain unknown.

Multivariable modeling (Figure 5) identified log(DAP) as the strongest predictor (adjusted fold change 1.57, P < 0.001), reflecting that total radiation output directly drives scatter exposure, as DAP encompasses beam intensity and field size. This aligns with prior studies that linked higher DAP to elevated IE doses during SHIs.2,6

Head-of-bed IE positioning was associated with approximately 2-fold radiation exposure (n = 17; 95% CI: 1.06-4.43; P = 0.035), mechanistically because the IE stands directly over the scatter source, with exposure exacerbated by limited shielding space. Head-of-bed positioning was more common in TTVI (13.2%) and TMVI (10.2%) than in LAAO (3.4%; P = 0.049), likely selected in extreme steep RAO cases where TEE probe manipulation was difficult or based on IE preference. Compared with LAAO, TMVI (2.27-fold, P < 0.001) and TTVI (2.70-fold, P = 0.005) remained independent predictors of increased chest-level radiation exposure reflecting procedural complexity, higher DAP, and RAO-dominant views.

Half-shield-only use was the strongest modifiable risk factor, independently associated with 3.78-fold higher chest-level radiation dose (95% CI: 1.51-9.44, P = 0.005) compared with adjustable full/combination shielding. This represents the largest effect size in the model, highlighting that consistent implementation of full shielding is one of the most effective modifiable strategies to reduce occupational exposure for IEs. IEs should remain aware of the elevated radiation risk associated with head-of-bed positioning and prioritize consistent use of full shielding to reduce scatter. Full shields, although effective, impede TEE probe manipulation, causing musculoskeletal strain. Future shielding innovations therefore must balance maximal radiation protection with maneuverability to avoid musculoskeletal strain and injury.

The lack of a protective effect from disposable radiation pad drapes (P = 0.484) contrasts with findings in coronary literature and suggests that disposable drapes alone may be insufficient for the unique scatter geometry faced by IEs during SHIs.12 Additional dedicated studies are needed.

Interestingly, steep RAO was associated with higher chest-level doses in univariable analysis (P < 0.001) and yielded the highest raw/normalized doses (Figure 4), consistent with prior studies.2,6 Similarly, ICE use showed a significant univariable association with higher chest-level dose (P < 0.001). However, both lost independent significance after multivariable adjustment (steep RAO P = 0.276 and ICE use P = 0.403). This attenuation is explained by high collinearity with TTVI procedures, which used steep RAO in most cases and ICE exclusively. This highlights that procedure type, through its associated geometry and higher DAP, is the dominant driver of higher radiation exposure, with steep RAO and ICE use serving as proxies rather than independent risk factors.

The multivariable model explained approximately 30% of the variance in chest-level radiation dose. The remaining ∼70% variance is likely attributable to unmeasured operator-specific factors, including individual TEE probe handling technique, exact shield geometry, IE positioning per case, table height, and variations in fluoroscopy pulse rate and settings. Consistent with the exploratory nature of the study, the model prioritized actionable, a priori-selected predictors rather than exhaustive explanatory power.

Fluoroscopy time, trend, and C-arm projections

Analysis of chest-level dose vs fluoroscopy time (Figure 6) further clarifies the mechanistic drivers. Raw chest-level dose correlated moderately with fluoroscopy time overall (P < 0.001), with the strongest associations in TMVI (ρ = 0.43) and TTVI (ρ = 0.52). After DAP normalization, however, the correlation weakened substantially (P = 0.004) and lost significance within procedure types. This indicates that procedural radiation output (higher DAP), rather than fluroscopy duration alone, drives the increased radiation exposure in TTVI and TMVI. Temporal trends in normalized exposure (Supplemental Figure 2) revealed additional insights. Over the 13-month study period, median DAP-normalized chest-level dose showed a significant downward trend for LAAO (P = 0.026), consistent with accumulating operator experience and IE radiation safety awareness. However, this trend could also reflect changes in shielding practices, operator rotation, or equipment calibration during the study period. The study cannot distinguish among these possibilities. No significant trends were observed for the more complex TMVI (P = 0.958) or TTVI (P = 0.209) procedures, likely reflecting greater complexity, case-to-case variability, steep RAO C-arm angulations, and procedural demands.

IE vs sonographer exposure

IEs received approximately 10- to 50-fold radiation doses (paired P < 0.001) (Figure 3), mechanistically from closer proximity to the C-arm, confirming prior data.8 Sonographer chest doses were markedly lower overall and were highest during TAVR procedures (median 10 μSv) compared with LAAO, TMVI, and TTVI (typically 0-2 μSv). Notably, the median sonographer chest-level radiation dose during TAVR exceeded the median IE chest-level dose during LAAO (9 μSv). This unexpected finding highlights an important and readily modifiable occupational radiation risk for sonographers, who frequently perform preprocedure, intraprocedure, and postprocedure transthoracic echocardiography scanning in close proximity to the C-arm, often without dedicated shielding. Pausing fluoroscopy during transthoracic echocardiography scanning is a simple, immediately actionable ALARA (as low as reasonably achievable) intervention that should be routinely implemented to minimize unnecessary scatter radiation exposure to sonographers.

Study Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This was a single-center study, and generalizability may be influenced by local equipment, operator preferences, and cardiac catheterization laboratory configuration. Two different single-plane fluoroscopy systems were used depending on procedure type. Although a newer fluoroscopy system was used for TMVI and TTVI procedures and an older system for LAAO and TAVR, the observed differences in radiation exposure are most likely explained by procedural complexity, longer fluoroscopy times, and steeper C-arm angulations required for mitral and tricuspid interventions rather than by the imaging systems themselves. Real-time dosimetry provided granular procedural data but did not capture ocular, thyroid, or hand/finger exposure. Upper-arm placement at chest level (the first reported in any SHI IE study) captured scatter during TEE probe manipulation but may have underestimated exposure at other anatomic sites. At our institution, IEs typically use the right arm for TEE probe manipulation (the same arm used for dosimeter) when positioned at the head of the bed and the left arm when positioned at the side of the bed. This practice, as well as operator handedness at other centers, may influence radiation exposure patterns.

The multivariable model explained 29.7% of variance (R2 = 0.297), indicating that unmeasured factors contributed to residual variance. Consistent with the exploratory nature of the study, the model prioritized actionable, a priori-selected predictors rather than exhaustive explanatory power. Univariable analyses reported medians, whereas the multivariable model estimates adjusted geometric means; however, the direction, magnitude, and statistical significance of key predictors were highly consistent across both approaches. Mild deviation from residual normality was noted in the lower tail, which is common with log-transformed occupational radiation dose data. The relatively small number of head-of-bed cases (n = 17) yielded imprecise estimates with a wide CI (1.06-4.43). Consequently, the large effect size observed for this predictor should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, the procedures in this study were performed using single-plane fluoroscopy. The expanding use of electrosurgical techniques and certain newer TMVR devices often requires biplane fluoroscopy, which can make effective shielding more challenging for the IE due to their proximity to the C-arm. This represents an important area for future investigation. Multiple statistical comparisons were performed. Given the exploratory nature of the study, no adjustment for multiple testing was applied, and the possibility of type I error cannot be excluded. Personal dosimetry is routine clinical practice in our laboratory. Although IEs were aware that data were being collected for this study, they did not have access to real-time radiation dose readings during procedures. Thus, any potential Hawthorne effect is expected to have been minimal. Finally, determining long-term cumulative exposure and career-level risk requires badge monitoring over years, which was beyond the scope of this procedure-level analysis.

Conclusions

IEs face procedure-dependent radiation exposure during transcatheter SHIs. Chest-level radiation doses remain substantially higher in TMVI and TTVI despite currently available shielding. Head-of-bed positioning and half-shield use are key modifiable drivers of increased exposure. Most importantly, the near absence of detectable pelvic radiation provides reassurance regarding reproductive safety, addressing a previously unaddressed concern with major implications for workforce diversity and retention.
