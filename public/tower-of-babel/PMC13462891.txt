Novel Detection Marker of Xanthomonas fragariae from Strawberry Plants Using SYBR Green Real-Time PCR
Authors: Han Suk Choi, Sujin Ki, Yong Hwan Lee, Hyeonheui Ham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Angular leaf spot, caused by the phytopathogenic bacterium Xanthomonas fragariae, results in considerable yield losses in strawberry production. This pathogen is designated as a quarantine pest in several countries, including South Korea. Therefore, early identification of this pathogen in young plants is essential for preventing disease spread and ensuring rapid eradication. However, intraspecific genetic variation among isolates from different geographic origins imposes challenges for the accurate diagnosis of X. fragariae. In this study, we developed a novel gene marker and primer set (XF-212F/R) through comparative genomic analysis of X. fragariae strains and related bacterial species for pathogen-specific detection. As a result, the XF-212F/R primer set amplified only X. fragariae strains without cross-reactivity to other Xanthomonas spp. or related bacteria. The detection limits of the SYBR Green real-time PCR assay were 1.41 × 102 plasmid copies/μL, 500 fg of genomic DNA, and 1.52 × 103 CFU/mL of bacterial cells. The assay also accurately detected the pathogen from cells directly extracted from infected strawberry leaves, enabling rapid detection without the need for DNA extraction. This diagnostic method showed improved coverage for X. fragariae strains and is suitable for early detection and disease surveillance in strawberry plants.

Strawberry (Fragaria × ananassa Duch.) is a major fruit crop cultivated worldwide across a broad range of climates and regions, with global production exceeding 10.7 million tons in 2024 (FAO, 2024). However, bacterial diseases continue to pose serious threats to strawberry cultivation. Among these, angular leaf spot (ALS), caused by Xanthomonas fragariae, can severely reduce yield, with losses of up to 75% in Wisconsin, USA (Epstein, 1966), and 45% in Jinju, South Korea, in 2012 (Yoon et al., 2016). The disease weakens plant vigor and degrades fruit quality, initially producing water-soaked lesions on the lower leaf surface. As the infection advances, the lesions enlarge, turn reddish-brown on the upper surface, and spread through the vascular tissues to other leaves, calyces, and crowns (Kastelein et al., 2014; Qiu et al., 2023; Wang and Turechek, 2020; Wang et al., 2018; Wei et al., 2024a).

X. fragariae was first reported in Minnesota, USA, in 1962, and has since been detected in New Zealand, Australia, Europe, Africa, the Americas, and Asia (EPPO, 2025a). The European and Mediterranean Plant Protection Organization (EPPO) listed X. fragariae as an A2 quarantine pathogen in 1978 (Kastelein et al., 2018). It currently classifies X. fragariae as a quarantine pest in many countries, including Korea, Morocco, Tunisia, Mexico, China, Norway, Israel, and New Zealand (EPPO, 2025b). The international spread of X. fragariae is thought to occur mainly through ALS-infected nursery stock, as the bacterium can survive asymptomatically in host plants (Turechek et al., 2013). Moreover, during asexual reproduction via stolons, X. fragariae can be transmitted to daughter plants, accelerating disease dissemination. Therefore, early detection and elimination of infected plants at the nursery stage, along with the propagation of disease-free plantlets, are essential, since ALS control becomes difficult once outbreaks occur.

Molecular diagnostic tools have become indispensable for detecting X. fragariae, as the pathogen often spreads through nursery propagation, and visual inspection alone is unreliable for identifying systemic infections (Turechek et al., 2008). Species-specific assays based on conventional polymerase chain reaction (PCR), nested PCR, and real-time PCR are widely used because of their high sensitivity, specificity, and practicality (Ham et al., 2024; Patel et al., 2022; Song et al., 2024; Venbrux et al., 2023). Diagnostic primers have been designed from several X. fragariae genomic loci, including hrp (Roberts et al., 1996; Stöger and Ruppitsch, 2004), gyrB (Weller et al., 2007), and RAPD-derived regions (Pooler et al., 1996; Turechek et al., 2008; Vandroemme et al., 2008; Wang and Turechek, 2020). However, recent reports describing genetically distinct Chinese X. fragariae strains, now recognized as a new subspecies (Wei et al., 2024b), indicate that the specificity of existing diagnostic markers may be reduced for these variants. Notably, the Chinese strains YL19, SHQP01, and YLX21 cause severe dry cavity rot symptoms on the strawberry crown, which are absent in other populations (Song et al., 2021; Wei et al., 2024b).

To address this limitation, the development of novel, pan-specific markers is crucial for the early detection of X. fragariae, enabling the rapid removal of infected plants and preventing the spread of newly emerging subspecies. Comparative genomics provides a systematic approach to identifying such markers through genome-wide analyses, allowing in silico evaluation of diagnostic specificity (Balone et al., 2024; Kim, 2025). A recent study developed a species-specific primer based on comparative genomic analysis of 81 X. fragariae genomes in the NCBI database (Wang et al., 2025); however, nucleotide mismatches remain at the primer-binding sites of newly reported subspecies.

Here, we describe the development of a new molecular marker derived from a pan-specific X. fragariae gene identified through comparative genomics and validated for specificity using in silico and in vitro analyses. Compared with previously reported primers, the XF-212F/R primer set exhibits improved specificity and is suitable for various PCR-based applications for the early diagnosis of ALS in strawberry plants.

Materials and Methods

Bacterial strains and DNA isolation

X. fragariae and other bacterial species evaluated in this study are listed in Table 1. X. fragariae strains (KACC 18320, KACC 18321, and KACC 18322) were cultured on sucrose peptone agar (SPA; sucrose, 20 g; peptone, 5 g; K2HPO4, 0.5 g; MgSO4·7H2O, 0.25 g; agar, 15 g/L; pH 7.2–7.4) for 4 days at 28°C. Other Xanthomonas, Pseudomonas, and Pectobacterium spp. were grown on tryptic soy agar (Difco, Detroit, MI, USA) at 28°C for 2 days. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from bacterial cultures using the Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Finally, DNA concentration and purity were measured with a Nanophotometer NP80 (Implen, Munich, Germany).

Specific gene and primer determination

Whole-genome sequences for the 39 currently available Xanthomonas reference genomes were retrieved from the NCBI GenBank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/) (Supplementary Table 1). Coding sequences (CDSs) from X. fragariae PD885 (GenBank accession: GCA_900183975.1) were compared with those from other Xanthomonas genomes using BLASTx (E-value = 1e-10) to identify loci unique to X. fragariae. For the single copy gene selection, we identified genes with a unique hit count of one from the comparative BLASTx analysis. Finally, a gene encoding a hypothetical protein (Protein ID: WP_145954069.1) was selected as the target region after excluding mobile genetic elements and confirming its species-specificity through BLASTn homology analysis. Primers were designed using the NCBI Primer3 tool with reverse e-PCR parameters (n = 2, g = 1) and an expected product size of 95–310 bp. The predicted amplicon was validated by BLASTn against the NCBI nucleotide database, optimized for somewhat similar sequences while excluding uncultured or environmental samples. Primer specificity and sensitivity were confirmed through conventional PCR and real-time PCR.

Gene fragment synthesis of X. fragariae strain YLX21

A gene fragment including the amplicon sequence of X. fragariae strain YLX21 (GenBank accession: GCA_026967555.1, location = 2763820–2764229) was synthesized as a custom service by Bionics Corp. (Seoul, Korea) to obtain a DNA template for PCR. Briefly, oligonucleotides were designed and synthesized to amplify the target region, which was then amplified using PrimeSTAR® HS DNA polymerase (R010A; TaKaRa Bio, Kusatsu, Japan). The 410 bp amplicon was cloned into the pUC57-Amp vector and transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α. Plasmid DNA was isolated using the NICSROprep™ Plasmid DNA Miniprep S&V Kit (Bionics), and PCR products were purified with the NICSROprep™ Universal Gel & PCR Clean-up Kit (Bionics). The synthesized target sequence was verified by Sanger sequencing (Bionics).

Specificity tests

Twenty-two X. fragariae and related bacterial gDNA samples (three target X. fragariae strains, one synthetic DNA fragment of YLX21, and 18 closely related species) were used as templates for specificity testing. Conventional PCR was performed on a C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) in 25 μL reaction volumes containing 5× buffer, 10 mM dNTP mix, 25 mM MgCl2, 10 pmol of each primer (XF-212F: 5′-AGC CGA CTG TGC GTG CGA GGT GA-3′, XF-212R: 5′-TAG GAG GCA GAG AGA TAC AGA TTC-3′), 5 U/μL DNA polymerase (GoTaq® Flexi DNA Polymerase; Promega), and 5 ng/μL of DNA template. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min; 35 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 62°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 60 s; and a final extension at 72°C for 60 s. PCR products were stained with LoadingStar (Dynebio, Seoul, Korea) and separated on 1% agarose gels to confirm product size.

Real-time PCR was performed using the same DNA templates as those utilized in conventional PCR on a CFX Opus 96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad). Each 20 μL reaction contained 10 μL of PowerUp™ SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA), 10 pmol of each primer, and 1 μL of 5 ng/μL template. Amplification conditions included an initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 20 s and 62°C for 30 s. Melting-curve analysis was performed from 65°C to 95°C with 0.5°C increments to confirm specificity.

Sensitivity tests

Xanthomonas fragariae strain KACC 18320 was used as the template to evaluate the sensitivity of the XF-212F/R primer set. To construct the cloned DNA, the 212 bp amplicon was purified using the AccuPrep PCR Purification Kit (Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea), ligated into the pGEM-T Easy Vector (Promega), and transformed into competent Escherichia coli DH5α cells by heat shock. White colonies were selected on SPA medium containing X-Gal/IPTG solution (GenDEPOT, Houston, TX, USA) with 50 μg/mL ampicillin. Plasmid DNA was purified using the GeneAll Hybrid-Q™ Plasmid RapidPrep Kit (GeneAll, Seoul, Korea).

Detection from infected strawberry plants

To obtain diseased plant material, X. fragariae (KACC 18320, 18321 and 18322) cells were adjusted to an OD600 of 0.1 in sterilized distilled water (SDW). A 10 μL aliquot of the bacterial suspension or SDW (negative control) was inoculated onto the lower leaf surfaces of 2-week-old strawberry seedlings (cv. Seolhyang). The inoculated plants were incubated in a growth chamber at 25°C, with up to 95% relative humidity and a 12-h light/12-h dark photoperiod for 10 days. Symptomatic and asymptomatic leaves were surface-sterilized in 70% ethanol to eliminate surface contaminants and epiphytic microorganisms, and five leaf discs (0.5 cm × 0.5 cm) were collected using a sterile scalpel. Next, these discs were immersed in 500 μL SDW for 30 min, and 50 μL of extracts were serially 10-fold diluted and spread onto SPA medium for colony counting. The remaining 400 μL of the suspension was processed for total gDNA extraction using the Taco Plant DNA/RNA Extraction Kit (GeneReach Biotechnology, Taichung, Taiwan) with the Taco automated extraction system. Detection limits for bacterial cells and total gDNA were determined by testing 10-fold serial dilutions as templates in real-time PCR.

Results

Specificity of the selected region and primer design

Comparative genomic analysis of Xanthomonas spp. identified a gene (Protein ID: WP_145954069.1; GenBank accession: GCA_900183975.1; location = 1347181–1347555) annotated as a hypothetical protein that was unique to X. fragariae strains. A BLASTn search of this gene sequence showed 100% identity with seven X. fragariae isolates (Supplementary Fig. 1A, 1B, Supplementary Table 2). In contrast, the newly reported Chinese X. fragariae subspecies SHQP01, YL19, and YLX21 exhibited 91.69–91.96% identity, while X. hortorum shared less than 79.49% identity. No other bacterial species were detected during the BLASTn search. The primers XF-212F/R were designed from this X. fragariae-specific sequence. The primer pair amplified a 212 bp fragment, and BLASTn analysis confirmed that the amplicon matched only X. fragariae strains among other bacterial sequences, showing greater than 92.42% identity (Supplementary Fig. 1C, 1D, Supplementary Table 3).

Furthermore, we downloaded the sequences of all representative Xanthomonas strains with complete or chromosome level genome assemblies to compare the sequence homology with the XF-212F/R primer binding sites. Consequently, significant homology was observed only with X. fragariae strains (Supplementary Fig. 2).

The binding sites of both primers aligned well with X. fragariae strains with several 5′-end mismatches in the reverse primer for strains YL19, SHQP01, and YLX21 (Supplementary Fig. 3A). We also analyzed the amplicon sequences of primer set XfOG4-F/R (Wang et al., 2025), developed by similar genomic methods. The XfOG4-F/R primers showed more than three mismatches in both forward and reverse binding sites for YL19, SHQP01, and YLX21, potentially reducing amplification efficiency (Supplementary Fig. 3B).

Specificity of the primer set

The specificity of the XF-212F/R primer set was verified in vitro using four X. fragariae strains (KACC 18320, KACC 18321, KACC 18322, and synthesized gene fragment of YLX21) and 18 nontarget bacterial species, including Xanthomonas, Pseudomonas, and Pectobacterium spp. listed in Table 1. PCR amplification produced a 212 bp band only for X. fragariae strains and not for nontarget species (Fig. 1). In addition, the primer set generated a 212-bp product from a synthesized gene fragment containing the X. fragariae YLX21 target sequence, which has the lowest sequence identity among Chinese strains. In the SYBR Green real-time PCR assay, amplification was detected exclusively for X. fragariae strains, with a melting temperature of 89.5°C (88°C for the synthetic DNA of strain YLX21), and no amplification was observed for the 18 non-target strains (Fig. 2). Collectively, in silico and in vitro analyses confirmed the high specificity of the XF-212F/R primer set, which successfully detected even the synthetic DNA fragment of the highly divergent YLX21.

Sensitivity of real-time PCR

The detection sensitivity of the XF-212F/R primer set was assessed using real-time PCR threshold cycle (Ct) values obtained from cloned DNA, genomic DNA (gDNA), and cell suspensions of X. fragariae KACC 18320. Concentrations resulting in inconsistent amplification were designated as ND (Not Determined) and were excluded from the determination of the detection limit. The reproducible detection limits were 1.41 × 102 plasmid copies/μL for cloned DNA, 500 fg for gDNA, and 1.52 × 103 CFU/mL for cell suspensions, with corresponding Ct values of 34.48 ± 0.22, 34.81 ± 0.18, and 35.90 ± 0.18, respectively (Table 2, Supplementary Table 4). The standard curves showed a strong linear correlation between Ct values and template concentration, with the following parameters: cloned DNA (R2 = 0.997, slope = −3.273, E = 102%), gDNA (R2 = 0.999, slope = −3.195, E = 106%), and cell suspensions (R2 = 0.998, slope = −3.390, E = 97%) (Fig. 3).

fragariae detection in strawberry plants

The XF-212F/R primer set was further validated for detecting X. fragariae in artificially infected strawberry leaves. Water-soaked lesions appeared only in pathogen-inoculated samples on the lower leaf surfaces (Fig. 4A–4D). Moreover, real-time PCR analysis of symptomatic leaf extracts showed similar amplification curves and a single melting peak at 89.5°C for all three X. fragariae strains (Fig. 4E–4G). Both plant extracts and total DNA, serially diluted 10-fold, were used as templates to determine the detection limits (Table 3). X. fragariae was successfully detected in leaf extracts across quantifiable ranges of 106 to 103 CFU/mL, with Ct values of 26.42 to 35.44 and a melting temperature of 89.5°C (Table 3). For total DNA, detection was achieved between 20 ng/μL and 200 fg/μL, with Ct values of 17.66 to 35.12 for strain KACC 18320. Altogether, real-time PCR using the XF-212F/R primer set enabled sensitive and consistent detection of X. fragariae from symptomatic strawberry leaves, both from total DNA extracts and directly from plant tissue without prior DNA purification.

Discussion

Population structure analyses of X. fragariae have shown that this species is divided into two major lineages comprising four subgroups (Gétaz et al., 2018). Comparative genomic studies further revealed substantial variation among strains (Gétaz et al., 2020). Moreover, newly identified strains from China, i.e., YL19, SHQP01, and YLX21, have increased the known intraspecific diversity of this pathogen, as these isolates are suggested to be new subspecies (Wei et al., 2024b). Despite these advances, diagnostic approaches for X. fragariae have largely focused on refining detection sensitivity through real-time PCR and loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) assays based on previously established targets such as hrp, gyrB, and RAPD-derived regions (Turechek et al., 2008; Vandroemme et al., 2008; Wang and Turechek, 2016; Wang and Turechek, 2020).

Effective detection of plant-pathogenic bacteria requires diagnostic tools that can accommodate genomic variability. Earlier markers were typically developed from functionally characterized loci such as housekeeping or hrp-related genes, or by DNA fingerprinting (Catara et al., 2021). However, the advent of high-throughput sequencing has provided extensive genomic data that enable the identification of novel, highly specific markers and facilitate in silico validation of primer specificity (Catara et al., 2021; Cubero et al., 2024; Davey and Valdivia, 2020). As a result, previously published X. fragariae primers targeting gyrB (Weller et al., 2007) and RAPD-derived products (XF241, XF245, and XF295) (Pooler et al., 1996) have been shown to lack sufficient conservation or species specificity across X. fragariae isolates (Wang et al., 2025).

In this study, we developed a novel specific primer set (XF-212F/R) through comparative genomic analysis demonstrating improved coverage for the detection of X. fragariae strains. The specificity of the XF-212F/R primer set was validated both in silico and in vitro, confirming that it exclusively amplifies X. fragariae. The BLASTn search with the amplicon sequence of XF-212F/R (Supplementary Table 3) showed that this primer set has 100% sequence identity with X. fragariae strains from both genetic lineages, Xf-CGr-I (PD5205, PD885, Fap21, and Fap29) and Xf-CGr-II (NBC2815, isolated from Spain) (Gétaz et al., 2018). In addition, we demonstrated the extended detection capability of our primer set, which provided stable detection of the synthetic DNA of the highly divergent Chinese strain YLX21 for the first time (Figs. 1, 2). However, a slight shift in the amplification and melting curves for the synthetic DNA construct of YLX21 was observed compared to the gDNA of other X. fragariae strains (Fig. 2). Differences in the genomic context of the synthetic DNA cloned into the pUC57-Amp may have altered the thermodynamic stability of the fragment during the reaction. Therefore, a melting temperature acceptance range of approximately 88.0–89.5°C should be considered when applying this assay. Moreover, further validation using authentic Chinese strains is required before this range is applied to routine surveillance or regulatory diagnostics, as differences in genomic architecture may affect the detection efficacy.

The XF-212F/R primer set demonstrated higher analytical sensitivity for genomic DNA, detecting as little as 500 fg, which outperformed the XfOG4-F/R primer (4 pg). However, the detection limit for bacterial cell suspensions (103 CFU/mL) was comparable between the two assays (Wang et al., 2025). Furthermore, the standard curves demonstrated the high diagnostic reliability of the XF-212F/R assay, with parameters consistently falling within the optimal range for qPCR (Efficiency = 90–110%; Slope = −3.1 to −3.6; R2 > 0.99). The minor variation in amplification efficiency and slope observed across different templates (cloned DNA, gDNA, and cell suspensions) can be attributed to differences in the relative purity and accessibility of the target DNA. Purified templates provide a more optimized matrix than whole bacterial cells, which may contain residual cellular components that slightly interfere with the initial PCR cycles. We further optimized the detection workflow using both total DNA and crude plant extracts as PCR templates. The real-time PCR assay achieved a detection limit of 103 CFU/mL from infected strawberry leaf extracts, consistent with previously reported sensitivities by Weller et al. (2007) and Wang et al. (2025). These results demonstrate that real-time PCR with the XF-212F/R primer set provides high diagnostic performance, combining strong specificity with excellent sensitivity for accurate detection of X. fragariae.

The newly identified species-specific gene (Protein ID: WP_145954069.1) and the XF-212F/R primer set developed here offer a robust and reliable tool for detecting X. fragariae, potentially including the genetically divergent Chinese strains. This primer set can complement existing diagnostic markers and is potentially adaptable to other molecular approaches such as TaqMan real-time PCR and live-cell detection. Together, these findings provide a foundation for early detection and surveillance of X. fragariae, supporting improved management and prevention of angular leaf spot in strawberry cultivation.
