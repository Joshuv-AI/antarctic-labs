Coupled Hg isotope dynamics reveal eruption pulses across the Permian–Triassic mass extinction
Authors: Kunio Kaiho, Jeroen E. Sonke, Stephen E. Grasby, Li Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Permian–Triassic mass extinction, the most severe biodiversity crisis of the Phanerozoic, is widely linked to Siberian Traps volcanism, yet mercury (Hg) isotope responses to eruptive phases remain poorly constrained. Here we identify a pronounced negative correlation between Δ199Hg and δ202Hg that occurs exclusively during three coupled volcanic–environmental–biotic crisis intervals ( ~ 150 kyr) and is absent under background conditions. Across multiple sections, crisis-interval samples converge between terrestrial, marine carbonate, and volcanic source fields, whereas background samples are more dispersed, indicating distinct isotopic behaviour. Hg/total organic carbon ratios normalized to background values strongly correlate with the Δ199Hg–δ202Hg relationship, linking Hg loading to isotopic systematics. These results reveal a previously unrecognized isotopic structure and suggest enhanced large-scale source control during crisis intervals, providing a framework for resolving eruptive pulses and tracing volcanism-driven environmental change.

Introduction

Over the past 500 million years, Earth’s biodiversity has undergone major fluctuations marked by episodes of diversification and five major mass extinction events1–4. The most severe of these occurred at the Permian–Triassic boundary (PTB) approximately 252 million years ago (Ma), eliminating more than 80% of species4–7. This extinction is now recognized to have unfolded in three distinct phases over ~150 thousand years (kyr). The first phase, the end-Permian Terrestrial Devastation (EPTD), is indicated by an initial negative carbon-isotope shift and a slight reduction in marine diversity within the Clarkina changxingensis to lower Clarkina yini zone (conodont zone). The second phase, the end-Permian extinction (EPE) within the top of Hindeodus parvus zone, represents the most severe global extinction event of the Phanerozoic for marine invertebrates and coincides with a pronounced negative δ13C excursion observed in many sections5,8,9. This interval is also characterized by elevated Hg/TOC ratios and Hg-isotope signatures indicative of volcanism10–16, together with spikes of coronene10 suggesting high-temperature heating of organic matter. The third phase, a secondary marine extinction event (eTE) within the Hindeodus parvus and Isarcicella staeschei, occurred ~60–70 thousand years after the EPE17. It is characterized by a prominent spike in terrestrial source (higher plants, fungi, lichens) input and further perturbations in multiple geochemical proxies10,18,19, indicating subsequent global warming, ocean stagnation, and the development of euxinic conditions18,20–23. These phased crises are closely linked to large-scale volcanism, with CO2 emissions acting as the primary driver of global warming and associated environmental disturbances such as ocean acidification and enhanced weathering6,10,24–32.

Extensive mercury (Hg) concentration data from PTB sedimentary successions show strong correlations between Hg enrichment and these biotic crisis intervals, supporting volcanism as a major driver of the three extinction phases. However, Hg/TOC ratios alone cannot distinguish among different types of magmatism and may be influenced by riverine Hg input and depositional redox conditions10–16,33. Recently, Hg isotopes—particularly Δ199Hg—have been increasingly investigated in PTB sections because they provide important constraints on Hg sources. Nevertheless, the limited temporal resolution of existing Hg-isotope datasets has hindered precise reconstruction of the relationships among volcanic activity, environmental stressors, and the timing of extinction pulses.

To address this gap, we conducted a high-resolution investigation of strata spanning the three major extinction phases, focusing on Hg concentrations, Hg isotopes, and biomarker proxies. This approach enables us to better evaluate causal links between environmental disturbances and biotic changes during the Permian–Triassic transition. Our study examines PTB sections at Meishan, Liangfengya (LFY), and Bulla, which are distributed across the eastern and western Tethys realms. These sections possess well-constrained high-resolution biostratigraphy and documented biotic events10,17,18, allowing detailed examination of the timing and causes of biotic turnover.

In this work, we analyse Hg isotopes and identify a pronounced negative correlation between Δ199Hg and δ202Hg that occurs exclusively during three coupled volcanic–environmental–biotic crisis intervals ( ~ 150 kyr) and is absent under background conditions. We show that this relationship defines a previously unrecognized isotopic structure, indicating enhanced large-scale source control or common governing processes during crisis intervals. These findings provide a framework for resolving eruptive pulses and tracing volcanism-driven environmental change.

Results and discussion

Geologic setting

This study is based on continuous, centimeter-scale sampling of sedimentary successions ( < 10 cm intervals across profiles 1.4–2 m thick) from three geographically distinct sections in the eastern and western Tethys regions. The Meishan section (31.0798°N, 119.7058°E; 26 samples) and the Liangfengya (LFY) section (29°30′29.65″N, 106°52′58.26″E; 20 samples) are located ~1000 km apart within the South China Block, whereas the Bulla section (46°33′52.16″N, 11°38′07.02″E; 16 samples) lies in northern Italy within the western Tethys realm (Fig. 1a). The end-Permian extinction (EPE) and the early Triassic extinction (eTE) are defined by marine extinction rates exceeding 0.25 per bed or sub-bed interval17. The end-Permian terrestrial devastation (EPTD), interpreted as a terrestrial source (higher plants, fungi, lichens) input event reflecting terrestrial ecosystem collapse10,34,35, corresponds to a decrease in δ13Ccarb from ~4‰ to ~2‰ across the Clarkina changxingensis to lower C. yini zones. The EPE, representing the major marine extinction horizon, is marked by a further decline from ~2‰ to ≤1‰ in the upper C. yini zone (Fig. 1c). The eTE is characterized by another decrease in δ13Ccarb from ~2‰ to ~1‰ within the upper Hindeodus parvus to Isarcicella staeschei zones (Fig. 1c). These three δ13Ccarb decreases coincide with reductions in marine species diversity, although the decline during the EPTD is relatively minor (Fig. 1c).

All three sections consist mainly of limestone and marl deposited in shallow-marine photic-zone environments, with estimated water depths of ~80–100 m at Meishan and LFY and ~20–30 m at Bulla (Fig. 1b)17,36. The successions, therefore, record shallow-marine depositional settings and capture the three major biotic crises: the EPTD, EPE, and eTE. Strata from the EPTD to the EPE consist predominantly of limestone, whereas lithologies between the EPE and eTE vary among sections—clay and marl at Meishan, limestone to marl at LFY, and marl to limestone at Bulla (Fig. 1c).

Quality assessments of geochemical proxies and Hg isotopes

Maximum temperature (Tmax) values, representing pyrolysis temperatures, range from 433–446 °C (442 ± 3 °C [1 SD], n = 19) at Meishan, 429–452 °C (450 ± 11 °C [1 SD], n = 6) at LFY, and 434–467 °C (451 ± 10 °C [1 SD], n = 10) at Bulla (Supplementary Table S1). These Tmax values are comparable among the three sections and indicate similar levels of thermal maturity, suggesting similar influence on Hg isotopic compositions. The observed Tmax ranges correspond to the interval during which decreases in Hg concentration and Hg/TOC ratios occur in heating experiments on five mudrocks with Tmax values of 424–444 °C37. These experiments also show that Δ199Hg remains unchanged within analytical uncertainty ( ± 0.07–0.08‰ [2 SD]), whereas δ202Hg may vary from −0.4‰ to +0.2‰ ( − 0.14 ± 0.25‰ [average ± 1 SD], n = 5), slightly exceeding analytical uncertainty ( ± 0.11–0.12‰ [2 SD]) during heating from untreated conditions to 460 °C, but without a systematic trend36. Consequently, δ202Hg values cannot be corrected for temperature effects. This variation is considerably smaller than the ~1‰ difference typically observed between marine carbonate and volcanic Hg signatures (Supplementary Table S2). Therefore, Hg isotopic anomalies can be used to identify the dominant Hg sources and controlling processes.

The average analytical recovery rates for Hg isotope measurements are 94%, 93%, 111%, and 98% for LFY, Meishan, Bulla, and all samples combined, respectively. To evaluate site-to-site variation, enrichment factor (EF) values of Hg/TOC and biomarker indices were used for comparison among sections.

Cross-plot analysis of total organic carbon (TOC) and S1 values (free hydrocarbons) across the three sections shows a strong positive correlation (R = 0.66; Supplementary Table 1), indicating that the detected hydrocarbons are indigenous. Previously reported vitrinite reflectance (Rc) and methylphenanthrene ratio (MPR) values, which serve as thermal maturity indicators10, indicate that all samples are mature, corresponding to early to peak oil generation stages38. These results rule out significant bias in Hg/TOC ratios and hydrocarbon index data due to thermal maturation.

Pyrolysis analyses reveal that total organic carbon (TOC) content ranges from 0.21–1.76% at Meishan (average: 0.66%, SD: 0.44), 0.15–1.04% at Liangfengya (LFY; average: 0.23%, SD: 0.19), and 0.07–2.09% at Bulla (average: 0.34%, SD: 0.40) (Fig. 2; Table S1). The revised TOC data reported here are generally higher than those in ref. 10 since no decarbonation or chemical washing was applied prior to pyrolysis, consistent with standard HAWK methodology (see Methods), resulting in lower Hg/TOC ratios (Fig. 2). Biomarker data from the Meishan and Bulla sections were integrated with those reported in refs. 10,39 while data from LFY are identical to those presented in ref. 10. The coronene/p ratio and the coronene index [coronene/(benzo[e]pyrene + beozo[ghi]perylene + coronene)] were employed to estimate high-temperature combustion events, and combustion temperature, respectively10, because experiments and thermodynamic simulations show that the temperature at which coronene begins to be observed is higher than the other six ring or less PAHs40. Coronene/p ratios show sporadically high values just before and during the EPE, as well as before and after the eTE at Meishan (Fig. 2a). Elevated coronene/p values are observed during the EPTD, EPE and before eTE at LFY (Fig. 2b). Elevated coronene/p values are observed during the EPTD, EPE and eTE at LFY and Bulla (Fig. 2c). Averaged values for enrichment factors of each environmental indicator—Hg/TOCEF and coronene/pEF—across the three sections show distinct peaks corresponding to the EPTD, EPE, and eTE events (Fig. 3). The EPTD coincided with elevated Hg/TOC values, a coal fly ash loading event, moderate coronene enrichment, and increased charcoal abundance, all of which indicate mantle plume-driven volcanic activity10,34,41. The highest Hg/TOCEF and coronene/pEF values in the EPE indicate that volcanic activity reached its maximum during this interval.

Mercury isotopic variations and its dynamics

Hg/TOC, coronene/p, coronene index, δ202Hg, and Δ199Hg values for both event and non-event intervals exhibit relatively large standard deviations (SDs, Fig. 2). The underlying cause of these variations may be linked to differences in the fallout and deposition of Hg, which was influenced by atmospheric circulation and seawater flow, including tidal currents, at each shallow marine site, e.g., the Bulla section was on a wave sorted shoal for the occurrences of well deposited ooids while the Meishan boundary intervals were somewhat bioturbated42,43. These factors likely controlled the extensity and intensity of Hg fallout during the explosive eruptions of the Siberian Traps, resulting in site-specific differences in Hg isotope signatures. The large variability in Hg/TOC, C/p, coronene index, and DBF/p values within a single volcanic event likely reflects the fractal temporal structure of global volcanic activity, characterized by clusters of eruptions on centennial timescales with multiple sub-peaks44. Volcanic activity across the Permian–Triassic boundary likely consisted of similar alternating eruptive and quiescent periods45,46. Accordingly, the large SDs reflect a natural consequence of the underlying geological processes rather than technical issues.

All δ202Hg values during the three crisis intervals at Meishan range from −1.21 to −0.87 (Figs. 2a and S2a), partly overlapping with volcanic δ²⁰²Hg values (−0.76 ± 0.24 ‰, 1 SD)47,48. However, most values from the LFY and Bulla sections are lower than typical volcanic signatures (Figs. 2b, 2c, S2b, and S2c) and instead fall within the range defined by terrestrial source rocks (organic-rich mudstones; −2.00 ± 0.70 ‰), marine carbonates ( − 1.74 ± 0.56 ‰), and mixed volcanic–photic zone euxinic sources ( − 0.88 ± 0.29 ‰) (Fig. 3a–c). These patterns indicate contributions from multiple Hg sources and transformation processes.

A key marine process influencing δ202Hg is the preferential biological uptake of isotopically light Hg, followed by the settling of organic matter and transfer of light Hg to sediments49. Samples from each section were collected from single outcrops lacking igneous dykes or beds, implying similar diagenetic histories. The potential influence of diagenesis was assessed using Tmax, which indicates uniformly low thermal maturity across the three sections. Therefore, the observed isotopic convergence is unlikely to result from diagenetic overprinting, particularly given the limited spatial extent of each section ( ~ 2–3 m wide and 1–2 m thick).

Across the three biotic crises, all Δ199Hg values—except for two samples in the lower part of the EPTD at Bulla—range from −0.03 to +0.05 ‰ at Meishan, −0.02 to +0.08 ‰ at LFY, and 0.00 to +0.08 ‰ at Bulla (Fig. 3). Approximately 85% of the event samples from the three crises fall within the volcanic Δ199Hg range, estimated at 0.03 ± 0.05 ‰ (1 SD) (Fig. 3)47,48. Whereas 47% of non-event samples deviate from volcanic Δ199Hg values (Fig. 3). Notably, near-zero Δ199Hg values (0.00 ± 0.02 ‰) are observed in all three sections at the onset of every event, except for the EPE at LFY (large red dots in Fig. 2). The Δ199Hg values of non-event samples show a threefold wider range ( − 0.13 to 0.26 ‰) than those of event samples ( − 0.05 to 0.09 ‰) (Fig. 3).

Distinct differences between Δ199Hg values in event and non-event samples demonstrate significant and systematic differences between event and non-event Hg isotope data: 1) Δ199Hg values in event samples are relatively consistent within each event across all sections, whereas those in non-event samples show substantially greater variability (Fig. 2); 2) Δ199Hg values in event samples are close to zero, consistent with previous studies (Fig. 2); 3) Event Δ199Hg values are distinct from the full range of non-event values. In the LFY section, although overall variability is limited, event samples define a clear trend along an oblique line between terrestrial source rocks (0.11 ± 0.10 ‰), marine carbonate (0.06 ± 0.08 ‰), and volcanic Δ199Hg (0.03 ± 0.05 ‰) values, indicating a volcanic signature. This pattern is not observed in non-event samples (Fig. 3b); 4) Even where 2 SD error bars overlap between event and non-event samples, the probability that the patterns described in points (1)–(3) arise by chance is thought to be very low.

Δ199Hg values would shift toward lower values by Hg(II) reduction under photic zone euxinia (PZE) conditions, and toward higher values in the absence of PZE22. Therefore, Δ199Hg values decrease during the EPE and its aftermath at Meishan, and during the eTE and its aftermath at LFY (Fig. 2), corresponding to photic zone euxinia and anoxic conditions in shallow marine environments39,50–53. In contrast, Δ199Hg values increase during the EPTD at Bulla under oxic conditions39 (Fig. 2).

Terrestrial Hg isotope compositions are constrained using δ202Hg and Δ199Hg data from Paleogene terrestrial mudrocks54, as modern soil datasets from southwest China55 are strongly influenced by anthropogenic Hg inputs and do not reflect preindustrial conditions56. Although modern soils consistently exhibit negative Δ199Hg values, terrestrial mudrocks integrate signals from both terrestrial vegetation and aquatic organic matter, providing a more representative isotopic composition during soil erosion and fluvial transport to the ocean. Accordingly, mudrock-derived Hg isotope data are used to characterize terrestrial Hg inputs. On this basis, a terrestrial source alone is unlikely to account for the observed decrease in Δ199Hg values.

These shifts in Δ199Hg likely contribute to the convergence toward oblique trends in δ202Hg–Δ199Hg space. Under oxic conditions associated with lower volcanic intensity, higher Δ199Hg values may coincide with decreased δ202Hg due to enhanced plankton uptake. Conversely, under anoxic conditions associated with intensified volcanism, lower Δ199Hg values may accompany increased δ202Hg as a result of reduced plankton activity, but also because PZE intrinsically leads to increased marine and sediment δ202Hg as light Hg(0) isotopes evade to the atmosphere57,58.

Correlation between δ202Hg and Δ199Hg corresponding with the biotic crises

Figure 3a–c show that δ202Hg values at Meishan cluster near −1‰, whereas those at LFY and Bulla range from −2‰ to −1‰; in contrast, Δ199Hg values exhibit greater variability among sections. During the EPE, Hg isotopic compositions from all sections converge along a mixing line between terrestrial source rocks−marine carbonate and volcanic end members in Δ199Hg–δ202Hg space, indicating a common or synchronous Hg source during this interval (Fig. 3e). Although clear regional differences among Meishan, LFY, and Bulla are evident before and after the EPE, isotopic variability is markedly reduced during the extinction interval itself, as shown by contraction from the green to the red field followed by expansion to the pale-green field (Fig. 3e).

A similar reduction in regional variability is observed during the EPTD relative to the post-EPTD interval (Fig. 3d) and during the eTE relative to the pre-eTE interval (Fig. 3f). These patterns indicate enhanced homogenization of Hg sources during all three crisis intervals. The strongest isotopic convergence occurs during the EPE, suggesting that this phase represents the most intense and widespread volcanic influence. Pre-event baseline values form broader triangular distributions (Fig. 3d–f), which become narrower during volcanic events and expand again in post-event intervals. This pattern indicates that a single dominant Hg source prevailed during volcanic events, most likely of volcanic origin.

Compiled data from the three study areas show that a strong negative correlation between δ202Hg and Δ199Hg occurs exclusively during the three biotic crisis intervals. This pattern contrasts sharply with the weak correlations observed during pre-EPTD intervals and between crises, as well as the positive correlation characterizing the post-eTE interval (Fig. 4). Cross-plots of Δ199Hg versus δ202Hg for the EPTD, EPE, and eTE across all sections show negative correlations, with R = −0.64 (n = 9, p = 0.34) for the EPTD, −0.75 (n = 9, p = 0.013) for the EPE, and −0.51 (n = 6, p = 0.30) for the eTE (Figs. 4b, d, and f). Only the correlation for the EPE is statistically significant (P < 0.05). However, the standard deviations (SD) of δ202Hg and Δ199Hg in the EPTD and eTE are approximately half and slightly larger, respectively, compared with those in the EPE, indicating stronger convergence in δ202H and weaker convergence in Δ199Hg. Notably, convergence is evident in both the EPTD and eTE relative to pre-event intervals, as the SD values of δ202Hg and Δ199Hg are substantially smaller than those of background conditions (Fig. 4). No significant correlations of Δ199Hg and δ202Hg are observed during the three non-extinction intervals below the eTE, with R values of +0.10 (n = 6), +0.17 (n = 13), and –0.26 (n = 16) respectively (Fig. 4a, c, and e). This distinction suggests that volcanic activity played a dominant role during the three crises, whereas its influence was less significant during the pre-EPTD and inter-crises non-event beds (Fig. 4).

In contrast, post-eTE samples show a moderate positive correlation between Δ199Hg and δ202Hg (R = +0.46, n = 12, P = 0.13), unlike the negative or insignificant correlations observed elsewhere (Fig. 4). This positive correlation is likely related to the mudstone lithology at Meishan (see Fig. 2a) and is consistent with Phanerozoic non-carbonate Hg isotope signatures (Fig. 4).

For comparison, the compiled Δ199Hg and δ202Hg data from 26 global sections22 show similar cross-plot distributions but exhibit only weak correlations during both the event intervals (EPE–eTE) and the background non-event intervals (R = +0.17 and +0.02, respectively; Supplementary Fig. S1). The stronger correlations observed in the present study likely result from the higher stratigraphic resolution and the more precise identification of the three biotic crisis intervals. Overall, strong negative correlations between Δ199Hg and δ202Hg appear to be a distinctive geochemical feature of the three extinction events, as emphasized by the average correlation coefficients shown in Fig. 4.

These crises and non-crises intervals span ~1–60 thousand years (Fig. 4). Recent estimates of the mercury cycle indicate that enhanced Hg emissions can appear as distinct spikes in marine sediments within only a few thousand years, suggesting that the marine record is capable of capturing rapid shifts in mercury sources on the timescales discussed here59. The strong negative correlation between δ202Hg and Δ199Hg observed during the three biotic crisis intervals—each associated with volcanic episodes characterized by elevated Hg/TOCEF ratios ( > 3, indicative of volcanic input; Fig. 2)—indicates a shift in the dominant controls on Hg isotopic composition. During non-event intervals, Hg isotopes reflect the influence of multiple concurrent processes, whereas during crisis intervals they are governed by one or two prevailing mechanisms, most plausibly intensified volcanism.

Correlation between Hg/TOC EF and Δ199Hg–δ202Hg relationships

Higher Hg/TOCEF values correspond to stronger (more negative) Δ199Hg–δ202Hg correlation coefficients (Fig. 5). The correlation between Hg/TOCEF and the Δ199Hg–δ202Hg correlation coefficient is very high (R = 0.97; Fig. 5), indicating strong coupling between Hg loading and isotopic behavior. Figure 5 further indicates that the end-Permian extinction (EPE) represents the largest volcanic event, followed by the end-Permian terrestrial disturbance (EPTD), with the end-Triassic extinction (eTE) being the third in magnitude. The interval between the EPE and eTE reflects intermediate volcanic intensity, stronger than background non-event intervals but weaker than the three major volcanic–biotic crisis events.

The two lowest Δ199Hg–δ202Hg correlation coefficients during the pre-EPTD and pre-EPE intervals suggest contributions from multiple Hg sources. In contrast, the three most negative correlation coefficients indicate dominance of a single volcanic Hg source, variably diluted by background sedimentary inputs. The relationship between Hg/TOCEF and Δ199Hg–δ202Hg correlations across the Permian–Triassic boundary demonstrates that Hg isotopes provide a robust tool for identifying volcanic events because of the fractal nature of volcanic activity. This consistent coupling between Hg loading and isotopic behavior represents a central finding of this study.

Interpretation of high Δ199Hg–δ202Hg correlation during volcanic events

Mixing between volcanic atmospheric Hg(0) and deposited Hg(II), together with biological modification processes such as plankton uptake of isotopically light Hg, likely exerts the primary control on marine δ202Hg–Δ199Hg sedimentation patterns49. In marine carbonates, δ202Hg values commonly shift toward lower values through biological processing or early diagenesis, whereas large volcanic eruptions may reduce plankton abundance, slightly increasing δ202Hg values in marine sediments.

These large eruptive phases likely comprised multiple short-lived pulses, reflecting the fractal temporal structure of volcanic activity44–46. Because individual samples may integrate both high- and low-intensity eruptive subintervals, Hg isotopic values do not converge to a single point but instead define a broad distribution along an oblique trend between the volcanic δ202Hg–Δ199Hg signature and those of marine carbonates, terrestrial sources, and photic-zone euxinia (PZE) (Figs. 4b, 4d, 4f).

Large-scale episodic eruptions would homogenize local δ202Hg–Δ199Hg variability by introducing substantial Hg from a common volcanic source, while simultaneously mixing with background Hg deposited during short non-eruptive intervals within the broader volcanic phase. This mechanism accounts for the strong isotopic correlations observed during volcanic crisis intervals.

In contrast, the absence of such correlations during non-event intervals across geographically distant sections suggests weaker large-scale volcanic forcing and a greater influence of local controls, including variability in atmospheric Hg speciation, terrestrial Hg inputs, and oceanic redox conditions.

The degree of dilution of volcanic δ202Hg–Δ199Hg signatures by background Hg varies among sections and extinction phases, as shown in the cross-plots (Fig. 3). During the EPE, substantial dilution occurs in the LFY and Bulla sections (Fig. 3e), whereas the Meishan section shows minimal dilution (Fig. 3d), producing a strong linear relationship with a high correlation coefficient (R = −0.75; Fig. 4d). This contrast likely reflects differences in local redox conditions, with reduced biological uptake of light Hg isotopes under anoxic conditions at Meishan51,52 and enhanced uptake under more oxic conditions at LFY and Bulla39,53.

Another possible reason for the strong convergence of Hg isotope values along these red oblique trends during major eruptive phases is the increased input of terrestrial Hg associated with intense volcanic activity (represented by terrestrial mudstone54 rather than modern soils), which corresponds to the upper-left component of the red trend. This interpretation is supported by elevated terrestrial-source indicators, with enrichment factors (TSIEF) exceeding 3 during the three volcanic events in the three sections, except for the eTE at Bulla (Fig. 2; see “Methods”). Mixing between Hg derived from terrestrial source rocks and background Hg (e.g., marine carbonate Hg) forms the upper-left component of the red oblique trend, whereas volcanic emissions contribute to the lower-right component, as indicated in Figs. 4b, 4d, and 4f. Increased atmospheric Hg(0) emissions during eruptive events14,60 and decreases in Δ199Hg associated with the development of PZE after the EPE50–53 also appear to influence the values of the lower-right component along the red oblique trend. Considering that high terrestrial source input indicator values are also observed during the three events at Meishan, the contribution of terrestrial Hg may nevertheless be smaller than that from volcanic sources. In general, volcanic eruption–derived deposits tend to have higher mercury concentrations than surrounding terrestrial sediments, or they act as a more prominent source of mercury to the environment.

The particularly high correlation coefficients observed during the three volcanic–biotic crisis intervals—especially during the EPE—likely reflect the combined effects of pulsed volcanic emissions, biological fractionation of isotopically light Hg, and increased terrestrial inputs associated with ecosystem disturbance. If Hg isotope compositions could be resolved at the scale of individual eruptive pulses, they would likely converge toward a single point. A comparable point-like convergence has been reported for a single-event perturbation at the Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary impact61.

These results highlight an inter-section consistency in Hg isotope behaviour between event and non-event intervals, as well as the convergence of crisis-interval samples in Δ199Hg–δ202Hg space. Together, these patterns provide evidence for enhanced common source or process control at large spatial scales.

Methods

Field sampling

Each studied sample covers 1−2 cm, 2−4 cm, and 2−5 cm in height at Meishan (A section), LFY, and Bulla, respectively, without space between samples for the critical horizons (each bed was cut into several samples). Additionally, sporadic samples of 10 cm in height were taken from basal and top layers (Tables S1−S4a). Our Hg isotope data in the EPE have higher resolution than those in other studies14–16. The EPE likely occurred within a 1 cm interval, 2−3 cm below the top of Bed 24 at the Meishan A section, based on thin section observations62. Analyses of 1 cm interval samples are essential to detect the extinction event and the related environmental climate change. The previous highest resolution data of Hg isotopes12 at Meishan did not contain the extinction horizon, with samples ranging from 5.8 cm to 0.8 cm below the top of Bed 24. Our study samples span from 4−2 cm, 2−1 cm, and 1−0 cm below the top of Bed 24, encompassing the extinction horizon.

Pyrolysis analyses

Around 70 mg of each sample was analyzed by HAWK (Wildcat Technologies) programmed pyrolysis. The pyrolysis stage (under a He environment) involved the initial iso-temperature of 300 °C for 3 min to release free hydrocarbons in the samples, followed by ramping temperature at 25 °C/min up to 650 °C, releasing through thermal cracking hydrocarbons and the oxygen contained in pyrolizable kerogen. Then the oven was purged and heated from 300 to 850 °C with a heating rate of 20 °C per minute to measure the residual inert organic carbon. The TOC was quantified as the sum of the total quantity of organic matter released during the pyrolysis and oxidation steps63. The hydrogen index (HI) and oxygen index (OI) represent the amount of hydrogen and oxygen relative to the amount of organic carbon, respectively. These pyrolysis data are shown in Table S1.

Hg content and Hg isotope analyses

Total Hg concentrations in dry sedimentary rock powders were determined by atomic absorption spectrometry using a DMA-80 (Milestone). Reference material NIST1632d coal (92.8 ± 3.0 ng g−1) was used for quality control, yielding good results (89.9 ± 4.1 ng g−1). Each sediment sample was measured at least in duplicate until obtaining a relative uncertainty less than 5% (1sd). For Hg isotope extraction, powdered rock samples (0.1 to 1.0 g) were combusted in a double-stage tube furnace according to published methods64. In short, samples were combusted (900 °C for 6 h) in pure O2 at 80 mL min−1, and purged and pre-concentrated into 7 mL of 40% v/v concentrated HNO3/HCl in a 2:1 ratio. Purging impingers were rinsed with a total volume of 7 mL of de-ionized water, diluting the sample to 20% v/v acid and a final Hg concentration of 1.0 ng g−1. The final trapping solutions were kept in a refrigerator at 2 − 4 °C until Hg isotope analysis.

Hg isotope ratios were measured on a Neptune Plus multi-collector inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (MC-ICPMS, Thermo-Finnigan) at the Observatoire Midi-Pyrénées, Toulouse, France. We used a CETAC ASX-520 autosampler and CETAC HGX-200 cold vapor system coupled with the MC-ICPMS.

Sample and standard signals of 1.0 ng g−1 Hg concentrations were generally 500 mV on the 202Hg isotope, at a sample introduction flow rate of 0.5 mL min−1, and after mixing with 3 wt% SnCl2 in 1 N HCl solution. Instrumental mass bias of MC-ICPMS was corrected by standard-sample-standard bracketing using NIST3133 Hg at matching concentrations. Biomarker analyses

Approximately 100 g of hand-sized rock samples from each of the Meishan and Bulla sections were powdered per sample, following the removal of any surface contamination. The powdered samples were extracted for 48 h using a Soxhlet apparatus and a dichloromethane:methanol mixture (7:1 v/v). The extracts were dried over Na2SO4 and concentrated by evaporation under reduced pressure. The extracts were transferred into 60 ml vials and carefully concentrated under a stream of N2. The concentrated extracts were separated into non-polar and polar fractions using silica gel chromatography. The non-polar fraction and polar fraction were obtained by elution with n-hexane: DCM (4:1) and DCM:MeOH (4:1), respectively. The non-polar fraction was analyzed by GC-MS on an Agilent 7890B GC interfaced to an Agilent 7000 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer operated with ionizing-electron energy of 70 eV and scanned from m/z 50 to 600 with a scan time of 0.34 s. We used a fused silica HP-5MS capillary column (length: 30 m, internal diameter: 0.25 mm, film thickness: 0.25μm) with helium as the carrier gas. Samples were injected at 50 °C, and the temperature was held constant for 1.0 min. Then the temperature was raised to 120 °C at a rate of 30 °C/min, to 310 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min, and finally held constant for 20 min. PAHs (three-ringed PAH: phenanthrene, five-ringed PAH: benzo[e]pyrene, 6-ringed PAH: benzo[ghi]perylene, seven-ringed PAH: coronene) and DBF were identified based on a comparison of mass spectra and relative GC scan numbers with published data65,66.

Enrichment factor (EF)

The enrichment factor (EF) is defined as the ratio of the average peak value during an event interval to the average background value (Supplementary Tables 3–8). Background Hg/TOC, coronene/pEF, coronene index, and biomarker values were defined as the mean of the lowest measurements ( < 25%, or <15% for the coronene index, of the average of the second- to fifth-highest values) prior to the EPE, or after the eTE where pre-EPE values were unavailable, as indicated in the Supplementary Tables. Dibenzofuran-to-phenanthrene (DBF/p) was used as the terrestrial source input (TSI) index. In the LFY section, the enrichment factor (EF) of the long-chain n-alkane ratio—(n-C₂₇+₂₉+₃₁)/(n-C₁₇+₁₉+₂₁+₂₇+₂₉+₃₁)—representing the terrestrial plant index (LNA/T), was used instead of DBF/p because DBF concentrations are low in this section. Original data are from refs. 10,39.
