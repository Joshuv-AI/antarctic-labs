Atrial fibrillation in Australia, New Zealand, Malaysia, and Singapore: unmet needs and future priorities
Authors: Jonathan W.S. Ong, Melissa E. Middeldorp, Hanis Hanum Zulkifly, Norlizawati Moktar, Gregory Y.H. Lip, Chi Keong Ching
Published: 2026

More than 52 million people were living with atrial fibrillation (AF) in 2021, and prevalence continues to rise with population ageing, increasing cardiometabolic disease, and improved detection.1 AF is now a leading contributor to stroke, heart failure, hospitalisation, and cardiovascular mortality worldwide, with a particularly substantial burden across the Western Pacific region.2 The four countries examined in this Commentary—Australia, New Zealand, Malaysia, and Singapore—all fall within the World Health Organization Western Pacific region, the geographic scope of this journal.

Contemporary management of AF is anchored on three core pillars: stroke prevention with appropriate anticoagulation, symptom control through rate or rhythm strategies, with increasing emphasis on earlier rhythm control in appropriate patients, and structured management of comorbidities and lifestyle risk factors.3, 4, 5 International guidelines increasingly emphasise integrated, patient-centred models of care that combine these elements to improve outcomes.3,4

Despite this strong evidence base and broad international consensus, AF management across Australia, New Zealand, Malaysia, and Singapore continues to be constrained by structural barriers—including national guidelines that predate key advances in the contemporary evidence base, fragmented digital infrastructure, and inequitable access to proven therapies—that limit the delivery of guideline-concordant care. Much of the regional evidence is derived from single-centre studies, selected cohorts, or extrapolation from populations outside the Western Pacific, and the absence of contemporary population-level AF data further limits efforts to inform policy.2,6 An overview of AF epidemiology and national management strategies is provided in the Supplementary material.

This Commentary examines the key unmet needs that persist across these four diverse Western Pacific healthcare settings and identifies priorities where system-level action could most meaningfully improve outcomes.

Unmet needs and future priorities in AF care

Across Australia, New Zealand, Malaysia, and Singapore, the principal challenges in AF management are no longer therapeutic but structural, reflecting gaps in implementation, access, and care coordination. Addressing these limitations will require system-level transformation. Early detection: from underdiagnosis to structured, technology-enabled detection

AF remains substantially underdiagnosed, particularly among populations at highest risk.2 In Australia and New Zealand, underdiagnosis persists in Indigenous and remote communities, where culturally appropriate screening models and technology-enabled outreach remain limited.7,8 These populations carry a disproportionate stroke burden, making early detection an important equity issue. Malaysia continues to rely on pulse palpation and opportunistic ECGs, a framework that predates modern approaches such as wearable-enabled and AI-assisted screening.9 A recent local review has highlighted the need to align national screening practices with contemporary international standards.10 In Singapore, low public awareness of AF symptoms, stroke risk, and the importance of anticoagulation contributes to delayed presentation and missed opportunities for early diagnosis in primary care.11 As a result, a substantial proportion of AF remains undetected across all four countries.

Transitioning from opportunistic screening to structured, risk-based detection strategies is thus an important priority.12 Future programmes should expand national screening initiatives, raise public awareness, and integrate validated digital tools—such as wearables, AI-assisted rhythm analysis, and automated alerts within healthcare systems—into routine care.12 These strategies must be culturally appropriate and adaptable across diverse healthcare settings, enabling timelier initiation of anticoagulation and rhythm control therapy.

Stroke prevention: improving anticoagulation consistency and quality

Significant gaps in stroke prevention therapy persist across all four countries. In Australia and New Zealand, anticoagulation remains underutilised among elderly and Indigenous populations, despite their elevated stroke risk.13,14 In Malaysia, limited access to DOACs and the absence of reimbursement mechanisms have maintained reliance on warfarin, restricting treatment options for patients who may benefit most from modern anticoagulation.9 In Singapore, DOAC underdosing and continued use of antiplatelet therapy remain prevalent.11,15 These patterns are consistent with the broader “East Asian paradox” of heightened sensitivity to both ischaemic and haemorrhagic complications recognised in regional AF cohorts, and are driven in large part by concerns about bleeding risk that have persisted despite growing evidence that off-label underdosing is associated with increased stroke risk without a proportionate reduction in bleeding.16,17 Taken together, these findings underscore the need for greater attention to prescribing quality.

Ensuring appropriate, safe, and guideline-concordant anticoagulation therefore remains a foundational priority.3,4 Future efforts should focus on improving prescribing quality, reducing inappropriate underdosing, strengthening decision-making frameworks for elderly and high-risk patients, and expanding equitable access to DOACs.11,15 Initiatives to harmonise stroke risk assessment practices and to embed digital decision support tools across diverse care settings may further enhance treatment consistency and reduce preventable strokes.12

Rhythm control: closing the implementation gap

The international shift toward earlier rhythm control strategies has not been uniformly realised across these four countries and the barriers are largely structural.5 Australia and New Zealand continue to face substantial geographic inequities, with rural and Indigenous communities experiencing limited access to electrophysiology services.18 In Malaysia, national guidance (last revised in 2012) predates contemporary evidence. As a result, ablation availability remains concentrated within select tertiary hospitals and is less frequently incorporated into routine management pathways.9 The need to update this guidance in line with contemporary international standards has been recognised locally.10 In Singapore, catheter ablation rates, though steadily increasing, remain lower than in many Western centres, suggesting that clinical decision-making has not yet fully aligned with contemporary evidence supporting early rhythm control.19 These disparities collectively delay timely rhythm control therapy and limit its long-term cardiovascular benefits.

Ensuring that evolving evidence on early rhythm control is translated into equitable clinical practice is therefore critical.5 Healthcare systems must expand electrophysiology capacity, particularly in underserved regions, and strengthen referral pathways between primary care and specialist centres.19 Increasing clinician awareness of contemporary indications for rhythm control, supported by national guidance aligned with current evidence, would help standardise practice and improve treatment consistency. Regional training collaborations can further build electrophysiology expertise and mitigate workforce limitations. Ultimately, better coordination between general practitioners, primary care teams, and specialist services—supported by digital infrastructure and formal referral frameworks—will be essential.

Lifestyle and risk factor management: moving toward integrated care

Despite strong evidence that structured lifestyle and risk factor management improves AF outcomes,20 implementation remains uneven across all four countries. In Australia and New Zealand, the adoption of comprehensive programmes has been constrained by resource limitations and reimbursement structures that provide limited support for preventive, lifestyle-focused care—despite landmark Australian studies forming much of the evidence base for this approach.21 In Malaysia, lifestyle and comorbidity management is governed by disease-specific national guidelines rather than an integrated AF pathway, resulting in parallel rather than coordinated care.9 In Singapore, comorbidity management is improving but remains variable, as sustained lifestyle counselling and risk factor optimisation depend heavily on practice setting and the level of coordination between primary care and specialist services.19 Collectively, this fragmented approach limits the potential for disease modification that structured interventions have been shown to achieve.

Moving towards integrated, multidisciplinary care pathways is essential to realising this potential.20,22 Future efforts should prioritise integrated pathways linking primary care physicians, cardiologists, nurses, pharmacists, and allied health professionals, supported by consistent referral processes and bi-directional communication. Embedding structured approaches to comorbidity optimisation—addressing weight, blood pressure, glycaemic control, sleep apnoea, and physical activity—as routine components of AF care would help ensure reliable delivery across settings. Clinical trial evidence from the Atrial fibrillation Better Care (ABC) pathway demonstrates that adherence to holistic or integrated care management is associated with improved clinical outcomes.22 Variants of the ABC acronym have been proposed (‘SOS’ or ‘CARE’) but are untested in trials or real world evidence. Shared-care models, including nurse-led AF clinics and primary care-specialist collaboration frameworks, may further enhance continuity and reduce fragmentation.

Digital infrastructure: enabling data-driven AF care

Across all four countries, digital infrastructure to support AF care remains variably developed. Australia and New Zealand lack population-level AF registries, limiting their ability to track longitudinal outcomes, monitor treatment patterns, and benchmark performance.6 In Malaysia, national guidance predates contemporary digital innovation and does not incorporate telemonitoring or decision support tools.9 Digital capability and interoperability remain uneven across care settings, particularly outside tertiary centres. In Singapore, AF-related data remain distributed across multiple electronic medical record systems and a dedicated national AF registry has yet to be established.19 Collectively, these gaps constrain progress towards more data-driven, coordinated AF care and limit opportunities for systematic quality improvement and collaborative research.

Strengthening digital infrastructure is a foundational enabler of more effective AF care delivery. Modern, interoperable data systems could support population-level monitoring, quality assurance, and collaborative research. Establishing national AF registries and improving digital linkages between healthcare settings would enable consistent data capture, benchmarking, and outcome tracking. Embedding validated digital tools—clinical decision support systems, telemonitoring platforms, and AI-enabled risk stratification—can enhance decision making and facilitate more proactive care.12 Digital capability must also extend beyond tertiary centres to include primary care and rural environments to ensure equitable implementation. A more connected digital ecosystem would further support shared learning and coordinated improvements in AF management across these and comparable Western Pacific settings.

Conclusion

AF presents growing challenges across Australia, New Zealand, Malaysia, and Singapore, yet these healthcare systems are well positioned to make meaningful progress. The gaps outlined in this Commentary are clear: persistent underdiagnosis, suboptimal anticoagulation quality, uneven access to rhythm control therapies, fragmented lifestyle management, and underdeveloped digital infrastructure. Importantly, these are structural barriers—not deficits in available therapies or evidence—and addressing them will require coordinated, system-level action. Progress will depend on updated and harmonised clinical guidance, sustained investment in workforce capacity and digital infrastructure, reimbursement models that support integrated care delivery, and mechanisms to monitor and improve guideline concordance.

Addressing these priorities will enable more consistent delivery of early detection, effective stroke prevention, timely rhythm control, and integrated comorbidity management across comparable Western Pacific settings. The coming decade offers a real opportunity to narrow disparities and improve long-term outcomes for people living with AF—provided that the commitment to system-level reform matches the strength of the available evidence.
