Molecular Mechanisms Underlying the Anti-Diabetic Effects of Astragaloside IV: A Focus on Signaling Pathways
Authors: Cong Chen, Peiyi He, Yang Liu, Jiahui Ma, Haiying Zhai, Rong Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes mellitus is a multifactorial metabolic disorder driven by dysregulated signaling networks, and its complications are closely associated with insulin resistance, metabolic imbalance, oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and disturbed cell-fate control. Astragaloside IV (AS-IV), a major bioactive saponin derived from Astragalus membranaceus, has attracted increasing attention as a multi-target candidate for diabetes and diabetes-related complications. This review summarizes recent advances in the signaling pathway–mediated anti-diabetic mechanisms of AS-IV, with particular emphasis on the integrated regulation of PI3K/Akt, AMPK, NF-κB, Nrf2/HO-1, mTOR, MAPK, and cell-fate-related pathways. Current evidence indicates that AS-IV improves insulin sensitivity and glucose metabolism through PI3K/Akt activation, regulates energy and lipid metabolism via AMPK-related signaling, suppresses inflammatory amplification by inhibiting NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and alleviates oxidative injury through activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant axis. In addition, AS-IV modulates autophagy, apoptosis, ferroptosis, and mitochondrial homeostasis, thereby contributing to organ protection in diabetic kidney disease, cardiovascular complications, retinopathy, neuropathy, and diabetic foot ulcers. Compared with previous reports that mainly focused on isolated pathways or single complications, this review highlights a network-level and multi-pathway integration perspective, emphasizing the cross-talk among metabolic regulation, inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, and programmed cell fate. Nevertheless, most current evidence remains derived from preclinical models, and important translational barriers, including low bioavailability, heterogeneous dosing regimens, insufficient pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic data, and limited clinical validation, still need to be addressed. Future studies integrating standardized disease models, multi-omics strategies, clinical samples, and well-designed prospective trials are required to clarify the therapeutic positioning of AS-IV and facilitate its translation as a potential multi-target agent for diabetes therapy.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a heterogeneous group of metabolic disorders characterized by chronic hyperglycemia, mainly including type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM).1 The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) reports that the global diabetic population has exceeded 500 million and is projected to approach 580 million by 2030 and exceed 700 million by 2045.2 Beyond defective insulin secretion and insulin action, DM is closely associated with dysregulated lipid metabolism,3 chronic low-grade inflammation,4 and oxidative stress,5 which collectively contribute to multisystem complications, including diabetic nephropathy,6 cardiovascular disease,7 retinopathy,8 and neuropathy.9

Current anti-diabetic therapies, including insulin and its analogs, biguanides, sulfonylureas, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, can effectively improve glycemic control.10 However, diabetes progression and diabetic complications are not driven by hyperglycemia alone. Persistent metabolic stress, inflammatory activation, oxidative injury, endothelial dysfunction, and progressive tissue remodeling may continue despite glucose-lowering treatment. In addition, some therapies are limited by hypoglycemia risk, treatment tolerance, declining efficacy, adverse effects, or insufficient protection against long-term complications.11 Therefore, agents capable of coordinately modulating multiple pathological pathways may provide complementary value in diabetes management.

Natural products derived from traditional herbal medicines have attracted increasing attention because of their multi-target and multi-pathway regulatory potential. Astragalus membranaceus, a commonly used “qi-tonifying” herb in traditional Chinese medicine, contains astragaloside IV (AS-IV) as one of its major bioactive constituents.12 AS-IV has been reported to exert antioxidant activity,13 anti-inflammatory activity,14 antifibrotic activity,15 and immunomodulatory activity.16 Accumulating evidence suggests that AS-IV may improve insulin resistance by regulating PI3K/Akt signaling,17 alleviate metabolic and inflammatory injury through AMPK/mTOR-mediated autophagy regulation,18 and protect pancreatic β cells via SIRT1/p53 and Akt/GSK3β/Nrf2 signaling pathways.19 These findings suggest that AS-IV may exert anti-diabetic effects through an interconnected signaling network rather than a single molecular target.

Nevertheless, existing studies have mainly focused on isolated pathways, individual organs, or single pathological processes, and the cross-talk among insulin signaling, energy metabolism, inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial homeostasis, and programmed cell fate remains insufficiently synthesized. Moreover, the translational value of AS-IV is still constrained by the predominance of preclinical evidence, low bioavailability, heterogeneous dosing regimens, insufficient pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic data, and limited clinical validation. Therefore, this review summarizes recent advances in the signaling pathway–mediated mechanisms of AS-IV in diabetes and its complications from a network-level and multi-pathway integration perspective. By highlighting key regulatory hubs, pathway cross-talk, evidence limitations, and translational challenges, this review aims to provide an integrated mechanistic framework for future drug development and clinical translation of AS-IV in diabetes therapy.

Chemical Constituents and Pharmacological Activities of AS-IV

Astragalus membranaceus contains diverse bioactive constituents, including triterpenoid saponins, polysaccharides, flavonoids, amino acids, and trace elements. Among these constituents, polysaccharides, saponins, and flavonoids are generally considered the major material bases underlying the pharmacological effects of A. membranaceus. With advances in extraction, separation, and structural elucidation techniques, more than 40 saponins have been identified from the dried roots of A. membranaceus, including astragalosides, acetylastragalosides, isoastragalosides, and cycloastragenol glycosides.20

Notably, the structural characteristics of AS-IV are also closely related to its translational limitations. As a saponin glycoside with relatively high molecular weight and complex glycosidic structure, AS-IV may exhibit limited intestinal absorption, extensive metabolism, and relatively low oral bioavailability, which may partly explain the gap between its promising preclinical effects and insufficient clinical validation. Therefore, linking its chemical structure to pharmacological activity and pharmacokinetic behavior is important for understanding both its therapeutic potential and translational challenges. Accumulating evidence in recent years highlights the potential of AS-IV as a candidate therapeutic agent for diabetes mellitus (DM) and its complications, but further studies are needed to clarify its structure–activity relationship, pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic characteristics, optimal dosing strategies, and clinical applicability.25

Regulatory Effects of AS-IV on Insulin Signaling

The phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) pathway is a central signaling hub in insulin action. Under physiological conditions, insulin receptor activation promotes insulin receptor substrate (IRS) phosphorylation, triggers the PI3K/Akt cascade, and facilitates GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane, thereby enhancing peripheral glucose uptake. In the liver, Akt activation suppresses FoxO1-mediated transcription of gluconeogenic enzymes, including phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) and glucose-6-phosphatase (G6Pase), thereby reducing endogenous glucose production. Impaired PI3K/Akt signaling is therefore closely associated with insulin resistance and the onset and progression of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).

Available evidence suggests that AS-IV improves insulin signaling through multi-level regulation of the PI3K/Akt axis rather than through a single linear mechanism. In a study investigating the pharmacodynamic basis of the anti-diabetic formula Xiaoke Fang, Ma et al reported that AS-IV, as a key active constituent, upregulated PI3K and Akt expression, increased their phosphorylation levels, enhanced glucose consumption, and restored impaired insulin signal transduction.26 These findings support the role of PI3K/Akt activation as a core mechanism underlying the insulin-sensitizing effect of AS-IV. However, PI3K/Akt activation by AS-IV appears to depend on multiple upstream regulatory inputs, including non-coding RNA regulation, adipokine-related signaling, and oxidative stress–associated pathways.27,28 An integrated schematic summary of these mechanisms is presented in Figure 2. At the post-transcriptional level, AS-IV has been shown to upregulate miR-21 and subsequently suppress phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN), a negative regulator of PI3K/Akt signaling. This releases the inhibitory constraint on Akt activation and contributes to improved insulin sensitivity in adipocytes. Interventional experiments further indicated that disruption of the miR-21/PTEN axis weakened the beneficial effect of AS-IV on insulin resistance, supporting a causal role for this regulatory route.17 In parallel, adipokine-related signaling also participates in AS-IV-mediated insulin sensitization. AS-IV was reported to upregulate C1q/TNF-related protein 3 (CTRP3), activate the CTRP3/PI3K/Akt pathway, and enhance glucose uptake; these effects were substantially attenuated by PI3K/Akt inhibition or CTRP3 gene silencing.29 Together, these findings indicate that AS-IV may converge on PI3K/Akt signaling through distinct upstream modules rather than directly activating a single target.

Oxidative stress–related signaling provides another important link between AS-IV, inflammation, and insulin resistance. Excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) and aberrant c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) activation can impair insulin signaling by promoting inhibitory phosphorylation of IRS-1. AS-IV has been shown to reduce ROS accumulation and suppress JNK activation, thereby weakening JNK-mediated IRS-1 inhibition, restoring PI3K/Akt signaling activity, and improving insulin sensitivity.30 Functionally, these upstream regulatory events converge on downstream metabolic effectors. AS-IV promotes GLUT4 membrane translocation to enhance peripheral glucose uptake and suppresses hepatic gluconeogenesis through the PI3K/Akt/FoxO1 axis. Ji Tianjiao et al further demonstrated that AS-IV downregulated PEPCK and G6Pase via PI3K/Akt/FoxO1 signaling, leading to reduced endogenous glucose production and ameliorated hepatic histopathological injury.31

Taken together, AS-IV appears to regulate insulin signaling through a multi-node network centered on PI3K/Akt. The miR-21/PTEN axis relieves negative regulation of Akt, CTRP3-related signaling enhances insulin signal transmission, and ROS/JNK inhibition protects IRS-1 from stress-induced impairment. These upstream mechanisms ultimately converge on GLUT4-mediated glucose uptake and FoxO1/PEPCK/G6Pase-mediated hepatic gluconeogenesis. Therefore, the anti-diabetic effect of AS-IV on insulin resistance should be interpreted as a network-level modulation of insulin signaling rather than as activation of an isolated PI3K/Akt pathway.

Regulatory Effects of AS-IV on AMPK Signaling

AMPK Activation by AS-IV and Metabolic Reprogramming

Impaired energy metabolism and disrupted lipid homeostasis are key pathological features of T2DM and its metabolism-related complications. As a master cellular energy sensor and metabolic integrator, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) regulates glucose utilization, fatty acid oxidation, lipid synthesis, mitochondrial function, and hepatic metabolic homeostasis. In diabetes, insufficient AMPK activity may contribute to excessive hepatic lipogenesis, impaired fatty acid oxidation, insulin resistance, and metabolic inflammation. Therefore, AMPK represents an important regulatory node linking energy metabolism with inflammatory and oxidative stress responses.

Current evidence suggests that AS-IV may improve metabolic dysfunction by activating AMPK-centered signaling networks rather than by regulating a single metabolic route. In a db/db mouse model of T2DM complicated by non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), AS-IV treatment reduced fasting blood glucose, corrected dyslipidemia, attenuated hepatic lipid accumulation, and improved histopathological injury.32 Mechanistically, AS-IV enhanced AMPK activity and downregulated the lipogenesis-related transcription factor sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (SREBP-1) and fatty acid synthase (FAS), thereby suppressing aberrant lipid synthesis and restoring hepatic lipid homeostasis.32 Network pharmacology analysis further suggested that AS-IV may interact with multiple targets, including VEGFA, Akt2, ROCK1, and STAT3, indicating that AMPK-related metabolic remodeling may be coupled with vascular regulation, insulin signaling, cytoskeletal stress responses, and inflammatory transcriptional programs.32

These findings indicate that AS-IV-mediated AMPK activation is not limited to lipid-lowering effects. Instead, AMPK may serve as a metabolic hub through which AS-IV coordinates glucose metabolism, lipid homeostasis, mitochondrial function, and inflammatory stress. However, current evidence is still largely derived from animal models and network pharmacology predictions. Further studies using pathway-specific inhibitors, genetic knockdown or overexpression models, and standardized dose-response designs are needed to determine whether AMPK activation is a primary driver or a downstream consequence of AS-IV-mediated metabolic improvement.

Regulation of the AMPK–mTOR–Autophagy Axis

Beyond its direct role in energy and lipid metabolism, AMPK dynamically interacts with mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) and the autophagy machinery to coordinate cellular stress adaptation and organ homeostasis. Under diabetic conditions, excessive nutrient stress and impaired AMPK activity may lead to abnormal mTORC1 activation, defective autophagic flux, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, and fibrotic remodeling. Therefore, restoration of AMPK-mTOR-autophagy balance may be particularly relevant to diabetic organ protection.

In models of diabetic nephropathy, AS-IV has been reported to activate AMPK while suppressing aberrant mTORC1 signaling, thereby restoring impaired autophagic flux and improving renal function and structural damage.33 Multiple studies across distinct experimental systems further suggest that AS-IV-mediated mTORC1 inhibition and autophagy restoration may delay glomerular and tubular injury and slow fibrotic progression.34,35 In hepatic injury associated with T2DM, AS-IV also promoted autophagy initiation through AMPK-dependent inhibition of mTOR phosphorylation, accompanied by improvements in insulin resistance, lipid metabolic disturbance, inflammation, and oxidative stress.18

Importantly, the AMPK-mTOR-autophagy axis may function as a point of convergence between metabolic stress and cell-fate regulation. By restoring autophagic flux, AS-IV may facilitate the clearance of damaged organelles, reduce mitochondrial ROS generation, and limit inflammatory amplification, thereby linking metabolic regulation with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. Nevertheless, autophagy has context-dependent effects in diabetic complications, and excessive or insufficient autophagy may both contribute to tissue injury. Future studies should therefore distinguish between autophagy initiation and complete autophagic flux, clarify tissue-specific differences, and determine the optimal intervention window for AS-IV.

Collectively, AS-IV appears to regulate AMPK-centered networks that integrate metabolic reprogramming with mTOR-dependent autophagy and stress adaptation. Through AMPK activation, AS-IV may suppress hepatic lipogenesis, improve lipid and glucose metabolism, restore autophagic flux, and reduce inflammation- and oxidative stress–related organ injury. However, the causal hierarchy among AMPK activation, mTOR inhibition, autophagy restoration, and downstream organ protection remains incompletely defined. More rigorous mechanistic validation and translational studies are required to determine the therapeutic relevance of AMPK-centered regulation in AS-IV-mediated diabetes protection.

Regulatory Effects of AS-IV on Inflammatory Signaling Pathways

Modulation of the NF-κB Inflammatory Hub

Chronic low-grade inflammation is a major driving force in the initiation and progression of diabetes and its complications. Nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) serves as a central inflammatory hub that integrates metabolic stress, oxidative injury, cytokine stimulation, and innate immune activation. Sustained NF-κB activation promotes the transcription of pro-inflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), thereby aggravating insulin resistance and accelerating multi-organ injury.

Experimental evidence indicates that AS-IV suppresses phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of key NF-κB subunits, accompanied by reduced expression of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, suggesting that inhibition of NF-κB-mediated inflammatory transcription is a core component of its anti-inflammatory activity in diabetes-related pathological conditions.36 At the systems level, network pharmacology combined with animal experiments further showed that Buyang Huanwu Decoction containing AS-IV modulates inflammation- and stress-related pathways, including AGE–RAGE, TNF, and HIF-1 signaling, while downregulating core targets such as IL-6, TNF-α, CASP3, and VEGFA.36 These findings suggest that AS-IV-associated anti-inflammatory effects may not be limited to direct NF-κB inhibition, but may involve coordinated regulation of inflammatory cytokines, vascular injury, apoptotic signaling, and stress-response networks. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations further indicated potential interactions between AS-IV and inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and TNF-α, providing complementary support for its multi-level modulation of inflammatory signaling.37

Overall, NF-κB appears to function as a convergence point through which AS-IV links metabolic stress, inflammatory amplification, oxidative injury, and organ damage. However, current evidence remains largely based on experimental and network pharmacology studies, and the direct causal relationship between AS-IV, NF-κB inhibition, and diabetes-related clinical outcomes still requires further validation.

Multi-Node Control of Inflammatory Cascades

Beyond modulation of the NF-κB hub, AS-IV may restrain sustained inflammatory cascades by regulating upstream inflammatory sensors, intercellular communication, and inflammasome activation. In high glucose–induced vascular endothelial injury models, AS-IV downregulated the purinergic receptor P2X7R and inhibited downstream p38 MAPK signaling, thereby restoring endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS)/nitric oxide (NO) function and reducing oxidative stress and inflammatory responses.37 This suggests that the P2X7R–p38 MAPK axis may act as an upstream inflammatory-sensing module through which AS-IV protects endothelial homeostasis.

At the level of intercellular communication, AS-IV has been reported to enhance mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) secretion of exosomes enriched in miR-146a-5p. These exosomes suppress aberrant NF-κB activation by targeting tumor necrosis factor receptor–associated factor 6 (TRAF6), thereby alleviating high glucose–induced endothelial inflammatory injury.38 This “MSC exosome–miR-146a-5p–TRAF6/NF-κB” axis indicates that AS-IV may also exert indirect anti-inflammatory effects by remodeling cell-to-cell communication within the diabetic microenvironment.

The NLRP3 inflammasome represents another inflammatory amplification platform closely linked to NF-κB activation, oxidative stress, and IL-1β maturation. AS-IV has been shown to inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation and thereby mitigate diabetes-related vascular inflammatory injury.39 Consistently, in models involving the nervous system, retina, and gestational diabetes, AS-IV reduced IL-6 and TNF-α levels, attenuated inflammatory responses, and improved metabolic or tissue injury–related phenotypes.40,41

Taken together, AS-IV appears to regulate inflammatory injury through a multi-node network rather than through a single anti-inflammatory target. P2X7R–p38 MAPK signaling may mediate upstream inflammatory sensing, TRAF6/NF-κB signaling may amplify cytokine transcription, and NLRP3 activation may promote inflammasome-dependent inflammatory injury. These modules are functionally interconnected with oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and tissue damage, providing a mechanistic basis for the broad anti-inflammatory effects of AS-IV in diabetes and its complications.

Organ-Specific Features of Inflammatory Modulation

Chronic inflammation is not merely a concomitant feature of diabetes but a causal mechanism driving structural damage and functional decline across organs, and its regulatory patterns vary among different diabetes-related tissues. In diabetic nephropathy, AS-IV upregulates the anti-aging protein klotho and suppresses aberrant NF-κB/NLRP3 inflammasome signaling, thereby improving podocyte function and alleviating inflammation-mediated renal injury.42 In gestational diabetes models, AS-IV mainly attenuates placental inflammation through inhibition of TLR4/NF-κB signaling.43 In the liver, its anti-inflammatory effects appear to be closely coupled with Nrf2/HO-1–mediated antioxidant defense, leading to reduced inflammatory cytokine expression, decreased hepatic glucose production, and improved metabolic homeostasis.44

Taken together, AS-IV suppresses NF-κB-related inflammatory amplification while exhibiting organ-specific regulatory features. In the kidney, its effects are linked to klotho/NF-κB/NLRP3-mediated podocyte protection; in the placenta, they are mainly associated with TLR4/NF-κB inhibition; and in the liver, they appear to involve coordinated anti-inflammatory and antioxidant regulation. This organ-specific pattern suggests that AS-IV-mediated inflammatory modulation should be interpreted as context-dependent network regulation rather than a uniform anti-inflammatory effect across all diabetic tissues.

AS-IV Regulates Oxidative Stress via the Nrf2 Pathway

Activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 Signaling Axis

Oxidative stress is a key driver of the onset and progression of diabetes and its complications. Sustained hyperglycemia induces excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which further promotes mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammatory amplification, lipid peroxidation, and cellular injury. Within the endogenous antioxidant defense system, the nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2)/heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) pathway functions as a central redox-regulatory hub that coordinates antioxidant gene transcription and cellular stress adaptation.

Current studies indicate that AS-IV activates the Nrf2/HO-1 axis and alleviates diabetes-associated oxidative injury across multiple organs and cell types. In podocyte injury models, AS-IV promotes Nrf2 activation and enhances transcription of antioxidant response element (ARE)-dependent genes, thereby reducing lipid peroxidation and increasing superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity; these effects are accompanied by improved mitochondrial membrane potential and reduced mitochondrial dysfunction.45 In models of systemic metabolic disturbance and hepatic injury, AS-IV similarly increases antioxidant enzyme activities, including SOD, glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), and catalase (CAT), reduces malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, and attenuates oxidative damage and apoptosis, with these protective effects closely associated with Nrf2/HO-1 activation.46 Evidence from diabetic nephropathy models further indicates that AS-IV upregulates renal Nrf2 and HO-1 expression, enhances antioxidant defenses, and suppresses inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and TNF-α, suggesting functional coupling between Nrf2-mediated antioxidation and inflammatory restraint.47

Collectively, AS-IV strengthens antioxidant defenses through activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 axis, but its effects extend beyond simple ROS scavenging. By improving mitochondrial function and limiting inflammatory mediator production, Nrf2/HO-1 activation may serve as a bridge linking oxidative stress control, mitochondrial homeostasis, and anti-inflammatory protection.

Nrf2-Related Antioxidant Regulatory Networks

Beyond the canonical Nrf2/HO-1 axis, recent studies suggest that AS-IV extends Nrf2 signaling into a multi-layered antioxidant network by regulating Nrf2 stability, mitochondrial quality control, ROS generation, and non-canonical mitochondrial pathways. This network perspective is important because oxidative stress in diabetes is closely intertwined with mitochondrial injury, inflammation, apoptosis, and ferroptosis.

At the upstream regulatory level, AS-IV inhibits diabetes-associated aberrant activation of glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β), thereby enhancing Nrf2 stability and nuclear translocation and upregulating downstream antioxidant factors such as HO-1 and NAD(P)H quinone dehydrogenase 1 (NQO1).48 Concomitantly, AS-IV reduces cellular and mitochondrial ROS levels and normalizes mitochondrial dynamics–related proteins, including MFN1, MFN2, and OPA1, indicating a role in maintaining mitochondrial structural and functional homeostasis.48 In the context of energy metabolism and mitochondrial function, AS-IV further promotes mitochondrial biogenesis through activation of the SIRT1/PGC-1α/Nrf1 axis and preserves mitochondrial DNA stability through the Nrf2–ARE/TFAM pathway, thereby jointly attenuating oxidative stress and inhibiting apoptosis.49,50

In addition to classical transcriptional regulation, AS-IV may expand its antioxidant effects through non-coding RNA-mediated mechanisms and metabolic-mitochondrial coupling. For instance, AS-IV induces endothelial progenitor cells to secrete exosomes enriched in miR-210, which suppresses NADPH oxidase isoform Nox2 to reduce ROS generation and improve endothelial function.51 Moreover, AS-IV may maintain mitochondrial RNA processing and functional homeostasis through modulation of the CPT1A-HSD17B10-MRPP1 axis, offering a non-canonical explanation for its antioxidant and mitochondria-protective effects.52 At the organ level, these Nrf2-related mechanisms also extend to the nervous system: AS-IV alleviates oxidative stress and inflammatory responses by modulating the Keap1-Nrf2-HO-1/NQO1 pathway and improves diabetes-associated cognitive dysfunction, suggesting that Nrf2-centered antioxidant regulation may have cross-organ relevance.53

Overall, Nrf2 appears to act as a central node through which AS-IV connects ROS control, mitochondrial quality maintenance, antioxidant enzyme induction, and inflammation restraint. However, the relative contribution of canonical Nrf2/HO-1 activation versus upstream regulators such as GSK-3β, SIRT1/PGC-1α, exosomal miRNAs, and mitochondrial RNA-processing pathways remains incompletely defined. Further studies are needed to clarify the causal hierarchy and tissue-specific relevance of these antioxidant mechanisms in diabetes and its complications.

Regulation of Programmed Cell Fate by AS-IV

Suppression of β-Cell Apoptosis

Pancreatic β-cell apoptosis is a key pathological event in the onset and progression of diabetes and is closely associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, an imbalanced Bcl-2/Bax ratio, and aberrant activation of the caspase cascade. Available studies indicate that AS-IV may protect β cells by coordinating mitochondrial protection, antioxidant defense, and apoptosis-related signaling rather than by regulating a single apoptotic marker.

In streptozotocin (STZ)-induced β-cell injury models, AS-IV markedly increased INS-1 cell viability and reduced apoptosis. At the molecular level, AS-IV downregulated Bax and cleaved caspase-3 while upregulating Bcl-2, thereby restoring the Bcl-2/Bax balance and attenuating mitochondria-dependent apoptotic signaling.19 Mechanistic investigations further showed that AS-IV activates SIRT1 and restrains excessive p53 activation, thereby weakening pro-apoptotic transcriptional programs; meanwhile, AS-IV enhances antioxidant defenses through the Akt/GSK-3β/Nrf2 axis and indirectly alleviates oxidative stress–induced mitochondrial injury.19 These findings suggest that β-cell protection by AS-IV involves functional coupling among SIRT1/p53-mediated apoptosis control, Akt/Nrf2-mediated antioxidant defense, and mitochondrial homeostasis.

Regulation of Autophagy and Ferroptosis

Beyond direct anti-apoptotic effects, AS-IV also remodels cell-fate homeostasis by regulating autophagy, ferroptosis, and mitochondrial quality-control networks. Autophagy is essential for eliminating damaged organelles and mitigating metabolic stress, whereas defective autophagic flux may aggravate mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS accumulation, and inflammatory amplification in diabetes-related tissues. Multiple studies indicate that AS-IV restores autophagic flux through the AMPK–mTOR and PI3K/Akt–mTOR axes, thereby ameliorating diabetes-associated organ injury rather than merely enhancing autophagy initiation.54

Ferroptosis has recently been recognized as a regulated cell death modality tightly linked to oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, iron metabolism, and mitochondrial dysfunction in diabetic complications. Current evidence suggests that AS-IV inhibits ferroptosis-related injury in multiple models by strengthening Nrf2-dependent antioxidant defenses, upregulating HO-1, NQO1, and GPX4, and reducing lipid peroxidation.55,56 This anti-ferroptotic effect appears to be closely coupled with mitochondrial quality control. In diabetic nephropathy models, AS-IV preserves mitochondrial functional homeostasis by promoting PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy and suppressing aberrant activation of the HIF-1α/HMOX1 axis, thereby indirectly limiting ferroptotic signal amplification.57 In addition, AS-IV suppresses Drp-1-mediated excessive mitochondrial fission, which may enhance the clearance of damaged mitochondria and reduce ROS-driven cell injury.58 Complementary findings also indicate that AS-IV reduces high glucose-induced ROS accumulation and inhibits ferroptosis by maintaining FOXM1 protein stability, extending the non-canonical mechanistic landscape of its cell-fate regulation.59

Overall, AS-IV-mediated regulation of autophagy and ferroptosis should be interpreted as part of a broader mitochondrial stress-response network. By restoring autophagic flux, improving mitophagy, suppressing excessive mitochondrial fission, and reducing lipid peroxidation, AS-IV may interrupt the vicious cycle among mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS accumulation, inflammatory activation, and regulated cell death.

Cellular Senescence and Homeostatic Maintenance

In addition to apoptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis, cellular senescence is an important pathological consequence of chronic metabolic stress and is closely intertwined with mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, telomere attrition, and disturbed iron homeostasis. Evidence indicates that AS-IV may protect against diabetes-related degenerative changes by maintaining telomere homeostasis and delaying senescence. Hong et al reported that AS-IV increased telomerase activity and upregulated telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) expression in high glucose-induced models, thereby attenuating telomere shortening and suppressing senescence-associated phenotypes; concurrently, AS-IV downregulated p16, Bax, and cleaved caspase-3 while upregulating Bcl-2, collectively improving cellular survival status.60

In cardiac models, AS-IV reduced iron load and oxidative injury and improved cardiac function through activation of the NRF2/ferroportin 1 (FPN1) axis.61 Although this study primarily focused on iron homeostasis, these findings suggest that AS-IV may indirectly delay diabetes-associated cellular senescence by alleviating oxidative stress and iron dysregulation, providing cross-mechanistic evidence for its cell-fate–modulating effects.

Overall, AS-IV appears to maintain cellular homeostasis by coordinating apoptosis suppression, autophagic flux restoration, ferroptosis inhibition, mitochondrial quality control, and senescence delay. These processes are not independent but are connected through shared upstream stressors, including hyperglycemia-induced ROS generation, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammatory amplification, and iron metabolic imbalance. Therefore, the cytoprotective effects of AS-IV in diabetes and related complications are better understood as integrated regulation of the inflammation–oxidative stress–mitochondria–cell fate axis. Organ-Protective Effects of AS-IV in Diabetic Complications

Diabetic Kidney Disease

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is not driven by a single pathological event; rather, it involves the progressive amplification of interconnected processes, including renal tubular epithelial apoptosis,62 mTOR-mediated autophagy and oxidative stress dysregulation,63 as well as altered tissue distribution and pharmacological exposure of active constituents in diabetic nephropathy models.64 These findings provide a mechanistic basis for interpreting AS-IV-related renoprotective effects from a multi-module perspective involving metabolic stress, oxidative injury, inflammation, autophagy, apoptosis, and fibrotic remodeling.

Metabolic and Endothelial Protection

Renal energy metabolic dysfunction and microvascular impairment are key early drivers of DKD. In type 2 DKD animal models, AS-IV reduced fasting glucose and the urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, while attenuating structural lesions such as glomerular hypertrophy, basement membrane thickening, and mesangial matrix expansion. Mechanistically, AS-IV upregulated AMPK/eNOS signaling and increased the p-AMPK/AMPK ratio, supporting a dual improvement in metabolic regulation and endothelial function through activation of the AMPK–eNOS axis.62

Beyond AMPK-related metabolic regulation, AS-IV also appears to restrain stress-activated MAPK–ERK signaling and downstream fibrotic responses. AS-IV suppresses aberrant activation of MEK/ERK/RSK2 and downregulates TGF-β1 during early DKD,63 while inhibition of galectin-3/ERK1/2 signaling is associated with improved renal function and histopathological injury.64 These findings suggest that MAPK–ERK-related pathways may connect metabolic stress, inflammation, and fibrotic remodeling in DKD. At the glomerular endothelial level, AS-IV increases tight-junction proteins, including ZO-1 and claudin-5, and modulates AKT/GSK3β-associated signaling, thereby alleviating oxidative stress and inflammation and stabilizing the filtration barrier.65

Antioxidant Defense and Podocyte Homeostasis

Oxidative stress is a critical driver of podocyte injury, filtration barrier disruption, and proteinuria in DKD. Available evidence suggests that AS-IV-related renal protection is closely associated with activation of Nrf2-dependent antioxidant defenses, increased HO-1, SOD, and GSH levels, and reduced ROS accumulation and lipid peroxidation, thereby mitigating oxidative renal injury.66,67 Mechanistically, AS-IV suppresses Bax/caspase-3 activation through a PPARγ-Klotho-FoxO1-Nrf2/HO-1 regulatory network, supporting coordinated antioxidant and antiapoptotic protection that preserves podocyte structure and function.68 In obese diabetic models, enhanced Nrf2–ARE activity is also accompanied by reduced albuminuria, suggesting that the Nrf2 axis may represent a transferable antioxidant hub across different DKD contexts.69 Cross-talk between Nrf2, AKT, GSK3β, and related nodes may further contribute to dual protection of podocytes and glomerular endothelium.65

Taken together, these findings indicate that AS-IV-mediated podocyte protection is not limited to ROS reduction alone. Instead, antioxidant defense, mitochondrial stabilization, apoptosis suppression, and endothelial barrier maintenance appear to converge on preservation of the glomerular filtration barrier.

Anti-Fibrotic Activity and EMT Suppression

Tubulointerstitial fibrosis and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) represent key pathological bottlenecks in DKD progression toward end-stage kidney disease. In obesity-associated DKD models, AS-IV and its derivatives inhibit TGF-β1/Smad activation and reduce inflammatory mediators, suggesting a potential structure–activity relationship in anti-fibrotic regulation.70 In HFD/STZ models, AS-IV improves albuminuria, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and serum creatinine (Scr), while suppressing TGF-β/Smad-linked oxidative stress and inflammation, supporting in vivo anti-fibrotic efficacy.66

At a finer mechanistic level, AS-IV suppresses high glucose–induced EMT by blocking mTORC1/p70S6K signaling.71 It also attenuates EMT through inhibition of the CX3CL1–RAF/MEK/ERK axis; notably, CX3CL1 overexpression reverses these protective effects, supporting a causal role for this pathway.72 In addition, AS-IV inhibits MAP4K3 and modulates H3K4me1-associated epigenetic modifications, providing a mechanistic rationale for coordinated epigenetic and signal-transduction regulation in renal injury.73 Findings implicating TGF-β/Smad modulation in other experimental settings further extend the mechanistic scope of its anti-fibrotic activity.74

Overall, the anti-fibrotic effects of AS-IV appear to involve multiple convergent routes, including TGF-β/Smad inhibition, mTORC1/p70S6K blockade, MAPK-ERK pathway regulation, and epigenetic modulation. These pathways jointly restrain EMT, inflammatory-fibrotic coupling, and extracellular matrix accumulation during DKD progression.

Inflammation and Cell-Fate Modules

Chronic inflammation and cell-fate dysregulation act as sustained amplifiers of DKD. AS-IV suppresses the PKC-β/NOX4/MAPK axis and reduces phosphorylation of ERK, p38, and JNK, thereby alleviating oxidative stress and inflammation while indirectly limiting apoptosis.75 Evidence from reviews and experimental studies also suggests that AS-IV may activate PI3K/Akt, inhibit NF-κB, and modulate TGF-β/Smad signaling to reduce tubular epithelial apoptosis, reflecting a network pattern in which anti-inflammatory, antiapoptotic, and anti-fibrotic effects operate in parallel.76

Regarding ER stress–inflammasome coupling, AS-IV reduces inflammation and pyroptosis and restores key structural proteins, such as Podocin/Nphs1, by inhibiting the IRE-1α/NF-κB/NLRP3 axis or the TXNIP/NLRP3/GSDMD axis.77,78 Autophagy also acts as a key bridge linking metabolic sensing to inflammatory amplification. AS-IV restores autophagic flux through AMPK/mTOR, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, and sirtuin-related pathways,79 and suppresses NLRP3/Caspase-1/IL-1β-mediated pyroptosis via activation of AMPK/ULK1-dependent mitophagy, thereby attenuating podocyte dedifferentiation and mesangial cell activation.80 Along the SIRT1/NF-κB axis, AS-IV upregulates SIRT1, suppresses p65 acetylation, and enhances autophagic activity; these effects are potentiated by SIRT1 agonists and attenuated by SIRT1 inhibitors or autophagy blockade, strengthening the mechanistic evidence for this pathway.81,82 In addition, inhibition of the ER stress–CHOP–apoptosis axis, including GRP78, p-PERK, ATF4, CHOP, and cleaved caspase-3, has been associated with improved renal function.83–85

Collectively, AS-IV appears to regulate DKD progression through interconnected inflammatory and cell-fate modules. PKC-β/NOX4/MAPK signaling links oxidative stress to inflammatory injury; NF-κB/NLRP3-related pathways amplify pyroptosis; AMPK/ULK1 and SIRT1-related signaling restore autophagy and mitophagy; and ER stress–CHOP inhibition restrains apoptosis. These mechanisms together support a multi-module model of AS-IV-mediated renal protection.

Lipid Metabolic Remodeling and Ferroptosis

Recent evidence has expanded the mechanistic landscape of DKD to include lipid metabolism–inflammation–ferroptosis coupling. Lipidomics analyses suggest that AS-IV upregulates the SIRT1/PPARG/ABCA1 axis, reduces podocyte lipid deposition, and improves the inflammatory microenvironment, indicating that lipid metabolic remodeling may contribute to podocyte protection.86 AS-IV also inhibits fatty acid transport protein 2 (FATP2), thereby reducing fatty-acid uptake, decreasing mitochondrial ROS generation, and suppressing NLRP3 pathway activation; the combined use of FATP2 inhibitors or siRNA further enhances these effects, supporting a mechanistic link between lipid uptake, mitochondrial oxidative stress, and inflammasome activation.87 Likewise, AS-IV downregulates CD36 and inhibits the CD36/NLRP3/IL-1β axis, further suggesting that disruption of lipid uptake-inflammasome coupling may be an important route for attenuating DKD-related inflammatory injury.88

At the level of ferroptosis, AS-IV modulates iron metabolism–related molecules, including HMOX1, FTH1, and TFR1, reduces Fe2⁺ burden and lipid peroxidation, and improves mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production, thereby suppressing high glucose-induced ferroptosis and alleviating mitochondrial dysfunction.89 Integrated proteomic and lipidomic analyses further suggest that AS-IV suppresses aberrant HIF-1α/HMOX1 activation, downregulates ACSL4/TFR1, upregulates GPX4/FTH1, and reverses the accumulation of multiple dysregulated lipid metabolites.90 These findings indicate that the HIF-1α–ferroptosis–lipid homeostasis axis may represent an emerging mechanism linking metabolic disturbance, iron dysregulation, mitochondrial injury, and renal protection in DKD.

Overall, AS-IV exhibits a network-oriented regulatory profile in DKD. AMPK-mediated metabolic sensing may provide a foundational layer, Nrf2-driven antioxidant defense and TGF-β/Smad-centered anti-fibrotic activity form major regulatory trunks, and these pathways are further integrated with MAPK/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling, ER stress, autophagy-pyroptosis coupling, and lipid metabolism-ferroptosis circuitry.91 Through this coordinated multi-module regulation, AS-IV may protect the glomerular filtration barrier, maintain tubular homeostasis, and slow tubulointerstitial fibrotic progression across different renal cell types and pathological stages.

Diabetic Cardiovascular Complications

Endothelial dysfunction represents an early and pivotal event in diabetic cardiovascular complications and is characterized by reduced nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, excessive oxidative stress, impaired vascular repair, and sustained inflammatory activation. Available evidence suggests that AS-IV may exert cardiovascular protection by coordinating endothelial homeostasis, cardiomyocyte fate regulation, and vascular inflammatory responses.67 Therefore, its cardiovascular effects should be interpreted as multi-level regulation of the diabetic vascular microenvironment rather than as a single pathway-specific action.

At the endothelial level, AS-IV improves endothelium-dependent vasorelaxation and contractile responses in the aorta, enhances eNOS phosphorylation and NO production, and reduces ROS/MDA levels and NOX2/NOX4 expression, supporting restoration of endothelial function through the NO/eNOS-oxidative stress axis.92 AS-IV also counteracts aberrant Calpain-1 activation, restores eNOS/NO signaling and antioxidant enzyme activity, and thereby interrupts the oxidative stress–proteolysis-endothelial injury cascade.93 In addition, exosomes derived from endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) loaded with AS-IV activate PI3K/Akt signaling through the miR-214/PTEN axis, promoting endothelial proliferation, migration, and tube formation and providing an intercellular communication-based explanation for vascular repair.94

In diabetic cardiomyopathy, the mechanistic spectrum of AS-IV further extends to ferroptosis, autophagy, and survival signaling. AS-IV downregulates CD36 to suppress ferroptosis and improve cardiac function,95 and it normalizes high glucose–induced excessive autophagy while strengthening antioxidant defenses through the miR-34a/Sirt1 and pAKT/Bcl-2/LC3-related axes.96 In vivo studies further support PI3K/Akt/eNOS as a key signaling hub mediating AS-IV-related cardioprotection, and the attenuation of this benefit by PI3K inhibitors strengthens the causal relevance of this pathway.97–99 Moreover, AS-IV suppresses IL-17-associated inflammatory signaling and downstream MAPK/NF-κB activation, suggesting parallel regulation of inflammatory and fibrotic remodeling programs.100

From a translational perspective, local delivery of AS-IV and intervention in vascular lesions have shown potential, including acceleration of diabetic foot ulcer healing and inhibition of atherosclerosis-associated inflammasome activation.101,102 However, current cardiovascular evidence remains largely preclinical, and a recent review also emphasizes that although AS-IV may concomitantly activate PI3K/Akt/eNOS and Nrf2/HO-1, inhibit NF-κB, and modulate MAPK signaling, limited bioavailability and insufficient clinical evidence remain major bottlenecks.67 Overall, AS-IV appears to protect against diabetic cardiovascular complications through coordinated regulation of endothelial repair, oxidative stress, inflammation, ferroptosis, autophagy, and PI3K/Akt/eNOS-mediated survival signaling, but further pharmacokinetic and clinical validation is still required.

Diabetic Retinopathy and Neuropathy

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) and diabetic neuropathies, including peripheral neuropathy and central cognitive impairment, constitute a highly disabling spectrum of diabetic complications. Although they affect distinct organs and present heterogeneous clinical phenotypes, these complications share several core pathogenic features, including sustained hyperglycemia-driven oxidative stress, chronic inflammatory signaling, mitochondrial dysfunction, microvascular injury, and dysregulated programmed cell death. Current evidence suggests that AS-IV may exert systems-level cytoprotection within the retinal neurovascular unit and the peripheral or central nervous system through coordinated regulation of multiple signaling pathways rather than through a single molecular target. In this framework, PI3K/Akt functions as a central survival and metabolic hub, while the Nrf2-driven antioxidant axis and MAPK/JNK-associated inflammation–apoptosis circuitry jointly form a key molecular scaffold for AS-IV-mediated protection in diabetic retinal and neurological complications.

Vascular Permeability and Angiogenesis

During DR initiation and progression, increased retinal vascular permeability, inflammatory leukocyte adhesion, and HIF-1α/VEGF-A-driven pathological angiogenesis are closely coupled, thereby promoting microvascular damage and secondary neural injury. In animal models, AS-IV attenuates retinal vascular leakage and dilation, reduces leukocyte adhesion and local inflammatory responses, and downregulates VCAM-1, HIF-1α, and VEGF-A expression, suggesting that inhibition of the HIF-1α/VEGF-A axis may simultaneously mitigate inflammation and aberrant angiogenesis during DR progression.103 In type 1 diabetic models, AS-IV likewise lowers IL-6, TNF-α, and MDA levels, increases SOD activity in retinal tissue, suppresses VEGFA expression, and improves retinal histopathology, further supporting a coordinated anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antiangiogenic protective profile.104 In addition, AS-IV improves retinal microvascular architecture by inhibiting NF-κB activation and enhancing Nrf2 signaling, indicating that coupled redox–inflammation regulation may be central to its protection of the retinal neurovascular unit.105

Taken together, these findings suggest that AS-IV-related retinal protection is not limited to antiangiogenic regulation. Instead, HIF-1α/VEGF-A suppression, NF-κB inhibition, Nrf2 activation, and reduced oxidative stress may converge to stabilize the retinal microvascular environment and reduce neurovascular injury.

PI3K/Akt Hub and miRNA Regulation

PI3K/Akt signaling occupies a central position in DR and diabetic neuropathies by integrating metabolic adaptation, cell survival, apoptosis, and autophagy balance. In a type 2 diabetic DR model induced by HFD/STZ, intravenous AS-IV ameliorates retinal structural damage and increases retinal thickness. Network pharmacology combined with experimental validation further suggests that AS-IV regulates PI3K/Akt signaling through core targets, including VEGFA, CASP3, HIF1α, STAT3, and AKT1, accompanied by reduced inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and TNF-α and alleviated oxidative stress.106 These findings implicate PI3K/Akt activation as an important molecular basis for retinal protection, while also linking this hub to angiogenesis, inflammation, oxidative injury, and apoptosis-related targets.

At the level of non-coding RNA regulation, AS-IV upregulates miR-128 and suppresses Bax/Bcl-2, Fas/FasL, and multiple caspase-related apoptotic signals, thereby reducing high glucose–induced apoptosis in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells.107 The same study links PI3K/Akt-mTOR signaling to apoptosis and autophagy phenotypes, suggesting that AS-IV may preserve RPE homeostasis through an miRNA-mediated apoptosis–autophagy coupling mechanism.107 Another network pharmacology-guided in vitro study further reinforces the hub role of Akt, showing that AS-IV activates Akt/Nrf2/HO-1 and Akt/GSK-3β signaling and increases HO-1 expression, thereby suppressing oxidative stress and reducing cellular injury and apoptosis risk.108 These data support the Akt–Nrf2 axis as a major trunk pathway connecting antioxidant defense, cell survival, and retinal cytoprotection.

Overall, PI3K/Akt-centered regulation appears to provide a mechanistic bridge between vascular injury, oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and autophagy in DR. However, much of the current evidence is derived from experimental and network pharmacology-based models, and further validation is needed to clarify the tissue-specific relevance and causal hierarchy of PI3K/Akt-related targets in diabetic retinal and neurological complications.

Mitochondrial Homeostasis and Antioxidant Defense

Beyond the PI3K/Akt hub, restoration of mitochondrial homeostasis and antioxidant defenses constitutes another major mechanistic layer by which AS-IV mitigates DR-related neural injury. Integrated network pharmacology combined with in vivo and in vitro evidence indicates that AS-IV regulates the SIRT1/PGC-1α/Nrf1-associated mitochondrial biogenesis and antioxidant network, reverses hyperglycemia-induced ROS accumulation and loss of antioxidant enzyme activity, improves mitochondrial function, and suppresses apoptosis of retinal ganglion cells.109 Its protective actions also involve coordinated modulation of AKT, HIF-1α, TGF-β1/Smad, and Notch signaling, suggesting that mitochondrial repair, antioxidant defense, angiogenic regulation, and fibrotic remodeling may be functionally interconnected in DR.109 Using a Chinmedomics strategy integrating metabolomics with herbal chemistry, further work showed that AS-IV concurrently modulates AMPK, PI3K/Akt, and ERK pathways, improves DR-associated metabolic dysregulation, and upregulates Nrf2 downstream antioxidant factors, such as GPX4 and GCLM, thereby suppressing oxidative stress and potentially intersecting with ferroptosis-related processes.110 These multi-omics findings support an integrated mechanism linking metabolic remodeling, mitochondrial protection, antioxidant defense, and cell-fate regulation.

Peripheral Neuroprotection and Autophagy Regulation

In diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN), Schwann-cell injury, demyelination, and axonal dysfunction are closely linked to oxidative stress amplification, mitochondrial impairment, apoptosis, and autophagy dysregulation. Evidence indicates that AS-IV enhances autophagic activity via miR-155-mediated PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling, reduces apoptotic markers such as c-C3 and BAX, and increases Bcl-2 levels, thereby attenuating myelin injury and improving neurological function in diabetic rats.111 These findings support a coherent regulatory chain in which AS-IV alleviates DPN through an miRNA-PI3K/Akt/mTOR-autophagy-antiapoptosis axis. In high glucose-injured Schwann-cell models, AS-IV additionally reduces ROS levels and corrects aberrant autophagy activation by downregulating mitophagy-related proteins such as PINK1/Parkin and LC3, improving mitochondrial morphology and membrane potential and thereby mitigating cellular injury.112 This suggests that AS-IV may protect peripheral nerves by coordinating oxidative stress control, mitochondrial quality maintenance, autophagy balance, and Schwann-cell survival.

Central Inflammation–Apoptosis Axis and Cognitive Protection

Diabetes-associated cognitive impairment is tightly associated with persistent oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and amplification of apoptotic signaling. In animal studies, AS-IV improves spatial learning and memory, increases SOD activity, decreases MDA content in hippocampal tissue, downregulates Bax, caspase-3, and p-JNK, and upregulates Bcl-2.113 These findings suggest that inhibition of JNK/MAPK signaling may mitigate oxidative stress-associated apoptosis and support synaptic plasticity, thereby contributing to neuroprotection. Notably, suppression of JNK-related signaling is not restricted to the central nervous system. In high glucose–induced endothelial injury and gestational diabetes-related models, AS-IV inhibits aberrant JNK activation, reduces IL-6 and TNF-α levels, and attenuates apoptosis, suggesting broader protection of neurovascular homeostasis through a JNK-driven inflammation–apoptosis axis.114

Additional evidence indicates that AS-IV may reduce neuronal apoptosis and improve diabetic encephalopathy–associated pathology by inhibiting the EGFR/JAK2/STAT3 pathway, providing an alternative mechanistic route for central nervous system protection.115 Moreover, observations that AS-IV reduces Aβ and tau accumulation and improves cognition suggest that it may modulate the TNF-α-driven EGFR/NF-κB inflammatory axis to improve the neuroinflammatory microenvironment and counter diabetes-related neurodegenerative changes.116 Although cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury is not diabetes-specific, relevant studies show that AS-IV modulates immune responses and antioxidant defenses through NF-κB, MAPK, and Nrf2 signaling and promotes neural repair.117 These findings may provide supportive evidence for the broader neuroprotective potential of AS-IV, but their direct relevance to diabetic neurological complications requires further disease-specific validation.

Overall, AS-IV exhibits a network-oriented regulatory profile in DR and diabetic neuropathies, centered on PI3K/Akt and integrated with Nrf2-driven antioxidant defense, SIRT1/PGC-1α/Nrf1-related mitochondrial biogenesis, HIF-1α/VEGF-A-mediated angiogenesis and permeability regulation, JNK/MAPK inflammation–apoptosis circuitry, and autophagy/mitophagy modules. Through this coordinated signaling architecture, AS-IV may protect the retinal neurovascular unit and the peripheral and central nervous systems. However, most current evidence remains preclinical, and further studies are needed to clarify tissue-specific mechanisms, optimal dosing, and clinical translatability.

Diabetic Foot Ulcer

Diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) is among the most disabling manifestations of diabetic peripheral vascular complications. Impaired wound healing in DFU is closely associated with chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, cellular senescence, mitochondrial dysfunction, ferroptosis, and insufficient angiogenesis. Recent studies suggest that AS-IV may promote wound repair not only through anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects but also by remodeling reparative signaling at the level of RNA epigenetic modification, providing a novel mechanistic framework for chronic diabetic wound intervention.118

Mechanistically, AS-IV upregulates methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) and enhances m6A modification of SIRT1 mRNA, thereby increasing SIRT1 mRNA stability and protein expression. Activated SIRT1 subsequently regulates the FOXO1/AMPK axis, promotes keratinocyte proliferation and migration under an advanced glycation end product (AGE)-injured microenvironment, suppresses local inflammation, and accelerates re-epithelialization and wound closure.118 Consistently, studies using ROS-responsive composite delivery systems show that targeted delivery of AS-IV to high glucose-injured endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) enhances angiogenesis, as reflected by increased CD31 and VWF expression, improves mitochondrial function, and suppresses ferroptosis.119 These findings suggest that the mitochondrial ROS-ferroptosis-angiogenesis module may provide an additional route through which AS-IV supports diabetic wound repair.

Collectively, AS-IV appears to regulate DFU healing through two potentially complementary mechanisms: m6A/SIRT1-driven signaling reprogramming and delivery-enhanced angiogenesis with anti-ferroptotic mitochondrial protection. However, current evidence remains limited and is mainly derived from experimental models. Further studies are needed to clarify optimal delivery strategies, dose-response relationships, wound-stage specificity, and clinical applicability before AS-IV-based interventions can be translated into DFU management.

Pathway-Target-Effect-Model Summary of AS-IV-Regulated Signaling Networks in Diabetes and Its Complications

From a translational perspective, the low oral bioavailability, complex metabolism, multi-peak pharmacokinetic profiles, and unclear exposure-response relationships of AS-IV remain major barriers. Future studies should incorporate standardized model selection, sample-size justification, bias control, reproducibility assessment, clinically relevant endpoints, and integrated PK-PD evaluation. Such work will be essential for determining whether specific AS-IV-regulated signaling pathways are clinically actionable and for identifying the patient populations, formulations, dosing regimens, and combination strategies most likely to support translation.

Another important limitation is the predominantly positive orientation of the current evidence base. Negative, neutral, or inconsistent findings are rarely reported, and publication bias cannot be excluded. Differences among in vitro systems, STZ-induced models, HFD/STZ models, db/db mice, organ-specific injury models, and compound-formula studies may also lead to divergent pathway activation patterns. Therefore, AS-IV-related mechanisms should not be interpreted as universally applicable across all diabetic contexts. Instead, pathway effects should be evaluated according to disease stage, tissue type, metabolic background, and intervention window.

Although the available studies generally support the protective role of AS-IV in diabetes and its complications, direct comparison among studies remains difficult because of substantial heterogeneity in disease models, animal strains, cell types, intervention doses, administration routes, treatment durations, and outcome measurements. In addition, many studies rely on short-term preclinical models, network pharmacology predictions, or limited pathway validation, whereas rigorous dose-response designs, genetic loss- or gain-of-function experiments, and pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic analyses are still insufficient. These limitations may partly explain why promising mechanistic findings have not yet translated into robust clinical evidence.

Conclusion and Future Perspectives

In recent years, increasing basic and translational studies have revealed the multi-pathway protective actions of AS-IV in diabetes and its complications. Overall, AS-IV does not appear to act through a single molecular target. Instead, it may exert network-level regulation centered on insulin signaling and energy metabolism hubs, particularly PI3K/Akt and AMPK, while also modulating inflammatory programs such as NF-κB and TLR4/MyD88, antioxidant defenses such as Nrf2/HO-1, programmed cell-fate pathways including apoptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis, and fibrotic remodeling pathways such as TGF-β/Smad and mTORC1. Through this integrated signaling architecture, AS-IV may contribute to multi-organ protection involving the kidney, cardiovascular system, retina, nervous system, and chronic wound microenvironment.

Building on this mechanistic framework, studies of combination regimens and tissue-repair strategies have further expanded the therapeutic potential of AS-IV. Available evidence suggests that co-administration of AS-IV with sitagliptin,120 ginsenoside Rg1,121 or insulin122 may yield benefits beyond monotherapy, particularly in improving metabolic derangements, oxidative stress, and endothelial function. In addition, AS-IV has been proposed as a functional enhancer that augments the reparative capacity of bone marrow–derived mesenchymal stem cells123 and adipose-derived stem cells124 in diabetic kidney disease models. Nevertheless, most studies remain focused on functional endpoints, whereas pathway-level complementarity, temporal dependence, dose-matching relationships, and potential interaction effects among different interventions have not been systematically resolved. Future work should therefore clarify whether AS-IV acts as a signaling hub, a pathway amplifier, or a context-dependent adjunct within combination-therapy paradigms.

Moreover, long-term outcomes of diabetic complications depend not only on global metabolic improvement but also on the stability of organ- and cell-specific structural units. In diabetic kidney disease, for example, the integrity of slit diaphragm proteins such as Nephrin and Podocin is a direct determinant of proteinuria and disease progression. Although AS-IV has been reported to preserve these structural elements and attenuate renal pathology,125 many studies still rely primarily on terminal readouts such as urinary protein, serum creatinine, and blood urea nitrogen. Consequently, causal validation along the “cellular barrier–molecular target–clinical phenotype” axis remains insufficient. Future studies should incorporate standardized tissue- and cell-specific endpoints, genetic interventions, selective pathway inhibition or activation, and longitudinal assessment to strengthen mechanistic rigor and testability.

At the translational level, the pharmacokinetic complexity of AS-IV remains a major challenge. Evidence indicates that, in multi-component formulations, AS-IV can exhibit multi-peak plasma concentration–time profiles and multi-phase metabolic features,126 suggesting that its dose–response relationship may not be linear. Meanwhile, although several key signaling pathways regulated by AS-IV have been repeatedly reported,127 the current evidence base is still dominated by in vitro and animal studies, with substantial heterogeneity in disease models, dosing regimens, routes of administration, intervention duration, and endpoint selection. These limitations restrict direct extrapolation to clinical diabetes management. Future efforts should integrate standardized PK–PD evaluation, clinical samples, multi-omics analyses, and real-world evidence to identify translatable biomarkers and define patient subgroups most likely to benefit.

In addition, emerging regulatory dimensions of AS-IV, such as post-translational modifications, remain incompletely explored.128 Expanding AS-IV sources and systematically interrogating structure–function differences may also facilitate quality control, standardization, and sustainable development.129 These issues are particularly important for natural product–based drug development, where chemical consistency, bioavailability, pharmacological exposure, and reproducible efficacy must be considered together.

Collectively, AS-IV has a well-supported multi-target mechanistic foundation and promising organ-protective potential in diabetes and its complications. However, its translation from mechanistic insight to clinical application will require higher-level causal evidence, standardized disease models, rigorous PK–PD evaluation, optimized formulation or delivery strategies, and well-designed prospective clinical studies. The key future task is not only to confirm that AS-IV regulates multiple pathways, but also to determine which mechanisms are clinically actionable, in which patient populations, and under what dosing and delivery conditions.
