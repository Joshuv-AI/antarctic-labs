In Vitro Simulated Digestion Unveils the Multifunctional Benefits of Freeze-Dried Cowpea Fiber: Prebiotic, Hypoglycemic, Antioxidant Activity, and Postbiotic-Mediated Anti-Inflammatory Effects
Authors: Chanikan Sonklin, Suchanaree Limsumphan, Nidtaya Srisuwan, Pinchuta Bunphueak, Vilai Rungsardthong, Peerapong Pornwongthong, Lueacha Tabtimmai, Saranya Sedtananun, Benjawan Thumthanaruk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cowpea (Vigna unguiculata) is an underutilized legume rich in dietary fiber (DF) and bioactive compounds. However, the functional potential of its insoluble DF (IDF), particularly that derived from immature or downgraded pods, has not been comprehensively investigated. This study aimed to investigate the physicochemical characteristics, functional properties, antioxidant activity, and biological effects of freeze-dried cowpea powder (CP) and its IDF-rich fraction (CP-IDF). CP was analyzed for proximate composition and techno-functional properties, including water retention capacity (WRC), oil retention capacity (ORC), and glucose-binding capacity. CP-IDF was prepared via sequential enzymatic hydrolysis and characterized for chemical composition, microstructure (scanning electron microscopy), crystallinity (X-ray diffraction), and functional groups (Fourier transform infrared). Simulated in vitro gastrointestinal digestion was performed to obtain digested CP-IDF, and the antioxidant activity of digesta supernatants was evaluated using DPPH and ABTS assays. Prebiotic potential was assessed using Bifidobacterium longum and Lacticaseibacillus casei, while anti-inflammatory activity was determined in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages via nitric oxide (NO) inhibition. Results showed that CP possessed high carbohydrate and fiber contents, with WRC (4.27 ± 0.31 g/g) and ORC (3.90 ± 0.19 g/g), indicating strong hydration and lipid-binding capacities. CP-IDF was rich in polysaccharides and uronic acids, consistent with pectic components. Structural analyses revealed a partial disruption of cellulose crystallinity after digestion. Antioxidant activity increased after simulated gastric and intestinal digestion, corresponding to enhanced release of phenolic compounds. Although CP-IDF was not directly fermented under the tested conditions, its fermentation-derived products (cell-free supernatants) exhibited biological activity. Specifically, these products demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects by modulating NO production in macrophages, suggesting a potential postbiotic mechanism. These findings highlight the value-added potential of cowpea-derived IDF as a functional ingredient with antioxidant and postbiotic-associated bioactivities.

Introduction

Cowpea (Vigna unguiculata), belonging to the Fabaceae family, demonstrates strong adaptability across varied climates, especially in heat- and drought-stressed regions of Africa, Latin America, Asia, southern Europe, and the United States. Cowpea is widely recognized as an important pulse crop that supports global food security and promotes human health, primarily through its diverse nutritional composition and nutraceutical potential. It is widely incorporated into diverse cuisines and snack products and can be consumed as whole seeds, in canned or frozen forms, or processed into flour for baking purposes. ,

Most aerial parts of this crop are edible, including leaves, pods, and seeds at different maturity stages, thereby offering food materials with distinct chemical compositions and processing responses. Although dry grains represent the dominant form consumed globally, immature pods and seeds are also utilized in several regions, particularly at early harvest stages, where compositional and functional attributes differ from those of mature grains. , The use of immature pods and grains as vegetables is particularly prevalent in Southeast Asia. The production of cowpea at the immature pod stage represents a sustainable agricultural strategy, as these products require lower water inputs and shorter cultivation periods, thereby enhancing resilience to climate variability and abiotic stress conditions.

Dietary fiber (DF) is an essential carbohydrate that supports multiple health-promoting functions. Unlike other carbohydrates, DF resists digestion and absorption in the gastrointestinal tract and undergoes partial or complete fermentation by intestinal microorganisms in the colon. In addition to their nutritional role, plant-derived foods contain numerous bioactive compounds that contribute to health-promoting effects. A substantial proportion of these phytochemicals is associated with the insoluble DF (IDF) fraction, underscoring the importance of IDF as a useful indicator of bioactive food components. , Intrinsic and intact plant fibers are naturally found in vegetables, whole grains, fruits, cereals, and flours and remain in their original form, mainly as insoluble fibers. Functional fibers, on the other hand, are isolated or synthetic fibers that must demonstrate a beneficial physiological effect to be recognized as a DF by the FDA.

In Thailand, cowpea is cultivated as a minor crop within rotation systems involving major crops such as cassava, rice, and maize. Predominantly grown in northern regions, cowpea is harvested at the immature pod stage for industrial processing, where pods meeting export quality standards are trimmed, frozen, and distributed to international markets, underscoring the need to understand the effects of processing on quality attributes and chemical compositions. This issue highlights the need for alternative utilization and processing strategies to improve the value and quality of the off-grade cowpea pods. Interestingly, cowpea pods, together with leaves and other aboveground tissues, are rich in DF, mainly in an insoluble form, whereas the soluble fraction contributes to viscosity in aqueous systems. Cowpea is widely recognized as a source of DF and bioactive compounds, including polyphenols with demonstrated antioxidant activity and associated health benefits. Previous studies have extensively characterized the physicochemical and functional properties of cowpea-derived materials, particularly from edible-grade sources. In contrast, downgraded or off-grade cowpea, which does not meet commercial quality standards, is typically discarded, despite being a low-cost and potentially valuable resource. As a result, these materials represent substantial and underutilized biomass within the agricultural supply chain.

However, their functional and biological potential remains largely unexplored. This gap highlights a clear opportunity for value-added utilization in the context of sustainable food systems. More importantly, current research has primarily focused on composition analysis or direct functional attributes, with limited insight into the structure–function relationship of IDF under gastrointestinal conditions. The influence of digestion-induced structural modification on the release of bound phenolic compounds and their subsequent bioactivity remains insufficiently explored. In addition, most research has focused on direct prebiotic effects, while the potential contribution of postbiotic-mediated biological activity derived from structurally recalcitrant and nonfermentable fiber fractions has not been addressed. To address these research gaps, this study aims to (1) investigate the physical and functional properties, including water retention capacity (WRC), oil retention capacity (ORC), and glucose adsorption capacity of freeze-dried downgraded cowpea referred to as intact cowpea fiber (CP); (2) characterize the physicochemical properties of IDF derived from freeze-dried cowpea (CP-IDF) (e.g., chemical compositions, microstructures, functional groups, and crystalline structures), and (3) investigate the probiotic stimulating effects and antioxidant activity of digesta obtained after simulated digestion of CP-IDF. By integrating compositional, structural, and functional analyses, this study provides a comprehensive understanding of the structure–function relationship of cowpea-derived DF. The findings support the valorization of downgraded cowpea as an underutilized biomass resource and highlight its potential applications in the development of functional food systems.

Materials and Methods

The overall experimental workflow is illustrated in Figure .

Schematic representation of the experimental workflow, including cowpea processing, in vitro digestion, fractionation of DF, and evaluation of physicochemical and bioactive properties.

Materials and Reagents

Fresh downgraded cowpea (V. unguiculata) was obtained from Lanna Agro Industry Co., LTD (LACO), Chiang Mai, Thailand. The samples were washed with running drinking water and air-dried at ambient conditions. Freeze-drying was performed using a freeze-dryer (GFD30S, Grisrianthong, Thailand) for 24 h to preserve the physicochemical properties and microstructure of cowpea. The freeze-dried cowpea was ground using a blender and sieved through a 45-mesh sieve to obtain a uniform powder, hereafter referred to as CP. The powder was packed in sterile containers, protected from light, and stored at −18 °C until further analysis.

Proximate and Chemical Properties Analysis

Proximate analysis: moisture, protein, fat, ash, total fiber, and total carbohydrate contents of fresh and freeze-dried samples were determined using AOAC methods. Protein was calculated using a nitrogen conversion factor of 6.25. Moreover, the chemical properties: total polysaccharide, total uronic acids, total soluble protein, and total phenolic contents (TPCs) of dried cowpea (CP) and dried cowpea IDFs (CP-IDF) were determined in triplicate. The total polysaccharide was analyzed using the phenol-sulfuric acid test. The M-hydroxydiphenyl method was employed to measure the uronic acid levels in the sample, where the standard used was galacturonic acid. Bradford’s method was utilized to assess the soluble protein concentrations, where bovine serum albumin was used as the standard. The content of total polyphenol was estimated by the Folin-Ciocalteu colorimetric method.

Functional Properties Analysis

Water Retention Capacity

The initial mass of the dry sample was recorded as M 1. The sample was placed in a centrifuge tube, and the combined mass was recorded as M 0. Distilled water was added, and the mixture was homogenized and allowed to hydrate for 18 h. After centrifugation, the hydrated residue in the tube was dried at 105 °C to a constant weight, which was recorded as M2. The WRC was calculated using the following equation WRC(g/g)=(M2−M0)M1

Oil Retention Capacity

For the determination of oil retention capacity (ORC), the sample was placed in a centrifuge tube, and the initial mass was recorded as M 0. Soybean oil (10 mL) was added into a centrifuge tube (M 1), and the mixture was magnetically stirred at 25 ± 1 °C for 12 h. After centrifugation, the precipitate was gently blotted to remove excess surface oil, and the final mass was recorded as M 2. The ORC was calculated using the following equation ORC(g/g)=(M2−M0)M1

Glucose Binding Capacity

The glucose adsorption capacity assay was conducted following a previously reported method with minor modifications from Tan et al. (2023). 50 mg of sample (m) was accurately weighed and added to 20 mL of glucose solution (C 0) at a concentration of 3 mg/mL. The mixture was stirred at 37 °C for 6 min and then centrifuged at 3780g for 20 min. The supernatant was collected to determine the glucose concentration (C 1) using the glucose oxidase–peroxidase method, and the glucose adsorption capacity was calculated according to the following equation GBC(mg/g)=(C0−C1)×0.02m

Preparation of Cowpea IDF-Rich Fraction

The cowpea IDF-rich fraction was prepared with slight modifications to a previously reported method. Cowpea powder (CP) was suspended in distilled water and subjected to sequential heating. After cooling, the pH was adjusted, and enzymatic hydrolysis was performed using α-amylase, followed by heat inactivation. The suspension was subsequently adjusted to alkaline conditions and incubated and then acidified and treated with glucoamylase, with enzyme activity terminated by heating. The hydrolysates were precipitated with ethanol and stored at low temperature. The resulting precipitates, designated as the IDF-rich fraction (CP-IDF), were collected by filtration and dried.

In Vitro Digestive Simulation of CP-IDF

The in vitro digestion of CP-IDF was conducted following the method of Yu and co-workers (2024), based on the INFOGEST protocol with minor modification. The compositions and preparation procedures of all digestion buffers used in each phase (oral, gastric, and intestinal) are described in the corresponding sections below. Structural analysis [scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Fourier transform infrared (FTIR), and X-ray diffraction (XRD)] of digested CP-IDF was carried out and compared to CP-IDF (before digestion). Changes in reducing sugar content (C r) of digested CP-IDF were examined by the dinitro salicylic acid method. In addition, TPC and antioxidant activity (DPPH and ABTS) were evaluated to assess the functional changes occurring during in vitro digestion.

Simulated Salivary Fluid Digestion

The digestion system was prepared by adding simulated salivary fluid (SSF) (35%, v/v), α-salivary amylase (150 U/mL), CaCl2 (1.5 mM), and deionized water to CP-IDF supernatant (5.0 mL, 25 mg/mL) in a digestion tube, resulting in a final reaction volume of 10 mL. After thorough mixing by vortexing, the suspension was incubated at 37 °C under shaking conditions (300 rpm) for 2 h. During the digestion period, aliquots were withdrawn at 30 min intervals and subjected to subsequent analyses.

Simulated Gastric Fluid Digestion

The gastric digestion system was prepared by combining a 10.0 mL aliquot of the CP-IDF salivary digest with simulated gastric fluid (SGF), pepsin, CaCl2, and deionized water to obtain final concentrations of approximately 38% (v/v) SGF, 200 U/mL pepsin, and 0.15 mM CaCl2 in a total reaction volume of 19.8 mL. Following vortex mixing, the pH of the suspension was adjusted to 3.0 using 1.0 mol/L HCl. The mixture was subsequently incubated at 37 °C under constant agitation at 300 rpm for 2 h. Aliquots were withdrawn at 30 min intervals during the gastric digestion phase and subjected to further analyses.

Simulated Intestinal Fluid Digestion

The intestinal digestion system was established by combining a 20.0 mL aliquot of the CP-IDF gastric digest with simulated intestinal fluid (SIF), trypsin, CaCl2, and deionized water to achieve final concentrations of approximately 30% (v/v) SIF, 20 U/mL trypsin, and 0.37 mM CaCl2 in a total reaction volume of 39.85 mL. The pH of the mixture was subsequently adjusted to 7.0.

Structural Analysis

Scanning Electron Microscopy

The differences among CP, CP-IDF, and DCP-IDF were examined using field-emission SEM (FEI-Quanta 450, USA) to observe their morphological characteristics under magnifications of 500× with an accelerating voltage of 10 kV.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy

The FTIR spectra of CP-IDF and DCP-IDF were determined by FTIR spectroscopy (INVENIO S, BRUKER, Germany). All samples were mixed with dried KBr across 4000–400 cm–1.

X-ray Diffraction

The crystalline structure of CP-IDF and DCP-IDF was determined by XRD using a Bruker AXS D8 ADVANCE diffractometer (Bruker, Germany) equipped with nickel filters. Measurements were conducted with Cu Kα radiation at 30 kV and 10 mA, using a step size of 0.02°, a scanning speed of 6°/min, and a diffraction range of 5–80° (2θ).

Chemical and Bioactivity Analysis

Change in Reducing Sugar Content (C r)

The change in reducing sugar content (C r) of digested CP-IDF was examined by the dinitrosalicylic acid method, as described by Miller.

Total Phenolic Content

The TPC was analyzed by the Folin–Ciocalteu standard method. Each sample (40 μL) was mixed with 10 μL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, and then 200 μL of 30% Na2CO3 was added. The absorbance of the mixture was measured at 725 nm after 30 min at room temperature. A gallic acid standard curve was generated. Results were represented as mg of gallic acid equivalents per gram of sample (mgGAE/g sample).

Analysis of Antioxidant Activity in CP-IDF Digest

The lysates collected from each phase of in vitro digestion were centrifuged to obtain the clarified supernatants for subsequent analyses. The antioxidant capacity was evaluated using the DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging assays.

The DPPH radical scavenging activity of IDF fractions was measured according to a modified literature method. Briefly, 20 μL of CP-IDF digesta was mixed with 20 μL of 0.1 mM DPPH in 95% ethanol and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was recorded at 517 nm. Lower absorbance indicated higher radical scavenging activity. Ethanol (95%) and gallic acid were used as the blank and positive control, respectively. The DPPH free radical scavenging activity was calculated as follows DPPHradicalscavengingactivity(%)=(absorbanceblank−absorbancesample)absorbanceblank

The ABTS radical scavenging activity of IDF fractions was determined according to previously reported methodologies. Briefly, 190 μL of ABTS radical cation solution (0.1 mM) was mixed with 20 μL of CP-IDF digesta, and then the mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at 734 nm against control (EtOH 70%). The results were expressed as the ABTS free radical scavenging activity, calculated as follows ABTSradicalscavengingactivity(%)=(absorbanceblank−absorbancesample)absorbanceblank

Functional Analysis

Prebiotic Activity of CP IDF-Rich Fraction

Bifidobacterium longum and Lacticaseibacillus casei were used to evaluate the prebiotic activity of CP-IDF. Each strain was individually inoculated into 50 mL of sterile de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) medium and cultured under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 48 h without agitation. Prior to experimentation, cultures were subcultured three consecutive times to ensure strain activation. Prebiotic activity was assessed using sugar-free MRS medium as the blank control (SF). Glucose (Glu), galactooligosaccharides (GOS), CP-IDF, and digested CP-IDF (DCP-IDF) were supplemented into sugar-free MRS medium at a final concentration of 0.5% (w/v). The media were sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, 1% (v/v) of diluted probiotic suspension was inoculated into each treatment condition, and cultures were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h under anaerobic conditions. Bacterial growth was quantified by serially diluting 100 μL of each culture and plating on MRS agar for colony enumeration. All experiments were performed in triplicate. For pH and lactic acid analyses, cultures were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min, and the resulting supernatants were collected and stored at −20 °C until analysis. The pH of the supernatants was measured using a calibrated pH meter. Lactic acid concentration was determined by titration with standardized NaOH solution to the phenolphthalein end point, and the volume of NaOH consumed was used to calculate lactic acid content according to the following equation Lacticacid(g/L)=VNaOH×MNaOH×90.08samplevolume(mL)×1000 where V NaOH is the volume of 0.1 M NaOH used in titration, M NaOH is the molarity of NaOH, and 90.08 g/mol is the molar mass of lactic acid.

Determination of NO Production

For cell culture production, RAW264.7 (ATCC TIB-71) was cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% antibiotic solution. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. RAW264.7 macrophages (1 × 105 cells per well) were seeded in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. After attachment, cells were treated with fresh medium containing 1% (v/v) cell-free supernatant (CFS) of CP-IDF prepared under each test condition. Diclofenac (DIC, 75 μg/mL) was used as a positive anti-inflammatory control. After 1 h of pretreatment, the cells were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 1 μg/mL) for 24 h. Untreated and LPS-stimulated cells served as negative and positive controls, respectively. Culture supernatants were collected to quantify NO production using the Griess Reagent System (Promega, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell viability was determined using the MTT assay. Briefly, 10 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well, followed by incubation for 3 h at 37 °C. After visible formazan crystal formation, the medium was discarded and replaced with 50 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to dissolve the crystals completely. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm with a reference wavelength of 630 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed as the percentage relative to the untreated control.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. The data shown are representative results. Values were calculated as the mean and standard deviation (SD). Statistical differences among groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance, followed by Dunnett’s posthoc test using the GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0). Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05 and highly significant at p < 0.01.

Functional properties provide key insights into interactions among the structural components of food matrices and their resultant physicochemical characteristics. The CP samples exhibited a WRC of 4.27 ± 0.31 g water/g sample and an ORC of 3.90 ± 0.19 g oil/g sample (Table ). WRC reflects the ability of hydrated materials to retain water under centrifugal or compressive forces, whereas ORC expresses the capability of the material to physically entrap lipids within its structural network. These functional behaviors are strongly associated with the presence of DF, particularly IDF, which contributes to water and oil binding through its porous microstructure, surface hydrophilicity, and rigid polysaccharide framework. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that IDF content correlates with enhanced WRC and ORC, as low solubility and structural integrity of IDF allow it to retain water within its capillary spaces while simultaneously immobilizing lipids. − The notable WRC and ORC values observed in CP align with these findings, suggesting that its fiber profile is likely dominated by IDF. This provides a strong rationale for further investigating the composition and structural characteristics of CP-derived IDF to better understand its functional contributions and potential applications in food formulation.

Effect of In Vitro Digestive Simulation on Structural Changes of IDF from Cowpea

Micromorphology Analysis

The micromorphology features of the samples were examined by SEM (Figure ). SEM provides direct evidence of the hierarchical organization of polysaccharides, offering insight into their structural arrangement within food matrices. The CP powder exhibited an intact morphology with well-preserved cell walls and a compact, bundle-like three-dimensional structure without noticeable cavities (Figure A). To obtain IDF (CP-IDF), digestible starch was removed by treatment with α-amylase and amyloglucosidase. SEM observation of CP-IDF revealed exfoliation and broom-like filament splitting patterns (red arrow), resulting in a roughened surface due to peeling of fibrous strands, likely caused by disruption of hydrogen bonding between microfibrils (Figure B). Enzymatic treatment further produced numerous cracks and loosened the overall structure, consistent with previous reports. Following simulated gastrointestinal digestion (saliva–gastric–intestinal), DCP-IDF displayed highly rough and fragmented surfaces (Figure C), indicating that acidic and enzymatic conditions disrupt the complex internal polysaccharide architecture, providing insight into the behavior of IDF during digestion. Thus, SEM observations indicated that IDF derived from cowpea (CP-IDF) exhibited a rough, exfoliated filamentous surface after enzymatic starch removal, which became further fragmented and loosened following simulated gastrointestinal digestion, reflecting the structural changes of IDF during digestion. Distinct surface morphologies of CP, CP-IDF, and DCP-IDF are shown in the SEM images, with yellow arrows indicating the highlighted structural features.

SEM images of CP (A), CP-IDF (B), and DCP-IDF (C) at 500× magnification. Red arrows indicate broom-like filament structures, while yellow arrows highlight representative surface morphological features.

Structural Organization Analysis

XRD is a robust analytical technique widely used to elucidate the crystallinity and structural organization of polycrystalline food biomaterials. In this study, the diffraction pattern of CP displayed a relatively broad peak centered at 22.0° (2θ), reflecting its low-ordered structural domains (Figure ). In contrast, CP-IDF exhibited distinct crystalline reflections at 15.8° and 22.0° (2θ), which correspond to the characteristic diffraction angles of cellulose I. Additionally, a diffuse noncrystalline peak at 34.7° (2θ) was observed, indicating the presence of amorphous regions within the DF matrix.

XRD patterns of CP-IDF and DCP-IDF. The red arrows indicate the sharp diffraction peaks observed in the samples.

Both intra- and intermolecular hydrogen bonds are present in cellulose via hydroxyl groups, which contribute to several ordered crystalline arrangements in DF. The reflection observed near 22°–23° (2θ) represents the crystalline portions of cellulose, which form tightly packed polysaccharide networks that reinforce the structural integrity of IDF. In contrast, the diffuse peak around 18° (2θ) arises from amorphous regions comprising loosely arranged noncrystalline cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. These amorphous domains are functionally important in food systems because they enhance the capacity of fiber to bind water, interact with other macromolecules, and influence hydration-driven textural changes. Therefore, in the CP powder, the peak at 22.0° (2θ) was attributed to cellulose crystals, a feature that aligns with the orderly surface morphology observed in the SEM analysis. This crystalline arrangement reflects the inherent rigidity of the native fiber matrix, which can influence hydration behavior and functional properties in subsequent food applications. For DCP-IDF, the broad peak spanning 15.8°–22° (2θ) suggested the coexistence of both crystalline and amorphous regions. This mixed pattern may result from simulated gastrointestinal digestion, which can disrupt the native structural organization and produce materials with reduced crystallization capacity. The experimental results showed that the crystalline structures of CP powder and DCP-IDF were altered after simulated gastrointestinal digestion. The broadening of XRD peaks indicated a partial disruption of the ordered cellulose domains and an increase in amorphous regions.

FTIR Analysis

The functional groups of the chemical structure of CP powder and DCP-IDF were analyzed using FTIR, as shown in Figure . In general, CP powder and DCP-IDF had similar absorption bands at 3415 cm–1 and 2923 cm–1, assigned to the vibrations of the O–H bond of the hydroxyl groups (O–H) and the vibrations of C–H of methyl groups and methylene groups. The absorption peak at 1635 cm–1 was regarded as the characteristic bending or stretching of aromatic hydrocarbons of lignin, which was also notably observed in CP powder and DCP-IDF. , The absorption peak at 1320 cm–1 was caused by the angular vibration of C–H, representing the typical structure of a carbohydrate skeleton.

FTIR spectra of the CP-IDF and DCP-IDF.

The region of frequency between 1200 and 800 cm–1 may be called the polysaccharide fingerprint. The CP powder presented weak absorption peaks at 1154, 1104, and 1019 cm–1 compared to DCP-IDF. However, CP powder showed a strong peak at 889 cm–1, which clearly showed characteristic absorption peaks of the β-glucoside bond. The intensity of this peak was reduced after gastric digestion, which was likely due to the breaking of the glycosidic bond. However, the overall functional group profile was largely preserved, suggesting that the hydrophilic moieties and reactive chemical groups within DCP-IDF remained structurally unchanged.

Effect of In Vitro Digestive Simulation of IDF from Cowpea on Phenolic Content and Antioxidant Activity

The Changes in Reducing Sugar (C r) and Total Phenol Content

The cleavage of glycosidic linkages and the gradual breakdown of CP-IDF during the digestive process are likely to contribute to variations in their reducing sugar contents (C r). Although the INFOGEST protocol recommends phase-specific incubation times, a fixed duration of 2 h was applied for each digestion phase in this study. The extended incubation during the SSF phase was intentionally selected to ensure sufficient α-amylase activity for effective hydrolysis of residual digestible components, thereby enabling a clearer evaluation of the indigestible DF fraction in subsequent stages. A gradual increase in C r was observed across the digestive phases, with minimal change in the oral stage, a moderate rise during gastric digestion, and the highest release occurring in the intestinal phase (Figure A). Although reducing sugar levels increased during digestion, the FTIR spectra of the insoluble fiber remained largely unchanged, indicating depolymerization without alteration of major functional groups. In contrast, XRD revealed higher semicrystallinity after digestion, suggesting that enzymatic activity preferentially degraded amorphous regions, while crystalline domains of the insoluble fiber were retained.

Change of reducing sugar content (C r) (A), TPC (B), DPPH radical scavenging activity (C), and ABTS radical scavenging activity (D) of CP-IDF after in vitro saliva and gastric and intestinal digestion (data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3), p < 0.05).

The TPC of the CP-IDF digesta supernatant showed no statistically significant difference after the simulated gastric and intestinal phases (Figure B). In contrast, following the simulated salivary digestion phase, the TPC increased markedly, reaching approximately 2-fold higher with statistical significance. These findings suggest that phenolic compounds are not chemically static during digestion but may undergo biotransformation processes that influence their structural state, thereby affecting their bioaccessibility and potential bioactivity.

However, the application of a fixed 2 h incubation time across all digestion phases represents a limitation of this static in vitro model. Under physiological conditions, oral transit is brief, whereas gastric emptying and intestinal transit times vary depending on the food matrix and individual physiological factors. These dynamic conditions can significantly influence the kinetics of macronutrient breakdown and the release of the bound phenolic compounds. Therefore, the standardized approach adopted in this study ensures analytical consistency and facilitates comprehensive release of components from the fiber matrix. It may lead to partial overestimation of early stage hydrolysis and phenolic release compared to physiological conditions. Nevertheless, this extended incubation provides a controlled framework to systematically evaluate the digestion-induced structural modifications and functional responses of the IDF, enabling clearer interpretation of structure–function relationships under standardized conditions.

In Vitro Antioxidant Activity after Digestion

An imbalance between free radical production and the antioxidant defense system of the body has been closely associated with the onset and progression of several chronic conditions including inflammation, cardiovascular dysfunction, cerebrovascular impairment, and cancer. Antioxidants act by scavenging excess reactive species and supporting redox equilibrium, thereby contributing to the maintenance of physiological stability and cellular protection. The DPPH and ABTS activity of the CP-IDF digesta supernatant at a duration of 0.5–2 h of incubation time is shown in Figure C,D. The higher DPPH activity of the CP-IDF digesta supernatant from the gastric and intestinal simulation process at 1.5–2 h incubation time was more than that of the saliva simulation process, which is consistent with the phenolic content of each fraction (Figure C). However, ABTS activity of the digesta supernatants of CP-IDF was higher in the saliva phase simulation process at 1.5–2 h incubation time when compared to the other two fractions (Figure D). The digestion process, whereby food is exposed to digestive enzymes and variations in pH, is crucial to causing the release of phenolic compounds from the molecules that have bound them. The released phenolic compounds are the main contributors to the antioxidant properties. Antioxidant compounds measured by the DPPH assay appear to differ from those detected using the ABTS method. Maintaining antioxidant activity after gastrointestinal digestion is essential, as antioxidants can neutralize excess reactive oxygen species (ROS), reduce oxidative stress, and protect against related disorders. Elevated ROS, however, may induce inflammation and contribute to conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease. Therefore, CP-IDF may contain phenolic compounds with potential applications in functional-food development.

Effect of Digested CP-IDF on Probiotic Fermentation

Fermentable DFs have been extensively investigated for their potential as functional food ingredients due to their ability to modulate gut microbiota. A defining characteristic of prebiotics is their capacity to selectively stimulate the growth of beneficial gastrointestinal microorganisms, thereby conferring health-promoting effects. To evaluate the prebiotic properties of DCP-IDF, representative colonic probiotic strains, B. longum and Lacticaseibacillus casei, were employed as fermentative models to assess microbial utilization and growth responses. Under glucose-supplemented conditions, both probiotic strains exhibited clear substrate preference, as evidenced by enhanced growth compared with sugar-free controls (Figure A,D). As expected, galactooligosaccharides (GOS) with a degree of polymerization (DP) ranging from 2 to 8, which are well-established prebiotics, significantly promoted the growth of both strains. Lacticaseibacillus casei exhibited a slightly faster growth rate in the presence of low-DP GOS (DP 2–3) compared with B. longum. In contrast, Bifidobacterium strains demonstrated a greater capacity to utilize GOS with higher DP (DP 2–8) and branched structures, which has been associated with the expression of membrane-associated glycoside hydrolase family 53 (GH53) endogalactanase (GalA). In contrast, supplementation with CP-IDF and DCP-IDF did not support probiotic growth in either strain after 24 h of fermentation. Lactic acid, a primary fermentative metabolite produced by probiotic microorganisms, contributes to a reduction in culture pH. Consistent with this relationship, pH reduction and lactic acid production were positively correlated under glucose-supplemented conditions (Figure B,E). GOS supplementation resulted in a modest but significant increase in lactic acid production by B. longum, whereas lactic acid production by L. casei was not detected under the experimental conditions tested (Figure C,F). In agreement with the growth results, CP-IDF and DCP-IDF were not fermented by either probiotic strain and did not result in detectable lactic acid production. Previous studies have reported that the prebiotic activity of cowpea is primarily associated with its resistant starch fraction and is highly cultivar-dependent. In addition, fortification of fermented milk with cowpea flour has been shown to enhance nutritional and functional properties by stimulating bifidogenic growth. Evidence supporting the prebiotic activity of cowpea DF is limited.

CP-IDF retained substantial structural rigidity after enzymatic digestion, which likely contributes to its limited fermentability and a lack of prebiotic activity. Although no pronounced macroscopic differences were observed between CP-IDF and DCP-IDF, SEM revealed a broomlike splitting pattern exclusively in CP-IDF, characterized by fragmented fiber bundles with frayed fibrils, indicating restricted microbial enzyme accessibility. Consistent with this observation, XRD analysis showed a higher amorphous content in DCP-IDF, suggesting that CP-IDF possesses a more ordered and crystalline cellulose structure. This rigid framework likely limits microbial colonization and enzymatic degradation during the fermentation. Additionally, lignin and bound phenolic compounds may further inhibit microbial attachment and hydrolysis, contributing to the recalcitrance of CP-IDF. Although DCP-IDF did not exhibit intrinsic prebiotic activity under the in vitro fermentation conditions tested, its partially amorphous structure may enhance the GBC. This property could enable DCP-IDF to retain fermentable sugars within the gastrointestinal tract, thereby indirectly supporting probiotic growth and activity in vivo rather than serving as a direct fermentable substrate. Collectively, these findings indicate that cowpea IDF does not exhibit prebiotic activity under the experimental conditions tested.

Anti-Inflammatory Activity of DCP-IDF-Supplemented Probiotic Supernatants

Although DCP-IDF did not exhibit prebiotic activity under the experimental conditions tested, as evidenced by the absence of increased lactic acid production or pH reduction, probiotic-derived CFSs may still possess biological activity. CFS contain fermentation-derived bioactive metabolites, such as organic acids, peptides, and enzymes, which can exert immunomodulatory effects independent of the presence of live bacterial cells. , Therefore, to further evaluate the bioactivity associated with insoluble cowpea DF fermentation, the anti-inflammatory effects of probiotic-derived CFS were investigated as postbiotic preparations. LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages were employed as an inflammatory model, with NO production used as an indicator of the inflammatory response (Figure ). As expected, NO levels were highest under LPS-stimulated conditions. DIC, used as a reference anti-inflammatory agent, significantly attenuated NO production. Notably, CFSs derived from both probiotic strains significantly reduced NO production under LPS-stimulated conditions. In particular, DCP-IDF-supplemented CFS fermented by B. longum and Lacticaseibacillus casei exhibited pronounced anti-inflammatory effects. Interestingly, CFS obtained from B. longum cultured under sugar-free conditions also attenuated released NO levels (Figure A), whereas this effect was not observed for L. casei, suggesting strain-specific differences in metabolite production (Figure B). These results are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the immunomodulatory potential of probiotic-derived CFSs. CFSs from various probiotic strains have been shown to suppress pro-inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, IL-8, and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), while enhancing anti-inflammatory responses in macrophage and intestinal epithelial cell models. However, CFS exhibited moderate cytotoxicity toward RAW264.7 cells (Figure C,D). This effect may be attributable to residual components of the culture medium or the presence of secreted bioactive compounds (cationic peptides) or constituents of MRS medium (glucose and Tween 80), which may affect cell integrity and osmotic balance. Similar dose-dependent reductions in RAW264.7 viability with corresponding decrease in inflammatory mediators have been reported. To address this concern, anti-inflammatory activity was evaluated based on NO production in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages in conjunction with cell viability data. NO levels were markedly reduced following treatment and were comparable to those of the DIC, indicating a potential anti-inflammatory effect. While reduced viability may partially contribute to decreased NO production, NO remained detectable across all treatment groups, confirming that a proportion of cells retained functional responsiveness. Therefore, the observed reduction in NO cannot be solely attributed to cell death, but likely reflects a combined effect of cytotoxicity and modulation of inflammatory pathways. Accordingly, the anti-inflammatory activity was interpreted alongside viability data to minimize overestimation. Collectively, these findings support the role of fermentation-derived metabolites in CFS as postbiotic agents capable of modulating inflammatory responses independent of live probiotic cells.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that processing and simulated gastrointestinal digestion markedly influence the physicochemical properties, structural organization, and bioactivities of cowpea-derived IDFs (CP-IDF). Freeze-drying effectively preserved key constituents and enabled the formation of structurally distinct fiber matrices, as made evident by SEM, FTIR, and XRD analyses. Simulated digestion induced structural disintegration and compositional transformation, including an increased reducing sugar content and liberation of bound phenolic compounds, which collectively contributed to enhanced antioxidant activity. These findings underscore the pivotal role of gastrointestinal digestion in modulating the structure–function relationship and improving the bioaccessibility of fiber-associated bioactives. Importantly, the digested fraction exhibited anti-inflammatory activity in RAW264.7 macrophages by suppressing NO production, suggesting postbiotic-related functionality independent of direct fermentation. From an application perspective, CP-IDF shows strong potential as a multifunctional ingredient for high-fiber bakery products, plant-based meat alternatives, and fiber-enriched powdered or beverage formulations. Its superior water- and oil-holding capacities further support its use as a texture-modifying and -stabilizing agent in complex food systems. Collectively, this study advances a digestion-driven paradigm in which structurally recalcitrant IDF can be transformed into bioactive and functionally relevant ingredients, bridging the gap between underutilized plant biomass and next-generation functional food designs. While this study provides valuable insights, the use of a static in vitro digestion model may not fully reflect physiological conditions. However, this controlled system enables a systematic evaluation of digestion-induced structural and functional changes. The limited fermentability of CP-IDF is attributed to its structurally recalcitrant nature, which may contribute to its functional stability and potential roles beyond direct fermentation. Future studies incorporating dynamic models and in vivo validation are warranted to further confirm these findings.
