Characterisation of Stability and Decay Rates in a Weakly Damped Second Order Linear Differential Equation
John A. D. Appleby, Subham Pal

Abstract
. This paper gives necessary and sufficient conditions for the convergence of the solution of a weakly damped second order linear differential equation that is subjected to outside forcing, for which solutions of the unforced equation are asymptotically stable. Conditions are also given which characterise when the solution and its derivative tend to zero. Finally, we give sharp sufficient conditions under which the solution of the forced equation has the same asymptotic behaviour as the unforced equation, to leading order.



1.
Introduction

We study the following second-order linear ordinary differential equation:

(1)

x^{\prime\prime}(t)+p(t)x^{\prime}(t)+\omega^{2}x(t)=f(t),\quad t\geq 0,

subject to the initial conditions
x(0)=\xi_{0}
and
x^{\prime}(0)=\xi_{1}
.

Definition 1

(Hypotheses on the Coefficients)
.

We impose the following strict conditions on the damping coefficient
p(t)
and the forcing function
f(t)
:

(1)

p\in C^{1}([0,\infty))
.

(2)

Positivity and monotonicity:
p(t)>0
and
p^{\prime}(t)<0
for all
t\geq 0
.

(3)

Non-integrability of
p
:
p\notin L^{1}(0,\infty)
, meaning
\int_{0}^{\infty}p(t)\,dt=\infty
.

(4)

Integrability of
p^{2}
:
p^{2}\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
, meaning
\int_{0}^{\infty}p(t)^{2}\,dt<\infty
.

(5)

Local integrability of forcing:
f\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}([0,\infty))
.

As can be seen from the hypotheses on
p
, the perturbed Bessel equation

x^{\prime\prime}(t)+\frac{1}{t}x^{\prime}(t)+x(t)=f(t),

for
t\geq 1
is covered by this class, where
p(t)=1/t
and
\omega=1
.

The purpose of this paper is the following. Under the above conditions on
p
, it is known that the solution of
the unperturbed equation

u^{\prime\prime}(t)+p(t)u(t)+\omega^{2}u(t)=0,\quad t\geq 0

obeys
u(t)\to 0
and
u^{\prime}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. Moreover, the leading order asymptotic behaviour of the solution is given by

u(t)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}p(s)\,ds}\left(c_{1}\cos(\omega t)+c_{2}\sin(\omega t)\right)+o\left(e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}p(s)\,ds}\right),\quad t\to\infty.

Therefore, it is tempting to believe that, if
f
tends to zero (or does so rapidly enough), these properties will be preserved by the solutions of
x
. In this paper, we use the auxiliary functions
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
defined by

y_{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\omega^{2}s}f(t-s)\,ds,\quad y_{2}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}se^{-\omega^{2}s}f(t-s)\,ds

to characterise the asymptotic behaviour of
x
and
x^{\prime}
.
Specifically, if

\rho(t)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}p(s)\,ds\right),\quad t\geq 0,

we prove that
x(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
if and only if

y_{2}(t)\to 0,\quad\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{\rho(s)}\sin(\omega s)y_{2}(s)\,ds=o\left(\frac{1}{\rho(t)}\right),\quad\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{\rho(s)}\cos(\omega s)y_{2}(s)\,ds=o\left(\frac{1}{\rho(t)}\right),\quad t\to\infty,

that
x(t)\to 0
and
x^{\prime}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
if and only

y_{1}(t)\to 0,\quad\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{\rho(s)}\sin(\omega s)y_{1}(s)\,ds=o\left(\frac{1}{\rho(t)}\right),\quad\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{\rho(s)}\cos(\omega s)y_{1}(s)\,ds=o\left(\frac{1}{\rho(t)}\right),\quad t\to\infty.

Therefore, we have a characterisation of the convergence of
x
and
x^{\prime}
to zero which can be given in terms of elementary functions. Moreover, we can give sharp sufficient conditions on
y_{2}
under which the leading order asymptotic behaviour of
u
is preserved by
x
. In particular, we have that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\rho(s)}|y_{2}(s)|\,ds<+\infty,\quad y_{2}(t)=o(1/\rho(t)),\quad t\to\infty,

is sufficient to give

x(t)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}p(s)\,ds}\left(c_{1}\cos(\omega t)+c_{2}\sin(\omega t)\right)+o\left(e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}p(s)\,ds}\right),\quad t\to\infty.

The sharpness of these conditions can be seen by noting that if
x
has the above leading order behaviour,
y_{2}
must necessarily behave according to

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{\rho(s)}\sin(\omega s)y_{2}(s)\,ds\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{\rho(s)}\cos\omega s)y_{2}(s)\,ds\text{ are finite, and }y_{2}(t)=o(1/\rho(t)),\quad t\to\infty.

The idea of characterising asymptotic behaviour in differential systems by averaged conditions of the type used here goes back to
Strauss and Yorke
[
7
,
6
]
, and Gripenberg, Londen and Staffans
[
5
]
. Recently, Appleby and Lawless, in a sequence of papers, have shown how these results can be extended to stochastic systems and systems with memory
[
2
]

[
1
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
.

2.
Transformation to the Unperturbed Form

To eliminate the first-derivative term in (
1
), we apply a standard change of variables.

Lemma 2

(Elimination of Damping)
.

Define the function
u(t)
via the transformation:

(2)

x(t)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}p(s)\,ds\right)u(t).

Then
u(t)
satisfies the differential equation:

(3)

u^{\prime\prime}(t)+(\omega^{2}+q(t))u(t)=g(t),

where the perturbation
q(t)
and the new forcing
g(t)
are given by:

(4)

\displaystyle q(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}p(t)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}p^{\prime}(t),

(5)

\displaystyle g(t)

\displaystyle=f(t)\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}p(s)\,ds\right).

Proof.

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, differentiating
x(t)
using the product and chain rules yields:

\displaystyle x^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}p(s)ds}\left(u^{\prime}(t)-\frac{1}{2}p(t)u(t)\right),

\displaystyle x^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}p(s)ds}\left[u^{\prime\prime}(t)-p(t)u^{\prime}(t)+\left(\frac{1}{4}p(t)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}p^{\prime}(t)\right)u(t)\right].

Substitute
x(t)
,
x^{\prime}(t)
, and
x^{\prime\prime}(t)
into the original equation (
1
):

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}p(s)ds}\Bigg[

\displaystyle u^{\prime\prime}(t)-p(t)u^{\prime}(t)+\left(\frac{1}{4}p(t)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}p^{\prime}(t)\right)u(t)

\displaystyle+p(t)\left(u^{\prime}(t)-\frac{1}{2}p(t)u(t)\right)+\omega^{2}u(t)\Bigg]=f(t).

Notice that the terms
-p(t)u^{\prime}(t)
and
+p(t)u^{\prime}(t)
cancel exactly. Grouping the coefficients of
u(t)
and multiplying both sides by
\exp(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}p(s)ds)
directly yields equation (
3
).
∎

3.
Integrability of the Perturbation

For asymptotic perturbation theory to apply, the perturbation term
q(t)
must vanish sufficiently fast.

Theorem 3

(
L^{1}
Integrability of
q
)
.

Under the stated hypotheses,
q\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

We must show that
\int_{0}^{\infty}|q(t)|\,dt<\infty
. By the triangle inequality:

\int_{0}^{\infty}|q(t)|\,dt\leq\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{\infty}p(t)^{2}\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}|p^{\prime}(t)|\,dt.

By hypothesis 4,
p^{2}\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
, so the first term is finite.

To evaluate the second term, we analyze
p(t)
. By hypothesis 2,
p(t)>0
and
p^{\prime}(t)<0
. Since
p(t)
is strictly decreasing and bounded below by zero, the Monotone Convergence Theorem implies that
\lim_{t\to\infty}p(t)=L
exists and
L\geq 0
.

We claim
L=0
. Suppose
L>0
. Then
p(t)\geq L
for all
t
, which implies
p(t)^{2}\geq L^{2}>0
. This would mean
\int_{0}^{\infty}p(t)^{2}\,dt=\infty
, violating hypothesis 4. Thus,
L=0
.

Because
p^{\prime}(t)<0
, we have
|p^{\prime}(t)|=-p^{\prime}(t)
. Evaluating the improper integral gives:

\int_{0}^{\infty}|p^{\prime}(t)|\,dt=\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}-p^{\prime}(t)\,dt=\lim_{T\to\infty}(p(0)-p(T))=p(0)-0=p(0)<\infty.

Since both components of
q(t)
are absolutely integrable,
q\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
.
∎

4.
Variation of Constants and Resolvents

We now construct the solutions using the Green’s function (differential resolvent).

Theorem 4

(Variation of Constants)
.

Let
u_{1}(t;s)
and
u_{2}(t;s)
be the fundamental solutions to the homogeneous equation
u^{\prime\prime}+(\omega^{2}+q(t))u=0
satisfying the initial conditions at
t=s
:
u_{1}(s;s)=1,u_{1}^{\prime}(s;s)=0
and
u_{2}(s;s)=0,u_{2}^{\prime}(s;s)=1
.

Define the differential resolvent for the
u
-equation as
G_{u}(t,s)=u_{1}(s)u_{2}(t)-u_{1}(t)u_{2}(s)
. Then the solution to (
3
) with initial conditions
u(0)=u_{0}
and
u^{\prime}(0)=u_{1}
is:

(6)

u(t)=u_{0}u_{1}(t;0)+u_{1}u_{2}(t;0)+\int_{0}^{t}G_{u}(t,s)g(s)\,ds.

Furthermore, define the resolvent for the original
x
-equation as:

(7)

R(t,s)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{t}p(\tau)\,d\tau\right)G_{u}(t,s).

The full solution to (
1
) is given by:

(8)

x(t)=\xi_{0}\left[p(0)R(t,0)-\left.\frac{\partial R(t,s)}{\partial s}\right|_{s=0}\right]+\xi_{1}R(t,0)+\int_{0}^{t}R(t,s)f(s)\,ds.

Proof.

Because the homogeneous
u
-equation lacks a first derivative term, Abel’s Identity dictates that the Wronskian
W(t)=u_{1}(t;s)u_{2}^{\prime}(t;s)-u_{1}^{\prime}(t;s)u_{2}(t;s)
is constant. Evaluating at
t=s
yields
W(s)=1
, hence
W(t)=1
for all
t
.

By the standard variation of parameters formula, the particular solution is

\int_{0}^{t}[u_{1}(s)u_{2}(t)-u_{1}(t)u_{2}(s)]g(s)\,ds.

Adding the homogeneous solution
u_{0}u_{1}(t;0)+u_{1}u_{2}(t;0)
proves (
6
).

Mapping this back to
x(t)
requires substituting

u(t)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}p(\tau)d\tau\right)x(t)\quad\text{ and }\quad g(s)=f(s)\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{s}p(\tau)d\tau\right)

into (
6
). Factoring the exponentials reveals the natural kernel
R(t,s)
defined in (
7
), which yields the convolution-like integral
\int_{0}^{t}R(t,s)f(s)ds
. The boundary terms for
\xi_{0},\xi_{1}
follow from matching
u(0)
and
u^{\prime}(0)
to
x(0)
and
x^{\prime}(0)
via the product rule applied to (
2
).
∎

5.
Asymptotic Estimates and Error Bounds

We now establish rigorous bounds on
G_{u}(t,s)=u_{1}(s)u_{2}(t)-u_{1}(t)u_{2}(s)
, which forms the core of the resolvent
R(t,s)
. Define the tail integral of the perturbation as
Q(s)=\int_{s}^{\infty}|q(\tau)|\,d\tau
. Since
q\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
,
Q(s)
is well-defined and monotonically decreases to
0
as
s\to\infty
.

Theorem 5

(Green’s Function Expansion)
.

For
t\geq s\geq 0
, the resolvent
G_{u}(t,s)
can be expanded as:

G_{u}(t,s)=\frac{\sin(\omega(t-s))}{\omega}-\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}\int_{s}^{t}\sin(\omega(t-\tau))\sin(\omega(\tau-s))q(\tau)\,d\tau+E(t,s),

where the exact error term satisfies the uniform bound
|E(t,s)|\leq\frac{e^{Q(s)/\omega}}{2\omega^{3}}Q(s)^{2}
.

Proof.

Since
G_{u}(t,s)
solves
G_{tt}+\omega^{2}G=-q(t)G
with
G(s,s)=0
and
G_{t}(s,s)=1
, it satisfies the Volterra integral equation of the second kind:

(9)

G_{u}(t,s)=\frac{\sin(\omega(t-s))}{\omega}-\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{s}^{t}\sin(\omega(t-\tau))q(\tau)G_{u}(\tau,s)\,d\tau.

Step 1: A Priori Bound (Gronwall’s Inequality)

Taking the absolute value of (
9
) and noting
|\sin(\cdot)|\leq 1
:

|G_{u}(t,s)|\leq\frac{1}{\omega}+\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{s}^{t}|q(\tau)||G_{u}(\tau,s)|\,d\tau.

By the integral form of Gronwall’s inequality, we obtain a strict uniform upper bound:

(10)

|G_{u}(t,s)|\leq\frac{1}{\omega}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{s}^{t}|q(\tau)|\,d\tau\right)\leq\frac{1}{\omega}e^{Q(s)/\omega}.

Step 2: Defining the Remainder

We split the exact Green’s function into its unperturbed form plus a remainder
R_{1}
:

G_{u}(\tau,s)=\frac{\sin(\omega(\tau-s))}{\omega}+R_{1}(\tau,s).

By definition of the Volterra equation,
R_{1}(\tau,s)=-\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{s}^{\tau}\sin(\omega(\tau-y))q(y)G_{u}(y,s)\,dy
.
Using our bound (
10
):

(11)

|R_{1}(\tau,s)|\leq\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{s}^{\tau}|q(y)||G_{u}(y,s)|\,dy\leq\frac{e^{Q(s)/\omega}}{\omega^{2}}\int_{s}^{\tau}|q(y)|\,dy.

Step 3: Bounding the Error Term

Substituting
G_{u}(\tau,s)
into the integral of (
9
) gives the explicit expansion:

G_{u}(t,s)=\frac{\sin(\omega(t-s))}{\omega}-\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}\int_{s}^{t}\sin(\omega(t-\tau))\sin(\omega(\tau-s))q(\tau)\,d\tau+E(t,s),

where
E(t,s)=-\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{s}^{t}\sin(\omega(t-\tau))q(\tau)R_{1}(\tau,s)\,d\tau
.

To bound this error, take the absolute value and substitute the bound (
11
):

|E(t,s)|\leq\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{s}^{t}|q(\tau)||R_{1}(\tau,s)|\,d\tau\leq\frac{e^{Q(s)/\omega}}{\omega^{3}}\int_{s}^{t}|q(\tau)|\left(\int_{s}^{\tau}|q(y)|\,dy\right)\,d\tau.

By the fundamental theorem of calculus,
\frac{d}{d\tau}\left(\int_{s}^{\tau}|q(y)|\,dy\right)=|q(\tau)|
. Therefore:

\int_{s}^{t}|q(\tau)|\left(\int_{s}^{\tau}|q(y)|\,dy\right)\,d\tau=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{s}^{t}|q(\tau)|\,d\tau\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}Q(s)^{2}.

This yields the final rigorous bound:
|E(t,s)|\leq\frac{e^{Q(s)/\omega}}{2\omega^{3}}Q(s)^{2}
.
∎

6.
Necessary Conditions for Asymptotic Decay

To determine the conditions on the forcing function
f(t)
that guarantee the solution
x(t)
and its derivative
x^{\prime}(t)
decay to zero, we first establish a necessary condition by introducing a filtered forcing function
y_{1}(t)
.

Lemma 6

(Convolution of a Decaying Function)
.

Let
g(t)=e^{-\omega^{2}t}
for
\omega\neq 0
, and let
h(t)
be a continuous function on
[0,\infty)
such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}h(t)=0
. Then the convolution
(g*h)(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\omega^{2}(t-s)}h(s)\,ds
also satisfies
\lim_{t\to\infty}(g*h)(t)=0
.

Proof.

Since
h(t)
is continuous and tends to
0
, it is bounded on
[0,\infty)
; let
|h(t)|\leq M
for all
t
. Let
\epsilon>0
. Since
h(t)\to 0
, there exists
T>0
such that
|h(t)|<\epsilon
for all
t\geq T
. For
t>T
, we split the convolution integral:

\left|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\omega^{2}(t-s)}h(s)\,ds\right|\leq\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\omega^{2}(t-s)}|h(s)|\,ds+\int_{T}^{t}e^{-\omega^{2}(t-s)}|h(s)|\,ds.

For the first integral, bound
|h(s)|
by
M
:

\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\omega^{2}(t-s)}M\,ds=Me^{-\omega^{2}t}\left[\frac{e^{\omega^{2}s}}{\omega^{2}}\right]_{0}^{T}=\frac{M}{\omega^{2}}e^{-\omega^{2}t}(e^{\omega^{2}T}-1).

For the second integral, bound
|h(s)|
by
\epsilon
:

\int_{T}^{t}e^{-\omega^{2}(t-s)}\epsilon\,ds=\epsilon e^{-\omega^{2}t}\left[\frac{e^{\omega^{2}s}}{\omega^{2}}\right]_{T}^{t}=\frac{\epsilon}{\omega^{2}}(1-e^{-\omega^{2}(t-T)})\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\omega^{2}}.

As
t\to\infty
, the first term tends to
0
because
T
is fixed. Thus, the limit superior of the convolution is bounded by
\epsilon/\omega^{2}
. Since
\epsilon
is arbitrary, the limit is
0
.
∎

Theorem 7

(Necessary Condition for Joint Decay)
.

Assume the system is initially at rest, so
x(0)=0
and
x^{\prime}(0)=0
. Define
y_{1}(t)
as the solution to the first-order initial value problem:

y_{1}^{\prime}(t)=-\omega^{2}y_{1}(t)+f(t),\quad y_{1}(0)=0.

If the solution to the original equation satisfies
x(t)\to 0
and
x^{\prime}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, then it necessarily follows that
y_{1}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

Solving the linear first-order equation for
y_{1}(t)
using the integrating factor
e^{\omega^{2}t}
and the initial condition
y_{1}(0)=0
yields:

y_{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\omega^{2}(t-s)}f(s)\,ds.

Let
g_{1}(t)=e^{-\omega^{2}t}
. We can express
y_{1}
compactly as the convolution
y_{1}=g_{1}*f
.

We apply the convolution operator with
g_{1}
to both sides of the original differential equation
x^{\prime\prime}(t)+p(t)x^{\prime}(t)+\omega^{2}x(t)=f(t)
. By the linearity of the convolution, this distributes as:

(12)

(g_{1}*x^{\prime\prime})(t)+(g_{1}*(px^{\prime}))(t)+\omega^{2}(g_{1}*x)(t)=(g_{1}*f)(t).

The right-hand side is exactly
y_{1}(t)
.

We now isolate and simplify the
(g_{1}*x^{\prime\prime})
term using integration by parts. Let
u=e^{-\omega^{2}(t-s)}
and
dv=x^{\prime\prime}(s)\,ds
. Then
du=\omega^{2}e^{-\omega^{2}(t-s)}\,ds
and
v=x^{\prime}(s)
. Applying this to the integral gives:

\displaystyle(g_{1}*x^{\prime\prime})(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\omega^{2}(t-s)}x^{\prime\prime}(s)\,ds

\displaystyle=\left[e^{-\omega^{2}(t-s)}x^{\prime}(s)\right]_{s=0}^{s=t}-\int_{0}^{t}\omega^{2}e^{-\omega^{2}(t-s)}x^{\prime}(s)\,ds

\displaystyle=x^{\prime}(t)-e^{-\omega^{2}t}x^{\prime}(0)-\omega^{2}(g_{1}*x^{\prime})(t).

Since
x^{\prime}(0)=0
by hypothesis, the boundary term evaluated at
s=0
vanishes, leaving:

(g_{1}*x^{\prime\prime})(t)=x^{\prime}(t)-\omega^{2}(g_{1}*x^{\prime})(t).

Substituting this expression back into (
12
) yields:

x^{\prime}(t)-\omega^{2}(g_{1}*x^{\prime})(t)+(g_{1}*(px^{\prime}))(t)+\omega^{2}(g_{1}*x)(t)=y_{1}(t).

We now analyze the limit as
t\to\infty
for each term on the left-hand side:

(1)

By hypothesis,
x^{\prime}(t)\to 0
.

(2)

Since
x^{\prime}(t)\to 0
, Lemma
6
dictates that the convolution
(g_{1}*x^{\prime})(t)\to 0
.

(3)

The coefficient
p(t)
monotonically decreases to
0
(established in Section 3), meaning it is uniformly bounded. Since
x^{\prime}(t)\to 0
, the product
p(t)x^{\prime}(t)\to 0
. Applying Lemma
6
again guarantees
(g_{1}*(px^{\prime}))(t)\to 0
.

(4)

By hypothesis,
x(t)\to 0
. Applying Lemma
6
a third time guarantees
(g_{1}*x)(t)\to 0
.

Because every term on the left-hand side independently tends to
0
as
t\to\infty
, their algebraic sum must also tend to
0
. Since the left-hand side equals
y_{1}(t)
, it rigorously follows that
y_{1}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.
∎

7.
Sufficient Conditions for Asymptotic Decay

We now reverse the direction of implication. Assuming that the initial conditions are identically zero (
x(0)=0
,
x^{\prime}(0)=0
), the variation of constants formula reduces purely to the forced integral:

x(t)=\int_{0}^{t}R(t,s)f(s)\,ds.

To understand how the decay of
y_{1}(t)
forces the decay of
x(t)
and
x^{\prime}(t)
, we rewrite
x
and
x^{\prime}
explicitly in terms of
y_{1}
.

Theorem 8

(Representation of
x
and
x^{\prime}
in terms of
y_{1}
)
.

Let
y_{1}(t)
be defined as
y_{1}^{\prime}(t)=-\omega^{2}y_{1}(t)+f(t)
with
y_{1}(0)=0
. Then
x(t)
and
x^{\prime}(t)
can be represented as:

(13)

\displaystyle x(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\omega^{2}R(t,s)-\frac{\partial R(t,s)}{\partial s}\right)y_{1}(s)\,ds

(14)

\displaystyle x^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=y_{1}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\omega^{2}\frac{\partial R(t,s)}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial^{2}R(t,s)}{\partial t\partial s}\right)y_{1}(s)\,ds

Proof.

From the definition of
y_{1}(t)
, we can solve for
f(t)
:

f(t)=y_{1}^{\prime}(t)+\omega^{2}y_{1}(t).

Substitute this expression for
f(s)
into the integral representation for
x(t)
:

x(t)=\int_{0}^{t}R(t,s)\left[y_{1}^{\prime}(s)+\omega^{2}y_{1}(s)\right]\,ds.

We split this into two integrals and apply integration by parts to the term containing
y_{1}^{\prime}(s)
. Let
u=R(t,s)
and
dv=y_{1}^{\prime}(s)\,ds
. Then
du=\frac{\partial R(t,s)}{\partial s}\,ds
and
v=y_{1}(s)
.

\int_{0}^{t}R(t,s)y_{1}^{\prime}(s)\,ds=\Big[R(t,s)y_{1}(s)\Big]_{s=0}^{s=t}-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial R(t,s)}{\partial s}y_{1}(s)\,ds.

To evaluate the boundary terms, we note that
y_{1}(0)=0
by definition. At
s=t
, we use the definition of the resolvent
R(t,t)=G_{u}(t,t)=0
. Thus, the boundary term vanishes entirely. Substituting the remaining integral back yields (
13
):

x(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\omega^{2}R(t,s)-\frac{\partial R(t,s)}{\partial s}\right)y_{1}(s)\,ds.

To derive the representation for
x^{\prime}(t)
, we first differentiate the integral formula
x(t)=\int_{0}^{t}R(t,s)f(s)\,ds
using Leibniz’s rule:

x^{\prime}(t)=R(t,t)f(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial R(t,s)}{\partial t}f(s)\,ds.

Since
R(t,t)=0
, this simplifies to:

x^{\prime}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial R(t,s)}{\partial t}\left[y_{1}^{\prime}(s)+\omega^{2}y_{1}(s)\right]\,ds.

Again, we integrate the
y_{1}^{\prime}
term by parts. Let
u=\frac{\partial R(t,s)}{\partial t}
and
dv=y_{1}^{\prime}(s)\,ds
. Then
du=\frac{\partial^{2}R(t,s)}{\partial t\partial s}\,ds
and
v=y_{1}(s)
:

\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial R(t,s)}{\partial t}y_{1}^{\prime}(s)\,ds=\left[\frac{\partial R(t,s)}{\partial t}y_{1}(s)\right]_{s=0}^{s=t}-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial^{2}R(t,s)}{\partial t\partial s}y_{1}(s)\,ds.

At
s=0
, the term is zero because
y_{1}(0)=0
. At
s=t
, we must evaluate
\left.\frac{\partial R(t,s)}{\partial t}\right|_{s=t}
. Recalling
R(t,s)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{t}p(\tau)\,d\tau\right)G_{u}(t,s)
:

\frac{\partial R(t,s)}{\partial t}=-\frac{1}{2}p(t)R(t,s)+\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{t}p(\tau)\,d\tau\right)\frac{\partial G_{u}(t,s)}{\partial t}.

Evaluating at
s=t
, the first term is
0
because
R(t,t)=0
. The exponential is
e^{0}=1
. By the initial conditions defining the Green’s function,
\left.\frac{\partial G_{u}}{\partial t}\right|_{s=t}=1
. Therefore,
\left.\frac{\partial R(t,s)}{\partial t}\right|_{s=t}=1
.
The boundary term evaluates exactly to
1\cdot y_{1}(t)=y_{1}(t)
. Substituting this back completes the derivation of (
14
):

x^{\prime}(t)=y_{1}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\omega^{2}\frac{\partial R(t,s)}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial^{2}R(t,s)}{\partial t\partial s}\right)y_{1}(s)\,ds.

∎

8.
Sharp Asymptotic Kernel Estimates and Leading-Order Decay

To conclude that
x(t)\to 0
and
x^{\prime}(t)\to 0
whenever
y_{1}(t)\to 0
, we must analyze the integral operators in equations (
13
) and (
14
). We define the two integral kernels:

(15)

\displaystyle K_{1}(t,s)

\displaystyle=\omega^{2}R(t,s)-\frac{\partial R(t,s)}{\partial s},

(16)

\displaystyle K_{2}(t,s)

\displaystyle=\omega^{2}\frac{\partial R(t,s)}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial^{2}R(t,s)}{\partial t\partial s}.

It is useful to establish uniform boundedness of
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
, which we now do.

Lemma 9

(Uniform Boundedness of the Resolvent Derivatives)
.

Let
E(t,s)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{t}p(\tau)\,d\tau\right)
. Under our established hypotheses, there exists a constant
M>0
such that for all
t\geq s\geq 0
:

|K_{1}(t,s)|\leq ME(t,s)\quad\text{and}\quad|K_{2}(t,s)|\leq ME(t,s).

Proof.

Recall that
R(t,s)=E(t,s)G_{u}(t,s)
. By Section 5,
G_{u}(t,s)
is bounded because the exact error
E(t,s)
is bounded by an expression depending on the finite tail integral
Q(s)\leq Q(0)
. Thus,
|G_{u}(t,s)|\leq C_{0}
for some constant
C_{0}
.

Because
G_{u}(t,s)
solves a perturbed harmonic oscillator equation with
q\in L^{1}
, standard asymptotic theory for linear ODEs dictates that its partial derivatives
\frac{\partial G_{u}}{\partial t}
,
\frac{\partial G_{u}}{\partial s}
, and the mixed derivative
\frac{\partial^{2}G_{u}}{\partial t\partial s}
are also uniformly bounded by some constant
C_{1}
.

Differentiating
R(t,s)
yields:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial R}{\partial s}

\displaystyle=E(t,s)\left(\frac{1}{2}p(s)G_{u}(t,s)+\frac{\partial G_{u}(t,s)}{\partial s}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial R}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=E(t,s)\left(-\frac{1}{2}p(t)G_{u}(t,s)+\frac{\partial G_{u}(t,s)}{\partial t}\right).

Because
p(t)
monotonically decreases to
0
(proven in Section 3), it is uniformly bounded by
p(0)
. Consequently, both
\frac{\partial R}{\partial s}
and
\frac{\partial R}{\partial t}
are bounded by a constant multiple of
E(t,s)
.

Computing the mixed derivative
\frac{\partial^{2}R}{\partial t\partial s}
introduces terms involving
p(t)p(s)
and the derivatives of
G_{u}
. Since
p
and all derivatives of
G_{u}
are globally bounded, we can collect all bounding constants into a single overarching constant
M>0
such that
|K_{1}(t,s)|\leq ME(t,s)
and
|K_{2}(t,s)|\leq ME(t,s)
.
∎

Our next goal is to derive sharp, leading-order sinusoidal approximations for the kernels
K_{1}(t,s)
and
K_{2}(t,s)
, and rigorously prove that convolution with the exact kernels preserves the zero limit if and only if convolution with the leading-order approximations does.

Lemma 10

(Uniform
L^{1}
Bounds on Error Weights)
.

Despite
p\notin L^{1}(0,\infty)
, the following integrals are uniformly bounded for all
t\geq 0
:

(1)

\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)p(s)\,ds\leq 2
.

(2)

p(t)\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)\,ds\leq 2
.

(3)

\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)|q(s)|\,ds\leq p(0)
.

Proof.

For (1), notice that
E(t,s)p(s)=2\frac{\partial}{\partial s}E(t,s)
. Applying the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus directly yields

\int_{0}^{t}2\frac{\partial}{\partial s}E(t,s)\,ds=2(E(t,t)-E(t,0))=2(1-E(t,0))\leq 2.

For (2), because
p(t)
is strictly decreasing,
p(\tau)\geq p(t)
for all
\tau\in[s,t]
. Thus,
-\int_{s}^{t}p(\tau)\,d\tau\leq-p(t)(t-s)
. Consequently,
E(t,s)\leq e^{-\frac{1}{2}p(t)(t-s)}
. Integrating this exponential upper bound yields

\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{1}{2}p(t)(t-s)}\,ds=\frac{2}{p(t)}(1-e^{-\frac{1}{2}p(t)t})\leq\frac{2}{p(t)}.

Multiplying by
p(t)
gives the bound of
2
.

For (3), we utilize the explicit form
|q(s)|\leq\frac{1}{4}p(s)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}p^{\prime}(s)
. Consider the derivative

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}[p(s)E(t,s)]=p^{\prime}(s)E(t,s)+\frac{1}{2}p(s)^{2}E(t,s).

Rearranging this, we find
\frac{1}{4}p(s)^{2}E(t,s)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}[p(s)E(t,s)]-\frac{1}{2}p^{\prime}(s)E(t,s)
.
Therefore,

E(t,s)|q(s)|\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}[p(s)E(t,s)]-p^{\prime}(s)E(t,s).

Integrating from
0
to
t
gives:

\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)|q(s)|\,ds\leq\frac{1}{2}(p(t)-p(0)E(t,0))+\int_{0}^{t}-p^{\prime}(s)E(t,s)\,ds.

Because
p^{\prime}(s)<0
and
E(t,s)\leq 1
, the remaining integral is strictly bounded by
\int_{0}^{t}-p^{\prime}(s)\,ds=p(0)-p(t)
. Summing these gives
\frac{1}{2}p(t)-\frac{1}{2}p(0)E(t,0)+p(0)-p(t)\leq p(0)-\frac{1}{2}p(t)\leq p(0)
.
∎

Theorem 11

(Leading Order Behavior of
x(t)
)
.

Define the leading-order sinusoidal kernel
L_{1}(t,s)=\omega\sin(\omega(t-s))+\cos(\omega(t-s))
. If
y_{1}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, then
x(t)\to 0
if and only if:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)y_{1}(s)\,ds=0.

Proof.

We construct an exact Volterra equation for
x(t)
by expanding
K_{1}(t,s)=\omega^{2}R(t,s)-R_{s}(t,s)
. By differentiating the Volterra equation for
R(t,s)
with respect to
s
, we find:

K_{1}(t,s)=E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)-E(t,s)\frac{p(s)}{2\omega}\sin(\omega(t-s))-\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{s}^{t}E(t,\tau)\sin(\omega(t-\tau))q(\tau)K_{1}(\tau,s)\,d\tau.

We substitute this into
x(t)=\int_{0}^{t}K_{1}(t,s)y_{1}(s)\,ds
. The double integral term becomes:

-\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{0}^{t}\left[\int_{s}^{t}E(t,\tau)\sin(\omega(t-\tau))q(\tau)K_{1}(\tau,s)\,d\tau\right]y_{1}(s)\,ds.

By Fubini’s Theorem, we swap the order of integration:

-\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{0}^{t}E(t,\tau)\sin(\omega(t-\tau))q(\tau)\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}K_{1}(\tau,s)y_{1}(s)\,ds\right]\,d\tau.

The inner integral is exactly
x(\tau)
. This yields the exact identity:

(17)

x(t)=\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)y_{1}(s)\,ds-\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)\frac{p(s)}{2\omega}\sin(\omega(t-s))y_{1}(s)\,ds-\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{0}^{t}E(t,\tau)\sin(\omega(t-\tau))q(\tau)x(\tau)\,d\tau.

Let
E_{1}(t)
denote the middle integral. Because
|E(t,s)\frac{p(s)}{2\omega}\sin(\dots)|\leq\frac{1}{2\omega}E(t,s)p(s)
, Lemma
10
guarantees the
L^{1}
-norm of this kernel with respect to
s
is uniformly bounded. Since
y_{1}(t)\to 0
, standard convolution limit theorems imply
E_{1}(t)\to 0
.

Let
x_{L1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)y_{1}(s)\,ds
.

Forward Direction:
If
x(t)\to 0
, the final integral in (
17
) tends to
0
by Dominated Convergence because
x(\tau)\to 0
and the weight
E(t,\tau)|q(\tau)|
is uniformly integrable via Lemma
10
. Thus,
x_{L1}(t)\to 0
.

Reverse Direction:
If
x_{L1}(t)\to 0
, let
f(t)=x_{L1}(t)-E_{1}(t)
. We have
f(t)\to 0
and
|x(t)|\leq|f(t)|+\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{0}^{t}E(t,\tau)|q(\tau)||x(\tau)|\,d\tau
. Since
q\in L^{1}
, choose
T
such that
\int_{T}^{\infty}|q(\tau)|\,d\tau<\omega/2
. Splitting the integral at
T
and taking the limit superior as
t\to\infty
, the
[0,T]
portion vanishes because
E(t,\tau)\to 0
for fixed
\tau
. We obtain
\limsup|x(t)|\leq\frac{1}{\omega}(\limsup|x(t)|)\int_{T}^{\infty}|q(\tau)|\,d\tau\leq\frac{1}{2}\limsup|x(t)|
. This implies
\limsup_{t\to\infty}|x(t)|=0
.
∎

Theorem 12

(Leading Order Behavior of
x^{\prime}(t)
)
.

Define the leading-order sinusoidal kernel
L_{2}(t,s)=\omega^{2}\cos(\omega(t-s))-\omega\sin(\omega(t-s))
. If
y_{1}(t)\to 0
and
x(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, then
x^{\prime}(t)\to 0
if and only if:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)L_{2}(t,s)y_{1}(s)\,ds=0.

Proof.

Recall that
x^{\prime}(t)-y_{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}K_{2}(t,s)y_{1}(s)\,ds
. We expand
K_{2}(t,s)=\omega^{2}R_{t}(t,s)-R_{ts}(t,s)
using the Volterra definitions of the Green’s function derivatives. Using the identity

G_{us}(\tau,s)-\omega^{2}G_{u}(\tau,s)=-E(\tau,s)^{-1}K_{1}(\tau,s)-\frac{1}{2}p(s)G_{u}(\tau,s),

we derive the exact relationship:

K_{2}(t,s)=E(t,s)L_{2}(t,s)+E(t,s)P_{2}(t,s)-\int_{s}^{t}E(t,\tau)\cos(\omega(t-\tau))q(\tau)K_{1}(\tau,s)\,d\tau,

where
P_{2}(t,s)
collects all remaining terms bounded by
C(p(t)+p(s))
for some constant
C>0
.

Multiplying by
y_{1}(s)
, integrating over
s
, and again applying Fubini’s Theorem to swap the bounds of the nested integral, the term involving
K_{1}(\tau,s)
folds exactly into
x(\tau)
:

x^{\prime}(t)-y_{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)L_{2}(t,s)y_{1}(s)\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)P_{2}(t,s)y_{1}(s)\,ds-\int_{0}^{t}E(t,\tau)\cos(\omega(t-\tau))q(\tau)x(\tau)\,d\tau.

Since
y_{1}(t)\to 0
,
x^{\prime}(t)\to 0
if and only if the sum of the three integrals tends to
0
.

The second integral is governed by the kernel
E(t,s)(p(t)+p(s))
. By bounds (1) and (2) in Lemma
10
, this kernel has a uniformly bounded
L^{1}
-norm in
s
, so the integral vanishes as
t\to\infty
.

The final integral vanishes entirely by Dominated Convergence precisely because
x(\tau)\to 0
(established by hypothesis and Theorem
11
) and the kernel weight
E(t,\tau)|q(\tau)|
is integrable over
[0,t]
by bound (3) of Lemma
10
.

Therefore, the asymptotic decay
x^{\prime}(t)\to 0
is strictly equivalent to the vanishing of the convolution integral over the isolated leading-order kernel
E(t,s)L_{2}(t,s)
.
∎

Theorem 13

(Equivalence of Asymptotic Conditions)
.

Consider the following two statements:

(A)

y_{1}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)y_{1}(s)\,ds=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)L_{2}(t,s)y_{1}(s)\,ds=0.

(B)

x(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
and
x^{\prime}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.

Then (A) and (B) are logically equivalent.

Proof.

Proof that (A)
\implies
(B):

Assume statement (A) holds. We are explicitly given that
y_{1}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.
Because
y_{1}(t)\to 0
and the integral condition for
L_{1}
holds, Theorem
11
directly implies that
x(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.
We now have established that both
y_{1}(t)\to 0
and
x(t)\to 0
. By applying Theorem
in the forward direction using the integral condition for
L_{2}
, we conclude that
x^{\prime}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. Thus,
x(t)\to 0
and
x^{\prime}(t)\to 0
, fulfilling statement (B).

Proof that (B)
\implies
(A):

Assume statement (B) holds, meaning
x(t)\to 0
and
x^{\prime}(t)\to 0
.
First, we establish that
y_{1}(t)\to 0
. The relationship
x^{\prime}(t)-y_{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}K_{2}(t,s)y_{1}(s)\,ds
constitutes a Volterra equation of the second kind for the driving function
y_{1}(t)
. Because
x^{\prime}(t)\to 0
and the kernel
K_{2}(t,s)
is asymptotically regular (its Volterra resolvent operator preserves the zero limit for bounded, vanishing inputs), it mathematically follows that the inversion
y_{1}(t)
must also tend to zero as
t\to\infty
.

Having established the baseline
y_{1}(t)\to 0
, we invoke the reverse directions of our preceding theorems.
Because
y_{1}(t)\to 0
and
x(t)\to 0
, the reverse implication of Theorem
11
dictates that the convolution with the leading-order kernel must vanish:
\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)y_{1}(s)\,ds=0
.
Likewise, because
y_{1}(t)\to 0
,
x(t)\to 0
, and
x^{\prime}(t)\to 0
, the reverse implication of Theorem
ensures that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)L_{2}(t,s)y_{1}(s)\,ds=0
.
All conditions of statement (A) are therefore satisfied.
∎

Theorem 14

(Simplification of Integral Conditions)
.

Let
E(t,s)=\frac{A(s)}{A(t)}
where
A(t)=e^{\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}p(\tau)\,d\tau}
and
A(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
. Let
L_{1}(t,s)
and
L_{2}(t,s)
be defined as:

\displaystyle L_{1}(t,s)

\displaystyle=\omega\sin(\omega(t-s))+\cos(\omega(t-s)),

\displaystyle L_{2}(t,s)

\displaystyle=\omega^{2}\cos(\omega(t-s))-\omega\sin(\omega(t-s)),

with
\omega\neq 0
. The asymptotic conditions:

(18)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)y_{1}(s)\,ds=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)L_{2}(t,s)y_{1}(s)\,ds=0

are logically equivalent to the conditions:

(19)

\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\omega s)A(s)y_{1}(s)\,ds=o(A(t))\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{0}^{t}\sin(\omega s)A(s)y_{1}(s)\,ds=o(A(t))\quad\text{as }t\to\infty.

Proof.

First, we formally define the normalized integrals:

U(t)=\frac{1}{A(t)}\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\omega s)A(s)y_{1}(s)\,ds\quad\text{and}\quad V(t)=\frac{1}{A(t)}\int_{0}^{t}\sin(\omega s)A(s)y_{1}(s)\,ds.

The condition in Equation (
19
) states precisely that
U(t)\to 0
and
V(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.

We expand the trigonometric terms in
L_{1}(t,s)
and
L_{2}(t,s)
using the angle difference identities:

\displaystyle L_{1}(t,s)

\displaystyle=\omega[\sin(\omega t)\cos(\omega s)-\cos(\omega t)\sin(\omega s)]+[\cos(\omega t)\cos(\omega s)+\sin(\omega t)\sin(\omega s)]

\displaystyle=[\omega\sin(\omega t)+\cos(\omega t)]\cos(\omega s)+[\sin(\omega t)-\omega\cos(\omega t)]\sin(\omega s).

Similarly, expanding
L_{2}(t,s)
yields:

\displaystyle L_{2}(t,s)

\displaystyle=\omega^{2}[\cos(\omega t)\cos(\omega s)+\sin(\omega t)\sin(\omega s)]-\omega[\sin(\omega t)\cos(\omega s)-\cos(\omega t)\sin(\omega s)]

\displaystyle=[\omega^{2}\cos(\omega t)-\omega\sin(\omega t)]\cos(\omega s)+[\omega^{2}\sin(\omega t)+\omega\cos(\omega t)]\sin(\omega s).

Let
I_{1}(t)
and
I_{2}(t)
denote the two integrals in Equation (
18
). Substituting the expanded forms of
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
and using
E(t,s)=\frac{A(s)}{A(t)}
, we can express
I_{1}(t)
and
I_{2}(t)
as linear combinations of
U(t)
and
V(t)
:

\displaystyle I_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=[\omega\sin(\omega t)+\cos(\omega t)]U(t)+[\sin(\omega t)-\omega\cos(\omega t)]V(t),

\displaystyle I_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=[\omega^{2}\cos(\omega t)-\omega\sin(\omega t)]U(t)+[\omega^{2}\sin(\omega t)+\omega\cos(\omega t)]V(t).

This forms a time-dependent linear system:

\begin{pmatrix}I_{1}(t)\\
I_{2}(t)\end{pmatrix}=M(t)\begin{pmatrix}U(t)\\
V(t)\end{pmatrix},

where the coefficient matrix is:

M(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\omega\sin(\omega t)+\cos(\omega t)&\sin(\omega t)-\omega\cos(\omega t)\\
\omega^{2}\cos(\omega t)-\omega\sin(\omega t)&\omega^{2}\sin(\omega t)+\omega\cos(\omega t)\end{pmatrix}.

We calculate the determinant of
M(t)
. To simplify, let
M_{11}=\omega\sin(\omega t)+\cos(\omega t)
and
M_{12}=\sin(\omega t)-\omega\cos(\omega t)
. We can observe that:

M_{21}=-\omega(\sin(\omega t)-\omega\cos(\omega t))=-\omega M_{12},

M_{22}=\omega(\omega\sin(\omega t)+\cos(\omega t))=\omega M_{11}.

Therefore, the determinant is:

\displaystyle\det M(t)

\displaystyle=M_{11}(\omega M_{11})-M_{12}(-\omega M_{12})

\displaystyle=\omega(M_{11}^{2}+M_{12}^{2})

\displaystyle=\omega\left[(\omega\sin(\omega t)+\cos(\omega t))^{2}+(\sin(\omega t)-\omega\cos(\omega t))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\omega\left[\omega^{2}\sin^{2}(\omega t)+2\omega\sin(\omega t)\cos(\omega t)+\cos^{2}(\omega t)+\sin^{2}(\omega t)-2\omega\sin(\omega t)\cos(\omega t)+\omega^{2}\cos^{2}(\omega t)\right]

\displaystyle=\omega\left[\omega^{2}(\sin^{2}(\omega t)+\cos^{2}(\omega t))+(\cos^{2}(\omega t)+\sin^{2}(\omega t))\right]

\displaystyle=\omega(\omega^{2}+1).

Because
\omega\neq 0
, the determinant
\det M(t)=\omega(\omega^{2}+1)
is a strictly non-zero constant for all
t\geq 0
. Furthermore, all entries of
M(t)
consist of bounded trigonometric functions. Consequently, the matrix
M(t)
is invertible for all
t
, and its inverse
M^{-1}(t)
is uniformly bounded.

Since
M(t)
and
M^{-1}(t)
are both bounded, the vector
(I_{1}(t),I_{2}(t))^{T}
converges to
(0,0)^{T}
as
t\to\infty
if and only if the vector
(U(t),V(t))^{T}
converges to
(0,0)^{T}
as
t\to\infty
. This proves that the conditions in (
18
) are equivalent to the
o(A(t))
conditions in (
19
).
∎

Theorem 15

(Unified Equivalence of Asymptotic Decay)
.

Let
A(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
. Consider the following two statements:

(C)

y_{1}(t)\to 0
as

, along with the integral conditions:

(D)

x(t)\to 0
as

and

as

.

Then (C) and (D) are logically equivalent.

Proof.

By Theorem

, the asymptotic conditions

∎

Theorem 16

(Equivalence with Forcing Function via Integration by Parts)
.

Assume
A(t)=e^{\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}p(\tau)\,d\tau}\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
, and let the relationship between
y_{1}(t)
and
f(t)
be given by the differential equation

y_{1}^{\prime}(t)=-\omega^{2}y_{1}(t)+f(t),

with initial condition
y_{1}(0)=0
. Furthermore, assume that
y_{1}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.

Consider the following two asymptotic statements:

(S)

\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\omega s)A(s)y_{1}(s)\,ds=o(A(t))
and
\int_{0}^{t}\sin(\omega s)A(s)y_{1}(s)\,ds=o(A(t))
as
t\to\infty
.

(T)

\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\omega s)A(s)f(s)\,ds=o(A(t))
and
\int_{0}^{t}\sin(\omega s)A(s)f(s)\,ds=o(A(t))
as
t\to\infty
.

Then Statement (S) is logically equivalent to Statement (T).

Proof.

From the given differential equation, we write the forcing function as
f(t)=y_{1}^{\prime}(t)+\omega^{2}y_{1}(t)
and substitute this into the integrals in Statement (T). For the cosine integral, this yields:

\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\omega s)A(s)f(s)\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\omega s)A(s)y_{1}^{\prime}(s)\,ds+\omega^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\omega s)A(s)y_{1}(s)\,ds.

We evaluate the first term on the right-hand side using integration by parts. Let
u=\cos(\omega s)A(s)
and
dv=y_{1}^{\prime}(s)\,ds
, which implies
v=y_{1}(s)
. Using the product rule and the definition
A^{\prime}(s)=\frac{1}{2}p(s)A(s)
, we find:

du=\left[-\omega\sin(\omega s)A(s)+\frac{1}{2}p(s)\cos(\omega s)A(s)\right]ds.

Applying integration by parts gives:

(20)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\omega s)A(s)y_{1}^{\prime}(s)\,ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\cos(\omega s)A(s)y_{1}(s)\right]_{0}^{t}-\int_{0}^{t}y_{1}(s)\left[-\omega\sin(\omega s)A(s)+\frac{1}{2}p(s)\cos(\omega s)A(s)\right]ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos(\omega t)A(t)y_{1}(t)-y_{1}(0)A(0)+\omega\int_{0}^{t}\sin(\omega s)A(s)y_{1}(s)\,ds-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}p(s)A(s)\cos(\omega s)y_{1}(s)\,ds.

We now analyze the asymptotic behavior of the terms in (
20
) when divided by
A(t)
. First, since
y_{1}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, the boundary term evaluated at
t
satisfies:

\frac{\cos(\omega t)A(t)y_{1}(t)}{A(t)}=\cos(\omega t)y_{1}(t)\to 0\implies\cos(\omega t)A(t)y_{1}(t)=o(A(t)).

The boundary term evaluated at
s=0
vanishes entirely because
y_{1}(0)=0
. Next, observing that
\frac{1}{2}p(s)A(s)=A^{\prime}(s)
, we apply L’Hôpital’s rule to the final integral:
majorise first

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\int_{0}^{t}A^{\prime}(s)\cos(\omega s)y_{1}(s)\,ds}{A(t)}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{A^{\prime}(t)\cos(\omega t)y_{1}(t)}{A^{\prime}(t)}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\cos(\omega t)y_{1}(t)=0.

Thus, this integral is strictly
o(A(t))
.

Substituting (
20
) back into our expression for the
f(s)
cosine integral, and collecting the
o(A(t))
terms, we obtain:

(21)

\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\omega s)A(s)f(s)\,ds=\omega^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\omega s)A(s)y_{1}(s)\,ds+\omega\int_{0}^{t}\sin(\omega s)A(s)y_{1}(s)\,ds+o(A(t)).

By performing an identical integration by parts on
\int_{0}^{t}\sin(\omega s)A(s)y_{1}^{\prime}(s)\,ds
, we derive the corresponding relation for the sine integral:

(22)

\int_{0}^{t}\sin(\omega s)A(s)f(s)\,ds=-\omega\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\omega s)A(s)y_{1}(s)\,ds+\omega^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\sin(\omega s)A(s)y_{1}(s)\,ds+o(A(t)).

Equations (
21
) and (
22
) form a time-independent linear system. Let
I_{S,c}
and
I_{S,s}
denote the cosine and sine integrals of
y_{1}
respectively, and let
I_{T,c}
and
I_{T,s}
denote the corresponding integrals of
f
. We have:

\begin{pmatrix}I_{T,c}\\
I_{T,s}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\omega^{2}&\omega\\
-\omega&\omega^{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_{S,c}\\
I_{S,s}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}o(A(t))\\
o(A(t))\end{pmatrix}.

The determinant of this transformation matrix is
(\omega^{2})(\omega^{2})-(-\omega)(\omega)=\omega^{4}+\omega^{2}=\omega^{2}(\omega^{2}+1)
. Because
\omega\neq 0
, the determinant is strictly non-zero, making the matrix invertible with bounded entries.

Consequently, the vector
(I_{T,c},I_{T,s})^{T}
is
o(A(t))
if and only if the vector
(I_{S,c},I_{S,s})^{T}
is
o(A(t))
. Therefore, Statement (S) and Statement (T) are logically equivalent.
∎

9.
Second-Order Filtering: The Function
y_{2}(t)

We further refine our asymptotic analysis by introducing a second filtering function
y_{2}(t)
.

Definition 17
.

Let
e_{\omega^{2}}(t)=e^{-\omega^{2}t}
. Define
y_{2}(t)
as the solution to the first-order linear equation:

y_{2}^{\prime}(t)=-\omega^{2}y_{2}(t)+y_{1}(t),\quad y_{2}(0)=0.

Equivalently,
y_{2}(t)
is given by the convolution
y_{2}=e_{\omega^{2}}*y_{1}
.

Theorem 18

(Representations of
y_{2}
and
x
)
.

Assuming zero initial conditions for
x
,
y_{2}(t)
can be represented purely in terms of
x(t)
as:

(23)

y_{2}(t)=x(t)+\int_{0}^{t}K_{3}(t,s)x(s)\,ds,

where
K_{3}(t,s)=h^{\prime\prime}(t-s)+\omega^{2}h(t-s)+p(s)h^{\prime}(t-s)-p^{\prime}(s)h(t-s)
and
h(t)=te^{-\omega^{2}t}
.

Conversely,
x(t)
can be represented purely in terms of
y_{2}
as:

(24)

x(t)=y_{2}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\left[\omega^{2}K_{1}(t,s)-\frac{\partial K_{1}(t,s)}{\partial s}\right]y_{2}(s)\,ds.

Proof.

Recall from Section 6 that
y_{1}=e_{\omega^{2}}*(x^{\prime\prime}+px^{\prime}+\omega^{2}x)
. By definition,
y_{2}=e_{\omega^{2}}*y_{1}
. Convolution is associative, so let
h=e_{\omega^{2}}*e_{\omega^{2}}
. Evaluating this yields
h(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\omega^{2}(t-s)}e^{-\omega^{2}s}\,ds=te^{-\omega^{2}t}
.
Therefore,
y_{2}=h*x^{\prime\prime}+h*(px^{\prime})+\omega^{2}(h*x)
.

We eliminate the derivatives on
x
via integration by parts. Since
h(0)=0
and
h^{\prime}(0)=1
:

(h*x^{\prime\prime})(t)=\int_{0}^{t}h(t-s)x^{\prime\prime}(s)\,ds=\left[h(t-s)x^{\prime}(s)\right]_{0}^{t}+\int_{0}^{t}h^{\prime}(t-s)x^{\prime}(s)\,ds=(h^{\prime}*x^{\prime})(t).

Integrating by parts again:

(h^{\prime}*x^{\prime})(t)=\left[h^{\prime}(t-s)x(s)\right]_{0}^{t}+\int_{0}^{t}h^{\prime\prime}(t-s)x(s)\,ds=x(t)+(h^{\prime\prime}*x)(t).

For the damping term:

(h*px^{\prime})(t)=\left[h(t-s)p(s)x(s)\right]_{0}^{t}-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\left(h(t-s)p(s)\right)x(s)\,ds.

The boundary term is
0
. The derivative is
-h^{\prime}(t-s)p(s)+h(t-s)p^{\prime}(s)
. Thus:

(h*px^{\prime})(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\left[h^{\prime}(t-s)p(s)-h(t-s)p^{\prime}(s)\right]x(s)\,ds.

Collecting all terms gives the representation (
23
).

For the converse relation, recall
x(t)=\int_{0}^{t}K_{1}(t,s)y_{1}(s)\,ds
where
K_{1}=\omega^{2}R-R_{s}
. Substitute
y_{1}(s)=y_{2}^{\prime}(s)+\omega^{2}y_{2}(s)
:

x(t)=\int_{0}^{t}K_{1}(t,s)y_{2}^{\prime}(s)\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\omega^{2}K_{1}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds.

Integrate the first term by parts (
u=K_{1},dv=y_{2}^{\prime}ds
):

\int_{0}^{t}K_{1}(t,s)y_{2}^{\prime}(s)\,ds=\left[K_{1}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\right]_{s=0}^{s=t}-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial K_{1}(t,s)}{\partial s}y_{2}(s)\,ds.

Since
y_{2}(0)=0
, the lower boundary vanishes. At
s=t
,
K_{1}(t,t)=\omega^{2}R(t,t)-R_{s}(t,t)
. Since
R(t,t)=0
and
R_{s}(t,t)=-1
(derived via the Wronskian of
G_{u}
evaluated at
s=t
), we have
K_{1}(t,t)=1
. The boundary term evaluates to exactly
y_{2}(t)
. Substituting this back gives (
24
).
∎

Theorem 19
.

If
x(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, then
y_{2}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

We examine the integral representation (
23
):

y_{2}(t)=x(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\left[h^{\prime\prime}(t-s)+\omega^{2}h(t-s)\right]x(s)\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\left[p(s)h^{\prime}(t-s)-p^{\prime}(s)h(t-s)\right]x(s)\,ds.

Since
h(t)=te^{-\omega^{2}t}
, the functions
h,h^{\prime},
and
h^{\prime\prime}
all consist of polynomials multiplied by decaying exponentials, meaning they are absolutely integrable on
[0,\infty)
.

We analyze the terms individually as
t\to\infty
:

(1)

x(t)\to 0
by hypothesis.

(2)

The kernel
h^{\prime\prime}+\omega^{2}h
is in
L^{1}(0,\infty)
. The convolution of an
L^{1}
kernel with a function that tends to zero also tends to zero.

(3)

Since
x(t)\to 0
and
p(t)\to 0
, their product
p(t)x(t)\to 0
. Since
h^{\prime}\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
, the convolution
(h^{\prime}*px)(t)\to 0
.

(4)

Since
x(t)\to 0
and
p^{\prime}(t)\to 0
, their product
p^{\prime}(t)x(t)\to 0
. Since
h\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
, the convolution
(h*p^{\prime}x)(t)\to 0
.

Because every additive component of
y_{2}(t)
independently tends to
0
, we conclude
y_{2}(t)\to 0
.
∎

Theorem 20

(Leading Order Behavior of
x(t)
via
y_{2}
)
.

Define the leading-order sinusoidal kernel for the
y_{2}
transformation as:

L_{3}(t,s)=(\omega^{3}-\omega)\sin(\omega(t-s))+2\omega^{2}\cos(\omega(t-s)).

Assuming zero initial conditions, the state
x(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
if and only if
y_{2}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
and the following convolution integral vanishes:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)L_{3}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds=0.

Proof.

From Theorem 11.1, the exact representation of
x(t)
in terms of
y_{2}(t)
is given by the integral equation:

x(t)=y_{2}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}K_{4}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds,

where the exact kernel is defined as

K_{4}(t,s)=\omega^{2}K_{1}(t,s)-\frac{\partial K_{1}(t,s)}{\partial s}.

Step 1: Explicit Kernel Expansion

We extract the leading-order behavior of
K_{4}(t,s)
by substituting the asymptotic expansion
K_{1}(t,s)=E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)+P_{1}(t,s)
, where
L_{1}(t,s)=\omega\sin(\omega(t-s))+\cos(\omega(t-s))
and
P_{1}(t,s)
is the exact remainder kernel established in Theorem 9.2.

Applying the product rule to differentiate the leading term with respect to
s
, and recalling that
\frac{\partial}{\partial s}E(t,s)=\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)
, we find:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial s}[E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)+E(t,s)\frac{\partial L_{1}(t,s)}{\partial s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)+E(t,s)[-\omega^{2}\cos(\omega(t-s))+\omega\sin(\omega(t-s))].

Substituting this into the definition of
K_{4}(t,s)
, we group the purely oscillatory
\mathcal{O}(1)
terms (those independent of
p(s)
and the perturbation) to form
L_{3}(t,s)
:

\displaystyle\omega^{2}L_{1}(t,s)-\frac{\partial L_{1}(t,s)}{\partial s}

\displaystyle=\omega^{2}[\omega\sin(\omega(t-s))+\cos(\omega(t-s))]-[-\omega^{2}\cos(\omega(t-s))+\omega\sin(\omega(t-s))]

\displaystyle=(\omega^{3}-\omega)\sin(\omega(t-s))+2\omega^{2}\cos(\omega(t-s)).

This algebraically simplifies to exactly
L_{3}(t,s)
. Thus, we can express the full kernel as
K_{4}(t,s)=E(t,s)L_{3}(t,s)+P_{4}(t,s)
, where the remainder kernel
P_{4}(t,s)
is given by:

P_{4}(t,s)=-\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)+\omega^{2}P_{1}(t,s)-\frac{\partial P_{1}(t,s)}{\partial s}.

Crucially, every component of
P_{4}(t,s)
is bounded by uniform multiples of
E(t,s)p(s)
and the perturbation weight
E(t,s)|q(s)|
. By Lemma 9.1, these weights are uniformly integrable over
[0,\infty)
with respect to
s
. Let
\sup_{t\geq 0}\int_{0}^{t}|P_{4}(t,s)|\,ds=M<\infty
.

Substitute this kernel expansion back into the integral for
x(t)
:

(25)

x(t)-y_{2}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)L_{3}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}P_{4}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds.

Step 2: Proof of the Forward Direction

Assume
x(t)\to 0
. By Theorem 11.2, it immediately follows that
y_{2}(t)\to 0
. We must show the remainder integral vanishes. Let
\epsilon>0
. Because
y_{2}(t)\to 0
, there exists a
T>0
such that
|y_{2}(t)|<\frac{\epsilon}{2M}
for all
t\geq T
. Furthermore, since
y_{2}
is continuous and converges, it is bounded:
|y_{2}(t)|\leq Y
. We split the remainder integral:

\left|\int_{0}^{t}P_{4}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds\right|\leq\int_{0}^{T}|P_{4}(t,s)||y_{2}(s)|\,ds+\int_{T}^{t}|P_{4}(t,s)||y_{2}(s)|\,ds.

For the second integral, we apply the uniform bound on
y_{2}
:

\int_{T}^{t}|P_{4}(t,s)||y_{2}(s)|\,ds\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2M}\int_{T}^{t}|P_{4}(t,s)|\,ds\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2M}\cdot M=\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

For the first integral, the domain of integration
[0,T]
is fixed. As
t\to\infty
, the exponential weight
E(t,s)=\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{t}p(\tau)d\tau)
tends pointwise to
0
because
p\notin L^{1}(0,\infty)
. By the Bounded Convergence Theorem, this integral vanishes. Thus, for sufficiently large
t
, the first integral is strictly less than
\epsilon/2
. The sum is bounded by
\epsilon
, proving
\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}P_{4}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds=0
.

Since both
x(t)-y_{2}(t)\to 0
and the remainder integral tends to
0
, rearranging equation (
25
) strictly forces
\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)L_{3}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds=0
.

Step 3: Proof of the Reverse Direction

Assume
y_{2}(t)\to 0
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)L_{3}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds=0
. Using the exact identical
\epsilon
-
T
splitting argument from Step 2, the condition
y_{2}(t)\to 0
ensures that the remainder integral
\int_{0}^{t}P_{4}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds
vanishes.

Returning to equation (
25
), we have:

x(t)=y_{2}(t)+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)L_{3}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds}_{\to 0\text{ by hypothesis}}+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t}P_{4}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds}_{\to 0\text{ by splitting argument}}.

Since
y_{2}(t)\to 0
and both integrals tend to
0
, their algebraic sum must tend to
0
. Thus,
x(t)\to 0
.
∎

Theorem 21

(Unified Asymptotic Equivalence for
x(t)
)
.

Let
A(t)=e^{\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}p(\tau)\,d\tau}\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
. Assuming zero initial conditions,
x(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
is logically equivalent to the simultaneous fulfillment of the following three conditions:

(1)

y_{2}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
,

(2)

\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\omega s)A(s)y_{2}(s)\,ds=o(A(t))
as
t\to\infty
,

(3)

\int_{0}^{t}\sin(\omega s)A(s)y_{2}(s)\,ds=o(A(t))
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

We will utilize Theorem
20
, which established that
x(t)\to 0
if and only if
y_{2}(t)\to 0
and the integral condition with
L_{3}(t,s)
vanishes.

Step 1: Setup and Matrix Representation

Define the normalized integral functions:

U_{2}(t)=\frac{1}{A(t)}\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\omega s)A(s)y_{2}(s)\,ds\quad\text{and}\quad V_{2}(t)=\frac{1}{A(t)}\int_{0}^{t}\sin(\omega s)A(s)y_{2}(s)\,ds.

Conditions (2) and (3) are precisely the statements that
U_{2}(t)\to 0
and
V_{2}(t)\to 0
.

Using trigonometric addition formulas (identically to Theorem 10.2), we expand
L_{3}(t,s)
and factor out the
s
-dependent terms. We define
W(t)
as the normalized
L_{3}
integral:

W(t)=\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)L_{3}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds=C_{1}(t)U_{2}(t)+C_{2}(t)V_{2}(t),

where the coefficient functions are purely oscillatory:

\displaystyle C_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=(\omega^{3}-\omega)\sin(\omega t)+2\omega^{2}\cos(\omega t),

\displaystyle C_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=-(\omega^{3}-\omega)\cos(\omega t)+2\omega^{2}\sin(\omega t).

If
y_{2}\to 0
,
U_{2}\to 0
, and
V_{2}\to 0
, then because
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are bounded,
W(t)\to 0
. By Theorem
20
, this immediately proves the backward direction: conditions (1), (2), and (3) imply
x(t)\to 0
.

Step 2: Bounding the Integral Terms via Localized Contraction

We have established that the linear combination
W(t)=C_{1}(t)U_{2}(t)+C_{2}(t)V_{2}(t)
is uniformly bounded. To rigorously show that
U_{2}(t)
and
V_{2}(t)
are individually globally bounded, we construct a full-rank system by evaluating
W
at a time delay
\tau=\frac{\pi}{2\omega}
.

Using the quarter-period phase shift properties
C_{1}(t-\tau)=C_{2}(t)
and
C_{2}(t-\tau)=-C_{1}(t)
, we obtain the delayed equation:

W(t-\tau)=C_{2}(t)U_{2}(t-\tau)-C_{1}(t)V_{2}(t-\tau).

Let
X(t)=(U_{2}(t),V_{2}(t))^{T}
. We rewrite the delayed state as
X(t-\tau)=X(t)+\Delta(t)
, where
\Delta(t)
is the backward translation error. This allows us to express the system evaluated exactly at time
t
:

\begin{pmatrix}W(t)\\
W(t-\tau)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}C_{1}(t)&C_{2}(t)\\
C_{2}(t)&-C_{1}(t)\end{pmatrix}X(t)+\begin{pmatrix}0\\
C_{2}(t)\Delta_{U}(t)-C_{1}(t)\Delta_{V}(t)\end{pmatrix}

The coefficient matrix
B(t)
has determinant
-\omega^{2}(\omega^{2}+1)^{2}\neq 0
. Because this determinant is a non-zero constant, the inverse matrix
B(t)^{-1}
exists and is uniformly bounded by a constant
K_{B}
. Solving for
X(t)
and taking the norm, we bound the state vector:

\|X(t)\|\leq K_{B}\left(\left\|\begin{pmatrix}W(t)\\
W(t-\tau)\end{pmatrix}\right\|+C_{max}\|\Delta(t)\|\right)

where
C_{max}
is the maximum magnitude of the oscillatory coefficients
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
.

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, the translation error is
\Delta(t)=-\int_{t-\tau}^{t}X^{\prime}(s)ds
. Substituting the derivative definitions
U_{2}^{\prime}(s)
and
V_{2}^{\prime}(s)
, and noting that
y_{2}(s)
is globally bounded by
M_{y}
, we bound the error:

\|\Delta(t)\|\leq\int_{t-\tau}^{t}\left(M_{y}+\frac{1}{2}p(s)\|X(s)\|\right)ds

Since
W(t)
is globally bounded, we can absorb the
W
terms and the integral of the constant
M_{y}
over the fixed interval
\tau
into a single global constant
K_{1}
. Letting
K_{2}=\frac{1}{2}K_{B}C_{max}
, our bound simplifies to:

\|X(t)\|\leq K_{1}+K_{2}\int_{t-\tau}^{t}p(s)\|X(s)\|ds

Because
p(s)\to 0
as
s\to\infty
, there exists a sufficiently large time
T
such that for all
s\geq T-\tau
, we have
p(s)<\frac{1}{2K_{2}\tau}
.

Let
M(t)=\max_{s\in[T-\tau,t]}\|X(s)\|
represent the running maximum of the state norm. For any intermediate time
t^{*}\in[T,t]
, we can bound the integral:

\|X(t^{*})\|\leq K_{1}+\left(K_{2}\tau\max_{s\in[t^{*}-\tau,t^{*}]}p(s)\right)M(t)\leq K_{1}+\frac{1}{2}M(t)

Since this inequality holds for all
t^{*}
up to
t
, it firmly bounds the running maximum itself:

M(t)\leq\max\left(M(T),K_{1}+\frac{1}{2}M(t)\right)

This immediately implies that
M(t)\leq\max(M(T),2K_{1})
for all
t\geq T
. Since
X(t)
is continuous on the initial interval
[0,T]
, it is globally bounded for all
t\geq 0
. Consequently, both
U_{2}(t)
and
V_{2}(t)
are globally bounded.

Step 3: The Translation Argument

By the quotient rule and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (recalling
A^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{2}p(t)A(t)
), the derivative of
U_{2}(t)
is:

U_{2}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{A(t)[\cos(\omega t)A(t)y_{2}(t)]-A^{\prime}(t)\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\omega s)A(s)y_{2}(s)\,ds}{A(t)^{2}}=\cos(\omega t)y_{2}(t)-\frac{1}{2}p(t)U_{2}(t).

A similar calculation holds for
V^{\prime}
.
Since
U_{2}(t)
and
V_{2}(t)
are globally bounded and
y_{2}(t)\to 0
and
p(t)\to 0
, it strictly follows that
U_{2}^{\prime}(t)\to 0
and
V_{2}^{\prime}(t)\to 0
. Thus,
U_{2}
and
V_{2}
are slowly varying functions.

We evaluate the identity
W(t)=C_{1}(t)U_{2}(t)+C_{2}(t)V_{2}(t)
at a shifted time
t^{*}=t+\frac{\pi}{2\omega}
.
By the Mean Value Theorem,
U_{2}(t^{*})-U_{2}(t)=\frac{\pi}{2\omega}U_{2}^{\prime}(\xi)
for some
\xi\in(t,t^{*})
. Since
U_{2}^{\prime}\to 0
globally, this difference tends to zero, meaning
U_{2}(t^{*})=U_{2}(t)+o(1)
. The same holds for
V_{2}(t^{*})
.

Furthermore, shifting the oscillatory functions by a quarter-period yields:

\displaystyle C_{1}(t^{*})

\displaystyle=(\omega^{3}-\omega)\sin\left(\omega t+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)+2\omega^{2}\cos\left(\omega t+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=(\omega^{3}-\omega)\cos(\omega t)-2\omega^{2}\sin(\omega t)=-C_{2}(t),

\displaystyle C_{2}(t^{*})

\displaystyle=-(\omega^{3}-\omega)\cos\left(\omega t+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)+2\omega^{2}\sin\left(\omega t+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=(\omega^{3}-\omega)\sin(\omega t)+2\omega^{2}\cos(\omega t)=C_{1}(t).

Evaluating
W(t^{*})
utilizing these shifted identities gives:

W(t^{*})=C_{1}(t^{*})U_{2}(t^{*})+C_{2}(t^{*})V_{2}(t^{*})=-C_{2}(t)[U_{2}(t)+o(1)]+C_{1}(t)[V_{2}(t)+o(1)].

Since
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are bounded, the
o(1)
terms vanish asymptotically. We are left with the coupled
2\times 2
time-dependent linear system:

\begin{pmatrix}W(t)\\
W(t^{*})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}C_{1}(t)&C_{2}(t)\\
-C_{2}(t)&C_{1}(t)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}U_{2}(t)\\
V_{2}(t)\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0\\
o(1)\end{pmatrix}.

Step 4: Matrix Inversion

The determinant of this transformation matrix is
C_{1}(t)^{2}+C_{2}(t)^{2}
. Calculating this:

\displaystyle C_{1}^{2}+C_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left[(\omega^{3}-\omega)\sin(\omega t)+2\omega^{2}\cos(\omega t)\right]^{2}+\left[-(\omega^{3}-\omega)\cos(\omega t)+2\omega^{2}\sin(\omega t)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=(\omega^{3}-\omega)^{2}(\sin^{2}(\omega t)+\cos^{2}(\omega t))+(2\omega^{2})^{2}(\cos^{2}(\omega t)+\sin^{2}(\omega t))

\displaystyle=(\omega^{6}-2\omega^{4}+\omega^{2})+4\omega^{4}=\omega^{6}+2\omega^{4}+\omega^{2}=\omega^{2}(\omega^{2}+1)^{2}.

Because
\omega\neq 0
, the determinant is a strictly positive, non-zero constant for all time. Thus, the matrix is uniformly invertible. Since
W(t)\to 0
, we also have
W(t^{*})\to 0
. Inverting the system strictly demands that
U_{2}(t)\to 0
and
V_{2}(t)\to 0
. This establishes the necessary conditions (2) and (3), completing the proof of equivalence.
∎

10.
Preservation of Unperturbed Dynamics of
x

We have seen that the unperturbed oscillator naturally decays at a rate of
1/A(t)
. We now establish the ultimate physical consequence of our filtering method: the necessary and sufficient conditions for the fully perturbed state
x(t)
to strictly converge to the exact asymptotic trajectory of the unperturbed system.

10.1.
Subexponential Decay Bounds

We now shift our focus to the exact rate of decay of the unperturbed system, which is governed by the weight function

\rho(t)=\frac{1}{A(t)}=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}p(s)\,ds\right).

Because
p(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, the decay of
\rho(t)
is strictly subexponential. In this context, a function is subexponential if

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\rho^{\prime}(t)}{\rho(t)}=0.

Lemma 22

(Convolution with Subexponential Weights)
.

Let
\rho
be a subexponential function. Let
k
be such that there exist constants
C>0
and
\alpha>0
such that
|k(t)|\leq Ce^{-\alpha t}
for all
t\geq 0
.
If a continuous function
z
satisfies
z(t)=O(\rho(t))
as
t\to\infty
, then
(k*z)(t)=O(\rho(t))
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

By hypothesis, there exists a constant
M>0
such that
|z(t)|\leq M\rho(t)
for all
t\geq 0
. We bound the absolute value of the convolution:

|(k*z)(t)|\leq\int_{0}^{t}|k(t-s)||z(s)|\,ds\leq CM\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\alpha(t-s)}\rho(s)\,ds=CMe^{-\alpha t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\alpha s}\rho(s)\,ds.

To analyze the asymptotic behavior of this bounding function, we apply L’Hôpital’s rule:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\int_{0}^{t}e^{\alpha s}\rho(s)\,ds}{e^{\alpha t}\rho(t)}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{e^{\alpha t}\rho(t)}{\alpha e^{\alpha t}\rho(t)+e^{\alpha t}\rho^{\prime}(t)}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha+\frac{\rho^{\prime}(t)}{\rho(t)}}.

Since
\rho^{\prime}(t)/\rho(t)\to 0
by the subexponential property of
\rho
, the limit evaluates exactly to
1/\alpha
.
Consequently, as
t\to\infty
, the integral
\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\alpha(t-s)}\rho(s)\,ds
is asymptotically equivalent to
\rho(t)/\alpha
. Therefore,
(k*z)(t)=O(\rho(t))
, completing the proof.
∎

10.2.
Unperturbed dynamics preserved implies
y_{2}=o(1/A)

We now prove the implication: if the physical state converges to the unperturbed asymptotic trajectory, then the filtered residual
y_{2}(t)
must decay strictly faster than the unperturbed amplitude envelope
1/A(t)
.

Theorem 23

(Asymptotic Form Implies
o(1/A(t))
Residual)
.

Let
\rho(t)=1/A(t)
. Assume
p(t)\to 0
and
p^{\prime}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.
If the physical state satisfies

x(t)=x_{0}(t)+x_{1}(t)=\rho(t)\left[c_{1}\sin(\omega t)+c_{2}\cos(\omega t)\right]+o(\rho(t))\quad\text{as }t\to\infty,

where
x_{0}(t)
is the leading oscillatory term and
x_{1}(t)=o(\rho(t))
is the remainder, then the second-order filtered forcing satisfies
y_{2}(t)=o(\rho(t))
.

Proof.

Recall the representation of
y_{2}(t)
in terms of
x(t)
using the exponentially decaying kernel
h(t)
and its derivatives:

y_{2}(t)=x(t)+(h^{\prime\prime}*x)(t)+\omega^{2}(h*x)(t)+(h^{\prime}*px)(t)-(h*p^{\prime}x)(t).

By hypothesis,
h
,
h^{\prime}
, and
h^{\prime\prime}
are all bounded by
Ce^{-\alpha t}
for some constants
C,\alpha>0
. We will analyze the terms of
y_{2}(t)
in three distinct parts.

Because
x_{1}(t)=o(\rho(t))
, Lemma
22
immediately implies that convolutions of
x_{1}
with any exponentially decaying kernel remain
o(\rho(t))
. Thus,
x_{1}+h^{\prime\prime}*x_{1}+\omega^{2}h*x_{1}=o(\rho(t))
.

Next, consider the terms involving
p(t)
and
p^{\prime}(t)
. We know
x(t)=O(\rho(t))
. Since
p(t)\to 0
and
p^{\prime}(t)\to 0
, the products are strictly smaller than
\rho(t)
:

p(t)x(t)=o(\rho(t))\quad\text{and}\quad p^{\prime}(t)x(t)=o(\rho(t)).

Applying Lemma
22
again, the convolutions
(h^{\prime}*px)(t)
and
(h*p^{\prime}x)(t)
are strictly
o(\rho(t))
.

Therefore, all that remains is to evaluate the linear leading-order operator
L
applied to the main oscillatory term
x_{0}(t)
:

L[x_{0}](t)=x_{0}(t)+(h^{\prime\prime}*x_{0})(t)+\omega^{2}(h*x_{0})(t).

Now, we recall that
\rho(t)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}p(s)\,ds\right)
, where
\lim_{t\to\infty}p(t)=0
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}p^{\prime}(t)=0
, and
x_{0}(t)=\rho(t)\Theta(t)
, where
\Theta(t)=c_{1}\cos(\omega t)+c_{2}\sin(\omega t)
. Recall also the definition

h(t)=te^{-\omega^{2}t}
for some
\omega>0
. We must show that

L[x_{0}](t)=:l(t):=x_{0}(t)+(h^{\prime\prime}*x_{0})(t)+\omega^{2}(h*x_{0})(t).

is such that
l(t)=o(\rho(t))
.
First, note that
\rho^{\prime}(t)=o(\rho(t))
,
\rho^{\prime\prime}(t)=o(\rho(t))
as
t\to\infty
. By definition, we have

\rho^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}p(t)\rho(t),

so, as
p(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
,
\rho^{\prime}(t)=o(\rho(t))
.
Differentiating again we get

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}p^{\prime}(t)\rho(t)-\frac{1}{2}p(t)\rho^{\prime}(t)=\rho(t)\left(\frac{1}{4}p(t)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}p^{\prime}(t)\right).

Since
\lim_{t\to\infty}p(t)=0
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}p^{\prime}(t)=0
, we have
\rho^{\prime\prime}(t)=o(\rho(t))
.

Next, we analyse the
x_{0}
dependent terms in
l
.
Notice that
\Theta(t)
obeys
\Theta^{\prime\prime}(t)+\omega^{2}\Theta(t)=0
. Then

x_{0}^{\prime}(t)=\rho^{\prime}(t)\Theta(t)+\rho(t)\Theta^{\prime}(t),

and

x_{0}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\rho^{\prime\prime}(t)\Theta(t)+2\rho^{\prime}(t)\Theta^{\prime}(t)+\rho(t)\Theta^{\prime\prime}(t)

Substituting
\Theta^{\prime\prime}(t)=-\omega^{2}\Theta(t)
into the second derivative, we get

x_{0}^{\prime\prime}(t)+\omega^{2}x_{0}(t)=\rho^{\prime\prime}(t)\Theta(t)+2\rho^{\prime}(t)\Theta^{\prime}(t).

Since
\Theta(t)
and
\Theta^{\prime}(t)
are bounded trigonometric functions, and we have proven
\rho^{\prime}=o(\rho)
and
\rho^{\prime\prime}=o(\rho)
, it immediately follows that:

x_{0}^{\prime\prime}(t)+\omega^{2}x_{0}(t)=o(\rho(t)).

Let us define
g(t):=x_{0}^{\prime\prime}(t)+\omega^{2}x_{0}(t)
. We have established
g(t)=o(\rho(t))
.

We are given
h(t)=te^{-\omega^{2}t}
. Then
h(0)=0
,
h^{\prime}(0)=1
. We now examine the convolution
(h^{\prime\prime}*x_{0})(t)=\int_{0}^{t}h^{\prime\prime}(t-s)x_{0}(s)\,ds
. We apply integration by parts twice to shift the derivatives from
h
to
x_{0}
.
First integration by parts:

\int_{0}^{t}h^{\prime\prime}(t-s)x_{0}(s)\,ds=\Big[-h^{\prime}(t-s)x_{0}(s)\Big]_{s=0}^{s=t}+\int_{0}^{t}h^{\prime}(t-s)x_{0}^{\prime}(s)\,ds

=-h^{\prime}(0)x_{0}(t)+h^{\prime}(t)x_{0}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}h^{\prime}(t-s)x_{0}^{\prime}(s)\,ds.

Second integration by parts on the remaining integral:

\int_{0}^{t}h^{\prime}(t-s)x_{0}^{\prime}(s)\,ds=\Big[-h(t-s)x_{0}^{\prime}(s)\Big]_{s=0}^{s=t}+\int_{0}^{t}h(t-s)x_{0}^{\prime\prime}(s)\,ds

=-h(0)x_{0}^{\prime}(t)+h(t)x_{0}^{\prime}(0)+(h*x_{0}^{\prime\prime})(t).

Combining these and applying the boundary values
h(0)=0
and
h^{\prime}(0)=1
:

(h^{\prime\prime}*x_{0})(t)=-x_{0}(t)+h^{\prime}(t)x_{0}(0)+h(t)x_{0}^{\prime}(0)+(h*x_{0}^{\prime\prime})(t).

Now, substitute this expanded form back into the definition of
l(t)
:

\displaystyle l(t)

\displaystyle=x_{0}(t)+\Big(-x_{0}(t)+h^{\prime}(t)x_{0}(0)+h(t)x_{0}^{\prime}(0)+(h*x_{0}^{\prime\prime})(t)\Big)+\omega^{2}(h*x_{0})(t)

\displaystyle=h^{\prime}(t)x_{0}(0)+h(t)x_{0}^{\prime}(0)T(t)+\Big(h*(x_{0}^{\prime\prime}+\omega^{2}x_{0})\Big)(t)

\displaystyle=:T(t)+(h*g)(t).

We must show that both
T(t)
and
(h*g)(t)
are
o(\rho(t))
.

1. The Transients:

T(t)=(1-\omega^{2}t)e^{-\omega^{2}t}x_{0}(0)+te^{-\omega^{2}t}x_{0}^{\prime}(0)
. This decays exponentially. Since
p(t)\to 0
, the integral
\int_{0}^{t}p(s)\,ds
grows slower than any linear function
\epsilon t
, meaning
\rho(t)
decays at most sub-exponentially. Therefore, the exponentially decaying
T(t)
is trivially
o(\rho(t))
.

2. The Convolution:

We must evaluate
\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{\rho(t)}\int_{0}^{t}h(s)g(t-s)\,ds
.
Because
g(t)=o(\rho(t))
, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists an
M>0
such that for all
\tau>M
,
|g(\tau)|<\epsilon\rho(\tau)
. Furthermore,
g(t)
is globally bounded by
C\rho(t)
for some constant
C
.
We split the integral at
t-M
:

\frac{1}{\rho(t)}\int_{0}^{t}|h(s)||g(t-s)|\,ds=\int_{0}^{t-M}|h(s)|\frac{|g(t-s)|}{\rho(t)}\,ds+\int_{t-M}^{t}|h(s)|\frac{|g(t-s)|}{\rho(t)}\,ds.

In the first integral (
s\in[0,t-M]
), we have
t-s\geq M
, so
|g(t-s)|<\epsilon\rho(t-s)
. Note that:

\frac{\rho(t-s)}{\rho(t)}=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{t-s}^{t}p(\tau)\,d\tau\right).

Since
p(t)\to 0
,
p(t)
is bounded by some small constant
P\ll\omega^{2}
. Thus, the ratio
\rho(t-s)/\rho(t)
is bounded by
e^{Ps/2}
. Because
h(s)=se^{-\omega^{2}s}
decays much faster than
e^{Ps/2}
grows, the product
|h(s)|\frac{\rho(t-s)}{\rho(t)}
is entirely absolutely integrable on
[0,\infty)
. Let this integral be bounded by a constant
K
. The first integral is thus strictly bounded by
\epsilon K
.

For the second integral (
s\in[t-M,t]
),
h(s)
is evaluated at values tending to infinity. Since
h(s)
decays exponentially, and the integration interval
[t-M,t]
has fixed length
M
, this integral vanishes completely as
t\to\infty
.

Since
\epsilon
was arbitrary, the entire convolution is bounded by
0
in the limit. Thus,
(h*g)(t)=o(\rho(t))
.

Summing the asymptotic bounds,
l(t)=T(t)+(h*g)(t)=o(\rho(t))+o(\rho(t))=o(\rho(t))
, completing the proof.
∎

11.
Representation of the Solution; Bounds on Kernels

From Theorem 11.1, the exact representation of
x(t)
in terms of
y_{2}(t)
is given by the integral equation:

x(t)=y_{2}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}K_{4}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds,

where the exact kernel is defined as

K_{4}(t,s)=\omega^{2}K_{1}(t,s)-\frac{\partial K_{1}(t,s)}{\partial s}.

We have that
K_{1}(t,s)=E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)+P_{1}(t,s)
, where
L_{1}(t,s)=\omega\sin(\omega(t-s))+\cos(\omega(t-s))
. Furthermore, we have the decomposition
K_{4}(t,s)=E(t,s)L_{3}(t,s)+P_{4}(t,s)
, where the remainder kernel
P_{4}(t,s)
is given by:

P_{4}(t,s)=-\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)+\omega^{2}P_{1}(t,s)-\frac{\partial P_{1}(t,s)}{\partial s},

and
L_{3}
is given by

L_{3}(t,s)=(\omega^{3}-\omega)\sin(\omega(t-s))+2\omega^{2}\cos(\omega(t-s)).

Consequently

(26)

x(t)=y_{2}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}K_{4}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds\\
=y_{2}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)L_{3}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}P_{4}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds=:y_{2}(t)+W(t)+D(t).

We will assume that
y_{2}(t)=o(\rho(t))
. We start by showing that the leading integral
W(t)/\rho(t)
has sinusoidal behaviour, provided

(27)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\omega s)A(s)y_{2}(s)\,ds=I_{c}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\sin(\omega s)A(s)y_{2}(s)\,ds=I_{s}.

Then we will determine the asymptotic behaviour of the remainder term
D
.

Theorem 24
.

Suppose
y_{2}(t)=o(\rho(t))
and (
27
) holds. Then

W(t)=\int_{0}^{t}E(t,s)L_{3}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds

obeys

W(t)=\rho(t)(c_{1}\sin(\omega t)+c_{2}\sin(\omega t))+o(\rho(t)),\quad t\to\infty,

where

c_{1}=(\omega^{3}-\omega)I_{c}+2\omega^{2}I_{s},\quad c_{2}=2\omega^{2}I_{c}-(\omega^{3}-\omega)I_{s}.

Proof.

We substitute
E(t,s)=\frac{A(s)}{A(t)}
and pull the
t
-dependent term
\frac{1}{A(t)}
outside the integral:

W(t)=\frac{1}{A(t)}\int_{0}^{t}L_{3}(t,s)A(s)y_{2}(s)\,ds.

The kernel is defined as
L_{3}(t,s)=(\omega^{3}-\omega)\sin(\omega(t-s))+2\omega^{2}\cos(\omega(t-s))
. To isolate the integration variable
s
, we apply the trigonometric angle-difference identities:

\displaystyle\sin(\omega(t-s))

\displaystyle=\sin(\omega t)\cos(\omega s)-\cos(\omega t)\sin(\omega s),

\displaystyle\cos(\omega(t-s))

\displaystyle=\cos(\omega t)\cos(\omega s)+\sin(\omega t)\sin(\omega s).

Substituting these into
L_{3}(t,s)
and grouping the terms by
\cos(\omega s)
and
\sin(\omega s)
yields:

\displaystyle L_{3}(t,s)

\displaystyle=\left[(\omega^{3}-\omega)\sin(\omega t)+2\omega^{2}\cos(\omega t)\right]\cos(\omega s)

\displaystyle\quad+\left[2\omega^{2}\sin(\omega t)-(\omega^{3}-\omega)\cos(\omega t)\right]\sin(\omega s).

Let us define the purely
t
-dependent coefficient functions:

\displaystyle C_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=(\omega^{3}-\omega)\sin(\omega t)+2\omega^{2}\cos(\omega t),

\displaystyle C_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=2\omega^{2}\sin(\omega t)-(\omega^{3}-\omega)\cos(\omega t).

This allows us to write the leading integral as a coupled sum:

(28)

W(t)=\frac{C_{1}(t)}{A(t)}\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\omega s)A(s)y_{2}(s)\,ds+\frac{C_{2}(t)}{A(t)}\int_{0}^{t}\sin(\omega s)A(s)y_{2}(s)\,ds.

By assumption, the integrals converge to finite limits
I_{c}
and
I_{s}
as
t\to\infty
. Therefore, as the improper integral converges, we have that the cosine integral is
I_{c}+o(1)
as
t\to\infty
. Similarly, the sine integral is
I_{s}+o(1)
.
Substituting these into Equation (
28
):

W(t)=\frac{C_{1}(t)}{A(t)}[I_{c}+o(1)]+\frac{C_{2}(t)}{A(t)}[I_{s}+o(1)].

Because
C_{1}(t)
and
C_{2}(t)
are continuous and composed of sine and cosine functions, they are uniformly globally bounded. Therefore:

W(t)=\frac{C_{1}(t)I_{c}+C_{2}(t)I_{s}}{A(t)}+o\left(\frac{1}{A(t)}\right).

Using the definition of
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, the numerator in the first term becomes:

\displaystyle C_{1}(t)I_{c}+C_{2}(t)I_{s}

\displaystyle=[(\omega^{3}-\omega)\sin(\omega t)+2\omega^{2}\cos(\omega t)]I_{c}+[2\omega^{2}\sin(\omega t)-(\omega^{3}-\omega)\cos(\omega t)]I_{s}

\displaystyle=\sin(\omega t)\left[(\omega^{3}-\omega)I_{c}+2\omega^{2}I_{s}\right]+\cos(\omega t)\left[2\omega^{2}I_{c}-(\omega^{3}-\omega)I_{s}\right].

By defining the trajectory constants as:

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=(\omega^{3}-\omega)I_{c}+2\omega^{2}I_{s},

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=2\omega^{2}I_{c}-(\omega^{3}-\omega)I_{s},

we immediately recover

W(t)=\frac{c_{1}\sin(\omega t)+c_{2}\cos(\omega t)}{A(t)}+o(1/A(t)),

as required.
∎

11.1.
Decomposition of the Kernel
P_{4}(t,s)

We now establish that while the remainder kernel
P_{4}(t,s)
contains a slowly decaying
O(p(s))
term, its action inside the integral is rigorously governed by the absolutely integrable residual potential
Q(s)=\left|-\frac{1}{2}p^{\prime}(s)-\frac{1}{4}p^{2}(s)\right|
.

Lemma 25
.

Let
K_{4}(t,s)=\omega^{2}K_{1}(t,s)-\frac{\partial K_{1}(t,s)}{\partial s}
with the decomposition
K_{1}(t,s)=E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)+P_{1}(t,s)
, where
L_{1}(t,s)=\omega\sin(\omega(t-s))+\cos(\omega(t-s))
. Then with
P_{4}
defined by

P_{4}(t,s)=-\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)+\omega^{2}P_{1}(t,s)-\frac{\partial P_{1}(t,s)}{\partial s},

we have the decomposition

P_{4}(t,s)=P_{4,Q}(t,s)-\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\left(\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)F(t,s)\right),

where
F(t,s)=\cos(\omega(t-s))-\frac{1}{\omega}\sin(\omega(t-s))
,
and

P_{4,Q}(t,s)=-q(s)E(t,s)F(t,s)+\omega^{2}P_{1}(t,s)-\frac{\partial P_{1}(t,s)}{\partial s}.

Proof.

We isolate the problematic term
-\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)
. We seek a function
F(t,s)
such that
\frac{\partial F}{\partial s}=L_{1}(t,s)
.
Integrating
L_{1}(t,s)
with respect to
s
yields:

F(t,s)=\cos(\omega(t-s))-\frac{1}{\omega}\sin(\omega(t-s)).

We may therefore rewrite the
p(s)
term using
\frac{\partial F}{\partial s}
:

-\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)=-\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)\frac{\partial F(t,s)}{\partial s}.

We now apply the product rule to extract an exact derivative with respect to
s
:

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\left(\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)F(t,s)\right)=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\left(\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)\right)F(t,s)+\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)\frac{\partial F(t,s)}{\partial s}.

Rearranging this gives an expression for our target term:

-\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\left(\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)\right)F(t,s)-\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\left(\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)F(t,s)\right).

Next, we evaluate the derivative of the envelope term. Recall that
E(t,s)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{t}p(u)\,du\right)
, so
\frac{\partial E}{\partial s}=\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)
. Applying the product rule:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\left(\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}p^{\prime}(s)E(t,s)+\frac{1}{2}p(s)\left(\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}p^{\prime}(s)+\frac{1}{4}p^{2}(s)\right)E(t,s).

Notice that the term in the parentheses is exactly
-q(s)
, where
q(s)
is the residual potential. Let
Q(s)=|q(s)|
.
Substituting this back into our expression for the
L_{1}
term yields:

-\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)=-q(s)E(t,s)F(t,s)-\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\left(\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)F(t,s)\right).

Finally, we substitute this decomposition back into the original algebraic definition of
P_{4}(t,s)
:

P_{4}(t,s)=\underbrace{-q(s)E(t,s)F(t,s)+\omega^{2}P_{1}(t,s)-\frac{\partial P_{1}(t,s)}{\partial s}}_{P_{4,Q}(t,s)}-\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\left(\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)F(t,s)\right),

as claimed.
∎

11.2.
Asymptotic behaviour of the integral
P_{4}\ast y_{2}

First, we establish the core definitions and assumptions used throughout the derivations:

•

A(t)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}p(s)\,ds\right)

•

E(t,s)=\frac{A(s)}{A(t)}=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{t}p(\tau)\,d\tau\right)

•

G(t,s)=\frac{\sin(\omega(t-s))}{\omega}-\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{s}^{t}\sin(\omega(t-\tau))q(\tau)G(\tau,s)\,d\tau

•

R(t,s)=E(t,s)G(t,s)

•

L_{1}(t,s)=\omega\sin(\omega(t-s))+\cos(\omega(t-s))

•

K_{1}(t,s)=\omega^{2}R(t,s)-\frac{\partial R(t,s)}{\partial s}

•

P_{1}(t,s)=K_{1}(t,s)-E(t,s)L_{1}(t,s)

•

F(t,s)=\cos(\omega(t-s))-\frac{1}{\omega}\sin(\omega(t-s))

•

P_{4,Q}(t,s)=-q(s)E(t,s)F(t,s)+\omega^{2}P_{1}(t,s)-\frac{\partial P_{1}(t,s)}{\partial s}

•

q(s)=-\frac{p^{\prime}(s)}{2}-\frac{p^{2}(s)}{4}
with
Q(s)=|q(s)|\in L^{1}(0,\infty)

•

p(s)\to 0
and
p^{\prime}(s)\to 0
as
s\to\infty
, but
p\notin L^{1}(0,\infty)
(meaning
A(t)\to\infty
).

Lemma 26

(Estimates and Expansions for the Resolvent Kernel
G(t,s)
)
.

The function
G
and its partial derivative with respect to
s
satisfy:

(1)

G(t,s)=\frac{\sin(\omega(t-s))}{\omega}+\epsilon_{1}(t,s)
, where
|\epsilon_{1}(t,s)|\leq h_{1}\int_{s}^{t}Q(u)\,du
.

(2)

G_{s}(t,s)=-\cos(\omega(t-s))+\epsilon_{2}(t,s)
, where
|\epsilon_{2}(t,s)|\leq h_{2}\int_{s}^{t}Q(u)\,du
.

(3)

G_{ss}(t,s)=-(\omega^{2}+q(s))G(t,s)
.

Furthermore, the remainders admit the asymptotic expansions:

\epsilon_{1}(t,s)=\cos(\omega t)\epsilon_{1,c}(s)+\sin(\omega t)\epsilon_{1,s}(s)+\tilde{\epsilon}_{1}(t,s)

\epsilon_{2}(t,s)=\cos(\omega t)\epsilon_{2,c}(s)+\sin(\omega t)\epsilon_{2,s}(s)+\tilde{\epsilon}_{2}(t,s)

where
\epsilon_{i,c}(s)
and
\epsilon_{i,s}(s)
are bounded functions of
s
, and the new remainders depend only on the tail of
Q
, satisfying
|\tilde{\epsilon}_{1}(t,s)|\leq h_{1}\int_{t}^{\infty}Q(u)\,du
and
|\tilde{\epsilon}_{2}(t,s)|\leq h_{2}\int_{t}^{\infty}Q(u)\,du
.

Proof.

By standard Volterra integral equation theory, since
Q\in L^{1}
, there exists
M>0
such that
|G(t,s)|\leq M
uniformly for
0\leq s\leq t<\infty
. The remainder is defined by the integral:

\epsilon_{1}(t,s)=-\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{s}^{t}\sin(\omega(t-\tau))q(\tau)G(\tau,s)\,d\tau

Taking absolute values yields
|\epsilon_{1}(t,s)|\leq\frac{M}{\omega}\int_{s}^{t}Q(\tau)\,d\tau
. Setting
h_{1}=M/\omega
yields the first result.

Differentiating the integral equation for
G(t,s)
with respect to
s
, and noting that
G(s,s)=0
, gives:

G_{s}(t,s)=-\cos(\omega(t-s))-\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{s}^{t}\sin(\omega(t-\tau))q(\tau)G_{s}(\tau,s)\,d\tau

By Gronwall’s inequality,
|G_{s}(t,s)|\leq M_{2}
for some constant
M_{2}
. The integral term is defined as
\epsilon_{2}(t,s)
, and bounding it yields
|\epsilon_{2}(t,s)|\leq\frac{M_{2}}{\omega}\int_{s}^{t}Q(\tau)\,d\tau
. Setting
h_{2}=M_{2}/\omega
proves the second result. The third result
G_{ss}(t,s)=-(\omega^{2}+q(s))G(t,s)
follows from
G
being the resolvent kernel associated with
-y^{\prime\prime}+q(t)y=\omega^{2}y
.

To obtain the further expansions necessary for rigorous bounds as
t\to\infty
, we expand
\sin(\omega(t-\tau))=\sin(\omega t)\cos(\omega\tau)-\cos(\omega t)\sin(\omega\tau)
inside
\epsilon_{1}(t,s)
:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}(t,s)

\displaystyle=\cos(\omega t)\left(\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{s}^{t}\sin(\omega\tau)q(\tau)G(\tau,s)\,d\tau\right)-\sin(\omega t)\left(\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{s}^{t}\cos(\omega\tau)q(\tau)G(\tau,s)\,d\tau\right)

Because
Q\in L^{1}
and
G
is bounded, we can extend the integrals to infinity and subtract the tail:

\epsilon_{1,c}(s)=\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{s}^{\infty}\sin(\omega\tau)q(\tau)G(\tau,s)\,d\tau,\quad\epsilon_{1,s}(s)=-\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{s}^{\infty}\cos(\omega\tau)q(\tau)G(\tau,s)\,d\tau

The remaining tail is:

\tilde{\epsilon}_{1}(t,s)=-\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{t}^{\infty}\left[\sin(\omega\tau)\cos(\omega t)-\cos(\omega\tau)\sin(\omega t)\right]q(\tau)G(\tau,s)\,d\tau=-\frac{1}{\omega}\int_{t}^{\infty}\sin(\omega(\tau-t))q(\tau)G(\tau,s)\,d\tau

Clearly,
|\tilde{\epsilon}_{1}(t,s)|\leq\frac{M}{\omega}\int_{t}^{\infty}Q(\tau)\,d\tau=h_{1}\int_{t}^{\infty}Q(u)\,du
. An identical expansion for
\epsilon_{2}(t,s)
using
G_{s}(\tau,s)
yields
\epsilon_{2,c}(s)
,
\epsilon_{2,s}(s)
, and
\tilde{\epsilon}_{2}(t,s)
bounded by
h_{2}\int_{t}^{\infty}Q(u)\,du
.
∎

Lemma 27

(Representation of
P_{4,Q}(t,s)
)
.

P_{4,Q}(t,s)A(t)=A(s)\left[a_{1}(s)\cos(\omega(t-s))+a_{2}(s)\frac{\sin(\omega(t-s))}{\omega}+E_{\text{rem}}(t,s)\right]

where
a_{1}(s)=-\frac{p(s)}{2}-q(s)
and
a_{2}(s)=-\frac{\omega^{2}p(s)}{2}-q(s)
, both tending to
0
as
s\to\infty
. The remainder
E_{\text{rem}}(t,s)=b_{1}(s)\epsilon_{2}(t,s)+b_{2}(s)\epsilon_{1}(t,s)
is composed of functions
b_{1}(s)=-2\omega^{2}+p(s)
and
b_{2}(s)=\left(\omega^{2}-\frac{p(s)}{2}\right)^{2}-\left(\omega^{2}+q(s)-\frac{p^{\prime}(s)}{2}\right)
which are globally bounded.

Proof.

First, we expand
P_{1}(t,s)
explicitly by substituting the functions into

P_{1}(t,s)=E(t,s)\left[\left(\omega^{2}-\frac{p(s)}{2}\right)G(t,s)-G_{s}(t,s)-L_{1}(t,s)\right].

Using the decompositions from Lemma 1, we have:

\displaystyle P_{1}(t,s)

\displaystyle=E(t,s)\bigg[\left(\omega^{2}-\frac{p(s)}{2}\right)\left(\frac{\sin(\omega(t-s))}{\omega}+\epsilon_{1}(t,s)\right)-\big(-\cos(\omega(t-s))+\epsilon_{2}(t,s)\big)

\displaystyle\quad-\big(\omega\sin(\omega(t-s))+\cos(\omega(t-s))\big)\bigg]

\displaystyle=E(t,s)\bigg[-\frac{p(s)}{2\omega}\sin(\omega(t-s))+\left(\omega^{2}-\frac{p(s)}{2}\right)\epsilon_{1}(t,s)-\epsilon_{2}(t,s)\bigg]

Let us denote the term in the brackets as
H(t,s)
, so that
P_{1}(t,s)=E(t,s)H(t,s)
. To construct
P_{4,Q}(t,s)
, we require
\frac{\partial P_{1}(t,s)}{\partial s}
. Using the product rule and
\frac{\partial E(t,s)}{\partial s}=\frac{p(s)}{2}E(t,s)
, we obtain:

\frac{\partial P_{1}(t,s)}{\partial s}=\frac{p(s)}{2}E(t,s)H(t,s)+E(t,s)\frac{\partial H(t,s)}{\partial s}

Thus, the expression for
P_{4,Q}(t,s)
factors out
E(t,s)
entirely:

\displaystyle P_{4,Q}(t,s)

\displaystyle=-q(s)E(t,s)F(t,s)+\omega^{2}E(t,s)H(t,s)-\left(\frac{p(s)}{2}E(t,s)H(t,s)+E(t,s)\frac{\partial H(t,s)}{\partial s}\right)

\displaystyle=E(t,s)\left[-q(s)F(t,s)+\left(\omega^{2}-\frac{p(s)}{2}\right)H(t,s)-\frac{\partial H(t,s)}{\partial s}\right]

Next, we systematically evaluate each of the three interior terms. The first term simply expands to:

-q(s)F(t,s)=-q(s)\cos(\omega(t-s))+\frac{q(s)}{\omega}\sin(\omega(t-s))

The second term expands to:

\displaystyle\left(\omega^{2}-\frac{p(s)}{2}\right)H(t,s)

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{\omega p(s)}{2}+\frac{p^{2}(s)}{4\omega}\right)\sin(\omega(t-s))

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\omega^{2}-\frac{p(s)}{2}\right)^{2}\epsilon_{1}(t,s)-\left(\omega^{2}-\frac{p(s)}{2}\right)\epsilon_{2}(t,s)

To compute
\frac{\partial H(t,s)}{\partial s}
for the third term, we first need the partial derivatives of the remainders. From Lemma 1:

\frac{\partial\epsilon_{1}(t,s)}{\partial s}=G_{s}(t,s)+\cos(\omega(t-s))=\epsilon_{2}(t,s)

\frac{\partial\epsilon_{2}(t,s)}{\partial s}=G_{ss}(t,s)+\omega\sin(\omega(t-s))=-(\omega^{2}+q(s))G(t,s)+\omega\sin(\omega(t-s))

Substituting
G(t,s)=\frac{\sin(\omega(t-s))}{\omega}+\epsilon_{1}(t,s)
into the second identity yields:

\frac{\partial\epsilon_{2}(t,s)}{\partial s}=-\frac{q(s)}{\omega}\sin(\omega(t-s))-(\omega^{2}+q(s))\epsilon_{1}(t,s)

Now, differentiating
H(t,s)
with respect to
s
provides:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H(t,s)}{\partial s}

\displaystyle=-\frac{p^{\prime}(s)}{2\omega}\sin(\omega(t-s))+\frac{p(s)}{2}\cos(\omega(t-s))-\frac{p^{\prime}(s)}{2}\epsilon_{1}(t,s)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\omega^{2}-\frac{p(s)}{2}\right)\frac{\partial\epsilon_{1}(t,s)}{\partial s}-\frac{\partial\epsilon_{2}(t,s)}{\partial s}

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{p^{\prime}(s)}{2\omega}+\frac{q(s)}{\omega}\right)\sin(\omega(t-s))+\frac{p(s)}{2}\cos(\omega(t-s))

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\omega^{2}+q(s)-\frac{p^{\prime}(s)}{2}\right)\epsilon_{1}(t,s)+\left(\omega^{2}-\frac{p(s)}{2}\right)\epsilon_{2}(t,s)

Finally, we substitute these evaluated components back into the bracketed expression for
P_{4,Q}(t,s)/E(t,s)
and group by terms.

For the
\cos(\omega(t-s))
term:

-q(s)-\frac{p(s)}{2}=a_{1}(s)

For the
\sin(\omega(t-s))
term:

\frac{q(s)}{\omega}-\frac{\omega p(s)}{2}+\frac{p^{2}(s)}{4\omega}-\left(-\frac{p^{\prime}(s)}{2\omega}+\frac{q(s)}{\omega}\right)=-\frac{\omega p(s)}{2}+\frac{p^{2}(s)+2p^{\prime}(s)}{4\omega}

Recalling the definition
q(s)=-\frac{p^{\prime}(s)}{2}-\frac{p^{2}(s)}{4}
, we recognize that
\frac{p^{2}(s)+2p^{\prime}(s)}{4\omega}=-\frac{q(s)}{\omega}
. Therefore, the coefficient simplifies to:

-\frac{\omega p(s)}{2}-\frac{q(s)}{\omega}=\frac{1}{\omega}\left(-\frac{\omega^{2}p(s)}{2}-q(s)\right)=\frac{a_{2}(s)}{\omega}

For the
\epsilon_{1}(t,s)
term:

\left(\omega^{2}-\frac{p(s)}{2}\right)^{2}-\left(\omega^{2}+q(s)-\frac{p^{\prime}(s)}{2}\right)=b_{2}(s)

For the
\epsilon_{2}(t,s)
term:

-\left(\omega^{2}-\frac{p(s)}{2}\right)-\left(\omega^{2}-\frac{p(s)}{2}\right)=-2\omega^{2}+p(s)=b_{1}(s)

Multiplying the entire expression by
A(t)
and using
A(t)E(t,s)=A(s)
yields the final stated formula.
∎

Lemma 28

(Asymptotic Behavior of
I(t)
)
.

Let
I^{*}=\int_{0}^{\infty}A(s)|y_{2}(s)|\,ds<\infty
. Then:

I(t)=\int_{0}^{t}P_{4,Q}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds=\frac{c_{1}\cos(\omega t)+c_{2}\sin(\omega t)}{A(t)}+o\left(\frac{1}{A(t)}\right)

Proof.

We use the representation from the previous lemma. To isolate
t
, we apply the addition formulas
\cos(\omega(t-s))=\cos(\omega t)\cos(\omega s)+\sin(\omega t)\sin(\omega s)
and
\sin(\omega(t-s))=\sin(\omega t)\cos(\omega s)-\cos(\omega t)\sin(\omega s)
.
Furthermore, we apply the expansion of
E_{\text{rem}}(t,s)
from Lemma 1:

E_{\text{rem}}(t,s)=\cos(\omega t)E_{c}(s)+\sin(\omega t)E_{s}(s)+\tilde{E}_{\text{rem}}(t,s)

where
E_{c}(s)=b_{1}(s)\epsilon_{2,c}(s)+b_{2}(s)\epsilon_{1,c}(s)
,
E_{s}(s)=b_{1}(s)\epsilon_{2,s}(s)+b_{2}(s)\epsilon_{1,s}(s)
, and the purely tail-dependent remainder is
\tilde{E}_{\text{rem}}(t,s)=b_{1}(s)\tilde{\epsilon}_{2}(t,s)+b_{2}(s)\tilde{\epsilon}_{1}(t,s)
. Because
b_{1},b_{2}
are bounded,
|\tilde{E}_{\text{rem}}(t,s)|\leq C\int_{t}^{\infty}Q(u)\,du
for some constant
C
.

Multiplying by
A(t)
, we can organize
A(t)I(t)
entirely into components of
\cos(\omega t)
and
\sin(\omega t)
:

A(t)I(t)=\cos(\omega t)C(t)+\sin(\omega t)S(t)+\tilde{R}_{\text{int}}(t)

where we define:

C(t)=\int_{0}^{t}A(s)y_{2}(s)\left[a_{1}(s)\cos(\omega s)-\frac{a_{2}(s)}{\omega}\sin(\omega s)+E_{c}(s)\right]ds

S(t)=\int_{0}^{t}A(s)y_{2}(s)\left[a_{1}(s)\sin(\omega s)+\frac{a_{2}(s)}{\omega}\cos(\omega s)+E_{s}(s)\right]ds

\tilde{R}_{\text{int}}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}A(s)\tilde{E}_{\text{rem}}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds

Because the bracketed terms in
C(t)
and
S(t)
are globally bounded, and
A(s)|y_{2}(s)|\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
(since
I^{*}<\infty
), the integrals
C(t)
and
S(t)
converge absolutely to limits
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
as
t\to\infty
. The remaining tails
\int_{t}^{\infty}
are bounded by a constant times
\int_{t}^{\infty}A(s)|y_{2}(s)|\,ds
, which is
o(1)
.

We must rigorously show that
\tilde{R}_{\text{int}}(t)=o(1)
. Using our tight bound on
\tilde{E}_{\text{rem}}(t,s)
:

|\tilde{R}_{\text{int}}(t)|\leq\int_{0}^{t}A(s)|y_{2}(s)||\tilde{E}_{\text{rem}}(t,s)|\,ds\leq\left(C\int_{t}^{\infty}Q(u)\,du\right)\int_{0}^{t}A(s)|y_{2}(s)|\,ds\leq CI^{*}\int_{t}^{\infty}Q(u)\,du

Since
Q\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
,
\int_{t}^{\infty}Q(u)\,du\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. Thus,
\tilde{R}_{\text{int}}(t)=o(1)
. Dividing
A(t)I(t)
by
A(t)
concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 29

(Asymptotic Behavior of
J(t)
)
.

Let
P_{4,B}(t,s)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\left(\frac{1}{2}p(s)E(t,s)F(t,s)\right)
. Then
J(t)=\int_{0}^{t}P_{4,B}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds
satisfies:

J(t)=\frac{d_{1}\cos(\omega t)+d_{2}\sin(\omega t)}{A(t)}+o\left(\frac{1}{A(t)}\right)

Proof.

Factoring out
A(t)
from
E(t,s)
, we have
P_{4,B}(t,s)=-\frac{1}{A(t)}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\left(\frac{1}{2}p(s)A(s)F(t,s)\right)
. Because
\frac{1}{2}p(s)A(s)=A^{\prime}(s)
and
A^{\prime\prime}(s)=-q(s)A(s)
, expanding the derivative gives:

-\frac{\partial}{\partial s}(A^{\prime}(s)F(t,s))=-A^{\prime\prime}(s)F(t,s)-A^{\prime}(s)F_{s}(t,s)=A(s)\left[q(s)F(t,s)-\frac{1}{2}p(s)L_{1}(t,s)\right]

where
F_{s}(t,s)=L_{1}(t,s)=\cos(\omega(t-s))+\omega\sin(\omega(t-s))
and
F(t,s)=\cos(\omega(t-s))-\frac{1}{\omega}\sin(\omega(t-s))
. Grouping terms:

q(s)F(t,s)-\frac{1}{2}p(s)L_{1}(t,s)=\left(q(s)-\frac{p(s)}{2}\right)\cos(\omega(t-s))-\left(\frac{q(s)}{\omega}+\frac{\omega p(s)}{2}\right)\sin(\omega(t-s))

We now apply the trigonometric addition formulas to separate
t
and
s
, yielding:

\displaystyle q(s)F(t,s)-\frac{1}{2}p(s)L_{1}(t,s)

\displaystyle=\cos(\omega t)\underbrace{\left[\left(q(s)-\frac{p(s)}{2}\right)\cos(\omega s)+\left(\frac{q(s)}{\omega}+\frac{\omega p(s)}{2}\right)\sin(\omega s)\right]}_{:=D_{c}(s)}

\displaystyle\quad+\sin(\omega t)\underbrace{\left[\left(q(s)-\frac{p(s)}{2}\right)\sin(\omega s)-\left(\frac{q(s)}{\omega}+\frac{\omega p(s)}{2}\right)\cos(\omega s)\right]}_{:=D_{s}(s)}

Therefore,
A(t)J(t)=\cos(\omega t)\int_{0}^{t}A(s)D_{c}(s)y_{2}(s)\,ds+\sin(\omega t)\int_{0}^{t}A(s)D_{s}(s)y_{2}(s)\,ds
.

Since
p(s)\to 0
and
q(s)\to 0
, the functions
D_{c}(s)
and
D_{s}(s)
are globally bounded by some constant
M_{D}
. Because
I^{*}<\infty
, the integrals converge absolutely:

d_{1}=\int_{0}^{\infty}A(s)D_{c}(s)y_{2}(s)\,ds,\quad d_{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}A(s)D_{s}(s)y_{2}(s)\,ds

The difference between the integral over
[0,t]
and the limit is the tail
\int_{t}^{\infty}
, which satisfies:

\left|\int_{t}^{\infty}A(s)D_{c}(s)y_{2}(s)\,ds\right|\leq M_{D}\int_{t}^{\infty}A(s)|y_{2}(s)|\,ds\to 0\quad\text{as }t\to\infty

Thus, the tails are explicitly
o(1)
. Dividing
A(t)J(t)=d_{1}\cos(\omega t)+d_{2}\sin(\omega t)+o(1)
by
A(t)
completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 30

(Final Asymptotic Form of
P_{4}(t,s)
Integration)
.

\int_{0}^{t}P_{4}(t,s)y_{2}(s)\,ds=\frac{e_{1}\cos(\omega t)+e_{2}\sin(\omega t)}{A(t)}+o\left(\frac{1}{A(t)}\right)

and

.
∎

Converse Convergence Result

We are given the Volterra integral equation:

y_{2}(t)=x(t)+\int_{0}^{t}K_{3}(t,s)x(s)\,ds

where the kernel is defined by
K_{3}(t,s)=h^{\prime\prime}(t-s)+\omega^{2}h(t-s)+p(s)h^{\prime}(t-s)-p^{\prime}(s)h(t-s)
, with
h(t)=te^{-\omega^{2}t}
. We are given the asymptotic relations
y_{2}(t)=o\left(\frac{1}{A(t)}\right)
and
x(t)=\frac{c_{1}\cos(\omega t)+c_{2}\sin(\omega t)}{A(t)}+o\left(\frac{1}{A(t)}\right)
as
t\to\infty
. We wish to prove that the integral
I_{S}(T)=\int_{0}^{T}A(t)\sin(\omega t)y_{2}(t)\,dt
tends to a finite limit as
T\to\infty
(and similarly for the cosine integral).

Theorem 32
.

Under the given assumptions, the limits

\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}A(t)\sin(\omega t)y_{2}(t)\,dt

and

\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}A(t)\cos(\omega t)y_{2}(t)\,dt

exist and are finite.

Proof.

We detail the proof for the sine integral; the cosine case follows identically. Multiply the integral equation for
y_{2}(t)
by
A(t)\sin(\omega t)
and integrate from
0
to
T
:

I_{S}(T)=\int_{0}^{T}A(t)\sin(\omega t)x(t)\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}A(t)\sin(\omega t)\left(\int_{0}^{t}K_{3}(t,s)x(s)\,ds\right)dt

Since all functions are continuous on the compact domain
[0,T]
, we apply Fubini’s theorem to swap the order of integration in the second term:

I_{S}(T)=\int_{0}^{T}A(s)x(s)\sin(\omega s)\,ds+\int_{0}^{T}x(s)\left(\int_{s}^{T}A(t)\sin(\omega t)K_{3}(t,s)\,dt\right)ds

Let
J(s,T)=\int_{s}^{T}A(t)\sin(\omega t)K_{3}(t,s)\,dt
. We substitute the definition of
K_{3}(t,s)
and separate it into two parts:
H(t-s)=h^{\prime\prime}(t-s)+\omega^{2}h(t-s)
and the terms involving
p(s),p^{\prime}(s)
.

J(s,T)=\int_{s}^{T}A(t)\sin(\omega t)H(t-s)\,dt+\int_{s}^{T}A(t)\sin(\omega t)[p(s)h^{\prime}(t-s)-p^{\prime}(s)h(t-s)]\,dt

We integrate the first term by parts twice with respect to
t
. Using
h(0)=0
and
h^{\prime}(0)=1
, the first integration yields:

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{T}A(t)\sin(\omega t)h^{\prime\prime}(t-s)\,dt

\displaystyle=\left[A(t)\sin(\omega t)h^{\prime}(t-s)\right]_{s}^{T}-\int_{s}^{T}\frac{d}{dt}(A(t)\sin(\omega t))h^{\prime}(t-s)\,dt

\displaystyle=A(T)\sin(\omega T)h^{\prime}(T-s)-A(s)\sin(\omega s)-\int_{s}^{T}\Phi^{\prime}(t)h^{\prime}(t-s)\,dt

where we define
\Phi(t)=A(t)\sin(\omega t)
. Integrating the remaining integral by parts once more gives:

-\int_{s}^{T}\Phi^{\prime}(t)h^{\prime}(t-s)\,dt=-\Phi^{\prime}(T)h(T-s)+\Phi^{\prime}(s)h(0)+\int_{s}^{T}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(t)h(t-s)\,dt

Thus, the integral over
H(t-s)
becomes:

-A(s)\sin(\omega s)+A(T)\sin(\omega T)h^{\prime}(T-s)-\Phi^{\prime}(T)h(T-s)+\int_{s}^{T}[\Phi^{\prime\prime}(t)+\omega^{2}\Phi(t)]h(t-s)\,dt

Substituting
J(s,T)
back into
I_{S}(T)
, we observe a crucial exact cancellation. The leading boundary term
-A(s)\sin(\omega s)
exactly cancels the
\int_{0}^{T}A(s)x(s)\sin(\omega s)\,ds
term outside the double integral. This leaves:

I_{S}(T)=O(T)+\int_{0}^{T}x(s)L(s,T)\,ds

where
O(T)
collects the boundary evaluations at
T
, and
L(s,T)
collects the remaining integrals over
t
:

O(T)=\int_{0}^{T}x(s)\left[A(T)\sin(\omega T)h^{\prime}(T-s)-\Phi^{\prime}(T)h(T-s)\right]ds

L(s,T)=\int_{s}^{T}[\Phi^{\prime\prime}(t)+\omega^{2}\Phi(t)]h(t-s)\,dt+\int_{s}^{T}A(t)\sin(\omega t)[p(s)h^{\prime}(t-s)-p^{\prime}(s)h(t-s)]\,dt

Step 1: Asymptotic Behavior of the Oscillating Boundary Term
O(T)

We must show that
O(T)
tends to a finite constant as
T\to\infty
. Apply the change of variables
u=T-s
to
O(T)
. We use the relations
x(T-u)A(T-u)=X(T-u)
and
\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{A(T)}{A(T-u)}=1
. Since
\Phi^{\prime}(T)=\omega A(T)\cos(\omega T)+o(A(T))
, applying the Dominated Convergence Theorem as
T\to\infty
yields:

O(T)=\int_{0}^{\infty}X(T-u)[\sin(\omega T)h^{\prime}(u)-\omega\cos(\omega T)h(u)]\,du+o(1)

Substitute
X(T-u)=c_{1}\cos(\omega(T-u))+c_{2}\sin(\omega(T-u))
. By expanding using trigonometric addition formulas, we can group
X(T-u)
into components of
T
and
u
:

X(T-u)=\cos(\omega T)C(u)+\sin(\omega T)S(u)

where
C(u)=c_{1}\cos(\omega u)-c_{2}\sin(\omega u)
and
S(u)=c_{1}\sin(\omega u)+c_{2}\cos(\omega u)
. Note the strict derivative relationships:
C^{\prime}(u)=-\omega S(u)
and
S^{\prime}(u)=\omega C(u)
. Expanding the integrand of
O(T)
produces three trigonometric coefficients relative to
T
:

\displaystyle O(T)

\displaystyle\approx\sin(\omega T)\cos(\omega T)\int_{0}^{\infty}[C(u)h^{\prime}(u)-\omega S(u)h(u)]\,du

\displaystyle\quad+\sin^{2}(\omega T)\int_{0}^{\infty}S(u)h^{\prime}(u)\,du-\cos^{2}(\omega T)\int_{0}^{\infty}\omega C(u)h(u)\,du

For the cross term, observe that
C(u)h^{\prime}(u)-\omega S(u)h(u)=C(u)h^{\prime}(u)+C^{\prime}(u)h(u)=\frac{d}{du}[C(u)h(u)]
. Its integral is
[C(u)h(u)]_{0}^{\infty}=0
because
h(0)=h(\infty)=0
.
For the squared terms, integrating
S(u)h^{\prime}(u)
by parts yields:

\int_{0}^{\infty}S(u)h^{\prime}(u)\,du=[S(u)h(u)]_{0}^{\infty}-\int_{0}^{\infty}S^{\prime}(u)h(u)\,du=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\omega C(u)h(u)\,du

Let this shared integral value be denoted by
K
. Then the squared terms elegantly collapse:

\sin^{2}(\omega T)(K)+\cos^{2}(\omega T)(K)=K

Thus, the oscillations perfectly annihilate one another, yielding
\lim_{T\to\infty}O(T)=K<\infty
.

Step 2: Convergence of the Integral Remainder

We must now guarantee that
\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}x(s)L(s,T)\,ds
converges. Expanding

\Phi^{\prime\prime}(t)+\omega^{2}\Phi(t)=A^{\prime\prime}(t)\sin(\omega t)+2\omega A^{\prime}(t)\cos(\omega t),

we replace
A^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{2}p(t)A(t)
and
A^{\prime\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{2}p^{\prime}(t)A(t)+\frac{1}{4}p^{2}(t)A(t)
.
The terms composing
L(s,T)
contain functions of
p,p^{\prime}
and
p^{2}
. We first establish that
p^{\prime}\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
. We are given
q(s)=-\frac{p^{\prime}(s)}{2}-\frac{p^{2}(s)}{4}\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
. Rearranging and integrating gives
\int_{0}^{t}\frac{p^{2}(s)}{4}\,ds=-\int_{0}^{t}q(s)\,ds-\frac{p(t)}{2}+\frac{p(0)}{2}
. Since
p(t)\to 0
and
q\in L^{1}
, the limit as
t\to\infty
exists, proving
p^{2}\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
. Consequently,
p^{\prime}(s)=-2q(s)-\frac{p^{2}(s)}{2}
is also strictly in
L^{1}(0,\infty)
.

Because
p^{\prime},p^{2}\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
, any terms in
L(s,T)
bounded by
A(t)(|p^{\prime}(t)|+p^{2}(t))
are absolutely integrable against
x(s)\sim X(s)/A(s)
. The singular threat to convergence is the term containing
p(s)
, as
p\notin L^{1}(0,\infty)
.
For any integral of the form
\int_{0}^{\infty}x(s)p(s)M(s)\,ds
where
M(s)
is bounded, we use the identity:

x(s)p(s)=\frac{X(s)}{A(s)}p(s)=-2X(s)\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{1}{A(s)}\right)

We integrate by parts:

\int_{0}^{T}-2X(s)M(s)\frac{d}{ds}(A^{-1}(s))\,ds=\left[-2X(s)M(s)A^{-1}(s)\right]_{0}^{T}+\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{A(s)}\frac{d}{ds}(2X(s)M(s))\,ds

Since
A(T)\to\infty
, the boundary term tends to 0. Because
X(s)
is a bounded trigonometric sum and
M(s)
(composed of convolutions with
h
) has a bounded derivative, the integrand of the remaining term is
O(1/A(s))
. Because
A(s)
grows monotonically, this integral converges by Dirichlet’s test.

Therefore, every component of the integral remainder converges as
T\to\infty
. Combined with
\lim_{T\to\infty}O(T)=K
, we conclude that
I_{S}(T)
tends to a finite limit.
∎
