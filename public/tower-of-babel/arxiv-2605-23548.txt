The number of Pfaffian orientations on punctured polygonally cellulated surfaces
Sajal Mukherjee, Pritam Chandra Pramanik, Arundhati Rakshit

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce the notion of Pfaffian orientations on (punctured) polygonally cellulated orientable surfaces, and provide an expression for the number of such orientations. This generalizes the notion of Pfaffian orientations on planar graph, where a planar graph is seen as a punctured 2 2 -sphere, embedded in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} . So as a direct corollary of our main theorem, we derive the number of Pfaffian orientations on a planar graph.



Keywords:
Pfaffian orientations, orientable cellulated surfaces, planar graphs, incidence matrix.

MSC 2020:
05C10, 52B05, 05A15.

1
Introduction

The problem of enumerating perfect matchings in planar graphs has originated in the realms of statistical mechanics and chemistry, more particularly, in the problems of dimer models. The notion of
Pfaffian orientations
plays a central role towards this end, in counting the number of perfect matchings in planar graphs. Kastelyn has proved the existence of Pfaffian orientations in any bipartite planar graph (see
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
). However, there has been no known result on the number of such Pfaffian orientations, as of now, to the best of our knowledge.

A
Pfaffian orientation on a planar graph
(see
[
1
]
) is defined as a choice of orientation on the edges of the planar graph such that each face of the graph has odd number of edges oriented in the clockwise direction with respect to that face.
Figure 1
demonstrates a Pfaffian orientation on a planar graph.
Figure 1
demonstrates a Pfaffian orientation on a planar graph.

Figure 1:
A Pfaffian orientation on a planar graph. The arrows represent the orientations on the edges.

In this paper, we introduce a more general notion of Pfaffian orientations on “punctured” polygonally cellulated surfaces, embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, whose
1
-skeletons are not necessarily planar, and provide a count of all such Pfaffian orientations on a given “punctured” polygonally cellulated orientable surface. Hence, we determine the count of Pfaffian orientations on a “punctured” polygonally cellulated
2
-sphere.

For any polygonally cellulated surface, the
2
-cells are termed as
faces
, the
1
-cells are termed as
edges
while the
0
-cells are termed as
vertices
. From here onwards, we will refer to polygonally cellulated surfaces as simply cellulated surfaces for our convenience.

Let
K
be an orientable, connected, closed, cellulated surface of genus
g
, embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, where the orientation of each face is determined by the right hand rule along the outward normal vector of the face.
Figure 2
illustrates the orientation of one such face.

Figure 2:
A face of an orientable cellulated surface is oriented following the right hand rule along the outward normal vector of the face. Here the upward arrow denotes the outward normal vector of the face and the curved arrow depicts the orientation of the face.

Now, we remove a face from the cellulated surface
K
. We call this a
“punctured”
cellulated surface and denote it as
K^{\prime}
. Next we define a Pfaffian orientation on
K^{\prime}
.

Definition 0.1
.

Let
K^{\prime}
be a punctured, orientable, connected, cellulated surface where each edge is assigned an orientation (or a direction), represented by an arrow on the edge. Such an assignment of orientation on the edges of
K^{\prime}
is said to be a
Pfaffian orientation
on
K^{\prime}
if for every face
f
of
K^{\prime}
, the number of edges
e
of
f
, whose orientations are opposite to the induced orientation of
f
on
e
, is odd.

A Pfaffian orientation on a planar graph can be equivalently seen as a Pfaffian orienation on a “punctured”
2
-sphere, embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

In Section
3
, we count the number of Pfaffian orientations on
K^{\prime}
and arrive at the following result.
For any cellulated surface
K
, we denote the set of vertices of
K
as
\mathcal{V}(K)
, the set of edges of
K
as
\mathcal{E}(K)
and the set of faces of
K
as
\mathcal{F}(K)
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
K
be an orientable, closed, connected, cellulated surface of genus
g
, such that
|\mathcal{V}(K)|=v
and
K^{\prime}
be the punctured, cellulated surface obtained by removing a face from
K
. Then the number of Pfaffian orientations on
K^{\prime}
, denoted by
\mu
, is given by,

\mu=2^{v-1+2g}.

As a corollary, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 1
.

The number of Pfaffian orientations on a punctured cellulated
2
-sphere embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, is given by,

\mu=2^{v-1},

where
v
is the number of vertices of the cellulated
2
-sphere.

We note here that the number of Pfaffian orientations on a punctured, orientable, cellulated surface, does not depend on the face being removed from the surface. Towards the end of Section
3
, we prove the existence of a Pfaffian orientation on a punctured, orientable, cellulated surface by providing an explicit construction.

2
Preliminaries

Let
K
be an orientable, connected, closed, cellulated surface. If an edge
e
is contained in a face
f
, we denote it as
e\subseteq f
. The following two simple properties of such a surface will prove to be useful, subsequently.

(a)

Each edge of
K
is contained in exactly two faces of
K
.

(b)

For any two faces
f_{0}
and
f_{n}
of
K
, there exists a sequence of faces and edges of the following form.

f_{0},e_{0},f_{1},\dots,e_{n-1},f_{n},

where
f_{i}\in\mathcal{F}(K)
,
e_{i}\in\mathcal{E}(K)
, for each
i
and
f_{i}\supseteq e_{i}\subseteq f_{i+1}
for each
i\in\{0,\dots,(n-1)\}
.

For a punctured, cellulated surface, an edge which is shared by exactly two faces of the surface, is termed as an
internal edge
, while an edge which is contained in exactly one face of the surface, is termed as an
external edge
.

Definition 1.1
.

(Incidence Number)
Let
K
be an orientable, cellulated surface with a given orientation on its cells. Let
e\in\mathcal{E}(K)
,
f\in\mathcal{F}(K)
. Then the
incidence number
of
e
with respect to
f
is defined as,

\langle f,e\rangle=\begin{cases}0,\text{ if }e\subsetneq f,\\
1,\text{ if the orientation of $e$ is the induced orientation of $f$ on $e$},\\
-1,\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Definition 1.2
.

The
incidence matrix
of a cellulated surface
K
is defined as a matrix where the columns correspond to all the faces of
K
, ordered as
f_{1},f_{2},\dots
, the rows correspond to all the edges of
K
, ordered as
e_{1},e_{2},\dots
, and the
(i,j)^{th}
entry is given by the incidence number of the
i^{th}
edge with respect to the
j^{th}
face, i.e.,

a_{ij}=\langle f_{j},e_{i}\rangle.

We denote this matrix as
[\partial(K)]
.

Now let us consider a punctured orientable surface
K^{\prime}
and consider the incidence matrix of
K^{\prime}
,
[\partial(K^{\prime})]
. We denote the
i^{th}
column vector of
[\partial(K^{\prime})]
as
\bar{f}_{i}
and the
i^{th}
row vector of
[\partial(K^{\prime})]
as
\bar{e}_{i}
. Let
|\mathcal{F}(K)|=p
. The following result regarding the rank of
[\partial(K^{\prime})]
, will prove to be crucial subsequently.

Lemma 1
.

The columns of
[\partial(K^{\prime})]
are linearly independent, i.e., the rank of
[\partial(K^{\prime})]
is
(p-1)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}(K)=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{p-1},f_{p}\}
and
\mathcal{F}(K^{\prime})=\mathcal{F}(K)\setminus\{f_{p}\}
. Let
\bar{f}_{1},\bar{f}_{2},\dots,\bar{f}_{p-1}
be the respective column vectors of
[\partial(K^{\prime})]
.

Let,

\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}c_{i}\bar{f}_{i}=0,

(i)

where
c_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
for each
i\in\{i,\dots,(p-1)\}
. Without loss of generality, let
c_{1}\neq 0
. As we noted earlier, there exists a sequence of faces and edges of
K
,

f_{1},e_{i_{1}},f_{i_{1}},e_{i_{2}},\dots,f_{i_{t-1}},e_{i_{t}},f_{p}

from
f_{1}
to
f_{p}
, where
f_{i_{j}}\in\mathcal{F}(K)
and
e_{i_{j}}\in\mathcal{E}(K)
for each
j\in[t]
. Without loss of generality we can assume that they are distinct. Since each
e_{i_{j}}
is shared by exactly two faces of
K
, which are precisely the cells preceding and following
e_{i_{j}}
in the sequence, therefore, in order to cancel the contribution of
e_{i_{1}}
in (i), we must have
c_{i_{1}}\neq 0
. Again, to cancel the contribution of
e_{i_{2}}
in (i), we must have
c_{i_{2}}\neq 0
. In this way we continue, till we have
c_{i_{t-1}}\neq 0
. As a result,
e_{i_{t}}
now has a non-trivial contribution in the sum, which cannot be cancelled,
e_{i_{t}}
being an external edge of
K^{\prime}
. This is a contradiction. Therefore
c_{1}=0
. In general,
c_{i}=0
for each
i\in[p-1]
. Thus the column vectors
\bar{f}_{1},\dots,\bar{f}_{p-1}
are linearly independent and hence the rank of
[\partial(K^{\prime})]
is
(p-1)
.
∎

It follows from the above result that the cardinality of any basis of the row space of
[\partial(K^{\prime})]
is
(p-1)
, which is same as
|\mathcal{F}(K^{\prime})|
. Let us consider a basis of the row space of
[\partial(K^{\prime})]
and denote the set of edges corresponding to this basis, as
R
. Now we construct a directed graph as follows.

Construction of a directed bipartite graph:
Let
G(K^{\prime})=(V,E)
be a directed bipartite graph, where the vertex set
V
is partitioned as
V=R\sqcup\mathcal{F}(K^{\prime})
and the edge set
E:=\{(f,e)\mid f\in\mathcal{F}(K^{\prime}),e\in R,e\subseteq f\}
. We will refer to
G(K^{\prime})
as simply
G
, when the surface is clear from the context. As we observed previously,
|R|=|\mathcal{F}(K^{\prime})|
, which makes
G
a balanced bipartite directed graph. Let
\mathcal{M}=\{M\mid M\text{ is a perfect matching in }G\}
. We will prove at the end of Section
3
that
\mathcal{M}\neq\emptyset
. Next, let
M\in\mathcal{M}
. For each directed edge
(f,e)\in M
, we reverse the direction of this edge in
G
. We denote this modified bipartite directed graph as
G_{M}
. We call a perfect matching
M
in
G

acyclic
if
G_{M}
is acyclic. Otherwise,
M
is called
cyclic
.

2.1
Some definitions and results of Graph theory

Let
G=(V,E)
be a directed graph, where
V
and
E
denote the set of vertices and the set of directed edges of
G
respectively. We also denote the set of vertices of
G
as
V(G)
and the set of directed edges of
G
as
E(G)
. Throughout the paper, we will refer to directed edges of a directed graph as simply
edges
and directed cycles as simply
cycles
. For any
u\in V
, let
N_{u}^{-}=\{v\in V\mid(u,v)\in E\}
and
N_{u}^{+}=\{v\in V\mid(v,u)\in E\}
. Then, the
out-degree
of
u
in
G
is defined as
|N_{u}^{-}|
and denoted as
\operatorname{outdeg}_{G}(u)
. The
in-degree
of
u
in
G
is defined as
|N_{u}^{+}|
and denoted as
\operatorname{indeg}_{G}(u)
. The elements of
N_{u}^{-}
are said to be the
out-neighbours
of
u
while the elements of
N_{u}^{+}
are said to be the
in-neighbours
of
u
. If
G
has no directed cycles, then we call
G
a
directed acyclic graph
. The following result is of immense importance, which we will use in the proof of
2
in Section
3
.

Proposition 1.1
.

A directed acyclic graph has atleast one vertex of out-degree
0
.

We refer to
[
5
]
, for more information on the basics of Graph Theory.

3
Enumerating Pfaffian orientations on a punctured cellulated surface

Let
K^{\prime}
be a punctured cellulated orientable surface. In this section, we construct a system of equations over the field
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, for such a cellulated surface, the solutions of which correspond to precisely the Pfaffian orientations on
K^{\prime}
. Next, we determine the dimension of the solution space, which gives us the number of solutions of this system.

3.1
Construction of a system of equations

Let
f
be a face of
K^{\prime}
. Then for each edge
e
of
f
, we set a variable
x(f,e)
which takes values as follows.

x(f,e)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\langle f,e\rangle=-1,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

Let
K^{\prime}
be equipped with an orientation on its edges. If this orientation is Pfaffian, then we observe that the variables with respect to
K^{\prime}
as mentioned before, should satisfy the following equations.
For
f\in\mathcal{F}(K^{\prime})
with edges
e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{k}
, we have,

x(f,e_{1})+x(f,e_{2})+\dots+x(f,e_{k})=1

(1)

For each internal edge
e\in\mathcal{E}(K^{\prime})
such that
e
is shared by the faces
f
and
g
, we have,

x(f,e)+x(g,e)=1

(2)

We note here that this system of equations are taken over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

We call
1
the set of
face equations
and
2
the set of
edge equations
. The face equations impose the condition of parity of the number of good cells with respect to each face of
K^{\prime}
, while the edge equations ensure that the orientation on the edges of
K^{\prime}
are coherent, which is necessary for orientability of the surface. Hence, we further observe that each solution of the system of equations formed by
1
and
2
corresponds to a Pfaffian orientation on
K^{\prime}
.

Now, the system of equation can be written as
AX=\mathbb{1}
where
A
is the coefficient matrix,
X
is the variable matrix. Let the dimension of
A
be
r\times s
. Then
\mathbb{1}
denotes a
r\times 1
matrix consisting of all
1
-s. We call
A
the
Pfaffian matrix
of
K^{\prime}
. We demonstrate this matrix with the help of an illustrative example.

Lemma 2

Let

K^{\prime}

3.2
Enumerating Pfaffian orientations on a punctured, cellulated, orientable surface

Let
A
be the Pfaffian matrix of
K^{\prime}
. In this subsection, we first show that
A
can be reduced to an equivalent matrix
A^{\prime}
. Next, we find out
\operatorname{nullity}(A^{\prime})
and hence, determine the number of solutions of the system
AX=\mathbb{1}
.
The following theorem states how the Pfaffian matrix of a punctured cellulated surface can be reduced to another equivalent matrix. For any matrix
B
, we denote the transpose of
B
as
B^{T}
.

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
K^{\prime}
be a punctured orientable cellulated surface with
|\mathcal{F}(K^{\prime})|=(p-1)
and
|\mathcal{E}(K^{\prime})|=d
. Let
A
be the Pfaffian matrix of
K^{\prime}
of order
r\times s
and
AX=\mathbb{1}
be the corresponding system of equations. Then
A
can be reduced to another equivalent matrix
A^{\prime}
where,

A^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}[\partial(K^{\prime})]^{T}&O\\
O&I_{s-d}\\
\end{bmatrix},

and
I_{s-d}
is the identity matrix of order
(s-d)
, by a sequence of column and row operations.

First, we order the faces of
K^{\prime}

f_{1},\dots,f_{p-1}

K^{\prime}

e_{j_{1}},\dots,e_{j_{l}}

(p-1)

A

Lemma 3
.

Let

Suppose the dimension of

\end{bmatrix}

Let,

\end{bmatrix}

Next, we prove the existence of a Pfaffian orientation on a punctured, cellulated, orientable surface and hence determine the number of Pfaffian orientations on such a surface using
3
.

We mention here, that Kastelyn has proved the existence of Pfaffian orientations in planar graphs. However, his proof will not work for Pfaffian orientations on punctured cellulated surfaces in general, since he used planarity to prove his result. We will use the notion of the directed bipartite graph and the associated acyclic perfect matchings on it, as described in Section
2
, to provide an explicit construction of a Pfaffian orientation on any punctured, orientable, cellulated surface. As before, let
K^{\prime}
be a punctured, orientable, cellulated surface, with
|\mathcal{F}(K^{\prime})|=(p-1)
.

First we recall our construction from Section
2
. We consider a basis of the row space of
[\partial(K^{\prime})]
and name the set of edges corresponding to this basis as
R
. We construct a directed balanced bipartite graph
G=(V,E)
, where
V=R\sqcup\mathcal{F}(K^{\prime})
,
E:=\{(f,e)\mid f\in\mathcal{F}(K^{\prime}),e\in R,e\subseteq f\}
and both
R
and
\mathcal{F}
are given an ordering on their vertices. The set of all possible perfect matchings on
G
is denoted as
\mathcal{M}
. We further recall that each
M\in\mathcal{M}
gives rise to a modified directed bipartite graph
G_{M}
. If
G_{M}
is acyclic, we call
M
an acyclic matching. Otherwise,
M
is called cyclic.

Now, for each
M\in\mathcal{M}
in
G
, we associate a permutation
\pi_{M}
as follows. If
(f_{j},e_{i})\in M
, where
f_{j}
is the
j^{th}
vertex in
\mathcal{F}(K^{\prime})
and
e_{i}
is the
i^{th}
vertex in
R
, then
\pi_{M}(i)=j
. We define a sign on
M
as
\Lambda(M)=\operatorname{sgn}(\pi_{M})\prod_{(f,e)\in M}\langle f,e\rangle
. The following lemma provides a crucial information on the sum of the signs of the perfect matchings in
G
, which establishes the existence of a perfect matching in
G
.

Lemma 4
.

\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\Lambda(M)\neq 0
.

Proof.

Let us consider
[\partial(K^{\prime})]
. Suppose,
R
is the set of edges corresponding to a basis of the row space of
[\partial(K^{\prime})]
. Let us denote the submatrix formed by all the columns of
[\partial(K^{\prime})]
and the rows of this basis as
\partial_{R}
. Without loss of generality, let us assume that the rows and columns of
\partial_{R}
are arranged in the order of the vertices of
R
and
\mathcal{F}(K^{\prime})
in
G(K^{\prime})
respectively. Therefore from
1
, we deduce that
\det(\partial_{R}(K^{\prime}))\neq 0
. We observe here that
\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\Lambda(M)=\det(\partial_{R}(K^{\prime}))
. Indeed, from the definition of determinant, we have,

\displaystyle\det(\partial_{R})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{|R|}a_{i\sigma(i)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{|R|}\langle f_{\sigma(i)},e_{i}\rangle.

We observe that the summands in this expression survive only when
e_{i}\subseteq f_{\sigma(i)}
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,|R|\}
. Equivalently, a summand corresponding to some
\sigma\in S_{n}
in the above expression is nontrivial iff
\{(f_{\sigma(i)},e_{i})\mid i=1,\dots,|R|\}
forms a perfect matching in
G(K^{\prime})
. The permutation associated with this perfect matching is precisely
\sigma
. Thus the expression reduces to,

\displaystyle\det(\partial_{R})

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\operatorname{sgn}(\pi_{M})\prod_{(f,e)\in M}\langle f,e\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\Lambda(M).

Hence, the result follows.
∎

Next, we construct a sign-inversing involution on the set of cyclic matchings of
G
, thereby establishing the existence of an acyclic perfect matching in
G
. This acyclic matching will help us to provide a Pfaffian orientation on
K^{\prime}
.

From here on, an ordered pair
(e,f)
will denote a directed edge from
e\in R
to
f\in\mathcal{F}
and
(f,e)
will denote a directed edge from
f\in\mathcal{F}
to
e\in R
.

We mention here that the proofs of
5
and
2
have been adapted from similar results in
[
4
]
. Before delving into these results, we note a couple of observations which will be useful subsequently. These observations follow directly from the construction of
G_{M}
and the properties of cellulated surfaces, as stated at the beginning of Section
2
.

Observation 1
.

Let
M
be a perfect matching in
G
. Then,

(a)

For each
v\in R
,
\operatorname{outdeg}_{G_{M}}(v)=\operatorname{indeg}_{G_{M}}(v)=1
.

(b)

For each
v\in\mathcal{F}
,
\operatorname{indeg}_{G_{M}}(v)=1
.

Lemma 5
.

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\mathcal{M}\\
M\text{ is cyclic}\end{subarray}}\Lambda(M)=0.

Proof.

Let
M\in\mathcal{M}
such that
M
is cyclic. We will construct another cyclic matching
\widetilde{M}
corresponding to
M
and show that this correspondence is in fact, a sign-inversing involution.

Let
C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{k}
be the directed cycles in
G_{M}
. We claim that the cycles in
G_{M}
are vertex-disjoint, i.e.,
V(C_{i})\cap V(C_{j})=\emptyset
, for each
i,j\in[k],i\neq j
. This is because, if
V(C_{i})\cap V(C_{j})\neq\emptyset
for some
i,j\in[k],i\neq j
, then, there must exist a vertex
v
in
C_{i}
with in-degree
2
, which we note from
1
, is not possible. Now, the vertex-disjointness of the cycles in
G_{M}
leads us to the observation that for any cycle
C
in
G_{M}
, the set of vertices
V(C)\cap\mathcal{F}
is unique. So, for each cycle
C
in
G_{M}
, we arrange the vertices of
V(C)\cap\mathcal{F}
in the lexicographic order and assign this tuple of ordered vertices to
C
. Let us denote this tuple corresponding to a cycle
C
in
G_{M}
as
t_{C}(G_{M})
. Now, we consider
\{t_{C}\mid C\text{ is a cycle in }G_{M}\}
and arrange the elements of this set in lexicographic order. Let
C^{\prime}
be the cycle corresponding to the first tuple in this order.
Now, we simply reverse the direction of the edges of
C^{\prime}
, keeping all other edges unchanged, and rename the resultant graph as
G^{\prime}
. We construct
\widetilde{M}
as ,

\widetilde{M}=\{(f,e)\in E(G)\mid(e,f)\in E(G^{\prime})\}.

It can be verified using
1
that
\widetilde{M}
is indeed a matching. Since, every cycle in a bipartite graph is of even length, therefore, from the construction of
G^{\prime}
, we observe that
|M|=|\widetilde{M}|
, which makes
\widetilde{M}
perfect. Indeed,
G_{\widetilde{M}}=G^{\prime}
.
Again, from the vertex-disjointness of the cycles, we observe that the vertex set of each cycle in
G_{\widetilde{M}}
is preserved under this operation. Let the cycle in
G_{\widetilde{M}}
, which has been obtained by reversing
C^{\prime}
, be denoted as
C^{\prime\prime}
. As a result,
t_{C^{\prime}}(G_{M})=t_{C^{\prime\prime}}(G_{\widetilde{M}})
and hence
t_{C^{\prime\prime}}
comes first in the lexicographic ordering of the tuples of all the cycles in
G_{\widetilde{M}}
. Therefore, reversing the direction of the edges of
C^{\prime\prime}
in
G_{\widetilde{M}}
, returns us
G_{M}
, which corresponds to the original matching
M
. So, this correspondence is an involution.

Now, we show,
\Lambda(M)=-\Lambda(\widetilde{M})
. Let
S=\{(f,e)\in M\mid(e,f)\in E(C^{\prime})\}
and
T=\{(f,e)\in\widetilde{M}\mid(e,f)\in E(C^{\prime\prime})\}
. A few observations are worthy of mention here. First, we observe that
M\setminus S=\widetilde{M}\setminus T
, which follows from the construction of
\widetilde{M}
. Next, we observe here that for every
(f,e)\in S
, there exists
f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
,
f^{\prime}\neq f
, such that
(f^{\prime},e)\in T
(follows from the construction of
\widetilde{M}
) and
\langle f,e\rangle=-\langle f^{\prime},e\rangle
(follows from the orientability of the surface). Similarly, for every
(f,e)\in T
, there exists
f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
,
f^{\prime}\neq f
, such that
(f^{\prime},e)\in S
and
\langle f,e\rangle=-\langle f^{\prime},e\rangle
. Further, it can be noted that
\pi_{\widetilde{M}}=\rho_{|S|}\circ\pi_{M}
, where
\rho_{|S|}
is a permutation of length
|S|
. Thus,
\operatorname{sgn}(\pi_{\widetilde{M}})=(-1)^{|S|+1}\operatorname{sgn}(\pi_{M})
. So, we have,

\displaystyle\Lambda(M)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{sgn}(\pi_{M})\prod_{(f,e)\in M}\langle f,e\rangle

\displaystyle=\operatorname{sgn}(\pi_{M})\left(\prod_{(f,e)\in M\setminus S}\langle f,e\rangle\right)\left(\prod_{(f,e)\in S}\langle f,e\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{sgn}(\pi_{M})(-1)^{|S|}\left(\prod_{(f,e)\in M\setminus S}\langle f,e\rangle\right)\left(\prod_{(f,e)\in T}\langle f,e\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{sgn}(\pi_{\widetilde{M}})\left(\prod_{(f,e)\in\widetilde{M}\setminus T}\langle f,e\rangle\right)\left(\prod_{(f,e)\in T}\langle f,e\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{sgn}(\pi_{\widetilde{M}})\prod_{(f,e)\in\widetilde{M}}\langle f,e\rangle

\displaystyle=-\Lambda(\widetilde{M}).

Thus, the contribution of the sign of each cyclic perfect matching
M
in the sum, gets cancelled by the sign of its corresponding cyclic perfect matching
\widetilde{M}
. This proves the result.
∎

Corollary 2
.

For any punctured cellulated orientable surface
K^{\prime}
, there exists an acyclic perfect matching in
G(K^{\prime})
.

Proof.

This follows as a direct consequence of
4
and
5
.
∎

We introduce a term here which we will use in the next proof. Let
f\in\mathcal{F}(K^{\prime})
, where
K^{\prime}
is a punctured, orientable, cellulated surface, with an orientation assigned on each of its edges. Then, an edge
e
of
f
is said to be a “good” edge of
f
if the orientation of
e
is opposite to the induced orientation of
f
on
e
. Thus, an assignment of orientations on the edges of
K^{\prime}
is Pfaffian, iff every face of
K^{\prime}
has odd number of good edges.

Theorem 2
.

Any punctured cellulated orientable surface admits a Pfaffian orientation.

Proof.

Our line of argument is aligned parallelly with Kastelyn’s proof of existence of Pfaffian orientations for planar graphs.

Let
K^{\prime}
be a punctured, cellulated, orientable surface and
G(K^{\prime})
be the directed bipartite graph associated with
K^{\prime}
, as described previously. Therefore, from
2
, it follows that there exists an acyclic perfect matching
M
in
G
. So,
G_{M}
is acyclic. Thus, we recall from
1.1
that there exists a vertex
f_{1}
in
G_{M}
with out-degree
0
. From
1
, we infer that
f_{1}\in\mathcal{F}
and further, there exists a unique
e_{1}\in R
such that
(e_{1},f_{1})\in E(G_{M})
. Now, if
f_{1}
has odd number of good edges then we keep the orientation of
e_{1}
unaltered. Otherwise, we reverse the orientation of
e_{1}
so that
f_{1}
has odd number of good edges. We now delete the vertices
e_{1},f_{1}
from
G_{M}
and name the resultant graph
G_{M}^{\prime}
.
G_{M}^{\prime}
is also acyclic and hence has a vertex
f_{2}
of outdegree
0
. We note from
1
that the deletion of
e_{1},f_{1}
does not affect the out-degree of the vertices in
R
and the in-neighbours of the vertices in
\mathcal{F}
, in
G_{M}^{\prime}
. So, again,
f_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
and there exists a unique
e_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
(e_{2},f_{2})\in E(G_{M}^{\prime})
. Now, as before, if
f_{2}
has odd number of good edges, then we keep the orientation of
e_{2}
unaltered, otherwise we reverse the orientation of
e_{2}
so that
f_{2}
has odd number of edges. A crucial observation here is that the reorientation of
e_{2}
does not disturb the orientation of the edges in
f_{1}
since
\operatorname{outdeg}_{G_{M}}(f_{1})=0
, which makes it impossible for
e_{2}
to be an out-neighbour and hence, an edge of
f_{1}
. So, now both
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
has odd number of good edges. Now, we delete
e_{2},f_{2}
from
G_{M}^{\prime}
and move on to the next vertex of outdegree
0
in the resultant acyclic graph, which by the same argument, is a vertex of
\mathcal{F}
. We continue the same procedure till all the vertices of
G_{M}
have been deleted. At each step, we ensure that the faces of
K^{\prime}
have odd number of good edges by an appropriate reorientation of its unique in-neighbour in
G_{M}
. The resulting orientation on the edges of
K^{\prime}
is hence a Pfaffian orientation on
K^{\prime}
.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove our main theorem.

Proof of
1
.

It follows from
2
that the number of Pfaffian orientations of
K^{\prime}
is given by the number of solutions of the system of equations
AX=\mathbb{1}
, where
A
is the Pfaffian matrix of
K^{\prime}
. Let
|\mathcal{F}(K)|=p
,
|\mathcal{E}(K)|=d
. Then, from
1.2
and
3
, we deduce that
\operatorname{nullity}(A)=(d-p+1)
.We recall that the Euler characteristic of
K
is given by
\chi(K)=(v-d+p)=(2-2g)
, where,
|\mathcal{V}(K)|=v
. Therefore, we have,
(d-p+1)=(v-1+2g)
. Hence, from
2
, we conclude that the number of solutions of the system
AX=\mathbb{1}
is given by
2^{v-1+2g}
. Hence, the result follows.
∎

We obtain
1
as an immediate result of
1
.
