Abstract
This report describes an automated framework for predicting the creep performance of 316H
stainless steel manufactured using Laser Powder Bed Fusion (L-PBF), and the application of
that framework to a graded L-PBF specimen produced by intentionally varying the process-
ing conditions along its length. A physics-based Crystal Plasticity Finite Element model,
calibrated for L-PBF 316H at 725◦ C in both the solution annealed and as-built conditions, is
used to simulate creep in microstructures representing the distinct regions of the graded spec-
imen without any further tuning. We also describe an improvement to the neural network
surrogate used in the calibration campaign, which now captures the tertiary creep regime
that the previous surrogate could not. The simulated creep curves agree with experimen-
tal creep data for as-built L-PBF 316H under the same conditions and shows the utility of
the framework to extrapolate across build conditions. The graded specimen itself, however,
shows considerably lower creep than comparable as-built material, and the origin of this dif-
ference was investigated. Finally, we describe a vacancy transport model under development
towards an effort to reduce the amount of experimental data the framework requires, so that
grain boundary cavity growth can be driven by computed vacancy fluxes rather than by
parameters calibrated against creep curves. Together these efforts work toward predicting
the performance of a new L-PBF materials with less calibration.

1 Introduction
This report presents work undertaken to automate the process of modeling creep in 316H
stainless steel manufactured by laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) by simplifying the calibra-
tion process for the models used in this process and reducing the amount of experimental
data such predictions require. Additively manufactured components develop microstructures
that depend strongly on the processing conditions used to build them. These those mate-
rials produce creep behavior that differs from the wrought material and varies from build
to build [2, 3]. Qualifying these materials for use in future advanced nuclear reactors using
experiments alone would require a creep testing campaign for every meaningful change in
build parameters, which is not practical. A physics-based model that responds correctly to
microstructure offers a way out, provided it can be shown to extrapolate from one set of
build conditions to another.
 The demonstration we have chosen for this capability is the graded L-PBF 316H chal-
lenge problem, in which the laser power density was intentionally varied along the length
of a single creep specimen. Since everything except the microstructure is held constant
within that specimen, and because digital image correlation data is available for the strain
measurements in each of the three regions, the specimen provides three independent creep
measurements against which a single calibrated model can be tested. In our previous re-
ports [4, 5] we described the tools needed to set this problem up: crystal plasticity finite
element (CPFE) simulation of L-PBF microstructures, the techniques required to represent
their characteristic features such as elongated grain morphology, grain boundary porosity,
and spatially varying crystallographic orientation, and a surrogate-based workflow for cali-
brating the constitutive model against creep data. Chapter 2 of the present report brings
these together and reports the outcome of the demonstration. We recalibrate the model
against standard, single-microstructure creep specimens in both the solution annealed and
as-built conditions, describe an improved surrogate model that finally captures the tertiary
creep regime, and then apply the calibrated model to the three graded regions without any
further tuning. The results show that the model reproduces the reference creep tests and
shows the model’s utility in extrapolating across build conditions while being calibrated on
microstructure data from one region.
 The second thread of this year’s work addresses the cost of the calibration itself. Even
with the surrogate approach, calibrating the model requires thousands of expensive CPFE
simulations followed by surrogate training and optimization, which sharply limits how readily
the pipeline can be applied to a new material. The most direct way to reduce that cost
is to reduce the number of parameters that must be fit to test data in the first place.
Chapter 3 describes our effort to do this on the grain boundary side of the model, where
the Sham-Needleman cavitation model [6] currently contributes several fitted parameters.
We are developing a stress-assisted vacancy diffusion capability in our MOOSE-based CPFE
framework so that cavity growth can be driven by computed vacancy transport rather than
by calibrated growth and nucleation parameters. We present the model formulation so far
and its verification against a published perforated plate solution [1].
 Chapter 4 summarizes the findings and outlines the work planned for the coming year.
Throughout, this work draws on contributions from several teams across the Advanced Mate-
rials & Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT) program, helping improve our creep prediction
ability for AM materials.

2 Creep in graded L-PBF Microstructure
The bulk of this year’s work was focused on the graded L-PBF 316H challenge problem. In
this problem, the laser power density was intentionally varied during the build so that the
microstructure changes along the length of a single creep specimen. A specimen of this kind
is a compact test of our modeling framework: the composition, the heat treatment, the test
temperature, and the applied load are all common to the three regions, so any difference in
the measured creep response must originate in the microstructure produced by the different
processing conditions. Our goals for this year’s work were therefore to predict the effect
of microstructure on creep behavior in the graded specimen using a model calibrated only
against standard, single-microstructure test data, and thereby to demonstrate the utility of
physics-based models by predicting creep deformation and damage in a complex specimen.

2.1 The graded specimen

The specimen and the three regions considered in this work are presented in Fig. 2.1. Three
laser power densities were used: 52, 71, and 135 J/mm3 , labeled P7, P8, and P9 respectively,
producing three distinct grain structures and, more importantly, three distinct levels of
porosity. Pre-creep X-ray computed tomography (XCT) of the specimen shows the porosity
distribution directly, and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) inverse pole figure maps
taken from each region show the accompanying change in grain morphology and texture. The
simulated microstructures for each of the three regions were generated by our collaborators
at Oak Ridge National Laboratory1 . These simulated microstructures, shown in the right-
hand columns of Fig. 2.1, are the data from which we generate the finite element meshes used
in the crystal plasticity finite element (CPFE) simulations. The three meshes contain 1989,
437, and 717 grains for the P7, P8, and P9 regions, respectively, each meshed with 350–400K
hexahedral elements. The mesh generation and crystallographic orientation mapping process
follows the procedure described in our previous works [5, 7].
 The porosity measured in each region, and the corresponding grain boundary cavitation
model parameters derived from it, are reproduced in Table 2.1. The cavitation model we use
is based on the Sham-Needleman equations [6, 8] and operates on cohesive zone elements at
the grain boundaries, so the measured volume fraction of porosity must be projected onto
the grain boundary surfaces to obtain an initial cavity half radius a0 and half spacing b0 .
The procedure for doing this is described in [5]. Note that the porosity is not monotonic
in the power density: the intermediate P8 region, at 71 J/mm3 , is very nearly fully dense
at 0.009% porosity, while the highest power density P9 region contains 0.637% porosity by
volume. Porosity at this level has little effect on tensile strength [9] but, as our earlier work
showed [7], a strong effect on creep life.
 The creep experiment on this specimen was conducted at 725◦ C on as-built material.
The load was held at 100 MPa for the first 100 hours and then raised to 150 MPa to drive
the specimen to rupture. Digital image correlation (DIC) was used to measure strain over
the entire specimen gage as well as separately within each of the three regions, making this
a useful validation case. These experimental curves are presented in Fig. 2.22 .

2.2 Calibrating the CPFE model for L-PBF 316H

Before the graded microstructures can be simulated, the constitutive model must be cali-
brated for L-PBF 316H. Figure 2.3 presents a schematic of the workflow used here to calibrate
the CPFE model parameters using creep data. The workflow was described in detail in [5]
and is only summarized here. Directly optimizing material parameters against creep data
using the CPFE model itself is not tractable, because each forward evaluation is an expensive
high-performance-computing simulation. Instead, we run a large database of CPFE creep
simulations on a heavily defeatured L-PBF representative volume element, sampling a nine-
dimensional parameter space using Latin hypercube sampling. The sampled parameters are
the grain boundary parameters a0 , b0 , and DGB , the grain bulk parameters τs , τ0 , b, γ̇0 , and
n, and the applied load. A neural network surrogate is trained on the resulting creep curves,
and the surrogate (which evaluates in milliseconds) is what we actually use to optimize the
model parameters using experimental creep data. Including the load as an input means a
single surrogate can be used to calibrate across loading conditions.
 The model was calibrated using creep data generated within the AMMT program on
three different machines – the Renishaw AM400, the EOS M290, and the GE Concept
Laser M2. The creep experiments used for the calibration were conducted at 725◦ C under a
100 MPa load. For the solution annealed condition, the material was annealed at 1100◦ C for
one hour. The experimental curves are cropped to the range over which the surrogate was
trained, interpolated onto a common time grid, and reduced to a median creep curve, which is
then interpolated at intervals of 0.2% strain. The parameters are obtained by minimizing the
mean squared error between the surrogate prediction and this median curve, subject to the
bounds in Table 2.2. Figure 2.4 presents the calibrated surrogate against the experimental
data for both solution annealed and as-built cases, and the resulting parameters are given
in Tables 2.3 and 2.4.
 The graded specimen was subjected to creep in the as-built condition without any an-
nealing, so calibrated parameters under this condition were used for the CPFE simulations.

2.4 An improved surrogate that captures tertiary creep

The surrogate described in the previous section was trained on creep curves discretized at
twenty equally spaced strain values between 0 and 4%, with the training set filtered to those
CPFE simulations that reached at least 4% strain. That filter was chosen arbitrarily and
it has an unintended consequence. Many of the retained curves never enter tertiary creep
regime within the strain window, so the surrogate learns primary and secondary creep well
but has seen very little damage accumulation. The trained surrogate, thus, cannot represent
tertiary creep well, and neither can a CPFE model calibrated using it. The other part of
the problem was the training data. When running thousands of simulations to generate the
training data, in favor of expediency, we set a stopping criterion for the simulations where
they would stop once the creep rate increased to 10% above the minimum creep rate. This
stopped the simulations soon after tertiary creep began but did not allow the creep curves
to hold any significant amount of data in the tertiary creep regime.
 We therefore regenerated the training data but changed the stopping criterion to 10× the
minimum creep rate. This allowed us to produce creep curves with a good amount of tertiary
creep. Then we changed the discretization to be relative to the time to the onset of tertiary
creep, ttert , rather than to absolute strain. The new filter retains only those simulations
whose strain rate at the end of the simulation is at least five times the minimum strain rate
reached during the simulation. Applying this criterion to the expanded database of roughly
4800 simulations leaves approximately 1200 usable cases, split 90:10 between training and
test. Figure 2.8 shows the full database and the filtered subset.

3 Reducing model calibration: vacancy diffusion and creep cavitation
Chapter 2 demonstrated that the CPFE framework can be calibrated against standard creep
specimens and then applied to new microstructures. What that demonstration also made
clear is how expensive the calibration is. Producing the calibrated parameters in Tables 2.3
and 2.4 required thousands of CPFE simulations on high-performance computing resources
to build the training database, training the surrogate on that database, and then optimizing
the surrogate against experimental creep data. Repeating that whole sequence for every new
material is not feasible. To reduce the time and cost of material model calibration, and to
generalize the applicability of the framework more broadly, the number of parameters that
must be tuned against test data has to come down. The work described in this chapter
targets the grain boundary side of the model. Our aim is a cavitation model with a firmer
physical basis, in which cavity growth follows from the transport of vacancies to the grain
boundary rather than from parameters fit to creep curves.

and thus do not capture a lot of the underlying physics leading to cavity formation and
growth. There are also assumptions in this model that help us capture the effect of these
cavities in the microstructure but do not accurately represent the underlying physics of
the problem. One such assumption is a pre-existing uniform distribution of cavities in the
microstructure. This assumption simplifies the problem by reducing it to representing the
effect of cavities in the constitutive response of the cohesive zone elements. This, however,
does not capture the nucleation and growth of these cavities as a result of vacancy transport

through the material. It also means nucleation and coalescence have to be represented

 To understand the phenomenon of vacancy transport, see Fig. 3.1, which shows the two
classical regimes: Nabarro-Herring creep, in which vacancies migrate through the grain bulk
from boundaries in compression and to boundaries in tension, and Coble creep, in which
the same redistribution occurs along the grain boundaries themselves [11]. These vacancies
eventually coalesce and start the process of void formation and cavity growth. Both are
driven by the local stress gradient in the material.
 Our goal here is to develop a multiphysics simulation of coupled vacancy transport and
creep cavitation, in which the driving force for cavity growth emerges from a computed
vacancy field rather than from calibrated parameters. The remainder of this chapter develops
the transport half of that coupling. Following Villani et al. [1, 12], we build a bulk model
without a source or sink term; the source and sink terms that represent vacancy generation
at dislocations and absorption at boundaries remain ongoing work.

3.4 A prescribed-stress demonstration

The first check on the implementation is a block subjected to a prescribed linear stress
field, with tr(σ) ramped from 0 to 100 MPa across the domain, a uniform initial vacancy
concentration cv0 = 10−4 , a vacancy diffusivity Dv = 10−10 m2 s-1 , and a temperature of
600◦ C. Because the stress field is imposed rather than solved for, this isolates the transport
equation from the mechanics and tests the drift term on its own.
 Figure 3.2 shows the result. The vacancy concentration develops the expected relationship
with the imposed hydrostatic stress, becoming enriched in the region of highest tension and
depleted where the trace of the stress is lowest, which confirms that the coupling term
is implemented and signed correctly and that the mobility factor keeps the concentration
bounded.

3.5 Verification against a perforated plate

To verify the implementation against a nontrivial, published solution in which the stress field
is solved rather than prescribed, we reproduced the perforated plate problem of Villani et al.
[1]. A plate containing a circular hole of radius Rh = 1 µm is loaded in tension to 158 MPa
along x, which strains the plate uniaxially by 0.2 % in the far field, and the resulting stress
concentration around the hole drives a redistribution of vacancies. The hole is small, so the
stress gradients it produces are steep and act as a strong driving force for the vacancy flux.
All material parameters were taken from [1], including the reference concentration c0 = 10−4
and the relaxation volume ηv = −0.05 (causing a vacancy drift in the opposite direction).
The normalized vacancy concentration cv /c0 are then directly compared with the literature
results. Two cases were run: a purely elastic plate, and an elastic-plastic plate examined
after unloading.
 Figure 3.3 presents the results alongside the corresponding fields from [1]. This loading
results in a compressive stress concentration at the hole boundary at θ = 0◦ and a tensile
concentration at θ = 90◦ , measured from the loading direction, so that vacancies are accom-
modated more easily where the lattice is in compression. The concentration therefore rises
above c0 at θ = 0◦ and falls below it at θ = 90◦ , and both the shape of the field and the range
of cv /c0 match the literature. The elastic-plastic case after unloading is more interesting,
because the residual stress field left by plastic deformation, and not the applied load, is what
drives the remaining vacancy redistribution. Our result again reproduces the published field,
with a narrower range of cv /c0 than the elastic case as expected, since plastic flow relieves
the stress concentration.

4 Conclusions and future work
4.1 Summary

This report describes an automated framework for predicting the creep properties of L-PBF
materials directly from microstructure, and its application this year to the graded L-PBF
316H challenge problem. The CPFE model was calibrated for L-PBF 316H at 725◦ C in both
the solution annealed and as-built conditions, using a neural network surrogate trained on
a database of CPFE simulations to make optimization against experimental creep curves
tractable. The calibration for the as-built material was then applied to microstructures rep-
resenting the three regions of the graded specimen with region-specific cavitation parameters
derived from the measured porosity. The simulated curves fall within the spread of creep
tests on as-built L-PBF 316H under the same conditions and show that the framework can
extrapolate across build conditions. Using microstructure from one region to calibrate, the
framework was able to predict creep in the other regions, producing creep responses similar
to other as-built samples. The graded specimen itself, however, showed lower creep than any
comparable as-built test, so the predictions agree with the reference creep data rather than
with the digital image correlation measurements. A size effect was ruled out as the expla-
nation by rerunning one region with free lateral surfaces and with the microstructure cut
in half, neither of which changed the predicted creep appreciably, leaving the low measured
creep rate as an apparently specimen-specific behavior that is not yet explained. While new
graded sample experiments are underway, our previous surrogate was substantially improved
this year: the original was trained on curves discretized at fixed strain values and generated
with a stopping criterion that halted simulations shortly after tertiary creep began, so it
never learned the tertiary regime. Regenerating the database with a later stopping point,
discretizing at fixed fractions of the time to onset of tertiary creep, and filtering on the final
strain rate rather than on strain produces a surrogate that reproduces tertiary creep on held-
out test data, and this surrogate has been used to recalibrate the model for solution annealed
L-PBF 316H. This calibration will be used to model creep in the new graded sample data
when it is available.
 The second thread of the year’s work addresses how much calibration and test data the
framework requires. A stress-assisted vacancy diffusion model is under development. This
model, which describes the evolution of the vacancy concentration field in the microstructure
as a result of the gradient of the hydrostatic stress, is being implemented in our MOOSE-
based CPFE framework. A preliminary version was implemented and verified against the
perforated plate problem of Villani et al. [1] in both the elastic and the elastic-plastic cases.
The motivation here is to drive cavity growth by a computed vacancy flux instead of modeling
the growth of a pre-existing distribution of cavities, which would remove those parameters
from the calibration problem and rest the cavitation model on transport physics, broadening
the range of materials to which this workflow can be applied.

4.2 Future work

The most immediate task is to model creep in the graded microstructure in the solution
annealed condition, once the test data becomes available. This case is more useful than
the as-built comparison reported here, as we have more and better reference creep data for

solution annealed material. Also, solution annealed L-PBF 316H is the focus of the program.
It has consistently shown better performance so far, and is more likely to be qualified and

 On the model development side, the next step for the vacancy diffusion capability of
Chapter 3 is to solve the transport problem concurrently with the crystal plasticity model,
so that vacancy diffusion is driven by the inhomogeneous stress field in a polycrystal. The
test problem being used to develop this coupling is the idealized hexagonal polycrystal shown
in Fig. 4.1, where the grain-to-grain variation in plastic response generates strong local stress
concentrations. Since the drift term scales with the gradient of the hydrostatic stress, these
are the sites at which vacancies should accumulate and cavities should form, and recovering
that localization is the near-term goal.
 The transport model then needs to be extended from the grain bulk, which is the Nabarro-
Herring mechanism, to the grain boundaries. Cavitation is a grain boundary process and
transport along the boundaries is orders of magnitude faster than through the lattice, so
the model needs to represent Coble transport explicitly, together with the boundary source
and sink terms that the present balance equation omits. Beyond that, the coupling needs to
act in both directions — at present the stress field drives the vacancy flux but the resulting
concentration field does not feed back to the local stress, and incorporating the lattice
distortion associated with the vacancy concentration as an inelastic strain in the mechanical
problem will close that loop.
