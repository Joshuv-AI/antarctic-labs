Smart grid integration by optimizing energy, carbon, and production in industrial systems with contextual reinforcement learning
Authors: Pravin Khope, Manisha Lande, Devesh Shrivastava, Shashi Bahl, Sameer S. Gajghate, Sagar D. Shelare, Muhamad M. Noor, Milon Selvam Dennison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Industrial manufacturers switching energy paradigms must make timely, context-aware smart grid judgments. Most reinforcement learning (RL) demand response algorithms ignore the contextual interaction between pricing signals, machine states, and environmental variability, flattening rich industrial realities into static state–action mappings. Dynamic grids’ cost and carbon optimization are limited. This gap is filled by an integrated, five-stage analytical pipeline for intelligent plant–grid interaction sets, Reinforcement-Based Contextual Demand-Response Optimization (CReDO-5). The pipeline begins with the Contextual Multimodal Grid-Plant Graph (COM-G³) which integrates price, weather, and process data into a temporal graph. Causal Counterfactual Replay Engine (CaCoRE) generates feasibility-aware counterfactual trajectories enabling accurate off-policy evaluation from the output. The Distributionally-Robust Multi-Objective Policy Search (DR-MOPS) module balances energy costs and CO₂ emissions in uncertain scenarios, using trajectories in process. The Market-Consistent Participation Calibrator (MCPC) ensures policy compliance with grid settlement and baseline criteria sets. Conformal-Safe Online Learning Controller (CSOLC) guarantees action-level safety in real-time adaptations. Using the proposed framework can reduce energy expenses by 15%, CO₂ footprint by 12%, and peak demand by 20% while maintaining production integrity in process. By merging causal reasoning, risk-aware optimization, and market alignment into a single RL framework, our work presents a new analytical foundation for robust, carbon-efficient industrial plant engagement in future smart grids.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57918-z.

Introduction

Sustainable manufacturing relies on decarbonizing industrial energy systems1,2. To become “smart” and “intelligent,” future industries will be able to take advantage of dynamic pricing and data-driven smart grids to act as smart consumers of electricity3,4. As renewable energy sources increase in their contribution to grid supply and create uncertainty in how much energy will cost at any given moment, manufacturers are forced to dynamically adjust energy costs and carbon intensity in real-time5–7. Conceptually, this is consistent with the overall objective of using “real-time” data for decision-making8. However, most traditional Demand-Response (DR) systems do not account for the multiple context-specificities present within an industrial environment. These include the states of the equipment/machines, the process constraints/limits, the local weather conditions, and the changing prices and volatility associated with purchasing energy from the grid9,10. The authors suggest that Reinforcement Learning (RL) has significant potential for developing real-time, adaptive control and energy optimization algorithms for industrial applications; however, they also point out that there are several structural oversimplifications associated with many current RL-based approaches to DR for industrial applications. These include the idea that the plant environment is one large homogeneous state space (i.e., all parts of the environment are in communication with each other) - while in fact there are many complex relationships among physical assets; market signals; and process flows. Also, many of these systems lack the market participation needs or carbon accounting methods required for maximum flexibility. Strategies for hierarchical load aggregation and time-division scheduling have been shown to illustrate how flexible loads can be used to participate in wholesale markets11. Additionally, many of the systems do not provide the causal validation or safety assurance required to operate in a mission-critical production environment. Zhou et al.12, emphasize that several risk-based maintenance and replacement scheduling methods, as well as a variety of reliability-aware decision-making processes based on uncertainty about operational conditions have been developed. If incorrect actions were taken through these systems, it would potentially impede production or violate Service Level Agreements (SLA).

The last ten years of research in artificial intelligence, cyber-physical systems, and sustainable energy studies have shown how intelligent automation could create a wide range of efficient, adaptable, and strong infrastructure systems13–16. Research at the intersection of edge computing, blockchain, digital twins, quantum learning, and cognitive security enabling smart energy and industrial systems. The resulting intelligent data integration frameworks will be strong, contextually aware decision-making systems that enhance operational intelligence and sustainability. Maciá-Lillo et al.3 built AI edge-cloud provisioning services for knowledge management in industrial ecosystems with hierarchical intelligence layers that scaled dynamically based on workload and context. In their study on smart urban traffic management with blockchain, IoT, and edge computing, Chen et al.17 created a framework for the distributed intelligence across the physical network. In this study they also showed how improvement in latency and transparency in data intensive applications such as smart traffic management can be achieved with edge level computation and blockchain consensus. Later, Alnuman et al.18 used probabilistic forecasting and systematic data processing to control intermittent power distribution resources in renewable energy systems. In their study they provided statistical support for the concept of contextual energy modeling and demonstrated that the uncertainty quantification would be equally important to deterministic optimization for future smart grid systems. After integrating fog computing with marketing data, Hornik et al.19 showed distributed AI might improve real-time strategic decisions outside of industrial use cases. Taghizadeh-Hesary and Rasoulinezhad20 showed these computational advancements in the EU’s renewable transition framework, where ESG-driven smart technologies expedited decarbonization. Technical optimization interacts with socio-economic and infrastructural reality, as Alrai et al.21 explored Jordanian grid renewable penetration effects.

As per Table 1, in recognition that digital intelligence must evolve responsibly in energy and industrial ecosystems, Achuthan et al.22 employed topic modeling to discover thematic intersections between machine learning and ethical technology governance sets. El-Agamy et al.‘s bibliometric meta-analysis8 on smart city digital twins demonstrated a surge in research and its value to smart infrastructure. Hassine et al.23 and Alfayan and Hagras24 used GAN-driven graph defenses and explainable AI architectures to safeguard smart grids from adversaries and decision opacity. Transformer-based fault detection methodologies have recently illustrated that learnable signal processing architectures can be effective at detecting grid disturbances25. Mathumitha et al.26 synthesized deep learning results for smart building energy forecasting, stressing model interpretability and forecasting stability as user confidence factors. Taloma et al.27 applied AI to water distribution networks, expanding utility resource management. Wen et al.28 then created hybrid and cloud-computing frameworks that help utilities govern computation, data flow, and stakeholder profitability using load forecasting, information management, and benefit evaluation. Additionally, several studies have shown that the use of hybrid LSTM-attention forecasting models has significantly increased the accuracy of load predictions to optimize the operation of power plants29. Nayak and Jaidhar30 showed the field’s growing emphasis on ethical and trustworthy intelligence in crucial energy infrastructure. Over two decades, they examined the transition from static control systems to adaptive, data-driven, self-healing energy networks in the process. BERT based architectures integrated in edge anomaly detection by Gao31 improved robustness and demonstrated how contextual embeddings may discover operational anomalies before they worsen in process. Task-oriented collaborative diagnostic networks have also significantly improved the ability to detect defects in an industrial inspection system by using a coordinated fusion of both the physical and informational aspects of the inspection process32,33. Imanifard et al.34 shown how AI is teaching intelligent grid managers with SmartGridAgent, a digital twin-based training platform driven by locally hosted large language models. Meanwhile, Kumar et al.35 studied quantum support vector machines for home energy forecasting, marking the quantum leap of predictive analytics beyond conventional computers. Photovoltaic Panel Segmentation Utilizing Deep Representation Learning (DRL) is another example of the application of Artificial Intelligence in Renewable Asset Monitoring36. After discussing data-driven electricity theft and anomaly detection, this method was used to appliance-level forecasting by Yadav et al.37 to provide granular demand profiling for decentralized control. The recent development of temporal deep learning architectures such as TCN-BiLSTM are improving the accuracy of fault predictions of industrial machinery through improved identification of sequential dependencies in the operation data38. Alsabah et al.39 examined health and energy ecosystems in connection, latency, and context sensitivity for IoT-driven smart healthcare systems. Andalib et al.40 improved this technique with blockchain-based access control in IoT contexts, a significant step toward safe and interoperable digital infrastructures in the process.

Khalid et al.41, and Salakapuri and Pavankumar42 show how rapidly Machine Intelligence has been developing within Communication and Energy Forecasting. The former demonstrated that deep learning helped improve privacy in ultra-dense networks (UDNs) to ensure Data Integrity in 6G Designs; the latter developed a Meta-Learning Framework with Temporal Aspects to achieve High-Precision Electrical Load Forecasting which is critical when dealing with Volatile Grid Conditions. Sinha et al.43 employed AI Interpretability and Signal Geometry to identify Dynamic Lissajous Patterns for Real-Time Power Quality Monitoring. Blockchain was used in healthcare data systems by Asante and Wu44, demonstrating secure decentralized paradigms’ adaptability. Advancements in voltage restoration are demonstrating a need for reliable control strategies to mitigate the impacts of faults in low-voltage distribution systems45. Achuthan et al.46 developed a hybrid defensive method that increases industrial resilience with digital twins and cybersecurity. Sajid et al.47 showed that AI-supported financial modeling could affect energy transition economics by linking financial inclusion, green growth, and sustainability. In a comprehensive AI-driven system security assurance study, Wen et al.48 discovered that automation trust changes with performance. Zainaddin et al.49 examined resource-constrained IoT wireless network congestion control. Human-centered AI and cyber-physical-social systems develop a cognitive approach to situational awareness that dynamically balances human intuition with algorithmic control, according to Alsamhi et al.50. To enhance the model’s ability to mimic real-world systems and be applicable across different environments, the framework suggested by Leng et al.51, utilizes physics-based learning by integrating physical boundaries (e.g. energy conservation, operating limits of equipment, thermal ramping rate limits and process continuity) into the learning pipeline. The use of these constraints will provide context for how states are represented within the environment, eliminate infeasible trajectories that violate physical laws and discourage physically implausible action choices from being included in policies created through optimization. Consequently, this approach will combine the use of physically based laws with data driven learning to increase robustness and cross-industry transferability. Zhou et al.52 approach toward increasing trustworthiness and transparency through ante-hoc interpretability, supports incorporating explainability into a model’s architecture at the time of model development instead of using the post-hoc method. As an extension to the proposed DR-MOPS framework, interpretable structure can be included as causal graph visualizations or as natural language descriptions for operators regarding policy actions. Through these extensions, increased operator trust, regulatory compliance, and greater human-AI collaboration will be achievable in the safety critical industrial environment supporting the industry 5.0 principles for developing trustworthy AI.

Model’s Empirical Review Analysis.

The Review reveals how most previous studies have addressed either Forecasting, Demand Response, Market Compliance, or Safety in isolation from each other. CReDO-5 addresses these gaps with a single integrated industrial energy-system framework addressing Contextual Representation, Causal Replay, Robust Multi-Objective Optimization, Market Calibration, and Safe Online Control. Early research established infrastructure and computing through distributed processing, renewable integration, and probabilistic modeling3,17,18. Multi-layered decision ecosystems with AI, security, and optimization were designed by the middle cluster8,21,24,27,31. In addition, the use of coordinated control strategies across Engine/Grid/Load/Storage Systems has further emphasized the importance of managing Multi-Energy Systems through an Integrated System53. Further research integrated human considerations, sustainability imperatives, and trust mechanisms into AI-driven control logic30,34,37,39,41,43,44,50. They signal a paradigm change in which next-generation smart systems adopt reinforcement learning, contextual reasoning, and explainable intelligence sets. Meta-trends are present in this corpus. Energy and grid optimization studies unite contextual reinforcement learning with causal modeling. Much research agrees that classical machine learning algorithms are powerful but inflexible54–56. Context-aware architectures that understand dynamic, non-linear feedback loops between environment, policy, and cost are filling this gap. The recent application of data-and model-driven techniques to accelerate the solution of large-scale Unit Commitment problems has further highlighted the importance of hybrid optimization under uncertainty57. Human-centered frameworks50, explainable AI24, and ethical cybersecurity46 foster trust. Self-optimizing, transparent systems require efficiency and accountability. Blockchain is essential for secure data sharing and decentralized intelligence across sectors for the process40,44. This temporal analysis indicates that smart technologies are helping sectors achieve adaptive equilibrium, where contextual intelligence balances environmental, economic, and operational goals. Automated sensing, inference, and response systems are changing traffic, industrial, healthcare, and communication networks58–60. Above interconnected studies indicate a consistent conceptual trend toward autonomous, explainable, and sustainable industry in process. These contributions provide the intellectual groundwork for sophisticated frameworks like CReDO-5, representing intelligent, data-driven sustainability’s past and future sets.

Recent research conducted with the use of Reinforcement Learning (RL) based models for demand response confirms the necessity for smart-grid systems to incorporate adaptability and constraint awareness. An industry-based study recently proposed a multi-agent deep-reinforcement-learning framework for heavy-industry companies. The framework formulated demand response as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), demonstrating how multiple agents can simultaneously schedule their production and minimize their respective costs for energy consumption using dynamic pricing61. Concurrently, researchers have also developed Constrained Reinforcement Learning (CRL), which is applied to the building sector for managing Demand Response (DR). For example, explicit inclusion of operational constraints within an agent’s training environment improves the training stability while reducing the variability in performance when compared to unconstrained baseline RL methods62. In addition to the above developments, several other recent research initiatives focused on extending RL-based DR methodologies towards Cyber Resilient Microgrids (CRMGs), Real Time Hybrid Price-Incentive Response Systems (RT-HPIRS), and Decentralized Renewable-Rich Grid Management (DRRMGM) using Multi-Agent Deep-Reinforcement-Learning (MA-DRL) frameworks; Federated Learning (FL); Secure Energy Trading Mechanisms (SETMs)63–66. While each of the previous studies demonstrates increasing levels of maturity for applying RL to DR, each of these studies focus on only one aspect of DR including Coordination, Security, Pricing, Constraint Handling etc. Conversely, the proposed CReDO-5 Framework integrates Contextual Multimodal Graph Representation (CMGR), Counterfactual Replay through Causal Learning (CFRC), Distributionally Robust Multi-Objective Policy Search (DRMOPS), Market-Consistent Participation Calibration (MCPC), and Conformal-Safe Online Learning (CSOL) into a unified End-To-End Architecture for Smart-Grid participation for Industrial Applications. Therefore, the larger scope of this paper differentiates it from other recent RL-based DR methodologies and enhances its applicability to Safety-Critical and Carbon-Aware Industrial Applications61,62,64,65.

The gap between academic demand-response optimization models and practice inspired this endeavor. Traditional frameworks simplify or externally depict contextual factors like dynamic pricing, weather variations, and process-specific restrictions, restricting adaptation and generalization under unpredictable operating conditions67–69. The proposed RL-based method differs from typical mathematical optimization methods in that they are generally based on explicit process model(s) and require repeated resolution of the problem with an update to uncertainty; as such, it can be used for sequential, closed-loop industrial demand response control and its adaptability to changing plant and grid states will be key. Industrial DR systems must balance cost, throughput, and carbon reduction in dynamically changing grid and market situations70,71. Many reinforcement learning methods turn multi-objective tasks into scalar rewards, distorting industrial sustainability trade-offs. Safety-critical production conditions make policy validation and trust crucial aspects challenging since standard RL lacks causal interpretability.

The contributions of CReDO-5 are based on three areas of research, each with its own well-established body of literature. They include graph-based encoding, counter-factual analysis, and distributionally robust optimization. Therefore, the novel contribution is the systematic combination of all three into a single closed-loop reinforcement-learning system for industrial-demand response. More specifically, CReDO-5 is the first to provide a complete framework that combines: (i) A dynamic multimodal graph representing the COM-G³ model of relationships between plants, markets and environment; (ii) A Feasibility-Constrained Counterfactual Replay Mechanism (CaCoRE) to enable causal valid Off-Policy Evaluation outside of stochastic replay mechanisms; and (iii) An Operationally Deployable Decision Layer where DR-MOPS, MCPC and CSOLC jointly embed Multi-Objective Robustness, Market Compliance and Conformal Safety into Policy Optimization. As opposed to previous works that have focused on addressing each element individually, CReDO-5 provides an End-To-End Architecture that Simultaneously Ensures Contextual Awareness, Causal Consistency, Regulatory Compliance and Real-Time Safety for Practical Deployment in Industrial Smart Grid Environments.

This study proposes five interconnected ideas to solve these difficulties in a new analytical framework. First, COM-G³ combines contextual and topological intelligence to analyze plant dynamics and tariff interdependencies across states. Second, causal counterfactual reasoning for data-efficient learning improves off-policy assessment without compromising process feasibility in CaCoRE. Third, DR-MOPS robustly optimizes energy cost, CO₂ emissions, and production continuity, ensuring policy reliability during stochastic disturbances. Distributionally Robust Frameworks for Multi-Period Energy Storage Planning further validated the significance of Uncertainty-Aware Optimization in Renewable-Dominated Systems72. Fourth, MCPC operationalizes market consistency by including regulatory and baseline compliance in the control plan. Last, conformal uncertainty quantification offers live action validation with statistical guarantees for CSOLC’s safe and adaptive deployment. These modules provide validated contextual intelligence from raw sensory data to operational decisions, creating a closed-loop optimization ecosystem. CReDO-5 affects theory and practice. For real-world grid involvement, it uses causally consistent, multi-objective reinforcement learning. Up to 15% energy cost reduction, 12% CO₂ emission reduction, and 20% peak demand suppression are achieved while preserving production stability. Scalable, autonomous, carbon-conscious industrial ecosystems use contextual graph modeling, causal replay, and conformal safety in process. It redefines smart factories’ energy market interactions, linking industrial competitiveness to global sustainability sets.

Implementation details and reproducibility

To fully support replication and to improve transparency, we have provided as much detail on how our dataset was created, the train/test splits, the models used, and all hyper parameters.

Dataset and preprocessing

Dataset is comprised of roughly 4.2-million-time aligned records that include industrial telemetry, energy pricing, weather data, and carbon intensity signals. Each stream of data has been time-synchronized to a one-minute resolution. We standardized the features of each record using zero-mean/unit-variance z-score normalization. As we had less than 8% missing value in the datasets, they were filled by K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) interpolation for k = 5.

Training–validation–testing split

To prevent temporal causality violations and to ensure that the model is trained with no future information during prediction, we have created our dataset in a time-ordered sequence for training (70% of dataset), validation (15%), and test (15%). The data has been split chronologically to allow the model to be trained on past data and will then use this information to predict the future.

Result and discussion

All experiments were conducted using the implementation setup described in Sect. 2.3. CReDO-5 validation trials replicated a dynamic smart-grid ecosystem with full-scale industrial manufacturing. A virtualized 15 MW discrete manufacturing facility with 27 production machines, 4 heating systems, and 3 auxiliary utilities was used for the experiments. A simulated real-time market interface mimicked European day-ahead and intra-day energy market pricing and imbalance circumstances for the plant. One-minute weather forecasts, price signals, and machine telemetry contextual datasets were collected for six months. The energy price series (Pt) was modeled using a deterministic tariff (Pt {DA}) and stochastic departure (Pt {RT}) from a truncated normal distribution with parameters (78 €/MWh) and (22 €/MWh). Weather inputs (Wt) were ERA5 reanalysis dataset temperature, humidity, and solar irradiance adjusted 0–1. Machine-state vectors (Xt) that captured binary operational states and continuous power consumption using an autoregressive process with lag 1 and Gaussian noise (N (0, 0.05)) replicated stochastic mechanical disturbances. ENTSO-E’s hourly marginal-emissions dataset was used to generate the carbon intensity signal (Ct) between 0.25 kg and 0.92 kg CO₂ per kWh, based on renewable generating fractions.

The contextual graph encoder (COM-G³) includes 112 nodes for machine clusters, utility links, and tariffs, and 347 edges for electrical feeders and material flows. The diffusion kernel width was 5 min, and the latent embedding dimension was 256 for each 15-minute temporal hypergraph snapshot. CaCoRE, a causal replay engine, created counterfactual trajectories from 1.2 million recorded transitions using a structural equation model trained with an Adam optimizer (learning rate = 1 × 10-4) across 200 epochs For distributional robustness in the DR-MOPS layer, the Wasserstein radius (0.15) was employed to build an ambiguous set with reward weights of 0.65 for cost, 0.25 for CO₂, and 0.10 for production violations in process The carbon-price multiplier ({CO₂}) ranged from 68 € to 105 € per ton during training, based on policy feedback. MCPC utilized 48-hour rolling average tariff baselines, penalty coefficients (\mu = 0.8), and a 6-step participation smoothing window. The final deployment module (CSOLC) on an edge device (NVIDIA Jetson AGX) achieved 95% safety coverage on action predictions using conformal quantile (\epsilon = 0.05). The temporal congruence between machine cluster #4 load curtailment and negative pricing and hourly patterns showing inverse correlations between renewable availability and marginal carbon intensity demonstrated non-trivial context–reward relationships. These experiments establish a realistic, data-rich evaluation environment for rigorously validating the performance, robustness, and interpretability of the CReDO-5 model in practical industrial grid interactions.

In this research, for obtaining contextual diversity and operational realism, real-world and synthetic datasets are used. IESD, UCI Smart Grid Stability Dataset, ENTSO-E Transparency Platform for market and carbon data, and ERA5 Reanalysis for weather variables were the key data sources. IESD provided 1-minute telemetry on electrical loads, production rates, and machine operational statuses across multiple industrial sectors, whereas UCI gave dynamic voltage and frequency metrics under grid stress. Over 4.2 million timestamped temperature, irradiance, power tariffs, carbon intensity, and machine-level load traces were collected. All data streams were aligned and normalized using a uniform temporal index and z-score transformation for cross-source uniformity. Strong correlations (− 0.74 between renewable generation % and marginal carbon intensity, + 0.62 between tariff volatility and aggregated plant load) demonstrate the applicability of this dataset ensemble for reinforcement-based contextual optimizations. Bayesian optimization and grid refinement tuned convergence stability and policy robustness hyperparameters. Contextual encoder (COM-G³) utilized latent embedding dimension of 256, graph convolution depth of 4, and temporal diffusion kernel of (5) minutes. The Optimization of CaCoRE involved a learning rate (1e-4), a batch size of 512, and a penalty for counterfactuals of 0.1. DR-MOPS (the policy optimizer) used a discount factor of 0.98, an entropy regularization of 0.003 and a Wasserstein robustness radius of 0.15. Market Calibration (MCPC) had a baseline smoothing window of 12 intervals, a penalty weight of 0.8 and a gradient clipping threshold of 0.5 as it was necessary to maintain stable optimizations at the bilevel level. CSOLC (the on-line controller) used a conformal quantile of 0.05, a range of adaptive learning rates (5e-3 to 1e-3), and a safety slack coefficient (0.2). These parameters resulted in a balance between exploration-stability, interpretability and regulatory compliance over the course of the training iterations per scenario set (75,000 iterations) ensuring that all the different contextual contexts converged. The proposed CReDO-5 framework was compared with three other complex reinforcement-based demand response models (Method3, Method8 and Method30 using contextual and operational dimensions. Each model was trained and tested under identical data sets and conditions to ensure that there were no limitations due to either process or context. The study studied Energy Cost Reduction, CO₂ Emission Reduction, Production Throughput Retention, Peak Demand Suppression, and Policy Robustness Index (PRI) in stable and fluctuating grid environments.

Hyperparameter & baseline detailed analysis

The CReDO-5 architecture had its expected values and variances for performance indicators calculated through 100 trial runs of a statistically valid approach. The average of normalized results across all trials under varying grid and environmental scenarios were utilized to estimate the Energy Cost Reduction (ECR), CO2 Reduction (CR), Peak Demand Suppression (PDS), and Production Throughput Retention (PTR) of the CReDO-5 architecture. The CReDO-5 architecture estimated values for ECR, CR, and PDS of 15.7% ± 1.4, 12.4% ± 1.6, and 22.3% ± 1.9, where ± represents the standard deviation of one run. As the variance in all core metrics was lower than 2%, it indicated policy convergence and minimal stochastic divergence during the CReDO-5 training process. The CSOLC module established stable safety limits post-deployment, with a production throughput retention of 99.1% ± 0.5 and a service-level violation rate variance of less than 0.2%. Low variance intervals indicate that the model is both robust and stable and thus minimizes the effects of contextual volatility due to rapid tariff changes or renewable intermittent power generation on decision quality sets. Using two-tailed paired t-tests and Welch’s ANOVA at a 95% confidence interval (α = 0.05), the statistical significance of performance differences between CReDO-5 and baseline models was verified. CReDO-5 provided an additional 1.4% point decrease in energy costs compared to baseline models, t (99) = 4.72, p = 0.004, which was found to be statistically significant. Statistically significant results were also found when comparing CReDO-5 and previous methods as to CO2 reductions, F (3,396) = 9.56, p < 0.01. This demonstrates that the model is superior to previous methods in terms of CO2 reduction and this superiority is not dependent on sample variability. Additionally, the model demonstrated a statistically significant mean difference of 3.4% from Method8 to peak demand suppression, p = 0.007, demonstrating its good performance in multiple contexts. Furthermore, all important indicator confidence intervals exclude zero, and thus demonstrate the resiliency of the improvements demonstrated above. The Cohen’s d values for ECR, CR, and PDS are 0.82, 0.77, and 0.88, respectively, and thus indicate that the contextual learning employed by the CReDO-5 architecture provides greater operational advantages than the use of incremental changes.

Validation in industrial grid system

To reinforce the validity of the experimental verification of the proposed framework, the performance of CReDO-5 was assessed against an industrial-grid validation testbed simulating a medium-sized ceramic products manufacturing plant consuming a maximum of 15 MW of electrical power. The plant had three main energy-consuming phases of operation: (i) firing in the kilns; (ii) grinding of raw materials; and (iii) firing of glazed coatings supported by smaller amounts of energy used to grind raw materials and mix batches. In addition to assessing the potential for reducing both energy costs and CO2 emissions in response to short-term price variability in the wholesale electricity market, the industrial-validation was designed to assess how well the proposed framework maintained both production continuity and ensured equipment safety when controlling energy usage from variable sources.

A smart grid simulation environment was developed which simulated real-time changes in the wholesale electricity market with price variability occurring every five minutes along with marginal carbon intensities varying over time. The input data used to create the contextual graphs included dynamic tariff signals Pt, kiln temperature profiles Xt, machine operating states, batch-schedule data, ambient-humidity Wt, and marginal carbon-intensity signals. These input data were then processed through the COM-G3 contextual-graph encoder to produce real-time representations of both the plant and grid-state. Real-time representations of both plant and grid-state produced by the contextual-graph encoder were then used as inputs to the DR-MOPS policy optimizer. MCPC provided enforcement of market-compliance baselines for the policy optimizer’s outputs, while CSOLC filtered unsafe control-actions prior to their implementation on actual devices.

Because chronological replay (rather than random sampling) was used to validate the proposed framework, there were no concerns about temporal-leakage. Training and validation data used to train the proposed framework did not include tariff, weather, and/or carbon-intensity information for time-periods subsequent to those used to train/validate the proposed framework. The action-space considered for each time-step consisted of four possible actions: delaying ignition of kiln-firing cycles; rescheduling auxiliary-processes (e.g., milling); curtailing loads; and adjusting participation coefficients. Kiln thermal-stability, completion times for production-batches, rated-capacity of equipment, and compliance with Service Level Agreement requirements were all treated as constraints. Any proposed-action that violated one or more of these constraints was rejected by the Conformal Safety Layer prior to its implementation.

On a typical workday, the model predicted that prices would reach a peak value of 185€/MWh at 13:00 h and that marginal CO2 intensities would be equal to 0.91 kg/kWh at the same hour. In this case, if the base-case production-schedule had been followed, kiln ignition would have occurred simultaneously with this high-priced-and-high-CO2 period. Therefore, CReDO-5 delayed ignition of kiln firing cycles by 40 min, while shifting lower-energy auxiliary-programs (including milling and material mixing) into the high-tariff period. When tariff values dropped below 118€/MWh and marginal CO2 intensities fell below 0.55 kg/kWh, the kiln-cycle was resumed. By doing so, daily energy costs were lowered by 16.1% while lowering CO2-emissions by 13.2% and production throughput deviated from scheduled throughput by less than 0.6% as shown in Fig. 14.

Fig. 14Industrial validation outcomes over 24 h.

Industrial validation outcomes over 24 h.

In addition to testing static responses to changing price environments, the industrial-validation also tested adaptive responses to changing process conditions. After ten days of normal operation, an unanticipated increase in ambient-humidity resulted in slower-than-normal kiln-heating rates and a slight reduction in thermal-efficiency for some kiln-based processes. The feedback-loop detected this shift via increased residual-error in kiln-temperature response. As a result, DR-MOPS increased the weighting factor for humidity context in the reward-function by 12%, thereby enabling the controller to consider moisture-sensitive kiln-behavior more heavily. At the same time, MCPC changed participation-coefficients to remain within a balance-penalty threshold of €65/MWh. Finally, CSOLC rejected aggressive load-shifting actions that would have placed excessive stress on the system at greater than 95% of its rated-equipment capacity.

Three practical benefits are demonstrated by these results: (1) CReDO-5 is able to convert contextual grid and plant information into actionable production-control commands rather than simply providing offline cost estimates; (2) CReDO-5 preserves production continuity while optimizing demand-response activity since it filters out unsafe or disruptive control-actions before they are implemented; and (3) CReDO-5’s closed-loop adaptation mechanism enables controllers to adapt to process-drift (such as humidity-induced delays in kiln-heating) without having to manually adjust controller parameters.

Limitations

Despite these capabilities and high empirical performance, CReDO-5 has limitations. The suggested approach requires rich, high-frequency contextual data, which restricts its usefulness in facilities with poor telemetry or data quality. Graph diffusion reconstruction of missing data adds 8–10% processing overhead to each training iteration. The model has excellent real-time responsiveness (average inference latency < 200 ms), but performance decreases when grid volatility exceeds σ > 50 €/MWh, causing cost fluctuations up to 2.3% above optimum. The conformal safety method is robust, although conservative action filtering reduces cost reductions in steady grid conditions. Each scenario requires 75,000 iterations and 11 h on an NVIDIA A100 GPU to train. Finally, causal replay improves off-policy evaluation but requires reliable counterfactual estimation, which makes weakly linked variables uncertain. Higher sensor integration, lightweight graph embedding methods, and adaptive causal inference methods can provide greater generalizability and adaptability to various industrial systems.

Future scope

While CReDO-5 is suitable for many regulatory environments, it could have a much broader scientific and practical application as an expansion of itself. Next, the CReDO-5 model would become a multi-agent demand response controller that receives real time feedback from the grid regarding DERs such as battery storage systems, rooftop solar arrays and fuel cell systems. We need to scale contextual intelligence across industrial clusters so that we can provide cross-plant collaborations via federated learning architectures to protect each plant’s data privacy. The contextual domain will be expanded to include supply chain logistics, production scheduling and material flow dynamics to support model adaptation in high dimensionality industrial settings. In addition to its potential benefits, recent developments in generative artificial intelligence (GenAI), have the potential to provide an additional method to make the suggested framework more adaptable and more robust. The GenAI enabled Smart Manufacturing Paradigm has inspired researchers to use GAN’s to generate Counter-Factually Realistic and Rare-Event Scenarios that could represent events that would not be represented in their historical data sets. By generating these realistic synthetic trajectories using GAN’s or similar technologies, we believe that they will be able to increase the efficiency of our offline training process, create opportunities to stress test our policies in response to extreme Grid Disturbances, and provide better generalization for unobserved operating conditions. Furthermore, if these methods are used in conjunction with Human-in-the-Loop and Industry 5.0 methodologies, we envision them providing more resilient, human centered and sustainable industrial energy systems73. It will cause under uncertainty with unambiguously interpretable explanations via probabilistic graphical models and causal policy gradients. Carbon pricing will add to the system by converting environmental optimizations to financial incentives by monetizing the reductions in CO2 emissions. This will result in a self-optimizing, carbon neutral manufacturing network with smart grid operations. The CReDO-5 (Collaborative Renewable Distributed Operations Framework) is likely to have the ability to extend its capabilities towards the Industrial Metaverse Paradigm for Industry 5.0. In terms of extending the use of the COM-G³ Graph in a Digital Twin of real time Plant-Grid Interactions, which would exist both physically as well as virtually; such extensions are possible with respect to supporting Immersive Simulation, Collaborative Optimization & Predictive Maintenance and Human-Centric Adaptive Industrial Energy Management74. Extending credo-5 towards high-performance manufacturing systems (HPMS) has the potential to addressed scalability in extreme throughput and precision-critical operations. Leng et al.75 note that these types of environments will require much greater flexibility, resilience, and reliability than current environments. The potential for ultra-low latency and robust real-time control in extremely fast-paced industrial settings also highlights the potential for this type of system using dynamic reward shaping and hardware-in-the-loop testing.
