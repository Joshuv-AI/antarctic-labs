Sarcopenia, cachexia and frailty in older cats: Mechanisms, assessment and therapeutic considerations
Authors: Marjorie Chandler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPractical relevance:Age-related muscle loss due to sarcopenia and cachexia is common in mammals and is likely a major contributor to frailty in older cats. Frailty adversely affects quality of life and is a predictor of survival time. In some cats, excess body fat is preserved but muscle mass is lost, a condition termed sarcopenic obesity. Although the mechanisms underlying sarcopenia and cachexia have been studied in humans, the pathophysiology has not been well studied in cats.Clinical challenges:Early diagnosis of muscle loss by a muscle condition score may aid therapeutic intervention, and epaxial muscle ultrasound offers a promising adjunctive diagnostic tool. In humans, muscle strength predicts adverse outcomes; however, direct measurement of this is difficult in cats. Furthermore, in humans, physical performance is used as an estimate of muscle strength; in cats, it may be possible to estimate strength and function by activity. The development and use of a frailty score, which incorporates activity along with other measurements, will provide a valuable tool, which may improve the assessment of the effects of sarcopenia in cats, including their quality of life and possibly survival time.Therapeutic considerations:Anabolic resistance to muscle synthesis may result in increased dietary protein requirements. Some older cats may also have decreased fat and protein digestion, sometimes concurrently with decreased serum cobalamin and vitamin E. These nutritional requirements should be addressed along with feeding management practices.Recent advances and future prospects:New therapeutic interventions for sarcopenia are being investigated for humans, often in conjunction with resistance exercise; however, their safety and efficacy have not been evaluated in cats. Further research in this area presents an opportunity to improve the health and longevity of ageing cats.Aims:The aim of this review is to describe the pathophysiology, diagnosis and potential treatment of feline muscle loss. Further aims are to encourage early diagnosis and treatment of muscle loss by clinicians and to inspire more research in this area.Keywords: Muscle loss, ageing, muscle condition score, senior, geriatric

Introduction

The term sarcopenia is derived from the Greek root words for ‘poverty of flesh’. In humans, it is defined as a loss of muscle mass, strength and function, occurring in ageing without overt disease as a contributing cause,1,2 whereas cachexia is muscle loss secondary to chronic inflammation or disease. 2 Frailty in humans is a state of increased vulnerability from age-associated declines in reserve and function resulting in a decreased ability to cope with everyday or acute stressors. 3

Sarcopenia in humans is a progressive skeletal muscle disease that increases the risks of frailty, mobility disorders, falls, fractures and cognitive decline. It is also associated with cardiac disease, respiratory disease, lowered quality of life and earlier death. 4 Human age-related sarcopenia is usually a slow process, although it becomes marked after 60 years of age. 5 As severe sarcopenia does not occur in all elderly people, there may be a genetic component. 6

While weight loss is common with sarcope-nia, it is possible to have excess body fat with muscle loss, termed sarcopenic obesity. Weight may remain stable in spite of muscle loss in these cases. Sarcopenia and obesity are distinct entities but share common pathophysiological features and risk factors such as lifestyle, ageing, increased inflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species, and endocrine alterations. Furthermore, the two conditions act synergistically, leading to a detrimental vicious circle (Figure 1). 7

Cachexia is often seen in cats with chronic diseases; for example, cancer, chronic kidney disease (CKD), congestive heart failure and diabetes mellitus. Cachexic cats often show weakness, anorexia and poor quality of life, which can contribute to an owner’s decision to euthanise the cat. Sarcopenia due to ageing and cachexia secondary to disease often occur together. 1

The muscle loss in sarcopenia and cachexia is a major component of frailty, which is progressive, usually age-related and increases the risk of adverse health outcomes. Frailty in humans is characterised by the presence of three or more of the following: unintentional weight loss, fatigue, muscle weakness, slowness and low physical activity levels. 8 Many of these clinical signs are often recognised in older cats. Frailty is also associated with malnutrition or poor appetite, which may occur more frequently in older cats.

This review describes the pathophysiology, diagnosis and potential treatment of feline muscle loss. It also aims to encourage both early diagnosis and treatment of muscle loss by clinicians and for more research to be carried out in the area of feline muscle loss.

Aetiology and mechanisms of sarcopenia and cachexia

Metabolic and muscle changes in sarcopenia, cachexia and frailty

Sarcopenia

The mechanisms of sarcopenia are complex, multifactorial and inter-related. They include: changes in type II muscle fibres; fat accumulation; increased cytokines (‘inflammaging’); decreased growth hormone (GH), insulin-like growth factor (IGF)-1 and testosterone; decreased physical activity (disuse atrophy); mitochondrial dysfunction; insulin resistance; decreased stem cell/progenitor cell (satellite cell) number and function (cellular ageing); oxidative stress; cellular senescence; and changes in protein synthesis and degradation, including anabolic resistance.1,6,9

Muscles are composed of fibres classified as slow-twitch (type I) and fast-twitch (type IIb) fibres according to their metabolism: slow-twitch fibres use aerobic (oxidative phosphorylation) respiration and fast-twitch fibres use anaerobic metabolism (glycolysis). In sarco-penia and cachexia, glycolytic type IIb muscle fibres (myofibres) are smaller and are preferentially lost over type 1 myofibres. 6

Myofibre loss can be accompanied by inflammation and infiltration of adipose tissue into muscle tissue (termed myosteatosis). Adipose tissue expansion and muscle loss in sarcopenic obesity causes increased proinflammatory cytokine levels (eg, tumour necrosis factor alpha [TNF-α], interleukin [IL]-1 beta ([β], IL-6), increased leptin, reduced adiponectin and intramyocellular lipid deposits. 7

Ageing humans have a progressive decrease in GH secretion, which acts directly on target tissues, including skeletal muscle. Many effects are also mediated indirectly by circulating (liver-derived) or locally produced IGF-1. Declining muscle tissue IGF-1 and anabolic cytokines reduce the synthesis of myofibrillar (eg, myosin heavy chain) and mitochondrial proteins. 6 In men, skeletal muscle contains numerous androgen receptors, and testosterone plays an important role in maintaining muscle mass and strength; therefore, age-related declines in testosterone likely contribute to the development of sarcopenia and possibly frailty. 10 Given that so many male cats are neutered, however, this is likely to be a less important factor in the feline population.

Decreased activity in humans – for example, due to osteoarthritis – is associated with reduced mitochondrial function, dysregulation of cellular redox status and chronic systemic inflammation, which cause the skeletal muscle intracellular environment to be more susceptible to reactive oxygen species-mediated toxicity. 11

In humans, ageing is also associated with reduced insulin sensitivity, which may contribute to the impairment of IGF-1 action. 12 In one study, mature cats (average age 5.8 years) had lower insulin-induced glycaemic changes compared with young cats, although the older cats had greater body weights. When the responses were balanced for weight, the differences were not significant. 13 Changes in insulin sensitivity and IGF-1 in senior cats have not been examined.

Aged mice have a reduced number and function of muscle satellite cells, the progenitor cells for muscle repair and maintenance, which then contribute to muscle inflammation and fibrosis. 14

In humans, exercise and ingestion of amino acids normally stimulate muscle protein synthesis, but these effects are decreased in the elderly. This ‘anabolic resistance’ contributes to sarcopenia. 6

Cachexia

The causes of cachexia are also multifactorial and overlap with those of sarcopenia, including the involvement of inflammatory and hormonal mediators, oxidative stress and upregulation of the ubiquitin-proteasome proteolytic pathway. Increased inflammatory and counter-regulatory mediators decrease metabolic adaptations for fat utilisation. Protein is then used as a primary energy source, causing rapid and often profound lean body mass catabolisation and muscle loss. 1 In cats, which preferentially use protein for energy, this may be especially pronounced.

Frailty

The loss of skeletal muscle mass and function is an important aspect of frailty. Frailty is largely responsible for the weight loss, weakness and impaired locomotion of elderly humans. Additionally, muscle-derived cytokines and growth factors may contribute to frailty by influencing metabolic homeostasis and systemic ageing. 15 Older Labrador Retrievers showed a decrease in epaxial muscle area compared with younger dogs, but with no difference in inflammatory mediators; 16 this has not been explored in cats. Cats over 12 years old often lose fat and lean tissue. By 15 years, cats typically have a mean lean body mass (LBM) of <2 kg, one-third less than the mean 3 kg they have as younger adults. The rate of decline is a predictor of survival time.17,18

Appetite effects on muscle loss

Poor appetite contributes to muscle loss. Appetite is affected by dysfunctions in satiety hormone regulation and fatty acid oxidation. Appetite is controlled by orexigenic hormones, which stimulate eating and satiety (anorexic) factors that decrease appetite (eg, adiponectin, serotonin, insulin and proopiomelanocortin). Some satiety factors may be increased in cachexia due to disturbances of hypothalamic pathways controlling energy homeostasis and appetite. The ‘brain–muscle axis’, which is coordinated by the hypothalamus, mediates the onset of anorexia, the balance between anabolism and catabolism, and tissue wasting by centrally influencing energy homeostasis. The hypothalamic melanocortin system, therefore, does not respond appropriately to energy depletion, and its activity instead promotes catabolism – that is, reduced energy intake, increased energy expenditure, increased muscle proteolysis and fat loss.1,6

Fatty acid metabolism within hypothalamic neurons affects food intake and energy metabolism in a leptin-independent way. Inhibition of fatty acid synthase blocks both upregulation of orexigenic neuropeptides and downregulation of anorexigenic neuropeptides, thereby decreasing food intake. Pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly TNF-α and IL-1, inhibit fatty acid oxidation. 19

Nutrient assimilation in older cats

With age, the incidence of decreased fat digestibility increases, affecting approximately 10–15% of mature cats (8–12 years of age) and 30% of geriatric cats (>12 years old).17,20 In some geriatric cats, fat digestibility is as low as 30% (a normal rate is 90% and more). 17 About 20% of cats over 14 years old have protein digestibility below 77% (a normal rate is 85% or more). Low protein and low fat digestibility often occur together.17,20 Many older cats also have low serum cobalamin and vitamin E. 17 An increased amount of faeces (possibly without diarrhoea) and low body weight may be the only signs of decreased digestibility.17,20

Vitamin D deficiency contributes to sarco-penia in humans, and in cats with poor fat digestibility vitamin D absorption may be decreased. 21

Diagnosis and assessment of muscle loss and muscle function

Cachexia and sarcopenia

In humans, three out of five criteria are required for a diagnosis of cachexia: low fat-free mass (ie, LBM), decreased muscle strength, fatigue, anorexia and increased markers of inflammation. 22 In its 2018 revised definition, the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People used low muscle strength as the primary parameter of sarcopenia as it is currently the most reliable and practical way to measure muscle function. While both muscle mass and strength are needed for optimal performance, decreases in maximal strength are three times greater than the decline in muscle mass during human ageing. 23 Muscle mass and strength appear to be independently regulated. During ageing, muscle strength is a better predictor of adverse outcomes, including mortality related to any cause. Muscle quality, the micro- and macroscopic aspects of muscle architecture and composition, is also impaired in sarcopenia. 24 However, muscle quality is technically difficult to use as a clinical assessment of sarcopenia. 24

Determination of muscle mass

Early diagnosis of muscle loss can be challenging as LBM loss may precede significant weight loss. Loss of appendicular skeletal muscle mass in humans has been evaluated using whole-body dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) or multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analysis. 24 While these techniques exist for cats and are used in research, they are not usually practical for clinical use.

Body condition score (BCS) systems evaluate adipose tissue, while the muscle condition score (MCS) system estimates muscle mass (see ‘Feline muscle condition score chart’ box). Muscle condition scoring can be quickly performed during a physical examination. Evaluation of muscle mass includes visual examination and palpation over the temporal bones, scapulae, lumbar vertebrae and pelvic bones. Muscle mass is assessed as normal (Figure 2), slight wasting, moderate wasting or severe wasting. 25

In a feline study, the correlation between MCS- and DEXA-measured LBM was modest, likely because skeletal muscle is only one component of LBM, which also includes visceral organs. Skeletal muscle typically comprises about 35–40% of LBM. The lack of a stronger correlation between DEXA-measured LBM and MCS does not decrease the usefulness of the MCS for clinical patients, as most of their protein catabolism involves skeletal muscle. 26 The correlation between DEXA-assessed LBM and MCS to assess feline sarcopenia has not yet been evaluated.

Ultrasound of the epaxial muscle height (EMH) in cats may provide a valuable quantitative determination of muscle mass. A study showed that MCS was significantly correlated with EMH. 27 In another study, EMH was better than vertebral epaxial muscle score and forelimb epaxial muscle score in evaluating cats with sarcopenia and cachexia. 28

Assessing muscle function and strength

As poor physical performance predicts adverse outcomes, it is used to estimate the severity of human sarcopenia. Initial signs of sarcopenia can include falling, weakness, slow walking speed, difficulty rising from a chair, unintentional weight loss and muscle wasting. 24 In humans, gait speed has been shown to predict adverse outcomes related to sarcopenia. 7

The 6-min walk test – that is, the distance walked in 6 mins – has been used in dogs to assess stamina, but is obviously less useful in cats unless they are harness trained as cats are more difficult to persuade to walk for 6 mins. 29 Assessment of a cat’s activity at home is valuable but may be compromised by personality, osteoarthritis or other disorders.

Carnitine as a biomarker

A study showed muscle carnitine was positively associated with relative muscle mass in mice, and plasma carnitine levels were lower in sarcopenic men than in a control group. 30 Serum carnitine has been explored as a biomarker for feline heart disease but not as a marker for sarcopenia. 31

Frailty

Frailty scores exist for humans and have been developed for dogs. These are based on either deficits in functional aspects of living (eg, cognition and activities) and/or phenotype (physical changes). 32 In humans, the Frailty Index measures deficits in physiological, psychological, cognitive and social function, and the Frailty Phenotype measures unintentional weight loss, weakness, poor endurance, slowness and low activity.8,33

In dogs, a frailty index was developed using a quantitative score of clinicopathological parameters and results of owner-completed questionnaires about their dog’s overall health, cognitive and locomotor status. 34 Another frailty index based on owner assessment of 33 parameters was used to quantify mortality risk in dogs. Frailty was strongly related to mortality within a 6-month period, although some frail dogs lived longer and some dogs with a low index died within the study period. 35 A canine frailty phenotype tool was developed using the following five domains: nutritional status, exhaustion, muscle weakness, activity level and mobility (representing slow walking speed). This frailty phenotype was also predictive of all-cause mortality within a 6-month period. 36 Another canine frailty phenotype tool was designed also using five criteria: weakness, slowness, poor endurance, low physical activity and shrinking (weight loss or muscle atrophy) as evaluated from physical examinations or performance tests to assign phenotypes (eg, ‘frail’ or ‘robust’). 37

A pilot study of a frailty score of older cats (11–20 years of age) used owner and veterinarian assessment of cognitive function, behaviour, eating behaviour, activity, body weight, unexplained changes in weight, BCS, MCS and the number of chronic diseases. Thirteen of 64 cats (20%) categorised by veterinarians to be frail died or were euthanased during the 6-month follow-up compared with only 1 of 54 cats (2%) who were not frail. 38

Decreasing risk of, and potential treatments for, sarcopenia and cachexia

Exercise is vital for muscle condition

While some cats can be exercised by using a harness, for most cats increasing activity levels is more feasible. This may involve play, interactive feeders and/or outdoor time. Treatment of arthritis is vital for activity and wellbeing and often needs to be addressed in older cats.

Stimulating appetite and improving calorie and nutrient intake

Decreased calorie intake is common in older cats and contributes to muscle loss. As arthritis or dental disease may make accessing food or eating uncomfortable, management should be addressed to ensure easy, comfortable food access and intake.

Healthy older people have decreased taste perception, which may also occur in cats. 39 Some cats will eat if a variety of foods are fed (eg, rotating foods). Flavour-enhancing pastes or ‘soups’ or small amounts of palatable foods added to their normal cat food may encourage intake, although it should comprise <10% of the total daily calories. Cats tend to like foods with a umami flavour found in ingredients with increased nucleotides and L-amino acids, such as tuna, which has a combination of high levels of inosine monophosphate and free L-histidine, producing a strong synergistic umami taste. 40 A valuable resource for owners on feeding management to encourage food intake is available from International Cat Care. 41

Fish oil supplements may increase palata-bility, although some cats dislike them, and they are beneficial in modulating inflammatory cytokines. Flax seed or other plant oils are ineffective omega-3 fatty acids for cats, as cats lack the delta-6 desaturase enzyme needed to convert these into longer chain omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids that exert greater anti-inflammatory effects. In dogs with heart disease, fish oil supplementation decreased

IL-1 concentrations and improved cachexia, although this has not been explored in cats. 42 When combined with antioxidants, arginine and B vitamins, fish oil improved the cognition of middle-aged and older cats, which could possibly aid appetite. 43

Mirtazapine has appetite-stimulating, antiemetic and antinausea effects and has proven efficacy for increasing cats’ appetites. The half-life is longer in cats over 10 years and those with CKD (12 and 15 h, respectively, compared with 10 h in younger cats). 44

Ghrelin, an endogenous hormone that increases during fasting, stimulates appetite, has anti-inflammatory effects and decreases muscle atrophy. 45 While ghrelin has a short half-life, the ghrelin selective receptor agonist capromorelin has shown some improvements in muscle mass and stamina in older humans. It has not been studied for this effect in cats, although it has been shown to increase appetite.46,47

For cats with suspected decreased protein and fat assimilation, management is similar as for those cats diagnosed with chronic enteropathy. This includes dietary management (eg, highly digestible, novel antigen or hydrolysed protein diets), repleting serum cobalamin and folate concentrations, vitamin E supplementation and possibly prebiotics or probiotics, or, less likely, antibiotic treatment or immunomodulation. 17

Anabolic resistance and dietary protein

Older humans may have anabolic resistance, a condition of decreased muscle amino acid utilisation, and increased amounts of dietary protein are required to stimulate muscle protein synthesis. The mechanisms responsible for anabolic resistance are not fully understood. They may include age-related declines in protein synthesis from decreased IGF-1, impairments in the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signalling pathway and impaired protein kinase B (Akt) signalling. Forkhead box O transcription factors are downstream targets of the insulin/ PI3K/Akt pathway and are major regulators of acute muscle atrophy.48,49

It is likely that increased amounts of highly digestible protein should be fed to many older cats to help support LBM. Adult cats require 5.2 g of protein/kg (7.8 g/kg0.75) body weight per day to maintain LBM; senior cats (without contraindications for increased dietary protein) may require more dietary protein. 50

Gut microbiotas are also implicated in possible mechanisms of anabolic resistance, either directly or indirectly, and a gut–muscle connection is likely. With age, gut bacteria producing short chain fatty acids decrease; these may have a role in protecting against muscle loss and frailty. Gut bacteria are affected by dietary protein and also affect proteoly-sis. 51 Because of the effects of gut bacteria, probiotics could have a future role in modifying the gut microbiome to help combat anabolic resistance. There are no current studies looking at the association between sarcopenia, frailty and the gut microbiome in cats.
