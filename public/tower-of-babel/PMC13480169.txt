Background

Before the arterial switch operation, atrial redirection via the Mustard or Senning procedures was the standard palliation for D-looped transposition of the great arteries (D-TGA).1 In the Mustard procedure, the atrial septum is excised, and prosthetic material is used to baffle deoxygenated blood from the vena cavae to the subpulmonic left ventricle and oxygenated blood from the pulmonary veins to the systemic right ventricle (Figure 1).1,2 The Senning operation results in similar anatomy and physiology but instead uses native tissue. These procedures result in many long-term complications including systemic right ventricular failure, atrial arrhythmias, and sudden cardiac death.2

D-Looped Transposition of the Great Arteries and the Atrial Switch

(A) D-looped transposition of the great arteries with cardiac segments [S,D,D]. The aorta arises from the right-sided morphologic right ventricle, the pulmonary artery arises from the left-sided morphologic left ventricle, and the aortic valve is located rightward and anterior relative to the pulmonary valve. (B) After atrial switch, the vena cavae are redirected via superior and inferior limbs of the systemic venous baffle to the subpulmonary left ventricle, and the pulmonary veins are redirected via pulmonary venous baffle to the subsystemic right ventricle (adapted with permission from Haeffele and Lui2).

Of the long-term complications of these procedures, baffle leaks are common, reported in as many as 50% to 67% of patients having undergone atrial switch.1,3, 4, 5 Similar to atrial-level shunts, baffle leaks carry the risk of paradoxical systemic embolism, subpulmonary ventricular volume overload, and cyanosis.1 Transcatheter baffle leak closure has been commonly reported with the use of Amplatzer septal occluder (ASO) devices or covered stents.1,4,6 Here we report the first-in-human use of a GORE CARDIOFORM ASD Occluder (GCA) to close a baffle leak. The GCA is softer and more compliant than other devices, chosen in this case because the baffle leak was directly adjacent to the tricuspid valve, so device contact with the valve was unavoidable.

Case Presentation

A 46-year-old woman with a history of D-TGA initially underwent balloon atrial septostomy on her first day of life, followed by atrial switch (Mustard) procedure at 18 months of age. She had no significant cardiovascular symptoms and had been followed by cardiac magnetic resonance with an elevated pulmonary-to-systemic flow ratio (Qp:Qs) of 1.4 to 1.8. No baffle leak or left-sided chamber enlargement had previously been identified. A routine surveillance echocardiogram demonstrated new subpulmonary left ventricular dilation, and follow-up magnetic resonance imaging showed a large defect in the inferior limb of the atrial baffle measuring 2.1 × 1.9 cm (with 2 adjacent smaller defects), Qp:Qs of 2.0 to 2.2, and subpulmonary left ventricular chamber enlargement with an indexed left ventricular end-diastolic volume of 95 mL/m2, increased from 79 mL/m2. She was referred for baffle leak closure.

By cardiac catheterization, hemodynamic assessment recorded mean superior vena cava and inferior vena cava pressures of 9 and 10 mm Hg, respectively; a subpulmonary left ventricular end-diastolic pressure of 6 mm Hg; a mean pulmonary artery pressure of 20 mm Hg; a pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 10 mm Hg; Qp:Qs of 1.9; and a pulmonary vascular resistance of 1.24 WU (2.4 indexed WU) under general anesthesia.

Angiography confirmed a sizable baffle leak near the site where the superior and inferior baffle limbs join as well as the location of the adjacent smaller leaks. By transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), the defect abutted the crux of the heart, adjacent to the atrioventricular valves, measuring 2.6 × 1.7 cm, with flow noted to be predominately from the pulmonary venous atrium to the systemic venous atrium. The inferior edge of the defect was in close proximity to the atrioventricular valves (Figure 2, Video 1).

Given the location of the baffle leak and potential impingement on the atrioventricular valves, alternative approaches to the stiffer standard transcatheter devices were discussed. As the patient wished for a nonsurgical intervention, the decision was made to proceed with the softer GCA to provide sufficient coverage of the defect while mitigating the likelihood of device-related interference or damage to the adjacent valves.

A 25-mm sizing balloon was positioned within the primary defect and inflated until there was minimal flow as visualized by TEE. A 44-mm GCA was advanced into the baffle leak using the unmodified standard delivery system (Video 2). Angiography and TEE confirmed a small residual leak at the inferior margin of the device, possibly from the additional inferior defect (Figure 3, Video 3). Compared with the pre-preprocedural TEE study, there was slight impingement on the tricuspid valve with an interval increase in tricuspid regurgitation from mild to moderate.

Approximately 2 months after device placement, she presented in atrial flutter. She was started on beta blockade and anticoagulation. TEE during atrial flutter demonstrated moderate-to-severe tricuspid regurgitation, and she underwent synchronized cardioversion. A computed tomography scan performed at this time confirmed the location of the device and noted a persistent tiny residual baffle leak measuring approximately 4 mm (Figure 4).

A follow-up transthoracic echocardiogram 4 months after her cardioversion showed improvement in her tricuspid regurgitation back to mild-to-moderate. Arrhythmia monitoring after cardioversion with an implantable monitor has shown no recurrence of arrhythmias, so ablation was not pursued due to lack of recurrence, also noting that there would be challenges in transbaffle puncture through the device and a possible decrease in available locations for safe and successful ablation.

Discussion

Preceding the widespread implementation of the arterial switch procedure in the 1980s, D-TGA was commonly palliated with atrial redirection procedures via either the Senning procedure, developed in 1957, or the Mustard procedure, developed in 1963.1,4 Although these operations significantly improved the lifespan for children born with this condition, they brought with them a variety of complications including systemic right ventricular failure, atrial arrhythmias, sudden cardiac death, baffle obstruction, and baffle leaks.1,2 The current survival rate is estimated at 65% at 40 years with arrhythmia-free survival 40% at 20 years and a median incidence of right ventricular dysfunction of 19% per cohort.7

Baffle-related complications make up 50% to 60% of the indications for reoperation, of which approximately half are baffle leaks.3,4 Akin to atrial septal defects, baffle leaks allow blood to pass from the pulmonary venous atrium to the systemic venous atrium, creating a physiologic “left-to-right” shunt that can lead to subpulmonary left ventricle volume overload.1 Throughout the cardiac cycle, there is an intermittent “right-to-left” shunt placing the patient at risk of cyanosis and paradoxical emboli.1,2

The 2025 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association Adult Congenital Heart Disease guidelines recommend regular screening for baffle leaks using echocardiography and consideration of TEE, cross-sectional imaging, and cardiac catheterization to identify targets for intervention.5 There is a class IC recommendation for baffle leak closure in symptomatic patients, and intervention is recommended for baffle leaks leading to left ventricular volume load, cyanosis, and paradoxical emboli in asymptomatic patients.5

Previously an indication for surgery, transcatheter closure of baffle leaks is now preferred given the benefits of avoiding repeat sternotomy or cardiopulmonary bypass.6 Prior studies on the efficacy of transcatheter baffle leak closure have shown that although small residual leaks are common in the short term, there is minimal periprocedural morbidity and mortality.4

Transcatheter closure is most commonly reported using the ASO devices for isolated baffle leaks, or a variety of covered stents for both isolated leaks and those with concurrent baffle limb stenosis.1,4,6 These approaches are effective; however, there is potential for device-related damage to adjacent structures and interference with valve function, which likely correlates with device stiffness. This is particularly true of the ASO and Occlutech devices, with which there are several cases of cardiac erosion and significant atrioventricular valve damage after device closure necessitating surgery.8,9 The GCA device used in this case is softer, and therefore, interactions with adjacent structures will theoretically be less troublesome.

There is a known association of atrial arrhythmia in patients after device closure of isolated patent foramen ovale in the general patient population, but there are no data on the association of baffle leak closure after atrial switch and atrial arrhythmia.10 Given that patients with an atrial switch have a reported atrial arrhythmia risk of 14%, of which 83% is atrial flutter, device closure of a baffle leak may further predispose these patients to have a higher risk of atrial arrhythmia.7 Given that surgical baffle revisions carry with them a variety of risks including perioperative mortality,1 and that this patient was not interested in surgery, the approach of using a GCA device when the ASO devices are not feasible is a viable alternative to manage this condition percutaneously.

Conclusions

Here we report a novel approach to transcatheter device closure of a baffle leak in a patient with D-TGA and a Mustard procedure. Because of the proximity of her sizable defect to the atrioventricular valves, she underwent novel use of a 44-mm GCA device for a baffle leak, with a minimal increase in tricuspid valve regurgitation. In atrial switch patients with suboptimal anatomy for standard transcatheter device closure, this GCA device may provide a suitable alternative to undergoing surgical repair.

Novelty of the Submission

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first-in-human use of a GCA device in a baffle leak. This device was chosen because of the proximity of the baffle leak to the tricuspid valve and the high risk for damage from a standard stiffer ASO device in a patient desiring to avoid surgery.

Future Directions

Successful use of a GCA device for baffle leak closure provides an additional tool for percutaneous closure of baffle leaks in a complex, high morbidity population in cases where surgical or traditional transcatheter approaches are not feasible.
