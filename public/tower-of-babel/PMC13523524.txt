IL-6 (-174 G/C) and IL-10 (-1082 G/A) polymorphisms and serum levels in Sudanese patients with end-stage renal disease and overt hepatitis B infection: a pilot comparative study
Authors: Omer Boshra Mohamedsalih Shmed, Waleed Abdelateif Hussein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) undergoing maintenance hemodialysis are at increased risk for persistent hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection. Host immunogenetic variations, particularly in cytokines regulating pro- and anti-inflammatory responses, may influence viral chronicity. This study evaluated the association of IL-6 − 174G/C and IL-10 − 1082G/A polymorphisms and their corresponding serum levels in Sudanese patients with overt HBV and ESRD-HBV.MethodsA case-control study was conducted among 68 HBV-infected individuals (31 ESRD-HBV and 37 overt HBV). Genotyping was performed using sequence-specific primer PCR (SSP-PCR), and serum cytokine concentrations were measured by ELISA. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software, applying non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum and Kruskal–Wallis tests.ResultsNo significant differences were observed between groups in IL-6 serum levels (p = 0.29) or genotype frequencies (p = 0.738), nor in IL-10 genotype distribution (p = 0.194). In contrast, IL-10 serum levels were significantly higher in the ESRD-HBV group compared to the overt HBV group (p = 0.00024). A significant genotype–phenotype association for IL-10 was identified within the ESRD-HBV cohort (p = 0.018), where carriers of the GA genotype exhibited higher serum IL-10 levels compared to AA and GG genotypes. This association was not observed in the overt HBV group (p = 0.33).ConclusionNo significant variation in IL-6 or IL-10 genotype frequencies was detected between the groups. Nevertheless, the IL-10 (− 1082G/A) polymorphism was related to differences in serum IL-10 concentrations among patients with concurrent HBV infection and ESRD. However, given the limited sample size and absence of key clinical confounders, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and require validation in larger, well-characterized cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12865-026-00854-4.Keywords: ESRD, Hepatitis B, IL-10, IL-6, Polymorphism, Sudan, Genotype–phenotype

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection represents a critical global public health threat, contributing to approximately 1.1 million deaths annually due to complications such as cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [1–4]. Sudan is classified as a region of high HBsAg endemicity, with seroprevalence rates reaching as high as 26% in certain areas [3, 5]. Within this landscape, patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) undergoing hemodialysis (HD) represent a highly vulnerable cohort [6]. The dialysis process, which involves blood recycling through plastic tubing and membranes, predisposes patients to blood-borne infections; studies in Sudanese renal units have reported HBsAg prevalence rates ranging from 7.6% to 45% [6–8].

The susceptibility of ESRD patients to persistent HBV infection is largely driven by a profound immune dysfunction termed uremic hypercytokinemia [9]. The accumulation of uremic toxins and exposure to bioincompatible dialysis membranes trigger a state of chronic systemic inflammation and premature immunosenescence [9, 10]. Central to this altered immune landscape are cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and interleukin-10 (IL-10) [9]. IL-6 is a pleiotropic pro-inflammatory cytokine that modulates the hepatic response to systemic infection, while IL-10 acts as a potent anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive agent [4, 11]. Critically, elevated IL-10 levels have been linked to the development of HBV chronicity by suppressing the adaptive immune responses necessary for viral clearance [1].

The regulation of these cytokines is heavily influenced by host genetic factors, specifically single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in their promoter regions [12]. Polymorphisms such as IL-10 − 1082 AG and IL-6 − 174 GC define high or low production phenotypes, which in turn affect disease susceptibility, progression to HCC, and patient survival [1, 11, 12]. Despite the high burden of both ESRD and HBV in Sudan, the specific immunogenetic interplay between these cytokine variants and HBV infection within the Sudanese population remains poorly understood [7, 13].

This study aimed to conduct an immunogenetic analysis of IL-6 and IL-10 in Sudanese ESRD patients with HBV infection. By utilizing a case-control design and a combination of sequence-specific primer PCR (SSP-PCR) for genotyping and ELISA for cytokine quantification, we sought to elucidate the association between these genetic markers and serum cytokine levels to better understand the pathophysiology of HBV in the uremic environment.

Materials and methods

Sample size and power analysis

The sample size was relatively limited, primarily due to the restricted number of HBV-infected ESRD patients available in the study centers during the study period (N = 68). To evaluate the statistical reliability, a post-hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). For the primary comparison of serum IL-10 levels between the two groups, the study achieved a high statistical power (1-β) of 90% at a significance level (α) of 0.05. For the genotype–phenotype association within the ESRD-HBV subgroup (n = 31), the power was approximately 72%. While the study is sufficiently powered for major cytokine comparisons, the sample size was relatively limited due to the restricted number of HBV-infected ESRD patients available during the study period; as such, subgroup genetic findings should be interpreted as exploratory.

Group definitions

Overt HBV infection was defined as persistent positivity for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) for more than six months. This term is used to differentiate it from occult HBV infection (characterized by negative HBsAg and positive anti-HBc). This design allows for the exploration of cytokine profiles and genetic variations in the context of the uremic environment of ESRD versus standard chronic infection.

Inclusion criteria

Participants were adults (≥ 18 years), HBsAg positive for at least six months, and provided written informed consent. ESRD-HBV patients were required to be on maintenance hemodialysis.

Exclusion criteria

Participants were excluded if they had co-infections (HCV, HIV), autoimmune diseases, chronic inflammatory disorders, malignancy, or were receiving immunosuppressive therapy.

Clinical and methodological considerations

Due to limitations in clinical data availability, detailed virological parameters (HBV DNA, HBeAg, fibrosis staging) were not consistently available. Additionally, factors related to hemodialysis (duration, membrane type) were not fully controlled. The ‘Overt HBV’ group was defined by HBsAg positivity for 6 months without clinical evidence of renal failure. Due to resource-limited settings, consistent data for HBV DNA viral loads and liver fibrosis staging (FibroScan or biopsy) were unavailable for all participants, which is a recognized limitation in the clinical stratification of this cohort.

DNA extraction

Peripheral blood samples were collected in EDTA-containing tubes for genetic analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted from 300 µL of whole blood using the G-spin™ Total DNA Extraction Mini Kit (Intron Biotechnology, South Korea), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and integrity of the extracted DNA were assessed by electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel using 5-in-1 loading dye [14]. All DNA samples were stored at − 20 °C until further PCR analysis.

Cytokine genotyping (SSP-PCR)

Genotyping of the IL-6 (− 174G/C) and IL-10 (− 1082G/A) polymorphisms was performed using the sequence-specific primer polymerase chain reaction (SSP-PCR) technique. PCR reactions were prepared in a final volume of 20 µL using 5× FIREPOL® Master Mix (Solis BioDyne, Estonia).

For IL-10 (− 1082G/A) genotyping, a common reverse primer (5′-CAG CCG TTC CAT TTT ACT TTC-3′) was used in combination with either the G-specific forward primer (5′-TAC TAA GGC TTC TTT GGG AG-3′) or the A-specific forward primer (5′-CTA CTA AGG CTT CTT TGG GAA-3′), generating a 550 bp amplicon [14].

In the IL-6 (− 174G/C) genotyping, each reaction contained a generic antisense primer (5′-GCC TCA GAG ACA TCA CCA GTC C-3′) and one of two allele-specific sense primers: the C-allele primer (5′-CCC CTA GTT GTG TCT TGC C-3′) or the G-allele primer (5′-CCC CTA GTT GTG TCT TGC G-3′), yielding a 240 bp PCR product [15].

PCR amplification was performed using a Techne Thermal Cycler (UK). Thermal cycling conditions for IL-10 consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 58 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s [14]. For IL-6, amplification involved 35 cycles with denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55.5 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s [15]. A final extension step at 72 °C for 5 min was applied for both reactions [14, 15].

PCR products were separated by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide in 1× TBE buffer at 100 V for 30 min. 100 bp DNA ladder was used as a molecular size marker for 240 bp PCR Product and 50 bp marker for 550 bp PCR product. Both ladders were calibrated against the same internal standards to ensure consistency in size determination.

To ensure genotyping accuracy, 10% of the samples were randomly selected for duplicate analysis. While specific internal control primers (e.g., human β-globin) were not multiplexed in every reaction, the presence of clear bands in the majority of lanes and consistent results in replicates confirmed the reliability of the SSP-PCR assay.

Successful amplification of the target gene regions was confirmed by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. For the IL-6 (− 174G/C) polymorphism, a distinct 240 bp band indicated successful genotyping (Fig. 1), whereas the IL-10 (− 1082G/A) polymorphism yielded a specific 550 bp amplification product (Fig. 2).

Serum cytokine quantification (ELISA)

Serum samples were obtained from blood collected in plain (clot activator) tubes and stored at − 20 °C. For the ESRD group, samples were collected pre-hemodialysis to avoid procedural influence on cytokine concentrations. Serum levels of IL-6 and IL-10 were measured using commercial ELISA MAX™ Standard Sets (BioLegend, USA; #430501 for IL-6 and #430601 for IL-10) were used to measure serum cytokine levels. The assay standard ranges were 7.8–500 pg/mL for IL-6 and 3.9–500 pg/mL for IL-10, with intra- and inter-assay variation typically maintained below 10% according to manufacturer protocols. All samples were analyzed in duplicate to ensure analytical reproducibility, and optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.0 [16]. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize genotype and allele frequencies. Differences in genotype and allele distributions between groups were assessed using the chi-square (χ²) test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Deviation from Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) was evaluated using the chi-square test to assess the representativeness of the study population. The normality of continuous variables (cytokine levels) was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the data significantly deviated from a normal distribution (p < 0.05), non-parametric tests were applied. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for pairwise comparisons of cytokine levels between the ESRD-HBV and overt HBV groups. For comparisons involving more than two genotype groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed.

Given the exploratory nature of the cytokine association analysis and the limited number of independent loci (IL-6 and IL-10), no formal correction for multiple comparisons (e.g., Bonferroni adjustment) was applied. However, exact p-values are reported to ensure transparent interpretation. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Genotyping and frequency distribution

The genotype and allele frequencies of the IL-6 (− 174G/C) and IL-10 (− 1082G/A) polymorphisms were analyzed in patients with end-stage renal disease and HBV (ESRD-HBV, n = 31) and those with chronic overt HBV (n = 37). As shown in Table 1, no statistically significant differences were observed in either genotype or allele distributions between the two groups (p 0.05).

Interleukin-6 (− 174G/C)

For the IL-6 − 174G/C polymorphism, the GC genotype was the most frequent in the ESRD-HBV group (45.2%), while the GG genotype was most common in the Overt HBV group (45.9%) (Table 1). The CC genotype was observed at similar frequencies in both groups (16.1% vs. 16.2%, respectively). The G allele was predominant in both cohorts (0.61 in ESRD-HBV and 0.65 in overt HBV), with no significant association with disease status (p = 0.761, Table 1).

Interleukin-10 (− 1082G/A)

Regarding the IL-10 − 1082G/A polymorphism, the GG genotype was the most prevalent in both ESRD-HBV and overt HBV groups (61.3% and 56.8%, respectively), followed by the GA genotype, which was more frequent in the overt HBV group (37.8%) compared to the ESRD-HBV group (25.8%). The AA genotype was less frequent in both groups. The G allele remained dominant in both ESRD-HBV and overt HBV patients (0.74 and 0.76, respectively), with no significant difference observed between groups (Table 1).

Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium

Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium analysis demonstrated that all loci were in equilibrium in both study groups (p 0.05), except for a borderline deviation observed for the IL-10 locus in the ESRD-HBV group (p = 0.068), while all other comparisons remained within expected distributions (Table 1).

Genotype frequency distributions showed no statistically significant differences between ESRD-HBV patients and Overt HBV patients for either IL-6 (p = 0.738) or IL-10 (p = 0.194)(Table 1).

Discussion

The clinical outcome of hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is determined by complex interactions between viral factors and host immune responses, particularly cytokine-mediated pathways [13, 17]. In this context, the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines is critical for disease progression [17].

In the present study, IL-6 serum levels (p = 0.29) and genotype distributions (p = 0.738) were not significantly different between the ESRD-HBV and overt HBV groups. This indicates comparable IL-6–mediated inflammatory activity in both populations, suggesting limited involvement of IL-6 variation in distinguishing these clinical conditions [18]. This finding aligns with previous studies reporting weak or inconsistent associations between IL-6 polymorphisms and HBV persistence [19–23].

In contrast, IL-10 levels were significantly higher in the ESRD-HBV group (p = 0.00024), indicating a more immunosuppressive cytokine profile. This suggests enhanced anti-inflammatory signaling that may favor viral persistence through the suppression of antiviral immune responses [1, 17, 24, 25]. Furthermore, a significant genotype–phenotype association was observed only in the ESRD-HBV group (p = 0.018), where GA genotype carriers exhibited higher IL-10 levels [26]. The IL-10–1082 A/G polymorphism is known to influence transcriptional activity, with the GA genotype typically associated with intermediate-to-high cytokine production [2, 26, 27].

The absence of this association in the overt HBV group (p = 0.33) suggests that the functional impact of IL-10 genetic variation is context-dependent. In ESRD patients, immune dysregulation and chronic inflammatory stress may amplify the phenotypic expression of IL-10 polymorphisms, revealing a gene-environment interaction that is not evident in non-ESRD HBV infection [1, 26, 28–30].

Study limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged:

Sample Size and Statistical Power:The study population (N = 68), particularly the ESRD-HBV subgroup, was relatively small, which limits the ability to detect modest genetic effects and increases the risk of type II error. Although post-hoc analysis indicated adequate power for primary cytokine comparisons, subgroup genetic associations remain exploratory and may be susceptible to overfitting. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require validation in larger cohorts.

Sample Size and Statistical Power:

The study population (N = 68), particularly the ESRD-HBV subgroup, was relatively small, which limits the ability to detect modest genetic effects and increases the risk of type II error. Although post-hoc analysis indicated adequate power for primary cytokine comparisons, subgroup genetic associations remain exploratory and may be susceptible to overfitting. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require validation in larger cohorts.

Absence of a Healthy Control Group:The lack of a healthy, non-HBV, non-ESRD control group limits the ability to determine whether the observed cytokine alterations—particularly the elevated IL-10 levels in ESRD-HBV patients—represent disease-specific immunological changes or reflect baseline differences between chronic disease states. Inclusion of a healthy control population would provide essential baseline reference values.

Unmeasured Clinical and Virological Confounders:A major limitation is the absence of key clinical and virological variables known to influence cytokine expression and HBV persistence. These include HBV DNA viral load, HBeAg status, liver enzyme levels (ALT/AST), duration of hemodialysis, residual renal function, dialyzer membrane type, and the use of antiviral or immunosuppressive therapies. The lack of these parameters precluded adjusted analyses and limits causal inference, restricting interpretation to associative findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this preliminary study found no significant differences in the distribution of IL-6 or IL-10 genotypes between the study groups. However, an association between IL-10 (− 1082G/A) genotype and serum IL-10 levels was observed within patients with concurrent overt HBV infection and ESRD, suggesting that genetic variation may be linked to cytokine levels in this specific clinical setting. However, given the modest sample size and the absence of healthy controls and additional clinical variables, the results should be interpreted cautiously. Larger, well-designed multicenter studies are needed to confirm these observations and to clarify whether such polymorphisms have clinical relevance for disease progression, prognosis, or personalized management.
