Event Driven Clustering Algorithm
David El-Chai Ben-Ezra, Adar Tal, Daniel Brisk

Abstract
This paper introduces a novel asynchronous, event-driven algorithm for real-time detection of small event clusters in event camera data. Like other hierarchical agglomerative clustering algorithms, the algorithm detects the event clusters based on their tempo-spatial distance. However, the algorithm leverages the special asynchronous data structure of event camera, and by a sophisticated, efficient and simple decision-making, enjoys a linear complexity of O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) where n n is the events amount. In addition, the run-time of the algorithm is independent with the dimensions of the pixels array.



Key words: neuromorphic camera, event camera, hierarchical clustering,
asynchronous algorithms, detection applications.

Introduction

Event camera is a novel technology that pose a paradigm shift in visual
acquisition. Instead of sampling the field of view in a certain frame-rate,
each pixel of event camera is asynchronously independent, and reports
an “event” each time it feels a brightness change. Event cameras
can easily capture fast motions, and enjoy some built-in systemic
advantages, such as high dynamic range, and temporal resolution of
microseconds. Due to these features, event cameras can potentially
bring added value for variety of tasks, such as: detection and classification,
tracking, motion segmentation, optical flow estimation, 3D and image

In this paper we describe a simple but sophisticated asynchronous

that depends only on the amount of the events, and not on the number
of clusters or the amount of pixels of the camera. This feature is
the main advantage of the algorithm over previous asynchronous methods,
and over other classical hierarchical clustering algorithms, making
it an optimal for real-time applications. To achieve that, the algorithm
uses the special temporal ordered structure of the output data of

is able to build the clusters without “looking back” to previous
events. In particular, the algorithm does not use any binning of the
timeline, and the decision-making is made along the events loop.

The clustering algorithm is aimed to detect small signals that naturally
looks like a cluster that is developed from a certain “root” event,
and detects the root of clusters that their size surpasses a predefined
threshold. A useful feature of the algorithm is that it gives the
root of any interesting cluster immediately after surpassing a predefined
events threshold, even before completion of the clustering process.
Like other tempo-spacial clustering algorithms, the algorithm depends
on two criteria: the tempo-spatial distance between the events, and
the predefined threshold for the cluster size.

2
Data interpretation and algorithm output

We begin with a brief description of how event cameras work. Each
pixel of an event camera keeps a reference illumination value, and
when it feels a brightness change with relation to that reference,
that surpass a predefined threshold, it takes two actions:

•

Updates the reference value to the current illumination value.

•

Reports an event, that includes the following information:

–

The timestamp of the event (in resolution of microseconds).

–

The pixel coordinates that reported the event.

–

The polarity of the event, namely whether the event was triggered
by a positive change in the illumination, or a negative one.

At the end, instead of frames, the output of the camera is an asynchronous
list of events, sorted by their timestamp, of the form

v_{i}=(t_{i},x_{i},y_{i},p_{i})

where
t_{i}
is the timestamp of the event,
(x_{i},y_{i})
are
the pixel coordinates, and
p_{i}
stands for the polarity, such
that
p_{i}=1
if the event is positive, and
p_{i}=-1
if it is
negative.

The output of the algorithm can be formulated naturally by thinking
on the output of the camera, which is the input of the algorithm,
in terms of graph theory. Let
\delta>0
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, and
let’s think about the output events of the camera
V=\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}

as vertices of a directed graph
G=(V,E)
, where the set of edges

E
is given inductively on
i
by the following procedure.

Suppose that
G_{k}=(V_{k},E_{k})
is the sub-graph of
G
with
vertices
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, and consider the vertex
v_{k+1}=(t_{k+1},x_{k+1},y_{k+1},p_{k+1})
.
Then:

1.

If there exists
T=(V_{T},E_{T})
, a connected component of
G_{k}
,
such that

•

\exists v=(t,x,y,p)\in V_{T}
such that
(x,y)=(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})
;
and

•

t_{k+1}-t_{\max\left\{j\,|\,v_{j}\in V_{T}\right\}}\leq\delta
.
Then:

G_{k+1}:=(V_{k}\cup\{v_{k+1}\},E_{k}\cup\{(v_{\min\left\{i\,|\,v_{i}\in V_{T}\right\}},v_{k+1})\}).

In other words, we add the vertex
v_{k+1}
to
V_{T}
, and we connect
it to the vertex
v_{\min\left\{i\,|\,v_{i}\in V_{T}\right\}}
,
which will be called the “root” of
T
. We remark it can be shown
that if there exists a connected component
T
that satisfies the
aforementioned conditions, it is unique.

2.

Otherwise, if there exists
T=(V_{T},E_{T})
, a connected component
of
G_{k}
, such that
\exists v=(t,x,y,p)\in V_{T}
with

•

x-d\leq x_{k}\leq x+d
and
y-d\leq y_{k}\leq y+d
; and

•

t_{k}-t\leq\delta
. Then:

G_{k+1}:=(V_{k}\cup\{v_{k+1}\},E_{k}\cup\{(v_{\min\left\{i\,|\,v_{i}\in V_{T}\right\}},v_{k+1})\}).

We remark that if there exist more than one connected component
T

that satisfy the aforementioned conditions, we connect
v_{k+1}

to the root of one of them arbitrarily.

3.

Otherwise:
G_{k+1}:=(V_{k}\cup\{v_{k+1}\},E_{k})
.

Then, it is easy to see that
G
is a polyforest, that is a DAG whose
underlying undirected graph is a forest. As was described above, every
connected component polytree
T=(V_{T},E_{T})
of
G
has a vertex
that is considered as the root of
T
:

v_{root}(V_{T})=v_{\min\left\{i\,|\,v_{i}\in V_{T}\right\}}\in V_{T}.

The algorithm described below is aimed to find all the roots of polytree
clusters
T
-s such that
|V_{T}|\geq n
for a predefined threshold

n\geq 3
. In other words, the main output of the algorithm is a sub-list
of
V
, consisting of all the desired roots of the clusters.

Event cameras often have noisy and sensitive pixels that fire events
in a high rate due to internal noise, that are not necessarily related
to a real brightness change. In real-world applications, one might
want to ignore clusters that are generated randomly by such pixels.
Hence, as those pixels tend to be isolated, it is useful to consider
clusters such that the amount of contributing pixels, is higher than
2, or any other predefined threshold. As will be described below,
the algorithm is designed to tackle this problem naturally, within
the event loop.

In view of the above, the full output of the algorithm is a list,
that we will call
Clusters
, such that each row
Clusters[j]
of
the list includes the following columns:

•

Clusters[j][0]
= the timestamp of the root (which is also the beginning
timestamp of the cluster).

•

Clusters[j][1]
= the x-coordinate of the root.

•

Clusters[j][2]
= the y-coordinate of the root.

•

Clusters[j][3]
= the timestamp of the last event related to the
cluster.

•

Clusters[j][4]
= the amount of events related to the cluster.

•

Clusters[j][5]
= the amount of pixels contributing to the cluster.

Remark: In this paper we preferred to present a version of the algorithm
that output only the roots of the clusters and not the clusters themselves.
However, if it is needed for a certain application, it is easy to
add some orders that build the clusters along the event-loop as well,
without ruining the
O(n)
complexity of the algorithm. We decided
to present the current version for two reasons. The first one is that
we think that in some sense it is more innovative to show a way to
find the properties of the clusters without finding the clusters themselves.
The second reason is that in our opinion, with this version, the core
idea of the algorithm is clearer.

3
Algorithm description

3.1
Constant parameters

As was described in the introduction, the algorithm is based on two
criteria: the tempo-spatial distance between the events, and the cluster
size. It implies that the algorithm has four constant parameters,
that are not being changed through the algorithm operation:

•

\delta
= the maximal temporal distance between events that are
related to the same cluster.

•

d
= the maximal spatial distance, in pixels, between the events
in the cluster.

•

n
= the minimal number of events in a desired cluster. We assume

n\geq 3
.

•

m
= the minimal number of pixels contributing to a desired cluster.

3.2
Dynamic arrays

Let
r
and
s
be the dimensions of the pixels array. The algorithm
uses a few
r\times s
arrays, that each of their entries stores
information that is related to the pixel in the camera that corresponds
to its location in the array. These arrays are dynamic, and are being
changed through the operation of the algorithm.

Array

Initial value

Meaning

TimeSurface

0

The timestamp of the last event reported by the pixel.

PointerX

-1

Pointers to the root of the last cluster the pixel was related to.

PointerY

-1

(Pointing on
(-1,-1)
means that the pixel is not related to any
cluster.)

grade

0

The events amount of the last cluster that was rooted to the pixel.

pixels

0

The number of pixels contributing to the cluster.

ClusterBegin

0

The cluster root’s timestamp, which is the timestamp of the cluster’s
first event.

ClusterEnd

0

The timestamp of the last event rooted to the cluster.

ClusterId

-1

The serial number of the last cluster rooted to the pixel.

Compatibility

0

The beginning timestamp of the last cluster the pixel was related
to.

3.3
Algorithm block diagram

The diagram in Figure
3.1
describes the decision-making process
of the algorithm. Some of the blocks in the diagram are colored, these
blocks describe stages in the diagram that we describe later in detail.
The yellow blocks describe conditions checked by the algorithm, the
blue ones describe a simple initialization operation, and the green
ones describe operations that are relates to the output clusters generation.

Along the sections we follow after an event
v_{i}=(t_{i},x_{i},y_{i},p_{i})

that was pulled out of the algorithm input. We remark that the algorithm
does not use the polarity of the event. In the sequel, we will provide
a full explanation of the boxes in the diagram.

Figure 3.1:
Algorithm block diagram.

3.3.1
Blue boxes - pointers initialization

Initializing the pointers of the pixel
(x_{i},y_{i})
includes the
following orders:

•

PointerX(x_{i},y_{i})=PointerY(x_{i},y_{i})=-1
.

•

Compatibility(x_{i},y_{i})=0
.

3.3.2
Yellow boxes - conditions

In the block diagram, there are three yellow boxes. In this subsection
we describe the orders in each of these boxes.

In the
FIRST
yellow

box of the diagram, the algorithm
checks if the pixel
(x_{i},y_{i})
is currently pointing on a cluster
root pixel
(x,y)
, but this pixel is already a root of a new cluster.
This check is intended to prevent the mistaken connection of the event

v_{i}
to a cluster rooted by
(x,y)
, that started to develop,
after the cluster that the pixel
(x_{i},y_{i})
is currently connected
to has ended. Here are the conditions:

•

We say that the pixel
(x_{i},y_{i})
is pointing on a cluster if

PointerX(x_{i},y_{i})\geq 0.

(3.1)

When the pixel is not pointing on a cluster, the two pointers are
initialized to
-1
. Hence, this condition can be replaced by
PointerY(x_{i},y_{i})\geq 0
,
or to require that the two pointers will be higher or equal to zero.

•

We say that the pixel
(x_{i},y_{i})
is pointing on a cluster that
has already been replaced by a new one, if

Compatibility(x_{i},y_{i})\neq ClusterBegin(PointerX(x_{i},y_{i}),PointerY(x_{i},y_{i})).

In the
SECOND
yellow box, the algorithm checks if the pixel

(x_{i},y_{i})
is pointing on a cluster (similar to the condition
in Equation
3.1
), and if yes, we check whether

t_{i}-ClusterEnd(PointerX(x_{i},y_{i}),PointerY(x_{i},y_{i}))\leq\delta.

In this case we say that the event
v_{i}
belongs to the cluster
that the pixel
(x_{i},y_{i})
is pointing on.

If we got to the
THIRD
yellow box, we already know that there
exists at least one
d
-radius neighbor
(x,y)
of
(x_{i},y_{i})

that satisfies the condition
t_{i}-TimeSurface(x,y)\leq\delta
.
In this case, we do the following:

•

We choose the pixel
(x,y)
for which the distance
t_{i}-TimeSurface(x,y)

is minimal. In the very rare case in which there are more than one,
we select the pixel that is related to the last event out of the options,
or use any other arbitrary selection policy.

•

We check whether the pixel
(x,y)
is already pointing on a cluster
(see Equation
3.1
).

3.3.3
Green boxes - actions

In the diagram, there are three green boxes: in the last one, we create
a new cluster, and in the two others we update a cluster.

In case we create a
NEW CLUSTER
, we can assume that in this
stage we have a
d
-radius neighbor
(x,y)
of
(x_{i},y_{i})

such that

t_{i}-ClusterEnd(PointerX(x,y),PointerY(x,y))\leq\delta.

In addition, in this case, we already checked that
(x,y)
is not
currently connected to any cluster. In view of that, we do the following
orders:

\displaystyle PointerX(x,y)

\displaystyle:=PointerX(x_{i},y_{i}):=x

\displaystyle PointerY(x,y)

\displaystyle:=PointerY(x_{i},y_{i}):=y

\displaystyle ClusterBegin(x,y)

\displaystyle:=TimeSurface(x,y)

\displaystyle Compatibility(x,y)

\displaystyle:=Compatibility(x_{i},y_{i}):=TimeSurface(x,y)

\displaystyle ClusterEnd(x,y)

\displaystyle:=t_{i}

\displaystyle grade(x,y)

\displaystyle:=2

\displaystyle pixels(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}1&(x,y)=(x_{i},y_{i})\\
2&(x,y)\neq(x_{i},y_{i})\end{cases}

UPDATING
a cluster is more difficult, as it requires not
only to update the cluster in the dynamic arrays, but also to update
the output list
Clusters
.

We start with updating the cluster in the dynamic arrays. As we said,
there are two green boxes that describe a cluster update:

1.

In the first case, we want to connect the event
v_{i}
to the cluster
that the pixel
(x_{i},y_{i})
is already pointing on. In this case,
denote

(P,Q):=(PointerX(x_{i},y_{i}),PointerY(x_{i},y_{i}))

and implement the following orders:

\displaystyle ClusterEnd(P,Q)

\displaystyle:=t_{i}

\displaystyle grade(P,Q)

\displaystyle:=grade(P,Q)+1

2.

In the second case we attach the new pixel
(x_{i},y_{i})
to the
cluster that its
d
-radius neighbor pixel
(x,y)
is pointing on.
In Particular, we also need to update the pointers of
(x_{i},y_{i})
.
Hence, denote

(P,Q):=(PointerX(x,y),PointerY(x,y))

and implement the following orders:

\displaystyle PointerX(x_{i},y_{i})

\displaystyle:=P

\displaystyle PointerY(x_{i},y_{i})

\displaystyle:=Q

\displaystyle Compatibility(x_{i},y_{i})

\displaystyle:=Compatibility(x,y)

\displaystyle ClusterEnd(P,Q)

\displaystyle:=t_{i}

\displaystyle grade(P,Q)

\displaystyle:=grade(P,Q)+1

\displaystyle pixels(P,Q)

\displaystyle:=pixels(P,Q)+1

As mentioned above, after updating the cluster in the dynamic arrays,
we need to update it in the output list
Clusters
. This update is
done only if the following conditions are being satisfied:

•

grade(P,Q)\geq n
.

•

pixels(P,Q)\geq m
.

In particular, no cluster enters the output list
Clusters
is these
two conditions are not being satisfied with relation to its root
(P,Q)
.
If the cluster satisfies these two conditions, there are two cases
in which we update the cluster in the list:

1.

In the first case, the cluster already exists in the output list
Clusters
,
and we only need to update its components in the list. This case is
equivalent to satisfying the following two conditions

\displaystyle ClusterId(P,Q)

\displaystyle>-1

(3.2)

\displaystyle t_{i}-Clusters[ClusterId(P,Q)][3]

\displaystyle\leq\delta

In this case, we update the details of the cluster in the output list

Clusters
by implementing the following orders:

\displaystyle Clusters[ClusterId(P,Q)][3]

\displaystyle:=ClusterEnd(P,Q)

\displaystyle Clusters[ClusterId(P,Q)][4]

\displaystyle:=grade(P,Q)

\displaystyle Clusters[ClusterId(P,Q)][5]

\displaystyle:=pixels(P,Q)

2.

In the second case, we need to update the output list
Clusters

when the cluster is still does not exist in the list. This case is
equivalent to the case in which one of the conditions in Equation

3.2
is not being satisfies. In this case, we create
a new cluster in the output list
Clusters
by adding a new row to
the list, and implementing the following orders:

\displaystyle ClusterId(P,Q)

\displaystyle:=number\;of\;rows\;in\;Clusters

\displaystyle Clusters[ClusterId(P,Q)][0]

\displaystyle:=ClusterBegin(P,Q)

\displaystyle Clusters[ClusterId(P,Q)][1]

\displaystyle:=P

\displaystyle Clusters[ClusterId(P,Q)][2]

\displaystyle:=Q

\displaystyle Clusters[ClusterId(P,Q)][3]

\displaystyle:=ClusterEnd(P,Q)

\displaystyle Clusters[ClusterId(P,Q)][4]

\displaystyle:=grade(P,Q)

\displaystyle Clusters[ClusterId(P,Q)][5]

\displaystyle:=pixels(P,Q)

Demonstration

In Figure
4.1
below, we illustrate on a 3D-graph how the output
of our clustering algorithm looks like. For the demonstration we took
the signal from a previous work of the authors
[
7
]
, that
was aimed to demonstrate a biases tuning heuristic for the parameters

Figure 4.1:
The clusters of the periodic signal originated by the incandescent
lamp. The purple X-signs mark the roots of the positive events clusters

Recalling the experimental setup, we used an incandescent lamp powered
by the 50 Hz sinusoidal electrical grid, that practically gives a
100 Hz periodic signal of the form
\left|\sin(x)\right|
. Then,
the signal was measured by an EVK4-HD camera of Prophesee which was
placed a few meters from the lamp. In order to get a small signal,
the lamp was covered and a small hole was made in the cover, such
that we got only a few dozens of events per period.

In Figure
4.1
one can see the events that were triggered by
10 periods of the lamp’s signal. In the figure, orange dots correspond
to positive events that present an increase of the illumination by
the signal, and blue dots correspond to negative events. For the convenience
of the presentation, the figure presents a 3D description of the events
within a small ROI of pixels out of the 1M pixel array of the camera.

Having this data, the algorithm was run on the
positive
events
of the data, under the constant parameters:
\delta=2\,ms
,

d=1
,
n=10
and
m=5
. Then, the algorithm found the roots of
every period of the signal, which is the first positive event among
the orange clusters. Those roots are marked by a purple X-signs.

5
Conclusion

In this paper we saw how one can interpret event clusters in terms
of trees, and define there root. Then, we presented an event driven
hierarchical agglomerative clustering algorithm for the asynchronous
data of event camera. We saw, how one can leverage the special structure
of the data, and by using the idea of “time surface” and an efficient
decision making, can find the roots of small event clusters with complexity

O(n)
. At last, we demonstrated the operation of the algorithm on
the event clusters originated by a 100 Hz signal of an incandescent
lamp powered by the electrical grid.
