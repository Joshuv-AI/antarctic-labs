Planar cell polarity-directed cell crawling drives polarized epithelial morphogenesis
Authors: Rishabh Sharan, XinXin Du, Liliya Leybova, Anyoko Sewavi, Brooke K Phillips, Abhishek Biswas, Danelle Devenport
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYDuring epithelial morphogenesis, cell polarity aligns individual cell behaviors into collective motions that shape developing tissues. Here, we combine experiments with computational modeling to investigate how cell-scale forces oriented by planar cell polarity (PCP) direct the collective, counter-rotational cell flows that occur during hair placode morphogenesis. Unexpectedly, we find that junctional myosin and PCP protein localization are not co-correlated with junction shrinkage, indicating the role of PCP during placode polarization is not to direct apical neighbor exchanges. Instead, we find that PCP directs anterior-directed crawling of placode cells along the basal surface of the tissue through a mechanism that requires integrins and the cell-crawling regulator Rac1. Modeling the placode as a three-dimensional continuum viscoelastic fluid, we find that active forces from cell crawling at the basal surface are sufficient to generate the experimentally observed counter-rotational cell motion at the apical surface. Our results show an unexpected role for PCP in epithelial morphogenesis, centering the basal surface as the site of force generation.

INTRODUCTION

How cells coordinate individual behaviors to generate tissue-scale morphogenetic change is a central question in developmental biology. Cell behaviors such as shape changes, proliferation, migration, and intercalation are sources of stress that deform tissue sheets, creating bends, folds, or elongations that contribute to organ shape. In epithelia, which are polarized along both apical-basal and planar axes, cell shape changes and movements arise through the asymmetric localization of force-generating cytoskeletal networks, whose position within cells determines the magnitude and directionality of epithelial deformations. For example, apical positioning of contractile actomyosin networks constricts cells, resulting in a concave tissue bend, or invagination.1 Additionally, localized actin polymerization to one pole of the cell in the plane of the tissue promotes directed migration or tissue extension.2–4 Thus, to ensure an epithelium bends or elongates reproducibly, individual cells must polarize the force-generating machinery in coordination across the cell collective.

In classic models of epithelial tissue elongation, such as Drosophila germband extension and amniote neurulation, planar polarity systems localize actomyosin cables to anterior-posterior cell junctions, promoting cell intercalation and tissue convergence to elongate the embryonic body axis.4–8 Actomyosin cables are positioned at the apico-lateral edges of intercalating cells, suggesting that forces at the apical surface drive intercalation. However, events at the basal surface are also implicated in force generation.8–10 There, intercalating cells display crawling behaviors on their basal sides, and neighbor exchanges often initiate basally, rather than apically. These studies challenged the classic model for planar polarity-driven morphogenesis, centering the basal, rather than apical, surface as the site of tissue-scale force generation.

Mammalian hair follicles serve as useful miniorgans for deciphering how cell polarity regulates force generation during morphogenesis. Hair follicles begin as circular epithelial thickenings known as hair placodes, which consist of at least two distinct cell types, inner and outer cells, organized as a radially symmetric disc.11,12 During placode budding, stereotyped in-plane cell rearrangements reshape the placode into an epithelial bud that bends with an anterior-facing tilt (Figure 1A). We term this process “hair placode polarization,” and it results in the characteristic, uniform alignment of body hairs across the epidermal surface.13,14 Planar cell polarity (PCP) directs in-plane, counter-rotational cell motion to reposition inner cells toward the anterior end of the bud and sweep outer cells posteriorly, resulting from polarized neighbor exchanges across the hair placode11,13 (Figure 1A). In the absence of PCP function, placode cell rearrangements are reduced or randomized, resulting in hair follicles that are misaligned relative to the body axes.13–15 While it is clear that PCP is required for the stereotyped pattern of cell motion, how PCP directs the in-plane intracellular forces that drive hair placode polarization, and whether these occur apically or basally, remains unknown.

Given the junctional localization of PCP proteins, we initially hypothesized that PCP directs actomyosin to apico-lateral cell boundaries in hair placodes to promote junction remodeling and in-plane cell rearrangements. However, through fixed and live imaging of myosin during placode polarization, we show that myosin is not asymmetrically localized to PCP-enriched junctions. Additionally, although myosin enrichment correlates temporally with junction shrinkage in intercalating cells, PCP protein localization does not. Instead, through three-dimensional (3D) volumetric reconstructions and live imaging, we find that hair placode cells display oriented protrusions along their basal surfaces, suggesting that cell crawling may, in fact, be the driving force of placode polarization. Through continuum mechanics modeling, we show that cell crawling is sufficient to drive the experimentally observed counter-rotational pattern of cell motion and that non-uniform material properties within the placode and surrounding tissue allow for stronger and more robust apical counter-rotational flows. Using inhibitors targeting integrin activity and the cell-crawling regulator Rac1, we show that cell crawling is required, indeed, for hair placode polarization. We conclude that the function of epidermal PCP proteins is to direct cell crawling of placode cells toward the anterior, resulting in the collective cell rearrangements that accompany hair placode polarization.

RESULTS

Myosin does not correlate with PCP protein localization at cell junctions during hair placode polarization

Cell intercalation in epithelial tissues is often driven by polarized actomyosin-based junction contraction.2,4–8,16 Therefore, we initially hypothesized that PCP proteins may promote neighbor exchange in hair placodes by directing myosin contractility to PCP-enriched junctions. If our hypothesis is correct, myosin is expected to exhibit polarized localization or activity at PCP-enriched anterior-posterior junctions (also referred to as vertical junctions), as observed in chick and Xenopus neural tube closure.6,7 Furthermore, we would expect PCP protein localization to correlate with both myosin localization and junction shrinkage.

To visualize myosin localization, we utilized a transgenic mouse strain in which GFP is endogenously fused to the N terminus of non-muscle myosin heavy chain II-B (Myosin IIB-GFP)17 and quantified its asymmetry at placode cell edges co-labeled with Celsr1 to visualize PCP protein localization14 and E-cadherin to mark epithelial junctions.18–21 As expected, and in contrast with E-cadherin, which is uniformly distributed, Celsr1 displayed asymmetric localization and was enriched along vertical junctions14 (Figures 1B and 1D). Surprisingly, Myosin IIB-GFP was not asymmetrically distributed to vertical junctions and was uniformly distributed around the cell membrane (Figure 1C). Additionally, the distribution of activated myosin II marked by phosphorylated myosin light chain (p-MLC) was not planar polarized, nor did it co-localize with Celsr1 (Figure S1). Thus, unlike Celsr1 and the other core PCP proteins,14,22 non-muscle myosin II is not planar polarized in the placode epithelium.

We then considered the possibility that PCP proteins and myosin dynamics might correlate specifically with junction remodeling, as previously observed during Xenopus neural tube closure.6 To test whether PCP proteins and myosin dynamics correlate with junction shrinkage, time-lapse imaging was performed on live skin explants from mouse embryos expressing Myosin IIB-GFP or Fz6-3xGFP together with membrane-tdTo-mato (mTomato),17,22,23 which enabled us to simultaneously visualize cell edges together with myosin or a PCP protein as hair placode cells undergo neighbor exchanges (Myosin IIB-GFP; mTomato and Fz6-3xGFP; mTomato) (Figure S2A; Videos S1 and S2). Using the membrane channel to visualize junction behavior, we categorized a junction as stable if it was not part of a neighbor exchange or dynamic if its length underwent significant growth or shrinkage. For each junction and time point, we measured the mean Myosin IIB-GFP or Fz6-3xGFP fluorescence intensity in the junction region normalized to the average stable junction intensity for each time-lapse movie (Figures 1E, 1G, S2B, and S2C). As expected, Myosin IIB-GFP intensity was significantly higher during junction shrinkage and growth compared with stable junctions (Figure 1F). Conversely, Fz6-3xGFP intensity was similar between junction behaviors (Figure 1H). Although a statistically significant difference was observed between Fz6-3xGFP shrinking and stable junction states, the effect size was notably smaller compared with the difference between Myosin IIB-GFP groups. These results indicate that although myosin does correlate with junction dynamics, a similar correlation between junction behavior and PCP protein localization is not observed, suggesting that PCP does not direct myosin localization to dynamic junctions. As PCP is required for hair placode polarization13,14 but does not appear to do so by directing junction remodeling, we turned to investigate other potential roles for PCP during hair placode polarization.

Placode epithelial cells form anterior-directed protrusions from their basal surfaces during placode polarization

Thus far, our studies of cell rearrangements have focused on events at the apico-lateral region of cell junctions, where forces driving junction contraction are localized in other systems.2,5,7,24,25 Considering that cell movements could be driven by events positioned elsewhere along the apical-basal axis, we generated 3D reconstructions of polarizing hair placodes (Figure 2A; Video S3).11 In volumetric reconstructions of individual epithelial cells, we found that cells of polarizing placodes formed pronounced protrusions at their basal surfaces that are anterior directed, resulting in a slanted epithelial cell shape (Figure 2B; Video S4). To measure the direction and magnitude of basal surface displacement relative to the apical surface, we calculated the basal-apical offset, which we define as the difference between the x-component of the centroid for the apical (xA) and basal (xB) surfaces (Δx = xA - xB). Hence, a positive (negative) value of the offset corresponds to the basal (apical) surface being more anterior (Figure 2C). Whereas non-placode, interfollicular epithelial (IFE) cells were not strongly offset in either direction, nearly all inner cells of polarizing placodes showed an offset of the basal surface toward the anterior. Outer cells had a broad distribution of offsets (Figure 2D), consistent with previous observations that outer cells are distinctly shaped from other cells, with their apical surfaces reaching inward and above inner cells.11 Notably, inner cells were the only group with a mean offset outside of one standard deviation from 0, meaning that inner cells, and not outer cells, have a statistically significant anterior displacement of the basal relative to the apical surface (Figure 2D). These results suggest that inner cells may be crawling along the basal surface toward the anterior. From this, we hypothesize that the counter-rotational pattern of cell motion during placode polarization may be due to anterior-directed crawling of inner cells that displace surrounding outer cells toward the posterior.

Since 3D reconstructions from fixed epidermal samples capture just a snapshot of placode cell morphology, we performed live imaging of developing placodes to test whether the basal surfaces of inner cells are consistently anteriorly directed throughout polarization. From time-lapse confocal z-stacks spanning the full apical-to-basal thickness of the polarizing placode, we extracted and segmented the apical- and basal-most planes (Figure 2E, top and center rows; Video S5) and paired the apical and basal surfaces of each cell per time point (Figure 2E, bottom row). At each time point during placode polarization, the basal surfaces of many cells were more anteriorly displaced than the apical surface. To quantify this, we calculated the basal-apical offset for inner and outer cells, classified according to their distance from the placode center11 (Figure 2F; STAR Methods). Over the course of placode polarization, the median basal-apical offset of inner cells was consistently positive (basal anteriorly displaced). Conversely, the median offset of outer cells hovered around zero. Thus, anterior displacement of the basal surface in inner cells persists over the course of placode polarization, suggesting that polarized crawling by inner cells may be driving the observed counter-rotational cell flows. Moreover, these data also suggest that inner cells are likely the main force-generating cells driving polarization, while outer cells likely play a more passive role. This corroborates previous results in mutant embryos showing that loss of inner but not outer fates blocks placode cell rearrangements.11

Celsr1 is required for the formation of basal protrusions

We next asked whether the core PCP pathway is required for the formation of anterior-directed basal protrusions, given its essential role in placode polarization. To investigate this, we generated 3D reconstructions of hair placodes from embryos lacking Celsr1 (Celsr1KO/KO; mTomato), the upstream-most core PCP protein.26,27 In the absence of Celsr1 function, PCP establishment is completely abolished in the skin epidermis, and asymmetric localization of Fz6 and Vangl2, as well as hair follicle polarity, is lost.14,26,28 We found that, in contrast to cells of wild-type (WT) polarizing placodes, cells of Celsr1KO/KO placodes were relatively columnar in shape and did not form protrusions from their basal surfaces (compare Figures 2A and 2B and Videos S2 and S3 and Figures 3A and 3B and Video S6). Celsr1KO/KO placode cells displayed an inward curvature, but their orientations were radially symmetric around the placode, rather than directed toward the anterior (Figure 3B). Calculating the basal-apical offset, we found that offset values were close to zero for both inner and outer cells in a Celsr1KO/KO placode, showing that neither cell type had a significant bias for anterior-directed protrusions. This contrasts with WT polarizing placodes, where inner cells displayed a clear bias of the basal surface toward the anterior (Figure 3C). These data suggest that both the formation and orientation of basal protrusions require Celsr1 and likely PCP function more broadly.

PCP components asymmetrically localize along the basolateral surface of placode epithelial cells

Although PCP proteins are restricted to the apico-lateral region of cell junctions in Drosophila,29 in hair placodes, PCP proteins are distributed along the entire lateral surface of adjoining anterior-posterior cell edges (Figure 4A).14,22 To investigate whether opposing PCP complexes are indeed partitioned to opposite sides of placode cells at their basolateral edges, we examined Fz6 and Vangl2 localization in chimeric epidermal tissues generated by mixing embryos that co-express Fz6-3xGFP and tdTom-Vangl2 with unlabeled WT embryos (Fz6-3xGFP;tdTom-Vangl2: WT chimera).27 The resulting mosaically labeled epidermis enabled us to simultaneously visualize opposing PCP components in cells surrounded by unlabeled neighbors. Similar to the surrounding IFE cells, we observed that in placode cells, Fz6-3xGFP was localized posteriorly and tdTom-Vangl2 was localized anteriorly and at both apical and basal surfaces (Figure 4B). To quantify their asymmetry, we calculated the ratio of fluorescence intensity at posterior versus anterior cell edges for both Fz6-3xGFP and tdTom-Vangl2 at the apical and basal-most planes. We found that at both planes, Vangl2 was consistently polarized to the anterior edge of cells, while Fz6 was polarized to the posterior edge (Figures 4C and 4D). Thus, anterior and posterior components of the PCP complex display unipolar, planar-polarized localization all along the lateral edges of placode cells, including at the basolateral surface, where anterior-directed protrusions are observed.

3D continuum mechanics modeling shows that cell crawling is sufficient to generate apical flows

Our data thus far suggest that cell crawling by inner cells along the extracellular matrix (ECM) may be the driving force behind placode polarization. To test the hypothesis that directed cell crawling at the basal surface is sufficient to generate cell flows apically, we developed a 3D continuum mechanics model of the hair placode, focusing on in-plane deformations (Figure 5A). The mouse epidermis is a multi-layered epithelium, where the basal-most layer consists of the IFE and placode cells. Apical to those are suprabasal cells. Basal to the epidermis is the dermis, separated by a basement membrane. These tissues are modeled as a system consisting of a flat viscoelastic slab situated between two fluid layers and solid hard walls (Figure 5B).30

The viscoelastic slab (region S) represents a hair placode and the surrounding IFE cells. To model early placode polarization, we assume that the placode remains flat and does not buckle out of plane. The placode and IFE cells are modeled as an Oldroyd-B viscoelastic fluid, a continuum model derived from the coarse-graining of spring-like polymers embedded in a viscous fluid. Our description does not model individual cells. Instead, we track points embedded in the fluid that move via the velocity field. These points are initialized in a pattern of vertical hexagonal prisms, each of which represents the shape of a cell, which deforms over the course of the simulation. Since we hypothesized that inner cell crawling provides the driving force for placode polarization, we specify active, basally localized forces from only the inner cells (Figures 5C and 5D; Video S7). See STAR Methods and Methods S1 for model details.

We found that within a wide range of parameters, simulations broadly resembled the early stages of placode polarization. Specifically, while cells were initialized to be columnar, as the simulation progressed, the basal surface of cells became more anteriorly displaced compared with their apical surface, similar to confocal data (compare Figures 5E and 2B). Although we did not observe T1 transitions, this is not unexpected, as modeling neighbor exchanges may require specifying local junctional forces or forces from cell cortex rigidity.30 Our observations show that a 3D continuum description can effectively model the transmission of forces from one surface of the tissue to another.

We then explored model parameters that can affect cell shapes and patterns of motion (Video S8). Specifically, we modulated the magnitude of the active crawling force (Fx) and the viscosity of the apical fluid layer (ηA). The force Fx is of interest since it varies greatly between different migrating cell types and is unknown for placode cells. Meanwhile, ηA models how tightly placode cells adhere to the overlying suprabasal layer, the strength of which might be regulated.11,31,32 Finally, we varied H2 the thickness of the apical fluid layer, as during polarization, outer cells bend inwardly to form a canopy above inner cells,11 potentially altering this thickness.

We first performed simulations varying apical adhesion interface parameters ηA and H2 (fixing Fx). To visualize patterns of motion, we obtained velocity fields at the apical- and basal-most surfaces of the model placode. Figure 6A and Video S9 (left and right) show velocity vector fields relative to the IFE (STAR Methods) from two dimensionless parameter sets, one with low ηA = 0.05 and moderate H2 = 0.5 and the other with high ηA = 2.0 and low H2 = 0.05 (Figure 6A, left and right columns). Both simulations resulted in similar velocity patterns at the basal surface, showing vortical and anterior-ward motion (Figure 6A, bottom row). By contrast, velocities at the apical surface for the first simulation (low ηA, moderate H2) displayed counter-rotational, vortical flows, whereas apical velocities for the second simulation (high ηA, low H2) displayed nearly uniform anterior-ward flow (Figure 6A, top and middle left versus right). We visualized the vorticity of apical flow using the curl of the apical velocity field (STAR Methods). In the first simulation with notable vortical flow, we observed two clear zones of substantial vorticity in opposing directions above and below the placode midline (Figure 6B, left; Video S10, left). This contrasts with the second simulation with nearly uniform flow, where we observed that vorticity is near negligible (Figure 6B, middle; Video S10, middle). Our continuum modeling shows that altering parameters associated with the geometry and properties of the apical adhesion interface can affect the formation of vortical velocities. Moreover, we show that basal crawling forces are sufficient to generate counter-rotational apical flows.

To compare different simulations with varying parameter values, we developed three metrics, basal-apical offset (BA offset), apical shear, and apical:basal shear (A:B shear), to quantify cell shapes and flow patterns (Figure 6C; Equations S83, S84, and S85 in Methods S1 for metric definitions). This BA offset is similar to the BA offseat analyzed for experimental data in Figures 2C–2F and 3C. Apical shear describes the difference in the distance traveled by the center cell compared with the northernmost cell at the apical surface. A greater apical shear corresponds to a stronger vortical apical flow. Finally, the A:B shear is a metric for the similarity between the apical and basal vorticity, as it computes the ratio between the apical and basal shear.

We ran simulations varying Fx = 24 to 128 and ηA = 0.001 to 0.6. We used ηB = 0.2 for the viscosity of the basal fluid layer, as it is expected to be less viscous than the tissue (η = 1 from nondimensionalization). We fix H2 = 0.5, the moderate value (Figure 6A). We defined realistic parameters as those that generated simulations with metrics that fell approximately within realistic metric ranges (Figures 6D–6F and 6H–6J). In Figures 6D–6F, many simulations with realistic metric values are in the parameter space of ηA < ηB = 0.2. As ηA increases past ηB = 0.2, we see significant changes in all three metrics out of realistic value regimes. Interestingly, the observation that realistic parameters mostly satisfy ηA < ηB is consistent with measured protein binding strengths, as cell-basement membrane interactions (basal) are stronger than cell-cell interactions (apical).33–35 Examining the force Fx, we find that the BA offset strongly depends on Fx, apical shear only weakly depends on Fx, and the A:B shear ratio is nearly independent of Fx. From the BA offset plot, realistic parameter values can be found for all ranges of Fx; however, at lower Fx, the realistic parameter range of ηA is broader. If the viscoelastic relaxation time τp were known for the hair placode, we could further constrain Fx based on the time needed for the tissue to deform to the expected shape. These results show that we can simulate slanted cell shapes and counter-rotational flow consistent with experimental metrics for a sizable range of parameter values.

By visual inspection, even in simulations with notable vortical flow, the counter flow was not as prominent as observed experimentally (Figures 6A and 6B, left). This led us to investigate varying the material properties of the placode and IFE. Previous studies have found that spatial differences in tissue stiffness play a role in morphogenesis.36–40 Hence, to test the role of varying stiffness between cell types, we performed simulations where the density of Oldroyd-B polymers ϕ was spatially non-uniform (Figure 6G; Methods S1). We assigned the IFE to have high stiffness ϕ = 18 in dimensionless units, inner cells to have moderate stiffness ϕ = 9, and outer cells to have low stiffness ϕ = 1. This contrasts with our previous simulations, where ϕ = 9 everywhere (uniform stiffness). In non-uniform tissue stiffness simulations, we again observed counter-rotational surface velocities and slanted cell morphologies (Figure S3; Video S9, right). Notably, there is clear posterior-ward motion seen in both velocity fields and cell outlines (compare Figures 5D, 6A [left], S3A, and S3B).

Importantly, in the non-uniform stiffness simulations, we found two zones of strong vorticity in the apical plane that had larger magnitudes compared with those of uniform stiffness simulations (compare Figure 6B, left and right). Additionally, we observed that high vorticity was restricted to a circular region comprising the inner and outer cells surrounded by the stiffer IFE (Figure 6B, right; Video S10, right). To explore how non-uniform tissue stiffness affected apical vorticity broadly, we measured apical vorticity (STAR Methods) in simulations varying parameters Fx and ηA as before. We found that apical vorticity was higher in non-uniform stiffness simulations compared with uniform simulations (Figure 6K). Exploring the parameter space of Fx and ηA to compare uniform and non-uniform stiffness simulations for the other three metrics (Figures 6D–6F and 6H–6J), we find a significant increase in the range of realistic parameters when stiffness is non-uniform. Based on these results, we hypothesize that confinement by a stiff IFE together with softer outer cells allows the inner cells to displace the outer cells more effectively, creating a stronger counter-rotational flow. This may indicate that a non-uniform pattern of tissue stiffness is a closer representation of the actual hair placode than the uniform case. Non-uniform tissue properties could increase vorticity at the apical surface due to basal forces and may contribute to robustness in polarization.

Rac1 and integrin function are required for hair placode polarization

Given that basal crawling forces are sufficient to generate the movements associated with placode polarization in our computational model, we next asked whether cell crawling is necessary for polarization in vivo. Cells migrate on ECM substrates using integrin-mediated attachment. We therefore tested whether integrins are required for placode polarization by culturing embryonic day (E)15.5 skin explants in the α6 integrin inhibitor GoH3. Following a 22 h culture in 10 μg/mL GoH3, explants were fixed and labeled for Sox9, which marks posteriorly displaced cells of the placode following polarization (Figure 7A). Using the posterior:anterior ratio of Sox9 fluorescence intensity as a measure of placode polarity, we observed a loss of posterior-biased Sox9 localization in placodes from GoH3-treated explants (Figures 7B and 7C).

In addition to their basal cell-ECM attachments, migrating cells typically display front-rear polarity with a protrusive front and a contractile rear.41–43 At the front, lamellipodia extensions are initiated by Rac1-dependent branched actin, while rear retractions form through RhoA-dependent actomyosin contractility.44 In previous work, we showed that placode polarization requires contractile actomyosin,13 but it remains unknown whether leading edge regulators such as Rac are also required. To determine whether Rac1 activity is required for hair placode morphogenesis, we cultured E15.5 Fz6-3xGFP; mTomato skin explants in varying concentrations (10, 25, or 50 μM) of the Rac1 inhibitor NSC 23766 (Figure 7D). Whereas Fz6-3xGFP localization remained planar polarized in explants cultured with NSC 23766 (Figure S4), we observed a dose-dependent inhibition of placode polarization upon treatment with NSC 23766 (Figures 7D and 7E). In the presence of 25 or 50 μM, but not 10 μM NSC 23766, placodes that had been specified but not yet polarized at the start of inhibitor treatment failed to polarize, such that Sox9-expressing cells remained localized in rings around inner cells (Figures 7D and 7E).

To investigate whether the loss of placode polarization upon Rac1 inhibition was due to a defect in anterior-directed cell crawling, we performed live imaging of NSC 23766-treated Fz6-3xGFP; mTomato skin explants and calculated the BA offset of placode cells over a 10-h time period. In contrast to untreated controls, in which inner cells displayed median BA offset values greater than zero at all time points and were thus anterior directed, the median inner cell offset values of Rac1-inhibited placodes oscillated around zero and were therefore undirected (compare Figures 2F and 7F). These results indicate that Rac1 is required for the anterior displacement of the basal surface of inner cells. Moreover, placode polarization requires both integrin-mediated adhesion and Rac1-dependent crawling movements, consistent with the idea that crawling along the ECM could serve as the main force driving placode polarization.

DISCUSSION

The mechanisms driving cell intercalation have been broadly categorized as contraction based or crawling based.45 In contraction mode, cell intercalation occurs through polarized myosin contractility at apical junctions, leading to orientated neighbor exchanges.4,7,16,46 Conversely, in crawling mode, intercalation occurs through migratory protrusions and adhesion with neighboring cells and the ECM.47–50 As epithelial cells adhere tightly to one another, whereas mesenchymal tissues consist of more loosely connected migratory cells, these distinct modes of intercalation had been the prevailing models for epithelial and mesenchymal cell rearrangements, respectively. However, it is now clear that epithelia and mesenchymal cells can employ both modes of intercalation. In X. laevis gastrulation, contraction-based junction remodeling integrates with cell protrusions to drive neighbor exchanges in the mesoderm, a mesenchymal tissue.46,51,52 Conversely, basal crawling-like behaviors are observed in several examples of epithelial intercalation.8–10,53 It is interesting to note that early studies investigating epithelial neighbor exchanges suggested a “cortical tractoring” mechanism during neural tube closure, where the basal and lateral, not apical, surfaces were the sites of force generation.54–57 Our study, showing basal crawling of placode epithelial cells undergoing neighbor exchange, lends further support to the idea that the basal surface can be the site of force generation in epithelial morphogenesis.

Together with prior work,11,13 we propose a model for how PCP-directed basal forces drive rearrangements in the hair placode: (1) every basal epidermal cell, including the hair placode, is planar polarized, with Vangl2 localization defining the anterior and Fz6 defining the posterior face of each cell. (2) Inner cells of the hair placode, which are transcriptionally distinct from outer cells and the IFE, form PCP-directed protrusions at their basal surfaces and crawl toward the anterior. (3) As inner cells crawl basally, their apical surfaces remain adhered to a suprabasal cell cluster, causing placode cells to slant along the apical-basal axis. (4) These active crawling forces generate deformations, including in outer cells, and create mechanical stress. (5) To relieve stress, cells undergo neighbor exchanges, which likely involves myosin activity to remodel junctions.58 In this way, basally generated crawling forces and downstream apical neighbor exchanges result in the stereotyped pattern of counter-rotational flow.

Critical to this model is that basal forces ultimately result in neighbor exchanges at the apical surface. As demonstrated by our 3D continuum model of the hair placode, forces applied at the basal surface are sufficient to generate flows apically, akin to the experimentally observed counter flow by outer cells. Our model further showed how changes in the material properties of the tissue, such as the viscosity of the apical adhesive interface, greatly affected the correspondence of apical and basal flows and the formation of apical vortices. Previous studies have shown that this apical adhesion interface is indeed regulated during placode polarization, both at the protein and tissue levels. Placode cells and their overlying suprabasal cells express different classical cadherins.32,59 Additionally, outer cells form a canopy above inner cells, reducing the contact area between inner cells and the suprabasal cells.11 In line with this past work, our continuum modeling suggests that reducing inner cell adhesion to surrounding cells may be required for tissue flows during placode polarization.

Spatial differences in tissue stiffness are known to contribute to morphogenesis,36–40 and our model illustrates the importance of non-uniform material properties in placode polarization. In otherwise identical simulations, regimes where tissue stiffness was non-uniform had a larger space of realistic parameters compared with uniform stiffness and resulted in stronger posterior-ward counter flows, which were more similar to experiments.11,13 Confinement by a stiff IFE, as suggested by our model, could work in concert with a contractile ring of dermal fibroblasts that promote hair placode downgrowth.60 Thus, one prediction from the model is that differential material properties between cell types may be regulated to increase the robustness of polarization. One such possible regulation is shown by E- and P-cadherin expression in opposing radial gradients. The disruption of this gradient results in delayed cell flows.11,31,32 Additionally, outer cells are more proliferative than inner cells,61,62 a property associated with increased tissue fluidity.63,64 Different material properties have been shown to promote counter-rotational flows in gastrulating chick embryos,65,66 where a tensile supracellular actomyosin ring surrounds the epiblast.63,67 Although supracellular cables coordinate intercalation in numerous other tissues,2,5,67,68 we did not observe an equivalent structure in the hair placode.

By coupling basal crawling forces with cell polarity, stereotyped, directional tissue movement can emerge. We propose that the core PCP system establishes a front-rear polarity in each migratory cell of the placode, aligning the direction of migration with the anterior-posterior axis. In crawling cells, Rac1 typically acts at the front of the cell to generate branched actin-based lamellipodia, while in the rear, RhoA promotes actomyosin-based contractions.3,9,69–74 We have shown that Rac1, RhoA, and myosin activities are all required for hair placode polarization.13 Together with other studies on actin regulators,75,76 these findings indicate that PCP proteins likely regulate both branched actin and contractile actomyosin in the hair placode. Across model organisms, PCP components have been shown to interact directly or genetically with regulators of contractile actin77–85 and branched actin,77,78,86–89 supporting the idea of PCP-mediated Rac/Rho front-rear polarity. Notably, the Fat2-Lar planar polarity system in flies has been shown to direct front-rear migration and basal crawling during Drosophila follicle cell rotation of the egg chamber.3,90,91 We propose the core PCP system orients migration through Vangl2-dependent protrusions and Fz6-coupled contractions, providing a new avenue to study mechanisms of planar-polarized collective cell movements.

METHOD DETAILS

Production of chimeras

E15.5 Fz6-3xGFP;tdTom-Vangl2: WT chimeric embryos produced through aggregation of previously cryopreserved embryos as described in Basta et al.27 Morula stage (E2.5) embryos were used to create aggregation chimeras using protocols described in Behringer et al.100 Briefly the zona pellucida was removed from morula stage embryos by exposing the embryos (in small batches) to Acid Tyrodes solution (Sigma MR-004-D) for 5 to 15 s. As soon as the zona was removed, the morulas were moved to M2 media and washed through a series of M2 and then KSOM Embryomax drops before being moved to a previously prepared aggregation plate with one aggregation well per droplet of KSOM Embryomax. One morula from each genotype was carefully placed into each aggregation well so that the two morulas would aggregate into a chimera. Embryos were cultured overnight and assessed for aggregation and development into a blastocyst. Successfully aggregated chimeras that had developed into blastocysts (now stage E3.5) were transferred into the uterus of 2.5 dpc pseudo-pregnant CD1 females and dissected 12 days later at E15.5. Embryos were genotyped for Fz6-3xGFP and tdTomato-Vangl2 following dissection.

Whole-mount immunostaining

E15.5 embryos were dissected in PBS with Ca2+ and Mg2+ (PBS++) and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde/PBS++ for 1 hour and then washed in PBS at room temperature. Back skins were then dissected and blocked overnight at 4°C. Blocking solution consisted of 2% normal goat serum, 2% normal goat serum, 1% bovine albumin, 1% fish gelatin in PBT2 (0.02% Triton in PBS). Primary antibodies were added to blocking solution and incubated overnight at 4°C. Skins were washed and then secondary antibodies were added to PBT2. Skins were mounted in Prolong Gold or VectaShield. The following primaries were used: rabbit anti-Sox9 (1:1000, Millipore # AB5535), rat anti-E-Cadherin (1:1000, Invitrogen # 14-3249-82, clone DECMA-1), guinea pig anti-Celsr1 (1:1000, D. Devenport), rabbit anti-Myh9 (1:500, BioLegend, 909801), rabbit anti-pMRLC (phospho S19) (1:500, Abcam, ab2480), chicken anti-GFP (1:2000, Abcam, ab6556), rabbit anti-RFP (1:200, Rockland 600-4010379). Appropriate Alexa Fluor-488, -555, or -647 (Invitrogen) were used at 1:2000. Hoechst (Invitrogen #H1399) was used at 1 μg/mL with secondary. Z-stack images were acquired on an inverted Nikon A1R or A1R-Si confocal microscope controlled by NIS Elements software using a Plan Apo 60/1.4NA, Plan Apo 100/1.45NA oil immersion objective.

Live imaging

Live imaging was performed as previously described.13 A 1% agarose gel with F-media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum was used for culture. Back skins were then dissected from E15.5 embryos and placed on the agar pad. The gel was sandwiched on a 35mm lummox dish (Starsted, product number: 94.6077.331) and imaged with an inverted Nikon inverted CSU-W1 SoRa Spinning Disk with Perfect Focus using a Plan Apo 20/0.75 NA air objective with a 2.8x zoom lens added to the light path. Explants were kept in a humid chamber with 5% CO2 at 37°C over the course of imaging. Timepoints were acquired every 20 minutes with a 2 μm step size for a total of 13-15 planes for at least 14 hours. Images were processed using NIS Elements and ImageJ. The MultiStackReg ImageJ plugin101 was used to correct for x-y drift using the dermis as a reference point. Positions were rotated using the average angle of guard hairs surrounding the placode of interest, to ensure the anterior was to the left. A single z-plane movie was created by following either the apical-most or basal-most surface of the placode through time.

3-D continuum mechanics model

Here we briefly describe the model and its implementation. See Methods S1 for more details. The viscoelastic slab (region S) represents a hair placode and the surrounding IFE cells. The apical fluid layer (region A) represents the apical adhesion interface between the placode cells and the suprabasal cells. The apical hard wall corresponds to the bulk of the suprabasal layer that remains static during placode polarization. The basal fluid layer (region B) represents the adhesion interface between the basal surface of the placode/IFE cells and the underlying basement membrane. The basal hard wall represents the basement membrane itself, which resists motion. Note that both hard walls represent the point where no movement occurs during the early stages of placode polarization, as we have not observed significant in-plane deformations within either the suprabasal cells or the basement membrane. We estimated that the thickness of region A (H2) is ~0.2-2 μm and the thickness of region B (H) is ~0.5 μm from confocal and electron microscopy.102

We use nondimensionalization to render our parameters dimensionless. To nondimensionalize our model equations, we rescale time by the relaxation time of the Oldroyd-B polymers τ = τp, length l0 by the radius of a typical placode cell (~4 μm), and force by a force scale F0 that is proportional to the viscosity of the tissue η; that is, F0=ηℓ02τp. Following the nondimensionalization procedure, our parameters become dimensionless. We model the sheetlike epidermis as infinitely periodic in the x and y directions while having a finite thickness in the z direction.

Hexagonal prisms initiated near the center of the simulation domain represent the inner cells while the surrounding hexagonal prisms represent the outer cells. The region surrounding the outer cells represents the IFE, where we do not track points. Cell shapes and positions are used to specify the location and profile of forces. Cell crawling in our model is prescribed based on findings in lamella-based migration. As such, we model crawling as phases of anterior-directed forces in the anterior half of a cell, followed by anterior-directed forces in the posterior half of a cell. We implement crawling forces as monopoles, since protrusive and contractile machinery are anchored to the basement membrane, assumed to be rigid. Hence, the force that the cell applies to the basement membrane is not modeled. Forward force at the front occurs through actin polymerization driving protrusions, while forward force at the rear occurs through myosin motors contracting actin filaments (Figure 5C). See Methods S1 for more details on force implementation.

Simulations were performed in a custom Python code (Python 3.10) using the Dedalus package to solve partial differential equations.96 Force field Fx, and solutions for ϕ (density of Oldroyd-B polymers) and u (velocity field) are output from simulation every 10 iterations. Cell outlines consist of material points that track fluid flow. These points are moved every iteration by the interpolated velocity95 using the second-order Adams-Bashforth method. Coordinates of tracked points are output every 10 iterations.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Inhibitor treatment quantification

Hair placode polarity was quantified by a custom MATLAB script. Briefly, hair follicles and placodes were identified by thresholding on Sox9 channel intensity. A bounding box was then drawn around each placode. Polarity was calculated as the ratio of average Sox9 intensity on the posterior half of the bounding box and the anterior half.

Image segmentation and polarity analysis

Segmentation of hair placode cells of fixed and live images was performed using Cell Pose21 with the membrane-tdTomato, membrane-eGFP, or E-cadherin channels. Segmentation masks were hand corrected in ImageJ using the Tissue Analyzer V2 plugin.19

Polarity analysis was performed using Tissue Analyzer V2 in ImageJ19 or QuantifyPolarity.94 Tissue Analyzer was used to calculate the axis and magnitude of polarity of Celsr1, E-cadherin, Myosin IIB-GFP, and Fz6-3xGFP. Polarity measurements were calculated using QuantifyPolarity for p-MRLC was performed using the PCA method, due to the punctate distribution. Circular histograms plotting the data were generated using MATLAB,97 with average polarity magnitude indicating the angle and strength of polarity.20 Polarity magnitude values from QuantifyPolarity and Tissue Analyzer V2 are normalized differently and cannot be compared directly.

Junction analysis

Junction analysis was performed using ImageJ. Junctions of interest from time-lapse images were identified as either stable or dynamic using the membrane-tdTomato channel. Stable junctions were defined as junctions where length remained approximately consistent and was not associated with a neighbor exchange event for at least six consecutive time points (120 minutes). Dynamic junctions were defined as junctions associated with junction shrinkage to a vertex or a complete neighbor exchange. Junctions were then tracked for at least six frames (120 minutes) by drawing a rectangular ROI around the junction at each time point. Mean fluorescence intensity of either Fz6-3xGFP or Myosin IIB-GFP and junction length were measured for each time point. Measurements were then further processed using R 4.2.2.98 For each dynamic junction, time points were binned into shrinking or growing by calculating the difference in junction length between a given timepoint and the previous one. Mean intensity values for each junction and timepoint were then normalized by the mean intensity of stable junctions from the corresponding movie. Statistical significance was tested using the unpaired Mann-Whitney U-test. Plot was generated using ggplot2.103

Apical-basal tracking

From 3-D time-lapse images (13-15 z-planes) of a placode from a Fz6-3xGFP; mTmG embryo, the apical and basal most planes of the hair placode were identified for each timepoint. These planes were then made into two time-series, following the apical or basal surface until the basal-most surface was no longer in view. Each timepoint was then segmented based on membrane-tdTomato using CellPose21 and hand corrected using Tissue Analyzer V2.19 A custom MATLAB97 applet was designed to allow for matching cells in both movies by hand. Inner cells were defined as cells whose centroid is within 15 μm of placode center. Outer cells were defined as cells whose centroid is greater than 15 μm of placode center.11 Basal-Apical Offset over time plots were generated using MATLAB.

3-D fixed reconstruction

3-D reconstructions of the hair placode were generated as previous described.11 2D masks were generated using CellPose21 from membrane-tdTomato or stained E-cadherin and hand-corrected for each frame in the z-stack (z-step of 0.25 μm or 0.5) using Tissue Analyzer V2.19 The cells were first identified in each of the frames using the MATLAB97 function bwlabeln. The regionprops function was then used to obtain the centroid of the cells in each z-slice. We implemented a distance-based heuristic algorithm to connect the centroids of the cells across the z-slices and create the 3-D track of each cell. These centroid-connected cells were color-coded with the same color across frames, labeled and viewed in MATLAB’s sliceViewer for manual verification and correction. To produce a smooth 3-D reconstruction of the cells and the placode, we added 5 (if z-step was 0.25 μm) or 10 (if z-step was 0.5 μm) interpolated slices between each consecutive real slice of the z-stack. The interpolated slices smoothly bridge the gap between the shape changes of each cell as they are tracked from frame to frame. The real data and interpolated slices were merged, generating a 3-D matrix. The 3-D view was constructed by plotting the isosurface of each cell. Each cell was assigned to a rainbow color based on distance from the centroid of the cell to the center of the placode/IFE region. Basal-Apical Offset for each cell was calculated as the x-component of the centroid of the apical minus that of the basal surface.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and graphical representations were performed Prism 10 (GraphPad). All statistical details of experiments can be found in the figure legends, including the statistical tests used, exact p vales, exact value of n, what n represents, and dispersion and precision measures (e.g., mean, median, SD, SEM, confidence intervals). Significance was defined as p<0.01.

Quantification of 3-D continuum simulations

Basal-Apical Offset (BA Offset), Apical Shear, Apical:Basal Shear Ratio (A:B Shear), and Apical Vorticity were calculated using a custom MATLAB script.97 An A:B Shear close to 1 corresponds to having minimal difference between apical and basal flows. Typically in simulations, the A:B Shear is less than 1, as counter-rotational flow is typically weaker at the apical surface. All metrics are computed when the central inner cell reaches position x = −6. This corresponds to the center cell of the placode having traveled ~24 μm along the AP axis, consistent with the average displacement observed over ~10 hours during an average time-lapse of hair placode polarization (Figure 2E). Cell flow velocities relative to the IFE were obtained from taking the raw velocity and subtracting out the average motion at the northernmost and southernmost edges. This gives the ability to examine relative motion within the placode, rather than the overall anterior-ward movement. See Methods S1 for details of metric calculations.

Vorticity (∇ × u) of apical and basal surface velocities were calculated as the curl of the velocity field in Python. Large absolute values of vorticity correspond to more vortical flows, negative values correspond to clockwise flow, and positive values correspond to counterclockwise flow. Vorticity fields were averaged over a time interval of t = 0.5 in a MATLAB script, termed 〈∇×u〉t. The metric Apical Vorticity for Figure 6K was calculated for each simulation as the average of the top 1% of |〈∇×u〉t|. Plots of cell outlines, phase diagrams for metrics, and density plots showing simulated fields (FX,ϕ,u〈∇×u〉t) are generated using custom Mathematica scripts.99 Color map for velocity vector direction uses a perceptually uniform color scheme.104
