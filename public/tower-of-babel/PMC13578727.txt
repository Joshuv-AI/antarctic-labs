Strength behaviour of Clayey Soil modified with processed periwinkle shell ash
Authors: Samuel Akpasam Assam, Jonah Chukwuemeka Agunwamba, Moses Udo Akpabio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The study of Periwinkle shell has continued to attract improved research around and within the people. These shells are produced worldwide on a daily basis, mainly from domestic sources, markets, restaurants, open fields, landfills, and eateries and can be harmful if disposed of indiscriminately. This study aimed to utilize processed periwinkle shell ash as a sustainable and innovative material to improve the characteristics of clay soil for stabilization purposes and to preserve pavement structure. The clay soil was collected as an undisturbed sample using an auger from Ntak Inyang in Uyo, Nigeria. The laboratory tests included the California bearing ratio test, Unconfined compressive strength test, durability test, sieve analysis and consistency limit test. Microstructural analyses such as Scanning Electron Microscope and X–Ray Diffraction were performed to identify functional groups and mineralogical changes present in the clay-PSSA admixtures. The results obtained at 5.5% stabilization showed improvements in the UCS from 392.77 kN/m2 to 460.60 kN/m2 and in the CBR from 32.07% to 61.60% and 21.00% to 46.81% for unsoaked and soaked samples respectively. The OMC increased from 15.36% to 17.02% and the MDD increased from 1.61 g/cm3 to 1.88 g/cm3 as the percentage of stabilizer increased. Based on the results, 5.5% stabilization was observed to yield optimum results for road work. Therefore, in order to protect the environment from the harmful effects of cement stabilization, there is an urgent need to replace conventional materials with locally available sustainable and economically viable alternatives. This substitution will help reduce the overreliance on cement usage worldwide. The novelty of this research lies in facilitating the hydration of CaCO3 and Ca(OH)2 compounds which leads to the formation of calcite minerals through carbonation reactions in stabilized clays during chemical processes.

Introduction

Maintaining and upgrading road structures may not always be cost effective, leading to large budgets for governments worldwide. Thus, implementing a systematic approach to lower costs and increase lifespan can significantly improve pavement systems providing comfort, safety and fulfilling their purpose1. Recent studies by Etim2 have shown that incorporating sustainable waste materials into problematic soils can significantly improve subgrade performance while reducing construction costs and environmental impacts. This is beneficial for rural road applications3. It is often challenging to find soil suitable for road construction due to low bearing capacity and strength. Therefore, it is essential to enhance the strength characteristics of the soil to withstand expected loads4,5. Recent advances in soil behaviour characterization have provided deeper insight into how material composition and physicochemical interactions govern strength development and long-term performance in geotechnical systems6.

Numerical and microstructural modelling studies have emphasized the importance of bonding, particle interaction, and cementation mechanism in controlling the mechanical response of treated geomaterials. Furthermore, recent trends in geotechnical research have highlighted the use of data driven frameworks for evaluating material degradation and performance, which complement experimental investigations7,8.

Clay soils can be improved through stabilization by altering its characteristic properties9. Compaction and the use of admixtures are ways to stabilize weak soil encountered during construction10,11. However, cement and lime stabilization are the most commonly used practices12,13. The environment we live in has made the use of cement more challenging due to the harmful effects of carbon dioxide release into the atmosphere, which can be very dangerous to human health14.

The periwinkle shell is black in colour and is typically found below the sea-bed living underwater among rocks and stones15. These shells are primary produced using traditional methods16. If these waste shells are managed as a sustainable renewable material, they can help reduce environmental pollution17,18. The ash from the shells contains a high content of calcium oxide and may precipitate in the formation process of calcite through carbonation reactions and was confirmed by19. Calcium carbonate (CaCO3) is essential component of limestone20. This local material can be fully utilized by using these shells in both construction and non-construction materials21. Previous studies19–21 have confirmed that calcium oxide is a major mineral in periwinkle shell ash. This finding, has led to the suggestion of using periwinkle shells as admixtures in composite materials for various studies22. The main components of processed periwinkle shell ash are calcium, aluminum, silica and iron23. Previous studies have confirmed that using processed periwinkle shell ash at stabilization rate of 3% to 6% did not significantly yield satisfactory results for the CBR and UCS of the soil23. This study was supported by reference24, which demonstrated the potential and effectiveness of periwinkle shells in enhancing the compressive strength of soil. An optimal strength was achieved with a replacement rate of 4.5% to 5.5%. These findings were further confirmed by references25,26. Previous research studies have also shown that processed periwinkle shell ash can be a viable alternative to cement in concrete applications. Recent research has also indicated that incorporating local stabilizers can offer cost-effective advantages in improving subgrade soil conditions27,28. The differences in the relationship between the characteristic strength of the stabilized soil will help guide future studies in addressing knowledge gaps in this field. Therefore, in order to protect the environment from the harmful effects of cement stabilization, which has been deemed hazardous to human health, there is an urgent need to replace conventional materials with locally available sustainable and economically viable alternatives. This substitution will help reduce CO2 emissions, lower energy consumption and decrease the overreliance on cement usage worldwide. The objective of this study is to investigate the potential of processed periwinkle shell ash as beneficial additive for soil stabilization in highway subgrade construction. This study aims to advocate for the utilization of periwinkle shell ash as a potential local stabilizer, to enhance the strength characteristics of soils with high plasticity index and inadequate grain size distribution, particularly in road construction and enhancement projects.

Laboratory analysis

Laboratory tests were conducted on soil samples containing different percentages of processed periwinkle shell ash in accordance with references38,39. The specimens were thoroughly mixed, and water was added for proper hydration. The geotechnical and engineering properties of the specimens were determined using the British Standard compaction energy level.

Sieve analysis

Sieve analysis was conducted to determine the particle size distribution of the soil. The laboratory procedure involved a series of sieves with increasingly smaller openings to separate soil particles according to their sizes.

Detailed procedure: The soil sample was oven-dried, then pulverized into smaller particles to ensure that 85% of the total mass of soil passed through sieve number 200. The soil sample was thoroughly mixed, weighed (approximately 500g) and recorded as the initial weight.

Sieve setup: The sieves were assembled in decreasing order of size, with the largest opening number 4 sieve at the top and the smallest number 200 sieve at the bottom. A pan was placed below the number 200 sieve to collect the mass passing through it. The sieve sizes were cleaned to ensure no particle remained in them, in order to avoid interfering with the results.

Sieving: The measured soil sample (about 500g) was carefully poured into the top sieve, and then the stack of sieves was covered with a lid to prevent soil loss during mechanical or hand shaking. The sieve stack was placed in a mechanical shaker and shaken for 10-15 minutes.

Weighing and analysis: The mass of soil retained was weighed on each sieve and the pan. Next, the weight of soil retained was calculated on each sieve by subtracting the weight of the empty sieve from the weight of the sieve with the retained soil. Finally, the percentage of mass retained on each sieve was determined by dividing the weight retained by the total weight of the sample and multiplying by 100. A graph of cumulative percentage was plotted against the particle size to create a grain size distribution curve.

Liquid limit

The laboratory procedure for a liquid limit test of soil involves preparing a soil paste, placing it in a Casagrande cup, grooving it, and dropping the cup until the groove closes. The number of drops (blows) and the corresponding moisture content are recorded. This procedure is repeated at varying moisture contents, and the results are plotted to determine the liquid limit.

Detailed procedure: Approximately 250g of air-dried soil was poured through a sieve with a mesh size of 425µm. Then, distilled water was added to the sieved soil in an evaporating dish and mixed thoroughly to create a uniform paste.

Casagrande cup preparation: The soil paste was placed in the Casagrande cup and the surface was smoothed with a spatula. The depth of the soil did not exceed 8mm.

Grooving: A grooving tool was used to cut through the center of the soil paste, creating approximately 8mm deep, sharp groove. The number of blows required for the groove to close was then recorded.

Moisture content determination: The soil sample was taken from the Casagrande cup for moisture content determination, and the process was repeated approximately four times with varying moisture contents ranging between 10 and 4 blows.

Plotting and analysis: The percentage of moisture content was plotted against the number of blows on a semi-logarithmic graph. A line of best -fit was drawn through the plotted points. The liquid limit is the water content that corresponds to 25 blows.

Plastic limit

The plastic limit test determines the moisture content at which soil transitions from a plastic state to a solid state. It is conducted by manually rolling a soil sample into a thread until it crumbles.

Detailed procedure: The soil sample was mixed thoroughly until it reached the desired consistency.

Rolling: The soil was rolled between the fingers to maintain a smooth constant pressure rate of about 80-90 strokes per minute. The moisture content was determined at which the thread crumbles when rolled to a 3 mm thickness.

Plasticity index

The plasticity index test was determined by measuring the difference between the liquid limit (LL) and plastic limit (PL). The liquid limit was determined using the Casagrande method, followed by performing a thread rolling test to determine the PL, and finally calculating the plasticity index (PI) as PI = LL – PL.

Compaction test

The Proctor compaction test is a laboratory procedure used to determine the relationship between soil moisture content and its dry density. It involves compacting soil samples in a standard mould using controlled energy level, followed by measuring the density by adjusting its moisture content.

Detailed procedure: The soil sample was air-dried, then pulverized to ensure uniformity before conducting the test.

Moisture content adjustment: Water was added to the soil sample in increments, to achieve various levels of moisture content.

Compaction: The soil was placed in the compaction mould, followed by compacting each layer with 25 blows from a standard 5.5lb hammer. After compaction, the collar was removed, and any excess wet soil was trimmed, and its weight was measured.

Density calculation: The dry density of the compacted soil was calculated by dividing the dry weight by the volume of the mould. Next, the moisture content of the sample was determined by plotting the dry density versus moisture content for each sample to create a compaction curve. The peak of the compaction curve was then determined, representing the maximum dry density achievable at the corresponding optimum moisture content.

California bearing ratio

The laboratory procedure for conducting the California Bearing Ratio (CBR) test involves preparing a soil sample in a cylindrical mould, compacting it, and then penetrating it with a standard plunger to measure the force required for a specific depth of penetration. The test is carried out by applying a controlled rate of penetration, typically 1.25 mm per minute, and recording the load at different depth of penetration. The CBR value is calculated by comparing the load needed for penetration with a standard load for high-quality granular material.

Detailed procedure: The soil sample was collected in its undisturbed state. It was then sieved through a 20mm sieve to ensure uniformity. Next, the standard Proctor test was conducted to determine the optimum moisture content and maximum dry density of the soil. The soil sample was then placed in a cylindrical mould, typically 150 mm in diameter and 150 mm in height. Compaction was carried out in layers, ensuring that the sample reached the specified dry density and moisture content.

CBR value calculation: The CBR value is determined by dividing the load recorded at a specific penetration depth by the standard load for that same penetration and then multiplying by 100. The standard loads are usually 1,000 psi (6.9 MPa) at 2.5mm penetration and 1,500 psi (10.3 MPa) at 5mm penetration. The CBR value is expressed as a percentage.

Unconfined compressive test

The unconfined compressive test is the most popular method of testing soil shear strength. In this test, a cylinder of soil without lateral support is compressed until failure occurs at a constant rate of strain. The compressive load per unit area required to cause fail in the specimen is referred as the unconfined compressive strength of the soil.

Detailed procedure: The protective cover (paraffin wax) was removed from the sampling tube. Next, the sampling tube is inserted into the extractor and the plunger is pushed until a small length of sample is visible. A wire saw was used to trim the protruding sample. Then, the plunger of the extractor was pushed again until a 75 mm long sample is extracted. The sample was cut out and placed on the split sampler to prevent it from falling. Finally, about 10 to 15 g of soil was extracted from the tube for water content determination. The diameter at the top, middle, and bottom of the sample was measured, and the average was recorded.

Specific gravity test

The specific gravity test was conducted using a pycnometer, a calibrated container used to measure the volume of water displaced by the soil. The procedure involved weighing the pycnometer, then weighing it with soil, followed by weighing it with water and soil. Finally, the specific gravity was calculated.

Detailed laboratory procedure: The soil was collected, and sieved to a particle size less than 4.75mm. The sample was oven-dried at 110°C for 24 hours until a constant weight was achieved. The pycnometer was calibrated so that the water inside was at a controlled temperature of 20°C ± 5°C. The initial weight (W1) and the weight of the pycnometer to a specific mark (W2) were recorded. The oven-dried soil was added to the pycnometer and the weight (W3) was recorded. This process was repeated and the final weight was recorded (W4).

The volume of water displaced by the soil was calculated by subtracting W2 from W4 and dividing by the density of water at the measured temperature. Then, the density of the soil particles was calculated by subtracting W1 from W3 and dividing by the volume of water displaced. Finally, the specific gravity was calculated by dividing the density of the soil particles by the density of water.

Chemical analysis test

The chemical properties of the stabilizer were determined in accordance with reference40. Chemical analysis was conducted using atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS), scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) methods recommended by references40 and41, respectively.

The atomic absorption spectrometer (AAS) was utilized for the chemical property test. The first step in the test involved the digestion process during which the samples were subjected. This included taking a specific quantity of the sample and measuring out 100ml into a 125ml beaker. Then 0.5ml of nitric acid (HNO3) and 5ml of hydrochloric acid (HCl) were added. The mixture was heated to approximately 90°C, allowed to cool, and then filtered to remove particles before undergoing the atomic absorption spectrometer test. This machine was used to identify the elements present in the materials. The process involved directing a beam of light onto a chopper, passing through a flame, then through a monochromator and finally to a dial gauge. The percentage at which these elements reacted with one another was determined. For example, if copper was found in the material, the machine would provide a readout value.

The XRD test was conducted using a Goniometer Mini-Flex 300/600 at the Ministry of Science and Technology in Akwa Ibom State, Nigeria. The XRD scan slide was load into the diffractometer. The periwinkle shell was finely pulverized, and its average bulk composition was determined. A reflection transmission spinner stage with a 2θ configuration was used. The 2θ scan was programmed to start at 40 and end at 750, with a step size of 0.0251510 and a scan duration of 7.56 seconds per step. The tube was set to a current of 40mA and a voltage of 45kV. A programmable divergent slit with a 5mm width mask was applied. The intensity of the diffracted X-rays was continuously recorded as both the sample and detector rotated through their respective angles based on atomic spacing in the mineral.

Durability test

The method recommended by reference37 was used to study and evaluate the wetting and drying durability of the cured mixture samples over a period of 7 days, followed by 12 testing cycles. Each cycle lasted 24 hours with approximately 4 – 5 hours of immersion in water per cycle. The durability of the treated soil was determined by calculating the difference in strength loss during the immersion test, as confirmed by reference37. The soaked CBR was divided by the unsoaked CBR to determine the stability difference between the wetted and dried mixture samples.

The calcination of periwinkle shells, which are primarily composed of calcium carbonate (CaCO3), involves the decomposition of CaCO3 into reactive CaO. This CaO then hydrates with water to form alkaline Ca(OH)2. Calcium hydroxide subsequently precipitates in pozzolanic reactions to create cementitious compounds within the soil matrix.

It is important to note that calcite mineral, a major component of limestone is utilized in cement production48, and not all the calcium oxide in the clay will react during the chemical processes. This research introduces an intriguing innovation as an emerging alternative to cement for stabilizing clay soil, offering benefits such as low cost, serviceability, durability, and quality. While calcium carbonate is a stable compound that does not chemically react with Ca(OH)2 under normal conditions, it undergoes decomposition into reactive CaO during calcination treatment.

The effect of periwinkle shell ash on the geotechnical properties of the clay soil

Consistency limits

The effect of periwinkle ash on the liquid limit of the stabilized soil

Figure 2 shows the variations in the liquid limit in relation to the percentages of processed periwinkle shell ash. As shown in Fig. 2, the liquid limit indicated a 10.5% decrease in moisture content at 0% to 7% PPSA. At 5.5% stabilization, the decrease was 17%, indicating a stable state. This discovery exceeds findings from previous studies. A high liquid limit will inevitably result in low strength, poor performance and instability due to high moisture content. This is because soils with a high liquid limit expand and contract significantly with moisture changes, compromising the structural integrity of the road. The decrease in liquid limit is attributed to the positive impact of the pozzolanic reaction of the processed periwinkle shell ash with the clay soil, which has a higher water absorption capacity51. This means that, a 7% (high) PPSA content may lead to over-flocculation, pH imbalance, particle interference, reduced soil cohesion, strength, increase voids and disrupted soil matrix51.Fig. 2Shows the variation in liquid limit that occurs with the addition of different percentages of processed periwinkle shell ash (PPSA).

The effect of periwinkle ash on the plastic limit of the stabilized soil

Figure 3 shows that the plastic limit (PL) decreased by 32% to 22%, indicating its plastic state at 10% with the addition of 0% to 5.5% processed periwinkle shell ash. Additionally, it increased by 8.6% with 7% PPSA added content. According to the results, the plastic limit falls within the recommended range for subbase materials. However, a high plastic limit can result in low strength, poor performance, and road instability, ultimately leading to pavement failure. The decrease in the plastic limit is attributed to PPSA absorbing excess water, making the soil more prone to brittleness, cracking and shrinkage.Fig. 3Shows how the plastic limit varies with the addition of different percentages of processed periwinkle shell ash (PPSA).

The effect of periwinkle ash on the plasticity index of the stabilized soil

Figure 4 shows the variation in the plasticity index of the various ash contents. The plasticity index decreased by 9.1% as the processed periwinkle shell ash content increased from 0% to 7%. At 5.5% PPSA content the difference was 2.1%, indicating a moderate and significant result. Additionally, at 4%, 4.5% and 7% soil stabilization, the PPSA water holding capacity was reduced due to excess Ca2+ ions from the periwinkle shell ash causing flocculation, which decreased the clay water absorption capacity52. This decrease was moderate and better than the findings in reference53. The results indicate that the plasticity index was less than 12% for subgrade materials, suggesting moderate plasticity which is good for road projects. This reduction in consistency limits is attributed to the positive impact of the pozzolanic reaction of the processed periwinkle shell ash with the clay soil, which possesses a higher water absorption capacity according to reference53.Fig. 4Shows the variation in the plasticity index that occurs with the addition of different percentages of processed periwinkle shell ash (PPSA).

The maximum dry density (MDD) increased by 1.61g/cm3 to 1.73g/cm3 from 0% to 7% as shown in Fig. 5. Conclusion

1. In this study, the soaked California bearing ratio showed a significant improvement from 21% to 35.81%, while the unsoaked CBR increased from 32.07% to 41.60%. Additionally, the unconfined compressive strength improved from 392.77kN/m2 to 460.60kN/m2. The maximum dry density increased from 1.61 g/cm3 to 1.88 g/cm3, and the optimum moisture content rose from 15.36% to 17.02%. The liquid limit decreased from 50.40% to 40.40%, the plastic limit decreased from 32.00% to 29.00% and the plasticity index decreased from 18.40% to 11.40% at 5.5% processed periwinkle shell ash content. These results demonstrate a significant improvement in the CBR exceeding the recommendation for highway subbases.

2. The study showed a superior load bearing capacity of 61.6% and 46.81% for unsoaked and soaked CBR at 5.5% stabilization respectively. The use of PPSA as a local stabilizer resulted in CBR values that exceeded those of other related studies in terms of performance. SEM analysis showed that the primary minerals responsible for activating the stabilized soil in PPSA were CaCO3 and Ca(OH)2. As the mineral content increased, dryness decreased, affecting the particle orientation of the stabilized samples. This led to an increase in maximum dry density, while the plasticity index and liquid limit decreased, leading to increased material strength. Furthermore, XRD analysis showed a strong correlation between the mineral components in the shell ash, indicating that they are lighter in weight and thermally stable.

3. It was observed that a 5.5% processed periwinkle shell ash (PPSA) content can enhance the characteristic strength of soil for road construction and upgrading. This 5.5% water– to PPSA content ratio yielded the best results for the California bearing ratio and unconfined compressive strength. SEM analysis of the stabilized soil showed the formation of calcite, and a decrease in soil structure resulted in slight alterations in the functional groups of the stabilized soil sample at the 5.5% optimal ratio. The results highlighted potential influencing factors, such as grain-size distribution, soil plasticity, and the presence of cementitious compounds that caused the pore spaces to be filled with a denser clay matrix.

4. The novelty of this research lies in facilitating the hydration of CaCO3 and Ca(OH)2 compounds to improve and enhance the strength properties. This leads to the formation of calcite minerals through carbonation reactions in the stabilized clays during chemical processes. This facilitation has helped produce higher results compared to other local stabilizers for CBR and UCS values of Ntak clayey soils. The CaCO3 and Ca(OH)2 play the role of cementation in the stabilized clay and are the major influencing factors for the strength development of stabilized soil. Calcite is a significant component of limestone and is used in the production of cement.

5. This study showed significant improvement in strength. The unconfined compressive strength (UCS) value was 67% higher than in previous studies on local stabilizers. Additionally, the plasticity index decreased 7 times more than in hydrated lime stabilized soils at a dosage of 5.5%, and the performance was remarkably significant. At 4.5% and 7% soil stabilization, the PI results were moderate as recommended by the Nigeria General Specifications for Roads and Bridges. Processed periwinkle shell ash (PPSA) is considered a more sustainable alternative, because it is readily available at a lower cost and can be sourced locally, especially in Nigeria and other developing countries. The environment we live in has made the use of cement more challenging due to the harmful effects of carbon dioxide (CO2) release into the atmosphere, which can be very dangerous to human health.

6. Despite the merits of the locally processed stabilizer (PPSA), it is not without limitations. Therefore, it is important to experimentally determine the mixture design variables (such as PPSA ratio, CBR and UCS) for a specific clay. The stabilized soil did not achieve the recommended 80% durability index as suggested by British Standards for conventional materials. However, it did exceed the marginal durability index value, which is recommended for soil improvement in road construction. This allows. for field trials to be conducted in areas with limited rainfall. These potential weaknesses or shortcomings may impact the reliability, validity or generalizability of the findings.
