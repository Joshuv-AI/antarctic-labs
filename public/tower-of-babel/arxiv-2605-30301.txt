Remarkably, we show that this dimensional overhead can be entirely avoided when ‖ L ‖ ∞ 2 = O ⁡ ( 1 / d ) \|L\|_{\infty}^{2}=O(1/d) , a condition satisfied with high probability for random Lindblad operators, yielding a typical-case sample complexity of O ⁡ ( t 2 / ε ) O(t^{2}/\varepsilon) . \displaystyle\|X\|_{p}\coloneqq\left(\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[\left(XX^{{\dagger}}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

II.2
Sample-based quantum simulation

Sample-based simulation
42
;
34
;
68
;
25
aims to implement target dynamics by means of a fixed quantum channel
{\cal P}^{(n)}
acting on an arbitrary input state
\rho
and
n
identical copies of a program state
\pi
, up to an error
\varepsilon
. It may therefore be viewed as a form of
\varepsilon
-programmable quantum simulation
48
;
4
;
31
, with the key structural constraint being that the program is provided as i.i.d. copies of a single quantum state. The main quantity of interest is then the
minimum
number of program-state copies,
n
, required to simulate the dynamics for evolution time
t
with error at most
\varepsilon
in the diamond distance. This motivates the definition of sample complexity as the minimum
n
for which the target dynamics can be approximated to an accuracy of
\varepsilon
, where the minimization is taken over all
(n+1)
-partite completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) maps
25
.

Definition 1
(Sample complexity of sample-based quantum simulation)

The sample complexity of sample-based quantum simulation is defined as the minimum number
n^{\ast}_{d}(t,\varepsilon)
of program states
\pi
required to realize a quantum channel
e^{{\cal L}_{\pi}t}
, a one-parameter semi-group generated by a linear superoperator
\mathcal{L}_{\pi}
corresponding to
\pi
and acting on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
, within an error
\varepsilon
in the normalized diamond distance. Formally, the sample complexity
n^{\ast}_{d}(t,\varepsilon)
is defined as

\displaystyle n^{\ast}_{d}(t,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\inf_{\mathcal{P}^{(n)}\in\rm{CPTP}}\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\sup_{\mathcal{L}_{\pi}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathcal{P}^{(n)}\circ\mathcal{A}_{\pi^{\otimes n}}-e^{\mathcal{L}_{\pi}t}\right\|_{\diamond}\leq\varepsilon\right\},

(7)

where the appending channel
\mathcal{A}_{\pi^{\otimes n}}
for the quantum state
\kappa
on an arbitrary input state
\zeta
is defined as

\mathcal{A}_{\kappa^{\otimes n}}(\zeta)\coloneqq\zeta\otimes\kappa^{\otimes n}.

(8)

Sample-based simulation has several features that make it attractive for near-term noisy intermediate-scale (NISQ) devices. Unlike gate-based simulation, it replaces long coherent gate sequences with the preparation of i.i.d. copies of a program state, a resource that may be easier to generate and stabilize experimentally. Moreover, because the program is supplied as i.i.d. copies, the sample-based setting is compatible with purification or distillation-type preprocessing of the program resource, which can be used to boost its fidelity prior to the simulation
5
;
7
;
73
.

A further advantage is a form of quantum privacy
1
;
34
;
51
. As in quantum learning settings where useful processing can be performed without fully revealing the underlying quantum data, sample-based simulation allows one to implement dynamics without exposing a complete description of the program state. Indeed, reconstructing an unknown program state
\pi
by tomography generally requires substantially more copies than are needed simply to use
\pi
as a simulation resource. We note that the program state for simulating a general Lindbladian needs to be a
d^{2}
-dimensional quantum state, as explained in detail in Appendix
A
. Meanwhile, quantum state tomography requires at least
{\Omega}(d^{2}r/\varepsilon^{2})
copies for a
d^{2}
-dimensional rank-
r
quantum state
27
;
28
;
74
;
52
;
2
, whereas the simulation task can require fewer copies. Consequently, sample-based algorithms enable accurate simulation without fully learning the program state that encodes the dynamics.

A lower bound on sample complexity follows from Def.
1
, by comparing quantum state and channel discrimination. Here we summarize the result on the lower bound established in Ref.
(

n^{\ast}_{d}(t,\varepsilon)\geq 10^{-4}\,\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon},

for the case of dephasing Lindbladian on projected two-dimensional Hilbert space, given the condition

\mathcal{L}_{\pi}=-i[\pi,\cdot]

without changing the scale of the dynamics. The sample complexity lower bound in this case is also

;
25

;

;

Review of wave matrix Lindbladization

In the WML algorithm, we set

This can be extended to general Lindbladian dynamics with multiple dissipative and coherent terms

\widetilde{e^{\mathcal{L}\Delta}}(\rho)\coloneqq\operatorname{Tr}_{23}[e^{\mathcal{M}\Delta}(\rho\otimes\pi_{L})],

(11)

where

is the bipartite and pure program state in a
d\times d
-dimensional Hilbert space. Here, we adopt the following convention for vectorization of operators:

\displaystyle|{L}\rangle

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(L\otimes I\right)|\Gamma\rangle,

(12)

\displaystyle|\Gamma\rangle

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{d}|i\rangle|i\rangle,

(13)

and we assume that the program states are normalized, i.e.,
\left\|L\right\|_{2}=1
. Also,
{\mathcal{M}}
is a fixed Lindbladian generator independent of the target Lindbladian dynamics, given as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\omega)

\displaystyle\coloneqq M\omega M^{{\dagger}}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{M^{{\dagger}}M,\omega\right\},

(14)

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\left(I_{1}\otimes|\Gamma\rangle\!\langle\Gamma|_{23}\right)\left(\mathrm{SWAP}_{12}\otimes I_{3}\right).

(15)

Throughout the paper, we will refer to

\displaystyle M(\rho\otimes\pi_{L})M^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=L\rho L^{\dagger}\otimes\ket{\Gamma}\!\bra{\Gamma}/d,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{23}M^{\dagger}M(\rho\otimes\pi_{L})

\displaystyle=L^{\dagger}L\rho.

This leads to the fact that (

Suppose that the Lindbladian

Proof.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|e^{\mathcal{L}t}-\left(\widetilde{e^{\mathcal{L}\Delta}}\right)^{n}\right\|_{\diamond}

IV
Typical-case scaling for random Lindbladian

The sample complexity in Definition
1
requires the simulation error to be bounded from above by
\varepsilon
for every possible quantum channel
e^{\mathcal{L}_{\pi}t}
specified by
\pi
. Although such a worst-case guarantee is operationally strong, it may be overly restrictive in practice, because, in reality, physical noise models are rarely adversarial. In many situations, dissipative channels arise from complex environments whose effective descriptions resemble random operators with finite moments
58
;
9
;
21
;
37
;
59
;
16
;
46
. This motivates us to investigate typical behavior of the sample complexity when Lindbladian operators are drawn from random distributions. We therefore introduce a
relaxed
notion of sample complexity for quantum simulation, which requires the simulation error to be bounded with high probability over the choice of program state.

Definition 7
(Typical sample complexity of sample-based quantum simulation)

The typical sample complexity of sample-based simulation
\overline{n}_{d}(t,\varepsilon,\delta)
is defined as the minimum number of program states needed to realize a channel
e^{\mathcal{L}_{\pi}t}
within error
\varepsilon
, with a probability of at least
1-\delta
over a uniformly random choice of the program state
\pi
:

\overline{n}_{d}(t,\varepsilon,\delta)\coloneqq\inf_{\mathcal{P}^{(n)}\in\rm{CPTP}}\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\Pr_{\pi\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H}_{d})}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathcal{P}^{(n)}\circ\mathcal{A}_{\pi^{\otimes n}}-e^{\mathcal{L}_{\pi}t}\right\|_{\diamond}\leq\varepsilon\right]\geq 1-\delta\right\},

(42)

where
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
denotes a uniform distribution on
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
.

We note that the sample complexity in Def.
1
and the typical sample complexity in Def.
7
are related as

n^{\ast}_{d}(t,\varepsilon)\geq\overline{n}_{d}(t,\varepsilon,\delta),

(43)

where the equality holds by taking
\delta=0
. Throughout this paper, we will omit
\delta
if the implicit meaning is clear; i.e., we will abuse notation for typical sample complexity as
\overline{n}_{d}(t,\varepsilon)
if typicality is implied in the context.

Following Defs.
1
and
7
, we consider (normalized) dynamics parameterized by a program state. Since the matrix elements of the program state are those of the Kossakowski matrix that fully describes Lindbladian dynamics (see Appendix
A
for details), we can consider a distance measure on program states, instead of that on normalized Lindbladian generators. This kind of randomness, based on the state-to-dynamics relation, is common when addressing random quantum channels
9
;
47
;
21
;
37
. We therefore take the Haar-random measure as we focus on pure program states; i.e.,
\ket{L}
is a Haar-random pure state vector on
\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Equivalently,
|L\rangle
may be generated by drawing a random vector from a rotation-invariant ensemble, commonly chosen to be a vector with independent complex Gaussian entries, followed by normalization
66
;
76
;
37
. Therefore, sampling
L
from the Ginibre ensemble and normalizing it is equivalent to sampling the program state vector
\ket{L}
uniformly and identifying the coefficients of
\ket{L}
in the product basis with the matrix entries of
L
. For general dissipative Lindbladians, we can consider the induced measure on mixed states for program states
76
.

Theorem
8
establishes a high-probability upper bound on the sample complexity. Its proof relies on a concentration bound for the normalized operator norm of a Ginibre ensemble, as given in Proposition
9
.

Theorem 8
(Typical-case sample complexity of WML)

Let
d\geq 2
, and let
G\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
be a complex Ginibre matrix whose entries are independent complex Gaussian random variables with mean zero and unit variance, and define
L\coloneqq\frac{G}{\left\|G\right\|_{2}}
.
Then, for all
t\geq 0
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and
\delta\in(0,1)
, the typical case sample complexity given by Def.
7
satisfies

\overline{n}_{d}(t,\varepsilon,\delta)\leq 7\left(1+\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{d}\right)\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon}.

(44)

In particular, as the system dimension
d
increases, the correction term
\log(2/\delta)/d
vanishes asymptotically, and the sample complexity scales as

\overline{n}_{d}(t,\varepsilon,\delta)=O\!\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right).

(45)

Proof.
By Proposition
9
, the Frobenius-normalized Ginibre operator
L=G/\left\|G\right\|_{2}
satisfies, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\left\|L\right\|_{\infty}^{2}=\frac{\left\|G\right\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\left\|G\right\|_{2}^{2}}\leq\frac{16}{d}+\frac{8\log(2/\delta)}{d^{2}}.

(46)

Conditioned on this event, we apply the WML sample-complexity upper bound in Theorem
3
.
For a fixed Lindblad operator
L
, the number of WML samples sufficient to achieve diamond-distance error at most
\varepsilon
is bounded by

n_{d}(t,\varepsilon)\leq\frac{2d+3}{8}\left\|L\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon}.

(47)

Substituting the high-probability bound on
\left\|L\right\|_{\infty}^{2}
gives

\displaystyle n_{d}(t,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2d+3}{8}\left(\frac{16}{d}+\frac{8\log(2/\delta)}{d^{2}}\right)\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon}

(48)

\displaystyle=\left[4+\frac{6}{d}+\left(\frac{2}{d}+\frac{3}{d^{2}}\right)\log(2/\delta)\right]\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon}.

(49)

For
d\geq 2
, this is bounded from above by

n_{d}(t,\varepsilon)\leq 7\left(1+\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{d}\right)\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon}.

(50)

By Def.
7
, this implies

\overline{n}_{d}(t,\varepsilon,\delta)\leq 7\left(1+\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{d}\right)\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon}.

(51)

By noting that the correction term
\log(2/\delta)/d
vanishes asymptotically as
d
grows, we obtain

\overline{n}_{d}(t,\varepsilon,\delta)=O\!\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right),

(52)

which completes the proof.

Proposition 9
(Concentration of the normalized operator norm)

Let
d\geq 2
, and let
G\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
be a complex Ginibre matrix whose entries are independent complex Gaussian random variables with mean zero and unit variance, and define
L\coloneqq\frac{G}{\left\|G\right\|_{2}}
.
Then, for all
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\left\|L\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\leq\frac{16}{d}+\frac{8\log(2/\delta)}{d^{2}}.

(53)

Proof.
We estimate the normalized operator norm of the Frobenius-normalized Ginibre operator
L=G/\left\|G\right\|_{2}
.
Since
\left\|L\right\|_{\infty}^{2}=\left\|G\right\|_{\infty}^{2}/\left\|G\right\|_{2}^{2}
, it suffices to control the operator norm of
G
from above and the Frobenius norm of
G
from below.

For the numerator, we use the one-sided Gaussian concentration inequality for the operator norm of a complex Ginibre matrix.
Specifically, for every
u\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\|G\right\|_{\infty}>2\sqrt{d}+u\right)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{u^{2}}{2}\right),

(54)

which follows from the Gaussian concentration argument underlying Ref.
(
65
, Corollary 7.3.2)
.

For the denominator, we use that
\left\|G\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i,j}|G_{ij}|^{2}
. Since
G_{ij}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
, the random variables
|G_{ij}|^{2}
are independent exponential random variables with mean one. Hence
2\left\|G\right\|_{2}^{2}
is
\chi
-squared with
2d^{2}
degrees of freedom. By the Laurent–Massart
\chi
-squared inequality
(
38
, Lemma 1, Eq. (4.4))
, for every
x>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\|G\right\|_{2}^{2}<d^{2}-d\sqrt{2x}\right)\leq e^{-x}.

(55)

Equivalently, for
\eta\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\|G\right\|_{2}^{2}<(1-\eta)d^{2}\right)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{\eta^{2}d^{2}}{2}\right).

(56)

Now let us define

X(u,\eta)\coloneqq\frac{(2\sqrt{d}+u)^{2}}{(1-\eta)d^{2}}.

(57)

By the preceding two tail bounds and the union bound, for any
u\geq 0
and
\eta\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{\left\|G\right\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\left\|G\right\|_{2}^{2}}>X(u,\eta)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\|G\right\|_{\infty}>2\sqrt{d}+u\right)+\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\|G\right\|_{2}^{2}<(1-\eta)d^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{u^{2}}{2}\right)+\exp\!\left(-\frac{\eta^{2}d^{2}}{2}\right).

(58)

For a given
\delta\in(0,1)
, we choose

u=\sqrt{2\log(2/\delta)},\qquad\eta=\frac{\sqrt{2\log(2/\delta)}}{d}.

(59)

Each exponential term is then bounded by
\delta/2
, which leads to

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{\left\|G\right\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\left\|G\right\|_{2}^{2}}>X(u,\eta)\right)\leq\delta.

(60)

Suppose first that
\eta\leq 1/2
.
Then
(1-\eta)^{-1}\leq 2
, and therefore

\displaystyle X(u,\eta)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\left(2\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{2\log(2/\delta)}\right)^{2}}{d^{2}}

(61)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\left(8d+4\log(2/\delta)\right)}{d^{2}}

(62)

\displaystyle=\frac{16}{d}+\frac{8\log(2/\delta)}{d^{2}},

(63)

where we used
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
in the second inequality.
Consequently, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\left\|L\right\|_{\infty}^{2}=\frac{\left\|G\right\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\left\|G\right\|_{2}^{2}}\leq\frac{16}{d}+\frac{8\log(2/\delta)}{d^{2}}.

(64)

This completes the proof.

A similar dimension-independent typical-case scaling also holds for more general subgaussian ensembles, up to constants depending only on the subgaussian norm bound.
We include this extension in Appendix
C
.

V
Worst-case scaling under adversarial Lindbladians

Lemma
2
gives a diamond-distance error bound for WML whose leading prefactor is proportional to
d\left\|L\right\|_{\infty}^{2}
. In Section
IV
, we showed that this dimension dependence is canceled with high probability for Frobenius-normalized Ginibre Lindblad operators, since
\left\|L\right\|_{\infty}^{2}=O(1/d)
. It is therefore natural to investigate the worst-case behavior of WML, where the operator-norm contribution is of constant order. A canonical way to realize this regime is to consider low-rank Lindblad operators because
\left\|L\right\|_{\infty}^{2}=\Theta(1)
for a Frobenius-normalized rank-one operator
L
.

We establish the following dimension-dependent lower bound on the WML sample complexity for this rank-one Lindblad operator, which exhibits linear scaling with the dimension.

Theorem 10
(Lower bound for WML on an adversarial Lindbladian)

Let us define the sample complexity of the WML algorithm as

n_{\mathrm{WML}}(t,\varepsilon)\coloneqq\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\sup_{\mathcal{L}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{d})}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\widetilde{e^{\mathcal{L}t}}-e^{\mathcal{L}t}\right\|_{\diamond}\leq\varepsilon\right\},

(65)

for
d\geq 2
and all
t,\varepsilon>0
,
Then, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, the lower bound of the sample complexity of WML is given as

n_{\mathrm{WML}}(t,\varepsilon)\geq\frac{d}{32}\,\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon},

(66)

which can be obtained from an adversarial Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
with a rank-one jump operator
L\coloneqq|u\rangle\langle u|
, where
|u\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
\left\||u\rangle\right\|_{2}=1
.

Proof.

D
.

VI
Implications and applications

In this section, we explain protection against tomography of program states in sample-based simulation, related to what has been discussed previously in the context of density matrix exponentiation
34
and WML
51
;
52
. As an alternative method to sample-based Lindbladian simulation, quantum state tomography can be performed on i.i.d. copies of program states, in order to obtain an estimate
\widetilde{\pi}
of
\pi
, or equivalently, an estimate
\widetilde{L}
of
L
. We recall that the dimension of the program state
\pi
is at most
d^{2}
for
d
-dimensional Lindbladian dynamics (see Appendix
A
). Therefore, the sample complexity of program state tomography should be lower bounded by
\Omega(d^{2}r)

27
;
28
;
74
. More specifically, Ref.
(
52
, Eq. (2.84))
proved that

n=\Omega\left(\frac{d^{2}(t-\varepsilon)^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}\log(d^2t/\varepsilon)}\right)

(67)

samples are necessary to achieve
\frac{1}{2}\left\|e^{\mathcal{L}t}-e^{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}t}\right\|_{\diamond}\leq O(\varepsilon)
, where
e^{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}t}
is a channel that approximates the dynamics based on the jump operator
\tilde{L}
estimated from program state tomography. However, the sample complexity of sample-based Lindbladian simulation is typically upper bounded by
O(t^{2}/\varepsilon)
, and
O(dt^{2}/\varepsilon)
even for the worst case. Therefore, for a sufficiently large
d
, this strict gap between the Lindbladian-simulation upper bound and the program-state-tomography lower bound provides a form of quantum privacy
1
;
52
. This clear distinction between the sample complexities of simulation and tomography was not evident prior to this work, since the previously best-known sample complexity upper bound for Lindbladian simulation,
O(d^{2}t^{2}/\varepsilon)

25
, scales quadratically with the dimension.

VII
Concluding remarks

We established improved upper bounds on the sample complexity of sample-based Lindbladian simulation based on Wave Matrix Lindbladization (WML). Our main contribution was to improve the dimension dependence of the sample complexity, yielding the upper bound
n^{\ast}_{d}(t,\varepsilon)=O(d\|L\|_{\infty}^{2}t^{2}/\varepsilon)
. Our result tightens the gap between previously known upper and lower bounds while preserving the optimal
O(t^{2}/\varepsilon)
scaling inferred from a sample-based simulation framework. We further demonstrated that the dimension dependence of the sample complexity vanishes in the typical case for random Lindblad operators. In this case, we obtain the sample complexity
O(t^{2}/\varepsilon)
that does not increase by the dimension, with high probability
1-\delta
. This provides rigorous evidence that realistic noise models are typically less complex than worst-case adversarial instances and possess structural features that enable WML to achieve substantially lower sample complexity. On the other hand, we also identified an explicit case in which linear scaling of the sample complexity is unavoidable for WML, thereby revealing a strict gap between the worst- and typical-case complexity of the algorithm. Our findings strengthen the theoretical foundation of sample-based open-system simulation by providing a rigorous analysis of sample complexity and opening a new research direction toward exploring the complexity dichotomy between typical and adversarial Lindbladian dynamics.

Several directions remain open. First, it would be valuable to extend the present analysis to general Lindbladians with multiple dissipative terms and coherent Hamiltonian contributions. Second, it is natural to ask whether our newly obtained upper bounds, in the typical and worst case, match the corresponding sample-complexity lower bound. Currently available analysis on the lower bound is restricted to
d=2

(
25
, Theorem 5)
.
We conjecture that the remaining
O(d)
multiplicative gap between the WML-based upper bound and the algorithm-independent lower bound is irreducible. The intuition is that, as the operator norm of the Lindblad operator decreases, different dissipative channels become harder to distinguish, while fewer samples are required to achieve the same level of channel error. Since sample-complexity lower bounds are closely related to channel distinguishability
68
;
25
, this tradeoff suggests that the
O(d)
gap may be intrinsic. Finally, further investigation of experimentally motivated noise models may help connect these complexity-theoretic results to practical simulation strategies for near-term quantum devices, including continuous-variable quantum systems.

Appendix A
State to dynamics correspondence in sample-based Lindbladian simulation

In this section, we explain why program state for dissipative Lindbladian dynamics needs to be
d^{2}
dimensional quantum state. The general GKSL form is composed of coherent and dissipative parts
26
;
41
,

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{U}+\mathcal{L}_{D},

(68)

where the coherent part is

\mathcal{L}_{U}[\rho]=-i[H,\rho],

(69)

and the dissipative part is

\mathcal{L}_{D}[\rho]=\sum_{n,m=1}^{d^{2}-1}K_{mn}\left[F_{n}\rho F_{m}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{F_{m}^{\dagger}F_{n},\rho\}\right].

(70)

Here,
H\in\mathfrak{su}(d)
,
\{F_{n}\}_{n=1}^{d^{2}-1}
is an arbitrary (Hermitian) Hilbert-Schmidt basis on
\mathfrak{su}(d)
such that
\operatorname{Tr}(F_{n}F_{m}^{\dagger})=\delta_{mn}
, and
K
is a positive semi-definite matrix known as the “Kossakowski matrix,” or the GKS matrix.
\mathcal{L}
becomes Lindbladian if and only if there exists a positive semi-definite matrix
K
and a Hermitian matrix
H

62
;
14
;
21
. We can partially integrate the coherent parts with the dissipative parts in a unified form as

\mathcal{L}[\rho]\coloneq-i[H^{\prime},\rho]+\sum_{m,n=0}^{d^{2}-1}\Pi_{mn}\left[F_{n}\rho F_{m}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{F_{m}^{\dagger}F_{n},\rho\right\}\right]

(71)

with a coefficient matrix

\Pi=\begin{bmatrix}\Pi_{00}&-ih^{T}\\
ih&K\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0.

(72)

Here,
F_{0}
commutes with any
F_{n}
, e.g.,
F_{0}=\tfrac{\mathbb{I}_{d}}{\sqrt{d}}
, and
h
is a
(d^{2}-1)
-dimensional real vector. The positive semi-definiteness condition implies that
\Pi_{00}\geq h^{\dagger}K^{-1}h
. This suggests that there exists a quantum
program
state

\pi\coloneq\frac{1}{\operatorname{Tr}\Pi}\sum_{m,n=0}^{d^{2}-1}{\Pi_{mn}}\ket{F_n}\bra{F_m}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{d\times d})

(73)

with complete information of a dissipative Lindblad generator up to normalization. This motivates to sample-based Lindbladian simulation, as sample-based Hamiltonian simulation
42
;
34
;
68
;
25
utilize
\sigma\coloneq\frac{H-\sigma_{0}\mathbb{I}}{\operatorname{Tr}[H-\sigma_{0}\mathbb{I}]}
as a program state. Non-scalar residual coherent term,
H^{\prime}\coloneq H-\sum_{m}h_{m}F_{m}F_{0}
, should be considered when
\Pi_{00}
cannot be bounded, e.g., with separate sample-based Hamiltonian simulation and Trotterization techniques
52
. Otherwise, we can set
H^{\prime}=0
.

By diagonalizing
\Pi
, we recover a dissipative Lindbladian as linear combination of
single
dissipative terms
\mathcal{L}^{(1)}
, where

\mathcal{L}^{(1)}_{k}(\rho)\coloneqq L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},\rho\right\},

(74)

and
\Pi_{mn}=\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}u^{\ast}_{mk}u_{nk}
,
L_{k}=\sum_{m}u_{mk}F_{m}
.
The corresponding program states then become an ensemble of pure states

\pi_{L_{k}}=\ket{L_k}\bra{L_k}

(75)

with distribution
\{\gamma_{k}\}
. If a sample-based simulation of a single dissipator is achievable, then simulation of general Lindbladian dynamics is also possible, e.g., via linear combinations of unitary operations (LCU)
(
52
, Algorithm 1)
. Although the extension to general Lindbladian simulation is also feasible via direct preparation of the mixed state
\pi=\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\pi_{L_{k}}
, simulating coherent and dissipative terms simultaneously may be inefficient because
\Pi_{00}
may diverge to maintain the positive semi-definite property of the program state. From the above discussion, we focus on a single dissipative Lindbladian, without loss of generality.

Appendix B
Proof of Lemma
6

As mentioned in the main text, higher-order terms in
(M,M^{\dagger})

\displaystyle M^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}M,

(76)

\displaystyle M^{\dagger}MM^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=dM^{\dagger},

(77)

Let us define the following five linear maps acting on an arbitrary quantum state

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{0}(\rho)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\rho,

(78)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}(\rho)

\displaystyle\coloneqq M\rho M^{\dagger},

(79)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}(\rho)

Appendix C
Typical-case bound for subgaussian Lindblad operators

For completeness, we provide a subgaussian extension of the typical-case bound. Here,
\|\cdot\|_{\psi_{2}}
denotes the standard subgaussian norm, i.e.,

\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\inf\left\{t>0:\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{X^{2}/t^{2}}\right]\leq 2\right\},

(113)

and for a complex random variable
X
, we use
\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}\coloneqq\||X|\|_{\psi_{2}}
.

Theorem 11
(Subgaussian typical-case WML bound)

Let
d\geq 2
, and let
L^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
be a random matrix whose entries are independent, mean-zero, unit-variance complex subgaussian random variables satisfying
\|L^{\prime}_{ij}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K
, and define
L=L^{\prime}/\left\|L^{\prime}\right\|_{2}
.
Then there exists a constant
C_{K}>0
, depending only on the subgaussian norm bound
K
, such that for any
t\geq 0
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and
\delta\in(0,1)
, the typical case sample complexity given by Def.
7
satisfies

\overline{n}_{d}(t,\varepsilon,\delta)\leq C_{K}\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon}\left(1+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{d}\right).

(114)

In particular, for fixed
K
and
\delta
, as the system dimension
d
increases, the correction term
\log(1/\delta)/d
vanishes asymptotically, and the sample complexity scales as

\overline{n}_{d}(t,\varepsilon,\delta)=O\!\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right).

(115)

The proof of Theorem
11
relies on the following concentration bound for the normalized operator norm of a random Lindblad operator.

Proposition 12
(Subgaussian concentration of the normalized operator norm)

Let
d\geq 2
, and let
L^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
be a random matrix whose entries are independent, mean-zero, unit-variance complex subgaussian random variables satisfying
\|L^{\prime}_{ij}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K
, and define
L=L^{\prime}/\left\|L^{\prime}\right\|_{2}
.
Then there exists a constant
C_{K}>0
, depending only on the subgaussian norm bound
K
, such that for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\left\|L\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\leq C_{K}\left(\frac{1}{d}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{d^{2}}\right).

(116)

Proof of Theorem
11
.

By Theorem
3
, for each fixed Lindblad operator
L
, it suffices to take a number of WML samples bounded by

n_{d}(t,\varepsilon)\leq\frac{2d+3}{8}\left\|L\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon}\leq d\left\|L\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon}.

(117)

From Proposition
12
, we have that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\frac{\left\|L^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\left\|L^{\prime}\right\|_{2}^{2}}\leq C_{K}\left(\frac{1}{d}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{d^{2}}\right).

(118)

Substituting this bound into the preceding WML sample bound gives, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

n_{d}(t,\varepsilon)\leq C_{K}\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon}\left(1+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{d}\right).

(119)

By Def.
7
, this implies that the typical sample complexity satisfies

\overline{n}_{d}(t,\varepsilon,\delta)\leq C_{K}\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon}\left(1+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{d}\right).

(120)

As a result, for fixed
K
and
\delta
, the correction term
\log(1/\delta)/d
vanishes asymptotically as
d
grows, yielding

\overline{n}_{d}(t,\varepsilon,\delta)=O\!\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right),

(121)

which completes the proof.

Proof of Proposition
12
.

We estimate the operator norm of the Frobenius-normalized random operator
L=L^{\prime}/\|L^{\prime}\|_{2}
. Since
\left\|L\right\|_{\infty}^{2}=\frac{\|L^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\|L^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}}
, it suffices to upper bound
\|L^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
and lower bound
\|L^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}
.
For the numerator, the standard operator-norm bound for subgaussian random matrices gives universal constants
C,c>0
such that, for every
u\geq 0

(
65
, Theorem 4.4.3)
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\|L^{\prime}\|_{\infty}>CK\sqrt{d}+u\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-c\frac{u^{2}}{K^{2}}\right).

(122)

For the denominator, since the entries have unit variance,
\mathbb{E}\|L^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}=d^{2}
.
Moreover, the square of a subgaussian random variable is sub-exponential, and a standard Hanson–Wright concentration bound
29
gives a universal constant
c^{\prime}>0
such that, for every
\eta\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\|L^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}<(1-\eta)d^{2}\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-c^{\prime}\frac{\eta^{2}d^{2}}{K^{4}}\right).

(123)

Equivalently, this follows by applying the Hanson–Wright inequality to
\|L^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}=\innerproduct{L'}{L'}

(
65
, Theorem 6.2.2)
.

Let us define

X(u,\eta)=\frac{(CK\sqrt{d}+u)^{2}}{(1-\eta)d^{2}}.

(124)

By the preceding two tail bounds and the union bound, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{\|L^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\|L^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}}>X(u,\eta)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\|L^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}>CK\sqrt{d}+u\right)+\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left\|L^{\prime}\right\|_{2}^{2}<(1-\eta)d^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\!\left(-c\frac{u^{2}}{K^{2}}\right)+2\exp\!\left(-c^{\prime}\frac{\eta^{2}d^{2}}{K^{4}}\right).

(125)

For a given
\delta\in(0,1)
, choose

u=C_{1}K\sqrt{\log(4/\delta)},\qquad\eta=C_{2}K^{2}\frac{\sqrt{\log(4/\delta)}}{d},

(126)

where
C_{1},C_{2}>0
are universal constants chosen sufficiently large so that the right-hand side is at most
\delta
.
Using
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
and the bound
\frac{1}{1-\eta}\leq 2
for
\eta\leq 1/2
, we obtain

\displaystyle X(u,\eta)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(CK\sqrt{d}+u)^{2}}{d^{2}}

(127)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4C^{2}K^{2}}{d}+\frac{4u^{2}}{d^{2}}

(128)

\displaystyle\leq C_{K}\left(\frac{1}{d}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{d^{2}}\right),

(129)

where
C_{K}>0
depends only on
K
.
Therefore, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\left\|L\right\|_{\infty}^{2}=\frac{\|L^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\|L^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}}\leq C_{K}\left(\frac{1}{d}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{d^{2}}\right).

(130)

This completes the proof.

Appendix D
Proof of Proposition
10

We prove Proposition
10
by evaluating the WML error for a particular rank-one Lindblad operator and input state.
The argument proceeds in four steps.
We define the jump operator as

L\coloneqq|u\rangle\langle u|,

(131)

where
\ket{u}
is a normalized pure state, i.e.,
\langle u|u\rangle=1
. We also take the initial state
\rho=\ket{u}\bra{u}
. The exact evolution of the state under the Lindbladian leads to

\rho_{\mathrm{ex}}(t)\coloneq e^{\mathcal{L}t}[|u\rangle\langle u|],

(132)

where the WML algorithm leads to the state

(133)

D.1
Exact Lindbladian action

Since

, we have

\mathcal{L}(\ket{u}\bra{u})=L\ket{u}\bra{u}L^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L^{\dagger}L,\ket{u}\bra{u}\}=0.

(134)

Therefore, under the exact Lindbladian dynamics, the initial state remains unchanged, as

\rho_{\mathrm{ex}}(t)=\ket{u}\bra{u},

(135)

for all

.

D.2
Exact
n
-step WML state on the invariant two-dimensional space

From the analytic one-step formula for WML derived in Appendix
B
, we have

\widetilde{e^{\mathcal{L}\Delta}}(\rho)=\rho+a\,\frac{1}{2}\{\ket{u}\bra{u},\rho\}+b\,L\rho L^{\dagger}+c\,\mathcal{D}_{1}[L\rho L^{\dagger}],

(136)

where

\mathcal{D}_{1}(X)\coloneqq\frac{\mathbb{I}}{d}\operatorname{Tr}(X),

(137)

and

\displaystyle a=(e^{T\Delta})_{20}=\frac{e^{-d\Delta/2}-1}{d/2},

(138)

\displaystyle(e^{T\Delta})_{10}=-\frac{e^{-(d-1/d)\Delta}-1}{d-1/d},

\displaystyle b=\frac{2}{d^{2}-2}(e^{T\Delta})_{20}+\frac{d^{2}}{d^{2}-2}(e^{T\Delta})_{10},

\displaystyle c=-\frac{d^{2}}{d^{2}-2}\Bigl((e^{T\Delta})_{20}+(e^{T\Delta})_{10}\Bigr).

\varepsilon>0
, we have
n_{\varepsilon}\geq N_{d,t}
, and hence (
170
) implies

\frac{1}{2}\|\rho_{\mathrm{WML}}^{(n_{\varepsilon})}(t)-\rho_{\mathrm{ex}}(t)\|_{1}\geq\frac{d-1}{16}\,\frac{t^{2}}{n_{\varepsilon}}.

(175)

Combining the two inequalities, we obtain

\varepsilon\geq\frac{d-1}{16}\,\frac{t^{2}}{n_{\varepsilon}}.

(176)

Consequently, the sample complexity is lower bounded as

n_{\mathrm{WML}}(t,\varepsilon)\geq n_{\varepsilon}\geq\frac{d-1}{16}\,\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon}

(177)

for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
.
Finally, for
d\geq 2
, we have
d-1\geq d/2
. This leads to

n_{\mathrm{WML}}(t,\varepsilon)\geq\frac{d}{32}\,\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon}\qquad\text{for sufficiently small }\varepsilon>0,

(178)

which completes the proof.

Appendix E
Alternative realization of WML via Stinespring isometry

Physical realization of WML Lindbladian evolution
\mathcal{M}
may not be straightforward in quantum computers, where operations are inherently unitary. Recently, it was shown how to simulate
e^{\mathcal{M}\Delta}

(
61
, Appendices B and C)
. In this section, we propose another method that utilizes the structure of
M
.

One example that bypasses the problem is to consider the Stinespring isometry of the dynamics. Since the WML Lindbladian only contains a single jump operator
M
, a single ancillary qubit is enough to realize the Stinespring isometry. Formally, the isometry is defined as
V_{\delta,M}\coloneqq U_{\delta,M}(\mathbb{I}_{0123}\otimes\ket{0}_{a})
such that
e^{\Delta\mathcal{M}}=\operatorname{Tr}_{a}V_{\delta,M}(\penalty\ \cdot\penalty\ )V_{\delta,M}^{\dagger}
. Here,
U_{\delta,M}
is a strong evolution of a block-encoded Hamiltonian, sometimes referred to as
J-matrix

17
;
14
;
61
:

U_{\delta,M}=\exp\!\left[{-i\sqrt{\delta}\begin{bmatrix}0&M^{\dagger}\\
M&0\end{bmatrix}}\right].

(179)

Again, due to the repetitive structure of higher orders of
M
, we can exactly express the unitary with a few terms:

U_{\delta,M}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{I}-\left(\frac{1-\cos\sqrt{\delta d}}{d}\right)M^{\dagger}M&\penalty\ *\penalty\ \\
-i\frac{\sin\sqrt{\delta d}}{\sqrt{d}}M&\penalty\ *\penalty\ \end{bmatrix}.

(180)

Therefore, letting
\Delta=2\tfrac{1-\cos\sqrt{\delta d}}{d}
, the Stinespring isometry for a single step evolves the input state according to the WML Lindbladian operator up to first order:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{a}V_{\delta,M}\rho V_{\delta,M}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\rho+\Delta\mathcal{M}(\rho)+\frac{\Delta^{2}d}{4}\left(\frac{M^{\dagger}M\rho M^{\dagger}M}{d}-M\rho M^{\dagger}\right),

(181)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{23a}V_{\delta,M}\rho\otimes\pi_{L}V_{\delta,M}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\rho+\Delta\mathcal{L}(\rho)+\frac{\Delta^{2}d}{4}(\mathcal{D}_{1}-\mathcal{I})(L\rho L^{\dagger}).

(182)

We refer to Appendix
B
for the detailed derivation. Again, using the cyclic property of trace, we conclude for the Stinespring expansion,

\widetilde{e^{\mathcal{L}\Delta}}=\mathcal{I}+\Delta(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{D}_{1})\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}

(183)

for
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}[\rho]=\mathcal{L}[\rho]-\frac{\Delta d}{4}L\rho L^{\dagger}
, which satisfies

\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}-\mathcal{L}\right\|_{\diamond}\leq\frac{\Delta d}{4}\left\|L\right\|_{\infty}^{2}.

(184)

This again leads to Lemma
6
, such that the sample complexity upper bound remains
O(d\left\|L\right\|_{\infty}^{2}t^{2}/\varepsilon)
. The scaling behavior remains the same even when using a more advanced method for simulating
e^{\Delta\mathcal{M}}
, e.g., polynomial-depth approximation via direct decomposition
M
, or LCU
(
61
, Appendices B and C)
.
