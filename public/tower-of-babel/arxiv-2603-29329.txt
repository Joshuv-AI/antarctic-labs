Abstract
. We build blowing-up solutions to the critical elliptic system with Neumann boundary condition, { − Δ ​ u 1 + λ ​ u 1 = u 1 3 − β ​ u 1 ​ u 2 2 in ​ Ω , − Δ ​ u 2 + λ ​ u 2 = u 2 3 − β ​ u 1 2 ​ u 2 in ​ Ω , ∂ u 1 ∂ ν = ∂ u 2 ∂ ν = 0 , on ​ ∂ Ω , \begin{cases}-\Delta u_{1}+\lambda u_{1}=u_{1}^{3}-\beta u_{1}u_{2}^{2}&\text{in }\Omega,\\ -\Delta u_{2}+\lambda u_{2}=u_{2}^{3}-\beta u_{1}^{2}u_{2}&\text{in }\Omega,\\ \frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial\nu}=\frac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial\nu}=0,&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases} when λ > 0 \lambda>0 is sufficiently large in a competitive regime (i.e. β > 0 \beta>0 ) and in a domain Ω ⊂ ℝ 4 \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{4} with smooth protrusions.

1  Introduction

In this paper, we investigate the following critical elliptic system with Neumann boundary conditions:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u_{1}+\lambda u_{1}=u_{1}^{3}-\beta u_{1}u_{2}^{2}&\text{in }\Omega,\\
-\Delta u_{2}+\lambda u_{2}=u_{2}^{3}-\beta u_{1}^{2}u_{2}&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\partial_{\nu}u_{1}=\partial_{\nu}u_{2}=0,&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
is a smooth bounded domain,
\lambda>0
is a large parameter, and
\beta
is a real coupling constant.

Systems of this type arise naturally in the study of coupled Schrödinger equations and appear in various contexts, such as nonlinear optics and Bose–Einstein condensates (see, for instance,
[
7
,
9
]
). Our primary interest lies in the construction of boundary blow-up solutions to (
1.1
) by means of the Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction method.

A classical prototype for (
1.1
) is the scalar Neumann problem:

(1.2)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u+\lambda u=u^{q},\quad u>0,&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\partial_{\nu}u=0,&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
q\in(1,\frac{N+2}{N-2}]
if
N\geq 3
, or
q>1
if
N=1,2
. Problem (
1.2
) is closely related to activator–inhibitor systems and pattern formation theories; we refer the reader to
[
12
]
and the references therein for a detailed overview.

The subcritical case (
q<\frac{N+2}{N-2}
if
N\geq 3
) has been extensively studied, leading to a vast literature concerning the existence, multiplicity, and concentration behavior of solutions. An exhaustive list of these results can be found in the Ni’s survey
[
12
]
.

In contrast, the critical case
q=\frac{N+2}{N-2}
poses significant challenges due to the inherent lack of compactness. Adimurthi and Mancini
[
2
]
, along with Wang
[
21
]
, established foundational existence results for large
\lambda
. Further insights into symmetry and singular behavior were provided in
[
8
,
11
]
. The role of boundary geometry and mean curvature was further elucidated in
[
3
,
1
,
4
,
23
]
, while high-energy and multi-peaked solutions were explored in
[
22
,
24
]
.

Notably, Rey
[
16
]
constructed multi-peak solutions in dimensions
N\geq 6
, showing that distinct non-degenerate critical points of the mean curvature with positive values can generate solutions that blow up at these points as
\lambda\to\infty
. The lower-dimensional cases (
N=3,4,5
) are considerably more delicate. As pointed out by Rey in
[
17
]
, constructing solutions that blow up at a single point requires a profile that is a highly accurate refinement of the one used in higher dimensions. Furthermore, Wei and Yan
[
25
]
constructed arbitrarily many boundary peak solutions concentrating at strict local minima of the mean curvature. Related results for slightly subcritical and supercritical problems were obtained in
[
14
,
15
,
6
]
.

Compared to the scalar equation, the theory for coupled Neumann systems is far less developed. In the subcritical regime, singularly perturbed coupled Schrödinger systems have been studied via variational methods
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
, yielding least energy solutions as well as synchronized and segregated multi-peak solutions. However, the critical case remains poorly understood. Recent variational results for critical Neumann systems appear in
[
5
,
10
]
, where
[
10
]
establishes the existence of non-constant least energy solutions in both cooperative (
\beta<0
) and competitive (
\beta>0
) regimes. To the best of our knowledge, the existence of boundary blow-up solutions to (
1.1
) remains an open problem; this paper aims to provide a first step in this direction.

Motivated by the scalar constructions in
[
17
,
25
]
and the reduction scheme for critical Schrödinger systems in
[
13
]
, we construct solutions
(u_{1\lambda},u_{2\lambda})
to (
1.1
) that blow up at different boundary points for large
\lambda
. Our main result is as follows:

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that the mean curvature function
H
admits two distinct strict local maximum points
\xi_{i}^{*}
with
H(\xi_{i}^{*})>0
for
i=1,2
. For any
\beta>0
, there exists
\lambda_{0}>0
such that for any
\lambda\in(\lambda_{0},+\infty)
, system (
1.1
) admits a solution
(u_{1\lambda},u_{2\lambda})
such that
u_{i\lambda}
blows up at
\xi_{i}^{*}
as
\lambda\to+\infty
.

Remark 1.2
.

In an ellipsoid, system (
1.1
) always admits blowing-up solutions, as the mean curvature possesses at least two strict local maxima with positive values. As expected, the number of solutions increases with the geometric complexity of the domain. For instance, in a domain with several protrusions (see Figure
1.2
), the mean curvature attains local maxima at the tip of each protrusion. In such cases, if there are
k
such points, system (
1.1
) admits
\frac{k(k-1)}{2}
blowing-up solutions for
\lambda
sufficiently large.

Remark 1.3
.

The proof relies on a classical Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction procedure and is closer in spirit to
[
17
]
than to higher-dimensional constructions, as a modified ansatz involving Bessel functions must be incorporated from the outset. The main difficulty arises from the lower-dimensional setting (
N=4
), where the standard Talenti bubble is no longer adequate. To overcome this, we employ a modified ansatz that accounts for higher-order terms in the expansion, requiring a delicate analysis of boundary effects and component interactions.

Remark 1.4
.

We expect Theorem
1.1
to hold for more general stable critical points of
H
(e.g., non-degenerate ones). However, this remains an open problem even for the scalar equation (
1.2
). From a technical standpoint, extending the result would require a
C^{1}
-expansion of the reduced energy defined in (
3.3
), which likely necessitates a further refinement of the ansatz.

Remark 1.5
.

Our results extend to systems with
d\geq 2
components:

-\Delta u_{i}+\lambda u_{i}=u_{i}^{3}-\sum_{j\neq i}^{d}\beta_{ij}u_{i}u_{j}^{2}\quad\text{in }\Omega,\quad\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial\nu}=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.

If the mean curvature has
d
distinct strict local maxima with positive values, there exists a solution where each component
u_{i}
blows up at a corresponding maximum in a fully competitive regime (
\beta_{ij}>0
).

Remark 1.6
.

It is an interesting open question to study the asymptotic behavior of the least energy solutions found in
[
10
, Theorem 1.5]
as
\lambda\to\infty
. Specifically, it remains to be determined whether the components blow up, and if so, whether their concentration points collapse or remain distinct.

The paper is organized as follows: Section 2 presents the functional setting and the ansatz. Section 3 is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Technical estimates for the finite-dimensional reduction are collected in Appendices B and C. Appendix A contains the main properties of the correction of the ansatz.

2  Preliminaries

2.1  The ansatz

We want to build the solution to (
1.1
) of the form

u_{i}=V_{\lambda,\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}+\psi_{i},\quad i=1,2

where the main term is

V_{\lambda,\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}:=U_{\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}-W_{\lambda,\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}.

Here
U_{\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}
is the
bubble

U_{{\delta_{i}},{\xi_{i}}}(x)=\alpha\,\frac{{{\delta_{i}}}}{{\delta_{i}}^{2}+\left|x-{\xi_{i}}\right|^{2}},\ \alpha:=2\sqrt{2}

which solves the critical problem

-\Delta U_{{\delta_{i}},{\xi_{i}}}=U_{{\delta_{i}},{\xi_{i}}}^{3}\ \hbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{4}.

The correction term
W_{\lambda,\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}
has been introduced by Rey in
[
17
]
. It is defined as

(2.1)

\displaystyle W_{\lambda,\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}(x):=\alpha\,\lambda\delta_{i}W\left(\sqrt{\lambda}|x-\xi_{i}|\right),\quad\text{$i=1,2,$}

where
W
is the radial solution of

\displaystyle-\Delta W+W=\frac{1}{|x|^{2}},\quad\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{4}$.}

The properties of
W
are listed in Appendix
A
.

It is useful to point out that our ansatz
V_{\lambda,\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}
solves

(2.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\Delta V_{\lambda,\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}+\lambda V_{\lambda,\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}=U^{3}_{{\delta_{i}},{\xi_{i}}}+\lambda\left(U_{{\delta_{i}},{\xi_{i}}}-\frac{\alpha\,\delta_{i}}{|x-\xi_{i}|^{2}}\right),\quad&\text{ in $\Omega$,}\\
\partial_{\nu}V_{\lambda,\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}=\partial_{\nu}U_{{\delta_{i}},{\xi_{i}}}-\partial_{\nu}W_{\lambda,\delta_{i},\xi_{i}},&\text{ on $\partial\Omega$.}\end{cases}

The two concentration points
\xi_{i}\in\partial\Omega
are different and the concentration parameters
\delta_{i}
are

\delta_{i}:=\frac{d_{i}}{\lambda\ln\lambda},\ \hbox{with}\ d_{i}>0.

In the following we will always choose

\bm{d}:=(d_{1},d_{2})
and
\bm{\xi}:=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
in the set

\mathcal{O}_{\eta}=\left\{\left(\bm{d},\bm{\xi}\right)\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{+}\right)^{2}\times(\partial\Omega)^{2}:\,\left|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\right|\geq 2\eta,\,\xi_{i}\in\partial\Omega,\,\eta<d_{i}<1/\eta,\quad i=1,2\right\}

for some
\eta\in(0,1).

The remainder term
\psi_{i}\in K_{i}^{\perp}
satisfies some standard orthogonality conditions which will be made clear below.
Let us introduce the functions

(2.3)

\displaystyle Z_{0,i}:=\delta_{i}\frac{\partial U_{\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}}{\partial\delta_{i}},\quad Z_{j,i}:=\delta_{i}\frac{\partial U_{\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}}{\partial t_{j,i}},\qquad\text{$j=1,2,3$ and $i=1,2$,}

where
t_{j,i}
represents the orthonormal system of coordinates on the tangent space to
\partial\Omega
at
\xi_{i}
. We set

K_{i}:={\rm span}\left\{Z_{0,i},\,Z_{j,i},\,j=1,2,3\right\},\ K_{i}^{{\perp}}:=\left\{\psi\in H^{1}(\Omega)\Big|\,\left\langle\psi,Z_{j,i}\right\rangle=0,\,j=0,1,2,3\right\}.

where

(2.4)

\langle u_{1},u_{2}\rangle=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\nabla u_{2}+\lambda\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{1}u_{2}.

2.2  Setting of the problem

Given
\lambda>0,
let
H^{1}(\Omega)=H^{1}_{\lambda}(\Omega)
(in the following we will omit the dependance on
\lambda
) be the Hilbert space equipped with the inner product defined in (
2.4
) and the induced norm
\|u\|^{2}=\langle u,u\rangle.

For any
s\in[1,+\infty)
, the space
L^{s}(\Omega)
is equipped with the standard norm

\|u\|_{s}=\left(\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|u|^{s}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}}.

It is useful to introduce the adjoint operator of the embedding
i:H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{4}(\Omega)
, i.e. the operator

i_{\lambda}^{*}:L^{\frac{4}{3}}(\Omega)\to H^{1}(\Omega)
defined as

\displaystyle i_{\lambda}^{*}(f)=u

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad{\left\langle u,\varphi\right\rangle}=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f(x)\,\varphi(x)dx,\quad\forall\varphi\in{H}^{1}\left(\Omega\right)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad u\in H^{1}(\Omega)\ \hbox{solves}\ -\Delta u+\lambda u=f\ \hbox{in}\ \Omega,\ \partial_{\nu}u=0\ \hbox{on}\ \partial\Omega.

It is immediate to check that

\displaystyle{\left\|i_{\lambda}^{*}(f)\right\|\leq c\left\|f\right\|_{{{4}/{3}}},\quad\forall f\in L^{\frac{4}{3}}(\Omega),}

where the constant
c>0
does not depend on
\lambda
.

Now, using
i_{\lambda}^{*}
, we can rewrite (
1.1
) as

(2.5)

\begin{cases}u_{1}=i_{\lambda}^{*}\left(u_{1}^{3}-\beta u_{1}u_{2}^{2}\right),\quad&\text{ in $\Omega$, }\\
u_{2}=i_{\lambda}^{*}\left(u_{2}^{3}-\beta u_{2}u_{1}^{2}\right),\quad&\text{ in $\Omega$. }\end{cases}

To proceed with the reduction, we split the space
H:=H^{1}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)
equipped with the norm

\left\|(u,v)\right\|_{H}=\|u\|+\|v\|

into the sum of
\bm{K}_{\bm{d},\bm{\xi}}:={K_{1}}\times K_{2}
and
\bm{K^{\perp}}_{\bm{d},\bm{\xi}}:=\left({K_{1}}\times K_{2}\right)^{\perp}={K^{\perp}_{1}}\times K^{\perp}_{2}
.
Furthermore,
we introduce the projection maps

{\bm{\Pi}_{\bm{d},\bm{\xi}}}:={\Pi_{1}}\times{\Pi_{2}}:H\mapsto\bm{K}_{\bm{d},\bm{\xi}}\ \hbox{and}\ \bm{\Pi^{\perp}}_{\bm{d},\bm{\xi}}:={\Pi^{\perp}_{1}}\times{\Pi^{\perp}_{2}}:H\mapsto\bm{K^{\perp}}_{\bm{d},\bm{\xi}}.

Our approach to solve the problem (
2.5
) will be to find, for suitable
\left(\bm{d},\bm{\xi}\right)\in\mathcal{O}_{\eta}
and for large
\lambda>0
, a function
\bm{\psi}:=\left(\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\right)\in\bm{K^{\perp}}_{\bm{d},\bm{\xi}}
such that

(2.6)

\displaystyle\Pi^{\perp}_{\bm{d},\bm{\xi}}\left(\bm{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\psi})-\bm{\mathcal{E}}-\bm{\mathcal{N}}(\bm{\psi})\right)

\displaystyle=0

(2.7)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\bm{d},\bm{\xi}}\left(\bm{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\psi})-\bm{\mathcal{E}}-\bm{\mathcal{N}}(\bm{\psi})\right)

\displaystyle=0.

Here the linear operator
\bm{\mathcal{L}}=\left(\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}\right)
is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{\psi}):=\psi_{i}-i_{\lambda}^{*}\left(3V_{\lambda,\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}^{2}\psi_{i}-\beta\left(V_{\lambda,\delta_{j},\xi_{j}}^{2}\psi_{i}+2V_{\lambda,\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}V_{\lambda,\delta_{j},\xi_{j}}\psi_{j}\right)\right),\ i=1,2,\ j\not=i,

the error term

\bm{\mathcal{E}}=\left(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}\right)
is defined as

\mathcal{E}_{i}:=i_{\lambda}^{*}\left(V_{\lambda,\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}^{3}-\beta V_{\lambda,\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}V_{\lambda,\delta_{j},\xi_{j}}^{2}\right)-V_{\lambda,\delta_{i},\xi_{i}},\ i=1,2,\ j\not=i

and the non-linear term

\bm{\mathcal{N}}=\left(\mathcal{N}_{1},\mathcal{N}_{2}\right)
is defined as

\mathcal{N}_{i}(\bm{\psi}):={i_{\lambda}^{*}\Big(3V_{\lambda,\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}\psi_{i}^{2}+\psi_{i}^{3}-\beta\left(V_{\lambda,\delta_{i},\xi_{i}}\psi_{j}^{2}+2V_{\lambda,\delta_{j},\xi_{j}}\psi_{i}\psi_{j}+\psi_{i}\psi_{j}^{2}\right)\Big)},\ i=1,2,\ j\not=i.

Finally, it is useful to introduce some notations for the mean-curvature function. For any
\xi\in\partial\Omega
, by the translation and rotation, we can assume that
\xi=0
. Then for any
\eta>0
small enough, we denote

\displaystyle\Omega\cap B_{\eta}(0)=\left\{\,x=(x^{\prime},x_{4})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}\;:\;x\in B_{\eta}(0),\;x_{4}>g\left(x^{\prime}\right)\,\right\},

with

\displaystyle g\left(x^{\prime}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}g_{i}\,x^{2}_{i}+\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq\ell\leq 3}g_{ij\ell}\,x_{i}\,x_{j}\,x_{\ell}+O\!\left(|x^{\prime}|^{4}\right).

Moreover, let
H(\cdot)
be the mean curvature of the boundary, we have

\displaystyle H(0)=\frac{2}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{3}g_{i},\qquad H^{\prime}(0)=\frac{2}{3}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}g_{jji}+\sum_{j=i+1}^{N-1}g_{ijj}+3g_{iii}\right).
