Chronic intramuscular abscess masquerading as soft tissue neoplasm on biopsy and hydatid cyst on imaging: a diagnostic challenge and pitfall
Authors: Simeon Mulugeta Mengistu, Daniel Hailemichael, Habtamu Yenealem, Fedil Nuredin, Hidaya Yahya, Mekonen Eshete
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Chronic intramuscular abscess is a rare condition that may clinically and radiologically mimic neoplastic or parasitic lesions, creating significant diagnostic challenges.Case presentation:An 18-year-old immunocompetent male presented with a progressively enlarging swelling over the medial aspect of the right thigh for 18 months. An initial incisional biopsy suggested a benign hamartomatous lesion, whereas magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) favored a hydatid cyst. Surgical exploration revealed a thick-walled intramuscular cystic lesion containing dark grayish fluid. Histopathologic examination and cytology confirmed a chronic walled-off intramuscular abscess (chronic pyomyositis). Complete excision was performed. Following histopathologic confirmation, albendazole therapy was discontinued, and the patient was treated with antibiotics. The postoperative course was complicated by hematoma formation requiring evacuation, after which recovery was uneventful.Discussion:Chronic pyomyositis may present without systemic inflammatory signs and can mimic neoplastic or parasitic lesions both clinically and radiologically. In this case, prolonged disease progression, normal laboratory findings, and cystic MRI appearance contributed to diagnostic discordance. The misleading biopsy findings were likely related to the sampling of peripheral reactive fibrous tissue rather than the purulent core. This case highlights the limitations of imaging and biopsy in complex cystic soft tissue lesions.Conclusion:Chronic intramuscular abscess should be considered in the differential diagnosis of cystic soft tissue masses, particularly in endemic regions where hydatid disease is prevalent. Careful clinicoradiologic correlation and definitive histopathologic evaluation remain essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.

Introduction

Soft tissue masses of the extremities encompass a broad differential diagnosis, including neoplastic, infectious, and parasitic etiologies. In endemic regions such as Ethiopia, intramuscular hydatid disease caused by Echinococcus granulosus, although rare, remains an important consideration in cystic soft tissue lesions because of the risk of intraoperative rupture and anaphylaxis[1–4]. Chronic pyomyositis represents another uncommon cause of intramuscular swelling and may present insidiously without systemic inflammatory signs, thereby mimicking neoplastic or parasitic pathology[5–8].

Evaluation of cystic intramuscular lesions can be challenging because imaging findings often overlap among infectious, parasitic, and neoplastic processes[9]. In addition, a biopsy may be misleading because of sampling error, particularly when only peripheral fibrous tissue is obtained rather than central purulent material[10].

We report a case of chronic walled-off intramuscular abscess in an immunocompetent young adult, which was initially interpreted as a benign hamartomatous lesion on biopsy and radiologically favored to represent a hydatid cyst, highlighting the diagnostic pitfalls associated with complex intramuscular cystic lesions.

This case has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 guidelines[11].

Case presentation

An 18-year-old male presented with a gradually enlarging swelling over the right medial thigh of 18 months’ duration. The mass was initially pea-sized but had enlarged rapidly during the preceding three months, associated with mild pain and discomfort exacerbated by ambulation. There was no history of fever, weight loss, night sweats, cough, trauma, or contact with tuberculosis patients. The patient had no prior similar lesions, history of rupture, or recurrent swellings. He had no known chronic medical illnesses or previous surgical history. Family history was non-contributory.

On physical examination, a firm, immobile, mildly tender, round mass measuring approximately 8 × 10 cm was noted over the medial aspect of the right thigh (Fig. 1a). A small scar was present over the anterior thigh, corresponding to a previous incisional biopsy. The overlying skin was otherwise normal, with no ulceration, punctum, draining sinus, erythema, or warmth. No inguinal lymphadenopathy was identified, and the systemic examination was unremarkable. Routine laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, renal function tests, liver function tests, and serum electrolytes, were within normal limits. Ultrasound demonstrated a large, encapsulated intramuscular cystic lesion with internal echoes suggestive of an organized fluid collection.

A previously performed incisional biopsy revealed skeletal muscle fragments with lobules of adipose tissue and nerve bundles with minimal inflammatory infiltrate. A benign hamartomatous lesion, with a possible differential diagnosis of intramuscular lipoma, was suggested (Fig. 2a,b). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated a right proximal medial thigh intramuscular cystic lesion measuring approximately 13 cm in maximal dimension, with a thick, smooth, multilayered wall, low signal intensity on T1-weighted imaging, and high signal intensity on T2-weighted imaging. No definitive daughter cysts or internal septations were identified. Adjacent infiltrative changes involving the adductor musculature, with multifocal cystic changes, were also noted (Fig. 3). Given the imaging appearance and regional endemicity, the radiologic impression favored an intramuscular hydatid cyst. Following informed consent, surgical exploration was performed under general anesthesia. A longitudinal incision was made over the anteromedial thigh. The femoral neurovascular bundle was identified and protected. The lesion was located within the adductor compartment beneath the gracilis muscle and was surrounded by a thick fibrous capsule (Fig. 1b,c). Careful dissection separated the lesion from surrounding structures and the posterior intermuscular septum while preserving the sciatic nerve.

During dissection, the capsule was inadvertently breached, releasing dark grayish, non-foul-smelling fluid (Fig. 1f). The fluid was carefully suctioned to minimize contamination. The lesion was completely excised (Fig. 1d,e), and the cavity was irrigated with normal saline, hydrogen peroxide, and povidone–iodine solution. Hemostasis was secured, a drain inserted, and the wound closed in layers (Fig. 1g).

Because hydatid disease was strongly suspected preoperatively, albendazole, 400 mg orally twice daily, was initiated empirically.

Histopathologic examination of the excised specimen demonstrated skeletal muscle fibers with variable interfiber fibrosis, intense lymphoplasmacytic infiltration, and focal collections of neutrophils (Fig. 2c–e). Cytologic examination of the aspirated fluid showed sheets of neutrophils without parasitic elements (Fig. 2f). These findings confirmed the diagnosis of chronic walled-off intramuscular abscess (chronic pyomyositis).

Following definitive diagnosis, albendazole therapy was discontinued, and the patient received intravenous antibiotic therapy for 2 weeks.

On postoperative day 10, the patient developed pain and swelling at the operative site. Ultrasound demonstrated a postoperative hematoma, which required surgical evacuation. Bleeding from muscle fibers was controlled, and a compressive dressing was applied. Subsequent recovery was uneventful, with complete wound healing and no evidence of recurrence or infection at the 2-week follow-up. The patient reported satisfactory functional and esthetic outcomes. Follow-up at 3 and 6 months showed no evidence of recurrence.

Discussion

Chronic pyomyositis is an uncommon infection of skeletal muscle that may occasionally present as a chronic walled-off abscess. Unlike the classical presentation, characterized by fever, leukocytosis, and localized inflammatory signs, chronic forms may progress insidiously over months or years with minimal symptoms and normal laboratory findings. Such atypical presentations can mimic neoplastic or parasitic lesions and contribute to diagnostic delay, particularly in immunocompetent individuals[5–8].

The present case posed a significant diagnostic challenge because of discordant clinical, radiologic, and pathologic findings. The lesion demonstrated slow enlargement over 18 months without systemic manifestations of infection. MRI revealed a large intramuscular cystic lesion with a thick, multilayered wall and surrounding muscular changes. Although MRI is the imaging modality of choice for evaluating soft tissue masses, imaging features of chronic abscesses frequently overlap with those of hydatid cysts and cystic soft tissue tumors. In endemic regions, the possibility of hydatid disease may further influence radiologic interpretation toward a parasitic diagnosis, particularly when a cystic lesion is identified[2–4,9].

An additional diagnostic pitfall was the initial incisional biopsy, which suggested a benign hamartomatous lesion. Definitive histopathologic examination of the excised specimen, however, demonstrated fibrosis, lymphoplasmacytic inflammation, and focal neutrophilic infiltrates consistent with a chronic intramuscular abscess. This discrepancy was most likely related to sampling error, as biopsy specimens obtained from the peripheral fibrous capsule may fail to capture the central inflammatory or purulent components of a heterogeneous lesion. Similar limitations of biopsy have been recognized in the evaluation of complex soft tissue masses and emphasize the importance of clinicoradiologic correlation[10,12].

The diagnosis was ultimately established by histopathologic examination of the excised lesion, which excluded parasitic and neoplastic pathology. This case highlights the limitations of both imaging and biopsy in the evaluation of cystic intramuscular masses and underscores the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis, particularly in regions where hydatid disease is endemic. Complete surgical excision served both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes, allowing definitive diagnosis and appropriate postoperative management. Conclusion

Chronic intramuscular abscess can closely mimic both soft tissue tumors and hydatid cysts clinically, radiologically, and even on preliminary biopsy. This case demonstrates the diagnostic limitations of imaging and biopsy in complex cystic intramuscular lesions. Accurate diagnosis requires thorough clinical assessment, careful interpretation of radiologic findings, awareness of endemic diseases, and definitive histopathologic confirmation. Early multidisciplinary evaluation is essential to avoid diagnostic delay and inappropriate management.
