 ABSTRACT

This study applies statistical and machine learning methods to analyze natural gas pipeline incidents in
the United States using the Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration (PHMSA) Gas
Distribution Incident Dataset (2010–2024). The dataset includes over 600 variables describing incident
characteristics, infrastructure attributes, and contributing factors associated with unintentional gas
releases. The objective is to assess whether these features can reliably predict the underlying cause of
pipeline failures. Multinomial logistic regression and Random Forest models were developed to classify
incident causes, including excavation damage, corrosion, equipment failure, and natural forces. Results
show that excavation damage is both the most frequent and most predictable cause, with models
achieving strong performance for this category. However, when excavation damage is excluded, model
accuracy declines significantly, with some models performing near random levels. Across all approaches,
severe class imbalance and limited variability in key predictors constrain predictive performance. Pipeline
age and diameter emerge as the most influential variables, but they provide insufficient discriminatory
power to distinguish among less frequent failure types. These findings indicate that non-excavation-
related incidents are rare, heterogeneous, and weakly represented in the dataset, limiting the effectiveness
of machine learning classification. Overall, this study highlights the structural limitations of the PHMSA
dataset for predictive modeling and underscores the need for improved data balance and feature
enrichment. The results reinforce excavation damage prevention as the most impactful strategy for
reducing pipeline incidents.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Natural gas is an important source of energy for residential, commercial and industrial use, as well as for
power generation on the electric grid. Disruptions or interruptions to gas supplies have the potential to
curtail use for metered customers, but also for electricity generators. Thus, understanding potential
sources of disruptions to the pipeline system is important in determining needed investments to upgrade
or replace components of the system. This study examines a federal government database collecting
information on natural gas pipeline incidents using a variety of modeling methodologies with the aim of
evaluating types of hazard risks to the system.

 2. PHMSA DATA ANALYSIS SUMMARY

2.1 PHMSA DATA

The Pipeline and Hazardous Material Safety Administrations (PHMSA)’s Gas Distribution Incident
Dataset (January 2010 – 2024) is a federally maintained, publicly-available dataset that is part of
PHMSA’s national incident reporting program, with over 600 variables documenting incidents related to
unintentional releases of natural gas. Pipeline operators of regulated pipeline systems are required to
submit standardized incident reports (Form PHMSA F 7100.1) following any event meeting specific
reporting thresholds. These include fatalities or injuries, unintentional gas releases, property damage,
explosions or fires, and significant operational interruptions. These 600+ variables can be grouped into
nine categories: operator information, incident details, corrosion and material issues, environmental and

 5

weather factors, pipe and fitting details, reporting and notification, damage cause, discovery location
information, and other/unspecified.

This dataset has been used in safety analyses because it enables researchers and policymakers to evaluate
incident trends, infrastructure aging, high-risk materials (such as cast iron or bare steel), excavation-
related vulnerabilities, and geographic or seasonal patterns. It also supports the development of predictive
models, risk indices, and cost assessments for regulatory decision-making and asset-management
planning.

Several recent studies have relied on the PHMSA gas distribution incident dataset to evaluate patterns,
causes, and consequences of failures in the U.S. natural gas distribution system. In these studies,
PHMSA’s dataset provides a nationally standardized, operator-reported record of unintentional releases,
equipment failures, excavation damage, and other incident types, enabling researchers to investigate both
temporal trends and risk factors.

Using an older version of the PHMSA incident database, Siler-Evans et al (2014) [1] examined about
40,000 incident occurrences from 1968 through 2009 using statistical assessments of conformance to
power law distributions in accident trends and severity. Their analysis determined that hazardous liquid
accidents decline significantly over the four decades covered in the incident database. They found that
accident frequencies and severities were mixed over time, with accident frequencies declining for injuries
and fatalities, while incidents on transmission lines and property damage increasing over the same span.
Further, while accidents were declining, those incidents that did occur often were of greater severity in
terms of property damage.

In a combined study of transmission and distribution lines in the United States, Vetter et al (2019) [2]
analyzed trends in “significant” pipeline failures from 2010 through 2017. Significant events are those
that result in a fatality, injury, or damage to property in excess of $50,000. The authors note that since
roughly 2008, incident levels have remained relatively constant after declining the previous 40 years and
that significant events accounted for approximately 50% of all pipeline incidents.

Li et al. (2021) [3] used the PHMSA natural gas distribution incident data from 2010 to 2019 to model
incident severity using a Geographically and Temporally Weighted Ordered Logistic Regression
(GTWOLR) model. They combined structured PHMSA fields with text-mined narrative attributes to
demonstrate that the determinants of incident severity vary significantly spatiotemporally. They found
that older infrastructure, excavation damage, gas explosions, iron pipes, and longer operator response
times are strongly associated with high-severity events.

Xiao et al. (2025) [4], analyzed several decades of PHMSA gas distribution incident data. Using zero-
inflated Poisson models to estimate casualties, they found that national incident counts and rates have
declined over time, but economic consequences remain highly variable due to rare, high-impact, major
events. Their study also emphasizes differences in outcomes by pipeline material, age, incident location,
and cause, underscoring the importance of modeling pipeline attributes when evaluating failure
consequences.

In an earlier study, Xiao et al (2023) [5] looked at factors affecting gas transmission, focusing on the
inclusion of operational and environmental conditions in conjunction with physical pipeline features. The
studies not only include PHMSA’s standard causal factors of corrosion, equipment failure, incorrect
operation, material/weld failure, and outside force damage but include a set of background factors such as
age, diameter, pressure ratio, air temperature, humidity, location class, and incident area. An important
finding in their analysis is that large diameter pipelines suffered fewer incidents, but that when incidents
did occur they were of greater severity in injuries, fatalities and property damage.

 6

A recent study by Liu (2025) [6] applied the PHMSA gas distribution incident dataset to develop a hybrid
forecasting and pattern-mining framework. Using Seasonal Auto-Regressive Integrated Moving Average
(SARIMA) to predict short-term failure rates and association rule mining to uncover causal structures,
this study identified distinct seasonal patterns and reveals differences in the risk signatures of leaks,
mechanical punctures, and ruptures. These results show how spatiotemporal and operational factors
jointly influence failure likelihood and provide evidence for cause-specific, time-sensitive preventive
strategies in distribution networks.

An earlier study by Bianchini et al. (2018) [7], which used a portion of the PHMSA dataset (years 2010–
2015) involved a statistical assessment of incident rates in distribution systems. Their findings showed
higher incident rates and consequences in older, small-diameter, low-pressure pipelines, supporting the
view that infrastructure age and configuration are critical to understanding system vulnerability.

Lastly, Halim et al (2020) [8] used comparative database analysis, hierarchical cause classification, and
statistical dependency analysis to build a conceptual causal framework that links multiple contributing
factors to pipeline incidents and supports future risk prediction. This approach relies upon methods
developed to understand interactions between variables and determining causation with varying degrees
of probability. The authors suggest implementing causal models that rely on understanding the
relationship between causal factors found in sources such as the PHMSA database and background factors
that can influence or contribute to the occurrence of an incident.

Overall, these studies, using the PHMSA gas distribution incident dataset highlight the use of this dataset
as a significant resource for evaluating pipeline risks. Existing studies have applied methods ranging from
spatiotemporal regression, zero-inflated count models, and text mining to time-series forecasting and
association rule extraction. The following machine learning (ML) modeling of incident causes, including
the treatment of excavation damage, are intended to complement the existing methodological landscape
by offering a complementary machine-learning-based characterization of predictive factors and
classification performance. This aligns with, and extends, ongoing efforts to identify the operational,
environmental, and infrastructure-related features that most strongly distinguish incident types in modern
gas distribution systems.

2.2 PHMSA DATA ANALYSIS

A variety of analysis techniques, focusing on Machine Learning (ML) methods, were chosen for this
analysis. The following sections contain the modeling methods, initial findings using the chosen method,
along with figures and tables.

2.2.1 Multinomial Logistic Regression (Logit Model)

A multinomial logistic regression model was developed to predict the cause of natural gas pipeline
unintentional releases using a categorical dependent variable using variables such as pipe diameter,
material, incident area type, and pipeline age (Table 1). Regarding the model performance, the overall
accuracy was 0.59 (59%), the macro average (across all classes) had a precision at 0.12, a recall at 0.16,
and an F1-score at 0.13. In terms of the weighted average (which accounts for class imbalance), the
precision was at 0.38, recall was at 0.59, and the F1-score was at 0.46. In terms of class-specific results,
class 1 (excavation damage) precision was at 0.62, recall was at 0.99, and the F1-score was at 0.76.

The model performed reasonably well, capturing nearly all true cases (high recall). Regarding classes 2–8
(other causes such as corrosion, equipment failure, natural forces, etc.), the precision and recall are near
zero. The model fails to predict these causes, despite them being present in the dataset. Regarding insights
from the confusion matrix, out of 152 total cases: most true positives are concentrated in class 1

 7

(excavation damage). Other causes (classes 2–8) are consistently misclassified as class 1. This indicates a
strong class imbalance and lack of model generalizability.

Regarding the model coefficients (selected observations), pipe diameter and material show small but
varying effects across causes. Incident area type appears to have large negative coefficients for some
classes (i.e. -1.27 for class 1, -0.97 for class 5), suggesting strong associations in specific cases. Pipe age
contributes minimally, with near-zero coefficients across most classes (Figure 1).

Ultimately, the model overfits to the dominant category (excavation damage), where performance is
acceptable (62% precision, 99% recall). However, it fails to distinguish other causes of failure. This
makes the model unreliable for general classification of release causes. It is likely that the issue stems
from severe class imbalances (class 1 dominates the dataset), and insufficient discriminatory power of
predictors for less common causes. Improvements to the model include addressing the class imbalance by
applying resampling methods such as oversampling minority classes, under sampling class 1, or Synthetic
Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE). Also, the use of class-weighted logistic regression to
penalize misclassification of minority classes or the incorporation of additional predictors (i.e.
maintenance history, corrosion data, environmental factors, operating pressure cycles) and the creation of
interaction terms between variables (i.e. material × age). Ultimately, the multinomial logistic regression
only performs well for predicting excavation damage (class 1), but is ineffective for other causes. While
this highlights excavation damage as the most learnable category, the current model is not suitable for
general predictive use without addressing class imbalance and enriching predictor variables.

A multinomial logistic regression (logit) model was applied to predict the cause of unintentional natural

In terms of the model performance (Figure 2), the overall accuracy of 0.12 (12%) is far too low to be

0.09. Regarding the weighted average performance, the precision is 0.10, the recall is 0.12, and the F1-

and F1 of approximately 0.00, which means that the model failed to classify correctly. Regarding the
confusion matrix, most predictions were scattered or defaulted incorrectly across classes. The model
failed to separate minority causes and misclassification was widespread.

Regarding model coefficients (from selected observations), the pipe diameter effects vary by class (i.e.
positive for class 4 and class 8, negative for others). Material had a strong positive association for class 4
(0.417), but negative for class 3 and class 7. Incident area type had a strong positive influence for class 5

The model is essentially incapable of predicting non-excavation causes of pipeline releases. With only

results. The model indicates that pipe diameter, material, and incident area type may influence
classification, but these signals are too weak for robust prediction. Ultimately, excluding excavation
damage, the multinomial logistic regression does not provide predictive power for identifying other
causes of pipeline failures. Performance is dominated by class imbalance and insufficient explanatory
variables.

A Random Forest classifier was trained using pipeline incident attributes: pipe age, pipe diameter,
material, and incident area type to predict unintentional release. When excavation damage was excluded
from the dataset, the model only achieved an accuracy of 31.3%, which indicates limited predictive
performance.

Based on these results (Table 2), when exploring the causes of unintentional releases, excluding the
occurrence of excavation damage, the physical characteristics of pipelines, especially age and diameter,
were found to be the most influential. However, the comparatively low importance of material and area
type implies that those features may offer limited discriminatory value in the absence of excavation-

2.2.5 Logit Model Alternatives using Data Subset

A Multinomial Logit Model (MNL) approach was tried as a tree-based method (i.e. Random Forest,
Gradient Boosting) often handle categorical imbalance better, and hierarchical models or cause-specific
binary classifiers (one-vs-rest approach) and may offer more insight into relationships within the dataset.
The MNL model was used to predict the cause of unintentional release using four independent variables:
pipe diameter, material involved, incident area type, and pipeline age (Table 4). Although the model
achieved a classification accuracy of 49%, several characteristics of the dataset strongly influenced model
behavior and limited interpretability.

The first limitation of the MNL involves the severe imbalance within the predictor variables. Nearly all
incidents in the dataset involve carbon steel, leaving almost no variation in the material involved.
Similarly, most incidents occur underground, which restricts the ability of the incident area type variable
to differentiate among aboveground, transition-area, and underground events. Although variables such as
pipeline age and pipe diameter offer more variation, their distributions still overlap substantially across
failure categories. Because MNL models depend on meaningful variation across predictors to detect
differences in outcomes, this lack of diversity in the input variables weakens the model’s explanatory and
predictive power, leaving age and diameter as the only moderately informative features.

Another challenge comes from the highly imbalanced distribution of the dependent variable. The dataset
is dominated by two causes of failure: corrosion failure (331 records) and excavation damage (190
records). In contrast, several important categories, such as equipment failure, incorrect operation, and
other outside force damage, contain less than a dozen records each. This imbalance makes it difficult for
the model to learn class patterns, which results in biases in the model towards predicting major causes.
The result is that rare, but operationally important, incident types are not captured effectively in the
model’s structure.

Based on the classification report, the model performs relatively well for the most common category,
corrosion failure, achieving a precision of 0.55, recall of 0.65, and an F1 score of 0.60. It also shows
moderate performance for categories such as excavation damage, material failure of pipe/weld, and other
outside force damage, which have enough observations for the model to discern partial patterns in the
data. However, for several other incident types, including equipment failure, incorrect operation, natural
force damage, and other incident cause, the model demonstrates zero predictive capability. Precision,
recall, and F1 scores all fall to zero, confirming that the model cannot identify these classes at all (Figure
5). This imbalance is further illustrated by the macro-average F1 score of 0.24, which reflects an
unbalanced performance, compared to the higher weighted F1 score of 0.45, which is inflated by the large
majority classes.

Overall, these limitations highlight the structural constraints imposed by the dataset. While the MNL
model can produce reasonable predictions for the most common causes of unintentional release, primarily
corrosion and excavation damage, it entirely fails to identify less frequently occurring incident types. The
near uniformity of key predictor variables, combined with extreme outcome imbalance, leaves the model
with insufficient information to reliably differentiate among failure causes. As a result, the MNL model’s
predictive accuracy should be interpreted with caution, and additional data, rebalancing strategies, or
alternative modeling approaches are likely needed to obtain more comprehensive and reliable insights in
future research.

Despite achieving moderate overall accuracy, the MNL model’s performance is primarily driven by the
dataset’s dominant categories, with limited predictive value for less frequent failure types. Severe
imbalances in both predictor and outcome variables restrict the model’s ability to detect subtle patterns,
leading to strong performance for corrosion incidents, but near-zero predictive capability for other causes.
As a result, the MNL results should be interpreted with caution and complemented with methods
designed for imbalanced classification or enriched datasets.

2.2.6 Random Forest

Using the specified variables used in the MNL, a Random Forest model approach resulted in an overall
accuracy of 48%, similar to the performance of the MNL model, but with some notable differences in
how it handles class imbalance and predictor variation. Random Forests are more flexible and non-linear
than MNL models, yet its performance is still strongly constrained by the dataset’s structure. The severe
imbalance in predictor variables, namely most records being carbon steel and underground, means that the
model again relies mainly on variation in pipe age and pipe diameter, as the other predictors provide
almost no discriminatory value. Although tree-based models can theoretically detect subtle interactions
among variables, the lack of diversity in material and incident-area categories limits their effectiveness.

As with the MNL analysis, the dependent variable (cause of unintentional release) is highly imbalanced,
and this strongly affects the model’s performance. Corrosion failure (72 test samples) and excavation
damage (38 samples), dominate the model’s predictions. Rare classes, such as equipment failure,
incorrect operation, other incident cause, and natural force damage, have extremely small sample sizes in
the test set, ranging from 2 to 11 observations. Because Random Forest models require enough examples

 15

to form meaningful divisions, these minor categories cannot be learned effectively, leading to the model
predicting them poorly, or not at all.

The classification report reflects the imbalance. The model performs most strongly for corrosion failure,
with precision of 0.65, recall of 0.61, and an F1 score of 0.63, which is consistent with the category’s
large representation (Figure 6). Material failure of pipe/weld and excavation damage show moderate
performance, with F1 scores of 0.55 and 0.41, respectively. However, the model has zero predictive
capability for several categories: equipment failure, incorrect operation, and natural force damage all have
precision and recall of 0.00, indicating that the model never predicted these classes. Even for categories
such as other incident cause and other outside force damage, F1 scores remain low (0.14 and 0.32),
reflecting inconsistent classification. The macro-average F1 score of 0.27 shows weak balanced
performance across all classes, while the weighted F1 score of 0.48 is elevated by the model’s strong
performance on the majority categories. Overall, the model exhibits the same structural limitations
observed in the MNL model analysis. While it performs reasonably well for the most common incident
types, particularly corrosion and excavation damage, it fails to recognize minority classes due to their
extremely limited representation in the dataset. The near uniformity of key predictors further restricts the
model’s ability to differentiate among causes, leaving pipe age and diameter as the only consistently
informative features. As a result, although Random Forests are typically more robust when analyzing
complex relationships, the dataset’s imbalance and lack of variability constrain their effectiveness,
producing results that should be interpreted with caution.

 3. INSIGHTS FROM THE ANALYSIS

The results across all modeling approaches point to several clear and consistent insights regarding the
causes of unintentional releases in natural gas transmission and gathering systems. First, the
overwhelming dominance of excavation damage in the dataset, and the strong predictive performance of
models for this single category, indicate that third-party interference remains the most common and most
predictable driver of release events. When excavation damage is included in the model, it becomes the
defining contributor, overshadowing all other causal variables. When it is removed, no alternative cause
emerges as statistically or operationally significant, and model accuracy decreases. This suggests that,

 16

aside from excavation-related events, the pipeline system exhibits a high degree of engineering integrity,
with failures distributed across numerous rare categories that lack clear separable signatures.

Second, the severe imbalance in both predictor and outcome variables constrains the ability of all
statistical and machine-learning models to learn meaningful structure in the data. Variables such as
material type and incident area type offer almost no discriminating value due to their uniformity across
records. Even pipe age and diameter, normally informative predictors, show overlapping distributions
across causes, which limits their utility. As a result, model behavior reflects the underlying weak signal:
rare causes appear essentially random with respect to available predictors, making it difficult to identify
any reliable causal patterns distinct from excavation damage.

Overall, these insights suggest that the greatest contributor to unintended releases is not the pipeline
network or operator practices, but rather external human activity that interacts unpredictably with pipeline
infrastructure. The low model performance for non-excavation causes aligns with the interpretation that
these events occur infrequently, arise from diverse mechanisms, and lack sufficient representation or
signal strength to be modeled effectively. Consequently, the analytical results reinforce the notion that the
pipeline system is structurally robust and that reducing overall incident rates requires prioritizing
excavation damage prevention, public awareness, and early detection strategies, rather than modifications
to core pipeline attributes.

In addition to these analytical findings, the results from these analyses contribute meaningfully to the
broader literature that relies on the PHMSA dataset. While prior studies have focused primarily on
incident severity, long-term statistical trends, or spatiotemporal forecasting, relatively few have
undertaken a detailed cause-classification analysis using machine-learning methods on PHMSA
transmission and gathering incidents. This work adds a distinct perspective by evaluating the predictive
structure of pipeline attributes relative to failure causes, rather than modeling consequences or historical
rates. The findings from this research demonstrate that, in contrast to severity or frequency modeling,
prediction is fundamentally limited by the structure of the dataset itself, revealing important constraints
that have not been explicitly documented in earlier research. By showing that excavation damage is the
only reliably predictable cause, and that other failure mechanisms lack sufficient signal for differentiation,
this analysis highlights the boundaries of what PHMSA data can currently support in predictive analytics
and identifies where additional data collection or feature enrichment would be most impactful.

 4. CONCLUSION AND NEXT STEPS

The results from both the Multinomial Logit and Random Forest models highlight important limitations
in the current dataset and offer insights for future work. Because rare incident causes remain extremely
underrepresented, model performance is dominated by the majority classes, producing imbalanced
predictions and obscuring potential relationships among the minor categories. Expanding the dataset,
either by incorporating additional years, merging with supplemental PHMSA operational datasets, or
obtaining operator-level records, would increase the number of examples for rare failures and enhance
model stability. Additionally, addressing imbalance through resampling, synthetic data generation (such
as Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique [SMOTE]), or class-weighted modeling, is essential for
improving the detection of infrequent, but operationally significant causes.

Future analyses should consider enriching the feature set. The current variables capture only a narrow
slice of the pipeline system’s operational context. Adding predictors such as environmental conditions,
soil type, maintenance history, corrosion monitoring data, pressure cycles, leak detection records, and
surrounding land-use characteristics could provide models with meaningful structure to distinguish
among non-excavation failure mechanisms. Additionally, alternative modeling frameworks, including

 17

gradient boosting methods, such as XGBoost, LightGBM, hierarchical models, or one-versus-rest
classifiers, may be better suited to handling heterogeneous data and imbalanced outcomes.

Overall, the analytical results underscore a key operational insight: excavation damage is both the most
common cause and the only reliably predictable one, while other causes occur too infrequently, and with
too little distinguishing information, to model effectively. Accordingly, safety and mitigation strategies
should focus on improving excavation awareness, locating practices, and third-party damage prevention.
As more comprehensive data become available, future modeling efforts may be able to identify clearer
patterns among the less frequent causes of unintentional release, supporting more targeted risk-reduction
strategies.

This study contributes to existing research using the PHMSA incident dataset by extending analytical
efforts beyond descriptive statistics, severity modeling, or temporal trend analysis, and applying
classification-focused, machine-learning models to assess whether the underlying attributes of pipelines
can meaningfully predict the cause of an unintentional release. The results show that while PHMSA’s
incident dataset is rich in incident-level detail, it contains limited predictive structure for distinguishing
among non-excavation failure causes. This insight is valuable for future research because it clarifies the
current predictive limits of the dataset and points to specific gaps, such as insufficient operational,
environmental, and maintenance variables, that hinder deeper causal inference. By demonstrating where
PHMSA data succeeds and where it falls short for machine-learning applications, this study provides a
foundation for improving future data collection practices and developing next-generation models that can
more effectively support pipeline safety, risk assessment, and infrastructure resilience initiatives.
