EMA-Nesterov: Stabilizing Nesterov’s Lookahead for Accelerated Deep Learning Optimization
Chung-Yiu Yau, Dawei Li, Athanasios Glentis, Valentyn Boreiko, Hoi-To Wai, Mingyi Hong

Abstract
Lookahead-based acceleration methods, such as Nesterov’s momentum, are widely used in optimization, but they often become unreliable in deep learning training mainly due to stochastic gradient noise and non-convex loss landscapes. In particular, standard lookahead relies on short-horizon update signals (e.g., differences between consecutive iterates), which are inherently noisy and can lead to unstable extrapolation directions. This work revisits Nesterov’s acceleration from a trajectory perspective and argues that effective acceleration in deep learning should harness the low-frequency trends of optimization trajectories rather than extrapolating noisy one-step updates. Leveraging this insight, we propose EMA-Nesterov , a simple modification that replaces the standard Nesterov’s lookahead direction with an exponential moving average (EMA) of parameter updates. This yields a stabilized lookahead direction that captures and harnesses the evolving trend of the training trajectory through a low-pass filter, while remaining adaptive to progressive changes via the geometric weighting structure of EMA. We show that EMA-Nesterov retains a theoretical accelerated convergence rate in convex problems that is analogous to Nesterov’s accelerated gradient method. Furthermore, we provide empirical evidence on language model pre-training to verify that EMA-Nesterov is broadly applicable across a range of fine-tuned base optimizers, including Adam, SOAP, Muon, as well as complex optimizers that achieve state-of-the-art performance on optimization benchmarks (NanoGPT). Compared to prior lookahead methods, EMA-Nesterov achieves better performance by avoiding the instability of short-horizon lookahead and the non-adaptivity of long-horizon lookahead.

Generally speaking, lookahead acceleration constructs an extrapolated solution by moving beyond the current iterate along a direction induced by past optimization trajectories. In the classical Nesterov’s accelerated gradient method, this extrapolation direction is given by the difference between consecutive iterates, i.e.,
\mathbf{x}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t-1}

One key insight behind why such lookahead direction can accelerate base optimizers is that extrapolating past iterates allows the algorithm to follow the persistent trend of the optimization trajectory, thereby moving more aggressively along stable descent directions. However, under the deep learning optimization trajectory, Nesterov’s lookahead can be unstable due to oscillating optimizer update that overshoots the minima under pre-conditioning, as similarly observed in stochastic optimization
(
18

Instead, exponential moving average of the past (stochastic) gradients has been a key component for modern optimizers such as Adam and Muon.

Our Contributions

The contribution of this paper is summarized below.

•

We introduce a trajectory-based perspective of lookahead acceleration for deep learning optimization. We show that classical short-horizon lookahead becomes unstable in deep learning optimization due to oscillatory high-frequency trajectory components, motivating a low-pass filtering view of acceleration.

•

We propose
EMA-Nesterov
, a lightweight single-loop acceleration method that replaces long-horizon extrapolation with an exponential moving average of optimization trajectories, i.e., for a given base optimizer function

\displaystyle={\cal A}_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{t}+\beta_{t}\mathbf{m}^{t}),\quad\mathbf{m}^{t+1}=\gamma\mathbf{m}^{t}+(1-\gamma)(\mathbf{x}^{t+1}-\mathbf{x}^{t}),

exhibits a low-passed lookahead direction that can adapt to a varying trend in the training trajectory.

•

We demonstrate that

Understanding Lookahead Acceleration in Deep Learning

This section studies the recent prior arts on lookahead acceleration and compares them with the classical Nesterov’s lookahead algorithm from a trajectory perspective.
We then identify key insights in the prior arts and suggest how they may inspire new algorithms.

To fix notations, we consider the stochastic optimization problem: for random sample
\xi
drawn from some distribution
{\cal D}
,

f^{\star}:=\textstyle\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}~f(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim{\cal D}}[f(\mathbf{x};\xi)],

(2)

where
f(\mathbf{x};\xi)
is differentiable w.r.t.
\mathbf{x}
. Here, we may denote
{\cal A}(\mathbf{x};\xi,{\bf s})
as an optimizer function that takes the current model state
\mathbf{x}
and returns the next model state, via consuming a sample of stochastic objective function gradient evaluated on
\mathbf{x}
with randomness
\xi
, while the optimizer update is conditioned on its internal states
{\bf s}
. For example,
{\cal A}(\mathbf{x};\xi,{\bf s})=\mathbf{x}-\alpha_{t}(\gamma{\bf s}+(1-\gamma)\nabla f(\mathbf{x};\xi))
with learning rate
\alpha_{t}>0
produces the next model state using momentum SGD under gradient momentum rate
\gamma\in[0,1)
and momentum buffer
{\bf s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For simplicity, in the rest of this paper, we shall use the notation
{\cal A}_{t}({\bf x}):={\cal A}({\bf x};\xi^{t},{\bf s}^{t})
.

We begin our endeavor through studying the lookahead algorithm in

14
;
20
. With a lookahead step size
\beta>0
and an optimizer
{\cal A}_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
(e.g., vanilla/momentum SGD, Muon, etc.) for (
2
), they proposed

\displaystyle\textbf{for every}

\displaystyle t=nK,n=0,...,\lfloor T/K\rfloor,

(3)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t+K}

\displaystyle={\cal A}_{t+K-1}(~\cdots~{\cal A}_{t+1}({\cal A}_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{t}))),\quad\mathbf{x}^{t+K}=\mathbf{x}^{t}+\beta(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t+K}-\mathbf{x}^{t}).

For every
K
steps of optimizer update
{\cal A}_{t}
, the accumulated update
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t+K}-\mathbf{x}^{t}
is applied with a small step size
\beta\leq 1
, which means that the algorithm’s solution only interpolates between
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{t+K}
and
\mathbf{x}^{t}
.
The key property of (
3
) is that the long-horizon update direction
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t+K}-\mathbf{x}_{t}
averages optimization trajectories across multiple iterations, thereby suppressing high-frequency oscillations in stochastic optimization. As a result, the extrapolation direction becomes substantially more stable than the one-step lookahead direction used in classical Nesterov acceleration (similar to the case of Figure
1
(right)).
Importantly,
(
20
, Proposition 2)
demonstrated that the above scheme achieves a
{\cal O}(\beta)
-factor variance reduction. That being said, as demonstrated in
(
20
, Appendix A)
, (
3
) only exhibits a slightly improved empirical convergence speed over vanilla SGD in the quadratic setting,
suggesting an ‘unaccelerated’ rate of
f(\mathbf{x}^{T})-f^{\star}={\cal O}((1-1/\kappa)^{T})
, where
\kappa
is the condition number of
f(\mathbf{x})
.

For further insights, we compare (
3
) to the classical Nesterov’s accelerated gradient method by reducing (
3
) to the special case with
K=1
. Notice that in this case, (
3
) is equivalent to:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{t}+\beta({\cal A}_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\mathbf{x}^{t})=\underbrace{{\cal A}_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{t})+(\beta-1)({\cal A}_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{t})-\mathbf{x}^{t})}_{\text{lookahead position}}.

(4)

On the other hand, Nesterov’s lookahead algorithm
(
13
)
can be written as:

\mathbf{x}^{t+1}={\cal A}_{t}(~\underbrace{\mathbf{x}^{t}+\beta^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t-1})}_{\text{lookahead position}}~),\quad\beta^{\prime}>0.

(5)

It is known that (
5
) achieves an accelerated convergence rate: with an appropriate
\beta^{\prime}
and
{\cal A}_{t}
utilizing deterministic gradient, one has
f(\mathbf{x}^{T})-f^{\star}={\cal O}((1-\sqrt{1/\kappa})^{T})
for strongly convex

.

Comparing (
4
) and (
5
) on their corresponding lookahead position,
we note that (
4
) can be regarded as a
pessimistic
lookahead algorithm since it uses the reversed Nesterov’s lookahead direction when
\beta-1
becomes negative.
This also implies that (
4
) may not be able to achieve acceleration when using a conservative lookahead step size
\beta\leq 1
. Meanwhile, Nesterov’s lookahead (
5

lookahead direction as it fully trusts the update direction of past iteration with any

As seen,

3.1
Exponential Moving Average Lookahead

Alternative to long-horizon lookahead, we replace the lookahead direction with a low-pass filter that is adaptive to the recent signals — the exponential moving average (EMA) filter: for
t\geq 1
and let
\gamma\in[0,1)
,

\textstyle\mathbf{m}^{t+1}=\gamma\mathbf{m}^{t}+(1-\gamma)\Delta\mathbf{x}^{t+1}=:{\rm EMA}_{\gamma}(\{\Delta\mathbf{x}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{t+1}).

(8)

In particular, one has
{\rm EMA}_{\gamma}(\{\Delta\mathbf{x}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{t+1})=(1-\gamma)\sum_{i=1}^{t+1}\gamma^{t+1-i}\Delta\mathbf{x}^{i}
.
Notice that (
8
) is a low-pass filter with the geometric coefficients
\{\gamma^{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
that is anticipated to filter out the high-frequency fluctuations while putting larger weights on recent lookahead signals
\Delta{\bf x}^{i}
, i.e., it aligns more with the recent trend of
\Delta\mathbf{x}^{i}
as illustrated in the right figure of Figure
3
. The low-pass behavior of EMA filter can be analyzed by its transfer function
H(z)=\frac{1-\gamma}{1-\gamma z^{-1}}
on the input signal
z=\exp(i\omega)
of frequency
\omega\in[0,\pi]
, where the output energy
|H(\exp(i\omega))|
is decreasing as
\omega
increases from
0
(low frequency) to
\pi
(high frequency).
We replace the lookahead direction of Nesterov’s algorithm by
{\bf m}^{t}
and propose

10
) satisfies

f(\mathbf{x}^{T})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})={\cal O}\left(L/({(1-\gamma)^{3}T^{2}})\right)\quad\text{for any $\gamma\in[0,1)$, $T>0$.}

(13)

See Appendix
B.2
for the proof. Unlike the strongly convex case, Theorem
2
guarantees an accelerated convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/T^{2})
for all
\gamma\in[0,1)
, i.e., it converges faster than gradient descent of rate
\mathcal{O}(1/T)
on convex objective function for large enough
T=\Omega(1/(1-\gamma)^{3})
.

3.3
Practical Implementation of Lookahead Step Size Scheduling

It is generally understood that the early stage of deep learning optimization is unstable.
As a remedy, we will schedule the lookahead step size
\beta_{t}
by three stages: 1) the warm-up stage where
\beta_{t}=0
for
t\leq T_{w}
; 2) the lookahead stage where
\beta_{t}=\beta\cdot\alpha_{t}/(\max_{k}\alpha_{k})
for
T_{w}<t\leq T_{r}
such that the lookahead step size relatively follows
\alpha_{t}/(\max_{k}\alpha_{k})
the learning rate schedule; and 3) the early rest stage where
\beta_{t}=0
for
t>T_{r}
. The early rest stage is crucial to allow stable convergence to sharp local minima during the learning rate decay phase, as the EMA lookahead direction cannot quickly adapt to the local geometry near sharp minima. This is further validated by the observation in Figure
8
, where the EMA lookahead direction leads to sharp increase in loss in later stage of the training. In our experiments, the scheduling of
\beta_{t}
(
T_{w}
and
T_{r}
) roughly follows the learning rate schedule as the learning rate schedule implicitly controls when stable training occurs. Details of the actual schedule used in our experiments can be found in Appendix
C.4
.

Conclusion

We proposed a lightweight wrapper algorithm called

that is motivated by the low-pass filtering of lookahead directions in deep learning setting.

achieved acceleration in practical deep learning problems and theoretical convex problems.

Appendix A
Reduction of Existing Algorithms into Lookahead Form

In this section, we include the missing proofs for reducing the existing algorithms into its lookahead form.

A.1
SNOO

SNOO
[
10
]
(originated from DiLoCo
[
3
]
in federated learning) utilizes the
K
-step base optimizer update as a pseudo-gradient oracle for Nesterov’s algorithm. In particular, for any base optimizer
{\cal A}_{t}(\cdot)
and
K>0
inner steps, we can reduce the SNOO algorithm as the following lookahead algorithm with lookahead step size
\beta>0
by the derivation in Proof
1
.

\displaystyle\textbf{for every}

\displaystyle t=nK,n=0,...,\lfloor T/K\rfloor,

(14)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{\theta}}^{t+K}

\displaystyle={\cal A}_{t+K-1}(~\cdots~{\cal A}_{t+1}({\cal A}_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{t}-\beta{\bf b}^{t})))

\displaystyle{\bf b}^{t+K}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{t}-\tilde{\mathbf{\theta}}^{t+K}

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{t+K}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbf{\theta}}^{t+K}

\displaystyle\textbf{end for;}

\displaystyle\textbf{return}~\mathbf{x}^{T}-\beta{\bf b}^{T}.

Proof 1
.

The original SNOO algorithm can be summarized as follows:

\displaystyle\textbf{for every}

\displaystyle t=nK,n=0,...,\lfloor T/K\rfloor,

(15)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{\theta}}^{t+K}

\displaystyle={\cal A}_{t+K-1}(~\cdots~{\cal A}_{t+1}({\cal A}_{t}(\mathbf{\theta}^{t})))

\displaystyle{\bf b}^{t+K}

\displaystyle=\beta{\bf b}^{t}+(\mathbf{\theta}^{t}-\tilde{\mathbf{\theta}}^{t+K})

\displaystyle\mathbf{\theta}^{t+K}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\theta}^{t}-\eta(\beta{\bf b}^{t+K}+(\mathbf{\theta}^{t}-\tilde{\mathbf{\theta}}^{t+K}))

\displaystyle\textbf{end for;}

\displaystyle\textbf{return}~\mathbf{\theta}^{T}.

with momentum
\beta>0
and Nesterov’s learning rate
\eta>0
. The base optimizer
{\cal A}_{t}
is applied for
K
times consecutively from
\mathbf{\theta}^{t}
to produce
\tilde{\mathbf{\theta}}^{t+K}
. However, it is non-trivial to understand the effect of Nesterov’s momentum that is updated only after every
K
stochastic gradient samples. To simplify the algorithm, we apply a change of variable
\mathbf{\theta}^{t}=\mathbf{x}^{t}-\beta{\bf b}^{t}
to revert the transformation of
15
into the following lookahead formulation:

\displaystyle\textbf{for every}

\displaystyle t=nK,n=0,...,\lfloor T/K\rfloor,

(16)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{\theta}}^{t+K}

\displaystyle={\cal A}_{t+K-1}(~\cdots~{\cal A}_{t+1}({\cal A}_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{t}-\beta{\bf b}^{t})))

\displaystyle{\bf b}^{t+K}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{t}-\tilde{\mathbf{\theta}}^{t+K}

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{t+K}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{t}+(\beta-\eta\beta-\eta){\bf b}^{t+K}-(\beta-\eta\beta){\bf b}^{t}

\displaystyle\textbf{end for;}

\displaystyle\textbf{return}~\mathbf{x}^{T}-\beta{\bf b}^{T}.

Then, when
\eta=1
, we have
\mathbf{x}^{t+K}=\mathbf{x}^{t}-{\bf b}^{t+K}
and SNOO is reduced to (
14
).

\square

A.2
GPA

GPA
[
1
]
adopts the primal-averaging framework which approximate Nesterov’s lookahead. By the derivation in Proof
2
, we can reduce GPA into the following lookahead algorithm for learning rate
\gamma_{p}>0
and EMA rate
\beta>0
:

\displaystyle\textbf{for every}

\displaystyle t=0,...,T-1,

(17)

\displaystyle{\bf z}^{t+1}

\displaystyle={\bf z}^{t}-\gamma_{p}\nabla f({\bf y}^{t};\xi^{t})

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\beta{\bf x}^{t}+(1-\beta){\bf z}^{t+1}

\displaystyle{\bf y}^{t+1}

\displaystyle={\bf y}^{t}+(1-\beta)({\bf z}^{t+1}-{\bf z}^{t})+\beta(1-\beta)({\bf z}^{t+1}-{\bf x}^{t})

\displaystyle\textbf{end for;}

\displaystyle\textbf{return}~\mathbf{x}^{T}.

Proof 2
.

We summarize the GPA algorithm as follows:

\displaystyle\textbf{for every}

\displaystyle t=0,...,T-1,

(18)

\displaystyle{\bf y}^{t}

\displaystyle=\beta_{y}{\bf x}^{t}+(1-\beta_{y}){\bf z}^{t}

\displaystyle{\bf z}^{t+1}

\displaystyle={\bf z}^{t}+{\cal A}_{t}({\bf y}^{t})-{\bf y}^{t}

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\beta_{x}{\bf x}^{t}+(1-\beta_{x}){\bf z}^{t+1}

\displaystyle\textbf{end for;}

\displaystyle\textbf{return}~\mathbf{x}^{T}.

for momentum
\beta_{x}\in[0,1),\beta_{y}\in[0,1]
.
Now apply
[
1
, Proposition 1]
to obtain the following equivalent algorithm when
{\cal A}_{t}({\bf y})={\bf y}-\gamma_{p}\nabla f({\bf y};\xi^{t})
:

\displaystyle\textbf{for every}

\displaystyle t=0,...,T-1,

(19)

\displaystyle{\bf z}^{t+1}

\displaystyle={\bf z}^{t}-\gamma_{p}\nabla f({\bf y}^{t};\xi^{t})

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\beta{\bf x}^{t}+(1-\beta){\bf z}^{t+1}

\displaystyle{\bf b}^{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma_{m}}({\bf x}^{t}-{\bf x}^{t+1})=\beta{\bf b}^{t-1}+\nabla f({\bf y}^{t};\xi^{t})

\displaystyle{\bf y}^{t+1}

\displaystyle={\bf y}^{t}-\gamma_{m}(\beta{\bf b}^{t}+\nabla f({\bf y}^{t};\xi^{t}))

\displaystyle\textbf{end for;}

\displaystyle\textbf{return}~\mathbf{x}^{T}.

with
\beta=\beta_{x}=\beta_{y}
and
\gamma_{m}=(1-\beta)\gamma_{p}
. To obtain (
17
), we first prove that
{\bf x}^{t}={\bf z}^{t}+\gamma_{p}\beta{\bf b}^{t-1}
because

\displaystyle{\bf z}^{t}+\gamma_{p}\beta{\bf b}^{t-1}

\displaystyle={\bf z}^{t}+\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}({\bf x}^{t-1}-{\bf x}^{t})

(20)

\displaystyle={\bf z}^{t}+\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}({\bf x}^{t-1}-\beta{\bf x}^{t-1}-(1-\beta){\bf z}^{t})

(21)

\displaystyle=\beta{\bf x}^{t-1}+(1-\beta){\bf z}^{t}={\bf x}^{t}.

(22)

Then, to simplify
{\bf y}^{t+1}
, we observe that

\displaystyle{\bf b}^{t}

\displaystyle=\beta{\bf b}^{t-1}+\nabla f({\bf y}^{t};\xi^{t})=\beta{\bf b}^{t-1}+\frac{1}{\gamma_{p}}({\bf z}^{t}-{\bf z}^{t+1})=\frac{1}{\gamma_{p}}({\bf x}^{t}-{\bf z}^{t+1}),

(23)

therefore,

\displaystyle{\bf y}^{t+1}

\displaystyle={\bf y}^{t}-\gamma_{m}\beta{\bf b}^{t}-\gamma_{m}\nabla f({\bf y}^{t};\xi^{t})

(24)

\displaystyle={\bf y}^{t}-\frac{\gamma_{m}\beta}{\gamma_{p}}({\bf x}^{t}-{\bf z}^{t+1})-\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\gamma_{p}}({\bf z}^{t}-{\bf z}^{t+1})

(25)

\displaystyle={\bf y}^{t}+(1-\beta)({\bf z}^{t+1}-{\bf z}^{t})+\beta(1-\beta)({\bf z}^{t+1}-{\bf x}^{t}).

(26)

\square

Appendix B
Convergence Proof

Notation.
We denote
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{2}
as the vector 2-norm.

B.1
Strongly Convex

Under an appropriate choice of Lyapunov function
{\cal E}_{t}
where

{\cal E}_{t}:=f(\mathbf{x}^{t})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|{\bf v}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|^{2}

(27)

where
{\bf v}^{t}:=\mathbf{x}^{t}+c{\bf m}^{t}
for some
c>0
, we provide a proof of linear convergence towards the optimal solution as follows.

First, we expand the one step evolution of every variable as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{t+1}=\mathbf{y}^{t}-\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})=\mathbf{x}^{t}+\beta\mathbf{m}^{t}-\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t}),

(28)

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\gamma\mathbf{m}^{t}+(1-\gamma)(\mathbf{x}^{t+1}-\mathbf{x}^{t})

(29)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:x_one_step}}}{{=}}\gamma\mathbf{m}^{t}+(1-\gamma)(\beta\mathbf{m}^{t}-\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t}))

(30)

\displaystyle=(\gamma+(1-\gamma)\beta)\mathbf{m}^{t}-(1-\gamma)\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t}).

(31)

Then, the one step evolution of
\mathbf{v}^{t}
can be derived as

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{t+1}+c\mathbf{m}^{t+1}

(32)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{t}+\beta\mathbf{m}^{t}-\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})+c\left[(\gamma+(1-\gamma)\beta)\mathbf{m}^{t}-(1-\gamma)\alpha\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\right]

(33)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{t}+[\beta+c(\gamma+(1-\gamma)\beta)]\mathbf{m}^{t}-\alpha[1+c(1-\gamma)]\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t}).

(34)

Now we wish to find some
c>0
such that the above equation can be equivalently reduced to

\mathbf{v}^{t+1}=(1-r)\mathbf{v}^{t}+r\left(\mathbf{y}^{t}-\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\right)

(35)

for some
r>0
. In this regard, we expand the R.H.S. of (
35
) and obtain

\displaystyle(1-r)(\mathbf{x}^{t}+c\mathbf{m}^{t})+r\left(\mathbf{x}^{t}+\beta\mathbf{m}^{t}-\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\right)

(36)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{t}+[(1-r)c+r\beta]\mathbf{m}^{t}-\frac{r}{\mu}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t}).

(37)

Then we are left with matching the coefficients between (
34
) and (
37
), i.e., we find a set of
c,r,\beta
such that

\displaystyle\beta+c(\gamma+(1-\gamma)\beta)=(1-r)c+r\beta

(38)

and

\displaystyle\alpha[1+c(1-\gamma)]=\frac{r}{\mu}\Leftrightarrow r=\alpha\mu[1+c(1-\gamma)].

(39)

By choosing

c=\frac{1-\gamma-r}{(1-\gamma)r},

(40)

we deduce that

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=\alpha\mu\left(1+\frac{1-\gamma-r}{r}\right)=\alpha\mu\frac{1-\gamma}{r}

(41)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow r=\sqrt{\alpha\mu(1-\gamma)}

(42)

will match the gradient term coefficient as written in (
39
). Meanwhile, back to the coefficient of
\mathbf{m}^{t}
term in (
38
), we get

\displaystyle\beta+\frac{1-\gamma-r}{(1-\gamma)r}(\gamma+(1-\gamma)\beta))=(1-r)\frac{1-\gamma-r}{(1-\gamma)r}+r\beta

(43)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\beta(1+\frac{1-\gamma-r}{r}-r)=\frac{1-r}{r}\cdot\frac{1-\gamma-r}{1-\gamma}-\gamma\cdot\frac{1-\gamma-r}{(1-\gamma)r}

(44)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\beta(\frac{1-\gamma}{r}-r)=\frac{(1-\gamma-r)^{2}}{r(1-\gamma)}

(45)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\beta(1-\gamma-r^{2})=\frac{(1-\gamma-r)^{2}}{(1-\gamma)}

(46)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\beta=\frac{(1-\gamma-r)^{2}}{(1-\gamma)(1-\gamma-r^{2})}

(47)

will match the
\mathbf{m}^{t}
term coefficient as well. Next, using (
35
) and the equality
\|(1-\theta){\bf u}+\theta{\bf w}\|^{2}=(1-\theta)\|{\bf u}\|^{2}+\theta\|{\bf w}\|^{2}-\theta(1-\theta)\|{\bf u}-{\bf w}\|^{2}
for any
{\bf u,w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\theta\in[0,1]
, we observe the evolution of the Lyapunov function by expanding

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}^{t+1}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|^{2}

(48)

\displaystyle=\|(1-r)(\mathbf{v}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star})+r(\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}-\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t}))\|^{2}

(49)

\displaystyle=(1-r)\|\mathbf{v}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|^{2}+r\|\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}-\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}-r(1-r)\|\mathbf{v}^{t}-\mathbf{y}^{t}+\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}.

(50)

Notice that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}-\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\mu^{2}}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}-\frac{2}{\mu}\left\langle{\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})}\ \middle|\ {\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}}\right\rangle

(51)

Under
\mu
-strong convexity, we can combine the above with

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\geq f(\mathbf{y}^{t})+\left\langle{\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})}\ \middle|\ {\mathbf{x}^{\star}-\mathbf{y}^{t}}\right\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|^{2}

(52)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow-\left\langle{\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})}\ \middle|\ {\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}}\right\rangle\leq f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})-f(\mathbf{y}^{t})-\frac{\mu}{2}\|\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|^{2}

(53)

and obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}-\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\mu^{2}}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}-\frac{2}{\mu}(f(\mathbf{y}^{t})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})).

(54)

Putting together (
50
) and (
54
) yields

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}^{t+1}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1-r)\|\mathbf{v}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{r}{\mu^{2}}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}-\frac{2r}{\mu}(f(\mathbf{y}^{t})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star}))

(55)

\displaystyle\quad-r(1-r)\|\mathbf{v}^{t}-\mathbf{y}^{t}+\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}.

(56)

Meanwhile, under
L
-smooth assumption,

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}^{t+1})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq f(\mathbf{y}^{t})+\left\langle{\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})}\ \middle|\ {\mathbf{x}^{t+1}-\mathbf{y}^{t}}\right\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\mathbf{x}^{t+1}-\mathbf{y}^{t}\|^{2}-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})

(57)

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{y}^{t})-\alpha\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha^{2}L}{2}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})

(58)

\displaystyle\leq f(\mathbf{y}^{t})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}

(59)

when
\alpha^{2}L/2\leq\alpha/2\Leftrightarrow\alpha\leq 1/L
. Combining (
56
) and (
59
) together gives us the recursion of the Lyapunov function as

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{t+1}

\displaystyle\leq(1-r)(f(\mathbf{y}^{t})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star}))+(\frac{r}{2\mu}-\frac{\alpha}{2})\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}+\frac{\mu(1-r)}{2}\|\mathbf{v}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|^{2}

(60)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\mu r(1-r)}{2}\|\mathbf{v}^{t}-\mathbf{y}^{t}+\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}

(61)

\displaystyle=(1-r){\cal E}_{t}+(1-r)(f(\mathbf{y}^{t})-f(\mathbf{x}^{t}))+(\frac{r}{2\mu}-\frac{\alpha}{2})\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}

(62)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\mu r(1-r)}{2}\|\mathbf{v}^{t}-\mathbf{y}^{t}+\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}

(63)

\displaystyle\leq(1-r){\cal E}_{t}+(1-r)\left\langle{\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})}\ \middle|\ {\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t}}\right\rangle-\frac{\mu(1-r)}{2}\|\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t}\|^{2}

(64)

\displaystyle\quad+(\frac{r}{2\mu}-\frac{\alpha}{2})\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}-\frac{\mu r(1-r)}{2}\|\mathbf{v}^{t}-\mathbf{y}^{t}+\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2},

(65)

where the last inequality is due to
\mu
-strong convexity.
Now recall by the definition of
\mathbf{v}^{t}=\mathbf{x}^{t}+c\mathbf{m}^{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{t}+\beta\mathbf{m}^{t}=\left(1-\beta/c\right)\mathbf{x}^{t}+\beta/c\cdot\mathbf{v}^{t}

(66)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\mathbf{v}^{t}

\displaystyle=c/\beta\cdot\mathbf{y}^{t}-\left(c/\beta-1\right)\mathbf{x}^{t}

(67)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\mathbf{v}^{t}-\mathbf{y}^{t}

\displaystyle=\left(c/\beta-1\right)(\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t}).

(68)

This allows us to expand

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}^{t}-\mathbf{y}^{t}+\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(c/\beta-1\right)^{2}\|\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\mu^{2}}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}

(69)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2}{\mu}\left(c/\beta-1\right)\left\langle{\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t}}\ \middle|\ {\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})}\right\rangle.

(70)

Putting it back to the recursion of the Lyapunov function, we get

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{t+1}

\displaystyle\leq(1-r){\cal E}_{t}+[(1-r)-(c/\beta-1)r(1-r)]\left\langle{\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})}\ \middle|\ {\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t}}\right\rangle

(71)

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\frac{\mu(1-r)}{2}+(c/\beta-1)^{2}\frac{\mu r(1-r)}{2}\right)\|\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t}\|^{2}

(72)

\displaystyle\quad-\left(-\frac{r}{2\mu}+\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{r(1-r)}{2\mu}\right)\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}.

(73)

By
\left\langle{\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})}\ \middle|\ {\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t}}\right\rangle\leq\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|\|\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t}\|
, we rewrite the residual by defining
C:=(1-r)(1-r(c/\beta-1))
,
A:=\frac{\mu(1-r)}{2}(1+r(c/\beta-1)^{2})
,
B:=\left(-\frac{r}{2\mu}+\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{r(1-r)}{2\mu}\right)
so that

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{t+1}\leq(1-r){\cal E}_{t}+|C|\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|\cdot\|\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t}\|-A\|\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t}\|^{2}-B\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}.

(74)

To achieve
{\cal E}_{t+1}\leq(1-r){\cal E}_{t}
, our last step is to show

\displaystyle|C|\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|\cdot\|\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t}\|-A\|\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t}\|^{2}-B\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}\leq 0

(75)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow A\left(\|\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t}\|^{2}-\frac{|C|}{A}\cdot\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|\cdot\|\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t}\|+(\frac{|C|}{2A}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|)^{2}\right)

(76)

\displaystyle\quad+(-\frac{C^{2}}{4A}+B)\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}\geq 0

(77)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow A\left(\|\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t}\|-\frac{|C|}{2A}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|\right)^{2}+(B-\frac{C^{2}}{4A})\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}\geq 0.

(78)

Since it is obvious that
A\geq 0
, we are left with verifying
B-C^{2}/(4A)\geq 0\Leftrightarrow 4AB-C^{2}\geq 0
. We begin by simplifying each constant as

B=\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{r^{2}}{2\mu}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:r_choice}}}{{=}}\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{\alpha\mu(1-\gamma)}{2\mu}=\frac{\alpha\gamma}{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:r_choice}}}{{=}}\frac{r^{2}\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)\mu},

(79)

\displaystyle c/\beta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:c_choice},\eqref{eq:beta_choice}}}{{=}}\frac{1-\gamma-r}{(1-\gamma)r}\cdot\frac{(1-\gamma)(1-\gamma-r^{2})}{(1-\gamma-r)^{2}}=\frac{1-\gamma-r^{2}}{r(1-\gamma-r)}

(80)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow c/\beta-1=\frac{(1-r)(1-\gamma)}{r(1-\gamma-r)}

(81)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:c_beta_1}}}{{=}}(1-r)(1-\frac{(1-r)(1-\gamma)}{1-\gamma-r})

(82)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-r}{1-\gamma-r}\cdot(1-\gamma-r-1+r+\gamma-r\gamma)

(83)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma r(1-r)}{1-\gamma-r},

(84)

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:c_beta_1}}}{{=}}\frac{\mu(1-r)}{2}(1+r\cdot\frac{(1-r)^{2}(1-\gamma)^{2}}{r^{2}(1-\gamma-r)^{2}})

(85)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu(1-r)}{2r(1-\gamma-r)^{2}}(r(1-\gamma-r)^{2}+(1-r)^{2}(1-\gamma)^{2}).

(86)

Therefore,

\displaystyle 4AB-C^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{r\gamma(1-r)}{(1-\gamma)(1-\gamma-r)^{2}}(r(1-\gamma-r)^{2}+(1-r)^{2}(1-\gamma)^{2})-\frac{\gamma^{2}r^{2}(1-r)^{2}}{(1-\gamma-r)^{2}}

(87)

\displaystyle=\frac{r\gamma(1-r)}{(1-\gamma-r)^{2}}\left[\frac{r(1-\gamma-r)^{2}}{1-\gamma}+(1-r)^{2}(1-\gamma)-\gamma r(1-r)\right].

(88)

Using

\displaystyle r(1-\gamma-r)^{2}+(1-r)^{2}(1-\gamma)^{2}-(1-\gamma)\gamma r(1-r)

(89)

\displaystyle=r(1-\gamma-r)^{2}+(1-r)(1-\gamma)[(1-r)(1-\gamma)-\gamma r]

(90)

\displaystyle=r(1-\gamma-r)^{2}+(1-r)(1-\gamma)(1-r-\gamma)

(91)

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma-r)(1-\gamma-r^{2}),

(92)

to c
we obtain

\displaystyle 4AB-C^{2}=\frac{r\gamma(1-r)(1-\gamma-r^{2})}{(1-\gamma-r)(1-\gamma)}.

(93)

Since
0\leq r\leq 1,0\leq\gamma\leq 1
, by plugging in
r=\sqrt{\alpha\mu(1-\gamma)}
we have
1-\gamma-r^{2}\geq 0\Leftrightarrow\gamma\leq 1
. Meanwhile,
1-\gamma-r\geq 0\Leftrightarrow\gamma\leq 1-\alpha\mu
. Therefore, we have
4AB-C^{2}\geq 0
and this completes the proof.

\square

B.2
General Convex

For general convex objective function, we aim to adopt another choice of Lyapunov function
\Phi_{t}
where

\Phi_{t}:=A_{t}(f(\mathbf{x}^{t})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star}))+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}^{t}+c_{t}{\bf m}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|^{2}_{2}

(94)

with some
A_{t},c_{t}>0
determined later. Then, we can guarantee a sublinear convergence. Below we present the proof.

Define the auxiliary sequence

\mathbf{v}^{t}:=\mathbf{x}^{t}+c_{t}\mathbf{m}^{t}.

We first expand the one step evolution of every variable as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{t}+\beta_{t}\mathbf{m}^{t}-\frac{1}{L}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t}),

(95)

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}^{t+1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:x_one_step_convex}}}{{=}}\gamma\mathbf{m}^{t}+(1-\gamma)(\mathbf{x}^{t+1}-\mathbf{x}^{t})

(96)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\gamma+(1-\gamma)\beta_{t}\bigr)\mathbf{m}^{t}-\frac{1-\gamma}{L}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t}).

(97)

Then, the one step evolution of
\mathbf{v}^{t}
can be derived as

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{t+1}+c_{t+1}\mathbf{m}^{t+1}

(98)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{t}+\beta_{t}\mathbf{m}^{t}-\frac{1}{L}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})+c_{t+1}\left[\bigl(\gamma+(1-\gamma)\beta_{t}\bigr)\mathbf{m}^{t}-\frac{1-\gamma}{L}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\right]

(99)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{t}+\left[\beta_{t}+c_{t+1}\bigl(\gamma+(1-\gamma)\beta_{t}\bigr)\right]\mathbf{m}^{t}-\frac{1+(1-\gamma)c_{t+1}}{L}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t}).

(100)

By the definition of
\beta_{t}
,

\displaystyle\beta_{t}\bigl(1+(1-\gamma)c_{t+1}\bigr)+\gamma c_{t+1}=c_{t}.

(101)

Thus

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{t}+c_{t}\mathbf{m}^{t}-\frac{1+(1-\gamma)c_{t+1}}{L}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})

(102)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{v}^{t}-\eta_{t}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t}),

(103)

where

\eta_{t}:=(1+(1-\gamma)c_{t+1})/L.

Since
\mathbf{v}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t}=c_{t}\mathbf{m}^{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{t}+\beta_{t}\mathbf{m}^{t}=\mathbf{x}^{t}+\frac{\beta_{t}}{c_{t}}(\mathbf{v}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t})=\left(1-\frac{\beta_{t}}{c_{t}}\right)\mathbf{x}^{t}+\frac{\beta_{t}}{c_{t}}\mathbf{v}^{t}.

(104)

We now find a sequence
A_{t}
so that the following holds:

\displaystyle A_{t+1}\left(1-\frac{\beta_{t}}{c_{t}}\right)\leq A_{t},\qquad A_{t+1}\frac{\beta_{t}}{c_{t}}=\eta_{t}.

(105)

In fact, define a sequence
A_{t}=a_{t}/L
as follows:

\displaystyle a_{t+1}:=\frac{c_{t}q_{t}^{2}}{c_{t}-\gamma c_{t+1}},\qquad t\geq 0,

(106)

where

\displaystyle q_{t}:=1+(1-\gamma)c_{t+1}.

(107)

Specifically, for
t=0
, choose
a_{0}\geq a_{1}-q_{0}
(for example, choose
a_{0}=a_{1}
). For the given choice of
c_{t}
, one can easily verify

\displaystyle c_{t}-\gamma c_{t+1}=(1-\gamma)\left(1+\frac{t}{4}\right),\qquad q_{t}=1+(1-\gamma)c_{t+1}=\frac{5}{4}+(1-\gamma)\left(1+\frac{t}{4}\right).

(108)

Therefore, by (
106
), (
107
) as well as the definition of
\beta_{t}
and
\eta_{t}
,

\displaystyle A_{t+1}\frac{\beta_{t}}{c_{t}}=\frac{a_{t+1}}{L}\cdot\frac{\beta_{t}}{c_{t}}=\frac{1}{L}\frac{c_{t}q_{t}^{2}}{c_{t}-\gamma c_{t+1}}\cdot\frac{c_{t}-\gamma c_{t+1}}{c_{t}q_{t}}=\frac{q_{t}}{L}=\eta_{t}.

(109)

We now show
A_{t+1}\left(1-\frac{\beta_{t}}{c_{t}}\right)\leq A_{t}
, or equivalently,
a_{t+1}-q_{t}\leq a_{t}
. When
t=0
, the inequality holds due to our choice of
a_{0}
. For
t\geq 1
, put
s_{t}:=1+\frac{t}{4}
. Then a direct calculation gives

\displaystyle a_{t}-(a_{t+1}-q_{t})=\frac{8(1-\gamma)^{3}s_{t}(16s_{t}^{2}-1)+36(1-\gamma)^{2}s_{t}(4s_{t}-1)+25\gamma}{64(1-\gamma)^{2}s_{t}(4s_{t}-1)}.

(110)

Since
t\geq 1
, we have
s_{t}\geq 5/4
. Also
0<1-\gamma\leq 1
.
Therefore every term in the numerator is nonnegative and the denominator is
positive. Hence
a_{t}-(a_{t+1}-q_{t})\geq 0
. Thus (
105
) holds.

Given the choice of
A_{t}
, we now prove the Lyapunov function
\Phi_{t}
decreases.

Since
f
is
L
-smooth and

\mathbf{x}^{t+1}=\mathbf{y}^{t}-\frac{1}{L}\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t}),

we have

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}^{t+1})\leq f(\mathbf{y}^{t})-\frac{1}{2L}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}.

(111)

Furthermore, since

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{t}=\left(1-\frac{\beta_{t}}{c_{t}}\right)\mathbf{x}^{t}+\frac{\beta_{t}}{c_{t}}\mathbf{v}^{t},

(112)

we have by convexity that

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{y}^{t})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\beta_{t}}{c_{t}}\right)\bigl(f(\mathbf{y}^{t})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\bigr)+\frac{\beta_{t}}{c_{t}}\bigl(f(\mathbf{y}^{t})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{\beta_{t}}{c_{t}}\right)\left[f(\mathbf{x}^{t})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})-\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\middle|\mathbf{x}^{t}-\mathbf{y}^{t}\right\rangle\right]+\frac{\beta_{t}}{c_{t}}\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\middle|\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\beta_{t}}{c_{t}}\right)\bigl(f(\mathbf{x}^{t})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\bigr)+\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\middle|\frac{\beta_{t}}{c_{t}}(\mathbf{y}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star})-\left(1-\frac{\beta_{t}}{c_{t}}\right)(\mathbf{x}^{t}-\mathbf{y}^{t})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\beta_{t}}{c_{t}}\right)\bigl(f(\mathbf{x}^{t})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\bigr)+\frac{\beta_{t}}{c_{t}}\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\middle|\mathbf{v}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\right\rangle.

(113)

Multiplying (
B.2
) by
A_{t+1}
and then using (
105
) and (
111
),
we obtain

\displaystyle A_{t+1}\bigl(f(\mathbf{x}^{t+1})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq A_{t}\bigl(f(\mathbf{x}^{t})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\bigr)+\eta_{t}\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t}),\mathbf{v}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\rangle-\frac{A_{t+1}}{2L}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}.

(114)

On the other hand, by (
103
) we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{v}^{t+1}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{v}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|^{2}-\eta_{t}\left\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\middle|\mathbf{v}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\right\rangle+\frac{\eta_{t}^{2}}{2}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}.

(115)

Adding (
114
) and (
115
) yields

\displaystyle\Phi_{t+1}\leq\Phi_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{A_{t+1}}{L}-\eta_{t}^{2}\right)\|\nabla f(\mathbf{y}^{t})\|^{2}.

(116)

It remains to check that

\displaystyle\frac{A_{t+1}}{L}\geq\eta_{t}^{2}.

(117)

Since
A_{t+1}=a_{t+1}/L
and
\eta_{t}=q_{t}/L
, this is equivalent to

a_{t+1}\geq q_{t}^{2}
, which apparently holds by our choice of
a_{t+1}
in (
106
). Combining with (
116
), we show that

\displaystyle\Phi_{t+1}\leq\Phi_{t}.

(118)

Consequently,

A_{t}\bigl(f(\mathbf{x}^{t})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\bigr)\leq\Phi_{t}\leq\Phi_{0}
, and therefore

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}^{t})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\leq\frac{\Phi_{0}}{A_{t}}=\frac{L\Phi_{0}}{a_{t}}.

(119)

Next, we lower bound
a_{t}
. For
t\geq 1
, by (
106
) we have

\displaystyle a_{t}=\frac{c_{t-1}q_{t-1}^{2}}{c_{t-1}-\gamma c_{t}}.

(120)

Recalling
s_{t-1}=1+\frac{t-1}{4},

a direct calculation can verify

\displaystyle c_{t-1}\geq s_{t-1},\qquad q_{t-1}\geq(1-\gamma)s_{t-1},\qquad c_{t-1}-\gamma c_{t}=(1-\gamma)s_{t-1}.

(121)

Thus

\displaystyle a_{t}\geq\frac{s_{t-1}\bigl((1-\gamma)s_{t-1}\bigr)^{2}}{(1-\gamma)s_{t-1}}=(1-\gamma)s_{t-1}^{2}\geq\frac{(1-\gamma)t^{2}}{16}.

(122)

Therefore by (
119
) and (
122
), we obtain

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}^{t})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\leq\frac{16L\Phi_{0}}{(1-\gamma)t^{2}}.

(123)

(
123
) provides the
O(1/t^{2})
rate of the algorithm. We further upper bound
\Phi_{0}
to provide the
\gamma
-dependency. Note that
\mathbf{m}^{0}=0
. Since
f
is convex and
L
-smooth and

\mathbf{x}^{\star}
is a minimizer, we have

f(\mathbf{x}^{0})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\leq\frac{L}{2}\|\mathbf{x}^{0}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}.

(124)

Combining (
94
) and (
124
) yields

\Phi_{0}=\frac{a_{0}}{L}\bigl(f(\mathbf{x}^{0})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\bigr)+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}^{0}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{a_{0}+1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}^{0}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}.

(125)

Now we calculate
a_{0}
. By the definition of
c_{t}
,
a_{t}
and
q_{t}
(recall (
106
) and (
107
)), we have

c_{0}=1+\frac{\gamma}{4(1-\gamma)},\qquad c_{1}=\frac{5}{4}+\frac{\gamma}{4(1-\gamma)},\qquad q_{0}=1+(1-\gamma)c_{1},\qquad a_{1}=\frac{c_{0}q_{0}^{2}}{c_{0}-\gamma c_{1}}.

(126)

We need
a_{0}\geq a_{1}-q_{0}
. By simply choosing
a_{0}=a_{1}
, a direct calculation yields

a_{0}=a_{1}=\frac{(4-3\gamma)(9-4\gamma)^{2}}{64(1-\gamma)^{2}}\leq\frac{81}{16(1-\gamma)^{2}}.

(127)

Therefore, by combining (
123
), (
125
) and (
127
), we get

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}^{t})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\star})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{16L}{(1-\gamma)t^{2}}\cdot\frac{a_{0}+1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}^{0}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}

(128)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{16L}{(1-\gamma)t^{2}}\cdot\frac{\|\mathbf{x}^{0}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{97}{16(1-\gamma)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{97L}{2(1-\gamma)^{3}t^{2}}\|\mathbf{x}^{0}-\mathbf{x}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}.

This completes the proof.

\square

We remark that in the source code of Muon, the convex combination between
