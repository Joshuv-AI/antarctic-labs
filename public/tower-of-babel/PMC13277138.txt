Critical evaluation of “Diffuse reflectance spectroscopy for enhanced diagnostic precision in breast cancer”
Authors: Zubaida Bibi, Kausar Ali
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the article by Feenstra et al. investigating the integration of diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) into vacuum-assisted breast biopsy (VABB) to improve diagnostic precision in breast cancer [1]. The authors should be commended for their innovative optical introducer design, rigorous histopathologic correlation, and comprehensive machine-learning–based analysis addressing an important clinical challenge. Nevertheless, several limitations merit further consideration when interpreting the translational implications of the findings.

First, the investigation was conducted exclusively on ex vivo lumpectomy specimens, which might not accurately reflect the physiological and procedural circumstances of in vivo VABB. Variations in blood perfusion, tissue oxygenation, and mechanical compression are known to alter optical tissue properties and can influence both spectral characteristics and classification performance [2]. As a result, real-time clinical biopsy settings may not immediately benefit from diagnostic accuracy observed ex vivo.

Second, although multiple measurements were obtained from individual patients, classification performance was evaluated at the spectrum level rather than the patient level. In the absence of dedicated patient-level validation strategies, such as leave-one-patient-out cross-validation, intra-patient spectral similarity may lead to overestimation of model performance. This methodological limitation is widely recognized in medical machine-learning research as a common source of optimistic bias [3].

Third, although diffuse reflectance spectroscopy interrogates a three-dimensional tissue volume, ground-truth labels were obtained from two-dimensional histological sections. This inherent spatial discordance introduces labeling uncertainty that may impact both model training and the reported diagnostic performance, particularly when assessing low tumor-percentage thresholds. Similar challenges have been reported across optical spectroscopy and hyperspectral imaging studies relying on histopathologic correlation [4].

Lastly, despite the use of repeated cross-validation, the absence of independent or external validation constrains the generalizability of the findings. Predictive models frequently demonstrate reduced performance when applied to independent cohorts, underscoring the importance of external validation before clinical implementation [5].

In conclusion, the aforementioned limitations underscore the necessity of prospective in vivo research, patient-level validation, and external cohort testing to completely prove clinical value, even if this study offers strong proof of concept evidence supporting DRS guided VABB.
