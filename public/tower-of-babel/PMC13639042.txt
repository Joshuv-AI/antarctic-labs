Auditory cueing modulates motor performance and brain activity in early Parkinson’s disease—a kinematic and fMRI study
Authors: Andrea Gardoni, Elisabetta Sarasso, Marco Forghieri, Andrea Grassi, Roberta Balestrino, Silvia Basaia, Chiara Tripodi, Elisa Canu, Maria Antonietta Volontè, Davide Corbetta, Massimo Filippi, Federica Agosta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cues are specific references that support motor performance by improving speed, timing, and amplitude while reducing variability. This study aimed at investigating the effects of external auditory cueing on bradykinesia-related kinematic deficits and their neural correlates during a hand-tapping task in people with Parkinson’s disease (pwPD) and healthy controls (HC). Twenty-six pwPD and 20 HC performed a right hand-tapping fMRI task either in a self-paced condition (NOCue) or following an auditory cue (Cue). Hand-tapping kinematics were objectively quantified using an optical-fiber glove. PwPD showed reduced hand-tapping amplitude and a greater sequence effect on amplitude than HC during NOCue condition. Cueing attenuated the sequence effect on amplitude in pwPD. During the NOCue condition, pwPD showed reduced activity of pallidum/thalamus relative to HC. No group differences emerged during Cue condition. During Cue relative to NOCue condition, pwPD showed higher activity of pallidum, parietal, temporal, sensorimotor, insular, and cerebellar regions, alongside reduced activation in of paracentral gyrus and cerebellum. Higher activity of pallidum correlated with better motor performance and higher positive effects of cueing. Our results demonstrated that auditory cueing reduces bradykinesia in pwPD possibly by facilitating basal ganglia activity and reducing the need of movement programming.

Introduction

Bradykinesia has been recently redefined to include a broader complex of motor alterations rather than the classical progressive reduction in amplitude and/or speed with the repetition of movement1. The “bradykinesia complex” encompasses bradykinesia, hypokinesia characterized by reduced movement amplitude, akinesia, oligokinesia defined as a reduction or absence of spontaneous or automatic movements, as well as sequence effect and hesitations or halts. This constellation of motor features reflects a network-level dysfunction involving impaired motor scaling, altered sequence generation, defective sensorimotor integration, and reduced ability to maintain movement rhythm over time1–3. Bradykinesia is the cardinal motor manifestation in people with Parkinson’s disease (pwPD) and a major contributor to disability in daily activities4,5. There is a great interest in understanding the neural correlates of this phenomenon, and in providing valid measurement tools and rehabilitation strategies6–9. Among rehabilitation strategies for pwPD, external cueing is widely employed. Cues are described as specific reference points aimed at supporting and enhancing motor performance10. They are classified into three primary modalities: visual cues, auditory cues, and somatosensory cues. The primary neurophysiological explanation for the effectiveness of external cueing is that cues promote a shift from impaired neural pathways to intact ones, effectively redirecting behavior from habitual patterns to more goal-directed actions11. Rhythmic auditory cues involve the connections between the auditory and motor systems by using metronomes or familiar music to deliver the external cues. The optimal frequency for spontaneous human movement typically falls within cycles of 500–600 ms, consistent with the 2-Hz human resonance theory, a phenomenon observed across behaviors such as walking and clapping and reflected in the preferred tempo of most music. This strategy has been shown to improve movement speed, timing, and amplitude, while also reducing variability in gait, as well as in upper limbs and finger movements12.

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has provided valuable insight into the altered motor circuitry in pwPD. Across investigations, differences converged in the posterior putamen, precentral gyrus, and cerebellum13. Studies have used a variety of tasks to assess brain activity related to movement and to the effects of cueing14–16. When focusing on hand movement, studies have documented aberrant activation patterns and altered connectivity in key regions for temporal processing such as supplementary motor area (SMA), basal ganglia, cerebellum, and right inferior frontal and parietal cortices in pwPD compared to healthy controls during cued tasks17. However, brain activity differences can reflect either true differences in neural control or simply differences in executed kinematics (e.g., slower speed, smaller amplitude, or variable movement)9,18–20. Simultaneous acquisition of high-resolution kinematics and fMRI addresses this matter but remains rare, limiting the interpretation of the results. Magnetic resonance-compatible devices, such as optical fiber data gloves, allow direct recording of amplitude, timing, and progressive amplitude/speed decrement during in-scanner tasks. Using this methodology, we recently compared pwPD and matched healthy controls performing hand tapping movements during fMRI9. PwPD showed reduced amplitude, greater sequence effect, and concurrent reductions in activation of SMA, frontoparietal cortex, thalamus/pallidum, and cerebellar motor areas9.

Nonetheless, most published works still treats kinematics and imaging separately or uses only gross behavioral measures, leaving a gap in our mechanistic understanding of how cueing alters both movement execution and its neural substrates21.

The present study addressed these gaps using an optic fiber glove to extract objective kinematic parameters during a hand tapping fMRI task to compare internally generated (self-paced) and externally cued movement in pwPD and matched healthy controls. The study aimed at determining the effects of auditory cues on motor performance and on underlying neural activity.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the neural and kinematic correlates of repetitive hand tapping with and without auditory cues in pwPD using an MRI-compatible optic fiber glove that was previously used and validated9,22,23. The study included patients with early PD and with right upper limb motor involvement. We found that external auditory cues could modulate both movement performance and underlying brain activity in pwPD.

Consistent to what we previously showed, during self-paced tapping, pwPD patients exhibited movement alterations typical of the bradykinetic complex and particularly hypokinesia and sequence effect manifested as reduced movement amplitude and a greater tendency to progressively decrease amplitude with repetitions2.

At the neural level, pwPD and healthy controls showed a pattern of activation consistent with previous extensive evidence on brain regions associated with motor task performance, such as the primary sensorimotor cortex, SMA, basal ganglia, and cerebellum14,24,25.

However, during self-paced tapping, pwPD showed reduced activation of pallidum/thalamus relative to healthy controls, which was correlated with the reduced movement amplitude measured with the optical glove. Sequential movements highly depend on basal ganglia-cortical interaction as they require the correct implementation and control of movement amplitude and frequency15.

Specifically, pallidum is a key structure that contributes to the regulation of movement amplitude by modulating the inhibitory output of the basal ganglia toward thalamo-cortical motor circuits, thereby scaling motor commands and ensuring stable execution of sequential movements over time26. Under physiological conditions, the SMA and the basal ganglia form a functional loop that acts as an internal cueing system, in which continuous pacing-related adjustments are crucial for sustaining ongoing movements. With practice, this process becomes progressively automatized; however, it deteriorates in PD as a consequence of basal ganglia degeneration16,27.

Moving to the primary aim of this study, during hand tapping with auditory cueing, subjects revealed changes in both movement execution and sensorimotor neural processing. Indeed, both between- and within-group analyses revealed a significant reduction of the sequence effect in pwPD. Specifically, while during self-paced hand tapping they showed a progressive reduction of movement amplitude with repetition, during the cued task they tended to increase amplitude, similarly to healthy controls. This occurred despite a slight reduction in movement speed associated with following the imposed rhythm, which likely reflects a functional trade-off to maintain amplitude rather than a deficit in performance. This result aligns with the “stabilizing role” of cues proposed by Ginis and colleagues, suggesting that auditory cueing supports more appropriate timing and amplitude, stabilizing spatiotemporal parameters of movement10,28.

Moreover, during the Cue task, pwPD did not differ from healthy controls in either average movement amplitude or neural activity. We can speculate that external auditory pacing may compensate for impaired internal motor regulation by facilitating basal ganglia–sensorimotor processing and, consequently, motor output in pwPD29–32. However, this interpretation should be considered cautiously, as it is solely based on the absence of significant between-group differences (that does not imply equivalence).

As expected during a task involving hearing, similar to healthy subjects, pwPD exhibited increased activation in temporo-parietal regions involved in auditory processing. Previous studies have suggested that rhythmic auditory cues may exert network-level effects in pwPD, enhancing interactions between auditory and executive networks that, in turn, modulate cortico-cerebellar circuits33. Consistent with entrainment models, such auditory stimulation can synchronize and stabilize motor responses in pwPD34, possibly reducing another relevant aspect of the bradykinesia in pwPD that is dysrhythmia35,36. In support of this previous evidence, we found that during Cue condition relative to NOCue task, pwPD showed increased activity of basal ganglia, precentral gyrus, and motor part of cerebellum (lobule) VIII, which are key structures for sensorimotor integration, timing, and movement execution28,37,38. Interestingly, the increased activity of the pallidum was correlated with the reduced sequence effect on amplitude that we observed with cueing in pwPD supporting its stabilizing effect on movement. Increased activity of pallidum was mainly located in the external portion. The involvement of external globus pallidus is consistent with previous evidence showing that its activity is related to movement speed and amplitude, particularly during cued movements39.

Moreover, during the cued condition compared to self-paced tapping, pwPD showed decreased activity in the paracentral gyrus, SMA, and cerebellar lobules IV–VI, which may reflect a reduced demand for movement planning and a shift of motor control toward more efficient sensorimotor integration networks. This observation aligns with previous evidence: a study on the neural correlates of the sequence effect during handwriting in pwPD demonstrated a link between micrographia and dysfunction of basal ganglia motor circuits, coupled with reduced connectivity with the pre-SMA and cerebellum40. Similarly, a recent meta-analysis reported decreased SMA and cerebellar activity during finger tapping with auditory cues relative to self-paced tasks, supporting the idea that cueing may streamline motor execution by optimizing the recruitment of sensorimotor networks14.

A major strength of the study lies in the concurrent acquisition of fMRI data and detailed kinematic recordings of hand movements. This multimodal approach addresses a clinical limitation of most fMRI investigations in pwPD, which typically lack simultaneous high-resolution behavioral data41. To further study PD-associated brain pathophysiology, other MRI approaches like DTI or volumetry may be added in future studies to further correlate the activation patterns to microstructural/structural damage associated with the disease. The study also has some limitations. The sample size was relatively small, reflecting the inherent challenges of recruiting pwPD who can tolerate and successfully complete fMRI trials. Moreover, the inclusion criteria defined a specific sample of pwPD with right upper limb motor involvement, reducing generalizability of results. To maximize the robustness of our findings, fMRI results were reported using FWE correction. The enrolled participants were characterized by a relatively short disease duration (mean 4 years), which may have facilitated the detection of early features of the bradykinesia complex, such as the sequence effect42. At the same time, the ability to capture these subtle kinematic alterations in an early-stage cohort represents a major strength of the study. Future study could assess a pwPD with different characteristics and ideally also follow patients longitudinally to track how bradykinesia and its response to cues evolve with disease progression. To minimize medication effects on brain activity, patients were tested in the OFF state, nevertheless, given the ample range of LEDD in our sample and the influence that it could have on motor performance, we assessed the correlation between LEDD and kinematic and fMRI data and found no significant result. Future investigations should extend this paradigm to patients in the ON state and compare both conditions. Furthermore, the study focused exclusively on auditory cueing; investigating the effects of different types of cues (e.g., visual) or varying cue parameters (e.g., frequency) would offer a more comprehensive understanding of cueing effects. Finally, we only assessed the immediate effects of auditory cueing, so conclusions regarding long-term retention, generalization to complex tasks, or durability of neural changes cannot be drawn. Another limitation of the study is the fixed order of the tasks: the improvements could partly reflect repetition of the task rather than effects of cueing. However, the simplicity of the task and the familiarization before the acquisition limited possible learning effects. Moreover, the effects of fatigue were mitigated by the acquisition of other MRI sequences between the two tasks to provide some minutes of rest. A further limitation concerns the potential circularity in the brain-behavior correlation analyses, as the regions of interest and the kinematic variables derived from the task are not fully independent. We chose to use a hypothesis-driven approach and limit the correlations to data significantly different within/between-group to reduce the number of correlations and consequently the risk of false positives or confounding results.

In conclusion, these findings suggest that external auditory cues may modulate both motor performance and brain activity in pwPD. Specifically, during their application, cues might stabilize motor performance and counteract bradykinesia by maintaining movement amplitude. Possible mechanisms underlying these movement improvements may be a re-engagement of the pallidum/thalamus, reduced demand for movement planning, and more efficient recruitment of motor resources. This study provides additional insight into the mechanisms underlying cueing, reinforcing the idea that rhythmic auditory stimulation could be considered as tool to support motor control in rehabilitation settings.

Methods

Subjects and study design

This study is part of a randomized controlled trial that investigated the effects of physiotherapy on upper limb function in pwPD (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT04876352). Right-handed outpatients with idiopathic PD43 were recruited at the Movement Disorder Unit, Neurology Department, IRCCS Ospedale San Raffaele, Milan, Italy, according to the following inclusion criteria: Hoehn & Yahr (H&Y) stage ≤3 while on medication; age ≤85 years; right-side motor involvement based on H&Y and MDS-UPDRS-III; and handwriting difficulties (MDS-UPDRS II.7 ≥ 1). Age- and sex-matched right-handed healthy controls were recruited through word of mouth among non-consanguineous relatives and institute staff.

Exclusion criteria for both pwPD and healthy controls included: Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) < 24 in pwPD or <28 in healthy controls; cerebrovascular disorders; a history of alcohol and/or psychotropic substance abuse; and failure to provide oral and written informed consent. All participants (pwPD and healthy controls) underwent dexterity and upper limb motor functional assessments, respectively with Purdue Pegboard Test (PPT) and Manual Ability Measure questionnaire (MAM-36), neuropsychological screening with the MMSE and brain MRI scans. PwPD also received a neurological assessment: an experienced neurologist administered H&Y staging and MDS-UPDRS parts II and III in both the ON and OFF medication states (the latter conducted at least 12 h after the regular evening dose of dopaminergic medication, or 24 h for dopamine agonists).

The study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee for human research (Ethical Committee IRCCS San Raffaele Scientific Institute, Milan, Italy; No. 68/int/2019), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion in the study. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki.

MRI protocol

Using a 3.0 Tesla Philips Intera scanner (Ingenia CX, Philips Medical System, Best, The Netherlands), brain MRI scans were obtained during OFF time (at least 12 h after the regular evening dopaminergic therapy administration, except for dopamine agonists that required 24 h discontinuation), to mitigate the pharmacological effects on neural activity. Both functional and structural MRI sequences were acquired. The following structural brain MRI sequences were acquired to exclude subjects with eventual structural brain alterations and/or excessive vascular lesions: (i) 3D T2-weighted: repetition time (TR) = 2500 ms, echo time (TE) = 330 ms, flip angle = 90°, 192 contiguous sagittal sections, thickness = 1 mm, field of view (FOV) = 256 mm × 256 mm, matrix = 256 × 258, voxel reconstruction = 0.9 mm × 0.9 mm × 1 mm; (ii) 3DT1-weighted: TR = 7.1 ms, TE = 3.2 ms, flip angle = 9°, 204 contiguous sagittal sections, thickness = 1 mm, FOV = 256 mm × 240 mm, matrix = 256 × 240, voxel reconstruction = 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm; (iii) 3D Flair: TR = 4800 ms; TE = 269 ms; flip angle = 40°; 192 contiguous sagittal sections; thickness = 1.5 mm; FOV = 256 × 256 mm; matrix = 256 × 256, voxel reconstruction 1 × 1 × 1.

Functional MRI study

FMRI was obtained using a T2* weighted echo planar imaging sequence with the following parameters: TE = 35 ms, TR = 1572 ms, flip angle = 70°, FOV = 240 × 240 mm, matrix = 96 × 94, 48 contiguous axial sections, thickness = 3 mm, voxel reconstruction 2.5 × 2.5 × 3 mm. During the fMRI hand tapping task, participants were instructed to open and close their right hand as ample and fast as possible for about 10 s (following the performance guidelines of MDS-UPDRS item 3.5) while wearing an MRI-compatible optical fiber glove (NOcue condition). Participants then performed the same maximal-amplitude hand tapping movements but following an external auditory cue (metronome with electronic beep) presented at 2 Hz (Cue condition). Patients were asked to open or close their right hand every time they heard a sound in the headphones. The 2 Hz represents an ideal frequency that allows pwPD to correctly react to each single cue44. A block design (ABAB) was used during the tasks: activation periods (A) corresponded to the execution of the hand tapping (six hand tapping periods lasting about 10 s each), while rest periods (B) represented resting periods without movements (six resting periods lasting about 10 s each). Subjects wore also an MRI-compatible headset which projected the commands to start and stop the task and a fixed cross during both the hand tapping movements and the resting period. Sandbags were used to position participants’ hands and prevent unwanted movements. Before entering the scanner, patients familiarized themselves with the hand movement required during the task both with and without cues to make sure they were able to correctly perform the requested movement in each condition.

Optical fiber glove

As previously reported9, we employed the optical fiber 5DT Data Glove 14 Ultra (Fifth Dimension Technologies Inc., Orlando, USA), a one-size hand motion-capture device designed to measure finger flexion and abduction. Flexion of the fingers was detected at the metacarpal and proximal interphalangeal joints through 14 optical fiber sensors with a 12-bit A/D resolution (typically 10-bit range), integrated into a stretchable Lycra glove that ensures proper fitting. Fiber loops were connected to an LED, and the glove estimated finger flexion indirectly based on the amount of light returned to a phototransistor. The system was connected to a computer through a full-speed USB cable. A custom software application was developed specifically to record the glove’s signals.

Hand tapping kinematic parameter analysis

Hand tapping kinematic parameters recorded with the optical fiber glove were processed using custom-made software. Each subject completed six hand-tapping periods for each condition. The software automatically identified amplitude peaks and counted the number of gestures based on the alternation between high peaks (open hand) and low peaks (closed hand), considering only movements with >20° excursion at the metacarpophalangeal joints. Peak detection was performed using a threshold-/crossing-based approach rather than derivative-based or template-matching methods. First, the raw signal was smoothed online by a moving average. Candidate movement segments were then identified from the intersections between the raw signal and its moving average, while enforcing a minimum temporal distance between consecutive crossings based on the estimated main frequency. Within each segment, local minima and maxima were extracted from the raw signal, and only segments showing sufficient excursion relative to the moving average were retained. Gestures were finally defined by pairing each peak with the preceding trough; very small movements were excluded using a minimum-amplitude threshold, and abnormally large events were removed as amplitude outliers. An operator visually inspected the amplitude and speed profiles for every hand tapping period on a computer screen to confirm that the automated detection procedure functioned properly. For each hand tapping period, the software extracted the number of hand tapping movements, the average amplitude and speed across all fingers, and the tendency to change movement speed and/or amplitude with repeated execution (sequence effect). Sequence effect was calculated as the slope of a linear regression fitted across successive movements, with movement time as the independent variable and amplitude (or speed) as the dependent variable. Parameters from the hand tapping periods in each condition were then averaged for the analyses.

Functional MRI analysis

FMRI data were analyzed using SPM12 software (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm; Wellcome Trust Center for Neuroimaging, London, UK). Prior to statistical analysis, all images were realigned to the first one to correct for subject head motion (all study participants showed maximal head movements lower than 3 mm in each direction), slice-timing corrected, coregistered to subject’s 3DT1, spatially normalized into the standard MNI (Montreal Neurological Institute) space and smoothed applying a 8-mm 3-D Gaussian filter to improve signal-to-noise ratio and accommodate residual inter-subject anatomical variability following spatial normalization45. The signal variations of the BOLD effect associated with the execution of each task (considering head movement parameters as confounds) were evaluated voxel by voxel using the general linear model (GLM) and Gaussian field theory. Specific effects were tested applying appropriate linear contrasts. Significant hemodynamic changes for each contrast were evaluated using a nonparametric permutation-based approach, that is, Statistical non-Parametric Mapping (SnPM), a toolbox for SPM46.

One-sample t tests were used to assess the average fMRI activity during each task, while the paired t test was used to evaluate within group contrasts between Cue and NOCue conditions; differences between groups (healthy controls vs pwPD) were assessed using a two-sample t tests including education as covariate. Differences between the pwPD and healthy controls in Cue vs NOCue conditions were evaluated using a GLM model, in which group and condition were included as distinct factors (2 × 2 factorial design) with education as covariate.

To restrict the search space and reduce multiple comparisons, a mask was generated using the AAL atlas and implemented in SnPM via the WFU PickAtlas. The mask included the bilateral precentral and postcentral gyri, SMA, paracentral lobule, superior and inferior parietal cortices, caudate, putamen, pallidum, thalamus, insula, Heschl’s gyrus, superior/middle/inferior temporal cortices, and the cerebellum; these areas were selected as part of the sensorimotor network and involved in the processing of auditory information33,47. Supplementary Figure 1 reports the fMRI analyses results with the applied mask. For all permutation-based contrasts, nonparametric testing was performed with 5000 random permutations. Findings are shown FWE corrected at voxel level (p < 0.05). Only clusters exceeding 10 voxels were retained for interpretation.

We overlapped clusters of fMRI results to the AAL atlas to identify the motor areas showing significant differences in between-group (pwPD vs healthy controls) or within-pwPD (Cue vs NOCue). Mean t values were extracted from those motor regions to perform correlation analyses with kinematic data.

Statistical analysis

Age and education were compared between groups using analysis of variance (ANOVA) models on rank-transformed data (p < 0.05). Given the different education between pwPD and healthy controls, education-adjusted ANCOVA models (p < 0.05) on rank-transformed data or χ2 test for categorical variables (p < 0.05) were used to compare clinical and kinematic data between groups. We reported both results surviving Bonferroni correction for number of groups, and uncorrected results for exploratory interpretations.

General linear models for repeated measures on rank-transformed values were used to compare the effect of auditory cues within group; the same models, with education as covariate, were used to compare the effects of auditory cues between groups (interaction group*condition).

Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used for correlation analyses between significant fMRI and kinematic results, correlation analyses were Bonferroni corrected for number of conditions (Cue, NOCue, Delta Cue-NOCue). All statistical analysis were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 30.0 software.
