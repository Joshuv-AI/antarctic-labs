Noise-Driven Differentiation via Gene Frustration and Epigenetic Fixation
Davey Plugers, Kunihiko Kaneko

Abstract
Gene expression in cells is stochastic, yet differentiation is robust. We propose a mechanism in which frustrated genes with weakly stable intermediate expression undergo noise-driven switching between basins of attraction, followed by irreversible fate fixation through slow epigenetic feedback. Regulatory interactions amplify effective noise and promote differentiation. We derive analytic expression for the logarithmic dependence of differentiation time on noise strength and input-dependent cell-fate selection, and demonstrate homeorhesis, the dynamical robustness of the epigenetic landscape.

In the present Letter, we analyzed how a weakly expressed frustrated-gene state bifurcates into stable on/off expression states through noise and slow epigenetic fixation. Although the process is triggered by noise, the onset time of bifurcation is robust and varies little across samples. This robustness of the time course and onset time supports Waddington’s concept of homeorhesis. We analytically estimated the differentiation time and found that it depends logarithmically on the nonlinearity, epigenetic timescale, and noise magnitude, leading to a universal scaling form. Because this logarithmic dependence is weak, the differentiation time is not highly sensitive to these parameters, which further supports the robustness of the differentiation process.

By changing the input to the gene through
c_{i}
, the fraction of each differentiated cell type changes, and one type disappears beyond a threshold value. This behavior is also estimated analytically, and the phase diagram with respect to the threshold
c_{i}
and the noise level
\sigma
is obtained analytically. Altogether, we derived the parameter dependence of a robust Waddington landscape and verified the results numerically.

Although we illustrated differentiation into two types using a single gene, the present study provides a basis for differentiation driven by noise and epigenetic fixation in more general and complex gene regulatory networks. The present model offers a minimal framework and can readily be extended to differentiation into multiple cell types. Incorporating spatial patterning and signal-induced differentiation into this framework may provide these processes with robustness to noise through
\theta
, which will be explored in future work. In this sense, homeorhesis is expected to emerge more generally in such systems.

\boxed{t_{c,Dr}=\frac{1}{\nu(\beta_{\rm eff}-1)}\ln\left[\frac{\beta_{\rm eff}c_{i}\pm(\beta_{\rm eff}-1)}{\beta_{\rm eff}^{2}c_{i}}\right]}

(27)

Bias-dependent differentiation probability.—

We determine the probability of differentiation to
\theta_{i}=\pm 1
for various parameter values. To do so, we use the backward Kolmogorov equation, integrating backward from
\theta_{i}=1
and estimating the probability that the system was previously at
\theta_{i}
. We use Eq. (
3a
) and write it as:

\displaystyle d\theta=a(b+\lambda\theta)dt+\tilde{\sigma}dW_{t}

(28)

\displaystyle a=\frac{\nu}{1-\beta J_{ii}}

(29)

\displaystyle b=\beta c_{i}

(30)

\displaystyle\lambda=\beta-1+\beta J_{ii}

(31)

This then gives the backward Kolmogorov equation:

d\theta_{t}=\mu(\theta_{t})dt+\tilde{\sigma}dW_{t}

(32)

Where we then wish to determine the probability of differentiating to one side:

\mathbb{P}(\theta_{0})=\mathbb{P}\{\text{hit $\theta=+1$ before $\theta=-1$ $|$ $\theta(0)=\theta_{0}$}\}

(33)

To determine the probability in the final equilibrium state, we need to solve for:

\mu(\theta)u^{\prime}(\theta)+\frac{\tilde{\sigma}^{2}u^{\prime\prime}(\theta)}{2}=0,\textbf{ }\textit{ }u(-1)=0,u(1)=1

(34)

Asserting
a\sigma\neq 0
, we rewrite and solve for
v(\theta)=u^{\prime}(\theta)

\displaystyle v^{\prime}(\theta)+\frac{2(b+\lambda\theta)}{a\sigma^{2}}v(\theta)=0

(35)

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(\theta)=v(\theta)=C\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{2b\theta+\lambda\theta^{2}}{a\sigma^{2}}\right)

(36)

Integrating this then gives us:

u(\theta)=\int_{\theta_{L}}^{\theta}C\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{2b\tau+\lambda\tau^{2}}{a\sigma^{2}}\right)d\tau+D

(37)

To estimate our coefficients
C
and
D
, let us set
\theta_{L}=-1
such that
D=0
since
u(-1)=0
. We can estimate
C
from
u(1)=1
such that:

C=\frac{1}{\int_{-1}^{1}\exp\left(-\frac{2b\tau+\lambda\tau^{2}}{a\sigma^{2}}\right)d\tau}

(38)

This then gives the differentiation probability:

\mathbb{P}(\theta_{0})=\frac{\int_{-1}^{\theta_{0}}\exp\left(-\frac{2b\tau+\lambda\tau^{2}}{a\sigma^{2}}\right)d\tau}{\int_{-1}^{1}\exp\left(-\frac{2b\tau+\lambda\tau^{2}}{a\sigma^{2}}\right)d\tau}

(39)

Rewriting this into error functions and converting to the original reduced parameters we obtain:

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{2b\tau+\lambda\tau^{2}}{a\sigma^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{b^{2}}{a\sigma^{2}\lambda}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{a\sigma^{2}}\left(\tau+\frac{b}{\lambda}\right)^{2}\right)

(40)

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{\lambda}{a\sigma^{2}}=\frac{\mu}{\tilde{\sigma}^{2}},\quad\epsilon=\frac{b}{\lambda}=\tilde{c}

(41)

\boxed{\mathbb{P}(\theta_{0})=\frac{\operatorname{erf}\!\left(\sqrt{\mu/\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}\left(\theta_{0}+\tilde{c}\right)\right)-\operatorname{erf}\!\left(\sqrt{\mu/\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}\left(-1+\tilde{c}\right)\right)}{\operatorname{erf}\!\left(\sqrt{\mu/\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}\left(1+\tilde{c}\right)\right)-\operatorname{erf}\!\left(\sqrt{\mu/\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}\left(-1+\tilde{c}\right)\right)}}

(43)
