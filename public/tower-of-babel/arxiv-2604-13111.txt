Linear Response for Contracting on Average Iterated Function Systems
Jianning Fu

Abstract
. Consider the following probabilistic contracting on average iterated function system Φ = { f i ​ ( x ) = λ i ​ x + d i , i = 1 , 2 ; p = ( 1 2 , 1 2 ) } , \Phi=\left\{f_{i}(x)=\lambda_{i}x+d_{i},\;i=1,2;\;\;p=\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)\right\}, where the contraction ratios λ 1 , λ 2 \lambda_{1},\lambda_{2} are such that 0 < λ 1 < 1 < λ 2 0<\lambda_{1}<1<\lambda_{2} and λ 1 ​ λ 2 < 1 \lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}<1 . Denote by μ λ 1 , λ 2 \mu_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}} its stationary measure. 1.
Introduction and Statement of Results

To state the main results, let us first recall some basic facts of self-similar sets and measures. Let
m\geq 2
and
d\geq 1
be positive integers and consider
m
maps
\mathcal{F}=(f_{1},\cdots f_{m})
acting on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with each map
f_{i}
having a contraction ratio
0<r_{i}<1
. That is,

\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad||f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(y)||=r_{i}||x-y||,\quad i=1,\cdots m.

Such a tuple of maps
\mathcal{F}
is called a
self-similar iterated function system
(self-similar
IFS
or
IFS
for short).

It is well known
[
12
]
that there exists a unique compact set
K=K_{\mathcal{F}}
, called the
attractor
of
\mathcal{S}
, such that

K=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}(K).

Throughout, we always assume that not all
f_{i}
’s share the same fixed point, so that the attractor of
\mathcal{S}
is not a single point.

One can view the attractor via symbolic dynamics as follows. Define
\mathscr{A}=\{1,\cdots m\}
to be the alphabet,
\Sigma=\mathscr{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
the shift space and
\sigma:\Sigma\to\Sigma
the left shift. Consider the map
\Pi=\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
that sends any word
i=(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots)\in\Sigma
to

\Pi(i)=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{i_{1}}\circ\cdots f_{i_{n}}(0)=:\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}}(0).

Note that since each
f_{i}
is contracting,
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{i_{1}}\circ\cdots f_{i_{n}}(x)
exists and is independent of the choice of
x
, justifying the above definition. The attractor
K
is exactly the image of the projection
\Pi
.

An additional probability structure can be put on IFS. Namely, if
\mathcal{F}=(f_{1},\cdots,f_{m})
is a self-similar IFS and
p=(p_{1},\cdots,p_{m})
is a probability vector, then we call
\Phi=(\mathcal{F},p)
a
probabilistic IFS
. A Borel probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R^{d}}
is called the
stationary measure
for the IFS
\Phi
, if

\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}(f_{j})_{*}\mu.

That is, if we consider the Markov process where for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it is mapped to
f_{j}(x)
with probability
p_{j}
, then
\mu
is the corresponding stationary measure. It is well known that such measure is unique, so it is indeed “the” stationary measure. Moreover, it can also be described symbolically. Define
\nu=p^{\mathbb{N}}
to be the Bernoulli measure on
\Sigma
. Then

\displaystyle\mu=\Pi_{*}\nu.

We refer to
[
5
]
,
[
8
]
for the aforementioned properties as well as more discussions on (probabilistic) IFS.

The notion of attractor and stationary measure can be generalized to the case where not all
r_{i}
are less than
1
. However, we require that the IFS
contracts on average
, meaning that

\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\log r_{j}<0.

In this case, there is also a unique invariant measure
\mu

[
13
]
, which is still characterized by
\mu=\Pi_{*}\nu
. One slight difference is that in the contracting on average case, the projection
\Pi
is only defined for
\nu
-almost every words. In addition, its support is no longer compact.

Many results in the contracting case can be generalized to this stationary measure, including but not restricted to its dimension, density, order of tail and absolute continuity (with respect to Hausdorff measure of suitable dimension)
[
11
]
[
16
]

[
9
]

[
14
]

[

]

[
17
]
.

In the present work, we study the linear response problem for stationary measures of a contracting on average IFS. In order to keep the notation simple,
we restrict ourselves to the following system

\Phi=\left\{f_{i}(x)=\lambda_{i}x+d_{i},\;i=1,2;\;\;p=\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)\right\},

where the contraction ratios
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
are such that
0<\lambda_{1}<1<\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}<1
. We discuss in Section
7
more general setting where our approach works.

Denote by
\mu_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}
the stationary measure of this IFS. Consider the map

(1.1)

h=h_{\phi}:\epsilon\mapsto\int\phi\,d\mu_{\lambda_{1}+\epsilon,\lambda_{2}},

defined for
\epsilon
satisfying
(\lambda_{1}+\epsilon)\lambda_{2}<1
, where
\phi
is a function of a certain regularity. By linear response theory, we mean studying sufficient regularity conditions on
\phi
so that
h_{\phi}^{\prime}(0)
exists
and calculating the value of the derivative.

For many smooth or piecewise smooth dynamical systems, there is a well-developed linear response theory, originated in Ruelle’s work
[
18
]
and developed by many others including but not restricted to
[
7
,
10
,
2
,
3
]
. There are also examples of failure of linear response, see for example

[
4
,
1
,
6
]
. However, not much is known about the analogous question for contracting (on average) IFS, and it is tempting to develop an analogous theory. Our method is based on the tail property of
\mu_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}
and large deviation theory, and
may work in a more general setting.

In contrast, in a separate work we will show that for Bernoulli convolutions, to get (almost everywhere) differentiability of
h_{\phi}
we only need Hölder continuous observable
\phi
. Moreover, differentiability with Hölder observable is related to the dimension of the measure.

To state the main results we need additional notation. By the symbolic characterization, one get the symbolic space
\Sigma=\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
equipped with the probability measure
\nu=(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})^{\mathbb{N}}
and the projection

\Pi(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}d_{i_{j}}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{j-1}}=:X.

Moreover,
\mu_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}=\Pi_{*}\nu
will be the law of the random variable
X
.

If
\epsilon
is sufficiently small so that
(\lambda_{1}+\epsilon)\lambda_{2}<1
,the random variable

X(\epsilon):=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}d_{i_{j}}\lambda_{i_{1}}(\epsilon)\cdots\lambda_{i_{j-1}}(\epsilon)

is well-defined almost everywhere, where
\lambda_{1}(\epsilon)=\lambda_{1}+\epsilon
and
\lambda_{2}(\epsilon)=\lambda_{2}
, and has law
\mu_{\lambda_{1}+\epsilon,\lambda_{2}}
.
With these notations, we can write

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi\,d\mu_{\lambda_{1}+\epsilon,\lambda_{2}}=\int_{\Sigma}\phi(X(\epsilon))\,d\nu.

Since
X(\epsilon)
is a formal power series in
\epsilon
, one can take derivative formally. It is therefore natural to ask whether

(1.2)

h^{(l)}(0):=\frac{d^{l}h}{d\epsilon^{l}}(0)=\int_{\Sigma}\frac{d^{l}}{d\epsilon^{l}}(\phi\circ X(\epsilon))|_{\epsilon=0}\,d\nu,\;l=1,2,\cdots

under suitable assumptions on the test function
\phi
and
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
. We will show three instances of equality (
1.2
) being true,.

On the other hand, if
\mu_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}
is too concentrated on some intervals with small Lebesgue measure, it is possible to construct a
C^{\infty}
, bounded test function
\phi
whose derivative is large around these points, so that
h_{\phi}
is not differentiable at
0
and, in particular, equation (
1.2
) fails. We will give two sufficient conditions on
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
for such
\phi
to exist.

Now we state the main results. We start with three cases where equation (
1.2
) is true.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<\lambda_{1}<1<\lambda_{2}
are such that
\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{1}^{r}+\lambda_{2}^{r}}{2}<1
. Then for any function
\phi\in C^{r}(\mathbb{R})
with
||\phi^{(r)}||_{C^{0}}<\infty
, the average
h(\epsilon):=\int\phi\,d\mu_{\lambda_{1}+\epsilon,\lambda_{2}}
is differentiable at
0
up to order
r
and the derivatives are given by

\displaystyle h^{(l)}(0)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\frac{\,d^{l}}{\,d\epsilon^{l}}(\phi\circ X(\epsilon))|_{\epsilon=0}\,d\nu

(1.3)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\sum_{k_{1}+2k_{2}+\cdots lk_{l}=l}L(k_{1},\cdots,k_{l})\phi^{(k)}(X)\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}}\,d\nu,\;l=1,2,\cdots,r.

The second equality above is simply the Faa di Bruno’s formula for higher derivative of composition of two functions: the summation is over all non negative integers
k_{1},\cdots,k_{l}
with
k_{1}+2k_{2}+\cdots lk_{l}=l
. Moreover,
L(k_{1},\cdots,k_{l}):=\frac{l!}{k_{1}!(1!)^{k_{1}}\cdots k_{l}!(l!)^{k_{l}}}
,
k:=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{l}

and
X^{(j)}
is defined as

X^{(j)}:=\frac{\,d^{j}}{\,d\epsilon^{j}}X(\epsilon)|_{\epsilon=0}=\frac{j!}{\lambda_{1}^{j}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}\binom{o(m)}{j}

where
o(m)
denotes the number of
1
’s among
i_{1},\cdots,i_{m}
.

In the above theorem, the set of assumptions
\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{1}^{r}+\lambda_{2}^{r}}{2}<1
and
||\phi^{(r)}||_{C^{0}}<\infty
serve as some kind of “moment” condition to make sure that the right hand side of (
1.1
) is finite. Another instance of such sufficient condition is the following, in which one gets infinite differentiability:

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose
d_{1}=1,d_{2}=1
,
0<\lambda_{1}<1<\lambda_{2}
and

are such that
\frac{\lambda_{1}^{t}+\lambda_{2}^{t}}{2}<1
. Note that this implies
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}<1
and the stationary measure
\mu=\mu_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}
is well defined. Take the test function to be
\phi(x)=x^{t},x\geq 1
and define
h(\epsilon)=\int\phi(x)\,d\mu_{\lambda_{1}+\epsilon,\lambda_{2}}
for

small. Then

is infinitely differentiable at
0
and the derivative coincide with the formal derivative:

\displaystyle h^{(l)}(0)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\frac{\,d^{l}}{\,d\epsilon^{l}}(\phi\circ X(\epsilon))|_{\epsilon=0}\,d\nu

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\sum_{k_{1}+2k_{2}+\cdots lk_{l}=l}L(k_{1},\cdots,k_{l})\phi^{(k)}(X)\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}}\,d\nu,\quad\forall l\in\mathbb{N}.

Here, all the notations are the same as in Theorem
1.1
.

Apart from moment conditions as in the above two results, that
\phi
being compactly supported can also lead to the formal derivative being integrable and therefore validity of linear response, as the following result shows:

Theorem 1.3
.

0,1
Assume

are such that
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}<1
and

Note that in the above three theorems, the assumptions remain valid if

Next are the two instances of non-differentiability.

.

Assume

Remark 1.1
.

The above give rise to examples of (partially) hyperbolic solenoidal attractor where linear response fails for smooth observables. To see this, consider the skew product representation of the IFS, which is the map
F:[-1,1]\times\mathbb{R}\to[-1,1]\times\mathbb{R}
,

F(x,y)=\begin{cases}(2x+1,f_{1}(y)),\text{ if }x\in[-1,0]\\
(2x-1,f_{2}(y)),\text{ if }x\in(0,1]\end{cases}.

Then
\rho=\rho_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}:=Leb\times\mu_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}
is the unique SRB measure of

. And
\Phi(x,y):=\phi(y)
is a
C^{\infty}
observable that fails linear response.

The proof of the three affirmative results follow the same strategy. We will show that under the assumption of these theorems,

is exponentially close to its finite approximation

Then we show that the derivative can be computed using finite approximations. As for the two non-differentiable examples, the construction takes advantage of the fact that the partial sum
\displaystyle X_{N}=\sum_{m=0}^{N}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}{d_{i_{m+1}}}
can be large while the scaling factor

\Lambda_{N}=\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}
is tiny, and that the probability of this happening is not too small because of the self-similar structure of

. These will be explained in more detail in individual sections below.

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}c\circ f_{i_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{i_{n}}(0)=c(\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{i_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{i_{n}}(0))=c\circ\Pi(i),

Proof of Lemma

Note that in the left hand side of the target inequality, the number of summands is bounded by some constants depending on

which is less or equal to

We first estimate

.

Note that
\displaystyle X^{(j)}-X_{n}^{(j)}=\frac{j!}{\lambda_{1}^{j}}\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}\binom{o(m)}{j}=:R_{n}^{(j)}
, and we compute

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}}-\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}}=\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X_{n}^{(j)}+R_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}}-\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}}

\displaystyle=(\sum_{s_{1}=0}^{k_{1}}\binom{k_{1}}{s_{1}}(X_{n}^{(1)})^{k_{1}-s_{1}}(R_{n}^{(1)})^{s_{1}})\cdots(\sum_{s_{l}=0}^{k_{l}}\binom{k_{l}}{s_{l}}(X_{n}^{(l)})^{k_{l}-s_{l}}(R_{n}^{(l)})^{s_{l}})-\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{matrix}s_{1},\cdots,s_{l}\\
0\leq s_{i}\leq k_{i},\forall i\\
s_{1}+\cdots+s_{l}>0\end{matrix}}\prod_{j=1}^{l}\binom{k_{j}}{s_{j}}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}-s_{j}}(R^{(j)}_{n})^{s_{j}}

Again, note that in the last summation, the number of summands is uniformly bounded by some constant depending on
l
, and the coefficients
\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{l}\binom{k_{j}}{s_{j}}
are also uniformly bounded in terms of
l
. Therefore we can further reduce the problem to estimating

\int\phi^{(k)}(X)\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}-s_{j}}(R^{(j)}_{n})^{s_{j}}.

Since
\phi
is compactly supported, there exists

such that supp

. Hence the above integral is in fact

(2.4)

\int\phi^{(k)}(X)\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}-s_{j}}(R^{(j)}_{n})^{s_{j}}1_{\{X\leq M\}}

where
1_{A}
is the characteristic function of set

.

Note that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\phi^{(k)}(X)1_{\{X\leq M\}}X^{(j)}_{n}

\displaystyle=\phi^{(k)}(X)\frac{j!}{\lambda_{1}^{j}}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}\binom{o(m)}{j}1_{\{X\leq M\}}

\displaystyle\leq||\phi^{(k)}||_{C^{0}}\frac{j!}{\lambda_{1}^{j}}n^{j}M{\leq}Kn^{l}.

Here we used the fact that
\binom{o(m)}{j}\leq(o(m))^{j}\leq n^{j}
and
K
is a constant depending only on
\phi
and
l
.

To estimate the remaining terms, we have for

(2.6)

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{l}(R^{(j)}_{n})^{s_{j}}=\prod_{j=1}^{l}\left(\frac{j!}{\lambda_{1}^{j}}\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}\binom{o(m)}{j}\right)^{s_{l}}

(2.7)

\displaystyle\leq L\prod_{j=1}^{l}(\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}C(m)\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}})^{s_{j}}=L(\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}C(m)\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}})^{s}

where
\displaystyle C(m):=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\binom{o(m)}{j}
,

.

We wish to show exponential smallness of
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}C(m)\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}\right)^{s}\right]
. To this end for any large
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we will find a uniform upper bound for

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{m=n+1}^{N}C(m)\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}\right)^{s}\right]

We need to distinguish the cases

\frac{1}{s}+\frac{1}{s^{*}}=1
. By Hölder inequality, for any

(2.8)

\displaystyle(\sum_{m=n+1}^{N}C(m)\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}})^{s}

(2.9)

\displaystyle\leq(\sum_{m=n+1}^{N}(\rho^{m})^{s^{*}})^{\frac{s}{s^{*}}}\left[\sum_{m=n+1}^{N}\left(\frac{\lambda_{i_{1}}}{\rho}\right)^{s}\cdots\left(\frac{\lambda_{i_{m}}}{\rho}\right)^{s}(C(m))^{s}\right]

\displaystyle I(\rho):=\left(\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}(\rho^{m})^{s^{*}}\right)^{\frac{s}{s^{*}}}<\infty

\displaystyle\theta_{i}:=\frac{\lambda_{i}^{s}}{\rho^{s}}
,

Claim:

\mathbb{E}[\theta_{i_{1}}\cdots\theta_{i_{m}}1_{\{X\leq M\}}]\leq Ce^{-\gamma m}.

Proof of Claim:
Let
\kappa=\kappa(\rho)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\theta_{i_{1}}\cdots\theta_{i_{m}}1_{\{\theta_{i_{1}}\cdots\theta_{i_{m}}\leq e^{\kappa m}\}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\theta_{i_{1}}\cdots\theta_{i_{m}}1_{\{\theta_{i_{1}}\cdots\theta_{i_{m}}\leq e^{-\kappa m}\}}]+\mathbb{E}[\theta_{i_{1}}\cdots\theta_{i_{m}}1_{\{e^{-\kappa m}<\theta_{i_{1}}\cdots\theta_{i_{m}}\leq e^{\kappa m}\}}]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\kappa m}+e^{\kappa m}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{m}\log\theta_{i_{t}}}{m}\geq-\kappa\right)

Note that
\theta_{i_{t}}
are i.i.d with
\mathbb{E}[\log\theta_{i_{t}}]=\frac{s}{2}\log\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}+s\log\frac{1}{\rho}
. Thus by standard large deviation theory, if
\rho
is close enough to
1
and
\kappa
is so small that

, then there exists
\delta>0

such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{m}\log\theta_{i_{t}}}{m}\geq-\kappa\right)\leq e^{-\delta m}.

Therefore,

(2.12)

\mathbb{E}[\theta_{i_{1}}\cdots\theta_{i_{m}}1_{\{\theta_{i_{1}}\cdots\theta_{i_{m}}\leq e^{\kappa m}\}}]\leq e^{-\kappa m}+e^{-(\delta-\kappa)m}.

Note that
\delta
increases as
\kappa
decreases. Therefore, we can first fix a set of
\kappa
and
\rho

-\kappa-\left(\frac{s}{2}\log\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}+s\log\frac{1}{\rho}\right)>0

\kappa

2.11

remains valid with the same
\delta

\kappa

Then

\mathbb{E}[\theta_{i_{1}}\cdots\theta_{i_{m}}1_{\{\theta_{i_{1}}\cdots\theta_{i_{m}}\leq e^{\kappa m}\}}]\leq e^{-\kappa m}+e^{-(\delta-\kappa)m}\leq 2e^{-\frac{\kappa}{2}m}:=2e^{-\gamma m}.

Also note that,
\delta
increases as
\rho
increases, so if we can fix the above choice of
\kappa
and increase
\rho
to make

, while the large deviation inequality (
2.11

\rho

(2.13)

\mathbb{E}[\theta_{i_{1}}\cdots\theta_{i_{m}}1_{\{\theta_{i_{1}}\cdots\theta_{i_{m}}\leq e^{\kappa m}\}}]\leq 2e^{-\gamma m}.

with the same

.

We are now ready to prove the claim. First we show that

Now fix a

\displaystyle\int\sum_{m=n+1}^{N}\left(\frac{\lambda_{i_{1}}}{\rho}\right)^{s}\cdots\left(\frac{\lambda_{i_{m}}}{\rho}\right)^{s}(C(m))^{s}1_{\{X\leq M\}}

\displaystyle=C(m)^{s}\mathbb{E}[\sum_{m=n+1}^{N}\theta_{i_{1}}\cdots\theta_{i_{m}}1_{\{X\leq M\}}]

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{m=n+1}^{N}C(m)^{s}e^{-\gamma m}

In the case

2.4.
Proof of Lemma
2.2

Since the summation
\sum_{k_{1}+2k_{2}+\cdots lk_{l}=l}
and the coefficients
L(k_{1},\cdots,k_{l})
are governed by
l
, which is fixed, it suffices to prove that on each summand, the limit can be computed by taking the formal derivative:

(2.26)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n}(\epsilon))^{k_{j}}-\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n})\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}})

=\int\phi^{(k+1)}(X)X^{(1)}\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}}+\int\phi^{(k)}(X)\sum_{j=1}^{l}(k_{j}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}X^{(j+1)}\prod_{i\neq j}(X^{(i)})^{k_{i}}).

To that end, we similarly divide the difference into two parts.

\displaystyle\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n}(\epsilon))^{k_{j}}-\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n})\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int(\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))-\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}))\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}+\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))(\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n}(\epsilon))^{k_{j}}-\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}))

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle G_{1}+G_{2}.

First we compute

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon}G_{1}

\displaystyle=\int\phi^{(k+1)}(X_{n}+\xi_{n}(X_{n}(\epsilon)-X_{n}))\frac{X_{n}(\epsilon)-X_{n}}{\epsilon}\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}}

\displaystyle=\int\phi^{(k+1)}(X_{n}+\xi_{n}(X_{n}(\epsilon)-X_{n}))(X_{n}^{(1)})+\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}S_{n,t}\epsilon^{t})\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}}1_{\{X_{n}\leq M\text{ or }X_{n}(\epsilon)\leq M\}}.

Here
\displaystyle S_{n,t}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}^{t+1}}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}\binom{m}{t+1}
.

We first show that the terms containing
S_{n,t}
’s converge to zero. For each
1\leq m\leq n
, since
n=\mathcal{O}(\log\frac{1}{|\epsilon|})
and
o(m)\leq n
, if

is small enough,

\frac{\lambda_{i_{1}}(\epsilon)\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}(\epsilon)}{\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}}=\frac{(\lambda_{1}+\epsilon)^{o(m)}\lambda_{2}^{m-o(m)}}{\lambda_{1}^{o(m)}\lambda_{2}^{m-o(m)}}=\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda_{1}}\right)^{o(m)}\to 1,\text{ as }\epsilon\to 0,

Hence
X_{n}(\epsilon)\leq M
implies
X_{n}\leq 2M
, and we have

S_{n,t}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}^{t}}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}n^{t+1}\leq\frac{2n^{t+1}}{\lambda_{1}^{t}}M.

Since
n
is of order
-\log|\epsilon|
, it is not hard to show that

(2.27)

\phi^{(k+1)}(X_{n}+\xi_{n}(X_{n}(\epsilon)-X_{n}))\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}S_{n,t}\epsilon^{t}\to 0,\text{ a.e. as }\epsilon\to 0,\text{ and it is bounded.}

Also, note that

\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}}1_{\{X_{n}\leq M\text{ or }X_{n}(\epsilon)\leq M\}}\leq\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}}1_{\{X_{n}\leq 2M\}}

and the right hand side converges both a.e. and in
L^{1}
. To see the convergence in
L^{1}
, observe that it is increasing in
n
, and the pointwise limit

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}}1_{\{X\leq 2M\}}
is integrable, by the argument used for deriving (
2.16
). Thus it is convergent in
L^{1}
by the Monotone Convergence Theorem. Moreover, the left hand side converges a.e., so by Lemma
2.3
,

(2.28)

\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}}1_{\{X_{n}\leq M\text{ or }X_{n}(\epsilon)\leq M\}}\text{ converges in }L^{1},

which combined with (
2.27
) allows us to apply Lemma
2.4
to conclude

(2.29)

\int(\phi^{(k+1)}(X_{n}+\xi_{n}(X_{n}(\epsilon)-X_{n}))(\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}S_{n,t}\epsilon^{t})\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}}1_{\{X_{n}\leq M\text{ or }X_{n}(\epsilon)\leq M\}}\to 0.

On the other hand, we have

\begin{cases}\phi^{(k+1)}(X_{n}+\xi_{n}(X_{n}(\epsilon)-X_{n}))\to\phi^{(k+1)}(X)\text{ a.e. and is bounded}\\
(X_{n}^{(1)})^{k_{1}+1}\prod_{j=2}^{l}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}}1_{\{X_{n}\leq M\text{ or }X_{n}(\epsilon)\leq M\}}\xrightarrow{L^{1}}(X_{n}^{(1)})^{k_{1}+1}\prod_{j=2}^{l}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}}1_{\{X\leq M\}}\end{cases}

where the second convergence follows from the same computations as in (
2.16
)
and the generalized dominated convergence lemma
2.3
. Therefore by Lemma
2.4
,

(2.30)

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int\phi^{(k+1)}(X_{n}+\xi_{n}(X_{n}(\epsilon)-X_{n}))(X_{n}^{(1)})\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}}1_{\{X_{n}\leq M\text{ or }X_{n}(\epsilon)\leq M\}}

\displaystyle=\int\phi^{(k+1)}(X)(X^{(1)})^{k_{1}+1}\prod_{j=2}^{l}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}}1_{\{X\leq M\}}=\int\phi^{(k+1)}(X)(X^{(1)})^{k_{1}+1}\prod_{j=2}^{l}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}}.

Combining (
2.29
) and (
2.30
) gives

(2.31)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{G_{1}}{\epsilon}=\int\phi^{(k+1)}(X)(X_{n}^{(1)})^{k_{1}+1}\prod_{j=2}^{l}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}}.

Finally, we compute
G_{2}
. First a simple calculation shows

X_{n}^{(j)}(\epsilon)=X_{n}^{(j)}+\frac{j!}{\lambda_{1}^{j}}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\binom{o(m)}{j}\frac{(o(m)-j)}{\lambda_{1}}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}\epsilon\right)+P_{n}^{(j)}=:X_{n}^{(j)}+T_{n}^{(j)},

where the term
P_{n}^{(j)}
contains all terms with power of
\epsilon
at least
2
. Now

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n}(\epsilon))^{k_{j}}

\displaystyle-\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}=\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n}+T_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}}-\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{l}(\sum_{s_{j}=0}^{k_{j}}\binom{k_{j}}{s_{j}}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}-s_{j}}(T_{n}^{(j)})^{s_{j}})^{k_{j}}-\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{matrix}0\leq s_{i}\leq k_{i},i=1,\cdots,l\\
s_{1}+\cdots+s_{l}>0\end{matrix}}\prod_{j=1}^{l}\binom{k_{j}}{s_{j}}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}-s_{j}}(T_{n}^{(j)})^{s_{j}}.\quad\quad\quad(*)

The terms in the right hand side of
(*)
that contains
\epsilon^{t},t\geq 2
will contribute
0
in the limit

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{G_{2}}{\epsilon}
, for the same reason as in (
2.29
). Therefore

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{G_{2}}{\epsilon}=

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{matrix}0\leq s_{i}\leq k_{i}\\
s_{1}+\cdots+s_{l}=1\end{matrix}}\!\!\!\!\prod_{j=1}^{l}\binom{k_{j}}{s_{j}}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}-s_{j}}\left[\frac{j!}{\lambda_{1}^{j}}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\binom{o(m)}{j}\frac{(o(m)-j)}{\lambda_{1}}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}\epsilon\right]^{s_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))\sum_{1\leq j\leq l,k_{j}\geq 1}k_{j}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{l}(X_{n}^{(i)})^{k_{i}}}{X_{n}^{(j)}}X_{n}^{(j+1)}\quad\quad\quad(**)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))\sum_{1\leq j\leq l,k_{k}\geq 1}(\prod_{i=1,i\neq j}^{l}(X_{n}^{(i)})^{k_{i}}\frac{\,d}{\,d\epsilon}(X_{n}^{(j)}(\epsilon))^{k_{j}}|_{\epsilon=0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq j\leq l,k_{j}\geq 1}\int\phi^{(k)}(X)k_{j}\prod_{i=1,i\neq j}^{l}(X^{(i)})^{k_{i}}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}X^{(j+1)}.\quad\quad\quad(***)

Here to get
(**)
we used the fact that

\frac{j!}{\lambda_{1}^{j}}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\binom{o(m)}{j}\frac{1}{\lambda}_{1}(o(m)-j)\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}=\frac{(j+1)!}{\lambda_{1}^{j=1}}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\binom{o(m)}{j+1}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}=X_{n}^{(j+1)},

and
(***)
holds for the same reason as before. This together with (
2.31
) completes the proof. ∎

2.5.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.3
.

Proof.

Note that in Lemma
2.1
, the constant
c
and
\theta
depends continuously on the parameters
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
. Therefore, we may choose a pair of
c,\theta
such that Lemma
2.1
is true for
\epsilon
sufficiently small.

Next, choose
n=n(\epsilon)=2\log_{\theta}|\epsilon|
in Lemma
2.1
. Note that

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{c\theta^{n}}{\epsilon}=0
.
Hence

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{h^{(l)}(\epsilon)-h^{(l)}(0)}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left[\frac{h^{(l)}(\epsilon)-\mathbb{E}[\sum_{k_{1}+2k_{2}+\cdots lk_{l}=l}L(k_{1},\cdots,k_{l})\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n}(\epsilon))^{k_{j}}]}{\epsilon}\right.

\displaystyle-

∎

3.
Examples of Non-Differentiability

3.1.
Overview

In this section we prove Theorem
1.4
and
1.5
. We begin by recalling the precise statements and roughly describing how the assumptions will come into use.

Theorem
1.4
.

Assume
0<\lambda_{1}<1<\lambda_{2}
are such that
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}<\frac{1}{4}
. Then there exists a smooth, bounded function
\phi
such that
h(\epsilon):=\int\phi\,d_{\lambda_{1}+\epsilon,\lambda_{2}}
is not differentiable at
0
.

Theorem
1.5
.

Assume
0<\lambda_{1}<1<\lambda_{2}
are such that
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}<1
and
\log_{2}\lambda_{2}>1+\log_{\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}}\lambda_{2}
. Then there exists a smooth, bounded function
\phi
such that
h(\epsilon):=\int\phi\,d_{\lambda_{1}+\epsilon,\lambda_{2}}
is not differentiable at
0
.

The assumptions in the last two results reflect the local thickness of
\mu
. In particular, they imply that the partial sum
\displaystyle X_{N}=\sum_{m=0}^{N}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}
can be large while the scaling factor
\Lambda_{N}=\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}
is tiny, and that the probability of this happening is not too small, thanks to self-similar structure of the random variable
X
. This leads to the stationary measure of certain small intervals being large compared to their length, and in turn produces non-differentiability.

In addition, the assumption
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}<\frac{1}{4}
implies that the
Lyapunov dimension
of
\mu
, which in our setting is simply equal to
entropy over Lyapunov exponent:

\displaystyle\frac{h_{\nu}}{\chi}=\frac{\log 2}{-\frac{1}{2}\log\lambda_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\log\lambda_{2}}
, is less than
1
. On the other hand, the Lyapunov dimension is an upper bound for the Hausdorff dimension of the stationary measure of an iterated function system. In particular,
\mu_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}
has Hausdorff dimension less than one and is therefore singular (with respect to Lebesgue measure). For more details of Lyapunov dimension, we refer to
[
5
, theorem 3.2.1]
as well as Chapter
10
of the same reference. On the other hand, having Lyapunov dimension less than
1
is not a necessary condition for such thickness, as Theorem
1.5
shows, since we can take, for instance,
\lambda_{1}=\frac{1}{11},\lambda_{2}=10
.

A key step of the proof is to reduce both theorems to the existence of a family of special test functions

\{\phi_{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Under either assumptions of Theorem
1.4
and
1.5
, there exists a family of smooth, nonnegative functions
\{\phi_{N}\}
with the following properties.

(1)

There exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
N
and all
\epsilon
with
|\epsilon|<\epsilon_{0}
,
h_{N}^{\prime}(\epsilon)
exist and are nonnegative. Here
h_{N}(\epsilon)=h_{\phi_{N}}(\epsilon):=\int\phi_{N}\,d\mu_{\lambda_{1}+\epsilon,\lambda_{2}}
.

(2)

\displaystyle\sum_{N}h_{N}^{\prime}(0)=\infty
.

(3)

\displaystyle\phi:=\sum\phi_{N}
is a smooth and
\mu_{\lambda_{1}+\epsilon,\lambda_{2}}
-integrable for small enough
\epsilon
.

In the rest of this section, we first show how to deduce the main results from Proposition
3.1
in §
3.2
. Then we list several preliminary lemmas in §
3.3
before proving Proposition
3.1
under the assumption of each theorem in §
3.4
and §
3.5
respectively.

3.2.
Proposition
3.1
implies the main Theorems

Proof.

Given
\{\phi_{N}\}
and
\phi
satisfying the assumptions of Proposition
3.1
, define
h(\epsilon)=\int\phi\,d\mu_{\lambda_{1}+\epsilon,\lambda_{2}},0<|\epsilon|<\epsilon_{0}
. We will show that
h
is not differentiable at
0
.

Let
F(\epsilon,N):=\frac{h_{N}(\epsilon)-h_{N}(0)}{\epsilon},N\in\mathbb{N},0<|\epsilon|<\epsilon_{0}
. Note that

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}F(\epsilon,N)=h_{N}^{\prime}(0),\;\;\forall N\in\mathbb{N}

and

F(\epsilon,N)\geq 0,\;\;\forall 0<|\epsilon|<\epsilon_{0},\;N\in\mathbb{N}

since
h_{N}^{\prime}(\epsilon)\geq 0
for all
0<|\epsilon|<\epsilon_{0}
.
Thus

\displaystyle\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{h(\epsilon)-h(0)}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\int\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\phi_{N}\,d\mu_{\lambda_{1}+\epsilon,\lambda_{2}}-\int\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\phi_{N}\,d\mu_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}(\int\phi_{N}\,d\mu_{\lambda_{1}+\epsilon,\lambda_{2}}-\int\phi_{N}\,d\mu_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}})}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}\int F(\epsilon,N)\,dc(N)\quad c\text{ is the counting measure on }\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\geq\int\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}F(\epsilon,N)\,dc(N)\quad\text{ by Fatou's Lemma }

(3.1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}h_{N}^{\prime}(0)=\infty,

which implies
h
is not differentiable at
0
. Note that the second equality holds because

\displaystyle\sum_{N}\int\phi_{N}\,d\mu_{\lambda_{1}+\epsilon,\lambda_{2}}
is absolutely convergent.
∎

3.3.
Preliminary Lemmas

In this section we collect several lemmas needed for the proof of Proposition
3.1
. Recall that for a fixed choice of
0<\lambda_{1}<1<\lambda_{2}
with
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}<1
,
we defined the random variable

X(\epsilon)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{i_{1}}(\epsilon)\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}(\epsilon)

where
\lambda_{1}(\epsilon)=\lambda_{1}+\epsilon
and
\lambda_{2}(\epsilon)=\lambda_{2}
, so that
\mu_{\lambda_{1}+\epsilon,\lambda_{2}}
is the law of
X(\epsilon)
. We also defined

X^{(1)}(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}(\epsilon)}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i_{1}}(\epsilon)\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}(\epsilon)o(m)

to be the formal derivative of
X(\epsilon)
with respect to
\epsilon
. Here
o(m)
stands for the number of
1
’s in
i_{1},\cdots,i_{m}
. For simplicity,
X
and
X^{(1)}
will stand for
X(0)
and
X^{(1)}(0)
respectively.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
be such that
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}<1
. Suppose
\phi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and its derivative
\phi^{\prime}
is compactly supported. Then the function
h(\epsilon):=\int\phi\,d\mu_{\lambda_{1}+\epsilon,\lambda_{2}}
is differentiable at
\epsilon=0
and the derivative is given by

h^{\prime}(0)=\int_{\Sigma}\phi^{\prime}(X)X^{(1)}\,d\nu.

Here
\nu=(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})^{\mathbb{N}}
is the Bernoulli measure on
\Sigma=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.

This is essentially Theorem
1.3
, with the only difference being that only
\phi^{\prime}
is assumed to be compactly supported, not
\phi
itself. But this matters little since we can prove the lemma following the same scheme.

More precisely, suppose
M>0
is such that
\phi^{\prime}|_{(M,\infty)}\equiv 0
. Then
\phi
restricted to
(M,\infty)
is constant. Recall that in the proof of Theorem

1.3
, we defined

X(\epsilon)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{i_{1}}(\epsilon)\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}(\epsilon),\quad X=X(0)

as well as the finite truncate

X_{N}(\epsilon)=\sum_{m=0}^{N}\lambda_{i_{1}}(\epsilon)\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}(\epsilon),\;X_{N}=X_{N}(0)\quad N\in\mathbb{N}.

Then we showed that

\int_{\Sigma}\phi(X)\,d\nu-\int_{\Sigma}\phi(X_{N})\,d\nu

is exponentially small and

\frac{\int_{\Sigma}\phi(X_{N}(\epsilon))\,d\nu-\int_{\Sigma}\phi(X_{N})\,d\nu}{\epsilon}

converges to the actual derivative, if
N
is chosen appropriately, see lemma
2.1
and
2.2
respectively. Moreover, in that proof, the only place we have used
\phi
being compactly supported is to reduce the above two quantities into

\int_{\Sigma}\phi(X)-\phi(X_{N})\,d\nu=\int_{\Sigma}\left(\phi(X)-\phi(X_{N})\right)1_{\{X_{N}\leq M\text{ or }X\leq M\}}\,d\nu

and

\int_{\Sigma}\phi(X_{N}(\epsilon))-\phi(X_{N})\,d\nu=\int_{\Sigma}\left(\phi(X_{N}(\epsilon))-\phi(X_{N})\right)1_{\{X_{N}(\epsilon)\leq M\text{ or }X_{N}\leq M\}}\,d\nu

for some
M>0
. But these are still true under our current setting of Lemma
3.1
, since
\phi
is constant on
(M,\infty)
. Therefore the proof of Theorem
1.3
goes through to give the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

There exists a constant
c>0
such that with probability
1
,
X^{(1)}\geq cX
.

Proof.

With probability
1
,
1
will appear among
i_{1},i_{2},\cdots
. Assume that the first occurrence is at
i_{k}
. Then

X=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\lambda_{2}^{j}+\lambda_{2}^{k-1}\lambda_{1}(1+\lambda_{i_{k+1}}+\lambda_{i_{k+1}}\lambda_{i_{k+2}}+\cdots)

and

\displaystyle X^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\lambda_{2}^{k-1}\lambda_{1}(1+\lambda_{i_{k+1}}o(k+1)+\lambda_{i_{k+1}}\lambda_{i_{k+2}}o(k+2)+\cdots)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{2}^{k-1}(1+\lambda_{i_{k+1}}+\lambda_{i_{k+1}}\lambda_{i_{k+2}}+\cdots)

Note that

\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\lambda_{2}^{j}\leq\lambda_{2}^{k-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{2}^{-j}=\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\lambda_{2}-1}\lambda_{2}^{k-1}

and

S_{k}:=1+\lambda_{i_{k+1}}+\lambda_{i_{k+1}}\lambda_{i_{k+2}}+\cdots\geq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{1}^{j}=\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{1}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{2}^{k-1}(\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\lambda_{2}-1}+\lambda_{1}S_{k})\leq\lambda_{2}^{k-1}(\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\lambda_{2}-1}\frac{S_{k}}{\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{1}}}+S_{k})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{2}^{k-1}S_{k}(\frac{\lambda_{2}(1-\lambda_{1})}{\lambda_{2}-1}+1)=\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}(1-\lambda_{1})}{\lambda_{2}-1}+1\right)X^{(1)}.

Hence
c:=(\frac{\lambda_{2}(1-\lambda_{1})}{\lambda_{2}-1}+1)^{-1}
meets the requirement of the lemma.
∎

3.4.
Proof of Proposition
3.1
, under the assumption
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}<\frac{1}{4}

Proof.

Let us define the random variables

\displaystyle X=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}

and for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

X_{N}=\sum_{m=0}^{N}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}},\>\Lambda_{N}=\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{N}},\quad Y_{N}=\sum_{m=N+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i_{N+1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}.

Note that

•

\displaystyle X=X_{N}+\Lambda_{N}Y_{N}
;

•

X_{N}
and
\Lambda_{N}
are independent of
Y_{N}
;

•

Y_{N}
and
X-1
has the same distribution.

Let
\theta:=\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}
and fix
\rho\in(\theta,\frac{1}{2})
(this is where we used the assumption
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}<\frac{1}{4}
). By Cramér’s theorem, there exists
\delta>0
such that

(3.2)

\mathbb{P}\{\Lambda_{N}\leq\rho^{N}\}\geq 1-e^{-\delta N}\>\text{ for all sufficiently large }N.

For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, consider positive integers
M
satisfying the following two properties:

(3.3)

\begin{cases}M\mathbb{P}\{X_{N}\geq M\}\geq N^{-\frac{1}{2}},\\
\mathbb{P}\{X_{N}\geq M\}\geq 2e^{-\delta N}.\end{cases}

It is not hard to see that for sufficiently large
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the set of positive integers satisfying
(
3.3
) is non-empty and finite, so we are able to define
M(N)
to be the largest one of them.

Next, define
A_{N}
to be the set of values that
X_{N}
can take that are not less than
M(N)
. Then by construction,

(3.4)

\mathbb{P}\{X_{N}\geq M(N)\}=\mathbb{P}\{X_{N}\in A_{N}\}\geq\max\left(2e^{-\delta N},\frac{1}{M\sqrt{N}}\right).

Next, let
r>0
be such that
\mathbb{P}\{X-1\leq r\}=\frac{1}{2}
. For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, define

B_{N}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\text{dist}(x,A_{N})\leq r\rho^{N}\}.

Finally, let
\phi_{N}
be a smooth function such that
\phi_{N}(-2r)=0
and that its derivative
\phi_{N}^{\prime}
satisfies

•

0\leq\phi_{N}\leq 1
;

•

\phi_{N}|_{B_{N}}\equiv 1
;

•

supp
\phi_{N}
is contained in the
2r\rho^{N}
-neighborhood of
A_{N}
. That is,
\phi^{\prime}_{N}
is zero on the set
\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\text{dist}(x,A_{N})>2r\rho^{N}\}=:B(A_{N},2r\rho^{N})
.

Note that since supp
\phi_{N}^{\prime}
is contained in
[-2r,\infty)
, we have
\phi_{N}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\phi_{N}^{\prime}(t)\,dt
.

Suppose
M(N)
is well-defined for all
N\geq N_{0}
. We claim that
\{\phi_{N}\}_{N\geq N_{0}}
satisfies the assumptions of Proposition
3.1
. These are clearly smooth. We verify conditions
(1)-(3)

below.

Proof of
(1)
. Since
\phi_{N}^{\prime}
is compactly supported, by Lemma
3.1

h_{N}
is differentiable at all
\epsilon
satisfying
(\lambda_{1}+\epsilon)\lambda_{2}<1
and the derivative is given by

h_{N}^{\prime}(\epsilon)=\int_{\Sigma}\phi^{\prime}_{N}(X(\epsilon))X^{(1)}(\epsilon)\,d\nu,

which is clearly non-negative since
\phi_{N}^{\prime}\geq 0
and
X^{(1)}(\epsilon)>0
. This establishes property
(1)
of Proposition
3.1
.

Proof of
(2)
We wish to derive a lower bound for
\phi_{N}^{\prime}(0)
so that
\displaystyle\sum_{N}\phi_{N}^{\prime}(0)=\infty
.
For simplicity, write
p_{N}:=\mathbb{P}\{X_{N}\geq M(N)\}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{X_{N}\geq M(N),\Lambda_{N}<\rho^{n}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{X_{N}\geq M(N)\}+\mathbb{P}\{\Lambda_{N}<\rho^{n}\}-\mathbb{P}\{X_{N}\geq M(N)\text{ or }\Lambda_{N}<\rho^{n}\}

\displaystyle\geq p_{N}+1-e^{-\delta N}-1

(3.5)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}p_{N}\quad\text{ since }p_{N}=\mathbb{P}\{X_{N}\geq M(N)\}\geq 2e^{-\delta N}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{X\in B_{N}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{X_{N}+\Lambda_{N}Y_{N}\in B_{N}\}\geq\mathbb{P}\{X_{N}\in A_{N},\Lambda_{N}\leq\rho^{N},Y_{N}\leq r\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{X_{N}\in A_{N},\Lambda_{N}\leq\rho^{N}\}\mathbb{P}\{Y_{N}\leq r\}\geq\frac{1}{2}p_{N}\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{4}p_{N}.

Then

\displaystyle h_{N}^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}\phi^{\prime}_{N}(X)X^{(1)}\geq\int_{\{X\in B_{N}\}}\phi^{\prime}_{N}(X)cX

(3.6)

\displaystyle\geq c(M(N)-r\rho^{N}))\mathbb{P}\{X\in B_{N}\}\geq\frac{c}{4}p_{N}\frac{M(N)}{2}\geq\frac{c}{8}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}.

The first inequality above is due to Lemma
3.2
. In the last inequality we have used (
3.3
). Finally, the penultimate inequality holds for all sufficiently large
n
, because of the following

Claim
:
\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}M(N)=\infty
.

Proof of Claim.
Otherwise there exists
M_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and a sequence
\{N_{k}\}
going to infinity such that
M_{k}:=M(N_{k})<M_{0}
. We aim to reach a contradiction. By definition, for each
k

4.1.
First derivative formula.

In this section we start the proof of (

here, and postpone the complete proof to §

,
as we will take advantage of several estimates from §

Let us state Theorem

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose
0<\lambda_{1}<1<\lambda_{2}
are such that
\frac{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}}{2}<1
and
\phi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is such that
||\phi^{\prime}||_{C^{0}}<\infty

\displaystyle h(\epsilon)=h_{\phi}(\epsilon):=\int\phi\,d\mu_{\lambda_{1}+\epsilon,\lambda_{2}}

Here

where

Since we are free to make a change of variable, we may assume without loss of generality, that
d_{1}=1,d_{2}=1
, see the discussion after Theorem
1.3
in §

still has bounded derivative.

The idea of proving Theorem

is similar to that of Theorem
1.3
. We shall approximate the actual integral by a finite truncate, and then calculate the derivative using this finite truncate. More precisely, we have the following two lemmas, which are virtually identical to those in §
2
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle X_{n}(\epsilon):=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}(\epsilon)\cdots\lambda_{i_{j-1}}(\epsilon)
and write

for

and

Moreover, the constants

\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}
and

be such that there exists some constant

satisfying
\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\log|\frac{1}{\epsilon}|\leq n\leq d\log|\frac{1}{\epsilon}|
. Then the following limit holds:

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\int_{\Sigma}\phi(X_{n}(\epsilon))\,d\nu-\int_{\Sigma}\phi(X_{n})\,d\nu}{\epsilon}=\int_{\Sigma}h^{\prime}(X)X^{(1)}\,d\nu.

In the following subsections, we first show how to deduce Theorem
4.1
assuming the above two lemmas, which is a simple version of the one we did at the end of §
2
. Then we turn to the proof of the two lemmas.

We end this subsection with a binomial identity to be used in the proof.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
t\leq j\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds

\sum_{k=t}^{j}k(k-1)\cdots(k-t+1)\binom{j}{k}\lambda_{1}^{k-t}\lambda_{2}^{j-k}=j(j-1)\cdots(j-t+1)(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})^{j-t}.

Proof.

By the Binomial identity,

\displaystyle(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})^{j}=\sum_{k=0}^{j}\binom{j}{k}\lambda_{1}^{k}\lambda_{2}^{j-k}.

Differentiating both sides

times with respect to

, we get the desired identity.
∎

Proof.

We first compute

(4.1)

where

(4.2)

(4.3)

and

denotes the binomial coefficients
(we set

By Taylor’s theorem, there exists

such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\frac{\phi(X_{n}(\epsilon))-\phi(X_{n})}{\epsilon}\,d\nu

\displaystyle=\int\phi^{\prime}(X_{n}+\xi_{n}(X_{n}^{\prime}-X_{n}))a_{n,1}\,d\nu+\int\phi^{\prime}(X_{n}+\xi_{n}(X_{n}(\epsilon)-X_{n}))\sum_{t=2}^{n-1}a_{n,t}\epsilon^{t-1}\,d\nu

\displaystyle:=J_{1}+J_{2}.

We claim that

. Fix any

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}^{t}}\sum_{j=t+1}^{n}\int\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{j-1}}\binom{o(j-1)}{t}.

Moreover, for any

\displaystyle\int\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{j-1}}\binom{o(j-1)}{t}=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{j-1}\sum_{k=t}^{j-1}\binom{j-1}{k}\lambda_{1}^{k}\lambda_{2}^{j-1-k}\binom{k}{t}

Lemma 5.3
.

For any

,

and

(a_{1}+\cdots a_{n})^{\alpha}\leq a_{1}^{\alpha}+\cdots+a_{n}^{\alpha}.

Proof.

If
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=0
then the inequality is trivially true. Otherwise, the lemma is equivalent to

1\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{a_{i}}{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}}\right)^{\alpha}.

Note that
(\frac{a_{i}}{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}})^{\alpha}\geq\frac{a_{i}}{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}},i=1,\cdots,n
, since for each

and

. This immediately implies the lemma.

Lemma 5.4
.

For any
a\in\mathbb{R}
and any

, we have

(5.1)

X^{a}-Y^{a}\leq\max\{a,1\}X^{a-1}(X-Y),\text{ if }a\geq 0

(5.2)

Proof.

If

. It follows that

X^{a}-Y^{a}\leq X^{a}-X^{a-1}Y=X^{a-1}(X-Y)

as desired.

If

such that

and the first inequality is proved.

If

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
a,b,j
be any non-negative integers. Then

\binom{a}{j}\leq 3^{j}\left(\binom{a-b}{j}+1\right)(b^{j}+1).

Note that we adopt the convention
\binom{u}{v}=0
if
v>u
.

Proof.

If
a<j
or
b=0
or
j=0
, then the inequality is trivially true. So let’s assume
a\geq j
and
b,j\geq 1
.

If
a-b\leq j+1
, then

\binom{a}{j}=\frac{a(a-1)\cdots(a-j+1)}{j!}\leq\frac{(b+j+1)(b+j)\cdots(b+2)}{j(j-1)\cdots 1}\leq 3^{j}b^{j}<3^{j}\left[\binom{a-b}{j}+1\right](b^{j}+1)

where the penultimate
inequality is because
\frac{b+k+1}{k}\leq 3b
for any
k\!\!\in\!\!\{1,\cdots,j\}
and
b\geq 1
.

If
a-b\geq j+2
, then

\frac{\binom{a}{j}}{\binom{a-b}{j}}\!=\!\frac{a(a-1)\cdots(a-j+1)}{(a-b)(a-b-1)\cdots(a-b-j+1)}=\!\!\!\!\prod_{x=a-b-j+1}^{a-b}\!\!\!\left(1+\frac{b}{x}\right)\leq(2b)^{j}\!\!<\!\!3^{j}\left[\binom{a-b}{j}+1\right](b^{j}+1)

where the penultimate inequality is true since
1+\frac{b}{x}\leq 2b
, for any
x\geq 3
and
b\geq 1
. The lemma if proved.
∎

k>t
.

If
k\leq t
, we can further assume
t>k
, because otherwise
\phi^{(k)}(X)-\phi^{(k)}(X_{n})
is zero and
J_{2}=0
. Now

\displaystyle|(\phi^{(k)}(X)-\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}))\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}|

\displaystyle\preceq(X^{t-k}-X_{n}^{t-k})\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}

\displaystyle\preceq X^{t-k-1}(X-X_{n})\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}

(5.21)

\displaystyle\leq X^{t-k-1}(\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}K_{l}(m))(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}K_{l}(m))^{k}

where in the second inequality we have used Lemma
5.4
.

Now there are two sub-cases. If
t\geq k+1
, then we are in the same situation
as in the second term in (
5.6
), with
k
replaced with
k+1
and
s
replaced by
1
. Therefore, there exists
C=C(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},t,l)>0
and
\theta=\theta(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},t,l)\in(0,1)
such that

(5.22)

J_{2}\leq C\theta^{n},

proving that
J_{2}
is exponentially decaying in the case
k\leq t
.

If
k<t<k+1
, write
\beta:=t-k\in(0,1)
. We have

(5.23)

\displaystyle X^{t-k-1}(X-X_{n})(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}K_{l}(m))^{k}

(5.24)

\displaystyle=(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}K_{l}(m))^{t-\beta}\frac{\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{n}}(\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i_{n+1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}})}{X^{1-\beta}}

(5.25)

\displaystyle\leq(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}K_{l}(m))^{t-\beta}(\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{n}})^{\beta}(\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{n+1}}\cdots\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{m}}).

Moreover, the right hand side is exponentially small if we can show that

\int(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}K_{l}(m))^{t-\beta}(\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{n}})^{\beta}\text{ is exponentially small }

and

\int(\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{n+1}}\cdots\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{m}})\text{ is uniformly bounded.}

Now the former can be proved exactly the same way as (
5.8
), and the latter is obvious since
\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\beta}+\lambda_{2}^{\beta}}{2}<1
by monotonicity of the function
x\mapsto\frac{\lambda_{1}^{x}+\lambda_{2}^{x}}{2}
. Thus
J_{2}
is exponentially small in the case
t\geq k
.

If
k>t
, then

\displaystyle|(\phi^{(k)}(X)-

\displaystyle\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}))\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}|\preceq(X_{n}^{t-k}-X^{t-k})\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}

\displaystyle\preceq X_{n}^{t-k}X^{-1}(X-X_{n})(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}K_{l}(m))^{k}

(5.26)

\displaystyle\leq X_{n}^{t-k}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}K_{l}(m)\right)^{k}\frac{\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}}{X}

where the middle inequality uses Lemma
5.4
. Define
\beta=\frac{t}{k+1}\in(0,1)
and rewrite (
5.26
) as

\displaystyle\frac{(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}K_{l}(m))^{k}}{X_{n}^{(1-\beta)k}}\frac{\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}}{X^{1-\beta}}\frac{X_{n}^{\beta}}{X^{\beta}}

\displaystyle\leq(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}}{X_{n}^{1-\beta}}K_{l}(m))^{k}\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{m}}\leq(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{m}}K_{l}(m))^{k}\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{n}}\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{n+1}}\cdots\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{m}}.

since
X\geq\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}
for all
m
and
X>X_{n}.

Note that

\frac{\lambda_{1}^{(k+1)\beta}+\lambda_{2}^{(k+1)\beta}}{2}=\frac{\lambda^{t}_{1}+\lambda^{t}_{2}}{2}<1\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\beta}+\lambda_{2}^{\beta}}{2}\leq\kappa^{\prime\prime}:=\max_{u\in\mathbb{Z}:t+1<u\leq l}\left\{\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\frac{t}{u}}+\lambda_{2}^{\frac{t}{u}}}{2}\right\}<1.

Therefore, the above right hand side is almost the same as the
right hand side in
(
5.17
), with
k
replaced with
k+1
and
s
replaced with
1
.
The only difference is that here the tail term is
\displaystyle B_{n}^{\prime}:=\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{n+1}}\cdots\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{m}}
versus

\displaystyle B_{n}=\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{n+1}}\cdots\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{m}}(1+(o(m)-o(n))^{l})
in
(
5.17
).
But clearly
B_{n}^{\prime}\leq B_{n}
, so by the same reasoning as in (
5.3
), there exists
C=C(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},t,l)>0
,
M=M(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},t,l)>0
and
\theta=\theta(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},t,l)\in(0,1)
such that

\int(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{m}}K_{l}(m))^{k}\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{n}}\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{n+1}}\cdots\lambda^{\beta}_{i_{m}}\leq MC\theta^{n}.

This immediately implies
J_{2}\leq MC\theta^{n}
as desired. The proof is complete.
∎

5.4.

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma

, we wish to show, for any given tuple of integers
(k_{1},\cdots k_{l})
satisfying

(5.27)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n}(\epsilon))^{k_{j}}-\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n})\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}\right)

=\int\phi^{(k+1)}(X)X^{(1)}\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}}+\int\phi^{(k)}(X)\sum_{j=1}^{l}(k_{j}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}X^{(j+1)}\prod_{i\neq j}(X^{(i)})^{k_{i}}),

where
\displaystyle k:=\sum_{i=1}^{l}k_{i}
. As before, we divide the difference into two parts.

\displaystyle\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n}(\epsilon))^{k_{j}}-\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n})\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int(\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))-\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}))\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}+\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))(\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n}(\epsilon))^{k_{j}}-\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}))

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle G_{1}+G_{2}.

We first deal with
\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}G_{2}
. By a simple calculation, for
j=0,\cdots,l
we have

X_{n}^{(j)}(\epsilon)=X_{n}^{(j)}+\sum_{r=1}^{n}b^{(j)}_{n,r}\epsilon^{r}=:X^{(j)}_{n}+T^{(j)}_{n},

where

(5.28)

b^{(j)}_{n,r}:=\frac{j!}{\lambda_{1}^{r+j}}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}\binom{o(m)-j}{r}\binom{o(m)}{j}.

By the same reasoning as in Lemma
2.2
, it suffices
to calculate the limit

(5.29)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int X^{t-k}_{n}\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}-s_{j}}(T^{(j)}_{n})^{s_{j}}

for any given integer tuple
(s_{1},\cdots,s_{l})
with
0\leq s_{i}\leq k_{i},i=1,\cdots,l
and
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{l}s_{i}>0
.
Note that we can write
X_{n}^{t-k}
instead of
X^{t-k}_{n}(\epsilon)
since
\frac{X_{n}}{X_{n}(\epsilon)}\to 1
as
\epsilon\to 0
. To see this, note that
n=\mathcal{O}(\log\frac{1}{|\epsilon|})
and
o(m)\leq m
. Thus if
|\epsilon|
is small enough, we have for any
1\leq m\leq n
,

\frac{\lambda_{i_{1}}(\epsilon)\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}(\epsilon)}{\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}}=\frac{(\lambda_{1}+\epsilon)^{o(m)}\lambda_{2}^{m-o(m)}}{\lambda_{1}^{o(m)}\lambda_{2}^{m-o(m)}}=\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda_{1}}\right)^{o(m)}\to 1,\text{ as }\epsilon\to 0.

Hence
\frac{X_{n}(\epsilon)}{X_{n}}\to 1
as
\epsilon\to 0
.

We claim that

(5.30)

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int X^{t-k}_{n}\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}-s_{j}}(T^{(j)}_{n})^{s_{j}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,\text{ if }\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{l}s_{j}\geq 2\\
\sum_{1\leq j\leq l,k_{j}\geq 1}\int X^{t-k}\prod_{i\neq j}(X^{(i)})^{k_{i}}k_{j}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}X^{(j+1)},\text{ if }\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{l}s_{j}=1,\end{cases}

which agrees with taking formal derivative.

Note that for any
j\in\{1,\cdots,l\}
and
r\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
,

b^{(j)}_{n,r}=\frac{j!}{\lambda_{1}^{r+j}}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}\binom{o(m)-j}{r}\binom{o(m)}{j}

\leq\frac{l!}{\lambda_{1}^{l}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}^{r}}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}(o(m)-j)^{r}o(m)^{j}\preceq\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}^{r}}o(n)^{r}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}(1+o(m)^{l+1}).

Therefore

(5.31)

|(\sum_{r=1}^{n}b^{(j)}_{n,r}\epsilon^{r})^{s_{j}}|\preceq\left(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}(o(m))^{l}\right)^{s_{j}}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{n}o(n)^{r}\left(\frac{|\epsilon|}{\lambda_{1}}\right)^{r}\right)^{s_{j}},\quad j=1,\cdots,l.

On the other hand, we have

\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}-s_{j}}\leq(\sum_{m=1}^{n}{\lambda_{i_{1}}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}K_{l}(m))^{k-s}\preceq(\sum_{m=1}^{n}{\lambda_{i_{1}}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}(1+o(m)^{l+1}))^{k-s},

where
s:=s_{1}+\cdots+s_{l}
. Taking products, we see that

X^{t-k}_{n}\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}-s_{j}}|(T^{(j)}_{n})^{s_{j}}|\preceq X_{n}^{t-k}(\sum_{m=1}^{n}{\lambda_{i_{1}}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}(1+o(m)^{l+1}))^{(k-s)+s}(\sum_{r=1}^{n}o(n)^{r}(\frac{|\epsilon|}{\lambda_{1}})^{r})^{s}

\leq X_{n}^{t-k}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{n}X_{n}(1+o(m)^{l+1})\right)^{k}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{n}n^{r}(\frac{|\epsilon|}{\lambda_{1}})^{r}\right)^{s}=X_{n}^{t}\sum_{m=1}^{n}(1+m^{l+1})^{k}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{n}n^{r}\left(\frac{|\epsilon|}{\lambda_{1}}\right)^{r}\right)^{s}

\preceq X_{n}^{t}(1+n^{l^{2}+l+1})\left(\sum_{r=1}^{n}n^{r}\left(\frac{|\epsilon|}{\lambda_{1}}\right)^{r}\right)^{s}

If
s\geq 2
, it follows that

(5.32)

\displaystyle|\frac{1}{\epsilon}X^{t-k}_{n}\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}-s_{j}}(T^{(j)}_{n})^{s_{j}}|

\displaystyle\preceq(1+n^{l^{2}+l+1})\left(\sum_{r=1}^{n}n^{r}\frac{|\epsilon|^{r(1-\frac{1}{rs})}}{\lambda_{1}^{r}}\right)^{s}\int X_{n}^{t}

and the right hand side converges to
0
as
\epsilon\to 0
, since
\int X_{n}^{t}
is uniformly bounded,
n
is of order
\log\frac{1}{|\epsilon|}
and
1-\frac{1}{rs}\geq\frac{1}{2},\forall r\geq 1
.

The remaining terms in
G_{2}
correspond to those tuples where one of
s_{j}
is one and the others are zero. They sum up to

\displaystyle\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))\sum_{1\leq j\leq l,k_{j}\geq 1}k_{j}\prod_{i\neq j}(X_{n}^{(i)})^{k_{i}}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}T_{n}^{(j)}.

Fix any
j\in\{1,\cdots,l\}
with
k_{j}\geq 1
, it suffices to show that

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))k_{j}\prod_{i\neq j}(X_{n}^{(i)})^{k_{i}}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}T_{n}^{(j)}=\int\phi^{(k)}(X)k_{j}\prod_{i\neq j}(X^{(i)})^{k_{i}}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}X^{(j+1)}.

Observe that

\frac{1}{\epsilon}T^{(j)}_{n}=b^{(j)}_{n,1}+\sum_{r=1}^{n-1}b^{(j)}_{n,r+1}\epsilon^{r}\quad\text{ and }\quad b^{(j)}_{n,1}=X_{n}^{(j+1)}.

Thus it suffices to show

(5.33)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))k_{j}\prod_{i\neq j}(X_{n}^{(i)})^{k_{i}}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{n-1}b^{(j)}_{n,r+1}\epsilon^{r}\right)=0,

and

(5.34)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))k_{j}\prod_{i\neq j}(X_{n}^{(i)})^{k_{i}}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}X_{n}^{(j+1)}=

\int\phi^{(k)}(X)k_{j}\prod_{i\neq j}(X^{(i)})^{k_{i}}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}X^{(j+1)}.

The first identity can be proved in the same way as (
5.32
) so we omit its proof. For the second identity, we discuss separately the cases
t\geq k
and

.

If

, then

\displaystyle\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))k_{j}\prod_{i\neq j}(X_{n}^{(i)})^{k_{i}}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}X_{n}^{(j+1)}

\displaystyle\preceq(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}K_{l}(m))^{t-k+\sum_{i\neq j}k_{i}+(k_{j}-1)+1}

\displaystyle=(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}K_{l}(m))^{t}

where the inequality follows by
\displaystyle\ \lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{X_{n}{(\epsilon)}}{X_{n}}=1
,

and

\displaystyle X_{n}^{(j)}\leq\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}K_{l}(m)
for

. Since
\frac{\lambda_{1}^{t}+\lambda_{2}^{t}}{2}<1
, the above right hand side converges a.e. and in

as

\phi^{(k)}(X)k_{j}\prod_{i\neq j}(X^{(i)})^{k_{i}}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}X^{(j+1)}
as

. Hence the generalized dominated convergence theorem implies

5.34

If

, then define
\beta=\frac{t}{k}\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))k_{j}\prod_{i\neq j}(X_{n}^{(i)})^{k_{i}}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}X_{n}^{(j+1)}

\displaystyle\preceq\frac{(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}K_{l}(m))^{\sum_{i\neq j}k_{i}+(k_{j}-1)+1}}{X_{n}^{k-t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}K_{l}(m))^{k}}{X_{n}^{k(1-\beta)}}

as

(
5.34

.

It remains to calculate

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int(\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))-\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}))\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}

which implies the desired result. Moreover, (
5.35
) can be proved in the same way as (
5.32
), and (
5.36

5.34

with
0\leq s_{j}\leq k_{j}
,
j=1,\cdots,r-1
, the following integral

\int\phi^{(k)}(X)\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}-s_{j}}(R^{(j)}_{n})^{s_{j}}.

Since
\phi^{(r)}
is bounded and
k\leq r-1
, there exists some constant
M
such that

(6.2)

\phi^{(k)}(x)\leq Mx^{r-k},\;\forall x\geq 1,\;\forall\,0\leq k\leq r.

Since
X\geq 1
by definition, we have

\int\phi^{(k)}(X)\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}-s_{j}}(R^{(j)}_{n})^{s_{j}}\leq M\int X^{r-k}\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}-s_{j}}(R^{(j)}_{n})^{s_{j}}.

With
\frac{\lambda_{1}^{r}+\lambda_{2}^{r}}{2}<1
, we are in the same situation as in
(
5.3
) and the rest of the proof follows verbatim.

Next we consider
J_{2}
. We have

|(\phi^{(k)}(X)-\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}))\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}|=\phi^{(k+1)}(X_{n}+\xi_{n}(X-X_{n}))(X-X_{n})\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}

\leq M(X_{n}+\xi_{n}(X-X_{n}))^{r-k-1}\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}\leq MX^{r-k-1}\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}},

where in the last inequality we have used that
r-k-1\geq 0
. Now we are in the same situation as in the second line of
(
5.3
), so the proof follows by the same argument. This finishes the proof of Lemma
6.1
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
6.2
.

As in the proof of Lemma
5.2
, we only need to show, for any given tuple of integers
(k_{1},\cdots k_{r-1})
satisfying
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}(r-1)k_{r-1}=r-1
, that

(6.3)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n}(\epsilon))^{k_{j}}-\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n})\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}\right)

=\int\phi^{(k+1)}(X)X^{(1)}\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}}+\int\phi^{(k)}(X)\sum_{j=1}^{l}(k_{j}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}X^{(j+1)}\prod_{i\neq j}(X^{(i)})^{k_{i}}),

where
\displaystyle k:=\sum_{i=1}^{l}k_{i}
. As before, the left hand side is divided into two parts.

\displaystyle\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n}(\epsilon))^{k_{j}}-\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n})\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int(\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))-\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}))\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}+\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))(\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n}(\epsilon))^{k_{j}}-\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}))

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle G_{1}+G_{2}.

We deal with
G_{2}
first. Recall for
j=0,\cdots,l
the notations

X_{n}^{(j)}(\epsilon)=X_{n}^{(j)}+\sum_{r=1}^{n}b^{(j)}_{n,r}\epsilon^{r}=:X^{(j)}_{n}+T^{(j)}_{n},

and

(6.4)

b^{(j)}_{n,r}:=\frac{j!}{\lambda_{1}^{r+j}}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m}}\binom{o(m)-j}{r}\binom{o(m)}{j}.

By the same reasoning as in Lemma
5.2
, it suffices to calculate the limit

(6.5)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n})\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}-s_{j}}(T^{(j)}_{n})^{s_{j}}

for each given tuple
(s_{1},\cdots,s_{r-1})
. We claim that

(6.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n})\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}-s_{j}}(T^{(j)}_{n})^{s_{j}}

(6.7)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,\text{ if }\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}s_{j}\geq 2\\
\sum_{1\leq j\leq r-1,k_{j}\geq 1}\int\phi^{(k)}(X)\prod_{i\neq j}(X^{(i)})^{k_{i}}k_{j}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}X^{(j+1)},\text{ if }\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}s_{j}=1,\end{cases}

which agrees with taking formal derivative.

The case
\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}s_{j}\geq 2
holds since
\phi^{(k)}(X_{n})\leq MX_{n}^{r-k}
and the rest of the proof follows the same lines as in the first case in (
5.30
).

As for the case
\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}s_{j}=1
, similar as before we need to show that for each
k_{j}\geq 1
,

(6.8)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))k_{j}\prod_{i\neq j}(X_{n}^{(i)})^{k_{i}}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{n-1}b^{(j)}_{n,r+1}\epsilon^{r}\right)=0,

and

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))k_{j}\prod_{i\neq j}(X_{n}^{(i)})^{k_{i}}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}X_{n}^{(j+1)}=\int\phi^{(k)}(X)k_{j}\prod_{i\neq j}(X^{(i)})^{k_{i}}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}X^{(j+1)}.

To prove the first identity, note that
\displaystyle\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))\leq MX_{n}(\epsilon)^{r-k}\leq 2MX_{n}^{r-k}
, where the second inequality holds for sufficiently small
\epsilon
, since
\frac{X_{n}(\epsilon)}{X_{n}}\to 1
as
\epsilon\to 0
. The rest part of the proof is the same as the proof of (
5.33
).

To prove the second identity, note that the integrand on the left hand side converges a.e. to the integrand on the right hand side. Moreover, since
X_{n}(\epsilon),X_{n}\geq 1
and
\frac{X_{n}(\epsilon)}{X_{n}}\!\!\to\!\!1
as
\epsilon\to 0
, we have

\phi^{(k)}(X_{n}(\epsilon))k_{j}\prod_{i\neq j}(X_{n}^{(i)})^{k_{i}}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}T_{n}^{(j)}\leq 2k_{j}MX_{n}^{r-k}|\prod_{i\neq j}(X_{n}^{(i)})^{k_{i}}(X_{n}^{(j)})^{k_{j}-1}T_{n}^{(j)}|.

Since
\frac{\lambda_{1}^{r}+\lambda_{2}^{2}}{2}<1
, the above right hand side converges both a.e and in
L^{1}
, as the proof of identity (
5.34
) shows. Therefore, the generalized dominated convergence theorem
2.3
confirms the second identity.

Finally we consider
G_{1}
. As in the proof of (
5.2
), it suffices to show

(6.9)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int(\phi^{(k+1)}(X_{n}+\xi_{n}(X_{n}(\epsilon)-X_{n}))\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}(\sum_{r=1}^{n-1}b^{(0)}_{n,r+1}\epsilon^{r})=0

and

(6.10)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int(\phi^{(k+1)}(X_{n}+\xi_{n}(X_{n}(\epsilon)-X_{n}))\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)}_{n})^{k_{j}}X_{n}^{(1)}=\int\phi^{(k+1)}(X)\prod_{j=1}^{l}(X^{(j)})^{k_{j}}X^{(1)}.

Given the proof of(
5.35
) and (
5.36
), it is enough to observe that

(\phi^{(k+1)}(X_{n}+\xi_{n}(X_{n}(\epsilon)-X_{n}))\leq 2MX_{n}^{r-k-1},\text{ for sufficiently small }\epsilon.

The proof of Lemma
6.2
is complete.
∎

Lemmata
6.1
and
6.2

establish
Theorem
1.1
.
∎

7.
Extensions and open problems

We end by discussing several generalization of the previous results.

7.1.
Differentiability in other directions

In this subsection we discuss differentiability with respect to
\lambda_{2},d_{1},d_{2}
.

First, one could define
g_{\phi}(\epsilon)=g(\epsilon)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi\,d\mu_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}+\epsilon}
and study the derivative with respect to
\lambda_{2}
. All the above theorems remains valid with the same proof, and the formulae for the derivative is replaced with the formal derivative taken with respect to
\lambda_{2}
.

Likewise, if one studies the derivative with respect to
d_{1}
or
d_{2}
, then the same assumptions give the same conclusions in each theorem, with the derivative equal to the formal derivative. But in this case, the formal derivative becomes much simpler. For example, let us consider the derivative with respect to
d_{1}
. That is, define

h(d)=\int_{\Sigma}\phi(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d_{i_{n}}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{n-1}})\,d\nu

where
d_{1}=d,d_{2}=d_{2}
and
d_{2}
is given. By taking formal derivative, we can prove

h^{\prime}(d)=\int_{\Sigma}\phi^{\prime}(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d_{i_{n}}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{n-1}})\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{n-1}}\delta_{n,1}\right)\,d\nu

where
\delta_{n,1}=1
if
i_{n}=1
and is zero otherwise. If we take higher order derivatives, whenever a derivative hits the term
\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{n-1}}\delta_{n,1}
it yields zero. So, under the correct assumptions, we have

h^{(l)}(d)=\int_{\Sigma}\phi^{(l)}(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d_{i_{n}}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{n-1}})\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{n-1}}\delta_{n,1}\right)^{l}\,d\nu.

The above discussion readily implies that, if
d_{1},d_{2},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
are
C^{1}
(resp.
C^{l}
) functions defined on some neighborhood
(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
of
0
, then
h_{\phi}
will be differentiable (resp.
l
-times differentiable) with the derivative coinciding with the formal derivative, under the same assumption as in the main theorems. For instance, if
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, then
h_{\phi}
is
C^{\infty}
, and

h_{\phi}^{\prime}(0)=\int_{\Sigma}\phi^{\prime}(X)\left(\lambda_{1}^{\prime}(0)\frac{\partial X}{\partial\lambda_{1}}+\lambda_{2}^{\prime}(0)\frac{\partial X}{\partial\lambda_{2}}+d_{1}^{\prime}(0)\frac{\partial X}{\partial d_{1}}+d_{2}^{\prime}(0)\frac{\partial X}{\partial d_{2}}\right)\,d\nu(i).

Here
\displaystyle X=X(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},d_{2},d_{2};i)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}d_{i_{m}}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m-1}}
with
i=(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots)\in\Sigma:=\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. And
\frac{\partial X}{\partial\lambda_{j}},\frac{\partial X}{\partial d_{j}},j=1,2
are the formal derivative, taken by viewing
X
as a power series. For higher order derivatives one gets formal derivatives of higher order.

7.2.
Multiple maps

Next, we address the case of multiple maps
f_{i}(x)=\lambda_{i}x+d_{i},i=1,2,\cdots,k
assigned with probability
(p_{1},\cdots,p_{k})
. Here we assume
k\geq 2
,
\displaystyle\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
and there is at least one contraction and one expansion among
f_{1},\cdots,f_{k}
. Moreover, the system is contracting on average:
\displaystyle\sum_{i}p_{i}\log|\lambda_{i}|<0
and
f_{i}
’s do not share the same fixed point.

We claim that in this case, the
actual derivative equals formal derivative

assertions in our main results remain valid, under mild change of the assumption. More precisely:

Theorem
1.3
remains true if in addition we assume that each
\lambda_{i},d_{i}>0,\forall i
. This is because the key Varadham’s lemma-type of estimate (see
2.3
) will still be true, given that

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{N}d_{i_{m}}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m-1}}
is monotone increasing in
N
and the systems is contracting on average.

Theorem
1.1
will hold if we replace the assumption
\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{1}^{r}+\lambda_{2}^{r}}{2}<1
by
\displaystyle\sum_{i}p_{i}|\lambda_{i}|^{r}<1
. To see this, observe that the proof of the original theorem relies critically on two facts:

(1)

|\phi^{(l)}(x)|\preceq|x|^{r-l}+1,l=0,1,\cdots,r
, since
||\phi^{(r)}||_{C^{0}}<\infty
.

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{1}^{t}+\lambda_{2}^{t}}{2}<1,\forall 0<t\leq r
. And this is due to the behavior of the function
\displaystyle f(t):=\log\frac{\lambda_{1}^{t}+\lambda_{2}^{t}}{2},t\geq 0
:
f(0)=0,f^{\prime}(0)<0
,
f^{\prime\prime}>0
and
f(\infty)=\infty
. Item
(2)
will follow from these by an elementary analysis.

These two facts together yield a uniform integrability estimate, which essentially finishes the proof. Now under the new setting, we have two similar facts that guarantee uniform integrability:

(1)

|\phi^{(l)}(x)|\preceq|x|^{r-l}+1,l=0,1,\cdots,r
, since
||\phi^{(r)}||_{C^{0}}<\infty
.

(2)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}|\lambda_{i}|^{t}<1,\forall 0<t\leq r
.

The first claim is clearly true. The second claim holds since its left hand side comes from a moment generating function.
Namely, consider a random variable
Y
which takes values

\log|\lambda_{i}|
with probability
p_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots k

and define

\displaystyle f(t)=\log\mathbb{E}[e^{tY}]
for
t\geq 0.

Then a direct computation gives

f^{\prime}(t)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[e^{tY}Y]}{\mathbb{E}[e^{tY}]},\quad f^{\prime\prime}(t)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}e^{tY}]\mathbb{E}[e^{tY}]-\mathbb{E}[Ye^{tY}]^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[e^{tY}]^{2}}.

It readily follows that
f(0)=0
,
f^{\prime}(0)<0
(by contracting on average),
f(\infty)=\infty
(since there is at least one expansion). Moreover,
f^{\prime\prime}>0
by Cauchy-Schwartz inequality. Thus the second item follows by simple analysis.

Finally, Theorem
1.2
will also be true, if one changes the assumption
\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{1}^{t}+\lambda_{2}^{t}}{2}<1
into
\displaystyle\sum_{i}p_{i}|\lambda_{i}|^{t}<1
, by the same argument as above.

7.3.
Degenerate case: Common Fixed Point

So far we have always assumed that the maps
f_{i}
do not share a common fixed point. It turns out if there are only two maps, then linear response still holds, even in the degenerate case.

More specifically, let
f^{t}_{1}(x)=\lambda_{1}(t)x+d_{1}(t),f^{t}_{2}(x)=\lambda_{2}(t)x+d_{2}(t);\;p=(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
be a
C^{\infty}
one parameter family of maps, with
0<\lambda_{1}<1<\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}<1
uniformly for
t\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
. Moreover assume that at
t=0
the two maps have a same fixed point, or equivalently,
\displaystyle\frac{d_{1}(0)}{1-\lambda_{1}(0)}=\frac{d_{2}(0)}{1-\lambda_{2}(0)}
. We also assume this degeneracy is isolated, so that
\displaystyle\frac{d_{1}(t)}{1-\lambda_{1}(t)}\neq\frac{d_{2}(t)}{1-\lambda_{2}(t)},\;\forall t\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\setminus\{0\}
.

Denote the stationary measure for
\{f_{1}^{t},f_{2}^{t};p\}
by
\mu_{t}
. The goal is to study the differentiability of
h(t)=h_{\phi}(t)=\int\phi\,d\mu_{t}
at
t=0
.

We claim that Theorems
1.3
,
1.2
and
1.1
remain true. The reason is that the proofs of these three theorems are based on the following two facts:

(1)

\mu_{t}
is the law of
X(t)=X(t;i)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}d_{i_{m}}(t)\lambda_{i_{1}}(t)\cdots\lambda_{i_{m-1}}(t),\;i=(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots)\in\Sigma
. Here recall that
\Sigma=\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
,
\nu=p^{\mathbb{N}}
, and
i=(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots)\in\Sigma\text{ has law }p
.

(2)

After a change of variable, one can make
d_{1},d_{2}
to have the same sign, so that
\sum_{m=1}^{N}d_{i_{m}}(t)\lambda_{i_{1}}(t)\cdots\lambda_{i_{m-1}}(t)
is monotone in
N
.

In the present case, fact
1
survives because when
t=0
,
\mu_{t}=\delta_{x_{0}}
is the delta mass on the fixed point
x_{0}=\frac{d_{1}(0)-d_{2}(0)}{\lambda_{2}(0)-\lambda_{1}(0)}
, which is indeed equal to the law of

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}d_{i_{m}}(0)\lambda_{i_{1}}(0)\cdots\lambda_{i_{m-1}}(0)
. To see this, note that for any fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

\sum_{m=1}^{N}d_{i_{m}}(0)\lambda_{i_{1}}(0)\cdots\lambda_{i_{m-1}}(0)=\sum_{m=1}^{N}d_{i_{m}}(0)\lambda_{i_{1}}(0)\cdots\lambda_{i_{m-1}}(0)+\lambda_{i_{1}}(0)\cdots\lambda_{i_{N}}(0)x_{0}-\lambda_{i_{1}}(0)\cdots\lambda_{i_{N}}(0)x_{0}

=f_{i_{1}}^{0}\circ\cdots f_{i_{N}}^{0}(x_{0})-\lambda_{i_{1}}(0)\cdots\lambda_{i_{N}}(0)x_{0}\\
=x_{0}-\lambda_{i_{1}}(0)\cdots\lambda_{i_{N}}(0)x_{0}.

As
N\to\infty
, the above discrete measure converges weakly to
\delta_{x_{0}}
because
\mathbb{E}[\log\lambda_{i}(0)]\!\!<\!\!0
.

The second fact can be compensated as follows. Define a family of change of variables
c^{t}(x)=x+\frac{d_{2}(t)}{1-\lambda_{2}(t)}
. One can easily check that
f_{i}^{t}\circ c^{t}=c^{t}\circ\tilde{f}_{i}^{t}
for
i=1,2
where
\tilde{f}_{1}^{t}(x)=\lambda_{1}(t)x+\epsilon(t),\tilde{f}_{2}^{t}(x)=\lambda_{2}(t)x
and
\epsilon(t)=\frac{d_{1}(t)(1-\lambda_{2}(t))-d_{2}(t)(1-\lambda_{1}(t))}{1-\lambda_{2}(t)}
.

Denote by
\tilde{\mu}_{t}
the stationary measure for
\{\tilde{f}_{1}^{t},\tilde{f}_{2}^{t};p\}
. By the same argument at the beginning of §
2
, for any
\phi
,

h_{\phi}(t)=\int\phi\,d\mu_{t}=\int\phi\circ c^{t}\,d\tilde{\mu}_{t}=\int\phi(x+\epsilon(t))\,d\tilde{\mu}_{t}(x).

Moreover, for the system
\{\tilde{f}_{1}^{t},\tilde{f}_{2}^{t};p\}
, the translation factors
d_{1}(t)=\epsilon(t),d_{2}(t)=0
always have the same sign, which leads

\forall M>0,\;\;\mathbb{E}[\sum_{m=N}^{\infty}d_{i_{j}}(t)\lambda_{i_{1}}(t)\cdots\lambda_{i_{m-1}}(t)1_{\{|X(t;i)|\}\leq M}]\leq C\theta^{N}

for some
C=C(M,\lambda_{1}(0),\lambda_{2}(0))>0
and
\theta=\theta(\lambda_{1}(0),\lambda_{2}(0))\in(0,1)
, uniformly for
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
. This in turn yields uniform integrability and one can argue the same way as in Lemma
2.1
and Lemma
2.2
to show that, if
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}
, then
\tilde{h}_{\phi}(t):=\int\phi\,d\tilde{\mu_{t}}
is
C^{\infty}
at
0
and the derivatives coincide with formal ones.

Therefore, linear response holds for the original system
\{f_{1}^{t},f_{2}^{t};p\}
by chain rule:

h_{\phi}^{\prime}(t)=\int\phi^{\prime}(x+\epsilon(0))\epsilon^{\prime}(0)\,d\tilde{\mu}_{0}(x)+\frac{d}{dt}\int\phi(x+\epsilon(0))\,d\tilde{\mu}_{t}=\int\phi^{\prime}(x)\epsilon^{\prime}(0)\,d\tilde{\mu}_{0}(x)+\tilde{h}^{\prime}_{\phi}(0).

The last equality holds because
\epsilon(0)=0
. Higher order derivatives can be computed in the same way.

7.4.
An open Question

We end this section by pointing out a case we do not know how to deal with. Note that in Theorem
1.3
, we relied on the fact that
d_{1},d_{2},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
so that the sum
\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{N}d_{i_{m}}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m-1}}
is monotone increasing in
N
. And we are able to assume
d_{1},d_{2}>0
because there are two functions thus a change of variable makes them positive (see the discussion at the beginning of §
2
). In general, if the IFS consists of more than two maps, and the
\lambda_{i}
’s and
d_{i}
’s are such that

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}d_{i_{j}}\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{m-1}}
can have alternating terms, then the proof does not work. The author does not know whether Theorem
1.3
will still hold in this case.
