II. ACCOMPLISHMENTS
1. What were the major goals of the project?

This project was part of the “Center for Integrated Simulation of Fusion Relevant RF Actuators”
SciDAC-4 project, led by Dr. Paul Bonoli (MIT). Rather than use an acronym (CISFRRFA), the
project will be referred to in this document as the “RF-SciDAC4”.

The larger SciDAC-4 project goals were to:
 1. Develop an integrated simulation of the antenna + sheath + scrape-off-layer + core plasma
 system which fully utilizes leadership class computing resources to move towards a
 quantitative predictive capability for the response to RF power.
 2. Work closely with the SciDAC-4 Whole Device Modeling (WDM) community to make
 both our new code development efforts, as well as the established hierarchy of RF tools,
 available within their environment, and to utilize WDM technologies to implement the
 couplings below.
 3. Validate this predictive capability on appropriately diagnosed experiments including
 dedicated RF test stands, linear devices, and existing tokamaks.
 4. Use these tools to inform design of robust, impurity-mitigating RF heating and current
 drive sources for future fusion devices.
A general overview of Tech-X efforts toward the achievement of these goals (primarily carried
out by Dr. David Smithe, PI, and by Dr. Thomas Jenkins, Senior Research Scientist) is presented
in the Tech-X Statement of Work for the RF-SciDAC4 project, in which contributions in each of
the four main project thrusts (bolded) are outlined. These are:
Area 1. Nonlinear sheath boundary condition modeling efforts
- Implement parameterized nonlinear sheath boundary condition (SBC) in Vorpal
Area 2. Ponderomotive force modeling efforts
- Verify analytical and reduced models for ponderomotive force (joint effort with Lodestar
Research)
Area 3. RF + sputtering/impurity generation modeling efforts
- Develop a model for the angle and energy distribution (IAED) of ions entering the sheath
Area 4. RF + turbulence/transport modeling efforts
- Formulate and verify the data exchange methods needed for code couplings
- Explore the effects of static SOL turbulence profiles on RF wave propagation and scattering
(joint effort with LLNL-FES)

The work carried out in each of these areas will be summarized in this report.

2. What was accomplished under these goals?

Area 1. Nonlinear sheath boundary condition modeling efforts

Throughout the project, Tech-X participated in nearly weekly Zoom meetings focused on
benchmarking several new codes that use the sheath boundary condition of Myra et al. Tech-X
had instituted a rudimentary form of this boundary condition in the VSim (Vorpal) code several
years ago, and had performed complex geometry simulations on the ITER ICRH antenna. Since
that time, however, portions of the VSim infrastructure that were used to perform those simulations
have changed, specifically with regard to the accessibility of cut-cell data near metallic boundaries.
This older code was reworked to function with the current VSim architecture, and a more general
form of the Myra sheath boundary condition was implemented in VSim.

Exercising these new boundary conditions, Tech-X participated in a benchmarking activity led by
Dr. John Wright of MIT, “A Benchmarking Suite for Verification of Radio Frequency Sheath
Model Implementations”, in which a series of benchmark problems using the Myra boundary
condition explored a range of physical phenomena associated with the presence of a non-linear RF
sheath. These phenomena included both quasi-conducting and quasi-insulating sheaths, in both
one- and two-dimensional cases, and spanned a range of background magnetic field orientation

angles relative to the material surface (from perpendicular to oblique). Cases which give rise to
sheath plasma waves were also considered. Although VSim simulations were not carried out for
every case proposed in this benchmarking activity, Tech-X has demonstrated good agreement of
VSim both with other codes used in the benchmark suite (RFSol, Petra-M, Stix) and with
theoretical predictions for the physical behavior of the sheath in a number of cases. In Figure 1
below, we compare data from a particular sheath problem posed and modeled by Kohno1 with data
from a VSim model of the same case. Despite the highly nonlinear nature of the sheath/plasma
interaction in this case, VSim was able to reproduce Kohno’s results without difficulty.

Figure 1: (top) X-component of the electric field in the presence of a driving antenna (x=1.0 m) and a nonlinear sheath boundary
condition applied at a conducting wall (x=1.2 m), as in Figure 6 of the Kohno reference below. (bottom) VSim (Vorpal) successfully
reproduces the nonlinear sheath/plasma interaction of this case.

A publication summarizing the results of this benchmarking activity, led by Dr. John Wright, is
reportedly in preparation; Tech-X has already contributed figures, data, and text to this effort.

Together with this benchmarking activity, explicit kinetic particle-in-cell modeling was carried
out in VSim for a cross-code comparison of nonlinear sheath behaviors with fluid and hybrid
fluid/kinetic models. This effort was led by Dr. Jim Myra of Lodestar Research Corporation, who
also carried out theoretical predictions for the sheath behavior that agree well with those observed
by the different codes. This application of VSim differs from that used in the Kohno benchmark

Area 2. Ponderomotive force modeling efforts

The focus of this project task was an investigation of the ponderomotive forces that arise on
plasmas in front of antenna structures. This focus [in collaboration with Drs. Maxim Umansky,
Tom Rognlien, and Andris Dimits, from Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL)]
included simulations of canonical geometry layouts, the development of two-way code coupling
formalism between VSim and the UEDGE/BOUT++ codes, interpretation of the physics results
from the coupled codes, and improvements with regard to understanding the underlying theory of
the ponderomotive forces. Much of the work performed in this area overlaps strongly with the
broader RF + turbulence/transport modeling tasks that we will describe in Area 4 below; this
section will focus primarily on theory and simpler simulations.
In the project’s early years, Dr. Smithe visited LLNL once or twice a year, however, during the
later part of the project travel was interdicted due to the Covid-19 pandemic. Drs. Smithe, Jenkins,
and Umansky (and occasionally others) thus conducted the majority of their collaborative efforts
via a weekly conference call in lieu of the usual travel. In many regards, this more frequent
interaction was highly beneficial. Key developments were also periodically presented at RF-
SciDAC4 monthly team meetings. A slide deck from one such meeting has been included in
Appendix A of this report and provides useful context for the technical discussion in this section.
An important improvement to the understanding of ponderomotive forces arose from simulations
with a simple canonical geometry, namely a single strap, in a box recessed into a metal wall, with
no Faraday Shields, as in Slide 10 of Appendix A. In this case, we saw “negative” ponderomotive
force on the electrons, i.e., forces pulling electrons toward the antenna and the regions of stronger
field. Normally one thinks of ponderomotive forces as pushing particles away from regions of
stronger field, regardless of whether the species is electrons or ions.
At the same time this was occurring, the theoretical analysis of these forces was augmented to
separate out terms which were proportional to
 Density times Gradient{Jitter-Velocity-Squared}
from terms which are
 Jitter-Velocity-Squared times Gradient{Density};
see Slide 4 of Appendix A for the math. The former term is the traditional single-particle
ponderomotive force picture, but the latter can also occur in the proper fluid theory of the
ponderomotive force.
It was realized that there is a case where the Gradient{Density} term can be significant, but only
in the case of circularly polarized jitter-velocity, which is in fact, the case for ICRH and helicon
frequencies. In addition, part of this term carries a B-cross-jitter-velocity, and so reverses with
sign of the charge. Furthermore, in a diverted plasma, the pedestal density gradient can be quite
large, and this was also the case for our simulations. Thus, for electrons, we saw the “negative”
ponderomotive force in the pedestal region where the density gradient is high, which then reverses
at the top of the pedestal to become a more conventional outward pushing ponderomotive force in

the core region. For ions, the ponderomotive force always imparts an outward push, though the
density gradient effect does enhance this in the pedestal region.
This simultaneous analytical and modeling discovery is all the more remarkable in that the analysis
uses linear frequency-domain theory with complex amplitudes, and some very careful work with
the Conjugate{Vjitter}-times-Vjitter terms, while the modeling was done in time-domain with all
real numbers. This cross-check between such disparate techniques helps validate the time-domain
implementation in particular.
While it has long been known that a “negative” ponderomotive force was theoretically possible,
we now know the detail of how that happens, and have an example of such, in a quotidian
application that is common in the real world. This is an important discovery.
The next question we worked hard to address is whether the magnitude of the ponderomotive
forces is sufficient to matter, that is, in comparison to the background pressure. We used the model
simulations to get an estimate of the force-per-megawatt power, for Fast Wave ICRH, and also for
helicon frequencies, and then scaled this so that there would be a megawatt per square-meter of
power coming from our antenna box. Also, at about this time, we began to look at coupling to the
UEDGE/BOUT++ codes, in active use at LLNL, to explore the physics associated with the
modification of background plasma profiles induced by ponderomotive force.
In these slow-time codes, the Vorticity Equation is used. Some time was spent by Tech-X
researchers trying to understand the nature of the Vorticity Equation, which is a stand-in for
Poisson’s equation, for slow-time scale charge, current, and plasma potential behavior under the
quasi-neutrality condition. Thus, “Div-J” in the Appendix A slides refers to a vorticity source,
which derives from summation of the electron and ion ponderomotive forces. Tech-X provided
order-of-magnitude range for a vorticity source in UEDGE simulations, localized to the outboard
side where an antenna box would be. Slides 8-9 of the Appendix show the UEDGE simulations
results, across 3 orders of magnitude, and shows that starting around a vorticity source threshold
of about 1000 Amps/meter-cubed, there is distortion of the plasma potential.
In the Vorpal simulations, measurements of ponderomotive force (transformed into a vorticity
source) were made at several locations around the antenna box, and more importantly, distance
from the box, to sample up through the pedestal density. As expected, the forces are strongest
right at the box, and drop by an order of magnitude (~0.05) as we pass through the pedestal to the
core region. It was found that for just the Fast Wave, the level of the ponderomotive force was
likely too small to matter significantly. However, for a Fast-plus-Slow wave combination, it is
likely the forces will meet the criterion.
It was also noticed that the ponderomotive forces are significantly stronger as we go up in
frequency, so that for the helicon, they more easily surpass the threshold for disturbing the
equilibrium.
Considerable effort was put into trying to improve the understanding of the field patterns of the
ponderomotive forces and vorticity sources near the antenna box, as there was significant
oscillatory noise-like behavior occurring in the areas of most interest. For example, it is known

that sharp corners can create 2h-type noise (plus-minus in adjacent cells) in the time-domain
plasma algorithm. In addition, the computed vorticity source seemed to show considerably more
such noise than the ponderomotive forces from which it derived, likely due to cancellation of the
previously mentioned density-gradient term, which is opposite for the two species. This made it
difficult to estimate when and where the vorticity source was exceeding the threshold that modifies
the equilibrium, since the noise-like behavior could be larger than the threshold, introducing
uncertainty as to whether that was real or a non-physical computational effect. Ultimately, two
efforts helped to resolve the noise-like behavior issues. Firstly, the sharp square corners on the
antenna box were rounded. This noticeably reduced the level of noise-like behavior; however,
somewhat surprisingly, there remained considerable oscillatory behavior. So secondly, the 1D
spatial resolution of the simulation was nearly doubled. If this was 2h-wave noise, we would
expect to see halving of the noise-like behavior’s wavelength. This was not observed; instead, the
pattern remained, now resolved at 4 points per wavelength or better. Thus, our conclusion is that
this oscillatory behavior is physical, though we do not yet have a concise explanation of it, in
physical terms. However, if true, then it means that the magnitude of the ponderomotive and
vorticity sources are on the high side of our more cautious estimates. In particular, the helicon
vorticity source is quite significant.
We also experimented with alternate, more direct diagnostics, for quicker estimation of what
constituted appreciable ponderomotive force effects. One such diagnostic is a “Jitter Temperature”
diagnostic, which simply takes (1/2)-mass-times-jitter-velocity-squared, and converts that energy
to a temperature, which could then be comparted to the edge temperature at that same location. As
might be expected, for the Fast Wave ICRH case, jitter temperature right at the antenna box can
be on the order of 1 eV, but drops rapidly away from the antenna, see Slide 25 of Appendix A.
In addition to this basic simulation work, Dr. Smithe examined the ponderomotive force
analytically. Part of this effort led to the understanding (actually prediction) of how the “negative”
ponderomotive force for electrons was occurring, due to the density gradient. However, a similar
sign flip in part of the toroidal component of electron ponderomotive force remains unexplained
(as there is no density gradient in the toroidal direction). Additional work has helped to more
precisely pin down the nature of the force with regard to the equilibrium and perturbed
distributions and the traditional Reynolds Stress term. In the end, ponderomotive force is really
just part of the lab-frame-to-fluid-frame transformation of pressure, with the key difference being
the “jitter velocity” of that transformation. This additional theory work also helped to identify
what part of the pressure is due to the jitter velocity, and what part of the pressure is due to
perturbed density times the unperturbed temperature. Examples using “jitter distributions” have
been studied, and demonstrate that cold plasma waves tend to look like the traditional “jitter
distribution”, while the ponderomotive force looks more like the traditional Reynolds Stress.
Another example was considered that looks more like a warm plasma wave, such as a Bernstein
wave, and in this case, the traditional “jitter distribution” is inadequate; the result is more like the
traditional Reynolds Stress, but with a non-unity multiplicative coefficient. This calls attention to
a possible error in fluid codes, which often assume more of a traditional jitter behavior.

Area 3. RF + sputtering/impurity generation modeling efforts

This work focused on the development of an interface between Tech-X’s VSim code and the GITR
(Global Impurity TRansport) code. In this effort, Dr. Smithe collaborated with Dr. Atul Kumar of
Oak Ridge National Laboratory. GITR takes as inputs the background plasma profiles,
equilibrium, geometry, and surface models, and then uses these to perform large-scale simulation
of plasma induced erosion, plasma transport of those impurities, sputtering, and redeposition. The
physics implemented in GITR is based on the trace-impurity assumption, i.e., that the density of
generated impurities has a negligible effect on the background plasma parameters.

To provide input for Dr. Kumar’s GITR simulations, VSim computes (using plasma density and
magnetic field profiles, and antenna operating parameters) the local value of the sheath potential
on a material surface. The VSim representation of sheath potential is somewhat complex; sheath
potential is physically a sub-grid quantity, but to capture it on the VSim grid nodes we assign it to
be positioned at the plasma-adjacent nodes of edges that are cut by the material geometry. An
effective grid length that enables accurate sheath electric fields to be computed from the sheath
potential on this grid is also used in the calculation. On a triangular or quadrilateral ‘patch’
associated with VSim’s cut-cell geometry, local values of density, magnetic field strength and
orientation, and scalar sheath potential can be passed to GITR, together with geometric information
about the size and spatial orientation of this patch. GITR can then compute local erosion flux and
the associated sputtered impurity content, and it can also compute the subsequent transport
associated with the released impurities.

Unfortunately, the computational resources required for a single GITR run are high enough that
computing local erosion/emission fluxes for every cut-cell patch in the VSim geometry
(numbering in the thousands to tens of thousands for a representative ICRF antenna) is unfeasible.
Several ways to deal with this bottleneck have been developed – one, by only computing
erosion/emission on prominent antenna elements nearest to the core plasma/limiter (an approach
which Dr. Kumar has used with good success to model the WEST tokamak), and two, to develop
a triangle decimation algorithm that can be applied to the VSim data to reduce it to a more tenable
size. Dr. Smithe has developed such a decimation algorithm, which combines nearly-parallel
adjacent triangular surfaces (consistent with the smoothness of typical ICRF antenna geometries),
and has successfully provided a VSim dataset of feasible size to Dr. Kumar.

As part of the RF-SciDAC4 and RF-SciDAC5 efforts, Dr. Kumar has subsequently developed the
STRIPE (Simulated Transport of RF Impurity Production and Emission) framework, which
integrates multiple physics modules associated with RF sputtering and impurity generation:
SolEdge3x for scrape-off-layer plasma profiles, COMSOL (or VSim, using the tools developed in
this task) for 3D RF rectified sheath potentials, RustBCA for erosion yields and surface
interactions, and global impurity transport for 3D ion energy-angle distributions and impurity
transport. This work has thus enabled the use of VSim in a broader, whole-device-modeling-style
framework for the modeling of RF-induced impurity emission and transport.

Area 4. RF + turbulence/transport modeling efforts

Tech-X efforts for this task are strongly related to the work on ponderomotive forces discussed
under Area 2 above. However, the emphasis here is not on theory, but rather on the development
of a two-way code coupling between VSim and the UEDGE/BOUT++ codes. This work was done
in a collaboration between Tech-X (Drs. Jenkins and Smithe) and LLNL (Drs. Umansky and
Dimits). Under RF-SciDAC4, the coupling between VSim and UEDGE was the primary focus3.
In this coupling scenario, which is file-based, UEDGE computes an equilibrium solution to
edge/SOL transport equations, given the geometry, sources, and relevant forces. The density
profile of the equilibrium is exported in a form readable by VSim. VSim then propagates RF
waves through this geometry/profile configuration; it also computes the amplitude of the time-
averaged ponderomotive force, and exports this force data to a format readable by UEDGE. With
this data, UEDGE can then compute adjustments to the original equilibrium that arise from these
forces, and a modified density profile is generated and sent to VSim. This iterative process was
initially envisioned to continue until convergence is achieved. However, we found it difficult to
obtain steady-state solutions to the UEDGE transport equations that were consistent with the
calculated ponderomotive forces, for reasons that will be explained below.
The coupled UEDGE/VSim simulations were carried out in a 2D slab, which physically represents
the edge/SOL of a tokamak plasma that has been ‘unwrapped’, as shown in Figure 3. As is often
the case, translating between UEDGE’s unusual combined domain of core, SOL, and private flux
regions and the more traditional configuration space domain used in the time-domain code, was a
somewhat tedious task. Nevertheless, it was achieved, in the manner shown in the Figure.
Toroidal and curvature effects are thus neglected in these simulations (and the degree to which
they contribute to ponderomotive forces is a topic of interest for future research). RF waves are
emitted from the antenna aperture (shown in green). Although the simulations did not require
excessive computational resources, they were nevertheless time-consuming. The UEDGE
simulations, which were required to run in serial, take about 10 wallclock hours to complete, using
grids of approximately 2000 cells. The VSim simulations run in parallel, using 32 cores for
approximately 48 hours (~1500 core hours in total). A 3-4 step UEDGE/VSim iterative sequence
can thus take up to two weeks of runtime.

In carrying out the coupled UEDGE/VSim simulations, Drs. Jenkins (Tech-X) and Umansky
(LLNL) observed that convergence to a static plasma equilibrium was not always attainable. In
their simulations, cases where plasma density near the antenna aperture was high enough to be
above the lower-hybrid resonance (S=0) revealed that the effect of the ponderomotive force was
to slightly elevate the density in front of the aperture. In this case, convergence to a static
equilibrium can be obtained. However, when the plasma density near the antenna aperture was
below the S=0 resonance, the computed ponderomotive force was shown to significantly lower the
density, hollowing out the profile. Subsequent UEDGE/VSim iterations yielded further decreases
in the density without attaining a steady-state profile, suggestive of an instability rather than a
physical steady-state; we speculate that the relevant physical process these simulations are trying
to match is the expulsion of density in its entirety from a region immediately in front of the antenna.
(This outcome cannot be attained in the simulations as a physically meaningful steady-state, due
to the 2D slab nature of the simulations. Nevertheless, the physical trend is unambiguously clear.)
Thus, we have fascinating bifurcative behavior, wherein if the density is below the S=0 density
threshold, ponderomotive force tends to further reduce the density in front of the antenna aperture,
while if the density is above the S=0 threshold, ponderomotive force tends to further increase the
density in this region. This physics strongly validates the concept of neutral gas puffing envisioned
for the ITER ICRF antenna, in which good RF/plasma coupling is attained by maintaining a
sufficiently high plasma density (via gas injection and subsequent ionization) near the antenna
aperture. These UEDGE/VSim studies of the ponderomotive force have revealed that propping
up the density in this manner is likely to yield more stable and predictable behavior in the plasma
edge.

Dr. Jenkins was invited to present the results of these studies in two invited conference talks in
2022, one at the Sherwood Fusion Theory Conference held in Santa Rosa, California4, and the
other at the RF-Topical Conference held in Annapolis, Maryland5. The latter talk additionally
discussed the relevance of these results to the NSTX-U experiment and has led to a publication6.
The relevance and applicability of these results to ongoing NSTX-U research also led to Dr.
Jenkins’ subsequent inclusion as a coauthor on an overview paper7 discussing open physics
questions in the NSTX-U experiment.
Supplement

For the project Supplement, considerable work has been done to rework the linear plasma update
(originally developed by Dr. Smithe8) to be efficient on GPUs, and also to remedy the
susceptibility of the algorithm to checkerboarding. To this end, a version of the update called the
“Spider Update” or triad update, was developed and implemented in VSim. This update reduced
the potential checkerboarding operation to cell octants (where 8 triad combinations of the three
field components are taken together), however, the octants exist only momentarily on the gpu
scale, and are filtered prior to storing in memory at the cell scale. This approach has shown
promise in alleviating the need for filtering at the cell level, thus using the speed of the GPU to
resolve a long-standing undesirable aspect of the computation.

Significant code development was carried out by a Tech-X intern, Jason Lin, who implemented
nearly a dozen GPU-based updaters for field manipulations, including Maxwell Equation updates
and cold-plasma updates. Jason also performed some of the first input-file based simulations with
these new GPU capabilities, utilizing the complete workflow sequence from input file to
visualization.

As well, this Supplement funding was used to support the development of multi-device code which
allows for arbitrary CPU/GPU configuration in VSim runs, with automatic decomposition based
on the timing ratios of available devices. The developed code is limited in that it models only
scalar and vector fields on the GPU (i.e., it does not model GPU particles); however, work to
generalize its capabilities has continued subsequently under separate funding.

Extension

Research efforts during the project extension focused on two topics – previous plasma-material
interaction project efforts were re-examined in the context of their relevance to high-field devices,
and initial studies of impurity transport and convective cell behavior in the plasma SOL/edge were
carried out.

Tech-X again collaborated with LLNL scientists (Drs. Maxim Umansky and Benjamin Dudson),
together with Dr. Jim Myra of Lodestar Research, to carry out initial studies of convective cell
behavior and impurity transport. A suitable geometry for these studies was identified, consisting
of a 3D flux-tube model with an RF antenna and limiter plates. It was verified that this model
could be run both in VSim and in Hermes (a turbulence/transport code developed under the
BOUT++ framework). Unlike the ponderomotive force simulations described above (in which
equilibrium profiles computed by LLNL codes are imported by VSim, and forces computed by
VSim are used to modify these equilibrium profiles), the initial VSim/Hermes model developed
was neither coupled nor wholly self-consistent. Nevertheless, VSim runs were used to compute
approximate values for the RF sheath potential on plasma-facing limiter surfaces, and comparable
RF potentials, imposed on the limiter surface during the Hermes run, were decidedly shown to
influence both the turbulent spectrum of the edge plasma and the transport of emitted impurities.
As earlier alluded to, this work has continued under the RF-SciDAC5 effort, and has led to at least
one publication in the Plasma Physics and Controlled Fusion journal (first author Dr. Umansky).
Since the bulk of the efforts that led to that paper were carried out under separate funding, it will
not be listed as an RF-SciDAC4 product; we mention it here only to note that the initial efforts
described here have later proved fruitful.

For the high-field device work, a notable outcome has been achieved - VSim capabilities
developed during the RF-SciDAC have been applied to model sheath potential formation and
impurity production in the SPARC high-field tokamak. Here we feel it important to clarify that to

V. IMPACT
1. Impact on principal discipline
The primary disciplines for this project are RF Power in Magnetically Confined Fusion Plasmas,
Antenna and SOL and Edge Plasma Interactions, and Whole Device Modeling.

The research proposed will for the first time make it possible to explore the self-consistent
interaction of RF power with the short mean free path scrape-off layer, including the effects of
plasma sheaths, ponderomotive forces near an antenna, and turbulence and transport. The new
simulation capabilities will make it possible to answer critical questions relating to how RF power
modifies properties of the SOL, and how, in turn, the SOL affects the propagation and absorption
of RF waves. Targeted problems include the impact of high-power RF systems on plasma-facing
materials, including high-Z impurity sputtering and transport induced by large RF-induced sheath
potentials, localized thermal loads, and antenna damage. The high geometric fidelity required to
describe the 3D magnetic field and 3D solid geometry of the RF launching structures and the
surrounding vacuum vessel also require leadership class computing.

2. Impact on other disciplines
Generally speaking, fusion plasma edge conditions overlap colder denser plasma regimes used in
many plasma processing and industrial applications, including semi-conductor fab. Progress in
plasma edge modeling made in this project can likely carry over to analogous progress in plasma
processing modeling applications.

this project provides validation of the software for general use.
