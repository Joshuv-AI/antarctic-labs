Special N-extremal solutions to indeterminate moment problems
Christian Berg, Ryszard Szwarc

Abstract
For an N-extremal solution μ \mu to an indeterminate moment problem it is known by a theorem of M. Riesz that the measure ( 1 + x 2 ) − 1 ​ d ​ μ ​ ( x ) (1+x^{2})^{-1}d\mu(x) is determinate. For 0 < α < 1 0<\alpha<1 we show by contradiction that there exist indeterminate N-extremal solutions μ \mu such that ( 1 + x 2 ) − α ​ d ​ μ ​ ( x ) (1+x^{2})^{-\alpha}d\mu(x) is determinate, and there exist also indeterminate N-extremal solutions μ \mu such that ( 1 + x 2 ) − α ​ d ​ μ ​ ( x ) (1+x^{2})^{-\alpha}d\mu(x) is indeterminate. Explicit examples of such measures are so far only known when α = 1 / 2 \alpha=1/2 . For indeterminate Stieltjes moment problems and for N-extremal solutions μ \mu , we show that ( 1 + x 2 ) − 1 / 2 d μ ( x ) (1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu(x) is indeterminate except when μ = μ F \mu=\mu_{F} is the Friedrichs solution in case of which ( 1 + x 2 ) − 1 / 2 d μ F ( x ) (1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu_{F}(x) is determinate. We identify the Friedrichs and Krein solutions for some indeterminate Stieltjes moment problems.

Introduction and main results

Let
\ell^{2}
denote the Hilbert space of square summable complex sequences
c=(c_{n})_{n\geq 0}
. The standard orthonormal basis of
\ell^{2}
is denoted
e_{n},n=0,1,\ldots
. Furthermore,

\mathcal{F}:=\spn\{e_{n}\mid n\geq 0\}

(1)

is the dense subspace of sequences with only finitely many non-zero terms.

Let
\mathcal{M}^{*}
denote the set of positive measures on
\mathbb{R}
with infinite support and moments of any order. For
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
the corresponding moment sequence
s=(s_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is defined by

s_{n}=\int x^{n}\,d\mu(x),\quad n\geq 0.

(2)

A sequence
s=(s_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of real numbers is a moment sequence of the form (
2
) if and only if
s
is positive definite in the sense that

\sum_{j,k=0}^{\infty}s_{j+k}c_{j}\overline{c_{k}}>0\;\mbox{for all}\;c\in\mathcal{F},c\neq 0.

This is the famous theorem of Hamburger, see
[
19
]
.

A measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
and the corresponding moment sequence
s
is called
determinate
if there is only one measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
such that (
2
)
holds, and
indeterminate
if there are more than one such measure. In the indeterminate case the set
V=V(s)
of measures
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
satisfying (
2
) is an infinite convex set.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{d}^{*}
resp.
\mathcal{M}_{i}^{*}
denote the sets of determinate resp. indeterminate measures in
\mathcal{M}^{*}
.

The following elementary observations are used throughout the paper, and
\mathbb{C}[x]
denotes the set of polynomials in
x
with complex coefficients:

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
satisfy
\mu\leq\nu
.

(i)

\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{d}^{*}\implies\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{d}^{*}
.

(ii)

\mathbb{C}[x]\,\mbox{dense in}\;L^{2}(\nu)\implies\mathbb{C}[x]\,\mbox{dense in}\;L^{2}(\mu)
.

For
a\in\mathbb{R}
let
\kappa_{a}
denote the translation of
\mathbb{R}
given by
\kappa_{a}(x)=x+a
.

Lemma 1.2
.

For
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
and an arbitrary translation
\kappa_{a}
we have

(i)

\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{d}^{*}\;(\mbox{resp.}\;\mathcal{M}_{i}^{*})\implies\kappa_{a}(\mu)\in\mathcal{M}_{d}^{*}\;(\mbox{resp.}\;\mathcal{M}_{i}^{*})
.

(ii)

\mathbb{C}[x]\,\mbox{dense in}\;L^{2}(\mu)\implies\mathbb{C}[x]\,\mbox{dense in}\;L^{2}(\kappa_{a}(\mu))
.

Concerning the proof of (ii) we use that the set of continuous functions with compact support
C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
is dense in
L^{2}(\mu)
for any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
and that
C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathbb{C}[x]
are invariant under translations.

To
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
there is a corresponding sequence
(p_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of orthonormal polynomials satisfying

\int p_{n}(x)p_{m}(x)\,d\mu(x)=\delta_{n,m},

(3)

uniquely determined by (
3
), if
p_{n}
is required to be a polynomial of degree
n
with positive leading coefficient. Clearly
p_{0}=s_{0}^{-1/2}
. In the indeterminate case the polynomials
p_{n}
are independent of
\mu\in V
and they satisfy the three term recurrence relation

xp_{n}(x)=a_{n}p_{n+1}(x)+b_{n}p_{n}(x)+a_{n-1}p_{n-1}(x),n\geq 0,\quad a_{-1}:=0,

(4)

where

a_{n}=\int xp_{n}(x)p_{n+1}(x)\,d\mu(x)>0,\quad b_{n}=\int xp_{n}(x)^{2}\,d\mu(x)\in\mathbb{R},\quad n\geq 0.

The corresponding Jacobi matrix is the tridiagonal matrix of the form

J=\begin{pmatrix}b_{0}&a_{0}&0&\ldots\\
a_{0}&b_{1}&a_{1}&\ldots\\
0&a_{1}&b_{2}&\ldots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

(5)

It is easy to see that the proportional measures
\lambda\mu,\lambda>0
lead to the same Jacobi matrix
J
, and the well-known Theorem of Favard (see
[
19
, Theorem 5.14]
) states that any matrix of the form (
5
) with
a_{n}>0,b_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
comes from a unique moment sequence
(s_{n})
as above, normalized such that
s_{0}=1
. In the following we shall always assume that this normalization holds, and consequently the solutions
\mu
of (
2
) are probability measures and
p_{0}=1
.

For later use we need the polynomials
(q_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of the second kind defined by

q_{n}(z)=\int\frac{p_{n}(z)-p_{n}(x)}{z-x}\,d\mu(x),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},\mu\in V.

(6)

The Jacobi matrix acts as a symmetric operator in the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}
with domain
\mathcal{F}
given in (
1
), and the action is multiplication of the matrix
J
by
c\in\mathcal{F}
considered as a column, i.e.,

(Jc)_{n}:=a_{n-1}c_{n-1}+b_{n}c_{n}+a_{n}c_{n+1},\quad n\geq 0.

(7)

The Jacobi operator associated with
J
is by definition the closure
(T,D(T))
of the symmetric operator
(J,\mathcal{F})
.

It is a classical fact that the closed symmetric operator
(T,D(T))
has deficiency indices either
(0,0)
or
(1,1)
. These cases occur precisely if the moment sequence (
2
) is determinate or indeterminate. See
[
1
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
19
]
for details. Recent results about indeterminate moment problems can be found in

[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
.

The domain
D(T)
of the Jacobi operator has been studied in
[
8
]
and
[
9
]
.

In the indeterminate case the set
V
of solutions to (
2

[
19
, Section 7.4]

\int\frac{d\mu_{\varphi}(x)}{x-z}=-\frac{A(z)+\varphi(z)C(z)}{B(z)+\varphi(z)D(z)},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R},

(8)

where

is a Pick function or

. When

is a real constant

or

of the Jacobi operator

(T,D(T))

are entire functions defined in terms of the polynomials

The set of indeterminate and N-extremal measures is denoted
\mathcal{M}_{i,0}^{*}
.
We have chosen this symbol because the measures in
\mathcal{M}_{i,0}^{*}
are called
of order 0 in
[

.

Marcel Riesz proved the following theorem in 1923, see
[
17
]
:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
. The polynomials are dense in
L^{2}(\mu)
if and only if the measure
(1+x^{2})^{-1}d\mu(x)
is determinate and if and only if
\mu
is either determinate or indeterminate and N-extremal.

We summarize some of the properties of the N-extremal measures
\mu_{t}
, which can be found in
[
1
]
and
[
19
]
.

Proposition 1.4
.

(i) The solution
\mu_{t}
is a discrete measure with support equal to the countable zero set
\Lambda_{t}
of the entire function
B(z)+tD(z)
, with the convention that
\Lambda_{\infty}
is the zero set of
D
. We have
\Lambda_{t}\subset\mathbb{R}
for
t\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
.

(ii) The support of two different N-extremal solutions are disjoint and interlacing. Each point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
belongs to the support of a unique N-extremal measure
\mu_{t}
, where

t\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
is given as
t=-B(x_{0})/D(x_{0})
if

and

if
D(x_{0})=0
.

(iii) If
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
belongs to the support of the N-extremal measure
\mu_{t}
, then the measure
\mu_{t}-\mu_{t}(x_{0})\delta_{x_{0}}
is determinate.

Putting
z=0
in (
8
), which is possible when

\int\frac{d\mu_{t}(x)}{x}=t,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(9)

We fix a number

in two disjoint subsets:

\displaystyle A(\alpha)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{i}^{*}\mid(1+x^{2})^{-\alpha}d\mu(x)\in\mathcal{M}_{i}^{*}\},

(10)

\displaystyle B(\alpha)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{i}^{*}\mid(1+x^{2})^{-\alpha}d\mu(x)\in\mathcal{M}_{d}^{*}\}.

(11)

We have

A(0)=\mathcal{M}_{i}^{*},\;B(0)=\emptyset,\;A(1)=\mathcal{M}_{i}^{*}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{i,0}^{*},\;B(1)=\mathcal{M}_{i,0}^{*}.

By Lemma

(12)

Furthermore, for

the following defining equation for

(13)

There is no apparent relation between the moments of
\mu
and

Proposition 1.5
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{i,0}^{*}
.

(a) Assume
a(\mu)<1
. For
0<\delta<1-a(\mu)
we have

a((1+x^{2})^{\delta}d\mu(x))=a(\mu)+\delta.

(14)

(b) Assume

. For

a((1+x^{2})^{-\delta}d\mu(x))=a(\mu)-\delta.

(15)

Proof.

(a)
: Note that
(1+x^{2})^{\delta}d\mu(x)\in\mathcal{M}_{i,0}^{*}
. It is indeterminate because it majorizes
\mu
. Choosing
\varepsilon>0
so small that

we know that
(1+x^{2})^{-(a(\mu)+\varepsilon)}d\mu(x)
is determinate, and then

is dense in

It is now easy to establish (
14

(b)
: Note that
(1+x^{2})^{-\delta}d\mu(x)\in\mathcal{M}_{i,0}^{*}
and the proof is analogous to the proof of (a).
∎

3
The Stieltjes case

Let
s
be a normalized indeterminate moment sequence. Introducing

\xi(\mu):=\inf\supp(\mu),\quad\mu\in V,

(18)

then
\xi(\mu)\in[-\infty,\infty)
. We say that
s
is a Stieltjes moment sequence
if there exists a measure
\mu\in V
with
\supp(\mu)\subseteq[0,\infty[
, so
s
is Stieltjes iff
\xi(\mu)\geq 0
for at least one
\mu\in V
.

In the following we assume
s
to be a Stieltjes moment sequence.

The indeterminate moment sequence
s
can be determinate as a Stieltjes moment sequence (det(S) in short), if there is only one measure
\mu\in V
supported by
[0,\infty)
, and indeterminate as a Stieltjes moment sequence (indet(S) in short), if there are more than one measure
\mu\in V
supported by
[0,\infty)
.

In the det(S) case the N-extremal measure
\mu_{\infty}
is the unique solution supported by
[0,\infty)
.

As explained in
[
11
]
and repeated in
[
2
]
(and going back to Chihara with another terminology and proof), there is a quantity
\alpha(s)\leq 0
defined by

\alpha(s):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{p_{n}(0)}{q_{n}(0)}=\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{D(x)}{B(x)}.

(19)

The problem is det(S) iff
\alpha(s)=0
and indet(S) iff

\alpha(s)<0
.

Since the Nevanlinna parametrization used here is related to the Nevanlinna parametrization used in
[
1
]
by replacing
t
by
-1/t
we define

F(s):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-1/\alpha(s),&\alpha(s)<0,\\
\infty,&\alpha(s)=0.\end{array}\right.

(20)

We then get that the indeterminate N-extremal solutions
\mu_{t}
given in (
8
) are supported on
[0,\infty[
iff

F(s)\leq t\leq\infty
. For a proof see
[
2
]
and references therein.

In the indet(S) case the solution
\mu_{\infty}
is called the Krein solution and we also denote it
\mu_{K}
. It is concentrated in the zeros
0=d_{1}<d_{2}<\cdots
of
D
. The measure
\mu_{F}=\mu_{F(s)}
is called the Friedrichs solution, because as proved in
[
16
]
, it corresponds to the Friedrichs extension of the Jacobi matrix. See details in
[
2
]
with further references. The following is proved there:

Proposition 3.1
.

(i)
:
\mu_{F}
is concentrated in the sequence
\xi_{1}<\xi_{2}<\cdots
defined by

\xi_{k}=\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n,k},\quad k=1,2,\ldots,

(21)

where
x_{n,k},k=1,2,\ldots,n
denote
the
n
zeros in increasing order of the polynomial

.

(ii)
:
\xi_{1}=\xi(\mu_{F(s)})>0
and for all solutions
\mu\in V
to the indeterminate Hamburger problem we have
\xi(\mu)<\xi_{1}
unless

.

(iii)
: When

increases from

to
\infty
, then

decreases from
\xi_{1}
to

.

(iv)
: For

then

has one negative mass-point equal to

, and when

to

decreases from

to

.

In the det(S) case we may say that the Krein and Friedrichs solutions coincide with the N-extremal solution

xd[\sigma_{K}-\sigma_{K}(0)\delta_{0}](x)=xd\sigma_{K}(x).

This shows that
xd\sigma_{K}(x)
is an indeterminate N-extremal solution to the moment sequence
s
, and we have
\eta_{2}=\xi(xd\sigma_{K}(x))>\xi_{1}=\xi(\mu_{F(s)})
,
which contradicts that the Friedrichs solution has the greatest lower bound among the solutions, see Proposition
3.1
(ii).

(b)
: Since
x^{-1}d\mu_{t}(x)\leq\xi(\mu_{t})^{-1}d\mu_{t}(x)
, we see that the polynomials are dense in
L^{2}(x^{-1}d\mu_{t}(x))
, so
x^{-1}d\mu_{t}(x)
is an N-extremal solution for
(
22
).

The measure
(1+x^{2})^{-1}d\mu_{t}(x)
is determinate by Theorem
1.3
and so is
x^{-2}d\mu_{t}(x)
, since
(1+x^{2})/x^{2}
is bounded above on
\supp(\mu_{t})
. By part (a) it follows that
x^{-1}d\mu_{t}(x)
is the Friedrichs solution for (
22
).

We finally need to show that
\tau_{t}
is N-extremal. This follows from Lemma 2 p. 113 in
[
3
]
. For the benefit of the reader we give a self-contained proof. Assume for contradiction that
\tau_{t}
is not N-extremal for (
22
). Then there exists an N-extremal solution
\kappa
with
\kappa(0)>t-F(s)
by
[
19
, Theorem 7.15]
. If we subtract the measure
(t-F(s))\delta_{0}
from
\kappa
and
\tau_{t}
we get two measures
\kappa-(t-F(s))\delta_{0}

and
x^{-1}d\mu_{F(s)}(x)
with the same moments and they are equal because the latter is determinate. However, the two measures cannot be equal as the first has mass at 0 and the second not.

(c)
: The given measure has the moments (
22
), but since the mass at zero is less than
t-F(s)
it cannot be N-extremal.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

The authors of
[
10
]
introduced a density index for a measure
\sigma
on
[0,\infty)
with moments of all orders and for which the polynomials are dense in
L^{2}(\sigma)
, namely

\delta(\sigma):=\sup\{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\mid\mathbb{C}[x]\;\mbox{dense in}\,L^{2}(x^{k}d\sigma(x))\}.

(23)

It was proved that for the N-extremal solutions
\mu_{t},F(s)\leq t\leq\infty
supported by
[0,\infty)
one has

\delta(\mu_{F(s)})=1,\;\delta(\mu_{\infty})=2,\;\delta(\mu_{t})=0\;\mbox{for}\;F(s)<t<\infty.

(24)

Theorem
3.3
(a) can be deduced from these results about the density index in the following way:

From
\delta(\mu_{t})=0
we know that
\mathbb{C}[x]
is not dense in
L^{2}(xd\mu_{t}(x))
, but then

x/(1+x^{2})d\mu_{t}(x)
is indeterminate as well as
(1/x)d\mu_{t}(x)
because
x^{2}/(1+x^{2})
is bounded above and below on
\supp(\mu_{t})\subset[\xi(\mu_{t}),\infty)
.

From
\delta(\mu_{F(s)})=1
we get that
x/(1+x^{2})d\mu_{F(s)}(x)
is determinate and so is
(1/x)d\mu_{F(s)}(x)
.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
s
be a normalized Stieltjes moment sequence which is
indet(S). Then the measures
xd\mu_{t}(x),F(s)\leq t\leq\infty
are solutions to the shifted moment sequence
(s_{n+1})_{n\geq 0}
.

The measure
xd\mu_{F(s)}(x)
is an N-extremal solution,
xd\mu_{\infty}(x)=xd\mu_{K}(x)
is the Friedrichs solution. For
F(s)<t<\infty
the measures
xd\mu_{t}(x)
are non N-extremal solutions.

Proof.

From (
24
) we get

\delta(xd\mu_{F(s)}(x))=0,\;\delta(xd\mu_{\infty}(x))=1,

while the polynomials are not dense in
L^{2}(xd\mu_{t}(x))
when
F(s)<t<\infty
.
∎

From the Stieltjes moment sequence
s
of Theorem
3.3
we derive a family of Stieltjes moment sequences and determine their Friedrichs solutions.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
s
be a normalized Stieltjes moment sequence which is indet(S). For
t\in[F(s),\infty)
let
c_{t}=\xi(\mu_{t})
denote the smallest mass point in the support of
\mu_{t}
.

The sequence
\tilde{s}(t)
given by

\tilde{s}(t)_{n}=s_{n+1}-c_{t}s_{n},\quad n\geq 0

(25)

is a Stieltjes moment sequence which is determinate when
t=F(s)
and indet(S) when
t>F(s)
.

(a) For
t=F(s)
the unique solution to the moments (
25
) is

(x-\xi_{1})d\mu_{F(s)}(x),

(26)

where
\xi_{1}=\xi(\mu_{F(s)})
, cf. (
21
).

(b) For
F(s)<t<\infty
and
t^{\prime}\in[F(s),t]
the measures

\nu_{t^{\prime}}:=(x-c_{t})d\mu_{t^{\prime}}(x)

(27)

are solutions to the moments (
25
) and

\nu_{t}=(x-c_{t})d\mu_{t}(x)
is the Friedrichs solution. The measure
\nu_{F(s)}
is N-extremal while the measures
\nu_{t^{\prime}}
are non N-extremal when
F(s)<t^{\prime}<t
.

Proof.

Assume first
t=F(s)
. The measure (
26
) is positive and has the moments (
25
).

By Corollary
3.5
the measure
xd\mu_{F(s)}(x)
is indeterminate and N-extremal. By Proposition
1.4
(iii) the measure

xd\mu_{F(s)}(x)-\xi_{1}\mu_{F(s)}(\xi_{1})\delta_{\xi_{1}}

is determinate. We have

(x-\xi_{1})d\mu_{F(s)}(x)\leq xd\mu_{F(s)}(x)
. As the measure on the left vanishes at
\xi_{1}

we get

(x-\xi_{1})d\mu_{F(s)}(x)\leq xd\mu_{F(s)}(x)-\xi_{1}\mu_{F(s)}(\xi_{1})\delta_{\xi_{1}},

and therefore
(x-\xi_{1})d\mu_{F(s)}(x)
is determinate.

Assume next
F(s)<t
and
F(s)\leq t^{\prime}\leq t
.

Since
c_{t^{\prime}}\geq c_{t}
the measures

\nu_{t^{\prime}}
are positive with the moment sequence (
25
), showing that it is a Stieltjes moment sequence which is indet(S).

The measure
\mu_{t}-\mu_{t}(c_{t})\delta_{c_{t}}
is determinate by Proposition
1.4
(iii), and since

x^{-1}d\nu_{t}(x)=x^{-1}(x-c_{t})d\mu_{t}(x)=x^{-1}(x-c_{t})(d\mu_{t}(x)-\mu_{t}(c_{t})\delta_{c_{t}})\leq\mu_{t}-\mu_{t}(c_{t})\delta_{c_{t}},

we see that the measure
x^{-1}d\nu_{t}(x)
is determinate. By Theorem
3.3
(a) we get that
\nu_{t}
is the Friedrichs solution to the moments (
25
).

The properties of the measures
\nu_{t^{\prime}}
for
F(s)\leq t^{\prime}<t
are seen as in
Corollary
3.5
.
∎

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
s
be a normalized indeterminate moment sequence which is Stieltjes.

(a) If the problem is indet(S), then the measure
(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu_{F(s)}(x)
is determinate while
the measures
(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu_{t}(x)
are indet(S) and N-extremal for
F(s)<t<\infty
.

The measure
(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu_{K}(x)
is indeterminate, N-extremal and det(S).

(b) If the problem is det(S) and hence
F(s)=\infty
with the only Stieltjes solution
\mu=\mu_{F(s)}
, then
(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu(x)
is determinate.

(c) The measures
(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu_{t}(x)
are indeterminate and N-extremal for
-\infty<t<F(s)
.

In particular
\mu_{F}\in B(\frac{1}{2})
, hence
a(\mu_{F})\leq 1/2
, while
\mu_{t}\in A(\frac{1}{2})
, hence
a(\mu_{t})\geq 1/2
for
t\in\mathbb{R}^{*}\setminus\{F(s)\}
.

Proof.

(a)
: Assume first that the problem is indet(S). The function
h(x)=\sqrt{1+x^{2}}/x
is decreasing on the interval
(0,\infty)
and hence for
c>0

1<h(x)\leq h(c),\quad c\leq x<\infty.

For
F(s)\leq t<\infty
we have using (
18
)

(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu_{t}(x)\leq x^{-1}d\mu_{t}(x)\leq h(\xi(\mu_{t}))(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu_{t}(x),

and it follows from Theorem
3.3
that the measures
(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu_{t}(x)
are indet(S) and N-extremal for
F(s)<t<\infty
and determinate for
t=F(s)
.

Let
\kappa_{1}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the translation
\kappa_{1}(x)=x+1
. The image measures

\kappa_{1}(\mu_{t})
are supported by
[1,\infty)
, when
F(s)\leq t\leq\infty
with moments

\tilde{s}_{n}=\int(x+1)^{n}\,d\mu_{t}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}s_{k},

and
\tilde{s}
is indet(S) with Friedrichs solution
\kappa_{1}(\mu_{F(s)})
, but
\kappa_{1}(\mu_{K})
is not the Krein solution, since 0 is not in the support. Therefore

y^{-1}d\kappa_{1}(\mu_{K})(y)
is indet(S) and

\kappa_{-1}\left(y^{-1}d\kappa_{1}(\mu_{K})(y)\right)=(1+x)^{-1}d\mu_{K}(x)

(28)

is indeterminate. However

(1+x)^{-1}d\mu_{K}(x)\leq(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu_{K}(x),

so also
(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu_{K}(x)
is indeterminate.

We claim that it is also det(S) and hence N-extremal. Indeed, assume for contradiction that it is indet(S) and let
\sigma_{F}
denote the corresponding Friedrichs solution. The measures
xd\sigma_{F}(x)
and
x(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu_{K}(x)
have the same moments, and they are different since their supports are disjoint. However, we have

x(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu_{K}(x)=x(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d[\mu_{K}-\mu_{K}(0)\delta_{0}](x)\leq\mu_{K}-\mu_{K}(0)\delta_{0},

and since the last measure is determinate by (iii) in Proposition
1.4
,
so is also
x(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu_{K}(x)
, which is a contradiction.

(b)
: Let us next assume that
s
is det(S) and let
\mu
be the only Stieltjes solution. In this case
\mu
is N-extremal concentrated in the zeros of
D
. Since
\xi(\nu)<0
for all solutions
\nu\in V\setminus\{\mu\}
, the measure
\kappa_{1}(\mu)
is indet(S), and it must be the Friedrichs solution for the moments
\tilde{s}
. By Theorem
3.3

y^{-1}d\kappa_{1}(\mu)(y)
is determinate and so is the translate under
\kappa_{-1}
, which by (
28
) equals

(1+x)^{-1}d\mu(x)
. As

(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}\leq\sqrt{2}(1+x)^{-1},\quad x\geq 0,

we get that
(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu(x)
is determinate.

(c)
: Consider the N-extremal measure
\mu_{t}
for
-\infty<t<F(s)
so
\xi(\mu_{t})<0
. Put
a=-\xi(\mu_{t})
. Then the translated measure
\kappa_{a}(\mu_{t})
is supported by
[0,\infty)
with mass at 0. It is the Krein solution of an indeterminate Stieltjes moment problem, and therefore
(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\kappa_{a}(\mu_{t})(x)
is indeterminate, N-extremal and det(S) by part
(a)
. By Lemma
1.2

\kappa_{-a}\left((1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\kappa_{a}(\mu_{t})(x)\right)=(1+(x+a)^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu_{t}(x)

is indeterminate and N-extremal and so is
(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu_{t}(x)
.
∎

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
s
be a normalized indeterminate moment sequence which is Stieltjes and let
\mu\in V
be a solution supported on
[0,\infty)
. Assume that the measure
(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu(x)
is indeterminate. Then it is the Friedrichs solution of the corresponding moment sequence if and only if
\mu
is N-extremal.

Proof.

Assume first that
(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu(x)
is the Friedrichs solution of the corresponding moment problem assumed to be indeterminate. By Theorem
3.8
the measure
(1+x^{2})^{-1}d\mu(x)
is determinate and therefore
\mu
is N-extremal by Theorem
1.3
.

Assume next that
\mu
is N-extremal. Then
c:=\xi(\mu)
is the smallest mass-point of
\mu
. By assumption
(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu(x)
is indeterminate, and it is also N-extremal since it is majorized by
\mu
.
Assume for contradiction that
(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu(x)
is not the Friedrichs solution. Thus there is an N-extremal measure
\sigma
with
\xi(\sigma)>c
, and the moments
of
(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu(x)
and
\sigma
coincide. Therefore the moments of

(x-c)(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu(x)
and
(x-c)d\sigma(x)
coincide. However,

(x-c)(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu(x)=(x-c)(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d[\mu-\mu(c)\delta_{c}](x)\leq\mu-\mu(c)\delta_{c},

and we conclude that
(x-c)(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu(x)
is determinate. We get a contradiction as the supports of that measure and
(x-c)d\sigma(x)
are disjoint.
∎

If we apply Theorem
3.9
to
\mu_{t},F(s)<t<\infty
from Theorem
3.8
we get:

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
s
be a normalized indeterminate Stieltjes moment sequence which is indet(S). For
F(s)<t<\infty
the measure
(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu_{t}(x)
is the Friedrichs solution of the corresponding moment problem.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
s
be a normalized indeterminate moment sequence which is Stieltjes.
Let
f
be a bounded non-negative Borel function on
[0,\infty)
such that the measure
f(x)d\mu_{F(s)}(x)
is indeterminate. Then it is the Friedrichs solution of the corresponding moment problem.

Proof.

Let
C=\sup_{x\geq 0}f(x)
. By Theorem
3.8
the measure
(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu_{F(s)}(x)
is determinate. So is
(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}f(x)d\mu_{F(s)}(x)
as

(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}f(x)d\mu_{F(s)}(x)\leq C(1+x^{2})^{-1/2}d\mu_{F(s)}(x).

The measure
(1+x^{2})^{1/2}f(x)d\mu_{F(s)}(x)
is larger than
f(x)d\mu_{F(s)}(x)
and hence indeterminate, and it is N-extremal by Theorem
1.3
. By Theorem
3.9
we conclude that
f(x)d\mu_{F(s)}(x)
is the Friedrichs measure of the corresponding moment problem.
∎

In analogy with Theorem
1.6
we have the following result about indeterminate Stieltjes problems.

Theorem 3.12
.

Let
0<\alpha<1
be given. There exists a Stieltjes moment sequence
s
which is indet(S) and an N-extremal solution
\mu
with
\xi(\mu)>0
such that
x^{-\alpha}d\mu(x)
is determinate. Such a measure
\mu
is the Friedrichs solution and belongs to
B(\alpha/2)
. In particular
a(\mu)\leq\alpha/2
.

Proof.

Assuming that the result does not hold, we get that for any indet(S) moment problem and any N-extremal solution
\mu
with
\xi(\mu)>0
the following is true:

x^{-\alpha}d\mu(x)\;\mbox{is indeterminate},

and necessarily indet(S) since
\xi(x^{-\alpha}d\mu(x))>0
.

As
x^{-\alpha}
is bounded above on
[c,\infty)
for any
c>0
, the measure
x^{-\alpha}d\mu(x)
is also N-extremal with the same support as
\mu
. By iteration we get that
x^{-k\alpha}d\mu(x)
is indeterminate and N-extremal for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
. For
k
so large that
k\alpha\geq 2
this implies that
(1+x^{2})^{-1}d\mu(x)
is indeterminate in contradiction to Theorem
1.3
.

Since
x^{-1}d\mu(x)
is majorized by a multiple of
x^{-\alpha}d\mu(x)
, also
x^{-1}d\mu(x)
is determinate. By Theorem
3.3

\mu
must be the Friedrichs solution.
∎

Combining Theorem
3.12
with Theorem
3.8
we get:

Corollary 3.13
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary. There exists a normalized indeterminate Stieltjes moment sequence
s
which is indet(S) such that
a(\mu_{F})<\varepsilon
while
a(\mu_{t})\geq 1/2
for

.

We shall now see that there exist Friedrichs solutions
\mu_{F}
such that

is arbitrarily close to

.

Proposition 3.14
.

For any
0<\delta<1/2
there exists a Friedrichs solution

\mu_{F}
to an indet(S) Stieltjes moment problem with
a(\mu_{F})\in[1/2-\delta,1/2]
.

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that the result does not hold. Then there exists

0<\delta<1/2
such that for all Friedrichs solutions
\mu
to indet(S) Stieltjes moment problems we have
a(\mu)<1/2-\delta
. Starting from such a Friedrichs solution
\mu
we shall construct another Friedrichs solution
\mu_{1}
with
a(\mu_{1})=\delta+a(\mu)
.

Since
a(\mu)<1/2-\delta<1-\delta
, we get by Proposition
1.5
that

\mu_{1}:=(1+x^{2})^{\delta}d\mu(x)
belongs to
\mathcal{M}_{i,0}^{*}
, has the same support as
\mu
and satisfies
a(\mu_{1})=\delta+a(\mu)<1/2
.
The last inequality shows that
\mu_{1}
is a Friedrichs solutions and hence by our assumption

a(\mu_{1})=\delta+a(\mu)<1/2-\delta
, i.e.,
a(\mu)<1/2-2\delta

a(\mu)<1/2-2^{k}\delta,k=1,2,\ldots

.
∎

4
Examples of Friedrichs and Krein solutions

Example 4.1
.

Quartic rates.

Transforming the moment problem for quartic birth and death rates in
[
11
]
by the mapping
\tau_{a}(x)=ax
, where
a=(K_{0}/\pi)^{4}
,
K_{0}=\Gamma^{2}(1/4)/(4\sqrt{\pi})

we get a normalized Friedrichs solution of the form

\mu_{F}=\frac{4\pi}{K_{0}^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2k+1)\pi}{\sinh((2k+1)\pi)}\delta_{(2k+1)^{4}}.

(29)

The corresponding Krein solution is

\mu_{K}=\frac{\pi}{K_{0}^{2}}\delta_{0}+\frac{4\pi}{K_{0}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2k\pi}{\sinh(2k\pi)}\delta_{(2k)^{4}}.

(30)

We know from Theorem
3.8
that
\mu_{F}\in B(\frac{1}{2}),\mu_{K}\in A(\frac{1}{2})
.

Consider the measures

\mu_{c}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2k+1)\pi}{\sinh((2k+1)\pi)}(2k+1)^{c}\delta_{(2k+1)^{4}}.

(31)

From Corollary
3.5
we know that

\mu_{0},\mu_{4}
are indeterminate and N-extremal, while
\mu_{-4}
is determinate by Theorem
3.3
.

From this we clearly get that
\mu_{c}
is indeterminate for
c\geq 0
and determinate for
c\leq-4
. We are not able to decide for which value of
c\in(-4,0)

\mu_{c}
changes from indeterminate to determinate.

In the next examples we need the notation from the
q
-calculus of
[
14
]
.
For a fixed number
0<q<1
and
z\in\mathbb{C}
we define

(z;q)_{0}=1,\quad(z;q)_{n}=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(1-zq^{k}),\quad n=1,2,\ldots,\infty.

(32)

The Gaussian binomial coefficient is defined as

\left[\begin{array}[]{ll}n\\
k\end{array}\right]_{q}=\frac{(q;q)_{n}}{(q;q)_{k}(q;q)_{n-k}},\quad 0\leq k\leq n<\infty.

(33)

Example 4.2
.

Al-Salam–Carlitz moment problem.

Assume that
0<q<1<a<1/q,
which is the case of the Al-Salam–Carlitz moment problem in the indet(S) case, cf.
[

, Proposition 4.5.1]

The Friedrichs solution is given as

\mu_{F}=(q/a;q)_{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a^{-k}q^{k^{2}}}{(q/a;q)_{k}(q;q)_{k}}\delta_{aq^{-k}-1},

while the Krein solution is given as

\mu_{K}=(aq;q)_{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a^{k}q^{k^{2}}}{(aq;q)_{k}(q;q)_{k}}\delta_{q^{-k}-1}.

The Friedrichs parameter is given by

F(s)=(q;q)_{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k}}{(a-q^{k})(q;q)_{k}},

cf.
[
11

.
We have
\mu_{F}\in B(\frac{1}{2})
and
\mu_{K}\in A(\frac{1}{2})
.

Example 4.3
.

Stieltjes–Wigert moment problem.
We refer to
[
13
]
for this moment problem. For
0<q<1
we have the density

v_{q}(x)=\frac{q^{1/8}}{\sqrt{2\pi\log(1/q)}}x^{-1/2}\exp\left(-\frac{\log^{2}(x)}{2\log(1/q)}\right),x>0

with moments

s_{n}=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{n}v_{q}(x)\,dx=q^{-n(n+1)/2},n=0,1,\ldots,

(34)

cf.
[

To describe the Friedrichs and Krein solutions we need Ramanujan’s function

\Phi(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\frac{q^{k^{2}}}{(q;q)_{k}}x^{k},

(35)

The zeros of
\Phi
form the sequence
\xi_{1}<\xi_{2}<\ldots
described in Proposition

as limits of the zeros of the orthonormal Stieltjes–Wigert polynomials

p_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}\sqrt{q^{n}/(q;q)_{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left[\begin{array}[]{ll}n\\
k\end{array}\right]_{q}(-1)^{k}q^{k^{2}}x^{k},

cf.
[
13

. Detailed information about the zeros

can be found in

]
, where

The Friedrichs parameter is given as

, cf.
[

.
Three N-extremal solutions
\mu_{t}
are known corresponding to the parameters

(the Friedrichs solution
\mu_{F}
),

and
t=\infty
(the Krein solution
\mu_{K}
)
concentrated in the sequences
(\xi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
,
(q\xi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
0,(\xi_{n}/q)_{n\geq 1}
respectively.

It is worth recalling that the zeros
\xi_{n}
are well separated in the sense that
\xi_{n+1}/\xi_{n}>q^{-2}
.

We also recall that the mass at a point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
of an N-extremal solution is given as
\rho(x_{0})
, where

1/\rho(x_{0})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}^{2}(x_{0}),

and therefore

\mu_{F}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho(\xi_{k})\delta_{\xi_{k}},\quad\mu_{1}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho(q\xi_{k})\delta_{q\xi_{k}}

and

\mu_{K}=\rho(0)\delta_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho(\xi_{k}/q)\delta_{\xi_{k}/q}.

We know that
\mu_{F}\in B(\frac{1}{2})
and
\mu_{1},\mu_{K}\in A(\frac{1}{2})
.
