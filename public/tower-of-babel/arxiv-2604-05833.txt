A forward analytical model for the optical selection bias on galaxy cluster lensing profiles
M. Costanzi, H.-Y. Wu, J. H. Esteves, S. Grandis, C. To, M. Aguena

Abstract
Cluster catalogs selected by optical properties are subject to selection biases, primarily arising from unresolved systems along the line of sight. These biases affect key observables for cluster cosmology, such as the lensing signal and clustering statistics. In this work, we present a fully predictive forward analytical model to quantify the impact of optical-selection bias due to projection effects on cluster density profiles. This is achieved by introducing a scale-dependent parametrization of the optical cluster bias, whose small- and large-scale behaviour is set by the amplitude of projection effects, and by expressing the two-halo component of the density profile in terms of the contributions from off-axis halos along the line of sight. As a case study, we consider a DES Y3-like cluster catalog and validate our model against simulated samples. Our model successfully captures the dependence of the two-halo component on richness boosts induced by projections, as well as its evolution with richness and redshift. It also recovers the overall bias in the projected density profile relative to a randomly selected sample with the same mass distribution. The framework presented here provides a consistent methodology for modeling the impact of line-of-sight structures on the observed richness and density profiles of optically selected clusters, directly linking selection biases to the underlying cosmology and survey specifications.

I
Introduction

Galaxy clusters are multi-component systems made of stars, galaxies, hot plasma (the intra-cluster medium), and dark matter. This multi-component nature allows us to detect and study these systems at different wavelengths, such as X-ray, millimeter and optical bands.
In photometric surveys, galaxy clusters are detected as overdensities of galaxies, and the richness of the system – a weighted sum of the number of putative member galaxies – is often used as a mass proxy for sample selection. Therefore, systems with their main axis aligned along to the line of sight, more concentrated, and with rich structures in projection have a larger chance to be detected and included in the catalog
(
Wu et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2023b
;
Lee et al. 2025
, e.g.)
. In particular, projection effects due to the limited resolution achievable along the line of sight have been shown to bias high the cluster density profile of optically-selected clusters by
10-20\%
on scales relevant for mass calibration through weak lensing measurements
(
Sunayama et al. 2020
;
Wu et al. 2022
;
Salcedo et al. 2024
, e.g.)
. If not properly modeled these effects can lead to biases in the cluster mass estimates
(
Nyarko Nde et al. 2025
, e.g.)
, which, in turn, translate into systematic biases in the cosmological parameters inferred from optical cluster catalogs
(
DES Collaboration et al. 2020
)
.

Several approaches have been proposed to model or calibrate this selection bias:
Salcedo et al. 2024
used a simulation-based approach to emulate stacked cluster lensing profiles and infer observable-mass relation parameters for the DES Y1 cluster sample;
Sunayama 2023
quantified the large-scale boost of the cluster lensing profile from SDSS data using cluster-galaxy cross-correlation functions
(
Zhou et al. 2024
, see also)
. Conversely, in the cosmological analyses of
Sunayama et al. 2024
and
DES Collaboration et al. 2025
the authors assumed an analytical template for the selection bias and marginalized over its model parameters.

In this paper, we propose an analytical derivation to estimate the selection bias of optically selected clusters, without free parameters, which depends upon the assumed halo occupation distribution, cosmological model, cluster finder, and survey specifications. We focus on the bias induced by projection effects, which affect scales relevant for weak lensing mass calibration.
In particular, we aim to reproduce the optical selection bias on cluster radial density profiles due to correlated structures as a function of richness and redshift.
To this end, we first model the clustering bias of a richness-selected sample, and then derive the corresponding projected density profile.
As a case study, we assume an optically selected cluster catalog built with the red sequence Matched-filter Probabilistic Percolation cluster finder algorithm
(
Rykoff et al. 2014
, redMaPPer;)
using Dark Energy Survey
1

)
. Comparing our model predictions to synthetic data, we show the capability of our model to reproduce the scale dependence and amplitude of the bias, as well as its dependence on the strength of projection effects. Despite its simplicity – e.g. we neglect orientation and concentration bias – the model provides a useful tool to estimate the impact of projection effects on cluster density profiles on scales relevant for their mass calibration, which can be tuned to the specifics of the cluster finder and survey data.

IV
. Finally, section
V

II
Model

Our model builds on the observation that the same line-of-sight structures that contribute to the observed richness also affect the projected density profile (see e.g. figure
1
). Consequently, a larger projection-induced boost in richness,
\Delta^{\rm prj}
, implies a more crowded environment along the line of sight, which in turn enhances the projected density profile. Therefore, if we can predict the mean number of halos projected along the line of sight of a cluster as a function of
\Delta^{\rm prj}
and angular separation
\theta
from its center, we can also predict their contribution to the observed density profile. To this end, we first derive an expression for the optical cluster bias as a function of
\Delta^{\rm prj}
and projected angular separation (section
II.1
). This relation then allows us to estimate the mean contribution of correlated structures to the projected density profile by summing the contributions of halos projected at different off-axis angles
\theta
(section
II.2
).

The framework developed in this work builds upon the approach introduced by
Costanzi et al. 2019
, in which the observed richness,
\lambda^{\rm ob}
, is treated as a noisy measurement of the intrinsic (true) richness,
\lambda^{\rm tr}
– i.e. the unobservable true number of member galaxies satisfying the survey selection criteria – which depends on cluster mass.
Specifically, we assume
\lambda^{\rm ob}=\lambda^{\rm tr}+\Delta^{\rm prj}
, where
\Delta^{\rm prj}
accounts for the contribution from projected structures along the line of sight
(
Farahi et al. 2016
;
Zu et al. 2017
;
Wojtak et al. 2018
, e.g.)
, while
\lambda^{\rm tr}
includes any other source of intrinsic scatter.
The stochastic nature of
\lambda^{\rm ob}
is described by decomposing the probability of observing a system of mass
M
and redshift
z
with richness
\lambda^{\rm ob}
as the convolution:

P(\lambda^{\rm ob}|M,z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda^{\rm tr}\ P(\lambda^{\rm ob}|\lambda^{\rm tr},z)P(\lambda^{\rm tr}|M,z)\,.

(1)

Here
P(\lambda^{\rm tr}|M,z)
encodes the intrinsic richness-mass relation, while
P(\lambda^{\rm ob}|\lambda^{\rm tr},z)
accounts for the observational scatter, which depends on the specifics of the survey and cluster-finding algorithm. The latter has been calibrated in the literature either through simulation-based approaches
(
Costanzi et al. 2019
;
Maturi et al. 2019
, e.g.)
or via multi-wavelength observations
(
Grandis et al. 2025
, e.g.)
.

Throughout this work, we neglect the cluster photometric redshift uncertainty, and set
z^{\rm ob}
equal to the true cluster redshift. The latter has been shown to be a subdominant systematic in current cluster cosmology studies
(
DES Collaboration et al. 2020
;
Bocquet et al. 2024
;
Lesci et al. 2025
, e.g.)
, but it can be restored in the following equations at the expense of an additional marginalization over the distribution
P(z^{\rm ob}|z^{\rm tr})dz^{\rm tr}
. The formalism outlined below is general and does not rely on assumptions specific to a particular optical survey. The specific model choices and survey-dependent ingredients adopted for this study are presented in section
III
.

II.1
Optical cluster bias

Given two tracers of the matter density field, the mean number of tracer ”1” neighbors that each tracer ”2” has within a volume element
dV
, separated by a distance
r
, is:

dN(r)=\bar{n}_{1}\left[1+b_{1}\,b_{2}\xi(r)\right]dV\,,

(2)

where
\bar{n}_{1}
is the mean number density of tracer ”1”,
\xi
is the matter correlation function, and
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
the biases of the two tracers. For our purposes – predicting the mean number of halos in projection onto an optical cluster –
b_{1}
is the halo bias and
b_{2}=b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}
, the optical cluster bias, which we aim to model as a function of observable cluster properties.

To this end, we start by expressing the noise on the observed richness due to projection effects,
\Delta^{\rm prj}
, as the weighted sum of the foreground and background cluster galaxies falling within the target cluster area
(
6
, see also Appendix A in)
. Specifically, for clusters at redshift
z^{\rm ob}
with observed richness
\lambda^{\rm ob}
, and true richness
\lambda^{\rm tr}
, the mean
\Delta^{\rm prj}
due to projected systems reads:

\Delta^{\rm prj}(\lambda^{\rm ob},\lambda^{\rm tr},z^{\rm ob})=\lambda^{\rm ob}-\lambda^{\rm tr}=2\pi\int dz\frac{dV}{dzd\Omega}\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\sin\theta\\
\cdot\int d\lambda\,\rho^{\rm prj}(\lambda,z,\theta|\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})n^{\rm prj}(\lambda,z,\theta|\lambda^{\rm ob},\lambda^{\rm tr},z^{\rm ob})\,,

(3)

where
dV/(dzd\Omega)
is the comoving volume element per unit redshift and solid angle,
n^{\rm prj}(\lambda,z,\theta|\lambda^{\rm ob},\lambda^{\rm tr},z^{\rm ob})
is the mean number density of halos at redshift
z
with true richness
\lambda
, at projected angular distance
\theta
from the target cluster center, while
\rho^{\rm prj}(\lambda,z,\theta|\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})
is the mean richness contamination contributed by each halo in projection
2
2

2

To avoid clutter in the notation, we denote the true richness of the target cluster as
\lambda^{\rm tr}
, while using simply
\lambda
for the true richness of projected halos.
. This quantity is specific to the cluster finder and survey data under consideration. We factorize the latter term as:

\rho^{\rm prj}(\lambda,z,\theta|\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})=w_{z}(z,z^{\rm ob})f_{A}(\lambda,z,\theta,\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})\lambda\,,

(4)

where
w_{z}(z,z^{\rm ob})
is the mean fraction of richness that a cluster will absorb from a halo at
redshift
z
, assuming perfect alignment of the two systems. The second term in equation
4
accounts for the geometry of the systems, and corresponds to the mean fraction of galaxies of a halo falling within the target cluster projected area.

The mean number density of halos in projection is computed according to the cluster-halo correlation function as:

n^{\rm prj}(\lambda,z,\theta|\lambda^{\rm ob},\lambda^{\rm tr},z^{\rm ob})=\int dMn(M,z)P(\lambda|M,z)\,\cdot\\
\left[1+b(M,z)b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}(\lambda^{\rm ob},\lambda^{\rm tr},z^{\rm ob},\theta)\xi_{\rm NL}(z,z^{\rm ob},\theta)\right]\,

(5)

where
n(M,z)
represents the halo mass function,
P(\lambda|M,z)
the probability distribution of the true richness at fixed halo mass and redshift,
b(M,z)
is the halo bias, and
\xi_{\rm NL}(z,z^{\rm ob},\theta)
the non-linear matter correlation function at
z^{\rm ob}
and comoving separation:

\Delta\chi(z,z^{\rm ob},\theta)=\sqrt{\chi(z)^{2}+\chi(z^{\rm ob})^{2}-2\chi(z)\chi(z^{\rm ob})\cos\theta}\,.

Finally,
b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}
is the scale-dependent bias for clusters with
\lambda^{\rm ob}
,
\lambda^{\rm tr}
and
z^{\rm ob}
that we seek to predict. To account for exclusion effects, we include the condition

b(M,z)b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}(\lambda^{\rm ob},\lambda^{\rm tr},z^{\rm ob},\theta)\xi_{\rm NL}(z,z^{\rm ob},\theta)=-1

for comoving distances
\Delta\chi
smaller than the cluster radius
R_{\lambda}
defined by the detection algorithm.

Since only structures projected within
R_{\lambda}
contribute to
\lambda^{\rm ob}
, and since correlated structures are typically distributed along filaments rather than isotropically around the cluster, the selection at fixed
(\lambda^{\rm ob},\lambda^{\rm tr})
preferentially picks systems with either denser (
\lambda^{\rm ob}-\lambda^{\rm tr}\gg 1
) or emptier (
\lambda^{\rm ob}\simeq\lambda^{\rm tr}
) lines of sight. As a consequence, the modulus of
b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}
is expected to decrease with projected distance from the cluster center, eventually reaching a constant value,
b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}^{\infty}
, beyond
R_{\lambda}

(
Zeng et al. 2023
;
Sunayama 2023
;
To et al. 2025
, see also)
.
We therefore propose the following sigmoid functional form:

b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}(\lambda^{\rm ob},\lambda^{\rm tr},z^{\rm ob},\theta)=b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}^{0}(\lambda^{\rm ob},\lambda^{\rm tr},z^{\rm ob})\,+\\
\frac{b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}^{\infty}(\lambda^{\rm ob},\lambda^{\rm tr},z^{\rm ob})-b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}^{0}(\lambda^{\rm ob},\lambda^{\rm tr},z^{\rm ob})}{1+e^{-k(\theta-\theta_{0})}}\,,

(6)

where
k
controls the steepness of the transition,
\theta_{0}
defines its midpoint, while
b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}^{0}
and
b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}^{\infty}
represent the asymptotic values on small- and large-scale, respectively. The model is motivated by the analysis of the ratio of projected correlation functions measured from our mock cluster catalog (see section
III.1
) using a richness-selected and a random-selected sample with the same mass distribution (see Appendix
A
for details). In particular, the mock data are well described by the sigmoid model setting the shape parameters to
k=2.5/\theta_{\lambda}
and
\theta_{0}=\theta_{\lambda}/2
, where the cluster angular size is given by
\theta_{\lambda}=R_{\lambda}/D_{a}(z^{\rm ob})
.
As for the large-scale bias,
b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}^{\infty}
, it is well approximated by the relation:

\displaystyle b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}^{\infty}(\lambda^{\rm ob},\lambda^{\rm tr},z^{\rm ob})=b_{\rm eff}(\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})\left[1+0.13\delta^{\rm prj}\right]

\displaystyle\delta^{\rm prj}(\lambda^{\rm ob},\lambda^{\rm tr},z^{\rm ob})=\frac{\lambda^{\rm ob}-\lambda^{\rm tr}}{\Delta^{\rm prj}_{\rm RND-sel}(\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})}-1

(7)

where
\delta^{\rm prj}
corresponds to the excess of
\Delta^{\rm prj}
compared to randomly selected sample with the same mass distribution (see figure
9
). The effective bias is computed by weighting the halo bias by the corresponding mass distribution:
b_{\rm eff}(\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})=\int dMP(M|\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})b(M,z^{\rm ob})
, while the mean contribution to the richness for an unbiased (mass-selected) sample,
\Delta^{\rm prj}_{\rm RND-sel}
, is derived from equations
3
-
5
, by replacing
b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}
with
b_{\rm eff}
.

Using our model for
b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}
, we can invert equation

\displaystyle b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}^{0}(\lambda^{\rm ob},\lambda^{\rm tr},z^{\rm ob})

\displaystyle=\frac{(\lambda^{\rm ob}-\lambda^{\rm tr})-\langle\Delta^{\rm prj}_{\rm bkg}\rangle-b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}^{\infty}I_{1}}{I_{2}-I_{1}}

(8)

\displaystyle\langle\Delta^{\rm prj}_{\rm bkg}(\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}[1],

\displaystyle I_{1}(\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}\!\left[b(M,z)\,\frac{\xi_{\rm NL}(z,z^{\rm ob},\theta)}{1+e^{-k(\theta-\theta_{0})}}\right],

\displaystyle I_{2}(\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}\!\left[b(M,z)\,\xi_{\rm NL}(z,z^{\rm ob},\theta)\right],

where we define, for convenience, the operator
\mathcal{P}[\cdot]
as the line-of-sight integral over the halo population weighted by the projection kernel
\rho^{\rm prj}
:

\mathcal{P}[X]\equiv\int dz\,\frac{dV}{dz\,d\Omega}\int dM\,n(M,z)\int d\lambda\,P(\lambda|M,z)\\
\cdot 2\pi\int d\theta\,\sin\theta\;\rho^{\rm prj}(\lambda,z,\theta|\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})\,X(M,z,\theta|z^{\rm ob})\,.

(9)

From equation
8

b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}^{0}
, depends on the richness boost,
\Delta^{\rm prj}=\lambda^{\rm ob}-\lambda^{\rm tr}
, relative to the expected contributions from the background,
\langle\Delta^{\rm prj}_{\rm bkg}\rangle
, and correlated large-scale structures,
b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}^{\infty}I_{1}(\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})

\lambda^{\rm ob}

II.2
Projected density profile

Following the same line of thought outlined in section
II.1
we can construct a model for the mean azimuthally-averaged
projected density profile of an optically selected cluster having
\lambda^{\rm ob}
and
z^{\rm ob}
.

We start by splitting the mean projected density profile into two contributions
(
Oguri & Hamana 2011
, e.g.)
:

\langle\Sigma(R|\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})\rangle=\langle\Sigma^{\rm 1h}(R|\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})\rangle+\langle\Sigma^{\rm prj}(R|\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})\rangle\,,

(11)

where the first term represents the contribution of the one-halo term, and can be computed by properly weighting the projected density profile of halos with mass
M
and redshift
z^{\rm ob}
,
\Sigma(M,z^{\rm ob})
, with the underlying mass distribution at fixed
\lambda^{\rm ob}
:

\langle\Sigma^{\rm 1h}(R|\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})\rangle=\int dMP(M|\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})\Sigma(R|M,z^{\rm ob})\\
=\frac{1}{n(\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})}\int dMP(\lambda^{\rm ob}|M,z^{\rm ob})n(M,z^{\rm ob})\Sigma(R|M,z^{\rm ob})

(12)

The second term – commonly referred to as the two-halo term – accounts for the contribution from correlated large-scale structures. In this work, we express it as an integral over the contributions, at projected distance
R
, from halos along the line of sight centered at different angles
\theta
from the target cluster. Using the cluster bias
b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}
derived in the previous section to compute the number of halos in projection to a target cluster with (
\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob}
), the 2-halo term reads:

\langle\Sigma^{\rm prj}(R|\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob})\rangle=\int d\theta\sin\theta\int dz\frac{dV}{dzd\Omega}\int dM\ n(M,z)\\
\left[1+b(M,z)b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}(\lambda^{\rm ob},z^{\rm ob},\theta)\xi_{\rm NL}(z,z^{\rm ob},\theta)\right]\Sigma_{\rm mis}(R|M,z,\theta,z^{\rm ob})\,.

(13)

The term
\Sigma_{\rm mis}(R|M,z,\theta,z^{\rm ob})
is the surface mass density contributed at the projected distance
R
from the target cluster by halos in projection with mass
M
, at redshift
z
, and miscentered by an angle
\theta
relative to the target cluster center
(
Yang et al. 2006
;
Johnston et al. 2007
, e.g.)
:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\rm mis}(R|M,z,\theta,z^{\rm ob})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Sigma\left(R_{h}|M,z\right)\,{\rm d}\varphi,

(14)

\displaystyle R_{h}=\frac{\sqrt{\theta_{R}^{2}+\theta^{2}-2\theta\theta_{R}\cos\varphi}}{D_{A}(z)}\,;\quad\theta_{R}=\frac{R}{D_{A}(z^{\rm ob})}\,,

where the integral over
\varphi
accounts for the contributions of systems at different azimuthal positions. Equation
14
properly accounts for the different redshifts of the target cluster and the halos in projection.

For illustrative purposes, we show in figure

the resulting mean density profile for clusters with observed richness

at redshift

, along with its two components,

and

.

III
Case study: redMaPPer DES Y3

To test our model, we assume a DES Y3-like cluster catalog, built
using the redMaPPer cluster finder
(
Rykoff et al. 2014
)
.
redMaPPer is a photometric cluster finding algorithm that identifies galaxy clusters as overdensities of red-sequence galaxies and then calculates the richness as the sum of the membership probabilities of all galaxies in the cluster field. The sum extends over all red-sequence galaxies above a fixed luminosity threshold and within an empirically calibrated cluster radius
R_{\lambda}=(\lambda^{\rm ob}/100)^{0.2}h^{-1}\ \rm{Mpc}
.
Without loss of generality, we make the following model choices:

•

We use the Tinker halo mass function and halo bias models
(
Tinker et al. 2008
;
Tinker et al. 2010
)
;

•

We model the projected density
\Sigma(R|M,z)
assuming a spherical NFW profile
(
Navarro et al. 1997
)
.

•

The intrinsic richness–mass relation is modeled using a conventional halo model parameterization, with
\lambda^{\rm tr}=\lambda^{\rm cen}+\lambda^{\rm sat}
where
\lambda^{\rm cen}
and
\lambda^{\rm sat}
are the number of central and satellite galaxies respectively.
\lambda^{\rm cen}
is assumed to be a deterministic function of mass, with
\lambda^{\rm cen}=1
for
M\geq M_{\rm min}
and
\lambda^{\rm cen}=0
otherwise.

\lambda^{\rm sat}
is a random variable with an expectation value

\langle\lambda^{\rm sat}|M,z\rangle=\left(\frac{M-M_{\rm min}}{M_{1}-M_{\rm min}}\right)^{\alpha}\left(\frac{1+z}{1+z_{*}}\right)^{\epsilon}

(15)

where
M_{1}
is the characteristic mass at which a halo of mass
M
has on average one satellite galaxy, and the pivot redshift is set equal to the mean redshift of the sample
z_{*}=0.45
.
To allow for super-Poisson halo occupancies, we model
P(\lambda^{\rm tr}|M,z)
as the convolution of a Poisson and a Gaussian distribution, where the scatter of the latter is given by
\sigma_{\lambda}\langle\lambda^{\rm sat}|M,z\rangle
.

•

To mimic the percolation scheme adopted by redMaPPer
\rho^{\rm prj}(\lambda,z|\lambda^{\rm tr},z^{\rm ob},\theta)
is set to zero if
\lambda\geq\lambda^{\rm ob}
, that is, only lower-ranked clusters can contribute to the richness of a given object
3

V
Summary and conclusions

In this paper, we presented an analytical framework to predict the selection bias affecting the projected density profiles of photometric cluster catalogs due to projection effects. In particular, we modeled the scale-dependent clustering bias of optically selected clusters,
b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}
, as a function of the richness contamination induced by line-of-sight structures. This relation was then used to compute the mean contribution of correlated structures to a cluster projected density profile by integrating the off-axis density profiles of projected halos.

As a case study, we considered a DES Y3-like photometric cluster sample and generated a synthetic cluster catalog that self-consistently accounts for projection effects on both richness and projected density profiles. Motivated by the analysis of the cluster correlation function of richness-selected and random-selected mock samples, we introduced a sigmoid parameterization to describe
b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}
as a function of projected angular distance from the cluster center. We showed that this model successfully reproduces the dependence of the cluster bias on the magnitude of projection effects,
\Delta^{\rm prj}=\lambda^{\rm ob}-\lambda^{\rm tr}
. The distortion induced by selection effects is strongest at small projected separations but persists out to scales of
R\simeq 10h^{-1}\ \rm{Mpc}
. We further found that the large-scale value of the optical cluster bias can be well approximated by a linear function of
\delta^{\rm prj}
, the excess of richness bias induced by projection effects relative to a random sample with the same mass distribution.

From the model for
b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}
, we predict the mean number of halos projected along the line of sight to a target cluster with given
\lambda^{\rm ob}
and
z^{\rm ob}
, and thus estimate their contribution to the projected density profile. Consistent with their clustering properties, the projected component of the profile,
\langle\Sigma^{\rm prj}\rangle
, depends on the magnitude of
\Delta^{\rm prj}
, implying that, on average,
\lambda^{\rm ob}
-selected clusters reside in denser environments than random-selected samples with the same mass distribution. All model predictions accurately reproduce the corresponding measurements from the synthetic DES Y3 cluster catalog, including their scale dependence up to
R\sim 10h^{-1}\ \rm{Mpc}
and their evolution with richness and redshift. In particular, the model captures the characteristic bell-shaped bias observed in the total density profiles of optically selected clusters.

The simulation tests performed here were designed to validate the core analytical equations governing the small-scale selection bias (Eq.
8
) and the projected component of the density profile (Eq.
13
). In general, the magnitude of
b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}
and of the projected density bias predicted by the model depends on the specifics of the cluster finder and survey, primarily through the projection kernel
w_{z}(z,z^{\rm ob})
and the conditional probability distribution
P(\lambda^{\rm ob}|\lambda^{\rm tr})
. Both quantities can be empirically calibrated using simulation-based approaches or multi-wavelength data. The framework is also readily generalizable to alternative optical mass proxies, such as the probability-weighted stellar mass
\mu_{\star}

(
Esteves et al. 2025
)
or the number of galaxies
N_{\rm gals}
used by algorithms like WaZP
(
Aguena et al. 2021
)
, provided that the definition of the projection weight
\rho_{\rm prj}
(equation
4
) is adapted accordingly.

Despite its simplicity—for example, the present analysis neglects cluster miscentering as well as selection effects related to halo orientation and triaxiality—this work provides a physically motivated framework to interpret and predict selection biases in the weak-lensing profiles of photometric cluster catalogs.
In cosmological applications, it is important to account for systematic uncertainties associated with the modeling of projection effects, including the choice of projection kernel and geometric factor
f_{A}
, the calibration of
P(\lambda^{\rm ob}|\lambda^{\rm tr})
, and the assumed functional form of the optically selected cluster bias
b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}
. Moreover, given the large number of nested integrals required to compute the model predictions, direct implementation in cosmological parameter-inference pipelines may be computationally expensive. For such applications, we provide in Appendix
C
a double power-law fitting function that approximates the bias in the projected density profile. The parameters of this fitting function can be assigned priors calibrated from our model, enabling efficient marginalization over systematic uncertainties in the projection modeling, as well as over HOD and cosmological parameters in cosmological analyses.

Finally, although this framework was developed to describe projected density profiles of optically selected clusters on scales
R\lesssim 10h^{-1}\ \rm{Mpc}
, it could in principle be extended to model their large-scale clustering properties. We caution, however, that the accuracy of the large-scale cluster bias prescription,
b_{\lambda^{\rm ob}}^{\infty}
, has not been tested across different cosmologies and may require further refinement for precision clustering analyses.

\displaystyle-\varphi_{\rm min}(R)=\arccos\!\left(\frac{\vartheta^{2}+\theta_{\lambda}^{2}-\theta_{h}^{2}}{2\vartheta\theta_{\lambda}}\right).

Appendix C
Analytical fitting function for the selection bias on projected density profile

For practical purposes, we provide here an analytical expression to approximate the optical selection bias on the projected density profile. The bias can be described by a double power-law with smooth transition, namely:

\mathcal{B}_{\rm sel}(R)=A\left(\frac{R}{R_{0}}\right)^{\alpha}\left[1+\left(\frac{R}{R_{0}}\right)^{\gamma}\right]^{(\beta-\alpha)/\gamma}+1

(23)

where
A
sets the amplitude,
\alpha
controls the slope at small radii,
\beta
the slope in the outer region,
R_{0}
defines the transition scale, and
\gamma
determines the smoothness of the transition.
Setting the transition scale
R_{0}
to the comoving cluster radius
R_{\lambda}(1+z)
, the best-fit parameters derived considering the full mock catalog are:
A=0.10
,
\alpha=0.92
,
\beta=-0.53
,
\gamma=4.1
(see figure
10
).
For completeness, we note that the same functional can also approximate the bias on the excess surface density profile,
\Delta\Sigma
, a quantity more directly related to the observable weak-lensing shear profile.
