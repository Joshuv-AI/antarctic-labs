Optical Spectral Transmission Imaging in Patients with Osteoarthritis: A Comparative Study with Joint Ultrasound and Clinical Markers
Authors: Konstantinos Triantafyllias, Stefanie Liverakos, Khalid K Altamimi, Ann-Kathrin Druck, Svea Gauch, Ioannis Parodis, Dionysis Nikolopoulos, Christian Geber, Muthuraman Muthuraman, Andreas Schwarting
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of optical spectral transmission (OST) in patients with hand osteoarthritis (OA) and to assess its associations with clinical findings, joint ultrasound (US) markers of active OA, and patient- and disease-related characteristics.MethodsIn this exploratory pilot study, consecutive patients with OA and healthy controls underwent OST measurements using the HandScan® device. Clinical examinations and inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate) were obtained. A subset of patients underwent standardized joint US [greyscale (GSUS)/power Doppler (PDUS)]. Correlations between OST and clinical, anthropometric and US parameters were examined, and receiver operating characteristics (ROC) assessed discriminative ability. Linear regression was used to evaluate confounding effects.ResultsA total of 2910 joints of 97 patients with OA were examined via OST and compared to 3300 joints of 100 control subjects. OST values were significantly higher in patients with OA than in controls (10.39 ± 1.82 vs. 7.51 ± 3.80; p < 0.001), and this difference remained significant after adjustment for potential confounders (β = 1.698; 95% CI 0.149–3.247; padj = 0.032). OST showed a weak but significant correlation with PDUS when distal interphalangeal (DIP) and proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joints were analysed together (ρ = 0.11, p = 0.014). At the DIP joint level, OST correlated weakly but significantly with both GSUS (ρ = 0.19, p = 0.004) and PDUS (ρ = 0.22, p = 0.001). Moderate correlations with hand size were observed in patients with OA (ρ = 0.36, p < 0.001) and controls (ρ = 0.40, p < 0.001). ROC analysis revealed a moderate discriminative ability [patients vs. controls: area under the curve (AUC) 0.718; 95% CI 0.645–0.792; p < 0.001].ConclusionOST correlated with US synovitis markers and demonstrated a moderate/significant capacity to distinguish patients with OA from controls. As a rapid, non-invasive and examiner-independent technique, OST may represent a useful adjunctive tool in the diagnostic assessment of hand OA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40744-026-00867-w.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Optical spectral transmission, HandScan, Joint ultrasound, Diagnostic imaging

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Hand osteoarthritis (OA) is a highly prevalent musculoskeletal condition associated with pain, functional impairment and relevant healthcare burden, while objective, rapid and examiner-independent tools for assessing disease activity are still limited.To evaluate whether optical spectral transmission (OST) can reliably detect inflammatory and structural disease activity in hand OA and distinguish patients with OA from healthy controls.What was learned from the study?OST values were significantly higher in patients with OA compared with healthy controls and showed a moderate ability to discriminate between both groups (AUC 0.718; 95% CI 0.645–0.792). OST also showed weak but significant correlations with ultrasound markers of synovitis (GSUS and PDUS), particularly at the DIP joints.These findings suggest that OST may detect aspects of inflammatory joint activity and could serve as a rapid, non-invasive and examiner-independent adjunct tool in the diagnostic assessment of hand OA. The observed weak-to-moderate correlations support its potential complementary role alongside established clinical and imaging assessments, while further studies are needed to define its standalone diagnostic value.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most prevalent chronic joint disease worldwide and is characterised by progressive degeneration of articular cartilage, osteophyte formation, subchondral bone remodelling and varying degrees of synovitis [1]. It is a leading cause of adult chronic pain and long-term disability [2, 3]. In 2020, approximately 595 million people worldwide were affected by OA, with hand OA cases projected to increase by 48.6% by 2050 [4]. The pathophysiology of OA involves mechanical, inflammatory, metabolic and age-related processes promoting structural damage in affected joints [5]. Metabolic factors, including obesity and components oft he metabolic syndrome, accelerate disease progression [6], while genetic factors account for 40–80% of OA development, particularly in hand and hip OA [7].

The knee is the most commonly affected joint, followed by the hand and hip. Hand OA predominantly involves the distal (DIP) and proximal (PIP) interphalangeal joints and the thumb base [8, 9]. Synovitis may develop throughout the disease course and is linked to structural and clinical progression [10]. The lifetime risk of hand OA is estimated at up to 40% [11], often leading to pain, stiffness, reduced grip strength and impaired hand function [12]. Timely assessment and targeted treatment of synovitis may help prevent progressive joint damage [13].

Diagnostic assessment of hand OA remains challenging, as clinical findings, radiographic changes and laboratory tests do not always provide clear differentiation from other joint disorders [14]. Radiography remains the clinical standard [15]; however, it detects only relatively late structural changes and lacks sensitivity for early disease. Moreover, not all patients with radiographic evidence of OA present clinical symptoms, and not all patients with joint symptoms show radiographic OA signs [15, 16]. Magnetic resonance imaging is sensitive but costly, impractical for routine evaluations and frequently performed unilaterally [17]. Joint ultrasound can detect synovial hypertrophy and power Doppler signals in certain subsets of patients (e.g. those with active OA), as well as early contour irregularities. However, the findings are highly operator-dependent, and the procedure is time-consuming, particularly when multiple joints are examined with thorough scoring [18]. Given these limitations, there is a demand for novel, rapid and examiner-independent diagnostic tools capable of identifying OA changes earlier and potentially monitoring disease progression.

The HandScan® imaging modality (Demcom/Hemics; Netherlands) is based on the modern technology of optical spectral transmission (OST). OST measures the blood-specific absorption of light transmitted through a tissue in an objective manner [18]. In case of joint inflammation, the absorption is reduced as a result of inflammation-associated changes in vascularity [17, 19]. Since inflamed or structurally altered joints exhibit characteristic alterations in vascularity [19], OST has been shown to detect synovitis in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and other inflammatory arthropathies, where it correlates with clinical disease activity scores and power Doppler ultrasound (PDUS) [17, 20–22]. To date, however, OST has rarely been studied in OA, and its role in degenerative, rather than inflammatory, arthropathy remains unclear. To our knowledge, only one study has explored this topic, suggesting a possible association between OST and ultrasound-detected synovitis in OA [23]. However, these findings have not been validated.

Therefore, the primary aim of this exploratory pilot study was to evaluate whether OST could discriminate between patients with OA and healthy controls. Secondary aims included investigating the correlations between OST measurements and structural and inflammatory changes observed through joint ultrasound, as well as clinical and anthropometric factors. Through this approach, we seek to clarify how osteoarthritic changes influence OST measurements and to determine whether OST may serve as both a diagnostic and monitoring tool in OA, ultimately advancing its clinical application.

Methods

Study Population

In this exploratory pilot study patients with clinically suspected hand OA were consecutively recruited at the Rheumatology Center of Rhineland-Palatinate in a real-life clinical setting. Recruitment was performed according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Referring clinicians were not involved in the study design, patient selection, recruitment procedures or study assessments, thereby minimizing the risk of preselection bias.

All participants underwent plain X-ray examinations of both hands in two planes, and only those with radiographic confirmation of OA were included in the study, resulting in a final OA cohort of 97 patients. OST examinations were performed on all patients with OA and on a healthy control group, which was recruited from hospital staff and community volunteers. All potential healthy control subjects underwent a structured clinical evaluation prior to inclusion. Individuals showing clinical signs suggestive of hand OA, those with other wrist or finger pathologies, those reporting joint pain and those with any known inflammatory rheumatic disease were excluded. To further ensure eligibility, participants completed a brief questionnaire covering their relevant medical history. Following this screening procedure, 100 individuals were enrolled in the healthy control group. An OA subset underwent an additional bilateral joint ultrasound [greyscale (GSUS) and power Doppler (PDUS)] examination of the PIP and DIP joints, on the same day as the OST and X-ray measurements. Exclusion criteria were any inflammatory rheumatic disease, recent trauma, prior joint surgery in the hands, active skin lesions impairing optical measurement, or inability to position hands correctly in the HandScan device.

The study was approved by the Standing Committee for Clinical Studies of Rhineland-Palatinate, Germany (Nr. 13042) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

Clinical and Laboratory Assessment

For all participants, age, sex, measured weight and height (BMI), nicotine use and diabetes mellitus were documented. Patients with OA underwent clinical joint examination, including tender joint count and swollen joint count. Patient-reported disease activity was recorded using a visual analogue scale. Laboratory tests included C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate.

Hand size was measured for each patient using surface area (cm2) covered by the palms on a standardized template, as this anthropometric characteristic has been previously shown to influence OST results [17].

OST Measurements

OST measurements were performed using the HandScan® device (Demcon/Hemics), as described previously [17]. OST examinations were conducted by a trained rheumatology nurse who was blinded to all other study assessments using the HandScan® device. The nurse completed structured training provided by Demcon/Hemics, including certified workshops totalling approximately 16 h. In addition, the nurse had substantial prior experience with OST, with more than 5000 examinations performed to date. Although repeated measurements were not performed in this study, previous studies have shown good to excellent reproducibility of OST measurements, with intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) ranging from 0.76 to 0.98 [21, 24].

Importantly, the OST scores are automatically generated by the HandScan® system without examiner interference, using standardized signal acquisition and automated analysis, which substantially reduces the potential for operator-dependent interpretation bias.

During the OST assessment, participants were instructed to place both hands in a standardized resting position within the HandScan® device. Although pain or stiffness could theoretically affect positioning, the procedure is brief and non-invasive, thereby minimizing discomfort-related movement. In addition, the HandScan® software automatically generates a warning message when specific joints are not correctly detected or positioned, allowing repositioning if required. If warning messages persisted despite repositioning, the measurement was not performed. The examiner could adjust hand positioning if required. During the measurement, the finger and wrist joints are illuminated with red and infrared light at wavelengths of 660 and 808 nm. A complete measurement lasts approximately 100 s. Light transmission is continuously recorded using a complementary metal-oxide semiconductor camera. The measurement consisted of three phases: an initial phase with cuff inflation to 5 mmHg for 10 s, followed by a second phase in which pressure was increased to 55 mmHg for 60 s to induce venous blood pooling in the examined regions. In the final phase lasting 30 s, cuff pressure was reduced again to 5 mmHg, allowing reperfusion. Integrated software automatically defined regions of interest, including the wrists, metacarpophalangeal joints (MCP I–V), and proximal interphalangeal joints (PIP I–V), as well as reference areas that are located distal to the examined joints. A comparison between the blood flow in the region of interest (ROI) and the reference areas serves as a control mechanism for the presence of impaired or increased peripheral blood flow due to systemic factors such as body temperature, diabetes mellitus, nicotine consumption or vasoactive drugs [18].

The traditional OST algorithm included wrists, MCP I–V and PIP I–V (Fig. 1). For this study, an adapted algorithm including the DIP joints was calculated in cooperation with the manufacturing company. Both algorithms provide an OST score for each individual joint based on a semi-quantitative scale ranging from 0 to 3. The traditional total OST score is calculated as the weighted average of all evaluated joints (two wrists, 10 MCP and 10 PIP joints) multiplied by 22 [20]. To allow direct comparability with the conventional total OST index, the total OST score of the new algorithm is calculated as the weighted average of the assessed joints, additionally including 8 DIP, and likewise multiplied by 22. In this way, the new algorithm is normalized to the pre-existing 22 joints, ensuring that the magnitude semantics of the OST score do not change despite the inclusion of DIP joints [18].

Ultrasound Subgroup

Ultrasound examinations were performed by a blinded experienced examiner [K.T., Rheumatologist, certified trainer of the German Society of Ultrasound in Medicine (D.E.G.U.M.)], who was blinded to the results of the other study measurements. US examinations were performed using a MyLab9 device (Esaote®). A 4- to 15-MHz linear array transducer was used for assessment of the wrist joints, while an 18-MHz linear transducer was applied for the finger joints; both transducers were operating at their maximum frequency.

The ultrasound protocol was in accordance with the EULAR guidelines regarding the positioning of the hands and scanning planes [25, 26]. Both GSUS and PDUS images were performed for the dorsal aspects of each joint (MCPs, PIPs, DIPs and wrists (radiocarpal/midcarpal joint recesses). Colour gain was set at the disappearance of colour noise. The pulse repetition frequency was set as low as possible to maximize sensitivity for low-flow synovial signals, corresponding to approximately 750 Hz. Doppler frequency was set according to the musculoskeletal preset of the Esaote MyLab9 system and was 8.3 MHz for the wrist joints and 12.5 MHz for the finger joints.

The size and position of the colour box were adjusted to include the subcutaneous tissue to detect artefacts caused by vessels above the joint. PDUS assessment was performed according to the methodology originally described by Szkudlarek et al. and subsequently endorsed by the EULAR—Outcome Measures in Rheumatology (OMERACT) group [25, 27]. Synovitis was evaluated according to OMERACT definitions, with greyscale and power Doppler findings assessed and classified independently. Doppler activity was graded semi-quantitatively on a scale from 0 to 3: grade 0, no flow in the synovium; grade 1, presence of single vessel signals; grade 2, confluent vessel signals occupying less than half of the synovial area; and grade 3, vessel signals present in more than half of the synovium [25, 27]. In addition, synovial hypertrophy on GSUS was graded using a 0–3 scale, where grade 0 indicated no synovial thickening and grades 1–3 represented mild, moderate and severe synovial thickening, respectively [27]. As GSUS grade 1 findings are commonly observed in healthy controls [15], ultrasound-defined joint inflammation was defined as GSUS synovitis > 1 or PDUS synovitis > 0 [28].

Statistical Analysis

The assumption of normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when normally distributed, or as median with interquartile range (25th–75th percentiles) when distributions were skewed. Categorical variables are presented as absolute numbers (n) and percentages (%). Comparisons of categorical variables were conducted using the chi-square test. Differences between patients with OA and control subjects were analysed using the t test for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test when they were skewed. Associations between OST values and clinical characteristics were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) or Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ), as appropriate. Multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate differences in OST values between the OST and control groups while adjusting for variables that have been discussed as possible confounders of OST in previous works. Prior to analysis, assumptions of multiple linear regression—homoscedasticity, independence of observations, normality of residuals, and linearity—were formally assessed.

To evaluate the diagnostic performance of OST, receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analyses were conducted to compare the patient and the control group. Additional joint-level ROC analyses were performed for the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) and distal interphalangeal (DIP) joints, using ultrasound findings as the reference standard.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 23.0 (IBM Corp., USA). Differences yielding a p value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Study Populations

OST measurements, together with clinical and laboratory assessments, were carried out in 97 patients with OA [68% female; median age 68.0 years (IQR 58–76)] and in 100 control subjects [80% female; median age 49.0 years (IQR 33.5–56.0)]. A total of 2910 joints were examined by OST in the OA group and 3000 joints in the control group (MCP, PIP, DIP and wrist joints). Ultrasound examinations were performed on 870 joints in an OA subgroup. On average, the OST assessment took approximately 3 min per patient, whereas the ultrasound examination, including detailed scoring and documentation of the 30 assessed joints, required about 10–15 min. OST Values in Patients with OA vs. Controls

Mean OST values were significantly higher in patients with OA compared to controls (10.39 ± 1.82 vs. 7.51 ± 3.80; p < 0.001). Patient-related and anthropometric characteristics may influence OST values. In line with previous work [22] suggesting patient-adjusted OST interpretation models, hand size was systematically measured and included as a covariate in the adjusted analysis together with sex, age, BMI, diabetes mellitus and nicotine use. After adjustment, OST values remained significantly higher in patients with OA than in controls, indicating that the observed difference was not solely explained by anthropometric or epidemiological factors (1.698; 95% CI 0.149–3.247; padj = 0.032) (Supplementary Table S1). Furthermore, male participants demonstrated significantly higher OST values than female participants in both groups. In the OST-OA group, the mean OST value was 12.61 ± 4.55 in men compared with 10.16 ± 3.16 in women (p = 0.007). Similarly, in the control group, men exhibited higher mean OST values than women (12.31 ± 4.40 vs. 7.46 ± 3.50, p < 0.001).

Correlations with Clinical, Anthropometric, and Joint Ultrasound Parameters

Hand size showed a moderate correlation with OST in both the OA group (ρ = 0.363, p < 0.001) and the control group (ρ = 0.473, p < 0.001). In the joint ultrasound subgroup (n = 29), both the GSUS scores (median 0.00, IQR 0.00–3.5) and the PDUS scores (median 0.5, IQR 0.00–4.50) were low. When DIP and PIP joints were analysed together, a weak but statistically significant correlation was observed with PDUS (ρ = 0.108, p = 0.014), whereas no significant correlation was found with GSUS (ρ = 0.059, p = 0.178).

At the DIP joint level, OST demonstrated weak but statistically significant correlations with both GSUS (ρ = 0.187, p = 0.004) and PDUS (ρ = 0.217, p = 0.001). In contrast, OST at the PIP joints showed no significant correlation with either PDUS (ρ = 0.033, p = 0.577) or GSUS (ρ = − 0.037, p = 0.531).

The relationships between OST and age, BMI, visual analogue scale (VAS), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), tender joint count (TJC) and swollen joint count (SJC) did not reach the set level of statistical significance (all p > 0.05) (Table 2).

ROC Analysis

To assess the diagnostic performance of OST, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed comparing the OA group with the control group. The overall diagnostic performance of OST was moderate/significant, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.718 (95% CI 0.645–0.792; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Additionally, a ROC analysis was conducted to compare the diagnostic performance of OST with joint ultrasound. Ultrasound-assessed joint inflammation was defined as GSUS synovitis > 1 and PDUS synovitis > 0. Two joint categories were evaluated, specifically the PIP and DIP joints, which are most commonly affected by OA. For the DIP joints, an AUC of 0.782 (95% CI 0.579–0.984; p = 0.011) (Fig. 3a) was observed, while the PIP joints demonstrated the lowest diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.586 (95% CI 0.472–0.701; p = 0.471) (Fig. 3b).

Discussion

In the present study, we were able to demonstrate that OST is significantly altered in patients with hand OA compared with healthy controls. Furthermore, OST values correlated with both greyscale and power Doppler ultrasound markers of synovial activity, as well as with patient characteristics such as hand size.

To the best of our knowledge, only one previous study has examined OST in hand OA [23]. Moreover, the present study provides the largest systematic evaluation of OST in OA to date, incorporating both clinical and joint ultrasound activity markers in a degenerative arthropathy, and is among the very few OST studies to include evaluation of the DIP joints, as the HandScan system was primarily designed for assessment of the MCP, PIP and wrist joints [29].

The higher OST values in patients with OA than in controls indicate that degenerative joint disease may have a measurable impact on light transmission. Importantly, the present findings suggest that the OST signal in OA is likely influenced by both OA-related structural changes and inflammatory/vascular alterations, as reflected by the observed associations with greyscale and power Doppler ultrasound synovitis. Thus, OST does not appear to be specific solely to inflammatory synovitis as described in RA, and other inflammatory arthropathies, but also responds to the combined structural and vascular modifications characteristic of OA [17, 23]. Because OA affects joint architecture through osteophyte formation, cartilage loss, subchondral bone remodelling, and intermittent low-grade inflammation, these changes likely alter optical density and light scattering in a manner that may elevate OST readings.

In the past, several explorations, including those of our working group, have shown an overall good diagnostic performance of OST both at the patient and joint level in inflammatory arthropathies [17, 18, 21, 22, 30]. Similarly, in the present study, ROC analyses revealed a moderate discriminative performance of OST during the comparison of patients with OA and healthy controls. This finding is notable given that OA is traditionally considered a low-grade inflammatory disease and that synovitis in OA is often intermittent and less pronounced than in inflammatory arthritides [31]. Importantly, in our cohort, OST values remained statistically significantly higher in patients with OA compared to controls even after adjustment for possible confounders, indicating that OA-related changes influence OST beyond patient-associated characteristics.

At the joint level, the diagnostic performance of OST measurements compared to US (reference standard) differed across joint groups, with a higher performance at the DIP level and a lower performance at the PIP level. These findings can be discussed in the context of the OA study by Besselink et al., where OST performed fairly for all joints together (AUC–ROC 0.74) and for PIP joints (AUC–ROC 0.69), while a poor diagnostic performance was shown for DIP joints (AUC–ROC 0.54) [23]. In the present study, we observed higher discrimination at the DIP joint level and lower performance for PIP joints. Several reasons could explain this finding. First, joint-level performance of OST is likely influenced by the distribution and severity of synovitis, which may vary substantially across cohorts and joint groups. Second, the cohorts differed: Besselink et al. included patients with primary hand OA with at least one clinically inflamed hand or wrist joint, whereas in our study, OA inclusion was based on radiographic confirmation, and inflammatory activity on ultrasound was generally low [32, 33]. Different magnitudes of OA changes between DIP and PIP joints could also have played a role.

The observed associations of OST with both PDUS and GSUS are in line with the concept that vascularity changes (captured by PDUS) and synovial thickening/effusion (captured by GSUS) can both affect the intensity of transmitted light through the joint [17]. In prior OST work, established associations of OST with both GSUS and PDUS activity have suggested that not only vascularity changes but also synovial thickening and joint effusion could decrease the intensity of the transmitted light through the joint; thus, GSUS should also be taken into consideration when performing comparisons between OST and ultrasound [17]. In the OA setting, this appears particularly relevant, as OA synovitis may be subtle and intermittent and therefore may not be captured sufficiently by PDUS alone [34]. In the present cohort, GSUS scores were generally low, and PDUS scores were very low, which likely attenuated correlation strength and reduced joint-level discriminatory performance. This also provides a plausible explanation for the lack of associations with systemic inflammatory markers, as OA is typically not accompanied by relevant elevations in CRP or ESR. Moreover, clinical soft tissue swelling in OA is often mild, inconsistent and not necessarily driven by active synovitis; therefore, swollen joint counts may not reliably reflect local inflammatory activity and could explain the missing associations with swollen joint counts (SJC) [33]. Nevertheless, despite low Doppler activity, the finding of statistically significant OST associations with both PDUS and GSUS at the DIP level supports the hypothesis that OST may reflect OA changes, including both structural and inflammatory components.

Interestingly, our group was the first to report that epidemiologic and anthropometric characteristics may influence OST values [17]. The present findings confirm that OST is affected by patient characteristics and that accurate interpretation of OST results requires that these factors be taken into account. In this OA cohort, hand size showed a moderate correlation with OST in both patients with OA and controls, suggesting that anatomical factors influence light transmission through the hand and, consequently, light absorption. Similar findings have been reported previously in inflammatory cohorts, where hand size (and often sex and BMI) correlated with OST, and mechanisms for these associations were considered unclear. Also in this exploration, an interaction effect between sex and OST values, with men having higher values, was shown. One possible explanation is that individuals with larger hands possess more substantial bony, synovial or tendon structures, resulting in increased light absorption and consequently higher OST values [17]. Importantly, in our exploration, the difference between the OA group and the control group persisted after adjustment for relevant patient characteristics and possible confounders, indicating that OA-related joint changes contribute to OST beyond anthropometric and epidemiological patient attributes.

While OST offers several practical advantages, including operator independence, rapid acquisition and non-invasive, radiation-free imaging, it is important to recognize its limitations compared to established imaging modalities. Joint ultrasound offers a more detailed morpho-structural evaluation and allows direct assessment of synovial hypertrophy and vascularity. Moreover, MRI provides sensitive detection of early inflammatory and structural changes, but is costly and not feasible for frequent follow-up. In this context, OST should not be viewed as a replacement for these modalities but rather as a complementary tool, especially in settings where a rapid, examiner-independent assessment is desirable. In clinical practice, such an approach could be useful for objective screening, for identifying patients with possible OA-related inflammatory/vascular changes or for supporting monitoring strategies. However, longitudinal data in OA are currently missing and will be crucial to define the role of OST in follow-up.

The present study has some limitations. Joint ultrasound was incorporated at a later stage of the study, which resulted in a smaller joint ultrasound subgroup. However, patients were recruited consecutively, and the same patients who underwent OST also underwent ultrasound examinations. Moreover, since ultrasound inflammatory activity was generally low, an underestimation of the association between OST and synovitis cannot be ruled out. Another limitation is that the adapted DIP algorithm required manual delineation of regions of interest, which can be done accurately but still may allow small discrepancies between manually selected regions of interest and automatically detected ones [18]. Furthermore, no hand-size-normalized OST score is currently available for hand OA. Therefore, we avoided to apply an unvalidated correction formula. However, findings were adjusted and controlled for the effects of several patient characteristics. Future studies should develop and validate standardized patient-adjusted OST reference models, including anthropometric variables such as hand size, to improve comparability across individuals.

Moreover, the trained study nurse conducting the OST examinations was aware of the case status of the control group, as they were recruited from hospital staff and their acquaintances; however, she was blinded to their individual medical histories, and all other clinical data, and OST results were generated automatically by the HandScan device, minimizing examiner bias.

Finally, the practical implementation of OST may be influenced by equipment acquisition costs, maintenance requirements and local availability of the device. These factors should be considered when evaluating its potential role in clinical practice. However, compared with MRI, OST is expected to be substantially less resource-intensive, as it requires shorter examination times and does not involve the same infrastructure requirements.

Conclusions

This study showed that OST values were higher in patients with OA than those in healthy controls and that OST was associated with joint ultrasound markers of synovitis (GSUS/PDUS). Moreover, OST demonstrated a moderate capacity to distinguish patients with hand OA from healthy controls and may thus prove to be a valuable complementary tool in the care of patients with OA.

This study was conducted as an exploratory pilot study. Replication and confirmation of these results in future studies is warranted.
