Enantioselective Electrocatalytic Hydrogenation of α,β-Unsaturated Esters and Allylic Alcohols
Authors: Wentao Xu, Ping Hu, Wei Gu, Manni Nie, Hance Zhu, Xiaotian Qi, Qingquan Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Asymmetric electrocatalytic hydrogenation has long been of interest in the field of electrosynthesis. Nevertheless, explorations of highly enantioselective asymmetric electrocatalytic hydrogenation reactions are rare. Herein, we report the enantioselective electrocatalytic hydrogenation of conjugated olefins with high enantioselectivity. The catalyst precursor comprises cost-effective, earth-abundant nickel with a strongly σ-donating bis-phosphine (Me-DuPhos) ligand, and protons and electrons are used together directly as the hydrogen source. This strategy avoids the need for highly pressurized H2, sensitive hydride sources, and expensive noble transition metals. Mechanistic studies confirm that suitable ligands must sufficiently stabilize the M–H intermediate to outcompete the hydrogen evolution reaction and avoid nickel deposition on the cathode. Optimal ligand properties are therefore crucial to promote reaction efficiency and maximize enantioselectivity. In addition, DFT calculations indicate that Ni(I)–H species might be the active intermediate and the enantiocontrol is determined by the stabilizing dispersion interaction between the substrate and the nickel(I) hydride.

Introduction

Electrochemical methods offer a controllable and infinite redox range, thereby enabling unique reactivity and flexibility that would otherwise be impractical to access1–5. Recent research has demonstrated that electrogenerated M–H species can effectively catalyze the electrohydrogenation of alkenes6–10, alkynes7, nitriles11, and arenes12. In this context, electrocatalytic hydrogenation using protons and electrons directly as hydrogen sources (rather than stoichiometric hydride reductants, and highly pressurized gas which is associated with serious safety concerns during transport, storage, and use) represents an attractive strategy for developing cost-effective and sustainable hydrogenation reactions13,14. Typically, electrons supplied at the cathode reduce high-valent metal ions to low-valent metal ions, which interact with protons to form critical M–H intermediates15,16. Subsequently, M–H species either participate in the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) or are intercepted by unsaturated compounds to achieve electrohydrogenation/hydrofunctionalization. Although seemingly straightforward, this concept has never been realized, and explorations of highly enantioselective asymmetric electrocatalytic hydrogenation of olefins have been rare since the pioneering effort in 1967 (Fig. 1)17–24. This may be because bulky chiral metal hydride complexes may favor the HER by interacting with sterically unhindered protons rather than hydrogenation reactions with sterically demanding substrates; this is a well-established process in sustainable energy conversion system15,16. In addition, direct proton reduction at the cathode is thermodynamically favorable (ΔGHER = − 20 to −60 kcal/mol)25, leading to hydrogen evolution and competing with the desired hydrogenation reaction. To address these challenges, it is crucial to select a suitable ligand that can stabilize the M–H intermediate while enhancing substrate-catalyst interactions. Moreover, the well-documented matched-mismatched effects, which depend on the chiral ligands structure and the metal center, also have a role in dictating the reaction efficiency and enantioselectivity26,27. Therefore, both the metal catalyst and its chiral ligand are key factors influencing asymmetric electrohydrogenation reactions.Fig. 1Electrohydrogenation of olefins through M-H species.a Electrohydrogenation of olefins mediated by M-H. b This work: Asymmertric electrohydrogenation of trisubstituted olefins.

Herein, we show an enantioselective electrocatalytic hydrogenation of trisubstituted olefins using earth-abundant nickel as the catalyst precursor and a strong σ-donating bisphosphine ligand, with protons and electrons serving as the sole hydrogen source (Fig. 1b). A range of α,β-unsaturated esters and allylic alcohols are efficiently hydrogenated with enantiomeric excess up to 97%.

Results and discussion

The present study began by investigating the electrochemical formation of M–H species. The target Ni–H species were detected by high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) after electrolyzing a bis-ligated Ni complex 1 [(L1)2NiI2)] in the presence of acetic acid (details are provided in the Supplementary Information (SI), Figs. S2–S4). Additionally, a distinct 1H-NMR signal at −13.1 ppm was observed, consistent with reported Ni–H complexes28, further confirming the electrolytic generation of an Ni–H species (Fig. 2a).Fig. 2Experimental verification.a Characterization of electrochemically generated Ni–H intermediates. b Preliminary experimental results. c Cyclic voltammetry of 1a.

Ethyl (E)−3-([1,1’-biphenyl]−4-yl)but-2-enoate (1a) was selected as the model substrate to assess the activity of the electrogenerated Ni–H species29–32. Notably, the carbonyl functionality of 1a allows the olefin to coordinate in a bidentate fashion, thus strengthening its interaction with the chiral catalyst33–35. The bis-ligated Ni–H species exhibits very low reactivity for the hydrogenation of olefins (Fig. 2b, entry 1), likely because the sterically hindered ligand environment disrupts substrate-catalyst interactions. The desired electrohydrogenation product 1 was obtained in 57% yield when a monoligated Ni complex-2 (L1NiI2) was used. When other bis(arylphosphine) ligands were used (e.g., L2), the conversion of 1a was relatively low (21%), indicating that HER dominated the cathode reactivity. These results support the utility of a strongly σ-donating diphosphine ligand that can provide sufficient steric hindrance to stabilize the Ni–H intermediate. Such ligand structures promote catalytic hydrogenation activity to ensure the reaction’s success. In addition, the very negative reduction potential of 1a (−1.87 V vs Ag/AgCl; Fig. 2c) suppresses interference from background reactions involving the direct electroreduction of olefins via electron transfer-proton transfer (ET-PT).

Investigation of the reaction conditions

Having established the proof of concept, the enantioselective electrocatalytic hydrogenation of conjugated olefin 1a was optimized. The corresponding hydrogenated products, i.e., chiral dihydrocinnamates, are important integral subunits in pharmaceuticals36–39 and natural products40–42, as well as building blocks in synthetic chemistry43. As shown in Table 1 (for details, see Table S1 in the SI), the solvent significantly influenced the enantioselectivity (entries 1–4). The weakly coordinating solvent 2-Me-THF provided higher enantioselectivity than N,N-dimethylacetamide (DMAc) and acetonitrile. A solvent mixture of 2-Me-THF/isopropanol further increased the reaction yield to 87% with 96% ee (entry 5). For other bis(arylphosphine) ligands, e.g., L2 and L3, the conversion of 1a was low (11% and 14%), indicating that HER was the dominant reaction at the cathode (entries 6 and 7, respectively). When the sterically encumbered Et-DuPhos (L4) was used in place of L1, the yield of 1 decreased significantly to 67%, albeit with a slight increase in enantioselectivity (98% ee, entry 8). In addition, the proton source was crucial for the reaction efficiency and enantioselectivity. When using TFA with a lower pKa, the protons are more susceptible to participating in the reductive hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) at the cathode, and these acidic protons are more likely to react with the nickel-hydride intermediate, resulting in a marked decrease in yield (entry 9). These results demonstrated that a weakly acidic proton source could suppress the undesired HER, thereby enhancing both the reaction efficiency and selectivity. Scope of substrates

The optimized reaction conditions were used to explore the scope of the asymmetric electrohydrogenation of minimally functionalized olefins (Fig. 3). A range of (E)−3-methylcinnamates having 3-aryl groups with either electron-donating groups (e.g., NR2, SMe, OR, alkyl) or electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., CF3, CO2Et, OCF3, amide) were hydrogenated with good to excellent enantioselectivity (2-14). Naphthyl (15) was also well tolerated in this protocol. Electron-poor heteroarenes, e.g., pyridine, remained intact after electrolysis (16), thus highlighting the potential applicability of this method for preparing more complex molecules. Cyclic olefins were also hydrogenated efficiently to afford tetraline derivatives in good yields with high enantioselectivity (17, 18). A series of cinnamates with various substituents (e.g., methyl, ethyl, isopropyl, benzyl) were compatible with the protocol and gave the corresponding products (19–22) in 52% to 85% yields with 92% to 97% ee. Notably, trimethylene oxide was preserved after the electrolysis (23). Cinnamates with an alkyl group at the β-position were also suitable substrates for this reaction, giving the corresponding hydrogenated products in good yields with 95% to 97% enantioselectivity (24–27).Fig. 3Substrate scope.Standard conditions: 1a (0.3 mmol, 1.0 equiv.), NiBr2·DME (10 mol%), L1 (12 mol%), TBAI (20 mol%), HOAc (10 equiv.), nBu4NBF4 (3.0 equiv.), anhydrous 2-Me-THF (7.5 mL), anhydrous iPrOH (0.5 mL), 1.0 mA, 50 h, argon, RT, Mg plate (1.5 cm × 1.5 cm × 0.5 mm) as the anode and graphite foam (1.5 cm × 1.5 cm × 3.0 mm) as the cathode, undivided cell. The developed enantioselective electrocatalytic hydrogenation reaction was also applicable to late-stage modifications of various pharmaceutical and natural product derivatives. A series of olefins derived from piperonyl alcohol, pyrrolidine, thiomorpholine and morpholine reacted smoothly following this protocol, furnishing the corresponding hydrogenated products with excellent yields and good enantioselectivity (28–31). To explore the practicality of the reaction, mmol-scale reactions were performed. The target products 1 and 7 were isolated in 71% to 81% yields with 96% ee (for details, see section 2.5 in the SI). Notably, the 7 is a key intermediate in the synthesis of various natural products, such as (-)-dehydrocurcumene, (-)-nuciferol, and (-)-ar-tumerone, among others44,45. Meanwhile, in order to determine the absolute configuration of the hydrogenation product, ethyl (R)−3-phenylbutanoate was synthesized using (R)−2-benzylpropanoic acid and ethanol. By comparing the HPLC profiles of ethyl (R)−3-phenylbutanoate, ethyl 3-phenylbutanoate, and the asymmetric hydrogenation product 9, the absolute configuration of the product was determined to be R (for details, see section 2.10 in the SI).

Furthermore, replacing the ester group in the olefin with a hydroxyl group also allows formation of the desired hydrogenated products; 32-35 were isolated in good yields with good enantioselectivity (Fig. 4). These compounds can be further transformed into bioactive molecules (e.g., (R)-RC-33)46 and high-value chemicals (e.g., Florhydral@)47. Meanwhile, by comparing the HPLC data of (R)−3-([1,1’-biphenyl]−4-yl)butan-1-ol, racemic 3-([1,1’-biphenyl]−4-yl)butan-1-ol, and product 32, the absolute configuration of the hydrogenated product was confirmed as the R configuration (for details, see Figs. S22–S24 in the SI). Attempts to further expand the substrate scope, using α,β-unsaturated nitriles, amides, or free acids, proved challenging for achieving efficient asymmetric hydrogenation of the corresponding alkenes (for details, see Fig. S31a in the SI). Mechanistic studies

Mechanistic investigations (Fig. 5) involving electrolysis on/off interval experiments demonstrated that continuous electrolysis was crucial for enantioselectivity in the developed system (Fig. 5a). These results suggested that the electrical current is the driving force for the electrocatalytic hydrogenation. The cathodic potential was maintained at approximately −1.60 V vs Ag/AgCl throughout the electrolysis (Fig. 5b). This is consistent with electroreduction of the nickel complex 2, rather than direct electroreduction of the olefin, thus ruling out the possibility that nickel acts as a chiral Lewis acid to induce enantioselectivity. Assessments of nonlinear effects revealed a linear relationship between the enantiopurities of L1 and the hydrogenated product 1, indicating that a monomeric nickel complex bearing a single chiral phosphine ligand was involved in the enantioselectivity-determining step (Fig. 5c)48. The cyclic voltammetry of (S,S)-Me-DuPhos·NiI2 has two reductive peaks, which were attributed to the Ni(II/I) and Ni(I/0) redox couples (Fig. 5d). Furthermore, the addition of acetic acids to the nickel complex solution increased the current associated with these redox peaks. The redox peaks were further enhanced following the addition of olefin 1a (Fig. 5e). These observations are consistent with the transient formation of a Ni–H species via protonation of a low-valent nickel intermediate, followed by olefin hydrogenation. In addition, the weakly coordinating carcarbonyl group in the olefin was essential for the reaction. Most likely, this carbonyl coordinated with the chiral catalyst to facilitate the subsequent hydrogenation process. This hypothesis was supported by the absence of product formation when using a non-coordinating olefin, i.e., (E)−1-methoxy-4-(pent-2-en-2-yl)benzene (36a). The exceptionally low conversion of 36a indicated that the nickel catalyst predominantly facilitated the HER, rather than olefin hydrogenation (Fig. 5f). Finally, replacing acetic acid with molecular hydrogen did not increase the yield or ee of hydrogenated product 1, thus excluding hydrogen gas generated in situ as the proton source (Fig. 5g; further details are provided in the SI, Section 2.9).Fig. 5Mechanistic studies.a Electrolysis on/off interval experiments. b Electrode potential over the course of electrolysis. c Nonlinear effect study of L1. d Cyclic voltammetry of (S,S)-Me-DuPhos·NiI2 (Ni-complex 2). e The effects of HOAc and 1a on the square wave voltammetry of Ni-complex 2. f Hydrogenation of olefins without the weakly coordinating group. g Hydrogenation of olefins with H2 replacing acetic acid. h SEM images of the pre-reaction cathode. i SEM images and EDS of the post-reaction cathode. Considering the ease of Ni(II) reduction at the cathode, it is reasonable to assume that Ni(II) cations could be deposited on the cathode, thereby enabling a heterogeneous metal nanoparticle-driven catalytic process49. Scanning electron microscopic (SEM) and energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) analysis of the cathode after the reaction indicated that there was no nickel deposition on the cathode (Fig. 5h, i). These results excluded a heterogeneous electrohydrogenation process on a coated cathode. Furthermore, control experiments on proton sources (for details, see Table S6 in the SI), reaction progress kinetic analysis (for details, see Tables S7–S11 and Figs. S15–S18 in the SI), differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (for details, see Fig. S25 in the SI), and cyclic voltammetry experiments on the interaction between Mg(ClO4)2 and 1a (for details, see Fig. S30 in the SI) provide effective supplementary evidence for elucidating the reaction mechanism.

Based on these experimental results, a plausible mechanism is proposed in Fig. 6. First, the monoligated Ni(II) complex is reduced at the cathode to form a low-valent nickel complex, which reacts with HOAc to furnish the Ni–H species. The Ni–H coordinates with the olefin, followed by an enantioselective conjugate addition, leading to the formation of a nickel-enolate intermediate. Finally, the nonselective protonation of the nickel-enolate intermediate releases the desired hydrogenated product and regenerates the nickel catalyst.Fig. 6Proposed mechanism.The possible mechanism for enantioselective electrocatalytic hydrogenation.

To elucidate the enantioselectivity control, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out to investigate the olefin migratory insertion with nickel-hydride species. As shown in Fig. 7a, coordination of olefin 1a to the Ni(I)-H species 37 give the tetra-coordinated Ni(I)-H intermediates 38 or 39. The formation of Ni(I)-H intermediate 38 is exergonic by 2.3 kcal/mol while the formation Ni(I)-H intermediate 37 is endergonic by 1.3 kcal/mol. Subsequent migratory insertion prefers to occur via a four-membered cyclic transition state TS-1R with an activation free energy of 4.8 kcal/mol. The S-pathway through TS-1S is kinetically less favored by 1.7 kcal/mol. This result is consistent with experimental observation. The nickel(II)-hydride mediated migratory insertion mechanism was also taken into consideration (Fig. 7b). The activation free energies of TS-2S and TS-2R are much higher than that of TS-1S and TS-1R. Moreover, the outcome of enantiocontrol in this case is in contradiction with the experimental results. The structural analysis of TS-1S and TS-1R unveils that the aromatic hydrogen of the olefin fragment in TS-1S is closer to the nickel hydride fragment than that in TS-1R (Fig. 7c), which could cause more significant steric repulsion. Distortion-interaction analysis suggests that the higher energy of TS-1S mainly originates from the smaller interaction energy between the olefin fragment and the nickel hydride fragment (ΔΔEint = 2.6 kcal/mol, Fig. 7d). To ascertain the factor dominating the interaction energy (ΔEint), quantitative steric-electronic effect dissection (QSED)50 is carried out through the marriage of distortion-interaction analysis and energy decomposition analysis (EDA). In this way, the explicit contributions from distortion (ΔEdist), Pauli repulsion (ΔEPauli), electrostatics (ΔEelstat), polarization (ΔEpol), charge transfer (ΔEct), and dispersion interaction (ΔEdisp) to the energy difference between the two transition states are determined. As shown in the pie chart (Fig. 7d), the sector corresponding to the dispersion interaction has the largest proportion. Therefore, the greater dispersion interaction is the dominant factor that favors TS-1R. In summary, this report describes a novel approach for attaining the highly enantioselective electrocatalytic hydrogenation of olefins. The electroreductive strategy developed herein directly employs protons and electrons as the hydrogen sources, without requiring expensive silanes/boranes or highly pressurized H2. Mechanistic studies suggest that a nickel hydride species generated on the cathode is a key intermediate. Moreover, a monomeric nickel complex bearing a single chiral phosphine ligand is involved in the enantioselectivity-determining step. Furthermore, DFT calculations reveal that the enantioselectivity is attributed primarily to the substrate–nickel(I) hydride interaction energy, even though the possibility of a nickel(II) hydride pathway cannot be entirely ruled out. A series of α,β-unsaturated esters were well tolerated in the developed protocol. Research on the asymmetric electrohydrogenation of other unsaturated bonds is ongoing in our laboratory.

Methods

General procedure

In an oven-dried two-necked backflow cell (20 mL) equipped with a Teflon-coated magnetic stir bar and two electrodes (Mg plate (+) (15 mm × 15 mm × 0.5 mm) and graphite felt (-) (15 mm × 15 mm × 3 mm)). Olefin (0.3 mmol, 1.0 equiv.), TBAI (20 mol%, 22.2 mg) and nBu4NBF4 (0.9 mmol, 3.0 equiv., 296.3 mg) were added in a glovebox. The reaction cell was sealed and moved out from the glovebox. Afterwards, the pre-catalyst solution (it was prepared by a mix of NiBr2·DME (12 mol%, 9.3 mg) and L1 (12 mol%, 11.1 mg) in anhydrous 2-Me-THF (7.5 mL) under argon atmosphere, which was stirred for 10 min.), isopropyl alcohol (0.5 mL) and AcOH (3.0 mmol, 10.0 equiv., 177 μL) were added to the reaction cell via syringe. The reaction mixture was stirred for 3 min and was electrolyzed at a constant current of 1.0 mA until passing 6.2 F/mol of charge (around 50 h) at room temperature. After the reaction, the reaction mixture was concentrated under reduced pressure (the residual product on electrodes was rinsed with ethyl acetate), and purified by silica gel column chromatography (eluted with ethyl acetate/petroleum ether) to afford the pure product. Further details regarding the methods can be found in the supplemental information.
