Earthquake preparedness in the context of Maslow’s hierarchy of needs theory and the risk perception paradox: the role of socioeconomic inequalities in Türkiye
Authors: Ali Utku Şahin, Hasan Yücel, Bircan Kara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarthquake preparedness in Türkiye is widely promoted through national awareness campaigns; however, household-level preparedness remains limited. Drawing on Maslow’s hierarchy of needs and the Risk Perception Paradox, this study examines how socioeconomic inequalities are associated with earthquake preparedness self-efficacy, preparedness behaviors, and perceived barriers and motivations at the household level.MethodsThe research universe was defined using the Türkiye Earthquake Hazard Map by identifying 325 districts located in high-hazard zones (≥ 0.4 g at DD-2 design value, 475-year return period). A 19-item structured questionnaire was developed and administered online between May and September 2025, yielding responses from 1,193 households across high-risk districts. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, cross-tabulations, and chi-square tests (p < 0.05).ResultsThe findings indicate high earthquake salience: 80.9% of respondents reported prior earthquake experience and 93.1% perceived local earthquake risk. However, only 19.3% considered themselves sufficiently prepared, while 47.9% reported being partially prepared. Perceived constraints were substantial: 89.7% reported insufficient financial resources, 83.8% insufficient time, and 69.2% concerns about sustaining daily life. Preparedness self-efficacy was significantly associated with gender, age, and monthly household income. Training participation was also significantly associated with higher self-perceived preparedness, while homeownership was associated with greater knowledge of building earthquake resistance.ConclusionsHousehold earthquake preparedness in Türkiye appears to be constrained not only by awareness deficits but also by socioeconomic inequalities related to time, financial resources, and housing security. These findings suggest that awareness campaigns should be complemented by targeted education, financial support mechanisms, tenant-inclusive housing safety measures, and community-based preparedness practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28211-5.

Introduction

Disasters are increasingly understood not as the direct result of hazards alone, but as outcomes produced through the interaction of hazards with exposure, vulnerability, and capacity conditions [1, 2]. From this perspective, earthquake risk is not determined only by seismic hazard, but also by the social and economic conditions that influence who is exposed, what resources households possess, and how effectively they can take protective action. This approach is particularly important for household earthquake preparedness, because preparedness requires not only awareness but also time, financial resources, housing security, and access to reliable information.

Social vulnerability literature consistently shows that disaster risk is unevenly distributed across income, education, employment, gender, housing, and access-to-resource lines [3–8]. Socioeconomically disadvantaged groups may face greater difficulty in translating risk awareness into preparedness behavior, even when they recognize the hazard. Recent disaster risk studies therefore challenge the assumption that “more information” automatically produces “more preparedness” and emphasize the role of cost, time, institutional trust, and everyday survival constraints in household-level action [9].

Türkiye provides a critical context for examining this relationship. Located in a highly seismic region, the country has experienced severe earthquake losses, and earthquakes remain the dominant natural hazard in national risk profiles [10, 11]. Since the 1999 earthquakes, Türkiye has adopted an integrated disaster management approach and has implemented national awareness campaigns to encourage individual and household preparedness [12–15]. However, the persistence of low preparedness at the household level suggests that public awareness alone may not be sufficient to overcome the structural barriers that constrain action.

The Risk Perception Paradox offers a useful framework for understanding this gap. Wachinger et al. [16] define the paradox as the situation in which individuals perceive a risk but do not take corresponding protective measures. This may occur when individuals accept the risk, transfer responsibility to authorities, or lack sufficient resources to act. In the Turkish context, Şahin [17] previously argued that resource scarcity and more immediate daily concerns may help explain why high risk perception does not necessarily translate into preparedness behavior.

Maslow’s hierarchy of needs provides an additional interpretive lens for this argument. Maslow [18] identifies physiological and safety needs as basic needs. When households struggle to meet daily needs or maintain economic security, disaster preparedness may be postponed even when the risk is recognized. This does not mean that Maslow’s hierarchy is measured as a psychometric scale in the present study. Rather, it is used as a theoretical framework for interpreting how basic-needs pressure, safety concerns, and socioeconomic constraints may influence the prioritization of earthquake preparedness. In this sense, the study draws on Maslow’s hierarchy as a conceptual framework for interpreting how unmet basic needs may influence preparedness priorities, rather than as a direct empirical measurement of hierarchical need structures.

Türkiye’s socioeconomic conditions further justify this focus. Recent income and poverty statistics indicate substantial income inequality and economic vulnerability, with one-fifth of the population classified as poor according to the 60% median-income threshold [19, 20]. These conditions suggest that the Risk Perception Paradox may operate not only as a cognitive or perceptual problem, but also as a socioeconomic one. Accordingly, this study aims to examine the relationship between households’ socioeconomic characteristics, earthquake risk perception, preparedness self-efficacy, perceived barriers, motivations, and preparedness behaviors in Türkiye.

Methodology

Study design

This study is a cross-sectional analytical study designed to examine associations between socioeconomic variables and earthquake preparedness outcomes. Data were collected through a structured online survey administered to a geographically dispersed sample of households in seismically active regions of Türkiye. Because data collection was conducted online, the sample should not be interpreted as fully representative of the general Turkish population.

Research universe and sample selection

As the research study conducted is related to earthquake risk, according to the Türkiye Earthquake Hazard Map [21] made available by the Presidency of Disaster and Emergency Management, coordinates with earthquake risk of 0.4 g or higher at a DD-2 design value and a 475-year period were identified, and the 325 different points identified were listed according to which province and district they belonged to.

Subsequently, based on the population data for 2025 published by the Turkish Statistical Institute [22] and the average household size per province [23], the research universe was established by determining the number of households in 325 different districts.

This resulted in the creation of the “Structured Data Collection Form on the Effect of Socioeconomic Status on Individual Preparedness for Earthquakes.” To establish content validity, the draft was submitted to an expert panel (n = 5) consisting of academics specialized in disaster management and social sciences. In this evaluation, a threshold-based decision process was followed: items scoring 9 and 10 were accepted directly, items scoring between 5 and 8 were revised based on qualitative expert recommendations, and items scoring below 5 were considered for exclusion. Consequently, items that required modification were refined in accordance with the experts’ feedback to ensure structural and semantic accuracy. Notably, preparedness self-efficacy was assessed using a global single-item measure. While multi-item scales provide granular detail, this approach was selected to maximize the response rate and minimize respondent fatigue within a large national sample facing diverse socioeconomic challenges. Literature confirms that global single-item measures can effectively capture holistic self-perceptions in disaster preparedness contexts. To finalize the instrument, a pilot test was conducted with a small group of 20 participants to verify cognitive clarity and response duration. Following the pilot test, necessary refinements were implemented based on the feedback received from the pilot participants regarding the clarity of certain questions.

Ethical considerations and data collection procedure

After these final adjustments, the “Structured Data Collection Form on the Effect of Socioeconomic Status on Individual Preparedness for Earthquakes” was deployed. The data collection form states that participation in the study is voluntary and that no personally identifiable information is collected. Ethical approval for the use of this form was obtained from the Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee of Social Sciences and Humanities of Hatay Mustafa Kemal University. Data was collected online between May 2025 and September 2025. The online survey method was preferred because the study targeted geographically dispersed households across 325 high-risk districts in Türkiye. Conducting face-to-face sampling across such a large area would have required substantial financial and logistical resources beyond the scope of this unfunded study. Therefore, online data collection provided a practical way to reach a large number of households within a limited time frame. The data collection process was terminated once data from 1,193 households was collected, thereby significantly exceeding the minimum required sample size and ensuring robust statistical power.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics including frequencies (n) and percentages (%) to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics and survey responses. Cross-tabulations and chi-square tests were performed to examine associations between categorical variables. To assess the practical significance of these associations, Cramer’s V values were calculated and interpreted as effect size measures (negligible: <0.10; small: 0.10–0.29; medium: 0.30–0.49; large: ≥0.50).

It should be noted that multiple chi-square tests were conducted without adjustment for multiple comparisons. While this approach may increase the risk of Type I error, the bivariate analyses were conducted for exploratory purposes to identify candidate variables for multivariate modeling.

In addition, a binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify the independent predictors of earthquake preparedness while controlling for potential confounding variables. The dependent variable was self-rated earthquake preparedness, coded as 1 = “prepared” and 0 = “not prepared or partially prepared.” Variables that were theoretically relevant and/or statistically significant in the bivariate analyses were included in the regression model. The results were presented as Odds Ratios (ORs) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs). Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, Nagelkerke R², and the area under the ROC curve (AUC). All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0. Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05.

The descriptive findings indicate a clear gap between earthquake risk awareness and household preparedness. Most respondents reported previous earthquake experience either personally or through someone close to them (80.9%), and a large majority perceived earthquake risk in their area of residence (93.1%). However, only 19.3% considered themselves sufficiently prepared, while 47.9% reported being partially prepared. This pattern is consistent with the Risk Perception Paradox, as high perceived risk coexists with limited self-reported preparedness. The most frequently reported barriers were insufficient financial resources (89.7%), insufficient time (83.8%), and concerns about sustaining daily life and meeting basic needs (69.2%).

The analyses were organized around seven outcome domains: preparedness self-efficacy, perceived barriers to preparedness, perceived factors contributing to preparedness, motivations for better preparedness, the relationship between training and self-perceived preparedness, the relationship between homeownership and perceived building safety, and reported preparedness behaviors.

Factors contributing to preparedness - socioeconomic and demographic data

Source: Authors’ own work. Effect size interpretation based on Cramer’s V: Negligible (< 0.10), Small (0.10–0.29), Medium (0.30–0.49), Large (≥ 0.50)

Chi-square analyses (Tables 4 and 5) show that self-efficacy and barriers differ significantly according to sociodemographic variables. Men, young adults (aged 18–25), and high-income households (≥ 120,001 TL) reported significantly higher self-efficacy, though the effect sizes were relatively small (Cramer’s V = 0.085 for gender, V = 0.093 for age, V = 0.106 for income). In contrast, concerns about sustaining daily life and resource inadequacy were most pronounced among men, full-time workers, and middle-low income groups (40,000–80,000 TL), with small to moderate effect sizes (Cramer’s V = 0.169 for gender, V = 0.136 for age, V = 0.166 for income, V = 0.104 for employment). While information stood out as the key factor enabling preparedness for full-time workers (68.7%) (Table 6), motivations for increasing preparedness differed: Men preferred financial incentives, while women and highly educated groups strongly supported making preparedness a legal requirement.

Participant motivations for better earthquake preparedness varied significantly across sociodemographic lines. While men were more likely to favor financial incentives, women and highly educated individuals strongly advocated for making preparedness a legal requirement and organizing awareness campaigns. Furthermore, the highest income group (≥ 120,000 TRY) prioritized the frequency of local training, demonstrating a small but meaningful effect (Cramer’s V = 0.112), whereas vulnerable groups living in dormitories or shelters emphasized the absolute necessity of meeting basic daily needs before considering disaster preparedness (Cramer’s V = 0.111) (Table 7).

A statistically significant association was observed between receiving earthquake preparedness training and preparedness self-efficacy.; participants with training predominantly rated themselves as fully or partially prepared (Cramer’s V = 0.222, indicating a small effect size). Although participation in official training programs was relatively common across various age groups (Cramer’s V = 0.158), full-time employees (Cramer’s V = 0.160), and homeowners (Cramer’s V = 0.169), a critical gap remains: approximately one-third of full-time workers and tenants reported receiving no education at all. Homeownership was also significantly associated with knowledge regarding structural safety. Over half of the homeowners were confident in their building’s earthquake resistance, whereas the vast majority of tenants either had no information or knew their buildings were non-resistant (Cramer’s V = 0.194, small effect) (Table 8).

Specific preparedness behaviors are deeply stratified by demographics. Women were significantly more proactive in practical measures, such as designating emergency contacts, preparing earthquake kits, learning fire extinguishment, and knowing the locations of utility valves, though the effect sizes were generally small (Cramer’s V ranging from 0.060 to 0.130). Increases in age and education correlated with a higher likelihood of purchasing earthquake insurance, mapping escape routes, and managing utility valves, with small effect sizes (Cramer’s V = 0.122–0.236 for education, V = 0.122–0.125 for age), though younger adults [18–25] were the most likely to undergo search and rescue training. Finally, financial capacity appeared to be associated with the type of preparedness behavior reported by respondents: while middle-income earners and full-time workers focused on low-cost practical steps like locating utility switches (Cramer’s V = 0.116), the highest income groups and property owners dominated cost-intensive measures, predominantly securing furniture and purchasing earthquake insurance. Notably, property ownership demonstrated the strongest effect on earthquake insurance acquisition (Cramer’s V = 0.330, representing a medium effect size—the only medium-sized effect observed across all preparedness behaviors). However, for low-cost practices like preparing disaster kits or family plans, tenants and owners showed similar behavioral rates (Table 9). To identify the independent predictors of earthquake preparedness, a binary logistic regression analysis was conducted. The dependent variable was self-rated earthquake preparedness, coded as 1 = “prepared” and 0 = “not prepared or partially prepared.” The model included sociodemographic variables (gender, age, education, income, housing status), earthquake-related variables (prior earthquake experience, risk perception, disaster training, building resilience perception), and perceived barriers grounded in Maslow’s hierarchy of needs framework (insufficient information, time, resources, trust in government, and basic needs concerns).

Three variables emerged as significant independent predictors of earthquake preparedness:

Disaster training was the strongest predictor. Participants who had received any form of disaster preparedness training were nearly three times more likely to report being prepared compared to those who had not received training (OR = 2.776, 95% CI = 1.895–4.065, p < 0.001).

Perception of building resilience was also a strong predictor. Participants who believed their building was earthquake-resistant were more than three times more likely to report being prepared compared to those who did not know their building’s resilience status (OR = 3.151, 95% CI = 2.251–4.410, p < 0.001).

Basic needs concerns emerged as a significant barrier. Participants who reported that concerns about sustaining daily life hindered their preparedness efforts were approximately half as likely to report being prepared (OR = 0.528, 95% CI = 0.380–0.733, p < 0.001).

Notably, sociodemographic variables such as gender, age, education, income, and housing status did not emerge as significant independent predictors in the multivariate model, although some had shown significant bivariate associations. Similarly, prior earthquake experience and risk perception were not significant predictors when other variables were controlled.

Our findings indicate that participants’ levels of “preparedness self-efficacy” differ significantly according to socioeconomic and demographic characteristics. Accordingly, self-efficacy is associated with gender, age, and monthly household income variables, though with relatively small effect sizes (Cramer’s V = 0.085 for gender, V = 0.093 for age, V = 0.106 for income); it is understood that men, the 18–25 age group, and the income group of 120,001 TRY and above consider themselves more competent in terms of earthquake preparedness. In terms of factors preventing preparedness, significant differences were found in gender, age, employment status, and income variables, with small effect sizes (Cramer’s V = 0.169 for gender, V = 0.136 for age, V = 0.104 for employment, V = 0.166 for income). While men stated that they were unable to prepare due to concerns about sustaining daily life and lack of financial resources, concerns about meeting basic needs and lack of information were prominent in all age groups. The income variable yielded the expected results; concerns about daily life, financial inadequacy, and lack of information were found to be more prevalent among those earning between 40,000 and 80,000 TRY, which is below the poverty line. In terms of the reasons for being prepared, a significant difference was found only in employment status (Cramer’s V = 0.184, small effect); 68.7% of full-time workers stated that having sufficient information was the main reason for being prepared. It can be argued that these findings are meaningful in the context of gender roles, the risk perception paradox, and the hierarchy of needs.

Significant differences were found in the relationship between factors affecting participants’ motivation to better prepare for earthquakes and socioeconomic and demographic variables, specifically in terms of gender, educational status, income, and home ownership, though most effect sizes were negligible to small (Cramer’s V ranging from 0.066 to 0.112). Accordingly, men indicated at a higher rate that their tendency to prepare would increase if financial support/incentives were provided, while women preferred at a higher rate that preparation be made a legal requirement.

As educational levels increase, there is a growing tendency to prefer awareness campaigns and the legal obligation option. A significant relationship was observed between receiving earthquake preparedness training and self-efficacy (Cramer’s V = 0.222, small effect); participants who received training were more likely to assess themselves as fully or partially prepared. This finding suggests that preparedness training was significantly associated with higher preparedness self-efficacy. However, because the study is cross-sectional and based on self-reported preparedness, this association should not be interpreted as evidence that training directly caused higher preparedness.

A significant difference was also found between home ownership and knowledge of the earthquake resistance of the building in which one lives (Cramer’s V = 0.194, small effect); property owners were more likely to state that their buildings had been inspected and were resistant, while tenants were more likely to respond “I don’t know” or “not resistant.” It could be argued that this situation may be related to socioeconomic factors. This pattern may indicate that tenants have less access to reliable information about building safety or less autonomy in housing-related decisions. However, whether limited housing choice directly explains this pattern requires further investigation.

Finally, earthquake preparedness practices vary widely according to gender, age, education, employment, income, and home ownership, with effect sizes ranging from negligible to medium (Cramer’s V = 0.060–0.330); in general, level of knowledge/education, economic capacity, and housing security emerge as key factors that jointly shape preparedness behaviors and self-efficacy assessments. Notably, the relationship between property ownership and earthquake insurance acquisition demonstrated the strongest effect observed in this study (Cramer’s V = 0.330, medium effect), underscoring the critical role of housing tenure in shaping cost-intensive preparedness behaviors.

Overall, the findings show that earthquake preparedness in this sample is characterized by a gap between high-risk awareness and limited self-perceived preparedness. Preparedness self-efficacy, perceived barriers, motivations for better preparedness, training participation, perceived building safety, and reported preparedness behaviors were associated with different sociodemographic variables. These results suggest that household preparedness should be interpreted not only as an individual knowledge or awareness issue, but also as a socioeconomic and housing-related issue. Nevertheless, the findings should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than causal relationships.

Discussion

This study contributes to the literature on the socioeconomic dimensions of household earthquake preparedness in Türkiye. The findings indicate that self-perceived preparedness is associated with gender, age, and household income. More broadly, the results suggest that household preparedness should not be evaluated only through individual awareness or knowledge, but also in relation to the economic resources, housing conditions, and social positions that may enable or constrain preparedness action.

The gender-related findings require careful interpretation. A notable finding was the divergence between preparedness self-efficacy and practical preparedness behaviors. Men reported higher levels of preparedness self-efficacy, whereas women more frequently reported several household-level preparedness behaviors, including preparing an earthquake kit, designating an emergency contact person, knowing utility valve locations, and receiving basic search and rescue training. This pattern suggests a possible distinction between perceived preparedness and behavioral preparedness. It may also reflect gendered divisions of household safety, caregiving responsibilities, and everyday risk management practices. Previous studies have shown that gender roles, household responsibilities, economic resources, and disaster policy design may influence preparedness perceptions and actions in different ways [24–36]. Similar gender-based differences in preparedness and response behaviors have also been reported in previous disaster research [37]. Therefore, the present findings should not be interpreted as indicating that one gender is generally “more prepared” than the other. Rather, they suggest that preparedness self-efficacy and preparedness behavior may operate as distinct dimensions across gendered household roles.

Consistent with previous studies, education and income appear to be important correlates of earthquake preparedness [32, 38–44]. Higher education may support preparedness by improving access to information, risk comprehension, and the ability to evaluate protective measures. Higher income may make some preparedness actions more feasible, particularly those requiring financial expenditure, such as earthquake insurance, furniture securing, or structural safety-related measures. However, because the present study is cross-sectional, these relationships should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence that education or income directly increases preparedness.

The findings regarding homeownership and employment status suggest that earthquake preparedness is a multidimensional process associated not only with individual preferences but also with structural inequalities. Homeowners, likely due to their higher housing autonomy, possess more knowledge about the structural integrity of their buildings and a higher level of preparedness [40, 45, 46]. Similarly, full-time employment may be associated with preparedness by providing better access to information and institutional training opportunities [34, 38, 47].

Taken together, the findings support a cautious interpretation of the Risk Perception Paradox in the Turkish earthquake context. High levels of earthquake experience and perceived local risk did not correspond to equally high levels of self-reported preparedness. The reported barriers—financial insufficiency, lack of time, and concerns about sustaining daily life—are consistent with the idea that risk perception may fail to translate into preparedness when households face competing priorities or insufficient resources. These findings also appear consistent with Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, particularly the argument that individuals may prioritize immediate physiological and economic concerns over longer-term safety-oriented preparedness behaviors. Maslow’s hierarchy of needs helps interpret this pattern by suggesting that households under basic-needs pressure may postpone preparedness behaviors even when they recognize the earthquake risk. However, this theoretical link should be understood as interpretive rather than as direct measurement of Maslow’s hierarchy.

Overall, the findings suggest that earthquake preparedness is not simply an individual choice or an awareness problem. It is also associated with socioeconomic position, housing status, access to information, and perceived capacity to act. For this reason, disaster risk reduction policies may be more effective when they combine awareness-raising with targeted support for households facing financial, informational, and housing-related constraints.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, data were collected through an online survey, which may have resulted in the underrepresentation of older adults, individuals with limited internet access, lower educational groups, and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations. In addition, the sample included a relatively high proportion of university-educated participants, limiting the generalizability of the findings to the broader Turkish population. Therefore, the findings should not be interpreted as nationally representative estimates of household earthquake preparedness in Türkiye. Rather, they should be interpreted as evidence of statistically significant associations and vulnerability patterns within a large, geographically dispersed online survey sample. This limitation is also sociologically meaningful because digital inequality itself may constitute an important dimension of disaster vulnerability and access to preparedness information. Second, the study relied on self-reported responses, which may be subject to reporting and social desirability biases. Third, due to the cross-sectional design of the study, causal inferences cannot be established, and the findings should therefore be interpreted as associative rather than causal relationships. Finally, although the study was conceptually informed by Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, the findings should be interpreted as theoretically aligned with the framework rather than as a direct empirical validation of the theory.

A limitation of this study is that multiple chi-square tests were conducted without adjustment for multiple comparisons (e.g., Bonferroni correction). This approach may increase the risk of Type I error inflation, meaning that some statistically significant bivariate associations may have occurred by chance. We chose not to apply a formal correction for multiple comparisons for the following reasons. First, the bivariate analyses were conducted for exploratory purposes to systematically examine associations across a range of theoretically relevant variables grounded in Maslow’s hierarchy of needs and the risk perception paradox framework, rather than for confirmatory hypothesis testing. Second, overly conservative corrections such as Bonferroni may lead to Type II errors (false negatives), potentially obscuring important associations relevant for disaster preparedness policy and practice. Third, we subsequently conducted a binary logistic regression analysis (Table 10) that simultaneously included all relevant predictors, thereby controlling for confounding and reducing the likelihood of spurious associations. The multivariate results revealed that only three variables remained statistically significant independent predictors, whereas many variables that showed significant bivariate associations did not retain significance in the multivariate model. Fourth, we reported effect size measures (Cramer’s V) for all chi-square tests, allowing readers to assess the practical significance of associations beyond statistical significance alone. Readers are encouraged to interpret the bivariate findings as hypothesis-generating and to prioritize the multivariate results, which provide more robust evidence for independent predictors of earthquake preparedness.

Conclusion

Despite widespread earthquake risk awareness in Türkiye, household-level preparedness remains limited. Interpreted through the Risk Perception Paradox and Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, the findings suggest that unpreparedness is not only a matter of insufficient information, but also a matter of perceived time constraints, financial limitations, daily-life pressures, and housing-related insecurity. Awareness campaigns therefore need to be complemented by policies that address the structural conditions that limit preparedness capacity. Targeted and accessible training, financial incentives for low-income households, tenant-inclusive housing safety measures, and community-based preparedness practices may help reduce preparedness gaps. Gender-sensitive preparedness policies may also be necessary, given the observed differences between self-perceived preparedness and practical preparedness behaviors. Future research should examine these relationships using longitudinal or mixed-method designs to better understand how socioeconomic constraints influence actual preparedness behavior over time.
