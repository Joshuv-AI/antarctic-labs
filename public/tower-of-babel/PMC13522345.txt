Staged screw removal in external plate fixation for distal tibial fractures: a finite element study on adaptive biomechanics
Authors: Mingming Wang, Huadong Yan, Jianhua Wang, Yuanzheng Song, Hua Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-energy distal tibial fractures present treatment challenges due to poor soft tissue coverage and risk of delayed union. External locking plates offer stability while preserving biology, yet the optimal strategy to dynamically modulate fixation rigidity in accordance with healing progression remains unclear. We constructed finite element models of a comminuted distal tibial fracture incorporating callus at four healing stages (0, 1, 3, and 6 months). Seven screw configurations and the effect of an intact fibula were evaluated under 800 N axial load. Outcome measures included fracture site displacement, stress shielding rate, and stresses in callus and implant. In early healing (0–1 month), displacement exceeded 1 mm even with 3- or 4-screw constructs, and plate stress approached 2900 MPa, underscoring the need for protected weight-bearing. By 3 months, all configurations maintained sub‑millimeter displacement. Reducing screws from a 4-screw to a 2-screw configuration at this stage significantly lowered the stress shielding rate from 76.3% to 71.8% and increased callus stress by approximately 14%, without compromising stability. An intact fibula consistently reduced displacement and implant stress by 50–80%. This study provides biomechanical support for an “adaptive fixation” strategy: initial rigid fixation with protected weight-bearing, followed by staged screw removal after callus maturation (around 3 months) to reduce stress shielding and enhance mechanobiological stimulation. The presence of an intact fibula substantially improves construct stability and should be considered in clinical management.Keywords: Distal tibial fracture, External fixation, Callus, Finite element analysis, Stress shielding, DynamizationSubject terms: Engineering, Health care, Materials science, Medical research

Introduction

High-energy trauma, such as road traffic accidents or falls from height, is a primary cause of comminuted fractures of the distal tibia. These fractures represent a significant clinical burden, accounting for approximately 15% of all skeletal fractures1, with 20–40% being open injuries frequently accompanied by severe soft-tissue compromise2. The paucity of muscular coverage over the anteromedial aspect of the distal tibia, coupled with its tenuous periosteal blood supply, creates a vulnerable biological environment when combined with such trauma. This significantly elevates the risk of complications including delayed union, non-union, and infection3–5. Consequently, the central challenge in management lies in providing sufficient mechanical stability to maintain fracture reduction while simultaneously maximizing the protection and promotion of the surrounding soft-tissue envelope.

The external locking plate technique, a modified form of external fixation, has emerged as a promising solution in this context6–9. By positioning a locking plate in an extra-cutaneous plane, this method combines the stability of fixed-angle constructs with a minimally invasive approach, offering a valuable therapeutic alternative for distal tibial fractures with compromised soft tissues. Fracture healing is a biologically complex process profoundly influenced by its mechanical environment. Secondary (callus) healing, in particular, relies on appropriate mechanical stimulation at the fracture site. Evidence suggests that controlled axial micromotion within the range of 0.2–1.0 mm, or interfragmentary strain between 2% and 10%, can effectively promote callus formation and mineralization10,11. Conversely, excessively rigid fixation, often due to the high elastic modulus of metallic implants compared to bone, can induce a pronounced “stress shielding” effect12. This reduces mechanical stimuli to the healing bone, potentially delaying callus maturation and remodeling.

Most existing biomechanical studies on distal tibial fracture fixation have focused on comparing the static stability of different internal fixation constructs13,14 or evaluating the performance of a single configuration at an isolated time point15–17. However, fracture healing is a dynamic continuum characterized by the evolving material properties of the callus. A critical gap exists in understanding how a single external fixation system can be dynamically adapted postoperatively to meet the changing mechanical demands of different healing stages—a concept known as “dynamization.” Furthermore, many computational models simplify the anatomy by omitting the fibula, neglecting its potential role in load sharing and overall construct stability, which may limit the clinical translatability of their findings.

Finite element analysis (FEA) provides a powerful, controlled platform to simulate such dynamic biological processes and compare multiple fixation scenarios under standardized conditions. This study employed FEA to systematically simulate the healing process of a comminuted distal tibial fracture. We developed models incorporating callus with time-dependent material properties and comparatively analyzed the biomechanical performance of various external plate-screw configurations across key healing stages. The influence of an intact fibula was specifically investigated. The primary objectives were to quantify the effects of different configurations on: (1) the control of interfragmentary displacement (micromotion), (2) the degree of stress shielding, (3) the modulation of stress distribution within the callus and tibia, and (4) the mechanical state of the fixation device itself. The findings aim to provide quantitative biomechanical evidence to guide clinicians in implementing staged, “adaptive fixation” strategies, facilitating a rational transition from initial mechanical stability to subsequent biologically favorable fixation.

Materials and methods

This study integrates a retrospective clinical review with computational biomechanical simulation. The clinical analysis provided a realistic epidemiological context and healing timeline to inform the parameters of the finite element models.

All patients received standardized surgical treatment, which included the use of a locking plate as an external fixator. As this technique is one of the established treatment options for such complex fractures at our institution, detailed verbal and written informed consent was obtained from all patients preoperatively. This consent explicitly explained alternatives (e.g., traditional external fixator, intramedullary nail), potential risks (including implant failure, stress shielding, need for secondary surgery), and the specific nature of its use as an external fixator. This clinical treatment pathway and consent process were approved by the Ethics Committee of Tengzhou Central People’s Hospital Affiliated to Jining Medical University (Approval No.: 2023-Ethical Review-33). All procedures involving human data adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all clinical participants and the volunteer for CT data acquisition.

Clinical data

We retrospectively included patients with open distal tibial fractures treated with external locking plate fixation at our institution between October 2021 and October 2024. Inclusion criteria were: radiographically confirmed open distal tibial fracture (Gustilo type I-IIIA); age ≥ 18 years; and absence of concomitant severe vascular or neurological injury. Exclusion criteria included: comorbid severe systemic disease, pathological fracture or malignancy, severe osteoporosis, and incomplete follow-up data.

A total of 26 patients were finally included. Their baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. All patients underwent standardized surgical treatment and postoperative follow-up, with their fracture union status recorded. Figure 1 presents imaging data from a typical case.

Clinical practice regarding screw removal

In our previous practice, screws were removed en bloc after radiographic consolidation, with partial removal reserved for persistent infection. Some patients with partial removal for infection eventually healed without evident loss of reduction. While these uncontrolled, anecdotal observations cannot demonstrate safety or efficacy, they, together with the notable delayed union rate (19.2% >9 months, 50.0% at 6–9 months; Table 1), raised the question of whether staged rigidity reduction could improve the late mechanical environment. Building on prior biomechanical evidence that stress and strain evolve with callus maturation, we hypothesized that staged partial screw removal—adaptive fixation—could maintain initial rigidity while reducing stress shielding once the callus had gained sufficient stiffness. This hypothesis and the clinical healing timeline motivated the present FE study and guided the choice of four evaluation time points (0, 1, 3, and 6 months).

Finite element model development

For the computational simulation, computed tomography (CT) scan data of the right lower leg were obtained from a healthy adult male volunteer (age 40 years, height 176 cm, weight 75 kg) using a 128-slice dual-source spiral CT scanner (Siemens; slice thickness 0.625 mm). The tibia and fibula were segmented and reconstructed into three-dimensional (3D) surface models using Mimics 21.0 (Materialise, Belgium) through threshold-based and region-growing algorithms. Surface smoothing and topology optimization were performed in Geomagic 2017 (3D Systems, USA). The processed models were imported into SolidWorks 2021 (Dassault Systèmes, France), where a transverse osteotomy was created 50–60 mm above the distal tibial articular surface to simulate a comminuted metaphyseal fracture with a 10 mm gap16. A 9-hole lateral distal femur Less Invasive Stabilization System (LISS) locking plate and corresponding locking screws were modeled at 1:1 scale based on manufacturer dimensions. The healing callus was represented as a simplified spindle-shaped solid bridging the fracture gap18(Fig. 2).

Model assembly and grouping

The components (tibia with or without fibula, callus, plate, and screws) were assembled in SolidWorks. The plate was positioned on the medial aspect of the tibia, maintaining a fixed bone-plate distance of 20 mm. A dual-grouping strategy was employed: models were first categorized into seven groups based on the screw configuration in the proximal fragment. These included four 2-screw configurations (T1 + 2, T1 + 3, T1 + 4, T2 + 3), one 3-screw configuration (T3), one 4-screw configuration (T4), and a no-fixation control group (T0). Each fixation configuration was then analyzed in two conditions: a “Tibia-only” model and a “Tibia-Fibula combined” model (incorporating an intact fibula), resulting in a total of 14 analytical models (Fig. 3).

Material properties and meshing

All assembled models were imported into ANSYS Workbench 2019 R2 (ANSYS Inc., USA) for finite element analysis. All materials were defined as isotropic and linearly elastic. The specific material properties for bone and implants are listed in Table 218–20. To simulate the dynamic healing process, the elastic modulus of the callus was prescribed at four time points (0, 1, 3, and 6 months), progressively increasing from 0.2 MPa (granulation tissue) to 1000 MPa (relatively mature bone)16,18,21–25(Table 3). Contact interactions were simplified as follows: “Bonded” contacts were defined between the screws and the plate threaded holes, between the proximal and distal articular surfaces of the tibia and fibula, and between the callus and the tibial bone fragments.

Meshing, boundary conditions, and analysis

The models were meshed using 4-node tetrahedral elements, with local refinement applied to the fracture region, callus, and screw-bone interfaces to ensure accuracy (Fig. 4). A mesh convergence analysis was performed using an adaptive refinement method. The mesh density in key regions (fracture fragments, callus, screw-bone interfaces, and plate) was iteratively increased. The simulation was considered converged when the change in key outcome parameters (maximum fracture site displacement and maximum von Mises stress in the callus and plate) between two consecutive refinements was less than 5%26,27. All models satisfied this criterion, confirming the mesh independence of the results.

Meshing of the tibia, callus, plate, and assembled model.

For boundary conditions, all degrees of freedom on the distal tibial articular surface were constrained. A vertical axial load of 800 N was applied to the proximal tibial articular surface to simulate single-leg stance loading. This load was distributed with 60% on the medial plateau and 40% on the lateral plateau28–30.

Sensitivity analysis for contact assumptions

To evaluate the influence of the bonded contact assumption, a sensitivity analysis was performed using the T4 configuration at the 3-month healing time point as a representative case. The screw-bone and callus-bone interfaces were changed to frictional contact, with a coefficient of friction of 0.2. All other material parameters, loading, and boundary conditions remained identical to those of the primary bonded model. This analysis was designed to assess whether potential interfacial micromotion would alter the key conclusion regarding the biomechanical safety of staged screw removal.

Results

Model validation

To validate the overall model’s mechanical response under load, an intact tibia-fibula model was first subjected to the 800 N axial load (60%/40% distribution). The simulation results showed that stress concentration in the tibial shaft primarily occurred at the anteromedial crest, consistent with the classic stress pattern of bone under axial compression. Furthermore, qualitative comparison of our 0-month Tibia-only model with a T4 configuration to published FE studies on external plate fixation for tibial fractures16,19,32 showed consistent trends in the magnitude of fracture gap displacement and internal fixation stress levels, indirectly confirming the biomechanical plausibility of our model setup.

Sensitivity analysis for contact conditions

The frictional contact sensitivity analysis (outlined in the Methods) revealed that, relative to the original bonded model, the fracture gap displacement increased by approximately 8–12%.Notably, the displacement for all configurations at 3 months remained below 1 mm, which confirms that the core conclusion-staged screw removal at 3 months is biomechanically safe-is robust.

Fracture site stability

Fracture gap displacement decreased progressively with healing time across all configurations (Figs. 5 and 6). During the early healing phase (0 and 1 months), total displacement exceeded 1 mm even with the more rigid 3-screw (T3/T3F) and 4-screw (T4/T4F) constructs, indicating substantial system micromotion under load. At 0 months, the 4-screw configuration provided significantly greater stability than the 3-screw configuration (P < 0.05).

During mid-to-late healing (3 and 6 months), the vertical displacement and total displacement for all configurations dropped below 1 mm, and there were statistically significant differences between the configuration with the maximum and minimum displacement (P < 0.05). In the Tibia-only models, displacements ranked from largest to smallest as: T1 + 2 > T2 + 3, T1 + 3, T1 + 4, T3 > T4. In the Tibia-Fibula combined models, a similar ranking was observed: T1 + 2 F > T2 + 3 F, T1 + 3 F, T1 + 4 F, T3F > T4F (Fig. 6). In Fig. 5, the larger error bars for T4/T4F at 3 and 6 months indicate a more uneven displacement distribution across the fracture surface with the 4‑screw construct (likely due to concentrated load transfer near the plate), whereas 2‑screw configurations allow more uniform micromotion.

The fracture gap displacement distribution maps further revealed that at 3 and 6 months post-operation, regardless of fibular inclusion, the point of maximum displacement was located on the medial side of the tibia, points of higher displacement were on the anterior side, and the point of minimum displacement was on the lateral side (plate side) (Fig. 7).

Stress shielding effect

The stress shielding rate was calculated relative to the fracture site stress in the no-fixation model (T0/T0F). Results showed that the stress shielding rate decreased significantly as healing progressed. In the early postoperative period (0, 1 months), the shielding rate was extremely high (all above 85%, exceeding 97% at maximum). By 3 months post-operation, a marked decrease in shielding rate was observed.

At the same healing time point, the number of screws was the primary factor influencing stress shielding. In Tibia-only models at 3 months, the stress shielding rate for 2-screw configurations ranged from 71.82% to 74.64%, for the 3-screw configuration (T3) was 75.12%, and for the 4-screw configuration (T4) was 76.28%. In Tibia-Fibula combined models, the shielding rates for all configurations were generally 3% to 5% lower than their Tibia-only counterparts. Among all configurations, the T1 + 2 configuration with the fewest screws produced the weakest stress shielding effect, while the T4 configuration with the highest symmetry produced the strongest shielding (detailed values are provided in Tables 4 and 5).

Callus stress distribution

The equivalent stress within the callus increased as its material modulus increased (i.e., with healing progression). At any given healing stage, the callus stress in Tibia-only models was significantly higher than in the corresponding Tibia-Fibula combined models (Fig. 8). During mid-to-late healing (3, 6 months), the region of maximum stress concentration in the callus was primarily located on the lateral side of the tibia. Among different fixation configurations, the stress level sustained by the callus varied distinctly: callus stress was highest in the T1 + 2 configuration and lowest in the T4 configuration, with other configurations in between. For example, in the 6-month Tibia-only model, the maximum callus stress was 2.96 MPa for T1 + 2 and 2.60 MPa for T4 (Figs. 8, 9 and 10). This indicates that fixation with fewer screws transfers more load to the callus, providing greater mechanical stimulation.

Tibial stress distribution

As fracture healing progressed and the callus gradually bore load, the peak stress in the tibia itself showed a decreasing trend (Fig. 8). Tibial stress in Tibia-only models was consistently higher than in Tibia-Fibula combined models. Regarding stress distribution patterns, in Tibia-only models, except for the T4 configuration, the maximum stress point for other configurations appeared around the screw holes distal to the fracture line. For the T4 configuration, the stress concentration point shifted to the most proximal screw hole. In Tibia-Fibula combined models, stress concentration locations were more dispersed and varied considerably between configurations (Figs. 9 and 11). This suggests that the intact fibula effectively shares the load, alters the tibial stress pattern, and reduces local stress concentration.

Tibial equivalent stress distribution nephograms for different configurations at 3 and 6 months post-operation.

Implant system stress

The peak stress in the plate and screws gradually decreased as the fracture healed, indicating a gradual transfer of load from the implant system to the bone (Fig. 8). During early healing (0, 1 months), implant stress was extremely high, with peak stress in Tibia-only T3/T4 models approaching 2900 MPa. By 3 and 6 months post-operation, stress levels dropped substantially to below 200 MPa. In all models, implant stress in Tibia-only models was significantly higher than in the corresponding Tibia-Fibula combined models. Among different configurations, the T1 + 2 configuration with fewer screws experienced higher plate stress than configurations with more screws (T3, T4) (Figs. 9 and 12).

Discussion

Comminuted distal tibial fractures with soft tissue injury are challenging to manage due to poor local soft tissue coverage and blood supply, leading to high rates of delayed union and nonunion. Using a locking plate as an external fixation device for this fracture type can minimize periosteal damage, preserve blood circulation, reduce iatrogenic injury, and offers advantages like a low profile, angular stability, and ability to be concealed under clothing, improving patient acceptance. This treatment modality has gained recognition and application among many scholars6–9,33–35.

The screw configurations examined (T1 + 2, T1 + 3, T1 + 4, T2 + 3) were chosen to simulate clinically plausible partial screw removal patterns while retaining the external plate. For example, T1 + 2 represents an extreme reduction retaining only the two end screws; T3 and T4 represent pre-dynamization rigidity. This spectrum allows systematic evaluation of different degrees of dynamization, directly informing our clinical “adaptive fixation” strategy.

Total displacement exceeded X‑axis displacement in all cases, confirming non‑axial (Y/Z) micromotion from eccentric fixation and asymmetric loading. At 3 and 6 months, Y/Z displacements were substantially smaller than total displacement, and total displacement remained below 1 mm for all configurations—well within a biologically acceptable range. To avoid redundancy, only total and X‑axis displacement are shown in Fig. 6. Moderate axial motion promotes healing while excessive shear is detrimental; our data confirm that non‑axial components were safe.

This study employed finite element analysis to systematically evaluate the biomechanical performance of various screw configurations in external plate fixation for comminuted distal tibial fractures throughout a simulated dynamic healing process. The core findings reveal the complex trade-off between stability, stress shielding, and mechanical stimulation, and clarify the critical influence of fibular integrity and configuration adjustments. These results provide direct evidence to support the clinical implementation of individualized, staged “dynamization” strategies.

Fracture healing is a continuous process coupling biology and biomechanics. This study found that in the early postoperative period (0–1 months), even with “rigid” 3- or 4-screw configurations under simulated 800 N standing load, the total displacement at the fracture gap generally exceeded 1 mm, far beyond the reported optimal range of axial micromotion (0.2–1.0 mm) for promoting callus formation10,12,36. This is primarily attributed to the long lever arm and eccentric fixation inherent to external plating. More critically, the implant system sustained extremely high stress during this phase, with peak plate stress in the 0-month Tibia-only model approaching 2900 MPa and still exceeding 1500 MPa at 1 month. For reference, the tensile strength of cortical bone is approximately 135 MPa37, and the fatigue endurance limit of common implant stainless steel (e.g., 316 L) typically falls within the 200–300 MPa range38. Although peak stresses from FE analysis require cautious interpretation, this stress magnitude strongly suggests that during early healing, both the fixation device and the screw-bone interface are at high risk of mechanical overload. Therefore, our results provide strong biomechanical support for the clinical practice of strictly avoiding full weight-bearing in the early postoperative period (recommended first 2 months), mandating protected partial weight-bearing with crutch assistance. This is essential to prevent compromised early callus formation from excessive micromotion and to avert potential implant fatigue failure or peri-screw bone resorption due to cyclical high stress.

Our study simulated the most common clinical scenario in which a concomitant fibular fracture is internally fixed, thereby restoring its mechanical integrity. This modeling choice is supported by previous work from Orth et al., who demonstrated that the tibial stress distribution is comparable between an intact fibula and a plated fibula17. Consistent with this load-sharing function, our results clearly show that an intact fibula acts as a synergistic stabilizer, substantially improving the mechanical environment of the entire construct. Compared to the Tibia-only models, the Tibia-Fibula combined models exhibited reduced fracture gap displacement, lower peak implant stress, and a more uniform tibial stress distribution at all healing stages; for instance, at 0 months post-op, peak plate stress was reduced from approximately 2871 MPa to 376 MPa. These findings underscore that in clinical settings where fibular fixation is unsuccessful or not performed, the external tibial plate must bear the vast majority of the load independently, thereby heightening the risk of fixation failure. Consequently, a thorough assessment and active restoration of fibular mechanical integrity should be regarded as a key step in optimizing the biomechanical environment for tibial fracture healing. It must be noted, however, that our intact fibula model represents a best-case scenario, and for complex cases with a persistently unfixed or poorly fixed fibula, the mechanical reality likely lies between our “Tibia-only” and “Tibia-Fibula combined” simulations. Future studies with more granular models are warranted to quantify the impact of varying degrees of fibular fixation quality on overall construct stability.

As fracture healing progresses into the mid-to-late stages (3–6 months) and callus stiffness increases significantly (elastic modulus from 0.2 MPa to 1000 MPa), the core contradiction of the fixation system gradually shifts from “providing absolute stability” to “reducing stress shielding to promote bone remodeling.” According to Wolff’s law, bone growth and reconstruction require continuous mechanical stimulation31. Our study found that at 3 months post-operation, the stress shielding rate was as high as 76.3% for the symmetric 4-screw fixation (T4), while it could be reduced to approximately 71.8% for the T1 + 2 configuration with the fewest screws. Concurrently, callus stress in the T1 + 2 configuration was about 14% higher than in the T4 configuration. More crucially, this reduction in stress shielding and increase in mechanical stimulation was not achieved at the expense of stability. At 3 months, X‑axis displacement of T2 + 3 was not significantly different from T3. Retaining two proximal screws in T2 + 3 provides effective ‘double‑point’ fixation against bending and shear. Adding a fourth screw (T4) exceeds a critical threshold: stability gains are minimal while stress shielding rises substantially. Thus, reducing from T3 to T2 + 3 maintains competence, confirming that staged dynamization is safe. At 3 and 6 months, fracture gap displacement for all configurations was controlled within the safe threshold of 1 mm. This provides a clear biomechanical operational window for clinical implementation of “dynamization”: once fracture healing enters the callus consolidation phase (radiographic evidence of bridging callus, approximately 8–12 weeks post-operation), staged and strategic removal of specific screws can be considered. Our results suggest that preferentially removing screws proximal to the fracture, especially those closest to the fracture line (e.g., transitioning from T4 to T1 + 2 or T2 + 3 configurations), can actively transfer more physiological load to the healing callus while maintaining sufficient clinical stability. This may promote further callus maturation and remodeling, potentially shortening healing time and reducing long-term stress-shielding-related complications.

The biomechanical properties of implant materials, particularly their elastic modulus, are a core factor influencing the mechanical environment of fracture healing39. Our FE models were set with stainless-steel material. Stainless steel, due to its higher stiffness, generates a stronger stress shielding effect. However, higher stiffness also implies better initial stability. Among commonly used metallic implants, titanium alloys35(e.g., Ti-6Al-4 V, elastic modulus ~ 110 GPa) and commercially pure titanium40(e.g., TA3, elastic modulus ~ 102–106 GPa) have significantly lower elastic moduli than stainless steel, theoretically reducing stress shielding41. Some clinical studies suggest that for certain fracture types, titanium plates may promote earlier callus formation by allowing more beneficial micromotion42. This indicates that material selection requires a trade-off between “initial stability requirements” and “mid-to-late stress shielding risk.” Provided initial stability is achieved, choosing plates made of pure titanium could further reduce stress shielding and accelerate callus formation.

This study has several limitations. First, the models used linearly elastic material properties and simplified callus geometry, not capturing the nonlinear biological processes of callus growth and resorption. Second, loading was limited to static axial load, ignoring dynamic/torsional loads and muscle forces. Third, contact interfaces (screw-bone, callus-bone) were assumed fully bonded, neglecting micromotion and loosening. Fourth, the conclusions are from simulations and require validation via in vitro experiments and clinical studies. Fifth, the geometry came from a single healthy CT scan; while this eliminates anatomical variation and enables controlled comparisons, it does not represent patient-specific anatomy. Clinical data only defined healing time points, not subject-specific geometries. Sixth, a sensitivity analysis with frictional contact (friction coefficient = 0.2) showed displacement increased by 8–12% but remained within safe limits, supporting the robustness of our main conclusion. Nevertheless, more refined contact conditions should be used in future studies. Future research should develop multi-scale models integrating callus biology, simulate full gait cycles, perform in vitro validation, and ultimately conduct a randomized controlled trial of staged dynamization fixation based on our biomechanical evidence.

Conclusion

This study provides systematic biomechanical evidence supporting a dynamic, staged fixation strategy for high‑energy comminuted distal tibial fractures treated with an external plate. The findings indicate that during the early postoperative period (≤ 2 months), substantial fracture‑site micromotion and critically high implant stresses necessitate protected weight‑bearing to avoid mechanical overload. Fibular integrity proved crucial for construct stability and stress reduction, underscoring the importance of its anatomical restoration when fractured. As healing progresses into the consolidation phase (from approximately 3 months), planned staged screw removal—such as transitioning from a 4‑screw to a 2‑screw configuration—can effectively lower stress shielding and enhance mechanical stimulation to the callus without compromising stability. Together, these results outline a biomechanically rational pathway to transition from initial mechanical stability to subsequent biological adaptation, optimizing the healing environment for complex distal tibial fractures.
