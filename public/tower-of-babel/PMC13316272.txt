Genetic ablation of Sfxn5 induces mitochondrial dysfunction and precipitates lethal metabolic crisis in mice
Authors: Huan Zhang, Cuihong Wang, Yumeng Zhang, Yiming Xiang, Sijia Fan, Ling Meng, Jingjing Qin, Jiashi Guo, Zhenting He, Ziling Deng, Lei Zhao, Ling Lei, Siyuan Hou, Ming Zeng, Qiuqiong Wang, Chunguang Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMitochondrial disorders frequently manifest with life-threatening hepatic metabolic crises. Using a global Sfxn5-knockout mouse model, we investigated the systemic consequences of disrupting this mitochondrial inner-membrane transporter through integrated biochemical, metabolomic, histological, and ultrastructural analyses. Sfxn5 deficiency resulted in complete postnatal lethality accompanied by severe metabolic collapse and progressive multi-organ dysfunction. The liver emerged as the primary site of pathology, exhibiting marked mitochondrial structural damage and widespread disruption of central metabolic pathways, including the tricarboxylic acid cycle, fatty acid β-oxidation, and ammonia detoxification, leading to hyperammonemia and systemic metabolic stress. Importantly, liver-specific reconstitution of Sfxn5 partially restored mitochondrial metabolic function, substantially reduced hyperammonemia, and alleviated multi-organ pathology. Altogether, these findings identify hepatic mitochondrial dysfunction as the central driver of Sfxn5-dependent lethality and establish a critical role for Sfxn5 in maintaining mitochondrial metabolic homeostasis during early postnatal life.Subject areas: pathophysiology, metabolomics

Introduction

Mitochondria, double-membrane organelles, serve as essential cellular hubs for the production of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) through oxidative phosphorylation and for the biosynthesis of essential cellular metabolites.1 Consequently, they are crucial for energy homeostasis, cell differentiation, and cellular viability. Pathogenic mutations in mitochondrial genes cause mitochondrial diseases (MDs), which exhibit significant clinical heterogeneity and a broad range of onset timing.1,2 The phenotypic severity is associated with tissue-specific gene expression and functional redundancy, posing considerable diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.3 Organs with high mitochondrial content, such as the brain, liver, and heart, are particularly vulnerable to mitochondrial dysfunction.4,5 This dysfunction, resulting from the impairment of critical metabolic pathways, can lead to fatal biochemical derangements and organ failure, exemplified by cardiomyopathy and hepatic encephalopathy.6,7,8 Hepatic encephalopathy is primarily induced by hyperammonemia, which leads to astrocyte dysfunction, cerebral edema, and impaired neurotransmission, ultimately resulting in cognitive and neuromotor deficits.

Clinically, MDs frequently manifest as life-threatening hepatic metabolic crises with high mortality. Examples include Alpers-Huttenlocher syndrome caused by mutations in polymerase gamma (POLG), presenting with refractory epilepsy, rapidly progressive liver failure, and neurological deterioration9; fatty acid oxidation disorders such as long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase/very long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (LCAD/VLCAD) deficiency,10 which precipitates Reye-like syndrome during catabolic stress, often progressing rapidly to fatal outcomes; and neonatal mitochondrial complex I deficiency, manifesting as lactic acidosis with respiratory failure, cardiomyopathy, and hepatic dysfunction, typically leading to fatal multiorgan failure within days of onset.11 These presentations underscore the critical, often fatal, role of mitochondrial dysfunction-induced metabolic crises, particularly in early life. They highlight that acute metabolic decompensation in MDs typically progresses rapidly and irreversibly, emphasizing the paramount importance of early recognition and prompt intervention.

The Sideroflexin (SFXN) family consists of five evolutionarily conserved inner mitochondrial membrane transporters that critically regulate mitochondrial homeostasis and metabolic function.12 Among these, SFXN1 functions as a serine transporter for one-carbon metabolism and serves as a TIM22 complex substrate essential for mitochondrial complex III assembly.13,14 SFXN2 orchestrates mitochondrial iron trafficking for heme biosynthesis while maintaining ATP production and mitophagic flux under oxidative stress.15 SFXN3 maintains cristae architecture to support neuronal axon development,16 whereas SFXN4 mutations are associated with severe mitochondrial disorders (MDs).17 In contrast to these relatively well-studied paralogs, SFXN5 remains poorly understood despite evidence implicating it in mitochondrial citric acid transport18 and uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1)-mediated thermogenesis via phosphoglyceride metabolism in brown adipose tissue.19 Our prior work demonstrated that SFXN5 governs neutrophil membrane dynamics through a citric acid-acetyl-CoA-cholesterol biosynthetic axis, establishing its importance in coupling mitochondrial metabolism to cellular function.20 Nevertheless, the developmental requirements of SFXN5 remain unresolved, largely due to the current lack of appropriate genetic model systems.

In this study, we established an Sfxn5-knockout (KO) murine model that exhibits postnatal lethality, characterized by classic hepatic metabolic crisis. Mechanistic investigations demonstrate that Sfxn5 deficiency leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and disrupts key biochemical pathways, including the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, fatty acid oxidation, sterol metabolism, and the urea cycle.

Results

Global Sfxn5 knockout causes premature lethality in mice

To define the developmental and organismal roles of Sfxn5, we first mapped its tissue distribution across major parenchymal organs in adult mice, and detected robust SFXN5 protein expression in the brain, liver, lung, and kidney (Figure S1A). Zebrafish have two homologs of the mammalian Sfxn5 gene: sfxn5a and sfxn5b, and the expression level of sfxn5b was much higher than that of sfxn5a.20 Consistent with mice, zebrafish sfxn5b transcripts predominantly localized to the brain, pronephric region (primordial kidney), and hepatic tissues (Figure S1B).

We subsequently generated Sfxn5 global KO mice (Figure S1C) and achieved a robust KO efficiency as evidenced by the absence of SFXN5 proteins in the major organs (Figure S1D). Genotypic analysis across 10 independent litters demonstrated Mendelian distribution of Sfxn5-KO alleles (Figure 1A). Unexpectedly, Sfxn5-null pups displayed a striking sex ratio imbalance (male: female ≈ 4:1) (Figure 1B). Complete lethality was observed in Sfxn5-KO mice between postnatal days 23 and 27 (P23-27) (Figure 1C). Further observations showed that Sfxn5-KO pups exhibited severe cachectic manifestations, with body weight reduced by approximately 40% relative to littermate controls at terminal stages (Figures 1D and 1E). Longitudinal growth monitoring revealed no significant abnormalities in KO pups during the first two postnatal weeks. However, a progressive growth retardation emerged at P18, characterized by sustained negative weight gain trajectories (Figure 1F). This failure-to-thrive phenotype correlated temporally with the onset of terminal morbidity.

Following the natural weaning transition (≥P17), a period marked by declining maternal milk production and the shift to independent feeding in pups, we investigated whether the lethal phenotype resulted from impaired nutritional acquisition. Although Sfxn5-KO pups demonstrated intact self-feeding capacity, they maintained a profoundly emaciated phenotype. Artificial nutritional supplementation moderately improved body weight (Figure S2A) but failed to extend survival (Figure S2B), indicating that starvation was not the primary cause of mortality.

Additionally, we found that all moribund Sfxn5-KO pups exhibited severe ataxia (Video S1) and hypoglycemia (Figure 1G). While transient weight gain was observed in Sfxn5-KO pups following high-fat diet and high glucose (HFD+HG) intervention, survival outcomes remained unaffected (Figures 1H and 1I). Notably, Sfxn5 deficiency induced marked hyperlactatemia (Figure 1J). Given that MDs are typically associated with lactic acidemia, these findings implicate impaired mitochondrial metabolism in the pathophysiology of Sfxn5 KO.

Sfxn5-deficient pups show abnormal development in multiple organs

We next conducted pathological analyses of major organs collected from Sfxn5-KO pups at P23, the time point at which the first mortality was observed. Macroscopic examination revealed that Sfxn5-KO pups exhibited a markedly reduced spleen size with a translucent appearance compared with their littermate controls. Additionally, both the kidneys and liver displayed a pronounced darkened reddish discoloration, while no overt morphological abnormalities were observed in the heart, lungs, or brain (Figure 2A). Given the substantial difference in body weight between genotypes, visceral organ masses were normalized to body weight and expressed as organ indices (Figure 2B). Notably, Sfxn5-KO pups exhibited a >30% reduction in splenic index. The renal index showed a modest increase (Figure 2B), which was consistent with histological evidence of vascular congestion (Figure 2C). In parallel, the brain index was significantly elevated, likely reflecting disproportionate brain growth secondary to generalized developmental growth retardation in the KO pups.

Histopathological analysis (Figure 2C) revealed significant architectural disorganization in the spleens of Sfxn5-KO pups, characterized by effacement of the red-white pulp demarcation. Consistent with gross morphological observations, the kidneys exhibited marked erythrocyte congestion, and the livers showed pronounced sinusoidal congestion. Notably, cardiac examination identified myocardial hypertrophy in KO pups, a hallmark manifestation of MDs.21 Analysis further uncovered severe pulmonary hypoplasia in the KO group, while brain tissue showed no significant pathological alterations. These data indicate that the lethality mediated by Sfxn5 deficiency is attributable, at least in part, to multi-organ dysfunction resembling that of classical MD.

Sfxn5 deficiency precipitates mitochondria-driven hepatic failure in pups

To assess whether Sfxn5 deficiency leads to mitochondrial dysfunction-associated pathology in organs with high mitochondrial demand, we performed comprehensive histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses of the heart, brain, and liver. In the heart, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy identified by H&E staining was corroborated by markedly elevated expression of atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP, Figure S3A) and brain natriuretic peptide (BNP, Figure S3B) in Sfxn5-KO pups. The robust expression of SFXN5 in the brain (Figure S1A), as supported by brain atlas databases22 and Zeisel’s single-cell studies23 indicating high expression levels in astrocytes, prompted our investigation into the effects of Sfxn5 deficiency on astrocyte development. We assessed the expression pattern of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), a well-established marker for astrocytes. Notably, a significant increase in GFAP+ cells was observed in the hippocampus of Sfxn5-KO pups, accompanied by a morphological transition from a ramified to a rounded shape compared to wild-type (WT) controls (Figures S3C and S3D). These alterations suggest that Sfxn5 deficiency induces reactive astrogliosis, a characteristic response of the central nervous system to neurotoxic insults.

Notably, given that SFXN5 is highly expressed in the liver (Figure S1A), we investigated the impact of Sfxn5-KO on liver function in pups. Plasma biochemical profiling of P26 mice (Table S2) revealed that Sfxn5 deficiency induced marked hepatic dysfunction, as characterized by elevated levels of total bilirubin (TB, Figure 3A), alanine aminotransferase (ALT, Figure 3B), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST, Figure 3C), along with a reduction in plasma triglycerides (TG, Figure 3D). Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining confirmed a significant increase in hepatocyte apoptosis in KO pups (Figures 3E and 3F). Furthermore, oil red O staining revealed characteristic microvesicular steatosis (Figures 3G and 3H), indicative of severe impairment in mitochondrial β-oxidation, a specific hallmark of mitochondrial dysfunction. To corroborate these findings, we performed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) on hepatocytes. Compared to WT controls, Sfxn5-deficient pups exhibited marked mitochondrial ultrastructural defects, including swelling, cristae loss, and membrane disintegration (Figure 3I). Collectively, these results provide compelling evidence that Sfxn5 deficiency precipitates liver failure in neonatal mice. Consistent with these observations, we detected a markedly elevated blood ammonia level in KO pups, indicating the development of hyperammonemia (Figure 3J).

To define the earlier developmental window at which Sfxn5 deficiency begins to exert pathogenic effects, we examined pups at P11, one week prior to the onset of overt body weight divergence. Consistent with our earlier observations, both gross inspection and histopathological evaluation revealed comparable organ morphology and developmental progression between Sfxn5-KO and WT pups at this stage, with no apparent pathological lesions (Figures S4A and S4B). In parallel, body weights did not differ significantly between the two genotypes (Figure S4C).

We next assessed a panel of biochemical parameters at P11. Analysis of cardiac stress markers (ANP and BNP; Figures S4D and S4E) and hepatic function indices—including blood ammonia (Figure S4F), ALT, AST, TG, and total bilirubin (TBIL) (Figures S4G and S4H)—revealed a selective and significant increase only in AST levels in Sfxn5-KO pups, whereas all other parameters remained comparable to WT controls. Importantly, at this early stage, ATP levels (Figure S4I) in the brain were comparable between WT and Sfxn5-deficient pups, and no significant differences were observed in astrocytic GFAP (Figure S4J) or neuron-specific nuclear protein (NeuN) (Figure S4K) staining, which indicate that both cerebral energy metabolism and neural integrity remain largely intact in Sfxn5-KO pups at P11.

Together, these findings indicate that early functional alterations following Sfxn5 loss are primarily confined to the liver, preceding detectable changes in brain energy metabolism or neural integrity, as well as overt structural abnormalities and systemic pathology at later stages.

Metabolic profiling reveals mitochondrial dysfunction-driven hepatic failure in Sfxn5-deficient pups

Given the established role of SFXN5 as a mitochondrial citric acid transporter,17,18 we performed untargeted metabolomic profiling to evaluate the metabolic consequences of Sfxn5 deficiency in vivo. Liver metabolomics of Sfxn5-KO pups revealed extensive metabolic reprogramming, with 164 metabolites upregulated and 191 downregulated (Figure 4A and Table S3). Metabolite classification showed that ∼26% of differentially abundant metabolites were carboxylic acids and derivatives (Figure 4B), consistent with the reported role of SFXN5 in mitochondrial citric acid transport.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis identified significant suppression of oxidative phosphorylation (Figure 4C, red arrow), accompanied by a marked reduction in hepatic ATP levels in Sfxn5-KO pups (Figure 4D). Differential metabolites included canonical TCA cycle intermediates, such as increased citric acid and decreased fumaric acid (Figures 4E and 4F), indicating disruption of TCA cycle flux. In addition, subcellular metabolite analysis revealed altered citric acid distribution, with decreased mitochondrial citric acid (Figure S5A) and increased cytosolic citric acid (Figure S5B) in Sfxn5-deficient livers. In line with these changes, phosphoric acid—a substrate essential for ATP synthesis—was reduced (Figure 4G), further supporting impaired mitochondrial bioenergetics. In parallel, pathways related to coenzyme A and cofactor biosynthesis were downregulated (Figure 4C, blue arrows). Correspondingly, pantothenic acid (vitamin B5) and niacin levels were significantly decreased (Figures 4H and 4I), suggesting limited availability of key metabolic cofactors required to sustain mitochondrial energy metabolism. These convergent metabolic features prompted us to directly evaluate mitochondrial respiratory function. Using an oxygen consumption rate (OCR)-based assay, we found that Sfxn5 deficiency led to a significant reduction both in basal respiration (Figure S5C) and carbonyl cyanide 4-(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (FCCP)-stimulated maximal respiration (Figure S5D). In contrast, both non-ATP-linked respiration following oligomycin treatment (Figure S5E) and non-mitochondrial respiration following rotenone and antimycin A treatment (Figure S5F) were comparable between WT and KO groups. Collectively, these findings demonstrate a global impairment of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation in Sfxn5-deficient cells. Consistent with this overall respiratory defect, direct enzymatic analysis revealed a significant reduction in mitochondrial complex III (cytochrome bc1 complex) activity in Sfxn5-KO livers (Figure 4J).

Notably, fatty acyl species accounted for ∼18% of all differential metabolites (Figure 4B), indicating broad lipid metabolic dysregulation. Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) pathway analysis showed enrichment of carnitine-related pathways (Figure 4K, black arrow), prompting further examination of mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation (FAO). Consistent with defective FAO, all seven significantly altered medium- and long-chain acylcarnitines were elevated in Sfxn5-KO livers (Figure 4L), including a ∼48-fold increase in (7E)-5-hydroxydodec-7-enoylcarnitine (C12:1-OH). Accumulation of multiple medium- and long-chain acylcarnitines represents a canonical metabolic signature of defective mitochondrial β-oxidation.

HMDB pathway analysis also indicated perturbation of the urea cycle (Figure 4K, pink arrow). Consistently, hepatic expression of carbamoyl phosphate synthetase 1 (Cps1), the rate-limiting enzyme initiating the urea cycle, was markedly upregulated in Sfxn5-KO livers (Figure 4M), a compensatory response commonly associated with increased ammonia burden.24,25 Given that the urea cycle is highly ATP-dependent and that hyperammonemia was observed in Sfxn5-deficient pups (Figure 3J), these data suggest that impaired mitochondrial energy production secondary to defects in oxidative phosphorylation, TCA cycling, and FAO compromises hepatic ammonia detoxification capacity. Together, these mitochondrial metabolic failures converge to drive hepatic dysfunction and contribute to liver failure in Sfxn5-deficient pups.

Brain metabolomics reveal Sfxn5-dependent perturbations under hepatic metabolic collapse

Given that Sfxn5-deficient pups develop profound hepatic mitochondrial failure, hyperammonemia and widespread hepatic metabolic perturbations—factors known to influence brain metabolic homeostasis26—prior to neurological deterioration, and considering the high expression of Sfxn5 in the brain, we next examined the impact of Sfxn5 loss on cerebral metabolism. Untargeted metabolomics identified 152 upregulated and 156 downregulated metabolites in KO brains (Figure 5A and Table S4). Metabolite classification revealed a distribution similar to that observed in the liver, with ∼16% of differential metabolites annotated as fatty acyls and ∼28% as carboxylic acids and derivatives (Figure 5B). Pathway analysis further highlighted perturbations in cofactor metabolism (Figures 5C and 5D, blue arrow) and the TCA cycle (Figure 5C, red arrow), including canonical metabolites such as pantothenate (Figure 5E), niacin (Figure 5F), and citric acid (Figure 5G), which exhibited alterations comparable to those observed in the liver.

Given the prominent alteration of fatty acyl species, we further focused on acylcarnitines. In the brain, five of seven significantly altered medium- and long-chain acylcarnitines were elevated in Sfxn5-KO tissues (Figure 5H). Notably, (7E)-5-hydroxydodec-7-enoylcarnitine (C12:1-OH) exhibited a markedly greater increase in the brain (KO/WT ≈ 996-fold) than in the liver. The concordant accumulation of multiple medium- and long-chain acylcarnitines across tissues represents one of the canonical metabolic signatures of impaired mitochondrial β-oxidation.

In parallel, pathway analysis revealed a prominent enrichment of sterol-related pathways (Figure 5D, green arrow). Notably, a brain-specific metabolic alteration was observed in sterol biosynthesis, characterized by a marked reduction in cholesterol (Figure 5I) levels accompanied by accumulation of its biosynthetic intermediate zymosterol (Figure 5J). These changes are consistent with our previous report linking Sfxn5 deficiency to cholesterol insufficiency.19 Given the essential role of cholesterol in myelin architecture and synaptic membrane organization, such a deficiency is expected to result in hypomyelination, leading to motor dysfunction and ataxia. Consistent with this notion, Sfxn5-deficient mice developed pronounced tremors at later stages (Video S1). Blood-brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction is a recognized feature of hepatic encephalopathy.27,28 In line with this, we detected a significant reduction in claudin-5 (Cldn-5), a brain microvascular endothelial cell-specific tight junction protein,29 in Sfxn5-deficient brains (Figure 5K). Together with systemic hyperammonemia, these findings indicate compromised BBB integrity in Sfxn5-KO mice.

These metabolic abnormalities were accompanied by pathological cellular responses in the brain. Expression of aquaporin-4 (Aqp-4), a key mediator of astrocytic edema,27,30 was significantly increased (Figure 5L), along with robust upregulation of the astrocytic activation marker GFAP (Figures S3C and S3D). Collectively, these data indicate that intrinsic metabolic defects within the brain, together with hyperammonemia secondary to hepatic mitochondrial dysfunction, act in concert to promote severe neurological deterioration in Sfxn5-deficient mice.

Sfxn5 deficiency-induced immune dysfunction minimally contributes to the lethality of KO pups

Consistent with the known immunological consequences of mitochondrial dysfunction, Sfxn5-KO pups displayed splenic hypoplasia (Figure 2A), suggesting impaired immune competence. Hematological analysis at P26 revealed pronounced leukopenia (Figure S6A), evidenced by significant reductions in both absolute neutrophil and lymphocyte counts (Figures 6A and 6B). Flow cytometry further confirmed decreased mature neutrophils and pan T cells in peripheral blood (Figures S6C–S6E), while T cell subsets remained unaltered (Figures S6F and S6G). In parallel, elevated erythrocyte counts (Figure 6C), increased hemoglobin levels (Figure S6B), and the presence of Howell-Jolly bodies in peripheral erythrocytes (Figures 6D and 6E) indicated splenic hypofunction, consistent with the observed atrophy. Although splenic neutrophil percentages were reduced and pan T cell proportions increased (Figures 6F, 6G, and S6H), T cell subsets again remained unchanged (Figures S6I–S6K).

Given the marked neutropenia and erythroid abnormalities, we next examined bone marrow transcriptional regulators. Efficient Sfxn5 KO in bone marrow was confirmed (Figure S7A). Unexpectedly, erythroid differentiation markers, including Gata1 and Klf1, exhibited no significant transcriptional changes (Figures S7B and S7C). By contrast, myeloid regulators (Cebpa, Spi1, Mpo) were significantly upregulated (Figures S7D–S7F), paralleling an increased neutrophil frequency in KO bone marrow (Figures 6H and 6I). To assess whether defective maturation contributed to peripheral neutropenia, bone marrow smears were analyzed and revealed a predominance of banded/annular neutrophils in KO pups (Figures 6J and 6K). This immaturity was corroborated by reduced expression of Cxcr2 and Cd101, markers of maturation (Figures S7G and S7H). Together, these findings demonstrate that Sfxn5 deficiency induces granulocytic maturation arrest and bone marrow retention, thereby explaining the profound neutropenia observed in peripheral blood.

To determine whether neutrophil maturation defects directly contributed to lethality, we tested whether granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) supplementation, a classical enhancer of granulocyte differentiation and maturation, could rescue survival. G-CSF treatment produced only a modest extension of lifespan (Figures 6L and 6M), indicating limited benefit and prompting evaluation of whether immune abnormalities were primary or secondary. In lethally irradiated WT recipients, bone marrow transplantation (BMT) from WT or Sfxn5-KO donors (Figure S8A) resulted in normal survival beyond 12 weeks for both groups (Figure S8B). Flow cytometry further showed comparable lymphocyte and neutrophil proportions between WT BMT and Sfxn5-KO BMT mice (Figures S8C and S8D). These findings collectively indicate that immune dysregulation is a secondary consequence of systemic metabolic failure, rather than the primary driver of lethality in Sfxn5-deficient mice.

Liver-specific restoration of Sfxn5 ameliorates multi-organ failure

To determine whether hepatic Sfxn5 loss is the key driver of the systemic metabolic collapse and multi-organ failure observed in Sfxn5-KO pups, we performed liver-specific overexpression of Sfxn5 using an adeno-associated virus (AAV) under a hepatocyte-specific promoter. Given that Sfxn5-KO pups begin to exhibit substantial weight loss by P18, control AAV (KO-Ctrl) or Sfxn5-overexpressing AAV (KO-Sfxn5-AAV) was administered via retro-orbital injection at P18, and tissues were collected at P26 (Figure S9A). Robust hepatic overexpression and specificity of Sfxn5 were confirmed (Figure S9B).

Liver-targeted Sfxn5 overexpression did not significantly affect body weight or overall size (Figures 7A and S9C). However, organ examination revealed a clear rescue of splenic atrophy and pallor in KO pups (Figure 7B), accompanied by correction of the spleen index (Figure S9D). Histological analyses demonstrated that myocardial hypertrophy was alleviated to some extent, and that the architecture of the spleen, lung, and kidney appeared more comparable to that of WT controls (Figure S9E). Notably, Sfxn5-AAV partially reduced plasma ALT/glutamate pyruvate transaminase (GPT) and AST/glutamate oxaloacetate transaminase (GOT) levels (Figures 7C and 7D) and significantly ameliorated hyperammonemia (Figure 7E), indicating partial restoration of hepatic function. Histological analyses further showed reduced hepatocellular death (Figures 7F and 7G) and diminished ballooning lipid droplets in Sfxn5-AAV-treated KO livers (Figures 7H and 7I), yielding an architecture more comparable to WT controls. At the molecular level, Sfxn5 overexpression partially restored mitochondrial complex III activity (Figure 7J) and hepatic ATP levels (Figure 7K). Consistent with improved metabolic function, expression of key metabolic regulators, Cps1 (urea cycle), was shifted toward WT levels (Figure 7L). We next evaluated whether secondary systemic abnormalities were also improved. Compared with KO-Ctrl pups, those receiving Sfxn5-AAV showed partial normalization of peripheral blood leukocyte, neutrophil, and erythrocyte counts (Figures 7M, 7O, and S9H), as well as bone marrow neutrophil profiles (Figures S9F and S9G). Extrahepatic phenotypes were likewise mitigated. Cardiac stress markers ANP/BNP were reduced (Figure S9I), and the elevated GFAP expression in KO brains was partially corrected (Figure 7P). Importantly, hepatic Sfxn5 restoration abolished the overt tremor observed in KO pups by P26 (Video S2).

Discussion

This study identifies SFXN5 as an essential, non-redundant factor for postnatal survival. Global ablation of Sfxn5 results in early lethality accompanied by a pronounced hepatic metabolic crisis, progressive cachexia, and multi-organ failure, phenocopying key features of human MD. Mechanistically, loss of SFXN5 disrupts multiple core metabolic pathways—including the TCA cycle, fatty acid β-oxidation, and the urea cycle—thereby precipitating severe hepatic mitochondrial dysfunction. The resulting hepatic failure, together with hyperammonemia-induced neurotoxicity, collectively drives the fatal outcomes observed in Sfxn5-deficient pups. Our findings demonstrate that the liver serves as the principal organ orchestrating Sfxn5-dependent lethality and underscore a fundamental role for SFXN5 in sustaining mitochondrial metabolic homeostasis in both liver and brain.

The phenotype of Sfxn5-null mice exhibits remarkable parallels to severe infantile MDs. The postnatal progression—from cachexia, hypoglycemia, and hyperlactatemia to rapid lethality—recapitulates the clinical trajectory of neonatal mitochondrial complex I deficiencies.31,32 Multi-organ pathology, including hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, hepatic microvesicular steatosis with extensive apoptosis, and splenic hypoplasia, further mirrors the systemic involvement that characterizes MDs.4,33 Notably, the terminal presentation of ataxia, hyperammonemia, and neuroinflammation is highly reminiscent of hepatic encephalopathy, a catastrophic complication frequently associated with liver-centric metabolic collapse in MDs such as those arising from POLG mutations.9

A central conclusion of this study is that the liver functions as the metabolic epicenter of pathology in Sfxn5 deficiency. Although Sfxn5 is expressed across multiple tissues, hepatic impairment clearly represents the earliest and primary site of dysfunction. Subclinical liver injury was detectable prior to overt phenotypic decline, as reflected by an isolated elevation of AST at P11, preceding any measurable differences in body weight or histopathology. As the disease progressed, this early biochemical abnormality evolved into fulminant hepatic failure, characterized by marked transaminase elevation, hyperbilirubinemia, extensive hepatocyte apoptosis, and critically, loss of ammonia-detoxifying capacity. Metabolome profiling indicates that this hepatic failure is rooted in profound mitochondrial dysfunction. Functionally, mitochondrial respiratory capacity is broadly compromised by Sfxn5 deficiency, as evidenced by reduced basal and maximal OCR, indicating a global impairment of oxidative phosphorylation. Disruption of the TCA cycle and subsequent ATP depletion undermined the energetically demanding urea cycle, leading to progressive and ultimately lethal hyperammonemia.34 In parallel, impaired mitochondrial β-oxidation—evidenced by microvesicular steatosis and a shift in acylcarnitine species31,35—eliminated a major source of metabolic fuel during postnatal life. Together, these defects define a cascade of hepatic metabolic collapse that aligns with features of human mitochondrial hepatopathies. Notably, the immune phenotypes observed in Sfxn5-null pups, including neutropenia and splenic hypoplasia, appear to be secondary rather than primary defects. Although neutropenia may contribute to terminal vulnerability, the maturation arrest observed in bone marrow progenitors and the restoration of splenic architecture upon liver-specific Sfxn5 re-expression strongly indicate that these abnormalities arise downstream of systemic metabolic failure rather than from intrinsic defects in hematopoietic cells. This conclusion is further supported by BMT experiments, which show that Sfxn5-deficient hematopoietic cells retain full reconstitution capacity in a WT host, confirming that their developmental potential remains intact in the absence of metabolic collapse.

Successful partial rescue through hepatocyte-specific Sfxn5 reconstitution provides direct genetic evidence that hepatic Sfxn5 deficiency is the principal driver of postnatal lethality. Restoring Sfxn5 expression in the liver not only ameliorated hepatic histopathology and improved biochemical indices, but also conferred measurable benefits to extrahepatic systems—attenuating cardiac stress responses, reducing cerebral astrogliosis, and partially correcting hematological deficits. These systemic improvements highlight the liver’s dominant role in orchestrating metabolic homeostasis during early postnatal life. In this context, the brain abnormalities and aberrant metabolite accumulation observed in Sfxn5-null pups are most plausibly interpreted as secondary manifestations of hepatic metabolic failure, rather than primary neurodevelopmental defects, further supported by the early emergence of hepatic dysfunction prior to detectable brain alterations. Nonetheless, the possibility that intrinsic neurological phenotypes emerge at later developmental stages cannot be excluded and warrants targeted longitudinal evaluation.

Our findings also reveal conserved pathomechanisms within the SFXN family. While SFXN1-4 regulate serine transport, iron trafficking, and cristae integrity,13,14,15,16,17 SFXN5 emerges as a guardian of hepatic mitochondrial homeostasis. Phenotypic overlap between Sfxn5-KO mice and SFXN4-mutant patients—including oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) defects, lactic acidosis, and neurological abnormalities—reinforces the family’s conserved regulatory role. The male-biased lethality here contrasts with female-specific SFXN4 mutations,17 suggesting sex-dependent metabolic demands worthy of further study. Absence of reported human SFXN5 mutations may indicate embryonic lethality rather than functional redundancy.

In conclusion, this study redefines SFXN5 from a putative mitochondrial transporter to a critical regulator of postnatal metabolic adaptation. We provide the first in vivo evidence demonstrating that SFXN5 is indispensable for mammalian postnatal survival and establish a mechanistic link between SFXN5-associated mitochondrial dysfunction and fatal hepatic metabolic crisis. Future studies should further elucidate the precise molecular functions of SFXN5, explore its potential contribution to currently unresolved human mitochondrial disorders, and assess the therapeutic efficacy of liver-targeted intervention strategies.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although we have initiated mechanistic analyses, the precise role of Sfxn5 in mitochondrial respiratory function remains incompletely defined. Loss of Sfxn5 was associated with reduced complex III activity, which likely represents one component of a broader mitochondrial respiratory defect rather than a direct regulatory effect on a specific respiratory complex. Consistent with this, OCR-based analyses indicate a global impairment of mitochondrial function in Sfxn5-deficient tissues. Future work should therefore dissect how Sfxn5 contributes to mitochondrial respiratory function, including potential roles in complex assembly, stability, or higher order organization, by defining its interactome and assessing its influence on the organization and integrity of respiratory supercomplexes. Second, although hepatocyte-specific re-expression of Sfxn5 partially rescued multi-organ failure and ameliorated systemic metabolic defects, significant metabolic perturbations persisted in the brain. Our data indicate that hepatic dysfunction precedes detectable alterations in brain energy metabolism and neural markers at early stages, supporting a model in which liver pathology represents an initial driver of systemic abnormalities. Nevertheless, whether the later stage cerebral metabolic dysregulation further contributes to disease progression or primarily reflects downstream effects of hepatic failure remains to be fully defined. Targeted temporal and cell-type-specific studies will be required to further clarify the contribution of these neurological alterations.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mice and zebrafish

Sfxn5-knockout (KO) mice were generated by Cyagen Biosciences (Strain S-KO-15572) on a C57BL/6NCrl background. These mice harbored a 1.6-kb deletion spanning exons 4–5 of Sfxn5. Mice were genotyped at postnatal day 7 (P7) using the following primers: SFXN5-F1, 5′-TCAAAGTTCCCTTGGTTAGAGAACA-3′; SFXN5-R1, 5′-CAGGTCTAGATGCAATGGCTTAGG-3′; and SFXN5-R2, 5′-TAGTTTAAGCTCTATGAGCCATGC-3′. Sfxn5-KO pups were co-housed with their littermate controls under identical conditions. Mice were analyzed at the indicated postnatal stages, as specified in the corresponding methods and figure legends. Because Sfxn5-KO pups showed an abnormal sex ratio, with a male-to-female ratio of approximately 4:1 as described in the results, only male Sfxn5-KO pups and sex-matched littermate controls were used for subsequent phenotypic, biochemical, histological, behavioral, and metabolomic analyses.

Eight-week-old C57BL/6NCrl mice (Strain 213; Charles River) were used for bone marrow transplantation experiments. Both male and female mice were randomly included.

All mice were housed under standard specific pathogen-free conditions at 25°C with a 12-h light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water.

AB wild-type zebrafish larvae were maintained under standard conditions at 28.5°C, pH 7.2–7.4, conductivity 500–550 μS/cm, and a 14-h light/10-h dark cycle. Zebrafish larvae were used at 5 days post-fertilization (dpf).

All animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chongqing Medical University (IACUC-CQMU-2024-02020).

No human participants, human samples, cell lines, or clinical trial data were included in this study.

Method details

Whole-mount in situ hybridization (WISH)

Digoxin-labeled sfxn5b antisense/sense RNA probes were synthesized by RT-PCR amplification of zebrafish cDNA using primers: sfxn5b FOR: 5′-GCGGGAATTCAATGTGGTGGGATCCTCAC-3′, sfxn5b REV: 5′-CCGCGAATTCTTCCATATCATTGACCGTACAT-3′, followed by in vitro transcription (DIG RNA Labeling Kit; Roche). WISH was performed as previously described.36 In detail, AB wild-type embryos were fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4°C, rinsed in phosphate-buffered solution (PBS) containing 0.1% Tween 20, dehydrated through methanol gradients, and stored at −20°C. After rehydration, embryos were proteinase K-digested (Beyotime) and hybridized in HM hybridization buffer (65% (v/v) formamide, 5x SSC, 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20, 50 μg/mL heparin, 0.5 mg/mL tRNA, and 9.2 mM citric acid) plus sfxn5b probes (68°C). Signals were detected with BCIP/NBT (Solarbio), and stained embryos were mounted in 4% PFA for imaging (Zeiss SteREO Discovery V12).

L-lactate and blood ammonia assay

Blood samples were collected from mouse pups at P26 into EDTA-Na2 tubes. Plasma was isolated by centrifugation 1,000 × g for 15 min. L-Lactate levels were quantified using an L-Lactate Assay Kit (Beyotime) based on the WST-8 enzymatic reaction according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Plasma samples were prepared as described above, 50 μL of the sample was added to the designated sample wells of a 96-well plate, followed by the addition of 50 μL of WST-8 Working Solution to each well. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 30 min in the dark, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm (Tecan, Infinite E plex).

Blood ammonia levels were quantified using a Blood Ammonia Colorimetric Assay Kit (Elabscience) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Plasma samples were prepared as described earlier, and 300 μL of acid reagent was added to 100 μL of sample. After vortex mixing, the tubes were centrifuged at 1,100 × g for 10 min. Chromogenic agents were added, followed by mixing with a microplate reader for 5 s, and incubation at 37°C for 25 min. The OD value was measured at 635 nm (Tecan, Infinite E plex).

Wright-Giemsa staining

The blood and bone marrow smears were air-dried, stained with Wright-Giemsa solution (Baso) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. After drying, smears were imaged under a Leica DM6B fluorescence microscope.

Hematological parameter assessment

Blood drops were obtained after a 0.5 mm section of the tail tip. Blood glucose was measured using a CONTOUR NEXT blood glucose meter (Ascensia, Bayer). EDTA-K2-anticoagulated blood was analyzed on a BC-5000 Vet hematology analyzer (Mindray). Heparinized plasma was assessed for metabolic parameters using an SMT-120VP analyzer (Seamaty).

Additionally, plasma alanine aminotransferase (ALT/GPT, Elabscience), aspartate aminotransferase (AST/GOT, Elabscience) activities, total bilirubin (TBIL, Elabscience) and triglyceride (TG, Elabscience) were supplemented with quantitative assays using dedicated colorimetric kits, strictly adhering to the manufacturer’s operating procedures, all OD value were measured by Tecan, Infinite E plex. In detail, blood samples for ALT/GPT, AST/GOT, TBIL and TG were collected from mouse pups into EDTA-Na2 tubes and plasma was isolated by centrifugation 1,000 × g for 15 min.

For ALT/GPT or AST/GOT, add 20 μL of substrate solution (pre-heated at 37°C for10 min) and 5 μL of sample, mix well, then incubate at 37°C for 30 min. Add 20 μL of chromogenic agent to each well. Mix well with microplate reader for 10 s, incubate at 37°C for 20 min. Add 200 μL of alkali working solution to each well. Mix fully with microplate reader for 10 s, stand for 15 min at room temperature and measure the OD value at 510 nm.

For TBIL (E-BC-K760-M), take 80 μL of acid agent into 0.5 mL EP tube, add 160 μL of working solution into sample tubes. Add 30 μL of sample into sample tubes. Mix fully, incubate at 37°C for 5 min. Then, add 20 μL of stop solution into each tube and incubate at 37°C for 5 min. Take 250 μL of reaction solution into the corresponding wells and measure the OD values of each well at 565 nm with microplate reader.

For TG (E-BC-K261-M), take 2.5 μL of sample to the wells. Add 250 μL of enzyme working solution to each well and incubate at 37 °C for 10 min, then measure the OD values of each well with microplate reader at 510 nm.

Organ index

Tissues were collected from pups. Organ indices were calculated as: Organ Index = 100 × [Organ Weight (g)/Body Weight (g)].

Mitochondrial complex III activity assay

Liver mitochondrial complex III (coenzyme Q-cytochrome c reductase) activity was measured using a commercial assay kit (Elabscience) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The reduction of cytochrome c was monitored by measuring the absorbance at 550 nm in a microplate reader (Tecan). Enzyme activity was calculated from the linear change in absorbance and normalized to total protein content. In detail, homogenize 50 mg of fresh liver tissue in 900 μL of extraction solution A using a Dounce homogenizer at 4°C. Centrifuge the homogenate at 600 × g for 5 min, discard the pellet, and collect the supernatant. Subsequently, centrifuge the supernatant at 15,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, discard the supernatant, and retain the pellet. Resuspend the pellet in a mixture of 200 μL of extraction solution B and 10 μL of extraction solution C, followed by sonication for 1 min. Centrifuge the sonicated mixture at 11,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C and collect the resulting supernatant. To prepare the total enzyme activity sample, mix the supernatant with an inhibitor control solution in a 1:1 volume ratio (20 μL each) thoroughly. Incubate the mixture at room temperature, protected from light, for 5 min before use. Add 10 μL of reaction working solution into each well of a 96-well microplate, followed by 90 μL of buffer solution and 20 μL of the total enzyme activity sample. Then add 40 μL of substrate A to each well. Mix thoroughly using a microplate shaker for 5 s. Measure the absorbance of each well at 550 nm using a microplate reader at 10 s and 70 s after initiation of the reaction.

Citric acid assay

Citric acid levels were measured using a commercial citric acid assay kit (Solarbio, BC2155) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Hepatocytes were collected from livers of pups at P26 and processed on ice. For total cellular citric acid extraction, cells were lysed in extraction buffer followed by centrifugation at 11,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for analysis.

For cytosolic citric acid measurement, the supernatant obtained after initial centrifugation was directly used. For mitochondrial citric acid measurement, the remaining pellet was further processed with mitochondrial extraction reagents, followed by centrifugation at 11,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was collected as the mitochondrial fraction.

Samples were mixed with assay reagents and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 545 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan, Infinite E plex). Citric acid levels were calculated based on the standard curve and normalized as indicated.

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) assay

OCR was measured using a commercial fluorometric OCR assay kit (Elabscience, E-BC-F070) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Hepatocytes were seeded in black-walled, clear-bottom 96-well plates at an appropriate density and cultured overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After removal of culture medium, 100 μL of pre-warmed working solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, compounds were added as indicated (FCCP: 2 μM, oligomycin: 1 μM, rotenone: 0.5 μM, antimycinA: 0.5 μM), followed by the addition of sealing solution to each well to create a closed environment. Fluorescence signals were measured using a microplate reader (Tecan, Infinite E plex) in kinetic mode at 37 °C (excitation: 405 nm; emission: 650 nm), with readings taken every 2 min for at least 90 min. OCR was calculated based on the linear change in fluorescence intensity over time.

ATP assay

Liver or Brain ATP content was quantified using a commercial ATP detection kit (Beyotime) based on the luciferin-luciferase bioluminescence method. Add 200 μL of lysis buffer per 20 mg of fresh tissue. After thorough homogenization, centrifuge the lysate at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C and collect the supernatant. Add 100 μL of ATP assay working solution to each well of a 96-well plate. Incubate the plate at room temperature for 3-5 min to eliminate background ATP. Subsequently, add 20 μL of the sample to each well, mix immediately by gentle pipetting, and measure the luminescence by microplate reader (Tecan, Infinite E plex) after a delay of at least 2 s.

Histology, immunofluorescence, and imaging

Brain, heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney tissues were fixed in 4% PFA, and paraffin-embedded sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E). For Oil Red O staining, tissues were embedded in OCT compound, cryosectioned, and stained with Oil Red O to visualize neutral lipid accumulation.

For Immunofluorescence, the paraffin-embedded sections were dewaxed with xylene and rehydrated through an alcohol gradient. Endogenous peroxidase activity and antigen repair were blocked prior to blocking the non-specific antibody binding site on the section with 5% fetal bovine serum albumin. The sections were incubated with a GFAP antibody (servicebio), NeuN antibody (servicebio) or TdT-mediated dUTP-biotin nick end labeling (TUNEL) (servicebio). Images were acquired using a Leica DMi-8 microscope and analyzed blindly using ImageJ software.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Liver tissue blocks were fixed with 2% glutaraldehyde. The samples underwent various processing steps, such as fixation, gradient alcohol dehydration, and displacement, and were scanned for mitochondria using a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, HT7800/HT7700).

Western blot

Cells or tissues were homogenized in a RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. 20 μg per lane of total protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies against Sfxn5 (Abcam), β-Actin (Abclonal), β-Tubulin (Affinity), α-Tubulin (Affinity), Flag (Abclonal), and Gapdh (Servicebio) (Listed in the key resources table). Membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit/mouse secondary antibody (Abclonal) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (Beyotime) and imaged with a Bio-OI chemiluminescence detection system. Band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH), normalized to Gapdh, β-Actin α-Tubulin or β-Tubulin as loading controls.

Flow cytometry

Peripheral blood samples were collected in EDTA-K2 anticoagulant tubes and subjected to erythrocyte lysis using ACK buffer (150 mM NH4Cl, 10 mM KHCO3, 0.1 mM EDTA, pH 7.4). Bone marrow cells were isolated by flushing tibiae with ice-cold PBS supplemented with 2% FBS, followed by erythrocyte lysis under identical conditions. Splenic tissues were immediately harvested post-euthanasia, mechanically dissociated through 70-μm cell strainers, and processed with ACK lysis buffer to generate single-cell suspensions. Fluorophore-conjugated antibodies (Listed in the key resources table) were used for staining. For intracellular Foxp3 staining, cells were fixed/permeabilized (Invitrogen kit). All procedures were performed at 4°C unless specified. Data were acquired on a Guava easyCyte or Cytek Aurora flow cytometer.

Total RNA isolation and qRT-PCR

Total RNA from bone marrow and tissues was extracted with TRIzol and reverse transcribed using RT Master Mix for qPCR II (Med Chem Express). Real-time PCR amplification was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) with a Bio-Rad CFX96 system. Relative expression was quantified by a 2−ΔΔct method. The primers used for qRT-PCR were listed in Table S1.

Rescue experiments

Rescue experiments were conducted in Sfxn5-KO pups starting at postnatal day 18 (P18). Artificial Feeding Group: Received three daily intragastric administrations of milk (200 μL per dose) via oral gavage. High-Fat High-Glucose Diet (HFD+HG): Fed a high-fat diet (45% kcal from fat) ad libitum, supplemented with 10% glucose (w/v) in drinking water. Regular Chow (RC) Control: Maintained on standard rodent chow (10% kcal from fat) with plain water. G-CSF Treatment: Pups were injected i.p. with recombinant murine granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF; PeproTech) at 50 μg/kg or saline vehicle, three times weekly, and continuously measured weight mass and glucose.

For adeno-associated virus (AAV) -mediated liver-specific re-expression of Sfxn5, global Sfxn5-KO pups at P18 were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg). The pups were randomly divided into two groups: one group received 50 μL of purified AAV8 viral particles (2×1011 viral genomes in sterile saline) encoding mouse Sfxn5 with a C-terminal 3×FLAG tag under the control of the liver-specific thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG) promoter (pcscAAV-TBG-Sfxn5-3xFLAG-tWPA2; Shanghai Obio Biotechnology) via retro-orbital injection (KO-Sfxn5-AAV group). The other group received an equivalent dose of empty vector (pcscAAV-TBG-3xFLAG-tWPA2) and served as the KO control (KO-CTRL). All animals were maintained under standard conditions until the experimental endpoint at P26.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Metabolomics was performed by Bioprofile technology (Shanghai, China). Metabolites were extracted from the brain and liver tissues of WT or Sfxn5-KO pups at P26, and chromatographic separation was performed on an SHIMADZU LC-30 UHPLC system. Mass spectra were acquired in positive/negative ESI modes using a QE Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) with an HESI source. Raw data were processed using MS-DIAL for peak alignment, retention time correction, and area extraction. Metabolites were identified via exact mass and MS/MS spectral matching (HMDB, MassBank, GNPS, and in-house BP-DB). The chosen level of significance was p < 0.05. Cluster analyses using the R package were carried out using the identified differential metabolites. The differential metabolite data were subjected to pathway analysis using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database and Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) database to identify the perturbed biological pathways.

Bone marrow transplantation

8-week-old recipient mice (C57BL/6N) were lethally irradiated with 9.5 Gy from an X-ray source (RS2000pro, Rad Source). Bone marrow cells (BMCs) were harvested from the femurs and tibias of littermate donor pups (P21, WT or Sfxn5-KO). After filtration, the BMCs were resuspended in sterile saline. Bone marrow transplantation was performed by retro-orbital injection with BMCs (5 × 106 cells per recipient Mouse). Recipient mice were administered drinking water containing 0.125 g/L enrofloxacin and 10 g/L sucrose orally for 2 weeks.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism 9. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Comparisons between two groups were assessed using Student’s t test. Comparisons among multiple groups were assessed using two-way ANOVA analysis followed by Bonferroni Post Hoc Test or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Differences were considered significant if p < 0.0001: ∗∗∗∗, p < 0.001: ∗∗∗, p < 0.01: ∗∗, p < 0.05: ∗.
