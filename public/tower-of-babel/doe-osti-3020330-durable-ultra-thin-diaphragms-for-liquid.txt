Abstract
W. L. Gore & Associates assembled a world class team (De Nora Tech, LLC; New York University; and
thyssenkrupp nucera) to develop durable, ultra-low resistance diaphragms that dramatically improve system
efficiency, dynamic response, and operational flexibility. To enable the use of ultra-thin diaphragms, an optimized
electrode package was chosen as part of an electrode down select study. Ultra-thin diaphragms based on
microporous polymer scaffolds imbibed with zirconia nanoparticles were developed and tested. Performance and
durability on par with incumbent materials was demonstrated. A transparent electrolyzer to directly observe in-situ
diaphragm-bubble interactions was designed and built, and simultaneous optical and electrochemical performance
tests were performed. A Pressurized Alkaline Water Electrolysis (PAWE) single cell test station was designed to
support advanced R&D in high-pressure electrolysis, particularly for evaluating novel electrode and separator
configurations under elevated temperature and pressure operating conditions. Due to early project termination, the
original scope could not be fully achieved.

1. Summary
W. L. Gore & Associates assembled a world-class team to develop durable, ultra-low resistance diaphragms that
dramatically improve system efficiency, dynamic response, and operational flexibility. The project will advance
progress toward six specific DOE targets for Next-Generation LAWE. (1) Performance ≥ 2.0 A/cm2 @ 1.7 V, (2)
degradation ≤2.1 mV/khr, (3) enabling of dynamic operation, (4) Bubble Management, (5) high temperature
operation, (6) enabling of pressure differentials.

The team consisted of De Nora Tech, LLC (De Nora); New York University (NYU); and thyssenkrupp nucera
(nucera). Mechanistic insights into bubble phenomena like nucleation, growth, and release were developed in
collaboration with NYU using a custom-designed electrochemical flow cell with in-situ optical microscopy and
applied to optimize diaphragm structure and composition. De Nora supplied state-of-the-art electrodes tailored to
the needs of ultra-thin separators, and nucera advised the project team to ensure industry relevant LAWE testing
under both standard and enhanced operating conditions (e.g., enabling high temperature and differential pressure).

The intended work for this project is separated into 3 phases. (i.e., Phases 1-3). The Phases incorporate milestones
and are separated by clear Go/No-Go decision points. Due to the early termination of the project, Phase 1 was partially
completed, and the remaining two phases were not started. A detailed summary of milestones and completion status
is provided in Appendix A. Phase 1 focused on developing process-structure-property relationships for individual
DOE targets while expanding LAWE test capacity & capabilities. Phase 2 intended to focus on meeting combined
DOE targets and developing protocols for enhanced operating conditions. Phase 3 intended to focus on demonstrating
DOE targets have been met and raising TRL by testing pilot-scale prototypes in a small stack.

The following information in this section summarizes the work completed in Phase 1.

Milestone 1 Objective: Electrode packages systematically compared in-situ and ex-situ and 2-3 sets down-
selected to meet individual DOE targets.
A study was conducted to identify an electrode substrate package that supports the use of ultrathin diaphragms.
Testing consisted of measuring ohmic contribution from a polarization curve slope and then evaluating the tested
diaphragms for pinholes. A calendared #40 woven wire cathode and expanded metal anode (non-die side facing the
diaphragm) were chosen as the optimized substrate configuration to enable the use of ultrathin diaphragms. This
electrode package did not generate pinholes when a 20.5 µm diaphragm was used in the test cell. It also produced a
lower ohmic resistance when compared to a baseline electrode package paired with a AGFA UTP 200 diaphragm.

Milestone 3 Objective: Flow cell has been optimized, simultaneous EIS & optical microscopy demonstrated, plus
control of bubble size, quantity, & flow rate.
An electrochemical flow cell enabling high speed optical observation of gas bubble emissions and simultaneous
electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements was developed by NYU. Significant increases in
bubble population were noted with increased current density. EIS measurements indicated reduced ohmic resistance
due to bubbles when flow rate of liquid electrolyte was increased.

Milestone 4 Objective: Diaphragm prototypes systematically compared in-situ and ex-situ and 2-3 sets have been
down-selected for benchmarking.
Gore produced a series of ultra-thin diaphragm prototypes for testing with the optimized electrode configuration.
These prototypes varied the polymeric scaffold type, zirconia nanoparticle loading level, and employed various
surface treatments. Prototypes were first characterized at Gore, then the most promising samples were sent to
DeNora for electrochemical evaluation. Ultra-thin PTFE/ZrO2 diaphragm combined with surface treatments to
reduce bubble effects showed initial electrochemical performance comparable to the control AGFA UTP separator
at normal operating conditions. The most promising samples ran for 2 weeks in an electrolyzer to probe voltage
stability and gas crossover safety, with no apparent degradation noted. In comparison, polyethylene scaffold based
diaphragms showed significant voltage rise over the 2 week electrolyzer durability test.

2. Introduction
Clean hydrogen is a versatile energy carrier with unique potential to abate CO2 emissions in multiple industries.
Liquid Alkaline (LA) Water Electrolysis (WE) is the most mature technology for producing clean hydrogen at
gigawatt scale, but performance is limited by the high resistance of state-of-the-art separators (e.g., Zirfon®
diaphragms). The W. L. Gore & Associates Clean Energy Business Group (Gore) has a 30-year history of developing,
scaling, and manufacturing cutting-edge separators, such as industry-leading GORE-SELECT® membranes. Gore has
conducted significant exploratory research on ultra-thin diaphragms for over 2 years. Projections with further
diaphragm improvements and including bubble management indicate the potential to meet or even exceed U.S.
Department of Energy (DOE) targets. However, overcoming remaining performance barriers, integrating state-of-the-
art electrodes, enabling enhanced operating conditions, and demonstrating durability in a commercially relevant
system still pose significant challenges. Therefore, in a comprehensive approach to achieve the DOE targets for next-
generation LAWE, Gore assembled a world-class team to develop and optimize durable, ultra-thin diaphragms for
high performance and durable operation under dynamic, higher-temperature, and differential-pressure conditions. Our
team consisted of an academic-industrial-National Lab consortium comprising Gore; De Nora Tech, LLC (De Nora);
New York University (NYU); thyssenkrupp nucera (nucera); and H2NEW.

In collaboration with the partners listed above, the project intended to advance six DOE targets for Next-Generation
LAWE. Targets for (1) performance ≥ 2.0 A/cm² @ 1.7 V, (2) degradation ≤ 2.1 mV/khr, and (3) elevated
temperature operation were to be enabled by Gore’s core technology for stabilizing ultra-thin composite materials
through microporous reinforcement (including, but not limited to, expanded polytetrafluoroethylene or ePTFE),
combined with De Nora’s expertise in tailoring electrodes for ultra-thin separators and nucera’s deep system
knowledge. The target for (4) improved bubble management was supported by Gore’s 50-year history of optimizing
gas–liquid interactions in membrane systems (e.g., GORE-TEX® Fabrics) and fundamental studies of bubble
dynamics at NYU. Finally, the targets for (5) differential pressure and (6) dynamic operation was to be addressed
through the unique pore structure of Gore’s prototypes, which demonstrate extremely low liquid permeance and
high bubble point.

The project was structured into three phases (Phases 1–3), each defined by specific milestones and separated by
Go/No-Go decision points. Due to early termination on 15 July 2025, this report addresses only the work completed
in Phase 1. A detailed summary of milestones and completion status is provided in Appendix A. Phase 1 emphasized
development of process–structure–property relationships for individual DOE targets and expansion of LAWE test
capacity and capabilities. Phase 2 intended to integrate DOE targets and establish protocols for enhanced operating
conditions, while Phase 3 was designed to demonstrate target achievement and advance TRL through pilot-scale stack
testing.

In Phase 1, Gore iteratively produced ultra-thin diaphragms at lab scale, leveraging the “Reinforced Colloidal Glass
Membrane” technology, and optimized the diaphragms on parallel tracks for (1) performance ≥ 2.0 A/cm2 @ 1.7 V,
and (2) degradation ≤2.1 mV/khr. De Nora applied existing, state-of-the-art catalytic coatings to substrates. Materials
were thoroughly characterized ex-situ (Gore, De Nora), and tested under standard LAWE conditions (De Nora,
H2NEW). De Nora and Gore began expanding existing LAWE test capacity by designing and building (with guidance
from nucera) test stations to enable enhanced operating conditions (i.e., (3) elevated temp. up to 120-150C, (5)
differential pressures up to 30 bar). NYU leveraged existing analytical equipment (microscopy, EIS) and 3D printing
capabilities to design, build, and start up an electrochemical flow cell to study interactions related to (4) improved
bubble management. Development of a technoeconomic model (leveraging an external consultant to formalize &

extend project team expertise) to guide further material development was not performed due to early termination of
the project.

Phase 2 was not started due to early termination of the project. In Phase 2, Gore intended to iteratively combine the
best attributes of the down-selected diaphragms with a goal of meeting the DOE targets for (1) performance and (2)
degradation in a single material, while also leveraging the NYU cell to improve (4) bubble management. The key
material properties to enable (3) elevated temp, (5) differential pressures, and (6) dynamic operation were to be
benchmarked and studied using the new test stations developed in Phase 1. To mitigate risk, project data was expected
to be used to regularly update the technoeconomic model and our understanding of process-structure-property
relationships, both of which would be critical for managing any property trade-offs to achieve all 6 challenging DOE
targets in a single diaphragm/electrode combination. Furthermore, up to 3 material sets were to be optimized in
parallel, and the collective material and system expertise of the project team was expected to provide multiple
approaches to solve problems or break trade-offs (e.g., a durability issue arising from an ultra-thin diaphragm may be
resolved by changing aspects of the diaphragm, electrode package, operating conditions, or some combination of the
three). Finally, Gore intended to evaluate the pilot-scale prototyping equipment and order long-lead-time items that
would be needed for scale-up. The expected result of Phase 2 was to be a down-selection of the most promising 1 or
2 diaphragm/electrode combinations that meet or approach the combined DOE targets, the corresponding process-
structure-property relationships, a refined technoeconomic model, and ordered critical items to allow modification of
process equipment.

Phase 3 was not started due to early termination of the project. In Phase 3, Gore planned to down-select the most
promising 1 or 2 diaphragm/electrode combinations at lab scale, while in parallel upgrading pilot-scale, roll-to-roll
(R2R) process equipment. Up to 2 diaphragms were then be scaled up, thoroughly characterized, and tested against
targets (1) to (6) under standard and enhanced LAWE conditions for up to 5000h at De Nora & H2NEW. A final,
comprehensive technoeconomic analysis was expected to quantify the impact on LAWE capital and operating costs.
At this point the project team would have demonstrated all DOE targets with a single diaphragm; produced a roll of
down-selected diaphragm and successfully tested it in a small stack; and a completed a comprehensive
technoeconomic analysis of the impact of the innovation on electrolyzer operating and capital cost.

Within this paper, procedures are provided to perform prototype sampling, electrochemical evaluation, ex-situ
diaphragm testing, and bubble management analysis. The Optical Flow Cell and Advanced Test Stations were custom
built for this project, but due to the early termination of the project, both pieces of equipment were not fully completed.
Results are supplied that assess electrode performance, lab scale ultrathin diaphragms performance, and flow cell
bubble interactions. Based on these results, conclusions are drawn to suggest an optimized electrode package, potential
diaphragms that would meet the required DOE targets, and flow cell bubble dynamics that can be used to improve
future optimization efforts.

3. Methods, Assumptions, and Procedures
 3.1. Sample Preparation
 Microporous polymer scaffolds comprising either expanded tetrafluoroethylene or stretched polyethylene
 were restrained in an 8-inch aluminum hoop using a closed loop of steel spring. The membranes were
 tensioned by hand until the surface was flat and wrinkle free.

 An imbibing fluid was prepared by combining a colloidal dispersion of yttria-stabilized zirconia
 nanoparticles (ZRYS3 available from Nyacol Nano Technologies, Inc.) with an aqueous wetting package as
 follows. The nanoparticles were nominally 3 mol% yttria, 32 nanometers in diameter (D), and the as-
 received colloidal dispersion was 52 mass% in water. To the as-received colloidal dispersion, 3 mass% 1-
 hexanol and 3 mass% Tergitol TMN-10 were added, and the mixture was agitated vigorously to emulsify
 the 1-hexanol. About 1 g of the mixture was pipetted onto a first surface of the restrained ePTFE
 microporous polymer scaffold and spread evenly using the side of a plastic rod until the mixture fully
 wetted the restrained ePTFE microporous polymer scaffold (about 30 seconds). Excess imbibing fluid was
 removed by wiping the surface of the ePTFE microporous polymer scaffold with a lint-free cloth. The 8-
 inch hoop was flipped over such that a second surface of the ePTFE microporous polymer scaffold was
 facing upwards. About 0.5 g of the mixture was pipetted onto the second surface of the restrained ePTFE
 microporous polymer scaffold and spread evenly using the side of the plastic rod (about 30 seconds).

 3

 Excess imbibing fluid was carefully removed from both surfaces of the ePTFE microporous polymer
 scaffold by wiping them with a lint-free cloth.

 The imbibed ePTFE microporous polymer scaffold was then dried in an oven set to 65˚C until visibly dry
 (about 10 minutes and then heat treated in a hot air convection oven at 150˚C for an additional 30 minutes
 to remove the dispersing ligands from the surface of the zirconia ceramic. The result was a composite
 membrane comprising a continuous ceramic phase. The composite membrane is also referred to as a
 membrane-reinforced opaloid (MRO), where opaloid refers to the close-packed arrangement of the
 nanoparticles in the final article.

 Some samples received an additional thermal pressing post-treatment step designed to render the surface of
 the composite membrane more aerophobic than the as-imbibed composite membranes comprising low-
 surface energy polymers. This thermal pressing post-treatment is referred to in this report as ST1.

 Other samples received a hydrophilic surface metallization applied to the microporous polymer scaffold
 before the imbibing steps. This pre-treatment is referred to in this report as ST2.

3.2. Pinhole Evaluation
 Pinhole defects were evaluated visually using a light table. Non-conforming samples were discarded before
 testing. Pinholes also manifest as very low air flow resistance.

Step 1: Electrolyte Recirculation
At the start of the test the two diaphragm pumps that feed to the anolyte and catholyte chamber are turned
on and the flow rate is set to 75 ml/min. The cells draw electrolyte from a 5 L tank containing 30 wt%
KOH. The cells are allowed to fill with electrolyte and once the chamber is full the heaters are turned on.

The heaters used are two 250 W cartridge heaters that are inserted into the anolyte and catholyte chamber
thermowells in the AWE cells. Two k-type thermocouples are used to measure the anolyte and catholyte
temperature and a temperature controller will control the temperature setpoint of the cell. The cell
temperature set point is set to 88 °C. The electrolyte is then allowed to circulate through the system for 30
minutes when the temperature has been reached to allow for the diaphragm time to hydrate and the system
to reach a steady state.

Step 2: PEIS
Next EIS is conducted on the AWE cells. The equipment used for this step is an HCP-803 BioLogic
Potentiostat. The software used to conduct this experiment is EC-lab. A Potentiostatic Electrochemical
Impedance Spectroscopy (PEIS) technique is run. The full list of parameters is available in Table 2. For
this experiment the current carrier cables and the voltage sensor were set up in a two-electrode
configuration with the cables attached to the anode and cathode frames of the AWE cell.

The purpose of this experiment is to study ohmic contribution of the LAWE diaphragm in a zero-gap cell
configuration at standard industrial conditions (30 wt% KOH, 88 °C). Two separate EIS tests will be
conducted in this protocol; this step aims to measure the ohmic contribution of solely the diaphragm and
avoids applying a high enough voltage difference that would lead to electrolysis and generate hydrogen and
oxygen gas in the cell. This gas can adhere to the diaphragm and creates void space in the electrolyte
adding additional ohmic impedance to the cell. To avoid this a voltage of 1 V is applied across the cell and
a frequency range of 20kHz to 100 mHz is scanned.

Step 3: Steady State Current Hold
The next step of the experiment is to break in the diaphragms and electrodes to their steady state conditions.
This is done by conducting a 3-day current hold at industry operating conditions of 8 kA/m2. For this step
the cell is disconnected from the potentiostat and is connected to a rectifier (Ametek XG8-187.5 rectifiers).

Step 4: Polarization Curve
After the break in step a polarization curve measurement is conducted. This step uses the same rectifier.
This is a current controlled experiment in which the current is stepped through several setpoint values in the
current density range of 0.05 kA/m2 – 14 kA/m2. From 2-14 kA/m2 a 1 kA/m2 current density step is done
from each set point. From 0.5-2 kA/m2 a 0.5 kA/m2 current density step is done from each set point. Below
0.5 kA/m2, 0.1 kA/m2 and 0.05 kA/m2 setpoints are conducted. At each set point in the protocol the current
is held for 5 minutes, and the voltage is recorded at the end of the 5-minute hold. The ohmic contribution to
the polarization curve (kf) was calculated by fitting a function of the form I = a+b*V+c*ln(V), where I is
the galvanostatic set point, V is the voltage, and b=kf. The initial voltage of electrolysis is represented by a
and is typically near the thermoneutral voltage. The charge-transfer resistance coefficient is estimated by c.

Step 5: Gas Chromatography
The next step is to measure both the hydrogen and oxygen purity of the cathode and anode gas outlet. The
generated electrolyte and gas mixture are plumbed to a gas liquid separator. The separator has a septum
fitting on the gas side of the separator in which gas samples are collected using a 100mL syringe. The
sample is then injected into a gas chromatograph for analysis. The gas is injected into a drying column in
series prior to the GC to remove the water vapor in the sample. The instrument used is an Agilent
Technologies 7890B GC system. The hydrogen and oxygen purities and the anode and cathode gases are
measured at both 2 kA/m2 and 12 kA/m2.

Step 6: GEIS Analysis
At the end of the test the AWE cell is disconnected from the rectifier cables and is reattached to the HCP-
803 BioLogic Potentiostat. For this experiment ohmic contribution of the diaphragm + bubble impedance is
measured. The bubble impedance is dependent on the current density, so three GEIS protocols are run in
succession at 3 current densities (5 kA/m2, 30 kA/m2, and 60 kA/m2) to measure the effect. A 5-minute
current hold is done prior to each EIS measurement to allow for the bubble to reach steady state. For full
conditions see Table 3 for details of EIS measurement. The data work up is the same as described in the
PEIS protocol.

3.4. Electrochemical Durability Testing
 The durability protocol is a method used to study the stability of the LAWE diaphragm. This is a two-week
 protocol that studies the cell voltage drift of the AWE cell containing the experimental LAWE diaphragm.
 In addition, the ohmic contribution and gas crossover are measured at the beginning and end of the 2-week
 protocol to understand the stability of both parameters. The main test results collected are the cell voltage
 drift with an experimental diaphragm. This is calculated by taking the difference in voltage over time at 8
 kA/m2 recorded during the polarization curves at 3 days online and 14 days online. Day 3 cell voltage is
 chosen as the onset value as there is a large voltage break in during the first two days of steady state current
 hold. This result is reported in μV/h. The electrodes and cell package are the same as described in BOL test
 protocol.

3.5. Standard Force to Short Test
 To quantitatively evaluate the resistance to short-circuits formed by penetrations of the electrode meshes
 through the diaphragms, a test was developed using an Instron force meter. A top probe using a 14mm
 diameter hemispherical point is lowered at 0.1mm/sec onto a diaphragm sample placed on a 40TPI, 165um
 stainless steel woven mesh as a representative textured base. The mesh sits on a flat steel surface. (See
 Figure 1) A micro-ohmmeter measuring the electrical resistance between the top probe and the bottom
 steel surface was connected as an auxiliary input to the Instron force meter to allow simultaneous
 monitoring of the resistance and the compressive force on the sample. When a sharp drop in the resistance
 (to <1000 Ohm) occurs, a short circuit condition due to membrane puncture is present, and the force
 associated with the condition is recorded.

3.7. Ionic Resistance Test
 To measure the inherent ionic resistance of the diaphragm samples, an ionic resistance test based on the
 method employed in Landesfeind 2016 – “Tortuosity Determination of Battery Electrodes and Separators
 by Impedance Spectroscopy” was devised. A 40mm punch is used to cut a disc of diaphragm material.
 This sample is pre-wet with isopropyl alcohol, which is then exchanged with an excess of 10milliSievert-
 cm conductivity standard fluid (Fisher Scientific). The wetted diaphragm sample is placed on a 316
 Stainless Steel bottom plate with walls for retaining additional conductivity standard fluid. A top electrode
 comprising a 20mm Stainless Steel Disc, jacketed with PTFE is placed on top of the diaphragm sample,
 followed by a 1kg weight to ensure good contact pressure. (see Figure 3) A PalmSENS portable EIS unit
 was used to sweep a 10mV RMS signal from 15kHz to 150kHz. A line fit was used to estimate the real
 resistance intercept of the Nyquist plot, which corresponds to the real ionic resistance of the diaphragm
 separator.

3.8. Air Permeability Test
 Air permeability was assessed by a Gurley Precision Instruments airflow meter (See Figure 4), which uses
 a fixed weight to displace a known volume of air through the sample and measures the time elapsed. The
 permeability unit “Gurley-seconds” is a measure of flow resistance equivalent to 7840 Rayls
 (Pascals*seconds/meters).

3.9. Bubble Point Test
 The largest pore size present in a sample was assessed using a bubble point test. A Quantachrome
 Porometer (see Figure 5) using a 10cSt silicone oil was employed, and bubble point pressure was
 determined as the first of 15 successive flow measurements greater than 2 standard deviations of all
 previous points.

 Figure 5: Quantachrome Porometer

3.10. Contact Thickness
 Diaphragm samples were analyzed with a Mitutoyo Litematic handheld micrometer with a measuring force
 of 0.01 Newtons to determine the contact thickness.

3.11. Absolute Tensile Strength Test
 Tensile strength (N/mm) was determined in each direction by measuring the maximum tensile load of a
 sample using ASTM D638-5, then dividing the load by the original gauge width of the sample.

 A Kruss DSA100 drop shape analyzer in “captive bubble” orientation was used to evaluate the bubble
 contact angle. DI water was used as the liquid medium, and bubble volume was 2uL.

3.14. Advanced Test Station (Milestones 7 & 8)
 The Pressurized Alkaline Water Electrolysis (PAWE) single cell test station is designed to support
 advanced R&D in high-pressure electrolysis, particularly for evaluating novel electrode and separator
 configurations under elevated operating conditions. The system enables testing at pressures up to 30 bar
 and temperatures up to 150°C using a 30 wt% KOH electrolyte. Its architecture also allows for differential
 pressure operation with the intent to test the robustness of thin separator materials that were being
 developed by Gore. A key innovation of the lab test system is the isolation of the high-pressure zone to the
 cell and immediate flow path, allowing the broader balance of plant (BoP) to remain at atmospheric
 pressure. This design reduces material costs, enhances safety, and simplifies maintenance.

 11

The system incorporates a single high-pressure pump, three back pressure regulators (BPRs), and heat
exchangers to manage pressure and temperature independently on the anolyte and catholyte flow paths. The
electrochemical cell is modular and configurable, supporting various electrode architectures including
expanded mesh, felt, and woven wire mesh designs. Safety is a central design principle, with multiple
hardware and software interlocks, in-line hydrogen and oxygen LEL/UEL sensors, nitrogen purge circuits,
emergency stops, and software-controlled shutdowns integrated into the control system. The system is also
designed for unmanned, long-duration operation, with automated data logging and remote monitoring
capabilities.

All the main process equipment has been installed and plumbed, and this core BoP is complete (See Figure

4. Results & Discussion
 4.1. Electrode Package Down Select (Milestone 1)
 Two studies were conducted to identify an electrode substrate package that supports the use of ultrathin
 diaphragms. Construction details of the of the ultrathin diaphragms (DOE 1, DOE 2, and DOE 3) used in
 these studies can be found in Table 8 and Table 9. The first study (Initial Electrode Down Select) evaluated
 the catalyst coated substrates in test cells with a 40 µm thick PTFE based diaphragm (DOE 3). After down
 selecting the most promising substrate candidates, a second study (Electrode Optimization) was performed
 by evaluating the down selected substrates in test cells with a 20 µm thick PTFE based diaphragm (DOE
 2).

 Testing performed during the Initial Electrode Down Select and the Electrode Optimization studies
 consisted of measuring ohmic contribution from a polarization curve slope and then evaluating the tested
 diaphragms for pinholes. In addition, ex situ force-to-short testing was also performed. An optimal
 substrate package would not generate pinholes in the diaphragm, provide high force to short values, and
 exhibit low ohmic resistance. Where possible, a replicate test cell of each electrode package was evaluated.

 Test methods to measure ohmic contribution, force to short, and pinholes can be found in the following
 sections of the Assumptions, Methods, and Procedures areas of this report:
 • Electrode Down Select Protocol

 • Pinhole Evaluation

 It should be noted that the Modified Force to Short Test was used for evaluating the expanded metal
 substrate, whereas the Standard Force to Short Test was applied to all other substrates.

4.3.3. Durability Testing
 Electrochemical durability testing (See Table 11) was completed for 3 membranes: DOE-2, DOE-7, and
 DOE-15. DOE-7, a polyethylene-based scaffold, was chosen to test the viability of a non-fluoropolymer
 scaffold. DOE-7 shows considerable and unacceptable upward voltage drift and increasing ohmic
 contribution, indicating degradation of the diaphragm. In contrast, DOE-2 and DOE-15, ePTFE scaffolds
 with modified surfaces (ST1 and ST2, respectively) show improvement in overall electrolyzer performance
 and over the course of the test, with a slight improvement in ohmic contribution. Overall kf for these

5. Conclusions
 5.1. Electrode Package Down Select (Milestone 1)
 The calendared #40 woven wire cathode and the expanded metal anode (non-die side facing the diaphragm)
 were chosen as the optimized substrate configuration to enable the use of ultrathin diaphragms. This
 electrode package did not generate pinholes when a 20.5 µm diaphragm was used-- in the test cell.
 Although the force-to-short values for the calendared #40 woven wire were lower than those of nickel foam
 or nickel felt, its performance was deemed acceptable, as no pinholes were observed in a test cell using a
 20 µm diaphragm. For diaphragms thinner than 20 µm, further evaluation of nickel foam or felt may be
 warranted, particularly if a pressurized cell configuration is employed which could reduce ohmic losses.

 5.2. Optical Flow Cell (Milestone 3)
 Flow cell work validated the concept of simultaneous electrochemical measurement and optical
 characterization, with early results using Zirfon UTP220 suggesting physical manipulation of bubbles (e.g.
 by increased liquid flow rate) can be detected electrochemically. The optical capability of this cell can
 resolve to the level of individual bubble tracking, or on bulk parameters (e.g. turbidity). Bubble formation
 at the electrode is difficult to resolve, especially at high current density, due to small initial bubble size and
 high bubble count. Implementation of the planned improvements to the system (e.g. materials of
 construction, use of improved ultra-thin membranes) are necessary to determine bubble dynamics and
 interactions at more industrially relevant conditions. For any future work, model electrode systems aimed at
 improved visualization (e.g. a single nickel wire) may allow for advanced optical techniques such as
 automated individual bubble tracking.

 5.3. Lab Scale Prototyping (Milestone 4)
 Electrochemical testing indicates that ionic conductivity similar or slightly superior to Zirfon UTP 220 can
 be achieved using ultrathin nanoparticle-based PTFE reinforced separators. A significant challenge to
 achieving positive results is the inherent hydrophobic nature of the microporous PTFE scaffold. If
 hydrophilic ceramic nanoparticles do not uniformly imbibe into the PTFE scaffold, then pockets of
 unwetted diaphragm may exist, and raise overall ionic resistance. This may be one explanation as to why
 these membranes, despite being significantly thinner (20µm versus 220µm) offer similar resistance in-situ.
 Another explanation is that the overall porosity of the close-packed spheroidal nanoparticles is significantly
 lower (~30%) than the porosity of Zirfon (~55%). These close-packed spheroidal (opaloid) regions may
 exhibit a significant comparative disadvantage in tortuosity, as the phase inversion process used to produce
 Zirfon tends to produce finger-like pores oriented in the transmembrane direction.

 The hydrophobicity of PTFE can produce significant attraction to gas at the surface, leading to clinging
 bubbles that disrupt liquid electrolyte paths for ionic conductance. The hydrophilic surface treatments (ST1
 and ST2) were necessary to achieve electrochemical performance equivalent to Zirfon UTP-220 when
 employing PTFE scaffolds. Gas crossover was slightly higher for ultra-thin diaphragms compared to Zirfon
 UTP-220, likely due to reduced thickness, but was well within safe parameters.

 22

Electrochemical durability testing indicated that polyethylene scaffold based composite diaphragms
degrade significantly over the course of 2 weeks in an electrolyzer test. In contrast, the PTFE based
composite diaphragms showed perfect electrochemical stability over the same test.

Additional advantages (e.g., high temperature operation, differential pressure operation) of the nanoporous,
high temperature tolerant PTFE-ceramic construction were not investigated before project termination.

Significant progress towards a scalable, optimized ultra-thin diaphragm was achieved before the
termination of the project. A thickness between 20 and 40 µm was selected as providing a good balance of
mechanical properties, ionic conductance, and gas purity. Both ST1 and ST2 appeared to offer durable
surface hydrophilicity. Composite membrane properties could at least in part be modulated through
targeted choice of ePTFE scaffold.
