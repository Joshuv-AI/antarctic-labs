Batched Kernelized Bandits: Refinements and Extensions
Chenkai Ma, Keqin Chen, Jonathan Scarlett

Abstract
In this paper, we consider the problem of black-box optimization with noisy feedback revealed in batches, where the unknown function to optimize has a bounded norm in some Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS). We refer to this as the Batched Kernelized Bandits problem, and refine and extend existing results on regret bounds. For algorithmic upper bounds, ( 27 ) shows that B = O ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ T ) B=O(\log\log T) batches suffice to attain near-optimal regret, where T T is the time horizon and B B is the number of batches. We further refine this by (i) finding the optimal number of batches including constant factors (to within 1 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) 1+o(1) ), and (ii) removing a factor of B B in the regret bound. For algorithm-independent lower bounds, noticing that existing results only apply when the batch sizes are fixed in advance, we present novel lower bounds when the batch sizes are chosen adaptively, and show that adaptive batches have essentially same minimax regret scaling as fixed batches. Furthermore, we consider a robust setting where the goal is to choose points for which the function value remains high even after an adversarial perturbation. We present the robust-BPE algorithm, and show that a suitably-defined cumulative regret notion incurs the same bound as the non-robust setting, and derive a simple regret bound significantly below that of previous work.



1
Introduction

Optimizing a black-box function with costly and noisy evaluations is a common problem, and has wide applications such as hyperparameter tuning
(
36
)
, A/B testing
(
24
)
, recommendation systems
(
26
)
, and reinforcement learning
(
28
)
.
Bayesian optimization (BO) has been a highly successful approach that assumes a prior distribution on the function, sequentially observes noisy data to compute the posterior distribution of the function, and uses the posterior for decision-making
(
19
)
.
BO has been widely considered in two distinct settings: the Bayesian setting that assumes the function is drawn from a Gaussian process (GP), and the non-Bayesian setting that assumes the function lies in a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) and has bounded RKHS norm. In this paper we focus on the latter, which is also known as kernelized bandits. More precisely, we consider a practically motivated variation where the noisy observations are not revealed immediately, but in batches, which we refer to as the
batched kernelized bandits
. The motivation is that collecting samples in batches can be beneficial or even essential since they can be done parallel
(
23
;
3
)
.

In the case that the batch sizes are fixed (i.e., must all be chosen prior to taking any samples),
(
27
)
presents the BPE algorithm, and shows that it achieves near-optimal
O^{*}(\sqrt{T\gamma_{T}})
regret with
B=O(\log\log T)
batches, where
T
is the time horizon,
\gamma_{T}
is the maximum information gain (see Section
2.1
), and
O^{*}
hides dimension-independent logarithmic factors. The upper bounds are also accompanied by near-matching lower bounds for common kernels.

In this paper, we continue this line of work by refining and extending the results on batched kernelized bandits.
Our contributions are as follows:

•

For algorithmic upper bounds, we refine the upper bounds in
(
27
)
by modifying the BPE algorithm therein.
In particular, we identify the optimal number of batches not only in the
O(\cdot)
sense but including precise constants (to within
1+o(1)
), and we remove an unnecessary factor of
B
in the regret bound. Some simple supporting experiments are also given in Appendix
E
.

•

For algorithm-independent lower bounds, we present novel lower bounds that hold even when batch sizes are chosen adaptively.
The bounds have an additional polynomial dependence on
B
, but this is mild since we can assume
B=O(\log\log T)
and rely on standard (fully sequential) lower bounds for larger
B
. Our findings show that despite being more general, adaptive batches are essentially no more powerful than fixed batches in the minimax sense.

•

We extend batched kernelized bandits to a robust setting, where the goal is to choose points for which the function value remains high even after an adversarial perturbation. In this setting, we present the robust-BPE algorithm, and show that it achieves a (suitably defined) cumulative regret matching that of the non-robust setting, and also significantly improves over
(
5
)
in terms of simple regret.

1.1
Related Work

Upper bounds for kernelized bandits.
The celebrated work of
37
presents GP-UCB, an algorithm that chooses the next point to evaluate based on upper confidence bounds
(
2
;
1
;
15
)
. It is shown that GP-UCB achieves

regret with high probability, where
O^{*}
suppresses dimension-independent logarithmic factors, and
\gamma_{T}
is the maximum information gain. A problem with the
O^{*}(\sqrt{T}\gamma_{T})
scaling is that it can fail to be sublinear for the Matérn kernel for many configurations of the smoothness parameter
\nu
and dimension

Attempts to address this issue come mainly in two directions. In the first direction, several ensuing works improve the regret bounds to
O^{*}(\sqrt{T\gamma_{T}})

(
42
;
21
;
32
;
10
;
27
;
41
;
33
)
. For instance,
(
42
)
presents the SupKernelUCB algorithm, which is a kernelized version of an LinUCB
(
12
)
, and combines upper confidence bounds with action elimination;
(
27
)
considers a different approach (which is also the simplest approach to attain near-optimal regret) by iterating between sampling points with maximum uncertainty and eliminating suboptimal points using confidence bounds, which crucially relies on a sharp confidence interval from
(
39
)
.
1
1

1

A similar sharp confidence interval can also be inferred from
(
42
)
.
In the second direction, several works improved the bounds on
\gamma_{T}

(
21
;
40
)
. For example, for the Matérn kernel,
(
37
)
has
\gamma_{T}=O\bigl(T^{\frac{d(d+1)}{2\nu+d(d+1)}}(\log T)\bigr)
, which
(

)
improves to
\gamma_{T}=O\bigl(T^{\frac{d}{2\nu+d}}(\log T)^{\frac{2\nu}{2\nu+d}}\bigr)
.

Combining the
O^{*}(\sqrt{T\gamma_{T}})
regret bound with improved regret bounds on

leads to
O^{*}\bigl(\sqrt{T(\log T)^{d}}\bigr)

As we will discuss, these upper bounds nearly match the corresponding lower bounds, which means

More recently,
(
20
)
improves the regret upper bounds in terms of their dependence on the RKHS norm, which is orthogonal to our work since we consider the RKHS norm to be a fixed constant.

Lower bounds for kernelized bandits.
As an early work,
(
7
)
presents tight lower bounds on the simple regret for the Matérn kernel in the noiseless setting.
(
34
)
extends the results in
(
7
)
to the noisy setting, and to the SE kernel, and considered both simple regret and cumulative regret. Specifically,
(

)
shows that the expected cumulative regret is lower bounded by
\Omega\bigl(\sqrt{T(\log T)^{d/2}}\bigr)
for the SE kernel, and
\Omega\bigl(T^{\frac{\nu+d}{2\nu+d}}\bigr)

(
9
)
complements
(
34
)
by presenting high probability regret lower bounds of the same order as well as extensions to two robust settings. More recently,
(
27
)
presents lower bounds for batched kernelized bandits in the regime
B=O(1)
, while relying on the general (not necessarily batched) lower bounds in
(
34
)
for growing
B
.

Batched settings.

Some earlier works have studied batched kernelized bandits where every batch has some fixed length
(
13
;
16
;
22
)
, which means the number of batches is
\Theta(T)
. In contrast, we are interested in making the number of batches small (which is often highly desirable in practice, e.g., in clinical trials
(
18
)
), and we allow the batch sizes to differ (e.g., increase over time).
Then, the most relevant existing work is
(
27
)
, which characterizes both upper bounds and lower bounds for cumulative regret when the batches lengths are pre-specified (i.e., fixed). As discussed above, the upper bounds are near-optimal when
B=O(\log\log T)
(due to matching
(
34
)
), and refined lower bounds are also derived for
B=O(1)
.

However, a notable gap in
(
27
)
is that no lower bounds are given for
adaptively-chosen
batch sizes, which in principle could lead to algorithms with lower regret. The difficulty of lower bounds for adaptive batches is addressed in the finite-arm multi-armed bandit problem in
(
18
)
, which presents minimax and problem-dependent lower bounds for both fixed batches and adaptive batches. The main idea of
(
18
)
is to “change measure”, i.e., reduce the adaptive batches chosen by an algorithm to “reference batches” that we can control and have nice properties, and relate the probabilities on different reward distributions to a simpler “base” distribution. We similarly “change measure” in our analysis, but in an arguably simpler way, as we consider
\Theta(BM)
hard functions where
M
is the number of disjoint regions of the input domain by some partition, whereas they consider
\Theta(BM^{2})
hard instances in the case of
M
arms.
Moreover, our analysis has substantially different details due to the infinite-arm nature of our setup (as opposed to finitely many independent arms).
Earlier works on batched multi-armed bandits include
(
11
;
30
;
17
)
, and our choice of batch sizes is inspired by
(
11
)
.

Robust settings.
The robust setting we consider extends kernelized bandits by seeking points whose function values remain high even after an adversarial perturbation. This setup is introduced in
(
5
)
, and while other robust settings also exist (e.g.,
(
4
)
), they are beyond our scope.
In this setting and other related settings, it is most common to focus on the simple regret
r_{\xi}(x^{(T)})
, which is incurred on a single point
x^{(T)}
reported after
T
rounds, where
\xi
is a constant denoting the extent of perturbation
(
5
;
35
;
29
)
.
In this paper, however, we consider a suitably defined notion of cumulative regret
R_{\xi,T}
, which ensures consistency with our regret bounds for (non-robust) batched kernelized bandits. Moreover, noting that the best simple regret is no higher than the best normalized cumulative regret
(
6
)
, our result directly leads to a near-optimal bound on simple regret as well.

2
Problem Setup

We first introduce the setup for batched kernelized bandits, which we refer to as the standard setting. Then we introduce the robust setting discussed above.

2.1
Standard Setting

We consider the problem of sequentially optimizing a black-box function
f(x)
over some domain
\mathcal{X}\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
with time horizon
T
and number of batches
B
. To avoid trivial cases we require
T\geq B\geq 2
.

Given
T
and
B
, an algorithm splits
T
into
B
batches by choosing times
t_{1},\ldots,t_{B}
, subject to
0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{B}=T
, and for convenience we define
t_{0}=0
. This means the
i
-th batch is
\{t:t_{i-1}<t\leq t_{i}\}
, and has batch size
N_{i}=t_{i}-t_{i-1}>0
.
2
2

2

The algorithm designer may want to choose batch sizes as a function of
T
without explicitly being given
B
(e.g., see (
5
) and (
6
) below). In such scenarios, we assume for convenience that
B
equals the exact number of batches needed for such a choice. (An alternative approach would be to say that
B
is allowed to be higher and some batch lengths are zero, but this would complicate the notation.)

We denote
[T]=\{1,\ldots,T\}
. At the
start
of each time
t\in[T]
, an algorithm selects a point
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
to query
f
, which incurs noisy response
y_{t}=f(x_{t})+z_{t}
, where the noise
z_{t}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2})
is independent for all
t\in[T]
.
3
3

3

For our upper bounds, we can trivially generalize
z_{t}
to be i.i.d
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian for all
t\in[T]
.

We note that
y_{t}
may not be immediately observable to the algorithm at the
end
of time
t
, i.e., after choosing
x_{t}
. Instead, if
t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}]
, then the algorithm observes
y_{t}
at the end of time
t_{i}
.
Depending on when the
t_{i}
values are chosen, we consider two settings:

•

Fixed batches: all
t_{i}
are predetermined.

•

Adaptive batches:
t_{1}
is predetermined, and for
i\geq 2
,
t_{i}
is chosen at the end of time
t_{i-1}
(i.e., after receiving the observations from the
(i-1)
-th batch).

The algorithm aims to find
x^{*}\in\argmax_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x)
, an (arbitrary) maximizer of
f
, which we measure by cumulative regret
R_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}
, where
r_{t}=f(x^{*})-f(x_{t})
. We also define
R^{i}=\sum_{t=t_{i-1}+1}^{t_{i}}r_{t}
to be the
cumulative regret
in the
i
-th batch. In some cases we also consider
r^{(T)}=f(x^{*})-f(x^{(T)})
, the
simple regret
of an additional point
x^{(T)}
output after
T
rounds.

As for the smoothness of
f
, we assume
\lVert f\rVert_{k}\leq\Psi
, which means
f
lives in an RKHS where the kernel is
k
, and its RKHS norm is upper bounded by
\Psi>0
. We also assume that the kernel is normalized such that
k(x,x)\leq 1,\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
.
We consider two types of kernels, namely the squared exponential (SE) kernel and the Matérn kernel:

\displaystyle k_{\text{SE}}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\exp\biggl(-\frac{(d_{x,x^{\prime}})^{2}}{2l^{2}}\biggr)

\displaystyle k_{\text{Mat\'{e}rn}}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\biggl(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}d_{x,x^{\prime}}}{l}\biggr)^{\nu}J_{\nu}\biggl(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}d_{x,x^{\prime}}}{l}\biggr),

where
d_{x,x^{\prime}}=\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert
,
l>0
is the length-scale,
\nu>0
is a smoothness parameter,
\Gamma
is the Gamma function, and
J_{\nu}
is the modified Bessel function.
We let
\gamma_{t}=\max_{A\subseteq\mathcal{X}:\lvert A\rvert=t}I(f_{A};y_{A})
be the maximum information gain
(
37
)
, where
f_{A}=[f(x_{t})]_{x_{t}\in A}
,
y_{A}=[y_{t}]_{x_{t}\in A}
, and
I(\cdot;\cdot)
is the mutual information (
14
, Sec. 2.3). Two useful known upper bounds for
\gamma_{t}
are

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}^{\text{SE}}=O\bigl((\log t)^{d+1}\bigr),

(1)

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}^{\text{Mat\'{e}rn}}=O\bigl(t^{\frac{d}{2\nu+d}}\left(\log t\right)^{\frac{2\nu}{2\nu+d}}\bigr),

(2)

where the first bound is from
(
37
)
, and the second is from
(
40
)
.

Throughout the paper, we treat the parameters
(d,\nu,l,\Psi,\sigma)
as fixed and known constants, and focus on how the regret depends on
T
and
B
. For the dependence on
B
, we will differentiate between “constant
B
”, where
B
remains constant as
T
grows,
and “growing
B
”, where
B
grows with
T
as a function of
T
, e.g.,
B=O(\log\log T)
.
We use
O^{*}(\cdot)
to hide dimension-independent logarithmic factors in
T
, and use
O(\cdot),\Omega(\cdot),\Theta(\cdot),o(\cdot)
,
\omega(\cdot)
as commonly defined.

2.2
Robust Setting

The robust setting inherits the standard setting, but with the modified goal of selecting points whose function value remains high even after a suitably-constrained adversarial perturbation
(
5
)
.
More formally, we consider
d:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, a known function that measures the “distance” between elements in
\mathcal{X}
, and let
\xi
be a constant.
For each
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we define
\Delta_{\xi}(x)=\{x^{\prime}-x:x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X},d(x,x^{\prime})\leq\xi\}
, with the understanding that
x+\Delta_{\xi}(x)
is the set of possible perturbed queries subject to a “distance” upper bound
\xi
.
The goal is to find a
\xi
-robust optimal point:

\displaystyle x^{*}\in\argmax_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\min_{\delta\in\Delta_{\xi}(x)}f(x+\delta).

(3)

At time
t
, the algorithm chooses
x_{t}
and
\delta_{t}
, queries
f
at
x_{t}+\delta_{t}
, and incurs a noisy response
y_{t}=f(x_{t}+\delta_{t})+z_{t}
that is revealed at time
t_{i}
if
t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i}]
.
Then, we measure a tuple
(x_{t},\delta_{t})
by the
\xi
-regret:

\displaystyle r_{\xi}(x_{t})=\min_{\delta\in\Delta_{\xi}(x^{*})}f(x^{*}+\delta)-\min_{\delta\in\Delta_{\xi}(x_{t})}f(x_{t}+\delta),

(4)

which is defined with respect to
x_{t}
only, and considers the worst-case
\delta
instead of
\delta_{t}
. Similarly, for the sequence
(x_{1},\delta_{1}),\ldots,(x_{T},\delta_{T})
selected by the algorithm in time horizon
T
, the cumulative
\xi
-regret is defined as
R_{\xi,T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{\xi}(x_{t})
. We also define
R^{i}_{\xi}=\sum_{t=t_{i-1}+1}^{t_{i}}r_{\xi}(x_{t})
to be the cumulative
\xi
-regret in the
i
-th batch. Along with the cumulative regret, we will consider the simple regret
r_{\xi}(x^{(T)})
of an additional point
x^{(T)}
reported after
T
rounds, as was considered in
(
5
)
.

The preceding cumulative regret measure may appear somewhat non-standard in that it consists of the algorithm choosing both
x_{t}
and
\delta_{t}
but only measuring regret with respect to
x_{t}
. The role of
\delta_{t}
is to “simulate” perturbation from the adversary, and a similar notion was used in a multi-agent problem in
(
38
)
. Importantly, even if one is only interested in the simple regret
r_{\xi}(x^{(T)})
, the cumulative regret
R_{\xi,T}
will still serve as a helpful stepping stone towards that (see Remark
9
).

In the standard setting, we will first give refined algorithmic upper bounds with fixed (i.e., pre-specified) batch sizes.

3.1
Preliminaries and Existing Results

(
27
)
presents the Batched Pure Exploration (BPE) algorithm.
BPE maintains a set of potential maximizers
\mathcal{X}_{i}
. In each batch, BPE explores points in
\mathcal{X}_{i}
with maximum posterior variance. Then, at the end of the batch, BPE computes confidence bounds to eliminate suboptimal points from
\mathcal{X}_{i}
. The updated
\mathcal{X}_{i}
is used to initialize the next batch.
BPE is non-adaptive in each batch, which allows the use of tight confidence bounds from
(
39
)
, which turn out to be crucial for near-optimal regret.
More details of the BPE algorithm are given in Appendix
A
.
The performance of BPE crucially depends on the batch sizes (Proposition
2
in the appendix), and for growing
B
,
(
27
)
considers

\displaystyle N_{i}=\min\biggl\{\big\lceil\sqrt{TN_{i-1}}\big\rceil,T-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}N_{j}\biggr\},

(5)

with
N_{0}=1
and
i\in[B]
.
For these batch sizes,
(
27
)
shows that BPE terminates with no more than
\lceil\log_{2}\log_{2}T\rceil+1
batches (i.e.,
B\leq\lceil\log_{2}\log_{2}T\rceil+1
),
and achieves the following.

Lemma 1
.

(
27
, Theorem 1)

Under the setup of Section
2.1
and using the batch sizes defined in (
5
),
for any
\delta\in(0,1)
,
the BPE algorithm yields with probability at least
1-\delta
that

\displaystyle R_{T}=O\Bigl((\log\log T)\bigl(\Lambda+\sqrt{\log\log\log T}\bigr)\sqrt{T\gamma_{T}}\Bigr),

where
\Lambda=\Psi+\sqrt{\log(|\mathcal{X}|/\delta)}
.

Remark 1
.

(
27
)
discusses some approaches
(
21
;

)
to extend the result to the continuous domain
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]^{d}
. For example,
(

)

An implication of Lemma

3.2
Refinements

We refine the regret bounds in Lemma
1
by generalizing the batch sizes in (
5
). Specifically, we consider

\displaystyle N_{i}=\min\biggl\{\lceil T^{1-a^{i}}\rceil,T-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}N_{j}\biggr\},

(6)

where
a\in(0,1)
is a parameter to the algorithm and
i\in[B]
. By “generalizing”, we mean that choosing

is equivalent to (
5

Similar to (
5

6

\displaystyle B\leq\lceil\log_{1/a}\log_{2}T\rceil+1,\quad B\geq\Bigl\lfloor\log_{1/a}\Bigl(\frac{\log T}{\log\log T}\Bigr)\Bigr\rfloor,

(7)

and hence

B=\bigl(\log_{1/a}\log T\bigr)(1+o(1))=\frac{\log_{2}\log_{2}T}{\log_{2}(1/a)}(1+o(1)).

(8)

Compared to (
5
), our upper bound on

, and will be complemented by a matching lower bound establishing that the constant in

cannot be reduced further (see Remark
5

Furthermore, the following theorem provides a slightly better regret bound for

, along with an analog for

Theorem 1
.

Under the setup of Section

and

.
In addition, using batch sizes defined in (

Remark 6
.

Compared with Lemma

However, we are justified to restrict

The full proof of Theorem
2
is provided in Appendix

, which follows from the discussion in Section
4.1
(but the details are nearly all distinct) and is also inspired by the batched multi-armed bandit problem
(
18

Recall that for fixed batches,
we know which batch is bad beforehand
, i.e.,
t_{i-1}<T_{i-1}
and

for some

. Therefore, we can construct

However, this argument breaks down for adaptive batches, as

In more detail,

-th batch is bad, and is parameterized by

\mathbb{P}_{f}(E\cap A)+\mathbb{P}_{0}(E^{c}\cap A)\geq\int_{A}\min\{d\mathbb{P}_{f},d\mathbb{P}_{0}\}

where

to change measure from
\mathbb{P}_{f}

\mathbb{P}_{0}

Then, by suitably choosing

for some “bad events”

Appendix A
The BPE Algorithm

In this section we present the BPE algorithm
(
27
)
and some relevant facts.
First, we note that while the theorem is for
f
(the black-box function to optimize) lying in some RKHS, the BPE algorithm models
f
as though it were drawn from a Gaussian process (GP) with prior mean
\mu_{0}=0
and kernel function
k(\cdot,\cdot)
.
For a sequence of sampled points
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})
and their noisy observations
\mathbf{y}_{t}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{t})
, the posterior distribution is also a GP, with posterior mean
\mu_{t}(\cdot)
and posterior variance
\sigma_{t}(\cdot)^{2}
given by:

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{k}_{t}(x)^{T}(\mathbf{K}_{t}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{t})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{t},

(15)

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}(x)^{2}

\displaystyle=k(x,x)-\mathbf{k}_{t}(x)^{T}(\mathbf{K}_{t}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{t})^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{t}(x),

(16)

where
\mathbf{k}_{t}(x)=[k(x_{i},x)]_{i=1}^{t}
,
\mathbf{K}_{t}=[k(x_{t},x_{t^{\prime}})]_{t,t^{\prime}}
, and
\lambda>0
is a free parameter we choose to be
\sigma^{2}
to simplify the expression for
\beta
(which only affect constant factors).
Next, we introduce some notations used by BPE:

•

\sigma^{i}_{j-1}(\cdot)^{2}
is the GP posterior variance computed using only the first
j-1
points sampled in the
i
-th batch, and we define
\sigma^{i}_{0}(\cdot)^{2}
to be the prior variance
\sigma_{0}(\cdot)^{2}
. Moreover,
\sigma^{i}(\cdot)^{2}
is the posterior variance computed using all
N_{i}
points sampled in the
i
-th batch, and similarly
\mu^{i}(\cdot)
is the posterior mean using those points.

•

\mathrm{UCB}_{i}(x)
and
\mathrm{LCB}_{i}(x)
are upper and lower confidence bounds for the
i
-th batch, where

\displaystyle\mathrm{UCB}_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\mu^{i}(x)+\sqrt{\beta}\sigma^{i}(x),

(17)

\displaystyle\mathrm{LCB}_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\mu^{i}(x)-\sqrt{\beta}\sigma^{i}(x),

(18)

with
\beta=\bigl(\Psi+\sqrt{2\log\frac{|\mathcal{X}|B}{\delta}}\bigr)^{2}
, where
\delta\in(0,1)
.

We present the BPE algorithm in Algorithm
2
. Note that we do not need the time horizon
T
as an algorithmic input, because such information is already contained in the batch sizes (
\sum_{i=1}^{B}N_{i}=T
).

\mathcal{X}

Proof.

We will prove the first two properties for the SE kernel, as the proof for the Matérn kernel is entirely analogous.

Proof of the first property.

,
Notice

Proof of the second property.

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\log\Bigl(T^{\frac{1-\eta^{i}}{1-\eta^{B}}}(\log T)^{\frac{d(\eta^{i}-\eta^{B})}{1-\eta^{B}}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\frac{1-\eta^{i}}{1-\eta^{B}}\log T

\displaystyle=\Theta(\log T).

(59)

Proof of the third property.

Lemma 6

Under the preceding definitions,

t

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{0}^{s}\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(P_{0}(\cdot|x_{s}),P_{m}(\cdot|x_{s})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{0}^{s}\biggl[\sum_{j=1}^{M_{i}}\mathbb{I}(x_{s}\in\mathcal{R}_{j})D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(P_{0}(\cdot|x_{s}),P_{m}(\cdot|x_{s})\right)\biggr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{M_{i}}\mathbb{E}_{0}^{t}\biggl[\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbb{I}(x_{s}\in\mathcal{R}_{j})D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(P_{0}(\cdot|x_{s}),P_{m}(\cdot|x_{s})\right)\biggr]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{i}}\mathbb{E}_{0}^{t}\bigl[N^{t}_{j}\bigr](\bar{v}^{j}_{m})^{2},

Appendix C

In this section, we discuss a modification of the robust-BPE algorithm presented in Section

Recall that in the
i

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\xi,i}\leftarrow\cup_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{i}}\{x+\delta:\delta\in\Delta_{\xi}(x)\}.

Now we claim that we only need to explore the (potentially) smaller set:

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\xi,i}\leftarrow\cup_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{i}}\Bigl\{x+\delta:\delta\in\Delta_{\xi}(x),\mathrm{LCB}_{i-1}(x+\delta)\leq\min_{\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta_{\xi}(x)}\mathrm{UCB}_{i-1}(x+\delta^{\prime})\Bigr\},

\delta

To see this, note that if

\displaystyle f(x+\delta)\geq\mathrm{LCB}_{i-1}(x+\delta)>\min_{\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta_{\xi}(x)}\mathrm{UCB}_{i-1}(x+\delta^{\prime})=\mathrm{UCB}_{i-1}(x+\delta_{U})\geq f(x+\delta_{U}).

(166)

We proceed to show that this change does not affect Theorem
3

First, Lemma
8
still holds because the each point with the minimizing
\delta

\displaystyle\mathrm{LCB}_{i-1}(x+\delta_{f})\leq f(x+\delta_{f})\leq f(x+\delta_{U})\leq\mathrm{UCB}_{i-1}(x+\delta_{U})=\min_{\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta_{\xi}(x)}\mathrm{UCB}_{i-1}(x+\delta^{\prime}).

(167)

Second, Lemma
9

because robust-BPE still samples points with maximum uncertainty from
\mathcal{X}_{\xi,i}

Third, validating Lemma

\mathcal{X}_{\xi,i}
. In other words, we need to show

, and

\mathcal{X}_{\xi,i}
for all

.
This is shown as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{LCB}_{i-1}(x+\delta_{U})\leq\mathrm{UCB}_{i-1}(x+\delta_{U})=\min_{\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta_{\xi}(x)}\mathrm{UCB}_{i-1}(x+\delta^{\prime}),

(168)

\displaystyle\mathrm{LCB}_{i-1}(x+\delta_{f})\leq f(x+\delta_{f})\leq f(x+\delta_{U})\leq\mathrm{UCB}_{i-1}(x+\delta_{U})=\min_{\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta_{\xi}(x)}\mathrm{UCB}_{i-1}(x+\delta^{\prime}),

(169)

\displaystyle\mathrm{LCB}_{i-1}(x+\delta_{L})\leq\mathrm{LCB}_{i-1}(x+\delta_{U})\leq\mathrm{UCB}_{i-1}(x+\delta_{U})=\min_{\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta_{\xi}(x)}\mathrm{UCB}_{i-1}(x+\delta^{\prime}).

(170)

As a result, the proof of Theorem
3
is unaffected, and we have the same theoretical guarantee.

D.1.2

For the batch sizes defined above, we note that there is potentially an “overflow” issue that

10
10

10

We avoid this issue by first specifying the end of each batch, i.e.,
t_{i}

i\in[B]

\eta=\frac{\nu}{2\nu+d}
