Sealing the neck: a new balloon-based non-surgical solution to a well-known radial artery complication: a case report
Authors: Alexandre Natalis, Gregory A Sgueglia, Oliver Gach, Claudiu Ungureanu, Giuseppe Colletti, Milenko Zoran Cankovic, Andreas Mitsis, Gavin Paul Raphael Manmathan, Deepti Ranganathan

Introduction

Transradial access (TRA) is recommended by contemporary European Society of Cardiology guidelines as the standard access for coronary angiography in both acute and elective settings, and randomized evidence supports lower bleeding and vascular complications compared with femoral access.1,2

Radial artery pseudoaneurysm is a rare access-site complication of TRA, reported in approximately 0.009%–0.05% of cases, compared with 0.2%–3% following femoral access.3,4 Although surgical repair was historically considered the gold standard for complex pseudoaneurysms,3,4 contemporary non-surgical options include ultrasound-guided compression,5 percutaneous thrombin injection,6 and endovascular covered stent exclusion,7 each with specific limitations and risks depending on neck anatomy and patient frailty.

We therefore report a simple percutaneous strategy to seal a small pseudoaneurysm neck by prolonged balloon inflation across the neck under ultrasound monitoring, performed through ipsilateral distal radial access (DRA).

Case presentation

A 93-year-old woman was admitted with acute-onset chest pain and ST-segment elevation in the inferior leads on electrocardiogram. Emergency coronary angiography was performed via the right radial artery (TRA). The radial puncture was successful at first attempt without any need for ultrasound guidance, and a 7F Slender® sheath was inserted. The angiogram revealed a sub-occlusion of the mid right coronary artery (RCA) (Figure 1A), with a TIMI 1 flow distally, that was rapidly tackled using a 6Fr Judkins Right 3.5 guiding catheter. A primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) was performed, implanting a drug-eluting stent after successful thrombus aspiration (Figure 1B). Initial peri-procedural anticoagulation was administered at a dose of 90 U/kg, while no additional dosing was needed because of the rapidity of the procedure. Post-procedure, haemostasis was achieved with a TR Band® device inflated with 10 cc of air, obtaining a patent compression with its complete removal, in the absence of any evident bleeding, after 2 h as recommended per best clinical practice.8

Coronary angiography showing the culprit lesion: (A) the first available angiogram of the RCA right after guidewire crossing (the culprit lesion had been first visualized with a diagnostic catheter under fluoroscopy but unfortunately the clip had not been stored); (B) the RCA after the primary PCI where a 2.75 × 48 mm Drug-eluting stent has been implanted and then properly post-dilated with a 3 mm NC balloon.

Forty-eight hours later, a pulsatile swelling just proximal to the puncture site was noted. The mass was non-tender, without erythema or bruit (Figure 2A). The right hand was warm and well perfused, with palpable radial and ulnar pulses. Neurological examination of the hand was normal.

Pseudoaneurysm diagnostics: (A) physical aspect of radial artery pseudoaneurysm; (B) ultrasonography confirmed a small neck pseudoaneurysm; (C, D) angiography demonstrating a small neck proximal radial artery pseudoaneurysm;.

The patient had a history of hypertension, dyslipidaemia, and chronic kidney disease stage 3a. She was on aspirin and a statin before admission. At the time of PCI, she received dual antiplatelet therapy and unfractionated heparin.

Bedside duplex ultrasonography revealed a small-neck radial artery pseudoaneurysm arising at the puncture site (Figure 2B; Supplementary material online, Video S1). The neck was narrow (<1 mm), and there was characteristic yin–yang flow within the sac. Radial artery diameter was 2.5 mm at the lesion site. Ulnar artery flow was patent.

An initial attempt at prolonged ultrasound-guided compression was unsuccessful, with persistent flow into the sac after 20 min.

Given the failure of compression and the patient’s advanced age, dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT), and small-neck anatomy, we selected a fully percutaneous endovascular approach via ipsilateral distal radial artery access in the anatomical snuffbox.

The distal radial artery was punctured under ultrasound guidance and a 5F Slender® sheath was inserted. Selective injection of the radial artery clearly delineated the pseudoaneurysm neck (Figure 2C and D; Supplementary material online, Video S2). A 2.5-mm semi-compliant balloon (sized 1:1 to the radial artery) was positioned across the neck and inflated to exclude flow (Figure 3A and B). Although ultrasound monitoring documented thrombus formation within the pseudoaneurysm within 3 min (Figure 3C; Supplementary material online, Videos S3–S5), balloon inflation was prolonged to 10 min to ensure complete thrombosis of the entire pseudoaneurysm cavity and to minimize the risk of thrombus fragmentation and distal embolization. After balloon deflation, repeat angiography confirmed complete exclusion of the sac with preserved antegrade radial artery flow (Figure 3D and E; Supplementary material online, Video S6).

Sealing the pseudoaneurysm’s neck. (A) The pseudoaneurysm can be seen; (B) angioplasty performed with the use of a 1:1 ratio semi-compliant balloon at the site of the neck for 10 min; (C) live clot formation visualization in the pseudoaneurysm with the balloon shaft ‘double contour’ visible inside the radial artery (*); (D and E) final angiography and echography performed confirming the sealing of the pseudoaneurysm and patency of radial artery without complications.

The procedure was well tolerated, and the patient was discharged 48 h later on dual antiplatelet therapy (Aspirin and Clopidogrel).

At 30 days, echo colour Doppler confirmed radial artery patency and demonstrated a small residual subcutaneous cavity related to clot lysis (Figure 4; Supplementary material online, Video S7). Clinically, the swelling was smaller and non-pulsatile.

Echo colour Doppler at 1 month demonstrating patent radial artery and an isolated subcutaneous cavity related to the blood clot lysis at 30 days.

At 3 months, ultrasound revealed complete resolution of the mass with a fibrotic remnant and complete occlusion of the radial artery at the puncture site (Figure 5A). The patient remained asymptomatic, with intact hand perfusion via the ulnar artery (Figure 5B; Supplementary material online, Video S8).

3-Month follow-up: (A) Echo colour Doppler at 3 months revealing a complete reabsorption of the mass with only some fibrotized structure left behind (triangle) and a totally occluded radial artery (star); (B) physical aspect at three months demonstrating a complete recovery.

Discussion

Radial artery pseudoaneurysm is a rare complication of TRA access, with an incidence of 0.009%–0.05% compared with 0.2%–3% for femoral access.3,4 Predisposing factors include large sheath size relative to artery diameter, inadequate haemostasis, and ongoing antithrombotic therapy.

Although the smallest feasible sheath should generally be favoured in elective procedures, in acute ST-elevation myocardial infarction it is standard practice in our centre to routinely use a 7Fr Slender sheath because, despite an outer diameter comparable to a conventional 6Fr radial sheath, they still allow, when required, the use of a 7Fr guiding catheter avoiding the need for a potential cross-over to a larger sheath and hence reducing any potential delay to timely treat the culprit lesion.

Ultrasound-guided arterial puncture is often used in our centre for most elective cases, while in those cases where an emergency coronary angiography is needed, it is reserved for transfemoral access to avoid any delay in reopening the occluded culprit artery.

Pseudoaneurysm’s management is determined by neck size, sac dimensions, symptom burden, and patient comorbidity. While small-neck lesions may resolve with compression, large-neck lesions or those in anticoagulated patients often require more invasive interventions.

Treatment options include ultrasound-guided compression, which is non-invasive but time-consuming, less effective in anticoagulated patients, and potentially painful5;

Thrombin injection is highly effective but carries a risk of distal embolization or anaphylaxis6,9; while the use of a covered stent, which seals the neck, but systematically leads to stent deformation, because of the superficial course of the artery, and radial occlusion.7

In the present case, an initial attempt at ultrasound-guided compression failed, while thrombin injection and covered stenting were considered less attractive given their above-mentioned limitations.

Although aware that surgical repair represent an effective definitive solution, we decided to leave it as a last resort because of its invasiveness opting rather for an attempt at a purely haemostatic ‘internal exclusion’ strategy.

This is based on transient lumen flow interruption across the pseudoaneurysm neck using an indwelling sheath/catheter, which has been described in the forearm circulation, including radial and ulnar pseudoaneurysms.10,11

Finally, our group previously reported an upsized-sheath internal exclusion strategy for large-neck pseudoaneurysms via DRA12

Following the same principle of temporary intraluminal neck exclusion, we decided to attempt a new but rather simple balloon-based approach, adapting it to small-neck lesions while using a relatively short, ultrasound-monitored balloon inflation. Our balloon-based approach offers several advantages: temporary internal occlusion across the neck promotes thrombosis without leaving a permanent implant; ultrasound guidance provides real-time confirmation of thrombus formation; DRA allows treatment without disturbing the proximal puncture site and facilitates early mobilization; and the technique has potential as an adjunct to thrombin injection for high-risk necks to reduce the risk of embolization.

A relevant limitation is the occurrence of late radial artery occlusion (RAO) at 3 months. RAO is a recognized complication after transradial coronary procedures, with reported incidence of 7.7% up to 24 h and 5.8% at up to 30 days.13 Measures known to reduce RAO include adequate intraprocedural anticoagulation and, importantly, patent haemostasis, which has been shown to lower RAO compared with fully occlusive compression.8

In our case, RAO was not an intended outcome: the radial artery was patent at 30-day Doppler follow-up, and the patient remained asymptomatic with preserved hand perfusion via the ulnar artery at 3 months. Although DRA has been associated with lower RAO rates vs. conventional radial access in meta-analyses of randomized trials, it does not fully eliminate the risk.14

Specifically to our case, several factors may have contributed, including repeat instrumentation of the same radial segment (index PCI followed by treatment) or balloon-generated dissections with subsequent occlusive evolution. Despite RAO might definitely be an issue, especially in younger patients, where it might limit the availability of the safest vascular option should any future percutaneous interventions be needed, some recent registries have shown an extremely high success rate in recanalizing the occluded radial artery.15

Another potential limitation might be related to the neck size, the balloon dimension, and length. Specifically, the balloon should be selected according to the radial artery and pseudoaneurysm anatomy.

Despite early independent reports suggesting that a purely haemostatic approach—based on internal exclusion of the pseudoaneurysm—may be feasible, further data are still required to better define its safety and efficacy.

Conclusions

Balloon angioplasty across the neck of a small-neck radial artery pseudoaneurysm, performed via DRA is an effective and minimally invasive technique that achieves rapid thrombosis and promotes definitive closure. In selected cases, it may represent a valuable addition to the interventional toolbox for the management of radial artery complications but potential risk of late RAO must be considered.
