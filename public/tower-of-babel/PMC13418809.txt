Periodontitis-associated gut microbiota disrupts glucose homeostasis through SCFAs depletion and inflammation in germ-free mice
Authors: Zhonghan Xu, Yujie Yao, Wei Wei, Xinyue Wang, Meng Du, Hui Jia, Jun Bao, Faming Chen, Xin Tong, Lili Li, Fuhua Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeriodontitis is increasingly linked to systemic conditions such as diabetes, yet the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. This study aimed to determine whether gut microbiota mediates the impact of periodontitis on glucose homeostasis.MethodsGerm-free (GF) mice were obtained and maintained in sterile isolators (GemPharmatech, China) and colonized with microbiota derived from donor mice with ligature-induced periodontitis (GF-LIG) or healthy controls (GF-CON). Donor faecal samples were collected from donor mice with ligature-induced periodontitis or healthy controls. Fasting blood glucose (FBG), serum insulin, homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) and β-cell function (HOMA-β), as well as glucose tolerance, were evaluated. Correlation analyses were performed to explore associations between microbial composition, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), and inflammatory markers. SCFA-producing bacteria were supplemented to assess their potential protective effects.ResultsCompared with GF-CON mice, GF-LIG mice exhibited significantly higher FBG, serum insulin, HOMA-IR, HOMA-β, and impaired glucose tolerance. The SCFA-producing genus Lachnospirace-ae_NK4A136_group was negatively associated with FBG, HOMA-β, and serum levels of interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-17 A, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α. Moreover, serum levels of IL-1β, IL-17 A, and TNF-α were positively correlated with HbA1c and HOMA-β. Notably, supplementation with SCFA-producing bacteria significantly reduced FBG and HbA1c levels in periodontitis-affected mice.ConclusionThese findings suggest that gut microbiota mediates the impact of periodontitis on glucose homeostasis, potentially through SCFAs depletion and inflammation. Restoration of SCFA-producing bacteria may represent a promising microbiota-targeted strategy for mitigating metabolic disturbances associated with periodontitis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08936-w.

Background

Periodontitis is a chronic disease affecting approximately 57.3% of the global population [1]. It’s not only the leading cause of dental loss in adults, but also impacts systemic diseases [2]. Epidemiological studies have demonstrated a strong association between periodontitis and impaired glucose homeostasis, with individuals affected by periodontitis showing a 4.77-fold [2.69–8.46] increased risk of developing diabetes [3]. Moreover, interventional studies have reported that periodontal treatment can improve glucose homeostasis in patients with periodontitis [4], indicating the involvement of periodontitis in glucose control.

Despite this well-established association, the underlying mechanisms linking periodontitis to glucose dysregulation remain poorly understood [5]. Emerging evidence suggests that the gut microbiota may play an important role in mediating this relationship [6, 7]. The oral–gut axis has been proposed as a key pathway through which oral pathogens and inflammatory signals influence distal microbial ecosystems and host metabolism. For example, oral bacteria can be swallowed and translocate to the gastrointestinal tract, leading to gut microbiota dysbiosis and altered host immune responses. Consistent with this concept, several studies have shown that periodontitis is associated with changes in gut microbiota composition, which are accompanied by impaired glucose regulatio [8–10]. A recent study by Kubota-Takamori et al. [11] further demonstrated that periodontitis induces gut microbiota dysbiosis alongside increased glucose variability and insulin resistance, reinforcing the potential link between periodontal inflammation, microbiota alterations, and metabolic dysfunction.

However, important gaps remain. Current evidence is largely associative, and it is still unclear whether gut microbiota plays a causal role in periodontitis-induced glucose dysregulation, and through which mechanisms this effect may occur. In particular, the roles of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria, gut barrier function, and inflammation in this process have not been fully elucidated. Therefore, establishing a causal link and clarifying the underlying mechanisms are essential.

Germ-free (GF) mice are considered a gold standard model for investigating the causal role of gut microbiota in host physiology [12, 13]. Therefore, in this study, we utilized a GF mouse model to determine whether periodontitis-associated gut microbiota directly influences glucose homeostasis and to explore the underlying mechanisms.

In addition, given the established role of SCFAs in maintaining gut barrier integrity and modulating inflammatory responses [14, 15], we further investigated whether supplementation with the SCFA-producing bacterium Clostridium butyricum MIYAIRI 588 (CBM588) could mitigate microbiota-associated metabolic disturbances.

CBM588 has been widely reported to exert beneficial effects on gut health, including enhancing SCFAs production, strengthening epithelial barrier function, and reducing inflammation [16, 17], making it a suitable candidate for evaluating microbiota-targeted therapeutic strategies.

By integrating germ-free microbiota transplantation with targeted microbial intervention, this study aims to establish a causal link between periodontitis-associated gut microbiota and glucose dysregulation, and to define the underlying mechanisms and therapeutic potential.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

All animal procedures adhered to ethical standards set by the National Institutes of Health (NIH, publication no. 85 − 23, revised 1996) and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing University (IACUC-D2303078, China), in line with ARRIVE reporting criteria. The sample size was determined based on our previous study using a similar experimental design [18], in which significant differences in fasting blood glucose were consistently detected with 6–8 animals per group following 4–6 weeks of periodontitis induction. Accordingly, a group size of n = 7 animals was used in the present study.

Animal models and grouping

Male C57BL/6J mice, aged seven weeks, were sourced from GemPharmatech (China) and kept under SPF conditions (3–4 animals per cage). A high-fructose diet (60%) was provided throughout the experiment. After one week of acclimatisation, fourteen mice were randomly split into two groups (n = 7 each): control (CON) and ligature-induced periodontitis (LIG). At week 8, faecal samples were collected, pooled by group, and preserved in saline containing 30% glycerol and 0.1% cysteine.

Induction of periodontal inflammation

Ligature placement around the maxillary second molars was used to induce local inflammation. Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg, Xiya) combined with atropine sulfate monohydrate (0.1 mg/kg, KingYork). Then, ligation was performed on the maxillary second molars using 5 − 0 silk thread (Jinhuan). Ligatures were examined every other day and re-tied immediately if dislodged.

Microbiota transfer to germ-free hosts

Ten male germ-free C57BL/6J mice (7 weeks old) were housed in sterile isolators (GemPharmatech, China) with access to autoclaved food and water. They were randomly assigned to receive microbiota from either healthy (GF-CON, n = 5) or periodontitis-model (GF-LIG, n = 5) donors. Faecal suspensions were administered by oral gavage three times per week for eight weeks, following a previously validated approach [19–21].

Preparation of donor microbiota

Faecal pellets collected from former experiment were processed for microbiota transplantation following a previously described method [19]. Briefly, frozen stool samples were rapidly thawed at 37 °C, then processed under anaerobic conditions. After mechanical dispersion, the suspension was filtered (70-mesh), centrifuged (4000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C), washed with sterile saline, and resuspended in preservation buffer. Aliquots were stored at − 80 °C for transplantation.

SCFA-producing bacteria intervention

To investigate the influence of SCFA-producing bacteria, twenty-eight male C57BL/6J mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 7 each): CON, LIG, Clostridium butyricum MIYAIRI 588 (CBM588) alone (CP group), and a combined LIG + CBM588 treatment group (LP). Periodontitis was induced over nine weeks. The CBM588 (Miyarisan, Japan) was delivered by gavage three times weekly during the final five weeks. All mice were euthanised at the endpoint.

Culture and identification of CBM588

CBM588 was cultured as described previously [22]. Briefly, the CBM588 powder (Miyarisan, Japan) was reconstituted in 10 mL of sterile Reinforced Clostridial Medium (RCM; Hopebiol, Qingdao) and incubated overnight at 37 °C under facultative anaerobic conditions. The resulting culture was streaked onto RCM solid medium and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C to obtain single colonies. Colonies were then selected and subcultured. Bacterial density was measured at OD600. Cultures in logarithmic phase (OD600 = 0.6–0.8) were centrifuged (1500 g, 15 min, 4 °C), resuspended in buffer, and adjusted to 10⁹ CFU/mL. For identification, genomic DNA was extracted, PCR products were sequenced (ABI3730-XL), and species confirmed via NCBI database alignment.

Tissue collection and colon length measurement

All mice were humanely sacrificed by administration of an overdose of pentobarbital sodium(200 mg/kg, Xiya) in accordance with the AVMA Guidelines on Euthanasia (2020) [23], ensuring complete loss of consciousness prior to death. The maxillae were harvested bilaterally, with one side stored at − 80 °C and the contralateral side fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological analysis. The abdominal cavity was opened to expose the gastrointestinal tract. The caecum and proximal colon were identified, and the colon was excised from the caecum to the rectum. Colon length was measured immediately under minimal tension. All measurements were performed using a standardised protocol and in a blinded manner to ensure consistency across groups.

Then, a 2 mm segment of proximal colon was collected and placed in TRIzol reagent for RNA extraction and qPCR analysis, while an additional 5 mm segment was flushed with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Caecal contents were collected into sterile microcentrifuge tubes and stored at − 80 °C for microbiota analysis.

Micro-CT Imaging

Maxillae, fixed for 72 h, were scanned using the Skyscan 1172 system (Bruker, Belgium) with a resolution of 18 μm. The cemento-enamel junction to alveolar bone crest (CEJ–ABC) distance was measured using CTAn and DataViewer software to quantify bone loss. All image analyses were performed in a blinded manner to minimise observer bias.

Tissue processing and staining

Following decalcification (Servicebio, Wuhan), maxillae were embedded and stained with H&E. Colon tissues were processed similarly. In immunofluorescence experiments, ZO-1 and Occludin were fluorescently labeled using specific antibodies from Abcam (UK), and imaged with a Nikon (Japan) laser scanning confocal microscope.

Gene expression analysis

RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), and purity was verified via Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific). Reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara), and qPCR reactions were set up using SYBR® Select Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). Gene expression was normalised to GAPDH and calculated using the ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Blood glucose measurement

Fasting blood glucose and HbA1c were measured using a handheld meter (BioHermes, China). For glucose tolerance testing, fasted mice received oral glucose (25%, 10 µL/g), and blood glucose was monitored at 0, 30, 60, and 120 min. For insulin sensitivity assessment, mice fasted for 4 h received intraperitoneal insulin (0.75 IU/kg), with glucose levels recorded at the same intervals.

Serum analyte detection via ELISA

Blood was collected via retro-orbital bleeding under anaesthesia. After centrifugation (3000 rpm, 15 min), serum was analysed using ELISA kits (Jianglai Biotech, Shanghai) to quantify insulin, endotoxin, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-17, MCP-1, and IL-1β, in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols.

Sequencing and analysis of 16 S rRNA

Cecal microbial DNA was isolated by the QIAamp DNA Stool Kit (TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing, China). The quantity and quality of extracted DNAs were determined using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA). The bacterial 16 S rRNA V3-V4 region was amplified using the primers 338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’), which were tagged with unique 7-bp barcodes for multiplex sequencing. The PCR products were refined using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and their concentrations were measured with the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). After quantifying the purified amplicons, they were pooled in equal amounts and processed for paired-end 2 × 250 bp sequencing using the Illumina NovaSeq platform, using the NovaSeq 6000 SP Reagent Kit (500 cycles). Sequencing and data analyses were carried out at Personal Biotechnology Co. (Shanghai, China).

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis of microbiota data

Raw sequencing data were processed using QIIME2 (version 2022.11). After demultiplexing, primer sequences were trimmed using the cutadapt plugin, followed by quality filtering, denoising, merging, and chimera removal using the DADA2 plugin to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Non-singleton ASVs were aligned using MAFFT and used to construct a phylogenetic tree with FastTree2. Taxonomic classification was performed using a naïve Bayes classifier trained on the SILVA database (release 132).

Alpha diversity metrics, including Chao1 richness, Shannon diversity, Simpson index, and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, were calculated within QIIME2. Beta diversity was assessed using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity and UniFrac distances, and visualised by principal coordinates analysis (PCoA). Differences in microbial community structure among groups were evaluated using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA).

Differential taxa were identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe), with an LDA score threshold of 2.0 and default parameters. LEfSe integrates non-parametric statistical testing (Kruskal–Wallis test followed by pairwise Wilcoxon tests) with linear discriminant analysis to identify features with both statistical significance and biological relevance. For comparisons of microbial relative abundance, statistical significance was assessed using appropriate non-parametric tests (e.g., Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Kruskal–Wallis test), and P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Given the exploratory nature of the microbiome analysis and the use of LEfSe for differential feature selection, multiple comparison correction was not applied.

GC–MS analysis of SCFAs

Approximately 20 mg of faecal material was weighed. A 1 mL aliquot of 0.5% (v/v) phosphoric acid and a sterile steel bead were added. The sample was vortexed for 10 min, followed by 5 min of sonication and centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. A 100 µL portion of the supernatant was mixed with 500 µL of methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) containing the internal standard. This mixture was vortexed for 3 min, sonicated again for 5 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for a further 10 min. The resulting upper phase was used for GC–MS/MS analysis [24–26]. Quantification was performed in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode [26, 27].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Correlation analyses were conducted in SPSS 23.0 (IBM, USA), with correlation coefficients used to evaluate variable associations. Group comparisons were made using unpaired t-tests or one-way ANOVA, depending on context. Data normality was checked via the Shapiro–Wilk test, and variance homogeneity via Levene’s test. If normality assumptions were violated, non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis) were applied. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (n = 5–7 animals/group). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Experimental periodontitis leads to blood glucose elevation

Animal experimental flowchart (Fig. 1A). Micro-CT and H&E staining confirmed the successful induction of periodontitis. The LIG group showed significantly greater alveolar bone resorption at multiple sites around the second molars compared to controls (Fig. 1B, C). Histological analysis revealed tissue detachment, gingival recession, and collagen degradation (Fig. 1D). We observed impaired glucose homeostasis in mice with experimental periodontitis, as evidenced by elevated fasting blood glucose and HbA1c levels in the LIG group compared with CON group (Fig. 1E, H). Glucose tolerance was impaired, with significantly increased AUC values observed in the LIG group at both 5 and 9 weeks compared with CON group (Fig. 1F–G, I-J).

Experimental periodontitis alters gut microbiota composition

We observed alterations in the gut microbiota in mice with experimental periodontitis. Alpha diversity analysis showed higher observed species, Shannon, Simpson, Chao1, Faith’s phylogenetic diversity (Faith_pd), and Pielou’s evenness (Pielou_e) indices in the LIG group compared with the CON group (Fig. 2A). Beta diversity analysis further demonstrated a separation in microbial composition between the two groups (Fig. 2B, C).

Differential abundance analysis identified Lactobacillus, Roseburia, Escherichia-Shigella, and Muribaculaceae as enriched in the LIG group, whereas Ileibacterium, Bifidobacterium, Acetatifactor, Patescibacteria, and Candidatus Saccharimonas were enriched in the CON group (Fig. 2D). Functional prediction analysis showed differences in microbiota-associated metabolic pathways between groups (Fig. 2E), including increased representation of poly (3-hydroxybutyrate) depolymerase and trimethylamine: corrinoid methyltransferase pathways in the LIG group (Fig. 2F).

Transplantation of periodontitis-associated gut microbiota disturbs glucose homeostasis of germ-free mice

Animal experimental flowchart (Fig. 3A). We observed alterations in glucose homeostasis in germ-free mice following transplantation of periodontitis-associated microbiota. FBG in GF-LIG mice was significantly higher than in GF-CON at weeks 2, 4, and 9 (Fig. 3B). HbA1c levels were also higher in GF-LIG group at weeks 7 and 9 (Fig. 3C). Glucose tolerance was comparable between groups at week 3 (Fig. 3D, E), but differences were observed at week 8, with significantly higher glucose levels and increased AUC in the GF-LIG group (Fig. 3F, G). Insulin tolerance testing at week 10 showed significantly increased AUC values in the GF-LIG group (Fig. 3H, I). Serum insulin levels, as well as HOMA-IR and HOMA-β indices, were significantly higher in the GF-LIG group compared with the GF-CON group (Fig. 3J–L).

Periodontitis-associated gut microbiota impairs gut barrier and increases circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines in germ-free mice

We observed alterations in gut barrier integrity and inflammatory profiles in germ-free mice following transplantation of periodontitis-associated microbiota. H&E-stained colon sections showed no obvious tissue damage or inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 4A). Colon length was significantly shorter in the GF-LIG group than in the GF-CON group (Fig. 4B). Gut barrier integrity was assessed by immunofluorescence staining for ZO-1 and Occludin (Fig. 4C, E). ZO-1 gene expression was comparable between groups (Fig. 4D), whereas Occludin expression was significantly reduced in the GF-LIG group (Fig. 4F).

We observed elevated inflammatory marker levels in both colon and serum following transplantation of periodontitis-associated microbiota. In the colon, IL-6, IL-10, IL-17 A, IL-23, CSF-1, and CXCL-1 levels were significantly higher in the GF-LIG group compared with the GF-CON group (Fig. 4G). In serum, endotoxin, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-17 levels were also significantly increased in the GF-LIG group (Fig. 4H).

Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group and SCFAs are associated with altered glucose homeostasis

We examined associations between gut microbiota, circulating cytokines, and glucose homeostasis. The abundance of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group was negatively correlated with FBG and HOMA-β (Fig. 5A), whereas Desulfovibrio and Bacteroides were positively associated with serum insulin, HOMA-IR, and glucose levels during the insulin tolerance test (Fig. 5A).

SCFA levels in cecal contents were measured in germ-free mice. Acetic acid levels were significantly lower in the GF-LIG group compared with the GF-CON group, while propionic acid, isobutyric acid, valeric acid, butyric acid, and total SCFAs showed decreasing trends (Fig. 5B–I). SCFA-producing bacteria ameliorate glucose dysregulation induced by periodontitis-associated microbiota

To determine whether depletion of SCFA-producing bacteria mediates the impact of periodontitis on glucose homeostasis, we supplemented mice with CBM588, a well-characterized SCFA-producing bacterium, based on a previous study [22]. Animal experimental flowchart (Fig. 6A). Supplementation with CBM588 significantly lowered blood glucose in periodontitis mice. Both FBG and HbA1c levels were significantly reduced in the LP group compared to the LIG group (Fig. 6B–C). H&E-stained colon sections showed no obvious structural differences across groups (Fig. 6D), whereas colon length was significantly greater in the LP group compared with the LIG group (Fig. 6E). Immunofluorescence staining for ZO-1 and Occludin (Fig. 6F, G), together with gene expression analysis (Fig. 6H, I), showed significantly higher levels in the LP group. Pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-17 A, and TNF-α were significantly reduced in the colon of LP mice (Fig. 6J), and circulating endotoxin, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17, and TNF-α levels were also lower (Fig. 6K).

Discussion

This study investigated whether gut microbiota play an independent role in mediating the impact of periodontitis on glucose homeostasis. Our results provide strong support for this link, demonstrating that microbiota sourced from periodontitis-affected mice impair glucose homeostasis when transplanted into germ-free recipients. Notably, these mice exhibited elevated blood glucose, reduced glucose tolerance, hyperinsulinaemia, and diminished insulin sensitivity—hallmarks of a diabetogenic effect driven by microbial dysbiosis.

Although our previous research has demonstrated that manipulating gut microbiota can influence the impact of periodontitis on blood glucose homeostasis [18], it remains unclear whether gut microbiota serve as an independent pathway through which periodontitis disrupts glucose homeostasis. Germ-free mice are considered the gold standard for studying the function of gut microbiota in disease models [12, 13]. By employing germ-free mice in this study, we were able to isolate the specific contribution of the gut microbiota. Transplantation of periodontitis-associated gut microbiota into germ-free mice clearly identified gut microbiota as an independent mediator of periodontitis-induced glucose dysregulation.

Notably, the composition of the gut microbiota in germ-free mice post-transplantation did not entirely replicate that of the donor microbiota. This discrepancy may be attributed to the unique immune landscape of germ-free mice, which lack normal microbial interactions during immune system development [28]. These findings are consistent with earlier reports indicating that transplanted microbiota often undergo modifications when introduced into a new host, particularly one with an immunologically naïve environment such as germ-free mice [29]. To address this potential limitation, we increased the frequency of microbiota transplantation, as suggested by previous studies, to enhance the similarity between the transplanted microbiota and the donor composition [13].

Regarding how gut microbiota influences blood glucose homeostasis, previous studies have reported multiple pathways, including the production of metabolic byproducts such as SCFAs, gut hormones, and their impact on the intestinal mucosal barrier function [30]. Notably, our findings revealed that the relative abundance of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, a known SCFA-producing bacterial group (primarily acetate, butyrate, and propionate), was significantly reduced in the GF-LIG group. Furthermore, elevated FBG and HOMA-β were negatively associated with changes in Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group. Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group belongs to the phylum Firmicutes that is closely associated with metabolic health [31]. Studies have shown that Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group is significantly depleted in diabetic patients compared with healthy individuals [32, 33]. Restoring the abundance of Lachnospiraceae through dietary modification or probiotic supplementation has been reported to improve insulin sensitivity and reduce blood glucose levels [34].

The deficiency of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group may disrupt glucose homeostasis through multiple mechanisms. First, gut microbiota dysbiosis can impair the gut barrier, increasing permeability [35, 36] and allowing endotoxins to enter the circulation, triggering chronic low-grade inflammation and promoting insulin resistance [37, 38]. Our findings support this, as germ-free mice colonized with periodontitis-associated microbiota showed elevated endotoxin levels and systemic pro-inflammatory cytokines. Second, Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group are important producers of SCFAs, which serve as energy sources for intestinal epithelial cells and help regulate insulin sensitivity [39, 40]. In the GF-LIG group, acetic acid levels were significantly reduced, with downward trends in propionic acid and total SCFAs, all inversely correlated with serum insulin and HOMA-IR. Finally, these bacteria have anti-inflammatory properties by promoting regulatory T cell function [41, 42]. To test the hypothesis that diminished SCFAs underlies the glycaemic disturbances linked to periodontitis, we CBM588, a recognised SCFA-producing bacterium.

Interestingly, supplementation with C. butyricum MIYAIRI 588 significantly lowered blood glucose levels in periodontitis-affected mice, suggesting that the SCFAs deficiency induced by periodontitis-associated microbiota may contribute to impaired glucose homeostasis.

Although Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group emerged as the principal microbial signal associated with glucose dysregulation in our study, its direct use in intervention experiments remains limited due to its classification as an uncultured taxon lacking a defined strain and comprehensive phenotypic characterization. We therefore employed CBM588 as a functionally relevant and experimentally tractable surrogate to investigate whether supplementation with a butyrate-producing bacterium could mitigate the metabolic and intestinal alterations induced by periodontitis-associated microbiota.

Consistent with this rationale, CBM588 supplementation significantly increased intestinal butyrate levels, supporting the involvement of SCFA-related mechanisms in mediating the observed protective effects. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution. CBM588 has been reported to exert pleiotropic effects beyond SCFAs production, including modulation of host immune responses and enhancement of epithelial barrier function [43]. Therefore, the beneficial effects observed in the present study are likely mediated through multiple interconnected pathways rather than a single mechanism.

Taken together, our results provide supportive evidence for the role of butyrate-producing bacteria in mitigating microbiota-induced metabolic dysfunction, but do not establish a definitive causal mechanism. Future studies incorporating comprehensive metabolomic profiling and targeted mechanistic approaches will be necessary to further dissect the relative contributions of microbial metabolites and host immune pathways.

Despite the strength of our findings, several limitations should be noted. In this study, a high-fructose diet was used, which may act as a potential confounding factor, as it can independently alter gut microbiota and induce insulin resistance. However, this dietary condition was intended to establish a metabolically susceptible background that better reflects clinical settings, where periodontitis often coexists with dietary risk factors and metabolic disorders [44, 45]. Such conditions are also commonly used to enhance model sensitivity [46, 47], allowing microbiota-driven metabolic effects to be more readily detected. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that this approach may introduce confounding effects, and future studies under standard dietary conditions are needed to further delineate the independent contribution of periodontitis-associated microbiota. A second limitation is about germ-free mice. Although germ-free mice offer a powerful model for studying microbiota–host interactions, their physiology differs from that of conventional mice and humans, limiting generalizability. While we identified Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group as a key player, other unexamined taxa may also contribute to glucose metabolism. Moreover, SCFA-producing bacterial supplementation only partially reflects the complexity of gut microbial dynamics.

Conclusion

In summary, gut microbiota contribute to periodontitis-associated glucose dysregulation, as supported by microbiota transfer in germ-free mice. This is associated with reduced SCFA-producing bacteria and increased inflammation, and can be alleviated by supplementation with SCFA-producing bacteria, supporting a mediating role of gut microbiota and SCFA-producing bacteria.
