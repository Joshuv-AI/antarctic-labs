Prospective validation of an AI algorithm to identify adult-onset type 1 diabetes misclassification: protocol for a non-interventional multicentre study
Authors: Irene Brusini, Suyin Lee, Alvi Lai, Amanda Sees, Matthew Hackenberg, Greg Liptak, Ajay D Rao, Catherine Anastasopoulou, Nadejda Leavitt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAdult-onset type 1 diabetes (T1D) is often misclassified as type 2 diabetes (T2D), resulting in delayed treatment, missed opportunities for referrals to specialists and increased risk of complications including diabetic ketoacidosis. An electronic medical record (EMR)-based algorithm—originally trained on a large national EMR dataset to identify likely misclassified adult-onset T1D cases—was tested and retrained on a health information exchange (HIE) dataset from HealthShare Exchange (HSX). Promising results were achieved on historical data, particularly when using the retrained algorithm. However, its prospective validation is essential to more reliably assess its clinical utility and real-world precision in flagging high-risk patients for clinician review.Methods and analysisThis is a prospective, multicentre, non-interventional cohort study in two HSX-member healthcare organisations (HCOs) in southeastern Pennsylvania. At the onset of the study, all adult T2D patients are scored by the algorithm analysing HIE data on relevant predictors found in the 24-month lookback period. Patients meeting a prespecified score threshold estimated in retrospective testing to yield 10% recall will be presented to designated endocrinology or primary care providers for structured chart review, attribution confirmation and guideline-concordant follow-up (including autoantibody testing where appropriate). The primary endpoint is positive predictive value for confirmed adult-onset T1D among flagged patients. Secondary endpoints characterise operational cascade metrics (attribution, provider recommendation, test ordering/results and diagnosis updates) along with 95% CIs. Exploratory endpoints will assess provider adoption, interpretability and workflow integration via structured provider interviews.Ethics and disseminationThis study was reviewed and approved by Advarra Institutional Review Board (protocol Pro00075945). The Institutional Review Board waived patient informed consent and granted a full waiver of HIPAA authorisation for patient records, while providers were required to provide written informed consent. HSX data were accessed and shared under its member-defined use cases. Findings will be disseminated via peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations. Reporting will follow Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidance for cohort studies.

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is often viewed as a childhood-onset disease, but a substantial proportion of cases actually present in adulthood with more than half of all new T1D cases occurring in adults.1 In the USA, about 37% of adults with T1D report being diagnosed after age 30,2 which is consistent with international findings that roughly 40% of T1D cases emerging after young adulthood are initially classified as type 2 diabetes (T2D).1 The risk of misclassification increases with older age at onset, in part because adult-onset autoimmune diabetes can have a more indolent presentation (sometimes termed latent autoimmune diabetes in adults, or LADA) and may not immediately require insulin1 as these patients are often assumed to have T2D based on age alone.3 T1D also represents a relatively small fraction (~5%) of total diabetes cases in adults,3 which can obscure the true prevalence of adult-onset T1D.

Misclassifying adult-onset T1D as T2D can have serious implications. Patients may be managed with oral hypoglycaemic agents or other T2D therapies that are insufficient for autoimmune beta-cell failure, delaying necessary insulin treatments and unintended risks such as worsening hyperglycaemia and, in some cases, diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). T1D misclassification is associated with an estimated 18% increased risk of DKA.4 Patients who experience DKA at diagnosis have higher initial HbA1c levels, loss of residual β-cell function and protracted recovery.4 Recurrent DKA further increases the risk of cerebral oedema, cognitive impairment and organ damage.4 Each hospitalisation for DKA in the USA costs approximately US$29 981.5 An estimated US$1 in US$4 of direct medical spending on T1D is related to DKA management, and in those with repeated DKA, this may rise to US$1 in every US$2.5 Prolonged mismanagement of T1D can also lead to preventable microvascular and macrovascular complications,3 with retinopathy, nephropathy and cardiovascular events, potentially bringing additional long-term costs.

Primary care providers (PCPs) are the front-line for diagnosis, treatment and long-term management of T2D. Primary care is the dominant entry point for initial T2D diagnosis, creating risk for delayed identification of T1D and referrals to Endocrinology for insulin initiation and complex disease management. Misclassification of T1D as T2D can also be attributed to limitations in diagnostic heuristics. There is often over-reliance on a patient’s age and phenotype (assuming any older or overweight adult must have T2D). Confirmatory tests such as C-peptide levels are not routinely performed for adults with new-onset diabetes.3 A patient may suffer multiple DKA episodes over several years before antibody and C-peptide tests are finally checked.3 Until recently, there was no robust evidence-based guideline on identifying adult-onset T1D. Historically, clinical definitions of diabetes subtypes were developed around paediatric cases or obvious phenotypes.6 Many clinical features traditionally thought to distinguish T1D (leanness, acute presentation or ketosis) have overlap with T2D or may not be present in all T1D adults.6 Even when adult-onset T1D is suspected (eg, when a patient fails oral therapy), confirmation is sometimes delayed or not pursued due to cost or insurance coverage, or the misconception that the results would not change management. Recent consensus recommendations by the American Diabetes Association and European Association for the Study of Diabetes have started to advocate for routine islet autoantibody testing in adults with new-onset diabetes when T1D is even remotely suspected.7 Implementation of these recommendations, however, is still nascent.

With the availability of longitudinal national EMR data and health information exchange (HIE) data, and in collaboration with Breakthrough T1D (formerly known as the Juvenile Diabetes Research Foundation), IQVIA has developed an artificial intelligence (AI) algorithm to identify adult-onset T1D patients misclassified as T2D. The algorithm, originally trained on a large national EMR dataset,8 was retrained and retrospectively validated on HIE data captured through HealthShare Exchange (HSX), a regional HIE covering healthcare organisations (HCOs) in southeastern Pennsylvania.9 This allowed the algorithm to learn from HSX-specific dynamics and care practices. While retrospective validation has shown promising results in identifying misclassified cases, we have not assessed the algorithm’s clinical utility, real-world precision or usability by frontline providers.

During this study, we aim to prospectively evaluate the Adult T1D Detection algorithm for detecting misclassified cases among patients initially diagnosed with T2D. We will assess real-world impact of the algorithm on health outcomes when implemented at the point of care and evaluate its utility in supporting clinicians to consider prioritising patients for T1D assessment and potential reclassification. Providers will review algorithm-flagged cases using their own clinical judgement and, if appropriate, initiate confirmatory testing. We will also gather qualitative insights on implementation feasibility, provider trust in algorithmic support and data completeness.

Objectives

The primary objective of this study is to prospectively evaluate the precision—defined as the positive predictive value (PPV)—of the algorithm when integrated into real-world clinical practice. Specifically, the study aims to measure the proportion of patients flagged by the algorithm who are ultimately confirmed to have a true diagnosis of T1D, based on structured chart review and follow-up testing where clinically appropriate.

Additionally, the study will assess several operational and clinical process metrics, including the proportion of patients attributed to managing providers, presence of existing T1D diagnoses not captured in HIE data, provider recommendations for follow-up, and availability and results of autoantibody testing during the follow-up period. We will also gather qualitative feedback from designated providers on the interpretability, usability and workflow integration of the algorithm.

By addressing these objectives, it will be possible not only to demonstrate whether the algorithm can reliably identify misclassified individuals at the point of care but also if clinicians can effectively act on these insights within routine workflows, and if anything can be done to further improve the algorithm’s clinical workflow integration in the future. Over time, this AI-driven approach has the potential to shift diabetes management upstream, enabling earlier and more accurate diagnosis, more appropriate treatment initiation and reduced risk of preventable complications.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This is a prospective, multicentre, non-interventional study designed to evaluate the performance of the Adult T1D Detection algorithm9 when implemented as an additional input to standard clinical practice in a real-world setting in the USA. The study does not require any deviation from routine standard of care and is considered to pose no more than minimal risk to patients. Study oversight was performed by Advarra Institutional Review Board. The study began in January 2025 and initial protocol approval was provided on 7 May 2024, lasting through 7 May 2025. Two 1-year continuing reviews were then approved by Advarra Institutional Review Board on 28 April 2025 and 15 April 2026, lasting through 15 April 2027, to allow for a study extension to complete data analysis.

Dataset

The study will be conducted across two US HCOs located in southeastern Pennsylvania, both of which are members of HSX. The study will leverage HIE data routinely collected at the point of care in HSX-member HCOs and transmitted in real time to HSX via HL7 messages. Relevant data elements include diagnoses, treatments, laboratory results and procedures that are all used as predictors by the algorithm. A data dictionary and full list of algorithm inputs are provided in online supplementary table 1 and online supplementary list 1, respectively. Although data sharing agreements exist between HSX and its member HCOs, the completeness of data across different types of records received by HSX can vary across participating HCOs. For example, one HCO may share complete laboratory results feeds, while another may provide none. These inherent limitations of HIE data underscore why model retraining was particularly important on this dataset, as well as the need for prospective validation to assess the impact of such data gaps on clinical utilisation of the algorithm.

Study time period

The active study period at each health system will span 6 months from the initial deployment of the algorithm to the final collection of provider feedback. HIE data from the 24 months preceding the algorithm deployment will be extracted to serve as the lookback period for generating algorithm inputs. The algorithm will be deployed once at the start of the study; this deployment date will serve as the index date. Outcomes will be reviewed and recorded by designated providers within each HCO at two time points: 3 months and 6 months post-deployment. See figure 1 for an overview of the study timeline.

High-risk patient identification and provider attribution

The algorithm will analyse the latest available data aggregated by HSX and assign a risk score to patients for being misclassified adult-onset T1D. Patients with a risk score above the 10% recall threshold—as estimated during retrospective validation9—will be included in the study for further evaluation. This threshold corresponds to the score cut-off at which the algorithm was estimated to identify approximately 10% of adults who truly had T1D misclassified as type 2. As described by Brusini et al,9 this threshold was found to provide a good balance between PPV (estimated to be 18.3% on retrospective HSX data) and screening capacity at the two HCOs participating in the present prospective study, and therefore was deemed appropriate to use for this prospective validation.

For each patient, the algorithm will also produce a set of key predictors (eg, most recent body mass index, most recent A1c result, evidence of insulin use, etc.) to support interpretability and transparency.

Sample selection

Study population and sample size justification

A formal sample size calculation is not required for this prospective, non-interventional study. The objective is to estimate the PPV of the algorithm when deployed in routine clinical practice, not to test a prespecified hypothesis. As such, the focus is on feasibility and estimation of precision rather than statistical power.

Based on retrospective data from HSX, it is anticipated that the algorithm will flag approximately 150–230 patients per HCO using a 10% recall threshold. This threshold reflects a pragmatic balance between clinical manageability and algorithm sensitivity. The resulting total sample—approximately 300–460 patients across two HCOs—is aligned with the operational capacity of designated providers at each site to conduct structured chart reviews and follow-ups over the 6-month study period. All patients will already be under the care of providers within the participating HCOs, and therefore, no explicit patient consent or enrolment will be required.

In addition to the primary analysis, two designated providers per HCO (four in total) will be enrolled to participate in structured interviews. These interviews will capture qualitative feedback to inform exploratory endpoints related to algorithm interpretability, usability and integration into clinical workflow. The number of participating providers is expected to yield sufficient insight into clinician experience and contextual factors influencing adoption.

Interventions to be measured

The intervention under evaluation is the algorithm-generated list of flagged patients, shared with designated providers in each HCO. The algorithm itself is not a prescriptive tool and does not dictate diagnostic or therapeutic actions. It serves as a decision-support input, identifying patients who may benefit from additional review and confirmatory testing based on standard clinical guidelines. No study-specific treatments will be administered, and no investigational medication will be involved.

Data analysis plan

Quantitative data analysis

Descriptive statistics will be used to summarise the study population and all outcome measures. Analyses will be conducted separately for each participating HCO and then aggregated across sites to characterise the full cohort.

Continuous variables (eg, age) will be summarised using means and SD, or medians and ranges where appropriate. Categorical variables (eg, provider attribution, test ordering, confirmed diagnosis) will be reported as counts and percentages. Subgroup analyses may be presented where relevant (eg, by provider type or presence of endocrinology relationship), although no formal stratification is planned.

For each primary outcome, the proportion of patients meeting the relevant endpoint will be calculated, and 95% CIs will be estimated using binomial or Poisson distribution methods, depending on the data structure. No formal hypothesis testing is planned, as the study is exploratory in nature and intended to estimate the real-world PPV of the algorithm in practice.

These interviews will support exploratory objectives, including assessment of the algorithm’s clinical interpretability, usability and integration into routine care.

Only designated healthcare providers participating in chart review and interviews are required to give informed consent to participate. They will receive a written information sheet outlining their role in the study and consent will be documented prior to participation.

Patient confidentiality will be strictly protected throughout the study. HSX will assign a unique study identifier to each patient and remove all identifiable information—except date elements required for algorithm performance—prior to data transfer. All data transmitted to IQVIA will be handled as if fully identifiable and managed within a secure Azure-based environment with two-factor authentication, encrypted file transfers and access restricted by internet protocol whitelisting.

Safety monitoring is not applicable to this study, as it is non-interventional and does not involve administration of medications or medical procedures. No adverse event reporting is planned.

Findings from the study will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations. All outputs will adhere to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for cohort studies. A final study report will be generated at study completion, summarising cohort characteristics, endpoint findings and qualitative insights. No patient-level data will be published.
