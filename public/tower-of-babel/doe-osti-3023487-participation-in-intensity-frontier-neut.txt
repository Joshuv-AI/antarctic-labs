1 Introduction
 The flagship currently-running experiment at Fermilab is the NuMI Off-axis νe Appearance
(NOvA) experiment. Together with the (complementary) T2K experiment in Japan, it provides
the opportunity for improved measurement precision on neutrino mixing parameters and addresses
topics of strong interest: whether neutrino masses follow a normal (NH) or inverted (IH) hierarchy;
whether the ν3 state contains a symmetric mixture of νµ and ντ (“maximal mixing,” pointing to a
possible new symmetry of nature), or, if not, what is the octant of θ23 ; and whether neutrino mixing
violates CP symmetry. Recent results from both experiments suggest we may be on the verge of
important discoveries. Towards the end of the decade, these experiments will be superseded by
DUNE in the U.S. and HyperK in Japan.
 Over the course of the project, our group supported multiple Ph.D. students on NOvA and
DUNE beam monitoring and DUNE Near Detector physics. IIT Physics Profs. Pavel Snopok and
Yagmur Torun collaborated on NOvA and DUNE. Snopok co-convened the NOvA Beam Simulation
and Monitoring group. Torun participated in Test Beam analysis. IIT Research Prof. Zelimir
Djurcic, a DUNE and NOvA collaborator, a staff scientist at ANL and leader of the ANL neutrino
team, played key roles in DUNE design, prototyping, and physics efforts.
1.1 NOvA and DUNE Detectors and Accelerator Neutrino Physics Program
 Among current beam experiments, NOvA [1] has the longest baseline, giving it the best mass-
hierarchy sensitivity. It uses Fermilab’s NuMI beam and two totally active scintillator detectors
separated by 810 km: a 14-kton far detector sited in Ash River, MN, and a 0.3-kton near detector
at Fermilab to monitor the unoscillated ν(ν) flux. The detectors are placed 14.6 mrad off the NuMI
beam axis to reduce backgrounds by narrowing the neutrino energy spectrum (to 20% RMS width),
and maximize flux at the Eν = 2 GeV first maximum of the neutrino-oscillation probability at the
chosen baseline. NOvA’s design goal is to observe νe (ν e ) appearance and νµ (ν µ ) disappearance
 • νe Appearance: Determine the octant of θ23 , the mass hierarchy, and (using the reactor
 measurement of θ13 ) constrain δCP .
 • νµ Disappearance: Improve the precision of atmospheric oscillation parameters ∆m232 , θ23 .
 • Neutral Current: Search for a possible event deficit indicating mixing with sterile neutrinos
 and constrain 3 + 1 mixing model parameters.
 DUNE is designed to definitively measure the physics parameters currently probed by NOvA.
DUNE will surpass NOvA’s sensitivity via longer baseline (1300 vs. 800 km), higher beam power
(1.2 MW or more vs. the current 0.9), larger detector mass (40 kt vs. NOvA’s 14), and use of high-
resolution liquid-argon TPCs rather than NOvA’s segmented liquid-scintillator detectors. It will use
the wide-band, on-axis, LBNF neutrino beam. NOvA’s narrow-band, off-axis beam suppresses the
background from neutrinos at energies above the oscillation maximum, but in DUNE, with its longer
baseline, the wide-band beam will have an advantage, allowing systematic-uncertainty reduction by
means of measurements at multiple oscillation maxima. Introducing LBNF technology into NuMI
will result in a gradual upwards evolution of the NuMI beam power, reducing DUNE/LBNF project
risk and allowing NOvA to exploit improvements ultimately intended for LBNF and DUNE.
NOvA. The NOvA detectors employ charged-particle detection in liquid scintillator [2]. A key
challenge is identifying electron-neutrino appearance events, signaled by a single energetic electron
or positron in the final state, as unambiguously as possible. The main background is neutral-
pion production in neutral-current interactions; thus, the detector has been designed for effective
discrimination between electron- and π 0 -initiated electromagnetic showers. This is achieved via
the high granularity and low average atomic number (Z) of NOvA’s extruded, liquid-scintillator-
filled, 3.9 × 6.6 cm2 PVC tubes [3]. Each far-detector extrusion is 15.6 m long, and planes with
cells oriented vertically and horizontally alternate, providing 3-dimensional imaging of each event.

DUNE. DUNE is designed for precise measurements of oscillation amplitudes using a high-intensity
neutrino beam from Fermilab and large LArTPC detectors placed 1300 km from the beam source [7].
The DUNE concept is expected to lead to unprecedented sensitivity to CP violation effects in the
lepton sector and to help shed light on the matter–antimatter asymmetry in the Universe.
 The DUNE FD, to be built at the Sanford Underground Research Facility (SURF) in Lead,
South Dakota, will detect neutrino and antineutrino interactions with four modules, each contain-
ing 17 kton of (LAr). The first module to be constructed is a single-phase time projection chamber
(TPC). This technology was recently prototyped in the ProtoDUNE-SP LArTPC, installed and op-
erated at the CERN Neutrino Platform in a specially constructed beam delivering charged pions,
kaons, protons, muons, and electrons with 0.3 to 7 GeV/c momenta. The measured values for in-
coming particles’ momentum and type meet or exceed the DUNE FD specifications, in several cases
by large margins [8]. With these results, ProtoDUNE-SP’s successful operation and data analysis
demonstrated the effectiveness of the single-phase horizontal drift (HD) DUNE FD design [8]. A
prototype for a second DUNE FD single-phase LArTPC design, the Vertical Drift (VD) module [9],
was recently constructed and will begin operating at CERN in late 2024.
 The Near Detector (ND) is critical to realizing DUNE science, with the goal of measuring the
neutrino beam near the source before significant oscillations in order to form a prediction of the
beam distributions at the Far Detector. The ND will be housed in an underground hall on the
Fermilab site. Two of the main ND subsystems in Phase I of DUNE are a liquid argon TPC
(ND-LAr) and the Temporary Muon Spectrometer (TMS). DUNE Near Detector development and
prototyping is actively pursued with the ND-LAr prototype ArgonCube2x2 Demonstrator in the
NuMI beam, and with TMS design and prototyping.
 The initial science program is centered on the physics milestone of δCP resolution < 20◦ (at
δCP = −90◦ ) [7]. To meet this target, DUNE will need to control systematic uncertainties at both
the Near and Far Detectors, and understanding neutrino interactions in argon will be critical.

2 Accomplishments and Contributions
2.1 NuMI and LBNF Beam Monitoring and Simulation and NOvA Flux
 (Snopok, Yu, Chirco)
 To pursue the most pressing questions in neutrino oscillations, NuMI/NOvA and LBNF/DUNE
programs will operate at ever-increasing beam power, now officially over 1 MW for NuMI and
1.2 MW and beyond for LBNF. After the new 1-MW class NuMI target with wider fins was installed
in 2019, the NOvA Beam Simulation group carried out a detailed study of the changes in the
neutrino beam parameters resulting from the use of the new target, and it was demonstrated that
the neutrino flux was affected by < 3%.
 As the MINOS near detector previously used to track the beam quality for NOvA was decom-
missioned in 2019, the muon and hadron monitors [10] in the NuMI beamline have become essential
sources of information about both the primary and the neutrino beam. Both types of monitors
are arrays of ionization chambers (7×7 pixels for the hadron monitor and 9×9 pixels for the three
identical muon monitors, each pixel a 3 by 3 inch square sensor). Each ionization chamber con-
sists of two parallel-plate electrodes separated by a 3-mm gap. The chambers are filled with He
gas. NOvA uses Fermilab’s NuMI neutrino beam [11], created by 120 GeV protons from the Main
Injector striking a 1.2-m-long graphite target, shown schematically in Fig. 2. Two magnetic horns
focus pions and kaons produced in the target. The focused mesons decay in a 675-m-long decay
pipe to produce mainly muons and muon neutrinos. The horns can be operated with “forward horn
current” (FHC), focusing π + and K + , which decay to muon neutrinos, or “reverse horn current”
(RHC), producing muon antineutrinos.
 The Long Baseline Neutrino Facility (LBNF) will use a new beamline at Fermilab to deliver
a neutrino beam to DUNE. Similarly to NuMI, a 60–120 GeV proton beam will hit a solid target
to produce mesons, which will be focused along a 194-meter-long decay pipe to create muons and
neutrinos. LBNF will operate at 1.2 MW initially but is designed to support up to 2.4 MW with
an upgrade [12]. The LBNF beam will pass through a Near Detector (ND) at Fermilab on its way
to the Far Detectors at SURF.
2.1.1 NOvA Muon Monitor Simulation and Data Analysis
 Three muon monitors (abbreviated as MM1–MM3 or MM in the text below) are located down-
stream of the NuMI hadron absorber and separated by 12 and 18 m of rock, hence sensitive to
muons of different momenta. MM provide essential information to monitor the beam and target,
maintain the highest quality beam, and identify issues with the beamline alignment early, which is
crucial to achieving NOvA experiment goals. The MINOS Near Detector was previously used to
monitor the quality of the neutrino beam. The off-axis NOvA Near Detector cannot be used for
the same purpose because its spectrum has limited sensitivity to beam configuration changes [13].

primary beam spot size, and target degradation over time on the downstream beam quality. The
results of these studies were presented at the 2021 and 2022 NuFact conferences [15] and Yu’s Ph.D.

2.1.3 Improving Neutrino Flux Predictions for NOvA and DUNE using NA61 and
 EMPHATIC data
 Snopok is a co-convener of the NOvA Beam Simulation working group. This role implies
managing the neutrino flux simulation with the new NuMI target with associated systematic un-
certainties, minimizing said uncertainties wherever possible, maintaining the Monte Carlo simu-
lation tool-chain, and improving the Package to Predict the FluX (PPFX) code [16]. PPFX is
an experiment-independent neutrino flux determination package for the NuMI beam that provides
a correction for hadron production mismodeling using almost all relevant external data. Using
Geant4 and the FTFP BERT hadronic model, it propagates the uncertainties of the NuMI beam-
line. It returns a set of correction values to be used as weights for the neutrino yield. It is necessary
to understand the neutrino flux of the NOvA experiment because neutrino-nucleus cross-section
measurements rely strongly on this parameter. This is also a challenge since the uncertainties
associated with the production and attenuation of the hadrons significantly affect the neutrino
spectrum. Therefore, a robust prediction of the flux is crucial for future neutrino experiments, such
as DUNE. The results of the Geant4 simulations will be validated against available experimental
data (NA49, NA61, EMPHATIC). One outcome of this study will be a new central value for the
flux for DUNE, which is based largely on simulation at the moment. This central value could then
be used for subsequent DUNE analyses.
 DUNE will lead to a comprehensive understanding of neutrino oscillations and search for be-
yond the Standard Model physics on many fronts. The experiment will face many challenges that
must be addressed over the next decade. One challenge is the poor a priori knowledge of the

Neutrino flux uncertainties represent a risk to DUNE’s primary goal of measuring neutrino CP vio-

uncertainties improves the physics reach of most DUNE near detector analyses. New hadron pro-

predictions and ND measurements. DUNE flux uncertainties can be improved, and the following
are reasonable expectations assuming all the existing experimental datasets are incorporated into
the code: 10% uncertainty for kaon absorption (currently 60–90% for momenta below 4 GeV/c,
12% for momenta above 4 GeV/c); 10% on quasielastic interactions (down from 40%); 10% on
p, π, K + [C, Fe, Al] → p + X (down from 40%); and 20% on p, π, K + [C, Fe, Al] → K ± + X (down
from 40%). The results of such an improvement (by up to a factor of ≈ 2) are shown in Fig. 6. A
working group was formed that included members from different neutrino experiments who were
interested in a better flux model using PPFX. It includes people from DUNE, NOvA, MINERvA,
and even from SBN for their analysis of NuMI neutrinos. The results of this effort have had an
immediate impact on flux predictions.
 IIT organized a two-day PPFX workshop in January 2023 at IIT Rice Campus, near Fermilab.
This location simplified the logistics for the participants who did not have access to Fermilab. A
significant part of the workshop was dedicated to a PPFX tutorial for new students.
 Recent experiments like NA61/SHINE [17–20], HARP [21,22], and MIPP [23–25] have reported
new data with improved cross-section and hadron production measurements. Over the past year,
Snopok has been co-advising a Panjab University GS Bhumika Mehta, who was tasked with in-
corporating the 120-GeV NA61 data into PPFX and producing a corresponding reweighter for the
Geant4 MC simulation. We expect this work to be completed within the next few months. This will
be the first new PPFX reweighter since MIPP and NA49 reweighters, and it will provide important
information on how to streamline the process for the EMPHATIC experiment.
 EMPHATIC (Experiment to Measure the Production of Hadrons At a Test-beam In
Chicagoland) [26] aims to take measurements of coherent elastic and quasi-elastic (forward scat-
tering) interactions of hadrons with beam momenta 2–120 GeV/c on several target species. EM-
PHATIC uses a table-top-sized spectrometer located at the Fermilab Test Beam Facility (FTBF).
The spectrometer for Phase 1 included ≤100 mrad acceptance and 250 million triggers were col-
lected during three runs from 2022 to 2023. Thin-target data was collected for graphite, beryllium,

CH2, and water targets. EMPHATIC will begin taking data again in 2025 for Phase 2, where it
will move to a different location at FTBF. Phase 2 will involve a new magnet, new SSDs, and make
the first-ever measurement of the hadron spectrum downstream of a NuMI target and unpowered
horn. Additionally, a motion table has been commissioned to sweep through the acceptance of the
horn. Preparations for Phase 2 are being carried out, which include additional thin-target data
and measurements using the NuMI target and horn with a small-acceptance spectrometer. Phase
2 is projected to begin in early 2025 and the data provide the potential for further improvements
of the flux prediction and the reduction of flux uncertainties.
 This program of measurements has the potential to reduce DUNE flux uncertainties by ap-
proximately a factor of two and set the stage for lower uncertainties in the future. These reduced
flux uncertainties lower the risk to DUNE’s three-flavor oscillation measurements and expand the
possibilities for observing new physics beyond the Standard Model at DUNE. Furthermore, the
measurements will have the auxiliary benefit of similarly lowering uncertainties on neutrino flux
predictions used by experiments such as NOvA, MINERvA, MINOS, and SBN experiments using
data from the NuMI beamline.

2.2 NOvA Operations and Test-Beam Program (Torun)
 The NOvA Test Beam (TB) used a tagged beam of electrons, muons, pions, and protons to
enable a detailed understanding of the Near/Far detectors’ response to different particles. The final
data set includes about 34,000 tagged beam particles. TB data will directly improve the uncer-
tainties for muon energy scale, hadron energy scale, and detector response, which together account
for more than half the total systematic error for sin2 θ23 and over 90% for |∆m232 |. NOvA’s goal is
to reduce the systematic errors to a level comparable to the statistical precision. These are dom-
inated by a 5% difference between the calibrated detector response for muons and protons, which
should be resolved by TB data. TB data will also have an important impact on NOvA’s oscillation
measurements by verifying and improving the collaboration’s AI/ML-based particle identification
algorithms. NOvA has a mature, complex, and highly optimized workflow for reconstructing neu-
trino interaction events. A convolutional neural network (CNN) [4] is used for flavor tagging
events (as CC-νµ , CC-νe , NC), similar to the approach used in DUNE [27]. A well-reconstructed
muon track is required for CC-νµ events with cosmic ray rejection by a boosted decision tree. For
CC events, the hadronic energy Ehad is measured through calorimetry with ∼30% resolution. A
context-enriched CNN [28] is used to identify electromagnetic and hadronic clusters as e, µ, γ, π, p.
TB data will provide an event library to study and improve the machine learning algorithms used

 7

in analysis. TB data offers the promise of similar validation for other aspects of NOvA’s AI/ML
analysis chain, such as energy estimation techniques using CNNs [29] or LSTM (long short-term
memory) recurrent neural networks (RNNs) [30]. In addition to muons that stop in the TB detector
with momenta reconstructed externally upstream, TB data includes cosmics recorded during and
in between beam spills as well as a large sample of horizontal “halo” muons that go all the way
through the detector during each spill. Together with protons reconstructed in the beamline, these
events can help with the proton/muon energy scale discrepancy in NOvA analysis.

2.3 DUNE Near Detector Muon Spectrometer (Torun, Turnberg)
 The DUNE Near Detector (ND) [56] will be used to monitor the beam, measure the neutrino
flux, predict the neutrino spectrum at the Far Detector (FD) and provide high-statistics neutrino
interaction samples. A fixed assembly (SAND) with a tracker and an EM-calorimeter will be used
to monitor the beam and provide neutrino interaction samples in multiple materials. The rest of
the ND (PRISM) is movable transverse to the beam. The core element in PRISM, a liquid argon
TPC (ND-LAr), provides the target and the tracking medium for neutrino interactions with FD-like
systematics. In order to extend acceptance for muons beyond a few hundred MeV/c which are not
contained in ND-LAr, and control wrong-sign backgrounds, a magnetic muon spectrometer, called
TMS (The Muon Spectrometer) [57], is included downstream of ND-LAr as part of the Phase I
DUNE design. A high-pressure gaseous argon TPC is under consideration as a future upgrade.

TMS Design: TMS is a magnetized steel-scintillator range stack with transverse dimensions of the
readout planes matching ND-LAr (7×3 m2 ) and enough depth to stop muons of up to 5 GeV energy.
TMS was designed for minimum risk and cost. An earlier version of the design is shown in Fig. 8.
The structure includes 50 thin (1.5 cm) layers of steel upstream to determine the curvature sign of
the muon track at low energy and thicker layers downstream (34 4-cm-thick plates followed by 16
8-cm-thick ones), used for muon energy measurement through the range. The distribution of steel
plate thickness across the layers is a compromise between reconstruction performance (which could
be improved by additional tapering with thinner layers up front for low-momentum muons) and
mechanical support and practical (cost, procurement) considerations. The plates are magnetized
with two sets of three coils with the polarities arranged to minimize the fringe field around the
structure and the field in the active region is about 1 T. Most scintillator planes are made up of six
panels tiled along the horizontal axis, each containing 32 3.15-m-long extruded plastic scintillator
strips that are 3.6 cm wide by 1.7 cm thick with a centrally located hole for 1.4-mm-diameter
wavelength shifting fiber. The strips are configured to measure the coordinate in the bend (x)
direction and panels in alternating layers are tilted by ±3◦ to provide stereo views for vertical (y)
position information. Some of the scintillator planes have the panels arranged so that the strips
are horizontal, to provide hits with better vertical position resolution along the muon track.
Readout: The design includes a SiPM (MPPC) photodetector mated to one end of each fiber.
These will be mounted on printed circuit boards in groups of 8. Commercial off-the-shelf com-
ponents will be utilized for data flow. The front-end electronics utilizes 64-channel CAEN A5202
boards which include 13-bit ADCs and integrated programmable bias power supplies for the MP-
PCs. CAEN DT5215 data concentrator units will be used to provide the data path for 8 groups of
16 daisy-chained front-end boards.
Simulation: A detailed model of TMS and ND-LAr is available for GEANT4 simulations. Figure 9
shows simulated neutrino interactions in ND-LAr with tracks reaching TMS. Studies of acceptance,
sign determination, and energy resolution for muons have been carried out. Due to high statistics
at the ND, the efficiency does not need to be very high. Acceptance corrections due to size and
fiducial volume, for example, can be determined with small uncertainty. However, it’s important to
have full coverage in terms of the phase space, including muon kinematics, with no holes compared
to the FD. Simulations should have enough detail to reveal any gaps in acceptance or systematic
bias which would have to be addressed by iterating TMS design as needed.

TMS as beam monitor: While the primary function of TMS is to complement ND-LAr to match
FD performance, it can also help monitor the beam. TMS contains about 750 tons of steel and
35 tons of scintillator. At 1.2 MW beam power, about 120 interactions per spill (0.2 per 19 ns RF
bucket) are expected on the steel. Changes in various beam parameters (position, horn current,
etc.) would be reflected in event rates and muon spectra. While preliminary results indicate that
the energy resolution in the current design is likely adequate for beam monitoring, additional studies
are needed.

 Detector response and detector physics effects, neutrino event final-state interactions, and recon-
struction and selection effects modify the apparent number of reconstructed charged tracks in each
neutrino interaction. As a result, the measured multiplicity distribution represents a detector-level
observable rather than the true particle-level spectrum. To enable direct comparison with genera-
tor predictions and to extract physically interpretable quantities, these systematic effects must be
corrected using an unfolding procedure. As shown in Figure 10, we demonstrate the unfolding pro-
cedure with the event selection described above, by accounting for detector response and detector
physics effects only. Other systematic uncertainties (GENIE neutrino generator neutrino interac-
tion modeling systematic uncertainties, and the final-state interactions systematic uncertainties)
will be included in the future, with works underway.
2.4.2 DUNE AI/ML Accomplishments and Contributions
 In pixelated LArTPCs such as the 2x2, electromagnetic showers frequently appear as spatially
separated fragments with varied morphology and orientation, challenging traditional assembly al-
gorithms. The SPINE framework—an end-to-end, sparse CNN and GNN pipeline—has emerged
as a powerful solution for semantic segmentation, clustering, graph-based aggregation, and particle
identification in LArTPC data. Our group is performing optimization studies targeting the GrapPA
Shower module with the HPC deployment on Argonne’s Polaris (allocation granted to Z. Djurcic
et al.), and the impact on downstream physics analyses.
