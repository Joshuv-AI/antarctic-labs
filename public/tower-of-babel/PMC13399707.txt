Hip Arthroscopists Consistently Use Capsular Closure With Preference for Interportal Capsulotomy
Authors: Cory J. Call, Matthew L. Kuik, Alison M. Mueller, Ryan M. Degen, Andrea M. Spiker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo examine recent trends of capsular management in hip arthroscopy over a 5‐year period (2018‐2023), both within the United States and internationally utilizing a repeated, international, cross‐sectional survey.MethodsA repeated cross‐sectional survey was distributed via Qualtrics to a cohort of surgeons identified through a PubMed search of hip arthroscopy publications. The survey was administered in 2018, 2020, and 2023, with additional distribution through the American Orthopaedic Society for Sports Medicine listserv in later years. The 29‐question survey assessed surgical experience, capsular management techniques, postoperative protocols, and complications. Responses were analyzed for trends over time.ResultsA total of 74, 78, and 83 surgeons completed the survey in 2018, 2020, and 2023, respectively. Interportal capsulotomy was the most commonly used technique across all time points. Capsular closure was performed in the majority of cases, with 50% of surgeons reporting always performing closure in 2018 and 2020, increasing to 67% in 2023. T‐capsulotomy was the second most utilized technique, with ≥75% of surgeons who used it reporting always performing capsular closure. Of 52 reported postoperative dislocations, 27 (52%) occurred in cases where capsular closure was performed.ConclusionsInterportal capsulotomy remains the preferred technique for joint access during hip arthroscopy, with consistent use across the 5‐year study period. Capsular closure was routinely performed regardless of capsulotomy type, and although survey responses suggested a trend toward more consistent closure practices, no statistically significant change in closure frequency was observed. Surgeons performing T‐capsulotomy were more likely to report routine closure than those using interportal capsulotomy.Clinical RelevanceUnderstanding evolving trends in capsular management provides insight into current surgical practices and may inform future guidelines in hip preservation surgery.

Hip arthroscopy has proven to be an effective treatment for hip pathology associated with femoroacetabular impingement. 1 Over time, the technical elements of this procedure have evolved, with more recent recognition of the important role the hip capsule plays in postoperative joint stability and improvement in functional outcomes for patients. 2 , 3 The hip capsule is comprised of both superficial (iliofemoral, ischiofemoral, and pubofemoral) and deep (zona orbicularis) ligamentous components. 4 Multiple anatomic studies have elucidated the role of the hip capsule in maintaining stability. 5 , 6 , 7 The body of literature supports the important role of the hip capsule and specifically the ability of the iliofemoral ligaments to restrict external and internal rotation range of motion (ROM), 6 as well as restricting traction and preventing displacement of the hip joint. 7

During hip arthroscopy, capsulotomy allows for visualization, ease of access, and the ability to perform arthroscopic procedures of the central and peripheral hip compartments. Multiple techniques have been described in creating capsulotomies 8 , 9 , 10 and their subsequent repair. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 An interportal capsulotomy is the most common technique, followed by a T‐capsulotomy (Figure 1). 18 Despite the identification of these techniques, there is little evidence supporting the decision‐making behind which approach to utilize. Studies have identified that capsular repair allows for restoration of capsular integrity after capsulotomy by restoring original joint kinematics and reducing excessive joint movement. 19 , 20 , 21 Despite these findings in biomechanical research, capsular closure remains controversial in clinical practice and not all hip arthroscopists uniformly perform this technical element. 18 , 22 , 23 , 24 Specifically, a survey of hip arthroscopists identified 1.9, 8.1, and 2.4 times higher odds of routinely closing the capsule for those who believed there is sufficient literature regarding the importance of this technique, those who practice in the United States relative to international surgeons, and those who received hip arthroscopy training during residency or fellowship, respectively. 24 However, this survey provided only a single time point and did not investigate changes in practice patterns that occur over time.

The purpose of this study was to examine recent trends of capsular management in hip arthroscopy over a 5‐year period (2018‐2023), both within the United States and internationally utilizing a repeated, international, cross‐sectional survey. We hypothesized that most surgeons would perform capsular repair as a routine part of their management and that this trend would grow over time with repeated survey distribution.

METHODS

Participants

This study was an international cross‐sectional survey conducted using the online software Qualtrics. The survey components were generated by the senior author (A.M.S.), who is dual‐fellowship trained in orthopedic sports medicine and hip preservation. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained, with exemption from continuing review. A list of surgeons was compiled by identifying the corresponding authors’ contact information for hip arthroscopy publications on PubMed. The survey was initially distributed in 2018 via email to this list. An identical survey was sent out in 2020 and 2023 to the same list; however, it was also distributed through the American Orthopaedic Society for Sports Medicine (AOSSM) listserv via approved organizational survey distribution. Inclusion for this study required completion of an orthopaedic residency, meaning that individuals undergoing training at the time of survey distribution, such as current residents, were excluded. Completion of a hip arthroscopy fellowship program was not a requirement in this study. No compensation was offered for participation in this study. The first page of the survey required the participant to provide consent before advancing through the questions.

Survey Structure

The survey comprised 26 questions, plus 5 optional clinical vignettes that focused on managing specific patient populations and hip‐related injuries (Supporting Information). The survey was designed to address the common discrepancies in management seen in practices in the United States and internationally, with the aim of understanding the different surgical approaches utilized by hip arthroscopists. Questions regarding capsular management focused on recent changes in surgical approach, type of capsulotomy performed, and details on capsular closure if they perform this technique. Respondents would only provide insight into capsular closure for the capsulotomy technique that they regularly or sometimes perform. Responses for the degree of hip flexion/extension when capsular closure is performed were only collected in 2018 and 2020. Participants were then asked about postoperative protocols and the occurrence of postoperative complications. The survey also included basic demographic questions on surgical experience and volume, type of practice, and arthroscopy training. Many questions required the selection of a multiple‐choice answer, with the option of including free‐text responses to further expand if applicable. The survey took approximately 5 minutes to complete.

Statistical Analysis

For specific variables, descriptive measures were recorded as frequencies and percentages for categorical data, or mean and standard deviation for continuous data. Chi‐square tests of independence were performed separately for interportal capsulotomy, T‐capsulotomy, and capsular closure of each capsulotomy technique. To compare trends between interportal and T‐capsulotomy technique utilization, a 3‐way contingency table was constructed (technique × year × response). The table was analyzed using a chi‐square test of independence. Standardized residuals were examined to identify specific response categories and time points contributing to significant differences. Residuals with an absolute value greater than 2 were considered indicative of a meaningful deviation from expected frequencies. The level of significance was set at α ≤ 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using R (Version 4.4.1; R Core Team).

RESULTS

Respondents

A total of 74, 78, and 83 hip arthroscopists completed the survey in 2018, 2020, and 2023, respectively. For surveys sent to the compiled email list in 2018, a response rate of 43% was attained. Response rates from the AOSSM organizational survey distribution tool could not be assessed. The characteristics for respondents are outlined in Table 1, including type of fellowship training, practice type, case volume, and practice experience. In 2018, 70% of respondents were from the United States, with the remaining individuals from Brazil, Canada, Denmark, France, Germany, South Korea, Spain, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom. Seventy‐seven percent of surgeons were from the United States in the 2020 survey, with 23% from Canada, Chile, Colombia, Denmark, France, Germany, Mexico, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom. Respondents to the 2023 survey included 78% from the United States and 22% from Denmark, Canada, Chile, Colombia, France, Spain, and Switzerland.

Across all surveys, >70% of all respondents were fellowship‐trained in hip arthroscopy (Table 1). Of note, the percentage of respondents who were not fellowship‐trained increased from 20.9% in 2018 and 23.4% in 2020 to 27.7% in 2023. Respondents represented a diverse range of practice types (Table 1). Data was also captured on practice experience, specific experience related to hip arthroscopy, and volume of hip arthroscopies (Table 1).

Capsulotomy Technique Utilization

Table 2 describes the type of capsulotomy technique utilized in clinical practice. Specific to interportal capsulotomy, at least 80% of surgeons used this technique across all time points. In 2018 and 2020, 7.4% and 7.8% of respondents, respectively, stated that they sometimes perform an interportal capsulotomy, whereas in 2023, no surgeons selected this response. For those who reported never utilizing interportal capsulotomy, alternative techniques included L‐shaped capsulotomy, outside‐in technique, longitudinal capsulotomy, ballooning, selective incisions, thinning technique, portal enlargement without connection, or T‐capsulotomy alone. Although a chi‐square test of independence did not reveal a statistically significant difference in interportal capsulotomy utilization across survey years (P = .081), the data suggest a potential trend toward decreased use of the “sometimes” category and a modest increase in “never” responses over time.

Capsulotomy Technique Utilization in Clinical Practice

In contrast, T‐capsulotomy utilization showed a statistically significant shift over time (P < .001). Although 29.8%, 25.9%, and 31.3% of surgeons reported routinely using this technique in 2018, 2020, and 2023, respectively, the proportion of those who used it “sometimes” declined from 28.4% in 2018 and 24.7% in 2020 to 0% in 2023. This was accompanied by a marked increase in “never” responses. For surgeons who never use a T‐capsulotomy, some reported using an L‐capsulotomy, suture suspension, periportal or capsular retraction stitches, and suspension sutures, although most used an interportal capsulotomy alone.

When comparing trends between interportal and T‐capsulotomy utilization, a highly significant difference in response distributions between techniques over time was observed (P < .001). Standardized residuals indicated that interportal capsulotomy was significantly more likely to be used “regularly” across all years (residuals: +4.79 in 2018, +4.90 in 2020, and +5.25 in 2023) and significantly less likely to be reported as “never” (−4.39, −4.52, and −3.22, respectively). In contrast, T‐capsulotomy was more likely to be used “sometimes” in 2018 and 2020 (residuals: +4.97 and +4.26), but this dropped sharply in 2023 (−3.45), accompanied by a significant increase in “never” responses (+7.28). These findings highlight a divergence in clinical practice, with interportal capsulotomy remaining consistently favored, although T‐capsulotomy appears to be falling out of favor.

Regarding clinical vignettes, interportal capsulotomy with capsular repair was selected the most for all scenarios at all time points, followed by T‐capsulotomy with capsular repair (Figure S1). There were varying results among the other treatment choices for the given cases.

Capsular Closure Technique Utilization

Responses to capsular closure management, differentiated by type of capsulotomy performed, are shown in Table 3. For those who perform interportal capsulotomy, more than half of surgeons reported that they always close the capsule (Table 3). In 2018 and 2020, less than 10% of respondents stated that they never perform capsular closure, with less than 5% of surgeons selecting this response in 2023 (Table 3). No statistically significant difference was observed in the distribution of capsular closure frequency responses across the 3 survey years (P = .499), suggesting that surgeon preferences for interportal capsular closure have remained relatively consistent over time. The most commonly reported number of sutures used for capsular closure after interportal capsulotomy was 2 or 3, with ranges of 2 to 5 for 2018 and 1 to 5 for both 2020 and 2023 (Table 3). The most common hip flexion position during capsular closure was 30° to 35°, 20° to 25°, and 40° to 45°, as well as 10° of hip extension (Figure 2).

Specific to those who perform T‐capsulotomy, a majority (≥75%) of surgeons reported always closing the capsule, and 5% or fewer stated never performing capsular closer (Table 3). No statistically significant difference in the distribution of closure frequency responses across the 3 survey years was observed (P = .857), indicating that surgeon‐reported practices for T‐capsulotomy closure have remained stable over time. For capsular closure following T‐capsulotomy, the most commonly reported number of sutures used was 2 or 3, with ranges for 2018, 2020, and 2023 of 1 to 5, 1 to 7, and 1 to 6, respectively (Table 3). The most common hip flexion position during capsular closure was 30° to 35°, 40° to 45°, and 20° to 25°, as well as 0° to 10° of hip extension (Figure 3).

In 2018, 16 surgeons (21%) reported recent changes in capsular management, with 8 surgeons (50%) reporting that they began closing more capsules and/or are performing more robust repairs. Three surgeons (19%) reported that they were closing the capsule less, with 2 stating they only do so in patients with hyperlaxity or dysplasia and 1 surgeon leaving the capsule open in cases of mild osteoarthritis. There were 2 surgeons (12.5%) who stated they had switched from simple to figure of 8 sutures for closure. In 2020, 19 surgeons (24%) reported recent changes in their capsular management. Of these, 13 surgeons (68%) began closing more capsules and/or are performing more robust repairs. Three surgeons (16%) stated changing the type of suture used. Two surgeons (11%) decreased the frequency in which they closed the capsule, with 1 surgeon using selective closure and 1 surgeon never closing the capsule. One surgeon stated they do not violate the capsule, therefore not using an interportal capsulotomy or T‐capsulotomy. From the 2023 survey, 16 surgeons (20%) reported making recent changes in their capsular management. Seven surgeons (44%) stated changing the type of capsulotomy they performed. Three surgeons (19%) changed the type of suture they used for capsule closure. One surgeon (6%) began closing the capsule, and 1 surgeon (6%) stated they no longer performed capsular closure. Additionally, 1 surgeon (6%) said they began performing capsular plication in patients with microinstability of the hip.

Postoperative Management

Postoperatively, ROM was always restricted by 63%, 57%, and 48% of surgeons in 2018, 2020, and 2023, respectively. Restrictions were similar across all time points, including 90° of hip flexion, limited external rotation and hip extension, and no hyperextension. Duration of motion restrictions ranged from 2 to 6 weeks. In 2018 and 2020, respectively, 26% and 31% of respondents reported never restricting ROM postoperatively. In 2023, 4 surgeons reported only restricting motion during capsular plication cases. In 2018, 2020, and 2023, 23%, 19%, and 21% of respondents, respectively, reported having patients utilize a postoperative brace in all cases. A brace was never used by 54%, 53%, and 51% of respondents in 2018, 2020, and 2023, respectively.

Complications

In 2018, 15 postoperative dislocations were reported among 12 surgeons. The capsule was repaired in 60% of these cases. Of these dislocations, 1 case was published. In 2020, 15 surgeons reported 19 postoperative dislocations. The capsule was repaired in 53% of these cases. Of these dislocations, 3 cases were published. From the 2023 survey, 18 postoperative dislocations were reported among 18 surgeons. The capsule was repaired in 44% of these cases. Four of these cases were published.

DISCUSSION

The principal finding of this study is that most surgeons continue to use an interportal capsulotomy for joint visualization during hip arthroscopy, with this technique remaining consistently favored across all survey years. Capsular repair was reported as a routine element of care regardless of the capsulotomy type, supporting the primary hypothesis that most surgeons perform capsular closure as a standard part of capsular management. Notably, surgeons who perform T‐capsulotomy were more likely to report always closing the capsule compared with those using an interportal capsulotomy. Although the majority of respondents reported routinely performing capsular closure at all time points, no statistically significant change in closure frequency was observed over the 5‐year study period. These findings suggest that capsular repair has remained a consistent standard of care rather than a growing trend, reflecting stable preferences and reinforcing the perceived importance of capsular integrity in hip arthroscopy.

Overall, the results of this study reflect and build upon current literature on the surgical management of the hip capsule during hip arthroscopy. 24 Kerzner et al. 24 conducted a large, cross‐sectional, multinational survey and identified that capsular management practices vary significantly based on geography and surgeon training background, with US‐based and fellowship‐trained surgeons more likely to perform routine capsular closure. Our study complements and extends these findings by using a repeated survey design to evaluate how capsular management practices have evolved over time. Although Kerzner et al. provided a valuable snapshot of global practice patterns, our longitudinal approach reveals that capsular closure has remained a consistent standard of care over a 5‐year period, rather than showing significant growth. Additionally, our study highlights a divergence in capsulotomy technique preferences, with interportal capsulotomy remaining dominant and T‐capsulotomy use declining. Together, these studies underscore the ongoing variability in capsular management and the influence of both surgeon‐specific and temporal factors on clinical decision‐making. Recent literature continues to support capsular closure due to its association with improved functional outcomes and restoration of native hip joint biomechanics. 20 , 21 , 24

Of note, for both interportal capsulotomy and T‐capsulotomy, a wide range of responses were found for the number of sutures utilized in routine closure, which may introduce variability in the quality of capsular repair. This range may necessitate further studies to report on the number of sutures utilized during capsular closure, rather than only stating if a repair was performed. Regardless of capsulotomy type, it was identified that most hip arthroscopists who routinely perform capsular closure prefer to position the hip joint in 25° to 35° of hip flexion while performing this technique, with some favoring positions of 40° to 45° of hip flexion, and only a minor proportion preferring 10° of hip extension. Positioning of the hip during closure has clinical relevance, as it may influence the tension of the capsule. 6 , 25 , 26 Performing closure in less flexion is thought to better replicate the native tension of the iliofemoral ligament and anterior capsule, potentially reducing the risk of postoperative stiffness or instability. 4 , 6 , 25 Although direct comparative studies are limited, it is generally accepted that performing capsular closure in excessive extension may overtension the capsule, potentially limiting postoperative ROM, whereas closure in excessive flexion may result in residual laxity. 25 , 26 These biomechanical considerations underscore the importance of standardizing not only the decision to close the capsule but also the technique and joint positioning during repair.

These studies coincide with the comparison of outcomes between capsular repair and nonrepair. Frank et al. 2 investigated partial versus complete repair in patients who underwent a T‐capsulotomy. Of 32 patients who received complete capsule repair, none required a revision, whereas 4 patients (13%) required revision after partial capsular repair. 2 Of note, those who underwent a complete capsular repair also had significantly higher patient‐reported outcomes and satisfaction at final follow‐up. 2 Similarly, in a 2‐year follow‐up of high‐level athletes, Hassebrock et al. 3 found that complete capsule closure after hip arthroscopy was associated with superior patient‐reported outcomes compared with nonclosure. Repairing the capsule not only has implications for postoperative hip stability and improved patient‐reported outcomes but potentially may reduce the need for revision and additional surgeries. 27 In contrast, Domb et al. 11 found no differences in patient‐reported outcomes when comparing capsular repair and unrepaired capsulotomy at 2‐year follow‐up. Although results of the 5‐year comparison study did find that the unrepaired group had inferior patient‐reported outcomes, these patients still met the minimal clinically important difference and patient acceptable symptom state criteria and had revision rates and complication rates similar to those of the capsular repair group. 28 In this study, the choice of capsule management was made on a patient‐specific basis from clinical, radiographic, and arthroscopic findings, 11 , 23 , 28 suggesting that the decision for capsular repair should be individualized. The conflicting view of whether a capsular repair should be performed in routine clinical practice is reflected within our survey results, with some surgeons noting they perform a capsular closure either occasionally or most of the time. For example, some respondents reported only closing the capsule in patients who had distinct features such as hyperlaxity, dysplasia, or mild osteoarthritis. Although much of the recently published literature favors closing the capsule, survey results suggest that there is no standardized approach and that patient characteristics may dictate the surgeon's decision‐making for capsule management. This is further evident in responses to the clinical vignettes presented in the survey, which identified that the surgical strategy may change between cases.

One of the major complications that can occur after hip arthroscopy is hip instability, manifested as joint subluxation or dislocation. Although relatively rare, these events can have significant clinical consequences. Current research on atraumatic hip instability and subluxation emphasizes restoring capsular integrity, particularly in patients with hyperlaxity. 23 , 29 Although capsular repair is favored by most surgeons, there may be certain indications in which it is not recommended. For instance, leaving the capsule open has been suggested to be advantageous in individuals with preoperative stiffness or poor capsular compliance. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 A recent systematic review identified 11 cases of postoperative instability (9 dislocations and 2 subluxations), with interportal capsulotomy used in 10 of those cases and T‐capsulotomy in 1. 34 Notably, only 2 of these cases involved capsular closure, both following an interportal capsulotomy, suggesting that both the decision to close and the quality of closure may influence outcomes. 34 Our survey data revealed that in 2018 and 2020, the majority of reported dislocations occurred despite capsular repair, whereas in 2023, most dislocations occurred in cases where the capsule was left open. Importantly, instability and dislocation risk after hip arthroscopy is multifactorial and not well understood. Patient‐specific factors such as generalized ligamentous laxity, acetabular dysplasia, and female sex have been associated with increased risk of postoperative instability. 23 , 35 Additionally, surgical technique, including the extent of capsulotomy, completeness of repair, and hip position during closure, may influence capsular tension and joint stability. 25 , 26 Our findings suggest that although capsular repair is an important component of hip stability, it alone may not be sufficient to prevent dislocation.

Limitations

The formatting of the survey for this study represents a limitation, as the multiple‐choice design allowed for objective data collection but was restrictive and could lead to potential bias. Although the free‐text option allows for more insight and subjective data collection, these responses provided more ambiguity and were not utilized by all participants. A small portion of respondents did not provide responses to specific questions and may have led to participation bias. The inability to calculate the response rate for time points of 2020 and 2023 is a major limitation in this study. Most participants were identified through a PubMed literature review of corresponding authors for hip arthroscopy and related topics. Several authors identified were either current resident at the time of survey distribution, did not perform hip arthroscopy, or had an out‐of‐date email listed. As the 2020 and 2023 versions of the survey were additionally sent out via an approved organizational survey distribution tool within the AOSSM, members of the organization would take the study voluntarily. Unfortunately, due to the nature of this survey distribution route, a response rate could not be obtained. Additionally, the process of identifying respondents for the 2018 survey via a PubMed review of corresponding authors and by utilizing the AOSSM distribution list is subject to selection bias, as this does not include other professional organizations or surgeons who are not members of AOSSM or who have not published literature. Lastly, although inclusion for this survey required respondents to have completed an orthopaedic residency and self‐report being orthopaedic surgeons with expert knowledge and experience in the field of hip arthroscopy, as the survey was anonymous, it was not feasible to confirm respondents’ training level or practice type.

CONCLUSIONS

Interportal capsulotomy remains the preferred technique for joint access during hip arthroscopy, with consistent use across the 5‐year study period. Capsular closure was routinely performed regardless of capsulotomy type, and although survey responses suggested a trend toward more consistent closure practices, no statistically significant change in closure frequency was observed. Surgeons performing T‐capsulotomy were more likely to report routine closure than those using interportal capsulotomy.
