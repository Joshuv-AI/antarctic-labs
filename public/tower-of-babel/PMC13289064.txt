Factors associated with psychological burden among informal caregivers in outpatient palliative care: a prospective observational study of patient–caregiver dyads in Germany
Authors: Leopold Hentschel, Beate Hornemann, Ulrich Schuler, André Wellesen, Charis Haering, Birgit Helbig, Maria Janisch, Katharina Schütte, Pauline Langguth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInformal caregivers are of utmost importance in providing sufficient care for palliative outpatients, yet are at risk for psychological burden themselves which might limit their capability to maintain their support.AimThis project aims to identify structural and psychological factors that are associated with the psychological burden of informal caregivers.DesignThe prospective observational study recorded illness-related and structural data. Multiple logistic regressions were performed to identify relevant factors for increased psychological burden.Setting/participantsThis monocentric study recruited 250 adult patients of a specialised outpatient palliative care team and 152 caregivers between April 2021 and August 2022.ResultsOf 250 patients screened for participation, 152 caregiver–patient dyads were included in this study. Above-threshold depressivity was reported by 31%, anxiety by 33% and an elaborated psychological distress was stated by 82% of informal caregivers. Multivariate analyses revealed a significant association of above-threshold depressivity with higher daily caregiving time (OR: 3.215; p=0.026) and being highly burdened by COVID-19 (OR: 1.142; p=0.048). Perceived higher burden from COVID-19 seemed to be protective concerning elevated anxiety (OR: 0.859; p=0.024). High distress was non-significantly associated with higher education (p=0.056) and support by a professional nursing service (p=0.069). The statistical models explain 19% (anxiety, depressivity) and 21% (distress) of the total variance.ConclusionAs the psychological burden of informal caregivers in an outpatient setting remains high, it tends to be slightly below those strains reported by informal caregivers in inpatient or curative settings. This project adds to the scarce knowledge about associated factors. Further research could address how to implement these results into daily clinical practice, for example, accounting for risk-factors in discharge routine.Trial registration numberNCT05082389.Keywords: COVID-19, Caregivers, PALLIATIVE CARE, Patient Reported Outcome Measures, Prospective studies

Background

Given that the majority of patients prefer end-of-life care in a familiar environment,1 2 a robust support network is essential. In Western countries, professional services are increasingly overstretched as survival rates rise,3 often at the expense of increased treatment-related toxicities.4 Consequently, informal caregivers have become pivotal, providing the lion’s share of caregiving.5 Beyond emotional and social support, they assist with a wide range of activities of daily living, thereby enabling patients to remain in their preferred surroundings. While caregiving may foster a sense of meaning, commitment or self-esteem,6 7 it can also contribute to a considerable emotional, physical and financial burden8,10

Specifically, caregivers frequently report significant fatigue, sleep disturbances and exhaustion.11,13 Across various settings of palliative services, between 54% and 96% of informal caregivers report elevated distress.14,17 Two studies report increased depressivity in four of 10 informal caregivers in a palliative care ward,14 15 while 28% of informal caregivers of patients treated at home report elevated depressivity.18 Clinically relevant anxiety has been reported in 43–47% of an inpatient-setting,14 15 while one-third of informal caregivers caring at home report this symptom.18 Furthermore, the COVID-19 pandemic additionally challenges informal caregivers. Yet data concerning the psychological state of informal caregivers remains descriptive and exploratory, elevated levels of depressivity and anxiety19,21 were reported while overall well-being remained unchanged in 64% of informal caregivers.22

Several factors that might be of relevance for the psychological state and a functional coping with caregiver burden are being discussed. As quality of measures, care-setting, indication and population remains heterogenous, data should be perceived with caution.

Risk factors associated with elevated psychological distress in a sample of caregivers of predominantly patients with gastrointestinal and lung cancer were younger age of patients, informal caregivers being female and younger.23 The results regarding younger age of informal caregivers (<60 years) were replicated in a sample of inpatient palliative patients by Oechsle and colleagues.15 Furthermore, being married to the patient, more caregiving hours and more frequent visits by professional caregivers are associated with a higher distress.16 24 Regarding depressive symptoms and anxiety, a recent meta-analysis25 reported female gender and the patient’s emotional state as being moderately associated factors while aversive changes to informal caregiver’s life were associated to an even greater extent.

Overall, there is increasing, yet limited data about the psychological well-being of informal caregivers of those patients receiving professional support by a specialised outpatient palliative care (SOPC) team and the respective factors that are associated with a decreased emotional status. Therefore, this study aims to describe the psychological well-being of informal caregivers and to identify factors that might contribute to an elevated level of psychological burden.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as a prospective, monocentric observational study. The study was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05082389).

Data was obtained longitudinally from three sources: caregiver, nurse and routine data provided in patients’ files. Caregivers answered questionnaires at baseline, after 3 and 6 months. Monthly, data from the patients’ files as well as nurse-reported data was assessed for 6 months. The participation of respective informal caregivers was stopped in case the patient deceased. Details are presented in figure 1. The present analysis focuses on cross-sectional data collected at the initial assessment (T1), as the availability of IC-reported data declined at T4 and T7. Full details on study-design are provided in the study’s protocol.26

Data acquisition. IC, informal caregiver.

Participants

Participants were primary caregivers recruited from an SOPC service, which provides home-based symptom management to prevent unnecessary hospital admissions.27 The primary caregiver was identified as the adult person who provides support for most of the time.

Study measures

Structural and disease-related data was derived from patients’ health-records.

Psychological distress was assessed applying the Distress Thermometer (DT),28 a well-established and broadly used questionnaire. It consists of a visual analogue scale on which persons rate their distress from 0 to 10 with higher scores indicating higher burden. A threshold of ≥5 is recommended in Germany to identify cases of clinical relevance.28

Anxiety was measured with the Generalised Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7).29 It encompasses seven items summing up to a score from 0 to 21 with higher scores expressing more severe anxiety. A cut-off of ≥10 is recommended to identify cases of clinical relevance.30 Depressivity was assessed with the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9).31 It consists of nine items which sum up to a single sum score ranging from 0 to 27 with higher scores indicating a higher level of burden. A cut-off of ≥10 is recommended to identify cases of clinical relevance.32

Due to the absence of a psychometrically validated instrument at the time of the study, the influence of the COVID-19 pandemic was assessed administering four self-developed items considering changes in professional support structures, worries about an infection, psychological burden due to regulations (online supplemental file S1). The infection domain was captured with two separate items: one addressing worries about becoming infected oneself and another addressing worries about the informal caregiver becoming infected. Each statement can be answered on a verbally linked scale from ‘not all’ to ‘completely’. A mean score was calculated across all items ranging from 0 to 4 with higher scores indicating higher impact of COVID-19.

Statistical analysis

For the calculation of GAD-7 and PHQ-9 total scores, participants with two or more missing items on a given scale were excluded from the computation. If only one item was missing, it was imputed using the mean of the remaining items for that individual. For all subsequent analyses, pairwise deletion was applied.

To identify factors associated with high mental burden of informal caregivers, bivariate statistics were calculated. Depending on the data’s level of measurement, statistically significant differences between groups (distress: cut-off <5 and ≥5; depressive and anxiety symptoms: cut-off <10 and ≥10) were assessed by either chi-square tests or Mann-Whitney U tests (two-tailed). The results were expressed as frequencies with percentages or a mean with SD. Factors with a p value less than 0.05 were regarded significant in the bivariate analyses. To identify potential associated factors for inclusion in the multivariate models and to avoid overfitting, variables with a p value of up to 0.2 were considered for inclusion, with a maximum of six variables per model, based on the recommended practice of 20 participants per included parameter.33 If more than six variables met this criterion, those with the lowest p values were selected. Variables were entered into the models stepwise.

Multiple logistic regression models were calculated with moderate to severe depressive and anxiety symptoms as well as high distress being the dependent variables. Informal caregivers with none or mild symptoms were classified as reference groups, respectively. Variables in the logistic regression models were included based on their significance in bivariate analysis. In addition, perceived burden related to the COVID-19 pandemic was included as a confounding variable in all multivariable analyses, irrespective of its statistical significance, due to its potential impact on mental health outcomes. Exploratively, a multiple linear model with the dependent variable overall mental burden was calculated. For this purpose, the variable mental burden is created as a new target variable—a composite score from the values of the GAD-7 and PHQ-9 questionnaires for explorative purpose. Beforehand, Cronbach’s alpha was calculated to determine the internal consistency. Due to excellent internal consistency, the composite score was considered suitable for further explorative analysis. Variables included in the linear regression model were selected based on existing literature.

Analysis was controlled for the score assessing burden due to COVID-19. The strengths of associations were expressed as ORs with 95% CIs. The data were analysed using SPSS V.27 (SPSS).

Patient and public involvement

The public and patients or their caregivers were not involved in the design, study conduction or reporting of study results.

Psychological burden of informal caregivers

Mean level of distress was 6.52 (SD=2.23). A substantial majority of caregivers (82.4%) met the criteria for clinically significant distress (DT ≥5). Prevalence of moderate to high depression was 30.6%. The most prevalent symptoms were sleep disturbances (64.8%), fatigue (40.4%) and anhedonia (64.8%).

Prevalence of moderate to high anxiety was 32.8%. Most frequent symptoms occurring were difficulty relaxing (38.5%), nervousness, tension and anxiety (32.8%), as well as excessive worrying (30.4%).

Group comparisons for distress, depressive and anxiety symptom levels

Contrasting to those informal caregivers with non-elaborated distress, burdened informal caregivers were significantly younger, were more likely employed, were more likely to have children in their household and reported higher burden because of COVID-19. Furthermore, a significant group difference was observed regarding the distribution of educational levels, clinically significant distressed informal caregivers appear to have more frequently intermediate to high educational backgrounds (each p<0.05).

Informal caregivers with moderate to high depressive symptoms were younger and more likely women compared with those informal caregivers with no or low depressive symptoms (each p<0.05).

Informal caregivers with moderate to high anxiety symptoms were younger, cared for significantly younger patients, had a higher care time and reported higher burden because of COVID-19 compared with those informal caregivers with no or low depressive symptoms (each p<0.05).

Factors associated with elevated psychological burden

In terms of factors associated with increased distress, based on significance levels (p<0.200), use of a care service as well as age, education level, employment status and distress due to COVID-19 were included in multivariate analyses. No variable was significantly associated with high distress (table 4). Although not statistically significant at the standard level, there was a statistical trend of the use of a nursing service (p=0.069) as well as high educational level (p=0.056) with above-threshold distress. The regression model explained 21% of the total variance (Nagelkerke’s R² = 0.210).

Regarding above threshold depressivity, based on significance levels (p<0.200) care time, gender and age of informal caregivers, age of patients as well as burden because of COVID-19 were included in multivariate analyses. In the multivariate regression model higher care time (12–24 hours) as well as higher burden because of COVID-19 was significantly associated with moderate to severe depressive symptoms in informal caregivers. The regression model explained 19% of the total variance (Nagelkerke’s R²=0.187).

Concerning anxiety, based on significance levels (p<0.200) care time, gender and age of informal caregivers as well as patients’ gender and burden because of COVID-19 were included in multivariate analyses. In the multivariate regression model higher COVID-19-related burden was paradoxically associated with lower anxiety levels. The regression model explained 19% of the total variance (Nagelkerke’s R²=0.194). Details are reported in table 4.

Composite score ‘Psychological Burden’

The score ‘Composite Psychological Burden’ aims to merge the three different psychological outcomes anxiety, depressivity and distress into a more global outcome. To assess the internal consistency of the newly formed score Composite Psychological Burden, Cronbach’s alpha was calculated for a total of 16 items. None of the items exhibited a discriminant validity of 0.3. The internal consistency was excellent (Cronbach’s alpha=0.902). Concerning this novel composite score, only COVID-19 burden was of relevance with higher scores being associated with higher Psychological Burden. Details are reported in table 5.

Discussion

Main findings and added knowledge

Our data depicts a high psychological burden of informal caregivers taking care for their loved-ones in an outpatient setting. More than 80% of informal caregivers experience elevated distress, which is less than numbers reported by Oechsle and colleagues for specialised inpatient care which state elevated distress in up to 95% of all informal caregivers.15 34 To the best of our knowledge, little is known about reasons for this difference.35 Paradoxically, distress levels appear higher in inpatient settings despite the availability of more intensive professional support. Still, those patients that need to be taken care in an inpatient setting might suffer from more severe symptoms.

Our data suggests elevated symptoms of anxiety and depression in around one third of informal caregivers which aligns closely to those numbers (anxiety: 33%, depression: 28%) reported by Götze for informal caregivers in a specialised outpatient setting,18 slightly below those percentages (anxiety: 47%, depression: 39%) reported for inpatient settings14 15 and far below those two thirds of informal caregivers with a high risk for depression or anxiety reported in a mixed-setting36 while a current review37 reports a slightly higher number (depression: 38%) for ICs within both, palliative and curative, setting. A current meta-analysis describing prevalence of depressive symptoms of caregivers of cancer patients38 reports a relevant extent in 42% which is also slightly above our numbers. Of course, the work of Bedaso and colleagues lacks comparability as they describe caregivers of patients with cancer (treated in both, curative or palliative, intents and excluding informal caregivers of patients with a non-malignant disease) and use a broad variety of questionnaires.

Our findings add to the very rare data available in an outpatient setting. As the psychological burden of informal caregivers remained underassessed39 which might also result from a lack of sufficient instruments, recently a screener became available to be administered in busy clinical routine.34

Identifying factors associated with increased distress in informal caregivers, higher education and the use of nursing services showed non-significant trends towards an association with distress. Interestingly, in a post hoc survey by Grande and coworkers, a professional nursing-service do not either decrease or increase psychological burden of informal caregivers.40 As a nursing-service predominantly cares for the patient not for the informal caregiver’s mental well-being, one might assume that highly distressed informal caregivers are more likely to use a professional nursing service rather than this support increases distress.

Daily care-time was significantly associated with a more than threefold risk of high depressive symptoms. This partly contradicts findings that were reported by Higginson and colleagues that identified higher ‘informal care costs’, operationalised as weekly care-time, being associated with less grief and higher positive aspects of caregiving41 while being non-associated with caregiver-burden. The latter also being reported by a Portuguese bicentric study,36 research needs to precise the concept of ‘daily care-time’ in terms of what actually is being measured such as ‘being on call’, helping with daily activities, supporting in medical or nursing activities in order to extract what actually might be burdensome and what might be protective against psychological distress.

Regarding the impact of COVID-19 on the psychological state of informal caregivers our study yields mixed results. While a higher burden caused by COVID-19 was associated with a slightly increased probability of suffering from an above-threshold depressivity, a higher COVID-19-related burden appeared to be inversely associated with severe anxiety symptoms. As the observation of reduced anxiety levels despite a high reported COVID-19 burden appears counterintuitive, no explanatory data are available for this specific context. Accordingly, this paper can only offer cautious hypotheses regarding potential underlying mechanisms. It is conceivable that informal caregivers developed a degree of habituation to pandemic-related fears over time, whereas depressive symptoms, such as reduced energy levels, may have intensified as cumulative exhaustion increased. Consequently, the present findings should be interpreted as a limited contribution to the emerging literature. Therefore, this paper can add to the emerging body of data highlighting novel burdens of informal caregivers during a global pandemic such as fearing for their own life,42 facing solely virtual palliative outpatient support instead of personal visitation of their professional caregivers42 43 and being restrained from other social support44 and the balance between preventing infection and remaining visits of friends and relatives.21 This project adds quantitative measures to this specific setting of outpatient-palliative care in predominantly patients with cancer during the COVID-pandemic.

Strengths and limitations

Yet this study was conducted at a single institution, which limits generalisability, it included—to the best of our knowledge—one of the largest samples in an SOPC setting.

This project was conducted while the COVID-19 pandemic emerged. As caregivers’ burden tends to have been increased systematically during the COVID-19 pandemic21 and might have been biased compared with non-pandemic years, we aimed to assess that pandemic-related burden as well. Due to the lack of a psychometrically validated questionnaire, we applied a self-developed four-item assessment which most likely lacked validity and comparability.

As there usually is a close relationship between staff of an SOPC team and the respective patients and their relatives, we cannot completely avoid socially desired responses. Furthermore, additional concepts more specific for informal caregivers of patients with a life-threatening illness such as hopelessness, preloss grief or death-anxiety45 could be assessed in further research.

The response rate might be perceived as lower compared with other settings (ie, inpatient palliative care, curative treatment settings or studies including non-malignant diseases) of observational studies. Nevertheless it seems well in line with the rate reported by Götze and colleagues in a similar setting.18

Conducting longitudinal studies in a specialised palliative care setting presents several challenges, including a decline in the number of patients and a high psychological and physical burden for patients and ICs.46 As with other studies in comparable settings, this project has experienced a decline in ICs participating at time points four and seven, which has limited the availability of sufficient data for longitudinal analysis. Finally, the outcome variables (distress, anxiety and depression) were dichotomised based on established clinical cut-offs in order to identify clinically relevant symptom burden. While this approach enhances clinical interpretability and applicability, dichotomisation may result in a loss of information and reduced statistical power compared with analyses using continuous measures.

Clinical implication

As palliative care intends to provide support for the unit of care, a routine assessment of the informal caregivers’ burden needs to be established. With the recently developed questionnaire for Caregiver Burden in Palliative Care (CAREPAL-8),34 a screening tool is available, yet not validated, that might be of clinical importance and economic to administer as it divides informal caregivers into four categories which might guide to the support needed. Identifying those informal caregivers in need, interventions can be applied which seem to target successfully depressivity, anxiety and mental health.47

Conclusion

Psychological burden remains high in informal caregivers of palliative outpatients, yet rates of anxiety, depressive symptoms and distress tend to be below those of caregivers of inpatients or in curative settings. Higher caregiving time and feeling burdened by COVID-19 is associated with severe depressive symptoms, while the latter seems to alleviate anxiety symptoms. Further work needs to be dedicated towards routinely assessing the psychological burden with validated measures as well as providing adequate interventions.
