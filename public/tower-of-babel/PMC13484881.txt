Predicting Acute Kidney Injury After Lung Transplantation: Development and Internal Validation of Perioperative Prediction Models
Authors: Shogo Trevena, Gregory I Snell, Glen P Westall, Mohammad Asghari‐Jafarabadi, Mark A Shulman, Tim G Coulson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAcute kidney injury (AKI) is common following lung transplantation (LTx), and is associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Existing renal injury risk models have not systematically evaluated perioperative variables. We aimed to develop comprehensive preoperative and early postoperative prediction models for AKI and renal replacement therapy (RRT) following LTx.MethodsAdult LTx recipients at a single Australian tertiary center (January 2016–April 2025) were included in a retrospective cohort study. The KDIGO 2012 serum creatinine criteria were used to define AKI. Stable predictors identified via penalized regression with bootstrap resampling were incorporated into multivariable logistic regression models. Bootstrap internal validation provided optimism‐adjusted performance estimates, and decision curve analysis evaluated net clinical benefit.ResultsOf 534 patients, 216 (40.4%) developed AKI, and 47 (8.8%) required RRT. The preoperative AKI model, incorporating baseline demographics, comorbidities, and laboratory variables, achieved a C‐statistic of 0.632 (95%CI: 0.589–0.679). The postoperative AKI model demonstrated improved discrimination (C‐statistic 0.741, 95%CI: 0.697–0.795), incorporating preoperative variables such as chronic lung allograft dysfunction as a transplant indication, alongside procedure duration and novel early postoperative predictors: P/F ratio and cumulative noradrenaline dosage. The RRT models achieved C‐statistics of 0.720 (95%CI: 0.643–0.796) and 0.847 (95%CI: 0.781–0.917). Calibration slopes ranged from 0.889‐0.949. Decision curve analysis demonstrated net clinical benefit over treat‐all strategies across clinically relevant threshold probabilities.ConclusionsEarly postoperative models demonstrated superior discrimination and clinical utility, with timepoint‐specific risk stratification capturing distinct perioperative determinants of renal injury. These models provide a foundation for external validation and targeted nephroprotective research in LTx.Keywords: acute kidney injury, lung transplantation, multivariable analysis, perioperative risk, prediction modelling, renal outcomes, renal replacement therapy

Introduction

Lung transplantation (LTx) is one of few treatment options to improve quality of life in end‐stage lung disease, with over 4600 lung transplants performed worldwide each year [1]. With the number of LTx recipients growing, efforts toward optimizing perioperative outcomes are vital. Despite advances in surgical techniques and perioperative management, acute kidney injury (AKI) complicates over half of all lung transplants and is associated with increased mortality, longer hospital stays, progression to chronic kidney disease (CKD), and greater healthcare costs [2, 3, 4]. Up to 9.3% of all LTx patients require renal replacement therapy (RRT) postoperatively, which carries an additional mortality, morbidity and economic burden [2]. Early identification of patients at high risk of renal injury postoperatively, and an enhanced understanding of AKI risk in the LTx cohort, could enable targeted nephroprotective strategies and lead to optimization of resource allocation and outcomes [5].

The development of AKI in LTx is multifactorial, and a result of a complex interplay between patient characteristics, intraoperative determinants and early postoperative factors [6]. LTx often involves long, complex procedures with immense surgical and physiological insults, further exacerbated by additional postoperative factors that derange renal function. Despite high AKI rates following LTx and numerous identified risk factors, the relative significance of variables across perioperative timepoints and their ability to predict renal injury remains poorly characterized. Existing risk models for renal injury after LTx have not investigated variables across the perioperative course or prioritized routinely available clinical variables, potentially limiting their practical utility [7, 8, 9]. Evaluating AKI prediction at distinct perioperative stages may help identify a model that balances prediction accuracy with clinically actionable lead time, offering a pragmatic compromise between these two competing priorities. This may also improve characterization of the temporal development of AKI across the perioperative course in LTx. To our knowledge, no studies exist that investigate perioperative renal dysfunction following LTx in an Australian context.

We hypothesized that renal injury risk evolves across the perioperative period and that determinants of AKI and RRT requirement are distinct, necessitating timepoint‐specific assessment for accurate prediction. Accordingly, we aimed to develop and internally validate independent prediction models at both preoperative and early postoperative timepoints for prediction of AKI and RRT requirement following LTx, and to evaluate their comparative performance and utility.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of all adult (≥18 years of age) LTx patients at a single tertiary center in Australia between January 2016 and April 2025. Patients who received hemodialysis prior to surgery, died within 48 h postoperatively, received simultaneous transplantation of other organs, or were missing key serum creatinine (SCr) data were excluded. This study was approved by Alfred Hospital Ethics Committee (project no. 666/20). Given the retrospective study design and use of routinely collected clinical date, the requirement for informed consent was waived. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines [10].

Perioperative Management and Immunosuppression

This study was conducted at the Alfred Hospital, a high‐volume lung transplant center, typically performing over 70–100 transplants annually. Patients received a standard triple immunosuppressant regimen consisting of prednisolone tapering to 0.3 mg/kg daily, mycophenolate 1000–1500 mg daily and tacrolimus (trough level 10 ng/ml in first two weeks). Immunosuppression was tailored depending on rejection history, infection, bone marrow suppression, and renal impairment. Induction immunosuppression with basiliximab was given as a calcineurin inhibitor sparing agent according to clinical judgement. The majority of cases were undertaken without extracorporeal support, with the perioperative considerations around extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) in LTx having been discussed elsewhere [11]. Postoperative fluid, inotropic and vasopressor management have previously been described and targeted specific hemodynamic goals [12].

Outcomes

Any‐stage AKI was defined using the 2012 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines, determined by changes from baseline SCr (value taken closest to the start of surgery) and the maximum SCr value within seven days following surgery [13]. An increase in SCr by ≥ 26.5 µmol/l within 48 h or ≥ 1.5 times baseline within seven days was used [13]. Urine output criteria were not utilized due to inconsistent recording, potential confounding by perioperative diuretic use, and to be consistent with existing research that utilized creatinine criteria only.

The requirement for RRT was defined as the initiation of RRT at any point postoperatively within the index hospital admission.

Data Collection and Variable Definition

Data were obtained from electronic medical records. Preoperative and intraoperative variables were available for all patients. Some postoperative variables were not electronically recorded until September 2018, meaning postoperative data was subject to temporal restriction. To maximize statistical power, analyses were stratified by data availability. As such, any analyses using preoperative and intraoperative variables only, utilized the full sample size, whilst analyses involving postoperative data used a restricted sample.

Baseline patient data included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), time on the waitlist (days), primary transplant indication, procedure type (number of lungs) and comorbidities. Comorbid conditions were determined by ICD‐10 codes and preadmission clinic notes. Preoperative laboratory data were the laboratory results collected closest to the start of surgery and included white cell count (WCC), hemoglobin (Hb), platelet count, and SCr. The use of ECMO prior to transplant was also recorded.

Intraoperative data included operative factors such as procedure duration (hours) and use of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Intraoperative physiological parameters included the percentage of surgical time with: SpO2<90%, mean arterial pressure (MAP) <65 mmHg, and central venous pressure (CVP) between 20 and 50 mmHg. Laboratory values obtained from intraoperative arterial blood gases included the maximum lactate, minimum pH, and minimum Hb. The administration of intraoperative vasopressor and inotrope medications, as well as the transfusion of blood products, was also collected.

All postoperative variables were collected within four hours from the end of surgery. Postoperative laboratory values collected were maximum WCC, minimum Hb, minimum platelet count, maximum lactate, and minimum PaO2/FiO2 (P/F) ratio. Data for vasopressor and inotropic support was also recorded.

Statistical Analysis

Missing data for candidate predictors was minimal (<5%), so a complete‐case analysis was performed. All available data was used. Consideration was given to model complexity based on the number of events per variable (EPV), in an effort to balance parsimony with predictive performance [14]. For relevant analyses, a p‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using Stata (18.5. StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX).

Baseline characteristics were compared between outcome groups using Student's t‐test, Welch's t‐test or Mann‐Whitney U test (continuous variables, depending on normality and equality of variance) and chi‐square or Fisher's exact test (categorical variables). Continuous variables were reported as mean (SD) or median (IQR) and categorical variables as count (%).

Model development and validation were undertaken independently at two timepoints: immediately prior to surgery (preoperative model using data only available preoperatively) and at four hours postoperatively (early postoperative model using all available perioperative data).

Modern variable selection techniques, like least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, are less prone to forming mis‐calibrated models compared to more traditional methods [10]. As such, LASSO was used for variable selection with the optimal penalty parameter (λ) selected via 10‐fold‐cross‐validation, wherein the λ value that minimized cross‐validation prediction error, was selected [15, 16]. For multi‐level categorical variables, LASSO was applied to individual indicators. When multiple indicators were selected, the complete variable was retained; when only one indicator was selected, it was included as a binary predictor. The variables with non‐zero coefficients were considered for stability assessment.

Variable selection stability analysis can further improve estimation of model structure [17]. The LASSO selection procedure was repeated over 500 bootstrap resamples drawn with replacement from the original sample, with variables in each iteration recorded and their total respective inclusion (bootstrap inclusion frequency) calculated. Variables were retained if selected in ≥80% of bootstrap iterations (AKI models) or ≥85% (RRT models), thresholds within the recommended range where stability performance is relatively insensitive to specific values and model parsimony is achieved [17, 18]. The higher threshold for RRT accounted for lower event rates with the aim of achieving adequate EPVs. Stable variables were entered into standard multivariable logistic regression models to obtain final coefficients and odds ratios. Interaction terms were not investigated due to limited outcome events and our focus on developing parsimonious and clinically implementable models.

Internal validation via bootstrap resampling was used to estimate optimism in model performance, using 500 bootstrap resamples [19]. Within each bootstrap sample, the models were refitted and their performance evaluated in the bootstrap sample (apparent performance) and the original dataset (test performance) [19]. The difference between the apparent and test performance quantified optimism, with mean optimism calculated across bootstrap iterations. Optimism‐adjusted performance metrics, calculated by subtracting mean optimism from original apparent performance, are thought to provide more stable and generalizable estimates of model performance [19, 20].

Multiple performance metrics were evaluated to provide a comprehensive overview of model performance both in development and internal validation [20]. Discrimination was assessed via the C‐statistic, calibration was analyzed using the calibration slope, and the overall performance of the model was determined via the Brier score [20]. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed using optimism‐adjusted predictions to evaluate the potential clinical utility of the model across a range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities, with net benefit compared with treat‐all and treat‐none strategies [21].

Results

Cohort Description

A total of 721 patients underwent LTx at our institution within our specified study period. 125 patients were unable to be linked to some sources of data in electronic medical records, leaving 596 patients assessed for eligibility. Seven patients met the exclusion criteria and were subsequently removed. Of the 589 eligible patients, a total of 534 complete cases were left for analysis, with 216 (40.4%) experiencing any‐stage AKI within seven days postoperatively and 47 (8.8%) requiring RRT postoperatively. The cohort used for analyses involving postoperative data was composed of 376 patients, with 149 (39.6%) and 34 (9.0%) developing AKI and requiring RRT respectively. Prediction of AKI

Model characteristics for the prediction of AKI are seen in Table 2. The variable selection stability analysis identified five stable predictors of AKI preoperatively, with BMI and CKD being the most consistently selected variables (≥98% bootstrap inclusion). Four of the variables (BMI, CKD, sex, and waitlist duration) retained in the preoperative model also appeared in the early postoperative model. In addition to these consistent variables, the early postoperative model also included procedure duration, a primary transplant indication of CLAD, and three metrics collected within four hours postoperatively: the P/F ratio, platelet count, and cumulative noradrenaline dosage, comprising a nine‐variable model. The preoperative model demonstrated an apparent C‐statistic of 0.644, which corrected to 0.632 following optimism adjustment in bootstrap internal validation. In a similar trend, the early postoperative model's C‐statistic reduced from 0.761 to 0.741 with optimism correction. Figure 1 displays the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for each model. Prediction of RRT Requirement

Model characteristics for RRT prediction models are seen in Table S4. A primary indication of CLAD and pulmonary hypertension were identified as important predictors in variable selection stability analysis. Given the selection of multiple indicators, the complete categorical variable was retained. The early postoperative model recognized key physiological variables such as peak lactate and early postoperative P/F ratio to be the most consistently important predictors. The bootstrap inclusion percentages in variable selection stability analyses are seen in Figures S6 and S7.

Table 3 shows that evaluation of performance for RRT models showed a similar trend to AKI models’ performance. The validated C‐statistics were 0.720 and 0.847 for preoperative and early postoperative models respectively. Decision curve analysis displayed modest net clinical benefit over treat‐all and treat‐none strategies, with more pronounced benefit seen with the early postoperative model. The early postoperative model demonstrated a net benefit of 0.04 over a treat‐all strategy at a threshold of 0.10, representing a similar, albeit more modest, net improvement in correctly classifying patients at high risk of requiring RRT without increasing unnecessary interventions. Figures S8 and S9 display relatively less tightly clustered bootstrap validation performance compared to AKI models.

Discussion

Renal injury following LTx remains a common and detrimental postoperative complication, with significant implications for patient morbidity and mortality, as well as healthcare resource utilization. Previous work aiming to identify high‐risk patients for AKI after LTx has not comprehensively investigated clinically important factors across the entire perioperative course, potentially limiting their clinical utility and ability to characterize renal injury risk in this cohort. We developed and internally validated preoperative and early postoperative prediction models for both AKI and RRT requirement, capturing key predictors of renal injury, that may help identify high‐risk patients and allow for meaningful intervention. The parsimonious prediction models performed favorably in a comprehensive internally validated evaluation, with early postoperative models demonstrating superior performance over preoperative models. Within these models, important predictors of renal injury after LTx were identified, reinforcing prior findings whilst highlighting several novel associations that present targets for further research.

The models for AKI and RRT requirement achieved fair and good discrimination respectively, demonstrating similar or superior performance to prior models [7, 8]. Our early postoperative AKI model demonstrated the utility of intraoperative and early postoperative data as evidenced by the superior discrimination achieved when compared to both our own preoperative model (C‐statistic 0.741 vs. 0.632) and prior work incorporating genomics and preoperative features alone (C‐statistic 0.741 vs. 0.6988). This was similarly seen in RRT prediction where the inclusion of clinical data across the perioperative course within our early postoperative model conferred additional discriminative capacity compared with previous work (C‐statistic 0.847 vs. 0.7107). Internal validation revealed modest optimism across all four models, with calibration slopes ranging from 0.889–0.949, indicating modest overfitting and adequate model stability.

Prediction of RRT requirement demonstrated superior discrimination to AKI models, whilst being more parsimonious. The need for RRT reflects the most severe end of the spectrum of renal injury and hence represents a more homogenous and rarer outcome in comparison to any‐stage AKI, likely meaning that there was sufficient predictive signal in fewer variables.

Our AKI models identified several established risk factors for postoperative renal injury, including higher BMI, CKD, male sex, and waitlist duration [2, 8, 22, 23]; notably, higher BMI presents a modifiable preoperative risk factor, suggesting a potential target for optimization prior to transplantation. Contrasting previous findings [23], shorter waitlist duration was associated with increased AKI risk, potentially reflecting higher recipient acuity in those transplanted more urgently. In addition to these established predictors, our early postoperative model identified novel predictors: a primary transplant indication of CLAD, the P/F ratio within four hours postoperatively, and the cumulative noradrenaline dosage within four hours postoperatively. Chronic lung allograft dysfunction only emerged as a key predictor in the early postoperative model, suggesting that its preoperative predictive signal is largely captured by other predictors but that postoperatively it adds unique information beyond other variables. It is possible that a combination of factors inherent to the CLAD patient cohort, such as retransplantation, increased surgical complexity owing to adhesions or altered anatomy, and prolonged exposure to nephrotoxic medications such as calcineurin inhibitors preoperatively [24, 25], culminate in heightened vulnerability to postoperative renal dysfunction. The predictive value of the P/F ratio postoperatively supports established links between impaired oxygenation, mechanical ventilation duration, and AKI following LTx [2, 26]. Higher cumulative noradrenaline dosage reflects hemodynamic instability and a propensity towards multiorgan dysfunction, but may also suggest direct renal injury via α‐adrenergic‐mediated renal vasoconstriction, reducing glomerular filtration rate despite preserving perfusion pressures [27]. Of interest, evidence from cardiac surgery populations suggests that noradrenaline may confer greater renal injury risk compared to alternative vasopressors (such as vasopressin) in the hemodynamically compromised patient [28], warranting further investigation in the LTx setting.

Similar to previous studies, we identified primary transplant indication to be predictive of RRT requirement [7]. The stricter variable selection threshold used to develop our RRT models, in an effort to reduce overfitting risk, led to the exclusion of some clinical variables and explains some of the divergence in model structure when compared to the model by Grimm et al. [7]. The early postoperative model identified important physiological markers as key determinants of RRT requirement risk. Peak intraoperative and early postoperative lactate have been associated with RRT requirement in cardiac surgery populations [29, 30], reflecting cumulative physiological insult and hypoperfusion across the perioperative period, a mechanism likely to extend to LTx patients. When considered alongside impaired oxygenation early in the postoperative period, reflected by a lower P/F ratio, these variables capture key determinants of severe renal injury, whereby sustained hypoperfusion and hypoxemia converge to precipitate renal failure requiring support.

Performance evaluation and DCAs demonstrated that the early postoperative models conferred substantially greater net clinical benefit compared with both the preoperative models and a treat‐all strategy, underscoring the value of incorporating intraoperative and early postoperative data into risk stratification. Importantly, the models are distinct, independent, and non‐dynamic. The preoperative models aimed to identify high‐risk recipients who may benefit from heightened perioperative vigilance, optimization or early resource allocation. The postoperative models provide separate, updated risk estimates in the early postoperative period once intraoperative and early recovery data become available, allowing for more refined risk stratification. The superiority of the early postoperative models is clinically meaningful, as it captures a period during which renal injury pathways may still be evolving and remain potentially amenable to intervention, whilst maximizing prediction performance. For example, a protocol such as that outlined in the KDIGO cardiac care bundle which, having shown modest nephroprotective benefit in cardiac surgery patients [31], could extend to the similar surgical cohort of LTx patients. Beyond generic nephroprotective strategies, these findings raise mechanistic questions worthy of further investigation, including why CLAD patients carry elevated renal risk, how P/F ratio relates to renal injury and can therefore possibly be optimized, and whether alternative postoperative vasopressor regimes might attenuate renal dysfunction.

Our single‐center cohort and relatively modest number of outcome events, particularly for RRT requirement, may limit the generalizability of our findings. Though our methodology aimed to mitigate overfitting and ensure model robustness, external validation in independent cohorts is a necessary step. Omission of urine output criteria within our AKI definition may underestimate early postoperative AKI; however, urine output measurements were infrequently recorded and are also affected by perioperative diuresis. Initiation of RRT represents a treatment decision influenced by clinician judgement, which may introduce variability not fully captured in clinical data alone. Postoperative models were developed on a smaller cohort compared to preoperative models due to temporal restrictions in electronic recording of specific variables, potentially reducing statistical power. However, identical outcome rates were observed between cohorts and bootstrap validation demonstrated stable model performance, suggesting no selection bias. Unmeasured variables such as fluid balance, blood loss volumes, and comprehensive medication exposures, including quantitative calcineurin inhibitor exposure data, may contribute to renal injury risk but were not available for analysis, potentially leading to missed predictive relationships. Donor‐related variables such as donor age, type, ischemic time and other graft quality indicators were not available for analysis; however, as our models were designed to provide recipient‐focused risk assessment, this was consistent with the intended study aim. Given recent findings associating donor factors with renal dysfunction after LTx, further investigation of specific donor variables and how they relate to improving prediction of post‐transplant AKI may offer some merit. Whilst model performance and clinical benefit evaluation demonstrated favorable results, the absence of external validation in independent cohorts and sample size limitations reduce the generalizability of these findings, with prospective validation and exploration of additional candidate predictors required before clinical implementation can be considered.

We developed and internally validated perioperative prediction models for AKI and RRT requirement following LTx. Models displayed timepoint‐dependent improvements in discrimination, stable calibration, and favorable net clinical benefit in decision curve analysis, especially for the early postoperative models. These findings underscore the importance of timepoint‐specific risk assessment and suggest that the determinants of renal injury risk are context‐specific, differing both across perioperative stages and between AKI and RRT requirement as distinct outcomes. Identification of patients at high risk of postoperative renal dysfunction, through preoperative and early postoperative risk stratification, may facilitate timely implementation of nephroprotective strategies (such as hemodynamic optimization and targeted care bundles), as well as appropriate resource allocation, prior to the onset of clinically significant renal injury. Notable predictors of AKI, including early postoperative noradrenaline dosage and P/F ratio, as well as a primary transplant indication of CLAD, warrant further investigation. These models provide a foundation for external validation, mechanistic inquiry, and targeted interventional research aimed at improving risk prediction and ultimately reducing the burden of renal injury in this vulnerable population.
