Simultaneous power and data transfer over common bus using talkative bidirectional flyback converters for battery equalization
Authors: Jiting Wang, Bingkun Zeng, Lingyu Li, Jiande Wu, Yan Deng, Xiangning He, B. T. Maharaj, Ramesh C. Bansal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cell inconsistency is an inherent challenge for lithium-ion batteries during long-term operation, necessitating the use of battery equalization systems (BESs). In such systems, real-time data communication is essential for ensuring accurate system coordination and effective equalization performance. This paper proposes a distributed BES based on the talkative power converter (TPC) technique, employing a bidirectional flyback converter as the core equalization unit. In the proposed approach, the converter not only transfers energy among distributed battery modules but also modulates data signals for communication. This dual-function design eliminates the need for additional communication hardware or dedicated signal transmission circuits, thereby reducing the overall system complexity. On this basis, a distributed equalization system and its communication channel model are developed. The system noise sources are then analyzed and a noise suppression strategy is proposed to improve the communication reliability. Furthermore, a practical communication protocol tailored to the distributed architecture is designed. Finally, a hardware prototype is constructed and tested, achieving real-time data communication at a rate of 8.33 kb/s. The results verify the feasibility of using switching-ripple-based power and signal dual modulation for communication within distributed BESs.

Introduction

With the widespread adoption of electric vehicles and renewable energy, energy storage technologies have attracted significant attention from both academia and industry1. Owing to advantages such as high energy density, low self-discharge rate, and favorable temperature characteristics, lithium-ion batteries are extensively used in the energy storage systems2,3. However, the long-term operation of a lithium-ion battery pack inevitably leads to inconsistencies in the State of Charge (SOC) among individual cells4, preventing the complete utilization of the stored charge and causing potential safety hazards5.

To address these issues, battery packs are typically equipped with a battery equalization system (BES). A battery pack can be organized into multiple cells or several modules, each consisting of a number of cells. By balancing the SOC of the individual cells or modules, the BES effectively prevents overcharging and over-discharging, thereby extending the overall lifespan of the battery pack6–8. According to the system architecture, BESs can generally be classified into centralized and distributed structures. In distributed architectures, each cell or module is equipped with a dedicated equalizer board, and reliable real-time communication is required to exchange SOC information among different cells or modules. Therefore, the design of a safe, stable, and cost-effective communication scheme has become a critical challenge for distributed BESs9.

Communication schemes in distributed BESs can generally be classified into independent-channel and shared-channel schemes, depending on whether power and data are transmitted over the same physical path. Wired communication is widely adopted in BESs, and architectures based on the Controller Area Network (CAN) bus have been extensively investigated and successfully implemented10,11. Although the daisy-chain topology features a simpler circuit structure compared with CAN-based solutions12, both approaches require dedicated communication modules in addition to the power equalization hardware.

As the number of cells or modules increases, the system inevitably demands a large number of communication modules and interconnecting cables. The resulting wiring complexity not only increases manufacturing cost but also degrades system reliability due to the increased risk of connection failures.

In contrast to independent-channel schemes, shared-channel schemes feature lower hardware complexity, simplifying the system and reducing costs. Power Line Communication (PLC) was applied in BESs13–16. For instance, Reference17 established a small-scale PLC network for cylindrical batteries. However, traditional PLC schemes require additional signal modulation circuits, and the coupling between power and signals can lead to mutual interference, degrading the communication quality.

Power electronic converters inherently possess the capability to transmit data without requiring additional hardware or structural modifications. The technique of embedding information into the power waveform generated by a switched-mode converter is known as talkative power conversion (TPC), which is commonly realized through power and signal dual modulation (PSDM). By integrating power regulation and data transmission within a single modulation framework, PSDM eliminates the need for dedicated communication hardware or separate signal paths, thereby significantly reducing system complexity. The feasibility and effectiveness of this approach have been demonstrated in a wide range of applications18–20.

Recent studies have further investigated the application of TPC in BES. Reference21 applied TPC to the equalization circuits of series-connected battery strings, enabling simultaneous power and data transfer. Building on this work22, further refined both the equalization and communication strategies, achieving improvements in communication reliability and equalization speed. However, these schemes are primarily designed for cell-level equalization using a daisy-chain topology and are not suitable for module-level equalization.

Communication of battery equalization system

Power and information flow in battery equalization system

In the proposed BES architecture shown in Fig. 1, the bus functions simultaneously as a power transmission medium and a communication channel. A bidirectional power supply establishes the DC bus and enables energy input, output, and temporary storage within the BES. During power transfer, energy is discharged from high-SOC battery modules through BEUs to the bidirectional power source. When necessary, the stored energy is redistributed via the common bus and BEUs to battery modules with lower SOC, thereby achieving energy balancing across the battery modules.

By employing the PSDM technique, information is embedded into the switching ripple generated by the transmitting BEU and injected into the common bus concurrently with power transfer. The underlying principle of PSDM lies in the multiple degrees of freedom inherent in a PWM waveform, namely the duty cycle, switching frequency, and phase. In this scheme, the duty cycle is exclusively allocated for power regulation, while the switching frequency and phase are modulated to convey information, enabling simultaneous power and data transmission over the same bus.

Figure 2 illustrates the information flow within the BES. At the transmitter, the binary data is used to modulate the triangular carrier wave of the PWM generator. This modulation alters the frequency and phase of the gate drive signals, which subsequently induces corresponding variations in the converter’s input voltage or current ripple. Consequently, digital information is embedded directly into the power bus. The receiving BEUs filter the bus voltage to extract the ripple component at the designated communication frequency. This signal is then processed by specific demodulation algorithms to recover the transmitted data.

Data modulation in bidirectional flyback converter

The principle of data modulation and transmission using the BEU is detailed in Fig. 5. The transmitter performs digital modulation on the standard PWM power carrier. By altering the carrier’s frequency and phase according to the transmission data, a composite power-signal carrier is generated. This composite carrier is compared with the output of the PI controller to generate the gate drive signals. Consequently, the digital information is embedded into the frequency and phase of the switching ripple at the converter’s input and output ports, achieving simultaneous power conversion and data transmission.

Conventional digital modulation schemes include Amplitude Shift Keying (ASK), Frequency Shift Keying (FSK), Phase Shift Keying (PSK), and Differential Phase Shift Keying (DPSK). DPSK encodes data using the phase difference between two consecutive carrier groups. However, in the distributed BMS where multiple BEUs share the common DC bus, the standard DPSK scheme maintains the same switching frequency for transmitters. Consequently, the switching ripple from non-transmitting BEUs causes significant interference to the demodulation process. To mitigate this, a hybrid Frequency Hopping-Differential Phase Shift Keying (FH-DPSK) strategy is adopted. This strategy simultaneously uses frequency and phase to modulate the power carrier, ensuring robust communication in the multi-converter environment.

Fig. 6FH-DPSK modulation process for the power carrier. (Taking N = 4 as an example)

FH-DPSK modulation process for the power carrier. (Taking N = 4 as an example)

Data is transmitted in symbols, with each symbol consisting of N switching cycles. FH-DPSK demodulation is typically performed using a sliding window technique. In binary FH-DPSK, the first group of switching cycles serves as a reference phase. Subsequent data is encoded in the phase difference Δφ between consecutive carrier groups. A phase difference Δφ = 0 represents data “0”, while Δφ = π corresponds to data “1”. This differential encoding enables non-coherent demodulation, eliminating the need for carrier phase synchronization at the receiver.

As shown in Fig. 6, the FH-DPSK scheme uses both frequency and phase to achieve data transmission. This scheme preserves the duty cycle of the drive signals, thus maintaining the steady-state output of the BEU. However, variations in the frequency and phase of the drive signals affect the charging and discharging processes of inductors and capacitors in the circuit, introducing output transient disturbances.

Theoretical analysis indicates that disturbances induced by frequency shifting are generally smaller than the nominal output voltage ripple and can be neglected. In contrast, direct phase shifting introduces significant voltage spikes, often exceeding the switching ripple magnitude. In the improved FH-DPSK process, phase switching is achieved indirectly through the introduction of transitional carriers, resulting in the entire communication process only changing the power carrier frequency. By altering the frequency of several power carriers, indirect modification of the power carrier phase is accomplished without requiring abrupt phase jumps, as illustrated in Fig. 7. Fig. 11Demodulation flow of FH-DPSK. (taking N = 10 as an example)

Demodulation flow of FH-DPSK. (taking N = 10 as an example)

The receiver samples the filtered signal value s(th) at the current time instant th. The sliding demodulation window is defined over the interval [th−Tg, th]. By applying the demodulation algorithm to the filtered signal s(t) within this window, the real-time amplitude and phase of s(t) at the communication frequency f1 can be extracted. The specific data demodulation sequence is divided into the following four operational stages.

Stage I (Idle and Detection): When no BEU is transmitting, the amplitude A1 of the signal at frequency f1 remains below a preset threshold Am. Consequently, the receiver stays in the idle state. Once any BEU starts transmission, the amplitude A1 continuously increases until time t1, when it reaches the threshold Am. The receiver identifies a valid transmission start event.

Stage II (Reference Phase Extraction): Following detection, the demodulation window aligns to the interval [t2 ~ t2+Tg] as shown in Fig. 11. The algorithm calculates the amplitude and phase of the signal at f1. This initial phase value is stored as the reference phase for the subsequent differential decoding.

Stage III (Data Decoding): The demodulation window slides forward by a step length equal to the symbol duration TB. The algorithm processes the window [t2 + TB, t2 + TB+Tg] to extract the new phase. The receiver calculates the phase difference Δφ relative to the previous window. A Δφ approaching 0 is decoded as data “0”, while a Δφ approaching π is decoded as data “1”. This process repeats for every TB shift to recover the continuous data stream.

Stage IV (Termination): After the transmitter ends transmission, at time t4, the amplitude A1 falls below Am. At this point, the receiver concludes that the transmission has ended and returns to Stage I.

The system noise primarily includes the fundamental switching ripple and its higher-order harmonics, high-frequency switching transients, low-frequency control jitter, and background white noise. Since the communication frequency employed in this work is 83.3 kHz, radiated noise in the BES can be neglected, and only conducted noise is considered.

The switching ripple generated by non-transmitting BEUs constitutes the dominant noise source, classified as power noise. The noise consists of a fundamental component at frequency f0 and its harmonics at kf0. As derived in Sect. 2.3, by selecting the demodulation window length Tg that satisfies the orthogonality condition, the impact of the component at f0 and kf0 can be eliminated.

Noise induced by switching transients refers to oscillations caused by parasitic parameters during switching transitions. The frequency of this noise fds is significantly higher than the communication frequency and can be effectively suppressed by a low-pass filter. Steady-state jitter stems from limited controller resolution or limit cycles in digital control loop. For converters employing PI/PID control, the frequency of such noise is typically below half the switching frequency. The cutoff frequencies of filters should satisfy:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{{{f_1}}}{2}{\mathrm{<}}{f_{{\mathrm{HPF}}}}{\mathrm{<}}{f_1}{\mathrm{<}}{f_{{\mathrm{LPF}}}}{\mathrm{<}}{f_{{\mathrm{ds}}}}$$\end{document}

where fHPF represents the cutoff frequency of high-pass filter and fLPF represents that of low-pass filter. The system’s background noise is modeled as Additive White Gaussian Noise from the thermal noise of resistive elements and semiconductor devices. The amplitude probability of this noise follows a Gaussian distribution. The power spectral density Pn(f) (W/Hz) of Gaussian white noise is:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathrm{P}}_{\mathrm{n}}}(f)={n_0}(0<f<+\infty )$$\end{document}

where n0 is a constant. The white noise passes through the receiver’s filtering stage, which has an ideal low-pass gain ALPF and a high-pass gain AHPF. In summary, by selecting the suitable demodulation window length Tg, the interference from switching ripple at f0 and its higher-order harmonics can be eliminated. Furthermore, high-frequency parasitic ringing, low-frequency control jitter, and out-of-band white noise can be substantially suppressed by the hardware band-pass filters.

Conclusion

This paper presents a communication-integrated BES leveraging TPC technology. By implementing bidirectional flyback converters as TPCs, the proposed architecture enables simultaneous power transfer and data transmission without the need for dedicated communication cabling or separate circuitry. This integration substantially reduces hardware complexity and manufacturing costs, while preserving electrical isolation between battery modules. Building on these foundations, this paper introduces several innovations.

Firstly, to ensure robust communication in a distributed environment, an FH-DPSK modulation scheme is employed. This approach provides strong immunity to switching ripple interference from parallel, non-communicating converters and enables non-coherent demodulation, eliminating the need for precise phase synchronization at the receiver.

Secondly, a comprehensive channel model was also developed to characterize noise behavior and guide effective interference suppression strategies. In addition, a communication protocol was designed to coordinate orderly data exchange among the BEUs.

Finally, a distributed BES prototype comprising three battery modules was constructed to validate the proposed strategy. Experimental results demonstrate real-time data transmission at a bit rate of 8.33 kb/s and an equalization power of 10 W per unit. While the achieved transmission rate is well-suited for monitoring slow-varying SOC during the equalization process, it remains a limitation for more demanding high-speed diagnostic tasks, representing a clear objective for future performance enhancements. The successful balancing of SOC among the battery modules confirms the effectiveness and practicality of the proposed approach for next-generation battery management systems.
