Sharp bounds and geometric properties of the first non trivial Steklov Neumann Eigenvalue
Sagar Basak, Gloria Paoli, Rossano Sannipoli, Sheela Verma

Abstract
. In this article, we study the mixed Steklov–Neumann eigenvalue problem on doubly connected domains. First, we show that among all doubly connected domains in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} of the form B R 2 ∖ B R 1 ¯ B_{R_{2}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}} , where B R 1 B_{R_{1}} and B R 2 B_{R_{2}} are open balls of fixed radii satisfying B R 1 ¯ ⊂ B R 2 \overline{B_{R_{1}}}\subset B_{R_{2}} , the first non-zero Steklov–Neumann eigenvalue attains its maximal value when the balls are concentric. Next, we establish bounds for the first non-zero Steklov–Neumann eigenvalue on a doubly connected star-shaped domain contained in a hypersurface equipped with a revolution-type metric. We also derive the asymptotic behavior of the first non-zero Steklov–Neumann eigenvalue on a bounded domain with a spherical hole in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} as the radius of the hole approaches zero. Finally, we study the number of nodal domains of the eigenfunction corresponding to the first non zero Steklov–Neumann eigenvalue on a bounded domain in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} having a spherical hole. MSC 2020 : 35B40, 35J25, 35P15. Keywords : Laplacian eigenvalue, Steklov–Neumann boundary conditions, Isoperimetric inequalities, Star shaped domains.

1.
Introduction

Let
(M,g)
be a Riemannian manifold of
dimension
n
, and let
\Omega\subset M
be a bounded doubly connected
domain with Lipschitz boundary

\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}
, where
\Gamma_{1}
and

\Gamma_{2}
are disjoint. We consider the following Steklov-Neumann eigenvalue problem for the Laplacian

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\Delta u=0&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{1},\\
\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}=\mu u&\text{on }\Gamma_{2},\end{cases}

where
\nu
denotes the unit outward normal to the boundary
\partial\Omega
.

The weak formulation of (
1.1
) is given by

(1.2)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\nabla v\,dV=\mu\int_{\Gamma_{2}}uv\,dS,\quad\text{for all }v\in H^{1}(\Omega).

An equivalent spectral interpretation can be formulated in terms of the
Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator on
\Gamma_{2}
. For
F\in L^{2}(\Gamma_{2})
,
let
\widetilde{F}
denote its harmonic extension to
\Omega
satisfying
the Neumann condition on
\Gamma_{1}
. The Dirichlet-to-Neumann map, that is

D:L^{2}(\Gamma_{2})\to L^{2}(\Gamma_{2}),\quad F\mapsto\frac{\partial\widetilde{F}}{\partial\nu},

is compact, positive, and self-adjoint
(see
[
7
, Chapter III]
). Consequently, its
spectrum is discrete and can be arranged as

0=\mu_{0}(\Omega)<\mu_{1}(\Omega)\leq\mu_{2}(\Omega)\leq\cdots\nearrow\infty.

The eigenfunction corresponding to
\mu_{0}(\Omega)
is constant. The first
non-zero eigenvalue
\mu_{1}(\Omega)
admits the following variational characterization

(1.3)

\mu_{1}(\Omega)=\inf\left\{\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dV}{\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{2}}u^{2}\,dS}:\;u\in H^{1}(\Omega),\quad\int_{\Gamma_{2}}u\,dS=0\right\}.

1.1.
State of the Art

Spectral problems on doubly connected domains have been widely studied under
various boundary conditions. One of the earliest contributions is due to Pólya
[
29
]
, who derived upper bounds for the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of planar ring-shaped domains. In

[
23
]
it is proved that, among annular domains in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

obtained by translating the inner ball, the first Dirichlet eigenvalue is maximized when the balls are concentric. This optimality property was later
extended to space forms in
[
2
]
. For mixed Dirichlet–Neumann boundary conditions, Payne and Weinberger

[
28
]
established an analogous extremal result. The authors showed
that the concentric annulus maximizes the first Dirichlet–Neumann eigenvalue among multiply connected planar domains with prescribed geometric
constraints.

Mixed Steklov-type problems have also attracted considerable attention.
In
[
31
]
, the authors proved that concentric
annuli maximize the first eigenvalue of the Steklov–Dirichlet problem among
domains of fixed volume. An alternative proof and further extensions,
including the classical Steklov problem, were later provided
in
[
14
]
. Bounds and isoperimetric inequalities for higher
Steklov–Dirichlet eigenvalues on perforated domains were recently obtained
in
[
8
,
30
]
. Eigenvalue estimates for doubly connected star-shaped domains have been
studied in various geometric settings. In
[
15
]
,
upper bounds for the first Steklov–Dirichlet eigenvalue were derived,
showing that the corresponding concentric annulus provides an extremal
configuration. These results were later extended to non-Euclidean space
forms in
[
8
]
. Using related techniques,
lower bounds for the first Steklov–Robin eigenvalue were established
in
[
17
]
. The asymptotic behavior of Steklov-type eigenvalues on perforated domains has
also been investigated: in
[
30
]
, it was shown that,
as the radius of a spherical hole tends to zero, the first
Steklov–Dirichlet eigenvalue converges to zero, while the second eigenvalue
converges to the first non-trivial Steklov eigenvalue of the limiting
domain.
Other reference for mixed
Steklov–Dirichlet problems are the following

[
16
,
21
,
26
,
31
]
.

For eigenfunctions corresponding to the Steklov eigenvalues on a bounded simply connected domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the authors in
[
24
]
studied the structure of their nodal sets. The authors proved that the nodal set of an eigenfunction associated with the
k
-th Steklov eigenvalue on a bounded simply connected domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
divides
\Omega
into at most
k
subdomains, and moreover, that no nodal line forms a closed curve.
In
[
1
]
, the authors determined the number of nodal domains of an eigenfunction corresponding to the
k
-th Steklov eigenvalue on a simply connected bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. More recently, in
[
30
]
, the author generalized this result to the mixed Steklov–Dirichlet eigenvalue problem on domains with a hole in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Other types of mixed boundary conditions have been investigated in recent years: for instance, mixed Dirichlet–Neumann
problem
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
, and the mixed
Robin–Neumann problem

[
12
,
13
,
27
]
. These studies highlight the delicate dependence of eigenvalues on the geometry of the
domain and reveal the presence of extremal configurations analogous to those
of classical Dirichlet or Steklov problems.

The mixed Steklov–Neumann has been only partially studied in

[
6
,
9
,
20
,
22
]
and still presents several fundamental open questions. In particular, the ones concerning optimal shapes, sharp bounds, and asymptotic behavior. The aim of the present paper is to address these problems.

1.2.
Main Results

We now describe the main contributions of this work. Our first result concerns
a shape optimization problem for particular doubly connected domains in the Euclidean setting.
More precisely, we determine the configuration that maximizes the first
non-zero Steklov–Neumann eigenvalue among eccentric annuli with prescribed
radii. This provides the Steklov–Neumann counterpart of classical extremal
results known for Dirichlet, Dirichlet–Neumann, and Steklov–Dirichlet
problems. To state the first results, we need to introduce the following notation. Let
R>0
, we define
B_{R}(d)=y_{d}+B_{R}
, where
y_{d}=(0,0,\dots,d)
, the ball centered at the point
y_{d}
of radius
R>0
. If
d=0
, we will denote the ball by
B_{R}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
R_{2}>R_{1}>0
and let
\Omega_{d}=B_{R_{2}}(d)\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}},

where
B_{R_{1}}
and
B_{R_{2}}(d)
are such that
\overline{B_{R_{1}}}\subset B_{R_{2}}(d)
. Then,

(1.4)

\mu_{1}(\Omega_{d})\leq\mu_{1}(\Omega_{0}).

Theorem
1.1
shows that the concentric annulus maximizes the first non-trivial Steklov-Neumann eigenvalue in the class of all eccentric annuli. Despite the different spectral
nature of the problem, the extremal configuration coincides with that
observed for other boundary conditions, showing that the symmetry plays a decisive role
also in the Steklov–Neumann framework.

Our second result addresses eigenvalue estimates on more general geometric
settings. Motivated by recent advances on star-shaped domains for mixed
Steklov-type problems, see
[
9
]
, we derive explicit lower and upper bounds for the
first Steklov–Neumann eigenvalue on doubly connected star-shaped domains
embedded in hypersurfaces in the Riemannian manifold
M
, endowed with metrics of revolution.
Let
p\in M
and
\tilde{\Omega}_{\mathrm{out}}\subset M
be a star-shaped domain with respect to
p
. Let

B_{R_{1}}
denote the geodesic ball in
M
of radius
R_{1}
centered at
p
, such that
B_{R_{1}}\subset\tilde{\Omega}_{\mathrm{out}}
, and let us
define
\tilde{\Omega}=\tilde{\Omega}_{\mathrm{out}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}.

Let
B_{R_{M}}
be the smallest geodesic ball in
M
centered at
p
with radius
R_{M}
that contains

\tilde{\Omega}_{\mathrm{out}}
, and let
B_{R_{m}}
be the largest geodesic ball in
M
centered at
p
with radius

R_{m}
that is contained in
\tilde{\Omega}_{\mathrm{out}}
. With this setup, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
M
be a hypersurface of revolution with metric
g
and let
\tilde{\Omega}
,
B_{R_{1}}
,
B_{R_{M}}
, and
B_{R_{m}}
be defined as above. Then there exist two explicit and postive constants
C_{i}=C_{i}(g,R_{m},R_{M})
,
i=1,2
, such that

\displaystyle C_{1}\;\mu_{1}\!\left(B_{R_{m}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}\right)\;\leq\;\mu_{1}(\tilde{\Omega})\;\leq\;C_{2}\;\mu_{1}\!\left(B_{R_{M}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}\right).

These bounds reveal that the geometry of star-shaped domains can be
quantitatively controlled through suitable comparison with concentric
annuli. In particular, they extend known inequalities for
Steklov–Dirichlet and Steklov–Robin problems to the
Steklov–Neumann setting.

Our third result regards the asymptotic behavior of the spectrum when the
inner hole shrinks. Understanding this limit is crucial both from the
analytic and geometric viewpoint, as it clarifies the relation between the
Steklov–Neumann problem on perforated domains and the classical Steklov
problem on simply connected domains.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded, connected domain with Lipschitz boundary, and let
B_{r}
denote the ball of radius
r>0
centered at the origin such that
\overline{B_{r}}\subset\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}
.
Define the punctured domain

\Omega_{r}=\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}\setminus\overline{B_{r}}.

Then we have

\lim_{r\to 0}\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r})=\sigma_{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}),

where
\sigma_{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})
is the first non-trivial Steklov eigenvalue. Moreover, there exists a sequence of eigenfunctions
\{u_{1}^{r}\}_{r}
corresponding to
\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r})
and an eigenfunction
\overline{u_{1}}
corresponding to
\sigma_{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}),
such that
u_{1}^{r}
converges to
\overline{u_{1}}
in
H^{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}),
as
r\to 0.

The asymptotic convergence described in
Theorem
1.3
has significant geometric consequences.
In particular, it allows us to derive isoperimetric-type inequalities for
the first nonzero Steklov–Neumann eigenvalue on domains with sufficiently small
holes. More precisely, for
r
small enough, the eigenvalue

\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r})
can be controlled by the corresponding eigenvalue of a
concentric annulus satisfying either a measure or a perimeter constraint,
as established in Corollaries
5.1

and
5.2
.

These estimates should be compared with the classical inequalities
available for non-perforated domains. In the Steklov setting, in
[
10
]
a sharp upper bound is provided for the first non-trivial Steklov
eigenvalue under a volume constraint, while the Weinstock inequality
characterizes the ball as the maximizer under a perimeter constraint
(in dimension two for simply connected domains, see
[
32
]
, and in higher dimensions
within the class of convex sets,
[
11
]
). The results obtained here show that,
in the vanishing-hole regime, analogous comparison principles continue to
hold for the mixed Steklov–Neumann problem.

Finally, we investigate the number of nodal domains of the eigenfunctions corresponding to the first non-trivial Steklov-Neumann eigenvalue on a doubly connected domain. A nodal domain of a function is a connected component contained in
\Omega
where the function does not change sign (for the precise definition see Section
6
).

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded doubly connected domain with Lipschitz boundary. The nodal domains of the eigenfunctions corresponding to
\mu_{1}(\Omega)
are exactly
2
.

2.
Preliminary results

2.1.
Standard notations and some useful parametrizations in the Riemannian setting

Let
M
be a hypersurface of revolution with metric

g=dr^{2}+h^{2}(r)\,g_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}},

where
g_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}
denotes the standard metric on the unit sphere

\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, and
r\in[0,L]
for some
L>0
.
Further, we assume that the function
h:[0,L]\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
h(0)=0,h^{\prime}(0)=1
, and
h(r)
is an increasing function of
r.

Let us fix a point
p\in M
and consider
\tilde{\Omega}_{out}\subset M
a bounded, smooth, and star-shaped domain with respect to
p
. Let us introduce the following notation: we denote by
T_{p}M
the tangent space of
M
at the point
p\in M
and by
exp_{p}
the exponential map defined from
T_{p}M
to
M
.
Let
\|u\|:=\sqrt{\langle u,u\rangle}_{g}
. For each
p\in M
, define the unit sphere in the tangent space at
p
by

\displaystyle U_{p}M:=\{u\in T_{p}M:\|u\|=1\}.

Note that corresponding to each
u\in U_{p}M,
there exist a unique point
q\in\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{out}
such that
q=exp_{p}(r_{u}u)
for some
r_{u}>0.
Then the domain
\tilde{\Omega}_{out}
and its boundary
\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{out}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\tilde{\Omega}_{out}=\{exp_{p}(tu):u\in U_{p}M,0<t<r_{u}\},\quad\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{out}=\{exp_{p}(r_{u}u):u\in U_{p}M\}.

We denote by

(2.1)

\displaystyle R_{m}:=\min_{u\in U_{p}M}r_{u},\quad\displaystyle R_{M}:=\max_{u\in U_{p}M}r_{u}.

Let
\partial_{r}
denote the radial vector field starting from
p
, and let
\nu
be the unit outward normal to
\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{out}.
For any point
q\in\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{out}
, let
\theta(q)
denote the angle between
\nu(q)
and
\partial_{r}(q)
. Since
\tilde{\Omega}_{out}
is a bounded smooth star-shaped domain,

cos(\theta(q))=\langle\nu(q),\partial_{r}(q)\rangle>0,

that implies

\theta(q)<\frac{\pi}{2}
. By compactness of
\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{out},
there exist a constant
\alpha
such that
0\leq\theta(q)\leq\alpha<\frac{\pi}{2}
for all
q\in\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{out}.
Let
a=\tan^{2}\alpha
.
Thus, for all
q\in\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{out}
,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\|\overline{\nabla}R_{u}\|^{2}}{h^{2}(R_{u})}=\tan^{2}\!\big(\theta(q)\big)\;\leq\;\tan^{2}(\alpha)=a,

where
\overline{\nabla}R_{u}
denotes the component of
\nabla R_{u}
tangent to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. For more details, see Section 2 of
[
8
]
and the references therein.

2.2.
The radial case

Let
R>0
, we define
B_{R}(d)=y_{d}+B_{R}
, where
y_{d}=(0,0,\dots,d)
, the ball centered at the point
y_{d}
of radius
R>0
. If
d=0
, we will denote the ball by
B_{R}
. Let us fix
R_{2}>R_{1}>0
and for
d\in[0,R_{2}-R_{1})
, we denote the set
\Omega_{d}:=B_{R_{2}}(d)\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}
. Let us consider the following Steklov–Neumann eigenvalue problem on
\Omega_{d}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta u=0\quad\text{in}\,\,\,\Omega_{d}=B_{R_{2}}(d)\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}=0\quad\text{on}\,\,\,B_{R_{1}}\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}=\mu u\quad\text{on}\,\,\,B_{R_{2}}(d),\end{cases}

where
\nu
denote the outward unit normal vector to the boundary. For
d=0
, we have
\Omega_{0}=B_{R_{2}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}
. In this case,
\mu_{l}(\Omega_{0})
can be explicitly computed and it is given by (see
[
9
]
)

\displaystyle\mu_{l}(\Omega_{0})=\dfrac{l(l+n-2)\left(\left(\frac{R_{2}}{R_{1}}\right)^{2l+n-2}-1\right)}{R_{2}\left((l+n-2)\left(\frac{R_{2}}{R_{1}}\right)^{2l+n-2}+l\right)},\quad\quad l\geq 0.

Moreover, the corresponding eigenfunctions are of the form

w_{l}^{j}(t,\theta)=\bigg(r^{l}+\dfrac{lR_{1}^{2l+n-2}}{(l+n-2)r^{l+n-2}}\bigg)Y_{l}^{j}(\theta),

where
Y_{l}^{j}(\theta)
are the spherical harmonics, i.e. the eigenfunction of the Laplace Beltrami
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
l(l+n-2)
. The radial symmetry implies that
\mu_{l}(\Omega_{0})
has the same multiplicity of the eigenvalue
l(l+n-2)
. In particular, the multiplicity of the first non-zero Steklov-Neumann eigenvalue is
n,
and the corresponding eigenfunctions written in Cartesian coordinates are

u^{i}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})=\left(r+\frac{R_{1}^{n}}{(n-1)r^{n-1}}\right)\frac{x_{i}}{r},

where
i=1,2,\dots,n.

For a fixed
i\in\{1,2,\dots,n-1\},\,\,B_{R_{2}}(d)
is symmetric with respect to the hyperplane
\{x_{i}=0\}
, so

u_{n}^{i}(x_{1},\dots,-x_{i},\dots x_{n})=-u_{n}^{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{i},\dots x_{n}).

Using this condition we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R_{2}}(d)}u_{n}^{i}ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{R_{2}}(d)\cap\{x_{i}\geq 0\}}u_{n}^{i}ds\,\,+\int_{\partial B_{R_{2}}(d)\{x_{i}\leq 0\}}u_{n}^{i}ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{R_{2}}(d)\cap\{x_{i}\geq 0\}}u_{n}^{i}ds\,\,-\int_{\partial B_{R_{2}}(d)\{x_{i}\geq 0\}}u_{n}^{i}ds=0.

Therefore, for every
i\in\{1,2,\dots,n-1\}
, the eigenfunction
u_{n}^{i}
can be taken as a test function in the variational characterization of
\mu_{1}(\Omega_{d}).

2.3.
Some preliminary Lemmas

Let us recall the spherical coordinates in
n
dimensions, that are

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x_{1}=r\sin\theta_{1}\sin\theta_{2}\dots\sin\theta_{n-2}\sin\theta_{n-1},\\
x_{2}=r\sin\theta_{1}\sin\theta_{2}\dots\sin\theta_{n-2}\cos\theta_{n-1},\\
\quad\quad\vdots\\
x_{n-1}=r\sin\theta_{1}\cos\theta_{2},\\
x_{n}=r\cos\theta_{1},\end{cases}

where
(r,\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n-1})\in\mathbb{R^{+}}\times[0,\pi]\times,\dots,\times[0,\pi]\times[0,2\pi].

Let
P\in\partial B_{R_{2}}(d)
, and denote by
R_{d}(\theta_{1})
the distance between the origin
O
and the point
P
. Here,
\theta_{1}
represents the angle formed by the line segment
OP
with the
x_{1}
–axis. Then,

\displaystyle R_{d}(\theta_{1})=d\cos\theta_{1}+\sqrt{R_{2}^{2}-d^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{1}},

and

\displaystyle\sqrt{R_{d}(\theta_{1})^{2}+{R_{d}^{\prime 2}(\theta_{1})}}=R_{2}\left(1+\frac{d\cos\theta_{1}}{\sqrt{R_{2}^{2}-d^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{1}}}\right).

For more details, we refer the reader to
[
14
]
.

Since for every
i\in\{1,2,\dots,n-1\},
eigenfunction
u_{n}^{i}
can be used as a test function in the variational characterization of
\mu_{1}(\Omega_{d})
, we take
f=u_{n}^{n-1}
. Using spherical coordinates, we have

(2.3)

\displaystyle f(r,\theta)=\begin{cases}\sin\theta_{1}\left(r+\frac{R_{1}^{2}}{r}\right)\quad\text{for}\quad n=2,\\
\sin\theta_{1}\cos\theta_{2}\left(r+\frac{R_{1}^{2}}{r}\right)\quad\text{for}\quad n\geq 3.\end{cases}

Next, we recall the following lemmas, which will be used in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. A detailed proof of these two lemmas are provided in
[
14
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
n\geq 2
and
d\in[0,R_{2}-R_{1})
, define

\begin{cases}A_{1}(d)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{n-2}\theta_{1}\,\big(R_{d}^{n}(\theta_{1})-R_{1}^{n}\big)\,d\theta_{1},\\[8.0pt]
A_{2}(d)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\phi(\theta_{1})\ln\!\left(\frac{R_{d}(\theta_{1})}{R_{1}}\right)\,d\theta_{1},\quad\text{where}\quad\phi(\theta_{1})=-n\sin^{n}\theta_{1}+(n-1)\sin^{n-2}\theta_{1},\\[8.0pt]
A_{3}(d)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\psi(\theta_{1})\left(\frac{1}{R_{d}^{n}(\theta_{1})}-\frac{1}{R_{1}^{n}}\right)\,d\theta_{1},\,\,\text{where}\,\,\psi(\theta_{1})=(n-2)\sin^{n}\theta_{1}+(n-1)\sin^{n-2}\theta_{1}.\end{cases}

Then the following holds:

(1)

A_{1}(d)=A_{1}(0)
,

(2)

A_{2}(d)=0
,

(3)

A_{3}(d)\geq A_{3}(0)
, with equality if and only if
d=0
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For every
n\geq 2
and
d\in[0,R_{2}-R_{1})
, define

\displaystyle\begin{cases}V_{1}(d)&=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{n}\theta_{1}R_{d}^{n}(\theta_{1})\sqrt{R_{d}(\theta_{1})^{2}+{R_{d}^{\prime 2}(\theta_{1})}}\,\,d\theta_{1}\\
V_{2}(d)&=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{n}\theta_{1}\frac{d\cos\theta_{1}}{\sqrt{R_{2}^{2}-d^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{1}}}d\theta_{1}\\
V_{3}(d)&=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{R_{2}\sin^{n}\theta_{1}}{R_{d}(\theta_{1})^{n-1}\sqrt{R_{2}^{2}-d^{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{1}}}d\theta_{1}.\end{cases}

Then the following holds:

(1)

V_{1}(d)=V_{1}(0)
,

(2)

V_{2}(d)=0
,

(3)

V_{3}(d)\geq V_{3}(0)
, with equality if and only if
d=0
.

3.
Optimality of Concentric Annuli for the First Steklov–Neumann Eigenvalue

In this section, we give the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}})
be the admissible test function defined in (
2.3
) for
\mu_{1}(\Omega_{d})
. Then, using the variational characterization, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(\Omega_{d})\leq\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{d}}|\nabla f|^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R_{2}}(d)}f^{2}\,dS}.

To prove the Theorem, it suffices to prove the following two inequalities:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{d}}|\nabla f|^{2}\,dV

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega_{0}}|\nabla f|^{2}\,dV,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R_{2}}(d)}f^{2}\,dS

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\partial B_{R_{2}}}f^{2}\,dS.

We first prove (
3.1
).
For
n=2
, the gradient of
f
in polar coordinates
(r,\theta_{1})
is given by

\nabla f(r,\theta_{1})=\begin{bmatrix}\sin\theta_{1}\left(1-\dfrac{R_{1}^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)\\[4.0pt]
\cos\theta_{1}\left(1+\dfrac{R_{1}^{2}}{(n-1)r^{2}}\right)\end{bmatrix}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{d}}

\displaystyle|\nabla f|^{2}\,dV=

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{d}(\theta_{1})}\Big[\sin^{2}\theta_{1}\!\left(1-\tfrac{R_{1}^{2}}{(n-1)r^{2}}\right)^{\!2}+\cos^{2}\theta_{1}\!\left(1-\tfrac{R_{1}^{2}}{(n-1)r^{2}}\right)^{\!2}\Big]r\,dr\,d\theta_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Bigg[\frac{R_{d}^{2}(\theta_{1})-R_{1}^{2}}{2}+\frac{2R_{1}^{2}}{n-1}(1-2\sin^{2}\theta_{1})\ln\!\left(\frac{R_{d}(\theta_{1})}{R_{1}}\right)-\frac{R_{1}^{4}}{2(n-1)}\!\left(\frac{1}{R_{d}^{2}(\theta_{1})}-\frac{1}{R_{1}^{2}}\right)\Bigg]d\theta_{1}.

For
n\geq 3
, we have

\nabla f(r,\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n-1})=\begin{bmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\\[4.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{1}}\\[4.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{r\sin\theta_{1}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{2}}\\[4.0pt]
\vdots\\[4.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{r\sin\theta_{1}\cdots\sin\theta_{n-2}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{n-1}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sin\theta_{1}\cos\theta_{2}\left(1-\dfrac{R_{1}^{n}}{r^{n}}\right)\\[4.0pt]
\cos\theta_{1}\cos\theta_{2}\left(1+\dfrac{R_{1}^{n}}{(n-1)r^{n}}\right)\\[4.0pt]
-\sin\theta_{2}\left(1+\dfrac{R_{1}^{n}}{(n-1)r^{n}}\right)\\[4.0pt]
0\\[4.0pt]
\vdots\\[4.0pt]
0\end{bmatrix}.

Then, following a similar calculation that the reader can find in
[
14
]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{d}}|\nabla f|^{2}\,dV

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{(n-1)^{2}}\prod_{k=0}^{n-3}I_{k}\int_{0}^{\pi}\Bigg[\sin^{n-2}\!\theta_{1}\,\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{n}\big(R_{d}^{n}(\theta_{1})-R_{1}^{n}\big)

\displaystyle+2R_{1}^{n}\ln\!\left(\frac{R_{d}(\theta_{1})}{R_{1}}\right)\!\big(-n\sin^{n}\!\theta_{1}+(n-1)\sin^{n-2}\!\theta_{1}\big)

\displaystyle-\frac{R_{1}^{2n}}{n}\!\left(\frac{1}{R_{d}^{n}(\theta_{1})}-\frac{1}{R_{1}^{n}}\right)\!\big((n-2)\sin^{n}\!\theta_{1}+(n-1)\sin^{n-2}\!\theta_{1}\big)\Bigg]d\theta_{1},

where
I_{k}=\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{k}t\,dt
.
Hence,

\int_{\Omega_{d}}|\nabla f|^{2}\,dV=\frac{2}{(n-1)^{2}}\prod_{k=0}^{n-3}I_{k}\left[\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{n}A_{1}(d)+2R_{1}^{n}A_{2}(d)-\frac{R_{1}^{2n}}{n}A_{3}(d)\right],

where
A_{1}(d),A_{2}(d),A_{3}(d)
are defined in (
2.1
).
Applying Lemma
2.1
, we deduce

\int_{\Omega_{d}}|\nabla f|^{2}\,dV\leq\int_{\Omega_{0}}|\nabla f|^{2}\,dV.

Then, to establish (
3.2
),
if
\theta=(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n-1})
and
Q=[0,\pi]^{n-1}
, we know that

\int_{\partial B_{R_{2}}(d)}f^{2}\,dS=2\int_{Q}f^{2}(r,\theta)\,R_{d}(\theta_{1})^{n-2}\prod_{i=1}^{n-2}\sin^{n-1-i}\!\theta_{i}\sqrt{R_{d}(\theta_{1})^{2}+\big(R_{d}^{\prime}(\theta_{1})\big)^{2}}\,d\theta,

where
d\theta=d\theta_{1}\cdots d\theta_{n-1}
.
Using the explicit expression of
f
and following a similar calculation as in
[
14
]
, we obtain

(3.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R_{2}}(d)}f^{2}\,dS=2\prod_{k=2}^{n-1}\left(V_{1}(d)+\frac{2R_{1}^{n}R_{2}}{n-1}(I_{n}+V_{2}(d))+\frac{R_{1}^{2n}}{R_{2}(n-1)^{2}}V_{3}(d)\right),

where
V_{1}(d),V_{2}(d),V_{3}(d)
are as defined previously.
Then, by Lemma
2.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R_{2}}(d)}f^{2}\,dS\geq\int_{\partial B_{R_{2}}}f^{2}\,dS.

Finally, combining (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), we get

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(\Omega_{d})\leq\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{d}}|\nabla f|^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R_{2}}(d)}f^{2}\,dS}\leq\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{0}}|\nabla f|^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R_{2}}}f^{2}\,dS}=\mu_{1}(\Omega_{0}),

with equality if and only if
d=0
.
This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Bounds for Steklov–Neumann eigenvalues

Throughout this section, we consider
\tilde{\Omega}=\tilde{\Omega}_{\mathrm{out}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}
to be a star-shaped domain with a spherical hole, where
B_{R_{1}}\subset M
is the geodesic ball of radius
R_{1}
centered at a point
p
such that
\overline{B_{R_{1}}}\subset\tilde{\Omega}_{\mathrm{out}}.
We denote by
B_{R_{m}}
and
B_{R_{M}}
the geodesic balls in
M
, also centered at
p
, with radii
R_{m}
and
R_{M}
, defined in
2.1
, respectively.

4.1.
Useful results

Let
w
be a continuously differentiable function defined on the annular domain
B_{R_{M}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}
in
M
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla w\|^{2}\;=\;\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial r}\right)^{2}\;+\;\frac{1}{h^{2}(r)}\,\|\overline{\nabla}w\|^{2},

where
\overline{\nabla}w
denotes the component of
\nabla w
tangent to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
w
be a continuously differentiable function defined on

B_{R_{M}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}
. Then the two following inequalities hold:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}\|\nabla w\|^{2}\,dV\;\leq\;\int_{B_{R_{M}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}}\|\nabla w\|^{2}\,dV,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{out}}w^{2}\,dS\;\geq\;\frac{h^{n-1}(R_{m})}{h^{n-1}(R_{M})}\int_{\partial B_{R_{M}}}w^{2}\,dS.

Proof.

In order to prove the inequality (
4.1
), using the
polar coordinates, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}\|\nabla w\|^{2}\,dV

\displaystyle=\int_{U_{p}M}\int_{R_{1}}^{r_{u}}\Bigg[\Big(\frac{\partial w}{\partial r}\Big)^{2}+\frac{1}{h^{2}(r)}\|\overline{\nabla}w\|^{2}\Bigg]h^{\,n-1}(r)\,dr\,du,

and, since
r_{u}<R_{M}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}\|\nabla w\|^{2}\,dV

\displaystyle\leq\int_{U_{p}M}\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{M}}\Bigg[\Big(\frac{\partial w}{\partial r}\Big)^{2}+\frac{1}{h^{2}(r)}\|\overline{\nabla}w\|^{2}\Bigg]h^{\,n-1}(r)\,dr\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R_{M}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}}\|\nabla w\|^{2}\,dV,

which proves the claim.

Let us prove now (
4.2
). Recall that for every
q\in\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{\mathrm{out}},
there esist a unique
u\in U_{p}M
such that
q=exp_{p}(r_{u}u).
We use the notation
\theta_{u}
in place of
\theta(q)
and the representation of the surface
measure in polar coordinates
dS=\sec(\theta_{u})h^{n-1}(r_{u})du
(see
[
25
, p. 385]
). This gives

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{out}}w^{2}\,dS

\displaystyle=\int_{U_{p}M}w^{2}\,\sec(\theta_{u})\,h^{\,n-1}(r_{u})\,du.

Since
\sec(\theta_{u})\geq 1,r_{u}\geq R_{m}
, and
h(r)
is an increasing function of
r,
it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{out}}w^{2}\,dS

\displaystyle\geq\int_{U_{p}M}w^{2}\,h^{\,n-1}(R_{m})\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{\,n-1}(R_{m})}{h^{\,n-1}(R_{M})}\int_{U_{p}M}w^{2}\,h^{\,n-1}(R_{M})\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{\,n-1}(R_{m})}{h^{\,n-1}(R_{M})}\int_{\partial B_{R_{M}}}w^{2}\,dS.

This concludes the proof.

∎

To establish the lower bound, we need the following proposition.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\tilde{w}
be a continuously differentiable function defined on
\tilde{\Omega}
.
Then the following two inequalities hold:

(4.3)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R_{m}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}}\|\nabla\tilde{w}\|^{2}\,dV\;\leq\;\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}\|\nabla\tilde{w}\|^{2}\,dV,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R_{M}}}\tilde{w}^{2}\,dS\;\geq\;\frac{\sqrt{1+a}\ h^{n-1}(R_{m})}{h^{n-1}(R_{M})}\int_{\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{out}}\tilde{w}^{2}\,dS,

where constant
a
is defined in (
2.2
).

The proof is same as that of Proposition
4.1
and Theorem 2.1 in
[
8
]
.

4.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Proof of the upper bound.

Let
\psi_{1}
be the first eigenfunction corresponding to
\mu_{1}\!\left(B_{R_{M}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}\right)
.
We define a function
\psi
on
B_{R_{M}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}
as

\displaystyle\psi:=\psi_{1}-\overline{\psi_{1}},\qquad\overline{\psi_{1}}=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{out}}\psi_{1}\,dS}{\operatorname{Vol}(\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{out})}.

Since
\int_{\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{out}}\psi dS=0,
function
\psi
can be used as a test function for the variational characterization of
\mu_{1}(\tilde{\Omega}).

Applying Proposition
4.1
, we obtain

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(\tilde{\Omega})\leq\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}\|\nabla\psi\|^{2}\,dV}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{out}}\psi^{2}\,dS}\;\leq\;\frac{h^{n-1}(R_{M})}{h^{n-1}(R_{m})}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{B_{R_{M}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}}\|\nabla\psi\|^{2}\,dV}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R_{M}}}\psi^{2}\,dS}.

Since
\overline{\psi_{1}}
is constant, then

(4.6)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R_{M}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}}\|\nabla\psi\|^{2}\,dV=\int_{B_{R_{M}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}}\|\nabla\psi_{1}\|^{2}\,dV.

For the denominator, we compute

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R_{M}}}\psi^{2}\,dS

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{R_{M}}}\big(\psi_{1}^{2}+\overline{\psi_{1}}^{2}-2\psi_{1}\overline{\psi_{1}}\big)\,dS

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{R_{M}}}\psi_{1}^{2}\,dS+\overline{\psi_{1}}^{2}\,\operatorname{Vol}(\partial B_{R_{M}})-2\overline{\psi_{1}}\int_{\partial B_{R_{M}}}\psi_{1}\,dS.

By definition,
\psi_{1}
is a zero-mean function on
B_{R_{M}}
, and therefore the last term vanishes. Hence, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R_{M}}}\psi^{2}\,dS=\int_{\partial B_{R_{M}}}\psi_{1}^{2}\,dS+\overline{\psi_{1}}^{2}\,\operatorname{Vol}(\partial B_{R_{M}})\;\geq\;\int_{\partial B_{R_{M}}}\psi_{1}^{2}\,dS.

Therefore, from (
4.5
) we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}\|\nabla\psi\|^{2}dV}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{out}}\psi^{2}dS}\;\leq\;\frac{h^{n-1}(R_{M})}{h^{n-1}(R_{m})}\cdot\frac{\displaystyle\int_{B_{R_{M}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}}\|\nabla\psi_{1}\|^{2}dV}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R_{M}}}\psi_{1}^{2}dS}\;=\;\frac{h^{n-1}(R_{M})}{h^{n-1}(R_{m})}\,\mu_{1}(B_{R_{M}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}).

Hence,

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(\tilde{\Omega})\;\leq\;\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}\|\nabla\psi\|^{2}dV}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{out}}\psi^{2}dS}\;\leq\;\frac{h^{n-1}(R_{M})}{h^{n-1}(R_{m})}\,\mu_{1}(B_{R_{M}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}).

∎

Proof of the lower bound.

The proof follows using the same strategy as that of the upper bound, except that here we take
\tilde{\psi}_{1}
to be the first eigenfunction corresponding to
\mu_{1}(\tilde{\Omega})
and use the test function

\tilde{\psi}:=\tilde{\psi}_{1}-\overline{\tilde{\psi}_{1}}
, with

\overline{\tilde{\psi}_{1}}=\dfrac{\int_{\partial B_{R_{m}}}\tilde{\psi}_{1}\,dS}{\operatorname{Vol}(\partial B_{R_{m}})}
for the variational characterization of
\mu_{1}(B_{R_{m}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}})
. After an application of Proposition
4.2
,
the rest of the argument is identical to the proof of the upper bound.

∎

4.3.
Why do we need starshapedness?

Theorem
1.2
ensures that, whenever

\tilde{\Omega}_{\mathrm{out}}
is an open, bounded, star-shaped domain and
R_{1}>0
, the quantity

\mu_{1}(\tilde{\Omega})
is bounded away from zero. A natural question is whether this conclusion continues to hold for an arbitrary bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

with Lipschitz boundary. The answer is negative, even when
\tilde{\Omega}_{\mathrm{out}}
is assumed to be connected. This fact is illustrated by the following two-dimensional counterexample, that can be found in
[
19
]
in the Steklov setting in the non perforated case.

Proposition 4.3
.

There exists a sequence
\{\Omega_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
of open, bounded sets with Lipschitz boundary, such that
\mu_{1}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})\to 0
, as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be small enough. Let us consider the open rectangle centered at the origin of sides
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon^{3}

C_{\varepsilon}=\bigg(-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\bigg)\times\bigg(-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{2},\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{2}\bigg)

and let us define the two balls

B_{i,\varepsilon}=B_{r_{i}(\varepsilon)}(x_{i}(\varepsilon)),\qquad i=1,2,

with radii
r_{1}(\varepsilon)=r_{2}(\varepsilon)=\sqrt{(1+\varepsilon/2)^{2}+\varepsilon^{6}/4}
, centered at the points
x_{1}(\varepsilon)=(-1-\varepsilon,0)
and
x_{2}(\varepsilon)=(1+\varepsilon,0)
, respectively.
Let us consider the following sequence of perforated dumbells
\{\Omega_{\varepsilon}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, defined as follows

\Omega_{\varepsilon}=B_{1,\varepsilon}\cup C_{\varepsilon}\cup(B_{2,\varepsilon}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}(x_{2}(\varepsilon))}),

where
B_{R_{1}}(x_{2}(\varepsilon))
is the concentric ball contained in
B_{r_{2}(\varepsilon)}(x_{2}(\varepsilon))
of radius
0<R_{1}<1
. Let us consider the following continuous function

v(x,y)=\begin{cases}\sin\bigg(\displaystyle\frac{2\pi x}{\varepsilon}\bigg)&\text{in}\,C_{\varepsilon}\\
0&\text{elsewhere}.\end{cases}

Let us stress that
v(x,y)
is a good test function for the first non zero Steklov–Neumann eigenvalue, since

\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}\setminus\partial B_{R_{1}}(x_{2}(\varepsilon))}v\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\int_{\partial C_{\varepsilon}}v\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=2\int_{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\sin\bigg(\displaystyle\frac{2\pi x}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\,dx=-\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}\cos\bigg(\displaystyle\frac{2\pi x}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\Bigg|_{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}=0.

The denominator in the Rayleigh quotient of
\mu_{1}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})
becomes

\begin{split}\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}v^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}&=\int_{\partial C_{\varepsilon}}v^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=2\int_{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\sin^{2}\bigg(\displaystyle\frac{2\pi x}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\,dx\\
&=4\int_{0}^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\sin^{2}\bigg(\displaystyle\frac{4\pi x}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\,dx=4\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\frac{1-\cos\bigg(\displaystyle\frac{4\pi x}{\varepsilon}\bigg)}{2}\,dx=\varepsilon.\end{split}

Moreover, since

|\nabla v|^{2}=\bigg(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\bigg)^{2}=\bigg(\frac{2\pi}{\varepsilon}\bigg)^{2}\cos^{2}\bigg(\displaystyle\frac{2\pi x}{\varepsilon}\bigg),

The numerator is

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}|\nabla v|^{2}\,dx\,dy&=\int_{C_{\varepsilon}}|\nabla v|^{2}\,dx\,dy=\bigg(\frac{2\pi}{\varepsilon}\bigg)^{2}\int_{-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{2}}^{\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{2}}\int_{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\cos^{2}\bigg(\displaystyle\frac{4\pi x}{\varepsilon}\bigg)\,dx\,dy\\
&=2\bigg(\frac{2\pi}{\varepsilon}\bigg)^{2}\varepsilon^{3}\int_{0}^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\frac{1+\cos\bigg(\displaystyle\frac{4\pi x}{\varepsilon}\bigg)}{2}\,dx=2\pi^{2}\varepsilon^{2}.\end{split}

In this way, since
v
is zero on
\partial B_{R_{1}}(x_{2})
, we get

\mu_{1}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})\leq\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}=2\pi^{2}\varepsilon,

and eventually

\mu_{1}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})\to 0,\qquad\qquad\text{as}\;\;\varepsilon\to 0.

∎

The previous counterexample provides tell us that the first non zero Steklov–Neumann eigenvalue can become arbitrarily small when the domain is not star-shaped.

5.
Asymptotic behavior with respect to the radius of the hole

In this section, we study the behavior of the first non zero Steklov–Neumann eigenvalue and the corresponding eigenfunctions on a bounded domain with a spherical hole, as the radius of the spherical hole approaches to 0. This is the content of the Theorem
1.3
.

We denote by
\Omega_{r}=\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}\setminus\overline{B_{r}}
, where
\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an open, bounded and Lipschitz set and
B_{r}
is the ball centered at the origin of radius
r>0
such that
\overline{B_{r}}\subset\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}
.
We also denote the concentric annulus in the following way
A_{r,R}=B_{R}\setminus\overline{B_{r}}
, with
R>r>0
.

In order to prove Theorem
1.3
, we need to consider a particular sequence of eigenfunctions
\{u_{1}^{r}\}_{r}
corresponding to
\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r})
. Differently from the Steklov-Dirichlet case, in which it sufficed to extend the sequence of eigenfunctions to zero inside the hole, in this case the situation is a bit more delicate, since we need to extend them by harmonicity inside
B_{r}
. More precisely, we need control the rate of convergence of the gradient of the harmonic extension
h
in
L^{2}(B_{r})
as the radius
r
approaches zero and use the following technical Lemma, that is an application of
[
18
, Lemma 12]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any
0<r<R
, let
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{A_{r,R}})
be such that

\begin{cases}\Delta u=0&\text{in}\;\;A_{r,R},\\
\partial_{\nu}u=0&\text{in}\;\;\partial B_{r},\end{cases}

and let us consider the function
h
, which is the harmonic extension of
u
in
B_{r}
, that is

\begin{cases}\Delta h=0&\text{in}\;\;B_{r},\\
h=u&\text{on}\;\;\partial B_{r}.\end{cases}

Then, as
r/R\to 0
, we have

(5.1)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}}|{\nabla h}|^{2}\,dx\leq 5\bigg(\frac{r}{R}\bigg)^{n}\bigg(1+O\bigg(\frac{r}{R}\bigg)^{n}\bigg)\int_{A_{r,R}}|{\nabla u}|^{2}\,dx.

Now we present a proof of the main result of this section.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

In the proof, we will use the following notation: let
\varepsilon>0
be small enough and let us consider
\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}}=\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}\setminus\overline{B_{r_{\varepsilon}}}
, where
B_{r_{\varepsilon}}
is a ball centered at the origin with radius
r_{\varepsilon}
, with
0<r_{\varepsilon}<\varepsilon
, such that
\overline{B}_{r_{\varepsilon}}\subset\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}
, and
r_{\varepsilon}/\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
. From now onwords, we will consider
u_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}
as an eigenfunction corresponding to the first non zero Steklov–Neumann eigenvalue
\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})
, such that
\|u_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{out})}=1
.

Step 1. (An upper bound for
\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})
)
In this step we will prove that

(5.2)

\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})\leq\sigma_{1}(\Omega_{out}).

This follows immediately by choosing as a test function in the variational characterization (
1.3
) of
\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})
, an eigenfunction corresponding to the first non-trivial Steklov eigenvalue
\sigma_{1}(\Omega_{out})
, let us say
\tilde{u}_{1}
. Then,
\tilde{u}_{1}
is orthogonal to the constant function on
\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}},
that is

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}\tilde{u}_{1}\,dS=0.

Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})\leq\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}}}|\nabla\tilde{u}_{1}|^{2}\,dV}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}\tilde{u}_{1}^{2}\,dS}\leq\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}|\nabla\tilde{u}_{1}|^{2}\,dV}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}\tilde{u}_{1}^{2}\,dS}=\sigma_{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}),

for every
\varepsilon
small enough. Then, applying Brock inequality (
[
10
]
), we get

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})\leq\sigma_{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})\leq\sigma_{1}(B_{R})=\frac{1}{R},

for every
\varepsilon
small enough, where
B_{R}
is the ball having same measure as
\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}.

Step 2. (Convergence of the eigenvalues)
In the previous step, we have proved that the sequence of strictly positive eigenvalues
\{\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})\}_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded for small
\varepsilon>0
. Then, there exist a subsequence, still denoted by
\{\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})\}_{\varepsilon}
, such that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\inf\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})=\tilde{\sigma}.

Let us now extend by harmonicity
u_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}
inside
B_{r_{\varepsilon}}
, i.e. let us consider
the function
U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})
, defined as

\begin{cases}U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}=u_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}&\text{in}\;\;\overline{\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}}}\\
\Delta U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}=0&\text{in}\;\;B_{r_{\varepsilon}}.\end{cases}

We want to prove that
U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}
is equibounded in
L^{2}(\Omega_{out})
. If we consider the following eigenvalue problem with Robin boundary condition and Robin boundary parameter
\beta=1
,

\begin{cases}\Delta v=\lambda v&\text{in}\;\;\Omega_{out}\\
\partial_{\nu}v+v=0&\text{in}\;\;\partial\Omega_{out},\end{cases}

Then, the first Robin eigenvalue has the following variational characterization

\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{out})=\inf_{0\neq v\in H^{1}(\Omega_{out})}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{out}}|{\nabla v}|^{2}\,dx+\int_{\partial\Omega_{out}}v^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{out}}v^{2}\,dx}.

Since
U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{out})
, we have

\|U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{out})}^{2}\leq\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{out})^{-1}(\|\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{out})}^{2}+\|U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{out})}^{2}).

By the normalization condition, we know that

\|\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{out})}^{2}=\|\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{r_{\varepsilon}})}^{2}+\|\nabla u_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})}^{2}=\|\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{r_{\varepsilon}})}^{2}+\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}}).

Therefore

\|U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})}\leq\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{out})^{-1}(\|\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{r_{\varepsilon}})}^{2}+\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})+1).

Let us estimate now
\|\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{r_{\varepsilon}})}
. By applying Lemma
5.1
in the annulus
A_{r_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon}
, which is well contained in
\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}}
for
\varepsilon
small enough, we get

\begin{split}\|\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B_{r_{\varepsilon}})}&\leq 5\bigg(\frac{r_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\bigg)^{n}\bigg(1+O\bigg(\frac{r_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\bigg)^{n}\bigg)\|\nabla u_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(A_{r_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon})}\\
&\leq C\|\nabla u_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(A_{r_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon})}\leq C\|\nabla u_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})}=C\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}}),\end{split}

where we have used again that
A_{r_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon}\subseteq\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}}
. This proves that

\|U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})}\leq\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{out})^{-1}[(C+1)\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})+1].

By the step one,
\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})\leq\sigma_{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})
for
\varepsilon
small enough. This implies that
U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}
is equibounded in
L^{2}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})
and, therefore, there exists a subsequence still denoted by
\{U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\}_{\varepsilon}
converges strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})
to some
\overline{u_{1}}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})
and such that
\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\rightharpoonup\nabla\overline{u_{1}}
weakly in
L^{2}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}).
Moreover, by compactness of the trace operator we have that
\{U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\}_{\varepsilon}
converges strongly in
L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})
almost everywhere on
\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}.

Now we use a test function
\phi\in H^{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}),
in the weak formulation of the Steklov-Neumann eigenvalue problem (
1.2
).
Since
U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}
is an eigenfunction corresponding to the eigenvalue
\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}}).
Then,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}}}\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\,\,\nabla\phi\,dV=\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})\int_{\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\phi\,dS.

Since
\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}
converges weakly in
L^{2}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})
to
\nabla\overline{u_{1}},
then

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}}}\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\,\,\nabla\phi\,dV=\int_{\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}\nabla\overline{u_{1}}\,\nabla\phi\,dV,

and since
U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}
converges strongly in
L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\phi\,dS=\int_{\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}\overline{u_{1}}\phi\,dS.

Now, passing to the lim inf in (
5.3
) as
\varepsilon\to 0,
we get

(5.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}\nabla\overline{u_{1}}\,\nabla\phi\,dV=\tilde{\sigma}\int_{\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}\overline{u_{1}}\phi\,dS,\quad\text{for all}\quad\phi\in H^{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}).

This means that
\tilde{\sigma}
belongs to the spectrum of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator.
If
\tilde{\sigma}=0,
then from (
5.4
) we would have
\overline{u_{1}}
constant, and the orthogonality condition

\displaystyle 0=\int_{\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\to\int_{\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}\overline{u_{1}},\quad\text{as}\quad r_{\varepsilon}\to 0,

would imply that
\overline{u_{1}}=0
on
{\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}
. But this is not possible since the normalization condition holds. Therefore,
\tilde{\sigma}>0
and therefore
\sigma_{1}({\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}})\leq\tilde{\sigma}.

Then,

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}({\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}})\leq\tilde{\sigma}=\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})\leq\sigma_{1}({\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}).

Hence, we conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})=\sigma_{1}({\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}).

Step 3. (Convergence of eigenfunction)

We now recall the convergence properties of the sequence

\{U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\}
.
We have

\displaystyle U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\longrightarrow\overline{u_{1}}\qquad\text{strongly in }\quad L^{2}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}),

and

\displaystyle\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\rightharpoonup\nabla\overline{u_{1}}\qquad\text{weakly in }L^{2}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}).

Moreover, we obtained the strong convergence of the traces:

\displaystyle U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\big|_{\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}\longrightarrow\overline{u_{1}}\big|_{\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}\qquad\text{in }L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}).

To prove the strong convergence of
U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}
to
\overline{u_{1}}
in
H^{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})
, we only need to prove the strong convergence of
\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}
to
\nabla\overline{u_{1}}
in
L^{2}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}).
We consider
U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}
as a test function in the weak formulation of the classical Steklov eigenvalue problem on
\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}
, arriving to

(5.5)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}\nabla\overline{u_{1}}\,\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\,dV=\sigma_{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})\int_{\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}\overline{u_{1}}\,U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\,dS.

Then,

\displaystyle\|\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}-\nabla\overline{u_{1}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})}

\displaystyle=\|\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})}+\|\nabla\overline{u_{1}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})}-2\int_{\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}\nabla\overline{u_{1}}\,\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\,dV

\displaystyle=\|\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B_{r_{\varepsilon}})}+\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})+\sigma_{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})-2\sigma_{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})\int_{\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}\overline{u_{1}}\,U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\,dS.

Applying Lemma
5.1
and considering that
A_{r_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon}\subseteq\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}}
, we get

\begin{split}\|\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{r_{\varepsilon}})}&\leq 5\bigg(\frac{r_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\bigg)^{n}\bigg(1+O\bigg(\frac{r_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\bigg)^{n}\bigg)\|\nabla u_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{2}(A_{r_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon})}\\
&\leq 5\bigg(\frac{r_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\bigg)^{n}\bigg(1+O\bigg(\frac{r_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\bigg)^{n}\bigg)\|\nabla u_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})}\\
&=5\bigg(\frac{r_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\bigg)^{n}\bigg(1+O\bigg(\frac{r_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\bigg)^{n}\bigg)\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}}),\end{split}

which implies

\lim_{\frac{r_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\to 0^{+}}\|\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{r_{\varepsilon}})}=0.

Furthermore,

\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r_{\varepsilon}})\to\sigma_{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}),\quad\text{as}\quad\varepsilon\to 0,

and the normalization together with the strong convergence of the traces on
\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}
yields

\int_{\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}\overline{u_{1}}\,U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}\,dS\to\int_{\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}}\overline{u_{1}}^{2}\,dS=1.

Hence

\displaystyle\|\nabla U_{1}^{r_{\varepsilon}}-\nabla\overline{u_{1}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad\varepsilon\to 0.

∎

The asymptotic convergence established in Theorem
1.3

naturally leads to a family of isoperimetric inequalities for domains with
small holes, which we state below.

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
\Omega_{r}=\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}\setminus\overline{B_{r}}
, where

\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an open, bounded, and
connected set with Lipschitz boundary, and
B_{r}
is a ball of radius
r>0

centered at the origin such that
\overline{B_{r}}\subset\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}
.
Then, there exists
r_{2}^{M}=r_{2}^{M}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})
, depending only on
the geometry of
\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}
, such that for every

r\in(0,r_{2}^{M})
,

(5.6)

\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r})\leq\mu_{1}(A_{r,R_{M}}),

where
R_{M}>0
is the radius of the ball having the same measure as

\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}
. The equality case holds if and only if
\Omega_{r}=A_{r,R_{M}}
.

The next result is obtained by replacing the measure constraint with a
perimeter constraint and applying the Weinstock inequality (
[
11
]
).

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
\Omega_{r}=\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}\setminus\overline{B_{r}}
, where

\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an open, bounded, and convex
set, and
B_{r}
is a ball of radius
r>0
centered at the origin such that

\overline{B_{r}}\subset\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}
.
Then there exists
r_{2}^{P}=r_{2}^{P}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})
, depending only on
the geometry of
\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}
, such that for every

r\in(0,r_{2}^{P})
,

(5.7)

\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r})\leq\mu_{1}(A_{r,R_{P}}),

where
R_{P}>0
denotes the radius of the ball having the same perimeter as

\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}
. The equality case holds if and only if
\Omega_{r}=A_{r,R_{P}}
.

We will give only the proof of Corollary
5.2
and we stress that it is just a simple appilication of the Weinstock inequality and limiting arguments that follow from Theorem
1.3
. The proof of Corollary
5.1
follows the same arguments, where the Weinstock inequality is replaced by the Brock inequality.

Proof of Corollary
5.2
.

By the Weinstock inequality, since
\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}
is convex and has the same perimeter as
B_{R_{P}}
, we have

\sigma_{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})\leq\sigma_{1}(B_{R_{P}}),

and the equality holds if and only if
\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}=B_{R_{P}}
.

If
\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}=B_{R_{P}}
, then
\Omega_{r}=A_{r,R_{P}}
and equality holds trivially. Therefore we can assume that
\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}\neq B_{R_{P}}
. Then the Weinstock inequality is strict and there exists
\delta>0
such that

\sigma_{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})\leq\sigma_{1}(B_{R_{P}})-2\delta.

By Theorem
1.3
we have that

\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r})\to\sigma_{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})\quad\text{as }r\to 0^{+}.

Hence there exists
r_{1}=r_{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})>0
such that for every
r<r_{1}
,

\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r})\leq\sigma_{1}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})+\delta.

Therefore,

\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r})\leq\sigma_{1}(B_{R_{P}})-\delta.

Always by Theorem
1.3
, we have that

\mu_{1}(A_{r,R_{P}})\to\sigma_{1}(B_{R_{P}})\quad\text{as }r\to 0^{+},

and so there exists
r_{2}=r_{2}(\Omega_{\mathrm{out}})>0
such that for every
r<r_{2}
,

\sigma_{1}(B_{R_{P}})-\delta\leq\mu_{1}(A_{r,R_{P}}).

Hence, for every
r<\min\{r_{1},r_{2}\}=r^{P}_{2}
, we obtain

\mu_{1}(\Omega_{r})\leq\sigma_{1}(B_{R_{P}})-\delta\leq\mu_{1}(A_{r,R_{P}}),

which proves the desired inequality.
Conversely, if equality holds, then equality must hold in the Weinstock inequality, which implies
\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}=B_{R_{P}}
.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

In dimension two, Corollary
5.2
remains valid within
the class of planar simply connected domains (see
[
32
]
).

6.
About the nodal domains

Let
\Omega=\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}\setminus\overline{B_{r}}
and let us denote by
V_{k}
the eigenspace corresponding to
\mu_{k}(\Omega)
. Before stating the result, we introduce the notion of a nodal domain,

Definition 6.1
.

We will call
\Omega_{j}
a nodal domain of
u\in V_{k}
in
\Omega
, a connected component of the set
\{x\in\Omega:u(x)\neq 0\}
. In particular, we will denote by

(6.1)

\Delta_{k}=\max_{v\in V_{k}}\sharp\{\text{nodal domains of v}\}.

In this section, we study the number of nodal domains of the eigenfunctions corresponding to the first nonzero Steklov–Neumann eigenvalue.
In the radial case (see section
2.2
), we recall the explicit expression of the eigenfunctions given by

u^{i}_{n}(x)=\left(r+\frac{R_{1}^{n}}{(n-1)r^{n-1}}\right)\frac{x_{i}}{r}.

It is immediately evident that for every
i=1,...,n
, the eigenfunctions have exactly two nodal domains and that the nodal line is the set where
x_{i}=0
. This observation naturally raises the question of whether the same
property holds for a general doubly connected domain
\Omega
.
The answer to this question is affermative and it is given in the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

The arguments of the proof follow the idea contained in
[
1
,
24
]
, with modifications due to the presence of a hole in
\Omega_{\mathrm{out}}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

By contradiction, let us assume that there exists
u\in V_{1}
with
\Delta
nodal domains
\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{\Delta}
, such that

\Delta\geq 2+m_{0}=3,

where
m_{0}=1
is the multiplicity of the trivial eigenvalue
\mu_{0}(\Omega)
.
Let
u_{0}=1
the basis of the eigenspace
W_{0}
and let us consider the following function

v=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{\Delta-1}\alpha_{l}\cdot u\chi_{\Omega_{l}}&\text{in}\,\,\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{\Delta}\\
\displaystyle 0&$\text{in}$\,\,\Omega_{\Delta},\end{cases}

where
\chi_{\Omega_{l}}(\cdot)
is the characteristic function of the nodal domain
\Omega_{l}
and
\alpha_{l}\in\mathbb{R}
not all zero, for all
l=0,\dots,\Delta-1
. In particular, we can choose
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\Delta-1}
in such a way that

\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}vu_{0}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0.

This makes
v
an admissible function for
\mu_{1}
and hence, for every
l=0,\dots,\Delta-1
, an integration by parts gives

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega_{l}}|\nabla v|^{2}\,dx&=\int_{\partial\Omega_{l}}\frac{\partial v}{\partial\nu}v\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\\
&=\alpha_{l}^{2}\int_{\partial\Omega_{l}\cap\partial\Omega_{0}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}u\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\alpha_{l}^{2}\mu_{1}\int_{\partial\Omega_{l}\cap\partial\Omega_{0}}u^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.\end{split}

Summing up to
\Delta-1
, we get

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}=\mu_{1}\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}v^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Hence,
v
is an eigenfunction associated to
\mu_{1}
. Since
v\equiv 0
in
\Omega_{\Delta}
and it is harmonic, it must be
v\equiv 0
in
\Omega
, which is a contradiction. Therefore, we must have

(6.2)

\Delta_{1}\leq 1+m_{0}=2.

Next, let
v
be an eigenfunction associated to
\mu_{1}(\Omega)
. Since it holds

\int_{\partial\Omega_{0}}v\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0,

and by the regularity of
v
(
v
is a harmonic function, hence
C^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap C(\bar{\Omega})
), we have that

v
is sign changing, which means that
\Delta_{2}\geq 2
. Then applying the inequality (
6.2
), we have

\Delta_{2}=2.

∎

Remark 6.1
.

A notable difference between the Steklov–Dirichlet and the
Steklov–Neumann problems concern the structure of the nodal domains of
the associated eigenfunctions. In the Steklov–Dirichlet case, the
Dirichlet condition imposed on the inner boundary forces the eigenfunctions
to vanish there, which strongly influences both their sign distribution
and the geometry of their nodal sets. As a consequence, the nodal lines
tend to be anchored to the Dirichlet boundary, and their behavior is
closely tied to the topology of the perforation (see
[
30
, Proposition 2.5]
).

By contrast, in the Steklov–Neumann problem, the Neumann condition on the
inner boundary allows eigenfunctions to attain non-zero values on

\Gamma_{1}
. This additional flexibility leads to a markedly different nodal
structure, as nodal sets are no longer constrained to intersect the inner
boundary. The analysis of nodal domains therefore requires distinct
arguments, and classical techniques developed for the Steklov–Dirichlet
problem cannot be applied directly.
