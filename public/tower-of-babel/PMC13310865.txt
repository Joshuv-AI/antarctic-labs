Tamoxifen loaded lipid nanoparticles as a novel therapeutic approach enhancing apoptotic cell death in triple negative breast cancer cells
Authors: Sevval Gunbay, Sevgi Sarigul-Ozbek, Egemen Cakirli, Omer Berk Sala, Gulen Melike Demirbolat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tamoxifen (TMX), a selective estrogen receptor modulator, is widely used to treat hormone-dependent breast cancer (BC). Nevertheless, low bioavailability, and emerging deleterious effects considerably restrict TMX clinical application. This study aimed to develop and comprehensively characterize TMX-loaded solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) and nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs), to comparatively assess their physicochemical performance and to evaluate their therapeutic potential against triple-negative BC (TNBC). After production, those lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) were characterized in terms of particle size, polydispersity index, zeta potential, morphological investigation through transmission electron microscopy analysis, identifications of interactions via FT-IR and differential scanning calorimetry, entrapment efficiency, drug loading, drug release study, long-term stability for 7 months, cell viability and flow cytometry analysis to evaluate antitumor efficacy of drug carriers. TMX loaded LNPs (15 mg) were produced with small size (< 200 nm), homogeneous dispersion (< 0.3) and high entrapment efficiency (> 90%). FT-IR spectra and differential scanning calorimetry profiles confirmed TMX conjugation into LNPs. TMX showed higher release from NLCs, whereas SLNs showed retarded release. Particle concentration was reduced 10–30 times after TMX loading. Moreover, TMX-loaded LNPs effectively inhibited cell viability on TNBC (MDA-MB-231), mainly based on apoptosis. These findings suggest that TMX-loaded LNPs represent a promising preclinical strategy for enhancing TMX efficacy against TNBC cells.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04750-z.

Introduction and aim

Breast cancer (BC) is recognized as one of the most prevalent malignancies affecting women globally. It can be defined as the uncontrolled proliferation of cells and cell groups in breast tissue and the subsequent emergence of cancerous cell structures following this proliferation. BC is regarded as a heterogeneous disease that is composed of multiple molecular subtypes, each of which is associated with distinct biological characteristics and therapeutic responses. Among these subtypes, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is characterized by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression. Treatment options for BC are generally classified as surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and hormone therapy. However, the therapeutic applicability of these modalities is not uniform across all subtypes; in particular, TNBC has been associated with aggressive tumor progression, high metastatic potential, rapid development of chemoresistance, and poor clinical prognosis. Owing to the lack of ER, PR, and HER2 expression, the use of hormone-based and targeted therapies is considerably restricted in TNBC, which renders its clinical management particularly challenging [1, 2].

Hormone therapy is employed in patients whose tumors are sensitive to estrogen and/or progesterone, and it may involve the use of selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs). Tamoxifen (TMX), a SERM-type agent, is classified as a Biopharmaceutics Classification System class II compound owing to its low solubility and high permeability. This limited aqueous solubility results in a bioavailability of approximately 20–30%, reduces plasma levels, and ultimately contributes to therapeutic failure. Consequently, high doses of TMX are required for effective BC treatment. Nevertheless, long-term TMX administration has been reported to cause endometrial cancer, thromboembolic effects, liver cancer, and pulmonary embolism, among other adverse effects. Although the applicability of hormone-based therapies is intrinsically limited in TNBC, the development of alternative delivery strategies may enhance the therapeutic potential of existing anticancer agents in these aggressive tumors [2, 3].

The adverse effects of traditional treatments reduce cancer treatment success and efficacy. Toxic side effects represent a notable limitation in the field of cancer chemotherapy. Even brief treatment periods may result in serious health issues. Despite incomplete understanding of the side effects of prolonged or lifetime exposure to biodegradable nanosized particles, the multidisciplinary nanotechnology field offers considerable prospects for cancer treatment [4]. Innovative nanomaterial and nanocarrier development have enabled enhanced cancer drug delivery. The primary goal of most nanocarrier applications is to prevent rapid drug degradation after systemic delivery, enable therapeutic drug concentrations, and reach the tumor site while minimizing drug delivery to normal sites to reduce side effects [5].

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), which are highly adaptable nanocarriers, are lipid-based nanodelivery systems capable of entering the body and delivering medications. Because they can encapsulate medications within their lipid bilayers or aqueous cores, their special phospholipid bilayer structure provides a versatile option. By ensuring that medications reach their designated targets in a controlled manner, this offers precise control over drug release, improving therapeutic efficacy and lowering potential side effects. LNPs content and structure differ depending on the kind of lipids and production techniques [6].

Solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) have emerged as a particularly promising nanocarrier for enhancing the solubility, stability, and bioavailability of poorly water-soluble drugs. SLNs offer unique properties, including small size, a large surface area, a high drug loading capacity and interaction between phases at interfaces. These properties render SLNs a potentially efficacious means of enhancing the performance of pharmaceuticals. SLNs typically comprise physiologically tolerated lipids (e.g., fatty acids, triglycerides and waxes), surfactants, and aqueous dispersion media. The lipid core remains solid at room and body temperature, and functions as a matrix in which the active pharmaceutical ingredient can be embedded. Surfactants can markedly stabilize particles, prevent aggregation, and modulate surface charge [7, 8].

Nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) rank among the most promising nanocarriers for efficient, targeted cancer chemotherapeutic delivery. Solid and liquid lipids form the core matrix of these biocompatible and/or biodegradable lipid-based nanoparticles, which are then dispersed in a surfactant solution. NLC improves the water-based solubility of most hydrophobic medications for cancer [9].

This study aims to systematically investigate the formulation and process factors that have influence on the critical quality attributes for SLNs and NLCs. Although there is a considerable amount of literature based on TMX loaded SLNs and NLCs, this is the first study that investigates the potential efficacy of TMX on TNBC, to the best of our knowledge. Moreover, this study reveals for the first time that drug presence directly modulates particle concentration, while particle number in lipid nanoparticles is often assumed to be independent of drug incorporation. The observed differences between drug-loaded and drug-free systems highlight a previously overlooked link between formulation composition and nanoparticle concentration. Drug-free lipid nanoparticles, despite being composed of non-toxic components, induced cell death at high particle concentrations, highlighting the critical role of particle concentration. TMX was formulated in a lipid-based tumor selective carrier in low doses with the aim of maintaining therapeutic efficacy while reducing drug-related complications. Therefore, formulation ingredients were first selected by considering the saturation solubility of TMX in various surfactant solutions. NLCs and SLNs were then prepared using high-speed homogenization and solvent emulsification/evaporation methods. Formulations were characterized in terms of particle size, distribution, zeta potential, particle concentration, physical interactions, loading capacity, and loading efficiency. An in vitro release study was conducted. Furthermore, cytotoxicity and apoptosis studies were carried out on MDA-MB-231 a triple negative breast cancer (TNBC) cell line. To the best of our knowledge, this study provides the first comprehensive comparative evaluation of TMX efficacy in SLN and NLC formulations for TNBC therapy. In this article, LNPs refer to SLN and NLC drug carrier systems.

Materials and methods

Materials

TMX was purchased from Biosynth. Gattefosse (France) kindly provided glyceryl palmitostearate (Precirol® ATO 5), Diethylene glycol monoethyl ether (Transcutol® HP), Propylene glycol monocaprylate (Capryol® 90), medium chain triglycerides (Labrafac™ Lipophile WL 1349), corn oil PEG-6 esters (Labrafil® M2125 CS), mixture of propylene glycol monolaurate, ethyl cellulose and propylene glycol isostearate (Emulfree Duo), polyoxyethylene stearates (Gelucire 48/16), and PEG-8 caprylic/capric glycerides (Labrasol). Sigma Aldrich (Germany) supplied polyoxyethylene lauryl ether (Brij 35), polyethylene-polypropylene-polyethylene glycol (Poloxamer 188), and polyethylene-polypropylene-polyethylene glycol (Poloxamer 407). Methanol (CH3OH), dichloromethane (CH2Cl2) and all other organic solvents were of HPLC grade. The human TNBC cell line MDA-MB-231 (HTB-26) and the ER-positive BC cell line MCF-7 (HTB-22) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). Cell culture reagents, including Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) High Glucose and fetal bovine serum (FBS), were purchased from Gibco (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Penicillin–streptomycin solution (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin) was acquired from PAN Biotech (Germany). The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was acquired from Glpbio (USA), and the Annexin V-FITC and 7-AAD were purchased from Miltenyi Biotech and BD Biosciences, respectively.

Determination of TMX

A TMX standard solution was prepared using methanol and analyzed in the range of 190–900 nm with a UV spectrophotometer (Evolution 201, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The maximum absorbance value occurred at 300 nm, which was used to construct the calibration curve. Samples were prepared within the concentration range of 0.0078–0.056 mg/mL, and the absorbance of each solution was measured three times at 300 nm.

In addition, a TMX standard solution was prepared using dichloromethane and analyzed in the range of 190–900 nm. The maximum absorbance value occurred at 254 nm in which the samples in the concentration range of 0.002–0.007 mg/mL were measured three times to construct the calibration curve.

TMX standard solutions prepared in CH3OH having the concentration range of 0.00078- 0.1 mg/mL were analyzed by reversed-phase HPLC (1260 Infinity, Agilent Technologies, USA) in which the C18 column (KP RPPX 5 C18 column) (KP RPPX 5 μm) was used as the stationary phase. A mixture of a phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 3.1)/acetonitrile/methanol with a ratio of 45:50:5 v/v/v was used as the mobile phase with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min at 230 nm [10]. The buffer solution was prepared by dissolving 7.01 g sodium dihydrogen phosphate dihydrate (NaH2PO4.2H2O) in 1000 mL milli-Q water obtained via a water purifier system (Sartorius, Germany), followed by the adjustment of pH to 3.1 by adding phosphoric acid (85%) and then filtered with cellulose acetate 0.2 μm membrane filter (Sartorius–Germany). After filtration, all the components of the mobile phase were sonicated for 10 min before use in the HPLC system. All the chromatographic analyses were performed twice by injecting 20 µL of each sample at 25 °C.

Surfactants and liquid lipids screening for TMX saturation solubility

TMX solubility was studied in distilled water, different surfactants (Gelucire 48/16, Labrasol, Brij 35, Poloxamer 188, Poloxamer 407) and liquid lipids (Capryol 90, Transcutol HP, Labrafac Lipophile, Labrafil M). The present study aimed to investigate suitable surfactants and liquid lipids that could markedly enhance saturation solubility.

Following the preparation of aqueous solutions of surfactants (1%) by dissolving substances in distilled water, 10 mL solutions were placed in vials. 1 mg TMX was added to each vial to obtain the saturated solution (Fig. S1). Vials containing the mixture underwent magnetic stirring at 200 rpm for of 24 h at room temperature. After 24 h, the dispersions were centrifuged (Hermle) at 15,000 rpm for 5 min to pellet undissolved TMX before analysis [11]. The supernatant was diluted with methanol 15 times, and the amount of TMX was assessed using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Evolution 201, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

For TMX solubility of tamoxifen in liquid lipids, 500 µL of liquid lipids were placed in vials and a large amount of TMX (40 mg) was added into those vials. They were mixed at 200 rpm at room temperature for 24 h and then centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 5 min; the supernatant was collected. Capryol 90 and Transcutol HP were diluted with methanol, while Labrafac Lipophile and Labrafil M were diluted with dichloromethane. The amount of dissolved TMX was determined using a UV-vis spectrophotometer with either methanol (for Capryol 90 and Transcutol HP) or dichloromethane (for Labrafac Lipophile and Labrafil M) as blanks (Table 1).

LNP characterization

Particle size and size distribution

The particle size and size distribution were determined using an Anton Paar LiteSizer 500 particle size analyzer. The produced formulations’ particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) were determined by photon correlation spectroscopy a common technique used to ascertain the dimensions of particles within the submicron range. A disposable cuvette served as the measurement cell, and the detection angle was fixed at 90°–175 °. All measurements were conducted at ambient temperature (25 °C ± 0.5 °C). Prior to the measurements, all samples were diluted 30 times with distilled water. For each measurement, the baseline value was around 1.00 ± 0.01. The measurements were triplicated and average and the standard deviation were recorded [51].

Zeta potential

Zeta potential analyses were performed using a Litesizer™ 500 instrument (Anton Paar, Graz, Austria) employing the Laser Doppler Velocimetry method under the same dilution conditions as the particle size and distribution measurements. The measurements were conducted within an Omega cuvette, with the Smoluchowski approximation applied. Each measurement was performed in triplicate, and the mean value was recorded [46].

Particle concentration

Particle concentrations were measured with the DynaPro NanoStar instrument (Wyatt Technology, USA) with a JC-710 Quartz Cuvette. Prior to measurements, the samples were diluted 30 times with distilled water. After the samples were placed in the cuvette, analyses were performed at room temperature (25 °C ± 0.5 °C) for 10 runs. The mean and the standard deviation were recorded.

Morphological investigation

LNP morphological characteristics were investigated by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Talos L120C, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). The requisite quantity of drug-free NLCs, drug-free SLNs, TMX-loaded NLCs, and TMX-loaded SLNs were diluted with distilled water and subsequently deposited onto a carbon-coated TEM copper grid. After air drying, the grid was inserted into the TEM, and images were acquired at multiple magnifications [52].

FT-IR and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis

FT-IR spectroscopy was used to analyze free TMX, NLC-loaded TMX and SLN-loaded TMX. Formulations were scanned within the IR range of 500–4000 cm−1 to compare the TMX, free NLPs and drug loaded LNPs. The analysis was performed using a Nicolet IS10 FT-IR spectrometer (Thermo-Fisher Scientific, USA). The LNP dispersions were lyophilized without using cryoprotectant through the Alpha 1–4 LSC plus freeze-dryer (Lyovapor L-200, Buchi, Switzerland). A sample of 1 mL was transferred to a clear glass vial for freeze-drying. After freezing at − 70 °C, samples were stored at − 60 °C and 0.05 mbar for 2 d [53].

DSC analysis was performed using DSC-60 Plus (Shimadzu 6100). The heating rate was 20 °C/min, and the scan range was 25–400 °C [54].

Encapsulation efficiency and drug loading capacity

Direct and indirect approaches were used to calculate the amount of TMX in the LNPs. First, the LNPs were diluted to 15 mL with distilled water. Then, excess unloaded TMX was removed from the LNPs via ultrafiltration by centrifugation using Amicon® Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter Unit (Mwt cut-off: 10 kDa; Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). After transfer to the centrifuge tubes, the samples were centrifuged (Hermle Z 206 A) at 4000 x g, for 70 min (NLCs) or 45 min (SLNs). Consequently, the amount of TMX loaded in the LNPs was evaluated by analysis of both filtrate and samples which indicated measurements by direct and indirect methods, respectively. The indirect method involved diluting the filtrate with methanol before the analysis to quantify TMX. The direct method involved adding methanol (900 µL) to the sample (100 µL). TMX extraction from the LNPs involved 1 min vortexing, 10,000 rpm centrifugation for 5 min, and supernatant analysis for drug loading quantification. All samples were analyzed by reversed-phase HPLC under the stated conditions in Sect. 2.2.

In vitro release studies

TMX in vitro release from TMX-loaded LNPs was assessed using the dialysis-bag method in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) pH 5.5 and PBS pH 7.4 containing 1% (w/v) Tween 80. A certain amount of TMX-loaded SLNs and NLCs including the same amount of TMX was placed in a dialysis-bag (MWCO 12,000 Da) clamped at both ends and transferred to 60 mL beakers containing medium at 37 °C. In addition to LNPs, free TMX (0.792 mg/mL) was dispersed in the pH 7.4 PBS including 1% tween 80 and transferred to dialysis bag. The experiment was conducted in a water bath maintained at 37 °C with a rotation speed of 100 rpm. At designated time intervals (10, 20, 30 min, and 1, 2, 4, 6, 24, 72, 120, 144 h), 1 mL of the medium was removed and replaced with fresh medium [46]. The samples were HPLC analyzed as described in Sect. 2.2 to determine the amount of TMX present at each time interval.

Stability

The LNPs were stored at 4 ± 1 °C for seven months. The particle size and distribution were examined during storage for long-term stability analysis.

Cell culture studies

The human TNBC cell line MDA-MB-231 (HTB-26, ATCC, USA) and ER + BC cell line MCF-7 (HTB-22, ATCC, USA) were cultured in DMEM High Glucose (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin; PAN Biotech, Germany).

Cytotoxicity

The cytotoxic effects of TMX, TMX-loaded NLC, and TMX-loaded SLN formulations on MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells were assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) following the manufacturer’s instructions as previously reported [55]. Cells were seeded on a 96-well plate at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and incubated overnight to allow for cell attachment. Thereafter, the cells were treated with the respective formulations for 24 and 48 h under standard culture conditions. Following treatment, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were further incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer.

Antitumor efficacy in BC cell lines

MDA-MB-231 cells cultured in 6-well plates were harvested by trypsinization prior to apoptosis analysis. To collect adherent and non-adherent cells (including apoptotic and necrotic cells that detached into the supernatant), the culture medium was first collected into a 15 mL centrifuge tube. The cells were then washed once with 2 mL PBS per well, and the wash solution was added to the same tube containing the culture medium. The remaining adherent cells were detached using 500 µL of 0.05% trypsin-EDTA solution per well and incubated at 37 °C for 3–5 min until complete detachment was observed. Trypsin activity was neutralized by adding 1 mL of complete culture medium containing serum, and the cell suspension was transferred to the same collection tube. The combined cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, and the cell pellet was washed once with PBS to remove residual trypsin and debris.

For apoptosis staining, 1 × 106 cells were washed with 1 mL of 1× Annexin V Binding Buffer and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was completely aspirated, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 100 µL of 1 × Annexin V Binding Buffer. Five microliters of Annexin V conjugate were added to the cell suspension, mixed thoroughly, and incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Following incubation, cells were washed with 1 mL of 1× Annexin V Binding Buffer and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min. After complete removal of the supernatant, the cell pellet was resuspended in 500 µL of 1× Annexin V Binding Buffer per 1 × 106 cells. Immediately prior to the flow-cytometric analysis, 5 µL of 7-AAD was added to each sample.

For flow cytometry analysis, appropriate controls were prepared including unstained cells, single-stained controls (Annexin V-FITC only and 7-AAD only) for compensation settings, and untreated viable cells as negative controls. Compensation was applied prior to acquisition to correct for spectral overlap between the FITC and 7-AAD channels. Data were acquired using a Beckman Coulter flow cytometer and analyzed using CytExpert software. Cell populations were identified using standard quadrant gating as follows: viable cells (Annexin V−/7-AAD−), early apoptotic cells (Annexin V+/7-AAD−), late apoptotic/necrotic cells (Annexin V+/7-AAD+), and necrotic cells (Annexin V−/7-AAD+). A minimum of 10,000 events were acquired for each sample to ensure statistical reliability.

Statistical analysis

The data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of at least three independent experiments. Parametric comparisons between the experimental groups were performed using a two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, USA). Differences were considered statistically significant at P ≤ 0.05 (*), P ≤ 0.01 (**), P ≤ 0.001 (***), and P ≤ 0.0001 (****).

Results and discussion

Determination method for TMX

The TMX calibration curves of tamoxifen in methanol or dichloromethane were established by UV spectroscopy at 300 nm and 254 nm of maximum absorption (λmax), respectively. All measurements were taken in triplicate. The absorbance values were linear within 0.0078–0.056 mg/mL in methanol and 0.002–0.007 mg/mL in dichloromethane. For the former, the calibration curve yielded y = 2.9370x − 0.0095 with R2 of 0.9969, and for the latter, the graph produced y = 16.4500x − 0.0254 with R2 of 0.9989 (Fig. S2a and b). In the studied concentration range, the relative standard deviation was less than 1.12% for methanol and 0.70% for dichloromethane. For methanol measurements, the limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ) were 0.003 and 0.009 mg/mL, respectively. For dichloromethane measurements, the LOD and LOQ were 0.002 and 0.007 mg/mL, respectively.

In entrapment efficiency (EE) and in vitro release studies, the amount of TMX could not be determined quantitatively by UV spectroscopy because the LNPs included more than one compound and these compounds may interfere with TMX absorbance during the measurement. In addition, the amount of TMX might be lower than the detection limit of the spectroscopic method. Hence, the reversed-phase HPLC method was used for the quantitative determination of TMX for further studies. The TMX retention time was 14.24 min, with no overlapping peaks at this time in the analyzed samples (chromatogram, Fig. S3). The calibration curve created using TMX standard solutions showed a linear relationship within 0.00078- 0.1 mg/mL, and the obtained regression equation was y = 44.922x + 4.0578, with a correlation coefficient (R²) of 0.9998 (Fig. S4). The LOD and LOQ were computed as 0.0018 and 0.0054 mg/mL respectively. Similar slopes and responses of calibration curves obtained on different days demonstrated consistent chromatographic performance between days. These findings indicate that the method provides reproducible chromatographic responses for the quantitative determination of TMX.

Evaluation of TMX solubility in the presence of surfactant

Despite its poor intrinsic water solubility, TMX shows increased solubility with surfactant use. Within this framework, the saturation solubility of TMX in distilled water and in 1% solutions of Gelucire 48/16, Brij 35, Poloxamer 188, Labrasol and Poloxamer 407 were investigated. The solubility values were in the order of Gelucire 48/16, Brij 35, Poloxamer 407, Labrasol, water, and Poloxamer 188 from highest to the lowest (Fig. 2a). Although TMX solubility in water was 0.14 mM (equal to 0.052 mg/mL), it can be increased up to 1.10 mM (equal to 0.406 mg/mL) with using Gelucire 48/16 and up to 0.56 mM (equal to 0.208 mg/mL) using Brij 35. Therefore, these two surfactants showed superior effects on TMX water solubility compared with other surfactants.

TMX’s high lipophilicity makes its solubility strongly dependent on the dimensions and amphiphilic configuration of the hydrophobic cores formed by surfactants. As shown in Fig. 2a, Gelucire 48/16 provided the highest solubility enhancement among the tested surfactants and incorporated TMX effectively by forming broad, stable micelles [12]. This is due to its strong amphiphilic structure, which combines glyceride derivatives with long alkyl chains and hydrophilic PEG segments. Conversely, Brij can enhance solubility by providing a substantial hydrophobic core due to the presence of an extended alkyl chain, which can make more interaction with hydrophobic TMX, accompanied by an adequate hydrophilic balance due to the presence of an ethylene oxide chain. Poloxamer 407 can form micelles with its PEO–PPO–PEO triblock structure; however, the volume of the PPO core has a limited capacity to carry large aromatic molecules, such as TMX, resulting in a moderate increase in solubility. In contrast, Labrasol possesses a more dispersed structure, comprising glycerides and PEG esters, resulting in a limited hydrophobic core and only a low to moderate increase in solubility. As anticipated, pure water, devoid of a hydrophobic core, exhibited only a negligible increase in TMX solubility. Despite the structural similarity between Poloxamer 188 and Poloxamer 407, TMX solubility differed markedly between the two. The TMX- solubility difference between Poloxamer 188 and Poloxamer 407 likely reflects differences in repeating the propylene glycol unit number, which increases surfactant hydrophobicity and apparently enhance lipophilic TMX solubility. As a result, the solubility-enhancing power of surfactants largely depends on the hydrophobic core volume, chain length, HLB balance and micelle stability. The ranking in the graph clearly reflects these structural differences.

The solubility of TMX in liquid lipids (Capryol 90, Transcutol HP, Labrafac Lipophile, and Labrafil M) was also evaluated and ordered as follows: Capryol 90, Transcutol HP, Labrafil M and Labrafac Lipophile from highest to the lowest (Fig. 2b). TMX is highly lipophilic and therefore dissolves well in liquid lipids, but its solubility varies depending on lipid polarity. Capryol 90 is the lipophilic excipient that generally offers the highest solubility for TMX, due to its caprylic chain compatible with the lipid phase and its slightly polar propylene glycol derivative head group. Among the tested liquid lipids, Capryol 90 was the only -OH containing lipid, enabling hydrogen bonding with TMX oxygen of and increasing solubility. Transcutol HP often provides medium to high solubility because its semi-polar structure allows it to stabilize TMX in lipophilic and partially polar regions [13]. Labrafil M contains polar and lipophilic segments, so its solubility is generally moderate to high. However, Labrafac Lipophile’s high lipophilicity reduces polar group availability for TMX interaction, yielding moderate solubility TMX lipophilicity supports dissolution, but limited polar interactions constrain solubility.

LNP characterization

As a part of formulation optimization studies, the improvements implemented in the production methodologies aimed to reduce particle size, prevent lipid recrystallization and ensure a more stable lipid structure. Initially, a conventional emulsification–sonication approach was employed; however, the resulting particles exceeded 800 nm in diameter, falling outside the target size range (Table S2). The subsequent method introduced two critical optimizations. The incorporation of a high-speed stirring step at 30,000 rpm for 5 min and the modification of probe sonication parameters (from 70% amplitude for 10 min to 20% amplitude for 15 min) directly influence the droplet breakage efficiency and, consequently, the final particle size of the lipid-based carriers. The application of high-speed stirring introduced an intense shear force prior to sonication, thereby enabling a substantial reduction in the size of coarse emulsion droplets and the creation of a more uniform pre-nano emulsion [14]. This preliminary step served to reduce the workload for the subsequent sonication process, thereby facilitating the formation of smaller, more homogeneously distributed lipid droplets [15].

Altering the sonication conditions also contributed to particle size control. Despite amplitude a reduction from 70% to 20%, the sonication duration was extended to 15 min. Lower amplitude reduces overheating, risk while minimizing localized lipid recrystallization. Conversely, an increase in sonication time can counterbalance the diminished energy per pulse by ensuring a protracted duration of cavitation [16]. This combination resulted in a more controlled process of droplet disruption, preventing droplet coalescence and enabling the formation of smaller, more stable nanoparticles. Combined amplitude reduction and duration extension synergistically reduced droplet size, decreasing particle size and narrowing size distribution (Fig. 2c and d).

Ingredient selection: high TMX solubility in Gelucire 48/16 does not guarantee smaller NLC particle size because particle size control depends on emulsification dynamics, lipid phase viscosity, and surfactant interfacial behavior, not solubility alone [17]. Gelucire’s amphiphilic nature did not provide interface stabilization as effective as classical surfactants, so droplet coalescence may increase during emulsification. In contrast, Brij has lower viscosity, higher HLB value, and stronger surface activity, so it reduces interfacial tension more effectively, leading to the formation of smaller and more stable droplets. Therefore, even though its solubility capacity is higher than Brij’s, particle sizes are typically larger in formulations using Gelucire 48/16 (Table S2). Formulations containing Brij also showed the best results in SLN optimization studies (Fig. S5a–c).

In liquid lipid systems, Transcutol HP reduces viscosity and interfacial tension, disrupts lipid crystallinity and boosts drug distribution collectively yielding smaller particle sizes than other lipid combinations.

Figure 2 shows the formulation particle size (Fig. 2c), PDI (Fig. 2d), and zeta potential (Fig. 2e). SLN formulations form a more regular, rigid matrix due to solid lipid only mixtures, yielding larger particle sizes than NLC systems. Drug incorporation into the lipid matrix initially increased particle size, likely because TMX molecules inserted between lipid chains and expand the particle core. However, the particle size reduction was surprisingly remarkable after TMX increase from 10 to 15 mg. A similar pattern was observed not only in NLCs but also in SLNs. This can be explained by the interaction between the drug and the components that are common to both LNPs. This pattern suggests a more homogeneous drug distribution at high loading ratios, with TMX filling interchain spaces and promoting more regular, compact nanoparticles. Tamoxifen which is a lipophilic drug, was used in the prepared formulation in its base form (not citrate form). In this study, Tamoxifen base can make highly possible physical interaction with acidic lipids present in the prepared formulation, leading to a decrease in the particle size. Lipophilic drugs may alter the internal dynamics of the lipid matrix that lead to smaller, denser particles. Tamoxifen, being a highly lipophilic molecule, may act as a structural modifier within the lipid matrix. At higher concentrations (15 mg), TMX may promote improved packing of lipid chains, reduce internal void, lead to pack more tightly, and make the internal core more condense. Accordingly, it may contribute to the formation of smaller particles.

The drug-free and 15 mg-loaded formulations measured < 200 nm, whereas all other formulations measured < 400 nm. This size range supports passive tumor targeting because the enhanced permeation and retention effect markedly facilitates small particle penetration into the tumor microenvironment [18]. Due to their reduced particle size, these systems can evade uptake by the mononuclear phagocyte system. This leads to prolonged systemic circulation, increasing the likelihood of drug delivery to target tissues [19].

All SLN and NLC formulations were produced with lower than 0.3 PDI (Fig. 2d). The PDI values reflect the uniformity and width of the particle size distribution within the formulation and serve as a particle homogeneity indicator. PDI values below 0.3 in all formulations indicate that the nanoparticles have a narrow size distribution and that the systems are homogeneous. The literature indicates that PDI values below 0.3 reflect good colloidal stability and long-term physical stability [20]. This indicated that the tendency for particle collisions and aggregation was low and that dispersion could be maintained in a stable manner. The PDI values of all formulations were found to be below 0.3 and no statistically significant differences were observed among the groups. The PDI values were similar for both LNP systems, primarily because they were produced under similar conditions. Specifically, both formulations were subjected to probe sonication following homogenization. Therefore, it was concluded that no significant difference in PDI was observed between the formulations, likely as a consequence of the production process.

The zeta potential values were negative regardless of the lipid structure or TMX presence (Fig. 2e). Increasing the TMX amount in NLCs increased the relative negative charge. High absolute zeta potential values enhance colloidal stability by strengthening electrostatic repulsive forces between particles. However, in the SLN, as TMX increased, the zeta potential approached zero. In this state, the structure exhibits weak colloidal stability [21].

FT-IR and DSC

Figure 3i shows the FT-IR spectra of pure TMX, drug-free, and TMX-loaded SLN and NLC formulations. Drug free and TMX-loaded LNP spectra showed no appearance of new peaks or disappearance of observed peaks, indicating no chemical interaction between the TMX and the formulations. In the FT-IR spectrum of pure TMX, the peaks observed at 1245 & 1072, 1609 and 2750–2850 cm−1 were assigned to C–N & C–O-C, C=C and C-H stretching, respectively [22]. These peaks were absent in drug-free and TMX-loaded LNP FT-IR spectra because TMX active bonds were no longer available for stretching within the formulations. These observations lead to the physical dispersion of TMX into the formulations. The FT-IR spectra of the loaded formulations were largely dominated by broad aliphatic C–H stretching bands (≈ 2850–2920 cm−1) belonging to the lipid matrix and C–O–C stretching bands in the 1000–1200 cm−1 range corresponding to the presence of Brij 35 in both formulations (and Transcutol HP in only NLC) [23, 24]. The high degree of similarity between the drug-free and drug-loaded SLN/NLC spectra indicates TMX surface nonlocalization and lipid matrix entrapment via physical dispersion rather than surface adsorption. Furthermore, DSC analysis demonstrated the melting of pure TMX at 99 °C, but this peak disappeared in the analysis of TMX-loaded LNPs (Fig. 3g and h). These effects result from the change in crystalline nature of pure TMX into an amorphous structure upon loading into formulations. Overall, these observations support drug incorporation into LNPs via physical entrapment, most likely through TMX-LNP hydrophobic interactions.

EE and drug loading capacity

The EE and drug-loading capacity (DL) were calculated from HPLC-based TMX quantification after LNP purification. In this study, direct and indirect methods were applied to determine the loading amount of TMX in LNP formulations. Unencapsulated TMX was removed from the LNPs via centrifugal ultrafiltration (Amicon® Ultra-15). In the direct method, TMX was extracted from the LNPs by vortex mixing. EE was 97.15% in NLCs and 94.20% in SLNs among 10 mg loaded LNPs in NLC formulations. However, repeatable results could not be obtained for 15 mg loaded LNPs. Furthermore, the cumulative recovery of TMX from direct and indirect methods did not reach 100%. This discrepancy suggests a potential underestimation of the drug content in the 15 mg TMX-loaded LNPs, likely due to incomplete drug extraction during the direct measurement process, leading to apparent false-negative results (data not shown). In the direct method, extraction of TMX from nanoparticles after brief treatment with methanol may not be sufficient, especially for drugs such as TMX, which exhibit high hydrophobic properties and can form strong interactions with the lipid matrix. In LNPs formulations, the solid or semi-solid lipid core may limit the drug’s transition into the solvent phase, which may result in the drug’s actual amount being measured lower than it actually was during analysis.

The indirect method relies on the quantitative determination of free (unbound) TMX in the filtrate phase. Analyzing the free drug effectively separated from nanoparticles by ultrafiltration allows for an indirect but more reliable calculation of the loaded drug amount. Therefore, the indirect method was preferred in this study for calculating EE and DL and was used as the basis for evaluating the results. This approach minimized measurement errors arising from strong drug–lipid interactions, as it did not require complete extraction of the drug from the lipid matrix, particularly, for high drug content.

In vitro release study

In vitro release studies revealed differences between SLN and NLC formulations and demonstrated that the lipid matrix structure plays a critical role in TMX release behavior. Figure 4b illustrates the cumulative release profiles of TMX from LNPs, demonstrating distinct release behaviors depending on the carrier system. An in vitro drug release study revealed that free TMX exhibited limited solubility due to its hydrophobic nature, and the release did not reach 10% at pH 7.4 even after 6 days. At pH 5.5, although the release slightly increased, it remained below 20%. This observation is consistent with the literature and can be explained by the fact that TMX is a weak base and is soluble only under acidic conditions [25].

In contrast, SLNs and NLCs enhanced the release of TMX as a result of improved aqueous solubility. No significant release was observed from either SLN or NLC within the first 2 h regardless of the medium pH; however, a slow and sustained release profile was observed up to 144 h. For both systems, the release of TMX reached up to 20% at pH 7.4 for 144 h. At pH 5.5, a significantly higher release was observed, exceeding 30% for SLNs and close to 50% for NLCs. Higher TMX release from NLCs than that from SLNs release likely reflects a more disordered internal structure. The presence of liquid lipids within the solid lipid matrix in NLCs caused disruption of the crystal order and the formation of defects within the matrix, which facilitated drug diffusion from the matrix. Consequently, TMX showed a higher cumulative release over a longer duration. In contrast, SLN formulations have a more regular and tightly packed crystalline lipid matrix. This structure strengthened drug retention within the lipid core and limited the release rate. Accordingly, SLN release amounts plateaued over time, and total TMX release remained lower than in the NLC formulations. These findings suggest that LNPs may exhibit pH-responsive tumor selectivity and indicate their potential to prolong the therapeutic effect of TMX, minimizing dose-related toxic effects.

Stability

The physical stability of 15 mg TMX loaded formulations was evaluated during 7 months of storage at 4 °C by monitoring particle size and PDI (Fig. 4c and d). Particle size remained stable across formulations except drug-free SLNs. The PDI values also remained stable without meaningful variation. Although the zeta potential values of the formulations were close to neutral, the nanoparticles remained physically stable during storage for 7 months. This behavior may be attributed to the presence of the nonionic surfactant Brij 35, whose polyoxyethylene chains likely provide steric stabilization by forming a barrier around the particles. Such steric hindrance can effectively prevent aggregation even in systems with limited electrostatic repulsion [26]. Consistent with this interpretation, no significant change in particle size was observed during 7 months of storage.

Cytotoxicity

The viability effects of TMX against BC cell lines were evaluated using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. In ER-positive MCF-7 cells, the half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) were determined as 25.28 µM (95% CI: 19.41–33.00) at 24 h and 18.09 µM (95% CI: 15.25–21.65) at 48 h (Fig. 5a). In triple-negative MDA-MB-231 cells, the observed IC50 values were 41.49 µM (95% CI: 36.36–47.83) at 24 h and 27.21 µM (95% CI: 24.83–29.79) at 48 h (Fig. 5b). Non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals between MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 at both timepoints indicated that TMX exhibited significantly higher potency in ER-positive cells. A time-dependent reduction in IC50 was also supported by non-overlapping confidence intervals in MDA-MB-231 cells between 24 h and 48 h. In MCF-7 cells, overlapping confidence intervals between 24 h and 48 h indicated that the time-dependent IC50 reduction did not reach statistical significance under the present analytical framework, although a trend toward decreased IC50 at 48 h was observed. This observation is consistent with the established mechanism of TMX as a selective estrogen receptor modulator, where ER-positive cells exhibit greater sensitivity due to ER-mediated antiproliferative signaling [27]. TMX showed effective cytotoxicity on ER positive BC cells at lower doses and on TNBC cells at higher doses. Given that TNBC currently lacks validated endocrine-targeted therapies and represents a setting of high unmet clinical need, subsequent formulation efficacy and apoptosis studies were focused on MDA-MB-231 cells to evaluate the potential of TMX-loaded LNPs in this context.

To distinguish intrinsic carrier effects from drug-mediated activity, drug-free (blank) nanocarriers were examined (blank NLC and blank SLN; denoted as NLC-DF and SLN-DF). Surprisingly, initial assessments conducted under dosing-matched conditions corresponding to the IC50 and 2 × IC50 settings used for the drug-loaded formulations, but without particle-density normalization, revealed low apparent viability. Unexpectedly, a high rate of cell death was observed across all formulations after 24 h of incubation without TMX. At 24 h, viability values were 21.4 ± 1.0% (NLC-DF–IC50), 17.2 ± 0.3% (NLC-DF–2×IC50), 15.9 ± 1.0% (SLN-DF–IC50), and 13.6 ± 1.3% (SLN-DF–2 × IC50) (Fig. 6a) .A similar trend was observed at 48 h, with viability remaining below 20% across all groups (Fig. 6b). These reductions were attributed to unequal particle exposure (“particle overload”) rather than to genuine carrier-mediated toxicity, based on particle concentration analyses. TMX loading into LNPs reduced the number of carriers in the formulation, triggering unexpected cytotoxicity. A 10- to 30-fold difference was observed in the particle concentrations (the number of particles per mL; as seen in Table 3) between the drug-loaded and blank carriers. Considering that the high mortality observed at high concentrations might be due to particle-induced necrosis, the formulations were diluted 10- to 30-fold before repeating the cytotoxicity studies. This ensured that the particle count remained consistent across all tested formulations.

To address this confounding factor, drug-free formulations were re-evaluated following particle-density normalization. In marked contrast to the non-normalized groups, viability values at 24 h approximated control levels for most conditions (102.9 ± 1.4% for NLC-DF–IC50, 97.6 ± 1.9% for NLC-DF–2 × IC50, and 99.5 ± 1.2% for SLN-DF–IC50), whereas a moderate decrease was observed for SLN-DF–2 × IC50 (81.1 ± 1.2%) (Fig. 6a). Furthermore, at 48 h, no reduction in viability was detected in the particle-density–normalized groups; values above 100% were measured (110.1–122.0%) (Fig. 6b). Because the CCK-8 assay reflects metabolic activity, values above 100% were not interpreted as increased cell number and were considered compatible with assay behavior and experimental variability under non-cytotoxic exposure conditions. Overall, these findings indicate that particle-density normalization was required for accurate interpretation of blank-carrier biocompatibility.

Following the characterization of blank-carrier behavior, TMX-loaded LNPs were assessed in MDA-MB-231 cells at concentrations corresponding to the IC50 and 2 × IC50 of free TMX. Both formulations induced a pronounced reduction in cell viability. At 24 h, viability decreased to 16.1 ± 0.7% for NLC-15–IC50 and 14.6 ± 1.0% for NLC-15–2 × IC50, while SLN-15 produced comparable effects (15.9 ± 0.5% and 12.2 ± 0.3%, respectively) (Fig. 6c). Viability reduction was maintained at 48 h, with viability values remaining suppressed between 15.7% and 23.5% across all treatment groups (Fig. 6d).

Overall, these results indicate that TMX-loaded NLC-15 and SLN-15 produced marked viability reduction against MDA-MB-231 cells, while the blank-carrier experiments demonstrated that particle-density normalization constituted an essential control for correct interpretation of carrier biocompatibility.

The time-dependent antiproliferative activity of free TMX was observed in both BC models, with decreasing IC50 values from 24 h to 48 h in MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells. The higher IC50 values measured in MDA-MB-231 relative to MCF-7 were considered consistent with clinical evidence indicating that adjuvant TMX yields substantial benefit primarily in estrogen receptor (ER)-positive disease, whereas little or no benefit has been reported in ER-negative disease at the trial level [28]. In this context, the clear activity detected in the triple-negative MDA-MB-231 model was considered informative because TNBC has historically lacked validated endocrine targets and has been treated largely with chemotherapy, reflecting a more limited repertoire of targeted options compared with ER-positive disease [29, 30].

Similar marked reductions in cell viability were recorded for blank carriers without particle-density normalization and drug-loaded LNPs. After particle-density normalization, viability returned close to control levels for most conditions, indicating that the initial reductions were more compatible with particle overload than with intrinsic chemical cytotoxicity of the lipid excipients [31, 32]. Following clarification of blank carrier behavior, TMX-loaded nanocarriers (NLC-15 and SLN-15) produced profound reductions in MDA-MB-231 viability at concentrations matched to the IC50 and 2×IC50 of free TMX (lower than 25%, for all formulations). Given that particle-density–normalized blank carriers did not reduce viability under comparable exposure design, the observed effects were attributed primarily to the encapsulated drug rather than to the carrier system. However, despite TMX-alone viability reduction to 50% at this dose, carrier system administration markedly amplified cytotoxicity. This enhancement may have resulted from potentially improved cellular uptake, increased intracellular drug retention and/or more efficient drug–cell interactions facilitated by the LNP delivery system, although direct uptake quantification was not performed in the present study.

While direct ER-mediated pathway interrogation was not performed in the present study, ERα-independent TMX actions have been documented in ER-negative BC cells, including MDA-MB-231, through pathways separable from classical ERα antagonism. In particular, TMX-induced apoptosis in ER-negative models has been linked to CIP2A inhibition, PP2A activity restoration, and phospho-Akt signaling suppression, with pathway rescue experiments supporting causality [33]. Oxidative stress–associated mechanisms have also been reported as contributors to TMX-induced cytotoxicity in BC cells, supporting a framework in which pleiotropic, ER-independent stress responses may underlie activity in TNBC contexts [34]. Accordingly, the present findings in MDA-MB-231 were considered compatible with the reported ER-independent mechanisms, potentially amplified by delivery-related changes in effective cellular exposure.

Several TNBC-relevant modifiers were considered testable contributors to response heterogeneity. ERβ expression has been reported in TNBC with substantial heterogeneity and methodological caveats, implying that ERβ status and isoform context should be measured rather than assumed when ERβ-linked interpretations are proposed [35]. In parallel, MDA-MB-231 is widely used as a TP53-mutant (R280K) TNBC model, supporting the possibility that p53-contextual stress adaptation could influence responsiveness [36, 37]. Notably, ERβ1 has been reported to oppose mutant p53 transcriptional programs that promote invasiveness in TNBC cells, providing a mechanistic intersection between ERβ and mutant p53 biology that could be explored in follow-up profiling [38]. Finally, lipid-handling programs may influence interactions with lipid nanocarriers; LDLR has been implicated in TNBC growth of and may shape lipid uptake dynamics, thereby motivating future uptake-focused assays and lipidomic-informed analyses to connect lipid state with nanocarrier responsiveness [39].

Drug free and drug-loaded LNPs showed cytotoxic effects in cell culture studies, despite the absence of any toxic lipids or excipients in the formulation. However, particle concentration analyses revealed that drug free-LNPs, in particular, were characterized by a very high particle count. At such high particle concentrations, cells are simultaneously exposed to an excessive number of nanoparticles. This situation was consistent with necrotic cell death, characterized by the rapid disruption of membrane integrity and metabolic collapse, rather than controlled cell death. Indeed, the complete disappearance of cytotoxic effects when the dilution ratio of drug free LNPs was increased strongly supports that the observed cell death was a phenomenon related to high particle numbers rather than chemical toxicity. In contrast, the cytotoxicity observed in drug-loaded LNPs with lower particle densities resulted from the pharmacological effect of the encapsulated drug rather than the carrier system. These findings demonstrate that particle concentration is a critical parameter in in vitro cytotoxicity assessments of lipid-based nanocarriers as much as particle size and composition.

Lipid nanocarriers enhance the proapoptotic efficacy of TMX

The proapoptotic efficacy of free TMX and TMX-loaded lipid nanocarriers was evaluated using Annexin V–FITC/7-Aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD) dual staining followed by flow-cytometric quadrant analysis. The cell populations were categorized as follows: H1-LL (Annexin V−/7-AAD−) indicating live cells; H1-LR (Annexin V+/7-AAD−) representing early apoptosis; H1-UR (Annexin V+/7-AAD+) indicating late apoptosis/secondary necrosis; and H1-UL (Annexin V−/7-AAD+) representing necrosis.

In the control group, the population was predominantly localized in the live-cell quadrant (88.86 ± 5.46%), with minor fractions distributed in early apoptosis (9.60 ± 5.02%), late apoptosis (0.94 ± 1.63%), and necrosis (0.60 ± 0.74%) (Fig. 7a). Upon treatment with free TMX, a substantial shift toward apoptosis was observed; the viable cell fraction decreased to 53.48 ± 5.69%, while apoptotic populations increased markedly, particularly in the early apoptotic stage (35.98 ± 7.14%) and late apoptotic stage (9.32 ± 0.59%). Necrosis remained negligible (1.23 ± 0.86%) (Fig. 7b).

Treatment with lipid-based formulations further accentuated the apoptotic response. The NLC formulation induced a comparable distribution pattern, with 37.70 ± 5.11% live cells, 60.15 ± 7.40% early apoptotic cells, and 2.08 ± 2.28% late apoptotic cells, while necrosis remained minimal (0.07 ± 0.01%) (Fig. 7c). Similarly, in the SLN-treated group, cell viability was reduced to 29.37 ± 0.01%, accompanied by a pronounced increase in early apoptosis (68.28 ± 2.10%) and a moderate late-apoptotic fraction (1.80 ± 1.92%) (Fig. 7d).

When total apoptosis was defined as the sum of early and late apoptotic events, the apoptotic fraction increased from 10.54% in the control group to 45.30% with free TMX. This effect was further enhanced in the nanocarrier groups, reaching 62.23% for NLC and 70.08% for SLN (Fig. 7e). These results demonstrate that the lipid nanocarriers significantly potentiated the proapoptotic effect of TMX compared to the free drug, with cell death predominantly mediated through early apoptotic pathways.

In the Annexin V–FITC/7-AAD assay, the externalization of phosphatidylserine to the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane is recognized as an early hallmark of apoptosis, detected via Annexin V binding [40]. Concurrently, membrane integrity is assessed via 7-Aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD) exclusion. While cellular uptake of 7-AAD is interpreted as a loss of membrane integrity, the combined staining protocol allows for the differentiation of viable (Annexin V−/7-AAD−), early apoptotic (Annexin V+/7-AAD−), and late apoptotic/secondary necrotic (Annexin V+/7-AAD+) populations. Primary necrosis is typically inferred when 7-AAD positivity occurs without Annexin V binding (Annexin V−/7-AAD+) [41]. Within this interpretive framework, the distributions observed in the present dataset were dominated by Annexin V–positive populations with a comparatively limited 7-AAD-only component. Consequently, an apoptosis-forward phenotype was supported rather than a pattern driven primarily by acute membrane rupture.

The predominance of the early-apoptotic quadrant relative to the late-apoptotic quadrant was considered biologically plausible in terms of apoptosis kinetics. Phosphatidylserine exposure has been described as preceding overt membrane permeabilization, creating a temporal lag between initial Annexin V positivity and subsequent 7-AAD uptake as apoptosis progresses toward late stages and/or secondary necrosis [42]. Accordingly, the observed “early apoptosis–weighted” distribution is regarded as compatible with sampling at an early-to-intermediate window of the death program, rather than being taken as evidence that late apoptosis is absent [43]. The Annexin V/7-AAD readout is also time-sensitive; prolonged post-staining delays can artificially increase apparent 7-AAD positivity, necessitating strict adherence to handling protocols to ensure accurate quadrant interpretation [44].

A minor necrotic fraction was detected across conditions, and a modestly higher 7-AAD-only component was noted in specific formulation groups. Importantly, this observation should be contextualized with the cytotoxicity findings. Since particle-density–normalized blank carriers demonstrated biocompatibility in the CCK-8 assays, the cell death profile observed here is attributed primarily to the intracellular delivery of TMX rather than intrinsic carrier toxicity or physical stress.

Consistent with established interpretative frameworks, the marked dominance of Annexin V–positive populations over the 7-AAD-only fraction provides robust evidence for an apoptosis-driven mechanism [45]. Consequently, the flow cytometric profile indicates that the tested TMX-loaded formulations induce cell death predominantly via regulated apoptosis, largely capturing the cell population during the early stages of the process at the analyzed time point. The consistent directionality of the apoptotic response across treatment groups, together with the concordance with the CCK-8 viability data, supports the biological plausibility of the observed proapoptotic enhancement. Further in vivo validation is warranted to advance translational understanding of these findings.

Overall, concerns regarding the adverse effects of drugs persist and have not been fully resolved despite numerous attempts toward the treatment of breast cancer. Nanodrug delivery systems are capable of delivering lower concentrations of TMX to tumors over a prolonged period of time, limiting its dose-dependent toxicity, and specifically targeting to the tumors, obtaining higher accuracy and minimal off-target toxicity. Most previously reported TMX nanoformulations have been evaluated in estrogen receptor-positive (ER+) breast cancer models, particularly MCF-7 cells. In contrast, our study specifically focuses on triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC, MDA-MB-231), which is a more aggressive subtype with limited therapeutic options. Furthermore, our study provides a systematic and direct comparison between SLNs and NLCs under identical experimental conditions. This allows for a clearer understanding of how lipid matrix composition influences physicochemical properties, drug release, and biological activity on TNBC.

Another major novel finding of this study is that drug incorporation directly affects nanoparticle concentration, resulting in a 10–30-fold decrease in particle number upon TMX loading. To the best of our knowledge, this phenomenon has not been previously reported in any lipid based nanoparticles except exosomes. This observation challenges the common assumption that nanoparticle concentration is independent of drug loading and introduces a previously overlooked parameter in nanocarrier design. Unlike prior studies, we observed that drug-free lipid nanoparticles can induce cytotoxicity at their original forms which may present high particle concentrations. This finding highlights the importance of considering particle concentration as an independent biological factor, rather than attributing all effects solely to the encapsulated drug. This represents a critical and previously underexplored safety aspect.

A comparative overview of previously reported TMX nanoformulations is provided in Supplementary Table S4, which highlights the distinctions and advantages of the present system [46–50]. Taken together, these findings indicate that the novelty of this study lies not only in formulation development but also in providing new mechanistic insight and enhanced biological relevance, particularly in the context of TNBC and nanoparticle concentration-dependent effects.

Conclusion

TMX is a drug frequently used in hormone-positive BC, but its poor aqueous solubility and nonselective tissue distribution markedly limit its bioavailability and adversely affect its side-effect profile. Lipid-based carrier systems such as SLNs and NLCs have been reported to improve drug delivery, and the present in vitro findings suggest that such carriers may enhance the cytotoxic and proapoptotic activity of TMX against TNBC cells.

For this purpose, TMX-loaded SLN and NLC formulations were developed in this study, and optimization studies were conducted with respect to formulation composition and production methods. They were also comparatively investigated by characterization studies. Although these systems were also expected to be effective in hormone-positive BC, this aspect was excluded from the study scope, and the effects of these formulations on TNBC were evaluated instead.

For the first time, an association between particle concentration and cytotoxicity was established. The findings demonstrated that even if the formulation composition was considered safe, particle number itself may induce cytotoxic effects, highlighting the necessity of evaluating the impact of control formulations on cell viability.

While TMX alone was effective in TNBC cells only at higher doses, its incorporation into SLNs and NLCs markedly enhanced its cytotoxic activity against cancer cells and promoted apoptosis. The findings suggest that the developed LNPs enhanced the in vitro cytotoxic and proapoptotic activity of TMX in TNBC cells. Translating these findings warrants further in vivo validation.
