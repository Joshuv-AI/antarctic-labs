Enhancing perinatal care in Rwanda: building quality improvement into system strengthening
Authors: Lisine Tuyisenge, Jean-Petit Habonimana, Alexis Nsengiyumva, Francois Regis Cyiza, Emmanuel Manzi, Samson Desie, Alexandra Pledge, Sarah McMurtrie, Clemence Muzard-Banes, Marie Bontoux, Sebastian Taylor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRwanda has achieved substantial gains in maternal, newborn and child health over the last two decades. But reduction in neonatal mortality has slowed, with most deaths now occurring in hospitals and health centres. Mortality is mainly caused by prematurity, perinatal asphyxia, infection and gaps in quality of care. From 2017 to 2023, the Rwanda Pediatric Association and the Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health Global, in partnership with UNICEF, the Ministry of Health and Rwanda Biomedical Center, implemented a multiphase programme across 21 hospitals designed to enhance perinatal care, working intensively with nurses, midwives and doctors, integrating in situ clinical training and mentorship with hospital-led quality improvement (QI) processes. Using structured facility baseline assessments, programme-specific quality indicators and Health Management Information System data, we evaluated changes specifically in neonatal quality of care and mortality at 12 hospitals (selected for the original programme phase) where continuous neonatal data were available for the whole period. Substantial improvements were observed in core indicators of quality of care and mortality among newborns admitted to neonatal units decreased from 11.8% in 2017 to 6.2% in 2023 (a 47.5% reduction). QI processes also exposed systemic constraints—including workforce rotation, drug and equipment procurement variability, infrastructure limitations, deficiencies in equipment maintenance and gaps in referral coordination—that shape the feasibility and sustainability of clinical improvements.Keywords: Neonatology, Health services research, Low and Middle Income Countries, Child Health, Health Policy

What is new

Long-term partnership between professional paediatric bodies (the Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health (RCPCH Global) in the UK and the Rwanda Pediatric Association (RPA) in Rwanda), supported by UNICEF and government departments, developed a continuous multiyear programme which integrated multiple quality improvement (QI) interventions across the perinatal continuum of care at multiple secondary facilities in Rwanda. The programme enabled localised real-world assessment of sustainability/scalability of evidence-based QI concepts/models, both for clinical uptake and policy adoption.

Key statements

In Rwanda, following a global pattern, neonatal mortality reduction has been decelerating. As a result, government and partners have committed to enhancing quality of facility-based neonatal and perinatal care. The programme described here is a partnership between RCPCH, RPA, UNICEF, the Rwanda Biomedical Center (RBC) and Rwandan Ministry of Health. It presents quantitative outcomes of a multiyear, multisite secondary (hospital) perinatal QI intervention. Rwanda partners have, progressively, taken the lead in intervention strategy, design, measurement and scaling.

Background

Rwanda has become something of a byword for effective health and human development in sub-Saharan Africa over the last three decades—with an average 8% Gross Domestic Product growth over the last 20 years, substantial improvement in key determinants of population health and life expectancy rising from 46.9 to 69.6 years between 2000 and 2022.1 2 Over the same period, Rwanda has achieved some of the most impressive improvements in maternal, newborn and child health (MNCH) in modern history. Between 2000 and 2025, maternal mortality declined from 1071 to 149 per 100 000 live births, under-5 child mortality from 196 to 36 deaths per 1000 live births and neonatal mortality from 44 to 17 deaths per 1000 live births.1 3

However, in the last decade, the rate of reduction in maternal and neonatal mortality has slowed, with neonatal survival decelerating substantially after 2014–2015.4 5 As under-5 mortality has fallen across low- and middle-income countries, more complex issues around neonatal survival and development—in particular for smaller, preterm and sicker babies—have risen.6 In 2025, neonatal mortality in Rwanda (0–28 days) made up 47% of child deaths under age 5.1 7 8

Most deliveries in Rwanda are in hospitals or health centres, reaching 93% after 2021, and most maternal and newborn deaths now occur in hospitals—respectively, 83% and 85.9% between 2019 and 2023. Most newborn deaths occur in the early neonatal period, most on the day of delivery.9,11 In 2021, the main causes of neonatal death in Rwanda were complications associated with prematurity (46%), perinatal asphyxia (22.7%), infection (10%) and congenital abnormality. According to death audit cycles, the major contributory factors were ‘inadequate quality of care/workforce skills, non-adherence to guidelines and protocols and non-timely referrals’, as well as delays in care-seeking, delays in reaching higher-level facilities, insufficient follow-up during labour and inadequate newborn case management.12,14

These findings suggest that further investment in facility-based maternal and newborn care is still needed.15 Most recent Rwandan National Health Sector Strategies (respectively, 2019–2024 and 2025–2029) place workforce development as top-priority, with a particular emphasis on midwife-led and nurse-led care, improving perinatal care quality, supported through ‘cost-effective competence-based in-service training and mentorship including both in situ and virtual models of low dose-high frequency practical skills development’.16 17

Programme design and data

RCPCH Global has worked in partnership with the RPA for over a decade, supported by UNICEF and under the direction of the Rwandan Ministry of Health and RBC. In 2017, RCPCH Global and RPA were invited by the Rwandan Ministry of Health and UNICEF to develop a programme to improve the care for newborns in 12 neonatal units (NNUs) in district and provincial hospitals within and outside Kigali—the ‘Rwanda Neonatal Care Programme’—by providing practical training delivered in situ in trainees’ own hospitals, using hands-on ward-based teaching, mentorship and QI initiatives.

Rwandan clinicians were recruited as programme ‘champions’ in each hospital (where they continued to work full time with protected hours to provide training and implement QI interventions). They became, over time, the programme’s lynchpin, leading on data collection and analysis (including through maternal, perinatal and child death surveillance reviews (MPCDSR)). They also helped design QI initiatives, as well as developing into a clinical network between hospitals which were grouped into ‘clusters’ through regular meetings to compare challenges and share results. Champions were supported in the first phase by volunteer doctors and nurses recruited from the UK and seconded to hospitals for 6–12 months. These were, in turn, supported directly (in person and remotely) by a small group of senior consultant paediatricians and paediatric nurses from RPA and RCPCH who conducted joint quarterly field visits to review implementation and engage with hospital managers. Over time, with encouragement from MoH and RBC, the role of international professionals was reduced, shifting to recruitment of local Rwandan mentors working peripatetically with champions across facilities, and with increasing technical support on programme oversight and data analysis led by RPA.

The programme drew on evidence-based low-cost/high-impact neonatal care protocols designed for low/middle-income settings, and adapted to local Rwandan conditions, recognising that QI and system strengthening initiatives need to be as parsimonious as possible—closely fitted to existing facility processes and resources, delivered as far as possible without recourse to unsustainable external inputs.18,25 Rather than focusing on ‘stove-pipe’ interventions which concentrate on a single specific area of knowledge, clinical skill, equipment improvement or infrastructure, the programme was designed to integrate action across these dimensions. ‘Classroom’ teaching was combined with in-situ practical training and clinical mentorship on the ward, itself complemented by QI initiatives (including a range of relatively simple change interventions such as positioning of nurses’ desks to improve monitoring, strategic repositioning of mobile hand basins to reduce potential cross contamination, systematic tagging of the newborns with name and date of birth, regular refresher trainings and drills on critical skills, and review of re-stocking system) brought together in rolling ‘Hospital Improvement Plans’.26 27

Measurable objectives in the original RNC Programme included reduction in neonatal mortality and improvement in critical aspects of care quality including: recognition, resuscitation and management of the preterm/sick newborn; routine care for well newborns; reduction in hypothermia; management of hypoglycaemia and improved use of oxygen and respiratory support, antibiotics and intravenous fluids; improved infection prevention and control practices and management of sepsis; increased uptake of Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC); and improved safety at discharge. Given the critical role of parental knowledge in managing post-discharge risks, the programme aimed to maximise opportunities to engage with caregivers in supporting and, where possible, leading on delivery of care for their babies.28,34

Baseline measures were set during standardised ‘facility assessments’ in the initial implementation period. Quality of care (input) and mortality (outcome) operational data were sourced through the Rwandan Health Management Information System (HMIS), with permission from RBC, supported by a data manager, and with all data held in-country by RPA. Where HMIS did not record data points, in particular those relating to measurement of care quality, additional programme-specific indicators were created to fill gaps, and data on these were collected regularly by programme champions in each hospital. Care quality was captured through mixed methods, including assessment of knowledge and clinical skills through periodic scenario assessments, as well as retrospective audit of clinical practice (relative to Rwandan National Neonatal Care protocol) derived from randomly selected case notes (n=10) per month per NNU. Mortality on NNUs was captured through official data at facility and, over time, via HMIS.

Results

At its height, the programme covered 21 hospitals (almost half of the government hospitals in the country), delivering a wide range of maternal and neonatal care quality interventions. Here, though, we report results for the 12 originally selected hospitals giving continuous data between 2017 and 2023, with results relating to neonatal care quality and newborn mortality.

Quality of care

The quality-of-care indices are summarised in table 1. Between 2017 and 2023, staff knowledge and skills (recognition of the ‘sick baby’ and newborn resuscitation scenarios) improved—for resuscitation more strongly among nurses from a lower baseline (p=0.017). Hypothermia on NNUs fell substantially (p<0.001) (although hypothermia on admission fell more slowly (p=0.003), indicating wider challenges in hospital location and physical layout). Uptake of KMC for eligible babies rose strongly (p=0.003). Two areas of intervention—intravenous fluids and antibiotic usage—yielded non-significant results. Newborns identified with sepsis risk receiving correct dose of antibiotics improved but from a very high baseline, likely reflecting the widespread practice of prescribing antibiotics for virtually all NNU newborns as a precautionary measure, given the lack of laboratory testing capacity available and pressure on clinicians to mitigate any avoidable risk of neonatal clinical deterioration or death. While prescription of intravenous fluids rate improved, the degree of fluctuation over time meant that improvement did not appear as significant (table 1).

Neonatal mortality

During the period under review, there were 69 283 newborn infants admitted to the NNU. Overall mortality fell from 13.7% (n=8308) in 2016, before the start of the programme, to 11.8% in 2017, the first year of implementation (n=8240), and to 6.2% in 2023 (n=10 163, p<0.001). Variation in overall mortality rate change between hospitals over the period (HMIS data, 2016–2023)—from 79% to 21% of the baseline (2016) with the median of 51.7%—likely reflects small sample numbers in some hospitals, as well as natural volatility in neonatal death and idiosyncratic issues in individual facilities, as well as continuing challenges in wider social determinants of maternal/newborn health. Measured at catchment level (newborn deaths per 1000 live births in administrative districts served by programme facilities), mortality fell more modestly by 20% (p≤0.001), suggesting that the main programme impact was on hospital outcomes, with some potentially positive (but lesser) impact on wider population, again pointing to remaining challenges in creating a more joined-up mortality reduction strategy incorporating public/community and clinical/facility-based health (figure 1).

Mortality was highest among babies under 1 kg birth weight, changing only modestly from 80.5% to 75.6% between 2017 and 2022. Mortality rate among newborns between 1 kg and 2.49 kg fell most, from 17.1% to 10.8%; and among babies 2.5 kg and over from 7.3% to 4.8%.

Discussion

This multisited 6-year rolling programme of integrated interventions, focusing on improving the quality of neonatal (and ultimately perinatal) care in Rwanda’s network of provincial and district hospitals and health centres, produced some positive outcomes with evidence of sustained effect. This is consistent with similar initiatives undertaken in other countries within the East Africa region—emphasising the importance of context, and the critical need to fit interventions within the local health care system.35 36 Uptake of programme initiatives, led by Champions with support from hospital managers, and backed by active Ministry policy, were key ingredients in the implementation of a technical model advanced by the partnership of two professional paediatric partner agencies—avoiding the trap of short-run, externalised technical training intervention in favour of combined in-service training, practical mentorship and local data-driven QI.37 38

In reporting positive changes in healthcare quality and outcome within clinical facilities, it is important to recognise the contributory impact of wider social determinants on health of girls, women, mothers and babies. Over the last two decades, Rwanda has seen significant progress towards universal access in education and gender equity, falling fertility and rapid reduction in maternal mortality, backed by significant investment in the community health worker cadre, skilled attendance and/or institutional delivery accounting for above 90% of births, reinforced by a rigorous practice of maternal death audits driven from the highest levels of political and administrative government. We recognise that facility-level care is only one component of a truly integrated social health and welfare system, where delivery of day-to-day clinical care becomes, itself, a process of practice reflection, using localised data to identify and strengthen gaps or weaknesses in underlying system-level factors.

Conclusions

Reduction in mortality among newborn infants is a welcome result in all countries—especially in low-income countries with high mortality rates. Improved quality-of-care indicators are also reassuring. But there is a risk that quantitative results come to dominate and displace important opportunities to understand the true complexity of programmes and processes; the deeply structured and often highly nuanced administrative, financial, infrastructure, workforce, procurement and supervision politics, policies and systems on which the delivery of clinical care and improvement of outcomes rests.

Results presented here demonstrate the value of programmes focused on locally-led QI enhancing basic clinical care for newborns in producing substantial positive change in care practice standards and outcomes. But the results also point to deeper opportunities to support Rwanda’s system of government hospitals and referring primary care centres to form a supportive network (a ‘learning health system’) where new ideas and practices can be fostered and shared. In Rwanda, the active leadership of central government and its concerted commitment to evidence-based and equitable national healthcare services offer significant opportunities for structured, systematic scale-up of effective approaches identified in this way.

While this paper concentrates primarily on quantitative results of the programme described, it is paired with a ‘sister paper’ (Tuyisenge et al39), which presents deeper qualitative analysis of how programme interventions were implemented and how experience of QI initiatives on the ground can offer valuable insight into the underlying structural factors which determine the efficacy of change for mothers and their babies as they admit to, deliver in and discharge from health facilities in Rwanda and, perhaps, more widely in comparable low-/lower-middle-income country settings. In this way, the papers together aim to offer combined outcome and implementation analyses of how positive change can happen in neonatal (and perinatal) care delivery.
