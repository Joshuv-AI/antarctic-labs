Multi-Environment POMDPs with Finite-Horizon Objectives
Léonard Brice, Filip Cano, Krishnendu Chatterjee, Thomas A. Henzinger, Stefanie Muroya

Abstract
Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs) are systems in which one agent interacts with a stochastic environment, and receives only partial information about the current state. In a multi-environment POMDP (MEPOMDP), the initial state is unknown, and assumed to be adversarially chosen. In this work we focus on computing the optimal value and policy in MEPOMDPs with finite-horizon objectives. That problem is known to be 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{PSPACE} -complete in POMDPs. Our main results are as follows: (1) we establish that it is also 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{PSPACE} -complete in the more general setting of MEPOMDPs; (2) we present a practical algorithm and evaluate it on classical benchmarks, significantly outperforming the only previously known algorithm.



1
Introduction

Sequential decision-making under uncertainty is a central challenge in artificial intelligence research.

Markov Decision Processes
(MDPs) constitute a standard theoretical model to capture settings in which an agent interacts with a stochastic environment.
An MDP consists of a finite set of
states
and a finite set of
actions
; given a state, each action choice stochastically updates the state and gives per-stage rewards.
MDPs have already shown a wide range of applications, from management science to robotics
[
12
,
17
,
14
]
.
They also have many extensions: we discuss two prominent ones.

The first extension of MDPs is known as
partial-observation MDPs
(POMDPs).
While in MDPs the agent always has perfect observation about the state, in POMDPs, the agent only receives some information about the state.
POMDPs are remarkably expressive and have found applications in robotics
[
14
]
, healthcare
[
6
]
, and autonomous planning more broadly.

The second extension is to
multi-environment MDPs
(MEMDPs), where instead of a single environment, there are multiple possible environments that the agent is unaware of.
This setting was studied for two environments in
[
13
]
and later for many environments
[
5
,
18
]
.
MEMDPs have also been studied in several applications such as recommander systems and robot navigation
[
3
]
.

Combining the above two aspects leads to
multi-environment POMDPs
(MEPOMDPs).
Those were introduced in
[
1
]
, where it was established that they constitute a robust model that is equivalent to various natural settings, such as adversarial-belief POMDPs or multi-objective POMDPs.

Contributions.

In this work, we consider MEPOMDPs with finite-horizon objectives, where the agent’s goal is to maximise the reward sum for a finite-horizon length
k
.
We consider both exact and approximate computation of the optimal value the agent can achieve.
Our results are as follows.

Complexity.
We establish that both the exact and approximate problems are in
\mathsf{PSPACE}
, and therefore
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete, using the lower bound known for POMDPs.

Space-efficient algorithm.
While in the complexity result we present a non-deterministic algorithm, whose determinisation using standard techniques leads to an algorithm with substantial (albeit polynomial) space usage, we present a deterministic space-efficient algorithm.

Practical algorithm and experiments.
We then complement the above theoretical results with a time-efficient practical algorithm, and test its performance and scalability empirically. Our experiments show that our method obtains better performance than the previously existing state of the art tool.

Related works.

Complexity of POMDPs.

The landmark paper of Papadimitriou and Tsitsiklis
[
11
]
establishes that the decision version of value computation of POMDPs, under finite horizon, is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete.
In
[
9
]
, Madani, Hanks, and Condon show undecidability for the infinite-horizon case, with undiscounted and discounted payoff, a result that motivates the focus on finite horizon throughout our work.

Multi-environment MDPs.

The main works about MEMDPs have been described above
[
13
,
5
,
18
,
3
]
.
None of those papers consider partial observation.

Robust POMDPs.

In
[
10
]
, Osogami introduces
robust POMDPs
, i.e. POMDPs equipped with a continuous uncertainty set for the transition and/or observation probabilities.
Multi-environment POMDPs can be seen as a subcase of robust POMDPs, where the uncertainty set is finite.
In
[
7
]
, Bovy et al. propose a method to compute an optimal policy in a robust POMDP.
However, this result does not induce any computational complexity result for MEPOMDPs.

Multi-environment POMDPs.

MEPOMDPs were first studied by Bovy et al.
[
1
]
, who propose an exact algorithm for the finite horizon case, and an approximate one for the infinite-horizon discounted case.
However, that paper does not provide any complexity result, and the algorithm proposed is significantly slower than the one we present here, as we detail in
Section

6
.

2.1
POMDPs

We now define the model that is considered in this paper.

Definition 1

(Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP))
.

A POMDP is a tuple
\mathcal{P}=\langle S,A,\mathcal{O},\delta:S\times A\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(S),\mathsf{obs}:S\rightarrow\mathcal{O},r:S\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}\rangle
that consists of
(i) a finite set
S
of states,
(ii) a finite set
A
of actions,
(iii) a finite set
\mathcal{O}
of observations,
(iv) a stochastic transition function
\delta
,
(v) an observation function
\mathsf{obs}
, and
(vi) a reward function
r
.

Remark 1

(Equivalence with other models)
.

POMDPs are often described with more general observation and reward functions, that depend on states and actions, and have probabilistic outcome.
However, this is equivalent to the simple model that we consider (see Remark 4 in
[
4
]
).

A MDP is a special case of POMDPs where each state has a unique observation.

A
trajectory
of length
k\in\mathbb{N}
in the POMDP
\mathcal{P}
is a finite sequence

\tau=(s_{0},a_{0},s_{1},\dots,a_{k-1},s_{k})\in(S\cdot A)^{*}\cdot S
.
The protagonist of a POMDP aims at generating trajectories that maximise her
payoff
, i.e., the sum of the rewards collected on the way, which we write

\mathsf{payoff}(\tau)=\sum_{\ell\leq k}r(s_{\ell})
.

An
observation sequence
in
\mathcal{P}
is a finite sequence

(o_{0},a_{0},o_{1},\dots,a_{k-1},o_{k})\in(\mathcal{O}\cdot A)^{*}\cdot\mathcal{O}
.

The set of trajectories in the POMDP
\mathcal{P}
is denoted by
\mathsf{Traj}({\mathcal{P}})
, and the set of observation sequences by
\mathsf{ObsSeq}({\mathcal{P}})
.
We define
\mathsf{Traj}_{k}({\mathcal{P}})
as the set of trajectories of length
k
.
Each trajectory
\tau
is associated with an observation sequence, denoted by

\mathsf{obs}(\tau)=(\mathsf{obs}(s_{0}),a_{0},\mathsf{obs}(s_{1}),\cdots,a_{k-1},\mathsf{obs}(s_{k}))
.

Policies.

A
policy
in the POMDP
\mathcal{P}
is a function
\sigma:\mathsf{ObsSeq}({\mathcal{P}})\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(A)
.
It is
deterministic
if for every observation sequence
\omega\in\mathsf{ObsSeq}({\mathcal{P}})
, there is an action
a
with
\sigma(\omega)(a)=1
.
We denote the set of all policies on
\mathcal{P}
as
\mathsf{Pol}(\mathcal{P})
, and
the set of deterministic policies as
\mathsf{DetPol}(\mathcal{P})
.

Given a POMDP
\mathcal{P}
and an initial state
s\in S
,
each policy
\sigma
defines a probability distribution
\mathbb{P}^{\sigma}_{s}
over trajectories of length
k
, for each
horizon

k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Thus, we can define the expected payoff obtained when following that policy: we write it
\mathbb{E}_{s}^{k}(\sigma)
.
Throughout this paper, we focus on policy behaviors within a well-defined finite horizon
k
: we therefore assume that policies are described only on observations sequences of length bounded by
k
, which uses space
2^{O(k)}
.

2.2
Beliefs

A classical tool to study POMDPs is the notion of
belief
: from a known initial state, after having performed a sequence of actions and seen a sequence of observations, the agent can keep track of the probability of being in each state.

Definition 2

(Belief)
.

A
belief
in the POMDP
\mathcal{P}
is a probability distribution
\beta
over states, such that for all
s,s^{\prime}
in
\mathsf{Supp}(\beta)
, we have that
\mathsf{obs}(s)=\mathsf{obs}(s^{\prime})
.

We sometimes abuse notations and write
\mathsf{obs}(\beta)
for the unique observation associated to the belief
\beta
.
When the agent has the belief
\beta
, performs an action
a
and sees the observation
o
, she must update her belief to reflect her knowledge about which state may have now be reached.
We denote that updated belief by
\mathsf{update}(\beta,a,o)
, defined for each state
s
by:

\mathsf{update}(\beta,a,o)(s)=\frac{\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\beta(s^{\prime})\cdot\delta(s^{\prime},a)(s)}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s^{\prime\prime}\in S\\
\mathsf{obs}(s^{\prime\prime})=o\end{subarray}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\beta(s^{\prime})\cdot\delta(s^{\prime},a)(s^{\prime\prime})}

if
\mathsf{obs}(s)=o
, and
\mathsf{update}(\beta,a,o)(s)=0
otherwise.

2.3
Multi-environment POMDP

We can now define the model we focus on throughout this paper.

Definition 3

(Multi-environment POMDP)
.

A
multi-environment POMDP
is a pair
\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathcal{P},(s_{i})_{i\in[n]}\rangle
, with
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and where
s_{i}
is a state of
\mathcal{P}
for each
i
.

Remark 2

(Equivalence with other models)
.

There are several ways to define ME-POMDPs.
A first one would be to consider a single initial state but different transition functions
\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n}
.
A second one is to consider different reward functions.
A third one is to consider multiple initial states, or more generally initial beliefs.
Those three models are introduced in
[
1
]
, under the names
multi-environment
,
multi-objective
, and
adversarial belief POMDPs
, respectively.
The same paper establishes that all of them are equivalent, hence we chose the technically simplest one, with multiple initial states (i.e., adversarial belief) and refer to it as ME-POMDPs for consistency with the literature of MEMDPs.

2.4
Problem statement

Given an MEPOMDP
\mathcal{M}
, we define the
value
of
\mathcal{M}
as
v=\sup_{\sigma\in\mathsf{Pol}(\mathcal{P})}\min_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}^{k}_{s_{i}}(\sigma)
.

Problem 1

(Value problem)
.

Given an MEPOMDP
\mathcal{M}
and a horizon
k
, compute the value
v
.

We also consider the approximate version of that problem, where we are given a quantity
\varepsilon
, and ask for any quantity in the interval
[v-\varepsilon,v+\varepsilon]
.
For complexity purposes, we also consider the decision version of these problems.

Problem 2

(Threshold problem)
.

Given an MEPOMDP
\mathcal{M}
, a horizon
k
, and a threshold
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}
, do we have
v\geq\lambda
?

The approximate threshold problem is obtained by considering only instances that come with the guarantee that the quantity
\lambda
is at distance at least
\varepsilon
from the value—or equivalently, by considering that both answers are acceptable on instances that do not satisfy this guarantee.

Problem encoding.

For complexity results, all items in the instance are given with classical encodings, except for the horizon
k
, assumed to be given in unary.
This choice is motivated by the fact that the case
n=1
was also studied under this hypothesis
[
11
]
, and that to our knowledge, the complexity of that problem when
k
is given in binary remains unknown.
In the rest of this paper, we assume that we are given an instance of this problem, whose total size is denoted by
\mathcal{N}
.

The need for randomised policies.

While in POMDPs the value coincides with optimising over
\mathsf{DetPol}(\mathcal{P})
, for MEMDPs, randomised policies are more powerful: there exist MEPOMDPs where we have
v>\sup_{\sigma\in\mathsf{DetPol}(\mathcal{P})}\mathbb{E}^{k}_{s_{i}}(\sigma)
.
This is already known for multi-environment MDPs
[
13
]
.

Example 2
.

Consider the POMDP depicted by
Figure

1
, with horizon
1
and initial states
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
.
Remember that the double circles indicate a reward
1
, and that the other states give reward
0
.
With a single initial state, a deterministic policy would be sufficient to maximise the expected payoff: action
b
from
s_{1}
, action
a
from
s_{2}
.
However, if the initial vertex can be either
s_{1}
or
s_{2}
, none of those policies is satisfying.
On the other hand, randomising between actions

and
b
with probability

each guarantees expected payoff
0.5
.
Better: the policy that randomises uniformly between actions
c
and
d
guarantees an expected payoff
0.75
, even though the deterministic policies that play deterministically either
c
or
d
are not optimal neither from
s_{1}
nor from
s_{2}
.

In the next section, we define a few conceptual tool that we use to solve this problem.

3
Multi-belief, multi-expected payoff, and mixtures of policies

3.1
Multi-beliefs

The notion of belief is not sufficient to solve our problem.
Indeed, with an adversarially chosen initial state, the agent must consider separately the
n

environments
in which the initial state was
s_{1},\dots,s_{n}
, respectively; and for each of them, construct a different belief.
When performing actions and seeing observations, the agent can now get information of two kinds: (i) in each environment
i
, she must at each step update her belief; but (ii) some observation sequences may also be incompatible with some initial states, in which case the corresponding environment must be eliminated.
For example, in the POMDP depicted by
Figure

1
, if after performing action
c
, the agent sees the observation
o_{2}
, she knows that the initial state was
s_{1}
and must no longer consider the environment
2
.

Definition 4

(Multi-belief)
.

A
multi-belief
in the MEPOMDP
\mathcal{M}
is a tuple
\bar{\beta}=(\beta_{i})_{i\in[n]}
, where each
\beta_{i}
is either a belief or the symbol
\bot
, such that all beliefs
\beta_{i}\neq\bot
have the same observation.

The equality
\beta_{i}=\bot
means that the environment
i
is eliminated, i.e., that the agent learned that
s_{i}
was not the initial state.
For convenience, we write
\bot(s)=\bot
for every
s\in S
.
The initial multi-belief is
\bar{\beta}^{0}
, defined by
\beta^{0}_{i}(s_{i})=1
for each
i
.
The update function is extended to multi-beliefs by:

\mathsf{update}(\bar{\beta},a,o)=\left(\begin{cases}\bot&\text{if }\beta_{i}=\bot\text{ or }\sum_{s\in S}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in S\\
\mathsf{obs}(t)=o\end{subarray}}\beta_{i}(s)\cdot\delta(s,a)(t)=0\\
\mathsf{update}(\beta_{i},a,o)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\right)_{i\in[n]}

3.2
Multi-expected payoff

Similarly, the notion of expected payoff must be replaced by that of
multi-expected payoff
, defined as tuples of real values.
We also allow some coordinates to be set to
\bot
, for policies that are played starting from a multi-belief in which some environments have already been eliminated.
For simplicity, we use the conventions
\bot\cdot 0=0
and
\bot+x=\bot
and
\bot\cdot x=\bot
for every other
x
.

Definition 5

(Multi-expected payoff)
.

A
multi-expected payoff
is a tuple in
(\mathbb{R}\cup\{\bot\})^{n}
.
Given a multi-belief
\bar{\beta}
, policy
\sigma
, and horizon
\ell
, their multi-expected payoff is

\mathsf{mep}_{\bar{\beta}}^{\ell}(\sigma)=\left(\sum_{s}\beta_{i}(s)\mathbb{E}^{\ell}_{s}(\sigma)\right)_{i}
.

When
\ell=k
is clear from context, we write
\mathsf{mep}_{\bar{\beta}}(\sigma)
instead of
\mathsf{mep}_{\bar{\beta}}^{\ell}(\sigma)
.

2
can now be rephrased as follows: in the POMDP
\mathcal{P}
, does there exists a policy
\sigma
such that
\mathsf{mep}_{\bar{\beta}^{0}}(\sigma)\in\left[\lambda,+\infty\right)^{n}
?

3.3
Mixtures of policies

Finally, we will often need to define policies as a randomised combination of several policies.

Definition 6

(Mixture of policies)
.

Given a POMDP
\mathcal{P}
, some policies
\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{\ell}\in\mathsf{DetPol}(\mathcal{P})
, and some coefficients
\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{\ell}\in[0,1]
with
\sum_{i\leq\ell}\alpha_{i}=1
, we denote by
\sum_{i\leq\ell}\alpha_{i}\sigma_{i}
the policy
\sigma
that, for each
i
, follows the policy
\sigma_{i}
with probability
\alpha_{i}
.
The policy
\sigma
is then a
mixture
of
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{\ell}
.
The
degree
of
\sigma
is the minimal
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\sigma
is a mixture of
\ell
deterministic policies.

Remark 3
.

Every policy has degree at most
|A|^{|\mathcal{O}|^{k}}
.

Remark 4
.

Let
\sigma=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}\sigma_{i}
be a mixture of policies and
\bar{\beta}^{0}
be an initial product belief.
Then, we have that
\mathsf{mep}(\sigma,\bar{\beta}^{0})=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\cdot\mathsf{mep}(\sigma_{i},\bar{\beta}^{0})
.

4
Complexity

In this section, we show that the threshold problem and its approximate version are
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete.
The lower bound is a result of
[
11
]
, but the upper bound requires new techniques.

First, we note that it is sufficient to consider policies with a degree at most the number of initial states.
This is a consequence of Carathéodory’s theorem
[
8
]
: each policy is a mixture of deterministic policies, and its corresponding multi-expected payoff is a convex combination of their multi-expected payoffs. Since the multi-expected payoff of an arbitrary policy is a point in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, Carathéodory’s theorem states that said convex combination can be written using only
n+1
of the original points.

Lemma 1

(App.
B
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a POMDP, let
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}
, and
s_{1},\dots,s_{n}
be
n
initial states.
Let us assume that there exists a policy
\sigma
such that from every initial state
s_{i}
, following the policy
\sigma
, the expected payoff is at least
\lambda
.
Then, there exists a policy of degree at most
n
that offers the same guarantee.

This result should convey the intuition of why value computation in MEPOMDPs is not fundamentally harder than in POMDPs: although deterministic strategies are not sufficient here,
Lemma

1
shows that only a limited amount of randomisation is necessary.

From this result, a first naive algorithm can be proposed.
By
Lemma

1
, to recognize positive instances of
2
, it suffices to guess
n
deterministic policies
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n}
and to check that they can be mixed into a satisfying policy, i.e., that we have
\mathsf{Conv}\{\bar{x}^{1},\dots,\bar{x}^{n}\}\cap[\lambda,1]^{n}\neq\emptyset
.
Every deterministic policy can be described with the list of possible observation sequences, and for each of them, the action that is performed.
Such a description requires exponential space.
Once those policies are guessed, computing the associated multi-expected payoffs amounts to computing the expected payoffs in
n
Markov chains, which can be done in time polynomial in the size of the Markov chains, i.e., exponential in the size of the original POMDP.
Checking whether we have
\mathsf{Conv}\{\bar{x}^{1},\dots,\bar{x}^{n}\}\cap\left[\lambda,+\infty\right)^{n}\neq\emptyset
can then be done in polynomial time.
This algorithm shows therefore membership to the class
\mathsf{NEXPTIME}
.
To obtain a better upper bound, we note that we do not need the whole description of each deterministic policy, but just their multi-expected payoff, which we can represent using only polynomial space, as formalized in
Lemma

2
.
Therefore, we can guess policies one action at a time, and propagate the multi-expected payoffs instead of storing the whole policy.
Such an algorithm also requires the following lemma.

Lemma 2

(App.
C
)
.

Let
R=\max_{s\in S}|r(s)|
.
Let
X=\{\mathsf{mep}_{\bar{\beta}^{0}}(\sigma)\>\mid\>\sigma\in\mathsf{DetPol}(\mathcal{P})\}
.
There exists a polynomial
\pi
and an integer
\mathcal{C}\leq 2^{\pi(\mathcal{N})}
such that the set
N=\left\{p/\mathcal{C}\>\left|\>p\in\left\{-\mathcal{C}kR,\dots,\mathcal{C}kR\right.\right\}\right\}
contains
X
. Thus,
N
can be represented using
\pi(\mathcal{N})+2\mathcal{N}+1
bits.

7
Conclusion

We have considered the value computation problem in MEPOMDPs with finite-horizon objectives,
providing tight complexity bounds, a space efficient algorithm, and a time efficient algorithm that proves to be empirically more performant than previously existing tools.

Limitations.

From a theoretical perspective, our methods present a usual tradeoff between theoretical and practical performance:
Algorithm
2
, while asymptotically optimal, is unfeasibly slow in practice; and Algorithm
3
, while efficient in practice, uses exponential space in the worst case.
From a practical perspective, the main challenge for Algorithm
3
is scalability. In particular, the dependence on the horizon is quite strong, with the solved instances with longest horizon at
k=7
.

Future work.

Along with finite-horizon objectives, one can also consider discounted-sum objectives.
The exact problem for discounted-sum objectives is already undecidable for POMDPs
[
9
]
, but its approximate version can be reduced to finite-horizon objectives: our algorithm can therefore easily be applied (up to adding a discount factor) to solve that problem as well.
We leave the theoretical and practical complexities of this problem as future work.

recursively.
The
\textnormal{partialSum}(j,\ell,\bar{\beta},a,\bar{x}_{\mathsf{rem}})
procedure checks whether there is a strategy that starts with action
a
and such that

\bar{x}_{\mathsf{rem}}=\bigg(\sum_{j^{\prime}=j}^{|\mathcal{O}|}\sum_{s\in S}r(s)\beta_{i}(s)\>\>+\sum_{t\in S,\,\mathsf{obs}(t)=o_{j^{\prime}}}\beta_{i}(s)\cdot\delta(s,a)(t)\cdot x_{i}^{o_{j^{\prime}}}\bigg)_{i\in[n]}.

To do so, it checks, for every possible value in
N
, whether it can be achieved as a multi-expected payoff in a horizon of
\ell-1
steps from the updated multi-belief
\mathsf{update}(\bar{\beta},a,o_{j})
, and for those values where it is possible, whether there is a way to fill up the remaining
|\mathcal{O}|-j+1
terms in the partial sum to make it up to
\bar{x}_{\mathsf{rem}}
.

To check the space requirements, we need to check the space used by each procedure, and the size of the recursion stack.
Let
D=\pi(\mathcal{N})
(recall
Lemma

2
).

First, observe that the main procedure just requires storing
n
multi-expected payoff points, plus a high-level call to the
CheckAchievableValue
procedure.

Second, consider the space requirement for a single frame of both recursive procedures. In a single frame of the
CheckAchievableValue
procedure, the space requirement is that required to store the current multi-belief
\bar{\beta}
(
|S|\cdot nD
)
and the current multi-expected payoff value
\bar{x}
(
n^{2}D\leq|S|\cdot nD
),
plus the space required for a high-level call to
partialSum
.
In a single frame of
partialSum
, the space requirement is the same as for
CheckAchievableValue
: a multibelief and a multi-expected payoff.

Third, consider the size of the recursion stack.
If we consider the index
(\ell,j)
shared between the procedures
CheckAchievableValue
and
partialSum
(considering that
CheckAchievableValue
has always
j=0
), at every recursive call, either
j
increases with a call to
partialSum
(lines 15 and 29), keeping
\ell
fixed, or
\ell
decreases with a call to
CheckAchievableValue
(line 27), resetting
j
to
0
. Therefore, the recursion follows all the values of
(\ell,j)
in lexicographical order (descending for
\ell
and ascending for
j
), so the size of the recursion stack is always at most
|\mathcal{O}|\cdot k
.

Finally, the space requirement is the space requirement of each frame, times the size of the recursion stack, plus the space required for the main procedure:
|\mathcal{O}|\cdot k|S|\cdot n\cdot D+n^{2}\cdot D
.
∎

F.1
Algorithm choice

As noted in Sec.
5
, Algorithm
2
is very inefficient in terms of computation time, so we only consider Algorithm
3
for our empirical evaluation.

As for the comparison with
[
1
]
, a feature in their algorithm is that the user can specify a
gap
or
tolerance
, explicitly indicating how precise the approximation of the actual result is allowed to be.
In our experience with
[
1
]
’s tool, making this gap more or less restrictive had almost no effect in practice, both in the obtained value of the MEPOMDP, as well as in the computation time required.
We explain this phenomenon as a consequence of
[
1
]
being optimized for the infinite horizon discounted problem, where, according to the results presented in
[
1
]
, the gap does play a significant role.

As benchmarks, we used modified versions of standard problems in the POMDP literature: RockSample, introduced in

F.2.1
Rock Sample

The RockSample problem was introduced in
[
16
]
, and extended to the ME-POMDP setting in
[
1
]
. An instance consists of an
m\times m
grid with
t
rocks at known positions. The agent starts in the bottom-left cell, moves in the four cardinal directions, can exit through the right boundary, and may sample a rock when located on its cell. Rock qualities are hidden: initially exactly
g
of the
t
rocks are good and the remaining
t-g
rocks are bad. Sampling a good rock gives reward
+10
and changes that rock to bad; sampling a bad rock gives reward
-10
. For each rock, a check action returns a noisy good/bad observation whose correctness decreases with the distance between the agent and the queried rock.
We denote instances of the problem as
\mathsf{RS}_{m,g,t}
.
Note that, in our description, we use the instances corresponding to
\mathsf{RS}^{c}_{m,g,t}
in
[
1
]
, that is, with fixed rock positions.
We illustrate the problem in Figure
4
.
The initial states are those states in which the agent is located in the bottom-left corner and g out of t rocks are good.
The multiple initial states, i.e., the position of the rocks, are sampled in the same way as
[
1
]
.

F.2.2
Robot navigation

The Robot Navigation problem
[
2
, Appendix H.5]
describes a robot moving through a room, that has to reach different goals.
We use the
CIT
,
MIT
,
Pentagon
, and
SUNYSB
robot-navigation maps, illustrated in Figure
5
. Each map is a discretized indoor environment. A nonterminal state is a pair

s=(x,\theta),

where
x
is a traversable map location and
\theta
is one of the four cardinal orientations. The source construction also includes an absorbing terminal state, entered after the robot declares that it is at the goal. The concrete four-action instances use

A_{\mathrm{nav}}=\{\mathsf{forward},\mathsf{left},\mathsf{right},\mathsf{declare}\}
.
The first three actions are noisy motion actions, and
\mathsf{declare}
moves the process to the absorbing terminal state. The reward is zero except for
\mathsf{declare}
: declaring at the goal gives reward
+1
, while declaring away from the goal gives reward
-1
.

The motion model is specified by primitive outcomes. Let
N
denote no movement,
F
a one-cell forward move,
L
a
90^{\circ}
left turn,
R
a
90^{\circ}
right turn, and
A
absorption. The noisy outcomes are

\begin{array}[]{c|l}\text{Action}&\text{Primitive outcome distribution}\\
\hline\cr\mathsf{forward}&N:0.11,\quad F:0.88,\quad FF:0.01\\
\mathsf{left}&N:0.05,\quad L:0.90,\quad LL:0.05\\
\mathsf{right}&N:0.05,\quad R:0.90,\quad RR:0.05\\
\mathsf{declare}&A:1.00.\end{array}

For each nonterminal state
s
, let

\sigma(s)=(\sigma_{\mathrm{front}}(s),\sigma_{\mathrm{left}}(s),\sigma_{\mathrm{right}}(s))\in\{\mathsf{open},\mathsf{wall},\mathsf{doorway}\}^{3}

be the true local observation in the directions in front of the robot, to its left, and to its right. We replace the source observation table by the alphabet

\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{nav}}=\{\mathsf{open},\mathsf{wall},\mathsf{doorway}\}^{3}\cup\{(\mathsf{undet},\mathsf{undet},\mathsf{undet})\},

so
|\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{nav}}|=28
. Observations are independent of the action, and depend only on how many sides of the robot have open space, wall, or doorway. Let

\alpha_{\mathrm{ow}}(s)=\#\{i\in\{\mathrm{front},\mathrm{left},\mathrm{right}\}:\sigma_{i}(s)\in\{\mathsf{open},\mathsf{wall}\}\},

and let
\alpha_{\mathrm{door}}(s)=3-\alpha_{\mathrm{ow}}(s)
. The probability of observing the correct local signature is

p_{\mathrm{corr}}(s)=0.9^{\alpha_{\mathrm{ow}}(s)}0.7^{\alpha_{\mathrm{door}}(s)}.

Thus, for every action
a
, the probability to observe
o
is
p_{\mathrm{corr}}(s)
if
o=\sigma(s)
,
1-p_{\mathrm{corr}}(s)
if
o=(\mathsf{undet},\mathsf{undet},\mathsf{undet})
, and
0
otherwise.

For each map we choose a pair of initial states that are at a distance at most
d
to the goal and maximize the observational diversity.
Let
G_{E}
be the set of goal states for map
E
, and let
d_{E}
be shortest-path distance in the nonterminal state graph induced by positive-probability motion transitions, with the
\mathsf{declare}
transition excluded. The candidate set is

C_{E}=\{s\in S_{E}\setminus\{\bot\}:d_{E}(s,G_{E})\leq d\}.

In our test cases, the parameter
d
is either
1
,
2
, or
3
.
As initial states, we select the set
I_{E}=\{s_{E}^{(1)},s_{E}^{(2)}\}
defined as

\{s_{E}^{(1)},s_{E}^{(2)}\}\in\arg\max_{\{s,t\}\subseteq C_{E},\,s\neq t}D_{\mathrm{JS}}(\mathsf{obs}(s),\mathsf{obs}(t)),

where

D_{\mathrm{JS}}(P,Q)=\frac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(P\middle\|\frac{P+Q}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(Q\middle\|\frac{P+Q}{2}\right)

is the Jensen-Shannon divergence over
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{nav}}
.
In our test cases, the largest Jensen-Shannon divergence we observed is
\approx 0.204
.

F.2.3
Identification, Friend or Foe

The IFF problem (also referred to as
aircraft-identification
problem) models an incoming aircraft that may be a friend or a foe. A nonterminal state is
s=(\tau,d,v)
,
where
\tau\in\{\mathsf{friend},\mathsf{foe}\}
is the aircraft type,
d\in\{0,\ldots,D-1\}
is the discretized distance to the base, and
v\in\{0,\ldots,4\}
is the visibility of the base to the aircraft, i.e., how well does the aircraft see the base. We use
D=10
, so there are
2D\cdot 5=100
nonterminal states.
As in the source benchmark, there are four absorbing states:

\mathsf{base\mbox{-}safe},\quad\mathsf{base\mbox{-}destroyed},\quad\mathsf{foe\mbox{-}destroyed},\quad\mathsf{friend\mbox{-}destroyed},

for a total of
44
states. The action set is

A_{\mathrm{air}}=\{\mathsf{active},\mathsf{passive},\mathsf{noop},\mathsf{attack}\}
.
The actions
\mathsf{active}
and
\mathsf{passive}
are sensing actions;
\mathsf{active}
is more accurate but tends to increase visibility more than
\mathsf{passive}
. The action
\mathsf{noop}
uses no sensor, and
\mathsf{attack}
attempts to destroy the aircraft.

On nonterminal transitions that do not enter an absorbing state, the aircraft type
\tau
is unchanged. For
d>0
, the aircraft advances one distance bin toward the base with probability
0.8
, and remains at the same distance with probability
0.2
.
The distance transition is independent of
\tau
,
v
, and the action, conditional on not entering an absorbing state.
We modify the visibility transition by forbidding visibility decreases. Let the source visibility-increment probabilities over
\Delta\in\{-1,0,+1\}
be

\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}\text{Action}&q_{a}(-1)&q_{a}(0)&q_{a}(+1)\\
\hline\cr\mathsf{noop}&0.25&0.75&0.00\\
\mathsf{passive}&0.00&0.90&0.10\\
\mathsf{active}&0.00&0.05&0.95\\
\mathsf{attack}&0.00&0.20&0.80.\end{array}

For our benchmark, transitions to lower visibility are removed and the remaining probabilities are renormalized:

P(v^{\prime}=v+\Delta\mid v,a)=\frac{q_{a}(\Delta)\,\mathbf{1}\{\Delta\geq 0\}\,\mathbf{1}\{0\leq v+\Delta\leq 4\}}{\sum_{\delta\in\{-1,0,+1\}}q_{a}(\delta)\,\mathbf{1}\{\delta\geq 0\}\,\mathbf{1}\{0\leq v+\delta\leq 4\}}.

Consequently, for
v<4
,
\mathsf{noop}
leaves visibility unchanged with probability
1
, while
\mathsf{passive}
,
\mathsf{active}
, and
\mathsf{attack}
use the probabilities shown in the
0
and
+1
columns above. At
v=4
, all actions leave visibility at
4
with probability
1
.

The attack action may enter an absorbing state before the normal distance and visibility update is applied. At distance
d
, the probability of destroying the aircraft is

p_{\mathrm{hit}}(d)=\frac{(D-d)^{2}}{D^{2}}.

If the attack succeeds, the successor is
\mathsf{foe\mbox{-}destroyed}
when
\tau=\mathsf{foe}
, and
\mathsf{friend\mbox{-}destroyed}
when
\tau=\mathsf{friend}
. If the attack fails, the usual distance and modified visibility transitions are applied, conditional on failure. When a non-attacked friend is already at
d=0
, the next state is
\mathsf{base\mbox{-}safe}
with probability
1
. When a non-attacked foe is already at
d=0
, the next state is
\mathsf{base\mbox{-}destroyed}
with probability
0.25+0.1v
, and
\mathsf{base\mbox{-}safe}
otherwise.
The only nonzero rewards are the immediate rewards for entering absorbing states:

\begin{array}[]{c|r}\text{Absorbing state}&\text{Reward}\\
\hline\cr\mathsf{base\mbox{-}safe}&0\\
\mathsf{base\mbox{-}destroyed}&-100\\
\mathsf{foe\mbox{-}destroyed}&20\\
\mathsf{friend\mbox{-}destroyed}&-30.\end{array}

The observation alphabet is

\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{air}}=\bigl(\{\mathsf{friend},\mathsf{foe}\}\times\{0,\ldots,D-1\}\bigr)\cup\{\mathsf{nothing},\mathsf{absorb}\},

so
|\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{air}}|=2D+2=22
. The action
\mathsf{noop}
emits
\mathsf{nothing}
with probability
1
, and absorbing states emit
\mathsf{absorb}
with probability
1
. For sensing, let
\bar{\tau}
denote the opposite type and let
d^{+}=\min\{d+1,D-1\}
. We use the following modified observation probabilities:

\displaystyle\mathsf{obs}((\tau,d)\mid(\tau,d,v),\mathsf{active})

\displaystyle=0.9,

\displaystyle\mathsf{obs}((\bar{\tau},d^{+})\mid(\tau,d,v),\mathsf{active})

\displaystyle=0.1,

\displaystyle\mathsf{obs}((\tau,d)\mid(\tau,d,v),\mathsf{passive})

\displaystyle=0.8,

\displaystyle\mathsf{obs}((\bar{\tau},d^{+})\mid(\tau,d,v),\mathsf{passive})

\displaystyle=0.2.

All other type-distance observations have probability zero. For
d=D-1
, the reported distance
d^{+}
is clipped to the largest distance bin, matching the boundary convention of the discretized observation space. The attack action follows the source-domain convention: successful terminal outcomes emit
\mathsf{absorb}
, while unsuccessful nonterminal outcomes use the active-sensing observation model above.

Initial states in the aircraft-identification experiments are chosen from two foe states and one friend state. For parameters

0\leq d_{1}<d_{2}\leq 4,\qquad v_{1},v_{2}\in\{0,\ldots,4\},

we use the initial-state set

I_{d_{1},d_{2},v_{1},v_{2}}=\{(\mathsf{foe},d_{1},v_{1}),\ (\mathsf{foe},d_{2},v_{2}),\ (\mathsf{friend},d_{2},\bar{v})\},

where
\bar{v}
is any fixed visibility level for the friend state. The value of
\bar{v}
is immaterial for our initialization because the friend’s visibility does not affect the modified observation probabilities or the terminal reward associated with the friend/foe distinction.

Problem instances are labelled according to their initial state parameters, i.e.,
\mathsf{IFF}_{d_{1},d_{2},v_{1},v_{2}}
.
