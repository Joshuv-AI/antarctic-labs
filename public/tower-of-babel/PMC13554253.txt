Hydrochemical characterization and irrigation suitability mapping of groundwater in Pashupathihal subwatershed, karnataka
Authors: K N Jakir Hussain, B R Jagadeesh, M V Manjunatha, S S Gundlur, G R Rajkumar, P Priya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study comprehensively evaluated irrigation suitability of groundwater in the Pashupathihal Sub-watershed using GIS by examining thirteen strategically selected locations for key physicochemical parameters and established irrigation water quality indices. Hydrochemical facies were identified using Piper diagrams and irrigation suitability was classified using Wilcox and USSL plots in line with national and international standards. Results showed water table depths from 10.50 to 17.00 mbgl, while pH values (7.12-8.44, mean 7.62) indicated mildly alkaline conditions typical of semi-arid aquifers. EC varied from 1.27 to 5.98 dS/m (mean 2.75 dS/m), with 23.08% of samples falling into the saline category. SAR values ranged between 4.15 and 14.48 (mean 9.84), with nearly half of samples presenting moderate sodicity risk. All RSC values were below 1.25 mmol/L, signifying absence of carbonate hazards for irrigation. TH (19.25-60.63 mg/L) and TDS (812.80-3824.64 mg/L) corroborated elevated salinity in some samples. IWQI, 30.7 7% of samples qualified as Excellent and 53.85% as Good for irrigation, demonstrating overall suitability, with 15.38% of samples were classified as poor or unsuitable demonstrating well-balanced water chemistry with manageable risks. Hydrochemical facies (Piper) analysis identified Ca2+-Mg2+-SO₄2−Cl− as the dominant type, indicated geochemical evolution with increasing sulphate and chloride from mineral dissolution. Wilcox and USSL diagrams revealed that half samples had salinity and sodicity problem. Durov and Schoeller diagrams showed groundwater was predominantly of Ca2+-Mg2+-HCO3- type, reflecting fresh recharge water controlled by carbonate weathering and rock-water interaction. PCA indicated dominant control of salinity and hardness, with PC1 explaining 47.1% of variance through EC, TDS, Ca2+, Mg2+, SO42−, Cl- and TH, while five PCs together accounted for 93.18% variance, highlighting roles of alkalinity, sodium hazard, localized inputs, bicarbonate buffering and boron. Correlation heatmap and parameter index analyses confirmed strong interrelationships among major salinity ions, identifying TH, Ca2+, SO42− and Na+ as key contributors to groundwater quality variability. Spatial IWQI mapping showed 73.43 % of the area under moderate irrigation water quality, with poorer zones in central and downstream areas, and regression analysis revealed strong negative impacts of pH (R2 = 0.794) and SAR (R2 = 0.605), moderate influence of TH (R2 = 0.448) and weak contribution of TDS (R2 = 0.249) on irrigation water suitability. The Charge Balance Error (CBE) values (−6.61 to 7.80%, mean 1.09%) were within acceptable limits, confirming the analytical accuracy and reliability of the dataset, thereby supporting the validity of hydrochemical interpretations and emphasizing the need for appropriate management strategies.Keywords: Irrigation suitability, Hydrochemical characterization, Water quality indicesSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Hydrology

Introduction

Groundwater is a vital resource for global agriculture, providing a reliable source of irrigation across diverse agro-climatic regions. Its quality has a profound impact on crop productivity, soil health and the long-term sustainability of agricultural systems. With the growing demand for food, reliance on groundwater irrigation is increasing, highlighting the critical need for thorough quality evaluations1,2. This study utilizes irrigation-specific indices to appraise the suitability of groundwater for agricultural use, aiming to prevent environmental degradation and protect soil quality3,4. Understanding the hydrochemical characteristics of groundwater, shaped by the transformation of rainwater during recharge, is crucial for evaluating its suitability5,6. Salinity remains a significant concern, contributing to the degradation of millions of hectares of land annually worldwide7. Poor-quality irrigation water can contribute to soil deterioration, thereby hampering plant growth and reducing agricultural output. Key parameters in groundwater quality assessment include pH, salinity, permeability and the toxicity levels of ions such as Na+, Cl-, boron, nitrate and bicarbonate8, 9.

Numerous hydrochemical investigations across semi-arid regions of India have demonstrated that groundwater chemistry is predominantly governed by rock-water interaction, evaporation-crystallization processes, ion exchange reactions and anthropogenic inputs such as fertilizer application and irrigation return flows10–12. In crystalline hard rock terrains, prolonged interaction between infiltrating recharge water and silicate minerals particularly feldspars and ferromagnesian constituents along with dissolution of carbonate phases, commonly leads to enrichment of Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+, HCO3- and Cl- ions12,13. In northern Karnataka, especially within Dharwad district and adjoining regions, previous hydrogeochemical studies have reported moderate mineralization of groundwater with dominance of Ca2+-Mg2+-HCO3- and Na+-Cl- hydrochemical facies, indicating mixed geogenic and secondary salinization influences14,15. Reported Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) concentrations in parts of Dharwad district range between 300 and 1500 mg/L, reflecting slightly to moderately saline conditions, while Sodium Adsorption Ratio (SAR) values are generally within permissible limits but exhibit localized sodium enrichment in intensively irrigated pockets16. Similar investigations in adjacent districts such as Belagavi and Gadag have indicated increasing post-monsoon salinity trends attributed to excessive groundwater abstraction, evapotranspiration under semi-arid climatic conditions and limited recharge17. Regional hydrogeological assessments further confirm that groundwater chemistry in northern Karnataka is strongly influenced by weathering of granitic and gneissic formations of the Peninsular Gneissic Complex, where bicarbonate alkalinity dominates in recharge zones and progressive chloride enrichment is observed toward discharge areas along groundwater flow paths18,19.

Advancing sustainable irrigation and water use in agriculture requires evaluating the quality of irrigation water from groundwater and adopting appropriate mitigation strategies5,6. However, the process is often complicated by local variability, requiring site-specific approaches to effectively address regional challenges. The irrigation suitability of groundwater is commonly evaluated by applying a suite of hydrochemical indices, including Sodium Adsorption Ratio (SAR), Total Hardness (TH), Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) and Residual Sodium Carbonate (RSC)1,3,4. Additionally, graphical methods such as Piper trilinear and Wilcox diagrams are employed to interpret chemical composition and classify groundwater types for agricultural use. These tools enhance understanding of hydrogeochemical relationships among water samples and aid in identifying dominant water types and potential risks to soil and crop health. While Water Quality Indices (WQIs) provide an integrated measure of suitability, analyses yield deeper insights into underlying geochemical processes. However, the accuracy of these evaluations depends significantly on the quality and spatial representativeness of the data collected, as any inconsistencies can distort the final interpretation.

Groundwater quality is commonly evaluated by comparing individual physicochemical parameters with prescribed drinking water standards; however, such an independent assessment often fails to capture the interrelationships and combined influence of multiple variables on overall water quality. To overcome this limitation and enable a more integrated interpretation of complex hydrochemical data, multivariate statistical techniques such as principal component analysis (PCA), along with correlation heatmap analysis and regression modeling, are increasingly applied. PCA aids in reducing data dimensionality, identifying key parameters governing groundwater quality, and elucidating dominant hydrochemical processes and potential pollution sources. Correlation heatmaps provide a visual representation of the strength and direction of relationships among water quality parameters, while regression graphs quantify the influence of critical variables on water quality indices, thereby offering deeper insights into the factors controlling groundwater quality.

Although several hydrogeochemical studies have been conducted in Dharwad district and other semi-arid regions of northern Karnataka, most were limited to district- or taluk-level assessments and relied on descriptive statistics. Micro-watershed-scale investigations integrating hydrochemical indices, graphical facies analysis, multivariate statistical techniques, and GIS-based spatial mapping remain scarce for the Pashupathihal Sub-watershed. Seasonal variability in groundwater chemistry has also been inadequately addressed despite its influence under semi-arid conditions. Despite several hydrogeochemical studies in northern Karnataka, most are limited to district or taluk scales, lacking micro-watershed level analysis. There is insufficient integration of hydrochemical indices, graphical methods, multivariate statistical techniques, and GIS-based spatial mapping, which restricts comprehensive interpretation. The identification of dominant geochemical processes and the influence of seasonal variability remain inadequately addressed under semi-arid conditions. Moreover, the absence of spatial delineation of salinity and sodicity risk zones limits the applicability of findings for site-specific irrigation planning and sustainable groundwater management. This study presents an integrated micro-watershed approach combining in-situ field measurements, laboratory-based indices, PCA, correlation heatmaps, regression modeling, and GIS-based irrigation suitability mapping. Unlike previous works, it identifies dominant geochemical processes and spatially delineates salinity and sodicity risk zones, providing actionable guidance for site-specific irrigation planning and sustainable groundwater management.

This research was undertaken to address the increasing need for high-quality irrigation water assessments in the face of expanding agricultural demand and potential degradation of soil and water resources. The primary objectives of the study are to comprehensively characterize the hydrochemical profile of groundwater in the Pashupathihal Sub-watershed, evaluate its suitability for irrigation using internationally recognized indices and graphical techniques (such as SAR, RSC, TH, TDS, IWQI, Piper, Wilcox, USSL, Durov and Schoeller diagrams) and to identify critical salinity and sodicity risks that may affect crop productivity and soil sustainability under semi-arid conditions. This study aims to (i) assess the physicochemical characteristics of groundwater, (ii) identify hydrochemical facies using graphical approaches, (iii) evaluate irrigation suitability through standard water quality indices, and (iv) apply multivariate statistical techniques to determine the governing factors of groundwater quality. Additionally, GIS-based spatial analysis is employed to map irrigation suitability zones and support sustainable groundwater management in the study area.

Material and methods

Study area

The study was conducted in the Pashupathihal Sub-watershed, Dharwad district, Karnataka, covering an area of 6129.92 ha between 15°06’00"-15°12’30" N and 75°19’30"-75°25’30" E (Fig. 1). The landscape consists of gently sloping terrain with predominant sandy clay loam to clay soils, typical of the northern semi-arid region. Annual rainfall averages 669 mm and the study area is situated at an average elevation of approximately 753 m above mean sea level. Hydrologically, the eastern part of the district falls under the Malaprabha River sub-basin of the Krishna Basin (covering nearly 87% of the district), while the western part drains into the Kali River basin. Most streams in the region are ephemeral, reflecting the semi-arid climatic conditions. Thirteen representatively distributed sampling sites were precisely geolocated using GPS, ensuring spatial coverage of the sub-watershed.

Geological framework

Geologically, Dharwad district is predominantly composed of Archaean formations belonging to the Dharwar Supergroup and the Peninsular Gneissic Complex, which together constitute nearly 99% of the district’s lithology. The dominant rock types include gneiss-granite complexes and schists, with argillite (approximately 64%) and biotite gneiss (approximately 35%) forming the major lithological units. The Dharwar Supergroup is largely composed of fine-grained sedimentary formations such as argillites, derived from silt- and sand-sized particles. Banded Iron Formations (BIFs), characterized by alternating layers of iron oxides and silica-rich minerals, are exposed in localized pockets. Groundwater occurrence in the region is mainly controlled by weathered and fractured zones within these crystalline formations. Mineral weathering processes, particularly of feldspar and ferromagnesian minerals, play a significant role in controlling groundwater hydrochemistry (Fig. 1).

Geomorphological characteristics

Geomorphologically, the district forms part of the western ghat section (Sahyadris) of the Indian peninsula, characterized by rugged foothills in the western portion, an undulating central pediplain and an eastern maidan (plain) region. The lateritic uplands in the western sector are associated with brownish sandy soils exhibiting relatively high infiltration rates (19.62-3.6 cm hr-1). The eastern region is dominated by deep black cotton soils (Vertisols) with thickness ranging from 1.5 to 3 m, reflecting basaltic and argillaceous parent material influence. The drainage pattern is largely dendritic to sub-dendritic, structurally controlled by lithological variations. The pediplain landscape and residual hill features influence runoff generation, infiltration capacity and groundwater recharge dynamics across the sub-watershed.

Climatological conditions

Climatically, the region falls under the “Northern Transitional Zone” (Agro-climatic Zone-8) of Karnataka and is classified within Agro-Ecological Sub Region (AESR 6.4): North Sahyadris and Western Karnataka Plateau, characterized as a hot dry sub-humid eco-sub region. The mean maximum temperature ranges from 28°C to 38°C, while the mean minimum temperature varies between 16°C and 23°C. The average annual rainfall ranges from 800 to 1000 mm, primarily received during the southwest monsoon (June-September). However, the potential evapotranspiration (PET) demand is relatively high (1600-1700 mm), resulting in a moisture deficit condition. The Length of Growing Period (LGP) varies between 150 and 180 days.

Groundwater samples were collected after purging tube wells for 15 minutes to obtain sediment-free water during pre-monsoon seasons. Each sample was stored in 500 ml polyethylene bottles, pre-rinsed and preserved with 2-3 drops of toluene to prevent microbial activity. In the laboratory, water samples were analyzed for pH, electrical conductivity (EC) and total dissolved solids (TDS) using a portable pH/EC meter. Sodium and potassium concentrations were measured by flame photometry; calcium, magnesium, chloride, and bicarbonate by EDTA titration and standard volumetric methods; and sulphate by UV-Visible spectrophotometry. All instruments were calibrated with certified standards prior to sample analysis to maintain data accuracy and reliability, adhering to recent regional and international protocols.

Water quality indices

Groundwater samples were collected from 13 tube wells across the Pashupathihal Sub-watershed (Fig. 1) during the pre-monsoon season (2024-2025). Prior to sampling, each well was purged for 15 minutes to obtain sediment-free water, and samples were stored in pre-cleaned polyethylene bottles following standard protocols. Field parameters such as pH, electrical conductivity (EC, dS/m), and depth to water level (mbgl) were measured in situ using portable meters, while laboratory analyses were performed to determine major cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+, K+) and anions (HCO3−, CO32−, Cl−, SO42−) following standard titrimetric and spectrophotometric procedures (Table 1). Groundwater suitability for irrigation was assessed using multiple Water Quality Indices (WQIs), including Total Hardness (TH), Sodium Adsorption Ratio (SAR), Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) and Residual Sodium Carbonate (RSC), which capture salinity, sodicity and associated irrigation hazards. To enhance hydrochemical interpretation, graphical classification tools Piper trilinear, Wilcox and U.S. Salinity Laboratory (USSL) diagrams were generated using Diagrammes 6.77 software.

The Piper diagram20 distinguishes groundwater types by mapping the relative contributions of major cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+, K+) and anions (HCO32−, Cl−, SO42−) within specialized triangular and diamond fields, thereby revealing dominant hydrochemical facies and geochemical processes. The Wilcox diagram21 classifies irrigation suitability based on the interplay between sodium percentage and electrical conductivity, ranking groundwater quality from excellent to unsuitable while emphasizing the risk of sodium-induced soil structure degradation. The USSL diagram (U.S. Salinity Laboratory Staff, 1954) further refines this assessment by categorizing water samples according to both salinity and sodium hazards (conductivity and SAR), positioning groundwater in hazard zones (C1-S1 to C4-S4) that directly inform management practices and potential impacts on soil productivity. This integrated indexing and graphical approach enables robust, regionally relevant evaluation of groundwater for sustainable irrigation use.

Hydrochemical facies of groundwater were interpreted using Durov and Schoeller diagrams, which are widely employed graphical methods for the classification and comparison of water chemistry. The Durov diagram, a composite plot derived from two ternary diagrams of major cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, Na++K+) and anions (Cl−, SO42−, HCO3−+CO32−), together with a central square field, was used to delineate hydrogeochemical processes such as ion exchange, dissolution, and mixing trends22. Schoeller semi-logarithmic diagrams were prepared by plotting the ionic concentrations (meq/L) of major cations and anions against logarithmic scales, enabling a visual comparison of hydrochemical signatures between samples and identification of groundwater groups and geochemical evolution patterns23. These graphical tools provide complementary insights, with Durov plots highlighting hydrochemical facies evolution and Schoeller diagrams allowing rapid comparison of ionic dominance and water type across multiple samples.

Charge balance error (CBE)

It was determined as a quality control measure to evaluate the accuracy of hydrochemical data based on the principle of electroneutrality.

Multivariate statistical analysis

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to identify the key parameters controlling groundwater quality variability in the study area by transforming standardized physicochemical variables, including field parameters (pH, EC), major cations, anions, and irrigation indices, into a reduced set of uncorrelated principal components (PCs). Components with eigenvalues greater than 1 (Kaiser criterion) were retained and their cumulative variance contribution was evaluated to ensure statistical significance. PCA was performed using ORIGIN software (version 2019b; OriginLab Corporation, USA). Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted at a 95% confidence level (p < 0.05) to assess interrelationships among variables and results were visualized through a correlation heatmap generated using XLSTAT (Addinsoft, France) to identify dominant parameter clusters. Regression analysis, including linear and multiple linear models, was subsequently employed to quantify relationships between key hydrochemical parameters and groundwater quality indices, with model performance evaluated using coefficient of determination (R2) and significance levels. Spatial distribution maps of location with geology and irrigation indices were prepared using ArcGIS (version 10.8.1), where GPS-based sampling coordinates referenced to the WGS 1984 coordinate system were interpolated using the Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) method to depict spatial variability and delineate water quality index.

Results and interpretations

The depth to water level in the Pashupathihal Sub-watershed varied from 10.50 to 17.00 mbgl (mean: 13.50 mbgl), reflecting differences in local topography and recharge conditions (Table 3). Across the thirteen sampled sites, pH values ranged from 7.12 to 8.44, with a mean of 7.62, indicating a mildly alkaline nature influenced primarily by carbonate and bicarbonate buffering; such conditions promote nutrient availability while minimizing risks associated with extreme acidity or alkalinity, and are characteristically observed in semi-arid regions of Karnataka and throughout India12,27. Electrical conductivity (EC) levels spanned 1.27 to 5.98 dS/m (mean: 2.75 dS/m), with nearly a quarter of samples exceeding 4.0 dS/m, categorizing them as saline and less suitable for irrigation unless managed,elevated EC indicate increased total dissolved solids and underlying processes of mineral weathering, evapotranspiration and possible anthropogenic inputs, in agreement with recent regional hydrochemical assessments28.

The groundwater samples showed distinct variation in cation composition, with sodium (Na⁺), calcium (Ca2+), magnesium (Mg2+), and potassium (K+) being the main contributors. Sodium ranged from 10.82 to 34.16 mmol/L (mean 23.81 mmol/L), exceeding global irrigation water norms at several sites and highlighting a moderate risk for sodicity and potential soil structure issues. Calcium (4.18-13.56 mmol/L; mean 10.05 mmol/L) and magnesium (1.00-6.50 mmol/L; mean 4.03 mmol/L) were prominent, reflecting significant mineral weathering in the aquifer, which is a typical finding in semi-arid regions of India28. Potassium, while present at lower concentrations (0.016–0.052 mmol/L), remained below agricultural toxicity thresholds owing to clay fixation and low mobility, a trend confirmed in similar analyses in Gujarat and Andhra Pradesh28,29.

Anion analysis revealed significant spatial variability. Chloride (Cl-) concentrations ranged from 9.00 to 25.45 mmol/L (mean 15.67 mmol/L), while sulphate (SO42−) fluctuated between 6.63 and 18.27 mmol/L (mean

10.99 mmol/L). These peaks, especially at samples 3 and 12, suggest influences from anthropogenic runoff and natural sources like halite and gypsum dissolution. Bicarbonate (HCO3−), a primary buffering ion, varied from 4.15 to 10.35 mmol/L (mean 7.35 mmol/L), mirroring results from recent regional studies which find bicarbonate to be the most prevalent groundwater anion, often followed by chloride and sulfate. Carbonate (CO32−) remained low across samples (<1.10 mmol/L), ensuring minimal precipitation hazard a finding consistent with current trends in central and southern Indian aquifers30.

Sodium Adsorption Ratio (SAR) values spanned 4.15-14.48 (mean 9.84), with 46.15% of samples showed medium (SAR 10-18) sodicity risk indicating the need for gypsum amendments and careful crop selection. Residual Sodium Carbonate (RSC) was negative for all samples (-15.90 to 0.71, mean -6.87), confirming no carbonate hazard and safe calcium-magnesium status31. Total Hardness (TH) levels ranged from 19.25 to 60.63 mg/L (mean 37.64 mg/L), mainly in moderately hard to hard categories, with calcium and magnesium ions the principal contributors. India’s annual groundwater reports confirm moderate to hard groundwater status is typical in semi-arid and overexploited regions. Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) spanned 812.80–3824.64 mg/L (mean 1758.13 mg/L), supporting EC findings and raising caution for salt-sensitive crops, particularly where TDS exceeded 2000 mg/L28.

The Charge Balance Error (CBE) values of the groundwater samples ranged from −6.61 to 7.80%, with a mean value of 1.09%, indicating an overall good agreement between the total cations and anions (Table 3). The majority of the samples exhibited CBE within the acceptable limit of ±5%, confirming high analytical precision and reliability of the hydrochemical data. A few samples showed CBE values between ±5% and ±10%, which are still considered permissible for natural water systems due to inherent variability in groundwater chemistry, minor analytical uncertainties, and the possible presence of unmeasured ions such as nitrate, silica, or organic constituents. The relatively low mean CBE and moderate coefficient of variation (19.28%) suggest consistency in laboratory measurements and minimal systematic error. Negative CBE values observed in several samples indicate a slight dominance of anions over cations, whereas positive values reflect relative cation excess; however, these deviations are within acceptable hydrochemical limits.

The PCA results reveal that groundwater chemistry in the study area was primarily controlled by salinity-related parameters, as indicated by PC1, which explains 47.1 % of the total variance and shows very high positive loadings on EC, TDS, Ca2+, Mg2+, SO42−, Cl− and TH, suggesting strong mineral dissolution and ion exchange processes contributing to overall water hardness and salinization (Table 4). PC2 (20.9 %) was dominated by pH, CO32-, SAR and Na+, reflecting alkalinity and sodium hazard influences, likely associated with carbonate weathering and possible anthropogenic inputs such as agricultural return flows. PC3 (11.1 %) showed strong loading on K+ and moderate influence from Na+ and Cl−, indicating localized sources such as fertilizer application or silicate weathering. PC4 (7.7 %) was controlled mainly by HCO3- and IWQI, suggesting that bicarbonate buffering and water quality index variations are linked to natural hydrochemical evolution31. PC5 (6.3 %) was dominated by boron, implying an independent geogenic or agricultural origin of B in the groundwater28. The cumulative variance of 93.18 % indicates that these five components effectively capture the major hydrochemical processes governing groundwater quality and the weighting factors confirm the dominant role of salinity and alkalinity in shaping the overall water chemistry.

The scree plot clearly shows a steep decline in eigenvalues from the first to the second component, followed by a gradual leveling off after the fifth component, indicated that the first few components capture the major variability in the dataset (Fig. 2). The sharp drop after PC1 and PC2 suggests that these components contain the most significant information consistent with their high eigenvalues and dominant contribution to variance while subsequent components contribute progressively less, reflecting noise or minor hydrochemical variations. The point at which the curve begins to flatten (the “elbow”) occurs around PC5, supporting the retention of five principal components as meaningful, which aligns with the cumulative variance of over 93 % explained by these components in the PCA table28. This pattern confirms that groundwater chemistry is influenced by a limited number of major processes primarily salinity, alkalinity and mineral dissolution while additional components represent secondary or localized factors with minimal overall impact.

Moderate correlations involving Na+, SAR and CO32− suggest that sodium enrichment and alkalinity are influenced by carbonate weathering or anthropogenic activities like irrigation return flow. The bar chart of groundwater parameter index values showed that TH (0.9), Ca2+ (0.8), SO42- (0.8), and Na+ (0.7) had the highest index scores, indicated that hardness, calcium, sulphate and sodium were the dominant contributors to groundwater quality variations in the study area, reflecting strong mineral dissolution, ion exchange and salinization processes (Fig. 4). Moderate index values for Mg2+, SAR, Cl− and K+ (around 0.4-0.6) suggest secondary but notable influences linked to both geogenic sources and agricultural practices. Lower index values for parameters such as TDS, EC, RSC and B (0.1-0.3) indicate that while these factors were present, they exert comparatively weaker influence on overall groundwater quality variability, possibly due to spatial heterogeneity or lower concentrations relative to dominant ions29. The minimal impact of pH (0.2) and CO32- (0.3) indicated stable acid-base conditions and limited carbonate contributions. Overall, the pattern highlights that groundwater chemistry is primarily shaped by hardness-related components and major salinity ions, consistent with PCA and correlation findings.

The calculated Irrigation Water Quality Index (IWQI), varied from 10.09 to 55.59 (mean 33.45) (Table 5). Accordingly, 30.77% of samples were “Excellent” (IWQI ≤ 25) and 53.85% “Good” (IWQI 26-50), with 15.38% of samples were classified as poor or unsuitable demonstrating well-balanced water chemistry with manageable risks28. New research confirms IWQI is a practical tool for summarizing multi-parameter datasets and supporting decision making for crop and soil management. However, spatial variation in indices particularly salinity and sodicity risks requires region-specific interventions such as leaching, blending with low-salinity water, and crop rotation using salt-tolerant species29. These results align with recent hydrochemical evaluations in India that highlight the dominance of calcium and sodium among cations and bicarbonate and chloride among anions in semi-arid groundwater28,32. Irrigation indices (SAR, RSC and IWQI) remain the core metrics for sustainable water management, enabled by continuous monitoring and adaptation to seasonal trends and anthropogenic pressures.

The spatial distribution of the Irrigation Water Quality Index (IWQI) in the Pashupathihal Sub-watershed revealed clear variability in water suitability for irrigation, reflecting the influence of geological formations, soil characteristics and land-use patterns (Fig. 5). The IWQI values ranged from 15.95 to 55.09, with the majority of the area (4508.87 ha; 73.43 %) falling under the 35.38-46.72 class, indicated predominantly moderate irrigation water quality across the watershed. Areas with better water quality (15.95-26.36) were limited (154.28 ha; 2.51 %) and were mainly observed in upstream zones (southeast side), likely due to lower anthropogenic influence, reduced solute accumulation and better natural recharge conditions32. Regions classified under higher IWQI ranges (46.72-55.09), covering about 515.96 ha (8.40 %), were concentrated in the central (west side) and lower parts of the watershed, where intensive agricultural activities, fertilizer use and runoff accumulation contribute to higher concentrations of dissolved salts, chloride, bicarbonate and sodium factors that elevate IWQI values. The drainage network also influences these patterns, as water in low-lying areas tends to accumulate and concentrate salts through evaporation, especially in areas with black cotton soils known for poor permeability29.

Groundwater quality classification in the Pashupathihal Sub-watershed based on EC, SAR and RSC demonstrates notable variability in irrigation suitability (Table 6). For electrical conductivity (EC), 46.15 % of samples fell below 2.0 dS/m, categorized as excellent and suitable for all crops, while 30.77 % ranged from 2.0 to 4.0 dS/m (marginally saline), indicating permissible quality provided moderate leaching is practiced; the remaining 23.08% exceeded 4.0 dS/m, deemed saline and requiring active management to prevent soil salinization and yield decline. Sodium adsorption ratio (SAR) results indicated that 61.54 % of samples were in the low-sodium hazard range (<10), safe for irrigation, whereas 38.46 % were in the medium-risk (10-18) category, suggesting a need for regular monitoring and appropriate management such as gypsum application and selection of salt-tolerant crops; samples with SAR >18, though not observed in the dataset, would generally be considered unsuitable for irrigation due to high risk of sodicity-induced soil structural degradation33. All groundwater samples showed residual sodium carbonate (RSC) values below 1.25 mmol L-1, signifying the complete absence of residual carbonate hazard and confirming safe conditions for irrigation use a finding consistent with hydrochemical studies in similar semi-arid Indian regions28,31. Collectively, these results emphasize the importance of continuous monitoring and site-specific management, with international and national literature advocating tailored interventions (leaching, blending and crop selection) to mitigate emerging risks in regions with marginally saline or sodic groundwater.

The hydrochemical facies of groundwater, interpreted through the Durov diagram, indicate that the majority of samples fall in the Ca2+-Mg2+-CO3- type, suggesting that carbonate weathering and rock–water interaction are the dominant processes governing groundwater chemistry in the study area. These facies are typically associated with dissolution of calcite and dolomite, which contributes calcium, magnesium, and bicarbonate ions in stoichiometric proportions. A smaller cluster of samples trends toward the Na+-Cl- and Ca2+-Mg2+-SO42− facies, reflecting the influence of ion exchange, evaporative concentration, and possible anthropogenic sources such as agricultural return flows and domestic effluents. Similar patterns have been reported recently in hard-rock aquifers in south India, where inland salinity has emerged due to ion exchange and evaporation38.

From a water use perspective, Ca2+-Mg2+-HCO3− type waters are generally suitable for irrigation and domestic purposes, as they exhibit relatively low salinity and sodicity hazards (Fig. 10). However, samples plotting in the Na+-Cl− field indicate potential salinity and sodicity risks that could adversely affect soil permeability and crop productivity under long-term irrigation. The occurrence of SO42−-rich waters in some locations may be attributed to sulfide mineral oxidation or fertilizer leaching, underscoring localized anthropogenic influence. Moreover, large-scale modeling studies across India show increasing incidence of elevated electrical conductivity in groundwater, consistent with Na+-Cl− dominance under climatic stress and human impacts (). Overall, the Durov diagram demonstrates that while natural geochemical processes control the baseline groundwater chemistry, evaporation, ion exchange, and human activities play significant roles in altering water quality in specific zones, necessitating careful monitoring and management for sustainable utilization.

Durov diagram of the Pashupathihal sub-watershed.

The Schoeller semi-log diagram (Fig. 11) illustrates the ionic composition of groundwater samples, highlighting the dominance of major cations and anions. Across most samples, sodium + potassium (Na+ + K+) concentrations are consistently higher than calcium (Ca2+) and magnesium (Mg2+), indicating an increasing role of alkali elements in groundwater chemistry. This pattern suggests cation exchange processes, whereby Ca2+ and Mg2+ released from carbonate weathering are replaced by Na+ adsorbed on clay minerals (Appelo and Postma, 2005). Elevated Na+ in groundwater is often observed in semi-arid aquifers where prolonged water-rock interaction enhances base exchange reactions39.

Schoeller diagram of the Pashupathihal sub-watershed.

Among anions, bicarbonate + carbonate (HCO3− + CO32−) and sulfate (SO42−) show higher concentrations compared to chloride (Cl−). This suggests that carbonate weathering and gypsum dissolution are the main contributors to groundwater mineralization40. While chloride remains moderate, its occurrence indicates evaporative concentration and minor anthropogenic inputs from domestic or agricultural return flows38,41. Overall, the diagram shows that groundwater chemistry in the area is controlled by a combination of natural geochemical processes (carbonate dissolution, cation exchange, gypsum/anhydrite weathering) and anthropogenic inputs (chloride from agriculture and domestic effluents). The predominance of Na+ + K+ and HCO3- + SO42− indicates hydrochemical evolution towards Na-HCO3 and Na-SO4 facies in some wells, suggesting progressive mineralization and potential salinity risks for irrigation if these waters are used without management.

Discussion

Groundwater chemistry in the Pashupathihal Sub-watershed was fundamentally controlled by its geological and stratigraphic framework within the Deccan Plateau, where basaltic flows of the Deccan Traps constitute the principal aquifer system, with localized sedimentary exposures of the Kaladgi Group. The stratigraphic succession typically includes a weathered mantle overlying vesicular and massive basalt flows, with fractured and jointed horizons forming the main groundwater-bearing zones. Circulation through these weathered and fractured basalts facilitates silicate and carbonate weathering, releasing Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+ and HCO3−, which impart a slightly alkaline character typical of semi-arid basaltic aquifers. The hydrochemical characteristics therefore reflect strong lithological control combined with climatic factors, where prolonged water-rock interaction enhances hardness and dissolved solids and high evapotranspiration promotes salinity concentration. Although carbonate hazard remains negligible, moderate sodium enrichment in certain wells indicates active cation exchange within clay-rich weathered zones and possible agricultural return flows.

Multivariate analysis further substantiates that groundwater quality was predominantly governed by salinity hardness processes linked to basalt weathering, with secondary components representing alkalinity and localized anthropogenic influences, confirming that groundwater evolution in the sub-watershed is chiefly regulated by lithology, stratigraphic structure and semi-arid climatic conditions, with additional modification from agricultural activities. The parameter index values further emphasize that hardness-related constituents, particularly Ca2+, SO42−, Na+ and TH, are the principal contributors to groundwater quality variation, whereas pH and carbonate exert comparatively minor influence due to stable buffering conditions.

The correlation heatmap reveals strong positive relationships among EC, TDS, TH, Ca2+, Mg2+, SO42− and Cl−, confirming that these salinity-related parameters are largely derived from common geochemical processes such as mineral dissolution, evaporation and ion exchange within the basaltic aquifer system. The close association between EC and TDS reflects their shared role as indicators of total ionic strength, while moderate correlations among Na+, SAR and CO32− indicate that sodium enrichment and alkalinity are influenced by carbonate weathering and irrigation return flows. In contrast, weaker correlations of pH, K+ and B suggest localized or independent geogenic and agricultural sources.

The IWQI results showed that most samples fall within excellent to good categories, indicating generally suitable irrigation water; however, spatial variability highlights moderate to poorer quality in central and downstream zones where intensive agriculture, drainage accumulation and evapo-concentration elevate salinity and sodicity risks. Classification based on EC, SAR and RSC confirms the absence of carbonate hazard but identifies marginal salinity and medium sodium hazard in certain locations, requiring careful management. Regression analysis demonstrates that SAR and pH have the strongest influence on IWQI variability, highlighting the dominant role of sodicity and alkalinity over bulk salinity (TDS), while total hardness showed moderate contribution. Overall, groundwater suitability in the sub-watershed was primarily governed by hardness, sodium dominance and alkalinity, necessitating site-specific management and continuous monitoring to ensure sustainable irrigation practices.

The Piper diagram indicates that groundwater samples predominantly belong to the Ca2+-Mg2+-SO42—Cl− facies, reflecting the dominance of divalent cations and strong anions derived from basalt weathering, gypsum and halite dissolution and agricultural return flows, thereby suggesting moderate to high salinity influence. The Wilcox diagram and USSL diagram further classify most samples under high to very high salinity categories with low to medium sodium hazard, confirming that salinity is the principal constraint while sodicity poses a moderate risk requiring leaching and soil amendments. Interpretation through the Durov diagram shows that the dominant facies was Ca2+-Mg2+-HCO3−, indicating carbonate weathering and rock-water interaction as the baseline geochemical controls, whereas a subset of samples trends toward Na+-Cl− and Ca2+-Mg2+-SO42− types due to ion exchange, evaporative concentration and localized anthropogenic inputs. The Schoeller diagram supports this interpretation by revealing relative enrichment of Na+ + K+ over Ca2+ and Mg2+ in certain wells, suggesting active base exchange processes, while higher HCO3- and SO42− concentrations relative to Cl− indicate carbonate dissolution and gypsum weathering as major contributors to mineralization. Collectively, these graphical evaluations confirm that groundwater chemistry in the sub-watershed is primarily governed by carbonate weathering and mineral dissolution, with progressive evolution toward Na-dominated facies under the combined influence of ion exchange, evaporation and agricultural activities, thereby highlighting emerging salinity risks for irrigation sustainability.

Conclusion

The groundwater in the Pashupathihal Sub-watershed exhibits moderate to high salinity and sodicity hazards as indicated by a wide range in depth, pH, EC, cation and anion composition, and key irrigation indices. While the majority of samples are classified as suitable for irrigation (excellent to good by IWQI and CSSRI, Karnal standards), elevated sodium (SAR), salinity (EC and TDS) and dominant Ca2+-Mg2+-SO42—Cl− facies corroborated by Piper, Wilcox and USSL diagrams highlight localized risks for sensitive crops and soils due to mineral weathering, anthropogenic inputs and regional hydrogeology. The Durov and Schoeller diagrams reveal that groundwater is mainly of the Ca2+-Mg2+-HCO3− type due to carbonate weathering, but progressive shifts toward Na+-Cl− and Na+-SO42− facies from ion exchange, evaporation, and anthropogenic inputs indicate emerging salinity risks that require careful management. The integrated multivariate and regression analyses confirm that groundwater quality in the Pashupathihal Sub-watershed is primarily controlled by salinity, alkalinity, and sodicity processes governed by mineral dissolution, ion exchange, and carbonate weathering, with additional localized influences from agricultural inputs and irrigation return flow. The dominant role of EC-TDS hardness-major ions (Ca2+, Mg2+, SO₄2−, Cl−) identified through PCA, supported by strong inter-correlations, highlights evaporation-concentration and rock–water interaction as key hydrochemical drivers, while SAR, Na+ and HCO3- further shape the sodicity hazard influencing irrigation suitability. Regression analyses reinforce this pattern by showing that pH and SAR exert the highest control over IWQI, followed by moderate contributions from TH and minimal influence from TDS, emphasizing that ionic composition rather than total salinity is the critical determinant of irrigation water performance. Spatial IWQI patterns reveal that although much of the watershed possesses moderately suitable irrigation water, localized pockets of poor quality occur in central and downstream zones where low-lying, poorly drained Vertisols and intensive farming favor salt accumulation and sodium enrichment. Importantly, the Charge Balance Error (CBE) values (−6.61 to 7.80%, mean 1.09%) fall within acceptable limits, confirming the reliability of hydrochemical data and supporting the validity of interpretations. These findings, aligning with recent regional studies in semi-arid India, highlight the need for tailored management strategies including regular monitoring, soil amendments, leaching, blending with low-salinity water and crop selection actions essential to mitigate degradation, sustain productivity and ensure long-term water quality for agricultural use.
