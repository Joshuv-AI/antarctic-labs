Abstract

As part of this DOE Contract (Transformational Sorbent System for Post-Combustion Carbon
Capture, DE-FE0031734),TDA Research Inc. developed a transformational sorbent system for
post combustion CO2 capture process that captures more than 95% of CO2 emissions from a coal
fired power plant, recovering CO2 at 95% purity with a cost of CO2 capture significantly lower than
with amine-based system (~$30 per tonne (MT) of CO2 captured). TDA’s transformational sorbent
system uses a novel, highly stable, high-capacity metal organic framework (MOF) based CO2
sorbent in a new vacuum/concentration swing adsorption (VCSA) process that allows us to use
high efficiency vacuum pumps with a low auxiliary load. A pulverized coal fired power plant
equipped with TDA’s transformational sorbent system for post combustion CO 2 capture is
expected to efficiently produce electricity with a low Cost of Electricity (COE) and capture greater
than 95% of the CO2 from the power plant exhaust.

We increased the Technology Readiness Level (TRL) of TDA’s sorbent based transformational
CO2 capture process to TRL 5 (Basic technology components integrated and validated in a
relevant environment) from a TRL of 3 at the initiation of the project (Analytical and experimental
critical function and/or characteristic proof-of-concept validated), as defined by the U.S.
Department of Energy (DOE) Technology Readiness Assessment Guide. The technology is now
ready to begin work to reach “TRL 6 – Prototype validated in a relevant environment”.

We initially optimized and demonstrated the MOF sorbent performance at lab-scale and showed
that the sorbent maintains its capacity in the presence of flue gas contaminants (such as SO2,
and NOx). We then scaled-up the MOF sorbent production and carried out long term cycle tests
to demonstrate the life of the sorbent over 20,000 adsorption/desorption cycles. We designed the
VCSA cycles working with our partner University of Alberta (UOA). We also optimized the process
design and carried out a detailed cost analysis with our partner University of California Irvine (UCI)
to compare our technology against amines. We evaluated the MOF sorbent-based prototype unit,
first with simulated flue gas in the lab and then at Wyoming Integrated Test Center (WITC) in
Gillette, WY with actual coal-based flue gas for 2,300 hours. The sorbent maintained a stable
capacity with capture rates above 95% by vol. and produced a high purity CO 2 product. The
sorbent productivity was maintained at ~35 kg CO2/m3 per h with <5% loss in capacity over the
2,300 hour test with real coal derived flue gas containing ppm levels of NOx and SOx. Using the
data from the tests we optimized the VSA cycle design, finalized the process design, and carried
out an engineering analysis to fully assess the techno-economic viability of the new technology,
including an environmental health and safety (EH&S) assessment.

In the final techno-economic analysis (TEA) we showed that TDA’s MOF-sorbent based VCSA
process with 95% CO2 capture, producing 95% purity CO2 stream for sequestration achieves a
net plant efficiency of 32.68%. This MOF sorbent-based CCS has a 16.4% lower drop in net plant
efficiency compared to the DOE NO Capture Case B12A when compared to the drop in net plant
efficiency observed for the DOE Cansolv® Case B12B.95 with 95% CO2 capture. The cost of CO2
capture is estimated to be $30.4/tonne while the cost of CO2 avoided is $50.2/tonne. These costs
for TDA’s CO2 capture system are 18.5% and 18.9% lower than the corresponding costs of
$37.3/tonne and $61.9/tonne for the reference amine-based Cansolv® plant case B12B.95 (in the
Revision 4a DOE Study).

1. Executive Summary

As part of this DOE Contract, TDA Research Inc. (TDA) developed a transformational sorbent
system for post combustion CO2 capture process that captures more than 95% of CO2 emissions
from a coal fired power plant, recovering CO2 at 95% purity with a cost of CO2 capture significantly
lower than with amine-based system (~$30 per tonne (MT) of CO2 captured). TDA’s
transformational sorbent system uses a novel, highly stable, high-capacity metal organic
framework (MOF) based CO2 sorbent in a new vacuum/concentration swing adsorption (VCSA)
process that allows us to use high efficiency vacuum pumps with a low auxiliary load. A pulverized
coal fired power plant equipped with TDA’s transformational sorbent system for post combustion
CO2 capture is expected to efficiently produce electricity with a low Cost of Electricity (COE) and
capture greater than 95% of the CO2 from the power plant exhaust.

We initially optimized and demonstrated the MOF sorbent performance at lab-scale and showed
that the sorbent maintains its capacity in the presence of flue gas contaminants (such as SO 2,
and NOx). We then scaled-up the MOF sorbent production and carried out long term cycle tests
to demonstrate the life of the sorbent over 20,000 adsorption/desorption cycles. We designed the
VCSA cycles working with our partner University of Alberta (UOA). We also optimized the process
design and carried out a detailed cost analysis with our partner University of California Irvine (UCI)
to compare our technology against amines. We evaluated the MOF sorbent-based prototype unit,
first with simulated flue gas in the lab and then at Wyoming Integrated Test Center (WITC) in
Gillette, WY with actual coal-based flue gas for 2,300 hours. The sorbent maintained a stable
capacity with capture rates above 95% by vol. and produced a high purity CO 2 product. The
sorbent productivity was maintained at ~35 kg CO2/m3 per h with <5% loss in capacity over the
2,300 hour test with real coal derived flue gas containing ppm levels of NOx and SOx. Using the
data from the tests we optimized the VSA cycle design, finalized the process design, and carried
out an engineering analysis to fully assess the techno-economic viability of the new technology,
including an environmental health and safety (EH&S) assessment.

In the final techno-economic analysis (TEA) we showed that TDA’s MOF-sorbent based VCSA
process with 95% CO2 capture, producing 95% purity CO2 stream for sequestration achieves a
net plant efficiency of 32.68%. This MOF sorbent-based CCS has a 16.4% lower drop in net plant
efficiency compared to the DOE NO Capture Case B12A when compared to the drop in net plant
efficiency observed for the DOE Cansolv® Case B12B.95 with 95% CO2 capture. The cost of CO2
capture is estimated to be $30.4/tonne while the cost of CO2 avoided is $50.2/tonne. These costs
for TDA’s CO2 capture system are 18.5% and 18.9% lower than the corresponding costs of
$37.3/tonne and $61.9/tonne for the reference amine-based Cansolv® plant case B12B.95 (in the
Revision 4a DOE Study).

With the successful completion of the R&D effort, the technology readiness has been raised from
TRL 3 (Analytical and experimental critical function and/or characteristic proof of concept) to TRL
5 (Basic technology components integrated and validated in a relevant environment), as defined
by the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Technology Readiness Assessment Guide, which
indicates that the technology is now ready for to be advanced to “TRL 6 – Prototype validated in
a relevant environment”.

The transformational sorbent system being developed in this project for CO2 capture has
commercial applications in post combustion capture of CO2 from flue gas generated by pulverized
coal fired power plants and industrial coal-fired boilers. The CO2 captured can then be
sequestered (stored in underground reservoirs). In the near-term, the captured CO2 can be used
in enhanced oil recovery (EOR) and to produce industrial and food grade CO2.

2. Introduction

Fossil fuels supply over 85% of world’s energy and are likely to do so for the next several decades.
However, the large-scale use of fossil fuels is threatened by the real possibility of global climate
change. The major source for the anthropogenic CO2 emissions is the combustion of fossil fuels,
particularly the use of coal in power generation. Without reducing CO2 emissions of existing
power plants, it is impossible to reduce overall CO2 emissions. Therefore, we have developed a
cost-effective way to control CO2 emissions from existing coal-fired power plants. Our new post
combustion sorbent-based carbon capture system (CCS) captures more than 95% of the CO2
emissions from a coal fired power plant with 95+% purity on dry basis at a significantly lower cost
than existing amine-based systems due to our facile vacuum assisted low pressure steam
regeneration process that we refer to as a vacuum assisted concentration swing adsorption
(VCSA) process. Our process is a cost effective way to control CO2 emissions from existing coal-
fired power plants.

In several Small Business Innovative Research (SBIR) projects funded by DOE, DOD and NASA
(DE-SC0015169, N0017818C8009, NNX17CM55P). In the previous DOE project TDA developed
a robust family of MOF sorbents and demonstrated their techno-economic viability for CO2 capture
from low concentration emission sources (such as natural gas-fired power plants). In other
projects, we have shown the CO2 removal capability of these materials in life support applications
for spacesuits (in a VSA cycle with space vacuum for regeneration) and in the air revitalization of
submarines (in a temperature-swing cycle to remove 5,000 ppmv CO2 from the cabin air and
pressurize it to dissolve in seawater for rejection overboard without generating an acoustic trace
or bubbles). We also developed methods to fabricate the MOFs into mechanically strong pellets
to provide the robustness needed, meeting the stringent mechanical integrity requirements in the
high shock/vibration environments encountered during a rocket launch or a submarine.

In these projects, we showed that our MOF sorbents could achieve working capacities of 4.4%
wt. and 7.0% wt. when challenged with 1% to 16% vol. CO2, respectively (to our knowledge these
are the highest reported working capacities ever for a physical adsorbent operated under these
conditions). The tests were carried out in a bench-scale fixed-bed reactor under mild vacuum.
The sorbent maintained a stable capacity for more than 10,000 cycles under simulated flue gas
conditions. The preliminary process design for a coal-fired power plant using our MOF as a stand-
alone post-combustion CO2 removal system indicated significant energy savings and reduction in
CO2 capture cost against traditional solvent scrubbing systems. The most important cost saving
is due to the reduction of the energy input to support the sorbent regeneration. The low heat of
adsorption of CO2 (34-45 kJ/mol) indicated that the CO2 is not too tightly bounded to the surface,
so the energy input to regenerate the sorbent is relatively low (as the full recovery of this energy
at such a low regeneration temperature is not economically feasible). The sorbent-CO2 interaction
is however sufficiently strong that the sorbent can achieve high capacity and selectivity. While
we demonstrated the benefits of a similar physical adsorbent (TDA’s nitrogen functionalized
mesoporous carbon sorbent), (attracting CO2 with the right interaction energy) in an earlier
DOE/NETL project (DE-FE0007580), the new MOF sorbents represents a great potential to
reduce capital cost due to its much higher capacity for CO2 (a factor of 4 reduction in bed size).
The smaller MOF sorbent beds also improve plant efficiency due to the reduced parasitic load.
The adsorptive properties of the new sorbent and the cycle sequence proposed here allow us to
run a VCSA process, eliminating any steam purge, which further improves the efficiency. Our
initial calculations indicated that the VCSA process using our MOF sorbent will only consume 117
kWh/tonne of CO2 captured, which resulted in 30.4% net plant efficiency at 90% CO2 capture for
the baseline 550 MW sub-critical plant included in DOE Report (DOE/NETL-2010/1397 Rev.2a
Sep 2013).

3. TDA’s Process

Figure 2. TDA’s CCS integrated to a coal-fired power plant.
Figure 2 shows the schematic of the transformational VCSA process that integrates the novel
sorbent into a new or existing coal-fired power plant. The flue gas from the wet FGD scrubber
enters the adsorption bed, which removes 90% of the CO2 and a large amount of the H2O from
the flue gas (the TDA MOF can achieve over 99% CO2 removal efficiency if needed as there is
no equilibrium or process limitation that prevents a high capture efficiency). The sorbent
regeneration is carried out under a mild vacuum (0.1-0.2 psia) using commercially available
vacuum equipment to recover the CO2 and H2O from the bed; the CO2/H2O stream is compressed,
cooled and the water is condensed so that the CO2 can be recovered as a high-purity product.
The very high selectivity of the sorbent towards CO2 (against N2) ensures a very high purity CO2
product. After water condensation, the CO2 purity will be well above 95% vol. At the end of the
vacuum step, there is still significant amounts of CO2 adsorbed on the bed (as only mild vacuum
is applied to reduce the energy cost, the sorbent is not fully regenerated).

The regeneration process is completed by exposing the bed to the clean flue gas purge or a low
pressure steam purge. This purge step will remove any CO2 in the dead volume of the bed and
from the sorbent to ensure that very high removal efficiency can be achieved in the subsequent
cycle.

The VCSA-cycle consists of a 4-bed cycling schedule including cycle steps of adsorption,
pressure equalization, desorption under mild vacuum with purge, pressure equalization, and
purge gas pressurization to prepare for adsorption. The scheme utilizes pressure equalization
between beds instead of a co-current blow down step, before entering the desorption step of the
cycle. Each bed is at high pressure (~20 psia) during adsorption, followed by an equalization at
~10 psia, a purge down to 1-2 psia and an equalization back up to ~10psia followed by clean flue
gas pressurization up to 20 psia to prepare for adsorption in the next cycle. While a given bed is
in adsorption condition, the sorbent temperature increases with the top of the bed nearing the
inlet temperature (40ºC) while the rest of the bed remains around 30ºC. The total full cycle time
will be 3 minutes.

4. Project Objectives

The objective of this project was to develop a transformational sorbent system for post combustion
CO2 capture process that captures more than 90% of CO2 emissions from a coal fired power
plant, recovering CO2 at 95% purity with a cost of electricity at least 30% lower than with amine-
based system from DOE baseline Rev 4 basis study (i.e., < $32.2 per tonne of CO2 captured) and
showing potential to further lower the cost of CO2 capture to < $30/tonne of CO2 captured.

TDA Research, Inc. (TDA) worked with University of Alberta (UOA), University of California Irvine
(UCI) and the Wyoming Integrated Test Center (WITC) to develop, optimize and test the
transformational sorbent system. TDA developed a high-capacity solid sorbent, evaluate its cyclic
life, and show its performance both in lab and bench scale experiments using simulated flue gas
in the presence of impurities such as moisture, NOx, SOx and HCl. Collaborating with UOA, we
developed adsorption models and optimized the novel VCSA cycles to achieve the DOE targets
(90% capture with 95% CO2 purity, and later in the project increased our capture rates to 95%
capture with 95% minimum CO2 purity to match DOE’s latest Rev.4a basis Baseline study) while
minimizing the energy requirements. UCI and later our consultant Dr. Ashok Rao (retired Chief
Scientist of the Advanced Power and Energy Program at UCI) modeled the mass and energy
balances for a commercial coal-fired power plant integrated with our CO2 capture system and
calculated the plant efficiency. The experimental and simulation data was used to perform an
extensive engineering and economic analysis following the NETL’s “Carbon Capture and
Sequestration Systems Analysis Guidelines”. Wyoming ITC hosted a prototype unit to evaluate
the sorbent’s performance and the ability of the VCSA process to meet the DOE objectives using
actual coal-derived flue gas.

4.1 Work Plan

The proposed work was split into 3 budget periods. In Budget Period 1 (BP1) we optimized and
demonstrated sorbent performance at lab-scale and showed through computational modeling the
technical merit of the sorbent and the process. We assessed the impact of gas contaminants
(such as SO2, NOx and HCl) on sorbent performance.

In Budget Period 2, we scaled-up the sorbent production and evaluated its large-volume
production cost. We carried out long term cycle tests to demonstrate the life of the sorbent over
20,000 adsorption/desorption cycles. We designed and constructed a prototype system with fixed-
bed radial flow reactors that have low pressure drop while handling large flue gas flows. In BP2,
we designed the VCSA cycles working with our partner UOA. We also optimized the process
design and carried out a detailed cost analysis with UCI to compare our technology against amine
based solvent CO2 capture processes.

In BP3, we evaluated the prototype unit, first with simulated flue gas in the lab and then at WITC
with actual coal-based flue gas (for a minimum of 4,000 hours). We optimized the VSA cycle
design, finalize the process design, and carry out an engineering analysis to fully assess the
techno-economic viability of the new technology, including an environmental health and safety
(EH&S) assessment.

6.7 Task 7. Scale-up of Sorbent production (BP2)

The current bench-scale sorbent preparation batch size is ~100g of finished MOF sorbent per
batch and requires a minimum reactor volume of 20L (round bottom flask). The formation of the
interlaced structure requires the metal salt and the linker to each be dissolved in their own
dissimilar solvent, then layered within the reactor. MOF synthesis takes place at the interface
layer between the two solvents. Mixing of each layer can speed the synthesis but must not disturb
the solution interface or cause intermixing of the solvents.

Sorbent cost can be reduced by increasing the space-time-yield for the preparation is to minimize
the solvent volume – the solubilities of the salt and linker are both low in their respective solvents,
resulting in large reactor volume with respect to the product yield. One method of decreasing the
solvent volume is to present an overage of reactant to each solution. Each solution will maintain
a constant concentration of reactant, which should also improve the synthesis rate.

In these tests, we attempted to reduce solvent volume as described, using the current reactor
size of 20L. Comparisons between space-time yield and time yield for varied volumes of solvent
and varied temperature were made. Solution reuse and recycle was investigated to accomplish
this, with batch evaluation to determine any changes in the MOF performance. This data was
used to produce a minimum of four batches at the same scale and preparation conditions to show
a repeatable process and product before moving to the 160L filter reactor system.

Linker Synthesis
Another method for reducing the final product cost is
to find reductions in the raw material cost. The
highest cost input for the TDA MOF is the linker:
current cost projections show that the linker cost is
68% of the total raw materials cost. The same
equipment can be used to scale production of the
linker as an additional process step. The synthesis
takes place at slightly elevated temperatures over a
period of five days. The final purified linker product
would remain in the reactor at the end of synthesis
and would be ready for the introduction of its solvent
for the start of the TDA MOF synthesis. Preparation
of the final MOF using this method is projected to
reduce the raw material costs by 60%.

160L Filter Reactor System
The 160L equipment decreases the handling steps Figure 31. DPA synthesis.
involved in the sorbent synthesis by combining
multiple processes into a single unit. Our Hastelloy reactor provides the necessary resistance to

A process flow diagram describing the batch procedure to produce TDA MOF is shown in Figure

to the reactor, then the solvent is pumped in, starting the synthesis. To ensure enough time for
reactants to dissolve and precipitated MOF, total time in solution is 40 hours under agitation. The
final product is filtered to separate the MOF from the solvent, using high pressure gas to
pressurize the reactor headspace and expedite the filtration steps. The resulting cake is then
washed with additional solvent to remove any unreacted components. The wash step consists of
one solvent rinse of the cake, followed by repulp of the cake with another wash of solvent which
will undergo a final filtration after a 16 hour soak. All solvent recovered can be recycled into the
next prep to reduce or eliminate any solvent waste stream and return any unreacted materials to
the next preparation. Total turn-around time for a preparation was initially expected to be four

Synthesis Parameters

The parameters investigated were synthesis time, synthesis temperature, synthesis solvents, and
reactant concentrations. We looked at the space-time yield and the space yield for these preps
to determine the impact of these parameters. The first parameter was the reactant concentration.
The baseline preparations used a large excess of solvent for dissolving each reactant, resulting
in a space yield of 7g/L. Only one parameter step was investigated: room temperature saturation
for each reactant in its associated solvent. This increased the space yield to 79g/L. All
subsequent tests used saturated solutions for synthesis.

The next parameter of interest was the synthesis time. The baseline synthesis time was 7 days:
we investigated 5 days, 3 days, and 1 day for a series of preparation, all at room temperature
during synthesis (the baseline temperature). The results are shown on Figure 34. The space
yield data indicates that 3, 5, and 7 day preparations all result in the same amount of product, so
there is no advantage to longer preparation time when using yield as the metric. The 1-day preps
did show some variability, with the best exhibiting similar space yield as the longer preparations.
However, the space-time yield shows a significant improvement over the 3-day preparations for
even the worst case measured. This lowest space-time yield occurred on a sample in which the
reactants were added to the solvents, and then used for synthesis without filtration. In the
baseline preparation all dissolved reactants are filtered prior to synthesis to remove any
undissolved components or contamination, a time-consuming process. The relatively low impact
of removing the filtration step is important for future work on space-time yield improvement using
an excess of reactants in the solvents to run a constant-concentration synthesis. Due to these
results on synthesis time, all follow-up tests used a 1-day synthesis.

We ran cycles without the NOx and SOx to create a stable baseline before adding the

we increased the gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) and slowly reduced the cycle time. We

performance conditions (10 ppm NOx and SOx, 500 GHSV, initial cycle timing) and the
performance again rose to >4.0 wt% CO2 which demonstrates that the reduction in performance
in previous cycles were only due to the more severe process conditions and not due to
deactivation of the sorbent. We completed 20,000 cycles on 12/15/2023. The cycling results
demonstrated that the sorbent retains a capacity of ~4% wt. CO2 at the end of 20,000 cycles,
similar to the start in the presence of 10 ppm SOx and 10 ppm NOx (successfully completing

The Baseline 4-bed cycle scheme used in the field tests along with the schedule for the 4-beds
are shown in Figure 59. In this cycle scheme one bed is always under adsorption while one other
bed is always under desorption in the countercurrent blowdown/ evacuation step. Between these
two steps the Bed undergoes equalization i.e., the bed coming off the adsorption step (at high
pressure) equalizes in pressure with a bed coming off regeneration (at low pressure). After
countercurrent blowdown we have the purge step that either uses a clean flue gas purge for dry

Vacuum Pump Selection and Costs: TDA’s carbon capture process consists of a CO2
separation system that selectively removes CO2 from flue gas using a physical sorbent. The
sorbent is regenerated under a combination of vacuum and purge flow in a series of regeneration
steps. This vacuum is generated by a vacuum regeneration pumps. Hence, the vacuum pump
operation is critical to the performance of the carbon capture system. The cost and energy use
of the vacuum system are major contributors to the cost of the carbon capture process. Thus,

 67

TDA has carried out extensive optimization work for the vacuum pump design to reduce the
size/cost of the vacuum pumps and minimize the energy requirement for the vacuum process by
investigating the use of different types of the vacuum pump and their design configurations, as
well as various gas treatment methods (e.g., gas cooling methods, the use of water knockouts
and other heat exchange and condensate removal equipment). We investigated two types of
pumps for the vacuum swing process: mechanical vapor recompression pumps and dry screw
pumps. These were modeled from quotes obtained from manufacturers of these pumps.
Mechanical vapor recompression pumps (MVRs) tend to have high efficiencies and moderate
compression ratios. They can handle wet streams and can thus use direct water injection as a
means of cooling. However, they cannot tolerate large flow fluctuations. Dry screw pumps tend to
have lower efficiencies than the MVRs, but they can accommodate flow fluctuations and higher
compression ratios. They do not handle water as well and require indirect heat exchangers for
cooling.

Based on the specifications summary and the costs from the manufacturers we concluded that
the best vacuum pump configuration to be implemented in TDA’s process are mechanical vapor
recompressors (MVR) due to their high capacity resulting in less individual pieces of equipment,
high efficiency resulting in lower operating costs and over lower equipment costs. In our final
design we used three vacuum pumps operating in parallel per train resulting in a total of 12 pumps
for a total cost of $63.6 million ($5.3 million per pump) on $2018 basis. We used one blower each
per train, and the cost of each of the blowers was estimated to be $1.3 million on $2018 basis
based on quotes we received from Piller in 2024, resulting in a total of $6.2 million for the blowers
on $2018 basis.
