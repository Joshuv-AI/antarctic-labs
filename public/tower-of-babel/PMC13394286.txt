Sustainable and green catalytic cycloaddition reactions mediated by well-characterized ZnO@PDA-B nanocatalysts for the catalytic conversion of CO2 to cyclic carbonates
Authors: Gulistan Kochan, Eyyup Yasar, Ahmet Tumbul, Ahmet Kilic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The sustainable fixation of CO2 into organic cyclic carbonates as high-value chemicals represents a promising green strategy for achieving net-zero emissions and promoting 100% atom economy. At this point, we report a series of various boron-doped active ZnO@PDA-B(1–5) nanocatalysts prepared by the precipitation-calcination method for catalytic CO2 conversion. The structures of the rationally designed and highly effective ZnO@PDA-B nanocatalysts were characterized using FT-IR, Raman, TGA-DTA, XRD, SEM-EDX, TEM, XPS, BET, ICP-OES, and zeta potential analysis. The catalytic activity was evaluated in CO2-epoxide cycloaddition reactions, and the catalytic conversion and selectivity results were quantified by 1H NMR spectroscopy. Among ZnO@PDA-B(1–5) nanocatalysts, the quaternary phosphonium-containing ionic boron-doped nanocatalyst (ZnO@PDA-B5) exhibited the highest catalytic efficiency, enabling the synthesis of a broad range of internal/terminal cyclic carbonates with diverse functional groups. Under optimal conditions (10 mg catalyst, 100 °C, ambient CO2 pressure, 2 h), the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst achieved 96% conversion of epichlorohydrin (ECH) and 98% selectivity in the absence of solvent. The ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst also exhibits robust recyclability after five reaction cycles and long-term stability. Finally, due to the synergistic effect between the Lewis-acidic ZnO and B centers in the proposed reaction mechanism, both components contribute to epoxide activation, and the chloride anion of PPNCl promotes epoxide ring opening, leading to efficient CO2 incorporation in the CO2 fixation process.

Introduction

From the perspective of green chemistry principles and circular economy goals, the chemical conversion of CO2 into high-value-added products has attracted attention in recent years. On the other hand, CO2 is also considered a vital resource for addressing climate change and meeting the growing demand for valuable fuels and chemicals.1–3 In addition, chemical CO2 fixation is attracting interest as a means to address concerns and challenges in achieving the defossilisation goal by 2050, owing to its 100% atom economy, synthesis advantages, and mass-production targets.4 Paradoxically, CO2 is an inexhaustible, economically viable, non-toxic C1 source that can be converted into valuable chemicals, thereby contributing to sustainable ecological development.5–8 In this context, converting CO2 into value-added cyclic carbonates stands out as the most efficient, sustainable, and recyclable approach. Cyclic carbonates are used in polar aprotic solvents, pharmaceutical and cosmetic chemicals, electrolytes for lithium-ion batteries, reaction intermediates, monomers for polycarbonate synthesis, and in cleaning, coating, and adhesive processes.9–17

To date, numerous catalytic systems have been developed for sustainable CO2 cycloaddition reactions to overcome the thermodynamic and kinetic stability of CO2, including metal complexes, boron compounds, quaternary phosphonium/ammonium salts, N-heterocyclic carbenes (NHCs), and ionic liquids.18–37 However, due to the inherent disadvantages of these homogeneous or organocatalysts, such as difficulty in catalyst-product separation, low recyclability, and potential metal contamination, heterogeneous catalysts have begun to attract attention. Among these heterogeneous catalysts, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), covalent organic frameworks (COFs), and functional group-supported catalysts have begun to be used to synthesize cyclic carbonates via CO2 coupling with various epoxides under suitable conditions.38–42 Despite these developments and the advantages of various catalysts, critical challenges remain in converting CO2 into five-membered cyclic carbonates using suitable approaches. Recently, metal oxide-based compounds have been investigated as inexpensive, readily synthesized, stable, recyclable, and highly active heterogeneous catalysts for the conversion of CO2 into high-value-added cyclic carbonates under suitable conditions. Among these metal oxides, ZnO nanocatalysts have attracted considerable attention in the synthesis of cyclic carbonates from CO2 and epoxides due to their simple, controllable preparation, eco-friendliness, low cost, high thermal and chemical stability, tunable acid-base sites, and abundant oxygen vacancies.43–47 Additionally, ZnO was chosen for its amphoteric nature, which provides both basic sites for CO2 activation and acidic sites beneficial for the chemical coupling reaction. However, the pores on the surface of the ZnO in the structure provide excellent adsorption sites for CO2, and this adsorbed CO2 serves as the C1 building block for forming the target cyclic carbonate.43 On the other hand, the synergistic interaction between the Lewis acid sites of zinc and boron in the structure formed by the combination of ZnO and boron sources is expected to improve the overall catalytic performance for the CO2 cycloaddition reaction due to the high affinity for activation by CO2 on the catalyst surface.43,48 Moreover, it is predicted that adding Lewis-acidic boron compounds with unique electronic properties to ZnO structures will result in more Lewis acid–base dual active sites, more oxygen vacancies, and a larger surface area through morphological modification of the structure.43–45

To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the pioneering works on the preparation of five-membered cyclic carbonates via the cycloaddition reaction of CO2 and epoxides using robust, practical, cost-effective, and well-designed ZnO@PDA-B(1–5) nanocatalysts. The PDA in the prepared ZnO@PDA-B(1–5) nanocatalysts acts as a protective layer that prevents the aggregation of ZnO nanoparticles and stabilizes the ZnO centers, thereby reducing the likelihood of metal leaching under cyclization reaction conditions. Additionally, it polarizes CO2 due to the Lewis-basic nitrogen groups in its structure. On the other hand, the strong synergistic effect between ZnO and boron atoms in the structure promotes the formation of edge defects, creating more active sites through more extensive surface morphological modification, thereby increasing CO2 conversion activity and consequently catalytic efficiency. In addition, the designed ZnO@PDA-B type nanocatalysts enable easier overcoming of the thermodynamic stability and kinetic inertness of CO2 through precise, atomic-level electronic means. This sets a target for highly controlled, efficient, and selective industrial applications. When TBAI is tested alone as a catalyst, it exhibits relatively low catalytic activity in the chemical coupling of CO2 with epoxides. However, consisting of a homogeneous TBAI organic salt acting as a nucleophile with good separation capability and heterogeneous ZnO@PDA-B nanocatalysts providing Lewis acid active sites (e.g., Zn and B centers), performs the cyclization reaction of CO2 with epoxides as an alternative to toxic and expensive phosgene gas by using dry ice as a CO2 source to increase contact between CO2 and epoxides. In this study, the catalytic activity of ZnO@PDA-B(1–5) nanocatalysts was initially assessed for the chemical fixation of CO2 and epoxides at 100 °C and ambient CO2 pressure for 2 hours, with ZnO@PDA-B5 demonstrating the highest catalytic conversion and selectivity under solvent-free conditions. Later, the effects of some optimal parameters on catalytic conversion were investigated. ZnO@PDA-B5 is suitable for reuse studies because it is the most active catalyst. It exhibits pseudo-first-order kinetics under ambient conditions, offering promising opportunities for sustainable CO2 fixation across various industrial applications and synthetic chemistry research. Furthermore, this catalytic system not only demonstrates the feasibility of replacing traditional heterogeneous catalysts in some aspects, but also offers a new paradigm for the rational design of novel metal oxide-based catalytic platforms for sustainable CO2 utilization.

Experimental studies

Chemical reagents and instruments

The internal/terminal epoxides used in this study were supplied in commercial purity by commercial companies such as Merck, Across, or Sigma-Aldrich and were used as received without prior purification. In addition, the solvents and chemicals used to synthesize the target ZnO@PDA-B-type catalysts were of commercial purity and used without prior purification. The Burkle Snow Package Dry Ice Production Apparatus was used to produce dry ice, which served as the CO2 source for the chemical CO2 fixation studies, and CO2 gas of the required purity was obtained from commercial companies. In this study, only the characterization of the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst, the most active catalyst, was performed. Information regarding the characterization techniques is listed below in order. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) was performed on a Rigaku Ultimate III X-ray diffractometer possessing a CuKα radiation tube (λ = 1.54 Å) operating at 40 kV and 40 mA across a range of 20–80 (2θ) and a rate of 3 °C min−1. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) micrographs and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectra were obtained using a Zeiss Sigma 300 at 15 kV under high vacuum, and the samples were coated with gold. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) micrographs were recorded using a Hitachi HighTech HT7700. Nitrogen adsorption/desorption isotherms were recorded using a Micromeritics 3Flex Monosorb Surface Area Analyzer after the catalyst was degassed at 100 °C for 12 hours. The thermal properties of the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst were determined using a Shimadzu DTG-60/60H-TG-DTA thermogravimetric analysis instrument at a constant heating rate of 10 °C min−1 from 25 to 1000 °C in an N2 atmosphere, with an empty alumina crucible as the reference. Specs-Flex X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) equipment was used to determine the elemental composition and chemical states of the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst. The functional groups of the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst were determined using a PerkinElmer FT-IR spectrometer with a UATR polarizer over the frequency range of 4000 to 400 cm−1. Micro-Raman spectral studies were carried out on a Wltech alpha 300R spectrometer with a CCD detector over the range of 3500–500 cm−1. The Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZSP instrument performed the zeta-sizer studies. The Zn and B contents were determined using ICP-OES with a PerkinElmer 5300 instrument. The chemical cyclization reaction of CO2 with epoxides to form target cyclic carbonates was performed under suitable conditions (ambient CO2 pressure, 100 °C, 2 h). 1H NMR spectra of the crude reaction mixture were used to determine the catalytic conversion and selectivity of the five-membered cyclic carbonates. The 1H NMR spectra were acquired on an Agilent Technologies 400 MHz spectrometer. LC-MS/MS spectra of the obtained pure cyclic carbonates were recorded in methanol using a Shimadzu LC-MS/MS spectrometer in positive ESI-SIM mode. After obtaining the 1H and 13C NMR, FT-IR, and LC-MS/MS results of the pure cyclic carbonates for structural characterization under similar conditions (see SI file).

Preparation of ZnO@PDA-B(1–5) nanocatalysts

ZnO@PDA-B(1–5) nanocatalysts were prepared using a precipitation-calcination method with some modifications, as described in the literature.49,50 According to a typical procedure established for synthesizing ZnO, 10 g of zinc chloride (ZnCl2, ≥98% purity) salt was first added to the reaction flask and dissolved in distilled water. Following this, 5 mL of a 0.2 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution was added dropwise to the prepared solution. To achieve complete precipitation in the resulting chemical mixture, the reaction was stirred in a magnetic stirrer at 70 °C for 5–6 hours. To ensure complete precipitation, the mixture was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 15–20 minutes. The resulting precipitate was washed several times with distilled water and absolute ethanol, then dried overnight in an oven at 60 °C. The white solid material, dried in the oven, was ground into a powder in a mortar and then calcined in the ash furnace at 300 °C for 4 hours.51,52 After elucidating the structure of ZnO nanoparticles using various spectroscopic techniques, 0.18 g of ZnO was dispersed in 50 mL of ethanol in each reaction flask. Then, 100 mg of polydopamine hydrochloride (PDA, ≥98% purity) was dissolved in 20 mL of ethanol, and 100 µL of triethylamine (TEA) was added dropwise. All reactions were stirred at 50–60 °C for 24 hours. Then, 100 mg of different boron compounds (B1–B5, 99–97% purity) (1.62 mmol for B1, 0.28 mmol for B2, 0.23 mmol for B3, 0.22 mmol for B4, and 0.17 mmol for B5), dissolved in 10 mL of ethyl alcohol, were added dropwise to the solutions and stirred for 24 hours at room temperature. The resulting ZnO@PDA-B(1–5) nanocatalysts were collected by centrifugation and separated by filtration. To remove impurities, they were washed several times with distilled water and cold ethanol, then dried in an oven.49 The boron sources (B2–B5) used in this study were prepared according to methods described in previously published studies,53–56 but with modified stoichiometric and reaction conditions (Scheme 1). These boron compounds were chosen to compare commercially available and synthesized versions and to assess the effects of their distinct chemical structures on catalytic conversion.

General procedure for the chemical CO2 fixation studies

The chemical CO2 fixation catalytic reactions were conducted in a non-leaking 16 × 160 mm tube connected to a dry ice system as the CO2 source. Before the catalytic experiments were conducted, all non-leaking test tubes were purged with CO2 gas several times. The non-leaking tube was charged with various epoxides (7.80 mmol), ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst (10 mg), bis(triphenylphosphoranylidene)ammonium chloride (PPNCl, 97% purity) as a co-catalyst (0.078 mmol), and 3.5 g of dry ice as the CO2 source. All prepared test tubes were then placed in the oil bath on the temperature-controlled heater, and the catalytic process was initiated under identical conditions (Scheme 2). Catalytic epoxide conversion tests were performed by adjusting reaction conditions to enable the chemical cyclization of CO2 and epoxides to form cyclic carbonates under solvent-free conditions. After the chemical cyclization reactions were finished, the non-leaking test tubes were slowly brought to room temperature, and the CO2 pressure was slowly reduced. After incorporating an appropriate amount of the crude reaction mixture into 3 mL of CHCl3, the epoxide conversion of the crude mixture was measured by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Agilent Technologies 400 MHz spectrometer). Conversion/yield and selectivity were calculated from the integral areas of the proton signals of the characteristic epoxides and corresponding cyclic carbonates, as shown in the previous studies (Scheme 3).57–59 When no by-products are observed in the CO2 conversion studies, the selectivity is given as >99%. Reuse applications

For the reusability experiments of the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst, after the CO2 and ECH chemical conversion reaction was completed, the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst was separated from the reaction mixture by centrifugation and then washed with acetone (3 × 5 mL). The separated ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst was further dried at 60 °C for 24 h in a vacuum oven for subsequent reuse studies. Then, the dried ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst was reused for further catalytic runs under similar reaction conditions. Since PPNCl is highly soluble in the epoxide-based reaction system, the fresh PPNCl was added back into the test tube along with the recovered ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst in each reuse study. Subsequently, a portion of the crude cyclic carbonates formed by the cycloaddition reaction was collected, dissolved in 3 mL of CHCl3, and analyzed by 1H NMR spectroscopy.

Characterization of the ZnO@PDA-B(1–5) nanocatalysts

As shown in Fig. 1a, the FT-IR spectrum of ZnO@PDA-B5, the most active nanocatalyst, was obtained in crystalline powder form using the ATR technique in the frequency range of 4000 to 400 cm−1. The specific absorption peaks at 3676 and 1489 cm−1 are assigned to the stretching vibration of υ(O–H), likely originating from the atmospheric mixture in which the analyses were performed, which is adsorbed on the surface of the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst.59–61 In addition, stretching peaks were observed at 2990 and 2899 cm−1, which are assigned to the symmetric and asymmetric υ(C–H) vibrations due to solvent or surface contamination.44 In particular, when the FT-IR spectrum of the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst was examined, a new peak assigned to the υ(B–O) stretching vibration at 1250 cm−1 was observed, different from the vibration frequencies of ZnO.52,61 Additionally, the FT-IR spectrum of the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst shows a new peak at 479 cm−1, assigned to the stretching vibration of the crystalline hexagonal ZnO structure.60–62 On the other hand, new stretching vibration peaks appeared in the FT-IR spectrum of ZnO@PDA-B5 at 1058, 865, and 694 cm−1, respectively. These peaks most likely correspond to the υ(C–O), υ(B–O–C), and υ(B–C) vibrational modes, arising from sp2 hybridization and the structure of planar triangular boron compounds.59–61 The FT-IR spectral findings indicate the successful formation of the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst and the presence of boron nuclei in the structure. Thermal analysis measurements of the prepared ZnO@PDA-B5, the most active nanocatalyst, were performed using a TGA-DTA device, and the resulting thermograms are presented in Fig. 1c. When examining the DTA curves of the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst, the main exothermic peak is observed at approximately 380 °C, attributed to the presence of the B dopant and organic components in the ZnO lattice structure.63 In the TGA thermogram of the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst, a small weight loss was observed at the very beginning of the measurement, attributed to physically bound water, desorption of hydroxyl groups from ZnO, or a small amount of moisture. On the other hand, the weight loss of the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst begins at approximately 200 °C due to the decomposition of surface organics remaining from precursor salts, volatile solvents, or PDA compounds.59 In contrast, the main weight losses occurred between 320 and 525 °C due to the decomposition or removal of organic groups from the structure, accompanied by the conversion of ZnO to a crystallized hexagonal structure (Fig. 1c). Above approximately 500 °C, no significant weight loss was observed. These thermal changes in ZnO-based structures are consistent with previously published studies.59,62–65Fig. 1c shows the XRD pattern of the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanostructure, collected at a scan rate of 3° min−1 over the 2θ range of 20–80°. According to the XRD pattern, the nanostructure is wurtzite hexagonal, with the preferred orientation along the (101) Miller plane. No impurity peaks are observed in the XRD pattern compared with the standard XRD pattern of ZnO (JCPDS 36-1451). The Raman spectrum of the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst was investigated. The Raman studies of B-doped ZnO nanoparticles provide valuable insight into the structural and lattice properties. However, in the Raman spectra, the ZnO nanoparticle exhibited an E2 (high) vibrational mode, and the characteristic E2 (high) Raman mode of wurtzite ZnO, associated with Zn–O bond vibration, served as a precise indicator of the crystal order.73

Fig. 2(a and b) shows the morphology of the boron-doped ZnO nanoparticle captured using a scanning electron microscope at various magnifications. It is worth noting that the film surfaces consist of well-defined spherical particles at the nanometer scale, depending on boron incorporation. Boron incorporation at ∼1.16 wt% (as confirmed by EDX) appears to favor non-uniform flower-like particle shapes and reduced agglomeration due to growth dynamics and altered nucleation induced by B3+ substitution in the ZnO matrix.74 There is no evidence of phase segregation or irregular morphologies, indicating good dopant integration and phase purity. The rough particle surfaces and distinct boundaries suggest that boron incorporation affects the nucleation and growth process, possibly promoting anisotropic crystal properties.75 The XRD results support this phenomenon. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was performed to further validate the composition of the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanoparticle. The EDS results (summarized in the table within Fig. 2c) reveal that the material is primarily composed of 73.87 wt% Zn and 8.82 wt% O, confirming a ZnO-based matrix. The detection of 1.16 wt% boron clearly indicates successful incorporation into the ZnO lattice. Acting as a dopant at Zn sites, boron promotes the formation of oxygen vacancies and related defect complexes, causing a slight deviation from ideal ZnO stoichiometry. Additionally, 1.02 wt% nitrogen and 15.13 wt% carbon are present, attributable to the nitrogen- and carbon-abundant content of polydopamine (PDA) during synthesis and, to some extent, to intentional lattice doping. Overall, these compositional findings strongly support the formation of B-doped ZnO nanoparticles with the characteristic boron-induced defect chemistry.76,77 TEM images (Fig. 2d) also provide deeper insight into the internal morphology and size characteristics of boron-doped ZnO nanoparticles. The micrograph confirms that the particles are in the nanometer range and exhibit a pronounced tendency to aggregate, consistent with the SEM observations. Individual nanoparticles generally display an irregularly spherical morphology, with clearly distinguishable grain boundaries visible at high resolution. The darker regions observed in the TEM image are attributed to ZnO, while the lighter gray areas are attributed to PDA, as shown in Fig. 2d. The particle size distribution histogram shows that the nanoparticles range from 12.6 nm to 79.9 nm, with an average particle size of approximately 36.5 nm. Although a moderate degree of size dispersity is observed, the relatively narrow distribution and unimodal character indicate that the applied synthesis method offers effective control over particle dimensions.59,78

The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) survey spectrum of the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanoparticles verifies the presence of the key elements characteristic of the ZnO core and the polydopamine (PDA)-based surface modification. As illustrated in the survey scan (Fig. 3a), prominent peaks are observed for Zn, O, B, N, and C. High-resolution spectra provide further insight into the chemical states. The Zn 2p region (Fig. 3b) displays characteristic spin–orbit split peaks at approximately 1021–1048 eV, accompanied by a strong Zn LMM Auger signal, collectively confirming the dominance of Zn2+ within the ZnO lattice. The O 1s peak centered near 531 eV (Fig. 3c) is primarily assigned to lattice oxygen in the ZnO structure. Additionally, the O KLL Auger feature visible at higher binding energies in the survey spectrum reinforces the presence of oxygen in oxide environments.79,80 The B 1 s signal appears at a relatively low binding energy of ∼190 eV (Fig. 3d), indicating successful incorporation of boron into the ZnO matrix. The relatively low intensity of this peak suggests that boron is present as a dopant rather than forming a separate phase or compound.81 High-resolution scans of the N 1s and C 1s regions (Fig. 3e and f) reveal clear contributions from nitrogen and carbon, which are consistent with effective surface functionalization by the dopamine-derived PDA. These findings align well with previous studies on boron-doped ZnO nanostructures and polydopamine-modified metal oxides.82,83

Catalytic studies of the ZnO@PDA-B(1–5) nanocatalysts

Catalytic properties of ZnO@PDA-B(1–5) nanocatalysts

The catalytic performance of ZnO@PDA-B(1–5) nanocatalysts, whose structural characterizations were detailed using various techniques, was tested to synthesize multifunctional five-membered organic cyclic carbonates as fine chemicals in the cycloaddition of CO2 with epoxides under suitable conditions (ambient CO2 pressure, 100 °C, and 2 h) without any solvent system. To evaluate the catalytic performance of ZnO@PDA-B(1–5) nanocatalysts in the CO2 cycloaddition process, epichlorohydrin (ECH) is preferred as a representative commercial organic epoxide as the model substrate in CO2 cycloaddition conversion due to its high reactivity, industrial relevance, and structural representativeness compared to other epoxides and its ability to carry out a nucleophilic attack to initiate the opening of the epoxide ring due to the presence of chlorine as the electron-withdrawing group and high boiling point. In addition, the high boiling point of the ECH used in the catalytic CO2 fixation process can reduce quantitative errors in calculations of catalytic conversion and selectivity. However, bis(triphenylphosphoranyliden)ammonium chloride (PPNCl) was used as a co-catalyst in the chemical coupling reaction between CO2 and various internal/terminal epoxides, owing to its well-known role in CO2 fixation studies under ambient conditions. In the CO2 activation mechanism, the (PPN+) does not directly participate in the CO2 fixation processes, but rather the chloride anion (Cl−) participates in the mechanism by being able to make a nucleophilic attack on the epoxide molecule and thus open the epoxide ring.26,86 However, in photocatalytic CO2 cycloaddition processes (PPN+), or in a similar ammonium-based analogy, CO2 can interact with the catalyst to form reactive adducts, thereby substantially lowering the CO2 reduction potential in photo- and electrocatalytic CO2 conversion.87 The reason for selecting the ZnO@PDA-B type nanocatalyst in this study is that it possesses acidic and basic properties with catalytically active sites, which are vital for the conversion of CO2 and epoxides into cyclic carbonates used in many commercial applications. At this point, due to the strong synergistic effect resulting from the electronic and defect dynamics properties of the ZnO crystal structure and the doped boron compounds through morphological modification, ZnO@PDA-B(1–5) nanocatalysts are expected to exhibit strong catalytic properties in the chemical conversion of CO2 to cyclic carbonates due to their high surface area, oxygen vacancies, and Lewis acidity properties. These morphological modifications increase the exposure and accessibility of catalytically active sites and create additional micro- and mesoporous regions, thereby facilitating the diffusion of reactants and products during the cyclization of CO2 and epoxides. Furthermore, the PDA molecules in the ZnO@PDA-B(1–5) nanocatalyst structure can act as Lewis bases to form various cyclic carbonates from CO2 under suitable conditions. After the chemical synthesis of five-membered cyclic carbonates from CO2 and epoxides under appropriate conditions (ambient CO2 pressure, 100 °C and 2 h) (Table 2), the catalytic conversion and selectivity of cyclic carbonates were determined by 1H NMR characterization of the resulting crude product. Additionally, the FT-IR spectra, 1H and 13C NMR spectra, and LC-MS/MS spectrometry of the pure organic cyclic carbonates are provided in the SI file. Under the same reaction conditions, the catalytic performance of the ZnO@PDA-B(1–5) nanocatalysts was tested in the CO2 fixation reaction for synthesis of cyclic carbonates under solvent-free conditions and the obtained catalytic results are presented in Table 2. 10 mg each of ZnO@PDA-B(1–5) nanocatalysts were used in the process of adding CO2 to ECH, which was conducted at 100 °C and ambient CO2 pressure for 2 hours without any organic solvent (Table 2, entries 1–6). Among these nanocatalysts, ZnO@PDA-B5 was the most active catalyst in the presence of the PPNCl co-catalyst, and the target product, epichlorohydrin carbonate, was synthesized with 96% conversion and 98% selectivity (Table 2, entry 5). This catalytic tendency indicates that the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst, formed by doping ZnO@PDA with boron-containing phosphonium groups, has more catalytically active sites, sufficient Lewis-basic sites, and diverse boron species that create defects, thereby enabling effective substrate activation compared to other structures, due to the synergistic effect.59 While ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst alone shows almost no catalytic conversion (trace amount conversion) without any co-catalyst (Table 2, entry 6). Although it impedes nanocatalyst separation and reuse, the success of this catalytic system is critically dependent on the co-catalysis by organic bases. When PPNCl was used alone as a co-catalyst, the catalytic conversion was ∼78% (Table 2, entry 7). In addition, PPNCl is known to be inactive toward some epoxides but highly active toward others, such as ECH,88 which shows a significantly higher ECH conversion when used in combination with the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst. This high catalytic conversion highlights the critical role of synergistic Lewis acid–base cooperation within the ZnO@PDA-B5/PPNCl binary system, with PPNCl acting as a strong base that activates CO2via nucleophilic attack, thereby promoting the chemical coupling reaction pathway. In this study, increasing the amount of PPNCl base used as a co-catalyst from 25 mg to 45 mg decreased the catalytic conversion from 85% to 96%, but no significant change in selectivity was observed. Therefore, 10 mg of ZnO@PDA-B5, 45 mg of PPNCl base, 7.8 mmol of epoxide, and 3.5 g of dry ice as a CO2 source were used for all catalytic experiments under optimal conditions (ambient CO2 pressure, 100 °C and 2 h). After determining that the ZnO@PDA-B5/PPNCl dual catalytic system was the most active for the synthesis of high-value cyclic carbonates via the cyclization of CO2 and ECH, the effects of different epoxides, organic bases, reaction temperature, reaction time, and catalyst dosage on the catalytic conversion were tested, respectively.

To optimize the cycloaddition reaction conditions, we investigated the effects of reaction time, reaction temperature, catalyst dosage, co-catalysts, and CO2 dosage on the catalytic performance of the ZnO@PDA-B5/PPNCl dual-catalyst system under solvent-free conditions (Fig. 6). As summarized in Fig. 6a, the CO2 cycloaddition reaction time was an important factor in determining the conversion of CO2 and ECH to the corresponding cyclic carbonate (epichlorohydrin cyclic carbonate) under ambient conditions, without the need for any solvent. Since the cycloaddition reactions of CO2 to ECH under suitable conditions and without solvents are sustainable chemical reactions occurring in liquid phases, it was observed that increasing the reaction time from 0.5 to 2.0 hours resulted in higher catalytic conversion of the target cyclic carbonates (from 61% to 96%) and no significant change in their selectivity (trace amounts of diols were formed as a byproduct), depending on the potential increase in the solubility of the substituent and the contact surfaces.58,59 The 2 hour reaction time was identified as optimal because it achieved the highest conversion rate (96%) without unnecessary prolongation, balancing efficiency and process practicality.

Effects of reaction time (a), reaction temperature (b), catalyst dosage (c), various co-catalysts (d), and CO2 dosage (e) on the catalytic conversion using ZnO@PDA-B5/PPNCl dual catalyst system.

One of the most important factors affecting the catalytic conversion of CO2 into multifunctional cyclic carbonates is the cycloaddition reaction temperature under suitable conditions. As shown in Fig. 6b, increasing the reaction temperature from 25 °C to 125 °C resulted in a significant increase in the CO2 cycloaddition conversion rate from 7% to 96%, due to the increased kinetic energy of the reaction molecules as a result of the epoxides transitioning to the liquid phase at higher temperatures, becoming more fully activated.89,90 When the CO2 cycloaddition reaction temperature was increased from 100 °C to 125 °C, no significant increase in catalytic conversion was observed (Fig. 6b). Because higher reaction temperatures can lead to the formation of diols, dimers, or polycarbonates as byproducts, and because increased epoxide loss to the gas phase reduces the epoxide concentration around the catalyst, thereby decreasing the probability of catalytic conversion, the reactions were not carried out at higher temperatures.39,58,59,91 The optimum reaction temperature was determined to be 100 °C. At this point, the optimal reaction temperature for the chemical fixation of CO2 and ECH was determined to be 100 °C, with 96% conversion and 98% selectivity at ambient CO2 pressure, after 2 hours.

The effect of catalyst loading was also examined to elucidate the catalytic performance of the ZnO@PDA-B5/PPNCl dual catalyst system in the cycloaddition of CO2 and ECH (Fig. 6c). When the catalyst dosage was increased from 1.0 mg to 10 mg, the ECH catalytic conversion rapidly increased from 71% to 96% under optimized conditions (ambient CO2 pressure, 100 °C, and 2 h). According to the obtained catalytic conversion results, the morphological and structural modification in the presence of 10 mg ZnO@PDA-B5 and 0.045 g PPNCl increased the catalytic CO2 conversion activity under suitable reaction conditions due to the synergistic effect of creating more catalytically active sites, sufficient Lewis basic sites, and defects for effective substrate activation compared to other nanocatalysts.

In this part of our study, we started exploring the effects of bases such as tetrabutylammonium perchlorate (TBAP), potassium iodide (KI), tetrabutylammonium hydroxide (TBAH), tetrabutylammonium iodide (TBAI), and bis(triphenylphosphine)iminium chloride (PPNCl) as co-catalysts on catalytic conversion of CO2 and ECH coupling reaction under optimized conditions (ambient CO2 pressure, 100 °C, and 2 h) (Fig. 6d). When TBAP, KI, TBAH, and TBAI bases were used as co-catalysts in CO2 fixation studies, ECH conversions did not reach the targeted catalytic conversion rates (ranging from trace amounts to 93%). Therefore, PPNCl was identified as the best co-catalyst in the presence of the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst. Furthermore, Cl−, being smaller than Br− and I− and possessing a better leaving group than F−, was selected as a PPNCl co-catalyst due to its greater catalytic activity.92–94 According to these catalytic results, the presence of halide groups as nucleophiles in the co-catalyst positively contributes to the catalytic conversion during the opening of the epoxide ring, the rate-determining step in the mechanism of CO2 fixation reactions. Because in bases with this type of structure, the cation site stabilizes charged intermediates without directly participating in the reaction mechanism. At the same time, the halide anion (X−) helps open the epoxide ring by nucleophilically attacking the epoxide ring. It plays an important role in the catalytic cycle of the CO2 conversion mechanism.86,91,95

In the cycloaddition of CO2 with ECH to prepare five-membered cyclic carbonates under solvent-free conditions, using dry ice as the CO2 source afforded good catalytic conversion. It was determined to be easier to use than CO2-based methods. It is generally accepted that the catalytic conversion rate increases due to greater contact between CO2 and ECH in the liquid-phase reaction, resulting from the gradual increase in the amount of dry ice. Increasing the CO2 dosage from 1.5 g to 3.5 g increased the catalytic conversion from 75% to 96% under optimized conditions (ambient CO2 pressure, 100 °C, and 2 h). As shown in Fig. 6e, using 3.5 g of dry ice as the CO2 source achieved the best catalytic conversion, with 96% conversion and 98% selectivity.

Efficiency of ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst towards various epoxides

To evaluate the catalytic transformation and selectivity of the ZnO@PDA-B5/PPNCl dual catalytic system in the synthesis of cyclic carbonates via CO2 fixation to various epoxide substrates, we performed numerous catalytic experiments on various epoxides (epichlorohydrin (ECH), epibromohydrin (EBH), 2,3-epoxy-1-propanol (EPol), glycidyl propargyl ether (GPE), 1,2-epoxy pentane (EP), 1,2-epoxy hexane (EH), isobutylene oxide (IBO), and cyclohexene oxide (CHO)) (Table 3). The optimized conditions for catalytic tests performed on different epoxides were as follows: 10 mg ZnO@PDA-B5, 0.078 mmol PPNCl as co-catalyst, and 7.68 mmol epoxides, at ambient CO2 pressure, 100 °C, and for 2 hours. Catalytic conversion and selectivity of the synthesized cyclic carbonates were determined using integral fields obtained from 1H NMR spectra of the final products isolated from the crude reaction mixture (Table 3). As shown in Table 3, the internal/terminal epoxides were converted to organic cyclic carbonates with high conversion, and the trace amounts of cis-diols observed as byproducts under these conditions indicate high selectivity for cyclic carbonate formation. ZnO@PDA-B5/PPNCl dual catalytic system proved to be very effective in producing various cyclic carbonates with different functional groups, including electron-withdrawing –Cl, –Br, and –OH groups, by enhancing the electrophilic character of Cα and facilitating nucleophilic attack to open the target epoxide ring through the inductive effect of the electronegative atoms.96,97 At this point, since ECH, EBr, and EPol epoxides have low steric hindrance and small molecular size, they exhibited high catalytic conversions of 92–96% with ∼98% selectivity after 2 hours. On the other hand, 2,3-epoxy-1-propanol (GPE), also known as glycidol, is a highly valuable epoxide in industrial applications. It significantly affects the catalytic conversion of the target cyclic carbonate via intermolecular hydrogen bonding with CO2 molecules. In this CO2 cyclization reaction, GPE acts as both a substrate and a hydrogen-bond-donor catalyst in the CO2 fixation mechanism.98 When GPE was used as an epoxide in the cycloaddition reaction, it exhibited a relatively lower catalytic conversion with 51% conversion and 92% selectivity due to its inert structure originating from low steric hindrance alkyl groups, and the high mass transfer resistance provided by the higher molecular weight in its catalytic conversion.99 However, it was observed that the catalytic conversion began to decrease as the chemical coupling reaction time was extended. To further investigate the catalytic potential of the ZnO@PDA-B5 catalyst, terminal epoxides such as 1,2-epoxy pentane and 1,2-epoxy hexane were also tested. Here, the ZnO@PDA-B5/PPNCl binary catalytic system shows a lower catalytic conversion rate (64–70%) compared to epoxides containing electron-rich groups under similar catalytic conditions. This decrease in the rate of catalytic conversion can be attributed to restricted substrate diffusion to the active site due to longer alkyl chains and strong electron-donating properties.58 In addition, isobutylene oxide (IBO), which is sterically hindered and less reactive, was tested in the CO2 cyclization reaction and exhibited ∼99% selectivity with no byproducts, but showed a relatively low catalytic conversion rate of 30% due to the presence of electron-donating groups. However, the catalytic conversion decreased with increasing CO2 fixation reaction time. The catalytic conversion of cyclohexene oxide (CHO) to the corresponding bicyclic carbonates as internal epoxides was calculated to be a trace amount in terms of conversion and 99% in terms of selectivity, due to the ring size around the epoxide and high steric hindrance. In parallel, it was observed that the catalytic conversion of CHO increased as reaction time increased. In conclusion, the ZnO@PDA-B5/PPNCl dual catalytic system generally exhibits good catalytic performance in the CO2 cycloaddition reaction against some of the studied epoxides, depending on the reaction time, while showing poor catalytic performance against others. Among these internal and terminal commercial epoxides, ECH was determined to be the most active epoxide with 96% catalytic conversion and 98% ECHCC selectivity under optimized conditions (ambient CO2 pressure, 100 °C, and 2 hours).

Reusability and kinetic studies of ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst

After successfully synthesizing various cyclic carbonates under optimal conditions, our next goal was to investigate reusability and stability, which are important criteria for evaluating the performance of the ZnO@PDA-B5/PPNCl dual catalytic system (Fig. 7a–d). To recover and reuse the nanocatalyst used in the reuse study, organic liquids and solids were first separated from the mixture by centrifugation and filtration, then dried in an oven and prepared for the next cycle. However, PPNCl is highly soluble in the epoxide-based reaction system. While the solid ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst can be recovered after the cycloaddition reaction, PPNCl cannot be easily separated using conventional methods such as filtration or centrifugation. Therefore, in each reuse study, fresh PPNCl was added back into the test tube along with the recovered ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst.58,100 Since fresh PPNCl is added in every recycling experiment, only the heterogeneous catalyst is recycled. As shown in Fig. 7a, under standard conditions (100 °C, ambient CO2 pressure, 2 hours), the catalytic conversion of the CO2 cycloaddition reaction decreased to 88% after the 5th catalytic cycle. However, throughout the reuse experiments, the selectivity toward ECHC generally remained above 98%. In reusability studies, the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst has well preserved its stability, basic framework structure, and catalytically active sites;44,101 this is also supported by XRD, FT-IR spectra, and SEM images, as shown in Fig. 7b–d. A hot filtration (leaching) test was performed to determine the heterogeneous nature of the catalytic reaction (Fig. S1). Particles of the ZnO@PDA-B5 catalyst were removed by hot filtration 1 hour after the cycloaddition reaction, and the reaction progress stopped after filtration. However, the very slight changes in the catalyst's active components, particularly its zinc and boron content as determined by ICP-OES analyses, along with the absence of significant mass loss, also indicate that the catalyst has maintained its structural stability.58,102 To further confirm this hypothesis, ICP-OES analysis was performed on the filtrate after the cycloaddition reaction was completed and the catalyst was removed. The ICP-OES results confirmed that no significant amounts of zinc and boron species were detected in the reaction filtrate after catalysis. These findings further confirm the heterogeneous structure and good stability of the ZnO@PDA-B5 catalyst under the reaction conditions employed. These findings show that the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst design effectively mitigates active site leaching and structural degradation. Besides, this catalytic stability and performance highlight its promising potential for practical industrial applications.

Kinetic studies of the cyclization reaction between CO2 and ECH in the presence of the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst were conducted to discuss the possible reaction mechanism (Fig. 8). The CO2 fixation reaction was considered first-order in kinetic terms, and calculations were performed based on previous studies as a function of reaction time.57–59,103 Using equations reported in previous studies, a possible reaction mechanism has been proposed for the synthesis of commercial five-membered cyclic carbonates via the cycloaddition of CO2 and ECH under suitable conditions.104,105 In the kinetic process, a linear fit of ln(Ct/C0) versus reaction time was performed, and the slope is the kobs value. To calculate the activation energy (Ea), a linear fit of ln(k) versus 1/(K−1) was performed using the Arrhenius equation k = Ae−Ea/(RT). As a result, CO2 cycloaddition reactions were estimated to be pseudo-first-order, and the rate constant at 100 °C was 0.88 h−1 under similar reaction conditions (Fig. 8a). However, the activation energy (Ea) for the formation of epichlorohydrin carbonate (ECHC) was found to be 48.19 ± 2.0 kJ mol−1 using the Arrhenius equation, as shown in Fig. 8b.

Reaction mechanism studies of ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst

Based on the catalytic conversion performance and previous literature findings, a plausible reaction mechanism for the chemical coupling reaction of CO2 and ECH catalyzed by the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst and monitored by 1H NMR and FT-IR spectra during the catalytic fixation between 0 and 2 hours is presented in Fig. 9a and b. At the start of the CO2 cycloaddition to ECH, when only ECH was present in the medium (0 hours), the characteristic Ha proton showed a chemical shift of 3.14 ppm in the 1H NMR spectrum, as shown in Fig. 9a. In contrast, no characteristic chemical shift belonging to epichlorohydrin carbonate (ECHC) was detected under these conditions. As the reaction time increases (0–1.5 h), the integral intensity of the Ha proton of ECH at 3.14 ppm decreases, while the characteristic Hb peak of the targeted cyclic carbonate (ECHC) appears at 4.95 ppm. As the CO2 cycloaddition reaction progresses, the intensity of the characteristic peak gradually increases, indicating that the product (ECHC) generated from (ECH) accumulates over time. Upon examining the 1H NMR spectrum of the sample taken from the reaction medium at the end of the second hour, it was observed that the chemical shifts (Ha) belonging to (ECH) had almost disappeared, while the characteristic peaks (Hb) belonging to (ECHC) detected at 4.98 ppm had reached their peak, as shown in Fig. 9a. Monitoring the reaction over time using 1H NMR spectroscopy shows that the ZnO@PDA-B5/PPNCl catalytic system catalyzes the cycloaddition of CO2 to various epoxides under suitable conditions. Based on the above characterization findings and previous theoretical (DFT)/experimental studies,43,44,106,107 we propose the cycloaddition reaction mechanism for the ECH with CO2 catalyzed by the ZnO@PDA-B5/PPNCl dual catalytic system (Fig. 10). The CO2 fixation reactions in the presence of a ZnO-based catalyst and PPNCl co-catalyst occur in the following steps: epoxide activation, nucleophilic substitution, CO2 insertion, and ring closure. According to this sustainable mechanism, the cycloaddition reaction involves coordination of Lewis-acidic ZnO and boron centers to ECH, polarizing ECH and activating the epoxide ring as the rate-determining step. However, the Cl− anion of the PPNCl base opens the epoxide ring by nucleophilically attacking the sterically less hindered β-carbon atom of ECH, underscoring the necessity of PPNCl as a co-catalyst in the reaction mechanism. However, the cationic part (PPN+) of the co-catalyst does not directly participate in the catalysis (epoxide activation step). After ring opening, an intermediate chloroalkoxy anion bearing a C–Cl bond forms and is strongly coordinated to ZnO. At the same time, the acidic, nonpolar CO2 molecule is activated by the Lewis-basic PDA structure (Nucleophilic substitution step). In the subsequent step of the mechanism, the intermediate chloralkoxy anion reacts with the active CO2 species on the nanocatalyst surface to form a linear chlorocarbonate anion (CO2 insertion step). In the final stage, following the regeneration of the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst and PPNCl, the anionic intermediate undergoes intramolecular ring closure and Cl-elimination for intramolecular cycloaddition, followed by the formation of the targeted organic cyclic carbonate (Ring closure step).58,59 As a result of mechanical studies, it was also observed that the increased stability of the ring-opened epoxide, due to the synergistic effect of chemical interactions between Lewis-acidic ZnO or boron centers and alkoxides, is important in facilitating the insertion of CO2 molecules.108

Conclusion

In summary, a series of well-characterized ZnO@PDA-B(1–5) nanocatalysts have been prepared for the chemical cyclization of CO2 with various epoxides under ambient reaction conditions. Among the prepared catalysts, ZnO@PDA-B5 was identified as the most active nanocatalyst using techniques including FT-IR, Raman, TGA-DTA, XRD, SEM-EDX, TEM, XPS, BET, ICP-OES, and Zeta Potential analysis. Among the prepared nanocatalysts, the ZnO@PDA-B5 nanocatalyst was the most active, exhibiting significant catalytic performance in converting CO2 and ECH to cyclic carbonates (ECHC) via cyclization, as monitored by 1H NMR and FT-IR. The combination of a strong Lewis-basic PDA and strong Lewis-acidic boron compounds with ZnO has provided an environmentally friendly and sustainable catalytic system for CO2 fixation. Furthermore, due to the strong synergistic effect of the electronic and defect dynamics properties of the ZnO crystal structure and the boron compounds doped through morphological modification, ZnO@PDA-B nanocatalysts exhibited strong catalytic properties in the conversion of CO2 to cyclic carbonates. Moreover, the ZnO@PDA-B5/PPNCl dual catalyst system can be considered a preliminary study for its potential for practical industrial-scale use, with further comprehensive research, due to its good chemical stability, environmental friendliness, and reusability under CO2 fixation conditions. On the other hand, if the developed ZnO@PDA-B5/PPNCl catalytic system can be prepared more readily, achieve high yields at lower temperatures, and reach high catalytic efficiencies without the use of a co-catalyst, its potential for use in industrial sectors will increase. In conclusion, this study establishes a fundamental framework for the production of cyclic carbonates that are sustainable, high-value-added, and consistent with green chemistry principles. Future research aims to optimize catalysts under different conditions, assess catalyst stability over long cycles, and simulate reactions with bio-based epoxides under continuous flow applications or milder conditions.
