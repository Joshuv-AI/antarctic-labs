Area of Hölder curves and coarea formula on the Heisenberg group
Gioacchino Antonelli, Robert Young

Abstract
. We prove the coarea formula for Lipschitz maps from the subriemannian n n th Heisenberg group ℍ n \mathbb{H}_{n} to ℝ 2 ​ n \mathbb{R}^{2n} . Our result is new even when n = 1 n=1 and provides the simplest vector-valued instance of the coarea formula in subriemannian geometry. This answers a question left open in the works of Magnani, Kozhevnikov, Magnani–Stepanov–Trevisan, and Julia–Nicolussi Golo–Vittone. The main difficulty of the proof is that a fiber of a C H 1 C^{1}_{\mathrm{H}} map f : ℍ n → ℝ 2 ​ n f\colon\mathbb{H}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n} is typically an unrectifiable curve. Its measure depends on the symplectic area of its projection to ℝ 2 ​ n \mathbb{R}^{2n} . A bound on this area would imply the coarea formula, but examples of Kozhevnikov show that this area can be infinite or undefined. To overcome this, we introduce an integral that we use to define both the symplectic area of 1 2 \frac{1}{2} –Hölder curves in ℝ 2 ​ n \mathbb{R}^{2n} and the symplectic area of projections of vertical curves in ℍ n \mathbb{H}_{n} . Then, we give a geometric condition for this integral to converge. This yields, in addition, new results on the existence of the signed area of 1 2 \tfrac{1}{2} –Hölder planar curves that may be of independent interest. Finally, we use β \beta –number estimates from the Fässler–Orponen Dorronsoro Theorem to show that this geometric condition holds for almost every fiber.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The coarea formula

The coarea formula is a basic tool in geometric measure theory that relates the total measure of the fibers of a Lipschitz map
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
to the Jacobian
Jf
of that map
[
8
, Section 3.2]
. Many generalizations of the coarea formula to the setting of subriemannian manifolds are known, especially for Lipschitz maps from the
(2n+1)
–dimensional Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}_{n}
to
\mathbb{R}^{k}
with
k\leq n

[
20
, Theorem 1.1]
, or for maps
\mathbb{H}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
that satisfy additional regularity properties (for example, for
C^{1,\alpha}_{\mathrm{H}}
–regular maps in
[
14
,
15
]
). The case of Lipschitz maps from
\mathbb{H}_{n}
to
\mathbb{R}^{k}
with
k>n
has remained open for many years.

In this paper, we prove the following coarea formula for Lipschitz maps from
\mathbb{H}_{n}
to
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. We refer the reader to Section
2
for definitions and notation.
Recall that the Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{H}^{2n+2}
related to a left-invariant homogeneous distance is a Haar measure on
\mathbb{H}_{n}
, and
\mathcal{L}^{2n}
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. In this paper we normalize
\mathcal{H}^{2n+2}
so that it coincides with the Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}^{2n+1}
in exponential coordinates on
\mathbb{H}_{n}\equiv\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
. By
[
21
,
16
]
, a Lipschitz map
f\colon\mathbb{H}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is Pansu differentiable almost everywhere, so the Pansu differential
Df_{x}\colon\mathbb{H}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is defined for
\mathcal{H}^{2n+2}
–almost every
x\in\mathbb{H}_{n}
(see (
8
)). Let
D_{\mathrm{H}}f_{x}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
be the restriction of
Df_{x}
to the horizontal plane. This is a linear map, and we define
J_{\mathrm{H}}f(x)=\det D_{\mathrm{H}}f_{x}
. Let
\mathcal{H}^{2}
be the
2
–dimensional Hausdorff measure on
\mathbb{H}_{n}
, normalized so that if
I\subset\mathbb{H}_{n}
is a segment of the
z
–axis, then
\mathcal{H}^{2}(I)
is equal to its Euclidean length.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathbb{H}_{n}
be the
n
th Heisenberg group endowed with a left-invariant homogeneous distance. Let
f\colon\mathbb{H}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
be a Lipschitz map. For every measurable set
U\subset\mathbb{H}_{n}
,

\int_{U}|J_{\mathrm{H}}f(x)|\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{2n+2}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\mathcal{H}^{2}(f^{-1}(w)\cap U)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{2n}(w).

This result is new even when
n=1
. Even in this case, Theorem
1.1
was considered a challenging open problem since the earliest development of geometric measure theory on subriemannian Carnot groups; see, for instance, the introduction of
[
17
]
,
[
12
, Remark 1.5]
, and the introduction of
[
5
]
.

The main reason that the coarea formula for Lipschitz maps
\mathbb{H}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
poses a challenge is the lack of a good rectifiability theory for the fibers of these maps.
In
[
10
,
11
,
9
]
, Franchi, Serapioni, and Serra Cassano introduced a theory of rectifiability for surfaces in
\mathbb{H}_{n}
and in Carnot groups; see
[
3
]
for a survey on the subject. This theory covers horizontal surfaces (surfaces which are tangent to horizontal subgroups almost everywhere) and
H
–regular surfaces (surfaces whose tangents are transverse to a horizontal subgroup). This theory is foundational to geometric measure theory in Carnot groups (see also
[
1
]
), and it is used in the proofs of the coarea formula for Lipschitz maps
\mathbb{H}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
with
1\leq k\leq n

[
20
, Theorem 1.1]
, and for real-valued Lipschitz maps with an arbitrary Carnot group as domain
[
18
, Theorem 3.5]
.

The fibers of a Lipschitz map
f\colon\mathbb{H}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
are typically
vertical curves
which are not rectifiable in the sense above. A vertical curve satisfies a cone condition similar to the cone condition defining Lipschitz graphs or intrinsic Lipschitz graphs (see Section
2.2
); its only possible tangent subgroup is the center of
\mathbb{H}_{n}
, which is neither horizontal nor transverse to a horizontal subgroup. In fact, while rectifiable surfaces have integer Hausdorff dimension, vertical curves can have fractional Hausdorff dimension
[
2
]
. Even if
f
is
C^{1}_{\mathrm{H}}
(i.e.,
D_{\mathrm{H}}f_{x}
is defined everywhere and depends continuously on
x
), its fibers can have
\mathcal{H}^{2}
measure
0
or
\infty

[
14
]
. Consequently, previous results on the coarea formula have often either assumed some additional regularity on
f
(for instance, see
[
13
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
14
]
, or
[
15
]
) or led to a coarea-type inequality rather than an identity: see
[
17
, Theorem 2.6]
.

We solve this problem as follows. A vertical curve
\zeta\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{H}_{n}
with
z(\zeta(0)^{-1}\zeta(1))>0
projects to a curve
\gamma
in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. We connect the endpoints of
\gamma
by a line segment to obtain a closed curve
\hat{\gamma}
. If
\gamma
is Lipschitz, then
\hat{\gamma}
corresponds to a
1
–chain
[\hat{\gamma}]
and there is a
2
–chain
\beta
such that
\partial\beta=[\hat{\gamma}]
. Let
\omega
be the standard symplectic form on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
; then one can show

\mathcal{H}^{2}(\zeta)=z\left(\zeta(0)^{-1}\zeta(1)\right)-\int_{\beta}\omega;

see, e.g., Theorem
2.16
.

In general, however,
\gamma
may not be Lipschitz. A vertical curve has Hausdorff dimension roughly
2
, so
\gamma
is often
\frac{1}{2}
–Hölder. We thus define the symplectic area
S(\hat{\gamma})
of
\hat{\gamma}
as an integral that agrees with
\int_{\beta}\omega
when
\gamma
is Lipschitz; see Section
2.3
for details. Then, by work of Kozhevnikov
[
14
, Cor. 5.4.16]
, if
\zeta
is a fiber of a surjective
C^{1}_{\mathrm{H}}
–map for which
S(\hat{\gamma})
exists,
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\zeta)=z(\zeta(0)^{-1}\zeta(1))-S(\hat{\gamma})
: see Theorem
2.16
.

Since
\gamma
can be
\frac{1}{2}
–Hölder or worse,
S(\hat{\gamma})
may not exist; this is the case for the examples constructed in
[
14
]
and
[
2
]
. We must therefore give a criterion for when
S(\hat{\gamma})
exists and show that the criterion holds for almost every fiber of
f
. We can divide the proof into five rough parts.

(1)

As in the Euclidean case, we can reduce the coarea formula for Lipschitz maps to the coarea formula for
C^{1}_{\mathrm{H}}
maps
f\colon\mathbb{H}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
(Section
3
).

(2)

A coarea inequality due to Magnani
[
17
]
(Theorem
3.2
) implies the coarea formula when
J_{\mathrm{H}}f=0
.
We thus consider the case that
x\in\mathbb{H}_{n}
and
J_{\mathrm{H}}f(x)\neq 0
. After composing
f
with a linear map, we may suppose that
D_{\mathrm{H}}f
is close to the identity on a small ball
B
around
x
; then
f^{-1}(w)\cap B
is a vertical curve (see Section
2.2
) for all
w\in f(B)
.

(3)

Let
f^{-1}(w)\cap B
be a vertical curve and let
K\subset f^{-1}(w)\cap B
be a connected subset with
\diam(K)>0
. We parameterize
K
by a map
\zeta\colon[0,1]\to K
and use affine approximations of
f
to define a sequence
g_{i}
of piecewise-linear approximations of
\gamma=\pi\circ\zeta
(the projection of
\zeta
onto the horizontal plane). We can bound the geometry of the
g_{i}
’s in terms of the
\beta
–numbers of
f
. (For any ball
B\subset\mathbb{H}_{n}
,
\beta_{f}(B)
measures how well
f|_{B}
can be approximated by an affine function, see Section
4.1
for details.)

(4)

Let
\hat{g}_{i}
be the closed curve obtained by connecting the endpoints of
g_{i}
by a line segment. We show that the limit
\lim_{i\to\infty}S(\hat{g}_{i})
is bounded in terms of a sum of squares of
\beta
–numbers of
f
. Furthermore, when this sum is finite,
S(\hat{\gamma})
is well-defined and equal to
\lim_{i\to\infty}S(\hat{g}_{i})
. This is the most technically involved section of the proof, so we state the main results in Section
4.2
and prove them in Section
6
.

(5)

Finally, Fässler and Orponen’s version of Dorronsoro’s Theorem
[
7
]
gives a bound on sums of squares of
\beta
–numbers of
f
. We use this to show that for almost every
w\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, if
K\subset f^{-1}(w)\cap B
is connected, then
\pi(K)
has well-defined symplectic area. Estimating this symplectic area lets us prove Theorem
1.1
. (Section
5
)

While this strategy proves the coarea formula for Lipschitz maps
\mathbb{H}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, the case of maps
\mathbb{H}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
with
n\geq 2
and
n+1\leq k<2n
remains open. As with the case considered here, the fibers of a map
\mathbb{H}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
might be unrectifiable according to the theory developed starting in
[
10
]
, so it is possible that techniques in this paper will generalize to such maps.

1.2.
Area formula for
\frac{1}{2}
–Hölder curves

The core of this paper is the proof of Proposition
4.8
, which gives a condition for the existence of the symplectic area
S(\gamma)
of the projection of a vertical curve. The condition in Proposition
4.8
requires some additional notation and definitions, so we will not state it here. Nonetheless, the methods of Proposition
4.8
can be used to prove the following criterion for the existence of the signed area of a
\frac{1}{2}
–Hölder curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

For any continuous map
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we give a geometric meaning to the integral

A(\gamma)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}(\gamma_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\gamma_{1}),

(1)

as follows. For any partition
P=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\}
with
a=t_{1}<\dots<t_{k}=b
, we let

A_{P}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(\gamma_{1}(t_{i})\gamma_{2}(t_{i+1})-\gamma_{2}(t_{i})\gamma_{1}(t_{i+1})),

(2)

so that
A_{P}(\gamma)
is the signed area of the curve connecting
\gamma(t_{1}),\dots,\gamma(t_{k})
by line segments. Let
\mesh(P)=\max_{i}(t_{i+1}-t_{i})
. We say that
A(\gamma)
exists if there is an
L\in\mathbb{R}
such that for any
\epsilon>0
, there is a
\delta>0
such that if
\mesh(P)<\delta
, then
|L-A_{P}(\gamma)|<\epsilon
. In this case, we write

A(\gamma)=\lim_{\mesh(P)\to 0}A_{P}(\gamma)=L,

(3)

and call
A(\gamma)
the
(signed) area
of
\gamma
. When
\gamma
is Lipschitz, one can prove that
A(\gamma)
is the usual signed area of
\gamma
(see Section
2.3
).

It is natural to approximate
A(\gamma)
in terms of approximations of
\gamma
. For example, for
i\geq 0
, we can let
\gamma_{i}
be the piecewise-linear curve connecting
\gamma(0),\gamma(2^{-i}),\gamma(2\cdot 2^{-i}),\dots,\gamma(1)
. When
\gamma
is
\alpha
–Hölder, the region between
\gamma_{i}
and
\gamma_{i+1}
consists of
2^{i}
triangles with diameter of order
2^{-i\alpha}
, so

|A(\gamma_{i+1})-A(\gamma_{i})|\lesssim 2^{i}2^{-2i\alpha}.

When
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
, this difference decays exponentially, so
\lim_{i}A(\gamma_{i})
exists, and in fact one can use Young integration to show that
A(\gamma)=\lim_{i}A(\gamma_{i})
.

When
\gamma
is
\frac{1}{2}
–Hölder, however, this argument breaks down;
\lim_{i}A(\gamma_{i})
may not converge, and even when it converges,
A(\gamma)
may not exist (see Lemma
B.4
).

Nonetheless, we prove the following theorem, which gives a criterion for the existence of
A(\gamma)
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a
\frac{1}{2}
–Hölder curve.
Let

\gamma_{i}=\overline{\gamma(0),\gamma(2^{-i}),\gamma(2\cdot 2^{-i}),\dots,\gamma(1)}

be the curve connecting
\gamma(0),\gamma(2^{-i}),\dots,\gamma(1)
by line segments. For all
i\geq 0
and
0\leq j\leq 2^{i}-1
, let

\delta_{i,j}(\gamma):=\diam\left\{\gamma\big(j2^{-i}\big),\gamma\big((2j+1)2^{-i-1}\big),\gamma\big((j+1)2^{-i}\big)\right\}.

If

\sigma(\gamma):=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{i}-1}\delta_{i,j}(\gamma)^{2}<\infty,

(4)

then
A(\gamma)
exists and
A(\gamma)=\lim_{i}A(\gamma_{i})
.

The
\frac{1}{2}
–Hölder condition in Theorem
1.2
is sharp in the sense that for every
\epsilon>0
one can construct
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon)
–Hölder curves with
\sigma(\gamma)<\infty
for which
A(\gamma)
does not exist, see Lemma
B.4
and Lemma
B.5
.

Theorem
1.2
is a special case of Proposition
B.1
, which we will prove in Appendix
B
. We will also show that under the assumptions of Theorem
1.2
, we have that
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\gamma([0,1]))=0
, and if
\gamma
is a closed curve, then the winding number
\Wind(\gamma,z)
of
\gamma
around
z
is defined for almost every
z
and the signed area of
\gamma
satisfies

A(\gamma)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Wind(\gamma,z)\,\mathrm{d}z,

see Proposition
B.8
. For a discussion of how our result in Theorem
1.2
compares with probabilistic notions of integration, including the Itô integral for Brownian motion, see Section
B.4
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
The Heisenberg groups and general notation

In this paper, we fix
n\geq 1
a natural number. The
n
th Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}_{n}
is the unique simply connected Lie group whose Lie algebra is

\mathfrak{h}_{n}:=\mathrm{Lie}(\mathbb{H}_{n})=\langle X_{1},Y_{1},\ldots,X_{n},Y_{n}\rangle\oplus\langle Z\rangle,

with
[X_{i},Z]=[Y_{i},Z]=0
and
[X_{i},Y_{i}]=Z
for every
1\leq i\leq n
. We will identify
\mathbb{H}_{n}
with
\mathfrak{h}_{n}=\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
by means of exponential coordinates. Namely,

(x_{1},y_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{n},z)\leftrightarrow\exp(x_{1}X_{1}+y_{1}Y_{1}+\ldots+x_{n}X_{n}+y_{n}Y_{n}+zZ).

We denote the coordinate functions on
\mathbb{H}_{n}
by
x_{i},y_{i},z\colon\mathbb{H}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
. Let
\pi\colon\mathbb{H}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
,
\pi(a)=(x_{1}(a),y_{1}(a),\ldots,x_{n}(a),y_{n}(a))
and let

\omega((x_{1},y_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{n}),(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{y}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{n},\bar{y}_{n}))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\bar{y}_{i}-\bar{x}_{i}y_{i},

be the standard symplectic form on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. Then
[u,v]=\omega(\pi(u),\pi(v))Z
.
We can write the multiplication using the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula; for
a,b\in\mathfrak{h}_{n}
, we have

\exp(a)\exp(b)=\exp\left(a+b+\frac{1}{2}[a,b]\right).

Using the identification of
\mathbb{H}_{n}
with
\mathfrak{h}_{n}
, we write

a\cdot b=ab=a+b+\frac{1}{2}[a,b]=a+b+\frac{1}{2}\omega(\pi(a),\pi(b))Z,

(5)

or, in coordinates,

\displaystyle ab

\displaystyle=(x_{1}^{a},y_{1}^{a},\ldots,x_{n}^{a},y_{n}^{a},z^{a})(x_{1}^{b},y_{1}^{b},\ldots,x_{n}^{b},y_{n}^{b},z^{b})

\displaystyle=(x_{1}^{a}+x_{1}^{b},y_{1}^{a}+y_{1}^{b},\ldots,x_{n}^{a}+x_{n}^{b},y_{n}^{a}+y_{n}^{b},z(ab)),

where

z(ab):=z_{a}+z_{b}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{a}y_{i}^{b}-x_{i}^{b}y_{i}^{a}.

(We use
ab
and
a\cdot b
interchangeably.)
For
a\in\mathbb{H}_{n}
, we denote the one-parameter subgroup associated to
a
by
\langle a\rangle
. For
X\in\mathfrak{h}_{n}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we denote
X^{t}:=\exp(tX)
.

For
q\in\mathbb{H}_{n}
, we define the Korányi norm of
q
by

\|q\|_{\Kor}=\sqrt[4]{|\pi(q)|^{4}+16z(q)^{2}},

(6)

where
|\cdot|
denotes the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.
This gives rise to a left-invariant metric
d_{\Kor}(p,q)=\|p^{-1}q\|_{\Kor}
. Let us denote with
d_{cc}
the Carnot–Carathéodory metric on
\mathbb{H}_{n}
, so that
d_{cc}(p,q)
is the minimal length of a horizontal curve from
p
to
q
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any
p,q\in\mathbb{H}_{n}
,

d_{\Kor}(p,q)\leq d_{cc}(p,q)\leq 3d_{\Kor}(p,q).

Proof.

It suffices to consider the case
q=\mathbf{0}
. If
p\in\mathbb{H}_{n}
, then there is a horizontal geodesic
\gamma
from
\mathbf{0}
to
p
with length
d_{cc}(\mathbf{0},p)
. The length of a horizontal curve is the same in the Korányi and Carnot–Carathéodory metrics, so
d_{\Kor}(\mathbf{0},p)\leq d_{cc}(\mathbf{0},p)
.

There is a horizontal line of length
|\pi(p)|
from
\mathbf{0}
to
\pi(p)
, and since

\pi(p)^{-1}p=Z^{z(p)}=\left[\pm X_{1}^{\sqrt{|z(p)|}},Y_{1}^{\sqrt{|z(p)|}}\right],

we have
d_{cc}(\pi(p),p)\leq 4\sqrt{|z(p)|}\leq 2d_{\Kor}(\mathbf{0},p)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle d_{cc}(\mathbf{0},p)

\displaystyle\leq d_{cc}(\mathbf{0},\pi(p))+d_{cc}(\pi(p),p)\leq|\pi(p)|+4\sqrt{|z(p)|}

\displaystyle\leq 3d_{\Kor}(\mathbf{0},p),

as desired.
∎

Throughout this paper, we will write
d
for
d_{\Kor}
.

For
r>0
, let
S_{r}\colon\mathbb{H}_{n}\to\mathbb{H}_{n}
be the scaling automorphism:

S_{r}(x_{1},y_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{n},z):=(rx_{1},ry_{1},\ldots,rx_{n},ry_{n},r^{2}z).

A continuous distance
D
on
\mathbb{H}_{n}
is
homogeneous
if

D(S_{r}(a),S_{r}(b))=rD(a,b)

for every
a,b\in\mathbb{H}_{n}
and
r>0
. It is a standard fact that
d=d_{\Kor}
is a left-invariant homogeneous distance and that it is bilipschitz equivalent to any left-invariant homogeneous distance on
\mathbb{H}_{n}
. Throughout the paper we work without loss of generality with the Korányi distance.

The Hausdorff dimension of
\mathbb{H}_{n}
with respect to
d
is
2n+2
. Let
\mathcal{H}^{\ell}
denote the
\ell
–dimensional Hausdorff measure with respect to
d
. We denote by
\mathcal{L}^{\ell}
the
\ell
–dimensional Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
. In this paper we normalize the Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{H}^{2}
so that

\mathcal{H}^{2}(\{Z^{a}:a\in[0,1]\})=1

(7)

and normalize
\mathcal{H}^{2n+2}
to be equal to the Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}^{2n+1}
on
\mathbb{H}_{n}\equiv\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
. When we integrate over
\mathbb{H}_{n}
or
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, it is with respect to the appropriate Lebesgue measure unless otherwise specified.

Let
U
be a measurable subset of
\mathbb{H}_{n}
. We say that
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is
Pansu differentiable at
x\in U
if there exists a homogeneous (commuting with
S_{r}
for every
r>0
) homomorphism
Df_{x}\colon\mathbb{H}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
such that

\lim_{U\ni y\to x}\frac{|f(y)-f(x)-Df_{x}(x^{-1}\cdot y)|}{d(x,y)}=0.

(8)

If
Df_{x}
exists, then
Df_{x}(Z)=Df_{x}([X_{1},Y_{1}])=[Df_{x}(X_{1}),Df_{x}(Y_{1})]=0
. With a slight abuse of notation, we will denote with
Df_{x}
also the linear map
Df_{x}\circ\iota\colon\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, where
\iota\colon\{z=0\}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{\mathbb{H}}_{n}
. If
L\colon\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is linear, we denote with
\|L\|_{\mathrm{Op}}
the usual operator norm:

\|L\|_{\mathrm{Op}}:=\sup_{v:|v|=1}|L(v)|.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{H}_{n}
be an open set. We say that
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is
of class
C^{1}_{\mathrm{H}}
in
\Omega
if the map
x\mapsto Df_{x}
is continuous on
\Omega
. By
[
14
, Theorem 5.3.7]
, fibers of nonsingular
C^{1}_{\mathrm{H}}
maps are curves.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
14
, Theorem 5.3.7]
)
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{H}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
be a
C^{1}_{\mathrm{H}}
map and let
q\in\mathbb{H}_{n}
be a point such that
Df_{q}
is nonsingular. There is a neighborhood
W

2.2.
General facts on vertical curves

For

2.3.
Symplectic area

In this section, we define the symplectic area of a curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and prove some basic properties. We first recall the symplectic area of a Lipschitz curve.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
a<b
and let
\gamma\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
be a Lipschitz curve. Let

\zeta=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}(x_{i}\,\mathrm{d}y_{i}-y_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x_{i}),

(16)

so that
\,\mathrm{d}\zeta=\omega
is the standard symplectic form. We define

\hat{S}(\gamma)=\int_{\gamma}\zeta.

(17)

We extend
\hat{S}
to a class of continuous curves as follows.
For points
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
let
\overline{p_{1},\dots,p_{k}}
denote the piecewise-linear curve that connects
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
by line segments.

Definition 2.12
.

Let
a<b
and let
\gamma\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
be a continuous map. For any partition
P=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\}
with
a=t_{1}<\dots<t_{k}=b
, we let

\gamma_{P}=\overline{\gamma(t_{1}),\dots,\gamma(t_{k})},

parameterized so that
\gamma_{P}(t_{i})=\gamma(t_{i})
for all
i
. Then

\hat{S}(\gamma_{P})=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{x_{j}(\gamma(t_{i}))y_{j}(\gamma(t_{i+1}))-y_{j}(\gamma(t_{i}))x_{j}(\gamma(t_{i+1}))}{2}.

(18)

Let
\mesh(P)=\max_{i}\{t_{i+1}-t_{i}\}
. We say that
\lim_{\mesh(P)\to 0}\hat{S}(\gamma_{P})=L
exists if for any
\epsilon>0
, there is a
\delta>0
such that if
\mesh(P)<\delta
, then
|L-\hat{S}(\gamma_{P})|<\epsilon
. In this case, we write

S(\gamma)=\lim_{\mesh(P)\to 0}\hat{S}(\gamma_{P}),

and call
S(\gamma)
the
symplectic area
of
\gamma
.

Then the following lemma holds.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
\gamma\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
be a Lipschitz curve. Then the symplectic area
S(\gamma)
is finite and
S(\gamma)=\hat{S}(\gamma)
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma_{P}
be the piecewise-linear curve introduced in Definition
2.12
. Then

\hat{S}(\gamma_{P})=\int_{\gamma_{P}}\zeta=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{I}\left((\gamma_{P})_{x_{i}}(s)(\gamma_{P})_{y_{i}}^{\prime}(s)-(\gamma_{P})_{y_{i}}(s)(\gamma_{P})_{x_{i}}^{\prime}(s)\right)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Now notice that as
\mesh(P)\to 0
we have that
\gamma_{P}\to\gamma
uniformly and
\gamma_{P}^{\prime}\to\gamma^{\prime}
in
L_{1}
by the dominated convergence theorem. Thus, as
\mesh(P)\to 0
we have

\int_{\gamma_{P}}\zeta\to\int_{\gamma}\zeta=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{I}\left(\gamma_{x_{i}}(s)\gamma_{y_{i}}^{\prime}(s)-\gamma_{y_{i}}(s)\gamma_{x_{i}}^{\prime}(s)\right)\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty,

as desired.
∎

Since
\hat{S}(\gamma)=S(\gamma)
for Lipschitz curves, we will write
S(\gamma)
for the symplectic area of
\gamma
, regardless of whether
\gamma
is Lipschitz or not. Then
S(\gamma)
satisfies properties similar to
\hat{S}(\gamma)
.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be a closed interval. The following properties hold:

(1)

Let
I,J\subset\mathbb{R}
be compact intervals and let
\gamma\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
be a continuous curve. If
S(\gamma)
exists and if
\alpha\colon J\to I
is a monotone nondecreasing homeomorphism, then
S(\gamma\circ\alpha)=S(\gamma)
.

(2)

If
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are continuous curves,
S(\gamma_{1})
and
S(\gamma_{2})
exist, and
\gamma_{1}\diamond\gamma_{2}
is their concatenation, then
S(\gamma_{1}\diamond\gamma_{2})=S(\gamma_{1})+S(\gamma_{2})
.

(3)

Let
\gamma\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
be a closed curve with finite length. Then

|S(\gamma)|\leq\ell(\gamma)^{2},

where
\ell(\gamma)
is the length of
\gamma
.

(4)

Let
\gamma,\gamma_{i}\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
be curves with finite length, and assume that
\gamma_{i}\to\gamma
uniformly on
I
, and that
\sup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\ell(\gamma_{i})<\infty
. Then the symplectic area of
\gamma
exists and

\lim_{i\to\infty}S(\gamma_{i})=S(\gamma).

(5)

If
\alpha\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is a closed curve,
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, and
\beta(t)=\alpha(t)+v
, then
S(\alpha)=S(\beta)
.

Proof.

Items (1) and (2) directly follow from Definition
2.12
.

Let us prove Item (3). Up to reparametrization, and using Item (1), we can assume
\gamma\colon[0,\ell]\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is Lipschitz and
|\gamma^{\prime}(t)|=1
a.e., where
\ell=\ell(\gamma)
. Since
\gamma
is closed,
S(\gamma)=S(\gamma-a)
for every

, as an immediate consequence of (
17
). We translate
\gamma
so that
\gamma(0)=0
. Then

|S(\gamma)|=\frac{1}{2}\left|\int_{0}^{\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\gamma_{x_{i}}(s)\gamma_{y_{i}}^{\prime}(s)-\gamma_{y_{i}}(s)\gamma_{x_{i}}^{\prime}(s)\right)\,\mathrm{d}s\right|\leq\int_{0}^{\ell}|\gamma^{\prime}(s)||\gamma(s)|\,\mathrm{d}s.

(19)

Since
\gamma(0)=0
,
|\gamma(s)|\leq\ell
for all

, so
|S(\gamma)|\leq\ell^{2}
, as desired.

Next, we prove Item (4). Suppose that
\gamma_{i}\to\gamma
uniformly and
L
is such that
\ell(\gamma_{i})<L
. Since the length functional is lower semicontinuous, we have
\ell(\gamma)<L
as well. We first claim that if

, then

.

Suppose that

and let
\epsilon>0
. Let

Let

Next, let

Finally, to prove (5), note that if

f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
be a
C^{1}_{\mathrm{H}}
function. Then for every measurable set
U\subset\mathbb{H}
we have

\int_{U}|J_{\mathrm{H}}f(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\mathcal{H}^{2}(f^{-1}(v)\cap U)\,\mathrm{d}v.

Proposition
3.1
is a direct consequence of Proposition
4.1
, which we will prove in Sections
4
and
5
.

We recall two results from the literature. The first is a coarea inequality due to Magnani
[
17
]
. We state it for maps from
\mathbb{H}
to
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, but it holds more generally for Lipschitz maps between Carnot groups.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
17
, Theorem 2.6]
)
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
be a Lipschitz map. Then for every measurable set
U\subset\mathbb{H}

\int_{U}|J_{\mathrm{H}}f(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\mathcal{H}^{2}(f^{-1}(v)\cap U)\,\mathrm{d}v.

(23)

The second result is an extension theorem proved in
[
10
]
.

Theorem 3.3

(
[
10
, Theorem 6.8]
)
.

Let
H(\mathbb{H},\mathbb{R})
be the space of homogeneous homomorphisms
\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}
endowed with the distance coming from the operator norm. Let
F\subset\mathbb{H}
be a closed set and let
f\colon F\to\mathbb{R}
and
k\colon F\to H(\mathbb{H},\mathbb{R})
be continuous functions. For every compact set
K\subset F
and
\delta>0
define

\rho_{K}(\delta):=\sup\left\{\frac{|f(q)-f(p)-k(p)(p^{-1}\cdot q)|}{d(p,q)}:p,q\in K,0<d(p,q)<\delta\right\}.

(24)

If for every compact
K\subset F
we have that
\rho_{K}(\delta)\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
, then there is
\tilde{f}\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\tilde{f}\in C^{1}_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathbb{H};\mathbb{R})
and

\tilde{f}|_{F}=f,\qquad D\tilde{f}|_{F}=k.

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

The following proof is inspired by
[
18
, Theorem 3.5]
. Let
f\colon\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{H}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
be a Lipschitz function. By Theorem
3.2
it is enough to show that for every compact set
U\subset\mathbb{H}
,

\int_{U}|J_{\mathrm{H}}f(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\mathcal{H}^{2}(f^{-1}(v)\cap U)\,\mathrm{d}v.

(25)

Let
U\subset\mathbb{H}
be a compact set. By the Pansu–Rademacher Theorem
[
21
,
16
]
,
f
is differentiable almost everywhere. Let
Df\colon\mathbb{H}\to H(\mathbb{H},\mathbb{R}^{2n})
be its differential. Let
\epsilon>0
. By using Lusin and Egoroff’s theorems we can find a compact (since
U
is compact) set
F_{\epsilon}\subset U
such that
\mathcal{H}^{2n+2}(U\setminus F_{\epsilon})<\epsilon
,
Df
is continuous on
F_{\epsilon}
, and

\lim_{q\to p,q\in F_{\epsilon}}\frac{|f(q)-f(p)-Df(p)(p^{-1}\cdot q)|}{d(p,q)}=0,

where the last limit is uniform for
p\in F_{\epsilon}
. We thus apply Theorem
3.3
to each component of
f
with
F:=F_{\epsilon}
and
k:=Df|_{F_{\epsilon}}
. This produces a function
\tilde{f}_{\epsilon}\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
that is
C^{1}_{\mathrm{H}}
–regular such that

\tilde{f}_{\epsilon}=f\text{ and }D\tilde{f}_{\epsilon}=Df\qquad\text{on $F_{\epsilon}$.}

(26)

Therefore
J_{H}f=J_{H}\tilde{f}_{\epsilon}
on
F_{\epsilon}
. Further, for any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, if
x\in\tilde{f}_{\epsilon}^{-1}(v)\cap F_{\epsilon}
, then
x\in U
and
f(x)=\tilde{f}_{\epsilon}(x)=v
. That is,
\tilde{f}_{\epsilon}^{-1}(v)\cap F_{\epsilon}\subset f^{-1}(v)\cap U
.

By Proposition
3.1
and the fact that
f
is Lipschitz,

\displaystyle\int_{U}|J_{\mathrm{H}}f(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{F_{\epsilon}}|J_{\mathrm{H}}\tilde{f}_{\epsilon}(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{U\setminus F_{\epsilon}}|J_{\mathrm{H}}f(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\mathcal{H}^{2}(\tilde{f}_{\epsilon}^{-1}(v)\cap F_{\epsilon})\,\mathrm{d}v+\Lip(f)^{2n}\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\mathcal{H}^{2}(f^{-1}(v)\cap U)\,\mathrm{d}v+\Lip(f)^{2n}\epsilon.

Letting
\epsilon
go to zero, we get

\int_{U}|J_{\mathrm{H}}f(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\mathcal{H}^{2}(f^{-1}(v)\cap U)\,\mathrm{d}v,

as desired.

4.
The density of the coarea measure

As we saw in the previous section, the coarea formula for Lipschitz maps in Theorem
1.1
can be reduced to the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{H}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
be a
C^{1}_{\mathrm{H}}
map. Let
\mu_{f}
be the measure such that

\mu_{f}(U):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\mathcal{H}^{2}(U\cap f^{-1}(w))\,\mathrm{d}w

for any measurable
U\subset\mathbb{H}
.
For almost every
p\in\mathbb{H}
,

\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\mu_{f}(B_{r}(p))}{\mathcal{H}^{2n+2}(B_{r}(p))}=|J_{\mathrm{H}}f(p)|.

Therefore, by the Radon–Nikodym theorem,
\mu_{f}(U)=\int_{U}|J_{\mathrm{H}}f(p)|\,\mathrm{d}x
for any measurable
U\subset\mathbb{H}
.

Note that the function
w\mapsto\mathcal{H}^{2}(U\cap f^{-1}(w))
is
\mathcal{L}^{2n}
–measurable for every measurable set
U\subset\mathbb{H}
by the results in
[
8
, ¶2.10.26]
. We also have
\mu_{f}\ll\mathcal{H}^{2n+2}
due to Theorem
3.2
.

In the next two subsections, we will state some estimates that will be necessary to prove Proposition
4.1
, leaving some proofs to later sections. Then, in Section
5
, we will use these estimates to prove Proposition
4.1
.

As noted in the introduction, if
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is a
C^{1}_{\mathrm{H}}
map with
J_{\mathrm{H}}f(p)\neq 0
for all
p
, the fibers of
f
are
vertical curves
in the sense of
[
14
]
or
[
2
]
. While vertical curves are
C^{0}
–close to vertical lines, there can be a large difference between the measure of a vertical curve and the measure of a nearby vertical line. By Theorem
2.16
, this difference is determined by the symplectic area of the projection of the curve to
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. Thus, to prove Proposition
4.1
, we need to bound the symplectic area of projections of fibers of
f
.

Let
w\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and
B\subset\mathbb{H}
be a ball such that
f^{-1}(w)\cap B
is nonempty. In general,
f^{-1}(w)\cap B
may be disconnected; we let
K\subset f^{-1}(w)\cap B
be a connected subset with
\diam(K)>0
. In Section
4.1
, we will show that we can approximate the projection of
K
by a sequence of piecewise-linear curves
g_{i}
that converge uniformly. Furthermore, we can control the geometry of the approximations in terms of the
\beta
–numbers of
f
, which bound how well
f
is approximated by an affine map. In Section
4.2
, we state a geometric criterion for when a sequence
(g_{i})_{i}
of curves satisfies
S(\lim g_{i})=\lim_{i}S(g_{i})
. In Section
5
, we will show that for almost any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and any choice of
K
as above, this criterion is satisfied, and we will use it to prove Proposition
4.1
.

4.1.
Approximations of
f
and
f^{-1}(w)

In this section, we approximate the projection of
K
by a sequence of piecewise-linear curves
g_{i}
. The main goal of this section is to prove Proposition
4.3
, which lets us construct sequences of points that approximate
K
and bound their geometry, but before we can state the proposition, we will need to introduce some notation.

The
g_{i}
’s will be based on a sequence of nested partitions of
K
(similar to dyadic intervals), which we call a
dyadic patchwork
. We will write this patchwork as a map that associates each vertex of a rooted tree
\mathcal{T}
to a subinterval of
K
.

We treat a
rooted tree

\mathcal{T}
as a collection of vertices with a distinguished root
v_{0}
and a
parent map

\mathcal{P}\colon\mathcal{T}\setminus\{v_{0}\}\to\mathcal{T}
such that for every
v\in\mathcal{T}\setminus\{v_{0}\}
, there is some
i\geq 0
such that
\mathcal{P}^{i}(v)=v_{0}
. For
v\in\mathcal{T}
, we denote the
children
of
v
by
\mathcal{C}(v)=\mathcal{P}^{-1}(v)
. For
i\geq 0
, we denote the
i
th-generation descendants
of
v
by
\mathcal{C}^{i}(v)=\mathcal{P}^{-i}(v)
and let
\mathcal{T}^{i}=\mathcal{C}^{i}(v_{0})
. (In particular,
\mathcal{T}^{0}=\{v_{0}\}
.) Let
\gen\colon\mathcal{T}\to\mathbb{Z}
be the function such that
\gen(v)=i
for all
v\in\mathcal{T}^{i}
. For
k\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{T}^{>k}=\bigcup_{i>k}\mathcal{T}^{i}
(and likewise for
\mathcal{T}^{\geq k}
, etc.).
The tree structure induces a partial order on
\mathcal{T}
, with
\mathcal{P}(v)\succ v
for all
v\in\mathcal{T}\setminus\{v_{0}\}
and
v_{0}=\max\mathcal{T}
.

Definition 4.2
.

For
\mu>1
, a
\mu
–dyadic patchwork
for
K
is a tuple

(\mathcal{T},\{I_{v}\}_{v\in\mathcal{T}})

such that:

(1)

\mathcal{T}
is a rooted tree with degree at most
\mu
.

(2)

Each set
I_{v}
is a closed subinterval of
K
with distinct endpoints, and if
v_{0}=\max\mathcal{T}
, then
I_{v_{0}}=K
.

(3)

For each
v\in\mathcal{T}
, the intervals
\{I_{w}\}_{w\in\mathcal{C}(v)}
partition
I_{v}
, i.e.,
I_{v}=\bigcup_{w\in\mathcal{C}(v)}I_{w}
and the
I_{w}
’s overlap only at their endpoints.

(4)

For all
v\in\mathcal{T}^{i}
,

\diam I_{v}\in[\mu^{-1}2^{-i}\diam K,\mu 2^{-i}\diam K].

(27)

This is essentially a set of Christ cubes for
K
(see
[
4
, Sec. 3]
or
[
6
, Appendix I]
), with the additional condition that the intervals
I_{v}
are connected.

Let
\zeta\colon[0,1]\to K
be a monotone increasing parameterization of
K
(see Lemma
2.6
) and let
\gamma=\pi\circ\zeta
. For each
v\in\mathcal{T}
, let
J_{v}=\zeta^{-1}(I_{v})\subset[0,1]
. This is a closed interval, and for any
i\geq 0
, the set of intervals
\{J_{v}:v\in\mathcal{T}^{i}\}
forms a partition of
[0,1]
. For any
v\in\mathcal{T}
, let
m_{v}\in[0,1]
be the midpoint of
J_{v}
. If
\gen(w)=\gen(w^{\prime})
and
J_{w}\cap J_{w^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset
, then we say that
w
and
w^{\prime}
are
weak neighbors
(note that
w
may equal
w^{\prime}
).

If
C>0
and
\Lambda\colon\mathcal{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is a map such that for any
v\in\mathcal{T}
,

|\Lambda(v)-\gamma(m_{v})|\leq C2^{-\gen(v)}\diam(K),

(28)

then we call
\Lambda
a set of
C
–approximating points
or just
approximating points
for
\mathcal{T}
. This depends
a priori
on the choice of
\zeta
, but by (
27
), we have
\diam(\pi(I_{v}))\leq\mu 2^{-\gen(v)}\diam K
, so a different choice of
\zeta
would only change the choice of
C
.

We will construct
\Lambda
so that the geometry of
\Lambda
is bounded by the
\beta
–numbers of
f
, which measure how well
f
can be approximated by affine maps. We say that
\lambda\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is
affine
if there is a linear map
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and a
w_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
such that
\lambda(p)=h(\pi(p))+w_{0}
for all
p
. Let
\mathsf{Aff}
be the set of affine functions. (These are sometimes called
vertical affine
functions.)

For
x\in\mathbb{H}
and
r>0
, we let
B_{r}(x)
be the open metric ball of radius
r
around
x
. If
B
is a ball, we let
\rad(B)
be its radius and for
\rho>0
, we let
\rho B
be the ball of radius
\rho\rad(B)
with the same center. For a map
\phi\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and a ball
B\subset\mathbb{H}
, let

\beta_{\phi}(B)=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathsf{Aff}}\sqrt{\fint_{B}\frac{|\phi(p)-\alpha(p)|^{2}}{\rad(B)^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}p}.

(29)

This is a scale-invariant measure of how well
\phi
can be approximated by an affine function on
B
. We will denote
\beta_{\phi}(p,r):=\beta_{\phi}(B_{r}(p))
.

Now we can state the following proposition.

Proposition 4.3
.

There are
c_{0},C,\mu,\rho>0
with the following property. Let
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
be a
C^{1}_{\mathrm{H}}
map and let
B\subset\mathbb{H}
be a ball such that
\|D_{\mathrm{H}}f_{q}-\id\|_{\mathrm{Op}}<c_{0}
for all
q\in\rho B
. Let
K
be a connected subset of
B\cap f^{-1}(w)
with
\diam(K)>0
. Then there is a
\mu
–dyadic patchwork
(\mathcal{T},\{I_{v}\}_{v\in\mathcal{T}})
, a parameterization
\zeta\colon[0,1]\to K
, and a set of
C
–approximating points
\Lambda
such that for any
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
, if
v^{\prime}
is a weak neighbor of
v
or of a child of
v
, then

|\Lambda(v)-\Lambda(v^{\prime})|\lesssim\rad(\mathcal{B}(v))\beta_{f}(5\mathcal{B}(v)),

(30)

where for
v\in\mathcal{T}
,

\mathcal{B}(v):=B_{\mu 2^{-\gen(v)}\diam K}(\zeta(m_{v})).

(31)

Given such a map
\Lambda
and any
i\geq 0
, we can define a piecewise-linear curve
g_{i}=g_{\Lambda,i}\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
as follows. We label the elements of
\mathcal{T}^{i}
by
w_{1},\dots,w_{k}
so that the intervals
J_{w_{1}},J_{w_{2}},\dots
are in increasing order, let
s_{j}=m_{w_{j}}
for
j=1,\dots,k
, and let
s_{0}=0
,
s_{k+1}=1
. Let
g_{i}
be the piecewise-linear curve with

g_{i}(s_{j})=\begin{cases}\Lambda(w_{1})&j=0\\
\Lambda(w_{j})&j=1,\dots,k\\
\Lambda(w_{k})&j=k+1\end{cases}

(32)

such that
g_{i}
is affine on each interval
[s_{j-1},s_{j}]
. When
i=0
, we have
\mathcal{T}^{0}=\{v_{0}\}
, so
g_{0}
is the constant curve with value
\Lambda(v_{0})
.

In the rest of this section, we will explain the proof of Proposition
4.3
; in the next section, we will use the bounds in Proposition
4.3
to bound the symplectic area of the
g_{i}
’s.

The first step is to construct the dyadic patchwork. We suppose that
c_{0}<\frac{1}{20}
. Then, by Corollary
2.5
,
K
is a
2
–vertical curve. In Appendix
A
, we will prove the following.

Lemma 4.4
.

For any
\lambda>0
, there is a
\mu>0
such that if
K
is a
\lambda
–vertical curve, then it admits a
\mu
–dyadic patchwork.

We take
\mu
so that Lemma
4.4
holds with
\lambda=2
, so that
K
admits a
\mu
–dyadic patchwork.

Next, we construct affine approximations of
f
and use them to construct
\Lambda
.
For a function
\phi\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and a ball
D=B_{r}(p)
, let
\widehat{L}_{2}(D)
be the Hilbert norm

\|g\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(D)}^{2}=\fint_{D}g^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L},

corresponding to the uniform probability measure on
D
. Here
\mathcal{L}
is the Lebesgue
(2n+1)
-dimensional measure on
\mathbb{H}\equiv\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
. Let

\langle g,h\rangle_{D}=\fint_{D}gh\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L},

denote the inner product on
{\widehat{L}_{2}(D)}
. We define
\alpha_{\phi,D}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
to be the unique affine function such that
\alpha_{\phi,D}\circ\pi
is the orthogonal projection of
\phi
to
\mathsf{Aff}\subset\widehat{L}_{2}(D)
. That is,

\|\phi-\alpha_{\phi,D}\circ\pi\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(D)}\leq\|\phi-\alpha\circ\pi\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(D)},

for every affine function
\alpha\colon\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, and thus

\beta_{\phi}(D)=\frac{\|\phi-\alpha_{\phi,D}\circ\pi\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(D)}}{\rad(D)}.

(33)

We first note that if
D_{\mathrm{H}}f
is close to the identity near
D
, then
\alpha_{f,D}
is invertible.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
be a
C^{1}_{\mathrm{H}}
map, let
c>0
, and let
D=B_{R}(p)\subset\mathbb{H}
be a ball such that
\|D_{\mathrm{H}}f_{q}-\id\|_{\mathrm{Op}}<c
for all
q\in 5D
. For every
x\in D
,

|(\alpha_{f,D}(\pi(x))-\pi(x))-(f(p)-\pi(p))|\lesssim cR.

(34)

Consequently,

|\alpha_{f,D}(\pi(p))-f(p)|\lesssim cR,

(35)

and if
c
is sufficiently small, then
\alpha_{f,D}
is 2–bilipschitz, i.e.,

\frac{1}{2}|x-y|\leq|\alpha_{f,D}(x)-\alpha_{f,D}(y)|\leq 2|x-y|,

(36)

for every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

We defer the proof to Appendix
A
.

For
v\in\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{B}(v)
defined as in Proposition
4.3
, if
\alpha_{f,\mathcal{B}(v)}
is invertible, we define

\Lambda(v)=\alpha_{f,\mathcal{B}(v)}^{-1}(w)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}.

(37)

It remains to show that
\Lambda
satisfies the desired bounds.

We need two lemmas. First, we note the following equivalences.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
D
be a ball in
\mathbb{H}
. For any affine function
a\colon\mathbb{R}^{2n}\subset\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}
and any
\rho>0
,

\|a\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(D)}\approx\|a\|_{L_{\infty}(D)}

(38)

and

\|a\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(D)}\approx_{\rho}\|a\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(\rho D)}.

(39)

Proof.

Let
\mathsf{Aff}
be the subspace of affine functions and let
B_{1}=B_{1}(\mathbf{0})
be the unit ball. Then
\|\cdot\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(B_{1})}
,
\|\cdot\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(\rho B_{1})}
, and
\|\cdot\|_{L_{\infty}(B_{1})}
are all norms on
\mathsf{Aff}
. Since
\mathsf{Aff}
is finite-dimensional, all three norms are equivalent, with constant depending on
\rho
.

Let
g\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
be the translation and dilation such that
g(B_{1})=D
. For any
a\in\mathsf{Aff}
, we have

\|a\|_{L_{\infty}(D)}=\|a\circ g\|_{L_{\infty}(B_{1})}
,
\|a\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(D)}=\|a\circ g\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(B_{1})}
, and
\|a\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(\rho D)}=\|a\circ g\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(\rho B_{1})}.
Since
a\circ g\in\mathsf{Aff}
, this implies that
L_{\infty}(D)
,
\widehat{L}_{2}(D)
, and
\widehat{L}_{2}(\rho D)
are also equivalent with a constant depending on
\rho
.
∎

Second, we note the following bound on
\beta_{\phi}
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\rho\in(0,1)
. Suppose that
D,D^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{H}
are balls such that
D^{\prime}\subset D
and
\rad(D^{\prime})\geq\rho\rad(D)
. For any
\phi\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, we have

\beta_{\phi}(D^{\prime})\lesssim_{\rho}\beta_{\phi}(D)

(40)

and

\|\alpha_{\phi,D}-\alpha_{\phi,D^{\prime}}\|_{L_{\infty}(\pi(D))}\lesssim_{\rho}\beta_{\phi}(D)\rad(D).

(41)

Proof.

Since
\rho\rad(D)\leq\rad(D^{\prime})
, we have

\|g\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(D^{\prime})}=\mathcal{L}(D^{\prime})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|g\|_{L_{2}(D^{\prime})}\lesssim_{\rho}\mathcal{L}(D)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|g\|_{L_{2}(D)}=\|g\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(D)},

(42)

for any function
g
. In particular, using the latter inequality and (
33
), we have

\rad(D^{\prime})\beta_{\phi}(D^{\prime})=\|\phi-\alpha_{\phi,D^{\prime}}\circ\pi\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(D^{\prime})}\leq\|\phi-\alpha_{\phi,D}\circ\pi\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(D^{\prime})}\\
\lesssim_{\rho}\|\phi-\alpha_{\phi,D}\circ\pi\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(D)}=\rad(D)\beta_{\phi}(D)\leq\rho^{-1}\rad(D^{\prime})\beta_{\phi}(D).

(43)

We divide both sides by
\rad(D^{\prime})
to get (
40
).
Finally, using (
33
), (
42
), and (
43
), we have

\displaystyle\|\alpha_{\phi,D}\circ\pi-\alpha_{\phi,D^{\prime}}\circ\pi\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(D^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\leq\|\phi-\alpha_{\phi,D}\circ\pi\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(D^{\prime})}+\|\phi-\alpha_{\phi,D^{\prime}}\circ\pi\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(D^{\prime})}

(44)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\rho}\rad(D)\beta_{\phi}(D)+\rad(D^{\prime})\beta_{\phi}(D^{\prime})

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\rho}\rad(D)\beta_{\phi}(D).

Since
D^{\prime}\subset D
and
\rad(D^{\prime})\geq\rho\rad(D)
we have
D\subset 2\rho^{-1}D^{\prime}
. Thus
\pi(D)\subset\pi(2\rho^{-1}D^{\prime})=2\rho^{-1}\pi(D^{\prime})
, and by Lemma
4.6
, we have

\displaystyle\|\alpha_{\phi,D}-\alpha_{\phi,D^{\prime}}\|_{L_{\infty}(\pi(D))}

\displaystyle\leq\|\alpha_{\phi,D}-\alpha_{\phi,D^{\prime}}\|_{L_{\infty}(2\rho^{-1}\pi(D^{\prime}))}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\rho}\|\alpha_{\phi,D}-\alpha_{\phi,D^{\prime}}\|_{L_{\infty}(\pi(D^{\prime}))}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\rho}\|\alpha_{\phi,D}\circ\pi-\alpha_{\phi,D^{\prime}}\circ\pi\|_{\widehat{L}_{2}(D^{\prime})}.

Using the latter inequality and (
44
) we have

\|\alpha_{\phi,D}-\alpha_{\phi,D^{\prime}}\|_{L_{\infty}(\pi(D))}\lesssim_{\rho}\rad(D)\beta_{\phi}(D),

as desired.
∎

Finally, we prove Proposition
4.3

is homeomorphic to a closed disc.

is infinite, then

Proof.

The rectangles

–cells of a cellulation of
[0,1]\times(0,1]

; since a vertex

may have multiple children, the bottom edge of an

may be subdivided into multiple edges.

Then

is a union of
2
–cells of
\tau
. Let

be the relative boundary of

, which is a union of edges of
\tau

\tau

is a planar manifold with boundary. By the remarks before the lemma,

is simply connected.

If
\mathcal{S}
is finite, then

is a simply-connected compact planar manifold, so it is homeomorphic to a closed disc. This proves the first part of the lemma.

Suppose that
\mathcal{S}
is infinite. Then

is nonempty, so

is noncompact. A simply-connected planar manifold with an

.

Since

B.2.
Examples of Hölder curves without signed area

In this section, we study how the signed area of a curve is related to the signed area of its dyadic approximations. For a curve
f\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
k\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{D}_{k}f\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the
k
th dyadic approximation of
f
. That is,
\mathcal{D}_{k}f
is the piecewise-linear function such that
\mathcal{D}_{k}f(j2^{-k})=f(j2^{-k})
for all
j
and
\mathcal{D}_{k}f
is affine on
[j2^{-k},(j+1)2^{-k}]
for all
j
.

Recall that for any partition
P=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\}
,

A_{P}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(\gamma_{1}(t_{i})\gamma_{2}(t_{i+1})-\gamma_{2}(t_{i})\gamma_{1}(t_{i+1}))

and
A(\gamma)=\lim_{\mesh(P)\to 0}A_{P}(\gamma)
. In particular, if
\mathcal{P}_{k}
is the
k
th dyadic partition of
[0,1]
, then
A_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}(\gamma)=A(\mathcal{D}_{k}\gamma)
.

When
\gamma
is an
\alpha
–Hölder curve and
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
,
A(\gamma)
exists; in fact, if
P
and
P^{\prime}
are two partitions, then

|A_{P}(\gamma)-A_{P^{\prime}}(\gamma)|\lesssim\max(\mesh(P),\mesh(P^{\prime}))^{2\alpha-1},

(122)

with constant depending on the
\alpha
–Hölder constant of
\gamma
.
This is a standard consequence of Young’s theory of integration
[
22
]
.

We construct two examples. Our first example shows that (
122
) fails when
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
. In fact, we construct a curve
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
A(\mathcal{D}_{k}\gamma)=0
for all
k
, but
A(\gamma)
does not exist.

To explain our second example, let
\delta_{i,j}(\gamma)
and
\sigma(\gamma)
be as in Theorem
1.2
. Then the region between
\mathcal{D}_{i}\gamma
and
\mathcal{D}_{i+1}\gamma
consists of
2^{i}
triangles of diameters
\delta_{i,0}(\gamma),\dots\delta_{i,2^{i}-1}(\gamma)
, so

|A(\mathcal{D}_{i}\gamma)-A(\mathcal{D}_{i+1}\gamma)|\leq\sum_{j}\delta_{i,j}(\gamma)^{2}

and

|A(\mathcal{D}_{k+1}\gamma)|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{i}-1}\delta_{i,j}(\gamma)^{2}.

That is,
A(\mathcal{D}_{k+1}\gamma)
is a sum of areas of triangles. If
\sigma(\gamma)<\infty
, then the corresponding infinite sum converges absolutely.

It is natural to ask whether this absolute convergence condition (without the
\frac{1}{2}
–Hölder condition) implies
A(\gamma)=\lim_{k}A(\mathcal{D}_{k+1}\gamma)
. We will show that it does not, by constructing an example of a
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon)
–Hölder curve
\lambda\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\sigma(\lambda)<\infty
, but
A(\lambda)
does not exist.

Both of these examples are based on the following construction. For
i\geq 0
, let

a_{i}=\frac{1}{3}(1-4^{-i})\qquad b_{i}=\frac{1}{3}(1+2\cdot 4^{-i}),

(123)

so that
(a_{i})_{i}=0,\frac{1}{4},\frac{5}{16},\dots
and
(b_{i})_{i}=1,\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{8},\dots
are sequences of dyadic rationals converging to
\frac{1}{3}
. Let
\alpha\colon[0,\frac{1}{3})\cup(\frac{1}{3},1]\to\mathbb{R}
be the piecewise-linear function such that
\alpha(a_{i})=\alpha(b_{i})=i
and
\alpha
is affine on
[a_{i},a_{i+1}]
and
[b_{i+1},b_{i}]
for all
i
.

Lemma B.3
.

For each
i
, the graph of
\mathcal{D}_{2i}\alpha
is the piecewise-linear curve

\{y=\mathcal{D}_{2i}\alpha(x)\}=\overline{(a_{0},0),\dots,(a_{i},i),(b_{i},i),\dots,(b_{0},0)},

(124)

i.e.,
\mathcal{D}_{2i}\alpha=\min\{\alpha,i\}
,
and the graph of
\mathcal{D}_{2i+1}\alpha
is the piecewise-linear curve

\{y=\mathcal{D}_{2i+1}\alpha(x)\}=\overline{(a_{0},0),\dots,(a_{i},i),(b_{i+1},i+1),\dots,(b_{0},0)},

(125)

where
\overline{p_{1},\dots,p_{k}}
is the curve that connects
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
by line segments. In particular,
\|(\mathcal{D}_{n}\alpha)^{\prime}\|\leq 2^{n}
for all

Lemma B.6
.

Let

\sigma(\theta_{k_{i}})\leq 128k_{i}^{-1}
, so

\sigma(\gamma_{i+1})\leq(1+2^{-8(i-1)})\sigma(\gamma_{i})+128k_{i}^{-1}.

By induction on
i
, starting with the case
\sigma(\gamma_{0})=0
,

\sigma(\gamma_{i})\leq 128\left(\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}k_{j}^{-1}\right)\left(\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}(1+2^{-8(j-1)})\right)\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\frac{1}{k_{j}}<\infty.

Let
C=\sup_{i}\sigma(\gamma_{i})<\infty
.
Since
\gamma(t)=\gamma_{i}(t)
for
t\in 2^{-r_{i}}\mathbb{Z}
,

\sigma(\gamma)=\sup_{i}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{Q}^{<r_{i}}}\delta_{J}(\gamma)^{2}=\sup_{i}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{Q}^{<r_{i}}}\delta_{J}(\gamma_{i})^{2}\leq\sup_{i}\sigma(\gamma_{i})\leq C,

as desired.
∎

B.3.
Signed area and winding number

Let
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\cong\mathbb{C}
be a closed continuous curve and suppose that

z\notin\gamma([0,1])
. We define the
winding number
of
\gamma
around
z
as

\Wind(\gamma,z):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\big(\theta_{z}(1)-\theta_{z}(0)\big),

where
\theta_{z}\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is any continuous function such that

e^{i\theta_{z}(t)}=\frac{\gamma(t)-z}{|\gamma(t)-z|}.

Then
\Wind(\gamma,z)
is an integer which is independent of the choice of
\theta_{z}
. If
\gamma=(x(t),y(t))\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is Lipschitz continuous, then

\Wind(\gamma,\cdot)\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Wind(\gamma,z)\,\mathrm{d}z=A(\gamma)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left(x(t)y^{\prime}(t)-y(t)x^{\prime}(t)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t.

(139)

In this section, we will show that (
139
) holds for curves that satisfy the conditions in Theorem
1.2
. We prove the following.

Proposition B.8
.

Let
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a closed curve satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
1.2
. Then
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\gamma([0,1]))=0
,
\Wind(\gamma,\cdot)\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Wind(\gamma,z)\,\mathrm{d}z=A(\gamma).

(140)

Proof.

The fact that
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\gamma([0,1]))=0
will follow from Lemma
B.2
. Let
\epsilon>0
. By Lemma
B.2
, there is a
\delta>0
such that for every partition
P=\{t_{0},\dots,t_{k}\}
with
\mesh(P)<\delta
,

\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}|\gamma(t_{j+1})-\gamma(t_{j})|^{2}<\epsilon.

Let
P=\{t_{0},\dots,t_{k}\}
be such a partition. For each
j
, choose an interval
[a_{j},b_{j}]\subset[t_{j},t_{j+1}]
. Then

P^{\prime}=\{a_{0},\dots,a_{k-1}\}\cup\{b_{0},\dots,b_{k-1}\}\cup P

satisfies
\mesh P^{\prime}<\delta
. Let
t^{\prime}_{0},\dots,t^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}
be the elements of
P^{\prime}
in order; then

\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}|\gamma(b_{j})-\gamma(a_{j})|^{2}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k^{\prime}-1}|\gamma(t^{\prime}_{j+1})-\gamma(t^{\prime}_{j})|^{2}<\epsilon.

Since this is true for all choices of
a_{j}
and
b_{j}
, we have

\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\diam(\gamma([t_{j},t_{j+1}]))^{2}<\epsilon

for any partition with
\mesh(P)<\delta
. Therefore,
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\gamma([0,1]))=0
.

It follows that
\Wind(\gamma,z)
is defined for almost every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.
We claim that it satisfies (
140
). Let
\gamma_{i}
be the piecewise affine function defined in the statement of Theorem
1.2
. Since the
\gamma_{i}
’s converge uniformly to
\gamma
, if
z\notin\gamma([0,1])
, then
\Wind(\gamma_{i},z)\to\Wind(\gamma,z)
as
i\to\infty
. That is,

\Wind(\gamma_{i},z)\to_{i\to\infty}\Wind(\gamma,z),\qquad\text{for $\mathcal{H}^{2}$--a.e. $z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$}.

(141)

We will prove (
140
) using dominated convergence, but we must first bound
\sup_{i\geq 0}|\Wind(\gamma_{i},z)|
. For
i\geq 0
, and
0\leq j\leq 2^{i}-1
, let
T_{i,j}
be the (possibly degenerate) closed triangle with vertices
\gamma(j2^{-i})
,
\gamma((j+1)2^{-i})
, and
\gamma((2j+1)2^{-i-1})
. One can check that for every
i\geq 0
, and every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus(\gamma_{i}([0,1])\cup\gamma_{i+1}([0,1]))

|\Wind(\gamma_{i+1},z)-\Wind(\gamma_{i},z)|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{2^{i}-1}\chi_{T_{i,j}}(z).

Furthermore, since
\gamma_{0}
is constant, we have
\Wind(\gamma_{0},\cdot)=0
for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\gamma_{0}([0,1])
, and thus

\sup_{i\geq 0}|\Wind(\gamma_{i},z)|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{k}-1}\chi_{T_{k,j}}(z):=\mathcal{T}(z),

(142)

for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\cup_{i}\gamma_{i}([0,1])
. By (
4
) and the Euclidean isoperimetric inequality,
\mathcal{T}\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Hence, using Theorem
1.2
and dominated convergence, we conclude

A(\gamma)=\lim_{i\to\infty}A(\gamma_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eqn:AreaLipschitz}}}{{=}}\lim_{i\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Wind(\gamma_{i},z)\,\mathrm{d}z\\
=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\lim_{i\to\infty}\Wind(\gamma_{i},z)\,\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Wind(\gamma,z)\,\mathrm{d}z,

as desired.
∎

B.4.
Other approaches to signed area

While Section
1.2
gives one way to define the signed area of a curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, it is not the only way. The problem of defining integrals of the form
\int F(\gamma)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma
when
\gamma
is a curve with low regularity often occurs in probabilistic contexts, especially in the case that
\gamma
is a trajectory of a Brownian motion.

Theorem
1.2
does not cover the case of Brownian motion; indeed, a trajectory
\omega
is almost surely
\alpha
–Hölder continuous for every
\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
, but almost surely not
\frac{1}{2}
–Hölder continuous. In fact,
A(\omega)
is almost surely undefined. If
P_{i}
is the
i
th dyadic partition of
[0,1]
, the region between
\omega_{P_{i}}
and
\omega_{P_{i+1}}
consists of
2^{i}
triangles. We call the
j
th triangle
\Delta_{i,j}
; it has vertices
\omega(2j2^{-i-1}),\omega((2j+1)2^{-i-1}),\omega((2j+2)2^{-i-1})
and its expected area is
E[\mathcal{H}^{2}(\Delta_{i,j})]\approx 2^{-i}
. The orientation of
\Delta_{i,j}
is independent of
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\Delta_{i,j})
, and if

P^{\prime}_{i}=P_{i}\cup\{(2j+1)2^{-i-1}:\Delta_{i,j}\text{ is positively oriented}\},

then

E[A_{P^{\prime}_{i}}(\omega)-A_{P_{i}}(\omega)]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}E[\mathcal{H}^{2}(\Delta_{i,j})]\approx 1.

It follows that
\lim_{\mesh P\to 0}A_{P}(\omega)
almost surely diverges.

The key point here is that
A(\omega)
is only defined if
A_{P}(\omega)
converges in a strong sense. Weaker versions of convergence can still produce a limit. In particular, Itô showed that for any deterministic sequence of partitions
\{\Pi^{n}\}
of
[0,1]
with
\mathrm{mesh}(\Pi^{n})\to 0
, the stochastic Riemann sums

\sum_{t_{i}\in\Pi^{n}}\omega^{1}(t_{i})\bigl(\omega^{2}(t_{i+1})-\omega^{2}(t_{i})\bigr)-\omega^{2}(t_{i})\bigl(\omega^{1}(t_{i+1})-\omega^{1}(t_{i})\bigr)

converge in probability (or in
L_{2}
) to the Itô integral

\mathcal{B}:=\int_{0}^{1}B^{1}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}B^{2}_{t}-B^{2}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}B^{1}_{t}.

The theory of rough paths extends the notion of signed area to an even broader collection of paths, see for instance
[
FrizHairer
]
. In this light, Theorem
1.2
can be seen as an attempt to characterize the curves for which the signed area exists in the strongest sense.
