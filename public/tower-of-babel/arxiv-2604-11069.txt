Exact Outage Probability and Ergodic Capacity Analysis of NOMA in Rayleigh Fading Channels
Arafat Al-Dweik, Alok Kumar Shukla, Sami Muhaidat

Abstract
This work derives the exact outage probability (OP) and ergodic capacity (EC) for the near user (NU) in the widely adopted two-user downlink non-orthogonal multiple access (NOMA) over fading channels. By noting that the noise and fading become dependent after successive interference cancellation (SIC) , the exact analysis is derived by considering the joint probability density functions (PDFs) of the post- SIC noise and fading, which are typically considered to be independent and modeled using the same PDFs before the SIC . The derived exact PDFs are used to evaluate the impact of residual interference accurately. The derived interference and noise PDFs are used to derive an exact closed-form formula for NU outage and a single-integral expression for EC . Moreover, a closed-form accurate expression is derived for the EC . Unlike existing work, the derived formulae are parameter-free, leading to more accurate performance evaluation of such systems. Monte Carlo simulation results validate the derived analysis and demonstrate that legacy Gaussian/residual-factor models can significantly misestimate outage and EC at low–to–moderate signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs) and under unbalanced power allocation. Moreover, the obtained results show that the widely considered residual interference factor, which is bounded by [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] , is not sufficient to capture the actual impact of residual interference due to a SIC failure, and it cannot be treated as an independent variable because it depends on the power allocation, SNR and outage threshold. In addition to the fading-noise dependence, for two-dimensional modulations, the real and imaginary components of the noise become dependent as well.



I
Introduction

Non-orthogonal multiple access (NOMA)
has been extensively investigated for next-generation wireless networks due to its ability to serve multiple users over the same time–frequency resources, thereby improving spectral efficiency
[
16
]
. In downlink
NOMA
, the
near user (NU)
typically employs
successive interference cancellation (SIC)
to decode and cancel the
far user (FU)
signal before detecting its own. A critical challenge is error propagation because
SIC
errors cause residual interference, which can significantly degrade subsequent detection performance. Consequently, performance assessment must go beyond idealized assumptions and account for practical
SIC
behavior
[
20
]
. Beyond spectral‑efficiency gains, reliability under practical deployments and constraints, e.g., randomly deployed receivers and cognitive interference limits, has been investigated in
[
22
,
5
]
, while recent works highlight that the
SIC
stage itself can be a primary limiter or enabler of performance
[
14
,
15
]
.

Most analytical studies of the two-user power-domain
NOMA
adopt one of two models: perfect
SIC
, in which the interferer is always eliminated, or imperfect
SIC
, which considers cases in which the
SIC
is canceled correctly or incorrectly. For the unsuccessful cancellation, a fixed fraction of the
FU
power is lumped into a Gaussian disturbance via a tunable imperfection factor
[
20
,
21
,
3
]
. Although such models are convenient, they do not capture the statistics induced by hard-decision
SIC
. In
additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN)
channels, Al-Dweik
et al.

[
2
]
showed that the post-
SIC
disturbance is non-Gaussian; rather, it is a truncated-Gaussian mixture whose form depends on whether
SIC
succeeds or fails. At the error-rate level, explicitly modeling these distinct post-
SIC
regimes tightens analysis and reveals systematic deviations from legacy Gaussian assumptions
[
2
]
.

I-B

Motivation and Contributions

As can be noted from the surveyed literature, which is partially discussed in this article, the impact of
SIC
in
NOMA
is limited to the presence or absence of residual interference. That is, if the
SIC
process succeeded, then there is no residual interference. Otherwise, there is residual interference that affects the detection process for users who need to perform
SIC
. Nonetheless, the
SIC
process affects the noise and fading characteristics. Based on an extensive literature search, and to the best of the authors’ knowledge, there is no work that characterizes the noise and fading after
SIC
, apart from
[
2
]
, which considers the
AWGN
channel model. Consequently, the main contributions of this work are:

1.

Evaluates the impact of the
SIC
on the noise and fading, which is necessary to evaluate the performance of users who need to perform
SIC
. In particular, the work shows that noise and fading after
SIC
become dependent, and that their
probability density functions (PDFs)
differ from those before
SIC
.

2.

The residual interference is modeled accurately, where the impact of the unsuccessful interference cancellation may increase the interference power.

3.

Demonstrate that the
SIC
imperfection factor depends on the
SNR
, power allocation, and outage threshold, and thus should not be treated as an independent variable, which is the case in the majority of existing work.

4.

Prove that the typical range of the imperfection factor
[0,1]
is not sufficient to capture the impact of imperfect
SIC
for outage analysis, and should be set to zero for
EC
analysis.

5.

Derives the joint
PDF
of the noise and fading after
SIC
, and uses it to derive the marginal
PDFs
, which are used to derive exact expressions for the
OP
and
EC
for strong
NOMA
users.

6.

Simulation results verify the accuracy of the derived expressions while highlighting the divergence regions of conventional imperfect‑
SIC
models.

I-C

Terminology and Notation

Throughout this paper, the terminology used is as follows. A successful
SIC
indicates that the
SIC
process was correct for a particular symbol, while a
SIC
failure corresponds to an incorrect
SIC
. A perfect
SIC
indicates the assumption where all
SIC
processes are successful. In contrast, imperfect
SIC
indicates cases in which the analysis considers both successful and failed
SIC
processes. The term legacy refers to schemes in which the noise and fading statistics remain unchanged before and after
SIC
, and the imperfect
SIC
is modeled using a residual interference factor.

The conditional
PDF

f_{N|\mathcal{S}}(n)
is expressed as
f_{W}(w)
and

f_{N|\mathcal{F}}(n)
is expressed as
f_{Z}(z)
. The conditional noise and fading are respectively given by
N|\mathcal{S}\rightarrow W
and

N|\mathcal{F}\rightarrow Z
. The statistical average is denoted by
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
.

II
NOMA System Model and Legacy Analysis

This work considers the widely adopted two-user downlink
NOMA
, in which a base station transmits a superposition of the two users’ symbols, which can be written as

X=\sqrt{\alpha_{1}}s_{1}+\sqrt{\alpha_{2}}s_{2}

(1)

where
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are the powers of user
U_{1}
(
FU
) and
U_{2}
(
NU
), respectively, where
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=1
. For reliable
SIC
detection, it is typically considered that
\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}
. Both users are considered to employ
binary phase-shift keying (BPSK)
modulation, and thus
\{s_{1},s_{2}\}\in\{-1,+1\}
.
The exact
OP
and
EC
for
U_{1}
are already derived in the literature, and thus not considered in this work. The received signal at
U_{2}
is given by

y_{2}=h_{2}X+N_{2}

(2)

where
h_{2}
is the complex channel gain and
N_{2}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,2\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^{2})
is a circularly symmetric
AWGN
. After, channel phase,
\theta_{2}=\arg{\{h_{2}\}}
, estimation and compensation we obtain,

Y_{2}=\mathrm{e}^{-j\theta}y_{2}=\beta_{2}(\sqrt{\alpha_{1}}s_{1}+\sqrt{\alpha_{2}}s_{2})+N_{2}

(3)

where
\beta_{2}=|h_{2}|
and the
AWGN

N_{2}
notation remains unchanged because its
PDF
is phase invariant. Since the subsequent analysis focuses exclusively on
U_{2}
, the user index is omitted hereafter unless explicitly required for clarity.

To detect its own signal,
U_{2}
first detects
s_{1}
while treating
s_{2}
as unknown interference. For
BPSK
, the desired signal component is real, and thus, the detection is a simple sign decision on
Y\leftarrow\real(Y)
. Then, the estimated symbol
\hat{s}_{1}
is used to cancel the interference of
U_{1}
. The signal after
SIC
is given by,

\acute{Y}=Y-\beta\sqrt{\alpha_{1}}\hat{s}_{1}.

(4)

f_{\beta_{\mathcal{F}}}(\beta_{\mathcal{F}})=\frac{2\beta_{\mathcal{F}}}{\Omega~p_{\mathcal{F}}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\beta_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}}{\Omega}}Q\left(\frac{|X_{ij}|\beta_{\mathcal{F}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}}\right),\hskip 9.8666pt\beta_{\mathcal{F}}\geq 0

(19)

and the marginal

of

,

f_{Z}(z)=\frac{f_{N}(z)}{p_{\mathcal{F}}}\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{X_{ij}^{2}\Omega}\right)\right],\hskip 9.8666ptz<0.

.

For the other two cases of
X\in\{X_{00},X_{01}\}

)
\forall\{i,j\}

IV
Post-SIC SNR, SINR, and Outage Probability

The instantaneous
SNR
and
SINR
for the
\{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{F}\}
scenarios are derived as follows:

IV-A

Successful SIC

In the case of successful
SIC
, the interference is canceled, and thus the
SNR
is expressed as,

\gamma_{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{\alpha_{2}\beta_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[W^{2}]}.

(21)

As shown in (
21
), the second moment of the noise is used instead of the variance because the noise mean is not necessarily zero. The second moment of
w
can be evaluated as

\mathbb{E}\left[W^{2}\right]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}w^{2}f_{W}(w)dw.

(22)

By substituting (
17
) into (
22
) and evaluating the integral,

\mathbb{E}\left[W^{2}\right]=\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}}{2p_{\mathcal{S}}}\left(1+|{X}_{ij}|^{3}\left[\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{\bar{\gamma}\,{X}_{ij}^{2}+2}\right]^{\frac{3}{2}}\right).

(23)

Based on (
23
), it can be noted that
\mathbb{E}\left[W^{2}\right]<\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}
and
\lim_{\bar{\gamma}\rightarrow\infty}=\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}
. Such behavior is expected because the set of samples that corresponds to a successful
SIC
is generally less noisy compared to the original unsampled noise.

Remark 4
.

In the literature, e.g.
[
4
,
1
]
, the perfect
SIC
assumption is based on approximating exact performance by setting
\zeta=0
in (
6
), which implies that the residual error is discarded. However,
As can be seen from (
21
) and (
23
), the typical perfect
SIC
approach imposes two other overlooked approximations, which are the channel fading gain and noise after
SIC
retain the same
PDFs
before
SIC
.

OP
after a successful
SIC
can be computed based on (
21
). By defining
\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\mathbb{E}\left[W^{2}\right]}\triangleq\psi
, then

\displaystyle P_{O_{\mathcal{S}}}

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\gamma_{\mathcal{S}}<\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}\right)=\Pr\left(\psi\beta_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}<\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\beta_{\mathcal{S}}<\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}}{\psi}}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}}{\psi}}}f_{\beta_{\mathcal{S}}}\left(\beta_{\mathcal{S}}\right)d\beta_{\mathcal{S}}.

(24)

Substituting
f_{\beta_{\mathcal{S}}}\left(\beta_{\mathcal{S}}\right)
in (
16
) and evaluating the integral gives,

P_{O_{\mathcal{S}}}=\frac{1}{p_{\mathcal{S}}}\left[\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{e}^{\frac{-\epsilon^{2}}{\Omega}}\left[1+Q\left(\epsilon\sqrt{\dfrac{\bar{\gamma}{X}_{ij}^{2}}{\Omega}}\right)\right]\right.\\
\left.+\varphi\Phi\left(\epsilon\sqrt{\dfrac{\bar{\gamma}{X}_{ij}^{2}+2}{\Omega}}\right)\right]

(25)

where
\varphi=\sqrt{\frac{{X}_{ij}^{2}\bar{\gamma}}{{X}_{ij}^{2}\bar{\gamma}+2}}
and
\epsilon=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}}{\psi}}
.

IV-B

Unsuccessful SIC

In the case of
SIC
failure, the interference will be present, and thus

\gamma_{\mathcal{F}}=\frac{\alpha_{2}\beta_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}}{4\alpha_{1}\beta_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{2}\right]}

(26)

where the constant
4
represents the interference power due to the
SIC
failure, i.e.,
|2s_{1}|^{2}=4
. Similar to the case of
\gamma_{\mathcal{S}}
, the second moment is used to capture the conditional noise mean bias. By noting that
f_{Z}(z)
in (
20
) is one-sided, the second moment is computed as,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}z^{2}f_{Z}(z)dz

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}}{2p_{\mathcal{F}}}\left[1-\left|{X_{ij}}\right|^{3}\left[\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{2+\bar{\gamma}\left|{X_{ij}}\right|^{2}}\right]^{\frac{3}{2}}\right].

(27)

Therefore,

\displaystyle P_{O_{\mathcal{F}}}

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\gamma_{\mathcal{F}}<\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\frac{\alpha_{2}\beta_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}}{4\alpha_{1}\beta_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)}<\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\beta_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}<\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)}{\alpha_{2}-4\alpha_{1}\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}}\right).

(28)

Because
\beta_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\geq 0
, then from (
28
),

P_{O_{\mathcal{F}}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}1,&\alpha_{2}<4\alpha_{1}\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}\\
\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{\epsilon}f_{\beta_{\mathcal{F}}}\left(\beta_{\mathcal{F}}\right)d\beta_{\mathcal{F}}},&\text{Otherwise}\end{array}\right.

(29)

where
\epsilon=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)}{\alpha_{2}-4\alpha_{1}\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}}}
.
Consequently,
P_{O_{\mathcal{F}}}=1
given that

R>\log_{2}\left(\frac{\alpha_{2}}{4\alpha_{1}}+1\right).

(30)

Evaluating the integral in (
29
) gives,

P_{O_{\mathcal{F}}}=\frac{1}{2p_{\mathcal{F}}}\left(1-2\mathrm{Q}{\left({\frac{\left|X_{ij}\right|\epsilon}{\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}}}\right)}{\mathrm{e}^{-{\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\Omega}}}}\right)\\
-\frac{\left|X_{ij}\right|}{2p_{\mathcal{F}}\varphi_{\mathcal{F}}}{\mathrm{erf}\left(\epsilon\sqrt{{\frac{\left|X_{ij}\right|^{2}\bar{\gamma}+2\,}{2\Omega}}}\right)}

(31)

where
\varphi_{\mathcal{F}}=\sqrt{{\frac{\left|X_{ij}\right|^{2}\bar{\gamma}+2}{\bar{\gamma}}}}
.
Finally, the
OP
can be computed as,

\displaystyle P_{O}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}P_{O}\left(\mathcal{S},X_{ij}\right)+P_{O}\left(\mathcal{F},X_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(X_{ij}\right)\sum_{i}\sum_{j}P_{O_{\mathcal{S}}}\text{ }p_{\mathcal{S}}+P_{O_{\mathcal{F}}}p_{\mathcal{F}}

(32)

where
P_{O}\left(\mathcal{S/F},X_{ij}\right)
is the
OP
given a successful/failure
SIC
with
X_{ij}
being transmitted,
p_{\mathcal{S}}
is given in (
11
) and
p_{\mathcal{F}}=1-p_{\mathcal{S}}
, and the symbols
X_{ij}
are equiprobable. As shown in (
IV-B
), the exact
OP
is expressed in closed-form and is parameter-free.

V
Ergodic Capacity Analysis

Following the outage analysis approach, the
EC
of
U_{2}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\bar{C}

\displaystyle=\bar{C}_{\mathcal{S}}p_{\mathcal{S}}+\bar{C}_{\mathcal{F}}p_{\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\log_{2}(1+\gamma_{\mathcal{S}})\big]p_{\mathcal{S}}+\mathbb{E}\big[\log_{2}(1+\gamma_{\mathcal{F}})\big]p_{\mathcal{F}}.

(33)

It is worth noting that all terms in (
33
) are conditioned on
X_{i,j}
. To compute the expectation with respect to
\beta_{\mathcal{S}}
and
\beta_{\mathcal{F}}
,

\!\!\!\bar{C}=\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{\mathcal{S}}C_{\mathcal{S}}f_{\beta_{\mathcal{S}}}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}})\,d\beta_{\mathcal{S}}+\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{\mathcal{F}}C_{\mathcal{F}}f_{\beta_{\mathcal{F}}}(\beta_{\mathcal{F}})\,d\beta_{\mathcal{F}}

(34)

where
C_{\mathcal{S}/\mathcal{F}}=\log_{2}(1+\gamma_{\mathcal{S}/\mathcal{F}})
. By substituting the expressions of
\gamma_{\mathcal{S}}
and
f_{\beta_{\mathcal{S}}}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}})
, and noting that
\Phi(x)=1-Q(x)
, then

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\alpha_{2}\beta_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[W^{2}]}\right)f_{\beta_{\mathcal{S}}}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}})d\beta_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\Omega p_{\mathcal{S}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta_{\mathcal{S}}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\alpha_{2}\beta_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[W^{2}]}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\times\left[1-Q\left(\frac{|X_{ij}|\beta_{\mathcal{S}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}}\right)\right]\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\beta_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}}{\Omega}}d\beta_{\mathcal{S}}.

(35)

Using the distributive property of integration, the first integral in (
V
) can be written as

I_{1}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta_{S}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\alpha_{2}\beta_{S}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[W^{2}]}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\beta_{S}^{2}}{\Omega}}\,d\beta_{S}.

(36)

Substituting
t=\beta_{S}^{2}
and
\beta_{S}\,d\beta_{S}=\frac{dt}{2}
,

I_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\alpha_{2}t}{\mathbb{E}[W^{2}]}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{t}{\Omega}}\,dt.

(37)

which can be evaluated using
[
9
, 4.337-2]
as

I_{1}=\frac{\Omega}{2\ln 2}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathbb{E}[W^{2}]}{\alpha_{2}\Omega}}\,E_{1}\!\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[W^{2}]}{\alpha_{2}\Omega}\right).

(38)

The second integral in (
V
) includes the
Q
-function, and hence does not have a closed-form solution. Therefore, it can be evaluated efficiently using the Gauss–Laguerre or adaptive quadrature methods. Combining the two expressions gives

\bar{C}_{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{1}{p_{\mathcal{S}}\ln 2}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathbb{E}[W^{2}]}{\alpha_{2}\Omega}}\,E_{1}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[W^{2}]}{\alpha_{2}\Omega}\right)-\frac{2}{\Omega p_{\mathcal{S}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta_{\mathcal{S}}\\
\times\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\alpha_{2}\beta_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[W^{2}]}\right)Q\left(\frac{|X_{ij}|\beta_{\mathcal{S}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\beta_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}}{\Omega}}d\beta_{\mathcal{S}}.

(39)

The second component of the
EC
corresponds to
C_{\mathcal{F}}
, which has the same form as the second part of (
V
) that includes the
Q
-function, in addition to the more involved
\gamma_{\mathcal{F}}
expression. Therefore, the integral in this part can be evaluated numerically as well. The average overall capacity

\bar{C}_{A}=\Pr(X_{ij})\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\bar{C}_{\mathcal{S}}p_{\mathcal{S}}+\bar{C}_{\mathcal{F}}p_{\mathcal{F}}.

(40)

Although (
40
) is exact, it requires numerical integration. Therefore, deriving an accurate approximation would be very beneficial. Toward this goal, it should be noted that at
p_{\mathcal{F}}\bar{C}_{\mathcal{F}}\ll p_{\mathcal{S}}\bar{C}_{\mathcal{S}}
because at high
SNRs

p_{\mathcal{F}}\ll p_{\mathcal{S}}
and at low
SNR

\bar{C}_{\mathcal{F}}\ll\bar{C}_{\mathcal{S}}
. Therefore (
40
) can be approximated by

\bar{C}_{A}\approx\Pr(X_{ij})\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\bar{C}_{\mathcal{S}}p_{\mathcal{S}}.

(41)

Moreover, applying the Chiani approximation for the Q-function
[
7
]
,
Q(x)\approx\frac{1}{12}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}+\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{e}^{-2x^{2}/3}
,
the second integral in (
V
) can be written as

I_{2}\approx\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta_{\mathcal{S}}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\alpha_{2}\beta_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[W^{2}]}\right)\\
\times\left[\frac{1}{12}\mathrm{e}^{-\left(\frac{|X_{ij}|^{2}}{2\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\Omega}\right)\beta_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}}+\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{e}^{-\left(\frac{2|X_{ij}|^{2}}{3\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\Omega}\right)\beta_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}}\right]d\beta_{\mathcal{S}}.

(42)

By defining the general integral form
I(A,B)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta\log_{2}(1+A\beta^{2})\mathrm{e}^{-B\beta^{2}}d\beta
, where
A=\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\mathbb{E}[W^{2}]}
,
B_{1}=\frac{|X_{ij}|^{2}}{2\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\Omega}
, and
B_{2}=\frac{2|X_{ij}|^{2}}{3\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\Omega}
,
and using identity
[
9
, 4.337-2]
, we obtain

I_{2}\approx\frac{1}{4}\left[\frac{1}{3}I(A,B_{1})+I(A,B_{2})\right]

(43)

where

I(A,B)=\frac{1}{2\ln(2)B}\mathrm{e}^{B/A}E_{1}\left(\frac{B}{A}\right)

(44)

Expanding the result with the parameters
A,B_{1},B_{2}
:

\hskip-11.38109ptI_{2}\approx\frac{1}{8\ln(2)}\left[\frac{1}{3B_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{B_{1}}{A}}E_{1}\left(\frac{B_{1}}{A}\right)+\frac{1}{B_{2}}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{B_{2}}{A}}E_{1}\left(\frac{B_{2}}{A}\right)\right].

(45)

Finally,

\bar{C}_{A}\approx\frac{2}{\Omega}\Pr(X_{i,j})\sum_{i}\sum_{j}I_{1}+I_{2}.

(46)

VI
Post-SIC Noise and Fading PDFs with QPSK

Deriving the
OP
and
EC
for
QPSK
can be performed using the same approach as the
BPSK
. Nevertheless, the analysis is generally more tedious because the success and failure events become two-dimensional. Therefore, this section outlines the derivation of
\gamma_{\mathcal{S}}
while the
\gamma_{\mathcal{F}}
can be derived using the same approach.

VI-A

System Model with QPSK

With
QPSK
, the
NOMA
transmitted signal
X
is given in (
1
), but
\{s_{1},s_{2}\}\in\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\pm 1\pm\mathfrak{j})\}
. Consequently, the
16
constellation points are given by,

\left\{\frac{m_{1}\sqrt{\alpha_{1}}+m_{2}\sqrt{\alpha_{2}}}{\sqrt{2}},~\frac{m_{3}\sqrt{\alpha_{1}}+m_{4}\sqrt{\alpha_{2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\}

(48)

where
m_{i}\in\{1,-1\}
. Due to the symmetry of the performance with respect to the constellation point in each quadrant, without loss of generality, we
consider the top-right quadrant, which has
s_{1}=\frac{1+\mathfrak{j}}{\sqrt{2}}
, and thus

~~\left\{\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_{1}}+i\sqrt{\alpha_{2}}}{\sqrt{2}},~\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_{1}}+j\sqrt{\alpha_{2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\}\triangleq\{\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}\}

(49)

where
\{i,j\}\in\{1,-1\}
. The received signal at
U_{2}
after phase
equalization is similar to (
3
). After dropping the user index, the
unconditional joint
PDF
of the Rayleigh channel gain
\beta
and the
complex Gaussian noise
N
is given by,

f_{\beta,N}(\beta,n)=\frac{2\beta}{2\pi\Omega\sigma_{n}^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{\Omega}-\frac{|n|^{2}}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}\right)~

(50)

where
\beta\geq 0
and
N=N_{R}+\mathfrak{j}N_{I}
.

VI-B

Fading and Noise joint PDF,
f_{\beta_{\mathcal{S}},W}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}},w)

Given that
\hat{s}_{1}=s_{1}
, then the received signal
Y
is within the
decision region
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}
for
s_{1}=\frac{1+\mathfrak{j}}{\sqrt{2}}
. For
QPSK
,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}
is given by

\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}=\left\{\real(Y)>0\text{ , }\imaginary(Y)>0\right\}.

(51)

The condition
(\beta,n)\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}
simplifies to:
N_{R}>-\beta\lambda_{i}
and
N_{I}>-\beta\lambda_{j}
.
Using Bayes’ rule for continuous variables, the conditional joint
PDF
is the original
PDF
, truncated by the indicator function
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
of the decision region, and normalized by the probability of
success
p_{\mathcal{S}}
. Therefore,

f_{\beta_{\mathcal{S}},W}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}},w)=\frac{f_{\beta}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}})f_{N}(w)}{p_{\mathcal{S}}}~\mathbb{I}\big((\beta_{\mathcal{S}},w)\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}\big)

(52)

where
p_{\mathcal{S}}=\mathbb{E}_{\beta}[p_{\mathcal{S}}|\beta]
, the
indicator function
\mathbb{I}\big((\beta_{\mathcal{S}},w)\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{S}}\big)
implies that for a given
w
, the success condition is
satisfied only if
\beta>\max(0,-w_{R}/\lambda_{i},-w_{I}/\lambda_{j})
,
and

\displaystyle p_{\mathcal{S}}|\beta

\displaystyle=\left[1-Q\left(\beta\chi_{i}\right)\right]\left[1\!-\!Q\left(\beta\chi_{j}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=1\!-\!Q\!\left(\beta\chi_{i}\right)\!-\!Q\!\left(\beta\chi_{j}\right)\!+\!Q\!\left(\beta\chi_{i}\right)Q\!\left(\beta\chi_{j}\right)

(53)

where
\chi_{i/j}=\lambda_{i/j}/\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}
, and thus,
p_{\mathcal{S}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{\mathcal{S}}|\beta~f_{\beta}(\beta)d\beta
. The
integral of the first term in (
VI-B
) is equal to
1
, the second
and third terms can be evaluated using the identity
\mathbb{E}_{\beta}\left[Q(\beta\chi)\right]=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\mu\right)
, where
\mu=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda^{2}\,\bar{\gamma}}{2+\lambda^{2}\,\bar{\gamma}}}
. The
integral of the fourth term involves the product of two
Q
-functions. For
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j}
, the integral can be evaluated using the standard
identity for the average of the squared
Q
-function in Rayleigh fading,
derived from the Craig’s formula representation,
\mathbb{E}_{\beta}\left[Q^{2}(\beta\chi_{i})\right]=\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{\pi}\arctan(\mu_{i})
. Substituting the results and simplifying the expression yields,

~~p_{\mathcal{S}}|[\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j}]=~p_{\mathcal{S}}|\lambda_{i}=\mu_{i}+\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{\pi}\arctan(\mu_{i}).

(54)

For
\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}
, the integral of the fourth term does
not have a closed-form analytic solution
[
11
]
. Consequently, by
noting the geometry of the four constellation points, and noting that the first three dominant terms are given in closed-form, an accurate
approximation can be obtained as

~~~p_{\mathcal{S}}|[\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}]=\frac{1}{2}p_{\mathcal{S}}|\lambda_{i}+\frac{1}{2}p_{\mathcal{S}}|\lambda_{j}.

(55)

It can be noted that (
54
) is a special case of (
55
), and thus, both can be generally expressed as

~~~p_{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{1}{2}p_{\mathcal{S}}|\lambda_{i}+\frac{1}{2}p_{\mathcal{S}}|\lambda_{j}.

(56)

It is worth noting that (
56
) is exact when
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j}
and near exact
\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}
, with a

VIII
Conclusion and Future Work

This work developed an exact analytical framework for the
OP
and
EC
of the
NU
in a two-user downlink
NOMA
system over Rayleigh fading with
BPSK
modulation. By noting the impact of a successful and failure
SIC
processes on the statistical properties of the noise and fading, the joint
PDF
of noise and fading is derived and used to derive the marginal
PDFs
in the case of successful and failure
SIC
. Then, the marginal
PDFs
are used to derive the exact
OP
and
EC
and compare them to the legacy analysis, which considers that the noise and fading are independent of the
SIC
outcome. The main advantage of the derived expressions is that they do not require the imperfect
SIC
tuning factor.
To provide deep insights into the behavior of the legacy versus exact analysis, extensive numerical results were generated for a range of
SNRs
, power-allocation factors, and target rates. The results showed that
\zeta
cannot be selected arbitrarily because it depends on all system parameters, i.e.,
SNR
, target rate, and power allocation. In the case of
OP
, using
\zeta=0
provides accurate results for
R\geq 1
and
\alpha_{1}\gg 0.5
, regardless of the
SNR
. For the
EC
, using
\zeta=0
generally yields the most accurate results, whereas
\zeta>0
can significantly degrade the computed
EC
.

Future work may extend the analysis to higher-order modulations, multi-antenna and multi-user
NOMA
with various channel models. Moreover, the new exact analysis can be applied to evaluate the performance of existing works that use the
SIC
imperfection parameter
\zeta
in an arbitrary manner.

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
1

Let
\beta\sim\mathcal{R}(\Omega)
and
N\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^{2})
be independent Rayleigh and Gaussian random variables.Therefore, their joint
PDF
is the product of
their marginals

f_{\beta,N}(\beta,n)=f_{\beta}(\beta)f_{N}(n)

(66)

Given that
Y=\beta X_{ij}+N
,
X_{ij}\in\left\{X_{11},X_{10}\right\}>0
, then the necessary and sufficient condition for a successful

Y\geq 0
.
The condition

PDF

is a truncated version of
f_{\beta,N}(\beta,n),
where
Y\geq 0
corresponds to the region
n\geq-X_{ij}\beta
.
Therefore,

f_{\beta_{\mathcal{S}},W}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}},w)=\frac{f_{\beta,N}(\beta,n)}{p_{\mathcal{S}}}\text{, \ }n\geq-X_{ij}\beta

(67)

where
p_{\mathcal{S}}=\Pr(Y\geq 0)
,

\displaystyle p_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\beta}(\beta)\left[\int_{-X_{ij}\beta}^{\infty}f_{N}(n)\,dn\right]d\beta

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\beta}(\beta)\Phi\left(\frac{X_{ij}\beta}{\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}}\right)d\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{X_{ij}^{2}\bar{\gamma}}{X_{ij}^{2}\bar{\gamma}+2}}

(68)

To find the marginal PDF of
\beta_{\mathcal{S}}
, integrate over the
valid range of
n
,

\displaystyle f_{\beta_{\mathcal{S}}}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}})

\displaystyle=\int_{-X_{ij}\beta_{\mathcal{F}}}^{\infty}\frac{f_{\beta}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}})f_{N}(n)}{p_{\mathcal{S}}}dn

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{\beta}(\beta_{\mathcal{S}})}{p_{\mathcal{S}}}\Phi\left(\frac{X_{ij}\beta_{\beta_{\mathcal{S}}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}}\right)

(69)

The marginal
PDF
of
N
depends on the sign of
n
due to the constraint
\beta_{\mathcal{S}}\geq\max(0,-n/X_{ij})
.

•

Case 1:
n\geq 0
:
The condition
\beta\geq-n/X_{ij}
is always satisfied for
\beta\geq 0
. Thus,

\!\!f_{W}(w)=\frac{f_{N}(w)}{p_{\mathcal{S}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\beta}(\beta)\,d\beta=\frac{f_{N}(w)}{p_{\mathcal{S}}}\text{, }w\geq 0

(70)

•

Case 2:
n<0
:
The variable
\beta
is restricted to
\beta\geq-n/X_{ij}
. Thus

\displaystyle f_{W}(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{N}(w)}{p_{\mathcal{S}}}\int_{-n/X_{ij}}^{\infty}f_{\beta}(\beta)\,d\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{N}(w)}{p_{\mathcal{S}}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{w^{2}}{X_{ij}^{2}\Omega}}\text{, }w<0.

(71)

The probability of
SIC
error

p_{\mathcal{F}}=1-p_{\mathcal{S}}
. For the case of unsuccessful

is restricted to the region
X_{ij}\beta+N<0
, or
N<-X_{ij}\beta
. Since
\beta\geq 0
, this implies that

must be negative. Therefore,

f_{\beta_{\mathcal{F}},Z}(\beta_{\mathcal{F}},z)=\frac{f_{\beta,N}(\beta_{\mathcal{F}},z)}{p_{\mathcal{F}}}\text{, }\beta_{\mathcal{F}}\geq 0\text{, }z<-X_{ij}\beta_{\mathcal{F}}.

(72)

Integrating the joint PDF over
n
from
-\infty
to
-X_{ij}\beta_{\mathcal{F}}
gives the marginal
PDF
of
\beta_{\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle f_{\beta_{\mathcal{F}}}(\beta_{\mathcal{F}})

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{B}(\beta_{\mathcal{F}})}{p_{\mathcal{F}}}\int_{-\infty}^{-X_{ij}\beta_{\mathcal{F}}}f_{N}(n)\,dn

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{\beta}(\beta_{\mathcal{F}})}{p_{\mathcal{F}}}Q\left(\frac{X_{ij}\beta_{\mathcal{F}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}}\right)\text{, }\hskip 9.8666pt\beta_{\mathcal{F}}\geq 0.

(73)

For a fixed negative noise
n<0

X_{ij}\beta+N<0

of

f_{Z}(z)=\frac{f_{N}(z)}{p_{\mathcal{F}}}\int_{0}^{-n/X_{ij}}f_{\beta}(\beta_{\mathcal{F}})\,d\beta_{\mathcal{F}}.

(74)

Evaluating the Rayleigh integral (which is the Rayleigh CDF):

f_{Z}(z)=\frac{f_{N}(z)}{p_{\mathcal{F}}}\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{\Omega X_{ij}^{2}}\right)\right],\hskip 9.8666ptn<0

(75)

For

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
2

Complete Solution for the Second Moment of
W_{R}

The second moment is defined as
\mathbb{E}[W_{R}^{2}]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}W_{R}^{2}f_{W}(w_{R})dw_{R}
, where
f_{W_{R}}(w_{R})
is given in (
63
) and (
64
). The overall integral
can be decomposed into three primary terms,
I_{1}
,
I_{2}
, and
I_{3}
.
Integral
I_{1}
corresponds to the case of
w_{R}\geq 0
.
For
w_{R}\geq 0
,
f_{W_{R}}(w_{R})=\frac{f_{N_{R}}(w_{R})}{2p_{\mathcal{S}}}(1+\mu_{j})
,

I_{1}=\frac{1+\mu_{j}}{2p_{\mathcal{S}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}w_{R}^{2}f_{N_{R}}(w_{R})\,dw_{R}.

(76)

Since the integral of
w_{R}^{2}f_{N_{R}}(w_{R})
over the positive
half for a zero-mean Gaussian is
\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}}{2}
, then

I_{1}=\frac{1+\mu_{j}}{2p_{\mathcal{S}}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}}{2}\right)=\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}(1+\mu_{j})}{4p_{\mathcal{S}}}

(77)

For
w_{R}<0
, the integral can be divided into two integrals,
I_{2}
and
I_{3}
, where

I_{2}=\frac{\mu_{j}}{p_{\mathcal{S}}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}w_{R}^{2}f_{N_{R}}(w_{R})\Phi(\varpi_{1}w_{R})dw_{R}.

(78)

Using the auxiliary function
\Psi(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}},\varpi)=\int_{-\infty}^{0}w^{2}f_{N_{R}}(w)\Phi(\varpi w)dw
yields

I_{2}=\frac{\mu_{j}}{p_{\mathcal{S}}}\Psi(\sigma_{\mathrm{n}},\varpi_{1})

(79)

where
\varpi_{1}=\frac{\chi_{j}}{\lambda_{i}\mu_{j}}
and the kernel

\Psi(a,b)=\frac{a^{2}}{2}\left[\frac{1}{2}-\frac{ba}{\sqrt{2\pi(1+b^{2}a^{2})}}\right]

.

The last integral,

I_{3}=\frac{1}{p_{\mathcal{S}}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}w_{R}^{2}f_{N_{R}}(w_{R})\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{w_{R}^{2}}{\lambda_{i}^{2}\Omega}}\Phi(\varpi_{2}w_{R})dw_{R}

(80)

where
