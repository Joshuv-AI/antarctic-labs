Introduction
0B

The utility grid is constantly evolving with new generation sources and diverse loads from
backup generators, backup batteries, rooftop photovoltaics (PV), and flexible charging loads.
Each of these technologies presents consumers with an opportunity to work with their local
utility to promote grid stability, reliability, and affordability. Through a wide range of programs,
utilities are incentivizing the use of these resources to benefit the grid. However, especially for
devices with inverters that supply power back to the grid, it is important that these assets comply
with local grid codes to ensure human safety, power quality, and grid reliability. As technology
expands, individual sites—such as homes, buildings, or campuses with a single point of common
coupling (PCC)—are increasing the number of assets providing grid services. These multi-asset
sites will require solutions to ensure a cohesive response to grid codes, while navigating the
expanding landscape of communication protocols necessary for coordination with each asset.

One of the most promising new technologies that falls under this grid asset landscape is the
bidirectional electric vehicle (EV) coupled with bidirectional electric vehicle supply equipment
(EVSE) [1]. Bidirectional charging has multiple different topologies and applications. It is a
great opportunity for consumers to leverage the value of their EV battery during dwell periods
when the vehicle is not needed for driving. Additionally, bidirectional EVs and EVSE are most
valuable when their use is coordinated with the grid and optimized for all stakeholders through
the use of communication protocols and certifications. This communication ensures all
transportation needs can be met, while providing beneficial grid services and complying with all
necessary grid codes.

This report introduces the technologies that enable bidirectional charging and how these systems
operate. It then outlines the necessary interconnection codes and industry standards that govern
the operations of grid-tied inverters, including those in bidirectional charging. Implementing
multiple assets at a site presents a key challenge in the complex landscape of communication
protocols and proprietary systems designed to coordinate these assets. Finally, in Section 4 we
propose a single gateway solution to harmonize these communications between all assets across
an entire site for a cohesive response to grid codes and utility programs that is flexible enough to
support a range of market options.

1 Bidirectional Charging Overview
 9B

As electrification evolves, it is clear that EVs present a value proposition that expands beyond
transportation services: flexibility as an energy resource. The dense traction batteries that store
the majority of electricity needed to operate the vehicle present opportunities for flexible
charging or even the ability to act as a generation source. Bidirectional charging leverages the
energy stored in an EV battery and discharges that energy for use outside the vehicle. The
following sections outline different bidirectional charging applications, generically referred to as
vehicle to everything (V2X), as well as the different topologies that can enable V2X solutions.

1.1 Applications
 13B

V2X applications are primarily defined by the intended use of the discharged energy. There are a
wide range of V2X applications, as highlighted in Figure 1, from the simplest direct power from
the vehicle to individual loads (V2L) (most common in power outlets around the vehicle) to
some of the most complex solutions for off-grid island mode requiring grid-forming capabilities.
This report focuses on two applications—vehicle to grid (V2G) and vehicle to building (V2B)—
that both require a sustained grid connection but have energy objectives that are grid-centric and
building-centric, respectively.

 Figure 1. V2X applications

1.1.1 V2G
V2G applications focus on objectives that are developed from utility programs and often require
direct communications to or from utility systems. In these applications, the EV battery is
scheduled to serve as a utility energy asset, and remuneration is generally provided to the vehicle
owner for the grid services provided. The most common grid services provided from this
application are demand response and frequency support, but future programs could enable more
precise objectives that benefit distribution systems in addition to bulk power supply. Two key
considerations that must be supported for these applications are the source of utility
communication signals and the optimization of EV charging and discharging in coordination
with transportation and grid objectives.

1.1.2 V2B
V2B applications focus on objectives that are in coordination with site needs and constraints
during normal operations with a sustained connection to the local utility. In these applications the
EV battery is scheduled to serve as a site energy asset and is generally designed to reduce the
need for capacity investments or mitigate costly operating expenses. The most common site

 2

objectives are to either enforce a net power ceiling to avoid overloading service equipment (often
limited at the PCC) or optimize the site power profile to mitigate costly demand charges or peak
energy pricing. Two key considerations that must be supported for these applications are
centralized optimization and communication to all site assets (typically across multiple
communication protocols) and the forecasting of loads and generation sources to schedule
optimized operations of all energy assets.

1.2 Topologies and Hardware Requirements
 14B

In addition to the different applications, there are also different V2X topologies with unique
hardware requirements for each. These topologies are defined by the form of power that is
transferred between the EV and EVSE. Similar to the two conventional charging topologies,
V2X AC passes alternating-current (AC) power from the vehicle’s onboard charger to the EVSE,
while V2X DC passes direct-current (DC) power directly from the EV battery to the EVSE,
before the EVSE must invert this DC power to an AC power output for use on the grid. Each of
these topologies is outlined in Figure 2, with more details on each outlined below.

 Figure 2. V2X AC and DC EVSE hardware requirements

1.2.1 V2X AC
V2X AC charging provides a more economic approach to extract energy from an EV battery for
building or grid services. The most common architecture for this approach is for the onboard
charger that supports AC charging to also have conventional inverter capabilities that convert
DC power from the battery to AC power for discharging off the vehicle. This approach includes
much of the bidirectional hardware within the EV and simplifies the complementary off-vehicle
hardware that must be installed to facilitate. However, this simplified architecture does add some
complication for grid interconnections with the advent of a mobile inverter. This situation is
unique, compared to conventional systems with permanently installed grid-connected inverters.

1.2.2 V2X DC
V2X DC charging provides a more conventional approach to supply power for building or grid
services. The most common architecture for this approach is for DC EVSE to have bidirectional
charging capabilities that may supply or extract DC power directly to or from the EV battery.
This approach requires adaptations to conventional EV hardware designs, but must be supported
with more robust charging infrastructure capable of inverting DC power to AC power for
exporting to the grid. However, additional hardware requirements for V2X DC, compared to
V2X AC, result in a more conventional grid interconnection, with a permanently installed grid-
connected inverter at the point of interconnection.

2 Interconnection Codes and Industry Standards
 10B

As commercial and residential sites consider new energy technologies to support growing loads,
many will consider the use of distributed energy resources (DERs). A DER is a combination of
devices that can export power to the grid or local loads following the grid code, leveraging
energy storage or power generation. Some examples include battery energy storage systems,
natural gas generators, PV panels, and bidirectional EVSE. Examples of key DER elements are
AC to DC converters (namely inverters) and gateways (i.e., communication controllers).

Although these DER assets could be intended to either support site loads or provide grid
services, most applications will still operate in a grid-connected environment. Therefore, in order
to maintain power quality and reliability, these devices must be approved for interconnection by
the utility. The approval process differs across utilities. For example, the California Public
Utilities Commission (CPUC) uses a process called Rule 21[2] with different implementations
for each investor-owned utility under CPUC’s jurisdiction. 1 Rule 21 offers fast-track review to
 0F

facilitate the approval process, with one of the criteria being listing to UL 1741SB [3] to verify
the DER is “suitable” for grid interconnection.

UL 1741SB defines conformance test requirements to verify compliance with IEEE 1547-2018
[4]. It is one of the criteria for residential DERs under Rule 21 fast-track review. However, some
circumstances, such as V2G AC, involve a grid-connected inverter that is mobile, such as in the
EV, and not permanently installed. In these circumstances, the alternate certification is UL
1741SC. This certification is for the EVSE, which will be designed to verify the connected EV
contains an inverter that is designed and tested per the automotive standard, namely SAE J3072
[4]. SAE J3072 outlines the IEEE 1547 performance requirements for an EV’s inverter and is the
foundation for UL 1741SC’s verification process.

In addition to IEEE 1547, some sites with energy assets may be subject to power import and
export limits to maintain grid reliability and stability. UL 3141 [6] is a safety and performance
standard that defines the functionality for a power control system(PCS) to meet these power
import and export limits across a site of energy assets. Although these requirements are separate
from IEEE 1547, it is important to understand that a site may be subject to both standards.

As highlighted in Figure 3, all energy assets at a site connected to the grid must comply with the
requirements of IEEE 1547 to maintain grid reliability and stability. However, the certification
process for the testing to ensure these devices will support those requirements are different for
stationary and mobile DERs, requiring UL 1741SB and UL 1741SC, respectively. This figure
also highlights UL 3141 requirements that will be placed on the meter and supported by a site
PCS.

A unique element for V2G AC charging is the separation of the permanently grid-connected
asset, the EVSE, and the inverter that will provide AC power, which is inside the EV. This
scenario requires a pair of devices designed to work in harmony. All EVs intended for

2.3 UL 1741SC
 17B

While an SAE J3072-compliant EV is in principle compliant with IEEE 1547 and is evaluated by
UL 1741SB, it may not be listed. Listing has been utilized in interconnection approval processes
and in this situation requires UL 1741SC to address the gap and allow for the bidirectional
charging system to interconnect to the grid.

UL 1741SC is applicable to an AC EVSE intending to perform V2G AC along with an SAE
J3072-compliant EV. Besides referencing existing EVSE standards, it includes a concept called
oversight. The objective of oversight is to support the grid safety by:

 • Performing voltage and frequency trip as defined in IEEE 1547. 4 3F

 • Performing trip against line-to-neutral overvoltage. 5  4F

 • Preventing unauthorized discharge, aligning with SAE J3072.
As the EVSE only has a contactor to disconnect the EV from the grid, a trip will require opening
the contactor. To reduce the likelihood of breaking under load, oversight requires the EVSE to
ride-through (i.e., not trip) until a certain duration (e.g., 60 ms) before clearing time.

2.4 UL 3141
 18B

In addition to IEEE 1547, some utilities require systems to comply with power import and export
limits to support grid capacity constraints. UL 3141 is a safety and performance standard that
defines requirements for a PCS used in DER installations. The standard was developed to
address the increasing complexity of DER-integrated electrical systems and evolving grid
constraints that impose limits on power import and export at a PCC. A PCS is a system
consisting of one or more devices that actively monitor, limit, and control current or power on
conductors or bus bars as set forth by UL 3141. PCS functionality can be implemented for both
single-source and multisource electrical systems (as shown in Table 2) and is intended to ensure
safe, predictable operation under normal and dynamic grid conditions. Note that this document
uses the terms PCS and PCS controller interchangeably outside this section, except where
otherwise specified.

UL 3141 defines multiple PCS functional categories, each addressing specific control and
protection objectives, as defined in Table 2.

These PCS functional categories collectively can control power flow between DERs, local
electrical systems, and the grid. UL 3141 defines several key architectural and safety-related
constraints for PCS. For example, the PCS controller responsible for overload protection is
required to be located on-site, and reliance on cloud-based or web-dependent overload control is
not permitted. For PIL and PEL control functions only, PCS control settings may be adjusted
remotely by permission of the utility. While UL 3141 specifies the file format for scheduling
import and export, it does not impose a specific communication protocol. In the event of a
communication failure, the PCS must transition to a safe state that maintains overload protection
and enforces defined power limits. In addition, manufacturers are required to clearly list all
devices like external current sensors, PCS controllers, controllable loads, and inverters for proper
operation of PCS.

 11

Testing and Performance Framework:
UL 3141 provides a structured, testable framework for evaluating PCS functionality. Testing is
designed to verify that a PCS can accurately and safely provide overload protection and control
import and export of power to the grid with the following key testing categories:

 • General and normal operating tests: Verify that when a control command or step change
 is applied, the PCS responds within a defined time (typically ≤30 seconds, with some
 conditional exceptions) and maintains power or current at the testpoint (such as PCC)
 within ±7.5% of the set point.
 • Step-change tests: Evaluate PCS response to sudden changes at the testpoint (such as
 PCC) in load or generation, including load increases or decreases and rapid changes in
 DER output (e.g., PV inverter ramping).
 • Single-source PCS tests: Confirm correct overload protection and import/export limiting
 when only one source of supply is present.
 • Multisource PCS tests: Assess PCS coordination across multiple interacting sources,
 ensuring limits are maintained without instability or unintended overload.

3 Communication and Control Requirements
 1B

As mentioned in Section 2.1, IEEE 1547 defines the technical requirements for interconnection
of DERs with the grid. Employing relevant communication protocols plays a crucial role in
enabling the desired interaction with a DER and supporting necessary grid benefits. Hence, IEEE
1547 requires at least one of the following protocols to be implemented at minimum:

 • DNP3 over Ethernet.
 • SunSpec Modbus over Ethernet or RS-485.
 • IEEE 2030.5 over Ethernet.
The standard also allows the use of additional communication protocols, including proprietary
ones if there is a mutual agreement with the utility. The use of industry standard protocols
enables a more open market environment for consumers, while the option for proprietary systems
enables a more customized solution space allowing for the development of additional
functionality. This results in a diverse set of market options, limiting interoperability, and
potentially benefiting from a solution that communicates across multiple protocols.

A market study was performed to investigate the landscape of communication protocols
supported by inverters currently in production. The list of inverters for this study was compiled
from multiple sources including, but not limited to:

 1. UL-listed inverters in UL’s Product iQ database.
 2. Approved smart inverter list from PPL Electric Utilities [11]
 3. SunSpec Alliance contributing members [12].

3.1 Diverse Landscape
 19B

A total of 751 inverters from 50 companies have been analyzed in this study. This list comprises
PV inverters, battery inverters, microinverters, and bidirectional converters (including chargers).
Figure 9 shows the distribution of models that support nonproprietary communication protocols
versus proprietary ones.

 13

 Figure 9. Inverter communication protocol distribution
This shows that the vast majority of inverters currently in the market only support proprietary
communication protocols, with just 184 models supporting at least one nonproprietary protocol.
The study also reveals that SunSpec Modbus and IEEE 2030.5 are the most popular
nonproprietary communication protocols. In addition to these two, four nonproprietary protocols
outside of the three required by IEEE 1547 are also supported by inverters. These are Open
Charge Point Protocol (OCPP) 1.6j [13], Open Automated Demand Response (OpenADR) [14],
Process Field Network (Profinet), and Process Field Bus (Profibus), in decreasing order of
popularity according to the market study. The distribution of number of inverter models
supporting each of these six nonproprietary protocols is shown in Figure 10.

OCPP 1.6j is a popular communication protocol with increasing adoption across EVSE.
OpenADR is an open communications data model designed to facilitate demand response events
initiated by a utility upon customer-side equipment such as a smart thermostat, water heater,
solar/inverter system, lighting control, or industrial control. Profibus is a widely used, vendor-
independent, serial-based industrial protocol, while Profinet is the modern version of Profibus
based on industrial Ethernet. These two protocols are designed for communication in automation,
supporting a wide array of devices like drives, motors, input/output devices, sensors, robots, and
actuators.

Due to the differing motivation behind the definition of these six nonproprietary protocols, there
is no straightforward way to harmonize these communications. This is further exacerbated by the
45 distinct proprietary communication protocols used by the 567 models mentioned in Figure 9.

The sheer number of distinct proprietary communication protocols adds complexity in achieving

3.2 Key Protocols of Interest
 20B

The market study shows that SunSpec Modbus and IEEE 2030.5, which also form part of the
minimum required protocols per IEEE 1547, have the highest adoption rate of all nonproprietary
protocols. In addition to these two protocols supported by stationary DERs (PV and battery
inverters), OCPP 1.6 is a popular communication protocol used by EVSE to communicate with a
charge station management system or aggregator.

The newest version of this protocol, OCPP 2.1 [15], which was released in 2025, introduces key
messages and functional blocks such as bidirectional power transfer and DER control. These
enable exchange of crucial information such as nameplate information, power settings, and
parameter settings, thereby unlocking IEEE 1547 support. In contrast to OCPP 1.6, OCPP 2.1
also establishes native support for ISO 15118-2 [16] and ISO 15118-20 [17], which are popular
protocols used for EV-to-EVSE communications. Additionally, in-site communication is beyond
the scope of IEEE 1547 (explained in Section 2.1). Hence, leveraging OCPP 2.1 is a reasonable
choice to facilitate communication between the gateway and the EVSE for DER control and
between the charge station management system and EVSE for managed charging.

Thus, these three communication protocols, namely OCPP 2.1, SunSpec Modbus, and IEEE
2030.5, have been chosen to consolidate the interactions of multiple types of DER assets via the

 15

single gateway solution. Each of these three protocols and their path to support IEEE 1547 are
described in further detail in the following sections.

3.2.1 OCPP
OCPP is an open communications protocol developed by the Open Charge Alliance to enable
interactions between EVSE and the charge station management system. This protocol is designed
to be vendor agnostic and hence enables communication interoperability between any EVSE
manufacturer and relevant EV charging stakeholders. In the context of this report, OCPP will
enable the PCS gateway to coordinate with EVSE in compliance with IEEE 1547, while OCPP
may also be used for separate charge station management system objectives, such as access
control and charge session status metrics.

Released in 2015, OCPP 1.6 is the most widely deployed OCPP standard but lacks a few useful
features required to fully support IEEE 1547. On top of the smart charging capabilities defined in
OCPP 1.6, OCPP 2.0.1 introduced more capabilities such as frequency support and local load
balancing in addition to native support for bidirectional charging and ISO 15118-2
communications with the EV. OCPP 2.1 added native support for ISO 15118-20 enabling V2X,
and crucially, new functional blocks called “DER Control” and “Energy Management System”
explicitly enabling support for energy management using DERs. These additions in OCPP 2.0.1
and OCPP 2.1 enable compatibility with IEEE 1547. OCPP 2.1 Edition 1 is now officially
published as an IEC standard, IEC 63584-210: 2025 [18]. This section serves as an overview of
the protocol functionality in reference to its incorporation as part of a single gateway solution.

OCPP 2.1 describes the following message field definitions that can be used for supporting IEEE
1547 functions:

 • SetDERControl: This message is designed to communicate DER control settings such as
 voltage trip curves, frequency trip curves, reactive power curves and parameters, active
 power curves, and frequency droop, among other parameters. These settings can also be
 scheduled using the “start time” and “duration” fields.
 • ClearDERControl: This message allows the charge station operator to clear any default
 and/or previously set DER controls settings in any EVSE.
 • GetDERControl: This message enables the charge station operator to retrieve currently
 active DER settings of any EVSE. The EVSE then uses the ReportDERControl message
 to respond with the details of the currently active DER settings.
 • ReportDERControl: EVSE uses this message in response to a GetDERControl message
 initiated by the charge station operator to communicate its currently active DER settings.
 • NotifyDERAlarm: This message is used by the EVSE to inform the charge station
 operator of any grid event that has caused a fault. Additional information like DER
 control type and the type of grid event that caused this fault is also included as part of this
 message. An “ended” flag can be used by the EVSE to inform the charge station operator
 that this fault alarm has ended.
 • NotifyDERStartStop: This message is used by the EVSE to communicate which DER
 control is taking over and what caused it. Similarly, this message is also used to indicate
 when this event ends with an “ended” flag.

 16

 • DataTransfer: This message can be used by either EVSE or the charge station operator to
 initiate a vendor-specific information exchange. This message requires vendor-specific
 implementation on both the EVSE and charge station operator.
All messages in OCPP 2.1 are designed in a request/response pair format in which each
message’s “request” can be initiated by the EVSE, charge station management system, or both.
Some of the fields in each message’s field structure are defined as “optional.” This means that, in
practice, some of the fields in a specific message can be omitted by the sender. OCPP 2.1 defines
the information embedded within each of its message’s structure while considering the
constraints/limitations that arise from a request/response-based communication format.

OCPP Response to IEEE 1547
OCPP 2.1 has smart charging, bidirectional power transfer, and DER control functional blocks in
addition to the messages described above, with the intention to support IEEE 1547 functions
such as voltage-active power (V/W) and V/Q. Table B-1 and Table B-2 provide a detailed
mapping of OCPP 2.1 message fields to IEEE 1547 parameters for these two functions. These
two functions have been used in this report as an example to depict OCPP 2.1’s mapping with
IEEE 1547.

Additionally, OCPP 2.1 only defines the message structure but does not impose any
requirements on the protocol’s implementation, with the protocol allowing the use of some
message fields to be optional. OCPP 2.1 defines a prescribed sequence of information exchange
for various use cases and scenarios, but this description is limited to primary stakeholders only.
As a result, clarifying the practical challenges associated with implementation of interaction
between the EV charging stakeholders using OCPP2.1 to satisfy IEEE 1547 requirements is
crucial for designing a robust gateway solution.

Thus, implementation of interaction between the EV charging stakeholders using OCPP 2.1 to
satisfy IEEE 1547 requirements and the associated practical challenges will need to be clarified.

provides standardized communication mechanisms that can be used by a PCS to exchange
control commands, limits, and measurement data necessary to execute and verify power control
functions such as control of power import and export or protection against overload conditions
within a power system. The following bullet points describe information/messages that can be
used to support functions relevant to UL 3141.

 • Remote control: OCPP 2.1 supports remote transaction control, allowing the PCS to start

3.2.2 SunSpec Modbus
SunSpec Modbus is one of three protocol options that is required to be implemented at minimum
per IEEE 1547 and hence supports the compliance of a DER to IEEE 1547. SunSpec Modbus is
a protocol based on Modbus to exchange data. The physical layer can be Ethernet (and hence
Transmission Control Protocol/Internet Protocol [TCP/IP] can be used) or RS-485 per IEEE
1547. In Modbus, a device holding data (information) such as operational settings (e.g., V/Q
curve), measured values, and nameplate information is called a server. A client makes requests to
read and/or write to those pieces of information. Typically, a DER becomes the server, and an
upstream device such as the gateway becomes the client. This is illustrated in Figure 11.

A client specifies an address or a series of addresses to read the intended data or write a value (or
values) to update the data. Typically, data is 2 or 4 bytes, and the address is 4 bytes, allowing
smaller payload. Additionally, Modbus’s header is numeric-based, and hence the total data
length is compact. Therefore, Modbus (SunSpec Modbus) is ideal for in-device and in-site
communication. Indeed, Modbus can be found in factory automation.

While Modbus is a standardized protocol to exchange data (information), the information model,
also known as register map, is not standardized. Therefore, the register map is up to the vendor
of the device, causing interoperability problems between Modbus devices.

3.2.3 IEEE 2030.5
IEEE 2030.5 is another of three protocols required to be implemented at minimum per IEEE
1547 and hence supports the compliance of a DER with IEEE 1547. For IEEE 1547-related
information, it adopts generator sign convention 6 as IEEE 1547 does. To date, Rule 21 requires
 5F

IEEE 2030.5 by default to communicate with the local utility.

IEEE 2030.5 uses RESTful protocol to exchange data. One known example of such a protocol is
HTTP. Unlike SunSpec Modbus, a DER acts as a client and an upstream device, such as the
gateway, and a DER management system acts as a server. Thereby, a DER initiates the request to
get settings (i.e., HTTP GET), such as active power set point, V/Q curve, and maximum active
power, and pushes (HTTP PUT or POST) the update on the up-to-date settings and/or
operational information such as measured values.

Considering the above, it is not straightforward to change the settings such as active power set
point, as the timing to GET the setting is up to the client. To address the issue, IEEE 2030.5
defines the minimum polling requirement and offers a Subscription/Notification mechanism
where the client exposes its end point (i.e., Uniform Resource Identifier [URI]) so that the server
sends the notification over a new TCP link with a separate Transport Layer Security (TLS)
session.

3.3 Additional Considerations
 21B

While this section has demonstrated that the set of communication protocols chosen can facilitate
the required interaction, there are some additional implementation-related aspects that need to be
addressed:

 1. IEEE 1547, IEEE 2030.5, and SunSpec Modbus are all based on generator sign
 convention. In contrast, OCPP 2.1, ISO 15118-2, and ISO 15118-20 are based on load
 sign convention.
 2. The communication protocols mentioned so far are application-level protocols. These
 need to be used on top of underlying protocols (e.g., WebSocket, HTTP[S], TCP, serial)
 depending on the chosen network architecture.
 3. In addition to translating information from one protocol to another, the sequence of
 communication also forms a crucial part of enabling interaction across different protocols
 via the gateway.

Conclusion
1B

As site loads continue to grow in size the incentive and need for flexible local energy assets will
expand. These energy assets have the potential to benefit both site and grid operations, but must
comply with grid codes and interconnection requirements. Due to the diverse landscape of
energy assets, grid requirements, and coordination protocols, a new PCS gateway will be needed
to navigate this landscape and ensure a cohesive site response to local utility and on-site facility
needs. The National Lab of the Rockies is developing these technologies to simplify installation
of energy assets for consumers, as well as considering new coordination opportunities that will
expand UL 3141 PEL/PIL to support distribution capacity limitations.

Introduction
0B

The utility grid is constantly evolving with new generation sources and diverse loads from
backup generators, backup batteries, rooftop photovoltaics (PV), and flexible charging loads.
Each of these technologies presents consumers with an opportunity to work with their local
utility to promote grid stability, reliability, and affordability. Through a wide range of programs,
utilities are incentivizing the use of these resources to benefit the grid. However, especially for
devices with inverters that supply power back to the grid, it is important that these assets comply
with local grid codes to ensure human safety, power quality, and grid reliability. As technology
expands, individual sites—such as homes, buildings, or campuses with a single point of common
coupling (PCC)—are increasing the number of assets providing grid services. These multi-asset
sites will require solutions to ensure a cohesive response to grid codes, while navigating the
expanding landscape of communication protocols necessary for coordination with each asset.

One of the most promising new technologies that falls under this grid asset landscape is the
bidirectional electric vehicle (EV) coupled with bidirectional electric vehicle supply equipment
(EVSE) [1]. Bidirectional charging has multiple different topologies and applications. It is a
great opportunity for consumers to leverage the value of their EV battery during dwell periods
when the vehicle is not needed for driving. Additionally, bidirectional EVs and EVSE are most
valuable when their use is coordinated with the grid and optimized for all stakeholders through
the use of communication protocols and certifications. This communication ensures all
transportation needs can be met, while providing beneficial grid services and complying with all
necessary grid codes.

This report introduces the technologies that enable bidirectional charging and how these systems
operate. It then outlines the necessary interconnection codes and industry standards that govern
the operations of grid-tied inverters, including those in bidirectional charging. Implementing
multiple assets at a site presents a key challenge in the complex landscape of communication
protocols and proprietary systems designed to coordinate these assets. Finally, in Section 4 we
propose a single gateway solution to harmonize these communications between all assets across
an entire site for a cohesive response to grid codes and utility programs that is flexible enough to
support a range of market options.

 1

1 Bidirectional Charging Overview
 9B

As electrification evolves, it is clear that EVs present a value proposition that expands beyond
transportation services: flexibility as an energy resource. The dense traction batteries that store
the majority of electricity needed to operate the vehicle present opportunities for flexible
charging or even the ability to act as a generation source. Bidirectional charging leverages the
energy stored in an EV battery and discharges that energy for use outside the vehicle. The
following sections outline different bidirectional charging applications, generically referred to as
vehicle to everything (V2X), as well as the different topologies that can enable V2X solutions.

1.1 Applications
 13B

V2X applications are primarily defined by the intended use of the discharged energy. There are a
wide range of V2X applications, as highlighted in Figure 1, from the simplest direct power from
the vehicle to individual loads (V2L) (most common in power outlets around the vehicle) to
some of the most complex solutions for off-grid island mode requiring grid-forming capabilities.
This report focuses on two applications—vehicle to grid (V2G) and vehicle to building (V2B)—
that both require a sustained grid connection but have energy objectives that are grid-centric and
building-centric, respectively.

 Figure 1. V2X applications

1.1.1 V2G
V2G applications focus on objectives that are developed from utility programs and often require
direct communications to or from utility systems. In these applications, the EV battery is
scheduled to serve as a utility energy asset, and remuneration is generally provided to the vehicle
owner for the grid services provided. The most common grid services provided from this
application are demand response and frequency support, but future programs could enable more
precise objectives that benefit distribution systems in addition to bulk power supply. Two key
considerations that must be supported for these applications are the source of utility
communication signals and the optimization of EV charging and discharging in coordination
with transportation and grid objectives.

1.1.2 V2B
V2B applications focus on objectives that are in coordination with site needs and constraints
during normal operations with a sustained connection to the local utility. In these applications the
EV battery is scheduled to serve as a site energy asset and is generally designed to reduce the
need for capacity investments or mitigate costly operating expenses. The most common site

 2

objectives are to either enforce a net power ceiling to avoid overloading service equipment (often
limited at the PCC) or optimize the site power profile to mitigate costly demand charges or peak
energy pricing. Two key considerations that must be supported for these applications are
centralized optimization and communication to all site assets (typically across multiple
communication protocols) and the forecasting of loads and generation sources to schedule
optimized operations of all energy assets.

1.2 Topologies and Hardware Requirements
 14B

In addition to the different applications, there are also different V2X topologies with unique
hardware requirements for each. These topologies are defined by the form of power that is
transferred between the EV and EVSE. Similar to the two conventional charging topologies,
V2X AC passes alternating-current (AC) power from the vehicle’s onboard charger to the EVSE,
while V2X DC passes direct-current (DC) power directly from the EV battery to the EVSE,
before the EVSE must invert this DC power to an AC power output for use on the grid. Each of
these topologies is outlined in Figure 2, with more details on each outlined below.

 Figure 2. V2X AC and DC EVSE hardware requirements

1.2.1 V2X AC
V2X AC charging provides a more economic approach to extract energy from an EV battery for
building or grid services. The most common architecture for this approach is for the onboard
charger that supports AC charging to also have conventional inverter capabilities that convert
DC power from the battery to AC power for discharging off the vehicle. This approach includes
much of the bidirectional hardware within the EV and simplifies the complementary off-vehicle
hardware that must be installed to facilitate. However, this simplified architecture does add some
complication for grid interconnections with the advent of a mobile inverter. This situation is
unique, compared to conventional systems with permanently installed grid-connected inverters.

1.2.2 V2X DC
V2X DC charging provides a more conventional approach to supply power for building or grid
services. The most common architecture for this approach is for DC EVSE to have bidirectional
charging capabilities that may supply or extract DC power directly to or from the EV battery.
This approach requires adaptations to conventional EV hardware designs, but must be supported
with more robust charging infrastructure capable of inverting DC power to AC power for
exporting to the grid. However, additional hardware requirements for V2X DC, compared to
V2X AC, result in a more conventional grid interconnection, with a permanently installed grid-
connected inverter at the point of interconnection.

2 Interconnection Codes and Industry Standards
 10B

As commercial and residential sites consider new energy technologies to support growing loads,
many will consider the use of distributed energy resources (DERs). A DER is a combination of
devices that can export power to the grid or local loads following the grid code, leveraging
energy storage or power generation. Some examples include battery energy storage systems,
natural gas generators, PV panels, and bidirectional EVSE. Examples of key DER elements are
AC to DC converters (namely inverters) and gateways (i.e., communication controllers).

Although these DER assets could be intended to either support site loads or provide grid
services, most applications will still operate in a grid-connected environment. Therefore, in order
to maintain power quality and reliability, these devices must be approved for interconnection by
the utility. The approval process differs across utilities. For example, the California Public
Utilities Commission (CPUC) uses a process called Rule 21[2] with different implementations
for each investor-owned utility under CPUC’s jurisdiction. 1 Rule 21 offers fast-track review to
 0F

facilitate the approval process, with one of the criteria being listing to UL 1741SB [3] to verify
the DER is “suitable” for grid interconnection.

UL 1741SB defines conformance test requirements to verify compliance with IEEE 1547-2018
[4]. It is one of the criteria for residential DERs under Rule 21 fast-track review. However, some
circumstances, such as V2G AC, involve a grid-connected inverter that is mobile, such as in the
EV, and not permanently installed. In these circumstances, the alternate certification is UL
1741SC. This certification is for the EVSE, which will be designed to verify the connected EV
contains an inverter that is designed and tested per the automotive standard, namely SAE J3072
[4]. SAE J3072 outlines the IEEE 1547 performance requirements for an EV’s inverter and is the
foundation for UL 1741SC’s verification process.

In addition to IEEE 1547, some sites with energy assets may be subject to power import and
export limits to maintain grid reliability and stability. UL 3141 [6] is a safety and performance
standard that defines the functionality for a power control system(PCS) to meet these power
import and export limits across a site of energy assets. Although these requirements are separate
from IEEE 1547, it is important to understand that a site may be subject to both standards.

As highlighted in Figure 3, all energy assets at a site connected to the grid must comply with the
requirements of IEEE 1547 to maintain grid reliability and stability. However, the certification
process for the testing to ensure these devices will support those requirements are different for
stationary and mobile DERs, requiring UL 1741SB and UL 1741SC, respectively. This figure
also highlights UL 3141 requirements that will be placed on the meter and supported by a site
PCS.

A unique element for V2G AC charging is the separation of the permanently grid-connected
asset, the EVSE, and the inverter that will provide AC power, which is inside the EV. This
scenario requires a pair of devices designed to work in harmony. All EVs intended for

2.4 UL 3141
 18B

In addition to IEEE 1547, some utilities require systems to comply with power import and export
limits to support grid capacity constraints. UL 3141 is a safety and performance standard that
defines requirements for a PCS used in DER installations. The standard was developed to
address the increasing complexity of DER-integrated electrical systems and evolving grid
constraints that impose limits on power import and export at a PCC. A PCS is a system
consisting of one or more devices that actively monitor, limit, and control current or power on
conductors or bus bars as set forth by UL 3141. PCS functionality can be implemented for both
single-source and multisource electrical systems (as shown in Table 2) and is intended to ensure
safe, predictable operation under normal and dynamic grid conditions. Note that this document
uses the terms PCS and PCS controller interchangeably outside this section, except where
otherwise specified.

UL 3141 defines multiple PCS functional categories, each addressing specific control and
protection objectives, as defined in Table 2.

Testing and Performance Framework:
UL 3141 provides a structured, testable framework for evaluating PCS functionality. Testing is

 • General and normal operating tests: Verify that when a control command or step change

 within ±7.5% of the set point.
 • Step-change tests: Evaluate PCS response to sudden changes at the testpoint (such as
 PCC) in load or generation, including load increases or decreases and rapid changes in
 DER output (e.g., PV inverter ramping).
 • Single-source PCS tests: Confirm correct overload protection and import/export limiting
 when only one source of supply is present.
 • Multisource PCS tests: Assess PCS coordination across multiple interacting sources,
 ensuring limits are maintained without instability or unintended overload.

3 Communication and Control Requirements
 1B

As mentioned in Section 2.1, IEEE 1547 defines the technical requirements for interconnection
of DERs with the grid. Employing relevant communication protocols plays a crucial role in
enabling the desired interaction with a DER and supporting necessary grid benefits. Hence, IEEE
1547 requires at least one of the following protocols to be implemented at minimum:

 • DNP3 over Ethernet.
 • SunSpec Modbus over Ethernet or RS-485.
 • IEEE 2030.5 over Ethernet.
The standard also allows the use of additional communication protocols, including proprietary
ones if there is a mutual agreement with the utility. The use of industry standard protocols
enables a more open market environment for consumers, while the option for proprietary systems
enables a more customized solution space allowing for the development of additional
functionality. This results in a diverse set of market options, limiting interoperability, and
potentially benefiting from a solution that communicates across multiple protocols.

A market study was performed to investigate the landscape of communication protocols
supported by inverters currently in production. The list of inverters for this study was compiled
from multiple sources including, but not limited to:

3.2 Key Protocols of Interest
 20B

The market study shows that SunSpec Modbus and IEEE 2030.5, which also form part of the

protocols. In addition to these two protocols supported by stationary DERs (PV and battery

The newest version of this protocol, OCPP 2.1 [15], which was released in 2025, introduces key

also establishes native support for ISO 15118-2 [16] and ISO 15118-20 [17], which are popular
protocols used for EV-to-EVSE communications. Additionally, in-site communication is beyond
the scope of IEEE 1547 (explained in Section 2.1). Hence, leveraging OCPP 2.1 is a reasonable
choice to facilitate communication between the gateway and the EVSE for DER control and
between the charge station management system and EVSE for managed charging.

Thus, these three communication protocols, namely OCPP 2.1, SunSpec Modbus, and IEEE
2030.5, have been chosen to consolidate the interactions of multiple types of DER assets via the

3.2.1 OCPP
OCPP is an open communications protocol developed by the Open Charge Alliance to enable
interactions between EVSE and the charge station management system. This protocol is designed
to be vendor agnostic and hence enables communication interoperability between any EVSE
manufacturer and relevant EV charging stakeholders. In the context of this report, OCPP will
enable the PCS gateway to coordinate with EVSE in compliance with IEEE 1547, while OCPP
may also be used for separate charge station management system objectives, such as access
control and charge session status metrics.

Released in 2015, OCPP 1.6 is the most widely deployed OCPP standard but lacks a few useful
features required to fully support IEEE 1547. On top of the smart charging capabilities defined in
OCPP 1.6, OCPP 2.0.1 introduced more capabilities such as frequency support and local load
balancing in addition to native support for bidirectional charging and ISO 15118-2
communications with the EV. OCPP 2.1 added native support for ISO 15118-20 enabling V2X,
and crucially, new functional blocks called “DER Control” and “Energy Management System”
explicitly enabling support for energy management using DERs. These additions in OCPP 2.0.1
and OCPP 2.1 enable compatibility with IEEE 1547. OCPP 2.1 Edition 1 is now officially
published as an IEC standard, IEC 63584-210: 2025 [18]. This section serves as an overview of
the protocol functionality in reference to its incorporation as part of a single gateway solution.

OCPP 2.1 describes the following message field definitions that can be used for supporting IEEE
1547 functions:

 • SetDERControl: This message is designed to communicate DER control settings such as
 voltage trip curves, frequency trip curves, reactive power curves and parameters, active
 power curves, and frequency droop, among other parameters. These settings can also be
 scheduled using the “start time” and “duration” fields.
 • ClearDERControl: This message allows the charge station operator to clear any default
 and/or previously set DER controls settings in any EVSE.
 • GetDERControl: This message enables the charge station operator to retrieve currently
 active DER settings of any EVSE. The EVSE then uses the ReportDERControl message
 to respond with the details of the currently active DER settings.
 • ReportDERControl: EVSE uses this message in response to a GetDERControl message
 initiated by the charge station operator to communicate its currently active DER settings.
 • NotifyDERAlarm: This message is used by the EVSE to inform the charge station
 operator of any grid event that has caused a fault. Additional information like DER
 control type and the type of grid event that caused this fault is also included as part of this
 message. An “ended” flag can be used by the EVSE to inform the charge station operator
 that this fault alarm has ended.
 • NotifyDERStartStop: This message is used by the EVSE to communicate which DER
 control is taking over and what caused it. Similarly, this message is also used to indicate
 when this event ends with an “ended” flag.

 16

 • DataTransfer: This message can be used by either EVSE or the charge station operator to
 initiate a vendor-specific information exchange. This message requires vendor-specific
 implementation on both the EVSE and charge station operator.
All messages in OCPP 2.1 are designed in a request/response pair format in which each
message’s “request” can be initiated by the EVSE, charge station management system, or both.
Some of the fields in each message’s field structure are defined as “optional.” This means that, in
practice, some of the fields in a specific message can be omitted by the sender. OCPP 2.1 defines
the information embedded within each of its message’s structure while considering the
constraints/limitations that arise from a request/response-based communication format.

OCPP Response to IEEE 1547
OCPP 2.1 has smart charging, bidirectional power transfer, and DER control functional blocks in
addition to the messages described above, with the intention to support IEEE 1547 functions
such as voltage-active power (V/W) and V/Q. Table B-1 and Table B-2 provide a detailed
mapping of OCPP 2.1 message fields to IEEE 1547 parameters for these two functions. These
two functions have been used in this report as an example to depict OCPP 2.1’s mapping with
IEEE 1547.

Additionally, OCPP 2.1 only defines the message structure but does not impose any
requirements on the protocol’s implementation, with the protocol allowing the use of some
message fields to be optional. OCPP 2.1 defines a prescribed sequence of information exchange
for various use cases and scenarios, but this description is limited to primary stakeholders only.
As a result, clarifying the practical challenges associated with implementation of interaction
between the EV charging stakeholders using OCPP2.1 to satisfy IEEE 1547 requirements is
crucial for designing a robust gateway solution.

Thus, implementation of interaction between the EV charging stakeholders using OCPP 2.1 to
satisfy IEEE 1547 requirements and the associated practical challenges will need to be clarified.

3.2.2 SunSpec Modbus
SunSpec Modbus is one of three protocol options that is required to be implemented at minimum
per IEEE 1547 and hence supports the compliance of a DER to IEEE 1547. SunSpec Modbus is
a protocol based on Modbus to exchange data. The physical layer can be Ethernet (and hence
Transmission Control Protocol/Internet Protocol [TCP/IP] can be used) or RS-485 per IEEE
1547. In Modbus, a device holding data (information) such as operational settings (e.g., V/Q
curve), measured values, and nameplate information is called a server. A client makes requests to
read and/or write to those pieces of information. Typically, a DER becomes the server, and an
upstream device such as the gateway becomes the client. This is illustrated in Figure 11.

A client specifies an address or a series of addresses to read the intended data or write a value (or

3.2.3 IEEE 2030.5
IEEE 2030.5 is another of three protocols required to be implemented at minimum per IEEE
1547 and hence supports the compliance of a DER with IEEE 1547. For IEEE 1547-related
information, it adopts generator sign convention 6 as IEEE 1547 does. To date, Rule 21 requires
 5F

IEEE 2030.5 by default to communicate with the local utility.

IEEE 2030.5 uses RESTful protocol to exchange data. One known example of such a protocol is
HTTP. Unlike SunSpec Modbus, a DER acts as a client and an upstream device, such as the
gateway, and a DER management system acts as a server. Thereby, a DER initiates the request to
get settings (i.e., HTTP GET), such as active power set point, V/Q curve, and maximum active
power, and pushes (HTTP PUT or POST) the update on the up-to-date settings and/or
operational information such as measured values.

Considering the above, it is not straightforward to change the settings such as active power set
point, as the timing to GET the setting is up to the client. To address the issue, IEEE 2030.5
defines the minimum polling requirement and offers a Subscription/Notification mechanism
where the client exposes its end point (i.e., Uniform Resource Identifier [URI]) so that the server
sends the notification over a new TCP link with a separate Transport Layer Security (TLS)
session.

While this section has demonstrated that the set of communication protocols chosen can facilitate

 1. IEEE 1547, IEEE 2030.5, and SunSpec Modbus are all based on generator sign

 3. In addition to translating information from one protocol to another, the sequence of
 communication also forms a crucial part of enabling interaction across different protocols

standards to avoid vendor lock-in, as non-standardized protocols (i.e., proprietary protocols)
require a proxy from the vendor of the DER. 7 Note that the proxy also facilitates communication

The PEL/PIL PCS introduced in Section 2.4 can be leveraged to address those two. Indeed,

also enables the PCS to be a proxy to the AC voltage measurement for DERs.

As identified, the measured AC voltage needs to be communicated to the DERs for compliance
with IEEE 1547. Additionally, the limit for flex service/interconnection is communicated by the
utility. This calls out the need of having the “gateway” function described earlier into the PCS,
namely PCS gateway. With those combined features, the PCS gateway will:

 • Monitor the active power at the PCC and instruct one or more DERs for desired active
 power to meet flex service/interconnection (PCS PEL/PIL function).

 • Monitor the AC voltage at the PCC for the DERs to comply with IEEE 1547
 requirements.
 • Translate IEEE 2030.5 messages from the utility into the protocols that the DERs use
 (i.e., SunSpec Modbus, IEEE 2030.5, and OCPP 2.1) as identified in Section 3 to
 exchange:
 o The utility’s dynamic limit for flex service/interconnection with the utility.
 o Settings for IEEE 1547 functional requirements, such V/Q curve with the DERs.
 o AC voltage at the PCC with the DERs.
While these functionalities complement each other, there can be multiple active power set points
and/or limits that originate from different purposes. There are many sources beyond flex
service/interconnection-originated limit and IEEE 1547 requirements; the site or one or more
DERs may provide bulk system service, or PV can charge batteries during the day, including
EVs, and use the charged energy at night to reduce the electricity purchase. Those different
objectives need to be prioritized and harmonized. Notably, under IEEE 1547, active power is
modulated only during abnormal voltage or frequency conditions. The resulting active power set
point/limit is momentary and hence it does not influence active power during normal conditions.

The “harmonized” active power limit and/or set point needs to be provided to individual DERs. 8 7F

The allocation of those also needs to be computed.

As the central information hub, the PCS gateway understands the grid’s needs and each DER’s
needs, including requested state of charge of a battery (including EVs).

The National Laboratory of the Rockies has extensively researched smart charge management—
an algorithm that harmonizes the needs for a large load and a local distribution system (i.e.,
mitigating overcurrent at an overhead transformer), referencing EVs as an example of such a
load. This can support flex service, as it controls large loads to maintain the active power level
below the utility desired limit. Regardless of the limit level, smart charge management generates
set points for each DER. Hence, PCS and smart charge management complement each other to
generate an active power set point that can apply to normal conditions, and PCS can override or
limit the active power when necessary. Table 8 provides an example use cases that the smart-
charge-management-integrated PCS gateway supports.

Conclusion
1B

As site loads continue to grow in size the incentive and need for flexible local energy assets will
expand. These energy assets have the potential to benefit both site and grid operations, but must
comply with grid codes and interconnection requirements. Due to the diverse landscape of
energy assets, grid requirements, and coordination protocols, a new PCS gateway will be needed
to navigate this landscape and ensure a cohesive site response to local utility and on-site facility
needs. The National Lab of the Rockies is developing these technologies to simplify installation
of energy assets for consumers, as well as considering new coordination opportunities that will
expand UL 3141 PEL/PIL to support distribution capacity limitations.
