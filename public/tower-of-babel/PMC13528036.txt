Bridging policy and practice: a scoping review of health system implementation research practice in Ethiopia
Authors: Berhanu Fikadie Endehabtu, Emebet Birhanu Lealem, Destaw Fetene Teshome, Getasew Amare, Kassu Ketema Gurmu, Bejoy Nambiar, Mohammed Aliye, Binyam Tilahun, Kassahun Alemu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImplementation research plays a key role in bridging the gap between evidence and practice, particularly in low-resource settings. In Ethiopia, persistent challenges in healthcare access, quality, and system performance highlight the need for context-specific implementation evidence. Although implementation research has been advancing in the country, its scope, practice, and contribution to health system improvement remain insufficiently characterized and documented. This gap underscores the need to assess the landscape of healthcare and health system implementation research in Ethiopia to inform decisions about health policies, programs, and practices. Therefore, this scoping review aimed to comprehensively investigate the current landscape of healthcare and health system implementation research in Ethiopia.MethodsWe conducted a scoping review in accordance with Joanna Briggs Institute guidance and PRISMA-ScR reporting standards. A systematic search of major electronic databases and relevant sources was performed using the Population, Concept, and Context (PCC) framework, which involved keywords and MeSH terms. Two researchers independently screened the studies and extracted data using a piloted form, resolving and discrepancies through discussion. Extracted data included study characteristics, outcomes, contextual factors, and implementation strategies. We categorized implementation strategies using ERIC frameworks. The findings were analyzed through descriptive and thematic approaches and organized across key health system blocks and contextual dimensions.ResultsThe search identified 10,853 studies. One hundred sixty-four articles underwent full-text review, and 96 were included in the analysis. The studies included in the review used 40 mixed methods, 23 quantitative, and 33 qualitative approaches. According to the health system building blocks, 70 studies focused on service delivery, 16 on health information systems, and 5 on leadership or health financing. Among the service delivery studies, neonatal, child, maternal, and reproductive health were most commonly addressed topics. Key implementation outcomes included fidelity, feasibility, acceptability, sustainability, and cost-effectiveness. Forty-three studies used determinant, process, or evaluation frameworks, including Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research; Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance framework; Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, and Sustainment; Theoretical Domains Framework; Medical Research Council Framework; diffusion and social network theories; and Donabedian’s quality framework. Fifty studies involved stakeholders in implementation research. Global funders supported 72 studies. A total of 55 studies reported the use of implementation strategies, with 27 out of 39 maternal and child health studies including such strategies.ConclusionsThis review highlights implementation research conducted with a strong focus on service delivery, particularly in maternal, neonatal, and child health. Stakeholder engagement played a crucial role, while global funding supported research efforts. The broad use of implementation strategies demonstrates ongoing efforts to translate research into practice. These findings underscore the need for continued investment in implementation science to enhance healthcare delivery and outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s43058-026-00989-w.

Background

Ethiopia faces a triple disease burden of communicable diseases, noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), and injuries [1], complicating the public health landscape as the healthcare system must address multiple ongoing challenges [2, 3]. Addressing these complex health issues requires innovative, sustainable, and locally appropriate solutions that consider the unique socio-economic, cultural, and infrastructural contexts of the country [4].

Several evidence-based interventions and innovations are available globally and locally to reduce the disease burden and enhance the well-being of individuals and populations [5]. However, one of the greatest challenges facing the health community is effectively implementing these proven interventions and innovations in practice [6].

Considering these challenges, implementation research has emerged as a valuable approach to addressing health problems. Implementation research is defined as the scientific study of methods and strategies used to promote the systematic uptake, integration, and scale-up of evidence-based interventions into routine health practice and policy, with the goal of improving health services and population health outcomes [7]. It focuses not only on determining whether interventions are effective but also on understanding how to successfully implement interventions within actual health systems. It helps to recognize the role of contextual factors, their importance in the successes and failures of implementation strategies, and where adaptation is needed in different settings [8]. Moreover, implementation research can facilitate the scale-up and sustainment of evidence-based interventions and policies [9–11].

In Ethiopia, implementation research is vital due to challenges such as limited resources, a large rural population, and health disparities. It helps translate national health policies into effective local practices with in the country’s decentralized health system. Substantial efforts have been made to improve health outcomes through national health strategies, reforms, and programs, including the Health Sector Transformation Plans, revitalization of primary healthcare, and expansion of community-based services [12, 13]. Despite reforms, issues such as workforce capacity and resource availability impact implementation and documentation of implementation research. This gap highlights the need for more comprehensive research to inform decisions about health policies, programs, and practices. Therefore, this scoping review aims to explore and map the current landscape of implementation research in Ethiopia, serving as a foundational document to guide future research efforts in the field.

Review questions

This scoping review aimed to comprehensively explore the landscape of healthcare and health system implementation research in Ethiopia. It sought to identify key trends, gaps, and opportunities for advancing the field. The specific review questions considered in this review were:What are the characteristics of implementation research practices in Ethiopia?What methods and approaches have been most commonly used in implementation research studies?What frameworks, theories and models have authors used in their implementation studies?Who and how have stakeholders been involved in the implementation process of the implementation intervention?What implementation outcomes are measured and reported in the included studies?

Methods

This scoping review mapped the existing evidence on the landscape of implementation of healthcare and/or health system research in Ethiopia. It was conducted using the Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist [14] and was guided by the Joanna Briggs Institute’s scoping review guidance [15].

Protocol and registration

The protocol for this review was registered on the Open Science Framework [16], with the registration details available at https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/U93BQ, a widely recognized platform that promotes transparency and collaboration in research.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria for this review were based on the Population, Concept and Context (PCC) framework proposed by the Joanna Briggs Institute for scoping reviews (Table 1). The review included studies that contain relevant information about healthcare and/or health system implementation research involving human populations, regardless of age, sex or health condition. Studies were classified as implementation research if they addressed any aspects of implementation research questions, such as the adoption, integration, delivery, scale-up, sustainability, determinants, strategies, processes, or implementation outcomes of health interventions, programs, or policies in real-world settings. The review also included studies that explored any type of implementation research using quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-methods design. In terms of quantitative methods, studies using observational, quasi experimental, and experimental designs were considered. This scoping review also examined implementation research across the WHO health system building blocks, which encompasses health information, health workforce, medical products and technologies, leadership and governance, healthcare financing, and service delivery) [17].

We excluded studies not conducted in Ethiopia, studies unrelated to health or health systems, clinical efficacy studies conducted under highly controlled conditions without an emphasis on implementation, protocols, commentaries, editorials, conference abstracts without sufficient data. Additionally, studies that did not present relevant implementation research outcomes or processes were also excluded. Studies were not excluded based on lack of free full-text access; rather, we attempted to obtain full texts through institutional access and author contact before exclusion when articles remained unobtainable.

There were no restrictions by publication year. Only studies published in English were included due to feasibility and resource constraints for translation. We acknowledge that this may have excluded relevant studies published in Amharic or other local languages.

Information sources

Studies that used implementation research approaches were identified through electronic bibliographic database, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, PsycInfo, and Google Scholar. Reference list screening and cross-referencing were performed for included papers.

Search strategy

The search strategy was carried out by a paired research team (DFT and EB). The search followed a three-step approach. First, we conducted a preliminary search of relevant literature to identify key concepts and keywords. Secondly, using the insights gained from the initial search, we developed a detailed search strategy that incorporated the identified keywords and phrases. This search strategy was based on Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and text words found in the titles, abstracts, and keywords sections of documents related to implementation research conducted in Ethiopia. We subsequently adapted this search strategy for use in other databases and search engines to facilitate the retrieval of published studies. To increase sensitivity, we included all synonyms for “implementation research” and “Ethiopia”. It used Boolean operators (AND, OR) for checking availability of possible relevant implementation research articles conducted in Ethiopia. Thirdly, the list of references was retrieved not to miss relevant articles (Supplementary file 1).

Study selection

After the final searches were completed, all studies were transferred to Covidence for additional cross-referencing, duplicate removal, screening, and data extraction [18]. Two research team members (DFT and EB) then screened the articles based on titles and abstracts. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved by another team member (KA), who facilitated a discussion to reach consensus. The reviewers then conducted full-text screening for eligibility using a process similar to that used for title and abstract screening. Full texts of studies that were freely accessible and available from different research websites were downloaded and reviewed.

Data charting

Reviewers used a piloted data extraction sheet to extract data from articles included in the full-text review. The extracted data included authors’ name and publication year, study design, study outcomes, and evaluated health interventions (health program). We also extracted information on implementation characteristics (outcome-based, barrier, and enabler), tested implementation strategies and their features, such as the actors involved, actions taken, action targets, temporality, dose, implementation outcomes addressed, stakeholder involvement, and theoretical justification. Additionally, we documented the methodological approach used, the framework applied, source of funding, barriers, gaps mentioned, and limitations appraised by research team members.

Synthesis of results

Results were presented using the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram. We synthesized and summarized findings from the studies using descriptive analysis and narrative interpretation. We first summarized the characteristics of the included studies and their results. These was followed by a synthesis of the data to identify common themes, gaps, and trends in the implementation research landscape in Ethiopia. We mapped the range of health issues addressed, the types of implementation strategies used, and the effectiveness of these strategies. We also highlighted notable gaps in both methodological approaches and content areas, emphasizing under-researched topics and settings where future implementation research could have the greatest impact. These gaps were identified by mapping the included studies against national health priorities, the burden of disease, WHO health system building blocks, and population groups. Implementation strategies were extracted and categorized using the ERIC taxonomy. Reported strategies were mapped to ERIC-defined categories through an iterative process. These categories included training and education, clinician support, stakeholder engagement, evaluative and iterative strategies, contextual adaptation, interactive assistance, consumer engagement, infrastructure change, and financial strategies. Classification was reviewed by multiple reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through consensus. The categorized strategies were analyzed using descriptive statistics and thematic synthesis. To enhance analysis and clarity of reporting, data were further categorized across multiple dimensions, including health system building blocks, health condition/program area, population group, and stakeholder/interest-holder groups.

Results

Search results

The search identified 10,853 potentially relevant studies, including 3906 from Embase, 1786 from Scopus, 2118 from PubMed, 1476 from MEDLINE, 1280 from Web of Science, 177 from Google Scholar, and 110 from PsycINFO. A total of 4863 duplicate studies were removed. Of the 5990 studies screened based on their titles and abstracts, 164 were deemed eligible for full-text screening. Characteristics of included studies

The review included 96 studies, with 75 (78%) conducted in Ethiopia and the remaining being multinational studies that included Ethiopia. The methodologies varied: forty studies (42%) used a mixed-methods approach [19, 20], 23 (24%) used quantitative methods [21–23], and 33 (34%) used qualitative methods [24]. The designs of these studies included cross-sectional [25–30], economic evaluations [31], randomized controlled trials [32–37], quasi-experimental designs [38–40], longitudinal, retrospective, hybrid design [41], qualitative designs [42–44].

Of the studies, 24 (25%) included data from clients, such as mothers [25], fathers [25], grandparents, children, religious leaders, kebele administrators [45], and traditional birth attendants [25]. Twenty-one (22%) studies represented views of health professionals [25, 26, 46] and health extension workers [45], while 15 (16%) studies involved stakeholders. Additionally, nine (9%) studies included patients, health professionals; and stakeholders; seven (7%) included both clients and health professionals, and 21 (22%) studies included data from health professionals and stakeholders. Some research also collected data from the rural households [38], document review, and regional health office NTD staff [45].

Implementation research practices

The review identified ten key themes in implementation research. These themes include trends in healthcare and/or health system implementation research, focus area of the implementation research, the implementation interventions or programs that were evaluated, outcomes measured, both the facilitator and barriers to implementation, and implementation theories, frameworks, and models utilized. Additionally, the themes encompassed implementation strategies, stakeholder engagement, the research team, and the sources of funding for the implementation research.

Focus area of implementation research

Implementation research by health system building blocks

Of the total included implementation research studies, 70 (73%) of them focused on service delivery, 16 (17%) on health information systems (HIS), 5 (5%) on leadership and health financing [31, 47–49], 3 (3%) studies on healthcare providers’ capacity buildings [46, 50, 51], and 2 (2%) on medicine, supply, and technologies [52, 53].

Implementation research by health condition/program area

Of the 70 studies focused on service delivery, 39 (41%) were conducted on neonatal and child health [30, 54–70], reproductive and maternal health [21, 24, 25, 71–81], and maternal and child health [26, 33, 82–86]. Twelve (12%) studies were focused on non-communicable diseases program including NCDs service integration [29, 87], cancer [32, 88], mental health [28, 89, 90], podoconiasis [45, 91, 92], lymphedema [93], primary healthcare strengthening [94], nine (9%) on communicable diseases including tuberculosis [36, 43, 95], HIV [96], TB and HIV [35], Trachoma [97], malaria [98], and antisepsis and infection control [20], eight (8%) on nutrition [23, 34, 99–104], and two on environmental health [38, 105].

Implementation interventions or programs evaluated

The review identified various implementation interventions evaluated including Kangaroo mother care [30, 48, 66, 68–70], participatory antenatal care strengthening intervention [25], WHO surgical safety checklist [20], WASH intervention [38], evidence based clinical algorithms [52], optimizing the health extension program intervention [51], continuous quality improvement interventions [79], community engagement and defaulter tracing system [51, 65], supervisory and mentorship intervention package [81], Ethiopian primary healthcare clinical guideline (EPHCG) on primary healthcare strengthening [94], implementing integrated community case management (iCCM) on child health [49], holistic integrated care package [93], quality improvement interventions on urban health extension program (UHEP) [79].

More specifically, this review identified several evidence-based interventions related to maternal, neonatal, and child health. These included youth-friendly services [71], dynamic birthing positions [72], focused antenatal care package implementation, maternal nutrition interventions (MNIs) [75], basic emergency obstetric and newborn care services [26, 82, 84], integrated care pathway, Kangaroo mother care (KMC) [30, 66, 68–70], CRADLE Vital Sign Alert [74], service integration [78], community-based interventions for newborns in Ethiopia (COMBINE) [106], phone-based monthly unannounced pill counts [77], performance-based financing [47], possible serious bacterial infection guideline [64].

Implementation research in HIS also encompasses a wide range of interventions including eCHIS [41, 42, 107], urban community health information system (UCHIS) [19], CBMP [44], human development model interventions [39], training and post-training follow-up intervention [22], Performance based non-financial incentive intervention [40], Data-informed platform for health [108], on-site training-mentoring [109], electronic medical record [110], mHealth [111], performance monitoring teams (PMT) [112], and data for decision-making interventions [113].

In this review studies conducted on the prevention and control of communicable disease included implementation interventions such as guideline implementation [96], nutrition assessment, counseling, and support implementation [43], surgery, antibiotics, facial cleanliness, environmental improvement (SAFE) strategy [97], integrated care package [93], combination intervention package (CIP) [35, 37], digital adherence technologies [36], X-ray intervention, Artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) [95], and e-health educational intervention [46].

The included studies on nutrition program addressed various types of implementation interventions including community-based grain bank intervention [99], psychosocial stimulation interventions [23], community-based participatory nutrition promotion (CPNP) [34], mobile health and nutrition team service implementation [100], IYCF and nutrition-sensitive agriculture message [101], community-based infant and young child feeding intervention [102], adopting amylase-rich flour [103], and implementation of nutrition interventions [104].

Of the studies focused on NCDs the implementation intervention included NCD screening intervention with TB patient care [29], early palliative care in newly diagnosed cancer [32], community-based cancer prevention efforts [88], and NCDs integration with HIV care. One intervention (hypertension treatment based on 2020 ISH guidelines) was targeted on hypertension management [31], two studies focused on Ethiopian Primary Healthcare Clinical Guidelines on management of NCDs including mental Health [77, 90], and other two studies was the integration of primary mental healthcare [28, 89].

Studies identified implementation barriers and enablers

Of the included studies, 56 (58%) discussed enablers and barriers of implementation intervention. Of these, 35 (36%) studies used implementation research frameworks or models to identify implementation barriers and facilitators. Across studies, barriers and facilitators were reported at multiple levels of the implementation context, most commonly aligned with CFIR domains. These included individual characteristics, inner setting (organizational factors), outer setting (external environment), and implementation processes. In terms of distribution, one study focused on implementation process, individual characteristics, inner and outer setting, three (3%) studies on individual characteristics, 11 (12%) studies on individual characteristics and inner setting, 14 (15%) studies on individual characteristics, inner and outer settings, six (6%) studies on the inner setting only, eight (8%) studies on inner and outer settings, three (3%) on individual characteristics and outer setting, three on both individual characteristics, innovation characteristics, inner and outer settings and another three on barriers in the outer settings.

Implementation theory, frameworks, and models

Among included studies, sixteen studies employed the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) framework, the majority of the studies used it to identify both barriers and facilitators to the implementation of evidence-based interventions [22, 27, 41, 78, 86, 87, 95, 104, 107, 109, 112, 113], while others used it to identify only barriers to implementation [20, 27], adopt interventions [109, 111], and assess the scale up of the intervention [28]. Six studies utilized the Reach, effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation and Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework to conduct need assessment [46], identify implementation barriers and facilitators [21, 57], evaluate effectiveness [57, 81, 88], feasibility of the intervention [81], and examine the scale up of an intervention [62]. The other studies also used both CFIR with RE-AIM frameworks [58, 59, 66]. The Context and Implementation of Complex Interventions (CICI) framework [45, 91, 92] and the Medical Research Council (MRC) framework [74] were also used in a small number of studies to guide evaluation of complex interventions. Proctor’s implementation outcomes framework [89] was specifically applied to define and measure implementation outcomes. And Bowen feasibility framework [33] and Technological and systems, Economic, Legal, Operational and Schedule feasibility (TELOS) framework [114], which was applied to assess key feasibility domains such as acceptability, demand, implementation, practicality, and integration was also reported.

Process models were less frequently used. The Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, and Sustainment (EPIS) framework/model was applied to identify implementation challenges [29], evaluate implementation outcomes including effectiveness, fidelity, acceptability, appropriateness, sustainability [56], and feasibility [19, 56]. The ADAPT-ITT approach [24] was used to guide the adaptation of interventions to local contexts.

Theoretical approaches were used in a limited number of studies. These included the Theoretical domains framework (TDF) [52], TDF with combination of CFIR [90], Trans theoretical model, diffusion and social network theories [94]. Implementation strategies

Of the total studies included in this review, 55 (57%) utilized implementation strategies to change an implementation intervention (s) into practice. By program area, implementation strategies were reported in 27 of 39 maternal and child health studies, 5 of 9 communicable disease studies [20, 35, 96–98], 9 of 16 health information system (HIS) studies [22, 27, 39–41, 108, 109, 112, 113], 2 of 8 nutrition studies [99, 101], and 7 (7%)of 12 non-communicable disease studies [28, 29, 45, 90, 92–94].

The most frequently applied ERIC taxonomy category was train and educate stakeholders, which included training of healthcare providers, train-the-trainer approaches, simulation-based skills training, and multimedia education. The second most common category was support clinicians, which included mentoring, coaching, and supportive supervision. Other ERIC categories identified included develop stakeholder interrelationships, reflected in stakeholder engagement activities; change infrastructure, including supply chain strengthening and task shifting; use evaluative and iterative strategies, such as accountability mechanisms and audit-related approaches; and provide interactive assistance, including onsite technical support and implementation facilitation activities.

Sixteen studies mentioned single component strategies, most commonly within the “train and educate stakeholders” category including training [56, 63, 67, 81, 92], trainers of trainers [93], mentoring [113], hands on skills training using simulation technology [84], home visits [21], multimedia education [54], supply chain management [58], onsite interventional strategy [109], accountability [112], and task sharing [28].

Moreover, 39 studies mentioned multifaceted implementation strategies, most frequently combining training with supportive supervision, mentoring, and coaching. Across studies, training of healthcare providers at various levels of the health system was the most dominant ly mentioned strategy. In addition, 11 studies reported supporting clinicians, primarily through supportive supervision. Nine studies used support clinicians strategies in the form of mentoring, while three studies reported coaching. Four studies applied develop stakeholder interrelationships through broader capacity-building activities, and three studies used change infrastructure strategies focused on system strengthening to support implementation of health interventions or programs.

Stakeholder involvement

Of the 96 studies included in the review, 50 studies involved stakeholders in their implementation research. Stakeholder engagement was categorized as target groups, implementers, and consulted stakeholders. Thirteen studies included both national and international stakeholders and of these, eight studies engaged the Federal Ministry of Health of Ethiopia, universities, city administrators, and health care providers, primarily as implementers and coordinating bodies. Eleven studies engaged local stake holders, including health professionals (nine studies), community leaders (eight studies), health development army members (seven studies), women and father (four), traditional birth attendants (two studies), and clients and their families (one study). These groups were mainly reported as target beneficiaries and/or implementers of implementation strategies. Four studies involved both national and regional, six studies engaged regional stake holders, and two involved policy makers, developmental partners, and funders/donors, mainly in advisory or supportive roles.

The Federal Ministry of Health of Ethiopia was identified as a stakeholder in six studies related to HIS [19, 42], maternal and child health [69, 82], podoconiasis [64], and primary healthcare strengthening [94]. Regional health bureaus were involved in studies on on maternal and child health [26, 30, 69], routine health information system (RHIS) [108], infectious disease [64], and PHC [94]. District health office was were reported in seven studies, mainly in maternal and child health [26, 30, 54], mental health [28], RHIS [108], infectious disease [64], and PHC [94].

Research team

Of the 96 studies in the review, 38 reported collaboration between university-based research teams and project teams. Project teams refer to groups responsible for planning, coordinating, and implementing interventions within the study setting, while research teams are responsible for study design, data collection, analysis, and interpretation of findings. Twenty-five studies involved only university research teams, while 11 were comprised solely of project teams. Nine studies included international multidisciplinary teams. Four studies consisted of researchers from both universities and regional health bureaus, three involved researchers from the universities and the Ministry of Health of Ethiopia, and three included researchers from the universities, regional health bureaus, and the Ministry of Health.

Source of funding for implementation research

According to reviewed studies, a number of local and international funders funded the implementation research. Seventy-two were funded by international organizations based outside Ethiopia, five by local funders, and four of them funded by international organizations based in Ethiopia. Among the remaining studies, 15 studies did not mention any funding information. According to this review, Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation was found to be most dominant funder, which supported 20 implementation research studies.

Discussions

This scoping review aimed to synthesize the landscape of implementation studies conducted in Ethiopia by examining various aspect, including focus areas, implementation outcomes, barriers and enablers, levels of stakeholder engagement, intervention programs and associated implementation strategies, use of theoretical frameworks, and sources of funding.

The review found that implementation research in Ethiopia is predominantly concentrated in service delivery, with maternal and child health identified as the most frequently studied area, followed closely by health information systems. While implementation strategies were often employed, they were mostly limited to training and supervision, and the use of standardized implementation frameworks was inconsistent. Stakeholder engagement was reported in approximately half of the studies, with varying degrees of collaboration between research teams and implementation actors.

This review covered multiple health domains, providing useful insights into the current state of implementation research in these areas. Many studies focused on the donor driven public health areas or programs particularly neonatal, child, maternal, and reproductive health, indicating a strong emphasis on addressing these urgent global health issues [115]. This finding aligns with evidence from other low- and middle-income countries, where implementation research has historically focused on priority programmatic areas such as maternal, neonatal, and child health, and communicable diseases [116]. Similar patterns have been reported across sub-Saharan Africa, where research priorities are often shaped by global health funding streams and high burden diseases conditions [117]. However, postnatal care lacks implementation research evidence on effective strategies to improve coverage and outcomes.

There has been also significant progress in implementation research on HIS, such as electronic community health information systems (eCHIS) and training initiatives, which aligns with the growing trend of leveraging technology to strengthen health systems [118]. The growing number of implementation research publications on HIS and data-use systems in Ethiopia may partly reflect ongoing investments and technical support from international partners and other global health initiatives that have prioritized strengthening health information systems and data-use programs [119, 120].

In contrast, implementation research remains relatively limited in NCDs, mental health, health system governance, financing, human resources, and supply chain. This underrepresentation of NCD research may hinder efforts to comprehend and address the growing burden of these diseases, which are increasingly recognized as a major public health problem in Ethiopia [121]. This gap indicates the need for more implementation research in this area. The focus on integrated care packages and guidelines for managing NCDs [28, 29, 45, 87, 89, 93] reflects a notable shift toward more holistic health strategies. This approach is consistent with previous findings that emphasize the importance of comprehensive care in addressing the multifaceted nature of NCDs [122, 123].

This review identified community-based cancer prevention efforts [88] and early palliative care initiatives [32] as critical components of a comprehensive healthcare intervention designed to address the multifaceted challenges posed by cancer, which align with the Ethiopian national cancer control plan strategies [124, 125]. Such initiatives foster collective responsibility and support among community members, potentially leading to more effective prevention and intervention strategies.

Implementation research also addresses a limited range of communicable diseases; for example, although malaria remains a significant public health burden, there is insufficient focus on evaluating the effectiveness of interventions and promoting community engagement in malaria control efforts. The included nutrition program studies covered various implementation interventions but lacked evidence on multi-sectoral approaches, quality improvement initiatives, and service integration related to implementation, scale-up, and sustainability. Addressing these gaps is essential for informed decision-making and enhancing health system performance.

The use of implementation outcomes, such as fidelity, feasibility, acceptability, sustainability, and cost-effectiveness, underscores the importance of ensuring that interventions are effective not only in controlled settings but also in real-world environments [126]. Two-third of the studies reviewed assess implementation outcomes to evaluate the success or failure of the implementation process or the strategies used. Furthermore, the assessment of adoption, coverage, and appropriateness sheds light on the reach and relevance of the implemented strategies. This assessment ensures that the strategies meet the needs of the target population and promote equitable access to care [127]. However, only a few studies included evaluations of adoption, coverage, and appropriateness, which are essential for helping policymakers and practitioners make informed decisions about which interventions to adopt, scale up, or modify based on their effectiveness and suitability for specific contexts [126, 127].

Evaluation of service outcomes, including effectiveness of the intervention, their potential for scale-up, and their impact on the overall functioning of the health system [128], offers a comprehensive understanding of the interventions’ overall impact on healthcare delivery and population health. This holistic evaluation can inform evidence-based policymaking, resource allocation, and the scale-up of successful programs, ultimately leading to more sustainable and impactful healthcare solutions [129]. Of the studies that measured service outcomes, majority focused on evaluation of the effectiveness of an intervention. But few was mentioned on scale up of implementation strategies. A scoping review conducted in LMICs also revealed that implementation research has primarily focused on evaluating whether strategies for implementing evidence-informed health interventions are effective. However, relatively little attention has been given to issues of scale-up and sustainability, despite their critical importance for achieving long term impact and strengthening health interventions [130].

Implementation theories, frameworks, and models are essential for guiding and evaluating the implementation process [131, 132]. Nearly half of the studies reviewed used various frameworks, with CFIR, REAIM, and EPIS as the most frequently cited. Determinant frameworks help researchers identify specific barriers and facilitators to successful implementation, which is crucial for tailoring strategies and increasing the likelihood of desired outcomes [133]. The CFIR framework was extensively used across numerous studies, demonstrating its broad relevance in understanding the factors that influence the adoption, implementation, and sustainability of interventions. A scoping review conducted in Africa also revealed a similar trend, with the CFIR being the most frequently used implementation research framework [134]. However, many studies that examined the enablers and barriers of innovative health programs did not use appropriate frameworks.

The REAIM framework was also frequently used in the studies reviewed, often in conjunction with CFIR frameworks. This combination arises because the REAIM framework may not fully encompass the complexities of the implementation process, particularly regarding the identification of contextual facilitators or barriers. Additionally, the EPIS model was frequently used as it provides a systematic approach for navigating the various phases of implementation. Other frameworks such as the TDF, Proctor framework, and CICI, were also cited for evaluating specific implementation outcomes like acceptability and feasibility, and to understand the contextual factors influencing complex interventions. However, the findings highlight a significant gap in the landscape of implementation frameworks, particularly concerning framework integration, context specificity, and the holistic assessment of implementation processes. Moreover, most frameworks are fragmented or too generic, limiting their use in complex, low-resource settings like Ethiopia. There is a clear need for process-oriented frameworks covering the entire implementation cycle from planning to sustainability. The limited application of standardized implementation science frameworks in Ethiopian studies reflects a trend observed in other low- and middle-income countries, where implementation research often relies on descriptive methods instead of theory-driven frameworks. In contrast, studies conducted in more established implementation science settings more frequently apply structured frameworks such as CFIR to identify determinants of implementation and ERIC to guide and classify implementation strategies.

Generating a well-designed implementation strategy is crucial for overcoming barriers, ensuring sustainability, and maximizing the impact of health programs [135]. This review reveals that many studies incorporated such implementation strategies, highlighting the importance of strategic planning and action in ensuring that health interventions are successfully adopted and sustained in real-world settings. Over a third of the studies on maternal and child health used tailored implementation strategies to address challenges like access to care, workforce capacity, and community engagement. Similarly, more than half of the studies focused on communicable diseases and HIS also emphasized the importance of implementation strategies for improving disease prevention, control, and data quality in health facilities.

Single component strategies, such as training, mentoring, home visits, and supply chain management were found in nearly one third of studies as implementation strategies. Training was the most frequently mentioned strategy for enhancing healthcare provider competencies, while mentoring highlighted the need for ongoing support. In contrast, over two-thirds of the studies employed multifaceted strategies, such as training, supportive supervision, and mentoring, reflecting a more holistic approaches to overcome the various health systems barriers.

Effective health interventions require changes not only in individual behavior but also in organizational structures for long-term, sustainable improvements in health systems. Early scale-up planning is crucial for ensuring that interventions are adaptable and integrated into systems [136]. However, the review revealed that only about half of the studies mentioned specific implementation strategies, and organizational strategies like capacity building, system strengthening, and coaching were less frequently included. The limited application of structured implementation frameworks in Ethiopian studies suggests that implementation science is still in a developmental stage in this setting. Scoping reviews in LMICs revealed that implementation research often focuses on operational and individual level capacity building activities, with comparatively limited attention to system-level reforms, governance, financing, sustainability, and scale up strategies [134].

Stakeholder involvement in implementation research is crucial for making findings relevant and ensuring effective integration into practice. Over half of the reviewed studies actively engaged stakeholders in the implementation process, mainly local health professionals, community leaders, and members of the health development army, highlighting their key role in successful health intervention. Most studies that involved stakeholders engaged local stakeholders, with health professionals being the most included group. Moreover, community leaders and health development army members were also frequently mentioned, indicating that the engagement of community-based actors is seen as critical for the success of health interventions, especially in areas with significant community-based health programs. On the other hand, traditional birth attendants were less frequently involved, suggesting an underutilization of specific community-based stakeholders who are essential in specific health settings. Some studies did engage both national and international stakeholders, including the Ministry of Health Ethiopia and the regional health bureaus, to address maternal and child health (MCH), HIS, and PHC, which play a crucial role in ensuring that interventions align with national health policies and strategies. From an evidence-to-action perspective, close collaboration between the research team and stakeholders is essential. Involving stakeholders throughout the research process improves the relevance and adoption of findings while promoting shared ownership and accountability. This partnership bridges the gap between knowledge creation and practical use and should be a key focus in implementation research strategies. From a policy standpoint, there is a need for greater integration of implementation research into health system decision-making. Strengthening collaboration between universities, ministries of health, and implementing partners may enhance the uptake of evidence. Additionally, increased use of structured implementation frameworks could improve the scalability and sustainability of interventions.

In conclusion, this review highlights the extensive implementation research in Ethiopia since 2006, focusing on maternal, neonatal, and child health. Various methodologies were employed to assess key implementation outcomes including feasibility, fidelity, and sustainability, with significant stakeholder engagement and global funding supporting these efforts.

Findings from this review suggest the need to strengthen national capacity and professional networks for implementation research in Ethiopia to enhance the generation and use of context-relevant evidence. Integration of implementation science into Ethiopia’s primary health care, health information systems, non-communicable disease, and equity agendas would help to ensure that evidence generation is aligned with national strategic goals. Furthermore, sustained and dedicated funding mechanisms are required to support implementation research across the full continuum, from adaptation and strategy development to scale-up and long-term sustainability.

Strengths and limitations

This review provides a comprehensive mapping of implementation research in Ethiopia using a structured scoping methodology. However, several limitations should be considered. Potential publication bias and variability in reporting across studies may have affected the completeness of the synthesis. In addition, restrictions in the search strategy, including the inclusion of English-language studies only, may have led to underestimation of the quantity and diversity of implementation research. Reliance on implementation research-specific terminology for searching may have missed relevant studies that used practice, quality improvement, program evaluation, or health-system language without explicitly labeling the work as implementation research. Furthermore, although efforts were made to obtain full-text articles through institutional access and author contact, restricted access to some studies may have introduced selection bias
