Precision modification of heart failure signaling by CRISPR-Cas9 base editing
Authors: Tomonori Tadokoro, Ning Liu, Eric N Olson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeart failure remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and current therapies largely focus on symptom management and slowing disease progression rather than correcting the underlying molecular abnormalities. Recent advances in genome editing technologies have created new opportunities to treat heart failure. Among these approaches, CRISPR-Cas9 base editing has emerged as a particularly promising strategy because it enables precise nucleotide conversions without introducing double-strand DNA breaks and demonstrates relatively high efficiency in vivo.While correction of disease-causing mutations by CRISPR-Cas9 base editing represents an important application of genome editing, an alternative strategy is to directly modulate key signaling pathways that drive cardiac dysfunction. Protein kinase C alpha (PKCα) functions as a key regulator of cardiac contractility and pathological remodeling. Precision editing of phosphorylation sites that control PKCα stability or activation may therefore represent an effective strategy to suppress maladaptive kinase signaling in cardiomyocytes. This concept of “precision signaling modification” may provide a broadly applicable therapeutic approach for heart failure. Similar strategies may also be applicable to other signaling molecules, including Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II delta (CaMKIIδ), and illustrate the broader potential of signaling-focused genome editing approaches.Despite these advances, several challenges remain for clinical translation, including efficient delivery of genome editing components to the adult heart, long-term safety, and potential immune responses. Continued advances in delivery technologies and genome editing platforms may ultimately enable durable, potentially one-time therapeutic interventions for heart failure.Keywords: Translational medicine, Post-translational modification, Protein phosphorylation, Gene editing, Adeno-associated virus

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease accounts for approximately one-third of all deaths worldwide [1]. Heart failure (HF) is a complex clinical syndrome characterized by symptoms resulting from structural or functional impairment of ventricular filling and pump function [2]. HF has poor prognosis, with a 5-year survival rate of approximately 60% [3]. Current standard therapies have significantly improved outcomes by modulating neurohumoral pathways [2]. However, there remains a critical need for durable interventions that directly target intracellular signaling drivers of HF progression.

CRISPR-Cas9 has emerged as a powerful genome-editing tool, with rapid technological advances in recent years [4]. In particular, in vivo genome editing has shown potential for correcting genetic abnormalities associated with dilated cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and congenital heart disease [5–10]. Although many of these diseases are associated with genetic mutations, conventional therapies do not address the underlying genetic defects, and treatment has largely relied on supportive management of HF [2,11–13]. Consequently, for patients with these genetic predispositions, HF often progresses steadily despite lifelong adherence to multiple medications, severely impacting their quality of life [2].

CRISPR-Cas9–based gene-editing therapy enables targeting of genes inaccessible to conventional treatments [5–10]. However, patients with monogenic cardiomyopathies or congenital heart diseases represent only a small fraction of the total HF population. Moreover, developing a gene-editing strategy for individual mutations requires substantial effort and cost. Considering the large diversity of mutations, personalized approaches, including base editing, may not be feasible [14]. Rather than addressing each specific mutation, disrupting key pathogenic signaling pathways shared across different mutations could broaden the applicability of a single therapeutic approach. In this review, we discuss the precise targeting and modulation of pathogenic signaling pathways using CRISPR-Cas9–mediated base editing, as exemplified by a recent study targeting protein kinase C alpha (PKCα) [15].

Targeting PKCα and beyond: Precision modulation of pathogenic nodes

PKCα is a central node of cardiac signaling [16–20]. Among the PKC family members, PKCα is the most abundantly expressed in the heart and is activated in HF [21–26]. PKCα is critically involved in cardiac contractility. Overexpression of PKCα impairs cardiac function, and genetic deletion of PKCα prevents cardiac dysfunction and pathological remodeling in mice with transverse aortic constriction (TAC)–induced HF and dilated cardiomyopathy [27]. In contrast to PKCα, PKCβ and PKCγ have been shown to play cardioprotective roles, suggesting that PKCα-specific modulation could represent a promising therapeutic approach for HF. However, the potential clinical utility of ruboxistaurin, a widely employed pan-PKC inhibitor, is limited by the need for prolonged administration [28,29].

We recently reported a therapeutic approach employing adenine base editing to target PKCα at threonine-497 (T497), which precisely inactivates and destabilizes PKCα [15]. PKCα has three phosphorylation sites, T497, T637, and S657 [30–32]. Phosphorylation of T497 by phosphoinositide-dependent kinase 1 (PDK1) triggers subsequent phosphorylation, thereby stabilizing the protein [33–35]. Conversely, if phosphorylation does not occur, PKCα is degraded [16]. Thus, we hypothesized that converting T497, encoded by an ACC codon, to alanine (A) 497, encoded by a GCC, using CRISPR-Cas9 adenine base editing, would render PKCα phospho-resistant, thereby providing cardioprotection against HF. Consistent with this notion, germline T497A knock-in mice, generated using CRISPR-Cas9 homology directed repair (HDR), exhibited resistance to cardiac dysfunction and adverse remodeling induced by TAC, with near complete loss of PKCα protein even after TAC. To test translational feasibility, we packaged T497A adenine base editing components into adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9) using a split-intein trans splicing system under control of the cTnT promoter and systemically delivered them to wild-type neonatal mice at a dose of 3.0 × 1014 vg/kg. Edited mice showed ~52% T497A editing at the cDNA level and were similarly resistant to TAC-induced HF, with PKCα protein levels remaining low after TAC.

To examine the translational applicability of our genome-editing strategy to the human genome, human induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells carrying the T497A mutation were differentiated into cardiomyocytes and subjected to chronic angiotensin II-induced stress. These cells recapitulated the reduced PKCα protein levels and showed improved contractile function and calcium handling compared with wild-type controls stimulated with vehicle. Together, these results demonstrate that ablation of PKCα phosphorylation at T497 using CRISPR-Cas9 adenine base editing effectively confers cardioprotection against HF and represents a promising mutation-independent therapeutic approach (Fig. 1) [15].

Base editing still faces specific technological hurdles, including unedited alleles, bystander editing, and low-frequency insertions or deletions (indels) [36]. Importantly, in our study, unintended indels or other loss-of-function events resulting from inefficient editing are unlikely to be toxic. PKCα knockout mice maintain normal basal cardiac function and show cardioprotective effects. However, our strategy is fundamentally distinct from the knockout approach. While knockout eliminates Prkca expression, the PrkcaT497A mutation permits normal transcription and translation, and causes post-translational loss-of-function that destabilizes the protein without disrupting the underlying genetic architecture. Additionally, to further mitigate any remaining risks of bystander mutations, engineered Cas9 variants are continuously being developed to enhance overall editing fidelity [36].

In cardiovascular disease, one of the most significant successes of in vivo base editing is the therapeutic targeting of proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) for dyslipidemia. PCSK9 is a secreted protein that promotes the degradation of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) receptors, thereby increasing circulating LDL cholesterol levels [37]. Loss-of-function mutations in PCSK9 are known to result in markedly reduced LDL levels and a lower risk of cardiovascular events in humans [38,39]. Based on this knowledge, researchers used base editors to introduce single-nucleotide substitutions in PCSK9, resulting in durable inactivation of gene expression in the liver [40–42]. This breakthrough has turned the concept of one-shot therapy for lifelong therapeutic efficacy into a reality, with several clinical trials currently underway [43–46]. This success also represents an important precedent for our strategy of directly targeting cardiomyocytes, demonstrating the safety and durability of base editing.

Among previous studies, the work by Lebek et al. targeting CaMK2D (CaMKIIδ) is conceptually similar to our PKCα-directed approach. CaMKIIδ is a kinase critically involved in the pathogenesis of arrhythmias, HF, and ischemia–reperfusion injury [47–49]. However, pharmacological inhibitors have faced challenges in achieving sufficient specificity [50]. Using base editing, they modified key oxidative and autophosphorylation regulatory sites required for CaMKIIδ activation, thereby selectively disrupting pathological kinase hyperactivation and preventing cardiac disease [51–53]. Preserving basal CaMKII activity is essential for normal cardiomyocyte physiology, including the maintenance of intracellular calcium homeostasis and basal cardiac signaling [54,55]. Therefore, selectively blocking pathological hyperactivation through a specific amino acid substitution, while maintaining physiological signaling, represents a more refined and safer therapeutic approach.

Taken together, the studies targeting PCSK9 and CaMKIIδ, along with our work on PKCα, highlight an emerging paradigm for CRISPR-based genome editing. Conventional CRISPR–Cas9 genome editing that relies on double-strand breaks (DSBs) carries the risk of generating unpredictable indels. In contrast, base editing enables precise single-base substitutions without inducing DSBs, resulting in lower off-target risk and improved safety compared with conventional CRISPR–Cas9 [36,56]. To further support the safety of base editing, advanced genome-wide unbiased detection methods and prediction algorithms have been developed to rigorously identify potential off-target editing events. In parallel, various strategies are being explored to confine editing temporally and spatially, including the use of cardio-specific promoters, small-molecule regulators, anti-CRISPR proteins, or inducible physical triggers [57–64].

Moreover, base editing has enabled two forms of precise control over protein function: (1) Targeted Inactivation: As exemplified by CaMKIIδ, specific activation switches can be ablated while preserving the protein itself, (2) Post-translational Regulation: As demonstrated in our PKCα study, editing phosphorylation sites can modulate protein stability, resulting in suppression of pathological signaling. Such “precision signaling modification” may represent a powerful therapeutic strategy for non-genetic HF, beyond simple gene correction.

Prime editing is another genome-editing strategy, which allows a broader range of nucleotide modifications [56,65]. Whereas base editing can introduce only specific nucleotide substitutions, such as A-to-G or C-to-T conversions, prime editing enables more versatile and precise genome modifications without such constraints [56,65–67]. Despite its versatility, the therapeutic application of prime editing remains less optimized compared with base editing, particularly in terms of in vivo efficiency [68,69]. Nevertheless, overcoming these limitations represents an important challenge for future development.

Looking to the future

Efficient delivery of base editors to adult mice using AAV9 remains a formidable challenge [15]. In our study, systemic AAV9 administration in neonatal mice achieved efficient delivery of the base editor, resulting in significant phenotypic improvement. However, in clinical settings, treatment is typically initiated after the onset of HF. When AAV9 was administered to adult mice at the same dose per body weight immediately after TAC surgery, the amount of AAV genomes detected in cardiac tissue was comparable between neonatal and adult mice. However, mRNA and protein expression levels of the base editor were significantly lower in adult mice than in neonatal mice. One possible explanation is inefficient nuclear entry of AAV in adult cardiomyocytes. In addition, maturation of the immune system in adult mice may impair AAV9-mediated transduction [70]. An additional challenge posed by AAV delivery is the inability to terminate expression of the gene editor, leaving possibilities for long-term off-target toxicity and adverse immune responses.

Therefore, the development of alternative delivery systems capable of achieving efficient and transient cardiac transduction after systemic administration remains an important challenge. Potential candidates include newly engineered cardiotropic capsids such as MyoAAV, lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), and virus-like particles (VLPs) [71–74]. These delivery systems offer potential advantages, including improved cardiac tropism or larger packaging capacity and the possibility of repeated dosing. However, each approach also has platform-specific limitations. For example, engineered AAV capsids such as MyoAAV still retain the limited packaging capacity inherent to AAV vectors, whereas non-viral delivery systems such as LNPs and VLPs often lack sufficient cardiac tropism and remain less validated in vivo [4,73–77]. Though AAVs and LNPs are used clinically, translating them to human cardiac disease poses challenges [78,79]. Sufficient efficacy in larger human volumes often requires higher doses, risking cardiotoxicity, systemic adverse effects, and severe immune responses [4]. Furthermore, in the context of HF, where patients are already in a fragile physiological state, the safety of administering exogenous proteins that may trigger immune responses must be carefully considered [70,80,81]. Another concern relates to the “one-shot” nature of genome editing. Although long-term adverse effects of base editing were not observed with respect to cardiac function in mice, it remains unclear whether constitutive suppression of PKCα or CaMKIIδ activity in non-pathological physiological conditions may have unintended consequences. Further studies will be necessary to address these issues.

Conclusion

Genome editing technologies, particularly base editing, are beginning to reshape the therapeutic landscape of HF. Beyond the conventional management of disease symptoms, these approaches offer the potential to directly and durably modify pathological signaling within cardiomyocytes.

As discussed in this review, while personalized correction of disease-causing mutations represents an important application, strategies aimed at precisely modulating signaling targets such as PKCα and CaMKIIδ may provide a broadly applicable therapeutic approach for diverse forms of HF.

Despite these promising advances, important challenges remain, including efficient delivery to the adult heart, long-term safety, and immune responses to gene editing components. Continued development of next-generation delivery platforms and genome editing technologies will be essential to overcome these barriers. Nevertheless, genome editing is rapidly emerging as a promising strategy for durable, potentially one-time therapeutic intervention in cardiovascular disease.
