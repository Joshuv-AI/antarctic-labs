Liposomal mitoxantrone plus tislelizumab in patients with relapsed or refractory extranodal natural killer/T-cell lymphoma: a phase 1b/2 trial
Authors: Qihua Zou, Shenrui Bai, Liang Wang, Hongyan Tong, Dongfeng Zeng, Ying Zhao, Yuerong Shuang, Xiaobo Wang, Xiuhua Sun, Zhenya Hong, Zhigang Peng, Yu Yang, Runhui Zheng, Huiqiang Huang, Jianbo Liu, Chunmei Yang, Xi Li, Xin Gao, Hao Xu, Daoguang Chen, Xiaojie Fang, Man Nie, Jinni Wang, Xinna Gao, Jingjing Ye, Wei Zhang, Jianzhen Shen, Rong Tao, Hong Cen, Yuchen Zhang, Jun Cai, Yi Xia, Qingqing Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Treatment options for relapsed or refractory extranodal natural killer/T-cell lymphoma (R/R ENKTL) remain limited. In this phase 1b/2 trial (NCT05464433), we evaluated the safety and efficacy of liposomal mitoxantrone (Lipo-MIT) plus anti-PD-1 tislelizumab in this setting. During dose escalation, patients received Lipo-MIT (16 or 20 mg/m2) plus tislelizumab using a 3 + 3 design, followed by dose expansion at the recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D). The primary endpoint of the dose-escalation phase was dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs), which were assessed to determine the RP2D. The primary efficacy endpoint of the dose-expansion phase was the best complete response (CR) rate. Between July 23 2022 and November 28 2024, 40 patients received study treatment (dose-escalation, n = 6; dose-expansion, n = 34). No DLTs were observed and the RP2D of Lipo-MIT was 20 mg/m2. The prespecified primary efficacy endpoint was met, with a CR rate of 53% (21/40; one-sided 95% lower confidence bound, 38%). The median progression-free survival (secondary endpoint) was 8.2 months (95% confidence interval, 6.1-not estimable) after a median follow-up of 15.3 months. The most common grade ≥ 3 adverse events were leukopenia (53%), neutropenia (40%), and febrile neutropenia (18%). In conclusion, Lipo-MIT plus tislelizumab showed promising anti-tumor activity with an acceptable safety profile in R/R ENKTL.

Introduction

Extranodal natural killer/T-cell lymphoma (ENKTL) is an aggressive subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma that predominantly involves the upper aerodigestive tract1–3. ENKTL is more common in the East Asian and Latin American population4,5. Asparaginase or pegaspargase-based chemotherapy is the backbone of treatment for newly diagnosed ENKTL patients6–8, but ~40% of patients relapse within 5 years9. For patients who fail standard first-line therapy, there is no established salvage treatment strategy.

ENKTL is strongly associated with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection; the EBV-driven latent membrane protein 1 upregulates programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression on ENKTL cells, providing a mechanistic basis for programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/PD-L1 blockade in ENKTL10,11. Based on their efficacy in patients with relapsed or refractory (R/R) ENKTL, anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies are now recommended as a treatment option in the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) guidelines for patients with R/R ENKTL12,13. However, the complete response (CR) rate of single agent anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies was only 18% to 36%14–17. Given the relatively manageable toxicity profile of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies, the combination strategies are worth further exploration.

Liposomal mitoxantrone (Lipo-MIT) is a nano-liposomal formulation of mitoxantrone designed to reduce cardiotoxicity associated with the conventional mitoxantrone18. In a phase 2 trial, Lipo-MIT monotherapy achieved an objective response rate (ORR) of 52% and a CR rate of 33% in R/R ENKTL19. Tislelizumab is a fragment crystallizable region-engineered humanized anti-PD-1 antibody, and achieved an ORR of 32% and a CR rate of 18% in patients with R/R ENKTL in a phase 2 trial17. Furthermore, in preclinical studies, Lipo-MIT induces immunogenic cell death, thereby potentiating the anti-tumor activity of anti-PD-1 antibodies20,21, suggesting that their combination may provide potential synergistic therapeutic efficacy.

Here, we report the results of a multicenter phase 1b/2 trial evaluating the safety and efficacy of Lipo-MIT combined with tislelizumab in patients with R/R ENKTL. This regimen yields promising anti-tumor activity with an acceptable safety profile.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between July 23, 2022 and November 28, 2024, 45 patients with R/R ENKTL were screened. Forty-two patients met the eligibility criteria and were enrolled (Fig. 1). Two patients did not receive the study treatment (one withdrew consent due to rapid disease progression, and one developed uncontrolled hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis). The remaining 40 patients received induction therapy with Lipo-MIT plus tislelizumab. Demographics and baseline characteristics of the 40 patients are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 48 years (range 22-73). Twenty-one (53%) were male, 25 (63%) had stage III-IV disease, and 23 (58%) had refractory disease. Most patients had received P-GemOx-based regimens (Supplementary Table 1). The median number of prior systemic therapies was 1 (range 1–4). Treatment

In the dose-escalation phase, 6 patients received escalating doses of Lipo-MIT (16 or 20 mg/m2) in combination with a fixed dose of tislelizumab (200 mg). No dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) were observed (primary endpoint of the dose-escalation phase). The maximum tolerated dose (MTD) was not reached. The recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D) of Lipo-MIT was determined as 20 mg/m2 when given in combination with tislelizumab, following a discussion based on the cumulative safety data and prior clinical evidence. In the dose-expansion phase, 34 patients were treated, resulting in a total of 40 patients comprised the full analysis set (FAS).

The median number of induction therapy cycles was 6 (interquartile range [IQR] 2-6). Twenty-three (58%) patients completed all 6 planned induction cycles, whereas 17 discontinued induction therapy due to disease progression (n = 11), loss to follow-up (n = 2), withdrawal of consent (n = 2), or investigator decision (n = 2). Among the 23 patients who completed induction therapy, 18 proceeded to tislelizumab maintenance therapy and 5 did not undergo maintenance because of disease progression at the end of induction. Of the 18 patients who received maintenance therapy, 13 completed the planned maintenance course, 1 remained on treatment at data cut-off, and 4 discontinued maintenance therapy due to disease progression (n = 3) or patient refusal (n = 1) (Fig. 1).

Efficacy

For the primary efficacy endpoint, the best CR rate was 53% (21/40; one-sided 95% lower confidence bound, 38%; p < 0.001) in the FAS (n = 40). The best ORR was 73% (29/40; 95% CI 56%-85%) (Table 2). At the end of induction therapy, 16 (40%) and 21 (53%) patients achieved CR and objective response, respectively. One patient converted from partial to CR during maintenance therapy. The median time to response was 1.9 (95% CI 1.8–2.0) months, and the median time to CR was 3.8 (95% CI 3.2–5.8) months. The treatment response dynamics and individual patient trajectories are illustrated in the swimmer plot (Fig. 2). Given that two patients were not evaluable because they lost to follow-up before first post-treatment radiological assessment, the per-protocol set (PPS) contained 38 evaluable patients. The CR rate was 55% (21/38; one-sided 95% lower confidence bound, 41%) and the ORR was 76% (29/38; 95% CI 60%-89%) in the PPS (Supplementary Table 2). In the post-hoc subgroup analysis, a lower CR rate was observed in patients aged > 60 years (0% [0/7] vs 64% [21/33] for ≤ 60 years; p = 0.003). The CR rate also differed across PINK risk categories (low- vs intermediate- vs high-risk: 75% [3/4] vs 68% [13/19] vs 29% [5/17]; p = 0.048). Patients with prior exposure to anti-PD-1 antibodies tended to have a lower CR rate than those without (38% [8/21] vs 68% [13/19]; p = 0.055; Supplementary Fig. 1). Survival

As of the data cut-off date of August 31, 2025, the median follow-up was 15.3 (IQR 8.7-21.3) months, and 22 (55%) of 40 patients experienced disease progression. The median duration of response (DOR) and disease-free survival (DFS) were 22.2 (95% CI 6.1-not estimable) months and 26.7 (95% CI 11.3-not estimable) months, respectively (Fig. 3). The median progression-free survival (PFS) was 8.2 (95% CI 6.1-not estimable) months, and the median overall survival (OS) was not reached. The estimated 6-month and 12-month PFS rates were 66% (95% CI 53%-83%) and 50% (95% CI 36%-69%), respectively (Fig. 3). The estimated 6-month and 12-month OS rates were 92% (95% CI 85%-100%) and 84% (95% CI 73%-97%), respectively (Fig. 3). Among 22 patients who experienced disease progression, 18 received subsequent therapies, including immunotherapy-based regimens (n = 10), other chemotherapy regimens (n = 6), and small molecule targeted therapies (n = 2). The specific subsequent regimens are listed in Supplementary Table 3. Safety

All treated patients experienced at least one adverse event (AE) (Table 3). The most common (≥ 20%) any grade AEs were leukopenia (30 [75%] of 40 patients), neutropenia (28 [70%]), anemia (26 [65%]), pigmentation (18 [45%]), hypothyroidism (14 [35%]), albumin decreased (12 [30%]), thrombocytopenia (10 [25%]), fever (10 [25%]), aspartate aminotransferase elevated (10 [25%]), infection (9 [23%]) and alanine aminotransferase elevated (8 [20%]). The grade 3-4 AEs included leukopenia (21 [53%]), neutropenia (16 [40%]), febrile neutropenia (7 [18%]), infection (3 [8%]), anemia (2 [5%]), thrombocytopenia (2 [5%]), alanine aminotransferase elevated (2 [5%]). AEs in phase 1b and phase 2 are shown in Supplementary Table 4 and 5. No patients received primary granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) prophylaxis. Secondary G-CSF was administered in 6 patients following febrile neutropenia; the remaining 1 patient who developed febrile neutropenia did not receive G-CSF due to withdrawal of consent before further treatment. Treatment-related cardiotoxicity was observed in 4 patients (10%), all of which were grade 1-2. These cardiotoxicities included palpitations (2 [5%]), atrial fibrillation (1 [3%]), and QT interval prolonged (1 [3%]). No grade 3 or higher cardiac AEs were reported. Post-hoc exploratory analysis of multiplex immunohistochemistry

Multiplex immunohistochemistry was performed on baseline tumor specimens from 25 patients. Among these patients, the median proportion of CD8+ T cells was 10.0% (IQR 5.9%-15.6%), with a median density of 821 (IQR 246-1267) cells/mm2. The median proportion of PD-1+CD8+ T cells was 0.5% (IQR 0.1%-1.2%). The median proportions of PD-L1+CD56+ cells and PD-1+CD56+ cells were 1.4% (IQR 0.4%-2.2%) and 0.1% (IQR 0.1%-0.5%), respectively. The overall PD-L1+ cell proportion was 9.0% (IQR 4.2%-16.8%). Detailed median values and IQRs for all markers, including both proportion and density, in these 25 patients and stratified by response and progression status, are shown in Supplementary Table 9.

Patients who achieved CR had a median CD8+ T cell proportion of 12.8% (IQR 9.9%-19.4%) and a median density of 1072 (IQR 816-1409) cells/mm2, whereas those who did not achieve CR had a median proportion of 7.3% (IQR 2.2%–10.6%) and a median density of 454 (IQR 227–821) cells/mm2 (Supplementary Table 9). The level of PD-1+CD8+ T cell infiltration did not differ between patients with and without CR (Supplementary Fig. 2). Additionally, patients without disease progression had a median CD8+ T cell proportion of 16.3% (IQR 13.1%–19.6%) and a median density of 1320 (IQR 986–1571) cells/mm2; among those with disease progression, the corresponding values were 8.7% (IQR 3.3%–11.6%) and 619 (235–960) cells/mm2 (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this single-arm, phase 1b/2 trial, we determined the RP2D of Lipo-MIT when given in combination with tislelizumab in patients with R/R ENKTL in the dose-escalation phase, and further showed promising anti-tumor activity and an acceptable safety profile of this regimen in the dose-expansion phase.

The treatment options for R/R ENKTL patients who failed first-line asparaginase-based therapies are limited. Recently, several new drugs have shown preliminary anti-tumor activity for this group of patients, which mainly included immunotherapies14–17, histone deacetylase inhibitors22,23, anti-CD38 antibodies24,25, and DNA methyltransferase inhibitors26. Compared to the CR rates of previously reported single-agent anti-PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy (18% to 36%)14–17, Lipo-MIT (33%)19, chidamide (6%)22, and daratumumab (0%)24, the Lipo-MIT plus tislelizumab regimen in the current trial showed a numerically higher CR rate of 53%. A meta-analysis reported a pooled ORR of 47% and a 12-month PFS rate of 30% with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy in this setting27. In the current trial, the ORR and 12-month PFS rate were 73% and 50%, respectively. In addition, a small retrospective study of 6 patients with R/R ENKTL reported a 67% ORR with tislelizumab in combination with MIT-based regimens, including MIT plus pegaspargase and dexamethasone (n = 3), lipo-MIT (n = 1), lipo-MIT plus chidamide (n = 1), and lipo-MIT plus brentuximab vedotin (n = 1), suggesting potential anti-tumor activity of this combination28. These findings suggest that combination strategies may enhance anti-tumor activity and survival.

The efficacy of the study treatment was comparable to that reported in recent phase 2 trials of immunotherapy-based combinations, including sintilimab plus chidamide (CR rate 49%)23, cemiplimab plus isatuximab (51%)25, and tislelizumab plus gemcitabine/oxaliplatin (56%)29. Our cohort had a numerically higher proportion (53%) of patients who were refractory to or had relapsed after prior anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies therapy than those reported in studies of sintilimab plus chidamide (16%) or tislelizumab plus gemcitabine/oxaliplatin (3%), representing a population with poorer prognosis23,29. The clinical outcomes of R/R ENKTL patients who failed immunotherapy are dismal26,30. In the current trial, patients previously exposed to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies had numerically lower CR rate and ORR than in anti-PD-1/PD-L1-naïve patients (38% vs 68%; 62% vs 84%). Nevertheless, ~two-thirds of patients could still benefit from the addition of Lipo-MIT, and the median PFS reached 7.0 months. A similar finding was observed with sintilimab plus chidamide, for which CR rate and ORR were also numerically lower in anti-PD-1 treatment-exposed group than in anti-PD-1 treatment-naive group (33% vs 52%; 50% vs 61%)23. Among patients with R/R ENKTL previously exposed to anti-PD-l therapy, sintilimab plus DNA methyltransferase inhibitors achieved a median PFS of only 4.4 months26. The potential mechanisms involving the immune-escape phenotypes under immune-checkpoint blockade pressure included loss of antigen presentation through 2-microglobulin or HLA class I deficiency, disruption of IFN-γ/JAK-STAT signaling, and terminal T-cell exhaustion31,32. In contrast, the synergistic effect of Lipo-MIT and anti-PD-1 antibody may be partly explained by the increase of immunogenic cell death induced by Lipo-MIT20,21,33.

In subgroup analyses, patients aged > 60 years had a lower CR rate than those aged ≤ 60 years. However, dose modification (dose reduction or treatment interruption) was not more frequent in older patients (1/7 [14%] vs 14/33 [42%]; p = 0.224). Instead, most older patients discontinued treatment early, with only one patient completing all planned induction cycles (Supplementary Table 10). Given the high proportion of elderly patients who discontinued induction therapy, the association between age and treatment efficacy should be interpreted with caution. In addition, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) was not associated with dose modification (0: 6/15 [40%] vs 1: 9/25 [36%]; p = 0.800) or with CR rate (60% vs 48%; p = 0.462). A numerically higher rate of dose modification was observed in patients with prior ASCT (60% [3/5]) compared with those without (34% [12/35]), although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.345).

In this trial, no new safety signals were observed with the combination of Lipo-MIT and tislelizumab compared with previous trials of anti-PD-1 antibodies or Lipo-MIT. The rate of grade 3-4 neutropenia (40%) was numerically higher than that reported with single agent anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies (0-14%), which is expected given the addition of a cytotoxic agent to immunotherapy14–17. When compared with single-agent Lipo-MIT in R/R ENKTL, rates of grade 3-4 neutropenia (40% vs 41%), and thrombocytopenia (5% vs 12%) in the current trial were comparable19. Febrile neutropenia occurred in 7 patients (18%) and was generally manageable with supportive care, including G-CSF. Although this event is typically considered a DLT in the majority of phase 1 trials, in this trial, the 2 cases in phase 1b occurred outside the DLT observation period (cycle 1), and therefore did not meet the protocol-defined criteria for DLT.

Immune-related hypothyroidism occurred in 35% of patients, similar to the rate reported in the ORIENT-4 study (32%)15, but numerically higher than in other anti-PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy trials16,17. This difference may be attributable to the inclusion of chemotherapy, which itself can induce thyroid dysfunction and therefore increase the incidence of hypothyroidism with combination treatment34. Lipo-MIT-related skin pigmentation (35%) and cardiac AEs (10%) in the current trial were limited to grade 1-2, consistent with the rates reported for Lipo-MIT monotherapy (34% and 14%, respectively)19. In addition, the rates of neutropenia (70%), hypothyroidism (35%), elevated alanine aminotransferase (20%) in the present trial were comparable to those reported for other anti-PD-1-based combination regimens (44%–66%, 18%–25%, and 19%–26%, respectively)23,29. All AEs were manageable, and no treatment-related deaths occurred in the current trial.

The results of multiplex immunohistochemistry indicated an immune-active tumor microenvironment, which was characterized by enrichment of effector CD8+ T cells as well as higher PD-L1 and PD-1 expression in malignant CD56-positive natural killer cells, in patients achieving CR. These findings are consistent with an independent cohort from our previous trial of anti-PD-1-based therapy in ENKTL35, in which patients who achieved CR and did not have disease progression tended to have higher baseline densities of CD8+ T cells, whereas baseline PD-L1 combined positive score (reflecting PD-L1 expression on both tumor and immune cells) was not associated with either response or survival outcomes. Also, several previous literatures showed that PD-L1 expression alone had limited predictive value for immunotherapy36,37, which might be owing to the lack of cellular and spatial resolution to capture the diversity in different types of cells. In the current trial, PD-L1 expression was not associated with efficacy (p > 0.05), consistent with the results of Tian et al. and Huang et al.26,35. However, patients with CR tended to have higher PD-L1 expression in malignant CD56-positive natural killer cells, suggesting that PD-L1 expression within the tumor compartment may serve as a potential biomarker. It should be noted that while CD56 identifies the malignant population in ENKTL, CD56+ cells may also encompass non-malignant natural killer cells38. This underscores the need for more refined approaches to accurately characterize the tumor microenvironment and cellular heterogeneity. Post-progression biopsy samples are available in 4 patients, which could be used for future integrated multi-omic analyses, including genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, single-cell, and spatial approaches, to further elucidate mechanisms of resistance and inform rational combination strategies.

This trial has several limitations. First, the open-label, single-arm design may introduce potential bias. The efficacy and safety of the study treatment warrant validation in a large-scale phase 3 trial. Second, the post-hoc analyses of CR were conducted in a relatively small cohort and were not prespecified for confirmatory hypothesis testing. The limited number of patients and events may have resulted in imprecise estimates and increased susceptibility to bias across clinical subgroups. Third, the findings of multiplex immunohistochemistry were preliminary and were limited by small sample size, lack of paired pre- and post-treatment analyses, and restricted cellular resolution. Validation using longitudinal sampling and multi-omic profiling is warranted to better elucidate immune mechanisms underlying treatment response and inform combination strategies.

In summary, the trial demonstrated promising efficacy and survival outcomes with no unexpected toxicities, suggesting that Lipo-MIT plus tislelizumab regimen might be a potential therapeutic option for patients with R/R ENKTL.

Methods

Trial design and participants

This multicenter, single-arm, phase 1b/2 trial was approved by the institutional ethics committees of all participating centers (Ethics committee of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center; Ethics committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University; Ethics committee of The First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine; Ethics committee of Army Medical University Daping Hospital; Ethics committee of The First People’s Hospital of Foshan; Ethics committee of Jiangxi Cancer Hospital; Ethics committee of The Second Hospital of Dalian Medical University; Ethics committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology; Ethics committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University; Ethics committee of Fujian Cancer Hospital; Ethics committee of The Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University; Ethics committee of Qilu Hospital of Shandong University; Ethics committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital; Ethics committee of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital; Ethics committee of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center; and Ethics committee of Guangxi Medical University Cancer Hospital). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT05464433 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05464433); first posted on July 19, 2022. The first patient was enrolled on July 23, 2022, and the last patient was enrolled on November 28, 2024.

The trial was conducted at 16 centers across China (Supplementary Note 1). Patients aged 18–75 years with histologically confirmed ENKTL, R/R after at least one prior asparaginase-based therapy, were eligible. Refractory disease was defined as failure to achieve a CR to asparaginase-based chemotherapy or disease progression within 6 months after the last asparaginase-containing regimen. Relapsed disease was defined as disease recurrence after achieving a CR to initial therapy and occurring beyond 6 months of the last regimen. Additional eligibility requirements included an ECOG PS between 0 and 2; adequate liver, renal, and bone-marrow function; a life expectancy of at least 3 months; and at least one measurable lesion according to the Lugano 2014 criteria39. Key exclusion criteria included previous exposure to non-liposomal mitoxantrone at a cumulative dose of > 160 mg/m2, a cumulative doxorubicin dose of > 360 mg/m2, a diagnosis of aggressive natural killer cell leukemia, central nervous system involvement, and pregnancy or lactation. Full eligibility and exclusion criteria are described in the trial protocol (Supplementary Note 2).

Procedures

All patients received induction therapy with intravenous Lipo-MIT plus tislelizumab on day 1 in 28-day cycles for 6 planned cycles. Patients who achieved CR, partial response (PR), or stable disease (SD) after induction therapy proceeded to maintenance therapy with tislelizumab in 21-day cycles until disease progression or unacceptable toxicities, with a maximum total treatment duration of 12 months.

This trial consisted of a dose-escalation and a dose-expansion phase. The dose-escalation phase followed a standard 3 + 3 design for two Lipo-MIT dose levels (16 and 20 mg/m2). In dose-expansion phase, patients received Lipo-MIT at the RP2D. Tislelizumab was given at a fixed dose of 200 mg in all phases.

Dose for Lipo-MIT was adjusted according to the severity of AEs with the planned dose level of 20, 16, and 12 mg/m2. One-level reduction was permitted for grade 4 hematologic, or grade ≥3 non-hematologic toxicities at first occurrence, or grade 3 hematologic toxicities at second occurrence. Lipo-MIT was permitted to be reduced by no more than two dose levels, except for grade 4 non-hematologic toxicities at first occurrence, for which only one reduction was permitted. Dose modification for tislelizumab was not permitted. Specific dose adjustment, interruption and discontinuation criteria are shown in the trial protocol (protocol pp 32–36).

Tumor response was assessed by the investigator according to the Lugano 2014 criteria using positron emission tomography-computed tomography, computed tomography, or magnetic resonance imaging39. Assessments were performed every two cycles during the induction therapy, and every three months during the maintenance therapy. Safety was assessed by vital signs, patient-reported symptoms, physical examinations, laboratory tests, and clinical investigations. AEs were assessed throughout the treatment period and graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0. Follow-up was planned every 3 months during the first 2 years, every 6 months during years 3 to 5, and yearly thereafter. Disease stage was determined using the Ann Arbor staging system based on imaging at trial enrollment. Circulating EBV DNA copy number was measured at baseline by the real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction method. EBV DNA > 0 copies/mL was considered detectable.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry

Multiplex immunohistochemistry was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor samples obtained from available patients before enrollment. FFPE tissue sections (4 μm) were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer at sub-boiling temperature for 20 min. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min at room temperature, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking solution. The following day, slides were treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by fluorophore deposition using tyramide signal amplification (TSA). Between staining cycles, heat-mediated epitope retrieval was applied to remove bound antibodies while preserving the TSA signal. This sequential process of primary antibody incubation, HRP-based amplification, TSA development, and antigen retrieval was repeated until all markers were detected. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Multispectral images were acquired using the Vectra Polaris Automated Quantitative Pathology Imaging System (Akoya Biosciences). The primary antibodies used were anti-CD56 (CST, #3576, 1:200), anti-PD-1 (Proteintech, #66220-1-Ig, 1:6000), anti-PD-L1 (CST, #13684, 1:400), and anti-CD8 (CST, #70306, 1:400). The HRP-conjugated anti-mouse and rabbit secondary antibody (Panovue, #10013001050) was a pre-diluted working solution (1x) and was applied directly without further dilution. Tissue samples were consumed during the analysis and are no longer available.

Whole-slide imaging was conducted using the KF-FL-005 scanner (KFBIO, Ningbo, China). Image analysis was performed using Panoscore software (KFBIO, Ningbo, China) with automated cell segmentation and marker quantification. Analyses were performed across the entire scanned area (whole slide). Cell proportion was defined as the percentage of marker-positive cells relative to total nucleated cells within the analyzed area. Immune cell density was defined as the number of marker-positive cells per mm2.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint of the dose-escalation phase was DLTs, which in turn were assessed to determine the RP2D of Lipo-MIT when given in combination with tislelizumab. DLTs were defined as grade 3–4 non-hematological AEs (with protocol-defined exceptions); grade ≥ 3 febrile neutropenia not recovering to baseline within 7 days, grade 3 thrombocytopenia associated with bleeding tendency or requiring platelet transfusion; grade 4 neutropenia not recovering to baseline within 7 days; grade 4 thrombocytopenia or anemia within cycle 1.

The primary efficacy endpoint was the best CR rate. Secondary endpoints included best ORR (CR and PR), best disease control rate (CR, PR, and SD), PFS (from treatment initiation to first documented disease progression or death, whichever occurred first), OS (from treatment initiation to death from any cause), DFS (from first CR to first documented disease progression or death, whichever occurred first), DOR (from first response to first documented disease progression or death, whichever occurred first), and safety. Best response was defined as the best response achieved at any time during treatment according to the Lugano 2014 criteria. Exploratory post-hoc analyses were conducted to identify associations of treatment response with clinical variables and with immune cell components in the tumor microenvironment.

Statistical analysis

Dose-escalation phase followed the standard 3 + 3 design to enroll 6-12 patients. Sample size requirement in the dose-expansion phase was estimated based on the following assumptions: 1) CR rate of 28% for Lipo-MIT monotherapy40, and estimated target CR rate of 50% for Lipo-MIT plus tislelizumab; 2) one-sided α of 0.05 and 80% power; 3) 10% dropout rate. The calculation yielded 36 patients. The primary efficacy endpoint was clarified from ORR to best CR rate in a protocol amendment dated February 10, 2025, after completion of enrollment but before database lock and any efficacy analyses; the sample size assumptions and planned statistical test were based on CR rate and remained unchanged throughout the study.

The FAS comprised all eligible patients who received at least one dose of study treatment. The PPS included the patients who completed at least two treatment cycles without major protocol deviations. The safety analysis set (SAS) included all patients who received at least one dose of study treatment and had at least one post-treatment safety assessment. Efficacy analyses were conducted in the FAS and PPS, and safety analyses in the SAS.

Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as medians with IQRs. The CR rate was estimated with one-sided 95% CI using the Clopper-Pearson exact method. A one-sided exact binomial test was used to compare the observed CR rate with prespecified historical control rate of 0.28. Secondary efficacy endpoints were reported with two-sided 95% CIs. PFS, OS, DFS, and DOR were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Univariable Cox regression analysis was used to estimate HRs with corresponding 95% CIs for survival outcomes. Subgroup analyses were post-hoc and for signal generation only. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using Mann-Whitney U test. Two-sided tests were used, and nominal p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0 and GraphPad Prism 8.
