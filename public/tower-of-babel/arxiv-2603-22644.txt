Overfitting and Generalizing with (PAC) Bayesian Prediction in Noisy Binary Classification
Xiaohan Zhu, Mesrob I. Ohannessian, Nathan Srebro

Abstract
We consider a PAC-Bayes type learning rule for binary classification, balancing the training error of a randomized “posterior” predictor with its KL divergence to a pre-specified “prior”. This can be seen as an extension of a modified two-part-code Minimum Description Length (MDL) learning rule, to continuous priors and randomized predictions. With a balancing parameter of λ = 1 \lambda=1 this learning rule recovers an (empirical) Bayes posterior and a modified variant recovers the profile posterior, linking with standard Bayesian prediction (up to the treatment of the single-parameter noise level). However, from a risk-minimization prediction perspective, this Bayesian predictor overfits and can lead to non-vanishing excess loss in the agnostic case. Instead a choice of λ ≫ 1 \lambda\gg 1 , which can be seen as using a sample-size-dependent-prior, ensures uniformly vanishing excess loss even in the agnostic case. We precisely characterize the effect of under-regularizing (and over-regularizing) as a function of the balance parameter λ \lambda , understanding the regimes in which this under-regularization is tempered or catastrophic. This work extends previous work by 22 that considered only discrete priors to PAC Bayes type learning rules and, through their rigorous Bayesian interpretation, to Bayesian prediction more generally.

Consider the problem of supervised binary classification, where we seek to learn predictors

(a)

Formal Setup

The framework of the paper is the PAC-Bayesian perspective to supervised binary classification with continuous hypothesis classes. We observe
m
i.i.d samples
S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}
from a source distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
(X,Y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}
, where
\mathcal{X}
is some measurable space and
Y
is a binary label. Predictors (or hypotheses or classifiers) are maps
h:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
. Their performance is evaluated using the population error
L_{\mathcal{D}}(h)=\mathbb{P}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}(h(x)\neq y)
. To reduce clutter, we may sometimes omit the subscript
\mathcal{D}
. The observation-derived proxy for this is the training (or empirical) error
L_{S}(h)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}\{h(x_{i})\neq y_{i}\}
. In PAC-Bayes, instead of handling individual predictors, we consider distributions
Q
over predictors. The population and empirical errors are then understood as the respective expectations over a predictor drawn from
Q
, i.e., respectively
L_{\mathcal{D}}(Q)=\mathbb{E}_{h\sim Q}[L_{\mathcal{D}}(h)]
and
L_{S}(Q)=\mathbb{E}_{h\sim Q}[L_{S}(h)]
.

Given a (
prior
) distribution
\Pi
over the space of predictors, a learning rule
\hat{Q}
is then a sequence of maps
S\mapsto\hat{Q}(S)
, from

(\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\})^{m}
to (
posterior
) distributions over predictors. We are primarily interested in
\hat{Q}
that produce posteriors that are absolutely continuous with respect to
\Pi
and we measure the “complexity” of any produced
Q
via
\textsf{KL}(Q\|\Pi)
. For a given prior distribution
\Pi
, we consider the PAC-Bayes learning rule:

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{\lambda}(S)=\underset{Q}{\text{arg min }}J_{\lambda}(Q,S),\text{such that }L_{S}(Q)\leq 1/2,

(3)

where

\displaystyle J_{\lambda}(Q,S)=mH(L_{S}(Q))+\lambda\textsf{KL}(Q\|\Pi).

(4)

We require
L_{S}(Q)\leq 1/2
so that
H(L_{S}(Q))
is monotone in
L_{S}(Q)
—otherwise we might be tempted to choose a predictor with loss close to 1. For priors
\Pi
that are symmetric with respect to negating the predictions (roughly speaking, when
\Pi(h)=\Pi(\neg h)
), this can also be interpreted as post-learning negation where necessary.

To quantify inconsistency of a learning rule
\hat{Q}
, we measure it relative to an
L^{*}
achievable by a bounded-complexity posterior
Q^{*}
. We can think of
Q^{*}
as a competitor or oracle. Without loss of generality,
since our results do not depend on the level of boundedness, we require that
\textsf{KL}(Q^{*}\|\Pi)\leq 10
. More precisely, when we say “for a given
L^{\star}
”, we imply that the
instance

(\Pi,\mathcal{D})
belongs to:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(L^{*})=\left\{~(\Pi,\mathcal{D})~:\right.\left.\exists Q^{*}~:~L_{\mathcal{D}}(Q^{*})=L^{*},\ \textsf{KL}(Q^{*}\|\Pi)\leq 10~\right\}.

(5)

This perspective is particularly attuned to the lower bounds. Our upper bounds, on the other hand, apply regardless and imply (possibly tempered) learning for any bounded
\textsf{KL}(Q^{*}\|\Pi)
.

We can now precisely define just how inconsistent a learning rule can get when
L^{*}
is achievable, through its worst-case limiting performance. We can either bound the error uniformly across instances (
\overline{L}_{\infty}
) and then take the limit or we could take the limit per-instance and then bound it across instances (
\underline{L}_{\infty}
).

Definition 2.1

(Worst-case (uniform) limiting error)
.

For any
0<L^{*}<\frac{1}{2}
, regularization parameter
\lambda
, define the worst-case uniform limiting error of a learning rule
\hat{Q}_{\lambda}(S)
:

\overline{L}_{\infty}(\lambda,L^{*})=\underset{m\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}(\Pi,\mathcal{D})\in\mathcal{M}(L^{*})\end{subarray}}{\sup}\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[L_{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{Q}_{\lambda}(S))\right]

Definition 2.2

(Worst-case (per-instance) limiting error)
.

For any
0<L^{*}<\frac{1}{2}
, regularization parameter
\lambda
, define the worst-case per-instance limiting error of a learning rule
\hat{Q}_{\lambda}(S)
:

\underline{L}_{\infty}(\lambda,L^{*})=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}(\Pi,\mathcal{D})\in\mathcal{M}(L^{*})\end{subarray}}{\sup}\underset{m\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[L_{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{Q}_{\lambda}(S))\right]

3
Unifying MDL and PAC-Bayes Limiting Errors

We now unify the limiting errors of the PAC-Bayes learning rule (
2
) and its MDL counterpart, by closely paralleling the results of
22
. In particular, the upper bounds follow using similar arguments as in the latter, with subtle changes. For the lower bounds, the key change is that we need to show that the posterior concentrates on the desired subset of hypotheses. For that, we exploit the concavity of the entropy.
4
4

4

All replication of technical language is with permission. Key differences are highlighted in the proofs in the appendix.

First, we recall the following function
\ell_{\lambda}
for
0<\lambda<\infty
, introduced in
22
:

\displaystyle\ell_{\lambda}(L^{*})=\begin{cases}1-2^{-\frac{1}{\lambda}H(L^{*})},&\text{for }0<\lambda\leq 1\\
U_{\lambda}^{-1}(H(L^{*})),&\text{for }\lambda>1,\end{cases}

(6)

\displaystyle\textrm{where: }U_{\lambda}(q)=\lambda\textsf{KL}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{1+\left(\frac{\scriptscriptstyle 1-q}{\scriptscriptstyle q}\right)^{\frac{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda-1}}}\middle\|q\right)+\ H\!\left(\tfrac{1}{1+\left(\frac{\scriptscriptstyle 1-q}{\scriptscriptstyle q}\right)^{\frac{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda}{\scriptscriptstyle\lambda-1}}}\right).

(7)

We show that, just as in MDL, this function captures the extent of potential inconsistency for each
L^{*}
and each choice of
\lambda
, as we elaborate shortly. The precise statements behind this claim are as follows.

Theorem 3.1

(Agnostic Upper Bound)
.

(1) For any
0<\lambda\leq 1
, any source distribution
\mathcal{D}
, any distribution
Q^{*}
, any valid prior distribution
\Pi
, and any
m
:

\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}[L_{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{Q}_{\lambda}(S))]\leq 1-2^{-\frac{1}{\lambda}H(L_{\mathcal{D}}(Q^{*}))}+O\left(\frac{\textsf{KL}(Q^{*}\|\Pi)}{m}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{\log^{3}(m)}{m}}\right).

(8)

(2)
For any
\lambda>1
, any source distribution
\mathcal{D}
, any distribution
Q^{*}
, any valid prior distribution
\Pi
, and any
m
:

\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}[L_{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{Q}_{\lambda}(S))]\leq U_{\lambda}^{-1}\left(H(L_{\mathcal{D}}(Q^{*}))\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{(1-2L_{\mathcal{D}}(Q^{*}))^{2}}\cdot\left[\lambda\left(\frac{\textsf{KL}(Q^{*}\|\Pi)+\log m}{m}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{\log^{3}(m)}{m}}\right]\right).

(9)

Where
O(\cdot)
only hides an absolute constant, that does not depend on
\mathcal{D},\Pi
, or anything else.

While the above finite-sample bounds show that the limiting error will not exceed
\ell_{\lambda}(L^{*})
, we now show that there are worst-case instances that are arbitrarily close to
\ell_{\lambda}(L^{*})
, thus establishing a matching lower bound.

Theorem 3.2

(Agnostic Lower Bound)
.

For any
0<\lambda<\infty
, any
L^{*}\in(0,0.5)
and
L^{*}\leq L^{\prime}<\ell_{\lambda}(L^{*})
,
there exists
(\Pi,\mathcal{D})\in\mathcal{M}(L^{*})
such that

\mathbb{E}_{S}\left[L_{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{Q}_{\lambda}(S))\right]\rightarrow L^{\prime}
as sample size
m\rightarrow\infty
.

As an immediate consequence of
Theorem 3.1
(with
L_{\mathcal{D}}(Q^{*})=L^{*}
) and
Theorem 3.2
, we deduce that just as in MDL, the worst case limiting error is fully given by
\ell_{\lambda}(L^{*})
. Namely,
for any
0<\lambda<\infty
and any
L^{*}\in(0,0.5)
, we have

\underline{L}_{\infty}(\lambda,L^{*})=\ell_{\lambda}(L^{*}).

Moreover, observe that the finite-sample guarantee of
Theorem 3.1
has a rate of convergence that depends only on
\textsf{KL}(Q^{*}\|\Pi)
,
\lambda
, and
L^{*}
but not on
\Pi
and
\mathcal{D}
. (As with MDL, the dependence on
L^{*}
is only relevant as
L^{*}
approaches
1/2
and can be otherwise ignored.) This implies that the convergence is “uniform” and the order of the limits can be interchanged:

Corollary 3.2.1
.

For any
0<\lambda<\infty
, and any
L^{*}\in(0,0.5)
,

\begin{split}\ell_{\lambda}(L^{*})\leq\underline{L}_{\infty}(\lambda,L^{*})\leq\overline{L}_{\infty}(\lambda,L^{*})\leq\ell_{\lambda}(L^{*}).\end{split}

and so the inequality is actually an equality.

The results thus far characterize the behavior of the error for any
fixed

0<\lambda<\infty
(i.e. not varying with
m
). See
Figure

1
for an illustration. Two key conclusions from MDL thus extend to PAC-Bayes: if
\lambda\geq 1
, the limiting error is always less than
\frac{1}{2}
and the rule can be interpreted to experience
tempered overfitting
. If
\lambda<1
, this is only true when
L^{*}<H^{-1}(\lambda)
, otherwise the limiting error could exceed
\frac{1}{2}
. In fact, we notice that as
\lambda
approaches
0
(too little regularization),
\ell_{\lambda}(L^{*})
approaches
1
(largest error possible), hinting at
catastrophic overfitting
. Can this fate be avoided if we allow
\lambda_{m}
to decay to zero with
m
? The following result shows that we cannot, from a worst-case perspective.

Theorem 3.3
.

For any
\lambda_{m}\rightarrow 0
or
\lambda=0
, any
L^{*}\in(0,0.5)
, and
L^{*}\leq L^{\prime}<1
, there exists
(\Pi,\mathcal{D})\in\mathcal{M}(L^{*})
such that
\mathbb{E}_{S}\left[L_{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{Q}_{\lambda})\right]\rightarrow L^{\prime}
as sample size
m\rightarrow\infty
.

On the positive side, the second part of
Theorem

3.1
allows us to understand just how to take
\lambda_{m}\rightarrow\infty
(more and more regularization), in order to not only get a tempered convergence, but to get consistency or “learning”, i.e., reach
L^{*}
. By applying the mean value theorem on
U_{\lambda}^{-1}(H(\cdot))
in
Theorem

3.1
(2), we get:

Theorem 3.4
.

For any distribution
Q^{*}
, source distribution
\mathcal{D}
, valid prior
\Pi
, and any
\lambda>1
and m:

\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}[L_{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{Q}_{\lambda})]\leq L_{\mathcal{D}}(Q^{*})+O\Bigg(\frac{1}{1-2L_{\mathcal{D}}(Q^{*})}\cdot\left[\frac{1}{\lambda}+\lambda\left(\frac{\textsf{KL}(Q^{*}\|\Pi)+\log m}{m}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{\log^{3}(m)}{m}}~\right]\Bigg),

(10)

where
O(\cdot)
only hides an absolute constant, that does not depend on
\mathcal{D},\Pi
or anything else.

Similarly to MDL, to obtain consistency, we need a balanced
\lambda_{m}\rightarrow\infty
(just enough regularization) to make both
\frac{1}{\lambda}
and
\lambda\left(\frac{\textsf{KL}(Q^{*}\|\Pi)+\log m}{m}\right)
vanish. The optimal choice is
\lambda_{m}=\sqrt{\frac{m}{\textsf{KL}(Q^{*}\|\Pi)+\log m}}
and that gives us an overall rate of
\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{m})
. More generally, consistency can be achieved in the following range:

Corollary 3.4.1
.

For
1\ll\lambda_{m}\ll\frac{m}{\log m}
, and any
Q^{*}
with
\textsf{KL}(Q^{*}\|\Pi)<\infty
and
L_{\mathcal{D}}(Q^{*})<0.5
, we have
\underset{m\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}\underset{\Pi,\mathcal{D}}{\sup}~\mathbb{E}_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}[L_{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{Q}_{\lambda_{m}})]\leq L_{\mathcal{D}}(Q^{*})
.

To complete this picture, we show how
\lambda_{m}
growing too fast (too much regularization) is harmful and the PAC-Bayes rule
\hat{Q}_{\lambda_{m}}
exhibits
catastrophic underfitting
:

Theorem 3.5
.

For any
\lambda_{m}=\Omega(m)
with
\liminf\frac{\lambda_{m}}{m}>10
, any
L^{*}\in[0,0.5)
, and any
L^{*}\leq L^{\prime}<\frac{0.5-0.1L^{*}}{0.9}
, and for
q^{*}_{m}=\arg\min_{q}\lambda_{m}\textsf{KL}(q\|0.1)+mH(qL^{*}+(1-q)L^{\prime})
, there exists

(\Pi,\mathcal{D})\in\mathcal{M}(L^{*})

such that
\mathbb{E}L_{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{Q}_{\lambda_{m}}(S))=q^{*}_{m}L^{*}+(1-q^{*}_{m})L^{\prime}+o(1)
. In particular, if
\frac{\lambda_{m}}{m}\rightarrow\infty
, then
q^{*}_{m}\rightarrow 0.1
and
\mathbb{E}L_{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{Q}_{\lambda_{m}}(S))\rightarrow 0.1L^{*}+0.9L^{\prime}
as
m\rightarrow\infty
.

In this section, we have unified the limiting error behavior of the more general PAC-Bayes rule (
2
) with that of the MDL rule for discrete priors with point-mass posteriors, studied by
22
. To summarize this behavior, assuming
L^{*}<\frac{1}{2}
, we have catastrophic overfitting when
\lambda_{m}\to 0
(limiting error reaches
1
), tempered overfitting for fixed
\lambda\geq 1
(limiting error remains below
\frac{1}{2}
, tempered overfitting for
0<\lambda<1
only if
L^{*}<H^{-1}(\lambda)
, benign behavior when
\lambda_{m}\to\infty
but remains
o\left(\frac{m}{\log(m)}\right)
leading to consistency/learning (limiting error reaches
L^{*}
, with the best convergence rate achieved by
\lambda_{m}\approx\sqrt{m}
), and, lastly, catastrophic underfitting if
\lambda_{m}=\omega(m)
(limiting error reaches
\frac{1}{2}
).

With this unifying perspective on limiting error, which is not evident in advance, we are motivated in the rest of paper to establish more analogies across these paradigms. In particular, we turn our attention to asking how this PAC-Bayes rule relates to Bayesian prediction, and how the regularization parameter
\lambda
, which we see is crucial for consistency, can be interpreted from a probabilistic perspective.

4
PAC-Bayes as Empirical Bayes

It is well-known that the solution of the standard PAC-Bayes posterior has a Gibbs form
(
14
;
2
)
, where the exponential tilt is given by the errors
L_{S}(h)
. It turns out that the solution to the PAC-Bayes learning rule (
3
)is given by a similar ‘standard’ Gibbs posterior, also featuring
L_{S}(h)
in the exponent.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume
L_{S}(\Pi)<\frac{1}{2}
. Then, the solution of PAC-Bayes learning rule (
3
) is given by:

\displaystyle d\hat{Q}_{\lambda}(h)\propto d\Pi(h)2^{-\left(\frac{m}{\lambda}H^{\prime}(L_{S}(\hat{Q}_{\lambda}))\right)\,L_{S}(h)},

(11)

where
L_{S}(h)
serves as the energy function of the Gibbs measure and there exists a unique choice of inverse temperature
\frac{m}{\lambda}H^{\prime}(L_{S}(\hat{Q}_{\lambda}))
, that is self-consistent.

Proof.

This can be shown using a variational argument. A simple way to derive it is to let the Lagrange multiplier be
\mu
, and the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}=mH(L_{S}(Q))+\lambda\textsf{KL}(Q\|\Pi)+\mu(\int Q-1)
. Then by taking the derivative on a single predictor
h_{0}
, we have that
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial Q(h_{0})}=mH^{\prime}(L_{S}(Q))L_{S}(h_{0})+\lambda\left(\frac{1}{\ln 2}+\log\frac{dQ(h_{0})}{d\Pi(h_{0})}\right)+\mu
. Setting the derivative to zero yields that
d\hat{Q}_{\lambda}(h_{0})\propto d\Pi(h_{0})2^{-\frac{m}{\lambda}H^{\prime}(L_{S}(\hat{Q}_{\lambda}))L_{S}(h_{0})}
.

8
Summary

We have two major contributions in this paper. First, we provided a tight analysis, with matching upper bounds and worst-case lower bounds, on the limiting error of the PAC-Bayes learning rule
\hat{Q}_{\lambda}
, based on an arbitrary continuous prior, for any
0<\lambda<\infty
, and generalizes to any
\lambda
. In this sense, we fully extended the analysis of
22
to and unified its discrete MDL framework with a continuous PAC-Bayes type rule. We also considered a related learning rule
\hat{Q}^{\textsf{mod}}_{\lambda}
for which we establish the same lower bounds, but left the upper bounds as an open question.

Second, we provided a Bayesian interpretation of the PAC-Bayes predictor
\hat{Q}/\lambda
. Although the Gibbs form of the PAC-Bayes predictor is well known, and the name itself implies a strong relationship with Bayesian analysis, as far as we are aware an explicit link of this nature was not previously made. (Perhaps the closest past attempt at a Bayesian interpretation is the work of
5
, which however requires choosing log-likelihood as the loss function within a well-specified model, in contrast to the agnostic zero-one loss setting studied here.) In particular, we showed how these distinct PAC-Bayes bound minimizers correspond to Empirical Bayes and to the Profile Posterior, and how to interpret their general form with a regularization parameter of
\lambda\neq 1
.

To further complete the picture, in addition to adding the consistency characterization of Profile Posterior and the full Bayesian posterior, it would also be interesting to relate them explicitly to a more standard PAC-Bayes predictor minimizing
L_{S}(Q)+\lambda\textsf{KL}(Q\|\Pi)
, without an entropy transformation, which corresponds to a different choice of the Gibbs posterior temperature.

\displaystyle J_{\lambda_{m}}^{\prime\prime}(q)\geq\frac{1}{1-q}\left(\frac{\lambda_{m}}{q}-\frac{mL^{\prime}}{1-L^{\prime}}\right)\geq\frac{1}{1-q}\left(\lambda_{m}-\frac{mL^{\prime}}{1-L^{\prime}}\right).

(38)

Since
L^{\prime}<\frac{0.5-0.1L^{*}}{0.9}
,
L^{\prime}/(1-L^{\prime})\leq\frac{0.5}{0.4}=1.25
. Because
\liminf\lambda_{m}>10m
, there exists some
c>0
such that
J_{\lambda_{m}}^{\prime\prime}(q)>cm
for all
q\in(0,1)
for large
m
. So
J_{\lambda_{m}}
is strongly convex and any stationary point is the unique global minimizer.

Note that as
q\rightarrow 0^{+}
,
\tilde{J}_{\lambda_{m}}(q)\rightarrow-\infty
, and as
q\rightarrow 1^{-}
,
J_{\lambda_{m}}(q)\rightarrow\infty
. By the assumption
L^{\prime}<\frac{0.5-0.1L^{*}}{0.9}
,
L(0.1)<0.5
, and so
J_{\lambda_{m}}^{\prime}(0.1)\leq 0
. Because
J_{\lambda_{m}}^{\prime}(q)
is increasing in
q
, we must have
q^{*}_{m}\geq 0.1
. In particular, if
\frac{\lambda_{m}}{m}\rightarrow\infty
,
q^{*}_{m}\rightarrow\Pi(h_{0})=0.1
.

Because
L_{S}(h_{0})\xrightarrow{p}L^{*}
and
L_{S}(h_{1})\xrightarrow{p}L^{\prime}
, and the map
(a,b,q)\mapsto H(aq+(1-q)b)
is continuous on the compact set
[0,1]^{3}
,

\displaystyle U_{m}(S)\coloneqq\sup_{q\in[0,1]}\left\lvert{H(\hat{L}(q))-H(L(q))}\right\rvert\xrightarrow{p}0.

(39)

Set

\displaystyle\Delta_{m}(S)\coloneqq\sup_{q\in[0,1]}\left\lvert{J_{\lambda_{m}}(q,S)-\tilde{J}_{\lambda_{m}}(q)}\right\rvert=mU_{m}(S).

(40)

Then
\Delta_{m}(S)/m\xrightarrow{p}0
. By the strong convexity of
J_{\lambda_{m}}
,

\displaystyle J_{\lambda_{m}}(q)\geq J_{\lambda_{m}}(q^{*}_{m})+\frac{cm}{2}(q-q^{*}_{m})^{2},

(41)

for any
q\in[0,1]
. Since
\hat{q}_{m}
minimizes
\hat{J}_{\lambda_{m}}
, we have
\hat{J}_{\lambda_{m}}(\hat{q}_{m})\leq\hat{J}_{\lambda_{m}}(q^{*}_{m})
. Thus,

\displaystyle J_{\lambda_{m}}(\hat{q}_{m})-J_{\lambda_{m}}(q^{*}_{m})\leq(J_{\lambda_{m}}-\hat{J}_{\lambda_{m}})(\hat{q}_{m})-(J_{\lambda_{m}}-\hat{J}_{\lambda_{m}})(q^{*}_{m})\leq 2\Delta_{m}.

(42)

Combining (
41
) and (
42
), we have
\left\lvert{\hat{q}_{m}-q^{*}_{m}}\right\rvert^{2}\leq\frac{4\Delta_{m}(S)}{cm}
for all large
m
. Since
\Delta_{m}(S)/m\xrightarrow{p}0
, we have
\hat{q}_{m}\xrightarrow{p}q^{*}_{m}
.

By continuity of
L(q)
and that
\left\lvert{L(\hat{q}_{m})-L(q^{*}_{m})}\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert{L^{\prime}-L^{*}}\right\rvert\left\lvert{\hat{q}_{m}-q^{*}_{m}}\right\rvert
, we have
L_{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{Q}_{\lambda_{m}}(S))=L(\hat{q}_{m})\xrightarrow{p}L(q^{*}_{m})
. This implies that
\mathbb{E}L_{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{Q}_{\lambda_{m}}(S))=\mathbb{E}L(\hat{q}_{m})\rightarrow L(q^{*}_{m})
. In particular, if
\frac{\lambda_{m}}{m}\rightarrow\infty
,
q^{*}_{m}\rightarrow\Pi(h_{0})=0.1
. So
\mathbb{E}L_{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{Q}_{\lambda_{m}}(S))\rightarrow L(0.1)=0.1L^{*}+0.9L^{\prime}
.

It is important to note that in the definition of
\hat{Q}_{\lambda}
, we also require the distribution
\hat{Q}_{\lambda}
to satisfy
L_{S}(\hat{Q}_{\lambda})\leq\frac{1}{2}
(as in equation (
3
)). And we just showed
L_{S}(\hat{Q}_{\lambda_{m}})=\hat{L}(\hat{q}_{m})\xrightarrow{p}L(q^{*}_{m})
. Since
L(q)=L^{\prime}+q(L^{*}-L^{\prime})
is decreasing in
q
and
q^{*}_{m}\geq 0.1
, we have
L(q^{*}_{m})\leq L(0.1)<0.5
, and so
\hat{Q}_{\lambda_{m}}
is the output distribution as
m\rightarrow\infty
. This completes the proof for
Theorem 3.5
.

∎

Appendix D
Bayesian Interpretation Proofs

We now present the remaining proofs of the Bayesian interpretations of the learning rules from
Section 4
and
Section 5
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\hat{\eta}=\arg\max_{\eta}P(S|\eta)=\arg\max_{\eta}\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\Pi}P(S|\eta,h)
. Then
\hat{Q}_{1}(h)\equiv P(h|S,\hat{\eta})
.

Proof.

The likelihood of the model is defined as
P(S|\eta,h)=\eta^{mL_{S}(h)}(1-\eta)^{m(1-L_{S}(h))}=(1-\eta)^{m}2^{-mH^{\prime}(\eta)L_{S}(h)}\propto 2^{-mH^{\prime}(\eta)L_{S}(h)}
.
P(S|\eta)=\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\Pi}P(S|\eta,h)=(1-\eta)^{m}\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\Pi}[2^{mL_{S}(h)\theta}]
, where
\theta=\log\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}
. Denote
Z(\eta)=\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\Pi}[2^{mL_{S}(h)\theta}]
. To maximize
P(S|\eta)
over
\eta
, we take the logarithm and set its derivative to zero, and get
\frac{d}{d\eta}\log P(S|\eta)=-\frac{m}{1-\eta}+\frac{d}{d\theta}(\log Z(\eta))\frac{d\theta}{d\eta}=0
. Note that
\frac{d\theta}{d\eta}=\frac{1}{\eta(1-\eta)}
, and
\frac{d}{d\theta}\log Z(\eta)=\frac{1}{Z(\eta)}\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\Pi}[\frac{d}{d\theta}2^{m\theta L_{S}(h)}]=m\int\frac{2^{m\theta L_{S}(h)}\Pi(h)}{Z(\eta)}L_{S}(h)dh=m\mathbb{E}_{h\sim P(h|S,\eta)}[L_{S}(h)]
. Plugging it in yields that
\hat{\eta}=\mathbb{E}_{h\sim P(h|S,\hat{\eta})}[L_{S}(h)]
. Therefore,
P(h|S,\hat{\eta})\propto\Pi(h)P(S|\hat{\eta},h)\propto 2^{-mH^{\prime}(L_{S}(P(h|S,\hat{\eta})))L_{S}(h)}\Pi(h)
. Comparing with equation (
4
), it is easy to see that
\hat{Q}(h)\equiv P(h|S,\hat{\eta})
.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

Consider the prior
P_{\lambda}(\eta)\propto((1-\eta)^{\lambda}+\eta^{\lambda})^{-\frac{m}{\lambda}}
. Let
\hat{\eta}=\arg\max_{\eta}P(S|\eta)P_{\lambda}(\eta)=\arg\max_{\eta}\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\Pi}P(S|\eta,h)P_{\lambda}(\eta)
. Then
\hat{Q}_{\lambda}(h)\equiv P(h|S,\hat{\eta})\propto\Pi(h)P(S|\hat{\eta},h)
.

Proof.

Note that
\frac{d}{d\eta}\left(\log P(S|\eta)+\log P_{\lambda}(\eta)\right)=-\frac{m}{1-\eta}+mL_{S}(P(h|S,\eta))\frac{1}{\eta(1-\eta)}+\frac{d}{d\eta}\log P_{\lambda}(\eta)=0
, which follows from the proof of
Theorem 4.2
.

Using
P_{\lambda}(\eta)\propto((1-\eta)^{\lambda}+\eta^{\lambda})^{-\frac{m}{\lambda}}
, we get that

\frac{d}{d\eta}\log P_{\lambda}(\eta)=\frac{m}{1-\eta}+m\frac{1}{\eta(1-\eta)}\frac{e^{\lambda\theta}}{1+e^{\lambda\theta}},

where we again use the reparametrization with
\theta=\log\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}
. Substituting into the optimality condition above and solving for
e^{\lambda\theta}
, we obtain that the maximizer
\hat{\eta}
satisfies

\frac{1-\hat{\eta}}{\hat{\eta}}=\left(\frac{1-L_{S}(P(h|S,\hat{\eta}))}{L_{S}(P(h|S,\hat{\eta}))}\right)^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}.

Thus,
P(S|\hat{\eta},h)\propto 2^{-m\log\frac{1-\hat{\eta}}{\hat{\eta}}L_{S}(h)}=2^{-\frac{m}{\lambda}\log\frac{1-L_{S}(P(h|S,\hat{\eta}))}{L_{S}(P(h|S,\hat{\eta}))}L_{S}(h)}=2^{-\frac{m}{\lambda}H^{\prime}(L_{S}(P(h|S,\hat{\eta})))L_{S}(h)}
. Therefore,
P(h|S,\hat{\eta})\propto\Pi(h)P(S|\hat{\eta},h)\propto 2^{-\frac{m}{\lambda}H^{\prime}(L_{S}(P(h|S,\hat{\eta})))L_{S}(h)}\Pi(h)
, implying that
\hat{Q}_{\lambda}(h)\equiv P(h|S,\hat{\eta})
.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

For any fixed prior
P_{\lambda}(\eta)
which does not depend on
m
,
\frac{dP(h|S,\hat{\eta})}{d\hat{Q}_{1}(h)}\rightarrow 1
uniformly as
m\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

The first order condition gives us
-\frac{m}{1-\eta}+mL_{S}(P(h|S,\eta))\frac{1}{\eta(1-\eta)}+\frac{d}{d\eta}\log P_{\lambda}(\eta)=0
. A first-order expansion gives us that
\hat{\eta}=L_{S}(P(h|S,\eta))+O(\frac{1}{m})
. Plugging this, by Taylor expanding
H^{\prime}
at
L_{S}(P(h|S,\eta))
we have
P(S|\hat{\eta},h)\propto 2^{-mH^{\prime}(\hat{\eta})L_{S}(h)}=2^{-mH^{\prime}(L_{S}(P(h|S,\hat{\eta})))L_{S}(h)}2^{-O(1)}
. ∎

Theorem 5.2
.

Consider the prior
P_{\lambda}(\eta)\propto((1-\eta)^{\lambda}+\eta^{\lambda})^{-\frac{m}{\lambda}}
. Let
\hat{\eta}_{h}=\arg\max_{\eta}P(S\mid\eta,h)P_{\lambda}(\eta)
. Then

\displaystyle\hat{Q}^{\textsf{mod}}_{\lambda}(h)

\displaystyle\propto\Pi(h)P(S\mid\hat{\eta}_{h},h)P_{\lambda}(\hat{\eta}_{h})

\displaystyle\propto\max_{\eta}\Pi(h)P(S|\eta,h)P_{\lambda}(\eta).

Proof.

Let
\hat{\eta}_{h}=\arg\max_{\eta}\log P(S\mid\eta,h)+\log P_{\lambda}(\eta)=m\left(L_{S}(h)\log\eta+(1-L_{S}(h))\log(1-\eta)\right)-\frac{m}{\lambda}\log(\eta^{\lambda}+(1-\eta)^{\lambda})
. To take the derivative, we proceed similarly to the proof of
Theorem 4.3
, and obtain that the optimal
\hat{\eta}_{h}
satisfies:

\frac{1-\hat{\eta}_{h}}{\hat{\eta}_{h}}=\left(\frac{1-L_{S}(h))}{L_{S}(h)}\right)^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}.

Plugging this in, a few manipulations yield that
P(S|\hat{\eta}_{h},h)P_{\lambda}(\hat{\eta}_{h})=\left((1-L_{S}(h))^{1-L_{S}(h)}L_{S}(h)^{L_{S}(h)}\right)^{\frac{m}{\lambda}}=2^{-\frac{m}{\lambda}H(L_{S}(h))}
. So we have
\Pi(h)P(S|\hat{\eta}_{h},h)P_{\lambda}(\hat{\eta}_{h})=\Pi(h)2^{-\frac{m}{\lambda}H(L_{S}(h))}\propto\hat{Q}^{\textsf{mod}}_{\lambda}(h)
.
∎

Theorem 5.3
.

\frac{d\hat{Q}^{\textsf{Bayes}}_{1}(h)}{d\hat{Q}^{\textsf{mod}}_{1}(h)}\rightarrow 1
uniformly as
m\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

P(h\mid S)\propto\Pi(h)I(h)
, where
I(h)=\int P(S\mid\eta,h)P_{\lambda}(\eta)d\eta
.
\log I(h)=\log\int\exp\{m\Psi_{\lambda}(\eta,L_{S}(h))\}d\eta
, where
\Psi_{\lambda}(\eta,L_{S}(h))=(1-L_{S}(h))\log(1-\eta)+L_{S}(h)\log\eta
. Then by Laplace’s method,
\log I(h)=\log\int\exp\{m\Psi_{\lambda}(\eta,L_{S}(h))\}d\eta=m\max_{\eta}\Psi_{\lambda}(\eta,L_{S}(h))+O(\log m)
. And we can check that
\max_{\eta}\Psi_{\lambda}(\eta,L_{S}(h))=-H(L_{S}(h))
. Hence
\hat{Q}^{\textsf{Bayes}}_{1}=P(h|S)\propto\Pi(h)2^{-mH(L_{S}(h))(1+o(1))}
.
∎

Theorem 5.4
.

With the same prior
P_{\lambda}(\eta)=((1-\eta)^{\lambda}+\eta^{\lambda})^{-\frac{m}{\lambda}}
,
\frac{d\hat{Q}^{\textsf{Bayes}}_{\lambda}(h)}{d\hat{Q}^{\textsf{mod}}_{\lambda}(h)}\rightarrow 1
uniformly as
m\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

P(h\mid S)\propto\Pi(h)I(h)
, where
I(h)=\int P(S\mid\eta,h)P_{\lambda}(\eta)d\eta
.
\log I(h)=\log\int\exp\{m\Psi_{\lambda}(\eta,L_{S}(h))\}d\eta
, where
\Psi_{\lambda}(\eta,L_{S}(h))=(1-L_{S}(h))\log((1-\eta)+L_{S}(h)\log\eta-\frac{1}{\lambda}\log(\eta^{\lambda}+(1-\eta)^{\lambda})
. Then by Laplace’s method,
\log I(h)=\log\int\exp\{m\Psi_{\lambda}(\eta,L_{S}(h))\}d\eta=m\max_{\eta}\Psi_{\lambda}(\eta,L_{S}(h))+O(\log m)
. Notice that this is the same objective that
\hat{Q}^{\textsf{mod}}_{\lambda}
maximizes in
Theorem 5.2
, and we have checked that
\max_{\eta}\Psi_{\lambda}(\eta,L_{S}(h))=-\frac{1}{\lambda}H(L_{S}(h))
. Hence
\hat{Q}^{\textsf{Bayes}}_{\lambda}=P(h|S)\propto\Pi(h)2^{-\frac{m}{\lambda}H(L_{S}(h))(1+o(1))}
.
∎

Theorem 5.5
.

With any fixed prior that does not depend on
m
,
\frac{d\hat{Q}^{\textsf{Bayes}}(h)}{d\hat{Q}^{\textsf{mod}}_{1}(h)}\rightarrow 1
uniformly as
m\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

Denote the fixed prior by
P(\eta)
. Then
P(h\mid S)\propto\Pi(h)\int P(\eta)(1-\eta)^{mL_{S}(h)}\eta^{mL_{S}(h)}d\eta
. By Laplace’s method,
\int P(\eta)(1-\eta)^{mL_{S}(h)}\eta^{mL_{S}(h)}d\eta=\int P(\eta)\exp\{m(1-\eta)\log(1-L_{S}(h))+mL_{S}(h)\log\eta\}d\eta=P(L_{S}(h))2^{-mH(L_{S}(h))}(1+o(1))
as
L_{S}(h)=\arg\max_{\eta}(1-\eta)\log(1-L_{S}(h))+L_{S}(h)\log\eta
. Hence,
\hat{Q}^{\textsf{Bayes}}=P(h|S)\propto\Pi(h)2^{-mH(L_{S}(h))}
.
∎
