Local topological markers for Chern insulators in ribbon geometry
Maks Repše, Tomaž Rejec, Jernej Mravlje

Abstract
Local topological markers are used to characterize Chern insulators in the presence of spatial inhomogeneities, such as boundaries and disorder. In this paper, we study the local Chern marker in systems with partial translational symmetry. We express the local Chern marker in the hybrid position-momentum basis for both open and periodic boundary conditions. We calculate the local Chern marker for a Haldane model ribbon. We show that the behavior at the two boundaries is qualitatively different from fully open geometries. We further compare the local Chern marker with the local Středa marker and show agreement in the bulk and small deviations at the boundaries that diminish with increasing system size. The correspondence between the two markers remains good if disorder is introduced, provided its magnitude remains below large values that cause substantial change of the Chern number due to Anderson physics. Finally, by exploiting the numerical efficiency due to partial translational symmetry, we study equilibrium critical behavior and the Kibble-Zurek mechanism in a weakly disordered Qi–Wu–Zhang Chern insulator. We extract relevant scaling exponents from the local Chern marker configuration and show that they converge to the analytically predicted values with increasing system size.



I
Introduction

Local topological markers have been proven to be a useful tool for characterizing inhomogeneous topological systems. They were originally introduced in Chern insulators and used to recover the bulk Chern number
C
in systems without translational symmetry, such as disordered
Prodan et al. 2010
and finite
Bianco and Resta 2011
systems. The local Chern marker (LCM), introduced in Ref.
Bianco and Resta 2011
, is, in a sense, a spatially resolved density of
C
. Since its introduction, LCM has been connected to several observable quantities, such as orbital magnetization
Bianco and Resta 2013
, anomalous Hall conductivity
Marrazzo and Resta 2017
;
d’Ornellas et al. 2022
and circular dichroism
Tran et al. 2017
.

The wide adoption of LCM and related quantities has primarily focused on systems that feature no translational symmetry. That is, systems within open boundary conditions in all directions (fully-OBC) and/or disorder that breaks translational symmetry even in systems with periodic boundary conditions (PBC). In contrast, systems with partial translational symmetry have received far less attention. Examples of such systems include ribbons (2-dimensional systems that are infinite in one and finite in the other direction) and systems with inhomogeneities or disorder oriented in stripes. Moreover, partial translational symmetry is a natural description of interfaces
Irsigler et al. 2019
and domain walls between regions with different
C
, bulk systems in a homogeneous external magnetic field in the Landau gauge and periodically driven Floquet Chern insulators
Privitera and Santoro 2016
.

Here, we build on the work of Drigo and Resta
Drigo and Resta 2020
and use a formula for calculating LCM in systems with partial translational symmetry using the position-momentum (hybrid) basis. In Sec.
II
we review the derivation of the formula and present an approach for its numerical evaluation within both periodic and open boundary conditions. In Sec.
III
we explore the behavior of LCM in ribbon geometry and compare it to a different topological marker, the local Středa marker
Markov et al. 2024
, motivated by the experimentally measurable response of the system to an external uniform magnetic field. We show data that suggest that for moderately strongly disordered systems the two markers are equal in the thermodynamic limit. In Sec.
IV
we demonstrate the utility of partial translational symmetry
to study slow quenches over topological phase transitions. We accurately determine how correlation length scales with the quench duration in a model Chern insulator.
In Sec.
V
we present our conclusions.

Throughout the article, we set the reduced Planck constant
\hbar
and the lattice constant
a
to 1.

II
Local topological markers in position-momentum basis

II.1
Local Chern marker

We assume spinless and non-interacting electrons and start with the expression for the LCM in the position basis
Bianco and Resta 2011
;
Prodan et al. 2010

c(\mathbf{r})=2\pi i\sum_{\alpha}\expectationvalue{P\comm{-i\comm{\hat x}{P}}{-i\comm{\hat y}{P}}}{\vb r,\alpha},

(1)

where
\mathbf{r}=(x,y)
is the lattice vector and
\alpha
denotes the orbital index. We denote the number of unit cells in
x(y)
direction as
N_{x(y)}
. The Fermi projector
P=\sum_{E_{\psi}<\mu}\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}
projects onto the occupied subspace that is spanned by single-electron eigenstates
\ket{\psi}
of the system’s Hamiltonian with energies
E_{\psi}
below the Fermi level
\mu
.

Assuming translational invariance in the
y
direction, the Fermi projector can be decomposed into
N_{y}
independent blocks, indexed by momenta
k_{y}
from the Brillouin zone

P=\bigoplus_{k_{y}}P(k_{y}).

(2)

The first commutator in Eq. (
1
) decomposes into a direct sum of commutators
-i\commutator{\hat x}{P}\to\bigoplus_{k_{y}}-i\commutator{\hat x}{P(k_y)}
and the second commutator becomes
-i\commutator{\hat y}{P}\to\bigoplus_{k_{y}}\partial_{k_{y}}P(k_{y})

Prodan 2011
.
This yields the expression for the LCM in position-momentum basis
Drigo and Resta 2020

\begin{split}c(x)=\frac{2\pi i}{N_{y}}\sum_{k_{y}}\bigg\{\sum_{\alpha}\bra{x,\alpha}&P(k_{y})\\
\big[-i\commutator{\hat x}{P(k_y)}\,,\,&\partial_{k_{y}}P(k_{y})\big]\ket{x,\alpha}\bigg\}.\end{split}

(3)

Since
P(k_{y})
is periodic in
k_{y}
, its derivative can be calculated numerically using the spectral derivative
Širca and Horvat 2018
. We use the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
to determine the series of Fourier components
{\cal P}_{n}
of the projector. The
k_{y}
derivative of
P(k_{y})
can then be calculated as the inverse Fourier transform of the series

\partial_{k_{y}}P(k_{y})\to\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{in{\cal P}_{n}\big\}

(4)

If
N_{y}
is even, the series runs from
n=-N_{y}/2
to
N_{y}/2-1

1
1

1

In this case, the component
n=-N_{y}/2
should be set to 0. See Ref.
Širca and Horvat 2018
for details
. If
N_{y}
is odd, the series runs between
-(N_{y}-1)/2
and
(N_{y}-1)/2
.

The calculation of the commutator
\commutator{\hat x}{P(k_y)}
depends on the boundary condition in the
x
direction. In order to build some intuition it is useful to first consider a system with OBC in the
x
direction, in which the position operator
\hat{x}
is well defined. The
k_{y}
component of the projector (
2
) can be written as a matrix in the position basis

P(k_{y})=\sum_{x,x^{\prime}}\ket{x}P_{xx^{\prime}}(k_{y})\bra{x'},

(5)

where
\ket{x}
is an eigenstate of the position operator
\hat{x}\ket{x}=x\ket{x}
and
P_{xx^{\prime}}(k_{y})=\matrixelement{x}{P(k_y)}{x'}
. The commutator
\commutator{\hat x}{P(k_y)}
acting on a position eigenstate
\ket{x_0}
gives

\commutator{\hat x}{P(k_y)}\ket{x_0}=\sum_{x}(x-x_{0})P_{xx_{0}}(k_{y})\ket{x}.

(6)

Within PBC, the position operator is not well defined. However, Eq. (
6
) can still be used if we modify the distances
x-x_{0}
to account for periodicity. We define a symmetric
N_{x}\times N_{x}
Toeplitz matrix of distances between lattice sites
\Delta_{x}
with the first row

\begin{pmatrix}0&1&2&\cdots&M&-M&\cdots&-2&-1\end{pmatrix}

(7)

where
M=(N_{x}-1)/2
and
N_{x}/2
for
N_{x}
odd and even, respectively. In the latter case,
M
needs to be removed from the matrix and
-M
set to 0. Eq. (
6
) reduces to an element-wise product of matrices, denoted by
\odot
,

-i\commutator{\hat x}{P(k_y)}\to-i\Delta_{x}\odot P(k_{y}).

(8)

An alternative derivation of Eqs. (
3
) and (
8
) is provided in Appendix
A
.

Eq. (
8
) can also be used when calculating commutators
\commutator{\hat x}{P}
and
\commutator{\hat y}{P}
in a fully local basis, assuming PBC in both directions. In that case, the formula produces results that are equivalent to those obtained by the formula presented in Ref.
Prodan et al. 2010
. Furthermore, it could be useful when evaluating commutators of the type
\commutator{\hat x}{P}
, in order to calculate the local
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
topological marker in two-dimensional systems
Baù and Marrazzo 2024
or local topological markers in odd spatial dimensions
Hannukainen et al. 2022
within PBC.

II.2
Local Středa marker

While LCM is an excellent tool for diagnosing topological properties in theoretical and numerical applications, its experimental implementation remains unclear. Another local topological marker for Chern insulators that could readily be measured in an experiment, is the local Středa marker

c_{S}(\mathbf{r})=\phi_{0}\partialderivative{n(\vb r)}{\phi},

(9)

where
n(\mathbf{r})
is the local electron density,
\phi
an external uniform magnetic flux per unit cell,
\phi_{0}=h/e_{0}
the magnetic flux quantum and
e_{0}
and
h
unit charge and Planck constant, respectively. (Unless stated otherwise, all fluxes in the remainder of this paper are given per unit cell.) The derivative must be evaluated in the limit
\phi\to 0
and at a fixed chemical potential
\mu
. The local Středa marker has been connected to both LCM and orbital magnetization
Bianco and Resta 2013
. Recently, non-equilibrium behavior of local Chern and Středa markers in systems within fully-OBC has also been investigated
Markov et al. 2024
. We calculate
c_{S}
in the hybrid basis simply as

c_{S}(x)=\phi_{0}\partialderivative{\big(\sum_{k_y}{P_{xx}(k_y)\big)}}{\phi}

(10)

IV
Kibble-Zurek mechanism in a disordered Chern insulator

Local Chern marker has also been used to characterize non-equilibrium states after quantum quenches across topological phase transitions
Ulčakar et al. 2020
;
Yuan et al. 2024
. It was found that for slow quenches characterized by a duration
\tau
, the non-equilibrium state features a length scale that grows as the quench becomes slower, just as expected in systems with standard Landau order parameters. Small random disorder was introduced to reveal the length scale in real space. The length scale was proposed to be governed by the Kibble-Zurek mechanism, but
these predictions could be numerically tested for systems of moderate size only.
Here we take advantage of partial translational symmetry to show convergence of the results with the system size.

IV.1
Kibble-Zurek mechanism

Kibble-Zurek mechanism (KZM)
Kibble 1976
;
Zurek 1996
describes how the correlation length of a system
\xi
freezes-out during a slow quench across a critical point. It also predicts that
\xi
at the end of the quench scales with the duration of the quench (assuming a linear ramp)
\tau
as

\xi\sim\tau^{1/(1+\nu z)},

(12)

where
\nu
is the correlation length critical exponent and
z
the dynamical critical exponent
Dziarmaga 2010
.

As described below, the correlation length in a Chern insulator can be obtained from the LCM configuration. However, if the system is translationally invariant, the LCM configuration is homogeneous (as is any other spatially resolved quantity). The correlation length can be revealed by introducing inhomogeneities like random disorder in the system.
Since the disorder is needed only to reveal the underlying length scale (present in correlation functions or the Berry curvature
Chen 2016
), it can be chosen in a way that preserves translational symmetry in one direction. This symmetry can then be used to speed up numerical calculations and allow for the study of larger systems.

IV.2
Model

We use the Qi-Wu-Zhang model
Qi et al. 2006
and assume PBC in both directions. The disorder
\delta_{x}
is invariant under translations in the
y
direction, making
k_{y}
a good quantum number. The Hamiltonian can therefore be written in block-diagonal form in the
(x,k_{y})
basis as

H_{u}=\bigoplus_{k_{y}}H_{u}(k_{y}),

(13)

where
k_{y}
is an element of the Brillouin zone. Each block
H_{u}(k_{y})
has the form (the nearest neighbor hopping amplitude is set to 1)

\begin{split}H_{u}&(k_{y})=\sum_{x=1}^{N_{x}}\left(\ket{x+1}\bra{x}\otimes\frac{\sigma_{z}+i\sigma_{x}}{2}+{\rm h.c.}\right)+\\
&\sum_{x=1}^{N_{x}}\ket{x}\bra{x}\otimes[\cos k_{y}\sigma_{z}+\sin k_{y}\sigma_{y}+(u+\delta_{x})\sigma_{z}],\end{split}

(14)

where
x
is the cell index and Pauli matrices
\sigma_{x,y,z}
act on a two-dimensional orbital space. The disorder
\delta_{x}
is uncorrelated and uniformly distributed on the interval
[-\delta/2,\delta/2]
. We set disorder amplitude to
\delta=5\times 10^{-4}
throughout.

The phase diagram of the (clean) QWZ model features the following insulating phases: The system is a trivial insulator with
C=0
for
\absolutevalue{u}>2
and a topological insulator with
C=-1
and
+1
for
-2<u<0
and
0<u<2
, respectively.

V
Conclusion

In summary, we studied the local Chern and Středa markers in Chern insulators with partial translational symmetry. We presented a numerical formula for calculating LCM in the hybrid basis both within periodic and open boundary conditions.
The Haldane model was used to investigate the behavior of LCM on a ribbon. The behavior at the boundary was found to be qualitatively different from the fully-OBC case.
We compared LCM to the local Středa marker. The two markers were shown to give matching results, except at the boundary or when the disorder becomes very strong.

To show the advantages of using local topological markers in the hybrid basis we used LCM to determine equilibrium and dynamical critical properties of a Chern insulator and their relation via KZM. Considering a system with partial translational symmetry allowed for the simulation of larger systems. This enabled accurate determination of scaling exponents that agree closely with analytical predictions.

Topological markers in the hybrid basis that were investigated in this paper could be used for convenient and efficient numerical calculations in many different systems that feature a translationally invariant direction. The numerical methods presented could be extended to local topological markers for other classes of topological insulators.

Appendix A
Derivation of LCM in the hybrid basis

Assuming full translational symmetry, the Chern number is calculated as the integral over the 2-dimensional Brillouin zone (BZ)

C=\frac{i}{2\pi}\int_{\rm BZ}\differential\mathbf{k}\tr{P(\vb k)\comm{\partial_{k_x}P(\vb k)}{\partial_{k_y}P(\vb k)}},

(15)

where
P(\mathbf{k})=P(k_{x},k_{y})
is the Fermi projector in the reciprocal space and the trace is over the orbital space. On a finite
N_{x}\times N_{y}
mesh, the derivatives can be calculated using the spectral derivative

\partial_{k_{x}}P(\mathbf{k})\to\Delta_{k_{x}}P(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{x=1}^{N_{x}}i\tilde{x}P(x,k_{y})\frac{e^{ik_{x}x}}{\sqrt{N_{x}}},

(16)

where
P(x,k_{y})
is the partial Fourier transform of
P(\mathbf{r})
and

\tilde{x}=\begin{cases}\quad x\quad&\text{if}\ x<N_{x}/2\\
\quad 0\quad&\text{if}\ x=N_{x}/2\\
x-N_{x}\quad&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(17)

After an inverse partial Fourier transform,
P(x,k_{y})
becomes
P(x,k_{y})=\sum_{k_{x}^{\prime}}P(k_{x}^{\prime},k_{y})e^{-ik_{x}^{\prime}x}/\sqrt{N_{x}}
. Inserting into Eq. (
15
) and replacing the integral with a sum, we get

\begin{split}C=&\frac{2\pi i}{N_{x}N_{y}}\sum_{k_{x},k_{y}}\tr\{P(k_{x},k_{y})\\
&\Big[\sum_{q_{x}}\delta_{k_{x},q_{x}}\sum_{x}i\tilde{x}\sum_{k_{x}^{\prime}}P(k_{x}^{\prime},k_{y})\frac{e^{i(q_{x}-k_{x}^{\prime})x}}{N_{x}},\\
&\sum_{p_{x}}\delta_{q_{x},p_{x}}\Delta_{k_{y}}P(k_{x},k_{y})\Big]\delta_{p_{x},k_{x}}\Bigg\},\end{split}

(18)

where we have relabeled
k_{x}
to
q_{x}
in the first factor of the commutator and to
p_{x}
in the second factor and
\delta_{k_{x},q_{x}}=\sum_{x_{1}}e^{i(k_{x}-q_{x})x_{1}}/N_{x}
is the Kronecker delta. After expanding Kronecker deltas and some reordering of terms we get

\begin{split}C=&\frac{2\pi i}{N_{x}N_{y}}\sum_{k_{y}}\tr\{\sum_{x}\sum_{q_{x}}\frac{e^{iq_{x}(x-(x_{1}-x_{2}))}}{N_{x}}\\
&\sum_{k_{x}}\frac{e^{ik_{x}x_{1}}}{\sqrt{N_{x}}}P(k_{x},k_{y})\frac{e^{-ik_{x}x_{3}}}{\sqrt{N_{x}}}\\
&\Big[i(\widetilde{x_{1}-x_{2}})\sum_{k_{x}^{\prime}}\frac{e^{ik_{x}^{\prime}x_{2}}}{\sqrt{N_{x}}}P(k_{x}^{\prime},k_{y})\frac{e^{-ik_{x}^{\prime}x_{1}}}{\sqrt{N_{x}}},\\
&\sum_{p_{x}}\frac{e^{ip_{x}x_{3}}}{\sqrt{N_{x}}}\Delta_{k_{y}}P(p_{x},k_{y})\frac{e^{-ip_{x}x_{2}}}{\sqrt{N_{x}}}\Big]\Bigg\},\end{split}

(19)

where we have made use of the identity
\sum_{q_{x}}e^{iq_{x}(x-(x_{1}-x_{2}))}/N_{x}=\delta_{x,x_{1}-x_{2}}
. In Eq. (
19
) we recognize the transformation of
P(k_{x},k_{y})
into the hybrid basis,

P_{x_{1},x_{3}}(k_{y})=\sum_{k_{x}}\frac{e^{ik_{x}x_{1}}}{\sqrt{N_{x}}}P(k_{x},k_{y})\frac{e^{-ik_{x}x_{3}}}{\sqrt{N_{x}}},

(20)

and identify
-i(\widetilde{x_{2}-x_{1}})=-i\Delta_{x}
, where
\Delta_{x}
is the Toeplitz matrix defined in Sec.
II.1
. We are left with the expression for the Chern number in the hybrid basis

C=\frac{2\pi i}{N_{x}N_{y}}\sum_{k_{y}}\tr{P(k_y)\comm{-i\Delta_x\odot P(k_y)}{\Delta_{k_y}P(k_y)}}.

(21)

The expression for LCM in this basis follows directly from Eq. (
21

-i(\widetilde{x_{2}-x_{1}})

-i(x_{2}-x_{1})
, then Eq. (
