Mendelian randomization study on the effects of women’s reproductive traits and socioeconomic traits on pregnancy complications
Authors: Liwen Zhang, Yu Xia, Weidi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPregnancy complications are influenced by a complex interplay of socioeconomic and reproductive factors. Observational studies have suggested associations with socioeconomic traits—such as educational attainment (EDU), household income (INC), and physically demanding work (PDW)—as well as reproductive traits including age at first sexual intercourse (AFS) and age at first birth (AFB). However, observational findings are prone to confounding and reverse causation, making it difficult to establish causality. Therefore, this study applies Mendelian randomization (MR) to investigate the causal relationships between reproductive and socioeconomic traits and the risk of pregnancy complications.MethodsWe conducted univariable and multivariable MR analyses to assess the causal effects of reproductive traits and socioeconomic traits on pregnancy complications. Genetic instruments reaching genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10⁻8) were selected from large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) involving 263,615 to 766,345 participants for socioeconomic traits (EDU, INC, PDW) and 214,547 to 418,758 participants for reproductive traits (AFS, AFB). Outcome data were derived from GWAS based on biobank datasets for eight pregnancy complications: spontaneous abortion (SA), postpartum depression (PD), gestational diabetes (GD), Pre-eclampsia or eclampsia (PE), premature rupture of membranes (PRM), premature separation of placenta (PSP), intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP), and hyperemesis gravidarum (HG). Sample sizes ranged from 294 to 9,113 cases and 59,610 to 117,892 controls.ResultsUnivariable MR analysis identified significant associations, including AFB–PD(OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.81–0.98, P = 0.024 ), AFS–SA(OR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.62–0.96, P = 0.020 ), AFS–PD(OR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.36–0.59, P < 0.001 ), AFS–GD(OR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.48–0.82, P < 0.001 ), AFS–PRM (OR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.48–0.94, P = 0.020 ), EDU–SA (OR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.71–0.97, P = 0.020 ), EDU–PD (OR = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.46–0.67, P < 0.001 ), EDU–GD (OR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.56–0.83, P < 0.001 ), EDU–PRM (OR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.56–0.94, P = 0.010 ), INC–PD (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.48–0.70, P < 0.001 ), INC–GD (OR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.43–0.97, P = 0.030 ), PDW–PD (OR = 2.00, 95% CI: 1.06–3.77, P = 0.030 ), and PDW–PE (OR = 2.80, 95% CI:1.09–7.18, P = 0.030 ). Multivariable MR analysis confirmed independent protective effects of higher EDU on PD (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.37–0.91, P = 0.020 ), GD (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.35–0.96, P = 0.030 ), and PRM (OR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.24–0.81, P = 0.010 ). AFS was independently associated with an increased risk of PSP(OR = 1.86, 95% CI: 1.03–3.36, P = 0.040).Two-step MR mediation indicated that AFS partially mediates the EDU–outcome relationship for SA, PD, GD, and PSP (proportions mediated ~ 55–65% ), while the reverse pathway (EDU as mediator of AFS) showed smaller but directionally consistent mediation (~ 24–29% ).ConclusionLower EDU is causally associated with increased risks of PD, PE, and PRM. Educational level may influence reproductive timing, such as AFS, which in turn affects the likelihood of pregnancy complications. These findings highlight the potential benefits of targeted educational and social interventions in mitigating adverse pregnancy outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09499-y.Keywords: pregnancy complication, education attainment, postpartum depression

Introduction

Pregnancy is a complex and tightly regulated biological process that requires coordinated adaptations across multiple maternal systems to support fetal growth and development [1].Disruptions at any stage of gestation may lead to adverse pregnancy outcomes, which remain a major cause of maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality worldwide [2].Increasing evidence further suggests that pregnancy complications are associated with long-term health consequences for both mothers and offspring later in life [3]. A range of conditions, including PE, PD, PRM, and PSP, can substantially increase health risks during pregnancy and in severe cases become life-threatening for both mother and child [4]. The etiology of these complications is fundamentally multifactorial, involving genetic, clinical, and social determinants (e.g., socioeconomic background, education, and reproductive behaviors) [5–7]. Early identification of individuals at increased risk is critical for the prevention and management of adverse pregnancy outcomes and may enable timely clinical intervention and monitoring [8].Observational studies have consistently reported that pregnant women with lower socioeconomic status are at increased risk of pregnancy complications [9]. However, establishing causality remains challenging due to the heavy confounding and collinearity among education, income, and lifestyle factors in conventional designs [10]. To overcome these limitations, Mendelian randomization (MR) leverages inherited genetic variants as instrumental variables to strengthen causal inference [11, 12]. Because alleles are randomly allocated at conception, MR can reduce bias due to confounding and reverse causation compared with traditional epidemiological approaches. Expanding upon these findings, recent genetic epidemiological insights have further extended their focus toward social determinants, demonstrating that genetic liability to lower EDU directly elevates the risks of specific conditions like pre-eclampsia and GDM [13, 14]. Nevertheless, the existing literature predominantly isolates single endpoints and relies heavily on traditional univariable frameworks, which frequently restricts the exploration of underlying causal pathways to conventional somatic or lifestyle mediators, such as smoking and obesity.

The present study breaks through these methodological constraints by expanding the phenotypic and analytical lens. Leveraging the latest large-scale GWAS summary statistics, we conducted systematic two-sample univariable and multivariable MR analyses to deepen the current understanding across three distinct dimensions. On one hand, by constructing MVMR models, we successfully disentangled highly correlated and traditionally collinear socio-structural features—namely EDU, INC, and PDW—thereby confirming that the protective capacity of higher education against obstetric risks persists independently of material wealth or occupational physical stress. Concurrently, moving beyond standard metabolic pathways, our framework uncovers a novel behavioral-reproductive axis, providing the first genetic evidence that AFS acts as a critical intermediate behavioral milestone that accounts for approximately 55% to 65% of the educational effect. Furthermore, regarding phenotypic coverage, we established a comprehensive evaluation network spanning eight major complications, offering robust causal inference for crucial yet historically understudied conditions including PD, PRM, and PSP. Ultimately, by reconciling these independent causal vectors and underlying pathways, our findings not only refine the interpretability of traditional observational designs but also provide a critical scientific foundation for risk-stratified clinical interventions and public maternal health strategies.

Materials and procedures

Design of the research and sources of data

We conducted a two-sample MR analysis, incorporating five exposure variables and eight outcome variables, based on 13 publicly available GWAS summary statistics (Fig. 1). To minimize potential bias from population stratification, all exposure and outcome cohorts were restricted to individuals of European ancestry.

Schematic overview of the study design. Genetic instruments for reproductive and socioeconomic traits were selected from large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS)

The study population comprised 936,265 participants of European descent, including 30,589 cases and 905,576 controls. Data sources were obtained from the following repositories: https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets/, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-021-01135-3, and https://storage.googleapis.com/finngen-public-data-r5/summary_stats/R5_manifest.tsv. Detailed descriptions of each dataset are provided in Table S1. All original GWAS had received approval from relevant ethics committees, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

Definition of exposures and outcomes

To capture the multidimensional architecture of maternal life-course epidemiology within a unified framework, exposures were stratified across distinct structural, material, and behavioral domains, with comprehensive phenotypic characteristics detailed in Table S1. Socioeconomic indicators indexed specific institutional, financial, and occupational environmental dimensions, where EDU was defined as continuous years of formal schooling harmonized via the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) guidelines; INC was modeled as a standardized ordinal variable derived from self-reported annual gross income brackets; and PDW was analyzed as an ordinal frequency metric capturing occupational physical labor stress. Reproductive traits reflected critical chronological behavioral milestones, including AFS and AFB, both treated as continuous quantitative variables (in years) derived from baseline female retrospective recall data. Conversely, the eight examined pregnancy complications—SA, PD, GD, PE, PRM, PSP, ICP and HG—were operationalized as binary endpoints. To minimize recall and reporting biases, these clinical outcomes were ascertained through passive record linkage to nationwide electronic health registers, with cases and controls classified strictly via validated International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9 and ICD-10) diagnostic codes to guarantee robust phenotypic boundaries.

Genetic instrument selection

SNPs (single nucleotide polymorphism) associated with years of schooling, average pre-tax INC, and occupations involving substantial manual or physical labor were selected at the genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5 × 10⁻8). Two additional sets of SNPs corresponding to AFS and AFB were included to capture reproductive traits.

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning was performed using the European 1000 Genomes reference panel (r² > 0.001, clumping window < 5000 kb). For each locus, the SNP with the lowest P value was retained. Palindromic SNPs with minor allele frequencies between 0.45 and 0.55 were excluded. Missing SNPs in the outcome datasets were not replaced if their absence was deemed to have negligible impact. Weak instrument bias was considered minimal given the robust F-statistics outcome. Comprehensive details of the selected genetic instruments are provided in Table S2.

Main statistical analyses

Most analyses were performed using the Inverse Variance Weighting (IVW) method. Under the assumptions of balanced or no horizontal pleiotropy, IVW combines the Wald ratios of each SNP to derive an unbiased causal estimate. This method assigns weights to each ratio based on the inverse of its variance, such that more precise estimates (smaller variances) receive greater weight. This weighting accounts for heterogeneity in the precision of individual SNP estimates and yields a pooled causal effect that reflects the overall genetic association with the outcome of interest. In this study, IVW was applied to assess the relationship between genetic variants and the targeted outcomes in the MR framework [15, 16].

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness and consistency of the causal estimates, we applied four complementary sensitivity analysis methods: the weighted median [17], MR-Egger regression [11], weighted mode [18], and the Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) test [19, 20]. The weighted median method provides statistically consistent causal estimates when at least 50% of the total weight is contributed by valid instrumental variables—those fulfilling the core assumptions of relevance, independence, and exclusion restriction. Under this criterion, the median-weighted estimate is assured to originate from valid instruments, thereby remaining unbiased even if up to half of the instruments are invalid. MR-Egger regression enables both detection and correction of directional pleiotropy through its intercept term; however, this approach may reduce statistical power, and the presence of pleiotropy was formally assessed using the P-value for the intercept. The weighted mode estimator offers reliable inference when the most frequently observed causal effect among instruments arises from valid variants. The MR-PRESSO framework was used to detect horizontal pleiotropy by identifying outlier SNPs and recalculating causal estimates after their removal, with the MR-PRESSO distortion test quantifying discrepancies between original and corrected estimates [21]. Between-SNP heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic, while a leave-one-out analysis was performed to determine whether any single SNP exerted a disproportionate influence on the overall causal estimate. Finally, linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) was implemented to examine potential genetic correlations between exposure and outcome traits.

Multivariable MR

To investigate the direct causative relationship of reproductive traits and, separately, socioeconomic traits with the prevalence of pregnancy complications, we performed a MVMR study [22]. MVMR enables the identification of independent relationships between each risk exposure and outcome as well as the detection of the causation of several risk variables on pregnancy problems. Specifically, we conducted our analysis using four MVMR techniques. For our studies, we used R version 4.2.2’s Two Sample MR, MR-PRESSO, MR and MVMR packages. P < 0.05 was our threshold for statistical significance.

Mediation analysis

To explore the potential interplay between socioeconomic and reproductive traits in shaping pregnancy complications, we applied a two-step MR framework for mediation analysis. In this approach, the causal effect of an exposure on a mediator and the effect of the mediator on an outcome are estimated using separate MR models, and the indirect (mediated) effect is quantified as the product of the two coefficients. The total effect corresponds to the univariable MR estimate of the exposure on the outcome, while the proportion mediated is calculated as the absolute ratio of the indirect to the total effect. Standard errors and 95% confidence intervals for mediated effects were derived using the delta method. This framework allows assessment of whether part of the effect of one trait on pregnancy complications operates through another trait, thereby identifying potential overlapping causal pathways.

Result

GWAS of reproductive traits and socioeconomic traits

We identified a substantial number of genome-wide significant and independent SNPs (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) associated with the following traits: 220 SNPs for years of education, 184 SNPs for average total INC before taxes, and 22 SNPs for engagement in heavy manual or physical labor. For reproductive traits, we detected 307 independent SNPs associated with AFS and 121 SNPs associated with AFB at the genome-wide significance threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻8 (Table S2).

Univariable MR results

Using the IVW method as the primary analytical approach, we identified significant causal associations between socioeconomic or reproductive traits and pregnancy complications. Higher AFB was associated with a reduced risk of PD (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.81–0.98, P = 0.024). Later AFS was linked to lower risks of SA (OR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.62–0.96, P = 0.020), PD (OR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.36–0.59, P < 0.001), GD (OR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.48–0.82, P < 0.001), and PRM (OR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.48–0.94, P = 0.020). Higher EDU was inversely associated with SA (OR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.71–0.97, P = 0.020), PD (OR = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.46–0.67, P < 0.001), GD (OR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.56–0.83, P < 0.001), and PRM (OR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.56–0.94, P = 0.010). Higher INC was associated with lower risks of PD (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.48–0.70, P < 0.001) and GD (OR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.43–0.97, P = 0.030). In contrast, engagement in PDW was positively associated with PD (OR = 2.00, 95% CI: 1.06–3.77, P = 0.030) and PE (OR = 2.80, 95% CI:1.09–7.18, P = 0.030). (Fig. 2)

Sensitivity analyses using the weighted median, weighted mode, MR-Egger regression, and MR-PRESSO methods (Table S3) and the results generally supported the consistency of the IVW estimates in terms of direction and effect size. The MR-Egger intercept test showed no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy, indicating that the genetic instruments used in this study were not affected by horizontal pleiotropy. Additionally, MR-PRESSO outlier detection and leave-one-out analyses did not identify any single SNP driving the observed associations, further supporting the robustness of the findings.

Table S4 presents the results of tests for horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity, further validating the effectiveness of the genetic instruments used. These tests confirm that the assumptions underlying MR in this study were not violated. These findings support our causal inference hypothesis, confirming that the observed associations likely reflect causal relationships rather than being driven by confounding factors.

Socioeconomic traits

When EDU, average total INC before tax, and engagement in heavy manual or physical labor were modeled jointly, higher EDU emerged as an independent protective factor for several outcomes. Specifically, each standard deviation (SD) increase in genetically predicted education was associated with a reduced risk of PD (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.37–0.91, P = 0.020), GD (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.35–0.96, P = 0.030), and PRM (OR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.24–0.81, P = 0.010), independent of other socioeconomic traits.

Reproductive traits

When AFS and AFB were analyzed together, AFS demonstrated an independent association with an increased risk of PSP (OR = 1.86, 95% CI: 1.03–3.36, P = 0.040). This finding suggests that earlier initiation of sexual activity, independent of the timing of first birth, may contribute to elevated placental complication risk.

Taken together, our findings indicate that EDU functions primarily as a long-term protective factor for maternal health. While highly educated women may experience increased situational stress during pregnancy, such short-term exposures should be conceptually distinguished from the cumulative benefits of education across the life course. Failure to differentiate these levels of influence may partially explain the inconsistent associations reported in previous observational studies.

Mediation analysis of EDU and AFS on pregnancy complications Given that multivariable MR identified EDU and AFS as the principal independent factors within the socioeconomic and reproductive domains, respectively, we implemented two-step MR mediation to examine their interrelationship and joint effects on eight pregnancy complications. Two models were tested: (i) EDU as the exposure, AFS as the mediator (EDU→AFS→Outcome), and (ii) AFS as the exposure, EDU as the mediator (AFS→EDU→Outcome).

When AFS mediated the association between EDU and outcomes, significant indirect effects were observed for SA, PD, GD, and PSP, with mediation proportions of 65%, 61%, 57% and 55%, respectively. All effects were negative, indicating that higher EDU reduced the risk of these complications partly through delaying sexual debut. In the reverse direction, when EDU acted as the mediator, mediation proportions were smaller (24% for SA, 26% for PD, 28% for GD, and 29% for PSP) but showed consistent effect directions. This pattern suggests a reciprocal influence between EDU and AFS, with partially overlapping causal pathways impacting pregnancy complication risk. For clarity, mediation proportions are reported as absolute values, with the direction (negative mediation) specified in the text; 95% CIs are obtained via the delta method. Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the causal impacts of socioeconomic structural determinants and reproductive milestones on a comprehensive panel of eight pregnancy complications. Our primary univariable analyses demonstrated that higher EDU, higher INC, later AFS, and delayed AFB were significantly associated with reduced risks of several major gestational pathologies, whereas engagement in PDW explicitly elevated the risks of PD and PE.

Recent genetic epidemiological studies have documented that genetically predicted lower EDU increases the risk of adverse obstetric outcomes, including pre-eclampsia and GD [23, 24], At first glance, these findings may appear inconsistent with observational reports suggesting elevated psychosocial stress or metabolic burden among highly educated women during pregnancy. However, this apparent discrepancy is largely attributable to differences in causal interpretation. Observational associations are vulnerable to residual confounding and often capture short-term, context-specific exposures, whereas MR estimates the cumulative, lifelong effect of education. From this perspective, the adverse outcomes reported among highly educated women are more likely driven by situational pressures during pregnancy rather than a direct detrimental effect of EDU itself.

In previous observational studies, only lower levels of education were found to be associated with PD [25], There are also studies suggesting that additional education during pregnancy may be beneficial to maternal and fetal health [26], GD has traditionally been defined as the onset of glucose intolerance or the first detection of abnormal glucose intolerance in pregnancy, and is increasingly recognized as a risk factor for cardiovascular metabolism in both mother and offspring [27].In our study, we found that lower levels of EDU were more likely to cause GD, which may be due to the fact that women with higher levels of education were more likely to be in a state of anxiety and depression [28] .It has also been shown that women with GD are older, more EDU and have higher INC, which is consistent with our study [29]. In our study, we identified that lower EDU is causally associated with an increased liability to GD, a finding heavily supported by recent large-scale genetic epidemiological evidence [14, 30]. This causal link is highly plausible from a socioeconomic and behavioral perspective. Individuals with higher EDU typically possess greater health literacy, which facilitates precise adherence to optimized nutritional and lifestyle behaviors during active gestation, alongside maintaining a lower baseline BMI—factors that have been robustly identified in mediation frameworks as pivotal modifiable mechanisms mitigating metabolic and glucose dysfunction [31, 32]. Conversely, while some traditional observational literature suggests that highly educated cohorts face unique psychosocial stressors or distinct diagnostic access, our multivariable genetic evidence firmly establishes that the holistic lifelong socioeconomic disadvantage indexed by lower education represents the true, robust causal driver of gestational metabolic risk, fundamentally outweighing any localized psychological counter-currents.

PRM defined as rupture of membranes before 37 weeks of gestation, occurs in approximately 2–4% of all pregnancies and 40% of all spontaneous preterm births [33, 34], The pathogenesis is complex and includes inflammation and oxidative stress [35], However PRM can lead to preterm labor and affect subsequent development, resulting in physical, psychological and economic burdens. In our study, we found that lower levels of EDU were more likely to lead to PRM, which is in line with previous studies [36], This was also confirmed in a controlled study analyzing the causes of miscarriage and factors influencing it in older mothers [37]. We also found that lower levels of EDU were associated with PD, although many studies have now shown that higher levels of EDU are more likely to lead to PD [38, 39]., However, there are also studies showing that higher levels of EDU are negatively associated with PD [40]. Although direct clinical modifications of maternal EDU are unfeasible during active gestation, targeted prenatal educational interventions and tailored counseling can be dynamically offered to high-risk demographic strata [41].

In this study, we identified robust associations between univariate factors—EDU, AFS—and multiple pregnancy complications, with consistent trends indicating their influence on complication risk. Our analyses aimed to objectively disentangle the relationships between EDU, AFS, and adverse pregnancy outcomes, while also probing the interconnection between these two exposures. Our mediation framework inherently estimates the EDU→AFS pathway, which is directionally consistent with prior observational studies [42], indicating that higher EDU is generally associated with later sexual debut.

By applying mediation analyses while adjusting for other exposures, we found that AFS partially mediated the effect of EDU on pregnancy complication risk. This suggests that higher EDU may reduce the risk of complications partly through its influence on the timing of sexual debut. The substantial mediation proportions observed support the hypothesis that enhancing sexual education quality via prolonged schooling, thereby delaying AFS, could be an effective preventive strategy. AFS thus represents a key intermediate factor in the causal pathway linking EDU to pregnancy complications [43, 44].

Notably, a directional discrepancy emerged regarding the causal effect of AFS on PSP between our analytical frameworks. While univariable and mediation analyses suggested a total protective effect of later AFS against PSP (β= -0.18), this association reversed into an independent risk factor (OR = 1.86)in the multivariable model after controlling for educational attainment. This reversal strongly indicates a suppression effect (negative mediation). The crude protective correlation observed in univariable analysis is likely confounded by the robust positive correlation between a delayed AFS and higher EDU, the latter being a powerful protective determinant. Once this overriding socioeconomic confounding is rigorously controlled, the residual, independent biological or behavioral pathways unique to a later AFS—such as age-related psychological stress or unmeasured lifestyle factors—manifest as a distinct risk pattern. This complex interplay underscores that the biological net effect of reproductive timing requires cautious interpretation once stripped of its socioeconomic context.

From a public health perspective, elucidating and modifying this mediation pathway offers actionable prevention and intervention opportunities. Strengthening educational interventions early in life may yield dual benefits: postponing sexual debut and improving contraceptive uptake, thereby reducing the risk of pregnancy-related morbidity [45]. EDU can also enhance knowledge and comprehension of sexual and reproductive health, empowering individuals to make informed reproductive decisions [46]. Moreover, our findings align with prior reports showing that populations with poorer economic and educational conditions tend to have higher maternal morbidity and mortality due to pregnancy and childbirth complications. This underscores the urgency of implementing comprehensive, population-level educational initiatives early in life, not only to prevent pregnancy complications but also to reduce maternal mortality.

It was determined in our study that multiple exposure outcomes can cause postnatal depression under unifactorial circumstances. Therefore PD which is one of the most dangerous complications of pregnancy, must be managed. Social status is influenced by a range of factors, including income, type of occupation, level of education, and reproductive characteristics [47, 48]. PD can lead to various complications and negatively affect the physical health of women in later life, indicating a significant concern [49].

Our study has strengths and limitations. By utilizing an MR design, we minimized confounding risks, which often impact observational studies. However, despite the extensive sample size, numerous genetic instruments utilized for exposure and outcome assessment experienced varying degrees of low statistical efficiency and unclear phenotypes. Such limitations have the potential to discredit the findings of most correlation studies.

From a conceptual standpoint, genetic instruments for socioeconomic traits proxy an individual’s lifelong socio-behavioral liability rather than coding for institutional social constructs directly. These variants reflect polygenic behavioral and cognitive propensities that systematically shape socioeconomic trajectories, an analytical framework well-established in social genetic epidemiology. While horizontal pleiotropy diagnostics showed no systematic violations of core assumptions in our data, we explicitly acknowledge that complex social MR estimates can capture composite effects of direct causal pathways and intergenerational genetic nurture or dynastic confounding [50, 51]. Consequently, these findings should be interpreted as genetically conditioned lifelong liabilities influencing obstetric risks.

Several limitations warrant caution. First, our reliance on summary-level statistics precluded granular stratification by age or sex, as well as a thorough exploration of gene-environment correlations. Second, while restricting the analysis to cohorts of European descent effectively minimized population stratification bias, it inherently restricts the external generalizability of our causal inferences to diverse ancestral populations. Third, although utilizing passive registry linkages substantially minimizes individual recall bias compared to traditional self-reports, case ascertainment remains fundamentally bound to variations in regional diagnostic coding habits and localized healthcare accessibility across different biobank cohorts. Finally, despite our deliberate efforts to employ independent GWAS cohorts to minimize sample overlap and bias, and our implementation of comprehensive sensitivity testing that revealed no systematic violations, residual horizontal pleiotropy cannot be entirely discounted.

Conclusions

This study provides compelling evidence of the causal relationship between EDU and the risk of pregnancy complications. Specifically, lower educational levels are linked to a higher likelihood of GD, PRM, and PD. Furthermore, we identified that AFS plays a crucial role in mediating the effects of EDU on pregnancy outcomes. These findings underscore the importance of education in influencing reproductive health, highlighting the need for public health strategies that prioritize educational interventions to mitigate the risks associated with pregnancy complications.
