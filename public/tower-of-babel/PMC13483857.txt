Fusion of MRI-radiomics and clinical features to improve pathological T-stage prediction of rectal cancer: a multicenter study
Authors: Honglin Zhu, Jing Sun, Xiaobing Li, Shengdong Nie, Jing Gong, Tong Tong, Xiujuan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeveloping a rational model for precise preoperative staging of rectal cancer using magnetic resonance imaging is crucial for improving the therapeutic effect and prognosis of patients. To investigate the feasibility and efficacy of a T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) based radiomics model combined with clinical features for preoperative T1-2/T3 staging prediction in rectal cancer.MethodsA total of 968 rectal adenocarcinoma patients (T1-2 stage, n = 562; T3 stage, n = 406) were retrospectively enrolled in this study. We randomly divided it into a training cohort (n = 774) and an internal validation cohort (n = 194). Additional 82 patients from another center used as an independent external validation cohort. For each patient, 1210 radiomics features were extracted from T2WI images. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator were applied for feature selection. Support vector machine classifier was used to develop the radiomics model. Six clinical features were collected and selected to build the clinical model using the recursive feature elimination algorithm. A weighted fusion method was applied to combine the radiomics with clinical model to develop the fusion model. The performances of the above three models were evaluated using ROC, calibration, and decision curves.ResultsThe radiomics and clinical model yielded AUC of 0.858 (95% CI, 0.801–0.904) and 0.780 (95% CI, 0.715–0.836) in internal validation cohort, while the fusion model generated higher performance with AUC of 0.875 (95% CI, 0.820–0.918). The external validation cohort further validated the effectiveness of the fusion model with AUC of 0.828 (95% CI, 0.729–0.903). The calibration and decision curves confirmed that the fusion model was well calibrated and had clinical utility.ConclusionsCombined T2WI radiomics features and clinical features modeling can effectively predict T-stage (T1-2/T3) of rectal cancer and provide valuable reference for clinical decision making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16316-4.Keywords: Rectal cancer, Magnetic resonance imaging, T staging, Radiomics, Machine learning

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is one of the most common gastrointestinal malignancies, with rectal cancer accounting for approximately one-third of cases [1, 2]. The prognosis differs substantially by stage: early-stage localized disease has a 5-year survival rate of 90%, whereas this drops to 14% in the presence of distant metastases [3]. For locally advanced rectal cancer (T3/4 and/or N+), preoperative neoadjuvant therapy is recommended, whereas early-stage tumors can be treated with surgery alone [4, 5]. Therefore, accurate preoperative staging is crucial for selecting the optimal treatment strategy.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the preferred modality for primary staging of rectal cancer, with T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) serving as the key sequence for assessing tumor infiltration [6–8]. However, inflammatory reactions, peritumoral exudation, and desmoplastic fibrosis can mimic tumor invasion, leading to over- or under-staging. Accurate differentiation between T2 and T3 stage remains a major diagnostic challenge [9–11]. In routine clinical practice, MRI-based identification of T1-2 versus T3-4 rectal cancer achieves an accuracy of only 67.1%, with a sensitivity of 68.7% and specificity of 76.5% [12].

Radiomics enables quantitative assessment of tumor heterogeneity by extracting high-dimensional features from medical images [13–15]. Several studies have developed T2WI-based radiomics models for rectal cancer T-staging, but most have focused on differentiating pT1-2 from pT3-4 stages, with sample sizes typically below 300, and few have explored the combination with clinical features [9, 10, 16–19]. In this study, we use a large multicenter dataset to build models based on T2WI radiomics features and clinical features, and further fuse them to stage rectal cancer as T1-2 versus T3. Our primary objective is to assist clinicians in deciding whether neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) should be performed before surgery.

Methods

Patients

The research was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center and the requirement for written informed consent was waived. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. In this study, we retrospectively collected data of 1050 patients with rectal cancer from two centers, including Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (between April 2012 and December 2021, Center A) and Nantong Tumor Hospital (between January 2018 and November 2020, Center B). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients underwent surgery (Dixon or Miles) without any other treatment; (2) preoperative rectal MRI examinations (within six weeks before surgery) with both axial non-fat suppressed T2WI and diffusion weighted imaging (DWI); (3) postoperative pathological examination confirmed T1-3 stage rectal adenocarcinoma; (4) tumor location within 15 cm from the anal verge. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) insufficient image quality for T-staging diagnosis or feature extraction; (2) lesions invisible on T2WI or too tiny for segmentation; (3) incomplete image sequence or medical record; (4) patients with pathologically confirmed mucinous adenocarcinoma or signet ring cell carcinoma. Finally, 968 eligible patients from Center A were enrolled and randomly divided into a training cohort (n = 774) and an internal validation cohort (n = 194) at a ratio of 8:2. Stratified random sampling (stratified by pathological T-stage, T1-2 vs. T3) was used to ensure a balanced distribution of T1-2 and T3 cases between the two cohorts. Furthermore, 82 patients from Center B were used as an independent external validation cohort (n = 82).

Baseline clinical data included age, sex, tumor location (distance from anal verge), lesion length, and preoperative carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9) levels. Tumor location was measured from the lowest tumor edge to the anus on sagittal T2WI (Fig. 1 illustrates the measurement method) and categorized as lower (0–5 cm), middle (5–10 cm), or upper (10–15 cm) [20]. CEA and CA19-9 were dichotomized using clinical thresholds (5.2 ng/ml and 27.0 U/ml, respectively).All measurements were performed by experienced radiologists who underwent consensus pre‑training to standardise landmark identification. On sagittal T2WI, the anal verge and the lower tumor edge are typically well‑defined, even for low rectal tumors; cases with ambiguous boundaries were reviewed to reach consensus, ensuring good inter‑reader measurement consistency.

To mitigate the variability arising from multi‑scanner and multi‑parameter acquisitions, the following standardisation steps were applied: (1) intensity normalisation of all images to zero mean and unit variance; (2) resampling to a uniform isotropic voxel size of 0.7188 × 0.7188 × 1.5 mm³ using cubic B‑spline interpolation; (3) Z‑score standardisation of all extracted radiomics features.

A radiologist blinded to pathology results manually delineated the entire volume of interest (VOI) on axial T2WI using ITK-SNAP [21], with DWI as reference, avoiding intraluminal contents or gas. For inter-reader reproducibility, 229 random cases were segmented independently by a second radiologist. The mean Dice similarity coefficient was 0.901 ± 0.133, and the intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) for the selected features ranged from 0.840 to 0.973, indicating good to excellent agreement.

Radiomics feature extraction and selection

The overall radiomics workflow is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Radiomics features were extracted using PyRadiomics [22] from the manually segmented VOIs on axial T2WI. A total of 1210 features were extracted, including shape, first-order statistics, and texture features (GLCM, GLRLM, GLSZM, GLDM, NGTDM), derived from original, Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG, σ = 2–5), and wavelet-transformed images.

Features were normalized using z-score standardization. Dimensionality reduction and feature selection were performed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) with ten-fold cross-validation repeated three times.

For clinical features, continuous variables were compared using Mann-Whitney U test or independent t-test based on normality; categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. Features with significant differences (P < 0.050) were further refined using recursive feature elimination (RFE) with ten-fold cross-validation.

Classification model development

To address class imbalance in the training cohort, synthetic minority oversampling (SMOTE) [23] was applied exclusively to the training cohort to balance T1-2 and T3 samples (1:1). Support vector machine (SVM) classifiers with radial basis function (RBF), linear, and polynomial kernels were optimized using GridSearchCV with five-fold cross-validation repeated three times. Radiomics and clinical models were developed independently using the same process.

SVM was chosen as the primary classifier because of its well‑established advantages with high‑dimensional radiomics features: it is less prone to overfitting through margin maximisation, can model complex non‑linear relationships via kernel functions, and has shown robust generalisation in similar studies. A comprehensive comparison of seven classifiers on the training and internal validation cohorts is provided in Supplementary Table S1, confirming that SVM achieved the highest validation AUC with the smallest overfitting gap and the most clinically balanced sensitivity and specificity.

Hyperparameters were tuned using GridSearchCV with stratified 5-fold cross-validation repeated three times (shuffle enabled, random_state fixed), and model performance was evaluated by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The search space was defined as follows:

Statistical analysis

The discrimination performance of the models in predicting T-Stage was evaluated by plotting receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculating the area under the curve (AUC). We used the bootstrap resampling procedures with 1000 iterations to estimate the 95% confidence interval (95% CI). The corresponding accuracy (Acc), sensitivity (Sens), specificity (Spec), precision, F1 score were then calculated. We used a cutoff threshold of 0.5 to convert the predicted probability of the model into binary classification results. The DeLong test was used to statistically compare the AUCs between the models. The calibration curves were used to evaluate the fitness between the model predictions and the actual values, and their performance was compared using Brier score. The decision curve analysis (DCA) [24] was performed to determine the clinical usefulness of the fusion model by quantifying the net benefits at different threshold probabilities.

All statistical analysis was performed with Python (version 3.7.12, https://www.python.org/), SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27, New York) and MedCalc software (version 20.022, https://www.medcalc.org/). Several python packages were also used in our research, such as SimpleITK, SciPy, Scikit-learn [25], NumPy, Pandas etc. P < 0.050 was considered statistically significant using two-tailed testing. The two-way random absolute agreement ICC were calculated to evaluate the inter-observer variability in delineating the VOIs and extracting features (ICC < 0.50, poor agreement; 0.50 ≤ ICC < 0.75, moderate agreement; 0.75 ≤ ICC < 0.90, good agreement; ICC > 0.90, excellent agreement) [26].

Optimal radiomics and clinical features

Sixteen radiomics features were selected using LASSO, with coefficients shown in Fig. 3. The selected features were predominantly derived from LoG and wavelet-transformed images, and included first-order statistics, GLCM, shape, and GLSZM features. Inter-reader reproducibility was excellent: ICCs for these features ranged from 0.840 to 0.973. The two shape features (Maximum2DDiameterSlice, LeastAxisLength) indicated larger axial diameters in T3 tumors, consistent with deeper infiltration.

Boxplot of the selected radiomics features

After univariate screening and RFE with 3-time 10-fold cross-validation, the following four clinical features were finally enrolled in the clinical model: (1) tumor location (distance from the anal verge, continuous) (2), lesion length (continuous) (3), preoperative CEA level (continuous), and (4) preoperative CA19-9 level (continuous). Age and sex were excluded because they showed no significant between-group difference or contributed little to improving model performance.

Performance of classification models

The radiomics model achieved AUCs of 0.915 (95% CI: 0.893–0.934) in the training cohort and 0.858 (95% CI: 0.801–0.904) in internal validation. The clinical model performed significantly worse, with AUCs of 0.795 (0.764–0.822) and 0.780 (0.715–0.836), respectively. The fusion model, constructed by weighted combination of the two model outputs (ω = 0.55), yielded improved performance in internal validation (AUC = 0.875, 95% CI: 0.820–0.918), outperforming both the radiomics model (P = 0.253, DeLong test) and the clinical model (P = 0.032). Discussion

In this study, we developed SVM-based radiomics and clinical models, along with a weighted fusion model, to predict T1-2 versus T3 stage rectal cancer using a large multicenter dataset. The fusion model outperformed both individual models, achieving the highest AUC in internal (0.875) and external (0.828) validation, demonstrating the complementary value of combining radiomics with clinical features.

Compared with existing T2WI-based radiomics studies for rectal cancer T-staging (Table 4), our fusion model achieved superior or comparable performance with a more balanced sensitivity and specificity. Prior studies typically focused on differentiating T1-2 from T3-4, used smaller sample sizes (≤ 706), and often employed single-institution data with uniform imaging protocols [9, 10, 17, 18]. In contrast, our dataset included images from two centers with multiple scanner types and heterogeneous acquisition parameters, which enhances model generalizability. Furthermore, while some studies have combined radiomics with clinical features [9], our output-weighted fusion approach avoided feature overfitting and preserved the independent contributions of each modality.

Most selected radiomics features originated from LoG and wavelet-transformed images, which are known to capture tumor heterogeneity and edge characteristics. First-order and GLCM features dominated the selection, consistent with their reported robustness [27, 28]. Two shape features (Maximum2DDiameterSlice, LeastAxisLength) were also included, reflecting the larger axial dimensions of T3 tumors, which aligns with deeper infiltration. All selected features demonstrated high inter-reader reproducibility (ICCs > 0.75), supporting their reliability.

Among clinical features, tumor location, CEA, and CA19-9 were used as continuous variables to avoid information loss from discretization; this approach yielded better model performance than using categorical thresholds. The optimal classifier for both radiomics and clinical features was SVM with RBF kernel, indicating its ability to model complex relationships. For fusion, we adopted an output-weighted strategy rather than feature concatenation, which avoids feature selection bias and overfitting, and improved performance without requiring model retraining.

The staging of rectal cancer directly influences the choice of treatment strategy, specifically whether nCRT should be administered before surgery. nCRT is recommended for advanced rectal tumors (T3-4) to improve local control and reduce the risk of recurrence. However, if inappropriately applied to early-stage rectal tumors (T1-2), it may impose unnecessary financial burdens on patients and potentially elicit adverse effects. Unfortunately, T2 and T3 tumors often appear similar on MRI, which can confuse radiologists and lead to suboptimal staging accuracy. Hence, we endeavor to employ radiomics techniques to achieve accurate differentiation of primary T-stage of rectal cancer. The constructed model improves the accuracy of predicting the T1-2/T3 stage of rectal cancer, and helps to select the optimal clinical treatment plan and avoid under- or over-treatment.

This study has several limitations. First, we only used T2WI images, but in fact, images of other modalities such as DWI and ADC can also provide tumor stage information [19, 29]. In subsequent research, we may consider using multimodal radiomics features to enhance model performance. Second, the VOI of the tumor was delineated entirely manually, which is time‑consuming and highly operator‑dependent, limiting the large‑scale clinical applicability of radiomics. In future work, we plan to explore deep‑learning‑based semi‑automatic or fully automatic segmentation algorithms to improve efficiency and reproducibility. The current manually annotated data can serve as a reliable “gold standard” for developing and validating such automated methods. Third, we only analyze the tumor area, but expanding the VOI to the rectum or mesorectum for analysis can incorporate more peritumoral information and avoid precise delineation [30]. Therefore, multi-regional radiomics models can be implemented in the future. Fourth, this paper only analyzes the use of baseline images to predict T1-2/T3 stage of rectal cancer in order to select subsequent treatment options. However, this excluded some preoperatively treated high-risk T3 tumors that were more aggressive or had a poor prognosis.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed two prediction models: a T2WI radiomics model and a clinical feature-based model. To improve the model performance, we fused the two types of models to develop a weighted fusion model. The results demonstrated that fusion model can effectively predict preoperative T-stage (T1-2/T3) of rectal cancer, which can be used to assist oncologist in their clinical decision making.
