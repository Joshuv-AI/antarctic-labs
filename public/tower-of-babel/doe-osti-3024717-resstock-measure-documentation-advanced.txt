Executive Summary
The goal of this work is to develop energy efficiency, demand flexibility, and other retrofit end-
use load shapes (electricity, gas, propane, or fuel oil).

An “end-use savings shape” is the difference in energy consumption between a baseline building
and a building with an energy efficiency, demand flexibility, or other retrofit measure applied.
This results in a time-series profile broken down by end use and fuel (electricity or on-site gas,
propane, or fuel oil use) at each time step.

ResStock™ is a highly granular, physics-based, bottom-up model that uses multiple data
sources, statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the
annual subhourly energy consumption of the residential building stock across the United States.
The baseline model is intended to represent the U.S. residential building stock as it existed in
2018. The methodology and results of the baseline model are discussed in the final technical
report of the End-Use Load Profiles project1 (Wilson, et al. 2022).

This documentation focuses on a single end-use savings shape measure: advanced air sealing
with drill-and-fill wall insulation.

The advanced air sealing with drill-and-fill wall insulation upgrade demonstrated 22% total site
energy savings for the U.S. residential building stock (see Figure 3 for more details). These
savings are primarily attributed to:
 • 32% stock electricity heating savings
 • 37% stock natural gas heating savings
 • 39% stock fuel oil heating savings
 • 32% stock propane heating savings
 • 11% stock electric cooling savings.

1 Technology Summary
1.1 Introduction to Drill-and-Fill Insulation
Drill-and-fill insulation is a method used to insulate existing walls. The most common drill-and-
fill insulation materials are cellulose, fiberglass, and spray foam. For cellulose and fiberglass,
there are two main types of drill-and-fill installation: loose fill (also known as blown-in) and
dense pack, which requires professional equipment (North 2012). This measure upgrade models
cellulose as the drill-and-fill insulation material.

Drill-and-fill insulation can be installed from either the inside or outside of the building. In
multifamily buildings, drilling from the outside is more common because it allows occupants to
remain in place during the work is being performed, and reduces asbestos risk for installers (U.S.
Environmental Protection Agency 2011) (Antonopoulos, et al. 2019). For these reasons, external
drill-and-fill will be the focus of this upgrade. Drilling from the interior may be preferable in
certain situations, such as inclement weather, when siding is difficult to remove, or when the
home is vacant. Figure 1 shows a photo of a drill-and-fill retrofit in progress.

 Figure 1. Drill-and-fill insulation
 Photo by Dennis Schroeder, NLR # 28786

In a drill-and-fill retrofit, small round holes (typically 1- to 2-inch diameter) are drilled into the
exterior wall of the home between the studs (e.g., every 16 inches) (U.S. Department of Energy
2022). A hose is inserted into the wall, and insulation is then blown in. Both the small round
holes and the hose are visible in Figure 1.

Exterior wall studs in residential construction commonly have three widths: 2x3, 2x4, and 2x6.
The 2x3 and 2x4 studs are more common in mobile/manufactured homes and older residential
construction, whereas 2x6 studs are more frequently used in newer residential construction
(Krigger 2006) (Kochkin and Wiehagan 2014). Mobile/manufactured homes may use 2x2 studs,
but these are typically only for interior walls (Dickerhoof 1977). This ResStock 2025 Release 1
assumes that R-13 insulation can fit within a 2x4 wall. In cases where higher levels of insulation
are installed, OS-HPXML selects the appropriate wall construction set to ensure the desired R-
value is achieved. This may include changing the stud cavity depth—for example, changing a

 2

wall from a 2x4 to a 2x6 to meet the requirement. More information on this topic can be found
on the ResStock “Wood Stud Depth and Wall R-Value Calculations” page.2

When installing drill-and-fill insulation from the exterior of a building, the cladding material is
an important consideration. Exterior wood or vinyl siding is sometimes partially removed to
allow holes to be drilled in the exterior sheathing. Wood siding can also be drilled through and
then patched. Some types of metal siding may be removable, while others may not be (Allwein
and Biddle 2002). In homes with face brick (4-inch) as cladding over wood stud walls, drill-and-
fill can be performed by drilling smaller holes through mortar joints. Other construction types,
such as concrete masonry units (CMUs) or steel framing, are limited to insulation methods other
than drill-and-fill for retrofits (U.S. Department of Energy 2019).

Other ways to insulate a wall include adding rigid insulation board to the exterior or interior, or
removing interior finishes and adding batt insulation, among others (Baechler, et al. 2021)
(Antonopoulos, et al. 2019). Attic, crawlspace, basement, or other building insulation techniques
are not covered in this measure. Attic floor insulation for unfinished basements is addressed in a
separate measure documentation.3

There are safety concerns that must be addressed when performing drill-and-fill insulation. The
structural integrity of the exterior wall and cladding is important, as is any existing water damage
(Pfeiffer 2021). Asbestos is a health and safety concern for insulation installers and is more
common in homes built between 1930 and 1970 (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency 2011).
Mold or rotted wood may be found in stud cavities, which could also complicate retrofits
(Brozyna and Badger 2013). This is not a complete list of safety concerns that should be
addressed before upgrading a building’s insulation.

1.2 Drill-and-Fill Considerations
The thermal resistance of drill-and-fill insulation is measured by its R-value, which represents
the material’s ability to reduce heat flow (U.S. Department of Energy 2010). A higher R-value
results in less heat transfer. As a retrofit, drill-and-fill wall insulation can achieve up to R-13
cavity insulation in 2x4 exterior stud cavities (3.5 inches at R-3.7 per inch), which is the
maximum insulation level modeled in this measure (Holladay 2013).

Wall cavities that are insulated to levels less than R-13 (e.g., R-11, R-7) did not receive the drill-
and-fill upgrade in this ResStock measure. An experienced drill-and-fill practitioner noted that
installing additional drill-and-fill insulation into an already insulated wall could cause the
insulation batt to bunch, create uneven insulation levels, and lead to other issues. This is
supported by literature on retrofit projects that choose not to perform drill-and-fill insulation on
walls with existing insulation below R-13 (Frontier Energy, Inc., Misti Bruceri & Associates,
LLC 2022) (Antonopoulos, et al. 2022). However, there is some evidence that drill-and-fill can
be applied to walls with existing insulation, but this is not included in this measure (Holladay
2013).

Manufactured housing or mobile homes receive drill-and-fill insulation in this measure only if
the walls are uninsulated and wood stud. In practice, this can be extremely difficult and requires
caution (Carlson and Bemis 1979) (Judkoff, Hancock and Franconi 1990). As with all ResStock
results, this upgrade measure represents theoretical technical potential, and dataset users should
independently assess whether this measure is appropriate for their building segment of interest.

Studies often pair air sealing and drill-and-fill insulation and report combined energy savings.
One study modeled energy savings from drill-and-fill insulation across different climate zones
and found whole-home site energy savings ranging from 8.8% to 13.5% (Polly, et al. 2011).
Across the literature, drill-and-fill insulation upgrades are frequently paired with air sealing
measures (U.S. Department of Energy 2025) (Brozyna and Badger 2013) (Roberts and
Stephenson 2012). More information on air sealing modeling can be found in the advanced air
sealing documentation (Phoung, Lou and White 2025).

For modeling purposes, drill-and-fill insulation is assumed to be installed properly to a Grade 1
insulation level. According to the RESNET Grading system, Grade 1 assumes installation
follows the manufacturer’s instructions and fills the entire wall cavity ( Construction Instruction
Experience Center n.d.). However, a field study found that only half of all homes had Grade 1
insulation (North American Insulation Manufacturers Association 2017). In practice, a Grade 1
assumption may be optimistic, and insulation inspection should be conducted carefully (North
American Insulation Manufacturers Association 2017). This optimistic assumption about
proper insulation installation is a limitation of the work, and may cause modeled results to
be different than actual building retrofit results.

In summary, this upgrade measure assumes:
 • Advanced air sealing is applied to dwelling units with ACH50 infiltration rates of 6 or
 greater. This applies to 92% of all dwelling units and heavily influences the results.
 • Drill-and-fill insulation is applied to about 30% of dwelling units only
 • Drill-and-fill insulation uses cellulose up to R-13 on exterior above-grade walls that
 separate conditioned from unconditioned spaces only
 • Existing wall cavities with no insulation will receive the insulation, and walls with
 insulation less than R-13 will not
 • Proper installation is assumed, similar to Grade 1 RESNET installation.

1.3 Advanced Air Sealing Summary
As previously mentioned, drill-and-fill wall insulation is often installed along with air sealing
during retrofit projects. Therefore, this drill-and-fill insulation package is modeled with air
sealing. Full documentation for the advanced air sealing component of this package is reported
separately, but a summary is provided below (Phoung, Lou and White 2025).

Air sealing can take various forms, including weatherstripping, caulking, and spray foam. Two
common ways to describe air leakage rates are air changes per hour at 50 pascals (ACH50) and
cubic feet per minute at 50 pascals (CFM50). ACH50 is used to compare air leakage rates
between dwelling units of different sizes, and this metric will be used in this package.

 4

All dwelling units with an infiltration rate above 6 ACH50 will receive advanced air sealing and
then have an infiltration rate of 5 ACH50. This level of advanced air sealing, 5 ACH50, should
be considered very aggressive, similar to bringing all residential air leakage down to IECC 2021
new construction levels (3-5 ACH50 depending on climate). In reality, bringing infiltration rates
down to this level may require additional enclosure upgrades, such as over cladding of walls,
replacing windows, or other techniques that go beyond traditional air sealing practices. This
applies to 92% of all dwelling units (Phoung, Lou and White 2025).

3 Modeling Approach
This section explains how advanced air sealing with drill-and-fill wall insulation is functionally
modeled in ResStock using OpenStudio-HPXML.

Electrical panel constraint modeling was added to ResStock via OpenStudio-HPXML measures
in 2025 to assess whether the existing panel has enough capacity or breaker space to
accommodate new loads from an energy retrofit. For details on panel constraint modeling, refer
to the ResStock Technical Reference Guide (Reyna, Fontanini and Parker, et al. 2025). This
measure does not introduce new electrical loads or circuits, so it has no impact on the electrical
panel. However, electrical panel metrics are included as a standard output for this and all other
envelope measures. Any panel constraints appearing in the upgrade results reflect pre-existing
conditions, as this measure does not worsen them.

3.1 OpenStudio-HPXML Overview
The ResStock building stock energy model includes conditional probability distributions of
building characteristics that represent the U.S. residential building stock (Reyna, Fontanini and
Present, et al. 2024). These distributions are sampled to create a synthetic stock, with each
sample an option for each characteristic. ResStock uses the OpenStudio-HPXML (OS-HPXML)
workflow to translate these dwelling characteristics into building models that can be simulated
via OpenStudio® and EnergyPlus® using an HPXML (Home Performance eXtensible Markup
Language) file. The state of the OpenStudio-HPXML repository at the time the ResStock 2025
Release 1 dataset was created is available on the NLR ResStock Resources page on GitHub.5

3.2 ResStock Inputs for Advanced Air Sealing with Drill-and-Fill Wall
 Insulation
Building characteristics are assigned in ResStock and then translated into more detailed OS-
HPXML arguments that describe a ResStock parameter. The drill-and-fill wall insulation
measure leverages the “Insulation Wall” ResStock option that assigns wall assembly R-values

4.2 Stock Energy Impacts for Applicable Dwelling Units Only
This section presents results for the full ResStock 2025 Release 1, which includes 550,000
modeled samples. Advanced air sealing with drill-and-fill wall insulation was applicable to 92%
of dwelling units. In theory, advanced air-sealing was applicable to 92.2% of baseline dwelling
units, drill-and-fill wall insulation to 31% of baseline dwelling units, and about 30% of units
could receive both measures as shown in Table 3. Below are results for only the applicable
dwelling units.

4.2.1 Annual Stock Energy Analysis
The average baseline applicable dwelling unit consumed 24.51 MWh of site energy per year.
After air sealing and drill-and-fill wall insulation were applied to applicable units, the average
dwelling unit consumed 19.20 MWh of site energy per year. This represents an average 21.7%
decrease.

Figure 3 shows the average energy consumption by end use per dwelling unit before and after the
upgrade.

 Figure 3. Average energy consumption by end use group per applicable dwelling unit
Before the upgrade, the largest two end uses in the average dwelling unit were natural gas
heating and electric cooling. Other notable end uses included miscellaneous electric loads,
electric appliances, and natural gas hot water heating. Although these patterns remain in the

upgrade scenario, total site energy use is 22% lower. Significant savings are seen in heating and
cooling end uses, particularly electric heating (35%), natural gas heating (40%), fuel oil heating
(41%), propane heating (36%), electric cooling (11%), electric heating fan pumps (37%), and
electric heat pump backup (54%). Slight savings are observed in the electric cooling fans and
pumps (8%). End uses like water heating, lighting consumption, and miscellaneous electricity

The applicability logic states that only uninsulated wood stud walls are eligible for drill-and-fill
insulation, whereas all dwelling units with an infiltration rate of 6 ACH50 or higher are eligible
for advanced air sealing. This logic is clearly reflected in the results: uninsulated wood stud walls
show an average site energy savings of 32%. All other wall type and insulation combinations
show site energy savings ranging from 8% to 20%. Note that these results are more heavily
influenced by advanced air sealing, as its applicability exceeds 90%, whereas drill-and-fill
insulation applies to only about 30% of dwelling units.
