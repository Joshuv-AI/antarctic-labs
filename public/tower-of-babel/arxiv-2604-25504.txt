The Equivalence of Causal and Noncausal State Information on Bipartite Networks With State-Cognizant Receivers
Amos Lapidoth, Baohua Ni, Ligong Wang

Abstract
State-dependent bipartite networks with state-cognizant receivers and state-informed transmitters are studied. Such networks have no nodes that both transmit and receive. Examples are the multi-access channel, the broadcast channel, and the interference channel. Without computing the capacity region of the network, it is shown that if the state sequence is ergodic and autonomous, and if, conditionally on the state sequence, the network law is memoryless, then the network capacity region does not depend on whether the state information is provided to the encoders causally or noncausally.



I
Introduction

The study of state-dependent channels began shortly after the
inception of Information Theory. Early work by Shannon

[
1
]
established the single-user capacity for the causal
case, where the state sequence is independent and identically
distributed (IID), and where the transmitter is cognizant of the
past-and-present states. He showed that the capacity is achieved by
what we now call “Shannon strategies.” The case where the
transmitter is informed of the states noncausally was solved by
Gel’fand and Pinsker
[
2
]
. More involved results and a
detailed historical progression of channel coding with states can be
found in the survey paper
[
3
]
. See also

[
5
]
for some results on a class of state-dependent
broadcast channels in which the channel law from the strong receiver to
the weak receiver does not depend on the state. (For this class,
Steinberg fully characterized the capacity region in two cases: when
the non-causal state information is provided to both the encoder and
the nondegraded decoder, and when causal state information is
available only to the encoder.)

For the single-user channel with a state-cognizant receiver, causal
and noncausal state information at the transmitter are equally
beneficial (in terms of capacity); see, e.g.,
[
4
, Theorem
1]
. This was shown to also apply to some
multi-access channels (namely, those of a double state of
independent components)
[
4
, Theorem 5]
. Such results
are usually proved by computing the two capacities and showing that
they coincide.

But what about more intricate networks, such as the
Interference Channel or the general Broadcast Channel, where the two capacity
regions are not known? Are the two regions still equal? This question
is answered here in the affirmative using a proof technique that
bypasses the need for explicit expressions for the two capacities.
This result holds
for all state-dependent
bipartite
networks (e.g. the Multiple
Access Channel, the Broadcast Channel, or the Interference Channel,
but not the Relay Channel or the Two-Way Channel),
in which all nodes are either transmitters or receivers (but never
both). In fact—provided that the state sequence is unaffected by
the channel inputs—the
state sequence need not be IID; it suffices that it be ergodic, or at least that it satisfy the
weak law of large numbers. We do, however, assume that, conditional on the state sequence, the
network is memoryless.

III
Main Result and Proof

Theorem 1
.

The capacity region of a bipartite network, where all receivers are state-cognizant, is the same in the causal and noncausal settings:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\textnormal{c}}=\mathcal{C}_{\textnormal{nc}}.

(8)

Proof:

It suffices to show that

\mathcal{C}_{\textnormal{nc}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\textnormal{c}}
, as
the reverse inclusion holds because every causal encoding strategy
can also be employed with noncausal state information. Let the rates

\left(R_{\sigma}\right)_{\sigma\in[1:\Sigma]}

\mathcal{C}_{\textnormal{nc}}
and hence, given any
p>0
, for
sufficiently large

, there exist

5

3

. We will show
that, given any

\left(R_{\sigma}/(1+2\delta)\right)_{\sigma\in[1:\Sigma]}

achievable with causal state information, i.e., that, for this rate

, there exist
causal

encoding functions of the form (
4
), together with
corresponding decoding functions

\mathrm{Pr}_{\textnormal{c}}\{\mathcal{E}\}\leq 3p

By (

\displaystyle\Pr\left\{S^{n}\in\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{\delta}(P_{S})\right\}>\frac{1}{2},

Expressing

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}_{\textnormal{nc}}\left\{\mathcal{E}\right\}=\sum_{s^{n}\in\mathcal{S}^{\times n}}\mathrm{Pr}_{\textnormal{nc}}\left\{\mathcal{E}\,\middle|\,S^{n}=s^{n}\right\}\cdot\Pr\left\{S^{n}=s^{n}\right\}

(19)

if

We now turn to the decoders. Denote

\mathcal{A}\triangleq\left\{\textnormal{every $s\in\mathcal{S}$ appears at least $n\tilde{P}_{S}(s)$ times in $S^{(1+2\delta)n}$}\right\}.

(20)

By (

is close to

, for sufficiently large

to happen is at least

.
If
\mathcal{A}
is false, then all decoders declare an error. If
\mathcal{A}
is true, then all decoders reorder the channel uses to group the same state realizations together as above. This option is characterized by the mapping

g(i,s^{n})=\bigl(s,N(s_{i}\,|\,s^{i})\bigr).

(21)

For every

, the decoders keep the first

and discard the rest. On the channel outputs that they keep, Decoder

applies

to recover its desired messages. That is,

\phi_{b}\left(y_{b}^{n},s^{n}\right)=\tilde{\phi}_{b}\bigl(\{y_{b,\,g(i,s^{n})}\}_{i\in[1:n]}\bigr).

Provided that
\mathcal{A}
is true, the causal scheme described above has exactly the same error probability as the given noncausal scheme, when the latter is conditional on
S^{n}=\tilde{s}^{n}
, i.e.,

\mathrm{Pr}_{\textnormal{c}}\{\mathcal{E}\,|\,\mathcal{A}\}=\mathrm{Pr}_{\textnormal{nc}}\left\{\mathcal{E}\,\middle|\,S^{n}=\tilde{s}^{n}\right\}.

(23)

Indeed, for any
\{M_{\sigma}\}_{\sigma\in[1:\Sigma]}
, after reordering, the channel inputs in the causal case are exactly the same as those in the noncausal case specified to
S^{n}=\tilde{s}^{n}
, following the construction in (

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}_{\textnormal{c}}\{\mathcal{E}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}_{\textnormal{c}}\{\mathcal{E}\,|\,\mathcal{A}\}\cdot\Pr\{\mathcal{A}\}+\mathrm{Pr}_{\textnormal{c}}\{\mathcal{E}\,|\,\mathcal{A}^{\textnormal{c}}\}\cdot\Pr\{\mathcal{A}^{\textnormal{c}}\}

(24)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}_{\textnormal{c}}\{\mathcal{E}\,|\,\mathcal{A}\}+\Pr\{\mathcal{A}^{\textnormal{c}}\}

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}_{\textnormal{nc}}\left\{\mathcal{E}\,\middle|\,S^{n}=\tilde{s}^{n}\right\}+\Pr\{\mathcal{A}^{\textnormal{c}}\}

(26)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2p+p=3p,

(27)

establishing the desired bound.
