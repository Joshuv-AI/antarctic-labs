The association between adverse perinatal outcomes, obstructive sleep apnoea and the role of depressive symptoms
Authors: Karen Redhead, Jennifer Walsh, John P Newnham, Dana Hince, Peter Eastwood, Megan Galbally
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBoth obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) and depressive symptoms have been independently associated with adverse perinatal outcomes. However, defining the individual contribution of each condition to adverse perinatal outcomes is difficult because many pregnant women with OSA also have depressive symptoms. This observational study aimed to investigate the relationship between OSA in pregnancy and perinatal outcomes, accounting for the confounding effects of depressive symptoms.MethodsOne hundred twenty two pregnant women (≥ 26 weeks’ gestation) from a tertiary maternity hospital completed overnight monitoring of oxygen saturation and airflow and reported depressive symptoms. Medical records were reviewed, and maternal and newborn outcomes recorded. Binary logistic regression assessed the association of maternal and newborn outcomes with OSA, accounting for depressive symptoms.ResultsNewborns of mothers with OSA had 18 times the odds of having an Apgar score of < 7 at 5 min after birth after accounting for depressive symptoms and body mass index (aOR 18.41, 95% CI 1.17–289.29). Mothers with more frequent dips in oxygenation > 3% had higher birthweight infants after accounting for BMI and depressive symptoms (β = 0.75, 95% CI 0.20–1.29; p = 0.008). Women with depressive symptoms were almost 7 times more likely to have a preterm infant (aOR 6.85, 95% CI 1.57–29.95). Maternal OSA was not associated with gestational hypertensive disorders, gestational diabetes or caesarean delivery.ConclusionsThis study shows OSA relates to newborns with low Apgar scores after birth, while depressive symptoms relate to preterm birth. Considering this both psychological interventions and OSA treatment may be valuable in improving perinatal mental health and infant wellbeing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09399-1.

Background

Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) in pregnancy has been associated with adverse maternal and newborn outcomes. Maternal OSA measured in mid-pregnancy (27 weeks’ gestation) has been related to increased odds of pre-eclampsia (OR 1.94 [1.18, 3.23]), gestational hypertension (OR 1.73 [1.19–2.52]) and gestational diabetes (OR 2.79 [1.63, 4.77]) [1]. Maternal OSA is also related to adverse outcomes in newborns, including being born large for gestational age [2], with low birth weight, low Apgar scores [3], preterm [3, 4] or requiring admission to neonatal intensive care [4, 5] although there is inconsistency in the literature with some studies reporting no association between maternal OSA and adverse maternal and newborn outcomes [6, 7].

Our previous research has shown a significant overlap between OSA and depressive symptoms in pregnancy, with 50% of pregnant women experiencing depressive symptoms also having OSA [8]. Depressive symptoms have been independently associated with adverse maternal and newborn outcomes, including pre-eclampsia [9, 10], increased risk of gestational diabetes [11], reduced fetal growth [12] and preterm birth [13]. However, inconsistencies in the literature and methodological differences between studies make it difficult to assess the true impact of depressive symptoms on adverse maternal and newborn outcomes [14, 15].

Because OSA and depressive symptoms co-occur in a substantial proportion of pregnant women and are both independently associated with similar maternal and newborn complications, it is difficult to quantify the contribution of each to adverse maternal and newborn outcomes. It is possible that not controlling for the individual contributions of OSA and depression on adverse perinatal outcomes might explain some of the inconsistencies in the literature. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between OSA in pregnancy and maternal and newborn outcomes, while accounting for the potentially confounding effects of depressive symptoms.

Methods

Participants and data collection

Women ≥ 26 weeks pregnant were recruited from maternity wards and outpatient clinics at King Edward Memorial Hospital (KEMH), Perth, Western Australia, between April 2014 and December 2014. King Edward Memorial Hospital is the state’s tertiary referral centre for obstetrics, and therefore, the patient population is considered higher risk than the general antenatal population. Exclusion criteria included having a multiple pregnancy, insufficient English to complete questionnaires or currently undergoing treatment for OSA. The study was approved by the Women and Newborn Health Service Research Ethics Committee 2014003EW, the Human Research Ethics Office of The University of Western Australia RA/4/1/6764 and University of Notre Dame ethics 16,160 F.

Protocol

Following informed consent at either an outpatient appointment or on a maternity ward, participants were prepared for a ward or home-based overnight sleep study.

Sleep study

A sleep study was undertaken on each participant using the ApneaLink (ResMed, Sydney, Australia), which measures nasal airflow and oxygen saturation. Data were downloaded and imported into Profusion 3 software (Compumedics, Sydney, Australia) for manual scoring, as previously described [16]. Total monitoring time was calculated (after removing periods with missing or poor signals) and any study with a total monitoring time < 60 was excluded from subsequent analyses. Apnoea’s and hypopneas were scored according to the 2012 AASM Scoring Manual [17], with a hypopnoea defined as ≥ 30% drop in airflow for ≥ 10s associated with a ≥ 3% drop in the oxygen saturation. The total number of respiratory events and oxygen desaturations ≥ 3% were referenced to the total monitoring time to provide the Apnoea Hypopnoea Index (AHI) and Oxygen Desaturation Index (ODI) at 3% (ODI3%), respectively. Presence of OSA was defined as AHI ≥ 5 events per hour. Sleep studies were scored after women had given birth, and women identified as having OSA were sent a letter recommending follow-up with their general practitioner if they continued to experience symptoms.

Outcome measurements

Maternal and newborn hospital medical records were reviewed by one of two researchers, both blinded to the sleep results. Maternal data included estimated due date, smoking status, parity, age, ethnicity, body mass index (BMI) in the first and third trimesters, previous stillbirth, previous preterm birth, previous pre-eclampsia, maternal history of asthma, presence of chronic hypertension, gestational hypertension, current pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, insulin use, maternal postnatal sepsis, caesarean delivery, length of each stage of labour, and arterial and venous umbilical cord blood gases at delivery. Gestational age at date of sleep study and maternal age at estimated due date were also calculated. Newborn data included special care nursery admission and reason for admission, Apgar score at 1 and 5 min after birth, neonatal problems, birthweight, gestational age at birth and infant sex.

Specific measurements

EPDS

The EPDS is a 10-item self-reported depressive symptoms screening questionnaire that has been validated for the identification of depressive symptoms in pregnant and postpartum women [18]. This instrument was originally developed in the United Kingdom (U.K.) by Cox, Holden and Sagovsky in 1987 [19]. Each item on the EPDS is rated on a Likert scale. The most widely used threshold is 13 or more (≥ 13) although many studies suggest 10 or more to include minor depressive disorders and 15 or more in antenatal populations [20]. King Edward Memorial Hospital as well as many other health services use a threshold of ≥ 13 in both the antenatal and postnatal periods. This study also used the ≥ 13 threshold to define the presence of clinically significant depressive symptoms. At this threshold the positive predictive value is estimated to be 50% [21], meaning half of those who screen positive will meet the diagnostic criteria for depression.

Gestational hypertension

Gestational hypertension was defined as a new onset of blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg, measured on a least two occasions > 30 min apart and occurring after 20 weeks’ gestation [22]. Chronic hypertension was defined as hypertension diagnosed prior to pregnancy or before 20 weeks’ gestation.

Pre-eclampsia

Pre-eclampsia was diagnosed by hypertension arising after 20 weeks’ gestation accompanied by evidence of maternal or fetal organ dysfunction. Diagnostic criteria followed the Society if Obstetric Medicine of Australia and New Zealand (SOMANZ) guidelines for hypertensive disorders of pregnancy [22]. These criteria include proteinuria, renal or hepatic dysfunction, haematological complications, neurological features, pulmonary oedema, or fetal growth restriction.

Gestational diabetes

Gestational diabetes was diagnosed from oral glucose tolerance tests in which plasma glucose was measured initially after fasting and remeasured at 1 and 2 h following ingestion of a 75 g glucose drink. Diabetes was diagnosed when either fasting venous plasma glucose (PG) > 5.1 mmol/L, 1 h venous PG > 10.0 mmol/L or 2 h venous PG > 8.5 mmol/L [23].

Apgar score

Apgar scores were performed on each newborn infant assessing (1) colour, (2) heart rate, (3) reflexes, (4) muscle tone and (5) respiration, each of which was given a score of 0, 1 or 2. Apgar scores were recorded at 1 and 5 min after birth. An infant Apgar score of 7–10 at 5 min after birth was considered reassuring [24]. For this study, Apgar scores were categorised as less than 7 at 1 and 5 min after birth.

Neonatal complications

Infants were categorised as having a neonatal complication if they were identified with any of the following: jaundice requiring phototherapy, hypoglycaemia, sepsis, transient tachypnoea of the newborn, hyaline membrane disease, meconium aspiration, pneumothorax, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, patent ductus arteriosus, intravascular haemorrhage, seizures, weight loss > 10%, apnoea, necrotising enterocolitis or hypothermia.

Sepsis

Postnatal maternal sepsis was recorded based on medical records during postnatal inpatient stay. Sepsis was classified as being due to urinary tract infection, wound infection, fever from an undetermined source and other (includes endometritis and chorioamnionitis). Women who received prophylactic antibiotics were excluded from any analyses related to maternal postnatal sepsis.

Arterial umbilical cord blood pH and lactate levels

Cord blood was collected at delivery. This is standard procedure at KEMH. Both arterial and venous blood samples were obtained from a clamped segment of cord immediately after delivery. Umbilical arterial blood samples were validated as arterial by ensuring the pH was at least 0.02 lower in the artery than in the vein [25]. Arterial cord blood pH < 7.2 and increased lactate were used as outcome variables to suggest fetal compromise.

Statistical analysis

Not all data were distributed normally; consequently, a range of statistical tests, including Student’s t, Mann–Whitney U and chi-square, were used to compare the descriptive and clinical features of included and excluded participants. Continuous AHI and ODI measures were log-transformed using a natural logarithm for all analyses.

Presence of OSA was defined using the clinical cut-off AHI ≥ 5 events/hr and severity of OSA using the continuous AHI scale. Analyses were also performed using ODI3% to investigate outcomes related to intermittent hypoxia [26].

All statistics were performed using IBM SPSS 24 Statistics for Windows (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). A p value < 0·05 was considered significant.

Maternal outcomes

Binomial logistic regression was used to assess the association between adverse maternal outcomes, including the presence of chronic hypertension, gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, diabetes requiring insulin, maternal postnatal sepsis and caesarean delivery, with OSA (AHI ≥ 5), AHI and ODI3% in separate univariate models. These models were then adjusted for BMI and depressive symptoms, BMI and EPDS scores were treated as continuous variables in adjusted models.

Newborn outcomes

Binomial logistic regression was used to assess the association between admission to special care nursery, transient tachypnoea of the newborn, Apgar score of < 7 at 1 and 5 min after birth and neonatal problems with OSA (AHI ≥ 5), AHI and ODI3% in separate univariate models. These models were then adjusted for BMI and depressive symptoms. The multivariable model predicting transient tachypnoea of the newborn was also adjusted for caesarean delivery.

Data from ‘Australian national birthweight percentiles by sex and gestational age 1998–2007’ [27] were used to calculate birthweight Z scores. In a separate analysis, regression was used to fit a univariable model assessing the association of birthweight Z scores to AHI and ODI3%, respectively. Multivariable models were adjusted for BMI and depressive symptoms.

Elective caesarean delivery was included as an adjustment variable in the model assessing arterial cord lactate because the absence of labour can influence fetal acid–base parameters.

Binomial logistic regression was used to assess the association between umbilical arterial pH < 7.2 and AHI and ODI3% in separate univariable models. These models were then adjusted for BMI, depressive symptoms and elective caesarean. Separate univariable linear regression models were fitted to umbilical arterial blood lactate levels to assess the association with AHI and ODI3%. These models were then adjusted for BMI, elective caesarean and depressive symptoms.

Results

A total of 189 women consented to participate in the study (see Fig. 1). Sixty-three women were excluded because of insufficient data as a result of device failure or short use (< 60 min monitoring time) and two women were excluded because of twin pregnancies. A further two women had missing birth data. In total, 122 women were included in the final analyses.

Fig. 1Flow chart of participants in the study

Flow chart of participants in the study

There were no significant differences in age, smoking status, parity, ethnicity, history of asthma, hypertension, diabetes, previous stillbirth, preterm birth, pre-eclampsia or third trimester BMI between participants who were included (n = 122) or excluded (n = 67). There was a trend towards those who were included having a lower first trimester BMI, p = 0.050 (additional files, Table A1). Fewer included participants had gestational hypertension but no other clinical outcomes were different between included and excluded participants (additional files, Table A2).

Of the women included in the study, 16.4% (n = 20) had OSA (Table 1). Among the women with OSA 60% (n = 12) had mild disease, 5% (n = 1) moderate and 35% (n = 7) severe OSA. Depressive symptoms in this cohort have been reported previously [8]. In that publication, depressive symptoms (EPDS ≥ 13) were more common in women with OSA (54.5%) than in those without OSA (4.8%; χ² = 13.2, p < 0.001). As these analyses have already been published, depressive symptoms are not re-analysed in the present study. Women with OSA had higher BMIs, by 6.3 kg/m2 and 7.7 kg/m2 in the first and third trimesters, respectively, and were more likely to report a history of asthma. There was no association between ethnicity and OSA (p value = 0.063). However, OSA prevalence was higher in women of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander descent (5/11 = 45.4%) compared with all other ethnicities combined (14/105 = 13.3%).

Maternal outcomes and obstructive sleep apnoea

No relationship was found between gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, diabetes requiring insulin or caesarean delivery and OSA (Table 2). The odds of women being diagnosed with maternal postnatal sepsis during their hospital stay were 3.7 times higher in women with OSA. However, this relationship was not maintained after adjusting for BMI and depressive symptoms. Details regarding maternal postnatal sepsis separated by cause in those with and without OSA is available in additional files, Table A3.

In addition to analysing OSA at the clinical cut-off (AHI ≥ 5), increasing AHI (as a continuous variable) was investigated in a separate analysis as a predictor of gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, diabetes requiring insulin, maternal postnatal sepsis and caesarean delivery, both independently and after adjusting for first trimester BMI and depressive symptoms measured on the EPDS (additional files, Table 4). Increasing AHI was related to maternal postnatal sepsis although the relationship was not maintained after adjusting for BMI and depressive symptoms.

In a separate analysis (additional files, Table A5), increasing ODI3% was investigated as a predictor of maternal complications. A model accounting for depressive symptoms showed gestational diabetes was associated with increasing BMI and decreasing ODI3%. Additionally, in a multivariate model accounting for ODI3% and EPDS, increased BMI was associated with pre-eclampsia. No other associations were found in this cohort.

Newborn outcomes and obstructive sleep apnoea

After adjusting for BMI and depressive symptoms, maternal OSA was associated with 18 times greater odds of having an infant with an Apgar score of < 7 at 5 min after birth, but not at 1 min after birth. A greater proportion of mothers with OSA also had infants with transient tachypnoea of the newborn, although this relationship was not maintained after accounting for BMI, depressive symptoms and caesarean delivery (Table 3).

Cord blood gases

Cord blood gas results were available for 74 of the 122 women included in the study. No significant relationships were found between either having an umbilical cord blood pH ≤ 7.2 (see additional files, Table A7) or increasing umbilical cord blood lactate levels (continuous variable) (see additional files, Table A8) and either AHI or ODI3% in univariable models, or following adjustment for caesarean delivery, gestational age, BMI and depressive symptoms.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study assessing the relationship between OSA and adverse maternal and newborn outcomes while accounting for the influence of depressive symptoms. Such an adjustment is important because OSA and depressive symptoms frequently co-occur [8] and both are associated with many of the same adverse perinatal outcomes. We found increased depressive symptoms, but not OSA, was related to preterm birth. Specifically, women with depressive symptoms (EPDS ≥ 13) in late pregnancy were almost 7 times more likely to have a preterm infant. In our cohort, OSA was not related to gestational hypertension or diabetes. However, in a univariable analysis, positive associations were found between OSA and increased postnatal sepsis in mothers. Women with more oxygen desaturation in late pregnancy had higher birthweight infants and women with OSA were 18 times more likely to have a newborn with an Apgar score below 7 at 5 min after birth. However, no association was found with umbilical arterial cord blood pH ≤ 7.2 or increased lactate levels, indicative of perinatal hypoxia and metabolic acidosis, respectively. Our findings are consistent with previous research showing antenatal depression increases the likelihood of preterm birth [28]. However, in contrast to previous research, we found no association between OSA and preterm birth [3, 29, 30]. Notably, most studies showing a relationship between maternal OSA and preterm birth also reveal an association between OSA and pre-eclampsia. Pre-eclampsia often leads to preterm delivery. Indeed, the study by Louis et al. concludes that the relationship between OSA and preterm birth is secondary to pre-eclampsia in most cases [19]. In our study, OSA and pre-eclampsia were unrelated; this may explain the disparity between our findings and those of Louis et al. However, a significant data linkage study established a connection between OSA and preterm birth, even when adjusting for hypertensive disorders of pregnancy [30]. Given that OSA is seldom screened for during pregnancy, it is plausible that these women had diagnoses of OSA prior to pregnancy, indicating a potentially more severe and enduring condition than that observed in our study participants, the majority of who were identified with only mild disease in late pregnancy. Furthermore, previous research linking OSA to preterm birth has not considered the impact of depression or depressive symptoms (despite one study noting women with OSA were more likely to suffer from depression [29]). Thus, the reported association between OSA and preterm birth could be attributed to an overlap of OSA and depressive symptoms. The overall rate of preterm birth in our study was high, likely due to the participants being a high-risk group. Further research accounting for the role of depressive disorder and depressive symptoms in preterm birth is required.

Research investigating the relationship between maternal OSA with adverse maternal outcomes have shown conflicting results. Although the largest studies show maternal OSA is related to pre-eclampsia, gestational hypertension and diabetes, many smaller studies [31], including ours, show no such relationship. Interestingly, depressive symptoms are also related to pre-eclampsia [9, 10] and gestational diabetes [11]. A recent study examining the effects of OSA in pregnancy on hypertensive and normotensive women suggested that inconsistent findings may be explained by the inadequate adjustment of confounding variables [32]. Given the substantial overlap of OSA and depressive symptoms, it is possible that either or both in combination relate to hypertension, pre-eclampsia and gestational diabetes. However, our study found that neither OSA, increasing AHI, ODI nor depressive symptoms were related to pre-eclampsia, hypertension or gestational diabetes. Our cohort consisted predominantly of women with mild OSA measured in mid- to late pregnancy, with relatively few women screening positive for depressive symptoms and OSA. Because of this, it may be that relationships will be unmasked in larger cohorts with longer lasting and/or more severe disease. Depressive symptomatology is a potential confounding variable and larger studies should account for this when investigating the relationships of OSA to hypertension and diabetes.

Emerging evidence shows that surgical patients with OSA are at increased risk of infection [33, 34]. The greater propensity for infection among those with OSA is thought to result from adverse effects on the immune system such as increased systemic oxidative stress, endothelial damage and pro-inflammatory cascades, ultimately increasing an individual’s susceptibility to infection [33]. In univariable analysis, our study revealed a 3.5-fold increase in maternal postnatal sepsis in women with OSA, mainly due to the incidence of fever with an unknown origin. As fever is not always related to infection and can be related to stress [35], our findings may reflect a relationship between OSA and stress that is measured partially by depressive symptoms. Indeed, after accounting for BMI and depressive symptoms, OSA was no longer significantly related to maternal postnatal sepsis. Although only a small number of women in our study experienced postnatal sepsis our findings are similar to a previous retrospective study [29].

Our study revealed a positive association between ODI3% and higher birthweight infants, a finding supported by previous research demonstrating that OSA in late pregnancy is related to high birthweight infants [2]. It is possible that women with OSA have pre-diabetic changes which may increase fetal growth or, alternatively, a larger fetus may increase a mother’s susceptibility to OSA. Support for both hypotheses exists: OSA in pregnancy has been related to changes in glycaemic patterns [36], supporting the notion that pre-diabetic changes relate to OSA. Diabetes in pregnancy consistently relates to high birthweight infants [37]. Alternatively, evidence in the general population shows that increased abdominal girth leads to upward pressure on the trachea, reducing tension and increasing collapsibility of the upper airway [38]. Carrying a large fetus likely relates to greater abdominal girth; a similar reduction in tracheal tension, and an increase in upper airway collapsibility is likely to follow. Despite the positive association between ODI3% and birthweight found in our study, which also controlled for depressive symptoms, there is some contradictory evidence demonstrating a relationship between OSA and low birthweight infants [3]. Additionally, pregnant women living at high altitudes, with constant exposure to hypoxia, are reported to have small-for-gestational-age offspring [39]. It is plausible that a U-shaped relationship exists between hypoxia and fetal growth, where severe OSA persisting throughout pregnancy relates to reduced fetal growth, and mild OSA in late pregnancy is associated with high birthweight infants. Alternatively, the overlap with OSA and depression may explain discrepancies between studies, because depression has consistently been related to low birthweight infants [12].

Aligning with previous reports [3, 30], our study found that pregnant women with OSA were 18 times more likely to have newborns with Apgar scores < 7 at 5 min after birth. Apgar scores are routinely used to assess the condition of newborns at 1 and 5 min after birth. Scores can be influenced by many factors, including maternal sedation, gestational age, congenital anomalies and inter-observer variability. Despite this, evidence suggests that Apgar scores below 7 at 5 min after birth are predictive of poor newborn outcomes [40] and worse long-term cognitive outcomes [40]. In addition to Apgar scores, we assessed the relationship between OSA and umbilical cord lactate and pH; both of which are considered predictors of newborn outcomes. A lower arterial cord pH is indicative of perinatal hypoxia and a high lactate level is indicative of metabolic acidosis. Of the cord blood samples collected in our study (64% of women) we found no relationship between AHI or oxygen desaturations in the mother and arterial umbilical cord pH or lactate.

The strengths of our study include the objective measurement of OSA in 122 women. However, despite this large sample only a small proportion of women and infants had complications, which limited our statistical power and resulted in wide confidence intervals. Additionally, this was a high-risk sample recruited from a variety of clinics and wards at a tertiary maternity hospital and this may limit the generalisation of our findings to a lower risk group. The device used to measure OSA in this study did not measure electroencephalography; therefore, events causing arousal and no desaturation were not included in the AHI. As a result, the severity of OSA in this cohort may have been underestimated. It also means that associations related primarily to sleep fragmentation, rather than desaturation, may not have been detected, which should be considered when interpreting the findings. Regarding depression, we used a validated self-report measure of depressive symptoms designed to screen for probable perinatal depression. A positive screen is predictive of a clinical diagnosis in only about half of cases, meaning some misclassification is likely. This may have led to reduced associations or reflected association with broader psychological distress, such as stress or anxiety, rather than depression specifically. The addition of a diagnostic measure of depressive disorders in future research would be valuable.

In conclusion, this study identified a relationship between depressive symptoms in pregnancy and the incidence of preterm birth. We also found that women with more oxygen desaturations in late pregnancy had higher birthweight infants, and women with OSA were more likely to have a newborn with an Apgar score below 7 at 5 min after birth. Depressive symptoms are routinely screened for in pregnancy; however, sleep health is rarely assessed despite growing evidence linking OSA with adverse birth outcomes. These findings suggest that sleep should be considered during routine antenatal care and that women at higher risk are investigated for OSA. Increasing awareness of the potential impact of OSA on perinatal outcomes may support earlier identification and intervention. Combining psychological interventions with OSA treatment may be valuable in reducing adverse perinatal outcomes.
