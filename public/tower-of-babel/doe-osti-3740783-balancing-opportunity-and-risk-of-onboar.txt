 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The IAEA uses more than 1,000 surveillance cameras to meet its mandate. Machine learning has
exploded and offers tremendous opportunities in common commercial and industrial surveillance
applications. Inconclusive IAEA surveillance can have significant consequences; therefore, a minimum
baseline must be established that satisfies the IAEA’s surveillance requirements while balancing the
promise that machine learning offers. If machine learning is to be implemented onboard the Next
Generation Surveillance System successor, guardrails must also be developed and deployed to ensure the
IAEA can achieve conclusive results. These guardrails must be in place to cover some of the IAEA
surveillance use cases that may include self-contained deployments without access to wall power or a
network connection. Oak Ridge National Laboratory is designing a machine-learning-capable
surveillance system that emphasizes power-efficient operation to maximize battery life while also fully
leveraging power and network connections when available.

 1. CONSEQUENCES OF IAEA SURVEILLANCE FAILURES

The consequences of a failed surveillance system can be significant for the IAEA. Inconclusive
containment and surveillance were reported as “Problem 1” in the 1997 Safeguards Implementation
Report (SIR) and continued to be reported as a problem by the IAEA through the 2003 SIR [1]. The Next
Generation Surveillance System (NGSS) and its predecessor, the Digital Camera Module DCM-14,
demonstrated higher mean time between failures than the analog surveillance systems being used at that
time. However, recognizing that inconclusive surveillance can have severe consequences is essential:

 Inconclusive surveillance during [the open core] period [at a light water reactor] raises questions with
 respect to the possibility of diversion/substitution of irradiated core fuel, and the possibility of
 removal of unreported plutonium in concealed target materials. Full resolution of the
 diversion/substitution scenario requires verification of the spent fuel, which is both physically
 intrusive and time consuming for both the IAEA and the reactor operators. [1]

Integrated safeguards and the State-Level Approach may have modified how some safeguards
surveillance failures must be resolved, but surveillance failures typically continue to have high
consequences for the IAEA.

 3. RISKS AND RECOMMENDATIONS FOR ONBOARD MACHINE LEARNING FOR THE
 NGSS SUCCESSOR GIVEN CONSTRAINTS OF DIFFERENT DEPLOYMENT
 SCENARIOS

The challenges and opportunities for machine learning are different in each of these quadrants and should
be considered when drafting design requirements for a successor to the NGSS. For example, when
deployed with wall power, onboard operations can be less power constrained and could use extensive
onboard machine learning inference. However, if running from battery power alone, onboard operations
need to balance improved capabilities that machine learning may offer with assurance that the battery life
is sufficient to record images until the next maintenance opportunity (i.e., battery replacement). As
another example, when deployed with a network connection, footage could be sent back to a collect
computer with access to larger storage and potentially more extensive compute resources. Without a
network connection, the system is constrained by available onboard storage. Pursuing new machine-
learning opportunities must therefore be balanced with the assurance that onboard storage will record
sufficient surveillance images to draw safeguards conclusions until the next maintenance opportunity to
swap removable media (i.e., secure digital cards on the NGSS).

 3

Machine learning is a powerful tool but cannot be adopted on cameras without weighing the risk that
premature failures could lead to inconclusive surveillance. For example, could unexpected movements in
the field of view cause additional machine learning inference to run, draining a battery faster than
anticipated or filling the onboard storage before scheduled maintenance? What guardrails can be
implemented to assure the minimum footage is recorded on camera to support conclusive surveillance
review?

The challenges and opportunities of using machine learning onboard the camera need to be considered
separately for each combination of power and network constraints.

 Not power constrained or storage constrained (i.e., wall power with network connection)
 o Risk: This is the easiest risk to mitigate. Spurious motion detection, object detection, or object
 classification triggered by onboard machine learning poses a risk of filling the onboard storage
 before the next maintenance window. If the facility and surveillance system are configured for
 remote data transmission, the IAEA could know the local available storage is running low.
 o Recommendations:
  Send higher-frequency and higher-resolution images to the collect computer.
  Use machine learning at the collect computer to record enhanced footage (i.e., higher
 frequency, higher resolution, or both) around safeguards-relevant events so that inspectors
 have more confidence in their completeness.
  Replace the risk of running out of storage on the camera’s removable media with the risk of
 running out of storage on the collect computer.
  Store baseline footage onboard with a minimum picture-taking interval (PTI) that ensures
 safeguards conclusions can be drawn.
  Design guardrails to ensure onboard storage will be sufficient to capture a minimum PTI until
 the next scheduled maintenance.
  Use machine learning to annotate stored footage for more efficient review.
  Use machine learning to record enhanced footage (higher resolution, higher frequency, or
 both) around safeguards-relevant events so that inspectors have more confidence in their
 completeness.
  Configure data storage on the collect computer as a superset that may include data not
 available on the camera’s local copy. The image set on the camera’s removable media would
 only be necessary to fill gaps in case of a network failure between the collect computer and
 the camera.
 Power constrained but not storage constrained (i.e., battery power with network connection1)
 o Risk: Onboard machine learning poses a risk of depleting the battery before the next maintenance
 window. With a network connection, if the facility and system are configured for remote data
 transmission, the IAEA could know about imminent camera failure and send technicians to
 prevent failures.
 o Recommendations:
  Store baseline footage onboard with a minimum PTI that ensures safeguards conclusions can
 be drawn.
  Consider the power efficiency of sending images to the collect computer for inference
 compared with running inference onboard.
  If sending images to a collect computer is more power efficient:
 o Send enhanced footage (higher frequency, higher resolution, or both) to the collect
 computer while managing the power budget to meet the necessary deployment
 duration.

 4. OPPORTUNITIES

In certain scenarios, are battery power, onboard storage, or both currently insufficient to result in a
conclusive surveillance review? In certain scenarios, could machine learning extend battery life or
duration of onboard storage to better cover the period of interest? For example:

 What role could machine learning play in a battery-powered surveillance system to cover the open
 core period at light water reactors?

 What role could machine learning play in a battery-powered surveillance system during emplacement
 of used nuclear fuel in a deep geological repository?

5. OAK RIDGE NATIONAL LABORATORY’S APPROACH TO A PRELIMINARY DESIGN
 FOR AN NGSS SUCCESSOR

Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) is designing a machine-learning-capable surveillance system
that emphasizes power-efficient operation to maximize battery life while also fully leveraging available
power and network connections when they are present. The authors believe that by focusing on the
requirements of the self-contained surveillance system (gold star in Figure 2) and incorporating the ability

 6. CONCLUSIONS

The IAEA reviews footage from approximately 2,000 cameras to support its mandate of ensuring that
nuclear materials are used exclusively for peaceful purposes. Although machine learning offers
tremendous opportunities for commercial and industrial surveillance, the risks involved must be
appreciated. Numerous installations have power and network connections that may mitigate any risk
associated with performing inference on the camera, but some desirable installations may not have wall
power or a network connection. The IAEA will need to develop a strategy to balance the potential
benefits of machine learning with power and onboard storage constraints and with the risk of new
surveillance failure modes introduced by machine learning. One possible approach described previously
may be the use of guardrails around any onboard machine learning so that the camera has sufficient
battery and onboard storage to record the minimum footage required to draw safeguards conclusions and
using machine learning only for enhanced footage. To capture opportunities in which better battery life or
better storage efficiency enables new surveillance deployment scenarios, the ORNL team is focused on
developing a power-efficient surveillance system that incorporates advanced onboard machine learning
capabilities that can be scaled based on available power and storage constraints.
