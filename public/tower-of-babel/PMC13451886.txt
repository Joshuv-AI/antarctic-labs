KCNK4 c.730G>C/p.Ala244Pro Promotes Gingival Fibroblast Proliferation and Fibrosis Through MAPK/ERK1/2 Activation in Gingival Fibromatosis
Authors: Yantong Wu, Shuran Yang, Fanying Kong, Ming He, Yan Zhang, Shengnan Zhang, Hongxian Zhou, Ruoxi Yang, Yongjian Mao, Hong Shi, Jie Zhang, Mingzhu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and aimsKCNK4-associated gingival fibromatosis (K-GF) is a rare condition characterised by progressive gingival enlargement and excessive connective tissue accumulation. Although pathogenic variants in KCNK4 have been linked to syndromic gingival overgrowth, the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying the gingival phenotype remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to investigate the biological effects of a rare heterozygous KCNK4 c.730G>C variant identified in a paediatric patient with K-GF and to explore its potential role in gingival fibroblast dysfunction.MethodsWe employed primary fibroblasts, engineered mutant cell lines and animal models to investigate the KCNK4 mutation. The role of the ERK1/2 pathway was assessed using the inhibitor SCH 772984 and activator Ro 67-7476. Phenotypic effects on proliferation, apoptosis and fibrosis were evaluated via CCK-8, EdU, Annexin V-FITC, qPCR, Western blot, immunofluorescence, HE and Masson staining.ResultsThe KCNK4 c.730G>C mutation was associated with increased proliferative and fibrotic activity and reduced apoptotic capacity in gingival fibroblasts. Similar phenotypic changes were observed in engineered mutant cell models and, to a lesser extent, in the mouse model. Transcriptomic analysis and validation experiments indicated activation of the MAPK/ERK1/2 pathway. Pharmacological modulation further suggested that ERK1/2 signalling contributes to the proliferative imbalance of mutant gingival fibroblasts.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that the KCNK4 c.730G>C mutation may contribute to gingival fibromatosis by promoting gingival fibroblast proliferation and fibrosis, at least in part through MAPK/ERK1/2 pathway activation. This study provides mechanistic insight into K-GF and supports further investigation of ERK1/2-related signalling as a potential therapeutic direction.Clinical RelevanceThis study highlights a possible molecular mechanism underlying KCNK4-associated gingival overgrowth and suggests that pathway-based approaches may complement surgical management in future studies. However, further validation in additional patients and preclinical models is required before clinical translation.Key words: KCNK4-associated gingival fibromatosis, Gene mutation, Gene editing, Fibrosis, Proliferation; Apoptosis

Introduction

Gingival fibromatosis (GF), also referred to as hereditary or idiopathic gingival fibromatosis, is a rare disorder with an estimated incidence of approximately 1 in 750,000 individuals. It is characterised by progressive, benign gingival enlargement caused by excessive accumulation of collagen-rich connective tissue in the submucosa. GF may occur either as a familial condition1,2 or as a sporadic disease.3,4 Excessive gingival overgrowth impairs oral hygiene and promotes bacterial accumulation, gingival inflammation and periodontal pocket formation, thereby compromising oral health and aesthetics.5

Histologically, GF involves changes in both the epithelium and connective tissue. The epithelium shows irregular hyperplasia with elongated rete ridges extending into the lamina propria. The underlying connective tissue is characterised by abundant collagen bundles, increased fibroblast density and sparse vascularity.6,7

At present, gingival overgrowth is managed mainly by surgical excision. However, postoperative recurrence is common and many patients require repeated gingivectomies. This can impose substantial psychological and financial burdens. Therefore, elucidating the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying GF is essential for improving prevention, diagnosis and treatment.

Recent genetic studies have highlighted the central role of gene mutations in GF. To date, 5 susceptibility loci have been identified: 2p21, 5q13–q22, 2p22.3–p23.3, 11p15 and 4q12.1 Three causative genes, SOS1, REST and ZNF862, have been well characterised in nonsyndromic GF.1,8,9 In contrast, more genes have been implicated in syndromic GF. For example, FAM20A mutations cause enamel renal syndrome, which is characterised by amelogenesis imperfecta, gingival fibromatosis and nephrocalcinosis.10,11 To date, 31 genes have been statistically associated with syndromic GF.5

Among these genes, KCNK4, also known as TRAAK, encodes a neuronally expressed 2-pore domain K⁺ channel of the TREK subfamily. Missense mutations in KCNK4 can alter channel structure12 and have been associated with facial hypertrichosis, hyperpigmentation, intellectual disability and gingival overgrowth syndrome. Therefore, gingival overgrowth caused by pathogenic KCNK4 variants may be conceptualised as K-GF. However, the role of KCNK4 mutations in clinically diagnosed idiopathic or sporadic GF remains poorly defined.

Previous studies suggest that GF is closely related to abnormal fibroblast behaviour. Reported changes include increased fibroblast density, excessive or abnormal collagen synthesis,11,13,14 and reduced expression of matrix metalloproteinases involved in collagen degradation.15 These findings are supported by histological evidence of marked collagen accumulation.16, 17, 18 Other studies have implicated dysregulated cell proliferation and apoptosis,19 as well as enhanced epithelial proliferation in GF tissues.20 Nevertheless, the precise pathogenic mechanisms underlying GF remain incompletely understood.

In the present study, we performed whole-exome sequencing in a paediatric patient initially diagnosed with idiopathic gingival fibromatosis and identified a rare heterozygous KCNK4 c.730G>C mutation (rs1189909394). Compared with normal gingival fibromatosis (N-GFs), KCNK4-associated gingival fibromatosis (K-GFs) showed a profibrotic phenotype, including increased proliferation and fibrosis together with reduced apoptosis. By integrating patient-derived cells, engineered mutant cell models and a mutant mouse model, this study investigated the functional consequences and molecular mechanisms associated with the KCNK4 c.730G>C mutation. To our knowledge, this study provides one of the first functional and mechanistic investigations linking KCNK4 dysfunction to a clinically diagnosed idiopathic/sporadic form of gingival fibromatosis.

Methods

The case report and associated testing

Patient’s data

The KCNK4 affected patient was a 12-year-old female who presented with gingival fibromatosis, without obvious gingival inflammation. The control samples were obtained from 3 individuals with clinically noninflamed gingiva: an 8-year-old male who underwent gingival tissue removal for surgical exposure of an erupting permanent tooth, an 18-year-old female and a 22-year-old female who underwent crown-lengthening procedures due to insufficient clinical crown height before post-and-core crown restoration. All control gingival tissues showed no obvious clinical signs of inflammation.

Patient is served as normal controls. Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples of the proband and parents. After fragmentation, sequencing libraries were prepared and subjected to targeted enrichment of exonic and splice-site regions. The enriched libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq platform. Raw data were subsequently aligned, preprocessed and analysed for variant calling and annotation. Identified variants were classified according to the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG). Candidate pathogenic variants were validated by Sanger sequencing in all available family members. Sanger sequencing results were used to assess variant co-segregation with the disease phenotype and to determine the parental origin of de novo or compound heterozygous mutations. Pathogenicity assessment was supported by cross-species conservation analysis (CLUSTALW), in silico pathogenicity prediction (PolyPhen-2) and structural modelling (PyMol).

Patient’s sample

To compare patient and normal gingival tissues and fibroblasts, we collected gingival tissues from both groups and isolated fibroblasts for histopathological staining and cell culture. After deparaffinisation and rehydration, tissue sections were processed for histological staining. As regards Masson's trichrome staining, sections were sequentially stained with Weigert's haematoxylin, Ponceau S acid fuchsin and aniline blue according to standard protocols, with differentiation and bluing steps performed between staining procedures. Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was carried out using standard methods after the initial deparaffinisation and rehydration.

Primary gingival fibroblast culture

Primary cells were cultured using a modified explant method combined with Dispase II digestion. Briefly, human and mouse gingival tissues were minced and digested in 0.25% Dispase II at 4 °C overnight. Connective tissues were aseptically separated, finely minced and seeded into T25 flasks using an inverted culture method. Gingival fibroblasts were identified by positive immunostaining for vimentin (Proteintech Cat# 10366-1-AP, RRID: AB_2273020) and negative staining for pan-cytokeratin (Bioss Cat# bs-1712R, RRID: AB_10855057). In the human primary cell experiments, n = 4 represents biological samples, including 1 affected patient and 3 independent control individuals. The rarity of the disease limited the availability of additional affected cases. Technical replicates were used only to ensure experimental reliability and were not counted as independent biological replicates.

Fibroblast assays

Gene expression of KCNK4

In order to determine the expression of KCNK4 in K-GFs and N-GFs, we conduct a qRT-PCR experiment. Total RNA was extracted from frozen tissues or cells using the SteadyPure Universal RNA Extraction Kit (Accurate Biology) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara). qRT-PCR was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II on a QuantStudio 5 system to detect the expression of target genes including KCNK4 and BAX, with GAPDH as the endogenous control. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^(-ΔCt) method. Primer sequences and antibodies are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

As regards protein analysis, cells were lysed on ice in RIPA buffer supplemented with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g. Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay based on a standard curve measured at 562 nm after incubation at 37 °C for 30 minutes. All samples were analysed in triplicate. Detailed information on antibodies and primers is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

CCK-8 cell proliferation assay

In order to determine cell viability for proliferation and cytotoxicity assays a CCK-8 cellproliferation assay was performed where fourth-passage K-GFs and N-GFs were trypsinised, seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5.0 × 10³ cells/well and cultured in DMEM-F12 supplemented with 10% FBS. Cell proliferation was assessed at 4 time points using CCK-8 reagent (1:10 in serum-free medium) with incubation for 1 hour at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, with triplicate wells analysed per time point.

EdU cell proliferation assay

In order to quantify the actively proliferating gingival fibroblasts, cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 10⁵ cells/well and incubated with EdU (Beyotime, C0071S) for 12 hour. After fixation and permeabilisation, the Click reaction was performed, followed by DAPI staining. Images were acquired using fluorescence microscopy.

Immunofluorescence staining for Ki67

In order to quantify the actively proliferating gingival fibroblasts, cells were fixed, permeabilised and blocked before incubation with a Ki67 antibody (Proteintech Cat# 27309-1-AP, RRID: AB_2756525) at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with a fluorescent secondary antibody for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark. Cells were counterstained with DAPI, mounted and images were acquired by fluorescence microscopy for quantification of Ki67-positive cells.

Apoptosis analysis

In order to determine the actively apoptosis gingival fibroblasts, cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC in binding buffer (Glpbio, GK10037) for 15 minutes at room temperature in the dark. Apoptosis was immediately quantified by flow cytometry.

Engineered cellular models

In order to verify the causal relationship between the KCNK4 mutation and the phenotypic features of gingival fibromatosis functionally, KCNK4 function was investigated on N-GFs subjected to siRNA-mediated knockdown using si-KCNK4/Lipo3000 complexes. Gain-of-function experiments were performed by the transfection of a KCNK4 (A244P) point mutation expression vector into N-GFs, generating 4 experimental groups: blank, control, wild-type (WT) and mutant (MUT). Direct causal assessment was enabled by applying PE, a precise "search-and-replace" genome editing system.21, 22, 23 A PE construct based on the pGL3-U6-sgRNA-PGK-puromycin backbone was linearised with BsaI, ligated, transfected into cells and selected using puromycin. Positive clones showed 50% editing efficiency (Supplementary Figure 2 A-D) and monoclonal cell lines were subsequently established. All transfections were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Cells were incubated for 6 hour in complete medium before replacing it with fresh DMEM-F12 supplemented with 10% FBS.

Whole-cell patch-clamp recording

To verify the trend of potassium ion channel current changes caused by the KCNK4 mutation, whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed to evaluate the effect of the KCNK4 c.730G>C mutation on TRAAK potassium channel activity. HEK293T cells were transfected with pCAGGS-KCNK4-IRES-EGFP (wild-type) and KCNK4 mutation plasmids. EGFP-positive cells were selected for electrophysiological recording. A 100-ms voltage-step protocol was applied from −110 mV to +100 mV and potassium currents were recorded at each voltage step. Current amplitudes were normalised to cell capacitance and expressed as current density (pA/pF). Current–voltage (I-V) curves were generated to compare channel activity between wild-type and mutant KCNK4 groups.

RNA sequencing and pathway validation

To explore the key mechanism by which KCNK4 mutations cause gingival fibromatosis, total RNA was extracted from K-GFs and N-GFs using Takara according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and integrity were assessed before library construction. Transcriptome sequencing was performed by Novogene Co., Ltd. using an Illumina sequencing platform. After quality control and removal of low-quality reads, clean reads were mapped to the reference genome. Differentially expressed genes were identified using predefined criteria of |log₂ fold change| > 1 and adjusted P < .05. Multiple-testing correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Functional enrichment analyses were conducted using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases. Key transcriptomic findings were further validated by qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis. The RNA-sequencing data generated in this study are not publicly deposited due to patient privacy concerns. Requests for access to these data should be directed to the corresponding author.

Targeted activation and inhibition of the ERK1/2 pathway

In order to detect the regulatory effect of the ERK1/2 pathway on K-GFs and N-GFs, The Ras-Raf-MEK-ERK signalling cascade, a major MAPK pathway, regulates fibroblast functions including apoptosis suppression,24 proliferation,25,26 and fibrosis.27, 28, 29 The role of ERK1/2 in K-GF was assessed using K-GFs and N-GFs with ERK1/2 activator Ro 67-7476 (Ro) (MCE, HY-100403) at 10 μM30 or the ERK1/2 inhibitor SCH 772984 (SCH; MCE, HY-50846) at 300 nM.31 Proliferation markers (PCNA and MCM7), EdU incorporation, Ki-67 positivity and CCK-8 assay were assessed using established methods.

Animal study

Animal model and primary cell culture

This study adhered to the ARRIVE 2.0 Essential 10 checklist. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Guidance on the Operation of the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986 and associated guidelines, as well as EU Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes and the National Research Council’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kunming Medical University (No. KMMU20220214).

Male BALB/c mice aged 6 to 8 weeks were used in this study to minimise hormonal variability associated with the estrous cycle. Animals were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions with free access to food and water under a 12-hour light/dark cycle.

A BALB/c mouse model carrying the Kcnk4A245P mutation (The human KCNK4 A244P point is related to the mouse Kcnk4 A245P point) was generated using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated homology-directed repair. Founder mice were bred to obtain heterozygous offspring and subsequent intercrossing produced homozygous (Kcnk4A245P), heterozygous (Kcnk4A245P/+) and wild-type (WT) littermates. Genotyping was performed by PCR and sequencing of tail DNA (Supplementary Figure 3B). Exclusion criteria were predefined before experiments. Animals with failed genotyping or inadequate tissue quality were excluded from analysis. The exact number of animals used in each experiment is indicated in the corresponding figure legends. The genotype distribution showed no significant deviation from Mendelian ratios and no homozygous lethality. Wild-type and mutant littermates were age-matched and randomly assigned to experimental groups using a random number method. Sample size was determined based on preliminary experiments and previous studies using similar animal models while minimising animal use.

Following euthanasia, mandibles and maxillae were dissected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hour. Mandibles were decalcified for 3 to 4 weeks, embedded in paraffin and coronally sectioned at the first molar region for H&E staining. Maxillary gingival tissues were harvested for primary fibroblast culture. Culture procedures were performed as described for human primary gingival fibroblasts.

Gingival histological analysis of BALB/c-Kcnk4A245P knock-in mice

To compare pathological changes in gingival tissues between wild-type and mutant mice, histological evaluation and quantitative analysis were performed by investigators blinded to genotype and treatment allocation. Histological evaluation and quantitative analyses were performed by investigators blinded to genotype and treatment allocation. For histological quantification, 3 nonconsecutive sections per animal and 5 randomly selected microscopic fields per section were analysed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). Technical replicates were averaged prior to statistical analysis and were not treated as independent biological samples.

As regards immunohistochemical staining, antigen retrieval was performed in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) following deparaffinisation. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% H₂O₂. Sections were then sequentially incubated with p-ERK1/2 primary antibodies at 37 °C and secondary antibodies at room temperature, followed by DAB development and haematoxylin counterstaining. All stained sections were subsequently dehydrated, cleared in xylene and mounted with neutral balsam for microscopic examination.

CCK-8 cell proliferation assay, EdU cell proliferation assay, Annexin V-FITC apoptosis assay, western blot and qRT-PCR were performed to evaluate the proliferation, apoptosis and fibrotic marker expression in gingival fibroblasts isolated from mice of different genotypes. Primer sequences and antibody are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistics

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between 2 independent groups, an unpaired 2-tailed Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed data, whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data. For comparisons among multiple groups, 1-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used when data met parametric assumptions; otherwise, the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparison test was applied.

For CCK-8 proliferation assays measured at multiple time points, 2-way repeated-measures ANOVA was used to evaluate the effects of group, time and their interaction, followed by multiple-comparison correction where appropriate. For experiments involving both genotype and pharmacological treatment, 2-way ANOVA was used to assess main effects and interaction effects. Technical replicates were averaged before statistical analysis and were not treated as independent biological replicates. The unit of analysis and exact statistical test used are specified in each figure legend. Significance levels were set as *P < .05, **P < .01, ***P < .001,and ****P < .0001.

Results

Clinical characterisation of patient

A 12-year-old female patient presented with progressive gingival enlargement involving both the maxillary and mandibular gingiva. The patient was otherwise healthy and had no history of systemic disease, medication use or previous gingival surgery. The gingival tissue was enlarged to such an extent that it nearly covered the entire dentition (Figure 1A). Characteristic facial features were observed, including hypertrichosis of the eyebrows and eyelashes, a prominent vermilion border and retrognathia, consistent with previous reports.12 Functionally, gingival hyperplasia impaired mastication, necessitating annual gingival resection. No significant developmental delay, epilepsy or neurological abnormalities were observed in the proband (Supplementary Table 2).

The patient showed a number of clinical features: gingival hyperplasia, prominent vermilion borders of the upper and lower lips, low-set ears, hyperpigmented skin, thick eyebrows, thick hair, a receding chin and hypertrichosis on the back and limbs. (B) Histological analysis of normal gingiva and the patient's gingival tissue by paraffin section staining. B1, B2 H&E staining (10 × magnification). B3, B4 Masson's trichrome staining (10 × magnification). Scale bar: 200 μm. (C) Genetic sequencing of the patient and both parents identified a heterozygous G-to-C substitution at nucleotide 730 in exon 6 of the KCNK4 gene, resulting in a missense mutation causing an alanine-to-proline substitution at amino acid position 244 (p.Ala244Pro). The mutation was not detected in either parent who also showed no related clinical manifestations. (D) Structural analysis performed using PyMOL. The mutation induced a ring-like conformation at amino acid position 244, leading to the loss of hydrogen bond interactions with residues 241 and 242.

Patient gingival specimens exhibited epithelial hyperplasia characterised by irregular and elongated rete ridges extending into the subepithelial stroma. Masson staining demonstrated substantially increased collagen deposition in GF tissues compared to controls. A dense inflammatory infiltrate predominantly composed of lymphocytes and plasma cells was also observed (Figure 1B). All the symptoms consistent with the diagnosis of gingival fibromatosis. The paediatric patient required annual gingivectomy due to severe masticatory impairment caused by gingival overgrowth. The patient was followed postoperatively and showed satisfactory healing. No severe complications were observed during follow-up.

Genetic analysis

Trio whole-exome sequencing of the family identified a heterozygous c.730G>C mutation in the KCNK4 gene exclusively in the patient, consistent with a de novo mutation, which was confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Figure 1C). The mutation resulted in an amino acid substitution (p.Ala244Pro) and was associated with the clinical manifestations of significant gingival hyperplasia and generalised hypertrichosis. The mutation was predicted to be deleterious to protein structure and function (Figure 1D). According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines, the c.730G>C mutation was classified as pathogenic (PS2_Very strong + PS4_Supporting + PM2_supporting + PP3). The variant has been previously reported in KCNK4-associated FHEIG (Facial dysmorphism, Hypertrichosis, Epilepsy, Intellectual/developmental delay, Gingival overgrowth) -related phenotypes and it is extremely rare in population databases.12 According to the diagnostic criteria together with the genetic findings and the aforementioned clinical and pathological features, the patient was diagnosed with K-GF.

Expression analysis of the KCNK4 c.730G>C mutation

The effects of the mutation on gene expression and cellular function were assessed using primary gingival fibroblasts, transiently transfected normal gingival fibroblasts (N-GFs) and a prime-edited fibroblast model harbouring the KCNK4 c.730G>C mutation.

Expression analysis in primary gingival fibroblasts

Primary gingival fibroblasts were isolated from the patient (K-GFs) and healthy control (N-GFs). Cells from passages 3 to 4 were used for all downstream experiments. Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed the fibroblastic identity of the isolated primary cells, as they were positive for vimentin and negative for cytokeratin (Supplementary Figure 1A). Primary K-GFs exhibited an increased proliferation rate (Figure 2C) and a decreased apoptosis level (Figure 2B) compared to N-GFs. Furthermore, reduced KCNK4 expression, increased expression of the proliferation markers proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) and minichromosome maintenance complex component 7 (MCM7), increased expression of the fibrotic marker collagen type I alpha 1 chain (COL1A1/COL-1), decreased expression of the pro-apoptotic marker Bcl-2 associated X (BAX) and increased expression of the antiapoptotic marker B-cell lymphoma-2 (BCL-2) (Figure 2A, F) were observed. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that mutant gingival fibroblasts adopted a phenotype characterised by hyperproliferation, enhanced fibrotic activity and increased resistance to apoptosis.

Expression analysis of N-GFs following KCNK4 knockdown

Based on the previously observed low KCNK4 expression in K-GFs, it was investigated whether its knockdown would affect the proliferation, apoptosis and fibrotic activity in these cells, potentially leading to a phenotype resembling gingival fibromatosis. KCNK4-silenced cells were generated by transfection with si-KCNK4 for 96 hours (Supplementary Figure 1B) and the resulting functional changes were examined. The results indicated a downregulation in the expression of proliferation markers PCNA and MCM7, the fibrotic marker COL-1 and both antiapoptotic (BCL-2) and pro-apoptotic (BAX) factors following transfection (Supplementary Figure 1C). These results collectively suggested that KCNK4 regulated gingival fibroblast through a nonlinear mechanism. The observed reduction in KCNK4 expression may therefore represent only one of the effects exerted by this mutation in N-GFs.

Functional analysis of the KCNK4 c.730G>C mutation in cells

The KCNK4 c.730G>C (A244P) mutation was investigated using N-GFs transfected with wild-type or mutant vectors, creating 4 groups: blank, control, wild type (WT) and mutant group (MUT). The MUT group exhibited reduced KCNK4 expression and increased levels of TGF-β1 compared to the WT group (Figure 3A). Functional analyses revealed a proliferative and antiapoptotic phenotype: enhanced proliferation, increased S-phase cell population and decreased apoptosis were found in the MUT group (Figure 3A-C). The reduced apoptosis was further confirmed by qRT-PCR and flow cytometry (Figure 3A, Supplementary Figure 1D and E). These results indicated that the KCNK4 c.730G>C mutation drove N-GFs toward a gingival fibromatosis-like phenotype. Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings revealed that the A244P mutation substantially increased the KCNK4 current amplitude across the tested voltage range (−130 mV to +100 mV) compared with the wild-type channel. The A172E mutation also enhanced the current, albeit to a lesser extent than A244P (Figure 3D).

Homozygous and KCNK4 c.730G>C mutant cell lines were successfully generated using the prime-edition (PE) system involving the construction of a pGL3-U6-sgRNA-PGK-puromycin plasmid (Supplementary Figure 2A-C). Analysis of the prime-edited mutant cells revealed reduced KCNK4 expression accompanied by increased levels of PCNA, MCM7 and COL-1, upregulation of BCL-2 and downregulation of BAX (Supplementary Figure 2D), together with enhanced proliferation, fibrotic activity and suppressed apoptosis.

Characterisation and cellular function of the KCNK4 c.730G>C animal model

A Kcnk4 c.730G>C point mutation knock-in mouse model was generated using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing (Supplementary Figure 3A). Histological analysis of the maxillary gingiva from 6 to 8-week-old male mice revealed markedly thickened gingival epithelium in the mutant groups: Kcnk4A245P mutants showed the most severe phenotype, followed by Kcnk4A245P/+mice and then WT controls (Figure 4A, Supplementary Figure 1F, G, H). Primary GFs isolated from mouse gingival tissues revealed an abnormal Kcnk4 expression pattern, with the lowest levels observed in Kcnk4A245P/+, followed by Kcnk4A245P with a slightly higher expression and WT the highest (Figure 4B). Functionally, a consistent gene-dosage effect was observed: Kcnk4A245P exhibited the most enhanced proliferation and fibrosis with the most suppressed apoptosis, Kcnk4A245P/+ fibroblasts showed an intermediate phenotype and WT displayed the lowest proliferation/fibrosis and highest apoptosis (Figure 4C-F). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that the Kcnk4 c.730G>C point mutation promoted gingival fibroblast proliferation and fibrosis, inhibited apoptosis and induced gingival epithelial hyperplasia at the tissue level, supporting its functional role in driving gingival fibromatosis-like pathological changes.

Pathogenic mechanism of the KCNK4 c.730G>C mutation

Transcriptomic analysis revealed a significant enrichment of the MAPK/ERK1/2 signalling pathway in the patient's gingival tissues (Figure 5A-C), implicating its critical regulatory role in GF development driven by the KCNK4 mutation. p-ERK1/2 expression was significantly higher in patient-derived cells than in the normal control cells (Figure 5D and E), indicating constitutive activation of the pathway at the cellular level. A gene dosage effect (Kcnk4A245P > Kcnk4A245P/+ > WT) was observed, consistent with the in vitro findings and further supporting the MAPK/ERK1/2 pathway as a key mediator of the mutation-driven GF phenotype (Figure 5F).

Effects of MAPK/ERK1/2 pathway intervention on K-GFs and N-GFs

To evaluate the role of ERK1/2 signalling in gingival fibroblast proliferation, K-GFs and N-GFs were treated with Ro 67-7476 or SCH 772984. Ki67 and EdU staining showed that K-GFs had higher basal proliferative activity than N-GFs. Ro 67-7476 further enhanced proliferation in both cell types, with K-GFs showing a stronger response. In contrast, SCH 772984 markedly suppressed proliferation in both groups; after inhibition, K-GFs showed lower proliferative activity than untreated N-GFs, but remained slightly higher than SCH-treated N-GFs (Figure 6 A, B, Supplementary Figure 4 A-D). Consistently, cell-cycle analysis showed that Ro 67-7476 increased the proportion of cells in S/G2 phases, whereas SCH 772984 increased G1 arrest and reduced S/G2 populations. CCK-8 assays further confirmed that ERK1/2 activation promoted cell growth, while ERK1/2 inhibition suppressed viability, particularly in K-GFs (Figure 6 C-E). In addition, we also quantified the degree of phenotype reversal before and after intervention of the ERK1/2 pathway. SCH772984 reduced EdU-positive K-GFs from 21.14% to 3.30%, below N-GF levels (16.38%), indicating over-suppression (374.8% reversal). Ro 67-7476 increased EdU activity to 33.07% (250.6% exacerbation). Flow cytometry showed K-GFs had a higher proliferation index (33.31%) than N-GFs (27.02%, P < .01). Ro 67-7476 raised it to 37.05% (59.5% exacerbation), while SCH772984 lowered it to 25.11% (130.4% reversal). CCK-8 at 72 hour showed K-GFs had higher viability (7.215 vs 3.890, P < .01); Ro 67-7476 further increased it (9.147) and SCH772984 decreased it (2.606). Thus, ERK1/2 hyperactivation drives K-GF hyperproliferation and targeted inhibition reverses it.

These results suggest that activation of the ERK1/2 pathway is associated with KCNK4 mutation-associated gingival fibroblast activation. Although ERK1/2 inhibition partially attenuated the enhanced proliferative and fibrotic phenotypes observed in mutant gingival fibroblasts, these data do not establish ERK1/2 signalling as the exclusive causal pathway. Other signalling cascades may also participate in KCNK4 mutation-associated fibroblast activation. Therefore, our findings support a functional involvement of ERK1/2 signalling rather than exclusive causality.

Discussion

This study identified a de novo heterozygous KCNK4 c.730G>C mutation (p.Ala244Pro) in a patient with gingival fibromatosis. FHEIG syndrome is a multisystem disorder characterised by facial dysmorphism, hypertrichosis, epilepsy, neurodevelopmental delay and gingival overgrowth. The patient in this study presented with gingival overgrowth, hypertrichosis and characteristic facial features, but without obvious neurological or developmental abnormalities, suggesting an attenuated phenotype within the spectrum of KCNK4-associated disorders. This presentation differs from previously reported KCNK4-related FHEIG syndrome, in which gingival overgrowth is often accompanied by epilepsy, intellectual disability or neurodevelopmental delay.12

Clinically, the patient exhibited diffuse gingival enlargement that impaired masticatory function and required repeated gingivectomy. Histological examination revealed epithelial thickening, elongated rete ridges, dense connective tissue accumulation and inflammatory infiltration. Similar gingival thickening was also observed in the Kcnk4 mutant mouse model, although the phenotype was milder than that in the patient. Together with the cellular findings, these results suggest that the KCNK4 c.730G>C mutation is associated with abnormal gingival tissue remodelling.

Missense mutations in KCNK4 have been reported to alter TRAAK channel structure and function.12,32, 33, 34 In this study, patch-clamp analysis showed that the c.730G>C mutant mediated greater potassium current than the wild-type channel (Supplementary Figure 1D and E), supporting a gain-of-function effect at the channel level. Although KCNK4 expression was reduced in patient-derived gingival fibroblasts, this finding should not be interpreted as a simple loss-of-function mechanism. KCNK4 knockdown did not reproduce the mutant phenotype, but instead reduced proliferative and fibrotic capacities. Therefore, decreased KCNK4 expression may represent a secondary or compensatory response to altered channel activity rather than the primary pathogenic event. These findings support that the c.730G>C mutation enhances TRAAK channel function, subsequently affecting downstream signalling pathways such as ERK1/2 and promoting gingival fibroblast dysfunction. Consistently, in a genetically defined model created by prime editing, the KCNK4 c.730G>C mutation induced a pro-fibrotic state even in the context of reduced proliferation associated with cell passaging, supporting a direct role of this mutation in driving fibrosis, in line with known disease mechanisms previously reported.9,35,36

Transcriptomic analysis revealed enrichment of the MAPK/ERK1/2 signalling pathway in KCNK4-associated gingival fibroblasts. Pharmacological intervention further showed that ERK1/2 activation enhanced fibroblast proliferation, whereas ERK1/2 inhibition suppressed the proliferative phenotype. These findings suggest that ERK1/2 signalling serves as an important mediator of the proliferative imbalance observed in KCNK4 mutation-associated gingival fibroblasts. However, ERK1/2 signalling may not represent the only downstream pathway affected by the KCNK4 mutation. Notably, ERK1/2 inhibition attenuated, but did not completely abolish, the abnormal proliferative phenotype, suggesting that additional signalling pathways may also contribute to fibroblast dysregulation or that ERK1/2 inhibition was incomplete under the present experimental conditions. Therefore, the KCNK4 c.730G>C mutation may promote gingival fibromatosis through both ERK1/2-dependent and ERK1/2-independent mechanisms. Given the broad role of MAPK/ERK signalling in cell proliferation and tissue homeostasis, future studies may explore local delivery strategies for modulating ERK1/2-related signalling in gingival fibromatosis, such as topical gels, periodontal pocket delivery systems or biomaterial-based sustained-release platforms. However, these approaches remain theoretical and require systematic evaluation of safety, optimal dosage, delivery efficiency and therapeutic efficacy before any clinical application. Therefore, ERK1/2-targeted intervention should be regarded as a potential research direction rather than an established clinical strategy.

The present study also has several limitations. First, although no obvious gingival inflammation was detected during oral examination, histological analysis revealed inflammatory infiltration mainly composed of lymphocytes and plasma cells. Inflammatory mediators may influence fibroblast proliferation, collagen deposition, extracellular matrix remodelling and ERK1/2 activation and therefore may have contributed to the observed phenotype.37, 38, 39, 40 Second, the study was limited by a single affected patient, which reduced statistical power and limited generalisability. Additional patients carrying the same mutation were unavailable because of the extreme rarity of K-GF. Thirdly, despite no clinical inflammation in controls, age and clinical indication constraints hindered tissue collection under patient-matched conditions, limiting age and gender matching. Fourth, the mouse phenotype was milder than the human phenotype, suggesting possible species-specific differences in KCNK4 function in gingival tissues. Finally, although ERK1/2 inhibition attenuated K-GF proliferation in vitro, the broad physiological role of MAPK/ERK signalling in cell proliferation and tissue homeostasis warrants careful evaluation of potential off-target effects, safety, dosage and delivery strategies before clinical translation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings indicate that the KCNK4 c.730G>C mutation is associated with altered gingival fibroblast behavior and ERK1/2 pathway activation in K-GF. Although further validation is required, this study provides preliminary mechanistic evidence linking KCNK4 dysfunction to pathological gingival overgrowth and highlights ERK1/2-related signalling as a potential area for future investigation. Future studies should validate these observations in additional patients, expand genetic screening, refine disease models and investigate whether local ERK-targeted delivery systems may contribute to personalised therapeutic strategies for K-GF.
