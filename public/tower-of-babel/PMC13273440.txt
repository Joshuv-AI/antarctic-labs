Stereotactic electroencephalogram lead placement in patient with hemophilia B: illustrative case
Authors: Gunnar A Lee, Adam Giermasz, Kiran M Kanth, Vilija Abrute, David M Brandman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDMany surgeons have viewed hemophilia as a relative contraindication to elective cranial procedures given the risk of intracranial hemorrhage. However, there is a growing body of literature describing the successful perioperative management of hemophilia patients, thanks to multidisciplinary collaboration between neurosurgeons, hematologists, pharmacists, and other specialists. Previous reports have described how patients with hemophilia have navigated acute neurosurgical interventions, including trauma, tumor resection, deep brain stimulation, and surgical management of epilepsy.OBSERVATIONSIn this illustrative case, a multidisciplinary care team managed a patient with medically refractory epilepsy and hemophilia B undergoing stereotactic electroencephalography (sEEG) monitoring. Their neurological status and hematological markers were closely monitored through an 11-day hospital stay in the epilepsy monitoring unit. The key pharmacological intervention was recombinant factor IX replacement titrated to factor IX assay levels. The patient remained clinically stable without any hemorrhagic complications.LESSONSThis illustrative case demonstrates a successful management strategy for sEEG placement, monitoring, and removal in a patient with hemophilia B. Hemophilia B is bleeding disorder characterized by deficiency of factor IX. If untreated, the disease is associated with frequent spontaneous and traumatic bleeds in large joints. Intracranial bleeding occurs spontaneously in 2%–8% of patients.1 The current standard of care is to provide prophylactic factor replacement therapy, which has been shown to improve quality of life over on-demand regimens.2 The goal of hemophilia management in neurosurgical procedures is to prevent potentially devastating neurological injury from intracranial hemorrhage. Elective neurosurgical procedures should be approached cautiously and with multidisciplinary input. Here, we describe a case of successful multidisciplinary management of stereotactic electroencephalography (sEEG) placement, monitoring, and removal in a patient with a documented history of hemophilia B.

Illustrative Case

A 26-year-old male with medically refractory epilepsy also has a past medical history of hemophilia B (baseline factor IX level of 70% one-stage and 29% chromogenic, mutation F9 c.224G: a previously described mutation in families with mild hemophilia B). He had multiple daily seizures with impaired awareness. While patients with mild hemophilia B are not routinely placed on prophylactic infusions, the patient was prescribed weekly extended half-life recombinant factor IX (Alprolix) prophylactic infusions because of his seizure burden. He also has a history of significant anxiety, for which he self-medicated with large doses of marijuana and up to three packs of cigarettes daily.

His investigations demonstrated a left temporal pole cavernoma. While his seizure semiology was consistent with a left-sided temporal lobe onset, his inpatient video-EEG study demonstrated bitemporal onset. The lack of concordance of his preoperative investigations motivated the decision to proceed with sEEG implantation, focusing on bitemporal implantation. In the setting of his hemophilia, his care was also coordinated with the outpatient and inpatient hematology services to provide recommendations for the monitoring and management of potential hemorrhagic complications. The recommendations were to keep his factor assay level within the standard range of our reference laboratory, 68%–150% NHP (one-stage assay).

Within 1 hour prior to the start of the procedure, the patient received Alprolix 100 units/kg and the peak activity was 121%. The procedure itself was noncomplicated, with successful placement of 7 sEEG leads. There was no sign of unusual or excessive bleeding during the case. Postoperative imaging did not demonstrate any signs of hemorrhage. Twelve hours after the Alprolix initial dose, he received a follow-up dose (50 units/kg) and the peak activity was measured at 143% (one-stage assay).

The patient’s antiepileptic medications were gradually reduced. He was prescribed Alprolix 100 units/kg while in the hospital, eventually decreased to 80 units/kg by postoperative day 10, as guided by weekly factor IX trough levels.

He had two left mesial temporal onset seizures on postoperative days 4 and 5. By postoperative day 10, he did not have any additional convulsant events. Throughout his monitoring, the patient had progressively worsening anxiety (in the context of having stopped his marijuana use). He refused to stay in hospital any further. After multidisciplinary discussion, his monitoring was concluded. Accordingly, he went for sEEG removal on postoperative day 11. He received the 80-units/kg dose the morning of his procedure. Again, the procedure was uneventful, with removal of all 7 sEEG leads without any immediate signs of bleeding or hemorrhage clinically or radiographically verified by a postoperative CT study. He was recommended to stay for observation, but the patient left against medical advice the evening of his procedure due to his anxiety. Interestingly, he returned to the hospital hours later after suffering two seizures while waiting for his ride home. A head CT study was repeated during readmission, again without signs of hemorrhage.

The patient remained in the hospital until post-sEEG removal day 3, receiving the same 80-units/kg Alprolix dosing. He was discharged with five additional daily doses of Alprolix while at home. He had neurosurgical follow-up at 3 days and 7 weeks after discharge with no significant interval events and a plan for left temporal lobectomy in the future for definitive seizure treatment.

Discussion

Careful preoperative planning is essential when it comes to elective surgery for people with hemophilia, and expert consensus recommends collaboration at minimum between surgeons/interventionalists, hematologists, pharmacists, and nursing staff.3 In terms of specific recommendations for factor replacement, the World Federation of Hemophilia (WFH) has published guidelines for both minor and major surgical procedures, with intracranial procedures falling under the major category.4 For these procedures, the WFH recommends a preoperative peak factor activity level of 80%–100% for patients with hemophilia A and 60%–80% for hemophilia B. They also define postoperative goals, with hemophilia A patients recommended to maintain peak factor levels of 60%–80% on postoperative days 1–3 and 40%–60% for hemophilia B. On postoperative days 4–6, the recommended peak activity is 40%–60% in hemophilia A and 30%–50% in hemophilia B. The recommendations extend as far out to postoperative days 7–14, at which point hemophilia A patients should be maintained at 30%–50% peak factor activity and 30%–40% in hemophilia B. These goals are considered minimum thresholds for safety and minimization of hemorrhagic complications. In certain cases, even higher goals may be clinically reasonable, but they require thoughtful and careful consensus among the multidisciplinary teams, as higher goals lead to higher healthcare costs and resource utilization. In terms of unique applications to our sEEG experience, the unpredictably of when our patient was going to undergo explantation (after a sufficient number of seizures were collected) meant that a standard application of the WFH recommendations did not apply. We decided to retarget a higher peak factor activity goal on postoperative day 11 so the patient could safely undergo sEEG lead removal, as opposed to the standard, less stringent late postoperative goals.

There are other reports in the neurosurgical literature that have found success with similar strategies to our experience. Kocher and colleagues reported successful perioperative management for hemophilia A in 2 neurosurgical patients: one who underwent single-level anterior cervical discectomy and fusion and another who underwent resection of a left temporal astrocytoma.5 In the latter case, the patient was found to have a factor VIII activity of 36% and was started on a recombinant factor VIII regimen of 50 units/kg prior to surgery, and 25 units/kg 12 hours after surgery, followed by 25 units/kg daily on days 1–7 for a goal of > 60%. Similarly, no bleeding occurred during surgery or after. The authors concluded that their multidisciplinary strategy was vital in the successful treatment of their patients, which we would certainly agree with given our experience. Palsma et al. published a case of deep brain stimulation surgery for essential tremor in a patient with hemophilia A.6 A recombinant factor VIII infusion regimen was designed to achieve a preoperative goal of 100%, and a goal of 80%–100% thereafter, continued until postoperative day 10. They reported a standard volume of blood loss and no hemorrhagic complications. Gong and colleagues described 2 cases of drug-resistant epilepsy in pediatric hemophilia patients.7 The first patient was a 29-month-old male with hemophilia B and the second a 6-year-old male with hemophilia A. Both had preoperative MRI and FDG-PET scans revealing foci of encephalomalacia. Under the guidance of a pediatric hematology service, a preoperative regimen of factor replacement and prothrombin complex concentrate was prescribed, titrated to achieve normal factor assay levels. Both patients underwent temporo-parietal-occipital disconnection with minimal intraoperative blood loss and no postoperative hemorrhagic complications. Their infusion regimen continued for 1 week postsurgery. Of note, sEEG was not performed in the staging workup and preoperative planning for their drug-resistant epilepsy, as was described in our case report.

Observations

In our case illustration, our patient safely underwent placement, monitoring, and removal of sEEG leads with close monitoring of his factor IX assays and appropriate factor replacement. No hemorrhagic or clinical complications were observed. This illustration is meaningful, as the implications of maintaining external intracranial hardware for a prolonged period come with compounded risk of new bleeding, in addition to the need for removal.8 To our knowledge, this is the first case report of a hemophilia patient undergoing sEEG placement, monitoring, and removal.

Lessons

We utilized a recombinant factor infusion strategy based on WFH recommendations with a similar multidisciplinary framework implemented in previous studies. The novel contribution our illustrative case demonstrates lies in the nature of sEEG surgery and monitoring. Unlike other implantable intracranial devices (deep brain stimulation, responsive neurostimulation, etc.), sEEG leads are external and temporary. Trauma to sEEG leads during patient care and seizures can lead to intracranial trauma and hemorrhage, particularly in the hemophilia population. Furthermore, a separate procedure for removal also implicates an added risk of hemorrhage. This requires more stringent peak factor activity goals outside of the standard postoperative recommendations, as patients will require more stringent preoperative replacement as opposed to the more relaxed late postoperative goals defined by the WFH. In addition to this novel demonstration, our case contributes to the extant literature that a multidisciplinary approach to hemophilia management can help patients safely undergo a wide range of neurosurgical procedures.
