BCG vaccination and household TB exposure in childhood tuberculosis: a community-based case-control study in rural Bangladesh
Authors: Taifur Rahman, Satyajit Kundu, Ahmed Hossain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBCG vaccination and household exposure to tuberculosis (TB) are central to childhood TB control, yet their delivery and effectiveness vary considerably across communities. Evidence from community-based studies in high-transmission, resource-limited settings remains sparse, particularly among marginalised populations such as tea-garden worker communities. This study evaluates their protective effect against childhood TB in rural Bangladesh to address this gap.MethodsWe conducted a community-based case-control study in Moulavibazar District, enrolling children aged 2–14 years from January 8 to April 30, 2023. Cases had newly diagnosed, confirmed TB; controls were community-matched by neighborhood children without respiratory symptoms. We used multivariable logistic regression to calculate adjusted odds ratios (aORs). The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was used to assess model calibration. For the Random Forest model, variable importance was assessed using mean decrease in accuracy (MDA) with permutation-based testing.ResultsThe dataset comprised 207 cases and 207 controls, with a mean age of 10.09 ± 3.69 years among cases and 9.38 ± 3.37 years among controls. The multivariable analysis identified adolescent age (11–14 years vs. 2–5 years: aOR 2.53, 95% CI = 1.33–4.80), male sex (aOR 1.79, 95% CI = 1.19–2.71), and a family history of TB within the last 6 months (aOR 1.92, 95% CI = 1.22–3.03) as having an association with TB. BCG vaccination demonstrated a significant protective association, reducing the odds of TB by 57% (aOR 0.43, 95% CI = 0.20–0.93). A random forest model confirmed these as the most important predictors, achieving a classification accuracy of 71.6% (AUC = 0.74, sensitivity = 68.5%, specificity = 73.9%).ConclusionIn rural Bangladesh, childhood TB is associated with adolescent age, male sex, and household exposure. Intensifying universal BCG vaccination and prioritizing household TB exposure assessment, especially for adolescent boys, are critical to reducing the disease burden.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-14043-6.Keywords: Tuberculosis, Childhood tuberculosis, Case-control study, BCG Vaccination, Bangladesh

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global health challenge; with an estimated 10 × 6 million new cases and 1 × 6 million deaths reported worldwide in 2022 [1]. Children younger than 15 years account for approximately 11% of incident cases, corresponding to more than 1.2 million cases annually, with the majority occurring in low- and middle-income countries [2, 3]. In Bangladesh, TB remains a major public health concern despite a well-structured national control programme [4]. The country is listed among the 30 high TB-burden nations identified by WHO, and in 2023 reported more than 300,000 incident cases [1]. Children are estimated to account for 11–13% of this burden, and many deaths have occurred due to multidrug-resistant tuberculosis [5–8]. Because most children acquire infection from infectious adults, paediatric TB incidence is a key indicator of community transmission and epidemic control.

Rural and marginalised populations in Bangladesh—including those in tea garden communities—face compounded vulnerabilities arising from poverty, overcrowding, and poor access to healthcare [9]. These social determinants heighten the risk of ongoing transmission and limit the effectiveness of standard TB control strategies. Understanding risk and protective factors for childhood TB in such communities is essential to designing interventions tailored to the realities of high-burden, resource-constrained settings.

The Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) vaccine, administered at birth through the national Expanded Programme on Immunization, is known to protect against severe forms of TB in children, although its effectiveness against pulmonary TB and in high-transmission environments remains debated [10, 11]. Household contact tracing, which is a proxy to control household TB exposure, is another cornerstone of TB control programmes, aiming to identify and treat latent TB infection among contacts of index cases [11, 12]. Yet, evidence on its effectiveness in preventing paediatric TB in rural high-burden settings remains sparse.

Environmental and socioeconomic risk factors—including overcrowded housing, absence of a separate kitchen, and rural residence—have consistently been linked with increased TB transmission among children [13–16]. Younger children are particularly vulnerable to infection from household members, whereas older children may acquire infection from broader community exposure [16–18]. These patterns highlight the importance of interventions that address both household- and community-level determinants.

Despite recognition of the importance of BCG vaccination and household contact management, robust community-based data from Bangladesh are limited. Most available evidence comes from facility-based studies, which often overlook the most marginalised children who do not present to health services. To address this gap, we conducted a community-based case–control study in a rural, low-income population of Bangladesh. Our primary aim was to examine the independent association of BCG vaccination and recent family history of TB with childhood TB among children aged 2–14 years. Moreover, we additionally assessed the role of sociodemographic and environmental factors in shaping the odds of childhood TB in this setting.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a community-based, case–control study in Moulavibazar District, Bangladesh, from January 8 to April 30, 2023. Moulavibazar was selected because of its high tuberculosis (TB) burden and the presence of an established TB control programme operated by HEED Bangladesh. This programme serves hard-to-reach populations in the Sylhet division, including tea and rubber garden communities where many ethnic minority groups reside. The programme’s registry provided a sampling frame covering about 50,000 households across seven sub-districts. The study population comprised children of tea-garden workers or those living in the neighborhoods of tea gardens, a group living in densely settled communities characterized by overcrowded housing and low income. Reported household income did not exceed 20,000 Bangladeshi taka (about US$166) per month. Most fathers had limited formal education, with few progressing beyond incomplete secondary schooling. These socioeconomic circumstances result in families residing in small, overcrowded dwellings, which can facilitate the transmission of TB.

Sample size estimation

The sample size for this case-control study was calculated to ensure adequate statistical power to detect significant associations between a key exposure variable (family history of TB) and childhood tuberculosis. The desired power was set at 80% (β = 0.20), a conventional threshold for ensuring a high probability of detecting a true effect if one exists, and a two-sided alpha (α) level of 0.05 was chosen. The calculation was based on detecting an odds ratio (OR) of 2.0 or greater. This value was selected based on prior literature suggesting that established risk factors, such as a family history of TB, often confer at least a doubling of risk [19]. The proportion of controls with a positive family history of TB was assumed to be approximately 20% (p₀ = 0.20). Using the formula for unmatched case-control studies: n = (Z_(α/2)√(2\bar p(1-\bar p)) + Z_β√(p₀(1-p₀)+p₁(1-p₁)))2 / (p₁-p₀)2, where p₀ = 0.20 (expected exposure proportion in controls), p₁ = p₀×OR/(1+p₀×(OR-1)) with OR = 2.0, and \bar p = (p₀+p₁)/2 [20]. The required sample size was calculated to be approximately 200 cases and 200 controls. The final enrolment of 207 cases and 207 controls successfully met and slightly exceeded this calculated target. This sample size provides sufficient power to identify the primary factors of interest, such as family history of TB and BCG vaccination status.

Case and control selection

Potential cases were identified through active surveillance at Directly Observed Treatment, Short-course (DOTS) centres operated by HEED Bangladesh. Eligible cases were children aged 2–14 years residing in the study area, diagnosed with active TB between January 8 and April 30, 2023. Diagnosis followed WHO guidelines for paediatric TB and was based on clinical evaluation supported by sputum smear microscopy, GeneXpert MTB/RIF testing, or chest radiography [21]. A case was also defined as a presentation highly suggestive of TB, with initiation of a full course of anti-TB treatment by a senior physician from HEED Bangladesh and documented clinical response. As our objective is to identify determinants of TB notification under programmatic conditions rather than to evaluate diagnostic accuracy, any misclassification from clinically diagnosed cases is likely to be non-differential across exposure groups. Children with incomplete medical records or without parental consent were excluded. For each case, one control was recruited from the same community within or outside the tea garden. Controls were selected using a systematic random-walk method beginning in the case’s neighbourhood to minimise bias related to healthcare-seeking behaviour. Controls were required to be free of respiratory illness, as verified through a standardized checklist covering chronic cough (lasting more than 2 weeks), fever, night sweats, and weight loss. Excluding children with respiratory symptoms from the control group, while defining cases as those with TB, helps reduce misclassification bias by minimizing the likelihood of including undiagnosed TB cases among controls. All legal guardians of the selected cases and controls were informed about the purpose of the study, assured of the confidentiality of the information provided, and given verbal or written informed consent prior to participation. Participation was completely voluntary, and guardians could withdraw their consent from the study at any time without penalty or consequences.

Data collection

Four trained research assistants from North South University, blinded to case–control status, administered a pretested structured questionnaire to primary caregivers in the local language (Bengali or Sylheti). The questionnaire collected information on demographic characteristics (age, sex, residence within or outside tea gardens, household income, and household size), parental education and occupation, BCG vaccination status, and household exposure to TB.

BCG vaccination was confirmed through vaccination cards or health records; if unavailable, parental recall was supplemented by inspection for a BCG scar. Household contact tracing was defined according to national TB control programme guidelines as systematic screening of household members for TB symptoms after an index case diagnosis. Given that direct data on household contact tracing activities were unavailable, we used household TB exposure—defined as the presence of at least one TB case in the same household—as a proxy for household contact tracing in this study. Exposure to a household TB case was verified by medical records or self-report. Clinical data for cases, including diagnostic test results and treatment history, were abstracted from HEED Bangladesh DOTS centre records. The distance from each participant’s home to the nearest DOTS centre was approximately measured and categorised. The questionnaire and the data can be found at https://osf.io/u2kmv/files/osfstorage.

Primary exposure variables

The main exposures of interest were BCG vaccination status and household TB contact. BCG vaccination was classified as complete if verified by health records or the presence of a scar, and as incomplete otherwise. It was hypothesized to provide protection against TB in children. The sources of BCG vaccination are given in Supplement A. Household TB contact was defined as the diagnosis of TB in any household member within the preceding 6 months. This definition aligns with the Bangladesh National TB Control Programme’s household contact tracing strategy, which involves systematic screening of family members after the identification of an index case. In the absence of directly recorded contact-tracing data, this study treats household TB exposure—i.e., living in the same household as an individual with diagnosed TB—as a proxy indicator for household contact tracing, under the assumption that exposure implies at least some level of tracing and shared risk.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all categorical variables and presented as frequencies and percentages. Controls were recruited from the same neighborhoods as cases to enhance community-level comparability; however, no individual-level pair matching was performed. Thus, unconditional logistic regression was used for all analyses. The results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (ORs), with corresponding p-values. An OR > 1 indicates the factor increases the odds of having TB. An OR < 1 suggests the factor is protective, decreasing the odds of TB. Statistical significance is determined by the 95% Confidence Interval (CI); if the CI includes 1.0, the result is not statistically significant. Variable selection for the regression model was performed by Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) [22]. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. We performed a complementary analysis using a Random Forest (RF) model to assess the robustness of the logistic regression findings. RF was chosen for its ability to model complex, nonlinear relationships and identify important predictors, even in the presence of highly correlated variables. The RF model was implemented using the randomForest() function from the randomForest package in R. We used the following hyperparameters: ntree = 500 (number of trees), mtry = √p (number of variables randomly sampled as candidates at each split, where p = number of predictors), and nodesize = 5 (minimum size of terminal nodes). Variable importance was assessed using mean decrease in accuracy (MDA), and variables were ranked by MDA; only those with positive MDA values were retained. A threshold of 3 was used to distinguish important from negligible predictors. To evaluate the model’s accuracy, the data were split into a 70/30 ratio: 70% for training and 30% for testing. This approach allowed for validation of the model’s performance on unseen data, ensuring a more robust assessment of its predictive capabilities.

Findings from this case-control study indicate several significant determinants of TB risk. Age was an important factor: children aged 11–14 years had more than twice the odds of TB compared to those aged 2–5 years (OR 2.39, 95% CI = 1.29–4.41). Male sex was also strongly associated with case status (OR 1.79; 95% CI = 1.21–2.65). Socioeconomic indicators—including household income, household size, and parental education—suggested higher odds of TB; however, most estimates were insignificant, with confidence intervals overlapping unity, providing limited evidence for independent effects. Distance to the DOTS center and father’s occupation showed no meaningful association, suggesting that, in this high-burden setting, several socioeconomic indicators had confidence intervals overlapping unity, providing limited evidence of independent effects in the unadjusted analysis.

Living within a tea garden, compared with outside, did not increase the odds of TB (OR 1.11, 95% CI = 0.71–1.74). Neither higher household income nor greater parental education appeared protective. Larger households (≥7 members) were associated with slightly higher odds (OR 1.10, 95% CI = 0.64–1.89), although this difference was not statistically significant. Maternal employment outside the home was suggestively associated with an increased risk (OR 1.52, 95% CI = 0.98–2.36).

The strongest predictor was recent family history of TB: children with a household member diagnosed in the past six months had nearly double the odds of disease (OR 1.99, 95% CI = 1.29–3.07). This emphasizes the urgent need for systematic screening and follow-up of household contacts.

Dietary patterns showed complex associations. Poor protein intake (<4 days/week) was associated with lower odds of TB (OR 0.68, 95% CI = 0.46–1.01), although the result was borderline significant. This counterintuitive finding likely reflects reverse causation, as children with TB may have been given more protein after diagnosis.

Finally, BCG vaccination showed a significant protective effect (OR 0.49, 95% CI = 0.24–0.99), with vaccinated children having approximately half the odds of developing TB compared to those who were unvaccinated.

Adjusted analysis

Variable selection for the regression model was guided by a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG), which was used to assess potential multicollinearity and identify appropriate adjustment variables (see Supplement B for detailed results). The DAG represents the hypothesized causal structure in this case-control study, where Household TB exposure is the primary exposure and TB Case Status is the outcome. To estimate the unbiased effect of Household TB exposure on TB Case Status, all backdoor paths must be closed without blocking the causal pathway or introducing selection bias. Based on the DAG, adjustment for the child’s age, sex, history of BCG vaccination and socioeconomic indicators (e.g., family income or residence) is required in the multivariable logistic regression model to block these confounding pathways. Notably, there are no causal arrows from Household TB exposure to variables such as protein intake or parental education, indicating that these variables function as baseline confounders rather than mediators and are therefore not necessary for adjustment.

To ensure the regression model’s reliability, we assessed multicollinearity using variance inflation factors (VIFs); all variables had VIFs close to 1, indicating multicollinearity was not a concern in our analysis (Supplement C).

The multivariable logistic regression model identified several factors independently associated with childhood tuberculosis, as shown in Table 2. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (χ2 = 8.34, p = 0.40). Male gender was a significant risk factor, with boys having nearly twice the odds of tuberculosis compared to girls (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.79, 95% CI = 1.19–2.71). A strong age-gradient was observed, with adolescents aged 11–14 years having significantly higher odds of disease compared to children aged 2–5 years (aOR 2.53, 95% CI = 1.33–4.80). The most potent risk factor was a family history of tuberculosis within the preceding six months, which was associated with a near doubling of odds (aOR 1 × 92, 95% CI = 1.22–3.03).

In contrast, BCG vaccination demonstrated a substantial protective effect, reducing the odds of tuberculosis by more than half (aOR 0.43, 95% CI = 0.20–0.93). Several other factors, including residence within a tea garden, household size, father’s occupation, and distance to the nearest DOTS centre, showed no statistically significant association with tuberculosis status in the adjusted model. Although not statistically significant at the conventional level, children of working mothers showed a trend toward higher odds than children of housewives (aOR 1.96, 95% CI = 0.96–4.02).

Complementary machine learning analysis

We performed a complementary analysis using a Random Forest (RF) model to assess the robustness of the findings derived from logistic regression. The RF model achieved an accuracy of 71.6% (95% CI = 65.1–77.1%), sensitivity of 68.5%, specificity of 73.9%, positive predictive value of 72.4%, negative predictive value of 70.1%, and AUC of 0.74 (95% CI = 0.69–0.79). The Random Forest model achieved a modest accuracy of 71.6% on the test set (n ≈ 124), which is above the 50% baseline but indicates limited discriminatory power. The variable importance ranking was directionally consistent with the logistic regression findings, providing complementary rather than confirmatory evidence. The findings from Fig. 1 highlight key intervention points that persist across all adjusted models. Age, sex, family history of TB, and BCG vaccination were the top four predictors, with MDA values exceeding 3. Variables were retained if their Mean Decrease in Accuracy (MDA) exceeded a threshold of 3. Among the top predictors, MDA values ranged from 3.43 to 11.58 (representing the increase in misclassification error when the variable is permuted). Adolescent boys emerged as a particularly vulnerable group, supporting the need for age- and sex-targeted prevention strategies. Household TB exposure history was a strong discriminator, reinforcing the central role of systematic contact tracing. BCG vaccination remained a protective factor, underlining the importance of maintaining high coverage. Taken together, the model provides a data-driven rationale for prioritising adolescent-focused interventions, rigorous household contact management, and universal BCG vaccination in resource-constrained, high-burden settings. A similar finding is also observed using the mean decrease in Gini (Supplement D).

Discussion

This community-based case–control study provides consistent evidence on the determinants of childhood TB in a high-burden rural district of Bangladesh. Using both classical regression and machine-learning approaches, we consistently identified a core set of associated and protective factors. The results show that childhood TB is not a random occurrence but is shaped by demographic vulnerability, household exposure, and the presence or absence of key preventive interventions. The tea garden worker population studied here is a rarely examined group, with comparable findings reported in a similar community in India [23].

Our findings consistently identified increasing age as a major risk factor for childhood TB. In logistic regression, adolescents aged 11–14 years had more than twice the odds of TB compared with younger children, and the random forest model confirmed age as the strongest predictor for classifying TB status. Similar associations have been reported in previous studies [24–26]. This age gradient may reflect waning protection from infant BCG vaccination, age-specific social mixing patterns that increase exposure beyond the household, and biological changes associated with puberty [27, 28]. These results highlight adolescence as a critical but often neglected window for TB prevention within child health programs.

We also found that male sex was a significant independent associated factor, confirmed by both regression and random forest analyses, consistent with findings from a global study [29]. Although sex disparities are well described in adult TB epidemiology, evidence in children is less consistent [30]. The mechanisms are probably multifactorial, including social and behavioural differences that increase exposure in boys, as well as possible biological variation in immune responses. Further research is needed to clarify the pathways underlying this increased susceptibility.

Our models identified a recent family history of TB as the strongest risk factor, nearly doubling the odds of childhood disease. This finding underscores the high risk of transmission within households and highlights contact tracing as an essential frontline intervention. Hierarchical clustering further grouped family history with BCG status and demographic factors, suggesting these exposures are interlinked within broader pathways of vulnerability. Evidence from meta-analyses confirms that close contact with a known TB case—most often a household member—is the single strongest predictor of paediatric TB [31]. Each index case, therefore, represents a sentinel event that should trigger urgent household TB exposure assessment, screening, and provision of preventive therapy for all child contacts [32]. Failure to implement this systematically perpetuates cycles of transmission within already vulnerable families.

Our study provides consistent, confounder-adjusted evidence for the protective association of BCG vaccination. Logistic regression showed vaccination reduced the odds of TB by more than 50%, and the random forest model ranked it among the most influential predictors. Meta-analyses similarly report risk reductions of 18–37% for all forms of TB and 19–42% for pulmonary TB in children younger than 5 years [33, 34]. These findings reaffirm the value of universal BCG vaccination in high-burden settings, counter concerns around vaccine hesitancy, and highlight the importance of sustaining strong routine immunisation coverage. Notably, protection is greatest in early childhood and wanes with age, with little or no effect observed in adolescents and adults [35]. The BCG effect estimate is based on a small number of unvaccinated children (23 cases, 12 controls), making it unstable and vulnerable to residual confounding by healthcare access and other unmeasured factors. Given the near-universal coverage (~90%), these findings require confirmation in larger, more diverse populations.

Interestingly, many traditional socioeconomic indicators—such as parental education, family income, and household crowding—were not independently associated with TB in the multivariable model. The clustering analysis further suggested that many of these variables were correlated with each other, forming a socioeconomic dimension that, as a whole, was less discriminatory than the core biological and exposure-related factors. This does not imply that poverty is irrelevant; rather, in this uniformly low-income rural community, its effects may be pervasive, creating a high background risk. In such a context, the more proximate determinants of individual exposure (family history) and susceptibility (age, sex, vaccination status) become the primary differentiators between cases and controls.

A key strength of this study is its community-based design, which reduces the selection bias inherent in facility-based studies and provides a more accurate picture of the disease burden and its determinants in the population. The triangulation of findings using both regression and machine-learning models enhances the credibility of our conclusions.

However, our study has several limitations. First, BCG vaccination status was ascertained through a combination of vaccination cards, BCG scars, or caregiver recall, which may differ between cases (who were already engaged in the health system) and community controls. If health-system engagement led to better documentation or recall among cases, this could result in differential misclassification, potentially biasing the BCG estimate either toward or away from the null depending on the direction of the misclassification. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the BCG findings. Second, we were unable to account for all potential confounders, such as nutritional status or environmental tobacco smoke. Third, the borderline significance of a mother’s occupation as a risk factor in the regression model warrants further investigation with a larger sample size. Fourth, cases were identified through DOTS centers, while controls were recruited from the community, which may introduce selection bias if children with TB who do not present to DOTS centers differ systematically from those who do. However, our community-based control selection was designed to minimise this by matching on neighborhood. Fifth, parental recall of household TB exposure and dietary factors is prone to recall bias. To mitigate this, we cross-checked family TB history against available medical records wherever feasible and verified BCG vaccination status through objective measures—vaccination cards or scar inspection—rather than relying solely on parental report. Sixth, we could not account for a few unmeasured confounders, such as nutritional status, passive smoke exposure, and indoor air pollution, which may have biased our effect estimates. Finally, our study population comprised tea-garden worker communities in a single district of Bangladesh, which may limit generalisability to urban or non-agricultural rural populations.

We were unable to distinguish between laboratory-confirmed and clinically diagnosed TB cases because this information was not consistently available across all participating health facilities. This may introduce outcome misclassification, as clinically diagnosed cases could include individuals with other respiratory conditions, potentially biasing our estimates toward the null. Controls were also not systematically tested for TB using GeneXpert or chest X-ray; instead, they were screened using a symptom checklist. As a result, subclinical or asymptomatic TB among controls cannot be entirely ruled out. Any such misclassification would likely bias our effect estimates toward the null, potentially underestimating true associations. Future studies should prioritize laboratory confirmation of TB cases to strengthen diagnostic validity. Moreover, for the variable ‘family history of TB within 6 months,’ we cannot entirely exclude the possibility that some household members were diagnosed during or after the child’s contact investigation rather than before the child’s illness. This reverse temporality could inflate the observed association and is a recognized limitation in contact-based TB studies.

The study was conducted over a four-month period (January–April 2023). TB incidence in Bangladesh shows seasonal variation, with peak transmission often occurring during the winter and early spring months when indoor crowding increases. Our study period captures this high-transmission season but may not fully represent risk-factor distributions in other seasons. Although the data were collected after the COVID-19 pandemic, the pandemic may still have influenced tuberculosis detection [36]. Consequently, our findings may overestimate associations that are stronger during high-transmission periods and may not generalise fully to low-transmission seasons. A full-year study would be needed to capture annual variation.

Our finding of waning protection with age raises the question of whether a BCG booster in early adolescence might be beneficial in high-burden settings. Current WHO guidelines do not recommend BCG revaccination due to limited evidence of additional protection, but recent trials of novel TB vaccines in adolescents (e.g., M72/AS01E [37]) suggest that this age group may be an important target for new vaccine development. In the absence of a new vaccine, strengthening contact tracing and preventive therapy for adolescent household contacts remains the most feasible policy option

Conclusion

This study identifies a profile of the most vulnerable child to TB infection in this community: an unvaccinated adolescent boy with a recent family history of TB. Our results provide supporting evidence for two cornerstone strategies for TB control: universal BCG vaccination and aggressive household contact tracing and management. Public health efforts should be intensified to ensure every child receives BCG at birth and that each TB case in a household is associated with an onward transmission risk. Focusing resources on these evidence-based interventions will be essential to reducing the enduring burden of childhood tuberculosis in Bangladesh and similar settings worldwide.
