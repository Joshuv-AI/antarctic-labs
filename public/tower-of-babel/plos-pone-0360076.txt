Self-care practices of people living with HIV and hypertension or diabetes comorbidities in low-and-middle income countries: A systematic review

Andrew K Tusubira, Andrew S Ssemata, Anne R Katahoire, Carl May, Christine K Nalwadda, Moffat Nyirenda, Susannah Mayhew

ABSTRACT
Background: People living with HIV in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs) face a growing burden of hypertension and diabetes comorbidities. Although self-care is essential, limited evidence exists on how patients manage these conditions concurrently within resource-constrained settings. We examined self-care practices among people living with HIV and hypertension or diabetes comorbidities in LMICs and identified treatment burdens related to these practices. Methods: We searched Medline, EMBASE, PsycINFO, Global Health, Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL, WHO Global Index Medicus, African Journals Online and, Google Scholar for primary studies conducted in LMICs and published between January 2013 and March 2026, regardless of study design and data collection method. Two reviewers independently screened, extracted data, and assessed study quality, resolving discrepancies through discussions. We used thematic synthesis to analyze self-care practices, and mapped findings onto the Normalization Process Theory to identify associated treatment burdens. Confidence in the evidence was assessed using the GRADE-CERQual approach. Results: Thirteen qualitative studies from six African countries, comprising 458 participants, were included. Seven studies were rated high quality while six were moderate. Five themes emerged with moderate-to-high confidence in evidence: 1) Sense-making of illnesses and medications; 2) Divergent treatment approaches between HIV and hypertension or diabetes treatment; 3) Adoption of health-promoting behaviors, including lifestyle changes; 4) Coping with psychosocial distress, and 5) Strategies to navigate healthcare insecurities. Related treatment burdens were linked to prescribed healthcare tasks, disparities between HIV and hypertension/diabetes care, and patient-initiated efforts to compensate for gaps in hypertension/diabetes care. Conclusion: Self-care practices were diverse and shaped by individuals’ interpretations of illnesses, structural inequities, and the cumulative demands of multimorbidity. Strengthening consistent education on illness and medications, psychosocial support, and lifestyle guidance can improve equitable integrated care and support effective self-care. Future research should quantify self-care workload and treatment burden and evaluate integrated care models that reduce patient workload. Systematic review registration: PROSPERO: CRD42023463738.

Introduction Non-communicable diseases (NCDs), particularly hypertension (HTN) and diabetes mellitus (DM), are increasingly prevalent among people living with HIV (PLHIV) in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [ 1 , 2 ]. Projections suggest that up to 84% of PLHIV will develop at least one NCD by 2030 [ 3 ]. Globally, about 24% of PLHIV live with HTN and 9% with DM [ 4 , 5 ], with nearly 59% of those with HTN residing in sub‑Saharan Africa and HIV/DM comorbidity reaching up to 14.9% in some countries [ 1 , 4 , 6 , 7 ]. This growing multimorbidity is attributed to increased life expectancy from effective antiretroviral therapy (ART), ART-related metabolic effects, HIV-associated inflammatory and vascular changes, and lifestyle risk factors [ 8 – 10 ]. Coexisting HTN or DM complicates HIV care through polypharmacy, overlapping symptoms, and frequent hospitalizations, thereby reducing quality of life, heightening mortality and increasing economic strain on patients, caregivers, and health systems [ 11 ]. Consequently, global and national strategies now emphasize integrated, patient-centered care models to address multimorbidity [ 12 ]. Self-care is a core component of chronic disease management, enacted through informed decision-making, daily healthy behaviors and adjustments [ 13 , 14 ]. Self-care is broadly defined as the ability to care for oneself to achieve, maintain, or promote optimal health and well-being [ 13 ]. However, in LMICs, socio-cultural norms, financial constraints, and health system limitations influence how chronic conditions are managed [ 15 , 16 ]. In the context of multimorbidity, such as HIV and HTN or DM, self-care extends beyond adherence to a single treatment regimen to encompass continuous decision-making and a wider range of patient-driven activities embedded in daily life [ 17 ]. Moreover, health systems are increasingly shifting routine care tasks to these patients, placing substantial care burdens on them [ 18 ]. It is therefore critical to understand how they navigate these intersecting demands to design feasible, patient-centered interventions that fit patients’ real-life contexts. Efforts to integrate HTN and DM services into HIV platforms have expanded across LMICs, with evidence of benefits such as staff capacitation, improved screening, detection and continuity of NCD care [ 19 , 20 ]. However, HIV/NCD integration remains uneven and constrained by shortages of NCD medicines, infrastructure, clinical guidance, and human resources [ 19 , 21 ]. Consequently, more than half of PLHIV with HTN or DM have uncontrolled blood pressure or glucose despite good HIV outcomes among the same patients [ 22 , 23 ], highlighting persistent gaps in both service delivery and patient self-management. Effective self-care improves adherence and clinical outcomes while reducing health system burden, but it requires understanding patients’ lived experiences, challenges, and coping strategies [ 24 , 25 ]. For people living with multimorbidity, treatment burden further undermines their quality of life and overall health outcomes [ 18 ]. However, little is known about how PLHIV with HTN or DM concurrently navigate self-care demands within resource-constrained settings, and how these demands accumulate into treatment burden. This gap limits the design of feasible, patient-centered interventions and poorly aligned self-care demands can increase treatment burden, negatively affecting outcomes for both conditions. We therefore aimed to examine self-care practices among PLHIV with HTN or DM in LMICs, with a secondary aim of identifying the treatment burdens associated with these practices. The Normalization Process Theory (NPT) guided the identification of the treatment burdens [ 26 , 27 ]. NPT was selected because it provides a structured, empirically validated framework for understanding the work patients undertake to enact healthcare practices and has been applied to multimorbidity research in LMICs [ 28 , 29 ]. Unlike the Burden of Treatment Theory [ 30 ], which focuses primarily on capacity-demand imbalances, NPT enables systematic mapping of the specific tasks and processes through which treatment burdens arise and are sustained in everyday life [ 26 , 27 ]. Findings are intended to inform clinicians to improve patient self-management for multimorbidity, guide program implementers in strengthening self-care in HIV/NCD integrated care and identify research gaps to support the development of sustainable, patient-centered self-care strategies. Methods Design A qualitative systematic review using thematic synthesis conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 reporting guidelines and the Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research (ENTREQ) statement [ 31 ]. The complete PRISMA and ENTREQ checklists are provided in S1 Table and S2 Table , respectively. The review was conducted between September 2023 and March 2026 and was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO: CRD42023463738). Although the original protocol anticipated inclusion of qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods studies, only qualitative studies met the final inclusion criteria. Consequently, the review design and synthesis focused exclusively on qualitative evidence. Our primary review question focused on exploring self-care practices among individuals living with HIV and comorbid HTN or DM in LMICs. During synthesis, the demands associated with managing multiple illnesses consistently emerged across studies. In line with qualitative methodology [ 32 ], we iteratively expanded the analysis to identify these demands as treatment burden by mapping our thematic analysis findings onto the NPT [ 26 , 27 ]. NPT is a structured framework for understanding treatment burden and has been applied to multimorbidity research in LMICs [ 28 , 29 ]. Treatment burden was conceptualized as the healthcare workload or tasks that patients do to manage their conditions, consistent with established definitions [ 18 , 33 ]. Mapping findings onto this framework enabled systematic interpretation of how self-care practices translate into everyday burdens across patients’ lives. Eligibility criteria Population. Eligible studies included adults (≥18 years) living with HIV and HTN or DM or both comorbidities, irrespective of whether HTN or DM onset preceded or followed HIV infection. We focused on PLHIV because they represent a growing population experiencing chronic multimorbidity and they have established care platforms which can influence self-care. Adults were included because self-care responsibilities and treatment decision-making differ substantially from those of children and adolescents. We did not restrict inclusion by duration of diagnosis or treatment status (e.g., receiving treatment or not), as these characteristics were inconsistently reported and were not central to the review objective. Phenomenon of interest/outcome. We included studies reporting self-care practices or behaviors undertaken by individuals to manage HIV and comorbid HTN and/or DM. The primary outcome of this review was self-care practices for managing HIV and HTN or DM multimorbidity in LMICs. Self-care practices were defined as health-related actions or measures undertaken by individuals to manage illness, maintain health, or restore well-being, encompassing physical, psychological, spiritual and social strategies [ 34 ]. This review did not include studies reporting clinical or disease outcomes among PLHIV and HTN or DM comorbidities. Type of studies/ study design. We included primary studies employing qualitative, quantitative or mixed methods designs. Eligible quantitative studies comprised observational studies (cross-sectional, cohort and case-control studies), interventional studies and surveys. Qualitative studies were eligible regardless of design or data collection method. We excluded secondary research papers (including systematic reviews and protocols), policy analyses, editorials, commentaries, abstracts, conceptual papers, and studies involving non-human participants. Context. Only studies conducted in LMICs were included. We focused on LMICs because distinct health system constraints, resource limitations, and the evolving integration of HIV and NCD care shape self-care practices in these settings. Therefore, patient experiences and challenges in LMICs differ markedly from those in high-income counties, influencing how self-care is understood and practiced. We searched the following databases: Ovid Medline, Ovid EMBASE, Ovid Global Health, Ovid PsycINFO, Web of Science (including Social Science Citation Index), Scopus, CINAHL via EBSCO, WHO Global Index Medicus, Google Scholar and African Journals Online (AJOL). The AJOL was included to capture African research not indexed in major databases. Search strategy. Search terms were developed in consultation with an information specialist at the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine (LSHTM). The search strategy combined three key concepts and their synonyms: “self-care”, “human immunodeficiency virus,” and “hypertension” or “diabetes”, using Boolean operators. We modified search terms as required by each database and used both keywords and medical subject headings (MeSH). Some of the MeSH terms were: “Self-Care”, “Self-manage”, “self-monitor”, “self-medicate”, “adherence”, “PLWHIV”, “PLHIV”, “HIV”, “HIVAIDS”, “HIV Infection”, “acquired immune deficiency syndrome” “SIDA” “Hypertension”, “Blood Pressure”, “Diabetes”, “Diabetes Mellitus”, “high blood sugar”, “hyperglycemia”. Reference lists of included studies were also searched for possible papers. Given AJOL’s limited search and export functionality, a manual search was done and retrieved titles and abstracts. The literature search covered studies published between January 2013 and March 2026 in LMICs. The review involved a two-stage search process. An initial data search was conducted in December 2023. To ensure that the review incorporated the most recent evidence prior to submission, we conducted a final update of the literature search in March 2026 by re-running the same search strategies across all databases. Newly identified records were screened using the same eligibility criteria and incorporated into the final synthesis. The period from 2013 was chosen because it captures both the expansion of self-care concepts in literature and a period during which HIV/NCD integration has evolved [ 35 , 36 ]. There were no language restrictions to maximize the scope of relevant articles from LMICs. Full search strategies, including MeSH terms, are provided in S3 Table . Study screening and selection. Following the search, all citations were imported into EndNote X20 software (Clarivate Analytics) [ 37 ], and duplicates removed. Screening was managed using Rayyan software [ 38 ]. Two reviewers (AKT and ASS) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts. We resolved discrepancies through discussion and by involving a third reviewer (SM). Data extraction. Two reviewers (AKT and ASS) independently extracted the data using an extraction form guided by Cochrane [ 39 , 40 ], with discrepancies resolved through discussions with another reviewer (SM). Extracted data included authors and year of publication, country, study aim, study design, participant characteristics (including age and sex), participant NCD comorbidity, sample size, data collection and analysis methods, and key findings. We also extracted texts, quotations and relevant data on self-care practices. Inter-rater reliability. Study selection and data extraction were performed independently by two reviewers. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa. For full-text study selection, (36 articles assessed; 13 included) the kappa value was 0.84, and for data extraction across double-extracted items, kappa value was 0.80, indicating substantial agreement between reviewers. Quality assessment. Since only qualitative studies were included, we used the critical appraisal skills program (CASP) appraisal checklist for qualitative studies to assess the quality of the studies [ 41 ]. CASP checklist for qualitative research is a widely used critical assessment tool for evaluating the quality and relevance of qualitative research by assessing their methodological rigor (including assessing key aspects like recruitment, data collection, and analysis) and ensuring that ethical standards were met. Two reviewers independently assessed each article and assigned an overall quality ranking of “low”, “medium”, or “high” quality. We followed the score and classification system suggested by Butler et al [ 42 ]. Hence, every “Yes” scored 1 point, every ‘Can’t tell or Not sure’ scored 0.5 points and every ‘No’ scored 0. Articles were then classified into three categories following their total score: 1) high-quality paper (scored between 9.5 and 10), moderate-quality paper (scored between 7.5 and 9), low-quality paper (scored less than 7.5) and those under 6 points were to be excluded. Consistent with qualitative evidence synthesis methodology, no studies were excluded based on quality assessment scores, as excluding studies solely on methodological quality grounds may remove contextually rich evidence and introduce selection bias [ 43 ]. Instead, quality appraisal was used to assess methodological rigor and transparency and to inform the interpretation of the review findings through the Grades of Recommendation, Assessment, Development and Evaluation – Confidence in Evidence from Reviews of Qualitative Research (GRADE-CERQual) approach [ 44 , 45 ]. Notably, after the appraisal, all included studies were rated as either high or moderate quality, and no low-quality studies were identified. Data analysis. Since only qualitative studies were included, we conducted thematic synthesis following Thomas and Harden’s three stages: 1) line-by-line coding; 2) development of descriptive themes; and 3) generation of analytical themes [ 43 ]. The coding framework was developed iteratively. Initial line-by-line coding was conducted independently by two reviewers (AKT and ASS) on a subset of included studies to develop and pilot the preliminary coding framework. The two reviewers compared their coding, discussed differences in interpretation, and refined the coding framework through consensus. This piloting phase allowed reviewers to test and calibrate the coding approach, identify areas of ambiguity, and establish a shared understanding of the coding categories before proceeding to the full data set. The revised framework was then applied to all included studies and further refined iteratively as new codes and patterns concepts emerged from the data. Regular discussions between reviewers were held throughout the synthesis process to ensure consistency, challenge assumptions, and maintain reflexivity in interpreting the findings. Discrepancies in coding or thematic interpretation were resolved through discussion and consensus, with consultation from a third reviewer (SM) when agreement could not be reached. To address reflexivity and minimize reviewer bias, the team (AT, AS, SM and AK) maintained ongoing critical dialogue throughout the synthesis process, reflecting on how prior assumptions and disciplinary perspectives might influence interpretation of the data. This reflexive approach was embedded throughout the analysis rather than a single discrete step. This inductive approach enabled reviewers to move beyond descriptive findings to generate interpretative and analytical insights. Descriptive themes of self-care practices were then generated to summarize the meaning of the initial codes and code groups. These descriptive themes were further interpreted and organized into analytical sub-themes and overarching themes, providing a deeper understanding of the data. Although the review protocol planned to include both qualitative and quantitative studies, our search yielded only qualitative evidence. Consequently, the synthesis focused exclusively on qualitative findings using thematic synthesis, rather than narrative synthesis, or meta-analysis. All eligible qualitative findings were included to ensure comprehensive and unbiased synthesis. Contributions of studies to the final thematic findings. We assessed each study’s relative contribution to the final synthesized results: (a process proposed by Thomas and Harden [ 43 ]). We used the subthemes of our thematic analysis to determine each study’s contribution. These sub-themes represented granular analytical insights, allowing us to evaluate how each study informed the overall synthesis. Since our review had 17 sub-themes, a score of one meant that the study provided information to one sub-theme, while a score of 17 meant that that study contributed to all the sub-themes of this review. (This approach has been used in prior review studies to score the contribution of individual papers to review findings [ 46 , 47 ]). Confidence in the findings and identifying the treatment burden. We used the GRADE-CERQual approach to assess the level of confidence in each review finding under its respective theme [ 44 , 45 ]. We first created a summary of review findings, then evaluated each review finding across the four GRADE-CERQual components: 1) methodological limitations, 2) coherence, 3) adequacy of data, and 4) relevance [ 44 , 45 ]. For each component, we judged the level of concern (in terms of no/very minor, minor, moderate, or serious concern), and used these judgements to assign an overall confidence rating (high, moderate, low or very low), starting with high confidence and downgrading as concerns increased [ 44 , 45 ]. Confidence judgments were reached through discussion and consensus between two reviewers (AKT and ASS). To identify treatment burdens, findings with high or moderate confidence were onto the NPT four domains: coherence, cognitive participation, collective action, and reflexive monitoring [ 26 , 27 ]. Finding that did not align with these domains were categorized as “unallocated” following Chikumbu et al. [ 28 ]. We identified specific treatment burdens under each domain by capturing demands and tasks described to take patients’ time and efforts in each review finding. Results from the thematic analysis Five key themes emerged from the thematic synthesis: 1) Sense-making of illnesses and medications through grading and prioritization; 2) Divergent treatment approaches for HIV and NCD comorbidities amid polypharmacy; 3) Adoption of health-promoting behaviors; 4) Coping with psychosocial distress; and 5) Strategies to navigate healthcare insecurities. Themes are presented to reflect progression from pharmacological self-care practices (themes 1 & 2) to non-pharmacological and system-level strategies (themes 3, 4 & 5). Details of the themes and corresponding quotations are in S5 Table . Theme one: Sense-making of illnesses and medications by grading and prioritizing them Sense-making of illnesses and medications emerged as a foundational self-care practice through which patients interpreted disease severity, evaluated treatment effectiveness, and prioritized care activities, influencing subsequent medication use, lifestyle adjustments, and coping strategies. Grading and classifying the severity of illnesses. Patients commonly assessed and ranked their condition based on perceived severity. Across several studies, HIV was described as a less severe, and more manageable illness compared with HTN or DM, which were viewed as unpredictable and potentially fatal [ 48 – 51 , 56 , 58 ]. This perception shaped treatment priorities and adherence behaviors. In contrast, HTN and DM were associated with sudden death, reinforcing fear and urgency in their management [ 56 , 58 – 60 ]: “The dangerous disease nowadays is blood pressure, because even sitting here, … I can die. While HIV, you stay longer “ (Female, 70 years old; HTN [ 56 ]). Greater confidence in antiretroviral therapy than in NCD medications. Patients consistently expressed strong trust in ART, describing ART as highly effective, reliable, and lifesaving [ 56 , 57 , 59 ]. This confidence fostered strong adherence to ART and optimism towards HIV care [ 50 , 52 , 56 , 57 , 59 ]. In contrast, medications for HTN and DM were seen as less effective, prompting doubts in efficacy and, use of herbal remedies to manage their NCD comorbidities [ 56 , 59 , 60 ]: “I went to the herbal clinic in search of an alternative, hoping to get treated. Because all these medications (hypertension and diabetes medication) are just for pain relief and not curative. The herbal clinic has medications that could cure diabetes completely” (Male, 60 years; HTN). Prioritizing care for HTN and DM comorbidities. Grading illnesses influenced how patients allocated their time, emotional effort, and limited financial resources towards care [ 48 , 51 , 56 , 58 , 59 ]. Because HIV care routines were well-established, participants prioritized HTN and DM management due to their perceived immediacy and danger [ 51 , 58 , 59 ]. Some patients turned to dietary changes and traditional remedies, but they still indicated frustration due to poor health outcomes for the NCDs [ 50 , 51 ]: “With pressure, it does not go down …, I try to eat things I am told to eat but it is always high.” (Female, 48 years old; HTN [ 51 ]). Theme two: Divergent treatment approaches for HIV and NCD comorbidities amidst managing polypharmacy Building on patients’ sense-making of illnesses and medications, participants described distinct treatment approaches for HIV compared with HTN and DM. While ART was consistently emphasized as the central treatment for HIV, multiple approaches, including alternative therapies, were used for the comorbidities. Consistent ART adherence versus intentional or selective non-adherence to NCD medication. Patients demonstrated strong adherence to ART, which was widely perceived as lifesaving and easy to manage through routine pill-taking [ 49 , 50 , 52 , 56 , 57 , 59 ]. The free availability and reliable access to ART further reinforced consistent use [ 50 , 52 , 56 , 58 ]. In contrast, adherence to HTN and DM medications was consistently lower. Although none of the studies quantified this discrepancy, the pattern of non-adherence to NCD medications was observed, even where NCD medicines were accessible [ 48 , 52 , 57 – 59 ]. Non-adherence to NCD medicines was linked to the absence of symptoms, inconsistent advice from health workers, medicine stock-outs and side effects [ 48 , 52 , 57 – 60 ]: “I… do not use insulin so much because it affects my legs. They get [so] swollen that I can’t walk. So, I use it some days and skips other days” (Female, 50 years; HTN&DM [ 58 ]). A few patients with reliable access expressed strong commitment to adherence, often motivated by personal or family health histories [ 48 , 56 ]: “My father died of hypertension, and uncle had a stroke because of it, so I don’t miss the hypertension drugs” (Male, 52 years; HTN [ 56 ]). Use of alternative and complementary treatments. Low confidence in NCD medications led many patients to supplement or replace treatment with herbal remedies included consuming bitter herbs and vegetables (such as ginger, garlic, mululuza ( Vernonia amygdalina ), prayer, or consulting with traditional healers [ 52 , 55 , 57 , 59 , 60 ]. The belief that these remedies would cure NCDs and positive experiences of symptom relief often reinforced trust in these remedies: “I take the mushroom when I see that my heart rate is high. … it helps with blood pressure. When I don’t have it; I know I’m putting my life at risk. (Female, 60 years; HTN [ 57 ]). Stress reduction techniques to manage HTN. Some patients attributed HTN to emotional stress and adopted avoidance or withdrew strategies including avoiding conflicts, noisy environments, and stressful discussions to manage HTN [ 48 , 57 , 59 , 60 ]. “I should not think a lot, …neither should someone speak to me in a loud frightening way” (Female, 49 years; HTN [ 57 ]). Managing multiple medications (Polypharmacy). Patients adopted practical strategies to manage complex treatment regimens, including synchronizing medication schedules, storing medications together in “drug banks,” and involving family members in supporting treatment adherence [ 50 , 56 , 59 ]. However, concerns about pill burden and possible drug toxicity due to the many medicines led some to substitute them with physical exercises, herbal remedies, or to discontinue NCD medications [ 48 , 59 ]: “I refused taking medication for the virus and for pressure … that is too many drugs in your body” (Female, 42 years; HTN [ 48 ]). Theme three: Patients adopting health-promoting behaviors Patients engaged in a range of various non-pharmacological practices to manage HTN and DM, often based on health provider advice or self-initiation. Dietary modifications and challenges. Many patients attempted to change their diet by reducing salt, sugar, and fatty foods, eating smaller portions more frequently, drinking a lot of water to “flush out” medicines, and eating food before taking medicines to prevent side-effects [ 48 , 51 , 53 , 57 , 60 ]. However, food insecurity, alongside poverty, limited food options and social norms on food consumption undermined planned dietary changes and medicine adherence, particularly when medicines had to be taken with food [ 49 – 52 ]: “This is why I have to skip taking my insulin because my body gets weak, I shake and feel like going mad. … because I don’t have food” (woman, 46-years, DM [ 52 ]). Lifestyle adjustments and condition-specific practices. In addition to dieting, patients engaged in physical activity, reduced alcohol intake, and avoiding smoking [ 49 , 51 , 53 , 60 ]. Some patients also took immune boosters to enhance their immunity. We also observed condition-specific practices in this population. For HIV, patients emphasized safe sex and wound care [ 53 ]. For HTN and DM, self-monitoring of blood pressure and glucose was highlighted, though glucose monitoring was often limited by cost [ 50 , 59 ]. For DM only, additional practice was foot care [ 53 ]: “… for type two diabetes, … proper foot care, included washing and drying feet properly; and wearing the right shoes “to be safe ” [ 53 ]. Theme four: Coping with psychosocial distress Patients used psychological and social strategies to manage the emotional burden of multimorbidity. Acceptance as a coping mechanism. Acceptance of illness, particularly HIV, emerged as a key psychological adaptation that enabled patients to rebuild self-worth and engage in self-care [ 50 , 53 , 54 ]. Patients described reaching acceptance through counseling and gradual emotional adjustment [ 51 , 53 , 54 ]: “I could not accept living with HIV, but as time went by, I accepted … and that’s what I am now” (P1, Female, HTN [ 53 ]). Managing stigma. HIV-related stigma drove many patients to conceal their status and ART use, mostly through maintaining secrecy of status, hiding HIV medicines and social withdrawal [ 51 , 53 , 59 ]. In severe cases, some patients would physically withdraw from stigmatizing environments: “My brothers stigmatize me a lot. …, they insult me the whole night... So sometimes I don’t even stay at my own home.” (Female, 44 years [ 59 ]). Maladaptive coping. Some patients resorted to maladaptive coping strategies [ 51 , 60 ], particularly alcohol use to cope with depression associated with multimorbidity [ 51 ]. While drinking alcohol offered temporary relief from the feelings of hopelessness, it risked worsening their health outcomes [ 51 , 60 ]: “I do drink alcohol, … Yes, I sometimes have that problem [depression] I drink alcohol day before going to clinic” (Female, 48 years old; HTN [ 51 ]). Theme five: Strategies to navigate healthcare insecurities Patients described navigating systemic inequities between HIV and NCD services [ 50 , 52 – 56 , 58 , 59 ]. While HIV services were well-structured and free, HTN and DM care were poorly integrated and under-resourced. Navigating limited medicine availability. The most recurrent challenge was the inconsistent availability of essential medicines for HTN and DM. Many patients were told to purchase medications out-of-pocket, which was financially unsustainable [ 50 , 52 , 53 , 55 , 56 , 58 – 60 ]. Consequently, some patients resorted to visiting multiple clinics to find medicines, borrowing from peers, substituting prescribed drugs with painkillers and in more constrained situations, patients discontinued treatment altogether until they could afford refills [ 50 , 52 , 56 , 60 ]: “They give you one pack and tell you to buy the rest. You may not have money for buying drugs at that time.” (Woman, 42 years, DM [ 55 ]). These shortages were reported across both fragmented and integrated HIV/NCD service models, underscoring persistent inequities between HIV and NCD care systems [ 49 , 50 , 52 , 55 , 56 , 58 ]. Seeking multiple sources of care and information To address the limited access to comprehensive care for HTN and DM, patients often sought services across multiple health facilities for consultations, expertise treatment and information [ 52 , 59 , 60 ]. This strategy was particularly common among patients managing DM [ 52 , 58 ]: “The problem is that one doctor will tell you to do this, and another asks you to do a different thing” (woman, 46-years HTN, DM [ 58 ]). Financial coping strategies to sustain self-care Lastly, to meet self-care costs, especially for NCD medications, transport, and recommended diets, patients employed a variety of income-generating or resource-mobilizing strategies. These included working extra hours, renting out their rooms, selling farm produce or household assets, and borrowing money to sustain care [ 50 , 52 , 55 ]: I always try to ensure that I collect wood to sell so I can go to the clinic. Or if I cannot find anyone to sell to, I borrow money, even if I do not know how I am going to return it ” (Female, 46 years; HTN [ 50 ]). In some instances, some sought financial assistance from family, community, and church members to afford the cost of medication and consultations [ 60 ]. Cognitive participation highlighted patients’ efforts to engage in care through mobilizing family support, maintaining social relationships despite stigma, and navigating inconsistent provider communication. Collective action captured practical challenges in care, including polypharmacy and complex routines, side effects, lifestyle and dietary changes, and reliance on alternative therapies, all happening within under-resourced NCD-care. Reflexive monitoring illustrated how patients evaluated and adapted care practices in response to constraints, such as changing medication schedules and compromising diet. Discussion This review examined self-care practices and related treatment burdens among PLHIV with HTN or DM comorbidities in LMICs. Patients used both pharmacological practices (such as sense-making of illnesses and divergent treatment strategies) and non-pharmacological strategies (including lifestyle changes, psychosocial coping and navigating health systems) to manage HIV and NCDs concurrently. These findings add to the growing evidence that self-care for chronic conditions extends beyond medical management to include locally adapted practices for maintaining well-being [ 61 – 63 ]. Importantly, findings demonstrate that self-care practices for PLHIV and HTN and/or DM comorbidity are shaped by illness perceptions, structural constraints and the cumulative demands of managing multimorbidity, reflecting the influence of personal and environmental factors on self-care in the resource-limited settings [ 62 ]. Patients engaged consistently perceived HIV as more manageable than HTN or DM. Highlighting the success of long-standing HIV investments such as free ART, differentiated service delivery, peer support, and structured counselling that have improved trust, adherence, and retention in care [ 64 , 65 ]. In contrast, HTN and DM were viewed as life-threatening and therefore required greater attention, increasing the care burden associated with these NCDs, consistent with other studies in low-resource settings [ 66 , 67 ]. These perceptions likely reflect persistent gaps in NCD infrastructure, medicine availability, and patient support in many LMICs [ 68 , 69 ]. These findings therefore support calls to adapt successful HIV care strategies, such as decentralization, task-shifting and community engagement, within integrated HIV/NCD care models [ 70 , 71 ]. Adherence to medicines differed across conditions. While ART was consistently taken due to reliable access and strong confidence in its effectiveness, adherence to HTN and DM medications was often selective. Similar patterns of low adherence to NCD medication have been reported in resource-limited settings and are linked to asymptomatic disease, medicine side effects, inconsistent clinical advice, and financial barriers [ 60 , 63 ]. The use of herbal remedies, spiritual practices, or stress-relief strategies appeared to be adaptive responses to gaps in formal HTN and DM care and these remain common in African settings [ 72 , 73 ]. Leveraging existing HIV care platforms, integrated HIV/NCD services can strengthen health education, particularly medication counselling on disease progression, treatment benefits, side effects, and drug interactions, to improve treatment engagement, as demonstrated in successful HIV care across LMICs [ 74 , 75 ]. Lifestyle-related self-care practices, particularly dietary adjustments and physical activity, were commonly attempted but difficult to sustain due to poverty, food insecurity and limited social support, consistent with prior evidence in low-resource settings [ 76 , 77 ]. These findings suggest that lifestyle counselling, including dietary guidance, should be context-sensitive and linked to feasible local resources. Generic lifestyle advice without addressing structural constraints is unlikely to be effective [ 78 ]. Psychosocial coping emerged as an important but under-addressed component of self-care. While acceptance of illness and counseling supported emotional well-being, maladaptive strategies like secrecy about HIV status, social withdrawal, and alcohol use undermined adherence and health outcomes. HIV-related stigma is well-established as a barrier to treatment engagement in sub-Saharan Africa, driving ART concealment, missed appointments, and disengagement from care [ 79 , 80 ]. Moreover, noted alcohol use also poses significant physiological and pharmacological risks as it accelerates HIV, HTN and DM progression, reduces ART effectiveness and increases susceptibility to other conditions like liver disease [ 81 ]. These findings align with growing calls to embed mental health screening, peer-support networks, and structured stigma-reduction programs within integrated HIV/NCD care platforms [ 61 , 82 ], recognizing that psychosocial well-being is not ancillary to self-care but constitutive of it. The review highlights substantial care burdens driven by disparities between HIV and HTN or DM services. Patients often attended multiple clinics, borrowed medicines, and generated additional income to sustain care, coping practices reported among people with NCDs in resource-limited settings [ 83 , 84 ]. These burdens were far less evident in HIV care, underscoring persistent inequities within the integrated service delivery [ 19 , 21 ]. Mapping findings onto the NPT further showed how self-care tasks accumulate into daily workload, consistent with evidence that multimorbidity increases care burden in constrained health systems [ 28 , 85 ]. Strengthening equitable integrated HIV/NCD care through expanding access to HTN and DM medicines, health information and patient support within the same service platforms could reduce patient workload. Strengths and limitations This review provides a theory-informed synthesis of patient experiences across low-resource settings, offering transferable insights for integrated HIV/NCD care. However, included studies were geographically concentrated in Africa, which may limit transferability to other LMICs. This is because the health systems, HIV care infrastructure, NCD service availability and sociocultural contexts differ across LMIC regions and may influence the transferability of findings to Asia, Latin America, or other settings. Second, no eligible quantitative studies were identified. While qualitative studies provided rich insights into patients’ experiences, perceptions, and coping strategies, they did not allow estimation of the prevalence or magnitude of self-care practices or treatment burden. Findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting patient experiences and perceptions rather than condition-specific or population-level estimates of self-care practices. Future quantitative research should complement these findings by quantifying self-care practices and measuring the magnitude and distribution of treatment burden among PLHIV with NCD comorbidities in LMIC settings. Publication bias may exist if studies reporting negative self-care experiences or failures in HIV/NCD care remain unpublished. Also, subgroup comparisons by age, country, or other demographic characteristics were not feasible due to inconsistent reporting across studies. Finally, treatment burdens were derived from self-reported qualitative data and may not fully capture their intensity or fluctuation over time. Recommendations for practice and research Strengthening self-care among PLHIV care requires addressing key gaps in the NCD service delivery that drive patient workload. Firstly, ensuring a reliable and uninterrupted supply of essential HTN and DM medicines within HIV platforms will be critical for continuity of care and reducing treatment burden. In addition, structured medication counselling should be provided to address safe medicine use, potential drug interactions, side effects, and the risks associated with unregulated herbal remedies. Health education should explicitly address the perceived inefficacy on NCD medications and misconceptions about cure. Lifestyle guidance should be tailored to patients’ socioeconomic realities, recognizing that poverty and food insecurity often limit the feasibility of dietary advice and lifestyle recommendations. Where possible, dietary guidance should be linked to locally available resources, including community or food support programs. Furthermore, integrating psychosocial support and stigma-reduction interventions into HIV/NCD services is essential, as emotional distress, stigma, and maladaptive coping can undermine sustained engagement in care. Future research should build on these findings by quantifying self-care workload and estimating the magnitude and distribution of treatment burden among PLHIV and NCD comorbidities in LMICs. Implementation studies are also needed to evaluate whether health system strategies, such as task-shifting, decentralized service delivery, and differentiated care models, can effectively reduce patient workload and improve long-term self-care outcomes.. Conclusion Self-care among PLHIV and comorbid HTN or DM in LMICs is shaped by illness perceptions, structural inequities, and the cumulative demands of multimorbidity. Self-care practices included making sense of illnesses and medications, prioritizing treatment across conditions, adopting lifestyle changes, coping with psychosocial distress, and navigating healthcare insecurities. These practices generated substantial treatment burdens arising from prescribed healthcare tasks, limited support for HTN and DM care, and patient efforts to bridge service gaps. Effective self-care therefore requires more than patient-level interventions. Strengthening integrated care through reliable access to NCD medicines, structured medication counselling, psychosocial support, and context-sensitive lifestyle guidance is essential to reduce patient workload and enable sustainable self-management. Future research should quantify self-care workload and examine how treatment burdens accumulate and interact to influence adherence, coping and health outcomes to inform more effective integrated care models.
