1
Introduction

Let
\Omega
be a bounded, open and convex set in
\R^{d}
, we consider the Neumann-Laplacian eigenvalue problem

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\mu u&\text{ in }\Omega\\[2.15277pt]
\displaystyle{\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}=0}&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\end{cases},

(1.1)

where
\nu
is the outer normal to
\Omega
.
The embedding of
H^{1}(\Omega)
into
L^{2}(\Omega)
is compact, hence the spectrum is discrete and consists only on eigenvalues (ordered with multiplicity)

0=\mu_{0}(\Omega)<\mu_{1}(\Omega)\leq\mu_{2}(\Omega)\leq\ldots\leq\to+\infty,

Each eigenvalue can be characterized by

\mu_{k}(\Omega)=\min_{E_{k}}\max_{0\neq u\in E_{k}}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\left|\nabla u\right|}^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,dx},

(1.2)

where the minimum is taken over all the
k
-dimensional subspaces of
H^{1}(\Omega)
,
L^{2}
-orthogonal to the constant functions.

The spectrum of the Laplacian is widely studied in the literature in relationship with the geometry of the domain.
A natural question related to Polya’s conjecture is the following maximization problem

\sup\{\mu_{k}(\Omega){\left|\Omega\right|}^{\frac{2}{d}}\colon\Omega\subset\R^{d},\,\Omega\text{ open, bounded and Lipschitz}\},

(1.3)

where
\lvert\cdot\rvert
denotes the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure. The functional above is scale invariant since

\mu_{k}(t\Omega)=t^{-2}\mu_{k}(\Omega)
, for all
t>0
.

This problem was solved by Szegö
[
23
]
for planar, simply connected domains, while Weinberger
[
24
]
generalized the result in
\R^{d}
removing any topological restrictions :

\mu_{1}(\Omega){\left|\Omega\right|}^{\frac{2}{d}}\leq\mu_{1}(B){\left|B\right|}^{\frac{2}{d}},

(1.4)

where
B
is a ball in
\R^{d}
.

In
[
15
]
the authors find the sharp upper bound for
\mu_{2}
for simply connected planar domains, and in
[
8
]
in any dimension without topological restrictions, obtaining

\mu_{2}(\Omega){\left|\Omega\right|}^{\frac{2}{d}}\leq 2^{\frac{2}{d}}\mu_{1}(B){\left|B\right|}^{\frac{2}{d}}.

(1.5)

For higher eigenvalues, even the existence of maximizer of the quantity
\mu_{k}(\Omega){\left|\Omega\right|}^{\frac{2}{d}}
is an open problem. See
[
9
]
for an analysis of
\mu_{k}(\Omega)
for
k\geq 3

and
[
5
]
for a the optimization of
\mu_{k}(\Omega)
among planar convex domains with perimeter or diameter constraints.

In this context of optimization of Neumann eigenvalues, another relevant question regards the link between eigenvalues and diameter. Kröger
[
19
]
studied the maximization of the
k
-th Neumann eigenvalue in the class of convex sets of
\Omega\subset\R^{d}
,
d\geq 2
with prescribed diameter
D_{\Omega}
, obtaining sharp upper bounds
\mu_{k,d}^{*}
such that

D_{\Omega}^{2}\mu_{k}(\Omega)<\mu_{k,d}^{*}\qquad\forall\,\Omega\subseteq\R^{d},\,\Omega\text{ open, bounded and convex}.

(1.6)

A variational analysis of these bounds together with a fine description of a maximizing sequence were given in
[
16
]
.

We point out that in (
1.6
) the equality sign
is not attained for
d\geq 2
. Instead, it is asymptotically reached on a sequence of collapsing domains featuring a specific geometry, sequence which converges to a segment. From this perspective, there is a strong link between this question and the analysis of eigenvalues of a certain class of Strum-Liouville problems.
Incidentally, let us mention that the case
k=1
has been previously considered in
[
11
]
,
[
4
]

and was also studied in a nonlinear framework in
[
7
]
.

Once in front of a sharp inequality, as (
1.6
), one may wonder what can be said for the domains whose eigenvalue is "close" to the upper bound, namely to obtain a quantitative version of the previous inequality with a term which measures the "distance" between the set and the segment.

The purpose of this paper is to give such sharp quantitative inequalities, naturally involving the flatness of the domain.
Several examples of quantitative estimates can be found in the literature. See for instance
[
13
,
6
]
and the survey paper
[
18
, Chapter 7]
. Typically, in all these problems the target set is a ball and the "distance" to the ball is expressed by the Fraenkel asymmetry.
Let us underline that the main difference between the aforementioned estimates and ours is that in our case the inequality is achieved asymptotically for a sequence of thinning domains so that an optimal geometry does not exist. Results of this kind are not so common in literature (see for instance
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
) and the existing ones involve only the first and, sometimes, the second eigenvalue. In
[
2
]
, it is proved the existence of a dimensional constant
C(d)>0
such that for any
\Omega\subset\R^{d}
open, bounded and convex set

\mu_{1}(\Omega)\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{D_{\Omega}^{2}}+C(d)\frac{a_{2}(\Omega)^{2}}{D_{\Omega}^{4}}.

(1.7)

where
D_{\Omega}
,
a_{2}(\Omega)
are respectively the diameter and the second largest semiaxis of the John ellipsoid
of
\Omega
(see Section
2
for the definition of John ellipsoid).

In this paper we establish the following quantitative estimates of (
1.6
) :

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k,d\in\N
,
d\geq 2
. There exist a constant
C(k,d)>0
depending only on
k
and
d
such that for any
\Omega\subset\R^{d}
open, bounded and convex set, it holds

\mu_{k}(\Omega)\leq\frac{\mu_{k,d}^{*}}{D_{\Omega}^{2}}-C(k,d)\frac{a_{2}(\Omega)^{2}}{D_{\Omega}^{4}},

(1.8)

where
\mu_{k,d}^{*}
is the sharp upper bound for
\mu_{k}(\Omega)D_{\Omega}^{2}
given by
[
19
,
16
]
.

Moreover we establish that the exponent
2
on the flatness term can not be improved, in general.

Theorem 1.2
.

Exponent
2
in (
1.8
) over the second John dimension is sharp for
d=2
and
k=1
.

Since the proof of (
1.8
) is done by contradiction, it does not provide an explicit value of the constant
C(k,d)
.
Let us remark that it is the case in most quantitative isoperimetric inequalities, see e.g.
[
13
,
6
]
and the survey paper
[
18
, Chapter 7]
. Therefore, we propose in Section
4
to give an explicit value
of the constant
C(1,2)
(at least for domains with one axis of symmetry), we prove

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Omega
be a convex planar domain and assume that
\Omega
is symmetric with respect to the mediatrix of its diameter, then

D_{\Omega}^{2}\mu_{1}(\Omega)\leq 4j_{0,1}^{2}-0.432\frac{w_{\Omega}^{2}}{D_{\Omega}^{2}}

(1.9)

where
w_{\Omega}
is the width of the convex domain in the direction orthogonal to the diameter.

The value
0.432
is not sharp but, in order to make a comparison, it is worth to notice that the estimate of the constant
C(2)
in (
1.7
) is of order
10^{-16}
(see
[
2
]
) while its numerical estimate ranges between
5
and
6
. The sharp value of a constant similar to
C(2)
is only known in the purely geometric case corresponding to the
W^{1,1}
space, and equals
\frac{4}{3}
(see
[
10
]
).

The paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
we recall some notions and properties that will be used for the proof of Theorem
1.1
while Section
3
is devoted to the proof of the Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. Lastly in Section
4
we compute an explicit value of the constant
C(1,2)
by solving an interesting problem in the calculus of variations.

2
Notations and preliminaries

In this section we recall some useful results.

Convex geometry.

We start by recalling the Brunn-Minkowski inequality (see
[
14
]
,
[
22
]
).

Theorem 2.1

(Brunn-Minkowski inequality)
.

Let
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}\subset\R^{d}
two compact convex sets with non empty interior, then for each
0<\lambda<1
it holds

\mathcal{L}^{d}\bigl(\lambda\Omega_{1}+(1-\lambda)\Omega_{2}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{d}}\geq\lambda\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Omega_{1})^{\frac{1}{d}}+(1-\lambda)\mathcal{L}^{d}(\Omega_{2})^{\frac{1}{d}},

(2.1)

where
\cdot+\cdot
denotes the Minkowski sum among two sets and
\mathcal{L}^{d}
the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure.
Moreover equality in (
2.1
) holds if and only if
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
are homothetic.

Let
\Omega
be a open, bounded and convex set in
\R^{d}
, and
j<d
. We define for each
x\in\R^{j}
the section

S_{x}=\{x^{\prime}\in\R^{d-j}\,\colon\,(x,x^{\prime})\in\Omega\}.

We denote with
\omega\subseteq\R^{j}
the projection of
\Omega
onto the first
j
coordinates and define

p:\omega\to\R,\;\;p(x)=\mathcal{H}^{d-j}\bigl(S_{x}\cap\Omega\bigr).

In case
j=1
, the function
p^{\frac{1}{d-1}}
is usually referred as the profile function of
\Omega
.

As consequence of the Brunn-Minkowski inequality, we have the following

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\R^{d}
be a convex set and
p:\omega\to\R
defined as above. Then
p
is
\frac{1}{d-j}
-concave, i.e.
p^{\frac{1}{d-j}}
is concave.

The Hausdorff distance will be used, restricted to the class of convex sets.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\Omega_{1},\,\Omega_{2}\subset\R^{d}
two convex sets. The Hausdorff distance between
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
is defined as

d_{\mathcal{H}}(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2})=\inf\{\varepsilon>0\,:\,\Omega_{1}\subset\Omega_{2}+\varepsilon B_{1},\,\Omega_{2}\subset\Omega_{1}+\varepsilon B_{1}\},

(2.2)

where
B_{1}
is the ball of radius
1
in
\R^{d}
.

We recall the following compactness property of the Hausdorff distance (known as Blaschke selection theorem), see e.g.
[
17
, Theorem 2.2.25]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\{\Omega_{n}\}
be a sequence of convex sets contained in a fixed compact set
K\subset\R^{d}
. Then there exists a convex set
\Omega
contained in
K
and a subsequence, still denoted with
\{\Omega_{n}\}
, which converges in the sense of Hausdorff to
\Omega
.

The Sturm-Liouville problem
.
Let
m\in\N
,
\omega\subseteq\R^{j}
be an open, bounded and convex set,
p\colon\omega\to\R
, be a non negative
\frac{1}{m}
-concave function.
Then, the embedding of
W^{1,2}(\omega,p)
into
L^{2}(\omega,p)
is compact (see
[
2
, Lemma 7]
). Consequently, the following problem

\begin{cases}\displaystyle{-\mathrm{div}\bigl(p(x)\nabla u(x)\bigr)=\mu_{k}(\omega,p)p(x)u(x)}&\text{ in }\omega\\[6.02773pt]
\displaystyle{p(x){\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}}=0}&\text{ on }\partial\omega.\end{cases}

(2.3)

is well posed in a weak (variational) sense and has a sequence of eigenvalues, ordered with multiplicity

0=\mu_{0}(\omega,p)<\mu_{1}(\omega,p)\leq\dots\mu_{k}(\omega,p)\leq\dots+\infty

defined by

\mu_{k}(\omega,p)=\inf_{E_{k}}\sup_{0\neq u\in E_{k}}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\omega}{\left|\nabla\varphi(x)\right|}^{2}p(x)\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\omega}\varphi(x)^{2}p(x)\,dx}.

(2.4)

The infimum above is taken among all the
k
-dimensional subspace of
H^{1}(\omega)
that are
L^{2}
-orthogonal to constant functions with respect to
p
on
\omega
.

If
j=1
, problem (
2.3
) is a classical Sturm-Liouville problem. In this case we simply denote

\mu_{k}(\omega,p)=\mu_{k}(p).

Collapsing domains
.
Assume that
\{\Omega_{n}\}
is a sequence of bounded, open and convex sets in
\R^{d}
with
D_{\Omega_{n}}=1
. We denote
E_{n}
the John ellipsoid (see
[
12
]
) of
\Omega_{n}
, which satisfies
E_{n}\subseteq\Omega\subseteq dE_{n}
. Up to a rigid movement we can assume that for each
n\in\N

1.

E_{n}
is centered at the origin;

2.

E_{n}
is aligned with the axes;

3.

the semi-axis satisfy

1>a_{1}(\Omega_{n})\geq a_{2}(\Omega_{n})\geq\ldots\geq a_{d}(\Omega_{n})>0.

Up to a subsequence we can assume that

a_{i}(\Omega_{n})\to a_{i}\qquad\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}

such that

a_{1}\geq\ldots\geq a_{j}>0=a_{j+1}=\ldots=a_{d}.

The sequence
\{\Omega_{n}\}
is collapsing if
j<d
.

Thanks to the compactness property of the Hausdorff distance, we can also assume

\Omega_{n}\overset{\mathcal{H}}{\longrightarrow}\omega\times\{0\}^{d-j},

(2.5)

where
\omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{j}
is an open, convex set of diameter
1
.

For each
n
we introduce

T_{n}\colon\R^{d}\to\R^{d},\qquad T_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=\biggl(x_{1},\ldots,x_{j},\frac{x_{j+1}}{a_{j+1}(\Omega_{n})},\ldots,\frac{x_{d}}{a_{d}(\Omega_{n})}\biggr).

(2.6)

Essentially, when applied to
\Omega_{n}
, this function rescales the last

components in such a way that
\tilde{\Omega}_{n}:=T_{n}(\Omega_{n})
has a John ellipsoid with semiaxes

a_{1}(\Omega_{n}),\ldots,a_{j}(\Omega_{n}),1,\ldots,1.

So in particular,
\tilde{\Omega}_{n}
is a non degenerating sequence of convex open sets which can be assumed to converge to a set
\tilde{\Omega}
. Moreover, the projection of
\tilde{\Omega}
on the first
j
variables is
\omega
.
We introduce
p\colon\omega\to\R
,
p(x)=\mathcal{H}^{d-j}(S_{x}\cap\tilde{\Omega})
,
\forall x\in\omega
.
Corollary
2.2
ensures that
p
is a
\frac{1}{d-j}
-convex function.

We recall the following result from
[
2
, Proposition 8]
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Under the previous assumptions

\mu_{k}(\Omega_{n})\to\mu_{k}(\omega,p),

where
\mu_{k}(\omega,p)
is the
k
-th eigenvalue of the problem (
2.3
).
Moreover, if
(u_{k}^{n})
is a sequence of

-normalized eigenfunctions associated to
\mu_{k}(\Omega_{n})
, then defining

\tilde{u}_{k}^{n}\colon\tilde{\Omega}_{n}\to\R,\qquad\tilde{u}_{k}^{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=k_{n}u_{k}^{n}\bigl(x_{1},\ldots,x_{j},a_{j+1}(\Omega_{n})x_{j+1},\ldots,a_{d}(\Omega_{n})x_{d}\bigr),

where
k_{n}=\sqrt{\prod_{h=j+1}^{d}a_{h}(\Omega_{n})}
,
we get, up to extracting a subsequence, that

1_{\tilde{\Omega}_{n}}\tilde{u}_{k}^{n}\to 1_{\tilde{\Omega}}\tilde{u}_{k},\mbox{ strongly in }L^{2}(\R^{d}),

1_{\tilde{\Omega}_{n}}\nabla\tilde{u}_{k}^{n}\to 1_{\tilde{\Omega}}\nabla\tilde{u}_{k},\mbox{ strongly in }L^{2}(\R^{d}),

where
\tilde{u}_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}):=u_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{j})

is a k-th eigenfunction of problem (
2.3
) set on
(\omega,p)
.

Proof.

We briefly recall the main idea of the proof, since it is useful for our future analysis (for the full proof we refer to
[

It holds

\displaystyle{\left\|\tilde{u}_{k}^{n}\right\|}_{L^{2}(\tilde{\Omega}_{n})}^{2}

\displaystyle={\left\|u_{k}^{n}\right\|}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{n})}=1,

and

\forall i=1,\dots,j\;\int_{\tilde{\Omega}_{n}}\biggl({\frac{\partial\tilde{u}_{k}^{n}}{\partial x_{i}}}\biggr)\,dx=\int_{\Omega_{n}}\biggl({\frac{\partial u_{k}^{n}}{\partial x_{i}}}\biggr)\,dx

\forall i=j+1,\dots,d\;\int_{\tilde{\Omega}_{n}}\biggl({\frac{\partial\tilde{u}_{k}^{n}}{\partial x_{i}}}\biggr)\,dx=\bigl(a_{i}(\Omega_{n})\bigr)^{2}\int_{\Omega_{n}}\biggl({\frac{\partial u_{k}^{n}}{\partial x_{i}}}\biggr)\,dx.

Since

are uniformly convex and
\tilde{u}_{n}^{k}\in H^{1}\bigl(\tilde{\Omega}_{n}\bigr)

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{k}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{\tilde{\Omega}_{n}}

\displaystyle\to\tilde{u}_{k}\mathbbm{1}_{\tilde{\Omega}}

\displaystyle\bigl(\nabla\tilde{u}_{k}^{n}\bigr)\mathbbm{1}_{\tilde{\Omega}_{n}}

\displaystyle\to\nabla\tilde{u}_{k}\mathbbm{1}_{\tilde{\Omega}}

\displaystyle\text{ weakly in }L^{2}.

We can conclude

is constant in variables

The convergence of the gradients is strong in

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(\Omega)\leq\dfrac{4j_{0,1}^{2}\left[I_{1,1}(h)w+\frac{2\tau}{3}I_{3,1}(h)w^{3}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{5}I_{5,1}(h)w^{5}\right]+\frac{4\tau^{2}}{3}I_{3,0}(h)w^{3}}{I_{1,0}(h)w+\frac{2\tau}{3}I_{3,0}(h)w^{3}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{5}I_{5,0}(h)w^{5}}

\displaystyle=4j_{0,1}^{2}\dfrac{I_{1,1}(h)+\frac{2\tau}{3}I_{3,1}(h)w^{2}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{5}I_{5,1}(h)w^{4}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{3j_{0,1}^{2}}I_{3,0}(h)w^{2}}{I_{1,0}(h)+\frac{2\tau}{3}I_{3,0}(h)w^{2}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{5}I_{5,0}(h)w^{4}}.

We want to find an explicit constant
C
such that
\mu_{1}(\Omega)\leq 4j_{0,1}^{2}(1-Cw^{2})
that is equivalent to prove the following inequality

C\leq\frac{1}{w^{2}}\frac{\psi(1,h)+\frac{2\tau}{3}\psi(3,h)w^{2}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{5}\psi(5,h)w^{4}-\frac{\tau^{2}}{3j_{0,1}^{2}}I_{3,0}(h)w^{2}}{I_{1,0}(h)+\frac{2\tau}{3}I_{3,0}(h)w^{2}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{5}I_{5,0}(h)w^{4}}

(4.3)

for all
w\in(0,1]
and all
h
concave (taking values between
0
and
1
).
Let us first look at the denominator
D
that can be written

D(h)=\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}[h(x)+\frac{2\tau}{3}w^{2}h^{3}(x)+\frac{\tau^{2}}{5}w^{4}h^{5}(x)]J_{0}(2j_{0,1}x)^{2}dx

and since
z\mapsto z+\frac{2\tau}{3}w^{2}z^{3}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{5}w^{4}z^{5}
is increasing (its derivative is
(1+\tau z^{2}w^{2})^{2}
) and
h(x)\leq 1

we infer that
D(h)\leq D(1)
with

D(1)=\left(1+\frac{2\tau}{3}w^{2}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{5}w^{4}\right)I_{0,0}.

(4.4)

In the same way the last term in the numerator can be estimated by

\frac{\tau^{2}}{3j_{0,1}^{2}}I_{3,0}(h)\leq\frac{\tau^{2}}{3j_{0,1}^{2}}I_{3,0}(1)=\frac{\tau^{2}}{3j_{0,1}^{2}}I_{0,0}.

(4.5)

Now we concentrate on the three first terms in the numerator. We want to minimize their sum, with respect to the function
h
that leads
us to solve the following problem in the calculus of variations. We denote by
J
the sum:

J(h)=\psi(1,h)+\frac{2\tau}{3}\psi(3,h)w^{2}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{5}\psi(5,h)w^{4}

here,
w
is a fixed number between
0
and
1
and
\tau
has been defined in (
4.2
).

Proposition 4.2
.

Let us consider the following minimization problem

\min\{J(h),h:[0,\frac{1}{2}]\to[0,1],\mbox{ $h$ concave },h(0)\geq 0,h(1)=1\}.

(4.6)

Then the solution of (
4.6
) is the function
h(x)=2x
.

Proof.

We can rewrite
J(h)
as

J(h)=\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}[h(x)+\frac{2\tau}{3}w^{2}h^{3}(x)+\frac{\tau^{2}}{5}w^{4}h^{5}(x)](J_{0}(2j_{0,1}x)^{2}-J_{1}(2j_{0,1}x)^{2})dx.

Let us introduce the function
g(x)=J_{0}(2j_{0,1}x)^{2}-J_{1}(2j_{0,1}x)^{2}
, see its graph below.

Figure 5:
The graph of
x\mapsto J_{0}(2j_{0,1}x)^{2}-J_{1}(2j_{0,1}x)^{2}

The proof we give below works for any
\tau\in(-1,1)
. What makes the result non trivial is the fact that the function
g
changes its sign
at one point
x_{0}
.

The existence of a minimizer is standard, a minimizing sequence of concave function converges uniformly on every compact subset of
the interval
I=(0,1/2]

(because its derivative will be uniformly bounded on such a compact) and the limit
h^{*}
is concave and satisfies also
h^{*}(0)\geq 0,h^{*}(\frac{1}{2})=1
.
Therefore
h^{*}
is a minimizer.

Let us write the optimality condition, using the classical framework explained for example in

[
20
]
or
[
16
]
. First, the derivative of
J
with respect to
h
is given by

<dJ(h^{*}),v>=\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+\tau{h^{*}(x)}^{2}w^{2})^{2}v(x)g(x)dx.

Therefore, there exists a Lagrange multiplier
\xi\in H^{1}(I)
(corresponding to the concavity constraint) with
\xi\geq 0

and
\xi=0
on the support
S
of
h^{\prime\prime}
, and there exists two numbers
\alpha_{0}

and
\alpha_{1}
such that

\xi^{\prime\prime}=(1+\tau{h^{*}(x)}^{2}w^{2})^{2}g(x)+\alpha_{0}\delta_{0}+\alpha_{1}\delta_{1/2}.

(4.7)

We denote by
f(x)=(1+\tau{h^{*}(x)}^{2}w^{2})^{2}g(x)
and therefore, we have
\xi^{\prime\prime}=f(x)
on
I
.
The previous relation is valid on the whole interval
I
and shows, in particular, that
\xi
is a
C^{2}
function in the open interval.
The support
S
being closed, its complement
S^{c}
is a union of open intervals. Let us denote by
(a,b)
with
0<a<b<1/2
a possible
such interval in
S^{c}
with the assumption that
a
and
b
belongs to
S
. In particular
\xi(a)=\xi(b)=0
.
Moreover, since
\xi
is
C^{2}
and must remain non-negative outside
[a,b]
, this implies that
\xi^{\prime}(a)=\xi^{\prime}(b)=0
.
But it is easy to check that the four relations
\xi(a)=\xi(b)=\xi^{\prime}(a)=\xi^{\prime}(b)=0
would imply that the function
f

vanishes (at least) twice on the interval
(a,b)
. Now since
|\tau|<1,0\leq h^{*}\leq 1
and
0\leq w\leq 1
, we see that
(1+\tau{h^{*}(x)}^{2}w^{2})

does not vanish and since
g
vanishes only once, this situation is not possible:

S^{c}
does not contain any interior interval. On the other hand
S
does not contain neither any interval (where
\xi
would be identically 0).
Therefore, the only possibilities are:

(a)

S=\{0,a,1/2\}
for some point
a
in the interior of the interval, and
h^{*}
is affine on each interval
(0,a)
and
(a,1/2)
.

(b)

S=\{0,1/2\}
and
h^{*}
is affine on
I
.

We claim that we can rule out the situation where
h^{*}(0)=h_{0}>0
in both cases. Indeed, in that case, considering a deformation
v(x)

defined by (we recall that
x_{0}
is the zero of the function
g
)

v(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}x-x_{0}&\mbox{ if }x\leq x_{0}\\
0&\mbox{ if }x\geq x_{0}\\
\end{array}\right.

we see that
h+tv
is still concave for
t
small and the derivative
<dJ(h^{*}),v>
is clearly negative, yielding a non optimal
h^{*}
.

It remains to rule out the case of a function
h^{*}
piecewise affine with a corner at some point
a\in(0,1/2)
.
First of all, since
\xi
must remain non-negative and
\xi(a)=\xi^{\prime}(a)=0
for the same reasons than before, we must have

\xi^{\prime\prime}(a)\geq 0
. In view of the expression given in (
4.7
), this implies that
a\leq x_{0}
. The case
a=x_{0}

is not possible either since then we would have
\xi^{\prime\prime}(a)=0
and a negative third derivative at
x_{0}
contradicting
the sign condition for
\xi
. Therefore
a<x_{0}
.
Now, let us consider the following perturbation

v(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-x(x_{0}-a)&\mbox{ if }x\leq a\\
a(x-x_{0})&\mbox{ if }a\leq x\leq x_{0}\\
0&\mbox{ if }x\geq x_{0}\\
\end{array}\right.

this function
v
is continuous and always negative, the perturbation
h^{*}+tv
remains concave for
t
small (because the negative Dirac mass
of
h^{*}
at point
a
absorbs the positive Dirac mass of
v
), therefore one more time, we have

<dJ(h^{*}),v>
negative, yielding a non optimal
h^{*}
. As a conclusion, the only remaining possibility is
h^{*}(x)=2x
.
∎

As a consequence of the previous proposition, we have the following inequality

\psi(1,h)+\frac{2\tau}{3}\psi(3,h)w^{2}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{5}\psi(5,h)w^{4}\geq\psi(1,2x)+\frac{2\tau}{3}\psi(3,2x)w^{2}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{5}\psi(5,2x)w^{4}.

(4.8)

Now, it turns out that a classical relation of Bessel function is

\int_{0}^{j_{0,1}}t(J_{0}(t)^{2}-J_{1}(t)^{2})dt=0

that implies that
\psi(1,2x)=0
. Therefore, taking into account (
4.8
), (
4.5
), (
4.4
), the quotient
in (
4.3
) can be estimated from below by:

Q(w):=\frac{\frac{2\tau}{3}\psi(3,2x)+\frac{\tau^{2}}{5}\psi(5,2x)w^{2}-\frac{\tau^{2}}{3j_{0,1}^{2}}I_{0,0}}{\left(1+\frac{2\tau}{3}w^{2}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{5}w^{4}\right)I_{0,0}}

(4.9)

Now, an elementary study of the function
w\mapsto Q(w)
shows that it is increasing on the interval
[0,1]
, thus its minimal value is
obtained for
w=0
and

Q(0)=\left(\frac{2\tau}{3}\psi(3,2x)-\frac{\tau^{2}}{3j_{0,1}^{2}}I_{0,0}\right)/I_{0,0}.

The choice of
\tau
that we did at the beginning yields the better (maximal) value for
Q(0)

that is

M:=\frac{1}{3}j_{0,1}^{2}\frac{\psi(3,2x)^{2}}{I_{0,0}^{2}}\simeq 0.0186.

Multiplying this value by
4j_{0,1}^{2}
gives the value of the constant
0.432
stated in the theorem.
∎
