Unlocking multi-institutional insights into disease progression with PEAL as a lossless, one-shot federated learning solution
Authors: Yicheng Shen, Ji Soo Kim, Chongliang Luo, Scott L. Zeger, Robyn T. Domsic, Ami A. Shah, Jiayi Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multisite analysis of electronic health record (EHR) data presents unique opportunities for studying disease progression in real-world settings. However, privacy concerns, communication costs, and site-level heterogeneity pose significant challenges for analyzing longitudinal data. We introduce PEAL (Privacy-preserving Efficient Aggregation for Longitudinal data), a novel federated learning algorithm for fitting multi-level linear mixed-effects models with spline basis terms for nonlinear temporal trends. PEAL requires only a single-round transfer of summary statistics and produces results identical to using pooled individual participant data. Simulation studies demonstrate that PEAL accurately recovers fixed effects and variance components under realistic multi-level structures. We applied PEAL to real-world longitudinal datasets of systemic sclerosis patients from the Johns Hopkins and University of Pittsburgh Scleroderma Centers. This application shows our algorithm captures reasonable disease trajectories. Overall, PEAL provides a practical solution for distributed research networks studying rare diseases and time-evolving clinical outcomes by enabling lossless, communication-efficient, and privacy-preserving modeling.

Introduction

The increasing availability of large-scale observational clinical data, particularly electronic health records (EHRs) and administrative databases, has spurred growing interest in conducting multisite analyses within distributed research networks (DRNs) to advance biomedical and health services research 1–3. These expansive datasets offer key advantages in terms of larger sample size, higher statistical power, greater generalizability across diverse patient populations, and the ability to uncover clinically meaningful patterns that may not be evident in smaller, single-center studies 4,5. Leveraging these resources has facilitated investigations into rare diseases, treatment effect heterogeneity, and prediction of clinical outcomes and disease trajectories 6,7.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, an especially valuable feature of EHR data is their longitudinal nature, capturing repeated measurements for individual patients from multiple sites over time 8. This temporal dimension provides opportunities to examine disease progression, treatment effectiveness, and healthcare utilization patterns in ways that cross-sectional data cannot. In particular, repeated measures are used to address time-dependent confounding when studying relationships among patient characteristics, treatment exposures, and clinical outcomes, thereby supporting more personalized and timely diagnosis and interventions 9.Fig. 1Multisite hierarchical structure of EHR data.This diagram illustrates the nested data organization within the research network. Realizing the full potential of such longitudinal EHR data often requires collaboration across institutions. This has made DRNs key to modern clinical research, enabling large-scale, multisite analyses that draw on diverse patient populations and healthcare systems 3,10. In the meantime, collaborative analyses also introduce new challenges, including heterogeneity in populations, data structures, and coding practices, as well as strict restrictions on data sharing 11–13. Current regulatory frameworks, such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) in the US and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the EU, establish the essential privacy and security standards 14,15, underscoring the importance of developing privacy-preserving and communication-efficient decentralized approaches for analyses conducted within DRNs 16.

Federated learning (FL) provides a natural solution for analyzing multisite EHR data by keeping patient-level information localized at each site and only sharing model parameters or summary statistics, allowing collaborative model training and minimizing the privacy risks associated with traditional data-sharing methods 17,18. In a typical architecture where there exists one analysis center and multiple collaborating hospitals, each site independently generates summary-level statistics using its own patient-level data. Such architecture has been increasingly adopted in clinical and healthcare settings to safeguard patient privacy. For instance, Pan et al.19 demonstrated that federated learning using multisite patient trajectory data improves the prediction of Alzheimer’s disease progression from mild cognitive impairment compared to local models. In another application, Guo et al.20 developed a real-time medical data processing method that uses federated learning for continuous disease diagnosis model updates.

While FL methods overcome patient-level data-sharing constraints, they often involve extensive rounds of information exchange between local sites and the analysis center. From a statistical perspective, Louizos et al.21 reinterpreted the FedAvg algorithm by McMahan et al.22 as an Expectation-Maximization procedure for hierarchical latent variable models. The iterative nature of the statistical model, however, requires repeated rounds of communication between clients and the server to converge in the federated setting. On the deep learning side, Gürler and Rekik23 proposed a federated graph neural network framework for predicting brain graph evolution trajectories, which similarly depends on extensive iterative exchanges to synchronize local and global models. Together, these examples highlight a common limitation: implementing sophisticated models in FL often incurs high computational costs and substantial communication overhead given their complexity.

Reducing human-in-the-loop efforts, site-to-site coordination, and communication overhead to an absolute minimum is an equally important challenge in real-world collaborative clinical settings24. To reduce the burden of frequent, bandwidth-intensive exchanges between sites, several non-iterative and communication-efficient federated learning methods have been developed. For example, the ODAL and ODAC frameworks allow distributed estimation with only one-shot communication25–27. However, these approaches assume homogeneity of the population between sites, which limits their applicability in the complex and heterogeneous clinical networks observed in practice28,29. A key milestone in this line of research to account for between-site heterogeneity is the DLMM method proposed by Luo et al.30. Built on the two-level linear mixed-effects modeling (LMM) framework, DLMM provides a principled solution for modeling the between-site heterogeneity via random effects. While it requires only a single round of site-level summary statistics sharing, DLMM produces estimates identical to those obtained using pooled individual participant data (IPD) across sites. Nevertheless, DLMM is structurally confined to two levels of hierarchy (patients nested within sites) and thus ignores longitudinal dependencies from repeated measures.

While temporal dynamics are central to understanding the disease progression, few FL methods have adequately addressed the complexities of such truly longitudinal EHR data. Fed-GLMM, recently proposed by Yan et al.31, offers an approximate solution for fitting generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) in distributed settings. Although this method accounts for correlated data with repeated measurements, it does not explicitly model temporal structure, such as using splines, random slopes, autoregressive terms, or time-varying covariates. Explicit modeling of time is critical in the presence of non-linear or periodic trends, as it enables identification of non-stationary effects of risk factors on patient trajectories. In addition, its approximation lacks guarantees for lossless recovery of pooled-IPD estimates, which limits its reliability in high-stakes applications where statistical accuracy is important.

In real-world healthcare settings, routinely collected EHR data often exhibit a natural three-level hierarchical structure: repeated clinical observations are nested within individual patients, who are themselves clustered within healthcare sites. This multilevel structure demands statistical methods that can account for both longitudinal dependencies and heterogeneity across patients and institutions. Our motivating example draws on a real-world EHR dataset of patients with systemic sclerosis (scleroderma), a rare, chronic autoimmune disease with no known cure that requires long-term monitoring and care 32. As a heterogeneous disease, scleroderma is characterized by exaggerated fibrosis, vasculopathy, and dysregulation of the immune system. It can affect multiple organ systems, including the skin, lungs, heart, peripheral vasculature, gastrointestinal tract, kidneys, and musculoskeletal system, among others. Given the substantial heterogeneity of the disease, it is difficult to predict an individual patient’s disease course and identify optimal therapeutic strategies. However, there is great potential to improve predictive accuracy by leveraging an individual’s longitudinal data across multiple organ systems, as well as data from other patients, both within and across centers, who share similar clinical characteristics 33.

This study utilizes data from two major prospective longitudinal cohorts: the Johns Hopkins Scleroderma Center Research Registry 34, established in 1990 with over 4000 patients, and the University of Pittsburgh Scleroderma Center Databank 35, a contemporaneous cohort contributing data on over 1000 patients. Together, these registries capture deep phenotypic profiles with repeated measurements across multiple organ systems, such as the skin type, percent predicted forced vital capacity, and right ventricular systolic pressure over time. These data enable capturing both within-patient disease progression and between-group variability. Given the rarity of this and other autoimmune rheumatic diseases, harnessing deeply phenotyped longitudinal data from multiple centers has great potential to accelerate rheumatic disease research and enable more precise study of patients’ risks and optimal therapeutic approaches. To analyze such multisite data while addressing data-sharing barriers, we were motivated to develop a federated learning method to leverage multisite EHR data for robust and generalizable analysis while respecting privacy regulations.

In response, we propose and validate PEAL, a federated learning framework that resolves the challenges outlined above by bridging the gap between exact cross-sectional methods and approximate longitudinal approaches. Specifically, our PEAL algorithm: (1) incorporates an integrated distributed diagnostic step that examines site-level summaries to identify data sparsity and mitigate re-identification risks prior to modeling; (2) minimizes communication overhead through a communication-efficient, one-shot protocol; (3) advances beyond approximate methods by reconstructing the exact profile likelihood for a three-level longitudinal hierarchy with flexible random effects structures; (4) introduces a privacy-preserving approach for non-linear temporal modeling via natural spline knot selection, handling irregularly sampled visits without sharing individual event times; and (5) achieves the lossless property by producing parameter estimates identical to those obtained from a gold-standard pooled IPD analysis. We evaluate the empirical performance of the proposed method through simulation studies and a study of the risk factors associated with progression of systemic sclerosis-associated interstitial lung disease, as measured by pulmonary function trajectory over time.

Results

Overview of the PEAL Framework

The PEAL (Privacy-preserving Efficient Aggregation for Longitudinal data) framework features a distributed algorithm for fitting three-level linear mixed-effects models on multisite longitudinal EHR data while preserving patient privacy and minimizing communication overhead. The architecture of a research network in our context is that one single analysis center coordinates with multiple participating sites, where patient-level information remains local and is used to generate standardized summary statistics. Overview of the PEAL pipeline.

The process consists of two main stages for distributed learning of linear mixed models. Stage 1 focuses on exploring and preprocessing the data at local sites, and Stage 2 carries out federated estimation of parameters.

Specifically, in Stage 1, each clinical site first performs cohort extraction, data quality control, and feature construction. For cohort extraction, all sites apply the same predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria as well as the same set of covariates to extract data from their local databases. Following data extraction, quality checks and cohort diagnostics are conducted using code provided by the analysis center. For feature construction, in order to incorporate time-varying covariates into the model, each site reports site-specific histogram bin counts of time since onset (TSO) of disease-related activities, severity, or medication exposures. The bin counts are used to guide the placement of knots for the natural spline basis functions. This approach accommodates heterogeneity in visit patterns across sites without requiring access to IPD. By relying on each site’s empirical distribution of TSO, the spline basis is informed by the overall temporal trend of the trajectory, ensuring that the resulting model better captures temporal dynamics without being overly influenced by irregular or uneven visit schedules.

In Stage 2, the analysis center (or a designated coordinating site responsible for analysis) fits the multi-level linear mixed-effects model with the proposed federated learning algorithm. Each site now generates a set of summary statistics, where outcomes are demeaned, and continuous covariates are standardized, from its local longitudinal data matrices and transmits them to the analysis center. The center then performs maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) or restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of fixed effects and variance components, including site- and patient-level random effects.

Importantly, PEAL achieves two key properties essential for practical federated learning: lossless and one-shot 36,37. The “lossless" property refers to the mathematical and computational equivalence of the algorithm: despite data-sharing constraints, PEAL produces parameter estimates numerically identical to those from a gold-standard analysis where all IPD are pooled together. It is important to note that realizing this property in practice is conditional upon consistent data harmonization (identical variable definitions and preprocessing steps), correct model specification (matching design matrices for fixed and random effects across sites), and equivalent handling of missing data. The one-shot property, which is a unique derivation for linear mixed-effects models, eliminates the need for iterative rounds of communication between sites and the analysis center, thereby substantially reducing human-in-the-loop efforts from repeated coordination, data preparation, personnel training and turnover, and multiple IRB approvals 38. This makes the PEAL framework far more scalable and practical for large, multi-institutional research networks.

Federated modeling of Scleroderma disease progression

We applied PEAL to a multisite longitudinal dataset derived from two major scleroderma research registries: the Johns Hopkins Scleroderma Center (JHSC) and the University of Pittsburgh Scleroderma Center (UPSC). The JHSC registry contributes 17,169 records from 2633 patients, while the UPSC databank contributes 4613 records from 1079 patients. Both registries include routinely collected continuous clinical measures, for example, the percent predicted forced vital capacity (pFVC), diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (pDLCO), left ventricular ejection fraction (EF), and right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP), and have contributed extensively to our knowledge of scleroderma in prior studies 34,35,39,40. Here, we focus on the pFVC as a key indicator of interstitial lung disease (ILD) progression, which is one of the most common causes of death in patients with systemic sclerosis 41.

Missing data were handled using a complete-case approach for the current univariate pFVC analysis. All covariates included in the models were time-invariant baseline characteristics (age at onset, gender, race, autoantibody subtype, diffuse type). Therefore, missing covariates occurred only when a variable was completely absent for a patient, rather than intermittently across visits. For pFVC, visits with missing values were removed, and PEAL reconstructs the pooled-data likelihood conditional on the observed dataset. Thus, the lossless property in this case corresponds to equivalence with a pooled complete-case fit of the same observed data; it does not by itself address bias under informative missingness.

To rigorously validate the lossless property of PEAL before deploying it in a decentralized setting, we first conducted an internal validation study using the JHSC cohort only. In this controlled scenario, we manually partitioned the cohort into three “synthetic sites” following similar racial and demographic distributions observed in existing literature 35,42 (see details in Supplementary Table 1). Since full IPD were available for JHSC, we were able to fit both PEAL and the gold-standard pooled LMM43. By leveraging these two decentralized cohorts, we demonstrate PEAL’s ability to handle demographic heterogeneity typical of multi-institutional collaborations. The final combined cohort includes 3712 patients with complete longitudinal records for forced vital capacity, spanning over 30 years post-scleroderma onset.

As shown in Fig. 3c, some extent of between-site heterogeneity is observed. The age-at-onset distributions (left panel) show that both sites exhibit a peak onset between 40 and 60 years, but the UPSC cohort has a higher density of male patients across all age groups compared to JHSC. Racial composition (middle panel) confirms that JHSC contributes a larger share of non-White patients. Autoantibody prevalence (right panel) also varies, with JHSC showing a higher relative frequency of ACA compared to UPSC, where Scl-70 and RNAPol are slightly more prominent.

To implement PEAL, we first used local bin counts of TSO to construct site-shared natural spline basis functions (Supplementary Fig. 1). After computing and transmitting the site-level summary statistics (Table 2), we selected covariates based on a well-established body of literature suggesting that age at onset, race, gender, diffuse cutaneous subtype, and major autoantibody groups (ACA, Scl-70, RNA polymerase III) are among the strongest predictors of ILD progression and longitudinal lung function decline in scleroderma39,40. These covariates also define clinically meaningful patient subgroups routinely used in risk stratification. We modeled time since disease onset using natural cubic splines because pulmonary function trajectories in systemic sclerosis frequently exhibit nonlinear patterns, particularly during the early and mid disease phases. Including a patient-level random slope for time is of direct clinical relevance: clinicians can interpret patient-specific rates of decline as indicators of rapidly progressive disease. The final model’s robustness was evaluated in a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table 2). This combination of fixed effects, nonlinear temporal components, and patient-level random slopes reflects both clinical interests and previously validated prognostic structures in scleroderma. Figure 3d presents the estimated fixed effects of the regression coefficients and 95% confidence intervals. ACA positivity and White race are associated with a notably higher pFVC 34,44, while Scl-70 and diffuse skin subtype are linked with more deterioration 45,46. Estimates of variance components quantify the multilevel heterogeneity inherent in this multi-institutional network. The results reveal a substantial site-level random intercept variance (σSite-RI = 0.50) compared with the residual variance (σϵ = 0.35), indicating significant baseline variation between centers that may be attributable to site-specific factors or differences in pulmonary function testing protocols. The inclusion of this site-level random intercept accommodates the substantial sample size imbalance between JHSC and UPSC. While the larger JHSC cohort proportionally drives the precision of the global fixed effects, the random effects structure utilizes partial pooling to preserve the unique baseline characteristics of the smaller UPSC cohort, preventing the larger site from disproportionately overriding site-specific inferences. At the patient level, heterogeneity in baseline severity is the dominant variance component, with patient-level random intercept variances ranging from 0.77 to 0.79 across sites. Notably, we observe distinct heterogeneity in disease progression rates between the two cohorts: the random slope variance for UPSC (σPatient−RS = 0.33) is markedly higher than that of JHSC (σPatient−RS = 0.22). This indicates that the UPSC cohort exhibits greater variability in individual rates of lung function decline, consistent with its clinical composition of higher-risk diffuse cutaneous and Scl-70 positive patients who often display more heterogeneous disease trajectories.

Finally, to evaluate clinical relevance and interpretability, we used PEAL to estimate longitudinal pFVC trajectories across clinically meaningful subgroups at JHSC (Fig. 3e, f). For instance, White patients with positive ACA antibodies demonstrate a slower rate of decline, while those with Scl-70 and diffuse cutaneous disease exhibit steeper declines. Male patients and those from older age groups also show more pronounced declines. These stratified predictions demonstrate PEAL’s potential to produce individualized, clinically plausible disease progression profiles, offering a valuable tool for risk stratification and decision support in decentralized research settings.

Data are generated for three synthetic sites, each with 300–500 patients and 1–10 longitudinal records per patient. The fitted model includes six covariates (three binary, three continuous), a nonlinear spline effect for time, and both patient-level random intercepts and slopes with site-specific variance components. We provide more details of the simulation setup in Methods.

Discussion

In this work, we introduce PEAL as a privacy-preserving, communication-efficient algorithm for federated modeling of longitudinal EHR data using three-level linear mixed models. PEAL offers several key advantages in multisite research networks. First, it achieves the two most essential properties of federated learning methods in practice: lossless and one-shot, under the assumption of standardized cohort extraction, identical covariate definitions, and data quality control across the distributed research network. These properties not only ensure statistical accuracy in estimating the parameters in a three-level linear mixed-effects model for EHR-based association study, but also account for practical considerations such as human-in-the-loop involvement and coordination, thereby providing a scalable and practically integrated solution to large-scale and privacy-constrained clinical research. Second, a distinguishing feature of PEAL is its ability to accommodate rich temporal structure in longitudinal data. By incorporating natural spline basis functions, PEAL models the nonlinear nature of disease progression. Moreover, it flexibly accounts for multi-layered heterogeneity by including random intercepts at both the site and patient levels, along with patient-level random slopes to reflect variation in disease evolution across individuals.

Applied to both internal validation analyses and a decentralized analysis by collaborating with JHSC and UPSC, PEAL not only reproduces pooled-model estimates exactly but also generates clinically coherent insights into disease progression. The results identify autoantibody-specific and demographic risk patterns consistent with established literature: ACA or anti-RNA polymerase III positivity and White race are associated with a higher baseline of pFVC trajectory, whereas Scl-70 positivity, diffuse cutaneous subtype, and male sex are associated with worse progression. Particularly, subgroup-specific predicted trajectories reveal distinct slopes of decline, illustrating PEAL’s ability to produce individualized, risk-stratified forecasts without direct access to patient-level data. These findings underscore PEAL’s potential as a scalable and privacy-preserving analytical tool for multisite research networks, enabling clinically meaningful inference and prediction while efficiently maintaining data-sharing constraints.

In our real-world application, we opt to include random slopes only for the temporal variable (time since onset) to avoid model overparameterization and instability in variance component estimation. This is an important consideration in distributed settings where sample sizes may vary substantially across sites. While our pragmatic approach balances flexibility and computational tractability, future implementations may benefit from more rigorous specification of the random effects structure. For instance, likelihood ratio tests or model comparison criteria, as described by Luo et al.30 can guide the inclusion of random slopes for additional covariates based on empirical support.

Nonetheless, several limitations and opportunities for future development remain. First, although PEAL supports complex hierarchical structures and temporal modeling, it is currently designed only for analyzing a single continuous outcome. Motivated by the scleroderma dataset, where multiple biomarkers can be jointly leveraged to predict patient trajectories, extending the framework to accommodate multivariate longitudinal outcomes (e.g., pFVC and DLCO39) would substantially improve its utility for a wider range of clinical applications.

Second, the lossless nature of PEAL guarantees algorithmic equivalence to a pooled-IPD analysis; it thus does not confer robustness against underlying statistical biases. If the pooled data suffer from non-random missingness, measurement error, or if the chosen model is misspecified, PEAL will exactly reproduce the biased estimates of the same LMM. For instance, missingness in long-term EHR registries is often informative. In the scleroderma application, such informative outcome missingness may introduce bias: patients with actively deteriorating lung function undergo more frequent testing, potentially leading the model to overestimate the rate of decline. Conversely, if severe patients drop out of the registries due to mortality, the complete-case cohort may be subject to survivor bias, resulting in an underestimation of true disease severity. Therefore, while PEAL solves the computational and privacy barriers of distributed modeling, ensuring statistical validity still requires rigorous data harmonization, careful model selection, and appropriate handling of structural missingness or measurement error prior to model estimation50,51.

Additionally, in the current PEAL implementation, sites transmit summary statistics that are many-to-one functions of the underlying data and are comparable in granularity to standard descriptive tables commonly approved under institutional review board and data governance policies52. Stronger privacy-protection technologies can be integrated when required by a DRN, including homomorphic encryption and differential privacy methods53,54. In practice, the appropriate level of privacy protection should be determined jointly with data partners based on the sample size, disease rarity, and regulatory requirements to ensure flexible deployment while maintaining rigorous privacy protection and statistical validity.

Future extensions of PEAL can also integrate deep learning-based personalized federated learning frameworks55, allowing for client-specific adaptation in high-dimensional, nonlinear prediction tasks. Scaling PEAL to larger datasets with high-dimensional features may require more efficient optimization strategies or stochastic approximation techniques to reduce memory and computation burden at both local and central sites.

In rare and heterogeneous autoimmune rheumatic diseases, leveraging national and international data across multiple sites has great potential to accelerate scientific discovery of clinically relevant subgroups, precise and personalized prediction of future disease course, and the investigation of optimal therapeutic strategies. Our next step is to apply the proposed federated learning framework within rheumatology consortia, enabling large-scale, privacy-preserving, and collaborative research on rheumatic diseases. Related efforts have demonstrated the value of harnessing complex, longitudinal data for individualized care; for example, Kim et al.56 developed a personalized visualization and analysis tool that aggregates multisystem trajectories and delivers real-time risk estimates for scleroderma patients. By leveraging diverse, real-world data sources without data sharing, our initiative builds on this vision and has the potential to transform rare disease research, accelerate biomarker discovery, and ultimately improve patient care and clinician efficiency across the country.

In summary, PEAL addresses critical methodological and practical challenges in federated analysis of longitudinal EHR data. By combining flexibility, lossless and one-shot estimation, and privacy-preserving design, it offers a robust foundation for future methodological innovations and clinical applications, and marks a significant step toward efficient, trustworthy, and large-scale analytics across heterogeneous health systems.

Nonlinear temporal effect

In the federated learning setting, modeling outcome trajectories using natural spline terms for time poses a key challenge since IPD cannot be shared across sites. To address this, we adopt a data-adaptive yet privacy-preserving strategy that estimates knot locations based on the aggregated distribution of time since onset across all sites. Each site contributes its histogram through binned counts of TSO, enabling the analysis center to approximate the pooled distribution and determine knot positions without accessing raw IPD.

Accordingly, the temporal trend f(timehij; γ) in Eq. (1) is modeled using natural cubic splines, with interior knots placed at empirical quantiles of the pooled TSO distribution. Following the standard practices for flexible spline fitting 57,58, we place M knots at pre-specified percentiles to ensure they align with regions of high data density. This quantile-based approach allows the spline to capture complex, nonlinear temporal patterns while avoiding overfitting in sparsely observed regions 59. While estimating quantiles from binned histograms introduces slight quantization error compared to exact individual event times, natural cubic splines are robust to minor perturbations in knot locations. For example, Supplementary Fig. 1 illustrates this process: site-level TSO histograms are first generated locally, and their aggregated counts approximate the pooled TSO distribution, from which empirical quantiles (25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles) are selected to guide knot placement for the natural spline basis.

PEAL algorithm design

PEAL features a two-stage federated algorithm for fitting three-level LMMs across distributed sites without sharing individual patient data. The key design goals are: (1) privacy-preservation through exclusive use of summary-level statistics, (2) communication efficiency by minimizing coordination and requiring only one round of message passing, and (3) lossless estimation, reproducing results identical to centralized pooled analysis.

To explore the characteristics of patients across different clinical sites, we begin with a systematic evaluation of cohorts to ensure data readiness and gain insights into site-level heterogeneity60,61. This step is critical for validating representativeness, assessing potential imbalance and biases, and guiding model specification.

First, we examine the cohort size at each site to understand the relative availability of patient data and to ensure sufficient representation for downstream modeling. Disparities in sample sizes may reflect institutional differences in referral patterns, diagnosis practices, or data collection procedures, and can influence estimation stability in federated learning models.

Next, we explore the distribution of clinically relevant predictors. For example, we examine biomarkers by computing the prevalence of key scleroderma-related autoantibodies, including anti-Centromere, anti-topoisomerase I, and RNA polymerase III. Site-level variations in biomarker positivity may indicate differences in disease subtypes or patient selection, which are important considerations when interpreting longitudinal trajectories of pulmonary function.

We also summarize key demographic characteristics such as distributions of gender, race, and age at disease onset. Additionally, we could analyze calendar-year-specific incidence rates to understand the temporal dynamics of cohort enrollment and to assess potential shifts in diagnosis trends over time. These summaries help contextualize the cohort and evaluate generalizability.

To evaluate disease progression timelines, we focus on the time since onset of non-Raynaud’s symptoms, which reflects the duration between the disease onset and each clinical observation. We visualize the distribution of TSO using binned histograms at each site, and compute the global empirical quantiles to capture central tendencies and variability. This enables comparison of disease trajectories across sites and informs the appropriate placement of spline knots in the modeling stage.

Before analysis, all data undergo standardized processing to ensure consistency in variable encoding, scales, and time alignment across sites. We further restrict to complete cases to address missingness in key variables and outcomes.
