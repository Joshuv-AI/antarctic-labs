Re: the problem with calling it “unexplained infertility”
Authors: Meurig Thomas Gallagher, Eleanor Molloy, Laura L. Jones, Jackson C. Kirkman-Brown
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read your recent editorial addressing unexplained infertility, a topic in which we share your passion and interest. We would like to discuss further the proposition that the terminology itself contributes to patient confusion and anxiety.

Although the desire to provide couples with clarity and direction is essential, reframing unexplained infertility as a predominantly mechanical problem risks substituting speculation for evidence and may ultimately undermine informed, patient-centred care. Indeed, one must be careful not to overdiagnose slight phenotypes as though they are a concrete cause.

We do not believe that it is a disservice to patients to describe infertility as “unexplained” when definitive answers are unavailable. In this context, unexplained infertility is less a diagnosis than an acknowledgement of the limits of current investigation: we are unable to identify a single cause, or constellation of causes, that can be said to determine the difficulty conceiving. Using alternative, technical terminology such as “idiopathic,” rather than plain language, creates confusion and misunderstanding for patients unfamiliar with medical language.

Unpublished qualitative findings from the UNiTY trial (UNexplained InfertiliTY Treatment Trial1) indicate that receiving a diagnosis of unexplained infertility is itself complex. Some couples understood the diagnosis as provisional, a placeholder for causes not yet identified by clinicians. Others experienced it as ambivalent: reassuring in that no specific pathology had been detected, yet distressing because it offered neither explanation nor a clear treatment pathway. The absence of a defined cause consequently leaves couples oscillating between hope and discouragement on a month-to-month basis.

The impulse to identify a single, tangible cause is deeply human. Couples understandably want to be able to point to “the” factor responsible for their infertility, particularly if doing so suggests a clear treatment pathway. However, human fertility is complex, multifactorial, and not always reducible to “mechanical defects.” Assertions that “the vast majority” of unexplained infertility is mechanical in origin may provide psychological comfort, but such claims are not supported by robust evidence. To borrow ideas from Yuri Lazebnik,2 although we may desire to pin causes down to a single key factor, by doing so we will never “fix the radio”—instead, we must be aware of and acknowledge the complex multiplex of factors that make natural fertility such an astonishing feat, and it is up to us, as health care professionals, to reflect this complexity and potential for difficulties to couples in a measured and helpful way.

The literature consistently shows that in infertile couples, women experience a greater psychological burden and internalised blame,3 potentially reflecting the disproportionate research and clinical focus on female infertility. An unexplained infertility diagnosis, when framed in terms of what is not yet known but explained poorly and using medical jargon that is opaque for patients, risks further entrenching these beliefs.

Fundamentally, infertility itself is a period marked by uncertainty, confusion, and anxiety. These experiences cannot be eliminated simply by assigning an unproven diagnosis. There certainly is room for improvement in how unexplained infertility is communicated, and we can learn from existing models for handling uncertainty in diagnostics such as those of Meyer et al.4 They suggest that patient-centred care requires that clinicians explicitly acknowledge uncertainty, plan around it and clearly communicate next steps, concepts mirrored in the shared meaningful diagnosis model of Ledford et al.5 Even when well-intentioned, replacing uncertainty with the attribution of unproven mechanic factors may feel like a step forward for couples, but it does little to advance their understanding or support informed joint decision-making.
