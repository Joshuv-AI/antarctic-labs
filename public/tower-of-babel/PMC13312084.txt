Psychological distress: Bifactor modeling of the DASS-21 in Chilean healthcare workers
Authors: Marjory Güilgüiruca-Retamal, Paola Muzatto-Negrón, Adriana Acosta-Ramos, Jacqueline Espinoza-Ibacache, Cristian Pinto-Cortez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21) is widely used to assess mental health symptoms; however, its dimensionality in working populations, particularly healthcare workers, remains under debate.ObjectiveThis study aimed to examine the dimensional structure of the DASS-21 among Chilean healthcare workers to evaluate its suitability as a screening measure of psychological distress in occupational contexts exposed to crises, emergencies, or disasters.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted in 2021 using a non-probabilistic convenience sample of 239 healthcare workers from a hospital in northern Chile. Confirmatory factor analyses were conducted to test four competing models (unidimensional, three correlated factors, second order, and bifactor) using structural equation modeling. Model fit was evaluated using RMSEA, CFI, and TLI, complemented by auxiliary bifactor indices (ECV, ωH, ωHS, PUC, ARPB).ResultsAll models showed acceptable fit (RMSEA = 0.038–0.065; CFI = 0.974–0.992; TLI = 0.971–0.990). Although the bifactor model demonstrated the best global fit, auxiliary indices supported the dominance of a general factor (ECV = 0.87; ωH = 0.94), with limited reliable variance attributable to the specific factors (ωHS ≤ 0.16). Measurement invariance across sex was supported.ConclusionsIn this non-clinical sample characterized by low symptom severity, the DASS-21 operates primarily as an essentially unidimensional measure of psychological distress. From a nursing practice perspective, the total score may be used as a parsimonious screening indicator to support early detection, monitoring, and preventive mental health strategies among healthcare workers.

Background

The literature has highlighted the vulnerability of healthcare workers due to the mental health problems reported by this group as a result of the COVID-19 crisis and health emergency (Osorio et al., 2024; Wauters et al., 2023). Several studies confirm that healthcare workers experienced increased levels of stress, depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation in response to this critical event (Muñoz-Fernández et al., 2020; Urzúa et al., 2020). The Pan American Health Organization (2022) found that between 15% and 22% of healthcare personnel presented symptoms associated with depression, and up to 15% considered suicide during the COVID-19 pandemic. In their review, Wauters et al. (2023) note that during the pandemic, healthcare personnel were affected by stigmatization, stress, anxiety, and fatigue. At the national level, healthcare workers reported psychological distress, symptoms of depression, and suicidal ideation, with figures higher than pre-pandemic levels (Alvarado et al., 2021).

Valid and reliable instruments are essential for assessing mental states (Muñiz et al., 2015). During the pandemic, although several tools were used to evaluate healthcare workers’ mental health, the absence of psychometric analyses posed a challenge for accurately studying their psychological and working conditions. Among the recognized instruments for assessing mental health problems is the shortened version of the DASS-21 (Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995), which assesses symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress through 21 items.

The DASS-21 characterizes depression through the presence of symptoms such as sadness, loss of self-esteem, and low self-perceived probability of achieving life goals. Anxiety is measured through symptoms related to fear, anticipation of negative events, and situational arousal. The stress scale assesses the state of arousal, activation, and tension associated with a low threshold for frustration or anger (Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995). A distinguishing feature of the DASS-21 is that it has enjoyed several cross-cultural validations (Cao et al., 2023; Thiyagarajan et al., 2022), is a self-report instrument, simple, relatively brief, and has been used in diverse populations, from students (Antúnez & Vinet, 2012; Cao et al., 2023; Contreras-Mendoza et al., 2021) to workers (Al-Kalbani et al., 2022; Güilgüiruca et al., 2023).

From a psychometric perspective, although the DASS-21 has significant evidence supporting its original three-dimensional structure (Al-Kalbani et al., 2022; Antúnez & Vinet, 2012; Cao et al., 2023; Mellor et al., 2015; Tripathi et al., 2024), various studies have proposed alternative structures, leaving the scale’s dimensionality in question (Osman et al., 2012). Indeed, although the DASS-21’s main structural model (the oblique or three-factor model) has received consistent empirical support, its high inter-factor correlations remain a key limitation. To address this, some studies have tested a second-order model in which depression, anxiety, and stress load onto a higher-order factor—general distress (Caravaca-Sánchez et al., 2026; Henry & Crawford, 2005; Murphy et al., 2024; Osman et al., 2012; Soria-Reyes et al., 2024). It should be noted that this perspective lacks the cross-cultural recognition typically associated with validity processes, making it a less widespread and less robust alternative.

In an attempt to recover the original multifactorial structure of the DASS-21, studies using bifactor models aim to confirm the joint presence of the three uncorrelated factors and the general factor (Kia-Keating et al., 2018; Lee & Kim, 2022; Moore et al., 2017; Vaughan et al., 2020; Yılmaz Koğar & Koğar, 2023). However, the findings from this approach highlight the role of the general factor to the detriment of specific factors, which—in exhaustive analyses—tend to lose relevance, giving rise to the proposal of a single construct called general malaise or negative affect (González-Rivera et al., 2020; Güilgüiruca et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2020; Moore et al., 2017; Naumova, 2022; Osman et al., 2012; Zanon et al., 2021).

Indeed, in a study of samples from countries across three continents, Zanon et al. (2021) examined four theoretical models. Despite initially confirming a good fit of the bifactor model, the results of the invariance analysis, along with their cross-country dissimilarities, suggest using the DASS-21 as a general measure of distress. Following this line of thought, in a sample of hospital workers in China, Jiang et al. (2020) tested a bifactor model; however, they were unable to corroborate this structure. Instead, the findings supported the instrument’s unidimensional structure, in which the three DASS-21 factors are so strongly correlated that their independence cannot be sustained. Similar findings were reported by Cao et al. (2023), who observed poor discriminant validity, cross-factor loadings, and high correlations between factors among Chinese university students. The authors recommended using the construct of psychological distress. The same is true in a study of clinical samples from Brazil (Peixoto et al., 2021), in which, despite corroborating the bifactor model via CFA, the authors ultimately lean toward a unidimensional perspective after cross-loading various items.

Similar evidence has been reported in national samples. For instance, Güilgüiruca et al. (2023) found good fit indices for four theoretical models, including the oblique and bifactor models, among workers from a Chilean service-sector company. However, complementary analyses led them to dismiss the three-factor model due to low reliabilities, low explained common variance of the specific factors, low relative parameter bias, and a high percentage of item correlations. They concluded that the general factor plays a central role, while the specific factors are limited.

In samples of young people, Román et al. (2014) corroborated the three-factor model but recommended examining a potential second-order factor, which they termed negative affectivity. This aligns with Antúnez and Vinet (2012), who, in a study of Chilean university students, highlighted the relevance of a general factor given the presence of items loading simultaneously on more than one factor. From a clinical-practical perspective, one possible explanation for this situation is the recognized comorbidity between depression and anxiety. Indeed, more than 50% of patients diagnosed with depression also present with anxiety, which is associated with more severe depressive symptoms, a higher likelihood of suicidal ideation, and a greater impact on functionality and quality of life (Aragonès et al., 2009; Kessler et al., 2005; Rosellini et al., 2018). Indicators along the same lines show that 30% of Chilean university students have comorbidity with anxiety, depression, and stress symptoms, a condition that complicates the detection, differentiation, and management of these symptoms (Barrera-Herrera & San Martín, 2021).

The complexity of the DASS-21 constructs has given rise to other explanations regarding the strong association between its dimensions, including: a) that depression and anxiety are part of the same symptomatic continuum; b) that they are completely heterogeneous syndromes and only share some symptoms (Apóstolo et al., 2008; Apóstolo et al., 2011); and c) that both entities represent alternative manifestations of forms of vulnerability (Apóstolo et al., 2008; Apóstolo et al., 2011; Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995), which is consistent with the authors of the instrument and with the transdiagnostic approach. The transdiagnostic approach arises from the low reliability of diagnoses, high rates of comorbidity, and the increase in diagnostic categories (González Pando et al., 2018). According to this perspective, mental health problems, especially anxiety and depression, are underpinned by variables such as negative affect—aversive mood, anxiety, hostility, fear, and sadness (Clark & Watson, 1991), and along with negative mood, positive affect, intolerance of uncertainty, and anxiety sensitivity (González Pando et al., 2018). Therefore, understanding comorbidity in psychopathology based on shared elements (processes, variables) that cause or maintain symptoms and disorders (Mansell et al., 2009; Sandín et al., 2019) is key to a more in-depth analytical approach to the DASS-21. However, the transdiagnostic explanation could be a useful perspective for understanding mental health phenomena in the workplace, as it allows us to dispense with diagnostic categories in a field that requires a more generic and less stigmatizing understanding of workers. Thus, analyzing the structure of a scale as important as the DASS-21 will allow us to identify what we are measuring when administering this instrument, namely, whether it is a unidimensional screening scale that reports psychological distress or whether it reports symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress as clearly differentiated entities.

Based on this background, our study aimed to analyze the dimensional structure of the DASS-21 among healthcare workers at a hospital in northern Chile, to gather evidence supporting its use from a transdiagnostic perspective in healthcare workers and other occupational groups exposed to crises, emergencies, or disasters.

Methods

Design, Sample, and Procedure

This study used a cross-sectional survey design. A nonprobability convenience sample of 239 healthcare workers (75% women) from a hospital in northern Chile was included. The mean age was 32.13 years (SD = 9.2), and the average job tenure was 4.7 years (SD = 5.8). Of the participants, 28% had COVID-related fees, 20% were replacements, 20% were contracted, 18% received lump-sum fees, 11% were permanent; 42% were technical, 39% were professional, 12% were service assistants; 1% were physicians; 45% worked in the Emergency Medical Services (SAMU) and emergency services, 44% worked in patient management and care, and 11% worked in other services. The survey included sociodemographic variables (sex, age, and educational level) and employment variables (tenure and position), as well as items from the DASS-21. Inclusion criteria required at least one month of job tenure.

Instrument

The Chilean version of the DASS-21 was used (Román et al., 2016; Román et al., 2014). The scale has 21 items across three subscales: depression (7 items), anxiety (7 items), and stress (7 items). The response format is a 4-point Likert scale, where 0 indicates “Does not describe anything that happened to me or what I felt during the week” and 3 indicates “Yes, this happened to me a lot, or almost always.” The following are example items: “I felt sad and depressed” (depression), “I was worried about situations in which I might panic and make a fool of myself” (anxiety), and “I found it difficult to relax” (stress).

Data Analysis

SPSS version 24 (https://www.ibm.com/) was used to perform descriptive analyses (mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis). Skewness and kurtosis indices > 2 were considered indicative of a substantial departure from a normal distribution (George & Mallery, 2010). The statistical program Mplus version 7.0 (Muthén & Muthén, 2010) was used to conduct confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using structural equation modeling to assess the fit of four DASS-21 models, based on evidence in the literature. The unidimensional model (one general factor), the oblique model (three correlated factors), the second-order model (three second-order factors and one general first-order factor), and the bifactor model (one general factor orthogonal to the three uncorrelated specific factors).

The Unweighted Least Squares Mean and Variance adjusted (ULSMV) method was used, considered an appropriate estimator for non-normal distributions and categorical variables (Schumacker & Lomax, 2016). Model fit was evaluated using the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). Following conventional recommendations, CFI and TLI values ≥ 0.90 and RMSEA values ≤ 0.08 were considered indicative of acceptable model fit (Kline, 2005). Given that the models are not nested, comparisons were complemented using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The model presenting the lowest AIC, BIC, or sample-size–adjusted BIC values was considered to provide the best balance between model fit and parsimony (Akaike, 1987).

Specifically, bifactor modeling evaluates the joint influence of a general factor and uncorrelated specific factors (Canivez, 2016; Reise, 2012). The fit of this model follows the same acceptability criteria as those for traditional CFAs, although this study includes a set of auxiliary indices to assess the joint significance of the general factor and the specific factors(Reise, 2012). The auxiliary indices used in this study are the explained common variance (ECV) of the general factor, the percentage of uncontaminated correlations (PUC), and the omega coefficient of the subscales (ωHS) (Rodriguez et al., 2016; Smits et al., 2015). The acceptability of a bifactor model is corroborated by the presence of the general factor, with ECV > 0.60 and PUC > 0.80, and by the presence of the specific factors, with ωHS ≥ 0.30 (Smits et al., 2015). The reliability of the bifactor model (general and specific factors) will be estimated using the H coefficient, with values > 0.70 indicating adequate reliability (Raykov & Hancock, 2005).

On the other hand, the acceptability of a unidimensional model of the instrument is supported by ECV > 0.60, PUC > 0.80, and an omega coefficient for the general scale (ωH) ≥ 0.70 (Rodriguez et al., 2016; Smits et al., 2015). In turn, Relative Parameter Bias (ARPB) values between 10% and 15% would indicate no significant differences in the factor loadings between the unidimensional and bifactor models, confirming that the unidimensional perspective is the most pertinent (Rodriguez et al., 2016). Additionally, an analysis of the factor loadings (λ) of the items in the model with the best psychometric quality was considered to rule out negative, non-significant variances, or factor loadings with variable signs in the data (Heinrich et al., 2020). λ > 0.50 will be considered as evidence of validity (Hair et al., 2019).

Additionally, measurement invariance of the unidimensional model was examined across sex (men vs. women) using multi-group CFA. Configural, metric, and scalar invariance were sequentially tested. Changes in model fit were evaluated using ΔCFI ≤ .010 and ΔRMSEA ≤ .015 as criteria for invariance (Chen, 2007). Given the ordinal response format of the DASS-21 items, invariance conclusions were primarily based on changes in CFI, complemented by information criteria (AIC, BIC), rather than strict reliance on chi-square differences.

Ethical Consideration

Participation was voluntary following an institutional invitation, and all participants provided written informed consent. The study procedure, consent, data collection instruments, and invitation letters were approved by the Ethics Committee (CEC) of Universidad de Tarapacá (No. 29/2021).

Continuing with the bifactor model analysis, the ECV was estimated for the general factor (0.87) and for the three specific factors: depression (0.06), anxiety (0.03), and stress (0.02). The general factor had a representative magnitude and greater explanatory power than the other factors, supporting a unidimensional model of the DASS-21 (Table 3). In this case, the specific factors jointly explained 2% of the total variance, while the general factor explained 87% of the item variance. Similarly, we can observe in Table 3 that the highest hierarchical omega coefficient is that of the general factor (ωH = 0.94), while in the specific factors, they are considerably lower (ωHS depression = 0.15; ωHS anxiety = 0.07; ωHS stress = 0.02), failing to exceed the expected criterion (≥ 0.70). Table 3 also reports the percentage of uncontaminated correlations where 70% of the item correlations are not associated with the multidimensionality of the DASS-21 (PUC = 0.70). Evidence in favor, in both cases, of the unidimensionality of the instrument. The relative parameter bias (ARPB = 0.03) is extremely low, suggesting that the instrument’s multidimensionality is insufficient. In the same sense, only the general factor (H = 0.97) presents an adequate value for reliability, and the specific factors present prohibitive magnitudes (H ≤ 0.29). These elements are further configured into clear indicators of unidimensionality. Finally, the analysis of the factor loadings of the unidimensional model shows that the factor loadings (λ) are > 0.50, and the internal consistency analysis reports an ordinal alpha (αORD = 0.97), providing evidence of optimal reliability of the instrument under a unifactorial approach.

Discussion

The DASS-21 is an instrument used in diverse populations, including healthcare workers (Jiang et al., 2020; Nadeem et al., 2023; Thu Pham et al., 2023; Urzúa et al., 2020). Although its use is well recognized, the scale’s dimensionality remains a matter of debate from a psychometric perspective. An important methodological consideration concerns the pronounced floor effects observed in several DASS-21 items, reflected in high proportions of zero responses and marked positive skewness. Such distributional characteristics are common in non-clinical samples with low symptom severity and have been consistently reported in prior studies using the DASS-21 in working and student populations (Cao et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2020; Sinclair et al., 2012). Under conditions of restricted symptom variance, item correlations may be artificially inflated at the lower end of the scale, favoring the emergence of a dominant general factor. Therefore, the unidimensional structure observed in the present study should be interpreted as reflecting essential unidimensionality in a low-distress occupational sample, rather than as evidence against the instrument’s potential multidimensionality in clinical or high-symptom populations.

The objective of our study was to understand the structure of the DASS-21 among healthcare workers for future application in the healthcare sector, as well as in work settings subject to stress during crises, emergencies, or disasters. In this study, four of the most frequently used models for the DAS-21 were tested (one-dimensional, oblique, second-order, and two-factor models). After comparing the fit indices from the CFA, it can be concluded that although all models achieved adequate fit, the bi-factor model provided the best fit to the data. Despite this, and after analyzing complementary auxiliary indices, the bi-factor structure could not be sustained, thereby dismissing the role of depression, anxiety, and stress factors and ultimately supporting a one-dimensional structure for the scale. Specifically, the results of our study show that the DASS-21 in this sample of healthcare workers has a structure composed of a single factor that explains 87% of the variance, rather than the three factors proposed in the original DASS-21 model (Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995), which at the time explained just over 40% of the variance.

Furthermore, the extremely low magnitude of the Relative Parameter Bias (ARPB) confirmed that there were no significant differences between the factor loadings of the unidimensional and bifactor models; the high reliability obtained for the general factor (ωH = 0.94) contrasts with the low reliabilities of the specific factors (ωHS ≤ 0.15); and beecause 70% of item correlations are not associated with multidimensionality (PUCV = 70%), the superiority of the unidimensional perspective is suggested. It is worth noting that our findings are empirically supported in both international (Cao et al., 2023; González-Rivera et al., 2020; Jiang et al., 2020; Zanon et al., 2021) and national (Antúnez & Vinet, 2012; Güilgüiruca et al., 2023; Román et al., 2014) samples.

For example, internationally, Zanon et al. (2021) found results quite similar to ours. Indeed, the authors found that although the bifactor model yielded comparatively better fit indices, complementary analyses did not corroborate the existence of specific factors and only confirmed the general factor, leading them to favor a unidimensional approach. Along the same lines, the study by Jiang et al. (2020) identified such high correlations among factors among workers at a Chinese hospital that they ruled out the scale’s multidimensional argument. Along the same lines, among Chinese university students, Cao et al. (2023), despite initially confirming the three-dimensional structure, found poor discriminant validity, cross-factor loadings, and high correlations between factors, ultimately rejected the multifactorial perspective of the items and instead recommended that the DASS-21 be used as a general construct of psychological distress. Similar conclusions are raised by Peixoto et al. (2021), who also found evidence supporting the unidimensional approach in a Brazilian sample.

Following this approach, various findings are found in national samples. For example, the study by Güilgüiruca et al. (2023) proposes that a unidimensional construct of psychological distress would be the best alternative for understanding the structure of the DASS-21 in service sector workers. Along the same lines are studies in Chilean universities and adolescent populations (Antúnez & Vinet, 2012; Román et al., 2014), which, despite finding a good fit for the oblique structure, suggest that negative affectivity could be a latent variable underlying the three factors.

Our study’s findings add to other approaches that challenge the exclusively three-dimensional notion. Indeed, from a clinical perspective, the unidimensional argument is explained by the presence of high rates of comorbidity between diagnoses of depression and anxiety disorders, whose concomitance reaches around 50%, or even more (Aragonès et al., 2009; Kessler et al., 2005; Rosellini et al., 2018). This condition, in psychometric terms, is manifested in the high levels of shared variance present in these disorders (Osman et al., 2012; Sinclair et al., 2012), a situation that relativizes the exclusive assumption of the DASS-21’s three-dimensionality.

However, it is important to highlight that the unidimensionality of the DASS-21 coexists with various studies that have corroborated the presence of all three factors (Al-Kalbani et al., 2022; Antúnez & Vinet, 2012; Mellor et al., 2015; Tripathi et al., 2024), a situation that contributes to the instrument’s dimensionality being a constant subject of debate.

It is worth recalling the approaches of Zanon et al. (2021) regarding the importance of recognizing that the choice of structural models reflects the perspectives and comprehensive models of psychopathology used by researchers themselves. Thus, a unidimensional model offers a more integrated view of psychopathology, aligning with the foundations of the transdiagnostic perspective. Indeed, the transdiagnostic perspective emphasizes the convergences between anxiety disorders and mood states, rather than their differences (Barlow et al., 2020; Forbush & Watson, 2013; Henry & Crawford, 2005). Transdiagnostic variables such as negative and positive affect, intolerance of uncertainty, and anxiety sensitivity correspond to common processes across various mental health problems (González Pando et al., 2018), and are especially relevant for anxiety and depression due to the marked overlap of symptoms and etiopathogenic factors (Sandín et al., 2019).

Thus, a transdiagnostic approach in the workplace can help overcome the predominantly clinical focus on diagnostic categories by adopting a more generic focus. This would contribute to reducing the various stigmatization processes associated with mental health problems that workers in general are exposed to (Hanisch et al., 2016). This situation is often detrimental not only to employees but also to organizations, affecting productivity and costs (Dollard et al., 2014). Therefore, it is important to emphasize the transdiagnostic notion in the workplace to promote inclusive, discrimination-free work environments, especially in sectors such as healthcare, where employees’ mental health is key to fulfilling the public mandate they assume regarding the general health of the population.

Conclusion

The present study provides evidence that, in a sample of Chilean healthcare workers characterized by low levels of psychological symptoms, the DASS-21 operates primarily as an essentially unidimensional measure of psychological distress. The dominance of the general factor and the limited, reliable variance attributable to the specific depression, anxiety, and stress dimensions support the interpretability of the total score as a parsimonious screen indicator in occupational health contexts. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence that the DASS-21 is inherently or universally unidimensional. Rather, they suggest that under conditions of restricted symptom severity, such as those typically observed in non-clinical working populations, the instrument’s multidimensional structure may be functionally masked, giving rise to an essential unidimensionality (Reise, 2012; Rodriguez et al., 2016; Zanon et al., 2021). From a practical perspective, particularly in nursing and occupational health settings, the DASS-21 total score may facilitate early detection and monitoring of psychological distress among healthcare workers, supporting preventive and non-stigmatizing mental health strategies in high-demand work environments. Future research in clinical samples or populations exposed to higher psychological burden is required to determine whether the specific dimensions of depression, anxiety, and stress emerge more clearly under conditions of greater symptom variability.
