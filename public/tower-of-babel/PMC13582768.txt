The effects of glutamine supplementation on inflammatory and oxidative stress indices in chronic diseases: a systematic review
Authors: Sara Movahed, Alla Bahadorkhan, Valeri Malov, Maryam Emadzadeh, Naseh Pahlavani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic diseases are often associated with increased inflammation and oxidative stress, which contribute to disease progression. Glutamine, a conditionally essential amino acid, has been studied for its potential anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties in various chronic conditions. The present systematic review evaluates the effects of glutamine supplementation on inflammatory markers and oxidative stress indices in patients with chronic diseases. Systematic searches were performed in web databases; Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed/Medline until May 2025, to identify related randomized controlled trials (RCTs) according to the Cochrane Library and PICOS criteria (population: individuals > 18 years, intervention: glutamine, Comparison: placebo or control, Outcomes: inflammatory and oxidative stress markers in chronic diseases). The Cochrane collaboration tool was used to assess the risk of bias in clinical trials. Six RCTs that assessed the effect of glutamine supplementation on inflammation and oxidative stress markers were included in the study. In these studies, glutamine was administered to the participants through oral or parenteral routes. In three studies improve inflammation via significant reductions in CRP were observed. However, in three studies that examined TNF-α as an inflammatory marker, only one study found its levels to be significantly reduced. Also, of the two studies that examined oxidative stress levels, only one study significantly decreased the MDA and increased SOD levels, and in the other study, glutamine supplementation had no significant effect on glutathione levels. Our findings showed that glutamine supplementation might have a positive effect on inflammation and oxidative stress indices such as TNF-α, CRP, MDA, and SOD in some chronic diseases, however, these effects have not been shown in all studies, so more carefully designed clinical trial studies with different doses of glutamine on inflammation and oxidative stress in chronic diseases are needed. Introduction

Chronic diseases, such as such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), are characterized by persistent oxidative stress and low-grade systemic inflammation. These condition often associated with persistent inflammation and oxidative stress, which exacerbate tissue damage and contribute to disease progression (Brinks and Landwehr 2015; Liehn and Cabrera-Fuentes 2015). Oxidative stress and inflammation are key physiological responses that play critical roles in various pathological and physiological conditions, including chronic diseases, critical illness, intense exercise, and infections such as COVID-19 (Mohajeri et al. 2021a). In recent years, nutritional interventions have gained attention as potential adjunct therapies to modulate these pathological mechanisms (Gropper 2023). Among various nutrients, glutamine; a conditionally essential amino acid has been investigated for its immunomodulatory and antioxidant properties (Zhang et al. 2023; Aldarini et al. 2017).

Glutamine serves as a primary energy source for immune cells and enterocytes, and it plays a critical role in maintaining redox balance by influencing glutathione synthesis, the body’s most potent endogenous antioxidant (Matés et al. 2002). Preclinical studies suggest that glutamine supplementation may attenuate pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) and reduce markers of oxidative damage (e.g., malondialdehyde, protein carbonyl) in chronic disease models (Cruzat et al. 2018; Hasani et al. 2021). However, clinical evidence remains inconsistent, with some trials reporting significant benefits, while others show neutral or context-dependent effects (9). In chronic inflammatory diseases like IBD and rheumatoid arthritis, glutamine deficiency exacerbates mucosal damage and inflammatory cytokine production (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6). Glutamine supplementation has been shown to reduce intestinal permeability, suppress NF-κB activation, and enhance glutathione synthesis, thereby attenuating oxidative damage and inflammation (Zhang et al. 2022). Clinical trials suggest that glutamine supplementation may improve antioxidant capacity (e.g., TAC) and reduce systemic inflammation, potentially slowing disease progression (Heyland et al. 2015).

Despite the growing body of research, no comprehensive systematic review has yet synthesized the available data on glutamine’s dual effects on inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers across diverse chronic diseases. Existing reviews have often focused on specific conditions (e.g., critical illness, cancer) or limited outcomes, leaving a gap in understanding its broader therapeutic potential. This systematic review aims to evaluate the current evidence on glutamine supplementation and its impact on key inflammatory and oxidative stress indices in patients with various chronic diseases, providing insights for clinical practice and future research directions.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review was conducted following the Cochrane library guidelines to ensure methodological rigor and transparency (Stovold et al. 2014). Prior to conducting this systematic review, the study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews) under the registration number CRD420251049112.

Meta-analysis was considered but not performed due to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across the six included trials, including: (a) different chronic disease populations (stable vs. acute/surgical), (b) variable glutamine dosing regimens (0.5 g/kg to 30 g/day), (c) different routes of administration (oral vs. parenteral), (d) different outcome measurement time points (1 day to 6 weeks), and (e) inconsistent reporting of summary statistics (some studies reported only baseline vs. post-intervention without between-group comparisons). Under these conditions, pooling effect sizes would produce statistically unreliable and clinically misleading estimates.

Search strategy

A comprehensive systematic literature search for the relevant articles regarding glutamine supplementation was performed up to May 2025 in databases such as ISI Web of Knowledge, PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Cochrane Library database using various keywords, including Glutamine ("glutamine," "L-glutamine," "glutamine supplementation"), in combination with Chronic diseases ("cardiovascular disease," "diabetes," cardiopulmonary diseases," Rheumatoid arthritis, "COPD," gastrointestinal diseases", "inflammatory bowel disease," "cancer"), Inflammation & oxidative stress ("inflammatory cytokines," "TNF-alpha," "IL-6," “CRP’, "oxidative stress," "malondialdehyde," "glutathione," glutathione peroxidase," "total antioxidant capacity"). After eliminating duplicate references, the eligibility assessment of the identified studies was carried out independently by two investigators.

Our search strategy was structured using the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome, and Study Design (PICOS) framework. (Table 1). The detailed search methodology is provided in the online Supplementary Materials. After conducting the search, the results were imported into Covidence software (Covidence, Melbourne, VIC, Australia) to eliminate duplicates and facilitate screening. Two researchers (A.B. and S.M.) independently reviewed the titles and abstracts of the retrieved articles. Subsequently, both investigators evaluated the full texts of potentially relevant studies against the predefined eligibility criteria. To ensure comprehensiveness, we also manually searched the reference lists of all included full-text articles for additional relevant studies. Final study inclusion was determined by consensus between the two authors, with any discrepancies resolved by the senior author (N.P.). Eligibility criteria, study selection and screening

Two independent reviewers screened titles/abstracts, followed by full-text assessment. Discrepancies were resolved via consensus or a third reviewer. The selection process was documented using a Cochrane flow diagram.

The eligibility screening of relevant articles and data extraction were conducted based on specific criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) randomized clinical trials involving human subjects, with no restrictions on trial design (e.g., duration, placebo control, crossover, or blinding); (2) adult participants (age ≥ 18 years) diagnosed with chronic diseases (e.g., cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, cancer, COPD, IBD, and rheumatoid arthritis); (3) studies on glutamine supplementation, dose, formulation, frequency, and route of administration (Oral, enteral or parenteral glutamine supplementation in any form (e.g., L-glutamine, glutamine dipeptides) and dosage while studies on glutamine-rich foods needed to report the frequency and quantity provided—only studies where glutamine effects could be isolated from other nutrients were eligible, and (4) studies had to measure oxidative stress/redox status or inflammation markers. The outcomes of interest included changes in inflammatory biomarkers (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6, CRP) and oxidative stress indices (e.g., malondialdehyde [MDA], glutathione [GSH], total antioxidant capacity [TAC]). Eligible study types consisted of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) studies.

Studies were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) longitudinal observational studies, non-randomized clinical interventions, training studies, or investigations involving studies on acute conditions (e.g., sepsis, trauma) combined with glutamine supplementation; (2) research involving non-adult participants or acute patient populations; and (3) Animal or in vitro studies, review articles, case reports, or conference abstracts without full data. (4) Studies were also excluded if the inflammation or oxidative stress markers were not measured as primary or secondary outcomes.

Data extraction

After conducting the search, all records were exported to Covidence software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) which automatically identified and removed duplicate entries based on title, author, and publication year matching. Study selection was performed independently by three reviewers (A.B., V.M, and S.M.) at both title/abstract and full-text stages. Data extraction was conducted by one author (N.P.) and independently verified by a second author (S.M.). Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion; if consensus could not be reached, a fourth reviewer (M.E.) made the final decision.

The data extraction process was conducted systematically by the one author (N.P.), who compiled all relevant information into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, with subsequent independent verification performed by a second researcher (S.M.). Following our predetermined PICOS framework, we extracted comprehensive study details including authorship and publication year, study design characteristics and duration, participant demographics (sample size, gender distribution, and age range), specific intervention and comparator details (type, composition, dosage, and timing protocols), as well as all pertinent outcome measurements recorded both at baseline and during various post-intervention assessment periods. Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Cochrane Risk-of-Bias Tool, following the recommendations outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (Chandler et al. 2019). This comprehensive assessment tool examines six critical domains: selection bias, performance bias, detection bias, attrition bias, reporting bias, and other potential sources of bias specific to each research question. Special consideration was given to key methodological aspects including intention-to-treat analysis in parallel-group studies and adequate washout periods in crossover designs. Additional factors such as potential confounders (including dietary habits, physical activity levels, and treatment adherence) and the implementation of adverse effect monitoring were carefully evaluated when appraising study strengths and limitations. Two independent reviewers, blinded to each other's assessments, systematically categorized each study's risk of bias as high, unclear, or low, supporting their judgments with direct textual evidence from the articles. Following this rigorous evaluation process for all included studies. If a final conclusion was unable to be achieved, a third author was required to determine the final conclusion.

Results

Our systematic search yielded 2,068 articles from searched databases. Following duplicate removal, we screened 1,274 unique records through title and abstract evaluation, subsequently excluding 1,169 studies. The remaining 105 full-text articles underwent comprehensive assessment, with 99 excluded based on predefined criteria (detailed in Fig. 1). Ultimately, 6 studies met all eligibility requirements and were included in our systematic review.

Of the 6 included studies, all 6 studies used a parallel group design (Engel et al. 2009; Huang et al. 2018; Dock-Nascimento et al. 2012; Hond et al. 1999a; Lu et al. 2011; Mansour et al. 2015), 1 had three treatment arms (Engel et al. 2009), and 1 had four treatment arms (Dock-Nascimento et al. 2012).

The number of participants in this review was 328. The sample sizes were relatively good, with an average size of 54, ranging from 14–123. The average range of participant’s age 18–72 years. (Table 2).

Four studies were assessed to have overall low risk of bias and one at an overall unclear risk of bias, also one study assessed moderate risk of bias (Table 3). Main sources of bias related to selection, carry over effects, attrition and other sources of bias. Detection bias was generally low as all outcome measures were objective measures in 4 studies. Given the substantial heterogeneity across the six included studies, we conducted a descriptive qualitative subgroup synthesis to explore potential patterns based on administration route, disease acuity, and glutamine dose category. Table 4 summarizes the findings of this subgroup comparison. This descriptive subgroup synthesis reveals that positive effects were consistently observed only with parenteral administration in acute/surgical settings, while oral glutamine in stable chronic conditions showed minimal benefits. The present systematic review included six randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating the effects of glutamine supplementation on inflammatory and oxidative stress markers in diverse chronic conditions. The included studies demonstrated varying effects of glutamine supplementation across different patient populations. Engel et al. (2009) studied 51 cardiopulmonary bypass patients (mean age 70.2 years) receiving oral glutamine (0.5 g/kg/day for 3 days) and found no significant changes in CRP, IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, or IL-8 levels. In 66 type 2 diabetes patients (age 18–65 years), Mansour et al. (2015) reported no significant reduction in CRP levels after 6 weeks of oral glutamine (30 g/day). Similarly, Den Hond et al. (1999) observed no significant effect on CRP levels in 14 Crohn’s disease patients (mean age 31.6 years) following 4 weeks of oral glutamine (21 g/day). However, Dock-Nascimento et al. (2012) found a significant decrease in CRP levels in 24 laparoscopic cholecystectomy patients (mean age 41.5 years) after 1 day of oral glutamine (0.77 g/kg/day), though IL-6 and GSH levels remained unchanged. More pronounced anti-inflammatory effects were seen in surgical populations, as Lu et al. (2011) demonstrated reduced CRP and IL-6 levels in 50 postoperative gastrointestinal cancer patients receiving 7-day parenteral glutamine, despite no changes in TNF-α or IL-10 levels. The most comprehensive benefits occurred in the Huang et al. (2018) study of 123 colon cancer patients (age 32–69 years), where 7-day parenteral alanine-glutamine (100 ml/day) significantly decreased CRP, TNF-α, and (MDA levels while increasing superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity. (Table 2). In summary glutamine effects on inflammation showed mixed effects on CRP (reductions in surgical/cancer patients but not in T2DM or Crohn’s disease) and IL-6 decreased only in postoperative settings. As well as this effects on Oxidative Stress showed that parenteral glutamine consistently improved oxidative markers decrease MDA, increase SOD) in cancer patients, while oral routes had limited effects on GSH (Table 5).

Discussion

The present systematic review reveals that glutamine supplementation exerts context-dependent effects on inflammatory and oxidative stress markers in chronic diseases. While oral glutamine showed limited impact on CRP and cytokine levels in stable chronic conditions (e.g., diabetes, Crohn’s disease), more pronounced anti-inflammatory effect particularly reductions in CRP and IL-6 were observed in acute/surgical settings (e.g., postoperatively). Notably, parenteral administration demonstrated superior efficacy, not only attenuating inflammation (CRP, TNF-α) but also enhancing antioxidant defenses (increased SOD, decreased MDA) in cancer patients. These findings suggest that glutamine’s benefits may hinge on route of administration, disease acuity, and baseline oxidative stress severity, underscoring the need for tailored therapeutic approaches (Engel et al. 2009; Huang et al. 2018; Dock-Nascimento et al. 2012; Hond et al. 1999a; Lu et al. 2011; Mansour et al. 2015).

Across the six included RCTs, three key patterns emerged when critically analyzing the sources collectively: (1) study quality was generally acceptable (four low risk of bias), (2) positive effects were consistently observed only with parenteral administration in surgical/cancer populations, and (3) null effects predominated in oral supplementation studies for stable chronic conditions.

Although the following study did not meet our inclusion criteria (due to COVID-19 being an acute rather than chronic condition), it provides supportive mechanistic insight. In Mohajeri et al. study glutamine supplementation (10 g, three times daily for 5 days) in COVID-19 patients significantly reduced inflammatory markers, including IL-1β, TNF-α, and hs-CRP (p < 0.05), compared to controls. However, no significant changes were observed in oxidative stress indices (e.g., MDA and TAC). The findings suggest glutamine’s potential role in modulating inflammation during respiratory infections, possibly through immune regulation. These results align with broader evidence on glutamine’s anti-inflammatory effects in chronic diseases (Mohajeri et al. 2021b). In an animal studies, glutamine reduced levels of inflammation and oxidative stress in diabetic mice, indicating that glutamine may be effective in reducing complications of diabetes by improving macronutrients metabolism (Mahdavifard and Nakhjavani 2019; Liu et al. 2016). The randomized trial that conducted by Lu et al. investigated glutamine-enriched total parenteral nutrition (TPN) in 50 postoperative gastrointestinal cancer patients compared to standard TPN, glutamine supplementation significantly reducing inflammatory markers, including interleukin-6 (P = 0.01) and C-reactive protein (P = 0.013). Notably, the glutamine group had zero infectious complications versus four cases in controls (P = 0.037), though tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin-10 levels remained unchanged. These findings suggest glutamine-enriched TPN modulates postoperative inflammation and reduces infection risk in GI cancer patients, supporting its potential role in clinical nutrition protocols (Lu et al. 2011).

Of the two studies in our review that examined oxidative stress markers (Dock-Nascimento et al., 2012 and Huang et al. 2018), only Huang et al. reported significant improvements in antioxidant defenses. Consistent with this human finding, animal studies have reported similar patterns. In one animal study Szpetnar et al. demonstrated that glutamine supplementation partially improved SOD/GPx activity but failed to fully restore redox balance, this study suggests glutamine may modulate antioxidant defenses and neurotransmission (Szpetnar et al. 2016). Another animal study showed that oral supplementation with L-glutamine (either free or as L-alanyl-L-glutamine dipeptide) in endurance-trained rats enhanced the liver glutamine-glutathione (GSH) axis, increasing liver GSH by 90% and improving redox balance by reducing the GSSG/GSH ratio. Both supplement forms elevated plasma glutamine levels (~ 60%) and decreased ammonium concentrations, supporting glutamine's role in ammonia detoxification (Petry et al. 2015). Also in Lin et al. study showed that glutamine supplementation (1 g/kg/day) in high-fat diet-induced NAFLD rats could reduce hepatic oxidative stress (increased GSH, decreased MDA) and inflammation (lower TNF-α and NF-κB p65 expression) while improving liver histopathology compared to untreated NAFLD models (Lin et al. 2014). It appears that the effects of glutamine on oxidative stress vary depending on the type of disease and the dose used, and it is expected that the positive effects of glutamine will increase with increasing dose and in conditions where stress levels are high.

Although our review focused exclusively on chronic disease populations and therefore did not include the following study in healthy athletes, it provides supportive context. Nemati et al. reported that glutamine supplementation with doses of 0.3 g/kg of body weight per day for 14 days demonstrates promising anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, as evidenced by significant reductions in MDA and hs-CRP alongside increased TAC levels in healthy males following exhaustive exercise (Nemati et al. 2019). The observed elevation in glutathione levels further supports glutamine's role in enhancing endogenous antioxidant defenses, potentially through its involvement in glutathione synthesis. Interestingly, while glutamine effectively modulated key oxidative stress and inflammatory markers, it showed no significant impact on MMP-2 and MMP-9 levels, suggesting its effects may be pathway-specific (Nemati et al. 2019). These findings highlight glutamine's potential to mitigate oxidative stress and inflammation, which are hallmarks of various chronic diseases. However, the time-dependent nature of these biochemical responses and the study's focus on acute exercise in healthy individuals warrant further investigation in chronic disease populations to fully elucidate glutamine's therapeutic potential in conditions characterized by persistent inflammation and oxidative stress. The differential effects on various biomarkers underscore the need for targeted research to determine optimal dosing regimens and specific clinical applications in chronic disease management.

Among the six RCTs in our review, none specifically measured cancer-related fatigue as an outcome. However, supportive evidence from non-randomized studies can be informative. A cross-sectional study conducted by Schlemmer et al. investigated potential associations between plasma glutamine levels and cancer-related fatigue (CRF), nutritional status, and systemic inflammation in patients with solid tumors. The findings revealed that reduced glutamine concentrations were significantly correlated with elevated inflammatory markers (e.g., CRP), impaired physical function, and increased fatigue severity both physical and affective. The data support the hypothesis that tumor-induced systemic inflammation promotes a hypercatabolic state, leading to malnutrition and glutamine depletion, which may contribute to CRF (Schlemmer et al. 2015). These results suggest that optimizing glutamine availability could serve as a potential therapeutic strategy to ameliorate inflammation, fatigue, and metabolic deterioration in cancer patients (Schlemmer et al. 2015). Further research is warranted to explore causal mechanisms and clinical interventions. The two cancer-related RCTs in our review (Lu et al. 2011 and Huang et al. 2018) both demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects of parenteral glutamine. This clinical finding can be explained by basic science, as glutamine plays a dual role in cancer and immune cells, serving as a critical metabolic fuel. Cancer cells exhibit "glutamine addiction" by upregulating glutamine transporters (e.g., SLC1A5, SLC7A5) and metabolic enzymes, thereby depleting extracellular glutamine and impairing immune cell function. Glutamine catabolism in cancer cells generates pro-inflammatory metabolites (e.g., glutamate, α-KG), which activate inflammatory pathways (NF-κB, mTOR) and promote tumor-associated inflammation. Additionally, glutamine scarcity in the tumor microenvironment (TME) upregulates PD-L1 expression, facilitating immune evasion. Conversely, inhibiting glutamine metabolism in cancer cells (e.g., via GLS inhibitors like CB-839) restores glutamine availability for immune cells, enhancing anti-tumor immunity. Thus, targeting glutamine metabolism represents a promising strategy to modulate cancer-associated inflammation and improve immunotherapy efficacy (Wang et al. 2024). The glutamine ability to reduce oxidative stress and inflammation in chronic diseases appear promising, however results are too premature to incorporate in to clinical practice or to inform the development of health claims regarding glutamine supplementation.

Although only one study in our review (Mansour et al. 2015) specifically examined patients with type 2 diabetes in population with high cardiovascular risk and this study reported no significant reduction in CRP levels. Nevertheless, mechanistic studies suggest that glutamine supplementation may exert cardioprotective effects through multiple mechanisms. As a primary substrate for glutathione synthesis, glutamine enhances antioxidant defenses, reducing oxidative stress in cardiovascular tissues. It also modulates inflammatory responses by inhibiting NF-κB activation, thereby decreasing pro-inflammatory cytokine production (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) (Durante 2019). Glutamine improves endothelial function by upregulating nitric oxide (NO) production through eNOS activation, promoting vasodilation (Boelens et al. 2005). Additionally, it supports ATP production in cardiac myocytes during ischemia–reperfusion injury, maintaining cellular energy homeostasis. Glutamine's role in heat shock protein (HSP) induction helps protect cardiomyocytes from stress-induced damage (Zhang et al. 2009). Furthermore, it may reduce atherosclerotic plaque formation by decreasing endothelial adhesion molecule expression (e.g., VCAM-1, ICAM-1) (Durante 2019). These combined effects contribute to improved cardiovascular outcomes in chronic diseases. As well as glutamine supplementation can help mitigate oxidative stress by acting as a precursor to glutathione, a key antioxidant. Glutamine supplementation can also lead to decreased levels of MDA (a marker of lipid peroxidation) and increased levels of and glutathione. This interplay between glutamine, glutathione, and oxidative stress markers suggests that glutamine can play a role in protecting against oxidative damage (Amores-Sánchez and Medina 1999). According to reports of one experimental study glutamine reduces neutrophil activation and recruitment by modulating β2-integrin activity and chemotaxis, additionally, glutamine induces transcriptomic reprogramming, enhancing mitochondrial function and glutathione metabolism while suppressing ROS production and NET formation. Mechanistically, transglutaminase 2 acts as a downstream mediator of glutamine’s effects on neutrophils. The risk of bias assessment (Table 3) identified four studies with low overall risk, one with unclear risk, and one with moderate risk. The study with moderate risk (Dock-Nascimento et al. 2012) had unclear allocation concealment and potential selective reporting, which moderately reduces confidence in its positive findings. The study with unclear risk (Den Hond et al. 1999) had insufficient reporting of blinding procedures, limiting interpretability of its null results. The four low-risk studies provide the most reliable evidence, yet even among these, effect directions were inconsistent, further supporting our conclusion that current evidence remains hypothesis-generating.

The present study as a first comprehensive systematic review for assessing glutamine effects on inflammation and oxidative stress in chronic condition has several strengths include following Cochrane collaboration tool for to assess the risk of bias, employing rigorous screening and selection criteria to ensure only high-quality RCTs were included. As well as by analyzing diverse chronic diseases (e.g., diabetes, cancer, Crohn’s) and administration routes (oral/parenteral), the findings offer practical insights for tailoring glutamine therapy in different clinical contexts. The focus on both inflammatory and oxidative stress markers provides a holistic assessment of glutamine's effects.

An important limitation affecting interpretability of this synthesis is the substantial clinical heterogeneity across the six included trials. First, disease types varied considerably, ranging from stable chronic conditions (type 2 diabetes mellitus, Crohn's disease) to acute/surgical settings (postoperative cancer, cardiopulmonary bypass, cholecystectomy). Second, glutamine dosing regimens showed marked variation, from 0.5 g/kg/day for 3 days to 30 g/day for 6 weeks. Third, route of administration differed (oral vs. parenteral), with parenteral routes generally showing more pronounced effects. Fourth, baseline inflammatory status likely differed substantially across populations, as surgical patients typically exhibit higher perioperative inflammation than stable outpatients. Collectively, these sources of heterogeneity preclude direct comparison of effect sizes across studies and limit the strength of pooled conclusions.

The limited number of included trials (n = 6) reflects the current state of the literature on glutamine's effects on both inflammatory AND oxidative stress markers simultaneously in chronic diseases. Many existing RCTs examined either inflammation or oxidative stress, but not both, and were therefore excluded based on our PICOS criteria. Additionally, studies in acute critical illness (sepsis, trauma, burns) were excluded to avoid confounding by acute-phase responses that differ mechanistically from chronic disease pathophysiology. Thus, while our search identified over more than 2,000 records, only six met our rigorous dual-outcome inclusion criteria.

From a clinical perspective, the most actionable finding is that parenteral glutamine administration in surgical/acute cancer patients showed the most consistent benefits (reduced CRP, IL-6, and MDA), whereas oral glutamine in stable outpatients with diabetes or Crohn's disease produced minimal effects. This suggests that glutamine's efficacy may depend on the presence of an acute inflammatory stressor, rather than serving as an effective supplement for chronic low-grade inflammation.

The present study had several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, significant heterogeneity existed among included trials regarding glutamine dosage (ranging from 0.5 g/kg/day to 30 g/day), treatment duration (1 day to 6 weeks), and administration routes (oral vs. parenteral), making direct comparisons challenging and prevented a meta-analysis from being conducted in this study. Second, four of the six studies had relatively small sample sizes (n ≤ 50), potentially limiting their statistical power to detect subtle effects, particularly in chronic conditions like Crohn's disease. Third, the exclusive inclusion of English-language publications may have introduced language bias, while the lack of access to unpublished data could have resulted in publication bias favoring positive outcomes. Finally, variations in baseline patient characteristics and disease severity across studies may have influenced the observed treatment effects.

Conclusion

The current evidence on glutamine supplementation for modulating inflammation and oxidative stress in chronic diseases is preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Based on six heterogeneous RCTs (total N = 328), we observed context-dependent effects: parenteral glutamine in acute/surgical settings showed more consistent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant benefits, whereas oral glutamine in stable chronic conditions (diabetes, Crohn's disease) showed minimal impact. However, due to substantial clinical heterogeneity, small sample sizes in four of six studies, short intervention durations, and variability in dosing and administration routes, no definitive clinical recommendations can be made at this time.

Future research should prioritize adequately powered RCTs with standardized protocols, stratified by disease acuity and administration route, and should measure a core set of inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers to enable future meta-analyses. Clinicians should interpret existing findings cautiously and tailor any glutamine supplementation decisions to individual patient contexts, recognizing the current evidence base remains limited.
