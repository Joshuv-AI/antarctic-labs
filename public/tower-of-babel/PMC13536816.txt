Forensic dental age estimation in a Pakistani population: validation of Atlas methods and development of a population-specific reference model
Authors: Syed Abul Faraz, Imtiaz Ahmed, Syed Jaffar Abbas Zaidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate age estimation is essential in forensic and clinical contexts, particularly where birth records are unavailable. Dental development assessed through radiographic calcification is considered a reliable indicator. However, atlas-based methods have demonstrated variable accuracy across populations. This study aimed to evaluate the accuracy and agreement of the Schour and Massler chart and the London Atlas, and to develop a population-specific dental development scheme for a Pakistani cohort.MethodsA cross-sectional forensic validation study was conducted using 700 digital panoramic radiographs of individuals aged 4.0–17.9 years, stratified by age and sex. Dental age was estimated using both methods and compared with the chronological age. The analyses included paired t-tests, correlation, intraclass correlation coefficient, Bland–Altman analysis, and linear regression. The predictive performance was assessed using the mean absolute error and proportions within ± 1 and ± 2 years.ResultsBoth methods demonstrated strong correlations (r > 0.90) and high ICCs (> 0.85). The London Atlas showed lower mean bias (~ 0.2–0.4 years), narrower limits of agreement (± 1.5–2.0 years), and higher predictive accuracy (overall R² = 0.997; sex-stratified subgroup range: R² = 0.92–0.95) than the Schour and Massler method (overall R² = 0.982; sex-stratified subgroup range: R² = 0.85–0.90). Accuracy within ± 1 year was higher for the London Atlas (> 75%) compared with Schour and Massler (< 65%), while both exceeded 90% within ± 2 years. Population-specific analyses revealed a delay in early dental development.ConclusionsThe London Atlas demonstrated superior agreement and predictive performance; however, population-specific variations limit its universal applicability. The newly developed Pak Atlas provides a more accurate reference for forensic age estimation in the Pakistani population, with direct relevance to medico-legal age assessment.

Introduction

Age is a fundamental biological and social parameter that underpins legal identity, healthcare decision-making and societal functioning. The accurate determination of chronological age (CA) is essential for a wide range of applications, including civil registration, legal accountability, immigration processes, and access to education and employment [1–3]. In many low- and middle-income countries, civil registration and vital statistics systems remain incomplete, leaving large populations without reliable proof of CA [4–6]. Pakistan illustrates this challenge acutely: national survey data show that only 42% of children under five have their births registered, while coverage among infants under one year is approximately 35%, resulting in Pakistan having the largest number of unregistered children in South Asia [5, 7]. Consequently, many individuals lack verifiable documentation of their date of birth or possess records based solely on estimates [8]. In such contexts, alternative age estimation methods are essential and are routinely employed for forensic, medico-legal, and clinical decision-making, including skeletal, dental, radiological, and molecular approaches [9, 10].

Age estimation plays a pivotal role in forensic odontology, particularly in identifying unknown individuals, disaster victim identification, and legal cases involving age disputes [11, 12]. It is also increasingly relevant in the context of migration and asylum, where undocumented individuals require age verification for legal and humanitarian decision-making [13, 14]. In clinical practice, particularly in orthodontics and paediatric dentistry, an accurate assessment of biological maturity is essential for treatment planning, including the timing of interventions and evaluation of growth patterns [15–17].

Various biological indicators have been explored for age estimation, including somatic growth, skeletal maturation, and secondary sexual characteristics. However, these markers are highly influenced by environmental, nutritional, and socioeconomic factors, limiting their reliability [18, 19]. Although skeletal age assessment is widely used, it is subject to variability due to hormonal influences, malnutrition, and differences in ossification timing, often resulting in broader age ranges and reduced precision [20, 21].

In contrast, dental development, particularly tooth mineralisation, has been recognised as one of the most reliable indicators of CA [22, 23]. Tooth formation follows a continuous and genetically regulated process that is relatively resistant to environmental influences compared to other biological systems [24–26]. Studies have consistently demonstrated that dental age correlates more closely with CA than skeletal or somatic indicators, making it the preferred method for age estimation in both living and deceased individuals [27–29]. The resilience of dental tissues and their ability to withstand post-mortem changes further enhance their value in forensic contexts [30].

Over time, several methods have been developed to assess dental age, ranging from clinical observation of tooth eruption to radiographic evaluation of the stages of tooth formation. Early approaches based on tooth eruption were limited by their susceptibility to local and systemic factors, such as crowding, trauma, and disease, as well as their applicability to a restricted age range [22, 31, 32]. The advent of radiographic techniques, particularly dental panoramic radiography, has enabled visualization of internal tooth structures and provided a more reliable basis for assessing dental maturation through calcification stages [27, 33, 34].

Numerous atlas-based and scoring methods have since been proposed, including those by Schour and Massler [35], Wang et al. [26], and Moorrees et al. [32], each differing in the number and definition of developmental stages. While methods with fewer stages offer simplicity and higher reproducibility, they may lack precision, whereas more detailed staging systems improve accuracy but introduce observer variability [36, 37]. Among atlas-based approaches, the Schour and Massler chart has been widely used; however, it has been criticized for its limited sample size, unclear methodology, and gaps in age representation, particularly in adolescence [35, 38].

To address these limitations, AlQahtani et al. developed the London Atlas of Tooth Development, which incorporates a large sample size, uniform age distribution, and clear radiographic representation of tooth morphology [39]. This atlas has demonstrated improved accuracy compared to earlier methods; however, its applicability across diverse populations remains variable [40–42]. Studies have consistently reported differences in dental development across ethnic and geographic groups, emphasizing that methods validated in one population may not be directly transferable to another [43–45].

Similarly, population-specific atlases, such as the Turkish dental atlas by [46] and the WITS Atlas for South African populations by [47], have demonstrated improved accuracy within their respective populations, further reinforcing the importance of local standards. These studies highlight that both the timing and tempo of tooth development vary across populations, influenced by genetic and environmental factors, and that reliance on non-local references may lead to systematic errors in age estimation.

Despite the growing body of evidence supporting population-specific standards, comprehensive dental developmental schemes for the Pakistani population are still lacking. Existing studies in Pakistan have primarily focused on testing the applicability of international methods, such as Demirjian’s system, which has shown significant discrepancies when applied locally, including systematic overestimation or underestimation of age [48, 49]. This indicates a critical gap in the availability of validated, population-specific dental age estimation (DAE) tools.

Furthermore, many previous studies have relied predominantly on correlation-based analyses that do not adequately assess the agreement or clinical applicability. More robust analytical approaches that incorporate agreement measures, predictive accuracy, and individual-level variability are needed to ensure practical relevance in forensic and clinical settings are needed.

There is a clear lack of population-specific- dental development standards for the Pakistani population, and widely used DAE atlases, such as the Schour and Massler chart and the London Atlas, have only been minimally validated in local populations, raising concerns about their accuracy and applicability [50]. Previous studies have also made limited use of advanced statistical methods, particularly agreement analyses, prediction intervals, and measures of diagnostic accuracy, which are necessary for a rigorous evaluation of DAE techniques [48, 51, 52]. Moreover, no comprehensive radiographic, atlas-based- dental development scheme tailored to the Pakistani dentition is currently available.

Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the accuracy of existing DAE methods in forensic practice and to develop a population-specific dental atlas for the Pakistani population as a forensic reference. Specifically, this study sought to assess the accuracy and agreement of the Schour and Massler chart and the London Atlas in estimating dental age among Pakistani individuals and to compare the estimated dental age (EDA) with CA. This study also aimed to evaluate the predictive performance and clinical applicability of these methods using accuracy and prediction intervals. Furthermore, this study aimed to develop a population-specific- dental development scheme based on radiographic evaluation of tooth formation and eruption. Lastly, this study aimed to establish baseline dental developmental data for the Pakistani population to support future forensic, clinical, and anthropological applications.

Materials and methods

Study design

This forensic validation study of DAE methods was designed to evaluate and compare the performance of two established DAE methods, namely the Schour and Massler chart and the London Atlas of Tooth Development, against CA in a Pakistani population.

Study setting and population

This study was conducted at a tertiary care University. Digital dental panoramic radiographs of patients who attended the outpatient department (OPD) for diagnostic and treatment purposes were retrospectively evaluated. None of the panoramic radiographs were obtained specifically for research purposes; all images were retrospectively collected from routine clinical and diagnostic records of the patients.

Sampling strategy

This study employed a cross-sectional design with a non-probability convenience sampling approach, in which panoramic radiographs were collected from patients attending the OPDs for routine diagnosis and treatment planning.

A total of 700 panoramic radiographs were analysed of individuals aged 4–17.9 years. To enhance representativeness across developmental stages, the sample was stratified into predefined age categories, with efforts made to maintain approximately equal distribution across sexes within each age group. Therefore 14 age groups (category) from 4 years to 17.9 years, total of 700 subjects.

All panoramic radiographs included in the study belonged to unique individuals, as this was a cross-sectional study, and no longitudinal or repeated radiographs from the same participant at different ages were included. Eight permanent mandibular tooth positions were evaluated for each subject, resulting in 5,350 potential tooth observations across the complete dataset. This value represents the total number of tooth positions assessed, rather than implying that all teeth were developmentally visible or stageable in every age group. In younger individuals, particularly in the early age categories, third molars are frequently non-calcified or not radiographically visible because of their later developmental onset. Teeth with such conditions were recorded during the assessment and incorporated into the overall developmental analysis.

All radiographs were acquired by trained radiology staff following standardised institutional protocols, according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. During image acquisition, each participant was positioned in an upright standing posture with the chin placed on a designated support tray to ensure proper head alignment and reproducibility of the imaging conditions. Although a convenience sampling technique was utilised, consecutive eligible radiographs were included during the study period to reduce selection bias and improve the external validity of the findings.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated using PASS software (version 11) based on a two-sided paired z-test to detect differences between the CA and EDA. A minimum sample size of 572 subjects was required to achieve 95% statistical power, assuming a mean paired difference of 0.3 years and a standard deviation of paired differences of 1.7 years, at a significance level (α) of 0.05, as reported [3]. To ensure adequate representation across developmental stages and facilitate subgroup analyses, the sample was stratified by age and sex. A minimum of 50 subjects per age group were included, with an approximately equal distribution between men and women. The study population was divided into 14 age categories ranging from 4.0 to 17.9 years, resulting in a final sample size of 700 subjects, which exceeded the minimum required sample size and enhanced the precision and generalisability of our findings.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the relevant University’s Institutional Review Board.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria required that participants be between 4 and 17.9 years of age, have a high-quality- digital panoramic radiographs available, and possess a known and verified CA. The exclusion criteria were as follows: poor-quality- or distorted radiographs, developmental anomalies affecting dentition, and a history of systemic diseases influencing dental development. Radiographs with missing mandibular teeth that prevented accurate developmental assessment were excluded from the study.

The mandibular permanent dentition was selected because mandibular teeth generally demonstrate clearer radiographic visibility and reduced superimposition compared with maxillary teeth on panoramic radiographs, thereby improving staging reliability and minimizing observer-related variability. Furthermore, the use of permanent mandibular teeth is consistent with multiple previously published forensic dental age estimation studies utilizing radiographic developmental staging systems.

Primary teeth were not incorporated into the present atlas because of the increased biological variability associated with primary tooth exfoliation and eruption timing, which may be influenced by local factors such as premature extraction, retained deciduous teeth, crowding, trauma, and eruption disturbances. The study therefore focused on the more stable and reproducible developmental progression of permanent dentition across the selected age range.

Data collection and assessment procedure

Dental age was estimated by assessing the stage of tooth formation (calcification) of all developing permanent teeth in the right mandibular quadrant using the Schour and Massler chart [35] and the London Atlas of Tooth Development [39]. A bilateral symmetry assumption was applied, as previous studies have reported no statistically significant differences in tooth development between the right and left quadrants of the jaw [53]. When the right mandibular quadrant was unclear or difficult to interpret, the left mandibular quadrant was examined instead.

Each panoramic radiograph of a known CA was independently compared with the two atlases. The developmental stage that most closely resembled the radiographic appearance was selected, and the corresponding age category was assigned based on the results of each method. All observations were systematically recorded and entered into a structured data sheet using SPSS (version 21) for subsequent statistical analysis.

Examiner calibration and reliability testing

DAE was performed by assessing tooth formation and eruption of the permanent dentition using panoramic radiographs. All radiographs were evaluated using RadiAnt DICOM Viewer (version 3.2), which allows image manipulation features, including zooming, contrast adjustment, and negative display, thereby facilitating the detailed and accurate visualisation of dental structures.

The assessment was primarily conducted in the right mandibular quadrant. Each developing tooth, including both the crown and root, was evaluated according to the modified Moorrees’ stages of tooth formation (1963a, b), comprising 13 mineralisation stages [54]. Tooth eruption status was determined using the modified Bengston classification, which consists of four eruption stages [39, 55, 56].

The inclusion of subjects younger than 4 years was considered; however, this age group was excluded because of the limited availability of suitable radiographs in this age group. Only high-quality, non-distorted panoramic radiographs with clearly visible anatomical structures were included in the study.

The final sample consisted of 700 panoramic radiographs, with an approximately equal distribution of men and women. Each radiograph was assigned a unique serial number, and sex was coded as 1 for men and 2 for women. To ensure balanced representation, each age group included a minimum of 25 males and 25 females. The study population was categorised into 14 age groups at one-year intervals, represented by midpoint values (e.g. 4.5, 5.5, and 6.5 years), covering an age range of 4.0–17.9 years.

To ensure the consistency and reproducibility of the observations, two calibrated examiners independently assessed a subset of 10–15% of samples. Intra-examiner reliability was evaluated by re-assessing the same radiographs after a two-week- interval, whereas inter-examiner reliability was determined by comparing the independent evaluations of both examiners. The examiners were blinded to the CA of the participants during the dental age assessment to minimise observer bias. CA data were recorded separately and were not accessible to the examiners during radiographic evaluation and atlas matching procedures. Inter-examiner disagreements were resolved through joint re-evaluation and consensus discussions among the calibrated examiners. In cases requiring reassessment, the radiographs were independently reviewed again before a final agreement was reached. Reliability was quantified using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) for continuous variables and Cohen’s Kappa coefficient for categorical staging. An ICC or Kappa value of ≥ 0.80 was considered indicative of excellent agreement.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 21, and supplementary analyses were performed using R for advanced statistical modelling. A two-sided significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics were generated to summarise the datasets. Continuous variables, including CA and EDA, are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and, where appropriate, median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables, such as age group and sex, were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Validation of age estimation methods

Paired comparison analysis

A paired sample t-test was conducted to compare the CA with the EDA for the entire sample comprising all age groups combined, as well as separately for each age group. This method is commonly used in validation studies to assess systematic differences between two related measurements [57].

Definition of accuracy

An estimate was considered accurate when the dental age fell within the corresponding CA category, consistent with previously used categorical validation approaches in DAE studies [50, 58].

Accuracy and error metrics

To evaluate predictive performance, the mean difference (systematic bias), calculated as estimated age minus CA, mean absolute error (MAE), representing the average magnitude of error, and root mean square error (RMSE), reflecting overall prediction accuracy, were calculated. These measures are widely recommended for assessing the performance of age estimation models [59–62].

Correlation analysis

The association between CA and EDA was evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) for normally distributed data and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) for data that did not follow a normal distribution. Correlation analysis has been extensively used in DAE studies to assess the strength of the association between these variables [28, 44, 63].

Agreement and reliability analysis

The ICC was calculated using a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement to evaluate the agreement between CA and EDA between different methods. The ICC is regarded as a robust and reliable statistic for assessing measurement reliability in biological and clinical research [64, 65]. Furthermore, agreement was further examined using Bland–Altman analysis by calculating the mean difference and 95% limits of agreement, a method that is widely recommended for assessing the agreement between two quantitative measurement techniques [66, 67].

Regression and calibration analysis

To evaluate the predictive performance, linear regression analysis was performed with CA as the dependent variable and EDA as the independent variable. The coefficient of determination (R²) was used to quantify the proportion of variance in CA explained by the model, as regression-based approaches are commonly applied in age estimation studies to assess predictive accuracy [61, 68]. Furthermore, a calibration analysis was conducted to examine the agreement between the predicted and observed values across the age range [69].

Comparative analysis between methods

Differences between the Schour and Massler chart [35, 42, 50] and the London Atlas of Tooth Development [39] were assessed using paired sample t-tests and effect size estimation expressed as Cohen’s d. Such a comparative evaluation of DAE methods is essential because of the well-documented population-specific- variations in dental development [36, 42, 50].

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the performance of the methods across sex and age groups. Differences between males and females were assessed using independent sample t-tests, and variations across age groups were examined using an analysis of variance (ANOVA). Such subgroup analyses are necessary because population variability in dental development has been widely documented [53, 68].

Diagnostic accuracy

To assess clinical applicability, the proportion of dental age estimates falling within predefined error margins of ± 0.5, ± 1, and ± 2 years was calculated. These thresholds have been used in previous studies to evaluate the practical accuracy and usefulness of DAE methods in clinical and forensic settings [70]. The selected analytical approaches were chosen to reflect real-world forensic applicability, where individual-level accuracy and agreement are more critical than correlation.

Construction of population-specific dental scheme

Tooth evaluation

A total of 700 panoramic radiographs were analysed in this study. The radiographs used in this study were retrospectively collected over a three-year period, from 2018 to 2020. The sample was divided into 14 age groups ranging from 4 to 17 years (Table 1). The first five groups (4–8 years) included 250 dental panoramic radiographs, with 50 radiographs per group (250 × 7 teeth), while the remaining nine groups (9–17 years) included 450 radiographs, with 50 radiographs per group (450 × 8 teeth). Eight permanent mandibular teeth were assessed per subject, resulting in a total sample size of 5,350 teeth. Each tooth was evaluated using the modified Moorrees’ stages of tooth formation, comprising 13 developmental stages [32, 54, 71], along with the modified Bengston stages of tooth eruption, which include four stages [39]. Tooth formation staging systems, such as Moorrees’ classification, are widely regarded as the most reliable and reproducible methods for assessing dental maturity in clinical and forensic contexts [32].

Scoring system

Each developmental stage was assigned a numerical score to facilitate statistical analysis. This scoring approach allows for a quantitative comparison of developmental stages and has been adopted in multiple DAE methodologies [45, 72, 73].

Derivation of dental scheme

Descriptive statistical analysis was performed to determine the frequency distributions, median stages as representative values, and minimum and maximum stages of the dental development. The median stage for each tooth type within each age category category from 4.5 to 17.5 was used to construct the dental development scheme, in line with atlas-based methodological approaches [39]. Age groups were defined using midpoint values at one-year- intervals (e.g., 4.5, 5.5 years), which reflects the standard practice in dental developmental and maturity studies [46].

Graphical representation

A schematic dental development atlas was constructed based on the median stages, following methodologies similar to those of previously developed atlases, such as the London Atlas [39].

Data management and utilization

All developmental stages based on modified Moorrees’ and Bengston’s classifications were entered into SPSS (version 21). The median values and ranges were calculated for each tooth in all age categories.

These values were used to construct a population-specific dental development scheme, addressing the need for localised standards owing to documented inter-population variability in dental maturation [74, 75]. All data were anonymised and securely stored, following ethical research practices.

Results

A total of 700 panoramic radiographs were analysed, comprising an equal number of males (n = 350) and females (n = 350), with participants evenly distributed across 14 age groups ranging from 4.5 to 17.5 years (Table 1). The results are presented in two main sections: first, the validation of DAE methods, and second, the development of a population-specific- dental development scheme.

Validation of age estimation methods

When all age groups were analysed collectively, the Schour and Massler method showed a statistically significant difference from CA, with a mean difference of − 0.37 years (p = 0.001), indicating systematic underestimation. Sex-stratified subgroup analyses demonstrated comparable performance patterns in males and females for both methods, with no major clinically significant sex-related differences observed in the overall predictive accuracy. In contrast, the London Atlas demonstrated no statistically significant difference from CA, with a mean difference of − 0.06 years (p = 0.097). This finding suggests that the London Atlas exhibited better overall agreement with CA in the studied population.

Age group–wise analysis revealed distinct patterns for the two methods. For the Schour and Massler method, statistically significant differences were observed at 4.5 years (p = 0.012), 5.5 years (p < 0.001), 13.5 years (p < 0.001), and 17.5 years (p < 0.001). This method showed a tendency toward underestimation in both the early and late age groups, with increased variability in older adolescents. In comparison, the London Atlas showed statistically significant differences at 4.5 years (p = 0.007), 5.5 years (p < 0.001), and 8.5 years (p = 0.027), whereas no significant differences were observed in most age groups. The London Atlas demonstrated minimal bias across the mid-age categories, indicating a more consistent performance.

An analysis of the estimation bias trends across age ranges further highlights these differences. At early ages (4.5–5.5 years), both methods exhibited significant discrepancies, reflecting greater developmental variability during early childhood. During the middle age range (6.5–12.5 years), both methods showed improved agreement with CA and minimal differences. However, at later ages (13.5–17.5 years), the Schour and Massler method demonstrated increasing inaccuracy and variability. In contrast, the London Atlas maintained a relatively stable performance, highlighting its greater reliability in older age groups.

Construction of population-specific dental scheme

A total of 5,350 teeth, corresponding to eight permanent mandibular teeth from each of the 700 subjects, were evaluated using the modified Moorrees’ developmental stages [54, 56]. This value represents the total number of tooth positions assessed across the complete dataset, rather than implying that all teeth were developmentally visible in every age group. In the younger age categories, particularly before 8 years of age, third molars were frequently not radiographically visible because of their later developmental onset and were recorded accordingly. A clear chronological progression of dental maturation was observed across the studied age range.

During the early stages of development (4.5–6.5 years), crown completion predominated in the anterior teeth, whereas root initiation began in the incisors and first molars. In contrast, premolars and second molars were largely observed in the crown formation stage during this period.

In the mixed dentition stage (7.5–10.5 years), progressive root development was evident in both incisors and molars. The premolars showed a transition from crown completion to root initiation, and the second molars entered the active phases of development. Additionally, third molars began cusp formation at approximately 9.5 years of age. At the advanced developmental stage (11.5–13.5 years), most permanent teeth reached root completion (Rc) and apex closure (Ac). During this phase, the second molars were nearing completion, and the third molars progressed from crown formation to the early stages of root development.

In the late stage (14.5–17.5 years), complete maturation, characterised by apex closure, was observed in the incisors, canines, premolars, and first and second molars. At this stage, third molars remained the only teeth undergoing development, progressing from root initiation to advanced stages of root development, including half to three-quarters of the root formation (R½–R¾).

Tooth eruption pattern

The median eruption stages demonstrated a predictable and orderly sequence of dental eruptions across the age groups. Between 4.5 and 6.5 years of age, teeth were predominantly observed in the early eruption stages (stages 1–2). During the 7.5–10.5-year age range, active eruption was evident, with incisors and molars commonly reaching advanced eruption stages (Stages 3–4). From 11.5 years onwards, most permanent teeth fully erupted (Stage 4). In contrast, third molars show a delayed eruption pattern, with the initiation of eruption typically occurring after 10.5 years.

Comparison with London atlas (morphological agreement)

A comparison of the median developmental stages revealed good agreement during early childhood (4.5–6.5 years) and late adolescence (≥ 15.5 years). However, notable discrepancies were observed during the transitional phases between 8.5 and 13.5 years, particularly affecting the premolars, second molars, and third molars. These variations indicate population-specific differences in the timing and progression of dental maturation.

The key findings of this study indicate that the London Atlas demonstrated superior overall accuracy and consistency in estimating dental age compared with the Schour and Massler method. In contrast, the Schour and Massler method exhibited greater variability and reduced accuracy, particularly in older age groups. Although dental development generally follows a predictable chronological progression, variations are evident during transitional developmental stages, thereby supporting the need for population-specific- DAE standards.

Discussion

The present study was conducted to evaluate the accuracy of DAE methods and to construct a population-specific dental development scheme for a Pakistani cohort, with particular emphasis on variations in the timing and tempo of tooth formation and calcification (Table 1). The findings demonstrate that although both the Schour and Massler chart and the London Atlas exhibited strong correlations with CA, the London Atlas consistently demonstrated superior performance across multiple statistical parameters, including agreement, predictive accuracy, and clinical applicability (Table 2; Figs. 1 and 5).

Both methods revealed high correlation coefficients and excellent ICC values, indicating a strong linear association between CA and EDA; however, the correlation reflects an association rather than an agreement [58, 76]. This distinction was evident in the present study, where the London Atlas demonstrated minimal mean bias and narrower limits of agreement in the Bland–Altman analysis compared to the Schour and Massler method (Figs. 3 and 4). In contrast, the Schour and Massler method exhibited wider limits of agreement and increased variability, particularly in older age groups, indicating reduced reliability in late adolescence (Fig. 3). These findings are consistent with those of previous studies that highlighted the influence of population-specific variability on DAE methods [41, 71, 75].

A key contribution of this study is the development of the Pak Atlas of Tooth Formation and Eruption, which enabled a detailed comparison with established reference atlases, particularly the WITS Atlas (Fig. 7). This comparison revealed systematic differences in the timing of tooth development across multiple age groups, further supporting the need for population-specific standards (Fig. 7).

At 5.5 years, the reference population demonstrated delayed development of the mandibular central incisor, lateral incisor, canine, first and second premolars, and first molars compared with the WITS Atlas [47]. A similar trend persisted at 6.5 years, where the delayed formation of incisors and premolars was observed, while molars exhibited comparable developmental patterns. At 7.5 years, delayed development remained evident in the lateral incisors and canines.

By 8.5 years, the differences became more pronounced, particularly in the canines, second molars, and the third molars. Notably, while the WITS Atlas indicated early third molar initiation with a defined occlusal morphology, approximately 76% of the participants in the present study demonstrated no evidence of calcification at this stage. This finding highlights a significant delay in third molar development in the study population. At 9.5 years, delayed development persisted in the lateral incisor, second molar, and third molar, and similar trends were observed at 10.5 and 11.5 years, particularly affecting the canines, premolars, and third molars.

Interestingly, at 12.5 years, the second premolar appeared slightly advanced relative to the WITS Atlas, indicating that developmental variations were not uniformly delayed. At 13.5 years, the third molar in the WITS Atlas group remained slightly ahead of the reference population. However, from 14.5 years onwards, the developmental patterns of the studied population closely approximated those of the WITS Atlas, suggesting convergence in the later stages of dental maturation.

These findings collectively indicate that dental development in this population is characterised by early stage delays, followed by gradual alignment in late adolescence, particularly regarding third molar development. This observation aligns with the existing literature demonstrating that dental maturation is influenced by genetic, environmental, and ethnic factors [77–79] and supports previous reports of discrepancies when applying non-local atlases, such as the WITS or European standards [47].

The performance pattern observed in the present study, where the London Atlas demonstrated minimal mean bias and adequate accuracy across most age groups, with discrepancies concentrated in the early and late developmental phases, is broadly consistent with findings from multiple international validation studies. A strong body of evidence confirms that the London Atlas performs acceptably in diverse populations, though population-specific biases in the direction and magnitude of estimation error remain a recurrent theme warranting contextualisation. In the Iranian population, Ghafari et al. tested the London Atlas in 5–15.99-year-old children and reported that the atlas overestimated dental age, with the systematic bias being more pronounced in certain age subgroups; the authors concluded that the London Atlas nevertheless performed more accurately than the Smith method in that cohort [80]. In the Korean population, Cheong et al. evaluated 475 panoramic radiographs of children aged 4–15 years and found that the London Atlas produced a consistent overestimation across nearly all age groups, with the exception of the 15-year cohort. Despite this directional bias, the Londo atlas demonstrated superior consistency across age groups and no significant sex-related differences in estimation accuracy, leading the authors to conclude that it was applicable for forensic practice in Korea [81]. In the Saudi Arabian population, Bakhsh et al. examined 711 digital panoramic radiographs (ages 5–15 years) and demonstrated that the London Atlas produced mean differences of only 0.03 ± 0.34 years in males and 0.00 ± 0.34 years in females, with no statistically significant difference from chronological age in either sex. This study represents one of the most accurate performances of the London Atlas in a non-British population reported to date [82]. In the South African KwaZulu-Natal population, Ishwarkumar et al. applied the London Atlas to 760 panoramic radiographs aged 5.00–23.99 years and reported that the atlas overestimated age with a mean difference of − 0.85 to − 1.26 years, which was statistically significant, highlighting that the atlas may be less appropriate without local calibration in that population [83]. In the Thai population, Namwong and Manica tested the London Atlas on 111 digital panoramic radiographs of children aged 4.00–15.99 years and found that the atlas correlated with chronological age with a maximum discrepancy of 1.3 years. Notably, the atlas tended to underestimate age at 7, 8, and 9 years (− 0.5 years) in both sexes, while no significant effects of sex, age group, or their interaction were identified [84]. In the Malaysian population, Kuppusamy et al. evaluated 523 panoramic radiographs of children aged 4–16.99 years and found a mean absolute difference of 0.60 years, with no statistically significant difference between chronological and estimated dental ages (p = 0.240), and only minimal underestimation in both sexes. The authors concluded that the London Atlas was applicable in the Malaysian context [85]. In the Nepalese population, Dahal et al. tested the London Atlas in 350 digital panoramic radiographs of patients aged 4–24 years and reported acceptable overall agreement, with the atlas performing reasonably across most of the age range examined [86]. In the Eastern Turkish population, Koç et al. applied the London Atlas alongside Willems’ and Nolla’s methods to 919 panoramic radiographs of children aged 6–14 years and found a mean prediction error of 0.83 years for the London Atlas, placing it second in accuracy after the Willems method (mean error 0.80 years). The London Atlas showed overestimation in most age groups and demonstrated no statistically significant sex-related differences [87]. In the Australian population, Chua et al. retrospectively reviewed panoramic radiographs of 193 children aged 5–17 years and found that the London Atlas overestimated chronological age by a mean of 0.36 years overall; overestimation was statistically significant in the 6-, 7-, 8-, 10-, and 11-year age groups, with females tending toward overestimation and males toward underestimation [88]. In the South Indian (Kerala) population, Peedikayil et al. assessed 200 panoramic radiographs of healthy individuals aged 5–15 years and concluded that the London Atlas method yielded no statistically significant difference from chronological age. This study demonstrated acceptable accuracy in that South Indian cohort and supporting its clinical applicability [89]. In the Brazilian population, Dalben et al. applied the London Atlas Software App (2nd edition) to 1,104 digital panoramic radiographs of children aged 6–15.99 years and reported mean absolute errors of 0.56 years in females and 0.60 years in males (combined 0.58 years), confirming acceptable precision in the Brazilian population [90]. A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis by Jacometti et al., synthesising evidence across multiple populations globally, concluded that the London Atlas is an accurate age estimation method with acceptable error and bias values worldwide, though a slight non-significant tendency toward overestimation was noted in the pooled analysis [91]. These international comparisons support the present study’s findings. Overestimation has been documented in Korean, South African, Australian, and Turkish populations, while underestimation has been reported in Thai children and certain American sub-populations, indicating that bias is not uniform across groups. The minimal overall bias of -0.06 years and high accuracy observed in the present Pakistani cohort compares favourably with results from Saudi Arabia and Malaysia. It is also superior to the larger overestimations reported in South African and Australian samples. These cross-population comparisons further reinforce the necessity of population-specific calibration and contextualised interpretation in forensic dental age estimation.

Further analysis of tooth-specific trends revealed that third molars exhibited the greatest variability, particularly in the initiation and progression of calcification, consistent with prior studies identifying third molars as the most variable teeth in human dentition [43]. Transitional phases between 8.5 and 13.5 years also demonstrated notable inconsistencies in premolar and molar maturation, reinforcing the importance of evaluating age-specific developmental patterns rather than relying on generalised standards (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Bland–Altman analysis provided additional insights into the agreement between the methods. The London Atlas demonstrated a narrow distribution of differences with minimal systematic bias, indicating strong agreement and consistency across the age groups (Fig. 4). In contrast, the Schour and Massler method exhibited wider limits of agreement and greater dispersion, particularly in older individuals, reflecting reduced reliability (Fig. 3). These findings emphasise that agreement analysis is essential for validating age estimation methods, as reliance solely on correlation may overestimate accuracy [58].

Regression analysis further supported these findings, demonstrating strong linear relationships between CA and EDA for both methods. However, the London Atlas exhibited higher coefficients of determination (R²), indicating a better model fit and predictive accuracy (Figs. 5 and 6). Deviations from the regression line were more pronounced in the Schour and Massler method, particularly at higher ages, suggesting reduced predictive stability. It should be noted that the R² values presented in the figure legends (R² = 0.997 for the London Atlas; R² = 0.982 for the Schour and Massler method) represent the overall regression analyses performed on the full combined sample (n = 700). The lower R² ranges reported earlier (R² = 0.92–0.95 for the London Atlas; R² = 0.85–0.90 for the Schour and Massler method) were derived from sex-stratified subgroup analyses (males and females analysed separately), where the restricted variance within each subgroup predictably attenuates the coefficient of determination relative to the pooled analysis. Therefore, both sets of values are internally consistent and complementary; the overall R² reflects the global model fit, whereas the subgroup ranges reflect performance within more homogeneous analytical strata. Subgroup analyses further demonstrated that both males and females exhibited similar predictive performance trends, indicating that sex-related variations did not substantially influence the overall comparative findings of the study.

An important strength of this study is the incorporation of both CIs and PIs into the regression analysis. The CIs reflect the precision of the estimated regression line and indicate the accuracy of the model in predicting mean dental age. In this study, the London Atlas demonstrated narrower CIs (Fig. 6), indicating a greater model precision. In contrast, the Schour and Massler method showed wider intervals, suggesting reduced stability compared to the other methods (Fig. 5).

Prediction intervals yield critical insights into individual-level variability, which is central to both clinical and forensic decision-making. As illustrated in Fig. 6, the London Atlas demonstrated narrower prediction intervals, indicating greater reliability when applied to individual cases. In contrast, the Schour and Massler method exhibited wider prediction intervals, reflecting an increased uncertainty. This distinction is crucial because CIs reflect model accuracy, whereas prediction intervals reflect real-world applicability. Therefore, despite similar correlations, the London Atlas demonstrated superior clinical utility because of its narrower prediction intervals and consistent performance.

The diagnostic accuracy analysis further supported these findings, with the London Atlas demonstrating higher accuracy within ± 1 year than the Schour and Massler method, while both methods performed adequately within ± 2 years (Table 2). These results are consistent with previous studies reporting improved performance of the London Atlas in diverse populations, while emphasising the need for regional calibration [39, 68].

The Pak Atlas developed in this study represents a valuable population-specific tool for DAE (Fig. 7). Based on panoramic radiographs and standardised staging systems, it provides a practical and noninvasive approach suitable for both clinical and forensic applications (Fig. 7). However, atlas-based methods inherently rely on categorical staging and may be less precise than quantitative approaches that provide continuous outputs [46, 78]. Despite this limitation, their accessibility, cost-effectiveness, and applicability in mass screening and forensic contexts are significant advantages.

From an international forensic perspective, these findings are relevant in the context of increasing migration and medico-legal age assessment procedures involving South Asian populations, including Pakistani migrants and asylum seekers in Europe. Recent literature has emphasised the importance of population-specific validation because the application of non-local reference standards may introduce systematic errors in forensic age estimation across ethnically diverse groups [92]. In this context, the present study supports the need for local dental developmental references. This strengthens the broader forensic applicability of the Pak Atlas across regional and international age-assessment settings.

This study has certain limitations. The use of a single-centre sample and convenience sampling may limit generalisability, and the absence of external validation restricts its broader applicability. Additionally, observer-dependent interpretation of developmental stages may introduce variability. Another limitation is the exclusive use of mandibular permanent dentition, selected for its clearer radiographic visibility and reduced superimposition. Primary teeth were excluded due to greater variability in eruption and exfoliation influenced by local factors. The inclusion of maxillary dentition and primary tooth developmental/exfoliation patterns may provide additional developmental information, particularly in younger age groups. Future studies may expand the Pak Atlas by incorporating complete dentition analysis, including maxillary and primary teeth, as well as external multicenter validation across broader population subsets.

Although both methods demonstrated strong statistical associations with CA, the London Atlas provided superior agreement, predictive accuracy, and clinical reliability. The integration of advanced statistical analyses, including Bland–Altman plots and the distinction between CI and PI, strengthens the robustness of our findings. Importantly, the Pak Atlas highlights population-specific variations in dental development, emphasising the necessity of localised standards for accurate age estimation in clinical and forensic practice.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that population-specific variations significantly influence DAE methods by altering the timing and sequences of tooth development. Although both the Schour and Massler chart and the London Atlas exhibited strong correlations with CA, the London Atlas consistently provided superior agreement, precision, and individual-level reliability, as evidenced by narrower limits of agreement, higher predictive accuracy, and reduced variability across the age groups.

The findings further establish that dental development in the studied Pakistani population is characterized by delayed maturation in the early and transitional stages, particularly involving incisors, canines, premolars, and third molars, followed by convergence with established atlas standards in late adolescence. These systematic differences highlight the limitations of applying non-local reference atlases without calibration.

The development of the Pak Atlas of Tooth Formation and Eruption represents a key contribution of this study, providing a population-specific radiograph-based framework that captures developmental patterns with greater contextual accuracy. This atlas provides a practical and clinically applicable tool for age estimation in Pakistani children and adolescents, particularly in forensic, legal, and clinical settings, where precision at the individual level is essential.

Importantly, the integration of advanced statistical approaches, including Bland–Altman analysis and the combined interpretation of CI and PI, demonstrates that method selection should be guided not only by correlation but also by agreement and the real-world predictive performance.

This study demonstrates that population-specific variations significantly affect the accuracy of DAE in forensic practice. Although the London Atlas showed superior performance among existing methods, the newly developed Pak Atlas provides a more accurate and contextually appropriate reference for the Pakistani population. These findings signify the necessity of localized validation to improve evidential reliability in forensic age estimation.
