Electrochemical selective oxygen transfer enables energy-efficient environmental deoxygenation
Authors: Deming Li, Yi Tan, Xin Tang, Changming Zhao, Peng Li, Yilin Zhang, Guiqiang Li, Yu Zhang, Yuen Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deep deoxygenation, defined as oxygen level ≤ 1%, is increasingly pursued to suppress oxidation-driven spoilage and side reactions in applications such as food preservation, yet industrial practice still relies on nitrogen purging enabled by pressure swing adsorption. Because oxygen removal by dilution and displacement depends on maintaining a nitrogen–oxygen concentration gradient, this approach requires substantial nitrogen throughput and high power input. This burden is especially pronounced in cold chain logistics, where refrigeration already dominates operating cost and emissions. Here we report an electrochemical environmental deoxygenation system that selectively transports oxygen from the controlled environment to ambient air through coupled oxygen reduction and oxygen evolution reactions. This pathway reduces entropy generation and operates closer to the thermodynamic minimum than pressure swing adsorption. When integrated with low-oxygen, moderate-temperature preservation, the system reduces total energy use by ~ 36% versus conventional low-temperature operation while maintaining or improving preservation quality, supporting scalable, energy-efficient oxygen management.

Introduction

Deep oxygen removal is a routine yet energy-intensive requirement in applications ranging from food and pharmaceutical storage to oxidation-sensitive manufacturing, where residual O2 accelerates spoilage or unwanted reactions. High energy consumption and low efficiency represent inherent technical bottlenecks in conventional environmental deoxygenation, impeding sustainable transitions in industries demanding deep oxygen (O2) removal, such as steelmaking, chemicals, and food/pharmaceutical preservation1–4. Within these sectors, cold chain logistics—a core technology for preserving high-value perishables (a $200B global market with ~ 8% annual growth)—exemplifies a critical challenge: its conventional model relies excessively on energy-intensive Low-Temperature (LT) storage systems (Temperature ≤ 5 °C)5–7. These LT systems reportedly consume ~ 15% of global fossil fuels and account for ~ 2.5% of total CO2 emissions, placing them in conflict with global carbon neutrality objectives (Fig. 1a)8,9.Fig. 1Schematic diagrams of cold-chain preservation strategies.a Low-temperature preservation. b Electrochemical environmental deoxygenation-enabled low-oxygen, moderate-temperature preservation.

The Low-Oxygen Mid-Temperature (LOMT) preservation strategy presents a solution to this dilemma (Fig. 1b). This strategy leverages low-O2 atmospheres to effectively suppress microbial growth and metabolic activity in perishable goods10–12. Experimental evidence indicates that this approach can maintain or even enhance preservation quality compared to LT methods while reducing reliance on Low-Temperature refrigeration, offering potential for energy savings13–15. However, the large scale implementation of LOMT technology faces a central challenge: the energy-intensive nature of mainstream environmental deoxygenation technologies offsets the inherent energy-saving advantages of LOMT systems. Consequently, overcoming this critical bottleneck in deoxygenation efficiency is imperative to fully realize LOMT’s transformative potential in sustainable industrial practices.

Conventional industrial deoxygenation commonly relies on nitrogen purging supplied by onsite air separation units, often based on pressure swing adsorption (PSA)16. Because purging reduces O2 by dilution/displacement and therefore requires large N2 throughput17, the overall energy demand is constrained by the minimum work of air separation and is further increased in practice by compression and PSA regeneration losses18,19. Sustaining an O2–N2 gradient demands continuous injection of high-purity N2, while spontaneous mixing drives entropy generation (Sgen) and irreversible energy losses, making deep deoxygenation (O2 ≤ 1%) both energy‑intensive and costly20. In contrast, ideal separation would selectively target O2, demanding only the minimum separation work (Wmin) associated with the chemical potential difference, devoid of any irreversible processes. The N2 purging approach, based on macroscopic displacement rather than molecular selectivity, deviates from this ideal path. Consequently, PSA system exhibits high energy consumption, large irreversibility, and inefficient deep deoxygenation, rendering it inadequate for demanding applications like LOMT and highlighting the urgent need for efficient environment deoxygenation strategies.

Overcoming the intrinsic thermodynamic bottlenecks of conventional deoxygenation necessitates establishing the unidirectional O2 transport, key to resembling the theoretical optimal route. Electrochemical technology may present a promising pathway. In 1964, Langer et al. first proposed an electrochemical system utilizing coupled oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) and oxygen evolution reaction (OER) for O2 enrichment from air21. However, subsequent research mainly focused on high-purity O2 generation by this system22–24. While recent studies, including our team’s development of a multifunctional, bidirectional electrochemical oxygen conditioner for precise fermentation25, have begun to explore more flexible control of environmental oxygen concentrations. These studies have focused on multifunctional system design and flexibility in process management. Consequently, investigating the intrinsic energy-saving advantages of electrochemical systems in deep environmental deoxygenation and systematically investigating their underlying mechanisms, application potential, and the possibility of approaching the thermodynamic limit, remains a relatively unexplored area. This long standing research gap has impeded the development of electrocatalytic deoxygenation, leaving the energy and efficiency limitations of conventional methods fundamentally unaddressed.

This study presents systematic thermodynamic analysis validating the inherent energy efficiency superiority of electrochemical environmental deoxygenation (EED) system utilizing coupled ORR/OER. Thermodynamic modeling reveals that EED enforces a unidirectional O2 flux through ORR/OER-coupling, avoiding the dominant entropy generation from irreversible macroscopic gas mixing. EED fundamentally follows Faraday’s law in its oxygen-removal mechanism, whereby the electron requirement per mole of O2 is stoichiometrically fixed. As this Faradaic contribution dominates the overall energy consumption, the EED process exhibits an approximately linear scaling with the amount of O2 removed in practical operation. This prevents exponential energy escalation at high deoxygenation depths, enabling the separation pathway to closely approach the Wmin. A high-performance EED stack quantified the system’s thermodynamic advantage, yielding an energy deviation factor (i = Winput/Wmin ≈ 63–89), indicating a clear improvement in proximity to the Wmin relative to PSA systems (i ≈ 1012–2389). Furthermore, EED demonstrates performance improvements over conventional PSA, with improvements of 10 times in deoxygenation precision, 362 times in system compactness, and 38 times in energy efficiency. An application case in cold chain logistics demonstrates the EED’s economic and environmental potential: an EED-based LOMT strategy achieved 36% energy savings over conventional LT method with equivalent preservation quality. Techno-economic analysis indicates the widespread adoption of EED-LOMT could cut industry operational costs by 20% and CO2 emissions by 23%. This research establishes EED as an efficient industrial deoxygenation strategy, offering a promising pathway for energy conservation and decarbonization.

From a broader thermodynamic perspective, restricting deoxygenation processes to the direct transfer of target species (O2) avoids the significant mixing entropy generation associated with large-scale dilution, thereby driving the system closer to its thermodynamic optimum. Building upon this thermodynamic insight, we explore the fundamental thermodynamic advantages of utilizing a coupled ORR–OER in applications for efficient environmental deoxygenation. Specifically, the EED employs spatially decoupled ORR/OER half-reactions to realize electrically driven O2 directional transport (total reaction: O2 (air) → O2). In this design, the cathode is directly exposed to the O2-removal environment and the anode vents to ambient conditions, with the electrodes separated by a selective ion-exchange membrane that enables ion transport while blocking O2 gas crossover to prevent detrimental gas-phase back-mixing. By enforcing a “gas → ion → gas” oxygen conversion for O2, they are chemically locked into the reaction chain (Fig. 2, right): via ORR capture at the cathode (e.g., O2 + 2H2O + 4e− → 4OH–) and subsequent release at the anode by OER reassembly (e.g., 4OH– → O2 + 2H2O + 4e−). The system confines O2 ingress and egress to a single axis governed by the electrochemical potential field. This design establishes net unidirectional flow at the system level, enabling precise control over mass transfer without relying on stochastic diffusion.

Following the marked reduction in system entropy generation achieved via the 2ET pathway, further optimizations are required to address additional entropy accumulation arising from Rohm and Rmt. Adopting a zero-gap membrane electrode assembly (MEA) configuration (Supplementary Fig. S19) may lower interfacial contact resistance by tightly integrating the catalyst layers and membrane/electrolyte, ensuring minimal ion transport distances and significantly reducing ohmic drop30–32. To translate this design rationale into device level performance, we assembled a 4 cm2 single cell EED as a verification platform, which delivered 455 mA cm−2 at 1.2 V and sustained operation with only 2.5% decay over 350 h using O-CNTs-8h (Supplementary Figs. S20–22). To contextualize these metrics, Supplementary Table S9 benchmarks representative ORR/OER oxygen-transport and 2e−-ORR systems, contextualizing the current density, voltage, selectivity, and durability. For the concentration polarization, O2 mass transfer remains the key bottleneck (Fig. S23), shaped by two factors: (1) the electrolyte’s low O2 solubility, mitigated by introducing a gas diffusion electrode (GDE) with a microporous, hydrophobic design that enhances O2 transport and prevents flooding; and (2) the gradual depletion of ambient O2 during the removal process. In the EED, the cathodic flow field structure directly influences O2 transport efficiency33,34. To probe how cathode-side gas distribution and removal of trace liquid water influence O2 transport in the gas-fed MEA, we compared straight, serpentine, and interdigitated cathode flow fields (Supplementary Fig. S24)35.

As illustrated in Fig. 3b, the EED with three distinct flow field configurations exhibited working currents under varying cathode O2 concentrations (air, 8% O2, and 3% O2). All data in Fig. 3b were collected using a 40 cm2 active-area single cell, and the current density conversion is provided in Supplementary Fig. S25. Across all inlet compositions, the interdigitated design delivered the highest operating currents (17.093 A, 9.551 A, and 5.085 A under air, 8% O2, and 3% O2, respectively). The gap widened under O2-deficient feeds: at 3% O2, the interdigitated configuration operated 20.0 and 45.5% higher in current than the serpentine and straight designs, respectively. Furthermore, during constant-voltage stability testing at 1.2 V (Supplementary Fig. S26), the interdigitated flow field sustained the highest current density and stable performance, whereas the straight flow field design showed a 10.4% current drop and instability, consistent with less effective management of trace cathode-side liquid water under gas-fed operation (no liquid catholyte). This liquid water is primarily associated with membrane water transport/hydration needed to sustain alkaline 2e−-ORR near the membrane-catalyst interface; localized accumulation can intermittently impede gas transport in the channels/GDE. We further analyze the coupling between flow field pressure drop and through-plane convection using computational fluid dynamics (Figs. 3e–g, S34, and S35). To validate the practical O2 removal performance of each flow field, the three single-cell setups with different cathode flow channel of EED connected with a 5 L chamber were tested (Fig. 3c). The interdigitated flow field also showed the fastest O2 removal rate, reducing the chamber O2 concentration from 21 to 1% in only 1160 s, compared to 1400 and 1510 s for the serpentine and straight flow fields. Crucially, for applications requiring continuous airflow with adjustable O2 levels, the EED can achieve real-time precise adjustment of outlet O2 concentration by controlling the voltage and gas flow rate. Tests under different operating conditions (Supplementary Figs. S27–29) show that the interdigitated flow field consistently achieves the lowest outlet O2 concentration, proving that the interdigitated flow field has the highest O2 removal efficiency in low-O2 environments.

To identify the origin of the flow-field-dependent performance and stability differences, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was employed to further investigate the key factors influencing O2 removal performance in different flow field structures36–38. Supplementary Fig. S30 presents Nyquist plots under a bias of 0.6 V, demonstrating that the interdigitated flow field exhibits the lowest impedance. Then, data fitting was used to obtain Rohm, Rct, and Rmt (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Table S2)39. Rohm and Rct remain basically the same among the three flow fields, which is mainly due to their dependence on the electrolyte, membrane-electrode characteristics, and reaction activation energy, with minimal impact from the flow field structure. In contrast, Rmt reflects the ease of transfer for reactants and products at the electrode interface. The interdigitated flow field exhibits the lowest Rmt (0.11054 Ω), attributed to the stronger cross-flow-induced convection, which enhances reactant replenishment and reduces local transport limitations for O2 near the electrode surface. By comparison, the higher Rmt in straight and serpentine flow fields arise from their relatively linear flow paths, which induce localized O2 depletion zones and thereby compromise the homogeneity of O2 distribution.

Guided by the EIS-diagnosed mass transport limitation, we next combined gas-flow-dependent measurements with CFD analysis to visualize the O2 and pressure drop distributions and to rationalize the interdigitated design. At cathode gas flow rates from 0.1 to 0.5 L min−1, the interdigitated flow field consistently outperformed the serpentine and straight flow fields electrochemically (Supplementary Fig. S32). Measured outlet O2 concentrations also agree well with CFD predictions across all tested flow rates, and both indicate that the interdigitated flow field maintains lower outlet O2 concentrations, underscoring its superior overall O2 removal efficiency (Supplementary Fig. S33). The CFD contour plots provide more detailed insights into the O2 concentration and pressure drop distribution (Figs. 3e–g and S35). The straight and serpentine flow fields show marked unevenness in O2 concentration among their parallel channels, whereas the interdigitated flow field displays a more uniform O2 gradient. This improvement is attributed to the under-rib architecture of the interdigitated design, which drives cross-flow through the porous electrode and strengthens through-plane convective transport (Fig. 3g)40,41. The analysis of the uniformity index (UI) for O2 concentration distribution also confirms that the interdigitated flow field exhibits a more uniform gas distribution (0.880 at a flow rate of 0.5 L min−1) (Supplementary Fig. S34). As for pressure drop simulation results, the interdigitated flow field exhibits a higher pressure drop with a more uniform spatial distribution across the electrode42. The higher pressure drop serves as the driving force for under-rib convection, thereby accounting for the improved reactant replenishment, water removal, and overall mass-transport performance of the interdigitated flow field. This pressure-gradient-driven flow enhances through-plane (under-rib) convection and improves reactant replenishment within the catalyst layer42. Additionally, the larger pressure gradient promotes convective drainage and facilitates removal of retained cathode-side liquid water associated with membrane water transport/hydration (and occasional condensation/carryover), thereby lowering local liquid saturation in the pore network42,43. This is important because electrowetting under applied potentials can reduce the apparent contact angle and promote liquid-water intrusion into gas-filled pores, which can otherwise evolve into pore flooding/blockage44–46. At the tested cathode flow-rate setpoint (0.5 L min−1), the interdigitated plate is associated with a higher gas-side pressure drop (≈ 355 Pa) than the serpentine (≈ 70 Pa) and straight (≈ 1.4 Pa) designs (Fig. 3e–g). Within the tested flow rate setpoint and considering only pump electrical power, the corresponding pump electrical power spans 0.45–0.60 W across configurations under identical flow control in our setup, suggesting that pumping does not dominate the electrical power comparison within the operating window studied here. In summary, the key advantage of the interdigitated flow field is its facilitation of multidirectional infiltration, which promotes homogeneous O2 distribution throughout the catalyst layer and effectively reduces the mass-transport-induced irreversible losses in the EED system.

Assembly and testing of EED Stack

To further enhance O2 removal efficiency in storage environments, we assembled a compact stack comprising six 40 cm2 MEA single cells in a 0.2 M potassium carbonate electrolyte (Fig. 4a), to meet the rapid and safe O2 removal requirements of cold chain logistics. To align with application scenarios, all electrochemical measurements were conducted at 10 °C. This stack offers scalability, as the number of single cells can be adjusted to accommodate O2 removal needs of various scales. To verify the inherent thermodynamic advantages of macro-EED, we compared it with a conventional PSA-based N2 purging system, first examining each method’s precision in controlling O2 concentration. The two methods adopt fundamentally different control logics: PSA is typically operated in a sensor-feedback mode, stopping N2 supply and sealing the space once the sensor readout reaches the preset O2 concentration; whereas EED applies Faraday’s law to establish a stoichiometric correspondence between the target cumulative O2 removal amount and the required electron flux, under the specified operating protocol, and automatically shuts off once the transferred charge reaches the setpoint (chronocoulometry). We conducted experiments at preset O2 concentrations of 15, 10, and 5%, and measured the actual values by gas chromatography (GC) upon systems reaching steady states. The results (Fig. 4b) reveal that PSA-based control (O2 sensor response-dependent) yields noticeable deviations (deviation ratio of 4.5–7.5%), while EED-based control (with the O2 removal amount calculated from the transferred charge) achieves good agreement with the preset concentrations (deviation ratio of 0.4–0.7%). This discrepancy arises because the PSA process fundamentally relies on gradient-driven dilution; once the N2 flow injection ceases, spontaneous mixing persists among regions of differing O2 levels, leading to steady-state concentrations that always deviate from the setpoints (Fig. 4c, left). In contrast, EED (Fig. 4c, right) directly “locks in” the stoichiometric quantity of O2 to be removed, thereby rendering the final O2 concentration insensitive to local concentration gradients. These findings strongly support our central premise: the EED’s mechanism inherently bypasses the large-scale gas-phase mixing, simplifying O2 regulation and eliminating sensor-accuracy constraints, response delays, and mixing-diffusion errors. Additionally, EED exhibits a rapid startup capability: whereas PSA requires periodic adsorption–desorption cycles to generate N2, incurring inevitable startup delays, EED—driven by electrochemical reactions—achieves nearly instantaneous O2 removal response, offering flexibility for applications demanding rapid O2 level control (Supplementary Fig. S36). More importantly, EED is not constrained by the N2 purity requirements of conventional gas-purification processes, enabling easy attainment of ultra-low O2 concentrations at the ppm level (minimum to 20 ppm, compared to the PSA O2 removal limit of 1770 ppm; Supplementary Fig. S37 and Table S5). We further conducted practical O2 removal experiments to compare the actual energy consumption of PSA equipment and EED stacks. Notably, the EED stack achieves better O2 removal ability than conventional PSA N2 generators while occupying merely 1.6% of the equivalent PSA equipment volume (insets in Fig. 4d). In a 10 L sealed chamber, reducing O2 concentration from 21 to 1% required 307.9 Wh over 41.2 min for PSA, while EED achieved this with only 8.2 Wh within 6.9 min (including all gas-liquid circulation systems)—corresponding to a 97.3% reduction in energy consumption and an 83.3% reduction in processing time (Figs. 4d and S38). By defining the deviation multiple from the minimum separation work (i = Winput/Wmin), Fig. 4e reveals that EED achieves i values between 63 and 89, indicating the mechanism design and the subsequent optimizations for the EED in present work has brought its actual energy consumption quite near the theoretical limit. By contrast, PSA’s i ranges from 1012 to 2389, one to two orders of magnitude higher. To scientifically evaluate the technological applicability in space-constrained and energy-sensitive scenarios, this study conducted an O2 removal performance comparison based on two key metrics: (1) O2 removal capacity per unit equipment volume (L min−1 m−3), reflecting equipment compactness; and (2) O2 removal capacity per unit energy consumption (L kWh−1), characterizing energy conversion efficiency. Results show that EED achieves 481.9 L min−1 m−3 and 1219.5 L kWh−1, respectively, surpassing that of PSA by 362 times and 38 times (Fig. 4f). The above results collectively demonstrate the absolute thermodynamic advantages of EED’s mechanism from both energy consumption and efficiency perspectives.

Finally, we conducted two experiments to systematically validate EED’s engineering reliability. First, in a 10 L sealed chamber, we performed 30 consecutive O2 removal cycles (Fig. 4g, h), revealing that the initial current and removal time remained nearly constant in all cycles, thus demonstrating the system’s repeatability. Second, under a constant voltage of 7.2 V with air as the feed gas, EED operated continuously for 500 h with less than a 3% drop in current (Supplementary Fig. S39), further confirming its long-term stability. In summary, as shown in Table S5, EED markedly surpasses conventional PSA in terms of O2 removal efficiency, energy consumption, equipment volume, control precision, and response speed, underscoring its potential and relevance in low-O2 industrial applications.

The quantitative analysis demonstrates that widespread adoption of EED-LOMT preservation technology in cold chain logistics not only reduces operational costs and energy consumption but also plays a pivotal role in curbing carbon emissions. Furthermore, its inherent economic competitiveness enhances the market viability of electric refrigerated vehicles, accelerating their commercial deployment. Maintenance costs for EED include periodic replacement of stack components (e.g., the anion-exchange membrane), as detailed in the Supplementary Information.

Discussion

This study establishes a thermodynamic and mechanistic framework for low-O2 environmental regulation using EED, offering an alternative to N2 purging for deep O2 removal. Through reaction-pathway and mass-transport optimizations, the EED system operates closer to the thermodynamic benchmark and reduces practical power demand under the tested conditions. In a cold chain logistics demonstration, the EED-based LOMT strategy reduces total energy consumption by 36% compared to conventional LT preservation while maintaining equivalent or superior freshness performance. Under the scenarios analyzed here, deployment at scale is estimated to yield ~ USD 1.08 billion in annual operating savings (≈ 20%) and ~ 17.6 million tons of CO2 mitigation per year (≈ 23%); in the adoption model, these benefits correspond to an ~ 4.4 year earlier uptake of electric refrigerated vehicles at 60% market penetration. Beyond cold chain logistics, the same thermodynamic framing and electrochemical oxygen transport architecture may be applicable to other oxygen-sensitive processes requiring precise O2 control (e.g., semiconductor manufacturing and metallurgy), subject to application-specific integration and validation.

Methods

Chemicals

Ethanol (C2H6O), isopropanol (C3H8O), potassium hydroxide (KOH), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), formamide (CH3ON), Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, Folin phenol, metaphosphoric acid (HPO3), disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetate (EDTA-Na2) were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagents, China. Nafion solution (5 wt%) was purchased from Aldrich. Iridium oxide (99.9%) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. All reagents were of analytical grade and used as received unless otherwise stated. Deionized water produced by a Milli-Q system (Millipore, Billerica, MA) was used throughout the experiments.

Synthesis of oxidized carbon nanotubes (O-CNTs)

For a typical synthesis, 500 mg of carbon nanotubes are packed into a glass chromatography column. An ozone generator is used to continuously pump ozone into it at a flow rate of 1 L min−1, with an ozone concentration of 60 mg L−1.The ozone used in this study was generated by a commercial ozone generator (model DMN-3KS10, Anhui Tianchang Zhongrui Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., China), which produces ozone via a corona-discharge process using pure oxygen as the feed gas. The input is pre-humidified by bubbling through deionized water. The oxygen content of the O-CNTs is controlled by the duration of ozone exposure, resulting in a series of O-CNTs with varying degrees of oxidation.

Synthesis of iron-nickel layered double hydroxides (FeNi-LDH)

For a typical synthesis, 2.181 g of Ni(NO3)2·6H2O and 1.010 g of Fe(NO3)3·9H2O are dissolved in 20 mL of water to form a mixed salt solution. 40 mL of 23 vol% formamide aqueous solution is heated to 80 °C in a three-necked flask, and 2.5 M NaOH solution is added dropwise to adjust the pH to 10. Then, the mixed salt solution and NaOH solution were then alternately added into the three-necked flask while maintaining the pH around 10. After that, the solution was cooled to room temperature, followed by centrifugation and washing with deionized water three times. The resulting product is FeNi-LDH.

Characterization

Brunauer–Emmett–Teller surface areas were determined from nitrogen adsorption–desorption measurements collected on a Micromeritics Tristar II 3020 M instrument after degassing the samples at 300 °C for 3 h. Fourier-transformed infrared resonance spectra were recorded in transmission mode using a Nicolet 8700 spectrometer. Raman spectra were acquired on a Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution spectrometer with 532 nm laser excitation. Powder X-ray diffraction patterns were obtained using a Miniflex 600 diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation. Aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) imaging and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy mapping were performed on a JEOL JEM−2010 LaB6 microscope operated at 200 kV. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy analysis was performed on a PHI 5000 VersaProbe system with Al Kα radiation, and the C 1 s peak at 284.8 eV was used for calibration. Gas-phase oxygen concentrations were quantified using a PANNA A91 gas chromatograph equipped with molecular sieve 5 A, Porapak Q 80/100 mesh, SE-30, and HP-Al2O3/S columns, with ultra-high-purity argon as the carrier gas.

Electrochemical measurements

Electrochemical tests were carried out at room temperature on a CHI-760E electrochemical workstation using a conventional three-electrode configuration. The suspension was sonicated for at least 40 min to obtain a uniform catalyst ink. Then, 10 μL of the ink was drop-cast onto a glassy carbon disk electrode with a geometric area of 0.2475 cm2 and dried before electrochemical testing.

For ORR, a rotating ring-disk electrode consisting of a glassy carbon disk and a Pt ring electrode (E7R9, Pine Instrument Company; disk area: 0.2475 cm2) was used as the working electrode. MEA system measurements

The MEA system was assembled using two nickel-plated stainless-steel backplates containing a 4.0 cm2 serpentine flow field. The anion exchange membrane (AEM) was sandwiched between the cathode and anode electrodes; SustainionTM X37-50 Grade RT was used as the AEM. The cathode was configured as a gas-fed electrode, and continuous gas flow was supplied through the flow channel to enable oxygen transport between the gas phase and the catalyst layer. On the anode, the 0.2 M K2CO3 solution was used as the electrolyte (mildly alkaline supporting anolyte; not a buffer), and nickel foam was employed as the electrode. A Pt/C cathode was also tested as a reference under identical conditions, and the cathode catalyst was the only variable.

Single cell measurements

The single cell consists of an anode plate, gasket, nickel foam, AEM, GDE, and cathode plate stacked together. The anode plate adopted a serpentine flow field, while the cathode plate utilized straight, serpentine, and interdigitated flow fields, respectively to test the effect of different types of flow fields on O2 transfer efficiency. The gasket is made of FEP to prevent air and electrolyte leakage. The nickel foam acts as a porous current collector and is loaded with OER catalyst on its surface. All above flow fields have a working area of 40 cm2.

Stack measurements

The stack contained 6 single cells, each comprising a serpentine anode flow field and an interdigitated cathode flow field. The cells were assembled in series with insulating plates inserted between adjacent units to suppress inter-layer currents and associated side reactions. End plates and bolts were used to compress the stack and reduce contact resistance. During operation, a drying tube was installed at the gas outlet to remove residual alkaline vapor.

CFD simulation details

CFD software ANSYS Fluent 2024 was employed to simulate different scenarios. The viscous model was set as Laminar. The species transport model was adopted to calculate the reaction of oxygen depletion. The boundary condition of the inlet was set as velocity-inlet according to the experiments and the outlet was pressure-outlet of 1 bar. The solution method used the scheme of coupled in the pressure-velocity coupling. All the spatial discretization used second order upwind in the pressure, density, momentum, energy, etc. The computational convergence was set to 10−3 for the mass equation and 10−6 for all other equations. Mesh independence studies were conducted for all structures to ensure that the simulation results were independent of mesh size.

Product preservation effect test

Fresh produce (strawberries, broccoli, and lettuce) of comparable maturity and size, free of visible defects and mechanical damage, was used for controlled-atmosphere storage tests. For each product, samples were divided into 15 equal-mass portions and randomly allocated to three storage conditions (n = 5 independent portions per condition): (i) LT, 5 °C in ambient air; (ii) EED-LOMT, 10 °C under 5% O2; and (iii) 10 °C in ambient air (control). Portions were stored in 10 L sealed containers (1.0–1.5 kg per container). In the EED-LOMT condition, headspace O2 was set and maintained at 5% using the EED system and monitored with an in-container O2 sensor (Pulitong, China; ± 0.01% O2). Sampling was destructive: at each time point (days 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20), one independent portion per condition was removed, homogenized as a composite replicate, and used for indicator measurements. Vitamin C was quantified from the resulting homogenate using a molybdenum-blue colorimetric assay (adapted from Han et al. with minor modifications); for each replicate, the assay was performed in technical triplicate and averaged prior to statistical analysis. Storage temperature was controlled with a test chamber (DW-25, Huayi, China), and electrical energy consumption was continuously recorded using a power meter (Delixi, China) to obtain cumulative energy consumption for each storage condition. Detailed procedures for vitamin C extraction/color development, together with calculation/normalization of loss rates and statistical analysis, are provided in Supplementary Note 1 and Table S6.

Vitamin C content was determined using a molybdenum-blue colorimetric assay adapted from Han et al., with minor modifications primarily to stabilize ascorbic acid and minimize matrix turbidity/interference during colorimetry62,63. Briefly, fresh samples were homogenized, extracted in a metaphosphoric acid–EDTA solution, and the clarified extract was reacted with Folin-Ciocalteu reagent for color development; absorbance was recorded at 760 nm (A760). For each biological replicate, the assay was performed in technical triplicate and averaged prior to statistical analysis; full extraction and color-development procedures are provided in Supplementary Note 1.
