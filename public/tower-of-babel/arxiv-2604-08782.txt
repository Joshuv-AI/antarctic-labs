MT-OSC : Path for LLMs that Get Lost in Multi-Turn Conversation
Jyotika Singh, Fang Tu, Miguel Ballesteros, Weiyi Sun

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) suffer significant performance degradation when user instructions and context are distributed over multiple conversational turns, yet multi-turn (MT) interactions dominate chat interfaces. The routine approach of appending full chat history to prompts rapidly exhausts context windows, leading to increased latency, higher computational costs, and diminishing returns as conversations extend. We introduce MT-OSC , a O ne-off S equential C ondensation framework that efficiently and automatically condenses chat history in the background without disrupting the user experience. MT-OSC employs a Condenser Agent that uses a few-shot inference-based Condenser and a lightweight Decider to selectively retain essential information, reducing token counts by up to 72% in 10-turn dialogues. Evaluated across 13 state-of-the-art LLMs and diverse multi-turn benchmarks, MT-OSC consistently narrows the multi-turn performance gap—yielding improved or preserved accuracy across datasets while remaining robust to distractors and irrelevant turns. Our results establish MT-OSC as a scalable solution for multi-turn chats, enabling richer context within constrained input spaces, reducing latency and operational cost, while balancing performance.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable results on single-turn benchmarks, yet their performance can deteriorate significantly when task instructions or critical context are fragmented across multiple conversational turns—sometimes as early as the second exchange
(
Laban et al. 2025
;
Kwan et al. 2024
)
. Despite this, such multi-turn (MT) interactions are characteristic of real-world dialogue, where users naturally break up information, clarifications, and intent across several exchanges.

This fragmentation introduces two fundamental challenges: (1) Dispersed information across turns makes it increasingly difficult for LLMs to accurately recall and integrate crucial details, often leading to inconsistencies and degraded performance compared to single-message queries
(
Laban et al. 2025
)
; (2) The prevalent solution of concatenating the entire chat transcript consumes valuable context window and quickly becomes unsustainable, incurring excess latency, cost, and potential information loss as conversations grow.

Although ad-hoc summarization of prior turns has become common, especially for longer chats, these methods risk omitting essential context and are often insufficient for complex multi-turn reasoning. Prior work on chat compression
(
Gliwa et al. 2019
;
Bae et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2025
)
and retrieval-augmented dialogues
(
Xu et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2023
)
have explored strategies to shrink context size while preserving relevance. Nonetheless, such approaches frequently depend on model fine-tuning, explicit context selection, or complex hierarchical schemes, limiting their scalability and ease of integration.

In contrast, we address a complementary challenge to task decomposition research where prior work improves results by splitting a complex request into multiple focused prompts
(
Wen et al. 2024
;
Sarangi et al. 2025
;
Prasad et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025b
)
. Here, we focus on different, realistic scenario where information is
fragmented
over time and must be robustly integrated to avoid compounding errors.

We propose

, a
O

S
equential
C

MT-OSC operates entirely in the background, requiring no model modifications or fine-tuning and avoiding user-visible latency. Through a combination of a few-shot Condenser and a lightweight, data-driven Decider, MT-OSC dynamically preserves salient context, sharply reducing token overhead and safeguarding against information loss.
Our comprehensive evaluation spanning 13 SOTA LLMs and 10 diverse, multi-turn datasets (spanning underspecified MT samples

Related Work

A central theme in multi-turn research is compressing chat history into compact, reusable state representations instead of naively appending full transcripts
Gliwa et al. 2019
;
Feng et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2025a
. While there have been extensive work on model pre-training
Press et al. 2022
;
Su et al. 2023
and optimization for fine-tuning
Rafailov et al. 2024
to improve long context handling, the core challenge remains: preserving task-relevant context as conversations lengthen.

Bae et al. 2022
tackles this using finetuned T5 summarization model to retain only essential dialogue.

Zhang et al. 2022
leverage hierarchical encodings and attention for iterative refinement of conversation history.

Chen et al. 2025
introduce compressive memory by integrating user information with conversation summaries; their approach relies on supervised fine-tuning and preference optimization.

Jiang et al. 2024
proposed using a small language model to compute token perplexity and selectively remove low-perplexity tokens, also reordering relevant content to beginning or end to address the
lost in the middle

Liu et al. 2024a
;
Levy et al. 2024
issue for long-context scenarios; however, this method requires re-compression for each query, limiting the ability to cache context.
In contrast, our approach is distinguished by its lack of reliance on hierarchical encodings, model fine-tuning, heuristic-based compression, or manual context selection. Instead, our method operates unobtrusively in the background, enabling easy integration into any LLM-based chat system without specialized architectures or operational overhead.

Long context prompt compression methods
Pan et al. 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2024
compress very long inputs via token/segment pruning (often importance/perplexity-based) and filtering/reordering. This differs from short-horizon multi-turn settings, where redundancy is limited and correctness can depend on small but decisive cues (e.g., negations, corrections, key entity mentions); token deletion can therefore alter intent or break coreference, and reordering is less meaningful given the causal chronology of dialogue. As a result, these methods are not directly comparable baselines for MT-OSC, but are better viewed as complementary prompt-compression tools for very long histories.

Another influential line of research, retrieval-augmented conversation
Xu et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Aadhithya et al. 2024
;
Salama et al. 2025
, selectively re-injects relevant dialogue snippets using retrieval-based methods to address context growth and improve grounding. While retrieval helps limit prompt size, it may miss nuances spanning multiple turns or temporal dependencies, and often increases system complexity or user latency via real-time retrieval
Chen et al. 2025
. By contrast, our strategy offers a complementary alternative, maintaining a continuously updated, compact representation that eliminates the need for instantaneous look-up or large-scale context storage.

Recent work has focused on systematic evaluation of multi-turn context handling methods. MT-Eval
(
Kwan et al. 2024
)
benchmarks LLM abilities on dialogue recollection, expansion, refinement, and follow-up.
Zhang et al. 2025
stress evaluation criteria such as conversational quality, multi-turn reasoning, and tool usage.
Gorle et al. 2025
presents information loss study as multi-turn conversations progress.
Laban et al. 2025
present a sharding-based evaluation protocol where fully specified single-turn data is artificially split into underspecified multistep interactions to simulate realistic chat dynamics.
Baseline methods in
Laban et al. 2025
such as CONCAT and SNOWBALL, although useful for diagnosis, are infeasible for practical deployment: their token requirements scale linearly with chat length, and they often fail for episodic dialogues by repeatedly answering user’s older requests. Other proposed methods like RECAP intervene only at the final turn (unknown a priori), limiting their applicability for ongoing dialogues. Furthermore, recommendations such as "retry with more time" or "consolidate before retrying" require human intervention and lack systematic, automated mitigation strategies.

Overall, while prior work has elucidated the scope and severity of multi-turn performance decline, effective and generalizable solutions for automated multi-turn condensation remain relatively underexplored.
MT-OSC
fills this gap by providing a robust, scalable condensation process that streamlines multi-turn context integration without sacrificing accuracy, latency, or deployability. Appendix
A
shares extended details on related work.

Condenser:

This module leverages an LLM agent prompted in a few-shot style with explicit reasoning, tuned through curated exemplars to robustly condense chat history. We construct a diverse set of multi-turn chat examples to illustrate a spectrum of condensation policies: cases where key user text should be retained verbatim for future reference, scenarios requiring comprehensive consolidation, and situations where irrelevant turns may be safely excluded. Each hand-crafted example is paired with detailed rationales for information retention. This enables the Condenser to adapt dynamically to distinct dialogue patterns frequently observed in public multi-turn datasets, such as LMSYS-Chat1M
Zheng et al. 2024

4

Decider:

The Decider is a lightweight and configurable component that adaptively determines whether or not to apply condensation to a given conversation segment. Although condensation substantially reduces token load in many cases, certain dialogues where essential information is repeatedly referenced over several turns with dense context could risk information loss if condensed. To safeguard against this, the Decider employs a rule-based function that quantifies redundancy and novelty in assistant responses relative to prior user queries. Each user message
U_{i}

A_{i}

i

\gamma

\tau
tokens across 4 turns, condensation is withheld to avoid potential loss of repeatedly referenced information. This selective approach ensures the condenser maintains fidelity in information-dense dialogues.

4
Datasets

We evaluate MT-OSC across 10 datasets, selected to encompass a wide spectrum of multi-turn chat scenarios. These datasets include both sharded static benchmarks and resembling naturally occurring dialogues, representing chats of up to 12 user turns—beyond the multi-turn range typically covered in prior work.

Sharded Datasets.

We employ six prominent benchmarks
Laban et al. 2025
—GSM8K, BFCL-V3 Parallel, HumanEval (HEval), Spider, ToTTo, and Summary of Haystack (SoH)—in their sharded form. Each task, originally single-turn, is split into multiple turns to simulate underspecified, real-world multi-turn workflows, where instruction and context are fragmented across user utterances.

MT-EVAL Datasets.

We further adopt four datasets drawn from MT-EVAL
Kwan et al. 2024
, each reflecting a different facet of real conversational flow: recollection, refinement, expansion, and follow-up. Noting data scarcity in the recollection and expansion categories (each with only ten instances), we synthesize additional samples for these groups by generating new instances matched to the original style and distribution (see Appendix
C.2
).

Dataset Splits.

We partition all data into development, validation, and test subsets with no instance overlap in downstream evaluation. Seven datasets are held out exclusively for testing; the remainder are split to support model development and hyperparameter tuning. Our primary results are reported on a test set of 752 multi-turn chat samples with 4–12 user turns. Complete breakdowns of corpus sizes and splits are in Appendix
C
.

Robustness Evaluation Data.

The datasets are relatively clean without noise, distractions, or unnecessary context. To probe robustness, we inject simulated noise and redundancy between turns to mirror more realistic conversational messiness. These perturbations include: (
i
)
Repetition Infusion
—randomly duplicating
N
turns in each chat; (
ii
)
Filler Injection
—randomly inserting vacuous filler turns (e.g., “Um,” “Hmm”); (
iii
)
Contextual Diversion
—inserting topically related but task-irrelevant utterances. These methods are inspired by prior attempts to stress-test context robustness
Levy et al. 2024
;
Mirzadeh et al. 2025
, but are newly extended here to multi-turn settings (see Appendix
C.3
for prompt and method details,
C.3.1
for example of original vs. the three variants).

Evaluation Metrics.

We use a suite of established metrics tailored to each dataset: accuracy for code and math (HumanEval, BFCL, GSM, Spider, Recollection+); LLM-based quality ratings (10-point scale) for open-ended dialogic tasks (Refinement, Follow-up, Expansion+); BLEU score for ToTTo; and a composite joint-score F1 for SoH. Where prior rule-based metrics proved inadequate (e.g., GSM final answer matching), we augment with LLM-judge reviews for correctness. Full details are in Appendix
C.1
.

Unless otherwise stated, our main experiments use Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct. For aggregate performance and token analyses, we restrict to accuracy-based datasets and provide per-task breakdowns as appropriate.

Context Loss Mitigation:

The common reasons for drop in performance in multi-turn conversations is the risk of either failing to recall pertinent context or propagating incorrect assumptions—especially before users provide sufficient detail or clarification
Laban et al. 2025
. This issue is particularly prominent in sharded datasets such as GSM, BFCL, HumanEval, and Spider, where initial conversational turns frequently lack adequate context, causing large language models to make premature or unsupported inferences. As subsequent turns unfold and more information becomes available, models sometimes struggle to reconcile earlier assumptions, potentially leading to confusion or compounded errors.
Notably, in these challenging scenarios, MT-OSC offers a clear advantage by condensing chat history to include only the most salient and contextually important information leading to improved accuracy and more consistent reasoning, as demonstrated by our quantitative results. For illustrative cases where this approach led to correction of earlier misinterpretations, we provide detailed examples in Appendix

(Figure

Chat Efficiency for the End-User:

MT-OSC reduces the chat history included in the main chat prompt, thereby decreasing the number of tokens and consequently lowering latency for the end user. Prompt length is a primary contributor to Time To First Token (TTFT), which measures how quickly users begin to see the model’s output
Agarwal et al. 2023
. Empirical studies have shown a linear relationship between the number of prompt tokens and TTFT
Paruthi 2024
.
For chats with >=6 turns, if chat history comprises 4,000 tokens, MT-OSC saves an average of 1,782 tokens per exchange
(reducing from 4,000 to 2,218 tokens), which translates to
approximately 1.1-1.2 seconds in latency reduction
based on findings in
Paruthi 2024
. This reduction provides a tangible improvement in end-user experience.

In real-world applications, the number of turns persisted in a chat may often be restricted to conserve computational resources, with a limit of around 10 turns for many exploratory dialogues
Marino 2025
;
team 2025
. MT-OSC maintains low chat history token consumption, limiting history turns to <=5 when w=4, thereby enabling efficient operation within these practical constraints.

Asynchronous Execution Advantage

MT-OSC runs as a background process whose output is not required for the immediate next turn in the chat. Since its prompt length is typically comparable to or smaller than the main chat—often shorter due to additional system prompts and user typing delays—the condensation step usually completes while the next turn wraps. Its result is therefore available by the next-to-next turn.
This enables a one-off, asynchronous condensation step that
adds
no latency
to the user experience
: the conversation proceeds normally while MT-OSC executes in the background, and its output is seamlessly used in one-off subsequent turns.

. Importantly, MT-OSC effectively mitigates the impact of unnecessary information by condensing the chat history to retain only the most relevant content, maintaining stable performance benefits even on noisier data.

To test

Conclusion

Multi-turn conversations present persistent challenges for LLMs, resulting in both degraded task performance and unsustainable increases in prompt length as dialogues scale. To address these limitations, we introduce MT-OSC, background condensation framework that sequentially condenses chat history as conversations grow. MT-OSC achieves significant reductions in chat history token counts (up to 72% fewer tokens for 10-turn dialogues) while either preserving or enhancing accuracy across a diverse set of benchmarks and state-of-the-art LLM architectures.

Our evaluation demonstrates that MT-OSC not only narrows the multi-turn performance gap but also provides robust gains in settings with conversational noise or distractors, enabling more efficient and reliable interactions. Importantly, MT-OSC operates entirely in the background: it does not require model modification, retraining, or user intervention, and it delivers tangible improvements in latency and efficiency for real-world deployments.

Overall, MT-OSC provides a practical, scalable solution to the multi-turn context bottleneck, enabling broader applications of conversational AI in chat systems.

Limitations

This study utilizes publicly available datasets that are designed for comparing single-turn and multi-turn conversational interactions. These datasets typically contain fewer than 10–12 user turns per conversation and primarily feature clean, single-topic dialogues with minimal agentic behavior—that is, they involve straightforward LLM calls without exploring the tool-use capabilities that confer agentic properties to LLMs. While we applied data augmentation to assess robustness, the underlying data represents limitation in linguistic complexity and topic diversity that characterize more diverse real-world conversational settings.

For
future work
, model training can explicitly incorporate more complex multi-turn interactions, enabling forthcoming models to better handle the challenges presented by extended conversations.
Developing or incorporating datasets with longer, more diverse, and multi-topic conversations would provide a richer foundation for analysis and modeling. Further, leveraging data that reflects greater conversational complexity, including open-domain and agent-driven exchanges, could enhance the generalizability and applicability of future findings. Exploring methods such as topical decomposition may also provide valuable support for tackling the challenges posed by complex, multi-faceted dialogue interactions.

Appendix A
Extended related work

Chat systems in Natural Language Processing (NLP) (field comprising text and audio processing) have existed well before large language models (LLMs) became state of the art
Vaswani et al. 2017
;
Singh 2022
. NLP applications are widely used across industry verticals
Singh 2023
and diverse data sources, including business and finance, social media, and even unstructured data
Yang et al. 2025
;
Singh et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2024b
;
Singh 2021
, spanning task-oriented, data-analysis-focused
Singh et al. 2021
, and free-form settings, and now LLMs are heavily used across these data types and applications.
While LLMs offer substantial advantages along with some limitations
(
Yuan et al. 2026
)
, they have made real-world chat applications far more prevalent by reducing reliance on separate intent/entity detection models and hand-crafted rules to control behavior based on conversational signals. Still, reliance on single-turn benchmarks prevail and multi-turn chats underperform single-turn specified prompts.

Memory compression

Chen et al. 2025
introduce the idea of compressive memory where they integrate conversation summaries with information about the user. They use Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to train a Llama-2 model to summarize and compress memories without dependence on external modules.
Jiang et al. 2024
uses a small language model to calculate the perplexity of each token in the original prompt and then remove tokens with lower perplexities, and reorders relevant parts to the beginning and end to combat the "lost in the middle" problem for long-context scenarios. It requires re-compression for different questions, even with the same context, preventing caching of the context. In addition, aggressive compression can inadvertently remove key details, impacting accuracy for tasks requiring nuance.

Why long-context work is not competitive but complimentary

Long-context compression methods such as LLMLingua-2
Pan et al. 2024
and LongLLMLingua
Jiang et al. 2024
target a different regime of compressing very long inputs via token/segment pruning (often importance/perplexity-based) and, for LongLLMLingua, reordering/filtering. In short-horizon multi-turn setting, redundancy is limited and correctness often hinges on small but decisive state (e.g., negations, corrections like ’instead,’ key entity mentions), so token-level deletion can disproportionately change intent or break coreference; reordering is also less meaningful when history is short and chronologically causal. Therefore, they are not an apples-to-apples comparison and not a suitable baseline for MT-OSC; rather, they are best viewed as prompt-compression tools that may be used in addition to MT-OSC for very long histories.

Multi-turn sharded datasets and hinted solution limitation

Laban et al. 2025
formulates a unique evaluation method for underspecification in multi-turn conversations. They introduce a sharding process where they transform datasets with fully-specified instructions into sharded instructions that are revealed on subsequent turns.
The CONCAT method proposed in the paper could perform better in underspecified settings but is impractical for real-world scenarios, as users are unlikely to resend all prior context with each message. If automated, it is also ineffective for episodic multi-turn conversations, where the model may struggle to distinguish between the current and previous tasks. Additionally, the approach significantly increases the number of tokens in the conversation, reducing overall efficiency. There are two other methods mentioned in the paper, RECAP and SNOWBALL. Although RECAP performs better than SNOWBALL, its intervention is conducted on the last turn of the conversation, which is not known a priori, and both have been shown to have worse performance than CONCAT.
The two recommendations in the paper to mitigate information loss, "If time allows, try again." and "Consolidate before retrying.", are not principled approaches as they both require human evaluation and intervention.

C.2
MT-EVAL Synthesis

Two out of the four MT-EVAL datasets, namely
Recollection
and
Expansion
, originally contained only 10 samples each. To increase this number to 100 samples per dataset, we used the following methods:

MT-EVAL Recollection Dataset

Each sample consists of a user instruction followed by document text in all subsequent turns. The dataset contains 100 unique documents in total, with 10 documents (one per turn) used in each sample. To generate additional samples, we randomly selected 10 different documents from the pool of 100 for each new sample, while keeping the top-level user instruction and task unchanged.

MT-EVAL Expansion Dataset

Each sample begins with a document in the first turn, followed by 10 different questions related to that document in subsequent turns. These questions depend only on the initial document and are not order-sensitive. To create variants, we shuffled the order of the 10 questions for each sample, producing 9 new variants per original sample. This process, combined with the 10 original samples, resulted in a total of 100 samples for this dataset.

C.3
Generating distracting turns

We observe that most publicly available multi-turn datasets are relatively clean with no distractions or noise. To further assess the robustness of our experiments, we introduced simulated redundancy and task-irrelevant information between turns within the above datasets. This modification allowed us to evaluate performance in less curated, more realistic conversational settings, thereby ensuring our results generalize beyond cleaner, idealized data. Specifically, we introduced the following types of redundancy and irrelevant information into the multi-turn conversations:

1.

Repetition Infusion
: Repeated random turns within the multi-turn dialogues - for each multi-turn conversation, we randomly select N turns in the middle (not the first turn or the last turn) and repeat them once in the same position. N is set to be a portion of the total number of turns in the conversation.

2.

Filler Injection
: Inserted random turns containing only meaningless filler words, such as ’Um.’, ’Uh.’, ’Well.’, ’Anyway.’, ’Ok.’, ’Hmm.’, etc. - for each multi-turn conversation, we randomly select N turns in the middle (exclude the first turn) and add a randomly selected filler word as extra turn right before them.

3.

Contextual Diversion
:
For each multi-turn chat, we randomly select N turns from the middle (excluding the first and last) and add new, contextually relevant turns that aren’t essential for task completion. If a selected turn isn’t blank, we generate an extra turn based on its content and place it immediately after.

D.3
Decider analysis

Figure 12:
Percentage reduction in chat history tokens in MT-OSC over MT-baseline across datasets with and without Decider component.

Figure
12
shows that chat history tokens are reduced across different datasets when using MT-OSC, both with and without the Decider component, for conversations with six or more turns.

With the Decider, chat history tokens are reduced by an average of 32.19%. Without the Decider, the reduction averages 45.58% across all datasets (even the ones where no sample goes through the condenser in the w/ Decider case).

For datasets where some samples are condensed in w/ Decider case (BFCL, GSM, HumanEval, Refinement, Recollection+, and Expansion+), with Decider reduction averages 44.56% and without Decider reduction averages 52.07%. Note we didn’t include Spider as it has only 6 samples with >=6 turns so we excluded it in the calculation due to very low number of samples.

Example

To complement the Decider ablation results, we share a Sharded ToTTo dataset example (using chat model Llama-3.3-70B) illustrating how default condensation without Decider gate may lose salient multi-turn emphasis signals. In this setting, the final table description depends on information provided across all past turns, including a highlighted-cell view and additional metadata.

Example user-provided conversation turns:

Turn 1:
"I am providing you a table. You must produce a short one-sentence description of the table. The description should be at most 30 words…………..<data>"

Turn 2:
"Here’s 10 examples of descriptions of other tables. You can use it as references to understand how to describe the table: <data>"

Turn 3:
"I am now giving you a version of the table with some cells highlights. ……."

Turn 4:
"Here’s additional metadata about the table. The table_webpage_url is ……"

Turn 5:
"*Here’s additional metadata about the table. The table_page_title is 2012 in AFC."

Turn 6:
"Here’s additional metadata about the table. The table_section_title is Events list."

In this conversation, earlier turns center more around “AFC 3", while turns (4–6) provide page and section context that should be incorporated without overriding the highlight focus.

MT-OSC final output without Decider (always condense): “The table lists events on the 2012 in AFC Wikipedia page under Events list."

MT-OSC final output with Decider (in this case - skip condensation): “The table highlights AFC 3 event details in the Events list section of the 2012 in AFC Wikipedia page."

This example shows a typical Decider benefit: aggressive condensation may at time, for certain chat models, produce a less specific final description by losing or down-weighting earlier emphasis cues in comparison to cues shared in newer turns; the Decider helps avoid this potential failure mode.
