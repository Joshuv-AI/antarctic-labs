SNAP disruption during a government shutdown and implications for chronic disease self-management: a qualitative study
Authors: Elizabeth Whidden, Nicole Ellingson, Charlotte Babbin, Eliza W Kinsey, Aaron Richterman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGovernment shutdowns expose safety-net programs to unplanned disruptions, but little is known about how unexpected benefit interruptions affect chronic disease management. In November 2025, a shutdown-related delay in Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) benefit issuance disrupted food access for millions of Americans.MethodsWe conducted semi-structured interviews with 20 SNAP recipients living with HIV, nested within a 54-person cohort with daily antiretroviral therapy adherence monitoring. Interviews explored monthly SNAP cycle experiences, food-HIV management connections, and the November 2025 benefit delay. Pre-shutdown adherence is reported across the parent cohort; during-shutdown adherence is reported across 9 participants whose monitoring extended into the disruption.ResultsFour themes emerged: Routine food access required planning; the monthly SNAP cycle imposed cognitive and material burden affecting chronic disease self-management; the November 2025 disruption was qualitatively distinct because added uncertainty compounded burden and destabilized self-management; and community supports partially buffered consequences. Across the 54-person parent cohort, median pre-shutdown adherence was 72%; among 9 participants monitored during the disruption, median during-shutdown adherence was 40%.ConclusionsUnexpected benefit disruptions impose uncertainty that adds cognitive burden and upsets the routines essential for chronic disease management. Safety-net programs should prioritize benefit reliability alongside adequacy, with implications extending across chronic conditions that depend on daily self-management.Keywords: economic scarcity, HIV, medication adherence, supplemental nutrition assistance program

Introduction

US federal safety-net programs face recurring threats to benefit continuity, from appropriations lapses and administrative reforms to state-level modernization efforts and expanded work and reporting requirements. The health consequences of these disruptions are poorly understood, despite their growing policy salience. Food assistance is a case in point: The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) reaches more than 40 million low-income Americans,1 a population in which chronic conditions requiring consistent medication-taking, predictable nutrition, and routine care are common. Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program reduces food insecurity and poverty and is associated with multiple improved health outcomes.2,3 Whether and how unplanned interruptions in SNAP benefit receipt translate into changes in health-relevant behaviors is a question with implications for safety-net program design across agencies and conditions. In November 2025, a federal government shutdown-related delay in SNAP benefit issuance created an unexpected gap in food resources for millions of Americans.4 This disruption offered a rare opportunity to examine these health consequences in real time.

Most evidence about SNAP and health comes from studies of routine benefit disbursement. The SNAP benefit amounts frequently fall short of monthly household needs,5 producing predictable within-month patterns of relative adequacy after benefit receipt, followed by increasing scarcity as benefits are depleted—termed the “SNAP cycle.”6-14 Studies have shown that food spending, caloric intake, food quality, and certain health outcomes vary across the SNAP cycle,15,16 underscoring how benefit timing functions as a structurally imposed rhythm that shapes day-to-day decisions about food, finances, and health. Our prior research has documented that the SNAP cycle imposes dual cognitive and financial burdens of managing chronic diseases, with food and financial scarcity at the end of the benefit cycle adversely impacting disease management.17 Much less is known about what happens when SNAP is disrupted entirely. The few existing studies of prior SNAP disruptions have focused on food security, spending behavior, or participant experiences, with limited attention to health management or clinical outcomes.18

Food insecurity has been shown to impact chronic disease management through multiple pathways.17,19 Many people report poor tolerability of medications on an empty stomach, and patients may delay or skip doses if food is unavailable.19 Beyond this direct mechanism, theories of scarcity and cognitive function suggest that poverty and resource constraints impose a “bandwidth tax,” consuming cognitive capacity and executive function in ways that can undermine planning, decision-making, and the maintenance of beneficial routines, even among individuals who are aware of and motivated to sustain them.20,21 Consequently, managing chronic health conditions in the context of food insecurity requires not only adequate food intake but also the cognitive capacity to maintain consistent routines, anticipate future needs, and navigate complex systems of care.22

HIV represents a high-stakes example of routine-dependent chronic disease management: Sustained medication adherence and clinic engagement are essential for viral suppression and long-term health, and food insecurity has been consistently associated with worse HIV outcomes.23 In November 2025, in response to the government shutdown, we rapidly conducted qualitative interviews with SNAP recipients living with HIV enrolled in a longitudinal cohort study to examine how the shutdown-related benefit delay altered food access, psychological and cognitive burden, and chronic disease self-management.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a qualitative study nested within a longitudinal cohort of adults with HIV receiving SNAP benefits in Philadelphia and surrounding counties. The parent study (N = 54) was a 4-month prospective cohort study designed to characterize daily antiretroviral therapy (ART) adherence patterns and their relationship to socioeconomic stressors using electronic pill bottle caps (CleverCap Lite, Compliance Med Technologies). We report the qualitative study in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines (eAppendix 1).24

Participants and sampling

Parent cohort participants were recruited from the University of Pennsylvania Health System and from Philadelphia-area community-based organizations using electronic health portal outreach, in-person recruitment during clinic visits, and flyers. Eligibility criteria included: age ≥ 18 years; SNAP beneficiary; living with HIV; prescribed daily oral ART; self-report of missing ≥2 ART doses in the prior 30 days to capture a population at risk of adherence challenges and allowing for assessment of whether food access and SNAP benefit cycles predicted subsequent variation in medication adherence behavior; and residence in Philadelphia or a surrounding county. Exclusion criteria included inability to consent, non-English speaking, cognitive impairment, or receipt of Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF), whose different benefit distribution schedule could confound assessment of SNAP-specific benefit cycling.

For the qualitative sub-study, we purposively sampled 20 individuals from the parent cohort for in-depth interviews based on the availability of high-quality adherence data, to ensure the validity of triangulating qualitative narratives with objective adherence data. We defined high-quality adherence data as continuous device functionality and correct usage over the 4-month study period. Specifically, we excluded data where the device was lost, broken (without replacement), or where participants reported storing medication in a vessel other than the monitored bottle. Device integrity and correct usage were verified via monthly refill confirmation calls and weekly automated data audits to distinguish between missed doses and device nonuse. We employed a sequential sampling approach, reviewing participants in order and contacting those with high-quality adherence data until the target sample of 20 was reached. During this process, we skipped 13 participants who were unavailable (n = 7) or had poor data quality (n = 6). We contacted 23 participants with high-quality adherence data; 3 declined participation, prompting us to continue recruitment until we secured the final sample of 20. Because we used a sequential sampling approach, the remaining 18 participants were not contacted after the target sample of 20 participants was reached. Participants were contacted by phone; the interview study consent was reviewed, and verbal consent was obtained prior to the interview. Delayed SNAP payments in Pennsylvania were issued on November 13-14, following delays ranging from 1 to 12 days.25 Interviews were conducted between November 12 and November 24, 2025; one interview occurred prior to delayed payment distribution, while the remainder took place shortly afterward. Participants received $30 for completing the interview.

Data collection

Descriptive measures

We report participant characteristics measured in the parent cohort at enrollment, including sociodemographics, SNAP benefit amount, household food insecurity,26 alcohol use,27 HIV-related stigma,28 and measures of housing and psychosocial stressors.29-31

Objective adherence data

CleverCap electronic pill bottle caps transmit date and time of each bottle-opening event via cellular networks to a secure cloud platform. Days without an opening were classified as missed doses. Data were excluded only when participants were determined to not be using the device, for example, if there was a device malfunction or participant-reported use of a non-monitored container was confirmed. This ensured that missing data reflected technical issues rather than non-adherence. For participants on multi-pill regimens, a single once-daily ART medication was monitored.

Of the 54-person cohort with adherence monitoring, 9 participants had monitoring windows that extended into November 2025 and therefor contributed observations during the shutdown; the remaining 45 had completed their monitoring before that month. The 20 qualitative interviewees are a purposive subsample of the parent cohort. Of the 9 participants with during-shutdown monitoring, 7 were also among the 20 interviewees, and the other 2 were parent cohort participants who were not interviewed. The remaining 13 interviewees had completed monitoring before November 2025, contributing pre-shutdown observations but no during-shutdown observations. The pre-shutdown adherence sample (n = 54), the during-shutdown adherence sample (n = 9), and the qualitative interview sample (n = 20) therefore overlap but are not coextensive (Figure 1).

Qualitative data collection

Semi-structured telephone interviews lasting 30-60 minutes were conducted in English by 3 study staff (E.W., N.E., and C.B.), each with formal training in qualitative interviewing and analysis. None had prior relationships with participants. Interviews were scheduled within a few days of the initial scheduling call and conducted one-on-one with no other individuals present. No repeat interviews were conducted. Participants received gift card incentives that were either mailed or electronically transmitted. The interview guide was adapted from our prior studies of the SNAP cycle and health14,17 and explored food access, connections between food and HIV management, experiences of the monthly SNAP cycle, and experiences during the November 2025 SNAP benefit delay (eAppendix 2). Interviews were audio-recorded, professionally transcribed, and de-identified prior to analysis. Field notes were not collected. Transcripts were not returned to participants for review.

Analysis

Per-participant adherence was calculated as the proportion of observed days with a recorded bottle-opening event. Adherence data are reported descriptively for 2 periods: pre-shutdown adherence (any monitored days before November 1, 2025), summarized across the 54-person parent cohort, and during-shutdown adherence (days from each participant's expected benefit date through delayed benefit receipt in November 2025), summarized across the 9 participants whose monitoring windows extended into that month. The two samples overlap but are not coextensive, and the 2 summaries should not be interpreted as a within-person before-after comparison.

We used thematic analysis to identify patterns of meaning within the qualitative data.32 A codebook was developed including a priori codes based on domains covered in the interview guide developed with the content expertise of the researchers (deductive coding) and themes that emerged from the data (inductive coding). All transcripts were coded using NVivo 15 by research staff members E.W., N.E., and C.B.33 The first 3 transcripts were coded by consensus to refine code definitions; thereafter, 20% of the remaining transcripts were double-coded to maintain inter-coder agreement (κ > 0.75). Discrepancies were resolved by consensus with senior investigators (E.W.K., A.R.). We iteratively reviewed coded data to identify themes related to food access, coping strategies, SNAP experiences (including the SNAP cycle), psychological and cognitive mechanisms, food-HIV management connections, and the November 2025 disruption. Thematic saturation was reached because coding of additional transcripts after 16 interviews generated no substantively new themes.34 Representative quotations were selected by consensus among all coauthors. Participants were not contacted to provide feedback on the findings.

Researcher positionality

The research team included investigators with expertise with infectious diseases and poverty alleviation policy (A.R.), food policy and nutrition (E.W.K.), and health policy research (A.R. and E.W.K.). A.R. is a physician who provides HIV care in the same health system from which some participants were recruited but did not have clinical relationships or direct interactions with participants during the study. The team approached this work from a perspective informed by social determinants of health and structural drivers of health inequities, with attention to how policy design shapes lived experience.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Pennsylvania. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

Twenty participants completed interviews between November 12 and November 24, 2025. Participants had a median age of 57.5 years (IQR, 40-62); 12 (60%) were male sex; 16 (80%) were Black and median household size was 2 (IQR 1-3) (Table 1). Ten (50%) reported income less than 75% of the federal poverty level (∼$16 000 annually for a 2-person household), and all had low or very low food security. Monthly median SNAP benefits were $286 (IQR, 210-292), equivalent to about 20% of the median monthly income of participants. Participants reported substantial psychosocial burden: 11 (55%) reported moderate or serious psychological distress, and 16 (80%) reported moderate or high perceived stigma.

Across the 54-person parent cohort, median per-participant ART adherence in the pre-shutdown period was 72% (IQR, 52-87; 5675 person-days of observation). Among the 9 parent cohort participants whose monitoring windows extended into November 2025 (including 7 of the 20 interviewees), median adherence was 40% (IQR, 20-80; 76 person-days). The 9 parent cohort participants with during-shutdown monitoring were similar to the full parent cohort in age (median 55 vs 57.5 years), race (89% Black vs 80%), and median monthly SNAP benefits ($291 vs $286). Qualitative results

Qualitative findings are organized around four themes, each stated as an analytic claim about how SNAP structures health-relevant behavior: (1) the effortful work of routine food access among SNAP recipients, (2) the monthly SNAP cycle's interaction with chronic disease self-management, (3) the qualitatively distinct experience of the November 2025 shutdown-related benefit delay, and (4) the buffering role of community, clinical, and informal supports. Theme 1: routine food access by SNAP recipients required effortful, price-driven planning that prioritized survival over preference

Routine food access among SNAP recipients required effortful, price-driven planning that prioritized survival over preference, in a process shaped by limited resources, household needs, and neighborhood food environments. Shopping was strongly price-driven, involving multiple stores, timing around sales, and trade-offs to stretch budgets. As one participant explained, “you get $200-something on a food stamp card, [you] can’t just go to one store… I try to go to the store on a weekend… that's when I catch all the good sales” (participant 20, age 54, male, Black, 18% pre-shutdown adherence). Sales rather than preferences dictated purchases: “I go by what's on sale at the time. I try to get what's on sale, and then… I try to get pretty much the things that I know that I’m gonna need” (participant 17, age 38, male, Black, 74% pre-shutdown adherence).

Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program rarely met household needs on its own. Participants routinely supplemented SNAP-purchased food with food banks and informal support from family and friends, often planning the sequence deliberately: “I go to the food banks first, and what they don’t have, that I need, I’ll then go to the market and spend my [SNAP]” (participant 3, age 63, female, multiracial, 92% pre-shutdown adherence). Within these constraints, participants prioritized affordable staples (eg, milk, eggs, bread, cereal, meats, produce) over preferred items. Many described a desire to eat healthier that was consistently constrained by cost: “I’m trying to eat healthy, but eating healthy is more expensive than not eating healthy, so I have to pick between what healthy things I want” (participant 7, age 67, female, Black, 81% pre-shutdown adherence).

Across accounts, food access required sustained planning, mental accounting, and trade-offs even before any disruption. This constrained baseline shaped how participants experienced both the predictable monthly SNAP cycle and the November 2025 disruption.

Theme 2: the monthly SNAP cycle imposes recurring cognitive and material burden that interacts with chronic disease self-management

The monthly SNAP cycle organized food access into a predictable rhythm: brief stability immediately after benefit receipt followed by increasing scarcity as benefits were depleted. Participants approached the cycle strategically, typically beginning each month with a structured stocking-up trip focused on staples and bulk purchasing to extend food availability. As one participant described, “The week before is when I normally start jotting down what I’m going to shop for when I do get my [SNAP]. Say my [SNAP] came today and, yeah, if they came this morning, I’d try to be in and out by noon” (participant 10, age 65, male, Black, 59% pre-shutdown adherence). Despite this planning, benefits routinely fell short by month's end, prompting eating less, skipping meals, or limiting food choices: “I might eat a little less at times… I honestly do towards the end of the month” (participant 17, age 38, male, Black, 74% pre-shutdown adherence); “Oh my God, before I get my SNAP, it’d be difficult… I’m hungry… There's times where I went without food for two or three days” (participant 5, age 37, female, Black, 88% pre-shutdown adherence).

The SNAP cycle produced distinct cognitive burden. Participants described extensive mental accounting—actively mapping gaps, tracking remaining resources, and coordinating emergency resources—work that carried its own stress and fatigue. As one participant said, “I try to maneuver where I can make stuff last. And then sometimes I’ll eat nothing but turkey butts and beans for a couple of days. And a lot of chicken thighs. I’m so tired of chicken thighs I don’t know what do, so that has been – yeah. So I try to survive” (participant 7, age 67, female, Black, 81% pre-shutdown adherence).

This cyclical scarcity interacted directly with HIV self-management. Many participants required food to tolerate ART and described how end-of-month scarcity disrupted both medication timing and clinic attendance: “I need food every day, especially in the morning, to take my meds” (participant 11, age 61, female, Black, 68% pre-shutdown adherence). When food was unavailable, participants reported delaying or skipping doses: “If I’m changing my eating at the end of the month… that changes how I’m taking my medicine because I’m gonna take it while I’m eating… if I don’t have anything in the stomach, I’m not gonna take that medicine” (participant 17, age 38, male, Black, 74% pre-shutdown adherence); “I have a [medication] routine. When I don’t have any food, it messes up the routine” (participant 8, age 29, female, Black, 91% pre-shutdown adherence). Hunger and low energy also undermined motivation for clinical engagement and self-care: “If I don’t have enough food… I’m not going to that appointment. It makes my energy low where I don’t wanna get up and go to the doctors… I may have to reschedule my appointment because I’m hungry” (participant 5, age 37, female, Black, 88% pre-shutdown adherence). In some cases, the link between hunger, distress, and functioning was explicit in decisions about attending medical visits during the high scarcity period at the end of the month.

Across these accounts, the monthly SNAP cycle served as a predictable but burdensome rhythm of both material availability and recurrent cognitive load that destabilized routines essential for chronic disease self-management.

Theme 3: acute SNAP benefit disruption from the 2025 Government Shutdown was qualitatively distinct from routine scarcity because it added uncertainty that further destabilized chronic disease self-management

The November 2025 benefit delay was layered onto an already fragile monthly cycle, but participants described it as qualitatively distinct from routine end-of-month scarcity. The defining feature was uncertainty: Unlike the predictable timing of normal benefit depletion, the shutdown introduced doubt about whether and when benefits would arrive, amplifying fear and distress. One participant articulated the difference explicitly by contrasting the shutdown with a prior administrative lapse: “It was similar to when you forgot to reapply, and then you have to wait for them to give it to you… it was a lot worse, though, because I didn’t know when I was gonna get them. When I go and I have to reapply… I know if I go tomorrow and I put in my paperwork, at least in a day or so, my stuff will be up. With the government shutdown, we didn’t know when they were gonna be back… I was worried that we weren’t gonna get any, and how I was gonna really eat this month” (participant 17, age 38, male, Black, 74% pre-shutdown adherence). Others described being caught off guard (“It just caught us off guard. We wasn’t expecting it” [participant 14, age 30, male, Black, 67% pre-shutdown adherence]) and the acute distress the uncertainty produced (“It was nerve-racking” [participant 2, age 56, female, Black, 93% pre-shutdown adherence]).

Uncertainty translated into intensified behavioral responses to scarcity. Reliance on emergency food systems increased beyond what participants used during typical cycle-related shortfalls, sometimes requiring multiple food bank visits: “I had already went to three food banks. That Saturday, I was on my way to another one, and that's when [the benefits came in]” (participant 3, age 63, female, multiracial, 92% pre-shutdown adherence). Participants also described financial trade-offs during the disruption, including diverting cash that would otherwise be reserved for rent, bills, or transportation to food expenses: “Money got real tight—real, real tight—because I had to take money and buy food” (participant 11, age 61, female, Black, 68% pre-shutdown adherence); “I had to spend money — spend my check, spend cash on food, whereas that cash could have been spent to a bill” (participant 8, age 29, female, Black, 91% pre-shutdown adherence). One participant described drawing down savings: “[it was] a little scary because we had to dip into some savings stuff to get food… But I mean things are really tight, so we’re just trying to be as sparing as possible” (participant 9, age 59, male, Black, 99% pre-shutdown adherence).

The uncertainty also compounded the cognitive and emotional burden participants had described experiencing during the routine SNAP cycle. Where the monthly cycle produced fatigue and stress, the disruption produced acute distress, heightened worry, and a diminished sense of control. These experiences interacted with the same mechanisms that had operated during the end-of-month scarcity affecting HIV self-management: food unavailability affecting medication tolerability, disrupted meal-linked dosing routines, and depleted cognitive capacity for self-management and clinical engagement. One participant directly linked the disruption to medication-taking: “When the government shutdown came, it was hard for me to take my medication… I couldn’t eat, because I didn’t have money to buy it” (participant 5, age 37, female, Black, 88% pre-shutdown adherence). Another described the broader sense of dysregulation: “I feel like it was just the inconsistency of everything, just like the overall stress of it just being one more thing and needing to feel a sense of control, even though it's like – I really can’t explain why, just that I haven’t, and I haven’t been able to – even though I want to get back into the routine of taking [my medication], I just haven’t been able to do that” (participant 18, age 42, male, White, 8% pre-shutdown adherence).

Across these accounts, the qualitatively distinct feature of the shutdown was not the material scarcity itself (participants were already experienced in navigating cyclical scarcity), but the introduction of uncertainty about benefit timing. Uncertainty layered on top of routine scarcity intensified the behavioral, cognitive, and emotional mechanisms participants described during the monthly cycle, with consequences extending to medication adherence and broader self-care.

Theme 4: community, clinical, and informal supports partially buffered the consequences of routine scarcity and acute disruption

Across both routine cycle scarcity and the November 2025 disruption, participants described community, clinical, and informal supports as partial buffers. Three forms of support were prominent. The first was the community and institutional response—food banks, non-profits, and local organizations that intensified outreach during the shutdown: “The food bank and all of the different non-profits in the city, they came together and got different foods and stuff like that for the community” (participant 14, age 30, male, Black, 67% pre-shutdown adherence). The second was informal support from family and friends, which several participants identified as essential: “I've been depending on – my one friend has been helping me eat… Had he not done that, I would probably be hungry” (participant 17, age 38, male, Black, 74% pre-shutdown adherence). The third was household-level pooling of resources, including savings and shared funds: “Dipping into savings and then we just pooled money together to get what we needed” (participant 9, age 59, male, Black, 99% pre-shutdown adherence). Clinical supports—case management, care coordination, and clinic-based outreach—were also described as important when participants faced escalating barriers.

Supports were not uniform in their reach. Participants with stronger informal networks or financial reserves managed the disruption with less acute distress; those without such networks were more exposed. Even where supports were available, they buffered the material consequences of scarcity (eg, ensuring some food was on hand) more than the cognitive and emotional consequences (eg, uncertainty, worry, decision fatigue), which participants described as persisting alongside emergency food access. Prior experience navigating chronic resource constraints may have conferred coping capacity, but that experience shaped rather than eliminated the burden of the disruption.

Taken together, supports softened but did not erase the consequences of routine cycle scarcity and the November 2025 disruption. The material and the cognitive-emotional components of these consequences were not equally addressed; participants more often described successful coping with the former than with the latter.

Discussion

In this qualitative study of SNAP recipients with HIV, participants described how SNAP benefit timing structures a predictable monthly cycle that starts with a period of relative stability followed by increasing scarcity as time from benefit disbursement increases. In addition, participants highlighted how an unexpected delay in benefit disbursement introduced acute uncertainty that compounded stress, cognitive burden, and short-term coping demands, each with perceived links to ongoing health management routines. Together, these findings suggest that the reliability of food assistance, alongside benefit adequacy, may influence routine-dependent health behaviors. To our knowledge, this study is the first of its kind to explore the acute impacts of an unexpected disruption in SNAP benefits on chronic disease management.

Consistent with prior literature on the SNAP cycle,5,11,14-17 participants described deliberate, price-driven shopping strategies early in the cycle, followed by constrained choices, meal skipping, and16,17 increased reliance on emergency food resources as benefits were exhausted. Our findings extend this literature by documenting how these cyclical patterns directly intersect with medication-taking routines. This is consistent with Seligman et al.'s16 report of increased hypoglycemia-related hospitalizations at the end of the month, while further highlighting ART adherence as another health outcome vulnerable to SNAP benefit cycling.

These prior studies differ from ours in ways that shape interpretation and scope. For example, Seligman et al. used population-level administrative data on diabetes admissions, Kinsey et al. examined chronic disease self-management across a mixed condition sample, and Gosliner et al. documented general population SNAP recipients' shutdown experiences.16-18 Our focus on HIV—a condition requiring daily medication that is often food-dependent—allowed us to document specific mechanisms (medication tolerability, meal-linked dosing routines) that may operate similarly across other conditions requiring consistent daily self-management. Our pairing of qualitative accounts with objective daily adherence monitoring also provides a form of triangulation uncommon in prior SNAP cycle work.

This predictable scarcity required sustained planning, trade-offs, and mental accounting, which participants experienced as tiring and stressful. This burden is compounded by the extremely constrained income levels reported by many participants, with the majority reporting incomes at <75% of the federal poverty level. In this context, SNAP benefits function as a critical stabilizing resource, meaning that even predictable depletion could meaningfully destabilize financial margins. This baseline financial fragility likely amplified the impact of the November 2025 disruption, with unexpected scarcity layered onto already thin margins.

Participants' accounts also clarify pathways through which food insecurity can shape chronic disease self-management. Food availability influenced medication tolerability, daily routines, energy, and motivation, with participants describing delayed doses, altered medication timing, or difficulty maintaining appointments during periods of hunger and scarcity. While many participants emphasized strong commitment to adherence, their narratives suggest that recurring scarcity creates barriers that can undermine consistency, particularly for conditions that require daily routines and sustained follow-up.

The November 2025 shutdown-related benefit delay was described as distinct from end-of-month scarcity well documented in existing literature.6-14 While Gosliner et al.18 documented participant experiences during the 2018-2019 government shutdown, their work focused primarily on food security and spending behavior rather than chronic disease management or clinical outcomes. Our findings extend this work by demonstrating how uncertainty surrounding the timing of benefit restoration compounded cognitive burden beyond material scarcity alone. While the monthly cycle involves predictable depletion that requires strategic planning and mental accounting, the shutdown introduced uncertainty about whether and when benefits would arrive. The rapid recalculations and financial trade-offs reported by participants illustrate how unexpected benefit disruptions impose a dual burden: They not only deplete material resources but also consume the cognitive bandwidth needed to maintain the routines essential for chronic disease management. As a result, the health risks of safety-net instability may extend beyond nutritional deficits, operating through psychological distress and decision fatigue that undermine treatment adherence. Even when participants did not report immediate medication adherence lapses, they described how the convergence of food insecurity and acute distress reduced available cognitive capacity for self-care, disrupted routines, and made medication-taking more difficult. This suggests a mechanism by which unpredictable disruptions in benefit timing—distinct from routine scarcity—may increase health-related vulnerabilities for people with chronic diseases.

Taken together, participants' narratives point to a mechanism linking benefit timing to health: (1) predictable resource depletion across the month increases planning demands and constrains choices; (2) unexpected timing disruptions add uncertainty, augmenting cognitive load and stress; and (3) these combined pressures can destabilize daily routines that support chronic disease management. Objectively measured ART adherence was descriptively lower during the disruption period than in the preceding months, consistent with participants' qualitative accounts. These findings add to an extensive literature linking food insecurity to worse HIV outcomes and complement evidence that economic interventions can improve medication adherence and viral suppression.37,38

Although this study focused on HIV as a high-stakes example of routine-dependent chronic disease self-management, the mechanisms likely transfer to other conditions that depend on daily medication-taking, predictable nutrition, and sustained follow-up. Many commonly prescribed medications for diabetes, hypertension, and mental health conditions, for example, are best tolerated with food or are affected by inadequate nutritional intake, so food scarcity can translate into skipped, delayed, or reduced doses and worse disease control. Beyond medication tolerability, disrupted food access creates competing demands—additional trips for emergency food, cognitive load for budgeting, and financial trade-offs against copayments and transportation—that consume the attention and planning capacity required for daily self-management. The cyclical scarcity our participants described in the final week of each SNAP cycle is consistent with prior research documenting end-of-month patterns in hypoglycemia admissions, medication underuse, and acute care utilization across chronic disease populations.15-17 The November 2025 shutdown affected millions of SNAP recipients,4 many of whom manage one or more chronic diseases17; acute, unexpected disruptions of this kind layer on top of the routine cycle and compound the same mechanisms for any recipient whose health depends on a daily management routine.

Notably, while the government shutdown was an acute and highly visible event, the mechanisms it exposed are not unique to shutdowns. The SNAP recipients routinely experience unexpected benefit interruptions due to administrative burden (eg, missed recertification deadlines, delayed paperwork, or processing errors) that produce functionally similar gaps in food access.39 Indeed, several participants in this study described prior episodes of benefit loss due to administrative lapses, and the coping strategies documented here likely recur with any benefit disruption. However, the acute distress was qualitatively distinct: Unlike administrative errors where participants could anticipate a resolution timeline, the shutdown introduced uncertainty about if and when benefits would return. This lack of temporal predictability amplified psychological burden and decision fatigue far beyond routine administrative disruptions.

Participants also described buffering factors that supported continuity of care during both routine scarcity and unanticipated disruption, including social support, case management, community-based resources, and individual strategies such as budgeting and planning. In addition, participants' prior experience navigating chronic resource constraints may have conferred some capacity to manage acute benefit delays during the shutdown. These protective factors point to actionable targets for clinical and policy response. At the delivery-system level, health systems serving populations with high SNAP reliance could operationalize early-warning triggers (eg, announced shutdowns, state payment-system changes, or mass recertification drives) to identify patients whose treatment regimens are most sensitive to food disruption and mobilize case management, connections to food pantries or vouchers, and short-term clinic-based food or medication support preemptively. At the policy level, these findings support protecting SNAP benefits from appropriations-related disruptions through mechanisms such as advance appropriations or contingency authority for USDA during funding lapses. They also suggest that reforms increasing the administrative burden of maintaining SNAP eligibility (eg, shorter recertification intervals, expanded work and reporting requirements, or documentation-intensive reviews) can function as de facto benefit disruptions, with downstream consequences for chronic disease management similar to those documented here.

This study has limitations. It was conducted in one geographic area; while the shutdown was a uniform federal event, the baseline vulnerability to such disruptions varies by state due to differences in administrative stringency (eg, recertification hurdles, work requirements, and emergency protocols). Thus, the observed distress and coping capacity may differ in states with more restrictive policies compared to those with more supportive safety-net infrastructures. Interviews were conducted shortly after the disruption and may not capture longer-term impacts; however, this timing is also a strength, because it minimizes recall bias and captures lived experiences while the disruption, uncertainty, and coping responses were still salient.

Objective adherence data during the disruption period were limited to 9 parent cohort participants (76 person-days) of observation, drawn from incidental overlap between 4-month monitoring windows and the shutdown rather than from a selected subgroup. The pre-shutdown median (72%, N = 54) and during-shutdown median (40%, n = 9) are reported across overlapping but not coextensive samples and should not be interpreted as evidence of a within-person adherence change. These adherence findings provide descriptive context for the qualitative narratives rather than independent statistical evidence of widespread treatment disruption. Our purposive selection of qualitative interviewees with high-quality adherence data could in principle introduce selection bias if data quality is systematically related to participant characteristics; however, the magnitude of exclusion for this reason (6 of 54 parent cohort participants) was modest.

In summary, participants described the monthly SNAP cycle as producing predictable within-month scarcity and cognitive burden, and they experienced a shutdown-related benefit delay as an acute destabilizing event that amplified uncertainty and stress while forcing reliance on emergency resources and financial trade-offs. Participants described mechanisms by which disrupted food access and distress undermined chronic disease self-management, including reduced medication tolerability without food, diminished energy, and disrupted routines. These findings suggest that protecting health in the context of food insecurity may require attention not only to benefit levels but also to the stability and predictability of benefit disbursement, particularly for people living with chronic diseases.
