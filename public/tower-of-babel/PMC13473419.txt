Case Report: Congenital syphilis presenting with respiratory symptoms in an infant
Authors: Yi Feng, Yupeng Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCongenital syphilis is preventable but has diverse clinical manifestations. Although rhinitis or respiratory symptoms may be among the initial complaints, symptomatic congenital syphilis may already present with overt multisystem disease involving the skin, liver, hematologic system, lungs, skeleton, kidneys, and central nervous system. We report a male infant aged 1 month and 18 days who presented with nasal congestion and rhinorrhea. Initial examination and investigations revealed diffuse desquamation involving the axillae, buttocks, perineum, palms, and soles, together with clustered rashes. Additional findings included abdominal distension, hepatomegaly, pallor, anemia, thrombocytopenia, systemic inflammation, liver dysfunction, proteinuria and hematuria, pneumonia, long-bone periosteal proliferation, and cerebrospinal fluid abnormalities. Maternal history revealed untreated syphilis before and during pregnancy. The infant tested positive by the Treponema pallidum particle agglutination assay (TPPA), with a toluidine red unheated serum test (TRUST) titer of 1:512. These findings supported early congenital syphilis with multisystem involvement. He received intravenous penicillin G sodium at 50,000 U/kg per dose every 6 h for 14 days, together with supportive care. During treatment, he developed a fever consistent with a Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction. Clinical and laboratory abnormalities improved, and serial testing showed a progressive decline in the non-treponemal titer.ConclusionCongenital syphilis should be considered promptly when rhinitis or respiratory symptoms coexist with desquamating lesions, hepatomegaly or hepatopathy, anemia, thrombocytopenia, or other systemic abnormalities. Complete physical examination and review of maternal serology are especially important when prenatal care or transfer of maternal medical information has been incomplete. Early recognition, penicillin treatment, and structured follow-up are essential to prevent late sequelae.

Introduction

Congenital syphilis results from transplacental transmission of Treponema pallidum and remains an important preventable cause of fetal and infant morbidity. Despite established prenatal screening and effective penicillin treatment, cases persist when maternal infection is missed, inadequately treated, or not followed during pregnancy (1–6).

Clinical recognition can be difficult because many affected infants are asymptomatic at birth, and early manifestations are often nonspecific. These may include rhinitis, rash, hepatosplenomegaly, jaundice, anemia, thrombocytopenia, pneumonitis, long-bone abnormalities, and cerebrospinal fluid involvement (7–10). Rhinitis is a recognized early manifestation but should not be interpreted in isolation. When respiratory symptoms coexist with desquamating lesions, hepatomegaly or hepatopathy, anemia, thrombocytopenia, or other organ involvement, the presentation should be recognized as multisystem disease. Congenital syphilis should then be considered promptly.

We describe a Tibetan infant from a remote area of the Tibet Autonomous Region who presented with prominent nasal congestion. Initial evaluation also showed severe cutaneous, hepatic, hematologic, pulmonary, skeletal, renal, and cerebrospinal fluid involvement. Thus, respiratory symptoms served as the clinical entry point rather than an isolated or subtle manifestation. The case emphasizes complete physical examination and maternal serologic review when prenatal care is irregular or caregivers are unaware of the implications of maternal test results.

Case description

A male infant aged 1 month and 18 days was admitted with nasal congestion and rhinorrhea. He was born at 37 weeks and 4 days of gestation as the first child of the mother's second pregnancy. Birth weight was 2.405 kg, and admission weight was 2.89 kg. The infant was exclusively breastfed. Maternal records documented positive syphilis serology with a recorded titer of 1:4 before pregnancy and positive treponemal serology again at approximately 8 weeks of gestation. The mother received no anti-syphilitic treatment during pregnancy. However, the assay used for the recorded titer was not identified, and a quantitative maternal non-treponemal titer at delivery was unavailable.

Physical examination revealed widespread rash and desquamation. Desquamation involved the axillae, buttocks, perianal and perineal regions, palms, and soles; denuded areas appeared dark red. Clustered rashes affected the buttocks, perineum, and limbs, without exudation, ulceration, induration, or edema. The conjunctivae and nail beds were pale. The abdomen was distended, and the liver was soft and palpable 3 cm below the costal margin (Figure 1).

Clinical manifestations of congenital syphilis in the infant. Extensive desquamation of the palms (A) and soles (B), dark-red denuded skin (C), clustered rashes (D), and abdominal distension (E).

The infant did not undergo syphilis-directed evaluation or receive prophylaxis after birth.

Diagnostic assessment

Initial laboratory testing showed leukocytosis, anemia, thrombocytopenia, elevated high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, mild hyponatremia, elevated liver enzymes, hypoalbuminemia, coagulation abnormalities, proteinuria and hematuria, and increased cerebrospinal fluid cell count and protein. Selected key laboratory values at the initial evaluation are summarized in Table 2. Blood culture was negative. Infant syphilis testing was positive by the Treponema pallidum particle agglutination assay (TPPA), and the toluidine red unheated serum test (TRUST) titer was 1:512, numerically 128-fold higher than the recorded maternal titer of 1:4. Because the maternal titer was not measured at delivery and the assay used previously was unidentified, this difference could not be interpreted as a formal fourfold maternal-infant titer comparison (11, 12).

Chest imaging showed bilateral pneumonia, with marked hypoxemia, a room-air oxygen saturation of 75%, and an initial arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO₂) of 35 mmHg. Skeletal imaging demonstrated extensive periosteal proliferation involving the long bones and ribs. Periosteal thickening affected the femurs, tibias, humeri, radii, and clavicles, together with metaphyseal changes (Figure 2). Abdominal imaging showed fluid accumulation. Echocardiography, abdominal ultrasonography, and cranial magnetic resonance imaging showed no major abnormalities.

Despite the absence of a formal paired maternal-infant titer comparison, early congenital syphilis was strongly supported by untreated maternal infection and the infant's markedly elevated TRUST titer. The characteristic multisystem clinical, laboratory, radiographic, and cerebrospinal fluid findings provided further support.

Therapeutic intervention

The infant received intravenous penicillin G sodium at 145,000 U per dose every 6 h, equivalent to approximately 50,000 U/kg per dose based on the admission weight of 2.89 kg. Treatment continued for 14 consecutive days as definitive therapy for symptomatic early congenital syphilis with cerebrospinal fluid abnormalities (13). Cefoperazone-sulbactam (150 mg intravenously every 8 h) was administered empirically because concomitant bacterial pneumonia or sepsis could not initially be excluded. This decision was supported by bilateral pulmonary infiltrates and markedly elevated inflammatory markers. Vitamin K1 was administered for marked hypofibrinogenemia and a prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) to reduce bleeding risk. Intravenous immunoglobulin was given for severe thrombocytopenia in the setting of systemic infection. Human albumin was infused for severe hypoalbuminemia, and leukocyte-depleted red blood cells were transfused for anemia. Nutritional and fluid support was adjusted according to feeding tolerance. Respiratory support was escalated from low-flow nasal-cannula oxygen to non-invasive positive-pressure ventilation because of persistent hypoxemia and respiratory distress. Within 24 h after penicillin was initiated, the infant developed a fever of up to 38.5 °C. Given its temporal relationship to treatment and the high non-treponemal titer, a Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction was clinically suspected (14). The infant was managed with close monitoring, physical cooling, and supportive care. No prophylactic or therapeutic systemic corticosteroids were administered.

Follow-up and outcomes

The infant's respiratory symptoms, skin lesions, inflammatory markers, hematologic abnormalities, and biochemical indices improved after treatment and supportive care. Hepatomegaly regressed, liver enzyme concentrations decreased, and serum albumin increased, indicating progressive recovery of liver function. Monthly serologic follow-up showed a progressive decline in the non-treponemal titer. At the final documented follow-up, TPPA remained positive and TRUST was negative. The child continued regular follow-up and was reported to be developing appropriately. Continued surveillance remains necessary because late manifestations can emerge despite early treatment.

Discussion

This case shows that nasal congestion may bring an infant to medical attention even when initial evaluation reveals overt multisystem congenital syphilis. Respiratory symptoms coexisted with diffuse desquamation, hepatomegaly and hepatic dysfunction, anemia, thrombocytopenia, pneumonia, long-bone periostitis, renal abnormalities, and cerebrospinal fluid involvement. The presentation therefore reflected disseminated congenital infection rather than isolated or subtle respiratory disease. Congenital syphilis should be prioritized when rhinitis accompanies cutaneous, hepatic, hematologic, skeletal, or neurologic abnormalities.

Abdominal distension, hepatomegaly, marked hypoalbuminemia, and coagulopathy raised concern for impaired hepatic synthetic function and systemic infection-associated coagulation dysfunction. Together, these findings were compatible with syphilitic hepatic involvement in early congenital syphilis. Delle Cave et al. described hepatic manifestations ranging from isolated hepatomegaly to cholestasis and acute liver failure in affected infants. They emphasized that early recognition and treatment are essential to prevent rapid progression and fatal outcomes (15). Accordingly, infants with suspected congenital syphilis and hepatomegaly or abnormal liver indices require careful assessment and serial monitoring. Monitoring should include transaminases, bilirubin, albumin, prothrombin time (PT), international normalized ratio (INR), APTT, and fibrinogen.

The skeletal and pulmonary findings were also informative. Long-bone radiographic abnormalities are common in symptomatic early congenital syphilis and may provide a diagnostic clue when skin findings are subtle or overlooked (8, 16, 17). Pneumonia-like presentations may also delay diagnosis. Although classic “white pneumonia” is uncommon, pulmonary involvement should be considered when pneumonia coexists with rash, hepatosplenomegaly, anemia, thrombocytopenia, or maternal syphilis risk.

Maternal history is central to the diagnosis of congenital syphilis. Current guidance bases evaluation on maternal treatment status, infant examination, laboratory and radiographic findings, and comparison of maternal and infant non-treponemal titers. Prevention begins with timely maternal screening and treatment. Syphilis serology should be obtained at the first prenatal visit and repeated at 28 weeks of gestation and delivery for women at increased risk or in high-prevalence settings. Penicillin G is the antimicrobial with established efficacy for treating maternal infection and preventing fetal infection. Treatment should be appropriate for the maternal disease stage. In this case, irregular prenatal care, incomplete communication of maternal serologic results, and absent maternal treatment represented a preventable gap in the maternal-infant care pathway (11, 18, 19). The mother had documented but untreated syphilis before and during pregnancy, while the infant had a high TRUST titer and characteristic multisystem findings. This combination supported immediate treatment rather than watchful waiting.

This report integrates maternal history, infant physical findings, serology, laboratory abnormalities, radiographs, treatment response, and serologic follow-up. However, as a single case, it cannot establish the frequency of presentations initially dominated by respiratory symptoms. Maternal treatment and serologic follow-up after delivery could not be independently verified. Direct detection of T. pallidum by dark-field microscopy or lesion polymerase chain reaction (PCR), as well as placental testing and cerebrospinal fluid Venereal Disease Research Laboratory (VDRL) testing, was unavailable, limiting microbiologic confirmation of organ-specific involvement. Nevertheless, the overall clinical pattern and treatment response were highly compatible with early congenital syphilis.

Conclusion

Congenital syphilis should be prioritized when an infant has rhinitis or other respiratory symptoms together with desquamating lesions, hepatomegaly or hepatopathy, anemia, thrombocytopenia, or other systemic findings. Complete cutaneous, abdominal, skeletal, and neurologic assessment and review of maternal serology are especially important when prenatal care or transfer of maternal records has been incomplete. Strengthening prenatal screening and stage-appropriate maternal penicillin treatment, together with systematic evaluation of exposed neonates before discharge, is essential to prevent delayed diagnosis and severe infant disease.
