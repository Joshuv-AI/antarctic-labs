Health-related quality of life and adverse events during 14 days after receiving a live-attenuated influenza vaccine in Japanese children 4–15 years of age
Authors: Antonio Di Lorenzo, Nami Kurosawa, Akiyo Maeda, Sayaka Yoshida, Matteo Ferrara, Taito Kitano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNo data have been collected regarding the quality of life after live-attenuated influenza vaccine (LAIV). Our study investigates LAIV’s impact on the quality of life of children. This prospective, questionnaire-based study targeted children aged 4–15 y. Guardians responded to daily surveys for 16 d, starting the day before vaccination. The questionnaire included the EuroQoL 5-dimension Youth 3-Level (EQ-5D-Y-3L) tool and the EuroQoL Visual Analogue Scale (EQ-VAS) monitoring fluctuations of health-related quality of life (HRQL) and adverse events following immunization (AEFIs). Time series analysis and area under the curve (AUC) calculation were used to investigate the scores’ changes. Final analysis involved 92 patients who responded to at least half of the questionnaire’s iterations, including both day 7 and 14 after vaccination. One or more AEFIs were reported for 78 children (73.91%), with generally mild symptoms. In 30 cases, a significant impact on daily activities was described, and one serious AEFI was reported consisting of severe headache and pharyngodynia with fever, leading to emergency room access. EQ-VAS score dropped on the first 2 d after vaccination and recovered thereafter, reaching (and even exceeding) the pre-vaccination value on day 10. The EQ-5D-Y-3L score also declined, however, only on day 1 and recovered faster, reaching the pre-vaccination value already on day 5, undulating around the baseline level until day 10. A net loss of 0.01 quality-adjusted life days was identified (AUC: −0.0138). Our analysis showed a positive LAIV safety profile. Data from larger populations are needed to support clinical recommendations, promoting vaccine acceptance and fighting hesitancy.

Introduction

Influenza is currently recognized as a major determinant of morbidity and mortality worldwide.1 Its impact on public health is related to both yearly seasonal epidemics and rarer pandemics.2–4 In Japan, the last two seasons were characterized by the resurgence of influenza after the progressive lift of restrictions related to the Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) pandemic. In particular, according to official estimates of the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare, during the 2024/2025 season, 1,110,860 confirmed flu cases were reported by sentinel points of care.5 A sharp increase was also reported for the 2025/2026 season, when over 5 million cases were signaled.6

While epidemics classically impact older subjects and high-risk patients,1,7–11 influenza pandemics usually show different behaviors, with greater morbidity and mortality in younger patients. This difference might be caused by either the higher number of cases observed during pandemic instances or by increased severity of pandemic influenza viruses.12,13 However, recent studies tend to subvert this paradigm, showing a significant burden of seasonal influenza in children with no significant risk factors.14,15

Vaccination currently represents a highly cost-effective strategy for influenza prevention,16–18 as well as a tool for pandemic preparedness.1,2,19 Vaccination against influenza reduces utilization of healthcare services and therefore public expenditure.20,21 In addition to this, vaccination has been proven to positively impact sickness-related absenteeism, which is especially relevant when healthcare workers are involved in vaccination rollout,22 but also prevents economic losses in non-healthcare workplaces23 and improves school attendance during flu season, as well as hindering circulation of influenza in general due to children often acting as vectors for the spread of influenza to adults, especially within the same household.20–24

Vaccinating children has further implications. Apart from preventing the disease and its potential complications,14,15,25,26 it reduces the tendency of improper antibiotic use by reducing the number of viral respiratory infection for which antibiotics are improperly used, and therefore the risk of antibiotic resistance selection.27,28 Moreover, despite limited evidence, influenza is currently suspected to contribute to autoimmune pathways in the human body, promoting inflammation and therefore increasing the risk of developing diabetes mellitus, blood vessel damage with subsequent atherogenesis, and neurological conditions.29 Another important aspect of vaccination is to protect unvaccinated population through herd immunity (community immunity).30 Finally, children with clinical conditions characterized by an increased risk of respiratory illness such as asthma are especially vulnerable to complications in case of influenza and especially benefit from vaccination.31

The currently available live attenuated influenza vaccine (LAIV) was first introduced in Japan during the 2024/2025 flu season.32 It is administered as a nasal mist, and despite a real-world study by Rigamonti et al.33 estimating a real-world effectiveness of 43% (95% confidence interval, 32%−53%), meta-analyses have shown inconsistent results when comparing its effectiveness with that of the inactivated influenza vaccine (IIV).34–36 However, thanks to its different administration route, LAIV better mimics natural infection, inducing a robust IgA response as well as T-cell activation.37,38

Studies on LAIV have showed a consistently good safety profile. In particular, despite a significant odds of nasal congestion, myalgia and headache following the product’s administration, serious adverse events were reported as rare and generally undergoing full resolution.39 Moreover, LAIV has been proven effective and safe even in children suffering from asthma and other chronic conditions expected to possibly hinder the recipient’s immune response to a live respiratory pathogen.40

Despite the growing body of evidence regarding LAIV, little is known about its impact on quality of life of pediatric patients. The impact evaluation of vaccination on quality of life is crucial for comprehensive vaccine assessment such as benefit–risk and cost-effectiveness analyses. Apart from assessing the direct effects of a vaccine’s administration, quality of life outcomes can indeed be used to derive tools such as utility values, which are crucial for successive health economics evaluations.41,42 In this study, we aim to assess changes in perceived and objective quality of life, as reported by guardians of children receiving LAIV.

Methods

Study design, population and enrollment strategy

This is a prospective, population-based questionnaire study set in Nara Prefecture General Medical Center (Nara-ken Sōgō Iryō Center, Nara, Japan). The study population was represented by children aged 4–15 y who were immunized with LAIV during the 2025/2026 flu season. Enrollment started on 1 October 2025 and lasted until 31 December 2025. In observance of Japanese recommendations, a single dose of LAIV was offered to all subjects irrespective of their age.32 The administered vaccine was a trivalent product containing the A/Victoria/4897/2022 (H1N1) pdm09, A/Croatia/10136RV/2023 (H3N2) and B/Austria/1359417/2021 (B/Victoria lineage) viral subtypes.

Clear information was provided to all guardians regarding both the vaccine and the possibility of participating in the study, clearly stating that refusal of the latter would have had no influence on the vaccine’s offer to their child. Consent forms were provided and responses were collected. As an incentive to participate, a 2,000 JPY bonus was provided using hospital funds.

Before administration of the vaccine, a questionnaire was also administered regarding the child’s conditions during the previous day. The questionnaire was not paper-based, as it was built via the SurveyMonkey® tool. The first instance of it was accessible via QR-code. Study staff was provided with personal identification and a password in order to guarantee secure access and safeguard the patients’ personal data. Upon acceptance of participation, subjects were asked for their e-mail address to send daily links to answer their child’s health conditions.

The vaccine was administered by trained staff via nasal spray. Guardians of vaccinated children were instructed to wait for at least 15 min in the center’s lobby and report to the staff in case of any unforeseen occurrence. After returning home, guardians were sent daily instances of the study questionnaire and were asked to fill them in (details about the questionnaire content are provided in the following subsection). Questionnaire administrations stopped at day 14 post-immunization (16 instances in total) in order to capture possible late events related to the vaccine’s live‑attenuated nature.38

Exclusion criteria were the following: Known allergy to components of the study vaccine;Lack of fluency in Japanese language, hindering understanding of information pertaining to the vaccine and/or the study, as well as of the questionnaire;Impossibility to fill in the study questionnaire due to lack of a device allowing access to the online survey tool;Severe uncontrolled asthma and immune depression which might increase the risk for adverse events due to their interference with response to a live attenuated organism;Refusal to participate to the study.

Study questionnaire

The study questionnaire asked guardians of enrolled children to evaluate adverse events following immunization (AEFIs) in terms of both presence and severity.38 Severity is classified for all events as follows: grade 0, absence of the event; grade 1, mild adverse event with no significant impact on everyday life’s activities; grade 2, moderate adverse event with significant impact on everyday life’s activities; grade 3, severe adverse events, without need for emergency room access and/or hospitalization; grade 4, severe adverse events, requiring emergency room access and/or hospitalization. The sole exception was represented by fever, which was categorized based on the maximum temperature measured by the parent (grade 1: 37.0°C−37.9°C; grade 2: 38.0°C−38.9°C; grade 3: 39.0°C−39.9°C; grade 4: ≥40°C).

Further, the EuroQoL 5-dimensions youth 3-levels (EQ-5D-Y-3L) questionnaire43 was employed to assess the impact of vaccination on daily health-related quality of life (HRQL) scale and to calculate a utility value for the vaccination. The questionnaire specifically targets children aged 4 to 15 y, with different versions dedicated to younger and older subjects. More precisely, five dimensions are evaluated on a three-level scale: ability to walk independently, ability to carry out self-care activities, ability to conduct a normal daily routine, presence of bodily pain and/or discomfort, and feelings of anxiety/depression. In addition to this, the EuroQoL-Visual Analog Scale (EQ-VAS) asked caregivers to describe their child’s current general wellbeing with a score ranging from 0 (worst possible condition) to 100 (best possible condition). A full version of the daily questionnaire is reported as Additional Material 1. Adverse events and quality of life data were collected from day −1 to day +14.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were restricted to subjects who answered at least half of the questionnaire’s iterations (day of enrollment plus at least seven other days, for a total of 8 d out of 16). Missing data were not imputed in this study and excluded pairwise from analysis. Quantitative variables were described as means (standard deviations) (SD), while qualitative variables were synthesized as proportions (percentages). The raw score based on responses to the EQ-5D-Y-3L questionnaire was transformed into utility values using a Japanese value set, based on the work of Shiroiwa et al.44 Utility value ranges from 0.000 (worst possible functioning in all dimensions) to 1.000 (best possible functioning in all dimensions) and is employed in order to derive a single score to be used in cost-effectiveness analysis, while also allowing for time series analysis. The utility value and EQ-VAS of each survey from day 0 onwards were then confronted with baseline levels (responses pertaining to the day before immunization), subtracting the latter from the former in order to verify daily changes of both scores.

Inferential analysis was carried out exclusively for subjects who provided responses on both day 7 and 14. Standardized response means (SRMs) and Cohen’s effect size were calculated for changes of utility value and EQ-VAS scores on day 7 and 14, and effect size was calculated for each of these changes, and 95% CI was computed. Subjects were divided into two main groups based on AEFIs’ severity, with group 1 including subjects with no report of adverse events (maximum grade: 0) or with mild adverse events only (maximum grade: 1) over the whole study period. Group 2, on the other hand, included subjects who reported adverse events which impacted significantly their daily life and activities (grade 2, 3, and 4) at least once during follow-up.

For each subject, further, an AEFI score was computed based on the number and grade of adverse events, with each reported AEFI providing a score equal to its grade. Pearson’s correlation test was used to verify the correlation among changes in utility value and EQ-VAS scores on day 7 and 14 and AEFI score. Finally, time series analysis was performed on both the VAS score and utility value, using direct and indirect autocorrelation with lag = 1 d in order to study each score’s correlation with its own previous values, and loss/gain of quality-adjusted life days (QALDs) was calculated via the area under the curve (AUC). For clarity, direct autocorrelation refers to the direct effect of previous values of a variable on current ones, while indirect autocorrelation studies the effect of previous values arising from intermediate time points. Sensibility analysis was performed by confronting the overall QALDs with those of the first and second week of investigation.

Three decimal figures were used for EQ-5D-Y-3L scores only, in order to improve readability of results. A two-sided p-value < .05 was considered indicative of statistical significance. However, in order to account for the risk of type-I error inflation related to iteration of tests, a p-value < .01 was deemed appropriate for single tests. The study database was built via Microsoft Excel®, while all statistical analyses were carried out via STATA 18®.

Results

During the recruitment period, guardians of 122 children accepted to have their child vaccinated and to participate in the study. However, significant dropout was observed due to unresponsiveness, with missing information about over 50% of the study days for 28 subjects. Therefore, 98 children were included in the analysis (response rate: 80.33%).

The baseline VAS score was 90.95 ± 12.34, while the baseline EQ-5D-Y-3L score was 0.968 ± 0.029. On day 7, the scores reached 90.38 ± 13.64 and 0.968 ± 0.027, respectively, while on day 14, the VAS score was 91.95 ± 13.79 and the EQ-5D-Y-3L score was 0.969 ± 0.034. Guardians of 92 children provided their responses on both day 7 and 14 and were therefore included in inferential analysis. Out of them, 14 reported no AEFIs at all during the surveillance period, while 48 reported only mild AEFIs. Group 1 was therefore composed of 62 children. Group 2, instead, counted 30 subjects, 29 of which with reports of moderate-severity adverse events with impact on daily life’s activities. The guardians of the last subject reported serious adverse events with need for emergency room access (grade 4). In detail, this event consisted of an episode of pharyngodynia, headache and hyperpyrexia with fever exceeding 39°C, occurring on day 9 post-immunization. The child accessed emergency room due to the first two conditions, which were therefore assigned a 4-point severity score as per previously mentioned criteria. However, the subject was reportedly not hospitalized and all symptoms regressed within 48 h except for pharyngeal pain, which lasted until day 13, when it was not reported anymore. The child had experienced a full recovery of their daily activities by the end of the surveillance period. Overall, the reporting rate of AEFIs in the study population was 73.91%, with an average AEFI score of 12.55 (SD: 13.74). On day 7 and 14, the average change of the VAS score amounted to −0.45 (SD: 14.98) (SRM: −0.03) and 0.95 (SD: 15.76) (SRM: 0.06), respectively. Cohen’s effect size between Group 1 and 2 amounted to 0.09 (95% CI, −0.34–0.53) on day 7, and to 0.14 (95% CI, −0.30–0.57) on day 14. As far as the utility value based on EQ-5D-Y-3L is concerned, on day 7, a change of 0.001 (SD: 0.032) (SRM: 0.01) was observed, while on day 14, the average change amounted to 0.001 (SD: 0.040) (SRM: 0.03). Cohen’s effect size was −0.30 (95% CI, −0.74–0.14) on day 7 and −0.04 (95% CI, −0.48–0.39) on day 14.

Pearson’s correlation test showed significant direct correlation among the change of both the VAS and EQ-5D-Y-3L score both on day 7 and 14. Significance was retained for most correlations even considering type-I error’s inflation risk. On the other hand, the AEFI score was only significantly correlated with the change of the VAS score on day 7 (inverse correlation), and significance was lost after correction. Discussion

In our study, we investigated quality of life fluctuations following LAIV administration in children aged 4–15 y. To do so, guardians were invited to respond to a questionnaire including two separate scales for the assessment of overall fitness of children. EQ-VAS was employed to reflect the caregiver’s subjective perception of the child’s wellbeing, while the EQ-5D-Y-3L scale was used as a more objective tool, relying on actual data regarding self-sufficiency and adjustment to daily needs and activities. Out of an initial population of 122 subjects, over 50% of the daily questionnaire instances were completed for 98 children, and 92 were included in the final analysis due to completion of at least 8 d of the questionnaire, including at least day 7 and 14.

Mild AEFIs were fairly common. Moderate adverse events impacting daily routine were reported by approximately 30% of 29 subjects, and only one serious adverse event was identified, which was fully resolved by the end of the study period. Of note, a 4-point severity score was reported for two of the child’s symptoms (pharyngodynia and headache) due to the need for emergency room access, while fever scored 3 points based on the temperature reported by the parent (over 39.0°C, but less than 40.0°C).

Reporting rates appear to be significantly higher than those identified in existing evidence, in which the incidence of AEFIs is usually around 50%, even when active surveillance is employed.39,45–47 This could stem from the prolonged surveillance period, which may have selected the study population in terms of greater attention or concern of guardians about their child’s health status, thus leading to the report of very mild occurrences and driving reporting rates to higher levels. Indeed, serious adverse events’ reporting rates are in line with existing literature,45–47 therefore confirming LAIV’s positive safety profile.

In addition to this, an immediate drop was highlighted for both the VAS and EQ-5D-Y-3L scores during the first 2 d following immunization (Graph 1 and 2). This contradicts known evidence regarding live attenuated vaccines, since live pathogens usually take more than 48 h to determine noticeable adverse events in vaccinated subjects due to slower interaction with the host’s immune system.38,47 These were mostly mild and quickly improving AEFIs following LAIV. As confirmation of this aspect, both curves undergo a rapid improvement, with especially steep slopes for the VAS score from day 4 to 5 and day 9 to 10. A new fall on day 8 following immunization potentially manifesting the actual onset of vaccine-related events.

Our analysis highlighted a direct correlation among changes in the EQ-5D-Y-3L and VAS score on day 7 and 14 of the surveillance period, which is expected due to the guardians’ perception of their child’s wellbeing is influenced by the latter’s actual health status. However, it is interesting to notice that these scores’ changes were not correlated with the number and/or severity of experienced adverse events (p-value > .01 for all correlations). This phenomenon might have multiple explanations: first of all, the low severity of observed AEFIs might have rendered them uninfluential in determining actual changes in the study population’s quality of life; on the other hand, fluctuations in quality of life might have been exaggerated by caregivers themselves. The current literature has highlighted that patient-caregiver agreement in the EQ-5D-Y-3L questionnaire is not always ideal and may be hindered by psychosocial factors not included in our analysis.48,49

Individual AEFI scores for each patient could be therefore of limited significance: since they were self-assessed by guardians, the scores assigned to each event may refer to a subjective perception rather than to the actual severity of the event itself (with the sole exception of fever, which is objectively measured, and grade-4 AEFIs, which are classified as such due to a verifiable occurrence of hospitalization/emergency room access). However, the repetition in time of the same assessment allows to strengthen its meaningfulness, similarly to the way VAS scores are usually interpreted in time, rather than in single instances.

The main point of strength of this study is represented by the prolonged investigation of the participants’ quality of life and general health conditions via two separate instruments. Indeed, HR-QL investigations are typically performed at specific time points during follow-up, rather than employing daily instances. While these tools might be of limited significance by individual perception if used at a single point in time, their daily iteration minimizes this risk by assessing reported changes and provides a more accurate understanding of the impact of vaccination on daily life and activities. Using 16 time points (day −1 to day 14 after vaccination) made it possible to highlight both immediate and delayed AEFIs, thus providing a comprehensive overview of the vaccine’s safety profile. The use of EQ-5D-Y-3L instrument allowed for the estimation of utility value. The utility value data associated with pediatric vaccination is scarcely available in other studies.

Our study does present weaknesses to be addressed. First of all, the study population’s numerosity is not ideal. While the power and sample size calculation was not implemented in this study, the power of detecting a significant difference was expected to be small. This led to excluding data regarding specific adverse event reports, since their generalizability would be low and a population below 100 individuals would determine a significant underrepresentation of uncommon and rare adverse events. We could not adjust by a range of potential confounders (e.g., demographics and comorbidities) partly due to the small sample size. Antibody titers were not measured in this study, and the association of immunogenicity and quality of life could not be evaluated. However, to our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate quality of life following vaccination with LAIV in children between 4 and 15 y of age.

An additional issue is represented by the employed methods of data collection. As already mentioned, a significant number of guardians missed at least one iteration of the study questionnaire, thus generating missing data. An active surveillance system based on daily phone calls by staff members might have been another option;50 however, this would have been unfeasible due to organizational factors, and the web-based collection method was deemed more acceptable by caregivers due to convenience and perceived safeguard of privacy.

Further, the EQ-5D-Y-5L questionnaire is currently available, and it could further assess HRQL over five levels rather than three.51 However, utility value could not be estimated from the 5-level version during the 2025/2026 flu vaccination campaign. Future analysis should draw insight from the updated version of this tool, possibly comparing it with its previous iteration. Studies may provide further contribution to this field by adding data regarding comorbidities of vaccinated children, which were not made available to us and therefore could not be included in the present analysis. Moreover, information regarding previous influenza vaccination could allow to verify whether naïve subjects’ response differs from that of previously immunized children. Finally, the lack of causality assessment makes it difficult to properly assess the reported severe adverse event’s meaningfulness in terms of causal association to the vaccine’s administration.

In conclusion, our study highlights a good safety profile of the live attenuated influenza vaccination in children aged 4–15 y. The presence of up-to-date evidence representing local populations is instrumental to the promotion of vaccine uptake, and our research may help build the necessary body of data to reinforce vaccination practices. Japan is, still to date, a sensitive region as far as vaccine hesitancy is concerned, with public opinion often divided between the perception of collective responsibility and skepticism.52 In this context, this study provides a useful reference to be used by healthcare providers to encourage acceptance of vaccination by guardians of school-age children.
