Inexact versions of several block-splitting preconditioners for indefinite least squares problems
Mohaddese Kaveh Shaldehi, Davod Khojasteh Salkuyeh

Abstract




Abstract.
This paper introduces inexact versions of several block-splitting preconditioners for solving the block three-by-three linear systems arising from a special class of indefinite least squares problems. We first establish the convergence conditions for the corresponding stationary iterative methods. Then, it follows that under these conditions, all eigenvalues of the preconditioned matrices are contained within a circle centered at
(1,0)
with radius
1
. This property implies that these preconditioners are effective in accelerating the convergence of the GMRES method. Furthermore, we analyze the eigenpairs of the preconditioned matrices in detail and derive a theoretical upper bound on the number of GMRES iterations for solving the preconditioned systems. Ultimately, numerical experiments reveal the efficacy of the proposed preconditioners.

Keywords
:
Indefinite least squares problem, Block-splitting preconditioner, Stationary iterative method, Preconditioning techniques, Spectral clustering.

AMS Subject Classification
: 65F10, 65F50, 65F08.

1
Introduction

This paper is concerned with the indefinite least squares (ILS) problems of the form

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}(b-Ax)^{T}H_{p,q}(b-Ax),

(1)

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is a given large and sparse matrix with
m\geq n
,
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a given vector, and

H_{p,q}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is the signature matrix of the form

H_{p,q}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{p}&0\\
0&-I_{q}\end{pmatrix},\hskip 18.49988ptp+q=m.

Clearly, when either
p=0
or
q=0
the problem simplifies to the standard least squares (LS) problem. However, when
p,q>0
, the quadratic form in problem (
1
) is indefinite.
Such problems occur in numerous applications, including the solution of total least squares (TLS) problems
[
7
,
19
,
6
]
, and the area of optimization known as robust or
H^{\infty}
smoothing
[
8
,
17
,
6
]
.

The normal equations corresponding to the ILS problem (
1
) can be written as

A^{T}H_{p,q}Ax=A^{T}H_{p,q}b.

(2)

It is clear that unlike the classical LS problem, the ILS problem (
1
) is not guaranteed to have a unique solution. In addition, the Hessian of its objective function is
2A^{T}H_{p,q}A
. Consequently, there exists a unique solution to the ILS problem (
1
) if and only if
A^{T}H_{p,q}A
is symmetric positive definite (SPD). This condition is assumed to hold throughout this paper.

Many research efforts have been focused on numerical solutions to the ILS problem. For small and dense instances of the problem (
1
), direct methods based upon the QR decomposition by using the Householder transformation or the Cholesky factorization are trustworthy
[
5
,
4
,
6
,
20
]
. Nonetheless, for large-scale sparse problems, these methods become excessively costly because of their significant storage needs and computational expenses. Consequently, the development of efficient iterative methods, particularly Krylov subspace methods, has become a primary research focus in recent years.

Among the iteration schemes, matrix-splitting methods represent a classical family of solvers for the ILS problem. For instance, Liu and Liu proposed two block SOR-based algorithms for solving the system associated with the ILS problem
[
10
]
. Subsequently, to accelerate convergence, Song introduced the Unsymmetric SOR (USSOR) method, which employs two independent relaxation parameters
[
18
]
. However, the performance of the USSOR method is highly sensitive to the choice of its relaxation parameters and finding their optimal values remains a major challenge for large-scale systems.

More recently, the range of iterative methods for the ILS problem has further diversified. Zhang and Li have introduced randomized splitting methods
[
23
]
. Subsequently, Meng et al. have constructed several other schemes, including variable-parameter Uzawa method
[
11
]
, enhanced Landweber method with momentum acceleration
[
13
]
, and alternating direction implicit (ADI) method
[
12
]
.
Recently, an effective idea is to reformulate the normal equations (
2
) into a larger block three-by-three linear system, which is solved by GMRES method
[
15
,
16
]
using block-splitting preconditioners. In
[
22
]
, Xin et al. applied the shift-splitting iteration method to the aforementioned block three-by-three linear system and employed the induced preconditioner for this system.
In
[
21
]
, Xin and Meng presented three distinct block-splitting preconditioners. Subsequently, Li et al. proposed another preconditioner of this type
[
9
]
. In what follows, we explain their preconditioning techniques in more detail.

According to the structure of the matrix
H_{p,q}
, we partition the matrix
A
and the vector
b
as

A=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}\\
A_{2}\end{pmatrix},\hskip 9.24994ptb=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}\\
b_{2}\end{pmatrix},

where the matrix
A_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
has full column rank,
A_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times n}
,
b_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and
b_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
.
The reformulation of the ILS problem (
1
) as the following nonsingular block
3\times 3
linear system was first proposed by Xin and Meng
[
21
]

\mathcal{A}\mathbf{x}=\begin{pmatrix}I&A_{1}&0\\
0&P&A_{2}^{T}\\
0&A_{2}&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{1}\\
x\\
\delta_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}\\
A_{1}^{T}b_{1}\\
b_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\tilde{b},

(3)

where
P=A_{1}^{T}A_{1}
and
\delta=(\delta_{1};\delta_{2})=b-Ax
. For this block three-by-three linear system, they constructed three distinct block splittings
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}I&0&0\\
0&P&0\\
0&0&I\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&-A_{1}&0\\
0&0&-A_{2}^{T}\\
0&-A_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}:=\mathcal{M}_{BS1}-\mathcal{N}_{BS1},

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}I&0&0\\
0&P&A_{2}^{T}\\
0&0&I\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&-A_{1}&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&-A_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}:=\mathcal{M}_{BS2}-\mathcal{N}_{BS2},

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}I&A_{1}&0\\
0&P&0\\
0&0&I\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&-A_{2}^{T}\\
0&-A_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}:=\mathcal{M}_{BS3}-\mathcal{N}_{BS3}.

(6)

Subsequently, Li et al. proposed a block upper triangular (BUT) splitting with the aim of minimizing the norm of the difference between the matrix
\mathcal{A}
and the block-splitting preconditioner
[
9
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}I&A_{1}&0\\
0&P&A_{2}^{T}\\
0&0&I\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&-A_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}:=\mathcal{M}_{BUT}-\mathcal{N}_{BUT}.

(7)

Implementing the preconditioners
\mathcal{M}_{BS1},\mathcal{M}_{BS2},\mathcal{M}_{BS3}
, and
\mathcal{M}_{BUT}
requires solving inner linear systems with the coefficient matrix
P=A_{1}^{T}A_{1}
. The main issue is that the matrix
P
is not guaranteed to be well-conditioned, especially when the matrix
A_{1}
is ill-conditioned. Hence, in many large and practical ILS problems, the outer GMRES iterations may fail to reach a sufficiently accurate solution within a prescribed number of iterations, due to the accumulation of errors from solving the inner systems.

To compensate for this weakness, we introduce inexact versions of these block-splitting preconditioners. The main idea is to replace the matrix
P
within the block-splitting preconditioners
\mathcal{M}_{BS1},\mathcal{M}_{BS2},\mathcal{M}_{BS3}
, and
\mathcal{M}_{BUT}
, with a well-conditioned SPD approximation. Our theoretical analysis shows that, under the convergence conditions of the corresponding stationary iterative methods, all the eigenvalues of the preconditioned matrices are located within the circle centered at
(1,0)
with radius 1. This robust spectral clustering leads to accelerated convergence of the GMRES method. Furthermore, numerical experiments demonstrate the efficiency and robustness of the proposed preconditioners in comparison with recent methods.

The following standard notations will be employed throughout this paper. The symbols
I
,
A^{T}
, and
A^{*}
represent the identity matrix of the appropriate size, the transpose of a matrix
A
, and the conjugate transpose of
A
, respectively. The symbol
\|.\|_{2}
represents the Euclidean norm for vectors and the corresponding spectral norm for matrices. The spectrum, spectral radius and null space of
A
are denoted by
\sigma(A)
,
\rho(A)
, and
\mathcal{N}(A)
, respectively.
The spectral condition number of the matrix
A
is denoted by
\kappa(A)
,
which is defined as
\kappa(A)=\|A\|_{2}\|A^{-1}\|_{2}
.
For two column vectors
x
and
y
, we use
(x;y)
to denote the column vector
(x^{T},y^{T})^{T}.
Finally, the notation
A\succ 0
indicates that the matrix
A
is SPD.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
includes introducing our new block-splitting preconditioners, the convergence analysis of the corresponding stationary iterative methods, and implementaion of the proposed preconditioners. In Section
3
, we conduct a detailed spectral analysis of the preconditioned matrices; specifically, we analyze their eigenpairs and derive a theoretical upper bound on the number of GMRES iterations. Section
4
, presents some numerical experiments to illustrate the efficiency of the proposed preconditioners. Finally, Section
5
offers some concluding remarks.

2
The inexact block-splitting preconditioners

In this section, we introduce the inexact block-splitting (IBS) preconditioners. The construction of these preconditioners is based on replacing the matrix
P
in the block-splitting preconditioners
\mathcal{M}_{BS1}
,
\mathcal{M}_{BS2}
,
\mathcal{M}_{BS3}
, and
\mathcal{M}_{BUT}
, with a well-conditioned SPD approximation,
\hat{P}.
In other words, the IBS preconditioners are derived from the following matrix splittings

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}I&0&0\\
0&\hat{P}&0\\
0&0&I\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&-A_{1}&0\\
0&\hat{P}-P&-A_{2}^{T}\\
0&-A_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}:=\mathcal{M}_{1}-\mathcal{N}_{1},

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}I&0&0\\
0&\hat{P}&A_{2}^{T}\\
0&0&I\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&-A_{1}&0\\
0&\hat{P}-P&0\\
0&-A_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}:=\mathcal{M}_{2}-\mathcal{N}_{2},

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}I&A_{1}&0\\
0&\hat{P}&0\\
0&0&I\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&\hat{P}-P&-A_{2}^{T}\\
0&-A_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}:=\mathcal{M}_{3}-\mathcal{N}_{3},

(10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}I&A_{1}&0\\
0&\hat{P}&A_{2}^{T}\\
0&0&I\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&\hat{P}-P&0\\
0&-A_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}:=\mathcal{M}_{4}-\mathcal{N}_{4}.

(11)

The IBS preconditioners correspond to the matrices
\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2},\mathcal{M}_{3}
, and
\mathcal{M}_{4}
, which are termed the IBS1, IBS2, IBS3, and IBS4 preconditioners, respectively.

2.1
The IBS iterative methods

Each of the proposed block splittings, induces a stationary iterative method for solving the block three-by-three linear system (
3
). We term them the IBS iterative methods, which take the corresponding general form

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}=\mathcal{G}_{i}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}+\mathcal{M}_{i}^{-1}\tilde{b},\hskip 18.49988pti=1,2,3,4,

(12)

where
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
is an arbitrary initial guess and
\mathcal{G}_{i}=\mathcal{M}_{i}^{-1}\mathcal{N}_{i}=I-\mathcal{M}_{i}^{-1}\mathcal{A}
,
i=1,2,3,4
, are the corresponding iteration matrices.

Recalling that the matrices
P,\hat{P}
, and
A^{T}H_{p,q}A=A_{1}^{T}A_{1}-A_{2}^{T}A_{2}
are all SPD, we begin to analyze the convergence of the IBS iterative methods. For this analysis, we will need the following well-known lemma.

Lemma 1
.

[
1
]

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be the roots of the real quadratic equation
x^{2}-bx+c=0
. The modulus of both
\alpha
and
\beta
is less than one if and only if

|c|<1,\hskip 9.24994pt|b|<1+c.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathbf{x}^{*}
be the unique solution to the linear system (
3
). The IBS stationary iterative methods have the following convergence properties:

(i)

The IBS1 and IBS3 methods converge to
\mathbf{x}^{*}
for any initial guess if both the matrices
2\hat{P}-P-A_{2}^{T}A_{2}
and
\hat{P}-A_{2}^{T}A_{2}
are SPD, i.e.,

2\hat{P}-P-A_{2}^{T}A_{2}\succ 0\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\hat{P}-A_{2}^{T}A_{2}\succ 0\implies\rho(G_{1})<1\text{ and }\rho(G_{3})<1.

(ii)

The IBS2 and IBS4 methods converge to
\mathbf{x}^{*}
for any initial guess if the matrix
2\hat{P}-P+A_{2}^{T}A_{2}
is SPD, i.e.,

2\hat{P}-P+A_{2}^{T}A_{2}\succ 0\implies\rho(G_{2})<1\text{ and }\rho(G_{4})<1.

Proof.

For the zero eigenvalues of the iteration matrices, the proof of the theorem is obvious. Therefore, we prove each part of the theorem separately, only for nonzero eigenvalues of these matrices.

Proof of (i).
Let
(\lambda,\nu)
be an eigenpair of the iteration matrix
\mathcal{G}_{1}=\mathcal{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathcal{N}_{1}
, where
\lambda\neq 0
and
\nu=(x;y;z)\neq 0
. The eigenvalue equation
\mathcal{N}_{1}\nu=\lambda\mathcal{M}_{1}\nu
yields

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-A_{1}y=\lambda x,\\
(\hat{P}-P)y-A_{2}^{T}z=\lambda\hat{P}y,\\
-A_{2}y=\lambda z.\end{cases}

(13)

Note that for
\lambda\neq 0
, we have
y\neq 0
, otherwise, the first and third equation of (
13
) implies
x=0
and
z=0
, contradicting that
\nu\neq 0
is an eigenvector. Therefore, substituting the third equation of (
13
) into the second one, and pre-multiplying the resulting equation by
\lambda y^{*}
, yields

(y^{*}\hat{P}y)\lambda^{2}-(y^{*}(\hat{P}-P)y)\lambda-y^{*}A_{2}^{T}A_{2}y=0.

Since
\hat{P}
is SPD, both sides of the above equation can be divided by
y^{*}\hat{P}y
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}-\frac{y^{*}(\hat{P}-P)y}{y^{*}\hat{P}y}\lambda-\frac{y^{*}A_{2}^{T}A_{2}y}{y^{*}\hat{P}y}=0.

(14)

From Lemma
1
, the roots of this quadratic equation satisfy
|\lambda|<1
if and only if

\displaystyle\frac{y^{*}A_{2}^{T}A_{2}y}{y^{*}\hat{P}y}<1,

(15)

and

\displaystyle\left|\frac{y^{*}(\hat{P}-P)y}{y^{*}\hat{P}y}\right|<1-\frac{y^{*}A_{2}^{T}A_{2}y}{y^{*}\hat{P}y}.

(16)

Inequality (
15
) is equivalent to
y^{*}(\hat{P}-A_{2}^{T}A_{2})y>0
, which is satisfied if the matrix
\hat{P}-A_{2}^{T}A_{2}
is SPD.
Inequality (
16
) can be rewritten as

-y^{*}(\hat{P}-A_{2}^{T}A_{2})y<y^{*}(\hat{P}-P)y<y^{*}(\hat{P}-A_{2}^{T}A_{2})y.

The right-hand inequality simplifies to

y^{*}(P-A_{2}^{T}A_{2})y>0.

Since we assumed
P-A_{2}^{T}A_{2}
is SPD, this inequality always holds true. The left-hand inequality simplifies to

y^{*}(2\hat{P}-P-A_{2}^{T}A_{2})y>0,

which is satisfied if
2\hat{P}-P-A_{2}^{T}A_{2}
is SPD.
This completes the proof for the IBS1 method. For the IBS3 method, the result can be similarly obtaind, as the IBS1 method.

Proof of (ii).
Let
(\lambda,\nu)
be an eigenpair of
\mathcal{G}_{2}=\mathcal{M}_{2}^{-1}\mathcal{N}_{2}
, where
\lambda\neq 0
and
\nu=(x;y;z)\neq 0
. The eigenvalue equation
\mathcal{N}_{2}\nu=\lambda\mathcal{M}_{2}\nu
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-A_{1}y=\lambda x,\\
(\hat{P}-P)y=\lambda(\hat{P}y+A_{2}^{T}z),\\
-A_{2}y=\lambda z.\end{cases}

(17)

Substituting the third equation of (
17
) into the second one, yields

(\hat{P}-P+A_{2}^{T}A_{2})y=\lambda\hat{P}y,

By pre-multiplying the above equation by
y^{*}
, we can deduce that

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{y^{*}(\hat{P}-P+A_{2}^{T}A_{2})y}{y^{*}\hat{P}y}.

(18)

Thus, the condition
|\lambda|<1
is equivalent to

-y^{*}\hat{P}y<y^{*}(\hat{P}-P+A_{2}^{T}A_{2})y<y^{*}\hat{P}y.

The right-hand inequality is equivalent to

y^{*}(P-A_{2}^{T}A_{2})y>0.

The above inequality always holds since
P-A_{2}^{T}A_{2}
is assumed to be SPD. The left-hand inequality is equivalent to

y^{*}(2\hat{P}-P+A_{2}^{T}A_{2})y>0,

which is satisfied if
2\hat{P}-P+A_{2}^{T}A_{2}
is SPD. The proof for the IBS4 method is similar to that of the IBS2 method. Thus, the proof is completed.
∎

From Theorem
1
, we can obtain the following corollary for the IBS iterative methods.

Corollary 1
.

If
\hat{P}-P
is an SPD matrix, then all the IBS stationary iterative methods converge to the unique solution of (
3
) for any initial guess. Furthermore, all the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{G}_{2}
and
\mathcal{G}_{4}
are contained within the positive real interval
(0,1)
.

Proof.

Assume that the matrix
\hat{P}-P
is SPD. Since the sum of two SPD matrices is SPD, it follows that

\hat{P}-A_{2}^{T}A_{2}=(\hat{P}-P)+(P-A_{2}^{T}A_{2})\succ 0,

and consequently, we have

\displaystyle 2\hat{P}-P-A_{2}^{T}A_{2}=(\hat{P}-P)+(\hat{P}-A_{2}^{T}A_{2})\succ 0.

Moreover, since the sum of an SPD matrix and a symmetric positive semidefinite (SPSD) matrix is also SPD, then

2\hat{P}-P+A_{2}^{T}A_{2}=(2\hat{P}-P-A_{2}^{T}A_{2})+2A_{2}^{T}A_{2}\succ 0.

Therefore, all conditions of Theorem
1
are obtained, i.e.,
\rho(\mathcal{G}_{i})<1
,
i=1,2,3,4
.
In addition, the matrices
\hat{P}-P+A_{2}^{T}A_{2}
and
\hat{P}
are both SPD. Thus, from (
18
) we conclude that all the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{G}_{2}
and
\mathcal{G}_{4}
are located in the positive real interval
(0,1)
. This gives the desired result.
∎

2.2
Implementation details

We have already analyzed the convergence of the IBS iterative methods and proved that if the conditions of Theorem
1
are satisfied, then for each iteration matrix
\mathcal{G}\in\{\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2},\mathcal{G}_{3},\mathcal{G}_{4}\}
, we have

\rho(\mathcal{G})<1.

This implies that for the corresponding preconditioner
\mathcal{M}\in\{\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2},\mathcal{M}_{3},\mathcal{M}_{4}\}
, the eigenvalues of the preconditioned matrix

\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{A}=I-\mathcal{G}

are clustered within a circle of centered at
(0,1)
with radius
1
. This spectral clustering ensures that, the IBS preconditioners are effective in accelerating the convergence of a Krylov subspace method, such as GMRES, for solving the block
3\times 3
linear system (
3
). Applying such methods to solve the preconditioned system

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathcal{M}^{-1}\tilde{b},

(19)

requires, at each iteration, solving a system of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}z=r.

(20)

To detail the implementation of this procedure, we partition the vectors
z
and
r
as
z=(z_{1};z_{2};z_{3})
and
r=(r_{1};r_{2};r_{3}).
The algorithms for solving such systems are then given as follows:

Algorithm 1.
Solving
\mathcal{M}_{1}z=r

1.

z_{1}:=r_{1}
;

2.

Solve
\hat{P}z_{2}=r_{2}
for
z_{2}
;

3.

z_{3}:=r_{3}.

Algorithm 2.
Solving
\mathcal{M}_{2}z=r

1.

z_{1}:=r_{1}
;

2.

z_{3}:=r_{3}
;

3.

Solve
\hat{P}z_{2}=r_{2}-A_{2}^{T}z_{3}
for
z_{2}
.

Algorithm 3.
Solving
\mathcal{M}_{3}z=r

1.

Solve
\hat{P}z_{2}=r_{2}
for
z_{2}
;

2.

z_{1}:=r_{1}-A_{1}z_{2}
;

3.

z_{3}:=r_{3}.

Algorithm 4.
Solving
\mathcal{M}_{4}z=r

1.

z_{3}:=r_{3}
;

2.

Solve
\hat{P}z_{2}=r_{2}-A_{2}^{T}z_{3}
for
z_{2}
;

3.

z_{1}:=r_{1}-A_{1}z_{2}
.

According to the above algorithms, it follows that the implementation of the IBS preconditioners, necessitates solving inner linear systems with the coefficient matrix
\hat{P}
, which is SPD. Thus, the inner systems can be solved exactly using the Cholesky factorization and inexactly using the conjugate gradient (CG) method.

2.3
Choosing the matrix
\hat{P}

The matrix
\hat{P}
should be chosen such that it is more well-conditioned than the matrix
P=A_{1}^{T}A_{1}
. For this purpose, we set
\hat{P}=\alpha I+P
for some positive number
\alpha
. In this case, it is clear that
\hat{P}-P=\alpha I
is an SPD matrix. Thus, it follows from Corollary
1
that all the IBS iterative methods would be convergent.

It is worth mentioning that adding a positive value
\alpha
to the main diagonal of
P
improves its condition number. In fact, we have

\kappa(\hat{P})=\frac{\alpha+\lambda_{\max}(P)}{\alpha+\lambda_{\min}(P)}<\frac{\lambda_{\max}(P)}{\lambda_{\min}(P)}=\kappa(P).

To be more specific, if the matrix
P
is scaled such that its largest eigenvalue equals 1, then for the spectral condition number of
\hat{P}=\alpha I+P
we have (see
[
2
]
)

\kappa(\hat{P})=\frac{\alpha+1}{\alpha+\lambda_{\min}(P)}<\frac{\alpha+1}{\alpha}=1+\frac{1}{\alpha}.

For example, if we set
\alpha=0.2
, then the spectral condition number of
\hat{P}
is bounded by
6
.
In practice, a practical choice for the parameter
\alpha
, as suggested in
[
3
]
, is given by

\alpha=\frac{1}{\|A_{1}\|^{2}},

where
\|.\|
is a matrix norm. This choice is intended to balance the scale between the matrices
I
and
A_{1}^{T}A_{1}
, and it will be used for all the numerical examples throughout this paper.

3
Spectral analysis of the preconditioned matrices

In this section, we investigate the spectral properties of the IBS preconditioned matrices. First, in the following four theorems, we analyze the structure of the eigenpairs of these matrices.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that the conditions of Theorem
1
hold for the IBS1 method. Then, the preconditioned matrix
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathcal{A}
has
p+q-\text{rank}(A_{2})+h
linearly independent eigenvectors and the structure of its eigenpairs has the following properties:

(i)

The eigenvalue
1
has a geometric multiplicity of
p+q-\text{rank}(A_{2})
. The corresponding linearly independent eigenvectors include
p
vectors of the form
(x_{i};0;0)
and
q-\text{rank}(A_{2})
vectors of the form
(0;0;z_{i})
, where
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p}
and
\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{q-\text{rank}(A_{2})}
are bases for
\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\mathcal{N}(A_{2}^{T})
, respectively;

(ii)

Let
h

(0\leq h\leq n)
be the total number of linearly independent eigenvectors associated with the non-unit eigenvalues. Then, each non-unit eigenvalue,
\mu_{j},
has a geometric multiplicity
h_{j}
, where
0\leq h_{j}\leq h\leq n
. The corresponding
h_{j}
linearly independent eigenvectors are of the form
\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{j}-1}A_{1}y_{i};y_{i};\frac{1}{\mu_{j}-1}A_{2}y_{i}\right),
where
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{h_{j}}
is a set of linearly independent vectors satisfying the relation
(1-\mu_{j})(P-\mu_{j}\hat{P})y_{i}=A_{2}^{T}A_{2}y_{i}.

Proof.

Let
(\mu,\nu)
be an eigenpair of the preconditioned matrix
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathcal{A}
, where
\nu=(x;y;z)\neq 0
. The eigenvalue equation
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathcal{A}\nu=\mu\nu
can be equivalently written in the block three-by-three form as

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}I&A_{1}&0\\
0&P&A_{2}^{T}\\
0&A_{2}&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}=\mu\begin{pmatrix}I&0&0\\
0&\hat{P}&0\\
0&0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix},

(21)

The above equation can be equivalently rewritten as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}A_{1}y=(\mu-1)x,\\
(P-\mu\hat{P})y=-A_{2}^{T}z,\\
A_{2}y=(\mu-1)z.\end{cases}

(22)

We now analyze the system (
22
) for the cases
\mu=1
and
\mu\neq 1
.

If
\mu=1
, then we have

\displaystyle\begin{cases}A_{1}y=0,\\
(P-\hat{P})y=-A_{2}^{T}z,\\
A_{2}y=0.\end{cases}

(23)

Since
A_{1}
has a full column rank, the first equation of (
23
) implies that
y=0
. Substituting this into the second equation yields
A_{2}^{T}z=0
, which means
z\in\mathcal{N}(A_{2}^{T})
. Therefore, the linearly independent eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalue
1
include
p
vectors of the form
(x_{i};0;0)
, where
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p}
is a basis for
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and
q-\text{rank}(A_{2})
vectors of the form
(0;0;z_{i})
, where
\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{q-\text{rank}(A_{2})}
is a basis for
\mathcal{N}(A_{2}^{T})
. Consequently, the geometric multiplicity of the eigenvalue
1
is
p+q-\text{rank}(A_{2})
.

For
\mu\neq 1
, from (
22
) we have

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x=\frac{1}{\mu-1}A_{1}y,\\
(P-\mu\hat{P})y=-A_{2}^{T}z,\\
z=\frac{1}{\mu-1}A_{2}y.\end{cases}

(24)

Note that
y\neq 0
, otherwise we have
x=0
and
z=0
, which contradicts that
\nu
is an eigenvector. By substituting
z=\frac{1}{\mu-1}A_{2}y
into the second equation of (
24
), we obtain

\displaystyle(1-\mu)(P-\mu\hat{P})y=A_{2}^{T}A_{2}y.

(25)

Therefore, there exist
h
(
0\leq h\leq n
) linearly independent eigenvectors of the form

\left(\frac{1}{\mu-1}A_{1}y_{i};y_{i};\frac{1}{\mu-1}A_{2}y_{i}\right),

corresponding to non-unit eigenvalues
\mu\neq 1
, where the vectors
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{h}
are linearly independent vectors satisfying the relation (
25
). In fact, each distinct non-unit eigenvalue, say
\mu_{j}
, corresponds to
h_{j}
of these
h
linearly independent eigenvectors. Therefore,
h_{j}
is its geometric multiplicity.

Now, let us define
l=q-\text{rank}(A_{2})
and consider the coefficient vectors

c^{(1)}=(c_{1}^{(1)};\cdots;c_{p+l}^{(1)}),\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994ptc^{(2)}=(c_{1}^{(2)};\cdots;c_{h}^{(h)}).

To prove the linear independence of these
p+l+h
eigenvectors, it suffices to show that the linear combination

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{(1)}&\cdots&x_{p+l}^{(1)}\\
0&\cdots&0\\
z_{1}^{(1)}&\cdots&z_{p+l}^{(1)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
c_{p+l}^{{(1)}}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{(2)}&\cdots&x_{h}^{(2)}\\
y_{1}^{(2)}&\cdots&y_{h}^{(2)}\\
z_{1}^{(2)}&\cdots&z_{h}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
c_{h}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix},

(26)

implies that both vectors
c^{(1)}
and
c^{(2)}
are zero vectors.
We recall that the first
p+l
eigenvectors correspond to the eigenvalue
1
, while the remaining
h
eigenvectors correspond to the non-unit eigenvalues
\mu_{k},

k=1,\ldots,h
. Therefore, by pre-multiplying both sides of the equation (
26
) by the preconditioned matrix
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathcal{A}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{(1)}&\cdots&x_{p+l}^{(1)}\\
0&\cdots&0\\
z_{1}^{(1)}&\cdots&z_{p+l}^{(1)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
c_{p+l}^{(1)}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{(2)}&\cdots&x_{h}^{(2)}\\
y_{1}^{(2)}&\cdots&y_{h}^{(2)}\\
z_{1}^{(2)}&\cdots&z_{h}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{1}c_{1}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\mu_{h}c_{h}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(27)

Subtracting (
26
) from (
27
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{(2)}&\cdots&x_{h}^{(2)}\\
y_{1}^{(2)}&\cdots&y_{h}^{(2)}\\
z_{1}^{(2)}&\cdots&z_{h}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}(\mu_{1}-1)c_{1}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
(\mu_{h}-1)c_{h}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(28)

Since the columns of the matrix in (
28
) are linearly independent, and we know
\mu_{k}\neq 1
for all
k
, it follows that
c_{k}^{(2)}=0
for
k=1,\dots,h
.
Substituting the components of the vector
c^{(2)}=0
into (
26
), we get

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{(1)}&\cdots&x_{p+l}^{(1)}\\
0&\cdots&0\\
z_{1}^{(1)}&\cdots&z_{p+l}^{(1)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
c_{p+l}^{(1)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(29)

Since all the columns of the coefficient matrix in the above system are linearly independent, it follows that
c_{k}^{(1)}=0
for
k=1,\dots,p+l
. This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 6
.

Let the IBS preconditioners
\mathcal{M}_{i}
,
i=1,2,3,4
, be defined as in (
8
), (
9
), (
10
), and (
11
). Then, for each of the preconditioned matrices
\mathcal{M}_{i}^{-1}\mathcal{A}
,
i=1,2,3,4
, the minimal polynomial has a degree of at most
n+q+1
.

Proof.

The block
3\times 3
structures of the IBS preconditioned matrices are obtained as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{1}^{-1}\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}I&0&0\\
0&\hat{P}^{-1}&0\\
0&0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&A_{1}&0\\
0&P&A_{2}^{T}\\
0&A_{2}&I\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}I&A_{1}&0\\
0&\hat{P}^{-1}P&\hat{P}^{-1}A_{2}^{T}\\
0&A_{2}&I\end{pmatrix},

(30)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2}^{-1}\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}I&0&0\\
0&\hat{P}^{-1}&-\hat{P}^{-1}A_{2}^{T}\\
0&0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&A_{1}&0\\
0&P&A_{2}^{T}\\
0&A_{2}&I\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}I&A_{1}&0\\
0&\hat{P}^{-1}(P-A_{2}^{T}A_{2})&0\\
0&A_{2}&I\end{pmatrix},

(31)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{3}^{-1}\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}I&-A_{1}\hat{P}^{-1}&0\\
0&\hat{P}^{-1}&0\\
0&0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&A_{1}&0\\
0&P&A_{2}^{T}\\
0&A_{2}&I\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}I&A_{1}(I-\hat{P}^{-1}P)&-A_{1}\hat{P}^{-1}A_{2}^{T}\\
0&\hat{P}^{-1}P&\hat{P}^{-1}A_{2}^{T}\\
0&A_{2}&I\end{pmatrix},

(32)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{4}^{-1}\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}I&-A_{1}\hat{P}^{-1}&A_{1}\hat{P}^{-1}A_{2}^{T}\\
0&\hat{P}^{-1}&-\hat{P}^{-1}A_{2}^{T}\\
0&0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&A_{1}&0\\
0&P&A_{2}^{T}\\
0&A_{2}&I\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}I&A_{1}(I-\hat{P}^{-1}(P-A_{2}^{T}A_{2}))&0\\
0&\hat{P}^{-1}(P-A_{2}^{T}A_{2})&0\\
0&A_{2}&I\end{pmatrix}.

(33)

We can see that each IBS preconditioned matrix, denoted by
\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{A}
, gives a common block three-by-three structure of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{A}=\begin{pmatrix}I&\Psi\\
0&\Phi\end{pmatrix},

(34)

where,
\Psi\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times(n+q)}
and
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+q)\times(n+q)}
. This structure implies that the eigenvalue
1
has an algebraic multiplicity of at least
p
, and the remaining
n+q
eigenvalues are located in the spectrum of
\Phi
. Let
\mu_{k},
be an arbitrary eigenvalue of
\Phi
. Then, the characteristic polynomial of the preconditioned matrix
\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{A}
is

\displaystyle(\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{A}-I)^{p}\prod_{k=1}^{n+q}(\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{A}-\mu_{k}I).

(35)

Now, consider the polynomial
(\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{A}-I)\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{n+q}(\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{A}-\mu_{k}I)
of degree
n+q+1
, which devided the characteristic polynomial (
35
). It can be rewritten as

\displaystyle(\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{A}-I)\prod_{k=1}^{n+q}(\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{A}-\mu_{k}I)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\Psi\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{n+q}(\Phi-\mu_{k}I)\\
0&(\Phi-I)\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{n+q}(\Phi-\mu_{k}I)\end{pmatrix},

According to the Cayley-Hamilton theorem, this polynomial is equals to the zero matrix. Thus, the degree of the minimal polynomial of
\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{A}
is at most
n+q+1.
This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 2
.

The upper bound on the degree of the minimal polynomial, established in Theorem
6
, has a direct implication for the convergence of the GMRES method. Since the degree of the minimal polynomiale is equal to the dimension of the Krylov subspace
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{A},c)
for a generic vector
c
, the upper bound directly implies that the GMRES algorithm will terminate in at most
n+q+1
iterations (in exact arithmetic)
[
15
, Proposition 6.1]
.

4
Numerical experiments

In this section, we present some numerical examples to demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed preconditioners for solving the ILS problem (
1
).
It is well-known that applying a preconditioned Krylov subspace method, such as GMRES, requires solving a linear system of the form
\mathcal{M}z=r
at each iteration. For large-scale and sparse problems, solving these inner systems exactly is often computationally prohibitive. To overcome this, we solve the inner systems inexactly using a few iterations of CG method. Therefore, using of the Flexible GMRES (FGMRES) method is an ideal choice
[
14
,
15
]
. So, we employ the preconditioned version of it in our experiments.

We compare our proposed IBS preconditioners with two recent effective preconditioners, denoted by BS2 and BUT. The choice of BS2 is motivated by the observation in
[
21
]
that the BS1, BS2, and BS3 schemes exhibit similar performance, with BS2 being slightly more efficient. For all experiments, the initial guess is the zero vector, and the approximation matrix
\hat{P}
is chosen as

\hat{P}=\alpha I+P,

with

\alpha=\frac{1}{\|A_{1}\|_{1}^{2}}.

All computations were performed in
Matlab
R2022a on a laptop with a 2.30 GHz Intel(R) Core(TM) i3-2348 CPU and 4 GB of RAM, running a Windows 10 operating system.

The iteration of FGMRES method is terminated as soon as the relative residual norm

\text{RES}=\frac{\|\tilde{b}-\mathcal{A}\mathbf{x}_{k}\|_{2}}{\|\tilde{b}\|_{2}},

where
\textbf{x}_{k}
is the approximate solution at iteration
k
, satisfys
\text{RES}<10^{-8}
, or when the number of iterations exceeds 2000.
Furthermore, the inner CG iteration is terminated once the relative residual norm exceeds the tolerance of
10^{-3}
, or after a maximum of 1000 iterations.
The performance of all the preconditioned methods is evaluated based on the number of iterations (IT), the algorithm’s running time in seconds (CPU), and the relative error of the approximate solution (ERR), defined as

\text{ERR}=\frac{\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|_{2}}{\|x^{*}\|_{2}},

where
x_{k}
is the approximate solution, extracted from
\mathbf{x}_{k}
, and
x^{*}
is exact solution for the ILS problem (
1
). The reported IT and CPU values are the average over five consecutive runs. In all tables, the notation
\dagger
indicates that the corresponding method failed to converge to the solution of system (
3
) in 2000 iterations.

Conclusions

In this paper, we have proposed several inexact block-splitting preconditioners, denoted by IBS, for a class of large-scale indefinite least squares problems. A detailed theoretical analysis was provided, including the convergence conditions for the corresponding stationary methods and a detailed study of the eigenstructure of the preconditioned matrices. This analysis led to a theoretical bound on the number of GMRES iterations when our preconditioners are applied. Finally, numerical experiments demonstrated the efficiency and robustness of our proposed preconditioners. They performed well particularly on large sparse, and ill-conditioned problems, where many other preconditioners often fail and always perform worse. This shows the great potential as a practical tool for real-world ILS problems.
