 ABSTRACT

 Recommendations for the processing and testing of 1-kg size ceramic waste form
 products are made to focus on the effectiveness of simplified direct processing to
 generate a waste form with acceptable quality from the perspective of
 microstructure and chemical durability. Recipes are provided to enable direct
 comparisons with smaller materials made previously to identify factors associated
 with scale up, including composition uniformity, important to developing methods
 for full-scale implementation. Recommended product analyses include identifying
 major phases, distributions of key salt constituents, microencapsulation of sodalite
 grains by glass, and the amounts and distributions of halite inclusion phases. The
 large amount of material available for testing supports replicate tests to measure
 the precision achieved using key test methods appropriate for demonstrating
 product consistency. Standard durability tests are recommended for samples taken
 from various locations near the top, middle, bottom, and sides of the product. The
 methods used to produce the 1-kg materials and measured characteristics will
 inform development of full-scale production methods, such as direct processing
 using molten salt feed, and provide data supporting qualification as a waste form.

1. INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND

The glass-bonded sodalite ceramic waste form (CWF) was designed in the 1990’s to accommodate most of
the sodium and chloride ions in salt waste within synthetic sodalite (Na8[Al6Si6O24]Cl2) that is encapsulated
in a durable borosilicate glass matrix that accommodates the other salt cations (see Ebert 2005 and
references therein). Since it was first developed, the production method has changed as processes became
better understood and aspects of large-scale implementation were addressed. The efficiency of processing
and the quality of the product have been assessed continuously and comparatively as new methods were
developed from laboratory to engineering scales. Recent developments have been focused on simplifying
the production method for practical implementation in a product facility. This report provides
recommendation on the production and testing of 1-kg CWF materials made by directly processing a
mixture of Zeolite 4A, surrogate waste salt, and borosilicate glass using a method based on a fundamental
understanding of the processes that occur and demonstrated to be effective at small scales.
The generation of sodalite occurs by reaction Zeolite 4A with NaCl in the salt according to the reaction
stoichiometry given in Equation 1. The process was originally designed to occlude molten salt within the
Zeolite 4A cage structure by mixing the two components at approximately 500 °C to generate “salt-loaded
zeolite” in an initial pre-conditioning step. The salt-loaded zeolite was mechanically mixed with crushed
glass and then loaded into a canister that was sealed and processed at approximately 850 °C by using a hot
isostatic press (HIP). The resulted in a densified glass-ceramic material contained within the collapsed
canister. The canisters were sized so that two HIP CWF products could be placed in a standard short DOE
container for storage, transport, and disposal.

A “pressureless consolidation” (PC) method was later developed to directly process the mixture of salt-
loaded zeolite and glass in a full-size canister without using HIP (Priebe and Bateman 2007). The PC CWF
product had a similar microstructure and durability as HIPed material but was less dense. However,
challenges to implement the PC process in a hot cell are significant in terms of handling fine particulate
materials and multiple operation steps. A significant improvement in waste loading was made about
10 years ago by changing the binder glass used in the process. Specifically, the use of a high soda content
glass was demonstrated to increase the waste loading by about 150% (Ebert et al. 2016). The high soda
content increases the amount of sodalite that is generated by Reaction 1 due to in situ generation of NaCl
through ion exchange of salt cations with sodium in the glass. This enables immobilization of chloride in
salt waste streams devoid of NaCl.

2. DIRECT PROCESSING
It has been hypothesized by several researchers that the production of CWF could be further simplified by
omitting the pre-occlusion step and relaxing the reagent size and dryness control limits to facilitate material
handling in a hot cell environment, as recently proposed by Ebert (2024). The efficacy of directly processing
mixtures of various sizes and relative amounts of Zeolite 4A, salt, and glass to generate CWF material was
demonstrated at the laboratory scale by successfully synthesizing monolithic waste forms at 880 and 925 °C
and in both ceramic and steel crucibles (Gesualdi et al., 2025). Microstructure analysis by SEM and XRD
analyses confirmed that Zeolite 4A was converted to sodalite by reaction with NaCl in the mixture and
became microencapsulated within the glass phase along with small inclusions of halite that were generated
as byproducts during processing. As observed in CWF materials made using HIP and PC methods, both
glass and salt penetrated throughout the ~16 mesh-sized Zeolite 4A particles to microencapsulate the
micrometer-sized grains of sodalite that formed. Materials made with uncrushed ~4-mm-sized Zeolite beads
showed the generation of sodalite occurs by inward penetration of salt into the beads. It is hypothesized
that this corresponds to the uptake of molten salt into the Zeolite 4A pore structure during processing when
the mixture is heated to the salt melting temperature. That is, molten salt contacts both Zeolite 4A and glass
particles as the mixture is heated. The thickness of the sodalite layer indicates the extent of salt uptake either
due to kinetic limitations or the amount of salt available near enough to the zeolite surface due to physical
constraints. Therefore, the Zeolite 4A reagent should be sized small enough to facilitate in situ occlusion
of salt during processing by both the proximity of molten salt and the absorption rate. The molten salt and
molten glass are expected to be immiscible at the processing temperature (e.g., 925 °C) but may intermix.
Laboratory-scale products indicate the small amount of halite by-product inclusion phases distributed
throughout the bulk are compositionally distinct from the added salt.

It is expected that processing can be facilitated by directly adding molten salt (rather than crushed solid salt)
to a mixture of crushed Zeolite 4A and crushed glass that has been heated to near or above the salt melting
temperature but not as high as the temperature required to generate sodalite. The molten salt will be taken
up by the Zeolite 4A as it percolates through the particles of Zeolite 4A and glass. It remains to be
determined if physical mixing is necessary for adequate distribution. In a laboratory-scale test, the
percolation of molten salt poured on top of a mixture of Zeolite 4A and glass was determined to have been
effectively occluded in the Zeolite 4A throughout the bulk based on the generation of sodalite throughout
the product. A mixing device (e.g., an Archimedes screw) could be used to enhance the mixing at a larger
scale as the canister is being loaded, but measures to avoid clogging would need to be implemented.
Techniques for adding molten salt for small-scale and large-scale processing are being evaluated.

Salt loading of 10% or less were used to demonstrate effective processing in laboratory-scale products in
order to highlight sodalite generation, but higher salt loading are expected to be achievable. Modification
of process parameters such as the size and moisture content of Zeolite 4A or reduction in processing
temperature to 880 °C in small-scale scoping tests did not affect either the generation of sodalite or its
encapsulation in glass, but did increase the porosity of the product relative to those of materials made at
925 °C. However, since a densification of approximately 40% is achieved relative to the effective density
of the Zeolite 4A, salt, and glass mixture loaded into the canister, the slightly higher porosity is an
acceptable trade-off for processing at a lower temperature. Specifically, the additional densification
provided by HIP processing was determined to be unnecessary for EBR-II treatment. The sodalite
generation rate appears to be high and the required processing time is probably due to how quickly the
material at the core is heated. Using a lower processing temperature would decrease the time necessary to
heat the reagents to the required temperature and also the time necessary for the product to cool. This should
be evaluated using 1-kg products.

Following the success of 10-g products made by direct processing, two 100-g products and one 500-g
product were fabricated at Argonne and INL to identify and address possible effects of scale up (Ebert and

of these three materials were used to highlight the occurrence of these possible effects, but production of
1-kg products is necessary to indicate material or engineering issues that must be addressed for scaling
bench-scale processes to a larger scale. Insights gained and modifications used in producing small-scale
materials should be confirmed using 1-kg CWF products.

3. RECOMMENDED PROCESSING OF 1-kg PRODUCTS
Recommendations for the preparation of 1-kg scale products suitable for testing to further evidence the
suitability and address the engineerability of direct processing are based on (1) the success of generating
small and moderately-sized materials, (2) the consistent microstructures of directly-processed material
characteristics with those of materials made materials with HIP and PC processing, (3) a technical
understanding of the underlying chemical and physical processes affecting production and durability of
CWF materials, and (4) consideration of aspects likely to be important for implementation at industrial
scale.

It is not practical, beneficial, or necessary to simulate or represent some engineering challenges of full-size
processing at 1-kg or smaller scales, such as mixing and loading reagents, prior to confirming the suitability
of the waste form material to be produced and determining the requirements for the engineered methods.
For example, requirements for blending molten salt with premixed and pre-heated glass and Zeolite 4A
remain to be determined (including if they must be blended at all) and processing equipment remains to be
developed. The production and analyses of 1-kg size CWF products will support determining those
requirements and provide target material characteristics for developing large-scale material mixing and
loading techniques and equipment.

For making the initial 1-kg products, solid reagents are blended manually at room temperature, for example
by repeatedly pouring slowly from one container into the other, or by using a tumbler. This is expected to
be more efficient that manually “stirring” the mixtures as was done to mix 10-g materials. It is
recommended that the effectiveness of the mixing method be confirmed using small samples of unprocessed
mixtures that are fixed in epoxy and cross sectioned for microscopic analysis.

3.1 Equipment

Use of a furnace capable of heating to at least 925 °C housed in dry argon atmosphere chamber (<100 ppm
moisture) is required. The low moisture atmosphere is required to avoid moisture uptake by the hygroscopic
waste salt. Two containers each with a volume at least 2 liters for mixing 1 kg total reagents. Combining
several batches of smaller volumes of mixed reagents is recommended.

The suitability of the furnace to be used for processing the CWF materials should be confirmed prior to use,
including adequate power and temperature control, mass and volume capacity, and atmosphere control. A
small but measurable amount of salt is expected to vaporize during processing, which can be collected using
a lid on the canister. Although interruptions in heating cycle and unplanned cooling prior to attaining the
maximum temperature is not expected to prevent processing, it will likely affect the uniformity of the
product.

Cylindrical alumina crucibles or cylindrical Type 304 stainless steel canisters with capacity of at least 1 liter
(the fill density of the mixed reagents is approximately 1 cc/g and the density of the product is
approximately 2 g/cc) should be used. Interactions of the processed CWF with alumina, yttria, zirconia, and
steel canisters have been demonstrated to be insignificant. Stainless steel crucible represent the full-size
product, but are not necessary to evaluate characteristics of the CWF. The shape and aspect ratio of the
crucible will affect the heating profile of the additives. Waste forms are expected to be cast as cylinders
having a height that is about four times the diameter (e.g., < 1.2 feet in diameter and < 5 feet tall to fit two
waste forms in a standard short DOE container.). The radial dimension of the material being processed is
expected to determine the temperature profile achieved during processing if the entire volume is heated
uniformly. That heating profile will establish the processing time-at-temperature required for complete

conversion. Therefore, the height of the densified final product (densification of 60% can be assumed)
should be greater than the diameter.

3.2 Reagents and Preparation (Baseline)

The use of reagents and preparation methods demonstrated to be effective at small scales is recommended
to highlight effects of scaling up and product uniformity for initial products. Different salt compositions
and reagent ratios can then be used to determine factors such as salt loading capacities. Initial baseline
conditions are

 Zeolite 4A crushed and sized to -16 + 60 mesh and then dried at 300 °C for 2 hours
 High soda content borosilicate glass NBS4 crushed and sized to -16 + 60 mesh
 LiCl-KCl-based surrogate waste salt with known melting point crushed and sized to -16 + 60 mesh
 LiCl-based surrogate waste salt with known melting point crushed and sized to -16 + 60 mesh

 Mass ratio 52% Zeolite + 40% glass + 8% LiCl-KCl salt
 Mass ratio 52% Zeolite + 40% glass + 8% LiCl salt

The Zeolite 4A is crushed and sized to achieve occlusion of molten salt during low-temperature hold. The
-16 + 60 mesh sized fraction of Zeolite 4A has been shown to completely convert to sodalite within a few
hours. This probably indicates salt is occluded rapidly throughout the Zeolite 4A particle volume during
processing. Knowledge of the approximate melting temperature of the salt can be used to select an
intermediate hold temperature to promote rapid salt occlusion during processing prior to generating sodalite.
Depending on the waste salt composition, this is expected to range from below 450 °C for LiCl-KCl-based
waste salt to above 650 °C for LiCl-based waste salt. Using an intermediate hold temperature that is lower
than the salt melting point will not be effective and hold temperatures that are significantly higher than the
melting point will lower the viscosity of the molten salt to promote draining. Using the hold temperature to
process 1-kg products simulates the addition of molten salt in large-scale processes. Due to the significantly
different melting points, it is recommended that 1-kg products be made using both LiCl-KCl eutectic and
LiCl-based surrogate waste salts using appropriate processing conditions to span the possible range. An
upper limit temperature to salt occlusion is imposed by the glass because molten glass limits access of
molten salt to Zeolite 4A and impedes processing.

The mass ratio of Zeolite 4A and salt in the CWF recipe is selected to occlude an amount of salt
corresponding with the structural pore volume of the Zeolite 4A, although only NaCl reacts to form sodalite.
Higher salt loadings are achievable for larger products due to effects of the greater mass. Other cations form
other phases, such as Cs-pollucite, that do not sequester chloride (Lambregts and Frank, 2003; Ebert 2010).
The exchange of other cations in the waste salt with sodium in the glass generates NaCl during processing,
which then react with Zeolite 4A to generate sodalite. The exchanged waste salt cations are incorporated
into the glass phase or inclusion phases. Note that some phases in the waste salt (e.g., lanthanide chlorides)
remain solid during processing and are retained as inclusions in the glass.

The baseline ratios are intended to highlight the processing with less-than-bounding salt waste loadings to
provide confidence that the waste salt will be incorporated into the waste form. The initial CWF materials
will provide baseline values to which materials made with high salt loadings, lower processing temperatures,
and shorter thermal cycle durations can be compared. Crushed and sized Zeolite 4A, glass, and solid salt
reagents are mixed at room temperature then load mixture into crucible or canister for processing. Mixing
several smaller batches separately (e.g., five 200-g batches) and then mixing those batches prior to loading
is recommended to achieve a uniform final mixture of Zeolite 4A, glass, and salt particles. This represents
the target uniform physical distribution to be achieved by large-scale mixing of molten salt with a pre-

3.3 Thermal Cycle

Heat the mixture at approximately 10 °C/minute to a temperature 20 °C above the known or expected
melting temperature of the salt and hold for 30 ± 5 minutes to promote occlusion. The objective is for
molten salt to become absorbed in the neighboring Zeolite 4A particles without percolating through the
mixture of Zeolite 4A and glass particles. Similarly sized Zeolite 4A and crushed glass is used to maintain
an interparticle structure that retains the molten salt to promote occlusion in the Zeolite 4A. Both will
represent the direct addition of molten salt during full-size processing.

Resume heating the mixture at approximately 10 °C/minute to 925 °C and hold for 4 ± 0.5 hours to promote
generation of sodalite. It is expected that the conversion of salt-loaded Zeolite 4A to sodalite occurs quickly
at temperatures above 850 °C and that the processing time required for complete conversion will be
determined by the thermal conductivity of the reagent load and heating of material in the central core.
Conversion in the 500-g CWF material that was cast in an alumina crucible by heating at 925 °C for four
hours was uniform throughout the bulk (radially and axially) based on the microstructure. The top ~1 cm
layer contained detectable amounts of nepheline and halite, which are thermal decomposition products of
sodalite, but no unreacted Zeolite 4A or waste salt was detected. A processing temperature of 925 °C was
used to generate laboratory-scale products used to assess aspects of direct processing method, but
processing at 880 °C was demonstrated to generate an acceptable small-scale product. This indicates
material in the center of large product does not need to be heated to 925 °C to generate sodalite. This can
be utilized to lower the processing temperature below 925 °C or shorten the processing duration at 925 °C
to what is required to heat the core material to 880 °C. Both effects will shorten the overall process cycle
time. The interior microstructures of 1-kg scale materials will provide empirical evidence of effective
thermal cycles.

4. RECOMMENDED TESTING OF 1-kg PRODUCTS
Tests conducted with specimens prepared from 1-kg scale product materials to further evidence the
suitability of the process and the product and to address engineerability aspects of direct processing likely
to be important for implementation at industrial scale. Some physical characteristics are based on visual
observations, including the overall densification, the presence of loose material at the surface, separation
of the product from the crucible or canister walls, the condition of the outer crucible surface, etc. As
observed in 100-g and 500-g products, the color of material at the surface ranged from white, to reddish, to
greenish hues that is likely due to contaminants in the reagents, such as iron in the Zeolite 4A. To date, the
color has not been correlated with a physical characterization, the extent of reaction, or the generation of
by-products such as nepheline. It may be due to small composition differences not detected by SEM
analyses, such as the chlorine content of the sodalite grains, but is considered to be irrelevant to product
performance.

Assessing the quality of the CWF product material is most effectively done by sectioning the product
radially to characterize material at different depths and radial distances from the center. This is most
conveniently done using a high-speed diamond saw with water lubrication. However, since salt inclusion
phases (mostly halite) dissolve in water, smaller sections of material must be cut using absolute ethanol or
other non-aqueous fluid (e.g., hexanol) lubrication to generate surfaces that have not been contacted by
water for use in microstructure characterization and testing.

The same characterization and durability test methods used to characterize small laboratory-scale products
are recommended to characterize 1-kg products because the size of constituent phases and interfaces are on
the order of centimeters. Samples cut from different regions of the product are used to represent the large-
scale uniformity of product; specimens cut from those samples are used to compare microstructures and
aqueous degradation behavior. Aspects of the microstructures occurring in different regions of the product
are used to assess the possible occurrence of thermal gradients during processing as well as the overall
product uniformity.

4.1 Microstructure Characterization

Analyses of the key phases in the waste form confirms successful processing to incorporate and immobilize
the waste salt occurred. The primary product phases are sodalite (approximately 70 vol %), the glass matrix
that encapsulates and penetrates the sodalite domains (approximately 25 vol %), halite inclusions
(approximately 5 vol %), and trace amount of other oxide and chloride inclusion phases (< 1 vol %). In the
CWF materials, sodalite is generated with the intent of sequestering the chloride in the salt so that salt
cations dissolve in the glass phase. The conversion is indicated by the appearance of sodalite domains
(which match the overall size of the Zeolite 4A particles) and the presence of chlorine (and higher sodium
contents) either throughout or at the outer perimeter. Glass can also be seen to have penetrated between
individual sodalite grains within the aggregate. Although only NaCl and NaI react with Zeolite 4A to
generate sodalite, sodium in the glass is exchanged with salt cations to generate NaCl and supplement the
NaCl content of the salt. The thermal decomposition of sodalite generates nepheline and NaCl. Trace
amounts of nepheline have been detected in the outer surface region (~1 cm) of some products by using
XRD, but nepheline becomes incorporated within the microstructure and has no detectable effect other than
increasing the halite content. That is, micrometer-size grains of nepheline are not distinguishable from
grains of sodalite. We note that the presence of a small number of halite domains as inclusions in the glass
phases and within sodalite domains is an expected characteristic of CWF materials. The presence of halite
inclusions does not diminish the performance of a CWF material as a waste form because dissolution of
halite inclusions after the surrounding glass has dissolved to allow contact by seepage water is no different
than if the halite had been a constituent of the surrounding glass. That is, dissolution of the glass phase is

required for the release of constituents that are part of the glass structure and constituents of inclusion
phases encapsulated in the glass. Key aspects of the microstructure assessed by SEM/EDS analyses of
polished cross sections of material recovered from different regions of the product include the distributions
of halite and other inclusion phases and the continuity of the glass phase.

4.2 Corrosion Durability Testing

The objective of waste form processing is to distribute and immobilize the waste salt constituents in host
phases that inhibit their release in a disposal facility when contacted by water. The container in which
processed waste form canisters are placed for transport and storage provide the primary barrier to release
prior to final disposal. Several of those containers may be packaged together in a disposal package, possibly
with other waste form containers. For purposes of facility performance simulations, waste packages,
containers, and canisters are typically modelled to be breached by groundwater within approximately
10,000 years such that the waste form itself limits the release of radionuclides into seepage water to be
transported to the near-field environment thereafter.

For CWF materials, dissolution of the glass phase must occur to release radionuclides distributed in the
glass as part of the glass structure or within inclusion phases. Only chloride and iodide are sequestered in
sodalite and halite inclusions, and both phases are encapsulated by glass. Halite is highly soluble and will
dissolve when contacted by groundwater, but glass must dissolve for water to contact halite inclusions.
Sodalite is intrinsically soluble but has a low solubility limit and will be effectively insoluble in seepage
waters that fill breached waste canisters.

The chemical durability of the glass phase is determined primarily by the glass composition, which is
strongly influenced by the salt cations that dissolve into the glass during processing. Several test methods
have been developed to measure intrinsic dissolution behavior of glass waste forms and dependencies on
environmental conditions including pH, Eh, and temperature. These are not intended to represent specific
waste disposal scenarios. The ASTM C1308 test method provides a measure of the intrinsic dissolution rate
without attenuation by solution feedback from the test solution (ASTM 2025a). That method is very
sensitive to glass phase composition and can be used to distinguish the dissolution of halite exposed at the
specimen surface from the dissolution of sodalite and glass. Series of ASTM C1308 tests can be conducted
at different temperatures and in leachants providing buffered pH values to measure the temperature and pH
dependencies of the intrinsic dissolution rate (also referred to as the forward rate). The intrinsic dissolution
rate, temperature dependence, and pH dependence are used in affinity-based dissolution models for long-
term glass waste forms in disposal systems (Ebert 2026). The ASTM C1220 static immersion test method
can be used to compliment ASTM C1308 tests to confirm dissolution kinetics as dissolved species
accumulate and to differentiate dissolution of sodalite and glass domains. (ASTM 2025b).

The ASTM C1285 Product Consistency Test (PCT) Method B was developed as a variation of Method A,
which uses tightly-controlled test conditions as a regulatory test, to allow for the use of different test
parameter values, such as material size fraction, test temperature, test duration, and leachant composition
(ASTM 2025c). The PCT Method B is used to study dissolution in highly concentrated solutions from
which secondary phases can precipitate. In particular, PCT Method B tests are used to measure the effective
saturation concentrations of the glass phases in CWF materials at different temperatures and pH values, in
which sodalite does not dissolve. The saturation concentration is used in affinity-based dissolution models
for long-term glass waste forms in disposal systems (Ebert 2026). These test methods measure kinetics and
dependencies used to develop mechanistically-based degradation models for use in repository performance
simulations showing the materials are suitable waste forms.

Other attributes and properties of CWF materials related to waste form acceptance can be measured directly,
such as density, hardness, crystalline content, thermal stability and time-temperature-transformation

4.3 Regulatory Tests

The ASTM C1285 Product Consistency Test (PCT) Method A that was developed for high-level radioactive
waste glass has been shown to be applicable to CWF materials (Ebert et al. 2002). Replicate tests with
materials taken from replicate CWF products can be used to demonstrate the precision and sensitivity of
the PCT and the suitability for acceptance of actual waste forms. Note that the sensitivity of the PCT to
process control for high-level waste glass production is through the composition of the glass phase.
Application to CWF product would likewise be through the composition of the glass phase that includes
incorporated salt cations and inclusion phases.

The test is conducted by immersing a controlled mass of crushed material of a controlled size fraction in
demineralized water and measuring the solution concentration after reacting seven days at 90 °C. The
measured concentrations of alkali metals, boron, and silicon are used to calculate the mass of glass that
dissolved. The test method is applicable to CWF materials because the PCT response of CWF is dominated
by dissolution of the glass phase. Halite inclusions that are exposed at the surface of crushed CWF dissolve
immediately when contacted by water and can be measured in a rinse solution before the PCT is assembled.
Halite inclusions not exposed by crushing do not dissolve until the overlying glass dissolves during the test.
In the test and in performance simulations, it does not matter if a constituent is released from the volume
of dissolved glass or exposed halite inclusion.

Replicate PCT-A should be conducted using materials recovered from different regions of the 1-kg CWF
product to measure the variation in halide content (based on initial rinse solutions) and glass durability
(based on 7-day test responses). The variance in results for materials taken from different regions can be
compared to results of other tests with those materials called for in Section 4.2 that are more sensitive to
the glass composition (e.g., ASTM C1308) to assess the corresponding sensitivity of PCT results.

Although the CWF is not expected to contain RCRA-regulated characteristically hazardous constituents
(ignitability, corrosivity, reactivity, or toxicity), the Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP)
can be performed with material from 1-kg products to confirm levels of arsenic, barium, cadmium,
chromium, lead, mercury, selenium, and silver are below regulatory thresholds.

5. SUMMARY
Methods used to directly process CWF materials at small scales (10 – 100 g) can be modified to directly
process mixtures at the 1-kg scale. These include sizing pre-dried Zeolite 4A and glass additives to facilitate
in situ occlusion of crushed salt of a similar size while the mixture is heated, mechanically mixing the
Zeolite 4A, glass, and crushed salt prior to loading in the steel canister, and using the heating cycle to melt
and occlude the salt prior to heating to generate sodalite. The relative amounts of Zeolite 4A, glass, and salt
are selected based on reaction stoichiometries and geometric factors to encapsulate the sodalite within a
continuous glass matrix. The same proportions used to make small-scale CWF products can be used at the
1-kg scale. It is expected that the salt loading that can be achieved in large products will be higher than
those achieved in small products due to the greater total mass and contact area. The radial and axial
uniformity of the salt distribution will likely depend on the uniformity of the initial mixture. Based on
studies using small CWF materials, the Zeolite 4A and glass phases are not expected to move and the molten
salt will be quickly sorbed by the Zeolite 4A and not percolate very far. That can be confirmed with 1-kg
CWF materials cast in appropriate containers.

Product analyses focus on the uniformity of the sodalite and by-product halite distributions throughout the
bulk to indicate effective processing. For example, the microstructure in the center of the CWF product will
indicate if the temperature necessary to generate sodalite was achieved. It is expected that the microstructure
near the top surface will differ from that of the bulk due to the limited mixing that occurs during processing.
Large domains of unreacted salt will indicate processing was not successful. Standardized test methods
developed for high-level waste glasses that have been shown to be suitable for characterizing CWF
materials can be used to relate local differences in microstructures to chemical durability and acceptability
as a waste form. The large amount of source material can support extensive testing to demonstrate the
precision of alternative consistency test methods, including ASTM C1285 PCT and microstructural aspects,
demonstrate the suitability of CWF as a waste form, and demonstrate the benefits of the direct processing
approach.
