Identification and Enhancement of Kink Soliton Motions in a van der Waals Layer
Authors: Bo Li, Jae Whan Park, Han Woong Yeom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Topological solitons, which connect degenerate ground states with phase shifts, have the potential to carry topologically protected information for dissipationless informatics. While topological solitons have been discussed actively in one-dimensional materials, a soliton with a geometric Z topology has been recently found in a van der Waals material of 1T-TaS2 as a form of a mobile kink along domain walls in its charge-density-wave phase. However, details of kink kinetics and the mechanism of their motion have been obscured. Here, we reveal the kinetics and dynamics of kink solitons by tracking the motions of several kink solitons over a relatively long time using real-time scanning tunneling microscopy measurements. We disclose two different types of kink motions, that is, small (one unit cell) or large (a few unit cells) jumps. The energetics and atomistic details of kink jumps were revealed by density functional calculations. Furthermore, the average frequency of jumping modes is significantly enhanced by the injection of the tunneling current. Our work makes a step toward realizing control over the motion of kink solitons, which must be secured for soliton applications.

Introduction

A topological soliton bridges two degenerate but topologically distinct states with an inhomogeneous phase landscape, which is an important conceptual tool in many branches of science and technology. A topological soliton in electronic systems can be described by the Su–Schrieffer-Heeger (SSH) Hamiltonian, − which features a chain of alternating weak and strong bonds within a one-dimensional (1D) tight-binding approximation. This model predicts the emergence of a topologically nontrivial edge state when the chain is cut at a strong bond, which is pinned at zero energy as protected by particle-hole and chiral symmetries. The same is true for a domain wall (DW) with a phase shift in the bond alternation, which is called an SSH soliton. An SSH soliton is mobile and endowed with a Z 2-topology-protected electronic state within the band gap − and hosts a wide variety of exotic quantum phenomena, such as spin-charge separation, fractional charges, chiral solitons, and their molecules in a coupled double SSH chain. , While there has been various indirect evidence of SSH solitons in spectroscopic and transport measurements, recent microscopy works have identified individual solitons directly in self-assembled atomic chains, ,,− ultracold atoms, − artificial vacancy lines, , and graphene nanoribbons. ,

While the latter two cases are not mobile, deviating from the soliton theory, recent scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) works have visualized electronic soliton motions, opening a route to investigate soliton kinetics and dynamical interactions between solitons. ,,− These studies further suggested the possibility of utilizing individual electronic solitons to store, deliver, and manipulate information in a topologically protected way. The idea of using electronic solitons for quantum computation has also been theoretically proposed. ,

In utilizing topological solitons for informatics, one critical barrier can be fragile material systems, such as self-assembled atomic chains. In this respect, we note the recent observation of electronic solitons in a more robust van der Waals material of 1T-TaS2 as a form of kinks along charge-density-wave (CDW) domain walls (DWs). Different from the previously observed Z 2, Z 3, or Z 4 solitons, kink solitons have a geometric Z topology, which allows a large number of solitons to cascade to handle highly multilevel information. , However, while the mobility of kink solitons was indicated, which is crucial in soliton applications, details of their kinetics, dynamics, and controllability remain unexplored.

In this work, we have directly identified and artificially enhanced the motions of electronic kink solitons in this 2D van der Waals material by using scanning tunneling microscopy (STM). Specifically, we monitored in real time with STM the motions of kink solitons formed along the two most frequently observed types of CDW DWs. These measurements disclose different types of kink soliton motions, such as small and large jumps. The motions are largely limited to random hopping, suggesting strong pinning. The atomistic mechanism of the soliton jump between neighboring sites was disclosed by density functional calculations. Stepping further, we demonstrated that the average frequency of translational jumping motions of kink solitons is effectively enhanced by increasing the tunneling current from the STM tip. Our work provides an essential step toward understanding and manipulating the motions of kink solitons in a relatively robust van der Waals system, which might be useful for topologically protected multilevel informatics. ,−

Results and Discussion

1T-TaS2, a CdI2-type layered transition metal dichalcogenide (TMD) material with van der Waals stacking, is composed of layers of hexagonal tantalum (Ta) atoms coordinated octahedrally by layers of sulfur (S) atoms. It undergoes a characteristic series of symmetry-breaking phase transitions from a metal to a commensurate CDW phase through a nearly commensurate CDW phase. Within the commensurate CDW phase at low temperature, 12 Ta atoms shrink toward the central Ta atom, together with S layers swelled up along the c axis to form a 13×13 superstructure with a characteristic David star (DS) unit cell (Figure a). Under this distortion, 13 5d electrons of a unit cell, one from each Ta atom, form six pairs of filled bands and one half-filled band. The narrow bandwidth of this half-filled band, originating from the limited intersite hopping between neighboring DS with a large lattice constant, together with a strong on-site Coulomb repulsion, drives the formation of a Mott insulating state. Interesting quantum properties have been reported in the Mott insulating phase, such as the emerging superconductivity, − a quantum spin liquid phase, − and the stacking-order-dependent insulating nature. −

Due to the broken translational symmetry caused by the CDW formation, translative DWs of CDW domains are inevitable, and indeed, a variety of DWs were identified by STM studies. − The DWs could also be artificially created by electric and light pulses, and well-defined in-gap states were observed along them. , Figure b and c shows the high-resolution STM images of the two most popular types of DWs, called DW2 and DW4, which are uniquely defined by the relative positions of the DS clusters between two adjacent CDW domains (guided by yellow arrows in the figures). The DWs are also endowed with their own topological defects (two of them in Figure b), kinks, as observed frequently for DWs and dislocations in a wide variety of materials. Figure e and f shows STM images of a kinked domain wall (DW2) in the same field of view, taken with a time interval of about 30 min. As clearly shown, three kinks are observed in this particular section of a straight DW, which is bounded by the junctions with other DWs (one of them is shown in the upper right part of the image). The structure of the kinks is the same, as defined by shifting the DW2 in the transverse direction by two CDW unit cells (the left part of Figure d). , The kink connected by magenta dashed lines moves to the left by one CDW unit cell, and the other one connected by yellow lines on the right moves by four CDW units in Figure f. In contrast, the kink between the two moving ones does not show any apparent motion. Note also that one extra kink appears in the right part (Figure f), while the right-most kink moves by one CDW unit cell down. The mobility of kinks is well visualized in a sequence of successive scans with the same field of view over a longer period of several hours (see Supporting Information Video S1).

Similarly, Figure g and h shows two sequential STM images of a kinked DW4 region with a time interval of about 15 min. Six antikinks are observed here, three of which are mobile, and the other three (connected by white dashed lines) are static. The phase shifts of DW4 and antikinks are one primitive unit cell and one CDW unit cell along the transverse direction, respectively (the right part of Figure d). , The antikink connected by the magenta dashed line jumps to the right by two CDW unit cells, whereas the antikinks connected by orange dashed lines jump to the left and right, respectively, by one CDW unit cell. We note that all the above kink motions are much faster than the time scale of the present STM measurements, with the speed of tips typically 50 nm s–1, so that their motions are tracked only by the change of their positions between successive STM scans.

We then tracked the motions of individual kink solitons on many DW segments over a longer time span for both DW types. For example, we monitored the positions of five (seven) kink/antikink solitons in a DW2 (DW4) segment by 16 (86) serial STM images for 4 (21.5) hours in total time, as shown in Figure a (2b). For these particular DW segments, the total number of kinks is conserved, with no annihilation or creation events observed. Note also that these kinks do not collide or cross each other. We plot the position of each kink in the figure as a function of time. The five kinks of the DW2 segment exhibit translational motions along the DW axis (the x axis) except for the static x4 kink. The distance of each translational jump is just one DS unit cell (Δx = 1, called a small jump), and most of them are back-and-forth motions. Another example of kinks in DW2 is shown in Figure a, where the kinks exhibit small jumps consistently. The direction of the jump is random, without a preferential direction.

Figure b exhibits the motions of four antikinks (x1–x4) and three kinks (x5, x6, and x7) of the DW4 region over 21.5 h. Due to the lack of mirror or C 2 symmetry, the antikink is structurally distinguished from the kink, with a phase shift of half a CDW unit cell along the DWs. In this case, all kinks (antikinks) except x7 exhibit translational motion along the DW axis. More elaborately, the distance of each translation is a single unit cell (Δx = 1, called a small jump) or multiple unit cells up to four (Δx = 2, 3, or 4, large jumps). The kinks/antikinks x3, x4, x5, and x6 show only small jumps, but the x1 and x2 antikinks show both small and large jumps, respectively. Similarly, in Figure b and c, two other examples of kinks with large and small jumps are shown for two DW4 segments. Both of them show back-and-forth jumps, but with different Δx, that is, Δx = 3 and Δx = 1, respectively. Throughout the present measurements, the translative motions of the kinks are of a very limited random hopping type, which indicates the existence of a strong pinning potential. One can notice that the long jumps occur only for two specific kinks, while all the kinks have the same atomic structures. We thus guess that the pinning potential around a specific kink determines the length of the jumps. Since we do not observe any particular defects in most of the kinks observed, we suggest that the pinning potential is provided by the interlayer interaction, which has been established as strong in the present system. − That is, we assume that the point defects and DWs in the layer underneath affect the pinning potential. In the previous work, the strong coupling of DWs between the top and second layers was reported. Further studies are definitely requested on the details of the pinning mechanism.

To understand the mechanism of kink motions, we measured the average frequency and distance of the kink motion events as a function of tunneling current and temperature. Since the kink generation events at DW2 ends are relatively rare, the following results focus on pure translational modes of kink solitons. Here, the average frequency and distance are defined as the ratio of the number of translation events to the total time of measurements and the total distance of translation events to the number of translation events within one series of data, respectively. The average frequency of translational kink motions along DW4 is effectively enhanced by increasing the tunneling current (Figure a), especially for the tunneling current beyond 60 pA. A similar trend is observed for those in DW2, but the data are not as conclusive. This suggests that the kink translational motion is enhanced by the tunneling current, at least for the large tunneling current (the tunneling current in Figure c is 100 pA). On the other hand, as shown in Figure b, the average distances of translational kink motions for both types of DWs are independent of tunneling current within the error of our experiments, indicating that the tunneling current only affects the probability of kink translational motions, at least for DW4. This is consistent with the fact that the tunneling current lowers the activation barrier for the hopping motion. We also measured the temperature dependence of kink motions. The increase of the temperature from 78 K up to 140 K showed rather scattered data (Figure c), which may be due to the fact that we cannot follow the same DWs at different temperatures due to the thermal drift during the temperature increase of the microscope. We estimate that the temperature enhancement in this temperature range is marginal.

To investigate the dynamical nature of the kink, we performed DFT calculations using supercell structures containing a kink along DW2 and DW4 (see Supplementary Figure S2). For computational efficiency, we used small supercells (kink-to-kink distance of about 3 DS clusters), which reproduce the main features of a larger cell with only minor deviations (Supplementary Figure S3). In addition, we excluded extra electron correlation effects from our primary calculations because the dominant surface is a trivial band insulator in the most popular bilayer stacking configuration, and the energy barrier in this system is only marginally affected by electron correlation (see also Supplementary Figure S3). The DW2 structure consists of two symmetric imperfect DS clusters, each comprising 12 Ta atoms, as shown in Figure b (yellow imperfect DS clusters; see also Supplementary Figure S2). In the kink region, the imperfect DS clusters at the DW edges (the right cluster of the lower DW2 and the left cluster of the upper DW2) overlap, forming a smaller imperfect cluster with seven Ta atoms. The neighboring DW clusters preserve the DW2 structure but are slightly distorted outward to accommodate the kink cluster. Figure shows selected low-energy-barrier configurations of DW2 and DW4, chosen from the two possible kink and antikink configurations (see Supplementary Figure S4). The hopping energy barrier of the kink along DW2 (red) was obtained by linear interpolation between the initial and final states (Figure b). To achieve a one-step kink shift, the α cluster undergoes local distortions of Ta atoms ranging from 0.1 to 0.25 Å (arrows in Figure b), leading to the formation of a new kink, while the original kink cluster relaxes back into the DW2 configuration, forming the δ cluster at the final state. Simultaneously, the γ cluster at the domain boundaries transforms into a DW cluster, while the β cluster recovers the perfect DS structure. The intermediate state corresponds to two symmetric imperfect DS clusters at the kink, representing a mixed configuration of the α and δ DW2 clusters. The calculated hopping barrier of the kink is 8.1 meV/supercell (Figure a), which is significantly lower than that of 40.6 meV for a straight DW2. This reduction arises from structural differences; the maximum displacement of Ta atoms reaches 0.44 Å in the straight DW, whereas in the kink, it is only 0.25 Å (see Supplementary Figure S5). These results indicate that a collective shift of the entire DW is energetically unfavorable and that DW propagation instead occurs via kink dynamics.

We performed similar calculations for an antikink along DW4. The DW4 structure consists of a single row of imperfect DS clusters, each comprising nine Ta atoms, as shown in Figure d. In the antikink region, the originally perfect DS clusters at the DW edges overlap, forming two imperfect DS clusters along with one additional Ta atom. The transition process is analogous to that of DW2; the α′ cluster is divided to form a DW4 cluster (δ′) and the γ′ cluster at the domain boundaries transforms into the kink cluster, and the β′ cluster recovers the perfect DS cluster. The hopping energy barrier of the antikink is calculated to be 11.4 meV, with a maximum distortion of 0.27 Å, which is lower than the 23.8 meV barrier and the 0.41 Å distortion observed for the straight DW4 (Figure c and Supplementary Figure S5). The calculated hopping energy barrier of 8.1 (11.4) meV for a kink (antikink) along DW2 (DW4) predicts a hopping ratio of 1.7 (2.11) between 140 and 78 K through the Arrhenius relation D = D0 exp­(−E b/k B T), where D0 is the prefactor and E b is the energy barrier. This value is within the range of our experimental observation, as shown in Figure c. On the other hand, this Arrhenius analysis indicates that by lowering the temperature by about 10 K, one can reduce the thermally activated hopping by 1/10. This means that one can control the kink motion by tunneling current much more precisely at a lower temperature.

The effect of the temperature increase was also found in a different type of change in DWs and kink configurations. Figure d shows two sequential STM images scanned at 90 K with a time interval of about 15 min (indicated by the kinked color lines), which reveal the upward translation of the whole DW segment. This motion is well outside the kink motions discussed above. This is due to the annihilation of short DW segments (dashed circles). The total number of kinks is roughly conserved, while there are translational motions of the kink. Thus, the temperature effect on the DW configurations and kinks is beyond the simple kink motions at higher temperatures. Quantitatively speaking, the probability of the translation of the DW segments increases by five times from 78 to 90 K (blue circle in Figure S1 at 90 K). The DW segment that disappeared corresponds to a type DW1, which is energetically almost degenerate with DW4. The conversion between these two domain-wall types is energetically natural. The existence of various different DW structures, which is characteristic of the present system, provides this extra degree of freedom for the DW motion.

Conclusions and Outlook

We have directly identified the motions of electronic kink solitons formed along CDW domain walls of a 2D van der Waals material, 1T-TaS2, using real-time STM measurements. The kink solitons show limited jumping motions with a range of one to four CDW unit cells in a random direction, suggesting strong pinning. In addition, the kink soliton was observed to be generated or annihilated at the DW junctions. We further demonstrate that the probability of translational jumping motions of kink solitons is effectively enhanced by the STM tunneling current. The energetics and the atomistic details of the kink jumps were revealed by DFT calculations, which involve bond reconnections between the perfect and imperfect CDW clusters. To realize the ultimate aim of robust kink soliton informatics, the directions of future experiments can be toward the control over the direction and the range of translation motions and the controlled creation and annihilation of kinks by a tunneling current pulse. At the same time, an understanding of the defect-pinning mechanism and the control over pinning defects would be essential. Considering the crucial role of DWs in the present system for determining the macroscopic electronic properties and the related functionality such as neuromorphic devices and charge configuration memories, the microscopic controllability of individual DWs would open a new research direction.

Methods

STM Measurements: A 1T-TaS2 single crystal, grown by the chemical vapor transport method, was cleaved in the load-lock chamber at room temperature under a pressure of 1 × 10–9 Torr. The 1T-TaS2 single crystal sample with a fresh clean surface was then transferred into the scanning chamber of a commercial STM system (SPECS). The STM experiments were performed at liquid nitrogen temperature (78 K) without specification with a mechanically sharpened Pt–Ir tip. The STM images were obtained in constant-current mode, with a bias voltage applied to the sample with respect to the tip.

DFT calculations were carried out using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP), employing the projector augmented wave method and the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) generalized gradient approximation. A single-layer 1T-TaS2 was modeled with a vacuum spacing of about 24 Å. For the √13 × √13 CDW unit cell, we used a plane-wave cutoff energy of 259 eV and a 6 × 6 × 1 k-point mesh. Structural optimizations were performed by relaxing all atoms until the residual forces were below 0.02 eV/Å.
