Real-Time Algorithms for Model Predictive Control of Hybrid Dynamical Systems
Armin Nurkanović, Anton Pozharskiy, Moritz Diehl

Abstract
Model predictive control (MPC) of hybrid dynamical systems is challenging because the associated optimization problem is nonsmooth and the resulting feedback law is discontinuous. This paper develops real-time MPC algorithms for nonlinear hybrid systems modeled as dynamical complementarity systems. The resulting optimal control problems are formulated as mathematical programs with complementarity constraints (MPCCs). We show that the solution map of parametric MPCCs is discontinuous, and that standard nonlinear-programming-based approaches may become infeasible when the hybrid system switches. To address this, we introduce three real-time hybrid MPC schemes whose feedback phase solves a quadratic program with complementarity constraints per sample, yielding local discontinuous piecewise affine approximations of the MPC feedback law. Moreover, we derive continuity and differentiability results for parametric MPCCs, and establish conditions under which the approximation error of our new hybrid MPC algorithms remains uniformly bounded despite solution discontinuities. The algorithms are demonstrated on a robotic manipulation example, where contact sequences are discovered online.



I
Introduction

Model predictive control (MPC) is a feedback-control strategy that directly handles constraints and multivariable dynamics by repeatedly solving finite-horizon optimal control problems in real-time.
For linear and mildly nonlinear systems with continuous controls, it is a mature and widely applied methodology, with successful applications ranging from agile drone racing
[
1
]
to large-scale chemical plants
[
2
]
.

However, the dynamics of many practical systems cannot be described by smooth nonlinear differential equations because they exhibit nonsmooth
state-dependent events
, such as contact making and breaking in robotics
[
3
,
4
]
, phase transitions in chemical processes, and flow reversals in transport networks
[
5
]
.
This leads to
hybrid dynamical systems
, characterized by an interplay of discrete and continuous dynamics, which makes the application of MPC computationally challenging.
The theoretical properties of hybrid MPC are well studied
[
6
,
7
]
, and several computational advances exist
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.
However, unlike in the smooth MPC case
[
11
]
, there is currently no established generic real-time MPC framework for hybrid systems that provides accurate solution approximations at limited computational cost.
In this paper, we propose several hybrid MPC algorithms aimed at minimizing the computational load per sampling instant, while keeping approximation errors small and uniformly bounded.
We focus on the dynamic complementarity systems (DCS) formalism for hybrid systems, which covers most applications of interest and has favorable computational properties; cf. Section
II
.
We consider state-dependent discontinuities only, and do not treat discrete control decisions, which require a different set of modeling and analytic tools
[
12
,
13
]
.

Classical nonlinear MPC solves a sequence of related smooth parametric nonlinear programs (NLPs), where the parameter is typically the latest state estimate
x_{k}
.
Established real-time MPC algorithms exploit the similarity of neighboring problems and therefore restrict the amount of online computation
[
11
]
.
Most such methods combine the sequential quadratic programming (SQP)
[
14
]
method with the implicit function theorem (IFT)
[
15
]
applied to the necessary optimality conditions of the parametric NLP
[
16
,
17
]
to obtain a (piecewise) affine approximation of the optimal solution map.
This reduces the online feedback delay to the time needed for solving a single linear system or quadratic program (QP)
[
14
,
18
]
after the new state estimate
x_{k}
becomes available; cf. Sec.
VII
.

Discrete-time optimal control problems subject to DCS lead to parametric mathematical programs with complementarity constraints (MPCCs)
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
.
As for parametric NLPs, the SQP method can be applied to NLP reformulations of MPCCs, provided that a QP solver, capable of detecting a linearly independent active set basis, is available
[
22
]
.
However, interior-point QP solvers, which often perform better in MPC applications
[
23
]
, generally do not satisfy this requirement.
Even if such a QP solver is available, classical SQP-based methods face additional difficulties.
In particular, standard constraint qualifications (cf. Sec.
III
), which are necessary for the IFT-based sensitivity results underlying classical real-time MPC algorithms
[
16
,
17
]
, are violated by MPCCs at all feasible points.
On the other hand, compared to parametric NLPs, the parametric properties of MPCCs have received considerably less attention
[
20
,
24
,
25
]
.
We show that the solution map of parametric MPCCs, and thus the resulting hybrid MPC feedback law, is generically discontinuous and cannot be well approximated by QP steps alone.
We show that the QP subproblem is typically infeasible when the hybrid system must switch for a new state estimate
x_{k}
, cf. Figure
1
.

The central premise of this paper is that real‑time hybrid MPC must solve at least quadratic programs with complementarity constraints (QPCCs), and not ordinary QPs.
This view is consistent with several recent works that employ QPCCs in related settings
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
.
Section
VI
illustrates that a QPCC can produce discontinuous solution approximations and, unlike a QP, can detect new switching sequences.
Furthermore, solving a sequence of QPCCs
[
29
,
30
]
, i.e., the SQPCC method, is a natural extension of the SQP method
[
31
]
to MPCCs.
Despite their difficulty, MPCCs and QPCCs can now be solved reliably enough to support tailored real-time hybrid MPC algorithms
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
(cf. Section
VIII
).
Furthermore, MPCC-tailored methods based on continuous optimization techniques are usually orders of magnitude faster than integer methods applied to MPCC reformulations
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
.

Contributions.

This paper contributes as follows:

We extend existing sensitivity results for parametric MPCCs

,

]
.
In particular, under weaker assumptions than in

In Proposition

In Section

•

Section

Based on Theorem

None of the proposed algorithms requires an initial switching-sequence guess or heuristics for updating it, switches are discovered automatically online.

•

We provide an open-source prototype implementation of the proposed algorithms in the software package

[
41
]
, and illustrate in Sec.

II
Problem formulation

MPC requires the repeated solution of finite-horizon discrete-time optimal control problems (OCPs).
In this paper, we consider a hybrid OCP subject to a discrete-time dynamic complementarity system (DCS), to be solved at every sampling time
t_{k}
:

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\begin{subarray}{c}s,y,u\end{subarray}}{\mathrm{min}}\quad\textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\ell(s_{i},u_{i})+E(s_{N})

(1a)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\begin{subarray}{c}s,y,u\end{subarray}}}{\mathrm{min}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{min}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle s_{0}=x_{k},

(1b)

\displaystyle s_{i+1}=\phi_{f}(s_{i},y_{i},u_{i}),

\displaystyle\quad i=0,\ldots,N-1,

(1c)

\displaystyle 0=\phi_{\mathrm{int}}(s_{i},y_{i},u_{i}),

\displaystyle\quad i=0,\ldots,N-1,

(1d)

\displaystyle 0\leq\phi_{G}(y_{i})\perp\phi_{H}(y_{i})\geq 0,

\displaystyle\quad i=0,\ldots,N-1,

(1e)

\displaystyle g(s_{i},y_{i},u_{i})\leq 0,

\displaystyle\quad i=0,\ldots,N-1,

(1f)

\displaystyle r(s_{N}){\leq 0}.

(1g)

The vector
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
corresponds to the discrete-time differential states at time
t_{k}
, and
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
the discrete-time control input on the interval
t\in[t_{i},t_{i+1})
.
The vectors
s_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
collect the state trajectory for
i=0,\ldots,N
, and the vectors
y_{i}
collect all algebraic and possibly internal variables of the numerical integration method.
All these variables are collected in the vectors

s=(s_{0},\ldots,s_{N})
,

y=(y_{0},\ldots,y_{N-1})
, and

u=(u_{0},\ldots,u_{N-1})
.
The functions
\phi_{f}
,
\phi_{\mathrm{int}}
,
\phi_{G}
, and
\phi_{H}
define the discrete-time dynamic complementarity system (DCS).
The term
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}\to\mathbb{R}
is the running cost function for the
i
th stage, and
E:\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\to\mathbb{R}
is the terminal cost function.
The functions
g:\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{g}}
and
r:\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{r}}
define the path and terminal constraints in (
1f
) and (
1g
), respectively.

The DCS in (
1c
)–(
1e
) covers numerous hybrid dynamical systems, including discretizations of Filippov systems
[
42
]
, piecewise smooth systems
[
36
,
43
,
44
,
3
]
, complementarity Lagrangian systems (for robotics with friction and impacts)
[
45
,
4
]
, projected dynamical systems and (time-varying) sweeping processes
[
46
,
47
]
, relay systems
[
48
]
, mixed logical dynamical systems and max–min–plus scaling systems
[
49
]
, and hybrid systems with hysteresis
[
38
]
.
See
[
3
, Section 3]
,
[
9
]
, and
[
49
]
for equivalence results between DCS and the various other hybrid systems formalisms.

In our exposition, we highlighted DCS-induced MPCCs (
1
).
However, the proposed algorithms and our theoretical results cover also other sources of complementarity constraints, e.g., general piecewise smooth functions
[
50
, Chapter 11]
or logical path, vanishing
[
51
]
and state-triggered constraints
[
52
]
.

Here we assume that a discrete-time OCP (
1
) is given, but highlight that the time discretization of hybrid systems requires tailored transcription methods
[
5
,
43
]
to avoid the generation of spurious local minima
[
53
,
54
]
.

By defining
w=[s^{\top},y^{\top},u^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and suitable functions

f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
for the objective,

g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{g}}
for the equality constraints,
a selection matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{h}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
,

h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{h}}
for the inequality constraints, and

G,H:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
for the complementarity functions,
the discrete-time OCP (
1
) can be written compactly as a generic parametric MPCC:

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{subarray}}{\mathrm{min}}\quad f(w)

(2a)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{subarray}}}{\mathrm{min}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{min}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle h(w)+Mx_{k}=0,

(2b)

\displaystyle g(w)\leq 0,

(2c)

\displaystyle 0\leq G(w)\perp H(w)\geq 0.

(2d)

In ideal hybrid MPC, once a new state estimate
x_{k}
becomes available, one would instantaneously compute the global optimal solution
\bar{w}(x_{k})
and apply the first-stage control
\bar{u}_{0}(x_{k})
to the plant.
Since this is impossible in practice, as established in nonlinear MPC
[
11
]
, we derive algorithms that compute approximations
u_{0}(x_{k})\approx\bar{u}_{0}(x_{k})
of local minimizers of the MPCC (
2
) with as little computation effort as possible, while ensuring that the numerical error remains uniformly bounded, even if the hybrid dynamical system switches.

Note that if the complementarity active sets of the DCS in (
1
) were known in advance for some
x_{k}
, then the OCP would reduce to a standard NLP rather than an MPCC.
In other words, the switching sequence of the hybrid system would be fixed.
In Section
III-C
, we will see that such NLPs are called branch NLPs of the MPCC.
However, for every new state estimate
x_{k+1}
, such a fixed sequence does not have to remain feasible.
Hence, the goal of hybrid MPC is to implicitly discover a locally optimal switching sequence online.

III
Preliminaries on nonlinear programming

To state our novel results in a self-contained way, we review basic optimality and sensitivity results for standard nonlinear programs (NLP), as well as basic MPCC-tailored notions.

III-A

Regularity and local optimality

Consider the following parametric NLP:

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{subarray}}{\mathrm{min}}\quad f(w,p)

(3a)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{subarray}}}{\mathrm{min}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{min}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle h(w,p)=0,

(3b)

\displaystyle g(w,p)\leq 0,

(3c)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}}\to\mathbb{R}
, and
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{h}},\ g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{g}}
are twice continuously differentiable.
The vector
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}}
is a fixed parameter, and to keep the notation lighter we drop the dependencies on
p
whenever clear from the context.
The Lagrange multipliers for the equality and inequality constraints are denoted by
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{h}}
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{g}}
, respectively.
The feasible set of the NLP (
3
) is denoted by
\Omega:=\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid h(w)=0,g(w)\leq 0\}
.
The Lagrange function
L:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{h}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{g}}\to\mathbb{R}
of the NLP (
3
) reads as:

\displaystyle L(w,\lambda,\mu)

\displaystyle=f(w)+\lambda^{\top}h(w)+\mu^{\top}g(w).

The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions read as:

\displaystyle\nabla f(w)+\nabla h(w)\lambda+\nabla g(w)\mu=0,

(4a)

\displaystyle h(w)=0,

(4b)

\displaystyle 0\leq-g(w)\perp\mu\geq 0.

(4c)

A point
\bar{z}:=(\bar{w},\bar{\lambda},\bar{\mu})
satisfying these conditions is called a KKT point, and
\bar{w}
is called a stationary point.
If in addition a constraint qualification holds (defined below), the KKT conditions are first-order necessary conditions for optimality.
The active set at a feasible point
w\in\Omega
is defined as

\mathcal{A}(w)=\{i\in\{1,\ldots,m_{g}\}\mid g_{i}(w)=0\}
.
Its complement
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{C}}(w)=\{1,\ldots,m_{g}\}\setminus\mathcal{A}(w)
is the set of inactive constraints.
The strictly and weakly active sets at a KKT point
(x,\lambda,\mu)
are defined as

\mathcal{A}_{+}(w,\mu)=\{i\in\mathcal{A}(w)\mid\mu_{i}>0\},\,\mathcal{A}_{0}(w,\mu)=\{i\in\mathcal{A}(w)\mid\mu_{i}=0\}
.
We say that strict complementarity holds for a KKT point if

\mu_{i}>0,\ \forall i\in\mathcal{A}(w)
.
Next, we define some basic regularity concepts in nonlinear programming.

Definition 1

(LICQ)
.

At feasible point
w\in\Omega
, the linear independence constraint qualification (LICQ) is said to hold if the set of vectors
\{\nabla h_{i}(w)\mid i=1,\ldots,m_{h}\}\cup\{\nabla g_{i}(w)\mid i\in\mathcal{A}(w)\}
are linearly independent.

The LICQ is sufficient for unique Lagrange multipliers.
We use the notation
\nabla g_{{\mathcal{I}}}(w)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times|{\mathcal{I}}|}
for a matrix whose columns are the vectors
\nabla g_{i}(w),\ i\in{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m_{g}\}
.
Next, we define some second-order sufficient conditions (SOSC).
The tangent space of all equality and strictly active constraints is called the strong critical cone:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{S}}(w,\mu)=\{d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\nabla h(w)^{\top}d\!=\!0,\,\nabla g_{\mathcal{A}_{+}(w,\mu)}(w)^{\top}d\!=\!0\}.

The usual critical cone is defined by adding restrictions w.r.t. weakly active constraints:

\mathcal{C}(w,\mu)=\{d\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{S}}(w,\mu)\mid\nabla g_{\mathcal{A}_{0}(w,\mu)}(w)^{\top}d\leq 0\}
.

If strict complementarity holds we have that
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{S}}(w,\mu)=\mathcal{C}(w,\mu)
.
We will frequently use the so-called strong second-order sufficient conditions (SSOSC).

Definition 2
.

The SSOSC is said to hold at a stationary point
w\in\Omega
if there exist corresponding multipliers
(\lambda,\mu)
such that
(w,\lambda,\mu)
is a KKT point, and if the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle d^{\top}\nabla_{ww}^{2}L(w,\lambda,\mu)d>0,\ \forall d\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{S}}(w,\mu)\setminus\{0\}.

(5)

A less restrictive notion is the usual SOSC, which is obtained if we replace
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{S}}(w,\mu)
by
\mathcal{C}(w,\mu)
in (
5
).

(Theorem 2.4.5,

]
)
.

,

, and

in a neighborhood of

, if the SSOSC hold for problem (

is an isolated local optimal solution of (

) for

being the unique Lagrange multiplier vector associated with

;

(b)

there exist a neighborhood of

MPCC theory

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{subarray}}{\mathrm{min}}\quad f(w)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{subarray}}}{\mathrm{min}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{min}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle h(w)=0,

(7b)

\displaystyle g(w)\leq 0,

(7c)

\displaystyle G(w)\geq 0,

(7d)

\displaystyle H(w)\geq 0,

(7e)

\displaystyle G_{i}(w)H_{i}(w)\leq 0,\quad i=1,\ldots,m.

(7f)

This and other NLP reformulations of the MPCC
[
20
]
result in highly degenerate NLPs.
In particular, the constraints (
7d
)-(
7f
) lead to violation of the LICQ (and also weaker conditions) at all feasible points
[
20
]
.
As a result, classical NLP theory and algorithms usually cannot be applied successfully to this problem class.
Instead, applying tailored MPCC notions and algorithms resolves most of the issues.
They all rely on the piecewise nature of the MPCC’s feasible set.

III-C
1
First-order optimality conditions

Algebraic conditions for stationarity of regular NLPs are given by the KKT conditions (
4
).
For MPCCs, there are more nuances, and there are several first-order stationarity concepts, which we review briefly here.
For surveys see
[
20
,
21
]
.

We call an NLP regular if it satisfies some first- and second-order regularity conditions, e.g., LICQ and SSOSC.
Most of the MPCC theory relies on defining auxiliary regular NLPs related to the MPCC.
For this purpose, we define the following index sets which depend on a feasible point
w
of the MPCC (
2
):

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{0+}(w)

\displaystyle=\{i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\mid G_{i}(w)=0,H_{i}(w)>0\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{+0}(w)

\displaystyle=\{i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\mid G_{i}(w)>0,H_{i}(w)=0\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{00}(w)

\displaystyle=\{i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\mid G_{i}(w)=0,H_{i}(w)=0\}.

If clear from the context, we omit the argument
w
in the index sets.
We call indices
i\in{\mathcal{I}}_{00}(w)
degenerate index pairs, and indices
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}_{00}(w)
nondegenerate.
Most theoretical difficulties arise in the presence of degenerate indices.
To compactly define branch NLPs below, we define a few more index sets.
Let
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(w)=\{\tilde{\mathcal{I}}(w)\subseteq{\mathcal{I}}_{00}(w)\}
denote the powerset of the degenerate indices.
Then, at a feasible point
w\in\Omega
, we define the set of index sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(w)=\{{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}\mid\exists\,\tilde{{\mathcal{I}}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}(w),\,{\mathcal{I}}={\mathcal{I}}_{0+}(w)\cup\tilde{{\mathcal{I}}}\}.

Moreover, for a given
{\mathcal{I}}\in\mathcal{P}(w)
we define its complement
{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathrm{C}}=\{1,\ldots,m\}\setminus{\mathcal{I}}
.
The number of sets in
\mathcal{P}(w)
is equal to
N=2^{c}
, where
c
is the number of degenerate indices.

Let
\bar{w}
be a feasible point of the MPCC (
2
).
Using the partitions
{\mathcal{I}}\in\mathcal{P}(\bar{w})
, we define the
N
so-called branch NLPs, denoted by
{\mathrm{BNLP}}_{\mathcal{I}}
, obtained by fixing the corresponding branches of the L-shaped complementarity set:

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{subarray}}{\mathrm{min}}\quad f(w)

(8a)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{subarray}}}{\mathrm{min}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{min}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle h(w)=0,

(8b)

\displaystyle g(w)\leq 0,

(8c)

\displaystyle G_{i}(w)=0,\ H_{i}(w)\geq 0,\quad\forall i\in{\mathcal{I}}(\bar{w}),

(8d)

\displaystyle G_{i}(w)\geq 0,\ H_{i}(w)=0,\quad\forall i\in{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathrm{C}}(\bar{w}).

(8e)

Another useful NLP associated to the MPCC (
2
) is the so-called relaxed NLP (RNLP), which reads as:

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{subarray}}{\mathrm{min}}\quad f(w)

(9a)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{subarray}}}{\mathrm{min}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{min}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle h(w)=0,

(9b)

\displaystyle g(w)\leq 0,

(9c)

\displaystyle G_{i}(w)=0,\ H_{i}(w)\geq 0,\quad\forall i\in{\mathcal{I}}_{0+}(\bar{w}),

(9d)

\displaystyle G_{i}(w)\geq 0,\ H_{i}(w)=0,\quad\forall i\in{\mathcal{I}}_{+0}(\bar{w}),

(9e)

\displaystyle G_{i}(w)\geq 0,\ H_{i}(w)\geq 0,\quad\forall i\in{\mathcal{I}}_{00}(\bar{w}).

(9f)

Observe that if
{\mathcal{I}}_{00}(\bar{w})=\emptyset
, then there is only one BNLP and it coincides with the RNLP.
The point
\bar{w}
is a local minimizer of the MPCC if and only if it is a local minimizer of every
{\mathrm{BNLP}}_{\mathcal{I}}

[
19
,
20
]
.
Therefore, these auxiliary NLPs provide a natural framework for defining tailored MPCC notions.

Definition 3
.

The MPCC (
2
) is said to satisfy MPCC–LICQ at a feasible point
\bar{w}
if the corresponding RNLP associated with
\bar{w}
satisfies the LICQ at the same point
\bar{w}
.

Definition 4
.

The MPCC–Lagrangian is the standard Lagrangian for the RNLP, and reads as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(w,\lambda,\mu,\xi,\nu)&=f(w)+\lambda^{\top}h(w)+\mu^{\top}g(w)\\
&-\xi^{\top}G(w)-\nu^{\top}H(w),\end{split}

with the MPCC–Lagrange multipliers
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{h}}
,
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{g}}
,
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Note that the MPCC–Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
differs from the standard Lagrangian
L(\cdot)
for the NLP reformulation of the MPCC (
7
), by omitting the terms
G_{i}(w)H_{i}(w)
and their multipliers.
The MPCC–Lagrangian coincides with the usual Lagrangian of the
{\mathrm{BNLP}}_{\mathcal{I}}
and is independent of the index
{\mathcal{I}}
.

By applying the KKT conditions to the RNLP (
9
), we obtain the so-called strong stationarity (S-stationarity) concept.
In particular, a feasible point
\bar{w}
is called S-stationary if there exist Lagrange multipliers
\bar{\lambda},\bar{\mu},\bar{\xi}
and
\bar{\nu}
such that:

\displaystyle\nabla_{w}\mathcal{L}(\bar{w},\bar{\lambda},\bar{\mu},\bar{\xi},\bar{\nu})=0,

\displaystyle h(\bar{w})=0,

\displaystyle 0\leq\bar{\mu}\perp-g(\bar{w})\geq 0,

\displaystyle G_{i}(\bar{w})=0,\;\bar{\xi}_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\;H_{i}(\bar{w})\geq 0,\;\bar{\nu}_{i}=0,\;

\displaystyle\forall i\in{\mathcal{I}}_{0+}(\bar{w}),

\displaystyle G_{i}(\bar{w})\geq 0,\;\bar{\xi}_{i}=0,\;H_{i}(\bar{w})=0,\;\bar{\nu}_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\;

\displaystyle\forall i\in{\mathcal{I}}_{+0}(\bar{w}),

\displaystyle G_{i}(\bar{w})=0,\;\bar{\xi}_{i}\geq 0,\;

\displaystyle\forall i\in\mathcal{I}_{00}(\bar{w}),

\displaystyle H_{i}(\bar{w})=0,\;\bar{\nu}_{i}\geq 0,\;

\displaystyle\forall i\in\mathcal{I}_{00}(\bar{w}).

The last two conditions arise from complementarity slackness, but are fixed because the active sets are known at
\bar{w}
.
Weaker stationarity concepts can be derived from alternative auxiliary NLPs and from tools of nonsmooth calculus
[
20
]
.
They are distinguished by weaker sign conditions on
\bar{\xi}_{i}
and
\bar{\nu}_{i}
for
i\in{\mathcal{I}}_{00}(\bar{w})
, and are given as follows:

•

Weak Stationarity (W-stationarity): if
\bar{\xi}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\bar{\nu}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}_{00}(\bar{w})
.

•

Abadie Stationarity (A-stationarity): if
\bar{\xi}_{i}\geq 0
or
\bar{\nu}_{i}\geq 0
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}_{00}(\bar{w})
.

•

Clarke Stationarity (C-stationarity): if
\bar{\xi}_{i}\bar{\nu}_{i}\geq 0
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}_{00}(\bar{w})
.

•

Mordukhovich Stationarity (M-stationarity): if either
\bar{\xi}_{i}>0
and
\bar{\nu}_{i}>0
or
\bar{\xi}_{i}\bar{\nu}_{i}=0
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}_{00}(\bar{w})
.

The stationarity concepts are ordered according to decreasing strength as follows: S, M, C, A and W.
If
{\mathcal{I}}_{00}=\emptyset
, then all multiplier-based stationarity concepts coincide and they reduce to S-stationarity.
Except for S-stationarity, all other stationarity concepts may admit first-order descent directions
[
20
,
21
]
, even if MPCC–LICQ holds.
A stationarity concept, which does not suffer from this is the so-called B-stationarity, which we recall next.
We consider a definition due to Hu and Ralph
[
24
]
, formulated in terms of BNLPs.
For other equivalent definitions see, e.g.,
[
21
, Sec. 2.1]
.

Definition 5
.

A feasible point
\bar{w}\in\Omega
of the MPCC (
2
) is a piecewise stationary or B-stationary point of the MPCC (
2
) if
\bar{w}
is a stationary point of the
{\mathrm{BNLP}}_{\mathcal{I}}
for each
{\mathcal{I}}\in\mathcal{P}(\bar{w})
, that is for each partition, there exists Lagrange multipliers
\bar{\lambda}^{{\mathcal{I}}},\,\bar{\mu}^{{\mathcal{I}}},\,\bar{\xi}^{{\mathcal{I}}}
and
\bar{\nu}^{{\mathcal{I}}}
such that:

\displaystyle\nabla_{w}\mathcal{L}(\bar{w},\bar{\lambda}^{\mathcal{I}},\bar{\mu}^{\mathcal{I}},\bar{\nu}^{\mathcal{I}},\bar{\xi}^{\mathcal{I}})=0,

(10a)

\displaystyle h(\bar{w})=0,

(10b)

\displaystyle 0\leq\bar{\mu}^{\mathcal{I}}\perp-g(\bar{w})\geq 0,

(10c)

\displaystyle G_{i}(\bar{w})=0,

\displaystyle\forall i\in{\mathcal{I}},

(10d)

\displaystyle H_{i}(\bar{w})=0

\displaystyle\forall i\in{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathrm{C}},

(10e)

\displaystyle 0\leq H_{i}(\bar{w})\perp\bar{\nu}_{i}^{\mathcal{I}}\geq 0,

\displaystyle\forall i\in{\mathcal{I}},

(10f)

\displaystyle 0\leq G_{i}(\bar{w})\perp\bar{\xi}_{i}^{\mathcal{I}}\geq 0,

\displaystyle\forall i\in{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathrm{C}}.

(10g)

Under MPCC–LICQ, a B-stationary point
\bar{w}
has the same uniquely determined multiplier vector for every branch NLP
{\mathrm{BNLP}}_{{\mathcal{I}}}
with
{\mathcal{I}}\in\mathcal{P}(\bar{w})

[
19
, Proposition 4.3.5]
.
As a result, B-stationarity can be checked by verifying the S-stationarity conditions for a single branch NLP, which removes the combinatorial complexity.
Conversely, if
\bar{w}
is an S-stationary point of the MPCC (
2
), then it is also B-stationary
[
20
, Theorem 4]
.

In summary, B-stationarity is the tightest stationarity concept for MPCCs, and under MPCC-LICQ it is equivalent to S-stationarity.
This is the setting considered in this paper.

III-C
2
Second-order optimality conditions

The second-order sufficient conditions for MPCCs are also defined in terms of the RNLP and BNLPs.
They are formulated at an S-stationary point so that a single set of multipliers is valid for all BNLPs.
The strong MPCC critical cone is obtained as the union of the usual or strong critical cones of all
{\mathrm{BNLP}}_{{\mathcal{I}}}
at
\bar{w}

[
19
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{MPCC}}^{\mathrm{S}}(\bar{w})\!=\!\!\!\bigcup_{{\mathcal{I}}\in\mathcal{P}(\bar{w})}\!\!\Big\{d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \Big|\ \nabla h(\bar{w})^{\top}d=0,

\displaystyle\nabla g_{i}(\bar{w})^{\top}d=0,\ \forall i\in\mathcal{A}_{+}(\bar{w},\bar{\mu}),

\displaystyle\nabla G_{i}(\bar{w})^{\top}d=0,\ \forall i\in{\mathcal{I}}\cup\{j\in{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bar{\xi}_{j}>0\},

\displaystyle\nabla H_{i}(\bar{w})^{\top}d=0,\ \forall i\in{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathrm{C}}\cup\{j\in{\mathcal{I}}\mid\bar{\nu}_{j}>0\}\Big\}.

Definition 6

(MPCC–SSOSC)
.

The MPCC–SSOSC (or the piecewise SSOSC) holds at S-stationary point
\bar{w}
with multipliers

(\bar{\lambda},\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu},\bar{\xi})
if

d^{\top}\nabla_{ww}^{2}\mathcal{L}(\bar{w},\bar{\lambda},\bar{\mu},\bar{\xi},\bar{\nu})\,d>0\quad\forall d\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{MPCC}}(\bar{w})\setminus\{0\}.

The piecewise (S)SOSC implies the standard (S)SOSC for each BNLP.
It is sufficient for local optimality of an S-stationary point.
A stronger condition is the so-called RNLP-SSOSC, which is the usual SSOSC of the RNLP (
9
).

Next, we define strict-complementarity type conditions for MPCCs, referred to as upper-level strict complementarity (ULSC)
[
20
]
.
In addition, in this paper, we define a new weaker condition, the partial ULSC (PULSC).

Definition 7

(ULSC)
.

Let
\bar{w}
be an S-stationary point of the MPCC (
2
), with associated MPCC–Lagrange multipliers
(\bar{\lambda},\bar{\mu},\bar{\xi},\bar{\nu})
.
The upper-level strict complementarity (ULSC) condition holds if, for every
i\in{\mathcal{I}}_{00}(\bar{w})
, the multipliers satisfy the condition:

\bar{\xi}_{i}>0
and
\bar{\nu}_{i}>0
.

Definition 8

(PULSC)
.

Let
\bar{w}
be an S-stationary point of the MPCC (
2
), with associated MPCC–Lagrange multipliers
(\bar{\lambda},\bar{\mu},\bar{\xi},\bar{\nu})
.
The partial upper-level strict complementarity (PULSC) condition holds if, for every
i\in{\mathcal{I}}_{00}(\bar{w})
, the multipliers satisfy the condition:

\bar{\xi}_{i}>0
or
\bar{\nu}_{i}>0
.

IV
Sequential quadratic programming with complementarity constraints (SQPCC)

The SQPCC method was introduced by Scholtes
[
29
]
.
This method is a key ingredient for real-time hybrid MPC algorithms introduced in Sec.
VII
.
Here, we recall a recent local convergence result that does not require active-set stabilization, strict complementarity, or ULSC
[
30
]
.
Complementarity active-set changes in the SQPCC method are relevant, since they correspond to discovering new switches in real-time hybrid MPC.

In classical MPC, SQP iterations are closely linked to parametric NLP sensitivities and piecewise affine solution approximations, cf. Sec.
III-B
.
Due to the violation of constraint qualifications in NLP reformulations of MPCCs, this link is lost when SQP is applied to them.
This motivates the use of SQPCC, where an analogous relation to the parametric MPCC (
2
) is preserved, as shown in Sec.
V-B
.

The SQP method for standard NLPs is derived from a piecewise linearization of the KKT conditions.
In analogy to SQP, in the SQPCC method, the B-stationarity conditions (
10
) of the MPCC are structurally linearized, cf.
[
29
,
30
]
.
This corresponds to the B-stationarity conditions of a quadratic program with complementarity constraints (QPCC):

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\begin{subarray}{c}\Delta w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{subarray}}{\mathrm{min}}\quad\nabla f^{k,\top}\Delta w+\frac{1}{2}\Delta w^{\top}Q^{k}\Delta w

(11a)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\begin{subarray}{c}\Delta w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{subarray}}}{\mathrm{min}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{min}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle h^{k}+\nabla h^{k,\top}\Delta w+Mx=0,

(11b)

\displaystyle g^{k}+\nabla g^{k,\top}\Delta w\leq 0,

(11c)

\displaystyle 0\!\leq\!G^{k}\!+\!\nabla G^{k,\top}\!\Delta w\!\perp\!H^{k}\!+\!\nabla H^{k,\top}\!\Delta w\!\geq\!0,

(11d)

where
Q^{k}\approx\nabla_{ww}^{2}\mathcal{L}(w^{k},\lambda^{k},\mu^{k},\xi^{k},\nu^{k})
is a positive definite approximation of the exact Hessian.
It is important to note that the QPCC uses the Hessian of the MPCC Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
, and not the standard Lagrangian
L(\cdot)
of (
7
).
The parameter
x
is fixed in (
11b
), but we keep it for later reference.

The convergence properties of both the SQP and SQPCC methods depend on the problem functions used to define the quadratic subproblem.
We collect all relevant functions in

\displaystyle\Psi(z):=\Big(\nabla_{w}\mathcal{L}(w,\lambda,\mu,\xi,\nu),\,h(w),\,-g(w),\,G(w),\,H(w)\Big),

where
z=(w,\lambda,\mu,\xi,\nu)
collects all primal-dual variables of the MPCC.
The assumptions for convergence are formulated for
\nabla\Psi^{\top}(z^{k})
and its approximation
\nabla\tilde{\Psi}(z^{k})^{\top}
, where
Q^{k}
replaces the exact Hessian of
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
in the QPCC (
11
), cf. Eq. (
12
).
We discuss the assumptions below the theorem.

Theorem 2

(Theorem 5.1 in
[
30
]
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{h}}
,
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{g}}
,
H:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be twice continuously differentiable in a neighborhood of a point
\bar{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
\bar{w}
be a local solution of problem (
2
), satisfying MPCC–LICQ and MPCC–SSOSC with the associated unique Lagrange multiplier
(\bar{\lambda},\bar{\mu},\bar{\xi},\bar{\nu})\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{h}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{g}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Further, suppose there exist constants
\omega>0
and
\bar{\kappa}<\frac{1}{3\gamma}
, where
\gamma>0
is a Lipschitz constant of the BNLPs, and a sequence
\{\kappa^{k}\}
with
\kappa^{k}\in[0,\bar{\kappa}]
such that, for all
z^{k}
and
z
, the following inequalities hold:

\displaystyle\|\nabla\Psi(z)^{\top}-\nabla\Psi(z^{k})^{\top}\|\leq\omega\|z-z^{k}\|,

(12a)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\Psi(z^{k})^{\top}-\nabla\tilde{\Psi}(z^{k})^{\top}\|\leq\kappa^{k}.

(12b)

Then there exist a constant
\varepsilon>0
such that, for any starting point
z^{0}=(w^{0},\lambda^{0},\mu^{0},\xi^{0},\nu^{0})\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(\bar{z})
and
\bar{z}=(\bar{w},\bar{\lambda},\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu},\bar{\xi})
, there exists a (not necessarily unique) sequence

\{(w^{k},\lambda^{k},\mu^{k},\nu^{k},\xi^{k})\}\subset{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(\bar{z})
such that for
k=0,1,\dots
, the point
w^{k+1}
is an S-stationary point of the QPCC (
11
) with a positive definite Hessian approximation

, and
(\lambda^{k+1},\mu^{k+1},\nu^{k+1},\xi^{k+1})
is an associated Lagrange multiplier.
Moreover, each such sequence converges to
(\bar{w},\bar{\lambda},\bar{\mu},\bar{\xi},\bar{\nu})
, and the rate of convergence is linear, satisfying the contraction inequality

\displaystyle\|z^{k+1}-\bar{z}\|\leq\alpha^{k}\|z^{k}-\bar{z}\|+\beta\|z^{k+1}-\bar{z}\|^{2},

(13)

with constants
\alpha^{k}\in[0,1)
for all
k
and
\beta>0
.
If, in addition,
\{\kappa^{k}\}\to 0
, then
\alpha^{k}\to 0
and the convergence rate is superlinear; if
\bar{\kappa}=0
, then
\alpha^{k}=0
and the convergence rate is quadratic.

This theorem provides a local convergence estimate in (
13
) analogous to Newton/SQP local convergence estimates.
The MPCC-LICQ and MPCC-SSOSC are natural MPCC counterparts of the LICQ and SSOSC assumed in the local convergence of the SQP method
[
58
]
.
The
\kappa
–
\omega
conditions (
12
) are commonly employed in the analysis of Newton-type methods
[
58
]
, to which SQPCC also belongs
[
29
,
30
]
.
Condition (
12a
) is a Lipschitz assumption on
\nabla\Phi(z)^{\top}
and quantifies the nonlinearity of the problem data.
In turn, the compatibility condition (
12b
) bounds the error in the QPCC approximations, e.g., for the exact Hessian in the QPCC (
11
) we have
\bar{\kappa}=0
(and quadratic convergence), whereas for a Gauss–Newton approximation usually
\kappa^{k}>0
(and linear convergence).
The smaller the approximation error
\kappa^{k}>0
, the faster the linear convergence rate; cf.
[
30
]
for details.
Finally, we note that the SQPCC method does not require a global optimizer of the QPCC subproblems; S-stationarity is sufficient.
Under the given assumptions, this can be achieved by local QPCC solvers
[
39
,
35
]
, and keeping the iterates in
{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(\bar{z})
can be achieved by warm-starting the QPCC solver.

V
On parametric MPCCs

The main goal of this section is to characterize the continuity and differentiability properties of the solution map of a parametric MPCC in order to properly approximate it and quantify respective errors.
For the sake of generality, we regard a version of the MPCC (
2
) where all problem functions depend on a parameter
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}}

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{subarray}}{\mathrm{min}}\quad f(w,p)

(14a)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\end{subarray}}}{\mathrm{min}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{min}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle h(w,p)=0,

(14b)

\displaystyle g(w,p)\leq 0,

(14c)

\displaystyle 0\leq G(w,p)\perp H(w,p)\geq 0,

(14d)

We denote the branch NLPs corresponding to a feasible point
\bar{w}
with a parameter
\bar{p}
as

The optimal value function of (

.

V-A

Piecewise differentiability of MPCC solutions

The parametric MPCC literature has mainly studied the (directional) differentiability of the optimal value function of (

25

]

For real-time MPC, however, a key object is the local solution map

\mathrm{PC}^{1}

VI
Path-following via the SQPCC method

In the literature, continuously computing approximations to the solution map of a parametric optimization problem is referred to as path-following
[
11
]
.
This is what essentially all real-time MPC algorithms do, and here we investigate how this is done for MPCCs via the SQPCC method.

In many real-time MPC algorithms, only a single SQP iteration is carried out in the
feedback phase
(after a new state estimate
x_{k+1}
becomes available)
[
11
]
.
Here, we consider the generalization of this idea to the SQPCC method as a core building block for hybrid real-time MPC algorithms.
Section
VII
presents several algorithms based on this principle, which further improve the current linearization point through additional computations in the
preparation phase
(in-between samples, before the next state estimate is available).

In particular, we consider a parameter sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
in the parametric MPCC (
2
), with solutions denoted compactly by
\bar{z}^{k}=z(x_{k})
.

VII
Real-time algorithms for hybrid MPC

Building on the results of the previous sections on parametric MPCCs and SQPCC-based tracking, we propose several approximate hybrid MPC algorithms.
These methods generalize established real-time MPC ideas
[
11
]
to hybrid systems.

We consider a sequence of sampling times
\{t_{k}\}
and corresponding state estimates
\{x_{k}\}
.
The goal of real-time MPC algorithms is to compute, at each sampling time, a new solution approximation
z^{k+1}\approx\bar{z}^{k+1}
and to apply the corresponding feedback at time
t_{k+1}+t_{\Delta}
with as small a computational delay
t_{\Delta}
as possible.
To reduce the delay, the algorithms split their computations into a
preparation phase
, for
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})
, where all computations that can be carried out before a new state estimate
x_{k+1}
is available, and a
feedback phase
, for
t\in[t_{k+1},t_{k+1}\!+\!t_{\Delta}]
, which uses the minimal amount of computation required to obtain a good solution approximation at each sampling instant.
In hybrid MPC, this minimal computation is a single QPCC solve.

VII-A

The hybrid real-time iteration (HyRTI)

The classical real-time iteration (RTI) scheme for smooth nonlinear MPC performs a single SQP step per sampling interval
[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
.
In the hybrid setting, this idea extends to performing a single SQPCC iteration, which yields the Hybrid RTI (HyRTI).
Observe that, in optimal control problems (
1
) (both classical and hybrid), the new state estimate
x_{k+1}
enters the equality constraints (
2
) linearly.
Therefore, all function and derivative evaluations, depending on
z^{k}
, and required to set up the QPCC (
17
) can be carried out without knowledge of
x_{k+1}
.
Thus, we have the following computations in the HyRTI.

Preparation phase:

At
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})
construct the QPCC (
17
) at the linearization point
z^{k}
.

Feedback phase:

At
t=t_{k+1}
update the parameter to
x_{k+1}
, solve QPCC (
17
) and return
u_{k+1}
at
t=t_{k+1}\!+\!t_{\Delta}
.

HyRTI is the one-step SQPCC algorithm with conveniently ordered computations.
Hence, it follows from Theorem
5
, that the HyRTI has a bounded error over all iterates.
It can handle active-set changes both in the standard inequality but also in the complementarity constraints.
It is suitable for small to medium scale systems with fast dynamics.

VII-B

The hybrid advanced-step controller (HyASC)

The advanced-step controller for nonlinear MPC introduced in
[
18
]
solves in the preparation phase an optimal control problem with a predicted initial state
x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}
to convergence.
In the feedback phase, the active sets at this solution are fixed and, by applying the implicit function theorem, a tangential predictor is computed in the feedback phase
[
18
, Section 3]
.
Just like the QP, the usual tangential predictor cannot capture complementarity active-set changes, cf. Fig
1
.
Instead, we must solve a QPCC in the feedback phase.

We generalize this idea to the hybrid setting and introduce the hybrid advanced-step controller (HyASC), which requires the following computations.

Preparation phase:

For
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})
perform:

1.)

At
t=t_{k}
, using the previous state and controls
(x_{k},u_{k})
, predict the next state
x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}
.

2.)

Over
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})
, using the initial guess
z^{k}
, solve the OCP (
2
) with the parameter
x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}
to convergence and obtain
\bar{z}^{k+1}_{\mathrm{pred}}
.

3.)

Construct the QPCC (
17
) at the lin. point
\bar{z}^{k+1}_{\mathrm{pred}}
.

Feedback phase:

At
t=t_{k+1}
update the parameter to
x_{k+1}
, and during
t\in[t_{k+1},t_{k+1}+t_{\Delta}]
solve the QPCC (
17
) and return
u_{0}(x_{k+1})
.

The MPCC in step 2.) of the preparation phase can be solved with any common MPCC algorithm
[
21
,
33
]
.
This is the most computationally intensive part, and in its basic form, HyASC is more suitable for systems with slower dynamics.
The main benefit of HyASC is that the full MPCC solves can compensate errors that do not satisfy the conditions of Theorem
5
, provided that
x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}
is sufficiently close to
x_{k+1}
.

Lastly, we discuss the feedback-phase error when a QPCC is solved but the linearization point is
\bar{z}^{k+1}_{\mathrm{pred}}
instead of
z^{k}
.
Provided that
\|\bar{z}^{k+1}_{\mathrm{pred}}-\bar{z}^{k+1}\|<r_{z}<\varepsilon
, and that the remaining assumptions of Theorem
5
hold, the error estimate (
19
) applies.
In particular, the bound reduces to:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\bar{z}^{k+1}-z^{k+1}\|&\leq\eta_{1}^{k}\,\|x_{k+1}-x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}\|+\eta_{2}\,\|x_{k+1}-x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}\|^{2}\\
&+\alpha^{k}\delta_{k}+\sigma\delta_{k}^{2}.\end{split}

In addition, if no complementarity active-set changes happen between
x_{k+1}
and
x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}
, then
\delta_{k}=0
, and the bound is even tighter.
Further, using the exact Hessian yields in this case the bound
O(\|x_{k+1}-x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}\|^{2})
.

In practice, to reduce the feedback time in the HyASC, in the feedback phase one may solve a branch QP corresponding to the MPCC’s solution found in the preparation phase.
If the feedback QP introduces a large error, the next preparation-phase solve can compensate for it.

VII-C

The hybrid advanced step RTI (HyAS-RTI)

The advanced-step RTI (AS-RTI) combines the ideas of the ASC and RTI methods
[
40
,
61
]

In the feedback phase, it solves a QP as in RTI, while in the preparation phase it does not solve the problem to convergence but performs

for the feedback QP
[
40

61
]

These ideas extend naturally to the hybrid setting, yielding the hybrid AS-RTI (HyAS-RTI) method, with computations as follows.

Preparation phase:

For
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})
perform:

1.)

At
t=t_{k}
, using the previous state and controls
(x_{k},u_{k})
, predict the next state
x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}
.

2.)

Over
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})
, using the initial guess

3.)

Feedback phase:

For the sake of brevity, we consider only SQPCC iterations in the preparation phase.

VIII
Numerical example

In this section, we showcase the new hybrid MPC algorithms on a robotic manipulation example.
The example shows that the algorithms are able to discover new contact sequences online, react to disturbances, and handle changing references.

We consider a two-link robot modeling a finger, which is supposed to spin a fixed wheel.
This example is inspired by a similar example from
[
4
]
, and a full description of the dynamic equations is also available in the example’s GitHub repository.

The system has three degrees of freedom
q=(q_{1},q_{2},\theta)
, namely the two joint angles and the wheel angle.
The state consists of the positions and velocities, with
x=(q,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
, where
v=\dot{q}
.
The controls are the two joint torques
u\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
of the finger.
The wheel is not actuated, and it can be moved only by interaction with the finger through normal and frictional contact forces.
These forces are modeled via complementarity constraints, which make this a hybrid system.

The goal in the optimal control problem (OCP) is for the unactuated wheel to follow a time-varying reference, with minimal control effort of the finger.
The continuous-time OCP, subject to the finger-wheel complementarity Lagrangian system with friction and impacts, reads as:

IX
Conclusion and future work

This developed a comprehensive framework for real-time MPC algorithms for hybrid dynamical systems.
The key observation is that QP feedback corrections are, in general, insufficient across complementarity active-set changes, whereas QPCC subproblems retain the structure needed to capture switching.
On the theoretical side, we characterized local continuity, and discontinuity properties of parametric MPCC solution maps, showed how one-sided directional derivatives can be obtained from a single QPCC.
We derived local one-step error bounds for SQPCC-based path-following in the presence of solution jumps.
Based on this, generalizing established MPC paradigms, we proposed several real-time hybrid MPC schemes that use these QPCC corrections in the feedback phase and illustrated their behavior on an example.
An open-source implementation of the algorithm is also provided within
nosnoc
.

Future work will investigate the stability and robustness properties of our new algorithms.
In classical MPC, Riccati-based structure-exploiting methods led to speed-ups of up to two orders of magnitude over generic solvers
[
64
]
.
In our experiments, we used generic solvers.
Hence, we expect that similar developments for QPCCs and MPCCs will lead to comparable speed ups, and enable new challenging hybrid MPC applications.
