Multilayered control of hexose uptake and phosphorylation through specialized sugar kinases in Nakaseomyces glabratus
Authors: Jolien Vreys, Eline Schraepen, Tia Peterson, Stefanie Wijnants, Patrick Van Dijck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe opportunistic pathogenic fungus Nakaseomyces glabratus inhabits diverse host niches with fluctuating nutrient availability. Therefore, efficient control of glycolytic entry is essential, yet the regulatory principles governing hexose phosphorylation in this species remain incompletely understood. Here, we investigated the functional organization of sugar kinases in N. glabratus. Among five predicted sugar kinases, only three (Hxk2, Hxk2b and Glk1) catalyzed phosphorylation of glucose, fructose or mannose, whereas Hxk1 and Glk1b lacked detectable activity. Kinetic analyses revealed a functional specialization, with the hexokinases acting as high-capacity enzymes and Glk1 functioning as a high-affinity, low-capacity kinase optimized for low-sugar concentrations. Despite similar intrinsic kinetics among the hexokinases, Hxk2b emerged as the physiologically dominant enzyme, reflecting differential regulation rather than catalytic properties alone. Both hexokinases, but not Glk1, were strongly inhibited by trehalose-6-phosphate, linking glycolytic entry to trehalose metabolism. Consistent with this, perturbation of trehalose synthesis modulated hexose uptake, revealing that phosphorylation capacity is a major driver of sugar import, while metabolic feedback further constrains uptake. Nuclear localization of sugar kinases and condition-dependent expression patterns indicate additional regulatory layers. Together, our results demonstrate that N. glabratus controls glycolytic entry through a multilayered architecture integrating enzyme specialization, transcriptional tuning, trehalose-6-phosphate–mediated feedback and uptake coupling. Background

Nakaseomyces glabratus (formerly Candida glabrata) is an opportunistic fungal pathogen that causes both bloodstream and mucosal infections, including infections of the vaginal cavity, oral cavity and gastrointestinal tract [1]. These host niches differ markedly in nutrient availability and composition, requiring substantial metabolic flexibility for survival, persistence and pathogenicity [2, 3]. Compared with other pathogenic yeasts, N. glabratus has a relatively limited metabolic repertoire and therefore relies heavily on efficient uptake and utilization of host-derived carbon sources [4–7]. Consequently, precise regulation of nutrient sensing, uptake and metabolism is essential for adaptation to the diverse environments encountered within the human host [8].

During infection, N. glabratus experiences widely fluctuating sugar concentrations depending on the niche it inhabits. In the oral cavity and the gastrointestinal tract, sugars are transiently present and largely diet dependent. In the bloodstream, on the other hand, glucose levels are tightly maintained at approximately 3–5 mM (∼0.06–0.1%), whereas fructose and mannose levels are kept several orders of magnitude lower [9–11]. In vaginal fluids, glucose concentrations often fall below 0.05% and are influenced by host physiology, hormonal status and microbiota composition [12–15]. Notably, individuals with diabetes, who exhibit elevated blood and tissue glucose levels, have an increased risk of developing vaginal N. glabratus infections [16, 17]. Together, these conditions impose strong selective pressure on fungal systems that sense carbon availability and dynamically adjust uptake and metabolism, making such regulation a critical determinant of fungal fitness in vivo [7, 8].

Glucose is the preferred carbon source for most fungi and its presence triggers the glucose repression pathway, which inhibits the use of alternative carbon sources in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. This regulatory system ensures that glucose, a readily available and energy-efficient carbon source, is prioritized over less favorable alternatives by modulating the expression and activity of metabolic enzymes and transporters [18]. Accordingly, efficient control of glucose utilization is thus central to fungal carbon metabolism. Although glucose repression in N. glabratus has not been extensively characterized, it is presumed to involve a similar regulatory system [19].

The glycolytic pathway occupies a central position in fungal physiology by converting hexoses into pyruvate, thereby generating ATP and supplying intermediates for biosynthetic pathways, including the synthesis of lipids, amino acids, nucleotides and metabolites of the pentose phosphate pathway [20, 21]. Beyond its metabolic role, glycolysis has been increasingly linked to fungal virulence and several glycolytic enzymes have emerged as potential antifungal drug targets [22–25]. In the pathogenic fungus Candida albicans, glycolytic activity is upregulated during specific stages of infection [26, 27], underscoring the importance of tight regulation of glycolytic entry points in pathogenic contexts. Because glycolytic flux is primarily determined at its entry point, enzymes controlling sugar phosphorylation are uniquely positioned to influence glycolytic contributions to carbon metabolism.

Sugar phosphorylation is effectively irreversible under physiological conditions, placing sugar kinases in a key regulatory position where they determine whether imported sugars are retained and metabolized or effectively lost from the cell [28]. By rapidly converting free hexoses into phosphorylated forms, sugar kinases indirectly enforce directionality of uptake by maintaining a low intracellular free sugar pool, thereby limiting energetically wasteful cycles of sugar entry and exit [29, 30]. It has therefore been hypothesized that, although mechanistically distinct from bacterial phosphotransferase systems [31], sugar transport in fungi may be functionally coupled to phosphorylation [32–36]. Through this central positioning, sugar kinases function as metabolic gatekeepers that integrate carbon uptake with energy demand and metabolic safety, including avoidance of futile cycling and uncontrolled ATP consumption.

In model yeasts such as S. cerevisiae [37–49] and pathogenic fungi like C. albicans [50–52] and Aspergillus fumigatus [53], individual sugar kinases have been studied in the context of sugar phosphorylation as well as broader regulatory functions, including glucose repression, transcriptional control and metabolic signaling. In contrast, sugar kinase in N. glabratus remains largely unexplored at both the biochemical and functional levels. Although the genome encodes multiple hexose-phosphorylating enzymes, their individual and collective contributions to sugar utilization, uptake and growth have not been defined.

Here, we systematically analyze the sugar kinases of N. glabratus by integrating enzyme kinetics, sensitivity to trehalose-6-phosphate (T6P), growth phenotypes, expression dynamics, subcellular localization and sugar uptake. By examining these regulatory layers in parallel, we aim to define how sugar kinases collectively control carbon entry into glycolysis and how this control supports metabolic flexibility. Our findings reveal that sugar kinases are subject to multi-layered regulation and exhibit complementary roles, balancing efficient sugar utilization with metabolic regulation, thereby providing new insight into carbon metabolism in this pathogen.

Materials and methods

Identification, phylogenetic and sequence-based characterization of sugar kinases

Putative sugar kinases in N. glabratus were identified by BLASTp searches using protein sequences of characterized sugar kinases from S. cerevisiae and C. albicans, retrieved from the Saccharomyces Genome Database (SGD) and Candida Genome Database (CGD), respectively. Homologous sequences were identified by querying the N. glabratus proteome and candidate proteins showing significant sequence similarity were selected for further analysis.

Protein sequences of the identified sugar kinases were aligned using CLC Workbench 22 (QIAGEN). Based on these alignments, a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA12 software [54], after selection of the best-fitting substitution model (LG model with gamma-distribution). Branch support was assessed by bootstrap analysis with 1000 replicates and the resulting tree was visualized.

Multiple sequence alignments were performed using Clustal Omega (v2.1) with default parameters and pairwise amino acid percent identity matrices were calculated from the resulting alignments.

Physicochemical properties of the identified sugar kinases were calculated using the ExPASy ProtParam tool [55]. The analyzed parameters included molecular weight, theoretical isoelectric point (pI), aliphatic index and grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY). To examine sequence conservation and annotate functionally relevant residues, multiple sequence alignments were visualized using ESPript 3.2 [56], allowing identification of conserved glucose-binding and ATP-binding residues. Conserved protein domains were identified using the NCBI Batch Conserved Domain (CD) Search [57, 58], while conserved sequence motifs were detected using the MEME Suite [59]. Identified domains and motifs were visualized and annotated using TBtools v2.376 [60].

Strains and culture conditions

All strains used in this study are derived from the wild-type (WT) N. glabratus strain ATCC2001 and are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Cells were cultured in YP medium (1% (w/v) yeast extract, 2% (w/v) bacteriological peptone) or yeast nitrogen base (YNB) medium (0.17% (w/v) YNB without amino acids and ammonium sulfate, 0.5% (w/v) ammonium sulfate; pH 5.5 for liquid cultures or pH 6.5 for solid media). Media were supplemented with carbon sources depending on the experiment: 2%, 0.1% or 0.05% (w/v) glucose; 2% or 0.1% (w/v) fructose or mannose; or 3% (v/v) glycerol. For solid media, 2% (w/v) agar was added. All cultures were incubated at 37 °C.

Plasmid construction

For gene deletion, 500 bp upstream and downstream regions of the gene of interest (GOI) were PCR amplified from genomic DNA from the WT strain using primers ‘GOI up-FW’ and ‘GOI up-REV’ or ‘GOI down-FW’ and ‘GOI down-REV’. The amplified regions were cloned into BamHI-HF- (New England Biolabs, NEB) and XhoI-digested (NEB) pYC44, a plasmid containing a nourseothricin resistance marker flanked by flippase recognition target (FRT) sites, using Gibson assembly (NEB). Correct integration of the flanking regions into the plasmid was verified by PCR with primers ‘pYC44-up-FW’/ ‘pYC44-up-REV’ or ‘pYC44-down-FW’/‘pYC44-down-REV’.

For endogenous tagging of sugar kinases with mCherry, 500 bp downstream of the GOI and the GOI plus 500 bp upstream were PCR amplified from WT genomic DNA using primers ‘GOI-mCherry up-FW’/‘GOI-mCherry up-REV’ or ‘GOI-mCherry down-FW’/‘GOI-mCherry down-REV’. These fragments were cloned into the BamHI-HF- and XhoI-digested pYC56, a plasmid containing mCherry followed by a nourseothricin marker flanked by FRT sites, via Gibson assembly. Correct integration was verified by PCR using primers ‘pYC56-up-FW’/‘pYC56-up-REV’ or ‘pYC56-down-FW’/‘pYC56-down-REV’. All plasmids and primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S2 and S3.

Construction of N. glabratus mutant strains

For gene deletion and endogenous tagging of sugar kinases, N. glabratus cells were transformed by electroporation as previously described [61]. Deletion cassettes, consisting of a nourseothricin resistance marker flanked by FRT sites and 500 bp up- and downstream of the GOI and tagging cassettes, consisting of mCherry followed by a nourseothricin marker flanked by FRT sites positioned between 500 bp upstream of the GOI and the GOI plus 500 bp downstream, were excised from pYC44-GOI or pYC56-GOI, respectively, by EciI (NEB) digestion. For GLK1 deletion or tagging, in vitro assembled Cas9-ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes (Integrated DNA Technologies, IDT) were additionally employed. Following transformation, cells were plated on YP-Glycerol (YPG) agar medium containing 200 µg/mL nourseothricin. Correct integration of the deletion or tagging cassette was verified by PCR using primers ‘pYC44-down-FW’ and ‘GOI-REV’. Correct transformants were subsequently heat-shock transformed with pLS10, a plasmid encoding a flippase enzyme, and plated on YP-Glucose (YPD) agar medium with 300 µg/mL hygromycin. Successful marker excision was confirmed by PCR with primers ‘GOI-FW’ and ‘GOI-REV’. Loss of the pLS10 plasmid was achieved by growth in non-selective YPD medium and verified by replica plating on YPD agar supplemented with 300 µg/mL hygromycin. All oligonucleotides used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S3. For each strain, three biological replicates (independently constructed mutants) were generated.

Sugar kinase activity assays

Cells were grown in YNB medium supplemented with 3% glycerol at 37 °C, harvested and washed twice in imidazole buffer (50 mM imidazole, 10 mM MgCl₂, 100 mM KCl, 0.1 mM EDTA, pH 7.5). Cell pellets were resuspended in imidazole buffer containing 1mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, a protease inhibitor, to a final concentration of 100 mg wet weight/mL. Glass beads were added and cell were lysed using a FastPrep homogenizer (MP Biomedicals; 2 × 20s at 6 m/s).

Glucose and fructose phosphorylation activities were measured in cell lysates using an enzyme-coupled assay in which ADP production by the sugar phosphorylation was linked to NADPH formation via glucose-6-phopshate dehydrogenase (G6PDH; Roche) and, for fructose, phosphoglucose isomerase (PGI; Roche). Reactions were initiated by adding 20 µL of cell extract to 200 µL of test buffer. The glucose and fructose test buffer consisted of imidazole buffer, 2 mM ATP, 0.8 mM NADP+, 1.4 U/mL G6PDH, 14 U/mL PGI (added only for fructose kinase assays) and glucose or fructose at concentrations specified for each experiment. Mannose phosphorylation was determined by an enzyme-coupled assay in which ADP production by sugar phosphorylation was linked to NADH oxidation via pyruvate kinase (PK; Roche) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH; Roche). Reactions were initiated by adding 20 µL mannose (concentration dependent on experiment) to 20 µL of cell lysate and 180 µL of test buffer. The mannose test buffer consists of imidazole buffer, 2 mM ATP, 0.12 mM NADH, 10 mM phosphoenolpyruvate, 18 U/mL PK and 20 U/mL LDH. For kinase activity measurements of sugar kinase deletion strains glucose, fructose and mannose concentrations were used at a final concentration of 10 mM. For affinity determinations, glucose concentrations ranged from 10 µM to 5 mM, while fructose and mannose concentrations ranged from 10 µM to 20 mM fructose and mannose. When T6P inhibition was assessed, hexoses and T6P (purity ≥ 95%; Cayman Chemicals) were added at a final concentration of 0.5 mM each to the test buffers. NADPH production or NADH consumption was monitored spectrophotometrically at 340 nm every 20 s and kinase activity was calculated from the linear rate of absorbance change. Protein concentration was determined by the Lowry method [62]. Three independent biological replicates, each measured in three technical replicates, were analyzed across two independent experiments.

Growth phenotype assay

Overnight cultures grown in YP medium supplemented with 3% glycerol were washed twice with Milli-Q water. For growth in YNB medium supplemented with fermentable carbon sources (glucose, fructose or mannose), cells were diluted to an initial OD600 of 0.01 in 200 µL medium and growth was monitored by measuring OD595 every half hour in a Multiskan plate reader (Thermo Fisher Scienitfic). Each experiment was performed twice, with three independent biological replicates and three technical replicates per condition. For growth in YNB medium supplemented with 3% glycerol, cells were diluted to an initial OD600 of 0.2 in 5 mL medium in glass tubes to ensure sufficient oxygen availability. Growth was monitored by measuring OD600 after 2, 4, 6, 8, 24 and 48 h. The experiment was performed twice with three independent biological replicates.

Gene expression analysis

To examine the effect of carbon source availability on sugar kinase expression, cells were grown in YNB medium containing 3% glycerol to mid-exponential phase. Glucose, fructose or mannose was subsequently added to a final concentration of either 0.1% or 2%. Cells were incubated for an additional hour, after which they were harvested, washed with ice-cold Milli-Q water and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. To assess whether individual sugar kinases influence the expression of other sugar kinase genes, cells were grown to exponential phase in YNB medium supplemented with 2% glucose. Cells were then harvested, washed with ice-cold Milli-Q water and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. To assess differential GLK1 expression during uptake experiments, cells were grown in YNB medium containing 3% glycerol overnight. Cells were harvested, washed with ice-cold Milli-Q water and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and glass beads followed by cell disruption using a FastPrep homogenizer (MP Biomedicals; 20s at 6 m/s). Chloroform and isopropanol were used for phase separation and RNA precipitation, respectively. The RNA pellet was washed with 70% ethanol and treated with DNase. Complementary DNA was synthesized using the iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using GoTaq polymerase (Promega) on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 3 s and 60 °C for 30 s.

At least two reference genes (ACT1, RPL10 and/or UBC13) were included for normalization. Data were analyzed using qBase Software (Biogazelle) applying the ΔΔCt method with multiple reference gene normalization. Expression levels were normalized to the WT strain or 2% sugar condition, depending on the experimental setup. Fold change values were calculated relative to the reference condition after normalization. Three biological repeats, each with three technical repeats, were analyzed across two independent experiments. All primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Sugar kinases subcellular localization

Cells were grown to exponential phase in YNB medium supplemented with 2% glucose. Half of the culture was washed and resuspended in YNB medium supplemented with 0.05% glucose and allowed to grow for an additional 2 h, after which nuclear staining was performed. The other half of the culture was used immediately for nuclear staining. To stain the nuclei, 4-6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was added to the cells at a final concentration of 4 µg/mL for 10 min and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C with shaking (300 rpm). Microscopy images were acquired using a Fluoview FV1000 confocal microscope (Olympus IX81). mCherry was visualized with a 559 nm laser and BA575-675 emission filter, while DAPI was visualized with a 405 nm laser and 435–485 emission filter. A 60× oil-immersion objective was used. Mean nuclear and cytoplasmic fluorescence were quantified using ImageJ (v1.54 g) [63]. The nucleus of each cell was manually outlined based on the DAPI signal and whole-cell outlines were traced. The cytoplasmic region was obtained by subtracting the nuclear region from the whole-cell region. These masks were applied to the mCherry channel to measure mean fluorescence intensity. To account for background fluorescence, background intensity was subtracted from all measured intensities. Cells were selected based on successful DAPI staining, defined as a clear nuclear signal. Three independent biological replicates, analyzed across two independent experiments, were included for each strain. At least 15 cells were analyzed per biological replicate, condition, and experiment. For each biological replicate, mean fluorescence intensities were calculated as the average of the individual cell measurements.

Western blot

Cells were grown to exponential phase in YNB medium supplemented with 2% glucose. Half of the culture was washed and resuspended in YNB medium supplemented with 0.05% glucose and allowed to grow for an additional 2 h, after which the cells were harvested, washed with ice-cold PBS, and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. The other half of the culture was immediately harvested, washed with ice-cold PBS, and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Cell lysates were prepared in yeast lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 10% glycerol, 2.5 mM MgCl₂, 1% IGEPAL and cOmplete EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche), and cells were disrupted using glass beads in a FastPrep homogenizer (three cycles of 20 s at 6 m/s). Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce 660 nm Protein Assay Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and samples were adjusted to 2000 µg/mL. Samples were mixed 1:1 with 2× SDS sample buffer (250 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 50 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 0.5% bromophenol blue, 10% SDS and 50% glycerol) and boiled at 98 °C for 5 min. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE using NuPAGE Novex Bis-Tris protein gels (Invitrogen). A SeeBlue Plus2 Prestained Protein Standard (Invitrogen) was used as a molecular weight marker. Proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Hybond-C Extra; GE Healthcare). mCherry-tagged proteins were detected using a primary mCherry monoclonal antibody (16D7; Invitrogen) followed by an HRP-linked goat anti-rat IgG secondary antibody (Amersham ECL Rat IgG HRP-linked whole antibody; Cytiva). Membranes were developed using the WesternBright Quantum Western Blotting Detection Kit (Advansta) and signals were visualized using an LAS-4000 mini CCD imaging system (Fujifilm). The experiment was performed with three biological replicates for strains expressing the sugar kinase–mCherry fusions and two biological replicates for the WT strain.

Determination of sugar uptake activity

Overnight cultures in YNB medium supplemented with 3% glycerol were collected and washed twice in 25 mM MES (pH 6.0). Cells were resuspended to a concentration of 125 mg wet weight/mL in YNB medium without an additional carbon source and the dry weight of the cells was determined. Cells were incubated for 10 min at 37 °C. For uptake assays, 20 µL TRIS-MES was mixed with 10 µL of a 2.5 mM glucose or fructose solution containing 1 500 cpm/nmol glucose or fructose (PerkinElmer). Subsequently, 20 µL cell suspension was added and incubated for 10 s, after which uptake was terminated with ice-cold Milli-Q water. Cells were rapidly filtered over a glass fiber filter (Whatman GF/C). Filters were placed in scintillation vials containing 3 mL scintillation cocktail (Lumagel Safe, PerkinElmer) and radioactivity was counted using a Hidex 300 SL scintillation counter. To account for background signal, blank measurements were performed in which ice-cold Milli-Q was added prior to radioactive glucose or fructose. Due to practical constraints, one biological replicate per strain was used. Each strain was tested in at least three independent experiments, with three technical repeats and three blank measurements per experiment.

Statistics

GraphPad Prism version 10 was used for all statistical analyses. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The statistical tests used are specified in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

N. glabratus encodes five putative sugar kinases with distinct sequence features

Based on homology with sugar kinases previously identified in C. albicans (Hxk1, Hxk2, Glk1 and Glk4) [51, 52] and S. cerevisiae (Hxk1, Hxk2, Glk1 and Emi2) [49, 64], five putative sugar kinases were identified in N. glabratus: Hxk1, Hxk2, Hxk2b, Glk1 and Glk1b (Supplementary Table S4). A rootless maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree, including these proteins and previously characterized sugar kinases from C. albicans and S. cerevisiae, revealed two major clades corresponding to hexokinases and glucokinases (Fig. 1). Within the hexokinase clade, Hxk1 clustered with CaHxk1, whereas ScHxk1 clustered with the remaining hexokinases. Hxk1 appeared to be the most divergent hexokinase. Within the glucokinase clade, N. glabratus glucokinases clustered with ScGlk1 and ScEmi2, while the C. albicans glucokinase formed a separate subclade. Glk1b was the most divergent glucokinase.

A maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using protein sequences of known sugar kinases from S. cerevisiae and C. albicans and putative sugar kinases from N. glabratus. The tree was generated in MEGA12 using the LG substitution model with gamma-distributed rate variation and 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Bootstrap support values are indicated at the nodes. The scale bar indicates 0.5 substitutions per site.

Pairwise amino acid sequence identities among the deduced sugar kinase proteins of N. glabratus ranged from 14.85% to 85.39%, with the highest identity between Hxk2 and Hxk2b (78%) and the lowest between Hxk1 and Glk1b (Supplementary Table S5).

The coding sequence of the five putative sugar kinases ranged from 1,251 to 1,488 bp, encoding proteins of 416 to 495 amino acids (Glk1b < Hxk1 < Hxk2 = Hxk2b < Glk1) (Table 1). Predicted molecular weights ranged from 47,884 to 53,806 Da, consistent with typical sugar kinases sizes [65]. Four enzymes exhibited acidic predicted isoelectric points (pI 5.03–5.38), whereas Glk1b displayed a near-neutral pI of 7.56. Aliphatic indices ranged from 83.9 to 100.29 and GRAVY values indicated all enzymes were moderately hydrophilic (-0.083 to -0.333), with Hxk1 and Glk1b being less hydrophilic than the others.

Multiple sequence alignment of all sugar kinases revealed strong conservation of the glucose- and ATP-binding residues [52] in Hxk2, Hxk2b and Glk1 (Fig. 2A). In contrast, Hxk1 and Glk1b showed divergence in the ATP-binding sites, with Glk1b being the most divergent. Both proteins lacked key glucose-binding residues (Hxk1 lacking one, Glk1b lacking all). The N-terminal peptide of ScHxk2 (residues K7 to M16), previously shown to regulate glucose-dependent nuclear localization [44], was conserved in Hxk2 and Hxk2b, as was residue S15, which influences monomer–dimer balance and starvation-induced nuclear accumulation [44]. These features were absent from Hxk1, Glk1 and Glk1b. Motif analysis revealed that Hxk2, Hxk2b and Glk1 shared several conserved motifs, whereas most motifs were absent in Hxk1 and Glk1b (Fig. 2B). Pfam domain analysis further showed that Glk1b contains only a single hexokinase-2 domain, whereas the other enzymes possess both canonical hexokinase-1 and hexokinase-2 domains (Fig. 2C).

Only Hxk2, Hxk2b and Glk1 catalyze hexose phosphorylation

To determine which of the five putative sugar kinases can phosphorylate hexoses, single and multiple sugar kinase deletion strains were generated and assayed for glucose, fructose and mannose phosphorylation using enzymatic kinase activity measurements (Fig. 3A). Combined deletion of HXK2, HXK2b and GLK1 abolished all detectable kinase activity on the tested sugars, indicating that Hxk1 and Glk1b do not contribute to hexose phosphorylation.

Among the three active enzymes, Hxk2b contributed most strongly to total kinase activity: deletion of HXK2b reduced phosphorylation by 49%, 61% and 43% for glucose, fructose and mannose, respectively (Fig. 3A). In contrast, deletion of HXK2 or GLK1 alone did not significantly affect overall kinase activity relative to the WT. Strains lacking both HXK2 and GLK1 retained WT-level glucose and fructose phosphorylation, whereas mannose phosphorylation was reduced. Combined deletion of HXK2b with either HXK2 or GLK1 further reduced phosphorylation of all three sugars. Notably, fructose phosphorylation was exclusively mediated by hexokinases, as the hxk2Δ hxk2bΔ strain exhibited no detectable fructose kinase activity. Based on these results, we define Hxk2, Hxk2b and Glk1 as the functional sugar kinases of N. glabratus for the remainder of this study.

Sugar kinases display distinct substrate specificities and catalytic efficiencies

To further characterize substrate specificity, Michaelis-Menten kinetics were determined for each functional sugar kinase using glucose, fructose and mannose as substrates (Fig. 3B; Supplementary Table S6). Apparent substrate affinities differed markedly among sugars: fructose exhibited the highest apparent Km values, indicating lowest affinity, whereas mannose displayed the lowest Km values across all enzymes, indicating highest affinity. For both glucose and fructose, Hxk2 exhibited higher apparent Km values than Hxk2b, whereas mannose Km values were similar between the two hexokinases. Glk1 showed the lowest apparent Km values for glucose and mannose, consistent with efficient substrate binding under glucose-limited conditions. Fructose kinetics were not determined for Glk1, as this enzyme does not phosphorylate fructose.

Apparent maximal phosphorylation rates (Vmax) revealed a substrate preference for the sugar kinases of glucose > fructose > mannose, although Glk1 showed no detectable activity toward fructose. On glucose, Hxk2 displayed the highest Vmax, followed by Hxk2b, whereas on mannose the opposite pattern was observed. Hxk2 and Hxk2b exhibited comparable maximal rates on fructose. Glk1 consistently showed the lowest Vmax values for all substrates tested.

To integrate affinity and turnover, apparent catalytic efficiencies (Vmax/Km) were calculated (Fig. 3B). Apparent efficiencies for glucose and mannose phosphorylation were comparable, whereas fructose phosphorylation showed markedly reduced efficiency. Among the hexokinases, Hxk2 exhibited more than a two-fold higher apparent catalytic efficiency than Hxk2b on fructose.

Hexokinase activity is inhibited by trehalose-6-phosphate

T6P, a central intermediate of trehalose metabolism derived from glucose-6-phosphate, is a known inhibitor of fungal hexokinases and modulates glycolytic flux in several fungi [66–72]. To assess T6P sensitivity of N. glabratus sugar kinases, glucose, fructose and mannose phosphorylation were measured in double sugar kinase deletion strains expressing only a single functional enzyme, using equimolar concentrations of hexose substrate and T6P (Fig. 3C). T6P strongly inhibited hexokinase-mediated phosphorylation, whereas Glk1-mediated glucose and mannose phosphorylation remained unaffected. Among the hexokinases, Hxk2 was most sensitive to T6P, showing a 60% reduction in glucose phosphorylation, a 66% reduction on fructose and a 35% reduction on mannose. Hxk2b exhibited lower sensitivity, with activity reductions of 29% on glucose, 55% on fructose and 21% on mannose. These data demonstrate substrate-dependent sensitivity of N. glabratus hexokinases to T6P.

Sugar kinases are essential for growth on fermentable carbon sources

Sugar phosphorylation constitutes the first step of glycolysis and loss of sugar kinase activity is therefore expected to impair growth on fermentable carbon sources. To assess the physiological consequences of sugar kinase deletion, growth of strains lacking one, two or all three functional sugar kinases was examined in minimal medium supplemented with glucose, fructose and mannose at high (2%) and low (0.1%) concentrations (Fig. 4A-G). Consistent with the absence of detectable sugar phosphorylation activity, the hxk2Δ hxk2bΔ glk1Δ strain failed to grow on all tested fermentable carbon sources, confirming that N. glabratus relies exclusively on these three sugar kinases for hexose phosphorylation. The hxk2Δ hxk2bΔ strain, which retains ~ 20% residual glucose kinase activity showed a mild growth delay on 2% glucose (Fig. 4A), whereas no growth defect was observed at 0.1% glucose (Fig. 4D).

(H) Growth in YNB medium supplemented with 3% glycerol was monitored in test tubes and optical density was measured manually. Data represent the mean of three independent biological replicates from two independent experiments with three technical repeats (A–F) or from three independent experiments (H). Error bars indicate SEM.

Growth on fructose showed a strong dependence on HXK2b (Fig. 4B, E). Strains lacking HXK2b (hxk2bΔ and hxk2bΔ glk1Δ) exhibited a modest growth delay on 0.1% fructose, indicating that HXK2b contributes to efficient growth under fructose-limited conditions. Consistent with kinase activity measurements, the hxk2Δ hxk2bΔ strain displayed a severe growth defect, with very slow growth at 2% fructose and no detectable growth at 0.1%.

Growth on mannose revealed a more complex dependence on HXK2b (Fig. 4C, F). On 2% mannose, the hxk2bΔ strain exhibited a mild growth delay that was exacerbated in the hxk2Δ hxk2bΔ strain, whereas the hxk2bΔ glk1Δ strain displayed a severe defect. This was unexpected given that mannose phosphorylation activity was only moderately reduced (~ 20% relative to WT) in the hxk2bΔ and hxk2bΔ glk1Δ strains and approximately 20% residual activity remained in the hxk2Δ hxk2bΔ strain. At 0.1% mannose, a growth defect was observed only in the hxk2bΔ glk1Δ strain, indicating an increased reliance on Glk1 when HXK2b is absent under mannose-limiting conditions.

In the absence of an additional carbon source, no growth was observed for any of the strains (Fig. 4G).

To determine whether growth defects were specific to fermentable carbon sources, strains were also grown on the non-fermentable carbon source glycerol (Fig. 4H). Initial growth rates were comparable to WT across all strains, whereas modest differences in biomass were observed at later time points, most prominently in the triple sugar kinase deletion strain.

Sugar kinase expression responds to sugar concentration

Given the observed differences in substrate affinities, we next examined whether sugar kinase gene expression responds to changes in sugar availability. Expression of all sugar kinase genes was influenced by hexose concentration (Fig. 5A-C). HXK2 expression was consistently higher at 2% sugar than at 0.1% sugar, regardless of the sugar. In contrast, HXK2b expression was elevated under low sugar conditions, reaching statistical significance in fructose- and mannose-containing media and showing a similar, though non-significant, trend on glucose. GLK1 expression was strongly induced at low sugar concentrations, with transcript levels at 0.1% sugar exceeding those at 2% sugar by more than two-fold. Notably, GLK1 expression responded to fructose availability despite fructose not being a substrate of Glk1.

Sugar kinase expression is independent of the presence of other sugar kinases

In S. cerevisiae, sugar kinase gene expression can be modulated by deletion of another sugar kinase, potentially compensating for reduced phosphorylation capacity [46]. To determine whether a similar mechanism operates in N. glabratus, sugar kinase gene expression was measured in WT and single sugar kinase deletion strains grown in 2% glucose (Fig. 5D). Deletion of HXK2, HXK2b, or GLK1 did not significantly alter expression of the remaining sugar kinase genes. To exclude a potential regulatory role for kinases lacking detectable hexose phosphorylation activity, HXK1 and GLK1b deletion strains were also analysed. Their absence likewise did not affect sugar kinase expression, indicating that sugar kinase expression in N. glabratus is regulated primarily by sugar availability rather than by compensatory transcriptional responses.

Glucose availability differentially affects nuclear localization of sugar kinases

Sugar kinases have been described as moonlighting proteins in several organisms, performing functions beyond glycolysis [73]. In S. cerevisiae and C. albicans, hexokinases regulate glucose repression through glucose-dependent nuclear localization [37, 38, 42, 52]. As Hxk2 and Hxk2b contain a conserved N-terminal peptide associated with nuclear partitioning in S. cerevisiae, we examined whether N. glabratus sugar kinases display glucose-dependent subcellular localization. To this end, Hxk2, Hxk2b and Glk1 were C-terminally tagged with mCherry and localization was assessed under glucose-repleted (2% glucose) and acute glucose starvation (0.05% glucose) conditions (Fig. 6; Supplementary Figure S1). Under both conditions, all three sugar kinases were detected throughout the cell, however, nuclear-to-cytoplasmic distribution depended on glucose availability. Under high-glucose conditions, Hxk2 and Hxk2b were enriched in the nucleus relative to the cytoplasm, with nuclear enrichment increasing by ~ 10% upon glucose starvation. In contrast, Glk1 consistently displayed lower nuclear than cytoplasmic fluorescence, with nuclear levels ~ 10% lower than cytoplasmic levels under high glucose and decreasing further by ~ 10% under glucose starvation.

Western blot analysis of cells grown under the same conditions as those used for confocal microscopy detected only full-length enzyme–mCherry fusion proteins under both conditions, indicating that the observed fluorescence was not derived from cleaved free mCherry (Supplementary Figure S2).

Sugar phosphorylation drives sugar import

Hexose transporters (Hxts) mediate sugar uptake in N. glabratus. Once internalized, sugar kinases phosphorylate the imported sugars, thereby maintaining a low intracellular free sugar concentration and sustaining the concentration gradient driving uptake. To test whether sugar phosphorylation is required for efficient import, glucose and fructose uptake was measured in sugar kinases deletion strains. Uptake of each sugar was nearly abolished in strains lacking the enzymes required for its phosphorylation (glucose uptake in the hxk2Δ hxk2bΔ glk1Δ strain and fructose uptake in the hxk2Δ hxk2bΔ and hxk2Δ hxk2bΔ glk1Δ strains) indicating that sugar phosphorylation drives import (Fig. 7A).

Asterisks denote significant differences relative to WT (one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post hoc test, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001).

Deletion of HXK2 or HXK2b alone had little effect on glucose influx, whereas deletion of both hexokinases unexpectedly increased uptake despite the strain retaining only ~ 20% residual glucose kinase activity. Conversely, deletion of GLK1, alone or combined with a hexokinase deletion, reduced glucose import even though the glk1Δ strain displayed WT-like glucose kinase activity. Expression analysis confirmed that GLK1 transcript levels were unchanged in the hxk2Δ hxk2bΔ background, excluding compensatory upregulation (Supplementary Figure S3).

For fructose, strains lacking HXK2b, but still capable of fructose phosphorylation (hxk2bΔ and hxk2bΔ glk1Δ), also exhibited reduced fructose uptake, consistent with their kinase activity profiles, whereas deletion of GLK1 had no effect on fructose uptake. These results demonstrate that fructose import is primarily constrained by sugar phosphorylation. In general, fructose uptake was markedly lower than glucose uptake.

Hexokinase sensitivity to trehalose-6-phosphate modulates sugar import

The opposing glucose import phenotypes of hxk2Δ hxk2bΔ and glk1Δ strains suggested that factors beyond total kinase activity influence sugar uptake. Given that hexokinases are inhibited by T6P, whereas Glk1 is not, we tested whether T6P-sensitivity contributes to these phenotypes. TPS1 (produces T6P) or TPS2 (converts T6P into trehalose) were deleted in WT, glk1Δ and hxk2Δ hxk2bΔ backgrounds and sugar uptake was quantified (Fig. 7B). As expected, TPS1 deletion increased sugar import, consistent with relief from T6P-mediated inhibition, whereas TPS2 deletion reduced sugar import due to T6P accumulation. Glucose uptake in the glk1Δ tps2Δ strain and fructose uptake in all TPS2-deleted strains were nearly abolished, comparable to the hxk2Δ hxk2bΔ glk1Δ strain. These data demonstrates that non-T6P-sensitive sugar kinases are essential for maintaining import under T6P accumulation. Interestingly, TPS1 deletion in the hxk2Δ hxk2bΔ background further increased glucose uptake, whereas TPS2 deletion reduced it, suggesting that TPS1 and TPS2 influence hexose import through additional mechanisms beyond hexokinase inhibition.

Discussion

Efficient hexose utilization is critical for fungal growth, metabolic flexibility and pathogenicity [2, 3]. Despite glycolysis often being portrayed as a linear pathway, hexose entry represents a critical metabolic decision point, where carbon flux must be balanced against the risks of metabolic overload, energy depletion and accumulation of toxic intermediates. Therefore, multilayered control, including enzyme activity, feedback inhibition and expression dynamics, is essential. In this study, we demonstrate that N. glabratus has evolved to distribute this control across three sugar kinases (Hxk2, Hxk2b and Glk1), each specialized for distinct substrates and conditions.

Despite five predicted putative sugar kinases in N. glabratus, we showed that Hxk1 and Glk1b lack kinase activity towards glucose, fructose and mannose. The inactivity of Hxk1 and Glk1b is reflected in their divergent protein sequences and structural features. These include their being shorter, more distant phylogenetic relationships, reduced homology at ATP-binding sites, loss of glucose-binding residues, conserved motifs and functional domains and divergent predicted surface properties compared to the catalytically active sugar kinases. These observations argue against a role for Hxk1 and Glk1b in hexose phosphorylation and instead suggest that they may have adopted non-canonical or non-glycolytic functions. Building on this, phylogenetically, Hxk1 clusters with CaHxk1, an enzyme known to regulate several processes, including transcriptional control of hexose sugar kinases [51, 74, 75]. However, since deletion of HXK1 did not affect expression of other sugar kinases, this regulatory role appears to be absent. CaHxk1 is also known to phosphorylate N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) rather than hexoses enabling its utilization through the GlcNAc catabolic pathway. N. glabratus, however, is not known to utilize GlcNAc as carbon source for growth [76, 77]. Notably, a recent study identified Hxk1 as a GlcNAc kinase (renamed Ngk1), suggesting that it generates GlcNAc-6-phosphate for cell wall biosynthesis rather than contributing to glycolytic energy production [78]. This finding supports a specialized, non-glycolytic role for Hxk1 and highlights how gene duplication and specialization can shape sugar kinase function in species-specific ways, even among closely related fungi. The function of Glk1b remains elusive.

The kinetic properties of N. glabratus sugar kinases are shaped not only by substrate preference, but also by the chemical features of hexoses and the sugar environment encountered in the human host. Across all three active enzymes, phosphorylation efficiency followed a conserved pattern, with highest apparent affinity for mannose, intermediate for glucose and the lowest for fructose, consistent with intrinsic sugar structure. Mannose and glucose are both aldoses, while fructose is a ketose, which is less favorably accommodated in the sugar kinase active site. Nevertheless, sugar chemistry alone does not dictate the observed kinetic order. Physiologically, glucose is the most abundant hexose in the human body, whereas fructose and, even more so, mannose occur at much lower concentrations [9–11]. High catalytic capacity toward glucose therefore ensures rapid glycolytic flux under glucose abundance, whereas low affinity for fructose effectively deprioritizes its utilization in the presence of glucose. In contrast, the very high affinity but low maximal phosphorylation rate observed for mannose suggests a protective strategy: efficient scavenging at low concentrations combined with constrained flux to avoid toxic accumulation of mannose-6-phosphate [79].

The three active sugar kinases collectively support robust hexose metabolism across a wide range of environmental conditions. Their kinetic properties suggest complementary physiological roles: Glk1 acts as a high-affinity (low Km), low-capacity (low Vmax) kinase suited for efficient phosphorylation at low sugar concentrations, whereas the hexokinases function as high-capacity (high Vmax), low-affinity (high Km) enzymes that enable rapid sugar processing when substrate is abundant. This functional differentiation predicts that different kinases contribute preferentially depending on sugar availability.

Despite kinetic analyses providing interesting insights into the intrinsic features of the individual sugar kinases, they did not fully predict relative contributions to total hexose phosphorylation. While Hxk2 and Hxk2b showed similar apparent affinities and maximal phosphorylation rates, deletion of HXK2b led to substantial reductions in glucose, fructose and mannose phosphorylation, whereas deletion of HXK2 or GLK1 alone had no significant impact. Remarkably, Hxk2b alone was sufficient to maintain WT phosphorylation levels on glucose and fructose, marking it as the dominant hexose-phosphorylating enzyme, at least under the conditions tested. This dominance cannot be explained by kinetic properties alone and instead hints at additional layers of control, including regulatory sensitivity and transcriptional regulation. Functional dominance among sugar kinases is not unique to N. glabratus. In S. cerevisiae and C. albicans, Hxk2 likewise takes the lead in hexose phosphorylation [52, 80, 81], suggesting that unequal functional contributions among duplicated sugar kinases represent a conserved organizational principle.

The need for such regulatory control is underscored by the physiological consequences of excessive hexose phosphorylation. Uncontrolled sugar phosphorylation leads to hyperactivation of glycolytic flux, resulting in accumulation of metabolites in the upper part of glycolysis, depletion of ATP and inorganic phosphate and can ultimately trigger apoptosis [82, 83]. Accordingly, high-capacity hexokinases must be tightly controlled to match phosphorylation activity to metabolic capacity. In several fungi, T6P fulfills this role as a potent hexokinase inhibitor [66–71, 84]. Consistent with this role, a glucose pulse in S. cerevisiae diverts ~ 30% of incoming sugar into trehalose metabolism, linking hexose-6-phosphate production to T6P accumulation and feedback control of glycolytic flux [66, 83, 85]. Our data suggest that this regulatory mechanism also operates in N. glabratus, with Hxk2 and Hxk2b showing pronounced sensitivity to T6P, whereas Glk1 is insensitive. These profiles are in line with the kinetics of the sugar kinases. Feedback inhibition limits the activity of high-capacity hexokinases under sugar-rich conditions, preventing metabolic imbalance, while the T6P-insensitive Glk1 remains active during sugar limitation, when strong feedback control would otherwise restrict phosphorylation. The coexistence of T6P-sensitive and T6P-insensitive enzymes therefore allows glycolytic entry to be stabilized across both high- and low-sugar environments. Although T6P-mediated inhibition is common in fungi [66–72, 84], a T6P-insensitive hexokinase has been reported in Schizosaccharomyces pombe [84] and most mammalian hexokinases are likewise insensitive [86], underscoring the evolutionary flexibility of this regulatory mechanism.

In addition to regulation of the kinase activities by T6P, differential gene expression further shapes the observed phenotypes. Despite comparable in vitro phosphorylation kinetics, Hxk2 and Hxk2b are clearly distinguishable at the transcriptional level in response to environmental sugar availability, reinforcing that enzyme abundance contributes to their unequal impact. Similar partitioning of duplicated glycolytic enzymes has been described in S. cerevisiae, where gene duplication enables differential regulation under high- and low-glucose conditions [87]. Consistent with observations in S. cerevisiae [45, 46], sugar-rich conditions favor HXK2 expression, supporting rapid phosphorylation when substrate is abundant and consistent with its strong T6P sensitivity. Under low-sugar conditions (0.1%), which better reflect physiological sugar levels in the human host, HXK2b is upregulated, shifting hexose phosphorylation toward this isoform. This shift is likely advantageous, as Hxk2b is less sensitive to T6P inhibition allowing sustained phosphorylation when flux is low and metabolic control becomes more sensitive to feedback [88]. GLK1 expression is even more strongly induced under sugar limitation, consistent with its role as a high-affinity, low-capacity sugar kinase optimized for sugar scarcity. A similar induction has been reported in S. cerevisiae [46], while in C. albicans GLK1/4 expression appears less responsive to sugar concentration [52], underscoring species-specific regulation.

Interestingly, fructose stimulated GLK1 expression despite Glk1’s inability to phosphorylate this sugar, suggesting that its transcriptional regulation responds to overall carbon status rather than direct substrate utilization, a phenomenon also reported for glucokinases in C. albicans and A. fumigatus [52, 53]. In contrast, HXK2 expression is more sensitive to sugar identity, with stronger differences between low- and high-glucose conditions than between fructose and mannose conditions. This reflects the preferential utilization of glucose when it is abundant, whereas under sugar scarcity, Hxk2b and Glk1 respond to any available sugar to maintain glycolytic flux. By contrast, expression of the sole sugar kinase in Kluyveromyces lactis appears largely insensitive to glucose availability, further highlighting the diversity of regulatory strategies among fungi [89]. Together, these observations indicate that metabolic flexibility in N. glabratus arises from the coordinated interplay of intrinsic enzyme properties, transcriptional tuning and post-translational control by T6P, allowing the organism to maintain glycolytic flux across a wide range of sugar environments.

Notably, deletion of individual sugar kinases did not induce compensatory transcriptional upregulation of the remaining kinases. This implies that N. glabratus does not dynamically rebalance enzyme abundance to restore total activity but instead relies on pre-established specialization. In contrast, in S. cerevisiae, HXK2 deletion increases HXK1 and GLK1 expression, suggesting a compensatory network among these kinases [46]. In C. albicans, Hxk2 similarly lacks a compensatory role, but transcriptional control is mediated by Hxk1 [51, 52], which also regulates its own expression [74]. The lack of compensation in N. glabratus therefore likely amplifies the phenotypic dominance of Hxk2b and makes regulatory sensitivity, such as differential T6P inhibition, more decisive for physiological outcome. Importantly, our analysis was limited to mRNA levels and compensatory regulation could still occur post-transcriptionally, via combinatorial kinase loss or under different conditions.

The growth phenotypes of sugar kinase deletion strains reflects the combined effects of enzyme kinetics, substrate affinity, transcriptional regulation, T6P sensitivity and sugar availability. High-capacity hexokinases dominate glycolytic flux under sugar-replete conditions, whereas the high-affinity glucokinase Glk1 ensures efficient utilization of scarce sugars. Consistent with this functional specialisation among the sugar kinases, the hxk2Δ hxk2bΔ strain exhibited a growth delay on high glucose but not under glucose-limited conditions, where Glk1 expression is elevated and its insensitivity to T6P inhibition allows sustained phosphorylation. Growth on fructose relied almost exclusively on hexokinases, with Hxk2b playing a dominant role under limiting conditions, where it is highly expressed. Residual growth in the absence of both hexokinases may reflect alternative low-efficiency routes for fructose utilization or adaptive responses. Growth on mannose further highlighted the importance of high-affinity, T6P-insensitive kinase activity, as combined loss of HXK2b and GLK1 severely impaired growth. Growth on the non-fermentable carbon source glycerol confirmed that these defects are specific to glycolytic entry rather than general growth impairment. Mild late-stage differences likely reflect secondary effects on central carbon metabolism.

N. glabratus sugar kinases exhibit partial nuclear localization, a phenomenon observed for glycolytic enzymes in multiple eukaryotic organisms [73, 90–92]. Nuclear localization of glycolytic enzymes has been linked to metabolic sensing and moonlighting regulatory functions [93–96]. Additionally, it can modulate cytosolic glycolytic flux, as exemplified by mammalian hepatocytes, where glucokinase is reversibly retained in the nucleus during fasting and released upon glucose repletion [97]. In S. cerevisiae, however, the best-characterized nuclear role of sugar kinases is Hxk2-mediated glucose repression, which suppresses genes required for utilization of alternative carbon sources [37, 38, 42–44, 46, 98]. In N. glabratus, Hxk2 and Hxk2b showed increased nuclear enrichment upon acute glucose starvation, whereas Glk1 exhibited a comparable decrease in nuclear localization under the same conditions. This pattern contrasts with classical models in S. cerevisiae and C. albicans, in which nuclear accumulation of hexokinases is associated with glucose abundance and repression [42, 47, 50, 52]. Notably, a recent study in S. cerevisiae contradicts this view, reporting that hexokinases also accumulate in the nucleus under glucose starvation, suggesting that Hxk2 may contribute less to glucose repression than previously assumed [44]. The opposing but relatively modest redistribution of the sugar kinases suggests that, in N. glabratus, nuclear localization is not tightly coupled to glucose repression. Instead, nuclear presence may reflect broader metabolic or regulatory functions although functional consequences remain unclear. Conservation of the N-terminal peptide in Hxk2 and Hxk2b, despite these species-specific differences in nuclear behavior, supports the idea that nuclear functions of sugar kinases are context-dependent. This interpretation is consistent with the lack of transcriptional compensation observed in sugar kinase deletion strains.

Sugar phosphorylation is a major determinant of hexose uptake of N. glabratus. Loss of all glucose-phosphorylating enzymes or all fructose-phosphorylating enzymes resulted in a strong reduction in uptake of the respective sugar, consistent with previous observations in S. cerevisiae [99, 100], However, uptake phenotypes were not fully predicted by kinase activity alone. Deletion of GLK1 reduced glucose uptake, despite its minor contribution to total glucose phosphorylation, whereas deletion of both hexokinases, which severely reduced glucose phosphorylation, markedly increased glucose import. These findings indicate that sugar kinases influence uptake through mechanisms that extend beyond direct catalytic trapping of hexoses.

Trehalose metabolism emerges as a key integrator of sugar phosphorylation and uptake in N. glabratus. Deletion of TPS2, which elevates intracellular T6P levels, strongly reduced sugar uptake in strains relying exclusively on T6P-sensitive hexokinases, phenocopying strains unable to phosphorylate the sugar. These results support a model in which sugar phosphorylation remains a major driver of uptake, while T6P acts as a potent constraint on this process. Consistent with the importance of this regulatory role, deletion of TPS1, which prevents T6P production, has been reported to abolish growth on glucose in N. glabratus [101]. Unexpectedly, deletion of TPS1 or TPS2 continued to modulate glucose uptake even in strains lacking T6P-sensitive hexokinases, indicating that trehalose metabolism influences hexose import through additional mechanisms beyond direct hexokinase inhibition. Similar observations have been reported in S. cerevisiae, where trehalose pathway mutations affected uptake even when sugar kinase-null cells expressed a T6P-insensitive hexokinase from S. pombe [102]. Together, these findings suggest that trehalose metabolism exerts broader control over glycolytic entry, potentially by modulating downstream glycolytic flux, interacting with sugar kinases or transporters or influencing transporter–kinase complexes [36, 102, 103]. Physical interactions between hexokinases and hexose transporters have been reported in S. cerevisiae [36], raising the possibility that such complexes directly regulate transporter activity. Notably, when sugar phosphorylation is absent, as in the hxk2Δ hxk2bΔ strain on fructose, deletion of TPS1 or TPS2 no longer affects import, indicating that active sugar phosphorylation is required for trehalose metabolism–dependent modulation of uptake. Although transporter abundance and activity were not directly assessed here, known effects of sugar kinases on transporter expression in other fungi [52, 104–106] indicate that this level of regulation warrants further investigation.

Importantly, discrepancies between enzyme kinetics, uptake rates and growth phenotypes indicate that additional regulatory mechanisms operate in vivo. One such mechanism may be sugar kinase oligomerization, which has been shown to modulate enzymatic activity, stability and regulatory interactions in fungi [107, 108]. Although not examined here, condition-dependent changes in oligomeric state could further refine the physiological specialization of Hxk2, Hxk2b and Glk1.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that glycolytic entry in N. glabratus is governed by a multilayered regulatory network centered on functionally specialized sugar kinases. Complementary functions of Hxk2, Hxk2b and Glk1, combined with differential expression and T6P–mediated feedback, coordinates hexose phosphorylation and uptake with metabolic capacity. The physiological dominance of Hxk2b despite similar intrinsic kinetics highlights the central role of regulatory context in shaping enzyme function in vivo. Together, these findings identify glycolytic entry as an integrated control point that enables metabolic flexibility in the fluctuating and often sugar-limited host environment.
