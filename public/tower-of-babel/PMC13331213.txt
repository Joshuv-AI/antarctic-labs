Diagnostic yield and utilization patterns of head and neck computed tomography angiography
Authors: Kolos Kalman Nagy, Jantzen Brandt Orton, Abdul Awal, Roy Jacob, Laszlo Nagy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:This study evaluates the diagnostic yield and utilization patterns of head and neck computed tomography angiography (CTAHN) in screening patients in the emergency department (ED) for blunt cerebrovascular injury. This study was conducted at a Level 1 trauma center serving a large geographical area with a high number of regional transfers. This study evaluates the prevalence of CTAHN overutilization in the ED and examines whether diagnostic yield correlates with adherence to the modified Denver criteria, transfer status, and trauma severity.Material and Methods:A retrospective chart review was conducted of patients presenting to the ED between January 01, 2021, and December 31, 2023. Patients over the age of 16 were included in this study if they presented with blunt traumatic injury and subsequently underwent CTAHN or neck CTA (CTAN) imaging during their ED evaluation. A multivariable regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association between CTA yield and factors including patient transfer status, trauma severity designation, and adherence to the modified Denver criteria.Results:A total of 643 patients met the inclusion criteria. A substantial proportion (74.2%) of CTAHN and CTAN examinations yielded negative results, with significant correlation to nonadherence to the modified Denver criteria. We found no significant association between CTAHN yield and transfer status or trauma level designation. Of the patients found to have trauma related actionable findings, 71.8% had adherence to the modified Denver criteria during triage screening.Conclusion:A high proportion of CTAHN and CTAN studies ordered from the ED yield low-value diagnostic results, with overutilization closely linked to nonadherence to the modified Denver criteria. Our findings carry important implications for quality improvement efforts, including unnecessary radiation exposure to patients and reduced efficiency in the ED. Enhanced guideline compliance may serve as a modifiable target to reduce diagnostic imaging overutilization.

INTRODUCTION

Screening patients presenting to the emergency department (ED) for blunt cerebrovascular injury (BCVI) with computed tomography angiography (CTA) can be a critical step in the prevention of lethal outcomes.[1,2] However, over the past two decades, the utilization of head and neck CTA (CTAHN) has increased disproportionately relative to other neuroimaging modalities, with an alarming number of studies yielding negative or clinically insignificant findings.[3] One study found that from 2001 to 2015, head and neck CTA utilization in the ED increased 14,600% and 17,781%, respectively, for medicare beneficiaries.[4,5] Some estimate that up to 85% of CTA screenings may have negative or insignificant findings, a trend likely driven by the accessibility, rapid acquisition, and diagnostic efficiency of CTA in acute care settings.[6]

The Denver criteria are widely regarded as the gold standard for assessing the need for CTA screening following blunt trauma involving the head and neck and are endorsed by the Western Trauma Association and used by numerous institutions. Since the introduction of BCVI screening guidelines some 20 years ago, identifying at-risk patients has been the subject of debate, with some reports suggesting that current guidelines fail to identify up to 37% of all BCVI.[7,8] Some studies have argued that ordering CTA based on clinical suspicion alone, without extensive survey and strict adherence to formalized screening criteria, may be justified out of precaution and urgency to avoid missing occult vascular injuries. Consequently, the optimal strategy for BCVI screening remains a contentious issue, with varying perspectives on whether current protocols sufficiently capture at-risk patients.[9]

Reducing the frequency of low-yield CTA orders from the ED has the potential to improve triage efficiency, enhance diagnostic accuracy, and mitigate unnecessary healthcare expenditures and provider workload, ultimately advancing patient care and decreasing morbidity and mortality.[1] While there is growing literature on diagnostic imaging overutilization, the relationship between CTA use and the detection of clinically significant BCVI in the emergency setting has yet to be thoroughly explored. To our knowledge, this study is the first comprehensive evaluation of CTAHN diagnostic yield at a Level 1 trauma center serving a large and predominantly rural population with substantial healthcare disparities and a high volume of regional transfers. We aim to evaluate the prevalence of clinically significant CTAHN imaging findings in blunt trauma patients and to determine whether diagnostic yield is associated with nonadherence to the modified Denver criteria, patient transfer status, and trauma severity designation.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at University Medical Center in Lubbock, Texas, USA -- a level 1 trauma center serving a large geographical area with a predominantly rural patient population. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, with waiver of the informed consent requirement, to include all trauma patients for a period of 3 years who presented to the ED between January 01, 2021, and December 31, 2023, and underwent a CTAHN or neck CTA (CTAN).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they presented with traumatic injuries involving the head or neck and received either a CTAHN or CTAN during the ED evaluation. The following criteria were used to exclude CTA scans from this study: CTAHN or CTAN for patients ≤16 years oldNon-diagnostic scans due to significant motion artifact or suboptimal image qualityDuplicate or repeat imaging following an initial CTAHN or CTAN ordered in the EDNon-trauma related presentations, such as altered mental status, headache, weakness, focal neurological deficits, or other non-traumatic complaints.

Statistical analysis

The Stata/MP version 18.0 was used for statistical analysis, and listwise deletion was used to address missing data. Continuous variables, such as age, were presented as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate, using descriptive statistics to describe the study population. Categorical factors, including gender, trauma severity, patient transfer status, and adherence to the modified Denver criteria, were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Statistical tests such as Chi-square, Fisher’s exact test, t-tests, and analysis of variance were applied as appropriate to draw conclusions and identify correlations regarding the prevalence of low-yield imaging, diagnostic imaging overuse, adherence to guidelines, and diagnostic yield by trauma severity levels across various years. Factors contributing to low-yield or non-actionable CTA findings were examined using a multinomial logistic regression analysis. Potential contributing factors included patient demographics, trauma severity, timing of presentation, and compliance with the modified Denver criteria. To evaluate the degree of correlation between these variables and the likelihood of low-yield imaging, relative risk ratios (RRR) and confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. A significance criterion of p < 0.05 was applied to all two-tailed statistical tests. Assumptions regarding data normality were assessed, and when necessary, appropriate nonparametric methods were utilized. Adjustments for multiple comparisons, including the Bonferroni correction, were used as needed.

Criteria adherence

There were significant differences in the adherence to the modified Denver criteria; patients in the trauma related actionable findings group were more likely to adhere to guidelines during triage (p = 0.008) and meet the modified Denver criteria (p = 0.001). Additionally, meeting ≥2 criteria on the modified Denver criteria was associated with an even higher likelihood of actionable CTA findings (p = 0.005). Formal sensitivity and specificity calculations for the modified Denver criteria were not performed, as all patients in this cohort underwent CTA and no independent gold standard was available as a comparator. However, among patients with trauma related actionable findings, 71.8% met the modified Denver criteria at triage, demonstrating strong association with guideline adherence for clinically significant injury detection. Notably, 28.2% of trauma related actionable findings occurred in patients who did not meet criteria, consistent with prior literature demonstrating that rigid application of screening criteria may miss a subset of BCVI cases.

Age

Older age demonstrated an increased likelihood of incidental and non-trauma related findings. The mean age in this cohort was 46.1 ± 19.9 years, with the incidence of CTA findings being clearly correlated with age. The probability of incidental findings, non-trauma related actionable findings, and trauma related actionable findings rose with increasing age. Older individuals more commonly presented for falls as the mechanism of trauma and were more likely to have non-trauma related actionable findings that required care, likely reflecting age-related comorbid vascular disease or chronic degenerative changes.

Mechanism of trauma

Motor vehicle accidents (MVAs) were the predominant cause across all trauma mechanisms (69.2% overall) and accounted for 73.2% of trauma related actionable findings. In contrast, falls were more often associated with non-trauma related actionable findings (59.4%). Differences in trauma mechanism distribution were statistically significant (p = 0.001); for example, falls were more common in the incidental and non-trauma related actionable findings. Assault with a blunt object and near-hanging or strangulation injuries were rare and did not significantly contribute to outcomes.

Ethnicity

Significant variations were found by ethnicity (p = 0.003). Hispanics/Latinos were more prevalent in the negative group with 42.8% representation in that category. Caucasians/ whites were more prevalent in the non-trauma related actionable findings category. Those of Afro-American ethnicity accounted for a modest percentage of trauma related actionable findings and an intermediate proportion of negative and incidental results.

Other findings

Patients for whom only a CTAN scan was performed were 93% less likely to have incidental findings when compared to those for whom a CTAHN was performed, indicating that incidental findings are more frequent when the intra and extracranial vessels of the head are visualized. Women had a higher likelihood of trauma related actionable findings with twice as much representation than men (RRR = 2.13; 95% CI, 1.24– 3.67; p < 0.05). Trauma severity designation (p = 0.5), transfer status (p = 0.2), and prior head or neck surgery (p = 0.1) were not significant predictors of CTA outcome.

Clinical care point

Age, severity of vascular injury, and vessel injured were key predictors of clinical management. The majority of dissections, pseudoaneurysms, and occlusions were managed conservatively. A common regimen across all etiologies was antiplatelet management (e.g., aspirin ± clopidogrel) or frank anticoagulation (e.g., IV heparin bridged with warfarin ± factor Xa inhibitor if indicated). Beta blockers were used in conjunction with blood pressure elevation significantly increased the risk for mural stress. Therapy duration depended on persistence of vascular insult which was monitored with imaging on both an inpatient and outpatient basis, depending on the patient. Symptom management varied, with acetaminophen and opioids being used in varying degrees. Cases that required neurosurgery and/or neurointerventional radiology to assess the need for intervention encompassed, but were not limited to, cranial vessel rupture, expanding pseudoaneurysm, and basilar hypoperfusion. Age was the most significant predictor of mortality across all variables.

DISCUSSION

BCVI is used to characterize non-penetrating injuries to the carotid and vertebral arteries, as well as other major vessels of the head and neck.[10] BCVI can result from the pathological stretching, compression, and impingement of vessels because of high-force flexion, extension, rotation, or direct impact.[10,11] If unrecognized, BCVI can progress to thromboembolism, vessel occlusion, dissection, pseudoaneurysm formation, and ultimately stroke or death. Although historically considered rare and an uncommon cause of cerebral ischemia and ischemic stroke, emerging studies estimate that BCVI may occur in up to 2% of all hospital trauma admissions and up to 9% of patients with severe traumatic head injuries.[10] Early recognition of injuries and timely intervention remain salient and are crucial in preventing the progression of BCVI to hemorrhage, vessel occlusion, or stroke.[12] The carotid and vertebral arteries are deemed higher risk in developing cerebral ischemic effects compared to other vessels, and thus are often prioritized in initial assessments.[10,13]

CTA utilizes contrast material to produce images of intra and extracranial vasculature, and can play an indispensable role in the diagnosis, management, and prognostication of BCVI.[11] CTAHN is commonly used to assess clinically significant BCVI and a statistically insignificant incidence of strokes and other pathological outcomes have been observed in trauma patients with negative findings.[10] Its lower invasiveness, cost-effectiveness, and greater availability compared to other imaging modalities make CTA a preferred choice in acute traumatic settings. However, despite its advantages in availability and diagnostic yield, its use is not always essential or appropriate, and it may be its ease of access in the ED that has resulted in substantial overutilization.[6,11]

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate CTAHN and CTAN diagnostic yield for BCVI within an adult trauma cohort drawn from a large, predominantly rural catchment area, where triage variability and transfer patterns uniquely influence imaging practices. By delineating how nonadherence to BCVI screening criteria drives imaging overuse, this study reframes diagnostic yield as a quality of care metric rather than a purely radiologic outcome. Moreover, by integrating epidemiologic, operational, and imaging level data, it bridges the gap between radiologic performance metrics and systems-based clinical decision making in trauma care.

Of the 643 blunt trauma cases that met the inclusion criteria, 74.2% of the CTA screenings yielded negative results. This is consistent with previous studies reporting that up to 85% of CTA studies ordered in the ED yield insignificant or negative results.[6] According to our results, age, trauma type, and modified Denver criteria adherence emerged as the strongest predictors of diagnostic yield.

Our findings indicate that a significant (71.8%) percentage of patients with trauma related actionable findings met the modified Denver criteria (p < 0.005). Furthermore, meeting two or more criteria on the modified Denver criteria was associated with an even higher yield of trauma related actionable findings. These results suggest that guideline-concordant CTA use continues to provide meaningful diagnostic discrimination, even within heterogeneous trauma populations consistent with prior studies.[14] However, of the total patients in the cohort that met the modified Denver criteria (n = 358), only 14.2% (n = 51) were categorized as trauma related actionable findings, suggesting that improved adherence and targeted refinement of existing screening practices, rather than expansion, restriction, or neglect of current criteria, may best enhance utilization efficiency without compromising diagnostic safety.

In this study, not all patients (28.2%) with trauma related actionable findings met the modified Denver criteria during triage. The presence of trauma related actionable findings in patients who did not meet the modified Denver criteria likely reflects atypical injury mechanisms, delayed symptom evolution, or limitations inherent to criteria-based screening tools. For example, prior studies have demonstrated that BCVI may occur in the setting of low-mechanism trauma, suggesting the role of clinical judgment as a necessary adjunct to guideline-based screening.[8] Importantly, however, no increased morbidity attributable to traumatic vascular pathology was observed among patients with trauma related actionable findings who did not meet the modified Denver criteria in this cohort.

Previous studies have found that MVA accounts for nearly 50% of BCVI in acute traumatic settings. Our study agrees with these findings, as a larger proportion (73.2%) of patients in this study with trauma related actionable findings were victims of a motor vehicle associated accident. Conversely, it has been shown that lower mechanisms of injury also have a high contribution to the incidence of BCVI. Harper et al. wrote that no association exists between mechanisms of injury in patients who suffer BCVI and meet criteria for CTAHN or CTAN versus those who do not. Thus, low mechanisms of injury and little to no identifiable risk factors may demonstrate low utility in assessing patients for whom CTA screening is warranted. This may be especially pertinent when screening elderly patients in the ED.[8,14]

A greater portion of elderly patients fell into the incidental and non-trauma related actionable findings categories. Age has been documented as an independent and essential risk factor in the decline of vascular function. Vascular density in the cortex decreases with age, which may be congruent with and a causative factor in cortical atrophy and degeneration. The aged brain is more likely to exhibit arteriolar tortuosity, in the form of spirals, loops, and coils and can demonstrate unfavorable responses in counteracting stress and damage, with a propensity for hypertrophic, eutrophic, and hypotrophic vascular remodeling. Thus, vascular structures in an aged brain have a decreased caliber and intrinsic capability to counteract degenerative changes and trauma.[15] A significant portion of the elderly patients represented in this study were indicated for CTA screening as a result of falls, most of which were from ground level. This supports findings from Harper et al., who reported that elderly patients with ground-level falls have an elevated risk of BCVI despite low impact mechanism of injury.[8]

In this study, female sex was independently associated with higher rates of trauma related actionable findings compared to their male counterparts, a finding not widely reported in previous studies. Magnotti et al. found that gender is not independently associated with mortality after blunt trauma.[16] Whether this is due to a true biological phenomenon or sampling artifact unique to this study may require further investigation.

Our study revealed no significant association between a patient’s transfer status and CTA findings. Transfer status at a level 1 trauma center has been described as an associated factor for repeat imaging since prior imaging is not always sent with patients and is often of poor quality.[17] Our findings indicate that transfer status is a poor predictor of vascular injury risk and contributes to overutilization. Due to the retrospective and single-center nature of this study, we were unable to determine what percentage of transfer patients received duplicate imaging.

Trauma severity designation and previous head or neck surgery or trauma also showed no significant impact on variations in outcome and should thus not be considered in determining whether CTA screening is appropriate. These findings suggest that reliance on these factors does not enhance BCVI detection or diagnostic yield accuracy and should not be considered among other measures.

Previous studies have reported that nonphysician practitioners (NPPs) and ED residents order a significant proportion of CTA exams with non-trauma related findings or negative results. More experienced providers, e.g., ED attendings, have been found to order studies with relatively lower rates of negative or insignificant yield.[18] This study found no such correlation, as all neuroimaging studies ordered by residents or NPPs at our institution are overseen by attending physicians.

Defensive medicine, staff inexperience, and operational constraints, such as time pressures and overcrowding, are among the factors that drive diagnostic imaging overutilization, especially in the ED. Our findings of high nonadherence to the modified Denver criteria and the prevalence of negative or incidental results in the setting of trauma demonstrate that easy access to imaging without strict screening measures can exacerbate overuse.[19-21] Given the significant associations our study identified between patient age, mechanism of trauma, number of points met on the modified Denver criteria and CTA yield, these factors should be integrated into clinical decision making in the emergency setting to improve screening practices, minimize unnecessary imaging, and improve patient care efficiency.

This study has several limitations. The retrospective design introduces potential for information bias, particularly in the assessment of criteria adherence, which relied on documentation available in the electronic medical record. Because this study was a single-center analysis conducted on a largely rural population, generalizability to other settings such as urban academic institutions may be limited. While we included transfer patients, the absence of standardized access to outside imaging or referring documentation may have influenced repeat imaging rates or assessments on CTA appropriateness. Although adherence to the modified Denver criteria was systematically assessed, the retrospective nature of this study may not fully reflect real-time clinical judgment, triage constraints, or other nuances in the emergency setting.

CONCLUSION

Our findings suggest that nonadherence to screening guidelines is a modifiable factor contributing to the overuse of CTAHN imaging in the ED. Despite our institution’s designation as a Level 1 trauma center serving a large rural and medically underserved population, it shares similar constraints in diagnostic efficacy and driving variables in CTA overutilization as urban counterparts. Implementing interventions such as enhanced training for emergency providers, improved access to prior imaging, and stricter adherence to established clinical criteria could help mitigate diagnostic imaging overuse. These measures would not only reduce unnecessary imaging but would also maintain diagnostic accuracy and enhance patient care.
