Executive Summary
As part of the U.S. DOE EVs@Scale consortium, the Next-Generation Profiles (NextGen
Profiles [NGP]) project presents analysis and results from the characterization of high-power
conductive and wireless charging infrastructure. High Power Charging equipment is capable of
recharging electric vehicle traction batteries at power levels of 200kW and above.

Electric Vehicle Supply Equipment (EVSE) characterization involves evaluation over a wide
range of DC charging currents and voltages during nominal and off-nominal conditions. This
evaluation allows for a better understanding of the impact that high-power charging will have on
the electric grid. A common set of standard evaluation plans, procedures, and data requirements
is documented in EVSE Characterization: A NextGen Profiles Project Report (Carlson and Onar
2023), updated in EVs@Scale: NextGen Profiles: EVSE Characterization 2024 (Kogalur, et al.
2025) and were applied to the characterization in this document with minor updates and
improvements. This report covers all electrically conductive power transfer performance
characterization activities performed between October 2024 and September 2025 on the Delta
Electronics 350kW Electric Vehicle Charging System, consisting of power cabinet model EIDN-
U350KTA01 and dispenser model EIDD-U350SSUUAEG-350.

Key Findings include:

 • Output regulation
 • Efficiency and power factor
 • Load management
 • Grid resilience
 • Smart Charge Management (SCM) performance
 • Thermal control system performance
 • Multi-port simultaneous charging performance
 • Selected performance comparisons with other EVSEs characterized in the NextGen
 Profiles project.

Hot and cold temperature evaluation was not conducted on the Delta 350kW due to laboratory
limitations. Future research could include continued evaluation of the Delta hardware under off-
nominal temperature conditions including multi-port/multi-session simultaneous charge analysis,
in addition to collecting data on other high-power conductive chargers to augment the data
collected for the U.S. DOE EVs@Scale consortium NextGen Profiles project.

EVSE Characterization: A NextGen Profiles Project Report

1 EVSE Characterization Introduction
The high-power charging infrastructure performance characterization protocol is performed on
production and future charging equipment to better understand the impact of high-power Electric
Vehicle Supply Equipment (EVSE) on the electrical grid. These experiments enable current and
future modeling efforts by providing accurate, lab-collected performance data.

Using EV emulators, EVSE can be evaluated under repeatable and controlled conditions to
simulate, isolate and characterize charging behavior in Direct Current (DC) High Power
Charging (HPC) systems. Electrically conductive EVSE power transfer performance
characterization aims to explore EVSE performance across nominal and off-nominal conditions
over the full DC voltage and current range, including variations in ambient temperature, AC grid
input, Open Charge Point Protocol (OCPP) Smart Charge Management/Smart Energy
Management curtailment requests, multi-port/multi-session simultaneous charging and
conditions of high EVSE utilization. Interoperability and reliability experiments are outside the
scope of this report and off-nominal temperature evaluation was not performed due to laboratory
limitations associated with nonavailability of required equipment.

This report provides new data and results from the conductive charging EVSE characterization
study performed for the U.S. DOE EVs@Scale NextGen Profiles project, which aligns with
other reports from this project: EVs@Scale NextGen Profiles: High Level Analysis and
Procedures Report (Davidson, et al. 2024), EVs@Scale Next-Gen Profiles - EV Profile Capture
2023 (Thurston and Wells, EVs@Scale Next-Gen Profiles - EV Profile Capture 2023 2023),
EVs@Scale Next-Gen Profiles - Fleet Utilization 2023 (Wells and Thurston 2023). This report
updates and complements EVSE Characterization, A Next-Gen Profiles Project Report (Carlson
and Onar 2023) and EVs@Scale Next-Gen Profiles -EVSE Characterization 2024 (Kogalur, et al.
2025) and project results will be appended to the Next-Gen Profiles EVSE Characterization
dataset (Carlson, et al. 2025).

The Delta 350kW charger has two Combined Charging System Type-1 (CCS-1 or CCS)
connectors and is capable of simultaneous charging. To remain compatible with cross-report
comparisons of prior work, this report will keep a similar nomenclature. For example, Delta_H1
will refer to CCS-1 connection A, while Delta_H2 will refer to CCS-1 connection B.

Subsequent sections will outline more detail about how the data was collected; EVSE
Characterization, A Next-Gen Profiles Project Report (Carlson and Onar 2023) and EVs@Scale
Next-Gen Profiles -EVSE Characterization 2024 (Kogalur, et al. 2025) provide more detailed
explanations.

1.2 Measurement Parameters
1.2.1 Conductive EVSE Measurement Parameters
Approximately 30 parameters from Appendix A are collected using power analyzers and other
laboratory-grade sensors to quantify the system operation for EVSE characterization. Many of
the collected parameters are also collected during EV profile capture as described in EVs@Scale
Next-Gen Profiles - EV Profile Capture 2023 (Thurston and Wells, EVs@Scale Next-Gen
Profiles - EV Profile Capture 2023 2023) and EVs@Scale Next-Gen Profiles - EV Profile

such as battery temperature, are omitted as they do not apply to EVSE characterization. Other
EV emulator-specific parameters are recorded to quantify system performance. The Energy
Management Source Curtailment Request is only recorded during smart charge management
(SCM) evaluations.

A nominal experiment is conducted under ideal conditions that transfers the maximum possible
energy in the shortest amount of time possible. All nominal conditions EVSE performance
characterizations are performed by lining up the EVSE to a grid simulator that provides input 3-
phase 60Hz power in the form of a pure 277 VAC line-to-neutral (phase voltage) sine wave
where all three phases are 120 degrees out of phase with each other. The following requirements
must be met to complete a nominal conditions experiment:

 • Ambient temperature is 23℃ ±2℃
 • Grid AC conditions are 277VAC, 60Hz, no grid harmonics
 • All smart charge management power or current curtailment values are inactive
 • Only one charge session occurs at a time
 • No limits are placed on the EVSE cabinets or available DC current
The block diagram in Figure 7 shows an overview of the general evaluation setup which offers a
clear understanding of the key pieces of the equipment used and their connections. This diagram
also highlights the equipment placed inside the thermal chamber during off-nominal temperature
performance characterization.

 Figure 7. Block Diagram of Experiment Setup for EVSE Performance Characterization
AC measurements at the power cabinets and DC measurements at the kiosk (dispenser) are
performed using two different instruments and data from the Charge Control Box (CCB) vehicle
simulator is collected from a digital real-time simulator (DRTS). The clocks between these three
instruments are synchronized via a 10Hz TTL clock signal output by the DRTS. AC and DC
current signals are redundantly recorded on all three devices to synchronize readings on multiple
data logging devices to ensure all required experiment signals are recorded with high accuracy.
All OCPP commands are logged with timestamps in a local cloud influxDB instance which is
synchronized with SNTP to determine EVSE response latency to OCPP SCM command.

Off-nominal smart charge management response experiments are conducted using OCPP 1.6j for
the current and voltage evaluation conditions of Table 1. EVSE Power Transfer Characterization
Test Conditions. The smart charge management curtailment requests include TxProfile,
MaxChargePoint and TxDefaultProfile which are used for NextGen Profiles SCM experiments
to send curtailment commands of 54kW or 65A for as many as three curtailment profiles for each
of the four off-nominal Table 1 DC power transfer experiment conditions. The smart charge
management curtailment requests determine how power and/or current limits are applied and
prioritized where TxProfile is a per-charge session charging profile with the highest priority,
MaxChargePoint is a station- or connector-level ceiling with lowest priority that defines the
EVSE upper boundary of operating current or voltage and TxDefaultProfile is an enduring
default profile with medium priority.

2.2 Test Procedures and Results
2.2.1 EVSE Characterization at Nominal Conditions Power Transfer

2.2.1.1 Procedure
During nominal conditions EVSE characterization, emulated vehicle traction battery DC voltage
is set to one of several predefined values listed in Table 1 and DC current is stepped in 10A
intervals from the maximum available EVSE DC charging current to 50A where each current
step is no shorter than 180 seconds to allow the EVSE operating condition to stabilize into a
quasi-steady state condition. The evaluation procedures and requirements for the quasi-steady-
state experiments are as follows:

 8

 • The EVSE will soak at the ambient temperature (23°C ±2°C) for a minimum of four
 hours prior to experiment commencement.
 • The charge session can be continuous for the numerous evaluation parameters of quasi-
 steady-state experiments, or the operator may end the charge session and begin a new
 charge session as necessary. The EVSE does not need to soak for four hours at ambient
 temperature again prior to resuming steady-state evaluations.
 • Record the vehicle emulator make/model, EVSE make/model /firmware version, ambient
 temperature, day/time.
 • Data measurements are collected at a rate of 10 Hz.
 • Each quasi-steady-state DC current and DC voltage evaluation point is held for a
 minimum of 180 seconds. This is necessary to ensure the EVSE reaches steady-state
 operation.
 o Steady state is defined as +/- 0.2 ADC and +/-1 VDC over the final 30 seconds of
 the 180-second sample period.
 • The final 30 seconds of collected data are averaged to determine a single steady-state
 value for each measurement parameter for each quasi-steady-state evaluation point.

2.2.1.2 Results

2.2.1.2.1 Output Regulation and Ripple Content
Effective DC output regulation is important for ensuring controllability, efficiency, and battery
longevity during charging. Steady state deviation from commanded currents and ripple content
on the DC output are used as key metrics to evaluate the output regulation of the EVSE. Table 3
demonstrates that the Delta 350kW EVSE exhibits very low steady-state errors across the
evaluated DC voltage and current range at nominal temperature of 23°C at 650VDC. It is
observed that the largest magnitude DC current error is supplied at low DC load levels, i.e.,
≈50ADC. RMS current error of 1.5A across the entire range of EVSE operation is indicative of
exceptionally good EVSE current regulation when compared to the DC output current regulation
and DC output current ripple requirements of the SAE J1772 standard (Society of Automotive
Engineers 2025) and IEC 61851-23 standard (International Electrotechnical Commission 2023).

2.2.1.2.2 Efficiency Results
As the number of EVs on the road grows, EVSE manufacturers face the challenge of ensuring
their systems can reliably charge battery packs with a wide voltage range while maintaining high
efficiency. The conversion of AC to DC power across varying load conditions and DC output
voltages can be challenging, as discussed in (Gaxiola, Gadamsetty and Davidson 2025).

The efficiency curve for the Delta 350kW EVSE at nominal temperature of 23°C is presented in
Figure 9. The Delta 350kW is highly efficient in converting AC power to DC power; hitting
maximum efficiency of 96.4% at 80.14kW of average input AC power and at a load voltage of
850VDC. At lower DC voltages, like 300VDC, the efficiency peaks at 93.6% at 67.76kW of
average input AC power. Efficiency decreases rapidly at loads less than 50kW due to increased
relative impact of parasitic circuit conversion losses such as switching losses, transformer core
losses and magnetizing currents. During post-400VDC nominal conditions experiment analysis,
it was observed that recorded AC power was incorrectly computed with a lower value than
expected because recorded ϕB and ϕC RMS currents were 25A to 35A less than recorded RMS
current on ϕA; recorded currents were balanced on all other tests. This discrepancy caused
computed EVSE efficiency to be greater than 100% for the 400VDC nominal conditions
experiment and is consequently omitted from Figure 9, but is expected to have an efficiency
profile that spans from 20kW to 200kW with values that fall between the values of the 300VDC
and 650VDC efficiency profiles when conducted and measured accurately. It is also noted that

 10

the Delta 350kW EVSE has a minimum system efficiency > 92% at load levels of 50kW or
higher.

2.2.2.2.3 AC Harmonics Injection
As with all other EVSE performance characterizations, the off-nominal grid harmonics
evaluation is performed by lining up the EVSE to a grid simulator that provides input power in
the form of a pure 277 VAC line-to-neutral (phase voltage) sine wave where all three phases are

is started. After the EVSE is plugged in to the vehicle emulator, the charge session is allowed to

2.2.5.1 Procedure

 • Continue to collect data for 5 minutes after completion of charge event or until auxiliary

 • Stop data collection 60 seconds after the end of the evaluation.

Effective thermal management of charging conductors and cables is important for ensuring high

and have minimal maintenance requirements, cost and long useful lifespans. Actively cooled

users with the high standard of performance set by liquid-fueled vehicles that is expected by the
public.

Quantification of the amount of localized heating, effect of placement of EVSE cabinets on
EVSE performance and EVSE performance at off-nominal ambient temperatures are several
areas of inquiry not examined in this report worthy of further study.

2.2.6 EVSE Characterization at Off-Nominal Multi-Port Simultaneous Charging

2.2.6.1 Procedure
The Delta 350kW EVSE has two CCS connectors which are labeled connectors A and B. The
EVSE can charge a single EV using connector A or B or two EVs simultaneously using
connectors A and B, which splits the maximum 350kW power limit. Testing the simultaneous
charging ability of the Delta 350kW EVSE involves setting up two CCBs vehicle simulators to
charge under the steady state conditions listed in Table 13 and then stepping down the current of
one of the simulated vehicles in 10A increments until 50 A is reached; similar to the nominal

 32

power transfer experiment conditions procedure of Section 2.2.1. This evaluation requires
coordination of additional lab equipment to properly load both ports of the EVSE.

The procedure and requirements for evaluation of multi-port simultaneous charging are as
follows:

 • Record the vehicle emulator make/model for both connections, EVSE make/model and
 firmware version, ambient temperature, day/time, OCPP server version, etc.
 • Set voltage and current targets for both simulated vehicles in accordance with Table 13.
 • Begin collecting data for at least 60 seconds before plugging in.
 • Plug in EVSE connector A, begin charging sequence, and record plug-in time.
 • Observe load current ramp for connector A to ensure power is flowing before plugging in
 EVSE connector B.
 • Plug in EVSE connector B, begin charging sequence, and record plug-in time.
 • Observe load current ramp for connector B to ensure power is flowing.
 • Collect quasi-steady state power transfer data for at least 180 seconds.
 • Reduce current for the EVSE charging on connector B by 10A and collect steady-state
 power transfer data for at least 180 seconds to allow the system to reach quasi-steady

 • Stop charging with EVSE side B and unplug.
 • Stop charging with EVSE side A and unplug.
 • Continue to collect data for a minimum of 60 seconds after unplugging.

2.2.6.2 Results
Multi-port/multi-session evaluations were conducted for every experiment condition listed in
Table 13. No major issues were found in any of the experiments and the power output of CCS
connector A held steady while CCS connector B was swept through the load steps. Additional
lab equipment was required to load the second EVSE connection and record data making the
setup slightly more complex.

Figure 20 depicts the AC and DC voltage and current waveforms recorded during the Delta

Delta 350kW EVSE handles simultaneous charging sessions without major issue. It is observed

that a small perturbation occurs in the output current CCS connector B when current steps from

The Table 13 evaluation regimen revealed that when the second vehicle is plugged in to the
Delta 350kW EVSE, the EVSE’s 350kW charging capacity is divided in half between the two
vehicles: vehicle #1 can charge up to 175kW, and vehicle #2 can charge up to 175kW. This
limitation is enforced for the entirety of the time that the two vehicles are connected to the Delta
350kW EVSE: once one vehicle disconnects, the second vehicle can ramp up charging power to
the amount of power requested This limitation does not change depending on charging
capabilities of the connected EV’s: if vehicle #1 connects and charges at its maximum charging
power of 200kW and vehicle #2 connects and charges at its maximum charging power of
100kW, the by the EV within the limitations of the EVSE’s full power 350kW nameplate rating.
Delta 350kW EVSE should be able to provide the requested power of both vehicles
simultaneously, as the total requested 300kW of charging power is within the EVSE’s limit of
350kW. However, in practice vehicle #1 will charge at a rate of 175kW while vehicle #2 is
connected and charging at the requested maximum power of 100kW. A more effective EVSE
charging strategy would modulate available charging power between connected vehicles, so that
in the previous example, vehicle #1 can charge at its requested power of 200kW while vehicle #2
charges at its requested power of 100kW. Such a charging strategy would make more effective
use of installed DC charging infrastructure, minimize dwell times, station queueing and costs as
discussed in more detail in (Gaxiola, Gadamsetty and Davidson 2025). Consequently, additional
study that investigates how charging limits are communicated to vehicles makes
recommendations to optimize power delivery is warranted. Figure 21 depicts the behavior of
this charging strategy during the 400VDC/400VDC 500ADC multi-port/multi-session
experiment. Vehicle #1 plugs in and requests 200kW at 500ADC and 400VDC and receives that
power briefly until vehicle #2 connects and also requests 200kW at 500ADC and 400VDC. From
that point forward, both vehicles receive 175kW while vehicle #2 steps down requested charging
power and eventually disconnects. At that point, vehicle #1 resumes charging at 200kW until the
charge session is terminated.

4 Conclusion
EVSEs were operated under varied real-world conditions comprising a set of nominal and off-
nominal evaluations. This report complements the A NextGen Profiles Project Report and
EVs@Scale: NextGen Profiles: EVSE Characterization 2024 with additional experiment data,
outcomes and evaluation related to EVSE performance by summarizing all activities conducted
to date and presents new data and results collected between October 2024 and December 2025
on the Delta Electronics 350kW Electric Vehicle Charging System.

Under nominal conditions, the Delta 350kW EVSE demonstrated excellent precision and
accuracy in regulating DC output current with minimal steady-state error. On many metrics the
performance of the Delta 350kW EVSE met or exceeded that of EVSE1 and EVSE2. Efficiency
of all EVSEs followed a consistent trend, remaining lower at light loads and improving
significantly once the delivered power exceeded approximately 15% of the rated capacity while
power factor also improves with load; ≈ 95% at high voltage and power levels. Off-nominal
temperature performance of the Delta 350kW EVSE and quantification of the amount of
localized heating and effect of placement of EVSE cabinets on EVSE performance are areas of
inquiry worthy of further study.

Off-nominal characterization evaluations revealed that the Delta 350kW EVSE maintained
uninterrupted operation under all evaluated off-nominal conditions, indicating robust grid
operation. Delta 350kW EVSE off-nominal Smart Charge Management response experiments
performance was on par with other evaluated EVSEs; the OCPP 1.6J smart charging commands
showed successful execution with power reduction and restoration occurring promptly, though
TxProfile commands are not in the Delta 350kW EVSE’s implementation of the OCPP protocol.
The Delta 350kW EVSE is also the only EVSE characterized in the NextGen Profiles project to
date that implements multi-port/multi-session simultaneous EV charging; simultaneous EV
charging performance was satisfactory and improvements were identified to better utilize
available EV charging capacity for shorter dwell times and queueing.

Overall, these comprehensive findings from high-power charging systems highlight important
performance attributes of the next-generation EV charging technologies. The results underscore
the critical role of evaluating HPC EVSEs under several boundary conditions in understanding
system behavior in real-world applications. Evaluation of the Delta 350kW EVSE revealed
outstanding AC to DC power conversion performance and robust response to off-nominal grid
and operating conditions. Four failures of individual EVSE power modules during the course of
NextGen Profiles performance characterization present cause for concern and opportunity for
additional reliability evaluation and study. As EV charging moves toward higher power levels
and broader deployment, optimizing system-level design for reliability, thermal performance,
and grid responsiveness will be essential. Future research could include continued evaluations of
the Delta hardware under off-nominal temperature conditions including multi-port/multi-session
simultaneous charge experiments, in addition to collecting data on other high-power conductive
chargers to augment the data collected for the U.S. DOE EVs@Scale consortium NextGen
Profiles project.
