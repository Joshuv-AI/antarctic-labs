Student nurses’ knowledge and attitudes toward dementia care: a systematic review
Authors: H. S. Maliga S. K. Wijesiri, Thilini Kumari Weerasingha, Nimantha Karunathilaka, Sarath Rathnayake
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDementia is a global health problem. Providing care for people living with dementia (PLWD) is challenging for healthcare providers and caregivers. Nursing students should possess a strong knowledge base and positive attitudes toward caring for PLWD. This study aimed to synthesise evidence related to the knowledge and attitudes of nursing students toward dementia care.MethodsA systematic review was conducted. PubMed, MEDLINE, CINAHL, Web of Science, and PsycINFO electronic databases were searched. Peer-reviewed, full-text articles published in English from 2013 to 2026 were included. Rayyan software was used for data management, and the PRISMA guideline was followed. Narrative synthesis approach was used in data analysis.ResultsDatabase search identified 6020 sources. Following the removal of duplicates (n=1761), 4,259 records were screened by title and abstract, and 4,194 records were excluded. Full-text screening was conducted for 65 articles, and 28 articles that met the inclusion criteria were selected via databases (kappa value was 0.803). In addition, 12 articles were included via citation searching. Among the 40 included studies, 29 were cross-sectional, 10 were quasi-experimental, and one was a cohort study, with sample sizes ranging from 20 to 784 students. Overall, nursing students demonstrated low to moderate levels of dementia-related knowledge, with notable gaps in management of dementia, communication, medication-related care, and end-of-life care. Although attitudes toward PLWD were generally positive, some studies reported lower confidence in dementia care. Educational interventions, including structured clinical placements, mentorship, and targeted educational approaches, consistently improved knowledge and attitudes in the included quasi-experimental studies. Factors such as year of study, prior experience with dementia, gender, and urban residency were found to enhance nursing students’ knowledge and promote more positive attitudes toward dementia.ConclusionAlthough nursing students generally demonstrate positive attitudes toward PLWD, their dementia-related knowledge is often limited. The integration of dementia-specific content, experiential learning, and reflective practice into undergraduate curricula, along with interventions such as aged care placement, various forms of educational exposures and expanded clinical experience, is essential to equip future nurses with the skills and attitudes necessary for high-quality, person-centred dementia care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-05094-w.

Introduction

Dementia is a major global health challenge and is considered the seventh leading cause of death, disability, and dependency among older people worldwide [1]. Globally, nearly 57 million people were living with dementia, and 60% of them lived in low- and middle-income countries [1]. With the rapid growth of the older population worldwide, the prevalence of dementia is also increasing, placing a burden on individuals, families, communities, and healthcare systems [2]. People living with dementia (PLWD) usually report unmet needs due to the severity of symptoms, comorbid conditions and functional and cognitive decline associated with dementia [3, 4]. Moreover, the evidence shows that the increased number of older people living with dementia proportionately increases the number of hospital admissions [5, 6], and places considerable psychological and economic strain on the family caregivers [7]. Therefore, providing care for PLWD is considered a major challenge [3, 4]. Meanwhile, studies have shown that the increasing number of PLWD admitted to hospitals has increased the need for nurses who are adequately prepared to provide dementia care [8, 9]. Given the growing burden of dementia, knowledge, skills, and positive attitudes toward dementia care among nurses are essential for providing effective care.

Due to the rapid increase in dementia prevalence, role of the health care workers in minimizing disease progression, enhancing quality of life and providing family support is increasingly highlighted [3, 10, 11]. Previous studies have shown that limited dementia knowledge among nursing students is associated with lower confidence and may hinder their preparedness to provide dementia care in clinical settings [12–14]. Adequate dementia-related knowledge alone may not be sufficient for providing high-quality dementia care [12], as positive attitudes toward PLWD are also essential [15]. Previous studies have reported that first- and second-year nursing students often demonstrate negative to neutral attitudes toward dementia care, whereas attitudes become more positive during later years of study as students gain greater clinical exposure and educational experience [14, 16]. A negative attitude could result from exposure to negative role modelling during the students’ clinical training [16]. Inadequate mentoring and limited dementia-related knowledge among clinical staff may also negatively influence students’ clinical learning experiences in dementia care settings [17]. Managing PLWD who exhibit unpredictable behaviors is identified as a major challenge during the students’ clinical placements [11, 18]. However, available evidence suggests that dementia education is not sufficiently emphasized within undergraduate nursing curricula [11, 13, 19, 20] and limited dementia-related knowledge may negatively influence students’ confidence and interest in dementia care practice, increasing stress and the risk of burnout in the workplace [21, 22]. In contrast, nursing students with strong academic preparation and exposure to a well-structured curriculum generally demonstrate better knowledge and more positive attitudes toward dementia care [2, 19, 23]. Therefore, it is essential to provide adequate knowledge and guidance for nursing students in order to promote more positive attitudes [24].

Previous systematic reviews have primarily focused on specific aspects of dementia education among nursing students. For example, Adewuyi et al. reviewed the impact of experiential care learning on nursing students’ dementia-related knowledge and attitudes and did not specifically synthesise the evidence related to basic levels of knowledge and attitudes among students across different educational and clinical contexts [25].

Assessing the current level of dementia knowledge and attitudes among student nurses is important for understanding the competencies of future nurses working in hospitals or residential aged care facilities [2, 14, 26], although few studies found that student nurses’ knowledge of dementia care may be inadequate [2, 14, 27]. Enhancing student learning, planning curriculum, continuous education, and training to support the integration of knowledge and attitudes are essential for improving clinical experiences in dementia care, as students represent the future workforce. This systematic review aimed to synthesise existing evidence on student nurses’ knowledge and attitudes towards the care of people living with dementia.

Methods

We conducted a systematic review, following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [28]. The protocol was registered on the PROSPERO website (PROSPERO ID: CRD42023476123).

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria for the studies encompassed in this review were: (1) peer-reviewed, full-text journal articles published in English between 2013 and 2026; (2) Quantitative studies conducted among pre-registration nursing students (diploma or undergraduate degree programmes); (3) studies that evaluated nursing students’ knowledge and/or attitudes toward dementia care or people living with dementia; (4) cross-sectional, cohort, case-control, and quasi-experimental/interventional study designs.

The exclusion criteria were: (1) studies involving registered nurses, graduate nurses, other healthcare professionals, or mixed populations where nursing student data could not be extracted separately; (2) qualitative studies, systematic reviews, scoping reviews, editorials, letters, case reports, case series, conference abstracts, dissertations, and theses; (3) articles not published in English; (4) articles without accessible full text.

Search strategy

A search strategy was developed to identify published studies on knowledge and attitudes toward dementia care among student nurses. The search was conducted between December 2023 and May 2024 and was updated in March 2026. Five databases (PubMed, MEDLINE, CINAHL, Web of Science and PsycINFO) were searched using combinations of the following keywords: dementia OR Alzheimer* AND nurs* AND student* OR pupil* OR scholar* AND knowledge* OR awareness OR attitude* OR perception* OR opinion*. The MeSH terms used were dementia, “Alzheimer disease”, “dementia care”, aged, “student nurses”, students, nursing, knowledge, perception and attitudes (Supplementary file 1). The term ‘aged’ was included in the search strategy to focus the search on studies involving older adults with dementia.

Selection process

After the database search, reviewers conducted a thorough search for eligible studies by electronically screening the titles and abstracts of all identified articles. Rayyan software was used for data management. After removing duplicates using Rayyan software, sources were screened based on title and abstract (ti/ab) by two independent researchers (MW and TKW) concerning inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussions with a third researcher (SR). Full texts of potentially relevant articles were then downloaded and independently reviewed by two researchers (MW and TKW) using the same inclusion and exclusion criteria. The kappa value for the initial full-text screening was computed. The research team discussed the selected and excluded papers with reasons during a consensus meeting, and any disagreements were resolved.

Risk of bias (quality) assessment

The methodological quality of the studies was assessed by the Jonna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools [29–31]. The risk of bias was assessed independently by two researchers (MW and NK). JBI checklists for analytical cross-sectional, quasi-experimental and cohort studies were used for the quality appraisal. Discussion of any disagreement was discussed with a third researcher (SR). All the finalised articles were checked for quality (Table 1).

Data extraction

A data extraction template based on an Excel data sheet was developed in line with the study objectives, and it was reviewed by the research team. Data extraction included names of the authors, publication year, country, title, study aims, study setting, study design, number and characteristics of participants, data collection methods and tools used, major findings and conclusions. Two researchers (MW, TKW) extracted data from forty studies, with TKW extracting data from 50% of the studies. Disagreements between the two researchers were discussed, and the data extraction template was modified accordingly. The first researcher (MW) completed the data extraction. In addition, we contacted the authors of four studies to request missing mean (SD) scores; however, only one author responded and provided the mean (SD) scores.

Data analysis

A narrative synthesis approach was used to integrate the findings of the included studies. Information related to study characteristics, nursing students’ knowledge and attitudes toward dementia care was tabulated.

Results

Study selection

Figure 1 presents the study selection process. A total of 6020 studies were found through the databases searched, and 1761 duplicates were removed. Then 4259 studies were included for ti/ab screening, and 4194 sources were excluded based on ti/ab screening. Sixty-five articles were included for full-text screening. The initial kappa value for the full-text screening was 0.803. Following the citation searching of the selected 28 articles, 27 records were found. Among the 27 records, 25 reports were retrieved, and two were unable to be retrieved. The 25 retrieved records were assessed for eligibility, and 13 reports were excluded based on inclusion and exclusion criteria, and 12 articles were selected for the review from citation searching. Finally, 40 full-text articles were included for final data extraction. The reasons for excluded articles are presented in Fig. 1.

Study characteristics

Table 1 presents the characteristics of the included studies in this review. Among the 40 studies included, 29 were cross-sectional [2, 12–14, 23, 32–55], 10 were quasi-experimental studies [19, 56–64], and one was a cohort study [65]. Studies were conducted across various countries, including USA (n = 8), China (n = 5), Turkey (n = 4), Australia (n = 3), UK (n = 2), Saudi Arabia (n = 2), Korea (n = 2), Jordan (n = 2), Spain (n = 1), Israel (n = 1), Namibia (n = 1), Vietnam (n = 1), Taiwan (n = 1), Indonesia (n = 1), Nigeria (n = 1), India (n = 1), Malta (n = 1), Nepal (n = 1), Norway (n = 1) and samples obtained from both USA and Ireland (n = 1). Sample sizes in the cross-sectional studies ranged from 102 to 784 participants. In the quasi-experimental studies, sample sizes ranged from 20 to 121 participants. The cohort study included 488 nursing students [65]. Among the quasi-experimental studies, except for three studies [56, 61, 63], the sample size was below 100 [19, 57–60, 62, 64]. Among the included studies, studies were conducted recruiting nursing students in their final year [2, 12, 23, 32, 46, 55, 56, 62], and students were recruited from more than one academic year [2, 12, 14, 56, 59]. Studies were conducted in various settings including university or academic settings [12, 23, 32–36, 38, 39, 41, 44–46, 48–55, 57, 62–65], clinical settings or clinical placements [35, 37, 58], aged-care settings [19, 37, 57, 59] and community-based settings [60]. In knowledge assessment, the assessment tools used were Alzheimer’s Disease Knowledge Scale (ADKS) (n = 19) [2, 12–14, 23, 37, 38, 41, 42, 45–47, 50–54, 58, 65], Dementia Knowledge Assessment Scale (DKAS) (n = 7) [36, 40, 44, 48, 55, 56, 63], Dementia Knowledge Assessment Tool 2 (DKAT 2) (n = 2) [19, 57], Basic Knowledge of Alzheimer’s Disease (BKAD) (n = 2) [50, 64], Dementia Knowledge Questionnaire (DKQ) (n = 2) [49, 65], and UJA-Alzheimer’s Care Scale (UJA-ACS) (n = 1) [34]. In the assessment of attitudes toward dementia, the assessment tools used were Dementia Attitudes Scale (DAS) (n = 17) [2, 12, 14, 35–38, 41, 43, 44, 47, 48, 52, 53, 58, 60, 65], Dementia Care Attitude Scale (DCAS) (n = 3) [23, 46, 63], Attitudes to Dementia Questionnaire (ADQ) (n = 2) [32, 65], and Advanced Dementia Care Questionnaire (ADCQ) (n = 1) [23].

There were 34 articles that focused on nursing students’ knowledge levels [2, 12–14, 19, 23, 34, 36–38, 40–42, 44–50, 51–59, 61–65] and 25 articles focused on attitudes levels of student nurses [2, 12–14, 23, 32, 33, 35–39, 41, 43, 44, 46–48, 52, 53, 58, 60, 61, 63, 65]. Only one quasi-experimental study focused primarily on attitudes toward dementia care [60].

Risk of bias in studies

Based on JBI critical appraisal tools, overall, the cross-sectional studies demonstrated good methodological quality, with many studies clearly reporting inclusion criteria, measurement reliability, and valid assessment methods. However, several studies did not identify or address potential confounding factors. The quasi-experimental studies generally met the criteria for outcome measurement, reliability, and statistical analysis, although most lacked a control group and did not consistently report whether participants received comparable care apart from the intervention of interest. The cohort study demonstrated adequate methodological quality; however, reporting on confounding factors and follow-up completeness was unclear in several domains. A summary of risk of bias assessment of included studies is provided in Supplementary file 2. Key methodological limitations reflect the most frequently observed sources of bias across studies.

Synthesized findings

Knowledge toward dementia

Overall, most cross-sectional studies reported low to moderate dementia-related knowledge among nursing students, although knowledge levels varied according to educational level, country, and assessment instrument. Several studies reported a lower level of dementia knowledge among nursing students. For example, Aslan et al. reported a lower level of knowledge with the mean (SD) score of 13.01 (6.66) based on DKAS among nursing students in Türkiye [36]. Similarly, Alotaibai reported a mean (SD) ADKS score of 13.83 (3.29) out of 30 among undergraduate nursing students in Saudi Arabia [41], while Su et al. identified a low dementia knowledge score with a DKAS mean (SD) score of 10.3 (2.1) in Hong Kong, China [40]. In India, Poreddi et al. reported inadequate knowledge with an overall mean (SD) ADKS score of 16.8 (3.48) [13]. Pham et al. also reported inadequate dementia knowledge among diploma nursing students in Vietnam, with a total mean (SD) score of 20.82 (2.96) out of 34 using ADKS [2].

Several studies reported moderate levels of dementia knowledge. Laura et al. (2022) reported a moderate level of dementia knowledge among nursing students in Spain, with a mean (SD) score of 15.33 (3.69) based on the UJA-ACS [34]. Similarly, Scerri and Scerri found overall ADKS mean (SD) scores ranging from 18.35 (2.75) to 20.18 (3.48) across different academic years among Maltese nursing students [14]. Khatiwada et al. also reported moderate dementia knowledge among nursing students in Nepal, with an ADKS mean (SD) score of 19.64 (3.36) [52]. In Jordan, Aljezawi et al. reported an overall knowledge mean (SD) score of 18.3 (2.7) among nursing students [12], whereas Dong et al. found moderate knowledge levels among nursing students in China with a mean (SD) ADKS score of 18.44 (3.17) [53]. A few studies demonstrated comparatively higher levels of dementia knowledge. Romem et al. reported higher knowledge mean (SD) scores among advanced nursing practitioners, 24.92 (3.47), compared to nursing students, 22.3 (3.34), in Israel [37]. Similarly, Kada found relatively high ADKS mean (SD) scores of 24.52 (2.30) among final-year undergraduate nursing students in Norway [54]. Furthermore, Khait et al. demonstrated significantly higher dementia knowledge scores among senior nursing students compared to junior students, suggesting that academic progression and clinical exposure may positively influence dementia-related knowledge [55].

In all quasi-experimental studies, the knowledge levels of student nurses improved significantly following dementia-specific clinical placements [19, 57–60]. Most quasi-experimental studies used a pre-test post-test design to examine the effectiveness of supportive aged care placement, critical reflection opportunities, and different educational exposures and experiences. Several studies conducted in the USA, Australia and the UK found that post-test knowledge mean (SD) scores were higher than the pre-test mean (SD) scores [19, 57–60, 64]. More recent studies conducted in the USA, such as Morales et al. and Compton-Dooley and Little, further demonstrated that virtual simulation, e-learning modules, and dementia-specific communication training significantly improved nursing students’ dementia-related knowledge following pre-test post-test interventions [62, 63]. Baseline dementia knowledge levels among nursing students were generally reported as low to moderate prior to the interventions. For example, Eccleston et al. reported relatively low baseline D-KAT2 scores among nursing students before residential aged-care placements, with intervention group mean (SD) scores increasing from 13.44 (3.64) pre-placement to 17.81 (2.22) post-placement in Australia [19]. Similarly, Lea et al. demonstrated significant improvement in dementia knowledge among intervention students, with mean (SD) D-KAT2 scores increasing from 14.1 (3.3) pre-placement to 17.6 (2.3) post-placement following supported aged-care placements in Australia [57].

Moderate baseline dementia levels of knowledge were observed in several intervention studies. Kimzey et al. reported that senior nursing students in the USA who participated in Alzheimer’s disease clinical experiences demonstrated improved ADKS scores from a pre-test mean of 23.42 to a post-test mean of 25.58 following the intervention [58]. Likewise, Morales et al. found that dementia-focused e-learning and virtual simulation significantly improved DKAS scores among nursing students, with mean (SD) scores increasing from 34.73 (6.34) to 41.40 (5.38) in one intervention group [63]. Other quasi-experimental studies also demonstrated significant improvements in dementia-related knowledge following educational interventions. Wood et al. reported significant increases in knowledge scores following an educational program combining dementia theory and care-home experiences, with knowledge levels remaining significantly higher six months after baseline in the USA [59]. Similarly, Compton-Dooley and Little reported substantial improvement in nursing students’ knowledge related to dementia-associated brain changes, with mean knowledge scores increasing from 64.64 pre-intervention to 89.73 post-intervention in the USA [62]. Love et al. also found improved BKAD scores following educational interventions in the USA, with mean (SD) scores increasing from 23.7 (4.6) pre-test to 27.4 (3.4) post-test [64]. Furthermore, Cai and Chen reported that dementia care courses improved nursing students’ knowledge and perceptions regarding dementia care and filial responsibilities [61].

The cohort study conducted by Hebditch et al. in the UK reported modest improvements in dementia-related knowledge over time, with increases in mean (SD) scores observed in both the ADKS and DKQ across the study period [65].

Overall, the evidence suggests that nursing students generally demonstrated low to moderate dementia-related knowledge, while structured educational interventions and dementia-specific clinical experiences consistently improved knowledge outcomes across different educational settings.

Attitudes toward dementia

When focusing on cross-sectional studies, many studies demonstrated that nursing students generally held positive or moderately positive attitudes toward PLWD [12, 14, 32, 36, 52]. All respondents in the study conducted by Jansen et al. [32] reported positive attitudes toward PLWD; however, nursing students expressed significantly more positive attitudes than medical students. Furthermore, Jansen et al. (2013) suggested that although healthcare students hold positive attitudes toward PLWD, some clinical tasks remain challenging, and further basic training may be beneficial [32]. Similarly, Aslan et al. found that the nursing students had a moderately positive attitude mean (SD) score 82.53 (12.44) toward dementia care using DAS [36]. In the study by McKenzie and Brown, nursing students demonstrated relatively high levels of positive ageism mean (SD) scores 4.72 (0.93) and lower levels of negative ageism mean (SD) scores 2.37 (0.64) [33]. Romem et al. reported the lowest mean (SD) attitude score among nursing students (103.25 ± 14.88) and the highest among geriatric nurse practitioners (116.7 ± 13.5) [37]. Vasquez et al. reported that although 92% of respondents did not plan to work with PLWD immediately following graduation, and they would like additional dementia training incorporated into their regular curriculum (79%) and clinical training (62%) [35]. Therefore, the study suggested reducing the low interest and stigma related to older adult and dementia care by giving students structured clinical experiences to help them develop as future geriatric and dementia care nurses [35].

Positive attitudes toward dementia were also identified in studies conducted in Malta, Nepal, Jordan, Indonesia, China, and India. Scerri and Scerri reported an overall DAS mean (SD) score of 103.51 (13.43) among Maltese nursing students [14], while Khatiwada et al. found positive attitudes toward PLWD with a DAS mean (SD) score of 93.82 (10.09) in Nepal [52]. Likewise, Aljezawi et al. demonstrated positive attitudes among Jordanian nursing students, with an overall mean (SD) DAS score of 91 (15.6) [12]. In Indonesia, Sumandar et al. reported moderately positive attitudes among nursing students despite low levels of dementia knowledge [44]. Similarly, Poreddi et al. found that undergraduate nursing students held encouraging attitudes toward PLWD despite inadequate dementia-related knowledge in India [13]. Vasquez et al. reported that although many nursing students in the USA desired additional dementia education and clinical training, most respondents did not plan to work with PLWD immediately after graduation [35]. However, not all studies demonstrated positive attitudes. Tomas and Mangundu reported negative perceptions and attitudes toward dementia care among nursing students in Namibia, with perception scores ranging from 2.26 to 2.43 across several dementia care domains [39]. The authors emphasized the need for improved dementia-care training and support systems to enhance nursing students’ dementia care competencies and attitudes.

In quasi-experimental studies, nursing students’ attitudes toward PLWD generally improved following dementia-specific educational interventions, clinical placements, simulation-based learning activities, and reflective learning experiences. Most studies employed pre-test–post-test designs and reported significant improvements in students’ attitudes toward dementia care after the intervention. For example, studies conducted by Kimzey et al., Yordy et al. and Morales et al. in the USA reported improved attitudes toward PLWD following structured educational interventions, clinical learning experiences, and dementia-focused training programmes [58, 60, 63]. Similarly, Wang, Xiao and Huang reported moderately positive attitudes toward dementia among nursing students in China [23]. Overall, the findings suggest that dementia-specific educational programmes and meaningful clinical exposure can positively influence nursing students’ attitudes toward dementia care.

The cohort study conducted by Hebditch et al. in the UK found that nursing students generally demonstrated limited attitudes to pursue careers in dementia care [65]. Positive educational experiences, effective communication skills, and exposure to dementia care were associated with greater career interest, whereas limited confidence and perceived challenges of dementia care acted as barriers. The study highlighted the importance of curriculum enhancement and clinical exposure in fostering interest in dementia-related careers.

Factors influencing nursing students’ knowledge and attitudes

In addition to results related to levels of nursing students’ knowledge and attitudes, studies identified the factors influencing nursing students’ knowledge and attitudes toward dementia care. Factors such as female gender, higher year of study, and increased clinical hours were associated with higher dementia-related knowledge [34, 36]. Furthermore, higher knowledge levels, living in urban areas, and prior caregiving experience contributed to more positive attitudes toward dementia [36]. Educational interventions and clinical placements in aged-care or dementia-care settings consistently improved knowledge and attitudes [19, 57–60]. Effective communication skills and the challenging nature of dementia care work were also identified as contributing factors for pursuing careers in dementia-related fields [65]. When focusing on associated factors, having a family member with dementia and prior dementia education were both negatively associated with knowledge [36]. When focusing on attitudes toward working with older people, older students were more willing than younger students, whereas younger students reported emotional strain as a barrier [27]. Curriculum quality is also associated with attitudes towards selecting a career in dementia care [65]. Lack of knowledge and lack of experience were considered as factors associated with lower confidence and frustration in dementia care tasks [32, 35]. Therefore, the findings indicate that skills-based training and clinical exposure may increase nursing students’ confidence in dementia care and promote greater interest in pursuing dementia-related careers [14, 58, 63, 65]. McKenzie and Brown identified age and positive ageism as significant predictors of students’ attitudes toward dementia care, whereas negative ageism and aged-care placement completion were not significantly associated with career intentions [33]. Pham et al. found that female students and those with higher dementia knowledge demonstrated significantly more positive attitudes toward dementia care [2]. Similarly, Wang, Xiao and Huang identified gender, dementia education, and interest in learning more about dementia as significant associated factors [23].

Discussion

This systematic review aimed to synthesise evidence related to the nursing students’ knowledge and attitudes toward dementia care. Results were obtained from studies conducted in various countries, including the USA, China, Turkey, Australia, the UK, Saudi Arabia, Korea, Jordan, Spain, Israel, Namibia, Vietnam, Taiwan, Indonesia, Nigeria, India, Malta, Nepal and Norway. The sample sizes ranged from 20 to 784, comprising both junior and senior nursing students. The samples were selected from various clinical and educational settings, including universities, aged-care facilities and community settings. The selected studies used different instruments to assess knowledge (ADKS, DKAS, DKAT, BKAD, UJA-Alzheimer’s Care Scale, DKQ) and attitudes (DAS, DCAS, ADQ, ADCQ) of student nurses toward dementia care. The majority of the studies used ADKS (n = 19) and DAS (n = 17) to assess knowledge and attitudes toward dementia, respectively. However, the differences in findings may be due to variations in methodological aspects such as data collection instruments, sampling methods and sample size, study settings, and study durations of selected studies.

Knowledge and attitudes toward dementia

There is a need to address gaps in specific knowledge and attitude domains, as nursing students generally demonstrate low to moderate levels of dementia-related knowledge [19, 36, 37]. Studies showed that higher dementia-related knowledge and previous experience caring for PLWD were associated with more positive attitudes toward dementia care [12, 36, 52]. This may be because greater knowledge and exposure help nursing students better understand the needs of PLWD, thereby reducing stigma and increasing confidence in care provision [19, 57]. Several other studies have demonstrated that prior education about dementia can improve knowledge of dementia [14, 19, 49, 53, 66]. In addition, the results suggest that a well-supported clinical placement in a residential aged care facility can improve nursing students’ knowledge of dementia, particularly concerning aspects directly relevant to their clinical practice [19]. Therefore, facilitating both teaching and learning environments with clinical mentors during their practice and providing opportunities for more clinical exposure [57–60] in this area would provide more competent skills and better attitudes toward dementia care and managing the complex health-care needs of older adults [57]. Although a small number of studies reported relatively high dementia knowledge scores among senior nursing students or senior nurses [14, 23, 58], the overall evidence across the included studies suggests that nursing students generally demonstrate low to moderate levels of dementia-related knowledge [12, 13, 19, 53, 55]. Therefore, to enhance the knowledge of student nurses, studies mostly suggested focusing on conducting dementia related training, educational programs [27, 37, 57, 67], and educational interventions [19, 37, 58, 59]. Moreover, student nurses’ knowledge can be improved by refining undergraduate nursing curricula [34, 36] and incorporating dementia-specific competencies [35], which will be essential for addressing the challenges posed by the higher prevalence of dementia worldwide. Furthermore, although Hebditch et al. found that student nurses reported a lack of preference to work with older people with dementia [33, 65], they found that preference could be enhanced by introducing a well-developed curriculum for nurses. Ultimately, a systematic review conducted by Adewuyi et al. [25] supports these findings, suggesting that curriculum development and experiential learning can enhance nursing students’ competence in dementia care and bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and practice.

Some of the included studies also reported findings related to “positive ageism” among nursing students [27]. Although positive attitudes toward older adults may encourage empathy and interest in caring for them, the concept of positive ageism should be interpreted with caution. Positive ageism may unintentionally promote stereotypical views that older adults are generally dependent, vulnerable, or have similar care needs. Within dementia care, such assumptions may conflict with person-centred approaches that emphasize individuality, autonomy, dignity, and respect for the unique experiences of PLWD. Therefore, nursing education should aim not only to foster positive attitudes toward older adults but also to promote critical reflection and individualized, non-stigmatizing approaches to dementia care.

When focusing on educational interventions and their effectiveness, Lea et al. reported that a well-supported placement programme in residential aged care facilities has improved the knowledge and attitudes of undergraduates toward dementia care [57]. Recent evidence suggests that technology-enabled educational approaches to dementia education are effective. A systematic study conducted by Muirhead et al. found that technology-enabled dementia education is a practicable step to enhance knowledge, skills, and attitudes among health and social care professionals [68]. Several interventions were conducted related to web-based e-learning platforms and found that those were useful and highly influential toward improving empathy and awareness of caregivers and nursing students [69].

Overall, the quasi-experimental studies consistently demonstrated that dementia-specific educational interventions, including clinical placements, simulation-based learning, e-learning modules, communication-focused training, and experiential learning opportunities, significantly improved nursing students’ dementia-related knowledge. Although baseline knowledge levels were generally low to moderate across studies, post-intervention findings showed notable improvements in students’ dementia knowledge toward dementia care. The findings suggest that nursing students generally demonstrate positive or moderately positive attitudes toward PLWD, although educational preparation, clinical exposure, and perceptions of dementia care continue to influence their attitudes to engage in dementia-related practice.

As a summary, the results showed that nursing students scored low to moderate knowledge and moderately positive attitudes toward dementia care in this study. Dementia specific educational interventions, conducting skills-based training, providing opportunities for clinical exposure with experienced clinical mentors, refining undergraduate nursing curricula and incorporating available technologies would significantly enhance knowledge and attitudes among student nurses.

Limitations of evidence

The current systematic review has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, only studies published in the English language were included, which may have introduced language bias and excluded relevant evidence published in other languages. Second, the review included only quantitative studies and excluded qualitative research, thereby limiting exploration of nursing students’ lived experiences and contextual perspectives related to dementia care. Third, grey literature such as conference abstracts, theses, and unpublished studies was excluded, which increased the possibility of publication bias. Furthermore, despite a comprehensive database search, some potentially relevant studies may not have been captured. In addition, the substantial heterogeneity in study designs, sample characteristics, educational interventions, knowledge and attitudes assessment instruments, ranging from knowledge questionnaires to attitude scales, limits the comparability across studies. This variability limited direct comparability between studies and prevented meta-analysis.

Implications for nursing education and future research directions

The findings of the current systematic review can suggest several nursing education and research-related implications for the future. Academically, nurses’ education should be focused on integrating dementia as a core component of the curriculum rather than having it as an optional component. The special focus on clinical exposure and engagement of dementia specific mentors in clinical supervision is highlighted when planning the clinical placement. Curriculum design should also emphasize the development of empathy toward people living with dementia through experiential learning, reflective practice, simulation-based learning, and supervised clinical exposure. In addition to the level of nurses’ education, capacity building of healthcare professionals in dementia care is essential as part of continuous professional development towards enhancing knowledge, skills, and improving attitudes to provide compassionate care. The use of validated and standardized knowledge and dementia assessment tools, such as the UJA Alzheimer’s Care Scale, would improve the consistency and comparability of outcomes across studies and contexts. Thus, the tools are ultimately useful in generalizing the results. When focusing on future research directives, it is essential to conduct studies using longitudinal designs, which will enhance the actual and stronger outcome interpretations of educational interventions. Greater emphasis is also highlighted on culturally well-adapted interventions, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Furthermore, future systematic reviews should consider meta-analysis to quantify the effect sizes of specific educational strategies.

Conclusion

Although a few studies reported relatively high knowledge scores, the overall evidence indicates that undergraduate nursing students generally demonstrate low to moderate dementia-related knowledge despite reporting positive attitudes toward PLWD. Knowledge gaps were noted in managing complex care needs, medication-related care, and end-of-life care, while attitudes were positively influenced by prior clinical exposure, reflective learning, and dementia-focused education. Educational interventions, including structured clinical placements and mentorship, were shown to significantly improve both knowledge and attitudes, and experiential learning is essential for developing competent dementia-care skills among future nurses. Also, the findings of the current systematic review highlighted that there is an essential need for dementia specific curriculum content focusing on skills-based clinical training to prepare nurses equipped with knowledge, skills and better attitudes and preferences toward working with people with dementia. In addition, incorporating modern available technological approaches in educational interventions and undergraduate education would influence greater knowledge in nursing students and increase positive attitudes toward working in dementia clinical settings, which would be highly important to meet the care demand increasing with the higher prevalence of dementia.
