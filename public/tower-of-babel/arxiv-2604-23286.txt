Kronecker coefficients via the Giambelli identity for Schur functions


Abstract
One of the central open problems in both algebraic combinatorics and representation theory is to find a positive combinatorial rule for Kronecker coefficients g λ ​ μ ​ ν g_{\lambda\,\mu\,\nu} . A notable advance in this direction is due to Blasiak, who proved a combinatorial interpretation in terms of colored Yamanouchi tableaux for the case whereby one of the indexing partitions is hook-shaped. In this paper, we introduce a framework for the evaluation and combinatorial interpretation of Kronecker coefficients, combining a Schur function identity of Littlewood, the Giambelli identity for Schur functions, and Blasiak’s combinatorial rule. This framework reduces the study of Kronecker coefficients to alternating sums involving hook-indexed cases. As an application of this framework, we obtain combinatorial interpretations of g t , h ( 1 ) , h ( 2 ) g_{t,h^{(1)},h^{(2)}} for two-row partitions t t and hook-like partitions h ( 1 ) h^{(1)} and h ( 2 ) h^{(2)} satisfying natural conditions. More broadly, our approach provides a systematic method for extending hook-based combinatorial rules to wider families of Kronecker coefficients.

[

, it is remarkable how the multiplicities arising in decompositions of

Indeed, the problem of determining such a combinatorial interpretation is, to this day, one of the most important open problems in
representation theory and in algebraic combinatorics. This problem is often referred to as the

We introduce a framework for the evaluation and combinatorial interpretation of Kronecker coefficients, based on a combination of the
Giambelli identity
[
24
]
(cf.
[
36
, pp. 47, 61]
), a compatibility identity of Littlewood

[

relating the coproduct and the Kronecker product on

. This framework provides a systematic reduction of Kronecker coefficients indexed by
general partitions to alternating sums involving hook-indexed Kronecker coefficients and Littlewood–Richardson coefficients.
Although we focus on specific families of partition shapes in this paper, the underlying reduction mechanism applies more broadly
and suggests a general approach to extending hook-based combinatorial rules.

The character version of the Specht module decomposition highlighted in (

\chi^{\mu}\chi^{\nu}=\sum_{\lambda}g_{\lambda\,\mu\,\nu}\chi^{\lambda},

(6)

g_{\lambda\,\mu\,\nu}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\chi^{\mu}(\sigma)\chi^{\nu}(\sigma).

(7)

As emphasized by Lascoux
[
32

, by writing a given Kronecker coefficient as a combination of irreducible characters of

\mathfrak{S}_{n}

7

\mathfrak{S}_{n}

much in the way of insight when it comes to positivity properties of or combinatorial interpretations of

This highlights the interest in our Giambelli–Littlewood–Blasiak-based approach to the Kronecker problem.

1.1
Outline

Let
\lambda
be an integer partition written in Frobenius notation so that
\lambda=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d}\,|\,\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{d})
, for the number
d
of diagonal boxes of
\lambda
, and where
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
, respectively, denote
the arm and leg length of the
i^{\text{th}}
box, for
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
. The Giambelli identity may then be formulated so that

s_{\lambda}=\det\left(s_{\left(\alpha_{i}+1,1^{\beta_{j}}\right)}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq d}.

(8)

Rewriting (
8
) using the Leibniz formula, we find that

s_{\lambda}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{d}s_{\left(\alpha_{i}+1,1^{\beta_{\sigma(i)}}\right)}.

(9)

The identity

(s_{\lambda}s_{\mu})\ast s_{\nu}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\vdash|\lambda|\\
\eta\vdash|\mu|\end{subarray}}c_{\tau\,\eta}^{\nu}(s_{\tau}\ast s_{\lambda})(s_{\eta}\ast s_{\mu})

(10)

for partitions
\lambda
,
\mu
, and
\nu
is originally due to Littlewood in 1956
[
34
]
. The identity
(
10
) can be interpreted as a compatibility relation
(fg)\ast h=\sum(f\ast h_{(1)})(g\ast h_{(2)})
between the
Kronecker product and the coproduct
\Delta
on
Sym
, using Sweedler notation and writing
\Delta(h)=\sum h_{(1)}\otimes h_{(2)}
. A key to our construction in Section
4
is given by applications of the Littlewood rule in
(
10
). More precisely, we rewrite a Schur function using the Giambelli–Leibniz expansion in
(
9
) and then apply the Littlewood identity in (
10
) to expand the Kronecker product with a
fixed Schur function. Extracting coefficients yields an expression of a Kronecker coefficient (in which one of the indexing partitions
is a non-hook) as an alternating sum involving Littlewood–Richardson coefficients and hook-indexed Kronecker coefficients. The
latter admit combinatorial interpretations via Blasiak’s rule
[
10
]
, and we then reduce the resulting alternating sum in a
cancellation-free manner to obtain new combinatorial formulas.

We define a
near-hook
as an integer partition of the form
(a,b,1^{c})
for
a\geq b
, providing a natural extension of
hook-partitions. For the case of Schur functions indexed by near-hooks, the Giambelli–Leibniz expansion gives us that

s_{(a,b,1^{c})}=s_{(a,1^{c+1})}s_{(b-1)}-s_{(a)}s_{(b-1,1^{c+1})}.

(11)

Applying the above steps (as made more explicit in Section
4
), we obtain an alternating sum of the form

g_{\lambda\,(a,b,1^{c})\,\beta}=\sum_{\eta,\delta,\theta}c_{\eta\,\delta}^{\beta}g_{\theta\,(a,1^{c+1})\,\eta}c^{\lambda}_{\theta\,\delta}-\sum_{\eta,\delta,\theta}c_{\eta\,\delta}^{\beta}g_{\theta\,(b-1,1^{c+1})\,\delta}c^{\lambda}_{\eta\,\theta},

(12)

and an explicit version of this expansion is highlighted in Theorem
2
. Since both of the expressions

g_{\theta\,(a,1^{c+1})\,\eta}
and
g_{\theta\,(b-1,1^{c+1})\,\delta}
involved within the summands in (
12
)
admit combinatorial interpretations due to Blasiak
[
10
]
, the determination of a combinatorial interpretation for the
left-hand side of (
12
), for a given or restricted shape
\lambda
, reduces to the determination of cancellations
that can be formulated via bijections defined using
LR/Blasiak tableaux.

For the purposes of this paper, we restrict the application of our technique to special cases of (
12
). However, the
above framework, relying on a threefold application of the Giambelli–Leibniz expansion, Littlewood’s rule, and Blasiak’s combinatorial
interpretation, can be applied much more broadly. Conceptually, this framework reduces the Kronecker problem to the analysis of
cancellation phenomena in structured families of Littlewood–Richardson and Blasiak tableaux.

One might think that sums as in (
12
) would be intractable in terms of requiring large or infeasible amounts of
cancellation, in order to determine explicit and cancellation-free formulas from (
12
), especially since these kinds of
problems often arise in algebraic combinatorics (as explored, notably, by Benedetti and Sagan
[
8
]
in a different
context). However, by restricting the superscripting partition of an LR coefficient, sums as in (
12
) can be shown to
reduce/“collapse” in remarkable ways, in regard to the nonzero terms that remain.
The remarkable ways in which sums as in (
12
) simplify illustrate the
versatility and usefulness of our Giambelli–Littlewood–Blasiak-based approach.

2.2
Kronecker coefficients and Giambelli’s identity

The Giambelli identity, as given in Section
1.1
below, has previously been applied in the study of Kronecker coefficients, but in
a different way compared to our methods and results. In the work of Pak et al.
[
43
]
(cf.
[
45
]
),
the Giambelli determinantal identity is used to rewrite Schur functions in terms of hook-shaped components, which are applied in the
study of character values and associated asymptotic properties using the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule. While Pak et al. use the
Giambelli identity as a tool for facilitating character computations, our work uses Giambelli’s formula in a more structural and
fundamental way to obtain explicit expansions of Kronecker coefficients themselves. Our applications of Giambelli’s identity may also be
seen in relation to Eğecioğlu and Remmel’s combinatorial proof of this identity
[
20
]
, as their
combinatorial proof helps to explain how the structure of the Giambelli expansions can be applied in relation to our goals.

2.3
Kronecker coefficients and Littlewood’s rule

What we refer to as
Littlewood’s rule
, as given in (
10
), naturally arises in the context of Kronecker coefficients,
as it provides an expansion for mixed products of Schur functions involving the Kronecker and usual products on
Sym
. Our
work may be viewed as complementary to that of Tewari
[
55
]
, who studies near-rectangular shapes, whereas we focus on
near-hook shapes. In both settings, Littlewood’s identity in (
10
) is used to derive and reduce alternating sums of
Schur functions, although the combinatorial structures involved differ significantly.

There are many further and notable works on Kronecker coefficients involving the manipulation of sums derived from Littlewood’s rule.
For example, Pak and Panova’s paper
[
44
]
on unimodality properties related to Kronecker products connects with our
work in terms of how the Littlewood identity in (
10
) is key to the derivations of their main results, i.e., via
manipulations of sums obtained from Littlewood’s rule, producing alternating sums used, in turn, to obtain unimodality properties.
This reflects how we apply Littlewood’s rule via alternating sums as in (
12
).

Haiman’s introduction of mixed insertion laid the foundations for the work of Blasiak

may also be seen in relation to how Haiman’s work builds upon classical versions of

Based on extant research concerning Blasiak’s combinatorial interpretation,

2.5
Applications related to Kronecker coefficients

Kronecker coefficients are considered as providing a cornerstone of the discipline of geometric complexity theory

More broadly, since Kronecker coefficients arise via the decomposition of tensor products of irreducible representations of symmetric

In addition to the references cited above, there has been a variety of different research developments, over recent years, on Kronecker

The algebra

and referring to Macdonald’s text for background
[

]
and

, and for

not directly used in our work. The definition in (

) gives rise to the notational

h_{\lambda}=h_{\lambda_{1}}h_{\lambda_{2}}\cdots h_{\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)}}
for an integer partition

\lambda
, giving rise to the
complete homogeneous basis

\{h_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda}
of

, letting this basis
be indexed by the family of integer partitions, and writing
h_{()}=h_{0}=1
for the unique empty partition
()
. Again from the

h_{\lambda}h_{\mu}
of two complete homogeneous basis elements is

-basis element

\textsf{Sym}=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}\textsf{Sym}_{n}.

closely related to the

The algebra

Definition 7
.

(Mixed insertion)   Let
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{n}
be a colored word and let
T_{0}
be a colored tableau. We construct tableaux
T_{0},T_{1},\dots,T_{n}
recursively, in the following manner.

If
w_{i}
is unbarred, then we insert this character into the initial row of
T_{i-1}
. If
w_{i}
is barred, then we insert it into the initial
column of
T_{i-1}
. Whenever a character
\alpha
is bumped, we insert
\alpha
into the row immediately underneath if
\alpha
is
unbarred, and into the column immediately to the right if
\alpha
is barred. We then continue until no new letter is bumped. The
resultant tableau
T_{n}
is called the
mixed insertion tableau
of
w
This may be denoted as
T_{0}\xleftarrow{\operatorname{m}}w

[
10
, Definition 2.4]
(cf.
[
26
]
).

Definition 8
.

Let

be a colored word. We then form a sequence
P_{0}
,
P_{1}
,
\ldots
,
P_{n}
of tableaux recursively,
so that
P_{0}
is the empty tableau, and so that

P_{k}=P_{k-1}\xleftarrow{\operatorname{m}}w_{k},

(29)

where the right-hand side of (
29
) is used to indicate the mixed insertion of
w_{k}
into
P_{k-1}
according to Definition

7
. We then write
P(w)=P_{n}
and refer to this final tableau as the
mixed insertion tableau

of
w

[
10
, Definition 2.4]
.

Definition 9
.

A colored word
y
is Yamanouchi if, for every suffix
y_{k+1}y_{k+2}\cdots y_{n}
, its content forms a partition (see Section 3.1 of
Blasiak’s work
[
10
]
).

Following Blasiak
[
10
]
(comparing the following streamlined definition against the material in Section 3.1 in Blasiak’s
paper, and against Liu’s simplification
[
35
]
of Blasiak’s construction),
colored Yamanouchi tableaux
may be defined
as members in sets of the form

\operatorname{CYT}_{\lambda,d}=\left\{T=P(w)\,\Bigg|\,\begin{matrix}\text{$w$ is a colored word of content $\lambda$;}\\
\text{$\operatorname{tc}(w)=d$; and}\\
\text{$w^{\operatorname{blft}}$ is Yamanouchi.}\end{matrix}\right\}.

(30)

This is equivalent to the formulation used in Blasiak’s hook Kronecker rule
[
10
, Theorem 3.5]
.

For a tableau
T
, we write
T_{\operatorname{SW}}
in place of its most southwest entry. We impose the condition that the
southwest entry is unbarred, as in Blasiak’s combinatorial interpretation
[
10
]
. This leads us to the remarkable
result due to Blasiak (cf.
[
10
, Theorem 3.5]
) whereby

g_{\lambda\,(n-d,1^{d})\,\nu}=\#\{T\in\operatorname{CYT}_{\lambda,d}\,|\,\operatorname{shape}(T)=\nu,\,\text{$T_{\operatorname{SW}}$ is unbarred}\}.

(31)

For the purposes of this paper, we refer to the tableaux in the set on the right of (
31
) as
Blasiak tableaux
.
More specifically, for the parameters/variables presented in (
31
), we refer to the tableaux in the set on the right
of (
31
) as
Blasiak tableaux parameterized by
\lambda
,
(n-d,1^{d})
, and
\nu
.

.

Although Blasiak’s original definition imposes semistandard conditions

)) via the repeated applications of mixed insertion (and with the use of the

4
Near-hooks

A
near-hook
, for the purposes of this paper, refers to an integer partition of the form
(a,b,1^{c})
for
a\geq b\geq 2
. The
following Theorem may be seen as providing the basis for our subsequent results.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\nu\vdash n
and
\lambda\vdash n
, and let
a
,
b
, and
c
be such that
a\geq b\geq 2
and
a+b+c=n
. Then

g_{\lambda\,(a,b,1^{c})\,\nu}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\vdash n-(b-1)\\
\delta\vdash b-1\\
\theta\vdash n-(b-1)\end{subarray}}c_{\eta\,\delta}^{\nu}g_{\theta\,(a,1^{c+1})\,\eta}c^{\lambda}_{\theta\,\delta}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\vdash a\\
\delta\vdash n-a\\
\theta\vdash n-a\end{subarray}}c_{\eta\,\delta}^{\nu}g_{\theta\,(b-1,1^{c+1})\,\delta}c^{\lambda}_{\eta\,\theta}.

Proof.

From the consequence of the Giambelli–Leibniz expansion shown in (
11
), we write

s_{(a,b,1^{c})}\ast s_{\nu}=\big(s_{(a,1^{c+1})}s_{(b-1)}\big)\ast s_{\nu}-\big(s_{(a)}s_{(b-1,1^{c+1})}\big)\ast s_{\nu}.

(36)

In the formulation of the Littlewood identity in (
10
), we set
\lambda=(a,1^{c+1})
and
\mu=(b-1)
, yielding

\left(s_{(a,1^{c+1})}s_{(b-1)}\right)\ast s_{\nu}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\vdash n-(b-1)\\
\delta\vdash b-1\end{subarray}}c_{\eta\,\delta}^{\nu}\left(s_{(a,1^{c+1})}\ast s_{\eta}\right)\left(s_{(b-1)}\ast s_{\delta}\right),

(37)

and we find that (
37
) simplifies to

\left(s_{(a,1^{c+1})}s_{(b-1)}\right)\ast s_{\nu}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\vdash n-(b-1)\\
\delta\vdash b-1\end{subarray}}c_{\eta\,\delta}^{\nu}\left(s_{(a,1^{c+1})}\ast s_{\eta}\right)s_{\delta}

(38)

according to (
19
). Now, in Littlewood’s identity, we set
\lambda=(a)
and
\mu=(b-1,1^{c+1})
, producing the relation

\left(s_{(a)}s_{(b-1,1^{c+1})}\right)\ast s_{\nu}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\vdash a\\
\delta\vdash n-a\end{subarray}}c_{\eta\,\delta}^{\nu}\left(s_{(a)}\ast s_{\eta}\right)\left(s_{(b-1,1^{c+1})}\ast s_{\delta}\right),

(39)

and we see that (
39
) simplifies to

\left(s_{(a)}s_{(b-1,1^{c+1})}\right)\ast s_{\nu}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\vdash a\\
\delta\vdash n-a\end{subarray}}c_{\eta\,\delta}^{\nu}s_{\eta}\left(s_{(b-1,1^{c+1})}\ast s_{\delta}\right),

(40)

again according to the rule for Kronecker products associated with trivial characters. From the relation in (
5
), the coefficient of

s_{\lambda}
on the left-hand side of (
36
) is
g_{\lambda\,(a,b,1^{c})\,\nu}
. Applying
\langle s_{\lambda},\cdot\rangle
to both sides of each of (
38
) and (
40
), the desired result then follows from the
Littlewood–Richardson rule together with the expansion rule for Kronecker products of Schur functions in terms of the Schur basis.
∎

Lemma 1
.

Let
x\geq y
,
u\geq v
, and
d\geq e
, and let
d+e=x+y+u+v
. Then

c^{(d,e)}_{(x,y)\,(u,v)}=\text{{\bf 1}}_{\max(x+v,y+u)\leq d\leq x+u}.

(41)

Equivalently, we have that
c^{(d,e)}_{(x,y)\,(u,v)}\leq 1
with equality if and only if there exists an integer
k
satisfying both

0\leq k\leq\min(x-y,u-v)

and

(d,e)=(x+u-k,y+v+k).

Proof.

By the Jacobi–Trudi rule, we have that

s_{(x,y)}=h_{x}s_{(y)}-h_{x+1}s_{(y-1)},

so that

s_{(x,y)}s_{(u,v)}=h_{x}\big(s_{(y)}s_{(u,v)}\big)-h_{x+1}\big(s_{(y-1)}s_{(u,v)}\big).

(42)

The Pieri rule then gives us that

h_{m}s_{(u,v)}=\sum_{t=0}^{\min(m,u-v)}s_{(u+m-t,v+t)}+\sum_{\ell(\mu)\geq 3}a_{\mu}s_{\mu}

(43)

for certain coefficients
a_{\mu}
. By applying (
43
) to the right-hand terms in (
42
), and by then disregarding any
terms associated with
h_{x}s_{\mu}
or
h_{x+1}s_{\mu}
for
\ell(\mu)\geq 3
(since we are interested in an LR coefficient with a
two-row outer shape), we obtain the linear combination

\sum_{t=0}^{\min(y,u-v)}h_{x}s_{(u+y-t,v+t)}-\sum_{t=0}^{\min(y-1,u-v)}h_{x+1}s_{(u+y-1-t,v+t)}.

(44)

Applying the Pieri rule within both of the summands in (
44
), and then disregarding any terms indexed by partitions of
length at least
3
, a telescoping argument gives us that the remaining terms are of the forms

s_{(x+u,y+v)},\,s_{(x+u-1,y+v+1)},\,\ldots,\,s_{(x+u-m,y+v+m)},

writing
m=\min(x-y,u-v)
, so that

s_{(x,y)}s_{(u,v)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\min(x-y,u-v)}s_{(x+u-k,y+v+k)}+\sum_{\ell(\mu)\geq 3}b_{\mu}s_{\mu},

(45)

for certain coefficients

of the desired result.
