Effectiveness of prophylactic administration of intravenous paracetamol for prevention of post-spinal anesthesia shivering among parturients undergoing cesarean section at a tertiary specialized hospital: a prospective cohort study, 2025
Authors: Mussie Gezahegn Teklewold, Sulaiman Jemal Muzien, Meron Abrar Awol, Tenbite Daniel Desta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Spinal anesthesia (SA) often causes shivering, which can lead to harmful physiological effects. Recent research suggests that intravenous paracetamol may help prevent this complication, but evidence is limited in low-resource settings such as Ethiopia.Objective:This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of prophylactic intravenous (IV) paracetamol in preventing post-SA shivering in mothers undergoing cesarean section.Methods:A prospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary specialized hospital from January to April 2025. One hundred ASA Physical Status II parturients scheduled for cesarean delivery under SA were enrolled. Participants were divided into paracetamol (exposed) and non-paracetamol (unexposed) groups using systematic random sampling. Shivering incidence and severity, axillary temperature, mean arterial pressure, and intraoperative adverse effects were monitored at various intervals. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27. Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical data, and the Shapiro–Wilk test assessed normality. Independent-samples t-tests and Mann–Whitney U tests were used to compare group differences.Results:Shivering occurred in 30% of the paracetamol group versus 54% in the control group (P = 0.015). Shivering severity also differed significantly (P = 0.035).Conclusion:Prophylactic IV paracetamol significantly reduces the incidence and severity of shivering after SA in cesarean deliveries. These findings support its use and encourage further comparative studies with other agents.

Introduction

Cesarean section (CS) is a surgical procedure for delivering a baby through an incision in the mother’s lower abdomen and uterus. It is indicated for mothers whose labor is not progressing well because of fetal or maternal complications. Reports on global CS rates from 1990 to 2018 have shown increases. The number of CS procedures performed worldwide in 2015 was estimated to be 140 million, although delivery rates vary greatly around the globe. According to a cross-sectional study that was carried out using the 2019 Ethiopia Mini Demographic and Health Survey (EMDHS), 5.44% of births were by CS in Ethiopia. The prevalence of cesarean deliveries in Addis Ababa was 38.3%, with certain risk factors, such as maternal education and giving birth in a private healthcare facility[1].

CS may be carried out under general, epidural, or spinal anesthesia (SA). Anesthesia-related maternal mortality in the UK was reduced from 30–50 per 100 000 maternities per triennium in the 1970s to 19 per 100 000 maternities in the early 1980s as a result of the transition from general anesthesia (GA) to neuraxial anesthesia (NA). According to recent data, NA appears to be superior to GA in terms of fetal outcomes (Apgar scores and cord pH). Even though NA is better for cesarean section (CS), there are occasions in which GA is better, such as emergency cases in which NA is not possible in time or is contraindicated. There are several NA methods –combined spinal-epidural, epidural, and single-shot spinal techniques – that share common technical aspects[3,4].

SA is the most frequently used type of anesthesia for CS performed on lower segments. In contrast to the epidural technique, SA is more rapid and simple to carry out, with a definite end point. It is affordable and simple to use, and it is associated with reduced risks of maternal morbidity and mortality and neonatal depression, a minimal risk of regurgitation and aspiration of gastric contents, and a high success rate compared with to GA. However, it is not without adverse effects, such as shivering[4,5].

Shivering is an involuntary physiological reflex of the skeletal muscles that efficiently maintains body heat as part of the thermoregulatory system’s response to cold. Vasodilation, which promotes rapid heat loss and the transfer of body heat from the core to peripheral tissues, is the mechanism by which SA induces shivering. This leads to hypothermia and shivering. It is commonly graded using the Bedside Shivering Assessment Score (BSAS)[5–7]. BSAS has sufficient interrater reliability for use by a wide range of practitioners[7].

Apart from the discomfort resulting from post-SA shivering (PSAS), shivering also results in an increase in carbon dioxide production and oxygen utilization, leading to hypoxemia and an increase in lactic acid concentration, delayed wound healing, cardiac ischemia, and increased wound pain[8] Furthermore, shivering may make it challenging to monitor blood pressure, oxygen saturation, and ECG[9–11] Because shivering raises cardiac output and metabolic heat production by up to 600% above basal values, the mortality and morbidity rates and the length of hospital stay for patients with cardiovascular disease will increase[5].

Paracetamol is a popular, safe, and effective analgesic medication possessing antipyretic effects that lower the hypothalamic temperature set point by centrally mediated prostaglandin inhibition[6,8]. After intravenous (IV) infusion, it has a quick onset of action within 15–20 minutes, and its effect diminishes after 4 hours. Even though studies on the effectiveness of IV paracetamol for shivering prophylaxis are limited, existing research suggests its potential benefits. The literature on paracetamol’s effectiveness in preventing shivering is inconsistent, with some studies showing positive results and others showing no effect[12]. To the best of our knowledge, no published studies have investigated the prophylactic use of IV paracetamol for the prevention of shivering in Ethiopia. Therefore, this study aims to determine its effectiveness in preventing PSAS. This cohort study has been reported in line with the STROCSS 2025 guidelines[13].

Methods

This institution-based prospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary specialized hospital from January 1 to 1 April 2025 after approval by the Institutional Review Board of the Department of Anesthesia. This study included 100 parturients who were scheduled to give birth by CS under SA at TASH and who met the inclusion criteria during the study period.

Parturients with a history of alcohol abuse, hypersensitivity to paracetamol, liver disease, diabetes, hypertension, chronic lung disease, kidney disease, a body temperature greater than 38°C or lower than 36°C, or cardiopulmonary disease were excluded from the study. Mothers who received labor analgesia with opioids or had SA with opioid adjuvants such as fentanyl, morphine, pethidine, or dexamethasone were also excluded.

The sample size for this cohort study was determined using Epi Info, version 7.2.6.0. A 1:1 ratio of paracetamol-exposed and control groups was assumed, with a significance level of P < 0.05, a power of 80%, and a 95% confidence interval.

Based on estimates from a previous study conducted in Iran by Khalili et al, the incidence of shivering was anticipated to be 31.2% in the control group and 6.2% in the paracetamol-exposed group[14]. The minimum sample size, calculated by applying the Fleiss method with a continuity correction, was 90 participants, 45 in each group.

To account for potential missing data, the total sample size was 100 participants (50 in each group). A systematic random sampling method was employed to select 100 parturients from the daily schedule of both elective and emergency CS at the hospital.

Written questionnaires were used to gather the data. The questionnaires were adapted from previously conducted studies and were developed in English. Data collectors received thorough training on how to collect data on the severity of shivering, intraoperative hemodynamic parameters, and any adverse effects before data collection began.

Perioperative anesthetic management of mothers undergoing CS in TASH is carried out by anesthesiology residents and anesthesia professionals. Pre-anesthetic evaluation and planning of the appropriate anesthetic are done the day before elective CS. However, for emergency CS, pre-anesthetic evaluation is done in the waiting area immediately before bringing the patient to the operating room. The assigned anesthetist administers 30 mL of antacid suspension and a prokinetic (metoclopramide, 10 mg IV).

After the mothers entered the operating theater (OT), standard anesthesia monitoring (BP, SpO2, RR, and ECG) was applied, and baseline vital signs were recorded. Following this, they were preloaded with 500–700 mL of normal saline via bilateral IV lines and informed about positioning for SA. Under strict aseptic technique, SA was performed using 2.5 mL of isotonic bupivacaine at the L3–L4 interspace. The effectiveness of SA was confirmed by assessing motor, sensory, and autonomic blockade. The above activities were routinely performed at TASH.

Participants were prospectively included based on their exposure to prophylactic IV paracetamol, as determined by the attending anesthesia provider. Mothers who received 1 g of prophylactic IV paracetamol 10 minutes before SA during preloading were enrolled in the exposed group, while those who did not receive it were included in the non-exposed group. Data collectors evaluated shivering for 1 hour after SA in the two groups.

For both groups, the occurrence of PSAS was assessed and graded using a structured questionnaire based on the BSAS.

Baseline hemodynamic parameters (MAP, heart rate, axillary temperature, oxygen saturation, and respiratory rate) were documented before SA. After SA was given, the duration until shivering started, degree of shivering, the total amount of anti-shivering medication administered, and intraoperative adverse outcomes, such as nausea and vomiting, were all measured and documented. MAP and axillary body temperature were recorded at 5 minutes and then at 10-minute intervals for 1 hour after SA.

Shivering will be graded using the BSAS.

Grade 0: No shivering is noted on palpation of the masseter, neck, or chest wall (None).

Grade 1: Shivering localized to the neck and/or thorax only (Mild).

Grade 2: Shivering involves gross movement of the upper extremities in addition to the neck and thorax (Moderate).

Grade 3: Shivering involves gross movements of the trunk and the upper and lower extremities (Severe).

Under the supervision of a responsible anesthetist, three BSc Anesthesia professionals obtained data at their workplace. Every day, the principal investigator received reports from the supervisor on each completed questionnaire.

Following the principal investigator’s training of the data collectors, the data were gathered and entered into the prepared data abstraction tool. Several steps were taken to ensure data quality. The data abstraction tool was informally pretested on a small number of participants who were not involved in the final sample. The correctness, ease of understanding, consistency, and clarity of the questionnaires were examined, and in addition, the researcher oversaw the data collectors and verified each day that the data were complete.

Data analysis

After the data were obtained, they were analyzed using the SPSS version 27 statistical software package. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize and describe baseline parameters, age, and incidence of shivering in the paracetamol and unexposed groups and were presented as tables and figures. Categorical data, such as the degree of shivering, were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test and were presented as frequencies and numbers. Normality of continuous data (age, BMI, and duration of surgery) was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and Levene’s test was used to assess homogeneity of variance. Independent-samples t-test was used to compare the mean BSAS between the groups for normally distributed data, with results reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Mann–Whitney U tests were applied to non-normally distributed quantitative data, and the results were presented as the median and interquartile range (IQR).

As shown in Fig. 1, the incidence of PSAS is significantly lower in the paracetamol group (15 [30%]) than in the non-paracetamol group (27 [54%]), with a P-value of 0.015044. A chi-square test was employed, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Comparison of severity of shivering

There was a statistically significant difference in the distribution of shivering grades between the paracetamol and non-paracetamol groups (P-value = 0.035), as shown in Figure 2. In the paracetamol group, 12 participants (80%) experienced Grade 1 shivering, three (20%) had Grade 2, and none had Grade 3. In contrast, the non-paracetamol group had 10 participants (38.5%) with Grade 1, 15 (57.7%) with Grade 2, and one (3.8%) with Grade 3. A chi-square test was used, and values are presented as numbers and percentages. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Comparison of nausea and vomiting

There was no significant difference in the incidence of nausea between the paracetamol group (44%) and the non-paracetamol group (42%) (P-value = 0.84). Similarly, the incidence of vomiting was 20% in the paracetamol group compared with 12% in the non-paracetamol group (P-value = 0.28). A chi-square test was conducted; values are presented as numbers and percentages. A P-value value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Discussion

This prospective cohort study evaluated the effectiveness of IV paracetamol (IV PCM) for the prevention of PSAS among mothers undergoing CS. This study demonstrated a significantly lower incidence of shivering in the paracetamol group (30%) compared with the non-paracetamol group (54%; Fig. 1), with a P-value of 0.015, indicating statistical significance.

Shivering is a very common complication following induction of anesthesia owing to post anesthesia hypothermia and postoperative pain, and it causes distress for both patients and clinicians. Additionally, it may affect the readings on monitoring devices. Thus, prevention of shivering is important, both pharmacologically and nonpharmacologically. Even though several pharmacological agents have been used, the best treatment for post-anesthesia shivering is still being researched. The results of this study showed that IV paracetamol was effective in reducing the incidence and severity of PSAS. Paracetamol is a safe, effective, and widely used analgesic with antipyretic properties that inhibits prostaglandin synthesis to reduce the hypothalamic temperature set point[9,15,16].

The results of the current study are consistent with several previous studies that have demonstrated the antishivering effect of IV paracetamol when given before SA. Shivering has been found to occur in 40–70% of patients after anesthesia without any preventive measures [9]. This range includes the 54% incidence of shivering we found in the non-paracetamol group. Our results align with a double-blind, randomized controlled trial at Ain-Shams University Hospitals that investigated the effectiveness of paracetamol, dexamethasone, and a placebo in preventing PSAS during lower limb and lower abdominal surgeries. In that study, the incidence of PSAS was significantly reduced in the paracetamol group (15%) compared to the control group (77%) and the dexamethasone group (40%) (P < 0.001)[9]. Even though our study included parturients undergoing CS, we also noticed a significantly lower incidence of shivering among mothers taking paracetamol compared with mothers who did not. Despite the fact that prophylactic IV paracetamol consistently lowers shivering in both investigations, the incidence of shivering was somewhat higher in our paracetamol group. This may be due to variations in types of surgery, study populations, timing of paracetamol administration, and the controlled room temperature in their study.

Furthermore, the findings of our study are supported by a clinical trial conducted by Khalili et al at Al Zahra and Kashani Hospitals in Isfahan on the effect of IV paracetamol infusion before induction of anesthesia on core and peripheral temperature changes and postoperative shivering in patients undergoing GA. They found that 6.2% of patients who received paracetamol experienced shivering, compared with 31.2% in the control group. This difference was statistically significant (P = 0.02)[14]. Although the types of anesthesia (general versus spinal) and the types of surgery differ from those in our study, the preventive effect of paracetamol infusion on shivering remains evident in both studies. The higher incidence of shivering in the paracetamol group in our study is attributed to obstetric physiological changes, SA, the temperature of the fluids administered, room temperature, and differences in perioperative anesthetic management.

The results of this study also align with the findings of a prospective, double-blind randomized controlled trial conducted by Gholami and Hadavi at Besat Nahaja General Hospital in Tehran. They evaluated the prophylactic effect of IV paracetamol on post-anesthesia shivering in mothers undergoing elective CS under GA. A significantly lower incidence of shivering was observed among mothers receiving paracetamol (9.1%) compared with the saline group (50.9%) (P < 0.05)[17]. This demonstrates the preventive effect of paracetamol against shivering. However, the incidence of shivering in the paracetamol group was much lower than what we found. This disparity may be explained by several factors, such as the type of anesthesia (SA vs. GA) differences in study design, and the avoidance of cold fluid infusion in their study.

Unlike our study, a randomized controlled trial conducted in Thailand among 93 pregnant women revealed that there was no significant difference between the paracetamol group and the control group in the incidence of intraoperative or postoperative shivering. That study found 6% of mothers in the paracetamol group and 4% in the control group shivered intraoperatively, whereas 15% in each group experienced shivering in the PACU[18]. Variations in sample size, study design, timing of drug administration, and shivering evaluation techniques may be the cause of the inconsistency between the two studies.

In our study, we found that 80% of the mothers in the paracetamol group experienced only Grade 1 shivering, whereas a higher proportion in the non-paracetamol group experienced Grade 2 shivering (57.7%), with one case of Grade 3 shivering (3.8%) (P = 0.035). No cases of Grade 3 shivering were observed in the paracetamol group. These findings indicate that paracetamol infusion not only reduces the incidence of shivering but also minimizes the severity of PSAS among parturients undergoing CS.

In alignment with this study, a study by Wahdan et al[8] showed that prophylactic use of paracetamol significantly reduced the incidence and severity of shivering. They studied paracetamol (P) versus ondansetron (O) for prevention of postoperative shivering in liposuction surgeries under combined general-epidural anesthesia. The incidence of shivering and its severity were found to be reduced in groups P and O compared with the saline group, with incidences of 25% and 37.50% in groups P and O, respectively, versus 77.5% in the saline group. However, the study design, study population, and timing of paracetamol administration are different from ours.

Moreover, our study confirms previous findings of a study done by Esmat et al[9], comparing the effectiveness of paracetamol, dexamethasone, and control groups in reducing PSAS. In their study, they found clinically significant shivering (defined as Grade ≥ 2) in 15% of patients in the paracetamol group, 40% in the dexamethasone group, and 76% in the control group (P < 0.001). These results are comparable to those reported in our study. In our study, we found that only 20% of patients in the paracetamol group experienced clinically significant shivering, compared to 61.5% in the non-paracetamol group (P = 0.035).

Conclusion

Prophylactic administration of IV paracetamol is effective in reducing the incidence and severity of shivering following SA in mothers undergoing CS, without causing significant hemodynamic instability, nausea, or vomiting. Preoperative IV paracetamol for mothers undergoing CS before SA reduces the incidence of adverse physiological effects on hemodynamic and metabolic parameters. In addition, it is used as a baseline finding for further studies in multicenter institutions and for comparison with other drugs.

Strengths and limitations

The study was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of its findings. However, both groups had similar sociodemographic and baseline hemodynamic characteristics, allowing for a fair comparison. Consistent anesthesia and surgical techniques were used across the groups to ensure uniformity in treatment.
