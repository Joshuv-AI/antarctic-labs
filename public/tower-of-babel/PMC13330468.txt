Pheochromocytoma multisystem crisis presenting with abdominal pain in an adolescent: a case report
Authors: Xueli Quan, Zhiling Wang, Luwen Zhang, Chen Chen, Feng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPheochromocytoma is a rare neuroendocrine tumor in children, typically presenting with hypertension, headache, and palpitations. Presentation with abdominal pain as the sole initial symptom, rapidly progressing to catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy, cardiogenic shock, and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), is exceedingly uncommon and poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. We report a case of adolescent pheochromocytoma multisystem crisis to highlight the diagnostic pitfalls, pathophysiological cascade, and the dilemma of extracorporeal life support.Case presentationA 13-year-8-month-old male presented with a 3-day history of abdominal pain and poor responsiveness. Initial treatment for acute gastroenteritis was ineffective, and his condition rapidly deteriorated into cardiogenic shock and multiple organ failure, requiring mechanical ventilation and veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) prior to transfer to our hospital. The clinical course was characterized by sustained abdominal pain as the initial manifestation, absence of typical palpitations or headache, transient hypertension (175/109 mmHg) followed by circulatory collapse, and physical findings of a sedated state, mottled skin with extremity ecchymoses, muffled heart sounds, absent bowel sounds, and prolonged capillary refill time of 5 s. Bedside echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction of only 26%. Abdominal computed tomography and ultrasonography demonstrated a left adrenal mass. Plasma normetanephrine was markedly elevated at > 20.56 nmol/L (normal ≤ 0.9 nmol/L) and 3-methoxytyramine at 5.47 nmol/L (normal < 0.18 nmol/L), confirming the diagnosis of pheochromocytoma. VA-ECMO and mechanical ventilation were maintained. Multiple sessions of plasma exchange and continuous renal replacement therapy were instituted, alongside vasoactive agents. Surgical resection was planned but never performed due to progressive multiorgan deterioration. Despite 6 days of ECMO support and comprehensive therapy, the patient developed hepatic encephalopathy and digital dry gangrene. The family elected to withdraw care, and the patient died shortly after discharge.ConclusionsThis case illustrates that pheochromocytoma can present with abdominal pain as the sole initial symptom in adolescents, mimicking acute gastroenteritis. The rapid progression from catecholamine storm to irreversible multiorgan failure underscores the critical importance of early recognition of atypical presentations. While VA-ECMO may serve as a bridge to diagnosis and stabilization, it cannot reverse established multiorgan injury when timely surgical source control is not achieved.

Introduction

Pheochromocytoma (PCC) is a neuroendocrine tumor originating from chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla, which continuously or intermittently secretes large amounts of catecholamines, affecting the cardiovascular, digestive, nervous, and other systems with diverse clinical manifestations. The incidence of PCC in children is low, accounting for only 10%–20% of all PCC cases [1], and most pediatric patients present with typical symptoms such as hypertension, headache, and palpitations [1, 2]. Presentation with abdominal pain as the sole initial manifestation, rapidly progressing to catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy, cardiogenic shock, and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), has rarely been reported. Herein, we report a case of an adolescent with pheochromocytoma multisystem crisis (PMC) who presented with abdominal pain, was initially misdiagnosed as gastroenteritis, and was ultimately diagnosed with the support of veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO). This report aims to analyze the clinical characteristics and diagnostic and therapeutic lessons of this case, explore the value and limitations of ECMO in this condition, and raise clinical awareness of atypical pheochromocytoma in children.

Case presentation

A 13-year-8-month-old male was admitted with a 3-day history of abdominal pain and poor responsiveness. Three days prior to admission, the patient developed abdominal pain in the morning without an obvious trigger. He was treated with oral medications and intravenous fluids for “acute gastroenteritis” at a local clinic, but his condition progressively worsened, with the development of profuse sweating and poor responsiveness. He was subsequently referred to a local people’s hospital. During the course of illness, he experienced a transient hypertensive episode with blood pressure reaching 175/109 mmHg. Abdominal ultrasonography performed at the referring hospital revealed a hypoechoic mass in the left adrenal region, and myocardial enzymes were markedly elevated. Following transfer to a tertiary children’s hospital, his condition rapidly deteriorated, manifesting as cardiogenic shock (hypotension, severe metabolic acidosis, and respiratory failure). Echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of only 26%. Despite treatment with inotropic agents, plasma exchange, and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), his circulation could not be maintained. The extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) team from our hospital was deployed, and veno-arterial ECMO (VA-ECMO) was initiated at the referring hospital before transfer to our institution.

The patient had a one-year history of unexplained polydipsia, polyuria, weight loss, and reduced exercise tolerance, which had not received medical attention. There was no family history of hereditary diseases.

Multidisciplinary team consultation

On hospital day 3, a hospital-wide multidisciplinary team (MDT) consultation was convened, with the participation of specialists from radiology, medical oncology, gastroenterology, pediatric intensive care, surgical oncology, ophthalmology, endocrine genetics and metabolism, nephrology, and cardiology. The MDT consensus was as follows: the diagnosis of a left adrenal mass was unequivocal, and in conjunction with the markedly elevated plasma normetanephrine result returned on hospital day 4, the diagnosis of pheochromocytoma was confirmed. The patient’s multiple organ failure was attributable to pheochromocytoma crisis. Aggressive organ function support was recommended, with elective surgical resection of the tumor planned once the patient’s condition stabilized. Despite aggressive treatment including ECMO, plasma exchange, and CRRT, the patient’s condition continued to deteriorate, and surgical intervention was never performed.

Outcome and follow-up

Despite 6 days of aggressive multimodal treatment, including VA-ECMO support, the patient’s multiple organ failure continued to deteriorate progressively. After thorough discussion with the family, they elected to withdraw life-sustaining care. The patient was subsequently discharged against medical advice and died shortly thereafter due to refractory circulatory failure.

Discussion

Diagnostic delay and misdiagnosis analysis: pediatric pheochromocytoma presenting with abdominal pain as the sole initial manifestation

PCC typically presents with the classic features such as hypertension, headache, and palpitations. However, presentation with abdominal pain as the sole initial manifestation is uncommon. Diagnostic delays in PCC and paraganglioma (PPGL) are frequently encountered in clinical practice. Lenders et al. [3] reported a median diagnostic delay of approximately 3 years for PPGL, and autopsy studies suggest that roughly 0.05% of cases remain undiagnosed during life —highlighting the vulnerability of atypical PPGL to being overlooked. Ma et al. [4] noted that the intermittent nature of symptoms often contributes to diagnostic delay. Stachowicz-Stencel et al., in their review of pediatric PPGL, highlighted that mass effect-related symptoms such as abdominal pain and weight loss may be present in some pediatric patients, and clinicians should not readily exclude PPGL even in the absence of typical cardiovascular manifestations [5]. In the present case, the patient initially presented with abdominal pain and vomiting but no classic cardiovascular complaints, leading to a misdiagnosed of acute gastroenteritis. The likely mechanism involves catecholamine-driven visceral vasospasm, which induces ischemia and edema of the intestinal wall, resulting in abdominal pain, intestinal dilatation, and even a “pseudo-acute abdomen.” Ultrasonography performed at the referring hospital revealed bowel wall thickening in the appendiceal region; however, the abdominal signs were mild, demonstrating a “symptom-sign dissociation” phenomenon that is inconsistent with a primary acute abdomen. During the disease course, a transient episode of hypertension (175/109 mmHg) and a one-year history of polydipsia, polyuria, and weight loss were all important diagnostic clues that were not integrated in a timely manner. The diagnosis of pheochromocytoma was only considered after the transient hypertensive episode and the imaging finding of an adrenal mass. Notably, the hypermetabolic manifestations were an important diagnostic clue, related to the metabolic depletion state caused by excessive catecholamine secretion. This case reminds clinicians that pheochromocytoma should be included in the differential diagnosis for pediatric patients presenting with abdominal pain accompanied by abnormal blood pressure or hypermetabolic manifestations, and that one should not be satisfied with a purely localized gastrointestinal explanation. Functional imaging techniques such as ¹²³I-MIBG scintigraphy and ⁶⁸Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT can be valuable for detecting metastatic or multifocal disease in pediatric PPGL [6], although the fulminant course in this case precluded their use.

The waterfall-like evolution of the disease: From catecholamine crisis to irreversible end-organ damage

In the present case, the progression from abdominal pain to multiple organ failure occurred over just 3 days, illustrating the classic cascade of pheochromocytoma multisystem crisis (PMC). PMC is characterized by extreme blood pressure fluctuations and multiple organ failure, with the pathological core involving progressive target organ injury driven by massive catecholamine release [7]. Once organ damage surpasses a critical threshold, it may become irreversible even if catecholamine secretion is subsequently halted.Myocardial injury: Catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy in PPGL (CICMPP) has a prevalence of 8%–11% among PPGL patients and can manifest as dilated cardiomyopathy, Takotsubo cardiomyopathy (TCM), or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [8, 9]. In a systematic review by Aw et al [10], the clinical course of atypical TCM subtypes in PCC-related TCM was significantly more severe, with a markedly higher incidence of cardiogenic shock compared with typical TCM. Acute catecholamine stress leads to excessive β-adrenergic receptor activation, causing left ventricular myocardial stunning, whereas chronic catecholamine exposure results in myocardial fibrosis, inflammation, and necrosis, ultimately progressing to dilated cardiomyopathy with reduced ejection fraction [9]. In this case, the patient’s cardiac ultrasound findings and myocardial enzyme profile were both consistent with CICMPP. Multiple organ injury: Massive catecholamine release induces sustained systemic arteriolar vasospasm, tissue ischemia and hypoxia, and increased microvascular permeability, resulting in simultaneous damage to the liver, kidneys, coagulation system, and extremities. The 2024 PPGL published in Nature Reviews Endocrinology noted that up to 70%–80% of childhood PPGL are hereditary, with a higher incidence of metastatic and/or multifocal disease compared with adults [1]. In this case, the extreme elevation in liver enzymes, biliary-enzyme dissociation, hyperammonemia, coagulopathy, and digital dry gangrene suggest that the catecholamine storm had caused extensive endothelial injury and microcirculatory failure, potentially indicating progression to an irreversible stage.Transition from hypertensive crisis to shock: In the later stages of PMC, tumor hemorrhage and necrosis may lead to an abrupt cessation of catecholamine release. This, combined with a sharp decline in cardiac output secondary to heart failure and volume depletion from capillary leakage, together drives a rapid transition from hypertensive crisis to refractory cardiogenic shock. Notably, the underlying etiology remained unclear at the time of ECMO initiation; the diagnosis of pheochromocytoma crisis was only confirmed during the course of mechanical support. Despite maximal therapy, the patient developed irreversible multi-organ failure, including brain injury. Subsequently, after a difficult decision-making process, the family opted for withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy. Among PPGL-associated cardiomyopathies, patients with a chronic disease course have a higher in-hospital mortality rate, a higher incidence of cardiogenic shock, and a lower rate of postoperative left ventricular recovery compared with those with an acute course, underscoring the critical importance of early diagnosis [9]. In this case, the nearly one-year history of hypermetabolic symptoms strongly points to pre-existing chronic catecholamine-induced myocardial injury, which likely constituted a major reason for the persistent failure of cardiac function to recover.

Value and limitations of ECMO in pheochromocytoma crisis: reflections on therapeutic decision-making

Indications and timing of ECMO initiation

Veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) has become an important mechanical circulatory support modality for refractory cardiogenic shock. The hemodynamic characteristics of pheochromocytoma crisis are extremely complex. Wernhart et al. [11] used invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring to visually document cyclical blood pressure fluctuations in a patient, with systolic pressure oscillating between 45 mmHg and 310 mmHg every 15 min, reflecting the alternation between paroxysmal massive catecholamine release and depletion. This extreme blood pressure instability poses a significant challenge for the precise titration of vasoactive agents, with some patients alternating between shock and hypertensive crisis, making it difficult to achieve circulatory stability. Against this background, the main indications for ECMO include:refractory shock that cannot be maintained despite adequate fluid resuscitation and high-dose vasoactive agents;severe cardiac dysfunction with tissue hypoperfusion caused by catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy [12]; andas a bridge therapy [13] to allow time for medications to take effect, for the diagnosis to be clarified, or for surgical preparation [7, 14]. In the present case, the initiation of VA-ECMO at an external hospital for circulatory failure was justified; the indication was considered appropriate.

Clinical dilemmas during ECMO support

Failure to address the primary etiology: Surgical resection remains the only curative treatment for pheochromocytoma. According to the 2020 Chinese Expert Consensus, patients experiencing a pheochromocytoma crisis should first achieve hemodynamic stabilization, followed by elective surgery once stable [15]. In this case, the patient never met criteria for surgery. Consequently, ECMO-initially planned as a "bridge to surgery," ultimately became a "bridge to nowhere." This highlights a central dilemma: surgical manipulation during the crisis phase may provoke massive catecholamine release, potentially leading to catastrophic intraoperative hemodynamic collapse. For patients with medically refractory pheochromocytoma crises, should emergency surgery be performed under ECMO support? Preoperative medical optimization is essential for safely performing surgical resection [1]. The cornerstone of preoperative preparation consists of α-adrenoceptor blockers combined with volume expansion. In refractory catecholamine excess, metyrosine (a tyrosine hydroxylase inhibitor) can be added to reduce catecholamine synthesis. Unfortunately, due to the patient’s rapid progression to irreversible multiple organ failure, surgical conditions were never achieved, and preoperative pharmacological preparation could not be implemented. This further underscores the critical importance of early recognition and timely intervention.

Lessons from the negative outcome

Earlier recognition of pheochromocytoma might have permitted elective tumor resection, thereby preventing progression to an irreversible crisis. This adverse outcome highlights a critical limitation in the management of pheochromocytoma crisis: prognosis is primarily determined by the timeliness of diagnosis and targeted etiological intervention rather than by the sophistication of mechanical support. Notably, patients with refractory shock due to non-catecholamine etiologies—such as drug-induced multi-organ failure—have achieved survival with prompt comprehensive treatment alone, even in the absence of ECMO [17]. This contrast further corroborates the above conclusion.

In summary, veno-arterial ECMO (VA-ECMO) serves as an appropriate rescue bridge, providing a critical time window for diagnostic workup and multidisciplinary team (MDT) evaluation. Nevertheless, once irreversible organ injury has occurred, it cannot alter the final outcome.

Clinical alert: the identification pathway from atypical symptoms to fatal crisis and lessons learned

This case illustrates a distinct warning clue chain: hypermetabolic symptoms progressing to abdominal pain (driven by visceral vasospasm), followed by transient hypertension, cardiogenic shock, and ultimately multiple organ failure. At any point along this pathophysiological pathway, early recognition and timely intervention could have potentially altered the prognosis. Primary care pediatricians and general practitioners should maintain a high index of suspicion and include routine blood pressure measurement as well as adrenal imaging in the evaluation of such patients [1].The diagnosis and management of PPGL crisis is a complex process that requires multidisciplinary involvement. In critical and complex cases, the multidisciplinary team (MDT) approach plays an irreplaceable role. The prognosis of PPGL largely depends on early recognition and effective multidisciplinary collaboration; any delay at a single step may result in the loss of an opportunity for curative treatment [2].Although ECMO can provide a critical time window for diagnosis and subsequent treatment, it cannot replace etiological management. Genetic syndromes are more common in children with PPGL than in adults. Up to 80% of pediatric cases are hereditary and are associated with familial disorders such as von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease, multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN2), neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), and familial paraganglioma syndromes. Patients with these associated familial conditions should undergo targeted pathogenic variant testing for the causal genes [18, 19]. Accordingly, a brief genetic evaluation and counseling are recommended in the management of pediatric PPGL.

Limitations of this study

The patient did not undergo surgery and ultimately died after withdrawal of care; therefore, pathological confirmation of the diagnosis was not obtained. Genetic testing was also not performed. Notably, 70%–80% of PPGL cases in childhood are hereditary [1]. It is strongly recommended that genetic testing for PPGL-associated pathogenic genes be conducted in such patients, as well as in their first-degree relatives, to guide familial screening, management, and long-term follow-up.
