Combined oral contraceptive use as a cause of acute myocardial infarction in a young woman without traditional risk factors: a case report

Introduction

Combined oral contraceptives(COCs), containing estrogen and progestagen, are among the most widely used birth control methods globally, with a failure rate of less than 1 per 100 women per year when used correctly [1].

Despite their effectiveness, COCs have been linked to increased risk of arterial and venous thrombosis [2]. Arterial thrombosis can lead to serious events, such as myocardial infarction (MI) or stroke [1]. Although newer low-estrogen formulations may reduce this risk, the exact incidence of COC-related arterial thrombosis remains uncertain [3]. MI is rare among COC users, particularly in those without traditional cardiovascular risk factors, such as smoking, hypertension, or hyperlipidemia [4].

Here, we report the case of a 28-year-old woman without traditional cardiovascular risk factors who developed anterior wall MI after 13 years of continuous use of a second-generation COC.

Case presentation

On June 30, 2023, a 28-year-old non-smoking Iranian woman presented to the emergency department of our specialized cardiac center with 11 h of persistent, pressure-like chest pain localized to the left hemithorax and radiating to the left arm and interscapular region. She also reported dyspnea, nausea, and vomiting and denied fever, chills, or prior similar episodes. Her medical and family history were unremarkable, with no known cardiovascular risk factors. The patient was married, denied tobacco, alcohol, or illicit drug use, and had been using a second-generation COC containing 150 µg levonorgestrel and 30 µg ethinyl estradiol for 13 years, following a 21-day intake and 7-day pill-free regimen.

On examination, she was hemodynamically stable (heart rate: 81 bpm, blood pressure: 132/70 mmHg, respiratory rate: 20/min, oxygen saturation: 97% on room air, body max index: 25.88 kg/m2). Cardiac exam revealed normal S1 and S2 sounds, without any murmurs, rubs, or gallops. Pulmonary examination was unremarkable. Electrocardiogram (ECG) revealed ST-segment elevations in leads V2–V5 (Fig. 1). creatine Kinase–MB (CK–MB) was 86 IU/L (normal: 0–25), and troponin was weakly positive. Angiography revealed patent right coronary, left main, and left circumflex arteries. The mid-segment of the left anterior descending artery (LAD) had a thrombus with distal embolization and total occlusion and absence of distal flow (Fig. 2). A guidewire was easily advanced, and balloon angioplasty with a 2.0 × 12 mm balloon restored TIMI1 3 flow and resolved her symptoms (Fig. 2). No stent was deployed due to the patient’s young age, absence of traditional atherosclerotic risk factors, lack of angiographic evidence of underlying plaque or luminal irregularity, and restoration of optimal flow with minimal residual stenosis after plain old balloon angioplasty (POBA). As part of the etiological workup for myocardial infarction in a young patient without traditional cardiovascular risk factors, a comprehensive evaluation for inherited and acquired thrombophilia and other prothrombotic conditions was performed during follow-up. This assessment included measurements of protein C, protein S, and antithrombin III levels, testing for factor V Leiden mutation and prothrombin G20210A mutation, as well as screening for antiphospholipid antibodies and serum homocysteine levels. All results were within normal limits, and no evidence of an underlying hypercoagulable state was identified.

She was advised to discontinue oral contraceptives and enrolled in a cardiac rehabilitation program. At 3-month follow-up, Computed Tomography angiography showed a patent LAD with no stenosis. Echocardiography demonstrated improved LV systolic function (EF: 50–55%) with resolution of apical wall motion abnormalities.

Conclusions and discussion

While the risk of thrombotic complications from COCs is well-established (2), arterial thrombosis leading to MI remains rare, particularly in young, healthy women without cardiovascular risk factors [4]. This case adds to a small body of literature highlighting the potential for COC-induced arterial events in low-risk individuals.

COCs increase procoagulant factors, such as fibrinogen, prothrombin, and factors VII, VIII, and X, while moderately decreasing factor V. They also reduce natural anticoagulants, such as antithrombin and Tissue Factor Pathway Inhibitor (TFPI), further promoting thrombosis. Although they slightly elevate protein C levels, this effect is neutralized by increased levels of its inhibitors (α1-antitrypsin and α2-macroglobin) and a marked reduction in both total and free protein S [2].

Epidemiological data show that COC-associated MI risk, although low overall, increases substantially in women with cardiovascular risk factors. In healthy users, the absolute MI risk is about 1–2 per 10,000 women annually. However, it escalates sharply in smokers or those with hypertension, diabetes, or dyslipidemia [5–7]. Estrogen dose is a key determinant of arterial thrombotic risk in COC users. A Cochrane meta-analysis [1], demonstrated a clear dose–response relationship: COCs with 20 µg estrogen were associated with a relative risk (RR) of arterial thrombosis of 1.6 (95% CI 1.4–1.8), increasing to 2.0 (95% CI 1.4–3.0) for 30–49 µg and 2.4 (95% CI 1.8–3.3) for ≥ 50 µg. In contrast, while the type of progestin—especially later-generation agents, such as desogestrel and drospirenone—significantly affects the risk of venous thromboembolism, its effect on arterial outcomes such as myocardial infarction appears limited [1]. Recent evidence from a large nationwide prospective cohort study in Denmark (1996–2021) involving over 2 million women aged 15–49 years provides updated real-world incidence estimates for arterial thrombosis associated with contemporary modern low-dose combined oral contraceptives (COCs) [8]. In this study, the absolute risks among COC users were low, with standardized incidence rates of 39 per 100,000 person-years for ischemic stroke and 18 for myocardial infarction, compared to 18 and 8, respectively, among non-users. The adjusted incidence rate ratios were 2.0 (95% CI 1.9–2.2) for ischemic stroke and 2.0 (1.7–2.2) for myocardial infarction, corresponding to approximately 21 additional strokes and 10 additional myocardial infarctions per 100,000 person-years of use. Risks were modestly lower for formulations containing 20 µg ethinyl estradiol (adjusted RR 1.8–1.9 for stroke, 1.6 for MI) compared to 30–40 µg (RR 2.0 for stroke, 2.1 for MI), with no consistent differences attributable to progestin type. Notably, these elevated risks remained stable with increasing duration of use and were similar across age groups, even after adjusting for factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and smoking in subgroup analyses. Duration of use appears to affect venous but not arterial thrombosis, with arterial risk tied more closely to estrogen dose and pre-existing conditions, including hypertension or smoking [9].

In comparison with previous reports of COC-associated MI, our patient’s estrogen dose (30 µg) was similar to that in most cases (e.g., Rahhal et al., Aslan et al.) [4, 10], though some involved lower doses (20 µg) [11]. Unlike the majority of cases, which involved third- or fourth-generation COCs with progestins, such as desogestrel, norgestimate, or drospirenone (e.g., Koza et al., Braiteh et al.) [12, 13], our patient had been using a second-generation formulation with levonorgestrel, as reported by Cabou et al. [14]. In addition, the duration of use in our case (13 years) exceeded that in most prior reports. [4, 10, 12–14]. While many previously described patients were older than 35 years [12, 13, 15], our patient was 28—a relevant factor given that advancing age is itself a known risk for thrombosis [16]. The anterior MI observed here also mirrors most cases [4, 10, 15], whereas fewer reports have documented inferior [13] or lateral [14] infarctions. The absence of cardiovascular risk factors in our patient contrasts with prior cases, which often involved smoking, dyslipidemia, diabetes, or hypertension [11–14].

Although the exact mechanism by which oral contraceptives induce MI remains unclear, current evidence suggests a predominantly thrombotic rather than atherosclerotic etiology, likely driven by their prothrombotic effects [16]. In our case, angiography revealed thrombotic occlusion without plaque rupture or signs of atherosclerosis, supporting this mechanism. Given the lack of angiographic plaque, and successful reperfusion with minimal residual stenosis, balloon angioplasty without stenting was preferred to avoid long-term risks, such as restenosis or stent thrombosis. Oral contraceptives were discontinued, and antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy was initiated to prevent further thrombosis and support endogenous thrombus resolution.

Management followed European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines [17], including dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and ticagrelor for 1 year, along with strong counseling to discontinue COCs. Follow-up imaging confirmed thrombus resolution and improved LV function, supporting the chosen strategy.

This report describes a single case, and therefore, causality between combined oral contraceptive use and myocardial infarction cannot be definitively established.

In summary, this case illustrates that even in the absence of conventional risk factors, prolonged use of higher-estrogen COCs may precipitate thrombotic MI. Clinicians should consider this risk when prescribing hormonal contraceptives, particularly in long-term users, and carefully assess individual thrombotic profiles. The success of balloon angioplasty without stenting further highlights the importance of tailored management strategies in young patients with non-atherosclerotic, thrombosis-related MI.
