When is the Ellis semigroup a complete conjugacy invariant? Thanks: This work was supported by the EPSRC grant numbers EP/V007459/2. Reem Yassawi also thanks the CNRS for financial support for a longer visit.
Johannes Kellendonk, Reem Yassawi

Abstract
. The Ellis semigroup of a topological dynamical system contains algebraic, topological and dynamical information. It is invariant under conjugacy. Despite this wealth of structure, two non-conjugate dynamical systems can have the same Ellis semigroup. We identify a class of minimal dynamical systems inside which this cannot happen, that is, for which the Ellis semigroup is a complete conjugacy invariant.

37B02, 37B10, 37B52, 20M10, 20M35.

1.
Introduction

Let
T
be an abelian group acting on a compact Hausdorff space
X
by a continuous action
\sigma
. The
Ellis semigroup
E(X)=E(X,\sigma,T)
is the compactification of the action with the topology of pointwise convergence.
It is a right-topological monoid with an action
\sigma_{E}
of
T
given by left multiplication by
\sigma
. An
Ellis morphism
is a factor map

\Phi:(E(X),\sigma_{E},T)\rightarrow(E(X^{\prime}),\sigma^{\prime}_{E},T)

which is a monoid morphism.
Any factor map
\phi:(X,\sigma,T)\rightarrow(X^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},T)
between the dynamical systems induces an Ellis morphism. This suggests the question:
When is an Ellis morphism induced by
a factor map?
We identify in Theorem
3.5
a class of topological dynamical systems for which this is always the case.

Related to this question is the following: When is an algebraic isomorphism between two Ellis semigroups an Ellis morphism? Indeed, this work was motivated by the fact that the Thue-Morse shift and the
Rudin-Shapiro shift have algebraically isomorphic Ellis semigroups, but that the shifts are not conjugate. A direct check that the algebraic isomorphism is continuous seems out of reach, since the Ellis semigroups are not tame and have very complicated topologies. As the class of dynamical systems for which the Ellis semigroup is a complete invariant contains the two shifts, our results show that the algebraic isomorphism between the Ellis semigroups of the Thue-Morse and the Rudin-Shapiro shift cannot be continuous. To our knowledge, these questions have not been addressed in the literature.

We focus in this work on dynamical systems
(X,\sigma,T)
for which
X
is compact Hausdorff,
T
is abelian, and the action
\sigma
is minimal and not distal.
We include a section with examples of systems which satisfy at least some conditions of the class of systems to which our results can be applied.
In the second half of the paper we describe how to compute the algebraic structure of the Ellis semigroup for quasi-bijective primitive substitutions of constant length. This is a generalisation of earlier work on bijective substitutions
[
18
]
, and is required in order to describe the Ellis semigroup of the Rudin-Shapiro shift.

Let us give a rough overview of the strategy we use to extract from an
Ellis morphism
\Phi:E(X)\to E(X^{\prime})
a factor map
\phi:X\to X^{\prime}
such that
\Phi=\phi_{*}
(see Theorem
3.5
).
To exploit minimality, we restrict
\Phi
to a minimal left ideal
L
of
E(X)
, as
(L,\sigma_{L})
is a minimal component of
(E(X),\sigma_{E})
, and
L^{\prime}=\Phi(L)
then defines a minimal component of
(E(X^{\prime}),\sigma^{\prime}_{E})
.
Any factor map
\phi:(X,\sigma,T)\rightarrow(X^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},T)
between minimal systems is uniquely determined by its value at one point. In other words, given
x\in X
and
x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
there is at most one factor map
\phi
such that
\phi(x)=x^{\prime}
. Our aim is thus to obtain two such points
x
and
x^{\prime}
from
\Phi
.

Recall that
\phi
induces a factor map
\phi_{eq}:(X_{eq},\delta,T)\rightarrow(X^{\prime}_{eq},\delta^{\prime},T)
between the maximal equicontinuous factors of the systems.
If the system has a proximal pair (a pair of points which become arbitrarily close under the action of
T
), then there is a point
\xi\in X_{eq}
whose fibre
\pi_{eq}^{-1}(\xi)
under the factor map
\pi_{eq}:X\to X_{eq}
contains this pair. We call such a
\xi
a singular point. As factor maps preserve proximality, we see that if
\phi
is a conjugacy then

\phi_{eq}
identifies the singular points of
X_{eq}
with those of
X^{\prime}_{eq}
.
To take advantage of this singular structure we make two essential assumptions. The first, which we call
no extra spectrum
(NES), implies that the maximal equicontinuous factor of
(L,\sigma_{L})
is not larger than
X_{eq}
(which in terms of continuous dynamical spectra means that the spectrum of
(L,\sigma_{L})
equals that of
(X,\sigma)
), and the second is that
\Phi_{eq}
maps non-singular points to non-singular points. This latter condition is difficult to characterise in general, but we provide a sufficient criterion for it, namely that the singular points of
X_{eq}
form a single orbit under the automorphism group of
(X,T)
. In this case we say that the system is a
unique singular fibre
system (USF).

Finally we need to transfer the information related to the image of points
x
in singular fibres under the action of minimal idempotents of
L
. Namely, we need each such point to separate the idempotents of
L
. Again this is difficult to guarantee in general, but if the automorphism groups are sufficiently rich, this is satisfied.

We summarise the contents of this paper. In Section
2
, we set up notation, define some notions and describe the equicontinuous relation structure of minimal ideals of
E(X)
. In Section
3
we discuss when an Ellis morphism is induced by a factor map, and prove our main result, Theorem
3.5
. In Section
4
, we give classes of systems which will satisfy the conditions of Theorem
3.5
. In Section
5
, we discuss several examples, making use of the results in Section
6
, where we give a complete algebraic description of the Ellis semigroup of quasi-bijective substitution shifts, needed for some of the examples in Section
5
. Finally for the benefit of the readers (and authors!) we give a non-exhaustive glossary of notation in Section
7
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Dynamical systems

Throughout this work,
T
is an abelian group.
We look at
T
-actions
(X,\sigma,T)
on a compact Hausdorff space
X
. If
T
is understood we write
(X,\sigma)
and if both
\sigma
and
T
are understood then we write
X
. If
X
is metrisable and
d
a metric on
X
which generates its topology, a pair of points
x,x^{\prime}\in X
are
proximal
if
\inf_{t\in T}d(\sigma^{t}(x),\sigma^{t}(x^{\prime}))=0
. This notion can also be formulated for Hausdorff compact spaces by using uniformity structures
[
3
]
.
An action is
distal
if the proximality relation is trivial in the sense that distinct points are never proximal. We make it a standing assumption that our actions are not distal.

A dynamical system
(X,\sigma)
is called
equicontinous
if the family of homeomorphisms

\{\sigma^{t}:t\in T\}
is equicontinuous. If the action is transitive then this is the case if and only if, for any choice of
x_{0}\in X
there is an abelian group structure on
X
(denoted additively) such that
x_{0}
is the identity element and
\sigma^{t}(x)=x+\sigma^{t}(x_{0})-x_{0}
. This group structure is topological.

An equicontinuous factor of
(X,\sigma)
is a factor
\pi:(X,\sigma)\to(Y,\delta)
such that
(Y,\delta)
is equicontinuous.
An equicontinuous factor of
(X,T)
is
maximal
if any other equicontinuous factor of
(X,T)
factors through it. Maximal equicontinuous factors (MEFs) always exist, and in this paper we will denote the maximal equicontinuous space by
X_{eq}
and the factor map by
\pi_{eq}
. Given
\xi\in X_{eq}
we call
\pi_{eq}^{-1}(\xi)
the fibre of
\xi
.
It turns out that two proximal points are mapped to the same point under

\pi_{eq}
, or stated differently, they belong to the same fibre, see, eg,
[
2
]
.

A point
\xi\in X_{eq}
is called
singular
if the fibre
\pi_{eq}^{-1}(\xi)
contains at least two proximal points, otherwise it is called regular. The assumption we make, that
(X,\sigma)
is not distal, is to say that there is at least one singular point in
X_{eq}
, and then of course, its orbit under
\delta
consists of singular points.
We choose for the group structure on
X_{eq}
a neutral element which is a singular point and denote it by
0
.

Any factor map
\phi:(X,\sigma,T)\rightarrow(X^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},T)
between minimal systems maps
\pi_{eq}
-fibres of
(X,\sigma,T)
to
\pi_{eq}
-fibres of
(X^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},T)
; this follows e.g. from
[
3
, Theorem 8, Chapter 9]
. It hence
induces a factor map
\phi_{eq}:(X_{eq},\delta_{X},T)\rightarrow(X^{\prime}_{eq},\delta_{X^{\prime}},T)
, namely
\phi_{eq}(\xi)=\pi_{eq}(\phi(x))-\pi_{eq}(x)
where
x
is any point of
\pi_{eq}^{-1}(\xi)
.

The
automorphism group

{\mathfrak{a}}(X,\sigma)
of a dynamical system
(X,\sigma)
is the group, under composition, of all homeomorphisms of
X
which commute with
\sigma
. Assuming minimality of

(X,\sigma)
we denote
{\mathfrak{a}}_{eq}:=\{\alpha_{eq}|\alpha\in{\mathfrak{a}}\}
the corresponding automorphisms of
X_{eq}
. It can be seen as a subgroup of
X_{eq}
or as the quotient group

{\mathfrak{a}}/{\mathfrak{a}}^{fib}
where
{\mathfrak{a}}^{fib}\subset{\mathfrak{a}}
is the subgroup of
{\mathfrak{a}}
of elements which preserve the fibres.

Let
\pi_{eq}:(X,\sigma)\to(X_{eq},\delta)
be a maximal equicontinuous factor.
The
coincidence rank

cr(\xi)
of the fibre at
\xi\in X_{eq}
is the largest possible cardinality that a subset of
\pi_{eq}^{-1}(\xi)
can have which only contains pairwise non-proximal elements. By minimality,
cr(\xi)
does not depend on
\xi
and we denote it by
cr=cr(X)
.

2.2.
Matrix semigroups

No semigroup discussed in this work has a zero element.
A semigroup is called
completely simple
if it does not admit a proper bilateral ideal and contains an idempotent. This implies that the semigroup is a disjoint union of isomorphic groups. The way that multiplication of elements in different groups occurs is encoded in a matrix. This is the result of the
Rees-Suskevitch structure theorem which characterises completely simple semigroups as those which are isomorphic to
matrix semigroups

[
16
]
.

Matrix semigroups are defined as follows.
Let
G
be a group, let
I
and
\Lambda
be non-empty sets, and let

A=(a_{\lambda i})_{\lambda\in\Lambda,i\in I}
be a
\Lambda\times I
matrix whose entries lie in
G
. Then the
matrix semigroup

M[G;I,\Lambda;A]

is the set
I\times G\times\Lambda
together with the multiplication

(i,g,\lambda)(j,h,\mu)=(i,ga_{\lambda j}h,\mu).

The matrix
A
is called the
sandwich matrix
and the group
G
is called the
structure group
. The
little structure group
of
M[G;I,\Lambda;A]
is the subgroup
\Gamma
of
G
generated by the entries of the sandwich matrix
A
.

Example 2.1

(
\mathcal{M}_{2}
)
.

The simplest matrix semigroup which is not orthodox, meaning that its idempotents do not form a subsemigroup, is obtained when
I
and
\Lambda
contain two elements,
G=S_{2}
is the permutation group of two elements, and
A=\begin{pmatrix}e&e\\
e&\tau\end{pmatrix}
. Here
e
is the identity and
\tau
the transposition. This semigroup has
8
elements of which
4
are idempotents. We denote it by
\mathcal{M}_{2}
. Note that the little structure group of
\mathcal{M}_{2}
coincides with the structure group
S_{2}
.

2.3.
Ellis semigroup

A compact topological semigroup is a semigroup endowed with a compact topology, and
such that multiplication
(x,y)\rightarrow xy
is jointly continuous.
A compact right-topological semigroup is a semigroup endowed with a compact topology, and such that right multiplication is continuous.
Given a set
X
we let
{\mathcal{F}}(X)
denote the set of functions from
X
to itself with the topology of pointwise convergence. Composition gives it the structure of a right-topological semigroup. If
X
is compact then
{\mathcal{F}}(X)
is a compact right-topological semigroup. It is a compact topological semigroup if and only if
X
is finite.
Given a dynamical system
(X,\sigma,T)
, the family of homeomorphisms

\{\sigma^{t}|t\in T\}
is a subsemigroup of
\mathcal{F}(X)
.
The closure of
\{\sigma^{t}|t\in T\}
, denoted
E(X,\sigma,T)
, or simply
E(X)
if the rest is understood, is still a semigroup, called the
Ellis semigroup
of the dynamical system.
Since
X
is compact

E(X,\sigma,T)
is
a compact right-topological semigroup, by construction. It is also a dynamical system, with the action
\sigma_{E}:E(X)\rightarrow E(X)
defined by
\sigma_{E}^{t}(f):=\sigma^{t}\circ f
. The system
(E(X),\sigma,T)
is generally not minimal which is one of the reasons why we will work with a minimal left ideal
L
of
E(X)
.

The Ellis semigroup is closely related to the proximality relation
[
3
, Chapter 3, Proposition 8]
.
Two points
x
and
y
are proximal if and only if there exists
f\in E(X)
such that
f(x)=f(y)
.
In particular we see that, given any idempotent
p\in E(X)
and
x\in X
, the points
p(x)
and
x
are proximal.

Since the elements of
E(X)
are limits of generalised sequences of powers of
\sigma
, the automorphism group, viewed as a subgroup of
{\mathcal{F}}(X)
, lies in the commutant of
E(X)
.

2.4.
Morphisms of Ellis semigroups

As an Ellis semigroup has an algebraic, a topological and a dynamical structure, the natural notion of a morphism between Ellis semigroups respects all the three.

Definition 2.2
.

An
Ellis morphism

\Phi:(E(X),\sigma_{E})\rightarrow(E(X^{\prime}),\sigma^{\prime}_{E})
between the Ellis semigroups of two
T
-actions
(X,\sigma,T)
and
(X^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},T)
is a topological factor map which is a monoid morphism. More precisely, it satisfies

(1)

\Phi:E(X)\rightarrow E(X^{\prime})
is continuous,

(2)

\Phi(fg)=\Phi(f)\Phi(g)
and
\Phi({\mathbf{1}}_{X})={\mathbf{1}}_{X^{\prime}}
,

(3)

\Phi(\sigma_{E}^{t}(f))={\sigma^{\prime}_{E}}^{t}\Phi(f)
for each
t\in T
.

If such a morphism exists, then it is unique and we simply say that
(X^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})
is related to
(X,\sigma)
by an Ellis morphism.
If
\Phi
only satisfies (2) and (3) we call it an
algebraic morphism
.

Any factor map
\phi:(X,\sigma)\to(X^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})
induces an Ellis morphism

\phi_{*}:E(X)\to E(Y)
via
\phi_{*}(f)(y)=\phi(f(x))
where
x
is any pre-image of
y
under
\phi
. The question we analyse here is the converse, namely when an Ellis morphism is of the form
\Phi=\phi_{*}
where
\phi
is a factor map.

Given an Ellis morphism
\Phi:E\to E^{\prime}
,
to exploit minimality we restrict it to a minimal left ideal
L
. For that we
choose a minimal idempotent
e\in E(X)
(
e
is minimal in the sense that if
ep=pe=p
for any other idempotent then
p=e
) and define
L=Ee
.
While this and the definitions below depend on the choice of
e
, they do so only up to isomorphism.

The minimal left ideal
L
is a compact subsemigroup and the action of
\sigma_{E}
restricted to
L
(which we denote by
\sigma_{L}
) is minimal
[
3
]
.
Furthermore
{\mathcal{G}}:=eL
is the Rees structure group of the minimal bilateral ideal (the
kernel
)
\ker E
of
E
and we call it here simply the structure group
1
1

1

In the literature, the maximal equicontinuous factor of
(X,\sigma)
is sometimes called the structure group. For almost automorphic systems our structure group reduces to that, but otherwise not.

of
E
or of the system. In general,
{\mathcal{G}}
is not closed in
L
. A further group of interest is the little structure group
\Gamma
. As defined in Section
2.2
, it is the group generated by the matrix elements of the sandwich matrix of the matrix semigroup corresponding to
\ker E
. It is also the intersection of the closure of the semigroup
\langle J_{min}(E)\rangle
generated by the minimal idempotents of
E
with
{\mathcal{G}}

[

]
.

We define the same object in
E^{\prime}
using
e^{\prime}=\Phi(e)
as the idempotent, that is, we set
L^{\prime}=E^{\prime}e^{\prime}
,
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}=e^{\prime}L^{\prime}
and the little structure group
\Gamma^{\prime}
as above. The following is straightforward, and can be found, eg, in

.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose that
\Phi:E(X^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})\rightarrow E(X,\sigma)
is an Ellis morphism. Then

maps
{\mathcal{G}}
onto
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}

2.5.
The fibre structure of
L
and
{\mathcal{G}}

Let
\mathrm{ev}_{x}
be evaluation of a function
f
at the point
x
. We will use the evaluation map
\mathrm{ev}_{x}
to evaluate functions
f
belonging to a variety of spaces; if needed, we will state the domain of
\mathrm{ev}_{x}
.
For a minimal equicontinuous system
(Y,\delta)
and w.r.t. the group structure on
Y
, whose neutral element we denote by
0
,

\mathrm{ev}_{0}:E(Y)\to Y
is an isomorphism of topological groups
[
3
, Chap. 3, Theorem 6]
(this hinges on our assumption that
T
is abelian).
Let
x\in\pi_{eq}^{-1}(0)
. By definition of the induced map
{\pi_{eq}}_{*}:E(X)\to E(X_{eq})
the diagram

(2.1)

\begin{matrix}L&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{ev}_{x}}}{{\to}}&X\\
{\pi_{eq}}_{*}\downarrow&&\downarrow\pi_{eq}\\
E(X_{eq})&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{ev}_{0}}}{{\to}}&X_{eq}\end{matrix}

commutes. Define

\widetilde{\pi_{eq}}:=\mathrm{ev}_{0}\circ{\pi_{eq}}_{*}:L\to X_{eq}.

Then we obtain the exact sequence of compact semigroups

L^{fib}\hookrightarrow L\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\widetilde{\pi_{eq}}}}{{\twoheadrightarrow}}X_{eq}

where
L^{fib}\subset L
is the sub-semigroup of elements
which preserve a
\pi_{eq}
-fibre, and hence all
\pi_{eq}
-fibres.
We can view an element
f\in L^{fib}
as a function on
X_{eq}

whose value at
\xi\in X_{eq}
is given by the restriction of
f
to
e\pi_{eq}^{-1}(\xi)
. As
L^{fib}
commutes with the automorphism group we find that
\tilde{f}
must satisfy the following so-called covariance condition

(2.2)

\alpha\tilde{f}(\xi)\alpha^{-1}=\tilde{f}(\alpha_{eq}(\xi)),\quad\forall\alpha\in{\mathfrak{a}}.

Let us define

(2.3)

Cov(\prod_{\xi\in X_{eq}}{\mathcal{F}}(e\pi_{eq}^{-1}(\xi))):=\{(\tilde{f}(\xi))_{\xi}:\tilde{f}(\xi)\in{\mathcal{F}}(e\pi_{eq}^{-1}(\xi)),\tilde{f}\>\mbox{ covariant}\}

which we equip with pointwise multiplication and the topology of pointwise convergence. We have an injective continuous semigroup morphism

\mu:L^{fib}\to Cov(\prod_{\xi\in X_{eq}}{\mathcal{F}}(e\pi_{eq}^{-1}(\xi))),
defined by

(2.4)

\mu(f)=\tilde{f},\quad\tilde{f}(\xi)(x)=f(x).

Note that
Cov(\prod_{\xi\in X_{eq}}{\mathcal{F}}(e\pi_{eq}^{-1}(\xi)))
is a compact right-topological group.

Define
{\mathcal{G}}^{fib}:=eL^{fib}={\mathcal{G}}\cap L^{fib}
, a subgroup of the semigroup
L^{fib}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

{\mathcal{G}}^{fib}
is a compact right-topological group. If the coincidence rank is finite, it is a topological group.

Proof.

\mu({\mathcal{G}}^{fib})
is the intersection of
\mu(L^{fib})
with
Cov(\prod_{\xi\in X_{eq}}{\mathcal{F}}(e\pi_{eq}^{-1}(\xi)))
,
two compact right-topological groups.
Moreover each
e\pi_{eq}^{-1}(\xi)
contains
cr

Cov(\prod_{\xi\in X_{eq}}{\mathcal{F}}(e\pi_{eq}^{-1}(\xi)))
is a compact topological semigroup if

is finite.
In that case
\mu({\mathcal{G}}^{fib})
and hence
{\mathcal{G}}^{fib}
are also compact topological groups.
∎

As the restriction of
\widetilde{\pi_{eq}}
to
{\mathcal{G}}
is still surjective onto
X_{eq}
, we obtain the exact sequence of groups

(2.5)

{\mathcal{G}}^{fib}\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{G}}\twoheadrightarrow X_{eq}.

Note that, although
{\mathcal{G}}^{fib}

are compact,

We denote the smallest normal subgroup of

\overline{\text{ncl}_{G}(H)}

\overline{\text{ncl}_{G}(H)}

.

(X,\sigma,T)

2.6.
The maximal equicontinuous factor of
(L,\sigma_{L})

We investigate the maximal equicontinuous factor
\pi_{eq}:L\to L_{eq}
of
(L,\sigma_{L})
. For better distinction we denote the neutral element of
L_{eq}
by
e_{eq}
and fix the group structure on
L_{eq}
such that
\pi_{eq}(e)=e_{eq}
.

Theorem 2.6
.

There is a closed normal subgroup
H
of
{\mathcal{G}}
which contains
\Gamma
such that the semigroup morphism

L\ni f\mapsto ef\>mod\>H\in{\mathcal{G}}/H

is a maximal equicontinuous factor of
(L,\sigma_{L})
.

Proof.

We first show that
\pi_{eq}(g)=e_{eq}
for all
g\in\Gamma
.
To show the claim, let
p\in J_{min}
and let
(\sigma^{t_{\nu}})
be a converging net such that
\lim{\sigma^{t_{\nu}}}=p
in the topology of
E(X)
.
Then
\lim{\sigma_{L}^{t_{\nu}}}=\lambda_{p}
, the left multiplication with
p
, in the topology of the Ellis semigroup
E(L)
of
L
. As
p
is an idempotent we have
\lambda_{p}f=\lambda_{p}pf
for any
f\in L
. This shows that
f
and
pf
are proximal in
(L,\sigma_{L})
for all
f\in L
.
As the equicontinuous structure relation of
(L,\sigma_{L})
contains the proximality relation
[
3
]
we find that
\pi_{eq}(pf)=\pi_{eq}(f)
for all
f\in L
. This implies that
\pi_{eq}(g)=\pi_{eq}(ge)=\pi_{eq}(e)=e_{eq}
for all
g\in\langle J_{min}\rangle e
.
As
\pi_{eq}
is continuous this extends to the closure of
\langle J_{min}\rangle e
in
L
and hence
\pi_{eq}(g)=e_{eq}
for all
g\in\Gamma
.

We claim that
\pi_{eq}
is multiplicative: Let
f,g\in L
. There is an idempotent
p\in L
such that
pg=g
. As
L
is closed and minimal there is a net
(t_{\nu})_{\nu}
such that
f=\lim\sigma^{t_{\nu}}p
. Thus

\pi_{eq}(fg)=\lim\pi_{eq}(\sigma^{t_{\nu}}pg)=\lim\sigma^{t_{\nu}}\pi_{eq}(pg)

where we used continuity and
T
-equivariance of
\pi_{eq}
.
Using
\pi_{eq}(pg)=e_{eq}\pi_{eq}(g)=\pi_{eq}(p)\pi_{eq}(g)
we obtain

\pi_{eq}(fg)=\lim\sigma^{t_{\nu}}\pi_{eq}(p)\pi_{eq}(g)=\pi_{eq}(f)\pi_{eq}(g).

The above shows that
\pi_{eq}(f)=\pi_{eq}(ef)
for all
f\in L
and that
the restriction of
\pi_{eq}
to
{\mathcal{G}}
is a continuous group epimorphism.
Thus there is a closed normal subgroup
H
of
{\mathcal{G}}
, which contains
\Gamma
, so that
L_{eq}\cong{\mathcal{G}}/H
, and the isomorphism is given by
\pi_{eq}(f)\mapsto ef
mod
H
.
∎

As
X_{eq}
is an equicontinuous factor of
L
, by universality of the maximal equicontinuous factor, there is a factor map
\eta_{0}
making the following diagram commute, where to avoid ambiguity, we add an upper index to

3.
When is an Ellis morphism induced by a factor map?

Recall that we fixed a minimal idempotent
e
in
E(X)
to define

, and moreover, if
(X^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})
is related to
(X,\sigma)
by an Ellis morphism
\Phi
we set
e^{\prime}=\Phi(e)
.
The evaluation map

\mathrm{ev}_{x}:L\to X
, given by
\mathrm{ev}_{x}(f)=f(x)
, is a factor map between
(L,\sigma_{L})
and
(X,\sigma)
. It defines a closed equivalence relation

(3.1)

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{ev}_{x}}=\{(f_{1},f_{2})\in L\times L:f_{1}(x)=f_{2}(x)\}.

There is the analogous relation (
3.1
) but defined for

.

Lemma 3.1
.

If

(X^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})
is related to
(X,\sigma)
by an Ellis morphism
\Phi
, then
there is a factor map
\phi:(X,\sigma)\to(X^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})
such that
\phi_{*}=\Phi
if and only if there are
x\in eX
,
x^{\prime}\in e^{\prime}X^{\prime}

such that
\Phi\times\Phi(R_{\mathrm{ev}_{x}})\subset R_{\mathrm{ev}_{x^{\prime}}}
.

Proof.

Assume that there are
x\in eX
,
x^{\prime}\in e^{\prime}X^{\prime}

such that
\Phi\times\Phi(R_{\mathrm{ev}_{x}})\subset R_{\mathrm{ev}_{x^{\prime}}}
, i.e., if
f(x)=f^{\prime}(x)
, then
\Phi(f(x))=\Phi(f^{\prime}(x))
. Given
y\in X
, all elements of the preimage
\mathrm{ev}_{x}^{-1}(y)
belong to the same
R_{\mathrm{ev}_{x}}
-class. Therefore

\phi(y):=\mathrm{ev}_{x^{\prime}}(\Phi(\mathrm{ev}_{x}^{-1}(y))
is well defined, and
the following diagram commutes:

\begin{matrix}L&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Phi}}{{\to}}&L^{\prime}\\
\mathrm{ev}_{x}\downarrow&&\downarrow\mathrm{ev}_{x^{\prime}}\\
X&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phi}}{{\to}}&X^{\prime}\end{matrix}

Applied to
e\in L
and
x\in eX
, the diagram tells us that
\phi(x)=x^{\prime}
. Since
\mathrm{ev}_{x}
,
\Phi
and
\mathrm{ev}_{x^{\prime}}
are factor maps,
\phi
is also a factor map, where its continuity follows from the continuity of
\Phi
. By minimality of the actions
\phi
is the unique factor map satisfying the above.

We now show that
\Phi=\phi_{*}
, that is,
\Phi(f)(y^{\prime})=\phi(f(y))
for all
f\in E
,
y^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
and
y\in\phi^{-1}(y^{\prime})
. The commuting diagram tells us that this equation is satisfied for all
f\in L
if
y^{\prime}=x^{\prime}
and
y=x
. Let
f\in E
,
y^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
and
y\in\phi^{-1}(y^{\prime})
. By minimality of
\sigma
there is
f_{2}\in L
such that
f_{2}(x)=y
. Then

\Phi(f)(y^{\prime})=\Phi(f)(\phi(y))=\Phi(f)(\phi(f_{2}(x)))=\Phi(f)\Phi(f_{2})(x^{\prime})=\Phi(ff_{2})(x^{\prime})=\phi(ff_{2}(x))=\phi(f(y))
.

As for the converse, suppose that
\phi:X\to X^{\prime}
is a factor map and choose a minimal idempotent
e\in E
. Let
e^{\prime}=\phi_{*}(e)
. Let
x\in eX
and set
x^{\prime}=\phi(x)
. Then
e^{\prime}(x^{\prime})=\phi_{*}(e)(x^{\prime})=\phi(ex)=x^{\prime}
so that
x^{\prime}\in e^{\prime}X^{\prime}
.
Let
(f_{1},f_{2})\in R_{\mathrm{ev}_{x}}
, i.e.
f_{1}(x)=f_{2}(x)
. As
\phi_{*}(f_{1})(x^{\prime})=\phi(f_{1}(x))
we find

\phi_{*}(f_{1})(x^{\prime})=\phi_{*}(f_{2})(x^{\prime})
.
∎

3.1.
Conspiracy among the idempotents of
L

Definition 3.2
.

Let
J_{L}
denote the idempotents of
L
. We say that a point
x\in X

separates the idempotents
of
L
if
\mathrm{ev}_{x}:J_{L}\to X
is injective.

Note that if the system is not equicontinuous, then a
point in a regular fibre cannot separate the idempotents of
L
, as any idempotent acts as the identity on that fibre. Therefore,
the only other option would be that
L
has a single idempotent. However in this case all fibres are regular and therefore the system is equicontinuous.

Recall that a semigroup is completely simple if it has no nontrivial bilateral ideal and contains an idempotent. A minimal left ideal of the Ellis semigroup is completely simple. In completely simple semigroups, every element
f
has a unique normal inverse, i.e., an element
f^{-1}
such that
ff^{-1}f=f
,
f^{-1}ff^{-1}=f^{-1}
and
ff^{-1}=f^{-1}f
.
Let
{\mathcal{R}}
denote Green’s right ideal relation, which for a completely simple semigroup means
(f_{1},f_{2})\in{\mathcal{R}}
if
f_{1}^{0}=f_{2}^{0}
, where

f^{0}=f^{-1}f
. Note that the idempotent
f^{0}
satisfies
f^{0}f=f
. We can refine the relation
R_{\mathrm{ev}_{x}}
defined in (
3.1
): for
\xi\in X_{eq}
, set

R^{\xi}_{\mathrm{ev}_{x}}(L)=\{(f_{1},f_{2})\in L\times L:(f_{1},f_{2})\in R_{\mathrm{ev}_{x}}\mbox{ and }{\pi_{eq}}_{*}(f_{i})=\xi,i=1,2\}.

Keep in mind that
\widetilde{\pi_{eq}}(f_{i})=\pi_{eq}(f_{i}(x))-\pi_{eq}(x)
for any
x
.
When no confusion arises we simply write
R^{\xi}_{\mathrm{ev}_{x}}
for
R^{\xi}_{\mathrm{ev}_{x}}(L)
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
\xi\in X_{eq}
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

Each point in
\pi_{eq}^{-1}(\xi)
separates the idempotents of
L
.

(2)

For each

,
R^{\xi-\pi_{eq}(x)}_{\mathrm{ev}_{x}}\subset{\mathcal{R}}
.

(3)

For each
x\in\pi_{eq}^{-1}(\xi)
,
R^{0}_{\mathrm{ev}_{x}}\subset{\mathcal{R}}

Proof.

To see that (1) implies (2),
let
x\in X
and
(f_{1},f_{2})\in R^{\xi-\pi_{eq}(x)}_{\mathrm{ev}_{x}}
. This means that

f_{2}^{0}f_{2}(x)=f_{2}(x)=f_{1}(x)=f_{1}^{0}f_{1}(x)=f_{1}^{0}f_{2}(x),

and
\pi_{eq}(f_{i}(x))=\xi
. Assume that all points of
\pi_{eq}^{-1}(\xi)

That (2) implies (3) is obvious.
Finally we show that (3) implies (1). Let

and

with

.
∎

Lemma 3.4

Let

Theorem 3.5
.

Consider two systems

(2)

We aim to show that

We consider first the case that

4.
USF systems

The assumptions of Theorem
3.5
are a bit elusive.
The purpose of this section is to present a class of systems which satisfies them. The key properties of these systems which make this possible are, that the singular points of the system can be controlled by the automorphism group and that the automorphism group coincides with the center of the structure group of the Ellis semigroup. The latter property is a stronger form of
semi-regularity
, a notion that was recently proposed by Auslander and Glasner
[
4
]
.

As above, we chose a minimal idempotent
e
of
E
and identify the structure group with
{\mathcal{G}}=eEe
.
The following does either not depend at all on the choice of
e
, or only up to isomorphism.

Definition 4.1
.

The system

(X,T)
is called a
unique singular fibre
(USF) system if
X_{eq}^{sing}={\mathfrak{a}}_{eq}(0)
, i.e.,
X_{eq}^{sing}
is a single orbit under
{\mathfrak{a}}_{eq}
. We call it
strongly
USF if

X_{0}:=e\pi_{eq}^{-1}(X_{eq}^{sing})
is a single orbit under
{\mathfrak{a}}
.

If
cr=1
then any USF system is strongly USF.
We denote by
{\mathfrak{a}}_{0}
the restriction of
{\mathfrak{a}}
to
X_{0}
. Note that the restriction map is an isomorphism as by minimality an automorphism acts fixed point freely on its orbits. We also mention that
{\mathcal{G}}^{fib}
is trivial if
cr=1
. So the following discussion is interesting only for systems with
cr>1
.

4.1.
The structure of
{\mathcal{G}}^{fib}
for USF systems

Recall that

are the elements of the Ellis semigroup which preserve the MEF fibres and that
{\mathcal{G}}^{fib}=E^{fib}\cap{\mathcal{G}}
. We denote by

to the set

, and we note that it is continuous.
We denote by

the restriction of
{\mathcal{G}}^{fib}
to

.
Likewise, we denote by
\Gamma_{\xi}
the restriction of the little structure group

to

Lemma 4.2

Each
{\mathcal{G}}^{fib}_{\xi}
is isomorphic to

Proof.

Let
g\in{\mathcal{G}}
such that
\widetilde{\pi_{eq}}(g)=\xi
. Then

maps

bijectively onto

Recall the definition of

It has the following subgroup

Cov(\prod_{\xi\in X_{eq}}{\mathcal{G}}^{fib}_{\xi}):=\{(\tilde{g}(\xi))_{\xi}:\tilde{g}(\xi)\in{\mathcal{G}}^{fib}_{\xi},g\>\>\mbox{is covariant}\}.

Restricting

If

As idempotents act trivially on regular points, so

Theorem 4.4
.

If

is a USF system, then the following are equivalent:

(1)

has no extra spectrum,

(2)

\overline{\text{ncl}_{{\mathcal{G}}^{fib}_{0}}(\Gamma_{0})}={\mathcal{G}}^{fib}_{0}

“

Suppose that

\overline{\text{ncl}_{{\mathcal{G}}}(\Gamma)}={\mathcal{G}}^{fib}

\overline{\text{ncl}_{{\mathcal{G}}}(\Gamma)}
to information about
\overline{\text{ncl}_{{\mathcal{G}}^{fib}_{0}}(\Gamma_{0})}

The elements of

are products of elements of the form

“

4.4.

5.
Examples

We provide examples to which our theory can be applied. This is to say that inside the class of these examples we can say that two systems are conjugate if and only if their Ellis semigroups are related by an Ellis isomorphism. As Ellis semigroups are usually more complicated than dynamical systems, the strength of our result does not so much lie in the fact that we can prove that two systems are conjugate or not by comparing their Ellis semigroups. But it is interesting to see that there are non-conjugate dynamical systems which have
algebraically
isomorphic Ellis semigroups. Our theory then tells us that the algebraic isomorphism cannot be continuous.

Many of our examples are substitutional dynamical systems. For a brief recap of these systems, see Section
6.2
.

5.1.
Rudi and Morse

The Thue-Morse substitution
a\mapsto ab
,
b\mapsto ba
and the
Rudin-Shapiro substitution
a\mapsto ac
,
b\mapsto dc
,
c\mapsto ab
,
d\mapsto db
define shift dynamical systems
(X_{RS},\sigma)
and
(X_{TM},\sigma)
which satisfy all
conditions of Corollary
4.10
. For, it can be verified that they have concidence rank
2
. By Lemma
6.1
, they are a USF system. Furthermore, each of their automorphism groups is a semidirect product of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
with
\mathbb{Z}
, so they are strongly USF. That they have no extra spectrum can be seen by Examples
6.8
and
6.9
, and Corollary
6.3
.

As we will see in Section
6
, their Ellis semigroups are algebraically isomorphic. However, as is well known, these two systems are not conjugate. Indeed, while the continuous component of the spectrum of the Thue-Morse system is purely singular, that of the Rudin-Shapiro system is purely absolute
[
6
]
. Alternatively, one can compute explicitly that there is no conjugacy between them, as in
[
11
]
. It therefore follows from Corollary
4.10
that the
algebraic isomorphism cannot be continuous.

5.2.
(\alpha,\kappa)
-Sturmian systems (2-cut Sturmian systems)

Consider three real numbers
\alpha
,
\kappa
,
\phi
. Taken modulo
\mathbb{Z}
we interpret
\alpha
and
\phi
as angles in
\mathbb{S}^{1}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
and call

\alpha
the
rotation
angle and
\phi
the
parameter
angle.
\kappa
is supposed to lie in
(0,1)
and is called the cut value. These systems have been studied in
[
19
]
.

An
(\alpha,\kappa)
-Sturmian sequence with parameter
\phi
is a two-sided sequence in
\{a,b\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
of the form
\omega_{\phi}^{+}
or
\omega_{\phi}^{-}
where

\omega_{\phi}^{+}(n)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\!\!a&\!\!\mbox{if }0\leq\{n\alpha+\phi\}<\kappa\\
\!\!b&\!\!\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.\!\!\!\!\!\!\mbox{ , and }\>\>\>\>\omega_{\phi}^{-}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\!\!a&\!\!\mbox{if }0<\{n\alpha+\phi\}\leq\kappa\\
\!\!b&\!\!\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

We look here only at the cases in which
\alpha
is irrational so that the sequences are aperiodic. Note that if
\{n\alpha+\phi\}\notin\{0,\kappa\}
then

\omega_{\phi}^{+}(n)=\omega_{\phi}^{-}(n)
. The set of these sequences form a closed shift invariant subspace
X_{\alpha,\kappa}
of
\{a,b\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
(with product topology) on which the shift acts minimally. The associated minimal dynamical system
(X_{\alpha,\kappa},\sigma)
has the following well-known properties:

(1)

Its MEF is
(S^{1},+\alpha)
with the Euclidean topology and factor map
\pi_{eq}:X_{\alpha,\kappa}\to S^{1}
is given by
\pi_{eq}(\omega_{\phi}^{\pm})=\phi
.
A point
\phi
is singular if
\omega_{\phi}^{+}(n)\neq\omega_{\phi}^{-}(n)
for some
n
. The latter happens if
n\alpha+\phi=0
or
n\alpha+\phi=\kappa
.
Hence a singular fibre contains two points whereas a regular fibre contains a single point.

(2)

If
\kappa\in\alpha\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}
, we say that
\kappa
is of type
1
, and then there is only one orbit of singular points and the system is strongly USF. Otherwise there are two orbits of singular points.

(3)

If
\kappa\in\frac{1}{2}(\alpha\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z})
, we say that
\kappa
is of type
2
, then the automorphism group
{\mathfrak{a}}
is generated by
\omega_{\phi}^{\pm}\mapsto\omega_{\phi+\kappa}^{\pm}
. In particular,

{\mathfrak{a}}_{eq}(0)=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z})
mod
\mathbb{Z}
and the system is strongly USF.

(4)

In all other cases we say that
\kappa
is of type
3
. There are then two orbits of singular points but
{\mathfrak{a}}=\mathbb{Z}
and the system is not USF.

(5)

If
\omega_{\phi}^{+}
and
\omega_{\phi}^{-}
are not equal they form a proximal pair.
Moreover, they differ only at one or two positions, i.e., they are
bi-asymptotic
. These are the only proximal pairs.

We now describe the Ellis semigroup. Given that the system is almost automorphic and all proximal pairs are asymptotic, the Ellis semigroup is
nearly simple

[
9
]
and must have the algebraic form

E(X_{\alpha,\kappa})\cong\mathbb{Z}\sqcup(LZ_{n}\times S^{1})

where
LZ_{n}
is the left zero semigroup of
n
elements, with
n
being the number of minimal idempotents, and the product is defined by

\sigma^{n}(p,z)=(p,z+\alpha),\quad(p,z)(q,w)=(p,z+w).

As there are at most 2 singular fibre orbits and a singular fibre has 2 points, there can be at most
n=4
idempotents. In particular, the system is tame, i.e., its Ellis semigroup has cardinality
|\mathbb{R}|
.

The calculation of the Ellis semigroup for Sturmian systems was discussed in
[
15

and generalised in
[
1
]
. A sequence
(\sigma^{n_{\nu}})_{\nu}
converges in the pointwise topology in the following three situations:

(1)

The sequence of integers
(n_{\nu})_{\nu}
is bounded and hence converges to an integer
n
. Then
\lim\sigma^{n_{\nu}}=\sigma^{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

(2)

The sequence of integers
(n_{\nu})_{\nu}
is unbounded but the sequence of angles
\alpha n_{\nu}\in S^{1}
converges to a point
z\in S^{1}
from above, that is, almost all
\alpha n_{\nu}\geq z
(w.r.t. the order in a small neighbourhood of

which is induced by
\mathbb{R}
). In this case
\lim\sigma^{n_{\nu}}=(p^{+},z)
where

is the idempotent given by
p^{+}(\omega_{\phi}^{\pm})=\omega^{+}_{\phi}
.

(3)

6.5.

Recall from Proposition

that the algebraic structure of the kernel
\mathcal{M}(X_{\theta})
of the Ellis semigroup is uniquely determined by
\mathcal{M}_{0}^{fib}(X_{\theta})
and

\mathbb{Z}_{\bar{\ell},h}

. We now compute
\mathcal{M}^{fib}_{0}(X_{\theta})
.

Recall that since
\theta
has length
\ell
there are maps
\theta_{i}:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
such that

\theta(a)=\theta_{0}(a)\cdots\theta_{\ell-1}(a)

for all

.

\theta
is thus uniquely determined by what we call its

, namely its representation as a concatenation of
\ell

\theta=\theta_{0}|\theta_{1}|\cdots|\theta_{\ell-1}.

We use this bar notation to separate the columns, or to distinguish the concatenation
\theta_{0}|\theta_{1}
from the composition
\theta_{0}\theta_{1}
.
It follows from (
6.3
) that
the composition of two substitutions
\theta
,
\theta^{\prime}
of length
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
over the same alphabet, which we simply denote by
\theta\theta^{\prime}

in particular, the expansion of

is given by

(6.5)

(\theta^{2})_{0}|\cdots|(\theta^{2})_{\ell^{2}-1}=\theta_{0}\theta_{0}|\cdots|\theta_{\ell-1}\theta_{0}|\theta_{0}\theta_{1}|\cdots|\theta_{\ell-1}\theta_{\ell-1}

and iteratively we find, for any given

\ell^{n}
maps

corresponding to the expansion of
\theta^{n}
.

The semigroup

associated to a constant length substitution

(\theta^{n})_{i}
,

for all

and

Given the semigroup

of a quasi-bijective substitution in simplified form, let

,

be the set of consecutive elements in
{\mathcal{H}}_{-+}\times{\mathcal{H}}_{+}

We call

be defined as in (

) with

and

Theorem 6.2
.

Let

\displaystyle f_{({\mathfrak{i}},g,-)}(a\cdot b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{i}}g\phi(a)\cdot g\phi(a).

Combined with Proposition

Let

be a primitive aperiodic quasi-bijective substitution in simplified form. Suppose that the normal closure of the little structure group

on

If

is smaller then similar results as for bijective substitutions can be obtained by generalising the approach of

The first step in proving Theorem

is to show that there are elements of

is multiplicative.
Direct verification shows that

Lemma 6.4
.

The set
S^{(2)}_{\theta}
is invariant under diagonal right multiplication with elements from
{\mathcal{H}}_{-+}\cup{\mathcal{H}}_{+}
. Furthermore, if

\phi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{+-}
then the map
(L,R)\mapsto(L\phi,R\phi)
is a bijection between
S^{(2)}_{\theta}
and the set
S^{(2,-)}_{\theta}
of consecutive elements
(L,R)
which lie in
{\mathcal{H}}_{-}\times{\mathcal{H}}_{+-}
.

Proof.

Let
(L,R)
consecutive elements in
{\mathcal{H}}_{-+}\times{\mathcal{H}}_{+}
. By definition there are
n>0
and
0<i<\ell^{n}
such that
L=\theta^{n}_{i-1}
and
R=\theta^{n}_{i}
. Let
\theta_{j}
be a column map. By construction
L\theta_{j}
and
R\theta_{j}
are consecutive. Iteratively we find that

L\phi
and
R\phi
are consecutive for any
\phi\in S_{\theta}
. Now if
\phi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{-+}\cup{\mathcal{H}}_{+}
then
L\phi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{-+}
and
R\phi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{+}
. On the other hand, if
\phi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{+-}
then
L\phi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{-}
and
R\phi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{+-}
. We thus see that then
(L,R)\mapsto(L\phi,R\phi)
maps

to

. Diagonal right multiplication with

yields an inverse to this map, indeed,
\phi e_{-}\phi^{-1}e_{+}=e_{+-}e_{+}=e_{+}
and

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
({\mathfrak{i}},g)\in I_{\theta}\times G_{\theta}
.
Then
\mathcal{M}^{fib}_{0}(X_{\theta})

and

Proof.

Let

There are thus

in the topology of

Proposition 6.7
.

For any

to
