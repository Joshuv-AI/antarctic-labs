Acute Disseminated Encephalomyelitis Presenting as Fever of Unknown Origin in a 4‐Year‐Old Boy: A Case Report
Authors: Pedro Pau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 4‐year‐old boy presented with prolonged fever of unknown origin and systemic inflammation without early neurological signs. MRI revealed multifocal demyelination, and MOG antibodies were positive. He was diagnosed with ADEM and treated with steroids and IVIg, but relapsed twice, consistent with multiphasic disseminated encephalomyelitis (MDEM).Keywords: acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM), autoimmune, central nervous system, multiphasic disseminated encephalomyelitis (MDEM), neuroimaging

Introduction

Acute disseminating encephalomyelitis (ADEM) is an inflammatory demyelinating disorder which has a wide clinical presentation of polyfocal neurological deficits. ADEM is usually monophasic however can present as a relapsing form known as multiphasic disseminated encephalomyelitis (MDEM). Here we present a case of ADEM/MDEM that manifested as fever of unknown origin.

Case History/Examination

A previously well 4‐year‐old boy presented with prolonged fever of unknown origin. He first developed symptoms of fever, vomiting, poor oral intake, and lethargy. He had no coryzal, no cough, no diarrhea, no joint swelling, no B‐symptoms and no bruising or bleeding. He was initially seen by his GP and was also seen at a peripheral emergency department and was presumed to have gastroenteritis however presented to a tertiary pediatric unit due to ongoing fevers on Day 10 of illness and was admitted for further workup.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

His initial bloods showed leucocytosis with white cell count 40 × 109/L (RR: 4.7–12.3) of predominantly neutrophils (30.4 × 109/L, RR: 1.8–7.7). Inflammatory markers were elevated with CRP 23 mg/L (RR: < 1 mg/L) and ESR 99 mm/h (RR: 0–14) and there was also a microcytic, hypochromic anemia with hemoglobin 86 g/L (RR: 110–139 g/L), MCV 57 fL (RR: 74–86 fL), and MCHC 295 g/L (RR: 332–360 g/L). He was commenced on empirical intravenous Ceftriaxone and had extensive work up (urine culture, blood culture, stool culture, respiratory viral panel, EBV, CMV, Toxoplasmosis, Hepatitis B & C, ASOT, Mycoplasma, Quantiferon Gold, Q‐fever, Rickettsial, JC‐virus, Flavivirus serology, chest x‐ray, abdominal ultrasound) which all returned negative. He had an echocardiogram which was normal and was seen by multiple subspecialty teams including Infectious Diseases, Rheumatology and Ophthalmology to identify the cause of his fevers. There was no initial cause found and the initial impression was possibly an inflammatory cause such as juvenile idiopathic arthritis. He was discharged home after being afebrile for 24 h on Day 21.

He was then readmitted to hospital on Day 25 with return of his fevers and an erythematous rash over his left elbow which subsequently improved prior to commencement of antibiotics. Repeat bloods showed ongoing inflammatory process with persistent leucocytosis and raised inflammatory markers (ESR 95 mm/h). He was commenced on intravenous Cefazolin and the Oncology team was involved given then diagnostic dilemma and he proceeded to a bone marrow aspirate (BMA) and PET CT on Day 31 of illness to rule out a malignant process. The PET CT showed signal changes in the cervical cord at C3–C4 with asymmetrical multiple intracranial uptake of the brain concerning for a malignant process (Figure 1). The day following the BMA he developed bilateral lower leg pain and changes in his gait which then progressed into lethargy and weakness with fluctuating conscious state of GCS 13–15. On examination he had truncated ataxia, hypotonia and brisk lower limb reflexes with bulbar signs. Given the PET CT findings and clinical changes, an MRI brain and spine was performed under general anesthetic which showed widespread T2 FLAIR hyper‐intensities with gadolinium enhancement involving the white matter, basal ganglia and cerebrum (Figure 2). The MRI spine showed a focal T2 hyper intense lesion in the mid‐cervical cord at C2–C3 level. Lumbar puncture with CSF analysis showed mildly elevated protein of 0.56 g/L (RR: 0.15–0.42 g/L), negative oligoclonal bands with elevated neopterin of 137 nmol/L (RR: 6–30 nmol/L). The patient's serum MOG IgG antibody returned positive with FACS ratio 2.7 (RR > 2).

Conclusion and Results

The patient was commenced on 7 days of Methylprednisolone and IVIG (2 g/kg) with the diagnosis of ADEM. He had good recovery of function with physiotherapy, occupational therapy and speech therapy and was discharged home on Day 47 on oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg.

Following discharge his steroids were weaned to alternate day dosing of 0.5 mg/kg however he represented with two further episodes of demyelination. The first recurrence was 6 weeks after discharge where he presented with lethargy, insomnia, slurred speech, and left‐sided hemiparesis with an MRI brain showing new T2 hyperintensities in the right internal capsule and basal ganglia. The second recurrence was 4 weeks later where he presented with a prolonged focal seizure with impaired awareness requiring IV Midazolam and recommencement of Levetiracetam with MRI brain showing four new T2 hyperintensities in the right frontal region. He was recommenced on Prednisolone and given the third presentation of demyelination; the impression is of MDEM for which he has been commenced on a trial of monthly IVIg.

Discussion

This case highlights an unusual presentation of ADEM in which the initial clinical picture was dominated by several weeks of fever of unknown origin, marked systemic inflammation, and absence of early focal neurological deficits. ADEM is an acute onset inflammatory, demyelinating event that occurs in early childhood with the mean age of presentation is 5–8 years‐old and a slight male predominance [1]. There may be an antecedent infection, or more rarely a post‐vaccination which then triggers molecular mimicry and production of CNS autoantigens [1].

ADEM is a clinical diagnosis with no specific biological marker with brain imaging and autoantibody testing ancillary. Diagnosing ADEM can be challenging due to the wide range of clinical presentations with nonspecific signs such lethargy, fever, headache, and vomiting ranging to focal neurological signs. ADEM must have features of encephalopathy however this can initially be subtle such as lethargy, sleepiness, or irritability as seen in our case presentation.

The differential diagnosis of acute disseminated encephalomyelitis is guided by the presenting clinical features. Infectious causes, including meningoencephalitis, should be prioritized in the presence of fever and focal neurological signs. In cases characterized by persistent systemic inflammation without an identifiable infectious cause, noninfectious inflammatory and rheumatological conditions, such as juvenile idiopathic arthritis, must also be considered. In this case, the degree of leukocytosis, anemia, and persistent elevation of inflammatory markers appropriately prompted extensive evaluation for malignant causes, including BMA and PET‐CT imaging. The PET‐CT findings illustrate how neuroinflammatory lesions can exhibit increased metabolic activity and mimic neoplastic processes.

Timely diagnosis is important for early intervention and favorable prognosis. In the cohort described by Takahashi et al., diagnostic delay extended up to 21 days, with gastrointestinal and respiratory illnesses being the most common initial misdiagnoses. Similarly, our patient was initially treated for presumed gastroenteritis and inflammatory conditions, with neuroimaging deferred until neurological deterioration became evident [2].

The laboratory findings for ADEM can be variable. CSF studies can also be normal or show a pleocytosis and an elevated protein count [1]. Electroencephalogram will often demonstrate generalized slowing consistent with an encephalopathy. In terms of neuroimaging, MRI brain is of choice. In this patient, MRI was pursued following acute neurological deterioration, at which point it demonstrated the characteristic multifocal T2‐weighted and FLAIR hyperintense lesions with gadolinium enhancement involving supratentorial white matter, basal ganglia, and cervical spinal cord, confirming the diagnosis. In ADEM, the autoantibody against myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG‐IgG) is of particular interest, being identified in 40%–68% of patients [3]. MOG‐IgG positivity further supports the diagnosis of MOG‐associated demyelinating disease and is strongly associated with a relapsing course, particularly in pediatric populations.

The treatment of ADEM consists of high‐dose intravenous steroids, typically methylprednisolone 20–30 mg/kg/day for 3–5 days, followed by an oral prednisolone taper over 4–6 weeks [1]. For patients who are MOG‐Ab positive, one‐third will relapse with the highest risk within 2 months of treatment or in rapid prednisolone taper and thus these patients will often have a longer taper of oral steroids [4]. Addition of treatment with immunoglobulins (IVIg) is common with common regimes of 1–2 g/kg over 2–5 days [1]. For those with severe or refractory disease, plasma exchange (PLEX) may be performed to reduce circulating levels of pathological molecules.

ADEM is usually monophasic however can present as a relapsing form known as MDEM. Due to the fluctuating course of ADEM, a relapse is defined as a new clinical episode accompanied by radiological evidence appearing at least one‐month after the last attack [4]. For patients with MDEM, almost all are MOG‐Ab positive, and every relapse places them at risk of secondary neuroaxonal injury and thus long‐term cognitive impairment [5]. This highlights the importance of early recognition and adequate steroid treatment. Although MOG antibody status is not reported in this case, the relapsing course is highly suggestive of MOG‐associated disease, for which prolonged immunotherapy is often required.

This case has several limitations. Although MOG‐IgG positivity supports the diagnosis of MOG‐associated disease, antibody titres were not serially monitored, limiting correlation with disease activity and relapse risk. Early neuroimaging was not performed, contributing to diagnostic delay. As a single case report, generalizability is limited, and broader studies are required to better define atypical febrile presentations of ADEM.

This case highlights an unusual presentation of ADEM in which the initial clinical picture was dominated by several weeks of fever of unknown origin without early focal neurological deficits. The later development of recurrent demyelinating episodes, each supported by new radiological lesions, was consistent with MDEM. Similar atypical presentations have been reported in the literature with Asif et al. describing a pediatric case of ADEM initially presenting as a systemic febrile illness without early neurological features, highlighting the diagnostic challenge and potential for delay in recognition [6].
