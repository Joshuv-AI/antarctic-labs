Perspectives on Education From Gastrointestinal (GI) Cancer Patients in the Deep South: Qualitative Analysis
Authors: Gianina C Hernandez‐Marquez, Nataliya V Ivankova, Nathan C English, Wendelyn M Oslock, Alizeh Abbas, Alfonsus Adrian H Harsono, Bayley A Jones, Ivan I Herbey, Gurudatta Naik, Yu‐Mei M Schoenberger‐Godwin, Daniel I Chu, Maria Pisu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionHigh‐quality perioperative education for cancer patients undergoing surgery is associated with positive patient experiences and postoperative outcomes. Significant variations in the receipt and quality of education exist, and these gaps need to be better understood. We aimed to explore facilitators and barriers to high‐quality education from a cohort of gastrointestinal (GI) cancer patients.MethodsThis qualitative study is part of the Advancing Surgical Cancer Care and Equity in the Deep South (ASCENDS) study. Participants were English‐speaking adults (> 18‐years old) from Alabama and Mississippi diagnosed with stage I‐III colorectal, pancreatic, or esophageal cancer from 2018 to 2021. Individual semi‐structured interviews focused on identifying facilitators and barriers to cancer surgical care including those related to patients' education across the surgery process. Data were analyzed using inductive thematic and content analysis with NVivo 12.6 Plus.ResultsOf 36 GI cancer patients (19 colorectal, 10 esophageal, and 7 pancreatic) interviewed, most underwent surgery (n = 31, 86.1%); ages ranged from 44 to 87 years, 42% were female, and 30% identified as Black. Patients emphasized clear delivery of instructions as a major facilitator across the three surgical phases. A facilitating role of the key providers, mainly nurses, in the delivery of education and education materials was important during perioperative and postoperative phases. Reported barriers were related to receiving incomplete instructions and a rushed way instructions were communicated during the preoperative and perioperative phases.ConclusionGI cancer patients valued clear delivery of instructions supplemented with explanations provided in lay terms to better understand relevant care processes. Addressing barriers such as incomplete instructions and lack of thorough education, within specific surgical phases, may improve education delivery. Future research should explore more effective ways to deliver education in lay terms across the surgical process.Keywords: barriers, facilitators, GI cancer, high‐quality education, patient education, surgery

Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) cancers comprise over 25% of total cancer incidence and have an overall related mortality of 35%. Colorectal, esophageal, and pancreatic are among the five most frequent GI cancers [1]. Surgery is often the cornerstone of treatment for these cancers, and high‐quality education has been shown to lead to better surgical outcomes, especially within enhanced recovery programs (ERP) [2]. These evidence‐based programs consist of pre‐operative to post‐operative processes for which patients' understanding and compliance are essential, and that could not be implemented adequately without high‐quality patient education. Such education goes beyond knowledge delivery—it must be tailored to patient needs, considering baseline knowledge and comprehension levels, and designed to instill major behavioral change [3]. Additionally, structured preoperative education has been linked to more reasonable patient expectations, increased engagement, and improved satisfaction [4, 5, 6]. During the preoperative phase, education is particularly crucial as it helps patients anticipate and manage their surgical experience by providing access to health information, postoperative psychosocial support, and anxiety reduction strategies [7]. After surgery, education can also markedly improve patients' quality of life by increasing patients' awareness of disease and late complications, providing guidance on lifestyle and diet, improving patients' self‐care skills, and creating supportive familial and social environments [8]. Engaged and well‐informed patients are more likely to experience fewer postoperative complications and shorter hospital stays, leading to reduced healthcare costs and improved overall recovery outcomes [9].

Despite its critical role in improving patient outcomes, patient education throughout the surgical journey remains inconsistent, inadequate, and often ineffective [10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. While studies have reported that preoperative patient education is technically delivered, it remains insufficient as many patients continue to experience high levels of anxiety and confusion about their procedures [15], and frequently struggle to fully understand the information provided [7]. Persisting key gaps include unclear or inadequate instructions regarding the surgical process and inconsistencies in how staff members provide preoperative information [4]. Moreover, the factors that hinder or facilitate the delivery of high‐quality patient education are also still unclear. Such facilitators and barriers may differ for patients with different health literacy levels. Low health literacy created barriers in the preoperative (difficulty understanding diagnosis), perioperative (refusing aspects of care), and postoperative surgery phase (difficulty understanding postoperative complications) [16] specific health literacy‐sensitive education strategies that can effectively target patients across all literacy levels. To address these knowledge gaps, this study aimed to explore GI cancer patients' perspectives on facilitators and barriers to high‐quality pre to postoperative patient education across different levels of health literacy. Findings are important to inform future education improvement efforts across all surgical phases.

Methods

Study Design

This is an analysis of data from semi‐structured interviews with GI cancer patients recruited from Alabama and Mississippi as part of the Advancing Surgical Cancer Care and Equity in the Deep South (ASCENDS) study [17, 18]. The ASCENDS study investigated barriers to access to surgical care for three GI cancers (esophageal, pancreatic, and colorectal [CRC]) using a mixed‐methods approach, including quantitative surveys and qualitative interviews. The study was approved by the University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB) Institutional Review Board (IRB‐300005475). Participants provided informed consent for the interviews and received a monetary incentive for their participation.

Recruitment and Participants

Eligible participants were adult patients (aged 18 years or older) diagnosed with stage I to III esophageal cancer, pancreatic cancer, or CRC between 2018 and 2021. Eligibility criteria required participants to be English‐speaking and capable of completing a one‐hour interview. Patients were identified from hospital registries and the Alabama Statewide Cancer Registry, and by collaborating with other treatment institutions in Alabama and Mississippi. Potential participants received a mailed letter outlining the study's purpose and providing contact information, allowing them to either enroll in or decline participation. A few days later, they received a call from recruiters from the O'Neal Comprehensive Cancer Center's Participant Recruitment and Assessment (PRA) shared facility.

Interviews

The interview guide was informed by Torain et al.'s [19] surgical disparities and Thomas and Penchansky's [20] and Saurman's [21] access to care frameworks to investigate facilitators and barriers to surgical care across access to care domains. Partial results of the main analyses related to the domains of Availability, Accessibility and Affordability have been previously reported [17, 18]. For this analysis, we expand on previous findings about the Awareness domain, that is, the domain related to communication and information gaps that may exist between the health system and patients limiting patients' understanding of the care they are seeking or receiving [16]. Specifically, we focus on the following interview guide questions related to education and instructions for each of the surgical phases:

Interviews were conducted over the phone by a trained interviewer from the PRA shared facility who had extensive experience conducting interviews with cancer patients and previously worked as a cancer patient navigator. Training specific to the study topic and interview guide was provided by the investigators. To ensure the patients were comfortable before the interviews began, the interviewer established rapport by discussing the consent elements and allowing them to ask questions.

Health Literacy Assessment

The patient's health literacy level was assessed at the time of screening for ASCENDS eligibility using the BRIEF Health Literacy Screening Tool that identifies individuals who may struggle with understanding medical information, instructions, or self‐care tasks [22]. This tool consists of four questions rated on a 5‐point Likert scale. We identified three groups of participants based on the total score: patients with scores 4–12 were categorized as having inadequate health literacy, 13–16 as having marginal health literacy, and 17–20 as having adequate health literacy.

Analysis

All interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim by an independent commercial transcription company. Transcripts were first validated by comparing them with the original audio recordings to ensure transcription accuracy. A team of experienced researchers (IIH, GN, and NVI) analyzed verified transcripts using inductive thematic [23] and content analysis [24] methods using NVivo 12.6 Plus software (Lumivero), a comprehensive platform for qualitative data analysis. The analysis followed several key steps. Researchers first independently coded the transcripts to identify important points, recurring themes, and subthemes in the data. The constant comparative method [25] was employed to guide an inductive analytical process, ensuring data saturation and continuously comparing new information with previously coded data. Emerging codes and themes were regularly discussed to ensure that intercoder agreement was achieved at the recommended 90% threshold [26]. Content analysis was performed on the coded data to identify common and different patients' perspectives across the three surgery phases based on themes and subthemes generated from the thematic analysis using the counts of text references. Themes and subthemes were explored for the patient literacy level groups and across three surgical phases. The same intercoder agreement process was performed to confirm thematic saturation for education‐specific themes [27].

Facilitators

Three themes were highlighted as major facilitators of high‐quality education delivery by patients across inadequate and marginal health literacy levels and included clear delivery of instructions, key providers in delivery of education, and education materials throughout surgical phases. Additional quotes for facilitator themes can be found in Table S2.

Clear Delivery of Instructions

During the preoperative phase, patients indicated the importance of education on surgical approaches and treatment plans.

They [surgeon and team] just told me what's going on, what was going to happen, what they were going to do… They even told me, you know, your surgery could last five hours up to eight; it depends on what we get into and all of that. And I understood that, in case they had to do it differently, you know, if they had to lay me open. (Patient 17, marginal health literacy)

The importance of using visual aid materials was noted during this stage as patients expressed better understanding of which parts would be removed during surgery.

And he went over everything with me and with my daughters, he answered our questions; he had a diagram and he showed me everything that needed to be done. (Patient 4, unknown health literacy)

During the perioperative phase, education was focused on enhanced recovery program (ERP) components such as early mobility, pain block, and bowel preparation.

Well, they told me what all I needed to do. I didn't need to drink anything after midnight and that kind of stuff. They told me not to eat and everything. (Patient 17, marginal health literacy)

Patients also highlighted how repetition of instructions improved their understanding of perioperative steps to take and helped them feel prepared for surgery.

Everything they said was. Yes, ma'am. They kept going over and over it. They always repeated it four, five, six times. Drill it in your head. (Patient 2, unknown health literacy)

During the postoperative phase, education focused on ERP on early mobility and repetition of instructions.

Well, they told me not to—says, now you're going to be sore. Do not lift anything. And try to walk. Said, try to walk every day. And that's about all they told me… (Patient 23, inadequate health literacy)

The importance of using lay terminology to explain instructions was reported to contribute to better patient understanding of the care necessary for their recovery period.

…As far as he gave me my diet, he said, ‘You can resume eating as normal within a couple of days, but don't just go eat a whole pizza’, he was like being a little cautious…. (Patient 33, marginal health literacy)

Key Providers in Delivery of Education

Key providers in the delivery of education were a prominent theme during the perioperative and postoperative phases. During the perioperative phase, nurses played a vital role in facilitating the delivery of instructions and ensuring understanding of the care information.

…I went step by step with what the nurse told me to do. … I followed their instructions to the tee, and everything went fine… (Patient 29, marginal health literacy)

During the postoperative phase, nurses continued to play a key role but also surgeons and other team providers contributed to patient education.

The doctor. The doctor had talked to me. Then the nurses. I talked to the doctor, this. That's the doctor that did my surgery. He told me all that…. Yeah, he said you eat anything you want. But he said one thing, lay off the greasy food because it's not good for you. (Patient 11, marginal health literacy)

Education Materials

The importance of education materials was highlighted during the perioperative and postoperative phases. During the perioperative phase, written modalities of education materials were noted as aiding patients' understanding of their disease and prompting them to seek more information.

Because I didn't know really – I didn't know anything about the cancer, but now I know a lot. Like researching and just reading about it a lot. They gave me the brochures, and I just googled online and just started reading different stuff on cancer itself so I know more about it. That's why – I really feel great. (Patient 1, unknown health literacy)

During the postoperative phase, patients mentioned relying on both written and oral modalities of education materials in preparation for their postoperative course.

Well, I didn't have any problem understanding it. I guess with me being up there for a while – they had written instructions and then she explained it to me orally. I didn't have a problem with the instructions. (Patient 13, inadequate health literacy)

In summary, clear delivery of instructions emerged as key facilitator across all surgical phases. The provision of written and oral modalities of education materials and key providers in deliver education, particularly nurses, were deemed more important within the perioperative and postoperative phases. Facilitators were highlighted by patients reporting inadequate, marginal, and adequate health literacy levels.

Barriers

Two themes were identified as factors impacting high‐quality education delivery: incomplete instructions and communication of instructions. These themes emerged for the preoperative and perioperative phases.

Incomplete Instructions

Incomplete instructions were noted during the preoperative phase. Patients shared how lack of thorough preoperative education instructions led to insufficient understanding of bowel preoperative medications to be taken before their procedure.

Yeah. I cannot tell you what they [instructions] were though. I think they gave me – I do not know why I had to take a medication before I went to surgery. I know I did. I had to blow out again just like I did when I had the colonoscopy. (Patient 34, marginal health literacy)

Patients also shared how insufficient preoperative education instructions led to unclear expectations about surgical incision size.

I had total trust in him, and he told me everything, like I said, that he was going to do. And I really didn't expect it to be that big of a incision. It was like all the way from my belly button down to the bottom. But I guess that's just the way that they do it. They have to open it up that big to get that out. (Patient 22, marginal health literacy)

Communication of Instructions

During the perioperative phase, the manner of delivery of instructions was reported to contribute to delay in surgery.

… It was his office did not call me to do the proper prep for the surgery. I showed up to do the surgery… The nurse came in and said okay, you have already done this, this, and this. I said no. They said, you did not do any prep. I said nobody told me what to do. This is my first time to have surgery like this. She said, oh my gosh. He came in, and he was really upset… He said I cannot do surgery… (Patient 29, marginal health literacy)

Within the perioperative period, the rushed way of delivering instructions was reported to contribute to hinder decision‐making on obtaining treatment.

… as they were prepping my body… taking me right into surgery, there was an option given me, I believe it was like a pain block… Now, I'm already…you know, you're in that tense situation, you're lying there…, and they said something, you can choose to have this or not. And I asked what were the benefits, and they said, well… then you wake up from surgery, certain parts of the body are still asleep. That's the sense I got…and that it decreases the pain, the initial pain… And yeah, I was interested in that, but it was presented in such a way, in such a hurried way, I didn't have time to actually give any serious thought to it. (Patient 6, unknown health literacy)

In summary, barriers related to incomplete or not properly delivered instructions impact patient understanding of how to prepare for surgery or what care they need, leading to surgery delays and sub‐optimal care. The lack of thorough instructions was commonly noted only during the preoperative and perioperative surgical phases. Barriers to education were mostly described by participants that reported marginal health literacy level.

Discussion

In this sample of GI cancer patients from Alabama and Mississippi, multiple facilitators and barriers to high‐quality education were identified across the surgical process. GI cancer patients found benefit in clear delivery of instructions, reported nurses as the key providers in delivery of education, and appreciated written and oral modalities of education materials: these were important to the understanding of the surgical process. On the other hand, limitations were perceived regarding incomplete instructions and communication of instructions, especially by those reporting lower health literacy. Targeting the facilitators and barriers identified here will help improve our understanding of more effective ways to deliver high‐quality education across surgical processes.

Clear delivery of information, explanations in lay terms, repetition of instructions, and visual aids improved patients' understanding of operative education. Similar to our study, Ellis et al. reported how important it was to clearly deliver and reinforce preoperative instructions to patients undergoing orthopedic procedures [28]. Moreover, Phang et al. reported how the use of videos to supplement patient education written materials can make a positive difference in patient surgical outcomes [29]. Additionally, mostly nurses were reported as playing a vital role in facilitating instructions and cementing care information. Multiple studies have highlighted the benefits of nursing interventions on preoperative counseling, cooperative care management, and postoperative education [8, 30, 31]. Furthermore, these nurse‐driven interventions have led to improved quality of life in gastric surgery patients, reduced risk of postoperative infections in GI cancer surgery patients, and improved outcomes including shorter length of stay and lower postoperative pain [30, 31]. Our participants confirmed the pivotal role of nurses in surgery education and how their consistent availability and effective communication skills enhanced patient preparedness and experience throughout the surgical journey. Identifying efficient modalities, health literacy‐sensitive care education, and provider type for delivering high‐quality education can help us better allocate resources and potentially improve surgical outcomes.

Conversely, our findings highlighted how the lack of thorough preoperative and perioperative education or delivery of complete instructions in a manner not conducive to patients' understanding, for example, in a hurried manner, may lead to surgery or other treatment delays in part hindering patient's decision capacity. Harris et al. reported a similar scenario where delays and surgery care cancelations were attributed to lack of clear communication [32]. The authors also mentioned the importance of repetition of information and its contribution to patient understanding, a facilitator that also emerged in our study. Blondal et al. reported lack of thorough education on postoperative complications, pain management, and expected recovery time in their elective surgery patient cohort [33]. Interestingly, no barriers in high‐quality education were reported for the postoperative phase in our study, showing perhaps that education during that period is well delivered and information is understood by patients. Alternatively, patients might be receiving more education in this recovery phase through the multiple teaching strategies including, written resources, verbal delivery, and various sessions, that could explain the lack of barriers reported [34, 35]. Overall, targeting education at all points across the surgical journey, but particularly within the preoperative and perioperative phases, is critical to achieve greater patient comprehension. Our study highlights that considering patient's health literacy may be important throughout the surgical journey. Perhaps early screening of the health literacy level can help providers deliver health literacy‐concordant education throughout the surgical journey. In addition to establishing baseline literacy level assessments, it is important to train providers on effective health‐literacy consistent teaching methods and provide them with adequate resources, to enable patient involvement in their care.

Our study has several limitations. First, there is potential recall bias since our participants responded to these questions within a 5‐year span of receiving their cancer diagnosis and treatment. Secondly, based on the incomplete health literacy data and small subgroup sizes, we must be cautious when interpreting results by health literacy levels and avoid overgeneralization. Thirdly, since patients were recruited from Alabama and Mississippi, their views might not represent the experiences of those residing in other US states or of those from other Deep South states with different socioeconomic and healthcare conditions. Finally, the interview guides were developed to assess several domains of access to care: future research should more directly explore patient education and expand on specific aspects such as timing of education, patient‐provider communication interactions, and preferred learning modalities.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that, to improve the delivery of GI surgical cancer care education, we need to work towards standardizing clear delivery of instructions with oral and written modalities of education materials and ensure these meet the level of patient understanding. Moreover, since they serve as the main educators, surgical nurses' enhanced training on effective teaching methods is warranted. Addressing barriers, particularly those in the preoperative and perioperative phases, may lead to significant improvement in the delivery of high‐quality education for patients undergoing surgical and other cancer care.
