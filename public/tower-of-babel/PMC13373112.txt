Toxicity outcomes of concurrent radiotherapy with adjuvant capecitabine in triple-negative breast cancer (TROD 06-24)
Authors: Volkan Demircan, Berrin Benli Yavuz, Bekir Hakan Bakkal, Sukran Celikarslan, Sule Karabulut Gul, Huseyin Tepetam, Didem Colpan Oksuz, Alaettin Arslan, Ayse Altinok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), the subtype with the poorest prognosis, shows treatment outcomes closely tied to the response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Patients with residual disease post-treatment face a higher recurrence risk. In this subgroup, adjuvant capecitabine has been shown to improve disease-free and overall survival. Although concurrent use of capecitabine and radiotherapy (RT) is considered safe in gastrointestinal cancers, data on its safety in breast cancer are limited. This study aimed to evaluate toxicity outcomes associated with concurrent capecitabine and RT in TNBC.MethodsThis retrospective multicenter study included 95 patients with TNBC treated with concurrent adjuvant capecitabine and RT between 2018 and 2024 across eight medical centers. Toxicity was graded according to CTCAE v5.0. Univariable comparisons were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate. Multivariable analyses were performed using forward stepwise logistic regression for grade 2–3 dermatitis and Firth’s penalized likelihood logistic regression for grade 3 dermatitis. Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi 2.6.44, JASP 0.19.3, and Python 3.11.ResultsThe median follow-up was 25.3 months, and the median age was 49 years (range 29–81). Comorbidities were present in 33.7% of patients. RT was interrupted in 5.3% of patients; capecitabine was dose-reduced in 5 patients and discontinued in 2. Skin toxicity occurred in 86.3% of patients (grade 1, 44.2%; grade 2, 33.7%; grade 3, 8.4%), while 13.7% experienced no skin toxicity. Hematologic toxicity (any grade) was observed in 26.3% of patients and gastrointestinal toxicity in 8.4%, with no significant differences across skin toxicity strata. After multiplicity adjustment, clinical T3–4 stage, post-neoadjuvant ypT3-4 and ypN2-3 stage, and bolus application were significantly associated with higher-grade dermatitis (all q < 0.05). On multivariable analysis, advanced clinical T stage was the sole independent predictor of grade 2–3 dermatitis (OR 4.62, 95% CI 1.80-11.91, p = 0.002). For grade 3 dermatitis, Firth penalized regression identified total lymphatic irradiation (OR 9.93, p = 0.006) and bolus application (OR 7.08, p = 0.036) as independent predictors.ConclusionConcurrent capecitabine and RT in TNBC demonstrated a manageable acute toxicity profile in this retrospective multicenter cohort. Further prospective studies are needed to validate these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05481-4.Keywords: Radiotherapy, Capecitabine, Triple negative breast cancer, Side effects, Toxicity

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is the most aggressive molecular subtype of breast cancer, accounting for approximately 15–20% of all breast cancer cases [1]. In recent years, the standard treatment approach for early-stage TNBC has evolved with the incorporation of immunotherapy into neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimens. Based on the KEYNOTE-522 trial, neoadjuvant pembrolizumab combined with anthracycline- and taxane-based chemotherapy followed by adjuvant pembrolizumab has demonstrated significant improvements in both pathological complete response (pCR) and event-free survival and is now considered the current standard of care for high-risk early TNBC [2]. Reported pCR rates with conventional chemotherapy alone range from 20 to 60%, depending on regimen composition, including the use of taxanes, platinum agents, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, or nab-paclitaxel [3]. With the addition of immunotherapy, pCR rates increase substantially, reaching approximately 64.8% in the pembrolizumab–chemotherapy arm of KEYNOTE-522 compared with 51.2% with chemotherapy alone [2]. Despite these advances, about one third of patients still exhibit residual disease after neoadjuvant therapy, which remains strongly associated with higher recurrence rates and poorer survival outcomes [4]. Moreover, despite the demonstrated benefits of immunotherapy, its high cost and limited accessibility in many regions restrict universal implementation; therefore, adjuvant capecitabine continues to represent an important and widely accessible therapeutic option for patients with residual TNBC.

The CREATE-X trial was designed to evaluate the efficacy of adjuvant capecitabine in patients with HER2-negative breast cancer who had residual disease after neoadjuvant chemotherapy containing anthracyclines and/or taxanes. The study demonstrated a 9% improvement in disease-free survival (DFS) and a 5% improvement in overall survival (OS) in the capecitabine group. Notably, subgroup analysis revealed that the benefit was more pronounced in patients with TNBC, with improvements of 14% in DFS and 9% in OS compared to hormone receptor–positive subgroups [5].

Subsequently, the ECOG-ACRIN EA1131 trial compared adjuvant capecitabine with platinum-based chemotherapy in TNBC patients with residual disease. However, platinum agents failed to improve survival outcomes and were associated with increased toxicity [6]. Therefore, adjuvant capecitabine remains the standard of care for TNBC patients with residual disease following neoadjuvant chemotherapy.

While existing evidence is encouraging, the optimal timing and integration of capecitabine within the multimodality treatment pathway remains uncertain. In the CREATE-X study, patients received radiotherapy (RT) and capecitabine in varying sequences—some underwent RT prior to capecitabine, whereas others initiated capecitabine before RT [5]. Given its established radiosensitizing properties, capecitabine can potentiate the cytotoxic efficacy of RT [7]. It has been safely administered concurrently with RT in several malignancies, particularly within gastrointestinal cancer protocols [8–10]. Concurrent administration has also been investigated in the neoadjuvant treatment of locally advanced breast cancer and in the management of recurrent disease [11–14]. However, data addressing the role of combined capecitabine and RT in the adjuvant breast cancer setting remain limited, leaving this therapeutic strategy insufficiently defined.

The continuation of systemic therapy is essential for patients who exhibit an incomplete response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy. However, the impact of a 5–6-week delay in systemic treatment—due to the administration of RT—remains unclear in this specific patient population. Given the aggressive nature of this histologic subtype, the immediate initiation of systemic therapy following neoadjuvant treatment may offer improved clinical outcomes.

Several subsequent studies have suggested that concurrent administration of capecitabine and RT is both safe and feasible; however, these reports included relatively small patient cohorts, limiting the generalizability of their findings [15–17]. In light of this gap in the literature, we conducted a multicenter study across our country to evaluate the toxicity outcomes associated with the concurrent use of capecitabine and RT in patients with TNBC.

Methods

Data from 95 patients with TNBC who received concurrent adjuvant capecitabine and RT following neoadjuvant chemotherapy were collected from eight medical centers. Demographic information, pathological and clinical characteristics, RT doses, techniques and fields, chemotherapy regimens, toxicity profiles, and follow-up data were obtained from hospital medical records and electronic databases (Suppl Table-1).

This study included patients with histologically confirmed TNBC who demonstrated residual disease (primary tumor or lymph nodes) following standard neoadjuvant chemotherapy and subsequently received adjuvant RT concurrently with capecitabine. Eligible patients were required to undergo definitive breast or chest-wall surgery with axillary staging, have adequate clinical and treatment documentation, and receive capecitabine at standard adjuvant dosing during the RT course. Patients treated between 2018 and 2024 across participating centers were included. Exclusion criteria comprised patients who received capecitabine sequentially rather than concurrently with RT, those with incomplete toxicity or treatment data, individuals with metastatic disease at the time of adjuvant therapy, non-TNBC histology, and patients who had not received neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Patients who received concurrent pembrolizumab during RT were also excluded due to potential confounding effects on toxicity outcomes. Additionally, cases with insufficient follow-up or missing essential variables were excluded from the analysis. Accordingly, the present cohort may best reflect a pre-immunotherapy or limited-access real-world TNBC population rather than the entirety of contemporary TNBC practice.

Toxicity

Skin, gastrointestinal, and hematologic toxicities were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0. Toxicity data were extracted from patient files based on weekly clinical evaluations performed during the course of RT and at the first follow-up visit approximately one month after treatment completion. Toxicity grading was performed locally by treating radiation oncologists at each participating center according to CTCAE v5.0 criteria. No centralized toxicity adjudication or interinstitutional calibration process was performed. Although some patients had longer follow-up and chronic toxicity data available, the multicentric nature of the study and variability in documentation practices across centers limited the reliability and consistency of long-term toxicity assessments. Therefore, only acute toxicities were considered for analysis. The primary endpoint of this study was the incidence of acute treatment-related skin toxicity, particularly Grade ≥ 2 radiation dermatitis, during concurrent capecitabine-RT. Secondary exploratory endpoints included Grade 3 dermatitis, treatment interruptions, capecitabine dose modifications/discontinuations, and other systemic toxicities. Additional clinical events occurring during treatment were also documented. Interruptions in RT, as well as capecitabine dose reductions or treatment discontinuations, were recorded and evaluated.

Radiotherapy techniques

All patients treated with either conventional or hypofractionated RT regimens were included. Treatment fields were categorized as follows: (1) breast/chest wall only; (2) breast/chest wall plus peripheral lymphatics—defined as level I–III axillary lymph nodes and supraclavicular lymph nodes; and (3) breast/chest wall plus total lymphatics—peripheral lymphatics plus internal mammary lymph nodes. RT techniques, including intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT)/volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), field-in-field (FiF), and helical IMRT, were also documented. The preferred RT doses were 50 Gy in 25 fractions for conventional fractionation, and 40 Gy in 15 fractions or 42.5 Gy in 16 fractions for hypofractionated regimens. When indicated, an additional tumor bed boost of 10 Gy was delivered in 4 or 5 fractions. (Supplemantary Table-1)

RT planning and delivery were performed according to institutional protocols at each participating center. Treatment planning was CT-based in all patients. Multiple treatment platforms were used across centers, including linear accelerator–based 3D-CRT/field-in-field, IMRT/VMAT, and helical tomotherapy systems. Given the retrospective multicenter design, no centralized contouring atlas, dosimetric review, or machine-specific quality assurance harmonization was performed.

Chemotherapy

All patients in the study received neoadjuvant chemotherapy. The most commonly used chemotherapy regimens included four cycles of adriamycin plus cyclophosphamide administered every three weeks (AC q3 weeks), followed by twelve cycles of weekly paclitaxel (4 AC q3 weeks + 12 weekly P), or four cycles of adriamycin plus cyclophosphamide administered every three weeks followed by four cycles of paclitaxel administered every three weeks, with or without carboplatin (4 AC q3 weeks + 4 P q3 weeks ± carboplatin). Alternative chemotherapy regimens were required in only three patients. Capecitabine was administered following the CREATE-X regimen at a dose of 1,250 mg/m² twice daily on days 1–14 of a 21-day cycle, for a total of six to eight cycles [5]. RT is typically initiated during the first or second cycle of capecitabine and subsequently continued following completion of the RT course.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed at a two-sided significance level of p ≤ 0.05. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. The normality of continuous data was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk testing and visual inspection of skewness and kurtosis; values within ± 1.5 were considered to indicate an approximately normal distribution.

Between-group comparisons for the three skin toxicity categories (Grade 0–1, Grade 2, and Grade 3) were performed using one-way ANOVA for continuous variables, Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables with all expected cell counts ≥ 5, and the Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test (Monte Carlo simulation with 50,000 iterations) for sparse contingency tables. To account for multiple univariable comparisons across the 18 clinicopathologic and treatment-related variables, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (BH-FDR) procedure; both raw p-values and FDR-adjusted q-values are reported in the supplementary tables.

To identify independent predictors of skin toxicity, two separate multivariable logistic regression models were constructed. For the Grade 2–3 dermatitis endpoint (40 events among 95 patients; events-per-variable [EPV] = 13.3), a forward stepwise binary logistic regression was applied; candidate variables were selected based on univariable Pearson chi-square or Fisher-Freeman-Halton p < 0.10 together with a priori biological relevance. Entry and exit criteria into the multivariable model were set at p < 0.10 and p < 0.15, respectively, based on the likelihood ratio test. Model performance was evaluated using the model chi-square and Nagelkerke R², with effect estimates reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% Wald confidence intervals (CI).

For the Grade 3 dermatitis endpoint (8 events; EPV = 2.7), the standard maximum-likelihood logistic regression was considered inappropriate due to the high risk of overfitting and small-sample bias inherent in sparse data settings. Therefore, Firth’s penalized likelihood logistic regression was applied [33], which incorporates a Jeffreys-prior penalty to reduce small-sample bias and to stabilize parameter estimates when separation or near-separation is present. Confidence intervals were constructed using profile penalized likelihood, and statistical significance was assessed via penalized likelihood ratio tests. The three pre-specified variables (RT field [total lymphatic vs. other], bolus use, and ypT3-4 stage) were retained based on biological plausibility and univariable signal. Effect estimates from the standard and Firth models were compared to assess the impact of small-sample correction; the Firth-adjusted estimates are presented as the primary results for the Grade 3 model.

Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi (Version 2.6.44, The Jamovi Project, 2023, https://www.jamovi.org), JASP (Version 0.19.3, Jeffreys’ Amazing Statistics Program, 2024, https://jasp-stats.org), and Python 3.11 (scipy 1.11, statsmodels 0.14, with a custom implementation of Firth’s penalized regression based on the Heinze and Schemper algorithm).

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Bahçeşehir University Non-Interventional Studies Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2025-14/07).

All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Results

Patient and treatment characteristics

A total of 95 patients were included in the analysis, with a mean age of 49.9 ± 11.1 years. The majority of patients were ≤ 50 years of age (58.9%, n = 56), while 41.1% (n = 39) were older than 50 years. Comorbid conditions were present in 33.7% (n = 32) of the cohort.

Based on clinical staging, 11.6% (n = 11) had stage I disease, 58.9% (n = 56) stage II, and 29.5% (n = 28) stage III. Regarding systemic therapy, 72.6% (n = 69) received the 4 + 12 chemotherapy regimen, whereas 24.2% (n = 23) received the 4 + 4 regimen, and 3.2% (n = 3) received other regimens.

Surgical management consisted of mastectomy in 49.5% (n = 47) and breast-conserving surgery in 50.5% (n = 48). For axillary management, 42.1% (n = 40) underwent sentinel lymph node biopsy, while 57.9% (n = 55) underwent axillary lymph node dissection.

RT techniques included 3D-CRT/field-in-field in 61.1%, IMRT/VMAT in 23.2%, and helical tomotherapy in 15.8% of patients. Conventional fractionation was used in 63.2%, whereas hypofractionated regimens were delivered in 36.8% of patients (Suppl Table 1).

During treatment, 5 of the 95 patients required RT interruptions, all of which were due to skin toxicity. Capecitabine dose reductions were necessary in 5 patients, primarily because of Grade 3 skin toxicity. In addition, capecitabine treatment was discontinued in 2 patients, one due to hand–foot syndrome and the other due to Grade 3 neutropenia.

Skin toxicity distribution

Skin toxicity occurred at varying degrees across the cohort. Grade 0–1 toxicity was observed in 57.9% (n = 55) of patients, Grade 2 toxicity in 33.7% (n = 32), and Grade 3 toxicity in 8.4% (n = 8).

Univariable analysis demonstrated a significant association between clinical T stage and skin toxicity (p < 0.001; q = 0.005 after Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment). Advanced tumors (cT3-4) were present in 75.0% of patients with Grade 3 toxicity, compared with 16.4% among those with Grade 0–1 toxicity. Similarly, post-neoadjuvant pathological staging showed significant associations with toxicity severity, including ypT stage (p < 0.001; q = 0.006) and ypN stage (p = 0.009; q = 0.041). Among patients with Grade 3 toxicity, 62.5% had ypT3-4 disease, compared to 9.1% in the Grade 0–1 group.

Among RT-related parameters, bolus application demonstrated a strong correlation with higher toxicity grades (p < 0.001; q = 0.006). Bolus was used in 62.5% of patients with Grade 3 toxicity, compared with 16.4% of those with Grade 0–1 toxicity. The axillary RT approach was nominally associated with dermatitis severity in unadjusted analysis (p = 0.015) but did not retain statistical significance after multiplicity adjustment (q = 0.052), suggesting that this association may not be robust in this sample.

Conversely, age, comorbidity status, chemotherapy regimen, surgical approach, RT fractionation schedule, and RT technique were not significantly associated with skin toxicity grade (p > 0.05) (Suppl Table 1).

Independent predictors of skin toxicity

Grade 2–3 dermatitis. Multivariable forward stepwise binary logistic regression (entry p < 0.10, exit p < 0.15) identified advanced clinical T stage as the sole independent predictor of Grade 2–3 dermatitis (cT3-4 vs. cT1-2; OR 4.62, 95% CI 1.80-11.91, p = 0.002). The model demonstrated overall statistical significance (model χ² = 10.83, p = 0.001) with a Nagelkerke R² of 0.145. Candidate variables that reached univariable significance, including ypT3-4 stage and RT field, did not enter the final model after adjustment for cT stage, suggesting that their univariable associations were partly mediated by clinical tumor burden (Table 1, upper panel).

Grade 3 dermatitis. Given the low number of Grade 3 events (n = 8) and the resulting events-per-variable ratio of 2.7, Firth’s penalized likelihood logistic regression was applied to mitigate small-sample bias. Three pre-specified variables were included: RT field (total lymphatic vs. other), bolus application, and ypT3-4 stage. Total lymphatic irradiation (OR 9.93, 95% CI 1.85–82.03, p = 0.006) and bolus application (OR 7.08, 95% CI 1.15–51.76, p = 0.036) emerged as independent predictors of Grade 3 dermatitis, while ypT3-4 stage did not retain statistical significance (OR 5.71, 95% CI 0.97–35.14, p = 0.055). The penalized model demonstrated good overall fit (model χ² = 23.18, p < 0.001) (Table 1, lower panel).

Collectively, these findings indicate that advanced clinical T stage is the most consistent independent correlate of higher-grade dermatitis in this cohort, while total lymphatic irradiation and bolus application emerge as specific risk factors for severe (Grade 3) skin reactions. The disappearance of ypT3-4 as an independent predictor after Firth correction underscores the importance of small-sample-bias adjustment in sparse-data analyses.

Other toxicities

Other treatment-related toxicities were relatively uncommon. Hematologic toxicity (any grade) was observed in 26.3% of patients (25/95), while gastrointestinal toxicity was reported in 8.4% (8/95). No Grade 4 adverse events were observed.

Comparative analysis demonstrated no statistically significant differences between the skin toxicity groups with respect to gastrointestinal toxicities (diarrhea and abdominal discomfort), hematologic toxicities (neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia), or other treatment-related adverse events. (Suppl Table 1). Hand–foot syndrome was observed in only one patient, indicating a very low incidence of this capecitabine-related toxicity in our cohort. No cases of febrile neutropenia were observed.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the toxicity outcomes of 95 patients with TNBC who received concurrent capecitabine with RT. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the second-largest retrospective series to date assessing the safety of concurrent capecitabine administration during RT. In addition, we investigated clinical and treatment-related factors associated with the development of treatment-related toxicity. Our findings suggest that concurrent capecitabine administration during RT was feasible in this retrospective cohort, with toxicity rates generally comparable to previously published observational studies.

However, it is important to interpret these results within the context of evolving systemic treatment strategies in TNBC. Neoadjuvant pembrolizumab combined with chemotherapy has become a standard-of-care approach for high-risk early-stage TNBC, as recommended by current NCCN and ASCO guidelines, following the results of pivotal trials such as KEYNOTE-522 (2). Notably, patients receiving pembrolizumab were not included in our cohort, which represents a limitation of this study. The potential interactions between immune checkpoint inhibitors and concurrent capecitabine-based chemoradiotherapy, particularly in terms of toxicity and treatment tolerability, remain largely unexplored and warrant further investigation in future prospective studies.

Our findings demonstrate that concurrent capecitabine administration with RT is both feasible and well-tolerated. RT interruptions were required in five patients, while capecitabine dose reduction was necessary in five patients. Treatment discontinuation due to toxicity occurred in only two cases, indicating an overall manageable toxicity profile. No grade 4 toxicities were observed, and grade 3 skin toxicity was reported in only 8.4% of patients. In comparison, previous studies have reported rates of grade 3 radiation dermatitis ranging from 20% to 40% [15–17]. While differences in sample size may contribute to variations in toxicity rates, our results are generally consistent with the existing literature.

Radiation dermatitis is a well-recognized side effect of breast irradiation. Capecitabine, on the other hand, is associated with dermatologic toxicities such as dermatitis, photosensitivity, and hand-foot syndrome [18, 19]. Consequently, the concurrent use of RT and capecitabine has raised concerns regarding increased skin toxicity and overall tolerability. Although previous studies have suggested that this combination is both safe and tolerable, these findings are limited by small sample sizes [15, 17].

The incidence of grade 3 skin toxicity was relatively low in our cohort. Therefore, we performed two multivariable analyses to identify subgroups at higher risk for radiation dermatitis, evaluating grade 2–3 and grade 3 events separately. For grade 2–3 dermatitis, advanced clinical T stage (cT3-4) emerged as the sole independent predictor after forward stepwise selection. For grade 3 dermatitis, given the small number of events (n = 8; events-per-variable = 2.7), Firth’s penalized likelihood logistic regression was applied to mitigate small-sample bias. Total lymphatic irradiation and bolus application were independent predictors, while post-neoadjuvant ypT3-4 stage did not retain statistical significance (p = 0.055) after small-sample correction. These findings should be interpreted with caution, as the identified risk factors may exert overlapping or synergistic effects, and the absence of a control group limits the ability to delineate the independent contribution of each variable.

Several well-established predictors of radiation dermatitis have been reported in the literature, including body mass index (BMI), breast size, boost administration, bolus use, RT technique, fractionation, presence of hot spots, and skin pigmentation [20–22]. In addition, prophylactic use of moisturizing creams has been shown to reduce the incidence of dermatitis [23].

An unexpected finding of our study was the lack of association between RT fractionation and the severity of radiation dermatitis, despite consistent evidence from large randomized trials and meta-analyses showing that moderate hypofractionation reduces acute toxicity in breast cancer [24–27]. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, the concurrent use of capecitabine may attenuate the differential toxicity profile typically observed between conventional and hypofractionated schedules, as fluoropyrimidines can potentiate radiation-induced skin reactions [7]. Second, our cohort consisted exclusively of patients with residual TNBC following neoadjuvant chemotherapy, a population with larger tumors, more extensive surgery, and more frequent nodal irradiation—features independently associated with dermatitis and potentially overshadowing the influence of fractionation. Third, heterogeneity in technique, bolus use, and chest wall irradiation across centers may further dilute fractionation-related differences. Thus, while fractionation is an important determinant of toxicity in standard adjuvant RT, its impact may be less pronounced in the setting of concurrent capecitabine.

In addition, the clinical rationale for using capecitabine concurrently with RT warrants further elaboration. Although the CREATE-X trial administered capecitabine sequentially, concurrent delivery may offer theoretical benefits by avoiding a prolonged interruption in systemic therapy among patients with residual TNBC, a group at high risk of early recurrence. The potential radiosensitizing properties of capecitabine further support this approach, although prospective evidence remains limited.

Several prospective studies have investigated the feasibility of concurrent capecitabine and RT in breast cancer. Woodward et al. conducted a phase II trial of preoperative capecitabine with concurrent RT in locally advanced breast cancer, demonstrating acceptable toxicity and encouraging local control [13]. Similarly, Gaui et al. evaluated second-line neoadjuvant capecitabine with concurrent RT in anthracycline-resistant disease and reported manageable adverse events [12]. A randomized controlled trial from Egypt also showed that concurrent capecitabine with hypofractionated post-mastectomy RT was safe and feasible, further supporting this strategy [28]. More recently, the DEGRO expert group comprehensively reviewed the evidence surrounding sequential versus concurrent administration of capecitabine and antibody-drug conjugates during RT, emphasizing that simultaneous treatment may be preferable in selected high-risk settings [29]. Collectively, these studies reinforce that concurrent capecitabine-RT is feasible, though data remain limited and heterogeneous. Our findings contribute substantially to this growing body of evidence by providing real-world outcomes from one of the largest cohorts reported to date.

More recently, the largest cohort study to date comparing concurrent versus sequential administration of capecitabine with RT was published by Kim et al. [16]. With a median follow-up of 41.8 months, the study demonstrated significantly improved disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) in the concurrent RT plus capecitabine group. Specifically, DFS increased from 64% to 75%, and OS improved from 78% to 87%, with both differences reaching statistical significance (p = 0.045 and p = 0.006, respectively). Importantly, the incidence of grade ≥ 2 toxicities did not differ significantly between the two groups. These findings support the clinical relevance of concurrent RT and capecitabine administration as a potentially more effective treatment strategy without compromising safety.

The optimal dose of capecitabine in breast cancer remains uncertain and continues to be debated. Although the conventional regimen of 1,250 mg/m² twice daily was historically established in early phase studies, emerging evidence suggests that lower doses may provide comparable efficacy with improved tolerability. For example, a phase II study in metastatic breast cancer demonstrated that reduced-dose capecitabine (1,000 mg/m² twice daily) achieved response rates similar to standard dosing while substantially reducing treatment-related toxicity, particularly hand–foot syndrome and gastrointestinal adverse events [30, 31]. More recently, pharmacologic analyses have further highlighted the complex pharmacokinetics of capecitabine and the large interpatient variability in drug metabolism, influenced by factors such as DPYD genotype, renal function, body composition, and treatment adherence, making the identification of a universally optimal dose challenging [32]. Consequently, there remains no clear exposure–efficacy relationship, and excessive dosing may paradoxically compromise treatment efficacy by increasing toxicity-related interruptions. Taken together, these findings underscore the ongoing controversy regarding the ideal capecitabine dose in breast cancer and highlight the need for prospective dose-optimization studies and pharmacogenetically guided treatment strategies to better individualize therapy.

Our findings are generally consistent with the existing literature, particularly regarding the associations between dermatitis risk and factors such as bolus use, total lymphatic irradiation, and advanced clinical tumor stage. However, the retrospective multicenter design of this study necessitates cautious interpretation of the results. Several important confounding variables—including breast size, body mass index (BMI), prophylactic skin-care practices, and detailed dosimetric parameters—were not uniformly available across participating centers and may have influenced toxicity outcomes. In addition, substantial heterogeneity existed in RT-related variables, including treatment techniques, fractionation schedules, nodal target volumes, bolus application, and surgical approaches. Furthermore, detailed dosimetric information such as hotspot distribution, breast/chest-wall dose homogeneity, skin dose metrics, and organ-at-risk constraints could not be consistently collected.

Another important limitation is the absence of centralized toxicity review. Because dermatitis grading was performed by different treating physicians across participating institutions using retrospective CTCAE-based assessments, interobserver variability may have influenced the reported toxicity rates. Moreover, toxicity evaluation relied exclusively on physician-reported CTCAE grading without patient-reported outcome or quality-of-life assessment; therefore, subjective symptomatic burden may have been underestimated.

The absence of a control group receiving sequential capecitabine and RT further limits the ability to make direct comparisons or determine the independent contribution of concurrent capecitabine administration to observed toxicities. In addition, detailed treatment-compliance parameters—including interruption duration, cumulative RT prolongation, relative dose intensity, and delivered cumulative capecitabine exposure—were not uniformly available because of the retrospective multicenter design.

The median follow-up duration was also relatively limited for the assessment of late radiation-related adverse effects. Consequently, important long-term endpoints such as fibrosis, cosmetic outcomes, cardiac toxicity, pulmonary toxicity, and lymphedema could not be reliably evaluated. Therefore, the present study should primarily be interpreted as an analysis of acute treatment-related toxicity.

Another important consideration is fluoropyrimidine-related pharmacogenetic toxicity risk. During the study period, routine pretreatment DPYD genotyping was not uniformly implemented across participating centers. However, recent regulatory recommendations and emerging clinical practice guidelines increasingly support DPYD-guided fluoropyrimidine dosing to reduce severe treatment-related toxicities. Therefore, the absence of systematic DPYD testing represents an additional limitation of the present study.

Despite these limitations, the sample size of our cohort is substantially larger than those reported in previous studies, allowing us to provide more robust real-world observational toxicity data. Collectively, our findings suggest that concurrent capecitabine administration during RT demonstrated a manageable acute toxicity profile in this retrospective multicenter TNBC cohort.

Conclusion

This multicenter retrospective study provides descriptive real-world toxicity data regarding concurrent capecitabine and adjuvant RT in patients with TNBC. The incidence of high-grade toxicities was low, with only a small proportion of patients requiring dose modifications or discontinuation. Our findings align with and extend the existing literature by providing evidence from one of the largest retrospective cohorts to date on this topic. Importantly, multivariable analysis identified advanced clinical T stage (cT3-4) as the sole independent predictor of grade 2–3 dermatitis, while total lymphatic irradiation and bolus application were significant predictors of grade 3 dermatitis after Firth penalized likelihood analysis [33].

Despite the limitations inherent in the retrospective and multicentric design, and the lack of a control group, our study contributes valuable real-world data supporting the tolerability of concurrent capecitabine and RT in this high-risk population. Future prospective randomized trials are warranted to validate these findings and to further define optimal patient selection and treatment protocols for concurrent administration.
