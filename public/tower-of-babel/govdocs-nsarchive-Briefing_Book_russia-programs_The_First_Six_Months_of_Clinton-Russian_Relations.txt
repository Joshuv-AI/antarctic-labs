<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>President Clinton calls his Russian counterpart to inform him about the Americans’ new “more active” policy on Bosnia and to invite Russia to cooperate closely with the United States. He tells Yeltsin that the U.S. full-time representative for Bosnia talks, Reginald Bartholomew, will travel to Moscow to brief Yeltsin about U.S. thinking and to “get your thoughts first hand.” Clinton wants to strengthen the Vance-Owen plan for de facto partition of the former Yugoslavia (negotiated by former U.S. Secretary of State Cyrus Vance and former British Foreign Minister David Owen) and to enforce the “no-fly” zone over Bosnia, preventing Serbian bombing. <div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>This memo from National Security Adviser Anthony Lake outlines U.S. goals for the first Clinton summit with the President of Russia and explains the Russian domestic context. Clinton is going to meet “a survivor of the Communist political system” who just experienced “two of the most tumultuous weeks in Russian political life since August 1991.” The parliament tried but failed to impeach Yeltsin, and now he is facing a country-wide referendum—a vote of confidence—on his leadership and his economic reforms. Yeltsin needs the summit to boost his domestic standing and to bring back U.S. commitments to provide economic assistance. But, in a larger perspective, what he needs is a close partnership with the U.S. where the two countries could cooperate as equals. Lake stresses the need to cooperate on foreign policy and security issues and points to areas where Russian cooperation is vital, such as “Yugoslavia, Iraq, North Korea or Somalia.” Lake advises the President that “the first point you should be stressing is that this is very much a meeting of equals.” He prepares Clinton for possible “surprises,” such as a possible inability to conclude the agreement on highly enriched uranium and the “space trade agreement” granting Russia access to the commercial space launch market.</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>This is the second meeting of the first summit between Clinton and Yeltsin (the earlier one-on-one is still, astoundingly, classified at the Clinton Presidential Library, 30 years later). According to the account published in Strobe Talbott’s 2002 memoir, the first “restricted” session featured discussion of the U.S. aid package to Russia, during which Yeltsin specifically asked for assistance with housing for officers who were being brought back to Russia from Europe and the Baltics. “Tents, Bill! Can you imagine? They’re living in tents!”<a href="#_edn3" name="_ednref3">[3]</a></p>stance with housing for officers who were being brought back to Russia from Europe and the Baltics. “Tents, Bill! Can you imagine? They’re living in tents!”<a href="#_edn3" name="_ednref3">[3]</a></p>

<p>After the first meeting, the delegations enjoyed a boat ride around Vancouver Island. Talbott remembers Yeltsin downing many drinks and says that, since that moment, “[k]eeping count of Yeltsin’s intake was to become a standard feature of summitry.” Nonetheless, the Russian president was quite eloquent by the time of the working dinner.</p>

<p>The expanded meeting is mainly devoted to economic issues. Yeltsin is accompanied by his young finance minister, Boris Fedorov. Fedorov describes his financial reform plan that focuses on reining in inflation and trying to move the Central Bank to support the reform and not bow to the parliament. Fedorov already had a separate meeting with U.S. Secretary of the Treasury Lloyd Bentsen focused on the Russian economic reform known as “shock therapy” and possible ways for the Clinton administration to support it. The Russians are counting on financing from the G-7, which will be meeting in Japan in April and then with Yeltsin in July. Clinton promises to expand the U.S. aid package and to encourage direct investment in Russia. Yeltsin expresses very optimistic views regarding the prospects of Russian economic reform in the next five years: “I am certain that in five years we will be competing in the arts, in jazz, in oil, and we will catch up with you.” He later amends, maybe not jazz.</p>Russian economic reform in the next five years: “I am certain that in five years we will be competing in the arts, in jazz, in oil, and we will catch up with you.” He later amends, maybe not jazz.</p>

<p>The conversation is relaxed, very friendly and full of jokes. Yeltsin makes clear his disdain for his vice president, Alexander Rutskoy, and the Russian parliament. He has just had a showdown with them on an impeachment vote and is campaigning against them in a crucial referendum scheduled for April 25. His U.S. interlocutors agree with him and give advice on engaging the public before the referendum. Among the important outcomes of this session is the agreement to set up the Gore-Chernomyrdin (the U.S. vice president and the Russian prime minister) commission that would become an extremely successful format for problem solving at a sub-presidential level.</p>

<p>At the end of the meeting, the presidents discuss the situation in Bosnia and the rejection of the Vance-Owen plan by the Serbs notwithstanding Russian pressure. Clinton proposes to lift the arms embargo for Bosnia, but Yeltsin is not ready to agree. He wants Kozyrev to make one more effort to reach a diplomatic solution. Yeltsin believes that the “arms in that area can make for thousands of deaths, especially if you know the Moslems.” Clinton responds, “They kill each other at abandon.”</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>This is truly a marathon conversation—Yeltsin calculated that they covered 52 questions in three hours. What was intended to be a conversation about security expanded to cover all the important issues in U.S.-Russian relations. Yeltsin, in front of his team, goes on the attack by demanding a repeal of the Cold War-era Jackson-Vanik amendment and export restrictions imposed by the Coordinating Committee for Multilateral Export Controls (COCOM). COCOM is especially painful for the new Russian economy, as it prevents other countries from supplying vitally important technologies that Russia needs to address its technological backwardness. Yeltsin brings with him “five pages of material outlining restraints on Russia.” In addition, Ambassador Vladimir Lukin points to the “Captive Nations” law, referring to the Baltics, and says “we’re treated as a communist power while China is not.”</p>ining restraints on Russia.” In addition, Ambassador Vladimir Lukin points to the “Captive Nations” law, referring to the Baltics, and says “we’re treated as a communist power while China is not.”</p>

<p>Ironically, in this conversation, the Russian representatives argue for free market competition in defense production while the U.S. is not willing to allow such a capitalist element in U.S.-Russian relations. One of the few fields where Russia is competitive with the U.S. is the defense industry, and to keep it alive, Yeltsin argues Russia should be able to sell weapons abroad and wants Clinton to “agree that firms should be able to compete directly on market terms, with quality and price determining who wins.” Clinton promises to “look into” those questions but, as he pointed out in his memoirs, he could not spend his political capital to try to repeal the Cold War legislation while at the&nbsp;same time putting together any kind of large aid package for Russia.<a href="#_edn4" name="_ednref4">[4]</a></p>

<p>Yeltsin is brimming with concrete proposals for cooperation in space and in defense, but the Cold War legislation interferes with most of them. Both sides are committed to the highly enriched uranium (HEU) proposal known as “Megatons to Megawatts—in which HEU taken from demobilized nuclear weapons is down-blended into fuel rods—but the intense negotiations between Russian Minister of Atomic Energy Viktor Mikhailov and U.S. Undersecretary of State Lynn Davis held the day before failed to produce any compromise, mainly because of the low price the United States is willing to pay for the down-blended uranium. Mikhailov interjects, saying, “We dream of free competition, especially with high value-added products and technologies in U.S. markets.”</p>ted States is willing to pay for the down-blended uranium. Mikhailov interjects, saying, “We dream of free competition, especially with high value-added products and technologies in U.S. markets.”</p>

<p>Yeltsin tries to persuade Clinton to agree to a ban on nuclear testing or at least to the extension of the moratorium until 1994. Moving to arms control, the Russian president tells his counterpart that the Russians have already destroyed 3,000 nuclear warheads and were “working to destroy our heavy missiles.” On biological weapons, Yeltsin says “we shut all the doors, and the personnel were dispersed to other institutions.” He invites U.S. inspection teams to “come and look whenever you want […] any time and in any town […] There is no longer any sin on our souls.” On missile defense, he proposes building a joint defense system “that is two times cheaper than it otherwise would have been.” One proposal that grabs Clinton’s attention is to put nuclear weapons on a “not combat-ready” status and to “give thought to taking multiple warheads off” alert status. Yeltsin wants to do it all fast, while he is still president, and Clinton agrees they should hurry.</p>

<p>Clinton moves the conversation to other global issues where the U.S. needs Russian cooperation: North Korea, the Middle East peace process, Iraq, Libya, and Bosnia. Yeltsin agrees to support all the UN resolutions and U.S. initiatives and to cut ties with former Soviet clients, even with Iraq, although it owes Russia $2.5 billion. The Russian president is at the same time extremely cooperative and also very demanding on issues where the U.S. cannot immediately deliver.</p>, even with Iraq, although it owes Russia $2.5 billion. The Russian president is at the same time extremely cooperative and also very demanding on issues where the U.S. cannot immediately deliver.</p>

<p>In an unprecedented way, Clinton and Yeltsin discuss what they would say during the closing press conference to present their desired image of a full partnership and unanimity on all the issues they discussed. The tone of the meeting is cheerful and friendly with light jokes and a genuine commitment to find mutually beneficial solutions.</p>

The results led to early elections for a new parliament (those would take place in December 1993) but not for the presidency (those Yeltsin would postpone until 1996). This is a very personal call that characterizes the emerging U.S.-Russian relationship. Yeltsin says that the Russian people were pleased with the summit in Vancouver and understood Clinton’s sincerity; they felt “close to [Clinton] as the President of the United States.” Clinton says he “felt close to the Russian people too.”</p>

From the outset, Clinton asks Yeltsin to focus on the Russian deal to supply missile technologies to India. Without Russia canceling the deal, the U.S. might have to apply sanctions, and Clinton would be limited in his ability to provide financial assistance. This issue is very difficult for Yeltsin because, as he explains, the contract was signed by the Soviet Union, not Russia, and Russia “just cannot break this agreement, [Russia] cannot break [her] relations with India.” The agreement is also very important to the Russian defense industry, which is trying to maintain production and keep its highly trained specialists. Clinton pushes hard on this issue and links it to the decision to give Russia access to the market for space launches and oil and gas investment.</p>

<p>Yeltsin wants to leave the issue of proliferation to experts and instead talk about the “practical integration of Russia into the world economy.” He attempts, unsuccessfully, to persuade Clinton to sign a declaration “not of the seven leaders but of the eight leaders” in Tokyo, essentially giving Russia a de facto membership. The presidents decide that a delegation consisting of Lynn Davis, Strobe Talbott and the vice president’s national security adviser, Leon Fuerth, would come to Russia to negotiate the India missile technology issue and Russian relations with Iraq and Iran.</p>avis, Strobe Talbott and the vice president’s national security adviser, Leon Fuerth, would come to Russia to negotiate the India missile technology issue and Russian relations with Iraq and Iran.</p>

<p>Clinton believes that the G-7 meeting will be very productive for Russia, that the G-7 would agree on providing a privatization fund (but smaller than they hoped), and that the IMF would approve $1.5 billion in aid even before the meeting in Tokyo. Clinton also assures the Russian president that his administration was working on repealing all Cold War legislation, including the Jackson-Vanik amendment.</p>

<p>Yeltsin is pleased to hear these strong statements of support in advance of the G-7 meeting but expresses his main worry to Clinton: “I wouldn’t like to be in a situation […] like Gorbachev was in when he attended the summit in London [in July 1991], where he came away with nothing.” Clinton reassures his Russian partner: “That won’t happen.”</p>
