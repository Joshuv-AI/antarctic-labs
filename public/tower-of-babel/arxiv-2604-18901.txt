Harmful Intent as a Geometrically Recoverable Feature of LLM Residual Streams
Isaac Llorente-Saguer

Abstract
Harmful intent is geometrically recoverable from large language model residual streams: as a linear direction in most layers, and as angular deviation in layers where projection methods fail. Across 12 models spanning four architectural families (Qwen2.5, Qwen3.5, Llama-3.2, Gemma-3) and three alignment variants (base, instruction-tuned, abliterated), under single-turn, English evaluation, we characterise this geometry through six direction-finding strategies. Three succeed: a soft-AUC-optimised linear direction ( 𝐰 opt \mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{opt}} ) reaches mean AUROC 0.982 0.982 and TPR@1%FPR 0.797 0.797 ; a class-mean probe ( 𝐰 LDA \mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}} ) reaches 0.975 0.975 and 0.706 0.706 at < 1 <\!1 ms fitting cost; a supervised angular-deviation strategy reaches AUROC 0.962 0.962 and TPR of 0.607 0.607 along a representationally distinct direction ( 73 ∘ 73^{\circ} from projection-based solutions), uniquely sustaining detection in middle layers where projection methods collapse. Zero-shot and surface baselines reach TPR below 0.05 0.05 on all models. Detection remains stable across alignment variants, including abliterated models from which refusal behaviour has been surgically removed. Removing the mechanism that makes a model refuse does not remove the representation that identifies what it is being asked: harmful intent and refusal are functionally dissociated. A direction fitted on AdvBench transfers to held-out HarmBench and JailbreakBench with AUROC ≥ 0.961 \geq 0.961 on average across models. The same picture holds at larger scale: across Qwen3.5 at 0.8B, 2B, 4B, and 9B parameters, AUROC remains ≥ 0.98 \geq 0.98 , cross-variant transfer stays within 0.018 0.018 AUROC of own-direction performance, and the 9B variant exhibits the cleanest cross-variant transfer in our entire experimental set. This is consistent with a simple account: models acquire a linearly decodable representation of harmful intent as part of general language understanding, and alignment then shapes what they do with such inputs without reorganising the upstream recognition signal. As a practical consequence, AUROC in the 0.97 0.97 + regime can substantially overestimate operational detectability; TPR@ 1 % 1\% FPR should accompany AUROC in safety-adjacent evaluation. Code and reproduction scripts at https://github.com/isaac-6/harm-directions .



1
Introduction

Transformer residual streams appear to encode semantic properties
of input text as approximately linear directions in activation
space
(
14
;
5
;
16
)
.
For safety-relevant concepts, this structure has direct practical
implications: if harmful intent is linearly decodable from a
single layer, a lightweight probe over cached activations can
flag harmful prompts before generation begins, without a second
model and without significant latency overhead.

Recent work has begun to map this structure.

1
identified a refusal direction whose
ablation releases base-model behaviour in aligned models.

21
showed, through steering and
reply-inversion experiments on instruction-tuned 7–8B models,
that harmfulness and refusal occupy distinct linear subspaces at
distinct token positions, and proposed using the harmfulness
direction as an intrinsic input-side safeguard.
Together with broader representation-engineering
work
(
23
)
, these results establish that
harmful intent is linearly decodable from transformer activations.

Three questions remain open.
First, does the harm direction generalise across architectures
and alignment variants, or does each model encode harm in a
model-specific way?
Mechanistic evidence to date has been concentrated at a single
scale and family.
Second, what is the geometric relationship between the harm
direction and the dominant variance of safe-prompt activations?
Zero-shot methods that use benign references alone achieve
limited discriminability
(
10
)
,
suggesting the two may be nearly orthogonal, but the geometry has
not been systematically characterised.
Third, how reliable is activation-based detection under realistic
deployment constraints, where false alarms must remain extremely
low?
Prior activation-based detection work primarily reports ranking
metrics (AUROC) rather than operating-point metrics
(TPR@
1\%
FPR)
(
11
;
9
;
4
)
,
and ranking performance in the
0.97
+ regime can mask substantial
operational variation.

This paper addresses all three.
We evaluate six direction-fitting strategies on 12 models spanning
four architectural families (Qwen2.5, Qwen3.5, Llama-3.2,
Gemma-3) and three alignment variants (base, instruction-tuned,
and abliterated), under a strict three-way data split that
separates fitting, validation, and evaluation at the dataset
level. We further extend the evaluation to Qwen3.5 across a 0.8–9B parameter range to assess whether the findings hold at multiple and larger scales.
Two supervised projection strategies, the class-mean difference
and a soft-AUC-optimised direction, recover a stable harm
direction with only 100 labelled examples per class.
A supervised angular-deviation strategy achieves comparable AUROC
along a representationally distinct direction and recovers harm
in layers where projection-based methods collapse.
We characterise the directions’ geometry, evaluate their transfer
across datasets and alignment variants, and report operating-point
metrics alongside AUROC to expose gaps that ranking metrics hide.

The resulting picture is consistent with the following account:
transformer models acquire a linearly decodable representation of
harmful intent as part of general language understanding, and
alignment preserves this decodability. In three of our four
families alignment leaves the direction largely in place; in
Gemma-3 it rotates the representation substantially while keeping
it linearly recoverable. Safety training shapes what models do
with harmful inputs; it does not eliminate the upstream signal that
identifies them.
The practical consequence is a detector that is cheap to fit,
cheap to run, interpretable as a single direction, and resilient
to the interventions most likely to hide the signal.

Contributions.

1.

Cross-architecture, cross-alignment evaluation.

We evaluate linear harm-direction recovery across 12
models (four architectural families, three alignment
variants, including abliterated models), and show that
detection performance is stable across alignment
interventions, including the surgical removal of
refusal behaviour, establishing that harm and refusal
are functionally dissociated representations.

2.

Geometric characterisation.

The harm direction is nearly orthogonal to the leading
principal component of benign-prompt activations (
82^{\circ}

on average), and a supervised angular-deviation strategy
recovers harm along a representationally distinct
direction, revealing a second geometric mode through which harm is detectable that dominates in layers where projection-based
detection fails.

3.

Operational evaluation and cross-dataset
generalisation.

We report TPR@
1\%
FPR alongside AUROC and show that the
two can diverge substantially even in the AUROC
0.97
+
regime; low-FPR metrics should accompany AUROC in
safety-adjacent evaluation.
A direction fitted on AdvBench transfers to held-out HarmBench and JailbreakBench with AUROC
\geq 0.961
on average across models.

4.

Scale stability.
Across Qwen3.5 at 0.8B, 2B, 4B, and 9B parameters, an
11\times
range, AUROC remains
\geq 0.98
on all variants and cross-variant transfer remains within
0.008
AUROC of own-direction performance. The 9B variant exhibits the cleanest transfer in our entire experimental set (all off-diagonal AUROCs within
0.002
of the diagonal), suggesting the harm representation becomes more entrenched with scale rather than more fragile.

2
Background and related work

2.1
Linear representations and activation-based probing

Transformer residual streams encode high-level semantic properties
in approximately linear subspaces.

14
observed linear arithmetic in word
embeddings;
5
formalised a superposition
account of how many concepts can share a representational space.

16
demonstrated linear encoding of emotion,
gender, and factual properties in LLM activations.

23
introduced
"representation engineering", deriving control vectors for
safety-relevant concepts by contrasting activations of positive
and negative examples, and demonstrated steering applications.
These results collectively establish the Linear Representation
Hypothesis
(
5
;
15
)
: many
interpretable concepts correspond to linear directions in
activation space, making projection-based probing a natural tool.

Closest to our work within this thread,

1
showed that a refusal direction
exists in aligned-model residual streams and can be surgically
removed by projecting it out; this is the procedure that produces
the abliterated model variants we evaluate.
This identifies a specific linear feature of safety-trained
behaviour.
Our work examines a distinct feature: the representation of
harmful intent in the input, rather than the downstream
decision to refuse.
Unlike prior activation-based work, which has focused primarily on
output steering and behavioural control, we treat harmful intent
as a geometric property of input activations and evaluate
cross-dataset detection under operating-point constraints.

2.2
Harmful intent and refusal as separately encoded features

21
provide the most direct antecedent to
the present work.
Using causal steering and reply-inversion experiments on
instruction-tuned 7–8B models (Llama-3-8B-Instruct,
Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct), they showed that harmfulness and refusal
occupy distinct linear subspaces at distinct token positions.
They proposed "Latent Guard", an input-side safeguard that
uses the harmfulness direction at the last instruction token.
The mechanistic evidence in that paper establishes the separability
of the two features within individual aligned models.

The present paper is complementary.
Where
21
characterise the mechanistic
relationship between harmfulness and refusal at a single scale,
we evaluate detection reliability across a wider model set (12
models, four architectural families, three alignment variants
including abliterated), across datasets (AdvBench for fitting;
HarmBench, JailbreakBench, and XSTest held out), and under
operating-point metrics (TPR@
1\%
FPR alongside AUROC).
Our finding that detection is stable across abliterated variants
constitutes independent evaluation-level evidence for the
functional dissociation that
21

establish mechanistically.

2.3
Linear probing and a direct predecessor

Linear probing via discriminant-style directions has a long
history in neural analysis.
The Fisher Linear Discriminant is the optimal classifier for two
Gaussian classes with shared covariance, and discriminant-style
probes have been used to localise factual
knowledge
(
6
;
13
)
and
syntactic structure
(
19
)
.
In the harm-detection setting we find, consistent with this
tradition, that the class-mean difference recovers a direction
within
9^{\circ}
of a soft-AUC-optimised
direction
(
2
)
, and that both recover
stable harm directions across models.

The direct predecessor to the present work is LatentBiopsy
(
10
)
, which introduced a
zero-shot angular-deviation detector using only normative
references.
The present paper extends that framework in three ways:
(i) by introducing supervised direction-finding methods, both
projection-based and angle-based, that substantially outperform
the zero-shot baseline;
(ii) by expanding the model set from 6 to 12 and adding the
abliterated alignment variant;
(iii) by introducing cross-dataset held-out evaluation (HarmBench,
JailbreakBench, XSTest) where the original work evaluated on a
single harmful source.

Other contemporaneous work.

Several recent papers detect harmful or undesirable input
properties via activation differences, including work on truth
probing
(
11
)
and inference-time
intervention
(
9
;
4
)
.
These focus on output steering or internal-state probing rather
than pre-generation detection, and they report ranking metrics
rather than operating-point performance.
A full head-to-head comparison with dedicated classifiers addresses
a different research question and is discussed in Section
5.6
.

3
Methods

3.1
Setup and notation

Residual-stream activations aggregate information from attention
and MLP sublayers, which makes them a common locus for probing
semantic directions
(
5
;
23
)
.

Let
h_{\ell,t,d}
denote the
d
-th dimension of the residual stream
at layer
\ell
and token position
t
, and let

f_{\ell}(\mathbf{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
denote the element-wise max-pooled
activation in response to prompt
\mathbf{p}
:

f_{\ell}(\mathbf{p})_{d}=\max_{t}h_{\ell,t,d}
.
Max pooling produces a fixed-dimensional summary by retaining the
maximum activation in each residual-stream dimension across all token
positions.
Given a fit set

\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{H}}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{+}\}_{i=1}^{n_{+}}

of harmful activations and

\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{N}}=\{\mathbf{x}_{j}^{-}\}_{j=1}^{n_{-}}

of benign activations at layer
\ell
, we seek a unit-norm
direction
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
whose scalar projection

s(\mathbf{p})=f_{\ell}(\mathbf{p})\cdot\mathbf{w}

provides a reliable harmful-intent score.

Prompt normalisation.

All prompts are normalised by stripping terminal punctuation
characters (
[.,;:!?]
) before any processing.
AdvBench harmful prompts uniformly terminate with a full stop while
Alpaca instructions typically do not; without this step, a trivial
punctuation classifier can achieve near-perfect separation between
the two sources.

3.4
Activation extraction and caching

Activations are extracted using forward hooks on each transformer
block’s output, max-pooled over the token dimension, and cached
to disk per model.
Caching ensures that all direction strategies operate on identical
activation tensors and avoids repeated forward passes during
ablations.
Direction fitting and scoring are pure NumPy operations on the
cached tensors.
All experiments are seeded globally (Python, NumPy, PyTorch) at
seed 42 to ensure reproducibility.

3.5
Direction strategies

We evaluate six direction strategies spanning zero-shot to
gradient-optimised.
They fall into two families:
projection-based
methods, which
score prompts by signed projection onto a direction, and

angle-based
methods (marked
\theta
), which use unsigned
angular deviation.

PC1 (normative),
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{PC1}}
.

The leading principal component of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{N}}
.
Zero-shot: requires no harmful examples.

\theta
-normative (zero-shot).

The reference direction is the normalised normative centroid

\mu_{\mathcal{N}}/\|\mu_{\mathcal{N}}\|
and the score is the unsigned angular
deviation

s(\mathbf{p})=\arccos\!\bigl(\bar{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{p})\cdot\bar{\mu}_{\mathcal{N}}\bigr)
, where
\bar{(\cdot)}
denotes
\ell_{2}

normalisation.
Zero-shot: requires no harmful examples.
This is the detection function of the original LatentBiopsy
system
(
10
)
.

Mean difference,
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
.

\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}=\frac{\hat{\mu}_{\mathcal{H}}-\hat{\mu}_{\mathcal{N}}}{\|\hat{\mu}_{\mathcal{H}}-\hat{\mu}_{\mathcal{N}}\|},

(1)

where
\hat{\mu}_{\mathcal{H}}
and
\hat{\mu}_{\mathcal{N}}
are the sample class
means (harmful and benign, respectively).
Under the assumption of equal spherical within-class covariances

\Sigma_{+}=\Sigma_{-}=\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}
, this is the Fisher
Linear Discriminant direction; in that regime,
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
is a
sufficient statistic for two-class discrimination.
Fitting cost is
O((n_{+}+n_{-})\,D)
.

Soft-AUC optimised,
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{opt}}
.

We maximise a soft-AUC surrogate (a sigmoid-smoothed approximation
to the Mann–Whitney
U

statistic
(
2
)
) via Riemannian gradient
ascent on the unit sphere:

\hat{U}(\mathbf{w})=\frac{1}{n_{+}n_{-}}\sum_{i\in+,\,j\in-}\sigma\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{w}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j})}{\tau}\right),

(2)

where
\sigma
is the logistic sigmoid and
\tau=1.0
is a
temperature parameter.
We warm-start from
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
and optimise with projected gradient
steps on the unit sphere.
Implementation details (Riemannian gradient, early stopping,
numerical safeguards) are given in Appendix
A
.

\theta
two-class optimised.

The same optimisation procedure as
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{opt}}
, applied to the soft-AUC
of
angular
deviation scores

s(\mathbf{p})=\arccos(\bar{f}_{\ell}(\mathbf{p})\cdot\mathbf{w})

rather than projection scores.
Warm-started from the
\theta
-normative centroid direction.

Baselines.

Random
: a uniformly random unit vector drawn at seed 42,
providing a chance-level lower bound.

Perplexity
: the mean per-token negative log-likelihood of
the prompt under the same model whose activations are probed.
Prior work has shown that perplexity-based filters fail on fluent
harmful prompts
(
22
)
; we include this baseline
to verify that surface-level fluency carries no harm signal in our
evaluation setting.

3.6
Scoring, metrics, and layer selection

For projection-based strategies (
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{PC1}}
,
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
,
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{opt}}
) the
detection score is
s(\mathbf{p})=f_{\ell}(\mathbf{p})\cdot\mathbf{w}
; for angle-based strategies (
\theta
-normative,

\theta
two-class) it is the unsigned angular deviation defined
above.
Because some strategies may produce scores negatively correlated
with harm depending on the layer and direction, we apply a sign
correction
s\leftarrow-s
when AUROC
<0.5
; the reported

effective AUROC
is

\max(\mathrm{AUROC},\,1-\mathrm{AUROC})
.

We report two primary metrics.

Effective AUROC
summarises discriminability across all
thresholds.

TPR@1%FPR
is the true-positive rate at a false-positive
rate of 1%, reflecting deployment constraints where false alarms
must remain extremely low. TPR@1%FPR is estimated from the empirical ROC curve (scikit-learn’s roc_curve) by linear interpolation at the operating FPR = 0.01.
For each model and strategy we compute stratified bootstrap 95%
confidence intervals (1,000 resamples, stratified by evaluation
source to preserve dataset proportions) for both metrics.

Layer selection.

The operating layer is selected using the validation set
(50 harmful + 50 benign, disjoint from both the fit and
evaluation sets).
At each layer
\ell
, the mean-difference direction
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
is
fitted on the fit set and scored on the validation set;
the operating layer is

\ell^{*}=\argmax_{\ell}\mathrm{AUROC}_{\mathrm{val}}(\ell)
.
All strategies are then fitted and evaluated at this single shared
layer per model variant.
A shared operating layer avoids per-strategy layer confounds and
ensures that direction angles are comparable across methods.

\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
is used for layer selection because it is computationally
inexpensive, avoiding gradient optimisation at every layer.

Inference and fitting cost

On an NVIDIA RTX 3070 Mobile for Qwen2.5-0.5B, the marginal cost
of detection over an ongoing forward pass is
4
ms (activation
hook) plus
0.006
ms (score computation). A standalone pipeline
that runs the forward pass solely for detection takes
26
ms
end to end. Offline fitting consists of 200 forward passes
(
5.2
s) plus the direction computation:
<0.1
ms for
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}

and
6.6
s for
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{opt}}
at
D=896
. In deployments where the
model is already generating, both supervised projection strategies
add essentially no latency. Reproducing the main results (layer selection, fitting and inference)
across all 12 models takes
\approx 36

5
Discussion

5.1
Harmful intent is linearly recoverable in the residual stream

The two supervised projection strategies achieve mean AUROC above

0.97
using only 100 labelled examples per class, across 12 models
and four architectural families.
The mean-difference direction
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
lies a mean of
9.1^{\circ}
of the
Soft-AUC-optimised direction
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{opt}}
(maximum
24.7^{\circ}
across all 12 models), and the
two strategies achieve nearly identical AUROC (a difference of

0.007
).
This angular proximity implies that
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
is close to the AUROC-optimal direction under our objective, despite being derived from
first-moment statistics alone.
Linear separability of harmful intent is thus a robust property of
residual-stream activations, not an artefact of any particular
fitting procedure.
Because Soft-AUC is warm-started from
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
, the small angle does not directly establish that

\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
is near the AUROC-optimal direction; the optimiser may simply have stayed near its initialisation. What the angle does show is that the gradient descent moves
\approx 9^{\circ}

when starting from the first-moment estimate; combined with the small AUROC gap of
0.008
(Main results
4.1
), this is consistent with

\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
sitting near a broad basin of comparable-AUROC solutions under the Soft-AUC objective. A stronger test would compare

\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
to a Soft-AUC direction initialised from scratch or from random directions; we leave this to future work.

The representational geometry of harmful intent, however, varies
substantially across models.
The angle between the normative and harmful first principal
components ranges from
39^{\circ}
(Llama-3.2) to
78^{\circ}
(Qwen3.5),
reflecting qualitatively different class geometries.
That a single algorithm (
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
) nonetheless recovers near-optimal directions across this heterogeneous set suggests the
linear-discriminability property is resilient to the specific shape
of the class manifolds.

Where
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
falls short, it does so in ways that are not predicted
by its own theoretical assumption.

\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
assumes equal spherical within-class covariance; one would
therefore expect
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{opt}}
to improve over
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
most in models
where this assumption is most violated.
Computing the effective rank of within-class covariance
(
(\sum\lambda_{i})^{2}/\sum\lambda_{i}^{2}
) at the operating layer
for all 12 models, we find no such relationship.
Gemma-3 base exhibits the largest between-class rank asymmetry
(
41.2
normative vs
31.7
harmful) yet a negligible TPR gap
between the two strategies (
0.003
); Qwen2.5-instruct exhibits
near-equal ranks (
31.9
vs
31.0
) yet the largest TPR gap
(
0.190
).
Whatever drives the Soft-AUC advantage, it is not
covariance mismatch in the direction the theory predicts.
The sample-efficiency pattern (Figure
7
) is
consistent with this: the
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{opt}}
advantage grows with
n
and is
negligible at
n=10
, suggesting
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{opt}}
exploits
higher-order structure that only stabilises with sufficient
data.
Identifying this structure is a natural next step.

5.2
AUROC and TPR@1%FPR measure different things

Every supervised strategy in our evaluation achieves AUROC above

0.96
, so AUROC alone would suggest the strategies are
interchangeable. TPR@1%FPR disagrees:

\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
reaches TPR
0.706
and
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{opt}}
reaches
0.797
, a gap that
far exceeds the
0.007
AUROC difference.
The most extreme case is PC1, which achieves AUROC above
0.6
on
every model but TPR below
0.04
: non-negligible ranking, but zero
operational utility.

AUROC integrates ranking quality across all thresholds, most of
which are irrelevant to deployment.
A safety detector cannot operate at
20\%
false-positive rate.
TPR@1%FPR, or analogous low-FPR metrics, measures what matters
operationally: whether the detector fires on real harm without
overwhelming the benign traffic with false alarms.
The two metrics are positively but loosely correlated across our
models (Figure
3
), and the correlation
weakens as AUROC approaches the ceiling, precisely the regime in
which the field is increasingly working.

The score distributions in Figure
4
make the
mechanism visible.
Qwen2.5-instruct under
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
achieves AUROC
0.975
, but the
right tail of the benign distribution extends into the harmful
distribution; the
1\%
FPR threshold falls inside this overlap
region, achieving a TPR of
0.654
.
Under
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{opt}}
the overlap shrinks and the threshold clears it,
lifting TPR from
0.654
to
0.834
.
Llama-3.2-instruct shows clean separation under both strategies
and the two give near-identical TPR (
0.857
).
The AUROC–TPR dissociation therefore reflects real structure in
the score distributions, not measurement artefact.
For the detection literature generally, we suggest that headline
AUROC numbers should be reported alongside at least one
low-FPR operating-point metric.

5.3
Harmful intent and refusal are functionally dissociated

Detection performance is stable across alignment variants.
For
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{opt}}
, the maximum within-family AUROC range is
0.013
;
for
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
it is
0.029
.
Abliterated models, those from which the refusal direction has
been removed
(
1
)
, detect
harmful intent as reliably as their instruction-tuned and base
counterparts.
Removing the mechanism that produces refusals does not eliminate the
residual
-
stream features that allow a probe to recognise the type of
instruction being issued.
Harmful intent and refusal, therefore, behave as functionally dissociated
features of the residual stream.
This is consistent with the findings
of
21
that harmfulness and refusal occupy
separate linear directions at distinct token positions.

A mechanistic interpretation is that harmful instructions are
semantically coherent text that appears abundantly in pretraining data,
so models learn to recognise the intent of a prompt as part of general
language understanding.
Safety training then modifies the output distribution to suppress
harmful completions without reorganising the upstream geometry that
encodes intent.
Abliteration perturbs this geometry, but the induced shift is smaller
than the base→instruct shift, and detection performance remains stable
across it.
Under this account, models acquire a harmful-intent representation as a by-product of language understanding, and alignment primarily shapes output behaviour without reorganising the upstream recognition signal. Our evidence is consistent with this account (preserved detection across alignment variants, and largely preserved direction geometry in three of four families). It does not rule out alternatives in which alignment reshapes the geometry while preserving linear separability, which is precisely what we observe in Gemma-3.

The cross-variant transfer experiment sharpens the picture.
For Qwen2.5, Qwen3.5, and Llama-3.2, a direction fitted on the base
model transfers to instruct and abliterated variants with at most

0.010
AUROC degradation and no sign reversals, despite
base–instruct angles ranging from
17^{\circ}
to
39^{\circ}
.
Gemma-3 is the exception: base and instruct directions diverge by

73^{\circ}
and transfer AUROC drops by up to
0.077
.
Yet each Gemma-3 variant’s own direction achieves comparable
detection performance (
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
AUROC
0.969
,
0.979
, and
0.977
for base,
instruct, and abliterated respectively).
Alignment in Gemma-3 geometrically transforms the harm
representation within activation space while preserving its linear
discriminability: the harm feature persists, but it has moved.
Two observations rule out pure rotation as the alignment mechanism.
First, own-direction AUROC varies across variants; a pure rotation
would preserve separability exactly, since rotations preserve inner
products. Second, cross-variant transfer is asymmetric: the base
direction scores
0.922
on instruct data, while the instruct
direction scores
0.889
on base data (bootstrap 95% CIs within

\pm 0.007
); a pure rotation would predict symmetric transfer.
Both observations are consistent with a non-rotational
transformation (rescaling, subspace restructuring, or partial
reorganisation), though our cross-variant metric cannot distinguish
among these alternatives.

Instruct and abliterated directions are consistently closer to each
other than either is to base (instruct–abliterated angles range
from
11^{\circ}
to
42^{\circ}
, and instruct
\rightarrow
abliterated
AUROC transfer degrades by at most
0.003
across all four
families).
Abliteration, therefore, modifies the harm representation only
incrementally beyond what instruction tuning has already done, even
in architectures where alignment substantially reorganises the
geometry.

These dissociation patterns (small cross-variant angles, clean AUROC transfer, preserved TPR for instruct
\rightarrow
abliterated) persist and strengthen at larger scales. At Qwen3.5-9B, every off-diagonal AUROC lies within 0.004 of the diagonal (Table
5
), the cleanest transfer in our experimental set. One interpretation: larger models may encode harmful intent more robustly, making alignment interventions relatively smaller perturbations to a more entrenched representation. The functional separation between harm and refusal, therefore, appears to be a stable property of transformer representations, not a small-model artefact.

5.4
Angular geometry is a complementary mode of harm representation

The
\theta
two-class strategy is perhaps the paper’s most unexpected
result.
It reaches AUROC
0.962\pm 0.027
across models, within range
of the supervised projection strategies, while operating on
unsigned angular deviation rather than signed projection.
Its direction vector lies at
73^{\circ}
from
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
on average, making it
representationally distinct and competitive.

The Qwen2.5 valley profile provides the cleanest evidence that
this distinctness is mechanistically meaningful.
All three Qwen2.5 variants show a 19-layer plateau where
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}

and
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{opt}}
degrade to AUROC just below
0.80
, reproducible across
base, instruct, and abliterated.
The
\theta
two-class optimiser does not share this degradation:
it sustains AUROC above
0.95
within the plateau.
The class information is present in these layers; it is simply
less recoverable by signed projection onto a linear direction.
At these depths, angular-deviation probes recover harm more
effectively than projection probes, indicating that the geometric
signal usable for detection changes form across the network.

One hypothesis is that Qwen2.5’s middle layers compress semantic
information into a distributed, high-dimensional code optimised
for next-token prediction rather than categorical discrimination.
The recovery at layers 21–22 would then correspond to the model
reconstructing categorical features needed for output generation.
The supervised angular strategy succeeds in the plateau because it
does not require an axis-aligned representation; any direction that
preserves the angular relationship between harmful and benign
manifolds suffices.
Testing this account directly, for example by measuring intrinsic
activation dimensionality across layers or by probing the
within-class angular structure of the plateau representations,
is a natural direction for future work.

The
\theta
two-class strategy’s TPR@1%FPR is lower than that of
the projection strategies (
0.607
vs
0.706
–
0.797
), so it
should not replace them operationally.
But it reveals that harm can be encoded in more than one geometric
mode, and it provides a tool for detecting harm in layers where
projection-based methods fail.
For architectures exhibiting valley-like profiles, using both
strategies may yield stronger detection than either alone.
Testing whether projection and angular scores are complementary in
the signal-theoretic sense, for example by scoring each prompt with
a 2D classifier over both, is a natural follow-up.

5.5
Practical guidance for deployment

Several operational conclusions follow directly from the
experiments.

Fit
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{opt}}
, not
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
, when data allows.

At
n=100
per class,
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{opt}}
improves TPR by
0.093
on average
and reduces cross-model TPR standard deviation by
1.8\times
.
Fitting cost is seconds per model, and that would be a one-time action.
The
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}
direction is adequate as a warm start.

Fit on the deployment variant.

Cross-variant AUROC transfer is largely robust (at most
0.010

drop for three of four families), but TPR transfer can collapse
even when AUROC does not.
The Gemma-3 base
\rightarrow
instruct transfer loses
0.576
TPR
while losing only
0.057
AUROC.
Where variant-specific fitting is impossible, the
instruct
\rightarrow
abliterated direction transfers better than
base
\rightarrow
instruct or base
\rightarrow
abliterated in every family, consistent with the
smaller angular shift between instruct and abliterated.

Report TPR alongside AUROC.

As Section
5.2
argues, AUROC in the
0.97
+
regime tells the reader little about operational detectability.
TPR@
1\%
FPR, or equivalent low-FPR metrics, should accompany
AUROC in deployment-adjacent evaluation.

5.6
Limitations and future work

Adversarial robustness and language.

All evaluation prompts are clean, single-turn, English imperative
instructions.
Whether the harm direction remains detectable under adversarial
suffixes
(
24
)
, multi-turn escalation,
stylistically diverse harm, or cross-lingual substitution is
untested.
Prior work on perplexity-based filters
(
22
)

suggests that fluent adversarial prompts are particularly
challenging; whether activation-based detection inherits this
vulnerability is unknown.

Comparison with dedicated classifiers.

We do not benchmark against Llama Guard
(
8
)
,
ShieldGemma
(
20
)
, or WildGuard
(
7
)
.
These are trained classifiers with substantially more parameters
and labels; our method’s value proposition is latency and
interpretability, not raw accuracy.
A head-to-head comparison under matched compute budgets is a
natural next experiment.

Pooling and extraction.

Element-wise max pooling is susceptible to single-token outliers
and may be exploitable by adversarial token insertion.
Position-specific extraction (e.g., last instruction token, as
in
21
) may prove more robust.
Comparing pooling strategies under both clean and adversarial
conditions is a reasonable next step.

Minor methodological caveats.

Shared-layer selection per model variant may be suboptimal for
individual strategies, particularly in the valley regime; a
larger validation set or per-strategy layer selection could
mitigate this.
The 12 models form a structured set with shared base weights, so
grand mean
\pm
standard deviation conflates between-family and
within-family variance; the per-model scatter in
Figure
3
provides the disaggregated view.

6
Conclusion

Across 12 models and four architectural families, extended across Qwen3.5 from 0.8B to 9B parameters, harmful intent is geometrically recoverable from residual streams as a direction that is stable, fits from a hundred labels per class, and lies nearly orthogonal to the dominant variance of benign-prompt activations.
The direction survives instruction tuning, survives abliteration of
the refusal behaviour, and transfers across harm benchmarks the
fitter never saw.

This is consistent with a simple picture. Models acquire the
capacity to recognise harmful intent as a by-product of general
language understanding; alignment then modifies what they do with
such inputs, while leaving the input-side representation linearly
decodable. Harm recognition and refusal behaviour thus appear as functionally dissociated features of the representation. In three of our four families, alignment leaves this
representation largely in place; in Gemma-3 it rotates the
representation substantially while keeping it recoverable.
This picture is consistent with the three observed patterns: 1) a hundred-example probe suffices,
2) fitted directions transfer across alignment variants, and 3) removing a model’s refusal behaviour leaves the detection signal intact.

Two methodological points follow. First, projection is not the only
geometric mode of harm encoding. A supervised angular-deviation
strategy achieves comparable detection along a representationally
distinct direction, and recovers harmful prompts in the middle
layers of Qwen2.5, where projection-based methods degrade sharply. Whether
this second mode is architecture-specific or a general feature of
transformer representations is an open question. Second, in the
AUROC
0.97
+ regime, headline ranking metrics can substantially
overstate operational detectability; low-FPR metrics should
accompany AUROC in safety-adjacent evaluation.

These results establish the geometric picture across 12 small-model variants spanning four architectures and across Qwen3.5 at 0.8B–9B, under clean, single-turn, English evaluation. Extension to adversarial prompts and to stylistically or linguistically diverse harm are natural next steps.

Appendix A
Soft-AUC optimisation details

The Soft-AUC objective (Equation
2
) is optimised
via Riemannian gradient ascent on the unit sphere

\mathcal{S}^{D-1}
.
At each step, the Euclidean gradient
\nabla\hat{U}
is projected
onto the tangent space of the sphere at
\mathbf{w}
to obtain the
Riemannian gradient:

\nabla_{R}\hat{U}=\nabla\hat{U}-(\nabla\hat{U}\cdot\mathbf{w})\,\mathbf{w}.

(3)

The objective is a sigmoid-smoothed approximation to the
Mann–Whitney
U
statistic, where each pairwise margin

(x_{i}-x_{j})\cdot\mathbf{w}
is scaled by a fixed temperature

\tau=1.0
before the sigmoid.
The update rule is

\mathbf{w}\leftarrow\mathrm{unit}(\mathbf{w}+\eta\,\nabla_{R}\hat{U})
,
where
\mathrm{unit}(\cdot)
denotes
\ell_{2}
normalisation and

\eta=0.05
is the step size.

The optimisation is warm-started from
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{LDA}}

(Equation
1
).
Early stopping is triggered when the Riemannian gradient norm
satisfies
\|\nabla_{R}\hat{U}\|<10^{-5}
for 20 consecutive
steps, up to a maximum of 300 steps.
If a NaN gradient is encountered at any step, the optimiser
reverts to the previous valid iterate and terminates, ensuring
numerical stability on arbitrary inputs.
