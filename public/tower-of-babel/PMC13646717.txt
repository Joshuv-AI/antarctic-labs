Design Principles of Plant Thermotolerance: Integrating Photosynthesis, Reproduction, and Field Resilience in a Warming Climate
Authors: Yuchen Qu, Wataru Yamori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGlobal food security is increasingly threatened by climate change, as rising temperatures compromise the yields of major staple crops, including wheat, rice, and maize. Enhancing plant thermotolerance has therefore become a critical priority for sustaining agricultural productivity. However, plant heat‐stress responses have often been described as fragmented and pathway‐specific, limiting their translation into effective crop improvement strategies. Here, we synthesize current knowledge of plant responses to heat stress and reframe them as an integrated set of design principles centered on preserving photosynthetic carbon gain under high temperature. By doing so, we provide a collection of insights that may help guide future research efforts and the development of strategies for improving plant thermotolerance. We organize major defense strategies into six functional domains: (i) membrane systems and structural integrity, (ii) photosynthetic regulation, (iii) protective metabolites and hormonal signaling, (iv) reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging, (v) protein homeostasis, and (vi) transcriptional and post‐transcriptional regulation. Rather than treating these responses as independent pathways, we emphasize their temporal hierarchy, energetic costs, and functional interconnections, highlighting their shared objective—maintaining CO2 assimilation, energy balance, and biomass accumulation as thermal damage accelerates. We further discuss how insights from mutagenesis, transgenic approaches, and targeted genetic modification can be translated into crop improvement, clarifying opportunities and trade‐offs that emerge when thermotolerance is engineered at distinct physiological nodes. Together, this design‐centered framework provides a unifying conceptual and practical roadmap for developing high‐yielding, heat‐tolerant cultivars, offering actionable guidance for sustaining crop productivity in a warming world.

Introduction

The global population is projected to approach 10 billion by 2050 (UN, 2019), yet food insecurity remains persistent. After declining between 2005 and 2015, the number of undernourished people increased again after 2016 and exceeded 811 million by the end of 2020 (FAO 2019). In parallel, arable land per capita has steadily decreased since the early 1990s (FAO 2019). These trends intensify pressure on agricultural systems to sustain productivity with increasingly limited land resources.

Climate change adds a rapidly escalating constraint. Heat stress is already a major driver of yield instability and crop failure. In maize (Zea mays), temperatures above 38°C can markedly reduce yield, with field studies reporting losses of up to 75% under extreme heat (Lizaso et al. 2018). Wheat (Triticum aestivum) and rice (Oryza sativa) can be even more heat‐sensitive under certain developmental stages. Rice yields decline when day/night temperatures exceed 28°C/21°C and drop sharply above 34°C/27°C (Krishnan et al. 2011; Fahad et al. 2018), while wheat yields decrease when growth temperatures rise above ~31°C, even during short heat episodes (Urban et al. 2018). On a global scale, each 1°C increase in mean temperature is predicted to reduce wheat production by 6%–10% (Asseng et al. 2015; García et al. 2015). By the end of the 21st century, global mean surface temperature is projected to increase by ~1.8°C under optimistic mitigation scenarios and by up to 5.7°C if emissions remain unchecked (Masson‐Delmotte et al. 2021). Models integrating thousands of datasets predict that warming will severely threaten the production of major staple crops, including rice, wheat, maize, and soybean (Glycine max) (Zhao et al. 2017; Makowski et al. 2020; Hasegawa et al. 2022; Tran et al. 2025). Under pessimistic scenarios, global staple calorie supply may decline substantially (Figure 1), emphasizing the urgency of improving thermotolerance.

Plants withstand heat through a multi‐layered defense system that spans membranes, metabolism, redox control, proteostasis, and gene regulation. For clarity, we organize these processes into six functional categories (Figure 2): (1) membrane systems and structural integrity; (2) photosynthetic regulation; (3) protective metabolites and hormonal regulation; (4) ROS scavenging; (5) protein homeostasis; and (6) transcriptional and post‐transcriptional regulation. Importantly, these responses do not function as isolated pathways. Instead, they are interconnected layers that collectively stabilize cellular structure, sustain energy metabolism, and preserve growth under elevated temperature.

We reinterpret these heat‐responsive processes through a unifying framework: preservation of photosynthetic carbon gain under high temperature. From this perspective, diverse responses—from lipid remodeling and redox regulation to proteostasis and transcriptional reprogramming—operate as coordinated design elements that converge on sustaining CO2 assimilation, energy balance, and biomass accumulation. Evidence from genetic modification further indicates that enhanced photosynthetic performance under heat translates into increased growth or yield, highlighting both opportunities and constraints in engineering thermotolerance (Figure 2; Table 1, and Supporting Information S1: Table 1).

To integrate these diverse responses into a coherent conceptual framework, we further classify thermotolerance mechanisms according to response timescales and associated physiological costs. To place these responses into context, plants, as shaped by evolutionary processes, do not typically respond to diverse stresses through a single unified strategy. Some responses are rapid and low‐cost, occurring within seconds of stress exposure. These immediate reactions rely on a limited set of pre‐existing resources, such as DNA, RNA, or inactive proteins that can be rapidly activated, replenished, or recycled, thereby mitigating transient challenges without substantially disrupting basal metabolism. Conversely, other responses operate over longer timescales, requiring additional time to establish appropriate signaling pathways and resource allocation patterns. These longer‐term adaptations reprogram multiple metabolic processes to cope with prolonged stress, prioritizing survival and reproduction over biomass production. As summarized in Figure 2, plant heat‐stress responses are temporally stratified, ranging from rapid, low‐cost regulatory layers to slower, resource‐intensive protective systems. Design principles of plant thermotolerance.

Plant thermotolerance arises from the coordinated deployment of multiple defensive strategies that differ in response speed, energetic cost, and functional scope. Six design principles were distilled from experimental and physiological evidence: 1. Protection of photosynthetic carbon gain as the primary objective; 2. Rapid deployment of low‐cost defenses as a first line of protection; 3. Selective activation of resource‐intensive universal through genetical regulations defenses under prolonged stress; 4. Maintenance of functional redox balance rather than indiscriminate ROS elimination; 5. Coordinated protection across photosynthesis, membranes, and proteostasis; 6. Use of regulatory hubs with chemical regulators to balance growth and defense. As an overarching principle, thermotolerance strategies should be optimized according to the target production environment.

Membrane Systems and Structural Integrity as the Front Line of Heat Damage

Cellular membranes define organellar compartmentation, regulate transport, and provide platforms for multi‐protein machineries. Under heat stress, membranes are among the earliest targets because elevated temperature increases lipid disorder and destabilizes embedded complexes. Membrane disruption not only perturbs transport and signaling but also accelerates failure of high‐energy conversion processes on or near membranes, including photosynthesis and respiration, thereby amplifying ROS production and secondary damage (Allakhverdiev et al. 2008). ROS can initiate lipid peroxidation chain reactions, generating toxic aldehydes and undermining homeostasis beyond the initial site of injury (Das and Roychoudhury 2014).

A key determinant of membrane heat tolerance is lipid composition, particularly the balance between saturated and unsaturated fatty acids. While unsaturated lipids support function at low temperatures, excessive unsaturation under heat can exacerbate fluidization and destabilize membrane protein complexes. In chloroplasts, such destabilization can impair the architecture and function of PSII/PSI and associated electron‐transport components, promoting redox imbalance and ROS generation (Sato et al. 2003; Falcone et al. 2004; Routaboul et al. 2012; Caffarri et al. 2014; Hiremath et al. 2017). Consistently, downregulation of fatty acid desaturases (e.g., FAD3‐2, FAD5/6/7/8) often improves photosynthetic performance and growth under heat stress, likely by restraining over‐fluidization (Murakami et al. 2000; Falcone et al. 2004; Routaboul et al. 2012; Hiremath et al. 2017). However, the relationship is not monotonic: loss of fad3‐2/fad7‐2/fad8 can improve short‐term photosynthesis yet compromise longer‐term growth and survival, implying that a basal level of polyunsaturated lipids remains essential under prolonged stress (Routaboul et al. 2012). Thus, heat‐resilient membranes require an optimized, not minimized, degree of unsaturation.

Beyond bulk fatty‐acid composition, specific lipid classes support thylakoid stability. Sulfoquinovosyl diacylglycerol (SQDG) contributes to PSII structural integrity and recovery from heat inactivation, likely by direct association with PSII and by modulating local thylakoid physicochemical properties (Sato et al. 2003). This lipid dependence becomes especially consequential because PSII maintenance under heat requires coordinated turnover of damaged components and efficient trafficking/import of nuclear‐encoded proteins (Sato et al. 2003; Allakhverdiev et al. 2008).

Heat tolerance also depends on lipid‐associated proteins whose protective roles extend beyond fluidity control. Non‐specific lipid transfer proteins and temperature‐induced lipocalins can improve heat resilience, with impacts on photosynthesis and lipid peroxidation that appear not to rely on selective transfer of unsaturated lipids (Chi et al. 2009). These proteins likely constrain oxidative membrane damage and coordinate antioxidant capacity, consistent with the broader linkage between redox control and membrane protection (Das and Roychoudhury 2014).

Membrane systems further include vesicular trafficking, which supports homeostasis and turnover during heat stress. In rice, OsRab7 overexpression improves photosynthesis, growth, survival, and yield under heat, accompanied by reduced lipid peroxidation and enhanced antioxidant activities (El‐Esawi and Alayafi 2019). Structural proteins influencing cellular architecture can also enhance photosynthesis and growth under heat and correlate with improved plastid functionality and ROS management (Xu et al. 2014; Soda et al. 2018). Together, these findings support a membrane‐centric view in which lipid composition, oxidative protection, trafficking, and structural reinforcement cooperate to prevent early collapse of organellar integrity.

[Design principle] Membrane physical properties change almost immediately under heat stress, and membrane remodeling is among the fastest defensive adjustments. Lipid composition can shift within ~10–15 min of heat exposure (Figure 2). Because these responses can leverage pre‐existing proteins/metabolites and a limited set of enzymes, their direct costs can be relatively low. Engineering strategies should therefore aim to optimize lipid remodeling and reinforce membrane‐protective modules—including lipid‐associated proteins and vesicle trafficking—to preserve thylakoid and organellar integrity during heat stress (Box 1; Supporting Information S1: Table 2).

Photosynthetic Regulation as a Key Determinant of Plant Vitality Under Heat Stress

Photosynthesis is the primary entry point of energy and carbon into plant biomass and thus a major determinant of yield under heat. Heat‐induced yield loss frequently reflects reduced cumulative carbon gain driven by impairment of photosynthetic energy conversion, electron‐transport regulation, and CO2 assimilation (Crafts‐Brandner and Salvucci 2002; Wahid et al. 2007; Allakhverdiev et al. 2008; Qu et al. 2023). Importantly, the light reactions and Calvin–Benson cycle are tightly coupled: disruption of stromal metabolism restricts NADP+ regeneration, increases excitation pressure, and accelerates ROS formation in the thylakoid (Yamori 2016). When optimal growth temperatures are exceeded by 5°C–10°C, a rapid ROS burst can occur, causing oxidative damage—particularly to PSI and PSII—and further constraining carbon assimilation (Pucciariello et al. 2012; Das and Roychoudhury 2014). These primary disruptions propagate through metabolism and ultimately suppress growth and yield.

Heat sensitivity is evident when pigment metabolism is perturbed. Chlorophyll‐b‐deficient mutants show pronounced photosynthetic decline after heat exposure, associated with PSI acceptor‐side limitation and insufficient ΔpH formation (Brestic et al. 2016). Chlorophyll turnover is also critical; a missense mutation in chlorophyll synthase disrupts cycling after heat, leading to reaction‐center damage and bleaching (Lin et al. 2014). Conversely, downregulation of chlorophyll degradation pathways can maintain chlorophyll pools and improve photosynthetic performance and growth under heat (Yu et al. 2022).

PSII integrity and repair are recurrent bottlenecks. Overexpression of PSII core components enhances PSII repair and activity, improving photosynthesis, growth, and yield under heat stress (Chen et al. 2020; Hassan et al. 2021), whereas defects in stabilizing subunits compromise PSII assembly and accelerate heat vulnerability (Pigolev and Klimov 2015; Bayro‐Kaiser and Nelson 2020). These patterns align with the view that thermotolerance depends strongly on maintaining PSII turnover capacity (Allakhverdiev et al. 2007; Ding et al. 2023).

Heat also increases the risk of electron‐transport imbalance when metabolic sinks are restricted, promoting overreduction and ROS formation. Linear electron flow alone may not maintain ATP/NADPH balance and can promote PSI overreduction under stress (Yamori and Shikanai 2016; Yamori et al. 2016). Cyclic electron flow (CEF) generates additional proton motive force and relieves PSI acceptor‐side pressure. Both NDH‐dependent and PGR5‐dependent CEF contribute to thermotolerance, whereas deficiencies impair photosynthesis under heat (Zhang and Sharkey 2009; Essemine et al. 2011). Ferredoxin sits at the hub of these routes, and PETF overexpression can improve growth under heat, likely by enhancing electron routing and limiting ROS accumulation (Lin et al. 2013; Mondal and Bruce 2018).

On the stromal side, heat can impose constraints via RuBP regeneration and Rubisco activation. Thermosensitive PRK mutations cause rapid photosynthetic collapse under heat, and reductions in PRK activation are observed even in wild type (Bayro‐Kaiser and Nelson 2020; Lin et al. 2013). SBPase overexpression partially sustains photosynthetic capacity under heat and has been associated with mitigation of PRK decline (Lin et al. 2013). Rubisco activation frequently becomes a dominant limitation because Rubisco activase (RCA) is heat‐labile despite relatively heat‐stable Rubisco (Barta et al. 2010; Qu et al. 2023). Heat can shift RCA toward less soluble states and reduce activation efficiency (Qu et al. 2023). Engineering approaches—RCA overexpression or thermotolerant RCA variants—repeatedly improve photosynthesis and growth under heat (Kurek et al. 2007; Yamori et al. 2012; Scafaro et al. 2018; Suganami et al. 2020, 2021; Chaudhary et al. 2021; Qu et al. 2021). More importantly, the benefits of RCA overexpression extend beyond controlled stress conditions, improving photosynthetic performance under more moderate and agriculturally realistic environments, such as fluctuating light regimes in the presence of elevated CO2 concentrations (Yamori et al. 2026a). However, RCA overexpression can also reduce Rubisco content, implying feedback between activation state and Rubisco accumulation that may impose trade‐offs (Fukayama et al. 2018; Qu et al. 2021). Rubisco performance has also been successfully improved under both current atmospheric CO2 concentrations (381 μmol mol−1) and projected future levels (549 μmol mol−1) by introducing targeted mutations at specific sites. These improvements were achieved despite the limited efficiency of natural selection, which is unlikely to keep pace with the rapid increase in atmospheric CO2 concentrations (Yamori et al. 2026b).

[Design principle] Photosynthetic acclimation to heat typically unfolds over tens of minutes to hours (Figure 2) and is biologically costly because it involves coordinated regulation and turnover of multiple proteins and broad metabolic adjustment. Sustained carbon gain under heat therefore requires multi‐node protection—integrating thylakoid stability, electron‐transport balance, RuBP regeneration, and Rubisco activation—to maintain redox homeostasis and CO2 assimilation (Box 1; Supporting Information S1: Table 2).

Protective Metabolites and Hormonal Regulation of Essential Bioprocesses

Unlike membrane stability and photosynthetic regulation, protective metabolites and hormonal pathways rarely determine thermotolerance in isolation. Instead, they operate as distributed buffering systems that stabilize core physiological processes—especially photosynthesis and membrane integrity—when heat accelerates molecular damage and metabolic imbalance.

Plants accumulate diverse low‐molecular‐weight compounds under heat stress, including proline, glycine betaine, sugars, and related osmolytes, which contribute to osmotic adjustment, redox buffering, and stabilization of protein complexes (Wahid et al. 2007). These metabolites do not directly increase carbon fixation capacity; rather, they help maintain photosynthetic function by preventing secondary damage to thylakoid membranes, stromal enzymes, and redox carriers. Proline metabolism has been proposed to link cytosolic redox balance with photosynthetic recovery through NADP+/NADPH buffering, whereas glycine betaine stabilizes protein conformation and can preserve Rubisco activase solubility under heat stress, indirectly sustaining CO2 assimilation (De Ronde et al. 2004; Yang et al. 2005).

Sugars similarly act as osmoprotectants and signaling molecules. Altered sucrose and starch dynamics under heat stress can modulate sink–source relationships and feedback regulation of photosynthesis. In rice, increased sucrose accumulation has been associated with improved photosynthetic performance and growth under heat stress, suggesting that carbon allocation itself can buffer thermal perturbation (Kaneko et al. 2014; Inomata et al. 2018).

Phytohormones provide an additional regulatory layer that coordinates growth–stress trade‐offs. Brassinosteroids, abscisic acid, and auxin‐related pathways modulate photosynthetic efficiency, membrane stability, and developmental timing under heat stress, but their outcomes are context‐dependent and species‐specific (Singh et al. 2016; Fang et al. 2020; Sadura et al. 2019). This diversity highlights that hormonal regulation is not a linear ‘tolerance booster’ but a system that reallocates resources between carbon gain, growth, and survival depending on environmental context.

[Design principle] Accumulation of compatible solutes and hormonal reprogramming generally requires hours to reach physiologically effective levels and imposes substantial carbon, nitrogen, and energy costs (Figure 2). Effective thermotolerance therefore depends on selectively strengthening buffering capacity to stabilize photosynthesis and membranes during sustained heat, while minimizing chronic penalties to growth and yield (Box 1; Supporting Information S1: Table 2).

ROS Scavenging Relieves Plants From Heat Damage While Shaping Stress Signaling

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are unavoidable byproducts of energy metabolism and increase sharply under heat stress due to imbalances in photosynthetic and respiratory electron transport (Mittler 2017). ROS therefore occupy a dual position in thermotolerance: excessive accumulation damages lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, yet controlled ROS production serves as a central signal that activates heat‐responsive transcriptional and metabolic programs.

Heat stress enhances ROS generation in chloroplasts, mitochondria, and peroxisomes, particularly when photosynthetic carbon assimilation is constrained and electron sinks become limiting. In chloroplasts, overreduction of the electron transport chain promotes superoxide and singlet oxygen formation, which can rapidly damage PSI and PSII if not properly constrained (Das and Roychoudhury 2014). Consequently, ROS homeostasis is tightly linked to maintaining photosynthetic carbon gain under elevated temperature.

Plants deploy enzymatic ROS‐scavenging systems—including superoxide dismutase, ascorbate peroxidase, and related antioxidant pathways—to constrain ROS levels within a functional range. Enhanced expression of these enzymes often improves photosynthetic performance and growth under heat stress (Lee et al. 2007; Kim et al. 2010). However, complete suppression of ROS signaling is neither feasible nor desirable. Notably, mutants with partially reduced antioxidant capacity can display enhanced thermotolerance, consistent with the idea that elevated basal ROS signaling can prime stress‐responsive gene expression (Miller et al. 2007).

In addition to primary ROS detoxification, detoxification of secondary damage products is crucial. Lipid peroxidation generates reactive aldehydes that amplify cellular injury under heat stress; aldo‐keto reductases and related systems mitigate this secondary carbonyl stress and support sustained photosynthetic activity and growth (Turóczy et al. 2011). The proper functioning of the ROS‐scavenging system and the effective removal of ROS‐related byproducts rely heavily on the sufficient accumulation of specific metabolites. The levels of these metabolites are strongly influenced by key hormones, such as melatonin, as discussed in the previous section, as well as by associated changes in gene expression, which will be addressed in the following sections (Gao et al. 2024).

[Design principle] ROS signals rise rapidly after heat exposure, whereas inducible antioxidant capacity typically develops over hours to days and incurs moderate metabolic costs (Figure 2). Thermotolerance depends on maintaining ROS within a signaling‐competent yet non‐damaging range—by combining primary ROS control with detoxification of secondary oxidative products—rather than indiscriminate ROS elimination (Box 1; Supporting Information S1: Table 2).

Protein Homeostasis Preserves Photosynthetic and Metabolic Function Under High Temperature

Elevated temperature destabilizes protein structure, accelerates misfolding, and disrupts multi‐subunit complexes, making protein homeostasis a central constraint on plant performance under heat stress. Because photosynthesis relies on large, dynamic protein assemblies with high turnover, impairment of proteostasis rapidly translates into reduced carbon assimilation and growth.

Heat shock proteins (HSPs) and molecular chaperones constitute the first line of defense against protein denaturation. Chloroplast‐localized small HSPs and DnaJ‐family proteins stabilize photosystem components, limit ROS amplification, and preserve thylakoid integrity, thereby sustaining photosynthetic activity under heat stress (Kim et al. 2012; Kang et al. 2017). Several chaperones also influence pigment metabolism and antenna stability, linking proteostasis to light harvesting and energy distribution (Ji et al. 2021; Sun et al. 2022).

Proteostasis further depends on selective degradation of irreversibly damaged proteins. Proteases such as Deg and FtsH participate in photosystem repair by removing dysfunctional components, enabling continuous replacement and recovery of photosynthetic capacity under heat stress (Barker et al. 2006; Chen et al. 2018; Yue et al. 2023). Beyond organelles, autophagy contributes to thermotolerance by clearing aggregated proteins and maintaining cellular homeostasis during prolonged stress (Zhou et al. 2014).

A key practical implication is that proteostasis typically does not ‘enhance’ photosynthesis in benign conditions; instead, it sets the recovery rate and completeness of photosynthetic and metabolic systems after heat episodes. Plants with insufficient proteostasis capacity often show delayed recovery even after temperatures return to optimal levels, leading to cumulative yield losses across repeated heat events.

[Design principle] Some HSPs are induced within minutes of heat exposure, but robust proteostasis reinforcement imposes high energetic and nitrogen costs due to sustained protein synthesis and turnover (Figure 2). Strengthening thermotolerance therefore requires optimizing the balance between chaperone capacity, repair efficiency, and selective degradation—supporting rapid recovery while minimizing growth trade‐offs (Box 1; Supporting Information S1: Table 2).

Gene Transcription and Post‐Transcriptional Modifications Regulation Coordinate Whole‐Plant Heat Stress Responses

While membranes, photosynthesis, metabolites, ROS homeostasis, and proteostasis define the operational capacity of plant cells under heat stress, transcriptional and post‐transcriptional regulation provide the integrative control layer that coordinates these processes across tissues, organelles, and time scales. Heat tolerance at the whole‐plant level emerges not from individual stress‐response genes, but from regulatory networks that balance carbon gain, growth, and survival.

Transcription factors (TFs) constitute central hubs in these networks. Many TFs modulate thermotolerance by sustaining photosynthesis, redox homeostasis, and protein stability, whereas others act as negative regulators whose suppression enhances heat resilience, reflecting pervasive growth–defense trade‐offs embedded in regulatory architecture (Ambavaram et al. 2014; Xue et al. 2015; Casaretto et al. 2016; Ning et al. 2020; Zhao et al. 2021; Mubarok et al. 2023, 2024; Ding et al. 2023; Balfagón et al. 2024; Lopes Hornai et al. 2024). Importantly, many TFs influence multiple downstream processes simultaneously—photosynthesis, ROS scavenging, proteostasis, and hormone signaling—highlighting their role as system‐level coordinators rather than pathway‐specific regulators.

The canonical heat shock factor–heat shock protein (HSF–HSP) axis represents a dominant transcriptional module in plant heat responses. This axis integrates upstream signals such as Ca2+, ROS, nitric oxide, and phytohormones, rapidly activating chaperone networks that stabilize proteins and membranes under elevated temperatures (Ohama et al. 2017; Ding et al. 2023). Functionally, the HSF–HSP system links early stress perception to downstream protection of photosynthetic carbon gain by preserving proteostasis and constraining oxidative escalation.

Post‐transcriptional regulation further refines these responses. RNA processing, splicing, stability, and translational control determine whether protective proteins accumulate quickly enough to counteract heat‐induced damage. In chloroplasts, translational capacity is particularly critical because photosynthetic proteins require continuous replenishment under stress. Enhanced expression of plastid elongation factor EF‐Tu improves photosynthesis and growth during and after heat stress, whereas thermosensitive mutations in plastid translation factors severely constrain photosynthetic recovery (Fu et al. 2008; Bayro‐Kaiser and Nelson 2020). Ribosomal components such as RPS1 similarly contribute to maintaining chloroplast function under heat stress (Yu et al. 2012).

Post‐translational modifications and targeted protein degradation add additional regulatory layers. SUMOylation, phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and F‐box–mediated proteolysis modulate the activity, stability, and localization of transcription factors, chaperones, and redox regulators (Li et al. 2013; Mishra et al. 2017; Li et al. 2018; Yu et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2022). Although causal chains often remain unresolved, these mechanisms are increasingly recognized as levers that tune intensity, duration, and specificity of heat responses.

[Design principle] Transcriptional and post‐transcriptional responses are among the fastest and most energy‐efficient layers of heat adaptation, with gene expression changes detectable within minutes of heat exposure (Figure 2). Effective engineering should therefore prioritize regulatory hubs that coordinate chloroplast function, proteostasis, and redox balance, enabling flexible activation of higher‐cost defenses only when needed (Box 1; Supporting Information S1: Table 2). It is also noteworthy that alterations in the expression of specific genes can persist beyond the initial stress event and contribute to stress memory, thereby influencing plant tolerance over an extended period (Nishad et al. 2025). Such responses may enhance or impair subsequent plant performance, depending on the environmental context and the underlying regulatory mechanisms.

Concluding Remarks and Future Perspectives

This review organizes plant heat‐stress tolerance into six interconnected functional categories and reframes thermotolerance as a system‐level design problem centered on preserving photosynthetic carbon gain under high temperature. The framework emphasizes that heat defenses are not additive modules; rather, they are temporally stratified and physiologically coupled layers that stabilize cellular structure, maintain energy and redox balance, and sustain CO2 assimilation (Figure 2, Box 1; Supporting Information S1: Table 2). From this perspective, membrane remodeling, redox regulation, proteostasis, and transcriptional control converge as coordinated elements that delay damage, enable repair, and support recovery, ultimately shaping cumulative carbon gain and yield stability.

A practical implication is that many mechanisms underlying heat tolerance are not heat‐specific. ROS accumulation, protein misfolding, membrane destabilization, and transcriptional reprogramming represent shared physiological challenges across diverse stresses, including drought, excess light, salinity, and biotic attack (Mittler 2017). Consequently, interventions that enhance thermotolerance often confer broader stress resilience, consistent with the frequent co‐occurrence of heat tolerance with multi‐stress resistance (Pandey et al. 2015). At the same time, the growth penalties associated with constitutive defense activation highlight a central constraint in crop improvement: robust tolerance must be achieved without chronic diversion of resources away from biomass accumulation and reproduction. Continuous engagement of stress‐response networks typically reduces carbon allocation to growth and reproduction, shifting plant strategies from productivity toward survival (He et al. 2022). The design principles proposed here therefore emphasize selective deployment, regulatory control, and coordinated protection rather than maximal activation of any single defense layer.

Genetic enhancement of photosynthetic performance under heat provides a particularly actionable route, yet it is highlighted an important nuance: increases in photosynthetic capacity do not always translate proportionally into yield gain at coherent efficiency (Figure 3; Table 1, Supporting Information S1: Table 1). Improvements in photosynthesis resulting from modifications to specific factors—particularly those related to photosynthetic regulation and protective metabolites and hormonal regulation—show a stronger positive correlation with overall plant growth. In contrast, modifications associated with protein homeostasis, gene transcription and post‐transcriptional regulation, or membrane systems and structural integrity exhibit weaker positive correlations between enhanced photosynthetic performance and growth outcomes (Figure 3). In some cases, specific modifications may enhance photosynthesis in the short term but fail to translate into improved growth, or may even impair overall plant performance over the longer term (Table 1, Supporting Information S1: Table 1). This variability likely reflects sink limitations, allocation trade‐offs, broader regulation network and stress‐induced constraints outside the photosynthetic apparatus. Accordingly, engineering strategies should be evaluated at whole‐plant and seasonal scales, integrating photosynthesis with transport, sink strength, and resource economics. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that, in grain crops, where seed yield represents the ultimate economic output, the capacity to allocate photosynthetically derived assimilates to reproductive organs is critical. More specifically, pollen development, germination, and fertilization are highly sensitive to temperature fluctuations. Consequently, ensuring reproductive success represents a distinct research priority, governed by physiological constraints that extend beyond carbon assimilation and energy acquisition alone, yet equally essential for sustaining crop productivity under future climate conditions (Tushabe and Rosbakh 2024). An alternative and complementary route is to reduce heat stress exposure through controlled‐environment agriculture. Plant factories enable precise regulation of temperature, light, water, nutrients, and atmospheric composition, potentially minimizing the need for costly defense mechanisms (Kozai et al. 2019). Under such optimized conditions, traits that would be disadvantageous in open‐field agriculture—such as reduced stress signaling, lower antioxidant capacity, or Rubisco variants optimized for high CO2—may become advantageous. This contrast reinforces over all principles in Box 1, namely that the optimal thermotolerance strategy depends on the production environment. We also emphasize the importance of distinguishing between laboratory‐based stress treatments and practical field conditions. In natural environments, plants are frequently exposed to multiple interacting stresses rather than heat stress in isolation, resulting in substantially greater complexity. Consequently, the trade‐offs associated with different adaptation and mitigation strategies should be evaluated within the context of these combined‐stress scenarios.

Looking forward, rational development of heat‐tolerant crops will benefit from integrating molecular genetics, physiology, and systems‐level modeling. Rather than focusing on single genes or isolated pathways, future strategies should prioritize network‐level optimization that preserves photosynthetic carbon gain while minimizing growth penalties. By framing thermotolerance as a design problem—balancing speed, cost, robustness, and environmental context—this review aims to provide a conceptual foundation for developing crops capable of sustaining productivity in an increasingly warming world.
