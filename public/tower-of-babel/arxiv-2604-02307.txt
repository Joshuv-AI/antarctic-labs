Detecting Symmetry-Resolved Entanglement: A Quantum Monte Carlo Approach
Kuangjie Chen, Weizhen Jia, Xiaopeng Li, René Meyer, Jiarui Zhao

Abstract
Symmetry and entanglement are two fundamental concepts in quantum many-body physics. Their interplay is captured by symmetry-resolved entanglement, which decomposes the total entanglement into contributions from different symmetry sectors. Computing symmetry-resolved entanglement in strongly interacting higher-dimensional quantum systems remains challenging. Here, we introduce a quantum Monte Carlo (QMC) approach for computing symmetry-resolved Rényi entropies (SRRE) in large-scale interacting systems by measuring disorder (symmetry-twisted) operators on replica manifolds and reconstructing SRRE from the corresponding charged moments. We apply this method to the transverse-field Ising model (TFIM) in one and two dimensions. In one dimension, we recover the conformal-field-theory prediction for the logarithmic scaling of the disorder operator and observe the expected approach to entanglement equipartition. In two dimensions, our data provide numerical evidence consistent with entanglement equipartition at the (2+1)D Ising critical point. We further apply the framework to the 1D Heisenberg chain and obtain results consistent with the expected asymptotic scaling and finite-size corrections in the U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) -resolved sectors. Our work establishes a practical numerical route to symmetry-resolved entanglement in interacting lattice models and provides a useful framework for future studies beyond one dimension.



Introduction.
—
Entanglement entropy (EE) is a powerful probe of quantum many-body states, offering insights that transcend the paradigm of conventional order parameters
8
;
1
;
26
. Its scaling with subsystem size offers an effective organizing principle, uncovering universal features of distinct quantum phases. While EE typically obeys an area law in ground states of local gapped Hamiltonians
16
, deviations from this behavior often signal more exotic forms of quantum matter. For example, topological entanglement entropy characterizes topologically ordered phases
23
;
20
, while logarithmic corrections carrying universal information can arise in (1+1)D conformal field theories (CFTs), in systems with Nambu-Goldstone modes, and for entangling boundaries with corners
24
;
44
;
45
;
37
;
14
. As a result, entanglement scaling has become a valuable framework not only for classifying equilibrium phases, but also for diagnosing nonequilibrium phenomena such as measurement-induced phase transitions, which emerge from the interplay between unitary evolution and local measurements
36
;
42
;
31
.

In recent years, this framework has been extended by incorporating the role of global symmetries, yielding the notion of
symmetry-resolved entanglement

25
;
18
;
43
;
6
;
10
;
17
;
13
;
7
;
19
, which resolves the total entanglement entropy into contributions from different symmetry sectors, providing a more refined probe into the entanglement structure of a quantum state. A significant discovery in this field, rigorously established within conformal field theory, is the
equipartition of entanglement

43
;
13
. This principle states that, in certain (1+1)D CFTs, at least the leading-order contribution to the entanglement entropy is equally distributed among all symmetry sectors. Current research frontiers are rapidly expanding its scope to encompass systems with non-Abelian symmetries
4
, entanglement dynamics in non-equilibrium settings
30
, and the resolution of multipartite entanglement
21
. Furthermore, experimental proposals for measuring symmetry-resolved entanglement have begun to bridge the gap between theory and experiment, establishing it as a promising and experimentally accessible probe for quantum simulators
28
.

However, while the understanding of symmetry-resolved entanglement in one-dimensional systems is now well-established, extending these insights to higher-dimensional interacting systems still presents a formidable challenge. Computing symmetry-resolved entanglement in interacting two-dimensional quantum systems remains particularly challenging. Analytical approaches such as CFTs are most powerful in (1+1)D, while numerical workhorses like exact diagonalization are severely limited by system size. Tensor network methods for two-dimensional systems are often truncated at small bond dimensions, which limits the precision with which entanglement structures can be resolved
34
;
35
;
32
. Consequently, fundamental questions—such as the explicit scaling forms of symmetry-resolved entanglement with subsystem sizes and whether the entanglement equipartition persists at (2+1)D quantum critical points—remain largely unexplored and lack direct numerical verifications. This calls for scalable numerical methods that can access symmetry-resolved entanglement in interacting systems beyond 1D.

In this article, we introduce an unbiased quantum Monte Carlo (QMC) algorithm for computing symmetry-resolved Rényi entropies in interacting systems beyond one dimension. Building on the charged-moments framework
18
, we show that, in an
n
-replica geometry, the charged moment is related to the expectation value of a subsystem disorder operator, which is therefore naturally accessible to QMC sampling. The symmetry-resolved contribution hidden in the total Rényi entropy can then be obtained by Fourier transforming the charged moments, which are conjugate to the moments projected onto the corresponding symmetry sectors.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We first introduce the concept of symmetry-resolved entanglement and the equipartition of entanglement entropy. We then present our general QMC framework for computing symmetry-resolved Rényi entropies. Next, we briefly describe the models studied in this work, namely the 1D and 2D quantum Ising models and the 1D Heisenberg chain. Finally, we present the results for these models and discuss limitations and future directions. In Appendix
A
, we benchmark our QMC-based method against exact diagonalization.

Symmetry-Resolved Entanglement Entropy.
—

For a bipartite quantum system described by the density matrix
\rho

A

\rho_{A}=\bigoplus_{q}\tilde{\rho}_{A}(q)\,,

(1)

where

\rho_{A}=\bigoplus_{q}P(q)\rho_{A}(q)\,,

(2)

where
\rho_{A}(q)=\tilde{\rho}_{A}(q)/\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{\rho}_{A}(q)]
is the normalized RDM block and
P(q)=\mathrm{Tr}[\tilde{\rho}_{A}(q)]

General Methods.
—
Our method starts with the charged moment defined as
18

Z_{n}(\alpha)=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}^{\,n}\,e^{i\alpha Q_{A}})\,.

(7)

Treating
\alpha
as a continuous variable in
(-\pi,\pi)
, the Fourier transform of
Z_{n}(\alpha)
yields

Z_{n}(q)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{d\alpha}{2\pi}e^{-i\alpha q}Z_{n}(\alpha)=\mathrm{Tr}(\Pi_{q}\rho_{A}^{n})\,,

(8)

where
\Pi_{q}
is the projector onto the subspace with
Q_{A}=q
.

For a discrete
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
symmetry, the integral in Eq. (
8
) is replaced with a discrete Fourier transform

Z_{n}(q)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{-i\frac{2\pi k}{N}q}\;Z_{n}\!\left(\alpha=\frac{2\pi k}{N}\right)\ =\mathrm{Tr}(\Pi_{q}\rho_{A}^{n}),\

(9)

where
q=0,...,N-1
and
\alpha
can only take discrete values
2\pi k/N
with
k=0,...,N-1
.

It then follows immediately that

Z_{1}(q)=\mathrm{Tr}(\Pi_{q}\rho_{A})\equiv P(q),

(10)

and

Z_{n}(q)=P(q)^{n}\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\rho_{A}(q)^{n}\right].

(11)

Thus the
n
-th SRRE reads

S_{n}(q)=\frac{1}{1-n}\ln\!\frac{Z_{n}(q)}{\big[Z_{1}(q)\big]^{n}}\,.

(12)

Based on the above equations, the
core
of our method is to measure
Z_{1}(\alpha)
and
Z_{n}(\alpha)
separately in QMC simulations, and then obtain
Z_{1}(q)
and
Z_{n}(q)
by Fourier transforming these charged moments according to Eq. (
8
). The main advantage of this approach, as we will show, is that within the QMC framework
e^{i\alpha Q_{A}}
can be regarded as a physical observable defined on the
n
-sheeted Riemann surface associated with
\rho_{A}^{\,n}
. As a result,
Z_{n}(q)
for multiple values of
q
can in principle be extracted from a single QMC simulation, rather than requiring a separate simulation for each
q
.

For
n=1
, the charged moment reduces to

Z_{1}(\alpha)=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}e^{i\alpha Q_{A}})=\langle e^{i\alpha Q_{A}}\rangle_{Z}
, namely the expectation value of
e^{i\alpha Q_{A}}
in the standard QMC ensemble, as illustrated in Fig.
1
(c). This quantity coincides with the expectation value of the nonlocal disorder operator associated with the underlying symmetry, which has been extensively studied in a variety of quantum many-body systems
46
;
39
;
40
;
11
. In the present work, however, we use it simply as an auxiliary quantity for accessing the SRRE.
For
n\geq 2
, we have

Z_{n}(\alpha)=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}^{n}e^{i\alpha Q_{A}})}{\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}^{n})}\,\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}^{n})=\langle e^{i\alpha Q_{A}}\rangle_{Z_{A}^{(n)}}e^{(1-n)S_{A}^{(n)}}\,,

(13)

where
\langle\cdots\rangle_{Z_{A}^{(n)}}
denotes the normalized expectation value in the QMC ensemble defined on the corresponding
n
-sheeted replica manifold. Equivalently, if
Z
is the ordinary partition function and
Z_{A}^{(n)}
denotes the partition function on the glued replica manifold, then
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}^{n})=Z_{A}^{(n)}/Z^{n}
. In this work we focus on the case
n=2
. The corresponding simulation is performed in a two-replica ensemble, in which subsystem
A
is glued together along the imaginary-time direction, as illustrated in Fig.
1
(c).

Since the scaling forms of

Models.
—
The first model we consider in this work is the transverse-field Ising model (TFIM) in 1D and 2D with the following Hamiltonian:

H=-J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\sigma_{i}^{z}\sigma_{j}^{z}-h\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{x}\,,

(15)

where
\langle i,j\rangle
refers to the nearest-neighbors and the Hamiltonian possesses the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
global symmetry. The system undergoes an ordered-to-disordered phase transition when tuning the magnitude of
h/J
. The
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry is spontaneously broken inside the ordered phase and maintained both at the critical point and inside the disordered phase. We perform all calculations at the corresponding critical points of the model, namely
h_{c}/J=1
for the 1D chain and
h_{c}/J\approx 3.044
for the square lattice
5
;
27
.

For the TFIM, the global
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
spin-flip symmetry splits the Hilbert space of subsystem
A
into even (
q=0
) and odd (
q=1
) parity sectors. The corresponding symmetry operator restricted to
A
is
X=\prod_{i\in A}\sigma_{i}^{x}
. Consequently, the discrete Fourier transform in Eq. (
9
) reduces to two charged moments only, evaluated at
\alpha=0
and
\alpha=\pi
, corresponding to the identity operator and
X
, respectively.

From the two key observables,
\langle X\rangle_{Z}
and
\langle X\rangle_{Z_{A}^{(2)}}
, we can reconstruct the symmetry-resolved moments for the charge sectors
q=0,1
:

\displaystyle Z_{1}(q)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1+(-1)^{q}\langle X\rangle_{Z}\bigr)\,,

(16a)

\displaystyle Z_{2}(q)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\,e^{-S_{A}^{(2)}}\bigl(1+(-1)^{q}\langle X\rangle_{Z_{A}^{(2)}}\bigr)\,.

(16b)

Substituting these expressions into Eq. (
12
) yields the closed-form result:

S_{2}(q)-S_{A}^{(2)}=-\ln\!\left[\frac{1+(-1)^{q}\langle X\rangle_{Z_{A}^{(2)}}}{\bigl(1+(-1)^{q}\langle X\rangle_{Z}\bigr)^{2}}\right]-\ln 2\,.

(17)

Therefore, for the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
TFIM, the subtracted symmetry-resolved Rényi entropy can be obtained directly from disorder operator expectation values measured in the single- and two-replica ensembles, without explicitly performing the Fourier transform or separately evaluating the total Rényi EE.

The second model we consider is the spin-
\frac{1}{2}
antiferromagnetic Heisenberg chain, whose low-energy physics is described by a
(1+1)
D conformal field theory. The Hamiltonian reads

H=J\sum_{i=1}^{N}\vec{S}_{i}\cdot\vec{S}_{i+1}\,,

(18)

with periodic boundary conditions,
\vec{S}_{N+1}=\vec{S}_{1}
. Although the model exhibits full

-L_{A}/2
to

Discussions.
—
In summary, we have developed a scalable and unbiased quantum Monte Carlo approach for computing symmetry-resolved Rényi entropies in interacting quantum many-body systems. By relating charged moments to expectation values of subsystem disorder operators on replica manifolds, the method provides a practical route for computing symmetry-resolved entanglement in settings that are difficult to access with existing analytical and numerical approaches. More broadly, it establishes a direct connection between field-theoretic formulations of symmetry-resolved entanglement and large-scale lattice simulations.

Our results provide representative benchmarks of the method in several settings. For the 1D TFIM, we recover the CFT-predicted logarithmic scaling of the disorder operator. For the 1D Heisenberg chain, our data are consistent with the characteristic double-logarithmic subleading contribution and the
\mathcal{O}(1/\ln L)
finite-size corrections associated with
U(1)
symmetry. In the 2D TFIM at criticality, the data are well described by an area law supplemented by a subleading correction consistent with a logarithmic form within the accessible system sizes. For all models studied here, the subtracted SRREs in different symmetry sectors converge toward a common asymptotic value, providing numerical evidence consistent with entanglement equipartition.

The present framework can be extended in several concrete directions. A natural next step is to generalize the method from Abelian symmetries to non-Abelian ones, where symmetry resolution is organized by irreducible representations rather than a single conserved charge. Such an extension would make it possible to study how entanglement is distributed among different representation sectors in interacting lattice models. Another important direction is to apply the method to additional
(2+1)D
critical systems in order to clarify the structure of subleading corrections to symmetry-resolved entanglement and to test the robustness of entanglement equipartition beyond the Ising universality class. It would also be interesting to use this approach in sign-problem-free models with more exotic ground states, such as topological phases or deconfined quantum critical points, where symmetry-resolved entanglement may provide information that is not accessible from conventional entropy measures alone. More broadly, the method provides a practical numerical tool for studying the interplay between symmetry and entanglement in strongly correlated quantum many-body systems.
