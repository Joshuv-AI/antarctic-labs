Toward Hop-Independent Fidelity in Quantum Data Centers: Resource Requirements for Entanglement Purification
Mohadeseh Azari, Anoosha Fayyaz, Amy Babay, David Tipper, Prashant Krishnamurthy, Kaushik Seshadreesan

Abstract
Quantum data-center networks must distribute entanglement between QPUs over paths whose length grows with system scale, but each entanglement-swapping step reduces the quality of the raw end-to-end state. Topology, multiplexing, and repeated connection attempts can increase the number of raw end-to-end copies available for a request, yet they do not answer the central resource question: whether those copies are sufficient to remove, via entanglement purification, the fidelity loss caused by multi-hop distribution. We study this question through a topology-independent black-box model of the network. Each elementary link is modeled as a Werner state with parameter w 0 w_{0} , so ideal swapping over an ℓ \ell -link path produces equal-quality raw copies with Werner parameter w 0 ℓ w_{0}^{\ell} ; purification succeeds if it outputs at least one state with Werner parameter at least w 0 w_{0} with probability at least p th p_{\mathrm{th}} . We compare recursive BBPSSW purification with higher-order r r -to- 1 1 bilocal-Clifford purification protocols of Jansen et al. , using an all-in recursive schedule whose success probability is computed by exact dynamic programming. The resulting resource landscapes show a threshold structure governed by the Werner entanglement condition w 0 ℓ > 1 / 3 w_{0}^{\ell}>1/3 and demonstrate that multi-copy purification substantially improves both feasibility and copy efficiency. Across the evaluated grid, the Jansen family requires fewer copies than BBPSSW at more than 96 % 96\% of shared feasible points; at p th = 0.70 p_{\mathrm{th}}=0.70 , the median copy budget drops from 268 268 to 30 30 . These results provide a quantitative purification-resource benchmark for assessing whether future quantum data-center architectures can practically support hop-independent end-to-end entanglement quality.

Entanglement purification provides a natural mechanism for converting multiple imperfect entangled copies into fewer copies with higher quality. Foundational purification protocols established that noisy entanglement can be probabilistically distilled using local operations and classical communication

Although the present work focuses instead on multi-hop entanglement distribution inside quantum data-center networks, the same basic tension appears: swapping creates lower-quality end-to-end states, and purification requires multiple copies to recover quality. Recent work on quantum-network purification has examined practical constraints such as noisy operations, finite memories, and scheduling

These lines of work establish the purification mechanisms, yet they do not directly address this paper’s central question: before committing to a topology, multiplexing strategy, or scheduling policy, how many raw end-to-end copies does a multi-hop Quantum Data Center Network require to achieve fidelity-scalable entanglement distribution?

We formalize this question through the notion of
hop-independent fidelity
. Throughout this work, let
\ell
denote the number of elementary links along an end-to-end path, and let
w_{0}
denote the Werner parameter of a successfully generated elementary link. Under ideal entanglement swapping, the raw end-to-end Werner parameter over an
\ell
-link path is modeled as

w_{\mathrm{raw}}(\ell)=w_{0}^{\ell}.

(1)

We say that a purification procedure achieves hop-independent fidelity for the pair
(w_{0},\ell)
if, using
n_{0}
raw end-to-end copies of quality
w_{0}^{\ell}

w_{\mathrm{out}}\geq w_{0}

(2)

with success probability at least
p_{\mathrm{th}}
. The elementary-link quality
w_{0}
is therefore the target benchmark. This choice is stricter and more scale-aware than requiring the end-to-end state to exceed a fixed application-level threshold: the goal is not merely to keep the final state usable, but to remove the quality penalty associated solely with traversing multiple links.

This paper studies hop-independent fidelity in a topology-independent black-box model. Figure

In the black-box model, we do not explicitly model how routing is selected, how many paths are used, or how multiplexing, scheduling, and memory constraints interact. Instead, the network core is treated only as a copy-supply mechanism that provides raw end-to-end Werner states over a path of length
\ell
. We then isolate the purification resource requirement itself: for a given elementary-link quality, path length, purification protocol, and target success probability, what is the minimum number of raw end-to-end copies required to recover elementary-link-level quality? This abstraction gives a necessary resource benchmark for any concrete copy-supply mechanism, including topology-induced path diversity, multiplexed attempts, repeated routing trials, or memory-assisted scheduling.

The contributions of this paper are as follows. First, we introduce a black-box formulation of hop-independent fidelity based on elementary-link Werner-parameter recovery. Second, we compute the minimum number of raw end-to-end copies required to satisfy both the fidelity condition

Our results show that the feasibility of hop-independent Werner-parameter recovery is governed primarily by the raw end-to-end quality
w_{0}^{\ell}
and by the purification dynamics. The boundary of the feasible region closely tracks the entanglement threshold of the Werner input states, while increasing the required success probability mainly increases the number of copies needed rather than qualitatively changing the feasible region. Across the regimes studied, multi-copy

1

These results provide a topology-independent resource benchmark for assessing whether future quantum data-center architectures can practically support high-quality end-to-end entanglement over multiple hops.

II
Black-Box Analysis of Hop-Independent Fidelity

As a first stage toward understanding hop-independent fidelity, we separate the quality-recovery problem from the network mechanism that supplies raw entangled pairs. This separation does not imply that topology is irrelevant: a concrete architecture ultimately determines whether the required copies can be generated with sufficient probability and timing. Rather, the goal of the black-box analysis is to identify the purification resource requirement itself. We therefore represent the network only through the resource it presents to the purification layer: a collection of raw end-to-end Werner
states obtained across an
\ell
-link path.

For an elementary-link Werner parameter
w_{0}
, ideal swapping over
\ell
links produces raw end-to-end states whose quality decreases with path length. The central question is whether a finite number of such raw copies can be converted into at least one output state whose Werner parameter again matches the elementary-link value
w_{0}
. We use the minimum required number of raw copies as the resource measure and require the recovery to succeed
with probability at least
p_{\mathrm{th}}
.

This section develops the corresponding resource model. We first define the black-box interface between the network and the purification layer. We then specify the Werner-state assumptions and the purification maps used for both recursive BBPSSW and higher-order
r
-to-
1
protocols. Finally, we describe the numerical search used to compute the minimum copy budget and use it to characterize the feasible operating region for hop-independent
Werner-parameter recovery.

II-A

Black-Box View

The black-box model should be interpreted as an interface model between the network layer and the purification layer. For a requested QPU pair, we condition on the availability of
n_{0}
raw end-to-end entangled copies generated over paths of the same length
\ell
. Under the ideal-swapping model used below, these copies are represented as identical Werner states with raw end-to-end parameter
w_{0}^{\ell}
. They are therefore equal-quality inputs to the purification stage. The physical and architectural mechanisms that create them—including routing, path diversity, multiplexed link attempts, memory scheduling, and repeated connection trials—are treated in the present black-box analysis as external copy-supply mechanisms rather than as explicitly modeled network processes.

This modeling choice isolates the purification requirement. For fixed elementary-link quality
w_{0}
, path length
\ell
, purification protocol, and success threshold
p_{\mathrm{th}}
, the black-box problem is to find the smallest copy budget
n_{0}
that can recover elementary-link quality. The answer does not claim that a particular topology, by itself, can supply those copies. Rather, it specifies the copy-supply requirement that a network implementation would need to meet, whether through topology-induced path diversity, multiplexing, repeated attempts, memory resources, or a combination of these mechanisms. In this sense, the black-box analysis gives a necessary resource benchmark for assessing whether hop-independent fidelity is practical in a concrete architecture.

The abstraction also separates two distinct failure modes. If no tested purification configuration can raise the raw end-to-end Werner parameter back to
w_{0}
, then the bottleneck is fidelity recovery: the available input quality is too low for the purification family under consideration. If such a configuration exists but its success probability is small, then the bottleneck is operational: more purification attempts, and hence more raw copies, are required to reach the target success probability. The remaining subsections make these two conditions explicit and compute the
minimum copy budget required to satisfy both.

II-B

Werner-State Model and Purification Maps

Each successfully established elementary link is modeled as a two-qubit
Werner state

\rho_{W}(w_{0})=w_{0}\ket{\Phi^{+}}\bra{\Phi^{+}}+\frac{1-w_{0}}{4}I_{4},

(3)

where
\ket{\Phi^{+}}
is a Bell state and
I_{4}
is the
4\times 4
identity operator. The parameter
w_{0}
is the elementary-link Werner parameter and serves as the quality benchmark that purification must recover. The numerical study focuses on the high-quality operating regime
w_{0}\in[0.5,1]
, which is the regime relevant for link-level recovery in the multi-hop settings
considered here.

It is useful to distinguish the Werner parameter from the Bell-state fidelity. For the convention in Eq. (
3
), the fidelity with respect to
\ket{\Phi^{+}}
is

F=\bra{\Phi^{+}}\rho_{W}(w_{0})\ket{\Phi^{+}}=\frac{1+3w_{0}}{4}.

(4)

Thus, the Werner-state entanglement boundary corresponds to
w_{0}>1/3
, or equivalently
F>1/2
. This distinction is important because the numerical range
w_{0}\in[0.5,1]
is an operating-regime choice, whereas the analytical boundary used later in the resource landscapes follows from
the entanglement condition
w_{\mathrm{raw}}>1/3
.

We consider an end-to-end connection over a path of length
\ell
, where
\ell
denotes the number of elementary links along the path. Under ideal entanglement swapping, the raw end-to-end Werner parameter is

w_{\mathrm{raw}}(\ell)=w_{0}^{\ell}.

(5)

Accordingly, the purification input consists of
n_{0}
identical raw end-to-end Werner states, each with parameter
w_{0}^{\ell}
. The copies are therefore equal-quality inputs corresponding to the same path length in the black-box model. A purification procedure achieves hop-independent fidelity for the pair
(w_{0},\ell)
if it produces at least one output state satisfying

w_{\mathrm{out}}\geq w_{0}

(6)

with probability at least
p_{\mathrm{th}}
. Here
p_{\mathrm{th}}
is the operational success threshold, and
n_{0}
is the raw-copy budget supplied to the purification stage before the protocol begins.

The model is intentionally idealized in order to isolate the resource cost of purification. We assume identical elementary-link Werner parameters, ideal entanglement swapping, ideal local operations inside the purification protocol, and no memory decoherence or storage loss. After each purification step, the output is represented by its Werner-twirled form, so that state quality remains described by a single Werner parameter. These assumptions remove device-level imperfections other than the initial degradation introduced by multi-hop swapping.

We compare two purification families. The first is the Bennett
et al.
BBPSSW protocol, used as the canonical
2
-to-
1
recursive baseline
[
3
]
. The second is the family of higher-order
r
-to-
1
protocols of Jansen
et al.
, obtained through bi-local Clifford distillation
[
12
]
. For the Jansen protocols, we evaluate the output-Werner-parameter and success-probability maps reported in Tables 5 and 6 of Appendix E of Ref.
[
12
]
. When multiple candidate maps are available for a given block size, the numerical search selects the configuration that minimizes the required copy budget while satisfying both the fidelity and success-probability constraints. The implementation used to generate the numerical results is made available in the accompanying code repository

[
1
]
.

A single purification round with block size
r
is represented by two maps,

w^{\prime}=f_{r}(w),\qquad p_{\mathrm{round}}=g_{r}(w),

(7)

where
w
is the Werner parameter of the input copies,
w^{\prime}
is the Werner parameter of the output copy conditioned on success, and
p_{\mathrm{round}}
is the success probability of the round. For BBPSSW,
r=2
. For the Jansen family,
r
is the number of input copies consumed by one
r
-to-
1
purification round.

For the BBPSSW baseline, we use the standard Werner-state recurrence. Using the fidelity variable
F=(1+3w)/4
, one successful BBPSSW round maps two identical Werner states to an output with fidelity

F^{\prime}=\frac{F^{2}+\frac{1}{9}(1-F)^{2}}{F^{2}+\frac{2}{3}F(1-F)+\frac{5}{9}(1-F)^{2}},

(8)

and round success probability

g_{2}(F)=F^{2}+\frac{2}{3}F(1-F)+\frac{5}{9}(1-F)^{2}.

(9)

The output fidelity is then converted back to the Werner parameter using

w^{\prime}=\frac{4F^{\prime}-1}{3}.

(10)

II-C

Recursive Purification Schedule

A single purification round may improve the raw end-to-end state but may not be sufficient to recover the elementary-link Werner parameter. We therefore allow purification to be applied recursively. The recursion depth
k
denotes the number of purification levels. At each level, available copies are grouped into disjoint blocks of size
r
, and one
r
-to-
1
purification attempt is applied to each block. Successful outputs from one level become
the input copies for the next level.

The numerical analysis uses an
all-in
schedule. At each purification level, all currently available copies are used immediately to form as many
r
-copy blocks as possible. Any leftover copies that cannot form a complete block are not used at that level. This differs from partitioning the initial copy budget into independent complete depth-
k
trees of size
r^{k}
. The all-in schedule pools all successful outputs after each level before forming
the next set of purification blocks.

This choice is motivated by the structure of recursive purification. The early purification levels act on the lowest-quality states, since their inputs are closest to the raw value
w_{0}^{\ell}
. These levels therefore tend to have the largest attrition. Allocating surplus copies at the earliest levels is resource efficient because it increases the number of intermediate successful outputs available for later, higher-quality purification levels. By contrast, a fixed collection of independent depth-
k
trees reserves copies for complete trees in advance and cannot pool intermediate successes across trees. The all-in schedule used here is therefore a natural copy-efficient strategy for the black-box resource calculation.

For fixed block size
r
and recursion depth
k
, the Werner parameter evolves deterministically conditioned on success. Starting from the raw end-to-end Werner parameter

w^{(0)}=w_{0}^{\ell},

(11)

the Werner parameter after purification level
j
is

w^{(j)}=f_{r}\!\left(w^{(j-1)}\right),\qquad j=1,\ldots,k,

(12)

and the corresponding single-block success probability at that level is

p_{j}=g_{r}\!\left(w^{(j-1)}\right).

(13)

The final Werner parameter after
k
levels is

w_{\mathrm{out}}(w_{0},\ell,r,k)=w^{(k)}.

(14)

A purification configuration is fidelity-feasible only if

w_{\mathrm{out}}(w_{0},\ell,r,k)\geq w_{0}.

(15)

The success probability is computed by tracking the full probability distribution of the number of copies that survive each purification level. Let
N_{j}
be the random variable denoting the number of available copies after level
j
, with
N_{0}=n_{0}
. Suppose that, before level
j
, there are
N_{j-1}=n
available copies. The all-in schedule forms

b(n)=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{r}\right\rfloor

(16)

complete purification blocks. Each block succeeds independently with probability
p_{j}
, because all blocks at level
j
act on copies with the same Werner parameter
w^{(j-1)}
. Therefore, conditioned on
N_{j-1}=n
, the number of outputs that survive to the next level is binomially distributed:

N_{j}\mid N_{j-1}=n\sim\mathrm{Binomial}\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n}{r}\right\rfloor,p_{j}\right).

(17)

This equation is the one-level transition rule for the all-in schedule. For example, if
n
copies are available at level
j
, then only
\lfloor n/r\rfloor
purification attempts can be made; the possible number of surviving outputs is any integer
m\in\{0,\ldots,\lfloor n/r\rfloor\}
.

Let

q_{j}(m)=\Pr[N_{j}=m]

(18)

denote the probability that exactly
m
copies are available after level
j
. The initial distribution is deterministic:

q_{0}(m)=\begin{cases}1,&m=n_{0},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(19)

Using the law of total probability and the binomial transition in Eq. (
17
), the distribution after level
j
is

q_{j}(m)=\sum_{n\geq 0}q_{j-1}(n){\left\lfloor n/r\right\rfloor\choose m}p_{j}^{m}(1-p_{j})^{\left\lfloor n/r\right\rfloor-m}.

(20)

The summation marginalizes over all possible numbers
n
of copies that could have reached level
j
. For each such
n
, the binomial factor gives the probability that exactly
m
of the
\lfloor n/r\rfloor
purification blocks succeed. The binomial coefficient is taken to be zero when
m>\lfloor n/r\rfloor
, so impossible transitions do not contribute.

After
k
purification levels, the protocol succeeds if at least one copy remains. Hence the total success probability of the all-in recursive schedule is

P_{\mathrm{succ}}(n_{0};w_{0},\ell,r,k)=\Pr[N_{k}\geq 1]=1-q_{k}(0).

(21)

The operational feasibility condition is therefore

w_{\mathrm{out}}(w_{0},\ell,r,k)\geq w_{0},\quad P_{\mathrm{succ}}(n_{0};w_{0},\ell,r,k)\geq p_{\mathrm{th}}.

(22)

This formulation makes the two bottlenecks explicit. The first is a fidelity bottleneck: the recursive map must be capable of raising the raw Werner parameter
w_{0}^{\ell}
back to at least
w_{0}
. The second is an operational bottleneck: even when the target Werner parameter is reachable, enough raw copies must be supplied so that the all-in schedule produces at least one successful final output with probability at least
p_{\mathrm{th}}
.

As a representative numerical example of the all-in calculation, consider
w_{0}=0.9327
,
\ell=9
, and
p_{\mathrm{th}}=0.75
. The raw end-to-end input to purification has Werner parameter
w_{0}^{\ell}=0.5343
. For this operating point, the resource-optimized Jansen configuration uses block size
r=4
and recursion depth
k=2
. Conditioned on success, the first purification level maps the input Werner parameter to
w^{(1)}=0.7247
with single-block success probability
p_{1}=0.2318
, and the second level maps it to
w^{(2)}=0.9327
with single-block success probability
p_{2}=0.4188
. Thus, after two purification levels, the output recovers the elementary-link Werner parameter.

The copy budget is determined by the all-in schedule. With
n_{0}=216
raw copies, the first level forms
\lfloor 216/4\rfloor=54
four-copy purification blocks. The number of first-level successes is therefore distributed as
\mathrm{Binomial}(54,p_{1})
. These successful outputs are then pooled and regrouped into four-copy blocks for the second purification level, whose block success probability is
p_{2}
. Evaluating the dynamic program in Eq. (
20
) gives
P_{\mathrm{succ}}=0.7527
for
n_{0}=216
. By contrast, with
n_{0}=215
raw copies, only
\lfloor 215/4\rfloor=53
first-level blocks can be formed, leaving three unused copies at the first level, and the resulting success probability is
P_{\mathrm{succ}}=0.7452
. Hence, for this Jansen configuration and threshold
p_{\mathrm{th}}=0.75
, the minimum copy budget is
n_{0}=216
.

For each operating point, the black-box resource problem is to find the smallest raw-copy budget
n_{0}
for which at least one candidate purification configuration satisfies the operational feasibility condition in Eq. (
22
). Here, a candidate configuration means one choice from the finite search set: a purification family, a block size
r
, and a recursion depth
k
. The search is therefore performed over the protocol family, purification block size
r
, recursion depth
k
, and copy budget
n_{0}
. For each candidate
(r,k)
, we first evaluate the deterministic Werner-parameter recursion in Eq. (
12
). If the resulting output parameter fails to satisfy
w_{\mathrm{out}}(w_{0},\ell,r,k)\geq w_{0}
, the configuration is discarded before any copy-budget search is performed. Otherwise, the corresponding level-dependent success probabilities
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\}
are passed to the all-in dynamic program of Eq. (

).

The remaining task is to find the smallest
n_{0}
such that

P_{\mathrm{succ}}(n_{0};w_{0},\ell,r,k)\geq p_{\mathrm{th}}.

(23)

Unlike an independent-tree model, this success probability does not have a closed-form expression in terms of a fixed tree success probability. Extra copies are used at the earliest purification levels and are pooled through the all-in schedule. Therefore, for each candidate
(r,k)
, we evaluate

directly using the dynamic program. Since adding more raw copies cannot decrease the probability of producing at least one final purified output,

is monotone non-decreasing in
n_{0}
. We exploit this monotonicity by bracketing a feasible copy budget and then applying binary search to identify the minimum feasible
n_{0}
.

III
Discussion

The black-box analysis in this paper deliberately separates the purification-side resource requirement from the network-side mechanism that supplies raw end-to-end copies. This separation makes the minimum copy budget transparent, but it also leaves several important system-level questions open. The most immediate next step is to open the black box and ask whether concrete quantum data-center network architectures can supply the required
n_{0}
raw copies efficiently. Different architectures may do so in different ways: path diversity may provide parallel end-to-end copies, multiplexed elementary links may increase the number of successful raw states per time window, repeated routing attempts may accumulate copies over time, and quantum memories may store successful states until enough inputs are available for purification. A topology-aware extension of this work would therefore compare candidate QDCN topologies by the copy budgets they can support as a function of path length, elementary-link success probability, memory capacity, and scheduling policy.

A natural topology-aware continuation is to instantiate the copy-supply side of the model in concrete QDCN architectures. Recent QDC infrastructure work, including Cisco-led studies, has proposed dynamically switched quantum data center networks and benchmarked representative architectures such as QFly, BCube, Clos, and Fat-Tree under optical loss, contention, and scheduling constraints
[
21
,
20
]
. These architectures provide different tradeoffs between path length, path diversity, shared BSM resources, and switching overhead, all of which affect whether the black-box copy budgets computed in this paper can be supplied efficiently. In particular, BCube-like server-centric designs are an important candidate for follow-up study because their structured path diversity can provide multiple end-to-end copies for the same QPU pair
[
11
]
. We study this topology-aware extension, including BCube-based copy supply and recursive network-level fidelity recovery, in a companion journal manuscript currently in preparation
[
2
]
.

A related issue is that the present resource calculation is naturally a single-demand analysis. For one requested QPU pair, especially a worst-case diameter-apart pair, a topology-aware implementation may need to activate a large set of candidate paths or reserve a large fraction of the available network resources in order to generate the
n_{0}
raw copies required by the black-box benchmark. In this sense, the topology-aware realization of the black-box copy budget may resemble a flooding or resource-reservation procedure for a single end-to-end demand. This is appropriate for measuring the intrinsic resource requirement of hop-independent fidelity, but quantum data centers must ultimately support many QPU–QPU demands in parallel. Multiple demands could be served by time-division access, where requests share the same physical resources across different time windows, or by network-division access, where disjoint regions, path sets, wavelengths, or memory banks are allocated to different demands. Prior work on multi-flow entanglement routing and spatial or time multiplexing provides useful starting points for this direction
[
15
,
16
]
. Integrating such demand-level scheduling with the purification copy budgets computed here is an important next step toward a complete QDCN resource model.

The purification schedule itself also leaves room for improvement. This paper uses an all-in recursive schedule, in which all available copies are used as early as possible and successful outputs are pooled before the next purification level. This choice is motivated by the fact that the first purification levels act on the lowest-quality states and therefore suffer the largest attrition. However, the all-in rule is not claimed to be globally optimal over all possible purification policies. More general adaptive schedules could decide when to store, discard, regroup, or delay intermediate outputs based on the realized number of successes at each level. Such policies may reduce the required copy budget, especially in regimes where the success probabilities vary strongly between purification levels.

Another future direction is to compare where purification should be applied inside the entanglement-distribution pipeline. The present black-box model assumes that routing and entanglement swapping first create raw end-to-end copies, and purification is then applied to those end-to-end states. This swap-then-purify abstraction is natural for isolating the end-to-end copy requirement, but it is not the only possible architecture. A network could instead purify elementary links or intermediate segments before swapping, or use a hybrid strategy in which purification is applied at multiple scales. These alternatives introduce different tradeoffs between memory time, classical communication, local operation overhead, and the quality of the states entering later swapping operations. Comparing purify-then-swap, swap-then-purify, and multi-level hybrid schedules would clarify whether the black-box end-to-end copy budget can be reduced by moving purification earlier in the network.

The present model also treats all raw copies as identical Werner states generated over paths of the same length. This gives a clean benchmark, but real QDCNs will generally produce heterogeneous inputs: different paths may have different lengths, elementary links may have different Werner parameters, and storage time may vary across copies. A more complete model should therefore allow purification with unequal-quality inputs, imperfect Bell-state measurements, local gate noise, finite memory coherence, and nonuniform link-generation probabilities. These effects are likely to increase the required resources and may change which purification block sizes and recursion depths are optimal. Extending the dynamic-programming framework to heterogeneous input states would make the benchmark more directly applicable to device-level network simulations.

Finally, the current analysis focuses on bipartite entanglement resources and discards copies that cannot be grouped into a full purification block at a given level. A richer architecture could store and reuse leftover entanglement, recycle unused paths, or exploit multipartite entangled states when they are available. Multipartite resources may be especially relevant in data-center settings where many QPUs participate in distributed algorithms and where the same physical fabric must support both pairwise and multi-party entanglement requests. Understanding how leftover bipartite and multipartite resources can be recycled into future purification or routing decisions could further reduce the effective cost of hop-independent fidelity.

IV
Conclusion

This paper developed a topology-independent purification-resource benchmark
for hop-independent fidelity in multi-hop quantum data-center networks. We
modeled each elementary link as a Werner state with parameter
w_{0}
and
treated an
\ell
-link end-to-end connection as providing raw copies with
Werner parameter
w_{0}^{\ell}
. The goal was to determine the minimum number of
raw end-to-end copies needed to produce at least one purified output with
Werner parameter
w_{\mathrm{out}}\geq w_{0}
and success probability at least

p_{\mathrm{th}}
.

We compared recursive BBPSSW purification with higher-order

r
-to-
1
bilocal-Clifford purification protocols using an all-in recursive
schedule. The success probability of this schedule was computed by exact
dynamic programming over the number of surviving copies at each
purification level. The resulting resource landscapes show that
hop-independent Werner recovery has a threshold-like structure governed by
the raw end-to-end quality
w_{0}^{\ell}
and the Werner entanglement boundary

w_{0}^{\ell}>1/3
. Increasing the success threshold mainly increases the raw
copy budget after fidelity recovery becomes possible, while the existence of
a feasible solution is controlled primarily by the raw Werner parameter and
the purification dynamics.

Across the evaluated regimes, higher-order multi-copy purification
substantially reduces the required copy budget relative to recursive
BBPSSW and achieves recovery with shallower purification schedules. These
results identify the purification-side resource requirement that future
QDCN architectures must meet through topology, multiplexing, repeated
connection attempts, memory-assisted scheduling, or combinations of these
mechanisms. They therefore provide a quantitative starting point for
designing network architectures that can support hop-independent
end-to-end entanglement quality in practice.
