Concentration of General Stochastic Approximation Under Heavy-Tailed Markovian Noise
Shubhada Agrawal, Siva Theja Maguluri, Martin Zubeldia

Abstract
We establish maximal concentration bounds for the iterates generated by stochastic approximation algorithms with general step sizes, where the noise has a finite-state Markovian component plus a Martingale-difference component. When the Martingale-difference noise is bounded, we show that the tail of the error can be sub-Gaussian, sub-Weibull, or something lighter than any Pareto but heavier than any Weibull, depending on the step size sequence and on whether the random operator is almost surely contractive, almost surely non-expansive, or expansive with positive probability. Our analysis relies on a novel Lyapunov function involving the moment-generating function of the solution to a Poisson equation, together with an auxiliary projected algorithm. We complement the upper bounds with worst-case examples showing that qualitatively sharper bounds are impossible. We further study the case of unbounded Martingale-difference noise when the average operator is contractive, and the step sizes are of order 1 / k 1/k . In this setting, we show that if the random operator is almost surely non-expansive, then the error tail is at most three times heavier than the noise tail, whereas if the random operator is expansive with positive probability, then the error may have substantially heavier tails. These results are obtained through a novel black-box truncation argument that reduces the unbounded-noise setting to the bounded-noise case.



1
Introduction

The stochastic approximation (SA) method, introduced by
55
, is a fundamental framework for stochastic iterative algorithms and underlies many methods in modern optimization and machine learning
(
34
;
57
;
32
;
47
)
. Formally, SA provides an approach to solving fixed-point equations of the form

\bar{F}(x)=x,

where
\bar{F}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is (possibly) nonlinear. When
\bar{F}
can be evaluated exactly, the equation can be solved via the classical fixed-point iteration
x_{k+1}=\bar{F}(x_{k})
, which converges to the unique fixed point under standard contraction assumptions
(
1
)
. In many practical settings, such as reinforcement learning or large-scale stochastic optimization, the operator
\bar{F}
cannot be computed exactly due to limited information or computational constraints. In such cases, one may only have access to noisy approximations of
\bar{F}(x)
given by
F(x,S)+Z
, where
S
and
Z
are random elements defined on probability spaces
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, respectively. Therefore, a stochastic approximation recursion is used, which is of the form

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\alpha_{k}\big(F(x_{k},S_{k})+Z_{k}-x_{k}\big),

(1)

where
\{S_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
and
\{Z_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
are random sequences on
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, respectively,
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is a sequence of positive stepsizes, typically
\alpha_{k}=\alpha/(k+h)^{z}
with
\alpha,h>0
and
z\in(0,1]
. In this work, we will assume that
\{S_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is a finite-state Markov chain, and that
\{Z_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is a Martingale-difference sequence. Note that this subsumes the special case of i.i.d. noise.

The recursion given in Equation (
1
) subsumes several classical algorithms. When
\bar{F}(x)=-c\nabla J(x)+x
for a smooth and strongly convex function
J:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and some
c>0
, it coincides with stochastic gradient descent (SGD)
(
8
;
37
)
, which can be viewed as a contractive SA scheme
(
56
)
. In reinforcement learning, algorithms such as
Q
-learning
(
65
)
and temporal-difference learning
(
60
)
also take the form of Equation (
1
), with
\bar{F}
corresponding to the Bellman operator
(
5
;
3
)
. Understanding the convergence properties of the iterates
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
generated by Equation (
1
) is thus of fundamental importance.

Classical results on stochastic approximation focus on asymptotic convergence and almost sure stability
(
55
;
63
;
44
;
7
;
35
)
. More recently, attention has shifted toward non-asymptotic or finite-sample analysis, which provides explicit bounds on
\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|_{c}
, where
x^{*}
satisfies
\bar{F}(x^{*})=x^{*}

(
6
;
58
;
13
)
. Such results yield quantitative rates of convergence and are directly applicable in high-dimensional learning settings.

Depending on the mode of convergence considered, two classes of bounds are commonly studied. The first concerns mean-square error bounds, i.e., estimates on
\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|_{c}^{2}]
, which have been extensively developed in the literature
(
58
;
6
;
17
;
13
;
64
)
. The second concerns high-probability or tail bounds of the form

\mathbb{P}(\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|_{c}\leq\epsilon),

which quantify both accuracy and confidence. High-probability guarantees are, however, significantly more difficult to establish. For instance, in the special case of the law of large numbers, viewed as an SA recursion with
\alpha_{k}=1/(k+1)
, obtaining an
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
mean-square rate is straightforward, whereas deriving exponential concentration inequalities such as Hoeffding’s
(
31
)
, Chernoff’s
(
18
)
, or Bernstein’s
(
4
)
requires stronger probabilistic control. More recently,
15
obtained such high-probability bounds under restrictive assumptions on the noise and step sizes, which we generalize in key directions that are essential for applications such as Reinforcement Learning algorithms with function approximation, and SGD with heavy-tailed noise. A detailed review of related results is provided in Section
1.3
.

1.1
Our contributions

Throughout, we assume that the dynamics are driven by finite-state Markovian noise, a setting that arises naturally in many applications such as Reinforcement Learning. In addition, the updates may contain an additive Martingale-difference noise term. In this paper, we develop maximal concentration bounds for: (1) exponentially stable SA algorithms in the presence of bounded additive Martingale-difference noise, and (2) contractive SA algorithms with
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
step sizes in the presence of arbitrary unbounded, identically-distributed, additive Martingale-difference noise. We analyze three regimes for the random operator
F
: almost surely contractive, almost surely non-expansive, and expansive with positive probability, under various step-size schedules. The main contributions of this work are summarized below.

1.

General SA with finite-state Markovian and additive bounded Martingale-difference noise.
We establish high-probability bounds for SA algorithms under diminishing step sizes of the form
\alpha_{k}=\alpha/(k+h)^{z}
, where
\alpha,h>0
and
z\in(0,1]
. In particular, we show the following.

•

When
z=1
, our bound is sub-Weibull in general, and becomes sub-Gaussian in the special case where the random operator
F
is almost surely non-expansive. These bounds are (order-wise) the best possible, as they match the lower bound established in
15
in the case of a special counter-example.

•

When
z\in(0,1)
, our bound is either sub-Gaussian when the random operator
F
is almost surely contractive, sub-Weibull when it is almost surely non-expansive, or something heavier than any Weibull but lighter than any Pareto when
F
can be expansive with positive probability. Moreover, we show that it is inevitable to obtain such distributions in all cases.

Our results provide bounds on the entire tail of the iterates as our step sizes do not depend on either the desired accuracy level or the probability tolerance level. 3.

Methodological Contributions.
To overcome the challenge of having Markovian noise, general step sizes, and potentially unbounded added noise, we develop new proof ideas to tackle each of these challenges. These new ideas involve bounding the moment-generating function (MGF) of the convergence error plus a correction term, which serves as a novel Lyapunov function in our analysis, and the construction of two key auxiliary coupled iterates, that are easier to analyze, in order to deal with general step sizes and with added unbounded noise.

We now provide more details about the challenges and our technical contributions.

1.2
Challenges & Our Techniques

Obtaining high-probability bounds in such generality presents several challenges that cannot be tackled by the proof techniques developed in the previous literature and require new ideas.

Challenge
1
: General step sizes.
When the step sizes are of the order of
1/k^{z}
, for
z\in(0,1)
, and we have “multiplicative” noise, it is known that the error cannot be uniformly bounded by any sub-Weibull distribution (see Theorem 2.3 in
15
). In this case, the error can grow exponentially fast with positive probability, and thus the “bootstrapping” proof technique from
15
does not work. To overcome this, we define a coupled SA iteration such that, after each step of the SA recursion, the result is projected onto a ball of size
B
centered at the fixed point
x^{*}
. Since these new iterates are uniformly bounded, the variance of the noise is also uniformly bounded, which prevents the error from growing exponentially fast in time. This allows us to obtain tight high-probability bounds for the projected iterates. We then show that, if the ball radius
B
is sufficiently large, the projection step is inactive with high probability, and thus the projected iterates coincide with the original ones with high probability. Therefore, the high probability bounds for the former also apply to the latter.

Challenge
2
: Added noise with general distribution.
When the added noise has a general distribution, the MGF of the error might not exist, and thus current proof techniques (such as the one in
15
for sub-Gaussian noise) fail. To overcome this, we define new coupled iterates by truncating the original noise at an arbitrarily large value. This results in a sequence of iterates generated by an SA recursion with uniformly bounded noise, for which we already have high-probability bounds. In order to link the new iterates to the original ones, we take two steps:

1.

Since the truncation may introduce a bias in the noise, the expected truncated operator may have a different fixed point, to which the new iterates converge to. Thus, the first step is to bound this bias as a function of the truncation level, crucially exploiting the fact that the expected operator is a contraction (even with the added bias).

2.

The second step is to show that, for sufficiently large truncation levels, the noise is below these levels with high probability. Therefore, the original and the new iterates coincide with high probability, and the high-probability bounds for the former also apply to the latter.

Challenge
3
: Markovian noise.
When the noise is Markovian, the expected drift of the error
\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]
can be positive due to the bias introduced by the Markov chain. This means that the convergence is not monotone in expectation, as it is with Martingale-difference noise. This prevents us from using the same Lyapunov function as in
15
, where the noise is assumed to be a Martingale-difference, and thus unbiased. Also, averaging techniques as used in
58
to obtain mean-square bounds critically rely on the linearity of the expectation that is being bounded. Our approach is more similar to the one in
28
and
41
, where they use the solution to a Poisson equation to prove tight mean-square bounds and almost sure convergence in the presence of Markovian noise, respectively, again crucially using the linearity of the expectation.

Obtaining bounds on the distribution of an average of correlated iterates is a significant step over obtaining bounds on their variance. In order to overcome this, we introduce a new Lyapunov function that uses the solution of the Poisson equation for the underlying Markov chain of the noise to compensate for bias in the drift of the error. This allows us to obtain a one step recursion on the MGF of the error along with an additive corrective term, which leads to the desired high-probability bounds on the error itself.

1.3
Related Literature

Before presenting our problem setting and main results, we briefly summarize related work on establishing concentration bounds for stochastic approximation algorithms in the settings of stochastic gradient descent (SGD), linear SA, and reinforcement learning (RL).

Stochastic Gradient Descent.

A large body of work establishes exponential high-probability bounds for SGD and its variants. In
54
;
30
, exponential high-probability bounds are obtained for SGD with non-smooth but strongly convex objectives under conditionally unbiased noise and compact iterates. The analysis was extended in
29
to the case of sub-Gaussian noise and unbounded iterates, providing one of the few results with exponential bounds under unbounded noise. Exponential high-probability bounds were also established for ergodic mirror descent with Markovian, conditionally biased noise and uniformly bounded variance in
23
, again assuming compactness of the iterates. Polynomial high-probability bounds for SGD with heavy-tailed noise in linear models were derived in
43
. In
61
, mirror descent with constant step size and i.i.d. noise of uniformly bounded variance (either almost surely bounded or sub-Gaussian) was analyzed, and exponential high-probability bounds were obtained by selecting an appropriate constant stepsize.

Reinforcement Learning.

Temporal-difference (TD) learning was introduced as an SA method for policy evaluation in RL
(
60
;
59
)
. Mean-square bounds for TD learning were established in
6
;
58
, and high-probability bounds in
20
;
51
;
11
. In
51
, the least-squares temporal difference (LSTD) algorithm, which includes projection onto a compact set, was analyzed, yielding exponential high-probability bounds for the
\ell_{2}
-error with stepsizes of order
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})
. In
20
, TD learning with linear function approximation and i.i.d. sampling was studied, yielding maximal exponential bounds for the last iterate once the number of iterations exceeds a threshold of order
\log(1/\delta)
. In the off-policy setting, finite-sample mean-square bounds were obtained in
14
;
16
, although no results are available on high-probability bounds with faster-than-polynomial decay for off-policy TD learning, with or without function approximation.

One of the earliest works establishing exponential high-probability bounds in RL is
26
, which analyzes synchronous
Q
-learning with stepsizes of order
\mathcal{O}(k^{-z})
for
z\in(1/2,1)
and obtains exponential bounds for sufficiently large iterates. More recent works
(
40
;
38
;
39
)
analyze
Q
-learning with constant step size and uniformly bounded, Markovian, possibly conditionally biased noise, establishing exponential high-probability bounds at a prescribed runtime given sufficiently long execution.

Linear Stochastic Approximation.

For linear SA, first-moment bounds on the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the error with constant step size were established in
36
;
58
. These results imply high-probability bounds with polynomial rather than exponential tails. The strongest known exponential high-probability result for linear SA appears in
21
, which analyzes a two-timescale linear SA with decreasing stepsizes and multiplicative, almost surely bounded, Martingale-difference noise. In that setting, maximal exponential bounds were obtained for all sufficiently large iterates by selecting stepsizes depending on both the runtime and the confidence level. A related line of work studies products of random matrices and applies these results to linear SA. In
24
, linear SA with constant step size and Markovian, almost surely bounded noise was considered. High-probability bounds on products of random matrices yield sub-exponential tails when the random matrices are almost surely Hurwitz and polynomial tails when they are Hurwitz only in expectation. These results were extended to Polyak–Ruppert averaged iterates in
25
;
46
.

General Stochastic Approximation.

For general nonlinear SA under arbitrary norms and decreasing stepsizes,
13
;
17
provide bounds on the second moment of the error. These moment bounds yield high-probability estimates, though without exponential tails. In
62
, SA with decreasing stepsizes and Martingale-difference sub-exponential noise is analyzed, yielding maximal exponential high-probability bounds conditioned on the iterates being sufficiently close to the fixed point after a finite time. The follow-up work
(
10
)
considers multiplicative, almost surely bounded, Markovian noise and obtains maximal exponential bounds without conditioning, although the results hold only after a transient phase, and both the bound and probability depend on the random norm of the iterate after that phase.

In a related line of research,
53
studies a general SA algorithm under the infinity norm, where the noise has a uniformly bounded Martingale-difference component and a Markovian component determining the coordinate updates. The resulting random operator is a conditionally biased estimator of the underlying operator, and exponential high-probability bounds are derived under compactness of the iterates. Moreover,
45
considers a variance-reduced version of general SA in Banach spaces with constant step size and i.i.d. multiplicative, almost surely bounded noise. By appropriately selecting the step size and the averaging scheme, exponential high-probability bounds are obtained for the error.

Finally, there are three recent papers that are close to our work. In
15
the authors obtain high probability bounds for the case of contractive operators with bounded but “multiplicative” Martingale-difference noise, with
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
step sizes. The Martingale-difference and step size choice are critical constraints for many applications, such as Reinforcement Learning, where the noise is Markovian and the step sizes are larger. Furthermore, in
52
the authors obtain high-probability bounds for Markovian noise with
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
step sizes, albeit with a convergence rate of order
\mathcal{O}\Big(k^{-\ln^{-\nu}(k)/(1-\nu)}\Big)
for
\nu\in(0,1)
, which is slower than any polynomial (which is far from the true
1/k
rate). Lastly, in
49
, the authors obtain anytime high-probability bounds for the case of Martingale-difference multiplicative noise, but only with step-sizes of order
1/k
with constants that depend on the probability tolerance level
\delta
.

2
Concentration for general stochastic approximation

In this section, we present the mathematical model and assumptions for our general stochastic approximation iterates, our main high probability bound for the squared error, and illustrations and applications of these results.

2.1
Model and Notation

Given a time-homogeneous and ergodic Markov chain
\{S_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
defined on a finite state space
\mathcal{S}
with a transition probability matrix
P
, a sequence of
d
-dimensional random vectors
\{Z_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
independent from the Markov chain, an operator
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a sequence of step sizes
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
such that
\alpha_{k}=\alpha/(k+h)^{z}
for some
\alpha,h>0
and
z\in(0,1]
, we consider the general stochastic approximation algorithm defined by the recursive equation

x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\alpha_{k}\left(F(x_{k},S_{k})+Z_{k}-x_{k}\right),

(2)

with a deterministic initial condition
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
\pi
be the unique stationary distribution of the Markov chain
\{S_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
. We define the averaged operator
\bar{F}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

\bar{F}(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[F(x,S)\right],

where
S\sim\pi
. Moreover, it is known
(
22
, Section 21.2)
that, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exists a function
V_{x}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfies the following Poisson’s Equation

F(x,s)-\mathbb{E}\left[F(x,S)\right]+(PV_{x})(s)=V_{x}(s),\qquad\forall\,s\in\mathcal{S},

(3)

where
(PV_{x})(s)=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}P(s,s^{\prime})V_{x}(s^{\prime})
. Finally, let
\|\cdot\|_{c}
be a norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be the filtration such that
\mathcal{F}_{k}:=\sigma(S_{k-1},Z_{k-1},\dots,S_{0},Z_{0})
. We make the following assumptions.

Assumption 1

(Operator
\bar{F}
)
.

The following hold for the average operator
\bar{F}
.

1.

There exists a unique
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\bar{F}(x^{*})=x^{*}
.

2.

There exist constants
A_{3},B_{3}\geq 0
such that, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\|\bar{F}(x)-x^{*}\|_{c}\leq A_{3}\|x-x^{*}\|_{c}+B_{3}.

3.

There exist constants
L_{1},L_{2}\geq 0
such that, for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
s\in\cal S
,

\displaystyle\|V_{x_{2}}(s)-V_{x_{1}}(s)\|_{c}\leq L_{1}\|x_{2}-x_{1}\|_{c},\quad\text{and}\quad\|V_{x^{*}}(s)\|_{c}\leq L_{2}.

(5)

Assumption 2

(Bounded multiplicative noise)
.

There exist constants
A_{1},B_{1}\geq 0
such that, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
s\in\mathcal{S}
, we have

\|F(x,s)-\bar{F}(x)\|_{c}\leq A_{1}\|x\|_{c}+B_{1}.

Assumption 3

(Conditionally unbiased and bounded additive noise
Z
)
.

We have
\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]=0
for all
k\geq 0
. Furthermore, there exists a constant
B_{2}\geq 0
such that, for every
k\geq 0
, we have
\|Z_{k}\|_{c}\leq B_{2}
, almost surely.

Note that Assumption
3
implies that
\{Z_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is a Martingale-difference sequence.

Remark 1
.

Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
imply that, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
k\geq 0
, we have

\|F(x,S_{k})+Z_{k}-x^{*}\|_{c}\leq(A_{1}+A_{3})\|x-x^{*}\|_{c}+B_{123}+A_{1}\|x^{*}\|_{c},\qquad\text{a.s.},

where
B_{123}=B_{1}+B_{2}+B_{3}
. Note that, if
A_{1}+A_{3}<1
, then the noisy operator
F(\cdot,S_{k})+Z_{k}
is almost surely an approximate pseudo-contraction (approximate due to the presence of additive constants on the right-hand side). On the other hand, if
A_{1}+A_{3}>1
, then the operator can be expansive with a positive probability. Later, we see that in the latter case, the tail of the error is significantly heavier. In particular, the factor

D:=A_{1}+A_{3}-1

(6)

plays a significant role in determining the tail behavior of the error.

Assumption 4

(Lyapunov Function)
.

There exists a Lyapunov function
M(\cdot)
, a norm
\|\cdot\|_{s}
, and constants
\eta,L_{s},l,u>0
such that, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla M(x-x^{*}),\bar{F}(x)-x\rangle\leq-\eta M(x-x^{*}),

(7)

\displaystyle M(y)\leq M(x)+\langle\nabla M(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{L_{s}}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}_{s},

(8)

\displaystyle lM(x)\leq\|x\|^{2}_{c}\leq uM(x),

(9)

\displaystyle\nabla M(0)=0.

(10)

While this assumption seems hard to check in practice, it is quite straightforward in many cases. For example, when the average operator
\bar{F}
is a contraction with respect to a smooth norm
\|\cdot\|_{c}
, then it can be checked that this assumption is satisfied with
M(x)=\|x-x^{*}\|_{c}^{2}
and
\|\cdot\|_{s}=\|\cdot\|_{c}
. This and other examples are explored in detail in Section
2.3
.

Finally, we have the following assumption for the step sizes
\alpha_{k}=\alpha/(k+h)^{z}
.

Assumption 5

(Conditions on tunable parameters
\alpha
and
h
)
.

When
z=1
, we have

Remark 4
.

When the operator is expansive with positive probability, i.e., when

Remark 5
.

The presence of condition

2.3.2

In this subsection, we use Theorem

(or Corollary

Consider the special case of the setting in Section

F(x,s)=f(s)

, where
{S\sim\pi}
. Next, consider the iterates produced by Equation (

and step sizes
\alpha_{k}=1/(k+1)
. It follows that

, which is the empirical mean of

. In this case, the Ergodic theorem
(
22

states that
x_{k}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\bar{f}
as

.

In order to obtain high-probability bounds, we apply Corollary
2.4.2
with
\gamma_{c}=0
,
A_{1}=0
,
B_{1}=\max_{s\in\mathcal{{S}}}|f(s)-\bar{f}|
,
B_{2}=0
,
A_{3}=0
,
B_{3}=0
,
L_{1}=0
, and
L_{2}=\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}|V(s)|
, where
V(s)
is a solution to the Poisson Equation
f(s)-\bar{f}=V(s)-[PV](s)
. For these constants, Corollary
2.4.2
simplifies to the following high-probability bound for the empirical average estimator for a stationary mean.

Corollary 2.4.2
.

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists k\in\mathbb{N}:\left|\frac{1}{k}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k-1}f(S_{i})-\bar{f}\right|^{2}\geq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log\left(\frac{k}{\delta}\right)}{k}\right)\right)\leq\delta.

Several previous works have established concentration results for the empirical average of samples from a DTMC in a fairly general setting. Our bound in Corollary
2.4.2
recovers those of, for example,

9
;

2.3.3
Linear Stochastic Approximation

We now consider the problem of finding the solution to a linear system of equations. For
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We are interested in finding
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
Ax^{*}+b=0
(or equivalently, the fixed point
x^{*}
for
Ax^{*}+b+x^{*}=x^{*}
). However,
A
and
b
are unknown. Instead, we have an oracle that, when queried at time
k\geq 0
, returns
A_{k}x+b_{k}
, where
A_{k}=A(S_{k})
and
b_{k}=b(S_{k})
are noisy versions of the unknown
A
and
b
, and
S_{k}
is the state of the underlying Markov chain at time
k
which we assume to have a finite state space and to be ergodic. Note that
(A_{k},b_{k})
has a Markovian dependence over time
k
. Below, we state our assumptions on
A
and
b
.

Assumption 7
.

We have
\mathbb{E}\left[A(S)\right]=A
and
\mathbb{E}\left[b(S)\right]=b
, where
{S\sim\pi}
. Moreover, the matrix
A
is Hurwitz, i.e., the eigenvalues of
A
have strictly negative real parts.

It is well known that, under Assumption
7
, the following stochastic approximation algorithm converges to
x^{*}
:

x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\alpha_{k}(A_{k}x_{k}+b_{k}).

In particular, Assumption
7
ensures the stability of the iterates obtained by Equation (
2.3.3
) (cf.,
58
). In fact, when
A
is Hurwitz, for the
d\times d
identity matrix

, the Lyapunov equation
A^{T}P+PA+I_{d}=0
has a unique positive definite solution, denoted by
\bar{P}
. Then, the asymptotic convergence of the iterates given by Equation (
2.3.3
) to
x^{*}
follows from the ODE method
7
.

In what follows, we present a tight concentration result for
x_{k}
. To this end, we first show that Equation (
2.3.3
) is a special case of contractive SA from the previous section, that is, the noise and the operator satisfy assumptions
2
,
3
, and
6
. Since
\bar{P}
is positive definite, we define the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\bar{P}}
with respect to
\bar{P}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\|x\|^{2}_{\bar{P}}=x^{T}\bar{P}x
. In this case, we use the norm
\|\cdot\|_{c}=\|\cdot\|_{\bar{P}}
. Since the underlying Markov chain has a finite state space, assumptions
2
and

2.3.4
Polyak-Ruppert Averaging

A key technique to improve the asymptotic variance of the iterates of a SA algorithms is Polyak-Ruppert averaging
(
50
)
, where, given the iterates defined by Equation (
2
), one computes the averaged sequence

y_{k}:=\frac{1}{k}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k-1}x_{i}.

Polyak-Ruppert averaging has been shown to yield the optimal asymptotic covariance for SA algorithms in various settings. However, until recently, its non-asymptotic high-probability behavior remained poorly understood. Recently,
33
have shown that Polyak averaging preserves the high-probability guarantees of the unaveraged iterates up to constant factors, when the underlying noise is a Martingale-difference sequence. Since i.i.d. noise is a special case of Markov noise as well as of Martingale-difference noise, combining their result (Corollary 4.2) with our bounds for step sizes with
z<1
in Theorem
2.2
, we get the following corollary for the Polyak-averaged iterates of SA with i.i.d. noise.

Corollary 2.5.1
.

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\alpha_{k}=\alpha/(k+h)^{z}
for some
z\in(0,1)
, where
\alpha
and
h
satisfy the conditions in Assumption
5
. Under assumptions
2
,
3
,
6
, and i.i.d. noise sequences
\{S_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
and
\{Z_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
, for
x_{0}\neq x^{*}
, we have the following: for
\delta\in(0,1)
and
k\geq 0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\|y_{k}-x^{*}\|_{c}^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}{k}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{f_{z}(\delta)^{2}}{k^{\min\{2z,2-z\}}}\right),

where

f_{z}(\delta)=\begin{cases}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right),&\text{ if }D<0,\\
\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{1/z},&\text{ if }D=0,\\
e^{\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1-z}{\beta z}}},\quad\text{for }\beta\in(0,1),&\text{ if }D>0.\end{cases}

From the above corollary, we see that although Polyak averaging is known to achieve the desirable asymptotic variance, its squared error exhibits significantly heavier tail than that of the unaveraged algorithm with
z=1
, especially when the operator is expansive with positive probability
(D>0)
. Hence, in such expansive settings, Polyak averaging may, in fact, be an undesirable choice. A natural direction for future research is to design algorithms that, in addition to achieving the optimal asymptotic variance, improve tail behavior.

2.4
Proof sketch for theorems
2.1
and
2.2

In this section, we provide a proof sketch of theorems
2.1
and
2.2
. We first establish the result in the simpler regime

, and subsequently, extend the argument to the remaining cases.

•

Case 1 (
D<0
).
We first show that, if the squared error

of the iterates generated by Equation (

, then the MGF of

is upper bounded by a constant. This requires the careful bounding of the drift of this expression plus a correction term based on the solution to the Poisson equation of the Markov chain of the noise.

From the constant bound on the MGF of

it follows that, for any

, the squared error

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T_{k}\alpha_{k}\log(1/\delta))
with probability at least

. Since

is of order

, this high-probability bound improves upon the original almost-sure bound

by a factor of order

. This is given in Theorem
A.2
in Appendix
A.3
.

Thus, to obtain a high-probability bound, it suffices to identify a non-decreasing almost-sure bound
T_{k}
. Lemma
A.1
provides such a worst-case bound on

, which holds almost surely and it is constant in

when

. Then, applying Theorem
A.2
with this almost-sure bound as the initial sequence

yields the bounds in Theorem

, Part

, and Theorem
2.2
, Part

.

•

Case 2 (

).

In this case, the almost sure upper bounds for the iterates are not constant in

as in the case

. For

, the almost sure upper bound scales as

when

and as

when

. For

, it grows as
\mathcal{O}(e^{k^{1-z}})
when

, and as

when

. To overcome this, we establish our high-probability bounds in two steps.

1.

Step 1 (Auxiliary algorithm).
We consider an auxiliary SA algorithm that projects its iterates at each time step onto a ball centered at

with a radius

. Applying Theorem

(from Case 1 above) with the constant sequence

and Theorem

and

We introduce the auxiliary algorithm and establish its properties in Appendix

. The proof of Theorem

(Part

(parts

and

), appears in Appendix

3
Concentration for contractive operators with unbounded identically distributed noise

In this section we present a high-probability bound for the error
\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|_{c}
for the case where step sizes are of order
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
, and where Assumption
3
on the additive noise
\{Z_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is relaxed to include arbitrary unbounded distributions, at the cost of strengthening Assumption
4
on the average operator
\bar{F}
to the case where it is a contraction (i.e., when
\bar{F}
satisfies Assumption
6
).
In particular, instead of Assumption
3
, we assume the following.

Assumption 8

(Generalized additive noise)
.

We have that
\{Z_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
are identically distributed, with
\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]=0
for all
k\geq 0
. Furthermore, there exists a non-negative random variable
W
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\|Z_{0}\|_{c}>z\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(W>z\right),\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{R}.

Under this relaxed assumption, we derive high-probability bounds for
\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|_{c}
when the averaged operator remains contractive, with the bounds expressed directly in terms of the tail behavior of
\|Z_{k}\|_{c}
. The resulting Theorem
3.1
thus substantially generalizes Theorem
2.4
for contractive average operators, beyond the bounded-noise regime.

3.1
Main results

We now present our main concentration result for this section. First, we introduce some notation. For any
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,1)
, let

denote the quantile satisfying

\mathbb{P}\left(W\leq B(\delta^{\prime})\right)=1-\delta^{\prime}.

We define a function

where

6

and Jensen’s inequality imply that

g(\delta^{\prime})\geq\frac{\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{0}{\bf 1}_{\{\|Z_{0}\|_{c}\geq B(\delta^{\prime})\}}\right]\right\|_{c}}{1-\gamma_{c}}.

Moreover, we have the following assumption on the step sizes

Assumption 9

(Conditions on tunable parameters
\alpha
and

)
.

We have

3.2
Proof of Theorem
3.1

We prove the concentration result in Theorem
3.1
in two steps. In the first step, we consider an auxiliary algorithm where the sequence
\{Z_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
of additive noise is truncated at an appropriate level
B>0
, so that the norm of the truncated noise is almost surely upper bounded by
B
. Since the additive noise is not necessarily symmetric around
0
, this truncation might introduce a bias, and the auxiliary algorithm might converge to the fixed point
\tilde{x}
of a different contractive operator. Moreover, since the noise of the auxiliary algorithm is almost surely bounded, Theorem
2.4
yield a high-probability bound for the auxiliary iterates around
\tilde{x}
.

As a second step, we relate the auxiliary iterates to the original iterates for an appropriate choice of the truncation level
B
. To this end we show that, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
we can choose the truncation level
B
sufficiently large so that the truncation is never activated with probability at least
1-\delta
, at least for the first
T
steps. Hence, the auxiliary iterates coincide with the original iterates of Equation (
2
) with probability
1-\delta
, up to time
T
. Moreover, we show that the distance between the two fixed points
x^{*}
and
\tilde{x}
is small (bounded by quantities dependent on the noise distribution). Consequently, the high probability bound obtained in the first step also holds for the original algorithm, with some adjustments for the introduced bias. This yields the concentration results stated in Theorem
3.1
.

Remark 7
.

We use the contractive property of the average operator (Assumption
6
) only to bound the bias introduced in the auxiliary iterates due to truncation of the additive noise. If the additive noise at each step is symmetric around
0
, no bias is introduced in the auxiliary iterations, and its fixed point
\tilde{x}
coincides with the original fixed point
x^{*}
. In this case, we don’t need Assumption
6
. Instead, assumptions
1
and
4
suffice, and the auxiliary iterates actually concentrate around the true fixed point
x^{*}
.

We now present the two steps of the proof in detail, introducing the auxiliary algorithm, bounding the bias in the fixed point arising from the noise truncation, and showing that the original and auxiliary iterates coincide with high probability.

3.2.1

represents a quantile for

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{B}^{c}_{T}(\delta^{\prime})\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\,k\in\left\{0,1,\dots,T-1\right\}:\|Z_{k}\|_{c}>B(\tfrac{\delta^{\prime}}{T})\right)

(26)

Recall from Section

is large.

Lemma 3.4
.

For the non-negative function

in Equation (

), the following holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\,k\leq T:\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|^{2}_{c}>2f(k,\delta^{\prime\prime},\delta^{\prime})+2g^{2}(\tfrac{\delta^{\prime}}{T})\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\,k\leq T:\|\tilde{x}_{k}-\tilde{x}\|^{2}_{c}>f(k,\delta^{\prime\prime},\delta^{\prime})\right)+\delta^{\prime}.

The proof relies on the fact that the iterates

and
\tilde{x}_{k}
of the original and the auxiliary algorithm coincide on the set

, whose complement has probability at most

, and it is given in Appendix

.

Note that the first term on right-hand side of Lemma

corresponds to the probability that the iterates of the auxiliary algorithm introduced in Step 1 exceed

in Equation (

4
Conclusion and Discussions

In this paper we establish maximal concentration bounds for exponentially stable SA algorithms with general step sizes of order
\mathcal{O}(1/k^{z})
for
z\in(0,1]
, in presence of finite-state Markovian noise as well as bounded Martingale-difference additive noise. For contractive SA with
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
step sizes, we further relax boundedness assumptions and derive the first concentration guarantees in the presence of general heavy-tailed noise models, including sub-Weibull and sub-Pareto distributions. Methodologically, our analysis introduces new proof techniques to address the combined challenges posed by Markovian dependence, general step sizes, and potentially heavy-tailed noise. The main ingredients include bounding a modified log-moment generating function derived via Poisson equation arguments, together with an analysis of coupled auxiliary iterates.

On the applications side, we instantiate our general bounds for contractive as well as linear SA. A noteworthy consequence of our results is that, when combined with the findings of
33
, they reveal a subtle phenomenon: although Polyak averaging achieves optimal asymptotic variance, the resulting estimation error remains heavy-tailed under typical step-size schedules such as
\mathcal{O}(1/k^{z})
for
z\in(0,1)
.

We conclude by outlining several directions for future work. First, it would be interesting to explore whether Polyak averaging can be modified to simultaneously retain optimal asymptotic variance while producing lighter-tailed error. Second, applying our concentration framework to reinforcement learning algorithms such as TD and Q-learning, to get explicit dependence on the problem parameters like state space, mixing time, etc., remains a compelling next step. Finally, as seen in Section
3.1.1
, our bounds in Theorem
3.1
are not tight in certain regimes. Sharpening these estimates will likely require fundamentally new analytical tools and is left for future investigation.

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{(N+1)^{k}}\exp\left(\lambda\log\left[(k+h)^{z}x_{0}^{2}\exp\left(\frac{2}{1+\epsilon}\sum_{i=k_{0}(\epsilon)}^{k-1}\alpha_{i}(a+N-1)\right)\right]^{\beta}\right)

(56)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{(N+1)^{k}}\exp\left(\lambda\log\left[(k+h)^{z}x_{0}^{2}\exp\left(\frac{2(a+N-1)\alpha}{(1-z)(1+\epsilon)}\Big((k+h)^{1-z}-(k_{0}(\epsilon)+h)^{1-z}\Big)\right)\right]^{\beta}\right)

(57)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\lambda\left[\log((k+h)^{z}x_{0}^{2})+\frac{2(a+N-1)\alpha}{(1-z)(1+\epsilon)}\Big((k+h)^{1-z}-(k_{0}(\epsilon)+h)^{1-z}\Big)\right]^{\beta}-k\log(N+1)\right),

where Equation (
54
) follows from the update equation (
50
) with
b=0
, Equation (
55
) follows from the distribution of
S_{k}
, Equation (
56
) follows from the facts that
a+N-1>0
and
1+x\geq e^{x/(1+\epsilon)}
for all
x>0
small enough, and Equation (
57
) follows from

\displaystyle\sum_{i=k_{0}(\epsilon)}^{k-1}\alpha_{i}\geq\int_{0}^{k}\frac{\alpha}{(x+h)^{z}}dx=\frac{\alpha}{1-z}\Big((k+h)^{1-z}-(k_{0}(\epsilon)+h)^{1-z}\Big).

Note that, if
\beta>1/(1-z)
, then

\displaystyle\liminf\limits_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\log\left[(k+h)^{z}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|_{c}^{2}\right]^{\beta}\right)\right]=\infty,\qquad\forall\,\lambda>0.

As a result, for any
\beta>1/(1-z)
, there do not exist constants
K_{1},K_{2}>0
s.t.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big((k+h)^{z}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|_{c}^{2}>\epsilon\Big)\leq K_{1}\exp\left(-K_{2}\log(\epsilon)^{\beta}\right),\qquad\forall\,\epsilon>0,\quad k\geq 0.

Equivalently, for any
\beta^{\prime}<1-z
, there do not exist constants
K_{1}^{\prime},K_{2}^{\prime}>0
s.t.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|_{c}^{2}\leq\frac{K_{1}^{\prime}\exp\Big(K_{2}^{\prime}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{\beta^{\prime}}\Big)}{(k+h)^{z}}\right)\geq 1-\delta,\qquad\forall\,\delta\in(0,1),\quad k\geq 0,

completing the proof.
∎

C.3
Bounding the Truncation Bias

In this section, we upper bound the bias term
g(\cdot)
, defined in Equation (
3.1
). Our bound depends on the tail of the random variable
W
introduced in Assumption
8
. Recall
B(\cdot)
and
g(\cdot)
from equations (
3.1
) and (
3.1
), respectively. Also recall
\mathcal{B}_{T}(\cdot)
from Equation (
3.2.1
). In the following sections, we instantiate an upper bound on
g(\cdot)
for different choices of the random variable
W
.

Lemma C.1
.

Suppose
W
is a Sub-Weibull random variable such that

\mathbb{P}\left[W\geq x\right]\leq pe^{-qx^{\frac{1}{\theta}}},\quad\forall x\geq 0,\text{ and some }\theta>\frac{1}{2},p,q>0.

Then for
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
B(\gamma)=\left(\tfrac{1}{q}\log\left(\tfrac{p}{\gamma}\right)\right)^{\theta}
, and for a constant
c>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[W{\bf 1}_{\{W\geq B(\gamma)\}}\right]\leq\frac{\gamma\theta c}{q}\left(\frac{1}{q}\log\left(\frac{p}{\gamma}\right)\right)^{\theta-1}.

Proof.

Clearly,
B(\gamma)=\left(\tfrac{1}{q}\log\left(\tfrac{p}{\gamma}\right)\right)^{\theta}
. This follows from the definition of
B(\cdot)
and the probability density function of
W
. Next,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W{\bf 1}_{\{W\geq B(\gamma)\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{B(\gamma)}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[W\geq x\right]dx\leq\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[W\geq K_{i}\right](K_{i+1}-K_{i}),

where
K_{i}:=B(\gamma/2^{i})
. Hence,
K_{0}=B(\gamma)
. Further, by choice of
B(\gamma)
, we have
\mathbb{P}\left[W\geq K_{i}\right]\leq\gamma/2^{i}
, and

\left|{K_{i+1}-K_{i}}\right|=\left(\frac{1}{q}\log\left(\frac{p2^{i+1}}{\gamma}\right)\right)^{\theta}-\left(\frac{1}{q}\log\left(\frac{p2^{i}}{\gamma}\right)\right)^{\theta}.

Below, we analyze the two cases
\theta\leq 1
and
\theta>1
separately.

Case 1:
\theta\in(1/2,1]
. Let
h(a):=a^{\theta}
, for
a>0
. For
1/2\leq\theta\leq 1
,
h
is a concave function. Thus, for any two points
a>0
and
b>0
,
h(a)\leq h(b)+h^{\prime}(b)(a-b)
, where
h^{\prime}(b)=\theta b^{\theta-1}
. Using this
\left|{K_{i+1}-K_{i}}\right|
is at most

\theta\left(\frac{1}{q}\log\left(\frac{p2^{i}}{\gamma}\right)\right)^{\theta-1}\left(\frac{1}{q}\log\left(\frac{p2^{i+1}}{\gamma}\right)-\frac{1}{q}\log\left(\frac{p2^{i}}{\gamma}\right)\right)=\frac{\theta}{q}\left(\frac{1}{q}\log\left(\frac{p2^{i}}{\gamma}\right)\right)^{\theta-1}\log(2),

giving

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W{\bf 1}_{\{W\geq B(\gamma)\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\theta}{q}\log(2)\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma}{2^{i}}\left(\frac{1}{q}\log\left(\frac{p2^{i}}{\gamma}\right)\right)^{\theta-1}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}\frac{\gamma\theta\log(2)}{q}\left(\frac{1}{q}\log\left(\frac{p}{\gamma}\right)\right)^{\theta-1}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{i}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma\theta c}{q}\left(\frac{1}{q}\log\left(\frac{p}{\gamma}\right)\right)^{\theta-1},

for a constant
c>0
, where
(a)
follows since
\theta-1\leq 0
.

Case 2:
\theta>1
. In this case,

\left|{K_{i+1}-K_{i}}\right|=\left(\frac{1}{q}\log\left(\frac{p2^{i+1}}{\gamma}\right)\right)^{\theta}-\left(\frac{1}{q}\log\left(\frac{p2^{i}}{\gamma}\right)\right)^{\theta}\leq\frac{\theta\log(2)}{q}\left(\frac{1}{q}\log\left(\frac{p2^{i+1}}{\gamma}\right)\right)^{\theta-1},

where we used the convexity of the map
x\rightarrow x^{\theta}
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W{\bf 1}_{\{W\geq B(\gamma)\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma\theta\log(2)}{2^{i}q}\left(\frac{1}{q}\log\left(\frac{p2^{i+1}}{\gamma}\right)\right)^{\theta-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma\theta\log(2)}{q}\left(\frac{1}{q}\log\left(\frac{p}{\gamma}\right)\right)^{\theta-1}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i+1)^{\theta}}{2^{i}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c\gamma\theta}{q}\left(\frac{1}{q}\log\left(\frac{p}{\gamma}\right)\right)^{\theta-1},

for a constant
c>0
.
∎

Lemma C.2
.

Suppose
W
is a heavy-tailed random variable such that

\mathbb{P}\left[W\geq x\right]\leq\frac{p}{x^{\theta}},\quad\forall x\geq 0,\text{ and some }\theta\geq 2,p>0.

Then for
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
B(\gamma)=\left(p/\gamma\right)^{1/\theta}
, and a constant
c>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[W{\bf 1}_{\{W\geq B(\gamma)\}}\right]\leq c\gamma^{\frac{\theta-1}{\theta}}\frac{p^{\frac{1}{\theta}}}{\theta}.

Proof.

The expression for
B(\gamma)
follows from the definition of
B(\cdot)
and the probability density function of
W
. Next,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W{\bf 1}_{\{W\geq B(\gamma)\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{B(\gamma)}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[W\geq x\right]dx\leq\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[W\geq K_{i}\right](K_{i+1}-K_{i}),

where
K_{i}:=B(\gamma/2^{i})
. Hence,
K_{0}=B(\gamma)
. By choice of
B(\cdot)
, we have
\mathbb{P}\left[W\geq K_{i}\right]\leq\gamma/2^{i}
, and

\left|{K_{i+1}-K_{i}}\right|=\left(\frac{2^{i+1}p}{\gamma}\right)^{\frac{1}{\theta}}-\left(\frac{2^{i}p}{\gamma}\right)^{\frac{1}{\theta}}.

Let
h(a):=a^{1/\theta}
, for
a>0
. Since
\theta\geq 2
,
h
is a concave function. Thus, for any two points
a>0
and
b>0
,
h(a)\leq h(b)+h^{\prime}(b)(a-b)
, where
h^{\prime}(b)=b^{1/\theta-1}/\theta
. Using this,
\left|{K_{i+1}-K_{i}}\right|
is at most

\frac{1}{\theta}\left(\frac{2^{i}p}{\gamma}\right)^{\frac{1}{\theta}-1}\left(\frac{2^{i+1}p}{\gamma}-\frac{2^{i}p}{\gamma}\right)=\frac{1}{\theta}\left(\frac{2^{i}p}{\gamma}\right)^{\frac{1}{\theta}},

giving

\mathbb{E}\left[W{\bf 1}_{\{W\geq B(\gamma)\}}\right]\leq\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma}{2^{i}}\frac{1}{\theta}\left(\frac{2^{i}p}{\gamma}\right)^{\frac{1}{\theta}}=\gamma^{\frac{\theta-1}{\theta}}\frac{p^{\frac{1}{\theta}}}{\theta}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{i\frac{\theta-1}{\theta}}}\leq c\gamma^{\frac{\theta-1}{\theta}}\frac{p^{\frac{1}{\theta}}}{\theta},

for a constant
c>0
.
∎

Appendix D
Certain Technical Lemmas

Lemma D.1
.

For
\alpha>0
,
h\geq 8
,
z\in(0,1]
, and for all
k\geq-1
,
\alpha_{k}=\alpha/(k+h)^{z}
or
\alpha_{k}=\alpha
, we have

\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\alpha_{k+1}}\leq 2.

Proof.

This trivially follows when
\alpha_{k}=\alpha
for all
k\geq 1
. Now consider the other case, in which
\alpha_{k}=\alpha/(k+h)^{z}
, for some
z\in(0,1]
, and consider the following inequalities:

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\alpha_{k+1}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{k+h+1}{k+h}\right)^{z}=\left(1+\frac{1}{k+h}\right)^{z}\leq 2^{z}\leq 2,

proving the desired bound.
∎

Lemma D.2
.

For any
\alpha>0
and
h\geq 8
, for all
k\geq 0
and
\alpha_{k}=\alpha/(k+h)^{z}
for
z\in(0,1]
, or
\alpha_{k}=\alpha
, we have

\alpha_{k-1}-\alpha_{k}\leq\frac{2\alpha^{2}_{k}}{\alpha}.

Proof.

The inequality trivially holds for
\alpha_{k}=\alpha
, for all
k\geq 0
. To see the inequality for the other case, that is, when
\alpha_{k}=\alpha/(k+h)^{z}
, consider the following inequalities:

\displaystyle\alpha_{k-1}-\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k}\left(\frac{\alpha_{k-1}}{\alpha_{k}}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k}\left[\left(\frac{k+h}{k+h-1}\right)^{z}-1\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}_{k}}{\alpha_{k}}\left[\left(\frac{k+h}{k+h-1}\right)^{z}-1\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}_{k}}{\alpha}(k+h)^{z}\left[\left(1+\frac{1}{k+h-1}\right)^{z}-1\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha^{2}_{k}}{\alpha}\frac{k+h}{k+h-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}_{k}}{\alpha}\left(1+\frac{1}{k+h-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\alpha^{2}_{k}}{\alpha}.

∎

Lemma D.3
.

For any
\alpha>0
, and either
\alpha_{k}=\alpha
for all
k\geq 0
, or for
h\geq 8
, and
z\in(0,1]
,
\alpha_{k}=\alpha/(k+h)^{z}
for
k\geq 0
, we have

\alpha_{k}\alpha_{k-1}\leq 2\alpha^{2}_{k}.

Proof.

This follows from the following:
\alpha_{k}\alpha_{k-1}=\alpha^{2}_{k}\alpha_{k-1}/\alpha_{k}\leq 2\alpha^{2}_{k}.

∎
