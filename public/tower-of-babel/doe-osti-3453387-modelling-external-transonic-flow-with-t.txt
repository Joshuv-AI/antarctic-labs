 Modelling External Transonic Flow with the
 OpenFOAM Toolbox
 By: Maxwell Zengel

ABSTRACT

 Most people are familiar with contrails, the visible clouds formed by

aircraft exhaust. Recent studies have examined contrails and contrail-induced

cirrus clouds because of their potential climate impact, which has been estimated

to account for approximately 30% of the total warming effect of aviation (Paoli

and Shariff, 2016). Large uncertainties remain because the persistence, spreading,

and long-term evolution of contrails are not fully understood. These uncertainties

are compounded by the role of wing tip vortices, whose velocity gradients disperse

and interfere with exhaust plumes. Current long timescale models of these

interactions do not exist and to reduce uncertainties on regarding climate impact,

long term simulations are needed. This work develops an OpenFOAM

computational fluid dynamics workflow for simulating the initial external

aerodynamics of a Boeing 737-like aircraft under cruise-altitude, transonic-flow

conditions. Specifically, this research evaluated solver settings like steady vs

transient states using a Reynolds averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) turbulence

model, with U (relative velocity) as 250 m/s downstream, patm (atmospheric

 1

 pressure) as 26.436 kPa as boundary conditions to represent cruise altitude

 conditions. Solver settings, mesh parameters, boundary conditions, timestep

 controls, and steady versus transient formulations were evaluated to improve

 stability and runtime. Preliminary results indicate that a transient solver with

 adaptive time stepping, refined wake-region meshing, and volumetric exhaust

 sources provides the most stable approach for this large-scale transonic simulation.

 The resulting workflow provides a foundation for future simulations that export

 aircraft wake fields into longer-time-scale models of contrail-vortex interaction.

INTRODUCTION

 Contrails are aircraft exhaust plumes that become visible when water vapor and exhaust

particles freeze under cold, high-altitude atmospheric conditions. These clouds can persist and

sometimes develop into contrail-induced cirrus clouds, which may trap outgoing radiation and

increase the climate impact of aviation. Paoli and Shariff (2016) estimated that contrails and

related cirrus clouds may contribute approximately 30% of the total warming effect of air

transport. However, large uncertainties remain because the long-term evolution of contrail-

induced cirrus clouds is not fully understood. One major source of uncertainty is the interaction

between exhaust plumes and wingtip vortices, which affects plume dispersion, wake structure,

and cloud evolution. Accurate initial aerodynamic simulations are therefore needed before

longer-time-scale contrail models can be developed. This paper summarizes progress toward

an OpenFOAM-based workflow for simulating external transonic aircraft flow with engine-

exhaust representation, angle of attack, and vortex-producing geometry. The paper covers mesh

 2

generation, solver selection, boundary-condition development, exhaust-source

implementation, transient and steady-state solver behaviour, and the current best-performing

simulation setup.

PHYSICAL AND COMPUTATIONAL MODEL

 The aircraft geometry was based on a Boeing 737-like model downloaded from

GrabCAD and designed by Paulo Franskowiak, with minor modifications made in SolidWorks.

The geometry was imported into OpenFOAM as an .stl file. A Boeing 737-like configuration

was selected because it represents a common commercial aircraft and provides a relevant

geometry for studying aircraft wakes. Boundary conditions were selected to approximate

cruise-altitude operation. External freestream velocity was set to 250 m/s at 26.436 kPa and

218 K. The aircraft geometry was tilted by 3˚ to represent angle of attack. Engine exhaust

conditions targeted a velocity of 410 m/s and temperature of 900 K. These conditions placed

both the external flow and exhaust flow in the transonic regime because of the high velocities

and temperature-dependent speed of sound.

 Representing engine exhaust introduced significant numerical challenges because the

simulation required rapid changes in velocity and temperature inside the aircraft wake.

Although inlet boundaries are common in computational fluid dynamics, this case required

exhaust injection within the computational domain rather than only at an external boundary.

Three approaches were tested: creating an internal face using blockMesh and baffles, importing

a separate .stl face and assigning patch-based boundary conditions, and defining source regions

through topoSetDict with corresponding source terms in fvModels. Each method was evaluated

as a possible way to represent jet-engine exhaust within the external-flow simulation.

 3

 Wingtip-vortex formation was also essential because these vortices control much of the

early aircraft-wake structure. Without an accurate initial representation of wingtip vortices,

longer-time-scale models cannot reliably predict how exhaust plumes disperse or interact with

the wake. The simulation therefore needed to capture both engine exhaust and vortex

formation. This requirement introduced additional challenges, including the need for sufficient

mesh refinement near the wingtips and flow conditions capable of generating realistic vortex

structures within a RANS model.

 All simulations were performed using OpenFOAM, an open-source computational fluid

dynamics package. OpenFOAM was selected because it provides flexible solver options,

customizable boundary conditions, integrated meshing tools, and parallel-computing support.

These capabilities made it suitable for developing a complex aircraft-wake simulation with

transonic flow, internal exhaust representation, and geometry-driven vortex formation.

 NUMERICAL METHODS

 OpenFOAM simulations were automated using a predefined Allrun script [1], which

executed the required commands in sequence. A typical workflow began with mesh and

geometry preparation using blockMesh, surfaceFeatures, snappyHexMesh, and topoSet. The

preprocessing stage then used decomposePar to divide the computational domain for parallel

execution. After decomposition, each processor advanced its assigned portion of the solution.

 While the above workflow simplifies that act of running commands to run a full

simulation, prior setup is still very necessary for a successful CFD simulation. An ASCII

formatted stl of the operating geometry must first be integrated into the working directory in

the /constant/triSurface directory. From here the blockMesh step begins the mesh creation.

During blockMeshing a predefined rectangular prism is loaded in as the boundary for your

mesh while also defining faces for the boundary condition placement. Then surfaceFeatures

and snappyHexMesh work together to create a model of the imported geometry while also

adding additional refinement onto other regions. Boundary conditions are then assigned to

faces in the initial steps of the foamRun command, the primary run command for

OpenFOAM v11 simulations customizable via the attached solver.

SIMULATION SETUP AND EVALUATION

 Completed simulations displayed values for velocity (U), temperature (T), pressure

(p), density (rho), turbulent kinematic viscosity (nut), turbulent kinetic energy (k), turbulent

thermal diffusivity (alphat), and specific rate of turbulence dissipation (omega). These

quantities were evaluated against expected flow behaviour and solver residuals to asses

simulation quality.

 Simulation settings were repeatedly tested to identify the most stable and physically

reasonable configuration. Because of the large aircraft geometry, transonic-flow conditions,

wingtip-vortex formation, and engine-exhaust representation, the best-performing setup

required a specific combination of solver settings, boundary conditions, mesh refinement, and

timestep controls.

OPENFOAM SOLVERS

 Initial tests used the OpenFOAM incompressibleFluid solver to establish the basic

workflow on a simpler case. The model was then transitioned to a compressible solver

because the expected Mach number was approximately Ma .85, well above the typical

incompressible-flow limit of Ma .3. Because OpenFOAM v11 does not include a dedicated

transonic solver for this workflow, the compressible fluid solver was selected. The high Mach

number increased numerical difficulty and produced pressure, velocity, and temperature

spikes that often caused simulation failure. Early tests used a steady-state formulation to

reduce computational cost, but the most important improvement occurred when the solver

was changed to a transient formulation by replacing steadyState with Euler in the ddtSchemes

dictionary in system/fvSchemes.

 6

 The switch to the Euler transient formulation was essential for continued model

development. In the transient formulation, high-speed flow entered and developed through

the domain over time rather than being imposed across the entire flow field during the first

steady-state iteration. This gradual development improved stability for the transonic case.

The change also required updates to fvSchemes, including gradient-scheme modifications,

and additions to fvSolution, including final-solver settings for each primary variable. The

most important transient-solver additions were adaptive time stepping [2] in controlDict and a

PIMPLE algorithm dictionary [3] in fvSolution.

Figure 2 (top): Example controlDict syntax used to implement adaptive timestepping.

Figure 3 (bottom): Example fvSolution syntax for the PIMPLE algorithm settings.

 Adaptive timestepping was implemented in system/controlDict by specifying a

maximum timestep, deltaT and maximum Courant number. The Courant number describes

how far information travels through the mesh during one timestep. If flow passes through too

many cells in a single timestep, numerical instabilities can develop. Limiting the maximum

Courant number therefore forced deltaT to decrease in small or high-speed cells, improving

stability. For these simulations, a maximum Courant number of 1 provided the best balance

between numerical stability and computational cost.

 7

 The PIMPLE dictionary was also required for the transient OpenFOAM v11 solver.

Its settings important for a precise and vital for a high-quality solution. Too few correctors

could allow poor pressure, velocity, or temperature predictions to destabilize the solution.

Too many correctors increased the cost of each timestep and could add many hours of

runtime over thousands of timesteps. The final PIMPLE settings therefore represented a

compromise between solver robustness and computational efficiency.

BOUNDARY CONDITIONS

 Boundary conditions strongly influenced both stability and physical realism. Multiple

boundary-condition combinations were tested on the inlet, outlet, aircraft surface, far-field,

and exhaust regions. This section summarizes the most important boundary-condition

choices, with particular focus on the methods used to represent engine exhaust.

 More than 20 boundary-condition combinations were tested. The most stable inlet

treatment used a time-dependent table ramp applied to a fixedValue condition. This ramp

gradually increased the inlet velocity during the transient simulation, allowing the flow to

develop before reaching the target freestream speed. Gradual acceleration reduced early-time

numerical spikes caused by high-speed flow interacting abruptly with the aircraft geometry.

The inletOutlet condition was also useful on outer-domain boundary faces because it allowed

flow to leave or re-enter the domain as needed, reducing artificial constraint at the far-field

boundaries.

Figure 4 : Boundary-condition excerpt from the 0/U file showing a table-based velocity ramp.

 Engine-exhaust representation was one of the largest modelling challenges. The two

most extensively tested methods were an internal patch-based inlet [5] and a volumetric

source region. Both approaches produced useful preliminary results, but the .stl patch method

was not stable in the compressible transonic solver and was successful only in preliminary

incompressible cases. The volumetric source method was more robust in the compressible

solver and became the preferred approach for the final simulations.

 9

Figure 5 : Internal inlet-face .stl files, shown in blue, positioned on the aircraft geometry,

shown in gray, and visualized in ParaView.

 For the internal patch method, exhaust faces were meshed into the geometry and

defined as patch surfaces in snappyHexMeshDict [6]. Boundary conditions could then be

assigned through the 0/ directory in the same manner as a conventional inlet. This method

was simple and relatively fast to configure, and it performed adequately in incompressible

test cases. However, it was not suitable for the final transonic simulations. In compressible

cases, the solver struggled to maintain stable flow through the internal patch, and the exhaust

jet cooled and decelerated too rapidly without additional local refinement. Because of these

limitations, the patch-based method was replaced by the volumetric source approach.

 10

Figure 6: Example snappyHexMeshDict syntax used to define engine-exhaust surfaces as

patches within the refinementSurfaces dictionary.

 The volumetric source method performed better for the final flow conditions. In

OpenFOAM v11, this method was defined in the /constant/fvModels [7] file, although the file

location can vary between OpenFOAM versions. The method referenced a cell zone

generated by topoSet and applied a source term within that region this. For this work, the

massSource model introduced prescribed exhaust flow into the surrounding external flow at a

specified location and time. This approach was easier for the compressible solver to handle

than the internal .stl patch method and produced stronger exhaust plumes that extended

farther into the wake. It also allowed the engine geometry to remain open [8] so that exhaust

was introduced inside the physical engine volume rather than only at the rear face. The main

limitation was that source conditions mixed immediately with surrounding flow, so precise

exhaust properties required iterative tuning.

MESH/GEOMETRY CREATION

 All meshes were generated using OpenFOAM utilities and the imported .stl geometry.

Mesh design became especially important after adaptive timestepping was introduced

because smaller cells force smaller timesteps at high flow speeds. As a result, very fine

meshes could require several days of runtime to simulate only 0.5 s0.5\ \mathrm{s}0.5 s of

physical time. A balance was therefore needed between computational cost and geometric

resolution. Coarser meshes were used for rapid testing, while finer meshes were reserved for

final simulations. The final mesh workflow used four OpenFOAM utilities: blockMesh,

surfaceFeatures, snappyHexMesh, and topoSet.

 The blockMesh utility created the computational domain in which the external

aerodynamics were solved. The domain was defined using a list of vertices, and each

boundary face was assigned a patch name for later boundary-condition specification. The

command also established the coarsest background mesh across the domain. The

surfaceFeatures utility then identified feature edges on the aircraft geometry and saved them

for use by snappyHexMesh, improving how accurately the mesh conformed to the aircraft

surface.

 The primary mesh generation occurred in snappyHexMesh. Several parameters were

adjusted during testing, including maxGlobalCells, which limits the total cell count;

maxLocalCells, which limits the number of cells per processor; and local refinement levels,

which control resolution around selected surfaces and regions. For example, additional

refinement zones were placed near the wingtips to improve wingtip-vortex resolution [9]. The

final mesh used aircraft-surface refinement level 4 and wingtip refinement level 5. The

largest final mesh contained 8 million cells and provided the best geometric representation of

 13

the aircraft while maintaining added resolution in the regions most important for wake

formation.

Figure 9: Mesh refinement near the wingtip generated using local refinement zones.

 The final meshing step used topoSet to define the cell zones required for the

volumetric exhaust sources. In this case, topoSet created cylindrical groups of cells aligned

with the engine geometry. These cell zones were later referenced by fvModels to apply

exhaust-source terms. After this step, the mesh and geometry were ready for simulation.

CONTINUATIONS

 Although the current workflow has improved significantly, additional work is needed

before it can support full contrail-evolution modelling. Future development will focus on

strengthening the initial wingtip vortices, adding exhaust-composition effects to the

volumetric source, and improving the coupling between heat, momentum, and mass injection.

The resulting flow fields will then be exported to a longer-time-scale model capable of

simulating interactions between wingtip vortices and contrails. These future steps are

necessary before the model can contribute directly to predictions of the environmental effects

of contrails.
