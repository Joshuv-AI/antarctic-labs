Assessing Covariate-Adjusted Risk Differences in Small-Sample Clinical Trials
Martin Schnuerch, Alex Ocampo, Klaus Kähler Holst, Christian Stock

Abstract
Binary endpoints are common in clinical trials and conditional odds ratios have traditionally been used to assess treatment effects. However, the interpretation of odds ratios is difficult, they are non-collapsible and rely on strong assumptions in order to be a relevant overall summary measure for the trial. As an alternative, risk differences have gained increasing prominence as a more interpretable, clinically meaningful and assumption-lean measure of treatment effects. This shift has also been motivated by new regulatory guidance, which emphasizes the relevance of marginal estimands and encourages covariate adjustment. Yet, covariate-adjusted inference for risk differences, particularly in smaller samples, has methodological subtlties and lacks well-established best practices. We conduct a simulation study comparing methods for estimating and testing risk differences in small-sample (N ≤ \,\leq\, 150) randomized clinical trials with prognostic categorical baseline covariates, focusing on exact unconditional tests, Mantel–Haenszel methods, and g g -computation (standardization) approaches. We find that several g g -computation approaches exhibit inflated Type-I error in very small samples when standard Wald-type inference is applied, whereas robust or penalized variants improve error control at the expense of power. Classical methods such as the Mantel–Haenszel and Suissa–Shuster tests remain robust but may forgo efficiency gains from covariate adjustment. Overall, our results indicate that much of the observed Type I error inflation reflects misalignment between estimand and variance estimation rather than small sample size alone. Based on these results, we provide practical recommendations to guide method selection that align the estimand, variance estimation, and inferential target.

1
Introduction

Binary endpoints are widely used in randomized clinical trials, and a common objective is to compare event probabilities between treatment arms. Examples include overall response in oncology and clinical remission in ulcerative colitis trials.

For such comparisons, odds ratios, particularly
conditional
odds ratios, have long been a preferred summary measure. Their popularity stems in part from the fact that they can be obtained straightforwardly from multivariable logistic regression models, in which regression coefficients represent conditional log odds ratios. Despite their frequent use, odds ratios have well-recognized limitations and have been subject to criticism with respect to interpretability, clinical meaningfulness, and underlying assumptions.
[
42
,
20
,
32
]
Odds ratios are difficult to interpret, because they do not directly quantify differences or ratios of event probabilities and therefore obscure information about baseline risk. They are often seen as an approximation of the relative risks of an event, which is only valid when the outcome is relatively uncommon, and they always exaggerate the strength of the relative risk. Moreover, odds ratios are non-collapsible, i.e. even when conditional odds ratios are constant across covariate strata, the corresponding marginal odds ratio generally differs and is trending closer to the null in the presence of any prognostic factor.
[
7
]

Recent guidance from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) on adjustment for covariates in randomized clinical trials highlighted the importance of distinguishing between conditional and marginal treatment estimands.
[
48
,
49
,
51
]
In this context, the terms
marginal
,
unconditional
, and
population-average
refer to the same population-level treatment effect, defined by averaging over the distribution of baseline covariates. While marginal and conditional estimands coincide in linear models without treatment-by-covariate interactions, this equivalence generally breaks down in nonlinear models, such as logistic regression for binary endpoints due to non-collapsibility. Although conditional effects are, in principle, most relevant from an individual patient perspective in a clinical setting, they depend on a correctly specified model and can therefore generally not be estimated reliably in a clinical trial. From a regulatory and public-health perspective, marginal estimands align most directly with the objective of quantifying average treatment benefit in the target population. It is also the estimand targeted by an unadjusted analysis. The FDA guidance favors approaches that provide valid inference under approximately the same minimal statistical assumptions as unadjusted analyses, which themselves are considered acceptable. Conditional estimands, in the FDA guidance on covariate adjustment, are framed primarily as supportive, exploratory, or subgroup-specific targets rather than default choices for primary inference. The focus on population-level summaries is consistent with the ICH E9(R1) estimand framework, which requires explicit prespecification of the population-level summary measure aligned with the clinical question of interest before choosing the estimator and estimation method.
[
17
]

As a consequence of the limitations of (conditional) odds ratios and stimulated by the recent FDA guidance, there has been increasing interest in assessing risk differences. Unlike odds ratios, they directly quantify absolute differences in event proportions and convey both baseline risk and treatment impact on an absolute scale. Further, they are collapsible and avoid interpretational ambiguities. Despite conceptual simplicity, estimation and inference for risk differences are not necessarily straightforward in the presence of covariates. Statistical methods to assess covariate-adjusted risk differences in clinical trials include Cochran-Mantel–Haenszel approaches (limited to categorical covariates),
[
29
,
28
,
1
]
and various implementations of
g
-computation (standardization),
[
39
,
26
]
and inverse probability of treatment weighting.
[
13
,
52
]
These methods may differ with respect to targeted estimands, reliance on modeling assumptions and computational complexity, and finite-sample behavior. As an alternative, one may forgo potential efficiency gains from covariate adjustment and apply (unconditional) statistical tests.
[
22
,
45
]

The challenges in assessing covariate-adjusted risk differences warrant closer scrutiny in small-sample settings. Trials with total sample sizes of N
\,\leq\,
150 are common in phase II development, and, more broadly, in rare diseases and pediatric indications, where feasibility constraints limit enrollment. First, many applied inferential procedures are asymptotically valid but provide no guarantees in finite samples.
[
4
]
In small trials, adjusted risk difference estimators may exhibit bias, non-normal sampling distributions, variance underestimation, miscalibrated
p
-values, and confidence interval undercoverage. For example, parametric variance estimators for
g
-computation based on the delta method, as well as variance estimators for the Mantel–Haenszel risk difference, are derived under maximum-likelihood theory assuming asymptotic normality, which in turn relies on large-sample properties. Similarly, the Cochran-Mantel–Haenszel test is based on a statistic that is asymptotically
\chi^{2}
-distributed and therefore requires a minimum amount of information per stratum to perform as intended. When sample sizes are small, events are sparse, or stratification leads to very small strata, these conditions may not be met. In such settings, degrees-of-freedom penalties and numerical instability could offset potential gains from stratification. Second,
g
-computation approaches for binary endpoints depend on a fitted multivariable logistic regression working model, which may suffer from instability, separation, or non-convergence in small samples. Under these circumstances, the comparative advantage of covariate-adjusted approaches over simpler unadjusted methods remains uncertain.

To address these concerns, we present a simulation-based comparative evaluation of a range of methods for estimating and testing risk differences in randomized clinical trials, particularly exact unconditional testing, Mantel–Haenszel methods, and
g
-computation (standardization) approaches. Our focus is on small-sample settings, where misalignment between the target estimand, point estimator, and variance estimator is most likely to be revealed, and that have been studied only to a limited extent. Our intent is to inform statistical practice by identifying approaches that target the intended estimand, exhibit robust inferential performance in small samples, and strike a balance between efficiency, robustness, and interpretability.

The remainder of this article is structured as follows: In Section
2
, we introduce a hypothetical trial that serves as a running example and basis for the subsequent simulation study and we discuss potential estimands of interest.
In Section
3
, we provide an overview of the statistical methods we evaluate in our study, focussing on underlying assumptions and targeted estimands.
In Section
4
, we describe our simulation study, comparing the included statistical methods on various dimensions, including Type I error rates and power, estimation bias, and confidence interval coverage, across multiple scenarios spanning different sample sizes and effect sizes.
In Section
5
, we report the results of the simulation study.
Finally, in Section
6
, we provide a discussion of the results, offer concrete practical recommendations for statisticians, and outline potential avenues for future research.

2
Hypothetical trial example and potential estimands

Consider a hypothetical two-arm randomized controlled trial with parallel groups, comparing an investigational treatment (
A=1
) with a control (
A=0
). Let
Y\in\{0,1\}
denote a binary primary endpoint indicating treatment response, where
Y=1
corresponds to response. The parameter of primary interest is the average treatment effect on the risk scale, defined as the risk difference

\mathrm{RD}=\Pr(Y=1\mid A=1)-\Pr(Y=1\mid A=0).

The trial objective is to assess superiority of the investigational treatment with respect to this estimand. This entails a two-sided hypothesis test

H_{0}:\ \mathrm{RD}=0\qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_{1}:\ \mathrm{RD}\neq 0,

together with estimation of
\mathrm{RD}
. Assume further that two binary baseline covariates
X=(X_{1},X_{2})
are measured and considered prognostically relevant, for example, sex (
X_{1}\in\{\text{male},\allowbreak\text{female}\}
) and disease severity (
X_{2}\in\{\text{mild},\text{severe}\}
). These covariates may be used as stratification factors in the randomization. Covariate adjustment for
X
is generally advisable to improve statistical efficiency while targeting the same marginal estimand
\mathrm{RD}
.

A critical step in selecting an appropriate statistical method for analysis is the clear definition of the estimand. An estimand provides a precise and comprehensive specification of what the trial aims to estimate, encompassing the treatment under investigation, the target population, the endpoint, the handling of intercurrent events, and the summary measure used to quantify the treatment effect
[
17
,
19
,
38
]
. Each of these components is important; however, in this work we focus on the choice of the summary measure and therefore use the term
estimand
in this broader sense.

To formalize different estimands of interest, we introduce potential outcomes notation. Let
Y^{(A)}
denote the
potential outcome
for an individual under treatment assignment
A
, where
A=1
represents the investigational treatment and
A=0
represents control. Within the potential outcomes framework,
[
16
]
the treatment effect is defined as a contrast between the outcomes that would be observed for the same individual under each treatment condition. In a two-arm randomized controlled trial, only one of the two potential outcomes is observed for each individual, while the counterfactual outcome remains unobserved.

Utilizing the potential outcomes framework, multiple estimands of interest can be defined, with a central consideration being how the treatment effect is characterized in relation to baseline covariates. A key distinction is between
marginal
and
conditional
estimands. Marginal estimands are defined by averaging over the distribution of baseline covariates in the target population, whereas conditional estimands describe treatment effects at fixed covariate values. Here, we focus on three estimands that are particularly relevant in the context of clinical trials, following Magirr et al.
[
27
]
. We present these estimands in terms of risk differences. However, the same estimands can equivalently be defined with respect to other contrasts of interest, such as risk ratios or odds ratios.

•

The
marginal treatment effect
(MTE), also referred to as the average treatment effect or population-average treatment effect, is defined as the contrast between marginal expected outcomes under each treatment:

\displaystyle\text{MTE}=\Pr\!\left(Y^{(1)}=1\right)-\Pr\!\left(Y^{(0)}=1\right).

(1)

This estimand addresses the question:
What is the average treatment effect if all individuals in the population were treated versus if all were untreated?

•

The
conditional treatment effect
(CTE) at covariate value
x
, denoted
\text{CTE}(x)
, is defined as the contrast between expected outcomes conditional on
X=x
:

\displaystyle\text{CTE}(x)=\Pr\!\left(Y^{(1)}=1\mid X=x\right)-\Pr\!\left(Y^{(0)}=1\mid X=x\right).

(2)

This estimand addresses the question:
What is the average treatment effect for individuals with covariate value
x
?

•

The
conditional population-average treatment effect
(CPATE) is defined as the average of the CTE over the empirical covariate distribution observed in the trial. Let
\underline{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
denote the observed covariate values in the trial. The CPATE is defined as the average CTE over
\underline{x}
:

\displaystyle\text{CPATE}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\text{CTE}(x_{i})

(3)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{\Pr\!\left(Y^{(1)}=1\mid X=x_{i}\right)-\Pr\!\left(Y^{(0)}=1\mid X=x_{i}\right)\right\}.

The CPATE is marginal with respect to treatment assignment but conditional on the observed covariate distribution and can be interpreted as addressing the question:
What is the average of covariate-specific treatment effects across the population as observed in the trial?
, thus bridging marginal and conditional perspectives.
[
27
]

The estimands discussed above do not constitute an exhaustive taxonomy but represent those most relevant for the present work. While a decision for or against a CTE may often appear straightforward guided by the clinical objective, the choice between MTE and CPATE is more subtle. Magirr et al.
[
27
]
argue that there is no universally correct choice between these estimands; rather, the estimand of interest should be transparently prespecified, and the statistical method should be selected to target that estimand. They further argue that the CPATE, although defined through covariate-specific effects, may reasonably be interpreted as a marginal estimand because it averages these effects over the covariate distribution observed in the trial. We concur with this perspective.
Indeed, from an estimation perspective the
g
-computation point estimator is algebraically identical whether interpreted as targeting the CPATE or MTE. The two estimands therefore differ not in the target of point estimation but in the inferential framework: the CPATE conditions on the observed covariate distribution, whereas the MTE accounts for its variability. Consequently, the choice between them is reflected in the variance estimator, confidence intervals, and hypothesis tests, which must be aligned with the chosen estimand.
Rather than advocating for a single default estimand, we emphasize the importance of acknowledging the diversity of clinically meaningful estimands, clearly communicating the chosen definition, and ensuring alignment between the estimand, the estimator, and the inferential procedure.

3.1
Suissa-Shuster test

The first approach we consider is the exact unconditional test for comparing two independent binomial proportions proposed by Suissa and Shuster.
[
45
]
Although it does not accommodate covariate adjustment, it serves as an important benchmark for evaluating the finite-sample properties of covariate-adjusted procedures, particularly with respect to Type I error control and power.

The Suissa–Shuster test provides a principled alternative to two common approaches in this setting:
asymptotic
tests (e.g.,
z
- or
\chi^{2}
tests) and
conditional
tests (e.g., Fisher’s exact test
[
10
]
). Unlike asymptotic tests, it does not rely on large-sample approximations. Instead, inference is based on the exact finite-sample distribution of the test statistic, deemed to maintain the nominal Type I error rate even in small samples.
[
45
]
Unlike Fisher’s exact test, it does not condition on fixed marginal totals. We include the Suissa-Shuster test because it is an exact unconditional procedure constructed directly on the risk-difference scale and therefore aligns the hypothesis test with the estimand of interest. By contrast, other exact unconditional tests, such as the Boschloo test, are based on an ordering induced by Fisher
p
-values and are only indirectly connected to inference on the risk difference.
[
22
]

To briefly outline the mathematical underpinnings and assumptions of the exact unconditional test, recall that the statistical hypothesis of interest is on the risk difference, i.e., the difference between two independent binomial probabilities. Let
\pi_{1}=\Pr(Y^{(1)}=1)
and
\pi_{0}=\Pr(Y^{(0)}=1)
denote the marginal response probabilities under treatment and control, respectively, and let
\delta=\pi_{1}-\pi_{0}
be the marginal risk difference. The marginal response probabilities can be reparameterized in terms of
\delta
and the overall response probability
\theta
:
\pi_{1}=\theta+\delta/2
and
\pi_{0}=\theta-\delta/2
. Under the null hypothesis,
\delta=0\iff\pi_{1}=\pi_{0}=\theta
, so the distribution of observed responses depends only on the nuisance parameter
\theta
.

Let
K_{1},K_{0}
denote the number of responses and
N_{1},N_{0}
the corresponding sample sizes in the treatment and control arm, respectively. Under the null hypothesis, the joint distribution of
(K_{1},K_{0})
is given by

P_{\theta}(K_{1},K_{0})=\binom{N_{1}}{K_{1}}\binom{N_{0}}{K_{0}}\theta^{K_{1}+K_{0}}\ (1-\theta)^{N_{1}+N_{0}-K_{1}-K_{0}}.

In contrast to a conditional test, which would fix
\theta
(e.g., using observed margins), but the Suissa-Shuster test achieves validity as an
unconditional
test by calculating the
p
value over the set of all possible values of
\theta
:

p=\sup_{\theta\in[0,1]}\sum_{(k_{1}^{{}^{\prime}},k_{0}^{{}^{\prime}}):\hat{\delta}\geq\hat{\delta}_{\text{obs}}}\Pr_{\theta}(k_{1}^{{}^{\prime}},k_{0}^{{}^{\prime}}),

where
\sup
denotes the supremum, that is, the largest tail probability across all possible
\theta
, ensuring an exact test without conditioning on margins.

We note that although exact unconditional tests are computationally more demanding than conditional alternatives, this limitation is largely irrelevant with modern computing resources. The main limitation is the lack of covariate adjustment, which may reduce efficiency and precludes correction for chance imbalances in prognostic covariates compared with adjusted methods.

3.2
Mantel-Haenszel methods

3.2.1
Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test

Another method that we consider falls under the umbrella of Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel (CMH) methods.
[
29
,
1
]
CMH methods are explicitly mentioned in the FDA guidance on covariate adjustment.
[
48
]
They encompass a family of stratified tests assessing the association between row and column variables across multiple 2
\,\times\,
2 tables. However, it is in fact important to distinguish between Cochran’s statistic and Mantel–Haenszel statistics (for a comprehensive overview of the specific assumptions underlying the procedures, see Qiu et al.
[
37
]
).

The test we consider here is commonly referred to as
CMH test
, although it is more accurately described as
Mantel–Haenszel (MH) test
.
[
37
]
Originally proposed as a test of conditional independence across a set of
2\times 2
tables, it is used in clinical trials to evaluate whether the null hypothesis of no treatment effect holds uniformly in all of the strata. This null hypothesis can equivalently be expressed in terms of a common conditional odds ratio, relative risk, or risk difference. Thus, in the present context, the test is formulated in terms of the strict null hypothesis that the conditional risk difference in each stratum is zero, rather than the marginal risk difference across strata.
Accordingly, the CMH/MH test is derived under a conditional framework and tests the null hypothesis of no treatment effect within each stratum. For collapsible effect measures such as the risk difference, this stratum-wise null coincides with the marginal null hypothesis of no average treatment effect, even though the test is derived conditionally, whereas for non-collapsible measures the test corresponds to inference on a conditional estimand. This distinction is reflected in the FDA guidance, which endorses CMH methods when interest lies in estimating a conditional treatment effect assumed to be constant across subgroups.
[
48
]

For each stratum
s
, let
n_{ijs}
denote the cell counts in a 2
\,\times\,
2 table denoting the number of observations for each treatment group
i
(treatment vs. control) and possible outcome
j
(response vs. no response). Treating the marginal totals as fixed, the MH test is based on the following test statistic,

\chi^{2}_{\text{MH}}=\dfrac{\left\{\sum_{s}n_{11s}-E(n_{11s})\right\}^{2}}{\sum_{s}\text{var}(n_{11s})},

which under the null hypothesis (and conditional on the marginal totals) is asymptotically
\chi^{2}
distributed with 1 degree of freedom.

The CMH/MH test is commonly used in clinical trials. It allows for covariate adjustment by analyzing stratified data. However, as it is based on discrete strata, adjustment is limited to categorical covariates. Moreover, the validity of the
\chi^{2}
approximation requires a sufficiently large number of observations, which is typically assessed using the Mantel-Fleiss criterion
[
28
]
. Finally, it is important to keep in mind that the MH test is derived under a conditional, stratified framework. For non-collapsible effect measures, it therefore corresponds to inference on a conditional estimand. For collapsible measures such as the risk difference, however, the stratum-wise null hypothesis coincides with the marginal null, even though the test itself is not a marginal procedure in the estimand-framework sense.

3.2.2
Mantel-Haenszel risk difference

We consider another member of the CMH family, namely the Mantel–Haenszel (MH) risk difference estimator. As the name suggests, in contrast to the MH test, this method is concerned with estimation rather than hypothesis testing and, more importantly, can be shown to target a different estimand. While the MH test evaluates the strict null hypothesis of no treatment effect within any stratum—corresponding to a stratum-wise, conditional null—the MH risk difference estimator is often assumed to target the same estimand by averaging stratum-specific risk differences under a homogeneity assumption (
\delta_{s}=\delta
for all strata). However, as recently demonstrated, this strong homogeneity assumption is not required.
[
37
]
Instead, the MH risk difference estimator can be interpreted as targeting an average treatment effect across strata, even in the presence of effect heterogeneity.
[
33
]
Depending on whether the covariates are considered fixed or random and on the choice of variance estimator, this average may be viewed as either the CPATE or the MTE; thus, the MH risk difference estimator admits multiple estimand interpretations.

For both estimands, the point estimator is a weighted mean of stratum-specific risk differences:

\hat{\delta}=\dfrac{\sum_{s}w_{s}\hat{\delta}_{s}}{\sum_{s}w_{s}},

where
w_{s}
denotes stratum-specific weights reflecting the sample size and allocation to treatment groups within each stratum.
Unlike the point estimator, the choice of the variance estimator depends on the estimand of interest.

A commonly used variance estimator, originally proposed by Sato, assumes a common risk difference across strata and ignores between-stratum heterogeneity.
[
43
]
This variance estimator is therefore appropriate when the CPATE is the estimand of interest. In contrast, Qiu et al. proposed a modified variance estimator that explicitly accounts for heterogeneity in stratum-specific risk differences and is thus appropriate when inference is directed at the MTE.
[
37
]
The situation where a single point estimator may be compatible with multiple estimands, but inference depends crucially on the choice of variance estimator is not uncommon and is discussed in detail by Qiu et al.
[
37
]
It also applies to the
g
-computation approaches discussed below, and it further speaks to the importance of aligning both estimation and inference.

3.3
G
-Computation

The
g
-computation (or
standardization
) approach is a general strategy for estimating causal treatment effects by combining an outcome regression model with an explicit averaging step over the covariate distribution.
[
40
,
47
,
49
]
In a randomized trial, randomization identifies the marginal mean potential outcomes
E\{Y^{(a)}\}
, and standardization estimates these means by predicting outcomes under each treatment level and averaging the predictions over the empirical distribution of baseline covariates in the study population.

In practice, standardization employs a working model for the conditional response mean given treatment assignment
A
and baseline covariates
\boldsymbol{X}
. For binary endpoints, a common choice is a logistic regression working model with a canonical link,

\text{logit}\{P(Y=1\mid A,\boldsymbol{X})\}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{A}A+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{X}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{X},

where
\text{logit}(q)=\log\{q/(1-q)\}
and
(\beta_{0},\beta_{A},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{X})
are estimated by maximum likelihood. Under randomization, and for canonical generalized linear models, the resulting
g
-computation estimator of marginal arm-specific means is consistent even when the working model is misspecified.
[
30
,
53
,
49
,
55
]

Given fitted coefficients
\hat{\beta}_{0},\hat{\beta}_{A},\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{X}
, the counterfactual (model-predicted) response probability for subject
i
under treatment level
a\in\{0,1\}
is

\hat{p}_{i}(a)=\text{expit}\bigl(\hat{\beta}_{0}+\hat{\beta}_{A}a+\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{X}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\bigr),

where
\text{expit}(u)=\{1+\exp(-u)\}^{-1}
. The standardized estimators of the marginal response probabilities are obtained by averaging these predictions over the observed covariate distribution,

\hat{\pi}_{a}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{p}_{i}(a),\qquad a\in\{0,1\},

and the standardized estimate of the marginal risk difference is

\hat{\delta}=\hat{\pi}_{1}-\hat{\pi}_{0}.

This procedure standardizes to the covariate distribution of the trial population (i.e., the empirical distribution of
\boldsymbol{X}
in the study sample), yielding a marginal (population-level) estimand with respect to that distribution.
[
18
,
49
]

While the above procedure describes the construction of the standardized point estimator of the marginal treatment effect
\hat{\delta}
,
g
-computation methods differ primarily in how they estimate
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\delta})
, construct tests and confidence intervals. A common approach is Wald-type inference based on delta-method variance estimators that treat
\hat{\delta}
as a smooth function of estimated regression parameters and account for their estimation uncertainty via sandwich formulas. Early implementations rely on model-based delta-method variances (e.g., Ge et al.
[
12
]
), whereas more recent work derives model-robust sandwich estimators using stacked estimating equations or influence-function representations, yielding valid inference under randomization even when the outcome working model is misspecified.
[
53
,
26
,
3
]
An alternative class of variance estimators is based on efficient or augmented influence functions for the standardized estimand; these estimators can offer improved robustness but may exhibit non-negligible finite-sample sensitivity, particularly when the working model is poorly behaved.
[
55
]
. As a further alternative, nonparametric bootstrap procedures are frequently used to approximate the sampling distribution of
\hat{\delta}
, and are straightforward to implement in practice. However, like the asymptotic variance estimators mentioned above, standard bootstrap schemes are formally justified only under simple randomization, with modifications required for covariate-adaptive or restricted randomization designs
[
44
,
49
]
. Small-sample performance can be sensitive to the inferential choice. Wald tests are known to perform poorly when sample sizes are small or when estimated probabilities are near the boundary, motivating alternatives such as robust score-test constructions for
g
-computation estimators
[
55
]
. Penalized working logistic models, e.g. using Firth’s method, can mitigate separation and small-sample instability; however at the cost of altering the estimation procedure and potentially the targeted estimand
[
15
,
36
]
.

The following subsections provide a concise overview of six select
g
-computation approaches, outlining their theoretical basis, implementation details, and practical considerations for use in clinical trial settings.

3.3.1
Ge et al.

The widely used approach described by Ge at al.
[
12
]
is based on a model-based estimator for the variance of
\hat{\delta}
, treating the observed covariate distribution as fixed.
Let
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\beta_{A},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{X})
denote the parameter vector of the logistic regression working model and
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\hat{\delta}
the gradient of the risk-difference estimator with respect to these model parameters, evaluated at
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
.
Since
\hat{\delta}=\hat{\pi}_{1}-\hat{\pi}_{0}
, the gradient is

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\hat{\delta}=\left(\dfrac{\partial\hat{\pi}_{1}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\dfrac{\partial\hat{\pi}_{0}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right).

Treating the covariates
\mathbf{X}
as fixed, Ge et al.’s variance estimator follows directly from the delta method:

\widehat{\text{Var}}_{\text{Ge}}(\hat{\delta})=\{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\hat{\delta}\}^{T}\ V(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\ \{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\hat{\delta}\},

(4)

where
V(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
is the model-based covariance matrix of the fitted logistic-regression coefficients.
Specifically,
V(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=I(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^{-1}
, that is, the inverse of the observed Fisher information matrix from the fitted model.
This matrix quantifies the sampling variability of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
under the assumption that the working model is correctly specified, and yields a variance estimate for
\hat{\delta}
conditional on the observed covariate distribution.
Consequently, as pointed out by Magirr et al.
[
27
]
, the
g
-computation approach with Ge et al.’s variance estimator targets a conditional average treatment effect (CPATE).
When this conditional estimand aligns with the trial objectives, the variance estimator is appropriate.
However, if the MTE is the estimand of interest, the estimator may underestimate the unconditional variance of
\hat{\delta}
because it ignores the additional variability arising from the empirical distribution of covariates and potential model misspecification
[
26
,
53
]
.

3.3.2
Liu and Xi

Liu and Xi propose an alternative variance estimator for
\hat{\delta}
that directly targets the
unconditional
variance of the
g
-computation estimator.
[
26
]
Their work is motivated in part by above mentioned observation that Ge et al.’s estimator relies on a model-based covariance matrix and conditions on the observed covariate distribution, thereby ignoring both potential model misspecification and sampling variability in baseline covariates.

To address these limitations, Liu and Xi introduce two key modifications. First, instead of the model-based covariance matrix
V(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
, they use a heteroskedasticity-consistent (sandwich) estimator of the covariance matrix,
V_{\mathrm{HC}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
, which does not rely on correct specification of the logistic regression working model. This model-robust covariance matrix replaces the inverse observed Fisher information and adjusts for residual heterogeneity, yielding a more reliable estimate of the sampling variability of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
in the presence of model misspecification.
Second, Liu and Xi add a variance component that captures the additional uncertainty arising from the randomness of the empirical covariate distribution.
A key observation is that the unconditional variance of
\hat{\delta}
satisfies the law of total variance,

\text{Var}(\hat{\delta})=E\!\left[\text{Var}(\hat{\delta}\mid\mathbf{X})\right]+\text{Var}\!\left[E(\hat{\delta}\mid\mathbf{X})\right].

The first term is the conditional variance of
\hat{\delta}
given the observed covariates, which is captured by Equation
4
. The second term reflects additional sampling variation because
\hat{\delta}
depends on the particular realization of baseline covariates in the sample. This term denotes the between-sample variability of the risk-difference estimator arising from variability in the covariate distribution.

Combining these two components yields the following, robust estimator of the unconditional variance:

\widehat{\text{Var}}_{\mathrm{Liu}}(\hat{\delta})=\{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\hat{\delta}\}^{T}\,V_{\mathrm{HC}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\,\{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\hat{\delta}\}\;+\;\hat{V}_{\mathbf{X}}(\hat{\delta}),

where
\hat{V}_{\mathbf{X}}(\hat{\delta})
estimates the component
\text{Var}\!\left[E(\hat{\delta}\mid\mathbf{X})\right]
.
Unlike Ge et al.’s estimator, which targets a
conditional
treatment effect such as the CPATE
[
27
]
, the Liu-Xi estimator targets an
unconditional
treatment effect (the MTE), making it appropriate when marginal inference is desired.
[
26
]

The additional covariate-variability term is of order
O(n^{-1})
and asymptotically negligible, but can be non-negligible in finite samples, particularly when covariates are strongly prognostic or sample sizes are small. Ignoring this component leads to systematic underestimation of the unconditional variance and, consequently, anticonservative inference. Consistent with this reasoning, Liu and Xi’s simulation studies show improved Type I error control relative to conditional variance estimators. They recommend the HC3 version of the sandwich estimator for small-sample settings, which we also apply in our simulations.
[
26
]

3.3.3
Ye et al.

Another robust variance estimator for the
g
-computation risk-difference estimator was proposed by Ye et al.
[
53
]
Their approach is a special case of the general, semi-parametric covariate-adjustment strategy introduced by Bannick et al.
[
3
]
It applies to two-arm randomized controlled trials with a binary endpoint and a logistic-regression working model under simple randomization, matching the setting considered here.
For this case, Ye et al. show that asymptotic variances and covariances of the
g
-computation estimators
\hat{\pi}_{1}
and
\hat{\pi}_{0}
of the marginal response probabilities are

\text{Var}(\hat{\pi}_{j})=\dfrac{1}{\rho_{a}}\text{Var}\{Y^{(a)}-\pi_{a}\}+2\ \text{Cov}(Y^{(a)},\pi_{a})-\text{Var}(\pi_{a}),\quad a=0,1,

where
\rho_{a}=P(A=a)
, and

\text{Cov}(\hat{\pi}_{1},\hat{\pi}_{0})=\text{Cov}\{Y^{(1)},\pi_{0}\}+\text{Cov}(Y^{(0)},\pi_{1})-\text{Cov}(\pi_{1},\pi_{0}),

and the corresponding variance estimator for the risk difference
\hat{\delta}=\hat{\pi}_{1}-\hat{\pi}_{0}
is

\widehat{\text{Var}}_{\mathrm{Ye}}(\hat{\delta})=\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\pi}_{1})-2\ \widehat{\text{Cov}}(\hat{\pi}_{1},\hat{\pi}_{0})+\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\pi}_{0}).

Like Liu and Xi’s estimator, this variance estimator targets the unconditional variance, and is therefore appropriate when the MTE is the estimand of interest. The variance decomposes into outcome noise, covariate-driven heterogeneity, and their interaction.

3.3.4
Steingrimsson et al.

An alternative approach to obtaining a robust variance estimate for the
g
-computation estimator of the risk difference is the non-parametric bootstrap, as proposed by Steingrimsson et al.
[
44
]
.
This method does not rely on a closed-form variance expression. Instead, it approximates the sampling distribution of the
g
-computation estimator empirically through repeated resampling of the observed trial data.

The non-parametric bootstrap is defined by the following computational steps: For each bootstrap replication
b=1,\ldots,B
,

1.

draw a bootstrap sample of size
n
by sampling patients with replacement from the original dataset,

2.

refit the working logistic model on the bootstrap sample to obtain new predictions
\hat{\pi}_{a}^{(b)}
, and

3.

compute the bootstrap estimator of the risk difference
\hat{\delta}^{(b)}=\hat{\pi}_{1}^{(b)}-\hat{\pi}_{0}^{(b)}
.

After
B
bootstrap repetitions, the bootstrap variance estimator is

\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathrm{boot}}(\hat{\delta})=\frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left(\hat{\delta}^{(b)}-\bar{\delta}_{\mathrm{boot}}\right)^{2},

where
\bar{\delta}_{\mathrm{boot}}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\hat{\delta}^{(b)}
.

This method yields a model-agnostic variance estimator that automatically accounts for the variability introduced by estimation of the working model, the distribution of covariates, and the randomization scheme.
Consistent with Steingrimsson et al.
[
44
]
, the bootstrap is particularly attractive when robustness is desired against model misspecification, when analytic variance expressions are difficult to derive, or when the asymptotic properties of the analytic estimator may not hold.

By incorporating variability from the covariate distribution, the resulting variance estimator targets the unconditional variance of the treatment effect and is thus appropriate when the MTE is the estimand of interest.
Computationally, the approach is more burdensome than analytic methods, since it requires repeated refitting of the working model. Nevertheless, for a single dataset, it is computationally well feasible on modern hardware, usually completing within a few seconds depending on the number of replicates.

3.3.5
Zhang et al.

A relatively new statistical approach to assessing covariate-adjusted risk differences in the context of
g
-computation was presented by Zhang et al.
[
55
]
Motivated by the well-known limitations of Wald-test based inference – namely, Type I error rate inflation and insufficient confidence interval coverage for small samples or parameter values near the boundaries of the parameter space – they develop a robust score test for
g
-computation estimators of unconditional treatment effects. The proposed test is grounded in the generalized score test for
M
-estimators
[
5
]
and it is valid under simple and stratified randomization schemes, even when the working logistic model is misspecified.
[
55
]

To assess whether a treatment effect exists, Zhang et al. construct a generalized score statistic for testing the null hypothesis
H_{0}\!:\ \delta=\delta_{0}
,

\chi^{2}_{\text{Zhang}}=\dfrac{(\hat{\delta}-\delta_{0})^{2}}{\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\delta})+\dfrac{(\hat{\delta}-\delta_{0})^{2}}{n}},

where
\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\delta})
denotes any valid variance estimator for
\hat{\delta}
(as justified in Zhang et al.
[
55
]
).
This statistic differs from the classical Wald statistic only through the additional term in the denominator.
Because this term increases the denominator when
\hat{\delta}
deviates from
\delta_{0}
, it acts as a finite-sample penalty that mitigates the inflation of the Wald statistic arising from model misspecification or adjustment for too many covariates.
Under the null hypothesis, the test statistic follows a
\chi^{2}
distribution with 1 degree of freedom.
For estimation, inverting the statistic yields a confidence interval with a simple closed form.

3.3.6
Firth

A final approach included in this work addresses a practical issue that may arise when sample sizes are small, namely convergence difficulties of the logistic regression working model caused by separation. In logistic regression models, separation occurs when a covariate or linear combination of covariates perfectly predicts the outcome. Under complete or quasi-separation, the maximum likelihood estimator does not exist in the usual sense and numerical optimization fails to converge. Such situations may arise in small-sample situations, especially when event rates are very low or when adjusting for multiple covariates.

To mitigate these convergence problems, we consider a penalized-likelihood method that combines the bias-reduction procedure of Firth
[
9
]
with the FLIC modification proposed by Puhr et al.
[
36
]
The resulting adjusted working model is a Firth-penalized logistic regression in which the Jeffreys prior penalty is incorporated through the FLIC adjustment to ensure stable estimation even when standard maximum likelihood fails. The regression coefficients are obtained by maximizing this modified penalized likelihood, which corrects the infinite-estimate problem introduced by separation while preserving the interpretability of the logistic working model.

Because the
g
-computation estimator depends only on the fitted linear predictor under each treatment arm, the Firth adjusted logistic model can be substituted seamlessly for the standard working model. The fitted values obtained from this penalized regression model then feed into the usual
g
-computation procedure to obtain an estimator of the marginal risk difference. For simplicity, we pair the Firth working model with the model-based conditional variance estimator by Ge et al., which yields inference on the CPATE, although any valid variance estimator could in principle be used.
[
12
]

4.2
Operating characteristics

To compare the statistical methods across the simulation settings described above, we evaluated a set of operating characteristics. Because our primary interest lies in statistical inference in the form of hypothesis testing – assessing whether or not a treatment effect exists – the central metric is the rejection rate.
Under the null hypothesis (
\delta=0
), this corresponds to the Type I error rate, whereas under alternative scenarios with
\delta\neq 0
it denotes statistical power.

In addition we examined the quality of point and interval estimation for all methods, excluding the MH test that does not afford an estimate of the risk difference. Estimation bias was quantified as the absolute deviation between the average estimated and the true risk difference, and precision was assessed using the root mean squared error (RMSE). We further evaluated the empirical coverage probabilities of nominal 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Together, these metrics characterize the accuracy and reliability of the estimators beyond their hypothesis-testing properties.

Further, we investigated potential computational challenges arising in the application of the
g
-computation approaches. Logistic regression models fitted within
g
-computation are susceptible to non-convergence and data-separation issues, particularly in small samples, when covariates are highly prognostic, or when too many covariates relative to the sample size are included. Computational issues like non-convergence impair feasibility, may lead to missingness, and potentially distort conclusions about the inferential properties of the methods investigated in the simulation. Yet, such issues are rarely reported or discussed in simulation studies
[
34
]
.
Therefore, we quantified the frequency of computational issues across all simulation scenarios and examined their downstream impact on the methods’ inferential characteristics. Thereby, we seek to ensure that our performance assessment reflects not only the theoretical behavior of the approaches under ideal conditions but also the practical limitations encountered when applying them in small-sample trial settings.

4.3
Reproducibility

All simulation results reported in this manuscript are reproducible. Simulations were conducted in the
R
statistical computing environment (version 4.5.0) using a high-performance computing infrastructure managed by the Slurm Workload Manager. The
R
scripts used to generate the simulated datasets, perform the analyses, and produce the results, together with documentation and replication instructions, are made available via a GitHub repository at
https://github.com/Boehringer-Ingelheim/binary-endpoints
. Further details on the employed specialized packages and functions used to implement the investigated methods are given in Appendix
A
.

5
Results

5.1
Computational issues

Before evaluating operating characteristics and estimation performance, we first examined potential computational issues associated with the statistical procedures under consideration, with particular focus on the logistic regression working models used in the
g
-computation approaches. A well-known phenomenon in small-sample logistic regression is complete or quasi-complete separation, which arises when a covariate or a combination of covariates perfectly predicts the binary outcome. In such situations, parameter estimates may diverge toward infinity, often accompanied by extremely large variance estimates, even when the optimization algorithm formally converges. Although separation does not necessarily imply non-convergence, it results in unstable parameter estimates and may compromise the reliability of downstream estimators that depend on the fitted working model.

Interestingly, non-convergence was rare in our simulation despite small sample sizes considered. Across all scenarios, only
0.0036\%
of the simulation runs resulted in a logistic regression model that failed to converge, all for a total sample size of
N=30
. In principle, these cases could be addressed using penalized likelihood methods such as Firth’s correction or by replacing the
g
-computation estimator with a simpler test such as Suissa-Shuster’s unconditional test as fallback strategies. Given the very low frequency of non-convergence, however, these remedies seem of limited practical relevance. We therefore decided to exclude all simulation runs that ended in non-convergence. Because non-convergence was extremely rare and restricted to
N=30
, exclusion is likely to have negligible impact on operating characteristics.

In contrast, separation, occurred much more frequently, as shown in Figure
1
. For small samples (
N=30,60
) in the presence of strongly prognostic covariates, separation was observed in up to more than 30% of simulations. As sample size increased, or when the data-generating treatment effect was larger, the incidence of separation declined markedly. Nonetheless, in realistic trial settings where sample sizes may be small and covariates may exhibit substantial prognostic value, separation remains a non-negligible concern.

When separation is caused by inclusion of covariates in the working model, one possible mitigation strategy is to remove problematic predictors, as suggested for example by Liu and Xi.
[
26
]
Importantly, however, our simulation results indicated that separation attributable to covariates did not materially affect performance of the
g
-computation approaches. When separation is driven by the treatment indicator, by contrast, it reflects an extremely strong observed treatment effect and typically affects all methods, often resulting in inflated Type I error rates. In such cases, excluding covariates does not alleviate the issue. For these reasons, we opted to retain all converged simulation runs, regardless of whether separation was present, when evaluating the performance of the
g
-computation estimators.

Figure 1:
Proportion of simulation runs with separation issues. Separation did not occur in sample sizes larger than 90.

Computational anomalies were not confined to regression approaches. The MH test also exhibited occasional failures. Specifically, in rare cases where only a single outcome category was observed across strata, the variance estimator collapsed, making computation of the
\chi^{2}
statistic impossible. This occurred in approximately
0.16\%
of runs, exclusively with a small sample size of
N=30
. These runs were excluded prior to summarizing the operating characteristics of the MH test. Overall, while numerical issues were infrequent for most methods, separation in logistic regression emerged as the dominant computational challenge in small-sample settings and warrants explicit consideration when interpreting inferential results.

5.2
Type I error rate

Across all scenarios with a true risk difference of zero, the observed Type I error rates exhibit a consistent pattern driven primarily by sample size and, to a lesser extent, by the prognostic strength of the included covariates (Figure
2
, top row).

At the smallest sample size (
N=30
), several
g
-computation approaches show pronounced Type I error inflation, particularly those relying on the (semi-)parametric variance estimators proposed by Ge et al. and Ye et al. Importantly, this inflation reflects variance underestimation rather than a failure of
g
-computation per se. The
g
-computation approaches based on the non-parametric bootstrap and the score test exhibit slightly smaller, but still clearly inflated Type I error rates relative to the nominal level. In contrast, the approaches proposed by Liu and Xi and the Firth-based
g
-computation method behave conservatively, with Type I error rates well below
0.05
in very small samples. As sample size increases, Type I error rates for all
g
-computation methods steadily converge toward the nominal level, with most approaches falling within an acceptable range around
0.05
by
N\geq 120
.

For the Mantel–Haenszel (MH) risk-difference estimator, the variance estimators proposed by Sato et al. and Qiu et al. display highly similar operating characteristics across all scenarios. At small sample sizes, both variants exhibit clear Type I error inflation, reaching levels comparable to those observed for the bootstrap-based
g
-computation approach. This inflation diminishes steadily as sample size increases and approaches the nominal level by
N\geq 120
. As for the other methods, the rate of convergence toward nominal Type I error control depends on the prognostic strength of the covariates, with accelerated convergence observed when covariates are more strongly prognostic.

The MH test maintains Type I error control close to the nominal level across all sample sizes and covariate settings, with only minor fluctuations around
0.05
. The Suissa–Shuster exact unconditional test is consistently the most conservative procedure, yielding Type I error rates below
0.05
across all scenarios, although these gradually approach the nominal level as sample size increases.

Taken together, these results suggest that (i) many
g
-computation approaches are liberal in very small samples irrespective of covariate prognostic strength, (ii) robust or penalized variants, such as those proposed by Liu and Xi or based on Firth’s correction, mitigate Type I error inflation at the cost of conservatism, and (iii) the MH and Suissa–Shuster tests remain robust, with the latter being characteristically conservative.

5.3
Power

Simulation results for statistical power are shown in the middle and bottom rows of Figure
2
. Power was evaluated under two treatment-effect scenarios: a small effect (
\delta=0.15
) and a large effect (
\delta=0.30
). For the smaller effect size, power was uniformly low across all methods, increasing from approximately
0.10
at
N=30
to about
0.60
at
N=150
. Although such operating characteristics are clearly suboptimal and indicate substantially underpowered trials, they nevertheless allow for a comparison of the
relative
performance of competing approaches. For the larger effect size, power covered a more practically relevant range from a sample size of
N=60
onwards, and increasing to nearly
0.99
for
N=150
.

Among the
g
-computation approaches, those based on (semi-)parametric variance estimators proposed by Ge et al. and Ye et al. consistently achieved the highest power across scenarios. These gains, however, came at the cost of Type I error inflation in small samples. At the opposite end of the spectrum, the more conservative procedures based on a Firth-penalized logistic regression working model and the variance estimator of Liu and Xi exhibited the lowest power when sample sizes were very small. Approaches using the non-parametric bootstrap or the score test fell between these extremes: their power was lower than that of the Ge et al.’s and Ye et al.’s methods, consistent with their more moderate Type I error inflation, but remained higher than that of the most conservative variants. As sample size, true effect size, and covariate prognostic strength increased, power rose rapidly for all methods and differences between approaches diminished accordingly.

For the MH risk-difference estimator, the variance estimators proposed by Sato et al. and Qiu et al. again showed nearly identical performance. In terms of power, they were broadly comparable to
g
-computation combined with either the non-parametric bootstrap or the score test across all scenarios. The MH test itself exhibited power similar to that of the Liu-Xi approach and the Firth-based
g
-computation method. In the settings considered here, and under a requirement for strict Type I error control, these results suggest that
g
-computation does not confer a substantial power advantage over the MH test, which appears more robust in small samples.
However, two qualifications of this comparison are important. First, the MH test targets a different estimand than
g
-computation—namely a common conditional treatment effect. Second, the present simulations considered only categorical covariates. As emphasized by Ge et al., the efficiency gains of
g
-computation over CMH-type methods become more pronounced when continuous covariates are available, as these would need to be discretized to be accommodated by CMH approaches.
[
12
]

Finally, the Suissa–Shuster exact unconditional test consistently yielded the lowest power across all scenarios. The gap relative to covariate-adjusted methods widened as covariates became more strongly prognostic, reflecting the fact that this procedure does not incorporate covariate information and therefore cannot benefit from their predictive value. Consequently, its power remained limited, and the discrepancy relative to adjusted procedures increased with covariate prognostic strength.

Overall, the results indicate that (i) for small effects and small samples, power is poor for all methods, and gains achieved by the most powerful procedures are accompanied by substantial Type I error inflation, whereas penalized or robust variants are more conservative and therefore less powerful; (ii) with larger effects, stronger prognostic covariates, and increasing sample size, power improves rapidly and differences among methods narrow, with MH risk-difference estimators matching the performance of
g
-computation using bootstrap or score-test inference and the MH test aligning with more conservative
g
-computation variants; and (iii) method selection should weigh both the targeted estimand and the covariate structure. In particular, when only categorical covariates are available and strict Type I error control is essential, the MH test provides a robust baseline, whereas the relative power advantages of
g
-computation can be expected to be more pronounced in the presence of continuous covariates.

5.4
Estimation bias

Beyond the binary decision of whether a treatment effect is statistically significant, precise estimation of the effect can be of key interest in clinical trials, particularly in non-confirmatory early-phase studies. To evaluate estimation performance across statistical approaches, we assessed bias, RMSE and coverage of 95% CIs for all methods that provide an estimate of the risk difference.

Estimation bias is defined as the deviation of the average estimated risk difference from the true value. Let
\hat{\delta}_{r}
denote the estimated risk difference in the
r
th simulation replication,
r=1,\ldots,R
, and
\delta
the true risk difference.
Bias was computed as

\text{Bias}=\left\{\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat{\delta}_{r}\right\}-\delta.

The RMSE, in turn, quantifies the overall estimation error by combining both bias
and
variance into a single measure.
It is defined as the square root of the average squared deviation of the estimator from the true value:

\text{RMSE}=\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}(\hat{\delta}_{r}-\delta)^{2}}.

Bias and RMSE for all methods under all simulated scenarios are displayed in Figures

and
4
, respectively.

), the picture changes for the
g
-computation estimator based on a Firth logistic regression working model. Here, considerable bias is visible, particularly with very small sample sizes. This behavior is expected: the Firth correction introduces intentional shrinkage to reduce variance, thus systematically pulling estimated response probabilities toward
.50
.
[
36
]
When both treatment and control arm estimates are shrunk toward the same central value, the resulting risk difference is biased downward.
At the same time, the method benefits from reduced variability, which is reflected in consistently lower RMSE compared with the other approaches. Both the magnitude of the bias and the degree of RMSE reduction decrease as sample size increases.

All other methods show no systematic bias across scenarios with a true effect. Their estimation performance is comparable, with RMSEs that decrease steadily as sample size grows. This pattern indicates that, apart from the known shrinkage tendency of the Firth-based estimator, all approaches provide accurate and increasingly precise estimates of the marginal risk difference.

To evaluate coverage performance across methods, we examined the empirical coverage of two-sided 95% CIs constructed for each simulated dataset. For all approaches except
g
-computation combined with the score test, Wald-type intervals were used. For the score-test-based
g
-computation estimator, confidence limits were obtained by inverting the corresponding
\chi^{2}
test statistic. Results are shown in Figure
5
.

Coverage of two-sided 95% confidence intervals for all considered statistical methods.

When the true treatment effect is zero, coverage rates simply reflect the observed Type I error rates: The
g
-computation estimators based on the Ge et al.’s and Ye et al.’s variance formulations, as well as the non-parametric bootstrap, show substantial undercoverage in small samples, reflecting their liberal testing behavior.
The MH risk-difference estimators perform slightly better but still exhibit clear undercoverage. CIs based on the inversion of the score-test statistic resulted in moderate undercoverage. In contrast, the Liu and Xi and the Firth-based estimators show slight overcoverage, in line with their conservative Type I error control. Across all methods, coverage values approached the nominal 95% level as sample size increased.

For scenarios with a true medium or large risk difference, the overall pattern remained largely consistent for the Ge et al. and Ye et al. estimators, the Mantel–Haenszel estimators, and the score-test-based approach, all of which continued to show undercoverage. The bootstrap method improved modestly, achieving coverage closer to the nominal level. Finally, the approaches based on Liu and Xi’s variance estimator and the Firth logistic regression model demonstrated excellent coverage performance across effect sizes, with empirical coverage rates very close to the nominal 95%. Thus, despite their conservative behavior and reduced power in hypothesis testing, these two estimators deliver reliable interval estimates when a true treatment effect is present even in the context of small to moderate sample sizes.

6
Discussion

6.1
Main insights

Invigorated by recent regulatory guidance, there has been growing interest in covariate-adjusted assessments of marginal risk differences for clinical trials with binary endpoints.
[
48
]
This development, in turn, has fostered a strong focus in the methodological literature on
g
-computation, which has emerged as a flexible and powerful framework for estimating causal effects in the context of non-linear endpoints and both continuous and discrete covariates. Much of the existing literature, however, centers on asymptotic theory, with only a few recent contributions, such as the work by Liu and Xi
[
26
]
, explicitly addressing also small-sample settings. For clinical trials with limited sample sizes, this represents a conceptual and practical challenge. First, it is unclear how well asymptotic approximations perform in the context of sparse data (see Magirr et al.
[
27
]
) Second,
g
-computation relies on a logistic regression working model that may fail to converge or whose parameter estimates may be unreliable due to complete or quasi-separation.
These concerns naturally raise the question of whether
g
-computation bears any practical advantage over comparatively simpler methods such as unconditional tests or CMH-type procedures in small-sample settings.
From a design-based perspective, however, regression-based covariate adjustment itself is justified by randomization and does not rely on correct specification of the working outcome model; the main risks arise from inappropriate variance estimation rather than from adjustment per se.
[
25
]

To address this question, we conducted a comprehensive simulation study comparing ten statistical procedures within the context of two-arm randomized controlled trials. Our evaluation focused on critical aspects of small-sample performance, including the frequency of computational problems, control of Type I error, statistical power, and the accuracy and precision of treatment-effect estimation.

Across the investigated scenarios, non-convergence of logistic regression models was rare, even under the smallest sample sizes. In contrast, separation leading to inflated variance estimates occurred with appreciable frequency when sample sizes were extremely small. Importantly, though, the negative impact of separation on the performance of affected methods was limited and even with total sample sizes as small as 60 patients,
g
-computation generally yielded reliable results. Nevertheless, we believe that practitioners should remain aware of potential computational anomalies and consider robust alternatives such as a Firth logistic regression working model or an assumption-lean procedure like the Suissa-Shuster test when necessary.

Regarding inferential properties, our results revealed considerable heterogeneity among methods. Several approaches exhibited marked inflation of Type I error rates in small samples, most prominently
g
-computation with the variance estimators proposed by Ge et al. and Ye et al., consistent with recent observations in the literature.
[
26
]
It is important to keep in mind, however, that our simulation framework evaluated inferential properties with respect to the MTE, which is not the targeted estimand for all of the methods considered. For example,
g
-computation with the variance estimator of Ge et al. targets the CPATE rather than the MTE. Thus, the observed inflation in Type I error for this approach may reflect the estimand mismatch rather than a fundamental deficiency of the method in the context of small samples (see also Magirr et al.
[
27
]
).

Score-test-based inference and the non-parametric bootstrap also showed elevated error rates at the smallest sample sizes, although both converged more quickly toward nominal levels as sample sizes increased.
Performance of the MH risk-difference estimator may be located somewhere in between, with moderate Type I error inflation that diminished with increasing sample size. In contrast, both the Suissa-Shuster exact unconditional test and the MH test demonstrated excellent to conservative Type I error control across all scenarios, though this robustness came at the cost of relatively smaller power. This is true in particular for the Suissa-Shuster test that does not incorporate any covariates and is thus insensitive to the availability of prognostic baseline information.

Finally, the more conservative
g
-computation approaches – namely those using the robust variance estimator of Liu and Xi or a Firth logistic regression working model – exhibited conservative Type I error behavior, especially in the context of very small samples, and accordingly reduced power, but excellent coverage. The Firth-based approach did, however, introduce a characteristic downward bias when the true risk difference was non-zero, a known consequence of shrinkage in predicted response probabilities toward
0.50
.
[
36
]
Nevertheless, both methods delivered the most reliable interval estimates under small-sample conditions, highlighting the familiar trade-offs between Type I error and statistical power, as well as between estimation bias and variance.

Our findings complement earlier work demonstrating the efficiency benefits of standardization-based estimators under randomization.
[
6
]
While these results establish consistency and asymptotic efficiency of marginal estimators, our simulations show that in small samples, inferential validity hinges critically on aligning the variance estimator with the estimand. Our findings are consistent with recent comparative work showing that, in randomized trials, covariate adjustment can improve efficiency but that inferential performance, particularly variance estimation and CI coverage, depends critically on modeling assumptions and is sensitive to misspecification.
[
46
]

6.2
Practical recommendations

A central aim of this work is to devise practical recommendations for the analysis of small-sample clinical trials with binary endpoints. Our findings reveal an unsettling, yet not unexpected insight: no single statistical method dominates across all relevant performance dimensions. Instead, the choice of a method necessarily entails balancing competing considerations such as computational stability, control of Type I error, statistical power, and the accuracy and precision of treatment-effect estimation. These characteristics must be weighed carefully in light of the specific trial objectives, particularly the estimand of interest and the role of prognostic covariates.

When strict Type I error control for inference on the MTE is of paramount importance, our results highlight several viable options. The Suissa-Shuster exact unconditional test stands out as a highly robust procedure that maintains nominal error control across all evaluated settings, albeit at the cost of not leveraging prognostic covariates and, consequently, reduced power. The appeal of such unconditional, design-based procedures lies in their transparency and minimal reliance on modeling assumptions, a perspective emphasized in critiques of regression adjustment in randomized experiments.
[
11
]
. Alternatively, to leverage prognostic information from categorical covariates, the CMH test also demonstrates reliable Type I error control. Its applicability may be limited, however, if sparse data result in too small cell counts or continuous covariates should be included in the analysis.
[
28
]
For these cases, robust
g
-computation combined with the Liu and Xi variance estimator or a Firth logistic regression working model offers a principled route to maintaining conservative error rates. These methods remain reliable even under pronounced small-sample conditions at the cost of reduced statistical power, although the advantage of covariate adjustment becomes evident once the sample size reaches moderate levels (
N\geq 90
).

For settings in which a balance between Type I error control and power is desired, our results suggest that
g
-computation combined with either the recently proposed score test or a non-parametric bootstrap procedure provides an appealing compromise. Both approaches exhibit moderate Type I error inflation for sample sizes
\geq 60
– less inflation than the MH risk-difference estimator with either of the two considered variance estimators while achieving substantially higher power. This performance is particularly notable given that our simulation scenarios were limited to categorical covariates; when continuous prognostic covariates are present, we anticipate that the efficiency gain of these
g
-computation methods will be even more pronounced.

When the primary focus is on accuracy in estimation rather than hypothesis testing, considerations shift toward bias and variance. Nearly all evaluated methods that produce a point estimate yield unbiased estimators, with the exception of
g
-computation based on a Firth logistic regression working model. The bias introduced by this procedure is intentional through the inclusion of a regularizing penalty term, but it is accompanied by reduced variance and consistently lower RMSE across all scenarios, as well as excellent confidence-interval coverage. Liu and Xi’s approach similarly provides very strong coverage performance without introducing bias.
Other methods, in contrast, show moderate to large undercoverage in small samples, with
g
-computation approaches based on the score test or non-parametric bootstrap achieving the closest approximation to nominal coverage levels in the considered sample-size range.

6.3
Consistency between estimation, testing, and confidence intervals

From a regulatory and scientific reporting perspective, it is desirable that a single analytical framework yields a coherent trio consisting of (i) a point estimator, (ii) a hypothesis test, and (iii) a confidence interval that are all aligned with the same estimand and based on compatible inferential logic. Inconsistencies between these components, such as testing one estimand while estimating another, or reporting confidence intervals that are not the inversion of the primary test, can complicate interpretation, undermine transparency, and raise concerns in regulatory review. Unadjusted and stratified design-based procedures illustrate these limitations clearly. In contrast,
g
-computation provides a unifying framework in which estimation, testing, and interval estimation are all derived from the same underlying standardized estimand. The standardized risk-difference estimator
\hat{\delta}=\hat{\pi}_{1}-\hat{\pi}_{0}
is well defined, interpretable on the marginal scale, and applicable to arbitrary covariate structures. The remaining question is how inference is constructed around this estimator in finite samples. Among the explored approached, two stood out as providing internally consistent inference within the
g
-computation framework: robust variance-based Wald inference targeting the MTE, as in Liu and Xi,
[
26
]
, and score-test–based inference, as proposed by Zhang et al.
[
55
]
The principal value of
g
-computation in small-sample trials is not adjustment per se, but coherence. When paired with an inferential procedure that respects the estimand and accounts for finite-sample uncertainty,
g
-computation offers a principled and credible route to reporting risk differences that integrates estimation, testing, and uncertainty quantification within a single framework.

6.4
Limitations and future directions

There are limitations of our study that merit consideration and point toward avenues for future research.

First, our simulations considered only categorical baseline covariates. This choice ensured that a controlled comparison across methods is possible, where differences in performance cannot be attributed to differences in the set of considered covariates. Nevertheless, it necessarily restricts the generalizability of our findings. In practical applications, continuous prognostic covariates are common, and their inclusion may alter the relative performance of the approaches considered herein. In particular, we expect the efficiency gains of
g
-computation relative to CMH-type procedures to be more pronounced when continuous covariates can be fully exploited without discretization (see Ge et al.).
[
12
]
)

Second, we restricted the simulated scenarios to correctly specified models in the sense that all covariate-adjusted procedures included the same set of covariates used for data generation. Because our primary aim was to compare methods rather than evaluate them on an absolute scale, this design choice ensured a level playing field across approaches. Nevertheless, model misspecification is an omnipresent concern in applied analyses.
[
46
]
The relative robustness of the methods – the
g
-computation variants in particular – to omitted covariates, incorrect link functions, or interactions not specified in the working model thus remains to be addressed.

Third, a further methodological dimension concerns the randomization scheme. Our simulations were based on simple randomization, which is arguably not the most prevalent choice in modern clinical trials. Instead, stratified or covariate-adaptive randomization schemes that are implemented to balance treatment assignment across key covariates are increasingly applied in practice. Variance estimators that appropriately account for stratified randomization are available, e.g., Wang et al.
[
50
]
, Bannick et al.
[
3
]
, or Zhang et al.
[
55
]
, and may further improve efficiency of statistical inference. Future work should therefore evaluate how stratified or covariate-adaptive randomization and appropriate strategies to adopt estimators accordingly affect the comparative performance of different statistical methods.

Fourth, finite-sample corrections for Wald-type inference based on sandwich variance estimators, such as the bias-adjusted variance and
F
-reference distributions proposed by Fay and Graubard, could in principle be applied to
g
-computation estimators, but require nontrivial choices regarding the effective number of independent contributions and parameter-specific degrees of freedom, and are not routinely used.
[
8
]
Closely related, Tsiatis et al. note that sandwich-based variance estimators for covariate-adjusted estimators can be downward biased in small samples and propose multiplicative variance inflation factors that depend on sample size and the number of fitted regression parameters.
[
47
]
A systematic evaluation of such corrections for marginal risk differences would constitute a separate investigation and was beyond the scope of the present study, which instead focused on methods with established design-based or randomization-based inferential guarantees.

Finally, several relevant approaches were not included in the present comparison. Most notably, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) and targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE) were not considered.
[
52
,
30
,
41
]
Previous work has shown that robust standard errors for IPTW were anti-conservative in small samples.
[
31
,
46
]
A number of related methods, such as overlap weighting, doubly robust estimating-equation approaches, and higher-order influence-function estimators, were also omitted. Their implementation, tuning, and inferential targets differ materially and would require separate, dedicated investigation. We further did not consider the approach proposed by Klingenberg, which targets risk differences under specific stratification and homogeneity assumptions and is closely related to MH-type constructions represented in our study.
[
21
]
Extending the simulation to include these methods would substantially broaden the scope and might have obscured a more focused comparison.

6.5
Conclusion

This study provides a large simulation-based evaluation of methods for covariate-adjusted inference on risk differences in small-sample randomized clinical trials. Rather than identifying a universally superior procedure, our results show that each has a distinct profile of advantages and disadvantages with respect to computational stability, Type I error control, statistical power, and estimation accuracy. Simple, highly robust procedures such as unconditional exact tests ensure strict error control but forego the efficiency gains available through covariate adjustment, whereas
g
-computation methods can capitalize on prognostic information yet require careful selection of variance estimators and working models to perform reliably in small samples. Our findings underscore the importance of selecting statistical methods that are explicitly aligned with the estimand of interest, the inferential objective, and operational constraints of the trial. When this alignment is achieved, covariate-adjusted analyses can deliver coherent point estimates, tests, and confidence intervals that are consistent with modern estimand-focused regulatory guidance. Overall, our results reinforce that method selection should be guided primarily by the estimand of interest and the inferential framework, rather than by covariate adjustment alone. By clarifying the trade-offs and providing empirically grounded properties and recommendations, this work supports principled and transparent analysis choices for small-sample clinical trials.
