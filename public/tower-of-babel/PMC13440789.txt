Radiation-induced cavernous malformation arising within vestibular schwannoma manifesting as subarachnoid hemorrhage: Case report and literature review
Authors: Yoshinari Hirano, Kentaro Mori, Shinichi Numazawa, Sadayoshi Watanabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Radiation-induced cavernous malformation within a vestibular schwannoma is rare, with only four previous cases reported.Case Description: A 65-year-old female developed a radiation-induced cavernous malformation within a vestibular schwannoma 2 years after gamma knife radiosurgery, initially presenting with mild facial palsy. Six days later, she was admitted with an acute severe headache, trigeminal neuropathy, complete abducens palsy, complete facial palsy, and deafness. Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging revealed subarachnoid hemorrhage as well as an intratumoral hematoma. Three weeks after the hemorrhage, the tumor was gross totally removed to reduce the mass effect and prevent recurrent hemorrhage. However, her symptoms persisted after discharge.Conclusion: A review of reported cases suggests that radiation-induced cavernous malformation within vestibular schwannoma shows a higher rate of hemorrhagic transformation (80%) with multiple cranial nerve dysfunction compared with radiation-induced cavernous malformation in the brain (approximately 20%). Although radiation-induced cavernous malformation in vestibular schwannoma is rare, long-term follow-up is warranted.

INTRODUCTION

Cavernous malformation is generally considered to be of congenital origin but may also occur as a delayed radiation-induced injury in the central nervous system (CNS). Radiation-induced cavernous malformations tend to occur in the cerebral hemispheres of patients who have undergone radiation therapy for CNS neoplasms. Children are more susceptible to developing radiation-induced cavernous malformations than adults.[6] Radiation-induced cavernous malformation associated with vestibular schwannoma is extremely rare, with only four cases reported to date.[1,4,5,10] We describe a case of radiation-induced cavernous malformation which occurred in a vestibular schwannoma and manifested as subarachnoid hemorrhage.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 65-year-old woman presented to a local hospital with left facial numbness and a 23-mm tumor in the left cerebellopontine (CP) angle. The tumor had compressed the pons (Koos grade IV). Her left hearing was preserved (15 dB), and she and her family were eager to pursue hearing preservation surgery. Therefore, partial tumor resection followed by gamma knife radiosurgery (GKRS) was performed. The pathological diagnosis was schwannoma. Subsequently, her hearing remained serviceable and the facial numbness resolved. The tumor gradually decreased in size. Two years after GKRS, the patient’s facial numbness recurred, accompanied by mild left facial palsy. Magnetic resonance (MR) imaging demonstrated the residual vestibular schwannoma and a newly developed hypointense lesion within it, raising suspicion of a radiation-induced cavernous malformation [Figure 1A]. Six days after the MR imaging, the patient developed a sudden severe headache and vomiting. Computed tomography and MR imaging revealed intratumoral hemorrhage associated with subarachnoid hemorrhage [Figure 1B and C]. The mass had compressed the pons, and mild hydrocephalus was noted. Neurological examination showed left trigeminal neuropathy, complete left abducens palsy, complete facial palsy (House– Brackmann grade IV), and hearing loss. Three weeks after the hemorrhage, surgical tumor removal was performed via a lateral suboccipital craniotomy. A gray-colored, well-demarcated tumor was identified in the CP angle, and an old blood clot was observed in the subarachnoid space. The tumor was opened, and the old blood within it was evacuated. The tumor capsule was removed. A red-colored mass around the internal auditory canal, suspected to be a cavernous malformation, was also removed. The trigeminal, abducens, and facial nerves were decompressed. The tumor was gross totally removed [Figure 1D]. Histopathological examination demonstrated tightly packed, thin-walled blood vessels without intervening neural tissue, consistent with cavernous malformation in addition to schwannoma [Figure 2]. The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient’s facial numbness subsided, but the facial and abducens nerve palsies remained unchanged at the time of discharge.

DISCUSSION

The mechanism underlying the development of cavernous malformation after radiation therapy remains unclear, but presumably chronic inflammation caused by radiation-induced tissue damage leads to vascular endothelial growth factor-dependent angiogenesis, resulting in the formation of cavernous malformations.[2] Sporadic cases of radiation-induced cavernous malformation in the adjacent brainstem have been reported after GKRS for vestibular schwannoma,[8,9] but cases arising within vestibular schwannomas are extremely rare, with only five reported cases, including the present case.[1,4,5,10] The clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Patient age ranged from 39 to 78 years (mean, 59.4 years), and four of the five patients were female. The interval between radiosurgery and cavernous malformation formation ranged from 2 to 16 years (mean, 9.2 years). The three previous cases showed intratumoral hemorrhage, whereas our case presented with both subarachnoid hemorrhage and intratumoral hemorrhage. Four cases were symptomatic and exhibited various cranial nerve dysfunctions in close proximity to the cavernous malformation. All tumors were surgically resected and histologically confirmed as cavernous malformations. The age distribution and female predominance of radiation-induced cavernous malformations within vestibular schwannoma are consistent with the general characteristics of vestibular schwannoma. A systematic review of radiation-induced cavernous malformations in brain tissue reported a mean latency of 9.2 years after radiation, with 21% presenting with hemorrhage and 33% symptomatic (e.g., focal neurological deficits and seizures).[6] Radiation-induced cavernous malformations within vestibular schwannomas develop after a similar latency period but appear to have a higher incidence of symptomatic hemorrhage (80%) compared with radiation-induced cavernous malformations in the brain. Interestingly, cases of cavernous malformation arising within vestibular schwannomas without prior radiation therapy have also been reported, and these lesions tend to show a high rate of hemorrhagic transformation, including subarachnoid hemorrhage.[3,7] Both cavernous malformations which arise spontaneously or develop following radiation therapy appear to carry a high risk of hemorrhage associated with cranial nerve deficits. Radiation-induced cavernous malformations within vestibular schwannomas are often large and symptomatic at presentation, so all reported cases have undergone surgical resection. Surgical removal is expected to alleviate the mass effect and prevent recurrent hemorrhage. Radiation-induced cavernous malformation within vestibular schwannoma is a rare but highly hemorrhagic condition associated with cranial nerve dysfunction, so long-term radiological follow-up is warranted. Given the high likelihood of hemorrhage and subsequent symptom development, an incidentally discovered radiation-induced cavernous malformation in a vestibular schwannoma may warrant consideration of early surgical intervention.

CONCLUSION

We reported a case of radiation-induced cavernous malformation arising within a vestibular schwannoma in a 65-year-old patient who presented with subarachnoid hemorrhage and multiple cranial nerve palsies. Radiation-induced cavernous malformation within a vestibular schwannoma is a rare but highly hemorrhagic condition associated with cranial nerve dysfunction, so long-term follow-up is warranted.
