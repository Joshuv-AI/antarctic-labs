Personality-Driven Student Agent-Based Modeling in Mathematics Education: How Well Do Student Agents Align with Human Learners?
Bushi Xiao, Qian Shen

Abstract
It is crucial to explore the impact of different teaching methods on student learning in educational research. However, real-person experiments face significant ethical constraints, and we cannot conduct repeated teaching experiments on the same student. LLM-based generative agents offer a promising avenue for simulating student behavior. Before large-scale experiments, a fundamental question must be addressed: are student agents truly credible, and can they faithfully simulate human learning? In this study, we built a Big Five Personality-based student agent model with a full pipeline of student-teacher interaction, self-study, and examination. To evaluate behavioral fidelity, we collected 13 empirical studies on Big Five traits and learning, and distilled them into 14 criteria. We found that the 71.4% of the student agents’ behavior was aligned with human learners.

Agent-Based Modeling (ABM) is a powerful simulation approach for complex systems. In the first work on generative agents,
20

27

Research on LLM-based math instruction shows that agent personality traits significantly change learning outcomes — students favor complementary personalities

13

Constructing a full-pipeline teaching agent is technically challenging, yet the value of building such agents is substantial: they enable simulation of learning trajectories over time, which is a genuine gap in the literature, and allow systematic testing of how different personality types respond to different teaching styles, something nearly impossible in human-participant studies. They also offer cost-effective alternatives to human experiments and may yield unexpected findings.

Nevertheless, researchers cannot directly use student agents for experiments before validating their effectiveness and fidelity. In this paper, we address this question by constructing ABMs grounded in the Big Five personality traits, evaluating their learning and exam performance across four mathematical domains: Algebra, Number Theory, Counting and Probability, and Geometry. We analyzed the results of student agents with different personalities after rounds of teacher interaction or self-study

During the learning round, student agents decide among three actions: asking the teacher, self-studying, or resting. When asking the teacher, the student selects a topic of interest, and the teacher agent explains the relevant knowledge, retrieves a closely related question from the question bank, and walks through the solution. The teacher agent doesn’t have a memory system, but is informed to explain the question adaptively to the student’s personality. During self-study, the student selects a topic to review and independently retrieves a related question to study. Resting skips the round entirely. At the end of each learning round, newly acquired knowledge is encoded into memory and merged with prior memory, with all entries vectorized for retrieval. During the exam round, student agents retrieve relevant memories to inform their responses to exam questions. The entire pipeline is more like a real person than a machine learning model, where the agent decides what to learn and what to recall during the exam.

Results and Discussion

We configured the teaching system to run for 10, 20, and 50 rounds, with each set of rounds run independently for 3 times. We also set up a control group without learning. Each exam is single-topic; a round consists of 100 questions. We then measured and recorded the learning outcomes of the student agents.

The F1 (macro F1) results from figure
2
show that for all four math types, all agents showed improvement after some rounds of learning. However, fewer rounds of learning often outperformed 50 rounds, suggesting that exam performance tends to drop after over-learning — possibly because once memory becomes saturated, the retriever struggles to retrieve useful information to assist with answering. We can also observe that agent performance varies across different math types: even though all math problems came from the same original dataset, agents’ understanding of geometry was far weaker than algebra, resulting in an average score difference of nearly 30%. Another notable finding is that learning significantly reduced the number of blank answers, though high-neuroticism student agents were an exception compared to other agents.

The bottom of Figure
2
illustrates interaction choices across personality types: high-conscientiousness agents interacted least with the teacher, while those high-agreeableness and openness showed a greater tendency to do so. Most notably, high-neuroticism agents engaged with the teacher in every single learning round — the highest rate among all five personalities. Regarding time cost, high-conscientiousness agents consumed the fewest timestamps during both learning and examination, while high-neuroticism agents consumed the most, consistent with procrastination patterns documented in the literature
(
23
;
9
)
. In terms of final average score rankings, high-extraversion student agents performed best overall.

To validate that agent behavior aligns with human learners, we collect 13 empirical studies in Table

Overall, student agents perform similarly to human learners across general dimensions, with minor discrepancies in the math domain. High-extraversion agent rather than high-conscientiousness achieved the best scores — though this is not entirely inconsistent with the literature:
18
reported that extraversion is associated with more effective student interactions, and
16
notes that extraversion is a stronger predictor of academic performance in early education, with conscientiousness becoming dominant only at higher levels. After analyzing the saved agent memory, we found that high-extraversion agents get an average longer teacher’s reply during learning rounds, while high-conscientiousness agents interacted least with the teacher and tend to study by themselves, which may partly explain their underwhelming performance.

The most anomalies also emerged in teacher-student interaction: agents high in neuroticism interacted most frequently, contrary to expectations.
5
mentioned that anxiety leads to executive help-seeking. Thus, we hypothesize that the high-neuroticism student agent’s model captured the anxiety-driven help-seeking tendency, but failed to simulate the help-avoidance mechanism in real-world humans. Low-quality interactions also diluted the agent’s learning memories, ultimately resulting in the weakest academic performance. In contrast, learning behavior and self-regulation aligned well with human patterns: agents high in neuroticism exhibited the most procrastination, while agents high in conscientiousness were the least prone to it.

Limitations

A key limitation of this study is the lack of a well-defined quantitative framework for evaluating how closely agent behavior mirrors that of human learners. Our current evaluation relies on manual scoring against findings from prior literature, partly because exam scores are straightforward to measure while learning behaviors are not, and partly because fine-grained behavioral data from human studies is rarely available. For instance, while the literature may report what proportion of students seek teacher help, it seldom captures the quality of those interactions or their specific content, leaving us to infer interaction quality indirectly from exam performance.

Personality prompts are static, but real human personalities change dynamically during learning, and agents cannot simulate these situational fluctuations in personality. Furthermore, it’s difficult to determine whether personality has a direct or indirect impact on the agent. While we can confirm a correlation between academic and exam performance and the established personality traits, the mechanism remains unclear. Potentially, we could explore whether personality prompts influence the model’s token probabilities.

On the other hand, these limitations point to promising directions for future work. One particularly interesting avenue is studying the interaction between student and teacher personality: are similar personalities more effective, or complementary ones? This kind of crossover design is difficult to run with human participants, but well-suited to agent-based simulation.

Appendix A
Computational Cost and Runtime

We conducted all experiments using one NVIDIA L4 GPU alongside the gpt-oss-120b API, where the GPU was used to embed the question bank into vectors and to perform retrieval for memory and teacher-student interactions. We used the GPT-oss-120b API as the backbone model. Running 50 learning rounds took approximately 20 minutes, while completing a 100-question exam took approximately 90 minutes. The exact time varied by agent personality: students high in Neuroticism consumed more time, while those high in Conscientiousness consumed less.

Appendix B
Timestamp Rules

During each learning round, the student agent autonomously selects one of three actions:

Self-Study:
The agent first decides what content to review, then the retriever fetches a relevant question (with answer) from the question bank based on the agent’s stated intent, which is subsequently stored in memory. This action consumes
2 timestamps
.

Ask Teacher:
The agent first decides on the topic or direction of the question. The teacher agent, who has been informed of the student’s personality profile prior to the interaction, responds accordingly and adapts their instruction to the student’s personality traits. The teacher then retrieves a relevant question from the question bank to illustrate the explanation, which the student stores in memory. This action consumes
3 timestamps
.

Take a Rest:
The agent skips the current round. This action consumes
1 timestamp
.

During the examination round, for each question, the student agent first reads the problem, then decides what memories to retrieve, and the retriever fetches the most relevant entries from the memory store. The agent then answers the question following the prompt using the retrieved memories. Under normal circumstances, answering one question consumes
2 timestamps
. If the memory retrieval step returns no response, the agent is granted one additional attempt to reconsider and re-retrieve, consuming an additional
1 timestamp
, for a total of
3 timestamps
for that question.

Appendix C
Question Domains

We do not rely on a large-scale dataset. Instead, we curate a question bank of several hundred problems per mathematical domain, each with detailed solutions and worked examples to support both self-study and teacher-agent explanations. The question bank is split into a development set and a test set. Not every development problem is encountered during learning — how much a student agent engages with the material depends on its personality — but we ensure no overlap between the problems seen during learning and those used in the exam. The statistical data for the question bank is shown in Table
2
.

Exam scoring is based on final answers only, with no assessment of intermediate steps. Since answers may be formatted differently by different agents, a response is counted as correct if it matches either the LaTeX or plain-text version of the reference answer. Per-question F1 is taken as the maximum across both formats, and the overall score is reported as the macro F1.

•

High Openness:

“You are a student with high openness to experience. When encountering a new concept, you naturally ask why rather than just accepting the procedure. High Conscientiousness:

“You are a student with high conscientiousness. You need to fully understand and consolidate each step before moving forward. You track what has and hasn’t been covered, and you feel uncomfortable leaving things unresolved. You rarely rush. Accuracy matters more to you than speed.”

•

High Extraversion:

“You are a student with high extraversion. You think by talking. You share unfinished thoughts, react out loud, and actively try to turn explanations into dialogue. You’re energized by back-and-forth exchange. You’re not afraid of being wrong in front of others. Silence feels unproductive to you.”

•

High Agreeableness:

“You are a student with high agreeableness. You prioritize harmony in the interaction. You acknowledge the teacher’s explanation before adding your own thoughts, and you soften any disagreement to avoid creating friction. You rarely push back directly. High Neuroticism:

“You are a student with high neuroticism. You care deeply about doing well, and that anxiety is visible in how you communicate. Appendix F
Sample Student-Teacher Interaction

The following is an example of a teacher-student interaction from student memory, learning round 4. The high-extraversion student agent chose to ask the teacher in the last timestamp, and asked the teacher about factoring quadratic expressions.

Student Query:
Can you explain how to factor quadratic expressions by completing the square, and maybe walk through an example step-by-step?

Retrieved Question from Question Bank:

(1)

Factorization:
2y^{2}-8

(2)

Solve the equation
x^{2}+4x+3=0

Teacher Response:

Hey there!
Let’s dive into “completing the square” together. I’ll walk you through the idea, then we’ll smash an example step-by-step. Feel free to shout out any thoughts, questions, or “wait, why did we do that?” as we go — think of this as a math jam session.

The big idea: turning a quadratic into a perfect square.

A quadratic
ax^{2}+bx+c
can often be rewritten as:

a\!\left(x^{2}+\frac{b}{a}x\right)+c=a\!\left[\left(x+\frac{b}{2a}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{b}{2a}\right)^{2}\right]+c.

Why? Because
(x+d)^{2}=x^{2}+2dx+d^{2}
. If we can make the “
x^{2}+\ldots
” part look exactly like the expansion of a square, we’ll have a
difference of squares
:

\underbrace{(\text{something})^{2}}_{\text{perfect square}}-\underbrace{(\text{another number})^{2}}_{\text{constant}}.

And a difference of squares factors instantly:

A^{2}-B^{2}=(A-B)(A+B).

That’s the “completing the square” trick in a nutshell.

Step-by-step recipe
(for a monic quadratic, i.e. leading coefficient
=1
):

1.

Start with
x^{2}+bx+c
.

2.

Take half of the linear coefficient
b
:
\dfrac{b}{2}
.

3.

Square that half:
\left(\dfrac{b}{2}\right)^{2}
.

4.

Rewrite as
\left(x+\dfrac{b}{2}\right)^{2}-\left(\dfrac{b}{2}\right)^{2}+c
.

Worked Example:
Solve
x^{2}+4x+3=0
.

The expression
x^{2}+4x+3
factors into
(x+1)(x+3)
, since
1\times 3=3
and
1+3=4
. Setting each factor to zero gives:

x_{1}=-1,\quad x_{2}=-3.

Note: The teacher agent was informed of the student’s personality profile (high Extraversion) prior to the interaction.
