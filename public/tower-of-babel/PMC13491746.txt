Association of maternal hemoglobin and ferritin levels with fetal cortical maturation: a prospective neurosonographic study
Authors: Murat Haksever, Deniz Taşdemir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the association between maternal hemoglobin and ferritin levels and fetal cortical maturation assessed by neurosonography between 30 and 34 weeks of gestation.MethodsThis prospective observational study included 187 singleton pregnancies between 30 + 0 and 34 + 6 weeks of gestation. Maternal hemoglobin and ferritin levels were measured at the time of fetal neurosonographic evaluation. Detailed neurosonography was performed using standardized axial and coronal planes to assess linear cortical measurements and semi-quantitative cortical maturation scores. Global cortical development and Sylvian fissure opercularization were graded using established scoring systems. Group comparisons were performed according to maternal anemia status (hemoglobin < 11 g/dL). Correlation, multivariable regression, receiver operating characteristic (ROC), dose-response, effect size, and sensitivity analyses were conducted.ResultsMaternal hemoglobin levels showed weak but statistically significant positive correlations with selected regional cortical parameters, including the frontal angle/parameter, cingulate sulcus depth, and cavum septi pellucidi (CSP) length. Maternal ferritin levels were not significantly associated with any cortical maturation marker. CSP length was significantly shorter in anemic pregnancies. In multivariable regression analysis, gestational age was the only independent predictor of the global cortical development score. ROC analyses demonstrated poor discriminative performance of hemoglobin and ferritin for identifying low global cortical development or reduced CSP length. Dose-response analyses across hemoglobin tertiles showed monotonic increases in selected regional cortical parameters, although effect sizes were small.ConclusionMaternal hemoglobin levels are weakly associated with selected regional neurosonographic markers of fetal cortical maturation during the third trimester, whereas ferritin levels show no significant association. Gestational age remains the primary determinant of global cortical maturation. These findings suggest subtle within-cohort associations rather than definitive evidence of overt abnormal cortical maturation.

Introduction

Fetal cerebral cortical maturation is a dynamic and tightly regulated developmental process that accelerates during the late second and third trimesters of pregnancy. Progressive sulcation, gyrification, and opercularization can be evaluated in utero using advanced prenatal neurosonography and fetal magnetic resonance imaging. Previous imaging studies have shown that major cortical landmarks, including the Sylvian fissure, insula, cingulate sulcus, calcarine sulcus, and cavum septi pellucidi, undergo predictable developmental changes with advancing gestational age, reflecting normal neurodevelopmental progression [1–3]. Accordingly, gestational age is considered the principal determinant of fetal cortical maturation.

Maternal iron status is a potentially modifiable factor that may influence fetal brain development through several biological mechanisms, including oxygen transport, neuronal energy metabolism, neurotransmitter synthesis, and myelination. Iron deficiency during pregnancy has been associated with adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes in infancy and childhood, and impaired fetal or neonatal iron availability may have persistent effects even after postnatal correction [4–6]. However, most available evidence derives from postnatal neurodevelopmental assessments rather than direct prenatal evaluation of fetal cortical maturation.

Prenatal neurosonography provides a unique opportunity to investigate fetal cortical development directly during gestation. Nevertheless, studies examining the relationship between maternal hematologic parameters and detailed neurosonographic markers of cortical maturation remain limited. In particular, the relative contribution of functional iron status, reflected by hemoglobin, and iron stores, reflected by ferritin, to fetal cortical maturation during the third trimester is not well established. Therefore, this prospective neurosonographic study aimed to evaluate the association between maternal hemoglobin and ferritin levels and fetal cortical maturation between 30 and 34 weeks of gestation using standardized linear and semi-quantitative cortical markers.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This prospective observational study was conducted at Şanlıurfa Training and Research Hospital between January 2025 and January 2026. Singleton pregnancies between 30 + 0 and 34 + 6 weeks of gestation were consecutively recruited during routine antenatal visits. Gestational age was determined according to first-trimester crown-rump length measurements and confirmed from medical records.

Pregnancies complicated by preeclampsia, fetal growth restriction, pregestational or gestational diabetes mellitus, major fetal structural anomalies, or known chromosomal abnormalities were excluded because of their established effects on fetal brain development. Multiple pregnancies and cases with inadequate image quality precluding reliable neurosonographic assessment were also excluded.

Laboratory assessment

Maternal venous blood samples were obtained at the time of neurosonographic evaluation. Maternal anemia was defined as hemoglobin < 11 g/dL, in accordance with third-trimester reference thresholds. Serum ferritin levels were analyzed as a continuous variable reflecting maternal iron stores. All laboratory measurements were performed using standardized automated methods in the hospital’s central laboratory.

Neurosonographic assessment

All fetal neurosonographic examinations were performed using a Samsung RS85 ultrasound system (Samsung Medison, South Korea) equipped with a high-frequency transabdominal transducer. All fetal neurosonographic examinations were performed by two experienced perinatologists who were blinded to maternal laboratory results at the time of image acquisition.

Standardized axial and coronal planes were obtained in accordance with established fetal neurosonography principles. Linear measurements included:

All measurements were recorded in millimeters and digitally stored for analysis.

In addition to linear measurements, fetal cortical maturation was assessed using semi-quantitative grading systems. Global cortical development was scored on the basis of sulcal complexity and cortical folding patterns, while Sylvian fissure opercularization was graded according to the degree of opercular coverage of the insula, with lower scores indicating less advanced maturation.

For assessment of interobserver reliability, a randomly selected subset of examinations was independently evaluated by a second experienced perinatologist blinded to the initial assessment and maternal laboratory data. Interobserver agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa coefficient and demonstrated excellent agreement for cortical maturation grading (κ = 0.84).

Ethical approval

The study protocol was approved by the Harran University Faculty of Medicine Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: HRÜ/25.02.55; Date: 27.01.2025). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with complementary Python-based analyses where required. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous and ordinal variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate.

Group comparisons between anemic and non-anemic pregnancies were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test or Welch’s t test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Associations between maternal laboratory parameters and cortical maturation measures were evaluated using Spearman rank correlation coefficients.

To identify independent predictors of global cortical development, multivariable linear regression analysis was performed, adjusting for gestational age at neurosonography, gravidity, and parity.

Optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden index. Dose-response relationships were explored by categorizing maternal hemoglobin levels into tertiles, with trend analyses performed using Spearman rank correlation. Effect sizes for group differences were calculated using Cliff’s delta.

To account for multiple comparisons, false discovery rate correction was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Sensitivity analyses excluding gestational age extremes (30 and 34 weeks) were performed to assess the robustness of the findings. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

As expected, maternal hemoglobin and ferritin levels were significantly lower in the anemia group (both p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this prospective neurosonographic study, we evaluated the association between maternal hemoglobin and ferritin levels and fetal cortical maturation between 30 and 34 weeks of gestation. Our results showed that maternal hemoglobin level was weakly associated with selected regional neurosonographic parameters, including the frontal angle/parameter, cingulate sulcus depth, and CSP length, whereas maternal ferritin level was not significantly associated with the cortical maturation markers evaluated. Importantly, gestational age was the only independent predictor of global cortical development in multivariable analysis.

The dominant effect of gestational age on global cortical maturation in our study is consistent with the established fetal neurodevelopmental literature. Cortical sulcation, gyrification, and opercularization follow a highly ordered temporal sequence during late gestation, and both prenatal ultrasound and fetal MRI studies have shown that these maturational changes are strongly age dependent [1–3, 7, 8]. Our findings therefore support the concept that gestational age remains the principal determinant of overall cortical development, whereas maternal hematologic factors may be associated only with subtle regional variation.

Iron plays a central role in fetal neurodevelopment through its effects on oxygen transport, mitochondrial metabolism, neurotransmitter synthesis, and myelination [4, 9–12]. Previous studies have linked maternal iron deficiency and anemia with adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes in offspring, particularly when fetal iron availability is impaired during critical developmental windows [5, 6, 11, 13]. However, much of this evidence is based on postnatal developmental assessment rather than direct prenatal imaging. Our study adds to the existing literature by examining detailed in utero neurosonographic parameters during the third trimester.

A key point in interpreting our findings is that the statistically significant associations observed were weak, and the effect sizes were small. Moreover, maternal hemoglobin and ferritin showed poor discriminative performance for identifying low global cortical development or reduced CSP length. Taken together, these findings do not support the use of maternal hemoglobin or ferritin as standalone predictors of clinically meaningful cortical maturation delay. Rather, they suggest subtle within-cohort associations between maternal hemoglobin and selected regional cortical markers.

The absence of significant associations between ferritin and cortical maturation measures is also noteworthy. Ferritin reflects iron stores, but it may be influenced by inflammation and physiologic changes during pregnancy [14]. In contrast, hemoglobin may more closely reflect functional oxygen-carrying capacity and thus may have a more immediate relationship with fetal cerebral physiology. This may partly explain why weak associations were observed for hemoglobin but not for ferritin.

The clinical relevance of small differences in specific prenatal cortical and midline measurements remains uncertain. Although these markers may reflect aspects of maturational progression in utero, direct evidence linking minor third-trimester differences in these individual neurosonographic parameters to later cognitive, motor, or behavioral outcomes is limited. Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating overt abnormal cortical maturation. Rather, they should be regarded as subtle relative differences observed within the study cohort.

This study has several strengths, including its prospective design, standardized neurosonographic protocol, assessment of both linear and semi-quantitative cortical maturation markers, and broad statistical evaluation incorporating regression, ROC, dose-response, effect size, and sensitivity analyses. Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study was conducted at a single center. Second, postnatal neurodevelopmental follow-up was not available, which limits interpretation of the long-term clinical significance of the prenatal findings. Third, maternal hematologic parameters were assessed at a single time point in late pregnancy. Finally, although interobserver agreement was available for cortical maturation grading, parameter-specific reproducibility data were not available for all individual linear measurements.

In conclusion, maternal hemoglobin levels were weakly associated with selected regional neurosonographic markers of fetal cortical maturation during the third trimester, whereas maternal ferritin levels were not significantly associated with these measures. Gestational age remained the primary determinant of global cortical maturation. These findings suggest subtle, region-specific within-cohort associations rather than definitive evidence of overt abnormal cortical maturation.
