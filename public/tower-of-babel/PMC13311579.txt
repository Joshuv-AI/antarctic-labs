Exploring factors that influence role transition from registered nurse to nurse practitioner in Quebec: a pre-implementation study using a cross-sectional survey design
Authors: Shannon McNamara, Lisa Cranley, Kelley Kilpatrick, Monica Parry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNurse practitioner (NP) education must address increasingly complex demands of the healthcare setting, as underprepared students demonstrate poor clinical performance and difficulties in making autonomous decisions. Educational preparedness is an important element impacting transition to practice. Difficulties during role transition impact intent to leave the profession, inability to implement advanced practice skills, anxiety and poor job satisfaction, leading to poor patient care and adverse outcomes. This study aims to explore factors that influence novice NP role transition in Quebec, Canada.Method and analysisThis protocol for a descriptive cross-sectional survey design with non-probability sampling will be used to recruit NPs working in their first NP position for less than 12 months in Quebec. English and French versions of the Novice Nurse Practitioner Role Transition (NNPRT) scale will be completed by a minimum of 50 NPs and simple linear univariate regression will be used for data analyses. The analysis will be undertaken in consultation with a biostatistician using SPSS V.29. Descriptive statistics will be used to describe and summarise the sample demographics, the total NNPRT score and each of the five factors (organisation alignment, mentorship, sense of purpose, perceived competency, self-confidence and compensation).Simple linear univariate regression will be used to explore the relationship between perceived competency and self-confidence and the role transition experience of novice NPs in Quebec.Ethics and disseminationSubmission to the University of Toronto Health Sciences Research Ethics Board was completed and approved prior to the start of the study (Protocol number 48493). This study started 28 July 2025 and remains in the participant recruitment phase. Full data analysis will be completed and submitted for publication at the end of the study.

Introduction

Critical thinking, essential to Nurse practitioner (NP) practice, can be developed through a wide variety of teaching and learning activities during the NP’s academic trajectory. Critical thinking is a term used to describe the higher order cognitive activities that are involved in the process of reaching an informed clinical decision. It is the foundation for the development of clinical reasoning, diagnostic reasoning and clinical judgement.1 NP education must address increasingly complex demands of the healthcare setting, as underprepared students demonstrate poor clinical performance and difficulties in making autonomous decisions.2 Teaching and learning activities should promote higher levels of critical thinking to better prepare NP students as they transition into practice.3 It is suggested that these activities can facilitate role transition early in the academic trajectory by employing the principles of evidence-based practice3 and by generating innovative teaching solutions.2 It is also important to incorporate an educational framework that can identify gaps so that these can be addressed by the teaching and learning activities. Barnes et al4 identified educational preparedness as an important element that impacts transition to practice. Restructuring NP education could influence the experience of novice NPs as they transition to their new role after graduation.2

The goal of NP education is to expose students to a broad range of topics that build a strong foundation of knowledge with a focus on the application of theory to clinical practice.5 6 The curriculum is designed to prepare students to become competent NPs capable of diagnosing and treating patients with a wide variety of healthcare issues through a combination of knowledge, skills and clinical performance.7 8 NP students are expected to build on their previous knowledge as a registered nurse, reflect on their learning to integrate new concepts, be critical thinkers and develop skills to retrieve and appropriately apply their knowledge to clinical practice settings.7 9 However, recent literature describes gaps in NP education that have yet to be addressed including pathophysiology, hands-on experience, skills-based internships and diversity of topics in the curriculum.1012

There are very few scales that measure the acquisition of skills, competencies, knowledge or critical thinking in NP education or in NP practice.5 Moreover, these scales are not consistently used when evaluating the core concepts included within the NP role, making it difficult to generalise findings across studies.41316 Barnes et al4 discussed the need for a validated scale to identify elements and approaches that could support the development of teaching and learning activities for novice NPs. A novice NP is defined as those who have less than 1 year of experience in an NP role.17 Role transition is defined as ‘a change in an individual’s identity within a certain context that occurs through the development of new knowledge and skills, as well as a change in behavior’.18

Barnes12 first studied the relationship of previous nursing experience and formal orientation on the outcomes of role transition for NPs. Difficulties during role transition have been identified as possible causes for intent to leave the profession, inability to implement advanced practice skills (eg, critical thinking), low self-confidence, anxiety, role confusion and poor job satisfaction, leading to poor patient care and adverse outcomes.4 17 19 Previous experience also influences role transition, including the types of clinical experiences, similarities between practice settings and graduate education and training.19 20 A wide variety of teaching and learning activities promote clinical reasoning, critical thinking, building of diagnostic reasoning and successful role transition. However, there is a gap in identifying factors affecting role transition and linking these to effective teaching and learning activities during NP’s transition to practice.

The Novice Nurse Practitioner Role Transition (NNPRT) scale4 was developed to provide insight into the complex process of role transition from expert nurse to novice NP and to guide stakeholders, educators and employers in the development of teaching and learning strategies to support novice NPs. The three domains in this scale include educational preparedness, role acquisition and job satisfaction.4 Within these domains, five factors are thought to influence the role transition experience: organisational alignment, mentorship, sense of purpose, perceived competence and self-confidence and compensation. Barnes et al4 suggest future research is needed to further understand the utilisation of the NNPRT scale so that teaching and learning strategies could be developed to support NPs entering the workforce. This study will further the knowledge of the role transition experience of novice NPs employed in Quebec, Canada, using the NNPRT scale.

Background

Critical thinking

Critical thinking has been identified in the literature as a core competency for NP practice, yet educators have lagged in integrating it into NP curriculum.1 21 Many adverse outcomes have been associated with poorly developed critical thinking such as diagnostic error, poor clinical judgement, difficulty integrating concepts, difficulty interpreting information and adverse patient outcomes.9 16 22 23 Critical thinking has also been identified as an influential component of poor role transition from nurse to NP.21 24

The nursing and medical literature suggests diagnostic reasoning, clinical decision making and clinical reasoning are all incorporated within the concept of critical thinking.1 25 Differing visions and interpretations of these concepts have led to a lack of consensus on their definitions, especially as they relate to NPs and NP practice. Clinical reasoning, defined by Smith et al1 is the synthesis of knowledge and experience in the application of critical thinking to a clinical situation. Critical thinking and clinical reasoning have been described in undergraduate nursing literature26 27 as well as in medicine,28 but these descriptions do not take into consideration the diagnostic reasoning or the advanced practice components of the NP role. Diagnostic reasoning is defined as that component of clinical reasoning that focuses on getting to the clinical diagnosis and determining relevant management of the clinical problem(s).1 Finally, clinical judgement is the observable outcome of the synthesis of critical thinking and clinical and diagnostic reasoning within a clinical practice setting.1 These terms are often used interchangeably as components of the NP clinical reasoning and critical thinking processes.

Literature review

A rapid review was completed as a first step to identify specific teaching and learning activities that promote critical thinking skills in NPs.29 A secondary aim of this review was to identify gaps in NP education related to the development of knowledge essential to critical thinking. Rapid reviews are used to provide relevant evidence in a timely and cost-effective manner. They are used to generate knowledge and identify knowledge gaps by synthesising findings and assessing the validity of research evidence.23 They are intended to respect the principles of knowledge synthesis with clear and predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria, with a risk of bias assessment and a systematic presentation of the results.

The rapid review was reported using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement30 with rapid review methodologies recommended by Tricco et al31 and the Cochrane Rapid Reviews Methods Group.32 Results suggested simulation to be an important component in NP education, necessary to evaluate skills and competencies, increase student confidence and apply knowledge to real world situations.33 34 Written activities were found to broaden thinking, identify cognitive bias and reduce diagnostic errors.35 36 Classroom activities were best used for developing self-confidence, diagnostic reasoning and problem-solving skills through interactions with classmates and professors.16 22 This is aligned with teaching and learning activities used with medical students, where classroom activities allow students to integrate knowledge into the process of clinical reasoning, differential diagnosis and diagnostic reasoning.37 Classroom activities have also been discussed as a tool to bridge the theory to practice gap in NP education by allowing students to apply their knowledge, make clinical decisions and demonstrate clinical competencies.38 All of the teaching and learning strategies identified in the review were found to be beneficial in developing certain aspects of critical thinking skills, although none were found to be superior to the others. Of the 12 studies included in the review, the majority were used to develop critical thinking skills for data collection (eg, history taking) and interpretation.1415 36 3942 Fewer studies16 22 24 35 looked to develop critical thinking in relation to determining a plan of care, making clinical decisions or in the application or dissemination of acquired knowledge. This review clearly identified that more research is needed to give educators insight into the best teaching and learning tools to be used for developing critical thinking across the NPs academic trajectory. The teaching and learning activities identified in this review align with the medical and advanced practice nursing education literature as they relate to the development of clinical reasoning, diagnostic reasoning and clinical judgement.27 34 37 43 44

In the early 2000s, critical thinking was of great interest when NP curriculum was being developed and refined. Topics such as simulations and telehealth gained in popularity and interest in developing critical thinking skills waned. However, there has recently been renewed interest in critical thinking in NP education, likely due to the complexity of patient care and the increased scope of practice for NPs.23 The literature has shown that poor role transition from nurse to NP can be linked to educational preparedness and poor development of critical thinking skills.4 21 24 As the body of literature on this subject grows, strategies to better address the challenges related to role transition will be developed, reducing diagnostic error and adverse patient outcomes.

Objectives

The overall purpose of this study is to explore factors that influence novice NP role transition in Quebec, Canada using a cross-sectional study survey design. Specific objectives are to (1) describe the educational preparedness, role acquisition and job satisfaction of novice NPs in Quebec, Canada, and (2) explore factors that influence the role transition experience of novice NPs in Quebec, Canada. A secondary objective is to detect misunderstandings, ambiguities and other difficulties respondents may encounter while completing the NNPRT questionnaire.

Conceptual model

The Practical, Robust Implementation and Sustainability Model (PRISM)45 will be used as a pre-implementation model to provide a structured approach to assess the context and identify potential barriers (eg, organisational alignment) and facilitators (eg, mentorship) to plan future intervention strategies to assist with NP role transition in Quebec, Canada.

This model was developed to provide a practical and actionable framework to guide the dissemination and pre-implementation planning process by addressing key contextual factors that could impact outcomes.46 The key elements of this framework include organisational and recipient perspectives of the intervention, the external environment, implementation and sustainability infrastructure, and the characteristics of the recipients.45 The PRISM model incorporates the perceptions and input of the recipients to align the intervention with the local context. This is particularly important to provide a better understanding of the factors that need to be addressed to implement interventions and recognise the need for customisation to maintain equity and fairness.47 48 This model was chosen for its focus on the pre-implementation phase of a project, its incorporation of recipient perspectives and contextual factors. A specific focus in this study is on the recipients, specifically the staff or the NPs working in their first NP position for less than 12 months in Quebec, Canada.

Design and methods

Study design

This descriptive study using a cross-sectional survey design will be reported using the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement and will follow the Dillman’s Tailored Design Method for web questionnaires.49 Dillman’s Tailored Design Method is a methodology for survey studies that can be applied from conception to data analysis to reduce survey error to acceptable levels.49 There are three fundamental considerations to the methodology: it is a scientific approach for conducting survey research with a focus on minimising survey error, it involves developing a procedure to encourage participants to complete the survey, and the procedures encourage the building of a social exchange to encourage responses.49 There are 32 guidelines for web-based questionnaires on which the detailed methodology of this study is developed. These pertain to every aspect of the survey design including recruitment (eg, communication with participants), survey completion (eg, question design, survey format), data collection and data analysis49 (figure 1).

Study design. AIPSQ, Association des Infirmiers et Infirmières Praticiennes Spécialisés du Quebec; OIIQ, Ordre des infirmiers et infirmières du Québec.

Setting

The survey will be web-based deployed through REDCap to all novice NPs meeting eligibility criteria, working in primary or specialty care in the province of Quebec, Canada. When participants access the link, they will be directed to the introduction page with a short overview describing the study, their rights as study participants, and the confidentiality of the responses. Dillman et al49 suggest that the welcoming screen is a key element in deciding if participants will complete the survey. It should have wide appeal, with the intent of building a social exchange to boost commitment to completing the survey. Survey completion will take approximately 5–10 min.

Participants

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria will be (1) working as an NP in the province of Quebec, (2) working in their first NP position and (3) working in the first NP position for less than 12 months.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria include (1) NPs who have been working in their position for more than 12 months, (2) graduates who have not yet started working, (3) participants who are not working in their first NP position and (4) NPs who work outside the province of Quebec.

Sampling

The method for sampling will be non-probability convenience sampling. Non-probability sampling is an easy method to collect data from participants who are the most accessible to the researcher.43 Participants will be recruited through the Association des Infirmiers et Infirmières Praticiennes Spécialisés du Quebec (AIPSQ) and the Ordre des infirmiers et infirmières du Québec (OIIQ) by means of newsletters, website posts and emails. To reach the NPs who are not part of the NP association, participants will be encouraged to refer colleagues to the study (ie, snowball sampling) by emailing the principal investigator. Social media platforms (eg, Facebook and LinkedIn) will also be used to increase visibility, advertise the study and boost recruitment. The principal investigator’s email will be included in the social media posts, and the study link will be sent to the potential participants by the principal investigator. This methodology is intended to reduce potential suspicious and fraudulent responses, including those initiated by robots. Strategies will also be used in designing the survey in REDCap to minimise suspicious and fraudulent responders.

The survey will be accessed through REDCap. Interested participants will confirm their eligibility, and when confirmed, they will access the consent and will be required to check a box indicating their accepting or declining to participate in the study. When consent is provided, they will be able to access the survey. If study participants have questions or concerns, they can also contact the principal investigator for further information or clarification. All participants will be able to download a copy of their signed consent form.

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients and the public (participants) were not involved in the design or conduct of this study. Findings will be shared with the participants, student NPs, NPs in practice and educators through dissemination and knowledge-translation activities. Future work will consider opportunities for participant involvement in study development and implementation.

Data sources and measurement

Instrument

The NNPRT scale4 is a 37-element scale that evaluates the three domains of educational preparedness, role acquisition and job satisfaction as they relate to NP role transition. The NNPRT has a 5-factor structure: (1) organisational alignment (nine items), (2) mentorship (four items), (3) sense of purpose (12 items), (4) perceived competence and self-confidence (seven items) and (5) compensation (five items). Each of the 37 items are scored on a 1–6 scale (1=very strongly disagree to 6=very strongly agree). The primary researcher of this project has received written consent by Dr Hilary Barnes to translate her scale into French, to use the scale in the context of this project and to publish the results (online supplemental appendix A, H. Barnes, personal communication, 11 July 2024).

Translation

The translation of the NNPRT scale from English to French was completed according to the guideline by Sousa and Rojjanasrirat.50 They describe a 7-step process for the translation and validation of scales and instruments. The first step was to translate the original scale into the target language by two independent bilingual translators. This was done by the principal researcher and by an NP colleague. Once the two translations were independently completed and then compared for ambiguities, discrepancies and meanings, the two translators then met to compare the versions and to achieve a consensus. This second step generated the preliminary translated version. The third step was to blind back-translate the preliminary version into the original version. It is suggested by the guideline that this be done by a translator, but the use of artificial intelligence was suggested for budgetary, ease of access as well as time restrictions. The back translation was done using DeepL with excellent accuracy. For the four statements that did not correspond when back translated, the original English version was put into the translator and the identical French version to our translated statement was obtained. The translation table is available in online supplemental appendix B. It was therefore determined that the French translation was accurate by the two independent translators. The remaining steps will involve pilot testing the translated version with a subset of participants and obtaining preliminary and full psychometric testing for the final version. Cronbach’s alpha will be calculated for reliability. Model assumptions, including linearity, homoscedasticity and normality of residuals, will be assessed using residuals vs predicted values plot, quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots and the Shapiro-Wilk test. Regression coefficients will be estimated using ordinary least squares and statistical significance will be set at a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. Model fit will be assessed using the adjusted R-squared (R2adj). Results will be presented as the regression coefficient with 95% CIs and p value.

Bias

Data integrity is at risk of bias with online, anonymous web-based surveys (Panesar & Mayo, 2023). Issues such as falsely claiming to meet inclusion criteria, multiple submissions by the same person and robot (bot) submissions are the most common threats to data integrity with web-based non-probability sampling.51 Strategies (eg, open text box answers, survey completion times) will be put in place to minimise fraudulent data.51 52 These strategies can help identify fraudulent responders for answers that do not pertain to the question or identify abnormal response times.51 Advanced settings in REDCap will also be used to mitigate the risk of bias. Offering an incentive as a raffle instead of an incentive for each completed survey may also deter fraudulent responders.51

Other sources of error in survey research include coverage error, non-response error and measurement error.46 Coverage error means that there are insufficient participants that are representative of the population of interest, while non-response error is poor response rate to the survey.52 Reminder emails are important for increasing response rate to web surveys.49 53 54 Therefore, a reminder email will be sent 1 week following the original email to allow for participants to respond.49 Further reminder emails will then be sent at 1 month and 6 weeks, based on response rates, or until the target response rate has been achieved.49 54 Participation after reception of the email tends to increase rapidly and then taper, thus the timing of the reminder emails will be important and targeted to be generated and sent at the beginning of the day for a higher response rate.49 54 The introduction and follow-up emails will be short in length and to the point to ensure that they are read.49 Measurement error is seen when the survey does not reflect the area of interest or elicits answers that may not be true.49 Using a previously validated instrument reduces measurement error by ensuring that the survey is reliable and valid.55 Other preventative measures to reduce bias include having a clearly identified population of interest, diverse recruitment strategies and having a user-friendly design.49 52

Study size

According to the OIIQ statistics on NPs,56 from March 2023 to March 2024 there were 152 graduates and 246 students enrolled in an NP programme in the province of Quebec. If current trends remain, it would be reasonable to assume that there will be approximately 150 NP graduates in 2024–2025. Sample size adequacy for survey studies using simple linear regression depends on the population size, confidence level and margin of error.57 58 The suggested sample size for an estimated population size of 150 with a confidence level of 95% and 5% margin of error was 80 participants.58 Using the Qualtrics sample size calculator, a confidence level of 95% and a 5% margin of error, the ideal sample size was determined to be 108 participants. Johanson and Brooks59 (2010) discussed sample sizes for revisions of existing scales. It was suggested that samples should be sufficiently large and representative of the population, emphasising the importance of representativity over quantity of respondents. They suggested that smaller sample sizes are acceptable, with a range from 30 to 50 participants when sampling is random. Similarly, Dillman et al49 discussed that at a high confidence level, a lower number of completed questionnaires can yield precise results in non-probability sampling as this method excludes a large population through the selection process. Their sample size table suggests a range between 71–80 participants for a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error. Wilson Van Voorhis and Morgan60 suggested a minimum number of 50 participants as a rule of thumb for studies using simple linear regression, assuming a medium to large effect size. Based on this information, it was determined that a minimum of 50 participants would be sufficient for answering the study question. Moreover, a minimum sample size of 50 would provide enough power to detect survey issues (ie, misunderstandings, ambiguities and other difficulties respondents may encounter while completing the survey).61 In a recent meta-analysis, current trends in online survey response rates were reported at 44.1%.53 Therefore, ensuring adequate reach in sampling was essential to ensure an adequate sample size for this cross-sectional survey designed study.49 53

Assuming a two-sided significance level of α=0.05 and 80% power, required sample sizes were calculated based on expected values of the coefficient of determination (R²) based on Cohen’s criteria.62 Detection of a small effect (R²=0.02) would require a sample size exceeding the available population, whereas detection of a medium effect (R²=0.13) would require approximately 55–60 participants, and a large effect (R²=0.26) would require approximately 30–35 participants. Given the maximum accessible sample of 150 participants, the study is adequately powered to detect medium to large effects but may have limited power to detect small effects.

Data collection

Data collection will begin once the study starts. Responses will be monitored to ensure reception of completed surveys, as well as identifying any errors that may be flagged by the REDCap system. Remedial action will be taken based on the error messages received (eg, character limits, browser incompatibilities).49 Confidential demographic information will be collected at the start of each survey (eg, age, sex, gender identity, time since beginning NP position, time since graduation, years of prior RN experience, type of prior RN experience, current practice setting, type of employment). Confidential information will be stored separately from survey data.

Statistical methods

The analysis will be undertaken in consultation with a biostatistician using SPSS version 29 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac, V.29.0. IBM Corp (2022)). Before analyses of the NNPRT, data will be cleaned and variables coded, including reverse coding of appropriate scale items. The total NNPRT Scale Score is calculated by summing the item scores and dividing by the number of items; a higher score indicates a more positive role transition experience. It is possible to obtain sub-scale scores using the same calculation method for scoring the entire NNPRT scale. Descriptive statistics will be used to describe and summarise the sample demographics, the total NNPRT score and each of the five factors (organisation alignment, mentorship, sense of purpose, perceived competency and self-confidence and compensation).

Simple linear univariate regression will be used to explore the relationship between perceived competency and self-confidence and the role transition experience of novice NPs in Quebec, Canada. The total NNPRT score will be the dependent variable and perceived competency and self-confidence will be the only independent NNPRT variable. This variable was chosen as it is the most aligned with critical thinking and the broader development of educational strategies. Missing data will be excluded from the regression analysis using listwise deletion. Answering this question will help inform the choice of teaching and learning activities to address the needs of novice NPs during role transition. The Cronbach alpha will be reported using the study sample from the survey. Quantitative analysis of the demographic data will be done using descriptive statistics using SPSSV.29.

Potential risks and benefits

Participation in this study is completely voluntary and poses minimal risks to participants. Confidential information will be stored separately from survey data and only linked using a unique identifier. The surveys will be distributed and submitted anonymously. If the survey elicits emotional responses, the survey can be stopped and continued at a different time. Participants will be given the opportunity to reach out to the research team if the survey solicited issues that they would like to address or discuss in person.

The benefits to the study are that it furthers the knowledge about the utilisation of the NNPRT scale, expands possible utilisations to French speaking users, and most importantly the results will describe those factors that influence novice NP role transition in Quebec, Canada. As this is a pre-implementation study, knowledge about the educational preparedness, role acquisition, job satisfaction and role transition experiences of novice NPs will help to inform future interventions that support NPs entering the workforce in Quebec Canada. For example, the Quebec Ministry of Health has expressed interest in using the translated NNPRT scale as an element in NP Role Transition Toolkit offered to employers hiring novice NPs to practice. Results could potentially have implications for other NPs practising in Canada.

Ethical considerations

Research Ethics Board

The researcher anticipates minimal risks to participants. Participation is voluntary and participants can withdraw without impact to the participant. Submission to the Research Ethics Board was completed and approved prior to the start of the study.

Data will be stored on a password protected personal computer which will be regularly backed up on a password protected external hard drive to prevent loss of data. Data will be simultaneously stored on the One Drive. Only the principal investigator and the research team will have access to the data. All data will be destroyed in accordance with the data management policy.

Dissemination

The results of this study will be published in a peer-reviewed journal. An abstract for a poster presentation has been accepted at the Canadian Association School of Nurses conference in June 2026 on the preliminary results. An abstract has been accepted for presentation of the results at the International Council of Nurses conference in September 2026. The results of this study will help inform NP curricular reforms and entry to practice orientation programmes to best meet the needs of novice NPs.
