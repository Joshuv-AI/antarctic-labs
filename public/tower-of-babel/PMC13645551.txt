Genome-wide identification of the aldo-keto reductase gene family and functional characterization of CsAKRIVB2 conferring cold tolerance in tea plant (Camellia sinensis)

Dandan Liu, Leigang Wang, Xiaoyu Jiao, Minghui Sun, Xu Ruan, Qian Wang, Wenjie Wang, Qiong Wu

Abstract

Aldo-keto reductases (AKRs) are a superfamily of NADPH-dependent oxidoreductases that play a crucial role in detoxifying reactive carbonyl species and maintaining redox homeostasis under abiotic stresses. However, systematic characterization of AKRs and their functional roles in cold stress responses in tea plants (Camellia sinensis) remain largely unexplored. We identified 48 CsAKR genes genome-wide and classified them into four groups. Structural and promoter analyses pointed to conserved protein backbones alongside diversified regulatory architectures. Expression profiling and RT-qPCR screening identified CsAKRIVB2 as a cold-inducible candidate, which contains seven ARE motifs, the highest counts in the family. The protein localized to both the nucleus and cytoplasm. CsAKRIVB2 overexpression consistently enhanced cold tolerance in E. coli, N. benthamiana, and transgenic Arabidopsis. Transgenic OE lines exhibited lower ROS and MDA levels, higher SOD/POD/CAT activities, and sustained expression of multiple cold-responsive genes, including COR15A, COR15B, RD29A, KIN1, and SnRK2.4, alongside upregulated antioxidant genes such as CAT1, APX2, FSD1, MDAR1, and GR2, under cold stress. These results demonstrate that CsAKRIVB2 functions as a positive regulator of cold tolerance and represents a promising candidate for breeding cold-tolerant tea varieties.

Introduction

Cold stress restricts the geographic distribution of plant species and reduces global crop yields (). It disrupts membrane integrity (), osmotic homeostasis (), and photosynthesis (), while also triggering excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including superoxide anion (O2-) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). These ROS damage membrane lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, leading to lipid peroxidation and cell death (; ). At low concentrations, ROS act as signaling molecules that activate stress-responsive genes, yet excess ROS cause oxidative damage under severe stress (; ). Maintaining ROS homeostasis is thus critical for plant survival under cold conditions. Plants have evolved both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems to scavenge excess ROS (; ), with superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT) serving as primary enzymes (; ). However, these defenses often fail under prolonged cold stress. Secondary toxic products, especially reactive carbonyl species (RCS) generated from lipid peroxidation, then require specialized detoxification mechanisms to preserve cellular integrity ().

The aldo-keto reductase (AKR) superfamily comprises NADPH(nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate)-dependent oxidoreductases that reduce carbonyl substrates to alcohols (). Present across prokaryotes and eukaryotes, AKRs detoxify reactive carbonyl species (RCS), such as malondialdehyde (MDA) and 4-hydroxynonenal (HNE), generated during lipid peroxidation under oxidative stress (). Together with aldehyde dehydrogenases and glutathione transferases, AKRs form a cellular detoxification network that protects plants against RCS (). Accumulating evidence indicates that AKRs contribute to abiotic stress tolerance. Genome-wide analyses have identified 30 AKR genes in Medicago truncatula (), 28 in tomato (), and 38 in sweet cherry (), with several showing responsiveness to salt, drought, and abscisic acid (ABA). In rice, the AKR gene OsKOB1 is upregulated under salt and drought stress, and mutant seeds show reduced osmotic tolerance and delayed germination (). Despite these findings, the functions of AKR genes in cold tolerance remain largely unexplored across species. This gap is particularly significant for cold-sensitive perennial crops like the tea plant.

Tea (Camellia sinensis (L.) O. Ktze) is a perennial beverage crop cultivated worldwide. As a subtropical evergreen, it is sensitive to low temperatures, and late spring frosts often damage tender shoots, reducing yield and quality (; ). Current cold-tolerance research in tea has focused mainly on the CBF/DREB pathway (; ) and downstream cold-responsive genes (; ). Much less is known about detoxification mechanisms that alleviate cold-induced oxidative damage, especially the elimination of toxic aldehydes from lipid peroxidation. A systematic genome-wide characterization of the AKR gene family in C. sinensis is needed to clarify the roles of CsAKR genes under cold stress.

In this study, we performed a genome-wide identification of AKR genes in the tea plant and characterized their phylogenetic relationships, gene structures, conserved motifs, and promoter cis-elements. Transcriptome profiling and RT-qPCR screening identified CsAKRIVB2 as a cold-responsive candidate, with its promoter enriched in ARE motifs. We assessed its function through heterologous expression in Escherichia coli, transient overexpression in Nicotiana benthamiana, and stable overexpression in transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana. Our results demonstrate that CsAKRIVB2 acts as a positive regulator of cold tolerance, providing insights into the role of AKR genes in plant stress adaptation and identifying a candidate for breeding cold-tolerant tea varieties.

Materials and methods

Genome-wide identification and physicochemical characterization of CsAKR genes

The HMM profile of the Aldo_ket_red domain (PF00248) was retrieved from the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/) () and used as a query to search against the C. sinensis ‘Shuchazao’ genome using TBtools V2.110 (), with an E-value threshold of < 1 × 10-5, a domain E-value threshold of < 1 × 10-5, and a minimum sequence length of 200 amino acids. Candidate sequences were validated using Pfam and NCBI CDD (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd). Following the criteria of , 48 non-redundant CsAKR genes were identified, renamed, and classified into four groups. Physicochemical properties were predicted using ExPASy (http://web.expasy.org/protparam/) (). Chromosomal locations were extracted from the genome annotation file (GFF3) and visualized using TBtools. The protein sequences of all identified CsAKR proteins are listed in .

Phylogenetic analysis

AKR protein sequences from Arabidopsis thaliana (22) and Oryza sativa (28) were downloaded from the Phytozome database (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov) (). A neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA7 () with the JTT model and 1,000 bootstrap replicates.

Gene structure, conserved motif, and cis-acting element analysis

Conserved motifs were identified using MEME (https://meme-suite.org/meme/), with the maximum number of motifs set to 12. Exon–intron structures were extracted from the GFF3 file and visualized using TBtools. The 2,000 bp promoter sequences upstream of the translation start site of all CsAKR genes were submitted to PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) to predict stress-responsive cis-acting regulatory elements. The raw annotations comprised 29 distinct element labels, which were consolidated into 19 major stress-related categories according to standard nomenclature. Synonymous labels from different species-specific libraries were merged into a single category (e.g., DRE (Dehydration Responsive Element), comprising DRE core and DRE1; MYC (Myelocytomatosis related element), comprising MYC and Myc; W-BOX (WRKY binding site, W box), comprising W box; and MYB (Myeloblastosis-related element), comprising MYB, Myb, MYB recognition site, MYB-like sequence, and Myb-binding site). For each gene and each element category, the motif count was obtained. To avoid double-counting, a single physical binding site annotated under multiple synonymous labels was counted only once: two records were treated as duplicates when they shared identical sequence, position, motif length, and strand. Stress-responsive cis-elements were visualized using TBtools.

Synteny and Ka/Ks analysis

Intraspecific (C. sinensis–C. sinensis) and interspecific (C. sinensis–C. oleifera, C. sinensis–C. chekiangoleosa) synteny analyses were performed using MCScanX within the TBtools suite (). Ka/Ks (nonsynonymous substitution rate/synonymous substitution rate) ratios and Ks values were calculated using the Simple Ka/Ks Calculator (NG). Divergence time was estimated as T = Ks/2λ, with λ = 6.5 × 10-9 ().

Transcriptome data analysis

Public transcriptome datasets were obtained from the Tea Plant Information Archive (TPIA) (https://tpia.teaplants.cn/) (), including tissue-specific profiles (), cold acclimation and de-acclimation profiles (), and salt and drought stress profiles (). Gene expression levels were quantified as FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads). For clustering analysis, genes with FPKM values below 10 across all samples were excluded to remove low-abundance transcripts. The remaining FPKM values were transformed using log2(FPKM + 1) and then subjected to Z-score normalization by rows. Heatmaps were generated using TBtools ().

Plant materials and stress treatments

Camellia sinensis cv. ‘Shuchazao’ cutting seedlings (two-year-old) were grown at 25 °C under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod. For cold treatment, current-year branches were placed at 4 ± 1 °C, and the second leaf was sampled at 0, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at –80 °C.

Arabidopsis thaliana (Col-0) seeds were surface-sterilized, sown on 1/2 MS medium, stratified at 4 °C for 2 days, and then transferred to a growth chamber (22 °C, 16 h light/8 h dark). Seven-day-old seedlings were transplanted into nutrient soil. For freezing treatment, four-week-old seedlings were subjected to –20 °C for 2 h, followed by 24 h recovery at 22 °C. For cold stress treatment, four-week-old seedlings were incubated at 4 °C. These two treatments were used to assess cold acclimation responses and whole-plant freezing survival, respectively. Nicotiana benthamiana plants were grown under the same conditions as the tea plants.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RNAprep Pure Plant Plus Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScriptTM RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). RT-qPCR was performed on a LightCycler® 96 system (Roche, Switzerland) using SYBR® Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (Accurate, Hunan, China). The 20-μL reaction mixture contained 10 μL of 2× SYBR Green Premix, 0.8 μL each of forward and reverse primers (10 μM), 2 μL of cDNA template, and 7.2 μL of RNase-free water. The PCR cycling program was: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Melting curve analysis was performed under the following conditions: 95 °C for 10 s, 65 °C for 60 s, and 97 °C for 1 s to verify the specificity of each primer pair. Primers used for qPCR are listed in . CsActin and AtUBQ10 served as internal reference genes for tea plant and Arabidopsis, respectively. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method (). For transgene expression analysis, WT Arabidopsis was included as a negative control; any amplification with Ct > 35 cycles was considered undetectable. For comparison among transgenic lines, relative expression levels were normalized to AtUBQ10 and then expressed relative to the OE1 line, which was set to 1.0. Each sample included three biological replicates, with three technical replicates per biological replicate.

Cloning of CsAKRIVB2 and vector construction

The coding sequence (CDS)(coding sequence) of CsAKRIVB2 (CSS0020849.1) was amplified from ‘Shuchazao’ leaf cDNA using KOD OneTM PCR Master Mix (TOYOBO, Japan). For subcellular localization, the CDS (without the stop codon) was cloned into the pCAMBIA1305-GFP vector to generate the 35S::CsAKRIVB2-GFP construct. For overexpression in A. thaliana, the CDS was cloned into pCAMBIA3301 carrying a GUS reporter gene to generate 35S::CsAKRIVB2. For prokaryotic expression, the CDS was cloned into pGEX-4T-1. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing. Primers used for vector construction are listed in .

Subcellular localization of CsAKRIVB2

The 35S::CsAKRIVB2-GFP and 35S::GFP constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 and infiltrated into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. At 48–72 h after infiltration, GFP fluorescence (green channel) and mCherry nuclear marker fluorescence (red channel) were visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope. For independent confirmation of nuclear localization, leaf tissues were infiltrated with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 10 μg/mL) using a syringe with 2–3 cycles of vacuum application and release, followed by incubation at room temperature in the dark for 5–10 min. DAPI fluorescence was visualized in the blue channel under the same confocal microscope.

Transient overexpression in N. benthamiana

The 35S::CsAKRIVB2 and 35S::00 (empty vector) constructs were transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101 and infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. At 2 days post-infiltration (dpi), plants were subjected to cold treatment at 4 °C for 4 h, while control plants were maintained under normal growth conditions. Leaves were then collected for nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) and 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining to detect superoxide anion and hydrogen peroxide accumulation, respectively.

Heterologous expression in E. coli

The pGEX-4T-1-CsAKRIVB2 and pGEX-4T-1 (empty vector) constructs were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3). Protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 17 °C for 16 h and verified by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Spot assays and growth curve analyses were performed as described previously () with minor modifications.

Generation of transgenic Arabidopsis lines

The CsAKRIVB2 coding sequence was cloned into the pCAMBIA3301 vector under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter to generate the 35S::CsAKRIVB2 construct. This vector carries the bar gene as a selectable marker conferring resistance to glufosinate ammonium for screening transgenic lines, and the gus reporter gene for histochemical GUS staining to qualitatively confirm transgene expression. The construct was verified by Sanger sequencing and then introduced into A. thaliana (Col-0) via A. tumefaciens-mediated floral dip transformation. Transgenic lines were screened on 1/2 MS medium containing 25 mg/L glufosinate ammonium. T3 homozygous lines were confirmed by GUS staining and RT-qPCR. Six T3 homozygous lines were obtained, and RT-qPCR confirmed CsAKRIVB2 transcript accumulation in all transgenic lines, whereas no specific amplification was detected in WT (wild-type) plants (Ct > 35 cycles, considered undetectable). Among the six lines, OE1 and OE9 were selected as representative lines with moderate and high transgene expression levels, respectively. For comparisons among transgenic lines, the expression level of OE1 was set to 1.0.

Physiological and biochemical assays

Relative electrolyte leakage was measured as described (). NBT and DAB staining were performed to detect O2- and H2O2, respectively. MDA, H2O2, O2-, SOD, POD, and CAT were measured using commercial kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China; ).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates. Data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-test (for pairwise comparisons) or one-way ANOVA (for multiple comparisons). Asterisks indicate statistically significant differences (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001).

Results

Genome-wide identification and chromosomal distribution of CsAKR genes

Using the PF00248 hidden Markov model profile, we identified 48 putative CsAKR genes from the Camellia sinensis genome. All candidates carried the conserved Aldo_ket_red domain (PF00248), as confirmed by Pfam and NCBI CDD. Following the nomenclature of , the 48 CsAKR genes fell into four groups (Groups I–IV), with 5, 2, 2, and 5 subfamilies, respectively. Group I was the largest, comprising 25 members in five subfamilies (IA–IE). Groups II and III each had only two members. The predicted CsAKR proteins ranged from 303 to 388 amino acids, with molecular weights of 34.10–43.75 kDa and isoelectric points (pI) of 5.01–9.38. lists detailed information for all CsAKR genes. These genes were unevenly distributed across 11 chromosomes. Chromosome 5 harbored the largest cluster, with 16 genes, whereas six genes mapped to unassembled contigs ().

Phylogenetic analysis

To investigate the phylogenetic relationships of the CsAKR family, we constructed a neighbor-joining tree using AKR protein sequences from C. sinensis (48), A. thaliana (22), and O. sativa (28). The 98 AKR proteins clustered into four distinct groups (Groups I–IV), with members from different species intermingled within each group (). Group I was the largest and contained five subfamilies (IA–IE). Most CsAKRIA members formed a cluster with several rice AKRs, suggesting that this subfamily predates the divergence of monocots and dicots. Group IV exhibited the widest distribution across the phylogenetic tree, with five subfamilies (IVA, IVB, IVD, IVE, and IVF). Within Group IV, the CsAKRIVB subfamily (CsAKRIVB1, CsAKRIVB2, and CsAKRIVB3) formed a strongly supported monophyletic clade with Arabidopsis AT2G37760.2 and AT2G37770.2, indicating a common ancestral origin. CsAKRIVD1 and CsAKRIVD2 sat extremely close on the tree, implying a recent duplication event, consistent with the segmental duplication identified in the synteny analysis (). Multiple CsAKRIVF members clustered with rice AKRs, suggesting possible orthology between the two species. Overall, the phylogenetic analysis revealed distinct subfamily divergence within the CsAKR family, reflecting a coexistence of functional conservation and diversification.

Gene structure, conserved motifs, and conserved domain analysis

To validate the phylogenetic classification, we examined the gene structures, conserved motifs, and conserved domains of CsAKR members. Twelve conserved motifs were identified, among which Motifs 1, 2, 3, 4, and 7 corresponded to the Aldo_ket_red domain (). Members within the same group shared similar motif compositions, whereas substantial variation existed between groups (). Group I members contained two to four Aldo_ket_red motifs, with subfamily IA possessing the highest number of motifs. Groups II and III, which were phylogenetically close, showed similar motif patterns. Group IV members harbored two to three Aldo_ket_red motifs. Conserved domain analysis confirmed that all CsAKR proteins carried the Aldo_ket_red domain ().

Exon–intron structures varied considerably among CsAKR genes (). Exon numbers ranged from 3 to 10. Group I members generally contained 4–10 exons, whereas Group IV members displayed more compact structures (3–9 exons). Detailed exon counts for each CsAKR gene are provided in . Collectively, the conserved motif patterns and gene structures supported the phylogenetic groupings. The consistent presence of the Aldo_ket_red domain across all members confirmed them as AKR family members.

Cis-acting regulatory element analysis

To explore the regulatory mechanisms of CsAKR genes, we analyzed cis-acting elements in the 2,000 bp promoter regions upstream of the start codon using PlantCARE. A variety of hormone-responsive, stress-responsive, and development-related elements were identified across the CsAKR promoters (; ). Among hormone-responsive elements, the ethylene-responsive element (ERE) and the abscisic acid-responsive element (ABRE) were the most prevalent, present in 42 and 35 CsAKR promoters, respectively. Methyl jasmonate-, salicylic acid-, and gibberellin-responsive elements were also detected, suggesting regulation by multiple hormone signaling pathways. For stress-responsive elements, the anaerobic responsive element (ARE) was the most widespread, found in 44 of the 48 CsAKR promoters. The CsAKRIVB2 promoter contained seven ARE motifs, the highest among all family members. The low-temperature-responsive (LTR) element, a cis-element directly associated with low-temperature responsiveness, was detected in 13 of the 48 CsAKR promoters. Notably, although CsAKRIVB2 was the most strongly cold-induced gene in our expression profiling, its promoter lacked an LTR element; by contrast, several other cold-responsive genes carried LTRs, including CsAKRIVF9 (two LTR motifs). The drought-inducible MYB-binding site (MBS) and defense/stress-responsive (TC-rich repeats) elements were also identified in subsets of CsAKR promoters. The enrichment of hormone- and stress-related cis-elements provides a transcriptional basis for the potential involvement of CsAKR genes in abiotic stress responses.

Synteny and Ka/Ks analysis of CsAKR genes

To investigate the evolutionary mechanisms underlying CsAKR gene family expansion, we performed intraspecific and interspecific synteny analyses. Five paralogous gene pairs, all representing segmental duplications, were identified within the C. sinensis genome (): CsAKRIA17/CsAKRIA19, CsAKRIVE1/CsAKRIVE2, CsAKRIVB1/CsAKRIVB2, CsAKRIVD1/CsAKRIVD2, and CsAKRIVF5/CsAKRIVF10, indicating that gene duplication contributed to family expansion. Of these, one pair (CsAKRIA17/CsAKRIA19) belongs to the IA subfamily (green line in ), whereas the remaining four pairs belong to the IV subfamily (red lines). The CsAKRIVD1/CsAKRIVD2 pair corroborates their close phylogenetic proximity (), where they formed a highly supported sister group. Interspecific synteny analysis identified 35 orthologous AKR gene pairs between C. sinensis and C. oleifera (Cs–Col) and 33 between C. sinensis and C. chekiangoleosa (Cs–Cch) (). All 68 orthologous pairs had Ka/Ks ratios below 1 (), indicating purifying selection as the predominant force maintaining AKR gene function across these species. Ks density distribution peaks occurred at 0.07 for Cs–Col and 0.09 for Cs–Cch (; ), corresponding to estimated divergence times of approximately 5.39 and 6.92 million years ago, respectively.

Expression profiles of CsAKR genes across tissues and under cold acclimation

To investigate the expression patterns of CsAKR genes, we analyzed transcriptome data across eight tissues and under cold acclimation. Genes with FPKM < 10 in all samples were excluded, resulting in 31 genes for tissue-specific analysis and 27 genes for cold acclimation analysis. The remaining genes were clustered into five distinct groups for each dataset ().

In tissue-specific expression (; ), Cluster I was predominantly expressed in mature leaves and fruits, with CsAKRIVF6 exhibiting the highest transcript abundance in mature leaves. However, some members within this cluster, including CsAKRIA5, CsAKRIA6, and CsAKRIB1, also showed high expression levels in roots. Cluster III displayed leaf-enhanced expression, with higher transcript levels in mature/old leaves but low expression in roots and flowers. Cluster IV exhibited constitutive expression across most tissues at moderate levels, including CsAKRIVA1, CsAKRIVB2, CsAKRIVF9, and CsAKRIC1. Cluster V showed flower- or fruit-preferential expression.

Under cold acclimation (; ), Cluster I was cold-repressed and its expression remained low upon de-acclimation. Cluster II showed de-acclimation-induced upregulation, including CsAKRIVB1. Cluster III displayed a cold-repressed pattern that was reversed by de-acclimation, with CsAKRIVF6 and CsAKRIVF7 markedly reduced during cold acclimation and rebounding upon de-acclimation; CsAKRIIB1 also belonged to this cluster. Cluster IV exhibited a cold-induced, de-acclimation-repressed pattern. Among them, CsAKRIVB2 showed the most pronounced induction, while CsAKRIVF9 showed only a modest increase, suggesting a weaker cold response. Cluster V showed cold-induced and sustained expression.

Under salt and drought stresses, CsAKRIVB2 also showed responsiveness (, ). Based on its cold-inducible and sustained expression pattern, promoter features, and multi-stress responsiveness, CsAKRIVB2 was selected for functional characterization.

RT-qPCR validation of candidate CsAKR genes under cold stress

To validate the transcriptome-based expression patterns, we selected 12 cold-responsive CsAKR genes for RT-qPCR analysis under cold stress (4 °C, 0–48 h). The selected genes were distributed across Clusters I, III, IV, and V and showed a response from CK(non-acclimated) to CA1(fully acclimated). The selected genes showed diverse expression trends that largely corroborated the transcriptome data (). Among them, CsAKRIVB2 displayed the most pronounced and rapid cold induction. Relative to the 0-h time point, its expression increased approximately 16-fold at 4 h, peaked at 24.8-fold at 12 h, and remained at 16.6-fold at 48 h. This rapid induction followed by sustained elevation was unique among all candidates tested. Other genes showed distinct trends: CsAKRIA3 and CsAKRIIIA1 were continuously repressed, whereas CsAKRIVF6 and CsAKRIVF7 exhibited an initial decline followed by recovery, but none matched the amplitude or persistence of CsAKRIVB2 induction.

Selection of CsAKRIVB2 for functional characterization

To prioritize candidates for functional analysis, we integrated transcriptomic profiling, RT-qPCR validation, and promoter architecture analysis. Among the cold-responsive CsAKR genes, CsAKRIVB2 exhibited several distinctive features. First, it showed the most pronounced and sustained cold induction among all family members in both transcriptome () and RT-qPCR data (), with 16-fold induction at 4 h, peaking at 24.8-fold at 12 h and remaining 16.6-fold elevated at 48 h. Second, its promoter harbors seven ARE motifs, the highest counts among all 48 CsAKR genes (; ). Third, CsAKRIVB2 belongs to the IVB subfamily, which is closely related to the Arabidopsis detoxification enzymes AKR4C8 and AKR4C9 (). Fourth, CsAKRIVB2 responded not only to cold but also to salt and drought stresses (), indicating broad-spectrum stress responsiveness. Although CsAKRIVF9 also showed cold-induced expression and its promoter contains an LTR element, its induction amplitude was lower than that of CsAKRIVB2, and its promoter contained fewer ARE motifs (two versus seven). Based on this integrated assessment, CsAKRIVB2 was selected as the most promising candidate for further functional characterization.

Subcellular localization of CsAKRIVB2

To determine the subcellular localization of CsAKRIVB2, we transiently expressed the 35S::CsAKRIVB2-GFP fusion construct and the 35S::GFP control in Nicotiana benthamiana leaf epidermal cells. Confocal microscopy showed that the free GFP control distributed uniformly throughout the nucleus and cytoplasm (). Similar to 35S::GFP, 35S::CsAKRIVB2-GFP was detected in both compartments (). Co-localization with the mCherry nuclear marker confirmed the nuclear presence of CsAKRIVB2-GFP, with overlapping GFP and mCherry signals in the merged images. DAPI staining independently confirmed the nuclear localization of CsAKRIVB2-GFP (). These results indicate that CsAKRIVB2 is a nucleocytoplasmic protein, suggesting potential roles in both the cytoplasm and the nucleus.

Heterologous expression of CsAKRIVB2 enhances cold tolerance and reduces ROS accumulation

To experimentally verify the role of CsAKRIVB2 in cold stress tolerance, we first examined its function through heterologous expression in E. coli and transient overexpression in N. benthamiana leaves. Heterologous expression of CsAKRIVB2 significantly enhanced the cold tolerance of recombinant E. coli cells, as shown by improved survival in spot assays and increased growth rates at low temperature ().

In N. benthamiana leaves transiently overexpressing CsAKRIVB2, NBT and DAB staining showed no difference between the 35S::00 empty vector control and 35S::CsAKRIVB2 under normal growth conditions. Under cold treatment (4 °C, 4 h), however, the 35S::CsAKRIVB2 leaves exhibited significantly lighter staining than the 35S::00 control (). NBT staining revealed sparse blue spots in 35S::CsAKRIVB2 leaves, in contrast to the large, intense blue patches in control leaves, indicating reduced superoxide anion (O2-) accumulation. DAB staining showed pale brown coloration in 35S::CsAKRIVB2 leaves, compared with dark brown precipitates in controls, reflecting decreased hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) levels. These results demonstrate that transient overexpression of CsAKRIVB2 alleviates cold-induced ROS accumulation in plant cells.

CsAKRIVB2 overexpression enhances cold tolerance and alleviates oxidative damage in transgenic A. thaliana

To further validate the function of CsAKRIVB2 in a stable genetic system, we generated transgenic A. thaliana lines constitutively overexpressing CsAKRIVB2. Six T3 homozygous lines were obtained, and RT-qPCR confirmed CsAKRIVB2 transcript accumulation in all transgenic lines, whereas no specific amplification was detected in WT plants (Ct > 35 cycles). Among the six lines, OE1 and OE9 were selected as representative lines with moderate and high transgene expression levels, respectively, for detailed characterization ().

Under normal growth, no phenotypic differences were observed among WT, OE1, and OE9. Following freezing treatment (–20 °C, 2 h) and 24 h recovery, however, OE1 and OE9 exhibited markedly better growth than WT (), with survival rates of 70.8% and 79.2%, respectively, compared with 41.7% for WT (; ).

Consistent with enhanced survival, OE lines showed reduced membrane damage under cold stress (4 °C, 4 h). Relative electrolyte leakage and malondialdehyde (MDA) content, an indicator of lipid peroxidation, were significantly lower in OE1 and OE9 than in WT (). DAB and NBT staining revealed less ROS accumulation in OE leaves under cold stress, with lighter staining intensity (). Quantitative measurements confirmed that H2O2 and O2- contents in OE lines were significantly reduced ().

To examine the enzymatic basis of enhanced ROS scavenging, we measured the activities of three key antioxidant enzymes. Under normal conditions, no significant differences were detected among genotypes. After cold stress, SOD, POD, and CAT activities in OE1 and OE9 were all significantly higher than in WT (). Collectively, these results demonstrate that CsAKRIVB2 overexpression enhances cold tolerance by strengthening the antioxidant enzyme system, thereby reducing ROS accumulation and alleviating oxidative membrane damage.

CsAKRIVB2 overexpression is associated with activation of the CBF-COR cold signaling pathway and upregulation of antioxidant enzyme-encoding genes

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the enhanced cold tolerance, we examined the expression of key genes in the CBF-COR (C-repeat Binding Factor/Cold-Regulated) cold signaling pathway and the antioxidant defense system by RT-qPCR.

Under cold stress (4 °C), CBF1, CBF2, and CBF3 were strongly induced at 4 h in both WT and OE lines, reaching 12.5-, 10.6-, and 12.8-fold increases in WT, respectively. By 24 h, their expression declined substantially in all genotypes, indicating that CsAKRIVB2 overexpression did not alter the pulsatile activation pattern of CBF transcription factors (). However, the downstream cold-regulated (COR) genes showed markedly enhanced and sustained activation in OE lines. KIN1, which dropped below the basal level in WT at 24 h, was upregulated to 4.7-fold and 9.1-fold of the WT level in OE1 and OE9, respectively. At 24 h, RD29A was induced 11.2-fold (OE1) and 4.9-fold (OE9) relative to WT, and COR15A was induced 1.4-fold in OE9. COR15B was also upregulated in OE1 at 24 h. SnRK2.4, encoding a kinase implicated in ABA-independent cold signaling, underwent severe cold-induced transcriptional repression in WT, with its transcript level plummeting to 3.8% of the basal level at 24 h. In OE1 and OE9, however, SnRK2.4 expression was maintained at 9.7-fold and 7.1-fold of the WT level, respectively ().

At 4 h of cold stress, multiple antioxidant enzyme-encoding genes were significantly upregulated in OE lines relative to WT (). FSD1 (encoding Fe-superoxide dismutase) was upregulated to 3.7-fold and 1.8-fold of WT levels in OE1 and OE9, respectively. CAT1 and CAT2 were upregulated to 10.9-fold and 2.5-fold of WT levels in OE1, while GR2 and MDAR1 reached 2.1-fold and 5.2-fold, respectively. APX2 showed exceptionally strong induction in OE1 at 4 h, reaching 357.5-fold of the WT level, and remained 13.6-fold higher at 24 h. These transcriptional changes aligned with the enhanced SOD, POD, and CAT enzyme activities observed in OE lines (), providing a molecular basis for the enhanced ROS-scavenging capacity.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that CsAKRIVB2 overexpression enhances cold tolerance, accompanied by reduced oxidative damage, increased antioxidant enzyme activities, and upregulated expression of multiple cold-responsive and antioxidant genes.

Discussion

We conducted the first systematic genome-wide identification of AKR genes in the tea plant, revealing 48 CsAKR members in four phylogenetic groups. Transcriptomic profiling, RT-qPCR, and functional assays identified CsAKRIVB2 as a positive regulator of cold tolerance. The enhanced cold tolerance of CsAKRIVB2-overexpressing lines was accompanied by sustained CBF-COR pathway activity and a strengthened ROS-scavenging system, although the causal hierarchy among these phenotypes is not yet resolved.

The tea plant genome contains 48 CsAKR genes. This number is larger than the 22 in Arabidopsis, 28 in rice, 30 in Medicago, 28 in tomato, and 38 in sweet cherry (; ; ). This suggests lineage-specific expansion in tea. As in other plants, Group I is the largest AKR subfamily (). In tea, Group I has 25 members. Phylogenetically, the CsAKRIVB subfamily forms a well-supported clade with Arabidopsis AKR4C8 (AT2G37760.2) and AKR4C9 (AT2G37770.2). These Arabidopsis proteins are aldehyde/ketone reductases with broad substrate specificity for cytotoxic carbonyls and are involved in stress-responsive detoxification (). Synteny analysis revealed five segmental duplication pairs. In each case, the duplicated genes lie on different chromosomes or at distal positions on the same chromosome, a typical pattern of segmental duplication (). No tandemly duplicated CsAKR pairs were found. Thus, segmental duplication, not tandem duplication, drove the expansion of this family. This is consistent with the tea genome’s history of recurrent whole-genome duplications (), which likely facilitated the retention and diversification of duplicated AKR genes. All orthologous AKR pairs between tea and C. oleifera or C. chekiangoleosa had Ka/Ks ratios below 1, indicating purifying selection. Their estimated divergence times match the evolutionary timeline of Camellia (). The CsAKRIVB1/CsAKRIVB2 pair has been retained under purifying selection, and the IVB subfamily occupies a conserved phylogenetic position. Together, these observations suggest that CsAKRIVB2 is a strong candidate for functional study.

Stress-responsive cis-elements are widely distributed across CsAKR promoters. Notably, the CsAKRIVB2 promoter lacks canonical LTR/DRE elements, indicating that its cold induction is unlikely to be mediated directly through the CBF/DREB–LTR pathway. Instead, the CsAKRIVB2 promoter contains seven ARE motifs, the most among all 48 CsAKR genes (; ).We note that PlantCARE annotates ARE as an anaerobic-responsive element (); however, ARE is also established as the antioxidant response element (core motif 5′-TGACnnnGC-3′) of the Nrf2/Keap1(nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2/Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1) pathway (), and functional antioxidant response elements have been identified in the promoter of the aldo-keto reductase AKR1B10 ().This high abundance may drive a feed-forward detoxification loop: cold-induced ROS activates ARE-binding transcription factors (e.g., TGA bZIP proteins), which are known to regulate redox-responsive genes (), thereby upregulating CsAKRIVB2. Consistent with this, CsAKRIVB2 is induced not only by cold but also by salt and drought (), suggesting that its primary regulatory input is oxidative/carbonyl stress rather than a cold-specific signal. Dissecting the functional significance of these cis-elements through promoter deletion and mutagenesis will be a focus of our future work. Recent evidence highlights the importance of RCS detoxification: accumulation of reactive carbonyls, not ROS itself, is the primary cause of growth inhibition under abiotic stress (). The lower MDA and ROS levels in OE lines () support this view. CsAKRIVB2 localizes to both the nucleus and cytoplasm, as do other plant AKRs (). This dual localization supports its two protective roles. In the cytoplasm, it detoxifies RCS from lipid peroxidation (). In the nucleus, it may scavenge genotoxic aldehydes that cross the nuclear membrane, thus protecting the transcriptional machinery from carbonyl damage (). Together, the ARE-rich promoter and the nucleocytoplasmic localization position CsAKRIVB2 at the interface of oxidative/carbonyl stress sensing, detoxification, and the protection of transcriptional machinery under cold stress. Importantly, CsAKRIVB2 is an NADPH-dependent oxidoreductase whose primary biochemical role is carbonyl detoxification. It is therefore unlikely to function as a direct transcriptional regulator, and the observed transcriptional changes in the OE lines are most parsimoniously interpreted as downstream consequences of its enzymatic activity—namely, the reduced carbonyl/oxidative stress—rather than as direct transcriptional regulatory functions of the protein itself.

An intriguing observation was the differential ROS accumulation between OE1 and OE9 under cold stress: OE9 accumulated higher H2O2 but lower O2- than OE1 (). This pattern may be explained by the kinetic relationship between O2- and H2O2 metabolism. O2- is the primary ROS species generated under stress and is rapidly converted to H2O2 by SOD (). In OE9, which exhibited higher SOD activity than OE1 (), the more efficient SOD-mediated dismutation of O2- likely accounts for the lower O2- accumulation (due to enhanced scavenging) and concurrently higher H2O2 levels (as a consequence of accelerated O2- conversion). Both OE lines maintained H2O2 levels significantly below WT, indicating that H2O2 scavenging systems are also enhanced in both lines. Thus, the differential ROS pattern likely reflects a shift in flux through the O2- -detoxifying versus H2O2 scavenging branches of the antioxidant network, rather than compromised ROS management in either line. Consistent with this interpretation, both OE1 and OE9 exhibited significantly improved physiological performance under cold stress compared with WT ().

CsAKRIVB2-overexpressing lines have lower electrolyte leakage and MDA content (), indicating that CsAKRIVB2 alleviates cold-induced membrane lipid peroxidation. MDA is not just a biomarker. It is a cytotoxic aldehyde that forms covalent adducts with proteins and nucleic acids, worsening cell damage (). Detoxifying MDA thus prevents secondary carbonyl toxicity. SOD, POD, and CAT activities increase () while ROS levels drop (). This may partly be attributable to changes in RCS-mediated regulation of these enzyme activities. Reactive aldehydes like HNE and MDA can covalently modify and inactivate antioxidant enzymes by carbonylating key amino acid residues (). For example, methylglyoxal, an RCS detoxified by AKRs, directly inhibits cytosolic ascorbate peroxidase through covalent modification (). By scavenging these aldehydes, CsAKRIVB2 may help keep these enzymes active. OE lines also show higher transcript levels of genes encoding antioxidant enzymes (). APX2 transcript increases 357.5-fold over the wild type. APX2 encodes a cytosolic ascorbate peroxidase and is a key stress-responsive isoform in Arabidopsis (; ). In tomato, SlAPX2 is positively regulated by ABA and enhances tolerance to heat and cold (), indicating that APX2 plays a conserved role in stress tolerance across species. This strong upregulation likely explains the lower H2O2 levels () and higher POD activity () in OE lines. CAT1, FSD1, MDAR1, and GR2 are also upregulated along with APX2. This suggests that CsAKRIVB2 activates antioxidant gene transcription via a redox-sensitive feedback loop, possibly through changes in H2O2 levels or the NADPH/NADP+ ratio caused by AKR activity. Thus, CsAKRIVB2 boosts antioxidant defense both by protecting enzyme activity post-translationally and by amplifying the transcription of antioxidant genes.

In CsAKRIVB2 OE lines, the transcript levels of downstream cold-responsive genes, including COR15A, COR15B, RD29A, and KIN1, were sustained at higher levels under prolonged cold stress, whereas the transient induction of CBF1–3 at 4 h was comparable between WT and OE lines (). This observation suggests that CsAKRIVB2 overexpression does not affect the initial activation of CBF transcription factors but rather helps maintain the downstream output of the CBF-COR pathway during extended cold exposure. The ICE1-CBF-COR cascade is a well-established regulator of cold acclimation (), and COR genes encode cryoprotective proteins that improve freezing tolerance (). The sustained expression of these COR genes in OE lines is consistent with the reduced electrolyte leakage and improved survival rates observed under freezing conditions (). We speculate that this effect may be attributed to the reduced carbonyl and oxidative stress in OE lines (), which could preserve the transcriptional machinery and cellular environment required for sustained COR gene expression, consistent with the nucleocytoplasmic localization of CsAKRIVB2 discussed above. However, the precise molecular link between CsAKRIVB2-mediated detoxification and COR gene expression remains to be elucidated.

Conclusion

This study provides the first genome-wide characterization of the aldo-keto reductase (AKR) gene family in Camellia sinensis. We identified 48 CsAKR genes, which fall into four phylogenetic groups. Evolutionary analyses showed that segmental duplication drove the expansion of this family, and all retained duplicates evolved under purifying selection. Among the family members, CsAKRIVB2 stood out: its promoter contains the highest number of ARE motifs, and its expression is strongly induced by cold. We tested its function through heterologous expression in E. coli, transient overexpression in N. benthamiana, and stable overexpression in transgenic A. thaliana. Overexpression of CsAKRIVB2 reduced cold-induced membrane lipid peroxidation (lower MDA content and electrolyte leakage), increased the activities of SOD, POD, and CAT, and boosted the transcript levels of antioxidant genes such as APX2, CAT1, and FSD1. In addition, under prolonged cold stress CsAKRIVB2 overexpression was associated with sustained CBF-COR cold signaling and the maintained expression of multiple cold-responsive genes, tentatively linking AKR-mediated detoxification to CBF-dependent cold signaling. CsAKRIVB2 is therefore a promising candidate for breeding cold-tolerant tea varieties, although the precise causal relationship between its detoxification activity and cold-responsive gene expression requires further validation. Future work should focus on three aspects: whether and how CsAKRIVB2 activity contributes to the sustained cold-responsive gene expression, the protein interaction network of CsAKRIVB2, and functional validation in tea plants using genetic approaches.
