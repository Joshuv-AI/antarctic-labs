Centrosome linker protein CEP68 modulates cellular stress responses via nuclear condensate formation and HSP27 interaction
Authors: Hui-Fang Hung, Peng-Peng Zhu, Craig Blackstone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCEP68 is best known for its role at the centrosome linker, but its functions beyond the centrosome remain largely unexplored. Here, we show that CEP68 also localizes to the Golgi apparatus and nucleus, where it contributes to the stress response. CEP68 associates with stress response proteins, modulating eIF2α phosphorylation and stress granule (SG) formation during oxidative stress. In the nucleus, CEP68 forms liquid-like nuclear condensates adjacent to nuclear speckles (NSs), influencing their protein dynamics. The small heat shock protein HSP27, which translocates to NSs during stress, interacts with CEP68 under normal conditions, with this interaction markedly enhanced during stress. HSP27 regulates CEP68 condensate dynamics, while CEP68 nuclear condensates displace HSP27 from NSs. Both neuropathy-associated HSP27 mutants and a disease-linked CEP68 variant alter their interaction. These findings uncover a new role for CEP68 in stress response and provide insights into HSP27’s functions in stress adaptation, neuropathy, and immune regulation.

Introduction

The centrosome is a non-membranous organelle in eukaryotic cells comprising a pair of centrioles surrounded by fibrous pericentriolar material. Initially identified as a microtubule organizing center (MTOC), the centrosome is essential for multiple biological functions at different stages of the cell cycle.1,2 As a micron-scale organelle, the centrosome has a unique structure characterized by a microtubule-based centriole core with a higher-order hierarchical assembly of pericentriolar material (PCM) around this core.2,3 Protein structure analysis of human centrosome proteins reveals that they contain a high percentage of intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) and coiled-coil domains,4 both of which are prone to forming biomolecular condensates through liquid-liquid phase separation.5

Pericentriolar scaffold proteins, including pericentrin,6 the CEP63-CEP152 complex,7,8 CEP57,9 and SPD-5 (in C. elegans)10 form liquid-liquid phase separation condensates in vitro. These condensates play a crucial role in maintaining centrosome structure and function within the cell. For example, pericentriolar scaffold proteins CEP63 and CEP152 interact through their coiled-coil domains and self-assemble into a cylindrical architecture that recruits downstream components for centriole biogenesis.7,8,9 Another multifunctional PCM scaffold protein, pericentrin, forms a lattice-like structure for anchoring various centrosome proteins and organizing microtubules. When cells enter mitosis, the pericentrin lattice structure progressively expands and reaches its maximal dimension in the G2 phase,11 which is essential for centrosome maturation. A more recent study found that the coiled-coil region of pericentrin undergoes liquid-liquid phase separation and is essential for recruiting downstream components and microtubule nucleation/organization.6 Together, these findings suggest that centrosome proteins may dynamically undergo phase separation in response to specific cellular signals in order to coordinate centrosome function.

Biomolecular condensates are involved in diverse biological processes, including signal transduction, RNA metabolism, and the regulation of gene expression.5 Interestingly, many centrosome proteins also localize to other organelles, including the nucleus, where their functions largely remain unclear. Given that centrosome proteins are highly conserved across species and predicted to form biomolecular condensates, we suspected that centrosome proteins might undergo phase separation outside the centrosome, contributing to additional regulatory functions beyond microtubule organization.

CEP68 was first identified as a centrosomal protein by proteomic analysis12 and later shown to function as a key structural component of the centrosome linker,13,14,15,16,17,18 which connects two centrioles to act as a single microtubule-organizing center during interphase. It directly interacts with rootletin (CROCC)18 and other proteins13,14,15,16,17,19 at the proximal ends of centrioles to form the proteinaceous filaments that link the two centrioles. The presence of CEP68 at centrosome linkers is also tightly controlled during the cell cycle. CEP68 is phosphorylated by PLK1 and subsequently degraded by the SCFβTrCP (Skp1-Cul1-F-box protein) ubiquitin ligase complex at prometaphase, ensuring bipolar spindle formation and proper cell division.17 After mitosis, CEP68 reassociates with the centrosome, maintaining centrosome cohesion.17,20 Indeed, loss of CEP68 at the centrosome causes premature centriole splitting16,19 and may lead to centrosome abnormalities including supernumerary centrosomes.

An antibody-based subcellular location survey of the human proteome found that CEP68 not only localizes at centrosomes but also strongly to nucleoplasm,21 where its functions remain unknown. Interestingly, polymorphisms in the human CEP68 gene are associated with inflammation-associated disorders22,23,24,25,26,27 including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID)-induced hypersensitivity reactions,22,23,24 atrial fibrillation26,27 (which could be initiated or exacerbated by chronic inflammation), and Kawasaki disease,25 a leading cause of cardiovascular disease in children that is characterized by inflammation of the blood vessels. Associated single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are found in untranslated regions (including the promoter region, introns, and 3′UTR) and a missense mutation has been identified in exon 2 (G74S).22,23

We hypothesized that the structurally highly-disordered centrosomal protein CEP68 may have additional molecular functions beyond its role at the centrosome, given its partial nuclear localization and potential involvement in non-canonical centrosome-related disorders. Here, we investigated the cellular localizations of CEP68 and analyzed its interactome to gain insight into its additional biological roles. Beyond its structural role at the centrosome linker, CEP68 contributes to stress responses through multiple mechanisms. In the cytoplasm, it associates with Golgi-localized stress granule (SG) proteins and modulates SG formation/dynamics during stress. In the nucleus, CEP68 forms liquid/gel-like condensates adjacent to nuclear speckles (NSs), altering their protein dynamics. Furthermore, CEP68 interacts with the small heat shock protein HSP27 in a stress-enhanced manner, with each affecting the other’s localization and properties. Notably, both neuropathy-associated HSP27 mutants and the hyperimmune response-related CEP68-G74S variant alter this interaction. Given HSP27’s multifaceted roles in stress and immune regulation, our findings uncover a new function of CEP68 and provide mechanistic insights into how HSP27 regulates stress adaptation, immune responses, and neural integrity.

Results

CEP68 localizes to nucleus and Golgi apparatus as well as the centrosome linker

The human CEP68 gene is located on chromosome 2 and generates multiple isoforms through alternative splicing. The two major isoforms, CEP68A (NCBI RefSeq Protein: NP_001306029.1) and CEP68B (NCBI RefSeq Protein: NP_001306030.1), share identical N and C termini and consist mainly of intrinsically disordered sequences, except for a spectrin repeat (SR) motif in the C terminus (Figures 1A and 1B).28,29 To investigate the cellular localizations and functions of CEP68, we utilized CRISPR-Cas9-mediated homologous recombination to endogenously tag CEP68 in human osteosarcoma U2OS and embryonic kidney epithelial HEK293 cells. To characterize the subcellular localization of endogenous CEP68 in cells, we performed immunofluorescence staining followed by confocal imaging. Detection of N- or C-terminal tags in CRISPR-edited CEP68 showed that CEP68 co-localized with rootletin (CROCC) at the centrosome linker (Figures 1C, 1D, and S1D), consistent with previous reports.18 In addition to its centrosomal localization, CEP68 was also detected at the Golgi apparatus, where it specifically co-localized with AKAP450 (Figure 1E), in addition to their co-localization at the centrosome (Figure S1E). AKAP450 (also known as AKAP350 or CG-NAP) is a large scaffold protein encoded by AKAP9 that localizes to both the centrosome and the Golgi apparatus. By organizing signaling complexes and microtubule-nucleation factors, AKAP450 plays a key role in the spatial regulation of microtubule organization/dynamics, Golgi organization, and centrosome integrity during cell polarization and cell-cycle progression. AKAP450 was identified as a CEP68 interactor through yeast two-hybrid assays, and depletion of AKAP450 leads to CEP68 displacement from the centrosome.30 Together, these results suggest that AKAP450 may recruit CEP68 to the Golgi apparatus.

To further examine a potential nuclear localization, nuclear/cytoplasmic fractionation followed by immunoblotting was conducted. Because centrosomes are tethered to the nuclear envelope,31 cells were treated with nocodazole and cytochalasin D to release centrosomes into the cytosol32 prior to fractionation. Surprisingly, a substantial portion of CEP68 was detected in the nuclear fraction, with a nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio of ∼1.4 (Figures 1F and 1G). To confirm this result and exclude the possibility that the immunoreactive signal originated from centrosome-associated CEP68, CRISPR-edited cells were treated with the Polo-like kinase 4 (Plk4) inhibitor centrinone-B to deplete centrosomes by blocking centrosome duplication during cell proliferation (Figures S2A and S2B).33 CEP68 was also detected in the nuclear fraction of centrosome-depleted cells, with a nuclear to cytoplasmic ratio of ∼1.3 (Figures S2C and S2D). Notably, nuclear CEP68 migrated slightly slower on SDS-PAGE than cytoplasmic CEP68 (Figure 1F), suggesting that post-translational modifications may regulate its nuclear import or retention.

CEP68 associates with stress response proteins

Using CRISPR-edited CEP68 cells, we found that CEP68 also localizes to the Golgi and nucleus, as shown by both immunofluorescence staining and subcellular fractionation. To date, the only well-characterized function of CEP68 has been its role at the centrosome linker, while its functions beyond the centrosome remain largely unexplored. To uncover novel functions of CEP68, we performed co-immunoprecipitation using a monoclonal anti-FLAG epitope antibody in CRISPR-edited U2OS cells expressing FLAG-tagged CEP68, as well as in parental U2OS cells expressing untagged CEP68 serving as controls. Immunoprecipitated proteins were subsequently analyzed by mass spectrometry to identify the CEP68 interactome. This analysis revealed 65 newly identified CEP68-interacting proteins along with five previously known interactors (Figure 1H and Table S1). Four of the known interactors are rootletin (CROCC),18 CEP215 (also known as CDK5RAP2),13 CEP250 (also known as C-NAP1),13,18 and pericentrin (PCNT),17 which are involved in centrosome linker and centrosome cohesion. Notably, 44% (31 out of 70) of CEP68-interacting proteins have the nucleus as their primary cellular localization (Figure 1I).

We performed gene enrichment analysis to explore potential biological functions of CEP68 and its interacting partners. Among the newly identified CEP68 interactors were several ribosomal proteins and transcription regulators, which are significantly enriched for RNA-binding (GO:Molecular Function analysis,34 padj = 1.219 × 10−4) and associated with eIF2 signaling (p = 1.35 × 10−7) in ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA).35 The eIF2 (eukaryotic initiation factor 2) initiation complex is essential for protein translation and also regulates stress responses by suppressing global translation while enhancing translation of stress-related transcripts.36 Interestingly, we identified AKAP450 (log2 abundance ratio = 5.37, p = 7.64 × 10−8) and its interactor Caprin137 (log2 abundance ratio = 2.67, p = 8.40 × 10−5) within the CEP68 protein interactome. AKAP450 and Caprin1 co-localize at SGs under stress conditions, and depletion of either protein affects SG formation.37,38 Thus, the CEP68 protein interactome indicates that CEP68 associates with RNA-binding proteins and SG-associated components, pointing to a potential role of CEP68 in cellular stress responses.

CEP68 modulates stress-induced eIF2α phosphorylation and stress granule formation

When cells encounter stress conditions, such as oxidative stress, heat shock, or nutrient deprivation, eIF2α becomes phosphorylated, leading to inhibition of global protein translation.36 This translational arrest promotes the formation of SGs, which are cytoplasmic condensates composed of stalled mRNAs, ribosomal components, and RNA-binding proteins. SG formation is a multi-step process; first, granule nucleating proteins (such as G3BP139) with IDRs associate with untranslated mRNAs and produce initial foci. These nucleation events are followed by the recruitment of additional SG components and subsequent fusion of the foci into larger, irregularly shaped granules ranging in size from 0.1 to 4 μm.39,40,41

To examine whether CEP68 is involved in the cellular stress response, oxidative stress was induced using sodium arsenite treatment.42 First, we monitored phosphorylation levels of eIF2α over time following sodium arsenite treatment in cell lines overexpressing either Emerald or Emerald-CEP68 and found that CEP68 overexpression significantly delays progression of eIF2α phosphorylation during stress (Figures 2A and 2B). To further investigate the role of CEP68 in eIF2α phosphorylation, CEP68 was knocked down using shRNA, and cells were treated with centrinone-B to exclude potential centrosome-related effects. Interestingly, depletion of CEP68 also slowed eIF2α phosphorylation following arsenite treatment, while basal levels of phosphorylated eIF2α were modestly elevated (Figures 2C–2E). Additionally, we examined whether CEP68 affects SG formation using immunofluorescence staining of G3BP1. Interestingly, overexpression of CEP68 altered the morphology of SGs, as indicated by a significant increase in SG circularity in cells expressing Emerald-CEP68 compared to those expressing Emerald alone (Figures 2F, 2G, and S2E). However, no significant differences were observed in the size or number of SGs between the two groups (Figures S2F and S2G).

Given that IDRs comprise 80% of the CEP68 protein sequence, we hypothesized that CEP68 directly interacts with SG components and affects SG assembly. Co-immunoprecipitation of CEP68 using CRISPR-edited CEP68 cells expressing GFP-G3BP1 revealed that CEP68 interacts with G3BP1 under both normal and stress conditions (Figure S2H). Thus, overexpression of CEP68 may alter SG dynamics and morphology through its interaction with G3BP1. While CEP68 protein levels and its cytosol-to-nucleus distribution remained unchanged following arsenite treatment compared to normal conditions, the cytoplasmic fraction of CEP68 migrates more slowly on SDS-PAGE, with an even more pronounced mobility shift observed in the nuclear fraction under stress (Figures S2C and S2D). To identify the modification responsible for this shift, we examined several major post-translational modifications. CEP68 could not be detected following immunoprecipitation of ubiquitinated or sumoylated proteins (data not shown). In contrast, phosphatase treatment resulted in a clear mobility shift of CEP68, indicating that CEP68 undergoes stress-associated phosphorylation (Figure S2I), which may affect its structure and/or its binding with other proteins.

CEP68 forms nuclear condensates under stress conditions

Since more than half of the endogenous CEP68 protein localizes to the cell nucleus (Figures 1G and S2D) and interacts with nuclear proteins (Figure 1I and Table S1), we investigated the dynamics of nuclear CEP68 under both normal and stressed conditions using immunofluorescence staining. However, possibly due to its low expression level and diffuse nuclear distribution, endogenous CEP68 could not be readily detected in the nucleus under either condition using conventional immunofluorescence microscopy. This likely reflects methodological limitations rather than true absence of nuclear CEP68. To overcome this limitation, we established stable U2OS cell lines expressing Emerald alone, Emerald-tagged CEP68 (Emerald-CEP68), or Emerald-tagged CEP68 fused with a canonical nuclear localization signal (Emerald-CEP68-NLS) for live-cell imaging and temporal tracking of CEP68 during arsenite treatment.

Under normal conditions, exogenously expressed CEP68 localizes prominently at the centrosome and is diffusely distributed in both the cytoplasm and nucleus (Figure 3A, top row). Interestingly, both wild type (Emerald-CEP68) and nuclear-localized CEP68 (Emerald-CEP68-NLS) began to form nuclear condensates within 10 min of arsenite exposure (Figure 3A, yellow arrows). The percentage of cells exhibiting CEP68 nuclear condensates increased with time (Figure 3B), from ∼5% under normal conditions to over 60% after 60 min, and more than 90% after 2 h of arsenite incubation. In addition, these condensates are dynamic and reversible, as they dissipate within 30 min of arsenite removal (Figure 3C). To ensure that condensate formation was not influenced by the large Emerald tag, we expressed FLAG-tagged CEP68 and observed similar nuclear condensates in response to arsenite treatment (Figure 3D). Using fluorescence recovery after photo bleaching (FRAP), we assessed the dynamic behavior of CEP68 within the nucleus. As expected, Emerald alone exhibited rapid and nearly complete fluorescence recovery, with a half-time (t1/2) less than 1 s under both normal and stress conditions (Figures 3E and 3F). In contrast, CEP68 displayed distinct dynamic properties depending on cellular conditions. Under non-stressed conditions, CEP68 was diffusely distributed in the nucleoplasm and exhibited rapid molecular exchange with nearly full fluorescence recovery and a half-time of 1.6 s. Upon stress, CEP68 formed nuclear condensates with liquid- or gel-like characteristics, showing ∼80% fluorescence recovery and a slower half-time of 2.6 s (Figures 3G and 3H), comparable to the dynamics of other liquid-like biomolecular condensates.5 These results suggest that nuclear CEP68 undergoes stress-induced liquid-liquid phase separation to form biomolecular condensates.

CEP68 nuclear condensates are adjacent to nuclear speckles

CEP68 nuclear condensates share features with stress-induced nuclear bodies (SNBs), which are transient and reversible nuclear sub-compartments typically formed on pericentric heterochromatic regions (e.g., Sat III repetitive sequences).43 Upon stress induction, heat-shock transcription factor HSF1,44 RNA-binding proteins (HAP/SAF-B45 and Sam6846), and splicing factors46,47 are recruited to SNBs at distinct time points, facilitating rapid shifts in gene expression through chromatin remodeling and modulation of transcription and RNA splicing.43,48 A recent study also reported that RepoMan49 forms stress-induced nuclear foci that are distinct from HSF1-associated SNBs. Importantly, CEP68 nuclear condensates represent a novel class of stress-induced nuclear foci, as they do not colocalize with either HSF1 (Figure S3A) or RepoMan foci (data not shown).

To better understand the biological relevance of CEP68 condensates, we examined their spatial relationship with known nuclear substructures. Immunofluorescence staining showed that CEP68 nuclear condensates frequently localize adjacent to SC35-labeled NSs (Figure 4A). Quantitative analysis revealed that the average distance between CEP68 condensates and the nearest NS was 0.38 μm, whereas the average distance to HSF1 stress foci was 1.98 μm (Figures 4B and 4C). This localization pattern was also observed in primary mouse neurons and human retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells expressing Emerald-tagged CEP68 constructs (Figures S3B and S3C). NSs are dynamic, membrane-less compartments enriched in RNA-splicing machinery, including non-coding RNAs, small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNPs), splicing regulators, and transcription factors.50 Since CEP68 localizes to the nucleus and forms nuclear condensates during stress, we assessed whether it affects RNA splicing by co-expressing CEP68 with an intron-containing luciferase reporter.51 No significant differences in splicing activity were observed between control and CEP68-overexpressing cells under normal or stress conditions (Figure S3D). Additionally, pulse-chase RNA labeling in cells expressing Emerald or Emerald-CEP68-NLS showed that CEP68 has no effect on global RNA synthesis under either condition (Figures S3E–S3G). During stress, cells typically suppress global transcription and translation to conserve energy and limit misfolded protein accumulation, while selectively upregulating stress response genes to promote cell survival. However, newly synthesized RNAs under stress are not enriched in CEP68 nuclear condensates (Figure S4H) using this RNA detection method. These findings indicate that CEP68 in the nucleus may not directly regulate global RNA synthesis or splicing.

HSP27 interacts with CEP68 and modulates condensate dynamics during stress

During cellular stress, the expression of HSPs is upregulated to facilitate the clearance and repair of damaged proteins. HSPs also associate with biomolecular condensates and negatively regulate their liquid-liquid phase separation. For example, HSP70 disperses HSF1-associated nuclear bodies,52 while HSP27 modulates the dynamics of FUS and TDP-43 condensates.53,54 Since CEP68 nuclear condensates dissipate following arsenite washout (Figure 3C), we tested whether HSPs interact with CEP68 and influence the dissolution of CEP68 condensates. To test this, GFP-HSP27 or GFP-HSP70 was expressed in CRISPR-edited CEP68 cells, and interactions were assessed by co-immunoprecipitation under normal and stress conditions. In unstressed cells, CEP68 interacted with both HSP27 and HSP70. Upon arsenite treatment, the CEP68-HSP27 interaction increased by ∼11-fold, whereas the CEP68-HSP70 interaction increased only ∼2-fold (Figures 4D and 4E). These results indicate that HSP27 plays a more specific role in modulating CEP68 condensate during stress. Consistently, FRAP analysis showed that HSP27 overexpression significantly reduced the immobile fraction of CEP68 condensates (Figures 4F and 4G), suggesting that HSP27 suppresses the phase transition of CEP68 into a more solid-like state.

A distinctive feature of HSP27 is its stress-induced phosphorylation and recruitment to NSs,55 where it facilitates the resumption of RNA splicing during recovery.56 Based on its NS localization and the increased interaction between HSP27 and CEP68 during stress, we examined whether CEP68 condensates affect the NS targeting of HSP27. Using immunofluorescence staining, we quantified HSP27 localization at NSs following arsenite treatment and found that HSP27 fluorescence intensity at NSs was significantly reduced in cells overexpressing CEP68-NLS compared to control cells (Figures 4F and 4I). A similar reduction in NS-localized HSP27 was also observed following heat shock (Figures S4A–S4C). In contrast, CEP68 knockdown resulted in an increased accumulation of HSP27 at NSs (Figures S4D–S4F). This effect was independently confirmed using a second antibody against HSP27 (Figures S5A and S5B). To assess whether CEP68 broadly affects NS composition, we also quantified the intensities of two core NS components, SON and SRRM2. Interestingly, we observed a reduction in SON intensity at NSs, whereas SRRM2 localization was not significantly altered (Figure S5).

To exclude the possibility that CEP68 alters the nuclear translocation of HSP27, we performed subcellular fractionation and assessed HSP27 distribution between cytosolic and nuclear compartments following arsenite treatment. CEP68 expression did not significantly alter the cytosol/nucleus ratio of HSP27 (Figures S6A an S6B). Together, these data suggest that HSP27 regulates CEP68 condensate dynamics. Conversely, CEP68 nuclear condensates may influence the nuclear function of HSP27 by directly interacting with and modulating its localization to NSs.

Neuropathy-associated HSP27 mutations exhibit differential binding affinities to CEP68

HSP27 participates in various physiological processes, including stress responses, immune responses, and cell fate determination (e.g., differentiation, proliferation and apoptosis).57 The activity of HSP27 in regulating these biological functions is strongly correlated with its phosphorylation state and the size of its oligomers. HSP27 dimerizes via its α-crystallin domain (ACD) and assembles into polydisperse, high molecular weight oligomers (typically 12–30 protomers, 500–1,100 kDa). While HSP27 is predominantly localized to cytoplasm under normal conditions, various stimuli (including oxidative stress) induce its phosphorylation at serines 15, 78, and 82, yielding dimers and smaller oligomers.57,58 Phosphorylation of these serine residues is essential for the localization of HSP27 to NSs.55

Since CEP68 condensates form adjacent to NSs and the interaction between CEP68 and HSP27 significantly increases under stress conditions, we investigated whether the phosphorylation state of HSP27 affects its binding to CEP68. The three serine residues in HSP27 were mutated to alanine (phospho-incompetent, HSP27-3A) or aspartic acid (phospho-mimic, HSP27-3D), and their association with CEP68 was examined using co-immunoprecipitation. There were no significant differences in CEP68 binding to wild-type and phospho-mutated forms of HSP27 under normal conditions. Moreover, both phospho-incompetent and phospho-mimic mutants showed decreased binding to CEP68 after arsenite treatment (Figures S6C and S6D). Thus, the phosphorylation state of HSP27 does not affect its binding to CEP68.

More than 40 mutations distributed throughout the human HSP27 sequence are associated with neurological disorders such as Charcot-Marie-Tooth 2F (CMT2F) and distal hereditary motor neuropathy II (dHMN II).59 Two early-onset HSP27 missense mutants, S135F (associated with CMT2F and dHMN II) and T180I (associated with dHMN II), exhibit opposite effects on the size of HSP27 oligomers. The S135F mutant, located within the ACD, destabilizes the dimer interface, leading to more monomers independent of HSP27 phosphorylation.60,61 In contrast, the T180I mutant in the charged C-terminal region, stabilizes large oligomers.62 Under normal conditions, both HSP27 wild type and two mutants are dispersed in the cytoplasm. Upon stress stimulation by arsenite treatment, these exogenously expressed HSP27 proteins form condensates that are distinct from G3BP1 related SGs (Figure 5A). Notably, ∼80% of T180I mutant cells formed condensates, whereas the S135F mutant HSP27 remained dispersed in the cytoplasm; ∼20% of wild-type HSP27 cells displayed condensates (Figure 5B). CEP68 variant shows increased interaction with HSP27 under stress

Human CEP68 SNPs are associated with hypersensitive immune reactions,22,23,24 while HSP27 is involved in various immune responses. SNPs in the untranslated regions may influence CEP68 protein expression levels, and we found that overexpression of CEP68 affects HSP27 NS localization during stress. Since the G74S missense conversion from a nonpolar (glycine) to polar amino acid (serine) may impact CEP68 protein structure, we tested whether this CEP68 variant shows differential binding affinity to HSP27. Exogenously expressed wild-type CEP68 and CEP68-G74S variant exhibited similar cellular localizations under normal culture conditions, and both formed nuclear condensates during stress (Figures S4 and S5). To assess the interaction between CEP68 variants and HSP27, we used an anti-FLAG antibody to co-precipitate CEP68 and HSP27. Under normal conditions, there were no significant differences in the amount of HSP27 interacting with these CEP68 variants. Interestingly, the CEP68-G74S variant interacted more robustly with HSP27 upon arsenite treatment (Figures 5E and 5F), consistent with its more pronounced effects on HSP27 NS localization (Figures S5A–S5B).

To summarize, in addition to its role at the centrosome linker, CEP68 also localizes to the nucleus as well as the Golgi apparatus, where it specifically co-localizes with the large scaffold protein AKAP450. We propose that CEP68 is transported with the AKAP450-Caprin1 complex to SGs and modulates SG formation/dynamics during stress. Furthermore, CEP68 forms nuclear condensates that exhibits enhanced interaction with HSP27, contributing to the cellular stress response.

Discussion

CEP68 has long been recognized as an essential structural component forming the proteinaceous centrosome linker that connects the two centrioles. Previous studies have focused on its role in the assembly and disassembly of centrosome linkers,13,15,16,17,18,19,20 which must be tightly regulated throughout the cell cycle. Here, we have identified a non-canonical centrosomal function of CEP68 in forming transient liquid/gel-like nuclear condensates that are localized adjacent to NSs under stress conditions. This finding is significant because CEP68 nuclear condensates are distinct from previously characterized SNBs, which are associated with heterochromatic regions and implicated in chromatin remodeling and transcriptional activation.

NSs are membrane-less granules positioned near active transcription sites and contain RNAs along with a diverse array of proteins involved in transcription, RNA splicing/processing/modification/export, and RNP assembly/export. These components enable NSs to coordinate gene expression through multiple mechanisms. NSs are irregular in shape and undergo continuous remodeling during interphase, across different cell-cycle stages, and in response to various stimuli. Dynamic molecular exchanges occur between NSs and the nucleoplasm even when splicing is inhibited, underscoring their adaptive role in modulating gene expression in response to cellular changes.50

The assembly and maintenance of membrane-less condensates are typically driven by interactions among IDR proteins and between IDR proteins and nucleic acids. The NS is a multilayered structure with a core enriched in SRRM2 and SON proteins, while RNAs are located at the periphery.63 Disruption of this organization by aberrant IDR condensates alters NS function. For instance, the Alzheimer’s disease-associated IDR protein tau localizes to NSs, and its aggregates in both the cytoplasm and nucleus recruit RNAs and NS components,64 suggesting that interactions with abnormal IDR proteins can affect the composition and dynamics of NSs. Consistently, we found that CEP68 nuclear condensates delocalize NS component SON and HSP27 from NSs (Figures 4H and S5). Furthermore, proteomic analysis revealed that CEP68 interacts with RNA-binding proteins and transcription factors (Figure 1H and Table S1). Together, these findings suggest that CEP68 may contribute to gene expression regulation by modulating NS dynamics or NS composition.

Post-translational modifications of IDR-containing proteins are increasingly recognized as key regulators of condensate dynamics, modulating their assembly, disassembly, and biophysical properties. Under stress conditions, both nuclear and cytoplasmic CEP68 exhibited molecular weight shifts on SDS-PAGE (Figure S2C), which were reversible upon phosphatase treatment (Figure S2I). Such stress-induced modifications and/or conformational changes may also contribute to the reduced recovery of CEP68 by immunoprecipitation observed under stress conditions (Figures 4D and 5C), potentially by decreasing accessibility of the FLAG epitope or affecting protein stability through increased phosphorylation. These observations indicate that CEP68 in both compartments responds to stress stimuli, potentially undergoing conformational changes and/or altered binding affinities with other proteins.

In line with our findings that CEP68 nuclear condensates modulate NS dynamics, CEP68 overexpression delays eIF2α phosphorylation and alters the morphology of cytoplasmic SGs (Figure 2). Because CEP68 associates with multiple SG components, including G3BP1 (Figure S2H), AKAP450, and Caprin1 (Figure 1H), it may affect SG dynamics through IDR-IDR interactions. Previous studies have shown that G3BP1 overexpression promotes the formation of large SGs and induces eIF2α phosphorylation even without arsenite treatment,65 suggesting that SGs may help sustain eIF2α phosphorylation during stress. Thus, the interaction between CEP68 and SGs may likewise modulate eIF2α phosphorylation. Alternatively, CEP68 may act directly on the translation machinery to modulate eIF2α phosphorylation and ribosome disassembly, as supported by our mass spectrometry data showing that CEP68 associates with ribosomal proteins. Moreover, given that local translation at the centrosome contributes to centrosome function,66,67 and that eIF2α has also been reported to regulate cellular function through RNA binding at the centrosome,68 centrosomes may represent an additional regulatory site for eIF2α phosphorylation and SG formation. CEP68 interacts with HSP27 under both normal and stress conditions, with this interaction markedly increasing during stress (Figures 4D and 4E). HSP27 is phosphorylated during stress, and co-immunoprecipitation analysis showed an increased amount of phosphorylated HSP27 bound to CEP68 (Figure 5E). Surprisingly, no difference was observed in CEP68 binding between the phospho-incompetent (HSP27-3A) and phosphomimic (HSP27-3D) mutants. Furthermore, both phosphomimic mutants showed decreased binding to CEP68 compared to wild-type HSP27 (Figures S5C and S5D). These findings suggest that the CEP68-HSP27 interaction is not solely determined by the phosphorylation state or oligomer size of HSP27. Next, we examined two HSP27 mutations associated with early onset CMT2F/dHMN II and found that the T180I mutation, despite having unchanged chaperone activity, is more prone to form cytoplasmic condensates and exhibits higher binding affinity to CEP68 compared to wild-type HSP27 or the S135F mutant (Figures 5C and 5D). Additionally, the CEP68-G74S variant, with a polar N-terminal structure that favors phase separation,22 shows stronger binding to HSP27 compared to the non-polar wild-type CEP68 (Figures 5E and 5F). These results suggest that the interaction between CEP68 and HSP27 is likely associated with their propensity to undergo phase separation. HSP27 mutations linked to CMT2F/dHMN II neuropathy are distributed across the entire gene sequence, and defective chaperone function does not appear to be the primary contributor to these disorders, as chaperone activity is not correlated with disease progression or pathogenesis. We suspect that some HSP27 mutations may have altered properties for forming condensates and/or affinity for other condensates. CEP68 could contribute to HSP27-associated CMT2F/dHMN II through its interaction with HSP27.

HSP27 is a multifunctional molecular chaperone that maintains cellular homeostasis, particularly under stress conditions. It ensures protein homeostasis by preventing protein aggregation, and it promotes cell survival through inhibition of apoptotic pathways. In addition to these protective roles, HSP27 also plays diverse roles in immune regulation, exhibiting pro- or anti-inflammatory effects depending on the context. Interestingly, CEP68 polymorphisms have been associated with immune responses, and the related CEP68-G74S variant exhibits altered binding to HSP27 compared to wild-type CEP68 (Figures 5E and 5F). Given that the interaction between CEP68 and HSP27 is specifically regulated by stress stimuli (Figures 4D–4G), and considering the known link between oxidative stress and immune responses that oxidative stress can act as a potent trigger for inflammatory pathways. CEP68 may play a pivotal role in fine-tuning multifaceted functions of HSP27, balancing cellular stress responses and immune homeostasis.

We used immunoblotting to detect endogenous CEP68 protein levels in subcellular compartments and found that the ratio of CEP68 distribution between the nucleus and cytoplasm was not significantly changed during arsenite-induced oxidative stress (Figures S2C and S2D). However, there was a significant increase in the nuclear localization of exogenously expressed CEP68-NLS after arsenite treatment (Figure S6A). We reason that NLS tagging may affect the nuclear export of CEP68, and we suspect that CEP68 is actively shuttling between cytoplasm and nucleus. Moreover, CEP68 expression affects eIF2α phosphorylation and the formation and dynamics of cytoplasmic SG formation during stress (Figure 2). Along with the findings that CEP68 condensates affects protein localization at NSs (Figures 4H, S4 and S5), we hypothesize that the formation and physical interaction of CEP68 condensates could represent a mechanism for modulating the formation of membrane-less compartments such as SGs and NSs. Given that CEP68 is conserved in vertebrates (Table S2), its widespread cellular distribution and dynamic movement could coordinate stress responses in both cytoplasm and in the nucleus.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that CEP68 contributes to stress responses by modulating protein dynamics of membrane-less granules, including SGs and NSs. Furthermore, its stress-specific interaction with HSP27 may be critical for fine-tuning HSP27’s activity, helping maintain the balance between cell survival and cell death.

Limitations of the study

While our study reveals a novel stress-induced response of CEP68 and its interaction with HSP27, several limitations remain. The interactions between CEP68 variants and disease-associated HSP27 mutants, as well as their biological significance, require further investigation. Given the multifaceted roles of HSP27, which depend on its expression levels and oligomerization states, and the fact that its function at nuclear speckles remains unclear, future studies combining in vitro-binding assays with RNA sequencing to monitor effects on RNA splicing and gene expression will be valuable for elucidating the underlying mechanisms.

In addition, we report that CEP68 localizes to the nucleus based on biochemical assays. However, due to the low abundance of CEP68 and the technical limitations of imaging nuclear proteins, we were unable to directly visualize endogenous CEP68 nuclear localization by microscopy.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cells were incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2. U2OS, HEK293, and RPE cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5A, DMEM, and DMEM/F12 medium, respectively, supplemented with 10% FBS and 100 units/mL penicillin-streptomycin. For inducing oxidative stress, cells were treated with 0.5 mM sodium arsenite (NaAsO2) at 37°C for 90 min, unless otherwise specified.

For exogenous express Emerald-CEP68 in mouse neurons. Primary hippocampal neurons were isolated from E18 wild-type mouse embryos as previously described71 and transfected by nucleofection following the manufacturer protocol (4D-Nucleofector, Lonza Bioscience). Transfected cells were then seeded onto poly-D-lysine-coated (0.2 mg/mL) coverslips and cultured for 5 days. Oxidative stress was induced by treatment with 0.5 mM sodium arsenite for 1 hr.

Method details

Plasmids and reagents

Full-length human CEP68A was generated by inserting the CEP68A specific fragment into CEP68B obtained from Transomic Technologies (gene ID 23177) using NEBuilder HiFi DNA assembly. CEP68A and CEP68B were then cloned to generate mEmerald-CEP68A/B-3xFLAG constructs. A c-myc nuclear localization sequence (NLS; PAAKRVKLD) was inserted to create mEmerald-NLS-CEP68A-3xFLAG using a site-directed mutagenesis kit. Constructs for expression of HSP27 mutants (EGFP-HSP27-3A, EGFP-HSP27-3D, EGFP-HSP27-S135F and EGFP-HSP27-T180I) were generated from EGFP-HSP27 using a site-directed metagenesis kit.

CRISPR-Cas9 mediated CEP68 gene editing

Guide RNAs targeting CEP68 gene were cloned into SpCas9 construct expressing adenoviral serotype 4 E1B55K protein to increase homologous recombination.72 For C-terminus tagging, mAID-3xFLAG-Spot-loxP-puromycin and mAID-3xFLAG-Spot-loxP-neomycin flanked by ∼200 bp homology arms were used as templates for homologous recombination. After co-transfection of SpCas9-gRNA and recombination templates with puromycin/neomycin resistant genes, CRISPR-Cas9 edited cells were selected using puromycin and geneticin. Puromycin and neomycin resistance cassettes were excised from the genome by transient expression of Cre recombinase. For N-terminal tagging, Cas9 with a targeting gRNA and a donor plasmid encoding a Halo tag flanked by homologous arms were co-transfected with ouabain marker-free co-selection system.73 Genomic DNAs were extracted from single-cell-derived colonies and screened by PCR to confirm successful genome editing.

The mAID sequence inserted at the C-terminus was designed for acute protein degradation of CEP68 using the auxin-inducible degron system.74 However, we found that auxin treatment induces HSP27 phosphorylation and was therefore not used in these experiments.

Identifying novel CEP68 interacting proteins using mass spectrometry

Parental and CRISPR-edited U2OS cells were harvested, rinsed with PBS twice and lysed in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris at pH7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Nonidet P-40, 0.5% DOC, 0.1% SDS, EDTA) supplemented with proteinase and phosphatase inhibitor. The cell lysates were rotated at 4°C for 2 hr and sonicated for 30 s. Cell debris was pelleted and removed by spinning at 5000 ×g for 5 min. Endogenous FLAG-tagged CEP68 and its interacting proteins were immunoprecipitated using an anti-FLAG antibody conjugated with agarose beads following the manufacture’s protocol. Quadruplicate samples for each group were cut out from 4-15% polyacrylamide gel, stained with Coomassie G-250, and submitted to the Protein/Peptide Sequencing Facility at NIH/NINDS for LC/MS/MS processing. In brief, protein samples were denatured, alkylated, and digested with trypsin. The system used for data acquisition is a 3000 Ultimate HPLC coupled with Fusion Lumos Mass Spectrometer. Database search and label-free quantification was performed using Proteome Discoverer 2.3 software. Protein ratios were calculated with normalization. Protein of interest were selected with a ratio of log2>2, Sum PEP score >20 and Masscot score >100. Candidates were also manually checked to make sure that at least one unique peptide was reliably matched to those proteins. GO term analysis was performed using g:Profiler,34 pathway enrichment was using QIAGEN IPA,35 and protein classifications were annotated using PANTHER.69

Subcellular fractionation and immunoblotting

Cells were counted and seeded at 5–8×104 cells/cm2 24 hr prior to the experiment. Cells were collected and divided equally for preparation of total lysates and subcellular fractions. Cell pellets were washed with ice-cold PBS and resuspended in ice-cold cytoplasmic extraction buffer (10 mM HEPES, pH 7.9; 10 mM KCl; 0.1 mM EDTA; 0.1 mM EGTA; 1 mM DTT; protease inhibitors). After incubation on ice for 15 min, Nonidet P-40 was added to a final concentration of 0.65% (v/v), and samples were vortexed briefly and centrifuged at 1,500 ×g for 5 min at 4°C to separate cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions. The supernatant (cytoplasmic fraction) was transferred to a pre-chilled tube, and the nuclear pellet was washed once with cytoplasmic extraction buffer and lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with 1% SDS, 1 mM DTT, nuclease, and protease inhibitors.75 Equal proportions of total, cytoplasmic, and nuclear fractions were loaded for western blot analysis. GAPDH and Histone H3/Lamin B were used as cytoplasmic and nuclear markers, respectively. Nuclear protein levels were normalized to Histone H3/Lamin B in the nuclear fraction relative to the total lysate, and cytoplasmic protein levels were normalized to GAPDH in the cytoplasmic fraction relative to the total lysate. Total protein levels were calculated as the average of the values normalized to Histone H3/Lamin B and GAPDH. Uncropped Western blot images are presented in Data S2 and have been deposited in Mendeley Data: https://doi.org/10.17632/s24sv7wms8.1.

Lentivirus production and CEP68 knockdown

Lentiviral shRNA constructs targeting GAPDH and CEP68 were co-transfected with packaging plasmids into 293T cells. Virus-containing culture media were collected at 72 hr post-transfection and added to CRISPR-edited U2OS cell culture at a 1:5 (v/v) 5 ratio. Cells with knockdown of the target genes were selected with puromycin treatment.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were grown on #1.5H cover glass and treated as indicated. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and stained as previously described.76 In brief, cells were permeabilized and blocked in PBS buffer with 0.1% Triton X-100 and 10% goat serum for 1 hr. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C and then with secondary antibodies for 1 hr at room temperature. Cells were mounted in Prolong Diamond after staining with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) for 30 sec. Images were acquired with a Zeiss 880 confocal microscope with a 63× objective (1.4 NA, Plan-Apochromat oil immersion) under conventional confocal or Airyscan mode. Fiji software was used for image processing and analysis.

Live cell imaging and fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) analysis

U2OS cells stably expressing Emerald, Emerald-CEP68A, Emerald-CEP68A-NLS or Emerald-CEP68A-NLS with mCherry-HSP27 were cultured on multi-well chambered coverslips and incubated in FluoroBrite imaging medium supplemented with 10% FBS, GlutaMAX, and 20 mM HEPES for 2 hr prior to imaging. For fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) experiments, 7.5 μm2 regions of interest (ROIs) within nuclear condensates formed after sodium arsenite treatment for 30–60 min, or within the nucleoplasm under normal conditions, were bleached at full laser power and then imaged continuously at ∼200 ms intervals. A region without bleaching was imaged simultaneously and used as a reference for overall photobleaching during image acquisition. These experiments were performed on a Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope equipped with a 40× objective (1.2 NA, C-Apochromat water immersion) at 37 °C. The normalized fluorescence intensity of the bleached region was calculated using Zen (blue edition) software, and the plateau level was determined by curve fitting to a one-phase association model (Y = Y0 + (Plateau − Y0) × (1 − exp(−K × x))) using GraphPad Prism.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Detailed number and method for statistical analysis are reported in figure legends and were performed in GraphPad Prism v.10.4.1.
