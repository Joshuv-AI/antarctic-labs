Epidemiology of pre-cancerous cervical lesions among women in Adama, Ethiopia
Authors: Tadesse Fikre Lema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical pre-cancer is a distinct change in the epithelial cells of the transformation zone of the cervix; the cells begin developing in an abnormal fashion in the presence of persistent or long-term high-risk oncogenic human papillomavirus infection. It is estimated that out of women infected with human papillomavirus, 10% will develop pre-cancerous changes in their cervical tissue. About 8% of the women who develop these changes will develop pre-cancer limited to the outer layers of the cervical cells, and about 1.6% will develop invasive cancer. The main purpose of the study was to determine the epidemiology of pre-cancerous cervical lesions among women in Adama, Ethiopia.MethodA cross-sectional study was carried out from April to May 2023. Interviewer administered, structured questionnaire was employed, and visual inspection with acetic acid applied for screening of the cervix. Systematic sampling technique was used to collect data from 383 participants. Data was entered into Epi Info version 7, and analysed using Statistical Package for Social Science version 26. Binary logistic regression for bivariate and multivariable analyses with adjusted odds ratios and 95% CIs were used to identify factors associated with the development of pre-cancerous cervical lesions. The level of significance of association was determined as a p-value < 0.05.ResultsThe overall prevalence of pre-cancerous cervical lesions was 12.5% (95% CI: 9.17%, 15.83%). Post-coital bleeding (AOR = 25.34: 95% CI: 6.22, 103.20), age at first sex (AOR = 3.96: 95% CI: 1.24, 12.69), lifetime sexual partner (AOR = 8.37: 95% CI: 1.00, 70.14) and HIV sero-status (AOR = 10.96: 95% CI: 2.25, 53.37) were identified as independent factors significantly associated with the development of pre-cancerous cervical lesions.ConclusionsThere was high prevalence of pre-cancerous cervical lesions among participants compared to a study finding obtained in North Ethiopia [18]. Conduct health education to the public on avoidance of identified cervical cancer risk factors. Multiple factors (post-coital bleeding, age at first sex, lifetime sexual partner and HIV sero-status) were identified as independent factors significantly associated with the development of pre-cancerous cervical lesions. Awareness creation campaigns and educational programs about the prevention of pre-cancerous cervical lesions and associated risk factors need to be implemented in the community.Keywords: Pre-cancerous, lesions, prevalence, risk factors, women, Ethiopia

Background

Cervical pre-cancer begins as abnormal cell changes on the surface of the cervix, usually in the transformation zone (TZ) (is the portion of the cervix between the new and original squamo-columnar junction (SCJ) where squamous metaplasia has occurred), where squamous and columnar cells meet, frequently because of persistent Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection [1]. These changes are called ‘pre-cancerous’ because they can develop into invasive cervical cancer. HPV is the most common viral infection of the reproductive tract and is the cause of pre-cancerous lesions to the cervix uteri that may progress to cancer [2]. Based on the clinical prognosis of their associated lesions, HPVs can be divided into three types: these are high-risk types (HR-HPVs), probably high-risk types (PHR-HPV), and low-risk types (LR-HPV). Almost all women are infected with HPV shortly after initiating sexual activity [1]. In addition to having penetrative sexual intercourse, the virus can also be transmitted by skin-to-skin contact in the genital regions close to the penis and vagina. Seven out of 10 of all cervical cancer cases reported throughout the world are caused by only two high-risk oncogenic types HPV-16 and HPV-18 [1]. Another four high-risk HPV types: 31, 33, 45 and 58 are less commonly found to be associated with cervical cancer, with particular types being more prevalent than others in certain geographical areas. Infection with other low-risk HPV types causes warts in the genital area or around the anus. The two known HPV types that cause most genital warts or condylomas are HPV6 and 11 [1]. HPV infections are asymptomatic in most women and do not develop cancer [3]. However, some women will experience persistent infection with certain high-risk HPV genotypes that go on to cause abnormalities in infected cells. Persistent HPV infection means the presence of type-specific HPV Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) on repeated clinical biological samples over a period of time, usually 6 months [2].

It is estimated that out of women infected with HPV, 10% will develop pre-cancerous changes in their cervical tissue [4]. These pre-cancerous lesions are observed most frequently between age of 30 and 40. About 8% of the women who develop these changes will develop pre-cancer limited to the outer layers of the cervical cells (carcinoma in situ [CIS]), and about 1.6% will develop invasive cancer unless the pre-cancerous lesion or CIS is detected and treated in time [4]. Progression to cervical cancer from premalignant lesions generally takes place over a period of 15 to 20 years in women with normal immune system while it takes only 5 to 10 years in those with weakened immune systems, such as those with untreated human immune deficiency virus (HIV) infection [5].

WHO states that cervical cancer is a leading cause of mortality among women. Therefore, early identification and treatment at its pre-invasive stage may benefit women and decrease the burden of morbidity and mortality resulting from cervical cancer [6]. There are no treatments for persistent HPV infections but the resulting pre-cancerous lesions can be easily removed using simple and effective outpatient procedures so they will not progress to cervical cancer [7]. The current recommended cervical cancer screening method in resource limited settings like Sub-Saharan Africa is a visual inspection of the cervix using acetic acid (VIA) [8]. Cervical cancer screening is recommended for every woman 30–49 years of age, and earlier than this age range (starting from 25 years) for HIV positive women [6].

Persistent HPV infection with the oncogenic genotypes results pre-cancerous lesions to the cervix uteri that may progress to cancer [2]. Cervical cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in 23 countries and is the leading cause of cancer death in 36 countries. The vast majority of these countries are in sub-Saharan Africa, Melanesia, South America, and South-Eastern Asia (6). The annual number of new cases of cervical cancer has been projected to increase from 570,000 to 700,000 between 2018 and 2030, with the annual number of deaths projected to increase from 311,000 to 400,000 [9]. More than 85% of those affected are young, undereducated women who live in the world’s poorest countries.

ICO/IARC Information Centre on HPV and Cancer (HPV Information Centre) World Summary Report 2023 estimates indicate that every year 604,127 women are diagnosed with cervical cancer and 341,831 women died from the disease in 2020 [10]. According to this summary report, annual estimates of new cervical cancer cases in African continent reaches 117,316 per 100,000 women per year, of these, Eastern Africa countries share the highest burden of the region 54,560 followed by Middle Africa countries which is 15,646.

The report of ICO/IARC HPV Information Centre shows that in the year 2018 about 6,294 new cervical cancer cases were diagnosed, and 4,884 cervical cancer deaths occurred annually in Ethiopia [11] which escalated to 7,445 and 5,338 respectively in the year 2020 according to Human Papillomavirus and Related Diseases in Ethiopia Summary Report 2023 [12].

One of the key aspects of prevention of cervical cancer which is a sequela of persistent infection with any of the high-risk HPVs (HR-HPVs) genotypes is the identification and treatment of the premalignant cervical lesion by cervical cancer screening [13]. It is well known that cervical cancer screening can reduce cervical cancer incidence and mortality [14]. Routine cervical cancer screening has been shown to greatly reduce both the number of cervical cancer cases and deaths from the disease.

The most important part of being screened for cervical cancer is early detection of pre-cervical cancer lesions if present and being treated in time before it worsens and turned to cancer form. It means that, if a woman is diagnosed with pre-cancerous cervical lesion, it is possible for her to prevent cancer development by getting treated early for the symptoms.

Hence, it is crucial to have a baseline prevalence estimation of pre-cancerous cervical lesions and associated risk factors which are invaluably essential to facilitate the effort in preventing and controlling the impact of cervical cancer in Ethiopia. Unfortunately, there were no much studies conducted regarding these critical issues in the current study area in particular and across the country in general. Therefore, this study was aimed to determine the epidemiology of pre-cancerous cervical lesions among women in Adama, Ethiopia. The findings of this study will provide objective evidence that could be used to develop appropriate guidelines, design policies and conduct further cervical cancer related researches based on the conclusions and recommendations drawn from this investigation.

Methods

Study design and setting

An institutional based cross-sectional study design was employed among 383 women from April to May 2023 aimed to determine the epidemiology of pre-cancerous cervical lesions among women in Adama, Ethiopia.

The study was conducted in Adama city, central Oromia region, Ethiopia which was founded in 1924. It is located at 8.54oN 39.27oE at an elevation of 1712 m, 99 km southeast of capital city Addis Ababa. The city sits between the base of an escarpment to the west, and the Great Rift Valley to the east. Based on the 2007 census conducted by the central statistical agency of Ethiopia, the city has a total population of 220, 212, of whom 108,872 were men and 111,340 women. Currently the total population size is estimated to be more than 324,000. With regard to Health facilities, the city has five private hospitals, two non-governmental reproductive health clinics, more than ninety private clinics, one public hospital (Adama hospital) and eight health centres (Geda, Adama, Boku Shenen, Hawas, Biftu, Dembela, Anole and Angatu). The study was conducted at three public health facilities in Adama city where cervical cancer screening is carried out, namely, Adama hospital, Geda and Adama health centres.

Inclusion criteria

Women aged 25 to 49 years who had resided in Adama town for at least six months and attended the selected health facilities during the study period were randomly selected.

Exclusion criteria

Confirmed cervical cancer patients, women at an advanced stage of disease who were too ill to participate, who were mentally unstable to consent for participation, and who were unable to communicate properly were excluded from the study.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using the Raosoft online calculator by considering the following assumptions: margin of error 5%, confidence level 95%, population size 96,432 (Women 25 to 49 years of age residing in Adama), response distribution 50%, and was determined to be 383. The sample size was proportionally allocated among the three studied health facilities based on their annual plan for cervical cancer screening. A systematic sampling technique was used to select participants at every Kth interval. N = 1,034 women based on the sum of a quarterly cervical cancer screening plan of the three chosen health facilities (Adama Hospital 420, Geda Health Centre 328, and Adama Health Centre 286). n = 383 (The minimum sample size calculated). An interval of K = N/n = 1,034/383 = 2.69 ≈ 3. The first woman to be included in the study was chosen randomly, by blindly picking one out of 3 pieces of papers numbered 1–3. Then every 3rd woman was taken until the samples reached 383. (Figure 1).

Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid (VIA)

VIA is one of the visual inspection methods for cervical cancer screening, which is easy to perform and can be done by health professionals of all levels. It is performed by application of acetic acid to the cervix and looking for the presence of an aceto-white reaction to detect pre-cancerous lesions of the cervix.

The visual inspection is made after applying 3–5% acetic acid to the uterine cervix. A minute later, the presence of any aceto-white change or reaction in the cells covering the cervix (epithelial cells) was considered as VIA positive. If an abnormal load of cellular proteins is present in the cervical epithelium, the acetic acid coagulates the proteins conferring an opaque and white aspect of the concerned area. VIA provides instant results, and those eligible for treatment can receive treatment of the pre-cancerous lesions using cryotherapy on the same day and in the same health facility. This “see and treat” method ensures adherence to treatment soon after diagnosis.

The primary goal of VIA screening is to identify abnormal cervical cells with severe cell changes (also called pre-cancerous lesions) caused by HPV, so they can be removed to prevent invasive cancers from developing [4].

Data collection approach and method

The study employed a questionnaire to collect data, which was adapted and modified from prior research on the subject matter [15]. Additionally, more development of the questionnaire was done using the Federal Ministry of Health Ethiopia (FMOHE) 2015 cervical cancer screening and treatment client intake form [4].

The questionnaire covered sociodemographic characteristics, reproductive history, risky sexual behaviour, and cervical cancer screening and treatment details of the study participants. The questionnaire first prepared in English; it was translated into the local languages of Amharic and Affan Oromo by native speakers with master’s degrees in those languages. It was then returned to English to ensure consistency and prevent misunderstandings. Pre-testing of the instrument was done on 5% of the sample size at Biftu Health Centre prior to the actual data collection period. The information obtained from the pre-test were not included in the actual data. During the pre-test, ambiguous or unclear words and sentences were rephrased and corrected in order to make the tool more understandable and suitable for use for both the data collectors and the respondents, and more importantly, to enhance its validity and reliability.

Data collection process

To collect the data, three female nurses with BSc degrees and one female nursing supervisor with an MSc degree were recruited. These nurses had vast experience of collecting data. To standardize the data collecting process, the researcher trained them for two days before the data gathering started. The fundamentals of conducting effective interviews and adhering to fundamental ethical standards were among the points addressed in the training. Three public health facilities in Adama city (Adama Hospital, Geda, and Adama Health Centres) where cervical cancer screening services are offered, were the sites of the data collecting activities. The researcher oversaw the procedures used to collect the data and provided support to the data collectors as needed. The actual data collection took place from April to May 2023.

Data quality control

The data’s quality was maintained prior to, during, and following the data collecting activities. Pre-data collection, adapting and modifying a structured questionnaire to suit for high-quality data collection, training of the supervisor and data collectors about the study’s objectives, methods, and ethical concerns, and pretesting the questionnaire on 19 (5%) of the sample size. During the data collection period, the data was checked for completeness and consistencies by the supervisor and the investigator through close follow up. After the data collection, the data was rechecked for its completeness and consistency by the supervisor and principal investigator.

Statistical analysis

The data were edited, verified as full, coded, and entered into a computer system using the Epi Info 7 software. The data were then exported to the statistical package for social science (SPSS) version 26 for analysis. The arithmetic mean and standard deviation were calculated. Frequency distribution was utilized to investigate the characteristics of study participants for all categorical variables, and the summary statistic was employed to describe the characteristics of study participants for all numerical variables. Binary logistic regression for bivariate and multivariable analysis with adjusted odds ratio (AOR) and 95% CI were used to see the effect of independent variables on the outcome variable. The Omnibus tests model of coefficient was checked for significant that was considered as (P < 0.05); it indicates the identified predictor in multivariable logistic regression would be better off. Variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to check multi-collinearity between independent factors. A minimum 1.085 and a maximum 5.312 of VIF were within the acceptable range of multi-collinearity. Furthermore, the final fitted model was assessed for goodness-of-fit using Hosmer and Lemeshow test. A Chi-square 7.552 and the corresponding p-value 0.478 which is a large p-value indicated a good fit. Variables with p-value of less than 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were selected as candidate for multivariable analysis. The selected variables then subjected to multivariate analysis to identify variables that have association with pre-cancerous cervical lesions after adjusting for confounding effects. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to declare significance of association.

Results

Socio demographic characteristics of the study participants

With a 100% response rate, 383 respondents aged 25 to 49 years took part in the study. The mean age was 36.7 with a standard deviation of 6.6 years. One hundred and one (26.4%) of the participants were at the age group of 30 to 34 years, followed by 87 (22.7%) at the age group of 35 to 39 years. All, 383 (100%) of the study participants were city residents of Adama. About 176 (46%) of the participants were at the Elementary/Junior (1–8) educational level. With regard to their occupation, unemployed participants comprised the largest proportion of the study participants 169 (44.1%). Slightly more than half of the participants 200 (52.2%) had a monthly income of 3000 to 5000 ETB (19.26 to 32.09 USD). (Table 1).

Prevalence of pre-cancerous cervical lesions

Study participants underwent VIA test to determine whether they have already developed pre-cancerous cervical lesions or not. The prevalence of pre-cancerous cervical lesions among women took part in the study was 48 (12.5%) (95% CI: 9.17%, 15.83%). (Figure 2).

Risk factors associated with pre-cancerous cervical lesions

To identify the important variables which were independently associated with pre-cancerous cervical lesions, binary logistic regression analysis was done, and in bivariate analysis: age group, marital status, contraceptive use, condom use, post-coital bleeding, age at first sex, lifetime sexual partner and HIV sero-status were the candidate variables selected for multivariable logistic regression analysis with a p-value of < 0.25.

In the multivariable analysis, after controlling for the effect of other confounding factors, four variables: post-coital bleeding, age at first sex, lifetime sexual partner and HIV sero-status were identified as independent factors significantly associated with pre-cancerous cervical lesion with a p-value of < 0.05.

Odds of having risk of pre-cancerous cervical lesion development among women who had history of post-coital bleeding were 25 times more likely to have risk of developing pre-cancerous cervical lesions compared to those women who had no history of post-coital bleeding (AOR = 25.34: 95% CI: 6.22, 103.20). Women who had history of early sexual debut before age 20 were almost 4 times more likely to have risk of developing pre-cancerous cervical lesion compared to those who had history of their first sexual intercourse at age 20 or latter (AOR = 3.96: 95% CI: 1.24, 12.69). Study participants who had history of more than one lifetime sexual partner were 8 times more likely to have risk of acquiring pre-cancerous cervical lesion compared to their counterparts (AOR = 8.37: 95% CI: 1.00, 70.14). HIV positive participants were almost 11 times more likely to have risk of developing pre-cancerous cervical lesion compered to their counterparts (AOR = 10.96: 95% CI: 2.25, 53.37). (Table 4).

Discussion

The study aimed to determine the epidemiology and risk factors associated with pre-cancerous cervical lesions among women in Adama, Ethiopia. Pre-cancerous cervical lesions are common, driven primarily by persistent HPV infection, with high prevalence in many regions, especially impacting younger women and those with multiple sexual partners or STIs. A number of factors play a significant role in the development of pre-cancerous cervical lesions such as: early sexual debut, having multiple sexual partners, exposure to sexually transmitted infections, smoking, immunosuppression: HIV/AIDS, Chronic corticosteroid use, etc. The type and pattern of sexual activity, especially in younger women, are major factors in determining whether a person becomes infected with HPV. The number of sexual partners that younger women have may be quite large, and each of their partners also may have had multiple partners. As a result, this pattern of sexual activity increases the risk of exposure to STIs, including HPV. Suppression of the immune system also is an important risk factor because it makes the cells lining in the lower genital tract (vulva, vagina and cervix) more easily infected by the cancer inducing types of HPV. There is substantial evidence that HIV positive women are at increased risk of developing precancerous lesions.

In the present study, prevalence of pre-cancerous cervical lesion among the total respondents took part in the study was 12.5%. This finding was almost in line with a study done in India 11.2% [16]. The finding was relatively higher than the finding obtained in Bamenda, Cameroon 3.3% [17]. It is also higher than the finding reported in North Ethiopia [18]. In contrast, the finding of this study was much lower than a study conducted in Israel with a prevalence of abnormal PAP LBC results 37% [19]. The difference might be because of the sample size difference, the study area, the time of the study done, and the screening method used.

In the present study, odds of risk of pre-cancerous cervical lesion development among studied women shows that respondents who had history of post-coital bleeding (AOR = 25.34: 95% CI: 6.22, 103.20), early sexual debut before age 20 (AOR = 3.96: 95% CI: 1.24, 12.69), history of having more than one lifetime sexual partner (AOR = 8.37: 95% CI: 1.00, 70.14) and being HIV positive (AOR = 10.96: 95% CI: 2.25, 53.37) were risk factors identified as having statistically significant association with development of pre-cancerous cervical lesion. The present study was somewhat in line with a study conducted in Kampala, Uganda that reveals that VIA positivity was associated with women who reported having more than one life-time sexual partners (AOR = 3.34, 95%CI: 1.38–8.12), and being HIV positive women (AOR = 4.55; 95%CI: 2.12–9.84) [20]. In contrast, our study was not consistent with a study done in Cameron that revealed factors associated with pre-cancerous cervical lesions were: age 1.85 [1.42–2.41; p = 0.001] and parity [OR = 1.46; 95% CI: 1.30–1.89; P = 0.004] [21]. The discrepancy might be due to difference in socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants, study area and study period differences.

Limitations of the study

This study attempted to base its generalisations on diverse scientific vantage points. However, it can be affected by the following limitations: The study was restricted to three public health institutions where cervical cancer screening service was given in the study area. Therefore, the findings may not be full reflection of other health facilities. The study was a cross-sectional study that cross-sectional study by its nature has a defect to detect casual or cause and effect relationships.

Conclusions

There was high prevalence of pre-cancerous cervical lesions among the women took part in the study. Post-coital bleeding, age at first sex, lifetime sexual partner and HIV sero-status were identified as independent factors significantly associated with the development of pre-cancerous cervical lesions. Therefore, organise and deliver awareness creation campaigns and education programs to the community about pre-cancerous cervical lesions, the risk factors, and the benefits of screening for cervical cancer stressing more emphasis on preventive measures. Organise and deliver population-based cervical cancer screening services. Link cervical cancer screening to other reproductive health services such as family planning, HIV care and treatment and STIs treatment.

The findings of this study will provide objective evidence that could be used to design appropriate strategies, develop guidelines and implement health care approaches in the clinical area to mitigate the impact of pre-cancerous cervical lesions and its sequela cervical cancer across the country. The findings of this scientific inquiry may also help shape the health policies and contribute to the country’s healthcare system. Moreover, it will also be used as a baseline to conduct further pre-cancerous cervical lesions studies based on the conclusions drawn from the investigation.
