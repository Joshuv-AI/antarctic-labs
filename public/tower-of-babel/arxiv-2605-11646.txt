Circles-foliated stationary surfaces of the Dirichlet energy
Rafael López

Abstract
. In Euclidean space we study surfaces with constant anisotropic mean curvature Λ \Lambda of the Dirichlet energy ∫ Ω ( | D ​ u | 2 + Λ ​ u ) \int_{\Omega}(|Du|^{2}+\Lambda u) . We prove the existence of non-rotational surfaces with Λ = 0 \Lambda=0 and foliated by a one-parameter family of circles contained in horizontal planes obtaining a geometric description of them. These surfaces extend the known Riemann examples of the theory of minimal surfaces to the anisotropic context of the Dirichlet energy. More general, we classify all surfaces with zero anisotropic mean curvature foliated by circles proving that either the surface is axially symmetric about the z z -axis or the surface belongs to one of the above examples. We also study the case that the anisotropic mean curvature is a non-zero constant.

1.
Introduction and statement of the results

Let
\Sigma
be a surface in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
given as the graph of a smooth function
u=u(x,y)
defined in a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
(x,y,z)
stand for the canonical coordinates of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. If
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, consider the energy functional

E[u]=\int_{\Omega}|Du|^{2}+\Lambda\int_{\Omega}u.

The first term of
E
is the
Dirichlet energy
of
u
. The integral
\int_{\Omega}u
represents the volume enclosed by
\Sigma
with
\Omega\times\{0\}
. Viewing
\Lambda
as a Lagrange multiplier, a critical point
\Sigma
of
E
is a critical point of the Dirichlet energy for variations that preserve the volume of
\Sigma
. Using calculus of variations,
u=u(x,y)
is a critical point of
E

if and only if
u_{xx}+u_{yy}=\Lambda/2
. Thus if
\Lambda=0
, critical points are locally graphs of harmonic functions on domains of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We can write
E[u]
as an integral on
\Sigma
by observing that the unit normal
\nu=(\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\nu_{3})
of
\Sigma
is
\nu=(-Du,1)/\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}
. Then the Dirichlet energy is a functional depending on
\nu_{3}
, namely,

(1)

\mathcal{F}(\Sigma)=\int_{\Sigma}\left(\dfrac{1}{\nu_{3}}-\nu_{3}\right)\,d\Sigma,

where
d\Sigma
is the area element of
\Sigma
. More generally, if
X\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an immersion of an oriented surface, one can consider energy functionals of type
\mathcal{F}(X)=\int_{\Sigma}F(\nu)\,d\Sigma
, where
F\colon U\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a positive smooth function on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. These functionals appear in some fluid phenomenons in physics and are called of
anisotropic
type because the surface tensions of interfaces
\Sigma
depend on the normal direction
\nu
of
\Sigma

[
24
]
.

The function
F
given in (
1
) satisfies an elliptic condition in the sense that the matrix
A:=D^{2}F+F\,\mbox{Id}
is positive definite, where
D^{2}F
is the Hessian of
F
. Ellipticity of
F
implies that the map
\xi\colon\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
defined by
\xi(\nu)=DF(\nu)+F(\nu)\nu
, where
DF
is the gradient of
F
in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, defines a convex surface called the
Wulff shape
. For general elliptic energies
\mathcal{F}
, if the domain of
F
is
U=\mathbb{S}^{2}
, then the Wulff shape is an ovaloid. The Wulff shape is the unique global minimizer to
\mathcal{F}
when a volume constraint is fixed. For the Dirichlet energy (
1
), and since
\nu_{3}\not=0
, the function
F
is defined only in a hemisphere of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
being the Wulff shape the paraboloid of equation
z=x^{2}+y^{2}
, after translations and rescalings in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. It was in
[
21
]
where critical points of energies
\mathcal{F}(X)=\int_{\Sigma}F(\nu)\,d\Sigma
were studied from the differential-geometric view point. The Dirichlet energy (
1
) appears in
[
21
, p. 709]
.

Critical points of a functional
\mathcal{F}
for compactly supported volume-preserving variations are characterized by the property that the function

\mbox{trace}_{\Sigma}Ad\nu=-2HF+\mbox{div}_{\Sigma}DF

is constant, where
H
is the mean curvature of
X
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an anisotropic surface energy. The
anisotropic mean curvature
of a surface
\Sigma
immersed in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is defined by

(2)

\Lambda=2HF-\mbox{div}_{\Sigma}DF.

A
stationary surface
, or critical point, of the functional
\mathcal{F}
is a surface with constant anisotropic mean curvature (CAMC). This extends the notion of the surfaces of constant mean curvature (CMC) because when
F\equiv 1
, the functional
\mathcal{F}
is simply the area of the surface and
\Lambda=2H
. A CAMC surface with
\Lambda=0
is called
anisotropic minimal surface
. By its implications in physics, the stability of CAMC surfaces has received special attention. We refer to the reader a series of works by Koiso and Palmer
[
10
,
11
,
13
]
and references therein. See also
[
1
,
4
,
5
,
8
,
23
]
.

In this paper we ask for the existence of stationary surfaces of the Dirichlet energy (
1
) constructed by a foliation of spatial circles of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The motivation of this question comes from the theory of CMC surfaces. In general, a surface
\Sigma
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is said to be a
cyclic surface
if
\Sigma
is foliated by a smooth one-parameter family of circles, where the circles need not have the same radius. Each one of the planes containing the circles are called planes of the foliation. A cyclic surface can be also viewed as a surface constructed by the movement in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
of a circle whose radius and center go changing along the movement. First examples of cyclic surfaces are the surfaces of revolution. In this case, the circles are situated in parallel planes and all them are coaxial, that is, the curve of centers is a straight-line orthogonal to each plane of the foliation.

The classification of cyclic surfaces with constant mean curvature
H
is known. If the surface is rotational, then it is a Delaunay surface: planes and catenoid (
H=0
) and cylinders, spheres, unduloids and nodoids (
H\not=0
). Besides the surfaces of revolution, the only cyclic CMC surfaces are a family of minimal surfaces (
H=0
) discovered by Riemann
[
22
]
and the round sphere
(H\not=0)
:
[
2
,
3
,
19
]
. Notice that any smooth one-parameter family of planes intersecting a sphere makes a foliation of the sphere by circles. The Riemann minimal examples play a remarkable role in the theory of minimal surfaces. These surfaces are embedded with an infinite number of planar ends. They alsoare periodic along a discrete group of translations. Generalizations of this classification to arbitrary dimensions and other ambients spaces have been obtained. Without aiming to give a complete list, we refer
[
6
,
7
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
20
]
.

The objective in this paper is the classification of all cyclic CAMC surfaces of the Dirichlet energy (
1
). Specially, our interest are the anisotropic minimal surfaces according to the Riemann minimal examples in the isotropic case.

The main result in this paper is to prove the existence of a family of non-rotational anistropic minimal surface foliated by circles contained in parallel planes. In fact, these planes must be parallel to the
xy
-plane. However, if the anisotropic mean curvature is a non-zero constant, we prove that the surface must be rotational.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Sigma
be an CAMC surface of the Dirichlet energy (
1
). Assume that
\Sigma
is foliated by circles contained in planes parallel to the
xy
-plane.

(1)

If
\Lambda\not=0
, then
\Sigma
is a surface of revolution about an axis parallel to the
z
-axis.

(2)

If
\Lambda=0
, then
\Sigma
is a surface of revolution about an axis parallel to the
z
-axis (a horizontal plane
z=c
or
z=c\log(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}})
,
c\not=0
) or the surface is parametrized by

X(s,\theta)=(a(s),b(s),s)+r(s)(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0),

where we have three types of families of surfaces. After translations of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, they are the following:

(a)

Type I.

(3)

r(s)=\frac{c}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\mu^{2}}\cos(cs)},\quad a(s)=\frac{c\lambda\tan(cs)}{\lambda^{2}+\mu^{2}},\quad b(s)=\frac{c\mu\tan(cs)}{\lambda^{2}+\mu^{2}}.

(b)

Type II.

(4)

r(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\mu^{2}}s+c},\quad a(s)=-\frac{\lambda}{(\lambda^{2}+\mu^{2})s+c\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\mu^{2}}},\quad b(s)=-\frac{\mu}{(\lambda^{2}+\mu^{2})s+c\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\mu^{2}}}.

(c)

Type III.

(5)

r(s)=\frac{c}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\mu^{2}}\sinh(cs)},\quad a(s)=-\frac{c\lambda\coth(cs)}{\lambda^{2}+\mu^{2}},\quad b(s)=-\frac{c\mu\coth(cs)}{\lambda^{2}+\mu^{2}}.

For all these solutions,
c,\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
with the condition
c,\lambda^{2}+\mu^{2}\not=0
. The domain
I\subset\mathbb{R}
of
s
is given to make sense to the functions
r
,
a
and
b
. In the three cases, all surfaces are embedded and the curve of centers is included in a vertical plane, which it is a plane of symmetry of the surface.

Following with the particular case
\Lambda=0
, the following result proves that for a cyclic anisotropic minimal surface, the planes of the foliation are all parallel to the
xy
-plane.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Sigma
be an anisotropic minimal surface of the Dirichlet energy (
1
). If
\Sigma
is a cyclic surface, then the planes of the foliation are all parallel to the
xy
-plane.

As an immediate consequence of Thms.
1.2
and
1.3
, we have the complete classification of the cyclic anisotropic minimal surfaces of the Dirichlet energy.

Corollary 1.4
.

If
\Sigma
be a cyclic anisotropic minimal surface of the Dirichlet energy (
1
), then
\Sigma
is a surface of revolution about an axis parallel to the
z
-axis or it is a member of the family of surfaces that appear in (2) of Thm.
1.2
.

When the anisotropic mean curvature
\Lambda
is a non-zero constant, we also obtain results of classification. However, as we will see along the development of the proofs, we will need to calculate the anisotropic mean curvature
\Lambda
for given parametrizations of the surfaces. This involves long and tedious computations. We have used a symbolic software as Mathematica to handle these cumbersome expressions
[
25
]
. In particular, when
\Lambda\not=0
, all these computations are more difficult than in the case
\Lambda=0
. We summarize the results when
\Lambda\not=0
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\Sigma
be a cyclic CAMC surface of the Dirichlet energy (
1
). If
\Lambda\not=0
, then the planes of the foliation must be parallel.
In addition, if these planes are parallel to the
xy
-plane, then
\Sigma
is a surface of revolution.

Notice that the second part of this theorem coincides with the statement (1) of Thm.
1.2
. For a full classification when
\Lambda\not=0
, it would only remain to prove that when the planes of the foliation are parallel, then they are parallel to the
xy
-plane.

We point out that CAMC surfaces of revolution have been also studied under the natural hypothesis that the surface energy
\mathcal{F}
is axially symmetric, that is, it is invariant by rotations about an axis
L
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}

[

,

,

. If

is the

\mathcal{F}(X)=\int_{\Sigma}F(\nu_{3})\,d\Sigma.

The Dirichlet energy (

) is thus axially symmetric with respect to the

-axis. However, notice that in principle there is not an
a priori
relation between the axis of
\mathcal{F}
and the rotation axis of a CAMC surface of revolution. For the Dirichlet energy, we prove that both axes must be parallel.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\Sigma
be a CAMC surface of the Dirichlet energy (
1
). If
\Sigma
is a surface of revolution, then its rotation axis is parallel to the
z
-axis.

The proof of Thm.
1.2
will be done in Sect.

)-(

is proved in Sect.

It would be desirable to extend all these results to other anisotropic surface energies. For example, Reilly also considered in

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we review some technicalities about the computation of the anisotropic mean curvature of a surface focusing in the Dirichlet energy. Let
\langle,\rangle
denote both the Euclidean metric of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the induced one on a surface
\Sigma
immersed in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Let
X\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an immersion of an oriented surface
\Sigma
. For an axially symmetric functional (
6
), the anisotropic mean curvature
\Lambda
is given by

(7)

\Lambda=\frac{h(v_{1},v_{1})}{\mu_{1}}+\frac{h(v_{2},v_{2})}{\mu_{2}},

where
h
is the second fundamental form of
\Sigma
with respect to an orthonormal frame
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
of
\Sigma
and
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are the principal curvatures of the Wulff shape
[
9
]
. The principal directions
\{E_{1},E_{2}\}
are

E_{1}=e_{3}-\nu_{3}\nu,\quad E_{2}=\nu\times E_{1},

where
e_{3}=(0,0,1)
and

\frac{1}{\mu_{1}}=(1-\nu_{3}^{2})F^{\prime\prime}(\nu_{3})+\frac{1}{\mu_{2}},\quad\frac{1}{\mu_{2}}=F-\nu_{3}F^{\prime}(\nu_{3}).

For the Dirichlet energy (
1
), the principal curvatures
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are

\frac{1}{\mu_{1}}=\frac{2}{\nu_{3}^{3}},\quad\frac{1}{\mu_{2}}=\frac{2}{\nu_{3}}.

Here it is understood that
\nu_{3}^{2}\not=1
in order to have
E_{1}\not=0
. If
\nu_{3}^{2}\equiv 1
, then
\Sigma
is a horizontal plane. In general, any plane is a stationary surface for
\Lambda=0
because the second fundamental form
h
is
0
identically.

Notice that
\{E_{1},E_{2}\}
is an orthogonal basis but no unitary. Thus the anisotropic mean curvature
\Lambda
in (
7
) writes as

\Lambda\nu_{3}^{3}=\frac{2h(E_{1},E_{1})}{|E_{1}|^{2}}+\frac{2h(E_{2},E_{2})\nu_{3}^{2}}{|E_{2}|^{2}}.

Since
|E_{1}|^{2}=|E_{2}|^{2}=1-\nu_{3}^{2}
, this equation is equivalent to

(8)

\Lambda\nu_{3}^{3}(1-\nu_{3}^{2})=2(h(E_{1},E_{1})+h(E_{2},E_{2})\nu_{3}^{2}).

For the computation of
h(E_{i},E_{i})
we use a parametrization
X=X(s,\theta)
of
\Sigma
. Notice that the tangent plane is spanned by
\{X_{s},X_{\theta}\}
. Here the subindices indicate the corresponding derivatives with respect to
s
and
\theta
, respectively. Let write
\{E_{1},E_{2}\}
in coordinates with respect to
\{X_{s},X_{\theta}\}
,

(9)

\begin{split}E_{1}&=c_{11}X_{s}+c_{12}X_{\theta},\\
E_{2}&=c_{21}X_{s}+c_{22}X_{\theta}.\end{split}

Then

h(E_{1},E_{1})=c_{11}^{2}h(X_{s},X_{s})+2c_{11}c_{12}h(X_{s},X_{\theta})+c_{12}^{2}h(X_{\theta},X_{\theta}),

h(E_{2},E_{2})=c_{21}^{2}h(X_{s},X_{s})+2c_{21}c_{22}h(X_{s},X_{\theta})+c_{22}^{2}h(X_{\theta},X_{\theta}).

Let
g_{ij}
be the coefficients of the first fundamental form of
X
and
\nu=X_{s}\times X_{\theta}/|X_{s}\times X_{\theta}|
. Then

\begin{split}h(X_{s},X_{s})&=\langle\nu,X_{ss}\rangle=\frac{\langle X_{s}\times X_{\theta},X_{ss}\rangle}{\sqrt{\mbox{det}(g_{ij})}}:=\frac{h_{ss}}{\sqrt{\mbox{det}(g_{ij})}},\\
h(X_{s},X_{\theta})&=\langle\nu,X_{s\theta}\rangle=\frac{\langle X_{s}\times X_{\theta},X_{s\theta}\rangle}{\sqrt{\mbox{det}(g_{ij})}}:=\frac{h_{s\theta}}{\sqrt{\mbox{det}(g_{ij})}},\\
h(X_{\theta},X_{\theta})&=\langle\nu,X_{\theta\theta}\rangle=\frac{\langle X_{s}\times X_{\theta},X_{\theta\theta}\rangle}{\sqrt{\mbox{det}(g_{ij})}}:=\frac{h_{\theta\theta}}{\sqrt{\mbox{det}(g_{ij})}}.\end{split}

Then Eq. (
8
) writes as

(10)

\Lambda\nu_{3}^{3}(1-\nu_{3}^{2})\sqrt{\mbox{det}(g_{ij})}=2(c_{11}^{2}hss+2c_{11}\,c_{12}hs\theta+c_{12}^{2}h\theta\theta)+2\nu_{3}^{2}(c_{21}^{2}hss+2c_{21}\,c_{22}hs\theta+c_{22}^{2}h\theta\theta).

We now particularize the case that the surface is a surface of revolution about the
z
-axis. Since
\nu_{3}\not=0
, the surface is locally a graph on the
xy
-plane at every point. Thus the generating curve writes as
\gamma(r)=(r,0,u(r))
,
r\in I\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}
. The parametrization of the surface is

X(r,\theta)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,u(r))
,
r\in I
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
. Now we can do a change of variables in the Euler-Lagrange equation
u_{xx}+u_{yy}=\Lambda/2
, or computing directly (
10
). In this case, if
W=1+u^{\prime 2}
, we have

\mbox{det}(g_{ij})=r\sqrt{W},\ \nu_{3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{W}},\ h(E_{1},E_{1})=\frac{ru^{\prime}u^{\prime\prime}}{W^{2}},\ h(E_{2},E_{2})=\frac{u^{\prime 3}}{W}.

Then (
10
) is

u^{\prime\prime}+\frac{u^{\prime}}{r}=\frac{\Lambda}{2}.

The solution of this equation is

(11)

u(r)=c_{1}\log(r)+\frac{\Lambda}{8}r^{2}+c_{2},\quad c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

Consequently, if
\Lambda=0
,
\Sigma
is a horizontal plane (
c_{1}=0
) or
z=c_{1}\log(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}})
. The Wulff shape appears by taking
c_{1}=0
and
\Lambda>0
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Suppose that
\Sigma
is a surface foliated by circles contained in planes parallel to the
xy
-plane. If all these planes coincide, then
\Sigma
is a horizontal plane which is trivially an anisotropic minimal surface. From now, we discard this situation. Since the planes of the foliation are parametrized by
z=s
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we can assume that the curve formed by all centers of these circles is
s\mapsto(a(s),b(s),s)
,
s\in I\subset\mathbb{R}
and
a,b\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
are smooth functions. If
r(s)>0
is the radius of each circle contained in the plane
z=s
, then a parametrization of
\Sigma
is

(12)

X(s,\theta)=(a(s),b(s),s)+r(s)(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0),

where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
. Notice that
\Sigma
is a surface of revolution about an axis parallel to the
z
-axis if and only if both functions
a=a(s)
and
b=b(s)
are constant. This in turn is equivalent to
a^{\prime}(s)=b^{\prime}(s)=0
identically in
I
. First computations are

\begin{split}X_{s}&=(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},1)+r^{\prime}(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0),\\
X_{\theta}&=r(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0),\\
\mbox{det}(g_{ij})&=r^{2}(1+\left(r^{\prime}+a^{\prime}\cos\theta+b^{\prime}\sin\theta\right)^{2}).\end{split}

Let

(13)

W=1+\left(r^{\prime}+a^{\prime}\cos\theta+b^{\prime}\sin\theta\right)^{2}.

The Gauss map of
\Sigma
is

(14)

\nu=\frac{1}{\sqrt{W}}(-\cos\theta,-\sin\theta,r^{\prime}+a^{\prime}\cos\theta+b^{\prime}\sin\theta).

In particular,

(15)

\nu_{3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{W}}(r^{\prime}+a^{\prime}\cos\theta+b^{\prime}\sin\theta).

The basis
\{E_{1},E_{2}\}
is

\begin{split}E_{1}&=\frac{1}{W}(\cos\theta\left(r^{\prime}+a^{\prime}\cos\theta+b^{\prime}\sin\theta\right),\sin\theta\left(r^{\prime}+a^{\prime}\cos\theta+b^{\prime}\sin\theta\right),1),\\
E_{2}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{W}}(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0).\end{split}

In terms of the basis
\{X_{s},X_{\theta}\}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{lll}&c_{11}=\dfrac{1}{W},&c_{12}=\dfrac{a^{\prime}\sin\theta-b^{\prime}\cos\theta}{rW},\\
&c_{21}=0,&c_{22}=\dfrac{1}{r\sqrt{W}}.\end{array}

For the computation of
h(E_{1},E_{1})
and
h(E_{2},E_{2})
, we find

\begin{split}h_{ss}&=-r(r^{\prime\prime}+a^{\prime\prime}\cos\theta+b^{\prime\prime}\sin\theta),\\
h_{s\theta}&=0,\\
h_{\theta\theta}&=r^{2}.\end{split}

Notice that
1-\nu_{3}^{2}=1/W
. We have

\begin{split}h(E_{1},E_{1})&=\frac{\left(a^{\prime}\sin\theta-b^{\prime}\cos\theta\right)^{2}-r\left(r^{\prime\prime}+a^{\prime\prime}\cos\theta+b^{\prime\prime}\sin\theta\right)}{W^{2}},\\
h(E_{2},E_{2})&=\frac{1}{W}.\end{split}

Using (
15
), equation (
10
) becomes

\Lambda r(r^{\prime}+a^{\prime}\cos\theta+b^{\prime}\sin\theta)^{3}=2(r^{\prime}+a^{\prime}\cos\theta+b^{\prime}\sin\theta)^{2}+2\left((a^{\prime}\sin\theta-b^{\prime}\cos\theta)^{2}-r(r^{\prime\prime}+a^{\prime\prime}\cos\theta+b^{\prime\prime}\sin\theta)\right).

We write this identity as an equation of type

\sum_{n=0}^{3}A_{n}(s)\cos(n\theta)+B_{n}(s)\sin(n\theta)=0.

Since the functions
\{\cos(n\theta),\sin(n\theta):0\leq n\leq 3\}
are linearly independent, all coefficients
A_{n}
and
B_{n}
must be
0
identically. For
n=3
we have

\begin{split}A_{3}&=\frac{1}{4}\Lambda ra^{\prime}(a^{\prime 2}-3b^{\prime 2}),\\
B_{3}&=\frac{1}{4}\Lambda^{2}rb^{\prime}(3a^{\prime 2}-b^{\prime 2}).\end{split}

(1)

Case
\Lambda\not=0
. Since
r\not=0
, both equations
A_{3}=0
and
B_{3}=0
imply
a^{\prime}=b^{\prime}=0
identically. This proves that the surface is rotational, obtaining (1) of Thm.
1.2
.

(2)

Case
\Lambda=0
. Now Eq. (
10
) is of degree
1
, namely,

(16)

(2ra^{\prime\prime}-4a^{\prime}r^{\prime})\cos\theta+(2rb^{\prime\prime}-4b^{\prime}r^{\prime})\sin\theta-2(a^{\prime 2}+b^{\prime 2}+r^{\prime 2}-rr^{\prime\prime})=0.

This gives

(17)

\begin{split}ra^{\prime\prime}-2a^{\prime}r^{\prime}&=0,\\
rb^{\prime\prime}-2b^{\prime}r^{\prime}&=0,\\
a^{\prime 2}+b^{\prime 2}+r^{\prime 2}-rr^{\prime\prime}&=0.\end{split}

If
a^{\prime}=b^{\prime}=0
simultaneously, then the surface is rotational and the equation for
r
is
r^{\prime 2}-rr^{\prime\prime}=0
. Its solutions is
r(s)=r_{0}>0
(horizontal plane) or
r(s)=c_{2}e^{c_{1}s}
,
c_{2}>0
. Then
z=s=\frac{1}{c_{1}}\log(r)
and this gives the first part of (2) in Thm.
1.2
: this coincides with the expression given in (
11
). If
a^{\prime}
or
b^{\prime}
is not
0
identically, an integration of the first two equations implies that there exist

4.1.
Claim: the planes of the foliation are parallel

In this part, we will assume that
\Sigma

\Lambda
a constant not necessarily

1.5
is also proved thanks to Thm.
1.2

The proof is by contradiction. Suppose that the planes are not parallel. Consider
s
the parameter of the foliation and let
\Gamma\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
\Gamma=\Gamma(s)
, be a smooth curve which is orthogonal to each
s
-plane. Without loss of generality, we can suppose that
s
is the arc-length parameter of
\Gamma
. Since the planes of the foliation are not parallel, then the curve
\Gamma
is not a straight-line, in particular, the curvature
\kappa
of
\Gamma
is non-zero. This also implies the existence of a Frenet frame
\{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{b}\}
, where
\mathbf{t}(s)=\Gamma^{\prime}(s)
and
\mathbf{n}
and
\mathbf{b}
are the normal and binormal vector of
\Gamma
, respectively. The Frenet equations of
\Gamma
are

\begin{split}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}&=\kappa\mathbf{n},\\
\mathbf{n}^{\prime}&=-\kappa\mathbf{t}+\tau\mathbf{b},\\
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}&=-\tau\mathbf{n},\end{split}

where
\tau
is the torsion of
\Gamma
. According to
\Gamma
, a parametrization of
\Sigma
is

X(s,\theta)=\mathbf{c}(s)+r(s)(\cos\theta\,\mathbf{n}(s)+\sin\theta\,\mathbf{b}(s)),

where
\mathbf{c}(s)
and
r(s)>0
are the center and the radius, respectively, of each circle of the foliation. Let us express the derivative
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}(s)
in terms of the Frenet frame,

(22)

\mathbf{c}^{\prime}=\alpha\mathbf{t}+\beta\mathbf{n}+\gamma\mathbf{b},

where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
are smooth functions on
s
.

We now compute all terms in Eq. (
10
). Using (
22
), the tangent basis
\{X_{s},X_{\theta}\}
is expressed in coordinates with respect to the Frenet frame by

\begin{split}X_{s}&=(\alpha-r\kappa\cos\theta,r^{\prime}\cos\theta-r\tau\sin\theta+\beta,r^{\prime}\sin\theta+r\tau\cos\theta+\gamma),\\
X_{\theta}&=(0,-r\sin\theta,r\cos\theta).\end{split}

The unit normal is

\nu=\frac{1}{\sqrt{W}}\left(r^{\prime}+\beta\cos\theta+\gamma\sin\theta,\cos\theta(r\kappa\cos\theta-\alpha),\sin\theta(r\kappa\cos\theta-\alpha)\right),

where

W=(r^{\prime}+\beta\cos\theta+\gamma\sin\theta)^{2}+\cos^{2}\theta(r\kappa\cos\theta-\alpha)^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta(r\kappa\cos\theta-\alpha)^{2}.

Notice that
\mbox{det}(g_{ij})=r^{2}W
. We now express
e_{3}
in coordinates with respect to the Frenet frame. Let

Following (
9

The calculations for

are some tedious and Mathematica can help. For example, expressions of

\begin{split}c_{21}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{W}}(e_{22}\sin\theta-e_{33}\cos\theta),\\

Using these

If
