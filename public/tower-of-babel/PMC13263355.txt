Liposomal doxorubicin plus nab-paclitaxel with/without chemoradiotherapy in head and neck adenoid cystic carcinoma: single-arm phase II study
Authors: Xinxin Zhang, Jian Wei, Nanxiang Chen, Kun Liu, Robert Liang, Lin Ma, Zhigang Song, Xiang Gu, Xiaohong Chen, Shiming Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Given no standard therapies, this single-arm, open-label, multicenter phase II study was conducted in unresectable locally advanced or recurrent/metastatic adenoid cystic carcinoma of the head and neck (LA or R/M ACCHN). Patients received chemotherapeutic nanomedicines (20 mg/m2 liposomal doxorubicin plus 120 mg/m2 nab-paclitaxel on days 1 and 8) for three 21-day cycles. Subsequently, patients eligible for radiotherapy received concurrent chemoradiotherapy (cCRT; helical tomotherapy or intensity-modulated radiotherapy and nab-paclitaxel), whereas those ineligible for radiotherapy continued 2–3 cycles of original chemotherapy. Between May 27, 2021, and November 13, 2024, 31 enrolled patients were evaluable for efficacy and safety. The primary endpoint objective response rate (ORR) was 90.3% (95% CI, 74.2–98.0%), with 15 (48.4%) complete responses and 13 (41.9%) partial responses, meeting the prespecified primary endpoint. The median duration of response was 18.5 (95% CI, 0–45.8) months. With a median follow-up of 15.7 months by data cutoff (June 25, 2025), the median progression-free survival (PFS) was 25.7 (95% CI, 6.5–44.8) months; the 1-year PFS was 77.1%. The median overall survival (OS) was not reached (NR), with a 1-year OS of 90.0%. Patients who received cCRT had a prolonged PFS (NR vs. 12.8 months) and OS (NR vs. 21.2 months) compared to those who did not. Grade 3 treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) occurred in 8 (25.8%) patients. No ≥grade 4 TRAEs or treatment-related deaths occurred. This study demonstrated tumor responses among the highest reported to date, with a manageable safety profile in unresectable LA or R/M ACCHN. Chemotherapeutic nanomedicines, especially sequential cCRT for appropriate patients, may represent an effective and safe option.

Introduction

Adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC), accounting for approximately 1% of head and neck cancers (HNC), is characterized by slow growth, perineural invasion, and an unpredictable and relentless clinical course.1 Despite aggressive local therapy (surgery and radiotherapy) for primary tumors, recurrent or metastatic (R/M) diseases occur frequently, eventually leading to progressive and life-threatening conditions.2 Nevertheless, there is no standard treatment for unresectable locally advanced (LA) or R/M ACC of the head and neck (ACCHN).

Recent efforts to improve clinical outcomes have focused on immunotherapies and/or targeted therapies.2–4 However, immunotherapies are defined by disappointing activity and resistance (objective response rate [ORR], less than 10%; median progression-free survival [PFS], below 4.5 months) due to ACCHN having low tumor immunogenicity and a lack of PD-L1 expression.3,5–9 Targeted therapies have encountered issues of toxicity that may hinder continuous use, accompanied by a modest ORR (mostly <20%) and mixed results for PFS (2.5–20 months; Supplementary Table S1).2,10,11 Apatinib achieved the best tumor response (46.2%) to date in R/M ACCHN, while probably compromising safety (18.5% discontinuation and 76.9% dose reductions).12 As such, highly effective and safe regimens for patients with unresectable LA or R/M ACCHN are still an unmet clinical need.

There is a consensus to reserve chemotherapy for patients with unresectable, symptomatic R/M, or rapidly progressing diseases.3,13,14 However, conventional chemotherapy for ACCHN was limited by modest antitumor activity and undesirable off-target toxicity (Supplementary Table S2). To overcome these limitations, coupling cytotoxic agents with nanosized drug delivery systems—referred to as chemotherapeutic nanomedicines—has emerged as a strategy to maximize the therapeutic index.15–17 Mechanically, chemotherapeutic nanomedicines increased the intratumoral accumulation of therapeutic agents through enhanced permeability and retention (ERP)-mediated passive tumor-specific targeting, improving efficacy and reducing off-target toxicities of the encapsulated drugs.18–21 In this context, we used the combination of liposomal doxorubicin and albumin paclitaxel (nab-paclitaxel), representing clinically used typical chemotherapeutic nanomedicines whose parent compounds have shown preliminary antitumor activity ACC.22,23 In addition, this proposed combination was supported by chemotherapy principles, where tumor progression was closely linked to the cell cycle and combining cell cycle-specific agents (e.g., taxanes) with cell cycle-nonspecific agents (e.g., anthracyclines) allowed for the elimination of tumor cells at different phases of proliferation, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy.24–26 More importantly, preliminary observations from our prior experience with liposomal doxorubicin plus nab-paclitaxel showed a complete response (CR) rate of up to 53.8% in LA or R/M ACCHN, suggesting potential antitumor activity.

Therefore, as an extension of previous work, we conducted a phase II study assessing the efficacy and safety of liposomal doxorubicin combined with nab-paclitaxel in unresectable LA or R/M ACCHN patients. Equally noteworthy, patients eligible for radiotherapy in this study were administered induction chemotherapy followed by concurrent chemoradiotherapy (cCRT), given the benefits in improving local control and reducing distant metastasis of the recommended sequential treatment approach in HNC.27

Results

Patient characteristics and treatment

Between May 27, 2021, and November 13, 2024, 31 patients were enrolled, received at least one dose of study treatment, and had safety records, comprising the full analysis set (FAS) and safety population (Fig. 1). Thirty-one patients completed a 3-cycle liposomal doxorubicin plus nab-paclitaxel regimen, and one patient discontinued treatment due to voluntary withdrawal; thus, 30 patients were assessed for subsequent therapies. Based on radiotherapy eligibility, 17 patients were ineligible for cCRT and continued chemotherapy alone, of whom 14 completed 6-cycle treatment; 13 were eligible for cCRT and received cCRT (helical tomotherapy [HT]: n = 11; intensity-modulated radiotherapy [IMRT]: n = 2). By the data cutoff (June 25, 2025), the median treatment duration was 5.0 (range, 2.5–7.1) months for nab-paclitaxel and 3.6 (range, 2.2–6.1) months for liposomal doxorubicin (Supplementary Table S3). The median dose for planning gross tumor volume (pGTV) was 70 Gy. Efficacy

Of 31 patients, 15 (48.4%) achieved CR, and 13 (41.9%) attained partial response (PR); the ORR was 90.3% (95% confidence intervals [CI], 74.2–98.0%), meeting the prespecified primary endpoint for response (Table 2). Therein, 71.0% (22/31) of patients experienced marked reductions (≥60%) in target lesions (Fig. 2a). Additionally, one (3.2%) case had stable disease (SD) as the best response, for a disease control rate (DCR) of 93.5% (29/31; 95% CI, 78.6–99.2%; Fig. 2b). Among 28 responders, the median duration of response (DOR) was 18.5 (95% CI, 0–45.8) months (Fig. 3a). The objective response occurred in 58.1% (18/31) of patients who completed 3 cycles of therapy and 92.6% (25/27) of patients who completed the whole study treatment. In addition, the primary lesions in the head and neck responded better to treatment than distant metastatic sites (Supplementary Table S5). With a median follow-up time of 15.7 (range, 3.0–49.0) months by the data cutoff, 12 (38.7%) PFS events were observed. The median PFS was 25.7 (95% CI, 6.5–44.8) months, with 6-month, 1-year, and 2-year PFS rates of 90.3, 77.1, and 52.6%, respectively (Fig. 3b). Of the eight (19.5%) deaths, seven were due to disease progression; one was attributed to a treatment-unrelated hemorrhage during the fourth cycle of chemotherapy, likely resulting from injury to the surrounding major blood vessels at the primary tumor site caused by prior radiotherapy and two prior radioactive particle implantations. The median OS was not reached (NR; Fig. 3c). In the exploratory subgroup analysis, numerically higher ORR (100.0% vs. 83.3%), PFS (NR vs. 12.8 months), and OS (NR vs. 21.2 months) were observed in patients without prior radiotherapy versus those who had a history of radiotherapy. Similar trends in ORR (95.5% vs. 77.8%) and PFS (NR vs. 10.4 months) were observed in patients with solid-type tumors, who exhibited numerically poorer survival outcomes (PFS, 7.7 vs. 21.1-NR months; OS, 8.6 vs. 33.3-NR months) than those with other histologic subtypes. The median PFS and OS were 12.8 and 21.2 months, respectively, in the patient subgroup with locoregional and metastatic disease, while both endpoints were NR in patients with locoregional disease only. Four patients with meningeal involvement who were ineligible for cCRT showed unfavorable ORR (25.0 vs. 100.0%), PFS (2.5 vs. 31.9 months; Supplementary Table S6), and OS (8.6 months vs. NR; Supplementary Fig. S1).

In 13 patients who received cCRT after 3 cycles of chemotherapy, the best ORR was 100.0% (95% CI, 75.3–100.0%), with 10 (76.9%) achieving CR and 3 (23.1%) achieving PR (Table 2). The median PFS and OS were NR, with 1-year and 2-year rates of both 100.0% (Fig. 3d, e). In 18 patients who did not receive cCRT, the ORR, median PFS, and median OS were 83.3%, 12.8 months, and 21.2 months, respectively. The estimated PFS rate was 61.7% at 1 year and 16.5% at 2 years. The 1-year and 2-year OS rates were estimated as 82.5 and 38.9%, respectively. These subgroup analyses are exploratory and should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample sizes and potential confounders of differences in baseline characteristics.

There were interesting observations during tumor response evaluation: some patients showed minimal size reduction after three cycles of initial chemotherapy and were assessed as SD, yet a marked decrease in tumor density was noted, as illustrated in a representative case (Supplementary Fig. S2a). An additional patient who was assessed as PR accompanied by a marked reduction in tumor density at the end of cCRT subsequently achieved CR during follow-up and maintained CR for 25 months until the data cutoff, possibly suggesting the importance of incorporating density when assessing the efficacy in ACC (e.g., Choi criteria). Moreover, as illustrated in one representative case (Supplementary Fig. S2b), despite multiple prior lines of therapy, patients who received 6 cycles of chemotherapy frequently experienced marked reductions in primary tumor size and achieved CR. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S2c, another heavily pretreated patient who received 6 cycles of chemotherapy exhibited favorable responses in parts of the tumor lesions but was ultimately assessed as disease progression due to increased meningeal involvement and new intracerebral lesions.

Safety

Among the safety population (n = 31), 29 (93.5%) experienced at least one any-grade treatment-related adverse event (TRAE), with 8 (25.8%) at grade 3 (Table 3). Grade 3 TRAEs included hand-foot syndrome (4/31, 12.9%), mucositis oral (3/31, 9.7%), and white blood cell decreased (1/31, 3.2%). No grade 4 or higher TRAEs occurred. One case required dose interruption, and 16 patients required dose reductions. None of the patients discontinued treatment or died due to TRAEs. Two patients experienced late-onset brain radiation injury at approximately 2 and 3 years after treatment: one 34-year-old female developed grade 1 toxicity and received two cycles of bevacizumab (7.5 mg/kg every 21 days), resulting in clinical and radiographic recovery. Discussion

To our knowledge, this was the first prospective study evaluating chemotherapeutic nanomedicines in ACCHN. With an ORR of 90.3%, this phase II study exceeded its prespecified primary endpoint, representing among the highest reported treatment responses among all systemic therapy studies in unresectable LA or R/M ACCHN. Accompanied by decreased compression of surrounding organs or tissues dependent on tumor shrinkage, the palliation or disappearance of pain and metastatic symptoms (e.g., dyspnea, abdominal distention) occurred in nearly all patients after 1–2 cycles of initial chemotherapy. Consistent with historical chemotherapy series,28–30 it potentially represented an indication for the feasibility of chemotherapeutic nanomedicines and improvement in patient quality of life. Deep (CR rate, 48.4%) and durable (DOR, 18.5 months) responses contributed to the promising PFS (25.7 months) and OS (median, NR; 1-year rate, 90.0%). Most subgroups appeared to benefit from the proposed regimen in this study, exhibiting a comparable ORR to that in the whole population. Four patients with meningeal involvement who were ineligible for cCRT and received chemotherapeutic nanomedicines alone exhibited poorer prognosis, possibly because the presence of the blood‒brain barrier or insufficient local blood supply hindered the effective delivery of the chemotherapeutic nanomedicines to the tumor sites. With favorable safety profiles, these results conferred by our approach were encouraging, especially considering that 25.8% of patients had refractory ACC to multiple treatment modalities (i.e., PD-1 inhibitors, VEGFR inhibitors), 25.6% failed prior to three lines of treatment, and 48.4% developed metastatic diseases. Given the lack of effective systemic therapies, our findings hold promise to define a new treatment paradigm toward a clinically meaningful improvement in patient outcomes.

Historically, systemic therapies in ACC studies were typically administered with continuous dosing schedules and have rarely reported significant treatment responses, mostly SD.3 Despite limitations of cross-study comparisons, our ORR of 83.3% in patients who received chemotherapeutic nanomedicines only appeared numerically higher than previously reported conventional chemotherapy (such as taxanes and anthracycline; Supplementary Table S1)22,23 and the most promising apatinib (46.2%; Supplementary Table S2) among ACC trials.28 In addition, these patients with chemotherapeutic nanomedicines only showed apparent numerical differences in PFS (12.8 months vs. 6-19.7 months) and OS (21.2 months vs. 16.6 months-NR) compared to ACC studies involving agents such as axitinib plus avelumab, dovitinib, and apatinib.5,28,31,32 Although these observations should be interpreted cautiously due to differences in study populations, study design, and small sample size, our findings should be under a positive view, especially considering that all treated patients with chemotherapeutic nanomedicines had received at least two prior lines of therapies, including those who had progressed on or were refractory to VEGFR/PD-1 inhibitors. The superiority reported here supported the premise of this study—chemotherapeutic nanomedicine tactics with tumor-targeted properties could achieve high-concentration drug accumulation at tumor sites, improving the therapeutic index.15,20,33,34 Given that tumor-targeted chemotherapeutic nanomedicine bears conceptual similarity to antibody‒drug conjugates (ADCs), our impressive results may inform the feasibility of ADCs against well-defined tumor targets for treating ACC. In parallel, tailoring the dosing schedules of liposomal doxorubicin and nab-paclitaxel from a conventional pattern (typically administered on day 1 of each cycle) to dose-dense administration on both days 1 and 8 may be responsible for the encouraging efficacy, consistent with the Norton-Simon hypothesis that increasing dosing frequency enabled continuous exposure to chemotherapy and facilitated the eradication of indolent cancer cells.35,36

A notable aspect of this study was the exploratory sequential treatment algorithm applied in ACCHN, in which patients eligible for radiotherapy received cCRT following three cycles of induction chemotherapeutic nanomedicine. In this subgroup, encouraging tumor shrinkage (ORR, 100.0%) and favorable survival outcomes (2-year PFS/OS rates, 100.0%) were observed, which was achieved using HT or IMRT after induction therapy, without the need for proton or heavy-ion radiotherapy. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Patients who proceeded to cCRT were generally less heavily pretreated (prior lines of 0–1) than those who received chemotherapeutic nanomedicine only (prior lines of ≥2), resulting in selection bias and baseline differences that may have influenced the interpretation of response and survival outcomes. Nevertheless, each patient subgroup warranted an optimized, tailored approach, and the sequential strategy of chemotherapeutic nanomedicine followed by cCRT for selected patients merits further validation in larger prospective studies. An expected observation was better objective responses at the primary sites and more SD at distant metastases, potentially explained by tumor dynamics under selection pressure.37,38 Specifically, in parallel with the shrinkage of the primary tumor, resistant cells presented by therapeutic pressure could compose part of the metastatic lesions following dissemination, metastasis, colonization, and interaction with the microenvironment, leading to heterogeneity and consequent differences in treatment sensitivity between primary and metastatic tumors.37–39 However, we cannot preclude later responses defined by RECIST because of substantial tumor density changes at target lesions, as supported by several representative cases—one SD case after 3 cycles of initial chemotherapy subsequently achieved CR, and the other PR case, accompanied by a marked reduction in tumor density at the end of cCRT, converted to CR. Such observation may reflect unique pathological features of ACC, where the prevalence of microscopic pseudocystic spaces, tumor necrosis, degeneration, and cystic changes resulted in firm, unencapsulated masses, leading to posttreatment residual mucin, fibrous tissue, or necrosis that possibly caused underestimation of response by size-based RECIST criteria. Thus, RECIST incorporating tumor densities, such as the Choi criteria, may be beneficial for efficacy assessments for ACC treatment.

The toxicity profiles identified here were consistent with the known spectrum of the individual treatment components,40–43 without unexpected or new safety signals. Grade 3 TRAEs in our study occurred infrequently and were managed through dose modifications or supportive care. No grade 4 or higher events or deaths due to TRAEs were reported here. These favorable safety profiles may be associated with tumor-specific targeting of chemotherapeutic nanomedicines.15,18,20,33

One limitation of phase II ACC trials was the small sample size, single-arm design, and heterogeneous cohort, which may affect efficacy interpretation due to the introduction of potential selection bias. However, such a study design is deemed a reasonable setup in rare cancers, given the challenges in large-scale patient recruitment.44 Second, an extended follow-up is required to assess long-term benefits and late toxicities. Moreover, although tumor responses were assessed by investigators in strict accordance with the RECIST criteria, the absence of an independent central review may lead to an overestimation of the ORR. In addition, the histopathological assessment of solid components was limited by the use of biopsy samples, which might not fully reflect the overall tumor architecture. In addition, further investigation of translational biomarkers to inform patient selection for our regimen is warranted. Finally, we did not capture quality-of-life data. In conclusion, this phase II study provides a valuable proof of concept in the management of unresectable LA or R/M ACCHN, demonstrating encouraging efficacy and manageable safety profiles. Continuous chemotherapeutic nanomedicines with liposomal doxorubicin plus nab-paclitaxel for heavily pretreated patients or sequential cCRT for those eligible for radiotherapy may represent a potential approach for improving clinical outcomes. However, these preliminary findings require further confirmation in larger, well-designed studies to inform whether this approach could be routinely recommended outside the clinical trial setting.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This was a single-arm, open-label, investigator-initiated phase II trial (ChiCTR2100044076) conducted at two sites across China, aiming to evaluate the efficacy and safety of liposomal doxorubicin plus nab-paclitaxel in patients with unresectable LA or R/M ACCHN. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committees of the Chinese People’s Liberation Army General Hospital (number: S2020-521-02) and other participating centers and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the Good Clinical Practice Guidelines, and applicable local laws and regulations. All patients provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Eligible patients were 18–70 years of age with histopathologically confirmed ACCHN, surgically unresectable or radiologically progressive R/M symptomatic diseases, and at least one measurable lesion per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumor version 1.1 (RECIST v1.1). Patients were required to have an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0–1, an estimated life expectancy of ≥3 months, adequate hepatic and renal functions, and a left ventricular ejection fraction ≥50% on echocardiography assessment. Patients with grade 2 or higher peripheral neuropathy according to National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0 (NCI-CTCAE v5.0), abnormal bone marrow hyperplasia and other hematopoietic dysfunctions, and a history of allergies or hypersensitivity reactions to study drugs or their excipients were excluded. Complete eligibility criteria are provided in the protocol.

Procedures

A regimen of chemotherapeutic nanomedicines consisting of 20 mg/m2 liposomal doxorubicin and 120 mg/m2 nab-paclitaxel was intravenously given on days 1 and 8 of the 21-day cycle for 3 cycles. The subsequent treatment schedule of continued chemotherapy alone or sequential cCRT was determined by the feasibility of radiotherapy, which was centrally evaluated by a multidisciplinary team (MDT) including radiation oncologists and radiologists. Specifically, patients eligible for cCRT received 120 mg/m² nab-paclitaxel (days 1 and 8 in a 21-day cycle for 2–3 cycles) concurrently with radiotherapy (HT or IMRT at a dose of 70 Gy to pGTV in 33 fractions). Patients ineligible for cCRT continued original chemotherapeutic nanomedicines for an additional 2–3 cycles. Detailed eligibility for cCRT and radiotherapy planning is provided in Supplementary Method S1.

Liposomal doxorubicin or nab-paclitaxel could be reduced to manage toxicity. Complete dose modification criteria are listed in the protocol. The tumor was assessed by the site investigator according to RECIST v1.1 using enhanced MRI at baseline, at the end of cycle 3, and 1 month after completion of study treatment. AEs were recorded until 28 days after the last dose of study treatment and graded according to the NCI-CTCAE v5.0. Patients who transitioned into the end-of-treatment visit phase were followed for survival every 3 months for year 1, every 4 months for years 2–3, and every 6 months for years 4–5 until death.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the ORR assessed by the investigator per RECIST v1.1, which was defined as the percentage of patients achieving CR and PR. Secondary endpoints included DCR (percentage of patients with CR, PR, and SD), PFS (time from the start of treatment to PD or all-cause death), OS (time from the start of treatment to death by any cause), post hoc DOR (time from the first documented response to PD or all-cause death), and safety.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was estimated based on the primary endpoint of ORR. Compared to the historical ORR of 25% with chemotherapy,45 we assumed that liposomal doxorubicin plus nab-paclitaxel could improve ORR to 50%. With a two-sided alpha level of 0.05 and a power of 80%, a target accrual of 26 patients was required for this phase II study. Considering an approximately 10% dropout rate, a total of 30 patients were planned to enroll in this study.

Efficacy was evaluated in the FAS, including enrolled patients who received at least one dose of study treatment. Safety was assessed in the safety population, which comprised the FAS population with safety records. Patient characteristics and safety outcomes were summarized using descriptive statistics, which were expressed as medians (ranges) for continuous variables and frequencies (percentages [%]) for categorical variables. The 95% CIs for ORR and DCR were calculated using the Clopper-Pearson method. PFS and OS were assessed using the Kaplan‒Meier method. Patients without OS events were censored at the date last known alive. Patients who were alive without disease progression were censored at the date of the last assessment. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS, version 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and a two-sided p value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
