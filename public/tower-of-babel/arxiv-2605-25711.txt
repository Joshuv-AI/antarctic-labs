Introduction

The systematic study of random graphs was initiated by Erdős and
Rényi in their seminal papers

[
14
,
15
]
, and has played an immense role in
modern discrete mathematics, computer science, and related areas

[
9
,
23
,
17
]
.
This work studies random graphs from the point of view of generic
rigidity in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a major subject in modern discrete geometry since the works of Laman
[
32
]
and Asimow–Roth

[
3
,
4
]
. While
1
- and
2
-dimensional
generic rigidity are well understood, characterizing generically rigid
graphs in dimensions
d\geq 3
remains a major open problem. Our work focuses on this regime of higher-dimensional rigidity of sparse random graphs.

A
d
-dimensional framework is a pair
(G,{\bf p})
, where

G=(V,E)
is a finite simple graph and
{\bf p}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The framework is called rigid if the only continuous motions
of the vertices in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
starting at

and preserving the edge lengths are
induced by isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Asimow and Roth showed that if

is generic then the rigidity of the framework
(G,{\bf p})
depends only
on the underlying graph
G
, and is equivalent to the rigidity matrix
R(G,{\bf p})
attaining a maximal rank, see
[
19
]
.

One of the classical results on the

random graph model, in which every edge is included
independently with probability

, is that a.a.s., i.e., with probability
tending to one as

, the graph is connected if and only if it
has no isolated vertices. Since graph connectivity is equivalent to

1
-dimensional rigidity, it is natural to ask for higher-dimensional
analogs: a.a.s.,
d
-rigidity occurs if and only if the
minimum degree of the graph is at least
d
. Indeed, this was first proved for
d=2
by Jackson, Servatius and Servatius
[
21
]
, and extended to all fixed dimensions

by Lew, Nevo, Raz and the author
[
33
]
(see also
[
27
,
25
]
). Rigidity of

has also been studied in other
settings where the minimum degree is not the only obstruction to its rigidity,
such as when

[
29
,
30
,
38
,
24
]
, and for non-generic embeddings
[
7
,
18
,
36
,
13
]
.

Another fundamental discovery in random graph theory is the phase transition in

This was extended to

The infinitesimal point of view of Asimow and Roth leads to several interesting notions in

Let

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Infinitesimal rigidity theory

Throughout this paper, we fix a dimension
d\geq 2
. Let
G
be an
n
-vertex graph and
{\bf p}:V(G)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
a generic embedding. We view every
{\bf p}(v)
as a column vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
and denote
{\bf p}_{uv}={\bf p}(u)-{\bf p}(v)
for every two adjacent vertices
u
and
v
.
The rigidity matrix
R=R(G,{\bf p})
of the
d
-framework
(G,{\bf p})
is the
|E|\times d|V|
matrix whose rows are indexed by edges
uv\in E
and whose columns are grouped into
|V|
blocks of size
d
, one block for each vertex.
For an edge
uv\in E
, the row corresponding to
uv
has
{\bf p}_{uv}^{*}
in the block corresponding to
u
,
{\bf p}_{vu}^{*}
in the block corresponding to
v
, and zero elsewhere.

Throughout this paper it will be more convenient to work with the
rigidity Laplacian

L=L(G,{\bf p})=R^{T}R
(defined more explicitly below), as it is a self-adjoint operator.
We denote by
Z=Z(G,{\bf p})
the kernel of
R(G,{\bf p})
; equivalently,
Z
is the kernel of the rigidity Laplacian
L(G,{\bf p})
.
A
dn
-dimensional vector
q
in
Z
can be viewed as assigning an
infinitesimal velocity vector
q(v)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to each vertex, describing
an infinitesimal motion of the vertices at
{\bf p}
under which all edge
lengths are preserved to first order. If
{\bf p}
is generic then the
isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
induce a
\binom{d+1}{2}
-dimensional subspace of

Z
, which we refer to as the trivial motions. More concretely, the
subspace of trivial motions is parametrized by a
d\times d

skew-symmetric matrix
A
and a vector
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, yielding a vector

q\in Z
defined by

q(v)=A{\bf p}(v)+t,\qquad v\in V.

The graph
G
is called
d
-rigid if
\dim Z=\binom{d+1}{2}
.

Another matrix that will play a role in this work is the orthogonal
projection matrix
P:\mathbb{R}^{dn}\to Z
. Intuitively, suppose that each vertex
has unit mass and that the edges are massless bars. If
f\in\mathbb{R}^{dn}
describes a vector of instantaneous external forces,
where
f(v)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the force applied to the vertex
v
, then

Pf
is the component of this force in the space of admissible
infinitesimal motions; equivalently, for unit masses, it is the resulting
instantaneous acceleration after imposing the bar constraints.
In particular, one may consider this locally. Let
P_{v,v}
denote the

d\times d
diagonal block of
P
corresponding to the vertex
v
.
If a force
f(v)
is applied only to
v
, then the resulting
instantaneous acceleration of
v
is

P_{v,v}f(v).
Thus, the trace of
P_{v,v}
quantifies the degrees of freedom that
v
has in the framework, and we will refer to it as the local flexibility of
v
; see Section
4
below for a formal definition and further discussion.

2.2.
Benjamini–Schramm convergence

A main idea in this work is to estimate the rigidity rank of a finite graph by its local structure.
For this, we work with the local weak topology of rooted graphs introduced in the influential paper
of Benjamini and Schramm
[
6
]
.
This point of view is closely related to the objective method of Aldous and Steele

[
2
]
, and has been used to study a variety of
parameters of sparse graphs which are governed by local limits. Of particular
relevance to us are results concerning spectral measures and kernels of
operators on locally convergent graphs; see, for example,

[
1
,
11
,
10
]
.

A rooted graph
(G,o)
is a graph with a root vertex
o\in V(G)
. Let
\mathcal{G}_{\bullet}
denote the set of isomorphism classes of locally finite rooted graphs
(G,o)
,
where isomorphisms are required to preserve the root.
For
(G,o)\in\mathcal{G}_{\bullet}
and an integer
r\geq 0
, we write
B_{G}(o,r)
for the rooted induced subgraph spanned by all vertices
at graph distance at most
r
from
o
; thus
B_{G}(o,r)\in\mathcal{G}_{\bullet}
as well. We metrize
\mathcal{G}_{\bullet}
by defining
the distance between
(G_{1},o_{1})
and
(G_{2},o_{2})
to be
2^{-r}
, where
r\geq 0
is the largest radius for which the
rooted balls
B_{G_{1}}(o_{1},r)
and
B_{G_{2}}(o_{2},r)
are root-preserving isomorphic. The induced metric is the

local topology
on
\mathcal{G}_{\bullet}
; with this topology,
\mathcal{G}_{\bullet}
is a Polish space, so the standard notions of tightness and convergence in distribution apply.
We say that a (possibly random) rooted graph
(G,o)
is the local weak (Benjamini–Schramm) limit of a sequence of (possibly random) finite graphs
G_{n}
if
(G_{n},o_{n})

converges in distribution to
(G,o)
in
\mathcal{G}_{\bullet}
, where
o_{n}
is chosen uniformly from
V(G_{n})
.

We mention two classical examples of local weak convergence from graph theory that appear in this paper. First,
G(n,c/n)
, where
c>0
is fixed, converges locally to a
Galton–Watson branching process with
\operatorname{Poi}(c)
progeny distribution (defined precisely in the next subsection) as
n\to\infty
.
Second, the uniform random
k
-regular
n
-vertex graph, where
k\geq 3
is fixed, converges locally to the infinite
k
-regular tree
\mathbb{T}_{k}
as
n\to\infty
.
Both these examples can be viewed as special cases of local weak convergence of uniform random graphs with a given degree distribution
to a corresponding unimodular Galton–Watson process.

2.3.
Galton–Watson processes, size-biasing and thinning

The graphs we study here converge locally to Galton–Watson processes.
A Galton–Watson branching process is a random rooted tree generated recursively by letting each vertex produce an independent number of children.
Let
X,Y
be probability distributions on
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
. Denote by
\mathrm{GW}(X,Y)
the distribution on random, locally finite rooted trees
T
generated
by the Galton–Watson branching process in which the root
o
has offspring distribution
X
, while every other vertex has offspring distribution
Y
.

Galton–Watson processes that arise as local limits of finite graphs are unimodular, which is equivalent to the condition that
Y
is the size-biasing of
X
:

Y(m-1)=\frac{m\,X(m)}{\mathbb{E}[X]}\,,\quad\forall m\geq 1\,.

(2.1)

Here we assume
\mathbb{E}[X]<\infty
.

Two main examples that we consider in this paper are:

(1)

X=Y=\operatorname{Poi}(c)
, for some fixed
c>0
, which is the
local weak limit of
G(n,c/n)
;

(2)

the
k
-regular tree
\mathbb{T}_{k}
, i.e.,

X(k)=Y(k-1)=1
, which is the local weak limit of the random

k
-regular graph.

An easy and fundamental fact about size-biasing is that for every
f:\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}\to\mathbb{R}
there holds,

\mathbb{E}[Xf(X-1)]=\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[f(Y)]\,.

(2.2)

In addition, we will use the operation of thinning, and its relation to size-biasing. Given
0\leq p\leq 1
, we define a coupled pair
(X_{p},X_{1-p})
by
first sampling
X
, and then, conditional on
X
, sampling
X_{p}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(X,p)
and setting
X_{1-p}:=X-X_{p}
. We can also view this pair as coupled with
X
.
The following claims are useful in our argument.

Claim 2.1
.

Suppose that
Y
is the size-biasing of
X
and
0\leq p\leq 1
. Then,

(1)

Y_{p}
is the size-biasing of
X_{p}
.

(2)

For every positive integer
d>0
there holds

(1-p)\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{P}(Y_{p}<d)=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{X_{p}<d}\cdot X_{1-p}]\,.

(3)

Suppose that
t\sim Y_{1-p}
and that conditioned on
t
,
h\sim X_{p}|\{X_{1-p}=t+1\}
. Then, the pair
(h,t)
is
(Y_{p},Y_{1-p})
-distributed.

Proof.

First, for every
t\geq 0
there holds

\displaystyle(t+1)\,X_{p}(t+1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{p}=t+1}\cdot X_{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{p}=t+1}\cdot X_{p}\mid X\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[X\cdot p\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(X-1,p)=t)\right]

\displaystyle=p\,\mathbb{E}[X]\cdot\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(Y,p)=t)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{p}]\cdot Y_{p}(t)\,,

as claimed. The third equality follows from linearity of expectation, since given
X
,
\mathbf{1}_{X_{p}=t+1}\cdot X_{p}
is a sum of
X
Bernoullis with probability
p\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(X-1,p)=t).
In addition, the fourth equality is derived by (
2.2
) using
f(k)=\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(k,p)=t).

Similarly, for the second item, by linearity of expectation,

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{X_{p}<d}\cdot X_{1-p}\mid X]=X\cdot(1-p)\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(X-1,p)<d)\,,

and by taking expectation over
X
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{X_{p}<d}\,X_{1-p}\right]

\displaystyle=(1-p)\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[X\cdot\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathrm{Bin}(X-1,p)<d\right)\right]

\displaystyle=(1-p)\,\mathbb{E}[X]\cdot\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathrm{Bin}(Y,p)<d\right)]\;=\;(1-p)\,\mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{P}\!\left(Y_{p}<d\right).

The transition to the second line is derived by (
2.2
) using
f(k)=\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(k,p)<d)
.

For the third item, note that for every
t,h
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{p}=h\mid X_{1-p}=t+1)

\displaystyle=~\frac{X(h+t+1)\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(h+t+1,p)=h)}{X_{1-p}(t+1)}

\displaystyle=~\frac{(1-p)(h+t+1)X(h+t+1)\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(h+t,p)=h)}{(t+1)X_{1-p}(t+1)}

\displaystyle=~\frac{(1-p)\mathbb{E}[X]Y(h+t)\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(h+t,p)=h)}{(t+1)X_{1-p}(t+1)}

\displaystyle=~\frac{Y(h+t)\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(h+t,p)=h)}{Y_{1-p}(t)}

\displaystyle=~\mathbb{P}(Y_{p}=h\mid Y_{1-p}=t)\,,

and the claim follows since we assume
t\sim Y_{1-p}
. Here, the second equality follows from a standard identity of Binomial distributions, the next equality holds since
Y
is the size-biasing of
X
, and the fourth equality is derived by the first item.
∎

3.
Infinite frameworks

An important idea in this work is to study rigidity of infinite graphs, and
infinite trees in particular. Rigidity of infinite frameworks was studied by
Owen and Power
[
37
]
and by
Kitson and Power
[
28
]
. The approach we
take here is closest to the notion of square-summable
infinitesimal rigidity from
[
37
]
.
However, we opt to give a self-contained treatment, partly since our focus is on the spectral-theoretic aspects of this notion.

The infinite graphs we consider are locally finite but may have unbounded degree. Consequently, their rigidity Laplacians may be unbounded operators. Accordingly, we begin by recalling some background material from the theory of unbounded operators
[
40
]
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex Hilbert space. The main issue with unbounded operators is that they cannot be defined on the entire space
\mathcal{H}
. Hence, an unbounded operator on
\mathcal{H}
consists of a domain
D
which is a linear subspace of
\mathcal{H}
, and a linear operator
L:D\to\mathcal{H}
. As we aim to apply spectral methods, we need to work with a self-adjoint operator. However, if
L
is not closed, that is, if the graph
\{(f,Lf)\mid f\in D\}
is not closed in
\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}
, then
L
cannot be self-adjoint, since the adjoint
L^{*}
is always closed.

Fortunately, in our setting,
D
is a
dense
subspace and
L
is symmetric. Therefore,
L
is closable, and we denote its closure by
\bar{L}
. The domain
D
is a
core
for
\bar{L}
.
We say that
L
is essentially self-adjoint if
\bar{L}
is self-adjoint.
In this case, the spectral theorem applies to
\bar{L}
. In particular, for any bounded Borel function
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
, the operator
g(\bar{L})
is well defined via the functional calculus. Moreover, for every vector
f\in\mathcal{H}
, there exists a finite Borel measure
\mu_{f}
on
\mathbb{R}
, called the spectral measure of
\bar{L}
with respect to
f
, such that

\langle f,g(\bar{L})f\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(\lambda)\,d\mu_{f}(\lambda)

for all bounded Borel functions
g
. In particular, if
g=\mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}
then
g(\bar{L})
is the orthogonal projection
P:\mathcal{H}\to\ker\bar{L}
and
\langle f,Pf\rangle=\mu_{f}(\{0\})
.

All the graphs
G=(V,E)
we consider
are locally finite on a countable vertex set. Let
{\bf p}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since we use spectral theory, we prefer to work over the complex numbers and consider the Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}:=\ell_{2}(V;\mathbb{C}^{d})\,.

This makes no difference to us since rigidity theories over
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
are identical. Denote by
D\subset\mathcal{H}
the subspace of finitely supported vectors. The rigidity Laplacian
L=L(G,{\bf p})
is given by a (possibly infinite) block matrix

L=(L_{uv})_{u,v\in V},\qquad L_{uv}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d},

acting on
f\in D
by

(Lf)(u)=\sum_{v\sim u}L_{uv}f(v),

where the sum is finite for each
u
. The block entries are given by

L_{uv}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{w\sim u}{\bf p}_{uw}{\bf p}_{uw}^{*},&u=v,\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle-\,{\bf p}_{uv}{\bf p}_{uv}^{*},&\{u,v\}\in E,\\[4.30554pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

(3.1)

The operator
L
is symmetric and defined on the dense domain
D
, since
L_{uv}=L_{vu}^{*}
for all
u,v\in V
. Therefore,
L
is closable, and we denote its closure by
\bar{L}
.

Definition 3.1
.

A
d
-framework
(G,{\bf p})
, where
G
is a locally finite graph on a countable vertex set, is called
self-adjoint
if
\bar{L}(G,{\bf p})
is a self-adjoint operator.

It is clear that if
G
is finite, or, more generally, if
G
has bounded degree and
{\bf p}_{uv},~uv\in E
are bounded, then
(G,{\bf p})
is self-adjoint. Indeed, in such a case the Rigidity Laplacian is a bounded operator.

Although self-adjointness of a framework is defined via the complex Hilbert
space
\mathcal{H}
, if a framework
(G,{\bf p})
is self-adjoint, we note that the
orthogonal projection

P=\mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}(\bar{L}):\mathcal{H}\to\ker(\bar{L})

onto the kernel of its rigidity Laplacian can be viewed as a real operator. Indeed, the
rigidity Laplacian has real entries, hence the projection
P
preserves the real subspace of
\mathcal{H}
.
Thus, when applied to real vectors,
P
is the orthogonal projection onto
the real kernel of
\bar{L}
.

3.1.
\ell_{2}
-infinitesimal motions

The following lemma extends the standard description for the kernel of the rigidity Laplacian from finite graphs to self-adjoint frameworks. In words, the kernel consists of all infinitesimal distance preserving motions that have a finite
\ell_{2}
-norm.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(G,{\bf p})
be a self-adjoint
d
-framework. Then,

\ker\bar{L}(G,{\bf p})=\{q\in\mathcal{H}\mid\langle q(u)-q(v),{\bf p}_{uv}\rangle=0,~~\forall uv\in E(G)\}\,.

Proof.

The dense subspace
D
of finitely-supported functions is a
core
for the self-adjoint operator
\bar{L}=\bar{L}(G,{\bf p})
. That is, for every
q
in the domain of
\bar{L}
there are functions
f_{n}\in D
such that
f_{n}\to q
and
Lf_{n}\to\bar{L}q
.

Suppose that
q\in\ker\bar{L}
, and consider functions
f_{n}\in D
such that
f_{n}\to q
and
Lf_{n}\to 0
. We have that

\sum_{uv\in E}|\langle f_{n}(u)-f_{n}(v),{\bf p}_{uv}\rangle|^{2}=\langle f_{n},Lf_{n}\rangle\to\langle q,\bar{L}q\rangle=0\,.

Hence, for every edge
uv\in E
,

\langle q(u)-q(v),{\bf p}_{uv}\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle f_{n}(u)-f_{n}(v),{\bf p}_{uv}\rangle=0\,.

On the other hand, suppose that
q\in\mathcal{H}
satisfies
\langle q(u)-q(v),{\bf p}_{uv}\rangle=0
for every edge
uv\in E
. Let
f\in D
. Then,

\displaystyle\langle q,Lf\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in V}\left\langle q(u),\sum_{v\sim u}{\bf p}_{uv}{\bf p}_{uv}^{*}(f(u)-f(v))\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{uv\in E}\left\langle q(u)-q(v),{\bf p}_{uv}{\bf p}_{uv}^{*}(f(u)-f(v))\right\rangle=0\,,

where the last equality follows from our assumption on
q
. Note that this summation is finite since
f\in D
. Therefore, by the definition of the adjoint,
\bar{L}^{*}q=0
, and the proof follows by the assumption that
\bar{L}
is self-adjoint.
∎

The following definition is natural, but will not be of much use in this work.

Definition 3.3
.

A self-adjoint
d
-framework
(G,{\bf p})
is called
\ell_{2}

strictly
d
-rigid
if

\ker\bar{L}(G,{\bf p})=\{0\}\,.

No finite graph is strictly-rigid since
\ker L
contains the trivial motions.
For infinite graphs, generic strict rigidity is more subtle since the trivial motions need not belong to

\ell_{2}
. Consequently,
\ker\bar{L}
may be trivial even when

{\bf p}
is generic, as the following example illustrates. The example
also shows that, on the other hand, there are generic
embeddings for which
\ker L
is non-trivial even for infinite graphs.

Example 3.4
.

Let
G
be a bounded-degree graph on
V=\mathbb{N}
such that the induced subgraph
G_{n}
on

[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
is
d
-rigid for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
{\bf p}:V\to[0,1]^{d}
be a generic embedding which is
well-spread
, in the sense that for some
\varepsilon>0
, and for every affine subspace
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exist infinitely many
v\in V
such that

\dist({\bf p}(v),F)\geq\varepsilon.

For example, draw
{\bf p}(v)
uniformly and independently from
[0,1]^{d}
for each
v\in V
.
By boundedness of
G
and
{\bf p}
, the framework
(G,{\bf p})
is self-adjoint. Let
q\in\ker(\bar{L})
. Restricting
q
to the induced subgraph
G_{n}
, the
assumption that
G_{n}
is
d
-rigid implies that
q|_{[n]}
is induced by a trivial motion. Since this holds for every
n
, it follows from the genericity of
{\bf p}
that
q
is induced by a single global trivial motion. That is, there exist a skew-symmetric matrix
A
and a vector
t
such that

q(v)=A{\bf p}(v)+t\qquad\text{for all }v\in V.

The well-spreadness assumption on
{\bf p}
implies that

\sum_{v\in V}\|A{\bf p}(v)+t\|^{2}=\infty

(3.2)

for all
(A,t)\neq(0,0)
. Since
q\in\mathcal{H}=\ell_{2}(V;\mathbb{C}^{d})
, this forces
A=0
and
t=0
, and hence
q=0
. Therefore,

\ker(\bar{L})=\{0\},

and consequently
\phi(v)=0
for every
v
.

Note that a different choice of a (possibly) generic embedding
{\bf p}
may lead to non-zero elements of
\mathcal{H}
arising from trivial motions: indeed, if there exist a skew-symmetric matrix
A
and a vector
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, not both zero, such that (
3.2
) converges, then the corresponding trivial motion belongs to
\mathcal{H}
and hence lies in
\ker(\bar{L})
.

In the example above, every vertex is completely constrained: no vertex
can move in any direction. However, this phenomenon can be localized to
some directions but not others. For instance, if we add a new vertex
v

and connect it to a neighbor
u
in the original graph, then the motion
of
v
is constrained to lie in the subspace orthogonal to
{\bf p}_{uv}
.
As before, such behavior cannot occur in finite frameworks, because
trivial motions are always present. The following lemma characterizes the
forbidden directions for a vertex.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
(G,{\bf p})
be a self-adjoint
d
-framework, and
v\in V(G)
a vertex. Let
P:\mathcal{H}\to\ker\bar{L}(G,{\bf p})

be the orthogonal projection, and
P_{v,v}
the
d\times d
block corresponding to
v
. Then, for every
x\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
,

x\in\ker P_{v,v}\iff\langle x,q(v)\rangle=0,~~\forall q\in\ker\bar{L}(G,{\bf p})\,.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{H}
be defined by
f(v)=x
and
f(u)=0
for every
u\neq v
. Then,

\|Pf\|^{2}=\langle f,Pf\rangle=\langle x,(Pf)(v)\rangle=\langle x,P_{v,v}x\rangle\,.

Therefore,
P_{v,v}x=0
if and only if
Pf=0
. Since
P
is an orthogonal projection, this is equivalent to the condition that for every
q\in\ker\bar{L}(G,{\bf p}),

\langle x,q(v)\rangle=\langle f,q\rangle=0\,,

as claimed.
∎

3.2.
Random frameworks

Due to the illustrated subtleties in generic rigidity of infinite frameworks,
and because it is better suited to our methods, we shall often restrict the class of embeddings considered in this work as follows.
Given a possibly random graph
G
and a probability measure
\nu
on

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write
(G,\nu)
for the random
d
-framework
(G,{\bf p})

in which the vectors
{\bf p}(v)
,
v\in V(G)
, are sampled independently
from
\nu
.

We shall mostly consider probability measures
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that we
call
admissible
: namely,
\nu
is supported on a bounded nonempty
open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and is absolutely continuous with respect to
Lebesgue measure on
U
. For example, one may take
\nu
to be the
uniform distribution on
(0,1)^{d}
.

4.
Infinitesimal local flexibility

We now introduce the notion of local flexibility, which plays a central
role in this work. Although the notion can be defined for finite
frameworks, we formulate it in a more general setting.

Let
(G,{\bf p})
be a self-adjoint
d
-framework.
Recall that

P=\mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}(\bar{L}):\mathcal{H}\to\ker(\bar{L})

and we denote by
P_{v,v}
the real
d\times d
block of
P
corresponding to a vertex
v
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
(G,{\bf p})
be a self-adjoint
d
-framework and
v\in V(G)
. The
local flexibility
of
v
in
(G,{\bf p})
is defined by

\phi_{G,{\bf p}}(v)={\mathrm{tr}}(P_{v,v})\,.

Observe that an alternative description of the local flexibility is given by

\phi_{G,{\bf p}}(v)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mu_{e_{v,j}}(\{0\})\,,

where
e_{v,j}
is the unit vector of the
j
-th coordinate in the block corresponding to
v
, and
\mu_{e_{v,j}}
is the spectral measure of
\bar{L}
with respect to
e_{v,j}
.

When the context is clear, we sometimes omit the subscript
G,{\bf p}
in
\phi
.

Intuitively, the local flexibility quantifies the degrees of freedom that
v
has in the framework.
In particular, it is easy to see that
d-\deg_{G}(v)\leq\phi(v)\leq d
. In a finite graph,
\phi(v)>0
due to the trivial motions.
But, Example
3.4
shows that the local flexibility can vanish in infinite graphs.

Crucially, by standard linear algebra, if
G
is finite then the dimension of the kernel of the rigidity matrix
R
is given by

\dim Z(G,{\bf p})=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\phi_{G,{\bf p}}(v)\,.

(4.1)

Hence, we may view the local flexibility of
v
as the local “contribution” of
v
to the dimension
Z
.

4.1.
Upper semi-continuity under Benjamini–Schramm convergence

We now show that the local flexibility
\phi_{G,{\bf p}}(v)
is a
local parameter
. That is, it is upper semi-continuous
under Benjamini–Schramm convergence. This is a crucial property for our applications, as it allows us to deduce results about the local flexibility of finite graphs from results about the local flexibility of their infinite local limit.

This is a direct adaptation to the rigidity Laplacian of previous results on the weak convergence of spectral measures of graph operators under local weak convergence (e.g.,
[
10
,
1
]
).

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that
(G,o)
is the local weak limit of a graph sequence
G_{n}
. Let
\nu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for which
(G,\nu)
and
(G_{n},\nu)
are all almost surely self-adjoint. Then,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{G_{n},\nu}(o_{n})]\leq\mathbb{E}[\phi_{G,\nu}(o)]\,,

where
o_{n}\in V(G_{n})
is a uniform random vertex.

Proof.

By the Skorokhod representation theorem, we may assume that
(G_{n},o_{n})\to(G,o)
almost surely in
\mathcal{G}_{\bullet}
. By choosing appropriate embeddings of all graphs into a common vertex set
V
, we may further assume that
o_{n}=o\in V
for all
n
, and that for every
r\geq 2
there exists
n_{r}
such that, almost surely,

B_{G_{n}}(o,r)=B_{G}(o,r)\qquad\text{for all }n\geq n_{r}.

(4.2)

Fix
j\in[d]
and let
e_{j}=e_{o,j}\in\ell_{2}(V;\mathbb{C}^{d})
be the unit vector corresponding to the
j
-th coordinate at the root
o
.

Let
{\bf p}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be such that
{\bf p}(v)
,
v\in V(G)
, are independent samples of
\nu
, and denote
{\bf p}_{n}={\bf p}|_{V(G_{n})}
.
By assumption, the frameworks
(G_{n},{\bf p}_{n})
and
(G,{\bf p})
are almost surely self-adjoint. Hence the associated rigidity Laplacians
\bar{L}_{n}=\bar{L}(G_{n},{\bf p}_{n})
and
\bar{L}=\bar{L}(G,{\bf p})
admit spectral measures
\mu^{(n)}_{e_{j}}
and
\mu_{e_{j}}
with respect to
e_{j}
.

From (
4.2
) and the assumption
{\bf p}_{n}={\bf p}|_{V(G_{n})}
, we deduce that for every
f\in D
,
L_{n}f=Lf
for all sufficiently large
n
. Since
D
is a core for all
\bar{L}_{n}
and
\bar{L}
,
[
40
, Theorem VIII.25(a)]
implies that
\bar{L}_{n}\to\bar{L}
in the strong resolvent sense. Therefore, we obtain almost sure weak convergence of the spectral measures
\mu^{(n)}_{e_{j}}\Rightarrow\mu_{e_{j}}
. By the Portmanteau theorem,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu^{(n)}_{e_{j}}(\{0\})\leq\mu_{e_{j}}(\{0\})\,.

Summing over
j=1,\dots,d
and recalling that
\phi_{G,{\bf p}}(o)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mu_{e_{o,j}}(\{0\})
, we obtain, almost surely,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\phi_{G_{n},{\bf p}_{n}}(o_{n})\leq\phi_{G,{\bf p}}(o)\,.

The proof then follows by Fatou’s lemma, using that
0\leq\phi_{G_{n},{\bf p}_{n}}(o_{n})\leq d
.
∎

5.
Local flexibility in trees

5.1.
Self-adjointness of trees

From a classical rigidity-theory perspective, finite trees are fairly easy to analyze and are usually not particularly interesting. However, from the perspective of local flexibility, trees—and infinite trees in particular—are nontrivial and very interesting to study.

We start this investigation by formulating a useful sufficient condition for self-adjointness of frameworks whose underlying graph
is an infinite tree
T
. We say that a finite subset
S\subset V(T)
that induces a connected subtree is
C
-bounded, for some
C>0
, if no vertex in
S
has more than
C
neighbors outside of
S
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
(T,o)
be a locally finite rooted tree and
{\bf p}:V(T)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\{{\bf p}_{uv}\mid uv\in E\}
is bounded. If there exists
C>0
such that
V(T)
is covered by
C
-bounded subsets containing
o
, then
(T,{\bf p})
is self-adjoint.

Proof.

By
[
40
, Theorem VIII.3]
, to show that
\bar{L}=\bar{L}(T,{\bf p})
is self-adjoint it suffices to prove that there is no non-zero vector
\psi
in the domain of
\bar{L}^{*}
for which
L^{*}\psi=\pm i\psi
. We consider the case
\bar{L}^{*}\psi=-i\psi
, and note that the proof of the other case is nearly identical. For a vertex
u
, let
\psi_{u}\in D
be defined by
\psi_{u}(u)=\psi(u)
and
\psi_{u}(v)=0
for every
v\neq u
. Therefore,

\bar{L}\psi_{u}(v)=L\psi_{u}(v)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{w\sim u}\langle\psi(u),{\bf p}_{uw}\rangle{\bf p}_{uw},&u=v,\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle-\,\langle\psi(u),{\bf p}_{uv}\rangle{\bf p}_{uv},&\{u,v\}\in E,\\[4.30554pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

Hence,

\langle\bar{L}\psi_{u},\psi\rangle=\sum_{v\sim u}\langle\psi(u),{\bf p}_{uv}\rangle\langle{\bf p}_{uv},\psi(u)-\psi(v)\rangle\,.

On the other hand, by our assumption on
\psi
,

\langle\bar{L}\psi_{u},\psi\rangle=\langle\psi_{u},\bar{L}^{*}\psi\rangle=+i\|\psi(u)\|^{2}\,.

Therefore, for any finite subset
S\subset V(T)
there holds

i\sum_{u\in S}\|\psi(u)\|^{2}=\sum_{uv\in E(S)}|\langle\psi(u)-\psi(v),{\bf p}_{uv}\rangle|^{2}+\sum_{uv\in E(S,S^{c})}\langle\psi(u),{\bf p}_{uv}\rangle\langle{\bf p}_{uv},\psi(u)-\psi(v)\rangle\,.

Here
E(S)
denotes the edges induced by subset
S
, and
uv\in E(S,S^{c})
denotes that
u\in S,v\notin S
and
u\sim v
. By considering the imaginary part of this equality and applying the triangle and Cauchy–Schwarz (CS) inequalities we obtain

\sum_{u\in S}\|\psi(u)\|^{2}\leq\sum_{uv\in E(S,S^{c})}\|\psi(u)\|\cdot\|{\bf p}_{uv}\|^{2}\cdot\|\psi(v)\|\,.

(5.1)

Suppose that
S
is
C
-bounded, and that
\|{\bf p}_{uv}\|^{2}\leq C
for all
uv\in E
. In such a case, we find by applying CS twice that

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in S}\|\psi(u)\|^{2}\leq~

\displaystyle C\cdot\sum_{u\in S}\|\psi(u)\|\cdot C^{1/2}\cdot\left(\sum_{v\sim u,v\notin S}\|\psi(v)\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle C^{1.5}\cdot\left(\sum_{u\in S}\|\psi(u)\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\cdot\left(\sum_{u\in S}\sum_{v\sim u,v\notin S}\|\psi(v)\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle C^{1.5}\cdot\left(\sum_{u\in S}\|\psi(u)\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\cdot\left(\sum_{v\notin S}\|\psi(v)\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\,.

The last inequality follows from the assumption that
S
induces a connected subtree, hence every
v\notin S
has at most one neighbor in
S
. A straightforward manipulation yields

\sum_{u\in S}\|\psi(u)\|^{2}\leq\frac{C^{3}}{C^{3}+1}\|\psi\|^{2}\,.

On the other hand, the family of
C
-bounded subsets that contain the root is closed under taking finite unions, whence our assumption that
V(T)
is covered by such sets implies that the supremum of
\sum_{u\in S}\|\psi(u)\|^{2}
over all
C
-bounded subsets
S
is
\|\psi\|^{2}
, yielding a contradiction.
∎

5.2.
Forward flexibility matrices and their recursive formula

For a rooted tree
(T,o)
and a vertex
v\in V(T)
we denote by
T^{v}
the subtree of
T
induced by
v
and its descendants — all the vertices
u
for which the shortest path in
T
from
o
to
u
passes through
v
—
and view it as rooted at
v
.
In addition, for an embedding
{\bf p}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote by
{\bf p}^{v}={\bf p}|_{T^{v}}
.

It is easy to see that if
(T,{\bf p})
is self-adjoint then so is
(T^{v},{\bf p}^{v})
. In such a case, we consider the orthogonal projection

P^{v}=\mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}(\bar{L}(T^{v},{\bf p}^{v})):\ell_{2}(V(T^{v});\mathbb{C}^{d})\to\ker(\bar{L}(T^{v},{\bf p}^{v}))\,,

and call its restriction
Q_{v}=(P^{v})_{vv}
to the
d\times d
block corresponding to the vertex
v
the
forward flexibility matrix
of
v
in
T
.
Note that
Q_{v}
has real entries, and recall that its trace is the local flexibility of
v
in
(T^{v},{\bf p}^{v})
.
In addition, by Lemma
3.5
, the kernel of
Q_{v}
consists of the forbidden directions for
v
in this framework
(T^{v},{\bf p}^{v})
.

The next theorem finds a recursive formula for computing
Q_{o}
using the vectors
{\bf p}_{ov}
and the matrices
Q_{v}
, over all children
v
of the root
o
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
(T,{\bf p})
be a self-adjoint
d
-framework whose underlying graph
T
is a tree rooted at
o
. Denote by

v_{1},\dots,v_{m}
the children of the root
o
. Let

H=\{\,i\in[m]\mid Q_{v_{i}}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}=0\,\},

W\leq\mathbb{C}^{d}
be the orthogonal complement of

\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}\mid i\in H\}
, and
P^{W}:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to W
be the orthogonal projection. Then,

(1)

Q_{o}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}=0
for every
i\in H
.

(2)

Q_{o}|_{W}=\left(\mathrm{Id}_{W}+\sum_{i\notin H}\frac{P^{W}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}(P^{W}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}})^{*}}{{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}Q_{v_{i}}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}}\right)^{-1}.

Proof.

Write
\bar{L}=\bar{L}(T,{\bf p})
and
\bar{L}^{v_{i}}=\bar{L}(T^{v_{i}},{\bf p}^{v_{i}})
for
i\in[m]
.
For the first item, let
i\in H
. For every
q\in\ker\bar{L}
there holds that
q|_{T^{v_{i}}}\in\ker\bar{L}^{v_{i}}
by
the characterization of the kernel in Lemma
3.2
. Hence, by Lemma
3.5
,

\langle q(v_{i}),{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}\rangle=\langle q|_{T^{v_{i}}}(v_{i}),{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}\rangle=0\,,

due to the assumption that
i\in H
. On the other hand, by Lemma
3.2
we have

\langle q(o)-q(v_{i}),{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}\rangle=0\,.

Therefore,
\langle q(o),{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}\rangle=0
. The claim follows from Lemma
3.5
.

We proceed to the second item. Let
P
be the orthogonal projection onto
\ker\bar{L}
.
For
z=\mathrm{i}\cdot s
with
s>0
real, set

R(z):=(\bar{L}-zI)^{-1}.

Define
h_{z}(\lambda):=-z(\lambda-z)^{-1}
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. By the functional calculus,

h_{z}(\bar{L})=-zR(z)
is a bounded operator. For
\lambda\neq 0
we have
h_{z}(\lambda)\to 0
as
z\to 0
, while
h_{z}(0)=1
.
Moreover,

|h_{z}(\lambda)|=\frac{|z|}{|\lambda-z|}\leq 1\,,

since
z
is purely imaginary. Thus
h_{z}\to\mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}
pointwise and
(h_{z})
is uniformly bounded, so by dominated
convergence in the spectral theorem,

h_{z}(\bar{L})f\to Pf\qquad\text{for every }f\in\mathcal{H}

as
z\to 0
. In particular, applying this entrywise to the
d\times d
block at
o
gives

-zR(z)_{o,o}\to P_{o,o}=Q_{o}\quad\mbox{as }z\to 0.

Similarly, let

R^{\prime}(z):=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bar{L}^{v_{i}}-zI\right)^{-1}.

Since
T
is a tree,
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bar{L}^{v_{i}}
is a direct sum, hence so is
R^{\prime}(z)
, and therefore for each
i\in[m]
,

-zR^{\prime}(z)_{v_{i}v_{i}}\to Q_{v_{i}}\quad\mbox{as }z\to 0.

We now derive the recursion. We can write

\bar{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bar{L}^{v_{i}}+M,

where
M
is the rigidity Laplacian of the star at
o
.
More explicitly, by letting
N_{i}={\bf p}_{ov_{i}}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}
for
i\in[m]
, we have

M_{uv}=\begin{cases}\sum_{j=1}^{m}N_{j},&(u,v)=(o,o),\\
-N_{i},&(u,v)=(o,v_{i})\ \text{or}\ (v_{i},o),\\
N_{i},&(u,v)=(v_{i},v_{i}),\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

By the tree structure,

R^{\prime}_{o,o}(z)=-z^{-1}I,\qquad R^{\prime}_{o,v_{i}}(z)=0,\qquad R^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{j}}(z)=0\ \ (i\neq j).

(5.2)

The second resolvent identity gives
R(z)-R^{\prime}(z)=-R(z)MR^{\prime}(z)
(we sometimes omit the dependence on
z
for brevity).
Recalling (
5.2
), we obtain that for every
i\in[m]
,

\displaystyle R_{o,v_{i}}

\displaystyle=-R_{o,o}M_{o,v_{i}}R^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}}-R_{o,v_{i}}M_{v_{i},v_{i}}R^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}}

\displaystyle=R_{o,o}N_{i}R^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}}-R_{o,v_{i}}N_{i}R^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}}.

so

R_{o,v_{i}}=R_{o,o}N_{i}R^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}}\,(I+N_{i}R^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}})^{-1}.

(5.3)

Note that in (
5.3
) we used that
I+N_{i}R^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}}
is invertible. This holds since
N_{i}={\bf p}_{ov_{i}}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}
, whence matrix determinant lemma yields

\det(I+N_{i}R^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}})=1+{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}R^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}.

For
z=\mathrm{i}s
with
s>0
, since
R^{\prime}
is the resolvent of a self-adjoint operator, we have

\Im\bigl({\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}R^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}\bigr)>0.

In particular,
{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}R^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}\notin\mathbb{R}
,
hence the determinant above is nonzero.

Next, using Sherman–Morrison for rank-one updates, (
5.3
) simplifies to

R_{o,v_{i}}=R_{o,o}\cdot\frac{N_{i}R^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}}}{1+{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}R^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}}\,.

(5.4)

Applying again
R-R^{\prime}=-RMR^{\prime}
for the
(o,o)
block, and using (
5.2
), gives

\displaystyle R_{o,o}+z^{-1}I

\displaystyle=-R_{o,o}M_{o,o}R^{\prime}_{o,o}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}R_{o,v_{i}}M_{v_{i},o}R^{\prime}_{o,o}

\displaystyle=z^{-1}\left(R_{o,o}\sum_{i=1}^{m}N_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}R_{o,v_{i}}N_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=z^{-1}R_{o,o}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(N_{i}-\frac{N_{i}R^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}}N_{i}}{1+{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}R^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}}\right)

\displaystyle=z^{-1}R_{o,o}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{N_{i}}{1+{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}R^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}}\,,

where in the third equality we used (
5.4
), and in the fourth equality we used

N_{i}R^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}}N_{i}=({\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}R^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}})\,N_{i}
.
Rearranging yields the recursion

-zR_{o,o}(z)\left(I+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}}{-z+{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}(-zR^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}}(z)){\bf p}_{ov_{i}}}\right)=I.

(5.5)

Now fix
w\in W
. Since
{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}w=0
for
i\in H
, multiplying (
5.5
) by
w
gives

-zR_{o,o}(z)\left(w+\sum_{i\notin H}\frac{{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}w}{-z+{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}(-zR^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}}(z)){\bf p}_{ov_{i}}}\right)=w.

Letting
z\to 0
along the imaginary axis, we use
-zR_{o,o}(z)\to Q_{o}
and
-zR^{\prime}_{v_{i},v_{i}}(z)\to Q_{v_{i}}
.
Moreover, for
i\notin H
we have
{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}Q_{v_{i}}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}\neq 0
by definition of
H
, hence the denominators converge to

{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}Q_{v_{i}}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}
. Therefore,

Q_{o}\left(w+\sum_{i\notin H}\frac{{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}w}{{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}Q_{v_{i}}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}}\right)=w\,.

Finally, by the first item we have
Q_{o}P^{W}=Q_{o}
, and clearly
P^{W}w=w
for
w\in W
. Hence, replacing
{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}
by
P^{W}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}
inside the
rank-one terms does not change the action on
W
, and we obtain

Q_{o}|_{W}=\left(\mathrm{Id}_{W}+\sum_{i\notin H}\frac{P^{W}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}(P^{W}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}})^{*}}{{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}^{*}Q_{v_{i}}{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}}\right)^{-1},

as claimed.
∎

In the next lemma we study the event that in the random framework
(T,\nu)
, the forward flexibility matrix of the root
o
of
T
is trivial.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\nu
be an admissible probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
(T,o)
be a rooted tree such that
(T,\nu)
is almost surely self-adjoint.
Then, almost surely,
Q_{o}=0
if and only if
o
has at least
d
children
v
for which
Q_{v}=0
.
In particular, the event
Q_{o}=0
is independent of
{\bf p}(o)
.

Note that for a fixed tree
(T,o)
, the lemma shows that
Q_{o}=0
is a tail event, and therefore admits a
0/1
law.
However, we do not know whether the probability of this event can depend on the admissible distribution
\nu
, or whether it depends only on the tree structure.

Proof.

First, we claim that almost surely, for every edge
uv\in E(T)
with
u
the parent of
v
, we have

Q_{v}{\bf p}_{uv}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Q_{v}=0.

(5.6)

Indeed,
{\bf p}(u)
is independent of
Q_{v}
, and by our assumption on
\nu
, the vector
{\bf p}_{uv}
almost surely does not belong to
\ker Q_{v}
, unless
Q_{v}=0
.

Denote by
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
the children of the root
o
. By (
5.6
), the set
H
from Theorem
5.2
is almost surely equal to

H=\{i\in[m]\mid Q_{v_{i}}=0\}.

(5.7)

Theorem
5.2
implies that
Q_{o}=0
if and only if the subspace
W
, which is the orthogonal complement of the linear span of
\{{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}:i\in H\}
, is trivial.
By our assumption on
\nu
, this occurs if and only if
|H|\geq d
, as claimed.
∎

5.3.
Galton–Watson frameworks

We now turn to study the local flexibility of a Galton–Watson
\mathrm{GW}(X,Y)
branching process
(T,o)
as defined in Section
2
.
Here
X,Y
are probability distributions on
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
that describe the offspring distribution of the root and every other vertex respectively.
Mostly, we will be interested in the case where
Y
is the size-biasing of
X
. Also, we restrict to frameworks of the form
(T,\nu)
, where
\nu
is an admissible probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

First, we state a sufficient condition for the self-adjointness of such frameworks.

Claim 5.4
.

Let
T\sim\mathrm{GW}(X,Y)
and
\nu
be an admissible probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
If
\mathbb{E}[Y]<\infty
then
(T,\nu)
is almost surely self-adjoint.

Note that if
Y
is the size-biasing of
X
then
\mathbb{E}[Y]<\infty
if and only if
\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]<\infty
.

Proof.

There exists
C\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\sum_{m>C}m\,Y(m)<1.

(5.8)

We claim that, almost surely, for every vertex
v
there exists a finite subtree
F^{v}
of
T
, rooted at
v
, such that every leaf of
F^{v}

has at most
C
children in
T
. Clearly, it suffices to prove this for every non-root vertex
v
.

Reveal the tree
T
from
v
outward, but whenever an explored vertex has at most
C
children, declare it a leaf and do not explore its children.
Let
F^{v}
be the subtree revealed by this process. Then
F^{v}
is a Galton–Watson tree rooted at
v
with offspring distribution
Y^{\prime}
given by

Y^{\prime}(m)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}(Y\leq C),&m=0,\\
0,&0<m\leq C,\\
Y(m),&m>C,\end{cases}

with expected number of offspring equal to

\mathbb{E}[Y^{\prime}]=\sum_{m>C}m\,Y(m)<1\,.

Hence,
F^{v}
is almost surely finite. Moreover, by construction, every leaf of
F^{v}
has at most
C
children in
T
.

Let
S_{r}\subseteq V(T)
consist of
o
and the union of
F^{v}
over all vertices
v
at distance at most
r
from
o
.
Then
S_{r}
induces a connected finite subtree, contains the root
o
, and it is
C
-bounded. In addition,
\bigcup_{r\geq 0}S_{r}=V(T)
.
Since
\nu
has a bounded support, the claim follows from Lemma
5.1
.
∎

The main result of this section solves the recursion in Theorem
5.2
for Galton–Watson frameworks in which
Y
is the size-biasing of
X
,
and the embeddings are sampled independently from an admissible distribution
\nu
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Suppose that
X
is a probability distribution on
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
with a bounded second moment,
Y
the size-biasing of
X
,
T\sim\mathrm{GW}(X,Y)
with root
o
, and let
\nu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as above. Then, there exists a solution
p\in[0,1]
to
p=\mathbb{P}(Y_{p}\geq d)
such that

\mathbb{E}[\phi_{T,\nu}(o)]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{X_{p}<d}\cdot(d-X_{p})]-\frac{1}{2}(1-p)^{2}\mathbb{E}[X]\,.

(5.9)

Proof.

Let
(T^{\prime},u)
denote a
\mathrm{GW}(Y,Y)
-distributed tree rooted at
u
. Then,
(T^{\prime},\nu)
is almost surely self-adjoint by Claim
5.4
.
Let
p_{Y}=\mathbb{P}(Q_{u}=0)
, where
Q_{u}
denotes the forward flexibility matrix of
u
in
T^{\prime}
.
We claim that the number of children
v
of
u
in
T^{\prime}
for which
Q_{v}=0
is
Y_{p_{Y}}
-distributed.
Indeed, the total number of children
v
of
u
is
Y
-distributed, and the events
Q_{v}=0
are independent, each with probability
p_{Y}
, since the subtree rooted at
v
is also
\mathrm{GW}(Y,Y)
-distributed.
By Lemma
5.3
we find that

p_{Y}=\mathbb{P}\!\left(Y_{p_{Y}}\geq d\right)\,.

(5.10)

If
p_{Y}=1
, then (
5.10
) gives

\mathbb{P}(Y\geq d)=1
, and hence

\mathbb{P}(1\leq X\leq d)=0
.
Substituting
p=1
into the right-hand side of
(
5.9
) therefore gives
dX(0)
.
On the other hand, the root
o
of
T
is isolated with probability

X(0)
. In this case its local flexibility is
d
. Otherwise,
o

has at least
d+1
children, and all their forward flexibility matrices
vanish. Hence, the local flexibility at the root is
0
by
Lemma
5.3
. Thus the expected local flexibility is
dX(0)
,
so (
5.9
) holds also in this case.

So we assume
p_{Y}<1
. The following distribution plays a key role in the proof: sample a rooted
\mathrm{GW}(Y,Y)
-tree
(T^{\prime},u)
and an embedding
{\bf p}:V(T^{\prime})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
by drawing i.i.d. samples from
\nu
. Let
\mathcal{D}^{Y}
denote the law of the pair
({\bf p}(u),Q_{u})
under this procedure, conditioned on the event
Q_{u}\neq 0
.
By Lemma
5.3
, the marginal law of
{\bf p}(u)
under
\mathcal{D}^{Y}
is
\nu
, since
{\bf p}(u)
is independent of the event
Q_{u}=0
.
However, the matrix
Q_{u}
itself may depend on
{\bf p}(u)
.

We now turn our attention to
(T,o)
. Note that
(T,\nu)
is almost surely self-adjoint by Claim
5.4
.
By (
5.6
), the subset
H
from Theorem
5.2
is almost surely equal to the subset of the children
v
of the root
o
for which
Q_{v}=0
. We claim that
|H|
is
X_{p_{Y}}
-distributed. Indeed, the total number of children
v
of
o
is
X
-distributed, and, as in
T^{\prime}
, the events
Q_{v}=0
are independent of probability
p_{Y}
.

Let
({\bf p}(u_{i}),Q_{u_{i}})_{i\geq 1}
be a sequence of i.i.d. samples from
\mathcal{D}^{Y}
, and
{\bf p}(o),{\bf p}(v_{i}),~i=1,\ldots,d-1
independently drawn from
\nu
. Via Theorem
5.2
, we can sample a matrix
Q
with the same distribution as the forward flexibility matrix
Q_{o}
of
o
in
(T,\nu)

using these random vectors and matrices:
Indeed, start by sampling
(h,s)\sim(X_{p_{Y}},X_{1-p_{Y}})
.
Viewing
h
as the size of
|H|
, we set
Q=0
if
h\geq d
.
Otherwise, view

(i)

v_{1},\ldots,v_{h}
as the children of the root
o

for which
Q_{v_{i}}=0
(note that
{\bf p}(v_{i})
is

\nu
-distributed even under the conditioning
Q_{v_{i}}=0
),

(ii)

u_{1},\ldots,u_{s}
as the remaining children of the root,
and

(iii)

Q_{u_{i}}
as the forward flexibility matrix of
u_{i}

in
T
(matching the definition of
\mathcal{D}^{Y}
).

In this realization,
W
is the orthogonal complement of

\mathrm{span}\{{\bf p}_{ov_{i}}:1\leq i\leq h\},

and has dimension
d-h
. Set

A_{i}=\frac{P^{W}{\bf p}_{ou_{i}}(P^{W}{\bf p}_{ou_{i}})^{*}}{{\bf p}_{ou_{i}}^{*}Q_{u_{i}}{\bf p}_{ou_{i}}}\,,\qquad i\geq 1\,,

which is a
(d-h)\times(d-h)
matrix acting on
W
. Theorem
5.2
implies that the random
d\times d
matrix
Q
defined by
Q|_{W^{\perp}}=0
and

Q|_{W}=\Bigl(\mathrm{Id}_{W}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}A_{i}\Bigr)^{-1}

has the distribution of the forward flexibility matrix
Q_{o}
of the root
o
in
T
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathrm{tr}}\,Q_{o}]

\displaystyle=~\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{h<d}\cdot{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\left(\mathrm{Id}_{W}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}A_{i}\right)^{-1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=~\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{h<d}\cdot{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\mathrm{Id}_{W}-\sum_{i=1}^{s}A_{i}\left(\mathrm{Id}_{W}+\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[s]\setminus\{i\}}A_{i^{\prime}}+A_{i}\right)^{-1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=~\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{h<d}\cdot(d-h)]-\mathbb{E}_{s\sim X_{1-p_{Y}}}\left[s\cdot f(s)\right]\,,

(5.11)

where
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

f(s)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{h<d}\cdot{\mathrm{tr}}\left(A_{s}\left(\mathrm{Id}_{W}+\sum_{i=1}^{s-1}A_{i}+A_{s}\right)^{-1}\right)~~\middle|~~s\right]\,.

In the align, the transition to the second line is by the equality
(I+S)^{-1}=I-S(I+S)^{-1}
which holds if
I+S
is invertible, and the next transition is by linearity,
\dim W=d-h
, and the fact that the
A_{i}
’s have the same distribution.

By (
2.2
), the equality
\mathbb{E}[X_{1-p_{Y}}]=(1-p_{Y})\mathbb{E}[X]
, and the first item in Claim
2.1
, we reformulate (
5.11
) to

\mathbb{E}[{\mathrm{tr}}\,Q_{o}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{X_{p_{Y}}<d}\cdot(d-X_{p_{Y}})]-(1-p_{Y})\mathbb{E}[X]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{t\sim Y_{1-p_{Y}}}\left[f(t+1)\right]\,.

(5.12)

We turn to study the expectation of
f(t+1)
, where
t\sim Y_{1-p_{Y}}
.
By definition, when evaluating
f(t+1)
we draw

h\sim X_{p_{Y}}\mid\{X_{1-p_{Y}}=t+1\}
. Therefore, by the third item of
Claim
2.1
, the joint distribution of
(h,t)
is

(Y_{p_{Y}},Y_{1-p_{Y}})
. Once
h
is sampled, both
W
and the matrices
A_{i}
are defined via the randomness
({\bf p}(u_{i}),Q_{u_{i}})_{i\geq 1}
together with the independent samples
{\bf p}(o)
and
{\bf p}(v_{i})_{i<d}
. We denote by
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
the expectation taken over
(h,t)\sim(Y_{p_{Y}},Y_{1-p_{Y}})
and all this auxiliary randomness, and obtain

\mathbb{E}_{t\sim Y_{1-p_{Y}}}\!\left[f(t+1)\right]=\mathbb{E}^{\prime}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{h<d}\cdot{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left(A_{t+1}\left(\mathrm{Id}_{W}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}A_{i}+A_{t+1}\right)^{-1}\right)\right].

(5.13)

Condition on the value of
h<d
. In such a case, we let
B_{t}
be the matrix of order
d-h
acting on
W
defined by

B_{t}=\mathrm{Id}_{W}+\sum_{j=1}^{t}A_{j}\,.

Also, denote
u=u_{t+1}
,
Q_{u}=Q_{u_{t+1}}
for brevity. By the Sherman–Morrison formula and the definition of
A_{t+1}
, we find that

A_{t+1}(B_{t}+A_{t+1})^{-1}=\frac{A_{t+1}B_{t}^{-1}}{1+\frac{(P^{W}{\bf p}_{ou})^{*}B_{t}^{-1}(P^{W}{\bf p}_{ou})}{{\bf p}_{ou}^{*}Q_{u}{\bf p}_{ou}}}\,.

Therefore, the cyclic property of the trace and a simple manipulation give

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left[A_{t+1}(B_{t}+A_{t+1})^{-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{(P^{W}{\bf p}_{ou})^{*}B_{t}^{-1}(P^{W}{\bf p}_{ou})}{{\bf p}_{ou}^{*}Q_{u}{\bf p}_{ou}+(P^{W}{\bf p}_{ou})^{*}B_{t}^{-1}(P^{W}{\bf p}_{ou})}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\bf p}_{ou}^{*}\tilde{Q}\,{\bf p}_{ou}}{{\bf p}_{ou}^{*}Q_{u}{\bf p}_{ou}+{\bf p}_{ou}^{*}\tilde{Q}\,{\bf p}_{ou}}\,,

(5.14)

where
\tilde{Q}
denotes a
d\times d
matrix defined by
\tilde{Q}|_{W^{\perp}}=0
and
\tilde{Q}|_{W}=B_{t}^{-1}
.

By combining (
5.13
) and (
5.14
) we find

\mathbb{E}_{t\sim Y_{1-p_{Y}}}[f(t+1)]=\mathbb{E}^{\prime}(\mathbf{1}_{h<d})\mathbb{E}^{\prime}\left[\frac{{\bf p}_{ou}^{*}\tilde{Q}\,{\bf p}_{ou}}{{\bf p}_{ou}^{*}Q_{u}{\bf p}_{ou}+{\bf p}_{ou}^{*}\tilde{Q}\,{\bf p}_{ou}}~~\middle|~~h<d\right]\,.

(5.15)

Crucially, observe that, by combining Theorem
5.2
and the definition of
B_{t}
, we can view
\tilde{Q}
as a forward flexibility matrix. More precisely, since
(h,t)\sim(Y_{p_{Y}},Y_{1-p_{Y}})
, we derive that, conditioned on
h<d
, the pair
({\bf p}(o),\tilde{Q})
is
\mathcal{D}^{Y}
-distributed.
In addition, it is independent of the
\mathcal{D}^{Y}
-distributed pair
({\bf p}(u),Q_{u})
(which by definition denotes the pair
({\bf p}(u_{t+1}),Q_{u_{t+1}})
).
Therefore, conditioned on
h<d
, the pair of positive random variables

{\bf p}_{ou}^{*}Q_{u}{\bf p}_{ou}
and

{\bf p}_{ou}^{*}\tilde{Q}\,{\bf p}_{ou}

are exchangeable: indeed, the pairs
({\bf p}(u),Q_{u})
and
({\bf p}(o),\tilde{Q})
are independent and
\mathcal{D}^{Y}
-distributed, and exchanging them only replaces
{\bf p}_{ou}
by
-{\bf p}_{ou}
. Hence,

\mathbb{E}^{\prime}\left[\frac{{\bf p}_{ou}^{*}\tilde{Q}\,{\bf p}_{ou}}{{\bf p}_{ou}^{*}Q_{u}{\bf p}_{ou}+{\bf p}_{ou}^{*}\tilde{Q}\,{\bf p}_{ou}}~~\middle|~~h<d\right]=\frac{1}{2}\,.

Combined with
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}[\mathbf{1}_{h<d}]=1-p_{Y}
which follows from (
5.10
), we find that (
5.15
) reformulates to

\mathbb{E}_{t\sim Y_{1-p_{Y}}}[f(t+1)]=\frac{1-p_{Y}}{2}\,.

Substituting this equality into (
5.12
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\mathrm{tr}}\,Q_{o}]

\displaystyle=~\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{X_{p_{Y}}<d}\cdot(d-X_{p_{Y}})]-\frac{1}{2}(1-p_{Y})^{2}\mathbb{E}[X]\,,

and together with (
5.10
), the theorem follows.

∎

6.
Applications

We now apply Theorem
5.5
to several families of both random and non-random graphs.

6.1.
Random graphs with a given degree distribution

In this subsection we describe a fairly broad family of graph sequences for which the trivial upper bound (
1.1
) for the
d
-rigidity rank is attained asymptotically.
This general treatment may have further applications, and may also shed light on the connection between the rigidity rank and the
(d+1)
-core in random graphs. We stress that some of the assumptions below are likely not optimal. However, since weakening them is not needed for the main theorems of the paper, we have not pursued this further.

Let
X
be a probability distribution on
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
and
Y
its size-biasing.
Assume that
X
has finite first and second moments. Denote by

\phi(p)=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{1}_{X_{p}<d}\,(d-X_{p})\big]-\frac{1}{2}(1-p)^{2}\mathbb{E}[X]

the right-hand side of (
5.9
). In addition, let
\hat{p}=\hat{p}(X)
be the largest solution in
[0,1]
for (
5.10
). We note that in Section
1
, the notation
\hat{p}
was used
for this same quantity in the special case
X=\operatorname{Poi}(c)
. The following condition on
X
will be used below.

Condition 6.1
.

\phi(p)
attains its maximum in
[0,1]
at either
p=0
or
p=\hat{p}
.

In the next subsections we show that the distributions
X
relevant to our main theorems (
X=\operatorname{Poi}(c)
and
X\equiv k
) satisfy Condition
6.1
using the following claim.

Claim 6.2
.

If
\phi^{\prime\prime}(p)
is unimodal, i.e., there exists
p_{*}\in[0,1]
such that
\phi^{\prime\prime}
is increasing on
(0,p_{*}]

and decreasing on
[p_{*},1)
, then Condition
6.1
holds.

Proof.

Straightforward calculations show that

\phi^{\prime}(p)\;=\;\mathbb{E}[X]\cdot\Big(\mathbb{P}\big(Y_{p}\geq d\big)-p\Big)\,,

and

\phi^{\prime\prime}(p)=\mathbb{E}[X]\cdot\left(p^{-1}d\cdot\mathbb{P}(Y_{p}=d)-1\right)\,,

(6.1)

for
0<p\leq 1
.

Note that since
d\geq 2
, we have
\phi^{\prime\prime}(0)=-\mathbb{E}[X]<0
,
\phi^{\prime}(0)=0
and
\phi^{\prime}(1)=\mathbb{E}[X]\big(\mathbb{P}(Y\geq d)-1\big)\leq 0
. Since
\phi^{\prime\prime}
is first non-decreasing and then non-increasing, and since

\phi^{\prime\prime}(0)<0
, the derivative
\phi^{\prime}
can have at most one local minimum
and at most one local maximum. Hence
\phi^{\prime}=0
has at most three solutions. If there is a unique solution
\hat{p}=0
then
\phi^{\prime}
is negative in
(0,1]
whence
\phi
is maximized at
0
. If there are two solutions:
0
and
\hat{p}
, then
\phi^{\prime}
is negative in
(0,\hat{p})
and in
(\hat{p},1]
whence
\phi
is maximized at
0
. If there are three solutions:
0,p_{1}
and
\hat{p}
, then the sign pattern of
\phi^{\prime}
is
(-,+,-)
on the intervals
(0,p_{1})
,
(p_{1},\hat{p})
,
(\hat{p},1]
, whence
\phi
is maximized either at
0
or at
\hat{p}
.
∎

It turns out that, in many cases, deciding whether
p=0
or
p=\hat{p}
maximizes

\phi
is precisely the question of whether the
(d+1)
-core of a corresponding random graph model has edge density smaller or greater than
d
. In such cases, maximizing
\phi
is the same as optimizing the
right-hand side of (
1.1
).

Figure 3
.

The function
\phi(p)
for
d=3
and
X=\operatorname{Poi}(c)
, where

c=5.1,5.5,5.9
. On the left,
c<\gamma_{3}
, and
p=0
is the only
solution of
\phi^{\prime}(p)=0
. In the middle,
\gamma_{3}<c<c_{3}
, two
additional critical points appear, but the value at
p=0
is still larger
than the value at
\hat{p}
. On the right,
c>c_{3}
, and the maximum is
attained at
\hat{p}
.

Definition 6.3
.

A (possibly random) graph sequence
G_{n}
with
|V(G_{n})|\to\infty
is called
(X,d)
-typical if the following conditions hold.

•

The local weak limit of
G_{n}
is the
\mathrm{GW}(X,Y)
-distributed rooted tree.

•

The number
m
of edges in
G_{n}
satisfies that a.a.s.
m=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[X]n+o(n).

•

Let
n_{d+1},~m_{d+1}
denote the number of vertices and edges in the
(d+1)
-core of
G_{n}
. Then, a.a.s.,

{n_{d+1}}=\mathbb{P}(X_{\hat{p}}\geq d+1)n+o(n)\,,\,\,m_{d+1}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]\hat{p}^{2}}{2}n+o(n)\,.

(6.2)

For example, every essentially high-girth family of
k
-regular graphs is

(k,d)
-typical. In addition,
G(n,c/n)
is

(\operatorname{Poi}(c),d)
-typical for every
c\neq\gamma_{d}

by
[
39
]
(their proof does not apply at
criticality).
More generally, the parameters in (
6.2
) appear in the work of Janson and Luczak on the
k
-core of random graphs
[
22
]
. Let
(d_{i})_{i\in[n]}
be non-negative integers with even sum such that
\sum_{i}\mathbf{1}_{d_{i}=s}=X(s)n+o(n)
as
n\to\infty
for every fixed
s
. Let
G_{n}
be the configuration-model random graph with degree sequence
(d_{i})
: attach
d_{i}
half-edges to vertex
i
, and pair all half-edges uniformly at random. It is proved in
[
22
]
that, under some technical assumptions,
G_{n}
is
(X,d)
-typical.

The next claim relates the two candidate values for maximizing
\phi
with the parameters of the
(d+1)
-core from (
6.2
).

Claim 6.4
.

Let
G_{n}
be an
n
-vertex
(X,d)
-typical (possibly random) graph. Then, a.a.s.,

(1)

dn-m=\phi(0)\cdot n+o(n)
, and

(2)

dn-m-dn_{d+1}+m_{d+1}=\phi(\hat{p})\cdot n+o(n)
.

In particular,
\phi(\hat{p})>\phi(0)
if and only if the asymptotic edge density in the
(d+1)
-core of
G_{n}
is greater than
d
.

Proof.

The proof follows a straightforward analysis. Note that by local weak convergence,

(1)

Since
X_{p}=0
if
p=0
, we have that
\phi(0)=d-\mathbb{E}[X]/2
. Hence, the claim follows from the second item in Definition
6.3
.

(2)

If
\hat{p}=0
, then
n_{d+1},m_{d+1}=o(n)
and the claim follows directly from the first item of this claim. Otherwise, note that
\mathbf{1}_{X_{p}<d}(d-X_{p})=d-\min(X_{p},d)
, so we derive

\phi(\hat{p})=d-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{X_{\hat{p}}\leq d}X_{\hat{p}}]-d\cdot\mathbb{P}(X_{\hat{p}}\geq d+1)-\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{2}+\hat{p}\mathbb{E}[X]-\frac{\hat{p}^{2}\mathbb{E}[X]}{2}.

We have
\hat{p}\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[X_{\hat{p}}]
, hence

-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{X_{\hat{p}}\leq d}X_{\hat{p}}]+\hat{p}\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{X_{\hat{p}}\geq d+1}X_{\hat{p}}]\,.

In addition, using the fact that
Y_{\hat{p}}
is the size-biasing of
X_{\hat{p}}
and (
2.2
), we find that

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{X_{\hat{p}}\geq d+1}X_{\hat{p}}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{\hat{p}}]\mathbb{P}(Y_{\hat{p}}\geq d)=\hat{p}^{2}\mathbb{E}[X]\,,

(6.3)

where the last equality follows from the fact that
\hat{p}
is a solution of (
5.10
). By combining these equalities we find that

\phi(\hat{p})=d-\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{2}-d\cdot\mathbb{P}(X_{\hat{p}}\geq d+1)+\frac{\hat{p}^{2}\mathbb{E}[X]}{2}\,,

and the claim follows.

∎

The main theorem of this subsection shows that under the assumptions above, (
1.1
) is asymptotically attained.

Theorem 6.5
.

Suppose that
X
satisfies Condition
6.1
, and
let
G_{n}
be an
n
-vertex
(X,d)
-typical (possibly random) graph. Then, a.a.s.,

\mathrm{rk}_{d}(G_{n})=m+\min\left(0,\,dn_{d+1}-m_{d+1}\right)+o(n)\,.

We start with the following easy claim in convergence of random variables.

Claim 6.6
.

Let
w
be a real number, and suppose that
Z_{n},W_{n}
are sequences of random variables satisfying
\max(0,W_{n})\leq Z_{n}
,
W_{n}\xrightarrow{p}w
and
\limsup\mathbb{E}Z_{n}\leq w
. Then
Z_{n}\xrightarrow{p}w
.

Proof.

Since
0\leq Z_{n}
, necessarily
w\geq 0
. For every
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}<w-\varepsilon)\leq\mathbb{P}(W_{n}<w-\varepsilon)\to 0.

Also,

\mathbb{E}Z_{n}\geq(w-\varepsilon)\mathbb{P}(W_{n}\geq w-\varepsilon)+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\geq w+\sqrt{\varepsilon},\ W_{n}\geq w-\varepsilon).

Taking limsup gives

w\geq(w-\varepsilon)+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\geq w+\sqrt{\varepsilon})\,,

hence
Z_{n}\to w
in probability.
∎

Proof of Theorem
6.5
.

Sample
{\bf p}_{n}:V(G_{n})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
by drawing each
{\bf p}(v)
from some admissible probability distribution
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let

Z_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\dim Z(G_{n},{\bf p}_{n})\geq 0\,,

and

W_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\max(dn-m,\,dn-m-dn_{d+1}+m_{d+1})\,.

First, since
{\bf p}_{n}
is almost surely generic,

\mathrm{rk}_{d}(G_{n})=dn-\dim Z(G_{n},{\bf p}_{n})

holds almost surely. Hence, by
(
1.1
), we find that
Z_{n}\geq W_{n}
holds almost surely.
Moreover, by Condition
6.1
and Claim
6.4
, we have that, as
n\to\infty
,

W_{n}\xrightarrow{p}\max\{\phi(p):p\in[0,1]\}\,.

(6.4)

Finally, equation (
4.1
) implies that

\dim Z(G_{n},{\bf p}_{n})=\sum_{o_{n}\in V(G_{n})}\phi_{G_{n},{\bf p}_{n}}(o_{n})\,.

In addition, since the local weak limit of
G_{n}
is a
\mathrm{GW}(X,Y)
-distributed tree
(T,o)
, we find by Lemma
4.2
and Theorem
5.5
that

\limsup\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}]=\limsup\mathbb{E}[\phi_{G_{n},{\bf p}_{n}}(o_{n})]\leq\mathbb{E}[\phi_{T,\nu}(o)]\leq\max\{\phi(p):p\in[0,1]\}\,.

By the previous claim, we find that a.a.s.

\dim Z(G_{n},{\bf p}_{n})=\max(dn-m,\,dn-m-(dn_{d+1}-m_{d+1}))+o(n)\,,

and the claim follows.
∎

We derive an analog corollary on the local flexibility of Galton–Watson trees.

Corollary 6.7
.

Suppose that
X
satisfies Condition
6.1
and that there exists a sequence of
(X,d)
-typical graphs. Let
(T,o)
be a
\mathrm{GW}(X,Y)
-distributed tree and
\nu
an admissible probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then,

\mathbb{E}[\phi_{T,\nu}(o)]=d-\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{2}+\max\left(0,-d\cdot\mathbb{P}(X_{\hat{p}}\geq d+1)+\frac{\hat{p}^{2}\mathbb{E}[X]}{2}\right)\,.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.5
,

\mathbb{E}[\phi_{T,\nu}(o)]\leq\max\{\phi(p):p\in[0,1]\}\,,

and by Condition
6.1
the maximum is attained at either
0
or

\hat{p}
. Additionally, the proof of Claim
6.4
gives

\phi(0)=d-\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{2}

and

\phi(\hat{p})=d-\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{2}-d\mathbb{P}(X_{\hat{p}}\geq d+1)+\frac{\hat{p}^{2}\mathbb{E}[X]}{2}.

It remains to show that the upper bound is attained. Indeed, let

G_{n}
be an
(X,d)
-typical graph sequence, and let
{\bf p}_{n}:V(G_{n})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be sampled according to

\nu
. By the local weak convergence of
G_{n}
to
\mathrm{GW}(X,Y)

and Lemma
4.2
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\phi_{T,\nu}(o)]\geq\limsup\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\dim Z(G_{n},{\bf p}_{n})=\max\{\phi(p):p\in[0,1]\},

where the last equality follows from the proof of Theorem

6.5
.
∎

6.2.
Erdős–Rényi random graphs

We proceed to the proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
regarding the
d
-rigidity phase
transition in
G(n,p)
.

Let
c>0
be fixed, and let
X=\operatorname{Poi}(c)
. In this case, the shifted size-biasing

Y
of
X
is also
\operatorname{Poi}(c)
. We first note that
X
satisfies Condition
6.1
. Indeed, since
Y\sim\operatorname{Poi}(c)
, we have

Y_{p}\sim\operatorname{Poi}(cp)
. Hence, using (
6.1
), we find that

\phi^{\prime\prime}(p)=c\left(\frac{e^{-cp}c^{d}p^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}-1\right)\,.

This function is unimodal in
[0,1]
and the claim follows from Claim
6.2
.

It is well-known that
G(n,c/n)
converges locally weakly to
\mathrm{GW}(X,Y)
, and the parameters of its
(d+1)
-core were found in
[
39
]
, showing it is
(X,d)
-typical for all
c\neq\gamma_{d}
.

Consequently, the following corollary is a special case of Theorem
6.5
.

Corollary 6.8
.

Let
c>0
be fixed, and let
G\sim G(n,c/n)
. Then, a.a.s.,

•

If
c<c_{d}
then
\mathrm{rk}_{d}(G)=(1+o(1))\frac{c}{2}n.

•

If
c>c_{d}
then

\mathrm{rk}_{d}(G)=(1+o(1))\left(\frac{c}{2}+d\,\mathbb{P}\big(\operatorname{Poi}(c\hat{p})\geq d+1\big)-\frac{c\hat{p}^{2}}{2}\right)n\,.

Formally, we are not able to apply Theorem
6.5
for
c=\gamma_{d}
, but the statement follows from the monotonicity of the rank.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The second item is already proved in Corollary
6.8
, so we focus on the first item.
Let
c<c_{d}
, and choose

c<c^{\prime}<c_{d}
. We sample
G^{\prime}\sim G(n,c^{\prime}/n)
, and obtain

G\sim G(n,c/n)
by retaining each edge of
G^{\prime}
independently with
probability
c/c^{\prime}
. By Corollary
6.8
, we have a.a.s.

\mathrm{rk}_{d}(G^{\prime})=(1+o(1))\frac{c^{\prime}}{2}n.

Therefore, for a generic placement
{\bf p}
, the left-kernel
S
of

R(G^{\prime},{\bf p})
satisfies

\dim S=m(G^{\prime})-\mathrm{rk}_{d}(G^{\prime})=o(n).

We now expose the retained graph by deleting the edges of
G^{\prime}
one at a
time in a uniformly random order, until each edge has been retained with
probability
c/c^{\prime}
. Let
S_{t}
be the left-kernel after
t
deletions.
We use the standard gap theorem for
k
-cores in random graphs:
There exists
\alpha>0
such that, a.a.s., every nonempty subgraph of

G(n,c^{\prime}/n)
with minimum degree at least
d+1
has at least

\alpha n
vertices
[
35
]
. Hence we may assume that every circuit of the
d
-rigidity matroid contained in
G^{\prime}
has at least

\alpha n
edges. Indeed, every such circuit has minimum degree at least

d+1
.

Thus, whenever
S_{t}\neq 0
, the current graph contains a circuit with at
least
\alpha n
edges. The next deleted edge belongs to this circuit
with probability bounded below by a positive constant depending only on

c^{\prime}
and
\alpha
. If this happens, then
\dim S_{t}
decreases by
one. Since initially
\dim S=o(n)
, while the number of deleted edges is a.a.s.

(1-c/c^{\prime}+o(1))|E(G^{\prime})|=\Omega(n)
, a Chernoff bound implies that a.a.s.
the left-kernel vanishes before the deletion process ends. Hence the
resulting graph
G\sim G(n,c/n)
is a.a.s.
d
-independent.
∎

We turn to the phase transition in the rigidity closure.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We start with item
(1b)
.
Choose
c<c^{\prime}<c_{d}
, and couple
G\sim G(n,c/n)
and
G^{\prime}\sim G(n,c^{\prime}/n)
by first
sampling
G
, and then sprinkling each missing edge independently with
probability
(c^{\prime}-c)/(n-c)
. Suppose that, with probability bounded away
from zero,

|\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G)\setminus E(G)|>\varepsilon n

for some
\varepsilon>0
. Conditional on this event, the sprinkling adds
at least one edge of
\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G)\setminus E(G)
with
probability bounded away from zero, implying that, with probability bounded away from
zero,
G^{\prime}
is not
d
-independent, in contradiction to Theorem
1.1
. In addition, it is easy to see that a.a.s., every
v
vertices in
G
span at most
O(v)
edges,
whence any clique in
\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G)
of size
v
uses at least
\Omega(v^{2})
edges outside of
G
. Hence, such a clique must have size
v=o(\sqrt{n})
. This concludes
(1b)
.

We turn to derive
(1a)
for
d\geq 3
since the case
d=2
was proved in
[
26
]
.
Every induced
d
-rigid subgraph spans a clique in the closure and therefore,
by item
(1b)
, is of size
o(\sqrt{n})
. In addition, it must have minimum degree at least
3
, in contradiction to the gap theorem for
k
-cores
[
35
]
.

Turning to item
(2)
, we start by giving a lower bound on the number of edges in
\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G)
.

Claim 6.9
.

A.a.s.,

\left|\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G)\right|\geq\left(\hat{p}^{2}-o(1)\right)\binom{n}{2}\,.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Choose
c^{\prime}
with
c_{d}<c^{\prime}<c
sufficiently close
to
c
so that

\frac{\rho(c)-\rho(c^{\prime})}{(c-c^{\prime})}<\frac{1-\hat{p}^{2}}{2}+\varepsilon,

(6.5)

where

\rho(c)=\frac{c}{2}-\frac{c\hat{p}(c)^{2}}{2}+d\,\mathbb{P}\big(\operatorname{Poi}(c\hat{p}(c))\geq d+1\big)

is the limiting normalized rank from Corollary
6.8
. This is
possible since

\rho^{\prime}(c)=\frac{1-\hat{p}^{2}}{2}.

Indeed,

\rho^{\prime}(c)=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2}-c\hat{p}\hat{p}^{\prime}+d\,\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Poi}(c\hat{p})=d)(\hat{p}+c\hat{p}^{\prime}).

The last two terms cancel since

d\,\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Poi}(c\hat{p})=d)=c\hat{p}\,\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Poi}(c\hat{p})=d-1),

and

\hat{p}^{\prime}=\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Poi}(c\hat{p})=d-1)(\hat{p}+c\hat{p}^{\prime})

by differentiating the fixed-point equation.

Couple the random graph process so that
G^{\prime}\sim G(n,c^{\prime}/n)
and

G\sim G(n,c/n)
, with
G^{\prime}\subseteq G
. Equivalently, after
sampling
G^{\prime}
, we sprinkle
T
additional random edges, where a.a.s.

T=(1+o(1))\frac{c-c^{\prime}}{2}n.

(6.6)

Suppose, for contradiction, that with probability bounded away from zero,

|\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G)|\leq\left(\hat{p}^{2}-4\varepsilon\right)\binom{n}{2}.

Let

G^{\prime}=G^{0}\subseteq G^{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq G^{T}=G

be the graphs obtained during the sprinkling process, where
G^{i+1}

is obtained from
G^{i}
by adding one uniformly chosen edge from

E(K_{n})\setminus E(G^{i})
. By monotonicity of matroid closure, on the
event above we have, for every
0\leq i\leq T
,

\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G^{i})\subseteq\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G),

and therefore

|\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G^{i})|\leq\left(\hat{p}^{2}-4\varepsilon\right)\binom{n}{2}.

Now expose the next edge as follows. Given
G^{i}
, let

a_{i}=\frac{\binom{n}{2}-|\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G^{i})|}{\binom{n}{2}-|E(G^{i})|}\geq 1-\hat{p}^{2}+4\varepsilon\,.

Draw
r_{i}\sim U[0,1]
. If
r_{i}\leq a_{i}
, choose the next edge uniformly
from

E(K_{n})\setminus\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G^{i}),

and hence the rank increases by one. Otherwise, choose it uniformly from

\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G^{i})\setminus E(G^{i}).

This gives exactly a uniformly chosen edge from

E(K_{n})\setminus E(G^{i})
.
Therefore the number of rank-increasing sprinkled edges is at least

\#\{i:r_{i}\leq 1-\hat{p}^{2}+4\varepsilon+o(1)\}.

By the law of large numbers and (
6.6
), this number is a.a.s. at least

(1-\hat{p}^{2}+3\varepsilon)T.

On the other hand, by Corollary
6.8
, a.a.s. the actual rank
increase from
G^{\prime}
to
G
is

\big(\rho(c)-\rho(c^{\prime})+o(1)\big)n<(1+o(1))\cdot(1-\hat{p}^{2}+2\varepsilon)T

due to (
6.5
) and (
6.6
),
yielding a contradiction. Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, the claim follows.
∎

Denote by
\widehat{V}_{r}
the set of vertices
v
in
G
whose
r
-neighborhood is a tree that contains a
d
-ary tree of height
r
rooted at
v
, and by
C_{r}
the set of vertices
v
whose
r
-neighborhood is not a tree.

Claim 6.10
.

Let
G
be a graph,
r\geq 2
an integer and
uv\in\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G)
. Then, either

\{u,v\}\subset\widehat{V}_{r}
,

\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,v)<r
, or

\{u,v\}\cap C_{r}\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

We show that if the
r
-neighborhood
of
u
is a tree which does not contain a rooted
d
-ary tree of height

r
, and if
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,v)\geq r
, then

uv\notin\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G)
.
Indeed, denote by
(T,u)
the
r
-neighborhood of
u
, rooted at
u
.
Embed
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
according to an admissible measure
\nu
.
Associate a
d\times d
matrix
Q_{x}
to every vertex
x
of
T
as
follows. If
x
has height
r
in
T
, set
Q_{x}=0
(which means that these boundary vertices are pinned); otherwise compute

Q_{x}
recursively, using the sampled embedding
{\bf p}
and
Theorem
5.2
. Since
(T,u)
does not contain a rooted

d
-ary subtree of height
r
, the recursion gives
Q_{u}\neq 0
.
In particular, there is a non-trivial infinitesimal motion
q
of
T

which preserves all edge lengths in
T
, satisfies
q(x)=0
for every
vertex
x
at height
r
, and satisfies
q(u)\neq 0
. Extend
q
to all
of
G
by setting
q(w)=0
for every vertex
w\notin T
. Since the boundary
vertices of
T
are fixed, this extension preserves the length of every
edge of
G
, hence
q\in\ker L(G,{\bf p})
.
In particular,
q(v)=0
while
q(u)\neq 0
. For a generic admissible
placement, the vector
q(u)-q(v)
is not orthogonal to

{\bf p}(u)-{\bf p}(v)
. Thus adding the edge
uv
to
G
increases its
d
-rigidity rank, and therefore
uv\notin\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G)
.
∎

Since
G\sim G(n,c/n)
converges locally weakly to

\mathrm{GW}(\operatorname{Poi}(c))
, we a.a.s. have
|C_{r}|=o(n)
for every fixed
r\geq 2
.
In addition, the expected number of pairs
uv
of distance less than
r
in
G
is
O(n)
and is therefore a.a.s. of size
o(n^{2})
. Therefore,
Claim
6.10
gives that for every fixed
r\geq 2
, a.a.s.

\left|\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G)\setminus\binom{\widehat{V}_{r}}{2}\right|=o(n^{2})\,.

(6.7)

In addition,
\mathbb{E}[|\widehat{V}_{r}|]/n\to q_{r}
, where
q_{r}
is the
probability that the first
r
generations of

\mathrm{GW}(\operatorname{Poi}(c))
contain a complete
d
-ary
tree rooted at the root. Moreover, a standard second moment argument
applies: for fixed
r
, the
r
-neighborhoods of two uniformly chosen
vertices are disjoint and asymptotically independent with probability

1-o(1)
. Hence a.a.s.

|\widehat{V}_{r}|=(1+o(1))q_{r}n\,.

(6.8)

In addition, the numbers
q_{r}
satisfy the recursion

q_{r+1}=\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Poi}(cq_{r})\geq d),\qquad q_{0}=1\,,

whence
q_{r}
converges to
\hat{p}.

Therefore, by taking
r\to\infty
,
(
6.7
)
and (
6.8
) imply that a.a.s.,

\left|\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G)\right|\leq\left(\hat{p}^{2}+o(1)\right)\binom{n}{2}\,,

(6.9)

matching the lower bound from
6.9
.

Next, we find a large clique in the closure.
Let
\varepsilon>0
.
By Claim
6.9
,
(
6.7
) and
(
6.8
) it holds that for all sufficiently large
r=r(\varepsilon)
, a.a.s. the subgraph of
\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G)
induced by
\widehat{V}_{r}
has edge density at least
1-\varepsilon^{2}
. By a standard counting argument, there exists a subset

A\subseteq\widehat{V}_{r}
with

|A|\geq(1-\varepsilon)(|\widehat{V}_{r}|-1)\,,

such that every
v\in A
has at least
(1-\varepsilon)(|\widehat{V}_{r}|-1)
neighbors from
\widehat{V}_{r}
in
\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G)
. Hence, the subgraph
H
of
\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G)
induced by
A
has minimum degree at least
(1-2\varepsilon)(|\widehat{V}_{r}|-1)
.
For a sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, the minimum-degree condition for rigidity from
[
31
]
holds, implying that the graph
H
is generically
d
-rigid. Since
H
is an induced subgraph
of
\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G)
, it follows that
A
induces a clique in

\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G)
. Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
and
r=r(\varepsilon)\to\infty
shows that
\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G)
a.a.s. contains a clique of size
(\hat{p}-o(1))n
. Note that no clique in the closure has a strictly larger density due to (
6.9
).

Let
\widehat{G}
denote the
((d+1)+d)
-core of
G
,
obtained from the

(d+1)
-core by repeatedly adding vertices with at least
d
neighbors in the already constructed graph.
It remains to show that the largest clique in the closure contains almost all the vertices of
\widehat{G}
. We claim that

V(\widehat{G})\subset\widehat{V}_{r}\cup C_{r}\,.

Indeed, suppose that
v\in V(\widehat{G})\setminus C_{r}
. If
v
is in the
(d+1)
-core and its
r
-neighborhood is
acyclic, then it contains the first
r
layers of the
(d+1)
-regular
tree, whence
v\in\widehat{V}_{r}
. Otherwise, fix an order in which the vertices of

\widehat{G}\setminus V(G_{d+1})
are added. Since
v
is not in the

(d+1)
-core, the
(d+1)
-core is unchanged after deleting
v
.
At the moment
v
is added, it has at least
d
neighbors that were
already present; by using the same earlier part of the addition order,
these neighbors belong to the
((d+1)+d)
-core of
G\setminus v
.
Moreover, since
v\notin C_{r}
, their

-neighborhoods in

are trees. We then proceed by induction on
r
to show
that
v\in\widehat{V}_{r}
.

Let
A
be a largest clique in
\operatorname{cl}_{d}(G)
. By the previous paragraph, a.a.s.

|A|=(\hat{p}+o(1))n.

Moreover, by (
6.7
), a.a.s.

has
o(n)
vertices outside

\widehat{V}_{r}
.
Therefore, using (

),

|\widehat{V}_{r}\setminus A|=(q_{r}-\hat{p}+o(1))n.

Since

, for every

and all sufficiently large fixed

, a.a.s.

|V(\widehat{G})\setminus A|\leq|\widehat{V}_{r}\setminus A|+|C_{r}|\leq(\varepsilon+o(1))n.

∎

6.3.
Essentially high-girth regular graphs

We now consider the special case where
X\equiv k
, and the shifted
size-biasing of
X
is
Y\equiv k-1
.
Therefore, by (
6.1
),

\phi^{\prime\prime}(p)=k\left(d\binom{k-1}{d}p^{d-1}(1-p)^{k-1-d}-1\right)\,.

For
k\geq d+1
, the function
\phi^{\prime\prime}
is strictly unimodal, hence Condition
6.1
holds by Claim
6.2
. If
k\leq d
, then

\phi(p)=d-\frac{k}{2}(1+p^{2})
attains its maximum at
p=0
, and Condition
6.1
holds.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

An essentially high-girth sequence of
k
-regular graphs is
(X,d)
-typical. Indeed, local weak convergence holds by definition of essential high-girth, and
m=kn/2
. In addition, the equation

p=\mathbb{P}(Y_{p}\geq d)=\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(k-1,p)\geq d)
has a unique solution
\hat{p}=0
if
k\leq d
, and the largest solution in
[0,1]
is
\hat{p}=1
if
k\geq d+1
. In the former case
n_{d+1}=m_{d+1}=0
and in the latter case
n_{d+1}=n,~m_{d+1}=m
, thus (
6.2
) is satisfied. The proof is completed by applying Theorem
6.5
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Since essentially high-girth
k
-regular graphs exist, we can apply Corollary
6.7
for
\mathbb{T}_{k}
and derive

\mathbb{E}[\phi_{\mathbb{T}_{k},\nu}(o)]=\max(d-k/2,0)\,.

By Lemma
5.3
, the event
Q_{o}=0
admits a zero-one law. Hence

Q_{o}=0
almost surely if and only if

\mathbb{E}[\phi_{\mathbb{T}_{k},\nu}(o)]=0,

which holds precisely when
k\geq 2d
.
In addition,
Q_{o}=0
almost surely if and only if
Q_{v}=0
almost surely
for every vertex
v
. The claim follows.
∎

7.
Discussion

Many fascinating open problems remain.

(1)

The
d
-rigidity phase transition in
G(n,p)
is now established, but both the subcritical and supercritical regimes should be better understood.
Does it hold that:

•

If
c<c_{d}
then
G(n,c/n)
is a.a.s. closed in the
d
-rigidity matroid?

•

If
c>c_{d}
then the
((d+1)+d)
-core a.a.s. induces a
d
-rigid subgraph?

These are the strongest possible statements in the respective regimes, and numerical simulations suggest that they hold.

(2)

Similarly,

•

Is a random
k
-regular graph independent and closed in the generic
d
-rigidity matroid for
k<2d
?

•

Is it a.a.s.
d
-rigid for
k\geq 2d
?

In this paper we only compute its
d
-rigidity rank asymptotically, and
prove the corresponding result for strict rigidity of the infinite
k
-regular tree.

(3)

Another natural direction is to extend the results of this work to global
rigidity. For instance, proving that for
c>c_{d}
, the
(d+1)
-core of

G(n,c/n)
, or some large subgraph of it, is a.a.s. globally
d
-rigid
would resolve the conjecture of

[
8
]
concerning the MLT of

G(n,c/n)

[
42
]
.

(4)

It would be interesting to generalize the methods of this paper to
other abstract
d
-rigidity matroids. The
d
-hyperconnectivity matroid
and the cofactor matroid are natural candidates, as both have concrete
matrix descriptions to which the spectral methods developed here may
transfer.

(5)

The fact that generic rigidity is a combinatorial property of the graph has
played a huge role in the development of combinatorial rigidity theory. Is
there a similar notion for infinite self-adjoint frameworks?
For instance, we know by Lemma
5.3
that in a
(T,\nu)
-embedding of a rooted tree

(T,o)
, the event
Q_{o}=0
admits a zero-one law. Does the same hold for all locally
finite graphs
G
? Moreover, even for trees, we do not know whether the answer can depend on
\nu
.
