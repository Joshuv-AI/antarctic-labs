1 Introduction
This report documents reaction rates and specific activities measured from activation and fission foil
dosimeters deployed in the alumina-only and alumina-graphite IER-622 PARADIGM configurations
[1]. Two irradiations were performed at the National Criticality Experiments Research Center
(NCERC) on May 6, 2025, and May 15, 2025. Identical sets of disc shaped dosimeters were irradiated
in each configuration. Each set contained a depleted uranium (DU) fission foil and high-assay-low-
enriched uranium (HALEU) fission foil. Both sets include a Cd-covered Au foil and an uncovered
Au foil, a Mn foil, a Cu foil, an Fe foil, an In foil, and eleven Ni foils. The sets were placed in slots
of a sample holder positioned near the center of the reactor. Reaction rates, specific activities, and
their comparison to simulated irradiation reaction rates are reported as well as a brief detailing of
the effective multiplication factor for both systems.

2 Dosimeter deployment
Dosimeters were deployed in both the alumina-only critical assembly and the alumina-graphite
critical assembly. Foils were arranged in circular sample plates with cutouts on the peripheries to
hold the dosimeters as shown in Figure 1. Six dosimeter materials were used, each sensitive to
different energies of the neutron flux spectrum. Tables 1 and 2 lists details of the dosimeters. In
both irradiations a Cd covered Au dosimeter was deployed along with an uncovered Au foil. Fission
foils were also deployed to acquire fission rates. Relevant properties for the fission foils deployed are
listed in Table 3.

3 Activation and fission product counting
Three high purity germanium (HPGe) detectors were used at the National Criticality Experiments
Research Center Counting Laboratory (NCERC-CL) to count the foil reaction products. The de-
tectors include an Ortec GEM40P4-76 ∼40% efficient detector cooled with an ICS-P4 (detector ID
Dr. Pepper) and two Ortec GEM50-83 ∼50% efficient, fixed cryostat detectors cooled via Mobius
Recycler Systems (detector IDs Detector 210 and Detector 211). Some reaction products were also
counted on an Ortec GEM40P4-83-ST ∼40% efficient detector, Baja Blast, cooled by an ICS-P4 or
a Canberra GC10023 >100% efficient detector, Gemma, cooled with a CP5-PLUS at the Nonpro-
liferation and International Security Center Counting Laboratory (NISC-CL) for cross-validation.
Fission foils were loaded onto a sample changer and counted multiple times on Dr. Pepper. The
DU foils were counted for three-hour increments and the HALEU foils were counted for five-hour
increments. Acquiring multiple gamma spectra for each foil was done to verify results, and the
time difference between same-foil counts allows us to down-select from the group to a subset where
the decay time from end of irradiation is ideal for the Ba-La fission product analysis discussed in
section 4

4.2 Data and Uncertainty
Each dosimeter was weighed at least five times on a Mettler-Toledo MS-205DU scale which reads
to 10 µg with quoted repeatability of 20 µg. Foil purity and composition are provided by the
manufacturers. Natural abundances, atomic masses, and half-lives are from IRDFF-II Table 6 [7].
Gamma intensities are from IRDFF-II decay data [7]. Mass attenuation coefficients are from NIST
XCOM [8]. Fission yields used to calculate the fission rates came from Table 3 in IRDFF-II [7],
with yields relevant to this work shown in table 4, and were weighted based on the HALEU and
DU compositions according to eq. (7).

Uncertainty is provided for the data from IRDFF-II. Foil purity uncertainty is treated as one-half
of the last significant figure quoted on the dosimeter certificate. The uncertainty on the dosimeter
mass is the standard deviation of the repeated measurements or the quoted repeatability, whichever
is larger for the five measured weights.
Net counts under a photopeak and its uncertainty are calculated by InterSpec. For isolated photo-
peaks, the peak area is calculated by summing the channel heights and subtracting the background
which is determined by a background fit, usually a linear step. In some cases there are overlap-
ping peaks from competing radioactive decay; when this happens the net area is calculated using a
peak fitting function. The uncertainty is handled in Interspec using a combination of the Poisson
statistical uncertainty from the counts themselves and the fit parameter uncertainties [4].
The variables in eqs. (1) and (4) – (7) are treated as independent. Thus, uncertainty is propagated
onto R and Asp via first order linear uncertainty propagation once the individual statistical and
systematic uncertainties have been quantified for each variable.

5.2 Simulation
The reaction rates were simulated using MCNP 6.3 [9] using a high fidelity model of both config-
urations. Nuclear data for the calculation was used from ENDF/B-VIII.0 for neutron transport
calculations [10]. The IRDFF-II ACE file [7] was used for the reaction rate calculation which was
calculated by specifying a F4 cell fluence tally in each dosimeter and the appropriate 100% abundant
dummy material for the reaction of interest. Estimates of the neutron emission rate in each slot for
both irradiations are shown in fig. 2. These flux estimates are calculated by dividing the measured
nickel reaction rates by their tallies calculated with MCNP, resulting in an estimate of the total neu-
tron emission rate at each nickel dosimeter location. The figure emphasizes the difference in scale of
neutron emission rate in each slot which should be kept in mind when comparing reaction rates for
dosimeters in different slots. Namely, based on the difference in R measured for the 58 Ni(n, p)58 Co
reaction in each slot, if comparing reactions or taking ratios, up to approximately a 25% measured
neutron flux spatial deviation will be built-in to the result for the alumina-only configuration and
a 38% neutron flux spatial deviation for the alumina-graphite configuration if not corrected for the
variation in emission.

6 Conclusion
Reaction rates and specific activities from the two 2025 PARADIGM irradiations were reported
with a brief account of the effective multiplication in Appendix B. The measured reaction rates and
their ratios were compared to simulations calculated using MCNP 6.3.1 with IRDFF-II cross section
libraries. Excellent agreement was shown between the measured and simulated fission rates with a
<4% difference for those in the faster alumina-only configuration while agreement remained reason-
able with <19% differences in the more intermediate alumina-graphite configuration. Reasonable
agreement was also found between the (n,p), (n, n′ ), and (n,2n) reactions for both configurations.
Despite good agreement between the simulated and measured fission rates and keff (see Appendix
B), and reasonable agreement for the threshold reactions, the C/E plots shown in figs. 3, 4a and 4b
show poor agreement between the simulated and measured reaction rates for the (n, γ) reactions.
The results presented support that the model is accurately representing the neutron multiplication,
but is poorly capturing one or more of the processes involved in simulating the neutron activation
reactions. Further work would need to be completed to identify which simulated processes are not
representative of the actual system. The spatially-dependent neutron emission rate estimates were
shown in fig. 2 and emphasize the dissymmetry in neutron population at the various dosimeter
locations. Yet, high quality reaction rates were successfully measured and reported for a novel
critical system designed to increase the contribution of intermediate energy neutrons to interactions,
addressing the interest of the broader community in intermediate neutron spectra by reporting
experimental reaction rates with well-quantified uncertainties.
