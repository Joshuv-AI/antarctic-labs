Reliability of surface topography for scoliosis assessment: a systematic review and meta-analysis based on the COSMIN guideline
Authors: Shihan Li, Jiaxin Zhong, Zhenwei Wang, Shan Liu, Ruixin Liang, Wei Liu, Joanne Y W Yip
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy designSystematic review and meta-analysis.ObjectiveTo propose a clear classification and definition of currently used ST indices, and to provide a comprehensive evidence assessment of the reliability of ST. Additionally, the study also aims to explore factors that may influence the reliability of these indices.MethodA systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed and Web of Science. Studies evaluating the reliability of ST indices were screened and selected. Data extraction and assessment of the quality of evidence were also performed following the COSMIN guideline framework. Extracted data included study characteristics and measurement properties. Measurement properties were evaluated according to the COSMIN quality criteria, and methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the COSMIN Risk of Bias checklist. Quality of evidence was graded using the GRADE approach. Meta-analysis was performed to synthesize the measurement properties.ResultsNineteen studies involving a total of 778 participants were included in this review. The findings indicated that most ST indices demonstrated sufficient reliability for clinical application, although the overall level of evidence was rated as low to moderate.Furthermore, the review suggested that factors such as the use of marker points, participant age, and the presence or absence of scoliosis did not significantly influence the reliability of ST. However, the scanning posture may affect the reliability of ST.ConclusionsThis review highlights ST as a potential radiation-free, informative tool for scoliosis assessment, providing comprehensive data on global musculoskeletal alignment changes with important clinical significance. However, high-quality studies and algorithm improvement are still needed to further explore ST application in screening, progress monitoring, and comprehensive scoliosis assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10097-4.Keywords: Scoliosis, Surface topography, Mass Screening, Reproducibility of Results, Moderator Variables

Introduction

Scoliosis is an abnormal lateral curvature of the spine, and adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is the most common subtype. AIS may progress rapidly between 11 and 14 years of age [1, 2]. AIS can lead to musculoskeletal symptoms and may affect the patient’s cardiopulmonary function [3, 4]. Therefore, the Society on Scoliosis Orthopaedic and Rehabilitation Treatment recommends early detection and intervention, as these are associated with improved clinical outcomes [1].

The clinical assessment and follow-up of scoliosis still rely heavily on repeated radiographic imaging [1], which creates a long-standing tension between the need for accurate monitoring and the desire to minimize radiation exposure. Moreover, radiographic imaging focuses on the lateral curvature of the spine in the coronal plane [5]. In reality, scoliosis involves three-dimensional alignment changes [6]. Therefore, there is a pressing need for a low-cost, radiation-free method to accurately and safely assess scoliosis as an add-on to current clinical protocols.

Surface topography (ST) is an emerging, radiation-free, fast, and non-contact technique for assessing three-dimensional alignment alterations in patients with scoliosis. Specifically, ST systems can generate detailed surface models of the back or even the entire body and reconstruct the underlying skeletal structures in individuals with scoliosis. Moreover, these systems can also generate several surface topography (ST) indices and provide information about asymmetries in both the back surface and the reconstructed spinal morphology [7]. However, it remains unclear to what extent spinal deformities are reflected in the ST of individuals with scoliosis. Moreover, the degree to which specific ST indices can be used to infer spinal deformity or scoliosis-related functional impairment is still uncertain. Addressing these issues is essential for clarifying the clinical applications of ST and guiding the development of related ST equipment. Therefore, a comprehensive assessment of the measurement properties of ST in scoliosis evaluation is needed.

Reliability, validity, responsiveness, and feasibility are all essential properties for evaluating a new test. A systematic review on the validity, responsiveness, and feasibility of ST indices has been published [7]. Among all measurement properties, reliability is considered the most fundamental of all measurement properties [8]. Previous studies have summarized the reliability of several ST indices, reporting generally good reliability [9, 10]. Several well-designed, high-quality studies have been published since then, providing further evidence supporting the reliability of ST indices [11, 12]. Nevertheless, no meta-analytic synthesis or formal quality assessment of the current evidence on the reliability of ST indices has yet to be performed. Furthermore, prior systematic reviews failed to provide a clear classification or definition of the included ST indices. Meanwhile, there is no consensus regarding the posture adopted during scanning in ST assessment and the potential influence of body markers on measurement outcomes. Moreover, previous studies have not examined whether the measurement properties of ST remain consistent across individuals of different ages or physical characteristics. These issues are crucial for establishing standardized protocols for scanning posture in ST assessment and for identifying the appropriate target populations for ST applications.

Therefore, this study aims to propose a clear classification and definition of currently used ST indices, accompanied by schematic illustrations, and to provide a comprehensive summary and quality assessment of the evidence on the reliability of ST based on the Consensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) guidelines. Additionally, the study also aims to explore factors that may influence the reliability of these indices. The findings are expected to provide evidence-based recommendations to support the further development of ST systems and to explore the potential use of ST in clinical practice for scoliosis assessment.

Method

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the guidelines outlined in the PRISMA-COSMIN. This review protocol has been registered at PROSPERO. The registration code is CRD42025633813.

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was performed in PubMed and Web of Science for studies published between January 1, 2010, and April 6, 2024. Furthermore, a manual search was performed on the citations of the included studies and the important review to identify any additional publications meeting the inclusion criteria. The detailed search strategy is shown in Appendix 1 [7].

Inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria

In the review, the publications with the following criteria were included: 1) the test index is the ST index; 2) the study involved the reliability of the ST index. The exclusion criteria were: 1) subjects having other spinal diseases, musculoskeletal pain, and neurologic deficits; 2) subjects with tattoos, pronounced muscles, operations, or scarring of the back.

Study selection and data extraction

Two independent reviewers selected the studies to be included in the analysis and completed data extraction. A third senior researcher adjudicated unresolved disagreements. The detailed data extraction form is shown in Appendix 2. Distinct from previous studies, the outcomes extracted in this review focus on relative reliability, including parameters such as the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), generalizability coefficient (φ), and Cohen’s kappa (κ) [8, 13]. Based on whether the rater is the same and the time interval between tests, reliability is categorized into four types: Intra-ST, Intra-LT, Inter-ST, and Inter-LT. Specifically, Intra-ST refers to intra-rater reliability in the short-term period, while Intra-LT refers to intra-rater reliability in the long-term period. Inter-ST denotes inter-rater reliability in the short-term period, and Inter-LT denotes inter-rater reliability in the long-term period. The time intervals were defined as follows. Short-term refers to an interval of less than one day between tests, allowing the evaluation of variability under functionally stable postural conditions [14, 15]. Long-term refers to an interval of more than one day. Intra-rater reliability is assessed by letting the same assessor repeat the measurement on the same subject after a specified time interval. Inter-rater reliability is assessed by having different assessors independently perform the measurements.

Methodological quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently assessed by two researchers using the COSMIN Risk of Bias checklist for Outcome Measurement Instruments. A detailed description of the methodological quality assessment process utilizing the COSMIN Risk of Bias checklist has been provided in Appendix 2 [7, 8]. It is noteworthy that the COSMIN guideline is not entirely explicit regarding the item “For continuous scores: was an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) calculated?” [8, 13]. Therefore, we also referred to the guidelines developed by Terry K Koo et al. for additional clarification [16].

Measurement properties quality assessment

The COSMIN criteria for good measurement properties standards were used to assess the measurement properties of the ST index. According to the COSMIN standards, the results of each study were classified as sufficient (+), insufficient (−), or indeterminate (?). Table 1 presents the modified COSMIN criteria for reliability [8]. The assessment results of each study were then summarized to provide an overall assessment of the quality of the ST indices. For reliability assessed in fewer than three studies, a qualitative summary was performed. When data from three or more studies were available, a meta-analysis was conducted. The detailed method has been described in 2.6.1 Quantitative summary of ST index. The overall assessment results were then categorized as sufficient (+), insufficient (–), inconsistent (±), or indeterminate (?).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R v4.2.2 software with the meta package (8.0–1) and the metafor package (4.6–0).

Quantitative summary of ST index

All correlation coefficients (Pearson's correlation, Spearman's correlation, and Intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC]) were initially transformed into Fisher’s z values for normalization. The mathematical formula of such transformation is “z = 1/2 ln((1 + ICC)/(1-ICC)),which has an approximate variance,(Var (z) = 1/(N-3))”, where N is the sample size [17]. For each ST index, when each study provided only one effect size, the inverse variance method and a random effects model were used to calculate the pooled estimates of the correlation coefficients. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic and the Cochrane Q test [18, 19]. In cases where a study reported multiple effect sizes for the same index (e.g., results from different postures using the same ST indices), dependency among the effect sizes could arise due to the similar experimental conditions within the same study, and the standard random-effects model would ignore this dependency. Therefore, the Three-Level Meta-Analysis Model was applied, as this model considers three different variance components distributed over the three levels of the model: sampling variance of the extracted effect sizes; variance between effect sizes extracted from the same study; and variance between studies [19, 20].

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were performed to investigate potential factors influencing the reliability of ST indices. Moderators included participants’ age (over 18 years or not), subject characteristics (subjects with scoliosis versus healthy subjects), whether anatomical marker points were utilized, and the posture adopted during measurement (the definition of different postures has been described in a previous publication [7]). The rationale for considering these moderators was that variations in these parameters may differentially affect the stability and accuracy of ST measurements.

The use of anatomical markers can potentially improve landmark identification and measurement accuracy but may reduce consistency due to postural shifts and operator variability, while also increasing scanning time and training requirements. In addition, considering that subjects of different ages may have varying levels of compliance, and that those with scoliosis tend to have poorer balance [21], as well as the possibility that different scanning postures may have different levels of stability, we also performed subgroup analyses on these moderators.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding studies with inadequate methodological quality, potential conflicts of interest that might bias the overall results, or those in which reliability was assessed using correlation coefficients such as Pearson or Spearman.

Grading the quality of evidence

The quality of evidence for the measurement properties of ST indices was assessed using the GRADE system. The detailed assessment method is the same as in the previous study [7]. The quality of evidence was finally classified into four levels: “high,” “moderate,” “low,” and “very low” [8].

Result

Description of included studies

As shown in Fig. 1, a total of 9281 studies were initially identified through database and citation searching. After removing duplicates and irrelevant studies based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 19 studies were finally included. Inter-rater agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa, resulting in values of 0.877 and 0.860 for study selection and methodological quality assessment, respectively, indicating an almost perfect level of agreement [22].

Most studies (15/19) included both female and male subjects, one study only included males [12], and three studies did not report subjects’ gender [23, 29, 37]. Over half of the included studies (10/19) did not report the BMI of the subjects. Two studies reported that subjects’ mean BMI was < 25 kg/m2, and two reported that it was > 25 kg/m2 [15, 36]. The remaining five studies covered BMI ranges both < 25 kg/m2 and ≥ 25 kg/m2 [11, 24, 25, 28, 33].

Two studies included only subjects whose Cobb angle was over 15° [23, 27], with four studies only reporting the mean Cobb angle over 15° [26, 28, 29, 32]. Negrini et al. included subjects with a Cobb angle ranging from 13.1–70° [28]. Terheyden et al. reported subjects with different scoliosis levels [26]. Eight studies only included healthy subject [12, 14, 15, 33–37], four studies did not report the severity of scoliosis [11, 24, 25, 31].

Regarding experiment settings, eleven studies were conducted in hospitals or other specialist centers [11, 23, 24, 26, 27, 29, 31, 33, 34, 37], and the remaining studies were carried out in university labs [12, 14, 15, 25, 28, 30–32, 35, 36]. Negrini et al. disclosed that one of the authors owns stock of lSlCO, consults Medtronic, and is on the Scientific Advisory Board of Janssen Pharmaceutical [28].

Description of included ST systems and ST indices

ST systems

This review included nine ST systems, four of which are commercial products: A Novel Three-Dimensional Imaging Mobile Surface Topography System: A commercial iPad-based 3D mobile scanning tool with Structure Sensor(MSTS) [12], the Biomolecular Design Competition system (BIOMODEL) [23], the Biometrical Holistic of Human Body (BHOHB) [29], and the Formetric-4D system. The remaining systems are self-designed ST systems, utilizing scanners such as the Torso Scan, 3DMDbody system [31], Artec Eva white light scanner [11], Artec Eva hand-held 3D scanner [30], Xtion Pro Live depth sensor [32], and the FastScan for scoliosis handheld scanner [34]. Among these, the Formetric-4D system is the most widely used. Ten studies have reported the reliability of ST indices derived from the Formetric-4D system [14, 15, 24–28, 33, 36, 37].

ST indices classification

Appendix 3 presents the included ST indices, along with their definitions and schematic diagrams. Based on the anatomical landmarkers involved and the characteristics of the ST indices, these ST indices were classified into three categories: “General Description Index”, “Indices describing asymmetry of upper body alignment”, and “Dynamic Index”. Figure 2 is a schematic diagram of the classification and definition of ST indices. “General Description Index” quantifies the 3D morphology of the back or spine, including parameters such as back surface area and spinal length. “Indices describing asymmetry of upper body alignment” included ST indices that describe the three-dimensional spatial asymmetry of local anatomical markers such as the shoulders, scapulae, spine, and pelvis. This category also included indices that describe the global asymmetry of the entire back or upper body, calculated through certain mathematical methods. According to the anatomical region assessed, the category “Indices describing asymmetry of upper body alignment” can be further divided into “Shoulder and scapular”, “Spine-related parameters”, “Pelvis”, and “Whole upper body asymmetry”. "Dynamic Index" includes ST indices that are obtained by measurements taken before and after specific movements under dynamic conditions.

“Whole upper body asymmetry”

Nine ST indices were included in this category. Discussion

Biomechanical alignment changes in scoliosis

Scoliosis is a three-dimensional deformity of the spine. It is characterized by lateral deviation of the spine, accompanied by vertebral rotation or deviations in the sagittal plane [39]. In thoracic scoliosis, these spinal deviations and rotations also lead to deformation and rotation of the rib cage. On the convex side, the ribs are pushed posteriorly, and the thoracic cage becomes narrowed, forming a prominent rib hump. In contrast, the ribs on the concave side are displaced laterally and anteriorly, causing the thoracic cage to deform outward and forward. Additionally, scoliosis is associated with paraspinal muscle asymmetry. Studies have shown that paraspinal muscles on the concave side exhibit muscle atrophy, increased fatty infiltration, reduced type I fibers, and reduced muscle activity [40]. This muscular imbalance could also contribute to pelvic asymmetry [41]. Research has also shown that scoliosis can negatively affect pelvic floor muscle activity and pelvic alignment [42]. These biomechanical changes collectively lead to the development of rib humps, pelvic asymmetries, and asymmetry of the lumbar dimples. Another frequently observed characteristic in subjects with scoliosis is shoulder imbalance, with prevalence reported to range from approximately 23% to 50% [43].

ST indices used in current studies and reliability of ST indices

ST serves as an objective method for evaluating the complex, three-dimensional changes in the above-mentioned global body alignment caused by scoliosis. Our research team systematically reviewed current evidence on the measurement properties of ST in scoliosis assessment. In this review, we provide an overview of the currently reported ST indices and their reliability. Reliability is the most fundamental measurement property, reflecting the overall consistency of a measure [44].

The anatomical landmarks involved in these parameters include the spinous process and vertebral body of the spine, the two posterior superior iliac spines (PSIS), the midpoint between the PSIS, the acromion process, and the inferior angle of the scapula. A small number of parameters also involve the nipple, lower sternum, jugular notch, axillary folds, and xiphoid process. The reference planes used in these parameters include the horizontal, frontal, and sagittal planes.

Parameters considered suitable for clinical application should demonstrate high stability and reliability. This review found that most ST indices demonstrated sufficient reliability for clinical application, supported by low to moderate-quality evidence. However, the reliability of some parameters was suboptimal, indicating that those parameters are not suitable for clinical application. These findings provide valuable insights for engineers in selecting appropriate and reliable parameters during the design and development of ST systems.

Specifically, our findings suggest that anatomical landmarks with greater mobility, such as the nipple, axillary folds, and inferior angle of the scapula, are unsuitable as reference points for postural asymmetry due to the instability of their associated parameters. Additionally, the parameters defined based on the frontal plane, such as shoulder rotation and scapular rotation, exhibited lower stability compared with the horizontal and sagittal planes. This may be attributed to the difficulty of accurately defining the frontal plane in 3D scanning. These findings suggest that the frontal plane may not be suitable as a reference plane because of its relatively low stability during 3D scanning.

In terms of “spine-related parameters”, those involving identification of key anatomical landmarks and describing the overall shape reconstruction, such as scoliosis angle and kyphosis angle, were generally satisfactory. However, vertebral rotation demonstrated lower reliability compared to other parameters, because accurate quantification of vertebral rotation requires detailed vertebral modeling rather than general spinal contour reconstruction. Moreover, morphological alterations of the vertebrae are also observed in individuals with scoliosis, including wedge-shaped changes of the vertebral body, a narrowed vertebral canal, a thinner lamina on the concave side, and deviation of the spinous process toward the concave side. These morphological variations further increase the difficulty of precise vertebral restoration. Further, algorithm improvement is needed in the reconstruction of vertebral details.

Meanwhile, most parameters that describe overall upper body asymmetry (e.g., POTSI and Suzuki) and trunk length (e.g., VP–SP%) involve the identification of multiple anatomical landmarks, resulting in greater measurement complexity and a higher potential for error, thereby reducing their stability. Although parameters are theoretically appealing, their limited stability significantly hinders their translation into clinical practice.

Moderators that influence the reliability of ST indices

According to the results of the heterogeneity tests (Q test and I2), the vast majority of meta-analyses of ST indices showed significant heterogeneity. This heterogeneity may originate from differences among the included studies, including variations in study design (such as experiment settings, ST systems, and the experience of the operators). In addition, differences in the measurement protocols adopted in different experiments (for example, the posture of the participants, whether anatomical landmarks were used, the degree of exposure of the tested area, and the stability of lighting conditions) may also contribute to the observed variability. Moreover, variations in participants’ characteristics (such as age, sex, or the presence of scoliosis) may further increase the heterogeneity across studies. Investigating these factors is crucial for the standardization of measurement protocols and the generalization of experimental results.

To further address this observed heterogeneity, this review also conducted subgroup analyses to explore whether the reliability of ST is consistent among subjects with different characteristics, and to investigate which factors during the scanning process may affect reliability. Our results indicated that the use of marker points does not influence the reliability of ST, which was consistent with the findings reported by Su et al. [9]. In addition, our findings suggest that differences in compliance due to age, as well as differences in balance ability between subjects with and without scoliosis, are not sufficient to affect the reliability of ST, which may be attributed to the rapid scanning speed of ST. Furthermore, our results showed that scanning posture of the subjects can influence the reliability of ST. This may be due to differences in the ability of subjects to reproduce certain postures, as well as variations in spinal shape and body surface contour under different postures [45, 46]. However, because of the relatively small number of included studies, subgroup analyses may have been underpowered, and the corresponding results should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Limitations

Meanwhile, this review also adopted the COSMIN guidelines to assess the quality of evidence regarding the reliability of ST. The findings indicate that the overall methodological quality of current studies is relatively low. Common methodological limitations include a lack of blinding and potentially unsuitable choices of data analysis methods. Consistent with previous research on validity and responsiveness, more than half of the studies on reliability did not report BMI, and several studies did not provide information on scoliosis severity [7]. Most studies also failed to report the background of the operators, despite both factors potentially influencing the reliability of ST systems. The absence of this information limits the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, current research has focused more on short-term intra-rater reliability, while data on long-term intra-rater reliability and inter-rater reliability are relatively scarce, even though these measurement properties are of significant importance for the clinical application of ST.

This review has several limitations. First, it is restricted to English-language publications. Second, the literature search was limited to two databases (PubMed and Web of Science). Although these databases provide broad coverage of science, medical and health-related research, additional databases such as EMBASE, CINAHL, and Scopus should also be considered in future reviews to enhance comprehensiveness.

Additionally, this study primarily focuses on relative reliability, with limited reporting on absolute reliability. In the subgroup analyses, the weighting of each parameter in the model was based more on the frequency of its occurrence rather than its importance in the assessment of scoliosis. Moreover, in this study, all time intervals greater than one day but less than one month were defined as representing long-term reliability. However, pooling studies with potentially heterogeneous time intervals under the category of “long-term” reliability may introduce bias, as it overlooks possible changes in participants’ functional status that could occur over time.

Furthermore, there is considerable variation in patient characteristics among the included studies, such as BMI, curve severity, and curve location. These factors make it difficult to fully interpret the results. Meanwhile, no minimum sample size threshold is specified for the included studies. Setting a minimum sample size threshold could potentially improve the robustness of the meta-analysis. Finally, this meta-analysis included 19 studies with a total of 778 participants; the relatively small overall sample size may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the results.

Summary and outlook on the measurement properties of ST indices

Previous studies have summarized the measurement properties of ST indices, including validity, responsiveness, and feasibility [7]. In addition, we explored the potential use of ST in screening, scoliosis progression monitoring, surveillance, cosmetic assessment, and musculoskeletal alignment assessment. The results indicated that some ST indices, such as the scoliosis angle and asymmetry index, demonstrated sufficient criterion validity. For responsiveness, the scoliosis angle exhibited inconsistent results when compared with the Cobb angle. The construct validity of both thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis was rated as sufficient when compared with similar parameters measured from X-rays. This study also suggested the potential of ST as a radiation-free and informative tool for scoliosis assessment, particularly in screening settings. But more high-quality studies are still needed to further investigate the measurement properties mentioned above and to strengthen evidence for its clinical applicability.

In the current study, our team summarized the reliability of ST, and more ST indices were included compared with previous reviews that reported validity, responsiveness, and feasibility. These parameters provided more comprehensive information on three-dimensional alignment alternation of individual with scoliosis, and studies have shown that most of them have excellent reliability. Although some of these parameters are innovative, they are unstable for various reasons and are not suitable as assessment tools, as discussed earlier.

Overall, our study provides a comprehensive summary of the measurement properties of ST indices. Our research could provide valuable references for the future development and parameter selection of ST systems. Although current research on ST has made progress, there are still certain limitations in studies on the measurement properties of ST in scoliosis assessment. Most studies on the validity and responsiveness of ST have focused on parameters that are similar to indices from X-ray, such as the scoliosis angle and thoracic kyphosis angle. Future research could explore ST-specific parameters, such as RMS from the color deviation map and the Asymmetry Index. At present, most studies compare the three-dimensional parameters obtained from ST with those derived from two-dimensional imaging. Although two-dimensional imaging remains the gold standard and serves as a universal language for academic communication, it is equally important to recognize and investigate the unique advantages of ST systems in evaluating three-dimensional musculoskeletal alignment. Additionally, high-quality studies investigating the correlation between ST parameters and subjective psychological factors (e.g., self-perceived image) are warranted. At present, most research has focused on the measurement properties of individual parameters. It remains unclear whether combining different categories of parameters could enhance measurement properties and provide more information, which parameters should be combined, and how to determine their respective weights. Future studies could address these important questions.

Conclusion

This review systematically summarized the reliability of ST indices. The findings indicated that most ST indices demonstrated sufficient reliability for clinical application, although the overall level of evidence was rated as low to moderate. More high-quality and well-designed studies are still needed to support the widespread clinical application of ST. Furthermore, the review suggested that factors such as the use of marker points, participant age, and the presence or absence of scoliosis did not significantly influence the reliability of ST. However, our results also showed that the scanning posture of the participant may affect the reliability of ST indices.

Overall, ST is an informative and radiation-free tool for scoliosis assessment, capable of providing a comprehensive evaluation of musculoskeletal alignment changes in individuals with scoliosis. ST shows potential for applications in scoliosis screening, progression monitoring, surveillance, cosmetic evaluation, and musculoskeletal alignment assessment, particularly in screening. Future research should focus on improving algorithms, optimizing study designs, and identifying better parameter combinations to further explore and enhance the clinical applications of ST.
