Clinical and Economic Inpatient Burden of Influenza and Influenza-Like Illness in Germany 2019–2025: Analysis of Nationwide Hospital Data
Authors: Anna C Meyer, Saskia Kiesel, Anahita Poshtiban, Moritz Wick, Oliver Damm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionData on the influenza burden in Germany during and after the global COVID-19 pandemic are limited. This study presents nationwide trends in the clinical and economic inpatient burden of influenza and influenza-like illness (ILI) in the German population from 2019 to 2025.MethodsWe conducted a descriptive, retrospective analysis of nationwide hospital data from the German Institute for Hospital Reimbursement covering six consecutive influenza seasons (2019–2020 to 2024–2025). Influenza/ILI hospitalizations were identified using the International Classification of Diseases version 10 (ICD-10) codes J09–J11 recorded as primary diagnosis. Outcomes included hospitalization counts, incidence rates, mean length of stay, ICU admissions, in-hospital mortality, and estimated costs, and were reported for three age groups (0–17 years, 18–59 years, ≥ 60 years). Regional variation across federal states was assessed using directly age-standardized rates.ResultsWe identified 240,646 influenza/ILI hospitalizations, with the highest burden observed in the 2024–2025 season (87,745 cases; incidence: 105.0/100,000 population). Less than 5000 cases were observed in both the 2020–2021 and 2021–2022 seasons. Across all seasons, adults aged ≥ 60 years accounted for 48% of hospitalizations and 92% of 10,054 in-hospital deaths. In this age group, in-hospital mortality ranged between 5.8% and 12.3% per season. Mean hospitalization costs per case increased during the study period and were highest in older adults, reaching €5430 in 2023–2024 and €5421 in 2024–2025. Total inpatient costs of influenza/ILI were highest in 2024–2025 and estimated at €390 million. Regional hospitalization rates varied considerably, but patterns were inconsistent across seasons.ConclusionsInfluenza/ILI causes a substantial and re-emergent inpatient burden in Germany, disproportionately affecting older adults. These findings highlight the need for sustained prevention efforts to reduce the burden of influenza/ILI in Germany. Further research is needed to understand the substantial regional disparities between federal states.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01384-7.Keywords: Hospitalization, Cost of illness, Length of stay, Mortality, Epidemiology, Respiratory infections

Key Summary Points

Evidence on the influenza burden during and following the COVID-19 pandemic in Germany is scarce.Based on nationwide hospital reimbursement data, this study describes the clinical and economic inpatient burden due to influenza and influenza-like illness (ILI) in Germany during 2019–2025.Despite a temporary decline in influenza/ILI hospitalizations between 2020 and 2022, the inpatient burden due to influenza/ILI in Germany is substantial with approximately 88,000 cases in the 2024–2025 season.Older adults contribute disproportionately to both the clinical and economic burden.Considering low, and currently declining, influenza vaccination uptake, public health measures to promote vaccination remain important.

Introduction

Influenza is a common acute respiratory infection associated with a considerable public health burden. In Germany, seasonal influenza has been linked to as many as 26,000 excess deaths annually and the German public health authority (Robert Koch-Institut, RKI) estimated that up to 9 million annual healthcare visits are attributable to influenza [1, 2].

Despite this, recent data on the influenza burden in Germany are scarce. Surveillance data published by RKI indicate low influenza activity between 2020 and 2022 [3], a period marked by the widespread implementation of interventions aimed at mitigating COVID-19 transmission. These interventions, including enhanced hygiene practices and public lockdowns, have presumably also prevented the spread of influenza viruses. Since 2022, increased influenza activity has been observed. According to RKI surveillance data, the 2024–2025 influenza season was particularly severe, even in comparison to pre-pandemic periods [3, 4]. However, surveillance data do not allow for a quantification of the clinical or economic influenza burden in the entire population during this time frame.

Hospitalizations are a major contributor to healthcare costs in Germany and to direct healthcare costs attributable to influenza/ILI [5–7]. A previous study reported up to 50,000 annual hospitalizations with primary influenza diagnoses between 2010 and 2019, with mean costs of approximately €3500 per case [8]. Although it is reasonable to assume that influenza epidemiology has changed since 2020 and healthcare costs have continued to increase in Germany, no studies have thus far assessed the national burden of influenza or influenza-like illness (ILI) in the post-pandemic period.

Annual vaccination is an effective strategy for preventing influenza disease. In Germany, vaccinations against influenza are recommended for all adults aged 60 years and older, among other high-risk populations. Public health authorities have set a target of 75% vaccination coverage in risk groups, but vaccination coverage remains substantially lower. Since 2021, coverage has declined continuously, reaching 34% among older adults during the 2024–2025 season [9]. Vaccination coverage is even lower in certain regions; in 2024–2025, vaccination rates reached no more than 26% among older adults in Germany’s two southernmost federal states, Bavaria and Baden-Württemberg, which comprise almost one-third of the total population [10].

The underlying causes of low influenza vaccination coverage in Germany are likely multifactorial. Previous studies have identified widespread misconceptions regarding the seriousness of influenza and skepticism about the benefits of vaccination as contributing factors [11, 12]. Notably, a multinational survey reported that the perception of influenza as a severe illness is less common among the German population compared to other countries [12]. These findings highlight the need for sustained public health communication to raise awareness of the influenza burden in the German population to promote vaccine uptake.

The present study aims to present nationwide trends in the inpatient burden of influenza and ILI in Germany during and after the global COVID-19 pandemic. Specifically, we describe the overall clinical and economic inpatient burden due to influenza/ILI in Germany in each influenza season from 2019–2020 to 2024–2025. Moreover, we present the proportion of this burden attributable to individuals aged 60 and older, the distribution of hospitalizations over the course of each season and describe regional differences across Germany’s 16 federal states. To explore the extent to which influenza/ILI contributes to the overall inpatient burden of severe acute respiratory infections (SARI) in Germany, we further present the proportion of influenza/ILI hospitalizations among all SARI as well as the proportion of SARI that cannot be attributed to a specific pathogen.

Methods

Study Design and Data Sources

The retrospective study is based on nationwide inpatient data covering the entire German population compiled by the Institute for Reimbursements in Hospitals (Institut für das Entgeltsystem im Krankenhaus, InEK) [13]. The database contains information from all public and private hospitals that are covered by health insurance in Germany. Since reporting to the database is legally required and serves as the basis for reimbursement, the data can be considered virtually complete.

The database allows for the extraction of aggregated data. Hospitalization cases can be filtered based on diagnoses recorded as primary discharge diagnosis according to the International Classification of Diseases Version 10, German modification (ICD-10-GM). Data are available on a case rather than patient level; repeated inpatient stays of the same person occur as distinct records and cannot be traced back to the same individual. Further available data relevant for this study include secondary diagnoses coded according to ICD-10-GM, dates of admission and discharge, geographical region of the hospital (federal state), the age group of patients, treatment at intensive care units (ICU), in-hospital mortality, and diagnosis-related group (DRG) codes, which are used for reimbursement purposes. Publicly available population statistics published by the German national statistics office (Statistisches Bundesamt, destatis) serve as denominators for the incidence calculation [14].

This descriptive observational study covers six consecutive influenza seasons from 2019–2020 to 2024–2025. Each influenza season spans a 12-month period from July 1st of a given year to June 30th of the following year. All analyses are conducted for each season separately.

Ethical Approval

As this study does not involve processing or reporting of individual patient data, obtaining ethical approval was not required.

Definitions

In our study, influenza/ILI is defined as an ICD-10-GM diagnosis of J09, J10, or J11 as the primary discharge diagnosis. Data on whether laboratory confirmation was obtained, or which tests were conducted during the diagnostic process are not available in the InEK database. According to ICD-10-GM, codes J09 and J10 should be assigned to cases in which the influenza virus was identified using laboratory tests whereas J11 should be assigned to influenza cases without virus confirmation. While diagnoses are made by physicians, it cannot be ascertained that the correct diagnosis is made in each case, and that laboratory confirmation was indeed obtained as indicated by ICD codes. As such, the identified hospitalizations—even those coded with J09 or J10—may also contain some cases of illness caused by pathogens other than influenza. We therefore refer to hospitalizations due to influenza or influenza-like illness (ILI). The recorded date of discharge was defined as the date of hospitalization.

SARI, which includes influenza, are defined as hospitalizations with an ICD-10-GM code of J09-J22 as primary discharge diagnosis [3]. We define unspecific SARI, i.e., SARI which cannot be attributed to a specific pathogen in the database as any SARI with a diagnosis of J12.9, J15.9, J18, J20.9, J21.8, J21.9, or J22 as primary discharge diagnosis. Although ICD-10-GM codes for COVID-19 have been available since 2020 (U07.1 and U07.2), these cannot be assigned as primary inpatient diagnosis in German hospitals for administrative reasons. COVID-19 cases are instead coded using secondary diagnoses of U07.1 and U07.2 together with a suitable primary diagnosis. In 2021, for instance, the most common primary diagnoses associated with a secondary diagnosis of COVID-19 in the InEK database were J12.8 (“pneumonia due to other viruses”), B34.2 (“Coronavirus infection without specified location”), and J22 (“unspecified acute lower respiratory infection”). Therefore, we excluded SARI with a secondary diagnosis of COVID-19 from the group of unspecific SARI.

Analyses and Outcomes

Incidence and Characteristics of Influenza/ILI Hospitalizations

The total number of hospitalizations due to influenza/ILI per season is reported for each season and stratified by age group (0–17 years, 18–59 years, and 60 years and older). The hospitalization incidence on the population level is calculated as number of hospitalizations divided by population size as of December 31st of the respective season and reported as number of hospitalizations per 100,000 inhabitants.

Rates for each federal state are reported from 2022–2023 onwards due to insufficient case numbers in earlier seasons and restricted to adults aged 60 years and older, for whom vaccination is generally recommended and vaccination coverage rates are published by RKI. To facilitate the comparison of incidence between federal states, direct age-standardization is used based on the age groups available in the InEK database (60–64 years, 65–74 years, 75–79 years, 80+ years). The total population of Germany served as standard population.

For each season, we report the age distribution of hospitalized patients, the mean length of stay (together with standard deviation), the proportion of patients with ICU treatment, and the proportion of patients dying before discharge (no information of mortality after discharge is available). To explore the occurrence of influenza/ILI in comparison to other SARI and the number of hospitalizations with unspecific diagnoses (that could potentially represent influenza infections), the proportion of influenza/ILI among SARI hospitalizations is calculated.

Estimation of Costs

In Germany, hospitalization costs are calculated using the DRG system. Each hospitalization case is assigned a DRG based on recorded diagnoses, procedures, patient age, and other factors. DRGs are associated with specific cost weights that reflect the average complexity of the case and economic resources required to treat such a case. DRGs are not directly linked to specific diagnoses, but represent broader groups created for reimbursement purposes. For example, an influenza hospitalization may be assigned to the same DRG as a hospitalization caused by another respiratory pathogen if both require similar clinical management. Likewise, uncomplicated influenza hospitalizations are assigned to different DRGs than complex cases that require intensive care and ventilation.

To determine the DRG-specific reimbursement amount the hospital receives for a hospitalization case, the cost weight of the assigned DRG is multiplied by the Bundesbasisfallwert (national base rate), a standardized monetary value set and adjusted annually. In 2024, for instance, the Bundesbasisfallwert was set to €4210.59 per hospitalization. In addition, the hospital receives a daily rate for patient care, which is also adjusted frequently. In 2024, the daily rate was €250 per day of hospitalization. The care revenue is calculated by multiplying the length of stay by the daily rate for patient care.

In accordance with a previous study on the inpatient burden of respiratory syncytial virus in children [15], we calculate the total economic cost of influenza/ILI hospitalizations by adding the DRG-specific reimbursement amount to the average care revenue multiplied by the number of cases for each DRG. The mean costs per hospitalization are calculated as the weighted average (weighted by the number of hospitalizations with a given DRG) of mean costs for each DRG. All cost estimates reflect the observed values during the respective season; no adjustments for inflation are made.

As DRG codes and associated reimbursement amounts change on a yearly basis, all cost estimations had to be conducted using preliminary data deliveries (“unterjährige Datenlieferung”). These data deliveries may differ somewhat from the final data reports available in InEK if late reports occur or corrections are made retroactively. Due to limitations inherent to the database, DRG codes are furthermore omitted if assigned to fewer than five cases. Therefore, all cost estimations are based on a somewhat smaller subset of cases. The proportion of cases with missing cost data is reported and these cases are excluded from the denominator when estimating mean costs.

Results

Observed Number of Influenza/ILI Hospitalizations and Clinical Characteristics

Across the six influenza seasons 2019–2020 to 2024–2025, we observed 240,646 hospitalizations with a main diagnosis of influenza (Table 1). With 87,745 hospitalizations, the highest number of cases was observed in the 2024–2025 season, whereas fewer than 5,000 hospitalizations were observed in each of the 2020–2021 and 2021–2022 seasons. Almost half of all hospitalizations (48.0%) occurred among individuals aged 60 years and older. In the total German population, the incidence of influenza/ILI hospitalizations reached 105.0 cases per 100,000 inhabitants in 2024–2025. In all seasons, incidence was considerably higher among children and older adults than among individuals aged 18-59. Most hospitalizations (92.4%) were coded with ICD-code J10, indicating influenza due to confirmed seasonal influenza viruses (Table S1). The share of cases coded with ICD codes J09 or J11 declined in the post-pandemic period. In 2024–2025, 95.5% of cases were assigned ICD code J10.

The mean length of stay ranged from 4.2 (SD: 4.7) to 6.2 (7.4) days and increased with age (Table 1). In each season, children had on average the shortest (mean ranging from 2.8 to 3.2 days) and older adults the longest hospitalizations (mean ranging from 7.8 to 8.5 days). Between 5.4% and 9.9% of cases received ICU treatment. The proportion of cases requiring ICU treatment was lower among children than among adults. While proportions with ICU treatment were higher among older adults than adults aged 18 to 59 from 2019–2020 to 2022–2023, no notable differences were observed in later seasons.

Altogether, 10,054 in-hospital deaths were observed, of which 41.8% occurred in the 2024–2025 season. Individuals aged 60 years and older accounted for 92.4% of all deaths. In this age group, seasonal in-hospital mortality ranged between 5.8% and 12.3%, while it ranged from 0.2% to 1.7% in the age group 18 to 59 years and did not exceed 0.2% among children.

Proportion of Influenza/ILI Hospitalizations Among All SARI

The proportion of influenza/ILI hospitalizations among all SARI varied between seasons ranging from 0.3% and 1.0% in 2020–2021 and 2021–2022, respectively, to 9.7%, 10.2%, and 14.1% in the three most recent seasons (Table S2). In each season, the proportion of influenza/ILI hospitalizations among all SARI was highest among children and lowest in older adults. The proportion of SARI that could not be attributed to specific pathogens based on assigned ICD codes varied substantially across seasons and age groups. Across all ages, the proportion of unspecific SARI cases ranged from 27.6% to 62.9% per season.

Temporal Distribution of Influenza/ILI Hospitalizations

Figure 1 shows the distribution of weekly hospitalization cases for each season. With more than 9,000 weekly hospitalizations, the highest case numbers were observed during calendar weeks 6–8 of 2025. In all seasons, except for the two pandemic seasons, almost all hospitalizations were clustered during the winter months. In the 2019–2020, 2023–2024, and 2024–2025 seasons, peak case numbers were observed in February, while peak case numbers were reached already in December during 2022–2023. During the latter season, a plateau of case numbers was observed until the beginning of April, albeit at a low level. Peak case numbers were considerably lower among older adults in the 2019–2020 compared to the latest three seasons.

Economic Burden

DRG data enabling cost estimation were available for 236,618 cases (98.3%). The primary reason for missing data was the omission of DRG codes when assigned to fewer than five cases. Figure 3 visualizes estimated mean costs stratified by season and age group. Detailed data on mean and total costs are provided in Table S3. Across all age groups, mean costs increased over time, although lower costs were observed during 2020–2021 and 2021–2022. Mean costs were consistently highest among older adults with a maximum of €5429.70 during 2023–2024 and lowest among children reaching a minimum of €1744.39 in 2020–2021. Extrapolating these mean costs to the total number of influenza/ILI hospitalizations observed resulted in total costs of approximately €390.4 million during the 2024–2025 season, of which €253.2 million (64.9%) were attributable to older adults.

Discussion

Despite a temporary decline in hospitalizations between 2020 and 2022, the clinical and economic inpatient burden due to influenza/ILI in Germany remains substantial. This is the first study assessing this burden in Germany during and after the global COVID-19 pandemic. Our work adds to previous research by providing detailed data on the temporal distribution of inpatient cases during six consecutive seasons, by describing their clinical characteristics, by providing updated estimations on inpatient costs, and by conducting a first analysis of regional variation across Germany’s federal states.

Using similar nationwide inpatient data, Goettler et al. previously investigated the clinical and economic burden of influenza-associated hospitalizations in Germany between 2010 and 2019 [8]. The authors reported peak annual hospitalization numbers, reaching approximately 50,000 cases during calendar year 2018 based on a comparable, albeit somewhat more conservative, method (not including ICD code J11; 5.2% of hospitalizations in our study). We found figures exceeding this number in three consecutive seasons with approximately 53,000, 58,000, and 88,000 inpatient episodes during 2022–2023, 2023–2024, and 2024–2025, respectively. While the upward trajectory observed during these seasons is unlikely to continue indefinitely, given the natural variability in influenza virus circulation, our data demonstrate that influenza/ILI represents a consistent and re-emergent public health issue in the post-pandemic era.

We show that the global COVID-19 pandemic temporarily altered the seasonality of influenza/ILI inpatient cases. In Germany, influenza typically peaks during the winter months. Though case numbers were generally low in 2020–2021 and 2021–2022, seasonal surges were observed in unusual time periods, i.e., between September and December 2020 and between March and June 2021. The 2022–2023 season occurred comparatively early, peaking already in December, but influenza/ILI-associated hospitalizations continued to occur until April. Influenza/ILI hospitalizations returned to typical patterns in the 2023–2024 and 2024–2025 seasons, during which a majority of hospitalizations were clustered between December and March. Overall, intra- and interseasonal trends observed in our study closely align with RKI surveillance of laboratory-confirmed influenza infections in Germany, with outpatient consultations, and with trends in influenza hospitalizations observed in sentinel hospitals [3, 4, 16].

Population immunity, shaped by prior infection patterns, vaccination uptake, and vaccine-strain alignment, likely contributed to the variation in influenza/ILI burden between seasons. Virological surveillance data by the RKI show that circulating influenza strains varied substantially across the study period, ranging from A(H3N2)- to A(H1N1)pdm09-dominated seasons and mixed influenza A/B seasons [3]. Notably, RKI reported good vaccine-strain match and average vaccine effectiveness in the seasons with the highest observed burden (2023–2024 and 2024–2025) [17, 18], arguing against vaccine mismatch as a primary driver of the re-emergent inpatient burden. Despite the considerable variation in circulating influenza stains, the average length of stay, mortality and ICU admission rates were overall similar throughout the study period.

From 2022–2023 onwards, peak weekly case numbers exceeded 7000 hospitalizations with a primary diagnosis of influenza. The true burden of influenza/ILI on the inpatient system is presumably even larger for several reasons. Firstly, cases in which influenza was assigned as secondary diagnosis were not included. Secondly, evidence suggests that complications, such as acute cardiovascular events [19, 20], contribute to the total inpatient burden attributable to influenza/ILI but are not captured in our study [2, 21, 22]. Finally, influenza/ILI likely causes a high demand for clinical resources in periods during which resources may already be strained by other infections and by higher levels of sick leave among healthcare staff.

Compared with Goettler et al.’s earlier study of influenza-associated hospitalizations in Germany [8], we found higher proportions of cases with ICU treatment and higher in-hospital mortality. Our data do not allow us to explore whether this reflects changes in circulating influenza viruses, population immunity, or a trend towards an increasingly vulnerable population since 2019. Population aging and an increase in comorbidity levels may have led to a higher complexity of hospitalized influenza/ILI cases over time. As an assessment of individual comorbidities was not possible in our data, further exploring this hypothesis remains an important avenue for future research.

Due to higher incidence, longer lengths of stay, more frequent ICU treatments, and higher mortality, older adults contribute disproportionately to the clinical and economic inpatient burden attributable to influenza/ILI. In 2024–2025, older adults accounted for approximately €253 million in estimated hospitalization costs, compared to €137 million among individuals aged 0–59. These numbers are in line with other studies showing that older adults account for the majority of the influenza burden in the population, including earlier studies from Germany [6, 8, 23, 24]. Preventing influenza in this age group is therefore important, not only to protect older adults but also to lower the clinical and economic burden on the healthcare system.

Despite a general recommendation for annual vaccination against influenza, vaccination coverage among older adults is insufficient. In 2024–2025, estimated vaccination coverage varied between 52% in the German state of Saxony-Anhalt and 20% in Baden-Württemberg [10]. Influenza vaccination rates are comparatively high in Eastern Germany while Western Germany demonstrates a distinct North-South gradient in coverage [10, 25, 26]. Bavaria has one of the lowest vaccination rates nationally. Correspondingly, we observed a comparatively high incidence of influenza/ILI hospitalizations in this region. By contrast, the neighboring state Baden-Württemberg also exhibits low vaccination coverage but hospitalization rates relative to other federal states were high in some seasons and low in others. Altogether, regional incidence patterns were inconsistent across seasons, and we found no apparent association between regional vaccination coverage and hospitalization incidence in older adults.

The efficacy and effectiveness of influenza vaccination have been established in various randomized trials and observational studies [27–30]. In Germany, influenza vaccine effectiveness was recently corroborated in two test-negative studies conducted by RKI for the comparatively severe 2023–2024 and 2024–2025 seasons [17, 18]. The lack of association between vaccination rates and incidence of influenza/ILI hospitalizations on the ecological level is therefore noteworthy. Since the magnitude of regional variation in hospitalization incidence (up to 200%) far exceeds what could plausibly be attributed to differences in vaccination coverage alone, other factors must contribute to observed differences between federal states. Indeed, socioeconomic indicators and healthcare-related factors, such as physician density, vary notably between German regions and could add to regional disparities in influenza/ILI burden [25]. In addition, variations in diagnostic testing protocols, clinical ICD-coding practices, influenza virus circulation, comorbidity levels, cultural determinants such as social contact patterns, household composition, and healthcare-seeking behaviors may play a role in shaping incidence patterns. Future investigation, for instance, utilizing data on vaccination status and other individual-level characteristics, is warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms behind regional differences in influenza/ILI burden in Germany.

This study is based on high-quality nationwide data. Since reimbursement of inpatient costs is directly linked to reporting, the database can be considered virtually complete. Our results may be used for health-economic modeling, resource planning, and for public health outreach aiming to promote vaccination uptake. However, some limitations need to be considered when interpreting our findings. The estimated mean costs may be biased and total inpatient costs underestimated as complete DRG data were not available for all cases. Moreover, incidence rates on the state level may be over- or underestimated if some individuals are hospitalized in states other than their place of residence. Data on individual-level vaccination status and comorbidity levels would likely offer valuable insights into observed patterns but were not available in the database. The most important methodological concern is arguably a misclassification of the underlying cause of hospitalization. Due to incomplete testing and potential concurrent infection with several pathogens, not all influenza/ILI-related inpatient episodes are assigned ICD-10-GM codes J09–J11 as primary discharge diagnoses. Indeed, we show that a notable proportion of SARI hospitalizations is coded with unspecific ICD-10 codes, suggesting that some influenza/ILI cases could have been missed, leading to an underestimation of the attributable burden in our study. On the other hand, our data may contain hospitalizations that are not caused by influenza. While diagnoses in German hospitals are assigned by physicians and we found the ICD-10-GM code J11, indicating cases without laboratory confirmation, to be rarely used, the data have not been validated against a gold standard. This, in turn, could lead to an overestimation of the influenza burden in our study. To our knowledge, quantitative information on which tests are conducted for diagnosing respiratory viruses in German hospitals is not available, but RKI reports that multiplex-PCR testing has been increasingly used following the COVID-19 pandemic [4]. Overall, the close temporal alignment of identified hospitalizations with surveillance of laboratory-confirmed influenza infections supports the validity of our data.

Conclusions

Influenza/ILI is a consistent and re-emergent public health problem following the COVID-19 pandemic with older adults contributing disproportionately to the clinical and economic inpatient burden. Close alignment between observed cases and national surveillance suggest good validity of administrative data to identify influenza-associated inpatient episodes in Germany. However, future research would greatly benefit from validation studies to strengthen the use of large-scale secondary data for research. Regional variation in hospitalization incidence is substantial, but patterns are inconsistent between seasons. Further research is necessary to understand differences in regional influenza/ILI burden between federal states.
