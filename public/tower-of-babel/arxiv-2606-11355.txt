Dual Gauss–Legendre polynomials
Paweł Woźny

Abstract
We define and investigate two families of dual polynomials associated with the Gauss–Legendre polynomials, which have recently found interesting applications in computer graphics. Using the presented results, one can derive representations of the Gauss–Legendre polynomials, construct the dual bases for Lagrange bases and solve certain approximation problems arising, for example, in CAGD.



1
Introduction

The
Gauss–Legendre polynomials
(
GL polynomials
for short)
and the
Gauss–Legendre curves
(
GL curves
for short)
associated with them were recently introduced by Moon et al. in
[
20
]

(see also
[
12
,
21
,
24
]
). Both GL polynomials
and GL curves possess several interesting properties and therefore constitute
attractive tools with numerous potential applications in numerical analysis and
computer graphics. For example, GL curves have very good shape control
properties. However, they do not lie within the convex hull of their control
points, but they closely follow the control polygon, even at high degrees.

The GL polynomials are related to the classical
Legendre polynomials

P_{k}\in\Pi_{k}\setminus\Pi_{k-1}

(k\in\mathbb{N})
which are orthogonal on

[-1,1]
with respect to the
L^{2}
inner product, i.e.,

\int_{-1}^{1}P_{i}(x)P_{j}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\delta_{ij}\frac{2}{2i+1}\qquad(i,j=0,1,\ldots),

and satisfy the normalization condition
P_{k}(1)=1

(k=0,1,\ldots)
. See, e.g.,

[
13
,
25
]
.

Here,
\Pi_{k}
denotes the space of univariate polynomials of degree at most

k
(
\Pi_{-1}:=\emptyset
), and
\delta_{ij}
is the
Kronecker delta

(
\delta_{ii}:=1
,
\delta_{ij}:=0
for
i\neq j
).

Assumption 1.1
.

From now on, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed. Let
\tau_{i}\equiv\tau^{(n)}_{i}

(i=1,2,\ldots,n;\ n>0)
,

-1<\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\cdots<\tau_{n}<1,

be the zeros of the
n
th Legendre polynomial, i.e.,

P_{n}(\tau_{i})=0

(1\leq i\leq n)
.

In this paper, we assume that the zeros
\tau^{(n)}_{i}

(i=1,2,\ldots,n)
, as
well as the
Gauss–Legendre weights
,

\omega^{n}_{i}\equiv\omega_{i}:=\frac{2}{nP_{n-1}(\tau_{i})P_{n}^{\prime}(\tau_{i})}\qquad(i=1,2,\ldots,n),

(1.1)

are known with high numerical accuracy, since they can be precomputed using,
for example, the results presented in
[
10
,
11
]
.

Remark 1.2
.

Recall that

Q^{GL}_{n}(f):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}f(\tau_{i})\approx\int_{-1}^{1}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

(1.2)

is the
Gauss–Legendre quadrature
, which is exact for all polynomials
of degree at most
2n-1
, i.e.,

\int_{-1}^{1}p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=Q^{GL}_{n}(p)

for every
p\in\Pi_{2n-1}
(see, e.g.,
[
7
]
).

The GL polynomials
F^{n}_{i}\in\Pi_{n}\setminus\Pi_{n-1}

(i=0,1,\ldots,n)
are
given by

F^{n}_{i}(t):=G^{n}_{i}(t)-G^{n}_{i+1}(t),

(1.3)

where
G^{n}_{0}(t)=-G^{n}_{n+1}(t)\equiv\frac{1}{2}
, and

G^{n}_{i}(t):=\frac{nP_{n-1}(\tau_{i})}{2}\int_{-1}^{t}\frac{P_{n}(x)}{x-\tau_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{2}

(1.4)

for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
.

For efficient methods for the evaluation of GL polynomials and their linear
combinations (in particular,
d
-dimensional GL curves), we refer the reader
to
[
4
]
(see also
[
24
]
).

In this paper, we define and study the
dual polynomials
for the
polynomials
F^{n}_{i}
and
G^{n}_{i}
with respect to the Jacobi inner product. The resulting dual systems are expected to be useful in
computer-aided geometric design (CAGD) and numerical analysis, particularly in
some approximation problems.

This work is motivated by the context outlined in
[
20
]
, where GL
polynomials were shown to inherit several structural properties of Bernstein
polynomials, which play a fundamental role in approximation theory and computer
graphics. In a related direction,
dual Bernstein polynomials

(see
[
15
]
)—defined via duality with respect to the (shifted) Jacobi
inner product—have proved to be a powerful tool, for example in addressing
the
degree reduction problem
for parametric
Bézier curves

(see
[
29
]
), which are central objects in CAD and CAGD (see, e.g.,

[
1
,
6
]
).

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
contains a brief
overview of the theory and applications of dual bases. In
Section
3
, which constitutes the main part of the paper, we
derive explicit representations for the dual
G^{n}_{i}
polynomials as well as for
the dual GL polynomials. Finally, Section
4
is devoted to
selected applications of the results presented in this article.

2
Dual bases

Let
B_{m}:=\{b^{m}_{0},b^{m}_{1},\ldots,b^{m}_{m}\}

(m\in\mathbb{N})
be a set of linearly
independent functions. Consider the space
{\cal B}_{m}:=\operatorname{span}B_{m}

with an inner product

\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>:{\cal B}_{m}\times{\cal B}_{m}\to\mathbb{R}
. The set

D_{m}:=\{d^{m}_{0},d^{m}_{1},\ldots,d^{m}_{m}\}\subset{\cal B}_{m}
is called a
dual
basis
for the basis
B_{m}
of the space
{\cal B}_{m}
with respect to the inner
product
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
if and only if

{\cal D}_{m}:=\mbox{span}\;D_{m}={\cal B}_{m}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\left<b^{m}_{i},d^{m}_{j}\right>=\delta_{ij}\quad(0\leq i,j\leq m).

The dual bases are commonly used, for example, in approximation theory,
numerical analysis, and CAGD. We now outline their two main properties.

1.

Representation.
Any function
f\in{\cal B}_{m}
can be represented
in the basis
B_{m}
as follows:

f=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left<f,d^{m}_{k}\right>b^{m}_{k}.

2.

Approximation.
For a given function
g
, an

optimal element

p^{\ast}\in{\cal B}_{m}
in the sense of the

least-squares
approximation has the form

p^{\ast}=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left<g,d^{m}_{k}\right>b^{m}_{k},

which means

||g-p^{\ast}||=\min_{p\in{\cal B}_{m}}||g-p||,

where
||\cdot||:=\sqrt{\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>}
.

Thus, the dual basis allows us to find the optimal element without using an
orthogonal basis. This is very attractive when we want to solve approximation
problems in a specific, non-orthogonal basis, which is often necessary. Note
that the incorporation of dual bases can very often result in algorithms with
lower computational complexity. For these reasons, methods for constructing
dual bases have been intensively studied in recent years. It should also be
mentioned that, for many important bases, the corresponding dual bases are
known explicitly, particularly in the polynomial case, which will be considered
in the following paragraph.

For efficient methods for construction of the dual bases and their further
properties and applications, see, e.g.,
[
27
,
28
]
, as
well as
[
2
,
3

,
19
,
22
,
23
,
26
,
29
,
30
]
(dual Bernstein polynomials),

[
8
]
(dual polynomial bases),
[
9
]
(dual B-spline functional),

[
31
,
32
,
33
]
(dual Wang-Bézier and
Bézier-Said-Wang type generalized Ball polynomials),
[
34
]

(dual NS power basis) and the references cited therein.

2.1
Dual polynomial bases

Let us consider the polynomial case, i.e.,
{\cal B}_{m}=\Pi_{m}
. Let

Q_{m}:=\{q_{0},q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}\}
be the set of orthogonal polynomials with respect
to the inner product
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
. This means that

\left<q_{i},q_{j}\right>=\delta_{ij}h_{i}\qquad(i,j=0,1,\ldots,m;\;h_{i}>0),

(2.1)

and
q_{i}\in\Pi_{i}\setminus\Pi_{i-1}

(0\leq i\leq m)
.

Using the following theorem, one can construct the dual basis of
B_{m}
in terms
of an orthogonal basis of
\Pi_{m}
.

Theorem 2.1

(see Lemma 2.1 in
[
15
]
)
.

Let
c_{ij}
be the coefficients in

q_{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}c_{ij}b^{m}_{j}\qquad(i=0,1,\ldots,m).

Then the elements
d^{m}_{i}

(0\leq i\leq m)
of the dual basis
D_{m}
are
given by

d^{m}_{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}h_{j}^{-1}c_{ji}q_{j}\qquad(i=0,1,\ldots,m).

We also have an identity connecting the three families of polynomials, namely
the elements of the basis
B_{m}
, the dual basis
D_{m}
, and the orthogonal basis

Q_{m+1}
.

Theorem 2.2

(see Lemma 2.2 in
[
15
]
)
.

The following identity holds:

k_{m}(x,y)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}b^{m}_{i}(x)d^{m}_{i}(y),

where
k_{m}(x,y)
is the
Christoffel–Darboux kernel
,

k_{m}(x,y):=\frac{\ell_{m}}{\ell_{m+1}h_{m}}\frac{q_{m+1}(x)q_{m}(y)-q_{m}(x)q_{m+1}(y)}{x-y}.

(2.2)

Here
\ell_{m}
and
\ell_{m+1}
denote the leading coefficients of the
polynomials
q_{m}
and
q_{m+1}
, respectively (see
also (
2.1
)).

3
New dual bases

We will use Theorem
2.1
to derive the dual polynomials for
the polynomials
G^{n}_{i}
and
F^{n}_{i}
with respect to the
Jacobi inner
product
,

\left<f,g\right>_{\alpha,\beta}:=\int_{-1}^{1}(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}f(x)g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\qquad(\alpha,\beta>-1).

(3.1)

Before doing so, however, a lemma and a corollary are also needed.

Lemma 3.1
.

The polynomials
G^{n}_{0},G^{n}_{1},\ldots,G^{n}_{n}
(see (
1.4
)) form
a basis of
\Pi_{n}
. Moreover, any polynomial
p\in\Pi_{n}
can be represented in
this basis as follows:

p(t)=(p(-1)+p(1))G^{n}_{0}(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}p^{\prime}(\tau_{i})\omega_{i}G^{n}_{i}(t)

(see Assumption
1.1
).

Proof.

Let us define

L^{n}_{i}(x):=\prod_{j=1\atop j\neq i}^{n}\frac{x-\tau_{j}}{\tau_{i}-\tau_{j}}

(3.2)

for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. Then

\omega_{i}=\int_{-1}^{1}L^{n}_{i}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\qquad(1\leq i\leq n)

(3.3)

(cf. (
1.1
) and Remark
1.2
). Obviously,
for every polynomial
p\in\Pi_{n}
, we also have

\Pi_{n-1}\ni p^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p^{\prime}(\tau_{i})L^{n}_{i}(x).

Observe that

L^{n}_{i}(x)=\omega_{i}\frac{nP_{n-1}(\tau_{i})}{2}\frac{P_{n}(x)}{x-\tau_{i}}

(3.4)

(cf. (
1.4
) and the proof of
[
20
, Lemma 5]
) and thus

p^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p^{\prime}(\tau_{i})\omega_{i}\frac{nP_{n-1}(\tau_{i})}{2}\frac{P_{n}(x)}{x-\tau_{i}}.

Integrating the above identity over
x\in[-1,t]
, where
-1\leq t\leq 1
,
yields

p(t)-p(-1)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p^{\prime}(\tau_{i})\omega_{i}\left(G^{n}_{i}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\right),

but

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}p^{\prime}(\tau_{i})\omega_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}p^{\prime}(\tau_{i})L^{n}_{i}(x)\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}p^{\prime}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\left(p(1)-p(-1)\right)

(see (
3.3
)) and hence the result.
∎

Taking into account that

G^{n}_{i}(t)+\frac{1}{2}=\sum_{j=i}^{n}F^{n}_{j}(t)\qquad(1\leq i\leq n)

(cf. (
1.3
)), as well as using the
partition of unity
property
for GL polynomials (
[
20
, §5.1]
),

\sum_{i=0}^{n}F^{n}_{i}(t)\equiv 1\qquad(t\in\mathbb{R}),

we obtain the following corollary, which is in agreement
with
[
20
, Theorem 7]
.

Corollary 3.2
.

If
p\in\Pi_{n}
then

p(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(p(-1)+\sum_{j=1}^{i}p^{\prime}(\tau_{j})\omega_{j}\right)F^{n}_{i}(t).

3.1
Jacobi polynomials

Recall that
Jacobi polynomials

P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}\in\Pi_{k}\setminus\Pi_{k-1}

(k\in\mathbb{N})
are
orthogonal with respect to the inner product (
3.1
), i.e.,

\left<P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k},P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{\ell}\right>_{\alpha,\beta}=\delta_{k\ell}h^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}\qquad(k,\ell=0,1,\ldots),

(3.5)

where

h^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}:=2^{\sigma}\frac{\Gamma(k+\alpha+1)\Gamma(k+\beta+1)}{k!(2k+\sigma)\Gamma(k+\sigma)}\qquad(k\in\mathbb{N})

(3.6)

with
\sigma:=\alpha+\beta+1
.

The
leading coefficient
of
P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}
is

\ell^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}=\frac{(k+\sigma)_{k}}{k!2^{k}}\qquad(k=0,1,\ldots),

(3.7)

where
(z)_{i}

(z\in\mathbb{R};\ i\in\mathbb{N})
denotes the
Pochhammer
symbol
,

(z)_{0}:=1,\qquad(z)_{i}:=z(z+1)\cdots(z+i-1)\qquad(i=1,2,\ldots).

We also have

P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(1)=\frac{(\alpha+1)_{k}}{k!},\qquad P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(-1)=(-1)^{k}\frac{(\beta+1)_{k}}{k!},

(3.8)

and

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(x)=\frac{k+\sigma}{2}P^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1)}_{k-1}(x),

(3.9)

where
k=0,1,\ldots
. Here, and in the sequel, we adopt the convention that

P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{-1}(x)\equiv 0.

(3.10)

Clearly, the Legendre polynomials belong to the Jacobi polynomials family,
since
P_{k}(x)\equiv P^{(0,0)}_{k}(x)
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

For further properties and applications of Jacobi polynomials, see, e.g.,

[
13
,
25
]
.

3.2
Dual
G^{n}_{j}
polynomials

The
dual
G^{n}_{j}
polynomials

dG^{n}_{j}(t;\alpha,\beta)\in\Pi_{n}

(0\leq j\leq n;\,\alpha,\beta>-1)
are defined to satisfy the following
conditions:

\left<G^{n}_{i},dG^{n}_{j}(\cdot;\alpha,\beta)\right>_{\alpha,\beta}=\delta_{ij}\qquad(0\leq i,j\leq n).

To derive an explicit representation of the dual polynomials

dG^{n}_{j}(t;\alpha,\beta)
, we apply Theorem
2.1
, where the
Jacobi polynomials—as satisfying the orthogonality
relation (
3.5
)—are expressed in terms of the
polynomials (
1.4
),

\displaystyle P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(-1)+P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(1)\right)G^{n}_{0}(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(x)\Big|_{x:=\tau_{i}}G^{n}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k!}((\alpha+1)_{k}+(-1)^{k}(\beta+1)_{k})G^{n}_{0}(t)+\frac{k+\sigma}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}P^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1)}_{k-1}(\tau_{i})G^{n}_{i}(t),

where
k=0,1,\ldots,n
, which follows from (
3.8
), the
relation (
3.9
) and Lemma
3.1
.

As a consequence, we obtain the explicit formula for the dual
G^{n}_{j}

polynomials.

Theorem 3.3
.

The dual polynomials
dG^{n}_{j}(t;\alpha,\beta)

(0\leq j\leq n;\,\alpha,\beta>-1)
are represented in the Jacobi polynomial
basis as follows:

dG^{n}_{j}(t;\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{1}{h^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{i}}c^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{ji}P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{i}(t)\qquad(j=0,1,\ldots,n)

(3.11)

(cf. (
3.6
)), and

c^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{ji}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\displaystyle\frac{1}{i!}((\alpha+1)_{i}+(-1)^{i}(\beta+1)_{i})&:&(j=0),\\[6.45831pt]
\displaystyle\omega_{j}\frac{i+\sigma}{2}P^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1)}_{i-1}(\tau_{j})&:&(1\leq j\leq n)\end{array}\right.

for
i=0,1,\ldots,n
(cf. the convention (
3.10
)).

Remark 3.4
.

Note that from the representation (
3.11
), it follows that

dG^{n}_{j}(t;\alpha,\beta)
can be evaluated for given
n
,
0\leq j\leq n
,

t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha,\beta>-1
in
O(n)
time using the well-known
Clenshaw algorithm (see, e.g.,
[
7
]
).

We show that there exists a closed-form expression for the dual polynomials

dG^{n}_{j}(t;\alpha,\beta)
if
1\leq j\leq n
.

Theorem 3.5
.

For
j=1,2,\ldots,n
, it holds that

dG^{n}_{j}(t;\alpha,\beta)=2\omega_{j}\frac{(n+1)(n+\sigma)}{h^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(2n+\sigma)_{2}}\frac{b^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n+1,j}P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(t)-b^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{nj}P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n+1}(t)-K^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(\tau_{j},t)}{\tau_{j}-t},

(3.12)

where

b^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{ij}:=\frac{1}{2}(i+\sigma)P^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1)}_{i-1}(\tau_{j})

(i=n,n+1)
, and

K^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x,y):=\frac{P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n+1}(x)P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(y)-P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x)P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n+1}(y)}{x-y}.

Proof.

From (
3.6
), (
3.7
) and Theorem
2.2
,
we have

2\frac{(n+1)(n+\sigma)}{h^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(2n+\sigma)_{2}}K^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x,y)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}G^{n}_{i}(x)dG^{n}_{i}(y;\alpha,\beta).

Differentiating with respect to
x
gives

2\frac{(n+1)(n+\sigma)}{h^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(2n+\sigma)_{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}K^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}^{-1}L^{n}_{i}(x)dG^{n}_{i}(y;\alpha,\beta)

(cf. (
1.4
), (
3.2
), (
3.4
)).
Since
L^{n}_{i}(\tau_{j})=\delta_{ij}
for
1\leq i,j\leq n
, it follows that

dG^{n}_{j}(t;\alpha,\beta)=2\omega_{j}\frac{(n+1)(n+\sigma)}{h^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(2n+\sigma)_{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}K^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(x,t)\Big|_{x:=\tau_{j}}\qquad(j=1,2,\ldots,n).

Using (
3.9
), we obtain the desired result.
∎

Summarizing this part of the paper, note that certain simplifications in the
representation (
3.12
) occur in the Legendre case
(i.e., for
\alpha=\beta=0
), because

K^{(0,0)}_{n}(\tau_{j},t)=\frac{P_{n+1}(\tau_{j})P_{n}(t)}{\tau_{j}-t}\qquad(0\leq j\leq n).

3.3
Dual Gauss-Legendre polynomials

Proceeding similarly as in §
3.2
, we can obtain an
explicit expression for the
dual GL polynomials

dF^{n}_{j}(t;\alpha,\beta)\in\Pi_{n}

(0\leq j\leq n;\,\alpha,\beta>-1)
satisfying
the conditions:

\left<F^{n}_{i},dF^{n}_{j}(\cdot;\alpha,\beta)\right>_{\alpha,\beta}=\delta_{ij}\qquad(0\leq i,j\leq n)

(cf. (
1.3
)).

Namely, the following theorem holds true. We omit its proof, as it is analogous
to the justification of Theorem
3.3
, however, we make use of
Corollary
3.2
(see also
[
20
, Theorem 7]
) in
Theorem
2.1
instead of Lemma
3.1
.

Theorem 3.6
.

The dual GL polynomials
dF^{n}_{j}(t;\alpha,\beta)

(0\leq j\leq n;\,\alpha,\beta>-1)
are represented in the Jacobi polynomial
basis as follows:

dF^{n}_{j}(t;\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{1}{h^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{i}}a^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{ji}P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{i}(t)\qquad(j=0,1,\ldots,n)

(cf. (
3.6
)), and

a^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{ji}:=(-1)^{i}\frac{(\beta+1)_{i}}{i!}+\frac{i+\sigma}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\omega_{\ell}P^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1)}_{i-1}(\tau_{\ell})

for
0\leq i,j\leq n
(cf. the convention (
3.10
)).

There is also a simple relation between the dual GL polynomials and the dual
polynomials introduced in §
3.2
.

Theorem 3.7
.

The following identity holds:

dF^{n}_{j}(t;\alpha,\beta)=dF^{n}_{j-1}(t;\alpha,\beta)+dG^{n}_{j}(t;\alpha,\beta),

where
j=1,2,\ldots,n
.

Proof.

It is enough to observe that

a^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{ji}=a^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{j-1,i}+\frac{i+\sigma}{2}\omega_{j}P^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1)}_{j-1}(\tau_{j})

and use (
3.11
) for
j=1,2,\ldots,n
.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

The last theorem implies that for given
n
,
0\leq j\leq n
,

t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha,\beta>-1
the value
dF^{n}_{j}(t;\alpha,\beta)

can be computed with
O(nj)
computational complexity
(cf. Remark
3.4
).

4
Applications

Let us point out some possible applications of the results presented in this
article. They are related to:
(i)
the representations of the
polynomials
G^{n}_{i}
and
F^{n}_{i}
;
(ii)
the degree reduction problem for
polynomials and parametric curves; and
(iii)
dual Lagrange bases.

4.1
Representations

Recently, in
[
4
]
, new representations of the polynomials
G^{n}_{i}
and

F^{n}_{i}
(see (
1.4
), (
1.3
)) were derived. More
precisely, in this article, the aforementioned polynomials, as well as their
derivatives, were expressed in the
shifted power
basis
(x+1)^{j}
and
the
symmetric Jacobi
basis
P_{j}^{(\alpha,\alpha)}

(cf. §
3.1
). Using these representations, it is possible to
efficient evaluate not only the polynomials
G^{n}_{i}
and
F^{n}_{i}
, but also their
linear combinations in one or many points and thus, in particular, render the

d
-dimentinal GL curve (see (
4.1
)).

We now show that using the dual bases one can give many other representations
of polynomials
G^{n}_{i}
(and consequently also of the polynomials
F^{n}_{i}
).

Let
b^{n}_{0},b^{n}_{1},\ldots,b^{n}_{n}
be a basis of
\Pi_{n}
. Let

d^{n}_{0},d^{n}_{1},\ldots,d^{n}_{n}
be its dual basis corresponding to the inner
product
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{0,0}
(see (
3.1
)), i.e.,

\int_{-1}^{1}b^{n}_{i}(x)d^{n}_{j}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\delta_{ij}

for
0\leq i,j\leq n
(cf. §
2
).

Then

(n+1)\frac{P_{n+1}(x)P_{n}(t)-P_{n}(x)P_{n+1}(t)}{x-t}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}d^{n}_{j}(t)b^{n}_{j}(x),

where
P_{k}
is the
k
th Legendre polynomial
(k=n,n+1)

(cf. Theorem
2.2
and (
3.6
), (
3.7
)).

Setting
t:=\tau_{i}
and remembering that
P_{n}(\tau_{i})=0
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n

(cf. Assumption
1.1
), we obtain

\frac{P_{n}(x)}{x-\tau_{i}}=-\frac{1}{(n+1)P_{n+1}(\tau_{i})}\sum_{j=0}^{n}d^{n}_{j}(\tau_{i})b^{n}_{j}(x).

Integrating the obtained identity over
x\in[-1,t]
, where
-1\leq t\leq 1
,
and using
[
4
, Eq. (4.10)]
gives

G^{n}_{i}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{n}d^{n}_{j}(\tau_{i})g^{n}_{j}(x),

where

g^{n}_{j}(x):=\int_{-1}^{t}b^{n}_{j}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\qquad(j=0,1,\ldots,n).

Summarizing, the coefficients of the expansion of the
polynomials (
1.4
) in the
g^{n}_{j}
basis are, in fact, evaluations
of the elements of the dual basis to the basis
b^{n}_{j}
at zeros of the
n
th
Legendre polynomial. In this context, the representations presented
in
[
4
]
can be also interpreted in this way.

4.2
Degree reduction

The
degree reduction
problem often appears in practical applications,
for example in approximation theory, numerical analysis, and CAGD, as it is
related to data compression and data exchange between different CAD or
computational systems.

In particular, the problem of
constrained degree reduction of
Bézier curves
has been extensively studied in recent years. See, e.g.,

[
29
]
(and the references therein), where a technique using the
so-called
constrained dual Bernstein polynomials
was applied,
resulting in an algorithm with low computational complexity and good numerical
properties.

Let us consider the degree reduction problems for polynomials given in
G^{n}_{i}

and
F^{n}_{i}
bases,

w_{n}(t):=\sum_{i=0}^{n}g_{i}G^{n}_{i}(t),\qquad v_{n}(t):=\sum_{i=0}^{n}f_{i}F^{n}_{i}(t)\qquad(g_{i},f_{i}\in\mathbb{R}).

The goal is to find polynomials
w^{\ast}_{m},v^{\ast}_{m}\in\Pi_{m}
, where
m<n

satisfying

||w_{n}-w^{\ast}_{m}||_{\alpha,\beta}=\min_{w\in\Pi_{m}}||w_{n}-w||_{\alpha,\beta},\qquad||v_{n}-v^{\ast}_{m}||_{\alpha,\beta}=\min_{v\in\Pi_{m}}||v_{n}-v||_{\alpha,\beta}.

Here

||f||_{\alpha,\beta}:=\left(\int_{-1}^{1}(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}f^{2}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\qquad(\alpha,\beta>-1)

(cf. (
3.1
)).

It is well-known (see Section
2
) that

w^{\ast}_{m}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}g^{\ast}_{j}G^{n}_{j}(t),

where

g^{\ast}_{j}:=\left<w_{n},dG^{m}_{j}\right>_{\alpha,\beta}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}g_{i}\left<G^{n}_{i},dG^{m}_{j}\right>_{\alpha,\beta}\qquad(0\leq j\leq m),

as well as

v^{\ast}_{m}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}f^{\ast}_{j}F^{n}_{j}(t),

where

f^{\ast}_{j}:=\left<v_{n},dF^{m}_{j}\right>_{\alpha,\beta}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}f_{i}\left<F^{n}_{i},dF^{m}_{j}\right>_{\alpha,\beta}\qquad(0\leq j\leq m).

The integrals
\left<G^{n}_{i},dG^{m}_{j}\right>_{\alpha,\beta}
and

\left<F^{n}_{i},dF^{m}_{j}\right>_{\alpha,\beta}

(0\leq i\leq n,\ 0\leq j\leq m)

can be computed, for example, using appropriate Gauss–Jacobi quadratures rules
with
n
nodes as
m<n
. In particular, if
\alpha=\beta
the quadrature
rule (
1.2
) can be applied (cf. also
Remark
1.2
).

Note that the considered problems are, among others, closely related to the
degree reduction problem of the parametric GL curves

{\sf P}_{n}:[-1,1]\to{\mathbb{E}}^{d}

(d\in\mathbb{N})

introduced in
[
20
]
,

{\sf P}_{n}(t):=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{\sf W}_{k}F^{n}_{k}(t)\qquad({\sf W}_{0},{\sf W}_{1},\ldots,{\sf W}_{n}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d}).

(4.1)

Remark 4.1
.

It would be interesting to propose a method for the degree reduction of GL
curves (
4.1
), which is similar to that described in
[
29
]

for parametric Bézier curves, where some nonstandard properties of the
(constrained) dual Bernstein polynomials and their relations with
the shifted Jacobi and Hahn orthogonal polynomials were used. Such a method
would probably have lower computational complexity, but it seems to be quite
challenging to derive an efficient algorithm in full detail. We leave this
issue for future research, as it requires further study of the properties of
the new dual polynomial families introduced in this article.

4.3
Dual bases for Lagrange polynomials

Observe that the technique used in the proof of
Theorem
3.5
allows us to derive a dual basis
for the
Lagrange polynomial basis
with respect to a chosen inner
product.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
be distinct points. We
define the Lagrange polynomial basis as follows:

\lambda^{m}_{i}(x):=\prod_{j=0\atop j\neq i}^{m}\frac{x-x_{j}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}\qquad(i=0,1,\ldots,m)

(cf. (
3.2
)). Certainly,

\mathrm{span}\{\lambda^{m}_{i}\,:\,0\leq i\leq m\}=\Pi_{m}
.

Let us fix an inner product

\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>:\Pi_{m}\times\Pi_{m}\to\mathbb{R}
. Let

d\lambda^{m}_{0},d\lambda^{m}_{1},\ldots,d\lambda^{m}_{m}\in\Pi_{m}
be the dual basis for
the Lagrange polynomial basis with respect to the given inner product. This
means that

\left<\lambda^{m}_{i},d\lambda^{m}_{j}\right>=\delta_{ij}

for
0\leq i,j\leq m
(see Section
2
).

We can give a closed-form expression for the dual polynomials
d\lambda^{m}_{i}

in terms of the orthogonal polynomials corresponding to the inner product

\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
.

Theorem 4.2
.

The dual polynomials
d\lambda^{m}_{j}
have the following form:

\displaystyle d\lambda^{m}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\ell_{m}}{\ell_{m+1}h_{m}}\frac{q_{m+1}(x_{j})q_{m}(t)-q_{m}(x_{j})q_{m+1}(t)}{x_{j}-t}

(4.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{m}h_{i}^{-1}q_{i}(x_{j})q_{i}(t),

(4.3)

where
j=0,1,\ldots,m
,
\ell_{m}
and
\ell_{m+1}
denote the leading
coefficients of the polynomials
q_{m}
and
q_{m+1}
, respectively. Here
polynomials
q_{0},q_{1},\ldots,q_{m+1}
form the orthogonal system with respect to
the inner product
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
,

\left<q_{k},q_{\ell}\right>=\delta_{k\ell}h_{k}\qquad(k,\ell=0,1,\ldots,m+1;\;h_{k}>0),

and
q_{k}\in\Pi_{k}\setminus\Pi_{k-1}

(0\leq k\leq m+1)
.

Proof.

From Theorem
2.2
, we have

\frac{\ell_{m}}{\ell_{m+1}h_{m}}\frac{q_{m+1}(x)q_{m}(t)-q_{m}(x)q_{m+1}(t)}{x-t}=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\lambda^{m}_{i}(x)d\lambda^{m}_{i}(t).

Setting
x:=x_{j}

(j=0,1,\ldots,m)
and taking into account that

\lambda^{m}_{i}(x_{j})=\delta_{ij}
, we obtain (
4.2
).

Equation (
4.3
) follows from the well-known representation of the
Christoffel–Darboux kernel (
2.2
) in the appropriate orthogonal
basis.
∎

5
Conclusions and future work

We derive explicit representations for two new families of dual polynomials
associated with the GL polynomials and the Jacobi inner product. These dual
polynomials can be used, in particular, for the solution of certain
approximation problems related to the degree reduction of parametric curves.
We also propose a general methods for deriving new representations of the GL
polynomials and constructing the Lagrange dual bases.

It seems possible to derive a recurrence relation for the dual polynomials

dG^{n}_{j}
with respect to
j
. Using such a recurrence, it will probably be
possible to evaluate all values
dG^{n}_{0}(t;\alpha,\beta),dG^{n}_{1}(t;\alpha,\beta),\ldots,dG^{n}_{n}(t;\alpha,\beta)
for a given

t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha,\beta>-1
faster than in
O(n^{2})
time
(cf. Remark
3.4
).

It would also be important to develop a detailed method for degree reduction
of GL curves that is numerically stable and has low computational complexity.

We leave these questions for future research.
