Riesz α \alpha -capacity of Cantor sets and cyclicity in Dirichlet-type spaces
Dimitrios Vavitsas, Jujie Wu, Konstantinos Zarvalis

Abstract
. We examine the threshold of the cyclicity for functions in Dirichlet-type spaces 𝒟 α \mathcal{D}_{\alpha} , α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \alpha\in(0,1] . Given a fixed α ∗ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \alpha^{*}\in(0,1] , we construct a holomorphic function f ∈ D α ∗ f\in D_{\alpha^{*}} which is cyclic in 𝒟 α \mathcal{D}_{\alpha} for all α < α ∗ \alpha<\alpha^{*} , but fails to be cyclic in 𝒟 α ∗ \mathcal{D}_{\alpha^{*}} . This function serves as a counterexample to the persistence of cyclicity at the critical index α ∗ \alpha^{*} . Throughout the construction process, we work with generalized Cantor sets and study their Riesz α \alpha -capacity.

1.
Introduction

This article explores the cyclicity of functions with respect to the
Dirichlet-type spaces

\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in(0,1]
, of the unit disc
{\mathbb{D}}
in the complex plane
{\mathbb{C}}
. Our objective is to discover whether cyclicity in these spaces is “closed” in the following sense: given a fixed
\alpha^{*}\in(0,1]
, does the cyclicity of
f
in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
, for all
\alpha<\alpha^{*}
, imply the cyclicity of
f
in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha^{*}}
? We will answer this question in the negative by constructing explicit counterexamples.

In order to produce our counterexamples, we shall work beforehand with
generalized Cantor sets
of the unit circle
{\mathbb{T}}\vcentcolon=\partial{\mathbb{D}}
. By the term generalized Cantor set, we mean Cantor sets produced as follows: Take the unit circle and remove an open subarc with
-1
in the middle, so that two closed arcs of equal length with common endpoint
1
remain. Then, remove two open subarcs from their middles, so that four closed arcs remain (two of them have common endpoint
1
). Continue this process inductively, and what remains is a generalized Cantor set in
{\mathbb{T}}
. Such sets are known to be Carleson sets.

We are going to study generalized Cantor sets with regard to their
Riesz

\alpha
-capacity
,
\alpha\in(0,1]
(the case
\alpha=1
yields the well-known
logarithmic capacity
). Riesz
\alpha
-capacity is a tool that gauges the size of subsets of the unit circle, but essentially differs from the Lebesgue measure. As a matter of fact, it provides a more delicate approach in measuring sets, since there exist compact subsets of the unit circle whose Lebesgue measure is zero, yet they are of positive Riesz
\alpha
-capacity for certain parameters
\alpha\in(0,1]
.

Our first objective is to make explicit constructions of generalized Cantor sets satisfying prescribed properties with respect to their Riesz capacities. More specifically, given
\alpha^{*}\in(0,1]
, we will create such a set
E_{\alpha^{*}}
whose Riesz
\alpha
-capacity vanishes for all
\alpha\in(0,\alpha^{*})
, whereas its
\alpha^{*}
-Riesz capacity is positive. In addition, whenever
\alpha^{*}\in(0,1)
we will proceed to a similar construction of a set
F_{\alpha^{*}}
whose Riesz
\alpha
-capacity vanishes if and only if
\alpha\in(0,\alpha^{*}]
.

Explicit constructions of such Carleson sets are of interest in their own right. However, due to the strong ties betweeen potential theoretic tools and functional analysis, we may utilize our generalized Cantor sets to produce results relative to spaces of holomorphic functions. As a matter of fact, the notion of Riesz
\alpha
-capacity is closely related to the cyclicity of functions in the Dirichlet-type spaces of the unit disc. This important topic has been at the forefront of many advancements, from the seminal paper
[
7
]
of Brown and Shields, to a wide variety of high-quality research conducted in the past decades; see
[
6
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
to name a few.

The Dirichlet-type spaces
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
, form a family consisting of Hilbert spaces of holomorphic functions in the unit disc. For the values
\alpha>1
, the space
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
is an algebra. On the other hand, whenever
\alpha\leq 1
,
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
is a Banach space which is not an algebra, and its cyclic vectors play a role similar to that played by invertible elements in algebras. Classical Hilbert spaces such as the Bergman, the Hardy and the Dirichlet spaces are all special instances of Dirichlet-type spaces; in particular, in terms of equivalent norms,
{\mathcal{D}}_{-1}
is the Bergman space,
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
is the Hardy space, and
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
is the Dirichlet space (denoted by
{\mathcal{D}}
). For a presentation of the theory concerning this family of weighted Dirichlet spaces, we refer the interested reader to
[
1
]
,
[
7
]
and
[
12
, Chapter 1]
. More information and concrete definitions follow in Section
2
.

We will mainly deal with Dirichlet-type spaces
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
where the parameter lies in the interval
(0,1]
. These spaces provide a natural scale of Banach spaces linking the classical Hardy space to the classical Dirichlet space. Let us point out that in the Hardy space (
\alpha=0
), a complete characterization of cyclicity was achieved by Beurling; see
[
4
]
. In fact, a function
f
is cyclic in
\mathcal{D}_{0}
if and only if it is outer. The characterization of cyclic in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
functions, for
\alpha\in(0,1]
, remains an open problem.

It can be easily deduced that if
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}
and
f\in\mathcal{D}_{\alpha_{2}}
is cyclic in
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha_{2}}
, then it necessarily belongs to and is cyclic in
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha_{1}}
as well. The exact converse trivially does not hold. So, it is natural to wonder whether the following statement holds:

Problem
.

Fix
\alpha^{*}\in(0,1]
and suppose that
f
is cyclic in
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
, for all
\alpha<\alpha^{*}
. Is
f
necessarily cyclic in
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha^{*}}
?

This question constitutes the main motivation of our work. We are going to answer it in the negative by providing two different explicit counterexamples. Given a function
f
as in the statement above, let us denote its
zero set
by
\mathcal{Z}(f)=\{z\in{\mathbb{D}}\vcentcolon f(z)=0\}
. A wide variety of published work points to the fact that the cyclicity of
f
in the spaces
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
is inextricably related to the size of
\mathcal{Z}(f^{*})
with respect to its Riesz
\alpha
-capacity, where
f^{*}
denotes the radial limit function of
f
. Recall that by Fatou’s Theorem,
f^{*}
is defined almost everywhere on
{\mathbb{T}}
.

With this in mind, we will construct, as we mentioned, a generalized Cantor set
E_{\alpha^{*}}\subset{\mathbb{T}}
that is a Carleson set and whose Riesz
\alpha
-capacity vanishes for all
\alpha\in(0,\alpha^{*})
, whereas its Riesz
\alpha^{*}
-capacity is positive. This will in turn lead to the construction of two fundamentally different functions whose zero set coincides with
E_{\alpha^{*}}
and which act as our desired counterexamples.

The first “threshold” function is constructed exclusively through the fact that
E_{\alpha^{*}}
is a Carleson set and is independent of the actual values of its Riesz capacities. Surprisingly, the resulting function
f
belongs to
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha^{*}}
, can be created to be as smooth as desired on the closed unit disc, but will still not be cyclic in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha^{*}}
. An important takeway is that cyclicity is not a “closed” notion even when the function we investigate satisfies nice differentiability conditions.

On the other hand, the second construction relies profoundly on the vanishing of the Riesz
\alpha
-capacity of
E_{\alpha^{*}}
for
\alpha\in(0,\alpha^{*})
. In fact, the very definition of the function that will play the role of our counterexample is based on the Riesz capacities of neighborhoods of
E_{\alpha^{*}}
with respect to the topology of
{\mathbb{T}}
. The resulting function
f
will belong to the space
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
, for all
\alpha<\alpha^{*}
, will actually be continuous up to the unit circle, and will be cyclic in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
, for all
\alpha<\alpha^{*}
. Nonetheless, either
f\notin{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha^{*}}
(and by extension it is obviously not cyclic in it), or
f\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha^{*}}
but is not cyclic in it.

In both cases, the non-cyclicity of the critical function in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha^{*}}
hinges on the positivity of the Riesz
\alpha^{*}
-capacity of
\mathcal{Z}(f^{*})
. Let us note that, in case
\alpha^{*}=1
, this question is related to the celebrated Brown–Shields Conjecture
[
7
, Question 12]
concerning the characterization of cyclic functions in
{\mathcal{D}}
because, in some sense, our constructions support its validity.

On another note, the interval of the parameters where a polynomial is cyclic in the unit disc setting, in the two-dimensional unit ball setting and in the bidisc setting is closed from above; see
[
3
,
5
,
7
,
20
,
21
,
32
]
for polynomials and a recent discussion with explicit examples in
[
31
]
. Nevertheless, it is natural to seek a counterexample to this phenomenon in the case of general holomorphic functions.

The article is structured as follows: In Section
2
we proceed to a consice presentation of the basic notions and tools we are going to use throughout the course of this work. Then, in Section
3
we will construct the suitable generalized Cantor sets required for solving the
Problem
. Next, in the final two sections of the paper, we will state and prove necessary lemmas, and we will construct our critical functions.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Riesz
\alpha
-energy and Riesz
\alpha
-capacity

We commence with a very useful tool from potential theory. As we have already mentioned in the Introduction, we will make use of the Riesz
\alpha
-capacity of sets on the unit circle. To properly define it, let
\alpha\in(0,1]
and consider the non-negative
kernel

K_{\alpha}:(0,+\infty)\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
given by

(2.1)

K_{\alpha}(t)\vcentcolon=\begin{cases}\log^{+}(2/t),\quad\quad\hskip 2.84544pt\text{for }\alpha=1\\
t^{\alpha-1},\quad\quad\quad\quad\hskip 5.69046pt\text{for }\alpha\in(0,1),\end{cases}

where
\log^{+}(x)\vcentcolon=\max\{0,\log x\}
, for
x>0
. Let
\mu
be a Borel probability measure supported on a compact set
E\subset{\mathbb{T}}
. Its
Riesz

\alpha
-energy
is defined through

(2.2)

I_{\alpha}[\mu]\vcentcolon=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}K_{\alpha}(|\zeta-\eta|)d\mu(\eta)d\mu(\zeta)=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}K_{\alpha,\mu}(\zeta)d\mu(\zeta),

where

(2.3)

K_{\alpha,\mu}(\zeta)\vcentcolon=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}K_{\alpha}(|\zeta-\eta|)d\mu(\eta)

is called the
Riesz

\alpha
-potential
of
\mu
. Then, the
Riesz

\alpha
-capacity
of
E
is given by

\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(E):=\inf\{I_{\alpha}[\mu]:\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)\}^{-1},

where
\mathcal{P}(E)
denotes the set of all Borel probability measures supported on
E
. Note that
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(E)>0
yields
I_{\alpha}[\mu]<+\infty
for at least one measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)
. On the other hand,
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(E)=0
if and only if
I_{\alpha}[\mu]=+\infty
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)
. In case
\alpha=1
, then
I_{\alpha}[\mu]
and
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(E)
are called the
logarithmic energy
of
\mu
and the
logarithmic capacity
of
E
, respectively. The definition of capacity may be extended to general sets (not necessarily compact) on the unit circle by means of the so-called
inner capacity
. Indeed, for any
E\subseteq{\mathbb{T}}
and any
\alpha\in(0,1]
, its inner capacity is given by

\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(E)\vcentcolon=\sup\{\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(F)\vcentcolon\,F\subset E,\,F\text{ compact}\}.

There exists a similar definition for the
outer capacity
, but for Borel sets, the two definitions coincide due to Choquet’s Theorem; see
[
12
, Definition 2.1.8]
and
[
23
, Theorem 5.1.2]
. From now on, for any Borel subset of the unit circle we are going to just use the term capacity for the inner capacity as well.

If
E\subset{\mathbb{T}}
is a compact set and
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(E)>0
, then an
equilibrium measure
for
E
is a measure
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(E)
such that
I_{\alpha}(\mu)\geq I_{\alpha}[\nu]
, for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)
. Evidently, if
\nu
is an equilibrium measure, the definition of capacity dictates that
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(E)=1/I_{\alpha}[\nu]
. In particular, any compact set
E\subset{\mathbb{T}}
has an equilibrium measure, say
\nu
, which satisfies the following properties:

(i)

K_{\alpha,\nu}(\zeta)\leq I_{\alpha}[\nu]
, for all
\zeta\in{\mathbb{T}}
;

(ii)

K_{\alpha,\nu}(\zeta)=I_{\alpha}[\nu],

\alpha
-almost everywhere
on
{\mathbb{T}}
, that is, on the whole
{\mathbb{T}}
except possibly for a set whose Riesz
\alpha
-capacity is zero.

The properties above constitute a slightly different version of Frostman’s Theorem. For a profound account of potential theory and the rich theory of capacity, we point the interested reader to
[
16
]
and the books
[
8
,
12
,
23
]
.

2.2.
Dirichlet-type spaces and cyclicity

Let
\mathrm{Hol}({\mathbb{D}})
denote the class of holomorphic in
{\mathbb{D}}
functions and by
A^{k}({\mathbb{D}})
the subset of
\mathrm{Hol}({\mathbb{D}})
consisting of all holomorphic functions with continuous derivatives of order less than or equal to
k
on the closure of the unit disc. In the case
k=0
, we write
A({\mathbb{D}})\vcentcolon=A^{0}({\mathbb{D}})
for the usual disc algebra. Furthermore, we use the notation
\mathcal{O}(\overline{{\mathbb{D}}})
for the class of all functions holomorphic in a neighborhood of
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
. Given
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
, a function
f\in{\mathrm{Hol}(\mathbb{D})}
with power series expansion

f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}a_{k}z^{k},\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}},

is said to belong to the
Dirichlet-type space

{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
provided that

(2.4)

\|f\|^{2}_{\alpha}\vcentcolon=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}(k+1)^{\alpha}|a_{k}|^{2}<+\infty.

If
f\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
, a direct consequence of (
2.4
) is that
zf\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
as well. In addition, (
2.4
) shows that the family of Dirichlet-type spaces is decreasing in the sense that
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha_{1}}\subset{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha_{2}}
, for any pair of real numbers
\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}
, since evidently
\|\cdot\|_{\alpha_{1}}\geq\|\cdot\|_{\alpha_{2}}
.

Cyclicity in Dirichlet-type spaces is usually defined with respect to the
shift operator
given by
T:{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}\rightarrow{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
,
T(f)\vcentcolon=zf
, which is a well-defined bounded linear transformation. We say that a function
f\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
, is a
cyclic
vector for
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
if

[f]_{\alpha}\vcentcolon=\mathrm{clos}\text{ }\mathrm{span}\{z^{n}f\vcentcolon n=0,1,...\},

where the closure is taken with respect to the norm, coincides with the whole space
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
. The space
[f]_{\alpha}
is closed and invariant under the action of the shift operator.

Remark 2.1
.

It is worth noting that
f
is cyclic in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
if and only if there exists a sequence of polynomials
(p_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
p_{n}f\rightarrow 1
in the
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
-norm. The same holds in case the convergence occurs weakly instead of in the
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
-norm. Equivalently,
f
is cyclic if and only if
1\in[f]_{\alpha}
. We refer to
[
7
, Proposition 5]
for more details.

Additionally, a function
h
of
{\mathbb{D}}
is called a
multiplier
of
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
provided
hf\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
whenever
f\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
. It is known by
[
7
, Proposition 7]
that multipliers leave any subspace
[f]_{\alpha}
invariant. In other words, if
h
is a multiplier and
g\in[f]_{\alpha}
, for some
f\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
, then
hg\in[f]_{\alpha}
as well. We will need this property of multipliers towards the end of our investigation. For more information regarding Dirichlet-type spaces and the corresponding cyclicity, we refer to
[
1
,
4
,
7
,
12
,
22
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
29
,
30
]
.

From now on, we mostly restrict ourselves to the Dirichlet-type spaces
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
, for
\alpha\in(0,1]
. Clearly
\|\cdot\|_{\alpha}
defines a norm and hence the family
({\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha},\|\cdot\|_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in(0,1]}
consists of Banach spaces of holomorphic functions. Moreover, the membership of a function in these spaces can be identified through the computation of an integral. Indeed, for
\alpha\in(0,1]
,
f\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
if and only if

(2.5)

\int_{{\mathbb{D}}}|f^{\prime}(z)|^{2}(1-|z|^{2})^{1-\alpha}dA(z)<+\infty,

where
dA
denotes the area Lebesgue measure. Fix such an
\alpha
and consider a function
f\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
. Then, its radial limit function
f^{*}
is defined through
f^{*}(\zeta)\vcentcolon=\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}f(r\zeta)
, for
\zeta\in{\mathbb{T}}
except possibly for a set of zero Riesz
\alpha
-capacity; see
[
8
, §V, Theorem 3]
which provides a sharper result than Fatou’s Theorem. Besides, even if the function
f
extends continuously to
{\mathbb{T}}
, we will maintain the notation
f^{*}\vcentcolon=f_{|_{{\mathbb{T}}}}
. As we said before, the cyclicity of
f
in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
strongly depends on the Riesz
\alpha
-capacity of the zero set
\mathcal{Z}(f^{*})
. A first remarkable result, resulting from the groundbreaking work of Brown and Shields, and demonstrating this dependence is the following:

Theorem 2.2

(cf.
[
7
, Corollary 1, Theorem 5]
,
[
13
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
and suppose that
f\in\mathcal{{\mathcal{D}}}_{\alpha}
is cyclic in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
. Then,
f
is an outer function and
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{Z}(f^{*}))=0
.

A partial converse statement is provided through
[
13
, Theorem 1.2]
which we state below. We also refer to
[
15
, Corollary 1.2]
for the classical Dirichlet space
{\mathcal{D}}
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
f\in\mathcal{{\mathcal{D}}}_{\alpha}
. Suppose that

(i)

f
is an outer function;

(ii)

|f|
extends continuously to
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
;

(iii)

\mathcal{Z}(f^{*})
is contained in a generalized Cantor set of zero Riesz
\alpha
-capacity.

Then,
f
is cyclic in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
.

The two theorems above and this interplay between cyclicity and Riesz
\alpha
-capacity will be crucial in the sequel.

3.
Generalized Cantor sets

We are ready to proceed to the main body of the article and the first objective of our work. In this section, we will describe the general process of constructing generalized Cantor sets, and then we will construct the explicit examples that we desire.

Since our Cantor sets will be eventually used as zero sets of radial limit functions, we want them to be subsets of the unit circle. Nevertheless, we will define them, as usual, on the rectilinear segment
[0,1]
, and then take their image through the transformation
T(\theta)=e^{i2\pi\theta}
,
\theta\in[0,1]
.

We mostly follow
[
12
, Section 2.3]
. To construct a generalized Cantor set, in the first step, we remove from
E_{0}^{1}\vcentcolon=[0,1]
a middle open segment of length
e_{0}\in(0,1)
so that two closed segments of equal length
d_{1}
remain. We denote them by
E_{1}^{1}
and
E_{1}^{2}
. Then, from each set
E_{1}^{j}
,
j=1,2
, we remove a middle open segment of length
e_{1}\in(0,d_{1})
, so that in total four closed segments of equal length
d_{2}
remain, which we denote by
E_{2}^{j}
,
j=1,2,3,4
. In general, in the
n
-th step of the construction, from each of the existing sets
E_{n-1}^{j}
,
j=1,\dots,2^{n-1}
(who have equal length
d_{n-1}
), we remove a middle open segment of length
e_{n-1}\in(0,d_{n-1})
, so that the sets
E_{n}^{j}
,
j=1,\dots,2^{n}
, remain and they all have equal length
d_{n}
. Set
E_{n}=\cup_{j=1}^{2^{n}}E_{n}^{j}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Then, the
generalized (middle third) Cantor set
associated to the sequence
\{E_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is the set

E\vcentcolon=\bigcap_{n=0}^{+\infty}E_{n}=\bigcap_{n=0}^{+\infty}\bigcup_{j=1}^{2^{n}}E_{n}^{j}.

Setting
d_{0}=1
and due to the way we constructed the Cantor set
E
, we may see that
d_{n}=\mathrm{diam}E_{n}^{j}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, for any
j=1,\dots,2^{n}
. In addition, we may write
e_{n}=\min\{\mathrm{dist}(E_{n+1}^{j},E_{n+1}^{k})\vcentcolon j\neq k,\,E_{n+1}^{j}\cup E_{n+1}^{k}\subseteq E_{n}^{i}\text{ for some }i\in 1,\dots,2^{n}\}
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By construction, the two sequences
\{d_{n}\}
and
\{e_{n}\}
are related through the formula

(3.1)

e_{n}=d_{n}-2d_{n+1},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

Remark 3.1
.

Clearly, the set
E
is linked to the sequences
\{d_{n}\}
and
\{e_{n}\}
. As a matter of fact, picking two such sequences, following the process described above leads to the construction of a generalized Cantor set in
[0,1]
. Of course, the sequences need to fulfill certain requirements. Firstly, we need
d_{0}=1
and
\{d_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,1)
. In addition,
d_{n}
need to decrease (not necessarily strictly) to
0
. Finally, the existence of the positive numbers
e_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, means that
d_{n}-2d_{n+1}>0
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.

Having our set on the segment
[0,1]
ready, we can move to the unit circle. The set
T(E)
associated to the sequence
\{T(E_{n})\}=\{\cup_{j=1}^{2^{n}}T(E_{n}^{j})\}
is a
generalized Cantor set
on
{\mathbb{T}}
. In similar fashion to the case of the real Cantor set above, set
\delta_{n}\vcentcolon=\mathrm{diam}T(E_{n}^{j})
, for any
j=1,\dots,2^{n}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Note that since our generalized Cantor sets are produced by removing middle third open segments in each step, for each
n
, the diameters of the sets
T(E_{n}^{1}),\dots,T(E_{n}^{2^{n}})
, are equal. Moreover, for each
n
, set

\epsilon_{n}\vcentcolon=\min\{\mathrm{dist}(T(E_{n+1}^{j}),T(E_{n+1}^{k}))\vcentcolon j\neq k,\,E_{n+1}^{j},E_{n+1}^{k}\subseteq E_{n}^{i}\text{ for some }i=1,\dots,2^{n}\}.

Evidently, the numbers
\delta_{n}
and
\epsilon_{n}
are related to the numbers
d_{n}
and
e_{n}
, respectively. We prove a first auxiliary lemma demonstrating this relation.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
E
be a generalized Cantor set on the segment
[0,1]
associated to
\{E_{n}\}
and
T\vcentcolon[0,1]\to{\mathbb{T}}
the transformation with
T(\theta)=e^{i2\pi\theta}
. Consider
d_{n},e_{n},\delta_{n},\epsilon_{n}
to be as above. Then:

(a)

|T(\theta_{2})-T(\theta_{1})|=2\sin(\pi|\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}|)
, for all
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in[0,1]
.

(b)

There exists an absolute constant
c>1
such that
\frac{1}{c}d_{n}\leq\delta_{n}\leq cd_{n}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(c)

There exists an absolute constant
c^{\prime}>1
such that
\frac{1}{c^{\prime}}e_{n}\leq\epsilon_{n}\leq c^{\prime}e_{n}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

(a) Fix
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in[0,1]
. Through trivial calculations,
|T(\theta_{2})-T(\theta_{1})|=|e^{i2\pi\theta_{1}}-e^{i2\pi\theta_{2}}|=\sqrt{2-2\cos(2\pi\theta_{1})\cos(2\pi\theta_{2})-2\sin(2\pi\theta_{1})\sin(2\pi\theta_{2})}
. The trigonometric identities
\cos x\cos y+\sin x\sin y=\cos(x-y)
and
2\sin^{2}(x/2)=1-\cos x
, provide the desired outcome.

(b) Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. As we said before,
d_{n}=\mathrm{diam}E_{n}^{j}
and
\delta_{n}=\mathrm{diam}T(E_{n}^{j})
, for any choice of
j=1,\dots,2^{n}
. Pick such a
j
in random and let
\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{n}
be the endpoints of the closed segment
E_{n}^{j}
. Obviously,
d_{n}=|\alpha_{n}-\beta_{n}|
, while
\delta_{n}=|T(\alpha_{n})-T(\beta_{n})|
, with the second equality holding since the set
E_{n}^{j}
is necessarily smaller than a half-circle. So, due to (a), we have
\delta_{n}=2\sin(\pi d_{n})
. But in order to have the Cantor set
E
, it is evident that
\lim_{n\to+\infty}d_{n}=0
. As a result,

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\delta_{n}}{d_{n}}=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{2\sin(\pi d_{n})}{d_{n}}=2\pi.

This last limit proves the existence of the desired absolute constant
c
. Note that for
n=0
, the non-univalence of
T
yields
\delta_{0}=0
, while
d_{n}>0
. Excluding this case, the constant exists for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(c) The proof follows the same procedure as in (b). So we omit it, for the sake of avoiding repetition.
∎

With Lemma
3.2
in our quiver, we can state a variant of
[
12
, Theorem 2.3.5]
which allows us to estimate the Riesz
\alpha
-capacity,
\alpha\in(0,1]
, of a generalized Cantor type set
E
on
{\mathbb{T}}
, just by observing its pre-image
T^{-1}(E)
on
[0,1]
. This is because by (b) and (c) of the previous lemma, the sequences
\{d_{n}\}
,
\{e_{n}\}
and
\{\delta_{n}\}
,
\{\epsilon_{n}\}
, respectively, are Lipschitz equivalent.

Lemma 3.3

(cf.
[
12
, Theorem 2.3.5]
)
.

Let
E
be a generalized Cantor set on the segment
[0,1]
and
T\vcentcolon[0,1]\to{\mathbb{T}}
the transformation
T(\theta)=e^{i2\pi\theta}
. Consider
d_{n}
and
e_{n}
to be as above. Then, for any
\alpha\in(0,1]
,

(3.2)

\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{K_{\alpha}(d_{n})}{2^{n+1}}\leq\frac{1}{\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(T(E))}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{K_{\alpha}(e_{n})}{2^{n}},

where
K_{\alpha}:(0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
is the kernel defined in (
2.1
).

Lemma
3.3
will play a crucial role in our aim of constructing appropriate Cantor sets satisfying specific capacity requirements. Its utility hinges on the fact that if the left-hand side of (
3.2
) diverges, then
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(T(E))=0
, whereas if the corresponding right-hand side converges, then
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(T(E))>0
. Note that the capacity of
T(E)\subset{\mathbb{T}}
is evaluated in terms of the properties of
E\subset[0,1]
. Another viable option would have been to define capacity in general, even for subsets of
[0,1]
and then correlate the positivity of the Riesz
\alpha
-capacity of a set in
[0,1]
with that of its image in
{\mathbb{T}}
; see
[
33
, Proposition 2]
for a relevant application.

We now construct our first generalized Cantor set. We exclude the case
\alpha^{*}=1
since the corresponding result is trivial.

Proposition 3.4
.

Fix
\alpha^{*}\in(0,1)
. There exists a generalized Cantor set
F\subset{\mathbb{T}}
such that
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(F)=0
, for all
\alpha\in(0,\alpha^{*}]
, whereas
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(F)>0
, for all
\alpha\in(\alpha^{*},1]
.

Proof.

In order to achieve our objective, we will first construct the corresponding generalized Cantor set of the segment
[0,1]
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
set

(3.3)

d_{n}\vcentcolon=\left(2^{-\frac{1}{1-\alpha^{*}}}\right)^{n}=2^{\frac{n}{\alpha^{*}-1}}.

Since
1-\alpha^{*}\in(0,1)
, the sequence
\{d_{n}\}
lies in
(0,1]
and strictly decreases from
1
to
0
. On top of that, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
,

\displaystyle e_{n}\vcentcolon

\displaystyle=d_{n}-2d_{n+1}=2^{\frac{n}{\alpha^{*}-1}}-2^{1+\frac{n+1}{\alpha^{*}-1}}

(3.4)

\displaystyle=2^{\frac{n}{\alpha^{*}-1}}\left(1-2^{1+\frac{1}{\alpha^{*}-1}}\right)=2^{\frac{n}{\alpha^{*}-1}}\left(1-2^{\frac{\alpha^{*}}{\alpha^{*}-1}}\right)>0,

where the positivity is due to the fact that
\alpha^{*}/(\alpha^{*}-1)<0
. As a result, through the process we described earlier, the sequences
\{d_{n}\}
and
\{e_{n}\}
define a generalized Cantor set
E
in
[0,1]
. Write
F\vcentcolon=T(E)
, where as usual,
T(\theta)=e^{i2\pi\theta}
,
\theta\in[0,1]
. We will now use Lemma
3.3
to estimate the Riesz
\alpha
-capacity of
F
, for the indices
\alpha\in(0,1]
.

We first deal with the case
\alpha\in(0,\alpha^{*}]
, so we aim to use the left-hand side of (
3.2
). Utilizing also the definition in (
2.1
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{K_{\alpha}(d_{n})}{2^{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{d_{n}^{1-\alpha}2^{n}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{2^{-n\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\alpha^{*}}}2^{n}}

(3.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{2^{n\left(1-\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\alpha^{*}}\right)}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{2^{n\frac{\alpha-\alpha^{*}}{1-\alpha^{*}}}},

where we have applied (
3.3
). Nevertheless, the exponent of
2
in the latter sum of (
3
) is non-positive, and hence the sum diverges. By Lemma
3.3
, we obtain
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(F)=0
.

On the other hand, for
\alpha\in(\alpha^{*},1)
, we are going to need the right-hand side of (
3.2
). This time, using (
3
), we infer

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{K_{\alpha}(e_{n})}{2^{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{e_{n}^{1-\alpha}2^{n}}=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{2^{-n\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\alpha^{*}}}\left(1-2^{\frac{\alpha^{*}}{\alpha^{*}-1}}\right)^{1-\alpha}2^{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left(1-2^{\frac{\alpha^{*}}{\alpha^{*}-1}}\right)^{1-\alpha}}\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{2^{n\frac{a-\alpha^{*}}{1-\alpha^{*}}}}.

This time, the exponent of
2
in the latter sum is strictly positive, regardless of
n
, so the sum converges. Thus, Lemma
3.3
certifies that
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(F)>0
.

Finally, for the case
\alpha=1
, following a similar procedure with the logarithmic capacity, we may once again prove that
\mathrm{cap}_{1}(F)>0
. As a consequence, the set
F
, which by construction is a generalized Cantor set of
{\mathbb{T}}
, satisfies the desired conditions.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

The usual Cantor set
C
on
{\mathbb{T}}
is a well-known example of a set satisfying the condition described by Proposition
3.4
for
\alpha^{*}=1-\log 2/\log 3
; cf.
[
12
, p. 27]
.

We proceed to a second, more involved, construction. The differentiating factor compared to the previous Cantor set, is that in the following result, the interval of the parameters
\alpha
where the capacity of
F
is zero will be open in
(0,1]
, while in the previous case it was closed. In general, Cantor sets where the aforementioned interval is closed are easier to construct. The difficulty principally lies in finding a set where the interval is actually open in
(0,1]
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Fix
\alpha^{*}\in(0,1)
. There exists a generalized Cantor set
F\subset{\mathbb{T}}
such that
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(F)=0
, for all
\alpha\in(0,\alpha^{*})
, whereas
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha^{*}}(F)>0
.

Proof.

As in the previous proposition, we first construct a generalized Cantor set of the interval
[0,1]
. Consider the sequence
\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n\geq 2}
with

(3.6)

\alpha_{n}=\frac{\sqrt{n}(1-\alpha^{*})}{\sqrt{n}-1},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\ n\geq 2.

By (
3.6
), it is evident that there exists some
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\alpha_{n}\in(0,1)
, for all
n\geq n_{0}
. Furthermore,
\{\alpha_{n}\}
is a decreasing sequence converging to
1-\alpha^{*}
, as
n\to+\infty
. Next, set
d_{0}=1
,

(3.7)

d_{n}=\left(2^{-\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}}\right)^{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\ n\geq n_{0},

and pick
d_{1},\dots,d_{n_{0}-1}
, arbitrarily in
(0,1)
, so that the inducing sequence
\{d_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}
is strictly decreasing to
0
. It is easy to check from (
3.7
) that for
n\geq n_{0}
, the sequence
\{d_{n}\}_{n\geq n_{0}}
is indeed strictly decreasing, is a subset of
(0,1)
, and
\lim_{n\to+\infty}d_{n}=0
. Therefore, adding the remaining
n_{0}
terms makes the whole construction possible. Now, as described in the beginning of this section, we construct a generalized Cantor set
E=\cap_{n=0}^{+\infty}\cup_{j=1}^{2^{n}}E_{n}^{j}
, where in the
n
-th step we remove middle open segments of length
e_{n-1}
and closed segments of equal length
d_{n}
remain. For the Cantor set
E
to be well-defined, we need
e_{n}=d_{n}-2d_{n+1}>0
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
; see Remark
3.1
. Clearly, the terms
d_{0},\dots,d_{n_{0}-1}
, can be picked so that this condition holds. For
n\geq n_{0}
, direct algebraic considerations on formulas (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) show that the existence of
a_{n}<1
is equivalent to
\alpha^{*}>\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
. On the other hand,
d_{n}-2d_{n+1}>0
is equivalent to
\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt{n}<\alpha^{*}.
But since
\alpha^{*}>\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
, for all
n\geq n_{0}
, the previous inequality holds too. So, the generalized Cantor set
E\subset[0,1]
is well-defined and in particular, for
n\geq n_{0}
,

(3.8)

e_{n}=\left(2^{-\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}}\right)^{n}-2\left(2^{-\frac{1}{\alpha_{n+1}}}\right)^{n+1}=\left(2^{-\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}}\right)^{n}\left[1-2^{1+n\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{n+1}}\right)-\frac{1}{\alpha_{n+1}}}\right].

As usual, with the aid of the transformation
T\vcentcolon[0,1]\to{\mathbb{T}}
, where
T(\theta)=e^{i2\pi\theta}
, the image
F\vcentcolon=T(E)
yields a generalized Cantor set on the unit circle. We will now measure its capacity
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(F)
, for the indices
\alpha\in(0,\alpha^{*}]
. First, suppose that
\alpha\in(0,\alpha^{*})
. By Lemma
3.3
, we have that

(3.9)

\frac{1}{\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(F)}\geq\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{d_{n}^{1-\alpha}2^{n+1}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{\left(2^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha_{n}}}\right)^{n}2^{n}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2^{1+\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha_{n}}}}\right)^{n}.

However, the sequence
\{\alpha_{n}\}
converges decreasingly to
1-\alpha^{*}
. Therefore, and since
\alpha\in(0,\alpha^{*})
, there exists
n_{1}\geq n_{0}
so that
\alpha_{n}<1-\alpha
, for all
n\geq n_{1}
. This leads to
2^{1+\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha_{n}}}<1
, for all
n\geq n_{1}
. Returning to (
3.9
), the latter sum diverges and consequently
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(F)=0
. The choice of
\alpha\in(0,\alpha^{*})
was arbitrary and hence the desired result holds for all such
\alpha
. We are left with estimating
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha^{*}}(F)
. This time, in view of Lemma
3.3

(3.10)

\frac{1}{\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha^{*}}(F)}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{e_{n}^{1-\alpha^{*}}2^{n}}=c_{n_{0}}+\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{2^{\sqrt{n}}\left[1-2^{1+n\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{n+1}}\right)-\frac{1}{\alpha_{n+1}}}\right]^{1-\alpha^{*}}},

where we have used (
3.6
), (
3.8
) and executed certain quick computations. We need to examine the part in brackets of the latter denominator in order to check the convergence of the corresponding sum. Using (
3.6
), taking limits as
n\to+\infty
, and keeping in mind that
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\alpha_{n}=1-\alpha^{*}
, we may see that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\left[1-2^{1+n\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{n+1}}\right)-\frac{1}{\alpha_{n+1}}}\right]^{1-\alpha^{*}}=\left[1-2^{-\frac{\alpha^{*}}{1-\alpha^{*}}}\right]^{1-\alpha^{*}}.

Applying this limit on (
3.10
), we may find an absolute constant
c>0
such that

\frac{1}{\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha^{*}}(F)}\leq c_{n_{0}}+c\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{+\infty}2^{-\sqrt{n}}<+\infty.

As a result, it is necessary that
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha^{*}}(F)>0
and thus
F
is the desired set.
∎

Finally, we deal with the instance when
\alpha^{*}=1
, in which case the corresponding Riesz capacity turns into the more common logarithmic capacity. The next result is the counterpart of Proposition
3.6
. A counterpart of Proposition
3.4
for
\alpha^{*}=1
does not require a proof, since there obviously exist Cantor sets with positive Riesz-
\alpha
capacity, for all
\alpha\in(0,1]
.

Proposition 3.7
.

There exists a generalized Cantor set
F\subset{\mathbb{T}}
such that
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(F)=0
, for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
, whereas
\mathrm{cap}_{1}(F)>0
.

Proof.

Working as in the previous theorem, we will first construct a generalized Cantor set
E\subset[0,1]
. Consider the sequences

(3.11)

d_{n}=\frac{1}{(n+1)^{n+1}},\quad e_{n}=d_{n}-2d_{n+1},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

These two sequences define a unique generalized Cantor set on the unit circle following the procedure described before, since easily
e_{n}>0
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Consider the transformation
T\vcentcolon[0,1]\to{\mathbb{T}}
with
T(\theta)=e^{i2\pi\theta}
and set
F=T(E)
, which is a generalized Cantor set on
{\mathbb{T}}
. Our goal is to utilize Lemma
3.3
once again. Indeed, for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, by (
2.1
) and (
3.11
) we have

\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{K_{\alpha}(d_{n})}{2^{n+1}}=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{[(n+1)^{1-\alpha}]^{n+1}}{2^{n+1}}=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{(n+1)^{1-\alpha}}{2}\right)^{n+1}=+\infty.

The left-hand side in Lemma
3.3
implies that
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(F)=0
, for any choice of
\alpha\in(0,1)
. We are left with evaluating
\mathrm{cap}_{1}(F)
. Using (
2.1
) and (
3.11
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{K_{1}(e_{n})}{2^{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\log\frac{2(n+1)^{n+1}(n+2)^{n+2}}{(n+2)^{n+2}-2(n+1)^{n+1}}}{2^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\log 2+(n+1)\log(n+1)+(n+2)\log(n+2)}{2^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{3(n+2)\log(n+2)}{2^{n}}

\displaystyle<+\infty,

where the first inequality follows from the relation
(n+2)^{n+2}-2(n+1)^{n+1}\geq 1
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, the second is trivial, while the finiteness of the latter sum can be inferred through the root test. Via the right-hand side in Lemma
3.3
, we find
\mathrm{cap}_{1}(F)>0
. Therefore, the set
F
is the desired set and the proof is complete.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

In both of the preceding propositions, we first worked on the interval
[0,1]
and afterwards we transferred the estimations to the unit circle by a proper transformation. Similarly, it is possible to work on any interval
[a,b]
in order to appropriately modify the size of
E
and its location on the unit circle. In other words, for any fixed
\alpha^{*}\in(0,1],
and for any open arc
I\subset{\mathbb{T}},
there exists a Cantor-type set
E\subset I
such that
\mathrm{cap}_{a}(E)=0,
for all
\alpha\in(0,\alpha^{*}),
and
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha^{*}}(E)>0.

4.
The first construction

To start this section, we need one final definition. Let
E\subset{\mathbb{T}}
be a closed set. Recall that
E
is characterized as a
Carleson
set if

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\log\Big(\frac{1}{\mathrm{dist}(\zeta,E)}\Big)|d\zeta|<+\infty.

It may be directly checked that every generalized Cantor set of the unit circle is a Carleson set. Therefore, the sets constructed in Propositions
3.4
,
3.6
and
3.7
are Carleson sets. For more information on Carleson sets we refer to
[
12
, Section 4.4]
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and consider a Carleson set
E\subset{\mathbb{T}}
. We may write
{\mathbb{T}}\setminus E=\cup_{j=1}^{+\infty}I_{j}
, where
I_{j}
is an arc on the unit circle with endpoints
a_{j},b_{j}
. Let
\phi_{E}\vcentcolon{\mathbb{T}}\to[0,+\infty)
be the function defined by

(4.1)

\phi_{E}(\zeta)=\begin{cases}|(\zeta-a_{j})(\zeta-b_{j})|,\quad\quad\hskip 2.84544pt\text{for }\zeta\in I_{j}\\
\quad 0,\hskip 94.74773pt\text{otherwise }.\end{cases}

Next, pick
N>2k
and consider the function
f_{E,N}:{\mathbb{D}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
, with

(4.2)

f_{E,N}(z):=\exp\Big(\frac{N}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{\zeta+z}{\zeta-z}\log\phi_{E}(\zeta)|d\zeta|\Big).

Following the proof of
[
12
, Theorem 4.4.3]
, the function
f_{E,N}
is outer, lies in
A^{k}({\mathbb{D}})
, and satisfies
\mathcal{Z}(f_{E,N}^{*})=E
; see also
[
8
]
. Note that
A^{k}({\mathbb{D}})\subset\mathcal{D}\subset{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
, for all
k\geq 1
and all
\alpha\leq 1
. Hence,
f_{E,N}
belongs to all the Dirichlet-type spaces we consider in the present work.

The following theorem provides our first critical counterexample described in the main
Problem
. We shall apply Theorem
2.3
in order to inspect the cyclicity of the aforementioned function
f_{E,N}
in the spaces
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Fix
\alpha^{*}\in(0,1]
. There exists a function
f\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha^{*}}
which is cyclic in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha<\alpha^{*}
, but is not cyclic in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha^{*}}
. In particular, for any fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the function may be chosen to belong to
A^{k}({\mathbb{D}})
.

Proof.

Let
E_{\alpha^{*}}\subset{\mathbb{T}}
be either the set constructed in Proposition
3.6
(
\alpha^{*}\in(0,1)
), or the set constructed in Proposition
3.7
(
\alpha^{*}=1
). Both sets are generalized Cantor sets and hence Carleson sets. So we may use (
4.1
) to define the corresponding functions
\phi_{E_{\alpha^{*}}}
. Fix
N>2k
. By (
4.2
), we have a function
f_{E_{\alpha^{*}},N}\in A^{k}({\mathbb{D}})\subset{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha^{*}}
and
\mathcal{Z}(f_{E_{\alpha^{*}},N}^{*})=E_{\alpha^{*}}
. Therefore, by Propositions
3.6
and
3.7
,
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{Z}(f_{E_{\alpha^{*}},N}^{*}))=0
, for all
\alpha\in(0,\alpha^{*})
, whereas
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha^{*}}(\mathcal{Z}(f_{E_{\alpha^{*}},N}^{*}))>0
. Hence, we may apply Theorem
2.3
to deduce cyclicity in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
, for all
\alpha\in(0,\alpha^{*})
. Furthermore, by Theorem
2.2
, the positivity of
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha^{*}}(\mathcal{Z}(f_{E_{\alpha^{*}},N}^{*}))>0
yields non-cyclicity in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha^{*}}
, something that completes the proof.
∎

5.
The second construction

We move on to a construction which is fundamentally different to the previous one. This second approach is inspired by and based on techniques and arguments introduced in
[
6
,
9
]
and
[
12
, Section 3.4]
. In these works, the authors deal with the classical Dirichlet space
{\mathcal{D}}={\mathcal{D}}_{1}
. We are going to appropriately modify the proofs in order to produce results about the Dirichlet-type spaces
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
, something that is partly motivated by
[
6
, Remark 2]
. In our setting, the key difference is that we require uniform estimates with respect to the parameters
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Therefore, we first need to develop the necessary corresponding tools.

We start with a brief mention of another tool of potential theory with great capabilities, the harmonic measure. Even though the harmonic measure may be defined for any domain whose boundary has positive logarithmic capacity, we will only need it in the setting of the unit disc. More specifically, let
E\subseteq{\mathbb{T}}
be Borel. Then, the
harmonic measure of

E

with respect to

{\mathbb{D}}
is the solution of the generalized Dirichlet problem with boundary values
1
on
E
and
0
on
{\mathbb{T}}\setminus E
. By definition, the harmonic measure is a harmonic function, whose values we denote by
\omega(z,E,{\mathbb{D}})
,
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
. It can be proved that

(5.1)

\omega(z,E,{\mathbb{D}})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{E}\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{|z-\zeta|^{2}}|d\zeta|,

where the integrand is the Poisson kernel. An important feature of harmonic measure that we are going to need is its following sense of continuity:

Theorem 5.1

(cf.
[
23
, Theorem 4.3.4]
)
.

Let
E\subseteq{\mathbb{T}}
be Borel and suppose that
\zeta
is in the relative interior of
E
in
{\mathbb{T}}
. Then
\lim_{z\to\zeta}\omega(z,E,{\mathbb{D}})=1
.

Before delving into the main results, we prove an auxiliary lemma that will be of use in the sequel. Let us note that such an inequality is also of fundamental importance with regard to the potential theory in the finite-dimensional ball setting.

Lemma 5.2
.

Fix
\alpha_{1}\in(0,1)
. There exists a constant
C\geq 1
depending solely on the choice of
\alpha_{1}
such that

\frac{1}{|1-z\overline{\eta}|^{1-\alpha}}\leq C\text{ }\mathrm{Re}\Big(\frac{1}{(1-z\overline{\eta})^{1-\alpha}}\Big),

for all
\alpha\in[\alpha_{1},1]
,
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
and
\eta\in{\mathbb{T}}
.

Proof.

Set
z=re^{i\varphi}\in{\mathbb{D}}
and
\eta=e^{i\theta}\in{\mathbb{T}}
, where
\varphi,\theta\in[0,2\pi)
and
r\in[0,1)
. Note that

1-z\overline{\eta}=1-re^{i(\varphi-\theta)}=1-r\cos(\varphi-\theta)-ir\sin(\varphi-\theta),

and thus

\mathrm{Re}(1-z\overline{\eta})=1-r\cos(\varphi-\theta)>0.

Thus, we may write

(5.2)

\arg{(1-z\overline{\eta}})=\arctan\frac{\mathrm{Im}{(1-z\overline{\eta}})}{\mathrm{Re}{(1-z\overline{\eta}})}=\arctan\frac{-r\sin(\varphi-\theta)}{1-r\cos(\varphi-\theta)}\in\left(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right).

In case
\alpha=1
, the statement of the lemma is trivially true for any
C\geq 1
. Next, pick
\alpha\in[\alpha_{1},1)
. One may see that

(5.3)

\mathrm{Re}\Big(\frac{1}{(1-z\overline{\eta})^{1-\alpha}}\Big)=\frac{\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i(1-\alpha)\arg(1-z\overline{\eta})})}{|1-z\overline{\eta}|^{1-\alpha}}=\frac{\cos((1-\alpha)\arg(1-z\overline{\eta}))}{|1-z\overline{\eta}|^{1-\alpha}}.

We wish to prove the existence of an absolute constant
C\geq 1
such that

1\leq C\text{ }|1-z\overline{\eta}|^{1-\alpha}\mathrm{Re}\Big(\frac{1}{(1-z\overline{\eta})^{1-\alpha}}\Big),

which, via (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) is equivalent to

1\leq C\cos{\Big((1-\alpha)\arctan\frac{-r\sin(\varphi-\theta)}{1-r\cos(\varphi-\theta)}\Big)},

and
C\geq 1
does not depend on
r\in[0,1)
,
\varphi,\theta\in[0,2\pi)
, and
\alpha\in[\alpha_{1},1)
. In particular, such a uniform constant exists if

(5.4)

\inf_{\alpha,r,\varphi,\theta}{\cos{\Big((1-\alpha)\arctan\frac{-r\sin(\varphi-\theta)}{1-r\cos(\varphi-\theta)}\Big)}}>0,

where
\alpha,r,\varphi,\theta
lie in the sets mentioned above. Indeed, since the cosine and arctangent functions are even and odd, respectively, we have

\cos{\Big((1-\alpha)\arctan\frac{-r\sin(\varphi-\theta)}{1-r\cos(\varphi-\theta)}\Big)}=\cos{\Big((1-\alpha)\arctan\frac{r|\sin(\varphi-\theta)|}{1-r\cos(\varphi-\theta)}\Big)}.

Furthermore,

(1-\alpha)\arctan\frac{r|\sin(\varphi-\theta)|}{1-r\cos(\varphi-\theta)}\in[0,(1-\alpha)\pi/2),

and hence, by the monotonicity of
\cos x
and
\arctan x
on
[0,\pi/2)
, we obtain

\cos{\Big((1-\alpha)\arctan\frac{r|\sin(\varphi-\theta)|}{1-r\cos(\varphi-\theta)}\Big)}\geq\cos\left((1-\alpha)\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\geq\cos\left((1-\alpha_{1})\frac{\pi}{2}\right)>0,

for all
r\in[0,1)
,
\varphi,\theta\in[0,2\pi)
, and
\alpha\in[\alpha_{1},1)
. This proves the assertion with
C=(\cos((1-\alpha_{1})\pi/2))^{-1}>1
.
∎

Before proceeding, we recall certain standard facts relative to the Gamma function. The binomial series formula

(5.5)

\frac{1}{(1-z)^{\beta}}=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\Gamma(k+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)k!}z^{k},

holds for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
and all positive real numbers
\beta
, where
\Gamma
denotes the Gamma function. Moreover, the Gamma function satisfies
\Gamma(k)=(k-1)!
, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, Gautschi’s inequality states that

(5.6)

k^{1-\beta}<\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+\beta)}<(k+1)^{1-\beta},

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and all
\beta\in(0,1)
; see
[
17
, p. 79, eq. (7)]
.

We are now ready to move on to the main part of this section. We will frequently work with the kernel, the potential, and the energy linked with Riesz
\alpha
-capacity; see Section
2
. For this reason, we will prove a handy lemma concerning the Riesz
\alpha
-energy of a Borel probability measure supported on a compact subset of
{\mathbb{T}}
.

Let
\mu
be such a measure. It is well-known to experts (see, for instance,
[
13
, p. 566]
) that given
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
I_{\alpha}[\mu]
is comparable to
\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{|\hat{\mu}(k)|^{2}}{(k+1)^{\alpha}}
, where

(5.7)

\hat{\mu}(k)\vcentcolon=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}{\bar{\zeta}}^{k}d\mu(\zeta),\quad k\in\mathbb{Z},

denotes
k
-th Fourier coefficient of the measure
\mu
and the comparability constants depend on
\alpha
. Nevertheless we are going to need a slightly modified variation of this comparability. For the sake of clarity and completeness, we follow a standard procedure to provide this comparability whenever
\alpha\in(0,1)
; see
[
12
, Theorem 2.4.4]
for the case
\alpha=1
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Fix
\alpha_{1}\in(0,1).
If
\mu
is a Borel probability measure on
{\mathbb{T}},
then there exists
C>0
depending solely on
\alpha_{1}
such that

\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\Gamma(k+1-\alpha)}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)k!}|\hat{\mu}(k)|^{2}\leq I_{\alpha}[\mu]\leq C\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\Gamma(k+1-\alpha)}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)k!}|\hat{\mu}(k)|^{2},

for all
\alpha\in[\alpha_{1},1).

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in[a_{1},1)
. Applying Lemma
5.2
and relations (
5.5
), (
5.7
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\Gamma(k+1-\alpha)}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)k!}r^{k}|\hat{\mu}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{1}{|1-r\zeta\overline{\eta}|^{1-\alpha}}d\mu(\zeta)d\mu(\eta)

(5.8)

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\Gamma(k+1-\alpha)}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)k!}r^{k}|\hat{\mu}(k)|^{2},

where
C
is the constant from Lemma
5.2
and depends exclusively on
\alpha_{1}
. Thus, we have established the comparabality of the double integral in (
5
) with the series in (
5
).
Moreover, if
I_{\alpha}[\mu]<+\infty
and
r\in(0,1),
then

(5.9)

\frac{2^{1-\alpha}}{(1+r)^{1-\alpha}}I_{\alpha}[\mu]\geq\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{1}{|1-r\zeta\overline{\eta}|^{1-\alpha}}d\mu(\zeta)d\mu(\eta),

where the inequality
|1-w|/|1-rw|\leq 2/(1+r)
, for all
w\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
and all
r\in(0,1)
, has been used. Combining (
5
), (
5.9
) and Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence Theorem yields the left-hand side of the assertion, while Fatou’s lemma provides the right-hand side.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

Utilizing Gautschi’s inequality (
5.6
) and the constant
C=(\cos((1-\alpha_{1})\pi/2))^{-1}>1
from the proof of Lemma
5.2
, we obtain

|\hat{\mu}(0)|^{2}+\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{|\hat{\mu}(k)|^{2}}{k^{\alpha}}\leq I_{\alpha}[\mu]\leq\frac{(\cos((1-\alpha_{1})\pi/2))^{-1}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{|\hat{\mu}(k)|^{2}}{(k+1)^{\alpha}},

for all
\alpha\in[\alpha_{1},1)
. This also yields the well known comparability in
[
13
, p. 566]
.

We continue with the following lemma which constructs a function belonging to prescribed Dirichlet-type spaces and satisfying certain useful properties.

Lemma 5.5
.

Fix
\alpha_{1}\in(0,1)
. Let
E\subset{\mathbb{T}}
be closed,
\alpha\in[\alpha_{1},1)
, and
\mu
be a Borel probability measure supported on
E
with
I_{\alpha}[\mu]<+\infty
. Then, the function
f_{\alpha,\mu}\vcentcolon{\mathbb{D}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
defined via the formula

(5.10)

f_{\alpha,\mu}(z)\vcentcolon=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{1}{(1-z\overline{\eta})^{1-\alpha}}d\mu(\eta),\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}},

is well defined and enjoys the following properties:

(\alpha)

f_{\alpha,\mu}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
and
\|f_{\alpha,\mu}\|_{\alpha}\leq CI_{\alpha}[\mu]^{1/2}
, where
C\geq 1
is a constant that does not depend on
\alpha\in[\alpha_{1},1)
;

(\beta)

|f_{\alpha,\mu}(z)|\leq 1/\textup{dist}(z,E)^{1-\alpha}
, for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
;

(\gamma)

\textrm{Re}f_{\alpha,\mu}(z)>0
, for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}};

(\delta)

there exists a constant
C\geq 1
, not depending on
\alpha\in[\alpha_{1},1)
, such that
\textrm{Re}f^{*}_{\alpha,\mu}\geq\frac{1}{C}K_{\alpha,\mu}
,
\alpha
-almost everywhere in
{\mathbb{T}}
;

(\epsilon)

\textrm{Re}f^{*}_{\alpha,\mu}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
.

Proof.

(
\alpha
) The function
f_{\alpha,\mu}
is holomorphic in
{\mathbb{D}}
with power series expansion given by

(5.11)

f_{\alpha,\mu}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\Gamma(k+1-\alpha)}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)k!}\hat{\mu}(k)z^{k},\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}},

something that can be computed by combining (
5.5
) with (
5.10
) and executing simple calculations. Hence, by (
2.4
) and (
5.11
),

\displaystyle\|f_{\alpha,\mu}\|_{\alpha}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}(k+1)^{\alpha}\frac{\Gamma(k+1-\alpha)^{2}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)^{2}(k!)^{2}}|\hat{\mu}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\Gamma(k+1-\alpha)}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)k!}\frac{(k+1)^{\alpha}\Gamma(k+1-\alpha)}{k!}|\hat{\mu}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\Gamma(k+1-\alpha)}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)k!}\frac{(k+1)^{\alpha}\Gamma(k+1)}{k^{\alpha}k!}|\hat{\mu}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{2^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\Gamma(k+1-\alpha)}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)k!}|\hat{\mu}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{1,\frac{2^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\right\}I_{\alpha}[\mu]

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{1,\frac{2}{\Gamma(1-\alpha_{1})}\right\}I_{\alpha}[\mu],

where in the second line we just did some recombinations, in the third we applied Gautschi’s inequality, in the fourth we made use of the facts that
k+1\leq 2k
and
\Gamma(k+1)=k!
, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, in the fifth we applied Lemma
5.3
, and in the last we made use of the fact that the Gamma function is decreasing in
(0,1),
and hence,
\Gamma(1-\alpha)\geq\Gamma(1-\alpha_{1}),
whenever
\alpha\in[\alpha_{1},1)
. Setting
C=\max\{1,\sqrt{2/\Gamma(1-\alpha_{1})}\}
, we obtain the desired absolute constant.

(
\beta
) Since
E
is the compact support of
\mu
, integration with respect to
\mu
outside of
E
yields zero. Thus, returning to (
5.10
), we get

|f_{\alpha,\mu}(z)|\leq\int_{E}\frac{1}{|1-z\overline{\eta}|^{1-\alpha}}d\mu(\eta)\leq\int_{E}\frac{1}{\textrm{dist}(z,E)^{1-\alpha}}d\mu(\eta)=\frac{1}{\textrm{dist}(z,E)^{1-\alpha}},

for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, since
\mu
is a probability measure and so
\mu(E)=1
.

(
\gamma
) Lemma
5.2
, in conjunction with (
5.10
), implies

\mathrm{Re}f_{\alpha,\mu}(z)=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\mathrm{Re}\Big(\frac{1}{(1-z\overline{\eta})^{1-\alpha}}\Big)d\mu(\eta)\geq\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{1}{C}\frac{1}{|1-z\overline{\eta}|^{1-\alpha}}d\mu(\eta)>0,

for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
.

(
\delta
) Since
f_{\alpha,\mu}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{a}
, the radial limit function
f^{*}_{\alpha,\mu}
exists in
{\mathbb{T}}
, except possibly on a set having Riesz
\alpha
-capacity zero. Pick a point
\zeta\in{\mathbb{T}}
such that
f^{*}_{\alpha,\mu}(\zeta)
exists. By Fatou’s Lemma, Lemma
5.2
and (
2.3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\textrm{Re}f^{*}_{\alpha,\mu}(\zeta)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{E}\liminf_{z\rightarrow\zeta}\textrm{Re}\Big(\frac{1}{(1-z\overline{\eta})^{1-\alpha}}\Big)d\mu(\eta)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{C}\int_{E}\liminf_{z\rightarrow\zeta}\frac{1}{|1-z\overline{\eta}|^{1-\alpha}}d\mu(\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C}K_{\alpha,\mu}(\zeta).

Note that by the proof of Lemma
5.2
, the constant
C
can be chosen to be
(\cos((1-\alpha_{1})\pi/2))^{-1}
, and hence its value does not depend on
\alpha\in[\alpha_{1},1)
, but merely on the initially fixed
\alpha_{1}
.

(
\epsilon
) By Fatou’s Lemma and Fubini’s Theorem, we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\textrm{Re}f^{*}_{\alpha,\mu}(\zeta)|d\zeta|

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{r\rightarrow 1^{-}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\textrm{Re}f_{\alpha,\mu}(r\zeta)|d\zeta|\leq\liminf_{r\rightarrow 1^{-}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{1}{|1-r\zeta\overline{\eta}|^{1-\alpha}}|d\zeta|d\mu(\eta),

and the last limit inferior is finite because
\alpha\in(0,1)
; see
[
28
, Proposition 1.4.10]
. Thus, we infer that
\textrm{Re}f^{*}_{\alpha,\mu}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
.
∎

With all the above lemmas and tools in hand, we are able to build the desired counterexample.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
E\subset{\mathbb{T}}
be a closed set for which we may find a strictly increasing sequence
\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,1)
such that
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\alpha_{n}=1
and
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E)=0
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, there exists
F\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
, for all
\alpha<1
, such that
\lim_{z\rightarrow\zeta}\mathrm{Re}F(z)=+\infty
, for all
\zeta\in E
. The function
F
may be chosen to be continuous on
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}\setminus E
.

Proof.

By assumption
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E)=0
. Therefore, we may create a sequence
\{E_{n}\}
of compact subsets of
{\mathbb{T}}
such that
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})\in(0,1)
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and

(5.12)

\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})\leq\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})^{1/2}<+\infty.

Each
E_{n}
can be chosen so that it is a neighborhood of
E
in the topology of
{\mathbb{T}}
, that is, each point of
E
belongs to the non-empty interior of every
E_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, with respect to the topology of the unit circle. Let
\nu_{n}
be an equilibrium measure of
E_{n}
with respect to
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})>0
. In particular, the potential of
\nu_{n}
satisfies

(5.13)

K_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}}=I_{\alpha_{n}}[\nu_{n}]=\frac{1}{\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})},

\alpha_{n}
-almost everywhere on
E_{n}
; see Section
2
. Following the notation in Lemma
5.5
, the functions
f_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}}
exist and
f_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}}\in{\mathrm{Hol}(\mathbb{D})}
. Thus, we may consider the function

f(z)\vcentcolon=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})f_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}}(z),\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}.

Lemma
5.5
(
\beta
) implies

|f_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}}(z)|\leq\frac{1}{\mathrm{dist}(z,E_{n})^{1-\alpha_{n}}}\leq\frac{1}{(1-|z|)^{1-\alpha_{n}}}\leq\frac{1}{(1-|z|)^{1-\alpha_{1}}},

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Combining this with (
5.12
) and using the Weierstrass M-test, we see that the partial sums of
f
converge locally uniformly to the well-defined function
f
. Then, Weierstrass’ Theorem certifies that
f\in{\mathrm{Hol}(\mathbb{D})}
. On another note,
\mathrm{Re}f_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}}
is positive (see Lemma
5.5
(
\gamma
)) and harmonic in the unit disc. Ergo, it may be written as its Poisson integral, that is,

\mathrm{Re}f_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{|z-\eta|^{2}}\mathrm{Re}f^{*}_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}}(\eta)|d\eta|.

However, by Lemma
5.5
(
\delta
), we have

\mathrm{Re}f^{*}_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}}\geq\frac{1}{C}K_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}},

\alpha_{n}
-almost everywhere on
{\mathbb{T}}
, where
C=\cos(((1-\alpha_{1})\pi/2))^{-1}
does not depend on
n\in\mathbb{N}
. As a result,

(5.14)

\mathrm{Re}f_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}}(z)\geq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{|z-\eta|^{2}}\frac{K_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}}(\eta)}{C}|d\eta|\geq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{E_{n}}\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{|z-\eta|^{2}}\frac{K_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}}(\eta)}{C}|d\eta|.

Applying (
5.13
) on (
5.14
) yields

\mathrm{Re}f_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}}(z)\geq\frac{1}{C\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{E_{n}}\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{|z-\eta|^{2}}|d\eta|.

Nevertheless, the integral at the right is exactly the harmonic measure of
E_{n}
with respect to the unit disc evaluated at
z
, i.e. the quantity
\omega(z,E_{n},{\mathbb{D}})
; see (
5.1
). In particular, we have

\mathrm{Re}f_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}}(z)\geq\frac{\omega(z,E_{n},{\mathbb{D}})}{C\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})},

for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
. Fix
\zeta\in E
and take limit inferiors to obtain

\liminf_{z\to\zeta}\mathrm{Re}f_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}}(z)\geq\frac{1}{C\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})}\liminf_{z\to\zeta}\omega(z,E_{n},{\mathbb{D}}).

Recall that the interior of each
E_{n}
contains
E
, and hence, the fixed point
\zeta
lies in the interior of every
E_{n}
. Therefore, Theorem
5.1
yields
\lim_{z\to\zeta}\omega(z,E_{n},{\mathbb{D}})=1
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Consequently,

(5.15)

\liminf_{z\to\zeta}\mathrm{Re}f_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}}(z)\geq\frac{1}{C\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})},\quad\text{for all }\zeta\in E\text{ and all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, by the definition of
f
,

\liminf_{z\rightarrow\zeta}\mathrm{Re}f(z)\geq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})\liminf_{z\rightarrow\zeta}\mathrm{Re}(f_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}})(z)\geq\frac{N}{C},

which in turn implies that

\lim_{z\rightarrow\zeta}\mathrm{Re}f(z)=+\infty,\quad\text{for all }\zeta\in E.

So,
f
satisfies one of the required conditions. Our next objective is to modify
f
accordingly, so that it is rendered continuous on
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}\setminus E
. Pick an increasing sequence
\{r_{n}\}\subset(0,1)
with
\lim_{n\to+\infty}r_{n}=1
and set

f_{n}(z):=f_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}}(r_{n}z),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

By (
5.15
), we may beforehand choose
\{r_{n}\}
, so that

\mathrm{Re}f_{\alpha_{n},\nu_{n}}(r_{n}\zeta)\geq\frac{1}{2C\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})},

for all
\zeta\in E
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, due to the compactness of
E
. Finally, set

F(z)\vcentcolon=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})f_{n}(z),\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}.

Exactly as before,
F\in{\mathrm{Hol}(\mathbb{D})}
, it is continuous on
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}\setminus E
, and

\lim_{z\rightarrow\zeta}\mathrm{Re}F(z)=+\infty,

for all
\zeta\in E
. It remains to prove that
F\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
, for all
\alpha\in(\alpha_{1},1)
. Note that
f_{n}\in\mathcal{O}(\overline{{\mathbb{D}}})
, and thus,
f_{n}
and the partial sums of
F
lie in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
, for all
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Pick
\alpha\in(\alpha_{1},1)
. Then, there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\alpha_{n}>\alpha
for all
n>n_{0}
. In light of the arguments in Lemma
5.5
(
\alpha
), we obtain that
f_{n}\in\mathcal{D}_{\alpha_{n}}
and in particular

(5.16)

\|f_{n}\|_{\alpha_{n}}\leq CI_{\alpha_{n}}[\nu_{n}]^{1/2}=C\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})^{-1/2},

where the equality is due to the definition of the equilibrium measure, and
C>0
does not depend on the sequence
(\alpha_{n})_{n\geq n_{0}}
. Indeed, the constant may be chosen equal to
\max\{1,\sqrt{2/\Gamma(1-\alpha_{1})}\}
. Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\|F\|_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\left|\left|\sum_{n=0}^{N}\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})f_{n}\right|\right|_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{n_{0}}\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})\|f_{n}\|_{\alpha}+\sum_{n=n_{0}+1}^{N}\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})\|f_{n}\|_{\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{n_{0}}\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})\|f_{n}\|_{\alpha}+\liminf_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\sum_{n=n_{0}+1}^{N}\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})\|f_{n}\|_{\alpha_{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{n_{0}}\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})\|f_{n}\|_{\alpha}+C\liminf_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\sum_{n=n_{0}+1}^{N}\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha_{n}}(E_{n})^{1/2}

\displaystyle<+\infty,

where we have consecutively used that
\|f_{n}\|_{\alpha}\leq\|f_{n}\|_{\alpha_{n}}
, for all
n>n_{0}
, relation (
5.16
), the fact that each
f_{n}
belongs to
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
, and finally relation (
5.12
). Thus,
F\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
. Since the choice of
\alpha\in(\alpha_{1},1)
was arbitrary, we infer that
F\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in(\alpha_{1},1)
. Finally, as we mentioned in Subsection
2.2
, Dirichlet-type spaces form a decreasing family, and hence
F\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha<1
. Summing up, the function
F
has all the desired properties and we are done.
∎

The theorem below provides the second critical counterexample described in the main
Problem
for
\alpha^{*}=1
. Its proof follows in the footsteps of
[
6
, Theorem A]
and
[
12
, Theorem 4.3.2, Theorem 9.2.7]
.

Theorem 5.7
.

There exists a function
f\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}\cap A({\mathbb{D}})
, for all
\alpha<1
, which is cyclic in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
, for all
\alpha<1
, but cannot be cyclic in
{\mathcal{D}}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.7
, there exists a generalized Cantor set (and by extension a Carleson set)
E\subset{\mathbb{T}}
such that
\mathrm{cap}_{\alpha}(E)=0
, for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
, but
\mathrm{cap}_{1}(E)>0
. Then, Theorem
5.6
is applicable and there exists
F\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}\cap C(\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}\setminus E)
for all
\alpha<1
, such that
\lim_{z\rightarrow\zeta}\mathrm{Re}F(z)=+\infty
for all
\zeta\in E
. The function
f\vcentcolon=e^{-F}
clearly belongs to
A({\mathbb{D}})
and satisfies
\mathcal{Z}(f^{*})=E
. Since
f^{\prime}=-fF^{\prime}
with
F\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
, for all
\alpha<1
, while
f
remains bounded, the integral in (
2.5
) provides at once the membership of
f
in every Dirichlet-type space
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
,
\alpha<1
. Revisiting the proof of Theorem
5.6
, we recall that
f=\exp(-\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}f_{n})
, where the sum contains functions
f_{n}\in\mathcal{O}(\overline{{\mathbb{D}}})
with
\mathrm{Re}f_{n}\geq 0
. In particular,
\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\|f_{n}\|_{\alpha}<+\infty
, for all
\alpha<1
. Set
F_{N}\vcentcolon=\exp(-\sum_{n=N}^{+\infty}f_{n})
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
F_{N}\to 1
pointwise and clearly
\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\|F_{N}\|_{\alpha}<+\infty
, for all
\alpha<1
. In view of
[
7
, p.272]
, this implies that for any choice of
\alpha<1
,

(5.17)

F_{N}\rightarrow 1\quad\text{weakly in }{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}.

Furthermore,
F_{N}/f=\exp(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}f_{n})
and thus
F_{N}/f\in\mathcal{O}(\overline{{\mathbb{D}}})
, for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
. This signifies that each function
F_{N}/f
acts as a multiplier in every space
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
; see
[
7
, p.273]
. Therefore
F_{N}=(F_{N}/f)f\in[f]_{\alpha}
, which is a sequentially weakly closed subspace of
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
(since
[f]_{\alpha}
is a closed convex subset). By (
5.17
) we understand that
1\in[f]_{\alpha}
and hence
f
is cyclic in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
, for all
\alpha<1
, by Remark
2.1
. If
f
does not belong in
{\mathcal{D}}
, we are done. Finally, if
f
actually belongs to
{\mathcal{D}}
, the positivity of the logarithmic capacity
\mathrm{cap}_{1}(\mathcal{Z}(f^{*}))=\mathrm{cap}_{1}(E)
yields that
f
cannot be cyclic in
{\mathcal{D}}
in view of Theorem
2.2
.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

One might wonder about the actual membership of
f
in the Dirichlet space
\mathcal{D}
. Suppose that
f\in\mathcal{D}
. If
F=-\log f\in\mathcal{D}
, then
f
must be cyclic in
\mathcal{D}
, see
[
2
, Theorem 4.4]
. As we mentioned, this cannot be true due to the positivity of the logarithmic capacity of
\mathcal{Z}(f^{*})
. Thus, either
f\in\mathcal{D}
and
F\notin\mathcal{{D}}
, or
f\notin\mathcal{D}
.

Finally, we may employ the same scheme for the parameters
\alpha^{*}\in(0,1)
in order to prove the following theorem whose proof we omit for the sake of avoiding repetition:

Theorem 5.9
.

Fix
\alpha^{*}\in(0,1)
. There exists a function
f\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}\cap A({\mathbb{D}})
, for all
\alpha<\alpha^{*}
, which is cyclic in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
, for all
\alpha<\alpha^{*}
, but cannot be cyclic in
{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha^{*}}
.

The function in the theorem above serves as a second critical counterexample in the main
Problem
for
\alpha^{*}\in(0,1)
.

6.
Further Questions and Remarks

There exists a function
f\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
, for all
\alpha<1
, but
f\notin{\mathcal{D}}
. Indeed, such a function is given simply by
f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{k}z^{k}
. In light of the results obtained in Theorems
4.1
,
5.7
and
5.9
, we may formulate another question.

Question
.

Fix
\alpha^{*}\in(0,1]
. Does there exist a function
f\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in(0,\alpha^{*})
, which is cyclic for all
\alpha<\alpha^{*}
, but
f\notin{\mathcal{D}}_{\alpha^{*}}
?

On another note, over the last decade, authors have also investigated cyclicity theory in several variables. A natural question would be whether the procedure described in this work can be extended in the setting of several complex variables.

More specifically, one might wonder about the construction of generalized Cantor sets in the unit ball or polydisc of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, satisfying certain Riesz
\alpha
-capacity properties for prescribed indices
\alpha
. Then, these generalized Cantor sets could be turned into boundary zero sets of a function and the relation between the cyclicity of the function and the positivity of the Riesz
\alpha
-capacity of the set could be studied; see
[
10
,
11
,
21
,
33
]
for related results involving Riesz
\alpha
-capacity and polynomials.

Let us note that Cantor-type sets, and especially corner-like Cantor sets in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, are of great importance in estimating fractional Lipschitz caloric capacities related to fractional heat equations. For instance, we refer the interested reader to
[
18
,
19
]
for recent work on this topic, as well as constructions and capacity estimates of hyper Cantor sets. Consequently, these works provide further motivation for extending the present framework to the multivariable setting.
