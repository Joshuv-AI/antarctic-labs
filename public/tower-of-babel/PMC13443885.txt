RGS22 is a metazoa-specific radial spoke component required for coordinated ciliary beating
Authors: Anxuan Fang, Jiajun Luo, Haomang Xu, Ye-Jun Peng, Wei Xu, Huiwen Cao, Wei Li, Jialei Dai, Zhipeng Shi, Yong Wang, Xiao-Meng Li, Huijie Zhao, Miao Gui, Chao Yu, Xueliang Zhu, Qianting Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In metazoan epithelia, arrays of motile cilia beat in a tissue-wide coordinated manner to drive unidirectional fluid flow. Whether radial spokes (RSs), which mediate mechano-signal transduction between the central pair and axonemal dynein arms, acquire metazoa-specific subunit(s) in evolution to facilitate this function remains poorly known. Here we report that Gα-GTPase activating protein family member RGS22 is a metazoa-specific component of RS1/2 complexes. Mouse Rgs22-deficient motile cilia exhibit ultrastructural defects, including deformation of the RS1 and disorganization of outer dynein arms. These abnormalities correlate with impaired planar polarity, manifested as unsynchronized ciliary beating and disrupted rotational polarity of basal bodies across the ependyma. The motility deficits further lead to progressive ciliary loss and shortening, resulting in postnatal hydrocephalus. Together, our data identify RGS22 an animal-lineage RS1/2 component required for normal RS architecture and associated with tissue-level ciliary motility and coordination phenotypes.

Introduction

Motile cilia are evolutionarily conserved organelles in eukaryotes. They usually project in large numbers (up to hundreds per cell) from the apical surface of metazoan epithelial cells. In mammals, such multiciliated cells (MCCs) are found in diverse tissues, such as brain ventricles, the trachea, the oviduct, and the vas deferens1,2. These motile cilia typically display coordinated, planar beating at both single-cell and tissue levels, generating directional fluid flows that drive fundamental physiological processes, including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation, mucus clearance, and gamete transport3–5. Sperm flagellum, a specialized type of motile cilia, is essential for effective fertilization as it executes the dual functions of progressive motility and forceful penetrating movement6. Impaired ciliary motility is the hallmark of primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD), an inherited human disorder characterized by a range of symptoms including situs inversus, male and female infertility, recurrent respiratory infections, and, less commonly, hydrocephalus7. These symptoms may occur singly or in combination8, reflecting the structural and functional heterogeneity among different types of motile cilia.

Ciliary motility hinges on the integrity of the highly conserved “9 + 2” axonemal structure, which consists of a central pair (CP) of singlet microtubules and 9 peripheral doublet microtubules (DMTs), decorated with radial spokes (RSs), outer dynein arms (ODAs), inner dynein arms (IDAs), and other appendages2,9–11. Along the longitudinal axis of the axoneme, the DMTs are arranged into 96-nm repeats comprising 4 ODAs, 7 IDAs, and 3 RSs12. The mechanoregulation of ciliary bending is proposed to involve dynamic collisions between the CP and RSs, which transmit mechanical signals between the CP and DMT-associated dynein arms13. RSs of choanoflagellates and metazoa, which are believed to share a common ancestor, are similar in morphology but differ markedly from those of other protist model organisms such as Tetrahymena and Chlamydomonas12. The global differences are largely attributed to evolutionary remodeling of individual subunits, including changes in their lengths and type11,13–15. Whether metazoan motile cilia acquire specific RS subunit(s) during the evolution from unicellular Opisthokonta, however, remains elusive.

Ciliary dysfunction, manifesting as abnormalities in ciliary number, length, motility, or beating pattern, often arises from defects in axonemal protein composition or assembly7. Recent advances in ciliary proteomics, driven by genetic studies of PCD, quantitative mass spectrometry, and high-resolution structural analyses integrating cryo-EM or cryo-ET with AI-based prediction, have accelerated the discovery of novel ciliary components7,10,16. A recent study resolving the 96-nm modular repeat of mammalian motile cilia identifies Regulator of G-protein signaling 22 (RGS22) as a constituent of both RS1 and RS2 complexes15. However, despite this structural insight, the physiological function and regulatory mechanisms of RGS22 in mammalian MCCs are largely unknown.

G protein receptor signaling (GPCR) is initiated when Gα subunits exchange GDP for GTP, triggering the dissociation of the inactive Gαβγ heterotrimer17. Regulator of G protein signaling (RGS) proteins are classically defined by their ability to bind active Gα subunits and stimulate their intrinsic GTPase activity, thereby terminating GPCR signaling18. RGS22 is predicted to function as a canonical RGS protein, based on its structural composition, which includes three tandem RGS domains and an additional domain belonging to the RGS superfamily19. The characteristic RGS domains in RGS proteins not only share a conserved core structure with a nine-α-helical bundle, but also achieve Gα-subtype specificity through conserved residues known as “selectivity barcodes” on the Gα-binding surface20. RGS22 is expressed in the testes, reportedly interacting with Gαs21. Moreover, Rgs22 deficiency causes hydrocephalus in rodents and is implicated in human azoospermia21,22. RGS22 is also detected in mass spectrometric results of motile cilia and predicted as a ciliary protein in the CilioGenics database (https://ciliogenics.com)23.

In this study, we demonstrate that RGS22 is a structural component of RS1/2 complexes specifically evolved in metazoa. While dispensable for ciliogenesis, Rgs22 deficiency in the mouse leads to critical ultrastructural defects, including bending or tilting of the RS1 distal region and disorganized ODAs. Multicilia exhibited poorly synchronized beating across ependymal tissues and a significant reduction in motility. Over time, ependymal MCCs manifested progressive shortening and depletion of motile cilia, culminating in hydrocephalus. Since all four RGS domains of RGS22 lack conserved residues critical for Gα binding, it is unlikely to function as a canonical RGS protein. Rather, our findings suggest that RGS22 reinforces structural stability of the RS to possibly facilitate coordinated cilia beating across epithelia.

Results

RGS22 is a metazoa-specific protein related to motile cilia

RGS22 is absent in protists, including choanoflagellates such as Salpingoeca rosetta and Monosiga brevicollis, which are closely related to metazoa, but is widely present in metazoan species that possess motile epithelial cilia and is highly conserved across species (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b)12,24. Beyond the previously predicted cluster of three C-terminal RGS domains (~120 amino acids each)18, our analysis revealed an additional N-terminal RGS superfamily domain. Thus, mouse RGS22 comprises four RGS domains in total, located at residues 408–530, 664–779, 846–975, and 1019–1141, each retaining canonical RGS structural homology (Supplementary Fig. 1a, c–e). However, none of these domains phylogenetically clustered within any of the four established RGS subfamilies (R7, R12, RZ, and R4) (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Furthermore, the conserved critical Gα-interacting residues were largely absent in the four RGS domains (D1–D4) of RGS22 (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Consistently, the electrostatic surface properties of D1–D4 also diverged markedly from those of typical RGS representatives (Supplementary Fig. 2c).

We next examined the expression pattern of RGS22 in mice. Consistent with previous studies21, Rgs22 mRNA was detected exclusively in adult mouse testes (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Its expression initiated at postnatal day 16 (PD16), and increased during testicular development from PD18 to PD69 (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Using a custom RGS22-specific antibody, we confirmed that RGS22 protein was accumulated in elongating spermatids and mature spermatozoa in adult mouse testes, and localized to flagellar axoneme, marked by acetylated-α-tubulin (Ac-tub) (Supplementary Fig. 3c–g). Intriguingly, immunohistochemistry (IHC) analyses also revealed RGS22 enrichment in the ciliary bundles of efferent duct epithelial cells (Fig. 1a). Further investigation showed that RGS22 was specifically expressed in multiciliated epithelial cells of the oviducts, brain ventricles, trachea, and nasal respiratory tracts (Fig. 1a, b and Supplementary Fig. 4a). During brain development, RGS22 expression coincided with ciliogenesis in ventricular ependymal cells (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 4b). At embryonic day 18.5 (E18.5), RGS22 was absent; its expression began postnatally and was observed in the cytoplasm of ependymal cells by PD1. Upon the robust differentiation of motile cilia from PD2 onward, RGS22 predominantly localized to the cilia, with residual expression in the cytoplasm (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 4b). A similar expression pattern was observed in cultured mouse ependymal cells (mEPCs). RGS22 was initially undetectable at 2 days post serum starvation (D2), but its expression progressively increased from D3 to D6, in parallel with ciliogenesis induction (Fig. 1d, e). By D8, RGS22 was clearly transported to ependymal cilia (Fig. 1e). In all, these results demonstrate that RGS22 expression is tightly associated with motile cilia and sperm flagella, both of which possess the characteristic “9 + 2” axonemal architecture.

RGS22 is an RS protein requiring AK8 for cilia localization

To determine the precise distribution of RGS22 in motile cilia, we employed super-resolution structured illumination microscopy (SIM) in mEPCs and immunoelectron microscopy (IEM) in spermatozoa. SIM analyses revealed that RGS22 formed discrete foci along the longitudinal axes of motile cilia (Supplementary Fig. 4c). These foci were mainly distributed in proximal and mid regions of axonemal central lumen, indicated by immunofluorescence signals of Ac-tub (axonemal marker), AK8 (RS marker), and Hydin (CP marker)9,25 (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 4d). Corroborating this distribution, IEM of sperm flagella showed that the majority of RGS22-immunogold particles (67% of 266 counted) were localized within the axonemal central lumen, as observed in both longitudinal and cross-sectional views (Fig. 2b–d). Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) analysis of spermatozoa indicated that RGS22 interacted with multiple axonemal components, particularly RS components, such as AK827,28, CFAP9129, EFCAB5, and RSPH3B (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Consistent with this, RGS22 ciliary localization was markedly disrupted in Ak8 knockout mEPCs (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 5b)28, reinforcing its association with the RS complexes as suggested by established axonemal structural models of motile cilia15. Similar to typical RS proteins, the ciliary localization of RGS22 was independent of the CP, as evidenced by its persistent signal in Wdr47 knockout mEPCs exhibiting the absence of central apparatus formation30 (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Notably, there were no G proteins identified among the IP-MS hits (Supplementary Data 1), in line with our structural analysis that RGS22 may not function as a canonical RGS protein.

Rgs22 deficiency causes hydrocephalus in mice

We generated a constitutive Rgs22 knockout mouse allele (Rgs22−) to investigate the physiological function of RGS22 in mammals (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 6a). By deleting exons 3–7, the mutant allele produced a truncated protein containing only the first 17 amino acids of RGS22, followed by 35 unrelated amino acids resulting from a frameshift (Fig. 3a). Heterozygous (Rgs22+/−) mice were viable, fertile, and phenotypically normal. Intercrossing of Rgs22+/− mice yielded Rgs22−/− (or Rgs22 KO) offspring at the expected Mendelian ratio (25.3% in 1105 pups; Supplementary Fig. 6b). Rgs22−/− mice consistently developed hydrocephalus, with approximately 50% succumbing before PD60, with peak mortality occurring between PD25 and PD45 (Fig. 3c). No sex-specific differences in survival or hydrocephalus severity were observed, with similar mortality rates (~50%) in both males and females within a 3-month observation period; the median lifespan of mice that died before PD60 was 36.5 days for males and 32.9 days for females (Supplementary Fig. 6c). Most Rgs22−/− mice could be visually distinguished from WT or heterozygous littermates by their dome-shaped skulls and reduced body size, which correlated with the severity of hydrocephalus (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 6d, e). Histological analyses revealed dilated lateral ventricles (LV), despite a patent ventricular system (Supplementary Fig. 6f). LV enlargement was absent at birth (PD1), but became apparent by PD8 and PD15 (Fig. 3e, f).

The severity of hydrocephalus typically reflects the degree of CSF flow abnormality, which is directly linked to impaired urotensin neuropeptide delivery and spine curvature, demonstrated in zebrafish models of idiopathic scoliosis31–33. The relationship between hydrocephalus severity and spinal abnormality is recapitulated in the Rgs22-knockout mouse model. Mice with severe hydrocephalus develop overt kyphosis rather than scoliosis, whereas those with mild hydrocephalus show no significant change in kyphosis index (Fig. 3g, h and Supplementary Fig. 6g), underscoring the critical role of CSF flow in spine development.

Rgs22 deficiency causes shortening of epithelial multicilia

IHC staining confirmed the absence of RGS22 throughout the ventricular system, including the epithelia of the LV, third ventricle (3V), aqueduct (AQ), and fourth ventricle (4V) (Supplementary Fig. 7a). In WT brains at PD15, ependymal epithelia were covered with dense arrays of RGS22-positive multicilia. In Rgs22−/− littermates, however, they appeared to be less abundant in MCCs (Fig. 4a). The integrity of ventricular epithelia, however, was maintained in Rgs22−/− brains, as evidenced by continuous lining of S100β-positive ependymal cells (Fig. 4b). Quantification analysis further indicated that the proportion of multiciliated ependymal cells progressively declined with age (Fig. 4c). Furthermore, multicilia were usually shortened comparing with the WT ones (Fig. 4a). Multicilia in Rgs22−/− ventricles at PD30 were shortened as well (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and quantification confirmed significantly reduced cilia number and length in PD30 Rgs22−/− ependymas (Fig. 4d–f). Similar results were observed in tracheal epithelia (Fig. 4g, h and Supplementary Fig. 8a–c), though mucus accumulation was not detected in the paranasal sinuses of Rgs22−/− mice (Supplementary Fig. 8d). To clarify whether or not the ciliary shortening is a direct effect of Rgs22 deficiency, we examined cultured mEPCs, in which serum starvation from D0 can induce multicilia formation from after D334,35. Immunostaining for S100β indicated that the ependymal cell differentiation was not affected at D0 (Supplementary Fig. 8e). Interestingly, both multicilia formation and the percentage of MCCs were comparable between Rgs22−/− and WT samples at D8 (Supplementary Fig. 8f, g). We further examined mEPCs at D10 with SEM and immunofluorescence microscopy. Although the average length of Rgs22−/− multicilia decreased slightly comparing with the WT one, the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 4i, j). Therefore, Rgs22 deficiency does not repress the initial MCC differentiation and multicilia formation.

In summary, RGS22 deletion comprises ciliary maintenance and length in vivo. While the initial multiciliogenesis appears unaffected, multicilia in brain ventricles and airways become structurally fragile, leading to progressive loss of MCCs following mouse development.

RGS22 strengthens structural integrity of ciliary RSs

To determine whether the vulnerability of motile cilia stems from structural defects and given that RGS22 is an RS component required for motile cilia function, we examined the axonemal architecture of motile cilia and sperm flagella following RGS22 deletion. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis showed that the characteristic “9 + 2” microtubule organization remained largely intact in Rgs22−/− samples, with clearly discernible DMTs, CP, RSs, ODAs, and IDAs (Fig. 5a, b). RGS22’s density in cryo-ET maps was notably lower in ependymal cilia compared to sperm flagella (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 9a)13,15,36,37, suggesting context-dependent structural flexibility. The plasticity appeared to be stabilized in flagella via interactions with its flagellum-specific partners such as EFCAB5 and TRiC15 (Fig. 5d). Reconstructed 3D tomograms confirmed the typical “9 + 2” axonemal arrangement in both WT and mutant cilia (Supplementary Fig. 9b, c). Nevertheless, Rgs22−/− cilia exhibited distinct ultrastructural anomalies, manifested by frequent absence or tilting of the RS1 distal region and partial loss of ODAs (Fig. 5e). Subtomogram averaging of Rgs22−/− axonemes further yielded a 96-nm repeat density map of DMTs and associated complexes, exposing significant deviations from the WT reference (EMDB: 37104)13 (Supplementary Fig. 9e, f and Fig. 5f). Specifically, the structural integrity of axonemal complexes was disrupted in the Rgs22−/− sample, as evidenced by the largely diminished RS1 and disorganized ODAs across DMTs, despite the presence of the ODA docking complex (ODA-DC) (Fig. 5f). In contrast, IDAs and the nexin-dynein regulatory complex (N-DRC) were not overtly affected (Fig. 5f).

To further investigate the molecular basis of the structural defect caused by RGS22 deletion, we performed immunofluorescent staining of core axonemal components in mEPCs at Day 10 post-starvation, the same time point used for cryo-ET analysis. First, central apparatus formation was not impaired by RGS22 deletion, as indicated by normal WDR47 staining30 (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Second, the ciliary localizations of key head and neck components of RS1/2, RSPH3B, AK8, and RSPH4A13,15, were unaffected (Fig. 6a, b and Supplementary Fig. 10b), suggesting the presence of the head and neck of RS1 and RS2. Similarly, the head and base components of RS3, AK7, and CFAP6115,38 were also properly localized (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 10c). Third, ODA heavy chain DNAH939 retained robust ciliary localization in Rgs22−/− mEPCs (Fig. 6d). RGS22 deficiency impairs coordinated ciliary beating

The development of hydrocephalus in Rgs22−/− mice in the absence of structural ventricular obstructions suggests impaired CSF circulation. As ependymal multicilia generally achieve tissue-wide directional beating to drive directional CSF flows in mice at PD21 or older3,40, we used mice at PD30 for the assays. While the overall fluid flows beneath Rgs22−/− ependymal surfaces were directional, bead velocities were significantly reduced, especially in tissues from mice with severe hydrocephalus (Fig. 7b, c and Supplementary Movies 1–3). We next examined ciliary beating by live imaging SiR-tubulin-labeled multicilia24. Ependymal cilia beat swiftly back-and-forth with a forward “power (or effective) stroke” and a backward “recovery stroke”1,2,41. Their beat directions are initially diversified and unified gradually in single MCCs. Ciliary beat directions among different MCCs across ependymal tissue plane become unified from PD212,3,24,40. In PD30 WT ependymas, coordinated directional beating within individual cells was clearly evident in pseudo-colored projections merged from three consecutive frames (Fig. 7d). By contrast, Rgs22−/− cilia displayed disorganized beating patterns, reflected by more heterogeneous color distributions in the same visualization (Fig. 7d). In Rgs22−/− mice with mild hydrocephalus, approximately 40% of ciliary bundles exhibited diverse beats, while the majority of the remainder showed directional but unsynchronized beats (Fig. 7d, e and Supplementary Movies 4–6). Ciliary bundles with diverse beats increased to about 80% in Rgs22−/− mice with severe hydrocephalus (Fig. 7d, e). Merged 20-frame projections showed that ciliary bundles in ependymal tissues from Rgs22−/− mice with increasing severity of hydrocephalus also produced increasingly shorter power stroke lengths (Fig. 7d, f), indicative of reduced beating amplitude.

In mouse ependymas, polarities of BBs, i.e., orientations of their basal feet (BFs), in each MCC change from initial diverse directions to a largely uniform one by PD21 to achieve rotational polarity, and rotational polarities in different MCCs across the tissue plane also become unified, a state termed planar cell polarity2,3,24,40,42. As ciliary beat direction also becomes coupled to the BB polarity by PD212,24, the lack of coordinated ciliary beating in PD30 Rgs22−/− ependymas (Fig. 7d, e) suggested defects in establishing rotational polarity. To verify this, we immunostained PD30 ependymal tissues for CEP164 and γ-tubulin to respectively label transition fibers (appendages surrounding the distal end of BBs) and BF tip24. 3D-SIM and quantification analysis confirmed similar BF orientations in individual MCCs and near uniform rotational polarities among different MCCs in the WT ependymas (Fig. 7g). In the Rgs22−/− ependymas, however, BF orientations tended to be diversified (Fig. 7g). On the other hand, BB numbers were similar between WT and Rgs22−/− MCCs (Fig. 7h), indicating that Rgs22 deficiency did not influence the BB biogenesis2,43.

Together, these results demonstrate significantly reduced ciliary beating amplitudes and widespread impairment of ciliary planar polarity in Rgs22−/−– ependymal tissues, leading to irregular ciliary motility and inefficient cilia-driven CSF flow.

Rgs22 knockout mice exhibit impaired sperm motility

Noting that 50% of Rgs22−/− mice survive beyond PD90 and RGS22 localizes to sperm flagella, we evaluated sperm function in adult males. Immunostaining confirmed the absence of RGS22 in Rgs22−/− elongating spermatids and mature spermatozoa (Supplementary Fig. 11). While Rgs22−/− males maintained complete fertility and produced spermatozoa with normal flagellar morphology and length (Supplementary Fig. 12a–e), they exhibited substantial functional deficits. Sperm counts were reduced, and progressive motility was impaired, along with altered kinematic parameters (VCL, VSL, VAP, and ALH) (Supplementary Fig. 12f). This motility defect directly affected fertilization competence: in vitro fertilization (IVF) assays revealed that only 28.1% of oocytes fertilized with Rgs22−/− spermatozoa developed to the 2-cell stage, compared to 83.8% in controls (Supplementary Fig. 12g–i). Cryo-focused ion beam (cryo-FIB) milling and cryo-ET analyses of Rgs22−/− mouse flagella showed normal gross axonemal assembly (Supplementary Fig. 9d). Subtomogram averaging yielded a 96-nm repeat density map of the DMT and associated axonemal complexes. Compared with WT (EMDB: 27444, PDB: 9FQR)15,44, the mutant exhibited weakened density of the distal regions of both RS1 and RS3, along with the loss of the RS2–RS3 bridge. (Supplementary Fig. 13). In contrast, the TRiC complex was preserved; the ODAs and IDAs also appeared unaffected (Supplementary Fig. 13). These findings demonstrate that Rgs22 deficiency impairs the structural integrity of flagellar RSs as well, resulting in defective flagellar motility and a PCD-like phenotype in fertilization competence.

Discussion

We demonstrate that RGS22 is exclusively expressed in MCCs and male germ cells, where it localizes to the central lumen of axoneme in an AK8-dependent manner and co-immunoprecipitates with RS components. This localization pattern is conserved across nearly all types of motile cilia, including those on the apical surface of brain ependyma, tracheal epithelium, efferent ductules and oviducts, as well as in sperm flagella (Figs. 1 and 2 and Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). Consistently, conventional knockout of Rgs22 in mice results in systemic PCD-like phenotypes, characterized by ciliary motility defect-induced postnatal hydrocephalus and reduced sperm count and motility (Figs. 3 and 4 and Supplementary Figs. 6 and 12). Cryo-ET studies further reveal abnormal RS morphologies in both ependymal cilia and sperm flagella (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 13). These results are further corroborated by recently published atomic models of the mammalian 96-nm axonemal repeat, which position RGS22 close to AK8 at the neck region of RS1/2 complexes (Fig. 5c, d)15 and establish RGS22 as a structural component of the RS essential for motile cilia function (Fig. 8).Fig. 8RGS22 is indispensable for coordinated ciliary beating to prevent hydrocephalus.RGS22 is specifically enriched in MCCs, and localizes to the RS1/2 complexes of ciliary axoneme. There, it strengthens structural integrity of RS1 against persistent hydrodynamic strains in brain ventricles. RGS22 is indispensable for coordinated ciliary beating to prevent hydrocephalus.

RGS22 is specifically enriched in MCCs, and localizes to the RS1/2 complexes of ciliary axoneme. There, it strengthens structural integrity of RS1 against persistent hydrodynamic strains in brain ventricles. In Rgs22 knockout mice, ciliogenesis proceeds normally in the ependyma; however, RS1 deformation probably causes structural fragility of axonemes, resulting in shortened motile cilia and progressive MCC loss. The remaining motile cilia exhibit uncoordinated beating and reduced motility, which further compromise CSF flow and lead to hydrocephalus of variable severity.

RGS22 functions to strengthen the structural integrity of RSs (Fig. 8). As RS structures are morphologically highly similar between metazoa and the choanoflagellate S. rosetta12, its absence in choanoflagellates (and other protozoa as well) (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b) does not suggest a role of RGS22 in the assembly and basic function of metazoan RSs. Indeed, as the head-and-neck regions of RS1 and RS2 are compositionally and structurally analogous to each other12,13,15, the unchanged ciliary abundance of their core distal region components (RSPH3B, RSPH4A, and AK8)13,15 and the presence of cryo-ET densities of RS2’s head and neck in Rgs22−/− axonemes (Figs. 5 and 6 and Supplementary Figs. 10 and 13) altogether indicate that RGS22 is dispensable for the assembly of these two RSs. Therefore, we attribute the markedly diminished distal density of RS1 in the 96-nm modular repeat of Rgs22−/− cilia and flagella (Fig. 5e, f and Supplementary Fig. 13) to mechanical strain-induced deformation. RS1 is more isolated from the other two RSs (Fig. 5c) and conformationally more dynamic13. We thus speculate that it probably shoulders higher mechanical stresses than RS2 in beating cilia and flagella and is more liable to bending or tilting. As RGS22 additionally forms the RS2–RS3 bridge in flagellar axonemes (Fig. 5c, d)15, the diminished distal density of RS3 in Rgs22−/− flagellar 96-nm modular repeat is similarly attributed to its impaired mechanical stability due to the loss of the bridge (Supplementary Fig. 13). In contrast, ODAs lack a direct relationship with RGS22 (Supplementary Data 1) and are not affected in Rgs22−/− sperm flagella (Supplementary Fig. 13). Their abnormalities in ependymal cilia (Fig. 5e, f) are thus attributed to indirect effect of Rgs22 deficiency. Ependymal cilia beat asymmetrically in a back-and-forth manner41, whereas sperm flagella generate symmetric bending waves travelling down the flagellum45. Their different beat patterns may on one hand, explain why flagellar RS3 specifically requires the RS2–RS3 bridge to strengthen its structural stability. On the other hand, beating cilia and flagella may exert different mechanical strains on their RSs and ODAs, thus differentially affecting these structures upon Rgs22 deficiency.

RGS22-strengthened RSs are probably important for the maintenance and function of epithelial multicilia (Fig. 8). First, Rgs22−/− multicilia fail to maintain the regular length in vivo. Similar to AK8 and its upstream RS component EFCAB1028, RGS22 is also dispensable for multicilia formation of cultured mEPCs (Fig. 4i, j). Therefore, the shortening of multicilia in Rgs22−/− brain ventricles and airways (Fig. 4a–h) can be attributed to structural fragility of RS1-deformed ciliary axonemes under persistent hydrodynamic strains generated by ciliary beating24,40. This probably further results in the reduction of MCCs with age (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 8a–c). In contrast, hydrodynamic forces generated by multicilia of cultured mEPCs are incomparable to those in brain ventricles40, thus unable to significantly induce the cilia shortening (Fig. 4i, j). Second, ciliary motilities in Rgs22−/− ependymas are impaired comparing to WT. Although Rgs22-deficient PD30 ependymal cilia retain a degree of directional back-and-forth movement, multicilia in a portion of MCCs still remained at initial uncoordinated beating stages (Fig. 7d, e)2,3. Even for those showing coordinated beating, power stroke lengths of multiciliary bundles were significantly reduced (Fig. 7f), probably due to reduced ciliary lengths (Fig. 4d, f and Supplementary Fig. 7). These defects collectively impair the ability of multicilia to efficiently drive CSF flow (Fig. 7a–c), leading to hydrocephalus (Fig. 3d–f), with the severity correlated with the extent of abnormalities in ciliary motilities (Fig. 7). As Rgs22−/− tracheal multicilia are also shortened (Fig. 4g, h), their functions are likely also impaired, but to an extent that is insufficient to trigger obvious PCD symptoms in the mice.

The establishment of tissue-wide coordinated ciliary beating is a gradual, laborious process requiring complicated interplays involving external hydrodynamic forces, planar cell polarity signaling, cilia, BBs, and cytoskeletons2,3,24,40. As indicated by the markedly reduced beads velocities (Fig. 7a–c), we reason that Rgs22−/− ependymal cilia are unable to generate sufficient hydrodynamic forces to induce rotational polarity of BBs, a critical step towards tissue-wide coordinated ciliary beating2,3,24,40, and thus are arrested in a premature state with multiciliary bundles displaying various beat patterns (Fig. 7d, e)2,24. Therefore, we conclude that RGS22 specifically reinforces the structural integrity of metazoan RSs, which in turn ensures proper maintenance and function of epithelial multicilia. Interestingly, its reduced abundance in ciliary distal region (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 4c, d) suggests that RGS22 preferentially reinforces RSs in proximal-to-mid ciliary region. How this contributes to ciliary motility remains to be clarified in the future.

Motile cilia-related gene knockout mice with severe hydrocephalus often manifest a dome-shaped head, reduced body size, brain degeneration, and high mortality24,25,28,30,46–48. While these phenotypes are clearly correlated with defects in cilia-driven CSF flow (Fig. 7)24,25,47, the underlying molecular mechanisms are not fully understood. Recently, mouse circumventricular gland subcommissural organ (SCO) has been reported to secrete peptides into CSF49. Ablation of the SCO also results in severe postnatal hydrocephalus accompanied with all the aforementioned phenotypes, and injection of three SCO-secreted peptides (thymosin β4, thymosin β10, and NP24) into SCO-ablated brain ventricles from PD0 alleviates the severity of hydrocephalus and the accompanying phenotypes49. Although the authors attribute the findings to neurotrophic effects of SCO-secreted peptides49, it remains possible that SCO-secreted peptides and proteins require cilia-driven CSF flow to reach their target sites. Consistently, cilia-driven CSF circulation has been shown to deliver Urotensin neuropeptides and other CSF constituents into zebrafish spinal cord to maintain straight body axis and promote embryonic growth31,33,50. Furthermore, the Reissner fiber, a thin extracellular polymer formed mainly by an SCO-secreted huge glycoprotein (SCO-spondin) along spinal cord central canal, also requires cilia-driven CSF flow to properly assemble for regulating axial straightening with CSF-contacting neurons32,33,51. Accordingly, defective CSF flow results in scoliosis in adult zebrafish, a phenotype that closely resembles adolescent idiopathic scoliosis in human31,33, and is correlated with kyphosis in adult mice (Fig. 3g, h)24. Future investigations, however, are required to elucidate detailed molecular mechanisms.

Dysfunctions of many RS components are known to result in multiple morphological abnormalities of the sperm flagella (MMAF)7,15. For instance, IQUB or LRRC23 depletion causes disruption of RS1 or RS3, respectively, leading to impaired sperm motility and male infertility52–54. Similarly, the loss of either EFCAB10 or AK8 leads to complete male infertility due to immotile spermatozoa27,28. RGS22 and AK8 also participate in two sperm-specific assemblies: the RS-tethered TRiC chaperone and the RS2–RS3 bridge, where they interact with the RS3 component EFCAB5 (Fig. 5c, d)15. However, Rgs22-deficient males retain complete fertility, exhibiting only minor impairments in sperm motility and in vitro fertilization capacity (Supplementary Fig. 12). Therefore, RGS22 does not function as a core structural scaffold in the flagellum, but rather reflects an evolutionary adaptation to optimize sperm performance.

The molecular function of RGS22 as a putative RGS protein has remained elusive. The recent study proposed that it may prevent hydrocephalus by acting as a negative regulator of lysophosphatidic acid receptor (LPAR) signaling22, based on established associations between LPAR overactivation and neonatal post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus55. Our findings, however, reveal that RGS22 diverges fundamentally from canonical RGS proteins. Unlike typical RGS proteins that contain a single RGS domain, RGS22 possesses four, none of which cluster phylogenetically with established RGS subfamilies (Supplementary Fig. 2a). More strikingly, all four RGS domains of RGS22 lack the conserved residues critical for Gα-binding in canonical RGS proteins20 (Supplementary Fig. 2b). This structural divergence parallels that of Axin, whose RGS domain likewise fails to interact with or stimulate Gα-GTP hydrolysis56. However, biomedical functions of the putative RGS domains in RGS22 have yet to be elucidated, highlighting an important direction for future investigation.

Methods

The animal care and experimental procedures were performed according to the guidelines of the Laboratory Animal Welfare of Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University (#ZJU20220143).

Mice

Mice (C57BL/6J) carrying the deleted allele of Rgs22 were generated by GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. via the CRISPR-Cas9 technique. The sgRNAs and targeting positions are illustrated in Fig. 3a. Heterozygous (Rgs22+/−) mice were intercrossed to generate Rgs22 knockout (Rgs22−/−) mice. Wdr47 knockout mice and Ak8 knockout mice were reported previously28,30. Sex was not considered in the analysis except for inherently sex-specific assessments (e.g., sperm analysis in males), as no general sex-specific differences were observed. All mice were maintained in an SPF facility under controlled environmental conditions: 12-h light/dark cycle, ambient temperature of 20-–22 °C, and relative humidity of 50–70%. Animals were provided with autoclaved food and water ad libitum. Tail tips or toe clips were subjected to genotyping. The genotyping primers were generated by SUNY Co., China, and are available upon request, with the sequences provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Fertility test

For the male fertility test, adult Rgs22−/− male or its littermate (Rgs22+/+ or Rgs22+/−) was crossed with 8-week-old WT females at the ration of 1:2 and the breeding cages were monitored continuously for 3–5 months. Fertility parameters were evaluated through daily vaginal plug checks, timed pregnancy confirmation via observed deliveries, and litter size quantification. The fertility rate was then calculated as the number of pups per litter.

Structure prediction, domain alignment, and visualization

Protein structures were obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database. The RGS domain annotations were retrieved from the NCBI Conserved Domain Database57. Sequence alignment of RGS domains was performed using the established method20. Due to substantial sequence divergence from canonical RGS domains, pairwise flexible jFATCAT alignment was performed with RGS4 as a reference to align the domains of AXIN1, AXIN2, and RGS2258. Conserved residues on the Gα binding surface were identified based on previously characterized positions20. Phylogenetic analyses were performed using MEGA11, with subsequent tree visualization in EvolView v359,60. All structural analyses and visualizations were conducted using PyMOL (Schrodinger, LLC, 2025) and ChimeraX (UCSF).

Semi-quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from mouse tissues using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, #74104). First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed with the PrimeScript™ II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (TaKaRa, #6210A). Semi-quantitative PCR was conducted using 2 × Taq DNA Polymerase Mix (A&G, #AG11019) following standard procedures (22–30 cycles). The primer sequences targeting Rgs22 and Gapdh were generated by SUNY Co., China, and are available upon request, with the sequences provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Mouse ependymal cells (mEPCs) culture

Mouse EPCs were cultured with a standard protocol30. Briefly, telencephalic tissues were micro-dissected in cold dissection solution (161 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 1 mM MgSO4, 3.7 mM CaCl2, 5 mM Hepes, and 5.5 mM Glucose, pH 7.4) under a stereo microscope. After the removal of the cerebellum, olfactory bulbs, and hippocampus, tissue fragments were digested in the dissection solution containing 10 U·mL−1 papain (Worthington, #LS003126), 0.2 mg·mL−1 L-Cysteine, 0.5 mM EDTA, 1 mM CaCl2, 1.5 mM NaOH, and 0.15% DNase I (Sigma, #D5025) for 30 min at 37 °C. Cells were dissociated by pipetting up and down 30 times with a 5-ml pipette and collected by centrifugation at 400 × g for 5 min at room temperature. Cells were resuspended with DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin and inoculated into the flask coated with 10 μg·mL−1 fibronectin (Sigma, #FC010). Neurons were shaken off and removed after culturing for 24 and 48 h after inoculation. The remaining cells were further cultured to confluency, then subjected to starvation in serum-free DMEM medium supplemented with antibiotics and cultured for an additional 10 days to induce multi-ciliation.

Spermatozoa protein fractionation

Epididymal spermatozoa were recovered in HTF medium (Nanjing Aibei, #M1135) at 37 °C for 60 min and sequentially fractionated using Triton ×-100 and SDS extraction buffers with a standard protocol26,53. In brief, cells were lysed in Triton buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 1% Triton ×-100, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF) and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min to separate Triton-soluble (supernatant) and Triton-insoluble (pellet) fractions. The insoluble fraction was further extracted with SDS buffer (Triton buffer plus 1% SDS) and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min to obtain SDS-soluble (supernatant) and SDS-resistant (pellet) fractions.

Western blotting

The spermatozoa fractions were either diluted or dissolved in SDS sample buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 2% SDS, 10% glycerol, 5% β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.01% bromophenol blue) to achieve equal volumes. mEPCs were also harvested in the SDS sample buffer. All protein samples were denatured at 95 °C for 10 min, then subjected to SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore) with the semi-dry transfer system (Bio-Rad). Membranes were successively incubated in blocking buffer (5% milk in TBS with Tween-20), primary antibody dilutions, and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer. The chemiluminescent signals were developed with SuperSignal™ West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher) and detected via ChemiScope S7 (Clinx) or X-films (Kodak). The antibodies and suggested dilutions are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Mice were anesthetized and underwent transcardiac perfusion first with saline and then 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) before dissection of brains and other tissues. Tissues were further fixed in 4% PFA overnight, dehydrated with a standard protocol of ethanol gradient, xylene, and paraffin, and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin-embedded sections (5 μm) were prepared and dried at 42 °C. Following a standard protocol of deparaffinization and rehydration, the slides were stained with hematoxylin for 30 s, washed thoroughly, and counterstained with eosin for 30–60 s. After washing and a dehydration protocol with ethanol gradient and xylene, the slides were mounted with neutral balsam (Solarbio, #G8590) and imaged with a Nikon microscope.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Paraffin-embedded sections of brains or other tissues were deparaffinized with xylene, rehydrated with ethanol gradient, washed, and subjected to antigen retrieval (10 mM Sodium Citrate Buffer, pH 6.0, 95 °C, 15 min). After natural cooling down to room temperature, the sections were subjected to peroxidase blocking (3% hydrogen peroxide, 10 min, RT). The slides were sequentially blocked with 10% goat serum (in PBS with 0.1% Tween-20) for 30 min, hybridized with primary antibody dilutions for 60 min, biotinylated secondary antibody dilutions for 30 min as well as avidin-biotin peroxidase-based detection reagent (Vectastain® ABC kit) for 30 min. The signals were developed with a DAB peroxidase substrate kit (Vector Laboratories) under a stereomicroscope and stopped with PBS washing. After counterstaining with hematoxylin, the slides were mounted for H&E staining. The antibodies involved are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Immunofluorescent (IF) staining

For paraffin-embedded sections, the deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen-retrieval procedures were similar to IHC staining. After cooling down to room temperature, the slides were blocked with 1% BSA (in PBS with 0.1% Tween-20) for 30 min, incubated with primary antibody dilutions for 60 min, and fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch) dilutions with DAPI (5 μg·mL−1) for 60 min, and finally mounted with 80% glycerol. For mEPCs and dissected lateral ventricle walls cultured in glass-bottom dishes, after fixation in 4% PFA for 10 min and permeabilization in 0.5% Triton ×-100 for 15 min, the blocking and antibody incubation procedures mirrored those for tissue sections. Processed slides were either imaged immediately or stored at 4 °C in darkness for 1-week preservation. Immunofluorescent images were obtained with an FV3000 confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus). For STED imaging, DAPI staining was omitted, and images were obtained with a TCS SP8 STED microscope (Leica). Super-resolution images were taken with Z-stack scanning set as 125-nm intervals under DeltaVision OMX SR system (GE Healthcare) equipped with a Plan Apo 60×/1.42 NA oil-immersion objective lens (Olympus), followed by a procession with SoftWoRx software to analyze the rotational polarity of basal bodies. 2D-SIM and 3D-SIM images were captured with Z-stack scanning set as 100-nm intervals under a commercial structured illumination microscope HIS-SIM (High Intelligent and Sensitive SIM) equipped with a 100×/1.5 NA oil immersion objective (Evident), and processed by Image J software to analyze the fine positioning of RGS22. The antibodies and suggested dilutions are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Tissue samples (telencephalon, trachea, and cauda epididymis) were processed for TEM analysis according to established protocols26. Briefly, following double fixation with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.0) for 4 h and 1% OsO4 (in PBS) for 1–2 h with intermediate PBS washes, samples were dehydrated through an ethanol gradient (30, 50, 70, 80%) and an acetone gradient (90, 95, 100, 100%); each step applies for 15–20 min. The samples were further infiltrated in Spurr’s resin/acetone (1/1) mixture for 1 h, Spurr’s resin/acetone (3/1) mixture for 3 h, and eventually embedded in Spurr’s resin at 70 °C overnight. Ultrathin sections (70–90 nm) were obtained using a Leica EM UC7 ultramicrotome, double-stained with uranyl acetate (saturated in 50% ethanol) and lead citrate (5–10 min each), and examined using either a Hitachi Model H-7650 TEM or JEOL 2100 plus TEM.

Immunoelectron microscopy

Spermatozoa were fixed with 4% PFA in PBS, permeabilized in 0.5% Triton ×-100, blocked in 0.1% BSA (diluted in PBS), immunolabeled with RGS22 antibody dilution and nanogold-conjugated secondary antibody dilution, then processed through standard TEM embedding and sectioning protocols prior to uranyl acetate/lead citrate staining.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Tissues were fixed following the standard TEM protocol (2.5% glutaraldehyde/1% OsO4), dehydrated through an ethanol gradient, and critical point dried (Hitachi HCP-2) before being sputter-coated with gold-palladium (Hitachi E-1010) for 4–5 min. The samples were imaged using a Hitachi SU-8010 field emission SEM.

Micro-CT imaging

Mice were anesthetized and carefully positioned on the micro-CT scanning bed to minimize artificial bending of spinal. Imaging and reconstruction were performed using U-CT-XUHR system (MILabs). Three-dimensional rendering and visualization of the reconstructed datasets were performed using 3D Slice (version 5.10.0)61. Quantification of the kyphosis index was performed based on the established method62.

High-speed live-cell imaging

Ependymal ciliary motility assay was carried out with an established protocol24. Whole mounts of lateral ventricle walls were dissected (500–1000 μm) and immersed with the ciliary face down into DMEM (containing 0.3 mg·mL−1 glutamine, 1% penicillin/streptomycin) in glass-bottom culture dishes. Cilia were stained with 100 nM SiR-tubulin probes (Spirochrome, #SC002) for 1 h and subjected to live imaging via Olympus Xplore SpinSR10 microscope equipped with an ORCA-Fusion camera (Hamamatsu), OBIS solid-state Laser, 4000 rpm CSU Disk Speed, and an incubation chamber (37 °C, 5% CO2, and 80% humidity) at 20 ms intervals. For fluid flow assay, ependyma tissue sections (250 μm) were obtained using a vibratome (Leica, VT1000S) and immersed (ciliary face down) into the culture medium in glass-bottom dishes. The ependymal cilia were labeled with 100 nM SiR-tubulin probe for 1 h, and fluorescent latex beads (250-nm diameter, Thermo Fisher, #F8809) were added at a 1:500 dilution. Time-lapse images were recorded using a spinning disk confocal microscope (Nikon, Ti2 Microscope) equipped with the Prime 95B (A17G203024) camera and analyzed with ImageJ software.

Immunoprecipitation and quantitative mass spectrometry (IP-MS)

To identify RGS22-binding proteins, IP-MS was performed on adult mouse testis lysates using anti-RGS22 antibody, with IgG serving as a negative control. The experiment, repeated three times with similar results, is represented by data from one biological replicate.

To conjugate antibodies, rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher, #10500 C) or anti-RGS22 antibody was incubated with Protein A magnetic beads (Invitrogen, #10001D) in PBS containing protease inhibitor cocktail at 4 °C for 8 h. Testes from two 8-week-old mice were homogenized in low-salt lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl, 0.1% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, 10 mM Na4P2O7, 1 mM DTT, and protease inhibitor cocktail) and clarified by centrifugation at 13,523 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was incubated with antibody-conjugated beads at 4 °C for overnight. Beads were then washed three times with wash buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 0.5% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM Na4P2O7, 10% glycerol) and once with PBS. Proteins bound on beads were subjected to mass spectrometry.

Proteins were precipitated with acetone, and the pellet was dried in a Speedvac for 1–2 min, then dissolved in 8 M urea, 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.5. Reduction and alkylation were performed by adding TCEP (5 mM final) and iodoacetamide (10 mM final), each incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The mixture was diluted fourfold and digested overnight with trypsin at 1:50 (w/w). The digested peptides were desalted on a home-made C18 column, dried with a SpeedVac and further loaded onto an Easy-nLC1200 system equipped with a home-made reverse phase C18 column (75 μm ID packed with ReproSil-Pur C18-AQ 1.9 μm resin, Dr. Maisch GmbH) and eluted with a 240 min gradient from 3 to 100% buffer B (buffer A: 0.1% formic acid in water; buffer B: 0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile). The eluted peptides were ionized and introduced into a Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, CA) using a nano-spray source with a 2.2-kV spray voltage. A full MS scan (m/z 300–1800) was followed by top-20 MS/MS events at 28% normalized collision energy. Full scan resolution was 70,000 (AGC target 3e6); MS/MS resolution was 17,500 (isolation window 1.8 m/z, AGC target 1e5). The maximum ion injection time was 50 ms for MS and 100 ms for MS/MS. Dynamic exclusion settings: charge exclusion 1, 8, and >8; exclude isotopes on; exclusion duration 30 s.

The acquired MS/MS data were analyzed against the UniProtKB Mus musculus database using MaxQuant V2.1.4.0 with default settings. Precursor and fragment mass tolerance were set to ±20 ppm, and the main search peptide tolerance was set to 4.5 ppm. Oxidation (M) and carbamidomethyl (C) were specified as variable and fixed modifications, respectively. Trypsin was set as the cleavage enzyme, allowing up to three missed cleavages. All identified proteins were filtered at a peptide-level FDR of <1%.

Cryo-ET sample preparation

After 10 days of serum starvation, mEPCs were harvested and mixed with 10-nm gold beads (Electron Microscopy Sciences, #25487) to a final concentration of 3–6 × 106 cells·mL−1. Cryo-ET grids (Quantifoil R2/1, Au 200-mesh) were prepared in a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) maintained at 100% humidity and 8 °C. 4-μL aliquots of the cilia suspension were applied to each grid, blotted from both sides for 1–3 s, and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane. Vitrified grids were stored in liquid nitrogen for further use.

Cryo-focused ion beam (cryo-FIB) milling of mouse sperm flagella

Sperm were isolated from 9 to 16-week-old C57Bl/6J mice. Briefly, fresh epididymides were minced with fine scissors and incubated in 10 ml of pre-warmed 1 × Krebs buffer (1.2 mM KH₂PO₄, 120 mM NaCl, 1.2 mM MgSO₄·7H₂O, 14 mM glucose, 1.2 mM CaCl₂·2H₂O, 5 mM KCl, 25 mM NaHCO₃) for 15 min at 37 °C to allow sperm release. The resulting suspension was filtered through a 150 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 1200 × g to pellet the sperm. After washing, the final sperm pellet was resuspended in 1.5 ml of HMKE buffer (10 mM HEPES pH7.2, 5 mM MgSO4, 50 mM KCl, 0.5 mM EGTA). For the cryo-EM grid preparation, 3.5 μl of the sperm suspension was applied to glow‑discharged EM grids (Quantifoil R2/1, Au 200-mesh) and incubated for 10 s. Grids were then blotted for 1–3 s and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane. To prepare the lamella for cryo-ET analyses, interactive cryo-FIB milling was conducted by combining automated programs (AutoTEM) with manual intervention using an Aquilos II cryo-FIB–SEM microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A platinum layer (~10 nm) was sputter coated, and a gas injection system was used to deposit the precursor compound trimethyl (methylcyclopentadi-enyl) platinum (IV) once the grids were loaded. Following optimization of focus and astigmatism, the entire grid was imaged by SEM at 377× magnification to obtain a panoramic overview. Regions appropriate for milling were selected based on the acquired map. The stage was then tilted to 6°, equivalent to a milling angle of 9° relative to the grid plane. FIB milling was performed with progressively decreasing beam current as thinning advanced: starting at 1.0 nA and reduced to 100 pA (~500 nm thickness), followed by a manual polishing step at 50–30 pA to achieve a final thickness of ~200 nm. Grids were stored in liquid nitrogen until further use.

Cryo-ET data collection and tomogram reconstruction

All tilt series were acquired on a 300-kV Titan Krios microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a Falcon 4i detector and Selectris X energy filter (silt width 20 eV). Data were recorded using Tomography 5 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a nominal magnification of 81,000×, corresponding to a pixel size of 1.505 Å. Each tilt series was collected using a modified dose-symmetric scheme63 over an angular range of −55° to 55° with 3° increments, and images were grouped in pairs on either side of 0° (0°, 3°, 6°, -3°, -6°, 9°, 12° …). At every tilt angle, images were recorded as a multi-frame movie comprising ten subframes. The total electron dose per tilt series was 100~110 e−·Å−2, with defocus values ranging from −2 to −5 μm. 4 tilt series of WT ependymal cilia, 93 tilt series of Rgs22−/− ependymal cilia, and 60 tilt series of Rgs22−/− sperm were collected. All movie frames were gain-corrected and subjected to beam-induced motion correction using MotionCor264. The contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated with CTFFIND465. Initial alignment and reconstruction of all tilt series were performed using AreTomo66. The resulting 3D tomograms were visually inspected in IMOD67, and those exhibiting poor target tracking, irregular defocus, incomplete axonemes, or suboptimal orientation were excluded. The selected tilt series were then realigned using patch tracking in IMOD, followed by 3D reconstruction in RELION568 with a binned pixel size of 10 Å. Good tomograms of Rgs22−/− ependymal cilia and Rgs22−/− sperm flagella were selected for subsequent subtomogram averaging. The data collection and processing parameters are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Subtomogram averaging

Both samples were processed using similar workflows. Subsequent subvolume extraction, classification, and refinement were carried out in RELION5. Briefly, subvolumes were manually picked from the DMTs at 8-nm intervals and extracted using a box size sufficient to contain a full 48-nm repeat unit (pixel size: 6.02 Å; box size: 120 pixels; dimension: 72.24 nm). An initial round of refinement was performed using a low-pass filtered (60 Å) reference map of mEPCs DMTs (EMD-39657). The 8-nm particles were then classified into five classes using a cylindrical mask applied over the microtubule inner proteins at the inner junction. The most populated class, representing the 16-nm repeat, was selected for further 3D refinement. These 16-nm particles were subsequently classified into five classes using a cylindrical mask covering the outer dynein arm of the A-tubule, and the most populated class (corresponding to the 48-nm repeat) was retained for downstream refinement. The selected particles were then re-extracted with a larger box size capable of accommodating a complete 96-nm repeating unit (pixel size: 6.02 Å; box size: 240 pixels; dimension: 144.48 nm). Supervised 3D classification focusing on the radial spokes yielded two class averages of the 96-nm repeating units, from which the most populated class was chosen for final refinement. Map resolutions were estimated using Fourier Shell Correlation (FSC) calculated between two independently refined half-maps (FSC = 0.143 criterion) in RELION5. For the Rgs22−/− ependymal cilia sample, 37,589 subvolumes were initially picked at 8-nm intervals, and 749 subvolumes were retained after 3D classification to 96-nm intervals. For the Rgs22−/− sperm sample, 10,530 subvolumes were initially picked at 8-nm intervals, of which 739 subvolumes remained after 3D classification to 96-nm intervals. All cryo-ET maps and atomic models were visualized in ChimeraX. Tomogram slices were displayed using IMOD. Graphs were plotted in Adobe Illustrator (Adobe).

Sperm analysis

Cauda epididymides were dissected and incubated in HTF medium at 37 °C for 30–60 min to allow spermatozoa release. Sperm concentration and motility parameters were quantified using computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA) with 300 µL semen samples tracked per sample, following the protocol established in previous study26. Additional sperm suspensions were smeared onto glass slides, air-dried, and fixed for subsequent H&E staining or immunofluorescence analysis.

In vitro fertilization (IVF)

IVF assays were conducted following standard methods69. Superovulation was induced in PD21–23 Wildtype (WT) female mice by intraperitoneal injection of 5 IU PMSG (Sansheng Pharmaceutical), followed 44–48 h later by 5 IU hCG (Sansheng Pharmaceutical). Cumulus-oocyte complexes (COCs) were collected from oviducts 16 h post-hCG administration and maintained in HTF droplets. For spermatozoa preparation, cauda epididymides and vas deferens from WT and Rgs22−/− males were dissected 1 h prior to COC collection, allowing spermatozoa capacitation in HTF medium at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Capacitated spermatozoa were then introduced to COC-containing droplets for fertilization. After 6 h, fertilized oocytes (zygotes) were washed and cultured in KSOM medium for embryonic development assessment.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates with consistent results, unless otherwise indicated in the figure legends. The statistical data are presented as means ± SD. Statistical comparisons between two independent groups were performed using the two-sided Mann–Whitney U test, except for survival analyses, which employed the Log-rank test. Significance thresholds are defined as follows: P < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (**), P < 0.001 (***), with P > 0.05 considered not significant (n.s.). The exact P-values were provided in the Source Data. For statistical analysis of rotational polarity, the BB polarity was depicted by unit vectors extending from the center of the CEP164 rings at the distal end of BBs to the center of γ-tubulin signals at the BFs. The mean vector length for BBs within individual cells was calculated to determine the rotational polarity in a single cell. The mean vector length in full-sized confocal micrographs containing a minimum of 7 ependymal cells was calculated to quantify the rotational polarity in an area.
