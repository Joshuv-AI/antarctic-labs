Carbapenemase-producing carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales in surgical intensive care unit patients in a tertiary hospital in India
Authors: Lindsey R. Hall, Emily E. Benedict, Fabia Edathadathil, Jobin J. Jacob, Devendhu Suresh, Yathu Krishna, Juby A. Varghese, Jacaranda van Rheenen, Ige A. George, Jennie H. Kwon, Margaret A. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carbapenemase-producing carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CP-CRE) transmission studies incorporating clinical and genomic data from hospitals in India are lacking. We prospectively enrolled surgical intensive care unit (SICU) patients in a tertiary-care hospital in India (July 29 to November 30, 2023) and collected peri-rectal swabs at SICU admission, SICU discharge, and hospital discharge, alongside SICU environmental sampling. CP-CRE isolates were whole-genome sequenced to investigate differences between community-acquired (CA), healthcare-associated (HCA), hospital-acquired (HA), and environmental isolates. Twenty-seven percent of participants were CP-CRE-colonized on SICU admission, with risk factors of previous hospitalization and exposure to ≥2 antimicrobials. Among 148 admission-negative patients, 115 had repeat PRS; 42 (36.5%) acquired CP-CRE (27 upon SICU discharge, 15 upon hospital discharge). blaNDM (84%, n = 103) and blaOXA (29%, n = 36) carbapenemase genes were present in the sequenced isolates; 20 isolates (16%) carried both. Highly similar E. coli and K. pneumoniae clusters suggest hospital transmission, although the sample size limited network inference.

Introduction

Carbapenemase-producing carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CP-CRE) infections are a significant threat to public health and rank among the top causes of hospital-acquired infections in numerous low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) with high infection burden. India is an LMIC and resource-limited setting where CRE infections are endemic and highly prevalent in healthcare facilities1. Due to resource and infrastructural constraints, such as multi-bed open wards, many CP-CRE prevention methods used in high-income countries are difficult to implement in India. In a multi-center intensive care unit (ICU) study involving 26 tertiary hospitals in India, Enterobacterales constituted 35% of hospital-onset bloodstream infections, with 72% of Klebsiella species and 58% of E. coli being carbapenem-resistant1.

A few studies have examined CRE colonization on admission to Indian hospitals2–4 and ICUs5–10, but critical gaps exist in our understanding of how CP-CRE is transmitted within hospitals in India. To our knowledge, only two studies have examined CRE acquisition in Indian ICUs5,8. However, these studies have been limited by the lack of application of high-resolution genomic methods to understand the transmission dynamics of CRE and have not examined reservoirs of CRE in the hospital environment.

In this work, we investigate the epidemiology and risk factors associated with CP-CRE peri-rectal colonization on admission and hospital acquisition among adult patients admitted to a gastrointestinal surgical ICU (SICU) in a tertiary-care hospital in India by incorporating genomic methods. Additionally, we examine potential environmental reservoirs of CP-CRE and their genomic relatedness to CP-CRE colonization isolates within the SICU.

Results

Peri-rectal swabs revealed high intake colonization with CP-CRE and cases of in-hospital CP-CRE acquisition

During the study period, 421 patients were admitted to the study SICU, and 203 (48.2%) consented to the study (Table 1). Of these 203 participants, 55 (27%) were colonized with CP-CRE on admission to the SICU (Fig. 1a, c). Among the 148 patients not colonized with CP-CRE, 115 (77.7%) underwent repeat peri-rectal swab testing after SICU discharge; of these, 27 (23.5%) patients had newly detected CP-CRE. Among these 27 patients, 19 (70.4%) had a positive CP-CRE swab collected on the SICU discharge day or within 48 h of SICU discharge, both within 96 h of the patient’s admission to the SICU. Among 88 (76.5%) patients not colonized with CP-CRE at SICU discharge, 45 (51.1%) underwent repeat peri-rectal swab testing at hospital discharge; 15 (33.3%; 15/45) had newly detected CP-CRE. Overall, among the 115 patients who underwent repeat peri-rectal swab testing, 42 (36.5%) had newly detected CP-CRE during the hospital stay. Initial species identification revealed E. coli as the predominant organism upon SICU admission, accounting for 49% (n = 27 of 55), while K. pneumoniae was identified in 16% (n = 9 of 55) of SICU admission-positive patients. Interestingly, 22% (n = 6 of 27) patients colonized with E. coli on admission were CA while all K. pneumoniae cases were HCA (Supplementary Data 1). K. pneumoniae and E. coli accounted for 38% (n = 16 of 42) and 33% (n = 14 of 42) of patients with HA isolates, respectively. Colonization with both CP-E. coli and CP-K. pneumoniae was identified in 29% (n = 16 of 55) of SICU admission-positive patients and 19% (n = 8 of 42) of patients with hospital acquisition. Overall, 190 environmental swab samples were obtained at eight time points and 11% (n = 22) were positive for CP-CRE. Each environmental swab produced, at most, one organism, most often K. pneumoniae (n = 8 of 22, 36%) and K. oxytoca (n = 4 of 22, 18%). All environmental CP-CRE were collected from the sinks located between patient beds and from the nurse breakroom sink (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Data 1, Supplementary Fig. 1). One sink drain, #3, had K. pneumoniae isolated at five time points, with two pairs of repeated samples (December 2022–January 2023 and October 2023–November 2023) falling within our threshold of 20 SNVs (see Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Fig. 2 for threshold determination).

Whole-genome sequencing revealed highly related CP-CRE clusters

A total of 144 CP-CRE isolates were obtained, comprising 122 from patients and 22 from environmental samples. WGS was conducted on 119 patient isolates, omitting three patient isolates that could not be recovered from the frozen stock. Five isolates failed quality control after genome assembly (Supplementary Methods), leaving 114 high-quality CP-CRE genomes for analysis. From the environmental samples, WGS was performed on eight K. pneumoniae and one E. cloacae isolate, selected for matching species identification found in patient isolates. The environmental E. coli isolate could not be recovered from frozen stock and therefore did not undergo WGS.

E. coli was the predominant species among patients (n = 66 of 114 isolates; Fig. 2a). Among patient-derived E. coli genomes, ST410 was the most common (n = 16, 24%), followed by ST167 (n = 11, 17%). While ST410 was dominant among HCA and HA E. coli isolates (n = 7/32, 22%; and 9/24, 38%, respectively), it was absent in CA E. coli isolates. Nine clusters (i.e., groups of 2 or more isolates) of highly genetically related isolates (<20 core gene SNVs; see Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Fig. 2 for threshold definition) were present in our cohort (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 3a). In cluster 1, three CA and three HCA isolates were collected temporally before one HA isolate. The HA isolate was collected from a SICU acquisition patient who had an overlapping SICU stay near a patient from whom one of the HCA isolates was collected. In cluster 5, the CA isolate was collected before two HA isolates, but there were no overlapping days of hospital stay amongst patients in this cluster. K. pneumoniae was the second most common species, with 36 isolates from patients and eight from the environment (Fig. 2a). We also identified one Klebsiella sp. 1098211 and three K. quasipneumoniae isolates, both cultured from patients. K. pneumoniae ST147 (n = 11, 30%) and ST16 (n = 10, 28%) were the most prevalent STs. Among K. pneumoniae isolates from patients, multiple isolates were separated by fewer than 20 core SNVs from their closest study neighbor, indicating highly similar strains of K. pneumoniae (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 3b). In cluster 1, the single HCA isolate was obtained temporally before the two HA isolates, though there was no overlapping hospital stay between the associated patients. In cluster 8, two HCA isolates were temporally obtained before the two HA isolates. One of the patients with a cluster 8 HCA isolate had an overlapping stay with a patient with a cluster 8 HA isolate. All four patients with cluster 8 isolates underwent colectomy procedures that occurred within a four-week period. The environmental K. pneumoniae clustered together (cluster 5, ST147; cluster 7, ST 1810), though other samples of ST147 formed a separate cluster of patient-only isolates (cluster 6). Both clusters of environmental isolates were collected from repeated sampling of a single sink.

Eight Enterobacter isolates namely E. hormaechei (n = 3), E. roggenkampii (n = 2), E. cloacae (n = 2), and E. sichuanensis (n = 1) were isolated from patients (Fig. 2a). The one sequenced Enterobacter sink swab was identified as E. chengduensis. There were no instances of closely related Enterobacter isolates that clustered within core SNV groupings.

CP-CRE isolates were resistant to many tested non-carbapenem agents

Among the 114 high-quality sequenced patient isolates, 86% (n = 98) were resistant to both tested carbapenems, ten isolates (9%) were resistant to only one tested carbapenem, four isolates (3%) were intermediate to one carbapenem and susceptible to the other, one isolate was intermediate to both tested carbapenems, and one isolate was susceptible to both carbapenems. All nine environmental isolates were resistant to meropenem, and eight (89%; all K. pneumoniae) were also resistant to imipenem. Susceptibilities to all tested antimicrobials are shown in Supplementary Fig. 4b and Supplementary Data 2.

Resistance proportion to ciprofloxacin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole was 86% (n = 98) and 89% (n = 102), respectively. Interestingly, the resistance proportion was lower for gentamicin and amikacin at 44% (n = 50) and 39% (n = 45), respectively. Tigecycline had the lowest resistance proportion of all antibiotics tested at 4% (n = 5). Of all 49 HA isolates, 40 (82%) and 44 (90%) were resistant to imipenem and meropenem, respectively. For the 50 HCA isolates, 47 (94%) isolates were resistant to imipenem and meropenem, respectively, with 45 (90%) resistant to both. All 15 CA isolates were resistant to meropenem, while 13 (87%) were resistant to imipenem.

Sequenced CP-CRE isolates encoded multiple clinically important ARGs and resistance-associated plasmids

The overall abundance of ARGs per isolate was significantly higher in environmental CP-CRE isolates compared to patient isolates from CA, HCA, and HA sources (p = 0.024, p = 0.031, and p = 0.031, respectively; Bonferroni-corrected Wilcoxon test) (Fig. 3a, b, Supplementary Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplementary Data 3). Beta-lactamase genes were encoded by all species, consistent with selective culturing for only CP-CRE (Fig. 3c, Fig. 4a–c). Various Ambler class A enzymes, including the highly clinically relevant blaTEM, blaSHV, and blaCTX-M family genes, were abundantly present across the majority (n = 100) of these isolates. The class B metallo-beta-lactamase blaNDM variants and Ambler class D carbapenemases from the blaOXA-48-like family (blaOXA-181, blaOXA-232, and blaOXA-484) were found in 84% (n = 103/123) and 29% (n = 36/123) of all isolates, respectively. Both NDM and OXA enzymes were present in 20 (16%) isolates. blaNDM-5 was the most common blaNDM gene variant, found exclusively in E. coli (n = 50) and Klebsiella (n = 15) isolates; blaNDM-1 was present in all nine Enterobacter isolates, including one environmental isolate. The most prevalent K. pneumoniae sequence types, ST147 and ST16, had a high rate of carriage of blaNDM (91% and 50%, respectively). Nearly all (96%, n = 118 of 123) isolates harbored at least one gene conferring resistance to one or more aminoglycoside antibiotics. The two most common non-CP resistance genes, the broad-spectrum aminoglycoside enzyme aac(6’)-lb-cr or aac(6’)-lb-cr5, and a quinolone resistance gene, typically plasmid-mediated within a gene cassette found in a class 1 integron12, were carried by 35 isolates, mainly E. coli (n = 15) and Klebsiella (n = 18). Concerningly, six E. coli and 14 Klebsiella carried rmt and blaNDM variants, conferring resistance to both aminoglycosides and carbapenems. Other clinically significant genes detected include the dfrA-family genes, primarily dfRA12, carried by 113 isolates, and a nearly universal presence of the multidrug efflux transporter EmrD (n = 114).

Plasmid carriage analysis revealed a high prevalence of putative plasmids associated with carbapenem and beta-lactam resistance genes across our study isolates (Fig. 4a–e, Supplementary Data 3). Notably, IncFIB and IncFII plasmids, present in 76% (n = 94) and 81% (n = 100) of isolates, respectively (Fig. 4d, e), have been widely recognized reservoirs for multiple beta-lactamase genes across Enterobacterales species13–17. Among these, 29 isolates carried IncFIB(K) plasmids, which have previously been linked to cephalosporin resistance in Salmonella in India18. In addition, ColKP3 plasmids, historically associated with various carbapenemase genes19,20, were prevalent in Klebsiella isolates (58%, n = 28 of 48), while Col(BS512) plasmids, though more common in Indian E. coli and not typically associated with carbapenemases21,22, were found in 39% (n = 26 of 66) of Escherichia isolates. These plasmid types have been implicated in the horizontal transfer of beta-lactam resistance and contribute to the interspecies spread of carbapenemases23–32. However, the inherent limitations of short-read sequencing render full plasmid assemblies less precise compared to long-read sequencing, potentially resulting in undetected plasmids and an inability to confidently localize ARGs to plasmids.

Discussion

Our study provides insights into CP-CRE transmission in an acute healthcare facility in India. We observed that 27% of patients were colonized with CP-CRE on SICU admission, and 36.5% of patients who were not colonized on admission and with at least one follow-up swab acquired CP-CRE during their hospital stay. Previous hospitalization and in-hospital exposure to two or more antibiotics were associated with CP-CRE colonization on SICU admission, consistent with previous studies33. We could not identify risk factors associated with newly detected CP-CRE colonization during hospitalization, which may reflect limited power, attrition in follow-up sampling, and potential misclassification of some early post-admission detections as incident acquisition. Additionally, the complexity of transmission networks which include community, healthcare, and hospital settings obscures transmission analyses. We identified predominantly E. coli from SICU admission swabs with 22% of patients having CA colonization, while K. pneumoniae was identified predominantly from SICU- and hospital-discharge swabs and any patients with K. pneumoniae colonization at SICU admission were not considered CA colonization. This suggests community circulation of E. coli and a potential hospital reservoir of K. pneumoniae. In the SICU environment, CP-CRE was isolated only from sinks, with Klebsiella constituting the majority of environmental isolates. Core gene SNV analysis showed several E. coli and K. pneumoniae isolates separated by fewer than 20 core gene SNVs; however, the small number of HA events and inability to obtain swabs on all patients limited our ability to identify hospital transmission networks. We identified blaNDM and blaOXA-48-like family carbapenemase genes most frequently in our study, with 16% of isolates carrying both carbapenemase types simultaneously, further limiting potential treatment options should these organisms cause infection. IncF and Col family plasmids were common, with Klebsiella isolates carrying more plasmid markers per isolate than Escherichia and Enterobacter isolates.

While we observed more than one-third of patients acquiring CP-CRE after SICU admission, the true acquisition proportion is uncertain, because we could not obtain peri-rectal swab samples after SICU discharge and at hospital discharge from 51% of patients who were not colonized with CP-CRE on admission. Previous studies from India reported an ICU admission CRE prevalence ranging from 6.6% to 19.8%5–9,33,34. Although we observed higher admission prevalence, there are methodological differences, such as inclusion criteria, medical versus surgical patients, and microbiological methods, as well as a rise in CRE prevalence over time35,36. Interestingly, while CP-CRE was associated with previous hospitalization in the study hospital, it was not associated with previous hospitalization in other hospitals. This is likely because the study hospital is a referral center with a high CP-CRE colonization burden, which could lead to a higher chance of transmission. Only two studies examined CRE acquisition during ICU stay in India, with acquisition rates of 11.6%5 and 12%8. We observed a higher acquisition proportion in SICU discharge alone; however, there are again methodological differences similar to other admission prevalence studies.

We found a proportionally similar makeup of species as reported previously for community CP-CRE carriage in India37. Among E. coli, ST410 was the most frequent sequence type, followed by ST167. ST410 was identified only in HCA and HA cases, while ST167 was identified among CA, HCA, and HA cases. E. coli ST410 and ST167 are globally disseminated high-risk strains38 and were the leading NDM-5-producing E. coli strains in the European Union between 2012 and 202139, similar to our study. These STs were some of the most frequent strains among bloodstream infection carbapenemase-producing E. coli in a South Indian hospital between 2006 and 201639. In the community, intestinal carriage of antimicrobial-resistant E. coli has been observed in pregnant people receiving care in Madhya Pradesh and in healthy adults in Chennai, Hyderabad, and Mumbai, consistent with our findings37,40.

Among K. pneumoniae isolates, ST147 and ST16 were the most frequent strains. ST147 K. pneumoniae was identified once in CA isolates and multiple times in HCA, HA, and environmental isolates, while ST16 strains were identified only in HCA or HA cases. ST147 and ST16 are both high-risk clones associated with hospital outbreaks and extensive drug resistance, though ST147 is globally disseminated while ST16 is emerging41–43. ST147 is endemic to Indian healthcare facilities44,45, while ST16 is being increasingly reported45,46. Our study joins this body of work noting the healthcare-associated pattern of K. pneumoniae carriage and infection in India. Enterobacter species were present but less prevalent members of CP-CRE in our study SICU, consistent with prior work47. Carbapenem-resistant E. cloacae has been identified previously in this region, as has E. hormaechei48,49.

In the SICU environment, none of the samples from the immediate patient environment, such as bedrails and bedside cabinets, were positive for CP-CRE. The presence of CP-CRE in sink drains is not surprising, as there was no protocol for cleaning sink drains in the study SICU at the time of the study, whereas the immediate patient environment was cleaned by environmental services staff four times a day with accelerated hydrogen peroxide. K. pneumoniae was the most frequent CP-CRE in sinks, with four different STs identified (ST17, ST147, ST1810, and ST437).

Overall, the CP-CRE isolates from patients in our study are phylogenetically similar to those found in other studies conducted in India (Supplementary Fig. 6)50,51. While the K. pneumoniae results are consistent with potential healthcare-associated transmission between patients, the E. coli results suggest both community and healthcare transmission events. Among highly related clusters, we observed that HA isolates were temporally obtained after CA or HCA isolates, with some patients having overlapping stays, supporting the possibility of hospital transmission. Additionally, in one cluster involving four K. pneumoniae ST16 isolates, all patients underwent colectomy surgery within a four-week period. Clusters of highly related K. pneumoniae isolates from sink drains were found from the same sink over time, suggesting the persistence of K. pneumoniae in SICU sink drains. Although there was similarity between sink and patient isolates at the level of sequence type, these isolates were not closely related by core gene SNV analysis and therefore do not support sinks as a source of patient colonization in this instance. However, intermittent environmental sampling may have affected these results.

The CP-CRE isolates contained a diverse array of clinically-relevant ARGs, conferring resistance to a wide spectrum of antibiotics including aminoglycosides, beta-lactams, fluoroquinolones, and sulfonamide drugs (Supplementary Fig. 5b, Supplementary Data 3). Environmental isolates carried significantly higher ARGs per isolate than patient isolates, which could be a result of long-term persistence in the hospital environment. The similarity in ARG burden and composition between CA, HCA, and HA isolates indicates that community- and healthcare-circulating CP-CRE present equally significant antimicrobial resistance threats. ARG burden in isolates in our study was similar to that of external studies, except for HA Klebsiella isolates in our study, which had higher ARG burdens compared to external isolates (p = 0.0021, Bonferroni-corrected Wilcoxon Test; Supplementary Fig. 7).

Among carbapenemases, blaNDM was the most frequent enzyme, followed by blaOXA-48 -like. Strikingly, 16% of isolates carried genes for both NDM and OXA-48-like family carbapenemases, which has been reported previously in CP-CRE from India51–53. Similar to a previous multi-center study profiling carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae from India52, none of the plasmid-encoded and clinically significant blaKPC genes were detected50. K. pneumoniae strains ST147 and ST16 had a high prevalence of blaNDM (91% and 50%, respectively), which aligns with previous studies of isolates cultured from India that revealed nearly universal carriage of these genes45,54–61. Additionally, blaNDM-5 was present in 75% (n = 50) of the E. coli isolates, consistent with global expansion of the dominant E. coli ST410 and ST167 strains62.

We recognize some key limitations of this study. Firstly, we enrolled only 48% of patients admitted to SICU, which could introduce some uncertainty about the CP-CRE admission and acquisition proportions. Similarly, we did not obtain repeat swabs on patients who were colonized with CP-CRE on admission, nor did we obtain swabs from 76 patients lost to attrition, leading to uncertainty of CP-CRE acquisition occurrence. These two limitations could have impacted our ability to identify the clinical risk factors associated with CP-CRE acquisition and to detect hospital transmission networks. Among 27 patients who were newly CP-CRE positive upon SICU discharge, 19 (70%) acquired CP-CRE within 96 h of SICU admission. This raises the possibility of admission CP-CRE potentially being misclassified as hospital-acquired due to low-level CP-CRE not detected by culture, followed by antibiotic exposure as part of routine surgical prophylaxis, resulting in CP-CRE expansion in the intestine to culture-detectable levels. Such a phenomenon has been reported with vancomycin-resistant Enterococci63,64. This could have impacted our assessment in identifying clinical risk factors. More sensitive methods, such as metagenomic sequencing of the peri-rectal swab sample, could avoid this potential misclassification. However, our use of selective agar allows for the detection of very low CFU levels, including those potentially below the detection thresholds of metagenomic sequencing, which are influenced by sequencing depth. Alternatively, enhancing detection sensitivity by incorporating a broth enrichment step could increase the likelihood of detecting low-abundance organisms, improving recovery rates and overall assay performance. Additionally, metagenomic approaches may detect DNA from nonviable organisms, which would not necessarily indicate active colonization. Although we performed environmental sampling in the SICU, we did not sample the inpatient wards where patients were later housed, which could represent another potential source of CP-CRE transmission. This could be another potential source of CP-CRE transmission and acquisition, which was undetected in this study. Future research should consider large-scale studies to identify risk factors for CP-CRE transmission, incorporating time-varying covariates and metagenomic sequencing to rule out the possibility of misclassification. Lastly, nine CRE isolates may have been misclassified as CP-CRE, since they did not undergo WGS to confirm carbapenemase genes.

In conclusion, we observed that over 25% of SICU patients were colonized with CP-CRE at admission, and over one-third acquired CP-CRE during hospital stay in a resource-limited acute healthcare setting. The genomic relatedness and collection timing among several E. coli and K. pneumoniae isolates is consistent with potential hospital transmission events; however, the small sample size limited our ability to identify clinical risk factors for CP-CRE acquisition and transmission networks during hospital stay. This work presents additional insight into CP-CRE colonization and acquisition in an ICU in India, highlighting the need for interventions to control the spread and decrease the prevalence of this pathogen.

This study shows that CP-CRE colonization and acquisition in Indian ICUs extend beyond typical hospital-based transmission, involving significant community sources. Unlike non-LMIC ICUs, where nosocomial spread dominates, a high proportion of cases here are community-onset, complicating control measures. Recognizing these differences is critical for designing targeted interventions across both hospital and community settings. Overall, the findings highlight the need for comprehensive strategies that address both reservoirs to manage CP-CRE spread in LMIC ICUs.

Methods

Study overview

This research was approved by the Human Research Protection Office at Washington University School of Medicine, the study hospital ethics committee, and the Indian Health Ministry’s Screening Committee. U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reviewed its involvement to ensure compliance with applicable federal law and CDC policy. We conducted an observational study at a 1250-bed private academic tertiary-care hospital in Kerala, South India. All SICU patients admitted between July 29 and November 30, 2023 (N = 421) were invited to participate, and 203 patients were enrolled after giving informed consent. At the time of SICU admission, patients and/or attendants were asked five questions if they gave consent for enrollment into the study: 1) whether they were transferred from another hospital or coming from home; 2) any history of hospitalization in the last one year; 3) any history of ICU admission in the last one year; 4) whether the patient is on long-term outpatient dialysis; and 5) if the patient had undergone any surgical procedures in the last 30 days. All other medical information, including prior antimicrobial exposure for patients with inpatient stays prior to the SICU, was obtained via chart review. We initially attempted to obtain antimicrobial exposure for all patients, but many patients were unsure if they were on antibiotics prior to hospital admission. We therefore restricted antimicrobial exposure information to patients who had antibiotic exposure in hospital where we could verify the information.

For included patients, peri-rectal swabs (HiMedia Laboratory Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India) were collected at SICU admission (within 24 h, typically before the procedure in the operating room), after SICU discharge (within 48 h if transferred to an inpatient ward), and on hospital discharge. If a swab tested positive for CP-CRE, no further specimens were taken from that participant. Swab samples were also obtained from the SICU environment between December 2022 and November 2023 for a total of eight samplings (December 2022 and January, March, April, August, September, October, and November 2023). Environmental sampling included time points prior to patient enrollment and was intended to characterize potential reservoirs and persistence of CP-CRE in the SICU environment rather than to directly attribute individual patient acquisitions to environmental sources. Swabs were taken from patient bed rails, bedside furniture, the nurse station computer and phone, as well as from seven sinks at various locations including between beds, the nurse station, breakroom, and clean utility rooms.

Microbial culturing

The peri-rectal and environmental swabs were plated on mSuperCarba selective agar medium (CHROMagar, Paris, France) within 24 h of collection and incubated under aerobic conditions at 35–37 °C for 18–24 h to select for CP-CRE. The potential CRE colonies were plated onto MacConkey agar before species identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing with the VITEK2 system (Supplementary Information, Supplementary Data 1). CP-CRE is defined as follows: 1) Enterobacterales isolates with carbapenemase gene detection in whole genome sequencing (WGS), or 2) Enterobacterales isolates recovered from selective agar, which are non-susceptible to either meropenem or imipenem and could not be recovered from the frozen stock for WGS or the genome was of low quality after WGS. Isolates meeting criterion (2) were included in epidemiologic prevalence and acquisition estimates. However, isolates without high-quality WGS data were excluded from genomic relatedness, antibiotic resistance gene (ARG) and plasmid analyses.

DNA extraction and whole-genome sequencing

WGS was attempted on all CP-CRE species obtained from patients. Among environmental CP-CRE isolates, WGS was restricted to E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and Enterobacter cloacae isolates, as these species were also identified from patients. Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp Blood Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration was determined using Qubit™ dsDNA Quantification Assay Kits (Thermo Fisher, USA). Sequencing libraries were created using the Nextera DNA Flex library preparation kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA) and sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) (Supplementary Information).

Read processing and genomic analysis

Adapter sequences were trimmed, and forward and reverse reads were paired and de novo assembled into draft genomes; low-quality genomes (>5% contaminated and/or <90% complete) were excluded from further genomic analyses. Isolate species were identified, and multi-locus sequence types (STs) were assigned using appropriate typing schemes. Protein-coding sequences were used as input for pangenome investigation and to align core genes (present in at least 95% of samples) used to construct species-specific phylogenetic trees. Phylogenetic trees across multiple genera were constructed using the same method, applying a core gene presence threshold of 95% across samples. Core gene single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) were used to quantify genetic relatedness between isolates using within-species pairwise comparisons. Putative plasmid contigs and ARGs were identified. Pairwise comparisons of ARG abundance were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Bonferroni correction for multiple testing (Supplementary). The trimmed sequencing reads and assembled genomes generated in this study have been deposited in the National Library of Medicine database under BioProject PRJNA1425091.

Risk factor analysis

Statistical comparisons were performed based on the determined origin of the CP-CRE isolate: community-acquired (CA), healthcare-associated (HCA), hospital-acquired (HA), and environmental65. CA is defined as a positive peri-rectal swab on SICU admission and none of the following: previous hospitalization in last year, chronic dialysis, transfer from outside hospital, procedures in the prior 30 days, presence of invasive devices, and total length of current hospital stay greater than 48 h prior to SICU admission. HCA is defined as a positive peri-rectal swab on SICU admission and at least one of the following: history of previous hospitalization in last year, chronic dialysis, transferred from outside hospital, procedures in the prior 30 days, invasive devices on SICU admission, or total length of current hospital stay >48 h prior to SICU admission. HA is defined as having a negative peri-rectal swab culture on SICU admission followed by CP-CRE positive peri-rectal swab at either SICU or hospital discharge. Univariate analysis and multivariable logistic regression were performed, and adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for CP-CRE positivity as described in the Supplementary.

Statistics and reproducibility

The study design is described in detail in the Study Overview section. Briefly, all patients admitted to the study SICU were approached and only the 203 who provided informed consent were included. The sample size was dictated by participant consent and availability for follow-up; no statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Isolate assemblies which failed quality control, described in the Read Processing and Genomic Analysis section and in Supplementary Information, were excluded from analysis. As there was no experimentation in our study, no randomization or blinding was performed.
