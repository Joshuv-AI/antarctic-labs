One-year impact on HPV vaccination coverage of a school- and primary care-based intervention: the PrevHPV cluster randomised trial
Authors: Aurélie Bocquier, Morgane Michel, Judith E Mueller, Maïa Simon, Amandine Gagneux-Brunon, Aurélie Gauchet, Serge Gilberg, Anne-Sophie Le Duc-Banaszuk, Sébastien Bruel, Bruno Giraudeau, Nathalie Thilly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine coverage (VC) remains suboptimal in many countries. The PrevHPV national research programme aimed to codevelop and evaluate a three-component intervention including: ‘education and motivation’ of adolescents, ‘at-school vaccination’, ‘general practitioners (GPs)’ training’. We assessed its impact on VC at one year through a cluster randomized trial (July 2021–April 2022) conducted in French municipalities (clusters) receiving 0, 1, 2, or 3 components. The outcomes (≥ 1-dose and full HPV VC at one year among 11–14-year-olds) were estimated using data from the national health reimbursement database and data collected during the trial. We performed intention-to-treat and post-hoc analysis adjusted for the dose of intervention using cluster-level linear models. Ninety-one municipalities were included. ‘At-school vaccination’ significantly increased ≥ 1-dose VC, in both intention-to-treat (+ 2.85 percentage points (pp), 95%CI = 0.62 to 5.09) and post-hoc analysis (+ 4.60 pp, 95%CI = 1.79 to 7.41). No significant effects were found for the other components. Results were similar for full VC. ‘At-school vaccination’ significantly increased VC only in municipalities with the lowest access to GPs. School-based vaccination was effective in increasing HPV VC in the medium term and may reduce VC healthcare access-driven territorial disparities. Challenges remain in parental engagement and consent for vaccination at school.Trial registration: Clinicaltrials.gov, NCT04945655. Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection is the most common viral infection of the reproductive tract and is a major public health issue1. Vaccination is the most effective primary prevention method and a safe strategy against infection2,3. Since 2006, most high-income countries have introduced HPV vaccination into their national vaccination schedules for adolescents4. However, HPV vaccine coverage (VC) has consistently remained lower than expected in many countries, particularly in France4,5, where the HPV vaccine was initially recommended for girls when it was introduced in 2007, and was recently (in 2021) included in the vaccine schedule for all adolescents aged 11–14 years, with two doses administrated 6 months apart. Besides, substantial social and territorial inequalities in HPV VC have been described in France and abroad6–10.

In 2018, HPV full VC among 16-year-old girls was only 24%11, leading French authorities to launch the national PrevHPV research programme to improve HPV vaccine acceptability and uptake among adolescents. The PrevHPV Study Group co-developed with stakeholders a multicomponent intervention12 and evaluated its effectiveness and implementation through a cluster randomised controlled trial (cRCT) conducted in 91 French municipalities13. Based on scientific evidence and a needs assessment14–16, the Study Group designed three components12: (1) an ‘education and motivation’ component to increase vaccination demand by improving adolescents’ and parents’ knowledge and attitudes; (2) an ‘at-school vaccination’ component to enhance geographical and financial accessibility to vaccination, in the French context where no school vaccination programme existed; and (3) a ‘general practitioners’ (GPs) training’ component to strengthen the healthcare offer by improving GPs’ knowledge of HPV and communication skills, as GPs prescribed most HPV vaccines in France10.

Evaluation of the PrevHPV trial’s primary outcome showed that the ‘at-school vaccination’ component significantly increased VC at two months, whereas the effects of the other components were not statistically significant (analyses conducted without considering the dose of intervention actually received, i.e., within a cluster, the extent to which the intervention was completed); the effect of the ‘at-school vaccination’ was also higher in municipalities with poor access to GPs17. When adjusting for the dose of intervention, ‘at-school vaccination’ still significantly increased VC (with a twofold effect), and ‘GPs’ training’ reached statistical significance, while ‘education and motivation’ remained non-significant18. However, it is plausible that the intervention, particularly the ‘education and motivation’ and ‘GPs’ training’ components, could significantly increase VC with some delay. Indeed, the vaccination pathway in France was somewhat complex at the time of the intervention and usually required two consecutive consultations with a physician. Moreover, although most French adolescents visit their GP at least once a year, the average number of consultations per year is approximatively two19, and GPs may face difficulties addressing HPV vaccination opportunistically20. The ‘education and motivation’ and ‘GPs’ training’ components could therefore increase families’ vaccine demand and the frequency with which GPs offer HPV vaccination during routine consultations over several months following the intervention.

In this context, the main objective of the present investigation was to assess the impact of the PrevHPV intervention on VC estimated one year after the end of the intervention, each of its components being applied alone or in combination. The secondary objective was to assess its impact on one-year VC, according to the adolescents’ sex, the municipalities’ socioeconomic level, and the municipalities’ access to GPs.

Methods

Study design

The PrevHPV trial was an open-label cRCT using an incomplete factorial design, conducted between July 2021 and April 2022 to evaluate the effectiveness, efficiency and implementation of the PrevHPV intervention under routine conditions. The study protocol has been previously published in detail13. Briefly, the clusters were municipalities. Inclusion criteria were: (i) located in one of the 14 selected French school districts (out of a total of 25); (ii) only one middle school in the municipality; and (iii) the head of the middle school agreed to participate in the trial. The first 91 municipalities (out of 351 randomly sampled from the 1205 eligible across France) whose schools volunteered to participate were included. Municipalities were then randomised by a researcher not involved in their selection into one of six groups (Fig. 1), using block randomisation (block size = 6), stratified by school district and the municipality’s socioeconomic level. The factorial design allowed for municipalities to receive zero (control group), one, two, or three components of the intervention, enabling the assessment of each component both individually and in combination. However, the factorial design was incomplete because the PrevHPV Study Group considered that the ‘at-school vaccination’ component should not be implemented without prior ‘education and motivation’ of pupils and their parents, in order to support informed decision-making.

The PrevHPV intervention

Details regarding the development and content of the three-component PrevHPV intervention have been previously published12.

The ‘education and motivation’ component was implemented in middle schools located in the selected municipalities (Groups 1, 2, 3 and 4—Fig. 1), between December 2021 and March 2022. Parents were first invited by the school to attend a 1.5-h web conference on HPV infection and vaccination, delivered by two experts. Subsequently, pupils in grades eight and nine (aged 13–14 years) participated in two 2-h educational group sessions on these topics during school hours. Each session employed participatory learning methods and e-health tools (e.g., serious game and videos), co-developed by the PrevHPV Study Group in collaboration with stakeholders.

The ‘at-school vaccination’ component involved offering the first dose of the HPV vaccine free of charge to eligible adolescents on the premises of middle schools in the selected municipalities (Groups 1 and 3—Fig. 1), following completion of the ‘education and motivation’ component, between March and April 2022. Adolescents were eligible if they had never received an HPV vaccine, were aged 11 years or older, had no contraindications, and if both parents had provided written informed consent. Vaccines were administered by health professionals from regional vaccination centres on prescheduled days.

The ‘GPs’ training’ component consisted of a voluntary individual e-learning training course lasting 3 to 4 h, between January and April 2022. All GPs practicing in the selected municipalities (Groups 1, 2 and 5—Fig. 1) were eligible, unless they planned to stop their practice before the end of the trial or practiced exclusively alternative medicine. The training covered up-to-date information on HPV infection and vaccination, an introduction to motivational interviewing techniques applied to vaccination, and a decision aid tool co-developed by the PrevHPV Study Group and stakeholders21. This tool was intended for use in routine practice to support shared decision-making on HPV vaccination with hesitant parents and/or adolescents.

Outcomes, data collected and sources

The outcomes were HPV VC, defined as receiving at least one dose (‘HPV ≥ 1-dose VC’) or two doses (‘HPV full VC’), among adolescents aged 11 to 14 years living in the included municipalities, one year after the end of the PrevHPV intervention (April 30, 2023). These two outcomes were selected in line with the French HPV vaccination schedule and the way French national statistics on HPV VC are reported22.

Two pseudonymised data sources were used to collect the data required to calculate the outcomes. First, the French national health reimbursement database (Système National des Données de Santé, SNDS) provided data on HPV vaccines dispensed in community pharmacies, as well as beneficiaries’ age, sex, and residential postcode (i.e., their municipality). We included HPV vaccines dispensed to adolescents aged 11 to 14 years living in the included municipalities, regardless of the location of their schools or of their GP practices. This database includes information on all healthcare services reimbursed by the statutory health insurance, covering over 99% of the French population23. A pseudonymised identification number enables linkage between the first and second doses of the HPV vaccine administered to each adolescent. Second, data on the number of adolescents who received the first dose of the HPV vaccine at school (not captured in the SNDS) in the municipalities randomised to implement this component were collected by the research team during the trial, along with their age, sex and municipality of residence.

As both data sources were pseudonymised, it was not possible to link a first dose of the HPV vaccine administered at school as part of the PrevHPV trial with a potential second dose received later outside of school. Consequently, for adolescents with only one dose recorded in the SNDS database, it was not possible to determine whether they had previously received a first dose at school. Therefore, the two HPV VC outcomes were calculated under the assumption that all adolescents who received their first dose at school also received a second dose outside of school within the following 12 months. This assumption was supported by national HPV VC statistics22 of the past five years, which indicate that approximatively 93% of French adolescents who initiated HPV vaccination (i.e., received the 1st dose) completed the full two-dose schedule.

The socioeconomic level of the municipality and access to GPs were assessed using two French ecological indices available in national public databases: (i) the French Deprivation Index (FDep), which is based on the percentage of blue-collar workers in the labor force, the percentage of high school graduates among individuals aged 15 years or older, the unemployment rate, and the median household income24, and (ii) the Local Potential Accessibility Index (LPA), which considers the number of GPs, their level of activity, the age of the population, and the distance to GPs’ offices within the municipality25.

Statistical analyses

The analyses were conducted on all 91 municipalities included in the trial. One of the randomisation groups consisted of municipalities assigned to be the control (Group 6) and analyses were performed using this group as a reference group. Two perspectives were used for analyses: (i) assuming that all municipalities received the highest dose of intervention (intention-to-treat analysis), and (ii) based on the actual dose of intervention received by each municipality (post hoc on-treatment analysis). The dose of intervention was calculated for each included municipality and for each component it was expected to implement. It was treated as a quantitative variable representing, from a population health perspective, the opportunity for adolescents to be vaccinated in a given municipality. For each municipality and each component, the dose of intervention ranged from zero to one—zero if the component was not implemented at all, and one if it was fully implemented. Schools that withdrew their consent prior to implementing the component were assigned a dose value of zero.

Descriptive analyses of baseline and follow-up characteristics of municipalities, schools and GPs, as well as the dose of intervention received and HPV ≥ 1-dose VC at baseline and at 12 months, were first conducted by group. Categorical variables were presented using counts and percentages, while continuous variables were described using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR: 1st and 3rd quartile), excepted for the dose of intervention which was expressed as means and standard deviations.

The impact of each component on HPV ≥ 1-dose VC and HPV full VC was evaluated using a cluster-level analysis, in which each municipality was weighted by its size (i.e., the number of adolescents aged 11–14 residing in the municipality). In the intention-to-treat analysis, a linear model including three fixed effects (one per component), adjusted for baseline HPV ≥ 1-dose VC, was fitted with and without interaction terms between components. A likelihood ratio test was used to compare the models with and without interaction terms. As the test was non-significant, interaction terms were excluded from subsequent analyses. Assuming no interaction, the overall impact of the three components corresponds to the sum of their individual impacts. It should be noted that because the components ‘at-school vaccination’ and ‘education and motivation’ were always implemented together, it was not possible to evaluate the interaction between them. We hypothesised that no interaction existed between these components. The impact for ‘at-school vaccination’ should therefore always be interpreted by considering it in adjunction to the impact of the ‘education and motivation’ component. In the post hoc on-treatment analysis, the model’s fixed effects were replaced by the dose of intervention actually received by each municipality for each component. We also re-fitted the models adjusting for the continuous variables FDep and LPA.

We also explored the effect of each component by sex (as HPV vaccination was recommended earlier for girls than for boys), municipalities’ socioeconomic level (FDep), and access to GPs in the municipality (LPA). For sex, as it is an individual-level variable, we re-ran cluster-level intention-to-treat and post hoc on-treatment analyses stratifying by sex (i.e. analyses re-ran for each sex). For FDep and LPA—both cluster-level variables—we fitted linear models including interaction terms between each intervention component and a dichotomised variable (only intention-to-treat analysis could thus be performed). For this, we dichotomised the FDep and LPA variables by grouping the two most disadvantaged quintiles according to the national distribution (FDep: quintiles 4 and 5, most deprived; LPA: quintiles 1 and 2, lowest access) versus the remaining three quintiles.

All analysis were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

Ethics and quality

The PrevHPV trial received approval from the French Ethics Committee ‘Comité de Protection des Personnes—CPP Sud-Est VI’ on 22 December 2020 (ID-RCB: 2020-A02031-38). In accordance with French law, no individual consent was required for this type of research. Parental consent was required only for the HPV vaccine administration as it is the case for all vaccinations. All participants (adolescents and their parents, GPs) were informed of their rights, including the right not to participate.

This trial complies with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guideline, and the extension for Cluster Randomised Trials was used as the reporting framework for the present article26,27.

Public involvement

Some adolescents, parents of adolescents, GPs, and other stakeholders were involved in the co-development process of the intervention components during an earlier stage of the PrevHPV research programme12. They were not involved in the study design, analysis or dissemination of results described in the present paper.

Following randomisation, 31 out of the 91 middle schools (34%) withdrew their consent before the start of the trial. Consequently, the dose of intervention for the ‘education and motivation’ and ‘at-school vaccination’ components, for middle schools concerned by these components, was zero. The mean dose of the ‘education and motivation’ component ranged from 0.23 to 0.28 depending on the group, while the mean dose of the ‘at-school vaccination’ component ranged from 0.44 to 0.72. Of the 199 GPs practicing in the 45 eligible municipalities, 43 participated in the online training (participating rate: 22%), with the mean dose of this component ranging from 0.20 to 0.31 depending on the group.

Impact on vaccination coverage in all 11–14 years old

HPV vaccination coverage (at least 1 dose)

At 12 months, the median HPV ≥ 1-dose VC ranged from 21.2 to 29.4% across the intervention groups, compared to 23.1% in the control group, with the highest VC observed in the two groups that implemented ‘at-school vaccination’ (Table 2). The ‘at-school vaccination’ component significantly increased HPV VC among adolescents aged 11–14 years, in both the intention-to-treat analysis (+ 2.85 percentage points (pp), 95%CI = 0.62 to 5.09) and the post-hoc on-treatment analysis (+ 4.60 pp, 95%CI = 1.79 to 7.41). No significant effects were found for the ‘education and motivation’ or ‘GPs training’ components (Table 3).

HPV full vaccination coverage (2 doses)

Results were similar to those observed with at least one dose of the HPV vaccine. The ‘at-school vaccination’ component significantly increased HPV full VC in both the intention-to-treat analysis (+ 3.45 pp, 95%CI = 1.72 to 5.18), and the post-hoc on-treatment analysis (+ 5.38 pp, 95%CI = 3.29 to 7.47). Sex-specific impact on vaccination coverage in 11–14 years old

Among girls

At 12 months, median HPV ≥ 1-dose VC ranged from 30.6 to 37.2% across the intervention groups, compared to 31.1% in the control group (Table 2).

The ‘at-school vaccination’ component significantly increased HPV ≥ 1-dose VC among girls aged 11–14 years in the post-hoc on-treatment analysis (+ 4.35 pp, 95%CI = 0.73 to 7.97) (Table 3). No significant effects were found for the ‘education and motivation’ or ‘GPs training’ components.

Among boys

At 12 months, median HPV ≥ 1-dose VC among boys ranged from 13.9 to 21.0% across the intervention groups, compared to 15.2% in the control group (Table 2).

The ‘at-school vaccination’ component significantly increased both outcomes among boys, in both the intention-to-treat analysis (HPV ≥ 1-dose VC: + 3.74 pp, 95%CI = 1.11 to 6.36; full VC: + 3.85 pp, 95%CI = 2.08 to 5.63) and the post-hoc on-treatment analysis (HPV ≥ 1-dose VC: + 5.37 pp, 95%CI = 2.07 to 8.66; full VC: + 5.78 pp, 95%CI = 3.62 to 7.94) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this municipality-level randomised trial conducted in France, which evaluated an intervention combining vaccine promotion in middle schools and GPs’ training, we observed that effects one year after implementation were similar to those reported immediately afterward. We found a significant positive impact of the ‘at-school vaccination’ component (systematically preceded by the ‘education and motivation’ component) on HPV VC (at least 1 dose and 2 doses) one year after the end of the intervention among adolescents aged in 11–14 years, both girls and boys. These findings remain consistent after adjusting for the municipality’s socioeconomic level and access to GPs. In contrast, we did not observe a significant impact of the ‘education and motivation’ or ‘GPs training’ components on HPV VC. All these main results are quite similar to those reported two months after the end of the intervention17,18. Results statistically significant in the post-hoc on-treatment analyses were also significant in the intention-to-treat analyses, although the effect size was smaller in the latter.

At-school vaccination

Our findings on the impact of at-school vaccination on HPV VC align with the literature5,28. Although statistically significant, the effect sizes observed at 12 months were smaller than those found at two months (Intention-to-treat analysis: + 5.50 pp, 95%CI = 3.13 to 7.88; Post hoc on-treatment analysis: 11.25 pp, 95% CI = 9.09 to 13.40). This is due to a dilution effect involving: (i) 11-year-old adolescents entering in the 11–14-year-old cohort during the year following the intervention, who do not receive at-school vaccination under routine practice, and (ii) 14-year-old adolescents leaving the cohort, who were vaccinated at school as part of the PrevHPV trial. This effect size may appear quite low compared with results achieved in countries that have long-established routine school-based vaccination programmes (e.g., UK, Australia, Canada)5,29. However, this result should be interpreted considering the French context. The last national school-based vaccination campaign, conducted in the 1990s, has remained associated with public controversy, including among school staff15,30, and vaccination has not been carried out in schools since then. Furthermore, the French population has also acquired an international reputation for vaccine hesitancy, with 41% expressing negative opinions about vaccine safety in 201631. Nevertheless, international studies provide insights into how school-based vaccination could be improved in France: e.g., providing information about HPV vaccination the school year preceding vaccination, using decision-aid tools to promote shared adolescent-parent decision-making, optimising consent forms return through incentives or electronic systems, and contacting parents who did not return consent forms using motivational interviewing techniques28,32–36. As decision-making process regarding HPV vaccination is influenced not only by convenience-related factors but also by a broader range of individual, social, and contextual determinants37,38, it would also be valuable to better understand decision-making in the context of school-based vaccination in France. Such insights may help identify strategies to improve the effectiveness of school-based vaccination campaigns, which were generalised in France in September 202339.

Furthermore, studies from multiple countries have reported social and territorial inequalities in HPV vaccination, with disadvantaged populations having lower HPV vaccine initiation and completion rates8–10. Our findings indicate that at-school vaccination did not contribute to reducing social health inequalities but did not exacerbate them either, in contrast to reports in literature describing lower participation in prevention programmes among individuals with lower socioeconomic status40,41. Conversely, a significant positive impact of at-school vaccination at one year was observed in municipalities with the lowest access to GPs, but not in those with the highest access, as already observed at two months. This suggests that this school-based strategy may help mitigate territorial health inequalities by providing vaccination independently of local GPs’ availability.

Education and motivation

In previous publications from the PrevHPV trial17,18, no significant effect of the ‘education and motivation’ component on HPV VC was reported at two months. We hypothesised at the time that this lack of effect may have been due to an insufficient follow-up period, as vaccination pathways in France in 2021–22 usually include two consecutive consultations: one for the prescription of the vaccine, which then has to be bought by the patient in a community pharmacy, and another for the administration of the vaccine. Moreover, French adolescents make relatively few visits to their GP, with a mean of only two consultations per year at that age19, and GPs may face difficulties in discussing HPV vaccination opportunistically20. However, even with a longer follow-up of one year after the end of the intervention, the ‘education and motivation’ component still did not increase HPV VC. Therefore, our hypothesis that improved knowledge and attitudes through this component would, over time, lead to more favourable behaviours and increase vaccination uptake could not be confirmed. This may be due to the fact that while the majority of adolescents did receive the component, parents were much harder to reach, with very few attending the webconference on HPV and vaccination. The literature has pointed out the crucial role of parents in HPV vaccination decision-making42 and so efforts must be made to increase their participation in this type of intervention to hopefully achieve higher impact. Nevertheless, although the ‘education and motivation’ component did not significantly increase HPV VC, other findings from the trial suggest that it significantly improved several determinants of vaccine uptake (e.g., knowledge about HPV infections and vaccination, perceptions of the benefit-risk balance, vaccination intention)43,44.

Moreover, our findings indicate that the ‘education and motivation’ component significantly decreased HPV VC (at least 1 dose) in the most socially deprived municipalities. This may represent an unintended consequence of the design choices made during the development of this component. Because we used discrete choice experiments (DCE) to identify key messages for parents and adolescents16,45 and co-constructed the intervention with the target populations, it is possible that participants engaged at both stages were not individuals from areas of high social deprivation and/or with low health literacy. Numerous studies have reported barriers to participation in clinical research among people with low socioeconomic status46–49, and it is reasonable to assume that similar barriers apply to public health research, in which literacy plays an important role. Consequently, the ‘education and motivation’ component may not have addressed the information needs of this population and may, for some unforeseen reason, have had the opposite effect. Research is needed to identify intervention that improve vaccine uptake in specific subgroups.

GPs training

Results for ‘GPs’ training’ were more mixed. Its impact on HPV VC at one year was at the threshold of statistical significance (p = 0.060) when adjusting for both the dose of intervention and the availability of GPs in the municipalities (+ 4.42pp, 95% CI = − 0.18 to 9.01). At two months, a significant effect of ‘GPs’ training’ (p = 0.049) was detected when adjusting for the dose of intervention (+ 3.56pp, 95%CI = 0.02 to 7.11)18. Results at one year suggest that this effect diminishes over time. Of greater concern, ‘GPs training’ was found to significantly decrease HPV VC specifically in municipalities with the highest access to GPs. We cannot explain this result, but it could be due to some unaccounted-for confounders. These factors might be related to GPs’ attitudes and perceptions toward vaccination, or professional characteristics (e.g., practice of alternative medicine) known to be associated with practices toward vaccination50.

Strengths and limitations

The PrevHPV trial is the first to evaluate a multicomponent intervention aimed at improving HPV VC in France in the medium term (one year after the intervention), using a high-level-of-evidence design. However, several limits should be acknowledged. First, the main limit is probably the inability to distinguish between the first and second vaccine doses in the SNDS for the groups receiving at-school vaccination. We hypothesised that all adolescents who received their first dose at school also received a second dose outside of school within the following 12 months. Although this assumption was supported by national HPV VC statistics22, we cannot be certain that adolescents’ behaviours in the context of the PrevHPV trial were the same as in real-life setting, and we have no means of verifying this. If this hypothesis is not correct, and some adolescents who received the first dose at school did not receive the second dose, our findings would indicate that the at-school vaccination had a greater impact on HPV vaccination initiation but a lesser impact on completion. Second, the impact of at-school vaccination presented here is based on the assumption that no interaction exists between this component and the ‘education and motivation’ component, which cannot be verified. The impact found for ‘at-school vaccination’ should therefore always be interpreted by considering it in adjunction to the impact of the ‘education and motivation’ component. Third, some of the adolescents residing in the participating municipalities did not attend school or consult a GP within their municipality, and were therefore not exposed to the intervention. This may have contributed to an underestimation of the intervention’s effect and could partly explain the lack of significant effect of the ‘education and motivation’ and ‘GPs’ training’ components. Fourth, the post-Covid-19 context greatly impacted participation in the PrevHPV trial by both schools and GPs, and we faced both high drop-out rates from schools and low participation to the e-training by GPs. To address this, we carried out a post-hoc analysis adjusting for the dose of intervention received at the municipality level. This resulted in a higher effect size for the at-school component but associations remained non-significant for the two other components. However, we acknowledge that some implementation gaps may not have been measured by the dose of intervention. Additionally, the observed increase in HPV VC in the control group, which may reflect the gradual nationwide upward trend since 201522, the recent extension of HPV vaccination to boys that occurred in 202151, and a possible Hawthorne effect52, may have reduced the study’s power to detect an effect of the intervention components and/or the effect sizes. Finally, the relatively short implementation period of the intervention components may have limited the magnitude of the effects; however, the intervention content and the duration of its implementation were co-developed with stakeholders to enhance acceptability and facilitate effective implementation in routine practice12.

In conclusion, the PrevHPV trial provides evidence that school-based vaccination is effective in raising HPV VC not only in the short term (2 months), but also in the medium term (12 months). The first results of the trial contributed to the political decision to generalise at-school HPV vaccination in France in September 2023, with both doses administered on school premises for free. However, remaining issues still need to be addressed, such as strategies to improve parental engagement and consent to vaccination at school, with particular attention to the most socially disadvantaged populations, as well as strategies to improve GPs’ participation to training.
