On the series expansion of the prime zeta function about s = 1 s=1 and its coefficients
Artur Kawalec

Abstract
In this article, we derive a series expansion of the prime zeta function about the s = 1 s=1 logarithmic singularity and prove general formula for its expansion coefficients, which is similar to the Stieltjes expansion coefficients for the Riemann zeta function. These results can also be viewed as a generalization of Mertens’s Theorems to higher order. We also numerically verify and compute the presented formulas to high precision for several test cases.



1
Introduction

Let
p=\{2,3,5,7,\ldots\}
be a sequence of primes and the prime zeta function

P(s)=\sum_{p}\frac{1}{p^{s}}

(1)

is defined as a generalized zeta series over primes, which converges absolutely for
\Re(s)>1
. Through the Möbius inversion formula as

P(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\log\zeta(ns)

(2)

the prime zeta can be further continued for
\Re(s)>0
. From this it appears that the prime zeta has logarithmic singularities (branch points) at
s=\frac{1}{n}
and
\tfrac{\rho}{n}
for all
n\geq 1
whenever
\mu(n)\neq 0
(
\rho
is a non-trivial root of the zeta function). As a result, the prime zeta is a multi-valued function. And also, there is a natural boundary line at
\Re(s)=0
, after which
P(s)
cannot be further continued to
\Re(s)\leq 0
because of a dense accumulation of singularities that form near
\Re(s)=0
[10, p. 215]. That’s why branch points due to trivial zeros of the zeta function don’t even enter
P(s)
.

In this article, we define a branch of
P(s)
by the series expansion about the
s=1
singularity as

P(s)=\log\left(\frac{1}{s-1}\right)+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}\frac{(s-1)^{n}}{n!}

(3)

The regular part of this series is analytic on a disk at
s=1
with radius
R=\frac{1}{2}
, but because of the log term, we can define a branch cut on the line
(\tfrac{1}{2},1]
inside the disk.

The main result of this article is we derive a general log-limit formula for these expansion coefficients as

Theorem 1
.

For
n=0
, the coefficient is:

\alpha_{0}=\lim_{x\to\infty}\Bigg\{\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1}{p}-\log\log(x)\Bigg\}-\gamma

(4)

where
\gamma
is Euler’s constant, and for
n\geq 1
the
n
th coefficient is:

\alpha_{n}=\lim_{x\to\infty}(-1)^{n}\Bigg\{\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\log^{n}(p)}{p}-\frac{\log^{n}(x)}{n}\Bigg\}

(5)

This limit formula is similar to the formula for Stieltjes constants in [1, p. 807], but it converges very slowly. But fortunately, the other representation by equation (2) involving the Möbius function has much faster convergence, which we used to compute
\alpha_{n}
to high precision in Table
1
. So based on that these expansion coefficients can also be expressed by

Theorem 2
.

For
n\geq 0
we have

\alpha_{n}=g_{n}+\frac{d^{n}}{ds^{n}}\Bigg[\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(k)}{k}\log\zeta(ks)\Bigg]_{s\to 1}

(6)

where
g_{n}
are the expansion coefficients of
\log\zeta(s)
about
s=1
(see Voros [11, p. 25]). The first few
g_{n}
coefficients can be generated as

\displaystyle g_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

(7)

\displaystyle g_{1}

\displaystyle=\gamma,

\displaystyle g_{2}

\displaystyle=-\gamma^{2}-2\gamma_{1},

\displaystyle g_{3}

\displaystyle=2\gamma^{3}+6\gamma\gamma_{1}+3\gamma_{2},

\displaystyle g_{4}

\displaystyle=-6\gamma^{4}-12\gamma^{2}_{1}-24\gamma^{2}\gamma_{1}-12\gamma\gamma_{2}-4\gamma_{3},

\displaystyle g_{5}

\displaystyle=120\gamma^{3}\gamma_{1}+120\gamma\gamma_{1}^{2}+60\gamma^{2}\gamma_{2}+60\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}+20\gamma\gamma_{3}+5\gamma_{4}+24\gamma^{5},

\displaystyle g_{6}

\displaystyle=-720\gamma^{4}\gamma_{1}-1080\gamma^{2}\gamma_{1}^{2}-240\gamma_{1}^{3}-360\gamma^{3}\gamma_{2}-720\gamma\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}-90\gamma_{2}^{2}+

\displaystyle-120\gamma^{2}\gamma_{3}-120\gamma_{1}\gamma_{3}-30\gamma\gamma_{4}-6\gamma_{5}-120\gamma^{6},

and so on, where they are expressed in terms of Stieltjes constants. These
g_{n}
coefficients and their variations (often written by
\eta_{n}
) can also be found in Maślanka [6] and Bombieri-Lagarias [2] as being defined coefficients for
\zeta^{\prime}(s)/\zeta(s)
function. They are generated by a recurrence relation of Coffey [3, p. 532] in terms of Stieltjes constants. The proof of Theorem 2 is to subtract the log singularity from
P(s)
and differentiating as

\alpha_{n}=\frac{d^{n}}{ds^{n}}\Bigg[P(s)-\log\left(\frac{1}{s-1}\right)\Bigg]_{s\to 1}

(8)

and substituting (2) for
P(s)
, since the log-zeta has similar series expansion

\log\zeta(s)=\log\left(\frac{1}{s-1}\right)+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}g_{n}\frac{(s-1)^{n}}{n!}

(9)

with
g_{n}
coefficients. This log-zeta series is analytic on a disk centered at
s=1
but with radius of convergence
R=3
due to being limited by the first trivial zero of the zeta at
-2
, and so, because of the log term we can define a branch cut
(-2,1]
for (9).

The first few cases of Theorem
1
and
2
are very well-known in the literature, i.e, the Mertens’s Theorems [12]. For
n=0
case is the harmonic sum of prime numbers

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to\infty}\Bigg\{\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1}{p}-\log\log(x)\Bigg\}

(10)

\displaystyle=0.26149721284764278375\ldots

where the constant
M
is known as the Meissel-Mertens’s first constant (OEIS A077761), see also [7]. The relation

\alpha_{0}=M-\gamma

(11)

shows that
\alpha_{0}
is offset by Euler’s constant

\gamma=0.5772156649015328606\ldots

(12)

From (6) the value of

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\log\zeta(n)

(13)

\displaystyle=-0.31571845205389007685\ldots

is the constant (OEIS A143524), which can be computed to high precision using this series involving the Möbius function.

For
n=1
the limit formula is

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to\infty}-\Bigg\{\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\log(p)}{p}-\log(x)\Bigg\}

(14)

\displaystyle=\gamma+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mu(n)\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(n)}{\zeta(n)}

\displaystyle=1.33258227573322088176\ldots

which is result of Mertens’s Theorem (OEIS A083343) [12]. And for
n=2
the formula is

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to\infty}\Bigg\{\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\log^{2}(p)}{p}-\frac{\log^{2}(x)}{2}\Bigg\}

(15)

\displaystyle=-\gamma^{2}-2\gamma_{1}+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mu(n)n\left[-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(n)^{2}}{\zeta(n)^{2}}+\frac{\zeta^{\prime\prime}(n)}{\zeta(n)}\right]

\displaystyle=-2.55510761544644523959\ldots

but such a constant doesn’t seem to be computed in the literature. And one more example, for
n=3
case we generate

\displaystyle\alpha_{3}

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to\infty}-\Bigg\{\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\log^{3}(p)}{p}-\frac{\log^{3}(x)}{3}\Bigg\}

(16)

\displaystyle=2\gamma^{3}+6\gamma\gamma_{1}+3\gamma_{2}+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mu(n)n^{2}\left[2\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(n)^{3}}{\zeta(n)^{3}}-3\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(n)\zeta^{\prime\prime}(n)}{\zeta(n)^{2}}+\frac{\zeta^{\prime\prime\prime}(n)}{\zeta(n)}\right]

\displaystyle=10.25382709691100753877\ldots

and so on. These
n
th log-zeta derivatives generate long such sequences of derivative terms in the series. In Table
1
we computed the first
10
coefficients numerically to high precision as a reference.

We also derived an alternate formula for these expansion coefficients during proof of Theorem
1
as follows

Theorem 3
.

\alpha_{n}=(-1)^{n}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\log^{n}(t)-n\log^{n-1}(t)}{t^{2}}[\pi(t)-\operatorname{li}(t)]\,dt

(17)

valid for
n\geq 0
as shown in equation (34) and the logarithmic integral
\operatorname{li}(x)
is defined in (21).

in next Section.

Proof of Theorem 1

To show that, let us recap some basic definitions. The prime counting function is defined by

\pi(x)=\sum_{p\leq x}1

(18)

for positive integer argument
x>0
, and by the average value

\pi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg[\sum_{p<x}1+\sum_{p\leq x}1\Bigg]

(19)

for positive real argument
x>0
. This means that its value is defined as an average of the two sides of the step whenever
x
is integer. The asymptotic formula is

\pi(x)=\operatorname{li}(x)+f(x)

(20)

where the logarithmic integral is defined by the P.V. integral function

\operatorname{li}(x)=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{0}^{x}\frac{1}{\log t}dt

(21)

for real
x>0
. One also has the relation

\frac{d}{dx}\operatorname{li}(x)=\frac{1}{\log x}.

(22)

And as for the remainder
f(x)
the best estimate is

f(x)=O(\sqrt{x}\log x)

(23)

assuming (RH). Also, the
m^{th}
derivatives of (1) are given by

(-1)^{m}P^{(m)}(s)=\sum_{p}\frac{\log^{m}(p)}{p^{s}}

(24)

for
\Re(s)>1
.

So we proceed proving equations (3-5) by carrying out appropriate Stieltjes integration. For the
m=0
case (see Pain [9]) we write

\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1}{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{1+\delta}^{x}\frac{1}{t}d\pi(t)=\int_{1+\delta}^{x}\frac{1}{t}d\operatorname{li}(t)+\int_{1+\delta}^{x}\frac{1}{t}df(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{1+\delta}^{x}\frac{1}{t\log(t)}\,dt+\left[\frac{1}{t}f(t)\right]_{1+\delta}^{x}-\int_{1+\delta}^{x}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{t}\right)f(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=\Bigg[\log\log(x)-\log\log(1+\delta)\Bigg]+\Bigg[\frac{1}{x}f(x)-f(1+\delta)\Bigg]+\int_{1}^{x}\frac{1}{t^{2}}f(t)\,dt

(25)

Since the lower boundary term diverges at
t=1
, we take the limit
t=1+\delta
as
\delta\to 0^{+}
. From (19) we have that

f(1+\delta)=-\operatorname{li}(1+\delta)

(26)

and because
\pi(1^{-})=0
,
\pi(1)=0
and
\pi(1^{+})=0
we take the limit from positive side. The series expansion of log integral is

\operatorname{li}(x)=\gamma+\log\log(x)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{[\log(x)]^{k}}{k\>k!}

(27)

so that near
1
we have

\operatorname{li}(1+\delta)=\gamma+\log\log(1+\delta)+o(1)

(28)

and as the divergent parts of the dominant and error terms cancel, we get

\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1}{p}=\log\log(x)+\gamma+\alpha_{0}+O\left(\frac{\log x}{\sqrt{x}}\right)

(29)

And the integral converges to the
0
th order coefficient

\alpha_{0}=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{2}}f(t)dt.

(30)

Now for the
m\geq 1
case, we insert the
\log^{m}(t)
factor in the integral and redo the calculation as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\log^{m}(p)}{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{x}\frac{\log^{m}(t)}{t}d\pi(t)=\int_{1}^{x}\frac{\log^{m}(t)}{t}d\operatorname{li}(t)+\int_{1}^{x}\frac{\log^{m}(t)}{t}df(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{x}\frac{\log^{m-1}(t)}{t}\,dt+\left[\frac{\log^{m}(t)}{t}f(t)\right]_{1}^{x}-\int_{1}^{x}\frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{\log^{m}(t)}{t}\right]f(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=A(x)+\frac{\log^{m}(x)}{x}f(x)-\int_{1}^{x}\frac{m\log^{m-1}(t)-\log^{m}(t)}{t^{2}}f(t)\,dt

(31)

and this integral identity can be generated as

\displaystyle A(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{x}\frac{\log^{m-1}(t)}{t}\,dt

(32)

\displaystyle=\frac{\log^{m}(x)}{m}

for
m\geq 1
. Then, it is readily seen that the limit

\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\log^{m}(p)}{p}-\frac{\log^{m}(x)}{m}=(-1)^{m}\alpha_{m}+O\left(\frac{\log^{m+1}(x)}{\sqrt{x}}\right)

(33)

exists as
x\to\infty
and converges to a constant

\alpha_{m}=(-1)^{m}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\log^{m}(t)-m\log^{m-1}(t)}{t^{2}}f(t)\,dt

(34)

for
m\geq 1
by the estimate (23). Now the next step is to connect this constant to the series expansion in (3) as follows:

\displaystyle P(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{1+\delta}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{s}}\,d\pi(t)=\int_{1+\delta}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{s}}d\operatorname{li}(t)\,+\int_{1+\delta}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{s}}df(t)

(35)

\displaystyle=\int_{1+\delta}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{s}\log t}dt\,+\Biggl[\frac{f(t)}{t^{s}}\Biggr]_{1+\delta}^{\infty}+s\int_{1+\delta}^{\infty}t^{-s-1}f(t)\,dt

for
\Re(s)>1
as
\delta\to 0^{+}
, where here again, the integrand diverges at
t=1
where it has a simple pole. The anti-derivative is related to the logarithmic or exponential integral function (21), but we express the anti-derivative in terms of the exponential integral as

\int_{1+\delta}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{s}\log t}dt=\operatorname{E_{1}}[(s-1)\log(1+\delta)]

(36)

by substitution
u=\log t
and
t=e^{u}
and
dt=e^{u}du
yields the definition

\operatorname{E_{1}}(z)=\int_{z}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-t}}{t}dt,\quad|\arg(z)|<\pi

(37)

of the exponential integral valid for all complex
z
, but with a branch cut on the negative real axis
(-\infty,0]
as defined in [1, p. 228]. The function
\operatorname{E_{1}}(z)
also admits the series expansion

\operatorname{E_{1}}(z)=-\gamma-\log(z)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-z)^{k}}{k\>k!}

(38)

And so, the result of (36) has the expansion

\displaystyle\operatorname{E_{1}}[(s-1)\log(1+\delta)]

\displaystyle=-\gamma-\log[(s-1)\log(1+\delta)]-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{[-(s-1)\log(1+\delta)]^{k}}{k\>k!}

(39)

\displaystyle=-\gamma-\log(s-1)-\log\log(1+\delta)+o(1)

where it diverges as
\delta\to 0^{+}
. The logarithm of product split is justified because
\arg(1+\delta)\to 0
. Then the second term arising from the boundary condition of the second integral in (35) also diverges

\displaystyle f(1+\delta)

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{li}(1+\delta)

(40)

\displaystyle=-\gamma-\log\log(1+\delta)+o(1)

As a result, on combining (39) and (40) one takes the limit

\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\Bigg[\operatorname{E_{1}}((s-1)\log(1+\delta))-f(1+\delta)\Bigg]=\log\left(\frac{1}{s-1}\right)

(41)

there is a cancelation of two divergent quantities for
\Re(s)>1
, where we recover the log term, but then the log term analytically extends to larger domain except for the branch cut
(-\infty,1]
. And so, this results in

P(s)=\log\left(\frac{1}{s-1}\right)+s\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{-s-1}f(t)\,dt

(42)

the integral in (42) converges for
\Re(s)>\tfrac{1}{2}
when one inserts (23) assuming (RH). As a result, it defines an analytical continuation of
P(s)
for
\Re(s)>\tfrac{1}{2}
with a branch cut
(\frac{1}{2},1]
.

It now remains to show that this integral admits the series expansion about
s=1
, so we consider

\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{-s-1}f(t)\,dt

(43)

which converges for
\Re(s)>\tfrac{1}{2}
assuming (RH). One then generates the exp-log expansion as

\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{-2}e^{-(s-1)\log t}f(t)\,dt=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{j}\frac{(s-1)^{j}}{j!}

(44)

where its expansion coefficients can be read off as

c_{j}=(-1)^{j}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\log^{j}(t)}{t^{2}}f(t)\,dt

(45)

but such a series is only limited to
|s-1|<1
domain. However, on writing integral term in (42) in
s-1
domain, then one needs to consider this form instead

\displaystyle s\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{-s-1}f(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=(s-1)\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{-s-1}f(t)\,dt+\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{-s-1}f(t)\,dt

(46)

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}[(s-1)t^{-s-1}f(t)+t^{-s-1}f(t)]\,dt

where we can generate a new series expansion as

\displaystyle s\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{-s-1}f(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(j+1)c_{j}\frac{(s-1)^{j+1}}{(j+1)!}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{j}\frac{(s-1)^{j}}{j!}

(47)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}mc_{m-1}\frac{(s-1)^{m}}{m!}+c_{0}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}c_{m}\frac{(s-1)^{m}}{m!}

\displaystyle=c_{0}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\left[mc_{m-1}+c_{m}\right]\frac{(s-1)^{m}}{m!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}y_{j}\frac{(s-1)^{j}}{j!}

by combining the expansions in (46). Here we’ve shifted index variable
m=j+1
in first series in (47) and re-labeled
m=j
in second series in (47). The new expansion coefficients are then

y_{m}=\begin{cases}c_{0}&\text{for }m=0\\
mc_{m-1}+c_{m}&\text{for }m\geq 1\end{cases}

(48)

where they are expressed by
c_{n}
coefficients, and by (45) these coefficients can be expressed by the integral

y_{m}=(-1)^{m}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\log^{m}(t)-m\log^{m-1}(t)}{t^{2}}f(t)\,dt

(49)

which matches the result in (34), hence for the
m\geq 0
we have
\alpha_{m}=y_{m}
. Hence this completes the proof.
