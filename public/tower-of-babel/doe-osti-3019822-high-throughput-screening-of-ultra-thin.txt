  Executive Summary:

 Current HVAC systems can constitute as much as half of a building’s total energy
consumption, depending on its use. More specifically cooling, particularly in hotter climates or for
energy intensive applications, like data centers, can comprise between 10-30% of the total energy
use. These cooling systems are largely comprised of compressor-based direct expansion systems
and chilled water systems that use refrigerants or water, respectively, to reject heat. . Electrocaloric
(EC) cycles present an eco-friendly alternative to these conventional processes.
 To activate these cycles, materials must be exposed to a high strength electric field that in
polarizes the structure of a material so that it can absorb and release heat. One of the most effective
form factors for EC materials are thin films since electrodes, which are applied to both sides of the
film, are close and therefore electric field strengths are high. This requires the development of
defect free films that will not short or exhibit dielectric breakdown. The manufacturing process
itself must also be scalable to ensure that it can be applied to typical HVAC system components
(such as ducts) and integrate into HVAC systems.
 This project introduced electrospray (ES) as a manufacturing process for thin film
electrocaloric materials. Working with a specific polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) terpolymer with
demonstrated EC properties, we electrosprayed thin polymer films and characterized them for their
thickness, morphology, dialectric breakdown strength, and temperature lift (an indication of a
film’s ability to increase in temperature under an electric field). The resulting films exhibit
superior thickness control, a smoother surfaces, and improved thermal and electrical properties
compared to solution casting (SC). Electrosprayed films also are conformal, meaning that they
are defect free, because of the electrical charge that must dissipate during processing. This means
that any gaps in the film are filled automatically by the charged droplets. The films thus exhibited
100 MV/m or greater breakdown strength.

 4

 We also found post-annealing of the films at 120˚C enhances the thermal and EC
performance, with films achieving a temperature change (ΔT) of 3.6˚C at 100 MV/m when tested
near room temperature. This work was published in Cell Reports Physical Sciences.
 We further explore the incorporation of liquid crystals into the thin films. Liquid crystals
(LCs) have been shown to exhibit electrocaloric behavior, but they cannot be formed into films
where they can be used under electric fields. Our goal was to incorporate LCs into these films to
enhance the electrocaloric effect. The work showed that the two materials were incompatible to a
certain extent due to phase separation during processing. Furthermore, the LC materials will often
“activate” (become electroactive) at different temperatures than the terpolymer matrix. While our
results indicated that incorporation of LCs did not have a profound impact on electrocaloric
performance, they also did not reduce performance. We believe that further exploration is needed
for LC containing EC films.
 The results of this work show that electrospray processing can create thin electrocaloric
films, with necessary breakdown strengths using one of the best EC polymers available today. We
found that electrospray processing may also enhance the EC effect, possibly due to the increased
order, or crystallinity, observed with electrosprayed films when compared to films made through
traditional casting. There are opportunities to translate this technology to commercial practice
with additional support and process integration work.

1. Introduction

 Conventional cooling technology used in heating, venting, and air conditioning (HVAC)
systems is based on a vapor compression cycle that has been estimated to consume more than more
than 20% of the electricity generated in the world and contribute significantly to greenhouse gases
emissions.1 Overall, air conditioning and electric fans accounts for about a fifth of the total
electricity consumption today, and energy consumption for cooling purposes in the world is
expected to triple by 2050.2 Vapor compression in the refrigeration industry only reaches 60% of
operating efficiency, and the efficiency tends to be 20-30% lower when the size of the system
reduced.1 The refrigeration market is also continuing to expand, with the compound annual growth
rate in the U.S. expected to increase by about 4.3% from 2023 to 20303, and this has the potential
to exponentially increase climate change.4 Solid-state cooling technology based on an
electrocaloric cycle is a possible alternative to vapor compression systems that may permit the
development of more efficient heat pumps with lower global warming potential and more
environmentally-friendly materials compared to the conventional vapor compression cooler.
 The basic theory behind electrocaloric devices is the conversion between thermal energy
and electrical energy in an electrocaloric cycle. The entropy of the system leads to the change in
thermal energy, and the energy can be modified by both the operating temperature and the applied
field.5,6 The cooling occurs through adiabatic depolarization whereas the heating occurs through
adiabatic polarization under a constant electric field.5 Figure 1 (adapted from reference6)
demonstrates the process of the EC cycle in detail.

2. Background

 Heating, venting, and air conditioning systems (HVAC) account for nearly 30% of the total
energy use in the United States, some 14 Quads of energy per year (14x1015 BTU’s/year) with an
attendant 3x108 tons of annual carbon emissions. Concomitantly, there is explosive growth for
HVAC systems in emerging markets https://www.renewaire.com/wp-
content/uploads/2018/09/White-Paper_HVAC-Trends_Final2.pdf with a total global energy
consumption expected for HVAC systems of approximately $207B/yr by 2022. The sale and
installation of vapor compression (VC) based cooling systems outranks all other modes of cooling
due to low cost, dependability, and efficiency. However, the continued proliferation of traditional
VC systems raises two concerns: (1) The prospect of a significant rise in the release of refrigerants
with high global warming potential (GWP) either through faulty assembly or when servicing. (2)
A further increase in worldwide energy consumption as HVAC systems become more widespread.
Overall VC efficiencies appear not to have a path toward stepwise improvements unless heat
exchanger size is increased dramatically. In addition; noise, vibration, weight, lack of modularity,
and limited portability act as drivers for seeking alternative forms to VP refrigeration and cooling.
 Thermoelectric cooling is the most pervasive Not In Kind (NIK) cooling technology,
finding applications as diverse in use as portable refrigeration to luxury seat cooling.
Unfortunately, the application of thermoelectric based cooling to widespread aerospace,
automotive, residential, or commercial building HVAC systems has not been economically or
technically viable because the electrical-to-thermal conversion efficiencies at temperatures 10-
15C above ambient for even the very best thermoelectric materials is only ~5%.
 In answer to this need, electrocaloric based cooling has rapidly emerged as a potential form
of NIK solid state refrigeration that might credibly offer an alternative to vapor compression based
HVAC. Electrocaloric based regenerative cooling, exploits the class of caloric materials which
undergo phase transitions between an electrically centrosymmetric phase having no inherent
electric dipole moment, to one in which a spontaneous electric polarization occurs as a result of
reducing the temperature below a critical, or “Curie” temperature.
 Ceramics or single crystals, such as lead magnesium niobate system (PMN) and
(Pb,Nb)(Zr,Sn,Ti)O3, have displayed large electrocaloric temperature lift. However, these
 7

inorganic materials contain a large amount of lead, which is increasingly disfavored for use in
technological systems due to more stringent environmental directives.4 Among all of the EC
materials that are promising working bodies for caloric-based technologies,7 polyvinylidene
homopolymers, copolymers, and terpolymers have become well-developed in this field due to their
large temperature cooling per volt and their scalability in size and shape.5
 Relaxor ferroelectric polymers like poly(vinylidene fluoride-trifluoroethylene-
chlorofluoroethylene) (PVDF-TrFE-CFE) have been recognized as one of the most promising
candidate materials for electrocaloric cooling due to the polymer’s large temperature change (∆T)
that occurs near room temperature with high thermal stability.8–10 In addition, the degree of
crystallinity of the polymer is also correlated with the EC effect, which has referred to the entropy
changes from the ordering and disordering of the dipoles in the polymers under an electric field.11
Thermal annealing of a PVDF-TrFE-CFE terpolymer has been shown to alter the degree of
crystallinity12 and crystal size,11,12 and recently it was found that the EC effect increased as the
polymer crystal size decreased13 and degree of crystallinity increased.14,15 The decrease in the
crystal size can lower the energy barrier for the transition between ordering and disordering and
the increase in the crystallinity can encourage the entropy changes to enhance the ∆T according to

 One disadvantage of current polymeric EC materials is that the bulk materials have a large
electrode spacing and require large electric fields, which challenges the safe operation of
electrocaloric devices. Another option for the design of EC materials in electrocaloric devices is
multilayer chips (MLCs). However, the structure of MLCs are not well-developed for EC devices,
and it is not easily amenable to manufacturing.1 Thus, thin films may be a solution to avoid the
need to apply large electric fields during the EC cycle, and they may be easier to manufacture at a
commercial scale for practical applications. In addition, the thin film possesses a fast heat
conduction, and the EC effect can be triggered easily at a lower applied field.1,16,17 Moreover,
polymer thin films can withstand extremely high fields and produce higher electrocaloric
temperature change,18 enhance film breakdown strength, and reduce film defects.19 This further
encourages the thin film fabrication for EC materials.
 To date, most EC experiments have focused on polymer thin film prepared by solution
casting. Traditional solution casting the polymer requires extended periods of time (e.g., hours to
days) to evaporate solvent and is therefore not ideal for commercial manufacturing, but this can
be adapted to be scalable (e.g., blade casting and bar casting) in industries. Other research has
demonstrated that alternative processing methods, including electrospining13 and spin-casting8,
can produce PVDF-TrFE-CFE films with a large EC effect. While spin casting does generate thin
films, it is not a scalable and continuous manufacturing process. As the demand for EC film is
increasing and there is a high potential for EC thin film use in practical applications.20 A high

 8

throughput thin film fabrication method with high quality and scalability is needed for the
manufacturing of EC coolers.

Solid-state electrocaloric cooling requires thin films to have a uniform thickness, a smooth surface,
and to be free of defects.T his is critical for avoiding short circuiting and dialectric breakdown
during operation. The surface morphology of dry terpolymer films prepared by solution casting
(SC) and electrospraying (ES) were analyzed using SEM (Figure 2). The ES films (Figure 2a)
were found to appear more uniform and smoother than the SC films (Figure 2b). The layer-by-
layer deposition and rapid solvent evaporation that occurs during electrospray processing are
thought to lead to the smoother and more uniform film appearance for ES films. “Crater” features
and rougher textures observed on the SC surface may be due to a slower solvent evaporation rate
that can cause drying defects,21–23 while the layer-by-layer deposition from ES films could limit
the amount of solvent with a rapid evaporation rate on each layer. The film preparation method
also affects the thickness consistency of the thin films. Using terpolymer films (1 inch x 1 inch)
prepared by ES and SC, film thickness was measured every 0.25 inches using FIB. The ES film
(Figure 2c) was found to display a thickness range of 400 nm, while the SC film displayed a
thickness range of 1600 nm for a film of the same size (Figure 2d). Electrosprayed films (Figure
2e) were smoother than solution casted films (Figure 2f) as measured using atomic force
microscopy (AFM). The root mean square (RMS) roughness from a film with area of 20 µm x 20
µm was 15.4  1.7 nm for ES and 135.7  19.4 nm for SC. In addition to increased uniformity,
fabricating films by electrospraying is faster than solution casting. For example, to generate a thin
film with a thickness of 5 µm, DMF takes about 24h to evaporate completely when solution casted,
whereas electrospraying takes less than an hour to create the terpolymer thin film. Therefore,
electrospray processing, as a faster thin film fabrication method, is a promising method for the
generation of electrocaloric thin films. Electrospray has been proven to be a successful method to

fabricate polymeric films with uniform thicknesses and void-free surfaces, and this method offers
the potential for scale-up to generate large film sizes.24,25

Characterization of thermal properties and crystal size of terpolymer films

Thermal analysis of the terpolymer films was conducted using differential scanning calorimetry
(DSC). The glass transition of the non-annealed samples was observed around -27.7˚C in the first

 10

heating trace and is consistent with the literature26 (Figure 3a). An additional endothermic peak
for the non-annealed sample was found at 40˚C, which is attributed to the ferroelectric phase
transition.27 From the first heating traces (Figure 3a,b) of the non-annealed films, a broad peak is
shown from -10˚C to 30˚C for the ES film (Figure 3a) and from -10˚C to 40˚C for the SC film
(Figure 3b). This temperature range is known for the Curie transition of PVDF-TrFE-CFE.28 The
decrease in the Curie temperature for the ES film suggests that less energy will be needed to orient
the alignment of electric dipoles in the ES film than in the SC film. The melting transition for both
ES and SC non-annealed films was found near 130˚C. However, the latent heat release of ES film
was 19.2 J/g, which is about 36% higher than the SC film (14.4 J/g).

 X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to characterize different phases in the films (Figure

Measurement of dielectric properties

The electrocaloric material operates by converting electrical energy to thermal energy, in which
the terpolymer thin films are characterized by dielectric constant and loss. For the unannealed
films, a lower degree of crystallinity and less latent heat releases upon melting were found from
the thermal analysis and are consistent with the literature.26 The observation that unannealed
samples show lower latent heats of fusion suggests that entropy changes during this transition will
also be lower and that the observed electrocaloric effect of unannealed samples will be smaller
than is observed for annealed samples. Dielectric measurement of the unannealed PVDF-TrFE-
CFE has been studied before and showed a lower capability of energy storage8 compared with the
films that were annealed.13 For films annealed at 60˚C, the SC film shows a slightly higher
dielectric constant than the ES film (Figure 4a), and the temperature-dependent dielectric loss of
both films has a similar value (Figure 4b). The higher dielectric constant on the SC film annealed
at 60˚C could be contributed to a smaller crystallite size (Table 1) compared with the ES film.
These results suggested that smaller crystallite size will require a lower energy barrier for the
dipole orientation and lead to an enhancement of the dielectric constant.13 For films annealed at
120˚C, the ES film exhibits the maximum dielectric constant of 54 at 30˚C for the experiment,
which is about 26% enhancement compared to the SC film (Figure 4c). In addition, the loss for
the ES film is much lower than the SC film (Figure 4d), where the loss of ES film is 84% lower
than SC film at 30˚C. These outcomes suggest that the film prepared using the ES technique and
annealed at 120 ˚C may have a greater ability to store electrical energy as well as have a lower
energy dissipation during the adiabatic polarization in the EC cycle compared to the films prepared
by solution casting. Because solution casted films are more variable in thickness, dielectric
constant measurements were completed at three different positions on a film and at three
temperatures to investigate measurement reproducibility (Figure S4). The standard deviation for
the dielectric constant was found to increase as temperature increased, however, all of the points
measured were found to follow the same trend with temperature.

The polymer films generated from electrospraying have demonstrated a higher enthalpy change in
melting compared with the solution casting method from the thermal analysis (Figure 3a, b). A
larger enthalpy change for the EC material is desirable in order to obtain giant EC performance
according to the EC theory and the literature data.10,13,15 Compared with the films prepared by
solution casting, the enhancement of the enthalpy change in the ES film may be explained by the
possibility of forming microparticles or nanoparticles when the concentration of the solution varies
between 2% to 10%.38 In this case, the sufficient surface tension of the particles favors the
entanglement of polymer chains, and the particle deposition also increases the potential to produce
chain entanglement.39,40 Substantial movement of polymer chains may be lower in ES films due to
polymer entanglement, which can restricts the mobility of polymer chains to frustrate the free
movement of polymer chains. The increase in difficulty in chain movement may lead to a higher
energy requirement for melting, resulting in a higher melting enthalpy. It is noted that the spray
processing in other studies is different from the electrospray technique. However, a spray process
is well-known to create polymer entanglement38 but the degree of entanglement will not be
discussed in this work. In addition, the rapid evaporation of solvent on the ES film may also limit
the mobility of the terpolymer chain from MPs, so the higher rigidity of the chains might also be
attributed to the enhancement of enthalpy compared with SC films.

It's also noted that the surface morphology of the ES films after annealed 120˚C for 18h was
different than SC films (Figure 6). While the crystallite sizes are similar for both films annealed

at 120˚C (Table 1), the direction of the crystallites appears different, with the aggregate crystallites
on the surface of the ES film (Figure 6a) appearing less randomly aligned than on the SC films
(Figure 6c). An ImageJ program was used to characterize the orientation of the crystallites. Three
locations on the SEM images of ES A120 (Figure 6b) were selected from the aggregates, and the
direction of the crystallites presented on the ES films were 72˚  9˚, 103˚  7˚, and 70˚  6˚.
However, the direction of the crystallites presented on SC A120 were 36˚  19˚, 156˚  11˚, and
35˚  11˚ based on the selected locations in Figure 6d. The ES films annealed at 120˚C tend to
have the crystallites aggregated and the direction analysis contains showed less variation (i.e. a
lower standard deviation in the direction measurement) that implies a more uniform directionality
in the aggregate than crystallites observed on the surface of SC films that were annealed at 120˚C.
In other words, the different groupings of crystal aggregates do not appear to have a preferred
direction, as evidenced by the different initial number, but the standard deviation in the
measurements does appear to be smaller in the ES films than in the SC films (Figure 6e and 6f)

Electrospray processing, a thin film fabrication method, was used for the first time to successfully
generate EC thin film materials. Results demonstrated that ES processing can be used to fabricate
terpolymer thin films with a more consistent thickness, uniform, and smoother surface than
solution-casted films. ES films a higher crystallinity compared with the SC films and applying a
higher annealing temperature can further enhance the crystallinity of the thin film, giving the
opportunity to raise the entropy change in the terpolymer thin film. When films are annealed at
60˚C, SC films appear to have a greater ability to store and recover electrical energy due to the
smaller crystallite size compared to the ES film. However, when the films are annealed at 120˚C,
ES film displayed larger dielectric responses and lower energy loss compared to the SC film. It
was also observed that the ES film annealed at 120˚C may also result in a more-ordered crystallite
and the alignment of crystallite may contribute to the enhancement of dielectric constant, although
more experiments are needed to determine if this alignment persists through the thickness of the
film. The EC temperature lifted for ES A120 using an IR camera showed a ∆TECE was 3.6˚C and
the EC strength ∆TECE/E of this film was 0.036˚C MV m-1 measured at 100 MV m-1 at room
temperature. These results appear to be similar to, or potentially exceeding, the existing literature
values for thin film electrocalorics measured at or near room temperature, which is an exciting
finding for a new fabrication method in this application space. While films prepared by ES
processing required less time to manufacture than solution casting in this work, it is noted that
advanced solution casting techniques were not employed. When considering manufacturability,
both electrospray processing and solution casting are more scalable than spin coating, but
electrospray processing can generate thinner films than solution casting and spin coating. This
study encourages further development of electrospray processing as a new method of fabricating
EC material thin films with high quality, which we envision to have the potential to meet
manufacturing demands in the practical application in the future.

 4. Key Achievements

 • Demonstration of new use case for emergent manufacturing process:
 Electrospray processing has been used to manufacture nanomaterials and to
 facilitate separations of macromolecules in mass spectrometry, but it is relatively
 new to polymer processing. This work establishes a new use case for electrospray-
 based manufacturing.
 • Successful synthesis of thin electrocaloric films: Electrospray processing of
 electroactive materials was demonstrated to create films with electrocaloric
 properties. These films were some of the thinnest ever derived for electrocaloric
 purposes.
 • Evidence of manufacturing-driven benefits: We showed that electrospray likely
 creates microscopic domains of crystallinity or order that may contribute to

 25

 enhance temperature lift during electrocaloric cycling. These benefits were
 demonstrated over traditional casting techniques.
 • Remarkable diaelectric breakdown strength: Working with exceptionally thin
 films increases the likelihood of defects or imperfections that can lead to dialectric
 breakdown. Our films exhibited remarkable strength and resiliency under high
 electric field conditions.
 • Challenges with LC blends: Blending LCs with the PVDF terpolymer proved
 difficult due to phase separation and lack of film stability.
 • LC Blends showed no improvement in performance: We determined that the
 LC blends may not be working because the activation temperature (i.e. the
 temperature at which the LCs will exhibit their electrocaloric behavior) is higher
 than the PVDF terpolymer. Our testing was done at room temperature while the
 LCs generally activate at higher temperatures. We were happy to see that the
 addition of the LCs did not reduce the temperature lift, more tests at higher
 temperature would be useful. We did not conduct these tests because we ran out of
 time and funds on the project.
 • Successful Dissemination of results: This work resulted in one refereed
 publication in Cell Reports Physical Science (IF 7.3) and 6 conference
 presentations (Poster and oral)

5. Benefits Assessment

 With the relatively small project, we did not conduct a full analysis of the benefits
 of this technology relative to existing HVAC-type systems. When the project started, our
 partner (RTX, then UTC) still had technology offerings in the HVAC space. Once they
 spun off Carrier, their interest in that business sector waned. While their interest remained
 in the technology at large, we never conducted a quantifiable benefits analysis.
 It is widely accepted that using electrocaloric cooling confers benefits over vapor-
 compression cooling (efficiency, lack of refrigerants, low noise, potential to miniaturize,
 reduced maintenance), many of these benefits are still hypothetical due to the challenges
 that continue to plague the space. High electric fields require power electronics that are
 expensive. Electrical breakdown and degradation under high electric fields is a challenge.
 Even with the higher efficiency, temperature lifts are still low compared to vapor
 compression systems and overall heat removal capacity, particularly for thin films, can be
 limited. A more expansive study with a techno-economic assessment is recommended to
 explore these tradeoffs more thoroughly.

6. Lessons Learned

 • Experimental Design: The blending of two electroactive materials requires
 careful experimental planning to ensure that we can test the properties of each
 26

 material individually. We believe that our composite materials may have binodal
 electrocaloric performance (the materials may exhibit cooling at multiple
 temperatures) because the LCs will cool at higher temperature than the PVDF
 terpolymer matrix. Such a material might have unique applications. We would still
 like to explore this.
 • Corporate engagement: Our collaboration with RTRC faltered after the first year.
 Their effort required too many resources given the overall project size. We should
 have kept the work largely in-house and worked with RTRC as a consulting partner
 for setting up our own equipment. At the time we did not have the confidence of
 being able to set up our own test rig. However, we found that we could build our
 own test system and even integrated a thermal camera into the system.

7. Conclusions

  We have demonstrated the use of electrospray processing to make thin film electrocaloric
  devices with an electroactive PVDF terpolymer. Unlike casting, electrospray processing
  enables the exquisite control of thickness in morphology in ways that benefit electrocaloric
  performance. These thin films exhibit remarkable dielectric breakdown strength which is
  critical for activating the material as a solid-state cooling device. We were particularly
  excited to see that thin film produced by electrospray processing exhibited better
  electrocaloric performance than films produced by casting. We attributed this to a certain
  level of order imposed by the electrosprayed film.

  We unfortunately did not see remarkable improvements in electrocaloric performance of our
  films with the incorporation of LCs. We attributed this to the higher activation temperature
  of the LCs that we used. We believe there are opportunities to explore other LCs or these
  LC containing films at higher temperatures.

  In all, we believe that this work has advanced the processing of electrocaloric materials which
  is, potentially a field that is underserved by this community. With most of the research
  focused on identifying new materials with exceptional electroactive properties, few seemed
  to be addressing manufacturing of the materials into functional systems. I hope that our
  work will spur some of this interest in electrocaloric material manufacturing.

  We also note that that the PI has launched a company (membraneX) commercializing
  electrospray technology for manufacturing thin films. While membrane applications are the
  likely first beachhead market for the company, the company is agnostic to the final
  application of electrospray. We would welcome an opportunity to consider
  commercialization of this technology through membraneX, though we would need an HVAC

 27

 partner. We have engaged with Trane on other discussions (after the conclusion of this
 project), and they might be a future partner.

 8. Future Work/Recommendations

 I would suggest future analysis of potential bimodal operation of our electrocaloric thin films
 containing LCs. I also believe there could be value in exploring other LCs with electrocaloric
 behavior and possibly tethering these LC covalently to electrocaloric polymers. This would
 prevent the loss of LC by leaching during operation. Such a synthesis process would require
 a longer-term effort. We also believe that we could apply these films to a heat exchange surface
 and demonstrate cooling on a larger scale.
