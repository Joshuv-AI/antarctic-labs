Mitochondrial transfer from human embryonic stem cell-derived mature cardiomyocytes to mesenchymal stem cells
Authors: Ye Seul Kim, Dasol Kim, Yoon Ji Jung, Jung Won Yoon, Yong Seong Kwon, Youngmi Kim Pak, Seong-Jang Kim, Jae Ho Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondria are crucial for energy metabolism and their dysfunction is implicated in the development of various human diseases. Direct mitochondrial transplantation has shown potential in reversing mitochondrial dysfunction in recipient cells. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) present a promising approach as donor cells for such transplantation. We have previously demonstrated that tomatidine, a natural steroidal alkaloid, promotes the differentiation of human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) into mature cardiomyocytes by enhancing mitochondrial quantity and function. In this study, we assessed the capacity of hESC-derived cardiomyocytes (hESC-CMs) and MSCs as donor cells for mitochondrial transplantation. Mitochondria were extracted from MSCs, immature hESC-CMs, and tomatidine-treated mature hESC-CMs. Treating MSCs with mitochondria derived from mature hESC-CMs led to a marked increase in mitochondrial protein levels, such as COX IV and MIC60, in the recipient MSCs, in comparison to those receiving mitochondria from immature hESC-CMs or MSCs. Transplantation of mature hESC-CM-derived mitochondria significantly enhanced the proliferation of recipient MSCs. These findings indicate that mature hESC-CMs are highly effective as donor cells for mitochondrial transplantation in addressing mitochondrial dysfunction.Keywords: Cardiomyocytes, Differentiation, Embryonic stem cells, Mesenchymal stem cells, Mitochondrial transfer

INTRODUCTION

Mitochondria are potent and dynamic organelles essential for converting nutrients into energy. To support cellular activity, mitochondria produce the majority of cellular energy, adenosine triphosphate (ATP), via oxidative phosphorylation (1). In addition to aerobic energy production, mitochondria are crucial in the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), maintaining calcium homeostasis, cellular signaling, and synthesizing important metabolites such as fatty acids, amino acids, pyrimidines, and steroid hormones (2). Consequently, mitochondrial dysfunction is linked with a variety of diseases, including metabolic disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, cancer, and aging (3). Mutations in mitochondrial DNA or in nuclear genes that encode mitochondrial proteins result in acquired or inherited mitochondrial diseases. These mutations inhibit mitochondrial biosynthesis and cause uncontrolled production of ROS, damaging lipids, proteins, and DNA (4). Consequently, damaged or dysfunctional mitochondria must be restored for the effective treatment of mitochondrial diseases.

Mitochondrial transfer from healthy donor cells to damaged recipient cells has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for mitochondrial dysfunction (5). This transfer restores aerobic respiration in cells with dysfunctional mitochondria, and protects them from damage and apoptosis (6). Additionally, mitochondrial transfer has been identified as a mechanism of mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-mediated cell therapy (7, 8). MSCs transfer functional mitochondria to recipient cells via tunneling nanotubes, extracellular vesicles, and cellular fusion (9, 10). With their high proliferative properties in vitro, MSCs are optimal donors for mitochondrial isolation and transfer (10). The in vivo transfer of mitochondria from bone marrow-derived MSCs to pulmonary alveoli has been shown to protect against acute lung injury (11). Furthermore, intracoronary injection of freshly isolated mitochondria from skeletal muscle reduces infarct size and enhances cardiac function following experimental ischemia-reperfusion injury (12), suggesting that transplantation of isolated mitochondria is beneficial for treating mitochondrial dysfunction. However, due to the immunogenic complications associated with using isolated mitochondria, healthy allogeneic mitochondrial cells, such as MSCs, can be transferred into the stem cells of MSCs before autologous stem cell transplantation.

Among the various cell types in the human body, cardiomyocytes (CMs) are rich in mitochondria due to their high energy demands. The transition of the fetal heart to the adult phenotype involves an increase in mitochondrial content via mitochondrial biogenesis, with mitochondria occupying 30% of the area of CMs (13). Consequently, CMs may serve as suitable donors for mitochondrial transplantation. Both human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells have the capacity to differentiate into CMs (14). However, CM-derived from stem cells often display the less mature phenotypes of embryonic or fetal CMs, including aspects of mitochondrial structure and function (15). Recently, we reported that tomatidine, a natural steroidal alkaloid, promotes the maturation of hESC-derived CMs (hESC-CMs) (16). Tomatidine-treated hESC-CMs (Toma-CMs) demonstrated an increase in both the number and size of mitochondria, accompanied by enhanced formation of mitochondrial lamellar cristae, indicative of maturation in hESC-CMs. While mature hESC-CMs are an effective source for mitochondrial transplantation, the extraction of mitochondria from mature hESC-CMs for this purpose has yet to be explored.

In this study, we aimed to investigate whether hESC-CMs are feasible as donor cells for mitochondrial transplantation. We isolated mitochondria from MSCs, immature hESC-CMs (control CMs), and Toma-CMs, and transplanted them into naïve MSCs to assess the impact of mitochondrial transplantation on the expression of mitochondrial proteins, mitochondrial membrane potentials, generation of free radicals, and cellular metabolic activity in the recipient MSCs.

RESULTS

Characterization of mitochondria in MSCs, immature, and mature hESC-CMs

To explore the utility of Toma-CMs as donor cells for mitochondrial transplantation, we assessed the structure and function of mitochondria in Toma-CMs, control CMs, and MSCs. H9 hESCs were differentiated into hESC-CM and subsequently matured using tomatidine. Transmission electron microscopy was employed to examine the mitochondrial structure in MSCs, control CMs, and Toma-CMs. Tomatidine treatment not only increased mitochondrial number but also enhanced mitochondrial cristae formation in hESC-CMs (Fig. 1A). In addition, the ratio of mitochondrial DNA to nuclear DNA was significantly elevated in Toma-CMs compared to control CMs (Fig. 1B). Mitochondrial area, perimeter, and circularity were also greater in Toma-CMs than in control CMs or MSCs (Fig. 1C). To evaluate mitochondrial ROS and membrane potential, cells were stained with MitoSOX and TMRM. Toma-CMs demonstrated higher levels of mitochondrial ROS and increased membrane potential than those in MSCs or control CMs (Fig. 1D). Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis validated the rise in mitochondrial ROS and membrane potential in Toma-CMs (Fig. 1E). Moreover, the mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate was higher in Toma-CMs than in control CMs and MSCs (Fig. 1F). These findings indicate that tomatidine treatment enhances mitochondrial quantity and functionality in hESC-CMs.

Isolation of mitochondria from MSCs and hESC-CMs

To isolate mitochondria from hESC-CMs and MSCs, cells were lysed and mitochondria were purified using differential centrifugation as outlined in Materials and Methods. Western blot analysis revealed that the expression level of cardiac troponin-T (cTnT), a cardiomyocyte specific protein, in the cell lysate of Toma-CMs exceeded that in control CMs and was minimally detectable in the isolated mitochondrial fractions from Toma-CMs and control CMs (Fig. 2A). Additionally, the expression levels of mitochondria-specific markers such as mitofilin (MIC60), cytochrome C oxidase subunit 4 (COX IV), and TOM20 were higher in the mitochondrial fractions compared to the cell lysates of MSCs, control CMs, and Toma-CMs (Fig. 2A). The expression of nuclear marker (lamin B1), endoplasmic marker (calnexin), and cytoplasmic markers (FAK and GAPDH) were minimally present in the mitochondrial fractions, suggesting a high enrichment of mitochondria. Consistently, the protein levels in the mitochondrial fractions derived from Toma-CMs surpassed those from MSCs and control CMs (Fig. 2B). To examine the metabolic activity, ATP levels were measured in the mitochondrial fractions, revealing that Toma- CMs had elevated ATP levels compared to those from control CMs and MSCs (Fig. 2C). These results suggest that Toma-CMs are effective donor cells for mitochondrial transplantation.

Transplantation of isolated mitochondria to recipient MSCs

To track mitochondrial transplantation, mitochondria were pre-labeled with MitoTracker Green FM (MTG) in donor MSCs. An MTG-positive signal was subsequently detected in the mitochondrial fraction (Fig. 3A), indicating that the MTG-positive fluorescence of mitochondria was retained after isolation. To investigate whether mitochondria isolated from donor MSCs could transfer to recipient cells, the endogenous mitochondria of the recipient cells were labeled with TMRM and then incubated with MTG-labeled donor mitochondria for one day. After a 24-hour incubation period, the MTG-positive exogenous mitochondria (green color) co-localized with the TMRM-positive endogenous mitochondria (red color) of the recipient MSCs (Fig. 3B), demonstrating the successful transfer of mitochondria from donor to recipient cells. To further verify this co-localization, z-stack confocal images of recipient MSCs transplanted with MTG-positive donor mitochondria were analyzed (Fig. 3C), revealing a high correlation between MTG-positive and TMRM-positive fluorescence signals in recipient cells, suggestive of co-localization between the transplanted exogenous and endogenous mitochondria.

To compare the efficiencies of mitochondrial transplantation among mitochondria isolated from MSCs, Toma-CMs, and control CMs, recipient MSCs were exposed to mitochondrial fractions derived from these three cell types. Mitochondria isolated from all three types were pre-stained with MTG (green) and subsequently treated with these MTG-labeled exogenous mitochondria transferred into TMRM-pre-stained recipient MSCs for 24 h. All MTG-labeled mitochondria from the three donor cell lines co-localized with the endogenous TMRM-positive mitochondria in the recipient MSCs (Fig. 3D). FACS analysis illustrated that the proportion of MTG＋TMRM＋ mitochondria in recipient MSCs transplanted with mitochondria from Toma-CMs was significantly higher than in those transplanted with mitochondria from MSCs or control CMs (Fig. 3E), suggesting that Toma-CMs serve as optimal donor cells for mitochondrial transplantation.

Effects of mitochondrial transplantation on mitochondrial function in MSCs

Next, we investigated the effects of mitochondrial transplantation on the expression of mitochondrial genes and functions, including ROS production, mitochondrial membrane potential, and metabolic activity, in recipient MSCs. To assess the impact on mitochondrial proteins, we measured the expression levels of MIC60 and COX IV, which are associated with mitochondria, in recipient MSCs at 24 h and 48 h post-transplantation using western blotting. The expression levels of MIC60 and COX IV increased following the transplantation of mitochondria derived from MSCs and hESC-CMs (Fig. 4A). Particularly, mitochondria isolated from Toma-CMs markedly enhanced the expression of MIC60 and COX IV in recipient MSCs, compared with those isolated from MSCs or control CMs.

To evaluate whether mitochondrial transplantation affects mitochondrial function in recipient MSCs, we analyzed the levels of mitochondrial ROS and membrane potential using mitoSOX and TMRM staining at 24 h post-transplantation. Transplantation with MSC-derived mitochondria elevated mitoSOX-positive ROS levels from 0% to 12.2%, whereas mitochondria from control CMs and Toma-CMs increased mitoSOX-positive signals to 47.5% and 90.1%, respectively (Fig. 4B, upper panels). Additionally, TMRM-positive mitochondrial membrane potential was enhanced by transplantation with mitochondrial fractions derived from MSCs, control CMs, and Toma-CMs (Fig. 4B, lower panels). Consistent with the mitoSOX-positive signals, the highest TMRM activity was observed in MSCs treated with mitochondria isolated from Toma-CMs (Fig. 4B). We also assessed the effects of mitochondrial transplantation on the cell number and viability of MSCs using the WST-8 assay. Treating MSCs with mitochondria from all three cell types significantly increased both cell number and viability, with the most pronounced effects observed following the transplantation of mitochondria isolated from Toma-CMs (Fig. 4C and D). These results imply that the transplantation of mitochondria isolated from Toma-CMs enhances mitochondrial function and metabolic activity in recipient cells.

DISCUSSION

Mitochondrial transplantation is an innovative approach for treating mitochondrial dysfunction and diseases (5, 6). Recipient cells primarily engulf exogenous mitochondria through macropinocytosis (17). Various techniques for mitochondrial transplantation, such as simple incubation and mechanical delivery, have been developed (18). It has been reported that low-speed centrifugation (19) and conjugation of mitochondria with cell-penetrating peptides (20) can also facilitate mitochondrial transfer into recipient cells. This study reveals that simple incubation adequately induced endocytosis of isolated mitochondria into recipient MSCs in vitro. Additionally, exogenously transplanted mitochondria co-localized with the endogenous mitochondria of recipient MSCs, suggesting successful integration. To mitigate any structural and functional changes in isolated mitochondria, we minimized the duration of mitochondrial isolation and transplantation. Further investigations, potentially involving chemical modifications or mechanical stimuli, may enhance mitochondrial transfer efficiency.

Various cell types, including MSCs, adipocytes, and astrocytes, have been utilized as donor sources for mitochondrial transplantation (11, 21, 22). Autologous transplantation of mitochondria from bone marrow MSCs into recipient MSCs from the same batch enhances proliferation, migration, and osteogenic differentiation by stimulating oxidative phosphorylation activity and ATP production in the recipient cells (23). Importantly, MSCs maintain their mitochondria in a dormant state and primarily rely on glycolysis due to their low energy requirement (7). During the differentiation process of stem cells, the cellular metabolism switches from glycolysis to predominantly oxidative phosphorylation, evidenced by enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis and transformation of mitochondrial morphology from fragmented to elongated forms to meet increased energy demands (24). Thus, mitochondria-rich cells, such as skeletal muscles and CMs, may serve as viable donor cells for mitochondrial transplantation. Transplantation of mitochondria extracted from mouse skeletal muscle significantly rejuvenates bioenergetically compromised skin fibroblasts carrying various mitochondrial DNA mutations (25). Moreover, mitochondria derived from human fetal CMs improve the therapeutic efficacy of human MSCs in a preclinical model of ischemic heart disease (26). This study demonstrated that mitochondria sourced from hESC-CMs are more effective for mitochondrial transplantation than those from MSCs. Moreover, treatment with tomatidine induced differentiation of hESCs into mature CMs, which contain abundant mitochondria exhibiting enhanced metabolic function. Furthermore, mitochondria from Toma-CMs stimulated mitochondrial function in recipient MSCs more effectively than those from control CMs. Mitochondria from Toma-CMs not only demonstrate superior functionality but also provide practical advantages. Tomatidine promotes mitochondrial biogenesis without requiring genetic manipulation, facilitating efficient mitochondrial production in CMs. Unlike MSCs, Toma-CMs can be derived from a single ESC colony under standardized conditions, ensuring a consistent mitochondrial yield. This uniformity minimizes donor variability and decreases the number of source cells required, supporting cost-effective and reproducible mitochondrial production for therapeutic applications. Taken together, these results suggest that Toma-CMs are highly effective donor cells for mitochondrial transplantation.

Under healthy physiological conditions, maintaining a proper balance between mitochondrial ROS and ATP production is crucial for cellular energy synthesis. Under pathological conditions, mitochondria-derived ROS leads to oxidative damage of the enzymes in the inner mitochondrial membrane (27). The transplantation of platelet-derived mitochondria enhances cell proliferation and migration by lowering ROS levels in dermal fibroblasts (28). In contrast, transplantation of macrophage-derived mitochondria has been found to promote ROS accumulation, activating ERK signaling and cell proliferation in recipient cells (29). In this study, we demonstrated that transplantation of mitochondrial fractions derived from MSCs and hESC-CMs increased mitochondrial protein expression, mitochondrial ROS production, mitochondrial membrane potential activity, and cell proliferation in recipient MSCs. Transplantation of mitochondria isolated from MSCs enhances mitochondrial biogenesis and improves respiration and cellular functions of recipient endothelial cells by inducing mitophagy (30). These findings imply that mitochondrial transfer mediates mitochondrial function by modulating intracellular ROS levels and mitophagy in recipient cells. Transplantation of mitochondria isolated from Toma-CMs significantly increased the levels of MIC60 and COX IV in recipient MSCs compared to those in control CMs, indicating that Toma-CMs are superior donors for mitochondrial transfer. To ascertain whether transplantation of mitochondria derived from Toma-CMs can enhance mitochondrial function, it is essential to measure mitochondrial activity in recipient cells post-transplantation.

In summary, this study is the first to demonstrate that mitochondria isolated from hESC-CMs can be utilized for mitochondrial transplantation. Additionally, tomatidine treatment not only induced phenotypic maturation of hESC-CMs but also enhanced the number and functionality of their mitochondria. The mitochondrial fraction from Toma-CMs contained a higher amount of mitochondrial proteins compared to that from control CMs. Consequently, mitochondria derived from Toma-CMs may serve as an effective therapeutic tool for enhancing the regenerative capabilities of MSCs.

Culture of mesenchymal stem cells

MSCs derived from human tonsil tissue were isolated from a patient with chronic tonsillitis undergoing tonsillectomy, as previously described (31). The ethical approval for studies involving human tonsils was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Pusan National University Hospital (1801-033-062). MSCs were subjected to a five-day culture in α-minimum essential medium (α-MEM) (#12000-022, Thermo Fisher Scientific) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% Pen-Strep (#15140-122, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37°C in an atmosphere of 5% CO2 and 95% humidity. The culture media were refreshed every two days, and upon attaining 80-90% confluence, the cells were harvested using trypsin-EDTA solution (#LS-015-02, Welgene) for reseeding at a 1:5 split ratio.

Differentiation of human ESCs to CMs

H9 hESCs were purchased from WiCell Research Institute (Madison, WI), and the study was approved by the public Institutional Review Board (P01-201410-ES-02). The hESCs were maintained on Matrigel-coated culture dishes in mTeSR1, with subculturing every five days. For cardiomyocyte induction, hESCs were initially exposed to RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with B27 minus insulin and 12 μM CHIR99021, a specific GSK3 inhibitor and promoter of Wnt-signaling, for 24 hours. This was followed by maintenance in a differentiation medium combining RPMI/B27 minus insulin with 5 μM IWR-1 endo and 50 μg/ml L-ascorbic acid for 48 hours. On day 3, the cells received additional treatment with RPMI containing 5 μM IWR-1 endo and B27 minus insulin. From days 6 to 10, differentiation was continued with RPMI/B27 supplement medium. In the final stage of differentiation, the cells were cultured in glucose-free DMEM (#11879-020, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 4 mM L-lactic acid, 500 μg/ml human albumin, and 213 μg/ml L-ascorbic acid for 4 days. To enhance CM maturation, cells were exposed to 1 μM tomatidine (Sigma) from day 6 of the differentiation process, as previously reported (16).

Mitochondrial labeling

To visualize mitochondria, cells were labeled with either MTG FM or tetramethylrhodamine (TMRM) according to the manufacturer’s protocol 24 hours before isolation and delivery of mitochondria. Briefly, cells were incubated in growth medium containing 400 nM MTG FM or 50 nM TMRM for 30 minutes at 37°C in 5% CO2. After staining, cells were washed three times with HBSS and resuspended in fresh, pre-warmed medium. Cells were either harvested or plated as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Mitochondrial transfer

Mitochondria were isolated from MTG-labeled MSCs and CMs using differential centrifugation after cell lysis. Briefly, donor cells (5 × 106 cells) were harvested and homogenized in 1 ml of mitochondrial isolation buffer (0.25 M sucrose, 0.025 M Tris, and 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.4). The homogenate was first centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 minutes, then the supernatant was further centrifuged at 9000 × g for 10 minutes. The pellet was resuspended in mitochondrial isolation buffer and centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 minutes. The mitochondrial pellet was resuspended again in mitochondrial isolation buffer, and protein concentration was assessed using the Bio-Rad protein assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA ). Before mitochondrial transfer, recipient MSCs (1 × 106 cells) were seeded onto cell culture dishes coated with 0.1% gelatin gel and prelabeled with TMRM. After staining, the recipient cells were washed three times with HBSS, incubated in fresh α-MEM, treated with the isolated mitochondrial fractions. After incubation of the recipient cells at 37°C for 24 h, the fluorescent images of the recipient cells were collected using a confocal microscope (ZEISS LSM700, Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Transmission electron microscopy

Cells were rinsed three times with HBSS, then scraped into 0.15 M cacodylate buffer to collect 5 × 106 cells. Cells were centrifuged, the supernatant discarded, and the pellet was incubated for 4 hours with fixation buffer (2% paraformaldehyde and 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.15M sodium cacodylate buffer, pH 7.4) at 4°C. After fixation, samples were dehydrated, immersed in resin to prepare ultrathin sections, followed by image acquisition using the transmission electron microscopy (TECNAI G2) was performed at the Brain Research Core Facilities of KBRI.

Flow cytometry (FACS) analysis

After labeling cells with MTG FM, TMRM, and MitoSOX Red mitochondrial superoxide probes, they were rinsed with HBSS and dissociated using trypsin-EDTA Solution 1× for 3 minutes, then resuspended in 300 μl of HBSS. MTG fluorescence was detected with a 488 nm laser and a 530/30 nm filter. Fluorescence of TMRM and MitoSOX was detected with the same laser and a 574/26 nm filter. Fluorescence intensity was measured using Attune NxT (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and analyzed with FlowJo (ver 10, Tree Star Inc. Ashland, OR, USA).

Western blot analysis

Cells were harvested after washing with HBSS and then lysed in a lysis buffer (0.1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 1 mM Na3VO4, 10 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl, 25 mM β-glycerol phosphate, 30 mM Sodium pyrophosphate, and 1% Triton X-100, pH 7.4), followed by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C to collect supernatant (cell lysates). Protein concentrations of the supernatant (cell lysates) and mitochondrial fractions were determined by Bio-Rad protein assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Equal amounts (5 μg) of lysates or mitochondrial fractions were loaded onto 8-12% tris-glycine SDS-PAGE for protein separation, transferring to PVDF membranes, and subsequent staining with 0.1% Ponceau S solution (#P3504, Sigma) to verify sample location. Following blocking with 5% nonfat milk for 1 hour, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies in 5% blocking buffer overnight, and secondary antibodies were applied for 2 hours at room temperature. Antibodies were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence Western blotting system (#GE-RPN2106; Amersham Biosciences, Piscataway, NJ, USA). Western blot images were captured using the ImageQuantTM 800 (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) and the band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (Image Processing and Analysis in Java, NIH).

Measurement of cellular respiration (Seahorse XFp)

Mitochondrial respiration was measured using an Agilent Seahorse XFp analyzer and Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (#10310-100, Seahorse Bioscience, MI, USA). The assay was conducted following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cardiomyocytes were seeded onto 0.1% gelatin-coated XFp cell culture plates at a density of 30,000 cells/well, with or without tomatidine, for one day in RPMI/B27 medium including 10 μM Y276362. This was followed by 2 additional days of incubation, with or without tomatidine, in RPMI B27 medium without Y27632. An equal number of MSCs were seeded onto different wells coated with 0.1% gelatin in α-MEM medium for 2 days. The day before the assay, the sensor cartridge was hydrated using XF calibrant at 37°C in a non-CO2 incubator. XF assay medium was prepared by adding 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 4 mM L-glutamine, and 10 mM D-(＋)-galactose; the pH was adjusted to 7.4, and the temperature was set to 37°C. One hour prior to analysis, the cells were washed twice with XF medium, and the medium was replaced with XF assay medium. The cells were then incubated at 37°C in a non-CO2 incubator. Thirty minutes before the analysis, 1 μM Oligomycin, 0.5 μM FCCP, and 1.5 μM each of a mix of Antimycin A and Rotenone were loaded into A, B, and C injection ports of the hydrated sensor cartridge in volumes of 20 μl, 22 μl, and 25 μl, respectively, using a 10× solution from the XFp Mito Stress Test Kit. After calibration, the cell culture plates were inserted into the instrument.

ATP assay

The ATP assay quantifies living mitochondria based on ATP content in isolated mitochondrial fractions. To measure ATP, either 2 × 104 cells or an equivalent amount of isolated mitochondria in mitochondrial isolation buffer were used. Each assay used 100 μl per well dispensed into a 96-well opaque-multi well plate. To establish the background luminescence, control wells contained only mitochondrial isolation buffer. Equal volumes of CellTiter-Glo (#G7570, Promega) reagent were added to each well. The plates and reaction mixtures were brought to room temperature to stabilize the luminescence signal, which was allowed to react for 10 minutes before luminescence was recorded.

Analysis of mtDNA-to-nDNA ratio

To analyze the mtDNA-to-nDNA ratio, cells were washed with HBSS and 500 μl of lysis buffer (10 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA, 0.3 M sodium acetate, 1% SDS, pH 8) was added along with 5 μl of proteinase K (#T4850, Sigma). The lysates were then incubated overnight at 37°C. The following day, 0.2 mg/ml RNase A was added to the lysates for 30 minutes at 37°C to degrade all components except DNA. To extract nuclear DNA (nDNA) and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), 500 μl of PCI solution (phenol, chloroform, and isoamyl alcohol in a 25:24:1 ratio; #P3803, Sigma) was added to the lysates and then centrifuged at 1500 × g for 5 minutes at room temperature. The upper phase was transferred to a new 1.5 ml tube, to which 500 μl of PCI solution was added, and the process was repeated. DNA was precipitated by adding 2.5 volumes of absolute EtOH containing 0.3 M sodium acetate (pH 5.2) and then washed with 70% ethanol before being dried. In the final step of DNA extraction, the DNA was dissolved in 50 μl of nuclease-free water, and its concentration was determined. To measure the ratio of mtDNA to nDNA, qPCR was performed using an ABI7500 (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) and SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (ABS-4309155, Applied Biosystems) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Data were normalized to nDNA expression and analyzed using the ∆ (∆ CT) method.

Measurement of viable cell number and cell viability

Viable cell number was determined using the Trypan Blue exclusion assay. To assess cell viability, MSCs were plated in 96-well culture plates at a density of 1 × 103 cells per well. Mitochondria were then added to each well and incubated for 1 day. Post-mitochondrial transfection, the cells were exposed to 100 μl of growth medium containing diluted WST-8 solution at 37°C and 5% CO2 for a duration of 0.5-4 hours. Absorbance was recorded at 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 600 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (TECAN).

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the mean ± S.D. Statistical significance was evaluated using the Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis test (with Dunn’s post-hoc) for n < 30, and one-way ANOVA (with Holm-Sidak’s post-hoc) for n > 30.
