Opportunities and Risks of Generative AI through the Health Information Journey
Matthew R. DeVerna, Harry Yaojun Yan, Kai-Cheng Yang, Filippo Menczer

Abstract
Artificial intelligence is fundamentally changing how health content is encountered and acted upon across both the information and healthcare ecosystems. AI systems now generate claims, curate information, interpret symptoms, synthesize evidence, and guide decisions, with significant opportunities and risks for the public. Potential benefits include improvements in access, comprehension, and continuity of care. At the same time, AI can introduce inaccurate or manipulative content that is difficult to distinguish from reliable guidance, and encourage automated decisions that affect care with little transparency or recourse. We introduce a four-stage framework to examine how these opportunities and risks unfold as the public moves through the information environment and into formal healthcare.



A Shifting Landscape

One of the authors was recently diagnosed with gallstones during a visit to the emergency room and told to “watch his diet.”
So he googled “diet for gallstones” and got an AI-generated summary that was easy to understand and cited an article from the Mayo Clinic describing a diet that allegedly would get rid of gallstones.
However, the AI summary failed to mention that the Mayo Clinic article actually debunked the remedy as ineffective and risky.
This highlights both the benefits and the risks of AI-generated health information: while the information was easy to digest, it was misleading.
Users might be enticed by an authoritative tone and believe the claim in a summary without checking the original source.
This is just one example of how AI systems are reshaping the way people encounter and engage with health information, which extends beyond search.
At the same time, AI is transforming how patients interact with care providers, navigate treatment decisions, and manage recovery.

Four Stages of AI Exposure

Let’s trace an individual’s health information journey as it moves from the broader information environment into the healthcare system.
We focus on four stages: idle exposure, information seeking, active care, and care management (see Figure
1
for an illustration).
This framework highlights analytically distinct health care settings in which generative AI systems increasingly mediate engagement with health information, providing a lens for assessing benefits and risks.

Idle Exposure
refers to periods when individuals are not actively seeking health information, but are nonetheless exposed to it through routine engagement with digital technologies.
At this stage, generative AI systems shape baseline awareness and understanding of health issues by altering the production, delivery, and reception of health content in the broader information ecosystem.

Information Seeking
begins when individuals intentionally seek out health-related information, such as symptoms, conditions, or care options.
Here, AI-mediated search engines and conversational systems increasingly guide sensemaking by synthesizing information, interpreting relevance, and suggesting possible next steps.

Active Care
marks the transition into the healthcare ecosystem and encompasses information exchanges and decision-making during formal clinical encounters with medical professionals.
In this stage, generative AI systems are embedded within clinical workflows, influencing documentation, communication, and decision support, even as clinicians retain responsibility for medical decisions.

Care Management
captures ongoing health management outside of formal clinical encounters.
During this stage, AI-mediated tools may support medication management, symptom monitoring, and post-care coordination, shaping longer-term engagement with care within and beyond clinical settings.

Stage I: Idle Exposure

Idle exposure to health-related content is poised to change rapidly as AI is integrated into everyday information systems, with the potential for societal opportunities as well as risks.

The most immediate opportunity lies in production.
Generative AI expands who can produce health information, how quickly it can be created, and how precisely it can be tailored.
Institutions, advocacy groups, commercial actors, and individual creators can now generate fluent, multilingual, and audience-specific health content at minimal cost.
For public health agencies and healthcare systems, this capacity enables communication that moves beyond generic advisories toward culturally adapted and linguistically tailored messaging.
Such capabilities may improve reach among historically underserved populations and help overcome language barriers.
For instance, the World Health Organization deployed AI-powered chatbots to provide real-time guidance and counter misinformation during the COVID-19 pandemic
70
; more capable generative systems could extend such efforts considerably in future emergencies.

Generative AI also increasingly mediates which health content people encounter and how they interpret it.
For instance, the AI system Grok, integrated directly into the Twitter/X platform, responds to user queries about posts in public feeds.
Those responses are visible to other users who never asked a question and never sought out health information
55
.
A user scrolling past a post about vaccine safety may come away with a reinforced or revised belief, depending on how the AI system responded.
Such systems can communicate accurate guidance and surface corrections to dangerous misinformation, but they can equally launder false claims with an appearance of polished authority
25
;
80
.
As AI-generated and AI-curated health content proliferates into more digital spaces, it will play an increasingly consequential role in determining which health claims people accept as true.

At the same time, researchers and developers have begun to explore whether LLM-based systems can rerank social media feeds to surface more accurate and pro-social content, given their capacity to interpret meaning and context
72
.
Such tools could take the form of browser extensions that sit on top of commercial platforms, or conversational interfaces built directly into those platforms, allowing users to actively shape what they encounter
95
.
For health information, the implications cut both ways: a user who instructs such a system to prioritize high-quality sources could construct an information environment substantially more accurate than what commercial algorithms provide, while one nudged toward untrustworthy content could accelerate the adoption of fringe health beliefs through confirmation bias
53
.

Even with the best intentions, personalized generation and curation at scale strain the review processes that have traditionally safeguarded health communication.
Public health agencies that once vetted a small set of advisories before release cannot feasibly check the millions of individualized variants AI systems might produce for different audiences, languages, and contexts; similarly, online platforms cannot meaningfully audit the moment-to-moment responses that feed-integrated systems like Grok generate for consumption by a global user base.
Therefore, scalable personalization can produce scalable error: subtle factual inaccuracies, outdated guidance, or miscalibrated recommendations that reach millions without ever being flagged by the editors, clinicians, or moderators traditionally responsible for catching them.

At the same time, generative AI dramatically lowers the cost and effort required to produce and spread content, reducing barriers for malicious actors.
These may include commercial operators promoting unproven remedies and state-sponsored actors with strategic interests in weakening population health, both of whom can flood digital spaces with targeted messaging at minimal cost.
Some social media accounts already consist largely or entirely of AI-generated text, images, or video, giving rise to “AI influencers” whose authoritative-sounding but synthetic health messages reach millions of users

In summary, generative AI systems update existing means of health content production and introduce new ones, leading to substantial quantitative and qualitative shifts of the information environment.
In this evolving landscape—where health authorities coexist with individual creators, AI influencers, and autonomous agents—users may find that health information is more accessible yet increasingly overwhelming.
They may also find it even more difficult to distinguish credible medical expertise from advocacy and automated output.
As a result, the public will need to recalibrate how they evaluate the credibility of health guidance and, ultimately, develop new forms of digital and health literacy to navigate this environment.

Stage II: Information Seeking

When people start to actively seek out health information, they will find that search engines now present “AI Overviews” above ranked results, synthesizing information from the web to directly answer their queries
106
.
Chatbot systems such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Google’s AI mode go even further, replacing ranked links altogether with conversational interfaces
32
.
Rather than retrieving documents for users to read and evaluate, these systems interpret and summarize them, providing users with easy-to-digest, synthesized responses.
Conversational AI tools are thus replacing traditional search engines as the first point of contact when people seek health information
41
.

Unsurprisingly, people use AI interfaces for health information and advice—about one-third of adults as of March 2026, according to a recent poll
59
.
Some people are even using chatbots to self-diagnose
6
.
As AI systems blur the boundary between information summary and care-oriented judgment, they potentially shape symptom interpretation and downstream decision-making

This shift comes with opportunities and risks.

A huge opportunity arises from the capacity of LLM-based systems to synthesize across a far broader range of sources than a person would typically consult, and to do so almost instantly—compressing what might otherwise be hours of reading into a single, accessible response.
Authoritative health content online is frequently written for clinicians, not patients, and physicians tend to rely on jargon that leaves patients confused or reluctant to ask follow-up questions
22
;
36
.
AI systems can translate such complex medical information into plain language, calibrated to the user’s level of understanding.
For the many people who leave clinical encounters without fully understanding their diagnosis or treatment options, AI systems offer something genuinely new: a patient interlocutor that can explain, re-explain, and adjust its language on demand
81
.

The interactive nature of conversational AI introduces benefits that go beyond information access.
For many users, the absence of friction and social pressure makes it possible to ask health questions at any time, anywhere, and with no waiting or embarrassment.
And unlike a hurried appointment at the doctor’s office or ER visit
40
, a well-functioning AI system can deliver responses for users to consume at their preferred pace.

These strengths come with corresponding fragilities.
AI systems can produce fluent, confident statements that are factually wrong
108
;
44
.
Because coherence is a property of the language model rather than a guarantee of accuracy, users may mistake polished language for reliability and forgo verification when it matters most

;
41

In health contexts, such errors can distort symptom interpretation or perceived urgency, especially when a single synthesized answer is presented without uncertainty cues
107
.
The same push toward synthesis can also compress nuance—collapsing qualified, context-dependent guidance into simplified claims that are easier to read but more likely to be misapplied.

AI systems also reflect knowledge cutoffs based on when they are trained
15
.
While much health guidance is relatively stable, recommendations do change; a system unaware of updated clinical guidance may present outdated information with the same confidence it applies to settled knowledge.
Even when AI systems use retrieval or web search, the capacity to draw on many sources simultaneously does not guarantee that reliable medical evidence will be prioritized

The resulting synthesis can obscure disagreement or evidence gaps

Worse, AI systems may not be able to recognize unreliable sources.
They are vulnerable to intentional manipulation through so-called “poisoning,” the creation of websites with false claims that are then used to train AI models

These risks are compounded by inconsistency.
Performance varies across providers, models, and even prompt phrasing, so the quality of AI-mediated health guidance is neither uniform nor predictable
31
.
This variability takes on an equity dimension: cross-linguistic evaluations show that non-English responses are substantially less accurate and less consistent than their English counterparts
43
.
The result is a new form of digital divide in which the populations who stand to benefit most from accessible health guidance (e.g., non-English speakers, those with lower health literacy, and communities with fewer healthcare resources) are precisely those most exposed to its least reliable outputs.
When users act on such inaccurate guidance, the downstream consequences extend well beyond the search interaction itself, including delayed care, inappropriate reassurance or escalation, and false beliefs that persist into clinical encounters
106
.

In sum, a major shift in active health information seeking is driven by the integration of AI systems.
While these systems streamline access and interpretation, they risk oversimplifying complex issues and obscuring errors, inconsistencies, and system fragilities behind an appearance of interpretive authority.
Because active health information seeking often informs downstream care decisions, this shift from health information retrieval to interpretation and prioritization makes evaluating the quality and effects of AI systems increasingly critical.

Stage III: Active Care

Generative AI is rapidly moving from the periphery of clinical care to its center.
In the United States, healthcare delivery is marked by persistent inefficiencies, including an understaffed workforce that remains heavily burdened despite rising levels of spending

;
71
.
Generative AI tools are well-positioned to target precisely these inefficiencies: they require minimal training to deploy, integrate readily into the mature information technology environments most healthcare organizations already maintain, and can automate or augment a wide range of routine tasks

Economic projections suggest that deploying modern AI systems could reduce healthcare spending by approximately 5–10%, largely by improving operations, administrative functions, and reimbursement processes—an estimated $200–360 billion dollars per year

On the user side, three in ten adults worldwide now use a mobile health app, over 1,200 AI tools have received FDA clearance

This adoption is accelerating fast enough that the JAMA Summit Report on Artificial Intelligence now posits that “every patient-clinician conversation in the US may soon be accompanied by a live interactive AI agent”

Understanding what the integration of generative AI into clinical settings means for patients requires distinguishing between implementations where AI operates at low risk, such as automating administrative tasks at the edges of care, and those where it operates at high risk, mediating or shaping clinical judgment itself.

Low-risk implementations have shown genuine promise

By 2025, 67% of a thousand surveyed physicians reported using AI daily in their practice, with 89% reporting at least weekly use, and 84% reporting that AI makes them better at their jobs

Much of this adoption has focused on reducing administrative and documentation burden.
For example, ambient AI scribes leverage speech recognition and language models to transcribe patient-provider conversations and generate structured clinical notes, producing meaningful reductions in physician workload and burnout

Stage IV: Care Management

Stage III examined AI’s role within formal clinical encounters, where the presence of a clinician provides a clear locus of accountability for AI-mediated decisions.
Stage IV concerns health care management activities that do not involve direct interactions with clinicians.
Accountability is diffused in this stage.
Responsibility shifts to software developers and insurers whose reasoning is often opaque to the patients affected by it.
AI systems are now rapidly expanding into this more ambiguous environment.

AI can operate across a wide range of health care management tasks that differ in their clinical stakes and degree of autonomy.
At the low end, AI can manage the administrative infrastructure of care management: scheduling, billing inquiries, and appointment reminders that carry limited clinical consequence
38

At higher levels of risks, AI can mediate with increasing autonomy how patients understand their own recovery, providing conversational guidance on medication side effects, drug interactions, and post-care

At the highest levels, AI systems can substitute for decisions once reserved for clinicians and insurers: adjusting treatment protocols, shaping medication adherence, and determining coverage, often with limited human review

At the level of individual self-management, patients are making consequential decisions based on AI guidance without clinical review.

At the institutional level, AI systems can make high-stakes administrative determinations with even less transparency.

Machine learning further compounds the opacity of these determinations: even developers may not be able to explain why a particular denial was generated, making accountability difficult to assign even in principle

These risk dynamics are unfolding within a broader policy environment that may accelerate rather than constrain them.

The accountability mechanisms that govern clinical AI, including clinician oversight and liability frameworks, do not follow the patient home.
What remains is a space in which AI systems operate at their highest autonomy and lowest oversight simultaneously, and in which the consequences of error fall most heavily on individuals least equipped to detect or contest them.

Conclusion

Across both the information environment and healthcare system, AI is introducing something categorically new: synthesizing health information and shaping health decisions.
This carries both great promise and substantial risk, with implications that differ depending on where the public encounters it.

In the information ecosystem, AI has the potential to democratize access to health information, reaching people who lack access to professionals or face language and literacy barriers.
But synthetic content and AI influencers blur the line between expertise and imitation, and AI-mediated search returns confident syntheses that may compress nuance, obscure uncertainty, or contain misinform.
These effects are distributed across many systems, entangled with individual behavior, and accumulate quietly across large populations.
The potential harms of AI to public health are therefore difficult to attribute and regulate.

In the healthcare ecosystem, AI has reduced documentation burden, supported clinical communication, and holds promise for extending care to underserved patients.
But as its reach extends into decisions about treatment, coverage, and care management, pressure to reduce clinician oversight will grow.

The expanding role of AI creates an ethical tension between access and harm.
Those who stand to benefit most from AI-mediated health information are also most likely to suffer from its failures: people facing language barriers, limited health literacy, or constrained access to care will rely most heavily on these systems and therefore be most exposed to their risks.
Worse, deploying imperfect systems to underserved communities may blunt incentives to expand human-delivered care, widening the very disparities AI is meant to close.

A further concern cuts across every stage: data privacy.
Health-related queries and interactions surface highly sensitive information about symptoms, diagnoses, and personal circumstances to platforms that largely operate outside the regulatory regimes governing clinical care.
Disclosed data may be retained, used for model training, sold to third parties, or leaked in breaches, leaving users vulnerable to insurance discrimination, blackmail, and identity theft.

Addressing these challenges requires more than technical improvements to individual systems
69
.
We need longitudinal research designs capable of capturing cross-stage effects, evaluation frameworks that foreground differential impacts across populations, and governance structures that assign accountability at each stage of the patient journey rather than only within clinical walls.
Concrete strategies have been proposed, but their implementation depends on incentive structures that currently favor commercial over public health goals
4
.
Regulatory frameworks face their own challenges.
For instance, the FDA is actively grappling with how to oversee generative AI tools whose outputs may change from one moment to the next
101
;
67
;
58
.
As AI becomes further embedded in how the public encounters and acts on health information, the adequacy of governance structures will matter as much as the performance of the systems they oversee.
