Association of Intimate Partner Violence With Substance Use Among Adults in Same-Sex and Opposite-Sex Partnerships
Authors: Pasangi S. Perera, Scott K. Proescholdbell, Mary E. Cox, Katherine M. McDaniel, Anna E. Austin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intimate partner violence (IPV) is linked to harmful drug use, especially in same-sex romantic partnerships, yet few studies use longitudinal data to evaluate this association over the life course. This study used the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health) to examine how IPV reported in Wave IV (2006–2008) relates to non-medical prescription and illicit drug use (drug use) in Wave V (2016–2018), stratified by same- and opposite-sex partnerships. Risk ratios (RR) adjusted for race/ethnicity, sexual orientation, age at IPV, prior substance use, child maltreatment, socioeconomic status, and social support were calculated. Sensitivity analyses additionally adjusted for gender and IPV type (physical or sexual). Among 7,318 participants, 23.0% reported experiences of IPV, 16.5% reported IPV perpetration, 13.7% reported both IPV experiences and perpetration and 13.4% reported drug use in Wave V. Same-sex partnership participants had higher prevalences of IPV experiences and perpetration (13.5% vs. 11.3%) and drug use (21.6% vs. 13.2%) than opposite-sex partnership participants. Overall, participants who reported IPV experiences only (RR = 1.27, 95% CI [0.99, 1.63]) and both IPV experiences and perpetration (RR = 1.27 [1.04, 1.56]) had higher relative risks of drug use than those who did not report any IPV. Results were similar by partnership type, with the exception that those in same-sex partnership who perpetrated IPV had a higher relative risk of drug use than those who did not report any IPV (RR = 1.22 [0.16, 9.04]). Participants who identified as female and experienced IPV (RR = 1.40 [1.03, 1.90]) or both experienced and perpetrated IPV (RR = 1.51 [1.19, 1.91]) also had a higher relative risk of drug use compared to females who did not experience or perpetrate IPV. These findings emphasize the need for tailored IPV and drug use interventions based on gender and type of romantic partnership.

Introduction

According to the Minority Stress Theory (MST), people who identify as lesbian, gay, or bisexual or are in a same-sex romantic partnership (i.e., sexual minority individuals) face unique stressors due to their perceived and actual identity in society (Frost & Meyer, 2023; Meyer, 2003). MST differs from other social stress theories as it holds that people in minoritized groups must adapt to greater amounts of and different types of stress than people in non-minoritized groups (Meyer, 2003). Specifically, in addition to daily life stressors, sexual minority individuals experience cumulative, chronic stress stemming from broader social norms and systems and structures of power that contribute to deeply ingrained internalized and externalized stigma, prejudice, and discrimination related to their minority group identity (Flentje et al., 2022; Frost & Meyer, 2023; Hatzenbuehler, 2009; Meyer, 2003). These unique, cumulative, and chronic stressors, rather than sexual minority identity itself, explain the disproportionate burden of adverse health outcomes among sexual minority individuals compared to their heterosexual peers (Frost & Meyer, 2023; Meyer, 2003). For example, MST explains the higher prevalence of mental health conditions among sexual minority individuals as compared to their heterosexual peers, which is due to both the proximal (i.e., internalized) and distal (i.e., external) stressors they experience, including a hostile social environment and discrimination based on their identity (Meyer, 2003). The MST highlights that sexual minority individuals may experience increased interpersonal victimization, internalized homophobia, and the need to conceal their sexual identity within society, resulting in increased psychological distress, and lowered self-esteem (Lehavot & Simoni, 2011). To combat these stressors, sexual minority individuals may turn to substance use as a mechanism to self-medicate, as a route to numb the pain caused by discrimination or to “un conceal” one’s identity (Lehavot & Simoni, 2011). Given these factors, substance use may be more widely accepted in sexual minority spaces, which may lead to unintended consequences (Lehavot & Simoni, 2011; Livingston et al., 2022).

Existing evidence indicates increased engagement in potentially risky health behaviors, including illicit drug use (i.e., use of crack/cocaine, methamphetamine, heroin, etc.) and non-medical prescription use (i.e., taking prescriptions in a manner or dose other than prescribed) among sexual minority individuals (Flentje et al., 2022; Frost & Meyer, 2023; Parent et al., 2018; Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2024). For example, adults who identify as sexual minority individuals (including but not limited to lesbian, gay, or bisexual) are two times more likely to either use illicit drugs or engage in non-medical prescription drug use compared to heterosexual adults (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2024). These drug use behaviors are also associated with comorbid mental and physical health conditions and increased risk of overdose and violence-related deaths (Carpenter et al., 2023; Griesler et al., 2019; Lewer et al., 2020; National Institute on Drug Abuse, 2020). Given these associations, identifying factors that contribute to risky substance use is critical for informing interventions that could reduce these behaviors and associated adverse outcomes (Baptiste-Roberts et al., 2017; Flentje et al., 2022; Frost & Meyer, 2023; Hatzenbuehler, 2009).

One stressor that may contribute to increased risk of drug use is intimate partner violence (IPV). Like illicit and non-medical prescription drug use, the prevalence of IPV is similar or higher among individuals who identify as sexual minorities or are in a same-sex romantic partnership compared to heterosexual individuals (Parent et al., 2018; Rollè et al., 2018; Stephenson & Finneran, 2017; Whitfield et al., 2021). National data show that 44% of lesbian women and 26% of gay men report having experienced IPV compared to 35% of heterosexual women and 29% of heterosexual men (Walters et al., 2013). Severe cases of IPV are also elevated for lesbian women (29% vs. 24%) and for gay men (16% vs. 14%) compared to their heterosexual peers (Walters et al., 2013). The higher prevalence of IPV among sexual minority individuals relates to the MST. Increased IPV perpetration can be linked with internalized homophobia, concealment and stigma as these stressors may cause increased tension, decreased communication, and lower self-esteem, which may affect romantic relationships and increase risk for IPV (Stephenson & Finneran, 2017). Heterosexist discrimination experienced proximally and distally can decrease self-confidence, which becomes a risk factor for experiencing IPV (Sarno et al., 2023). Importantly, individuals who identify as lesbian, gay, or bisexual or who are in same-sex partnerships may face barriers to seeking services and support for experiences of IPV, including stigma and discrimination from service providers and isolation due to social norms regarding typical IPV experiences (i.e., a woman experiencing IPV by a male perpetrator; Bates et al., 2024; D’Cruz et al., 2024; Walters et al., 2013; Whirry & Holt, 2020; Whitfield et al., 2021). Prior studies show that IPV is associated with increased substance use, with some studies finding that this association is stronger for individuals in same-sex partnerships as compared to those in opposite-sex partnerships (Ahmadabadi et al., 2019; Duncan et al., 2018; Gehring & Vaske, 2017; Kelley et al., 2015; Lewis et al., 2012; Roberts et al., 2003; Tubman et al., 2023; Walters et al., 2013). Via the MST, IPV may lead to loneliness, discrimination, or internalized stigma, with drug use functioning as a coping mechanism for these factors (Lewis et al., 2012).

There are some important methodological limitations to the existing research on the association of IPV with substance use among sexual minority individuals. First, most studies do not use longitudinal data sources that can confirm the temporality between experiences of IPV and substance use (Lewis et al., 2012; Roberts et al., 2003; Walters et al., 2013). The ability to establish temporality helps determine the potential causal or contributing role of IPV to later substance use behaviors and identifies specific points for intervention. In addition, many studies that examine IPV and substance use in individuals in same-sex and opposite-sex partnerships focus primarily on alcohol, tobacco, or marijuana use, excluding illicit drug use and non-medical prescription drug use, behaviors that are increasingly common and that may be more likely to result in severe outcomes like overdose death (Kelley et al., 2015). Finally, existing studies generally focus either on experiences of IPV or IPV perpetration separately, but not both (Ahmadabadi et al., 2019; Duncan et al., 2018). Given that some people both experience and perpetrate IPV within the same partnership and that both experiences and perpetration of IPV can lead to poor health outcomes, though to varying degrees and through different mechanisms, it is important to examine both (Ogden et al., 2022).

The Current Study

To address some of the limitations in the existing literature, the aim of this study was to use data from the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health) to examine the association of experiences and perpetration of IPV with later illicit drug use and non-medical prescription drug use (drug use) among adults, and to compare the strength and direction of associations for individuals in same-sex and opposite-sex partnerships. Add Health offers comprehensive data that can address existing limitations in evidence base, given the longitudinal nature of the data and measures captured. Previous studies (including studies with same-sex partnerships) have used Add Health to examine associations of IPV experiences and perpetration with substance use, finding that IPV is associated with later substance use (including alcohol, cigarettes, marijuana, and illicit drugs) among adolescents (Ahmadabadi et al., 2019; Gehring & Vaske, 2017; Nowotny & Graves, 2013; Roberts et al., 2003; Scheer & Mereish, 2021). Because these studies focused on adolescents, results may not generalize to later stages of the life course. This study examined this association among people in early to mid- adulthood and specifically focused on illicit drug and non-medical prescription drug use.

Methods

We used data from the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health). This longitudinal survey consists of a nationally representative sample of >20,000 adolescents in grades 7–12 during 1994–1995. Participants have been followed for five waves, with the last wave of data collection occurring from 2016 to 2018. Add Health survey questions collect a range of demographic, social, familial, socioeconomic, behavioral, psychosocial, cognitive, and health information (Harris et al., 2019). Information as to how variables from Add Health were specifically used and recoded for this study can be found in Supplemental Table 1.

Analytic Sample

The analytic sample from Add Health was based on Wave IV participants (N = 15,701; 2008–2009; ages 24–32 years). Participants were excluded from the analytic sample if they did not report any romantic partnership ever (N = 386), did not report a current partnership in Wave IV (N = 2,854), or were lost to follow-up at Wave V (N = 1,547), or were missing information on IPV and/or substance use (N = 3,596). We categorized participants as being in a romantic same-sex partnership or opposite-sex partnership at Wave IV based on reported sex for both the participant and their partner. As there was no question on the Wave IV survey about gender identity, we were unable to categorize participants as transgender, cisgender, or another gender identity.

Exposure

We assessed experiences and perpetration of IPV as reported by participants in a current romantic partnership at Wave IV. To measure IPV, Add Health used questions directly from the Revised Conflicts Tactics Scale (Straus, 1996). Questions included on the survey asked participants (1) “how often have” they either experienced the following behaviors (i.e., IPV experiences) or (2) engaged in following behaviors (i.e., IPV perpetration) in their current romantic partnership: (1) “threatened with violence, pushed or shoved, or thrown something that could hurt,” (2) “slapped, hit, or kicked,” and (3) “insisted on or made you have sexual relations when you didn’t want to.” We considered participants to have experienced IPV if they reported being subjected to at least one of these behaviors and to have perpetrated IPV if they reported engaging in at least one of these behaviors. From these questions, we created binary indicators (yes/no) for participants who (1) experienced IPV, (2) perpetrated IPV, and (3) experienced and perpetrated IPV.

Outcomes

We measured drug use as illicit drug use or non-medical prescription use as reported at Wave V (2016–2018; ages 32–42 years). We defined illicit drug use based on questions asking about past 30-day use of “cocaine, crystal meth, heroin, or other drugs (including LSD, PCP, ecstasy, mushrooms, and inhalants).” We defined non-medical prescription use based on questions reporting the use of prescription drugs, including “sedatives or downers (i.e., barbiturates, sleeping pills, Quaalude/ Seconal), pain killers or opioids (i.e., Vicodin, OxyContin, Percocet, Demerol, Percodan, or Tylenol with codeine), simulants or uppers (amphetamines, prescription diet pills, Ritalin, Preludin, speed), or tranquilizers (Librium, Valium, Xanax), that were not prescribed for them, in larger amounts or more often than prescribed, or only for the experience or feeling.” As limited participants in same-sex partnerships reported illicit drug use or non-medical prescription drug use, we combined these measures into one binary variable of illicit drug and/or non-medical prescription use (i.e., drug use) in the past 30 days.

Covariates

We identified potential confounders using a directed acyclic graph (DAG; Supplemental Figure 1) based on existing empirical evidence and subject matter expertise. We identified the following as confounders: child maltreatment (yes/no), prior substance use before Wave IV (yes/no), social support (yes/no), sexual orientation (straight/not straight/unknown), socioeconomic status (received public assistance/did not receive public assistance), age (at time of Wave IV interview), race (White/Black/Native American, Asian, or not any category listed) and ethnicity Hispanic or Spanish/Latino/not Hispanic or Spanish/Latino). Supplemental Table 1 provides details on confounders.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were generated using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). We first analyzed descriptive statistics of the study population, all Add Health participants in a romantic partnership at Wave IV and stratified according to whether participants were in same-sex or opposite-sex partnership. We then calculated risk ratios (RR) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using a log binomial regression model, comparing the risk of illicit drug or non-medical prescription drug use for individuals who: (a) experienced IPV only (without perpetration), (b) perpetrated IPV only (without experiencing IPV), and (c) both experienced and perpetrated IPV to those who did not experience or perpetrate IPV in their current romantic partnership. We next calculated RRs and 95% CIs to examine the above associations separately among those in opposite-sex and those in same-sex partnerships. We adjusted all models for the above-noted confounders.

We conducted several sensitivity analyses. First, given differences in experiences and perpetration of IPV and subsequent impacts on health outcomes by sex (male/female), we examined for potential effect measure modification by sex for the overall study population of participants in romantic partnerships. We did not examine for potential effect measure modification among those in opposite-sex and those in same-sex partnerships, given the small sample size of those in same-sex partnerships. We also conducted sensitivity analyses separately for specific types of IPV (i.e., physical and sexual), as associations may differ by type of IPV. We assessed the association between experiencing or perpetrating physical or sexual IPV, as compared to having not experienced or perpetrated physical or sexual IPV, and later illicit drug use or non-medical prescription drug use in one model. Specifically, we compared the risk of illicit or non-medical prescription drug use among those with: (a) physical IPV experiences, (b) physical IPV perpetration, (c) sexual IPV experiences, and (d) sexual IPV perpetration to those with no experiences or perpetration of physical or sexual IPV.

In all analyses, we accounted for the complex sample design of Add Health. To interpret results, we relied on the magnitude of the adjusted RR and the width and location of the corresponding 95% CI, in alignment with guidance from the American Statistical Association (Savitz et al., 2024; Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016). This study was reviewed and considered exempt by the Institutional Review Board at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill.

Results

Demographics of Add Health participants in a romantic partnership at Wave IV (ages 24–32 years) are shown in Table 1. Of 7,318 participants, 149 (2.0%) were in a same-sex partnership and 7,169 (98.0%) were in an opposite-sex partnership. Overall, 23.0% experienced IPV, 16.5% perpetrated IPV, and 13.7% both experienced and perpetrated IPV in their current romantic partnership. At Wave V (ages 32–42), 13.4% of participants reported drug use in the past 30 days. Compared to opposite-sex partnerships, participants in same-sex partnerships had a higher prevalence of both experiences and perpetration of IPV (13.5% vs. 11.3%) and illicit or non-medical prescription drug use (21.6% vs. 13.2%).

The adjusted association of experiences and perpetration of IPV with later illicit or non-medical prescription drug use for all participants in a romantic partnership is shown in Figure 1. Those who reported experiences of IPV only (RR = 1.27, 95% CI [0.99, 1.63]) and those who reported both experiences and perpetration of IPV (RR = 1.27 [1.04, 1.56]) had a higher relative risk of drug use compared to those who did not report experiencing or perpetrating IPV. Those who reported IPV perpetration only had a similar risk of drug use with those who did not report experiencing or perpetrating IPV (RR = 0.95 [0.67, 1.34]). Stratified by partnership type (Figure 1), results were similar among those in opposite-sex partnerships. Specifically, those in opposite-sex partnerships who reported experiences of IPV only (RR = 1.26 [0.99, 1.64]) and experiences and perpetration (RR = 1.27 [1.02, 1.59]) had a higher relative risk of drug use compared to those who did not report experiencing or perpetrating IPV, and those who reported IPV perpetration only had a similar risk of drug use compared to those who did not report IPV experiences or perpetration (RR = 0.95 [0.67, 1.36]). Among those in same-sex partnerships, results were similar for participants who reported experiences of IPV only (RR = 1.27 [0.56, 2.89]), and both experiences and perpetration of IPV (RR = 1.26 [0.48, 2.84]) compared to those who did not report any experiences or perpetration of IPV. Notably, participants in same-sex partnerships who reported perpetration of IPV only had a higher risk of later drug use compared to those who did not report experiencing or perpetrating IPV (RR = 1.22 [0.16, 9.04]). Importantly, due to the small sample size of those in same-sex partnerships, the 95% CIs were wide, indicating that point estimates were imprecise and should be interpreted with caution.

Sensitivity analyses examining the potential effect measure modification by gender are shown in Figure 2. Participants who identified as female and reported experiences of IPV only (RR = 1.40, 95% CI [1.03, 1.90]) and both experiences and perpetration of IPV (RR = 1.51 [1.19, 1.91]) had higher relative risks of later drug use compared to female participants who did not report experiences or perpetration of IPV. Among participants who identified as female, those who reported IPV perpetration only had a similar risk of later drug use as those who did not experience or perpetrate IPV (RR = 1.04 [0.71, 1.51]). Participants who identified as male and reported experiences of IPV only had a higher relative risk of later drug use compared to male participants who did not report experiences or perpetration of IPV (RR = 1.18 [0.94, 1.65]). Male participants who reported both experiences and perpetration of IPV (RR = 1.03 [0.68, 1.56]) and IPV perpetration only (RR = 0.75 [0.34, 1.70]) had relatively similar risks of later drug use compared to males who did not experience or perpetrate IPV.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to examine the association of experiences and perpetration of IPV with later illicit or non-medical prescription drug use among adults in romantic partnerships. We assessed these associations among those in same-sex and opposite-sex partnerships at the time of the IPV experiences or perpetration. We found that adults who reported experiences of IPV only or both experiences and perpetration of IPV had higher relative risks of later drug use compared to those who did not report any IPV. These results are consistent with previous studies (Ahmadabadi et al., 2019; Golder & Logan, 2011; Ogden et al., 2022; Stene et al., 2012). People in same-sex partnerships reported a higher prevalence of illicit drug use, non-medical prescription drug use, and both experiences and perpetration of IPV. In adjusted models, associations between IPV experiences only and both experiences and perpetration of IPV with later drug use were similar in magnitude for those in same-sex and opposite-sex partnerships. Notably, for the overall study population and stratified by partnership type, perpetration of IPV only was not associated with later drug use.

Overall, we found similar strength of associations between experiences of IPV only and both experiences and perpetration of IPV with later drug use among those in opposite-sex and same-sex partnerships. While these results were unexpected, previous studies have found similar risk factors for substance use and similar mechanisms underlying the association between IPV and substance use for those in same-sex and opposite-sex partnerships (e.g., mental health conditions, stigma) may lead to similar strength of associations (Kimmes et al., 2019). Previous studies have also found that individuals in same-sex partnerships may underreport IPV to a greater degree than those in opposite-sex partnerships given fear of experiencing stigma and discrimination related to their current relationship or sexual orientation status, which is deeply rooted in MST (Blosnich & Bossarte, 2009; Frankland & Brown, 2014; Kimmes et al., 2019; Meyer, 2003). This may have led to an underestimate of the strength of associations among those in same-sex partnerships, as those with experiences of IPV may have been included in the comparison group of those without experiences or perpetration of IPV.

We did not observe an association between IPV perpetration only and later drug use for the overall study sample of adults in romantic partnerships. Some previous research shows associations between IPV perpetration and later substance use behaviors (Choenni et al., 2017; Lipsky & Caetano, 2008; Melander et al., 2010) and disorders (Smith et al., 2012). These prior studies have primarily focused on marijuana or cocaine use with little attention to use of other illicit drugs and non-medical prescription drug use. Our combined outcome variable of illicit drug and/or non-medical prescription drug use may have obscured associations between IPV perpetration only and specific types of drug use (Choenni et al., 2017). However, small numbers by specific drug type precluded these analyses. It is also possible that IPV perpetration is underreported due to social desirability bias (Freeman et al., 2015; Visschers et al., 2017), potentially leading to null results. Last, our analyses examined the association of prior IPV perpetration with later substance use. While in this study we choose to evaluate the relationship between IPV (experiences and perpetration) and later substance use, existing literature shows that prior substance use is associated with later IPV, which could have affected results (Gilchrist et al., 2019; Kimmes et al., 2019). Of note, we adjusted for prior substance use as a confounder in multivariable analyses.

In analyses conducted separately for those in same-sex and opposite-sex partnerships, results suggested that IPV perpetration only is associated with an increased risk of later drug use among those in same-sex partnerships only. However, the 95% CIs for this result were particularly wide and thus should be interpreted with caution. In sensitivity analyses stratified by gender, we found that among both adults who identified as female and male, experiences of IPV were associated with an increased risk of later drug use, though the magnitude of the association was larger among female-identifying than among male-identifying adults. While both experiences and perpetration of IPV were associated with an increased relative risk of drug use among female-identifying adults, we did not observe associations among men. In addition, perpetration of IPV only was not associated with later illicit drug or non-medical prescription drug use among female- or male-identifying adults. These results are consistent with prior literature showing that associations between experiences of IPV and later substance use are stronger among those who identify as female than among those who identify as male (Ahmadabadi et al., 2019; Ansara & Hindin, 2011; Fagan & Wright, 2011). While we were unable to assess for effect measure modification by gender in analyses among those in same-sex and opposite-sex partnerships due to the small sample size of those in same-sex partnerships, a meta-analysis published in 2017 found that individuals in same-sex partnerships, particularly men who have sex with men, have an increased risk later substance use (Choenni et al., 2017). This highlights the importance of future research among those in same-sex partnerships, including ensuring collection of data from a sufficient number of both men and women in same-sex partnerships, to understand potential differential associations by gender.

In sensitivity analyses accounting for different types of IPV, both experiences of sexual and physical IPV were associated with an increased risk of later drug use, with slightly stronger associations for experiences of physical IPV than sexual IPV. Notably, a systematic review found prior studies have examined associations between experiences of physical and sexual IPV with illicit drug or non-medical prescription medication use (Ogden et al., 2022). One study found that lifetime experiences of psychological and physical IPV were associated with recent (past 24-month) crack use, while also finding no significant associations between sexual, stalking, or any IPV and crack use (Golder & Logan, 2011; Ogden et al., 2022). The second study found past-six-month sexual IPV to be associated with increased risk of subsequent crack/cocaine use and combined crack/cocaine and heroin use. However, no other forms of IPV (physical, injurious, verbal) were significantly associated with subsequent crack/cocaine and/or heroin use (Gilbert et al., 2012; Ogden et al., 2022). The final study found past-year IPV among women at the Veteran’s Health Administration was associated with higher odds of having a substance use disorder diagnosis in the following 6 months than those who did not experience past-year IPV (Dichter et al., 2017; Ogden et al., 2022). These results are consistent with our findings.

These results have important implications for future research, prevention, and intervention. Institutions and systems that are tasked with responding to and preventing IPV, such as healthcare or social services, can implement policies and practices that decrease stigma and isolation for people who have experienced IPV, especially for those who are within same-sex partnerships. In turn, this could allow for all people, including those in same-sex partnerships, to be able to report IPV while maintaining safety and autonomy. This early intervention could reduce a person’s chance of harmful substance-related outcomes including substance use disorders and fatal and non-fatal overdoses. Future research should include people who identify as transgender, non-binary, and other gender identities as these individuals experience higher rates of substance use and IPV. This could be done through oversampling sexual minority populations in a culturally sensitive manner, especially within national surveys. Finally, future research should incorporate trauma-informed approaches into SUD services, given the association between IPV and substance use, as well as the potential for increased use to progress into SUD.

Limitations

While there are many strengths to this study, this study also has limitations. The 95% confidence intervals for associations among those in same-sex partnerships were very wide given the small sample size. Thus, results specific to those in same-sex partnerships should be interpreted with caution. Alongside this, we did not include transgender identity in our analyses as the Add Health survey did not assess this. Future studies should include this as transgender people may be more susceptible to IPV and substance use via the MST (Frost & Meyer, 2023). Another limitation was the small number of participants who reported illicit drug or non-medical prescription drug use, which led to the creation of a single outcome variable instead of two separate variables. These frequencies were similar to other national datasets sources, including National Survey on Drug Use and Health (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2024). However, the small number contributed to imprecise results for some analyses and precluded analyses of specific substance use behaviors and by specific drug type. We used race and ethnicity as adjustment variables instead of effect measure modifiers, given the limited sample sizes of specific racial and ethnic groups (Ward et al., 2019). Adjusting for these factors potentially masks the effects among racial minorities, particularly Black and Latino women, who have an increased risk of IPV victimization, drug use, and the relationship between IPV and later drug use (Nowotny & Graves, 2013). Similarly, ethnicity in this analysis was based on an Add Health question that combined Latino and Hispanic identities instead of separating these distinct ethnicities. Ideally, these ethnicities would be accounted for independently to further explain the context of the IPV and drug use relationship, but this singular variable was used for model convergence purposes. Future research should account for the intersection and subgroup analyses of race, ethnicity, gender, and type of partnership to fully capture the implications of intersectional identities for these associations. While we used a DAG to identify a comprehensive set of potential confounders to include in multivariable analyses to adjust for potential bias due to confounding, there is still potential for bias due to unmeasured or poorly measured confounding. In addition, differential loss to follow-up within Add Health over time could bias results. However, previous research found that there was minimal bias due to non-response in Add Health, particularly for Waves III and IV (Andraca et al., 2005; Brownstein et al., 2018). Finally, we could not examine emotional IPV at all, given that this was not captured in Add Health. Participants who experienced or perpetrated emotional IPV only (and not physical or sexual IPV) would have been included in our denominator, which may shift results toward the null. Even with these limitations, this study still provides generalizable estimates within the United States that account for the temporality of IPV and drug use.

Conclusions

Overall, we found that experiences or experiences and perpetration of IPV were associated with later illicit or non-medical prescription drug use, with similar associations observed for those in both same-sex and opposite-sex partnerships at the time of the IPV. Perpetration alone was not associated with illicit drug or non-medical prescription drug use for the overall sample, while results suggested an increased risk specifically among those in same-sex partnerships. These results highlight the need for more research focused on gender and partnership types with respect to the complex associations between experiences and perpetration of IPV with later substance use.
