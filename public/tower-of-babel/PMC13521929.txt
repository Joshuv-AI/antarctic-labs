Near-atomic in situ architecture and membrane-coupled dynamics of the Vibrio cholerae sheathed flagellum
Authors: Wangbiao Guo, Jian Yue, Jin Hwan Park, Diana Valverde-Mendez, Jack M. Botting, Huaxin Yu, Avijay Sen, Rajeev Kumar, Jing Yan, Fitnat H. Yildiz, Jun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The sheathed flagellum of Vibrio cholerae is a self-assembling membranous organelle that coordinates flagellar assembly, sheath biogenesis, and rapid rotation. Here, we determine in-situ near-atomic structures of the sheathed flagellar motor within intact cells. The motor anchors to the outer membrane through lipidated HL-rings without forming a membrane pore, thereby enabling flagellar assembly to drive sheath formation. Conserved LP-rings function as slide-rotary bushings, permitting high-speed rotation within the bacterial envelope while retaining the ability to constrict and seal the lumen upon flagellar ejection. We further show that stator activation depends on a specific protein-protein interaction rather than peptidoglycan engagement. Together, these findings reveal how the distinctive architecture and dynamics of the sheathed flagellum support V. cholerae motility, environmental survival, and persistent colonization of the human gut.

Introduction

Vibrio cholerae is a highly motile, comma-shaped Gram-negative bacterium that has evolved intricate mechanisms to navigate aquatic environments and colonize the human intestine, causing severe and highly transmissible diarrheal disease1–3. Motility is central to its physiology and infection cycle, contributing to chemotaxis, colonization, biofilm formation, and virulence4–6. Unlike model organisms such as Escherichia coli and Salmonella enterica that possess unsheathed flagella, V. cholerae relies on a single polar flagellum encased in a membranous sheath and distinguished by Vibrio-specific features, including the H- and T-rings5. These adaptations anchor the flagellar motor to the outer membrane7–9 and facilitate recruitment of sodium-driven stator complexes8,10, enabling the generation of high torque11,12 and rapid rotation11–13. Early studies in marine Vibrio species indicated that the membranous sheath is not a simple extension of the outer membrane, as its lipopolysaccharide composition and protein content differ from those of the outer membrane14,15. The sheath is essential for the virulence of the fish pathogen V. anguilarum16 and enables V. cholerae to evade flagellin-triggered innate immune responses17.

A critical determinant of V. cholerae survival in aquatic habitats and infection in human hosts is its ability to transition from a motile planktonic state to a sessile, surface-attached state18. Under environmental stress such as nutrient depletion, cells actively shed the polar flagellum19, leaving a flagellar relic sealed by a plug-like structure9,19–23. Despite these insights, how assembly of the polar flagellum is coordinated with sheath biogenesis, how this specialized nanomachine operates within the bacterial envelope, and how it disassembles to form the flagellar relic remain poorly understood.

To address these questions, we integrated in-situ single-particle cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) and cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) with genetic and biochemical approaches to capture conformational states associated with flagellar assembly and disassembly in intact V. cholerae cells. We show that the sheathed flagellar motor anchors to the outer membrane via lipoprotein-based H- and L-rings without forming a membrane pore, thereby coupling flagellar assembly to sheath formation. We further demonstrate that the sodium-driven stator complex exhibits notable flexibility, accommodating membrane fluctuations while engaging in a specific interaction with the T-ring protein MotX to mediate stator recruitment and torque generation. Finally, our data support a model in which the motor achieves rapid rotation within the fluctuating bacterial envelope through a slide-rotary bushing mechanism.

Results

Two distinct in situ architectures of the flagellar motor in V. cholerae

To elucidate how the sheathed flagellum assembles and functions, we used an in-situ single-particle cryo-EM approach24,25 to determine near-atomic structures of the flagellar motor in intact V. cholerae cells (Supplementary Table 1). From 57,620 micrographs collected at the cell poles of a hyperflagellated ΔflhG mutant strain26, we identified 155,760 flagellar motor particles through a combination of manual picking, deep learning-based Topaz selection27, and iterative classification in cryoSPARC28. The resulting in-situ structure at local resolutions of 2.7–3.8 Å resolves the MS-ring, rod, hook, and flagellar outer membrane complex (FOMC) assembled around the rod. The FOMC is composed of the well-defined L-, P-, H-, and T-rings (Supplementary Figs. 1–3). We also resolved the C-terminal domain of a stator protein PomB (PomBC) at ~5 Å resolution in association with the T-ring, whereas the remaining stator densities are not resolved, likely due to flexibility. We next determined a second in situ structure at near-atomic resolution from 43,759 FOMC particles lacking the filament, hook, and rod. This FOMC adopts a closed conformation, characterized by a gate-like density at the base of the P-ring and closure of the outer membrane complex above the L-ring (Fig. 1d–f). Given its similarity to previously reported flagellar relics9,19–23, the structure likely represents a post-disassembly state. To test this interpretation, we combined live-cell fluorescence microscopy and cryo-ET to examine wild-type V. cholerae cells under nutrient-rich and nutrient-depleted conditions. Nutrient depletion significantly increases the frequency of flagellar shedding and the accumulation of closed FOMCs (Supplementary Fig. 4), consistent with previous observations9,19–23. We therefore conclude that this closed FOMC corresponds to the flagellar relic following flagellar ejection.

Together, these near-atomic in situ structures enabled modeling of large assemblies composed of more than 12 flagellar proteins and ~340 subunits (Supplementary Fig. 5) in two distinct functional states, providing a structural basis for the assembly, disassembly, and function of sheathed flagella.

These interactions differ markedly from those in unsheathed flagella (Supplementary Fig. 6a–c). As an example, in S. enterica, the L-ring forms an outer membrane pore through which the hook and filament assemble (Supplementary Fig. 6b)29–31, stabilized by hydrophobic interactions and lipidation of FlgH at Cys2230,31. In contrast, V. cholerae FlgH contains a long, flexible loop (residues 16-48), not resolved in our structure, enabling insertion of the lipidated Cys16 without pore formation (Supplementary Fig. 6a). This distinct anchoring mode represents a key adaptation for sheathed flagella.

The P-ring adopts open and closed conformations corresponding to assembled and disassembled states (Fig. 2c, g). In the closed state, GateI (H274-K314) and GateII (S140-N156) form β-sheet, structures that oligomerize into a constricted gate (~40 Å diameter), further sealed by densities likely corresponding to peptidoglycan (Fig. 2d, h; Supplementary Fig. 1l). In the open state, the rod occupies the lumen, GateI is partially disordered, and GateII forms a flexible loop (Supplementary Fig. 6d, e). Structural comparison indicates that GateI and GateII undergo ~90° upward movements and loop-to-β-sheet transition during closure (Supplementary Fig. 6f, g), consistent with the flagellar ejection model9,19–23, in which rod ejection triggers P-ring sealing to prevent periplasmic leakage.

To further investigate the roles of GateI and GateII, we constructed flgIΔgateI, flgIΔgateII, and flgIΔgateI/II mutants in which these regions were replaced by dipeptide (GS) linkers in both wild-type and ΔflhG backgrounds (Fig. 2i, j; Supplementary Fig. 7a-c). FLAG-tagged flgI variants showed comparable FlgI expression across strains, although local folding effects cannot be excluded (Fig. 2j). Functionally, the flgIΔgateI mutant exhibited ~40% reduced motility, whereas flgIΔgateII was similar to wild type (Fig. 2i). The flgIΔgateI/II double mutant phenocopied flgIΔgateI, indicating that GateI plays a dominant role in motility while GateII is largely dispensable. Consistently, cryo-ET analysis in the ΔflhG background showed that deletion of GateI markedly increased the fraction of motors stalled during rod assembly from 0.1 to 45% and motors in a disassembly state from 12 to 28%, whereas deletion of GateII had minimal effects (Fig. 2k, l; Supplementary Fig. 7d, e).

To assess effects on envelope integrity, we tested sensitivity to choline and vancomycin (Supplementary Fig. 8). Under stress conditions, ΔflgI, flgIΔgateI, and flgIΔgateI/II showed modest but reproducible growth defects, whereas flgIΔgateII resembled the parental strain. Increased sensitivity segregated with loss of GateI.

Together, these results indicate that GateI is the primary determinant of motility and contributes to maintaining envelope integrity, consistent with a role in P-ring sealing.

Architecture of the membrane-bound H-ring

The H-ring is essential for flagellar assembly and sheath biogenesis in Vibrio species7,32–34. It comprises at least three core components: FlgT, FlgO, and FlgP. FlgT forms a C26-symmetric ring encircling the LP-rings, whereas FlgO and FlgP assemble into two concentric outer membrane-associated rings with C58 symmetry (Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. 9). FlgT consists of three domains: FlgTN, FlgTM, and FlgTC (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). Compared with the V. alginolyticus structure (PDB: 3W1E)35, the FlgTN domain in V. cholerae is rotated upward, forming an additional concentric ring aligned with FlgTM (Supplementary Fig. 9e). FlgT attaches to the LP-rings through interactions between FlgTM and the LN loop of FlgH, burying ~762 Å2 per subunit (Fig. 3b, c). FlgTC, positioned beneath FlgTM, serves as a scaffold for T-ring assembly (Supplementary Fig. 10).

FlgP adopts a compact β-sandwich fold (β1-β4-β5 and β2-β3) and two α-helices (α1, α2) (Supplementary Fig. 9a, c). Its C-terminal loop (residues 131–145) inserts into inter-subunit clefts within the FlgT ring, mediating interactions and accommodating the symmetry mismatch between C58 FlgP and C26 FlgT. Two loop conformations are resolved: 26 loops extend toward FlgT, and 26 extend laterally along the ring, while six remain unresolved (Fig. 3b, d; Supplementary Fig. 11).

FlgO consists of two β-sheets and two α-helices (α1, α2). The first β-sheet interacts with both helices to form the core (Supplementary Fig. 9d), and the second β-sheet extends toward FlgP, stabilizing the FlgO-FlgP interface (Fig. 3e).

Both FlgO and FlgP are outer membrane lipoproteins7,33, anchored via conserved N-terminal lipidation at Cys16 and Cys18, respectively, consistent with diffuse densities near their N-termini (Fig. 3d, e; Supplementary Fig. 9c, d). Together with FlgH, they form a membrane-anchored scaffold that secures the FOMC and couples it to the outer membrane in both assembled and disassembled states (Fig. 3f, g). Consistent with this architecture, H-ring mutants fail to form normal sheathed flagella and instead produce periplasmic flagella7, suggesting that sheath formation is facilitated by stable anchoring of the H-ring to the outer membrane.

Sheath formation is tightly coupled to flagellar assembly

To understand how the membrane-anchoring scaffold coordinates sheath formation with flagellar assembly, we performed cryo-ET analyses of polar sheathed flagella. Although long flagella predominated, shorter nascent flagella in both wild-type and ΔflhG cells were selected for subtomogram analyses to capture distinct early assembly intermediates. The earliest stage resolved by cryo-ET and subtomogram averaging corresponds to the formation of the MS-ring and C-ring. Subsequent rod assembly provides a template for formation of an open FOMC (Fig. 4). As the hook elongates, it progressively pushes the outer membrane outward, generating a continuous sheath that envelops the growing hook (Supplementary Video 1). Notably, the emerging hook extends perpendicularly from the outer membrane in a straight conformation rather than a curved shape, suggesting that this geometry is energetically favorable in coordinating hook elongation with sheath formation. Following flagellar ejection, the FOMC seals its central lumen and persists as a flagellar relic that is subsequently covered by the outer membrane (Fig. 4c, d). Consistent with the crystal structure from V. alginolyticus (PDB: 2ZF8)36, MotY consists of two domains: an N-terminal MotYN and a C-terminal MotYC. MotYN interacts with FlgT and FlgI, anchoring the T-ring to the basal body (Fig. 5d, e; Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). Specifically, β3-β5 and α1-α2 of MotYN face FlgTC, while a loop (residues 91-102) extends into the cleft between FlgTC and FlgI-RBM1. MotYC adopts an α/β-sandwich fold containing a putative peptidoglycan-binding motif36 (Fig. 5e).

MotX, composed of tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) motifs, adopts an 11-helix helix-turn-helix architecture (Fig. 5f). Two adjacent MotX subunits form a dimer that defines the interaction site for a PomB dimer. One MotX subunit forms a tight interface with one PomBC protomer, involving the N-terminal loop (residues 25–31) and α3 helix (residues 58–69) of MotX and loops 194–208 and 238–240 of PomBC. The second PomBC protomer inserts into the cleft between adjacent MotX-MotY subcomplexes (Fig. 5g; Supplementary Fig. 12).

To assess the functional importance of the MotX-PomB interaction, we introduced point mutations in interface residues: MotX* (P29A, I58A, Q60A, D61A, A64R, I69A) and PomB* (P196A, F201A, Q203A, F206A, L239A) (Fig. 5h–k; Supplementary Fig. 10d). The resulting PomB* mutant was expressed at levels comparable to wild type but exhibits a non-motile phenotype in soft agar. Similarly, MotX* showed an almost complete loss of motility, despite retaining ~50% of wild-type protein levels (Fig. 5h–i; Supplementary Fig. 10e–g). Given that PomB and MotX are core components required for torque generation in the Vibrio sheathed flagellar motor36, we next examined how perturbing the MotX-PomBC interface affects swimming performance. Single-cell tracking revealed that wild-type cells display a bimodal speed distribution, with a low-speed fraction (~0-10 µm/s) and a high-speed fraction (~50–100 µm/s) (Fig. 5j). The low-speed fraction population overlaps with the non-motile ΔflaA control and likely reflects passive Brownian motion under our imaging conditions. ΔpomB and ΔmotX exhibited only background-level displacements, indistinguishable from ΔflaA, confirming that both proteins are essential for flagellar rotation (Fig. 5j, k). Consistent with soft-agar migration phenotypes, FLAG-pomB displayed a modest reduction in swimming speed relative to wild type, whereas FLAG-pomB* was essentially non-motile and phenocopied ΔpomB and ΔflaA. Similarly, motX-FLAG tracked like wild type, whereas motX*-FLAG behaved like ΔmotX and ΔflaA (Fig. 5k).

Together, these results suggest that MotX-PomB interaction is essential for stator recruitment and flagellar rotation, providing direct evidence that MotX, rather than peptidoglycan37–39, plays a central role in stator activation and torque generation in V. cholerae.

Slide-rotary bushing mechanism

To understand how the sheathed flagellar motor in Vibrio generates high torque11,12 and rapid rotation11,12, we determined 3.4 Å in-situ structures of the distal rod and hook, enabling model building of FlgG and FlgE (Fig. 6; Supplementary Fig. 13). Notably, PomBC dimers remain attached to the T-ring, whereas the rest of the stator complexes are not resolved (Supplementary Fig. 13; Supplementary Video 2), suggesting that flexibility of the PomAB stator complex enables interactions between PomB and MotX. This interpretation is consistent with previous findings that PomB and its homolog MotB possess flexible linkers that function as spring-like anchors40,41.

The distal rod surface is enriched in acidic residues, generating a predominantly negative electrostatic potential, whereas the inner wall of the LP-rings alternates between negatively and positively charged patches (Fig. 6c, d). The P-ring forms the closest contact with the rod, and four lysine residues in the D1 domain of FlgI create a ~ 20 Å-wide, positively charged tunnel surrounding the rod (Fig. 6e, f). These complementary electrostatic forces balance attraction and repulsion to maintain the coaxial alignment of the rod and stabilize its rotation, as proposed in S. enterica30,31,42. Moreover, the smooth, uniform surface of the distal rod enables the LP-rings to slide along it for ~20 Å, thereby accommodating fluctuations of the bacterial envelope. Together, these findings support a slide-rotary bushing mechanism, by which the flagellar motor achieves rapid rotation while remaining mechanically coupled to the bacterial envelope (Fig. 6g, h; Supplementary Video 2).

Discussion

The sheathed flagellum is a bacterial membranous organelle first described more than 70 years ago43,44. Unlike the well-studied unsheathed flagella in S. enterica and E. coli, the sheathed flagellum in V. cholerae has evolved distinctive adaptations that support sheath formation, high-speed rotation, and both sessile and motile lifestyles. By integrating in-situ single-particle cryo-EM and cryo-ET with genetic and biochemical approaches, we resolve the sheathed flagellum in unprecedented detail, providing insights into how this complex membranous organelle assembles, rotates, and disassembles in intact V. cholerae.

Our structures reveal that the conserved L-ring does not form a membrane pore but instead assembles beneath the outer membrane, positioned by a long lipidated N-terminal loop of FlgH (Fig. 3). Together with the lipidated H-ring, the motor is firmly anchored to the inner leaflet of the outer membrane. These architectures, along with early assembly intermediates, support a model in which hook elongation linearly displaces the outer membrane, thereby coupling sheath formation with hook assembly. Continued filament elongation then drives sheath extension (Supplementary Video 1). Because the sheath is continuous with the outer membrane while the lipidated H- and L- rings are stably anchored, rotation of the filament, hook, and rod is likely decoupled from the sheath.

High-speed rotation in Vibrio is powered by sodium-driven stator complexes13,45. Contrary to the prevailing model in which the peptidoglycan-binding domain of PomB (or MotB) anchors to the cell wall37–39, our structural and mutational analyses indicate that this domain primarily interacts with the T-ring component MotX. Disruption of this interface abolishes motility, despite a partial reduction in protein levels and potential effects on protein stability, highlighting a critical role for the T-ring in recruiting and activating stator complexes. This stator activation model is consistent with recent findings in Campylobacterota species25,46,47 and Borrelia burgdorferi48, where stator complexes associate closely with accessory proteins to generate high torque. To accommodate the mechanical demands during high-speed rotation, we propose a slide-rotary bushing model in which the membrane-bound LH-rings anchor to the outer membrane while sliding along the distal rod by up to ~2 nm, enabling the motor to adapt to envelope fluctuations (Supplementary Video 2). Similar conformational changes observed in V. alginolyticus8 and C. jejuni46 support a conserved strategy across diverse flagellar systems. Consistent with both the spring model41,49 and this slide-rotary bushing mechanism, the stator complex exhibits intrinsic flexibility that helps maintain PomB–MotX interactions, ensuring robust stator recruitment and maximal torque generation.

Flagellar ejection serves as a widespread adaptive response to environmental stress, yet how leakage is prevented after rod loss has remained unclear50. Our in-situ structures show that, upon rod ejection, two flexible segments of FlgI (GateI and GateII) tilt upward to form β-hairpins that constrict the P-ring pore, thereby sealing the complex. Given the high conservation of FlgI, this gating mechanism likely represents a general strategy across bacterial species.

Together, these findings define the atomic architecture and dynamics of the Vibrio sheathed flagellar motor and establish a mechanistic framework for its assembly, rotation, and disassembly. They also provide new insights into bacterial motile and sessile lifestyles, with implications for inhibiting flagellum-dependent infection in V. cholerae and related species.

Methods

Strain construction

Plasmid construction was performed using Gibson Assembly (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA), with primers designed using the NEBuilder Assembly Tool and listed in Supplemental Data Tables 1, 2. PCR amplification was performed using Q5 High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For each deletion construct, approximately 0.5-kb regions upstream and downstream of the target gene were amplified and assembled into the suicide vector pGP704sacB28 by Gibson Assembly. The knock-in constructs carrying point mutations or FLAG tags were synthesized and ordered from TWIST Bioscience (South San Francisco, CA). All constructs were verified by DNA sequencing (Azenta Life Sciences, Burlington, MA) prior to conjugation and allelic exchange51. Deletion and knock-in plasmids were introduced into recipient strains by biparental mating using E. coli SM10 λpir or S17-1 λpir as the donor. Donor and recipient cultures were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and incubated on LB agar plates at 37 °C for at least 6 h. Transconjugants were selected on Luria-Bertani Miller (LB) agar containing rifampicin (100 μg/mL) and ampicillin (100 μg/mL). Successful gene deletions or knock-ins were confirmed by PCR using the gene-specific delA and delD primer sets.

Bacterial culture

V. cholerae strains were cultured aerobically at 30 °C in LB medium, composed of 1% tryptone, 0.5% yeast extract, and 1% NaCl. Overnight cultures were prepared by streaking bacterial strains from frozen glycerol stocks onto LB agar plates, followed by incubation at 30 °C overnight. A single colony was then selected and streaked onto a fresh LB agar plate, which was incubated for an additional 12 h.

Cryo-EM sample preparation

V. cholerae cells were centrifuged at 2000 × g for approximately 5 min in 1.5 mL tubes, and the resulting pellet was gently rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The pellet was resuspended in PBS to a final optical density (OD600) of 1.0 for plunge-freezing preparation. Cryo-EM samples were prepared using copper grids with a holey carbon support film (200 mesh, R2/1, Quantifoil) and Lacey C grids (200 mesh, Cu, Ted Pella, Inc.). The grids were glow-discharged for ~30 s prior to sample application. A 5 µL aliquot of the cell suspension was applied to each grid, which was then blotted with filter paper (WhatmanTM) for approximately 6 s. Grids were rapidly plunged into a liquid ethane-propane mixture using a Leica GP2 plunger. During the plunge-freezing process, the GP2 environmental chamber was maintained at 25 °C and 90% relative humidity to optimize vitrification.

In situ cryo-EM data collection and processing

Cryo-EM specimen was imaged using a 300 kV Titan Krios electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a field emission gun and a post-GIF K3 Summit direct electron detector (Gatan). Images were recorded in nanoprobe mode with the following settings: magnification at ×81,000, spot size at 5, illumination aperture at 1.22 µm, slit width at 20 eV, C2 aperture at 50 µm, and objective aperture at 100 µm. A customized multishot script24 was used to directly image the cell tips on the cryo-EM grids with SerialEM52, applying defocus values from –1.6 µm to –2.2 µm. The total electron dose was ~70 e−/Å2. The effective pixel size was 1.068 Å per physical pixel (super-resolution mode, half physical pixel size). A total of 57,620 micrographs were collected from ΔflhG cells.

All micrographs were initially motion-corrected53 and then subjected to Patch CTF Estimation in cryoSPARC28 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Approximately 18,000 motor particles were manually picked initially, followed by particle picking across all micrographs using the machine-learning-based algorithm Topaz27. In total, 155,760 particles of the flagellar motor in the assembled, open state and 43,759 particles in the disassembled, close state were selected.

Micrographs were extracted with a box size of 1280 and binned to 320 in Fourier space. An initial model of the flagellar motor was generated by ab-initio reconstruction using ~20,000 manually picked particles, followed by heterogeneous refinement with C13 symmetry. Approximately 1000 particles showing clear LP-ring density were selected as the initial model. After including all particles and performing homogeneous and local refinement with C13 symmetry, we obtained global flagellar motor structures in both assembled and disassembled states at bin 4, achieving a resolution of 8.68 Å (FSC 0.143), the resolution limit at this binning stage.

Subsequently, we focused on resolving the LP-ring structure (Supplementary Fig. 1f-l). Micrographs were further re-extracted with a box size of 448 pixels. To remove unnecessary junk particles, a focused mask was applied on LP-ring to conduct 3D classification. Local refinement was conducted using C26 symmetry, which yielded high-resolution reconstructions of the LP-ring at 2.69 Å (EMD-72815) for the assembled-state with 112,879 particles and 2.75 Å (EMD-72834) for the disassembled-state with 31,668 particles, serving as the structural foundation for subsequent refinement of the H-ring and T-ring.

To determine the symmetry of two additional ring-like densities, presumed to correspond to FlgP and FlgO components of the H-ring (Supplementary Fig. 2a–d). Micrographs were re-extracted with a box size of 720 pixels and binned to 512 because of the larger diameter of H-ring. We performed symmetry expansion of a prime number, e.g. C53, C57, followed by focused 3D classification on the H-ring. Among that, a C58-symmetric ring was identified, enabling us to assign C58 symmetry to both FlgP and FlgO. Further local refinement improved the resolution of FlgO and FlgP structures within the H-ring to 3.45 Å (35,317 particles, EMD-72833) and 3.39 Å (24,036 particles, EMD-76766), respectively, in the assembled, open state and disassembled, close state.

The H-ring component FlgT is positioned at the periphery of the LP-ring and exposes C26 symmetry. However, due to its connection with FlgP with C58 symmetry, the resolution of FlgT in the initial reconstruction was low. To improve this, symmetry expansion was performed using C29, based on the coordinates of FlgP and FlgO. Focused 3D classification on the FlgT/P region was then carried out. Particles that retained clear C26 symmetry for FlgT were selected and subjected to local refinement using C1 symmetry. This approach yielded focused reconstructions of the FlgT and FlgP regions at 3.75 Å resolution (177,296 particles, EMD-72891), in the disassembled, close state (Supplementary Fig. 2f, g) and 3.54 Å in the assembled, open state (166,525 particles, EMD-76767). Compare the H-ring components (FlgT, FlgP and FlgO) at assembled and disassembled states, their conformations are identical at these two states, so we only showed one conformation at closed state. The H-ring at assembled state was further focused classified to evaluate its plasticity with OM (Supplementary Fig. 2h, j). T-ring is located below the H-ring and P-ring. To resolve its structure, we shifted the center to the T-ring region and re-extracted the particles using a box size of 720 pixels, binned to 512 pixels. Symmetry expansion was performed with C26, followed by focused 3D classification on the T-ring and PomBC region. This enabled separation of the C13-symmetric MotX dimers, after which local refinement was performed on the T-ring region. In the assembled state, PomBC-like dimers are clearly visualized. In contrast, in the disassembled state, only weak densities are observed below MotX, which are too fuzzy for reliable structural assignment. Final local refinement of the T-ring showed a global resolution of 3.60 Å for the assembled, open state (53,628 particles, EMD-72947) with C13 symmetry, and 3.57 Å for the disassembled, closed state (25,553 particles, EMD-72847) (Supplementary Fig. 2k-p).

For particles exhibiting PomBC and C13-symmetric MotX configurations, additional local refinement was performed to resolve the PomBC–MotX interaction. Particles were re-centered on a single PomBC–MotX bundle and re-extracted. Local refinements were conducted using a rotational search range of 4° and a translational search range of 2 Å. Due to the high flexibility of PomBC, the final local refinement is only reached to 4.98 Å resolution (697,164 particles, EMD-72848). To further validate these observations, genetic mutations were introduced. Further 3D classifications were conducted extensively to characterize its structural plasticity, with a focused mask on the T-ring-PomBC region (Supplementary Fig. 2q–s).

The central rotating components of the flagellar motor were also resolved. The RBM3 domain of FliF in MS-ring is separated by 3D classification that focused on MS-ring region with C34-symmetry based on the coordinates of LP-ring particles. Local refinement of MS-ring region improved the resolution to 3.75 Å with 44,428 particles (EMD-72840). Partial rod and hook region were solved with C1 symmetry (Supplementary Fig. 3). The LP-ring particles were symmetry expanded with C26 and then focus-classified on the rod region. One class exhibited prominent rod density. Subsequent rounds of local refinement allowed us to resolve the rod and hook structure of FlgG and FlgE at 3.44 Å resolution with 77,446 particles (EMD-72849). The local resolution of the central helix region of the rod and hook is relatively reliable, whereas the peripheral region appears fuzzy, likely due to particle orientation bias. Signal-to-noise ratio iterative reconstruction method was further used to improve the distortion of our cryo-EM maps54. After resolving the rod density, focused 3D classification was performed on the LP-ring to assess the relative rotation or sliding of FOMC with respect to the rod (Supplementary Fig. 13). Two conformations showing striking upward (EMD-72963) and downward (EMD-72961) displacement of the whole FOMC were identified, along with two other conformations in which the LP ring exhibited ~7° rotation while the rod remained stable. All cryo-EM maps were further processed using the deep learning-based refinement algorithm DeepEMhancer55.

Cryo-ET and subtomogram averaging

Frozen-hydrated specimens of ΔflhG V. cholerae cells were imaged on a 300 kV Titan Krios electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a field emission gun and a K3 Summit direct electron detector (Gatan). Tilt series were acquired using SerialEM52 in conjunction with the FastTomo script56, employing a dose-symmetric scheme. Imaging was conducted at a defocus of –4.8 µm, with a cumulative electron dose of ~70 e−/Å2 distributed evenly across 33 tilts, ranging from –48o to +48o in 3o increments. Raw frames were corrected for beam-induced motion using MotionCorr253, followed by tilt alignment and stacking in IMOD57. Tomographic reconstructions were generated from 8× binned tilt series using Tomo3D58. Initial subtomogram extraction was performed on 8× binned tomograms, focusing on flagellar motors. Subsequent 3D alignment and classification were conducted using the i3 software package59,60 to refine particle coordinates and eliminate low-quality particles. Final subtomogram extraction was carried out on unbinned tomograms based on the refined positions, and further subtomogram averaging was conducted at bin4. A total of 112 tilt series were acquired for the ∆flhGflgI∆gateI mutant, 84 for the ∆flhGflgI∆gateII mutant, and 151 for the ∆flhG strain. A total of 273 tilt series were collected from wild-type cells under nutrient-rich condition (LB medium) and 81 tilt series for nutrient-deplete condition (M9 medium) (Supplementary Table 3).

Modeling

The proteins of FlgH, FlgI, FlgT, FlgP, FlgO, MotY, MotX, PomB, FlgG, FlgE and FliF were applied to AlphaFold361 to generate structural prediction (Supplementary Fig. 15). The predicted models were fitted into the cryo-EM map using ChimeraX62 followed by interactive manual adjustment in Coot63 and real-space refinement in Phenix64. Final models as assessed by Phenix validation tools (Supplementary Table 1).

In detail, both assembly and disassembly states using 2.69 Å (PDB: 9YDO) and 2.75 Å (PDB: 9YDT) density maps (PDB: 9YDO) including 26 FlgH, 26 FlgI, 26 FlgT, 26 MotY, and 52 C-terminal segments of FlgP (residues 131–145) within the LPTH ring.

As the open and closed conformations of FlgO and FlgP are highly overall similar, only the open-state model was built and deposited. The models of 58 FlgO and 58 FlgP subunits were built from the 3.45 Å density map focused on the H-ring region in the open state (PDB: 9YDS). As for the T-ring, models were built from the 3.60 Å open-state map (PDB: 9YH0) and the 3.57 Å closed-state map (PDB: 9YEC), including 26 FlgH, 26 FlgI, 26 FlgT, 26 MotY, 26 MotX, and 52 C-terminal segments of FlgP (residues 131–145).Moreover, 27 FlgG, 5 FlgF, and 17 FlgE subunits were modeled from the 3.44 Å density map as described above (PDB: 9YEE). The RBM3 domain of 34 FliF subunits was modeled based on the 3.75 Å MS-ring map (PDB: 9YDX). A PomBC dimer, along with two MotX and two MotY subunits, was modeled from the 4.98 Å map (PDB: 9YED), with interpretation limited to backbone tracing due to resolution constraints.

The complete closed-state FOMC model was assembled based on a C1-symmetric FlgT/P density map (PDB: 9YFG). In addition, the EMReady2-refined composite map of the complete closed-state FOMC was deposited. Due to the use of a composite density map, no further model refinement was performed.

For the complete open-state FOMC, two composite models representing the upward (PDB: 9YH7; EMD-72963) and downward (PDB: 9YH6; EMD-72961) conformations were constructed. The assignment of upward and downward states was guided jointly by 3D classifications of the LP-ring (Supplementary Fig. 13c, d) and H-ring conformations (Supplementary Fig. 2i, j). The interface between C26 FlgT and C58 FlgO/P was defined using the C1-symmetric FlgT/P density map (EMD-76767). The EMReady2-refined composite maps of the complete open-state FOMC in both conformations were also deposited.

Sequence alignments were performed using Clustal Omega65. ChimeraX62 was used for cryo-EM map segmentation, visualization, and molecular surface rendering. Both ChimeraX62 and Blender 4.4 were used for animation.

Motility assays

Soft agar migration assays were conducted using LB medium containing 0.3% Bacto-Agar. Plates were prepared by pouring 150 mL of the medium into 150 × 15 mm petri dishes and left to dry at room temperature for a minimum of 24 h before use. Overnight cultures grown on LB agar at 30 °C were inoculated onto the motility plates by stabbing and incubated at 30 °C for 16 h. Migration zones were imaged using a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA), and migration diameters were normalized to wild type included on the same plate. Each experiment was performed with six biological replicates, and statistical analysis was carried out using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc tests in GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

Swimming behavior was assessed with slight modifications to a previously established method66. Overnight cultures were grown in LB medium at 30 °C for 16 h. These cultures were then diluted 1:10 in fresh LB medium to obtain an appropriate cell density for imaging. A 50 µL aliquot of the diluted culture was placed on a concave microscope slide (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and covered with an 18 × 18 mm coverslip. Imaging was performed on an AxioImager Z2 Widefield Microscope (Zeiss) using a ×20 objective lens. The focal plane was adjusted to approximately 50 µm below the coverslip, and time-lapse images were acquired at 54 frames per second over a 20-s interval. Cell trajectories were analyzed using the TrackMate plugin in ImageJ. Trajectories lasting between 0.2 and 2 s were included in the analysis, and mean swimming speed was calculated in µm/second. Each experiment was independently performed three times (n = 3 biological replicates), with at least 60 trajectories analyzed per biological replicate. For each biological replicate, the mean swimming speed was calculated from all tracked trajectories and used for statistical analysis. Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test in GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software). Exact P values are provided in the Source Data file.

Western blot

Whole cell lysates were prepared from the strains grown for 16 h in LB liquid medium. Cells were collected by centrifugation, and the pellets were resuspended in 2% (w/v) SDS, followed by heating at 95 °C for 10 min. After centrifugation to remove debris, protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce, Rockford, IL). Equal amounts of protein (40 µg) were loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels, separated by electrophoresis, and transferred to PVDF membranes using a semi-dry transfer apparatus (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) nonfat dry milk and probed with a monoclonal anti-FLAG antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) diluted 1:2,500. In parallel, the same samples were resolved on a separate SDS-PAGE gel and transferred under identical conditions, then probed with a mouse monoclonal antibody against the RNA polymerase α subunit (RpoA) (clone 4RA2, BioLegend, San Diego, CA) diluted 1:2,500. The RpoA immunoblot was used as a sample processing control. Protein bands were visualized by chemiluminescence using SuperSignal West Pico substrate (Pierce) and imaged with a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Band intensity was also quantified using the same imaging system.

Flagella staining and fluorescence imaging

Flagella were stained using an aldehyde-fixable membrane dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with minor modifications. Overnight cultures grown in LB medium were harvested by centrifugation (2000 × g, 5 min) and resuspended in 5 µg/mL FM1-43FX. After 1 min incubation on ice, formaldehyde was added to a final concentration of 4%, followed by an additional 10-min incubation on ice. Cells were pelleted again (2000g, 5 min) and resuspended in HBSS (Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution; 140 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM KCl, 0.03 mM Na2HPO4, 0.04 mM KH2PO4, 0.4 mM NaHCO3). Suspensions were mounted on 1% agarose pads and imaged using an AxioImager Z2 Widefield Microscope (Zeiss) with a 100× objective lens and the 38 HE eGFP filter set (excitation 450–490 nm, emission 500–550 nm) using an Axiocam 506 mono camera (Zeiss, 200 ms exposure time, depth of focus 0.90 μm, binning 1 × 1).

Cell labeling

Wild-type cells were imaged using confocal microscopy to observe the flagella shedding process. Cells from the frozen stock were grown shaking overnight at 30 °C in LB medium. Cells were then back-diluted 1:100 in M9 media (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 0.5% Glucose and 0.25% Casamino acids (CAAs) (BD), and grown shaking for 4–5 h at 37 °C. Cells (1, 2 and 3 µL) were pipetted onto the carbon side of gold EM-grids using cut pipette tips (to preserve the filaments). The grids were then inverted and placed on a glass bottom 96-well plate and topped with 100 µL of M9 media (no carbon source). FM1-43 membrane dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to each well to a final concentration of 5 µg/mL and mixed well using a cut pipette tip.

Confocal microscopy imaging

Fluorescence microscopy was performed using a Yokogawa W1 confocal scanner unit connected to a Nikon Ti2-E inverted microscope with a Perfect Focus System. Cells were excited by a laser at 488 nm with the corresponding filter. All fluorescent signals were recorded by a scientific complementary metal-oxide semiconductor camera (sCMOS, Hamamatsu). Confocal images were taken using a ×100 silicone oil objective (Lambda S 100XC Sil numerical aperture = 1.35). Each field of view was 130 µm × 130 µm; 12-14 locations were imaged for each experiment as technical replicates. During time course, imaging was performed every 5 min, overnight (10-12 h). Cells were incubated during imaging at 27 °C using an incubation chamber. Minimal photobleaching was observed under specific imaging condition. All images presented are raw images rendered with Nikon NIS-Elements.

Growth inhibition assay

Overnight cultures were grown in 5 mL LB medium at 30 °C. The cultures were then diluted into 25 mL fresh LB medium to an initial OD600 of approximately 0.01and grown to mid-log phase (OD600 ≈ 0.3). Subsequently, 1 mL of each culture was transferred to 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes and treated with vancomycin (0 or 150 μg/mL) or choline chloride (0 or 200 μM), as indicated. Treated cultures were immediately aliquoted into a 96-well plate (200 μL per well, in triplicate). Bacterial growth was monitored by measuring OD600 every 15 min using a PerkinElmer Victor3 multilabel counter (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA).
