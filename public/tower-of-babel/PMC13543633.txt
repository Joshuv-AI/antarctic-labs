A stratified urine-based molecular diagnostic and prognostic model for non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer management
Authors: Liqi Yi, Zhiyang Ma, Jianhua Zhang, Ying Zhan, Hanqing Xiao, Kaihan Dai, Yulai Liu, Yi Gao, Xiaoqun Yang, Dandan Cao, Danfeng Xu, Hai Huang

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) is characterized by a high recurrence rate requiring lifelong cystoscopic surveillance. Existing urine-based molecular assays mainly rely on mutations or methylation, which fail to capture large-scale genomic instability. Copy number variation (CNV) profiling offers complementary information on tumor evolution and aggressiveness, but its application in urinary diagnosis remains limited. We aimed to integrate CNV and DNA methylation signals from urinary DNA to establish a noninvasive and biologically informed stratified diagnostic model for NMIBC recurrence surveillance and risk stratification.MethodsUrine samples were prospectively collected from 91 patients (75 evaluable) between June 2021 and August 2023. Shallow whole-genome sequencing (sWGS) was used to detect CNVs at chromosomal arm and focal gene levels, while ONECUT2 promoter methylation was quantified by qPCR. Diagnostic and prognostic performance was evaluated by ROC analysis, Kaplan–Meier survival, and stratified recurrence assessment.ResultsWe evaluated a stratified diagnostic model combining CNV and ONECUT2 methylation testing in a cohort of 79 patients. CNV analysis alone showed high specificity (0.923) for NMIBC diagnosis. A combined model, using CNV as an initial screen followed by ONECUT2 methylation testing in CNV-positive cases, achieved a sensitivity of 0.783, specificity of 0.981, and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 0.911. This approach reduced the number of required ONECUT2 tests by 35% and identified a high proportion of true-negative patients (98.1%), which may help reduce unnecessary cystoscopy procedures. The model also demonstrated significant prognostic value, with the molecularly defined high-risk group showing significantly shorter recurrence-free survival (RFS) than the low-risk group (median RFS: 4.33 months vs. not reached; p < 0.001). Additional, in patients with initially negative cystoscopy after urine sample collection, the model demonstrated a predictive accuracy of 0.922 for recurrence, with molecular positivity observed a median of 9.6 months prior to clinical diagnosis.ConclusionsIntegrating CNV and DNA methylation profiling from urinary DNA provides a powerful and noninvasive molecular framework for NMIBC surveillance. By combining early epigenetic changes with genomic instability signals, this approach enhances recurrence risk assessment and enables earlier detection compared with conventional cystoscopy. Background

Bladder cancer is one of the most common malignancies of the urinary tract worldwide, with an estimated 573,000 new cases and 212,000 deaths annually, making it the 10th most prevalent cancer globally [1]. Approximately 70–75% of patients are diagnosed with non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) at diagnosis [2]. Despite appropriate treatment, including transurethral resection of the bladder tumor (TURBT) and adjuvant intravesical therapy, NMIBC is characterized by a high recurrence rate, with 50–70% of patients experiencing disease relapse and up to 20% progressing to muscle-invasive disease during long-term follow-up [3, 4]. Because of this high propensity for recurrence, patients require lifelong surveillance, typically involving frequent cystoscopy and urine cytology examinations. This results in a substantial and sustained burden for both patients and healthcare systems, as bladder cancer remains one of the most expensive malignancies to manage on a per-patient basis [5]. Improving recurrence monitoring and optimizing surveillance strategies are therefore key priorities in NMIBC management.

Current NMIBC surveillance is primarily based on cystoscopy and urine cytology. Although cystoscopy remains the gold standard, its invasiveness, high cost, and the need for lifelong repeated procedures impose a considerable burden on patients and healthcare systems [2, 5]. Moreover, flat or small lesions such as carcinoma in situ can be missed, potentially delaying diagnosis [6]. Urine cytology provides a noninvasive alternative but is limited by its low sensitivity for low-grade disease, typically around 16% to 84%, despite high specificity [7]. Several urine-based biomarker assays, including NMP22, UroVysion FISH, and BTA, have been introduced to complement cystoscopy. However, their only moderate diagnostic performance has prevented them from replacing cystoscopy in routine surveillance [2, 8]. These limitations highlight the urgent need for a sensitive, noninvasive, and reliable molecular diagnostic strategy to detect recurrence earlier and reduce reliance on repeated invasive procedures.

Urinary DNA testing has emerged as an important focus in bladder cancer surveillance because it captures tumor-derived molecular signals, including somatic mutations, DNA methylation changes, and structural genomic alterations. To date, most urinary DNA-based assays have focused on point mutations, methylation biomarkers, or combined mutation-methylation strategies, which have demonstrated promising performance for both diagnosis and disease monitoring [9–12]. Our previous study further supported this approach by showing that combining mutation and methylation biomarkers improved the detection and risk stratification of NMIBC recurrence [11]. However, these approaches generally do not fully leverage large-scale genomic instability signals such as focal and arm-level copy number variations (CNVs). Although CNV analysis has shown significant clinical utility in cfDNA from several malignancies, its application in NMIBC remains limited. In bladder cancer, genomic studies have shown that copy number variation (CNV) is one of the most frequent molecular alterations, with loss at 9p21 (CDKN2A) and gains at 1q, 5p, and 8q representing the most characteristic arm-level CNVs, which are strongly associated with tumor development, progression, and poor prognosis [13, 14]. In addition, several focal CNVs involving key oncogenic drivers—such as E2F3, MYC, CCND1, ERBB2, and CCNE1 have been recurrently reported [13]. Notably, Soave et al. detected amplifications of these genes in primary tumors, lymph node metastases, and matched serum cfDNA, demonstrating a high degree of concordance between tissue and liquid biopsy [15]. These findings highlight CNV profiling as an important strategy within liquid biopsy, with potential clinical implications. A few exploratory studies have attempted to apply CNV detection to urinary DNA [16], but these efforts have been limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous methodologies, and a lack of prospective validation, highlighting a critical evidence gap that warrants further clinical investigation.

Although preliminary studies have explored the use of CNV profiling in urinary cfDNA to capture molecular alterations associated with bladder cancer, its clinical utility in NMIBC surveillance and recurrence risk prediction remains largely unestablished. Existing work has primarily focused on demonstrating methodological feasibility with limited sample sizes and a lack of prospective validation. Meanwhile, urinary DNA methylation assays have demonstrated robust sensitivity and stability, but single-modality approaches may not fully reflect the underlying genomic complexity of tumors. Therefore, we aimed to integrate CNV profiling with DNA methylation analysis to improve the sensitivity and accuracy of molecular monitoring for recurrence risk stratification in patients with NMIBC.

Methods

Study participants

A total of 91 patients were enrolled from June 2021 to August 2023 from the main center Ruijin Hospital Shanghai Jiaotong University School of Medicine. Patients underwent imaging examinations, including ultrasonography, CT urography, magnetic resonance urography, and cystoscopy. Clinical diagnoses and recurrence were defined by cystoscopy and confirmed with pathological test.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Ruijin Hospital. The trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04994197). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study.

Patients were included if they presented with hematuria or were previously diagnosed with bladder cancer, were ≥ 18 years old, were able to given written informed consent and provided a minimum of 100 mL urine before cystoscopy. Exclusion criteria were inadequate urine samples, failure of the sWGS test, and a previous diagnosis of MIBC or other urinary tract cancers. Patients in the follow-up cohort were included if a conclusive recurrence status was obtained within the follow-up period, and those lost to follow-up were excluded.

Sample collection and DNA extraction

Prior to cystoscopy, 100 mL of urine was collected from each patient. Urine samples were centrifuged at 2000 g for 10 min within 24 h. Urinary sediment was washed once with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stored at -80 °C until DNA extraction. The QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (250) (Cat. # 51306, QIAGEN, Germany) was used to extract DNA from the urinary sediment. The Quant-iT dsDNA High-Sensitivity Assay Kit with the Qubit Fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA) was then used to quantify the extracted DNA.

sWGS library preparation and sequencing

Approximately 50-100ng genomic DNA was extracted and fragmented using Covaris M220 (150 bp to 200 bp fragment size). The fragmented DNA was end-repaired and extended with an ‘A’ nucleotide at the 3’end. After PCR amplification and purification, the library was prepared using the Hieff NGS OnePotTM II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Cat. #12204ES96, Yeasen, China). Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was performed on the purified libraries using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer at Genetron Health, China.

Trimmomatic (v. 0.33) [17] was utilized to filter out low-quality reads and adapters. The remaining clean reads were mapped to the human reference genome hg19 (UCSC) using bwa-mem (0.7.12-r1039) [18–20]. Duplicate reads were marked and removed using Picard (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). Indel-based realignment and base quality recalibration were performed using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK, v. 3.8) [21]. The mapping rates, coverage and duplicate rates were calculated with Qualimap (v. 2.2.1) [22].

Copy number variations analysis

Chromosomal arm-level CNV analysis

The statues of each chromosome arm were analyzed based on our experiences and previous reports [23–25]. Briefly, we generated a wig file with read counts in 500-kb windows across the genome using HMMcopy [26]. Copy number variations (CNVs) and tumor fraction (TF) were detected using ichorCNA [27] with default parameters. The status of bin was judged according to whether its log2 ratio(logR) is bigger than 0.1 (gain) or smaller than − 0.1(loss), respectively. Then the proportion of gain, p, was the total length of bins with gain divided by the whole arm length. The proportion of loss, q, was calculated in the same way. Finally, a chromosome arm CNV status was regarded as gained if p was bigger than 0.7, deleted if q was bigger than 0.7, or neutral if both p and q were smaller than 0.7.

Focal gene-level analysis

Focal gene amplifications were identified according to established methodologies from previous reports and our experiences [24, 25, 28], utilizing logR values and corrected copy number data. A focal amplification was defined as meeting all of the following criteria:

Corrected copy number ≥ 5 for the bin encompassing the gene;

Corrected copy number ≥ 5 for the bin encompassing the gene;

LogR value > 0.2 for the bin encompassing the gene;

LogR value > 0.2 for the bin encompassing the gene;

At least one of the four upstream and downstream bins adjacent to the gene has a copy number ≥ 5;

At least one of the four upstream and downstream bins adjacent to the gene has a copy number ≥ 5;

The length of the segment containing the gene is less than 70% of the entire chromosomal arm.

The length of the segment containing the gene is less than 70% of the entire chromosomal arm.

Selection of candidate molecular markers

Chromosomal arm-level CNVs

Arm-level copy number variation (CNV) analysis was performed on a dataset comprising 289 tumor and 360 normal samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas Bladder Cancer (TCGA-BLCA) cohort, utilizing a consistent methodological framework. Chromosomal arms were selected as candidates based on a two-step filtration process. First, arms demonstrating a statistically significant difference in CNV profile between tumor and normal samples (chi-square test, p < 0.05) were identified. Second, collinearity between candidate arms was assessed using the kappa statistic, with arms exhibiting high collinearity (kappa > 0.5) being excluded. Finally, a total of 34 chromosome arms were retained.

Focal gene-level CNVs

For focal gene CNV analysis, 216 tumor samples from the TCGA-BLCA dataset were interrogated. An initial candidate genes set was derived from the IntOGen (https://www.intogen.org). This set was subsequently filtered through sequential filtration: genes with a mutation frequency below 10% were removed; those demonstrating high pairwise collinearity (kappa > 0.6) was excluded; and genes co-located within the same genomic bin according to ichorCNA segmentation were consolidated to avoid redundancy. Finally, one internally identified gene (PTPRB) in our cohort was added after applying the same quality controls. This process yielded a final selection of 21 focal genes for downstream analysis.

Methylation detection of ONECUT2

The methylation status of the ONECUT2 promoter was detected according to a previous report [11].

Briefly, DNA was bisulfite-converted and purified according to the manufacturer’s instructions (EZ DNA Methylation-LightningTM Kit, ZYMO, D5031). The methylation status of ONECUT2 was detected by qPCR using a CFX96 system (Bio-Rad, USA) with the following cycling conditions: 95 °C for 3 min, 45 cycles (95 °C for 15s, 60 °C for 45s). A delta Ct ≥ 8 was considered positive for the presence of methylation, while a negative result was indicated by a lower delta Ct value. The primers used are listed in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Diagnostic accuracy was evaluated via its sensitivity, specificity, area under receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUC), positive Predictive Value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) with 95% Confidence Interval (CIs). Recurrence-free survival (RFS) was defined as the time from urine sample collection to the first pathologically verified recurrence. Survival curves were estimated by the Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank test was used to assess the statistical significance between different groups. Comparisons between two groups were made with Chi-square test to assess statistical significance. Kappa values were used to assess the agreement between the alteration of genes and chromosome arms with irr packages in R. A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All the statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.0.2) (https://cran.r-project.org/).

Results

Workflow of the study

The cohort construction process is illustrated in Fig. 1, with corresponding clinical characteristics summarized in Table 1. Initially, 91 patients were enrolled, comprising eight individuals presenting with hematuria and 83 with a history of recurrent non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC). Six cases were subsequently excluded due to inadequate DNA quality, and an additional ten were lost to follow-up, yielding a final analysis cohort of 75 patients. Within this cohort, 53 patients were male and 56 were over 60 years of age. During the follow-up period, cystoscopy and histopathology confirmed NMIBC in 23 patients, 60.87% (N = 14) of whom had high-grade tumors. CNV as a promising molecular biomarker for NMIBC diagnosis

A total of 34 chromosomal arms and 21 candidate focal genes were selected for CNV analysis. A sample was classified as CNV-positive if a CNV alteration was detected in any of the selected chromosomal arms or focal genes. Conversely, a sample was considered CNV-negative only when all assessed arms and focal genes showed no evidence of CNV. In our cohort, the top five chromosomal arms with the highest frequency of amplification were 8q (12%), 3q (9.3%), 1q (6.67%), 18p (6.67%), and 21q (6.67%); the arms most frequently deleted were 17p (14.67%), 8p (12.0%), 9q (10.67%), 9p (9.33%), and 11p (8.00%) (Fig. 2B). Analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data revealed a concordant pattern, albeit with higher frequencies (Student’s t-test, P < 0.001, Additional file 1: Fig.S1A). Similarly, the focal genes with the highest alteration frequencies in our cohort were UBR5 (8%), SDHC (6.67%), PTPRB (5.33%), DEK (4.00%), and XPC (4%) (Fig. 2B). TCGA data analysis confirmed a similar pattern with higher frequencies (Student’s t-test, P < 0.001, Additional file 1: Fig.S1B). Notably, the focal amplification of UBR5 and SDHC, as well as the gain of their respective chromosomal arm 8q and 1q, are significantly associated with poorer prognosis, indicating that amplifications/gain of these two genes and chromosomal arm synergistically promote tumor initiation and recurrence. (Figure 2C and F, Additional file 1: Table S3).

To evaluate the diagnostic contributions of chromosomal arm CNV, focal gene CNV, and ONECUT2 methylation to bladder cancer diagnosis, each molecular marker was analyzed separately. ONECUT2 methylation alone demonstrated a sensitivity of 0.87 and a specificity of 0.731. Diagnosis based solely on chromosomal arm CNV markers yielded a sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of 0.739, 0.923, 0.81 and 0.889, respectively. The integration of focal gene CNV increased the sensitivity to 0.783 (Fig. 2C). Analysis of the TCGA dataset corroborated these findings; diagnosis using chromosomal arm CNV markers alone achieved a sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of 0.938, 0.997, 0.996 and 0.952, respectively, which improved to 0.994 after incorporating focal gene CNV (Additional file 1: Table S4). No significant associations were observed between CNV status and patient age (Student’s t-test, P = 0.72) or gender (Chi-square test, P = 0.94).

Stratified diagnostic model for NMIBC patients

We next investigated whether an integrated approach combining CNV and ONECUT2 methylation could improve diagnostic accuracy. This integrated approach achieved a sensitivity of 0.783 and a notably high specificity of 0.981. Although, the sensitivity is lower than that of ONECUT2 methylation alone, the integrated approach achieves a superior balance by drastically reducing the number of false positives, as reflected in the high specificity and PPV (0.911) (Fig. 3A and C). A principal advantage of this stratified model is its cost-effectiveness; utilizing CNV for initial screening reduces the number of patients requiring subsequent ONECUT2 methylation testing (from 34 to 22) without compromising diagnostic performance, thereby lowering overall assay costs (Fig. 3A, Additional file 1: Fig.S2). Furthermore, the model identified a high proportion of true-negative patients (98.1%), suggesting its potential utility in reducing unnecessary cystoscopy procedures, although this requires validation in prospective clinical settings. Based on these findings, we propose a stratified diagnostic model for NMIBC that utilizes CNV as a first-line molecular marker, followed by ONECUT2 methylation for triage (Fig. 3D). In this workflow, urine samples were first subjected to sWGS for CNV detection. CNV-negative patients are classified as low-risk group, who may be considered for less intensive surveillance strategies. CNV-positive patients undergo subsequent ONECUT2 methylation testing. Patients with positive status for both markers are classified as high-risk group, suggesting a higher likelihood of recurrence and potentially warranting closer surveillance or further clinical evaluation. In contrast, patients negative for methylation are classified as low-risk, for whom routine follow-up may be appropriate. The clinical implementation of this workflow requires further validation in prospective studies.

Additionally, we observed one discordant case in a patient positive with CNV, ONECUT2 methylation, and a TERTp mutation (39.53%) who exhibited no clinical recurrence over 25 months of follow-up, potentially indicating the possibility of a truly false positive, or the bias of the lesion during sampling, or an insufficient follow-up period (Fig. 3E). Conversely, five patients with clinical recurrence lacked detectable molecular markers, likely due to low tumor fraction (Additional file 1: Table S5). Among these, one received an initial diagnosis of low-grade papillary urothelial carcinoma upon sampling prior to Transurethral Resection of Bladder Tumor (TURBT). Three subsequent cases were diagnosed with low-grade NMIBC at 12.2, 18.8 and 20.1 months during follow-up period., respectively. One was diagnosed with high-grade NMIBC at 3.6 months.

We further assessed the prognostic value of this model. Recurrence-free survival (RFS), defined as the time from urine sample collection to the first pathologically verified recurrence, was significantly shorter in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group (median RFS: 4.33 months [range: 0-11.1] versus not reached; log-rank test, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3F). A similarly significant difference was observed between patients with and without clinically positive findings (median 8.4-months [range: 3.67–18.8] vs. not reached; p < 0.001).

Performance of the stratified model for NMIBC diagnosis

Seventy-five urine samples were collected prior to cystoscopy. Among them, 17 were cystoscopy-positive, with 11 (11/17) were subsequently confirmed by pathology. 81.81% (9/11) were classified correctly as high-risk by the stratified diagnostic approach. The sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV of the stratified diagnosis for bladder cancer were 0.818, 0.844, 0.474 and 0.964, respectively, indicating its effectiveness and potential to preclude cystoscopy in a substantial proportion (81.8%) (9/11) of patients (Table 2).

Clinical performance of stratified model in NMIBC diagnosis

Notably, in the subgroup of eight patients presenting with initial hematuria, 4/8 were diagnosed as positive by cystoscopy, 3/4 were confirmed positive by pathology, and 3/4 were classified as high-risk by the stratified diagnostic approach. Notably, one patient with a negative cystoscopy status was identified as positive through the stratified method and later confirmed as pathological positive 11 months thereafter, suggesting that this stratified diagnostic strategy has potential for early detection in patients with initial hematuria.

The model also demonstrated utility in risk stratification by tumor grade. Among 11 patients with NMIBC, two of three with low-grade and seven of eight with high-grade disease were correctly classified as high-risk.

Clinical significance of stratified model in recurrence prediction

In the 64 patients with an initial negative cystoscopy after urine sample collection, the median follow-up time was 24.750 months (IQR = 23.167–40.592). Thirteen cases eventually experienced recurrence, with a median time to recurrence of 9.633 months (IQR = 7.733–13.800). Among these, nine had been stratified as high-risk by the model at the initial urine collection, with a median interval of 9.6 months (IQR = 7.333–11.067) between molecular positivity and clinically confirmed recurrence. This suggests that molecular signals may be detectable prior to clinical diagnosis (Fig. 4A, Additional file 1: Fig.S3). The predictive accuracy for recurrence was 0.922, supporting risk stratification based on molecular profiling (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, the CNV alteration of chromosome arm 18p, 3q, 17q and 8p, are significantly associated with poorer prognosis, suggesting an association with tumor recurrence. (Fig. 4E and H). In one illustrative case, the model detected molecular evidence of a tumor prior to its detection by CT, IHC, PET-CT, and pathological confirmation, even in the context of lymph node and lung metastases (Fig. 4I and Additional file 1: Fig.S4).

The stratified approach demonstrated efficacy in predicting recurrence in early-stage patients. Analysis of the 13 recurrence cases showed that the model identified 1 of 3 low-grade and 7 of 8 high-grade patients as high-risk. Among the 9 high-risk patients who experienced recurrence, 1 had low-grade and 7 had high-grade disease (Fig. 4C-D).

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated a risk stratification model that integrates CNV and DNA methylation signals derived from urinary DNA for postoperative surveillance of NMIBC. This stratified approach demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.882 (95% CI, 0.793–0.970), an overall accuracy of 92.0% (95% CI, 0.834-1), a sensitivity of 78.3%, a specificity of 98.1%, a PPV of 94.7%, and an NPV of 91.1%. Although the PPV in our cohort was relatively high, it should be interpreted with caution given the limited sample size and the relatively small number of recurrence events, which may lead to overestimation of performance. Importantly, the consistently high NPV suggests that the model may be particularly useful as a rule-out tool to identify patients at low risk of recurrence. These results highlight its potential to complement or partially reduce the reliance on invasive cystoscopy for tumor diagnosis and recurrence monitoring. Beyond diagnostic performance, the model also exhibited strong prognostic value. Molecular positivity was observed a median of 9.6 months prior to clinical or radiological recurrence, and patients stratified as high risk had significantly shorter recurrence-free survival compared with those in the low-risk group (median 4.33 months vs. not reached; p < 0.001). Together, these findings suggest that CNV-methylation-based risk stratification can not only enhance recurrence detection but also may facilitate earlier molecular detection of recurrence, supporting individualized surveillance and timely intervention.

In prior urinary genomics studies, mutation-only and methylation-only assays have each shown substantial clinical promise: multi-gene urinary mutation panels achieved high overall sensitivity and excellent detection of high-grade disease in hematuria cohorts and NMIBC surveillance [29], while in treatment-related settings (e.g., BCG-unresponsive NMIBC), pre- and post-treatment urinary mutation status correlated with recurrence risk, underscoring prognostic utility [30]. Complementarily, methylation-only assays such as utMeMA demonstrated high diagnostic/monitoring accuracy in independent cohorts [31], and the widely used EpiCheck showed high specificity and negative predictive value in multicenter follow-up studies, supporting reduction of unnecessary cystoscopies [32]. Building on these advances, combined mutation-plus-methylation strategies have become the prevailing approach: prospective real-world data indicate high sensitivity and NPV with potential to decrease surveillance cystoscopy burden [11], although other work suggests that when mutation testing is already highly sensitive, the incremental benefit of adding methylation may be modest, highlighting the constraint of tumor DNA abundance in urine [33]. Against this backdrop, our study moves beyond the established “mutation ± methylation” paradigm by integrating CNV with methylation, aiming to capture genomic-instability signals and thereby further enhance risk stratification and earlier molecular detection of recurrence in NMIBC. To better understand the biological rationale underlying this combined strategy, we next examined the complementary roles of DNA methylation and CNV in bladder cancer development, tumor evolution, and recurrence risk stratification.

From a molecular perspective, methylation and CNV represent two key layers of bladder cancer development and tumor evolution. DNA methylation alterations typically occur during the early stages of tumor evolution, are closely associated with tumor suppressor gene silencing and early clonal formation, and exhibit high stability and sensitivity, enabling their detection even at low tumor burden [31]. In contrast, CNVs reflect genomic instability and tumor heterogeneity and have been linked to aggressive biological behavior in bladder cancer [13, 34, 35]. Approximately 15% of NMIBC tumors undergo whole-genome doubling (WGD), and this high CNV burden is strongly associated with increased risk of disease progression [34]. MIBC tumors accumulate even more chromosomal copy number alterations and exhibit greater genomic instability [36]. Specific CNV events play critical roles in tumor invasion and progression: for instance, amplification of the cell cycle regulator CCNE1 and deletion of the tumor suppressor CDKN2A are strongly associated with NMIBC-to-MIBC progression [37]. Notably, in our cohort, focal amplification of UBR5 and SDHC, together with gain of their respective chromosomal arms 8q and 1q, was significantly associated with recurrence and poorer prognosis, suggesting that focal and arm-level CNV alterations may contribute to recurrence-related risk stratification. Beyond their prognostic value, CNVs can also shape the tumor immune microenvironment and mediate immune escape. For example, PPARG amplification can drive immune evasion and reduce responsiveness to immunotherapy [38], whereas TRAF2 amplification induces PD-L1 overexpression and contributes to an immunosuppressive tumor milieu [39]. Taken together, methylation provides early and stable epigenetic signals, whereas CNVs capture structural genomic instability associated with tumor evolution and aggressive biological features. Integrating these two molecular dimensions enables complementary information to be captured across different stages of tumor evolution, providing a biological rationale for NMIBC recurrence risk stratification and early molecular surveillance.

Our findings suggest that a combined CNV-methylation molecular testing strategy holds potential clinical utility in NMIBC surveillance and recurrence monitoring. Compared with conventional cystoscopy and urine cytology, this noninvasive approach provides tumor-specific molecular information, enabling earlier identification of high-risk patients and more precise risk stratification. For low-risk patients with negative results, it may support extending cystoscopy intervals and reducing unnecessary invasive procedures, whereas high-risk or positive patients may benefit from intensified monitoring or early intervention, ultimately improving surveillance efficiency and optimizing resource allocation. In addition, urine sampling is simple, well-tolerated, and repeatable, making this approach particularly suitable for longitudinal disease monitoring in real-world clinical settings. Notably, this strategy also extends beyond recurrence surveillance. In the future, serial CNV and methylation monitoring could be applied to dynamically assess therapeutic response to intravesical therapy, immunotherapy, or antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), offering an objective and minimally invasive tool to track tumor burden and treatment efficacy. With further technical standardization, cost reduction, and prospective validation, this approach could complement existing clinical methods and enable more personalized and adaptive management of NMIBC. Despite these promising findings, several clinically relevant challenges emerged during the study, which warrant careful consideration.

In this study, a few patients exhibited discordance between molecular test results and clinical follow-up findings. Some patients showed positive methylation or CNV signals in a single postoperative test, while no recurrence was observed on subsequent cystoscopy or imaging. Such “false-positive” results may reflect molecular recurrence occurring before clinical detection or be influenced by local inflammation or benign lesions, suggesting that urinary molecular testing may provide an early warning signal but should always be interpreted within the clinical context. Conversely, several patients with clinical recurrence had negative test results, which may be related to low tumor burden, insufficient cellular shedding, or tumor heterogeneity—inherent limitations of current liquid biopsy approaches. Importantly, some cases showed non-overlapping positivity between methylation and CNV, with positivity restricted to one marker type. This pattern reinforces the biological complementarity of methylation and CNV detection and highlights the advantage of a combined strategy over single-marker testing in improving sensitivity and recurrence risk identification.

This study has several limitations. First, the overall sample size was relatively limited, and the number of recurrence events became even smaller after further stratification by recurrence status, tumor grade, and molecular subgroup. This limitation is particularly relevant to the gene- and chromosome arm-specific Kaplan-Meier analyses, in which statistically significant differences were observed but were based on a relatively small number of events. Therefore, these gene- and arm-specific associations with shorter RFS should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive prognostic effects. In particular, the limited number of events may have reduced the statistical power and robustness of some subgroup analyses, contributed to imprecision in certain certain estimates, as reflected by relatively wide confidence intervals for key diagnostic metrics, and restricted our ability to draw firm conclusions across clinically relevant subgroups. Second, this was a single-center study, which may introduce potential selection bias related to patient composition, local clinical practice, and follow-up patterns. In addition, the lack of an independent external validation cohort limits the generalizability and reproducibility of the current findings in broader NMIBC populations. Third, the detection strategy focused solely on methylation and CNV, without incorporating other molecular biomarkers such as mutation profiles, RNA expression, or immune signatures, thereby providing an incomplete view of the tumor molecular landscape. Future studies should aim to expand the cohort size, incorporate multi-omics data, and validate the model prospectively to build a more comprehensive and dynamic recurrence risk prediction system. In addition, large-scale clinical studies are warranted to confirm the utility of CNV-methylation combined testing and explore its role in optimizing NMIBC surveillance strategies in real-world settings.

Conclusions

In summary, this study proposes a molecular monitoring and risk stratification strategy for NMIBC based on the integration of CNV and DNA methylation signals. By combining early epigenetic alterations with recurrence-related structural genomic instability, this approach has the potential to enhance recurrence risk assessment and enable earlier molecular detection of disease. It provides a valuable complement to conventional cystoscopy-based monitoring and lays a conceptual foundation for more personalized and adaptive surveillance strategies in NMIBC.
