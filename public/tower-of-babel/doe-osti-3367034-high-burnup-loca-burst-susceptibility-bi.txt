 ABSTRACT

Accurately assessing high-burnup fuel behavior during loss-of-coolant accidents (LOCAs) is essential for
understanding fuel fragmentation, relocation, and dispersal (FFRD) risks across the US light-water reactor
fleet. This work updates previous Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS)
Program multiphysics LOCA analyses for a pressurized water reactor (PWR) and a boiling water reactor
(BWR) by incorporating recent model and material property advancements in the BISON fuel
performance code, including a high-burnup structure (HBS) model, revised cladding burst criteria, and
updated thermal–mechanical correlations. This update was needed to support ongoing industry initiatives
and upcoming regulatory changes. Full-core, rod-resolved operating histories generated using Virtual
Environment for Reactor Analysis (VERA) and system-level LOCA conditions obtained from TRACE
were applied to statistically representative rod samples in BISON to evaluate burst behavior and FFRD
susceptibility. These calculations used two cladding burst correlations and three fuel pulverization models
so that the predictions of these models could be compared.

The updated PWR simulations show markedly improved numerical stability as the number of crashed
simulations decreased by 95% compared to the previous study, and hence higher confidence in results.
The updated PWR simulations predicted cladding bursts exclusively among once-burned, high-power
rods, with two different cladding burst models identifying the same burst-susceptible population.
Resulting FFRD susceptibility estimates are significantly reduced compared with earlier studies, driven
by cooler predicted fuel and plenum temperatures, lower hoop strains, and reduced fission gas release in
the updated models. In contrast, none of the BWR rods were predicted to burst under either burst
criterion, reaffirming minimal BWR FFRD susceptibility even with updated HBS and material models.
Comparisons between the PWR and BWR end-of-cycle predictions are made. Comparison with prior
work highlights significant shifts in PWR fuel performance metrics and confirmation of earlier BWR
conclusions.

Overall, the updated results underscore the importance of having high-resolution detailed modeling
capability and continuously integrating evolving material models and physics into high-resolution
multiphysics simulations. The unified assessment presented here strengthens confidence in predicting
high-burnup LOCA behavior by improving agreement between different cladding burst correlations.
These results also provide an improved foundation for future BISON model development, FFRD
susceptibility calculations.

 1. INTRODUCTION

In response to the US nuclear industry attempting to increase fuel utilization in the existing light-water
reactor (LWR) fleet, the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) recently released a draft regulatory
guide outlining approaches to address fuel dispersal during loss-of-coolant accidents (LOCAs) [1]. The
guide requires reactor operators seeking high burnup fuel applications to predict whether fuel dispersal
would take place and, if so, the amount of fuel that could disperse during a postulated LOCA. Higher
burnup fuel applications are needed to achieve longer cycle operations and higher power uprates. To
support the nuclear energy industry in this effort, the Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation
(NEAMS) Program has developed a high-resolution, multiphysics framework to predict fuel
fragmentation, relocation, and dispersal (FFRD) in pressurized water reactors (PWRs) and boiling water
reactors (BWRs). FFRD can occur when two conditions are met. First, the cladding bursts due to elevated
stresses caused by a LOCA. Second, the fuel has fragmented due to mechanical and irradiation damage.
The finer the fuel fragments, the more they are able to move through the rod and escape through the
rupture in the cladding. Therefore, FFRD is a concern for higher fuel burnups where fuel fragmentation is
finer.

 1

The NEAMS Program develops a suite of high-resolution, multiphysics tools to support these needs for
the LWR fleet. These tools include the Virtual Environment for Reactor Applications (VERA) [2,3] and
the BISON fuel performance code [4]. When combined with the NRC thermal-hydraulics system code,
TRAC/RELAP Advanced Computational Engine (TRACE) [5], these codes enable detailed rod-resolved
predictions of operating conditions, thermomechanical behavior, and transient/accident response.
NEAMS has previously demonstrated an integrated multiphysics methodology for assessing FFRD for
postulated large-break LOCAs in a PWR utilizing high-burnup fuel [6–11]. This methodology provided a
framework for identifying FFRD-relevant conditions—including cladding ballooning, burst timing, and
burst-dependent fragmentation metrics—across full-core operating histories.

Building on this infrastructure, Oak Ridge National Laboratory and Idaho National Laboratory (INL)
began extending the methodology to BWRs, where operational characteristics, coolant flow regimes, and
transient response differ from those in PWRs [12–15]. This work began by developing BWR capabilities
in VERA and establishing a normal operations model in VERA and BISON [12]. This included selecting
a statistically representative sample of fuel rods. The LOCA model was added and analyzed later using
TRACE [15]. Together, these efforts established the foundation for evaluating FFRD susceptibility in
BWRs using high-resolution multiphysics simulations.

The present work expands on this foundation by reevaluating both PWR and BWR high-burnup LOCA
analyses using the latest BISON models and physics capabilities. BISON is constantly being updated, and
new capabilities are being added to incorporate capabilities for industry initiatives (such as accident
tolerant fuels) and regulatory changes (such as increased enrichment and high burnup rulemaking).
Several updates introduced since the earlier PWR and BWR publications—including revisions to cladding
burst criteria, new high-burnup structure (HBS) models, and updated material property correlations—
motivate a reassessment of prior conclusions. Additionally, this work brings both reactor types into a
single, unified analysis for the first time, enabling direct comparison of high-burnup LOCA response
across PWR and BWR designs.

The remainder of this report is organized as follows: Section 2 describes the multiphysics framework and
the NEAMS code suite used in this work, Section 3 summarizes the PWR and BWR reactor designs and
high-burnup operating histories, Section 4 details the BISON simulation setup and highlights the code and
model updates since previous studies, Section 5 presents the updated steady-state and transient simulation
results for both reactor types, Section 6 compares these new results to the earlier findings and discusses
the effects of updated models on FFRD susceptibility, and Section 7 provides conclusions and outlines
future work required to improve predictive capability and enable higher burnup operation. The
infrastructure developed will also supports the ongoing power uprate effort and Executive Order 14302
requirements.

 2. MULTIPHYSICS CODE COUPLING FRAMEWORK

The multiphysics framework is based on three codes and includes five steps. First, VERA is used to
predict the full-core, rod-resolved reactor behavior during normal operation. Second, VERA data are
ported to BISON as boundary and input conditions using VERA’s VERAOneWay (VOW) module [3].
These data include the linear heat generation rate (LHGR), cladding surface temperature, and coolant
pressure. BISON is used to analyze the normal-operation fuel performance of a statistically representative
sample of rods. Third, TRACE is used to model the postulated LOCA scenario. VERA data are again
used to inform the model. Fourth, TRACE data are appended to the BISON simulations to create fuel
performance simulations for LOCA simulations. Two cladding burst models are used to predict cladding
failure. Fifth, three pulverization correlations are applied, taking data on the axial location of the cladding

 2

burst and the rod’s burnup profile to predict the FFRD susceptibility. These predictions are then used to
calculate the mass of fuel that could be dispersed in the postulated LOCA. This is needed for dose
predictions for this condition.

The remainder of this section provides summaries of the three codes. However, because only BISON is
used in this work, the VERA and TRACE summaries are brief.

2.1 VERA

VERA is a full-core, rod-resolved multiphysics reactor analysis tool [2,3,16]. It includes modules for
neutron transport, thermal hydraulics, crud deposition, and other physical effects. One of these modules is
VOW, which ports VERA results into other codes using a template input file and key word syntax. VERA
was developed at ORNL under the Consortium for Advanced Simulation of Light Water Reactors
(CASL), which has since been incorporated into NEAMS. VERA was originally developed with an
emphasis on PWRs, but beginning in 2019, BWR capabilities were added.

2.2 TRACE

TRACE is a best-estimate thermal hydraulics systems code developed by the NRC [5,10]. It combines the
capabilities of prior systems codes TRAC-P, TRACE-B, RELAP5, and RAMONA into a single
modernized computational tool. It is suitable for both PWRs and BWRs and has an extensive validation
basis in a range of operating and accident conditions, including large-break LOCAs.

2.3 BISON

BISON is a finite-element-based fuel thermomechanics code maintained at INL and developed
collaboratively at a wide range of laboratories, universities, and private parties [4,17]. BISON is built on
the Multiphysics Object-Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE) [18]. MOOSE is an open-source
finite element code designed for easy code coupling and efficient scaling on high-performance computing
systems. MOOSE and BISON both rely on a modular framework that allows individual material models
to be easily swapped out. Because of this flexibility, BISON has capabilities for a wide range of fuels,
claddings, and reactor types, including PWR and BWR fuels and claddings, tri-structural isotropic
(TRISO) fuels, metallic fuels, and others [4].

Due to BISON’s collaborative development, updates are applied to the code in real time rather than
relying on release versions. Git [19] is used to facilitate this and a robust testing and verification suite is
used to ensure quality assurance. Therefore, it is important that BISON analyses be kept up to date with
the latest BISON changes.

BISON was used to model a large-break LOCA in a PWR from 2021 to 2023 [6–8], with an update in
2024 to include an updated transient fission gas release (FGR) model [11]. A similar analysis on a BWR
was done in 2025–2026 [15]. Moreover, the FGR capabilities have recently been refactored, validated,
and the recommended modeling options have been updated [20]. Therefore, while more time has passed
since the PWR analysis, BWRs are a more active area of current development, and both simulation sets
have new models and capabilities that can be incorporated into the current work.

 3. PREVIOUS STUDIES

The framework described in Section 2 has been applied to two reactors. The first is a Westinghouse four-
loop PWR rated for 3,626 MWth. The reactor model was based on an existing reactor, except that the
18 month operating cycle was replaced with a hypothetical 24-month, thrice-through cycle. The extended
cycle was developed by a reactor operating utility and was meant to study rod behaviors at burnups that
exceed the current limit of 62 MWd/kgU [8]. The core consists of 193 1717 assemblies, for 55,777
individual rods.

One-quarter of the core was modeled in VERA for 15 cycles to facilitate a transition from the 18 month
cycle 16, which was operated in the PWR, to the 24-month cycle followed by three cycles operating on
the 24 month cycle. When the results were transitioned to BISON, only rods with average burnups greater
than 62 MWd/kgU were considered since the fine fragmentation is expected beyond this burnup level.
This filtered out all fresh (in their first irradiation cycle) rods. A statistically representative sample of 753
rods were modeled in BISON during normal operation. From this group, 281 rods were selected for
detailed TRACE LOCA analysis. Due to simulation crashes, only 242 rods were evaluated for the LOCA
analysis in BISON: 178 once-burned (in their second cycle) and 64 twice-burned (in their third cycle).

Two cladding burst models were used to predict cladding failures: the Chapman correlation (CC) [21,22]
and the strain rate criterion (SRC) [23]. The CC uses an empirical correlation of cladding hoop stress and
heating rate to calculate a failure temperature and predicts burst if the actual cladding temperature
exceeds the burst temperature. The SRC predicts cladding burst if the hoop strain rate exceeds 100 h-1.
The CC predicted 182 burst rods: 177 once-burned and 5 twice-burned. The SRC predicted that all 178
once-burned rods and no twice-burned rods would burst.

The second reactor is a General Electric (GE) BWR-4 rated for 3,515 MWth. The core consists of 764 92-
rod assemblies, for 70,288 rods. Each assembly consists of a 1010 grid with two water rods that each
take up four rod slots. This unit operates on a 24-month thrice-through cycle. Unlike PWRs, BWRs
include multiple rod lengths in the core. The longest are referred to as full-length rods, and shorter rods
are called partial-length rods. Eight high-burnup lead use assemblies (HBLUAs) were included in the
model to include more high-burnup rods. The HBLUAs were modeled to be in the core for three cycles.

One-quarter of the core was modeled in VERA for three cycles, starting with an approximation of
equilibrium operation. This technique, while less computationally expensive than running extra cycles to
come to equilibrium, introduced some error since the burnup of many of the rods in the first cycle was
only approximated. Two of the quarter-core assemblies were HBLUAs. Five rods were randomly selected
from each of the 191 assemblies in the quarter core for BISON analysis, resulting in 955 rods. This set of
rods included full- and partial-length rods. Due to numerical problems that affected ten of them, only 945
rods ran to completion. All 945 rods were modeled in TRACE, and 942 of them ran to completion in the
BISON LOCA analysis.

The CC was altered prior to the BWR BISON LOCA analysis. The experimental data upon which the
model is based were all gathered at temperatures of 700°C or more. However, the correlation allows for
bursts to occur at lower temperatures. Therefore, a minimum burst temperature of 700°C was added to the
model to prevent it from straying beyond its valid temperature range. Neither the updated CC nor SRC
predicted any cladding bursts among the BWR rods, including in the HBLUAs.

 4. SIMULATION SETUP

Both the PWR and BWR simulations assume a large-break LOCA at the moment before shutdown begins
at the end of a normal operating cycle. Only rods present in the cycle of interest are included in the
studies. The PWR rods only include rods with end-of-cycle (EOC) rod average burnups greater than
62 MWd/kgU, which are once- and twice-burned rods. The BWR rods contain rods from the entire core,
regardless of composition, enrichment, length, or burnup.

The purpose of this work is to provide updated simulation results for the PWR and BWR FFRD
susceptibility studies. A comparison of the original and updated simulations is given in Table 1. The
largest changes made to both simulations were the inclusion of a recently updated high burnup structure
(HBS) model that calculates the fraction of the fuel that has converted to HBS [24] and the refactored
FGR model [20]. These were the only significant additions to the BWR model since its last iteration in
January 2026 [15]. The PWR model underwent more updates. First, more updates and improvements
have been implemented in BISON. Second, the PWR model now includes the updated CC model
discussed above. The updated PWR simulation also uses an updated plenum temperature model [4] and a
standardized initial grain size. The original initial grain size had typos in the input files that caused the
materials models to use inconsistent values. This has been fixed in the updated version. Finally, a
transient FGR model was added [25].

Some adjustments were made to make the two simulation sets’ modeling assumptions more consistent.
The PWR UO2 thermal conductivity model was changed from the Nuclear Fuel Industry Research (NFIR)
formulation [26] to the Ronchi et al. formulation [27] and the mechanical contact model was changed to a
kinematic formulation [4] to match the BWR simulations. For more information on the PWR and BWR
simulations, see the published works [8,15].

FFRD susceptibility was calculated by first assuming that spacer grids would block pulverized fuel from
traveling. Pulverized fuel between spacers was assumed to be susceptible so long as cladding failure
occurred between the spacers. This is demonstrated in Figure 1. A second assumption was that burst was
instantaneous. The burst region was taken exclusively from the timestep in which cladding burst first
occurred anywhere on the rod. Any expansion of the cladding burst region that occurred later in the
simulation was not included. For each axial location within the susceptible region, the local, radially

converted into a mass using the height of the elements, fuel initial radius, and fuel initial density.

 5. SIMULATION RESULTS

5.1 PRESSURIZED WATER REACTOR

Of the 281 high-burnup PWR rods analyzed, 279 BISON simulations ran to completion. These consisted
of 75 twice-burned rods and 204 once-burned rods. The simulations that crashed were both once-burned
rods. One crashed during normal operation, and the other crashed in the first 30 s of the transient. The
cause of the crashes appeared to be numerical convergence issues. This is a large improvement over the
39 crashed simulations previously recorded.

The rod average burnup distribution of the rods is provided in Figure 2, sorted by the number of cycles
the rods have been irradiated. Recall that only rods with burnups greater than 62 MWd/kgU were
included in this set of simulations. Twice-burned rods follow a uniform distribution, while once-burned
rods peak at about 67 MWd/kgU.

 Figure 2. PWR burnup distribution.

The CC and SRC each predicted that all 204 of the once-burned rods would burst, which is more
consistent than the previous simulations. However, they did not always predict burst at the same location
or at the same time. In 142 of the rods, the CC predicted failure before the SRC, while in 21 rods the SRC
predicted failure first. In 40 rods, the CC and SRC predicted failure at the same time. Individual rods’
FFRD susceptibilities are shown in box and whisker plots in Figure 3. The CC predicted larger cladding
burst regions that encompassed multiple spacer grid regions (57 rods compared with 19 for the SRC), so
the FFRD susceptibility covers a wider range than the SRC predictions. This behavior is not necessarily
physically accurate because spacer grids are not included in these simulations. It is reasonable to expect
that spacer grids may block bursts from continued expansion or reduce axial gas communication.

BISON predicts that all high-burnup once-burned rods will burst, regardless of the cladding burst
correlation. This opens a potential pathway to simplify future analyses by assuming that all once-burned
rods fail, assuming a failure location, and calculating the FFRD susceptibility without running BISON
simulations. The obvious values for predicting the failure location are the location of peak cladding
temperature and location of peak burnup. However, the location of peak cladding temperature is most
accurately predicted by fuel performance codes such as BISON. Therefore, the analysis was repeated
assuming burst would occur at the location of peak burnup. The NRC RIL 1mm model predicted
individual rod FFRD susceptibilities in the range of 0.14–0.25 kg, the NRC RIL 2mm model predicted
0.18–0.29 kg, and the Turnbull model predicted about 0.003 kg. These correspond to the top quartile of
the CC-based predictions. Therefore, cladding burst correlations are necessary to accurately predict burst
locations and calculate the FFRD susceptibilities.

Figure 6 shows the peak fuel temperature. Its normal operation behavior matches the LHGR.
Temperatures at EOC fall between the 400°C to 1,300°C range but begin dropping once the LOCA
begins. After about 10–20 s, the fuel temperatures begin rising again as the coolant can no longer remove
decay heat. Figure 7 shows the peak cladding temperature. The cladding temperature is very consistent
during normal operation (between 300°C and 400°C). However, once the LOCA begins the temperature
equalizes with the fuel temperature, causing it to rise. Once the cladding temperature exceeds 700°C, it
becomes susceptible to burst according to the CC. Figure 8 shows the plenum temperature. This is the
average fill gas temperature in the rod. It closely mirrors the peak cladding temperature.

Figure 9 shows the peak hoop strain of the cladding. During normal operation the hoop strain is less than
1%, but once the LOCA begins and the cladding temperature rises, the once-burned rods experience
ballooning, which leads directly to bursts. Some rods approach 100% strain.

Figure 10 shows the relative FGR predictions during the simulations. During normal operation the FGR
generally rises but can fall at times as fission gas is generated but not released. The FGR has significant
spread during normal operation, falling anywhere from 1% to 7%. During the LOCA, burst release causes
most rods to experience some FGR, but it is generally much smaller than the FGR experienced during
normal operation. There is a strong correlation between FGR and time of failure. As the FGR increases,
the simulation ends sooner.

Figure 11 shows the fraction of the fuel that has been converted to HBS. Because HBS is a burnup-
dependent phenomenon, the trend only increases during reactor operation and is not affected by the
LOCA. By EOC, the HBS accounts for 25–40% of the fuel.

5.2 BOILING WATER REACTOR

Of the 945 BWR rods that were simulated for this study, 943 ran to completion. Of the 943 rods, neither
the CC nor SRC predicted any cladding bursts. Therefore, the BWR rods do not predict any dispersed
fuel. One important feature to note is that while the PWR rods were filtered to only include rods with
average burnups greater than 62 MWd/kgU, only a handful of BWR rods reached that burnup. The
burnup distribution of the BWR rods is shown in Figure 13.

Figures 14–21 show time-dependent simulation predictions for the eight parameters of interest. Figure 14
shows the rod average LHGR. At EOC, the LHGR falls in the range of 1–20 kW m-1. Similar to the PWR
LHGR, the LHGR drops by 99% once the LOCA begins. The peak fuel temperature, peak cladding
temperature, and plenum temperature are shown in Figures 15, 16, and 17, respectively. The temperature

1,300°C. The cladding temperature has a much more consistent temperature range of 300°C–350°C no

temperature, except with a slightly wider range of 290°C–400°C that drops during the cycle. The rods
with the highest plenum temperatures are partial length rods, because their plenums are heated by
surrounding full-length rods.

 6. COMPARISON WITH PREVIOUS RESULTS

As mentioned in Section 3, these simulations have all been run before, but the current results utilized new
and updated BISON models [17,20]. Therefore, the effect of these new updates can be measured by
comparing the present results with the previous results. The first notable comparison is that the new
version of the PWR experienced significantly fewer crashes, meaning the design space of operational
conditions can be more fully and robustly investigated. In the original simulations, the CC predicted that
177 out of the 178 once-burned rods would burst and that 5 out of 64 twice-burned rods would burst. The
SRC predicted all 178 once-burned rods and no twice-burned rods would burst. The full-core FFRD
susceptibilities from that work are shown in Table 3 [8]. Not only did the previous work predict far higher
FFRD susceptibilities, but the ranges are significantly larger than the current work, suggesting that the
updated simulations have lower uncertainties and greater consistency than the previous simulations. The
current work’s lower overall FFRD susceptibility is contributed to the fact that no twice-burned rods were
predicted to burst. The BWR rods were previously run earlier in 2026 and are therefore very similar to the
current results, except that the current results include the HBS model. The cladding burst predictions were
identical to the current results.

 7. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK

As part of the NEAMS Program’s investigation into FFRD, previous analyses of PWR and BWR cores
have been reevaluated using updated BISON models and capabilities. These simulations came about as
the final step in a multiphysics framework that included the NEAMS code VERA and the NRC code
TRACE. However, because those codes are under much less active development, they are not expected to
significantly change their predictions based on recent updates. The simulations include statistically
representative samples of high-burnup rods from a Westinghouse PWR and all rods from a GE BWR. The
BISON predictions from both reactors were analyzed separately and then together and finally compared
with the previous simulation predictions.

BWR predictions closely match the previous results from earlier in 2026, while the PWR predictions have
changed substantially since they were first evaluated in 2023. Most notably, the cladding bursts predicted
by the CC and SRC match much better in this version than in the previous version, and this results in the
full-core FFRD susceptibility being reduced substantially from 0–5,400 kg to 19–937 kg. In addition, the
current work now predicts that none of the twice-burned rods will fail. The LHGR and cladding
temperature are the predictions most strongly correlated with cladding burst. Also notable is that the PWR
simulations now predict slightly cooler fuel and plenum temperatures.

Both reactors now include predictions for HBS formation. The HBS fraction of the fuel is strongly
influenced by burnup and operating histories, which vary significantly between the two reactors. The
LOCA did not contribute to HBS formation.

Future work in this space will include the incorporation of additional recently developed BISON models,
including the addition of cladding liners to the BWR rods and a NEAMS-developed fuel microcracking
model incorporated into both reactors. Once these are available, they will be incorporated into the
simulations, and both sets of rods can be evaluated once again. In addition, BWR’s comparatively high
hoop strain and FGR should be investigated further.
