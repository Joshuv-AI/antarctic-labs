The Mihail–Vazirani conjecture and strong edge-expansion in random 0 / 1 0/1 polytopes
Micha Christoph, Sahar Diskin, Lyuben Lichev, Benny Sudakov

Abstract
We study the edge-expansion of the graph of a random 0 / 1 0/1 polytope P p d P^{d}_{p} , defined as the convex hull of a random subset of the points in { 0 , 1 } d \{0,1\}^{d} where every point is retained independently and with probability p p . This problem was introduced more than twenty years ago in a work of Gillmann and Kaibel, and has been extensively studied ever since. We prove that, for every fixed ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 and every p ∈ ( 0 , 1 − ε ] p\in(0,1-\varepsilon] , with high probability the graph of P p d P^{d}_{p} has edge-expansion Θ ⁡ ( d ) \Theta(d) . This improves the previously best known bound due to Ferber, Krivelevich, Sales and Samotij, and verifies, in a strong form, the celebrated Mihail–Vazirani conjecture for random 0 / 1 0/1 polytopes. Although the expansion factor Θ ⁡ ( d ) \Theta(d) is typically best possible for p ≥ 1 / 2 + ε p\geq 1/2+\varepsilon , we also show that the behaviour changes drastically at p = 1 / 2 p=1/2 . Namely, for every fixed ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 and every integer k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , if p ≤ 1 / 2 − ε p\leq 1/2-\varepsilon , then with high probability the graph of P p d P^{d}_{p} has edge-expansion Ω ⁡ ( d k ) \Omega(d^{k}) . Thus, random 0 / 1 0/1 polytopes exhibit an interesting phase transition at p = 1 / 2 p=1/2 .



1
Introduction

A
0/1
polytope
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the convex hull of a subset of the
hypercube
\{0,1\}^{d}
. Equivalently, every vertex of such a polytope has all
its coordinates equal to zero or one. These polytopes are central objects in
polyhedral combinatorics: many combinatorial structures can be encoded as
characteristic
0/1
vectors, and the corresponding polytope serves as a
geometric handle for the underlying combinatorial problem via linear
programming
[
26
]
. Prominent examples include the perfect matching
polytope, the matroid base polytope, the cut polytope, the stable set polytope,
and the order-ideal polytope; we refer to
[
26
]
for an extensive
account of their role in combinatorial optimisation.

The
graph

G_{P}
of a polytope
P
has vertices (resp. edges) given by
the set of vertices (resp. edges) of
P
.
The graph of a polytope
P
is also known as its
1
-skeleton
: it contains all faces of
P
of dimension at most 1.
A basic measure of connectivity of
G_{P}
is its

edge-expansion
(Cheeger constant) defined by

h(G_{P})\;=\;\min\bigg\{\frac{e(S,\,V(G_{P})\setminus S)}{|S|}:S\subseteq V(G_{P}),0<|S|\leq\frac{|V(G_{P})|}{2}\bigg\},

where
e(S,V(G_{P})\setminus S)
denotes the number of edges with one endpoint
in
S
and the other outside
S
.

A primary motivation for providing lower bounds for
h(G_{P})
is its tight connection to the mixing time of the lazy random walk on
G_{P}
:
indeed, such bounds readily result in upper bounds on the relaxation time (and hence, on the mixing time) via the discrete Cheeger inequality, see
[
21
, Sections 12-13]
.
Random walks on
0/1
polytopes are of significant algorithmic interest: they provide a standard method for
sampling an (approximately) uniformly random combinatorial object from the set encoded by the
vertices of that polytope. This approach was used, for example, by Jerrum, Sinclair, and Vigoda
[
16
]
to obtain a polynomial-time approximation
scheme for the permanent of a non-negative matrix. Beyond sampling, edge
expansion is intimately connected to several other fundamental properties of
graphs, including connectivity, diameter, and cycle lengths; we refer the reader
to the surveys
[
15
,
19
]
for a broad perspective on expansion in
graphs.

While it has long been known that high-dimensional hypercubes have edge-expansion
1
(following directly from Harper’s inequality
[
13
]
),
0/1
polytopes considered in the literature all seem to expand at least equally well.
This motivated Mihail and Vazirani
[
25
]
to
conjecture that this actually holds for all
0/1
polytopes.

Conjecture 1.1

(Mihail–Vazirani,
[
25
]
)
.

Every
0/1
polytope
P
satisfies
h(G_{P})\geq 1
.

A proof of Conjecture
1.1
would have far-reaching algorithmic
consequences
[
23
,
9
,
18
]
.
By now, it has been verified for several interesting families.
Kaibel
[
18
]
proved it for all
0/1
polytopes of dimension at most
five, all simple
0/1
polytopes, all hypersimplices, all stable set polytopes,
and all perfect matching polytopes. The conjecture was also established for
matching polytopes, order-ideal polytopes, and independent-set polytopes
in
[
24
]
, and for balanced matroid base polytopes
in
[
7
]
. A recent major breakthrough is the result of Anari, Liu, Oveis
Gharan, and Vinzant
[
2
]
, who proved the conjecture for the matroid base
polytope of
any
matroid using the theory of log-concave polynomials and
high-dimensional expanders. As a consequence, they obtained a fully polynomial
randomised approximation scheme (FPRAS) for counting the number of bases of any
matroid, resolving a long-standing open problem in approximate counting. Despite this progress, the conjecture remains wide
open in general; in particular, it is unknown for the cut polytope, the
travelling-salesman polytope, and most other polytopes arising in combinatorial
optimisation.

A natural important setting for Conjecture
1.1
, and one that captures the
“typical” behaviour of
0/1
polytopes, is the
random
0/1
polytope

P^{d}_{p}=\operatorname{conv}(Q_{p}^{d})
, where
Q_{p}^{d}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{d}
is
obtained by retaining each vertex of
\{0,1\}^{d}
independently with probability

p\in(0,1)
. Setting
p=1/2
here yields the uniform model. The study of
random
0/1
polytopes was initiated by Gillmann
[
9
]
, and the
question of whether the Mihail–Vazirani conjecture holds in this random setting
was asked explicitly more than twenty years ago by Kaibel and Remshagen
[
17
]
.

Progress on this question was first made by Leroux and
Rademacher
[
20
]
, who proved that, for every
p\in(0,1)
, the graph
of
P^{d}_{p}
has edge expansion at least
1/(12d)
with high probability (i.e. with probability tending to one as
n\to\infty
). This result was substantially
improved by Ferber, Krivelevich, Sales, and Samotij
[
8
]
, who showed
that for
p\geq 2^{-0.99d}
the edge expansion is bounded below by an

absolute positive constant
with high probability. At the other extreme,
when
p
is very small, the problem becomes more tractable: Bondarenko and
Brodskiy
[
6
]
showed that for
p\leq 2^{-5d/6}
the polytope graph is a
complete graph with high probability (so its expansion is as large as possible),
and a short combinatorial proof of a related threshold phenomenon was given
in
[
4
]
. Specifically,
[
4
]
shows that the graph of
P^{d}_{p}
is typically sparse when

\log(1/p)\leq(1/2-\varepsilon)d
and becomes dense when
\log(1/p)\geq(1/2+\varepsilon)d
,
and a similar “logarithmic” threshold for the graph being a clique is exhibited around
p=2^{-\delta d}
for
\delta\approx 0.8295
.

In this paper, we resolve the Mihail–Vazirani conjecture in the
random
0/1
polytope model. Our first main result confirms the conjecture in a very strong form.

Theorem 1
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
c=c(\varepsilon)>0
such that, for every
p\in(0,1-\varepsilon]
, whp

With high probability, that is, with probability tending to one as

For every

1

shows that the edge-expansion grows linearly

: a factor of

stronger than the conjecture itself
requires. Observe that this bound is tight up to the value of the constant

:
indeed, a simple second moment calculation shows that
Q^{d}_{p}
retains some vertex

together with all its

neighbours in

,
and thus the neighbourhoods of

This naturally raises the question whether the lower bound can be improved when

.
Our second result answers this question in the positive by exhibiting a striking

.

Theorem 2
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
and every integer

, there exists
c_{k}=c_{k}(\varepsilon)>0
such that, for every

with

Several comments are in place. As mentioned before, by
[
4
]
, when
p\leq 2^{-0.83d}
, we have that
P^{d}_{p}
is a clique with high probability. We note here that the key new ingredient is a coupling between
the long edges of the random polytope and mixed percolation on
hypercubes. Here, mixed percolation means that vertices and edges are retained
independently, with probabilities
p
and
q
,
respectively; we denote the resulting random subgraph of the
hypercube by
Q^{d}_{p,q}
. This defines a natural random graph
model on the hypercube which may be of independent interest. For a fixed odd distance
k
, let

Q^{d}(k)
denote the graph with vertex set
V(Q^{d})
in which two
vertices
u,v
are adjacent if they differ in exactly
k

coordinates. We exhibit many
m
-dimensional cubes inside

such that the restriction of the random graph to each
such cube has the law of
Q^{m}_{p,q}
; see
Lemma
6.4
. This reduces the geometric problem of
controlling long edges in the random polytope to a local
expansion statement for a locally i.i.d. model, proved in
Proposition
5.1
. Averaging these
local expansion estimates over a large overlapping family of
cubes, and then applying a renormalisation step together with
Lemma

, allows us to transfer the resulting
boundary edges back to
P_{p}^{d}
.

Let us further remark that, for Theorem
2
, essentially the same argument applies when
k=\Omega(\log\log d)
, which is optimal up to the value of the implicit constant for all fixed
p<1/2
. We nevertheless present the proof for arbitrary

for the sake of transparency and readability. The only required modification is to allow the parameters
q,\alpha
, and
\gamma
in Proposition

to tend to zero (specifically, with

). With this adjustment, the proof of the proposition — and consequently of the theorem –— goes through almost verbatim, with only minor changes in the application of Lemma

. We refer the reader to the statement of Lemma

to see explicitly where and why the condition

arises as a bottleneck in the argument.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we present an outline of the proof of the main theorems, and in Section
3
we collect relevant notation and several useful lemmas. The proof itself has three layers. First, in Section
4
we renormalise the sparse regime to a density bounded away from zero and show, via Lemma
4.1
, that expansion in the projected model lifts back to the original polytope. Second, in Section
5
we prove Proposition
5.1
, an expansion theorem for mixed vertex-edge percolation on the hypercube, which could be of independent interest. Third, and this is the key new structural input, in Section
6
we show that inside a carefully chosen family of subcubes of

(see Section
3
for notation), the long-edge graph of the random polytope is distributed as such a mixed-percolated hypercube. Section
7
then bootstraps these local expansion estimates into a global lower bound on the polytope boundary, from which Theorems
1
and
2
follow in Section
8
.

2
Proof outline

In this section, we present an outline of the proof of Theorems
1
and
2
.
We focus on Theorem
2
; at the end of the section, we explain how to derive Theorem
1
with minor modifications of the same ideas.

The first step is a renormalisation argument (used in
[
8
]
) whose purpose is to replace the original model by a coarser one where the relevant density parameter is of constant order.
Concretely, we partition the hypercube into
b
-dimensional fibres and collapse each fibre to a single vertex. A fibre is occupied if it contains at least one surviving vertex, so the projected model has effective density
\rho=1-(1-p)^{2^{b}}
.
The point of this reduction is that, after renormalisation, the density of surviving vertices belongs to a range where certain local expansion estimates, which we develop, can be applied;
this local expansion is lifted from the projected polytope to the original one via Lemma
4.1
.

After this reduction, the proof of Theorem
2
has three conceptual steps. The first is a local expansion result for mixed percolation on the hypercube
Q^{d}_{p,q}
(Proposition
5.1
, which may be of independent interest).
We show that whp any set whose vertices all have linear degree in
Q^{d}_{p,q}
must have neighbourhood of order
\Omega(|S|/\sqrt{d})
.
The proof is a thinning-and-sprinkling argument. If such a set
S
existed in
Q^{d}_{p,q}
, by moving to
Q^{d}_{p/2,q}
via vertex-thinning, it is not too unlikely that this set
S
has an empty neighbourhood in
Q^{d}_{p/2,q}
, that is, it becomes disconnected from the rest of the graph.
Moreover, one can show that typically it still retains many vertices of high degree (that is, proportional to
d
).
We show that having a set with many vertices of high degree and empty neighbourhood is very unlikely via a partitioning argument (in Lemma
5.2
) and a delicate structural analysis of
Q^{d}_{p,q}
(in Lemmas
5.3
–
5.4
).

The second step is to connect this local hypercube statement to the geometry of the random polytope.
The expansion we seek in
P^{d}_{p}
is not coming from the ordinary hypercube edges but from longer ones, created by the convex hull structure.
These long edges are difficult to control directly as they are globally dependent.
The key idea is to consider them inside carefully chosen subgraphs.
For a fixed odd distance
k
, Section
6
constructs a family
\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}
of
m
-dimensional cubes inside the graph
Q^{d}(k)
with vertices
V(Q^{d})
and an edge between any two vertices at Hamming distance exactly
k
.
Each cube
H\in\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}
is a small subgraph of the long-edge graph with the crucial property that

H\cap P^{d}_{p}
has distribution generated from mixed percolation on
Q^{m}

(which is the content of Lemma
6.4
).
This explains the relevance of our mixed-percolation estimates: they give a lower bound on the edge-expansion on each subcube.

The third step is the global amplification, carried out in Section
7
. The cubes in
\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}
are highly overlapping, so a single set
S
is seen many times from many different local viewpoints.
Our purpose is to average the local expansion contributions over the whole family of cubes (this is done in Lemma
7.1
).
For this, we need to ensure that many cubes have a moderate proportion of their vertices occupied by
S
, as this is the regime where our expansion estimates are applicable and efficient.
The remaining lemmas in Section
7
ensure this fact and show that no edge from
S
to its complement is counted too many times in the averaging procedure.
Together these conclusions ensure that the local
\Omega(|S|/\sqrt{d})
expansion inside cubes is amplified to the desired global expansion bound for
P_{p}^{d}
.

The proof of Theorem
1
follows the same general strategy with two changes.
First, thanks to Theorem
2
, we can assume that
p\geq 1/3
, so the renormalisation step in the proof of Theorem
2
is not needed.
However,
P^{d}_{p}
may now contain a few vertices which preserve their neighbourhood in
Q^{d}
.
To take this phenomenon into account, we use an expansion estimate for dense cubes from
[
8
]
(Lemma
3.8
) together with the same normalisation as in Theorem
2
, albeit with different parameters.
Combined with the prior “long edges” argument, this gives the lower bound
h(G_{P_{p}^{d}})=\Omega(d)
.

4
Renormalisation

This section provides the renormalisation step. We partition
Q^{d}
into
b
-dimensional fibres and choose
b
so that a fibre is occupied with probability
\rho=1-(1-p)^{2^{b}}
, where
b
is chosen such that the projected model lives at the regime where the mixed-percolation input from later sections will apply. The key point is that expansion in the projected polytope can be lifted back to the original polytope: Lemma
4.1
shows that every projected edge produces a genuine edge of
P_{p}^{d}
.

Partition the hypercube
Q^{d}=Q^{d-b}\times Q^{b}
into
2^{d-b}
subcubes of dimension
b
according to the projection of each vertex on the first
d-b
coordinates: in particular, the vertices of
Q^{d-b}
are identified with the
b
-dimensional cubes in the partition.
We define
Q\subseteq Q^{d-b}
to be the induced subgraph obtained by keeping only the vertices whose corresponding cube of dimension
b
in the above partition of
Q^{d}
contains at least one vertex present in
Q^{d}_{p}
.
Note that
Q
is a random subgraph of
Q^{d-b}
obtained after vertex percolation with parameter
\rho:=1-(1-p)^{2^{b}}
; in particular, we see
P^{d-b}_{\rho}
as the convex hull of the vertices of
Q
.
Further, we define a projection map
\pi:=\pi_{b,d}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d-b}
by setting

\pi((a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}))=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d-b}).

Lemma 4.1
.

For every edge
uv\in E(P^{d-b}_{\rho})
and every vertex

As

\langle\mathbf{v},u\rangle=\langle\mathbf{v},v\rangle=c\qquad\text{and}\qquad\forall z\in V(P^{d-b}_{\rho})\setminus\{u,v\},\;\langle\mathbf{v},z\rangle>c.

Define the vector
\mathbf{v}_{0}=(\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{d-b},0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and denote by

the
(d-1)
-dimensional hyperplane
\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\langle\mathbf{v}_{0},w\rangle=c\}
.
Then, by the choice of

R:=\pi^{-1}(\{u,v\})\subset H_{c}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\pi^{-1}(V(P^{d-b}_{\rho})\setminus\{u,v\})\cap H_{c}=\emptyset.

Further, note that
R\supseteq R_{p}:=V(P^{d}_{p})\cap H_{c}
and that

R
is isomorphic to
Q^{b+1}
. Indeed,
\pi^{-1}(u)
and

\pi^{-1}(v)
are
b

d-b

coordinates coincide with the ones of
u
and
v
, respectively. Since
u
and

form an edge in the convex hull, (possibly after affine transformation) these two subcubes together
form a
(b+1)

identify

throughout the rest of the proof.

To conclude, it suffices to show that

To this end, denote by
w^{\prime}\in V(P^{d}_{p})\cap\pi^{-1}(v)
a vertex such that

Then, the smallest subcube

of

contains the face

where each vertex in

is closer to

than

for the Hamming distance.
Thus,

is an edge in the graph of

∎

We will also make use of the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Fix
p\in(0,1-\varepsilon]
. Then, there exists
\alpha>0
such that the following hold with high probability.

•

If
p\geq 1/2-\varepsilon
, then the number of vertices in

with degree at least
(1-\alpha)d
is at most
2^{(1-\alpha^{3})d}
.

•

If
p\leq 1/2-\varepsilon
, then every vertex in

has degree at most
(1-\alpha)d
.

Proof.

We begin with the first item. By Lemma

, the probability a vertex has degree at least

is at most

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{Bin}(d,1-\varepsilon)\geq(1-\alpha)d\right]\leq\exp\left(-(\varepsilon-\alpha)^{2}d/3\right).

Thus, the expected number of such vertices is at most
2^{d-(\varepsilon-\alpha)^{2}d/3}
. Therefore, by Markov’s inequality, with high probability there are at most
2^{(1-\alpha^{3})d}
such vertices, where we choose
\alpha>0
sufficiently small with respect to
\varepsilon
.

For the second item,
the probability a vertex has degree at least
(1-\alpha)d
is at most

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{Bin}(d,1/2-\varepsilon)\geq(1-\alpha)d\right]\leq\sum_{i=(1-\alpha)d}^{d}\binom{d}{i}(1/2-\varepsilon)^{i}\leq\left[\left(2e/\alpha\right)^{\alpha}\cdot(1/2-\varepsilon)^{(1-\alpha)}\right]^{d}.

By choosing
\alpha>0
small enough with repsect to
\varepsilon
, the base of the above exponential is strictly less than
1/2
. Then, taking the union bound over the at most
2^{d}
vertices of the hypercube completes the proof.
∎

5
Expansion in the mixed-percolated hypercube

This section contains the local random-graph input for the rest of the paper.
We study mixed percolation on the hypercube, where vertices and
edges are retained independently, and prove that any set whose
vertices all have degree linear in
d
has external
vertex-neighbourhood of order
\Omega(|S|/\sqrt{d})
.
In particular, the goal of this section is to prove the following proposition, which may be of independent interest.

Proposition 5.1
.

Fix
p,q,\alpha\in(0,1]
and sufficiently small positive constants
\eta\coloneqq\eta(p,q,\alpha)
and
\gamma\coloneqq\gamma(\eta)
.
The following holds with probability at least
1-2^{-\eta d^{3/2}}
: for every set
S\subseteq V(Q^{d}_{p,q})
with
|S|\leq 3|V(Q^{d}_{p,q})|/4
and such that every
v\in S
has degree at least
\alpha d
in
Q^{d}_{p,q}
, we have
|N_{Q^{d}_{p,q}}(S)|\geq\gamma|S|/\sqrt{d}
.

Note that this bound is tight since the set of vertices below the middle layer(s) expands by a factor of
\Theta(1/\sqrt{d})
.

The proof of Proposition
5.1
is split into two regimes.
For very small sets, namely
|S|\leq d^{2}
, the statement is essentially a counting argument. Since the induced subgraph on
S
cannot contain too many edges by Harper’s inequality, while every vertex of
S
has degree at least
\alpha d
, most of the edges incident to
S
must leave
S
.
This already gives a much stronger lower bound on the size of the neighbourhood of
S
than the one claimed.

The interesting case is when
|S|
is larger. The proof then follows a thinning–and–sprinkling argument, in the spirit of
[
3
]
. Recall the sets
V_{p},E_{q}
from the notation section. Observe that if there was a set
S
with degrees at least
\alpha d
which did not expand well in
Q^{d}_{p,q}
, then when moving to, say,
Q^{d}_{p/2,q}
, it is not too unlikely that this set
S
has empty neighbourhood in
Q^{d}_{p/2,q}
, that is, it is disconnected from the rest of the graph there. On the other hand, one can show that typically, it still retains many vertices of high degree (that is, proportional to
d
). Now, fix some small density
\varepsilon>0
, and choose
p^{\prime},q^{\prime}
such that
(1-p^{\prime})(1-\varepsilon)=1-p/2
and
(1-q^{\prime})(1-\varepsilon)=1-q
, repsectively. This way,
V_{p^{\prime}}\cup V_{\varepsilon}
has the same distribution as
V_{p/2}
and
E_{q^{\prime}}\cup E_{\varepsilon}
has the same distribution as
E_{q}
. In Lemma
5.2
, we show that sprinkling by
(V_{\varepsilon},E_{\varepsilon})
does not typically create many new vertices of high degree inside a fixed candidate set. This lets us transfer any hypothetical bad set in
Q^{d}_{p,q}
to a comparable bad set in the partially exposed graph
Q^{d}_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}
, while retaining most of the vertices with degree
\Theta(d)
. Next, in Lemma
5.3
, we show that any such set, provided it consists mostly of high-degree vertices, must have a large matching leaving the neighbourhood of its large components. This is the input that turns ‘few external neighbours’ into a concrete obstruction: after sprinkling, a positive fraction of this boundary matching survives, so the set cannot remain disconnected. Finally, Lemma
5.4
combines these two statements. It shows that the event that a large set of mostly high-degree vertices is disconnected after sprinkling is extremely unlikely. This is the key probabilistic step that rules out bad sets in the range
|S|>d^{2}
.

We begin by showing that, with suitably high probability, only few vertices in
Q^{d}
are adjacent to many vertices in
V_{\varepsilon}
or incident to many edges in
E_{\varepsilon}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Fix
\varepsilon,\alpha\in(0,1)
with
\varepsilon\leq 8^{-8/\alpha^{2}}
and consider an integer
s\in\{d^{2},\ldots,2^{d}\}
.
Then, the probability that there are at least
s
vertices in
Q^{d}=(V,E)
, each with at least
\alpha d
neighbours in
V_{\varepsilon}
or incident to at least
\alpha d
edges in

.

Proof.

We say that a set

has the
\alpha
-star property if there are
|U|
vertex-disjoint stars in
Q^{d}
with centres in
U
and each with
(1-\alpha/2)d
leaves.
We show that, for every set
W^{\prime}\subseteq V(Q^{d})
with
|W^{\prime}|\geq s
, there exists
W\subseteq W^{\prime}
with
|W|=\alpha s/(4d)
having the
\alpha
-star property.
Indeed, fix a maximal subset
U\subseteq W^{\prime}
with this property and denote by

the corresponding star forest.
Suppose for contradiction that
|U|<\alpha s/(4d)
. Hence,
|V(F_{U})|\leq\left((1-\alpha/2)d+1\right)|U|<\alpha s/4
.
Further, by maximality of

\displaystyle e(W^{\prime}\setminus V(F_{U}),V(F_{U}))\geq|W^{\prime}\setminus V(F_{U})|\alpha d/2\geq(|W^{\prime}|-|V(F_{U})|)\alpha d/2\geq(1-\alpha/4)\alpha sd/2.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle|U|d^{2}\geq(1-\alpha/4)\alpha sd/2\implies|U|\geq\frac{(1-\alpha/4)\alpha s}{2d}\geq\frac{\alpha s}{4d},

Now, fix

as above, and set

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[X_{W}\geq\alpha^{2}s/8]\leq\sum_{i=\alpha^{2}s/8}^{\alpha s}\binom{\alpha s}{i}\varepsilon^{i}\leq 2^{\alpha s}\cdot\varepsilon^{\alpha^{2}s/8}\leq(2\varepsilon^{\alpha^{2}/8})^{s}\leq 4^{-s},

where for the last inequality we used that

. Since there are at most

is at most

.

Finally, assume that there are more than

\alpha d
neighbours in
V_{\varepsilon}
(in
Q^{d}
) or incident to at least
\alpha d
edges in
E_{\varepsilon}
, and denote this set of vertices by
W^{\prime}
.
Thus, there exists a subset
W\subseteq W^{\prime}
of size

having the
\alpha

is well-defined). Then, by assumption on

X_{W}=|V(F_{W})\cap V_{\varepsilon}|+|F_{W}\cap E_{\varepsilon}|\geq\frac{\alpha d}{2}\cdot|W|=\frac{\alpha^{2}s}{8},

which happens with probability at most
2^{-s}
, as required.
∎

6
Coupling mixed percolation and long edges in the polytope

This section contains the main new structural ingredient of our work.
For an integer
k\in[d]
, recall that
Q^{d}(k)
is the graph with vertex set
V(Q^{d})
and edges between every pair of vertices at Hamming distance
k
.
Although long edges in
P_{p}^{d}
are highly dependent in general, we show that this dependence disappears on a carefully chosen family of cubes inside
Q^{d}(k)
.
More precisely, for integers
k,m\geq 1
with
km\leq d
, we construct a family of subgraphs
\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}
of
Q^{d}(k)
, each isomorphic to
Q^{m}
.
Then, for each
H\in\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}
, Lemma
6.4
identifies the induced subgraph obtained by
p
-vertex-percolating
H
and restricting to the edges of
P_{k}
, with a mixed-percolated hypercube
Q^{m}_{p,q}
for a constant
q=q(p,k)>0
.
This exact coupling is what allows the local expansion theory from Proposition
5.1
to be imported into the polytope setting. The rest of the section introduces the family
\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}
and the combinatorial facts needed to make the promised coupling precise.

Fix
k,m\geq 1
. While
Q^{d}(k)
no longer has the same structure as the hypercube, it turns out that it contains a rich structure of subgraphs isomorphic to
Q^{m}
.
We first create the necessary setup to exploit this structure.
In the sequel, we associate the vertices of
Q^{d}
with
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}
in the usual way.
Note that
uv
is an edge in
Q^{d}(k)
if and only if
u
and
v
differ in exactly
k
coordinates. Further, for every subset
e\subseteq[d]
, we denote by
\mathbf{1}_{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}
the indicator vector of the set
e
.

First, we define a family of
m
possible “directions” of length
k
. To that end, let
\mathcal{D}_{k,m}
be the set of all (unordered) sets
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\}
of size
m
such that
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}
are disjoint subsets of
[d]
of size
k
.
For every
D=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\}\in\mathcal{D}_{k,m}
and
v\in V(Q^{d})
, we define the hypercube
Q(D,v)
, which contains all vertices of the form
v+\sum_{i\in I}\mathbf{1}_{e_{i}}
for every
I\subseteq[m]
, and for every
u,w\in V(Q(D,v))
, we have that
uw
is an edge if and only if
u+w=e_{i}
for some
i\in[m]
. We say that
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}
are the directions of
Q(D,v)
. Observe that
Q(D,v)
is a subgraph of
Q^{d}(k)
isomorphic to
Q^{m}
. Additionally,
Q(D,v)
does not depend on the choice of
v
in the sense of the following lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{D}_{k,m}
and
v\in V(Q^{d})
. Then, for every
u\in V(Q(D,v))
, it holds that
Q(D,v)=Q(D,u)
.

Proof.

Since
|V(Q(D,v))|=|V(Q(D,u))|=2^{m}
, it suffices to show
V(Q(D,u))\subseteq V(Q(D,v))
. Let
D=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\}
. Since
u\in V(Q(D,v))
, there exists
I\subseteq[m]
such that
u=v+\sum_{i\in I}\mathbf{1}_{e_{i}}
. Also, for every
u^{\prime}\in V(Q(D,u))
, there exists
I^{\prime}\subseteq[m]
such that
u^{\prime}=u+\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}}\mathbf{1}_{e_{i}}
. But then,
u^{\prime}=v+\sum_{i\in I}\mathbf{1}_{e_{i}}+\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}}\mathbf{1}_{e_{i}}=v+\sum_{i\in J}\mathbf{1}_{e_{i}}
, where
J
is the symmetric difference of
I
and
I^{\prime}
(and thus
J\subseteq[m]
). Consequently,
u^{\prime}\in V(Q(D,v))
.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

Lemma
6.1
has the following implication. Fix some
D\in\mathcal{D}_{k,m}
. Then, for
u,v\in V(Q^{d})
, we have that
Q(D,u)
and
Q(D,v)
are either the same or vertex-disjoint. As each
Q(D,v)
contains exactly
2^{m}
vertices, it follows that there are
2^{d-m}
different hypercubes whose directions are given by

and that these hypercubes partition

.

Let
\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}
be the family of hypercubes given by

varying over all possible choices of
D\in\mathcal{D}_{k,m}
and
v\in V(Q^{d})
. Note that, as described above,
\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}
contains
2^{d-m}
different hypercubes for every choice of

, so that
|\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}|=2^{d-m}|\mathcal{D}_{k,m}|
. This observation allows us to get the following bound on the size of
\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}
.

Lemma 6.3
.

For all integers
k,m\geq 1
with
km\leq d
,
|\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}|\leq e^{2d\log d}
.

Proof.

Since every element of
[d]
is in at most one of

, the number of choices of
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}
is at most
(m+1)^{d}\leq e^{d\log(m+1)}
(where every element is assigned to
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}
or to no set at all).
Since
|\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}|=2^{d-m}|\mathcal{D}_{k,m}|
, we get
|\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}|\leq 2^{d-m}\cdot e^{d\log(m+1)}\leq e^{2d\log d}
.
∎

The next lemma is the main conclusion of this section.
It shows that the interaction of the hypercubes in
\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}
with

follows that of mixed percolation on the hypercube, paving the way to utilise Proposition
5.1
in later arguments. For graphs
G_{1}\subseteq G_{2}
and an edge set

, recall that

is the spanning subgraph of
G_{1}
with edge set

.

Lemma 6.4
.

For every
H\in\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}
with
H=(U,F)
, denote
H_{p}=(U_{p},F)
.
Then, there is
q\geq(1-p)^{(2^{k}-2)}
such that
H_{p}[P_{k}]
has the same distribution as
Q^{m}_{p,q}
.

Proof.

The vertices of
H_{p}
are clearly present independently with probability
p
.
We show that, conditionally on its vertex set, the edges of
H_{p}[P_{k}]
are present independently and with certain probability

.
To this end, recall that, by Remark
3.7
the presence of an edge

in

depends only on the states of the vertices in
Q^{d}[u,v]
.
Hence, it suffices to show that the sets
\{V(Q^{d}[u,v])\setminus\{u,v\}:u,v\in V(H)\}
are all disjoint from each other and

: given this fact, each of them satisfies

with probability

, which then justifies the bound on

and suffices to conclude.

Denote by
D=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\}\in\mathcal{D}_{k,m}
be the directions of
H
so that

.
Also, fix two edges

with

and

.
First, suppose
e^{\prime}\neq e^{\prime\prime}
. By the definition of

, there exists
I\subseteq[m]
such that
x=u+\sum_{i\in I}\mathbf{1}_{e_{i}}
and

. Since the

are disjoint subsets of

, the restriction of one of

or

to the coordinates of

equals to that of

and

; assume without loss of generality that it is

. But then, the restriction of any vertex in

to

, besides

, is different from that of

and of

and hence also different from all the vertices in

. It follows that

and

do not overlap besides potentially on

If, on the other hand,

, then all of

are distinct. It follows that

and

differ on

. But all the vertices in

agree with

on

and all the vertices in

agree with

on

, implying that the cubes

and

are disjoint.
∎

We also remark that the edges in

do
not
percolate independently in general; Lemma

implies that joint independence holds for the sets of edges of each cube in
\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}
, and Proposition

will be applied exclusively in this restricted setting.

7
Expansion at distance
k

This section extends the local expansion available inside the cubes
H\in\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}
to global expansion in the distance-
k
graph.
We work with a graph
Q_{k}\subseteq Q^{d}(k)
which satisfies three important properties:
first,
Q_{k}
contains a significant proportion of the edges in
Q^{d}(k)
, second, after percolation, all cubes in the collection are “typical” in terms of their size and edge-expansion, and third, any intersections of cubes (smaller cubes themselves) also have “typical” size.
Here, the second property ensures proper local expansion, while the third property is used to derive that large sets
S
do not almost-fully intersect or remain almost-fully disjoint from almost-all cubes in the collection, thus allowing the promised local-to-global boosting.
Under these assumptions, the key lemma of this section (Lemma
7.1
) states that every set of vertices where each vertex has degree at least
\varepsilon\tbinom{d}{k}
, edge-expands by a factor of
\tbinom{d}{k}/d^{3}
.
The proof is an averaging argument over
\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}
: Lemmas
7.2
and
7.3
force many cubes into an intermediate regime where local expansion applies (thus contributing non-trivially to the global expansion in
Q_{k}
), while Lemma
7.4
shows that a high-degree vertex is bad in only a negligible fraction of the cubes containing it. A final multiplicity count turns these local boundary contributions into a global lower bound.

In this section, we work with an odd integer
k\geq 3
and
m\coloneqq d/(3k^{2})
.
Fix
p,\varepsilon,\gamma\in(0,1)
,
Q_{k}\subseteq Q^{d}(k)
and, for every

, write

for the graph with vertex set
V(H)\cap V(Q_{k})
and edge set
E(H)\cap E(Q_{k})
.
Throughout this section, we assume that
Q_{k}
satisfies the following three conditions.

(i)

It holds that
|V(Q_{k})|/2^{d}=p\pm o(1)
;

(ii)

For every
H\in\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}

and

with
|S|\leq 3|V(H\cap Q_{k})|/4
and such that every vertex in
S
has degree at least
\varepsilon^{2}d/(8k^{2})

, we have that
|N_{H\cap Q_{k}}(S)|\geq\gamma|S|/\sqrt{d}

(iii)

For every

, it holds that
|V(H\cap Q_{k})|=(1\pm\varepsilon)p2^{m}
and, for every
H,H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}_{k,m}
with
|V(H)\cap V(H^{\prime})|=2^{m-1}

|V(H)\cap V(H^{\prime})\cap V(Q_{k})|=(1/2\pm\varepsilon)p2^{m}

Before continuing, we remark that
P_{k}
(which, recall, is the subset of edges in

with endpoints at Hamming distance

)
is shown to satisfy each of the above conditions in Section

The following lemma is the main result of this section.

Lemma 7.1
.

Fix an odd integer
k
, constants

and

(i)
,
(ii)
and

To prepare the setup for the proof of Lemma

.

Fix a path from

Lemma 7.4

Fix an odd integer

As every

Q^{d}

We start this section with one last auxiliary lemma about the degrees in the polytope at distance

Recall that

Lemma 8.1
.

Fix an integer

and

in

.
We show that, for every

Fix a vertex

Denote by

Let

Finally, conditionally on the set

for

.

Proof of Theorem

.

Decompose

To set the argument up, we first establish the following three claims.

Claim 8.2

Whp, for every

|V(Q)\cap V(P^{d}_{p})|\leq d

Fix

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[|V(Q)\cap V(P^{d}_{p})|>d]\leq\sum_{i=d}^{2^{b}}\binom{2^{b}}{i}p^{i}\leq 2^{b}\left(\frac{ep2^{b}}{d}\right)^{d}<2^{b}\left(\frac{e}{10d}\right)^{d}.

A union bound over the

Claim 8.3
.

Whp, the number of

. Suppose then that

, whp there are at least

such

.
∎

Claim 8.4
.

Whp, every cube

, we get that whp every

.

We are ready to prove the theorem.
Consider

, whp it holds that

.

9
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we essentially resolve the problem of the edge-expansion of random
0/1
polytopes, proving in a strong form the Mihail–Vazirani conjecture in this setting;
in particular, we showed that, for a typical
0/1
polytope, the Mihail–Vazirani conjecture holds in a strong sense. We further establish that the edge-expansion exhibits a phase transition at
p=1/2
.

Our results suggest several open questions. First, for
p>1/2
, Theorem
1
leaves a gap between our
lower bound of
cd
and the upper bound of
d
. It would be interesting to understand the dependency between
c
and
p
. Second, Theorem
2
establishes super-polynomial expansion when
p<1/2
, and minor modifications suffice to establish the optimal exponent of
d
for constant
p
, but the precise growth rate as a function of both
p
and
d
remains open when
p=p(d)\to 0
fast enough.
It is natural to believe that this growth rate is given by the minimum degree of the graph of
P^{d}_{p}
, as this is the case for many mean-field random models with good expansion properties.
Confirming this suspicion and further showing that the minimum and the average degree of the graph are typically within a polynomial factor of
d
from each other would lead to significantly enhanced (polynomial) control on the mixing time of the lazy random walk on
P^{d}_{p}
.
