Application of particle filter algorithm based on chaotic sequences and improved t-distribution in UWB indoor positioning
Authors: Bing Li, Xingshao Chai, Shanshan Yang, Jingmei Zhou, Yanxin Hao, Chungang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ultra-wideband (UWB) technology has significant application value in indoor autonomous navigation and robot path planning. However, noise interference in complex environments often causes anomalies in UWB positioning data, severely reducing positioning accuracy and stability. A particle filter algorithm (LTPF) based on chaotic sequences and an improved t-distribution is proposed to address this issue. This algorithm applies a linear transformation to the Logistic map to generate chaotic sequences, thereby enhancing particle diversity and distribution. Additionally, it uses standardized residuals to improve the t-distribution and construct an observation likelihood function, thereby reducing the impact of noise points and outlier data. Experimental results show that compared to the PF, EKPF, KGAPF, and SKF-DMM algorithms, the LTPF algorithm achieves average positioning accuracy improvements of 47.80%, 40.53%, 43.73%, and 50.10%, respectively, effectively enhancing positioning accuracy and stability. Additionally, its computational complexity is equivalent to the PF algorithm, making it a viable method for UWB positioning in complex indoor environments.

Introduction

With the growing demand for high-precision indoor positioning, Ultra-Wideband (UWB) has emerged as the primary technology for indoor positioning due to its wide bandwidth, low power consumption, strong anti-interference capabilities, and centimetre-level positioning accuracy1. However, in practical applications, factors such as non-line-of-sight (NLOS) and multipath effects caused by obstacles in complex environments2, clock drift and synchronization drift in devices3, and height restrictions and unreasonable base station layouts during deployment4 can introduce noise points and abnormal data into UWB positioning data, significantly reducing positioning accuracy and stability5, thereby limiting the application of UWB in fields such as robot indoor autonomous navigation and path planning6. Therefore, researching UWB positioning methods suitable for complex indoor environments is significant.

Filtering algorithms7 are commonly used for optimizing positioning data processing. Standard filtering algorithms include the Kalman filter (KF)8 and particle filter (PF)9. Literature10 improved the standard Kalman filter (SKF) based on the global offset method and the measurement discard method, respectively, and compared the positioning performance of the two improved methods with that of SKF. Among them, the improved method based on measurement discard removes abnormal data by setting a threshold, demonstrating superior positioning performance. However, since SKF relies on the Gaussian distribution assumption and linearization approximation, its application in NLOS environments is limited. In contrast, particle filtering uses a Monte Carlo sampling strategy to estimate the posterior distribution of the target state, demonstrating greater adaptability and robustness in handling non-linear and non-Gaussian noise11.

In indoor positioning, particle filtering primarily faces the issues of particle degeneration and incorrect weight allocation. Both of these issues can lead to a decrease in the estimation accuracy of particle filtering, thereby reducing positioning accuracy and stability. Particle degeneration occurs because, during the iteration process, a small number of particles obtain excessively high weights. In contrast, the weights of most particles become very small, resulting in a reduction in the number of effective particles and a decrease in particle diversity12. Reference13 analyses the impact of particle likelihood probability distributions on filtering accuracy, proposes a hierarchical particle diversity metric, and sets a threshold for evaluating particle diversity after resampling. Gaussian random walks are employed to enhance diversity if particle diversity fails to meet the threshold. However, this algorithm relies on threshold settings, and improper threshold selection may result in ineffective improvements to particle diversity. Reference14 proposes a particle filtering algorithm based on local linear embedding, which reduces high-dimensional particle states to a low-dimensional Euclidean space while preserving the linear relationships between particles. Particles are then divided into a retention group and an adjustment group based on their weights, with smaller-weighted particles adjusted and mapped back to the high-dimensional space. Finally, the two groups of particles are merged after resampling for the following iteration calculation. However, this algorithm introduces additional parameters, and improper parameter settings may result in poor particle diversity enhancement effects. Reference15 proposes an improved particle filtering resampling method that uses high-weight particles as parent particles and constructs new particles based on a Gaussian distribution to maintain particle diversity. Reference16 combines the advantages of residual resampling and polynomial resampling to assess the degradation level of particles in the current state, thereby selecting the most suitable resampling strategy. Although these two methods reduce the loss of particle diversity compared to traditional resampling methods, they cannot avoid the sample depletion phenomenon caused by multiple copies of high-weight particles. Additionally, these methods typically use Gaussian distributions or other random distributions to generate particle sets, causing particles to cluster in local regions and exacerbating particle degradation.

Since particle weights depend on the degree of match between each particle and the observed data, the higher the match, the greater the weight. A significant deviation between the observed data and the actual location can lead to incorrect particles being assigned excessively high weights17. Reference18 uses the Kalman filter algorithm to construct an importance probability density function and introduces a genetic algorithm to perform selection, crossover, and mutation operations on the particle set during the resampling process, making the particle distribution approach the posterior probability distribution. Reference19 uses the extended Kalman filter (EKF) to update the sampled particles and constructs the importance probability density function using the EKF values, making the particle distribution approach the posterior probability distribution. Reference20 uses the firefly algorithm to evaluate the particle distribution to control the optimization intensity and detect the particle density around the optimal particle. When particles are overly aggregated, the elastic mechanism of the spring model is used to adjust the particle distribution, making it more reasonable. Reference21 proposes a particle filtering method based on an improved artificial bee colony algorithm. This algorithm simulates the foraging process of bees to guide particles toward regions with a high likelihood of posterior probability, thereby improving the rationality of the particle distribution. Although the above methods optimize the particle distribution by improving the importance of the probability density function and introducing intelligent optimization algorithms, they still cannot avoid the problem of incorrect weight allocation caused by significant observational data errors.

Therefore, to address the issues of particle degeneration and incorrect weight allocation in particle filter algorithms applied to UWB positioning, this paper proposes a UWB positioning method based on LTPF. The main contributions of this paper are as follows:To address the issue of particle degeneration, this paper proposes a method based on logistic mapping to enhance particle diversity. A chaotic sequence with good exploration properties is generated by applying a linear transformation to the Logistic mapping. This sequence is combined with positioning data to initialize the particle set. Additionally, the global error variation is analyzed, and a state transition equation is established based on the chaotic sequence, thereby optimizing the particle distribution while enhancing particle diversity.To address the issue of incorrect weight allocation, a method is proposed to improve the t-distribution-based observation likelihood function using standardized residuals. The observation likelihood function is constructed based on the t-distribution, and standardized residuals are used to analyze the error magnitude of the current observation data. Its positioning performance is tested in three simulation environments with different noise intensities and two real-world scenarios with varying environmental complexities. UWB positioning principles

Time of arrival positioning technology

UWB positioning primarily estimates the distance or angle between a target and an anchor node by measuring time-of-flight (TOF)22, time difference of arrival (TDOA)23, time of arrival (TOA)24, or angle of arrival (AOA)25, thereby determining the target’s position26. Among these, TOA is a positioning technique that determines the target’s position by measuring the time it takes for a signal to travel from the transmitter to the receiver. It calculates the distance between the tag and the base station by multiplying the signal propagation time by the propagation speed. Due to its high-ranging accuracy, TOA is widely used in UWB positioning systems27.

Improvements to the particle filter algorithm

Particle filter algorithm

Particle filtering (PF) is a recursive Bayesian estimation algorithm based on the Monte Carlo method. Its core idea is to represent the probability distribution of the target state using a set of random samples (called particles) and recursively update the particles’ weights and states to approximate the actual posterior distribution31. The main steps of the algorithm are as follows:Initialisation: At the initial time k = 0, N particles are randomly sampled from the prior distribution, and each particle is assigned the same weight. Typically, the prior distribution is assumed to follow a Gaussian distribution. Algorithm complexity analysis

The computational complexity of the LTPF algorithm is primarily influenced by the number of particles N and the time step k. During the initialization phase, N particles are generated based on a chaotic sequence and assigned uniform weights, with a computational complexity of O(N); during the prediction phase, particle states are updated using state transition equations, with a computational complexity of O(N); in the update phase, the particle weights are updated by constructing an observation likelihood function based on the t-distribution and standardized residuals, followed by normalization, with a computational complexity of O(N); finally, in the resampling phase, the particle set is updated using a systematic resampling method, and the final estimate is calculated using a weighted average method, with a computational complexity of O(N). The overall complexity of the LTPF algorithm is:42\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} O\left( k\cdot N \right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Therefore, the LTPF algorithm effectively improves positioning accuracy and stability while maintaining the same complexity as traditional particle filter algorithms.

Algorithm positioning performance comparison

To verify the positioning performance of the algorithm in different noise environments, three simulation environments with noise standard deviations of 0.2 m, 0.5 m, and 0.8 m were constructed in a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20m\times 20m\times 20m$$\end{document} space. The LTPF algorithm, the PF algorithm, the Improved Particle Filter (ISPF) with modified particle set initialization and state transition equations, and the Improved Likelihood Function Particle Filter (ILPF).

As shown in Fig. 4, the ISPF algorithm constrains the particle propagation process by improving the generation method of particle sets and the state transition model, thereby suppressing unreasonable and abrupt state transitions, resulting in a noticeable shift of the curve to the left. The ILPF algorithm adapts to outlier data by refining the observation likelihood function; its cumulative distribution function (CDF) curve is shifted to the left compared to that of the PF algorithm, mitigating the impact of outlier observations. The LTPF algorithm combines the ISPF and ILPF algorithms, maintaining a relatively forward-shifted and smooth CDF curve under all three noise conditions. Its cumulative probability exhibits more stable behavior as error increases, indicating that this method can effectively reduce positioning error.Fig. 4Comparison of CDF Curves.

Conclusion

UWB positioning in complex indoor environments is susceptible to NLOS, multipath effects, clock drift, and improper base station deployment, leading to significant deviations in positioning data and reducing positioning accuracy and stability. Therefore, this paper proposes a UWB positioning method based on LTPF. First, the importance of the particle filter algorithm for optimizing UWB positioning data processing is analyzed. Then, corresponding solutions are designed to address the issues of particle degradation and incorrect weight allocation in the application of the particle filter algorithm for positioning. Finally, simulations and actual experiments demonstrate that the LTPF method can significantly improve UWB positioning accuracy and stability. However, there are still some shortcomings in practical applications. For example, positioning accuracy is fundamentally constrained by the inherent ranging uncertainty of UWB hardware. In practical systems, the ranging error typically fluctuates around 0.1 m under ideal line-of-sight (LOS) conditions, which implies that the achievable positioning accuracy has a theoretical lower bound determined by the statistical characteristics of the measurement noise rather than a fixed constant value. Therefore, the proposed LTPF algorithm improves positioning performance by suppressing abnormal observations, instead of eliminating the inherent measurement uncertainty of the hardware. Furthermore, this paper does not provide a systematic analysis of the effects of the number of base stations, their spatial distribution, and their geometric structure. In addition, the current design of the positioning algorithm does not incorporate dynamic factors such as the time dimension and direction of motion. Future work will address these issues to further improve the performance of the positioning system.
