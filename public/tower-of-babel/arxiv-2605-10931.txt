Quantifying Concentration Phenomena of Mean-Field Transformers in the Low-Temperature Regime
Albert Alcalde, Leon Bungert, Konstantin Riedl, Tim Roith

Abstract
Transformers with self-attention modules as their core components have become an integral architecture in modern large language and foundation models. In this paper, we study the evolution of tokens in deep encoder-only transformers at inference time which is described in the large-token limit by a mean-field continuity equation. Leveraging ideas from the convergence analysis of interacting multi-particle systems, with particles corresponding to tokens, we prove that the token distribution rapidly concentrates onto the push-forward of the initial distribution under a projection map induced by the key, query, and value matrices, and remains metastable for moderate times. Specifically, we show that the Wasserstein distance of the two distributions scales like log ⁡ ( β + 1 ) / β ​ exp ⁡ ( C ​ t ) + exp ⁡ ( − c ​ t ) \sqrt{\nicefrac{{\log(\beta+1)}}{{\beta}}}\exp(Ct)+\exp(-ct) in terms of the temperature parameter β − 1 → 0 \beta^{-1}\to 0 and inference time t ≥ 0 t\geq 0 . For the proof, we establish Lyapunov-type estimates for the zero-temperature equation, identify its limit as t → ∞ t\to\infty , and employ a stability estimate in Wasserstein space together with a quantitative Laplace principle to couple the two equations. Our result implies that for time scales of order log ⁡ β \log\beta the token distribution concentrates at the identified limiting distribution. Numerical experiments confirm this and, beyond that, complement our theory by showing that for finite β \beta and large t t the dynamics enter a different terminal phase, dominated by the spectrum of the value matrix.

Main Contributions.

In this work, we study the evolution of tokens in deep encoder-only transformers at inference time by analyzing the mean-field token distribution
\rho_{t}^{\beta}
of the transformer continuity equation (
2
).
We quantify its concentration at the push-forward
\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0}
of the initial datum under the projection map (
4
), which is determined by the model weights, in terms of the temperature parameter
\beta^{-1}
and inference time
t
. Our proof leverages tools from the analysis of interacting particle systems, in particular, stability estimates in Wasserstein space involving a quantitative Laplace principle, and Lyapunov techniques to quantify convergence speeds.
Our main contributions are as follows.

•

Identification of
\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0}
as the stationary distribution of the zero-temperature continuity equation (
3
) for symmetric matrices
VB^{\top}
,
and proof of exponential convergence of the zero-temperature solution
\rho_{t}
to the dominant eigenspace of
VB^{\top}
in the Wasserstein distance.

•

Derivation of a quantitative Wasserstein stability estimate between solutions
\rho_{t}^{\beta}
of the transformer continuity equation (
2
) and the zero-temperature limit
\rho_{t}
.

•

Quantification of the approximation error between
\rho_{t}^{\beta}
and
\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0}
in Wasserstein distance as
\sqrt{\nicefrac{{\log(\beta+1)}}{{\beta}}}\exp(Ct)+\exp(-ct)
, indicating an interplay between temperature
\beta^{-1}
and time
t
.

•

Characterization of the non-asymptotic concentration regime of the mean-field transformer dynamics up to times
t=\mathcal{O}(\log\beta)
, corresponding to the early-phase behavior most relevant for practical finite-depth models.

•

Experimental identification of the terminal phase after times
t=\mathcal{O}(\log\beta)
, where concentration shifts
from the dominant eigenspace of
VB^{\top}
to the one of
V
.

Discussion of the assumptions.

Demanding invertibility of the product
B=Q^{\top}K
of the key and query matrices in
A1
of Assumption
1
is relatively common in the field, see, e.g.,
28
;
29
;
40

17

13

3

is invertible. The symmetry assumption on

from

It is important to note, however, that without an assumption of this form one cannot hope to be able to characterize the stationary distribution
\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0}
for (

2
. Still, the quantitative stability result from Proposition
6

W_{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\rho_{t})\leq 2\sqrt{\nicefrac{{\log(\beta+1)}}{{\beta}}}(e^{C_{1}t}-e^{C_{0}t})

A2
. As a consequence, whenever one can analyze the convergence of the zero-temperature solution
\rho_{t}
under more relaxed assumptions than
A2
, such results extend to
\rho_{t}^{\beta}
for large
\beta
by the triangle inequality. See also Appendix
C.2
for related numerical experiments.

Moving on to Assumption
2

\rho_{0}(E^{\perp}\cap{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}})=0
since otherwise the denominator would be zero on a set of positive measure.
This is necessary: Since
E^{\perp}\cap{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
is invariant for (
3

VB^{\top}
is symmetric, any mass initially supported on
E^{\perp}
remains there for all times and cannot flow toward
E
. However, Assumption
2
is stronger requiring that not too much mass accumulates close to
E^{\perp}\cap{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
either. This is necessary to prove Corollary
2
with Lyapunov techniques.
We suspect that it might be possible to weaken this assumption or even replace it by
\rho_{0}(E^{\perp}\cap{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}})=0
if one is willing to worsen or sacrifice having a convergence rate altogether.

Assumption
3
is only needed for the quantitative Laplace principle from Lemma
5

\rho_{t}^{\beta}
with zero mass on open subsets of the sphere, the vector field in (
2

3

\beta

13
also used Assumption
3
to show that
\rho_{t}^{\beta}
has a positive density for all finite times

, and similar assumptions were used in
14
;
40
;
19

One possible way to remove this assumption is to add noise, leading to noisy transformer models as recently considered in
10
;
40
;
42

This is also exploited in the analysis of the conceptually similar consensus-based optimization model
26
. Note, however, that the exponential convergence of the solution to the zero-temperature continuity equation (
3
) from Corollary
2
holds without Assumption
3
and is, in particular, valid for singular initial distributions concentrated on finitely many points as long as they do not lie on the lower-dimensional sphere
E^{\perp}\cap{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
.

Let us remark that at first glance it might seem like Assumptions
2
and
3
contradict each other. After all, the former prevents mass from accumulating close to

Example 3
.

Since Assumption
3
implies that
\rho_{0}\geq\ell_{0}\,\mathcal{H}^{d-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=2.58334pt}{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}

In fact, it suffices to check whether
\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\!\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|^{-2p}\!\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(x)<\infty

Note that, for

Our main result, Theorem

Related work

Let us now relate our results to previous works in the literature on the mathematical analysis of transformer models, and outline their connection to a class of interacting particle systems.

Mathematical analysis of transformers.

A central body of work models transformer layers through the lens of continuous dynamical systems and partial differential equations (PDEs).
This perspective was pioneered in
41
by linking attention to transport-type PDEs, and expanded in
29
by incorporating key architectural components such as layer normalization within a mean-field framework.
More recent contributions such as
17
provide a unified treatment of these approaches. Together, these works yield a concise mathematical description of transformer dynamics that serves also as our modeling foundation. The authors of
13
identify the zero-temperature limit (
3
) and establish convergence towards it as
\beta^{-1}\to 0
. However, their result is not quantitative, i.e., no rate in
\beta
nor its interplay with inference time
t
is provided. The analysis further identifies additional phases of the dynamics once the solution concentrates on the dominant eigenspace
E
of
VB^{\top}
. In contrast, our work takes low but non-zero temperature effects into account and provides quantitative convergence guarantees.
This perspective is motivated by the numerical evidence in Section
5
which suggests that
\beta^{-1}>0
may prevent full collapse onto the eigenspace
E
.
Relatedly,
16
also focuses on equation (
2
) with
\beta^{-1}>0
and characterize the stationary states of (
2
) in the case
B=\pm V
, where the system can be interpreted as a gradient flow of the energy
\mathcal{E}(\varrho)=\iint e^{\langle x,By\rangle}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(x)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(y)
, either maximizing it if
B=V
or minimizing it if
B=-V
. We discuss this case in Appendix
C.3
and only emphasize here that our results complement their theory in two ways: we go beyond the gradient flow setting by allowing
B\neq V
and, in particular, make concrete statements about the asymptotic behavior of
\rho_{t}^{\beta}
.

Besides mean-field descriptions, a complementary line of research investigates transformer dynamics at the finite-particle level, with an emphasis on qualitative and asymptotic behavior. In particular, clustering and concentration phenomena are analyzed in
29
;
40
;
19
, with extensions to causal attention settings in
32
;
1
. The long-time behavior of transformer dynamics has also been studied, including the emergence of metastability
27
;
3
,
multistability with several equilibria related to the spectrum of the value matrix
4
, and phase transitions
20
, as well as propagation-of-chaos results in large-token limits
14
. Our work differs by considering token distributions with positive densities and providing quantitative estimates of concentration phenomena in an initial non-asymptotic phase of the dynamics where the attention matrix
B
plays a crucial role.

Interacting particle systems.

Transformers share striking similarities with classical interacting particle systems (e.g., the Kuramoto model
37
), and optimization methods (e.g., gradient descent
46
or the Frank–Wolfe algorithm
3
). In particular, they are connected (as noted in
29
) to
consensus-based optimization
(CBO)
36
;
39
which we formalize and exploit in what follows. In line with our setting, we consider CBO constrained to the sphere, as in
25
.
The aim is to locate a global minimizer of some objective function
J:{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\to\mathbb{R}
by evolving a particle ensemble
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
that follows a coupled system of stochastic differential equations of the form

\dot{x}_{i}(t)=\mathrm{P}_{x_{i}(t)}\big(m_{\beta,\rho^{\beta,n}_{t}}\big)+\sigma\cdot\operatorname{noise}.

(5)

The so-called consensus point
m_{\beta,\varrho}
, defined for any probability measure
\varrho\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}})
, is a weighted mean to which particles with smaller values of
J
contribute more. In fact, particle weights can also be determined through a pairwise interaction via polarization, as proposed in
15
.
Mathematically, this amounts to kernelizing the consensus point as

m_{\beta,\varrho}^{\kappa}(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\frac{\kappa(x,y)\exp{(-\beta J(y))}}{\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\kappa(x,z)\exp{(-\beta J(z))}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(z)}y\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(y),

(6)

where
\kappa:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is some kernel.
With
\left\|{\mathord{\color[rgb]{0.67,0.67,0.67}\bullet}}\right\|_{B}\coloneqq\sqrt{\langle\mathord{\color[rgb]{0.67,0.67,0.67}\bullet},B\,\mathord{\color[rgb]{0.67,0.67,0.67}\bullet}\rangle}
being the energy-norm, we choose

J(x)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|{x}\right\|_{B}^{2},\quad\text{and}\quad\kappa(x,y)=\exp{\left(-\tfrac{\beta}{2}\left\|{x-y}\right\|_{B}^{2}\right)}.

In the noiseless case
\sigma=0
, equation (
5
) with consensus point as in (
6
) leads exactly to the transformer model (
1
) when
V\equiv\mathrm{Id}
and
B\equiv Q^{\top}K
is time-independent. This interpretation motivated us to leverage techniques from the mean-field analysis of interacting particle systems for optimization—most prominently, the quantitative Laplace principle and Lyapunov techniques—to analyze the transformer model (
2
). Apart from this, by embedding the self-attention dynamics (
1
) into the rich family of CBO models
26
;
38
;
15
, one can hope to derive more expressive transformers by changing the kernel, the objective function, or by adding additional drift terms and noise (the latter of which leads to noisy transformers as previously investigated in, e.g.,
42
;
10
;
24
;
43
;
35
;
33
;
40
;
2
).

Proof details for the main results

The proof of Theorem
1
relies on two key ingredients:
quantitative stability estimates for continuity equations in Wasserstein space which leverage a quantitative Laplace principle,
and Lyapunov-type estimates for the Wasserstein distance to control the convergence of the zero-temperature transformer continuity equation (
3
). While the former allow to control the Wasserstein distance between the flows
\rho_{t}^{\beta}
and
\rho_{t}
(see Proposition
6
),
the latter enable us to quantify the convergence rate of
\rho_{t}
to
\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0}
(see Proposition
8
in combination with Lemmas
7
and
9
).
Before presenting these key ingredients in Sections
4.1
and
4.2
, we give the proof of Theorem
1
, which demonstrates how those tools are used.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Denoting by
(\rho_{t}^{\beta})_{t\in[0,T]}
the solution of (
2
),
and letting
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
denote the solution of (
3
) with coinciding initial datum
\rho_{0}=\rho_{0}^{\beta}
, we can bound by the triangle inequality

W_{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0})\leq W_{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\rho_{t})+W_{2}(\rho_{t},\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0}).

(7)

The first term on the right-hand side of (
7
) can be estimated by employing the quantitative stability estimate in Wasserstein space from Proposition
6
for the continuity equations (
2
) and (
3
),
yielding

W_{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\rho_{t})\leq 2\sqrt{\tfrac{\log(\beta+1)}{\beta}}\left(e^{C_{1}t}-e^{C_{0}t}\right)

(8)

for all
t\in[0,T]
. For the second term on the right-hand side of (
7
), we first note that thanks to Proposition
8
it holds for the functional
\mathcal{V}_{p}
defined in (
14
) that

\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{t})\leq\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{0})\exp\left(-\tfrac{2p\gamma}{\sigma_{\max}(B)}t\right)\!.

(9)

With
\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{0})<\infty
as of Assumption
2
,
we have that
\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{t})<\infty
for all
t\in[0,T]
.
This implies that
\rho_{t}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\cap E^{\perp})=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
, since otherwise the denominator in the integrand of
\mathcal{V}_{p}
would be zero on a set of positive measure.
Since according to Lemma
9
it furthermore holds that
\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{t}=\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0}
for all
t\in[0,T]
,
an application of Lemma
7
together with the bound (
9
) shows that

W_{2}(\rho_{t},\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0})=W_{2}(\rho_{t},\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{t})\leq\sqrt{2\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{t})}\leq\sqrt{2\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{0})}\exp\left(-\tfrac{p\gamma}{\sigma_{\max}(B)}t\right)\!,

(10)

verifying that
\sqrt{2\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{t})}
is a suitable Lyapunov functional for
W_{2}(\rho_{t},\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0})
.
Inserting (
8
) and (
10
) into (
7
) concludes the proof.
∎

Quantitative estimates for the Wasserstein distance between the flows
\rho_{t}^{\beta}
and
\rho_{t}

To estimate the Wasserstein distance
W_{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\rho_{t})
of the solutions
\rho_{t}^{\beta}
and
\rho_{t}
of the transformer continuity equations (
2
) and (
3
) in terms of the temperature parameter

. Denoting by

a fully supported probability measure, for any fixed

the vector

m_{\beta,\varrho}(x)\coloneqq\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\frac{\exp\left(\beta\langle B^{\top}x,y\rangle\right)}{\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\exp\left(\beta\langle B^{\top}x,z\rangle\right)\!\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(z)}y\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(y)

(11)

converges as

to a maximizer of the function

y^{*}(x)\coloneqq\frac{B^{\top}x}{\left\|{B^{\top}x}\right\|},\qquad x\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}},

Lemma 5
.

Assume that the matrix
B
satisfies
A1
.
Let
\varrho\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}})
,

, and define the spherical cap

.
Then, for every
\beta>0
and every

, it holds

\big\|{m_{\beta,\varrho}(x)-y^{*}(x)}\big\|\leq\sqrt{r^{2}+\tfrac{2q}{\sigma_{\min}(B)}}+\tfrac{2e^{-\beta q}}{\varrho\left(\mathcal{B}_{r}(y^{*}(x))\right)}.

under invertibility assumptions on

and

for the proof.

.

Assume that the matrix

.

\rho_{t}

\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0}

6
Concluding remarks

We provided a quantitative analysis of token concentration phenomena in mean-field transformers, showing that their zero-temperature limit
\beta^{-1}\to 0
is accurate up to time scales of order
\log\beta
and identifying the corresponding limiting distribution as a projection of the initial distribution
\rho_{0}
onto the dominant eigenspace of the matrix
VB^{\top}
together with convergence rates thereto.
In addition, we provided experimental and theoretical evidence for a terminal phase in which the dynamics align with a dominant eigenspace of the value matrix
V
.

Extensions and limitations.

There are a few limitations of our work which constitute important directions to address in future work.
While the symmetry assumption on the matrix
VB^{\top}
enables us to characterize the stationary distribution of the zero-temperature continuity equation, it restricts the dynamics compared to the general case where no stationary distribution might exist, cf.
13
.
Analyzing the regime of finitely many layers and tokens, i.e., time and space discretizations of (
1
),
investigating multi-head attention, where an average over multiple attention heads precedes the projection in (
1
),
and enriching the dynamics by adding noise
10
;
40
;
42
or a multi-layer perceptron
5
,
are further important generalizations of our work. Besides this, a systematic understanding of Conjecture
1
and the quantification of the convergence in the terminal phase observed numerically, as well as its possible connections to the Oja flow of the value matrix
V
, see
4
, are also of interest.

A.1.1
A quantitative Laplace principle

We first give a proof of the quantitative Laplace principle on the sphere
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
, Lemma
5
,
which was originally introduced in
[
26
]
on the plane. Here we extend it to the sphere and specialize it to a certain linear function on the sphere.

Proof of Lemma
5
.

The proof follows the lines of
[
26
, Proposition 4.5]
, adapted to the sphere
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
.

To simplify notation, let us define for
x\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
the non-negative functions

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{x}(y)\coloneqq\left\langle B^{\top}x,y^{*}(x)\right\rangle-\left\langle B^{\top}x,y\right\rangle=\left\|{B^{\top}x}\right\|\bigl(1-\left\langle y^{*}(x),y\right\rangle\bigr),\qquad y\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}},

where we recall from (
12
) that
y^{*}(x)=\frac{B^{\top}x}{\left\|{B^{\top}x}\right\|}
.
With this definition,
we have the identity
e^{\beta\langle B^{\top}x,y\rangle}=e^{\beta\|B^{\top}x\|}e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}_{x}(y)}
.
Using this, we obtain

\displaystyle m_{\beta,\varrho}(x)=\frac{\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}_{x}(y)}y\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(y)}{\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}_{x}(y)}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(y)},

where we canceled
e^{\beta\|B^{\top}x\|}
in the numerator and denominator.
Using that we are on the sphere
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
, where
\|y-y^{*}(x)\|^{2}=\|y\|^{2}-2\langle y^{*}(x),y\rangle+\|y^{*}(x)\|^{2}=2-2\langle y^{*}(x),y\rangle
,
we notice that
\mathcal{E}_{x}(y)=\frac{\|B^{\top}x\|}{2}\|y-y^{*}(x)\|^{2}
.
With this we directly observe that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{x,r}\coloneqq\sup_{y\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(y^{*}(x))}\mathcal{E}_{x}(y)=\frac{\|B^{\top}x\|}{2}r^{2}.

The quantity
Z_{\beta}(x)\coloneqq\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}_{x}(y)}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(y)

can be lower bounded by

Z_{\beta}(x)\geq\int_{\mathcal{B}_{r}(y^{*}(x))}e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}_{x}(y)}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(y)\geq\inf_{y\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(y^{*}(x))}e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}_{x}(y)}\varrho(\mathcal{B}_{r}(y^{*}(x)))=e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}_{x,r}}\varrho(\mathcal{B}_{r}(y^{*}(x))),

(15)

where the first inequality is due to the integrand being positive.
Now let
\tilde{r}\geq r>0
.
We can decompose (
13
) into contributions from inside and outside of
\mathcal{B}_{\tilde{r}}(y^{*}(x))
.
By Jensen’s inequality we have

\displaystyle\|m_{\beta,\varrho}(x)-y^{*}(x)\|

\displaystyle\qquad\,\leq\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\tilde{r}}(y^{*}(x))}\|y-y^{*}(x)\|\,\frac{e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}_{x}(y)}}{Z_{\beta}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(y)+\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\tilde{r}}(y^{*}(x))^{c}}\|y-y^{*}(x)\|\,\frac{e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}_{x}(y)}}{Z_{\beta}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(y).

(16)

Inside
\mathcal{B}_{\tilde{r}}(y^{*}(x))
we have
\|y-y^{*}(x)\|\leq\tilde{r}
,
which allows to bound the first term in (
16
) by
\tilde{r}
since the integrand is nonnegative and

\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\tilde{r}}(y^{*}(x))}\frac{e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}_{x}(y)}}{Z_{\beta}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(y)\leq 1
.
For the second term in (
16
), we use the lower bound (
15
), which gives

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\tilde{r}}(y^{*}(x))^{c}}\|y-y^{*}(x)\|\,\frac{e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}_{x}(y)}}{Z_{\beta}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(y)

\displaystyle\qquad\,\leq\frac{e^{\beta\mathcal{E}_{x,r}}}{\varrho(\mathcal{B}_{r}(y^{*}(x)))}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\tilde{r}}(y^{*}(x))^{c}}\|y-y^{*}(x)\|\,e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}_{x}(y)}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(y)

(17)

\displaystyle\qquad\,\leq\frac{2}{\varrho(\mathcal{B}_{r}(y^{*}(x)))}\exp\left(-\beta\left(\inf_{u\in\mathcal{B}_{\tilde{r}}(y^{*}(x))^{c}}\mathcal{E}_{x}(u)-\mathcal{E}_{x,r}\right)\right).

To obtain the last inequality, we first note that
\|y-y^{*}(x)\|\leq 2
since
y,y^{*}(x)\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
.
Secondly, inside
\mathcal{B}_{\tilde{r}}(y^{*}(x))^{c}
we have
\mathcal{E}_{x}(y)\geq\inf_{u\in\mathcal{B}_{\tilde{r}}(y^{*}(x))^{c}}\mathcal{E}_{x}(u)
, or equivalently after taking the exponential
e^{-\beta\mathcal{E}_{x}(y)}\leq e^{-\beta\inf_{u\in\mathcal{B}_{\tilde{r}}(y^{*}(x))^{c}}\mathcal{E}_{x}(u)}
.
And thirdly,
\varrho\big(\mathcal{B}_{\tilde{r}}(y^{*}(x))^{c}\big)\leq 1
.
Combining (
17
) with the observation directly before allows us to conclude (
16
) with the bound

\displaystyle\|m_{\beta,\varrho}(x)-y^{*}(x)\|\leq\tilde{r}+\frac{2}{\varrho(\mathcal{B}_{r}(y^{*}(x)))}\exp\left(-\beta\left(\inf_{u\in\mathcal{B}_{\tilde{r}}(y^{*}(x))^{c}}\mathcal{E}_{x}(u)-\mathcal{E}_{x,r}\right)\right).

(18)

Now, since
\inf_{u\in\mathcal{B}_{\tilde{r}}(y^{*}(x))^{c}}\mathcal{E}_{x}(u)=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{B}_{\tilde{r}}(y^{*}(x))^{c}}\frac{\|B^{\top}x\|}{2}\|u-y^{*}(x)\|^{2}=\frac{\|B^{\top}x\|}{2}\tilde{r}^{2}
, we choose
\tilde{r}
such that

\displaystyle\inf_{u\in\mathcal{B}_{\tilde{r}}(y^{*}(x))^{c}}\mathcal{E}_{x}(u)-\mathcal{E}_{x,r}=\frac{\|B^{\top}x\|}{2}(\tilde{r}^{2}-r^{2})=q,

i.e.,
\tilde{r}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\|B^{\top}x\|}q+r^{2}}
.
With this, (
17
) is bounded by
\frac{2e^{-\beta q}}{\varrho(\mathcal{B}_{r}(y^{*}(x)))}

and we obtain for (
18
) the upper bound

\displaystyle\|m_{\beta,\varrho}(x)-y^{*}(x)\|\leq\tilde{r}+\frac{2e^{-\beta q}}{\varrho(\mathcal{B}_{r}(y^{*}(x)))}.

Noticing that
\|B^{\top}x\|\geq\sigma_{\min}(B)
gives (
13
), which concludes the proof.
∎

In order to employ Lemma
5
,
we require a lower bound for the mass
\rho_{t}^{\beta}\left(\mathcal{B}_{r}(y^{*}(x))\right)
which is uniform for finite time intervals
[0,T]
. Under Assumption

on

]
.

A.1.2
Stability estimates in Wasserstein space

We now have available all necessary technical tools to provide a proof of Proposition
6
.

Proof of Proposition
6
.

To keep the notation concise,
let us write

\displaystyle v_{\beta}[\rho_{t}^{\beta}](x)\coloneqq\mathrm{P}_{x}\big(Vm_{\beta,\rho_{t}^{\beta}}(x)\big)\quad\text{ with }\quad m_{\beta,\rho_{t}^{\beta}}(x)\coloneqq\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\frac{\exp\left(\beta\langle x,By\rangle\right)}{\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\exp\left(\beta\langle x,Bz\rangle\right)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}^{\beta}(z)}y\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}^{\beta}(y)

as well as

\displaystyle v_{\infty}(x)\coloneqq\mathrm{P}_{x}\left(Vy^{*}(x)\right)\quad\text{ with }\quad y^{*}(x)\coloneqq\frac{B^{\top}x}{\left\|{B^{\top}x}\right\|}

for the vector fields of the continuity equations (
2
) and (
3
), which can be written compactly with the above definitions as

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}^{\beta}=-\divtmp\big(\rho_{t}^{\beta}v_{\beta}[\rho_{t}^{\beta}]\big)

and
\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=-\divtmp\left(\rho_{t}v_{\infty}\right)
, respectively.

Denoting by
\pi_{t}\in\Pi(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\rho_{t})
the optimal coupling between
\rho_{t}^{\beta}
and
\rho_{t}
for the Wasserstein distance,
we differentiate the squared Wasserstein distance along the solutions to the associated continuity equations
[
6
, Theorem 8.4.7]
to obtain for almost every
t\in[0,T]
that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\rho_{t})&=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\iint_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\times{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\|x-z\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}(x,z)\\
&=2\iint_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\times{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\big\langle x-z,v_{\beta}[\rho_{t}^{\beta}](x)-v_{\infty}(z)\big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}(x,z)\\
&\leq 2W_{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\rho_{t})\sqrt{\iint_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\times{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\big\|v_{\beta}[\rho_{t}^{\beta}](x)-v_{\infty}(z)\big\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}(x,z)},\end{aligned}

where we used the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to obtain the last step. Since by the chain rule it holds that
\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\rho_{t})=2W_{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\rho_{t})\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}W_{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\rho_{t})
,
we can divide both sides by
2W_{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\rho_{t})
and get

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}W_{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\rho_{t})&\leq\sqrt{\iint_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\times{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\big\|v_{\beta}[\rho_{t}^{\beta}](x)-v_{\infty}(z)\big\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}(x,z)}.\end{aligned}

(19)

Let us now estimate the integrand in (
19
).
For this, we decompose this term into an approximation error due to the finite inverse temperature
\beta
, and a stability term.
More precisely, with the triangle inequality we have for all
x,z\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
that

\displaystyle\big\|v_{\beta}[\rho_{t}^{\beta}](x)-v_{\infty}(z)\big\|\leq\big\|v_{\beta}[\rho_{t}^{\beta}](x)-v_{\infty}(x)\big\|+\big\|v_{\infty}(x)-v_{\infty}(z)\big\|.

(20)

To estimate the approximation error, i.e., the first term on the right-hand side of (
20
),
we note after recalling
\|\mathrm{P}_{x}\|\leq 1
, that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \big\|v_{\beta}[\rho_{t}^{\beta}](x)-v_{\infty}(x)\big\|&=\big\|\mathrm{P}_{x}\big(Vm_{\beta,\rho_{t}^{\beta}}(x)\big)-\mathrm{P}_{x}\big(Vy^{*}(x)\big)\big\|\\
&=\left\|\mathrm{P}_{x}\left(V\big(m_{\beta,\rho_{t}^{\beta}}(x)-y^{*}(x)\big)\right)\right\|\\
&\leq\sigma_{\max}(V)\big\|m_{\beta,\rho_{t}^{\beta}}(x)-y^{*}(x)\big\|.\end{aligned}

(21)

We can now employ Lemma
5
to estimate for arbitrary
r>0
and
q>0
that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \big\|m_{\beta,\rho_{t}^{\beta}}(x)-y^{*}(x)\big\|&\leq\sqrt{r^{2}+\frac{2q}{\sigma_{\min}(B)}}+\frac{2e^{-\beta q}}{\rho_{t}^{\beta}\bigl(\mathcal{B}_{r}(y^{*}(x))\bigr)}.\end{aligned}

(22)

To estimate the second summand on the right-hand side,
we first note that for every spherical cap
\mathcal{B}_{r}(y)\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
with
y\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
it holds

\displaystyle\rho_{t}^{\beta}(\mathcal{B}_{r}(y))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{B}_{r}(y)}f_{t}^{\beta}(z)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(z)\geq\left(\inf_{z\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(y)}f_{t}^{\beta}(z)\right)\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathcal{B}_{r}(y))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\ell_{0}e^{-C_{0}t}\,\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathcal{B}_{r}(y))

with the second line being thanks to Lemma
10
.
To bound
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathcal{B}_{r}(y))
, we observe that for
r\geq\sqrt{2}
the spherical cap
\mathcal{B}_{r}(y)
includes the halfsphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{y}:=\{x\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\,:\,\langle x,y\rangle\geq 0\}
.
To see this, note that for
x\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{y}
we have

\displaystyle\left\|{x-y}\right\|^{2}=2-2\langle x,y\rangle\leq 2\leq r^{2}

and hence
x\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(y)
. In particular, the surface measure of
\mathcal{B}_{r}(y)
with
r\geq\sqrt{2}
can be bounded from below by half of the measure of the sphere
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
, i.e., the number
C_{d}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{\pi^{d/2}}}{{\Gamma\left(\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}\right)}}
where
\Gamma
denotes the Gamma function. Furthermore, for
r<\sqrt{2}
, there exists a dimensional constant
c_{d}>0
such that
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathcal{B}_{r}(y))\geq c_{d}r^{d-1}
, which can be seen by spherical integration. Using the above, the surface measure of such a spherical cap can be bounded from below as

\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathcal{B}_{r}(y))\geq\begin{cases}c_{d}r^{d-1},\quad&r<\sqrt{2},\\
C_{d},\quad&r\geq\sqrt{2},\end{cases}

and we can continue (
22
) by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \big\|m_{\beta,\rho_{t}^{\beta}}(x)-y^{*}(x)\big\|&\leq\sqrt{r^{2}+\frac{2q}{\sigma_{\min}(B)}}+\begin{cases}\frac{4e^{-\beta q}}{\ell_{0}e^{-C_{0}t}c_{d}r^{d-1}},\quad&r<\sqrt{2},\\
\frac{4e^{-\beta q}}{\ell_{0}e^{-C_{0}t}C_{d}},\quad&r\geq\sqrt{2},\end{cases}\end{aligned}

(23)

for all
r>0
and
q>0
. Let us now choose

\displaystyle q\coloneqq q_{\beta}\coloneqq\frac{d\log(\beta+1)}{2\beta}\quad\text{ and }\quad r\coloneqq r_{\beta}:=\sqrt{q_{\beta}}

and notice that by definition
e^{-\beta q_{\beta}}=(\beta+1)^{-\frac{d}{2}}
. Using these definitions in (
23
), we get

\displaystyle\big\|m_{\beta,\rho_{t}^{\beta}}(x)-y^{*}(x)\big\|\leq\sqrt{q_{\beta}\left(1+\frac{2}{\sigma_{\min}(B)}\right)}+\begin{cases}\frac{4(\beta+1)^{-\frac{d}{2}}}{\ell_{0}e^{-C_{0}t}c_{d}r_{\beta}^{d-1}},\quad&r_{\beta}<\sqrt{2},\\
\frac{4(\beta+1)^{-\frac{d}{2}}}{\ell_{0}e^{-C_{0}t}C_{d}},\quad&r_{\beta}\geq\sqrt{2},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\,\leq\sqrt{\frac{d\log(\beta+1)}{\beta}\left(1+\frac{2}{\sigma_{\min}(B)}\right)}+\begin{cases}\frac{4e^{C_{0}t}}{c_{d}\ell_{0}}\!\left(\frac{2\beta}{d\log(\beta+1)}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\!\!(\beta+1)^{-\frac{d}{2}},\quad&r_{\beta}<\sqrt{2},\\
\frac{4e^{C_{0}t}}{C_{d}\ell_{0}}(\beta+1)^{-\frac{d}{2}},\quad&r_{\beta}\geq\sqrt{2},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\,\leq C(d,\sigma_{\min}(B),\ell_{0})\sqrt{\frac{\log(\beta+1)}{\beta}}e^{C_{0}t},

where we used in the last step that
\frac{d\log(\beta+1)}{\beta}
dominates both
\left(\frac{2\beta}{d\log(\beta+1)}\right)^{d-1}(\beta+1)^{-d}
and
(\beta+1)^{-d}
for all
\beta>0
, and that
e^{C_{0}t}\geq 1
for all
t\geq 0
.
Using this, we can conclude (
21
) by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \big\|v_{\beta}[\rho_{t}^{\beta}](x)-v_{\infty}(x)\big\|&\leq C(d,\sigma_{\min}(B),\sigma_{\max}(V),\ell_{0})\sqrt{\frac{\log(\beta+1)}{\beta}}e^{C_{0}t}.\end{aligned}

(24)

For the stability term, i.e., the second term in (
20
),
we first notice that for all
x,z\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
it holds by the triangle inequality and reverse triangle inequality that

\displaystyle\|y^{*}(x)-y^{*}(z)\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{B^{\top}x}{\|B^{\top}x\|}-\frac{B^{\top}z}{\|B^{\top}z\|}\right\|\leq\frac{\|B^{\top}x-B^{\top}z\|}{\|B^{\top}x\|}+\|B^{\top}z\|\left\|\frac{1}{\|B^{\top}x\|}-\frac{1}{\|B^{\top}z\|}\right\|

\displaystyle=\frac{\|B^{\top}x-B^{\top}z\|}{\|B^{\top}x\|}+\left\|\frac{\|B^{\top}z\|-\|B^{\top}x\|}{\|B^{\top}x\|}\right\|\leq 2\frac{\|B^{\top}x-B^{\top}z\|}{\|B^{\top}x\|}

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{\sigma_{\max}(B)}{\sigma_{\min}(B)}\,\|x-z\|,

where we have used that
B
is invertible and hence
\|B^{\top}x\|\geq\sigma_{\min}(B)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
.
Moreover, one has for all
x,z\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
that

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{P}_{x}-\mathrm{P}_{z}\|=\|xx^{\top}-zz^{\top}\|\leq\|x(x-z)^{\top}\|+\|(x-z)z^{\top}\|\leq 2\|x-z\|.

Using these two auxiliary estimates, and recalling that
\|\mathrm{P}_{x}\|\leq 1
,
\|y^{*}(x)\|=1
, we obtain by the triangle inequality

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \big\|v_{\infty}(x)-v_{\infty}(z)\big\|&=\big\|\mathrm{P}_{x}\left(Vy^{*}(x)\right)-\mathrm{P}_{z}\left(Vy^{*}(z)\right)\big\|\\
&\leq\big\|\mathrm{P}_{x}\big(V(y^{*}(x)-y^{*}(z))\big)\big\|+\big\|(\mathrm{P}_{x}-\mathrm{P}_{z})(Vy^{*}(z))\big\|\\
&\leq\big\|\mathrm{P}_{x}\big\|\big\|V(y^{*}(x)-y^{*}(z))\big\|+\big\|\mathrm{P}_{x}-\mathrm{P}_{z}\big\|\big\|Vy^{*}(z)\big\|\\
&\leq 2\sigma_{\max}(V)\frac{\sigma_{\max}(B)}{\sigma_{\min}(B)}\|x-z\|+2\sigma_{\max}(V)\|x-z\|\\
&=2\sigma_{\max}(V)\left(1+\frac{\sigma_{\max}(B)}{\sigma_{\min}(B)}\right)\|x-z\|.\end{aligned}

(25)

Inserting (
24
) and (
25
) into (
20
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\big\|v_{\beta}[\rho_{t}^{\beta}](x)-v_{\infty}(z)\big\|

\displaystyle\quad\quad\,\leq C(d,\sigma_{\min}(B),\sigma_{\max}(B),\sigma_{\max}(V),\ell_{0})\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(\beta+1)}{\beta}}e^{C_{0}t}+\|x-z\|\right).

Exploiting this estimate in
(
19
) and abbreviating
C_{1}\coloneqq C(d,\sigma_{\min}(B),\sigma_{\max}(B),\sigma_{\max}(V),\ell_{0})>0
as well as
\tilde{C}_{1}\coloneqq 2C_{0}+C_{1}
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}W_{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\rho_{t})

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\left(W_{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\rho_{t})+\sqrt{\frac{\log(\beta+1)}{\beta}}e^{C_{0}t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{C}_{1}\left(W_{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\rho_{t})+\sqrt{\frac{\log(\beta+1)}{\beta}}e^{C_{0}t}\right),

(26)

where the last bound is trivial by the definition of
\tilde{C}_{1}
, and we also recall that—as
\pi_{t}
denotes the optimal coupling between
\rho_{t}^{\beta}
and
\rho_{t}
—we have
W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\rho_{t})=\iint_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\times{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\|x-z\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}(x,z)
. Using the integrating factor method with integrating factor
e^{-\widetilde{C}_{1}t}
, we multiply both sides of (
26
) by the integrating factor to observe that, after a little bit of algebra, (
26
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\left(e^{-\widetilde{C}_{1}t}W_{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\rho_{t})\right)\leq\widetilde{C}_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log(\beta+1)}{\beta}}e^{(C_{0}-\widetilde{C}_{1})t}.

Since
\widetilde{C}_{1}>C_{0}
, we can integrate this inequality in time to obtain that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} e^{-\widetilde{C}_{1}t}W_{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\rho_{t})&\leq\widetilde{C}_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log(\beta+1)}{\beta}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{(C_{0}-\widetilde{C}_{1})s}\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&=\frac{\widetilde{C}_{1}}{\widetilde{C}_{1}-C_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{\log(\beta+1)}{\beta}}\left(1-e^{(C_{0}-\widetilde{C}_{1})t}\right),\end{aligned}

where we used that
\rho_{0}^{\beta}=\rho_{0}
.
Dividing now both sides by the integrating factor and using the definition of
\tilde{C}_{1}
, we are left with

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} W_{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\rho_{t})&\leq\frac{\widetilde{C}_{1}}{\widetilde{C}_{1}-C_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{\log(\beta+1)}{\beta}}\left(e^{\widetilde{C}_{1}t}-e^{C_{0}t}\right)\\
&=\frac{2C_{0}+C_{1}}{C_{0}+C_{1}}\sqrt{\frac{\log(\beta+1)}{\beta}}\left(e^{(2C_{0}+C_{1})t}-e^{C_{0}t}\right)\\
&\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\log(\beta+1)}{\beta}}\left(e^{(2C_{0}+C_{1})t}-e^{C_{0}t}\right)\end{aligned}

for all
t\in[0,T]
,
which concludes the proof, upon renaming the constants.
∎

A.2
Convergence of the flow
\rho_{t}
to
\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0}

The aim of this section is to estimate the convergence of the flow
\rho_{t}
to
\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0}
, i.e., to estimate

W_{2}(\rho_{t},\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0})
by proving Proposition
8
in combination with Lemmas
7
and
9
.

As a first step, we derive in Proposition
8
a time-evolution inequality for the functional
\mathcal{V}_{p}
defined in (
14
), as we detail in Appendix
A.2.2
. Using
\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{t}=\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0}
, as demonstrated by Lemma
9
, it holds
W_{2}(\rho_{t},\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0})=W_{2}(\rho_{t},\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{t})
,
we verify in Lemma
7
that the functional
\mathcal{V}_{p}
is indeed a suitable Lyapunov functional for the flow
\rho_{t}
since
W_{2}(\rho_{t},\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0})=W_{2}(\rho_{t},\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{t})\leq\sqrt{2\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{t})}
,
where
\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{t})
decays exponentially fast thanks to Proposition
8
.

A.2.1
Auxiliary result for Lyapunov-type convergence estimates in Wasserstein space

We first show that
R_{p}
as defined in (
14
) can be regarded as a distance from
E
(despite not formally being a distance).

Proof of Lemma
7
.

Fix
x\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\setminus E^{\perp}
.
To simplify notation, let us use the orthogonal decomposition of
x
,

\displaystyle x=u+v,\qquad\text{with}\quad u=\mathrm{P}_{E}x\in E,\quad v=(\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{P}_{E})x\in E^{\perp},\quad\left\|{u}\right\|^{2}+\left\|{v}\right\|^{2}=1.

We note that
u\not=0
since
x\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\setminus E^{\perp}
.
Using that
\Pi(x)=\frac{u}{\left\|{u}\right\|}
,
a simple computation shows

\displaystyle\left\|{x-\Pi(x)}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\left(1-\left\|{u}\right\|\right).

Denoting

\displaystyle q(x)\coloneqq\frac{\left\|{(\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{P}_{E})x}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|^{2}}=\frac{\left\|{v}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{u}\right\|^{2}},

and observing that

1+q(x)=1+\frac{\left\|{v}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{u}\right\|^{2}}=\frac{\left\|{u}\right\|^{2}+\left\|{v}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{u}\right\|^{2}}=\frac{1}{\left\|{u}\right\|^{2}}

and hence

\left\|{u}\right\|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+q(x)}}
,
we have

\displaystyle\left\|{x-\Pi(x)}\right\|^{2}=2\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+q(x)}}\right).

Consider now the scalar function

\displaystyle f(q)\coloneqq 1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+q}},\qquad q\geq 0.

First,

f(q)\leq 1
for all
q\geq 0
.
Second, we claim that
f(q)\leq q
for all
q\geq 0
.
To verify this, define
g(q)\coloneqq q-\big(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+q}}\big)
.
It holds
g(0)=0
and
g^{\prime}(q)=1-\frac{1}{2}(1+q)^{-3/2}\geq\frac{1}{2}>0
for all
q\geq 0
,
showing that
g(q)\geq 0
,
or equivalently
f(q)\leq q
.
Hence
f(q)\leq\min\{1,q\}
.
Since
p\in(0,1]
, we have for all
q\geq 0
that

\min\{1,q\}\leq q^{p}
.
Indeed, if
0\leq q\leq 1
, then
q\leq q^{p}
, while if
q\geq 1
, then
1\leq q^{p}
.

Combining the above bounds shows
1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+q(x)}}\leq q(x)^{p}

and therefore

\displaystyle\left\|{x-\Pi(x)}\right\|^{2}=2\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+q(x)}}\right)\leq 2q(x)^{p}=2R_{p}(x),

validating the first part of the claim.

To prove the second part of the statement,
define the coupling
\pi\coloneqq(\mathrm{Id},\Pi)_{\sharp}\varrho

between
\varrho
and
\Pi_{\sharp}\varrho
.
Then, by definition of the Wasserstein distance,

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\varrho,\Pi_{\sharp}\varrho)

\displaystyle\leq\iint_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\times{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\left\|{x-y}\right\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\left\|{x-\Pi(x)}\right\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(x)\leq 2\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}R_{p}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(x),

which concludes the proof.
∎

A.2.2
Lyapunov-type convergence estimates in Wasserstein space

We now have available all the necessary technical tools to prove Proposition
8
.

Proof of Proposition
8
.

Let us denote by
\rho_{t}
the solution to the continuity equation (
3
) in the zero-temperature limit.

We first deal with the trivial case of
k=\dim E=d
in which case
VB^{\top}
equals a multiple of the identity matrix and hence the continuity equation (
3
) simplifies to
\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=0
(using that
\mathrm{P}_{x}(x)=0
).
Hence, trivially,
\rho_{t}=\rho_{0}
for all
t>0
and
\Pi=\operatorname{Id}
such that
\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{t})=0
and also
W_{2}(\rho_{t},\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{t})=0
for all
t\geq 0
.

Ideally, we would like to use
R_{p}
as defined in (
14
) as test function for the weak formulation of (
3
).
However, since
R_{p}
is infinite on
E^{\perp}
, this is not possible.
We therefore consider the regularization

\displaystyle R_{p,K}(x)=\min\left\{R_{p}(x),K\right\}

for
K>0
, and the corresponding regularized Lyapunov functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{p,K}(\rho)\coloneqq\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}R_{p,K}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(x).

Since
R_{p,K}
is a Lipschitz function, it is differentiable almost everywhere by Rademacher’s theorem.
Hence, by chain rule and using the weak formulation of (
3
), it holds for almost all
t\in[0,T]
that

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{V}_{p,K}(\rho_{t})=\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\frac{\left\langle\nabla R_{p,K}(x),\mathrm{P}_{x}\left(VB^{\top}x\right)\right\rangle}{\left\|{B^{\top}x}\right\|}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x),

(27)

and it remains to estimate the integrand from above.
We start by deriving an upper bound for the inner product
\langle\nabla R_{p,K}(x),\mathrm{P}_{x}(VB^{\top}x)\rangle
.
To simplify notation, let us use the orthogonal decomposition of
x\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
,

\displaystyle x=u+v,\qquad\text{with}\quad u=\mathrm{P}_{E}x\in E,\quad v=(\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{P}_{E})x\in E^{\perp},\quad\left\|{u}\right\|^{2}+\left\|{v}\right\|^{2}=1,

allowing us to write
R_{p}(x)=\frac{\left\|{v}\right\|^{2p}}{\left\|{u}\right\|^{2p}}=R_{1}(x)^{p}
.
Using the idempotency of
\mathrm{P}_{E}
,
we obtain for
p=1
and for all
x\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\setminus E^{\perp}
that

\displaystyle\nabla R_{1}(x)=\frac{2v\left\|{u}\right\|^{2}-2u\left\|{v}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{u}\right\|^{4}}=2R_{1}(x)\left(\frac{v}{\left\|{v}\right\|^{2}}-\frac{u}{\left\|{u}\right\|^{2}}\right).

(28)

Furthermore, by the chain rule, it holds that

\nabla R_{p}(x)=pR_{1}(x)^{p-1}\nabla R_{1}(x),

(29)

and for all
x\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
that

\displaystyle\nabla R_{p,K}(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{R_{p}(x)\leq K}\nabla R_{p}(x),

(30)

i.e.,
\nabla R_{p,K}(x)=0
if
R_{p}(x)>K
, which in particular holds for all
x\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\cap E^{\perp}
.
Let us now bound the quantity
\langle\nabla R_{p,K}(x),\mathrm{P}_{x}(VB^{\top}x)\rangle
.
First, note that
\langle\nabla R_{1}(x),x\rangle=0
since by (
28
)

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{v}{\left\|{v}\right\|^{2}}-\frac{u}{\|u\|^{2}},x\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle v,u\rangle}{\|v\|^{2}}+\frac{\left\|{v}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{v}\right\|^{2}}-\frac{\left\|{u}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{u}\right\|^{2}}-\frac{\langle u,v\rangle}{\|u\|^{2}}=0,

where we have used that
\langle u,v\rangle=0
by orthogonality.
Recalling that
\mathrm{P}_{x}(VB^{\top})x=VB^{\top}x-\langle x,VB^{\top}x\rangle x
, we compute using (
30
) for all
x\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
with
R_{1}(x)\leq K
that

\displaystyle\langle\nabla R_{1,K}(x),\mathrm{P}_{x}(VB^{\top}x)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla R_{1}(x),\mathrm{P}_{x}(VB^{\top}x)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla R_{1}(x),VB^{\top}x\rangle-\langle x,VB^{\top}x\rangle\langle\nabla R_{1}(x),x\rangle=\langle\nabla R_{1}(x),VB^{\top}x\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla R_{1}(x),VB^{\top}(u+v)\rangle=\langle\nabla R_{1}(x),VB^{\top}u\rangle+\langle\nabla R_{1}(x),VB^{\top}v\rangle

\displaystyle=2R_{1}(x)\left(\frac{\langle v,VB^{\top}u\rangle}{\left\|{v}\right\|^{2}}-\frac{\langle u,VB^{\top}u\rangle}{\left\|{u}\right\|^{2}}+\frac{\langle v,VB^{\top}v\rangle}{\left\|{v}\right\|^{2}}-\frac{\langle u,VB^{\top}v\rangle}{\left\|{u}\right\|^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=2R_{1}(x)\left(\frac{\langle v,VB^{\top}v\rangle}{\left\|{v}\right\|^{2}}-\frac{\langle u,VB^{\top}u\rangle}{\left\|{u}\right\|^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq-2(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})R_{1}(x)=-2\gamma R_{1}(x),

where we used (
28
) to obtain the fourth line
and for the fifth line that
by orthogonality and since
E
is an eigenspace of
VB^{\top}
,

\langle v,VB^{\top}u\rangle=\mu_{1}\langle v,u\rangle=0
as well as
\langle u,VB^{\top}v\rangle=\langle VB^{\top}u,v\rangle=\mu_{1}\langle u,v\rangle=0
.
To obtain the inequality in the last line, note that

\langle v,VB^{\top}v\rangle\leq\mu_{2}\left\|{v}\right\|^{2}

and

\langle u,VB^{\top}u\rangle=\mu_{1}\left\|{u}\right\|^{2}
,
since
E
is the dominant eigenspace of
VB^{\top}
.
Further recall the identity
\gamma=\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}
.

Combining (
29
) with the above inequality yields the case of general
p\in(0,1]
that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle\nabla R_{p,K}(x),\mathrm{P}_{x}(VB^{\top}x)\rangle&=\langle\nabla R_{p}(x),\mathrm{P}_{x}(VB^{\top}x)\rangle\\
&=pR_{1}(x)^{p-1}\langle\nabla R_{1}(x),\mathrm{P}_{x}(VB^{\top}x)\rangle\leq-2p\gamma R_{p}(x).\end{aligned}

(31)

Employing (
30
) together with (
31
) in (
27
)
and noticing that

\left\|{B^{\top}x}\right\|\leq\sigma_{\max}(B)\left\|{x}\right\|=\sigma_{\max}(B)
,
we obtain the differential inequality

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{V}_{p,K}(\rho_{t})

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\mathbbm{1}_{R_{p}(x)\leq K}\frac{\langle\nabla R_{p}(x),\mathrm{P}_{x}(VB^{\top}x)\rangle}{\left\|{B^{\top}x}\right\|}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{2p\gamma}{\sigma_{\max}(B)}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\mathbbm{1}_{R_{p}(x)\leq K}R_{p}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x).

Integrating this inequality in
t
yields by the fundamental theorem of calculus

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{p,K}(\rho_{t})-\mathcal{V}_{p,K}(\rho_{0})\leq-\frac{2p\gamma}{\sigma_{\max}(B)}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\mathbbm{1}_{R_{p}(x)\leq K}R_{p}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{s}(x)\,\mathrm{d}s.

(32)

Let us now take the limit
K\to\infty
.
Noticing that
R_{p,K}(x)
converges point-wise from below to
R_{p}(x)
as
K\to\infty
, by the Beppo Levi monotone convergence theorem,
\mathcal{V}_{p,K}(\rho_{t})\to\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{t})
and
\mathcal{V}_{p,K}(\rho_{0})\to\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{0})
. Analogously, since

\mathbbm{1}_{R_{p}(x)\leq K}R_{p}(x)
converges point-wise from below to
R_{p}(x)
as
K\to\infty
, by the Beppo Levi monotone convergence theorem it holds

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\mathbbm{1}_{R_{p}(x)\leq K}R_{p}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{s}(x)\rightarrow\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}R_{p}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{s}(x)=\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{s}).

Since this last convergence is also monotone for every
s
, we can apply the Beppo Levi monotone convergence theorem once again,
and from (
32
), after taking the limit
K\to\infty
on both sides of the inequality, infer

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{t})-\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{0})\leq-\frac{2p\gamma}{\sigma_{\max}(B)}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s.

An application of Grönwall’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{t})\leq\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{0})\exp\left(-\frac{2p\gamma}{\sigma_{\max}(B)}t\right),

which concludes the proof.
∎

A.2.3
Invariance of
\Pi
under the zero-temperature equation

Before giving the proof of Lemma
9
,
let us first verify the following technical result.

Lemma 11
.

Assume that the matrices
B
and
V

satisfy Assumption
1
.
Then, for
x\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\setminus E^{\perp}
, the Jacobian
D\Pi(x)
of
\Pi
as defined in (
4
) exists and equals

\displaystyle D\Pi(x)=\frac{1}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|}\left(\mathrm{P}_{E}-\frac{\mathrm{P}_{E}x\left(\mathrm{P}_{E}x\right)^{\top}}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|^{2}}\right).

Moreover, it holds

\displaystyle D\Pi(x)\mathrm{P}_{x}\left(\frac{VB^{\top}x}{\left\|{B^{\top}x}\right\|}\right)=0.

Proof.

To obtain the first claim,
we compute for
x\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\setminus E^{\perp}
with quotient rule that

\displaystyle D\Pi(x)=\frac{\mathrm{P}_{E}}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|}-\frac{\mathrm{P}_{E}x\left(\mathrm{P}_{E}x\right)^{\top}}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|^{3}}=\frac{1}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|}\left(\mathrm{P}_{E}-\frac{\mathrm{P}_{E}x\left(\mathrm{P}_{E}x\right)^{\top}}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|^{2}}\right).

For the second claim,
using that
\mathrm{P}_{x}(y)=y-\langle x,y\rangle x
and that
\langle\mathrm{P}_{E}x,x\rangle=\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|^{2}
,
we derive for arbitrary
y\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left(\mathrm{P}_{E}-\frac{\mathrm{P}_{E}x(\mathrm{P}_{E}x)^{\top}}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|^{2}}\right)\mathrm{P}_{x}(y)&=\mathrm{P}_{E}y-\frac{\langle\mathrm{P}_{E}x,y\rangle}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|^{2}}\mathrm{P}_{E}x-\langle x,y\rangle\left(\mathrm{P}_{E}x-\frac{\mathrm{P}_{E}x\langle\mathrm{P}_{E}x,x\rangle}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|^{2}}\right)\\
&=\mathrm{P}_{E}y-\frac{\langle\mathrm{P}_{E}x,y\rangle}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|^{2}}\mathrm{P}_{E}x.\end{aligned}

(33)

To simplify notation, let us use the orthogonal decomposition of
x
,

\displaystyle x=u+v,\qquad\text{with}\quad u=\mathrm{P}_{E}x\in E,\quad v=(\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{P}_{E})x\in E^{\perp},\quad\left\|{u}\right\|^{2}+\left\|{v}\right\|^{2}=1.

Specifically, for
\hat{y}=\frac{VB^{\top}x}{\left\|{B^{\top}x}\right\|}
,
we have
\hat{y}=\frac{\mu_{1}u}{\left\|{B^{\top}x}\right\|}+\frac{VB^{\top}v}{\left\|{B^{\top}x}\right\|}
.
Since
VB^{\top}v\in E^{\perp}
(here, we crucially use the symmetry of
VB^{\top}
),
it holds

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}_{E}\hat{y}=\frac{\mu_{1}u}{\left\|{B^{\top}x}\right\|}=\frac{\mu_{1}\mathrm{P}_{E}x}{\left\|{B^{\top}x}\right\|}\quad\text{ and hence }\quad\langle\mathrm{P}_{E}x,\hat{y}\rangle=\mu_{1}\frac{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{B^{\top}x}\right\|}.

Using those identities in the last step,
we have with (
33
)
that

\displaystyle D\Pi(x)\mathrm{P}_{x}(\hat{y})=\frac{1}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|}\left(\mathrm{P}_{E}-\frac{\mathrm{P}_{E}x(\mathrm{P}_{E}x)^{\top}}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|^{2}}\right)\mathrm{P}_{x}(\hat{y})=\frac{1}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|}\left(\mathrm{P}_{E}\hat{y}-\frac{\langle\mathrm{P}_{E}x,\hat{y}\rangle}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|^{2}}\mathrm{P}_{E}x\right)=0,

which concludes the proof.
∎

With this auxiliary technical tool, we can now provide a proof of Lemma
9
.

Proof of Lemma
9
.

To keep the notation concise,
let us write

\displaystyle v_{\infty}(x)\coloneqq\mathrm{P}_{x}\left(Vy^{*}(x)\right)\quad\text{ with }\quad y^{*}(x)\coloneqq\frac{B^{\top}x}{\left\|{B^{\top}x}\right\|}

for the vector field of the continuity equation (
3
).
Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}},\mathbb{R})
be a smooth test function and choose a smooth cut-off function
\eta\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}([0,\infty),[0,1])
satisfying

\displaystyle\eta(r)=0\quad\text{for }r\leq 1,\qquad\eta(r)=1\quad\text{for }r\geq 2,\qquad|\eta^{\prime}|\leq 2.

For
\delta>0
, define
\eta_{\delta}(r)\coloneqq\eta(r/\delta)
and

\displaystyle\varphi_{\delta}(x)\coloneqq\eta_{\delta}\left(\|\mathrm{P}_{E}x\|\right)\varphi(\Pi(x)).

Clearly,
\varphi_{\delta}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}},\mathbb{R})
and hence an admissible test function.
Using the weak formulation of (
3
) for
\varphi_{\delta}
,
it holds

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\varphi_{\delta}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\left\langle\nabla\varphi_{\delta}(x),v_{\infty}(x)\right\rangle\!\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x),

where we can compute by product and chain rule

\displaystyle\nabla\varphi_{\delta}(x)=\eta_{\delta}(\|\mathrm{P}_{E}x\|)\nabla(\varphi\circ\Pi)(x)+\eta_{\delta}^{\prime}(\|\mathrm{P}_{E}x\|)\varphi(\Pi(x))\,\nabla\|\mathrm{P}_{E}x\|,\qquad x\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}},

noting that
\nabla\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|
exists on the support of
\eta_{\delta}^{\prime}
.
Since by Lemma
11
,
D\Pi(x)v_{\infty}(x)=0
for all
x\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\setminus E^{\perp}
, we have for all
x\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\setminus E^{\perp}
that

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla(\varphi\circ\Pi)(x),v_{\infty}(x)\right\rangle=\left\langle D\Pi(x)^{\top}\nabla\varphi(\Pi(x)),v_{\infty}(x)\right\rangle=\left\langle\nabla\varphi(\Pi(x)),D\Pi(x)v_{\infty}(x)\right\rangle=0.

Furthermore, for
x\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\setminus E^{\perp}
we have
\nabla\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|=\frac{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|}
.
Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\varphi_{\delta}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\eta_{\delta}^{\prime}\left(\|\mathrm{P}_{E}x\|\right)\varphi(\Pi(x))\left\langle\frac{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|},v_{\infty}(x)\right\rangle\!\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x).

(34)

To simplify notation, let us use the orthogonal decomposition of
x
,

\displaystyle x=u+v,\qquad\text{with}\quad u=\mathrm{P}_{E}x\in E,\quad v=(\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{P}_{E})x\in E^{\perp},\quad\left\|{u}\right\|^{2}+\left\|{v}\right\|^{2}=1.

Using this notation and that
v_{\infty}(x)=\mathrm{P}_{x}\left(\frac{VB^{\top}x}{\left\|{B^{\top}x}\right\|}\right)=\frac{VB^{\top}x-\langle x,VB^{\top}x\rangle x}{\left\|{B^{\top}x}\right\|}
,
we compute

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|},v_{\infty}(x)\right\rangle=\frac{\langle u,v_{\infty}(x)\rangle}{\left\|{u}\right\|}=\frac{\mu_{1}\|u\|^{2}-\langle x,VB^{\top}x\rangle\|u\|^{2}}{\left\|{B^{\top}x}\right\|\left\|{u}\right\|}=\frac{\|u\|(\mu_{1}-\langle x,VB^{\top}x\rangle)}{\left\|{B^{\top}x}\right\|},

where we used in the second step that by the invariance of
E
and
E^{\perp}
, it holds
\mathrm{P}_{E}(VB^{\top}x)=\mu_{1}u
.
This allows us to bound for a constant
C>0
depending only on
B
and
VB^{\top}
that

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle\frac{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|},v_{\infty}(x)\right\rangle\right|\leq C\|u\|=C\|\mathrm{P}_{E}x\|.

Since
\eta^{\prime}_{\delta}
is supported in
A_{\delta}\coloneqq\{x\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}:\delta\leq\|\mathrm{P}_{E}x\|\leq 2\delta\}
,
we can bound (
34
) as

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\varphi_{\delta}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)\right|\leq C\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\underbrace{|\eta_{\delta}^{\prime}(\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|)|}_{\leq\frac{2}{\delta}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{\delta}}}\,|\varphi(\Pi(x))|\,\underbrace{\left\|{\mathrm{P}_{E}x}\right\|}_{\leq 2\delta}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)\leq C\rho_{t}(A_{\delta}),

(35)

where
C>0
is another constant depending solely on
B
,
VB^{\top}
, and the maximum of
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}},\mathbb{R})
on the compact sphere
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
.
It remains to estimate
\rho_{t}(A_{\delta})
.
Since on
A_{\delta}

it holds
\|\mathrm{P}_{E}x\|^{2}\leq 4\delta^{2}

and for
\delta\leq 1/4
moreover

\displaystyle\left\|{(\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{P}_{E})x}\right\|^{2}=\left\|{v}\right\|^{2}=1-\left\|{u}\right\|^{2}=1-\|\mathrm{P}_{E}x\|^{2}\geq 1-4\delta^{2}\geq 3/4,

we have for all
x\in A_{\delta}
that

\displaystyle R_{p}(x)=\frac{\|(\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{P}_{E})x\|^{2p}}{\|\mathrm{P}_{E}x\|^{2p}}\geq c_{p}\delta^{-2p}

for a suitable constant
c_{p}>0
.
We can therefore estimate

\displaystyle\rho_{t}(A_{\delta})=\int_{A_{\delta}}1\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)\leq\frac{\delta^{2p}}{c_{p}}\int_{A_{\delta}}R_{p}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)\leq\frac{\delta^{2p}}{c_{p}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}R_{p}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)=\frac{\delta^{2p}}{c_{p}}\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{t}).

Thanks to Proposition
8
,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{t})\leq\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{0})<\infty
,
and thus, as
\delta\to 0
,
we get from (
35
) that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\varphi_{\delta}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)\right|\leq C\delta^{2p}\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{0})\to 0,

(36)

where
C>0
now depends also on
p
.
By the fundamental theorem of calculus,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\varphi_{\delta}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)-\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\varphi_{\delta}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(x)=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}s}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\varphi_{\delta}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{s}(x)\right)\!\,\mathrm{d}s.

(37)

Recall that by definition of the cut-off function
\eta
it holds
|\varphi_{\delta}|\leq\varphi\circ\Pi
for any
\delta>0
and
\varphi_{\delta}\rightarrow\varphi\circ\Pi
pointwise on
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\setminus E^{\perp}
as
\delta\rightarrow 0
.
Since
\mathcal{V}_{p}(\rho_{t})<\infty
by Proposition
8
,
it holds
\rho_{t}(E^{\perp})=0
.
Hence,
\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}(\varphi\circ\Pi)(x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\backslash E^{\perp}}(\varphi\circ\Pi)(x)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)\leq\max_{z\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\varphi(z)<\infty
since
\varphi
is a smooth function on a compact space.
We can now pass to the limit
\delta\to 0
using the dominated convergence theorem to infer the convergence of the integrals on the left-hand side of (

) to
\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\varphi(\Pi(x))\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)-\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\varphi(\Pi(x))\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(x)
.
With the right-hand side of (
37
) vanishing due to (
36
) as
\delta\rightarrow 0
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}(\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{t})(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\varphi(\Pi(x))\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\varphi(\Pi(x))\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}(\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0})(x).

Since this holds for all smooth
\varphi
, we conclude that
\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{t}=\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0}
, as desired.
∎

Appendix B
Heuristics supporting Conjecture
1

In this section, we provide heuristics supporting Conjecture
1
. Some of them can actually be found in the recent work
[
4
]
, but we include them here for completeness.

One-point dynamics.

First, we consider the trivial case where the token distribution is completely collapsed. This captures the situation where the initial distribution
\rho_{0}
is just supported on a single point and approximates the more interesting situation where at some positive time
t>0
the token distribution is (almost) collapsed.

We can show that from there on the distribution aligns with the dominant eigenspace of
V
. To see this note that for
\varrho=\delta_{x_{0}}
with
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\exp(\beta\langle x,By\rangle)}{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\exp(\beta\langle x,Bz\rangle)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(z)}Vy\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(y)=Vx_{0}.

It is then easy to observe that the solution of (
2
) is given by
\rho_{t}^{\beta}=\delta_{x(t)}
where
t\mapsto x(t)
solves the Oja flow

\dot{x}(t)=\mathrm{P}_{x(t)}Vx(t),\qquad x(0)=x_{0}.

It follows from
[
13
, Lemmas A.13, A.15]
(or a straightforward computation using the quotient rule) that
x(t)=\frac{y(t)}{\left\|{y(t)}\right\|}
where
t\mapsto y(t)
solves the system of linear ordinary differential equations

\displaystyle\dot{y}(t)=Vy(t).

Assuming for simplicity that
V
is symmetric and that its largest eigenvalue is simple, it follows from the theory of linear ordinary differential equations that
y(t)=\exp(Vt)y(0)
and hence that
x(t)
converges exponentially fast to an eigenvector
v
lying in the eigenspace of
V
that corresponds to its largest eigenvalue as
t\to\infty
provided that
\langle x_{0},v\rangle\neq 0
. As a consequence,
W_{2}(\rho_{t}^{\beta},\delta_{v})=\left\|{x(t)-v}\right\|\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. This reasoning can be extended to more general classes of matrices
V
by using their Jordan normal form.

Some stationary distributions.

The above shows, in particular, that
\delta_{v}
is a stationary distribution of (
2
).
Let us now investigate slightly more complicated stationary distributions of this equation.
For this, let us assume that
\varrho=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{v_{i}}
where
v_{i}
are eigenvectors of
V
with eigenvalue
\lambda>0
.
Abbreviating
N(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(\beta\langle x,Bv_{i}\rangle\right)
, we can compute the drift vector field in (
2
) as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\exp(\beta\langle x,By\rangle)}{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\exp(\beta\langle x,Bz\rangle)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(z)}Vy\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N(x)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(\beta\langle x,Bv_{i}\rangle\right)Vv_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{N(x)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(\beta\langle x,Bv_{i}\rangle\right)v_{i},\qquad\forall x\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}.

Hence, for any smooth
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}},\mathbb{R})
, using that
\mathrm{P}_{v_{i}}(v_{i})=0
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left\langle\nabla\phi(x),\mathrm{P}_{x}\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\exp(\beta\langle x,By\rangle)}{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\exp(\beta\langle x,Bz\rangle)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(z)}Vy\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(y)\right)\right\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(x)

\displaystyle\qquad\,=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\nabla\phi(v_{i}),\mathrm{P}_{v_{i}}\left(\frac{\lambda}{N(v_{i})}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\left(\beta\langle v_{i},Bv_{j}\rangle\right)v_{j}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\,=\frac{\lambda}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{N(v_{i})}\left\langle\nabla\phi(v_{i}),\mathrm{P}_{v_{i}}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}\exp\left(\beta\langle v_{i},Bv_{j}\rangle\right)v_{j}\right)\right\rangle.

(38)

If
n=1
, meaning that
\varrho=\delta_{v}
for some
v\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
with
Vv=\lambda v
, then (
38
) equals zero.
This shows that
\varrho=\delta_{v}
is a stationary distribution of (
2
).

A slightly more complex example is
n=2
and
\varrho=\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta_{v}+\delta_{-v}\right)
, for which we have
\mathrm{P}_{v}(-v)=-v-\langle v,-v\rangle v=0
and
\mathrm{P}_{-v}(v)=v-\langle-v,v\rangle(-v)=0
.
Hence, (
38
) equals zero and
\varrho
is a stationary distribution of (
2
).
Such situation is depicted in Figure
2
.

These two classes of stationary distributions concentrated on eigenvectors of
V
were already identified in
[
4
, Lemma 7]
and were referred to as consensus and bipartite consensus therein.
In
[
4
, Theorem 2]
, it was also shown that consensus on an eigenvector with respect to the largest eigenvalue of
V
is a stable equilibrium and that bipartite consensus can be stable under certain conditions.

C.3
Gradient flow case

In this section, we study the alignment behavior in the gradient flow case, i.e.,
B=\pm V
. In particular, as highlighted in
[
16
]
, in this case the self-attention dynamics try to either maximize or minimize the energy

\mathcal{E}_{B}(\varrho)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}e^{\langle x,By\rangle}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(x)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho(y).

These experiments complement our theory by testing the predicted alignment behavior in the more specific gradient flow setting, where the dynamics admit an additional variational interpretation. In the following, we perform numerical experiments for four cases: maximization and minimization, each with positive and negative definite matrices. Throughout,
v_{\max}(B)
and
v_{\min}(B)
denote a unit eigenvector of
B
associated with its largest and smallest eigenvalues, respectively.

Maximization case.

For
B=V
, the self-attention dynamics try to maximize the energy
\mathcal{E}_{B}
and the optimal stationary state is a single Dirac at
\pm v_{\max}(B)
, i.e.,
\rho^{\text{opt}}=\delta_{v_{\max}(B)}
or
\rho^{\text{opt}}=\delta_{-v_{\max}(B)}
. However, the example in
[
16
, Figure 2]
suggests that, for large
\beta
, the stationary state supported fully on the dominant eigenspace of
B
is more likely to occur. This is consistent with Theorem
1
, since in this case
VB^{\top}=B^{2}
. We display this behavior in Figure
6
, where
the purple line displays the evolution of
{\mathcal{E}_{B}(\rho_{t}^{\beta,n})}/{\mathcal{E}_{B}(\rho^{\text{opt}})}
, i.e., the energy of the ensemble normalized by the theoretical best energy. For small values of
\beta
, the iteration indeed converges towards an energetically optimal state, which is a single Dirac. However, since the initial configuration is sampled uniformly on the sphere,
\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0}^{n}
is likely clustered both at
v_{\max}(B)
and
-v_{\max}(B)
, which causes the Wasserstein distance in Figure
6
to be large. For larger values of
\beta
, one instead observes convergence towards energetically suboptimal states, which are closer to
\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0}^{n}
.

On the other hand, if
B
is negative semi-definite, the optimal state is
\rho^{\text{opt}}=\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{v_{\min}(B)}+\delta_{-v_{\min}(B)})
, where in this case
v_{\min}(B)=v_{\max}(B^{2})
. In Figure
7
, we observe that even for small values of
\beta
, we indeed obtain convergence of
\rho_{t}^{\beta,n}
towards
\Pi_{\sharp}\rho_{0}^{n}
. Moreover, we also obtain convergence towards energetically optimal states for large values of
\beta
.
