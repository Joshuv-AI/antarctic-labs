Efficacy and safety of long-acting injectable paliperidone palmitate 1-month and 3-month formulations in the maintenance treatment of schizophrenia: a single-arm, open-label, interventional study in Rwandan healthcare settings
Authors: Rutakayile Bizoza, Jordy Mehawej, Emmanuel Musoni-Rwililiza, Larry Alphs, Ibrahim Turkoz, Liping Sun, Leslie Killion, Carla Decotelli-Mendes, Robert Karl Knight
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the long-term efficacy and safety of paliperidone palmitate once-monthly (PP1M) and once-every-3-months (PP3M) PP long-acting injectable antipsychotics in young adults with schizophrenia treated in routine Rwandan healthcare settings.DesignThis was a single-arm, open-label, multicentre interventional study consisting of two consecutive phases: an observation/run-in (OR) phase (weeks 1–24), during which participants received standard-of-care oral antipsychotics, and a lead-in/maintenance (LM) phase (weeks 25–66), during which participants first received PP1M for ≥17 weeks and subsequently transitioned to PP3M for maintenance treatment. For analysis, the full week 1–66 period was considered the follow-up (FU) phase.Setting/participants93 adults aged 18–35 years with schizophrenia were enrolled across five Rwandan centres; 86 (92.5%) completed the 66-week study.Endpoints and measurementsThe primary endpoint was the change in Clinical Global Impression–Severity of Schizophrenia (CGI-SS) score from baseline (week 1) to the end of the study (week 66). Secondary endpoints included changes in CGI-SS and Personal and Social Performance (PSP) scores in both OR and LM phases. Safety was assessed through treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), laboratory tests, vital signs, ECG and suicidality measured with the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale.Statistical analysisWithin-group changes were analysed using one-sample or paired Student’s t-tests, with a one-sided significance threshold of 0.025 for the primary endpoint. Descriptive statistics were used for safety outcomes.ResultsOver the 66 weeks FU period, CGI-SS improved significantly (mean change −1.4 (SD 0.69), 95% CI −1.50 to −1.22; p<0.001). Minimal improvement occurred during the OR phase (mean change −0.1), whereas the LM phase showed clinically meaningful improvements (mean change −1.2 (SD 0.67), p<0.001). PSP scores improved significantly during LM (mean+17.1 (SD 13.62), p<0.001) and across the entire FU period (mean+17.6 (SD 11.13), p<0.001). Overall, 75% of participants experienced ≥1 TEAE, mostly mild/moderate; the most common were asthenia (20.7%), nasopharyngitis (14.1%) and urinary tract infection (10.9%). Two deaths occurred during LM, neither related to study treatment.ConclusionsSwitching from oral antipsychotics to PP1M followed by PP3M resulted in significant improvements in symptom severity and psychosocial functioning over 66 weeks, with no new safety concerns in this real-world Rwandan setting.Trial registration numberNCT04940039.Keywords: PSYCHIATRY, Schizophrenia & psychotic disorders, Psychosocial Intervention, MENTAL HEALTH

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a mental-health disorder that significantly impairs an individual’s ability to learn, work, perform self-care, and establish interpersonal relationships.1 2 Its social and economic consequences are profound, impacting not only participants but also their families and society as a whole.2 The treatment of schizophrenia in Rwanda, as in many low-income countries, faces significant challenges including inadequate or constrained healthcare infrastructure, geographical barriers, limited trained mental health professionals, social stigma, limited understanding about mental disorders, and high rates of treatment non-adherence.3 4 Rwanda is a landlocked sub-Saharan country with a population of 13 million of which over 80% are dispersed rural residents served by 12 practising psychiatrists primarily located in the capital Kigali.5 Such constraints contribute to high rates of non-adherence to daily oral antipsychotics (OAPs), with subsequent elevated risk of relapse, hospitalisation and long-term functional decline.

At the same time, Rwanda’s decentralised, community-based healthcare extends services to rural areas through a robust nationwide network of community health workers, facilitating case identification, supervised administration, follow-up (FU) and coordination between primary care and mental-health services.6 7 This infrastructure offers a platform to test scalable delivery of long-acting injectables (LAIs) and to assess operational feasibility, training requirements and care coordination between primary care and mental health services. However, despite the inclusion of second-generation LAIs on the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines, they are not yet incorporated into Rwanda’s national treatment guidelines for schizophrenia which only include flupentixol decanoate and haloperidol decanoate a first-generation antipsychotic-LAIs.8

In Rwanda, while OAPs are a first-line treatment for schizophrenia, their daily dosing often results in poor adherence and increased relapse risk. LAI antipsychotics including those of paliperidone palmitate (PP) administered once a month (PP1M) or every 3 months (PP3M) or every 6 months (PP6M) offer a practical solution through less frequent, supervised administration, improving adherence and reducing relapses and hospitalisations.9,13 Although robust international evidence demonstrates reductions in relapse and hospitalisation compared with OAPs, data from African healthcare settings remain scarce. It is therefore uncertain whether benefits observed in higher-resource environments translate to health systems characterised by workforce shortages, constrained service capacity and high reliance on community-based FU. Given Rwanda’s combination of high adherence barriers, strong community-based delivery platforms, and a need for local data to inform policy, evaluating LAIs in routine Rwandan care is both timely and necessary.

Our previous open-label study of PP1M and PP3M aimed to assess the feasibility of conducting a large clinical trial in Rwanda was terminated early due to challenges in maintaining Good Clinical Practice requirements and regulatory standards with limited local resources.3 Although the study was terminated early, it provided valuable insights into areas for improvement in study execution within the Rwandan mental healthcare system, such as study governance, site infrastructure and trial conduct procedures. Building on this prior feasibility work conducted within Rwanda’s mental-health system3, the current CASPAR (Clinical Study to Assess the Treament of Schizophrenia with Paliperidone Palmitate in Rwandan Healthcare Setting) study was designed to generate Rwanda-specific, real-world evidence on PP LAIs. The CASPAR study evaluated the clinical, functional, and safety outcomes associated with switching from OAPs to PP LAIs in routine Rwandan healthcare settings. Over 66 weeks of FU, the study assessed changes in symptom severity using the Clinical Global Impression–Severity of Schizophrenia (CGI-SS) scale, changes in social and functional performance using the Personal and Social Performance (PSP) scale, and the safety and tolerability profile of PP. Participants initially remained on standard-of-care (SoC) OAPs during an observation/run-in phase (OR) before transitioning to PP1M followed by PP3M in the lead-in and maintenance (LM) phase, providing a pragmatic evaluation of LAI implementation in a low-resource environment.

Methods

Study participants

Young adults (men or women aged ≥18 to ≤35 years) with a current diagnosis of schizophrenia (by Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview-Screen/MINI [Module K]), at least moderately ill (CGI-SS score ≥4), requiring either treatment initiation/change in treatment to improve safety, efficacy, or adherence, and has a primary caregiver who is open to take part in this study were included. Participants had to be eligible to receive treatment within the Rwandan mental healthcare system and willing to receive injections and able to complete all self-administered questionnaires. Participants with a history of comorbid psychiatric and/or physical illnesses, organic brain syndromes, significant comorbid substance abuse or received any investigational intervention 30 days before the planned first study dose were not eligible to participate in the study. Participants with hypersensitivity, intolerance or allergies or prior poor response to risperidone PP were excluded (detailed inclusion/exclusion criteria are presented in online supplemental Table S1).

Study design

This single-arm, multicentre, open-label, interventional study in Rwanda assessed the PP LAIs (PP1M and PP3M) in participants with schizophrenia using MINI (Module K). The open-label design was chosen to reflect real-world treatment conditions and to accommodate operational limitations at participating sites, which restricted the feasibility of randomised or blinded designs. The study included three phases (figure 1): (1) ‘screening’ phase of up to 4 weeks, (2) followed by an OR (observation and run-in) phase (consisting of 24 weeks observation period with standard-of-care (SoC) and a 3-day run-in period) and (3) subsequent LM phase, consisting of a 17 weeks lead-in period (PP1M) and 24 weeks maintenance period (PP3M). The OR phase was 24 weeks in duration. This was followed by a run-in period of up to 1 week to allow for washout of previous antipsychotic medications and a 3-day treatment with oral risperidone to determine tolerability. As none of the patients had prior exposure to either risperidone or paliperidone all were included in the oral tolerance test. A minority of patients needed a washout prior to testing. The few days of variability in run-in duration was expected, operationally appropriate and does not affect the interpretation of study outcome. The entire study period (excluding screening) spanning through OR and LM phases from week 1 to week 66 (ie, end-of-study (EOS), was considered as the overall FU phase (for analysis purpose only), which analysed the long-term symptomatic response.

Study interventions

In the OR phase, participants continued their SoC treatment as prescribed by their physicians, using first-generation and second-generation OAPs. During the run-in period, participants without prior exposure to risperidone, oral paliperidone extended release or PP, received oral risperidone (3 mg/day for 3 days) to assess tolerability before initiating PP1M injections. Tapering of any previous OAPs can take place during the last 2 weeks of the observation phase. Participants who were already tolerant to risperidone or oral paliperidone proceeded directly to LM phase lead-in treatment period and received PP1M (50−150 mg eq.) doses for at least 17 weeks, with a maximum of 25 weeks. Initially, participants were given loading doses of 150 and 100 mg eq. on day 1 and day 8, respectively, into the deltoid muscle, followed by month (every 28±7 days) injections of 50, 75, 100, or 150 mg eq. into the deltoid or gluteal muscles, based on clinical safety, tolerability and efficacy. Once participants were stabilised on a PP1M dose (defined as receiving the same dose at two consecutive visits), they were transitioned to the maintenance period of the LM phase and received PP3M treatment (175–525 mg eq.). This was administered at a 3.5-fold multiple of the final stabilised PP1M dose, every 3 months (every 12 weeks±14 days), with dose adjustments made as needed. If participants did not achieve stabilisation with the PP1M treatment after four consecutive cycles (approximately 17 weeks), treatment was continued for an additional two cycles. If stabilisation was still not achieved, the participant was discontinued from the study.

Endpoints and assessments

The primary objective was to assess the long-term symptomatic response in participants with schizophrenia, measured by the change in CGI-SS score from baseline to EOS. Secondary objectives included analysing changes in CGI-SS and PSP score. CGI-SS is a well-established global severity rating used widely in schizophrenia trials; scale range 1–7 (None–Extreme). A within-participants change of 1 point on the CGI-SS equivalent to a one-category shift on the 1–7 CGI-SS scale was considered as a clinically meaningful improvement. PSP scale scores overall functioning in four domains (socially useful activities, personal/social relationships, self-care, disturbing/aggressive behaviours) on a 1–100 scale; higher scores = better functioning; interpretive bands used in this study were: poor (≤30), variable (>30 to ≤70), good (>70), a 10-point change in the total score was listed as a clinically meaningful within-participant change14 (used descriptively and for category shifts). Both instruments were administered using their standard versions in English, consistent with national clinical research practice in Rwanda. Participants were required to be fluent in Kinyarwanda, English, or French; however, all clinician-rated scales were completed by trained site psychiatrists or mental health nurses, ensuring consistent administration regardless of participant literacy level.

These analyses were conducted for OR phase, LM phase and overall FU (online supplemental Table S2). Safety was assessed throughout the study and summarised for OR, LM and overall FU phase. The number and percentage of participants who experienced treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), deaths, serious TEAEs and TEAEs leading to treatment discontinuation were summarised. Clinical laboratory tests, physical examinations, vital signs, pregnancy status, body weight changes and ECG findings were also assessed. Suicide-related thoughts and behaviours were assessed using the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) and summarised descriptively.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated based on the primary efficacy variable, that is, expected reduction in CGI-SS score from baseline to EOS (week 1 to week 66). It was anticipated that there would be an average reduction of 0.3 units in the CGI-SS score from baseline to EOS after switching from OAP medications to PP LAI. To achieve 80% power to detect a difference of −0.3 units between the null hypothesis mean of 0 and the alternative hypothesis mean of −0.3, with an estimated SD of 0.9 and a significance level of 0.025, a sample size of 75 was required, using a one-sided one-sample t-test. Considering a 25% dropout rate during the OAP treatment, a total of 100 participants were planned to be enrolled in the study. The assumed SD of 0.9 was derived from the open-label phase of the study for PP1M from Latin America.13

The subgroup analyses (by sex, age, time since diagnosis and type of OAP etc) are clinically relevant; however, the trial was prospectively designed and powered to address a single prespecified population-level hypothesis (the pooled change in CGI-SS beyond the prespecified threshold of −0.3). With a total sample of 93 participants (92 participants who entered LM), the study is not adequately powered for reliable inference within multiple small subgroups. Similarly, this study was not powered to evaluate clustering or between-centre differences. With only five participating sites and a modest number of participants at most sites, formal cluster-level analyses would be statistically unstable and of limited interpretability. However, to minimise centre-related bias, standardised rating instruments and centralised data review procedures were implemented.

Two analysis populations were defined for this study. Participants who entered OR phase and received at least one dose of OAPs during the observation period between OR phase baseline to the OR phase endpoint (week 1 to week 24) were referred to as ‘OR phase mid-term intention-to-treat (MITT)’ analysis set which was used for mid-term response analysis prior to PP1M initiation. All participants who entered lead-in period of LM phase and had received at least one dose of PP LAI between LM phase baseline to the LM phase endpoint (week 25 to week 66) were referred to as ‘LM phase ITT’ analysis set which was used for the primary efficacy endpoint and for long-term response and maintenance of treatment analyses. For analysis purpose, the primary population set used to analyse primary/secondary/safety endpoints from overall FU phase baseline to EOS (week 1 to week 66) to analyse long-term symptomatic response was referred to as ‘overall FU phase ITT’ analysis set.

In the primary analysis, the primary efficacy variable was tested using a one-sample, one-sided Student’s t-test with a significance level of 0.025. Additionally, actual values and changes from baseline in CGI-SS scores over the study visits were summarised for both observed and last observation carried forward (LOCF) data. Secondary analyses included within-group differences in CGI-SS scores, evaluated using paired t-tests, and summarised using frequency counts and percentages. Shifts from baseline in CGI-SS scores were categorised as worsening, no change or improvement. For PSP, actual values and changes from baseline were summarised for both observed and LOCF data and within-group differences were evaluated using paired t-tests. Shifts from baseline in PSP total scores were categorised as worsening, no change or improvement. For the paired t-tests, normality assumption on the paired differences (change from baseline) was evaluated. Normality was assessed both visually (histograms and Q-Q plots of the paired differences) and with the Shapiro-Wilk test.

The frequency counts and percentages of participants reporting each PSP decile and category were summarised. Safety and tolerability assessments were summarised using descriptive statistics. All statistical programming and analyses were performed using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute) or a later version.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research.

Results

Participant disposition

The study was conducted between 22 July 2021 and 16 April 2024, at five centres in Rwanda. Of the 100 participants screened, 93 participants were enrolled in the study (figure 1). From the OR phase, 92/93 (98.9%) participants continued to the LM phase; 1 (1.1%) participant discontinued early due to loss to FU. In the LM phase, 89/92 (96.7%) participants received PP3M. A total of 86/92 (93.5%) participants completed the study, while 6 (6.5%) participants discontinued early due to TEAEs, death or withdrawal by the participants (2 (2.2%) participants each).

Baseline and demographic characteristics

Of 100 screened, 93 participants were enrolled and comprised the OR phase MITT set; 92 continued into LM and comprised the LM phase ITT set (one participant discontinued after OR). All enrolled participants were Black Africans; ethnicity data were not collected in accordance with national ethical considerations. Because these are distinct analysis populations, we have summarised baseline characteristics once for each set (OR MITT, n=93; LM ITT, n=92). As expected for a single cohort progressing across phases, demographics were essentially similar across sets (eg, median age 29–29.5 years; 81.5%–81.7% male) (table 1). Total 10 (10.8%) participants had received ≥1 prior psychotropic medications (online supplemental Table S3). The mean duration of prior psychotropic treatment was 1657 days (median 685.5 days), with durations ranging from 22 to 5679 days. During the OR phase, 47 (50.5%) participants received one or more concomitant psychotropic medications which reduced to 21 (22.8%) participants during the LM phase (online supplemental Table S4).

Study intervention and modifications

All participants in the OR phase had at least one exposure to OAPs, with a mean (SD) duration of 173.40 (3.09) days and an average of 2.80 (1.53) OAPs received. The most used OAPs were risperidone (92 (98.92%)), haloperidol (52 (55.91%)) and chlorpromazine (46 (49.46%)).

In the lead-in period of LM phase, the mean (SD) duration of PP1M treatment was 125.80 (22.18) days and most (n=72, 78.3%) participants received five injections. In the maintenance period of LM phase, for PP3M, the mean (SD) duration was 165.30 (17.58) days and the majority (n=88, 98.9%) of participants received two injections. In the LM phase, dose modifications were required for all 92 (100%) participants who received at least one dose of PP1M while there were no dose modifications required for any of the 89 (100%) participants who started PP3M treatment.

Primary efficacy results

Mean change in CGI-SS:

In the overall FU phase, mean (SD) change from baseline in CGI-SS score from week 1 to week 66 showed significant and clinically meaningful improvement of −1.4 (0.69) with 95% CI −1.50 to −1.22), p<0.001 (table 2, figure 2).

Secondary efficacy results

Mean change in CGI-SS in OR and LM phase:

In the OR phase, the mean (SD) change in CGI-SS scores showed a non-clinically meaningful improvement of −0.1 (0.47), 95% CI −0.1931 to −0.0004, p=0.049, from week 1 to week 25; improvement was noted in 12 (12.9%) participants, no change in 78 (83.9%) and worsening in 3 (3.2%) participants. However, in the LM phase, the mean (SD) change from week 25 to week 66, in CGI-SS scores showed significant and clinically meaningful improvement of −1.2 (0.67), 95% CI −1.28, −1.10, p<0.001 (table 3); improvements were observed in 85 (92.4%) participants, no change in 6 (6.5%) and worsening in 1 (1.1%) participant.

PSP scale

In the OR phase, the mean (SD) improvement in PSP total score from week 1 to week 25 was not significant at 0.2 (5.82), 95% CI −0.99 to 1.40, p=0.736. In LM phase, significant and clinically meaningful improvements were observed with the mean (SD) change in PSP score from week 25 to week 66 of 17.1 (13.62), 95% CI 14.29 to 19.93, p<0.001 (table 3). In the LM phase, PSP scores improved in 67 (72.8%) participants, remained unchanged in 22 (23.9%) and worsened in 3 (3.3%) participants (table 3). Safety assessments

At least one TEAE was reported in the OR phase in 32/93 (34.4%) participants, in 62/92 participants (67.4%) during the LM phase and in 69/92 (75.0%) participants in the overall FU phase (table 4). The most frequently observed TEAEs (>10%) in the overall FU phase were asthenia (19 (20.7%)), nasopharyngitis (13 (14.1%)) and urinary tract infection (10 (10.9%)); most were mild or moderate in severity. In the LM phase, 2/92 (2.2%) participants discontinued PP due to TEAEs. Serious TEAEs were observed in 8/93 (8.6%) participants during OR, 4/92 (4.3%) during LM and 11/92 (12.0%) during overall FU phase. In all the phases, the most frequent serious TEAE was suicide attempt (OR: 2/93 (2.2%); LM: 2/92 (2.2%) each) and FU: 4/92 (4.3%)). Two deaths occurred during LM phase (ascariasis; completed suicide), neither considered related to study treatment; no deaths occurred in OR phase. The FU phase summary includes these same two LM phase deaths and does not indicate additional fatalities (table 4).

Extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS)-related TEAEs were observed in 5/93 (5.4%) participants in OR, 13/92 (14.1%) in LM, and in 16/92 (17.4%) participants in overall FU phase, including tremor in 9/92 (9.8%) participants, dystonia in 5/92 (5.4%) and parkinsonism in 2/92 (2.2%) participants. Prolactin-related AEs were reported in 3/17 (17.6%) female participants in the LM phase (and subsequently in the overall FU phase). These included hyperprolactinaemia in 2/17 (11.8%) and galactorrhoea in 1/17 (5.9%) female participants.

Clinical laboratory tests showed no clinically meaningful changes from baseline across chemistry or haematology parameters. Vital signs, including temperature, pulse rate, systolic and diastolic blood pressure and respiratory rate, remained stable throughout the study with no clinically significant mean changes observed. ECG assessments revealed no clinically significant abnormalities during any study period. In the OR phase, among participants with normal baseline BMI, 2 (2.7%) experienced a ≥7% decrease and 16 (21.6%) experienced a ≥7% increase in weight, while among those overweight at baseline, 2 (14.3%) had a ≥7% decrease in weight. In the LM phase, 4 (5.7%) participants with normal baseline BMI had a≥7% decrease and 15 (21.4%) had a ≥7% increase in weight; among overweight participants, 2 (11.1%) had a ≥7% decrease and 3 (16.7%) had a ≥7% increase in weight. On the C-SSRS, 25 of 26 participants (96.2%) recorded a score of 0 (no event) during post screening visits, and one participant (3.8%) reported ‘suicidal ideation with intent but no plan’ as the most severe category. The C-SSRS was completed by only 26 participants because it was added mid-study after few investigators noted suicidal-behaviour concerns; it had not been included at study initiation because an earlier feasibility study3 in Rwanda reported 100% ‘no event’ outcomes.

Discussion

The primary aim of the CASPAR study was to evaluate the long-term symptomatic and functional outcomes associated with switching from SoC OAPs to PP1M and PP3M LAIs in real-world Rwandan healthcare settings. The study was designed such that in the first 24 weeks of OR phase, participants continued their SoC OAPs which allowed for the assessment of effect of OAPs. Then in LM phase from week 25 to week 66, the participants were systematically switched to PP LAIs.

Across the 66 weeks FU period, treatment with PP LAIs led to clinically meaningful improvements in symptom severity, as demonstrated by a −1.4-point change in CGI-SS (exceeding the expected average 0.3-unit reduction typically observed with long-term symptomatic treatment) from baseline to week 66. These improvements were driven predominantly by treatment during the LM phase (weeks 25–66), where participants demonstrated significant reductions in CGI-SS (−1.2) and marked gains in functioning as measured by the PSP scale (+17.1 points). In contrast, no clinically meaningful symptomatic or functional benefits were observed during the OR phase (weeks 1–24), when participants remained on daily OAPs, highlighting the added value of LAIs in this setting.

The findings demonstrated marked improvements in overall clinical symptoms and social functioning following the switch from OAPs to PP LAIs.

The mean change in PSP scores suggests that transitioning from OAPs to LAIs enables participants to regain critical functional capabilities essential for societal reintegration.15 No significant functional improvements were observed during the OR phase, likely due to the inherent limitations of OAPs in maintaining adherence,16 which likely cause increased risk of relapses and hospitalisations.17 As PP LAIs reduce the risk of relapse, leading to lower hospitalisation rates, it can translate into improved social outcomes due to the functional recovery.1 18

These findings align with earlier clinical studies, which consistently highlight the effectiveness of LAI antipsychotics over OAPs.19,21 Large cohort studies in Finland and Sweden have demonstrated lower all-cause and psychiatric rehospitalisation rates with LAIs compared with OAPs.19 20 Our results are also consistent with a major placebo-controlled, multicentre study for PP3M,22 which reported symptomatic benefits of PP3M with a delayed time-to-relapse in adult schizophrenia participants.23 Additional real-world and economic evaluations—from the USA, Spain, South Korea and China—have similarly reported reduced healthcare utilisation, better clinical outcomes and favourable cost-effectiveness with PP formulations.21,24

However, research on LAI use in Africa remains scarce. Notably, 92.4% of participants showed significant symptomatic improvements in the LM phase, with only one participant experiencing worsening of symptoms. This low rate of symptom exacerbation suggests that dose stabilisation during the lead-in period was effective and that LAI treatment supports better symptom control and adherence than the prior treatments for these participants.24 25 As LAIs require less frequent interactions with healthcare providers, their use also alleviates burden on the healthcare system. This can be a key consideration in areas with limited resources or low numbers of mental health professionals.26

Treatment with PP1M and PP3M showed no new safety concerns in this real-world setting. The majority (94%) of participants completed the treatment without significant safety concerns. Over the 66-week long-term period, almost 75% of participants experienced 1 TEAE; the majority of them were mild/moderate. During LM, two deaths (ascariasis and completed suicide) were recorded, both were considered as not related to the study treatment. The safety profile including the TEAEs of special interest (EPS, prolactin and hyperglycaemia-related TEAEs) was consistent with other studies of PP including long-term studies.12 22 27

Importantly, interpretation of these results must be situated within the broader health system context. The Rwandan mental healthcare system is evolving but continues to face significant resource constraints, including a limited number of healthcare professionals trained to manage severe mental disorders such as schizophrenia. Despite these challenges, the Ministry of Health has demonstrated commitment to strengthening mental health services and exploring whether newer antipsychotic options including atypical agents and LAI formulations can be safely integrated to improve care outcomes. Current SoC for schizophrenia in Rwanda relies primarily on first generation OAPs (haloperidol, chlorpromazine), second generation oral agents (risperidone, olanzapine) Rwanda Standard Treatment Guidelines Mental Health Vol 9 March 2022 and first-generation antipsychotic LAIs (flupentixol decanoate and haloperidol decanoate). Second generation antipsychotic LAIs, including PP1M and PP3M, are not yet included in. The present study, therefore, provides critical country-specific evidence to inform policy considerations regarding adoption of newer LAIs into national guidelines.

Several strengths support the reliability of the study findings. The pragmatic, multicentre design reflects real-world implementation conditions and leveraged Rwanda’s community-based health-worker network, likely contributing to the exceptionally high retention rate (93.5% in LM). High dropout rate is a known challenge in long-term studies of chronic illnesses, including schizophrenia.28 Notably, very few participants dropped out from this long-term study compared with similar studies.29 The long study duration and comprehensive safety assessments further reinforce the robustness of the findings, with no new safety concerns emerging and most TEAEs being mild or moderate.

Nevertheless, certain limitations of this study warrant consideration. In this study, enrolment was limited to adults aged 18–35 years to ensure a more homogeneous cohort with fewer age-related comorbidities and to reflect the early-to-mid course of schizophrenia in our sample. This range also aligned with feasibility considerations in Rwanda’s resource-constrained healthcare system. However, this restriction may limit generalisability to older adults with schizophrenia. Given that Rwanda was a largely psychiatry research-naïve country at the start of the study, a more complex randomised design was deemed unsuitable3; the absence of randomisation limits the ability to control the confounding variables. The single-arm, open-label design was chosen based on insights from a feasibility study conducted in Rwanda.3 However, the open-label nature of the study may have introduced bias in reporting and assessing outcomes, as expectations regarding treatment effectiveness could have influenced participant perceptions and evaluations. In the absence of a comparator, these influences cannot be distinguished from true pharmacological effects, and the magnitude of change should therefore be interpreted with caution. Dose adjustments during the lead-in phase and careful monitoring during the maintenance phase likely contributed to the higher TEAEs observed in the LM phase than the OR phase. A further limitation is the use of CGI-SS and PSP instead of a more detailed scale such as PANSS. CGI-SS and PSP were selected for feasibility in Rwanda’s resource-constrained system, where complex multi-item assessments are difficult to implement.3 While pragmatic for low-resource settings, these scales lack the symptom-level detail provided by PANSS, limiting our ability to characterise baseline severity and assess domain-specific changes. In addition, between-participant variation in LM duration could, in principle, influence individual trajectories. The study did not include a formal inter-rater reliability assessment across sites for these scales, which may introduce variability in clinical ratings. Although all assessments were conducted by trained psychiatrists or mental-health nurses using standardised CGI-SS and PSP instruments, and centralised data reviews were performed to monitor for inconsistencies, some residual rater-related variability cannot be entirely ruled out.

Conclusions

This study involving participants with schizophrenia in Rwanda found that long-term treatment with PP LAIs (PP1M and PP3M) significantly improved symptom severity and social functioning, and that this treatment was generally tolerable, with no newly identified safety concerns. In summary, less frequent administration of PP LAIs compared with traditional OAPs and the improved clinical and social outcomes identified in this study may help to address the challenges of psychiatry healthcare in low-income countries including Rwanda.
