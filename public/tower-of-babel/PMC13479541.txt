Timing of a single dexmedetomidine bolus to attenuate remifentanil-induced hyperalgesia after thyroidectomy: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Xue Han, Wen Xu, Su Wang, Dinghui Guo, Meng Luo, Fang Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRemifentanil-induced hyperalgesia (RIH) is a frequent postoperative complication. Dexmedetomidine can attenuate RIH, but whether the effect depends on the timing of administration remains unclear. The study compared two clinically relevant timings of a single dexmedetomidine bolus in patients undergoing thyroidectomy.MethodsA total of 118 patients were randomized into three groups: Group I (dexmedetomidine before anesthesia induction), Group R (dexmedetomidine at skin closure), and Group C (control). The primary outcome was the mechanical pain threshold measured on the dominant forearm at 30 min postoperatively. Secondary outcomes included peri-incisional pain threshold, pain intensity, QoR-15 recovery score, hemodynamic stability, and adverse events.ResultsMechanical pain thresholds at 30 min postoperatively were significantly higher in Groups I and R than in Group C. Only Group R showed a lower incidence of peri-incisional hyperalgesia at 24 h (P = 0.004). Resting pain scores, rescue analgesic use, and QoR-15 total scores were comparable among groups. Introduction

Thyroid cancer is one of the most prevalent endocrine cancers, and radical thyroidectomy remains the primary treatment modality. However, patients often experience varying degrees of postoperative pain [1, 2]. Remifentanil, an ultra-short-acting µ-opioid receptor agonist, is widely used during general anesthesia because of its rapid onset and offset [3, 4].

Nevertheless, intraoperative remifentanil infusion, especially at high doses or for prolonged periods, can induce a state of heightened pain sensitivity termed remifentanil-induced hyperalgesia (RIH). This condition typically manifests during early (4 h to 2 days) and late (8–14 days) post-infusion periods [5]. RIH is distinct from acute opioid tolerance and primarily involves central sensitization and N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor activation in the spinal dorsal horn, leading to enhanced neuronal excitability and expanded hyperalgesia beyond the surgical site [6–8]. Timely preventive strategies may help reduce opioid-related pain amplification and improve postoperative recovery.

Dexmedetomidine, a highly selective α2-adrenergic receptor agonist, has sedative, sympatholytic, and analgesia-sparing properties and has been reported to attenuate RIH. Preclinical evidence suggests that dexmedetomidine may modulate central sensitization and reduce nociceptive facilitation [6, 9], potentially through interactions with NMDA receptor-related pathways [6, 8]. However, continuous intraoperative infusion carries risk of bradycardia and hypotension [9–11]. A single bolus may preserve intraoperative hemodynamic stability [12, 13], though its efficacy may depend on the timing of administration. While preemptive dosing targets central sensitization early, administration at wound closure coincides with peak surgical stress and inflammatory mediator release [14], potentially offering distinct protective mechanisms.

This single-center, randomized controlled clinical trial compared two clinically relevant timings of a single dexmedetomidine bolus (0.8 µg·kg-1) to mitigate RIH in patients undergoing radical thyroidectomy with remifentanil-based anesthesia. We hypothesized that the antihyperalgesic effect of dexmedetomidine would vary according to the timing of administration.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (Protocol No. XYFY-2023-KL026-02) on March 23, 2023, and was prospectively registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registration Center (ChiCTR2300071038; registration date: 30/04/2023). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. This study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines. No substantive amendments affecting the intervention, eligibility criteria, sample size, or primary sensory-threshold outcome were made after trial registration.

Study population

Patients aged 18–65 years with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I-II scheduled for radical thyroidectomy were enrolled. This age range was selected to reduce clinical heterogeneity and to align with previous studies of dexmedetomidine and remifentanil-induced hyperalgesia [6, 9, 15]. The surgical team and technique were identical across all patients, with procedures performed by senior surgeons using a standardized protocol. Exclusion criteria included: (1) prior thyroid surgery; (2) pregnancy or lactation; (3) cardiac disease (e.g., coronary artery disease, heart failure, significant arrhythmias), uncontrolled hypertension (defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 160 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 100 mmHg on two preoperative measurements), diabetes mellitus, high-degree atrioventricular block (≥ Mobitz II), or resting heart rate < 50 bpm; (4) preoperative laboratory abnormalities (e.g., abnormal blood routine, liver function, or electrolytes); (5) alcohol or opioid drug abuse; and (6) participation in other clinical trials. Patients who underwent endoscopic surgery, required reoperation for postoperative bleeding, or were admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) were excluded from analysis.

Randomization and preoperative management

Patients were randomly allocated (1:1:1 ratio) to one of three groups using computer-generated randomization lists (PASS 15.0). Allocation was concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque envelopes until the patient entered the operating room. The groups were defined as follows: (1) patients in Group I received dexmedetomidine before anesthesia induction; (2) patients in Group R received dexmedetomidine at skin closure (defined as the time of starting the suture of the incision after the surgeon placed the drainage tube); and (3) patients in Group C did not receive dexmedetomidine perioperatively.

Patients and outcome assessors were blinded to group assignment. Attending anesthesiologists were not blinded because they were responsible for administering dexmedetomidine according to the protocol and managing intraoperative hemodynamics. To ensure blinding of outcome assessment, all outcome data were recorded by a separate anesthetist who was blinded to the allocation.

Anesthesia technique

Patients were monitored by noninvasive blood pressure, pulse oximetry, electrocardiography (ECG), heart rate (HR), and bispectral index (BIS). Anesthesia was induced with midazolam 0.05 mg‧kg-1, etomidate 0.2 mg‧kg-1, sufentanyl 0.2 µg‧kg-1, and rocuronium 0.9 mg‧kg-1, followed by tracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation. Patients in Group I received dexmedetomidine (Yangtze River Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) 0.8 µg‧kg-1 over 12 min before anesthesia induction. Patients in Group R received the same dose over 12 min at skin closure. Patients in Group C did not receive dexmedetomidine. The dose and infusion duration were based on prior clinical evidence supporting its efficacy in attenuating remifentanil-induced hyperalgesia while maintaining hemodynamic stability [12]. Anesthesia was maintained with remifentanil 0.3 µg‧kg-1‧min-1 (Enhau Medicine Co., Jiangsu, China) and sevoflurane (Hengrui Medicine Co., Jiangsu, China). The initial end-tidal sevoflurane concentration was set at 1.3%, and the fraction of inspired oxygen was 60%. BIS was maintained between 40 and 60 by adjusting the sevoflurane concentration as needed. At suture initiation, tropisetron 2 mg was administered, and remifentanil and sevoflurane were gradually discontinued. Patients were transferred to the postanesthetic care unit (PACU) for at least 30 min after extubation. Rescue analgesia (flurbiprofen axetil 50 mg IV) was provided on patient request or when the numeric rating scale (NRS) was ≥ 4, and repeated administration was allowed. Intraoperative hypotension was defined as mean arterial pressure (MAP) < 65 mmHg. Bradycardia was defined as heart rate (HR) < 50 bpm. Vasopressor use followed institutional practice. Ephedrine (3–6 mg IV) was used primarily for hypotension accompanied by bradycardia (HR < 50 bpm) or in patients with baseline hypertension, whereas phenylephrine (40–80 µg IV) was used for hypotension with a normal or elevated heart rate.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the mechanical pain threshold assessed on the dominant forearm at 30 min after surgery. Mechanical pain threshold was measured using calibrated von Frey filament (0.008–300 g, Shengting Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at three forearm sites (3, 6, and 9 cm distal to the cubital crease on the dominant inner forearm) and three peri-incisional sites (2, 3, and 5 cm lateral to the midpoint of the incision). At each site, the filament was applied perpendicularly to the skin until it bent slightly, held for 2–3 s, and repeated three times with the same predefined procedure. The lowest force that elicited a pain response in at least 2 of 3 trials was recorded as the pain threshold for that site. The average of the three site-specific thresholds was calculated separately for the forearm and peri-incisional regions. To reduce measurement variability, fixed anatomical landmarks and an identical standardized protocol were used for all assessments. Hyperalgesia was defined as a postoperative pain threshold lower than the patient’s preoperative baseline [16, 17].

The secondary outcomes were as follows: pain threshold around the incision and forearm at 30 min and 24 h after surgery, hemodynamic parameters (BP and HR (before anesthesia induction, after tracheal intubation, at 30 min after the start of surgery, at skin closure, and at the time of extubation)), eye opening time (defined as the period between the end of surgery and the patient’s first eye opening), extubation time (defined as the period from the end of surgery to extubation), pain intensity recorded using the NRS at 0.5, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h after extubation in the patient’s resting state, QoR-15 total score assessed preoperatively and 24 h postoperatively, the incidence of rescue analgesia and adverse events (Drowsiness, Headache, Dizziness, Nausea/Vomiting, Respiratory depression). Higher QoR-15 scores indicate better postoperative recovery quality. The Ramsay Sedation Scale was used to assess sedation at 5, 10, 15, and 30 min in the PACU [17]. Drowsiness was defined as a Ramsay Sedation Score of 5–6. Respiratory depression was defined as drowsiness accompanied by a respiratory rate of less than 10 bpm.

Statistical analysis

In a preliminary pilot sample, the mechanical pain threshold in the dominant inner forearm at 30 min after surgery in the control group (n = 15) was 73.7 ± 22.4 g. This pilot estimate was used only as an initial reference for sample size planning and not as a stable estimate of the population baseline. Given the known imprecision of small pilot samples, we predefined a conservative target effect size of a 20% increase in mechanical pain threshold, rather than relying on the pilot mean alone. This assumption was also informed by the previous study by Lee et al. [9], in which dexmedetomidine improved mechanical pain thresholds by approximately 35% in patients receiving high-dose remifentanil. Based on these assumptions, 35 patients per group were required to achieve 80% power at a two-sided a level of 0.05. Allowing for a 10% dropout rate, we planned to enroll 40 patients per group.

A modified intention-to-treat analysis was used for all the primary and secondary outcome measures in our study. The pain threshold and hemodynamic data were analyzed by two-way repeated-measures ANOVA. The Bonferroni correction was applied for post hoc comparisons and the non-spherical hypothesis was corrected using the Greenhouse‒Geisser correction. Categorical variables were analyzed by Fisher’s exact test and the χ2 test. The normality of the data was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the Levene test was used to confirm the homogeneity of variance. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to analyze Ramsay and NRS scores at different time points. QoR-15 total scores were compared using one-way analysis of variance. Categorical variables are expressed as number (proportion). Quantitative variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range[IQR]). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0 for Windows (IBM Corp. Armonk, NY, USA) was used for all statistical analyses.

Results

The study included 123 patients from May to August 2023, but two did not meet the inclusion criteria, and one declined participation. A total of 120 patients were eventually randomized, but two patients were not included in the final analysis: one due to poor compliance and another due to reoperation for postoperative bleeding. Therefore, data for 118 patients were ultimately analyzed in this study (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics were comparable across groups (Table 1).

Postoperative pain threshold

Preoperative mechanical pain thresholds were comparable among the three groups at both the forearm and peri-incisional sites. For the primary outcome, the mechanical pain threshold on the dominant forearm at 30 min postoperatively was significantly higher in Groups I and R than in Group C (I vs. C, P = 0.022; R vs. C, P = 0.003, Bonferroni-corrected; Table 2). Similarly, peri-incisional mechanical pain thresholds at 30 min postoperatively were significantly higher in Groups I and R than in Group C. However, no significant between-group differences were observed at 24 h after surgery at either site. In all groups, postoperative pain thresholds at both the forearm and peri-incisional area were significantly lower than baseline values (P < 0.001), indicating postoperative hyperalgesia (Fig. 2).

Incidence of postoperative hyperalgesia and pain intensity

The incidence of postoperative hyperalgesia was significantly greater in Group C than in Group R at 30 min on the forearm (P < 0.001) and at both 30 min (P = 0.018) and 24 h (P = 0.003) in the peri-incisional area. No significant between-group difference was observed on the forearm at 24 h after surgery. The incidence of postoperative hyperalgesia did not differ significantly between Group I and R, except that Group R had a lower incidence in the peri-incisional area at 24 h (P = 0.011; Table 3). Resting NRS pain scores were low overall and did not differ significantly among the three groups at any postoperative time point. Likewise, the proportion of patients requiring rescue analgesia was similar across groups (Table 3). QoR-15 total scores were comparable among the three groups both before surgery and at 24 h postoperatively, indicating no detectable between-group difference in patient-centered recovery quality.

Hemodynamic and anesthesia recovery period data

Analysis of surgical and anesthetic data revealed significant differences among the three groups regarding hemodynamics and anesthesia recovery (Table 2; Fig. 3). Group R showed a more favorable intraoperative hemodynamic profile compared with Group I. As shown in Fig. 3A, mean blood pressure at skin closure in Group R was significantly greater than that in Group I (P = 0.002) by repeated-measures ANOVA. In terms of emergence from anesthesia, Group R exhibited prolonged eye opening (16.2 ± 9.47 vs. 10.78 ± 7.04 min, P = 0.011) and extubation time (16.18 ± 6.89 vs. 11.93 ± 6.67 min, P = 0.04) compared with Group I.

Consistent with the hemodynamic management protocol described in the Methods, ephedrine was used more frequently in Group I (23 [57.5%]) than in Group R (12 [30.0%]) and Group C (7 [18.4%]) (P = 0.001), whereas phenylephrine use did not differ significantly among groups (Group I: 18 [45.0%], Group R: 9 [22.5%], Group C: 12 [31.6%]; P = 0.090). According to our institutional protocol, ephedrine was preferentially used for hypotension accompanied by bradycardia or in patients with baseline hypertension, whereas phenylephrine was used for hypotension with normal or elevated heart rate. Importantly, no patient experienced prolonged hypotension (> 5 min) or required ICU admission, supporting the safety of this regimen in ASA I-II patients.

Postoperative adverse events

The incidence of postoperative adverse events within 24 h after the operation did not differ significantly among the three groups (Table 2). Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) occurred in 11 (27.5%) patients in Group I, 15 (37.5%) in Group R, and 15 (39.5%) in Group C, with no significant difference among groups (P = 0.488). Sedation levels assessed by Ramsay Sedation Scale showed a statistically significant difference among groups at 5 min in the PACU (P = 0.020), with Group R showing slightly higher scores (3 [3]) compared to Group I (3 [2, 3]) and Group C (3 [2, 3]). However, this difference resolved by 10 min, and no patient experienced excessive sedation (Ramsay score ≥ 5) or respiratory depression.

Although some established risk factors for PONV, such as smoking status and history of motion sickness, were not systematically recorded in this study, the groups were otherwise well balanced with respect to measured baseline characteristics (Table 1). Given the absence of significant differences in PONV incidence across groups, any potential imbalance in unmeasured risk factors is unlikely to have altered the overall conclusions regarding adverse events.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial suggests that a single 0.8 µg·kg⁻¹ dexmedetomidine bolus may attenuate early mechanical hyperalgesia, as assessed by quantitative sensory testing, in patients undergoing thyroidectomy. The main findings were based on quantitative sensory testing using von Frey filaments rather than on patient-centered pain or recovery outcomes. Administration at skin closure was associated with a lower incidence of peri-incisional h

yperalgesia at 24 h and a more favorable intraoperative hemodynamic profile than pre-induction dosing, albeit with modestly prolonged emergence times. However, these objective sensory-threshold differences were not accompanied by lower resting NRS pain scores, reduced rescue analgesic requirements, or improved QoR-15 recovery scores. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted as evidence of an antihyperalgesic effect detected by sensory testing, while the clinical analgesic relevance remains uncertain.

The preferential 24-hour effect observed with skin-closure administration may be related to the temporal alignment between dexmedetomidine administration and late intraoperative surgical stimulation or inflammatory signaling. RIH is primarily mediated by central sensitization via NMDA receptor activation in spinal dorsal horn neurons [18, 19]. However, surgical tissue injury triggers a cascade of peripheral inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α) that peak during mid-to-late surgery [20], while peripheral nociceptive pathways remain highly plastic during wound closure [21]. These processes synergistically enhance spinal NMDA receptor trafficking and microglial activation, thereby potentiating central sensitization [22]. Dexmedetomidine administered at skin closure, coinciding with this inflammatory peak, may interrupt this peripheral-to-central positive feedback loop. It does so by suppressing immune cell activation mediated by α2-adrenergic receptor and dampening nociceptor excitability, thereby reducing the afferent barrage that sustains spinal wind-up and microglial priming [20, 23, 24]. In contrast, pre-induction dosing may wane before the inflammatory peak. While this mechanistic interpretation aligns with preclinical models, it remains inferential in the absence of direct inflammatory or neurophysiological measurements.

The discrepancy between enhanced mechanical pain thresholds and unchanged resting NRS scores merits careful interpretation. Mechanical hyperalgesia, assessed via von Frey filaments [25–28], reflects neuroplastic changes and central sensitization, whereas resting pain is a subjective experience influenced by cognitive and emotional factors. Crucially, hyperalgesia may be more strongly correlated with movement-evoked pain (e.g., swallowing, coughing) than with resting pain [18, 29], a distinction particularly relevant after thyroidectomy. This interpretation aligns with the theoretical framework proposed by Wilder-Smith et al. [30], who emphasized that measures of neuroplasticity (e.g., mechanical pain thresholds) and clinical pain assessments provide distinct but complementary insights into nociception. Thus, one cannot serve as a surrogate for the other. Additionally, the low correlation between objectively measured pain thresholds and subjectively assessed pain intensity [28, 30], combined with the availability of on-demand rescue analgesia and the floor effect created by low baseline NRS scores (median 2–3 across groups), further limits the ability to detect between-group differences in patient-centered outcomes.

The robustness of the primary endpoint warrants cautious interpretation. Although von Frey filament testing is commonly used to evaluate postoperative hyperalgesia, it may be influenced by subject perception, assessor technique, local skin sensitivity, and inter-session variability. Therefore, the primary endpoint should be interpreted as a quantitative sensory signal of antihyperalgesia rather than as a stand-alone surrogate for clinical analgesic efficacy.

Regarding safety, dexmedetomidine exhibited timing- and dose-dependent effects on hemodynamics and emergence [14, 31]. The dose of 0.8 µg‧kg-1 used in our study was selected based on the clinical application of dexmedetomidine for attenuating remifentanil-induced hyperalgesia while preserving hemodynamic stability [12]. The increased intraoperative hypotension and ephedrine requirements observed in Group I align with the biphasic pharmacological profile of dexmedetomidine: initial peripheral vasoconstriction followed by central sympatholysis [32, 33]. Administering dexmedetomidine at skin closure (Group R), when sympathetic tone is already modulated by surgical stress, may mitigate this effect. Although Group R demonstrated prolonged eye-opening and extubation times compared with Group I, these delays were modest (approximately 4–5 min) and clinically manageable. Importantly, no patient experienced prolonged hypotension (> 5 min) or required intensive care admission, supporting the safety of this regimen in ASA I-II patients.

The relationship between dexmedetomidine dose and emergence delay warrants further consideration. Previous studies by Bicer et al. and Kim et al. confirmed that patients who received 0.75–1.0 µg‧kg-1 dexmedetomidine had prolonged extubation time [34, 35], whereas lower doses (0.2–0.5 µg‧kg-1) did not significantly affect emergence [15, 36]. Our findings suggest that the timing of a standard-dose intervention may influence the balance between sensory-threshold effects and perioperative hemodynamics. However, because the observed sensory-threshold differences did not translate into lower resting pain scores, reduced rescue analgesic use, or improved QoR-15 recovery scores, the clinical benefit of this timing-based strategy remains to be established.

Several limitations need consideration. First, the absence of movement-evoked pain evaluation limits direct clinical inference regarding functional recovery, especially after thyroidectomy, where swallowing- or coughing-related pain may be more clinically relevant than resting pain. Second, although mechanical pain threshold assessment with von Frey filaments is a validated method for evaluating postoperative hyperalgesia, it remains susceptible to inter-individual, inter-session, and operator-related variability. We observed a discrepancy between the preliminary pilot estimate and the corresponding control-group value in the main study, which likely reflects the limited precision of the small pilot sample together with possible between-phase measurement variability. Although the concurrent randomized design mitigates systematic bias, formal test-retest reliability, inter-rater reliability, and assessor calibration were not performed. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the robustness and reproducibility of the primary endpoint. Third, we did not measure plasma dexmedetomidine concentrations or tissue cytokine levels to directly validate the proposed peripheral mechanism. Fourth, our study included only patients aged 18–65 years. Therefore, the findings cannot be directly generalized to elderly patients, who may have altered pharmacokinetics, greater pharmacodynamic sensitivity to dexmedetomidine, and a higher vulnerability to adverse effects such as bradycardia, hypotension, and delayed emergence. Dedicated trials in elderly patients are required to determine whether the same timing strategy is effective and safe in that population. Lastly, this was a single-center trial with moderate sample size, so multicenter validation across diverse surgical procedures is warranted.

Conclusion

In adults aged 18–65 years undergoing thyroidectomy with remifentanil-based anesthesia, a single 0.8 µg·kg-1 dexmedetomidine bolus administered at skin closure was associated with improved quantitative sensory testing-derived measures of mechanical hyperalgesia, including a lower incidence of peri-incisional hyperalgesia at 24 h and a more favorable intraoperative hemodynamic profile than pre-induction administration. However, these findings were not accompanied by lower resting pain scores, reduced analgesic use or improved QoR-15 recovery scores. Thus, the results support a potential antihyperalgesic effect measured by mechanical sensory testing, but they do not establish a clinically meaningful analgesic or recovery benefit. Further studies incorporating movement-evoked pain assessment, formal sensory-test reliability measures, and broader patient populations, especially elderly patients, are needed to determine the clinical relevance and generalizability of this timing strategy.
