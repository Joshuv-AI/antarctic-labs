Statement Of Project Objectives
 A Modular System for Direct Conversion of Methane into Methanol via Photocatalysis

  A. OBJECTIVES
 In this project, the Recipient’s objective is to develop a liquid phase photocatalytic process
 for direct conversion of methane into methanol. The specific objectives are to:

 • Develop a bifunctional catalyst using a semiconductor photocatalyst architecture to
 facilitate methane activation to directly convert methane into methanol.
 • Develop a scalable reactor design to maximize mass transfer and methanol
 selectivity using an optimized photocatalyst.
 • Develop a conceptual process design for a modular system for flare gas utilization.
 • Conduct comprehensive techno-economic and commercial market assessments to
 position the technology for commercialization.

 In Budget Period 1 (BP1), which encompasses Tasks 1 through 4, the objective will be to
 develop and optimize the semiconductor catalyst for the effective conversion of photons
 into hydroxyl (•OH) radicals which support methane activation and a co-catalyst that
 selectively and with high yield converts the methyl radicals generated from methane
 activation into methanol and integrate these into a single bifunctional catalyst.

 In Budget Period 2 (BP2), the objective will shift to process development focusing on
 testing to identify optimal operating conditions, completing long-term testing at realistic
 commercial conditions, testing with simulated natural gas compositions and intermittent
 operation involving on/off and varying flow conditions. BP2, which encompasses Tasks 5
 through 8, as well as Task 1, will also include testing with the objective of enabling
 generation of the hydroxyl radical using water (H2O) instead of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)
 as the reagent. Process design, reactor design and a final techno-economic assessment
 (TEA) that incorporates a preliminary basic engineering design package (BEDP) for a
 methane-to-methanol (M2M) pilot test unit will also be performed in BP2.

  B. SCOPE OF WORK
 The scope of work focuses on development of the liquid phase photocatalytic process for
 direct conversion of methane into methanol. In BP1, the scope of work focuses on catalyst
 development and optimization. Development efforts will start with the semiconductor
 catalyst and evaluate semiconductor composition and synthesis approach to effectively
 optimize the creation of high-energy holes and electrons from incident photons. Controlled
 loading and proximity insertion of the co-catalyst clusters and molecular complexes in
 relation to the semiconductor catalyst will be developed. Finally, the integration of the
 semiconductor and co-catalyst will be evaluated and optimized the develop a leading
 bifunctional photocatalyst by the end of BP1. Ninety days prior to the end of BP1, a go/no-
 go decision will be made to determine whether to proceed to BP2 and Tasks 5 through 8.

 5

 In BP2, the effort will also focus on sequential development of the process to get it ready
 for commercial application. BP2 will begin with an effort to develop a detailed structural
 model and reaction mechanism for the reaction process. Using this fundamental
 understanding of the bifunctional catalyst and reaction mechanism, one final iteration of
 catalyst optimization will implement scaleup and large-scale production options in addition
 to maximizing methanol selectivity and yield. Other testing to be completed during BP2
 will include parametric testing of operating conditions to identify an optimal operating
 window, long-term testing at realistic commercial operating conditions, testing with
 simulated compositions of natural gas, and intermittent operating conditions involving
 cyclic on/off conditions as well as varying flow rates. To further enhance methane
 conversion, the Recipient will also explore the catalytic reaction in gas phase in addition
 to liquid phase, considering the low concentration of CH4 in the exhaust and air as well as
 low solubility of CH4 in water. Additionally, exploration of the reaction by decoupling
 photocatalysis and thermo-catalysis processes and co-catalysts integration to increase the
 CH4 conversion and tune the product selectivity will be performed. Further optimization
 on the photo-thermo-catalysis procedures is expected to show higher reaction conversion
 than the pure photocatalytic process. Potential reactor configurations will be evaluated to
 optimize methane mass transfer to the catalyst surface. The potential to use H2O rather than
 H2O2 as the reagent to produce the hydroxyl radicals will be evaluated for supporting
 remote site operation and operational cost savings. TEA studies that incorporate a BEDP
 for a M2M pilot test unit will be performed in BP2.

Budget Period 1
In Budget Period 1, the scope of the work will focus on the optimization of the methane
activation using H2O2 as the reagent for production of the hydroxyl (•OH) radical. The
work is divided into catalyst synthesis and characterization and catalyst screening for
methane activation.

Task 2.0 - Catalyst Synthesis
The bifunctionality of the catalyst includes the semiconductor catalyst, which converts the
incident photons into high-energy holes and electrons and promotes the formation of
hydroxyl radicals and subsequently methyl radicals and the co-catalyst, which functions in
the aqueous media to maximize selectivity and yield of methanol. To adequately optimize
this bifunctional catalyst, the Recipient plans to focus on the initial development efforts on
the individual function of the semiconductor catalyst and the co-catalyst. Because of this
division, the catalyst synthesis effort has two subtasks. A final subtask in the catalyst
synthesis evaluates the integration of the semiconductor catalyst and co-catalysts into a
single bifunctional catalyst material.

 7

 • Subtask 2.1- Semiconductor Catalyst Synthesis
 As the semiconductor photocatalyst is fundamental to the activation of methane,
 the initial efforts will focus on the semiconductor catalyst. In this effort, the
 Recipient will screen potential semiconductor materials for suitable valence and
 conducting bands that will support the generation of high energy holes and
 electrons suitable to produce the hydroxyl radical from H2O2. The Recipient will
 use conventional inorganic and colloidal synthesis techniques to modify the surface
 area, morphology, and surface characteristics of nanocrystals of the semiconductors
 to optimize interfacial band bending and charge transfer kinetics to maximize •OH
 radical production. By combining this catalyst synthesis effort with a multiplex
 fluorescence array screening process, the Recipient will be able to rapidly and
 effectively identify and quantify key features of the semiconductor and its
 production that yield the most effective photocatalyst.

 • Subtask 2.2 - Co-Catalyst Synthesis
 In this subtask, a series of biomimetic iron (Fe) and copper (Cu) clusters and
 molecular complex will be synthesized. By combining synthesis of these clusters
 and molecular complexes with routine characterization of the phase structure,
 morphology, metal sites amount, and surface area, the Recipient will identify
 optimal production routes for the synthesis of cluster and molecular complexes
 based on the physical properties, enabling the ability to tune the co-catalyst
 functionality, catalyst scaleup, and even catalyst cost. The efforts will include
 synthesis of zeolites-based biomimetic catalysts including Cu-MOR and Fe-ZSM-
 5. Cu-MOR here stands for a modernite zeolite combination with copper, while Fe-
 ZSM-5 means ZSM-5 zeolite and iron combination.

 • Subtask 2.3 - Integration of Semiconductor and Co-Catalyst into Bifunctional
 Catalyst
 In this subtask, different methods of loading and/or grafting of the semiconductor
 and co-catalyst into a single catalyst formulation will be investigated. The objective
 of this effort will be to insert the co-catalyst at controlled loading and within
 nanometer proximity of the semiconductor catalyst, while not adversely affecting
 the hydroxyl radical production performance of the semiconductor catalyst.
 Chemical and physical/structure analysis of the obtained photocatalyst
 formulations will be performed using multiple analytical systems available at the
 Recipient’s site, which include X-ray diffraction (XRD), transmission electron
 microscopy (TEM), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS).

Task 3.0 - Multiplex Fluorescence Array Screening
In this task, multiple catalyst samples will be deposited on a silicon nitride (SiN x)
membrane or within 96-, 384- or 1536-multiwell plate configuration that is commercially
available. The catalyst samples will be immersed in dilute solution of H2O2 or in pure H2O.
The catalyst samples will be exposed to ultraviolet (UV) light through the quartz plate. A
high-resolution camera underneath the well plate will detect fluorescence activity. Using
terephthalic acid to detect •OH radicals by the fluorescent product with a 426 nanometer

 8

(nm) emission and 312 nm excitation wavelength will provide a rapid screening
methodology for high throughput screening of catalyst sample. The O2- radical will also be
detected chemiluminescence method using luminol as a probe. This methodology will also
be used to evaluate changes in the photocatalytic radical production performance as co-
catalyst clusters and molecular complexes are integrated with the semiconductor catalyst.

Task 4.0 - Bifunctional Catalyst Testing
Catalytic measurement for samples of the bifunctional photocatalyst will be carried out
under light, H2O2, and ambient conditions. The experiments will be conducted using a
home-built immersion well reactor with a 365 nm mercury lamp. The gas products will be
detected using a gas chromatograph, and the liquid products will be identified using nuclear
magnetic resonance (NMR). A series of control experiments, including reactions under
dark, reactions without H2O2, reactions without catalyst as well as reaction without
methane, will be conducted to confirm the reliability of the measurements. After
confirmation of the reliability of the baseline system measurements, the system will be
used to optimize the performance of the bifunctional catalyst focusing on methanol yield.
A series of experiments will be conducted to measure the methane conversion and
methanol selectivity. These results will form the basis for the Go/No-Go decision from
BP1 to BP2.

Go/No-Go Decision Point:
The Recipient is not authorized to proceed beyond Budget Period 2 without written
authorization from the DOE Contracting Officer. Authorization to proceed will be based
on the following criteria:

 • Based on the methane conversion measured in Task 4, the Go/No-Go target for BP2
 would be between 7% to10% methane conversion.
 • Based on the methanol selectivity measured in Task 4, the Go/No-Go target for
 BP2 would be between 80% to 90% methanol selectivity under commercially
 reasonable operating conditions.

The Recipient will describe these results in the third Quarterly Progress Report to
DOE/NETL. Following the review of these results by the NETL Project Manager, the
Recipient will schedule a WebEx meeting with DOE/NETL team to provide a detailed
briefing on the project accomplishments in BP1 including progress made towards meeting
the Go/No-Go milestone. The Recipient will also submit a continuation application, as
required by DOE/NETL, 90 days prior to completion of the BP1 to request approval for
the BP2. The Recipient will only commence the activities for BP2 once a written
authorization from DOE/NETL contracting officer is received for BP2.

Budget Period 2
In BP2, the scope of work will focus on process development for commercial deployment.
Experimental data on catalyst structure and properties will be accumulated along with
operational data enabling determination of reaction mechanism. This information will be
used to complete the one more iteration of optimization of this bifunctional catalyst
formulation. With this leading bifunctional photocatalyst, operating conditions will be

 9

parametrically tested to identify the optimal window of operation for methanol selectivity
and yield. Other work will involve reactor design to maximize the transfer of methane to
the catalyst surface for reaction with the hydroxyl radicals to maximize the overall reaction
rate. Finally, the potential to replace H2O2 with water will be evaluated to improve the
opportunity to minimize the need for H2O2 reactant at remote sites. A conceptual process
design and TEA studies will also be performed.

Task 5.0 - Study of Structure-Property Relationship In this task, the optimized
photocatalysts will be studied in-depth to determine the active sites and detailed
mechanism in the selective methane oxidation. Various in-situ and ex-situ spectroscopic
and microscopic methods will be employed to unravel the origin of the high methanol
yields and selectivity, including x-ray diffraction (XRD, x-ray absorption near edge
structure (XANES) and extended x-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) (oxidation state
of the metals sites as well as their chemical coordination environment) and transmission
electron microscopy (TEM). The radical formation and reaction pathway will be probed
in-situ with electron paramagnetic resonance. This fundamental knowledge would be used
to create novel photocatalyst formulations with the highest methanol selectivity and yields
with synthesis techniques amenable to scaleup and cost-effective large-scale production.

Task 6.0 - Experimental Identification of Optimal Operating Window
The optimal bifunctional photocatalyst will be used to evaluate operating conditions
including H2O2 concentration and amount, pH of the solution, and light intensity for the
optimum methanol production. These tests will include long-term testing (>100 hours) to
demonstrate stability of catalyst performance and structure over extended periods of
operation with a simulated natural gas composition. Finally, ability of the bifunctional
photocatalyst to be used for varying natural gas availability like varying flow rate and
intermittent on/off operation will also be evaluated.

Task 7.0 – Process Design
The optimized bifunctional catalyst will be prepared in several forms to enable testing of
different reactor configurations that would enable the most effective mass transfer of the
methane onto the catalyst surface. Two specific configurations that have been identified
are a trickle bed system and the application of the bifunctional photocatalyst catalyst to
porous, transparent and flexible membrane such as nano-porous polyethylene (nanoPE),
which is hydrophobic and has high gas permeability but low water diffusivity and > 80%
transmission of light with wavelengths >200 nm.

Once an optimum reactor configuration is identified, it will be used to develop a conceptual
process design for the complete methane to methanol production flowsheet. This will
include methane feeding system, recovery of methanol from the reactor system, recycling
of oxidant and recycling of methane after removal of trace gases such as CO and CO2. This
conceptual design will be used to develop detailed mass and energy balances using an
ASPEN/HySYS system process simulator. The mass/energy balances allow sizing of major
equipment to obtain a factored cost estimate to develop overall capital expenditures (capex)
of the system. Similarly, space velocity in the reactor, conversion, and recycle rate of
methane will allow development of operating expenditures (opex) for this process. The

 10

capex and opex numbers will be used to develop the cost of methanol production. The
Recipient will also perform sensitivity analyses to determine the impact of various process
variables on methane production costs. The use of H2O2 adds significant cost to methanol
production, so the goal will be to test the optimized catalyst developed in Tasks 2-6 with
H2O and adjust operating conditions to optimize methane conversion and overall yield of
methanol as proposed in Task 8.

In addition to performing process design and TEA (Task 1.3), the Recipient will also
conduct a modularization study to understand how the Recipient can develop a compact
system utilizing advanced manufacturing techniques and advanced smart and intelligent
controls. It is possible that the semiconductor photocatalyst assembly as well as nanoPE
membranes can be mass produced using 3-D printing techniques. The Recipient will pay
particular attention at the research stage to keep the mass manufacturability of these key
reactor components in mind. Involvement of a commercial partner in this project will bring
focus on engineering for scaleup and deployment.

Task 8.0 - Evaluation of Water as Hydroxyl Radical Source
As water would be a preferred reagent for the generation of hydroxyl radicals especially at
remote sites and for lower operation costs as mentioned previously. The potential of
adjusting the semiconductor performance criteria and/or the addition of other
photocatalytic components to the catalyst will be evaluated as a means of enabling water
to be used as the reagent for the generation of hydroxyl radicals.

Task 9 - Leveraging Photoactivated OH-radicals for GHG-reducing Chemical
Transformations
To further enhance methane conversion, the Recipient will explore the reaction in gas
phase in addition to liquid phase, considering the low concentration of CH4 in the exhaust
and air as well as low solubility of CH4 in water. Catalysts will be exposed to water in the
reactor chamber with different CH4 concentrations and explore efficient catalyst design
principles for low-concentration methane conversion. O2 gas will also be co-fed into the
system to promote the •OH radical production by scavenging the generated photoelectrons.
The Recipient will also explore the reaction by decoupling photocatalysis and thermo-
catalysis processes and devise them independently to increase the CH4 conversion.
Rationalization and integration on the photo-thermo-catalysis functionalities are promising
pathways to accomplish higher reaction conversion than the pure photocatalytic reactions.

As •OH radical is key to the activation of methane, the Recipient will further optimize the
best-performing catalyst developed in BP1 by modifying its co-catalysts composition, the
coating materials on TiO2 as well as its surface characteristics to maximize •OH radical
production. Especially, the role of CH4/O2/water ratio, light intensity, and temperature on
the TiO2-supported co-catalysts would be explored. This can benefit from results obtained
Task 3 that multiplex fluorescence array developed in fast screening different
photocatalysts for •OH radical production. Based on the conversion of CH4 and product
yields will be quantified using gas chromatograph (GC, for gas products), and nuclear
magnetic resonance (NMR) and colorimetric method for different liquid products.

Task 10 - Development of Preliminary Basic Engineering Design Package for Pilot Test Unit

 11

The Subrecipient, with support from the Recipient, will develop a preliminary basic
engineering design package (BEDP) for a methane-to-methanol (M2M) pilot test unit for
an industry-related methanol production capacity. The purpose of this preliminary BEDP
is to determine a budget for the design, construction, and operation of the pilot test unit and
will include:

 • Reactor design specifications,
 • Process flow diagram with heat and material balance for each stream,
 • Preliminary piping, and instrumentation diagram (P&ID),
 • Equipment and instrument specifications.

The reactor design specifications will include sizing based on kinetic and performance
results collected from laboratory tests from earlier tasks. The critical aspects of the M2M
reactor design may include the UV flux, temperature control, the catalytic performance and
catalysts used, as well as the pressure, and fluid composition conditions for material of
construction selection. A flared methane gas source will be selected to estimate upstream
feed processing requirements. The expected composition based on laboratory yield results
will determine downstream processing, conditioning, and separation requirements in
addition to gas analysis equipment needed to quantify the performance of this modular
pilot test unit. Process control methodology will be determined to provide useful inputs
for future operation automation, where necessary instrumentation including pressure,
temperature, flow, and gas analysis sensors will be selected. Additional redundancy in
sensors and other passive safeguards, such as safety relief valves, will be incorporated in
the design based on a preliminary process safety review.

At the completion of the preliminary BEDP, a cost estimate for the fabrication,
procurement, and installation of the pilot test unit will be obtained. Startup and operating
costs will be also estimated based on the expected number of personnel and duration of test
campaigns. Results from the preliminary BEDP can inform the updates on the initial TEA
completed in Budget Period 1.

Task 11 – Experimental Testing of Photochemical Methane Removal in the Presence
of Other Gases
To understand light-driven methane conversion at concentrations <1000 ppm and at room
temperature, the Recipient will perform bench-scale experiments to quantify the efficacy—
including reaction rates, oxidation products, and apparent quantum efficiency—of
photocatalytic methane removal. The Recipient will use TiO2- and ZnO-based
photocatalysts in methane removal experiments, with dry methane in different
concentrations, ranging from 100 ppm to 1000 ppm, introduced as the input gas. The input
gas will be combined with 21% O2 (diluted by Ar) prior to flowing through the
photocatalysts. With exposure to ultraviolet (UV) light, the photocatalysts will generate
hydroxyl radicals that activate methane. To understand the impacts of other ambient gases
(e.g., CO, H2, H2O) on photocatalytic methane removal, the Recipient will co-feed these
gases into the photocatalytic reactors and evaluate their influence on the reaction rate,
oxidation products, and energy efficiency of photocatalytic methane removal.

 12

To enhance gas-phase free-radical reactions to boost methane removal, the Recipient will
extend the investigations using chlorine radicals (•Cl). The Recipient will introduce dilute
chlorine gas, which will be dissociated to release •Cl under UV illumination at room
temperature. This •Cl will be introduced into the set-up to activate methane for further
oxidation to CO and CO2. Experimental results will be summarized in terms of lessons
learned for optimizing photochemical methane removal under real-world conditions, where
other competing gases are present in the mixture.

Task 12: Review and Evaluation of Existing Methane Removal Technologies
The Recipient will conduct a comprehensive literature review of existing methane removal
technologies, including atmospheric oxidation, thermochemical approaches, and
photocatalytic methods. The Recipient will evaluate the strengths and limitations of each
methane removal approach; and consider the potential application of each approach for
removing methane from sources categorized as high, low, and atmospheric concentrations.
In this literature review, The Recipient will also provide an analysis of the energy
efficiency and technical bottlenecks of each methane removal approach; and potential
strategies for combining suitable methane removal technologies with direct air capture of
carbon dioxide.

To set the stage for future application of the most promising methane removal techniques,
the Recipient will develop a technoeconomic framework for system design and field
deployment. This will include performance targets and cost estimates to guide system
design toward the most practical, economically viable, and effective methane removal
technologies.

Executive Summary
 The oxidation of methane (CH4) is a chemical process of pivotal importance spanning chemical
industry, energy, and environment. Within this project, there are two main correlated themes with
a distinct focus on oxidizing CH4 at room-temperature. The first is the partial oxidation of methane
to value-added products like methanol. This reaction remains one of the Holy Grail in chemical
industry. The second goal is the oxidation of the dilute CH4 from pointe sources from natural gas
industry at room temperature. CH4 is a potent greenhouse gas with a high global warming potential
(GWP) around 84-87 when considering its impact over the next 20 years. Studies have shown that
by converting methane to CO2, CH4’s short-term global warming effects can be substantially
reduced.
 To activate CH4, our strategy is to leverage the free radicals, the natural sinks of CH4 in
the atmosphere. Our approach utilizes ultra-violet (UV) light to generate hydroxyl radicals,
chlorine radicals, and other radicals, which can activate methane to form methyl radicals, which
can be further converted either to methanol or carbon dioxide with assistance of O2. In the first
theme of CH4 oxidation to methanol in liquid environment (Task 1 to Task 8), we developed a
core-shell structured photocatalyst design and built a batch reactor that achieves both reasonable
selectivity and conversion efficiency in partial CH4 oxidation, which will have significant impact
on the US and global energy economy. We further elucidated its fundamental mechanism, in which
free radicals and a thin coating layer of amorphous silica play key roles in partial methane
oxidation, while the co-catalyst impacts the selectivity towards different oxygenates. Based on our
technical inputs, the team conducted a preliminary system design and techno-economic assessment
for partial oxidation of CH4 (Task 10).
 Built upon this foundation, we then explore the utilization of photocatalysts to generate
free radicals for a complete oxidation of the dilute CH4 to CO2 in a gas environment (Task 9 to
Task 11). We further expand our research by using UV light and chlorine gas to generate chlorine
radicals for a more efficient and humid-tolerant CH4 oxidation to CO2. In addition to CH4
oxidation, our studies also revealed the detrimental role of different co-feeding gases on CH4
oxidation. In addition to these technical developments, we also provide a summary and evaluation
of existing methane removal technologies (Task 12).
 In summary, our investigations on partial CH4 oxidation and CH4 removal utilizing free
radicals at room temperature combine technical development, techno-economic assessment, as
well as the overview and evaluation of existing technologies. The progress of this project offers
fundamental insights of free-radicals-mediated CH4 conversion, and engineering solutions for
selective partial oxidation of CH4 and effective total oxidation of CH4 to CO2.

Summary of Technical Progress
 Theme1: Photocatalytic upgrading of CH4.

including methanol.
 Reactor design: We first built a series of custom-designed batch reactors. Figure 1a

 Photocatalyst Design and Engineering: The catalyst we used is TiO2 (also known as
P25). A layer of amorphous silica (SiO2) with a thickness of 3 to 5 nanometers (nm) was coated
on TiO2, which gives rise to TiO2-composite oxides. We further loaded co-catalysts AuPd
nanoparticles on such composite. Figure 4 shows the transmission electron microscopy images of
AuPd nanoparticles, TiO2-composite oxides with amorphous SiO2 coating, as well as the final
composite photocatalyst with a loading of AuPd cocatalysts at a weight percentage of 1 %.

TiO2 composite oxides – TiO2 nanoparticles coated with a UV transparent oxide layer; (c) AuPd

 Based on such catalyst design, we first evaluate the roles of different co-catalysts and the
amorphous SiO2 coating on photocatalytic CH4 oxidation in the low-pressure reactor. As shown
in Figure 5, TiO2 alone leads to severe overoxidation of CH4 with CO2 as the main product.
Modifying TiO2 with different co-catalysts did not change the selectivity, while AuPd promoted
CH4 conversion. With the amorphous SiO2 coating, TiO2-composite oxides mitigated the
overoxidation issue, generating a mixture of methyl hydroperoxide (CH3OOH), methanol
(CH3OH), and formic acid (formate, HCOOH).

 CH4 Conversion Performances: Figure 7a shows the product yields and oxygenate selectivity
od TiO2@SiO2-AuPd catalyst, TiO2, and TiO2-AuPd catalyst in an optimized reaction condition (10 mg of
catalysts, 100 mL of H2O, 6.9 bar of CH4, 2.76 bar of O2, 1 h reaction time, reaction temperature of 25 ± 3
°C, light source of 365 nm UV LED, 130 mW/cm2). It is found that TiO2 and TiO2-AuPd has a lower
selectivity toward oxygenates, while TiO2@SiO2-AuPd enabled a high oxygenate selectivity with CO2 as a
minor byproduct. Among all the oxygenates, CH3OH and HCHO are the dominant products. Figure 7b
shows that decreasing the water volume from 100 mL to 20 mL decreased the oxygenate selectivity,
highlighting the key role of water in mitigating the overoxidation of oxygenates.

 The impact of the thickness of the amorphous SiO2 is further systematically investigated. Figure
7c shows that a thinner layer of 1.5 nm decreased the oxygenate selectivity, while a much thicker layer of
17.5 nm almost suppressed any obvious CH4 conversion. Figure 7d shows that increasing the UV light flux
from 130 mW/cm2 to 470 mW/cm2 would decrease the oxygenate selectivity. These investigations hinge on
an optimal operation window that takes reaction conditions, catalysts design, as well as UV flux into
account. Figure 8a compares CH4 oxidation performances of different photocatalysts, and it is found that
a physical mixture of TiO2@SiO2 and AuPd/SiO2 would not lead to the synergetic effect between the 5-nm
amorphous SiO2 coating and AuPd co-catalyst observed in composite catalyst TiO2@SiO2-AuPd.

 An annealing of the TiO2@SiO2 to 350 and 550 °C led to declined CH4 conversion (Figure 8b).
This indicates that the amorphous nature of SiO2 is another key factor in addition to the optimal thickness
of 5 nm (Figure 7c). For catalysts with SiO2 in different thickness, it is generally observed that increased
oxygen partial pressure from a low value to around 0.6 bar promoted product yields (Figure 8c), while
further increased oxygen partial pressure did not substantially increase CH4 conversion.

 Based on above investigations on catalysts design and reaction engineering, we proposed a
molecular-level reaction mechanism underlying the core-shell structured photocatalyst. As illustrated in
Figure 9, there are four steps involved in the photocatalytic methane upgrading to methanol. The first step

 23

is the photoexcitation occurring on TiO2, generating electron and hole pair. These electrons will react with
oxygen to form superoxide anion radical (O2•-), while the hole will react with water molecules to form
hydroxyl radicals (•OH). The presence of both radicals can be further confirmed by two spectroscopic
methods respectively (Figure 9 b, c). The ultraviolet–visible (UV-vis) absorption spectra of XTT-formazan
product confirmed the formation of O2•– over different photocatalysts under UV irradiation, while the
fluorescence spectra confirmed the production •OH radical from 7-hydroxycoumarin. The formation of the
hydroxyl radicals is further confirmed by the electron paramagnetic resonance study (Figure 10). The
hydroxyl radicals enable the cleavage of the first C-H bond in CH4 to form CH3 radicals, which will react
with O2•– and its protonated form OOH radicals and oxygen molecules to form CH3OOH and CH3OH. In
the last step is the overoxidation of CH3OOH and CH3OH by holes and free radicals and this process is
undesired.

 Based on above experimental data, we hypothesized the roles of AuPd cocatalyst and amorphous
SiO2 in this photocatalytic process, which is shown in the schematics in Figure 9 d, e. Free radicals can
diffuse from TiO2 surfaces to the solution through a thin and amorphous SiO2, while it would be much more
challenging for oxygen molecules to go through this barrier. With the presence of AuPd nanoparticles,
which are known to be able to activate oxygen molecules at room temperatures, the oxygen molecules can
be activated to form oxygen atoms. The transport of these oxygen atoms through the amorphous SiO 2 is
believed to be much faster, therefore enabling the electron reaction to form superoxide anion radicals, which
in turn promoted the other half-reaction to form hydroxyl radicals. The hydroxyl radicals are the key active
species to activate the C-H bond in CH4 in the solution, while the amorphous SiO2 worked as a physical
barrier and blocked the direct contact between photogenerated holes and the oxygenates, mitigating the
overoxidation. Such permeation behavior of key species and their selective transport through amorphous
SiO2 elucidated the key design principle in our photocatalysts.

 We further demonstrated that such design principle can be generalized to the selective oxidation of
ethane and noble-metal-free system with H2O2 as the source of hydroxyl radicals (Figure 11). In the latter,
the UV light induces the decomposition of H2O2 to form hydroxyl radicals and other radicals for the
activation and oxidation of CH4.

 We further compared the role of Au and Pd as co-catalyst. Figure 11 shows that under the
same conditions (TiO2@SiO2 support, mass loading, reactor conditions), Au is more effective than
Pd for CH4 conversion, while it gave rise to CH3OOH as the primary product. In comparison to
Au, Pd is more selective for CH3OH formation while less effective for CH4 conversion. By varying
the mass loading of Au1Pd0.8 on TiO2@SiO2, we found that more Pd favored a higher proportion

 29

of CH3OOH, further supporting the above observation that Pd plays a central role in controlling
the selectivity toward CH3OH, despite it is less effective in CH4 conversion than Au.

 Based on this insight, we further optimized the performance of the photocatalyst on
TiO2@SiO2-Au catalyst by systematically varying the UV flux and the thickness of the amorphous
SiO2. Figure 12 shows that a SiO2 coating of 8-9 nm can be ideally paired with a high UV flux
of 470 mW.cm-2 to achieve a high CH4 conversion of 0.93% with a high oxygenate selectivity, and
a higher CH4 conversion ratio of 1.5% by pairing a thicker SiO2 layer (13-14 nm) with a high UV
flux of 813 470 mW.cm-2. Such combination of effective CH4 conversion and high oxygenate
selectivity benchmarks performances of CH4 oxidation in existing studies.

Process design and tech-economics analysis
 Process Overview
 The process aims to produce methanol, a valuable liquid phase chemical, directly from
waste/otherwise-to-be-flared natural gas, a potent greenhouse gas, by employing a novel
photocatalytic reactor essentially operating at room temperature and low pressure. To aid the
selectivity of the desired methanol product, the catalyst in the photoreactor can either be structured
or highly dispersed on the membrane walls of the reactor vessels. The photons selectively activate
the co-catalyst surface, which reacts with the oxidizer (H2O2 or H2O or O2) to generate hydroxyl
radicals and provide high methanol selectivity, without the need to heat the bulk gas. This saves
energy and reduces the selectivity of thermally driven side reactions. Furthermore, a photoreactor
allows for the integration of renewable electricity, which eliminates on-site fossil fuel combustion,
and reduces the overall greenhouse gas footprint of the process.
 The proposed system is modular and could be used to expand existing methanol production
capabilities at a remote natural gas well facility. Methanol is a building block of the commodity

 31

chemical industry, more than 90 production plants are in operation worldwide nowadays, with a
combined production capacity of around 110 million metric ton per year (MMTA), while
providing upward of 90 000 jobs and creating roughly US$55 billion in economic activity1.

 Competitive Processes
 Direct conversion of methane to methanol remains a chemical challenge, due to the large
barriers in activating the inert methane molecules (C-H bond strength 440 kJ/mol) as well as the
higher reactivity of the C-H bond in methanol compared to those in methane. Liquid phase
reactions utilize solvation effect to stabilize methanol thus have higher selectivity but have
difficulties in methane activation and is limited by low methane solubility in water and rate of
methane transport to the catalytic surface. Due to these issues, current direct methanol processes
suffer from low catalyst activity, poor methanol selectivity, high severity of operating conditions,
and rapid catalyst deactivation2,3,4.
 Currently, industrial manufacturing approach is to produce methanol via an energy-
intensive two-step process consisting of methane reforming at 800°C and 30bar, followed by
methanol synthesis in conventional fixed bed reactors utilizing Cu-Zn catalysts, typically at 2,000
to 5,000 ton/day scale. While several companies have attempted to miniaturize the unit operations
in the large industrial plants to handle flare gas, these attempts have not been economical because
of the balance of plant systems like steam generation, syngas compression, methanol recovery,
etc.5

 Location and Siting
 The proposed process would be located adjacent to or within a new or existing natural gas
well facility and utilize waste/flare natural gas as a feedstock. Such facilities are readily available
in Texas and Louisiana along the US Gulf Coast, with established supply chains and abundant
sources of CH4 at the scale required.

 Techno-Economic Analysis

 Preliminary technoeconomic analysis was performed, in collaboration with the Casale
team, as part of Task 10 to (1) assess the feasibility of the photocatalytic methane-to-methanol
process and (2) verify and validate the initial vision and positioning described in the previous
section. Using the fundamental lab-scale data gathered at Stanford University along with the
process model described above and certain assumptions, the TEA was developed to obtain (1) total
capital cost and (2) operating cost of the plant, and (3) overall (or levelized) cost of methanol
production including the credits for co-producing formaldehyde. This TEA implies a level of
uncertainty considering the novelty and early stage TRL of the photocatalytic process. This
uncertainty leads to assumptions from both technical and economic aspects, which impacts the
accuracy of the cost estimate. The TEA is divided into two approaches:
 (A) Approach I: Based on the DOE/NETL guidelines and certain process and economic assumptions,
 the cost of methanol production ($/metric ton) of a Nth-of-a-kind unit was estimated.
 (B) Approach II: Based on the experience and input from the Casale team, Approach I was further
 improved using industrial estimations for the auxiliary units like the oxygen generation and water
 purification and storage tanks to refine the cost of methanol production ($/metric ton)

 37

Hence, this TEA helps establish the feasibility of this novel technology with respect to technical
soundness, operational flexibility, and economic viability.

 Reactor Configurations and Design Equations

 To design a pilot-scale reactor for photocatalytic oxidation of methane to methanol, we
considered two possible reactor configurations: a continuous microreactor with an immobilized
catalyst layer and a slurry reactor. The primary advantage of the microreactor is its greater
simplicity with respect to modeling and design compared to the slurry reactor. Because the catalyst
is immobilized, the scattering part of the RTE can be disregarded and the light flux intensity
depends only on the depth from the catalyst surface, allowing the light flux intensity to be explicitly
mathematically described.1 The reactors can easily be configured in a manner that allows the
incident light flux intensity to be known. The primary disadvantage of the microreactor is its
limited throughput.
 While a slurry reactor is a more traditional choice for a three-phase reaction and could be
implemented on a larger scale than the microreactor, it is a more complex system and would be
challenging from a design and modeling perspective. The catalyst is not immobilized and the
scattering part of the RTE must therefore be included when calculating the light flux intensity ϕ.
Moreover, the incident light flux intensity ϕ0 is not constant throughout the reactor, making its
mathematical description challenging. Finally, slurry reactors are typically operated in batch mode
due to the difficulty of continuous catalyst separation,4 increasing the operational complexity and
decreasing throughput relative to continuous operation.
 While the throughput of a single microreactor would be low, continuous microreactors
readily lend themselves to a modular configuration with multiple reactors connected in series.
Intermediate continuous separation steps could be included in such a configuration, increasing
overall yield, and helping address the central problem of the methane to methanol reaction: low
yield due to the requirement of operating each individual reactor at ultra-low methane conversion
to maintain selectivity.

 46

 Based on these considerations, we recommend a continuous microreactor with an
immobilized catalyst layer for scale up of the photocatalytic methane to methanol reaction.

of formaldehyde. The significant formation of formaldehyde indicates non-differential conversion,
with the concentrations of the intermediates peaking before eventually decreasing. However, for
operation of the pilot reactor, we recommend decreasing the conversion such that a negligible
amount of formaldehyde is formed, serving to both increase the methanol yield and allow
differential operation.

 We first built a flow reactor that allows us to evaluate the performance of solid

 Considering the detrimental effect of moisture and the challenge of utilizing photocatalysts

 Figure 23 shows the experimental set-up that was built in Majumdar Lab at Stanford
University to evaluate the impact of co-existing gases on photochemical CH4 oxidation. The
concentration of different gases (e.g., CH4, NH3, O2) were mixed and balanced by N2. Their
concentration was controlled using multiple mass-flow controllers (MFC). Furthermore, the water
vapor was introduced into the reactor using a humidifying bubbler. In the reactor part, 365 nm
ultraviolet (UV) light was introduced by using light-emitting diode (LED) panel. We used Cl2
sensor to monitor the concentration of Cl2 in the outlet. The unreacted Cl2 and HCl species are
treated with a NaOH bubbler before entering the gas analyzers. The final products and their
concentration were confirmed by gas analyzers, including Fourier Transform Infrared
spectroscopy (FTIR) and Cavity-ring down (CRD) sensor.

UV light. It was found that with about 30 part-per-million (ppm) CH4 and 30 ppm Cl2 introduced

(CO) and carbon dioxide (CO2), with the former as the major product. The carbon balance between
the depleted CH4 and as-formed CO and CO2 illustrates the effectiveness of using Cl radicals for
CH4 oxidation, with CO and CO2 as the final product.

of oxidation products. And it is found that with 50 ppm NH3 as the co-existing gas, tiny CH4

conversion (around 2 ppm) was observed in the presence of UV light. This suppression of CH4
oxidation was mainly because of a side reaction between NH3, as a reduction agent, and Cl radicals.

may also involve the direct reaction between NH3 and Cl2.

 In summary, we have built up an experimental platform that allows us to quantify the
impact of co-existing gas molecules on light-driven CH4 oxidation. It was found that Cl radicals
can effectively convert CH4 to CO and Cl2, with CO as the major product. Our systematic studies
evaluated the detrimental effect of NH3 on CH4 oxidation and removal using Cl radicals. It was
found that with 50 ppm NH3, CH4 conversion was almost suppressed. Our studies confirmed the
importance of understanding and controlling the emission of reductive gas molecules (e.g., NH3,
H2, CO) in light-driven CH4 removal. Our methodologies and insights studies can be also
transformable to other gas molecules, such as organic volatile compounds. These studies provide

 55

a conceptual framework to study CH4 removal under realistic conditions, where dilute co-existing
gas molecules and water molecules are both present.
