SagaQA : A Multi-hop Reasoning Benchmark for Long-form Narrative Understanding in TV Series
Galann Pennec Zhengyuan Liu, Nicholas Asher Philippe Muller Nancy F. Chen

Abstract
We introduce SagaQA , a long-form video benchmark for multi-hop reasoning over full-length TV series. Existing video reasoning benchmarks often emphasize local understanding of adjacent frames or clips. SagaQA addresses this gap by requiring high-level comprehension of extended multimodal narratives in entire TV shows. A distinguishing feature of SagaQA is the granularity of its reasoning steps. Our dataset necessitates long-range reasoning hops to connect information across completely different episodes. This requires models to reason over entire events and actions, demanding a deep understanding of the show’s narration and progression at a multimodal level. Motivated by recent progress in agentic methods, we further study how different planning strategies handle such complex reasoning. We categorize these approaches into three classes— Parallel , Sequential , and Hybrid planners—and evaluate their ability to generate coherent and complete reasoning plans. Our results on SagaQA suggest that hybrid planners consistently produce higher-quality plans and exhibit stronger capabilities for complex, high-level narrative understanding in TV shows. 1 1 1 Our code and benchmark are available at https://github.com/galannp/sagaqa

Introduction

Recent advancements in Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have significantly expanded the frontiers of video understanding, with models demonstrating impressive capabilities in visual reasoning
(
5
;
28
;
2
)
. However, a critical disparity remains between the ability to perform short-term visual perception and the capacity for long-term narrative comprehension. While existing benchmarks have successfully formalized stepwise causal reasoning within short videos of a few minutes
(
3
;
12
)
or multi-step reasoning over longer single-file narratives
(
38
)
, they typically rely on adjacent clips and low-level understanding.

To bridge the gap between short-context perception and long-form understanding, recent methodologies have largely bifurcated into efficient retrieval and agentic reasoning. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) approaches
(
23
;
31
;
16
)
and frame selection strategies
(
40
;
37
;
8
)
attempt to manage extreme token lengths by isolating key visual information. Simultaneously, agent-based frameworks
(
29
;
34
)
and tool-augmented reinforcement learning methods
(
43
;
41
)
have introduced interactive reasoning to navigate video content. Despite these innovations, evaluations remain constrained, as they often overlook the high-level narrative progression essential for comprehending complex, long-form content such as TV series.

In this paper, we present
SagaQA
, an abstractive question answering benchmark designed to evaluate multi-hop narrative reasoning over full-length TV series.
SagaQA
addresses key limitations of prior datasets by introducing a
multi-video
,
multi-hop
and
multi-modal
benchmark, where inputs are substantially longer and narratively more complex (see Table
1
). Each question targets a window of 20 consecutive episodes—approximately 20 hours of video—requiring models to perform “big-hop” reasoning to connect distant events from the same narrative chain. On average, answering a question from our dataset involves four reasoning hops, with relevant events potentially separated by up to 20 episodes. Therefore,
SagaQA
constitutes a particularly challenging benchmark, especially in comparison to prior work that does not require the same depth of reasoning over complex narrative video content (see Table
1
)—such as egocentric video understanding
(
32
)
or open-world internet video browsing
(
13
)
.

The dataset is constructed using the pipeline in Figure
1
. The resulting question-answer (QA) pairs undergo LLM-based filtering to ensure they satisfy the intended multi-hop reasoning criteria.

We study the performance of existing baselines on this benchmark including video planning methods, which we categorize into three classes:
Parallel
,
Sequential
, and
Hybrid
. Our results show that hybrid planners consistently outperform the other approaches, achieving more complete and accurate episode grounding by combining broad exploration of candidate video segments with focused reasoning over the most relevant ones. By moving evaluations toward long-term, event-driven comprehension,
SagaQA
provides valuable insights into the current limitations and future directions of multi-step reasoning in long-form video understanding.

Our contributions are as follows:

•

We introduce
SagaQA
, a novel benchmark specifically designed for evaluating long-form, multi-hop reasoning over TV series, addressing the gap between short-term visual perception and full-episode narrative understanding.

•

We systematically analyze video planning strategies, introducing the distinction between
Parallel
,
Sequential
, and
Hybrid
Planners.

•

We demonstrate that hybrid planners achieve superior performance by balancing efficient exploration with deep multi-hop reasoning, providing insights for future research in long-form video understanding.

Benchmark Construction

3.1
Dataset Description

Our dataset,
SagaQA
, contains 200 QA pairs spanning 4 years of the TV show
As the World Turns
(2007-2010), providing a sufficient resource for controlled evaluation of multi-hop reasoning across extended video narratives. Each sample consists of a question
Q
associated with a set of 20 consecutive episodes
\mathcal{E}_{20}
, for which the corresponding videos are provided. The task is framed as abstractive question answering; the ground-truth annotations include the answer
A
to the question and the subset
\mathcal{E}^{*}
of episodes that are relevant for answering it. Formally, a sample can be represented as (
Q
,
\mathcal{E}_{20}
,
A
,
\mathcal{E}^{*}
).

We impose three key constraints on the QA pairs in the
SagaQA
dataset:

•

Multi-Episode
The questions cannot be answered using a single episode.

•

Multi-Hop
The questions must integrate information from multiple audio-visual segments through a series of dependent reasoning steps that progressively build toward the final answer.

•

Multi-Modal
The questions cannot be answered from dialogue alone and require analyzing visual elements from the video.

In Appendix
A

Dataset Statistics

On average, each question involves approximately
4.2

The size of each hop, however, varies substantially within the 20-episode window. As illustrated by the distribution in Figure

Multi-Hop Video Reasoning with Agentic Planners

4.1
Video Planning

Long TV shows are too large for end-to-end processing by VLMs. Additionally, multi-hop questions often require step-by-step reasoning, making static single-turn retrieval highly ineffective
(
20
)
.
Multi-hop reasoning is commonly addressed through planning strategies in the literature. A planner aims to answer a composite question, i.e., a question that requires both independent (parallel) and dependent (multi-hop) reasoning steps to produce a final answer. Typically, it generates a set of queries that are executed by external tools and whose outputs are aggregated to construct a global answer. Video planners can be categorized into three types (Figure
3
).

Parallel Planner
A planner that decomposes a composite question into a set of simpler, independent subquestions, which are then answered in parallel
(
22
)
.

Sequential Planner
A planner that answers a composite question through a sequence of steps
(
43
;
41
;
29
;
26
)
. At each step (Figure
3
), the agent selects an action from a predefined set, generates a subquestion, and invokes the corresponding tools. Overall, the sequential planner issues queries one at a time, progressively building toward the final answer.

Hybrid Planner
We introduce the hybrid planner as a way to combine the strengths of both parallel and sequential approaches. Like the sequential planner, the agent selects an action at each step. However, similar to the parallel planner, it generates and executes multiple independent queries simultaneously at each step.
This design significantly reduces the length of reasoning chains. It mitigates the main limitations of sequential planning: 1) reasoning traces tend to drift and accumulate noise as depth increases, and 2) low cost efficiency due to the inability to execute queries in parallel. At the same time, it preserves the flexibility needed to handle multi-hop dependencies, which purely parallel planners fail to capture.

4.2
Video Planners Implementation

In this section, we describe each studied video planning strategy and provide full implementation details for all methods in Appendix
B.3
. In our experiments, we use the VideoRAG framework
(
23
)
as the only tool for retrieving video information. In the VideoRAG implementation, an LLM judges the confidence of the retrieved video segments. If all retrieved segments are assigned low confidence, the system reports back to the planner that no answer was found for the query (
✘

Parallel Planner + VideoRAG
We study two variants of this approach. The first implementation (Prompt 1) extracts keywords from the question, groups them by similarity, and generates one subquestion per group to query the VideoRAG. In the second implementation (Prompt 2), an LLM performs an end-to-end decomposition of the original composite question into subquestions.

Sequential Planner + VideoRAG
In this setting, we choose the set of actions from among (
BREAKDOWN
,
MOVE_ON
).

The
BREAKDOWN

✘

The
MOVE_ON

✔

STOP

Hybrid Planner + VideoRAG
Similar to the sequential planner, the agent dynamically selects between the
BREAKDOWN
and
MOVE_ON

Unlike the original implementation of VideoRAG, which relies on Gemma-2-9B

5.3
Results

We split the dataset into two subsets, using 50 QA pairs for validation and 150 for testing. For each tested baseline, we identify the set of episodes used to ground its generated answers. This predicted episode set is then compared to the annotations in
SagaQA
to compute the Precision, Recall, and F1 scores reported in Table

The Importance of Multimodality

We notice that all the episode grounding metrics drop when using TextRAG instead of VideoRAG, suggesting that visual information plays a meaningful role in answering the questions in
SagaQA
.

Hybrid Planning for Better Episode Grounding

The hybrid planner achieves substantially stronger episode grounding than the other evaluated video planners as indicated by higher F1 scores in Table

. This improvement indicates that it effectively combines the strengths of parallel and sequential strategies, leveraging both broad exploration and deep reasoning (see Section
6
).

Importantly, these observations hold consistently when replacing Qwen3-30B-Instruct with Mistral-Small-3.1-24B in all VideoRAG-based methods (see Appendix
C
).

6
Discussion

Exploration–Exploitation Trade-Off

Our experiments reveal a systematic trade-off across planners. Parallel planners issue multiple queries in a single step, enabling broad coverage of the search space but limiting their ability to perform iterative, multi-hop refinement. In contrast, sequential planners refine queries over multiple steps, but tend to over-exploit early high-confidence directions, repeatedly issuing similar queries and failing to adequately cover alternative directions. Our hybrid planner mitigates both limitations and achieves significantly higher F1 scores (Table
2
). To illustrate this behavior, we compare, in Appendix
D
, the reasoning traces of our hybrid planner with those of two sequential planners, highlighting the latter’s tendency to repeatedly issue similar queries instead of effectively expanding the search space.

Limitations in Answer Synthesis

Even when the planner retrieves the relevant video segments required to answer the question, effectively exploiting this evidence to produce a high-quality answer remains challenging. While episode grounding metrics vary significantly across the studied settings, abstractive metrics—such as ROUGE and METEOR—reported in Table
2
remain largely unchanged. This discrepancy suggests that current methods are limited not in retrieval, but in downstream information synthesis. In particular, even when the correct episode is identified, the planner may fail to capture the precise narrative pivot (e.g., a subtle facial expression or a key visual cue), since the agentic planner processes video segments as isolated units rather than as parts of a coherent temporal arc. This highlights the need for more structured post-retrieval reasoning and aggregation mechanisms.

Toward Structured and Hybrid Planning Strategies

The strong performance of the hybrid planner underscores the benefits of combining complementary reasoning paradigms. The parallel component acts as a broad exploration mechanism, identifying a diverse set of candidate video segments, while the sequential component serves as a knowledge refiner, ensuring that multi-hop dependencies are coherently connected. This division of roles suggests that future long-form agents should move beyond single-trajectory reasoning and instead adopt more structured exploration strategies, such as Tree-of-Thought
(
36
)
, tree-based or graph-based planning frameworks
(
33
)
. Further improvements could be achieved through targeted training, as well as the integration of specialized retrieval and grounding tools to enhance both coverage and reasoning consistency.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced
SagaQA
, a novel benchmark designed to push the boundaries of long-form video understanding from local perception to high-level narrative comprehension. By focusing on multi-hop reasoning across 20-episode windows—representing approximately 20 hours of continuous video—
SagaQA
challenges models to connect disparate events, character arcs, and multimodal cues that span significant temporal distances.

Our systematic evaluation of various video planning strategies reveals a critical
exploration-exploitation
trade-off in long-form reasoning. While parallel planners provide broad coverage of the search space, they often lack the depth required for complex causal chains. Conversely, sequential planners excel at refinement but are prone to reasoning drift and repetitive loops. We demonstrate that hybrid planners, which combine parallel exploration and sequential refinement, achieve superior performance, particularly in grounding answers to the correct episodes.

Our results suggest that, beyond retrieval, a key bottleneck lies in effectively synthesizing evidence across extended narratives. Even when relevant episodes are correctly identified, current models struggle to consistently aggregate this information into accurate answers. By releasing
SagaQA
and our evaluation framework, we aim to facilitate further research in long video reasoning.

Limitations

On the evaluation side, the proposed task-specific metrics—Episode Precision, Recall, and F1—are both challenging and sufficiently discriminative. However, the current annotations in
SagaQA
could be further manually refined by experts by grounding the answers at the clip level rather than entire episodes. These finer-grained annotations would allow for a more precise and informative evaluation than episode-level assessment.

The benchmark is built exclusively from TV shows (Section
3.3
). Soap operas have very specific narrative structures (e.g., strong cross-episode narrative continuity, dialogue-driven storytelling, and recurring characters). Models might overfit to this genre, and the results may not generalize to other video domains such as documentaries or instructional videos.

While 20 hours of video is long-form, the benchmark uses a fixed window of 20 consecutive episodes (Section
3.1
). In actual TV series, character arcs and plot hops can span hundreds of episodes over several years.
SagaQA
does not yet test
ultra-long
memory that spans the entire lifecycle of a TV series.

B.3.4
Hybrid Planner

The hybrid planner is a combination of both the parallel and sequential planners. We leverage the prompts of both methods in the implementation of the hybrid approach.

At any given step
i
, we proceed as follows:

•

Prompt the agent for the next action (
BREAKDOWN
or
MOVE_ON
), using the same prompt as the sequential planner in Appendix
B.3.3

•

If the action is
BREAKDOWN
, we use the prompt from the parallel planner to decompose the current query into a list of independent subquestions (Appendix
B.3.2
).

•

If the action is
MOVE_ON
, the agent processes the next available subquestion from the most recent
BREAKDOWN
. If no pending subquestions remain across all prior
BREAKDOWN
steps, the reasoning process stops.

As specified in Section
4.3
, we limit the maximum depth of the reasoning tree to 2 and do not allow any reasoning beyond that fixed limit.
