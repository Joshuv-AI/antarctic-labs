Widespread adhesion and iron acquisition traits in Escherichia coli strains obtained from irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) patients
Authors: Arezo Khadiv, Mohammad Yazdanmanesh, Hamid Heidari, Fateme Yazdani, Hamidreza Houri, Behzad Badakhsh, Sobhan Ghafourian, Hossein Kazemian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe intestinal microbiome is essential for maintaining a balanced and healthy gut environment. Certain Escherichia coli strains can disrupt it through various pathogenic factors and have been linked to gastrointestinal diseases. In this study, E. coli strains were isolated from individuals with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and healthy controls. Identification was performed using microbiological and molecular methods. The presence of key virulence factors, including adhesins (iha, lpfA, afaC, sfaDE, papC, focG, aafII), iron acquisition systems (chuA, iroN, fepC, irp2, iutA, ireA), hemolysin (hlyA), microcins and colicins (cva, colY), and multifunctional factors (malX, yajQ), was assessed by polymerase chain reaction.ResultsThe genes lpfA, papC, iroN, ireA, cva, and malX were found to be significantly more prevalent in IBS-associated isolates following Bonferroni correction (p < 0.0031). A higher prevalence of sfaDE, focG, chuA, and hlyA was also observed, though these differences were not statistically significant. Conversely, the genes iha and irp2 were significantly more common in E. coli isolates from healthy individuals. Notably, none of the isolates harbored the afaC and aafII genes. This study demonstrates significant differences in the distribution of specific virulence genes between E. coli isolates from IBS patients and healthy individuals. The higher prevalence of specific genes in IBS isolates may contribute to their pathogenic potential, whereas the frequent occurrence of iha and irp2 in healthy individuals may suggest a role in maintaining a balanced gut microbiome. These findings highlight distinct virulence gene profiles, suggesting a potential association between these E. coli factors and IBS disease status.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13104-026-07922-3.

Introduction

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a prevalent functional digestive disorder characterized by a range of gastrointestinal and neurological symptoms, including abdominal pain, diarrhea, constipation, changes in bowel habits, bloating, abdominal distension, and mood disorders [1]. The global prevalence of IBS ranges from 10% to 25%, with a higher incidence observed in women [1]. IBS pathogenesis involves diverse environmental and host factors, such as age, gender, socioeconomic status, food intolerances, antibiotic use, intestinal infections, increased intestinal permeability, activation of the intestinal mucosal immune system, and heightened digestive sensitivity [2]. IBS is classified into four subtypes based on stool form as assessed by the Bristol Stool Scale: predominant diarrhea (IBS-D), predominant constipation (IBS-C), mixed form (IBS-M), and unclassified form (IBS-U) [1]. Unlike specific organic pathologies such as inflammatory bowel disease and celiac disease, IBS does not have a unique pathological signature [1, 3]. However, recent evaluations have highlighted the significance of gastrointestinal microbiota and the alterations in its composition and function as critical components in the pathogenesis and pathophysiology of IBS [3]. An imbalance in the microbial community, referred to as dysbiosis, can adversely affect the intestinal mucosa and is commonly observed in various intestinal disorders, including IBS [4]. Intestinal barrier dysfunction has been extensively documented in patients with IBS and is significantly associated with microbial dysbiosis [4, 5]. Studies indicate that microbial diversity in stool samples from IBS patients is lower than that found in healthy controls [6]. Meta-analyses reveal decreased levels of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, alongside elevated levels of Escherichia coli and Enterobacter in the stool samples of individuals with IBS [6–8]. Pathogenic strains of E. coli reside in the lower intestine of humans; however, substantial evidence points to a significant increase in E. coli colonization within the large intestine of IBS patients [9]. Certain subgroups of E. coli are implicated in gastrointestinal diseases in humans, with many possessing specific pathogenic factors that influence the pathology of infections. E. coli isolated from IBS patients exhibit a mosaic of pathogenic traits linked to pathogenic biovars, including enterotoxigenic, enterohemorrhagic, and enteropathogenic E. coli [9, 10]. Among these, adherent-invasive E. coli (AIEC) has been increasingly implicated in intestinal dysbiosis and inflammatory bowel disease, and recent studies have also suggested its potential role in IBS pathophysiology. Additionally, enteroaggregative E. coli (EAEC) has been associated with IBS symptoms. Pathogenic strains of E. coli utilize specific adhesion factors to colonize atypical sites within the gastrointestinal tract. Many of these surface structures activate signal transduction pathways and induce the secretion of E. coli toxins [10]. IBS is characterized by an enhanced innate immune response marked by the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and nitric oxide radicals [11]. Both E. coli and other microbiota possess endogenous mechanisms or specialized molecules that enable them to survive in this unfavorable chemical environment while effectively neutralizing host-derived ROS [11]. For instance, several studies have demonstrated that bacterial siderophores can mitigate oxidative stress by neutralizing ROS [12, 13]. We hypothesize that specific pathogenic factors of E. coli contribute to IBS through mechanisms such as adhesion, iron acquisition, toxin production, and microbial competition. In light of the aforementioned information, it appears that the gastrointestinal microbiome plays a crucial role in the development of IBS. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the detection of major pathogenic factors, including adhesins (iha, lpfA, afaC, sfaDE, papC, focG, and aafII), iron acquisition factors (chuA, iroN, fepC, irp2, iutA, ireA), hemolysin (hlyA), microcins and colicins (cva and colY), and the factors with various functions (malX and yajQ), using the polymerase chain reaction method. Understanding these factors may clarify the role of E. coli virulence in IBS pathophysiology.

Materials and methods

Samples and strains

Stool samples were collected from 37 IBS patients (age range: 20–49 years, including 17 males and 20 females) diagnosed by a gastroenterologist based on the Rome III criteria and from 30 healthy individuals patients (age range: 20–50 years, including 13 males and 17 females) who served as the control group. The control group was matched with the IBS group for age and sex. The criteria for healthy individuals were no history of gastrointestinal disorders, no current gastrointestinal symptoms, no recent use of antibiotics or probiotics (within the last 3 months), and no chronic diseases. The same exclusion criteria regarding antibiotic and probiotic use (no use within the last 3 months) were also applied to the IBS patients. From each sample, a single colony with typical E. coli morphology was selected for further analysis. Samples were transported to the laboratory on ice, samples were immediately cultured by inoculating on MacConkey agar and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Phenotypic tests such as gram staining, glucose fermentation, oxidase reaction, amino acid deaminase and decarboxylase, acid production, indole production, and motility were performed and E. coli isolates were identified [14]. Molecular confirmation was performed using phoA gene polymerase chain reaction (PCR) as a supportive marker in conjunction with phenotypic and biochemical identification methods [15]. Each PCR run included a positive control and a negative control (nuclease-free water). Positive controls consisted of the following reference strains: E. coli ATCC 25,922 (for phoA and yajQ), E. coli CFT073 (for papC, sfaDE, focG, hlyA, chuA, iutA, ireA, fepC), E. coli E2348/69 (for lpfA), and E. coli O157:H7 EDL933 (for iha and irp2). For cva, colY, malX, afaC, and aafII, clinical isolates previously confirmed positive by PCR (based on expected band size) were used as positive controls.

DNA extraction and detection of virulence-associated genes

For DNA template preparation, a boiling lysis method was used. Two to three pure colonies of each E. coli isolate were suspended in 200 µL of sterile distilled water and adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard. The suspension was heated at 100 °C for 20 min in a dry bath, then immediately centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant containing crude DNA was carefully transferred to a new tube and used as the PCR template. DNA quality and approximate concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop™ spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA); however, due to the crude nature of the boiling lysis method, these values were used only for consistency checks and not for precise quantification. All samples showed a 260/280 ratio between 1.6 and 1.9. All PCR amplifications were performed in a final volume of 25 µL containing 12.5 µL of 2× PCR Master Mix (SinaClon, Tehran, Iran), 2.5 µL of the extracted DNA template, 1.5 µL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 1 µL of each forward and reverse primer (10 pmol/µL), and 4.5 µL nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at the gene-specific temperature (as listed in Table 1) for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s; with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Each PCR run included a positive control and a negative control. The housekeeping gene phoA was used as an internal amplification control for all DNA samples; only samples showing a clear phoA band (622 bp) were included in the final analysis. All PCR reactions were performed in duplicate to confirm reproducibility. In all cases, duplicate reactions yielded identical results. Any sample with discrepant results between duplicates would have been re-tested, but no such discrepancy occurred in this study. PCR products were separated by electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel stained with Safe Stain (SinaClon, Tehran, Iran), visualized under UV light, and compared to a 100 bp DNA ladder (SinaClon, Tehran, Iran). The presence of each gene was determined based on detection of a PCR product with the expected amplicon size. Absence of a band at the expected size was interpreted as a negative result. A representative agarose gel image for the irp2 gene is shown in Fig. 1.

Statistical analysis

The presence of each gene was compared in the isolates from IBS patients and healthy individuals. The data were imported into SPSS software (version 22) and a Fisher’s exact test (two-tailed) was used for all comparisons due to low expected cell counts in some genes. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Gene selection rationale

Gene selection was hypothesis-driven, based on three mechanisms: adhesion (colonization potential), iron acquisition (survival under inflammation), and bacterial competition (dysbiosis). These genes were chosen for their reported associations with intestinal inflammation and pathogenic E. coli.

Chord diagram visualization

A chord diagram was constructed to visualize the distribution of virulence-associated genes between IBS-associated and healthy-associated E. coli isolates. A binary presence/absence matrix was generated from PCR results for all 18 genes across 67 isolates (37 IBS, 30 healthy). The matrix was aggregated to calculate gene frequencies per group. The diagram was created using the R package circlize (version 0.4.15), where the left semicircle represents the two groups, the right semicircle represents individual genes, and chord width is proportional to the number of positive isolates for each gene within each group. Only genes with statistically significant prevalence differences (p < 0.05) were included for clarity.

Results

A total of 37 E. coli isolates were obtained from patients with IBS, and 30 E. coli isolates were collected from healthy individuals as control strains. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 2. All isolates were confirmed as E. coli via phenotypic tests (Gram staining, glucose fermentation, oxidase, indole production, and motility) and phoA PCR (Supplementary Table 1). The prevalence of virulence genes was compared between the two groups; odds ratios (OR), risk ratios (RR), and risk differences (RD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to quantify the magnitude of associations (Table 3).

Virulence genes were grouped according to their function: adhesins (iha, lpfA, afaC, sfaDE, papC, focG, aafII), iron acquisition systems (chuA, iroN, fepC, irp2, iutA, ireA), hemolysins (hlyA), microcins and colicins (cva, colY), and other functional genes (malX, yajQ). Among the adhesion genes, papC was the most common, identified in 23 IBS-associated E. coli isolates and 2 healthy-associated E. coli isolates. Regarding iron acquisition genes, irp2 was the most prevalent, found in 25 control E. coli isolates and 16 IBS-associated isolates. Furthermore, the colY gene was present in 15 isolates from healthy individuals and 14 isolates from IBS patients. The yajQ gene was the most frequent among all analyzed genes, with a total of 41 positive isolates. Finally, none of the isolates tested positive for the afaC or aafII genes (Table 2).

The distribution of virulence genes between IBS-associated and healthy-associated E. coli isolates is illustrated in a chord diagram (Fig. 2), which highlights genes with a significantly different prevalence between the two groups. Due to the small sample size and the presence of zero counts in the control group for several genes, the calculated odds ratios are inflated and exhibit very wide confidence intervals. Therefore, these effect sizes should be interpreted with caution and are presented only as descriptive measures.

Discussion

This study reveals notable differences in the genetic profiles of E. coli strains isolated from patients with IBS compared to those from healthy controls. Specifically, genes such as cva, iuta, chuA, iroN, lpfA1, papC, malX, ireA, focG, and sfaD/E exhibited significantly higher prevalence in E. coli from IBS patients (P < 0.05). These genes indicate a possible link between bacterial pathogenicity and IBS symptoms. However, this relationship is likely to be multifactorial rather than solely causative. Recent studies have revealed that Pathogenesis of IBS involves changes in the composition and function of the intestinal microbiota [30, 31]. If expressed under suitable conditions, the cva gene, responsible for colicin V production, may enhance competitive fitness against other microbial flora, which could potentially contribute to dysbiosis. Our findings align with other studies [32, 33], who noted that colicin production could enhance bacterial competitiveness in the gut environment, contributing to dysbiosis observed in IBS patients. Similarly, the iutA gene plays a critical role in iron uptake, essential for bacterial survival and proliferation, particularly under inflammatory conditions. Assuming functional expression, the increased prevalence of iutA in IBS isolates is consistent with Hagan et al.‘s findings that iron acquisition mechanisms may bolster pathogenicity and survival in such contexts [34]. Iron acquisition is further emphasized by the presence of the chuA and iroN genes, which help pathogenic strains overcome host defenses. A higher prevalence of chuA was observed in IBS isolates (64.9% vs. 30%, p = 0.005), but this difference did not remain statistically significant after Bonferroni correction (α = 0.0031). Therefore, it is discussed only as a trend. Nevertheless, previous studies have shown that chuA contributes to iron acquisition and virulence in other contexts [34]. Additionally, the significant presence of iroN in E. coli from IBS patients corroborates Caza and Kronstad’s work on iron acquisition systems, indicating a possible link to pathogenicity in IBS [35, 36]. The papC gene, associated with the adherence of E. coli to uroepithelial cells, may also play a role in colonization or inflammation within the gut. The increased prevalence of papC in IBS patients is consistent with Hultgren et al.‘s findings regarding its role in urinary tract infections, raising the possibility that analogous mechanisms—if operational in the gut—might be involved in gut-related pathologies [37]. Furthermore, malX, a gene linked to maltose transport and metabolism, indicates metabolic adaptations of E. coli in IBS patients, allowing these bacteria to thrive in altered gut environments. Our observation of elevated malX prevalence supports previous findings that metabolic adaptations, if required under specific gut conditions, may be vital for bacterial survival [38, 39]. The higher frequency of ireA in IBS isolates is consistent with Li et al., who demonstrated its role in iron regulation under inflammatory conditions [40]. Among the adhesion and colonization genes, lpfA1, focG, and sfaD/E are associated with fimbrial adhesins that facilitate bacterial adherence to the intestinal epithelium [41, 42]. If expressed and assembled into functional fimbriae, this adherence could promote inflammation and potentially contribute to IBS pathology. Our findings regarding the higher prevalence of lpfA1 in IBS strains correlate with Zhou et al., who demonstrated its role in adherence and colonization, which—if functional—could potentially exacerbate inflammation [43]. Similarly, our observations on focG support Lindberg et al. [42, 44] work on its role in adhesion and biofilm formation, which may contribute to the chronic inflammation observed in IBS. The increased prevalence of sfaD/E further correlates with Ananis et al., findings on fimbrial adhesions roles in bacterial adherence and pathogenesis [45]. Although sfaD/E and focG were more prevalent in IBS isolates (18.9% vs. 0%, p = 0.012 for both), these differences were not significant after Bonferroni correction and are therefore considered trends only. Conversely, the iha and irp2 gene, respectively known as pathogenic factor in pathogenicity of EHEC and avian pathogenic E. coli [46, 47], shows higher prevalence in healthy controls in our study. However, for iha, the difference (16.2% vs. 46.7%, p = 0.007) did not reach statistical significance after Bonferroni correction, so this finding should be interpreted cautiously as a preliminary trend. It is possible that, if expressed at appropriate levels and in the absence of other virulence factors, these genes may contribute to beneficial or commensal functions under normal physiological conditions. However, our finding is contradictory to a recent study that clearly demonstrated the association of irp2 with intestinal inflammation [48]. This discrepancy may be explained by the fact that gene presence does not always correlate with gene expression. Commensal E. coli may carry virulence genes but lack their functional expression. Due to sample limitations, we could not perform expression studies (e.g., Western blot) for irp2. Future studies should investigate irp2 expression levels in IBS-associated versus healthy-associated E. coli isolates.

If expressed in the absence of other pathogenic factors, iha may contribute to microbial balance by facilitating the colonization of non-pathogenic E. coli strains. This is particularly important for gut health, as beneficial bacteria often compete with pathogens for resources and attachment sites, thus inhibiting their growth. Irp2 gene, is crucial for iron regulation. Yilmaz et al., findings that proper iron regulation is vital for sustaining a healthy gut microbiota, as dysbiosis can lead to various gastrointestinal issues, including IBS [49].

The higher prevalence of iha and irp2 among control isolates may indicate roles in commensal adaptation and microbiome stability rather than in classical pathogenicity. This underscores the importance of ecological and host-specific contexts, and further functional studies are needed to clarify their dual roles in health and disease.

Although our findings indicate significant differences in virulence gene profiles between IBS patients and healthy controls, the cross-sectional nature of our study precludes conclusions about causality. It remains unclear whether the presence of these genes contributes directly to the pathophysiology of IBS, or whether the altered gut environment characteristic of IBS—such as microbiota shifts, inflammation, or increased intestinal permeability—selectively favors the colonization or expansion of certain strains of E. coli. The differences in the prevalence of these genes between IBS patients and healthy individuals highlight potential mechanisms.

Our results demonstrate associations rather than causation and should be interpreted as indicative of correlations between gene carriage and disease status, rather than as proof of direct pathogenic effects. Further studies are required to determine whether these genetic factors act as drivers or consequences of IBS.

Understanding these genetic factors could pave the way for new therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring a balanced microbial community in those with IBS [50, 51]. Gut microbiota plays an important role in the development of IBS, and there is even more potential as a therapeutic strategy for managing IBS by modulating gut microbiota [52, 53].

Future research should delve deeper into the functional roles of these genes and their interactions within the gut environment to enhance our understanding of microbial dynamics and their effects on host health. Previous studies have shown variable prevalence of virulence genes in E. coli strains isolated from individuals with IBS [50, 51]. In summary, the genetic differences identified between E. coli strains from IBS patients and healthy controls underscore the potential roles these virulence-associated genes play in the pathophysiology of IBS. Further exploration of these genetic factors may provide valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying dysbiosis and gut inflammation in IBS patients.

Conclusion

The comparison highlights how the genes identified in your study align with existing literature on their roles in virulence, adhesion, and metabolic adaptation. The findings suggest that E. coli strains from IBS patients possess certain genetic traits that may contribute to pathogenicity and dysbiosis, although after stringent Bonferroni correction only six genes (lpfA, papC, iroN, ireA, cva, malX) showed statistically significant associations. Other observed differences should be considered preliminary. Further research could explore these genetic factors’ functional implications and their potential as therapeutic targets or biomarkers for IBS management.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The study was cross-sectional and included only 37 isolates from patients with IBS, which may limit the generalizability of the results. In addition, it was based on culture-dependent methods and did not use metagenomic approaches to capture the full genetic diversity of the microbiome. Owing to budgetary and sample availability constraints, we were unable to perform several complementary analyses, including gene expression assays, the use of more specific markers, PCR screening for additional genes such as ibeA, capsule-associated genes, and ratA, as well as phylogrouping and pathotyping. Moreover, the specificity of all PCR reactions was not fully confirmed by amplicon sequencing, and only one strain was isolated from each individual, which may not fully reflect intra-individual diversity. Finally, the absence of functional assays limits interpretation of whether differences in gene carriage are associated with altered bacterial behavior.
