Comparative Evaluation
of Electron Paramagnetic Resonance
and Spectrophotometry for DPPH-Based Antioxidant Capacity Determination

Helvis L. S. Fontoura, Ana Flávia L. M. Nascimento, Francine A. F. Menezes, Pâmela V. Rocha, Stanislau Bogusz, Nádia R. Pereira, André O. Guimarães

Abstract

The DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl)
assay is one of the most
widely used methods for determining the radical scavenging capacity
(RSC), yet discrepancies between spectrophotometric and electron paramagnetic
resonance (EPR) measurements remain insufficiently addressed. In this
study, simultaneous RSC measurements were performed by EPR and spectrophotometry
to investigate the systematic differences between the methods. The
results demonstrate that spectrophotometric measurements can underestimate
RSC due to residual absorbance arising from neutralized DPPH and eventual
decomposition products, leading to significant differences in IC50 and antioxidant activity index (AAI) values compared with
EPR data. To overcome this limitation, three correction approaches
are proposed, improving agreement with EPR results and eliminating
statistical differences in IC50 and AAI values. These procedures
were validated using phenolic standards and further evaluated for
pigmented compounds using malvidin-3,5-diglucoside chloride and natural
jambolan (Syzygium cumini) juice, an
anthocyanin-rich sample. Although pigmentation affects high-concentration
measurements, it does not significantly influence IC50 and
AAI values after an appropriate absorbance correction. The proposed
methodologies enhance the accuracy of the spectrophotometric DPPH
assay and enable reliable comparison with EPR-based results, contributing
to the improved standardization of RSC measurements.

Introduction

Free radicals and related reactive species
have been extensively
studied due to their significant biological and pathological implications.
,
 These species contain unpaired electrons and readily react with
biomolecules, initiating chain reactions that generate additional
reactive intermediates. In humans, reactive oxygen species (ROS) and
reactive nitrogen species (RNS) are intrinsic by-products of cellular
respiration and are continuously generated under physiological conditions.
At basal concentrations, they function as signaling molecules that
regulate various cellular processes, including cell proliferation,
differentiation, and apoptosis.
−

 However, under pathological or
adverse physicochemical conditions, excessive free radical production
may overwhelm endogenous antioxidant defenses, ultimately resulting
in cellular damage. Radical-mediated damage can oxidatively modify
lipids, proteins, and DNA, processes that have been strongly associated
with aging and various human diseases.

In this context, antioxidants have attracted considerable
scientific
interest, since they can interact with free radicals, neutralizing
them before critical biomolecules are damaged. In addition to endogenous
antioxidants (enzymatic and non-enzymatic), accumulating evidence
suggests that the intake of foods and natural products with antioxidant
and other bioactive properties is associated with a reduced incidence
of several chronic diseases. Although
no single universal method can accurately assess antioxidant activity
and underlying mechanisms, screening assays have been developed to
identify potential antioxidant compounds. Several in vitro assays are widely used, including oxygen radical absorbance capacity
(ORAC), ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP), cupric-ion reducing
antioxidant capacity (CUPRAC), and radical scavenging assays based
on 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), and 2,2′-azino-bis­(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-
sulfonic acid) (ABTS).
−

 Among these, the DPPH assay is the most frequently
reported in the literature.

The use of DPPH as a method for
the antioxidant assay in biological
materials was first proposed by Blois in 1958. The method can be performed in both polar and nonpolar
organic solvents and is based on the reduction of the DPPH radical,
which results in a measurable color change. Upon reduction by an antioxidant,
the deep violet DPPH radical is converted to its non-radical form,
leading to a color change from violet to pale yellow. The stability
of the DPPH solution and its strong absorbance band around 515 nm
make it suitable for a straightforward and low-cost method. In addition
to the conventional assay format, recent studies have expanded its
applications. The method has been adapted for use in emulsions, for evaluating antioxidant interactions in binary
mixtures, and for pre-column coupling
with liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry, enabling the
identification of individual antioxidant contributions. The DPPH radical scavenging capacity (RSC) is
commonly expressed as the IC50 value, defined as the concentration
of sample required to reduce the initial DPPH radical concentration
by 50%. The IC50 is determined from a dose–response
curve plotting the percentage of DPPH scavenged against the sample
concentration. According to Scherer and Godoy, the antioxidant activity index (AAI) provides a more appropriate
basis for comparing the scavenging capacity of different samples,
given the lack of methodological standardization and the variability
in initial DPPH concentrations and sample-to-reagent ratios reported
in the literature. The AAI is calculated as the ratio of the initial
DPPH concentration to the IC50 value, thereby minimizing
the influence of assay conditions and improving comparability among
studies.

However, two important questions arise: (i) In spectrophotometric
measurements, it is observed that the RSC values reach a saturation
value below 100%. Although this phenomenon has been reported in the
literature,
−

 its potential to compromise the accuracy of the method
has not been thoroughly discussed. (ii) What happens when the method
is applied to colored natural extracts containing compounds whose
absorption band overlaps with that of DPPH. A typical example is the
presence of anthocyanins, a class of pigments responsible for the
red, purple, and blue coloration of many fruits, flowers, and vegetables.
Although interference from colored samples has been reported in the
literature, no consensus has been reached
regarding its impact on DPPH assay results. For instance, using Youden’s graphical method, concluded
that colored fruit extracts do not affect the results. In contrast,
other authors have applied the technique without addressing potential
color interference.
−

 An alternative approach is electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR),
which allows for direct access to the unpaired electron in the DPPH
radical form. EPR is a sensitive technique widely used for detecting
free radicals and other paramagnetic species.
,
 The intensity of the EPR signal is proportional to the number of
unpaired spins, making the technique suitable for antioxidant evaluation
using the DPPH assay,
,
 avoiding any interference from
sample pigmentation. However, studies comparing spectrophotometry
and EPR for the DPPH assay have reported significant differences in
the RSC values obtained by the two methods. These discrepancies remain
controversial, particularly because spectrophotometric measurements
may be affected by sample pigmentation. Some authors attribute these
differences to spectral interference caused by sample coloration,
,
 an effect not observed by Sanna et al. Overall, these studies suggest that EPR may provide a more reliable
approach for DPPH-based antioxidant evaluation.

This work aims
to explore and clarify the two questions concerning
the spectrophotometric method described above. For that, we perform
simultaneous measurements using UV-vis spectrophotometry and EPR for
the determination of the DPPH radical scavenging capacity. Standard
solutions of compounds exhibiting moderate to high antioxidant capacities
were prepared over a broad concentration range. Scavenging response
curves were constructed from the linear region to the saturation plateau,
enabling a comparison of the techniques, particularly through statistical
analysis of IC50 values. On the basis of the EPR measurements,
we propose correction procedures for the spectrophotometric data,
improving the accuracy and enhancing comparability between the two
techniques. The influence of pigmentation on the spectrophotometric
measurements was evaluated using malvidin 3,5-diglucoside chloride,
as an anthocyanin standard, and natural jambolan (Syzygium
cumini) juice, an anthocyanin-rich sample.
,
 Finally, we propose an experimental protocol to verify the existence
of pigmentation interference and, if it exists, to eliminate its effects
in order to obtain accurate results.

Materials
and Methodology

Standards and Reagents

The free
radical (DPPH) and
the high-purity standard compounds, such as ellagic acid (CAS 476-66-4), l-ascorbic acid (CAS 50-81-7), (+)-catechin (CAS 154-23-4),
gallic acid (CAS 149-91-7), caffeic acid (CAS 331-39-5), and malvidin
3,5-diglucoside chloride (CAS 16727-30-3) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.
Ethanol (95%) was used as the solvent and was obtained from Synth.

Jambolan Juice Preparation

Jambolan fruits were purchased
from a local market (Campos dos Goytacazes, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil).
The fruits were washed, sanitized, and frozen prior to pulp extraction.
The pulp was extracted by expression using a commercial fruit extractor,
as described by Mussi et al., and centrifuged
at 4,000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was collected and
stored at −20 °C as clarified juice until analyses.

Total monomeric anthocyanins content was determined by the pH-differential
assay and expressed as cyanidin-3-glucoside equivalents in μg/mL
of juice, according to Lee et al. The
total phenolic content (TPC) was determined by the Folin-Ciocalteu
assay and the results were expressed
as μg of gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per mL of juice. The
juice dry matter was determined by drying a 20.0 mL of sample aliquot
to a constant weight in an oven at 105 °C, with results expressed
as mg of solids per mL of juice. This dry matter content was used
to calculate the concentrations of the various juice dilutions used
in the antioxidant capacity measurements, expressed in mg of dried
juice/mL. The clarified juice contained (303 ± 11) μg/mL
of total anthocyanins, corresponding to (3.3 ± 0.1) μg/mg
of dried juice, and (1070 ± 50) μg GAE/mL of total phenolics,
corresponding to (11.6 ± 0.5) μg GAE/mg of dried juice.

Antioxidant Capacity Measurements

The scavenging of
DPPH in ethanolic solution (37.5 μg/mL) was used to evaluate
the antioxidant capacity of standard solutions and clarified jambolan
juice. Prior to the analysis, the juice was diluted with ultrapure
water at various concentrations. Ethanolic solutions of each standard
compound and diluted juice samples were prepared at different concentrations,
mixed with DPPH in 2 mL vials, and stirred for 40 min prior to analysis,
which was the time necessary to stabilization of the RSC, determined
based on previous measurements with different samples (data not shown).
The reaction mixtures were then measured simultaneously by EPR and
spectrophotometry, with each measurement performed in triplicate.
All reported concentrations of DPPH and samples correspond to the
final concentrations in the reaction mixture.

EPR measurements
were carried out at room temperature using a Bruker Elexsys E500 spectrometer
equipped with a high-sensitivity cavity operating at X-band (∼9.8
GHz). The spectra were recorded with a magnetic field modulation frequency
of 100 kHz, a modulation amplitude of 1 G, microwave power of 10 mW
and a scan time of 60 s. Absorbance at 515 nm was measured using a
kasuaki UV-Vis spectrophotometer (model V-1100PC).

Statistical Analyses and Data Treatment

All analyses
were performed at least in triplicate, and values were expressed as
mean ± standard deviation. The IC50 and AAI values
were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey test. The statistical analyses were performed using
R software through RStudio. Differences were considered statistically significant
at the 95% confidence level (p ≤ 0.05). EPR spectrum simulations
were carried out using the EasySpin package for MATLAB (The MathWorks,
Inc).

In addition to
ellagic acid, other phenolic standards were investigated to confirm
the residual absorbance effect in spectrophotometric measurements
and to validate the proposed correction procedure. Simultaneous RSC
measurements were performed for ascorbic acid, catechin, gallic acid,
and caffeic acids. The divergence between the EPR and spectrophotometric
curves was observed for all samples (Figure S2), and it was more pronounced for some samples than for others. The
RSC saturation values measured by the spectrophotometer ranged from
76% to 92%, meaning that the residual absorbance differs from one
sample to another. These differences are visually evident.

illustrates
that samples with completely neutralized DPPH showed different colors.
Ellagic acid and catechin turned red-brown, whereas caffeic, ascorbic,
and gallic acids turned yellow. This observation may be related to
the antioxidant mechanisms involved. The action of antioxidants can
be classified into two neutralization mechanisms: single electron
transfer (SET) and hydrogen atom transfer (HAT), in which the radical
(DPPH, in this case) can receive one electron or one proton, respectively.
Phenolic compounds can undergo both mechanisms, and DPPH in solution acquires a red-brown color for SET
and a yellow color for HAT. As discussed
for ellagic acid, the effect of the residual absorbance is not only
on the saturation values but also on the linear range of the data
(Figure S2). The IC50 and, consequently,
AAI values significantly differ between the EPR and spectrophotometric
methods for all samples. After correcting the curves (using F

corr
), the values no longer
show significant differences (), thus validating the procedure. Decomposition products
can also contribute to the residual absorbance, but as they are produced proportionally to the neutralized
radicals, the proposed corrections inherently account for them and
remain valid.

It is worth synthesizing the core aspects of the corrections
presented
above. The motivation for the proposed corrections stems from the
fact that spectrophotometry is the simplest, fastest, and most cost-effective
method for the DPPH assay, whereas EPR yields the most reliable RSC
values and is, therefore, used as the reference standard. The EPR
correction (i) has physical principles, with the absorbance being
weighted by RSC from EPR, so that its results validate the use of . The self-correction (ii)
is based on the same physical principles, but as the RSC values from
the spectrophotometer are underestimated, an iterative procedure is
necessary. Factor correction (iii) is an empirical and simpler approach.
The RSC values in the saturation range are forced to reach 100%, with
data out of the saturation proportionally increasing. In practical
terms, for the spectrophotometric-based assay, either self (ii) or
factor (iii) corrections can be used. Correction factor (iii) offers
the advantage of a single mathematical step, although both approaches
give similar results. Besides improving the accuracy of the spectrophotometric
method, data correction enables a comparison of the IC50 and AAI values obtained with this method to those reported in the
literature using EPR.

Conclusions

This study clarifies two overlooked aspects
in the literature,
namely, the underestimation of RSC values and the confounding effects
of sample pigmentation on results obtained via spectrophotometric
DPPH assays.

Briefly, our simultaneous measurements reveal a
clear distinction
between the EPR and spectrophotometric assays. EPR provides superior
accuracy as it directly measures the free radical concentration without
any residual signal. The DPPH assay by the spectrophotometric method
underestimates the RSC values because of the residual absorbance of
completely neutralized radicals and eventual decomposition products.
The validity of correcting the spectrophotometric RSC accounting for
the residual absorbance is confirmed by EPR correction (i), which
utilizes EPR data. Relying solely on the spectrophotometric method,
self-correction (ii) is proposed, which incorporates residual absorbance
correction through an iterative process. Although this approach is
effective and based on physical concepts, the iterative approach is
not straightforward. As an alternative, factor correction (iii) offers
an empirical approach through a significantly more straightforward
procedure, providing a practical tool for routine analysis. Besides
improving the accuracy of the spectrophotometric method, data correction
enables a comparison of results obtained with this method to those
reported in the literature using EPR. For further studies, chemical
analyses can provide relevant information so that the residual absorbance
allows one to infer which neutralization mechanism (SET or HAT) is
predominant in a specific assay.

Moreover, concerning pigmented
samples, we believe that the findings
address potential concerns regarding pigment interference in the spectrophotometric
method. As a case study, we showed that the pigment interference of
the pure standard malvidin 3,5-diglucoside chloride and jambolan juice
in the spectrophotometric RSC measurement occurs at high sample concentration,
when DPPH is completely neutralized, but it does not affect the IC50 and AAI values significantly. For other samples extracted
from fruits or vegetables in shades of purple, the influence is expected
to be small since there are many other antioxidant compounds. Nevertheless,
when pigment interference is suspected, before the correction (i,
ii, or (iii) discussed above, this interference can be eliminated
by subtracting the sample absorbance, and the results can be properly
analyzed.
