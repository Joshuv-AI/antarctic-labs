Strebluses A and B as tyrosinase inhibitors from the stems of Streblus ilicifolius (S. Vidal) Corn
Authors: Hai Xuan Nguyen, Dung Ngoc-Phuong Ho, Tam V.-T. Mai, Tho Huu Le, Truong Nhat Van Do, Tho H. Ho, Thinh T. Q. Le, Linh Tran, Nhan Trung Nguyen, Lam K. Huynh, Mai Thanh Thi Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tyrosinase inhibitors from natural sources are promising skin-whitening agents for treating pigment disorders. The phytochemical analysis of Streblus ilicifolius stems identified two new compounds, strebluses A (cp1) and B (cp2), with strong tyrosinase inhibitory activity. Their structures were determined using NMR, mass spectrometry, and absolute configurations by circular dichroism (CD) combined with ab initio calculations. In the tyrosinase inhibitory activity test, both compounds display more potent inhibitory activity (i.e., IC50 = 6.8 and 22.9 µM, respectively) than the positive control kojic acid (44.6 µM). Moreover, the results from combined different molecular modeling and simulation are consistent with the in vitro test and thus suggest mechanistic insights into tyrosinase inhibition. Specifically, molecular docking indicates stronger binding affinities for cp1 and cp2 (−9.97 and −9.83 kcal mol−1) than kojic acid (−5.69 kcal mol−1). The pharmacophore analysis also suggests that these two compounds have a similar inhibition mechanism as kojic acid, forming interactions with essential residues at the active site of tyrosinase, such as His259, Asn260, His263, Met280, and Ser282. Finally, the molecular dynamics (MD) simulation results consistently confirm the stability, which is due to the hydrogen bonding, of the binding complex of cp1 and cp2 with tyrosinase. Despite being isolated in bench-scale quantities (4.7 mg and 6.9 mg, respectively), the remarkable potency of these compounds provides an excellent structural foundation, serving as a valuable blueprint for future synthetic or biotechnological optimization to meet industrial cosmetic and pharmaceutical demands.

Introduction

Tyrosinase (EC 1.14.18.1), also known as polyphenol oxidase, is a binuclear copper-containing enzyme that catalyzes melanin biosynthesis. Abnormal melanogenesis can lead to pigmentation disorders such as melasma, freckles, and acne scarring, making tyrosinase an essential target for treating pigment-related conditions.1,2 Small molecules that inhibit tyrosinase activity, such as kojic acid, hydroquinone, azelaic acid, electron-rich phenols, and arbutin, have been widely studied for pharmaceutical and cosmeceutical applications.3–12 Kojic acid, together with tropolone, is commonly used as a positive control to compare the inhibitory strength of the finding inhibitors.13 Kojic acid demonstrates a competitive inhibitory effect on mushroom tyrosinase by its ability to chelate copper ions at the active site of the enzyme.14 However, these inhibitors often raise safety concerns, including toxicity and mutagenicity.15–20 In addition to their adverse side effects, those agents show insufficient penetrating power and poor efficacy in vivo.21–23 This has driven the search for safer, natural tyrosinase inhibitors with high potency and minimal side effects. In this context, recent comprehensive evaluations have highlighted natural polyphenols and stilbenes as the most effective structural classes for tyrosinase inhibition across both anti-melanogenic and anti-browning applications, though successfully translating these laboratory findings into clinical therapies remains a critical bottleneck.24

Streblus ilicifolius (S. Vidal) Corn., a member of the Moraceae family found in Vietnam, has shown promise as a source of anti-tyrosinase compounds. Previous studies on S. ilicifolius have identified several bioactive polyphenols and prenylated derivatives with strong tyrosinase inhibitory activity.25–31 In particular, eight new prenylated compounds (two coumarins and six stilbene-like derivatives)28–30 from this plant were evaluated, of which only one strebluse (strebluse C) showed potent activity (IC50 = 0.01 µM),28 while the others exhibited weak or negligible effects (IC50 > 100 µM). As part of our ongoing research on natural skin-whitening agents, we focused on the stems of S. ilicifolius to isolate and characterize compounds with potent tyrosinase inhibition.

In this study, we report on the isolation and structural characterization of two new compounds, strebluses A (cp1) and B (cp2), along with their tyrosinase inhibitory activities. Specifically, the objectives of this study are: (i) purifying and characterizing new compounds using a combination of experimental techniques, including methanol extraction, chromatography, NMR, and circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy, complemented by ab initio calculations; (ii) evaluating their inhibitory activity on tyrosinase; and (iii) elucidating their inhibition mechanism at the molecular level through molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations. These findings contribute to the discovery of safe and effective natural skin-whitening agents for pharmaceutical and cosmetic applications.

Experimental and computational details

Experimental section

General experimental procedures

Optical rotations were measured using a Jasco P-2000 digital polarimeter. CD spectra were recorded on a Chirascan CD spectropolarimeter. IR spectra were obtained with a JASCO FT/IR-6600 spectrometer in CHCl3 solution. NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance III 500 spectrometer using tetramethylsilane (TMS) as an internal standard, with chemical shifts expressed in δ values. HR-ESI-MS was carried out on a Micro O-QIITOF mass spectrometer. UV absorbance measurements were performed using a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer. Column chromatography used Silica gel 60 (40–63 µm, Scharlau) and LiChroprep® RP-18 (40–63 µm, Merck KGaA). Analytical and preparative TLC were performed on Kieselgel 60F254 or RP-18F254 plates (Merck KGaA). Mushroom tyrosinase (EC 1.14.18.1) (3933 U mL−1) and l-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine (L-DOPA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Kojic acid and DMSO were purchased from Merck. All other chemicals used were of the highest available grade.

Plant material

The stems of S. ilicifolius, collected at Hoai Nhon District, Binh Dinh Province, Vietnam, were identified. A voucher sample of the stems (MCE0052) was deposited at the Department of Medicinal Chemistry, Faculty of Chemistry, VNUHCM-University of Science, Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam.

Extraction and isolation

The powdered stems of S. ilicifolius were extracted using a Soxhlet apparatus with n-hexane, EtOAc, and MeOH, yielding n-hexane (64.8 g), EtOAc (117.2 g), and MeOH (378.0 g) extracts. The EtOAc-soluble extract (115.0 g) was fractionated on a silica gel column using MeOH : CHCl3 mixtures (v/v, 0 : 100 to 100 : 0) as the eluent, producing 18 fractions (fr.1–fr.18).

Fraction fr.15 (20.1 g) was further purified using silica gel chromatography with a MeOH : CHCl3 gradient system (v/v, 0 : 100 → 80 : 20), yielding 12 subfractions (fr.15-1 to fr.15-12a).

Tyrosinase inhibitory assay

The tyrosinase inhibitory activity was evaluated as described by Arung et al.32 Mushroom tyrosinase (Agaricus bisporus), a model enzyme with high similarity to human tyrosinase, was used.13,33,34 Test samples were dissolved in DMSO at concentrations of 100–1 µg mL−1 (or µM for pure compounds). The assay mixture consisted of 1450 µL of the test solution in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.8), 50 µL of enzyme solution (15 U mL−1), and 500 µL of substrate solution (1.5 mM L-DOPA in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 6.8). After preincubation at room temperature for 30 minutes, the reaction was initiated by adding the substrate solution and incubated for 7 minutes. Absorbance was measured at 475 nm using a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer. Kojic acid was used as positive control. Tyrosinase inhibitory activity was calculated as: inhibitory activity (%) = (1 − B/A) × 100, where A and B are the enzyme activities without and with the test sample, respectively. IC50 values were determined from the mean values of four independent experiments.

Computational details

Circular dichroism (CD)

In general, the CD calculations comprise two steps: first, the conformational analysis of the compounds to attain the possible conformer(s), and second, the UV/CD TD-DFT computation of each conformer(s). For the first step, the hybrid DFT with Becke's three-parameter hybrid method and the Lee–Yang–Parr exchange–correlation functional theory, B3LYP,35,36 accompanied with the Pople basis set, 6-31G(d),37 was used to obtain the optimized geometries and vibrational frequencies for each conformer. The resulting conformers were then used for the TD-DFT calculations of the UV/CD by using the CAM-B3LYP/6-311+G(d,p) level of theory (denoted as CAM-B3LYP/6-311+G(d,p)//B3LYP/6-31G(d)). Note that CAM-B3LYP functional38 was found to give good results in UV/CD calculations.39 The polarizable continuum model was used to take into account the solvent effects40,41 in all electronic structure calculations. All DFT calculations were calculated using the Gaussian 16 package.42

Molecular docking

In this study, the enantiomers of the two new compounds were examined against tyrosinase in silico via molecular docking to evaluate their binding affinities. The docking experiments were performed against the 3D structure of the common button mushroom Agaricus bisporus tyrosinase protein in complex with the inhibitor tropolone, which was solved by X-ray diffraction at 2.78 Å and published on the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB entry 2Y9X).43 In this complex, tropolone (another tyrosinase inhibitor), together with kojic acid and l-mimosine, which is often used as the positive control in studies comparing the inhibitory strength of the finding inhibitors, was found at the entrance of the active site of mushroom tyrosinase and exhibited mixed inhibition mode similar to kojic acid.13,44,45

The molecular docking experiment is carried out using AutoDock Vina version 1.2.3.,46 one of the fastest and most widely used open-source programs for molecular docking,47 to predict the binding affinity and molecular interactions of ligands on the target protein receptor. The docking process involves two main steps, which include (i) conformation search, followed by (ii) ranking the generated conformation via a scoring function.48 A Monte Carlo-based searching algorithm is employed to randomly generate the conformation of ligands for exploring the energy landscape.46 After the conformation search is done, a scoring function is used to score and rank the generated conformations and poses of the ligand.49 The implemented scoring function takes into account hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, hydrophobic interactions, van der Waals forces, a pairwise-additive desolvation term based on partial charges, and a simple conformational entropy penalty.46

For molecular docking, the mushroom tyrosinase structure was downloaded from the RCSB PDB entry 2Y9X, and prepared for docking using AutoDock Tools.50 All water, ions, and ligands co-crystallized in the complex were removed, only polar hydrogens were added, and an equally spread Kollman partial charge was assigned to the protein. As rigid docking was used in this experiment, the protein structure was treated as a rigid body, whereas the ligands were allowed to be flexible. For the ligands, the 3D structures of 16 isomers of compound cp1, the determined structure of compound cp2 (i.e., C-2(S), C-2′(S), C-3′(S), C-4′(S), and C-1″(S)), and the control kojic acid were built and geometrically optimized using B3LYP/6-31G(d, p) level of theory implemented in the Gaussian 09 package42 and eventually saved as PDB format. During the conversion step of the ligands, Gasteiger charges were added, and all rotatable bonds were treated flexibly to represent the flexibility of the ligand. The prepared PDB files of the protein and the ligands were converted to the PDBQT format and ready to be used for docking with Autodock Vina. In this experiment, the coordinates of the tropolone inhibitor were used to locate the center of the docking grid box having the dimensions of 25 × 25 × 25 (Å) and the resolution spacing of 1 Å (illustrated Fig. 19SA). To validate the reliability of the docking experiments, the crystal structure of urease with its co-crystallized ligand, in this case, tropolone, was re-docked. Root–mean–square deviation (RMSD) values were calculated between the docked and the original conformation of the known inhibitor tropolone in the complex. If the RMSD of the docked pose is less than 2.5 Å from the experimentally observed conformation, the docking experiment can be considered successful.51 The docking results were sorted from best to worst based on their docking energy values. Besides the binding affinity calculated from AutoDock Vina, a pharmacophore, which is an abstract description of the interactions of the docked compounds at the binding site, was generated by BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer52 for further analyses.

Molecular dynamics simulations

The most stable conformations of cp1 and cp2 in tyrosinase, obtained through molecular docking, were subjected to subsequent molecular dynamics simulations to investigate the stability of ligand binding. The CHARMM36 force field53 was assigned to tyrosinase. The automation scheme54 for CGenFF55 was used to assign atom types, partial charges, and structural parameters to the ligands. All force field assignment was performed using CHARMM-GUI.56,57 Each tyrosinase–ligand complex was solvated with the TIP3P water model58 in an 11 × 11 × 11 nm cubic box to ensure complete coverage of the complex. An additional 23 sodium ions were added to neutralize the net charge of the system. Then, a 0.15 M concentration of sodium chloride (110 sodium and 110 chloride ions) was added to approximate physiological ionic strength. The simulations underwent a three-stage equilibration process employing backbone and sidechain restraints of 400 kJ mol−1 and 40 kJ mol−1, respectively, and a timestep of 1 fs. First, all systems initially underwent energy minimization using the steepest descent algorithm until the maximum force was less than 1000 kJ mol−1 nm−1. Second, all systems were equilibrated in the NVT ensemble using a stochastic velocity rescaling (v-rescale) thermostat59 at 37 °C (310.15 K) for 125 ps. Third, all systems were equilibrated in the NPT ensemble using a stochastic cell rescaling (C-rescale) barostat60 at 1 bar for 125 ps, continuing with the same thermostat from the NVT equilibration. Ultimately, the production run was performed for 100 ns using the same thermostat and barostat as in the NPT equilibration, with the backbone and sidechain restraints turned off and a timestep of 2 fs. The LINCS algorithm61 was utilized to impose constraints on all hydrogen bonds. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the smooth particle mesh Ewald method.62 The van der Waals interactions were treated using a plain cutoff63 at 1.2 nm, in conjugation with a force switch to smoothly reduce the interaction strength starting from 1.0 nm. All simulations described above were carried out using GROMACS 2019.6 (ref. 64) during 100 ns.

Results and discussion

Compound cp1 was isolated as a yellow powder, and its HR-ESI-MS shows the molecular formula to be C35H28O10 (m/z [M + H]+ 609.1755). The IR spectrum displays absorption bands of hydroxyl (3351 cm−1), ketone (1631 cm−1), and phenyl (1454 cm−1) groups. The 1H- and 13C-NMR exhibited the presence of signal resonances for 39 carbons consisting of a chelated hydroxyl group [δH 12.15 (s, HO–C-5)] and the corresponding chelated carbonyl carbon [δC 197.6 (C-4)], five free hydroxy groups [δH 9.53 (brs, HO–C-7), 8.70 (brs, HO–C-14), 8.23 (brs, HO–C-7′), 8.48 (brs, HO–C-12′), and 8.43 (brs, HO–C-14′)], a tertiary carbon bearing two oxygen atoms [δC 103.1 (C-2′)], an oxymethine [δH 5.74 (dd, J = 13.3 & 3.0, H-2); δC 76.0 (C-2)], two methines [δH 3.31 (dd, J = 11.5 & 5.7, H-3′) and 2.99 (ddd, J = 11.5 & 11.5 & 5.5, H-4′); δC 37.1 (C-3′) and 28.3 (C-4′)], a methylene [δH 2.75 (dd, J = 17.1 & 3.0, H-3a) and 3.35 (dd, J = 17.1 & 13.3, H-3b); δC 42.5 (C-3)], a 1,2,3,5-tetrasubstituted benzene ring [δH 5.90 (d, J = 2.0, H-6) and 5.81 (d, J = 2.0, H-8); δC 165.3 (C-5), 96.7 (C-6), 167.3 (C-7), 95.8 (C-8), 164.7 (C-9), and 103.1 (C-10)], a 1,2,3,4-tetrasubstituted benzene ring [δH 6.50 (d, J = 8.4, H-15) and 7.22 (d, J = 8.4, H-16); δC 117.8 (C-11), 151.9 (C-12), 113.2 (C-13), 158.1 (C-14), 109.3 (C-15), and 127.8 (C-16)], a 1,2,4-trisubstituted benzene ring [δH 7.11 (d, J = 8.4, H-5′), 6.45 (dd, J = 8.4 & 2.4, H-6′) and 6.28 (d, J = 2.4, H-8′); δC 127.8 (C-5′), 109.9 (C-6′), 157.7 (C-7′), 104.0 (C-8′), 153.2 (C-9′), and 117.5 (C-10′)], another 1,2,4-trisubstituted benzene ring [δH 6.43 (d, J = 2.2, H-13′), 6.27 (dd, J = 8.6 & 2.2, H-15′) and 7.17 (d, J = 8.6, H-16′); δC 117.0 (C-11′), 157.4 (C-12′), 104.8 (C-13′), 160.0 (C-14′), 107.2 (C-15′), and 130.2 (C-16′)], so it is suggested that the compound comprises a flavanone, and a flavane. In the HMBC spectrum, the proton of the chelated hydroxy group δH 12.15 correlates with an O-bearing aromatic carbon δC 165.3 (C-5), a substituted aromatic carbon δC 103.1 (C-10), and an aromatic methine carbon δC 96.7 (C-6), indicating that the hydroxy group attached to C-5 together with two free hydroxy groups at C-7 and C-14 of the flavanone unit (from C-1 to C-16) like a steppogenin. Meanwhile, the flavane unit (from C-1′ to C-16′) is determined to be as a 7,2′,4′-trihroxyflavane because of three free hydroxy groups at C-7′, C-12′, and C-14′ based on the HMBC correlations in Fig. 1. Furthermore, these 1D-NMR data also reveal the appearance of resonances for five carbons corresponding to derivative of a prenyl group [δH 3.48 (dd, J = 5.7 & 5.7, H-1″), 6.45 (d, J = 5.7, H-2″), 2.01 (dd, J = 17.4 & 11.5, H-4″a), 2.70 (dd, J = 17.4 & 5.5, H-4″b), and 1.77 (s, H(Me)-5″); δC 35.0 (C-1″), 123.0 (C-2″), 133.7 (C-3″), 36.3 (C-4″), and 23.9 (C-5″)]. The methylene protons (H-4″) show HMBC cross-peaks with C-3′, C-4′, and C-10′ of the flavane unit; the methylene group thus bonded to C-4′. Besides, the methine proton (H-1″) showed HMBC correlations to C-3′ and C-4′ of the flavane unit as well as to C-12, C-13, and C-14 of the flavanone unit, forming a methylcyclohexene ring which is fused to the flavane unit at C-3′ and C-4′. This is confirmed by 1H–1H COSY spectrum which demonstrates a partial structure C(2″)H–C(1″)H–C(3′)H–C(4′)H–C(4″)H2 (Fig. 1) and a HMBC correlation of H-3′ to C-13. Finally, a dihydropyran ring is established by C-12 linkage of C-2′ through an oxygen bridge and the deshielded aromatic carbon with C-12 being O-bearing. The relative stereochemistry of cp1 was determined by the Nuclear Overhauser Effect Spectroscopy (NOESY) spectrum. The data display the Nuclear Overhauser Effect (NOE) correlations between H-3b/H-16, H-2/H-3a/H-16′, H-4″b/H-5′, and H-4″a/H-4′/H-3′/H-1″ (Fig. 2). The compound is, therefore, streblus A.

The tyrosinase inhibitory activities of compounds cp1, cp2, and the positive control (kojic acid) were evaluated across four concentrations (10–100 µM). Compound cp1 exhibited an inhibition rate exceeding 50% at 10 µM, while cp2 and kojic acid showed less than 50% inhibition at the same concentration. Further testing of cp1 at lower concentrations (1–10 µM) revealed it to be the most active compound, with an IC50 value of 6.8 µM. In comparison, cp2 showed moderate activity with an IC50 value of 22.9 µM. Both compounds demonstrated significantly stronger inhibitory activity than kojic acid, which had an IC50 value of 44.6 µM. These results suggest that streblus A (cp1) is a potent natural tyrosinase inhibitor.

Circular dichroism (CD)

To establish a solid structural foundation for our mechanistic analysis, quantum chemical calculations were employed to determine the absolute configurations of the isolated compounds, complementing our experimental data. Fig. S19 compares the calculated CD spectra of all 32 possible enantiomeric structures (25 = 32) of compound cp1, which contains five chiral centers, with the corresponding experimental spectrum. The shorthand notations for all optical isomers are listed in Table S1. Among these, 16 enantiomers (i.e., 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 18, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 28, 29, and 31) show calculated CD profiles consistent with the experimental data, characterized by a positive Cotton effect (CE) near 260 nm and a negative CE around 300 nm (Fig. S19a), although the corresponding experimental positive and negative Cotton effects are observed at approximately 220 nm and 280 nm, respectively. In contrast, the remaining 16 enantiomers (i.e., 6–17, 19, 27, 30, and 32) generally exhibit a negative CE near 260 nm (Fig. 3b), except for enantiomers 15 and 19. Notably, the CD response is particularly sensitive to the configuration at the C-2 stereocenter. Enantiomers with a C-2R configuration consistently display a positive CE around 260 nm (Fig. S19a), whereas those with C-2(S) configuration show a negative CE in this region (Fig. S19b), again excluding enantiomers 15 and 19. Based on these observations, the absolute configuration at C-2 of compound cp1 can be confidently assigned as (R). However, the configurations of the remaining stereocenters (C-2′, C-3′, C-4′, and C-1″) cannot be unambiguously determined from the present CD calculations.

Chemical structures of compounds cp1 (streblus A) and cp2 (Streblus B).

For compound cp2, TD-DFT calculations (Fig. S20) indicate that only seven of the 32 possible enantiomers reproduce the experimental CD spectrum. Among these candidates, enantiomer 19 – with the configuration C-2(S), C-2′(S), C-3′(S), C-4′(S), and C-1″(S) – exhibits the closest agreement with the experimental data in both spectral shape and relative intensity. The remaining enantiomers show noticeable discrepancies in either sign, band position, or magnitude of the Cotton effects. On this basis, the absolute configuration of cp2 is unambiguously assigned as C-2(S), C-2′(S), C-3′(S), C-4′(S), and C-1″(S).

Molecular docking and structure–activity relationships (SAR)

The docking model was validated with an RMSD of 0.04 Å between the docked pose and the experimental structure of the co-crystallized ligand, confirming the accuracy of the method (Fig. S21). The binding affinities and pharmacophore analyses for compounds cp1 and cp2 were evaluated against Agaricus bisporus tyrosinase and compared with the control compound, kojic acid. Docking scores revealed that cp1 and cp2 exhibited significantly stronger binding affinities than kojic acid (−10.0 and −9.8 kcal mol−1, respectively, compared to −5.7 kcal mol−1 for kojic acid). Among cp1's enantiomers, isomers cp1_24 and cp1_29 showed the highest binding affinities (−10.0 kcal mol−1), making them promising candidates for further development as tyrosinase inhibitors.

Visualization of the binding sites showed that cp1 and cp2 occupy the same active site cavity as tropolone (a co-crystallized inhibitor) and kojic acid (Fig. S22), suggesting a shared competitive inhibitory mechanism. A comprehensive, atomistic breakdown of the Structure–Activity Relationships (SAR) reveals how the unique chemical features of strebluses A and B drive this potent inhibition:

(i) Biflavanone dimerization and core scaffold alignment: the unique, rigid dimeric framework of these specific prenylated biflavanones provides an optimal spatial configuration. Unlike smaller monomeric phenolics, this expanded core scaffold (combining 7,2′,4′-trihydroxyflavane and 3′-prenylsteppogenin) occupies the extended active site cavity of tyrosinase perfectly, restricting the enzyme's conformational flexibility and forming enhanced π interactions within the pocket. Unlike single flavonoids, the dimeric structure of these biflavanones significantly blocks substrate access, aligning with prior studies.65,66

(ii) Hydroxyl (–OH) distribution and electrostatic anchoring: the specific geometric positioning of the peripheral hydroxyl groups on the Streblus core acts as a primary driver of activity. This exact spatial arrangement enables the formation of a highly targeted, low-energy hydrogen-bonding network with key catalytic pocket residues. Specifically, cp1 forms crucial hydrogen bonding with residue Ser282, while cp2 forms a targeted hydrogen bond with Asn260, effectively locking the compounds into the binding pocket (Fig. 4).

Molecular dynamics simulations

This tightly bound network is further corroborated by our molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, which evaluate the time-resolved stability of these interactions. The simulation trajectories confirm that the binding complexes for both cp1 and cp2 remain highly stable over time, minimally affecting the structural compactness of the enzyme complex.

Throughout the 100 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation, the binding complexes of both compound cp1 and cp2 showed great stability, confirmed by the calculated root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root–mean–square fluctuation (RMSF), the radius of gyration (Rg) and hydrogen bond analysis (Fig. 5a–c). The RMSD of both compounds is around 2.5 Å, indicating the stable conformation during the simulation. Generally, the RMSD values show that cp1 is more stable than the compound cp2. In addition, the Rg value with an average of 5.5 nm indicates that both compounds adopt a very stable compact structure, in which compound cp1 ensures significant compactness and rigidity compared to compound cp2. This finding highly indicates that structures of binding complexes of cp1 and cp2 are minimally affected during simulation.

Stability analysis of the cp1–tyrosinase and cp2–tyrosinase binding complexes during the 100-ns molecular dynamics simulation confirmed that both systems remained stable, as evidenced by RMSD values of approximately 2.5 Å, RMSF values of 0.2–2.0 Å, and a consistent radius of gyration (5.5 nm). Overall, cp1 exhibited greater conformational stability and rigidity than cp2. The number of hydrogen bonds fluctuated between 1 and 5 throughout the simulation, further supporting the sustained stability of both complexes.

Conclusions

The study identified two prenylated biflavanones, strebluses A (cp1) and B (cp2), from Streblus ilicifolius, a plant species in Vietnam. Their absolute configurations were determined using 1D/2D NMR, mass spectrometry, and ECD spectroscopy, supported by ab initio calculations. These compounds exhibited significantly stronger tyrosinase inhibitory activity (IC50 = 6.8 and 22.9 µM) than kojic acid (44.6 µM), with molecular docking and pharmacophore analysis revealing a substrate-competitive mechanism involving key active site residues of tyrosinase. Molecular dynamics simulations further confirmed the stability of their binding complexes due to hydrogen bonding. This study highlights the potential of these biflavanones as bioinspired skin-whitening agents and demonstrates the complementary role of theoretical and computational methods in elucidating their structure and biological activity.

In conclusion, while these findings establish the potent in vitro tyrosinase inhibitory activity and structural con`urations of the newly discovered compounds cp1 and cp2, further cell-based efficacy assays, comprehensive cytotoxicity evaluations, and in vivo studies are warranted. These investigations represent the next critical phase of our research pipeline to fully evaluate the safety, cell viability profiles, and translational potential of these biflavanones as therapeutic or cosmetic agents.
