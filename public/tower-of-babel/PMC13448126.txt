A Commentary on “Rates of Subsequent Lumbar Surgery Following Spinal Fusion in Patients With Parkinson Disease: A Propensity-Score Matched Nationwide Cohort Analysis”
Authors: Yusuke Nishimura
Published: 2026

Parkinson disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative condition with an estimated prevalence of 0.3% (higher in the population older than 60 years) [1]. The symptoms of PD have minimal impact on the everyday lives of some patients, whereas others experience severe functional limitations. The life expectancy of PD patients continues to improve because of progression of medical treatment options for PD [2]. Therefore, there are increased opportunities for PD patients to have elective spinal surgeries. PD brings unique challenges for spine surgeons associated with characteristics of PD, including progressive postural instability, paraspinal muscle dysfunction, osteoporosis and sagittal imbalance [3]. These typical PD symptoms substantially place more mechanical stress on the lumbar spine than the general population without PD. Consequently, PD patients experience a higher rate of postoperative surgical complications, longer hospital stays, higher hospital costs, and an overall increase in mortality [4]. Although numerous single-center studies have reported these unfavorable outcomes, high-quality population-based evidence has remained limited, as described by the authors. In this context, the present nationwide propensity-score matched cohort study represents an important contribution to the growing literature evaluating outcomes of spinal surgeries in patients with PD.

The authors [5] analyzed a nationwide Korean insurance database including 2,287 patients with PD who underwent lumbar fusion and compared them with 6,861 matched controls without PD. Their principal finding—individuals with PD have a 1.6 times higher risk of reoperation compared with those without PD during a 7-year follow-up—provides significant epidemiological evidence supporting previous observations that PD has an elevated risk of postoperative surgical complications and reoperation after spinal instrumented fusion [6]. The large sample size, nationwide coverage, and propensity-score matching reduced important baseline differences between the study groups and allowed a more balanced comparison of outcomes, although several clinically relevant variables, including diabetes mellitus, dementia, cerebrovascular disease, and osteoporosis were not fully balanced. Watanabe et al. [3] reviewed degenerative spine surgery in patients with PD and concluded that mechanical complications remain the predominant cause of postoperative failure, whereas appropriately selected patients can still achieve meaningful improvements in pain and neurological function. Furthermore, the recent systematic review and metaanalysis by Alissa et al. [6] demonstrated significantly increased rates of postoperative complications, revision surgery, and medical adverse events following instrumented spinal fusion in PD compared with non-PD patients. The consistency between these independent investigations and the present nationwide cohort supports the authors’ conclusion that these results highlight the need for developing a personalized approach for management of lumbar instrumented fusion in patients with PD and underscores the importance of interdisciplinary care and careful surgical planning. In patients with PD undergoing spinal surgery, optimal perioperative management of PD is as important as the surgical procedure itself. Therefore, multidisciplinary management involving spine surgeons, neurologists, and rehabilitation specialists may help reduce postoperative complications. Careful patient selection, surgical planning and restoration of sagittal alignment remain essential particularly in individuals with advanced comorbidities or severe sagittal imbalance. Optimization of bone health before surgery may also reduce implant-related complications.

Despite these important strengths, several limitations should be recognized. Administrative databases inevitably lack critical information including detailed clinical and radiographic data which allow us to analyze the mechanism of surgical complications. These unavailable variables may contribute to postoperative fusion failure. These important data may explain a difference in the observed surgical outcomes between patients with and without PD [7]. Another limitation is that the specific type of reoperation could not be determined from the database, such as evacuation of postoperative hematoma, surgical site infection, revision surgery for pseudarthrosis or implant failure from the index surgery, or another surgery for newly developed lumbar degeneration. Furthermore, whether or not osteoporosis treatment, restoration of global sagittal alignment, minimally invasive surgical techniques could affect revision rates remains to be seen in the present study. Another methodological consideration is the exclusion of patients who died during follow-up. Because mortality is generally higher among patients with PD, death may act as a competing event for subsequent lumbar surgery. Consequently, the reported reoperation rate should be interpreted with caution, as the direction and magnitude of this potential bias cannot be determined from the available data. Future studies incorporating competing-risk analyses are needed to clarify the mechanisms responsible for reoperation and to determine the impact of optimized PD management and advanced minimally invasive surgical techniques on surgical outcomes.

In conclusion, this study represents an important large-scale population-based contribution to the current evidence regarding lumbar fusion outcomes in patients with PD. Its findings reinforce accumulating evidence that PD significantly increases the risk of subsequent lumbar surgery and underscore the importance of careful patient selection and comprehensive multidisciplinary perioperative care. Future prospective multicenter studies incorporating disease severity, a variety of surgical techniques, spinopelvic parameters, bone quality, and patient-reported outcomes will be essential for establishing evidence-based surgical strategies for this increasingly prevalent and surgically challenging patient population.
