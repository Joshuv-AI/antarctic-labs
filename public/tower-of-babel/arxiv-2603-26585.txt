Abstract
Let K K be a compact, centrally-symmetric, strictly-convex region in ℝ 3 {\mathbb{R}}^{3} , which is a semi-algebraic set of constant complexity, i.e. the unit ball of a corresponding metric, denoted as ∥ ⋅ ∥ K \|\cdot\|_{K} . Let 𝒦 \mathscr{K} be a set of n n homothetic copies of K K . This paper contains two main sets of results: (i) For a storage parameter s ∈ [ n , n 3 ] s\in[n,n^{3}] , 𝒦 \mathscr{K} can be preprocessed in O ∗ ​ ( s ) O^{*}(s) expected time into a data structure of size O ∗ ​ ( s ) O^{*}(s) , so that for a query homothet K 0 K_{0} of K K , an intersection-detection query (determine whether K 0 K_{0} intersects any member of 𝒦 \mathscr{K} , and if so, report such a member) or a nearest-neighbor query (return the member of 𝒦 \mathscr{K} whose ∥ ⋅ ∥ K \|\cdot\|_{K} -distance from K 0 K_{0} is smallest) can be answered in O ∗ ​ ( n / s 1 / 3 ) O^{*}(n/s^{1/3}) time; all k k homothets of 𝒦 \mathscr{K} intersecting K 0 K_{0} can be reported in additional O ⁡ ( k ) O(k) time. In addition, the data structure supports insertions/deletions in O ∗ ​ ( s / n ) O^{*}(s/n) amortized expected time per operation. Here the O ∗ ​ ( ⋅ ) O^{*}(\cdot) notation hides factors of the form n ε n^{\varepsilon} , where ε > 0 {\varepsilon}>0 is an arbitrarily small constant, and the constant of proportionality depends on ε {\varepsilon} . (ii) Let 𝒢 ⁡ ( 𝒦 ) \mathscr{G}(\mathscr{K}) denote the intersection graph of 𝒦 \mathscr{K} . Using the above data structure, breadth-first or depth-first search on 𝒢 ⁡ ( 𝒦 ) \mathscr{G}(\mathscr{K}) can be performed in O ∗ ​ ( n 3 / 2 ) O^{*}(n^{3/2}) expected time. Combining this result with the so-called shrink-and-bifurcate technique, the reverse-shortest-path problem in a suitably defined proximity graph of 𝒦 \mathscr{K} can be solved in O ∗ ​ ( n 62 / 39 ) O^{*}(n^{62/39}) expected time. Dijkstra’s shortest-path algorithm, as well as Prim’s MST algorithm, on a ∥ ⋅ ∥ K \|\cdot\|_{K} -proximity graph on n n points in ℝ 3 {\mathbb{R}}^{3} , with edges weighted by ∥ ⋅ ∥ K \|\cdot\|_{K} , can also be performed in O ∗ ​ ( n 3 / 2 ) O^{*}(n^{3/2}) time.

Introduction

Problem statement.

Let
K
be a compact, centrally-symmetric, strictly-convex region in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, which
is a semi-algebraic set of constant complexity.
1
1

1

Roughly speaking, a semi-algebraic set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is the set of points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
that
satisfy a Boolean formula over a set of polynomial inequalities; the complexity of a semi-algebraic
set is the number of polynomials defining the set and their maximum degree.
See
[
13
]
for formal definitions of a semi-algebraic set and its complexity.

K
is the unit ball of a norm

\|\cdot\|_{K}
, and we denote its metric as
{\rm dist}_{K}
.

A
homothet
(or
homothetic copy
) of
K
is a scaled and translated copy of
K
, expressed as

K(c,\rho):=\rho K+c
, for
c\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and
\rho\geq 0
;
c
is the
center

of
K(c,\rho)
and
\rho
is its
size
. We represent
K(c,\rho)
as the point

(c,\rho)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
. Let
{\mathbb{K}}
be the set of all homothets of

K
, represented as the halfspace
x_{4}\geq 0
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
.
The
K
-distance

{\rm dist}_{K}(u,v)=\|u-v\|_{K}
between points
u,v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}

is the minimum value of
\rho
for which

v\in K(u,\rho)
(or, equivalently,
u\in K(v,\rho)
). Since
K
is strictly convex, the
triangle inequality
{\rm dist}_{K}(u,v)\leq{\rm dist}_{K}(u,w)+{\rm dist}_{K}(w,v)
is sharp unless
w
lies
on the segment
uv
.
The
K
-distance of a point
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
from a homothet
K(c,\rho)
is defined as

f_{c,\rho}(x)={\rm dist}_{K}(c,x)-\rho
,
and the
K
distance of another homothet
K(c^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
from
K(c,\rho)
is
f_{c,\rho}(c^{\prime})-\rho^{\prime}={\rm dist}_{K}(c,c^{\prime})-\rho-\rho^{\prime}
.
Since
K
is a constant-complexity semi-algebraic set,
f_{c,\rho}
is
a semi-algebraic function of constant complexity.
The homothets
K(c,\rho)
and
K(c^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
intersect if the distance between them is non-positive,
i.e.,
{\rm dist}_{K}(c,c^{\prime})\leq\rho+\rho^{\prime}
, or equivalently, the point
(c^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
lies
above the graph of
f_{c,\rho}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
, or vice-versa.

Let
\mathscr{K}=\{K_{1},\ldots,K_{n}\}\subset{\mathbb{K}}
be a set of
n
homothets of
K
, where

K_{i}=K(c_{i},\rho_{i})
. In this paper we study two classes of proximity problems related to
\mathscr{K}
.

Proximity queries.

We wish to preprocess
\mathscr{K}
into a data structure so that
various proximity queries for a query homothet
K_{0}
, such as intersection-detection
(determine whether
K_{0}
intersects any homothet of
\mathscr{K}
; if the answer is yes, return
one such intersecting homothet of
\mathscr{K}
),
intersection reporting (report all homothets of
\mathscr{K}
that intersect
K_{0}
),
and nearest-neighbor queries (report the homothet of
\mathscr{K}
at the
minimum
K
-distance from
K_{0}
),
can be performed efficiently. In addition, the data structure should
support insertions and deletions of homothets of
K
.

Searching in the intersection graph and related problems.

Let
\mathscr{G}(\mathscr{K})
denote the intersection graph
of
\mathscr{K}
, whose vertices are the homothets of
\mathscr{K}
and
(K_{i},K_{j})
is an edge if

K_{i}\cap K_{j}\neq\emptyset
. Similarly, for a threshold parameter
r_{0}
, the

r_{0}
-proximity graph

\mathscr{G}_{r_{0}}(\mathscr{K})
of
\mathscr{K}
consists of those edges
(K_{i},K_{j})

for which
{\rm dist}_{K}(K_{i},K_{j})\leq r_{0}
.
We study efficient implementation of graph-search problems on
\mathscr{G}(\mathscr{K})
,
like BFS or DFS.
We also study the
reverse shortest path
(RSP) problem on
\mathscr{K}
:
given two elements
K_{i},K_{j}\in\mathscr{K}
and a parameter
1\leq k<n
, compute
the smallest value
r^{*}
for which
\mathscr{G}_{r^{*}}(\mathscr{K})

contains a path from
K_{i}
to
K_{j}
with at most
k
edges.

Related work.

There is extensive work on nearest-neighbor (NN) searching in many different fields including
computational geometry, database systems, and machine learning. We refer the readers to
various survey papers
[
7
,
12
,
23
]
for a general overview of known results on this topic.
Here we briefly mention a few results that are most closely related to the problems studied
in this paper. Agarwal and Matoušek
[
11
]
presented a dynamic NN data structure for
a point set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, under the Euclidean metric, that answers a query in
O^{*}(n/s^{\phi})

time, where
\phi=1-\tfrac{1}{\lceil d/2\rceil}
and
s\in[n,n^{\lceil d/2\rceil}]
is a storage parameter,
using
O^{*}(s)
space and preprocessing; the (amortized) update time for insertions/deletions of
points is
O^{*}(s/n)
. The bounds were slightly improved –
n^{\varepsilon}
factors were replaced by

\log^{O(1)}n
factors – in
[
18
,
28
]
.
Kaplan
et al.

[
28
]
presented a dynamic NN data structure for a set
S
of points in

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
under fairly general distance functions. Its performance depends on the
complexity of the Voronoi diagram of
S
under that distance function. For the Minkowski
metric induced by a centrally-symmetric convex region, which is a semi-algebraic set of
constant complexity, their data structure can answer an NN query in
O(\log^{2}n)
time, and handle
insertion and deletion of a point in
O(\beta(n)\log^{5}n)
and
O(\beta(n)\log^{9}n)

amortized expected time, respectively, where
\beta(n)
, a variant of inverse Ackermann’s function, is
an extremely slowly growing function; see also
[
33
]
for slightly improved bounds.
These data structures, however, do not extend to
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.

The problem of NN searching under general distance functions
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
is much less understood.
By reducing NN queries to ball-intersection queries
[
10
]
and
using semi-algebraic range-searching data structures
[
3
,
35
,
2
]
, an NN query
amid the set
\mathscr{K}
of
n
homothets in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, as above, can be answered in
O^{*}(1)
time
using
O^{*}(n^{4})
space and preprocessing, or in
O^{*}(n^{3/4})
time using
O(n)
space and preprocessing.
Using recent standard techniques (see, e.g., the Appendix in
[
2
]
),
one can obtain a space/query-time trade-off, as well as handle insertions/deletions of objects.
Using a recent result by Agarwal
et al.

[
5
]
on the vertical decomposition of the lower
envelope of trivariate functions, an NN query amid
\mathscr{K}
can be answered in
O(\log^{2}n)
time
using only
O^{*}(n^{3})
space, but this data structure does not support efficient deletions
(insertions can be handled in a standard manner, using the dynamization technique
of Bentley and Saxe
[
15
]
). Furthermore, it was not known whether an NN query
amid
\mathscr{K}
can be answered in
O^{*}(n^{2/3})
time using
O^{*}(n)
space.

time for

[
8

9

29

.

The reverse shortest path (RSP) problem for unit disks was studied by

Our results.

Our main result is a dynamic data structure for answering intersection
and NN queries with a homothet of
{\mathbb{K}}
amid the set
\mathscr{K}
of homothets, as stated in the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
K
be a compact, centrally-symmetric strictly convex semi-algebraic set
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
of constant complexity. Let
\mathscr{K}
be a set of
n
homothetic
copies of
K
. For a storage parameter
s\in[n,n^{3}]
,
\mathscr{K}
can be maintained in a dynamic data
structure with
O^{*}(s)
storage, that can answer an intersection-detection or NN-query (under the
K
-distance)
with a homothet
K_{0}
of
K

in
O^{*}(n/s^{1/3})
time and that supports insertions/deletions in
O^{*}(s/n)

amortized expected time. It can report all
k
objects of
\mathscr{K}
intersecting
K_{0}
in additional
O(k)
time.

Plugging Theorem
1.1
into Eppstein’s technique
[
25
]
, or its enhancement by Chan
[
19
]
,
for maintaining bichromatic closest pairs, using
s=n^{3/2}
, we obtain:

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
K
be a compact, centrally-symmetric strictly-convex semi-algebraic set
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
of constant complexity. Let
\mathscr{K},\mathscr{K}^{\prime}
be two sets of homothets of
K

of combined size
n
. The
K
-closest pair between
\mathscr{K}
and
\mathscr{K}^{\prime}
,
under insertions/deletions of homothets (in
\mathscr{K}
and
\mathscr{K}^{\prime}
), can be
maintained in
O^{*}(n^{1/2})
amortized expected time per update.

Theorem
1.1
is obtained by presenting two data structures. The first
one (see Section
4.1
)
uses
O^{*}(n^{3})
space, answers a query in
O^{*}(1)
time, and handles an insertion/deletion in
O^{*}(n^{2})
amortized expected time.
(The data structure in
[
5
]
also obtains a similar space/query-time bound but it cannot handle deletions efficiently.)
This data structure is based on constructing
vertical shallow cuttings
, a notion originally introduced in
[
21
,
18
]
, of the graphs of the distance functions of
\mathscr{K}
. Roughly speaking,
for a parameter
t\geq 1
, a
vertical
(n/t)
-shallow
cutting
of
\mathscr{K}
is a collection of pairwise openly disjoint semi-unbounded
pseudo-prisms

that covers the region lying below the
k
-level of
the arrangement of
\mathscr{K}
, where
each prism, consisting of all points that lie
vertically below a constant-complexity semi-algebraic

3
-dimensional region, intersects the graphs of only
O(n/t)
functions of
\mathscr{K}
.
The random-sampling based technique used in
[
18
,
28
]
to construct a vertical shallow cutting
does not immediately extend to our setting because one
needs to decompose the
q
-level, for some parameter
q>0
, in the
4D arrangement of the distance functions of
\mathscr{K}

into
O^{*}(n^{3}(q+1))
constant-complexity cells. Although a recent
result
[
5
]
constructs such a decomposition of the
0
-level (with
O^{*}(n^{3})
cells), it does not
extend to larger values of
q
.
This problem remains elusive for arrangements of arbitrary trivariate semi-algebraic functions of constant complexity,
but we develop a desired decomposition technique for our setting
(in Section
2
) by exploiting the
structural geometric properties of the distance functions of
\mathscr{K}
. This result is one
of the main technical contributions of the paper. Using our result on the decomposition of the
q
-level,
we construct a vertical
(n/t)
-shallow
cutting of
\mathscr{K}
of size
O^{*}(t^{3})
, for any
t
(Section
3
).
We build a tree data structure using vertical shallow cuttings
and adapt the technique in
[
11
]
to handle efficient deletions of
homothets from
\mathscr{K}
; as noted, insertions are handled using the standard Bentley-Saxe technique
[
15
]
.

The second data structure is a linear-size partition tree, with
O^{*}(n^{2/3})
query time,
constructed on the point set
\mathscr{K}^{*}=\{(c_{i},\rho_{i})\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
,
that answers intersection-detection queries on
\mathscr{K}
with a homothet of

{\mathbb{K}}
as a query. The main
technical challenge we face here is the construction of a so-called
test set
of
\mathscr{K}

(see
[
37
]
and below) of size
r^{O(1)}
, which represents well the distance functions of all

(n/r)
-shallow
homothets in
{\mathbb{K}}
, i.e., the homothets that intersect
at most
n/r
elements of
\mathscr{K}
. By adapting the technique in
[
37
]
and again exploiting
the structural geometric properties of the distance functions, we show
(in Section
4.2
)
that a test set, of size
O^{*}(r^{4})
, with the desired properties can be constructed efficiently.

Using Theorem
1.1
and Corollary

, we obtain our second set of results

2
Decomposition of the
k
-Level of an Arrangement of Distance Functions

Preliminaries.

Let
\mathscr{K}=\{K_{1},\ldots,K_{n}\}
, where each
K_{i}

is a homothetic copy of
K
represented by a point
K_{i}^{*}=(c_{i},\rho_{i})
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
.
For
1\leq i\leq n
, set
f_{i}=f_{c_{i},\rho_{i}}
, and let

\mathscr{F}:=\mathscr{F}(\mathscr{K})=\{f_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
.
(As noted, distances from points
x
that lie inside some copy
K_{i}
, still measured by
f_{i}(x)
, are nonpositive.)
We will not distinguish between a function of
\mathscr{F}
and its graph.
For a point
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
,
K_{i}
is the
nearest neighbor
of
x
in
\mathscr{K}

(under the
K
-distance
{\rm dist}_{K}
), i.e.,
i=\arg\min_{1\leq j\leq n}f_{j}(x)
, if

f_{i}
appears on the lower envelope of
\mathscr{F}
at
x
.

The
level
of a point
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
in
\mathscr{A}(\mathscr{F})
is the number of functions in
\mathscr{F}
whose graphs lie below
p
.
For a parameter
k<n
, the
k
-level
of
\mathscr{A}(\mathscr{F})
, denoted
\mathscr{A}_{k}(\mathscr{F})
, is the
(closure of the) locus of all points on
\bigcup\mathscr{F}
whose level is
k
.
We define the
(\leq k)
-level
of
\mathscr{A}(\mathscr{F})
, denoted
\mathscr{A}_{\leq k}(\mathscr{F})
, to be
the (closure of the) set of all points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
of level at most
k
, i.e.,
the set of points that lie on or below
\mathscr{A}_{k}(\mathscr{F})
(not necessarily on
\cup\mathscr{F}
).
The projection of
\mathscr{A}_{k}(\mathscr{F})
onto
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
,
denoted by
\mathscr{M}_{k}=\mathscr{M}_{k}(\mathscr{K})
, is a subdivision of
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.
If a cell
\tau^{\downarrow}
of
\mathscr{M}_{k}
is the projection of a cell
\tau
of

\mathscr{A}_{k}(\mathscr{F})
that lies on the graph of
f_{i}
,
then
K_{i}
is the
k
-th nearest neighbor in
\mathscr{K}
(under the
K
-distance function)
of all points in
\tau^{\downarrow}
. Note that
A_{0}(\mathscr{F})
is the lower envelope of
\mathscr{F}
,
and
\mathscr{M}_{0}(\mathscr{K})
is the Voronoi diagram of
\mathscr{K}
(under the
K
-distance).

The decomposition.

The main technical result of this section is that
\mathscr{A}_{k}(\mathscr{F})
can be decomposed into

O^{*}(n^{3}(k+1))

elementary cells
, namely, constant-complexity semi-algebraic regions,
each homeomorphic to a ball; see below for a more precise definition.

Lemma 2.1
.

K_{i}
is contained in
K_{j}
if and only if
{\rm dist}_{K}(c_{j},c_{i})\leq\rho_{j}-\rho_{i}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
{\rm dist}_{K}(c_{j},c_{i})>\rho_{j}-\rho_{i}
. Let
x
be the intersection point of

{\partial}K_{i}
with the ray emanating from
c_{i}
in direction
c_{i}-c_{j}
, i.e., lying on the
line passing through
c_{i}
and
c_{j}
but not containing
c_{j}
. Because of the collinearity
of
c_{i}
,
c_{j}
, and
x
,

{\rm dist}_{K}(c_{j},x)={\rm dist}_{K}(c_{j},c_{i})+{\rm dist}_{K}(c_{i},x)={\rm dist}_{K}(c_{j},c_{i})+\rho_{i}>\rho_{j}.

Hence,
x\not\in K_{j}
and thus
K_{i}\not\subseteq K_{j}
. Conversely, assume
that
{\rm dist}_{K}(c_{j},c_{i})\leq\rho_{j}-\rho_{i}
.
Let
x\in K_{i}
be an arbitrary point. That is,
{\rm dist}_{K}(x,c_{i})\leq\rho_{i}
.
By the triangle inequality we have

\displaystyle{\rm dist}_{K}(x,c_{j})-\rho_{j}

\displaystyle\leq{\rm dist}_{K}(x,c_{i})+{\rm dist}_{K}(c_{i},c_{j})-\rho_{j}

\displaystyle\leq{\rm dist}_{K}(x,c_{i})+(\rho_{j}-\rho_{i})-\rho_{j}

\displaystyle={\rm dist}_{K}(x,c_{i})-\rho_{i}\leq 0,

i.e.,
x\in K(c_{j},\rho_{j})
. Since this holds for any point
x\in K_{i}
, we conclude that

K(c_{i},\rho_{i})\subseteq K(c_{j},\rho_{j})
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
K_{i}
and
K_{j}
be a pair of non-nested homothets in
\mathscr{K}
(i.e., neither of them lies inside the other). Then
f_{i}(c_{i})<f_{j}(c_{i})
.

Proof.

Assume to the contrary that
f_{j}(c_{i})\leq f_{i}(c_{i})
.
That is,

A key notion that we need for constructing the dynamic NN data structures of Section

. The

\sigma
.

Following the ideas in
[
28
]
, for a parameter

-shallow cutting as follows.
We set two parameters:

is a sufficiently large constant, independent

whose graph contains
\tau
. Finally, we erect a

Let

be the resulting set of pseudo-prisms. By Theorem

Range spaces and shallow
{\varepsilon}
-nets.

Let
\Sigma=(\mathsf{X},\mathscr{R})
be a (finite) range space, where
\mathsf{X}
is a set of objects and
\mathscr{R}\subseteq 2^{\mathsf{X}}

Let

-net

(i)

If

|R\cap\mathsf{X}|\leq\alpha(\zeta+1){\varepsilon}|\mathsf{X}|
, and

(ii)

If
|R\cap\mathsf{X}|\leq\zeta{\varepsilon}|\mathsf{X}|

.

Here

-nets: Property (i) (with

{\displaystyle O\left(\frac{d}{{\varepsilon}}\log\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}

\Xi

Dynamic Data Structures for Proximity Queries

In this section we describe a dynamic data structure for answering
intersection-detection

queries amid
\mathscr{K}
. Namely, for a query homothet
K_{0}\in{\mathbb{K}}
, determine whether
K_{0}
intersects
some homothet of
\mathscr{K}
, and, if the answer is yes, return such a homothet.
In addition to answering queries, the data structure can be updated efficiently
as a homothet
K_{0}\in{\mathbb{K}}
is inserted into
\mathscr{K}
or deleted from
\mathscr{K}
.
The data structure can be extended to answering
intersection-reporting
queries, namely,
reporting all homothets of
\mathscr{K}
that intersect
K_{0}
, at an additional cost of
O(k)
.
Using the parametric-search technique
(see, e.g.,
[
10
,
11
]
), a nearest-neighbor query under the
K
-distance can be reduced
to answering
O(\log n)
intersection-detection queries, so the cost increases by only a logarithmic factor.

As already noted, the insertion of a new homothet into
\mathscr{K}
can be handled using the standard
dynamization technique by Bentley and Saxe
[
15
]
(see also
[
11
]
).
That is, assume that we have an intersection-detection data structure that can handle deletions,
which can be constructed in
P(n)
time, so that a query can be answered in
Q(n)
time and an object can be deleted in
D(n)
time.
Then the amortized insertion time is
O((P(n)/n)\log n)
, the deletion time remains
D(n)
,
and the overall query time is
O(Q(n))
, assuming that
Q(n)=\Omega(n^{\varepsilon})
.
See
[
15
]
for details.

We first describe a data structure that answers a query in
O^{*}(1)
time using

O^{*}(n^{3})
space and handles a deletion in
O^{*}(n^{2})
amortized expected time, and then describe a linear-size data
structure that answers a query in
O^{*}(n^{2/3})
time and handles a deletion in
O(\log^{2}n)
amortized expected time.
Interpolating between these two setups, using standard techniques
[
2
]
,
for a storage parameter
s\in[n,n^{3}]
,
we can construct a structure of size
O^{*}(s)
, in
O^{*}(s)
expected time,
that answers a query in
O^{*}(n/s^{1/3})
;
the amortized expected update time is
O^{*}(s/n)
.

4.1

We follow the same approach of Agarwal and Matoušek for performing dynamic halfspace range

[

]
(slightly improved but more involved approaches were proposed
in
[

Data structure.

Let

Roughly speaking, we build a tree data structure on

That is, we start with

cutting
\Xi
of
\mathscr{F}
of size
O^{*}(t^{3})
, in
O^{*}(nt^{3})
time,
create a child for each prism

\tau
of
\Xi
, recursively construct the data structure for the conflict list

\mathscr{F}_{\tau}
of every
\tau\in\Xi
, and attach it as the subtree of the child corresponding
to
\tau
. The recursion proceeds until we reach cells
\tau
with
|\mathscr{F}_{\tau}|=O(1)
,
in which case we just store
\mathscr{F}_{\tau}
at
\tau
, as a list, say.

A query, with a copy
K_{0}=(c_{0},\rho_{0})
of
K
, is answered in a standard way, by
traversing the tree in a top-down manner. At each node (prism)
\tau
that we visit,
we know that
(c_{0},\rho_{0})\in\tau
. We check whether
(c_{0},\rho_{0})
lies in any
child prism of
\tau
, and, if so, recurse at that child. Otherwise, we locate the
child prism
\tau^{\prime}
for which
(c_{0},\rho_{0})
lies above
\tau^{\prime}
, and report any
element whose function belongs to the conflict list

\mathscr{F}_{\tau^{\prime}}
(or report all of them
in the reporting version).

However, this simple recursive approach runs into a technical difficulty
when we delete a function from
\mathscr{F}
.
When deleting a function from the root, the deletion has to be propagated to some of
its children, to their children, and so on. On average, each function appears in
the conflict lists of
O^{*}(t^{2})
prisms of
\Xi
. When such a “good” function is deleted,
the deletion is propagated to only
O^{*}(t^{2})
descendants, leading to
O^{*}(n^{2})
overall deletion
time, as can easily be verified. However, some of the functions (in fact, up to
n/t
of them) may appear
in the conflict lists of
\Omega(t^{3})
prisms of
\Xi
.
If these “bad” functions are the first
n/t
functions to be deleted and then the data structure
is reconstructed, the overall cost of the deletion operations will be too high.
As in
[
11
,
28
]
, we circumvent this difficulty by maintaining a partition of
\mathscr{F}
into a few
subsets and constructing a cutting for each subset that is good for all functions in that subset
(i.e., each function in the subset appears in only
O^{*}(t^{2})
conflict lists).

We now describe the data structure in detail. We fix a sufficiently large constant
t>0
.
The data structure is periodically reconstructed after performing some deletions. We use

m
to denote the size of
\mathscr{F}
when the data structure was previously reconstructed and
n

to denote its current size. We reconstruct the data structure after deleting
m/2t
functions
from
\mathscr{F}
. Thus
n\geq m(1-1/2t)
. Let
P(r)
denote the maximum time spent in constructing the
data structure for a set of
r
functions. We pay for the reconstruction cost by charging

2tP(m-m/2t)/m\leq 2tP(n)/n
time to each of the
m/2t
delete operations that occurred
before the reconstruction (the inequality holds because
P
is assumed to be superlinear).
The data structure for
\mathscr{F}
is a (recursively defined) tree
\Psi(\mathscr{F})
. We describe how a
subtree
\Psi(\mathscr{G})
, for a subset
\mathscr{G}
of
\nu
functions, is constructed.

If
\nu\leq n_{0}
, for some sufficiently large constant
n_{0}
, then
\Psi(\mathscr{G})
is a single
leaf, and we simply store
\mathscr{G}
at that leaf. So assume that
\nu>n_{0}
. The root of
\Psi(\mathscr{G})

stores the following data:

•

A partition of
\mathscr{G}
into subsets
\mathscr{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{G}_{u}
,
u\leq\lceil\log_{2}t\rceil
,
as described below.

•

For each
1\leq i\leq u
, a vertical
(\nu/t)
-shallow cutting

\Xi_{i}
for
\mathscr{G}_{i}
.
For each
\Delta\in\Xi_{i}
, we store its conflict list
\mathscr{G}_{i,\Delta}
.

•

For each
1\leq i\leq u
and
\Delta\in\Xi_{i}
, a pointer to the tree
\Psi(\mathscr{G}_{i,\Delta})
.

•

For each function
f\in\mathscr{G}_{i}
, the set
L_{f}\subseteq\Xi_{i}
of prisms
\Delta

crossed by the graph of
f
.

•

A counter
\chi
that keeps track of how many functions of
\mathscr{G}
can be deleted before
we reconstruct
\Psi(\mathscr{G})
.

After the construction of
\Psi(\mathscr{G})
, before any deletions occur, the following properties hold:

(P1)

The counter
\chi
is set to
\nu/2t
.

(P2)

For each
i
, the
x_{4}
-monotone surface formed by the pseudo-cones of
\Xi_{i}

(i.e., the ceilings of prisms of
\Xi_{i}
) lie above
\mathscr{A}_{\nu/t}(\mathscr{G}_{i})
.

(P3)

For every
i
and
\Delta\in\Xi_{i}
,
|\mathscr{G}_{i,\Delta}|\leq cn/t
,
for some suitable absolute constant
c
.

(P4)

Each function
f\in\mathscr{G}_{i}
appears in the conflict lists of at most

\kappa:=2C_{1}t^{2+\delta}

prisms of
\Xi_{i}
, where
C_{1}
and
\delta>0
are the constants appearing in
(
1
) below.

We now describe the construction of the partition
\mathscr{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{G}_{u}
, which proceeds
iteratively. Suppose we have constructed
\mathscr{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{G}_{i-1}
. Set

\mathscr{G}_{i}^{*}=\mathscr{G}\setminus\bigcup_{j<i}\mathscr{G}_{i}
and put
\nu_{i}=|\mathscr{G}_{i}^{*}|
.
If
\nu_{i}\leq\nu/t
, we stop. Otherwise, set
t_{i}=t\nu_{i}/\nu
and
k=\nu_{i}/t_{i}=\nu/t
.

We construct a vertical
k
-shallow cutting
\Xi_{i}
of
\mathscr{G}_{i}^{*}
of size
O(t_{i}^{3+\delta})
,
for an arbitrarily small constant
\delta>0
.
For each
\Delta\in\Xi_{i}
,
|\mathscr{G}_{i,\Delta}^{*}|\leq c\nu_{i}/t_{i}=c\nu/t
. We note that

\sum_{\Delta\in\Xi_{i}}|\mathscr{G}_{i,\Delta}^{*}|\leq O(t_{i}^{3+\delta})\frac{c\nu}{t}\leq C_{1}\left(\frac{t\nu_{i}}{\nu}\right)^{3+\delta}\frac{\nu}{t}\leq C_{1}\nu_{i}t^{2+\delta}.

(1)

A function
f\in\mathscr{G}_{i}^{*}
is called

if it appears in the conflict list of at
most
\kappa
prisms of
\Xi_{i}
and
bad
otherwise.
Let
\mathscr{G}_{i}
be the set of good functions of
\mathscr{G}_{i}^{*}
; set
\mathscr{G}_{i+1}^{*}=\mathscr{G}_{i}^{*}\setminus\mathscr{G}_{i}
.
By (
1
),
|\mathscr{G}_{i+1}^{*}|\leq\nu_{i}/2
. Hence,
u\leq\lceil\log_{2}t\rceil
.
It is easily seen that properties (P1)–(P4) hold after the construction.
This completes the description of the data structure. The total size and preprocessing time are
O^{*}(n^{3})
.

Query procedure.

Let
K(c_{0},\rho_{0})\in{\mathbb{K}}
be a query homothet.
Recall that
K(c_{0},\rho_{0})
intersects a homothet of
\mathscr{K}
if the point
(c_{0},\rho_{0})
lies above the
lower envelope of the corresponding set
\mathscr{F}
of functions. The query is performed as follows.
We search
\Psi(\mathscr{F})
in a top-down manner with
(c_{0},\rho_{0})
. Suppose we are at the root of a subtree

\Psi(\mathscr{G})
. If
\Psi(\mathscr{G})
is a leaf, we check all functions of
\mathscr{G}
in a brute-force manner and
return an answer in
O(n_{0})=O(1)
time. Otherwise, for each
i\leq u
, we check whether
(c_{0},\rho_{0})

lies above the lower envelope of
\mathscr{G}_{i}
. If the answer is yes for any
i
,
we return yes and also return an intersecting homothet (see below). Otherwise, we return no.

For a fixed
i\leq u
, we proceed as follows.
We search with
c_{0}
, in a brute force manner, in
O^{*}(t^{3})=O(1)
time,
to determine the pseudo-cone
\tau^{\downarrow}
in the projection of
\Xi_{i}

that contains
c_{0}
. Let
f_{i}
be the function associated with
\tau

the ceiling of
\Delta
lies in the graph of

.
We test whether
\rho_{0}\geq f_{i}(c_{0})

and

intersect, so we
return yes and report

as an intersection witness and terminate the query. If not,

lies in the semi-unbounded prism

,
and we continue the search recursively in the data structure

.

Let
Q(\nu)
be the maximum time spent by the query procedure in the subtree

storing at most
\nu
functions. Then we obtain the following recurrence:

Q(\nu)\leq\lceil\log_{2}t\rceil Q(c\nu/t)+O(t^{3+\delta}).

Its solution is easily seen to be
Q(\nu)=O(n^{\varepsilon})
, for any constant

and

are chosen sufficiently large.

3

3

As shown in

.
The same improvement can be obtained here, but, for simplicity of exposition, we stay with

Let

be a function that we wish to delete from

Again, we visit

(P2’)

, the

-monotone surface formed by the pseudo-cones of

The correctness of the query procedure follows from property (P2’). Following the analysis

, the amortized deletion time, including the time spent in reconstructing the data

.
Putting everything together, and combining this with the technique of Bentley and Saxe

for insertions, we obtain:

.

\mathscr{K}
can be preprocessed,

4.2
Linear-size data structure

Next, we present a linear-size dynamic data structure that supports intersection-detection queries
in
O^{*}(n^{2/3})
time each, and can handle updates (insertions and deletions) in
O(\log^{2}n)

amortized expected time per update.

For a homothet
K(c_{0},\rho_{0})
, let
f_{c_{0},\rho_{0}}^{+}=\{(c,\rho)\in{\mathbb{R}}\mid\rho\geq f_{c_{0},\rho_{0}}(c)\}

be the region lying above the graph of the distance function
f_{c_{0},\rho_{0}}
, which corresponds to the set of
homothets that intersect
K({c_{0},\rho_{0}})
. Put
\mathscr{K}^{*}=\{(c,\rho)\mid K(c,\rho)\in\mathscr{K}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
.
For a query homothet
K(c_{0},\rho_{0})
, we wish to determine whether any point of
\mathscr{K}^{*}

lies in

. We construct a linear-size partition tree for answering these queries,
following the same approach as in
[
11
,
34
,
37
]
. We need a few definitions.
Let
{\mathbb{F}}
be the set of distance functions corresponding to the homothets in
{\mathbb{K}}
.
Let

be a set of
n
points (representing homothets of
K
).
For a parameter

, we call a semi-algebraic set

.
A major ingredient of the approach in

that are

Formally, a finite collection

(i)

(ii)

(iii)

Sharir and Shaul

Test-set construction.

We describe an algorithm for constructing a test set
of size
O(r^{4})
with

and

.
Let
\mathscr{F}
be the set of distance functions of
\mathscr{K}
as above. We take a

of

and construct the (standard) 4D

By a standard random-sampling argument

5
Breadth-First Search, Reverse Shortest Paths, and Related Problems

In this section we present applications of our techniques to various basic graph algorithms
on intersection or proximity graphs of a set of homothets in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.

In this section we present applications of our techniques to the reverse shortest path problem.

Breadth-first search.

We follow the standard high-level approach. We run the BFS layer by layer, where the first layer
contains just the start object
K(c_{s},\rho_{s})
. Consider the step where we have constructed layer

L_{i}
and wish to construct the next layer
L_{i+1}
. Let
\mathscr{U}_{i}
denote the set of all members of

\mathscr{K}
that the BFS has not yet reached (up to this step). We maintain
\mathscr{U}_{i}
in the dynamic data
structure of Section
4
, which supports deletions and queries (insertions are irrelevant
here), where a query specifies an object
K(c_{0},\rho_{0})

(in
L_{i}
), and wants to determine whether
K(c_{0},\rho_{0})
intersects some object
K(c,\rho)
of

\mathscr{U}_{i}
, and, if so, to return one such object (or, alternatively, report all intersecting homothets
in
\mathscr{U}_{i}
). The output object, or objects, if they exist, are added to
L_{i+1}
,
and are immediately removed from the structure (and from
\mathscr{U}_{i}
), to make sure that they are not reported
again (by other elements of
L_{i}
, or by elements of future layers).

The BFS procedure executes a total of
O(n)
queries and deletions, therefore by Remark
1

(following Theorem
4.4
, with
s=n^{3/2}
),
the overall cost of the the algorithm is
O^{*}(n^{3/2})
, as stated in
Theorem
1.3
(a).

Depth-first search.

The same machinery can be used to perform depth-first search on the graph
\mathscr{G}(\mathscr{K})
.
At each step we are at some homothet
K(c_{0},\rho_{0})\in\mathscr{K}
, and want to find an intersecting neighbor
that has not yet been visited. As above, we maintain the set
\mathscr{U}
of all members of
\mathscr{K}
that
the DFS has not yet reached, using the dynamic data structure of Section
4
, with the
same choice of
s=n^{3/2}
. Using this structure, we find a neighbor of
K(c_{0},\rho_{0})

(if one exists), delete it from
\mathscr{U}
, and continue the search at that neighbor (or back up
to the parent node, or start a new DFS tree). As above, since we perform

O(n)
searches and deletions, the overall cost is
O^{*}(n^{3/2})
time.
This, together with the preceding analysis, establish Theorem
1.3
(a).

Reverse shortest paths.

We next use Theorem
1.3
to solve the corresponding reverse shortest path (RSP) problem,
defined as follows.
Let
\mathscr{K}
be as above, and let
r>0
be a parameter. We consider the setup where, for each

K(c,\rho)\in\mathscr{K}
, we expand its size by adding
r
to the size of each element of
\mathscr{K}
,
i.e., replacing each
K(c,\rho)
by
K(c,\rho+r)
.
Define
\mathscr{G}_{r}(\mathscr{K})
to
be the intersection graph of the expanded homothets in
\mathscr{K}
. We are then given two elements

K(c_{s},\rho_{s})
,
K(c_{t},\rho_{t})
of
\mathscr{K}
and an integer
1\leq k<n
, and seek the smallest value

r^{*}
for which
\mathscr{G}_{r^{*}}(\mathscr{K})
contains a path from
K(c_{s},\rho_{s}+r^{*})
to
K(c_{t},\rho_{t}+r^{*})
,
of at most
k
edges. This is the
reverse shortest path
(RSP) problem for that graph.

The decision problem associated with this RSP problem is, given
r
, to decide whether
\mathscr{G}_{r}(\mathscr{K})

contains such a path. If the answer is positive (resp., negative) then
r^{*}\leq r
(resp.,

r^{*}>r
). Solving this decision problem is trivially performed using BFS on
\mathscr{G}_{r}(\mathscr{K})
,
starting from
K(c_{s},\rho_{s}+r)
, so, by Theorem
1.3
, the resulting decision procedure
runs in
O^{*}(n^{3/2})
time.

We now solve the RSP problem using the shrink-and-bifurcate technique (see
[
14
,
20
,
27
]
),
adapted to the setup at hand. The
critical values
of
r
are values at which some comparison involving

r
that the BFS algorithm performs changes its outcome. These critical values are of several kinds.
First, these are
values of
r
at which two expanded homothets,
K(c,\rho+r)
,
K(c^{\prime},\rho^{\prime}+r)
, become externally
tangent to one another (they are disjoint for smaller values of
r
and intersect from this
r

and above). This happens when

{\rm dist}_{K}(c,c^{\prime})=(\rho+r)+(\rho^{\prime}+r)=\rho+\rho^{\prime}+2r,\;\;\text{or}\;\;r=\frac{1}{2}\left({\rm dist}_{K}(c,c^{\prime})-\rho-\rho^{\prime}\right).

(2)

Other kinds of critical values arise in comparisons that occur during the construction of, and
search in, the data structure of Section
4
. There are many kinds of such comparisons,
each of which either involves the manipulation of
O(1)
functions
f_{c,\rho}
(that is,

f_{c,\rho+r}
), or comparisons between points and functions, which determine whether the
point lies above, on, or below the function. The latter kind of comparisons gives rise to
critical values of the first kind (given in (
2
)).

The shrinking part of the technique receives a parameter
L
, and aims to construct an interval
I

that contains
r^{*}
and at most
L
other critical values. This procedure is based on a variant
of “distance selection”, which in turn is based on a procedure for counting the number of
critical values that are smaller than or equal to
r_{0}
, for some query parameter
r_{0}
.
This procedure is implemented using off-line semi-algebraic range searching.
Specifically, for critical values in (
2
), each homothet

K(c,\rho)
is mapped to the point
(c,\rho)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
, as before, and also to the range

\sigma(c,\rho)=\{(c^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}\mid f_{c,\rho}(c^{\prime})={\rm dist}_{K}(c,c^{\prime})-\rho\leq\rho^{\prime}+2r_{0}\}.

The goal is then to count the number of point-range containments. Each range is a semi-algebraic region
of constant complexity, and both points and ranges have four degrees of freedom. The general technique
for offline semi-algebraic range searching, as presented, e.g., in
[
2
, Appendix]
, runs in
this case in time
O^{*}(n^{8/5})
, which implies that, using the improved procedure
of Chan and Huang
[
20
]
, interval shrinking (restricted to this type of critical values)
can be performed in
O^{*}(n^{8/5}/L^{4/5})
expected time.

To handle critical values of other kinds, we observe that our additive expansion rule
subtracts the same value
r
from each of these functions, so comparisons involving any
set of
O(1)
functions can be determined independently of
r
. Put differently, we can
think of the process as constructing the data structure without
r
(i.e., with
r=0
),
in a manner that requires no simulation, and then query the structure with
K(c_{0},\rho_{0}+2r)
.
Hence the only
r
-dependent comparisons occur during a query.

Unfortunately, here there is a significant difference between the fast-query version of
the structure and the linear-space version. In the fast-query version, the query is with
the point
(c_{0},\rho_{0}+2r)
, and each
r
-dependent comparison tests the position of this point
(above, on, below) with respect to a function in
\mathscr{F}
. In other words, all the corresponding
critical values are of the kind in (
2
). In contrast, in the linear-storage version,
the query is with a surface, dual to the point
(c_{0},\rho_{0}+2r)
, and certain steps in the
execution of a query seek cells of the current decomposition that are intersected
by the surface or lie fully below it, and these operations, which do depend on
r
, generate
critical values that are not of the type (
2
).

In principle, this is not a real problem. As just briefly reviewed, the shrinking stage
(see
[
14
,
20
,
27
]
), relies on a “distance-selection” procedure, which is
implemented as an off-line semi-algebraic range-counting procedure, where the ranges
are defined by some semi-algebraic predicate of constant complexity. For critical
values of the former kind, the predicate is given by (
2
). For critical
values of the other kinds, we just have to use different predicates. Here too the
queries have four degrees of freedom (defined by the parameters
(c_{0},\rho_{0}+2r)
),
but the data objects (e.g., a vertex of a cell in the decomposition) may have many more
degrees of freedom, and this will slow down the procedure.

There are two ways out of this difficulty. One approach would be to bound the maximum
number of degrees of freedom of the data objects, which is some small constant, and then run the relevant range
searching procedure, using, e.g., the analysis in
[
2
, Appendix]
, with the
inferior time bound that it would yield. The other approach, which is the one that we follow,
is to give up in the simulation the use of the linear-space version of the structure,
and just use the fast-query version.

Recall that this version, applied to a set of
n
homothetic copies, uses a total of
O^{*}(n^{3})

storage and preprocessing, answers a query in
O^{*}(1)
time, and performs a deletion in

O^{*}(n^{2})
(amortized) time. To make it more efficient, we partition
\mathscr{K}
into
O(n/t)
subsets,
each consisting of at most
t
homothets, for a suitable parameter
t
that will be fixed shortly,
and construct a different structure for each subset.
A query has now to be performed on each of these structures, for a total of
O^{*}(n/t)
time,
while a deletion has to be performed only within its own subset, for a cost of
O^{*}(t^{2})

amortized expected time. Choosing
t=n^{1/3}
makes the two bounds asymptotically the same,
for a total cost (for
O(n)
queries and deletions) of
O^{*}(n^{5/3})
.

The standard analysis of the bifurcation stage
[
14
]
yields the bound
O^{*}(L^{1/2}D(n))
on its
running time, where
D(n)
is the cost of the decision procedure. Here however, we need to
modify the analysis because we have two versions of the decision procedure, the one that we
simulate, and the one that runs, without simulation, on some concrete values
r_{0}
of
r
.
The respective running time bounds of these versions are
D_{1}(n)=O^{*}(n^{5/3})
and

D_{0}(n)=O^{*}(n^{3/2})
.

Here is a sketch of the modified analysis (see
[
14
,
27
]
for full details).
The standard analysis uses two parameters
X
,
Y
, where
X
is a threshold on the
number of unresolved comparisons accumulated in a single phase of the bifurcation,
and
Y
is a threshold
on the total amount of simulation steps performed in a phase. The cost of a phase is

O^{*}(XY+D_{0}(n))
, where the second term results from the binary search for
r^{*}
at the
end of the phase. We take
XY=D_{0}(n)
. The number of phases is

{\displaystyle O\left(\frac{L}{X}+\frac{D_{1}(n)}{Y}\right)}
, which we optimize by making
these two terms equal, i.e., by choosing
{\displaystyle Y=\frac{D_{1}(n)X}{L}}
. Solving this
system of equations, we get
{\displaystyle X=L^{1/2}\left(\frac{D_{0}(n)}{D_{1}(n)}\right)^{1/2}}
,
and the overall cost is then

O^{*}\left(XY\cdot\frac{D_{1}(n)}{Y}\right)=O^{*}(D_{1}(n)X)=O^{*}(L^{1/2}D_{0}(n)^{1/2}D_{1}(n)^{1/2}).

In our case,
D_{1}(n)=O^{*}(n^{5//3})
and
D_{0}(n)=O^{*}(n^{3/2})
, so the overall bound is

O^{*}\left(\frac{n^{8/5}}{L^{4/5}}+L^{1/2}n^{3/4}n^{5/6}\right)=O^{*}\left(\frac{n^{8/5}}{L^{4/5}}+L^{1/2}n^{19/12}\right).

To balance the two terms, we choose
L^{13/10}=n^{1/60}
, or
L=n^{1/78}
, and the bound then becomes

O^{*}\left(n^{62/39}\right)
. That is, we have obtained the bound asserted in Theorem
1.4
.

Remark 2
.

When
K
is the Euclidean ball, the algorithm becomes simpler, and its performance can be
improved to
O^{*}(n^{56/39})
, as recently shown in
[
30
]
. The reason is that the intersection
detection subprocedure can be transformed to a dynamic halfspace range reporting in
{\mathbb{R}}^{5}
,
using a simple lifting transform. The latter task can be solved using the machinery of Agarwal and
Matoušek
[
11
]
, which leads to the above improved bound. See
[
30
]
for full details.

Minimum spanning forests and Dijkstra’s shortest path algorithm.

Let
P
be a set of
n
points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, and let
K
be as above. As we recall,
for a given
r_{0}>0
,
the
r_{0}
-proximity graph
\mathscr{G}_{r_{0}}(P)
(with respect to the metric
\|\cdot\|_{K}
)
is the graph whose vertices are the points of
P
, and whose
edges are all the pairs
(p,q)
such that
{\rm dist}_{K}(p,q)\leq r_{0}
.
We assign to each edge
(p,q)\in G_{r_{0}}(P)
the weight
{\rm dist}_{K}(p,q)
. We consider the problems
of computing a minimum spanning forest of
\mathscr{G}_{r_{0}}(P)
(note that
\mathscr{G}_{r_{0}}(P)
might be disconnected),
and of computing the shortest-path tree in
\mathscr{G}_{r_{0}}(P)
from some start point
K_{s}
.

For the minimum spanning forest problem,
we run Prim’s algorithm which, at each step, maintains the partition of
P

into the subsets
V
,
U
, of visited and unvisited points, respectively. The algorithm computes
the bichromatic closest pair (BCP)
(p,q)
in
V\times U
. If
{\rm dist}_{K}(p,q)\leq r_{0}
,
we add the edge
(p,q)
to the MST and move
q
from
U
to
V
. Otherwise, we
conclude that the vertices of
U
form a connected component of
\mathscr{G}_{r_{0}}(P)
, we discard
U
,
and continue running Prim’s algorithm on
V
(this case will not arise when
\mathscr{G}_{r_{0}}(P)

is connected). By Corollary
1.2
, the BCP of
U
and
V
can be maintained in
O^{*}(n^{1/2})

amortized expected time under insertion/deletion of points, so the overall expected run time
of the algorithm is
O^{*}(n^{3/2})
, as claimed in Theorem
1.3
(b).

The implementation of Dijkstra’s shortest-path algorithm is similar.
At each step of the algorithm, we have the sets
V
and
U
of nodes that the algorithm has already visited, and
those that it did not, respectively. Each point
p\in V
has a weight
\omega(p)
associated with it, equal to
the shortest distance from the start point
s
to
p
. We now compute the BCP in
V\times U
, where the
points
p\in V
are additively weighted by the corresponding distances
\omega(p)
, and the points
of
U
are unweighted. At each step, we wish to compute the weighted BCP of
V\times U
,
namely
(p^{*},q^{*})=\arg\min_{(p,q)\in V\times U}\left({\rm dist}_{K}(p,q)+\omega(p)\right)
.
If
{\rm dist}_{K}(p^{*},q^{*})\leq r_{0}
,
we add
q^{*}
as a child of
p^{*}
, and move
q^{*}
from
U
to
V
, with weight

\omega(q^{*})=\omega(p^{*})+{\rm dist}_{K}(p^{*},q^{*})
. Otherwise, we conclude that all remaining
points of
U
are farther than
r_{0}
from
p^{*}
, so none of them is adjacent to
p^{*}
.
We delete
p^{*}
from
V
and continue.

We implement the computation of
(p^{*},q^{*})
using Corollary
1.2
, as follows.
We choose a sufficiently large parameter
A
, larger than
\max_{p}\omega(p)
, which can easily be
computed in advance in
O(n)
time. Then we replace each point
p\in V
by the homothetic copy

K_{p}=p+(A-\omega(p))K
. Set
\mathscr{K}_{V}=\{K_{p}\mid p\in V\}
. It is easily seen that if

(K_{p^{*}},q^{*})
is the BCP of
\mathscr{K}_{V}\times U
, then
(p^{*},q^{*})
is the weighted BCP of

V
and
U
. Hence, each step of the
algorithm can be implemented in
O^{*}(n^{1/2})
amortized expected time.

At each step of the algorithm, we either move a point from
U
to
V
or delete a point from
V
.
Since each point of
P
is moved from
U
or deleted from
V
at most once, the overall
expected time is
O^{*}(n^{3/2})
, as claimed in Theorem
1.3
(b).
