Executive Summary
Load growth at the grid edge is driving increased attention to the distribution system and its
ability to enable customer technology adoption in an affordable and timely manner. Key
industry stakeholders, including electric utilities and regulators, can benefit from strategies to
manage and balance customer needs with infrastructure investments, such as demand
flexibility.

This report focuses on demand flexibility—the ability to reduce, shift, shed, generate, or
modulate loads in response to building and grid needs—to reduce the need for costly grid
upgrades by deferring investment needs and increase system reliability by shifting electricity
usage during periods of high risk. Specifically, we focus on the emerging characteristics of
business models for demand flexibility as a framework to understand how demand flexibility
programs generate value.

In this report, we focus on demand flexibility program stakeholder ecosystem management
strategies, which provide information on value delivery and describe how program
implementers leverage partner capabilities and collaborate to deliver customer and grid
benefits. This report discusses the role of stakeholder ecosystem management in demand
flexibility programs, identifies existing challenges to effective stakeholder management, and
describes lessons learned.

Stakeholder ecosystem management in demand flexibility business models

Managing demand flexibility program stakeholders effectively can contribute to market
development by enabling program partners to scale their operations to serve more customers
and expand to new regions. Program implementers can manage stakeholder ecosystems as
strategic partnerships between independent organizations, which can include partners with
well-established roles in the energy industry (e.g., utilities and program administrations) and
new industry players (e.g., technology companies providing innovative hardware and software
solutions, and property management organizations).

Stakeholder ecosystem management challenges

Challenges for effective stakeholder ecosystem management include:
 • Changing priorities of program partners and their impact on program implementation
 and the ability to deliver benefits to customers
 • Ensuring customers receive high-quality information and guidance when multiple
 program partners are communicating directly with participants
 • Managing relationships for stakeholders with significantly different business models and
 market strategies
 • Minimizing the impact of market gaps between program design functionalities and
 requirements, and available technologies and capabilities from vendors

      Business Models for Scaling Demand Flexibility – Volume III: Stakeholder Ecosystem | v

Stakeholder ecosystem management lessons learned

Lessons learned to support program implementation and scaling include:
 • Establishing and monitoring stakeholder roles and responsibilities throughout the life of
 the program
 • Developing a standardized program delivery process to clearly characterize
 requirements and milestones over time
 • Implementing strategies to identify, manage, and mitigate stakeholder risks
 • Establishing a program governance structure that is compatible with partner goals and
 priorities

This report is part of a series that includes reports on value propositions, customer relationship
management strategies, and program life cycle.

1. Introduction
Significant load growth is being driven by technology adoption at the grid edge, including
electric vehicles and high-performance heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC), as
well as larger loads such as data centers supporting artificial intelligence, and the increasing
electricity needs of the industrial sector (Pereira et al., 2025). Recent North American Electric
Reliability Council (NERC) estimates, released in 2024, show load growth is expected to lead to
a 15% increase in summer peak demand and 18% winter peak demand, over the next 10 years
for the U.S. and Canada (NERC, 2024). The pace and scale of expected load growth are
leading to increased attention on the electricity sector infrastructure and investments needed to
ensure it can provide service to customers in a reliable and affordable manner. At the grid
edge, distribution systems play a key role as the interface between customer loads and the
grid. Investments in distribution systems have increased in recent years and currently account
for the largest share of costs for U.S. investor-owned utilities. For instance, data from the
Edison Electric Institute for 2024 indicate that distribution system capital expenditures account
for 32% of total capital expenditures (EEI, 2024). This further emphasizes the need for
strategies that can enable additional load while managing the impact on affordability, such as
managing flexible loads through demand flexibility (EFI Foundation, 2024; Goldenberg et al.,
2018).

Demand flexibility is the ability of building electricity loads to change in response to building and
grid needs and objectives. Demand flexibility can drive affordability improvements by shifting
electricity consumption out of high-price periods, as well as by reducing the need for costly
infrastructure upgrades. Affordability benefits for customers result from a more cost-effective
use of utility infrastructure, which reduces the need for additional investments, such as
increased capacity to meet higher peak loads. Utility benefits derive from the ability to allocate
resources more effectively by being able to reduce, defer, or fully mitigate the need for
infrastructure upgrades (Schwartz & Leventis, 2020). Demand flexibility can cost-effectively
support grid reliability by reducing electricity demand in periods of high risk (Fox-Penner et al.,
2025; Jackson et al., 2021; LBNL, 2025; NERC, 2024; Norris et al., 2025; Pereira et al., 2025;
U.S. Department of Energy, 2024).

Building demand flexibility can be enabled by behind-the-meter distributed energy resources
(DERs) and results from responses to price-based signals (e.g., dedicated time-of-use rate
structures to incentivize customer behavior) or grid-based signals (e.g., grid operator requests
change load behavior to manage and mitigate operational challenges) aimed at delivering
benefits to the building occupants, owners, and the grid (Satchwell et al., 2021). DERs, in
combination with building controls, can deliver demand flexibility by reducing, shedding,
shifting, modulating, or generating load (See Table 1).

Recent studies have estimated the significant potential of demand flexibility to reduce system
costs and improve affordability (Murphy et al., 2024). For instance, the U.S. Department of
Energy estimated that demand flexibility could deliver system benefits worth $8-18 billion
annually by 2030 (Satchwell et al., 2021). Similarly, the Rocky Mountain Institute estimated that
demand flexibility from air conditioning and water heating could result in a ~8% decrease in
peak demand in the U.S. in the residential sector alone, valued at $13 billion per year (Bronski
et al., 2017). The Brattle Group estimated that cost-effective demand flexibility could reach
~20% of the U.S. peak load, resulting in $15 billion in annual benefits by 2030 (Hledik et al.,
2019).

In addition to the benefits and potential of demand flexibility, key industry stakeholders,
including state and utility decision-makers, are prioritizing demand flexibility. For example, a
recent survey by the Association of Energy Service Professionals found that 93% of the utilities
surveyed considered demand flexibility a medium-to-high priority for their business. Moreover,
49% of the utilities in the study indicated that they plan to implement a demand flexibility
program within the next three years (AESP, 2024). Similarly, an analysis by the Smart Electric
Power Alliance and the North Carolina Clean Energy Technology Center in 2024 found that 38
states took action to advance virtual power plants and support DERs, which can contribute to
demand flexibility (SEPA, 2025). These actions include utilities proposing new or expanding
existing programs (e.g., demand response programs, electric vehicle managed charging

programs, and multi-technology programs), and commissions and legislatures developing
frameworks and assessing the role of existing policies.

In this context, business models play an important role in ensuring that the significant demand
flexibility potential is realized (Li et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2021). Business models offer a
conceptual framework to understand how demand flexibility programs generate value to
customers, program implementers, and other key stakeholders and partners. Specifically,
business models provide insights into how program implementers (e.g., utilities, aggregators,
third-party program managers, etc.) create, deliver, and capture value (see Table 2). Value
creation and delivery encompass the decisions, resources, and processes program
implementers make to ensure that customers effectively benefit from program participation.
Value capture encompasses program implementer decisions related to implementation and
operation costs and program revenues. Collectively, these elements provide information
relevant to understanding how demand flexibility program implementers combine technologies
and capabilities in innovative offerings that generate value, including increased affordability and
reliability (Hamwi et al., 2021; Teece, 2010; Zott & Amit, 2010).

2. The role of stakeholder ecosystem management in
 demand flexibility business models
Demand flexibility programs often require alignment and close collaboration with multiple
partners from different organizations to achieve goals successfully. Program implementers can
leverage partner capabilities to deliver high-quality programs for customers while creating value
for all stakeholders. In this case, a stakeholder network includes program implementation
partners, as stakeholders who are directly involved in designing, implementing, operating, and
scaling activities, such as utilities, third-party program managers, aggregators, equipment
manufacturers, DER developers, marketing, education, and outreach experts, to name a few.
These stakeholders are the ones represented in the challenges and lessons learned in this
report. They provide critical skills and resources for program success (e.g., a partner with
significant experience and access to a pool of prospective program participants). Other relevant
stakeholders include state institutions, such as public utility commissions, for programs
requiring regulatory approval (e.g., commission approval for utility tariffs to implement program
incentives), as well as state energy offices.

Program implementers can bring together stakeholders operating across the demand flexibility
value chain. Text Box 1 describes the demand flexibility value chain, including key
stakeholders. The stakeholder network to deliver a demand flexibility program can include
organizations with well-established roles in the energy industry, such as utilities and program
administrators. Demand flexibility programs also create an opportunity for new industry players
to engage, such as companies developing new technologies and approaches for building
energy management and optimization, and property management organizations interested in
improving operational efficiency and unlocking savings in their portfolio.

Stakeholder relationships can be structured as strategic partnerships between independent
organizations, collaborating to combine skills and resources to deliver demand flexibility
programs. Bringing together stakeholders with complementary capabilities and shared priorities
can enable valuable synergies contributing to innovative program design and implementation.
However, this may also increase program complexity and requires program implementers to
balance benefits and risks when determining the stakeholders necessary to bring the program
to market (CPUC, 2022). For instance, a greater number of partners may increase the risk for
information asymmetries that can result in lack of alignment between stakeholders on program
priorities and goals, consequently impacting program effectiveness and customer experience
(Gerke et al., 2024). As a result, program implementers need to balance the operational
complexity resulting from the group of stakeholders working together with the added value of
the partnership to the program’s success (CALMAC, 2024; Satchwell et al., 2021).

3. Challenges
Through our analysis, we identified challenges for managing stakeholder ecosystems to ensure
all partners benefit from and contribute to program success. We identified challenges related to
stakeholder communication and alignment, vendor readiness, and stakeholder collaboration.

3.1 Stakeholder communication and alignment
Changes in program partners’ near-term priorities can impact program implementation
and value proposition. Program partners’ organizational strategies can evolve throughout a
program’s life cycle and significantly impact program success and customer satisfaction. This
may occur in any phase of the program. For instance, during the program design phase, a
program partner responsible for supporting customer engagement and feedback may change
its services, affecting program design. Similarly, during program operation, a program partner
responsible for device integration and data access may change its data sharing policy and
impact program delivery (U.S. DOE, 2024). One of the program implementers interviewed
shared the experience of their utility partner, who decided to abandon the program, which
significantly changed the scale of their program and required an adjustment to the program
strategy and a search for an alternative utility partner.

Changes in program partners’ long-term business models and strategic priorities can
pose risks to future scaling. Program implementers may design a program and invest in
program-enabling infrastructure (e.g., customer portals and mobile applications, and device
integration) with a long-term vision for implementation and scaling, with the expectation of
lasting relationships among program partners. For example, expecting foundational
investments into data processing capabilities to support program replication in new
geographies in the future, to provide value to customers and the grid. However, partners may
pivot strategy after the program concludes (e.g., after three years), impacting scaling efforts.
For example, non-utility program implementers developing a program in close collaboration
with a utility may be affected by a change in the utility’s planning and operational priorities that
can impact demand flexibility scaling. Particularly, this challenge can affect programs
implementing innovative services and technology packages, which may result in the need for
iterations on each partner’s role to ensure successful program delivery and scaling. Text Box 2
provides insight into utility demand flexibility developments. In some cases, utility priorities may
be shaped by regulatory actions. Text Box 3 provides an overview of actions related to virtual
power plants taken by utilities and states.

Information delivery through partners can impact quality of customer marketing,
education, and outreach efforts. Demand flexibility programs can establish partnerships with
stakeholders with existing relationships and open communication channels with potential
program participants. This can support program activities related to customer outreach,
education, enrollment, and onboarding. However, these third-party stakeholders may have to
balance multiple priorities when engaging with customers, which may impact their ability to
effectively share demand flexibility program information. For example, one of the program
implementers interviewed, who focused on residential new construction, shared the challenge
of relying on home builders to adequately inform prospective home buyers of the demand
flexibility technologies included in the house and their participation in the demand flexibility
program. They realized the home builder is already delivering a significant volume of
information about the property to prospective buyers, which may reduce their ability to
communicate program-related information effectively.

Large and complex stakeholder ecosystems can contribute to less clarity on stakeholder
leadership, roles, and responsibilities. Combining multiple stakeholders to deliver a demand
flexibility program can increase program management complexity and may result in less clearly
defined stakeholder roles and responsibilities, making it challenging to ensure partners are
aligned throughout the entire program life cycle. This may result in duplication of efforts or
program delivery blind spots. One of the program implementers interviewed described their
stakeholder network as one with many rapidly evolving partner strategies, including energy
management technologies, aggregators, utilities, and building operators, to name a few.

Stakeholders with different business models and goals require effective relationship
management. Orchestrating stakeholder relationships to deliver a successful program can be
challenging, particularly considering partners’ evolving business models. For instance, ensuring
stakeholders working together in a demand flexibility program agree on the priority and timing
of key program delivery activities can be challenging. This can be driven by internal decision-
making processes. Partner priorities may also be impacted by changing regulatory
requirements and policy goals. A program implementer shared that the challenge of managing
complex stakeholder groups can be greater for programs without a risk mitigation plan, such as
a plan for how to manage a stakeholder not performing their agreed role or abandoning the
program. Another program implementer, focused on a university campus, shared the challenge
of ensuring all the stakeholders understand the program value proposition within and across
partners. In this case, an initial lack of understanding of the program value proposition led to
not having the necessary stakeholder participate in decision-making, slowing progress.

3.2 Stakeholder readiness
Gap between vendor product capabilities and program requirements. Demand flexibility
programs aiming to implement novel approaches may face limited market response from
vendors, potentially signaling gaps in the market. For example, a program looking to procure an
energy management information system to enable demand flexibility across buildings (U.S.
DOE, 2021), may face a gap between its program design (e.g., technology package, control
architecture) and the capabilities of existing vendor offerings. As a result, program
implementers may have to dedicate greater efforts to engage in conversations with vendors to
gather information, identify any gaps, and determine their impact on the program, which may
include having to adjust the program design (e.g., reducing the types of DERs included in the
program or reducing the complexity of control strategies to reduce complexity) and value
proposition (e.g., reducing the number of strategies to be implemented to optimize grid services
with occupant comfort) (U.S. DOE, 2019).

Program partners may lack the capabilities needed to deliver demand flexibility
programs. Contractors, third-party program managers, and utilities may lack demand flexibility-
related capabilities. In some cases, these stakeholders may have historical experience with
energy efficiency programs, but not with programs implementing energy efficiency, demand
response, and other distributed energy resources. This may result in initial gaps in capabilities
that can affect program success.

3.3 Stakeholder collaboration
Unplanned external changes may affect the program's scope. Demand flexibility programs
can be impacted by external changes that require changes to program scope or feasibility. For
example, a program implementer launching a program for new construction shared their
experience with a change in building layout that impacted the program scope. The program
implementer had selected rooftop solar panels as part of their technologies to include in the
program. However, the building developer changed the layout of the roof area in a way that
reduced the space available and led to smaller panels being installed. In another case, a
program implementer was acquired by another company in the early stages of program
development, which resulted in changes to organizational priorities and reduced resources
available for the program.

Risk of partner underperformance requires the development of mitigation strategies.
Program partners may be responsible for performing critical program activities, such as
demand flexibility event management across a portfolio of distributed energy resources or
producing accurate forecasts of demand flexibility potential. These activities can have a direct
impact on the reliability of the program services. Program implementers may have to implement
risk mitigation strategies to address underperforming partners, which may require adapting the
program’s scope and value proposition.

4. Lessons learned
The list below highlights lessons learned related to demand flexibility stakeholder ecosystem
informed by program implementer experiences.

4.1 Stakeholder communication and alignment
Define and monitor stakeholder roles through the life cycle of the program. Program
effectiveness can benefit from clearly defining stakeholder roles and responsibilities. This can
be done by identifying the capabilities necessary to deliver a successful program and assessing
the ability of each program partner's skills and resources to deliver those needs. Program
implementers can follow this approach to determine partner strengths and allocate
responsibilities. This can also support the identification of capability gaps across stakeholders
and inform mitigating actions. Additionally, program implementers can implement a process to
monitor program and stakeholder capabilities throughout the program’s duration to identify any
changes in program needs (e.g., resulting from changes in program design, such as
technologies being installed, or customer segments targeted) and in partner skills and
resources (e.g., resulting from change in partner strategy or business model).

Identify and foster synergistic stakeholder relationships. When multiple stakeholders
collaborate to deliver a complex program, each partner must understand its role in the program
and resulting benefits. During the program team formation stage, program implementers can
prioritize identifying partners with complementary goals, skills, and resources, to establish
synergies that foster value creation. For example, a non-utility program implementer discussed
the value of partnering with a local utility to support technology implementation and program
operations. Another program implementer discussed that the value of the utility partnership
may depend on the customer experience with their local utility. Fostering value-creating
stakeholder partnerships can contribute to continued commitment to the program across
partners, supporting program success and scaling. One of the program implementers
interviewed shared the experience of partners losing interest when the benefits of participating
in the program were unclear, increasing the risk of a partner abandoning the program.

4.2 Stakeholder readiness
Develop a standardized program delivery process. Program implementers can support
stakeholder management and program success by clearly characterizing each phase of
program delivery. This can include key milestones to be achieved, and identify the resources
needed and stakeholder responsibilities in each phase. A standardized program delivery
process can contribute to effective stakeholder collaboration by reducing the need to design a
bespoke program delivery process for each program, particularly relevant to support scaling
(Long & Frick, 2025). Additionally, standardization efforts can contribute to improved cost-
effectiveness. Standardization can yield benefits throughout the program life cycle. For
example, standardizing program operations can increase trust in demand flexibility programs to
provide reliable grid services. Text Box 4 describes opportunities and recent progress to
standardize virtual power plant operations.

Establish a vendor engagement strategy to support market-program alignment. Program
implementers can implement a vendor engagement strategy that includes sufficient time for
iterative engagements with both prospective and selected vendors. This can support the
development of a collaborative relationship to align on the capabilities needed by the program
and those available by the vendor to support the use cases and technologies.

4.3 Stakeholder collaboration
Develop contingency plans to manage stakeholder risks. Program implementers can
identify actions to minimize and manage stakeholder risks. For example, identify the impact of
partners abandoning the program and develop feasible strategies to minimize the impact. This
may include reallocating responsibilities across partners, when feasible, or adjusting the
program scope, and identifying the impact on the value proposition and customer experience.

Allocate adequate resources for contracting and legal issues. Establishing partnerships
across stakeholders can require complex contracting processes that benefit from legal expert
support. Program implementers can consider this as part of their program development timeline
and determine resources needed.

Establish a program governance structure compatible with partner goals and priorities.
Stakeholder partnerships can be structured following different governance approaches. For
instance, program partners may establish a new entity as a joint venture to pool resources and
implement demand flexibility programs as one organization. Alternatively, partners may prefer a
more flexible approach by contracting to provide services or technologies to the program
implementer. Program implementers can consult prospective partners to identify a governance
structure that balances goals and priorities and contributes to program success. Implementing
a governance structure compatible with partner goals and priorities can contribute to ensure
partners remain committed to program success.

Text Box 4. Standardizing virtual power plant operations
 The U.S. Department of Energy in its 2023 “Pathways to Commercial Liftoff: Virtual Power
 Plants” report emphasized the importance of standardizing virtual power plant operations. In
 some cases, virtual power plants lack standards for communication, data collection, product
 certification, and distributed energy resource interconnection. Additionally, virtual power
 plants have been slow to implement cybersecurity measures (U.S. DOE, 2023).

 Establishing industry standards can mitigate existing challenges, including the perception
 that virtual power plants are less reliable than traditional assets due to significant variability in
 forecasting and evaluation methods, as well as publicly available data.

 • Opportunities to implement standards include developing and implementing:
 • Open-source distributed energy resource and virtual power plant analytical tools,
 such as modelling tools to develop forecasts and manage performance
 • Industry-wide service agreements, including performance guarantees
 • Distribution system reliability standards, designed in collaboration with industry and
 regulators
 • Distributed energy resource interconnection and data standards
 • Cybersecurity measures aligned with industry-leading practices
