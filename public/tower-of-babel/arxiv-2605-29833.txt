OmniMatBench : A Human-Calibrated Multimodal Reasoning Benchmark Across 19 Materials Science Subfields
Wanhao Liu, Jiaqing Xie, Qian Tan, Weida Wang, Jue Wang, Ran Sun, Zhuo Yang, Wanli Ouyang, Lei Bai, Tianfan Fu, Lu Chen, Xin Chen, Yuqiang Li

Abstract
As multimodal language models play an increasingly important role in scientific research, materials science offers a critical testbed due to its interdisciplinary, multimodal, and application-driven nature. However, existing materials benchmarks mainly focus on property prediction, knowledge QA, or characterization understanding, leaving the broader reasoning process from materials knowledge to application underexplored. To fill this gap, we present OmniMatBench , a human-calibrated multimodal reasoning benchmark for materials science. OmniMatBench contains 3,171 expert-curated QA and calculation problems across 19 materials-science subfields, spanning fundamental materials knowledge, structural and engineering materials, materials processing and manufacturing, and functional and applied materials. We evaluate 13 open-source and closed-source MLLMs and find that the best model achieves only a 0.372 overall score, revealing a substantial gap in current materials-science reasoning. Further analysis shows strong variation across subfields, fixed reasoning heuristics, uneven materials knowledge, and limited high-level knowledge application under formula-, retrieval-, and code-assisted settings. OmniMatBench provides crucial insights into the capabilities and limitations of current MLLMs and establishes a foundation for reliable AI assistants in materials-science research.

Dataset and Tasks

3.1
Data Collection and Processing

Figure 2:

Overview of the OmniMatBench data construction pipeline.

OmniMatBench is constructed through a multi-stage, expert-involved data construction pipeline in collaboration with materials-science specialists. As shown in Figure
2
, the source materials are selected from classical and used materials knowledge resources, and the resulting problems are organized to cover 19 subfields aligned with the Knowledge–Structure–Processing–Application perspective.

Experts first select, refine, and complete the domain knowledge from the source materials, and then classify each candidate problem into its corresponding subfield. For each problem, they identify the core formulas, common error types, and key concepts based on their domain expertise and experience, and distill them into scoring-oriented key points. Meanwhile, the complete problem provenance, adaptation rationale, and solution logic are organized into the corresponding proof file. The preliminary item pool and proof files are then checked by an LLM verifier to ensure consistency between the question and its visual, tabular, or formula inputs, agreement between the solution and the proof file, formatting correctness, redundancy control, and overall item quality. Items that pass this stage are further reviewed by materials-domain experts, who verify disciplinary relevance, formula correctness, reasoning validity, and answer consistency. Only samples that pass both automatic verification and expert validation are included in the final OmniMatBench.

3.2
Dataset Statistics

OmniMatBench contains 3,171 expert-curated questions across 19 materials-science categories, spanning four major groups:
Fundamental Materials Science
(FMS),
Structural and Engineering Materials
(SEM),
Materials Processing and Manufacturing
(MPM), and
Functional and Applied Materials
(FAM).
To address the limited coverage of specialized engineering topics in existing benchmarks
Weng et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Wu et al. 2025
, OmniMatBench includes underrepresented but engineering-critical subfields, such as gem materials and gemology, powder materials, welding technology and engineering, and nanomaterials and technology.
These areas often require specialized domain knowledge, engineering experience, and expert-verified solution procedures, making them difficult to curate at scale.
We therefore collaborate with materials-domain experts to systematically collect and supplement representative problems from these important subfields.

The benchmark includes 1,261 open-ended QA questions and 1,910 calculation (CAL) questions.
QA items are annotated with expert-curated key points covering core concepts, expected reasoning elements, and scoring criteria for rubric-based evaluation.
CAL items include structured answer slots for evaluating numerical answers, formulas, units, and output formatting.
Detailed category statistics are provided in Appendix Table
7
.

Annotation team.

The benchmark was constructed by a team of 8 doctoral researchers, 60 trained annotators, and 5 materials-domain professors, responsible for domain-level verification, formula validation, and ambiguity resolution.

Difficulty distribution.

Each problem is assigned an expert-labeled difficulty level: easy, medium, or hard.
Experts determine the difficulty based on the required domain knowledge, reasoning depth, multimodal interpretation, and calculation complexity.

Metrics.

OmniMatBench includes rubric-based QA and calculation-oriented CAL tasks.

For QA, each response is evaluated against expert-curated key points and the gold-standard answer.
We compute Precision, Recall, and F
1
:

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{\mathrm{correct}}}{N_{\mathrm{pred}}},\quad R=\frac{N_{\mathrm{correct}}}{N_{\mathrm{gold}}},\quad F_{1}=\frac{2PR}{P+R}.

(1)

Here,
N_{\mathrm{correct}}
is the number of correctly covered key points,
N_{\mathrm{pred}}
is the number of valid points in the model response, and
N_{\mathrm{gold}}
is the number of expert-annotated key points.
Recall measures completeness, while precision measures correctness and penalizes unsupported or irrelevant claims.

For CAL questions, each answer is parsed into structured slots and evaluated using strict exact-slot accuracy:

{\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{slot}}=\frac{1}{\sum_{i}|S_{i}|}\sum_{i}\sum_{j\in S_{i}}\mathbf{1}[\hat{z}_{ij}=z_{ij}].}

(2)

Here,
\hat{z}_{ij}=\mathrm{norm}(\hat{y}_{ij})
and
z_{ij}=\mathrm{norm}(y_{ij})
denote the normalized (standard) prediction and reference for slot
j
of item
i
. The overall score is computed as the equally weighted average of CAL exact-slot accuracy and QA Macro-F
1
:

{\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{overall}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{Acc}^{\mathrm{CAL}}_{\mathrm{slot}}+\mathrm{F}^{\mathrm{QA}}_{1,\mathrm{macro}}\right).}

(3)

5
Analysis

We further analyze model failures from three perspectives: representative case studies, formula/retrieval assistance, and code execution.

5.1
Case Studies: Failures in Materials-Specific Reasoning

Figure
5
presents three representative failure cases from different OmniMat domains.
These cases show that model errors are not limited to missing final answers, but often involve incorrect reasoning paths, incomplete materials-process understanding, and failures in scientific execution.

Case 1: Correct Answer with Incorrect Materials Reasoning.

The first case comes from Fundamental Materials Science and asks the model to select the best elastic-hinge material from an Ashby plot under given constraints on yield strength and elastic modulus.
The correct solution is a fixed-property Ashby plot selection: the model should match the given
\sigma_{y}
and
E
values to the closest material region and select PE.
However, the model over-applies a common material-index formulation for elastic-hinge optimization and derives an unnecessary criterion before reaching the final answer.
This reveals a tendency of MLLMs to follow familiar materials-design heuristics rather than adapt their reasoning to the task.
Importantly, the expert-curated key points explicitly indicate that this is not an MI optimization problem, allowing OmniMatBench to penalize superficially correct answers with incorrect reasoning.

Figure 5:
Representative error cases in OmniMatBench.
The examples show failures across the knowledge-to-execution process, including wrong material-selection criteria, missing device mechanisms, graph-reading errors, and incorrect numerical execution.

Case 2: Generic Materials-Processing Knowledge Instead of Process-Specific Understanding.

The second case is from Materials Processing and Manufacturing and requires understanding the structure and operation of a twin-screw extrusion–injection device from the figure.

6
Conclusion

We presented
OmniMatBench
, a human-calibrated multimodal reasoning benchmark for materials science. It contains 3,171 expert-curated problems across 19 materials-science subfields, spanning fundamental materials knowledge, structural and engineering materials, materials processing and manufacturing, and functional and applied materials.
OmniMatBench
evaluates MLLMs through rubric-based QA and calculation-oriented CAL tasks, covering materials understanding and scientific execution. We systematically evaluated 13 open-source and closed-source MLLMs, showing that the benchmark remains far from saturated: models perform better on common materials concepts but still struggle with specialized engineering scenarios, process-specific mechanisms, formula-plan selection, visual-parameter grounding, and structured scientific computation. These findings highlight the gap between scientific fluency and reliable materials-science reasoning and execution.

Limitations

Although
OmniMatBench
covers 3,171 expert-curated problems across 19 materials-science subfields, it remains a bounded sample of materials-science reasoning. We screened and balanced problems as much as possible while preserving evaluation quality, but variations in available domain knowledge still lead to some imbalance in question numbers and topic coverage. Some underrepresented subfields, such as nanomaterials and technology, and gem materials and gemology, are often overlooked by existing materials benchmarks but remain important for materials research and engineering applications. We therefore include these areas while maintaining the best possible balance under available high-quality sources.

OmniMatBench
focuses on expert-curated QA and calculation-oriented CAL tasks with reference solutions, rubrics, formula traces, and structured answer slots. QA scoring key points are cross-validated by materials-domain experts to improve consistency and reduce ambiguity. However, these expert-defined rubrics may still reflect subjective judgments and may underweight valid reasoning elements outside the annotated criteria. Future work can extend
OmniMatBench
to richer scientific modalities and more flexible evaluation methods.

Ethical considerations

OmniMatBench was constructed with the participation of 73 materials-domain annotators and validators, including domain experts and trained graduate-level contributors.
All contributors were informed of the purpose of the project and the intended use of their annotations for research and benchmark construction.
They were compensated according to local institutional standards and the amount of work completed.
The annotation process involved problem extraction, domain adaptation, proof-file construction, key-point annotation, and expert validation.
No personal or sensitive information about contributors is released, and the benchmark does not contain private user data.
The source materials were used only to construct expert-curated problem–solution pairs and evaluation rubrics, and all released items are reviewed to ensure scientific correctness, answer consistency, and appropriate use for academic evaluation.
We further apply both LLM-based verification and human expert validation to reduce noise, redundancy, and potential annotation errors.

Appendix D
Detailed Benchmark Analysis

OmniMatBench evaluates not only whether a model can answer a materials question, but also whether it can complete a case-based reasoning work-up: identify the relevant operation, apply it to the current problem, and output the result in the required answer slots. We therefore evaluate several inference-time assistance settings while keeping the benchmark questions, multimodal inputs, answer-slot constraints, and evaluation metrics unchanged.

Zero-shot inference

In the zero-shot setting, the model receives the problem statement, optional image or CSV input, and the final-answer schema. The model is instructed to place its reasoning in a
<think>
block and to output only the final filled answer structure inside
<answer>...</answer>
. If a
final_answer_format
is provided, the number, order, and grouping of output slots must exactly match the schema.

Formula hypergraph.

For calculation problems, we extract formula traces from reference solutions and organize them into a formula hypergraph. Each formula instance is represented as a hyperedge connecting input variables, intermediate quantities, and answer targets. Formula records also store slot notes, formula meanings, variable definitions, and source metadata when available. This representation allows retrieval at the level of solution operations rather than isolated equations.

Question-only formula retrieval.

In the non-oracle retrieval setting, the model receives formula candidates retrieved using only the current question text. Each candidate target document is built from the corresponding question, slot notes, and formula strings. We rank candidate targets using lexical overlap:

s(q,d)=\frac{|T(q)\cap T(d)|}{|T(q)\cup T(d)|}+0.01|T(q)\cap T(d)|,

(4)

where
T(q)
and
T(d)
are token sets for the query and candidate document. Retrieved formulas are presented as candidate solution templates, not as ground truth. The prompt explicitly instructs the model to verify whether each formula matches the current problem, variables, units, and rounding constraints before using it.

Oracle formulas and distractors.

We also evaluate controlled formula assistance. In the oracle-formula setting, the model receives the ground-truth formula traces for the current problem as method guidance, but it must still map variables, compute values, handle units, and satisfy the answer schema. In the oracle-with-distractors setting, the ground-truth formula set is hidden among distractor formula sets. Exactly one candidate set is correct, but the correct label is not revealed. This setting separates formula-plan recognition from numerical execution.

Two-round code augmentation.

For tool-augmented inference, we use a two-round protocol. In the first round, the model is required to generate Python code for solving the problem. We execute the generated code locally in a temporary working directory with a timeout, and then append the execution output to the conversation. In the second round, the model uses the execution result to produce the final answer in the same
<answer>
format as zero-shot inference. The prompt instructs the model to use only the Python standard library and avoid interactive input, network calls, and file writes. We also evaluate an oracle-formula code setting, where the ground-truth formulas are provided before code generation.

E.1
Overall Difficulty-wise CAL Analysis

Figure
7
summarizes the overall CAL strict-slot accuracy under different difficulty levels.
The most salient observation is the large gap between easy examples and non-easy examples.
Across all evaluated models, easy accuracy remains substantially higher, ranging from 28.4% to 39.7%.
In contrast, medium and hard accuracy are much lower, with most scores below 8% and all scores below 11%.
For example, Gemini 3.1 Pro achieves the highest easy accuracy of 39.7%, but its medium and hard accuracies drop to 7.6% and 9.4%, respectively.
A similar pattern appears for GPT-5.5 and Qwen3.6 Plus, whose easy accuracies are above 35%, while their medium and hard accuracies remain around 6–7%.
This suggests that current MLLMs can handle simpler CAL instances to some extent, but their strict slot-level reliability drops sharply once examples require more complex grounding, finer-grained slot selection, or less explicit evidence.

A second observation is that the difficulty trend is not strictly monotonic between medium and hard examples.
For most models, hard accuracy is slightly higher than medium accuracy; Intern-S1 Pro is the clearest case, obtaining 6.0% on medium examples but 10.9% on hard examples.
This does not imply that hard examples are intrinsically easier.
Rather, it suggests that strict-slot CAL difficulty is affected not only by the nominal difficulty label, but also by the distribution of categories, slot types, and textual cues within each split.
Some hard examples may contain more distinctive domain signals, while medium examples may involve more ambiguous slot boundaries or require more precise category-specific normalization.
Therefore, the difficulty-wise results indicate that CAL primarily exposes a robustness gap in structured slot grounding, rather than a simple monotonic degradation from easy to hard.

To ensure domain coverage and annotation quality, we involved a mixed team of materials-science annotators and validators, including 8 PhD students, 60 postgraduate and undergraduate students, and 5 materials-domain professors. Undergraduate and postgraduate annotators participated in problem collection, formatting, and preliminary consistency checks, while PhD students and professors were responsible for domain-level verification, formula validation, solution checking, and resolving ambiguous cases. This multi-stage process allowed us to combine scalable data construction with expert oversight.

Constrained Human Baseline.

OmniMatBench
. The sampled questions were drawn across both QA and CAL tasks and were balanced across the four coarse-grained materials-science groups whenever possible. Participants were given the same problem statements, multimodal inputs, and answer-format requirements as the evaluated models, but were not allowed to use LLM assistance. For QA questions, their responses were scored using the same expert-curated key-point rubrics as model responses. For CAL questions, their answers were parsed into the same structured answer slots and evaluated with the same strict exact-slot accuracy. We report the average score across the two participants as the human reference baseline.

This reference is intended to contextualize the strictness of the evaluation protocol, not to estimate an upper bound on expert materials-science performance. Participants solved the problems under constrained conditions without LLM assistance or external computational tools, and their answers were evaluated with the same strict slot and formatting requirements used for model outputs.
