Perioperative Applications of Electrical Stimulation in Flap Surgeries: Mechanisms and Benefits
Authors: Meijun Jiang, Shaoyun Zhang, Cong Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVE:To review the roles and applications of electrical stimulation in flap transplantation, addressing its potential to reduce flap necrosis.DATA SOURCES:Medical textbooks and a literature search were conducted in databases including PubMed, Google Scholar, Web of Science, and relevant surgical journals.STUDY SELECTION:Articles were screened using keywords including electrical stimulation, flap transplantation, angiogenesis, vasodilation, antibacterial, vasopasm, inflammation, wound healing, cell migration, and cell proliferation. Articles in which the title and/or abstract contained these key words were included in the review. More than 120 journal research articles met the criteria.DATA EXTRACTION:Research highlighted the benefits and molecular mechanisms of electrical stimulation on the healing progress of flap transplantation, which underwent a full-text review and summarization.DATA SYNTHESIS:Classify and descriptively summarize the articles based on perioperative factors closely related to flap survival. The mechanisms underlying these benefits are discussed, alongside their clinical significance.CONCLUSIONS:Preoperative electrical stimulation fosters angiogenesis and new blood vessel growth, whereas postoperative stimulation aids in infection control, edema reduction, and overall wound healing. Electrical stimulation shows significant promise in improving outcomes of flap surgeries.

INTRODUCTION

Flap transplantation is a widely used clinical technique for repairing complex soft tissue defects caused by cancer, trauma, congenital abnormalities, and other conditions. However, partial or total flap necrosis remains a major complication, with reported rates of 6.5% to 11.6% and 2.7% to 5.5% for partial and complete flap necrosis.1–3 Therefore, improving flap survival rates is a critical issue that demands urgent attention in the field of flap surgery.

Despite ongoing research, no significant breakthroughs have been made regarding the use of drugs to prevent flap necrosis. This challenge arises from the complex pathological and physiological changes that occur after flap transplantation, including phases of microcirculatory contraction, expansion, and exhaustion. Most drugs often target one of the many factors involved, making them insufficient to fully address the pathological and physiological processes associated with flap necrosis.

Electrical stimulation (ES)4 is widely employed by rehabilitation therapists to promote the healing of chronic wounds,5,6 foster peripheral nerve regeneration,7–9 facilitate spinal cord injury recovery,10–14 and enhance bone healing,15,16 among other applications. Recent studies indicate that ES may play a significant role in promoting flap survival. By reviewing recent literature on the effects of ES on tissue repair and regeneration, the authors aim to elucidate the benefits of ES for flap viability and its underlying mechanisms, ultimately proposing a novel perioperative management strategy to improve flap transplantation survival area in clinical practice.

METHODS

The authors focused on several factors closely related to flap transplantation viability: (1) factors controlling skin blood flow, (2) influences on capillary fluid exchange, and (3) factors affecting neovascular ingrowth into the flap. To this end, authors used keywords such as “electrical stimulation,” “skin flap,” “vasodilation,” “angiogenesis,” “vascular endothelial growth factor,” “endothelial nitric oxide synthase,” “calcitonin gene-related peptide,” “vasospasm,” “edema control,” “cell migration,” “antibacterial effects,” and “wound healing” for literature searches. The primary database searched was PubMed, supplemented by Web of Science and Google Scholar. After reviewing article titles and abstracts, all relevant studies, including clinical trials, animal studies, and cellular experiments, were included. Over 130 articles met the criteria, excluding outdated review articles not indexed by the Science Citation Index, resulting in a final summary of 64 relevant studies in this review.

RESULTS

In 1529, Scribonius Largus, a physician to a Roman Emperor, first documented the use of a live torpedo fish placed on the scalp to alleviate headaches.17 This fish has previously been reported to generate curative effects by its electric discharges.18 In the following centuries, ES is increasingly recognized and accepted as a valuable therapeutic modality. ES includes various types based on different current forms, locations, and treatment methods (Supplemental Table, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/NSW/A275).

Preoperation

Blood supply status at the donor site is critical for the survival area of the transplanted flap before flap surgery. Research highlights that flap necrosis often occurs in the choke zones between adjacent angiosomes.19,20 If the connecting vessels between adjacent angiosomes are reduced-caliber choke anastomosis vessels, preliminary procedures may be needed to convert them into true anastomotic vessels to improve the flap survival area. Currently, flap surgical delay is recognized as an effective treatment modality for dilating choke vessels and promoting neovascularization, although it requires a secondary surgery. ES, due to its noninvasive nature and ability to regulate blood flow and angiogenesis, presents a promising approach for preconditioning flaps.

Promotion of Vasodilation

Skin microcirculatory blood flow can be affected by various factors, including vascular smooth muscle tension,21 endothelial cell-mediated vascular contraction and relaxation response,22,23 vascular movement reflex triggered by venous pressure, neural regulation, fluid regulation, and metabolic factor regulation.24,25

ES enhances the release of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)26 from nerve endings, which leads to vasodilation by increasing cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) levels in endothelial cells and inhibiting the vasoconstrictor endothelin-1 (ET-1). ES also triggers the phosphorylation of neuronal nitric oxide synthase27 and endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS),28 leading to the production of nitric oxide (NO) that facilitates smooth muscle relaxation and regulates vascular tension. In addition, ES reduces sympathetic nerve activity, decreases vascular contraction, alleviates ischemia, and improves the viability of skin flaps.29 A recent study by Kapela et al30 used a detailed computational model to examine the conductive properties of intact microvascular endothelium. The results indicated that electrical signals transmitted through the endothelial layer play a crucial role in the axial propagation of vasodilation. This mechanism may also contribute to the regulatory effects of ES on vascular tone.

Promoting Neovascularization in Skin Flaps

Promoting Angiogenesis

Angiogenesis is the process by which new blood vessels are formed from preexisting capillaries or postcapillary venules. It involves the proliferation and migration of endothelial cells and the formation of tubular structures. This process is intricate and carefully regulated. Angiogenesis is vital for flap survival. Inadequate angiogenesis can cause flap ischemic necrosis. ES has been demonstrated to promote vascularization in the embryonic mesoderm,31 and in vitro experiments have shown that human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) can respond effectively to ES, forming vascular-like tubes in a 3-dimensional culture system.32

Recent research has clarified how ES promotes angiogenesis. Geng et al33 exposed fibroblasts and HUVECs to ES at 50 and 200 mV/mm. They observed a significant increase in the proliferation of both cell types. In addition, when HUVECs were cultured with a conditioned medium from ES-treated fibroblasts, ES could promote fibroblasts to secrete fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2). FGF2 then activated the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/extracellular regulated protein kinases (ERK) signaling pathway in HUVECs, resulting in the upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor-A expression and enhanced formation of functional vascular tubes, thereby promoting angiogenesis.

In addition to the previously discussed pathways, Chen et al32 demonstrated that ES can directly enhance vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression in 3D cultures of endothelial cells exposed to an external direct current electric field stimulation (EFS). After ES treatment, activation of vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-2, serine/threonine kinase (AKT), extracellular regulated protein kinases 1/2 (ERK 1/2), and c-Jun NH2-terminal kinase were observed, indicating that ES promotes angiogenesis through the VEGF-VEGFR2-AKT signaling pathway.

Promoting the Ingrowth of New Blood Vessels

The formation of new blood vessels in flaps largely relies on the directed migration of endothelial cells.34 This migration is a key feature of angiogenesis and an early step in blood vessel formation. Studies have demonstrated that electric fields ranging from 150 to 400 mV/mm can induce several physical changes in endothelial cells, including reorientation of cell axes, cell elongation, changes in cell shape, and directed cell migration. These alterations are closely linked to angiogenesis and the formation of new blood vessels.

Cunha et al34 found that electric fields can stimulate the release of interleukin (IL) 8. IL-8 and stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1) exhibit strong binding affinity to the chemokine receptors C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CXCR2) and C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4), respectively. This binding activates the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt signaling pathway and the Ras homolog gene (Rho)/Rho-associated coiled coil-forming protein kinase (ROCK) signaling pathway. These pathways promote cell migration, elongation, and orientation by reorganizing the actin cytoskeleton.

Phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) serves as a negative regulator of cell migration. Studies have indicated a substantial reduction in PTEN expression in different cells following EFS, leading to improved cell migration. Yan et al35 found that EFS-induced PTEN down-regulation is closely associated with the process of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT). EMT is a biological process where epithelial cells transform into cells with a mesenchymal phenotype through a specific program. To promote effective skin wound repair, keratinocyte cells located at the wound edge undergo EMT, leading to the loss of epithelial characteristics and the acquisition of mesenchymal features and migratory properties.

Based on the above discussion, it is evident that ES holds promise as an effective preoperative treatment modality in the field of flap surgery, ultimately enhancing the flap survival area (Figure 1).

PRECONDITIONING FLAP DONORS WITH ELECTRICAL STIMULATION CAN ENHANCE THE BLOOD SUPPLY AND PREVENT NECROSIS Before flap transplantation, preconditioning flap donors with electrical stimulation can enhance the blood supply to the flap and prevent necrosis following transplantation. First, electrical stimulation increases the levels of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), and nitric oxide (NO) in tissues while reducing the level of endothelin-1 (ET-1), resulting in vasodilation. Second, electrical stimulation promotes the proliferation, migration, and tube formation of vascular endothelial cells by upregulating the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which is crucial for angiogenesis. In addition, electrical stimulation fosters the growth of new blood vessels by upregulating the expression of interleukin-8 (IL-8), downregulating phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN), and enhancing cellular migration.

Postoperation

After flap transplantation, risks including vascular spasm, infection, arterial and venous crisis, and ischemia-reperfusion injury can result in flap necrosis.

Alleviating Vasospasm

Vasospasm, which could lead to ischemic damage and necrosis of the flap, often occurs during and within 48 hours after transplantation. Therefore, it is essential to mitigate vasospasm during surgery.

Pain is a primary factor contributing to the occurrence of vasospasm. Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) is a widely used clinical pain relief method, in which electrodes are placed on the skin surface to stimulate peripheral nerve fibers through electrical pulses. For instance, TENS can effectively promote wound healing and decrease the dosage of analgesic medications when applied postoperatively in acute trauma patients.36 Moreover, TENS can significantly reduce delivery time and alleviate maternal pain during childbirth.37

Regarding the pain-relief mechanism of TENS, some researchers proposed that the analgesic effect of low-frequency and high-intensity TENS mainly occurs by activating small-diameter nociceptive fibers (Aδ and C fibers), resulting in the release of opioid substances in the central nervous system, thereby reducing pain. In contrast, high-frequency and low-intensity TENS stimulates Aβ fibers while inhibiting the activation of nociceptive fibers (Aδ and C fibers) within the same neural segment, resulting in an analgesic effect.38 In addition, the effectiveness of TENS is affected by factors such as electrode placement, treatment parameters, pain types, and the physical condition of the patients.

Edema Control

During flap transplantation, the cut-off of sympathetic nerve fibers leads to the loss of nerve supply and disrupts normal vascular tension in the flap’s blood vessels. This causes vascular dilation, increased perfusion, and increased crystalloid osmotic pressure in the capillaries. In addition, when the flap is elevated, the tissue interstitial pressure decreases while the release of inflammatory factors increases capillary permeability. These changes disrupt the balance of fluid exchange between the capillaries and interstitial fluid, leading to fluid accumulation in the interstitium and the development of edema. Failure to control edema can cause compression of the vascular system by the accumulated fluid in the tissue interstitium, thereby exacerbating circulatory disorders and tissue ischemia.

To investigate the effectiveness of ES in reducing edema, Nakato et al39 induced abdominal edema in rats by injecting 0.9 mL of water-soluble contrast agent into the abdominal cavity. The treatment group received high-voltage electrical stimulation (HVES) with specific parameters: pulse amplitude at 90% of the animal’s movement threshold, frequency of 120 Hz, and pulse duration of 12.8 μs. The electrodes were positioned on the upper and lower abdomen to target the contrast agent nodule. The rats were subjected to continuous stimulation for 1 hour, and x-ray images were taken at 30-minute intervals during the 90 minutes following the initiation of stimulation, and for an additional half hour after the stimulation ended. At the 90th minute, the ES group exhibited a significant reduction in edema compared with the control group (P = .055). However, most cases of pathological edema involve inflammatory factors, whereas the edema model used in Nakato and colleagues’ study did not include inflammation. Therefore, the reliability of their conclusion remains uncertain. Ibey et al40 demonstrated that exposure to an electric field can reduce membrane electroporation in cells, thereby reducing the permeability of endothelial cells and minimizing fluid leakage into the tissue space.

Infection Control

Infection is a common complication after surgery, and prolonged wound infection can lead to flap necrosis. As far back as 1919, a report described the use of alternating current for milk disinfection.

The antimicrobial effects of ES are believed to arise from several proposed mechanisms. First, metal electrodes used in ES can release metal ions that disrupt the bacterial division process, resulting in bactericidal effects, a phenomenon known as the ion penetration effect. In addition, electric currents can increase the permeability of bacterial cell membranes, enhancing the penetration of antibiotics and facilitating their transport and absorption, thus potentially reducing the required dosage of antibiotics, referred to as the bioelectric effect. Furthermore, ES can cause movement of membrane proteins in bacteria, altering cell membrane structure, affecting the pH in the vicinity of the electrode, influencing bacterial metabolic activity, and producing electrolytic products such as hydrogen peroxide. These effects contribute to bactericidal activity and infection control by inducing cell membrane leakage and other antimicrobial actions.41

However, not all forms of ES have antimicrobial effects. Merriman et al42 found that anodic and cathodic direct current (DC) and high-voltage pulsed galvanic stimulation (HVPGS) exhibited antimicrobial effects, whereas low-voltage monophasic pulse current and low-voltage biphasic pulsed current did not demonstrate such properties. In addition, Barranco et al43 discovered that bactericidal effects were only observed with silver electrodes when exposed to weak DC of 0.4 and 4.0 mA. Thus, despite the numerous in vitro and in vivo experiments confirming the antimicrobial and infection control effects of ES, factors, including choice of electrodes, optimal waveform configurations, and parameter settings for the electrical current, still require further exploration.

In recent years, the medical field has witnessed significant advancements in tissue engineering biomaterials, leading to the emergence of wound dressings that integrate external ES, electrically responsive materials, and antimicrobial drug coatings. For instance, Croitoru et al44 used electrospinning to prepare a novel electrospun scaffold composed of polylactic acid/graphene oxide/quercetin. Antibacterial tests revealed a reduction rate of 41% to 76% for strains of Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Candida albicans. Overall, the application of external ES enhances the release rate and antimicrobial effects of drugs, whereas the use of biomaterials addresses the issues of electrode-tissue toxicity and biocompatibility. These 2 aspects complement each other and offer promising new strategies for wound dressing preparation, with the potential to address various challenges associated with postoperative infections.

Reducing Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury

During flap transplantation surgery, the skin flap inevitably experiences a period of ischemia or low perfusion due to its separation from the donor tissue. This leads to unavoidable ischemia/reperfusion injury (I/RI) and the subsequent release of inflammatory factors, which contribute to tissue cell apoptosis and endothelial cell damage, potentially resulting in flap necrosis. Therefore, it is crucial to mitigate inflammatory reactions and reduce I/RI to enhance flap viability.45

Previous studies have demonstrated that ES can enhance transcraniality, and electrical vagus nerve stimulation can reduce myocardial infarction size and fatal arrhythmias in myocardial I/RI.46 Wang et al47 further investigated the impact of ES on brain ischemia-reperfusion injury in rats in vivo. Using a transient middle cerebral artery occlusion rat model, they applied 20 Hz, 2 ms biphasic pulses of 100 μA current to the primary somatosensory cortex (S1 cortex) of rats for 30 minutes, starting from 30 minutes after reperfusion. The results indicated that S1 cortex ES significantly reduced infarct volume and improved function after brain ischemia-reperfusion, supporting the conclusion that S1 cortex ES can mitigate inflammation and cell apoptosis. This study provides strong evidence for the anti-inflammatory effect of ES in vivo, further supporting its protective effect against I/RI.

Promoting Wound Healing

Facilitating rapid wound healing and restoring skin barrier integrity are crucial for reconstructing the flap blood supply. The human body has an endogenous bioelectric system that supports the healing of bone fractures and soft tissue injuries. Bioelectricity accelerates wound healing by facilitating the migration of essential cells, thereby providing a theoretical basis for the clinical application of ES therapy.

Adunsky and Ohry48 conducted a study involving 63 patients with stage III pressure ulcers who had undergone debridement and dressing. Among them, 35 patients were randomly assigned to receive decubitus direct current treatment (DDCT), whereas the remaining 28 patients received placebo treatment for 45 days, followed by a 90-day follow-up period. The findings indicated healing rates of 44% and 14% in the DDCT and placebo groups, respectively. Mycielska and Djamgoz49 observed that the body generates endogenous direct current fields in the form of transepithelial potentials or neuronal field potentials. Various cells possess the characteristic of directed movement towards an electric field, known as galvanotaxis. Kloth50 demonstrated that neutrophils, macrophages, fibroblasts, and epithelial cells involved in wound repair exhibit positive or negative charges. When tissue repair requires the stimulation of autolysis, granulation tissue formation, anti-inflammatory activity, or re-epithelialization of the skin surface, ES can attract these cells to the injured tissue, thereby accelerating the healing process. In addition, exposure to low-intensity capacitive-coupled electric fields increased the synthesis rates of type II collagen, glycosaminoglycan, and type I collagen.51

In summary, ES can enhance flap survival area by alleviating vasospasm, reducing edema, antibacterial, promoting wound healing, and offering additional benefits (Figure 2). However, there is typically an inconsistency in the ES signals, including differences in the current type, waveform, and parameters. Thus, further research is required to standardize and optimize the clinical application of ES therapy.

SCHEMATIC ILLUSTRATION OF ELECTRICAL STIMULATION FOR IMPROVING FLAP SURVIVAL AFTER TRANSPLANTATION Postoperative complications, including infection, vasospasm, and edema-induced compression, can result in partial or complete flap necrosis. Various forms of electrical stimulation therapies have demonstrated potential in enhancing postoperative flap care by alleviating vasospasm, reducing edema, providing antimicrobial effects, and promoting wound healing.

DISCUSSION

Partial or total flap necrosis is a major cause of flap transplantation failure. Factors contributing to flap necrosis include infection, insufficient blood supply, high tension from edema, pain-induced vasospasm, and arterial/venous crisis. Loss of flap viability can substantially affect patients’ quality of life. Therefore, developing effective methods to improve flap survival remains a critical challenge in the field of plastic surgery.

The injury electric field in damaged tissues is a key factor in initiating tissue repair.52,53 ES can significantly enhance wound healing by delivering exogenous electrical signals to tissues, mimicking the natural bioelectric response of tissues to injury. Consequently, ES is commonly used for tissue regeneration and functional reconstruction.

Flap survival area is closely associated with factors such as blood flow, microcirculatory exchange of fluids across capillaries, and neovascularization within the graft. Animal studies have indicated that preconditioning with TENS at the skin graft donor site can significantly improve the survival area of transplanted skin grafts. These animal experiments demonstrated the potential of ES in the field of flap surgery (Table 1). Furthermore, ES promotes several beneficial effects: it promotes vasodilation by inhibiting sympathetic nervous tension, facilitates the release of CGRP from vascular tissue, and induces eNOS phosphorylation. In addition, ES can regulate skin graft blood flow by alleviating postoperative pain and improving vascular spasm. It also alleviates edema by regulating microcirculatory fluid exchange and decreasing endothelial cell permeability. In addition, ES enhances the pathological condition of skin grafts after transplantation by promoting neovascularization and integrating the graft. This is achieved through the activation of various signaling pathways, including MAPK/ERK, VEGF-VEGFR2-AKT, PI3K/AKT, and Rho/ROCK signaling pathways. Collectively, these signaling pathways promote cell proliferation while inhibiting apoptosis.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the heterogeneity of ES parameters (including current type, waveform, frequency, intensity, and duration) across studies makes it difficult to directly compare results or establish standardized clinical protocols. Second, most of the included studies are preclinical animal experiments or small-sample in vitro studies, whereas large-scale, randomized controlled clinical trials remain limited. Therefore, further well-designed, large-sample, and long-term follow-up clinical studies are needed to validate the efficacy and safety of electrical stimulation in flap surgery.

CONCLUSIONS

Overall, ES has demonstrated effectiveness in enhancing flap survival area before and after flap transplantation, providing significant benefits without notable side effects. ES is a highly promising adjunctive therapy in flap surgery.
