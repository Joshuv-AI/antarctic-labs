Chemotherapy After Colonic Stent in Patients With Unresectable Obstructive Colorectal Cancer: A Retrospective Multicenter Observational Study
Authors: Akihisa Matsuda, Norihiro Hanabata, Yorinobu Sumida, Naohiko Harada, Shinsuke Matsunaga, Gota Fujisawa, Koichi Miyahara, Akinari Takao, Hiroyuki Hisai, Toshio Kuwai, Shuji Saito, Shungo Endo, Yu Sunakawa, Takeshi Yamada, Hiroshi Yoshida, Hiroyuki Isayama, Yoshihisa Saida, Akihisa Matsuda, Akihisa Matsuda, Norihiro Hanabata, Yorinobu Sumida, Shinsuke Matsunaga, Gota Fujisawa, Akinari Takao, Hiroyuki Hisai, Toshio Kuwai, Shuji Saito, Shungo Endo, Hiroyuki Isayama, Yoshihisa Saida

ABSTRACT
Backgrounds and study aim Placement of a self-expandable metallic stent (SEMS) has become a common practice for obstructive colorectal cancer. However, the safety and efficacy of systemic chemotherapy after SEMS placement remain unclear. Patients and methods This multicenter retrospective cohort study was conducted by the Japan Colonic Stent Safe Procedure Group across Japan to investigate the SEMS-related complications and clinical outcomes of chemotherapy in patients with unresectable obstructive colorectal cancer. Results A total of 289 patients were included in the study. The median duration from SEMS placement to chemotherapy initiation was 11 days (range, 6–21 days). SEMS-related complications occurred in 30.1% of the patients, with the most common complications being perforation (8.7%; 95% CI, 5.9–12.5A%), re-obstruction (19.4%), and migration (20.8%). Among the patients with stent migration, 30% required additional intervention. The median progression-free survival (PFS) was 9.2 months (95% confidence interval [CI], 7.5–10.9 months), and the median overall survival (OS) was 17.3 months (95% CI, 14.1–20.5 months). No significant difference in perforation rate was observed with or without bevacizumab use (14.5% vs. 7.3%, P < 0.107). Factors influencing survival outcomes included the number of metastatic organs, presence of perforation, and performance of primary tumor resection (PTR). Survival analyses showed that patients who underwent PTR had significantly better PFS and OS; however, this result should be interpreted with caution due to potential selection bias and immortal time bias inherent in the retrospective design. Conclusions This multicenter retrospective study demonstrated that while chemotherapy after SEMS placement is generally feasible, clinicians should remain vigilant for complications like perforation, migration, and re-obstruction.

Introduction

The incidence of colorectal cancer in Japan has been increasing rapidly. 1 Among all patients with colorectal cancer, 7–29% present with acute colonic obstruction at initial diagnosis, 2 and approximately 35% of patients with obstructive colorectal cancer have advanced, unresectable disease (i.e., unresectable obstructive colorectal cancer [UROCC]). 3 Since the introduction of self-expandable metallic stents (SEMSs) for obstructive colorectal cancer in the Japanese national health insurance system in 2012, the use of these stents for palliative purposes has rapidly expanded. Owing to their favorable technical and clinical success rates, as well as their ability to maintain quality of life (QOL) by avoiding emergency surgery and stoma creation, SEMSs placement is strongly recommended in several guidelines, including those in Japan. 4 5

In recent years, systemic chemotherapy for unresectable or recurrent colorectal cancer has made remarkable progress. With the development of molecular-targeted agents, the median overall survival (OS) now exceeds 30 months. 6 Currently, the advancements in chemotherapy and the expanded use of SEMS placement have meant that these two treatment strategies are becoming increasingly intertwined. Nevertheless, there is a lack of robust global evidence regarding the safety and efficacy of systemic chemotherapy after SEMS placement.

Until recently, systemic chemotherapy for patients with UROCC could only be administered after emergency surgery, often including stoma creation. Compared with emergency surgery, colorectal stenting offers the advantages of being less invasive, avoiding stoma-related QOL deterioration, and allowing early initiation of systemic chemotherapy. 7 However, concerns remain regarding the risk of SEMS-related complications, including intestinal perforations associated with anti-angiogenic agents such as bevacizumab. 8 Therefore, evaluations of the safety and efficacy of systemic chemotherapy after SEMS placement are of great clinical importance.

Although several retrospective studies and meta-analyses have investigated the safety of systemic chemotherapy after SEMS placement, the available evidence remains limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous patient populations, and inconsistent definitions of SEMS-related complications. 9 10 11 In addition, few studies have simultaneously evaluated both complication profiles and survival outcomes in patients with UROCC, particularly in a real-world multicenter setting.

Therefore, the present study aimed to address these gaps by analyzing a large nationwide multicenter cohort with standardized data collection, focusing specifically on UROCC patients treated with SEMS followed by systemic chemotherapy. By doing so, we sought to provide a more comprehensive assessment of both safety and clinical outcomes in routine practice.

This retrospective study conducted by the Japan Colonic Stent Safe Procedure Group (JCSSPRG) aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of systemic chemotherapy after SEMS placement in a real-world setting.

Patients/Material and Methods

Study Design and Patient Enrolment

This multicenter retrospective cohort study was conducted by the JCSSPRG across Japan. A total of 26 centers participated in the study, comprising 8 university hospitals, 16 general hospitals, and 2 cancer center hospitals. The data for consecutive patients who met the inclusion criteria from April 1, 2012 to December 31, 2022 were extracted from the medical records in each center.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis with UROCC at the initial presentation; (2) SEMS placement between April 1, 2012 and December 31, 2022; and (3) systemic chemotherapy (first-line treatment) started within 3 months after SEMS placement. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age <20 years; (2) no SEMS placement due to migration or primary tumor resection (PTR) at the start of systemic chemotherapy; and (3) absence of at least one course of systemic chemotherapy. Severity of obstruction was categorized using the ColoRectal Obstruction Scoring System (CROSS), which was evaluated at the time of SEMS placement. In principle, patients with CROSS 0–3 were regarded as symptomatic obstructive CRC. A small number of patients with CROSS 4 were included if radiologic findings confirmed obstruction and SEMS placement was deemed clinically indicated. This definition is consistent with the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) guideline, which defines obstructive CRC as requiring both symptoms and radiologic evidence of obstruction. 5 Right-sided and left-sided obstructions were defined as obstruction from the cecum to the splenic flexure and obstruction from the descending colon to the rectum, respectively. The methods for SEMS placement were basically standardized using protocols posted on a JCSSPRG website ( https://colon-stent.com/?318_page.html ).

Ethics

The study protocol was approved by the Nippon Medical School Central Review Board (O-2022-554), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for written informed consent from the patients was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study. 12

Data Collection

Data were collected for the following variables of interest: patient and tumor characteristics, SEMS-related, systemic chemotherapy-related, and surgery-related variables, blood test results, and survival variables. Complete obstruction was defined as meeting at least one of the following criteria: (1) absence of flatus; (2) no passage of contrast agent through the stricture; and (3) inability to visualize the oral side of the stricture endoscopically. Although the absence of flatus alone could theoretically fulfill this definition, SEMS placement in clinical practice was always based on radiological and/or endoscopic confirmation of obstruction in addition to symptoms. This definition has also been consistently applied in many prospective multicenter studies conducted by JCSSPRG. SEMS-related complications, such as perforation, re-obstruction, migration, and bleeding, were assessed only after confirmation of safe SEMS placement and following initiation of first-line systemic chemotherapy ( Supplementary document ). Therapeutic response was evaluated according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST), version 1.1. 13 using Best Overall Response (BOR). BOR was defined as the best response recorded from the initiation of chemotherapy until disease progression/recurrence or the last evaluable assessment. Radiologic assessments were performed according to each institution’s routine practice; the exact timing of imaging was not systematically collected in this study. Therapeutic response was defined as complete or partial response (CR/PR), and disease control as CR, PR, or stable disease (SD). Progression free survival (PFS) was defined as the interval between first-line chemotherapy initiation and first objective evidence of disease progression or death from any cause. OS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to death from any cause.

Sample Size

This was an exploratory, retrospective multicenter study. A formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed; instead, all consecutive eligible patients treated during the study period at participating centers were included. Given its clinical seriousness, perforation was prespecified as the primary endpoint. The 5% perforation rate in the manuscript reflects a clinical benchmark informed by prior literature—reports of 2–3% with bevacizumab-based chemotherapy in unresectable nonobstructive CRC, and ~7% after stenting in obstructive CRC, including bevacizumab-treated cohorts. 11 14 15

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) and were compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used to compare discrete variables. The perforation proportion was summarized with 95% confidence intervals using the Wilson score method. No one-sample hypothesis test against a prespecified threshold was planned or conducted. Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan–Meier method, and the curves were compared using the log-rank test. Univariate analyses and multivariate analyses involving variables with values of P < 0.05 in the univariate analyses for Cox proportional hazards models were used to examine the associations between the selected variables and PFS or OS. All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Easy R), a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). EZR is based on R and R commander and was developed by Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Tochigi, Japan. 16

Results

Study Population

A total of 302 UROCC patients who received first-line systemic chemotherapy after SEMS placement were included in this multicenter retrospective cohort study. Ultimately, 289 patients were subjected to the analysis following the exclusion of 13 patients with insufficient clinical data. The background characteristics of the included patients are presented in Table 1 . The primary tumor location was right-sided in 63 (21.8%), left-sided in 224 (77.5%), and unknown in 2 (0.7%). The RAS status of the primary tumor was wild-type in 136 (47.1%), mutated in 119 (41.2%), and unknown including unmeasured in 34 (11.8%). The median duration from SEMS placement to initiation of chemotherapy was 11 days (IQR: 6–21). The chemotherapy regimens comprised monotherapy in 28 (9.7%), doublet therapy in 239 (82.7%), and triplet therapy in 22 (7.6%). Bevacizumab and anti-EGFR antibodies were used in 55 (19.0%) and 89 (30.8%), respectively. Among all patients and patients without perforation ( n = 264), PTR was performed in 101 (34.9%) and 90 (34.1%), respectively. The median durations from SEMS placement and from chemotherapy initiation to PTR were 146 days (IQR: 104–235) and 129 days (IQR: 86–222), respectively. Sixty-two (22.7%) patients had stoma creation.

Short-term Outcomes

Among the 60 patients with migration, 18 (30.0%) had migration requiring intervention. Therapeutic response and disease control were achieved in 163 (56.4%) and 228 (78.9%), respectively.

Survival Analysis

The Kaplan–Meier curves for PFS and OS are shown in Fig. 1A,B , respectively. The median PFS and OS were 9.2 months (95% CI, 7.5–10.9 months) and 17.3 months (95% CI, 14.1–20.5 months), respectively. Kaplan–Meier curves stratified by the number of metastatic organs (≤2 vs. ≥3), presence of perforation, and performance of PTR are shown in Fig. 2A–F . In the univariate analyses, patients with ≥3 metastatic organs, presence of perforation, and nonperformance of PTR had significantly worse PFS and OS compared with their respective counterparts. The median OS in the non-PTR group was only 11.6 months, whereas it reached 39.5 months in the PTR group ( P < 0.001). The impacts of bevacizumab and anti-EGFR antibody use on PFS and OS are shown in Fig. 2G,H , respectively. Use of anti-EGFR antibodies was associated with significantly better PFS and OS compared with nonuse of anti-EGFR antibodies ( P = 0.008 and P = 0.040, respectively). In contrast, use of bevacizumab had no significant impact on either PFS or OS. The multivariate analyses using Cox proportional hazards models for PFS and OS are shown in Table 4 . The analyses identified ≥3 metastatic organs, presence of perforation, and nonperformance of PTR as independent prognostic factors for both PFS and OS.

Discussion

While previous studies and meta-analyses have explored the safety of chemotherapy after SEMS placement, most were limited by small sample sizes and heterogeneous study designs. In contrast, the present study provides a relatively large, nationwide multicenter dataset focusing specifically on UROCC patients treated with SEMS followed by chemotherapy.

Importantly, our study enables a simultaneous evaluation of SEMS-related complications and survival outcomes under a unified study design, allowing identification of clinically relevant prognostic factors such as perforation and metastatic burden. In addition, the real-world timing of chemotherapy initiation after SEMS placement observed in this study offers practical insights that have been insufficiently addressed in prior literature.

This retrospective study clearly demonstrated that the rates of overall SEMS-related complications and perforation (latter defined as the primary endpoint) were 30.1% and 8.7%, respectively. These rates were generally consistent with those in previous studies. Furthermore, the number of metastatic organs, presence of perforation, and performance of PTR were identified as independent prognostic factors for both PFS and OS.

Studies that have investigated the safety and efficacy of systemic chemotherapy after SEMS placement have been limited by small sample sizes (<100 patients). 10 17 18 19 20 Nevertheless, survival was significantly better with chemotherapy than with best supportive care. 20 21 Theoretically, the administration of systemic chemotherapy, which prolongs the duration of SEMS implantation in vivo , may elevate the risk of adverse events associated with SEMS placement, including stent migration due to tumor shrinkage, re-obstruction from tumor ingrowth or outgrowth, and perforation, and the last is potentially life-threatening. 22 23 The chemotherapy-administered SEMS-related overall complication rate of 30.1% in the present study was slightly higher, but almost equivalent, to the rates in previous studies (16–42%). 18 19 24 However, some studies found that chemotherapy did not influence the risk of SEMS-related complications. These findings could arise through chemotherapy-induced tumor shrinkage, resulting in a reduction of re-obstruction from tumor ingrowth. 10 18 21 23 In parallel with that effect, the frequency of migration could be higher in the presence of chemotherapy-induced tumor shrinkage, but most of the migration occurred in asymptomatic patients (e.g., 70% in this study).

The safety of using bevacizumab, an anti-angiogenic agent, after SEMS placement has long been debated due to concerns about increased perforation risk, but no consensus has been reached. The perforation rate with bevacizumab-based chemotherapy in patients with unresectable nonobstructive colorectal cancer is reported to be 2–3%. 15 In patients who underwent SEMS placement, the reported perforation rates during chemotherapy without bevacizumab were 7.0–9.7%, compared to 7.3–15.4% with bevacizumab. 8 11 14 20 Despite these concerns, the 2020 guidelines from the ESGE state that chemotherapy after SEMS placement is safe and that anti-angiogenic agents can be considered, suggesting that the risk may be underestimated. 5 Perforation occurred in 8.7% of all patients. The 5% value referenced in the manuscript reflects a clinical benchmark from prior reports rather than a formal hypothesis-testing threshold. Given the exploratory design and absence of a priori powering to test a specific margin, the observed perforation rate (8.7% [95% CI, 5.9–12.5%]) should be interpreted as non-negligible, and conclusions regarding strict safety thresholds should be drawn with caution. In the present study, bevacizumab use was not significantly associated with overall SEMS-related complications or perforation. While operator expertise likely contributes to high technical success and periprocedural safety, perforation occurring after the initiation of chemotherapy is multifactorial (e.g., anti-VEGF therapy, tumor response/necrosis, stent characteristics and indwelling duration, stricture location) and may not be mitigated by procedural skill alone. In the present study, bevacizumab use was not significantly associated with perforation. Although the perforation rate was numerically higher in patients receiving bevacizumab compared with those not receiving bevacizumab (14.5% vs. 7.3%), this difference did not reach statistical significance and should be interpreted with caution. Treatment selection was at the discretion of physicians, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Therefore, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. Given the limited number of events, robust propensity score analysis was not feasible; therefore, results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Further prospective studies or larger datasets are required to clarify the potential impact of bevacizumab on perforation risk in this clinical setting.

The lack of available molecular-targeted therapy for RAS-mutant tumors due to bevacizumab avoidance may seem disadvantageous. However, this effect may be minimal, as most patients eligible for SEMS placement have left-sided obstruction (77.5% in our study), where the survival benefit of bevacizumab is limited compared to right-sided tumors. 25 26 Moreover, bevacizumab use showed no significant impact on PFS or OS in our study. Regarding anti-EGFR antibodies, both a previous study 18 and ours found no increased risk of SEMS-related complications or perforation (7.9% perforation rate with anti-EGFR antibody use in our study). Thus, anti-EGFR antibodies appear to be relatively safe during stenting. Although they were not independent prognostic factors in multivariate analysis, survival analysis demonstrated significant improvements in both PFS and OS with anti-EGFR antibody use.

In recent years, the oncological significance of PTR in patients with unresectable metastases has been questioned. The JCOG1007 trial demonstrated that PTR did not improve OS in asymptomatic patients with unresectable stage IV colorectal cancer, supporting less invasive management for early chemotherapy initiation. 27 However, the oncological influence of PTR in unresectable patients after SEMS placement was not investigated. In the present study, the absence of PTR had the greatest negative prognostic impact among all the variables examined (hazard ratio: 4.22 for PFS and 4.25 for OS), but this association should be interpreted with caution. Given the retrospective design, potential selection bias, such as favorable response to chemotherapy or better baseline status in the PTR group, cannot be excluded. Therefore, our data do not provide direct evidence that SEMS followed by PTR improves survival. Rather, we suggest that PTR may be beneficial in selected patients by reducing the risks associated with prolonged stenting, such as perforation or re-obstruction. This interpretation remains exploratory, and prospective studies are needed to validate the clinical benefit and optimal timing of PTR in this context. Taken together, the strength of this study lies not in introducing a novel concept, but in providing a robust real-world validation of existing concerns within a large and clinically relevant population.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective observational study, and therefore biases in terms of choice of intestinal decompression devices, SEMS type, and systemic chemotherapy may exist. Because various stent types were used across institutions, device-specific outcomes were not analyzed in this study, and we plan to report these findings in a separate subanalysis focusing on stent characteristics and related complications. Second, although perforation was defined as the primary endpoint, a multivariable analysis specifically evaluating risk factors for perforation was not performed. This was primarily due to the limited number of perforation events ( n = 25), which would not allow for a statistically robust multivariable model without risking overfitting. Therefore, future studies with larger sample sizes and a greater number of events are required to better identify independent risk factors for perforation. Third, the definition of “unresectable” was broad, as it included both cStage IV patients with distant metastases not amenable to curative resection and patients without metastases who were judged unresectable due to technical or patient-related factors (cStage II/III). Nevertheless, a sensitivity analysis limited to stage IV patients ( n = 275) showed results consistent with the main analysis, suggesting that inclusion of technically unresectable non-metastatic cases did not materially affect the overall conclusions (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2) . Fourth, the Japanese cohort and physicians potentially hamper the global application of the findings. Fifth, owing to the historical background of the study period, the BRAF mutation and microsatellite instability statuses were not assessed in many cases, making it difficult to evaluate their potential impacts on patient prognosis. Sixth, although PTR was independently associated with survival, collected data hampered to perform subgroup analyses based on the specific indications or timing of PTR. In addition, because PTR was a post-baseline, time-dependent exposure and time-stamped covariates were not systematically collected, we could not adequately address time-varying confounding or immortal time bias; therefore, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. This limitation hinders our ability to provide detailed guidance on optimal patient selection and timing for PTR.

In conclusion, this multicenter retrospective study demonstrated that while chemotherapy after SEMS placement is generally feasible, clinicians should remain vigilant for complications like perforation, migration and re-obstruction.
