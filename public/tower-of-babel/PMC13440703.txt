Odontogenic subdural empyema identified by masticatory muscle inflammation on magnetic resonance imaging: A case report
Authors: Yuko Kimura, Jun Tsugawa, Daito Nakamura, Hironori Fukumoto, Hayatsura Hanada, Inoue Ritsurou, Kimiya Sakamoto, Kouhei Nii, Yoshio Tsuboi, Toshio Higashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Subdural empyema secondary to dental infection is rare and often difficult to recognize early because initial symptoms may be nonspecific. Imaging findings that suggest an odontogenic source can support timely diagnosis and treatment.Case Description: A 70-year-old woman presented with right temporal pain, high fever, and impaired consciousness (Glasgow Coma Scale score of 11). Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed a right frontotemporal subdural fluid collection, along with high T2 signal intensity in the masseter and lateral pterygoid muscles, suggesting an odontogenic source. A diagnosis of subdural empyema secondary to dental infection was made, and emergency surgical drainage was performed, followed by targeted antimicrobial therapy and extraction of the infected teeth. Consciousness improved after treatment, and follow-up showed no recurrence.Conclusion: Inflammation of the masticatory muscles on MRI can provide an important diagnostic clue for identifying the infection source to dental-origin subdural empyema in patients presenting with temporal pain and altered consciousness, and may facilitate prompt surgical and antimicrobial management. Such extracranial inflammatory changes have been described in previous reports; however, their role as a primary diagnostic clue has not been fully emphasized.

INTRODUCTION

Intracranial subdural empyema is a rare, life-threatening infection that most often develops as a complication of otitis media or paranasal sinusitis, whereas cases secondary to dental infection remain uncommon.[2,5,9] Diagnosis of dental-origin subdural empyema is frequently delayed because early clinical manifestations are often nonspecific.[2,5,9]

Early recognition of the infection route enables timely surgical drainage and appropriate antimicrobial therapy, both of which are critical for improving prognosis. Early identification through imaging may therefore influence treatment strategy and neurological outcomes. Characteristic imaging features that distinguish dental-origin subdural empyema from other causes, however, remain insufficiently described in the literature.

This report describes a rare case of subdural empyema caused by direct extension of a dental infection, in which inflammatory changes involving the masticatory muscles, particularly the masseter and lateral pterygoid muscles, were identified on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and served as key diagnostic findings and supported early treatment.

CASE PRESENTATION

A right-handed 70-year-old woman developed right temporal pain 2 weeks before admission and did not seek medical care. Subsequently, she developed a high-grade fever of 40°C and was admitted to our hospital in a confused state. On examination, she was drowsy with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 11 (E4V2M5), neck stiffness, and a positive Kernig’s sign, without focal neurological deficits. Laboratory studies showed a marked inflammatory response, with a white blood cell count of 26,100/µL and a serum C-reactive protein level of 45.8 mg/dL, along with mild renal dysfunction. Head computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a right frontotemporal subdural fluid collection with mild leftward midline shift and swelling of the right masticatory muscles, suggesting a possible odontogenic source. No osteolytic lesion or bony destruction of the skull base was identified on CT. Brain MRI confirmed a right subdural collection showing relatively low T2 signal and restricted diffusion [Figure 1A and B], as well as high T2 signal in the adjacent masticatory muscles, particularly the masseter and lateral pterygoid muscles [Figure 1C and D], supporting a dental origin of infection and prompting early surgical drainage. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of the right subdural empyema secondary to dental infection was made, and emergency burr-hole drainage was performed within several hours of admission. Postoperatively, intravenous piperacillin was initiated. On postoperative day 3, the patient developed septic shock and seizures, leading to escalation of antimicrobial therapy to meropenem 4 g/day and vancomycin 1 g/day. Electroencephalography demonstrated intermittent epileptiform spikes in the right frontotemporal region. Levetiracetam was administered at a dose of 2,000 mg/day and continued throughout the hospitalization.

Magnetic resonance imaging on admission. (A) Axial T2-weighted image showing a right frontotemporal subdural collection (arrow) with mild mass effect. (B) Diffusion-weighted image demonstrating restricted diffusion within the subdural collection (arrow). (C) Axial T2-weighted image showing inflammatory changes involving the right masseter muscle (arrow) and lateral pterygoid muscle (arrowhead). (D) Coronal T2-weighted image confirming inflammatory involvement of the right masseter muscle (arrow) and lateral pterygoid muscle (arrowhead).

Culture of the drained pus grew Prevotella oris. Based on the results of antimicrobial susceptibility testing, antimicrobial therapy was changed to metronidazole 2,000 mg/day and biapenem 600 mg/day for 7 weeks, consistent with the recommended treatment duration for intracranial abscess. Dental evaluation identified a draining abscess, a periapical lesion of the upper right central incisor, and abscesses involving the first and second upper premolars, all of which required extraction. Oral sultamicillin tosylate at 1,125 mg/day was subsequently continued for the dental infection.

Follow-up MRI at 60 days showed complete resolution of the abscess [Figure 2A-B]. The patient had mild residual left hemiparesis, which may have been associated with status epilepticus during the initial clinical course, and began rehabilitation. No recurrence has been observed during follow-up.

Magnetic resonance imaging 60 days postoperatively. (A) T2-weighted images obtained 60 days after surgery show complete resolution of the right-sided subdural fluid collection. (B) No residual thickening or increased signal intensity appears in the right masticatory muscles, including the masseter and lateral pterygoid muscles.

DISCUSSION

This case demonstrates a rare and clinically important presentation of subdural empyema secondary to dental infection. Initial symptoms were limited to temporal pain and mild swelling, which contributed to delayed recognition of intracranial involvement. Characteristic imaging findings, including swelling and high T2 signal in the masticatory muscles, particularly the masseter and lateral pterygoid muscles, provided an early clue to the odontogenic source and led to prompt surgical drainage and a favorable outcome.

Subdural empyema accounts for approximately 15–20% of focal intracranial infections, and most cases arise from sinusitis or otitis media rather than dental disease. Reports of odontogenic subdural empyema remain scarce, although recent increases in reported cases likely reflect advances in imaging and earlier recognition. To better characterize this entity, we examined several recently reported cases. Most of the reports shared several features: direct spread from infected teeth into the intracranial space, good outcomes with prompt identification of the infection source and surgical intervention, and no recurrence after removal of the infected teeth.[1,4,6,10] These observations align with the present case and further support the diagnostic value of masticatory muscle inflammation on MRI as a useful clue to identifying the infection source, although similar findings have been described in previous reports. The relatively low T2 signal of the subdural collection in this case may reflect high protein content or viscous purulent material, which has been reported in some intracranial abscesses.

Subdural empyema remains a serious infection, with a reported mortality rate of 10–20% even with antimicrobial therapy.[1-6,9,10] Prior studies have emphasized that early identification of the infection source and prompt initiation of appropriate treatment are essential for improving prognosis.[2,3,5,13] Although selected reports describe successful medical management in carefully chosen patients without neurological deficits or midline shift, most of the studies recommend surgical drainage to identify the causative organism and reduce intracranial pressure.[5,8,11,12] In this case, the patient presented with impaired consciousness and imaging evidence of a leftward midline shift. These findings met accepted criteria for immediate surgical drainage. Early surgical intervention enabled identification of P. oris and guided targeted antimicrobial therapy, resulting in good clinical recovery. P. oris is an anaerobic gram-negative bacterium that forms part of the normal oral flora and is commonly associated with odontogenic infections. Although frequently isolated from dental and periodontal infections, intracranial subdural empyema caused by P. oris has rarely been reported. This case further underscores the importance of early surgical management when neurological deterioration occurs or mass effect is observed on neuroimaging.

The spread of odontogenic infection to the intracranial space can occur through several anatomical routes. Li et al. described four potential pathways: direct extension, hematogenous dissemination, lymphatic spread, and indirect spread through extraoral odontogenic infection.[7]

In our patient, MRI demonstrated inflammatory changes in the right masticatory muscles, particularly the masseter and lateral pterygoid muscles, extending toward the skull base, without evidence of sinusitis or otitis media. CT also showed no evidence of skull base osteolysis or bony destruction. These findings indicate that the infection most likely spread directly along the lateral skull base, possibly through the foramen ovale, and led to the formation of a subdural empyema. This interpretation is consistent with previous reports describing similar pathways of spread in odontogenic intracranial infections.[4]

Prominent inflammation of the masticatory muscles, particularly the masseter and lateral pterygoid muscles, on MRI served as an early diagnostic clue in this case and appeared before typical symptoms, such as severe headache or vomiting. This observation highlights the clinical value of careful assessment of extracranial soft-tissue abnormalities when odontogenic intracranial infection is suspected. Recognition of these early imaging features can support prompt diagnosis and timely surgical intervention.

Despite these observations, this report has several limitations. Because it describes a single case, the generalizability of the imaging findings remains uncertain and requires confirmation in larger case series. Nevertheless, clinicians may consider early MRI when patients present with temporal pain, facial swelling, or altered consciousness in the setting of suspected dental infection, as early recognition of these extracranial inflammatory changes may facilitate prompt diagnosis of intracranial complications.

CONCLUSION

This case demonstrates that odontogenic subdural empyema may present with atypical symptoms, and clinicians may overlook intracranial involvement when dental complaints are absent. Early MRI evidence of masticatory muscle inflammation, particularly involving the masseter and lateral pterygoid muscles, proved critical for identifying the dental origin and initiating timely surgery. Careful evaluation of extracranial soft tissues in patients who present with temporal pain and altered consciousness can accelerate diagnosis and improve clinical outcomes. Recognition of extracranial inflammatory changes on neuroimaging may therefore represent a useful diagnostic consideration in identifying the source of intracranial infection. Further studies involving larger case series are warranted to confirm the diagnostic value of these imaging findings.
