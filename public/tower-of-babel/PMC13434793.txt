Behavioral correlates of Purkinje cell ensemble covariance
Authors: Abdulraheem Nashef, Michael S. Spindle, Dylan J. Calame, Abigail L. Person
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purkinje cells (PCs) mediate adaptive cerebellar output through inhibitory control of the cerebellar nuclei. Because many PCs converge, their influence on target neurons likely depends on population activity. However, despite a large literature on individual PC relationships with behavior, little is known about how multiple PCs collaborate to influence movement. In simultaneously recorded PCs during mouse forelimb reach behavior, we found that different kinematic variables correlated either with PC firing rates or covariance: reach velocity and deceleration correlated with firing rates, while the coordination of these kinematic variables, which influences reach amplitudes, was related to covariance. In a model, physiological levels of PC covariance elevated nuclear firing rates, which could promote faster reach deceleration than the same conditions with low covariance. These findings suggest a role for coherent PC population activity to coordinate movement.

Introduction

Cerebellar output coordinates movement and modulates cerebral cortical dynamics1–5. This output is shaped by cerebellar Purkinje cells (PCs) activity. GABAergic PCs fire at high spontaneous rates, tonically inhibiting pacemaker neurons in the cerebellar output nuclei6–8. While PC firing suppression is one mechanism to disinhibit nuclear neurons, individual nuclear neurons receive convergent input from many PCs and excitatory mossy fibers, raising the importance of population activity in controlling cerebellar output9–12. Several studies support this population coding framework, where activity from multiple PCs improves linear decoding of kinematic variables such as eye, limb, and whisker velocity11,13–15. Beyond firing rates alone, the temporal structure of population activity also influences nuclear integration. A logical extension of the rate coding hypothesis of PC population activity predicts that broader-timescale covariance of convergent PCs will influence synaptic integration and nuclear output, since coordinated firing rate changes will exert more coherent control over nuclear neurons without interference from unmodulated convergent PCs. Moreover, synchronized inhibition across PCs can facilitate nuclear firing by transiently reducing summated tonic inhibition16–19, forming an additional mechanism of nuclear disinhibition14,15. It is not known whether coordination of PC population activity across different timescales correlates with cerebellar control of movement, and if so, whether such coding modalities preferentially structure specific kinematic variables.

Cerebellar output is organized into discrete modules that exert directional control over specific effectors, such as the eyes and limbs, through firing rate codes12,20,21. For limb control, electrophysiological and optogenetic studies in mice have identified directional output modules within the anterior interposed nucleus (IntA) that exert either an inward pull or outward push on the limb20,22. In many IntA neurons, firing rates scale linearly with outward deceleration (or inward acceleration)20,23. Within the deceleration module, PC rates inversely scale with deceleration, consistent with a disinhibitory relationship with nuclear coding14,20. Because PC activity is also correlated with peak velocity13—and faster reaches both accelerate and decelerate more steeply—endpoints remain relatively stable despite kinematic variability. These coordinated relationships between multiple phases of the reach, and their linear coding with PCs and IntA neurons, have led to the view that these regions regulate motor coordination by translating inputs to the cerebellum that convey corollary discharge and goal information into well-regulated PC pauses that precisely guide the limb to the target10,24. However, in some movements, kinematic scaling between peak velocity and deceleration is higher or lower than average, which can influence endpoints, leading to under- or overshooting targets. It is not known if cerebellar coding influences such kinematic scaling mismatches.

Thus, because both rates and covariance may influence nuclear firing rates which then influence kinematics, we asked whether PC covariance is associated with kinematic variables in addition to firing rate15–18,25. Here, we analyzed simultaneously recorded PCs during mouse reaching movements to examine both the presence and behavioral relevance of PC covariance during a reach-to-grasp task in mice. We used hierarchical clustering to identify groups of PCs with distinct reach-related firing patterns. We then performed pair-wise analyses of these PCs, within and between clusters, and found that when reach deceleration was faster than expected for a given peak velocity there was a pronounced increase in PC covariance, spanning 1 to 100 s of milliseconds. This observation suggested a role for varying PC covariance in adjusting the gain between kinematic variables within a movement. Computational modeling and dynamic clamp results support a model in which increased PC covariance enhances nuclear disinhibition to levels sufficient to account for the behavioral variables. These correlative data suggest that PC covariance may be a coding dimension that influences motor coordination, reinforcing a principle of PC population coding that is not evident from single PC firing rates alone.

Results

Purkinje cell activity patterns during reach in mice

We analyzed previously published13 recorded PCs with high density Neuropixels 1.0 probes in forelimb-associated lobules 4/5 and Simplex during skilled reaching movements in head-fixed mice (25 recording sessions from 7 mice). We identified PCs and putative PCs (together called PCs) based on firing patterns, spike waveform, and/or presence of complex spike-associated pauses13 (Fig. 1a; See Methods). During reach-to-grasp movements, mouse PC populations exhibit diverse simple spike firing patterns. We previously categorized these PC activity patterns as dominated by either firing rate suppression or facilitation13. However, this classification overlooked more complex time-varying firing dynamics that may shape distinct phases of reaching movements. Therefore, to gain a more detailed view of PC codes during reach, we performed hierarchical clustering on peri-event time histograms (PETHs) of reach-aligned PCs (Fig. 1b–c). Most reach-modulated PCs fell into one of four clusters (Supplementary Fig. 1), characterized by reach aligned-suppression (cluster 1); early-facilitation (cluster 2); early-suppression (cluster 3); or reach aligned-facilitation (cluster 4) (Fig. 1b–c). We observed PCs from multiple clusters in all 25 sessions (Fig. 1d) but with varying ratios across 7 mice (Fig. 1d, colored dots). Next, to analyze behavioral modulation of PC cofluctuation, we constructed joint peri-event time histograms- (jPETHs)1,26,27 aligned to reach endpoint for simultaneously recorded cell pairs, binned at 1 ms (Fig. 2c,d) and averaged across pairs. Because jPETHs are sensitive to shared event-locked firing rate modulation, we applied a “shift-predictor” that removes correlations expected from overlapping PETHs—which are maximized by our clustering method -- leaving residual structure that reflects trial-by-trial cofluctuation and fine temporal coordination. Shift-corrected jPETHs for Cluster 1, 2, and 1-2 pairs revealed a marked increase in PC cofluctuation during reach, evident as a prominent diagonal band (Fig. 2c,d blue, red, Fig. 2e,fn = 477 pairs). In contrast, reach-associated cofluctuation within cluster 3 and 4 PCs and other between-cluster PCs was modest or absent (Fig. 2c,d green and orange; Supplementary Fig. 3).

Although the shift predictor controls for shared event-locked firing rate changes, several additional confounds could in principle account for the observed jPETHs, including slow rate cofluctuations and shared response latency. To address these possibilities, we performed a set of shuffle-based analyses that control for known statistical confounds and provide structured baselines against which task-related changes in cofluctuation can be quantified. Each analysis controlled for firing rate fluctuations in a distinct manner, and all revealed that cofluctuation in Clusters 1 and 2 exceeded chance levels. We describe these controls in turn below.

Trial shuffling, which disrupts trial-by-trial coupling while preserving peri-event firing rate profiles, abolished signatures of cofluctuation in Cluster 1 and 2 jPETHs and between them (Supplementary Fig. 4a–d). Cofiring was significantly higher in simultaneously recorded pairs compared to trial-shuffled pairs (Supplementary Fig. 4a–d; mean ± s.e.m.; Difference between shuffle and measured: Cluster 1: −31.7 ± 1.9 sp2/s2, paired t-test; p = 1.7 × 10−47; Cluster 2: −36.2 ± 7.1 sp2/s2, p = 8.9 × 10−7; Cluster 1-2: −33.3 ± 2.3 sp2/s2, p = 6.7 × 10−40; 0-lag cofiring peak: Cluster 1: −36.3 ± 3.1 sp2/s2, p = 1.4 × 10−27; Cluster 2: −39.6 ± 6.9 sp2/s2, p = 4.3 × 10−8; Cluster 1-2: −47.7 ± 3.8 sp2/s2, p = 2.8 × 10−31).

To distinguish slow rate cofluctuations from finer temporal coordination, we next analyzed cofiring after randomly jittering spike times within a 75 ms window, which preserves broader covariance structure. Under this manipulation, observed 0-lag cofiring peaks in Clusters 1 and 2 remained significantly higher than jittered controls, such that above-chance 1 ms synchrony accounted for >50% of the total cofiring measured in the jPETH (Supplementary Fig. 4e–h; difference, jitter–measured: g: Cluster 1: −10.7 ± 0.8 sp2/s2, p = 6.3 × 10−35; Cluster 2: −11.5 ± 2.4 sp2/s2, p = 3.1 × 10−6; Cluster 1-2: −11.2 ± 1 sp2/s2, p = 1.3 × 10−24; h: Cluster 1: −17.8 ± 2.6 sp2/s2, p = 2.3 × 10−11; Cluster 2: −19.5 ± 5.9 sp2/s2, p = 0.001; Cluster 1-2: −27.1 ± 3.3 sp2/s2, p = 2×10−15). Finally, using a simpler jittering method where each spike is moved by a random shift between 1 and 75 ms, also accounted for >50% of the total cofiring (Supplementary Fig. 4i–l; difference, jitter–measured: i: Cluster 1: −14.2 ± 1 sp2/s2, p = 3×10−38; Cluster 2: −13.6 ± 2.5 sp2/s2, p = 2.1 × 10−7; Cluster 1-2: −14.3 ± 1.2 sp2/s2, p = 7.3 × 10−27; l: Cluster 1: −17.7 ± 2.5 sp2/s2, p = 1.1 × 10−11; Cluster 2: −18.8 ± 5.8 sp2/s2, p = 0.002; Cluster 1-2: −27.5 ± 3.3 sp2/s2, p = 6.4 × 10−16). This provides an estimate of millisecond-scale synchrony and suggested not all cofiring was a consequence of 1-ms synchrony; however, jitter-based approaches cannot fully dissociate precise synchrony from shared trial variability, and we return to longer-timescale covariance later.

We next computed jPETHs for randomly selected units that were not necessarily recorded simultaneously, a manipulation that preserves behavior-related firing rate fluctuations while disrupting spike timing relationships (Supplementary Fig. 5). Plotting excess cofiring (the difference between observed cofiring in simple spikes, in units sp2/s2, and that expected by chance) against firing rate revealed no monotonic relationship between these metrics, particularly across clusters. Notably, cofiring within Cluster 1 tended to be inversely correlated with firing rate, whereas within Cluster 2 these relationships ran in the opposite direction. The negative correlation between cofiring and firing rate in Cluster 1 contrasted with null expectations based on shuffled data (Supplementary Fig. 5d), in which increased firing rates lead to greater apparent synchrony.

Finally, the observed increase in cofiring was not attributable to latency covariation. Cofiring survived controls that (1) explicitly shifted spike times within each trial to behavioral onset (Supplementary Fig. 6a–c)28,29 and (2) replaced each trial’s activity with that from another trial matched for behavioral onset time (Supplementary Fig. 6d–f). Taken together, these analyses indicate that PC cofluctuation is structured, time-varying, and selectively expressed across PC clusters, consistent with shared inputs imparting covariance during reach.

To quantify reach-associated cofiring, we extracted the 0-ms lag values from jPETHs 100 ms before reach endpoint (black circle, Fig. 2e,g). A pair of PCs was classified as exhibiting significant cofiring if its 0-ms lag value exceeded the 95th percentile of values along the downward (anti-)diagonal, which captures lead–lag structure of cofiring around this shared reference time. Using this criterion, 43% of Cluster 1 pairs and 30% of Cluster 1 × 2 pairs exhibited significant cofiring 100 ms before endpoint (black circles in Fig. 2e and Fig. 2g; cluster 1: 34.2 ± 2.2 sp2/s2; cluster 2: 32.7 ± 4.5 sp2/s2; cluster 1-2: 39.8 ± 2.8 sp2/s2; p > 0.05 for all; exact values in legends).

Together, these results point to a robust, behaviorally linked increase in cofiring during reach in specific subgroups of PCs.

Distinguishing kinematic correlates of PC covariance from PC rates

If covariance plays a role in Purkinje cell population coding, it should have measurable behavioral consequences. Given the interest in 1-ms synchrony as a potential mechanism for modulating nuclear activity, we first focus on millisecond-scale cofiring, noting that cofiring values derived from jPETHs include both trial-by-trial cofluctuation and precise spike-timing. We return to these factors separately later. Previous studies set up strong predictions on how PC simple spike synchrony in this region might influence limb kinematics. First, in vitro studies show that increased synchrony enhances nuclear firing17,18. Second, higher firing rates in IntA during reach are associated with faster limb deceleration on outreach20. Combining these observations, we predicted that increased cofiring during the reach would facilitate more nuclear firing, strengthening the inward pull on the limb, resulting in faster deceleration and hypometric endpoints. To test these predictions, we first sorted reaches by limb displacement at endpoint, defined as the position of maximum outward extent (Fig. 3a–c). We then compared the jPETHs and cofiring between the top and bottom quartiles of the kinematic distributions. Consistent with our prediction, trials with hypometric endpoints showed higher cofiring and broader covariance across Cluster 1 + 2 PCs before the endpoint (Fig. 3a–c, shaded area mean ± s.e.m; undershoot: 185.2 ± 13.8 sp2/s2, overshoot: 86.1 ± 7.7 sp2/s2; p = 4.5 × 10−17), with only minor firing rate differences (firing rate change- overshoot: 2.03 ± 1.5 sp/s, undershoot: 6.5 ± 1 sp/s; p = 0.0003). Contrary to our prediction, however, when we performed the same analyses but stratified by deceleration rate, we observed little difference in cofiring in reaches from the top and bottom deceleration quartiles (Fig. 3e–g; 104.6 ± 8.3 sp2/s2, slow: 92.7 ± 9.8 sp2/s2; p = 0.2). Similarly, cofiring was comparable between low and high peak velocity reaches (Fig. 3i–k; fast: 88 ± 9.6 sp2/s2, slow: 73.8 ± 10.3 sp2/s2; p = 0.26). By contrast, firing rates scaled with these variables, with a larger rate suppression associated with faster deceleration (Fig. 3h; fast: −10.5 ± 1.4 sp/s; slow: 0.6 ± 1.6 sp/s; p = 1.5 × 10−33) and higher peak velocity (Fig. 3l, Peak velocity: fast- 11.7 ± 1.4 sp/s; slow: 22.4 ± 1.3 sp/s; p = 1.5 × 10−12). Similar relationships between cofiring and kinematics were also observed for Cluster 1 PCs analyzed as a segregated cluster but not Cluster 2 alone (Supplementary Figs. 7–8).

This mismatch—where cofiring was highest in reaches with hypometric endpoints despite normal deceleration and peak velocity—led us to explore alternative kinematic factors contributing to hypometric endpoints. A hallmark of coordinated reaches is the linear scaling of deceleration with peak velocity24. Despite this relationship, the gain between these variables fluctuates reach to reach. We reasoned that hypometric endpoints could arise if deceleration was disproportionately high for a given peak velocity, even if absolute deceleration was not higher than average. We quantified this ‘deceleration error’ as the residual of deceleration relative to the session-wide least-squares relationship between peak velocity and deceleration (Fig. 3m–o). Reaches with positive deceleration error have a high gain relating peak velocity to deceleration and vice versa. Note that this metric does not imply an erroneous reach. Interestingly, we found much higher cofiring in Cluster 1 + 2 PCs during reaches with disproportionately fast deceleration relative to peak velocity, and vice versa (Fig. 3m–o; high gain (positive deceleration error): 123.5 ± 10 sp2/s2, low gain (negative deceleration error): 60.7 ± 10.7 sp2/s2; p = 2 × 10−9). Indeed cofiring was linearly correlated with both endpoint position and deceleration error for Cluster 1 and Cluster 1 + 2 PCs, emerging 60 ms before movement endpoint (Supplementary Fig. 9). Importantly, cluster 1 + 2 PC firing rates were not significantly different across these conditions (Fig. 3p: high: 6.8 ± 1.1 sp/s; low: 5.7 ± 1.2 sp/s; p = 0.22). Similar relationships were seen for Cluster 1 neurons analyzed alone (Supplementary Figs. 7) but not Clusters 2 (Supplementary Figs. 8), 3 or 4 (not shown). Thus, cofiring is associated with faster deceleration for a given velocity, accounting for hypometric reach endpoints.

Our previous control analyses indicated that rate differences could not account for all synchrony, so to isolate the rate-corrected synchrony across these conditions, we computed rate-corrected CCGs. CCG peaks were also significantly higher during high-gain than low-gain reaches, indicating an increase in excess millisecond-scale synchrony (Fig. 4c–d). 0-lag rate-corrected CCGs averaged 4.8 ± 0.8 sp/s in high-gain conditions but 0.8 ± 1 sp/s in low-gain conditions (p = 0.001) (Fig. 4c). Jittering spike timing by 1 or 100 ms in the pseudo-populations in 4a selectively attenuated 0-lag peaks (Fig. 4d; (0-lag CCG: control: 22.6 ± 0.32 sp/s; 9 ± 0.37 sp/s with 1-ms jitter; p = 2 × 10−136).

Since both rate cofluctuations and rate-corrected CCGs were distinct across conditions, we next tested whether these values covary as well. We therefore plotted cofiring measured with jPETH diagonals against the 0-lag CCG peak values. There was a strong linear relationship between the two metrics (R2 = 0.64; p = 0.04; Fig. 4e), for high-gain reaches, but a weaker relationship and shallower slope for low-gain reaches (R2 = 0.02; p = 0.8). Together, these findings indicate that both fine-timescale synchrony and broader firing rate coordination are enhanced during high-gain reaches, consistent with a shared population-level mechanism of coherence that may influence motor output.

To test how PC covariance affects nuclear activity in the model, we applied PC spike trains from the pseudo-population of PCs built from simultaneously recorded pairs representing high- and low-gain reaches (Fig. 5e). As expected from previous studies, nuclear firing increased linearly with average PC 1 ms cofiring within the sampled population (Fig. 5f; slope=0.52 CbN spike per 1 sp2/s2 PC cofiring). To isolate the contribution of 1-ms spike-timing from broader covariance, we jittered spikes within increasingly broad windows. This approach also allowed us to assess how variance at broader timescales, inherently embedded in the recorded ensembles, influences model output. PC patterns with high-gain covariance drove higher firing rates in CbN compared to low-gain conditions (Fig. 5g, right axis), which mapped onto distinctly different estimated deceleration error (Fig. 5g, left axis). Jittering by just 1 ms reduced firing rates in high gain conditions by ~12 sp/s (−11.9 ± 0.3 sp/s; Fig. 5g right inset), but notably the drop in spike output continued as longer jitter windows were imposed (Fig. 5f, pink), indicating a strong effect of the broader PC covariance on CbN firing as well. PCs with covariance seen in low gain conditions, drove less nuclear firing overall and was less sensitive to jittering (Fig. 5f, green, −2.2 ± 0.2 sp/s with 1-ms jitter). Ultimately, this analysis revealed that PC covariance under 20 ms accounted for up to 25% of the firing rate differences seen across high- and low-gain reaches (with a mere 1-ms jitter accounting for 9 ± 1% of the difference), and that covariance under 80 ms accounted for positive deceleration error. These simulations suggest that PC coordination across timescales may contribute to nuclear output that structures reach kinematics.

The prior simulations focused on the consequences of removing physiologically realistic PC cofluctuations to model output; here, we shift focus to the inverse manipulation, introducing synchrony to PC spike trains that follow average firing rate changes. Model details were the same as above, except that rather than using recorded PC spike trains, we simulated PC activity using a Poisson process replicating the time-varying firing rates of 40 PCs randomly chosen from clusters 1 and 2 PCs in our recordings (Supplementary Fig. 10a). We applied physiological or stochastic levels of synchrony to these populations by altering the cofiring probability per millisecond and compared postsynaptic responses across the 2 s simulated epoch (Supplementary Fig. 10b, top). As expected16,17, PC firing rate suppression promoted nuclear firing through disinhibition (Supplementary Fig. 10b, bottom), with more firing during physiological PC synchrony than stochastic. Synchrony promoted longer inter-arrival-times for IPSCs (“ISIs”, Supplementary Fig. 10c; mean ± std; ISIs, stochastic: 1.07 ± 0.01 ms; physiological: 1.1 ± 0.02 ms), which in turn facilitated a higher average membrane potential in the postsynaptic cell (stochastic: −46.1 ± 2.5 mV; phys: −45.6 ± 2.5 mV) and consequently higher firing rates (Supplementary Fig. 10d; stochastic: 25.3 ± 11.2 sp/s; phys: 28.5 ± 11.4 sp/s). CbN firing rose linearly with CCG measurements in the physiological range (Supplementary Fig. 10e), with a rate-corrected CCG of 0.02 (similar to values seen in highly-synchronized or in CS-identified PCs, which might indicate a within-microzone PCs10) resulting in an excess of 10 sp/s in nuclear firing (Pearson’s correlation; r = 0.75; slope: 0.4 nuclear sp/s per 0.001 PC mean pairwise CCG increase). Broader synchrony windows of 5 ms, facilitated nuclear firing even more strongly18, suggesting that 1-ms synchrony reflects a lower bound of effects of PC covariance on nuclear disinhibition. We return to this effect more thoroughly below.

Finally, we tested CbN responses to PC rates and synchrony using dynamic clamp in acute adult mouse brain slices (Supplementary Fig. 10f). We assayed nuclear neuron responses to pools of 40 PCs, matching net firing rate patterns recorded in vivo. Inhibitory postsynaptic conductances were modeled with physiological time constants and amplitude17, specifically 2.5 ms IPSC decay times and 5 nS steady-state unitary conductance (See Methods). With no above-chance population synchrony, these naturalistic PC trains resulted in an almost complete suppression of spontaneous nuclear neuron firing, reducing the high spontaneous firing rate of 53.2 ± 6.6 sp/s at baseline to 2.34 ± 1 sp/s within the first second of the train, with no DC current injection. The suppression of PC net firing rates that occurred during reach partially disinhibited nuclear neurons from these depressed rates, promoting firing up to an average of 11.5 ± 2.3 sp/s at their peak during the maximal disinhibitory phase (Supplementary Fig. 10g,h). Adding physiological levels of 1 ms-synchrony to PC rate suppression increased nuclear firing rates 72.6%, to 20.4 sp/s (Supplementary Fig. 10i; no sync: 11.83 ± 3.04 sp/s. sync: 20.42 ± 3.41 sp/s from 80-100% velocity quintile; paired t-test: p = 1.28 × 10−4; n = 13 cells). These data suggested that both rate and synchrony promote disinhibition of nuclear firing. Finally, we explored the interaction between PC rates and synchrony in driving nuclear firing, varying both rate and synchrony across a broad range of values. Both PC firing rate and PC synchrony influenced nuclear firing, with the strongest disinhibition occurring with low PC rates and high PC synchrony, and the strongest inhibition with the converse pattern, high PC rates and low PC synchrony (Supplementary Fig. 10j–l; n = 17 cells; Two-ways ANOVA; F6(PC firing)=21.16, p = 1.24 × 10−19; F1(Covariance) = 24.06, p = 1.79 × 10−6). Together, these data show that synchrony enhances the efficacy of PC firing rate suppression on CbN neuron firing, such that rate changes will more powerfully disinhibit CbN firing in the presence of synchrony18.

Discussion

We found a relationship between Purkinje cell ensemble covariance and behavior. Using hierarchical clustering, we identified four classes of PCs in mouse intermediate cortical lobules based on firing rate patterns during reaching. Both rate and covariance were correlated with kinematic variables in mouse forelimb reaches but had interesting dissociations. While PC rate suppression strongly correlated with limb deceleration, covariance, including 1-ms synchrony, was more strongly related to deceleration gain – defined as disproportionate deceleration for a given peak velocity. High deceleration gain accounted for hypometric reach endpoints observed in conditions of high covariance. Finally, we linked high covariance and high deceleration gain using a computational model, finding that covariance across timescales spanning 1–200 ms can increase nuclear firing rates aligning with their roles in mediating deceleration. These results suggest that PC covariance structure in the intermediate cerebellum influences downstream cerebellar nuclear firing and subsequent kinematics14,15,33.

Across species, reaches show relatively symmetric early- and late-phase acceleration and deceleration phases, characteristic of their well-known bell-shaped velocity profiles, mediated by timed alternation of extensor and flexor muscles24,34–39. In uncoordinated reaches, or those that have pronounced online corrections, the early accelerative phase becomes mismatched with deceleration, resulting in asymmetric velocity profiles and inaccurate or variable endpoint positions40. The organization of these kinematic relationships is sensitive to cerebellar manipulations41–43, motivating mechanistic studies investigating the role of cerebellar computations in coordinating acceleration with deceleration. When we analzyed PC firing rates in relation to limb peak velocity, deceleration, and deceleration gain (Fig. 3), we found that PC rates correlated with peak velocity and deceleration rate but not deceleration gain. PC rate suppression occurred near peak velocity, preceding deceleration but after acceleration, arguing that PCs could contribute directly to the decelerative phase by responding to speed-related information entering the cerebellum via mossy fibers.

We found prominent beyond-chance covariance in Clusters 1 and 2 PCs and between them. This covariance structure was temporally dynamic during reaching movements and occurred independent of increasing firing rates. Unlike firing rates, which were strongly correlated with velocity and deceleration, covariance was not. Rather, PC covariance was related to the gain of the relationship between peak velocity and deceleration, such that high covariance occurred when deceleration was fast relative to a given peak velocity. In this sense, covariance constitutes an additional axis of control over cerebellar output. Rate and covariance modulation may therefore allow the cerebellum to superimpose flexible gain with learned motor control signals which would be encoded in synaptic weights. In this framework, PC firing rates report net parallel fiber drive through learned synaptic weights encoding feedforward control signals, whereas covariance, through unknown drivers, regulates the efficacy of mossy fiber excitation in downstream cerebellar output neurons. These observations also reinforce the emerging consensus around population coding among PC populations, with motor potent control signals emerging from the difference in firing rates across competing modules10,44. Notably, reflex gains that differ with sensory experience have also been suggested to be modulated by PC synchrony15. We observed excess 1-ms synchrony in conditions that also had broader high covariance, suggesting the possibility of shared underlying mechanisms, such as gap-junction mediated molecular layer interneurons (MLI) inhibition45, thus our observations point to a potentially shared role for covariance outside firing rates in regulating cerebellar output relative to input.

These data lead us to advance the following hypothesis for how PC population covariance influences reach kinematics: During reach, mossy fibers fire proportional to reach velocity, driving both PC and nuclear firing rates through learned synaptic weights46–48. This rate code would account for the linear relationship seen between velocity and deceleration, and eumetric endpoints, but would not account for variability of deceleration relative to peak velocity and variable endpoints. Under conditions of high PC covariance, the efficacy of velocity-tuned mossy fibers would increase in the nuclei, driving higher nuclear firing rates, boosting relative deceleration. This modulatory role of PC covariance on nuclear response gain could account for the excess deceleration we observed associated with high covariance. This conjecture should be tested when technologies improve to adjust population structure without influencing overall firing rates.

The presence of Purkinje neuron beyond-chance synchrony across different species10,14,49—at least in some cerebellar regions50—raises the important question of the circuit mechanisms that promote it. Many proposed mechanisms have received empirical support, including shared parallel fiber afferents49, shared climbing fibers51, shared MLI inputs45, as well as ephaptic and gap junctional coupling between cerebellar cortical cell types52–54. The latter phenomena have been shown to synchronize MLIs which, through feedforward inhibition, regulate spike-timing and synchrony in PCs by shortening integration time windows for coincident excitation53–59. The relationship between MLIs and synchrony is particularly intriguing given the enhancement of PC co-firing during firing rate suppression and longer-window covariance14. Given this collection of potential mechanistic drivers of synchrony, it is interesting to note that not all reach-related PCs synchronized. We saw little synchrony or broader covariance between Cluster 3 or 4 PCs, which may suggest fewer shared inputs or a more disbursed anatomical distribution. Neuromodulatory states may also influence PC synchrony or other population relationships. The cerebellum receives noradrenergic, cholinergic, dopaminergic and hormonal inputs which could influence input-output relations of cerebellar circuitry to enhance or suppress spike synchrony and/or influence the efficacy of PC synchrony on nuclear neurons60–64. That said, PC synchrony may not be either engaged or effective in cerebellar regions with slower linear coding, such as the vestibular flocculus and vestibular nuclei, where IPSC kinetics are either slower or have not been tested50,65,66. It will be interesting to investigate whether such circuit elements may be differentially organized in regions with or without regulated PC synchrony, or if mechanisms exist that explicitly degrade PC synchrony.

While these data suggest a role for PC synchrony in regulating reach kinematics in intermediate cerebellum, the generalizability of these observations is unclear. In the macaque flocculus, synchrony levels do not fluctuate with behavior and levels of synchrony are low, diminishing the likelihood that synchrony serves a role in structuring behavior there50. Moreover, IPSC kinetics and response to synchrony within different cerebellar nuclei vary65–67. Regional specializations across cerebellar cortical regions also exist, with distinct distributions of unipolar brush cells, zebrin striping, density of Golgi cells, and cerebello-olivary interactions68–71. These circuit motif variations may lead to distinct computations supporting behaviors with diverse control system requirements. Smooth pursuit eye movements, which require the floccular complex, are non-ballistic, thus inertial non-linearities are minimal. By contrast, saccadic eye movements, which are supported by the oculomotor vermis, require accelerative and decelerative signals to overcome inertial forces. It is possible that these or other unique control requirements require distinct neural mechanisms across cerebellar regions. In line with this reasoning, the present data corroborate Heck et al.49, finding evidence for behaviorally modulated synchrony during reaching movements, which also require marked control of accelerative and decelerative forces.

Overall, our study presents evidence indicating that PC covariance structure is strongly associated with kinematics and works with firing rate to regulate downstream neuron activity and control movement. It will be interesting for future studies to investigate similar coding principles in long-range inhibitory neurons in other brain regions72, such as striatal and pallidal regions of the basal ganglia, noting that both healthy and pathological states may be influenced by synchronization73–75. Another important avenue of future investigation will be whether differential sensitivity to synchronous firing between large premotor projection neurons and small nucleo-olivary neurons mediates multiplexed coding in the cerebellum67,76. Moreover, associating pathological synchrony with cerebellar disorders could be a fruitful avenue of investigation77–79.

Methods

Experimental methods

All procedures followed National Institutes of Health guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus. Animals were housed in an environmentally controlled room, kept on a 12 h light/dark cycle and had free access to food and water, except during behavioral training and testing times. Adult C57BL/6 (Charles River Laboratories) mice of either sex (5 males and 2 females) were used in the experiments, but sex was not considered in the study design. The present dataset comprises high-density recordings previously reported13. Accordingly, surgical procedures and details of task design have been described in detail in the prior publication.

Reaching task

Briefly, after headplating, mice recovered for 2 days, then were food restricted to 80–90% of their original weight for reach training. Mice were trained to sit in a headfixed setup, and were presented with food pellets (20 mg, BioServ #F0163). The position of each pellet was moved further from the face until the mice reached with their forelimb to retrieve the pellet. To encourage the animal to reach with their right hand, the pellet was positioned slightly to the right of center at a consistent position for each mouse ( ~ 1.2–2.5 cm from starting position). Sessions lasted for 20 successful reaches or 30 minutes. The training process lasted 15 days and mice were considered proficient once they achieved a 50% success rate for 3 consecutive days.

Kinematic tracking

The position of the hand was tracked in real time using an infrared-based machine-vision motion-capture system (6 Optitrack Slim3U Cameras mounted with LED ring arrays, Motive software) at 120 frames-per-second as previously described13,20. The cameras were positioned in front of and to the right of the mouse and focused on the space that covered the right forelimb reach trajectory. For camera calibration and kinematic tracking, retroreflective markers (1 mm) were affixed to the animal’s hand. To set the position and orientation of the cameras, a custom-built calibration wand and ground plane were used, in Optitrack Motive software. Motion tracking was recalibrated periodically to correct for drift. The spatial origin was defined as the center of the bar the mice held during rest. Spatial blocking and camera detection threshold were adjusted to prevent erroneous tracking of other minimally infrared-reflective objects.

High density electrophysiological recordings

Craniotomies were made over the cerebellum ipsilateral to the reaching arm of the mice and a custom-made recording chamber was implanted over the craniotomy targeting Lobule 4/5 and medial Simplex. The brain was covered with triple-antibiotic ointment (GlobePharma, Inc), and the recording chamber was sealed with Qwik-sil silicone (World Precision Instruments) to facilitate multiple recording sessions. During recordings, a Neuropixels 1.0 probe was lowered into the brain using a motorized micromanipulator (Sensapex uMp micromanipulator). Once the electrode shank spanned the PC layer, we waited 15 min for tissue to stabilize before recording. Electrophysiology data were acquired using an OpenEphys system (https://open-ephys.org). Data were sorted offline in Kilosort280 and manually curated in Phy (https://github.com/cortex-lab/phy).

Data analysis

Purkinje cell identification and inclusion criteria

Units were classified as PCs or putative PCs (collectively termed PCs) as in Calame et al.13, after spike sorting. Briefly, one of two criteria were met. In the first, we identified complex spikes (CS), by cross correlating units with low firing rate with high-firing units and looking for the presence of CS-related simple spike pause, as well as characteristic simple spikes and CS waveforms. With our recording method, the CS was not always present in the recording. In these cases, PCs were identified based on recording location and electrophysiological criteria using the firing rate, CV2 and median absolute difference from the median interspike interval (MAD) as described in Hensbroek et al.81. Specifically, cortical units with firing rate >40 sp/s, CV2 > 0.2 and MAD < 0.008 were labeled as PCs (see Extended Data Fig. 2 in Calame et al. 2023). This method was found to correctly identify 95% of CS-confirmed PCs13. To mitigate concerns that high density probes and associated sorting algorithms may inflate synchrony measurements by sampling and sorting the same unit twice, we only analyzed units that did not share contacts on the recording probe.

Time-resolved PC firing during task

To examine how the PC firing rate changes in time around reach endpoint, we computed peri-event time histograms (PETH). For each trial, we aligned spikes to the time of maximal outward position (endpoint), spanning −1 s before to 750 ms after endpoint. Data were binned at 1 ms (1 if it fired; 0 if not). We computed the average probability of firing in 1-ms bins across trials and multiplied by 1000 to achieve an estimate of rate (spike/second (sp/s))82. We smoothed the resultant function with a 51-point Gaussian kernel (matlab’s conv2.m), normalized by its sum such that it did not change the overall firing rate of the PC.

Joint peri-event time histogram

We quantified event-related changes in joint firing between pairs of PCs using jPETHs as described previously1,26. Briefly, we first computed PETHs for each neuron aligned to reach endpoint (or maximal velocity where indicated). For each trial, we then computed a matrix in which the (i,j)th bin represented the number of coincident events where one unit fired in time bin i and the second unit fired in time bin j. Single trial matrices were summed across trials to obtain the raw jPETH (jPETHraw). To estimate the expected coincident firing due to rate modulation alone, we computed a shift predictor based on the reach-aligned PETHs of each neuron. The predicted matrix (jPETHpredict) was defined as the outer product of the single-neuron PETHs, such that the (i,j)th bin was equal to PETH1(i)*PETH2(j), where PETH1 and PETH2 were the reach-aligned responses of units 1 and 2, respectively. We then obtained a corrected jPETH by subtracting the predicted from raw matrix (jPETHraw − jPETHpredict). The jPETH matrices were computed using a bin size of 1 ms and then smoothed with a two-dimensional Gaussian kernel (σ = 3 ms).

Synchrony-behavioral correlation

To quantify the relationship between neuronal co-firing and behavior, we correlated the diagonal of the jPETH matrix with kinematic variables stratified by quartile (visually described in Supplementary Fig. 8). For analyses of peak velocity and deceleration, jPETHs were aligned to peak velocity for both peak velocity. For, endpoint position and deceleration error, jPETHs were aligned to reach endpoint. We then computed the Pearson correlation coefficient (matlab corr.m) between the 1-ms-resolved jPETH with kinematic measurement across quartiles across different time bins.

Model of PC-nuclear integration

To model the consequences of PC cofluctuations across timescales we created PC pseudopopulations comprised of 20 pairs of simultaneously recorded Cluster 1-2 PCs. Trials were selected by having cofiring in the range of either high- or low-deceleration error (high: 150 ms pre-endpoint cofiring, between 93 and 153 sp2/s2; low: cofiring between 28.6 and 92.8 sp2/s2. These ranges are within 3 standard deviations of the mean cofiring for each condition (Fig. 3o). For each pair a random 15 trials were chosen to construct a paired firing rate of the two units, and these were combined across pairs to construct PC population spike trains to test in the model.

We then used these trains to examine integration of IPSCs in a model of the cerebellar nuclei. Nuclear cells were modeled using a reduced model of neuronal spiking dynamics30 which combines the simplicity of an integrate-and-fire model for spiking without explicit modeling of Hodgkin-Huxley type conductances. The spiking behavior of the model is set by a variety of parameters within a term U that determines the burstiness of the modeled neuron. Each PC spike within the population drove an IPSC with 2.5 ms decay time and 5 nS unitary conductance.

To relate the in silico model to dynamic clamp experiments, we used a different method to generate PC population activity. We first generated a kernel of time-varying firing rates in a population of 40 Purkinje cells from a random draw of 40 of our recorded PCs in clusters 1 + 2. This time-varying average firing rate was then used as the rate parameter passed to a Poisson random number generator (poissrnd.m in matlab). This process resulted in a collection of 40 simulated PCs, which had time-varying firing rates matching the average population activity, but with spike times randomized for each cell.

To match the in vivo calculated CCGs with synchrony in the modeled population (Fig. 2), we organized 40 units into 20 randomly assigned pairs. For each pair, we drew a CCG target value from the distribution of CCGs, then adjusted spiketimes such that the spiketrain matched the CCG value. To impose synchronize, we chose a random non-synchronous spike then moved a spike from the other unit to synchronize with it. This was performed as many times as necessary to match the CCG. Note that this process also preserves the overall firing rate. To test the relationship of nuclear firing to PC CCG peak values, we used the same procedure but systematically varied CCG peak values in modeled populations.

Acute brain slice preparation

Tissue preparation

Postnatal day 32–47 C57BL/6 mice of either sex (n = 22 mice, 10 females 12 males) were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane before transcardial perfusion with warmed Tyrode’s solution containing (in mM) 123.75 NaCl, 26 NaHCO3, 10 glucose, 3.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 1.5 CaCl2, and 1 MgCl2. Solutions were oxygenated with 95% O2, 5% CO2. Following perfusion, tissue was harvested and 300 µm thick parasagittal slices were made using a Leica VT1000 S vibratome (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Slices were incubated at 34 °C for 1 h before being stored at room temperature until recording (up to approx. 5 h).

Data acquisition

Slices were transferred to a recording chamber and were continuously perfused with oxygenated Tyrode’s solution at 32 °C - 34 °C which included synaptic blockers SR95531 (gabazine, 10 µM), 6,7-dinitroquinoxaline-2,3-dione (DNQX, 10 µM), and (R)-CPP (10 µM). All drugs were purchased from Tocris (Bristol, United Kingdom). Glass patch pipettes (Sutter Instruments BF150-86-7.5) were pulled using a Sutter P97 (Sutter Instrument, Novato, CA, USA) to produce tip resistances of 2 – 4 MΩ and were filled with a K-gluconate intracellular solution (290 mOsm, pH 7.37) consisting of 132 K-gluconate, 4.4 Na-gluconate, 4.4 NaCl, 2.2 MgCl2, 1.1 EGTA, 11 HEPES, 22 sucrose, 14 TRIS creatine phosphate, 4 Mg-ATP, 0.3 and TRIS-GTP. Stable whole-cell recordings were acquired at 100 kHz in pClamp 10.3 and pClamp 10.7 (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA) via an Axon 700B multiclamp amplifier and Digidata 1440 digitizer. Cells were visually identified and included for recording based on an access resistance below 20MΩ, the demonstration of a robust fast inward sodium current in voltage clamp, and overshooting action potentials in current clamp.

Dynamic Clamp

Dynamic clamp experiments were conducted based on hardware and software designs described previously85. IPSGs were simulated with characteristics described previously17, specifically, 5 nS peak conductance with a 100 µsec rise time and 2.5 msec decay time. IPSC commands were conveyed to the dynamic clamp from the digitizer via TTL pulses with a 10-microsecond pulse width. The 100 kHz sampling rate allowed us to simulate synaptic integration at instantaneous rates of up to 50 kHz and thus allowed us to model a pool of 40 convergent Purkinje cells with 1 millisecond synchrony. Purkinje cell trains were constructed based on previously published PC population firing rates during reaching movements13. Briefly, interspike intervals of a PC “seed” trace were established to reproduce a PETH from a given reach velocity range (0−20%; 60–80% and 80–100% peak speed). Spike times for 39 additional “PCs” within that “pool” were then generated from the seed trace, where new spike times were calculated by jittering each spike time as a random draw from a normal distribution with varying standard deviations, varied between 9 and 2 ms. This method allowed us to modulate population synchrony dynamically throughout the reach epoch by modulating the standard deviation of these jitter Gaussians, maintaining the net time-varying population activity observed in Calame et al.13. Finally, for experiments modulating PC rate and synchrony, PC pools with mean rates of 40–100 Hz in 10 Hz intervals were generated. Within these pools, mean covariance was swept from 0 to 2 spks/s.

Statistical analysis

Data presented in the manuscript treat each neuron as an independent sample. All data were tested for normality using Kolmogrov–Smirnov test to choose the proper statistical test. All t-tests or Wilcoxon’s signed rank are paired, unless stated otherwise.

No statistical tests were used to predetermine required sample size. Randomizations and controls are described in the main text and methods. The experimenters were not blind to conditions during experiments and outcome assessments. Nonparametric tests were used when the dataset violated normality assumptions. Corrections for multiple comparisons were used to adjust significance thresholds where noted.

All data analyses were made in Matlab (Mathworks, 2020). Raw data and waveform analysis were made in Python and Visual Studio.
