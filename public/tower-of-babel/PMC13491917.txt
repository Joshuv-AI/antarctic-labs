Distinct gut microbiome profiles characterize obese and non-obese patients with atherosclerosis: a metagenomic analysis
Authors: Argul Issilbayeva, Elizaveta Vinogradova, Laura Chulenbayeva, Samat Kozhakhmetov, Zharkyn Jarmukhanov, Gulzhan Myrzakhmetova, Aleksei Umriukhin, Saltanat Andossova, Makhabbat Bekbossynova, Almagul Kushugulova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity is widely recognized as an aggravating risk factor for atherosclerosis (AS), yet the effects of obesity on AS-associated microbiome dysbiosis are not sufficiently characterized. This study aims to identify the contribution of obesity-related dysbiosis in AS.MethodsUsing shotgun metagenomic sequencing, we studied gut microbiome composition and functional capacity across non-obese AS patients (AS-NOB, BMI < 30, n = 93), age-sex-matched non-obese controls (Ctrl-NOB, BMI < 30, n = 27), and obese AS patients (AS-OB, BMI ≥ 30, n = 68).ResultsGut community composition differed significantly across study groups (PERMANOVA F = 3.23, p = 0.001). Among metadata, obesity had the strongest effect (F = 3.1, p < 0.01) on the microbiome structure of AS patients. Furthermore, obese AS patients demonstrated a decrease in species richness and evenness (p < 0.05). Taxonomic and functional analysis further suggested that obesity does not simply aggravate AS-associated gut dysbiosis but instead redirects it towards a distinct community state, characterized by a Prevotella expansion that consistently opposes the rest of the bacterial community. Among AS microbiome markers, an increase was detected in Pseudomonadota (Proteobacteria), Bilophila, Dysosmobacter, and Faecalibacterium.ConclusionTaken together, these results suggest that pathological expansion of Prevotella, potentially in conjunction with reduced alpha diversity, may represent a putative indicator of increased risk in AS patients, particularly within populations where the Prevotella enterotype or subtype is prevalent, warranting further investigation.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08739-1.Keywords: Atherosclerosis, Obesity, Gut microbiome, Shotgun sequencing, Metabolic pathways, Metagenomics

Background

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 17.9 million deaths annually [1]. Despite advances in risk factor management and therapeutic interventions, the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis (AS) involves complex interactions between traditional risk factors, systemic inflammation, and emerging metabolic contributors [2, 3]. Obesity, affecting over 650 million adults globally [4] substantially amplifies atherosclerotic risk through mechanisms that extend beyond lipid abnormalities and hypertension. Recent evidence suggests that the gut microbiome plays a pivotal role in cardiovascular health by modulating host metabolism, immune responses, and production of bioactive metabolites [5–8], contributing to AS development through multiple pathways [9]. Specific microbial taxa have been associated with pro-atherogenic or cardioprotective effects, with compositional shifts observed in patients with established cardiovascular disease [10–12]. Obesity further complicates this relationship, as adiposity independently alters gut microbial composition, promotes inflammation, and disrupts metabolic homeostasis [13–15]. However, whether obese and non-obese individuals with atherosclerosis own distinct microbiome signatures remains poorly characterized.

Most existing microbiome studies in cardiovascular disease have focused on heterogeneous patient populations without stratifying by obesity status, limiting our understanding of how body mass influences microbial-host interactions in atherosclerosis.

We hypothesized that obese and non-obese patients with atherosclerosis would exhibit distinct gut microbiome profiles at both taxonomic and functional levels. Our study aims to identify the contribution of obesity-related dysbiosis to AS pathogenesis. To address this, we conducted a comprehensive metagenomic study comparing gut microbiome composition and functional capacity across three groups. Our findings reveal group-specific microbiome patterns that position obesity as a primary determinant of gut microbiome composition in atherosclerosis.

Methods

Study design and enrollment

To achieve the study goal, a cross-sectional study design was performed. The study enrolled 188 participants categorized into three distinct groups based on atherosclerosis diagnosis and obesity status. The study groups comprised: 27 controls without atherosclerotic disease and obesity (Ctrl-NOB), 93 patients with atherosclerosis but without obesity (AS-NOB). And 68 patients with atherosclerosis and obesity (AS-OB).

Participant recruitment was conducted at the University Medical Center, “Heart Center”, Nazarbayev University, Astana, Kazakhstan, from March to December 2024. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the National Laboratory Astana, Nazarbayev University (protocol No. 05–2023, approved 21 November 2023) and conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. Inclusion criteria required participants to be aged ≥ 18 years and willing to complete all study procedures.

Exclusion criteria were applied across all groups: consumption of antimicrobials, probiotic supplements, antiviral medications within 90 days before enrollment; documented inflammatory or autoimmune conditions; major chronic diseases; metabolic disorders; immunocompromised status; gastrointestinal surgical interventions or infections within the preceding six months; pregnancy or lactation; inability to provide informed consent.

All participants underwent a comprehensive clinical assessment, including physical examination, anthropometric measurements, and cardiovascular evaluation.

Atherosclerosis diagnosis was established according to European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines through clinical, imaging, and laboratory assessment.

All participants underwent a detailed medical history review, including prior cardiovascular events and revascularization procedures. The diagnostic evaluation comprised: coronary angiography, computed tomography coronary artery calcium scoring, Doppler ultrasonography of brachiocephalic and peripheral vessels, ankle-brachial index measurement, stress and resting echocardiography, 12-lead electrocardiography, and SCORE2/SCORE2-OP cardiovascular risk assessment. Laboratory evaluation included general blood and urine tests, lipid profile, glucose metabolism markers, and inflammatory markers.

Obesity classification followed World Health Organization (WHO) criteria: participants with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 were classified as obese, while those with BMI < 30 kg/m2 were classified as non-obese.

Control participants met stringent inclusion criteria: absence of atherosclerotic disease, no history of cardiovascular events, BMI < 30 kg/m2, and no use of cardiovascular or metabolic medications. The study flowchart is presented in Fig. 1.

Study flowchart

Biospecimen collection and processing

Fecal specimens were obtained within 48 hours of enrollment utilizing Zymo Research DNA/RNA Shield™ Collection Tubes. Participants received detailed written and verbal instructions for proper sample collection and storage. Specimens were transported to the National Laboratory Astana (NLA) within 24 hours of collection and immediately cryopreserved at −80 °C pending analysis.

Microbiome profiling and analysis

DNA extraction and sequencing

Microbial DNA was extracted from fecal samples using the ZymoBIOMICS DNA Miniprep Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocols. DNA quality and concentration were assessed via spectrophotometry, NanoDrop 2000, fluorometry, Qubit 3.0, and gel agarose electrophoresis.

Shotgun metagenomic libraries were constructed following established protocols. Paired-end sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 with a minimum sequencing depth of 6 Gb per sample to ensure adequate microbial genomic coverage.

Bioinformatic analysis

Raw sequencing data underwent quality control and preprocessing to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads using Trimmomatic (version 0.39). Taxonomic profiling was conducted using MetaPhlAn (version 4). Functional annotation was performed via HUMAnN 3 with the UniRef90 reference database for metabolic pathway identification.

Statistical analysis

Formal analysis and visualization were performed in Python v3.12.2, using NumPy v1.26.4, pandas v2.2.1, SciPy v1.15.1, scikit-learn v1.6.1, LightGBM v4.5.0, scikit-bio v0.6.3, Matplotlib v3.8.2, seaborn v0.13.2, and matplotlib_venn v1.1.1 libraries. phyloT v2 service was used for phylogenetic tree construction. Comparison of demographic parameters was conducted using independent t-tests with or without Welch’s correction, Mann-Whitney U tests, Fisher’s exact tests, or chi-square tests, where appropriate. Homoscedasticity was assessed using Levene’s test, and normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Demographic data were described using median and interquartile range (IQR) or n (%), as appropriate. Before analysis, taxonomic data were winsorized using ±3 IQR fences. Winsorization is a classic approach for data preparation. It shifts outliers toward more probable (but still extreme) values while retaining their relative ordering and the exact median of the overall sample. It is used to decrease the impact of outliers on analysis. Winsorization has recently been gaining more interest in microbiome analysis [16–18] and differential expression analysis [19] due to its ease and transparent implementation, retention of the exact sample median, and reduced false positive rate when parametric methods are used. Gut enterotype was estimated at the family level based on the most abundant taxa in each sample. Beta diversity was assessed using the Bray–Curtis distance on Hellinger-transformed data at the genus level. The significance of grouping was assessed using PERMANOVA with 999 permutations; equality of variances was checked using PERMDISP. Ordination was performed using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Alpha diversity was evaluated using the Observed and Pielou indices at the species level. Only taxa with at least 70% prevalence (in any group) were considered for differential analysis. Differential analysis of taxonomic and inferred pathway abundance data was conducted using the two-sample Hodges–Lehmann estimator (HLE), as described in Vinogradova E. et al. study [16]. This approach relies on the use of strictly non-overlapping distribution independent confidence intervals for difference between groups for differential analysis. The use of this method guarantees that only differences that have a clear non-overlapping visual separation between data points of groups are marked as differentially abundant. Accordingly, our recent research [16] indicates that this estimator yields a robust or even more stringent—estimate of differential abundance compared to that obtained using linear or no-parametric standard analyses with FDR correction. Only comparisons with 95% (α = 0.05) HLE confidence intervals (CIs) that excluded zero were considered significant. The results of differential analysis are reported using HLE pseudo-median and HLE IQR. A cladogram of differentially abundant taxonomic markers was built using linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) utilities [20]. Correlation analysis between taxonomic and inferred pathway markers was conducted using Spearman’s rho coefficient.

To account for possible confounders, we: (i) carefully matched the control group with the non-obese atherosclerosis group; (ii) separated the obese atherosclerosis group into a distinct category; and (iii) conducted three independent comparisons (Ctrl-NOB × AS-NOB; Ctrl-NOB × AS-OB; AS-NOB × AS-OB) to distill the effects of atherosclerosis from obesity, and obesity in atherosclerosis.

Gut microbiome characteristics

Gut microbiome diversity

Comparison of alpha diversity metrics across the study groups revealed a significant reduction in the unique taxonomic units in the AS-OB group relative to both Ctrl-NOB, p ≤ 0.01, and AS-NOB, p ≤ 0.01, subjects. Bacterial evenness was similarly reduced in AS-OB compared to AS-NOB (p ≤ 0.05), while no significant differences were found in the rest pairwise comparisons. Prevotella/Bacteroides (P/B) ratio did not differ significantly across study groups (p > 0.05), Fig. 2A.

Beta diversity analysis revealed a significant separation of microbial communities across the three groups. PCoA accounted for 43.6% of total variance (PC1 = 26.0%, PC2 = 17.6%), with significant differences in community compositions between study groups (PERMANOVA, F = 3.23, p = 0.001), while no significant differences in within-group dispersion were found (PERMDISP, F = 2.11, p = 0.154), Fig. 2B.

Additionally, PERMANOVA was performed for all metadata independently, with only BMI (p < 0.01) and sex (p < 0.05) associated with gut microbial community structure, Fig. 2C.

At the family level, Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae, Prevotellaceae, and unclassified Clostridia were the most abundant taxa across all three groups (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Table 3). Notably, Prevotellaceae showed a trend toward higher abundance across the groups (Ctrl-NOB: 6.94%, AS-NOB: 13.68%, AS-OB: 19.01%), while unclassified Clostridia showed a trend toward lower relative abundance in the same direction (Ctrl-NOB: 13.82%, AS-NOB: 7.55%, AS-OB: 5.85%). Notably, in all study groups, precisely in AS-OB, Prevotellaceae appeared to be the visually dominant family, progressively occupying a larger proportion of the microbial community at the expense of other families, Fig. 2D. Detailed descriptive statistics for all taxonomic levels are provided in Table S2.

Discussion

Obesity and atherosclerosis frequently coexist, however most microbiome studies related to atherosclerosis lack the examination of body weight’s specific contribution. We therefore studied whether obese and non-obese atherosclerosis patients differ in gut microbiome composition and function. So, we defined groups based on BMI threshold, precisely Ctrl-NOB (BMI < 30), AS-NOB (BMI < 30), and AS-OB (BMI ≥ 30). To account for possible confounders, we matched the control group by age, sex, and BMI with the non-obese atherosclerosis group, and by age and sex alone with the obese atherosclerosis group, and conducted three independent comparisons (Ctrl-NOB × AS-NOB; Ctrl-NOB × AS-OB; AS-NOB × AS-OB) to distill the effects of atherosclerosis from obesity, and obesity in atherosclerosis. This approach partly corresponds to a recent study by Palanisamy et al., focused on the gut microbiome’s role in obesity-related atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [21]. However, keeping overweight individuals within the non-obese groups, rather than excluding them, makes our design different, reflecting the real-world distribution of body weight in a cardiology practice more accurately than comparing only extremes.

Gut community composition differed significantly in our study across the three groups (PERMANOVA F = 3.23, p = 0.001). Among all metadata, BMI had the strongest effect (F = 3.1, p < 0.01), while sex was the second significant contributor (F = 2.0, p < 0.05). The fact that BMI outperformed key cardiometabolic markers as a community-level predictor is consistent with obesity being a main driver of microbiome structure in these patients. Sex differences in gut microbiome composition are well documented, with microbiome diversity and structure varying broadly based on gender across BMI categories [22].

Alpha diversity showed modest changes in our study, with the Observed index being lower in AS-OB compared to both other groups (p ≤ 0.01), suggesting a selective loss of rare taxa. The Shannon index was lower in AS-OB compared to AS-NOB, suggesting community disruption between these groups. This is notable because obesity alone does not reliably reduce diversity. Pinart et al. found no significant overall difference in Shannon diversity between obese and non-obese adults across 22 studies [23]. The reduction we observed may reflect the combined complex effect of atherosclerosis and obesity on the microbial ecosystem rather than either condition alone. The AS-OB group also had higher CRP and HbA1c and more hepatic steatosis, which could also contribute [21, 24].

Regardless of obesity status, both AS groups differed from controls in a consistent way with enrichment of Clostridium, Dysosmobacter, Faecalibacterium, Bilophila, and depletion of Bifidobacterium, Anaerobutyricum, Romboutsia, and the archaeon Methanobrevibacter. This core microbiome pattern is basically consistent with previous microbiome findings [25, 26], suggesting it is driven by atherosclerosis rather than obesity. Faecalibacterium, a butyrate producer, enrichment in atherosclerosis patients may represent a compensatory anti-inflammatory response [12], and its higher abundance in patients with higher physical activity and HDL is consistent with this idea, although it does not confirm it.

Dysosmobacter showed an inverse relationship with high LDL levels (p ≤ 0.01). Its enrichment in atherosclerosis patients may also reflect a compensatory response, in terms of metabolic disturbances associated with atherosclerosis and obesity. This interpretation corresponds to the recent Hase et al. study reporting that Dysosmobacter reduces fat mass, improves glucose tolerance, and decreases adipose tissue inflammation in high-fat diet-fed mice. Additionally, its abundance inversely correlated with BMI and hyperglycemia in people with metabolic syndrome [27]. These findings come from animal models, though, and cannot be inferred from our cross-sectional correlations, so the compensatory role stays a hypothesis.

Among the rest AS shared markers, Bilophila, in particular Bilophila wadsworthia was notable for its association with hypertension (p ≤ 0.01). This taxon is known for the ability to produce hydrogen sulfide, which could have pro-inflammatory effects [28], which at elevated concentrations also inhibits endothelial nitric oxide production and may contribute to endothelial dysfunction [29]. Whether Bilophila enrichment is a cause or consequence of hypertension cannot be determined from our data, but the consistency of this association across both AS groups makes it a focus for further investigation. This is partly consistent with Natividad et al., who demonstrated that Bilophila wadsworthia synergizes with high-fat diet to promote inflammation and metabolic dysfunction in murine models, suggesting its pathogenic potential may extend beyond dietary contexts [30].

Among obesity markers identified in our study, Prevotella was the most notable, and not solely because of its abundance. Prevotella is a well-studied obesity-related taxon, with its enrichment consistently reported in association with higher BMI, fat accumulation, and worse cardiometabolic profiles across human and animal studies [31–33]. Across every level of our analysis, Prevotella diverged from the rest of the community. In the PCA, Prevotella projected opposite to all other key genera, marking an isolated community-level pattern rather than just one of several obesity-associated taxa. It also showed the broadest negative correlation pattern among all key taxa, including the butyrate producers, which corresponds to accumulated studies on Prevotella’s proinflammatory, virulent features [34, 35]. At the family level, Prevotellaceae abundance was significantly associated with both higher BMI and sex, two variables that most strongly structured the overall microbiome community, consistent with, but not proof of, a key role for Prevotella in shaping the gut microbiome in atherosclerosis. Still, the enterotype distribution and the Prevotella/Bacteroides ratio did not differ significantly between groups, so this is a graded rise in Prevotella abundance, not a switch to a different enterotype.

Functional analysis was conducted focusing on inferred pathways that changed consistently across both atherosclerosis groups compared to non-obese controls, linking the key taxa identified in this study to a shared functional signature.

Bilophila showed the strongest positive correlation with L-citrulline metabolism predicted pathway abundance, which may reflect microbial consumption of this substrate, potentially limiting its availability. As citrulline is a key substrate for nitric oxide production, one speculative interpretation is that Bilophila enrichment in obese atherosclerosis patients may contribute to impaired vascular function, additionally to its pro-inflammatory properties [36].

Faecalibacterium followed a functional pattern largely similar to Dysosmobacter, with positive correlations with the inferred functional capacity in energy metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, and amino acid biosynthesis pathways, supporting their shared feature as butyrate producers with potentially protective roles in the atherosclerotic gut. Depletion of such taxa and their associated fermentative functions has been consistently reported in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [25, 37], which does not align with our findings and increases interest in them. Nonetheless, it may be hypothesized that a relative increase in Faecalibacterium observed in this study arises from a cross-feeding event, in which Prevotella serves as a primary complex fiber degrader while Faecalibacterium efficiently utilizes the resulting byproducts. Thus relative Prevotella expansion could have led to concurrent Faecalibacterium increase. Consistent with this, Faecalibacterium has been shown to possess an extensive enzymatic repertoire that targets β-mannooligosaccharides released by adjacent colonic bacteria. However, co-culturing isolates from atherosclerosis patients under diverse conditions is needed to prove this connection.

Across all functional pathway categories, Prevotella and Dysosmobacter showed consistently opposite correlation patterns. Prevotella correlated positively with the capacity for cell structure biosynthesis, SCFA fermentation, nucleotide biosynthesis, and some carbohydrate degradation pathways, while Dysosmobacter showed a very opposite functional capacity. Since Prevotella was enriched in obese atherosclerosis patients, this functional opposition suggests that Dysosmobacter may counterbalance the metabolic consequences of obesity-driven dysbiosis in AS patients. This opposing relationship corresponds to a previous study, reporting controversial interaction between pro- and anti-inflammatory taxa in cardiometabolic disease [24], though our data cannot confirm such an interaction, which stays a hypothesis.

However, since this study only involved analysis of inferred functional capacity, these observations cannot be interpreted as confirmatory evidence of direct mechanistic links.

Taken together, the results of the present analysis suggest that pathological expansion of Prevotella, possibly coupled with loss of diversity, could serve as a putative indicator of increased patient risk, particularly in populations where the Prevotella enterotype or subtype are prevalent.

Diet can serve as a significant and therapeutic modifier of microbiome composition, yet responses to dietary interventions are not consistently uniform among individuals [38]. Despite rapid advances in personalized and precision nutrition research [39], delivering personalized recommendations at the gut microbiome level remains a challenge [40].

Prevotella is a fiber-diet associated taxon. It possesses an extensive repertoire of carbohydrate-active enzymes, enabling proliferation on high-fiber diets through the metabolism of complex plant carbohydrates into organic acids, including short-chain fatty acids, fumarate, and succinate.

Consistent with Prevotella‘s carbohydrate utilization capacity, elimination of carbohydrates from the diet has been observed to trigger a sharp decrease in Prevotella abundance in the human microbiome, compensated by an increase in all other bacteria, with restoration to baseline levels four weeks after the dietary restriction ended [41]. Similarly, high-fiber diets were shown to increase metabolic activity of P. copri and exacerbate intestinal inflammation via succinate overproduction in a rheumatoid arthritis model [42]. Thus, while dietary fiber is generally associated with increased microbial diversity and gut health [43], its benefits may not be universal, particularly in cases of Prevotella overgrowth. Another study demonstrated that P. copri increased serum glucose, insulin and interleukin-1β levels in mice on a high-fat diet, but not on standard diet, indicating that Prevotella may exert conditional pathogenicity in the context of metabolic or related disorders [31].

Additionally, substantial functional variation exists across Prevotella species and strains. Prevotella species exhibit distinct, strain-specific preferences for different dietary fibers; therefore, the selection of the most beneficial dietary intervention strategy should potentially be informed by the patient’s microbiome structure [44].

Looking forward, continued research into microbiome modulation in atherosclerosis is warranted, investigating precision nutrition and development of novel precision probiotics. We hypothesize that, given the lack of consensus on fiber’s effects on Prevotella abundance, the choice between general carbohydrate-restricted diets, fiber-enriched diets, or specific-fiber regimens should be guided by individual microbiome profiling and personalized longitudinal microbiome testing to maximize benefits. Future interventional studies are urgently needed to establish evidence-based guidelines for modulating microbiome structure in the atherosclerosis patients in clinical settings.

This study has several important methodological limitations. Firstly, the cross-sectional design does not allow to estimate whether the taxonomic shifts observed in this study are the cause or consequence of atherosclerosis, nor to track possibly non-linear dynamics of taxonomic change within the natural history of atherosclerosis progression. Secondly, preliminary analysis suggested comparable enterotype distribution across study groups; however, the relatively small sample size (n = 27) of healthy matched individuals may not adequately capture all of the natural variability of the gut microbiome of the studied population, limiting generalizability. In addition, although the obese and non-obese atherosclerosis groups were comparable in age, sex, statin use, total cholesterol, LDL, and Lp(a), they differed in a few related parameters such as HbA1c, HDL, CRP, and the prevalence of hepatic steatosis and hepatomegaly. As these tend to accompany obesity, the differences between the groups likely reflect this broader profile rather than body weight alone, and some residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Finally, the study involved analysis of inferred functional capacity of taxonomic communities, without validating inferred pathways with targeted metabolomics, limiting our ability to confirm the proposed mechanistic links.

Conclusion

Taken together, these results indicate that pathological expansion of Prevotella, potentially in conjunction with reduced alpha diversity, may represent a putative indicator of increased risk in atherosclerosis patients, particularly within populations where the Prevotella enterotype or subtype are prevalent. The data further suggest that obesity does not simply aggravate atherosclerosis-associated gut dysbiosis but instead synergistically precipitate a collapse toward a distinct community state defined by Prevotella expansion. This state continuously opposes the remainder of the microbial community in both composition and function, thereby perpetuating dysbiosis and potentially exacerbating metabolic dysfunction. However, the cross-sectional design of this study does not allow causal inference, and further longitudinal studies are required to determine causality and make relevant assumptions.
