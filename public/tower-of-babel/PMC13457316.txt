A Comparative Evaluation of Xylitol, Sorbitol, and Isomalt-containing Candies and Their Effects on Salivary pH and Salivary Flow Rate in Children Aged 6–12 Years: An Ex Vivo Study
Authors: Takhellambam Arjun Singh, Swati Dwivedi, Vinod Kumar Upadhyay, Ahsan Abdullah, Sofia Mukhtar, Mohammed Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDental caries is one of the most widespread oral health problems in children, often resulting from the interaction between dietary sugars, oral bacteria, and salivary factors. Saliva plays a key role in protecting teeth by neutralizing acids and supporting remineralization. However, when children consume sugary foods and drinks—particularly those with sucrose—it can cause the salivary pH to drop, increasing the risk of tooth decay. Sugar substitutes such as xylitol, sorbitol, and isomalt are widely used in preventive dental care, but their effects on children's salivary pH and flow rate remain poorly understood.AimThis study aimed to evaluate and compare the effects of candies containing xylitol, sorbitol, and isomalt on salivary pH and salivary flow rate in children aged 6–12 years using an ex vivo model.Materials and methodsA total of 100 healthy children were randomly divided into four equal groups (n = 25): xylitol, sorbitol, isomalt, and control. Salivary samples were collected at four time points: before and after consuming a sweetened beverage, after the intervention (candy or water rinse), and 15 minutes postintervention. Salivary pH was measured using a calibrated digital pH meter, and flow rate was assessed by weighing collected saliva with a portable electronic scale. Children were kept under standard conditions to minimize variability, and a double-blinded approach was used. Data were analyzed using SPSS v25 and STATA v15, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsAll three polyol groups showed a significant improvement in salivary pH and flow rate after intervention when compared to the control group. Xylitol demonstrated the strongest ability to restore and buffer salivary pH, followed by isomalt and sorbitol. Sorbitol, however, produced the highest increase in salivary flow. At 15 minutes postintervention, pH levels in the polyol groups returned close to baseline, whereas the control group's pH remained low.ConclusionXylitol-containing candies proved to be the most effective overall, improving both salivary pH and flow—critical factors in reducing the risk of caries. Sorbitol and isomalt also showed beneficial effects, particularly in stimulating saliva. These findings support the targeted use of sugar-free polyol candies as part of caries-prevention strategies in children.Keywords: Chewables, Chewing gums, Isomalt, Pediatric dentistry, Preventive dentistry, Sorbitol, Sucrose, Xylitol

Introduction

Dental caries is the most prevalent oral disease in children worldwide, driven by both biological and environmental factors. Saliva plays a key protective role by buffering acids and offering antimicrobial defense, yet it can also contribute to caries when interacting with dietary sugars like sucrose, creating an acidic environment that promotes enamel demineralization.1–3 This can negatively impact a child's nutrition, self-esteem, academic performance, and overall health. Sucrose, the primary cariogenic sugar, lowers salivary pH through bacterial fermentation.4 Preventive measures such as fluoride use, dietary changes, and sugar substitution therapies have shown promise.5 Sugar substitutes, including both nutritive polyols like xylitol and sorbitol and nonnutritive options such as aspartame, are increasingly used for their health benefits, particularly in managing caries and systemic conditions such as diabetes.6,7

Xylitol, in particular, is a noncariogenic sugar alcohol that inhibits Streptococcus mutans and supports saliva production, which helps neutralize acids and repair enamel.8 Xylitol, sorbitol, isomalt, etc., containing sugar-free gums and candies, historically rooted in oral hygiene, are now widely endorsed by pediatric dental authorities and serve as delivery vehicles for therapeutic agents. However, excessive intake of polyols, especially in young children, can cause gastrointestinal side effects.9 Despite the availability of such alternatives, caries prevalence remains high due to inconsistent usage and a lack of understanding of how saliva's pH and flow rate—affected by diet, hydration, systemic health, and medications—influence caries risk. Therefore, more research is needed to optimize the use of sugar substitutes and deepen our understanding of saliva's role in dental caries prevention.

A standardized saliva collection protocol was followed, with children seated upright, consistent waiting times, and clear instructions. Calibrated instruments were used, including an auto digital pH meter (LT-11, Labtronics, India) for pH measurements and a portable electronic digital weighing scale (beatXP, India) for assessing salivary flow rate. Participants were randomly allocated to groups to reduce selection bias, and a double-blinded analysis was employed to minimize observer bias. Potential sources of variability included interindividual differences in baseline salivary pH and flow rate, intraindividual fluctuations due to hydration, stress, or minor dietary changes, minor instrumental measurement errors, and environmental factors such as room temperature and humidity. To minimize these variations, standardized collection conditions—including posture, timing, and instructions—were maintained, along with a fasting period of 2 hours before sample collection, consistent use of equipment for all measurements, and randomization to evenly distribute individual differences.

Discussion

Tooth decay, or dental caries, remains one of the most common chronic diseases worldwide, particularly affecting children during the development of their teeth. This complex condition arises from the interaction of specific oral bacteria, dietary sugars, especially sucrose, and host-related factors, ultimately leading to localized demineralization of the tooth's hard tissues.10

Sucrose is especially cariogenic due to its dual role: it acts as a fermentable carbohydrate and serves as a substrate for the formation of extracellular and intracellular polysaccharides (EPS and IPS) within dental plaque.10 These polysaccharides, particularly insoluble glucans produced by S. mutans, increase the density and structural stability of the biofilm, making it easier for bacteria to adhere to tooth surfaces. This thickened matrix also allows sugars to diffuse deeper, prolonging acid exposure and accelerating enamel breakdown.10

This aligns with the ecological plaque hypothesis proposed by Marsh, which suggests that prolonged acidic conditions can shift the microbial balance toward acid-producing and acid-tolerant species, thereby exacerbating caries development.10,11–16 Additionally, the presence of sucrose in the oral environment reduces critical mineral levels, such as calcium, phosphate, and fluoride, within the biofilm.10 This hinders the natural process of remineralization by promoting mineral dissolution at low pH, competing with enamel binding sites, and physically blocking remineralization with the EPS layer.10 As a result, limiting sucrose intake remains a key component in preventing dental caries.10 This is especially important in children aged 6–12, who are undergoing active oral development, including the eruption of permanent molars and changes in saliva composition.17,18 Early preventive measures during this stage can have lasting effects on oral health.

Saliva plays a critical protective role by buffering acids, helping to remineralize enamel, and clearing leftover sugars from the mouth.19 In recent years, sugar-free candies containing polyols, such as xylitol, sorbitol, and isomalt, have gained attention for their caries-preventive potential. These sugar substitutes are either nonfermentable or only minimally fermentable by oral bacteria, which helps interrupt the usual acid-production cycle.20,21 Xylitol, in particular, has demonstrated strong anticariogenic effects by inhibiting S. mutans, increasing salivary flow, and raising plaque pH.20–22

In the current study, xylitol emerged as the most effective polyol in promoting oral health, particularly by restoring and buffering plaque pH, a key factor in caries prevention. Sorbitol, on the other hand, was found to significantly stimulate salivary flow, an important protective mechanism against tooth decay. Isomalt demonstrated moderate effects, likely due to its intermediate fermentability and capacity to promote saliva production.20–23 These distinct outcomes underscore the unique properties of each polyol and suggest that their targeted use could enhance preventive strategies in dental care. Supporting this, Mäkinen et al. proposed that pentitols, such as xylitol, may be more effective in preventing dental caries than hexitols, such as sorbitol. This idea is backed by research showing that daily intake of 5 grams of xylitol, whether in chewing gum or lozenge form, can reduce tooth decay by 35 to 60% in children around the age of 10, with both delivery methods proving equally beneficial.

The current study further supports that xylitol had the strongest alkalizing effect, followed by isomalt and sorbitol, both of which caused moderate increases in plaque pH compared to the control group. At 15 minutes postintervention (T4), pH levels in the intervention groups returned close to baseline, while the control group remained at a lower pH—demonstrating the buffering benefit of polyol-containing candies. (Table 2 and Fig. 1) These results align with those of Muralikrishnan et al., Kumar et al., Jain et al., and Vantipalli et al.21,22,24,25

As shown in Table 3 and Figure 2, all polyols boosted salivary flow after use, with sorbitol showing the strongest effect—likely due to enhanced taste response, as past studies suggest. The observed increase in salivary flow after polyol intervention could be due to enhanced gustatory reflexes, as noted by Aguirre-Zero et al.23 Raju et al. found that sorbitol, when used in artificially sweetened chewing gums, was likely responsible for stimulating salivation.26

Interestingly, Aguirre-Zero's study comparing four gum-chewing regimens, i.e., no gum, sucrose gum, sorbitol gum, and xylitol gum, found no statistically significant effect on salivary flow among the groups.23 Similarly, studies by Söderling et al. reported no significant difference in salivary flow between sorbitol and xylitol.27 This suggests that the increase in salivation is more influenced by chewing and taste stimulation than by the specific polyol used, an observation consistent with the present study's findings. These results are in line with what the current study found, which also showed that chewing and taste stimulation are more closely linked to increased salivary flow than the type of polyol used.

Overall, the results highlight the importance of selecting appropriate sugar substitutes in preventive strategies, especially for at-risk populations such as children. Future studies should further explore the long-term clinical effects of polyol-based interventions in diverse populations to optimize caries management strategies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that xylitol is an effective and palatable sugar substitute, exhibiting sweetness comparable to sucrose and producing a mild cooling sensation. Among the polyols assessed, xylitol exerted the most favorable effects on oral health parameters, notably by enhancing salivary flow and facilitating the restoration of salivary pH—both critical mechanisms in the prevention of dental caries.

Recommendations include promoting polyol-based products, especially xylitol, for caries prevention, due to their ability to buffer oral acids and stimulate saliva. There is a need to increase the market availability of xylitol candies and tailor polyol use based on their distinct mechanisms—xylitol's antibacterial properties versus the saliva-stimulating effects of sorbitol and isomalt.

Limitations and Future Scope

The present study was limited by its short duration and small sample size, suggesting further research on long-term effects. Additionally, the high cost and limited availability of xylitol products may restrict access, particularly among children from lower-income groups, calling for strategies to improve affordability and distribution.
