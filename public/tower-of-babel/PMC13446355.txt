Upcycling Citrus aurantium leaf by‑products into bioactive phenolics: Ionic liquid-ultrasound synergy for green extraction, UPLC-QTOF-MS/MS profiling, and hypoglycemic activity
Authors: Yaru Ji, Xinyue Liu, Mengyang Hou, Shuai Hao, Yangyang Ye, Yu Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Large quantities of agri-food by-products arise from citrus processing, creating disposal challenges but also opportunities for valorization. To address this, this study aimed to recover bioactive phenolics from Citrus aurantium leaves (CAL) and to define their composition and bioactivity. First, was screened 16 ionic liquids and identified 1-hexyl-3-methylimidazolium chloride ([C6mim]Cl) as the optimal-polarity solvent for phenolic extraction. Building on this, an ionic liquid–ultrasound synergistic extraction (IL–USE) process was developed and optimized using single-factor experiments combined with response surface methodology. Under the optimal conditions (0.35 mol/L [C6mim]Cl, liquid-to-solid ratio 27 mL/g, ultrasonic power 280 W, 50 min, 55 °C), the total phenolics yield reached 80.57 ± 0.60 mg/g. Compared with ultrasound-assisted extraction and conventional heat-reflux extraction, IL-USE delivered higher yields. This enhancement was macroscopically reflected in the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis, which revealed more pronounced cell-wall disruption. For downstream enrichment, among five macroporous resins evaluated, HPD-826 performed best, and subsequent optimization of breakthrough and elution parameters afforded a CAL total phenolics (CAL-TP) fraction with 77.45 ± 1.63% purity. Furthermore, UPLC-QTOF-MS/MS tentatively identified 47 constituents, establishing a robust chemical profile of CAL-TP. Functionally, in insulin-resistant HepG2 cells, CAL-TP increased glucose consumption and glycogen synthesis and enhanced hexokinase and pyruvate kinase activities, thereby indicating hypoglycemic potential. Collectively, this study provides a green and efficient process that yields phenolic-rich fractions from CAL with defined composition and promising antidiabetic activity.

Introduction

Within the framework of a circular economy, waste valorization plays a crucial role by converting these underutilized agri-food residues into high-value products, thereby driving innovation in sustainable agriculture and green technologies [1]. This transformation is essential for advancing sustainable recycling protocols, optimizing resource efficiency, and ultimately generating both environmental and economic benefits [2]. Agri-food by-products constitute a rich reservoir of valuable compounds, encompassing carbohydrates, phenolics (particularly flavonoids and phenolic acids), essential oils, dietary fibers, and proteins, among others, many of which exhibit good biological properties suitable for valorization [3], [4].

Citrus, a cornerstone fruit crop in global agriculture, is widely cultivated in tropical, subtropical, and temperate zones, holding profound economic and cultural significance [5]. Bitter orange or sour orange, the fruit of Citrus aurantium, one of the most prominent citrus species, enjoys a worldwide reputation for its palatable flavor and health benefits [6]. The fruits and flowers of C. aurantium have attracted considerable research into their chemical constituents and biological activities [7], [8], [9]. C. aurantium leaves (CAL), often overlooked as a postharvest product, are produced in significant quantities, leading to environmental and economic issues regarding their disposal. Despite being largely unexplored, CAL has been identified as a source of valuable compounds, including phenolics [10], essential oil [11], [12], and terpenoids [13]. These compounds shows diverse biofunctional properties, including antifungal, antibacterial, antiviral, antiproliferative, antityrosinase, antioxidant, and antiaging properties [12], [14]. Haraoui et al. found that, among the Citrus species analyzed, CAL possessed the highest total phenolics (107 ± 2 mg/g) [15]. Given the considerable potential of CAL-derived phenolics, further investigation into their extraction, purification, and characterization is warranted to enable the high-value utilization of this underutilized agri-food byproduct.

Traditional extraction methods (e.g., refluxing, decoction) are simple to carry out, but their prolonged high temperature processing accelerates the degradation of thermolabile phenolics and reduces operational efficiency and recovery yields [16]. In contrast, ultrasound assisted extraction (UAE) has emerged as an environmentally friendly, energy efficient intensification technology widely used to recover bioactive constituents [17]. By coupling mechanical disruption with mild thermal acceleration, UAE increases yields while minimizing thermal exposure and solvent consumption [18]. Mechanistically, high frequency acoustic waves (>20 kHz) generate controlled cavitation, and the resulting transient microbubbles produce localized shear forces and microstreaming that enhance cell wall disruption, solute release, and interfacial mass transfer [19]. However, even under intensified conditions, conventional organic solvents often lack selectivity. Their broad solubility profiles can promote coextraction of impurities alongside target analytes, compromising both extraction efficiency and overall effectiveness [20]. To overcome this limitation, ionic liquids (ILs) have gained prominence as green solvents with negligible volatility, excellent thermal stability, and, most importantly, structural tunability [21]. By carefully pairing cations and anions or introducing functional groups, scientists can precisely tune polarity, solvation capacity, and interfacial behavior, thereby tailoring selectivity and efficiency for specific matrices and targets. This molecular level design flexibility has earned ILs the moniker “designer solvents” [22]. Accordingly, combining ILs with ultrasound to create ionic liquid ultrasound synergistic extraction (IL-USE) integrates solvent selectivity with process intensification, delivering a green, high efficiency route for natural product valorization [20], [23], [24]. For characterization of complex botanical extracts, UPLC-QTOF-MS/MS provides exact mass measurements and rich MS/MS spectra for structural elucidation and nontargeted screening. While Q-TOF instruments are generally less sensitive than triple quadrupole systems for trace level targeted quantitation, properly validated methods can still afford accurate quantitative profiling, enabling robust quality control [25].

Type 2 diabetes mellitus primarily results from insulin resistance with progressive β-cell dysfunction and is exacerbated by obesity, sedentary behavior, energy-dense diets, and genetic susceptibility [26]. Persistent hyperglycemia accelerates micro- and macrovascular injury, leading to cardiovascular disease, diabetic kidney disease, and retinopathy, which collectively impose a substantial public health burden [27]. Despite their efficacy in lowering blood glucose, long-term use of thiazolidinediones, sulfonylureas, and metformin is restricted by adverse effects and cumulative costs [28]. Increasing evidence indicates that plant-derived phenolics ameliorate metabolic dysfunction by targeting carbohydrate-digesting enzymes, insulin signaling pathways, oxidative stress, inflammation, β-cell survival, and gut microbiota composition [29]. Therefore, developing safe and cost-effective phenolic-based strategies for type 2 diabetes mellitus management is highly promising, as these compounds represent strong candidates for adjunctive therapy and disease prevention.

Therefore, this research strived to develop an eco-friendly and efficient method for recovering total phenolics from C. aurantium leaves (CAL-TP) using IL-USE. The IL-USE process was initially optimized through response surface methodology (RSM), along with the optimization of macroporous resin column chromatography to achieve high-purity CAL-TP. The phenolic profile was characterized by UPLC-QTOF-MS/MS analysis, thereby enabling a comprehensive quality assessment of the phenolic-enriched extracts. Finally, in vitro hypoglycemic activity of CAL-TP was investigated through an insulin-resistant HepG2 (IR-HepG2) cell model. The results of this study may facilitate the valorization of C. aurantium by-products and support the exploitation of CAL-TP for use in functional foods and antidiabetic therapies.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and equipment

Sixteen ionic liquids (ILs), detailed in Table S1, were obtained from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). 3-(4,5-dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) and standard compounds were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Five resins (HPD-100, AB-8, HPD-826, ADS-17, and ADS-7) were obtained from Nankai Hecheng Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was obtained from Tianhang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China), and Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) was obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Assay kits for pyruvate kinase (PK), glucose, glycogen, total protein, and hexokinase (HK) were obtained from Jiancheng Bioeng. Inst. (Nanjing, China).

Ultrasonic extraction was performed using an SK7210HP ultrasonic bath (53 kHz, 350 W; Kedao Ultrasonic Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Sample absorbance was measured with a BioTek Epoch 2 microplate spectrophotometer (Agilent BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA), and phenolic compounds were profiled on an Agilent 1290 Infinity II LC system coupled to a 6546 QTOF mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). SEM characterization was performed using a Sigma 300 scanning electron microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany).

Plant material

Citrus aurantium L. leaves were gathered from Hangzhou City, Zhejiang Province, in September 2024. Leaves were air dried until weight no longer changed, milled and sifted through a 50-mesh screen, then preserved in a temperature–humidity controlled cabinet for dry botanical materials for subsequent use.

Screening of ILs

Sixteen ILs with rationally designed cation–anion pairs were systematically screened to determine the ideal IL for CAL‑TP extraction. To assess extraction efficiency, 1.0 g CAL powder was dispersed in 20 mL of each IL solution (0.5 mol/L) and subjected to three successive ultrasonication cycles (210 W, 60 min each). Following extraction, samples were centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 5 min) to obtain supernatants for subsequent analyses.

IL-USE process optimization

Single-factor design

To preliminarily establish suitable ranges for five key parameters influencing CAL‑TP yield, each operating variable: [C6mim]Cl concentration (A, 0–1.0 mol/L), liquid‑to‑solid ratio (B, mL/g), ultrasonic power (C, W), extraction time (D, min), and extraction temperature (E, °C), was evaluated using single-factor experiments. Detailed factor levels and settings are provided in Table S2.

Response surface design and analysis

The preliminary single-factor tests established working ranges for four selected variables affecting CAL‑TP yield (Y): A, [C6mim]Cl concentration (0.2–0.6 mol/L); B, liquid‑to‑solid ratio (25–35 mL/g); C, ultrasonic power (245–315 W); and D, extraction time (30–60 min). These ranges were then used in RSM via a Box–Behnken design (BBD) to identify conditions that maximize Y. Conventional extraction methods

Heat‑reflux extraction (HRE)

Each sample (1.0 g) was extracted with 70% ethanol at a liquid-to-solid ratio of 27 mL/g for 2 h per cycle. The extraction was performed in three cycles. The combined extracts were centrifuged to remove particulates, and the clarified supernatant was analyzed to determine the CAL‑TP extraction yield.

Ultrasound‑assisted extraction (UAE)

Each sample (1.0 g) underwent ultrasound-assisted extraction with 70% ethanol at a solvent-to-solid ratio of 27 mL/g, using 280 W power for 50 min per cycle across three cycles. The extracts were pooled and clarified by centrifugation, and the resulting supernatant was analyzed to quantify the CAL‑TP yield.

Quantification of total phenolics

Total phenolics were quantified using our previously described method [30]. A 50 μL aliquot of the extract was blended with 200 μL of tenfold-diluted Folin–Ciocalteu reagent. After 3 min at room temperature, 1.0 mL of 7.5% (w/v) Na2CO3 was added. The mixture was vortexed and incubated for 1 h at ambient temperature in the dark. Absorbance (ATP) at 760 nm was measured, and the concentration (CTP) was calculated from a gallic acid calibration curve (ATP = 0.0026CTP + 0.0435, R2 = 0.9996; 25–500 μg/mL).

SEM characterization

The morphology of C. aurantium leaves, both before and after extraction, was examined using SEM. Samples were rinsed with deionized water, dehydrated through graded ethanol, freeze-dried, and sputter-coated with gold. The specimens were mounted on carbon tape and imaged under high vacuum conditions at 2 kV, with a working distance of 8.4 mm and an aperture of 30 µm. Secondary electron images were captured at 2,000× magnification to resolve microcracks and cavities. Each specimen was selected using systematic random sampling, and representative micrographs with scale bars are provided.

Screening of the optimal resin

Five parallel sets were prepared by dispensing 50 mL portions of the CAL-TP crude extract solution into 150 mL Erlenmeyer flasks, each containing hydrated resin equivalent to 1 g of dry mass. The mixtures were shaken at 120 rpm and 25°C for 10 h to reach adsorption equilibrium, after which the residual total phenolics concentration in the supernatant was estimated. The resins were then recovered by filtration, thoroughly rinsed with water, and immediately desorbed with 50 mL of 95% ethanol, with shaking continued under the same conditions for an additional 10 h.

Purification by column chromatography

An open glass column (22 mm i.d.) was packed with HPD‑826 resin, providing a packed bed volume (BV) of approximately 40 mL (height-to-diameter ratio of about 4.8). Chromatographic separation was performed at room temperature. During adsorption, the crude extract was loaded at 1.5–4.5 BV/h, and breakthrough was defined when the effluent CAL‑TP concentration reached 5% of the feed. After loading, the column was eluted sequentially with 5 BV of water, followed by 5 BV each of 10%, 45%, 75%, and 95% ethanol, all at a constant flow rate of 3 BV/h.

UPLC-QTOF-MS/MS analysis

Chromatographic separations were carried out at ambient temperature on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) with a 1 μL injection. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile (B). The pump ran at 0.3 mL/min with the gradient schedule: 0–2 min, 10%–20% B; 2–10 min, 20%–50% B; 10–12 min, 50%–95% B; 12–15 min, 95% B, 15–16 min, 95% B, 12–15 min, 95%–10% B, 16–18 min, 10% B. The MS operated in positive‑ion mode, scanning m/z 50–800 in auto MS/MS at 2 spectra/s. Source parameters were: N2 drying gas 320°C, 8 L/min; nebulizer 45 psi; sheath gas 350°C, 11 L/min; capillary 3,500 V; nozzle 1,000 V; collision energies 15 and 35 eV.

Hypoglycemic activity

Development of IR-HepG2 cell model

High–glucose DMEM enriched with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin was used for cell maintenance. To induce insulin (INS) resistance, log‑phase HepG2 cells were exposed for 36 h to a range of insulin concentrations. Glucose in the medium was quantified to determine the optimal INS dose for generating the IR‑HepG2 model.

Cell viability

Cell viability after exposure to purified CAL‑TP was assessed by MTT. A total of 3 × 104 HepG2 cells per well were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Subsequently, 100 μL of CAL-TP at concentrations ranging from 25 to 400 μg/mL was added for another 24 h. Afterward, 20 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was applied, followed by a 4 h incubation at 37°C. The supernatant was removed, 150 μL of DMSO was used to dissolve the formazan product, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm.

Quantification of glucose consumption and glycogen

In 6-well plates, HepG2 cells (3 × 104/well) were added and maintained for 24 h. Insulin resistance was induced as described in Section 2.11.1 for all groups except the normal control. Cultures were concurrently assigned to six groups: Normal control (NC; untreated HepG2), insulin-resistant model (IR; IR-HepG2 without drug), positive control (IR+MET; IR-HepG2 treated with metformin hydrochloride at 1.0 mM), and CAL-TP treatment groups at low, medium, and high doses (IR+25, IR+50, IR+100; IR-HepG2 treated with purified CAL-TP at 25, 50, or 100 μg/mL). After 24 h of exposure, cells were harvested, then the relevant indicators were quantified.

HK and PK activity

In 6‑well plates, HepG2 cells at 3 × 104/well were incubated for 24 h to allow adhesion, then processed as outlined in Section 2.11.3. Post 24 h of drug exposure, the cells were rinsed twice in PBS, detached with trypsin, and collected. PK and HK activities were measured in cell lysates with kit procedures provided by the manufacturer.

Statistical analysis

All measurements were performed with at least three independent replicates and are reported as mean ± SD. RSM was performed and interpreted using Design‑Expert (Stat‑Ease, Minneapolis, MN, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post hoc analyses (Dunnett's multiple comparisons test) was employed to assess statistical significance.

Results and discussion

Identifying the best ionic liquid

The yield of CAL-TP was highly sensitive to both the cation and anion identities in the IL (Fig. 1A). With the cation held constant, the extraction performance followed the sequence Cl− ≥ Br− > PF6− > BF4−. This trend could be attributed to the presence of halides (such as Cl− and Br−), which act as hydrogen-bond acceptors that enhance solvent-solute interactions with phenolics. In contrast, PF6− is intrinsically more hydrophobic, while BF4− is highly hydrophilic and strongly hydrated, thereby hindering the partitioning of phenolics into the extraction phase [31], [32]. Moreover, compared with halide-based systems, ILs bearing PF6− or BF4− anions were more viscous and had lower diffusion coefficients, which slowed mass transfer and reduced the CAL-TP yield. With the anion fixed, lengthening the imidazolium alkyl chain from [C2mim]+ to [C4mim]+ and [C6mim]+ increased extraction efficiency, whereas further extension to [C8mim]+ reduced it. The observed trend suggested that the extraction performance of imidazolium-based ILs might be closely related to the balance between polarity and hydrophobicity. Specifically, longer side chains appeared to reduce the overall polarity by increasing the hydrophobic character, which potentially attenuated the polar contribution of the imidazolium ring [33]. Consequently, [C6mim]Cl exhibited the most suitable polarity profile for solubilizing phenolics, aligning with the principle of similar compatibility.Fig. 1Effects of extraction parameters on CAL-TP yield.

Analysis of single‑factor effects

The CAL-TP yield initially rose with increasing concentrations of [C6mim]Cl but fell when concentrations exceeded 0.4 mol/L (Fig. 1B). This trend aligned with the results of luteolin extraction from peanut shells [34]. The early increase in yield was likely due to greater disintegration of the cellulose network and improved solubilization of phenolics at higher concentrations. However, at concentrations above 0.4 mol/L, the solution viscosity increased significantly, which hindered mass transfer and reduced the effectiveness of ultrasonic cavitation, ultimately decreasing extraction efficiency [35]. Consequently, a concentration of 0.4 mol/L was identified as optimal for extracting CAL-TP.

The CAL-TP yield surged markedly as the liquid-to-solid ratio rose from 15 to 30 mL/g. Beyond this point, the yield decreased with further increases in the liquid-to-solid ratio (Fig. 1C). Similar results have been reported for the IL-USE of lycopene from guava [23]. A low liquid-to-solid ratio led to incomplete extraction due to inadequate solvent for dissolving the analytes. Conversely, an excessively high ratio complicated the extraction process by introducing non-target products and hindering IL recovery [20].

The CAL-TP yield displayed a parabolic relationship with ultrasonic power, peaking at 280 W (Fig. 1D). This trend aligned with previous IL-USE studies on phenolics from Chaenomeles speciosa leaves [20]. A moderate increase in ultrasonic power appeared to enhance extraction efficiency and yield, which could be attributed to the intensification of cavitation effects and improved solvent penetration. However, excessive power might risk degrading the target molecules, potentially compromising both extraction efficiency and product quality [34].

The CAL-TP yield rose with extraction time, reached a maximum at 45 min, and then declined at longer times (Fig. 1E). This trend agreed with earlier IL-USE studies on Platycodon grandiflorum saponins [33]. The initial rise reflected improved solvent penetration and enhanced release of phenolics. Further prolongation increased coextraction of unwanted substances and facilitated the degradation of desired compounds, which reduced the yield [20].

Increasing the extraction temperature from 25 to 55 °C notably increased the CAL-TP yield, beyond which no further substantial change was observed (Fig. 1F). A comparable trend had been noted during the extraction of luteolin from peanut hulls [34]. The initial rise in yield was due to enhanced diffusivity and solubility at higher temperatures, which facilitated the release of phenolic compounds. However, excessively high temperatures were found to promote thermal degradation of sensitive constituents [35].

ANOVA for quadratic model.

The combined effect of two predictors on a response can be examined with response surface visualizations, including three-dimensional surface plots and two-dimensional contour maps derived from a fitted RSM model while the remaining factors are fixed at their coded center level. These plots reveal interaction strength, curvature, and regions where the response improves, helping to locate peaks, ridges, or saddle points. RSM in conjunction with a Box–Behnken design yields a second-order model and pairs graphical exploration with numerical optimization, using procedures such as desirability functions to determine factor settings that maximize the response [36]. Consistent with the ANOVA, the plots in Fig. 2 indicated that all four factors (A, B, C, and D) had significant main effects on CAL‑TP yield. Factor B exerts the largest influence, followed by factor A. For each factor, the yield increased to a peak and then declined as the level continues to rise, matching the trends observed in single‑factor experiments. Among the two‑factor interactions, the interaction between B and C had the strongest impact on CAL‑TP yield, with the interaction between A and C ranking second, while the remaining pairs were not statistically significant. RSM optimization identified optimal settings of 0.353 mol/L [C6mim]Cl, a liquid-to-solid ratio of 27.475 mL/g, ultrasonic power of 276.568 W, and an extraction time of 49.818 min, which predicted a CAL-TP yield of 81.318 mg/g.Fig. 2Visualization of CAL-TP yield via response surfaces and contour plots for pairwise interactions among independent variables.

Model verification

To verify the model under production-like conditions, validation runs were conducted using slightly adjusted parameters: 0.35 mol/L [C6mim]Cl, a liquid-to-solid ratio of 27 mL/g, an ultrasonic power of 280 W, an extraction time of 50 min, and an extraction temperature of 55 °C. Across three independent replicates, the CAL-TP yield was 80.57 ± 0.60 mg/g, which was highly consistent with the predicted value, thereby demonstrating the reliability and practical applicability of the model.

Comparison of extraction methods

With 70% ethanol and the same 27 mL/g solvent loading and three cycles as IL‑USE, yields were 42.48 ± 0.96 mg/g CAL‑TP for HRE (2 h) and 67.24 ± 0.70 mg/g for UAE (50 min), both statistically lower than IL‑USE. SEM revealed that the untreated, milled C. aurantium leaves displayed compact, smooth lamellar surfaces with intact cell walls and only minor wrinkling (Fig. 3A). After HRE, the walls began to delaminate, showing curled edges and limited debris (Fig. 3B). UAE produced markedly rougher surfaces with blocky fracture, abundant microcracks, and enlarged pores/cavities (Fig. 3C). IL‑USE caused the most severe disruption, with extensive interlamellar dissolution and tearing, collapse of wall structure, and interconnected cavities accompanied by loose debris (Fig. 3D). Differences in CAL‑TP yield could be attributed to a combination of solvent–matrix affinity, process intensification, and the physicochemical properties of the phenolic compounds. Specifically, ultrasound-induced cavitation is understood to generate microjets and localized hotspots, which likely ruptured plant tissues and accelerated diffusion. Furthermore, [C6mim]Cl was anticipated to interact strongly with cellulose, thereby potentially disrupting hydrogen-bond networks and increasing tissue swelling. This synergistic effect plausibly promoted preferential solvation and faster partitioning of phenolics into the liquid phase. These mechanisms explained the superiority of [C6mim]Cl over aqueous ethanol and of UAE over conventional HRE for recovering phenolics from C. aurantium leaves. Selection of the most suitable resin

Based on a comprehensive analysis of adsorption and desorption performance, HPD‑826 resin demonstrated the highest efficacy in purifying CAL-TP (Table 3). Its moderately polar nature aligned well with the intermediate polarity of target compounds, enabling effective adsorption through hydrophobic interactions, π–π stacking, and hydrogen bonding. It offered a high specific surface area (500–600 m2/g), providing abundant active sites, and a large average pore diameter (90–100 nm), facilitating molecular diffusion into the pores and enhancing adsorption kinetics and capacity. In comparison, the nonpolar HPD‑100 resin, despite its higher surface area (650–700 m2/g), exhibited lower selectivity and more difficult desorption for medium-polarity phenolics. The weakly polar AB‑8 resin possessed a considerably smaller pore size (13–14 nm), likely limiting access for larger phenolic molecules. Meanwhile, the moderately polar ADS‑17 resin and the highly polar ADS‑7 resin were both found to have relatively low specific surface areas (90–120 and 100–120 m2/g, respectively) and narrow pore diameters (25–30 nm), which restricted their overall adsorption and desorption capacities. Therefore, HPD‑826 resin represented an optimal balance of polarity, surface area, and pore structure, making it effective for adsorption and desorption of CAL-TP. Column chromatography purification process

Adsorption study

Flow rate had a marked effect on CAL‑TP adsorption by the HPD‑826 column (Fig. 4A). As the feed rate increased, the adsorption capacity steadily decreased. A similar phenomenon occurred in the purification of phenolics from Plantago depressa using the NKA-II resin column [30]. The breakthrough volume fell from about 19 BV at 1.5 BV/h to roughly 8 BV at 4.5 BV/h, indicating that longer residence times at lower feed rates favored mass transfer and binding. In contrast, higher feed rates shortened contact duration, limiting uptake and causing earlier breakthroughs. To balance throughput and performance, a feed rate of 3.0 BV/h was ultimately chosen, resulting in a breakthrough volume of approximately 17 BV.Fig. 4Leakage curve of CAL-TP on an HPD-826 resin column at flow rates of 1.5, 3.0, and 4.5 BV/h (A), and elution curve of CAL-TP on the same HPD-826 resin column at 3.0 BV/h (B).

Leakage curve of CAL-TP on an HPD-826 resin column at flow rates of 1.5, 3.0, and 4.5 BV/h (A), and elution curve of CAL-TP on the same HPD-826 resin column at 3.0 BV/h (B).

Gradient elution study

The elution process was strictly monitored in real time by measuring the total phenolic content in each fraction via the Folin-Ciocalteu method to ensure that only the highly enriched “peak” fractions were strategically collected. At a flow rate of 3 BV/h (Fig. 4B), the purification process comprised: (1) an initial washing stage with H2O (5 BV) and 10% ethanol (5 BV) to discard highly polar impurities (such as sugars, proteins, and salts) with negligible phenolic loss; (2) a selective elution stage using 45% ethanol (where 4 BV achieved near-quantitative collection and 5 BV ensured full recovery) and 75% ethanol (3 BV) to exhaustively desorb phenolics; and (3) a final resin regeneration stage using 95% ethanol to elute highly retained, low-polarity impurities (such as pigments, triterpenoids, and lipids), ensuring their complete exclusion from the final product.

Purification process validation

Using optimized HPD-826 column chromatography, CAL-TP was purified by loading 17 BV at 3.0 BV/h, followed by a desorption sequence: 5 BV of H2O and 5 BV of 10% ethanol to remove impurities, then phenolic elution with 45% ethanol (4 BV) and 75% ethanol (3 BV). The fractions collected from the shaded region in Fig. 4B were concentrated and freeze-dried. The resulting CAL-TP achieved a purity of 77.45 ± 1.63%, which was defined as the weight ratio (w/w) of total phenolics to the final dried extract, and a recovery of 84.09 ± 1.22%, thereby confirming the feasibility of this preparative method. It should be noted that this “total phenolics” purity was estimated using the Folin–Ciocalteu assay, which was not fully specific to phenolics and could be influenced by other reducing substances. Accordingly, the reported 77.45 ± 1.63% was interpreted as an estimate of overall reducing capacity (expressed as gallic acid equivalents) rather than as absolute phenolic purity.

The purified CAL‑TP extract was profiled by UPLC–QTOF–MS over an 18‑min chromatographic run (Fig. 5). To enhance the robustness of peak annotation, a comprehensive reference library of Citrus constituents was curated from multiple scientific databases and primary sources. Entries were standardized and compiled in a Microsoft Excel file containing compound names and synonyms, molecular formulas, exact (monoisotopic) masses, anticipated adducts for both positive and negative ESI, and literature references, with MS/MS fragments recorded where available. Feature‑to‑library matching was performed using high‑resolution accurate mass and retention characteristics. In addition, chemical profiling revealed that the purified CAL-TP was enriched with polymethoxyflavones (e.g., nobiletin and tangeretin) alongside flavonoid/flavanone aglycones (e.g., quercetin, naringenin, and hesperetin). A previous study indicated that these prominent constituents show great promise for the prevention and management of diabetes and its related complications [40]. Hence, it is reasonable to hypothesize that CAL-TP exerts potential hypoglycemic effects. To validate this hypothesis, a preliminary evaluation of its hypoglycemic activity was conducted.

Hypoglycemic activity analysis

Selecting the optimal INS concentration

After 36 h of exposure to different INS concentrations, HepG2 cells exhibited reduced glucose consumption compared to the NC group (Fig. 6A). Significant decreases were noted starting at 10-8 μg/mL (p < 0.001). The most pronounced reduction occurred at 10-7 μg/mL, where consumption dropped to 27.68% of the normal level. Increases to 10-6 and 10-5 μg/mL did not result in further reductions, indicating a plateau. Cell viability analysis

Following 24 h of exposure of HepG2 cells to varying concentrations of purified CAL‑TP, cell viability was measured (Fig. 6B). The results showed over 90% viability for concentrations of 25–100 μg/mL, with no statistical difference compared to the NC group, indicating that CAL‑TP did not exhibit apparent toxicity within this range. However, at 200 μg/mL (p < 0.001) and 400 μg/mL (p < 0.0001), cell viability decreased significantly. Therefore, 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL of CAL‑TP were used in subsequent studies.

Quantitative analysis of glucose and glycogen consumption

In Fig. 6C, glucose consumption in the IR group was markedly lower than in the NC group (p < 0.0001), confirming successful model induction. CAL‑TP at 50 μg/mL (p < 0.01) and 100 μg/mL (p < 0.0001) significantly increased glucose consumption versus IR group. These results indicated that CAL‑TP enhanced glucose metabolism and mitigated insulin resistance. Fig. 6D illustrated that IR-HepG2 cells contained less glycogen than the NC group (p < 0.0001). Metformin raised glycogen to a mid‑range level. CAL‑TP increased glycogen in a clear concentration‑dependent manner. At 100 μg/mL, glycogen levels were close to that of the metformin group but still below the NC group. These findings pointed to a partial rescue of glycogen formation and retention by CAL‑TP in IR‑HepG2 cells, thereby contributing to the maintenance of cellular glucose balance.

Effect on HK and PK activities

Relative to the NC group, the IR group exhibited significant reductions in both enzymes (p < 0.0001), consistent with impaired glycolysis (Fig. 6E and Fig. 6F). Metformin increased HK (p < 0.0001) and PK (p < 0.001) activities compared to the IR group. CAL‑TP elevated HK activity at 50 μg/mL (p < 0.01) and 100 μg/mL (p < 0.0001). For PK activity, CAL‑TP resulted in a pronounced concentration-dependent increase from 25 to 100 μg/mL. Overall, these data indicated that CAL-TP contributed to the partial recovery of glycolytic enzymes within IR-HepG2 cells, which in turn supported glucose utilization and metabolic balance.

Conclusions

With the expansion of C. aurantium cultivation, the resulting abundance of leaves as an agricultural by‑product presents both a management challenge and an opportunity for valorization. This study demonstrated a green, efficient workflow integrating IL‑USE and resin purification to produce a phenolic‑rich extract. Under optimized conditions, the CAL‑TP yield reached 80.57 ± 0.60 mg/g, and the purified fraction exhibited a phenolic content of 77.45 ± 1.63%. UPLC‑QTOF‑MS/MS tentatively characterized 47 chemical constituents, defining a comprehensive chemical profile of the purified CAL‑TP fraction. Studies on antihyperglycemic activity revealed that CAL-TP improved glucose consumption, stimulated glycogen production, and boosted the activities of HK and PK in IR-HepG2 cells. Consequently, this work laid the groundwork for the composition–activity relationship and standardized manufacturing of CAL‑TP, supported its potential as a phenolic‑rich antidiabetic candidate, and warranted additional animal studies and toxicological assessment. In parallel, subsequent studies should evaluate process‑sustainability aspects, including residue control, resin regeneration, scale‑up robustness, and techno‑economic analyses, under a rigorous quality‑control regime.
