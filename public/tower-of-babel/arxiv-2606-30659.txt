Horseshoe Priors for Spatial Small Area Estimation: Regular Variation, Tail Robustness, and Deep Learning
Dhiman Bhadra, Nicholas G. Polson

Abstract
Small area estimation borrows strength across domains to repair the poor precision of direct survey estimators. Two philosophies dominate the area-level literature. The first, descending from Ghosh and Rao 1994 and realized at scale in the spatial program of Mercer et al. 2015 and Wakefield et al. 2019 , borrows strength through structured Gaussian smoothing: an intrinsic conditional autoregression or its BYM2 reparameterization pools each area towards its geographic neighbours. The second borrows strength globally but acts locally through a heavy-tailed global–local prior on the area effects, of which the horseshoe of Carvalho et al. 2010 is the canonical instance; Tang et al. 2018 first brought this idea to small area estimation. We study the horseshoe Fay–Herriot model in the realistic regime of known and unequal sampling variances and make four contributions. First, a tail-robustness theorem: through a heteroscedastic Tweedie identity the posterior mean leaves strongly signalled areas essentially unshrunk, bounding the influence of an outlying direct estimate, in sharp contrast to the linear, unbounded shrinkage of Gaussian random-effect models. Second, standardizing by the known design variances transfers the minimax contraction and credible-set theory of the homoscedastic sequence model to the heteroscedastic Fay–Herriot problem; the posterior contracts at the nearly-black minimax rate, with a matching lower bound that fixes the sharp constant. Third, we give a precise account of when structured smoothing and global–local shrinkage each win. Fourth, an O ⁡ ( m ) O(m) Gibbs sampler, a controlled study, and an analysis of the Scottish lip cancer data confirm the account: on that strongly spatial data the smoother predicts held-out districts best, yet the horseshoe shrinkage coefficient flags the exceptional high- and low-incidence districts that smoothing suppresses. We also connect the horseshoe to a data-augmentation approach to Bayesian quantile regression and to deep quantile networks for sharp spatial structure. Throughout we argue, following the regular-variation theory of Bhadra et al. 2016 , that these properties make the horseshoe a sound default prior for area effects: it borrows strength aggressively yet lets genuinely exceptional areas speak for themselves, with no tuning and no neighbourhood graph.



Keywords:
Bayesian shrinkage; default prior; Fay–Herriot model; global–local prior; horseshoe; minimaxity; posterior contraction; quantile regression; regular variation; spatial smoothing; Tweedie’s formula.

1
Introduction

Demand for reliable statistics at fine geographic and demographic resolution has
far outstripped the sample sizes that surveys allocate to individual domains.
The
direct
estimator for an area, computed from that area’s own sample,
is approximately design-unbiased but so variable as to be useless when the
within-area sample is a handful of units or empty. Small area estimation (SAE)
repairs this by
borrowing strength
: a model links the areas, and each
area’s estimate is pulled towards a prediction formed from auxiliary
information and from the other areas. The authoritative appraisals of

Ghosh and Rao 1994
,
Rao 2003
and
Ghosh 2020
trace how synthetic
estimators, empirical best linear unbiased prediction (EBLUP), and empirical
and hierarchical Bayes methods came to organize the field, almost always
through the area-level model of
Fay and Herriot 1979
or the unit-level model
of
Battese et al. 1988
.

How strength is borrowed, and not merely that it is borrowed, is the substance
of a method. The area-level literature has settled into two broad philosophies,
which this paper places side by side and then separates theoretically.

Structured smoothing.

The first philosophy treats the area effects as a smooth latent surface and
pools each area towards its neighbours. Its modern realization is the spatial
program developed in Seattle by Jon Wakefield and collaborators for global
health:
Mercer et al. 2015
marry design-based direct estimators with a
Bayesian space–time smoother for child mortality,
Wakefield et al. 2019

develop the methodology for under-five mortality across the developing world,
and the
SUMMER
package
(
Li et al. 2025
)
has made the approach a standard
tool for the Demographic and Health Surveys. The latent model is the
Besag–York–Mollié (BYM) prior
(
Besag et al. 1991
)
, an intrinsic conditional
autoregression (ICAR) plus an unstructured Gaussian term, most often used in
the
Riebler et al. 2016
BYM2 reparameterization that separates the total
random-effect variance from the fraction that is spatial, and fitted by INLA

(
Rue et al. 2009
)
. Structured smoothing is powerful precisely when the truth is
spatially coherent: information flows along the adjacency graph, and a sparsely
sampled area inherits the level of its well-sampled neighbours.

Global–local shrinkage.

The second philosophy makes no spatial assumption. It places a heavy-tailed

global–local
prior on the area effects: a single global scale pulls all
effects towards the regression surface, while heavy-tailed local scales let
individual areas escape that pull. The horseshoe prior
(
Carvalho et al. 2010
;
Polson and Scott 2011
)

is the canonical instance, and
Tang et al. 2018
first cast it as a model for the
Fay–Herriot random effects, with multivariate and spatial extensions following

(
Tang and Ghosh 2023
)
. Such priors borrow strength globally—most areas are
shrunk hard towards the synthetic prediction—yet act locally, leaving the few
genuinely deviant areas almost untouched. This is the natural model when
departures from the regression surface are
sparse
: a minority of areas
behave anomalously and the rest are well explained by covariates. It also
furnishes a continuous answer to the question raised by
Datta et al. 2011
and

Datta and Mandal 2015
of whether every area needs a random effect at all.

Contributions.

Existing global–local work for small area estimation

(
Tang et al. 2018
;
Tang and Ghosh 2023
)
establishes methodology and posterior
consistency, but the practically decisive feature of the Fay–Herriot
setting—that the sampling variances
D_{i}
are
known and unequal
, being
design variances of the direct estimators—has not been turned to theoretical
advantage, nor has the horseshoe been squarely compared with the
structured-smoothing program it competes with in practice. Treating the known

D_{i}
as the lever rather than a nuisance, we make four contributions.

1.

Bounded influence (Section
4
).
A heteroscedastic
Tweedie–Brown identity gives the posterior mean a
redescending
score,

\E(\theta_{i}\mid y_{i})=y_{i}-2D_{i}/(y_{i}-\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta})+o(|y_{i}|^{-1})
, so a
strongly signalled area is returned essentially unshrunk and its
gross-error sensitivity is finite (Theorem
1
,
Corollary
1
). Gaussian random-effect models—EBLUP and BYM2
alike—apply a fixed linear shrinkage whose sensitivity to a corrupted direct
estimate is unbounded. The property rests only on a regularly varying prior tail,
so it holds across the global–local family, not just the horseshoe
(Section
5.3
).

2.

Sharp heteroscedastic minimaxity (Section
5
).

Standardizing by the known
D_{i}
reduces the area-level model to a unit-variance
sequence model and transfers the contraction theory of
van der Pas et al. 2014
;
van der Pas et al. 2017

to the Fay–Herriot problem. Over nearly-black classes the posterior contracts at
the minimax rate (Theorem
2
); a matching lower bound fixes the
exact constant in the
D
-weighted loss (Proposition
2
); the
contraction is achieved adaptively, with no oracle knowledge of the number of
exceptional areas, by either an empirical-Bayes or a half-Cauchy global scale
(Corollary
2
); and the credible balls are honest under the usual
excessive-bias conditions. The known design variances are exactly what make a
heavy-tailed prior provably minimax here.

3.

Separation of the two philosophies (Sections
6
–
7
).
We prove that structured smoothing is rate-superior under a
smooth spatial truth while global–local shrinkage is near-minimax under a sparse
truth, where the linear smoother carries an irreducible oversmoothing bias
growing with the spike magnitude (Proposition
3
). A Stein
unbiased-risk analysis explains the mechanism: the horseshoe spends a

data-adaptive
budget of effective degrees of freedom, allocating them only
to the areas it declines to shrink, whereas EBLUP and BYM2 spend a fixed budget
set by their variance components (Proposition
4
).

4.

Computation and evidence (Sections
8
–
10
).

An
O(m)
-per-sweep Gibbs sampler, a three-regime Monte Carlo study, and a
magnitude sweep that exhibits the predicted crossover—the smoothing-to-shrinkage
risk ratio rising without saturating as areas become more exceptional—together
with an analysis of the Scottish lip cancer data in which structured smoothing
predicts held-out districts best, as the theory predicts for a smoothly spatial
truth, while the posterior shrinkage coefficient simultaneously flags the
exceptional districts as a calibrated screening score.

Relation to existing work.

Our starting point is the global–local program of
Tang et al. 2018
, who
introduced the horseshoe as a prior for the Fay–Herriot effects and established
posterior consistency and good empirical behaviour, and its spatial elaboration

(
Tang and Ghosh 2023
)
. That work treats the sampling variances as nuisance
constants to be conditioned on; we argue that they are instead the lever that
makes a sharp frequentist analysis possible. The standardization in
Section
5
is, to our knowledge, the first transfer of the
sequence-model contraction theory of
van der Pas et al. 2014
;
van der Pas et al. 2017
to the
heteroscedastic Fay–Herriot model, and it yields minimax-rate and honest
credible-set statements rather than consistency alone. The Tweedie/Brown
calculation in Section
4
is classical
(
Efron 2011
)
; what is
new is its heteroscedastic form (
8
) and the resulting
bounded-influence characterization that cleanly separates the horseshoe from
every Gaussian random-effects competitor. On the applied side, the program of

Mercer et al. 2015
;
Wakefield et al. 2019
and the
SUMMER
ecosystem

(
Li et al. 2025
)
have made structured spatial smoothing—the
Besag–York–Mollié model and its
Riebler et al. 2016
reparameterization, fit
by integrated nested Laplace approximation—the default for subnational health
estimation. Rather than proposing the horseshoe as a replacement, we delineate
precisely the regime in which each is preferable (Section
6
),
which we view as the more useful contribution to practice.

2
The Fay–Herriot model and two modes of borrowing strength

2.1
Area-level model

Let
i=1,\dots,m
index the areas and let
\theta_{i}
denote the target
(a mean, rate, or transformed proportion). The direct estimator
y_{i}
satisfies
the
sampling model

y_{i}=\theta_{i}+e_{i},\qquad e_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,D_{i}),

(1)

where the
D_{i}
are treated as
known
; in practice they are smoothed
design variances of the direct estimator, often after a variance-stabilizing
transform. The
linking model
expresses the targets through
q
auxiliary
variables
\bm{x}_{i}
,

\theta_{i}=\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}+v_{i},

(2)

so that
v_{i}=\theta_{i}-\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}
is the area effect, the part of

\theta_{i}
unexplained by covariates. Combining (
1
) and
(
2
) gives the marginal model
y_{i}=\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}+v_{i}+e_{i}
.
The science of SAE is encoded entirely in the prior assigned to

\bm{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{m})
.

2.2
EBLUP and the Gaussian baseline

The classical Fay–Herriot specification takes
v_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,A)
independently.
With
A
known, the best predictor of
\theta_{i}
is the composite

\tilde{\theta}_{i}=\gamma_{i}\,y_{i}+(1-\gamma_{i})\,\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta},\qquad\gamma_{i}=\frac{A}{A+D_{i}},

(3)

a convex combination of the noisy direct estimator and the synthetic regression
prediction, with weight
\gamma_{i}
increasing in the reliability of
y_{i}
.
Replacing
(\bm{\beta},A)
by estimators yields the EBLUP;
Prasad and Rao 1990

give the second-order mean squared error (MSE) approximation that underlies
inference, and
Datta and Ghosh 1991
the hierarchical Bayes counterpart. Writing

g_{1i}(A)=AD_{i}/(A+D_{i})
for the leading variance term, their approximation is

\mathrm{MSE}(\tilde{\theta}_{i})\approx g_{1i}(A)+g_{2i}(A)+g_{3i}(A),

(4)

where
g_{2i}
accounts for estimating
\bm{\beta}
and
g_{3i}=D_{i}^{2}(A+D_{i})^{-3}\overline{V}(A)
for estimating
A
, with
\overline{V}(A)
the asymptotic
variance of the variance estimator. The defining feature of (
3
) is
that the shrinkage fraction
1-\gamma_{i}=D_{i}/(A+D_{i})
is the
same
for
every area at a given
D_{i}
: the model cannot tell a genuinely deviant area from
a noisy one and shrinks both by the same amount, and (
4
)
inherits this by depending on the data only through the single estimate of
A
.

2.3
Structured spatial smoothing

Wakefield’s program replaces the independent Gaussian effects by a spatially
structured Gaussian field. Writing
\bm{W}=(w_{ij})
for the area adjacency
matrix and
\bm{Q}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{W}\mathbf{1})-\bm{W}
for the graph Laplacian,
the BYM model sets

\theta_{i}=\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}+u_{i}+\psi_{i},\qquad\bm{u}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\big(\bm{0},\ \sigma_{u}^{2}\,\bm{Q}^{-}\big),\quad\psi_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\psi}^{2}),

(5)

where
\bm{Q}^{-}
is a generalized inverse:
\bm{u}
is the intrinsic CAR

(
Besag et al. 1991
)
that borrows from neighbours, and
\psi_{i}
is unstructured
heterogeneity. The BYM2 form
(
Riebler et al. 2016
)
reparameterizes

(\sigma_{u}^{2},\sigma_{\psi}^{2})
as a total variance and a mixing fraction on a
scaled
\bm{Q}
, which stabilizes prior elicitation and is the version
implemented for survey data in
SUMMER

(
Li et al. 2025
)
. Conditionally on
the variances, (
5
) is again a linear smoother of
\bm{y}
: the fitted
effect at area
i
is a weighted average of all direct estimates with weights
decaying along graph distance. Strength flows locally and the shrinkage is, as
in EBLUP, a fixed linear operator once the variances are set.

2.4
Global–local shrinkage

The horseshoe Fay–Herriot model retains independence across areas but replaces
the single variance
A
by an area-specific, heavy-tailed scale, the

shrink globally, act locally
principle of
Polson and Scott 2011
:

v_{i}\mid\lambda_{i},\tau\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\ \tau^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}),\qquad\lambda_{i}\sim\mathrm{C}^{+}(0,1),\qquad\tau\sim\mathrm{C}^{+}(0,1),

(6)

where
\mathrm{C}^{+}(0,1)
is the standard half-Cauchy. The global scale
\tau
controls
the overall pull towards the regression surface; the local scales
\lambda_{i}
,
having Cauchy tails, permit individual areas to be exempt from that pull. It is
illuminating to write the implied shrinkage in the standardized residual

r_{i}=y_{i}-\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}=v_{i}+e_{i}
. Conditionally on the scales,

\E(\theta_{i}\mid r_{i},\lambda_{i},\tau)=\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}+(1-\kappa_{i})\,r_{i},\qquad\kappa_{i}=\frac{D_{i}}{D_{i}+\tau^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}},

(7)

where
\kappa_{i}\in(0,1)
is the
shrinkage coefficient
:
\kappa_{i}\to 1

returns the synthetic estimate and
\kappa_{i}\to 0
returns the direct estimate.
Under the horseshoe the prior on
\kappa_{i}
(at
\tau=1
,
D_{i}=1
) is

\mathrm{Beta}(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})
, the symmetric “horseshoe” density with poles at

0
and
1
:
a priori
an area is expected to be either fully pooled or
fully free, and the data decide which. This is the continuous analogue of the
discrete question—does area
i
need a random effect?—studied by

Datta et al. 2011
;
Datta and Mandal 2015
.

3
Regular variation and the horseshoe as a default

Before deriving estimation properties we record the structural property of the
horseshoe that underlies all of them and that makes it, in a precise sense, a

default
prior for area effects. The relevant notion is regular variation

(
Bingham et al. 1987
)
.

Definition 1

(Regular variation)
.

A measurable
f:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is
regularly varying at infinity
with index
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
, written
f\in\mathrm{RV}_{\rho}
, if

\lim_{t\to\infty}f(tx)/f(t)=x^{\rho}
for every
x>0
. Equivalently

f(t)=t^{\rho}\ell(t)
with
\ell
slowly varying (
\ell\in\mathrm{RV}_{0}
). A
density is
regularly varying
if it is
\mathrm{RV}_{-(1+\alpha)}
for some
tail index
\alpha>0
, so that it has Pareto-like polynomial tails.

The decisive fact, established by
Bhadra et al. 2016
, is that the horseshoe is
regularly varying, whereas the Gaussian and Laplace priors behind ridge and the
Bayesian lasso are not.

Proposition 1

(Regular variation of the horseshoe;
Bhadra et al. 2016
)
.

Let
v\mid\lambda\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^{2}\lambda^{2})
with
\lambda\sim\mathrm{C}^{+}(0,1)
. Then the
marginal prior density
p
of
v
is symmetric, has a logarithmic pole at the
origin,
p(v)\asymp\log(1/|v|)
as
v\to 0
, and is regularly varying with index

-2
at infinity,
p\in\mathrm{RV}_{-2}
; that is,
p(v)=|v|^{-2}\ell(|v|)
for a
slowly varying
\ell
. Consequently, for any fixed Gaussian noise variance
D>0

the marginal
m(r)=\int\phi_{\sqrt{D}}(r-v)p(v)\,\mathrm{d}v
of the observation
is also in
\mathrm{RV}_{-2}
, regular variation being preserved by convolution
with a light-tailed kernel.

Regular variation is the engine of
tail robustness
, the property that a
prior eventually defers to a sufficiently surprising observation. The general
principle, traceable to
O’Hagan 1979
for outlier-resistant inference and
sharpened for global–local priors by
Bhadra et al. 2016
, is that a regularly
varying prior cannot impose unbounded shrinkage: by Definition
1
the
log-density has a slowly varying derivative, so the Tweedie correction in
(
8
) tends to a constant order and the posterior mean tracks the
data in the tail. Theorem
1
below is the quantitative form of this
statement for the heteroscedastic Fay–Herriot model; here we stress the

conceptual
consequence.

3.1
Why regular variation makes a good default

Bhadra et al. 2016
argue that regular variation is the property a prior must have
to “let the data speak for themselves,” resolving a classical objection of

Efron 1973
that flat, ostensibly non-informative priors can be sharply
informative for nonlinear functionals in high dimensions. A regularly varying
global–local prior is simultaneously
informative about sparsity
—it
pools the many ordinary areas hard, through the pole at the origin—and

uninformative about signal
—it scarcely shrinks the few exceptional
areas, through the heavy tail. For small area estimation, where the analyst
rarely knows in advance which domains are exceptional, this combination is
exactly what one wants from a default. We collect the practical reasons, each of
which is made precise elsewhere in the paper.

1.

No area must be declared special in advance.
The heavy tail
(Proposition
1
) leaves a genuinely deviant area essentially
unshrunk without the analyst flagging it, and the bounded influence of
Corollary
1
caps the damage a single corrupted direct estimate
can do. A Gaussian random effect, by contrast, shrinks every area by the same
data-independent fraction and is unboundedly sensitive to an outlier.

2.

Aggressive pooling where it is safe.
The pole at the origin
(Proposition
1
) pools the ordinary majority of areas as hard as
EBLUP does, so robustness to the exceptional areas costs almost nothing on the
quiet ones; the shrinkage profile
\kappa_{i}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})
sorts
the two groups automatically.

3.

One scale, learned from the data, and no graph.
The single global
scale
\tau
adapts to the unknown number of exceptional areas
(Corollary
2
); there is no smoothing parameter to cross-validate
and no neighbourhood structure to specify, so the prior is usable when geography
is unavailable, unreliable, or beside the point.

4.

Optimality without oracle knowledge.
Despite carrying no tuning,
the posterior is minimax over sparse classes with the sharp constant
(Theorem
2
, Proposition
2
) and its credible sets
are honest—guarantees a flat or Gaussian prior cannot match.

5.

The conclusions are not special to the horseshoe.
Every property
above follows from regular variation with a local pole, a class that also
contains the horseshoe
+

(
Bhadra et al. 2017
)
and the normal–exponential–gamma and
generalized double Pareto priors; the horseshoe is the most thoroughly
understood and the easiest to compute
(
Carvalho et al. 2010
;
Bhadra et al. 2019
;
Makalic and Schmidt 2016
)
, which
is why we adopt it as the representative default and discuss the family in
Section
5.3
.

6.

It is cheap and composable.
The
O(m)
Gibbs sampler of
Section
8
replaces only the prior on the area effects and leaves the
design-based first stage of a survey pipeline untouched, and the posterior
shrinkage coefficients
\kappa_{i}
are read off directly as interpretable
diagnostics.

The remainder of the paper turns each of these informal reasons into a
theorem, a risk calculation, or a simulation.

4
Tail robustness and bounded influence

We first isolate the qualitative property that separates global–local shrinkage
from the Gaussian smoothers of Sections
2.3
–
2.4
. Fix

\bm{\beta}
and work with the residual sequence
r_{i}=v_{i}+e_{i}
,

e_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,D_{i})
, under a generic scale mixture

v_{i}\sim\int\mathcal{N}(0,s^{2})\,\pi(\mathrm{d}s)
for the local prior
\pi
. Let

m_{i}(r)=\int\phi_{D_{i}+s^{2}}(r)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}s)
be the marginal density of

r_{i}
, where
\phi_{\sigma}
is the
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
density.

Lemma 1

(Heteroscedastic Tweedie identity)
.

For the model above, the posterior mean of the area effect is

\E(v_{i}\mid r_{i})=r_{i}+D_{i}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r_{i}}\log m_{i}(r_{i}),

(8)

and hence
\E(\theta_{i}\mid y_{i})=y_{i}+D_{i}\,(\log m_{i})^{\prime}(y_{i}-\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta})
.

Proof.

Since
r_{i}\mid v_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(v_{i},D_{i})
, the joint density factorizes as

\phi_{D_{i}}(r_{i}-v_{i})\,p(v_{i})
. Differentiating
m_{i}(r)=\int\phi_{D_{i}}(r-v)p(v)\,\mathrm{d}v
and using
\phi_{D_{i}}^{\prime}(r-v)=D_{i}^{-1}(v-r)\phi_{D_{i}}(r-v)
gives
m_{i}^{\prime}(r)=D_{i}^{-1}\int(v-r)\phi_{D_{i}}(r-v)p(v)\,\mathrm{d}v=D_{i}^{-1}\big(\E(v\mid r)-r\big)m_{i}(r)
.
Rearranging yields (
8
); the second statement follows from

\theta_{i}=\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}+v_{i}
.
∎

Equation (
8
) is the heteroscedastic form of Brown’s identity. The
estimator’s behaviour is governed by the tail of the marginal score

(\log m_{i})^{\prime}
. For a Gaussian effect
v_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,A)
one has

m_{i}=\phi_{A+D_{i}}
and
(\log m_{i})^{\prime}(r)=-r/(A+D_{i})
, so

\E(\theta_{i}\mid y_{i})=\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}+\{A/(A+D_{i})\}\,r_{i}
recovers
(
3
): the score is
linear
, the influence

\mathrm{d}\,\E(\theta_{i}\mid y_{i})/\mathrm{d}y_{i}=\gamma_{i}
is a constant
strictly below one, and the gap
y_{i}-\E(\theta_{i}\mid y_{i})
grows without bound as
the direct estimate moves into the tail. The same holds, coordinatewise, for the
BYM smoother. The horseshoe behaves oppositely.

Theorem 1

(Tail robustness of the horseshoe Fay–Herriot estimator)
.

Let
v_{i}
have the horseshoe prior (
6
) with
\tau
fixed and
D_{i}>0

known. Then the marginal
m_{i}
has polynomially heavy tails, with

m_{i}(r)\asymp r^{-2}
as
|r|\to\infty
, and consequently

(\log m_{i})^{\prime}(r)=-\frac{2}{r}+o(r^{-1}),\qquad\E(\theta_{i}\mid y_{i})=y_{i}-\frac{2D_{i}}{\,y_{i}-\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}\,}+o\!\big(|y_{i}|^{-1}\big)\;\to\;y_{i}.

(9)

Hence the influence
\mathrm{d}\,\E(\theta_{i}\mid y_{i})/\mathrm{d}y_{i}\to 1
and the
shrinkage gap
y_{i}-\E(\theta_{i}\mid y_{i})\to 0
: areas with strong signal are
returned essentially unshrunk, and the sensitivity of the estimate to its own
direct value is bounded over all
y_{i}
.

Proof.

Write
v=\tau\lambda z
with
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
\lambda\sim\mathcal{C}^{+}(0,1)
,
so
v\mid\lambda\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^{2}\lambda^{2})
and

p(v)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi_{\tau^{2}\lambda^{2}}(v)\,\tfrac{2}{\pi(1+\lambda^{2})}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda
. By Proposition
1
the prior
p
is regularly
varying with index
-2
:
p(v)=|v|^{-2}\ell(|v|)
for a slowly
varying
\ell

(
Bhadra et al. 2016
;
Bingham et al. 1987
)
. Convolution with the fixed light-tailed kernel

\phi_{D_{i}}
preserves both the index and the slowly varying factor, because the
contribution of the region
|v-r|>|r|/2
to
m_{i}(r)=\int\phi_{D_{i}}(r-v)p(v)\,\mathrm{d}v
is
O(e^{-r^{2}/8D_{i}})
, negligible against any polynomial, while on

|v-r|\leq|r|/2
one has
p(v)=p(r)\{1+o(1)\}
by slow variation and

\int\phi_{D_{i}}(r-v)\,\mathrm{d}v\to 1
. Hence
m_{i}(r)=r^{-2}\ell(r)\{1+o(1)\}
is
regularly varying with index
-2
, the analytic content of “the posterior mass
sits at
v\approx r
.” By the Karamata representation a regularly varying
function of index
\rho
satisfies
r\,(\log m_{i})^{\prime}(r)\to\rho
, here
\rho=-2
, so

(\log m_{i})^{\prime}(r)=-2/r+o(r^{-1})
. Substituting into (
8
) gives
(
9
). Finally
(\log m_{i})^{\prime}
is continuous and tends to
0
at

\pm\infty
, hence bounded on
\mathbb{R}
; and the influence

\mathrm{d}\E(\theta_{i}\mid y_{i})/\mathrm{d}y_{i}=1+D_{i}(\log m_{i})^{\prime\prime}(r)\to 1
since

(\log m_{i})^{\prime\prime}(r)=2r^{-2}+o(r^{-2})
.
∎

The contrast is the whole point of choosing the prior. A single county whose
direct mortality or poverty rate is genuinely extreme is, under EBLUP or BYM2,
dragged towards the fitted surface by a fixed fraction and towards its
neighbours by the graph; under the horseshoe it is left where the data put it,
while the quiet majority of areas, whose
r_{i}
are of order
\sqrt{D_{i}}
, are
shrunk hard. This is the redescending, bounded-influence behaviour familiar from
robust estimation, obtained here purely through the prior.

The qualitative statement can be sharpened into a uniform sensitivity bound,
which is what “bounded influence” means operationally for an area estimate.

Corollary 1

(Bounded sensitivity)
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
1
the posterior-mean map

y_{i}\mapsto\E(\theta_{i}\mid y_{i})
is continuously differentiable with

\sup_{y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}}\ \Big|\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y_{i}}\E(\theta_{i}\mid y_{i})\,\Big|\ =\ 1+D_{i}\sup_{r\in\mathbb{R}}(\log m_{i})^{\prime\prime}(r)\ <\ \infty,

(10)

and the gross-error sensitivity

\gamma^{\ast}_{i}=\sup_{r}|D_{i}(\log m_{i})^{\prime}(r)|
, the largest absolute change the prior
ever imposes on the direct estimate, is finite. By contrast, for the Gaussian
estimator (
3
) the analogous quantity

\sup_{r}|r-\E(v_{i}\mid r)|=\sup_{r}(D_{i}/(A+D_{i}))|r|=\infty
.

Proof.

The marginal
m_{i}
is a Gaussian scale-mixture, hence real-analytic and
strictly positive, so
\log m_{i}
is
C^{\infty}
and the derivative in
(
10
) exists everywhere. By Theorem
1
,

(\log m_{i})^{\prime}(r)\to 0
and
(\log m_{i})^{\prime\prime}(r)\to 0
as
|r|\to\infty
; both are
continuous, so each attains a finite supremum on
\mathbb{R}
, giving (
10
)
and the finiteness of
\gamma^{\ast}_{i}
. The Gaussian display is immediate from

\E(v_{i}\mid r)=\{A/(A+D_{i})\}r
.
∎

Corollary
1
is the precise sense in which the horseshoe is a

bounded-influence
procedure and the linear smoothers are not: an
adversarial corruption of one area’s direct estimate can move the EBLUP/BYM2
estimate of that area arbitrarily far, but moves the horseshoe estimate by at
most
\gamma^{\ast}_{i}
, a constant depending only on
D_{i}
and
\tau
. In small
area work, where a single misreported domain total is a routine occurrence, this
is a practical and not merely an asymptotic distinction.

5
Heteroscedastic posterior contraction and minimaxity

We now quantify estimation accuracy. The Fay–Herriot problem differs from the
sequence model on which horseshoe theory was built
(
van der Pas et al. 2014
;
van der Pas et al. 2017
)

only in that the noise variances
D_{i}
are known and unequal. This is exactly
the structure that lets us
standardize
. Put

s_{i}=\frac{r_{i}}{\sqrt{D_{i}}}=w_{i}+\varepsilon_{i},\qquad w_{i}=\frac{v_{i}}{\sqrt{D_{i}}},\quad\varepsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\ \text{i.i.d.}

(11)

The standardized signal
\bm{w}
has the same support—hence the same
sparsity—as
\bm{v}
, and recovering
\bm{\theta}
in the
D
-weighted loss

\sum_{i}(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i})^{2}/D_{i}
is equivalent to recovering
\bm{w}
in
ordinary squared error in the unit-variance model (
11
). The
prior on
w_{i}
is again horseshoe, with local scale
\lambda_{i}
unchanged and
global scale
\tau/\sqrt{D_{i}}
within a bounded factor of
\tau
under
Assumption
1
below, so the sequence-model theory applies with the
same calibration. We impose mild regularity.

Assumption 1

(Bounded heteroscedasticity)
.

There exist constants
0<\underline{D}\leq\overline{D}<\infty
, not depending on

m
, with
\underline{D}\leq D_{i}\leq\overline{D}
for all
i
.

Assumption 2

(Nearly black truth)
.

The true effect vector
\bm{v}_{0}
lies in

\ell_{0}[q_{m}]=\{\bm{v}:\#\{i:v_{i}\neq 0\}\leq q_{m}\}
with
q_{m}\to\infty
and

q_{m}=o(m)
.

Assumption
1
is automatic when design variances are bounded away
from zero and infinity after smoothing, the usual situation; it guarantees that
standardization neither creates nor destroys signal. Under
Assumption
2
the minimax risk of the standardized problem over

\ell_{0}[q_{m}]
is the classical needles-in-a-haystack rate
2q_{m}\log(m/q_{m})(1+o(1))

(
Donoho et al. 1992
)
.

Theorem 2

(Minimax contraction for the horseshoe Fay–Herriot posterior)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
–
2
hold and the horseshoe prior
(
6
) is used with a global scale
\tau=\tau_{m}
satisfying

\tau_{m}\asymp(q_{m}/m)\sqrt{\log(m/q_{m})}
, or with
\tau
given an independent
prior with sufficient mass near this value or estimated by marginal maximum
likelihood. Then for the standardized model (
11
) the full
posterior
\Pi(\cdot\mid\bm{s})
contracts about
\bm{w}_{0}
at the minimax rate:
for a sufficiently large constant
M
,

\sup_{\bm{w}_{0}\in\ell_{0}[q_{m}]}\E_{\bm{w}_{0}}\,\Pi\!\Big(\ \|\bm{w}-\bm{w}_{0}\|^{2}>M\,q_{m}\log(m/q_{m})\ \Big|\ \bm{s}\Big)\longrightarrow 0.

(12)

Consequently, in the original parameterization, the posterior mean

\hat{\theta}_{i}
(uniformly integrable on the contracting ball) has error

\Delta_{i}=\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{0i}
obeying

\sup_{\bm{v}_{0}\in\ell_{0}[q_{m}]}\ \E_{\bm{v}_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{D_{i}}\ \lesssim\ q_{m}\,\log(m/q_{m}),\qquad\sup_{\bm{v}_{0}\in\ell_{0}[q_{m}]}\ \E_{\bm{v}_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta_{i}^{2}\ \lesssim\ \overline{D}\,q_{m}\,\log(m/q_{m}).

(13)

Proof.

Standardization (
11
) maps the Fay–Herriot model to the
unit-variance sequence model with signal
\bm{w}_{0}
, whose sparsity equals that
of
\bm{v}_{0}
because
\sqrt{D_{i}}>0
. The horseshoe contraction theorem of

van der Pas et al. 2014
and its adaptive refinement
(
van der Pas et al. 2017
)
apply to

\bm{s}\mid\bm{w}_{0}
, giving (
12
) under the stated calibration of

\tau_{m}
; adaptivity to the unknown
q_{m}
is what licenses the empirical-Bayes
or hierarchical choice of
\tau
. Multiplying coordinate
i
by
\sqrt{D_{i}}

transports (
12
) to the weighted bound in (
13
), and

\Delta_{i}^{2}\leq\overline{D}\,\Delta_{i}^{2}/D_{i}
under Assumption
1

gives the unweighted bound.
∎

Remark 1

(Honest credible sets)
.

Under the excessive-bias-restriction / self-similarity conditions of

van der Pas et al. 2017
, the horseshoe credible balls for the standardized signal have
asymptotic frequentist coverage at least the nominal level and radius of minimax
order; standardization carries this to the
D
-weighted credible sets for

\bm{\theta}
. This is the theoretical counterpart of the strong empirical coverage
of the horseshoe under sparsity reported in Section
9
.

Remark 2

(Why the known
D_{i}
matter)
.

The reduction is available only because the
D_{i}
are known. With unknown
heteroscedastic variances no exact standardization exists and the minimax rate
itself changes. The Fay–Herriot premise—design variances supplied by the
survey—is therefore not a technical convenience but the structural fact that
makes the heavy-tailed prior provably minimax in this problem.

5.1
Calibrating the global scale

Theorem
2
requires the global scale to track the unknown
sparsity through
\tau_{m}\asymp(q_{m}/m)\sqrt{\log(m/q_{m})}
, which cannot be set a
priori. Two adaptive devices remove the oracle knowledge of
q_{m}
, and both are
what we use in practice.

Corollary 2

(Adaptive minimaxity)
.

Grant Assumptions
1
–
2
. Estimate the global scale by
marginal maximum likelihood,

\hat{\tau}=\arg\max_{\tau\in[1/m,\,1]}\prod_{i=1}^{m}m_{i}(s_{i};\tau)
,
truncated to the stated interval, and form the horseshoe posterior with

\tau=\hat{\tau}
; or place the half-Cauchy hyperprior
\tau\sim\mathcal{C}^{+}(0,1)

and use the full Bayes posterior. In either case the conclusion
(
12
)–(
13
) of Theorem
2
holds
without knowledge of
q_{m}
, uniformly over
\ell_{0}[q_{m}]
for every sequence

q_{m}\to\infty
,
q_{m}=o(m)
.

Proof sketch.

For the standardized data
\bm{s}
the two calibrations are exactly those analyzed
by
van der Pas et al. 2017
: the empirical-Bayes selector concentrates on a value of order

(q_{m}/m)\sqrt{\log(m/q_{m})}
with probability tending to one, and the full-Bayes
hyperprior assigns sufficient mass to a neighbourhood of that value while not
oversmoothing, by the same penalized-complexity argument. Either way the
plug-in posterior inherits the contraction rate of the oracle posterior. The
transfer to the
D
-weighted loss is the multiplication argument of
Theorem
2
.
∎

Corollary
2
matters for the comparison with smoothing: the
horseshoe needs no spatial tuning parameter and no neighbourhood graph, yet it
adapts automatically to how many areas are exceptional. Its single global scale
plays the role that the smoothing variance plays in BYM2, but is learned from the
data with a guarantee. We caution that the truncation lower bound
1/m
is not
cosmetic; without it the marginal likelihood can collapse
\hat{\tau}
to zero on a
null signal, the well-known degeneracy of the empirical-Bayes horseshoe, and the
half-Cauchy hyperprior is the safer default in small
m
.

5.2
A matching lower bound

Theorem
2
is an upper bound; it is worth recording that the rate
it achieves cannot be improved by any procedure, so the horseshoe is minimax and
not merely rate-adaptive within a suboptimal class.

Proposition 2

(Heteroscedastic minimax rate)
.

Under Assumption
1
, for the Fay–Herriot model
(
1
) with known variances
D_{i}\in[\underline{D},\overline{D}]
and
true effects in
\ell_{0}[q_{m}]
, the minimax risk in the
D
-weighted loss
satisfies

\inf_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}}\ \sup_{\bm{v}_{0}\in\ell_{0}[q_{m}]}\ \E_{\bm{v}_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{0i})^{2}}{D_{i}}\ =\ 2\,q_{m}\log(m/q_{m})\,\{1+o(1)\},

(14)

and the same order holds for the unweighted loss up to the constants

\underline{D},\overline{D}
. Hence the posterior mean of the calibrated horseshoe
of Corollary
2
attains the minimax rate, and the constant is sharp
in the weighted loss.

Proof.

Standardize as in (
11
); the weighted loss becomes ordinary
squared error for
\bm{w}_{0}
in the unit-variance sequence model, and the support
of
\bm{w}_{0}
equals that of
\bm{v}_{0}
since
\sqrt{D_{i}}>0
, so
\bm{w}_{0}\in\ell_{0}[q_{m}]
as well. The minimax risk for estimating a nearly-black vector in
the Gaussian sequence model over
\ell_{0}[q_{m}]
is
2q_{m}\log(m/q_{m})\{1+o(1)\}
by

Donoho et al. 1992
, which gives (
14
) exactly because standardization
is a measure-preserving bijection between the two problems. The unweighted
statement follows from
\underline{D}\,\|\hat{\bm{w}}-\bm{w}_{0}\|^{2}\leq\sum_{i}(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{0i})^{2}\leq\overline{D}\,\|\hat{\bm{w}}-\bm{w}_{0}\|^{2}
.
Attainment is Theorem
2
together with the standard conversion of
posterior contraction at rate
\epsilon_{m}^{2}
into a risk bound for the posterior
mean under the quadratic loss, valid here because the horseshoe posterior has
uniformly integrable normalized mass on the contracting ball.
∎

The point of Proposition
2
is that the known design variances do
not merely permit a convenient analysis: they pin down the exact minimax constant

2
in the weighted loss, and the horseshoe, with no knowledge of which areas are
exceptional or how many, matches it. Structured smoothing cannot make the same
claim over
\ell_{0}[q_{m}]
, which is the content of the next section.

5.3
Which global–local priors the theorems cover

Nothing in Theorems
1
–
2
is special to the
half-Cauchy local scale; both rest on two structural properties that a wide class
of global–local priors shares, and isolating them clarifies what the horseshoe
is doing and what would break if it were replaced. A prior

v_{i}\mid\lambda_{i},\tau\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2})
,
\lambda_{i}\sim\pi(\lambda)
,
in this class is characterized by the behaviour of
\pi
at the two ends.

Tail robustness
(Theorem
1
) needs only that the marginal

p(v)
be regularly varying at infinity with index
-(1+\eta)
for some

\eta\in(0,1]
, equivalently that
\pi(\lambda)
have a regularly varying right
tail of the same index. The redescending score is then

(\log m_{i})^{\prime}(r)=-(1+\eta)/r+o(r^{-1})
and the influence bound of
Corollary
1
holds verbatim; the horseshoe is the case
\eta=1
.
A prior with exponential or Gaussian
\lambda
-tails—ridge, or a normal prior
with estimated variance—fails this and reverts to unbounded linear shrinkage.

Minimaxity
(Theorem
2
) needs, in addition, an unbounded
spike:
\pi(\lambda)
must put enough mass near zero that the marginal has a pole,

p(v)\to\infty
as
v\to 0
, so that null areas are shrunk at the right rate. The
horseshoe’s
\mathrm{Beta}(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})
shrinkage profile delivers both ends at
once, which is why it is the canonical choice.

The hypotheses therefore hold, with the same proofs, for the horseshoe-plus, the
normal–exponential–gamma, and the generalized double Pareto priors, all of
which are regularly varying with index in
(0,1]
and have a local spike; they
fail for the Bayesian lasso (double-exponential), whose exponential tails give

(\log m_{i})^{\prime}(r)\to-\text{const}
, a bounded but non-redescending score, and for
the Strawderman–Berger and Dirichlet–Laplace priors only the local-spike
condition needs separate checking. The practical implication for small area work
is reassuring: the dichotomy between bounded-influence shrinkage and linear
smoothing that organizes this paper is a property of the heavy tail, not of the
particular prior, so the conclusions are robust to the analyst’s choice within
the heavy-tailed global–local family. We use the horseshoe because its
hyperparameters are the most thoroughly understood
(
Carvalho et al. 2010
;
van der Pas et al. 2017
)
and
its Gibbs sampler the simplest
(
Makalic and Schmidt 2016
)
, not because the theory
privileges it.

6
When does each philosophy win?

Theorems
1
–
2
concern a permutation-invariant
prior that ignores geography. Structured smoothing exploits geography and is
correspondingly better when geography is real. The following proposition makes
the trade-off precise; it is deliberately stated at the level of leading-order
risk so as to mirror the simulation of Section
9
.

Proposition 3

(Separation of regimes)
.

Consider the standardized model (
11
) on a connected graph with
Laplacian
\bm{Q}
.

(i)

(Smooth truth.)
If
\bm{w}_{0}
lies in the Sobolev-type ellipsoid

\{\bm{w}:\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{Q}\,\bm{w}\leq C\}
, the ICAR/BYM smoother with optimally
tuned variance attains risk of order
m^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
determined
by the spectral decay of
\bm{Q}
, whereas any permutation-invariant
shrinkage rule—the horseshoe included—has risk bounded below by a constant
multiple of
\min(1,\ \overline{D}\,q_{m}\log m/m)
scaled to the ellipsoid,
strictly larger in order. Smoothing wins.

(ii)

(Sparse truth.)
If
\bm{w}_{0}\in\ell_{0}[q_{m}]
with spikes of magnitude

a_{m}\to\infty
, the linear ICAR/BYM smoother incurs an oversmoothing bias on
each spike bounded below by
c\,a_{m}^{2}
for a graph-dependent
c\in(0,1)

that does not vanish, so its risk is
\Omega(q_{m}a_{m}^{2})
; the horseshoe posterior
mean has risk
O(q_{m}\log(m/q_{m}))
by Theorem
2
. For

a_{m}^{2}\gg\log(m/q_{m})
the horseshoe wins by an arbitrarily large factor.

Proof sketch.

(i) For a smooth signal the Laplacian penalty matches the truth and the linear
smoother is a regularized projection onto low-frequency graph harmonics; its
risk follows from the eigenvalue decay of
\bm{Q}
by the standard bias–variance
calculation for spectral smoothers. A permutation-invariant rule cannot use the
ordering of coordinates and is bounded below by the minimax risk over the
permutation orbit of
\bm{w}_{0}
, which for a smooth vector contains nearly-black
elements; this yields the stated lower order. (ii) Linearity forces the smoother
to apply a contraction with operator norm below one in the spike directions;
because a spike is high-frequency on the graph it is damped by a factor bounded
away from zero, leaving residual bias
\geq c\,a_{m}
per spike, hence squared bias

\geq c^{2}a_{m}^{2}
and aggregate risk
\Omega(q_{m}a_{m}^{2})
. The horseshoe rate is
Theorem
2
. Comparing the two gives the claim.
∎

Proposition
3
says the choice is not a matter of taste but of the
geometry of the truth. When deprivation or disease risk varies smoothly across a
map, Wakefield’s structured smoothing extracts a faster rate; when a handful of
areas are genuinely exceptional—a mining town, a border district, a reporting
artefact—the horseshoe protects them from being smoothed away. The hybrid that
adds local horseshoe scales to a spatial field
(
Tang and Ghosh 2023
)
is the
natural way to insure against both, at the cost of identifiability that we return
to in Section
12
.

7
Effective dimension and an unbiased risk estimate

The regime separation of Section
6
can be read through a single
scalar, the
effective dimension
of the fit, which also yields a practical
heteroscedastic risk estimate and a fair way to compare procedures of very
different form. Write the posterior-mean estimator coordinatewise as

\hat{\theta}_{i}=\E(\theta_{i}\mid\bm{y})
and recall the heteroscedastic Brown
identity (
8
). Stein’s lemma for the model

y_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta_{i},D_{i})
gives Mallows-style unbiased risk: for any weakly
differentiable
\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{y})
,

\mathrm{SURE}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big[(\hat{\theta}_{i}-y_{i})^{2}+2D_{i}\frac{\partial\hat{\theta}_{i}}{\partial y_{i}}\Big]-\sum_{i=1}^{m}D_{i},\qquad\E_{\bm{\theta}}\,\mathrm{SURE}=\E_{\bm{\theta}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i})^{2}.

(15)

The middle term defines the effective degrees of freedom

\mathrm{df}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})=\sum_{i}\partial\hat{\theta}_{i}/\partial y_{i}

(
Efron 2011
)
, the model’s data-driven complexity.

Proposition 4

(Effective dimension of the three estimators)
.

For the Gaussian/EBLUP estimator and for the BYM2 smoother, the effective
dimension is non-random given the variance components:
\mathrm{df}_{\mathrm{EB}}=\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}=\sum_{i}A/(A+D_{i})
, and
\mathrm{df}_{\mathrm{BYM}}=\operatorname{tr}\{\bm{S}\}
where
\bm{S}=(\bm{I}+\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{D})^{-1}
is the
linear smoother of (
3
) with
\bm{\Sigma}
the BYM2 covariance. For the
horseshoe,

\mathrm{df}_{\mathrm{HS}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\E\!\big[1-\kappa_{i}\mid\bm{y}\big]+\ \text{(score-covariance term)},\qquad\kappa_{i}=\frac{D_{i}}{D_{i}+\tau^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}},

(16)

and
\mathrm{df}_{\mathrm{HS}}
is itself a function of the data: it is small
when the data place every
\kappa_{i}
near one and grows as areas are freed.

Proof.

For a linear estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}=\bm{S}\bm{y}+\bm{b}
with
\bm{S},\bm{b}
not
depending on
\bm{y}
,
\partial\hat{\theta}_{i}/\partial y_{i}=S_{ii}
and

\mathrm{df}=\operatorname{tr}\bm{S}
; (
3
) has

S_{ii}=\gamma_{i}=A/(A+D_{i})
, and the BYM2 smoother is the stated ridge in the
GMRF precision, giving the two non-random expressions. For the horseshoe, apply
(
8
):
\hat{\theta}_{i}=y_{i}+D_{i}(\log m_{i})^{\prime}(r_{i})
, so

\partial\hat{\theta}_{i}/\partial y_{i}=1+D_{i}(\log m_{i})^{\prime\prime}(r_{i})
. Writing the marginal
score derivative through the conditional posterior variance,

D_{i}(\log m_{i})^{\prime\prime}(r_{i})=\Var(v_{i}\mid\bm{y})/D_{i}-1
, and

\Var(v_{i}\mid\bm{y})/D_{i}=\E[1-\kappa_{i}\mid\bm{y}]
plus the variance of the
conditional mean across the posterior of
(\lambda_{i},\tau)
, which is the
score-covariance term; summing gives (
16
). Data dependence is then
explicit through the posterior law of
\kappa_{i}
.
∎

The point is structural. EBLUP and BYM2 spend a
fixed
budget of degrees of
freedom set by the estimated variance components, distributed across areas by the
common fractions
\gamma_{i}
or the smoother diagonal; they cannot spend more on a
few exceptional areas without spending more everywhere. The horseshoe instead
allocates its budget adaptively, spending degrees of freedom only where

\kappa_{i}
is small. Equation (
15
) makes this comparable: each
procedure can be scored by the same unbiased risk estimate, with the horseshoe’s
data-dependent
\mathrm{df}_{\mathrm{HS}}
in place of the fixed

\operatorname{tr}\bm{S}
. In the sparse regime the adaptive allocation is exactly
what (
15
) rewards—a small fit-to-data gap on the spikes bought with
few global degrees of freedom—which is the risk-estimate counterpart of the
minimax statement of Theorem
2
and of the magnitude sweep
reported in Section
9.3
. We caution that
\mathrm{df}_{\mathrm{HS}}

is a measure of fitted complexity, not a consistent estimator of the number of
exceptional areas: strong signals inflate the global scale
\tau
and thereby
lift the
\kappa_{i}
of null areas, so the effective dimension can exceed the true
sparsity, a benign form of the global-scale contamination that motivates the
half-Cauchy hyperprior of Section
5.1
.

8
Posterior computation

The model (
1
)–(
6
) admits a simple and scalable Gibbs
sampler through the auxiliary-variable representation of the half-Cauchy

(
Makalic and Schmidt 2016
)
: if
x^{2}\mid a\sim\mathrm{IG}(\tfrac{1}{2},1/a)
and

a\sim\mathrm{IG}(\tfrac{1}{2},1)
then
x\sim\mathrm{C}^{+}(0,1)
, where
\mathrm{IG}
denotes the
inverse-gamma. Introducing
\xi_{i}
for
\lambda_{i}
and
\zeta
for
\tau
renders
every full conditional conjugate. With
r_{i}=y_{i}-\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}
and

D_{i}
known, one sweep is:

\displaystyle v_{i}\mid\cdot

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\!\Big((1-\kappa_{i})\,r_{i},\ (1-\kappa_{i})D_{i}\Big),\qquad\kappa_{i}=D_{i}/(D_{i}+\tau^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2});

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}\mid\cdot

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\!\Big((\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{X})^{-1}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{D}^{-1}(\bm{y}-\bm{v}),\ (\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{X})^{-1}\Big);

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}^{2}\mid\cdot

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{IG}\!\Big(1,\ \xi_{i}^{-1}+v_{i}^{2}/(2\tau^{2})\Big),\qquad\xi_{i}\mid\cdot\sim\mathrm{IG}\!\big(1,\ 1+\lambda_{i}^{-2}\big);

\displaystyle\tau^{2}\mid\cdot

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{IG}\!\Big(\tfrac{m+1}{2},\ \zeta^{-1}+\textstyle\sum_{i}v_{i}^{2}/(2\lambda_{i}^{2})\Big),\qquad\zeta\mid\cdot\sim\mathrm{IG}\!\big(1,\ 1+\tau^{-2}\big).

Each sweep costs
O(m)
for the effect and scale updates plus an
O(q^{3})
solve
for the
q
-dimensional regression, with
q
the number of covariates; the
sampler therefore scales linearly in the number of areas, in contrast to the

O(m^{3/2})
–
O(m^{3})
cost of dense spatial precision factorizations, although
INLA
(
Rue et al. 2009
)
mitigates this for sparse
\bm{Q}
. The shrinkage
coefficients
\kappa_{i}
are monitored directly and provide an interpretable
posterior summary: their distribution diagnoses how many areas the data deem
exceptional.

The inverse-gamma augmentation trades the heavy-tailed half-Cauchy conditionals
for conditionally conjugate updates, which keeps the sweep closed-form but can
slow mixing when an area sits near the boundary

after burn-in suffice; when mixing is a concern the slice sampler of

Neal 2003

is a drop-in alternative that avoids the
boundary slowdown. All summaries below are posterior means and equal-tailed

credible intervals from
4000
retained sweeps after a
2000

burn-in, and the code reproducing every number and figure is released with the

Application: lip cancer in Scotland

We analyze the Scottish lip cancer data of
Clayton and Kaldor 1987
, the
canonical spatial small area / disease-mapping data set and the original
motivating example for the Besag–York–Mollié model. For each of the
m=56

Scottish districts we have the observed and expected male lip-cancer counts

O_{i},E_{i}
over 1975–1980 and a covariate
x_{i}
, the percentage of the
workforce in agriculture, fishing and forestry (AFF), a proxy for outdoor
sunlight exposure. The data are strongly spatial: high-incidence districts
cluster in the rural north and along the coast.

The disease-mapping counts map directly onto the area-level model of this paper.
The direct estimator of the log relative risk and its known design variance are,
by the delta method,

y_{i}=\log\frac{O_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}}{E_{i}},\qquad D_{i}=\frac{1}{O_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}},

(17)

the continuity correction handling the two districts with
O_{i}=0
. The
D_{i}
are

known
and span more than a fortyfold range (
O_{i}
runs from
0
to
39
),
exactly the heteroscedastic Fay–Herriot premise of
Section
5
. We fit the direct estimator, EBLUP, a BYM model on the

real
district adjacency graph (
132
edges, between one and eleven
neighbours per district), and the horseshoe, each with intercept and the
standardized AFF covariate, by the samplers of Section
8
.

A smooth-spatial regime, as the theory predicts.

Because lip-cancer risk varies smoothly across the map, this is squarely the
regime in which Section

district is predicted from the others, BYM2 attains the lowest predictive mean

0.67

1.21
for

1.27
for the horseshoe and

mapping agency whose target is the smooth risk surface should use the spatial
smoother here—the honest conclusion our framework recommends, and one the
horseshoe does not overturn.

What the horseshoe adds: bounded-influence screening.

The horseshoe nonetheless supplies something the smoothers cannot: a per-district
diagnostic. Figure
4
(b) shows the posterior shrinkage
coefficient
\kappa_{i}
tracing the U-shape that the bounded-influence theory of
Section
4
predicts—districts whose direct estimate is far from
the regression surface,
high or low

ordinary districts are pooled. Ranked by
1-\kappa_{i}
, the five most exceptional
districts are those with
\mathrm{SMR}=4.5,\,3.5,\,3.2
(the rural
high-incidence outliers) together with two precisely-estimated low-incidence
districts (
\mathrm{SMR}=0.3,\,0.5

\kappa_{i}\leq 0.15
, are far below the bulk. These are exactly the districts a
spatial smoother would pull towards their neighbours and a screening exercise
would most want to see. The synthetic study of Section
9
confirms
the score is reliable: across
200
sparse-truth replications the ranking by

1-\kappa_{i}
recovers the genuinely exceptional areas with area under the ROC
curve
0.95
(standard error
0.004
). EBLUP and BYM2 offer no comparable
per-district signal, because their shrinkage fraction is common to all districts
at a given design variance.

The example thus illustrates both halves of the paper’s message on real data:
where the truth is smoothly spatial the structured smoother is the better
estimator and our framework says so, yet the horseshoe’s area-specific shrinkage
turns the same fit into a calibrated screening tool for the exceptional districts
that smoothing is designed to suppress. In a complete pipeline the two compose:
the horseshoe layer replaces only the prior on the area effects, leaves the
design-based first stage untouched, and its
O(m)
sampler adds negligible cost.

11
A research direction: deep learning for spatial effects

The two priors studied here borrow strength through an
explicit
model of
the area effects: a Gaussian Markov random field tied to a neighbourhood graph,
or a heavy-tailed product prior. A third route, which we sketch as a direction
for future work, is to borrow strength
implicitly
by learning a flexible
nonlinear map from spatial coordinates and covariates to the area rate, in the
spirit of the deep spatio-temporal models of
Dixon et al. 2019
. There the
hierarchical layers of a neural network are trained to predict spatio-temporal
flows and, notably, to capture the
sharp discontinuities
—congestion
fronts, regime changes—that smooth linear predictors blur. Subnational
indicators have the same structure: a coastline, an administrative border, or a
fault between a deprived and an affluent district is a spatial discontinuity that
a smoothing prior will oversmooth and a permutation-invariant prior, blind to
geography, cannot reconstruct at all.

A network trained with the
quantile
(pinball) loss
(
Koenker and Bassett 1978
)

inherits two features that matter for small area work: it returns a predictive
distribution rather than a point, and its ReLU architecture represents the area
surface as a continuous piecewise-linear function that can turn sharply across a
fault while remaining smooth elsewhere. We give a deliberately simple
illustration. On a
32\times 32
lattice (
m=1024
) a latent surface combines a
smooth gradient with a sharp curved fault of height
2
; we observe noisy rates

y_{i}=\theta_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}
,
\varepsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.4^{2})
. A two-hidden-layer
ReLU network (
64+64
units) takes the coordinates as input and is trained to
predict the conditional deciles
q\in\{0.1,0.5,0.9\}
; a radial-basis
kernel-ridge smoother, tuned by cross-validation, stands in for the linear
spatial smoothing of BYM2/INLA. To restore honest uncertainty we recalibrate the
network’s band by conformalized quantile regression

(
Romano et al. 2019
)
, using a held-out calibration split.

The behaviour (Figure

predicts. On the full grid the network and the smoother are comparable in
recovering the latent surface (mean squared error
0.083
versus
0.080
against

0.166
), but in a narrow band around

0.243
against the smoother’s
0.399

39\%

reduction: it keeps the cliff that the smoother washes out. The raw quantile band
is too narrow, covering
60\%
at the nominal

finite-sample failing of flexible quantile estimators—but a single conformal
adjustment raises empirical coverage to
0.75
at the cost of a wider, honest
interval, illustrating that the distributional output can be made calibrated
cheaply.

11.1
A Bayesian counterpart: data-augmented quantile regression

The network just described is trained by stochastic gradient descent and needs a
post-hoc conformal step to make its intervals honest. A fully Bayesian
construction supplies calibrated uncertainty automatically and inherits the
horseshoe’s tail robustness, through the data-augmentation
mixtures
envelope
of
Polson and Scott 2011b
. Their observation is that a regularized-loss
pseudo-likelihood of the form
\exp\{-\rho(u)\}
is often a location-scale
mixture of normals, so that conditioning on a latent scale renders it Gaussian
and brings the Bayesian machinery for linear models to bear. The pinball loss

\rho_{p}(u)=u\{p-\mathbf{1}(u<0)\}
of quantile regression
(
Koenker and Bassett 1978
)
is the
canonical example:
\exp\{-\rho_{p}(u)\}
is the asymmetric Laplace density, which
admits the representation

\varepsilon\;=\;\theta\,\omega+\kappa\sqrt{\omega}\,z,\qquad\omega\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1),\ z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),\qquad\theta=\frac{1-2p}{p(1-p)},\ \ \kappa^{2}=\frac{2}{p(1-p)},

(18)

a normal mean–variance mixture
(
Kozumi and Kobayashi 2011
)
. Writing the
p
th quantile
surface as
f_{p}(\bm{s})=\bm{b}(\bm{s})^{\top}\bm{\beta}
in a spatial basis
\bm{b}(\cdot)

(splines or radial bases in the coordinates), the area model becomes

y_{i}=\bm{b}(\bm{s}_{i})^{\top}\bm{\beta}+\theta\,\omega_{i}+\kappa\sqrt{\omega_{i}}\,z_{i}
, and
placing a horseshoe prior on
\bm{\beta}
closes a fully conjugate Gibbs sampler:

\displaystyle\omega_{i}\mid\cdot

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{GIG}\!\big(\tfrac{1}{2},\ \kappa^{-2}r_{i}^{2},\ \theta^{2}\kappa^{-2}+2\big),\quad r_{i}=y_{i}-\bm{b}(\bm{s}_{i})^{\top}\bm{\beta},

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}\mid\cdot

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\!\big(\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{W}(\bm{y}-\theta\bm{\omega}),\ \bm{\Sigma}\big),\quad\bm{\Sigma}=\big(\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{W}\bm{B}+\mathrm{diag}\{(\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2})^{-1}\}\big)^{-1},

with
\bm{W}=\mathrm{diag}\{(\kappa^{2}\omega_{i})^{-1}\}
and the local and global
scales
\lambda_{j}^{2},\tau^{2}
updated by the same inverse-gamma augmentation as in
Section
8
. The latent
\omega_{i}
are drawn from a generalized
inverse Gaussian, exactly as the SVM latent variables of
Polson and Scott 2011b
; the
heavy-tailed horseshoe on
\bm{\beta}
keeps the fitted quantile surface tail-robust
in the sense of Section
4
.

On the fault surface of Figure
5
(here a
20\times 20
lattice, a

10\times 10
radial basis) the sampler at
p\in\{0.1,0.5,0.9\}
behaves as the
theory promises for
uncertainty
: the
80\%
credible band attains
empirical coverage
0.80
, the nominal level, with
no
conformal
correction—in contrast to the network’s raw
0.60
. Its weakness is the dual of
the network’s strength: the smooth radial basis blurs the discontinuity (median
mean squared error
0.63
in the fault band, against the network’s
0.24
),
though it still denoises overall (
0.124
versus the direct
0.138
). The two
methods are thus complementary—the network captures sharp geometry, the
data-augmented horseshoe supplies calibrated, tail-robust uncertainty—which is
precisely why the synthesis we flag in Section
12
, a horseshoe prior
on the weights or outputs of a spatial network, is attractive.

We stress the limits of this illustration. With
m
in the dozens, the regime of
most small area applications, a network of this size would overfit hopelessly;
the example uses
m=1024
precisely because deep learning is data-hungry, and the
controlled lattice avoids the regularization and architecture search that real
deployment demands. The point is qualitative: deep learners offer a distinct,
covariate- and geometry-driven mode of borrowing strength, strongest exactly
where both competing priors are weak—at sharp spatial features—and, paired
with the data-augmented Bayesian construction above, they can be made to deliver
the honest interval estimates that small area estimation requires.

12
Discussion and future research

We have placed two ways of borrowing strength in small area estimation on a
common footing and separated them where it matters. Structured spatial smoothing
of the Wakefield type is rate-optimal when area effects vary smoothly across the
map; the horseshoe Fay–Herriot model is tail-robust and minimax when a minority
of areas are exceptional, the regime in which the constant linear shrinkage of
Gaussian random effects is most damaging. Regular variation
(Section
3
) is the single property behind the horseshoe’s robustness
and its standing as a default, and the known design variances of the survey are
what make the heavy-tailed prior provably minimax, through the standardization of
Section
5
.

Several directions for future research follow naturally.

1.

Spatial–global–local hybrids.
The model that adds local
horseshoe scales to a BYM2 field, begun by
Tang and Ghosh 2023
, should inherit
the smooth-regime rate while retaining bounded influence on spikes, provided the
global scale and the spatial variance are made identifiable—for instance by the

Riebler et al. 2016
variance split. A contraction theory for this hybrid, of the
kind proved here for the pure horseshoe, is open.

2.

Calibrated anomaly detection.
The bounded-influence expansion
(
9
) makes the posterior law of the shrinkage coefficient

\kappa_{i}
a continuous anomaly score, with
\Pr(\kappa_{i}<\tfrac{1}{2}\mid\bm{y})
a
Bayesian analogue of the
Datta et al. 2011
;
Datta and Mandal 2015
random-effect test;
calibrating that score in the heteroscedastic setting, perhaps by conformal
methods as in Section
11
, would turn screening into a procedure with
guarantees.

3.

Unit-level and non-Gaussian models.
The standardization argument
is specific to the area-level model with known
D_{i}
. Unit-level and generalized
linear mixed extensions, where the working variances are estimated and the exact
reduction of Section
5
breaks, require a perturbation analysis we
have not attempted; benchmarking horseshoe estimates to published direct totals,
so that aggregates are preserved, is a further requirement for official
statistics.

4.

Deep learning for spatial structure.
Section
11

suggests that deep quantile networks are most valuable exactly where both priors
struggle, at sharp spatial features, and that conformal calibration can supply
the honest bands they otherwise lack. A principled marriage—global–local
shrinkage on the weights or outputs of a spatial network, so that the borrowing
of strength is itself learned but remains tail-robust—is, to us, the most
promising synthesis, and connects this work to the broader program of regularized
deep learning for spatio-temporal data
(
Dixon et al. 2019
)
.

The central message is methodological: the choice between smoothing, shrinkage,
and learning is a choice about the geometry of the unknown surface, and stating
it that way lets the data—through the posterior distribution of the shrinkage
coefficients, or the fitted regions of a network—largely make the choice for
us.
