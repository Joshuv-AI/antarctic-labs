Displacement, deprivation, and loss of dignity: The psychosocial and material impact of genocide on elderly Gazans
Authors: Bilal Hamamra, Fayez Mahamid, Manal Ihbaisheh, Mariam AL-ashaal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Repeated cycles of conflict and forced displacement in the Gaza Strip have created a severe humanitarian crisis, with specific implications for the aging population. Older adults face distinct risks due to physical frailty, chronic health needs, and the breakdown of community support structures. This qualitative study investigates the psychosocial and material consequences of genocide on elderly Gazans, examining the impacts of displacement, deprivation, and loss of dignity. Through in-depth interviews with forty elderly men and women residing in shelters in Rafah, the research uncovers how repeated forced displacement has shattered the sense of home and security, amplifying chronic anxiety, grief, and disorientation. Systemic deprivation—including the collapse of health infrastructure and restricted access to essential medicines—further exacerbates physical suffering and a pervasive sense of helplessness. Overcrowded, unsanitary shelter conditions strip older adults of autonomy and dignity, intensifying psychological wounds. Despite severe hardship, many participants demonstrate resilience through faith, cultural values such as sumud (steadfastness), and mutual support, yet these coping strategies are stretched to their limits by ongoing violence and scarcity. The study calls for urgent, age-sensitive humanitarian responses to safeguard the health, dignity, and fundamental rights of Gaza’s elderly in the face of continued genocide and deprivation.

Introduction

The repeated cycles of violent conflict and forced population movement in the Gaza Strip have generated chronic humanitarian emergencies, particularly for the general elderly population in Gaza(WHO, 2024; Albelbeisi et al., 2025).The ongoing genocide in Gaza, which started in late 2023 and continues into 2025, have resulted in significant infrastructure destruction, prolonged displacement, and the near-total collapse of basic services including water, electricity, and healthcare access (WHO, 2024; HRW, 2024; Qutishat et al., 2025); as a result, older Gazans have experienced the erosion of their fundamental human needs and dignity (ReliefWeb, 2023; HelpAge International, 2023;Jamaluddine et al., 2025).

Displacement, a recurring trauma for many across generations, emerges as a central axis around which other hardships revolve (Doctors Without Borders, 2025;Mahamid, 2022; Asi, 2024). Forced evictions, often occurring under threats and with little warning, shatter not only physical structures but also routines of daily life, social connections, and cherished memories that homes represent for older individuals (HRW, 2024; Nijim, 2023;Abuward, 2025). These displacements were frequently driven by military directives from the occupying forces, requiring residents to travel from northern and central areas toward Rafah under perilous conditions. The journey often involved prolonged periods of walking or transport on animal-drawn carts, during which the elderly were exposed to physical exhaustion, dehydration, and the risk of detention or attack at military checkpoints. The loss of a home, for an elderly person in Gaza, often signifies the obliteration of a lifetime's work, a repository of familial history, and a crucial anchor of identity and security in an already precarious environment (IDMC Ajyal, 2025; Jamaluddine et al., 2024). This abrupt severance from familiar surroundings is repeatedly linked to acute psychological distress, including heightened anxiety, intense grief, and a pervasive sense of disorientation among the aged (Aldabbour et al., 2025; Albelbeisi et al., 2025).

The pervasive lack of safety that accompanies these cycles of violence and displacement is another critical stressor (HRW, 2024; Emina, 2025). Older individuals, often with limited mobility or pre-existing health conditions, describe the sheer terror of aerial bombardments, ground incursions, and the constant threat of injury or death (ReliefWeb, 2023; Abuward et al., 2025). This setting of perpetual insecurity undermines any semblance of normalcy and control, contributing significantly to trauma symptomatology (Aldabbour et al., 2025; Hamamra et al., 2025a;Jebril et al., 2023). Elevated rates of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, and generalized anxiety disorder were found among elderly Gazans who have experienced direct exposure to violence and multiple displacements (Aldabbour et al., 2025; Albelbeisi et al., 2025; Hamamra et al., 2025b). The trauma is often cumulative, with each new wave of conflict reactivating memories of past losses and fears, leading to a worsening effect on mental health (Doctors Without Borders, 2025; IDMC Ajyal, 2025). In the aftermath of major escalations, the inability to find safe refuge, even within designated shelters, has been shown to intensify feelings of helplessness and despair, directly impacting the psychological well-being of older persons (Albelbeisi et al., 2025; WHO, 2024; Irfan et al., 2025).

Conditions within shelters, frequently overcrowded and ill-equipped United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East (UNRWA) schools or makeshift encampments, present a further challenge of hardship that disproportionately affects the elderly (ReliefWeb, 2023; IDMC Ajyal, 2025; Aldabbour et al., 2025). There are severe deficiencies in these temporary accommodations, including inadequate sanitation, insufficient access to clean water and nutritious food, and a lack of privacy (HRW, 2024; WHO, 2024; Irfan et al., 2025). For older individuals with chronic illnesses such as diabetes, hypertension, or heart disease, the disruption to routine, diet, and, the lack of medicine, can have life-threatening consequences (Qutishat, 2025; WHO, 2024). Elderly people face immense struggles in managing their health under such conditions, often having to choose between seeking scarce medical attention and other essential needs (HelpAge International, 2023;Guillot et al., 2025).The indignity of queueing for hours for basic necessities, sleeping on thin mats on cold floors, and the inability to maintain personal hygiene are recurrent themes that emphasize the erosion of older adults’ personal dignity (ReliefWeb, 2023; HRW, 2024). This loss of autonomy and the forced dependence on aid systems are deeply humiliating for an age group that typically values self-reliance and respect (IDMC Ajyal, 2025; HRW, 2024;Albelbeisi et al., 2025).In a community-based cross-sectional study, Elsous et al. (2019) surveyed a representative sample of individuals aged 60 and above from all five governorates of Gaza using the World Health Organization’s Quality of Life instrument (WHOQOL-BREF). The findings revealed noticeable compromised levels of general satisfaction and well-being among older adults, highlighting the population’s high baseline vulnerability.

The severe shortage of essential medicines and healthcare services, worsened during periods of intense conflict and tightened siege, is a pressing issue for the elderly population (WHO, 2024; Qutishat et al., 2025). Access to medication for chronic conditions becomes extremely challenging, if not impossible, during and immediately after military offensives (HelpAge International, 2023; Doctors Without Borders, 2025). This interruption in care leads to the exacerbation of existing health problems, increased morbidity, and preventable deaths (WHO, 2024;Jamaluddine et al., 2025). Older Gazans express their anxiety about running out of vital medications and sometimes resort to desperate measures, such as rationing pills or relying on less effective alternatives (ReliefWeb, 2023; Doctors Without Borders, 2025; Emina, 2025). This medical precarity is not only a physical threat but also a continuous source of mental anguish, contributing to feelings of hopelessness and abandonment, further diminishing their quality of life and sense of self-worth (Qutishat, 2025; Doctors Without Borders, 2025). The psychological toll of watching one's health deteriorate due to a lack of accessible treatment, a situation directly resulting from conflict and blockade, is a severe assault on their dignity (Emina, 2025).

Despite these immense challenges, the remarkable resilience and coping mechanisms employed by elderly Gazans are evident, with individual spiritual practices often playing a central role (Hamamra et al., 2025b;Migdad et al., 2024). Mahamid et al. (2022) examined the interrelations among quality of life, depressive symptoms, hopelessness, resilience, and sense of belonging in 437 Palestinian older adults using structural equation modeling. Their results indicate that sense of belonging and resilience fully mediate the relationship between quality of life and negative mental health outcomes. Faith and religious observance provide a crucial source of comfort, meaning, and strength (Hamamra et al., 2025b; Mahamid et al., 2022). Prayer (Salah), recitation of the Quran, and a deep-seated belief in God's will (Qadar) are frequently cited by older individuals as vital tools for enduring immense suffering and loss (Hamamra et al., 2025b; Qutishat, 2025). This spiritual grounding helps many to maintain a sense of inner peace and perspective amidst chaos, offering a context for understanding their challenges and fostering hope for divine mercy and eventual justice (Migdad et al., 2024). Elderly individuals describe how turning to God helps them endure the loss of loved ones, homes, and physical well-being, framing their endurance as a form of spiritual steadfastness (Hamamra et al., 2025b; Migdad et al., 2024). This reliance on faith is not merely a passive acceptance of fate but an active, individual strategy for emotional regulation and maintaining psychological equilibrium (Qutishat, 2025).

Beyond spiritual coping, the Palestinian concept of sumud (steadfastness) is often implicitly and explicitly demonstrated by the elderly, who draw upon lifetimes of experience in facing their hardships (Hamamra et al., 2025b). In the Arab cultural setting, dignity (Karama) serves as a central moral value, implying a status of respect and autonomy that the elderly expect to retain. This concept relates directly to sumud, as maintaining one's dignity acts as a form of resistance against dehumanizing conditions. For the participants in this study, dignity was not an abstract concept but was operationally defined by the ability to preserve physical privacy, maintain personal hygiene, and secure daily necessities without resorting to begging or dependency. Elderly Gazans sustain small, familiar routines whenever possible—even amid severe disruption—to retain a sense of normalcy and agency. Storytelling and the sharing of personal and collective histories of resistance and survival also emerge as strategies through which older Gazans affirm their identity and transmit resilience to younger generations, thereby reinforcing their own sense of purpose and dignity (Migdad, 2024; Qutishat, 2025). Elderly Palestinians hold a specific social status as the custodians of collective memory and historical continuity. Their presence serves as a living link to the land, symbolizing a long history of endurance against occupation that commands respect within the family structure. Furthermore, the ability to find solace in simple acts of mutual support within fragmented communities, or even deriving strength from the responsibility of caring for grandchildren in the absence of parents, demonstrates adaptive capacities (Nijim, 2023). These coping strategies, however, are severely strained by the sheer intensity and chronicity of the violence and deprivation (Doctors Without Borders, 2025; Qutishat, 2025). The capacity for resilience, while remarkable, is not infinite, and the continuous assault on their well-being, safety, and dignity pushes many elderly Gazans to their limits, highlighting the urgent need for protective measures and sustained humanitarian and psychosocial support tailored to their specific vulnerabilities (WHO, 2024; IDMC Ajyal, 2025). While spiritual faith and inherent resilience offer vital internal resources, the external conditions of genocidal violence and systemic deprivation create a context where basic human dignity and mental health are under constant, severe threat for Gaza's elderly (HRW, 2024; Aldabbour et al., 2025). Therefore, the objective of this research is to investigate the psychosocial and material impacts of displacement, deprivation, and loss of dignity among elderly Gazans during the ongoing genocide.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study was designed to explore the psychosocial and material impact of displacement, deprivation, and loss of dignity among elderly Gazans during the ongoing genocide. Our research was guided by three central questions: (1) How do elderly Gazans experience and interpret repeated cycles of forced displacement? (2) In what ways do deprivation and loss of essential resources affect their physical and psychological well-being? (3) How do older adults perceive the erosion of dignity in the context of shelter life, chronic insecurity, and the breakdown of community structures?

Participants

We conducted in-depth interviews with forty elderly Gazans (22 men, aged 50–78, and 18 women, aged 55–76), all residing in temporary shelters in Rafah following displacement during the Israeli genocide on Gaza. While the chronological definition of elderly status is often set at 60 years, this study included individuals aged 50 and above. This inclusion criteria accounts for the accelerated physiological aging and health deterioration associated with chronic stress and harsh living conditions in Gaza, consistent with humanitarian frameworks that recognize 'older' status may begin earlier in protracted crises. Demographic information was collected from all participants, including age, gender, educational level, marital status, parenthood status, family size, and displacement-related family circumstances, including separation from family members due to forced displacement.

Participants were identified through daily interactions and community engagement by three female MA students from Gaza, themselves displaced and residing in the same shelters while pursuing online Clinical Psychology studies at [MASKED FOR PEER REVIEW]. This purposive, community-based recruitment is consistent with qualitative research in war-affected settings, leveraging insider access and lived experience to foster trust, authenticity, and participant comfort (Parker, 2005; Strauss and Corbin, 1990). The MA students’ proximity to participants’ daily realities enabled them to document both observed and personally recounted experiences of displacement, deprivation, and resilience.

Before participating, all individuals were informed of the study’s aims, procedures, and ethical protections, including confidentiality, voluntary participation, and the right to withdraw at any point. Informed consent was obtained from each participant in accordance with ethical protocols for research in conflict-affected populations.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted between November 2023 and January 2024. Using a semi-structured interview guide, the MA students carried out in-depth, face-to-face interviews in Arabic within the shelters. Interviews focused on the elderly participants’ experiences of forced displacement, daily challenges arising from deprivation of resources, their sense of dignity, and their coping mechanisms amid ongoing violence. The semi-structured format allowed for sensitivity and flexibility, which is essential when addressing trauma and resilience in war-affected populations (Parker, 2005).

Ethical considerations

Prior to each interview, participants received a clear explanation of the study’s aims, methodology, and their rights. Confidentiality and the right to withdraw at any stage were emphasized. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) [MASKED FOR PEER REVIEW], with all procedures fully complying with APA and Helsinki Declaration standards.

Data processing and analysis

With participants’ consent, all interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim in Arabic. To ensure accuracy and preserve cultural context, the initial coding and analysis were conducted in the original Arabic. Selected quotations were subsequently translated into English by the authors who hold degrees in English and Translation, focusing on conceptual equivalence to capture the participants' intended meanings. Thematic content analysis (TCA) was applied to the data using an inductive approach, where patterns were identified directly from the raw data rather than applying a pre-existing coding frame. Transcripts were open-coded by the research team manually, without the use of software, and codes were organized into key themes and subthemes reflecting the lived experience of displacement, deprivation, and dignity loss among elderly Gazans. This analytic approach is well-established for capturing the complex realities of marginalized and conflict-affected populations (Strauss and Corbin, 1990; Parker, 2005). The research team collaboratively reviewed coding and categorization to ensure consistency and reliability. Coding continued until thematic saturation was reached, indicating the adequacy and depth of the dataset (Strauss and Corbin, 1990).

Results

Participant characteristics

The study included forty displaced participants (22 men, 18 women) ranging in age from 50 to 78 years. Educational levels varied, with 10 participants having completed primary education or less, 18 having completed secondary education, and 12 holding university degrees. Twenty-eight participants were married and twelve were widowed. All participants were parents, with family sizes ranging from 3 to 9 children, although many reported separations from family members due to displacement. We identified 5 themes: 1. Displacement and the Erosion of Home; 2. The Collapse of Health and the Agony of Unmet Needs; 3. Humiliation, Hygiene, and the Assault on Dignity; 4. Fear, Insomnia, and Grief; 5. Endurance, Coping, and the Battle for Dignity.

Displacement and the erosion of home

The ongoing genocide has rendered displacement a persistent and brutal reality for Gaza’s elderly, stripping them of any sense of permanence or safety. This experience was universal among the sample, with every participant reporting the loss of their primary residence and the trauma of multiple forced evacuations. One survivor said, “We don’t know where to sit or where to go, there is no food, no good drink, life is very hard, and psychologically, we are very tired. We are ending our lives in terror and fear.” Displacement, for many, has turned life into a process of ongoing loss, marked by anxiety and exhaustion. Another explained, “We were forced to flee with my children and grandchildren to a relative’s land—more than a hundred people in one place, with one bathroom. Great suffering in going to the toilet and water shortage.” The absence of a stable, private home is not just a logistical issue but a source of shame and deprivation. A third reflected, “Every time I returned after displacement, I found my home robbed, and after the last displacement, I found everything stolen, nothing left to protect me in winter or summer.” The trauma of repeated uprooting is magnified by the violation of personal space and the loss of the last remnants of comfort. Yet, for some, the refusal to leave Gaza despite this devastation reflects a determination to practice sumud, holding onto their presence as an act of defiance.

Testimonies reveal not only material devastation but also deepening psychological wounds inflicted by repeated dislocation. One participant described, “We are displaced daily from area to area, walking long distances with no food or medicine, no strength to walk these distances, my house was demolished, and my body is weak from all this suffering.” Displacement is not a temporary hardship but a chronic, exhausting ordeal. Another said, “I had to sleep on the floor in schools or even on the street during displacement.” The lack of shelter is experienced as a complete stripping away of dignity, transforming elders from respected family members into refugees dependent on the most basic charity. “I miss sitting comfortably or having a clean place to rest. Every part of daily life now feels like a struggle.” Here, the loss of comfort and security is felt as a daily, ongoing assault.

The collapse of health and the Agony of Unmet needs

The destruction of Gaza’s health system has left elderly residents facing relentless pain and the terror of untreated illness, a dominant concern raised by the vast majority of participants (n = 35), particularly those dependent on medication for chronic conditions. One man said, “I need blood pressure and diabetes medicines and eye drops. I could not get these medicines, especially insulin injections. I had to reduce the use of injections, even though I would faint often from high sugar and had nothing sweet to help when I collapsed.” The inability to access medication has made survival precarious, instilling constant fear and physical weakness. Another explained, “I can’t provide my medicine; I can’t reach distant medical points because I have no money for transportation. Sometimes, I ask neighbors, but everyone says medicine is unavailable. I have severe pain in my back and legs and suffer from constant constipation—death, by God, would be more merciful than this life.” Unmet medical needs drive people to the brink of despair, with social isolation amplifying their suffering.

Some face complex and worsening conditions: “I am a cancer patient, my colon was removed, I have nerve problems, sciatica, diabetes, and high blood pressure—all chronic diseases, and I have no access to medication. My pain and illness have only increased with the war.” Ordinary illnesses are turned into instruments of torture, making everyday life a relentless struggle. The lack of clean water and sanitation further endangers health: “No clean water, it affected my ability and personal hygiene, leading to some infectious skin diseases.” Being unable to maintain basic hygiene is not merely uncomfortable but a source of shame and self-blame. Another said, “I have lost weight, my chronic diseases are worse, and my health has generally deteriorated because of hunger and lack of medicine.” Physical decline under siege is thus also a daily psychological reminder of deprivation and vulnerability.

Humiliation, hygiene, and the assault on dignity

The destruction of privacy and personal hygiene facilities has created a daily experience of humiliation among Gaza’s elderly, described by nearly all respondents as a primary source of psychological distress inherent to shelter life. One participant said, “We are more than a hundred people in one place with only one bathroom. It is great suffering to relieve oneself or go to the bathroom, with scarce water and no toilet paper.” Overcrowding does not just inconvenience but strips individuals of their dignity, making even the most basic acts of self-care public and shameful. Another recounted, “Even going to the bathroom at night, I have to walk a long distance to reach the camp bathroom. There’s no lighting, so I end up relieving myself in the tent in a primitive way, and I’m ashamed of this, but that’s our reality now.” Loss of privacy leads to internalized shame, as elders are forced into degrading adaptations that violate their sense of self.

The collapse of ordinary comforts and routines intensifies humiliation. “We sleep on the ground, no mattress, insects crawl on our bodies, we accept everything—the noise, the hardship, even cooking on firewood because we have no gas.” Constant exposure to dirt and discomfort leads to resignation and deepens a sense of worthlessness. “I have no proper clothes for winter or summer; my clothes are torn, and I feel exposed all the time.” Deprivation of even the most basic resources is felt as a personal affront, undermining confidence and social standing. “I can’t get up quickly or stand in line for the bathroom, and sometimes I have accidents. It’s humiliating to have others see me this way.” Losing control over personal needs leaves lasting psychological wounds and erodes self-worth.

Fear, insomnia, and grief

The psychological trauma inflicted on Gaza’s elderly is cumulative and all-encompassing, with every interviewee recounting episodes of extreme terror and sleep disturbance. One survivor said, “Psychologically, we are very tired, ending our lives in terror and fear.” Fear and exhaustion have become constant companions, replacing any sense of security or hope. Another described, “I cannot sleep, I cannot get even the simplest needs except by going far away.” Insomnia is not only a result of physical discomfort but also the outcome of relentless stress and hypervigilance. “After my son was martyred, the war became total psychological and emotional destruction for me.” Grief was a prominent subtheme, with more than half of the participants reporting the death of immediate family members. Personal loss in the context of genocide intensifies grief to an unbearable degree, turning bereavement into a form of ongoing psychological torture.

These traumas undermine the ability to function and destroy all remaining sources of comfort. One participant stated, “My heart is broken, we wish for death a thousand times, our situation is very difficult.” Despair is so pervasive that death is often seen as preferable to continued suffering. “I feel psychological and physical helplessness, exhaustion, we were already sick before the war, and with the war, our suffering has doubled. I lost my home and everything I own.” Repeated losses create a sense of powerlessness that erodes the will to endure. “There is no peace, no quiet, only the sound of bombing and people crying. Every day is worse than the last.” Psychological wounds remain open amid constant threat and presence of violence, preventing healing and intensifying hopelessness.

Endurance, coping, and the battle for dignity

Despite overwhelming hardship, most participants (n = 30) seek strength through faith, mutual support, and acts of resilience. One participant said, “Through praise, seeking forgiveness, patience, and prayer, and helping my children and grandchildren.” One woman described this persistence as an obligation, stating, "Our sumud is in our patience; we endure the hunger and the fear because this is our land, and we have nowhere else." Spiritual practices and caring for family offer a way to reclaim agency and meaning. However, a subset of participants (n = 10) expressed a sense of total depletion, indicating that their coping mechanisms had collapsed under the strain. Another explained, “We rely on God, and on support from neighbors and relatives, we share what we have, food and medicine, and try to remain calm.” Communal solidarity becomes a crucial psychological lifeline, helping elders feel less isolated.

Practical strategies for survival, though exhausting, help maintain a sense of purpose and resistance. “I faced challenges by bartering food with neighbors, and for medicine, I walked long distances to medical points. For food, I went to charity kitchens, even when supplies were scarce.” However, for many, survival now comes with humiliation and dependency. One said, “I have become a beggar in the streets; this is how I get scraps of bread, from the streets and from groups, just to provide something, even if it’s leftovers or no longer fit to eat, but this is all I can find.” Forced dependency is experienced as profound humiliation, eroding self-worth and social standing. Another stated, “I wear torn clothes, and as an old woman, I have nothing to wear on my feet as I walk.” Deprivation is not just about discomfort but is internalized as shame and exposure. Even so, many still assert, “We try to be patient, adapt, and get used to it.” Participants explicitly framed this endurance as sumud, with one declaring, "We remain on our land with patience, for this steadfastness is our only victory." This shows that, even in the face of relentless adversity, elders strive to maintain dignity and resilience, transforming endurance itself into an act of psychological defiance.

Discussion

This study provides a vital qualitative lens on the intersecting crises facing elderly Gazans amid the ongoing Israeli genocide, demonstrating how displacement, deprivation, and the erosion of dignity shape every facet of their lived reality. By examining personal testimonies within the framework of recent epidemiological, clinical, and humanitarian research, we gain a clearer, more nuanced understanding of the cumulative and multidimensional impacts experienced by this highly vulnerable population.

A striking theme is the centrality of displacement as a chronic, cyclical trauma, rather than an isolated event. This echoes the findings of Doctors Without Borders (2025); WHO (2024), and Abu Alamrain et al., which argue that repeated forced movement creates ongoing instability, undermines any sense of permanence, and disrupts the essential routines that foster a sense of safety and identity. For the elderly, these disruptions are magnified: homes are not simply physical structures but repositories of memory, continuity, and familial bonds. The literature (HRW, 2024; IDMC Ajyal, 2025; Jamaluddine et al., 2024) suggests that the loss of home in old age entails a deeper existential rupture, contributing to a sense of uprootedness and dislocation that can precipitate acute psychological distress, including anxiety, grief, and confusion. Our findings reinforce that the elderly in Gaza experience displacement as a daily, ongoing ordeal, not a discrete episode, with each wave compounding the losses of the last and intensifying feelings of vulnerability.

Deprivation of basic needs—especially health care, nutrition, water, and sanitation—emerges as a pervasive and deeply damaging feature of the current crisis, in line with Qutishat et al. (2025); Albelbeisi et al. (2025), and the WHO (2024). Participants described immense struggles in accessing medicine for chronic conditions, a finding corroborated by Emina (2025), who notes that blockades and infrastructural destruction transform manageable illnesses into life-threatening emergencies for Gaza’s seniors. This aligns with data from Elsous et al. (2019), who observed a marked deterioration in self-reported quality of life among the elderly, attributable to compromised access to essential health services and support. The anxiety surrounding medication shortages—often resulting in rationing, reliance on ineffective substitutes, or outright medical neglect—mirrors the documented mental health toll (Jamaluddine et al., 2025; HelpAge International, 2023). The persistent fear of illness and mortality in an environment of systemic deprivation amplifies feelings of powerlessness, hopelessness, and abandonment.

The collapse of living conditions in overcrowded shelters—including poor sanitation, lack of privacy, and unrelenting noise—further undermines dignity and self-worth. Recent reports (ReliefWeb, 2023; HRW, 2024; WHO, 2024) emphasize that such environments exacerbate pre-existing vulnerabilities, especially for the elderly, who often value self-reliance and routine. Our study’s findings reflect Ajyal (2025) and Elsous et al. (2019), showing that forced dependence on aid, the inability to maintain personal hygiene, and the humiliation of losing control over bodily needs collectively erode the personal autonomy and social status that older adults have spent a lifetime building. The literature recognizes that such indignities are not merely inconvenient, but constitute a sustained assault on mental health, fostering shame and self-blame that can contribute to depression, withdrawal, and diminished will to live. A recurring thread is the psychological sequelae of persistent fear, insomnia, and unresolved grief, which permeate the everyday existence of Gaza’s elderly. Quantitative and clinical studies (Aldabbour et al., 2025; Hamamra et al., 2025a, 2025b; Jebril et al., 2023) document significantly elevated rates of PTSD, anxiety, and depression in this demographic—findings strongly supported by our qualitative data. The chronicity and layering of trauma are especially noteworthy: as Verdeja (2025) and Doctors Without Borders (2025) have argued, ongoing violence and loss reactivate previous wounds, compounding psychological distress and often leading to a sense of futility and loss of meaning. Notably, even settings designed for protection, such as humanitarian shelters, frequently fail to provide the psychological respite or safety required for recovery, echoing Irfan et al. (2025).

Despite the overwhelming nature of these challenges, many elderly Gazans exhibit remarkable endurance and resourcefulness, drawing on a combination of spiritual practice, communal solidarity, and ingrained cultural values such as sumud (steadfastness). Our data supports the theoretical models proposed by Mahamid et al. (2022), confirming that spiritual connection and social belonging serve as essential, protective factors against total psychological collapse. The ability to maintain small routines, share stories of resistance, and find mutual support—even amidst deprivation—serves as an important buffer against despair. The literature affirms that these psychosocial resources provide a context for understanding suffering and foster hope for eventual relief or justice (Migdad et al., 2024; Qutishat, 2025). However, our data also highlight the limits of individual resilience: as Doctors Without Borders (2025) and HRW (2024) have cautioned, even the most adaptive coping mechanisms are ultimately strained by unremitting violence, deprivation, and humiliation. At the same time, it is important to acknowledge the limits of resilience discourse in this context. Overemphasizing older adults’ ability to endure can unintentionally normalize the structural violence imposed on them and shift attention away from the external political forces driving the genocide. Care is therefore needed to avoid romanticizing suffering or implying that psychological coping can replace physical safety and an end to violence.

What emerges from this synthesis is a picture of intersecting harms, with displacement, deprivation, and loss of dignity not as separate phenomena but as mutually reinforcing elements of a larger systemic assault. The existing literature—spanning humanitarian, clinical, and sociological domains—confirms that Gaza’s elderly were already a population at high risk (Elsous et al., 2019; HelpAge International, 2023), and the ongoing genocide has dramatically worsened their vulnerability on all fronts. The compounding effect of these stressors produces a “perfect storm” of suffering: eroding the foundations of health, autonomy, and selfhood, while undermining even the possibility of a dignified old age.

These findings carry critical implications for humanitarian practice, policy, and future research. In practice, humanitarian actors must move beyond generic aid packages and prioritize age-specific interventions, including the provision of incontinence supplies, assistive mobility devices, and chronic disease medications that are currently absent from standard relief distributions. Shelter management policies also require urgent revision to ensure designated, accessible sanitation facilities for the elderly, both to reduce infection risk and to preserve basic dignity. From a policy perspective, the international community must recognize that the deliberate deprivation of essential services for older adults constitutes a violation of international humanitarian law; accordingly, advocacy efforts should prioritize enforcing the protection of vulnerable demographic groups during active conflict. Finally, in terms of research, future scholarship should explore the long-term cognitive and physical sequelae of this enforced deprivation, with particular attention to how disruption of cultural roles shapes the post-conflict social reintegration of Palestinian elders.

This study’s qualitative approach provides in-depth insights into the psychosocial and material impact of genocide on elderly Gazans, yet some limitations should be noted. The sample, though carefully selected to capture the realities of displacement and deprivation, was limited to forty participants residing in Rafah shelters, and thus may not encompass the full diversity of experiences among Gaza’s elderly, particularly those in different regions or unable to access shelters. While community-based recruitment fostered trust and authenticity, it may have excluded the most isolated or severely ill individuals. The use of face-to-face interviews amid ongoing violence and instability may have shaped the willingness of participants to disclose deeply personal hardships, and privacy constraints could have affected the depth of responses. Despite these challenges, rigorous thematic analysis and the triangulation of perspectives with recent scholarly and humanitarian literature strengthen the credibility of our findings. Nonetheless, future research utilizing longitudinal and mixed-methods designs is needed to further illuminate the evolving needs and resilience of Gaza’s aging population.

Conclusion

This study highlights the intersecting effects of displacement, deprivation, and loss of dignity on the elderly population of Gaza during the ongoing genocide. Rather than reflecting isolated hardships, the findings show that the systemic destruction of residential stability and health infrastructure operates as a cumulative assault on the personhood of older adults. As older people are forced to move from being respected family anchors to dependent, displaced individuals in overcrowded shelters, the traditional social hierarchy is fundamentally disrupted, and their autonomy and established roles are steadily eroded.

The broader social significance of these findings extends beyond immediate physical mortality. As custodians of oral history and intergenerational cohesion, the degradation of Gaza’s elderly threatens the continuity of the Palestinian social fabric itself. Consequently, the study’s findings call for urgent, age-sensitive humanitarian responses to safeguard the health, dignity, and rights of Gaza’s elderly. Literature on humanitarian assistance in conflict settings demonstrates that targeted interventions, such as mobile clinics for non-communicable disease management and age-inclusive sanitation designs, are effective in reducing mortality and isolation (HelpAge International, 2023). Furthermore, psychosocial interventions that facilitate community-based peer support have been shown to bolster resilience among older adults facing displacement (WHO, 2024). Without such targeted support, the ongoing assault on the well-being of this vulnerable population risks erasing not only individual lives but also the cultural memory and intergenerational bonds so essential to Palestinian society.
