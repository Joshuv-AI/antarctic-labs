Improving the experience of male patients receiving radiotherapy to the proximal lower limb (thigh) by using an innovative positioning device, “ting-sling”
Authors: Rosemary Muk Ting, Elliott Hastings, Shane Zaidi, Aisha Miah, Claire Surji, Mandy Humphreys, Helen Taylor, Rita Simoes, Helen McNair
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA positioning device, the “Ting-Sling”, was created to move the external genitalia away from the treatment field and improve the experience of patients with sarcoma cancer. A five-point Likert scale questionnaire was used to evaluate the patient and RTT experience, positioning and modesty. Descriptive feedback was also collected. Ninety-eight percent of the patients and 87% of the RTTs, rated the Ting-Sling, as good or excellent. Patient satisfaction was high in dressing the external genitalia and preserving their modesty when receiving radiotherapy to the proximal lower limb (thigh).Keywords: Sarcoma, Positioning, Radiotherapy, Genital dressing, Patient experience

Introduction

Sarcoma is a rare cancer type, with about 5900 people diagnosed with sarcoma cancer annually in the UK [1]. Approximately 60% of the sarcomas arise in the limb [2]. Radiotherapy (RT), delivered either pre-operatively or post-operatively, improves local control rates to greater than 90% at 2 years [3]. Over the past years, RT techniques have evolved dramatically, allowing for a reduction in radiotherapy-related side-effects whilst keeping the overall tumour control.

Limb immobilisation in RT is challenging and may be subject to uncertainties during treatment delivery [4], [5]. Currently, there is a lack of specific guidance on immobilisation for limb sarcoma, predominantly due to the variety of anatomical locations and tumour sizes. Several studies have demonstrated that there is no consensus on the use of immobilisation devices in RT for patients with limb sarcoma [4], [5].

Additionally, there is no recommendation on how to move the external genitalia away from the treatment field, particularly when treating proximal lower limbs, often described as tumours in the upper thigh or groin areas. Our department experiences echoed these findings. The current protocol for thigh immobilisation is generalised, offering recommendations such as “patient comfort,” “minimum skin fold in the treatment volume”, “minimum collision risks”, “optimal beam arrangement”, and “genital dressing may be required for male patients.”

In clinical practice, the use of bandages, fabric sheets, paper or adhesive tape are strategies commonly employed to displace the genitalia from the treatment field. These measures, implemented by therapeutic radiographers/radiation therapists (RTTs), are often sub-optimal, requiring multiple adjustments that result in a long treatment timeslot. Additionally, this process can be uncomfortable and compromise patient dignity. At our department, we have recently developed a novel device, the “Ting-Sling”, designed to move the genitalia away from the radiation beam in male patients with sarcoma of the thigh.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to assess the feasibility of the Ting-Sling in displacing the genitalia out of the treatment field, and to evaluate patient and staff experience.

Methods

A pouch called the “Ting-Sling” (Fig. 1), named after the system creator, was devised. The Ting-Sling is customisable, with different sizes available to accommodate the variation in the size of the external genitalia. It consists of a soft fabric pouch with straps which is wipeable and washable. The pouch is single use per patient course and compatible with standard patient laundry, without loss of structural integrity or durability. All adult male patients with sarcoma of the thigh treated between January and October 2023 and who required the genitalia to be moved away from the treatment beam, were offered an individual Ting-Sling. Verbal explanation and written information were given during the pre-treatment information session by a pre-treatment RTT. Patients fitted their own Ting-Sling in the private changing cubicles prior to the planning CT scan and for each daily treatment. This pouch was then strapped to patient's abdomen or thigh and could be adjusted to move the genitalia out of the treatment field (Fig. 2a and 2b). In the treatment room, the pouch was then securely attached to a belt with straps, allowing patients to position the external genitalia themselves. The RTTs performed the final check that the genitalia was out of the treatment beam.

Patient setup and immobilisation

All patients were positioned supine. Depending on tumour location, patients were scanned and treated in either head-first or feet-first orientation.

A thermoplastic shell or pelvic immobilisation device was used for patient setup. Immobilisation was selected based on tumour location and the anatomical site involved; for lesions in the pelvis, groin, upper thigh, or extremities, an appropriate pelvic or limb immobilisation device was employed.

Treatment planning

Radiotherapy treatment planning was performed using volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) or three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3D-CRT), in accordance with institutional standards and target volume characteristics.

Dose and fractionation

Patients treated pre-operative RT received 50 Gy in 25 fractions and patients treated with post-operative RT received 60 Gy in 30 fractions or 66 Gy in 33 fractions delivered in two phases. And palliative patients received 36Gy in 12 fractions.

Treatment delivery

All treatments were delivered using image-guided radiotherapy, daily Cone Beam CT images, in accordance with institutional protocols, to ensure accurate target localisation.

Ting-sling assessment questionnaire

Patients and RTTs rated their experience on a five-point Likert scale questionnaire (Appendix A), with 5 representing an excellent experience and 1 a poor experience. The questions evaluated included overall experience, stability, positioning, putting on and removal, comfort, setup, confidence in reproducibility, and modesty.

Adjustments to the Ting-Sling made prior to, or during, the treatment by RTTs on set to check the filed clearance, were recorded. The questionnaires were distributed on the first three days of treatment and weekly thereafter. Descriptive statistics were calculated, and the qualitative feedback was analysed. The percentages of patients and staff responses for each score were calculated.

Results

Ten adult male patients with sarcoma of the thigh area used the Ting-Sling. Of these, seven patients answered the patient experience questionnaire. Three patients declined to complete the questionnaire. Fig. 3 shows the questionnaire results of patients' and RTTs' experiences.

Patient experience results

The median score for all questions was 5 (range: 3–5). Ninety-eight percent of the questions were scored 4 or 5. None of the patients scored the Ting Sling below 3. Patients' overall experience with the Ting-Sling and their modesty being preserved were excellent, both with 100% scoring 4.

In the first three patients treated, readjustment of the Ting Sling was required at the initial time points of the treatment course. For subsequent patients simplified set-up instructions and minor modifications in the shape of the Ting-Sling pouch were implemented (Appendix B). Thereafter, no readjustments were required for the remaining patients.

A total of 18 comments were fed back by four patients. Thirteen of these (72%) were written by three patients and reflected positive feedback of their experience with the Ting-Sling. Two patients suggested that practical demonstration sessions prior to treatment would be useful. The remaining comment recommended instructions on how to wear a Ting-Sling.

The majority (6 out of 7 patients) were able to “self-dress” the external genitalia away from the treatment field.

RTTs experience results

At each treatment fraction, the RTTs treating the patient completed an anonymous questionnaire. As RTT teams could vary between fractions, a different team may have completed the questionnaire at each session. A total of 47 questionnaires were returned, with 289 of 329 items (88%) completed. The 40 missing responses were attribute to either duplicate or non-applicable items in “self-dress” cases.

The overall median score for all questions was 4.5 (range: 2.5–5) (Fig. 3). The highest-scoring question related to patient modesty, with RTTs considering it was excellently preserved (median 5; range 2.5–5). The lowest scores were attributed to positioning (median = 4; range 2.5–5) and to removing the Ting Sling (median = 4; range 3–5).

Treatment verification images (Fig. 4a & 4b) illustrated the displacement of the genitalia achieved using the paper dressing and the Ting-Sling. Pre-treatment staff used paper dressing by default. The body contour, outlined in green on the CT scan with the paper dressing in place, served as a reference, and the blue area illustrated the first slice of the radiation dose. Application of the Ting-Sling effectively moved the genitalia further away from the treatment field, beyond the body contour delineated in green on the CT scan. The results demonstrated positive experiences for both patients and staff, with high scores in preserving modesty.

Discussion

We have shown that the Ting-Sling provides a simple solution to move the genitalia away from the treatment field while preserving patient modesty. The study adopted a patient-led approach to position the external genitalia away from the treatment beam, without reliance on testicular shielding or thermoplastic casts on genital area and included an evaluation of patient experience. The Ting-Sling endeavoured to provide patients with an excellent experience, enhancing their modesty and empowering them to feel in control of their treatment.

One patient with recurrent disease was treated with our previous paper dressing technique; and then the Ting-Sling was introduced for their re-irradiation. This patient, who experienced both techniques, described his previous experience with the paper dressings as “impersonal, inconsistent and the cause of considerable upset, often dreaded with awkward feelings”. When using the Ting-Sling, he felt it empowered him to help position the genitalia, felt more comfortable and that his dignity was preserved.

Studies describing immobilisation devices, genital positioning, or methods for preserving genital modesty during radiotherapy are scarce. Previous research has largely focused on evaluating the efficacy, efficiency, and influencing factors related to the clinical target volume (CTV) and planning target volume (PTV) margins of immobilisation devices for extremity soft tissue or lower limb sarcoma treatments [4], [5], [6], [7], [8].

Dickie et al. [6] investigated over 100 patients with extremity sarcoma immobilised using a customised device adapted from the thermoplastic CIVCO head-and-neck system to secure a limb. Compared with the standard method, which uses a thermoplastic cast fastened to a non-fixed plastic base, the customised device demonstrated improved adaptability and stability. Their findings indicated better treatment reproducibility, enhanced patient comfort, and a skin-sparing dose effect. However, the authors did not report any cases requiring genital dressing or addressing genital positioning.

A retrospective study by Mohamed et al. [7] evaluated the stability and accuracy of two immobilisation systems used for extremity sarcoma radiotherapy: VacLok and thermoplastic cast. Fourteen patients receiving radiotherapy to either the upper or lower limbs were included. Both systems demonstrated high reproducibility, with low translational and rotational setup errors, consistent with RTOG 063 data. While these findings supported the justification of CTV-PTV margins for this cohort, the study did not discuss margins or set-up considerations for treatment sites located near the genital region.

These studies provide important references for extremity sarcoma immobilisation and have contributed to the rationale for using robust immobilisation approaches in clinical practice, particularly the use of thermoplastic shells. However, both studies focused primarily on geometric accuracy and setup stability, and neither specifically addressed the additional challenges associated with limb immobilisation near the genital region. Our study therefore addresses a complementary gap in the literature by evaluating genital positioning in routine clinical practice, particularly from the patient perspective and comfort.

The most recent study, by Tabet et al. (2025), audited the effectiveness of a modified in-house bespoke immobilisation system for extremity sarcoma by retrospectively reviewing verification images. Their protocol incorporated the Orfit extremities immobilisation system together with a genital displacement device (GDD) to preserve genital positioning in male patients receiving lower limb radiotherapy. The performance of the GDD was assessed in 10 male patients (out of 20 total). A traffic light scoring system was used to compare genital position on CBCT images with the planning contours: Green: genitalia within the outlined structure; Amber: genitalia outside the outline but not close to the PTV; and Red: genitalia overlapping the PTV.

Tabet et al. (2025) represents an important step towards the formal assessment of genital positioning reproducibility in lower-limb sarcoma radiotherapy. Their study demonstrated high overall setup reproducibility using a customised immobilisation system incorporating an Orfit extremities immobilisation system and genital displacement device. However, despite acceptable overall treatment setup, genital positioning remained challenging, with only 37% of verification images in male patients scored as green and three red scores requiring re-setup and adjustment. These findings highlight that reproducibility of genital positioning remains a clinically relevant issue, even when robust immobilisation systems are used.

Our study builds on this evidence by focusing specifically on genital management strategies and their acceptability in routine clinical practice. While Tabet et al. primarily assessed imaging-based positional accuracy of the GDD, our work explores alternative genital dressing approaches and incorporates patient feedback on comfort, acceptability and dignity. This adds an important patient-centred dimension to the technical assessment of genital positioning, emphasising that effective immobilisation strategies for lower limb sarcoma radiotherapy must be balanced with patient experience.

This study contributes to the limited evidence by evaluating an innovative genital dressing method and patient's experience. The Ting-sling is feasible and addresses the clinical challenges, such us how patients' dignity, that were unreported in previous work.

Positioning the genitalia is a complex and sensitive subject for patients and RTTs. Both patients and RTTs often feel uncomfortable while displacing the genitalia out of the field. The Ting-Sling provides a patient-led approach, where patients dress their genitalia and RTTs perform a final check or minor adjustments to move the genitalia out of the field, which results in a better experience.

Two patients have provided feedback that an information or practical session would be helpful for understanding how to position the Ting-Sling. Since then, we been providing detailed instructions on how to use the Ting-Sling. As a result, we are currently creating instructions using infographics to improve patients' understanding of how to use the Ting-Sling.

A limitation of this study is the small sample size (n = 10) and the absence of a formal comparison with the previously used genital displacement method. Although there is a general perception within the department that the Ting-Sling is preferred over the former technique, this was not formally evaluated in this study. In addition, dosimetry data were not assessed, and therefore the potential impact of the Ting-Sling on genitalia sparing was not evaluated.

Conclusion

We have demonstrated that the novel genital positioning method, the Ting-Sling, is excellent in terms of comfort and modesty for both patients and RTTs. The Ting-Sling provides a standardised method for genital dressing in the irradiated thigh area. Future work includes developing infographics and instructions to demonstrate how to use the Ting-Sling, as well as conducting a dosimetry assessment.
