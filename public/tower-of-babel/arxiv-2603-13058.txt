Dynamic direct (ranked) access of MSO query evaluation over SLP-compressed strings
Martín Muñoz

Abstract
We present an algorithm that, given an index t t , produces the t t -th (lexicographically ordered) answer of an MSO query over a string. The algorithm requires linear-time preprocessing, and builds a data structure that answers each of these calls in logarithmic time. We then show how to extend this algorithm for a string that is compressed by a straight-line program (SLP), also with linear-time preprocessing in the (compressed encoding of the) string, and maintaining direct access in logtime of the original string. Lastly, we extend the algorithm by allowing complex edits on the SLP after the direct-access data structure has been processsed, which are translated into the data structure in logtime. We do this by adapting a document editing framework introduced by Schmid and Schweikardt (PODS 2022). This work improves on a recent result of dynamic direct access of MSO queries over strings (Bourhis et. al., ICDT 2025) by a log-factor on the access procedure, and by extending the results to SLPs.

Introduction

Evaluating queries built from monadic-second order logic (MSO) formulas is a well-studied task in database theory and algorithmics for formal languages.
A reason for this is that it serves as a pivot to other formalisms.
In particular, owing to the Büchi-Elgot-Trakhtenbrot theorem
[
9
]
, it is known that MSO over words is equivalent to finite state automata and regular expressions. This equivalence also extends to trees, as MSO queries over trees are equivalent to tree regular languages and visibly pushdown automata.
The reach of the task extends further, since the regex formulation means MSO evaluation has use in string processing; the tree formulation means it has use in XML processing; and very relevantly, MSO can be used to handle evaluation for regular spanners
[
14
,
7
,
23
]
.

The base evaluation task is: given a MSO formula
\varphi
, a data string
w
, does
w\models\varphi
hold? (understanding
w
as an ordered monadic structure in the typical sense).
A natural extension of this task is to instead query via a formula with open variables
\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
, and to compute the assignments
\mu
such that
w\models\varphi(\mu(x_{1}),\ldots,\mu(x_{k}))
.

Naturally, by this extension, the set of answers can become much larger than the input instance.
To overcome this, a great deal of works have studied different ways of extracting the answers without producing them all at once.
In MSO evaluation, we find works that explore the task of enumerating these answers one by one
[
3
,
12
,
1
]
; we also find
ranked enumeration
, where the answers have to be produced in order
[
7
]
; and
direct access
, where the set of answers is accessed on an arbitrary index
[
4
,
17
]
.

We identify two different tasks that share a name in the literature. First is
random access
: for the set of answers
S
, the goal is to construct an index between
1
and
|S|
such that each produces a different answer from the set. Second is
ranked access
: the task is the same but there is an order on the answers, and the index follows this order. The naming in the literature shows discrepancies and both tasks have also been named
direct access

[
11
,
8
,
10
,
5
]
.

We highlight the fact that, as noted in
[
11
]
, direct random access can simulate uniform sampling and enumeration; whereas direct ranked access encompasses quantile queries (e.g., the median of the answers), ranked enumeration and top-k answering.

On top of the evaluation task, some works venture into the realm of
incremental evaluation
. In other words, we allow changes in the data after it has been processed for the query.
For MSO queries, this has been abundantly explored in enumeration
[
2
,
19
,
21
,
30
,
24
]
. However, as far as we know, only one work
[
6
]
has studied direct access for MSO on an editable string.

The present work studies an algorithm for efficient direct ranked access on MSO queries. Further, we also consider a more complex setting: the string may have been compressed as a grammar with a singleton language
[
28
]
. Encodings of this form are typically called straight-line programs (SLP)
[
18
]
; they enjoy good compression bounds and have enticing algorithmic properties
[
5
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
20
]
.
Evaluation for MSO queries on SLP-compressed strings (and trees) has been already considered for enumeration
[
29
,
21
,
30
,
24
]
, yet we believe ours is the first work that approaches it from the angle of direct access.

Background

This work is an improvement of a result in
[
6
]
: an algorithm for dynamic direct access for MSO queries on strings. Their result achieves logsquare-access with log-updates on the size of the input string.
In this work, we reduce the logsquare-access to a single log factor, maintaining the log-updates.
Further, we include an extension to SLP-compressed strings which ends up appearing naturally due to the construction of the data structure.
We borrow various concepts and insights from this work.

Paper structure

On Section
2

On Section

On Section

and on Section

We give some closing words on Section

Strings and mappings

Given a finite alphabet
\Sigma
, a
string over
\Sigma
(or just a

) is a sequence
w=a_{1}\ldots a_{n}\in\Sigma^{*}
, where
\Sigma^{*}
denotes the set of finite sequences of elements in
\Sigma
. For such a

, we define its

as
n
, which we write by
|w|
. The string of length zero is denoted by
\varepsilon
, and we write
\Sigma^{+}
for
\Sigma^{*}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}
.
For strings
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
we denote their concatenation by
w_{1}\cdot w_{2}
.
For a string

, for any

we use the notation

, and

Fix an
n
. Given a finite set of variables
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
, a
mapping
of
X
onto
w
is a function
\mu:X\to[1,n]
.
Alternatively, we will define mappings as sets of the form
\{x_{1}\mapsto s_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\mapsto s_{k}\}
, for
s_{1}\ldots,s_{k}\in[1,n]
. A
partial mapping
is any subset of a mapping.
Partial mappings can be also understood as partial functions with domain
X
, of which the total ones are simply mappings.
Fix an order
\prec
over
X
such that
x_{1}\prec\cdots\prec x_{k}
.
We extend this order onto mappings as follows:
Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}:X\to[1,n]
be two mappings, then
\mu_{1}\prec\mu_{2}
iff
\mu_{1}(x_{1})=\mu_{2}(x_{1}),\ldots,\mu_{1}(x_{i-1})=\mu_{2}(x_{i-1})

and
\mu_{1}(x_{i})\prec\mu_{2}(x_{i})
for some
i\in[1,k]
.

Given an
x\in X
and
\alpha\subseteq[1,n]
, we call
x\Mapsto\alpha
a
mapping rule
.
We say that a (partial) mapping
\mu:X\to[1,n]

respects
a set
\tau
of mapping rules if for every rule
x\Mapsto\alpha\in\tau
it holds that
\mu(x)\in\alpha
. When
\alpha
is a singleton set, we may simplify the notation and instead of
x\Mapsto\{s\}
, write it as
x\mapsto s
.
For a fixed
X
,
a mapping rule is
functional
if it mentions every
x\in X
.
Two sets
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
of mapping rules are
equivalent
if every mapping respects
\tau_{1}
iff it respects
\tau_{2}
.
Observe that for every set of mapping rules, there is an equivalent functional one.

Vset Automata

Given a fixed set of variables
X
, a vset automata is a tuple
\mathcal{A}=(\Sigma,Q,\Delta,I,F)
where
\Sigma
is a finite alphabet,
Q
is a set of states
\Delta\subseteq Q\times(\Sigma\times 2^{X})\times Q
is the set of transitions, and
I,F\subseteq Q
are the sets of initial and final states, respectively.
A
run
\rho
of
\mathcal{A}
over
some string
w=a_{1}\ldots a_{n}
is a sequence

\rho=q_{0}\xrightarrow{a_{1}\,,\,S_{1}}q_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{2}\,,\,S_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{a_{n}\,,\,S_{n}}q_{n}

where
q_{0}\in I
and
(q_{i-1},(a_{i},S_{i}),q_{i})\in\Delta
for each
i\in[1,n]
. We say that
\rho
is
valid
if
S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{n}=X
, and for each
i,j\in[1,n]
it holds that
S_{i}\cap S_{j}=\emptyset
. In other words,
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}
forms a partition of
X
.
For a valid
\rho
, we extract a mapping
\mu_{\rho}
given by
\mu_{\rho}(x)=i
where
S_{i}
is the (only) set that contains
x
.
We also say that
\rho
is
accepting
if
q_{n}\in F
. The semantics of vset automata is given by a function

{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w):=\{\mu_{\rho}\mid\text{$\rho$ is a valid and accepting run of $\mathcal{A}$ over $w$}\}.

Given
w=a_{1}\ldots a_{n}
and
l,r\in[1,n]
where
l\leq r
we say that
\rho^{\prime}
is an
(l,r)
-partial run over
w
if there is a run
\rho
over
w
like above, and
\rho^{\prime}=q_{l-1}\xrightarrow{a_{l}\,,\,S_{l}}q_{l}\xrightarrow{a_{l+1}\,,\,S_{l+1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{a_{r}\,,\,S_{r}}q_{r}
. From an
(l,r)
-partial run
\rho^{\prime}
, we extract the partial mapping
\mu_{\rho^{\prime}}:=\{x\mapsto i\mid\rho^{\prime}\text{ has an $S_{i}\ni x$}\}
.

As in
[
6
]
, we deal with two restrictions on vset automata: we say that
\mathcal{A}
is
functional
if every accepting run of
\mathcal{A}
is necessarily valid; and we say that
\mathcal{A}
is
unambiguous
if it holds that for any two runs
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
that satisfy
\mu_{\rho_{1}}=\mu_{\rho_{2}}
, then
\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}
. Note that for every vset automata there is an equivalent functional unambiguous one
[
13
,
22
]
. In what follows, we focus only on vset automata that satisfy both restrictions.

We define

w.r.t. the order

.

Straight-line programs.

A
context-free grammar
is a tuple
G=(N,\Sigma,R,S_{0})
, where
N
is a non-empty set of non-terminals,
\Sigma
is finite alphabet,
S_{0}\in N
is the start symbol and
R\subseteq N\times(N\cup\Sigma)^{+}
is the set of rules.
As a convention, the rule
(A,\beta)\in R
will be written as
A\to\beta
, and we will call
\Sigma
and
N
the set of terminal and non-terminal symbols, respectively.
A context-free grammar
\mathfrak{S}=(N,\Sigma,R,S_{0})
is a
straight-line program
(SLP) if
R
is a total function from
N
to
(N\cup\Sigma)^{+}
and the directed graph
(N,\{(A,B)\mid(A,\beta)\in R\text{ and }B\text{ appears in }\beta\})
is acyclic.
For every
A\in N
, let
R(A)
be the unique
\beta\in(N\cup\Sigma)^{+}
such that
(A,\beta)\in R
, and for every
a\in\Sigma
let
R(a)=a
.
We extend
R
to a morphism
R^{*}:(N\cup\Sigma)^{*}\to\Sigma^{*}
recursively such that
R^{*}(u)=u
when
u
is a string over
\Sigma
, and
R^{*}(\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{n})=R^{*}\big(R(\alpha_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot R(\alpha_{n})\big)
, where
\alpha_{i}\in(N\cup\Sigma)
for every
i\leq n
.
By our definition of SLP,
R^{*}(A)
is in
\Sigma^{+}
, and uniquely defined for each
A\in N
.
Then we define the string encoded by
S
as
{\sf str}(S)=R^{*}(S_{0})
.

We say that a context-free grammar
G=(N,\Sigma,R,S_{0})
is in
Chomsky normal form
if rules in
R
are either of the form
A\to BC
and
A\to a
, for
A,B,C\in N
or
a\in\Sigma
.

We define the size of an SLP
\mathfrak{S}=(N,\Sigma,R,S_{0})
as
|\mathfrak{S}|=\sum_{A\in N}|R(A)|
.

Example.

Let
\mathfrak{S}=(N,\Sigma,R,S_{0})
be an SLP with
N=\{S_{0},A,B,C,D,S_{\sf a},S_{\sf b},S_{\sf c}\}
,
\Sigma=\{{\sf a},{\sf b},{\sf c}\}
, and
R=\{S_{0}\to AB\,,\,A\to DD\,,\,B\to CD\,,\,C\to DS_{\sf c}\,,\,D\to S_{\sf a}S_{\sf b}\,,\,S_{\sf a}\to{\sf a}\,,\,S_{\sf b}\to{\sf b}\,,\,S_{\sf c}\to{\sf c}\}
. We then have that
{\sf str}(D)={\sf ab}
,
{\sf str}(C)={\sf abc}
,
{\sf str}(B)={\sf abcab}
,
{\sf str}(A)={\sf abab}
, and
{\sf str}(\mathfrak{S})={\sf str}(S_{0})={\sf abababcab}
, namely, the string represented by
\mathfrak{S}
.

R
in Figure

(in the context of the main algorithm for SLPs).

Machine model

When dealing with an SLP
\mathfrak{S}
, it could be that the string
w
that it represents has length
2^{|\mathfrak{S}|}
.
In particular, it could happen that merely representing an index in
w
requires a linear number of bits with respect to
|\mathfrak{S}|
.

We handle this by defining a function
\kappa
such that
\kappa(T)
describes the cost of an arithmetical operation between numbers of value, and result, at most
T
.
We will deal with numbers that represent indices in
{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)
, so the largest value we may ever deal with is
T\leq|w|^{|X|}
.
We simply assume that every operation we perform has cost
\kappa(|w|^{|X|})
, and we omit this factor from the presented bounds, noting here that it should be regarded as implicit.

Note that, for a string
w
,
\kappa(|w|)
can be assumed to be constant, and this matches the typical assumptions
of operating in constant-cost registers of size
\log n
. However, this implies that the bounds contain an extra
|X|
factor, which, in fact, was properly accounted for in
[
6
]
.
We choose to keep the factor implicit nonetheless.

3
Direct access for MSO queries on strings

This section will be dedicated to proving the first main result in the paper.

Theorem 1
.

Fix a set of variables
X
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be an unambiguous vset automata and let
w\in\Sigma^{*}
.

1.

For a fixed order
\prec
over
X
, we can have direct (ranked) access on the set
{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)
in
O(|Q|^{\omega}\cdot|X|^{2}\cdot\log(|w|))
time
after
O(|Q|^{\omega}\cdot|X|\cdot|w|)
preprocessing.

2.

If
\prec
is given as input in the access phase, the problem requires
O(|Q|^{\omega}\cdot 2^{|X|}\cdot|w|)

preprocessing time, and the same direct (ranked) access bounds hold.

Structure of the solution.

First, we will describe a generic procedure to retrieve the
t
-th answer of the set
{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)
that assumes we can compute certain data from
\mathcal{A}
and
w
.
After this, we define a collection of matrices that describe the logical information that we need so we can retrieve this data.
Then, we will describe a data structure that stores a succinct version of these matrices.
Lastly, we will describe the actual access phase, which uses a caching strategy to both navigate the search space and
compute the relevant data in the same logtime pass.

Throughout the section, we will focus on proving item (1), which means that the order
\prec
is globally fixed over
X
. We will assume that
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
and that
x_{1}\prec\ldots\prec x_{k}
. At the end of the section, we will describe how to adapt the solution for item (2).

3.1
Template for the access phase

Let
t
be the queried index, and let
\mu^{*}
be the
t
-th mapping in the set
{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)
. The access phase consists in computing the values
\mu^{*}(x_{1}),\ldots,\mu^{*}(x_{k})
in order, one by one.

Algorithm 1

Simplified algorithm to retrieve the
t
-th element in
{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)
.

1:

procedure

(

)

3:

7:

then

8:

(l,r)\leftarrow(l,m)

9:

10:

11:

12:

13:

\{x_{1}\mapsto s_{1}\,,\,\ldots\,,\,x_{k}\mapsto s_{k}\}

The access phase is described in Algorithm
1
.
The base idea is to find
\mu^{*}(x_{1})
with a binary search; then find
\mu^{*}(x_{2})
with a binary search that assumes the value
\mu^{*}(x_{1})
is fixed; then
\mu^{*}(x_{3})
with a binary search that assumes
\mu^{*}(x_{1})
and
\mu^{*}(x_{2})
are fixed; and so on.
The first binary search looks for
\mu^{*}(x_{1})
by characterizing it as a value
s_{1}
that satisfies

\sharp{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)\langle x_{1}\Mapsto[1,s_{1}-1]\rangle<t\leq\sharp{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)\langle x_{1}\Mapsto[1,s_{1}]\rangle.

In the algorithm, we assume that we have access to a function
{\sf mid}:[1,n]\times[1,n]\to[1,n]
that assigns a certain middle point
m:={\sf mid}(l,r)
to every range
(l,r)
; for now, we only assume that this function is fixed at the beginning of the access phase, and that
l\leq m<r
.

The binary search in the algorithm iterates a range
(l,r)
that starts as
(1,n)
and is always guaranteed to contain the target value
s
.
As an invariant, we maintain the inequality
\sharp{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)\langle x_{1}\Mapsto[1,l]\rangle<t\leq\sharp{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)\langle x_{1}\Mapsto[1,r]\rangle
.
Each iteration decides whether to reduce
(l,r)
into its first half
(l,m)
or into its second half
(m+1,r)
, where
m={\sf mid}(l,r)
, by counting how many answers in
{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)
map
x_{1}
to the range
(1,m)
.
When the range reaches size 1 (i.e.,
l=r
), we have only one choice for
s_{1}
so we assign it to
\mu^{*}(x_{1})
.
Then, we subtract from
t
the number of answers that map
x_{1}
to a value lower than
s_{1}
and continue to look for
\mu^{*}(x_{2})
. Subtracting this value from
t
lets us focus on the slice of
{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)
that assumes that
x_{1}
is mapped to
s_{1}
. The rest of
\mu^{*}
is computed the same way.

In our running example, we can see the full list of mappings in
{\llbracket\mathcal{A}^{\sf ex}\rrbracket}(w_{0})
in Figure
1
(right). If the access index was
t=5
, then we would find
s_{1}=3
since
\sharp{\llbracket\mathcal{A}^{\sf ex}\rrbracket}(w_{0})\langle x_{1}\Mapsto[1,3-1]\rangle=3<5
and
\sharp{\llbracket\mathcal{A}^{\sf ex}\rrbracket}(w_{0})\langle x_{1}\Mapsto[1,3]\rangle=5\leq 5
.
Then, before the next iteration, we need to subtract the value
\sharp{\llbracket\mathcal{A}^{\sf ex}\rrbracket}(w_{0})\langle x_{1}\Mapsto[1,3-1]\rangle=3
from
t
, and do the binary search with
t=2
. This is because the search space was reduced to only the mappings that start with
x_{1}\mapsto 3
. In the end, we find
s_{2}=6
given that
\sharp{\llbracket\mathcal{A}^{\sf ex}\rrbracket}(w_{0})\langle x_{1}\mapsto 3\,,\,x_{2}\Mapsto[1,6-1]\rangle=1<2
and
\sharp{\llbracket\mathcal{A}^{\sf ex}\rrbracket}(w_{0})\langle x_{1}\mapsto 3\,,\,x_{2}\Mapsto[1,6]\rangle=2\leq 2
and so
\mu^{*}=\{x_{1}\mapsto 3,x_{2}\mapsto 6\}
,

Lemma 2
.

Algorithm

finds the
t
-th answer in
{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)
.

The generic algorithm gives us the following insight: the relevant slices of
{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)
are those defined by a fixed assignment for
x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1}
, and a rule of mapping of
x_{i}
somewhere inside the range
[1,m]
for some
m
. This will be crucial for our algorithm.

The running time of the procedure depends on the choice of
mid
and the time needed to compute the sizes of these relevant slices. We leave this analysis for later.

Links to the literature.

This idea of fixing a prefix of the variables to values, and then computing the size of the corresponding slice of
{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)
tends to be a core subroutine for direct access

.
We think that, conceptually, the most important refinement of our solution is to include the rule “map the next variable in the prefix to a range” to the core subroutine.

3.2
The
\mathcal{M}
matrices

As a way to bridge the gap between the relevant slices of
{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)
and the data structure that we will present, we introduce a useful collection of matrices in
\mathbb{N}^{Q\times Q}
.

First, we add some new features to mapping rules and their sets. We say that a mapping rule
x\Mapsto\alpha
is
inside

[l,r]
if
\alpha\subseteq[l,r]
. Also, we will compose functional sets
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
of mapping rules and define the operation
\tau_{1}\cdot\tau_{2}:=\{x\Mapsto\alpha\cup\beta\mid x\Mapsto\alpha\in\tau_{1}\text{ and }x\Mapsto\beta\in\tau_{2}\}
. Observe that if
\tau_{1}
is a set of mapping rules inside
[l,m]
and
\tau_{2}
is a set of mapping rules inside
[m+1,r]
, then
\tau_{1}\cdot\tau_{2}
is a set of mapping rules inside
[l,r]
.

For
1\leq l\leq r\leq n
, and a set
\tau
of mapping rules inside
[l,r]
, we define this matrix in
\mathbb{N}^{Q\times Q}
:

\mathcal{M}\langle l,r:\tau\rangle(p,q):=|\{\rho\mid\rho\text{ is an $(l,r)$-partial run of $\mathcal{A}$ over $w$ from $p$ to $q$ \,s.t.\! $\mu_{\rho}$ respects $\tau$}\}|

The principle behind these matrices is to group the partial mappings that make up the mappings in
{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)
by three criteria: the range inside
[1,n]
that the partial mapping was obtained from, the positions in
[1,n]
where the variables in
X
can appear, and the states that delimit their respective partial runs.

In our running example, we highlighted runs
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho_{3}
which render the first three mappings in
{\llbracket\mathcal{A}^{\sf ex}\rrbracket}(w_{0})
. Let us look at one
\mathcal{M}
matrix in particular: let
\tau_{x_{1}=1}:=\{x_{1}\mapsto 1\}
, and let
(l,r)=(1,5)
. Then we find that
\mathcal{M}\langle 1,5:\tau_{x_{1}=1}\rangle(q_{0},q_{1})=1
, since there is one
(1,5)
-partial run over
w_{0}
from
q_{0}
to
q_{1}
(it can be obtained from
\rho_{3}
); also, see that
\mathcal{M}\langle 1,5:\tau_{1}\rangle(q_{0},q_{2})=2
since this counts the
(1,5)
-partial runs obtained from
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
.

The matrices let us summarize a lot of the counting logic in one composition:

Lemma 3
.

Let
1\leq l\leq m<r\leq n
, let
\tau_{L}
be a functional set of mapping rules inside
[l,m]
and let
\tau_{R}
be a functional set of mapping rules inside
[m+1,r]
. Then:

\mathcal{M}\langle l,r:\tau_{L}\cdot\tau_{R}\rangle=\mathcal{M}\langle l,m:\tau_{L}\rangle\cdot\mathcal{M}\langle m+1,r:\tau_{R}\rangle

Lastly, we observe that the equality

\sharp{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}(w)\langle\tau\rangle=\sum_{p\in I,q\in F}\mathcal{M}\langle 1,n:\tau\rangle(p,q)

holds for any
\tau
given that
\mathcal{A}
is functional and unambiguous.

Logic of the stored matrices

We can interpret the logic of the data structure by an auxiliary set of mapping rules: for an

Intuitively, this set is a simple filter that disallows any partial mapping that mentions any variable

For a simpler notation, instead of

Complexity analysis

We briefly observe that every tree has a total of

\mathbb{N}^{Q\times Q}
which is computed by a single matrix multiplication. As we store a total of
k+1

O(|Q|^{\omega}\cdot|X|\cdot|w|)
.

3.4

Before moving on to describe the access phase in our algorithm, we introduce an auxiliary subroutine, described in Algorithm

.

A string database over

\mathbb{D}
for
D_{\mathfrak{S},\mathfrak{r}}
and
\mathcal{A}
that allows
O(|\psi|\cdot|Q|^{\omega}\cdot|X|\cdot\log|\max_{\psi}(D)|)
editing and
O(|Q|^{\omega}\cdot|X|^{2}\cdot|\max_{\psi}(D)|)
direct access.

5.1
Edits on strings

We framed our results on edits solely for SLPs given that they can be trivially adapted for words. However, we claim that the editing procedure on the string case can be simpler, and, in particular, be done in a way that keeps the number of nodes in the data structure unchanged.
The idea would be to use classic results for editing AVL trees, as it is done in
[
6
]
.
We defer further details to a future full version of this paper.

6
Conclusions and future work

We have presented a dynamic direct (ranked) access algorithm for MSO queries on strings and SLP-compressed strings. We improve on
[
6
]
by shaving a log-factor in the access procedure, and showing an extension to SLPs.
We note that the PhD theses of Bagan
[
4
]
and Kazana
[
17
]
both already had a version of direct access on MSO queries (even for trees), so the main contribution of our work is in adding the dynamic aspect, and the extension to SLPs.

Something of a blind spot of our work with respect to Bourhis et. al.’s
[
6
]
is that we do not say anything about query evaluation for semigroups—we actually did not see how our result gives further insights here. It would be interesting to have a characterization of which semigroups support log-time direct access and which do not by some lower bound.

For future work, we think that the main question is whether our techniques presented here can be extended to dynamic direct access for MSO queries on trees. We believe this to be possible, for example, via
[
19
]
. It would also prove technically challenging since this framework does not preserve contiguity, which is crucial in our solution.

Also, there is a clear shortcoming in the fact that the SLP needs to be
strongly balanced
to admit logtime updates.
In particular, this means that it does not work for an arbitrary SLP. We leave open the question of whether some weaker balancedness criterion for SLPs could achieve this.
