Hepatic CREB Binding Protein/E1A Binding Protein p300 Maintain Bile Acid Homeostasis Through Histone Acetylation-Mediated Kruppel-Like Transcription Factor 10–Bile Salt Export Pump Axis
Authors: Mingzhu Li, Yiru Wang, Libin Zhou, Chunxiang Sheng, Ruyuan Deng, Li Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsDisruption of bile acid metabolism contributes to various liver diseases. Although CREB binding protein/E1A binding protein p300 (CBP/p300) are known regulators of hepatic metabolism, their role in bile acid homeostasis remain elusive.MethodsMetabolomic and transcriptomic analyses on liver-specific CBP/p300 knockout mice were performed to assess bile acid metabolism. Cleavage Under Targets & Tagmentation (CUT&Tag) sequencing and chromatin immunoprecipitation–quantitative polymerase chain reaction were used to investigate the epigenetic mechanisms underlying CBP/p300–mediated bile acid regulation. A mouse model of cholestatic liver disease was established to validate the findings from the liver-specific CBP/p300 double-knockout mice by administrating a 0.1% 3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidine (DDC) diet.ResultsLiver-specific CBP/p300 deletion caused intrahepatic cholestasis due to impaired bile acid efflux, along with elevated plasma bile acid levels. Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses revealed decreased expression of bile acid synthesis and transport genes, including Abcb11 (encoding BSEP, the bile salt export pump). Mechanistically, CBP/p300 promoted Abcb11 transcription via H3K27 acetylation. In addition, CBP/p300 epigenetically regulated the transcription factor Kruppel-like transcription factor 10 (KLF10), which directly activated Abcb11 expression. Loss of CBP/p300 reduced H3K27Ac enrichment at both Klf10 and Abcb11 promoters, leading to their downregulation. Overexpression of Klf10 in CBP/p300–deficient hepatocytes partially rescued bile acid transport defects. Impaired Kruppel-like transcription factor 10-bile salt export pump (KLF10-BSEP) regulatory axis was also observed in a DDC-induced model of hepatic cholestasis.ConclusionsOur findings uncover a novel CBP/p300-KLF10-BSEP regulatory axis that orchestrates bile acid transport and highlight its therapeutic potential for cholestatic liver diseases.

SummaryOur study demonstrated that CBP/p300 were essential for hepatic bile acid homeostasis. Liver-specific CBP/p300 knockout mice developed severe intrahepatic cholestasis due to impaired bile acid efflux. Mechanistically, CBP/p300 orchestrated bile acid transport through a dual regulatory mechanism: directly promoting Abcb11 (BSEP) transcription via H3K27 acetylation and epigenetically activating the transcription factor KLF10. This CBP/p300–KLF10–BSEP regulatory axis represents a previously uncharacterized epigenetic pathway governing bile acid homeostasis with therapeutic potential for cholestatic diseases. What You Need to Know

Background

Bile acids are essential for lipid digestion and cholesterol homeostasis, but their dysregulation can lead to liver injury. Although CBP/p300 are known metabolic regulators, their role in bile acid homeostasis remains unclear.

Impact

Liver-specific deletion of CBP/p300 in mice causes intrahepatic cholestasis via suppressing BSEP through reduced H3K27 acetylation. CBP/p300 epigenetically activates the transcription factor KLF10, which directly drives Abcb11 expression, uncovering a novel regulatory axis controlling bile acid transport. Validation experiments indicate that the impaired KLF10–BSEP axis is also detected in a DDC–induced model of hepatic cholestasis, suggesting broader pathophysiological relevance.

Future Directions

The CBP/p300–KLF10–BSEP axis offers a potential therapeutic target for cholestatic liver diseases, which needs further investigation in patients.

Bile acids (BAs) are amphipathic molecules synthesized from cholesterol in the liver and play essential roles in emulsifying dietary fats, promoting lipid absorption, and maintaining cholesterol homeostasis.1,2 Their synthesis occurs mainly in hepatocytes through classical and alternative pathways.3,4 Once synthesized, bile acids are conjugated and secreted into bile ducts through bile salt transporters, eventually entering the intestine to facilitate lipid digestion.2 Disrupted BA synthesis, secretion, or transport leads to accumulation of potentially toxic BAs in hepatocytes, a condition known as cholestasis, which is a hallmark of many liver diseases. This accumulation can trigger oxidative stress, inflammation, and progressive liver injury.5,6

Obstruction of bile flow is the primary cause of cholestasis, leading to elevated BA levels in both the liver and serum, which can cause hepatocellular injury.7 The bile salt export pump (BSEP), encoded by ATP binding cassette subfamily B member 11 (Abcb11), is predominantly expressed in hepatocytes and functions as the main canalicular transporter for bile salts, particularly conjugated primary BAs.8 The substrates with the highest affinity for BSEP are taurocholic acid in humans and taurochenodeoxycholic acid (TCDCA) in mice.9,10 BSEP-mediated bile salt efflux represents the rate-limiting step in bile salt secretion and is the primary driving force for bile flow.11 Impairment of BSEP, whether due to structural alterations or functional inhibition, leads to intrahepatic bile accumulation. Thus, proper BSEP function is essential for maintaining BA homeostasis and preventing hepatocellular toxicity.11

Acetylation is a highly reversible post-translational modification regulated by histone acetyltransferases and histone deacetylases, and it plays a critical role in orchestrating metabolic homeostasis through modulation of gene expression.12,13 Garrido et al 14 demonstrated that the deficiency of microspherule protein 1, a regulator of histone acetylation, increased histone lysine acetylation of BA transport gene loci, leading to BA accumulation within hepatic sinusoids. These findings underscore the role of histone acetylation in regulating BA metabolism. CREB binding protein (CBP) and E1A binding protein p300 (p300) are acetyltransferases with high sequence homology found in humans and most higher eukaryotes.12 Recent experimental evidence suggests that CBP/p300 are key regulators of hepatic metabolic processes, including lipogenesis and gluconeogenesis.15 Moreover, emerging studies have implicated CBP/p300 in cholestatic liver disease. For instance, CBP has been shown to regulate BA synthesis through the β-catenin/Egr-1 signaling axis. Hepatocyte-specific CBP knockout in mice suppresses BA synthesis and attenuates cholestatic liver injury.16 However, CBP and p300 are highly redundant acetyltransferases.12 Consequently, single-gene knockout studies may be confounded by functional compensation, thereby failing to fully reveal their regulatory roles in BA homeostasis. Particularly, the specific role of CBP/p300 in regulating BA transport and related gene expressions remains largely unknown.

In this study, the liver-specific knockout of CBP/p300 in mice leads to severe intrahepatic cholestasis and disrupted hepatic BA homeostasis. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) reveals broad downregulation of genes involved in both primary BA synthesis and BA secretion pathways in CBP/p300-deleted mice. Further analyses using CUT&Tag and chromatin immunoprecipitation–quantitative polymerase chain reaction (ChIP-qPCR) provide mechanistic insights into how CBP/p300 safeguards BA homeostasis, particularly by regulating the expression of genes critical for BA transport.

Results

Liver-Specific Double Knockout of CBP/p300 Induces Severe Hepatic Cholestasis and Hepatocellular Injury

To explore the role of CBP/p300 in BA homeostasis, we generated liver-specific CBP and p300 knockout mouse models, including CBPLivKO, p300LivKO, and double knockout CBP/p300LivDKO mice (Figure 1A and B). Compared with wild-type (WT) controls, CBP/p300LivDKO mice exhibited markedly elevated plasma levels of total BA (TBA), total bilirubin (TBil) and direct bilirubin (DBil) (Figure 1C and D). Additionally, liver injury markers, such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and γ-glutamyltransferase (γ-GT), were significantly increased in CBP/p300LivDKO mice, indicating substantial hepatocellular damage (Figure 1E and F). However, no significant changes in these biochemical parameters were observed in CBPLivKO and p300LivKO mice (Figure 1C–F). These findings suggest that CBP and p300 exert a redundant effect on BA metabolism. Liver-Specific Deletion of CBP/p300 Disrupts Bile Acid Profiles in Plasma and Liver

To further assess the effects of hepatic CBP/p300 deletion on BA metabolism, we performed Q300-targeted metabolomic profiling of plasma and liver samples from WT and CBP/p300LivDKO mice (Figure 3A). A total of 189 and 213 metabolites were identified in plasma and liver, respectively. Principal component analysis showed distinct clustering between CBP/p300LivDKO and WT mice in both plasma and liver, indicating widespread metabolic alterations (Figure 3B and C). In plasma, 62 metabolites were significantly upregulated and 5 were downregulated in CBP/p300LivDKO mice compared with WT mice (Figure 3D). Similarly, hepatic metabolomics analysis identified 44 upregulated and 21 downregulated metabolites in knockout mice (Figure 3G). Consistent with the plasma findings, liver BA composition was significantly altered in knockout mice, showing enrichment in conjugated primary BAs (Figure 3H and I). Hepatic CBP/p300 Deletion Impairs Bile Acid Synthesis and Efflux

To investigate the mechanisms underlying the BA dysregulation observed in CBP/p300LivDKO mice, we performed hepatic transcriptomic analysis using RNA-seq. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of downregulated genes revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to primary BA biosynthesis and bile secretion (Figure 4A and B). To further dissect the functional consequences of CBP/p300 deletion, we integrated transcriptomic and metabolomics datasets, focusing on hepatic BA metabolism (Figure 4D). In the liver, primary BAs are synthesized through the classical and alternative pathways, with the former being the predominant route under physiological conditions.2 In the liver of CBP/p300LivDKO mice, expressions of key enzymes in both pathways, including cytochrome P450 family 7 subfamily A member 1 (Cyp7a1), cytochrome P450 family 27 subfamily A member 1 (Cyp27a1), and cytochrome P450 family 8 subfamily B member 1 (Cyp8b1), were markedly suppressed, as revealed by RNA-seq. Correspondingly, metabolomics analysis showed significant reductions in hepatic cholic acid (CA) and its derived metabolites taurocholic acid/glycocholic acid (Figure 4D). Unexpectedly, levels of chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) and its derivatives, including TCDCA, glycodeoxycholic acid, tauro-α/β-muricholic acid, and tauroursodeoxycholic acid were increased in the liver of CBP/p300LivDKO mice, despite overall suppression of the biosynthetic pathways (Figure 4D). Of note, marked repression of Cyp8b1, which governs the CA/CDCA ratio in the classical pathway,17 redirects biosynthetic flux toward CDCA and its derivatives. Nevertheless, it is reasonable to suppose that the increased BAs are attributed to impaired transport and secretion rather than enhanced synthesis.

Transcriptomic analysis revealed significant downregulation of genes involved in BA uptake and efflux. Notably, solute carrier family 10 member 1 (Slc10a1; encoding sodium taurocholate co-transporting polypeptide [NTCP]) and solute carrier organic anion transporter family member 1a1 (Slco1a1; encoding organic anion transporting polypeptide 1A1 [OATP1A1]), key transporters responsible for hepatic reabsorption of BAs, were significantly suppressed in knockout mice (Figure 4D). In addition, the expression of Abcb11, encoding BSEP, was also decreased (Figure 4D), potentially leading to the intrahepatic accumulation of primary BAs due to impaired canalicular efflux.

To validate these findings, we measured messenger RNA (mRNA) and protein levels of key regulators of BA synthesis and transport in both in vivo and in vitro models. In the livers of CBP/p300LivDKO mice, the expression of key BA synthesis genes, namely Cyp7a1, Cyp27a1, and Cyp8b1, was significantly reduced at both the transcriptional and protein levels (Figure 5A–C). Significant decreases were also observed in the expression of major genes related to hepatocellular BA uptake (Slc10a1, Slco1a1) and efflux (Abcb11). This was the case for the protein expression of BSEP, which is encoded by Abcb11(Figure 5A–C). To investigate alterations in BA transport in CBP/p300LivDKO mice, we analyzed the hepatic expression of key basolateral efflux transporters. Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis revealed that the expression of ATP binding cassette subfamily C member 3 (Abcc3; encoding multidrug resistance-associated protein 3 [MRP3]) was significantly decreased, whereas the expressions of ATP binding cassette subfamily C member 4 (Abcc4; encoding multidrug resistance-associated protein 4 [MRP4]) and solute carrier family 51 subunit beta (Slc51b; encoding encoding organic solute transporter beta [OSTβ]) were upregulated. Solute carrier family 51 subunit alpha (Slc51a; encoding organic solute transporter alpha [OSTα]) expression remained unchanged (Figure 5A). Consistent with the cholestatic phenotype, primary hepatocytes isolated from CBP/p300LivDKO mice exhibited a significant decrease of the TBA level and an increase of the TCDCA level (Figure 5D and E). AAV-Cre mice displayed similar patterns (Figure 5F–J). To confirm these results in vitro, we generated a CBP/p300 double-knockout model in primary hepatocytes using adenoviral delivery of Cre recombinase (Figure 5K and L). As expected, the in vitro results were largely concordant with the in vivo results (Figure 5M–O). Moreover, the concentration of TBA in primary hepatocytes treated with Ad-Cre was significantly lower than that in the Ad-green fluorescent protein (GFP) control group, whereas the concentration of TCDCA was higher (Figure 5P and Q). In WT samples, H3K27Ac signals were predominantly enriched at promoter regions of BA-associated genes (BAGs), with 45.37% of peaks located within 1 kb upstream of the transcription start site. Intronic and intergenic regions each accounted for 19.86% of the peaks, whereas exonic and the 3′UTR regions contributed 3.39% and 1.35%, respectively (Figure 6B). These findings highlight a strong preference for promoter-targeted H3K27Ac enrichment, indicative of active transcriptional regulation of BAGs in WT livers. Correlation analysis between H3K27Ac signals (CUT&Tag) and mRNA expression levels (RNA-seq) revealed a significant positive correlation for BAGs (r = 0.5912; P < .0001) (Figure 6C), indicating that transcriptional downregulation of these genes in CBP/p300LivDKO mice is at least partially attributable to reduced histone acetylation. Integrated analysis of CUT&Tag and RNA-seq data identified 50 BAGs with concordant reductions in both H3K27Ac enrichment and transcript expression. Functional enrichment analysis of these genes revealed significant associations with BA metabolism, including bile secretion and primary BA biosynthesis (Figure 6D), underscoring the critical role of CBP/p300 in maintaining epigenetic control over these pathways. Notably, H3K27Ac enrichment was significantly reduced at the gene loci of Cyp7a1, Cyp8b1, Abcb11, and Nr1h4, key regulators of BA synthesis and secretion, as visualized using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) (Figure 6E and F). This reduction was especially prominent in promoter-proximal regions, suggesting direct transcriptional repression upon CBP/p300 loss.

Given that both BA synthesis and secretion pathways were both suppressed in CBP/p300LivDKO mice with cholestatic features, it is likely that impaired BA efflux is a key driver of the phenotype. Because BSEP, encoded by Abcb11, is the primary transporter for BA export from hepatocytes, we further examined its regulation. ChIP-qPCR analysis confirmed a marked reduction in H3K27Ac at the Abcb11 promoter in CBP/p300LivDKO livers (Figure 6G), suggesting that CBP/p300 directly regulate Abcb11 transcription via H3K27 acetylation.

Together, these findings establish CBP/p300 as critical epigenetic regulators of BA metabolism genes, particularly through modulation of H3K27 acetylation at loci involved in BA synthesis and efflux.

To investigate how CBP/p300 regulate Klf10, we analyzed CUT&Tag data using IGV. CBP/p300 deficiency resulted in a notable reduction in H3K27Ac occupancy at the Klf10 gene loci, particularly in the proximal promoter region (Figure 8H), consistent with decreased Klf10 gene expression. To determine whether KLF10 could restore Abcb11 level in the absence of CBP/p300, we performed rescue experiments in primary hepatocytes from CBP/p300LivDKO mice. Overexpression of Klf10 partially restored Abcb11 expression at both mRNA and protein levels in CBP/p300-deleted hepatocytes (Figure 8K and L), along with decreased intracellular TCDCA level and increased TCDCA level in the culture supernatant (Figure 8M). Similar rescue effects were observed in primary hepatocytes from AAV-Cre model mice (Figure 8N–P). These results suggest that restoring Klf10 expression can partially reverse BA retention and alleviate cholestatic injury resulting from CBP/p300 loss.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the role of the histone acetylases CBP/p300 in hepatic BA metabolism, with particular emphasis on BA secretion and its regulatory mechanisms. Our major findings are as follows: (1) liver-specific deletion of CBP/p300 resulted in significant elevations in both plasma and hepatic BA levels and induced severe intrahepatic cholestasis; (2) CBP/p300 deficiency led to the global downregulation of genes involved in BA synthesis and transport, thus disrupting BA homeostasis; and (3) CBP/p300 directly regulate BSEP (Abcb11) expression by modulating H3K27 acetylation, and indirectly control it via transcriptional regulation of the upstream factor KLF10. This dual mechanism contributes to impaired BA clearance and intrahepatic accumulation in the absence of CBP/p300.

Cholestatic liver disease is characterized by impaired bile flow, leading to hepatic retention of BAs, hepatocellular damage, inflammation, and progressive fibrosis. Without effective intervention, cholestasis may progress to biliary cirrhosis and end-stage liver failure, representing a major global health burden.20 Currently, liver transplantation remains the only definitive treatment for advanced cholestatic liver diseases.21 Disruption of BA homeostasis—either due to impaired biosynthesis, secretion, or enterohepatic circulation—can cause BA accumulation in the liver and circulation.7 BAs are not only metabolic byproducts but also act as signaling molecules that regulate metabolism and energy balance. Failure to adapt to elevated BA levels can lead to hepatocellular injury and promote progression to fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma.22 Therefore, therapeutic strategies aimed at reducing intrahepatic and systemic BA accumulation are crucial for managing cholestasis. Growing evidence suggests the importance of targeting BA metabolism and its signaling pathways as promising avenues for treating cholestatic liver diseases.22,23

CBP/p300 are key transcriptional coactivators with intrinsic histone acetyltransferase activity that regulate gene expression by modifying chromatin structure and interacting with various transcription factors.15 Previous studies have primarily focused on their roles in hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism,24, 25, 26, 27, 28 but their function in BA regulation remains poorly understood. Our research fills this gap by demonstrating that hepatic CBP/p300 are essential for maintaining BA metabolic balance. In CBP/p300LivDKO mice, plasma TBAs, bilirubin, γ-GT, and ALP levels were significantly elevated (Figures 1 and 2), indicating cholestatic liver injury. These results strongly suggest that CBP/p300 are indispensable for both BA synthesis and efflux, and their loss leads to intrahepatic BA accumulation and cholestasis.

Liver-targeted metabolomics profiling revealed a significant decrease in CA and its conjugates, and a concurrent increase in CDCA and its derivatives in CBP/p300LivDKO mice (Figure 3). Because CYP8B1 determines the CA/CDCA ratio by directing the classical pathway, its downregulation is expected to favor CDCA synthesis.17 Indeed, CYP8B1 expression was substantially reduced at both mRNA and protein levels, suggesting a shift in BA composition. However, this alone does not explain the observed increase in total BA levels, as RNA-seq data revealed widespread suppression of genes involved in both classical and alternative BA synthesis pathways (Figures 4 and 5). These findings point to a general suppression of BA biosynthesis, which was further supported by significantly lower TBA levels in primary hepatocytes from CBP/p300 double knockout models. Interestingly, despite reduced synthesis, CDCA and its derivatives remained elevated (Figure 5), suggesting impaired BA transport as the underlying cause of BA retention.

BA homeostasis is tightly regulated by the enterohepatic circulation. Hepatic BAs derive from two sources: primary BAs synthesized de novo by hepatocytes and those recovered from the intestine via enterohepatic circulation.29,30 BSEP and multidrug resistance-associated protein 2 (MRP2) are transport proteins on the apical membrane of hepatocytes that actively efflux intracellular BAs into the bile canaliculus. BSEP functions as the principal canalicular transporter for hepatocellular BA excretion and is the key rate-limiting canalicular transporter responsible for bile salt efflux into bile ducts,31 whereas MRP2 serves an auxiliary role.32,33 In CBP/p300LivDKO mice, BSEP was significantly downregulated, whereas the expression of ATP binding cassette subfamily C member 2 (Abcc2; encoding MRP2) remained unchanged. Substantial reductions in BSEP function typically elicit hepatic compensatory responses to alleviate intrahepatic BA accumulation and hepatocellular injury. These responses include the upregulation of basolateral efflux pumps (eg, MRP3, MRP4, OSTα, OSTβ) to export BAs into the systemic circulation, and the downregulation of sinusoidal uptake transporters (eg, NTCP, OATP1A1) to reduce hepatic uptake.34 Consistent with these adaptive responses, CBP/p300LivDKO mice showed decreased expression of Slc10a1 (encoding NTCP) and Slco1a1 (encoding OATP1A1), and increased expression of ATP binding cassette subfamily C member 4 (encoding MRP4) and Slc51b (encoding OSTβ), indicating broad impairment of BA trafficking. However, these compensatory changes were insufficient to offset the BA burden caused by BSEP downregulation. Therefore, these findings suggest that both impaired reabsorption and defective secretion contribute to BA accumulation in CBP/p300LivDKO mice, in which BSEP may play a dominant role.

BSEP is exclusively expressed on the canalicular membrane of hepatocytes for active BA transport.35, 36, 37 Loss-of-function mutations in ABCB11 cause rare but severe cholestatic disorders, including progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis type 2, benign recurrent intrahepatic cholestasis, and intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy.38, 39, 40 FXR is a well-established transcription factor that regulates Abcb11.41, 42, 43 Fang et al 44 demonstrated that p300 served as a critical coactivator of FXR by acetylating histones at target gene promoters. Our findings revealed that CBP/p300 regulated FXR expression via H3K27Ac modification (Figures 6 and 7). To date, few studies have addressed whether BSEP expression is directly controlled by histone acetylation. Despite the restoration of FXR expression via Nr1h4 overexpression in primary hepatocytes from CBP/p300LivDKO mice, levels of Abcb11 mRNA and protein were not rescued (Figure 7). This finding suggests that CBP/p300 mediate BSEP transcription through a mechanism independent of FXR. Our study provides the first evidence that CBP/p300 directly modulate Abcb11 transcription through H3K27 acetylation (Figure 6), establishing an epigenetic mechanism underlying BA transport. Moreover, we identified KLF10 as a novel upstream transcription activator of Abcb11. Dual-luciferase reporter assays and ChIP-qPCR confirmed that KLF10 directly bound to the Abcb11 promoter and promoted its transcription (Figure 8). In CBP/p300LivDKO mice, both mRNA and protein levels of KLF10 were significantly reduced, and H3K27Ac level was decreased at the Klf10 locus. These results suggest that CBP/p300 indirectly regulate Abcb11 expression by modulating KLF10 through epigenetic mechanisms.

KLF10 is a zinc-finger transcription factor belonging to the SP/KLF family and has been implicated in cell proliferation, apoptosis, and metabolic regulation.45, 46, 47, 48, 49, 50 It acts as a downstream effector of the transforming growth factor β/SMAD signaling pathway, involving in the pathogenesis of metabolic disorders such as nonalcoholic steatohepatitis50, 51, 52 and certain cancers.45,53 Previous studies have shown that KLF10 regulates hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism, as well as mitochondrial function in skeletal muscle.54 Our results demonstrate that CBP/p300 promote KLF10 expression through H3K27 acetylation, which in turn modulates Abcb11 expression. Importantly, overexpression of KLF10 in vitro partially restored Abcb11 expression and improved BA efflux in CBP/p300 knockout models (Figure 8), further validating the CBP/p300-KLF10-BSEP regulatory axis. Furthermore, the transcriptional regulation of BSEP by KLF10 is observed in the DDC model (Figure 9). Therefore, the KLF10-BSEP axis may constitute a broadly relevant therapeutic target in cholestatic liver diseases beyond the specific context of CBP/p300 deficiency.

In conclusion, our study highlights a previously unrecognized role of CBP/p300 in maintaining BA metabolic homeostasis by a dual regulatory mechanism (Figure 10). On one hand, CBP/p300 govern the transcription of key BA synthetic enzymes and transporters via H3K27 acetylation. On the other hand, CBP/p300 regulate BA efflux by modulating KLF10-dependent expression of BSEP. Generation of gene knockout mouse model

Alb-Cre mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. Ep300flox/flox and Crebbpflox/flox mice were purchased from GemPharmatech. Ltd. Liver-specific CBP knockout (CBPLivKO) mice and liver-specific p300 knockout (p300LivKO) mice were generated by crossing of Crebbpflox/flox and Ep300flox/flox mice with Alb-Cre mice, respectively. Liver-specific CBP/p300 double-knockout (CBP/p300LivDKO) mice were generated by crossing Crebbpflox/flox/Ep300flox/flox mice with Alb-Cre mice. Cre-negative littermates from each breeding setup were used as WT controls, and all mice were kept on a C57BL/6J background.

3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidine–induced cholestasis mouse model

Male C57BL/6J mice (aged 8 weeks, weighting 20–25 g) were purchased from Jiangsu Huachuang Sino Pharma Tech Co, Ltd (permit number: SCXK-SU 2020-0009). Mice were randomly divided into NCD and DDC-treated groups. Mice in the DDC group were fed a diet supplemented with AIN-93G Purified Rodent Diet with 0.1% DDC (Dyets, D200309) for 4 weeks to induce cholestatic liver injury. The NCD group received AIN-93G Purified Rodent Diet (Dyets, AIN93G). At the end of the experimental period, mice were euthanized, and liver tissues and serum were collected for further analysis.

All mice were maintained in a specific-pathogen-free environment at 20 °C ± 2 °C and 40% to 70% relative humidity on a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle with ad libitum access to standard chow and water. All animal experimental protocols were conducted in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc.

Alb-Cre-F and Alb-Cre-R primers were used to detect the Alb-Cre allele (450 bp for Alb-Cre+/- and none for Alb-Cre-/-). In vivo hepatocyte-specific deletion of Crebbp/Ep300

To achieve hepatocyte-specific deletion of Crebbp and Ep300 in vivo, 16-week-old Crebbpflox/flox/Ep300flox/flox mice were injected via the tail vein with AAV2/8 vectors (OBiO Technology) carrying Cre recombinase under the control of the hepatocyte-specific thyroxine-binding globulin promoter at a dose of 5 × 1011 genome copies per mouse (AAV-Cre). Mice injected with control AAV2/8 vectors expressing no transgene served as negative controls (AAV-CON). Unless otherwise stated, all mice were fasted for 6 hours before euthanasia and tissues were sampled. The livers were precisely dissected, weighed, immediately immersed in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Plasma biochemical assays

Serum levels of liver function indicators including ALT, AST, ALP, γ-GT, TBA, TBil, and DBil were detected using Roche C702 autochemistry analyzer (Roche Diagnostics).

Bile flow measurement

WT and CBP/p300LivDKO mice were fasted for 6 hours prior to surgery with free access to water. The bile flow rate was measured according to the protocol published on bio-protocol.55 Briefly, mice were anesthetized with avertin 0.25 mg/g intraperitoneally. The common bile duct was incised with a pair of fine iridectomy scissors about 6 mm below the hilum of the liver. A polyethylene micro tube (Jingyu; PE0302) was passed through the incision and propelled toward the hilum for a distance of about 3 mm. Bile flow rate was recorded (μL/min/g liver weight).

Primary hepatocyte isolation

Primary hepatocytes were isolated and purified according to an improved protocol based on the classic 2-step collagenase perfusion technique.56 Hepatocytes were counted and plated on collagen-coated cell culture plates and wells for culturing and further experiments.

Cell culture

Primary hepatocytes were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco; 10,567) containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Human embryonic kidney (HEK293) cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco; 11995065) containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were grown at 37 °C in humidified incubators containing an atmosphere of 5% CO2.

For adenoviral transduction, primary hepatocytes were incubated overnight with adenoviral particles carrying the Adv-CMV-Cre-SV40-EGFP construct or the corresponding control vector Adv-CMV-MCS-SV40-EGFP (Genechem). Luciferase reporter assays were performed on HEK293 cells. RNA sequencing and data analysis

Library construction and sequencing were performed at Shanghai Sinomics Corporation. Briefly, total RNA was isolated from control and CBP/p300LivDKO mice livers (n = 3 per group). Sequence reads were aligned to the mouse genome version GRCm38.102 using Hisat2 software, and differentially expressed genes were calculated by DESeq2 under the following criteria: |log2FC| ≥ .584963 and P value < .05. GSEA was conducted using the GSEA software (https://www.broadinstitute.org/gsea/).

BAGs were collected from the GSEA database (http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/ gsea/index.jsp) using “bile acid” as the main search term. A total of 9 BA-related gene sets were found. These gene sets were comprised of genes that participate in BA biosynthesis, secretion, metabolism, and BA-related signaling pathways. After removing the overlapping genes, 97 BAGs were obtained.

Metabolomics analysis

Quantitative metabolomic analysis was conducted using a Q300 Kit (Metabo-Profile). All standards were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, Steraloids Inc, and TRC Chemicals and were accurately weighed and prepared at a concentration of 5.0 mg/mL. An appropriate amount of each stock solution was mixed to create stock calibration solutions. Mouse liver and plasma samples (n = 8 per group) were transferred to a new 96-well plate with 10 μL internal standards in each well for further liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry analysis using an ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry system (ACQUITY UPLC-Xevo TQ-S; Waters Corp). All prepared samples were analyzed within 48 hours of sample extraction and derivatization. Three types of quality control samples (test mixtures, internal standards, and pooled biological samples) were used for quality control. Internal standards were added to the test samples to monitor analytical variations during sample preparation and analysis. The raw data files obtained by ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry were processed using MassLynx software (v4.1; Waters) to perform peak integration, calibration, and quantitation for each metabolite. Metabolites with variable importance in projection (obtained based on the orthogonal projections to latent structures discriminant analysis model) ≥1.0 and P values < .05 (univariate analyses were based on whether the data were normally distributed) were considered to be differential metabolites.

Cleavage under targets & tagmentation assays

The CUT&Tag assay was performed as previously described57 with minor modifications, and was conducted by Jiayin Biotechnology, Ltd. In brief, frozen liver tissues of WT and CBP/p300LivDKO mice were collected to extract nuclei in wash buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5; 150 mM NaCl; 0.5 mM Spermidine; 1× protease inhibitor cocktail) and then combined with Concanavalin A-coated magnetic beads (Bangs Laboratories). Subsequently, cells were resuspended in dig wash buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.5; 150 mM NaCl; 0.5 mM Spermidine, 1× protease inhibitor cocktail, 0.05% Digitonin; 2 mM EDTA) and incubated with primary antibodies against H3K27Ac (Cell Signaling Technology; #8173, 1:100 dilution), or normal IgG (Millipore; 12-370, 1:50 dilution), and sequentially secondary antibodies (rabbit anti-mouse IgG H&L: Abcam; ab611709; anti-rabbit IgG antibody, goat monoclonal: Millipore AP132; 1:100 dilution). Samples were incubated with pA-Tn5 transposase. After transposon activation and tagmentation, DNA was isolated, amplified, and purified to construct a library. Sequencing libraries were constructed according to the manufacturer’s instructions and cleaned using XP beads (Beckman Coulter). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions and subjected to quality control. The clean reads were then mapped to the Mus musculus genome sequences using the BWA program. Peak calling with a cutoff q value <0.05 was performed using MACS2 software. Peak annotation was performed using Homer’s annotate Peaks.pl, and peak analysis was conducted using R. The criteria of FC ≥2 and P value < .05 were employed to identify differentially accessible peaks, which were visualized using IGV.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays

The chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay was conducted using an EZ-ChIP Chromatin Immunoprecipitation kit (Millipore, 17-371) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 30 mg of liver tissue was used for each ChIP assay, and the liver tissues were cross-linked in 1% formaldehyde for 15 minutes after homogenization, followed by quenching with 0.125 M glycine for 5 minutes at room temperature, washed 3 times with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline containing phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, and lysed on ice for 10 minutes in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0,10 mM EDTA pH 8.0, 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate). Subsequently, the lysed samples were sonicated with SANYO Soniprep 150 (5 Hz, 10 seconds on, 10 seconds off; 5 times), and an average fragment size of 100 to 1000 bp was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis. The samples were incubated with antibodies against H3K27Ac (Cell Signaling Technology; #8173, 1:100 dilution) or normal IgG. The precipitated chromosome was then pulled down, purified, and quantified using RT-qPCR. The occupancy of H3K27Ac on the Klf10 promoter or Abcb11 promoter was normalized to the signal from the IgG pulled-down chromatin.

Primary hepatocytes (1 × 107) were seeded on 15-cm plates and transfected with CV702-Klf10 for 12 hours. The cells were immediately cross-linked in 1% formaldehyde for 15 minutes, and cross-linking was then quenched with glycine. Following, the cells were washed and lysed in cell lysis buffer. Next, the lysed cells were sheared to 100 to 1000 bp via ultrasonic treatment. Sheared chromatin was incubated with Flag antibody (20543-1-AP; Proteintech, 1:50 dilution) and normal IgG. The precipitated chromosome was then pulled down, purified, and quantified using RT-qPCR. Statistical analysis

Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad) was used to perform statistical analyses. Data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Comparisons between 2 groups were performed using an unpaired 2-tailed Student t test, whereas differences among 3 or more groups were assessed through 1-way analysis of variance, followed by Fisher’s least significant difference test. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationships between BA species in plasma and liver. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess correlation between RNA-seq and CUT&Tag H3K27Ac for BA metabolism–related genes. Positive correlations are shown in red, whereas negative correlations are depicted in blue. The intensity of the color represents the strength of the correlation, with deeper shades indicating higher absolute R values. P < .05 was considered statistically significant. ∗P < .05; ∗∗P < .01; ∗∗∗P < .001. Each experiment was replicated at least 3 times for each condition.
