Therapist-guided virtual reality exposure therapy for fear of heights: evidence from a multiple-baseline single-case experimental design
Authors: Kristína Kvapil Varšová, Jiří Chmelík, Václav Bílý, Oto Janoušek, Kateřina Koros Bartošová, Vojtěch Juřík
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVirtual reality exposure therapy (VRET) has demonstrated efficacy for specific phobias, yet most platforms separate the therapist from the patient during exposure. This multiple-baseline single-case experimental design study (SCED; n = 3) examined a multicomponent therapist-led VRET intervention delivered in an original collaborative immersive virtual environment (CIVE) developed for fear of heights treatment. Three adults from the general population with fear of heights completed unguided baseline sessions, therapist-led CIVE intervention sessions, and self-led follow-up sessions conducted three weeks after the intervention. The intervention combined repeated VR exposure, shared therapist–participant presence inside the CIVE, therapist guidance, and real-time heart rate (HR)-informed support. HR was monitored across all phases, but it was integrated into real-time therapeutic guidance only during the intervention phase. Outcomes included maximum height reached, State-Trait Anxiety Inventory, Form Y-1 (STAI-Y1), Subjective Units of Distress Scale (SUDS), and HR. Descriptive and visual analyses suggested preliminary, heterogeneous trends toward higher maximum floors reached and lower self-reported anxiety during the therapist-led CIVE intervention phase. These patterns were more apparent for two participants, while one participant showed delayed improvement. Follow-up data suggested possible maintenance of gains. Exploratory analyses of HR–height coupling showed a mixed pattern, including a statistically significant reduction in coupling in one participant, a non-significant trend in another, and no evidence of reduced coupling in the third. Overall, the findings provide preliminary indications that a multicomponent therapist-led CIVE intervention may support exposure progress in some individuals with fear of heights. However, larger controlled studies are needed before conclusions about effectiveness can be drawn, and to isolate the contribution of individual intervention components.Keywords: Fear of heights, Cognitive-behavioral therapy, Biofeedback, CIVE, Virtual reality, Exposure therapySubject terms: Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Virtual reality (VR) has evolved from an experimental technology to a widely used platform for simulation, training, and research1–3. One feature that distinguishes VR from traditional screen-based or imaginal approaches is its ability to generate convincing environments that feel spatially present and responsive4,5. This capacity has made VR a valuable tool for studying fear, avoidance, and related emotional processes and has attracted increasing interest in psychotherapy, where it is used primarily for VR exposure therapy (VRET)6. As hardware improved, virtual environments evolved into sophisticated, engaging therapeutic settings. Yet one insight remained consistent: VR can reliably evoke emotional and physiological reactions, making it ideal for exposure-based learning7–10.

Some recent VRET interventions, particularly scalable or automated programs for specific phobias, have explored self-guided or automated formats, and progressively removed the human therapist from the exposure process11–13. Automated guidance can deliver structured tasks but often lacks emotional attunement, flexibility, and interpersonal support14,15. Some studies even suggest that reduced therapist involvement may be associated with lower adherence or higher dropout in digital mental-health interventions16–19. In our previous research, we found that therapist guidance did not diminish subjective fear, but it did support smoother physiological recovery and more regulated physiological stress patterns after exposure20. This suggests that the value of guidance could lie in helping individuals navigate anxiety effectively. Still, compared to in vivo exposure, the majority of VRET applications differ in one crucial way: the therapist and client do not share the same environment or the phobic stimulus in real time.

This contrast highlights a clear gap. If VR can evoke strong emotional and physiological reactions, and therapist guidance can support emotional processing, anxiety tolerance, and continued engagement during CBT and exposure-based treatment, what happens when therapist and client work together in the same virtual space14,21,22? Current VRET designs usually position the therapist outside the headset11,23,24. Only a handful of studies have explored shared virtual environments, and almost none have applied them to structured psychotherapeutic work across multiple sessions and VRET25. The present study, therefore, examines a different model: therapist and client working on VRET, and meeting together inside a collaborative immersive virtual environment (CIVE). In this study, we investigate how emotional, behavioral, and physiological responses evolve when exposure becomes shared rather than an isolated experience.

Virtual reality as a controlled environment

VR can create immersive, computer-generated environments that support a subjective sense of presence, meaning that users may experience the virtual situation as spatially surrounding and personally relevant, even while knowing that it is simulated4,26,27. In the present study, immersion refers to the technological capacity of the VR system to surround the user with stereoscopic visual input, motion tracking, and interactive spatial cues28,29, whereas presence refers to the user’s subjective experience of “being there” in the virtual environment4,30. In collaborative VR, co-presence further refers to the perceived sense of sharing the virtual space with another embodied user31–33. These distinctions are important for the present study because the therapeutic rationale of CIVE depends not only on the controlled simulation of height, but also on the possibility that therapist and participant perceive themselves as acting together within the same virtual context. Such controllability makes VR useful for studying emotional and physiological responses, as scenarios can be standardized, repeated, and modified with high fidelity34–36.

Current systems commonly integrate stereoscopic displays, motion tracking, and spatial audio to generate immersive environments with precise control over visual and situational parameters. Such controllability makes VR uniquely suited for studying human emotional responses, as scenarios can be standardized, repeated, and modified with high fidelity26,33. When paired with physiological monitoring, VR becomes a powerful platform for examining how emotional and bodily responses unfold in structured, replicable conditions34–36.

CBT for acrophobia and the role of exposure

Acrophobia, or fear of heights, is a specific phobia involving marked fear or anxiety in response to height-related situations, often accompanied by avoidance and somatic symptoms such as dizziness, trembling, sweating, increased HR, and feelings of instability37,38. In more severe cases, fear of heights can interfere with daily functioning, travel, occupational activities, and general mobility37–40. Cognitive-behavioral models suggest that acrophobic fear is maintained by catastrophic interpretations of height-related sensations, attentional biases toward threat, avoidance, and safety behaviors that limit corrective learning21,41. Exposure is a central component of CBT for specific phobias because it enables repeated confrontation with feared stimuli, disconfirmation of threat expectations, and new learning about safety41,42. However, in vivo height exposure can be logistically difficult to organize, unpredictable, constrained by safety considerations, and distressing for participants43. VR addresses some of these limitations by providing controlled, repeatable, and graded height scenarios that can reliably activate fear responses while allowing predictable manipulation of exposure intensity13,44,45. This alignment between exposure-based CBT principles and VR simulation provides the rationale for using VRET in fear-of-heights interventions.

Virtual reality exposure therapy: progress and remaining gaps

VRET is now well-established for several specific phobias, including acrophobia. Numerous studies have demonstrated its efficacy and acceptability, often comparable to or exceeding traditional exposure approaches23,46.

Nonetheless, most VRET platforms place the therapist outside the virtual environment, where they observe the session or provide verbal instructions without sharing the same perceptual context, or the platforms operate without any therapeutic involvement47,48. Automated agents can deliver procedural guidance and standardized exposure tasks, but they may be more limited in providing flexible interpersonal responsiveness, shared attention to the feared stimulus, and moment-to-moment adjustment based on the client’s verbal, behavioral, and physiological responses14,45.

Evidence from digital mental-health research suggests that therapist involvement can improve outcomes in some intervention formats16,49. In VR therapy, therapists actively tailor virtual scenarios and provide real-time feedback, yielding meaningful reductions in social anxiety and improvements in well-being50. More broadly, a comprehensive meta-review found that digital interventions delivered with therapist guidance tend to be more effective than unguided formats18. Even brief therapist support in guided internet-delivered CBT (for instance, weekly check-ins, feedback on exposure tasks, and motivational messaging) appears to enhance short-term outcomes compared with unguided formats51.

Beyond effectiveness, an important question concerns how immersive technologies may influence the therapeutic relationship itself. Evidence from phobia treatment suggests that client–therapist collaboration can remain strong in technology-based exposure settings15. For example, high collaboration scores were reported in both technology-assisted and face-to-face phobia therapy, although therapists reported more difficulty maintaining focus in the technology-based condition52. Similarly, augmented reality exposure therapy did not appear to negatively affect the patient–therapist relationship when compared with in vivo exposure, despite the limited sample size53. In addition, research in VR-based treatment for social anxiety disorder found that a strong working alliance can be established and maintained throughout treatment54. Together, these findings suggest that therapeutic alliance may depend less on the technology itself and more on whether the treatment format enables meaningful therapist involvement, collaboration, and shared emotional processing. Previous research suggests that therapeutic alliance may remain relevant in technology-mediated exposure settings; however, the present study did not directly assess alliance. Instead, therapist-led CIVE was examined as a way to preserve real-time therapist involvement, communication, and support during VR exposure15,52–54.

In our previous single-session VR height exposure study, psychological guidance was compared with no psychological guidance using self-reported anxiety and physiological outcomes (HR). Psychological guidance was associated with smoother physiological recovery, particularly faster post-exposure HR recovery, whereas subjective anxiety did not differ between guided and unguided conditions20.

However, to our knowledge, no research has examined repeated therapist-led VRET for fear of heights in which the therapist guides the course of exposure from within a shared collaborative virtual environment55. VR systems already allow real-time collaboration in shared virtual spaces, making this an important opportunity for examining how therapist involvement can be implemented inside VR exposure rather than outside it.

Collaborative immersive virtual environments

CIVE is a digital three-dimensional space in which two or more users are simultaneously present and able to interact with each other in real time, typically through avatars in a shared virtual environment31,33. Unlike single-user VR, CIVEs allow participants to share the same synchronized environment, communicate verbally or nonverbally, manipulate virtual objects, and coordinate joint tasks32,56. Through networked head-mounted displays (HMDs) and motion tracking, users observe one another as avatars whose movements, gestures, and spatial positions are updated in real time, producing a strong sense of co-presence57. Although collaborative and group-based VR applications have begun to emerge in the mental health field, including VR group therapy for depression, therapist-guided support groups, LGBTQIA + group therapy, wellbeing interventions for people with life-threatening illnesses, remote art therapy, and VR experiences designed to induce altered states of consciousness, their application to therapist-guided exposure therapy for fear of heights remains limited25,58–63. To our knowledge, no CIVE-based intervention has yet been developed and evaluated specifically for therapist-guided VRET for fear of heights.

Role of physiological feedback

The integration of physiological monitoring further expands the potential of VR and CIVEs. HR and other biosignals can be continuously recorded and displayed live or retrospectively, providing information about arousal, stress, and engagement. These data can be used to tailor interactions, adjust exposure intensity, and provide users with insight into their internal states64,65. In addition to absolute HR levels, psychophysiological and VRET research have emphasized the importance of examining how physiological responses change in relation to exposure intensity and recovery over time66,67. Continuous physiological monitoring may therefore help therapists identify moments when fear responses intensify, stabilize or decline during repeated confrontation with feared stimuli68,69.

Physiological feedback may be particularly valuable in VR-based exposure, as it offers continuous information about the client’s autonomic state during confrontation with feared stimuli. In phobia treatment, such assessment can help identify moments of heightened fear, support individualized adjustment of exposure intensity, and complement self-report measures, which may fluctuate due to avoidance, interpretation, or demand characteristics. Recent work has proposed combining VR serious games with physiological monitoring as an end-to-end framework for supporting exposure therapy in phobia treatment, suggesting that physiological signals can be used not only for retrospective assessment but also to adapt the therapeutic process in real time70.

These combined advantages are relevant to VRET for fear of heights and other anxiety disorders. A shared, controlled virtual environment enables real-time insight into the client’s emotional state while allowing therapist and client to work toward a common goal: engaging with, and gradually diminishing, the fear elicited by the phobic stimulus64,65. Within such a context, therapist-led VRET may enhance exposure processes by reducing premature avoidance, providing immediate support, and enabling dynamic adjustment of task difficulty based on observable emotional and physiological responses. These features may support greater behavioral approach, lower subjective anxiety across repeated sessions, and more adaptive physiological responding over time. Accordingly, the present study examined whether these patterns would emerge during therapist-led VRET in a CIVE.

Aim of this study

Although VR exposure is widely used for acrophobia, the role of therapists within the virtual environment remains largely unexplored. At the same time, VR platforms already support shared presence and collaboration inside a common virtual space33,71. However, no study has systematically examined how exposure unfolds when both therapist and participant work together in the same environment.

The present study examines how therapist-led VRET in CIVE shapes emotional and physiological adaptation during repeated VR exposure for fear of heights. Using a CIVE, we tracked self-reported anxiety and HR across baseline, intervention and follow-up phases to test whether shared presence and therapist-guided VRET in CIVE are associated with more favorable outcomes across repeated exposures compared to self-guided therapy. We also assessed treatment credibility and expectancy to characterize participants’ understanding and engagement with the intervention. By integrating subjective, physiological, and expectancy-related indices, the study provides a process-level account of therapist involvement in VRET. Based on this rationale, we expected the therapist-guided intervention phase to be associated with more favorable outcomes than the preceding self-guided baseline phase, specifically: (1) lower self-reported anxiety during intervention sessions relative to self-led baseline phase, (2) progressive increases in behavioral approach (higher floors reached), (3) reduced physiological reactivity during height exposure, reflected in lower HR–height coupling and/or faster post-exposure recovery, (4) maintenance of gains at follow-up, (5) treatment credibility and expectancy to remain moderate to high across the intervention, indicating that therapist-led VRET in a CIVE would be perceived as acceptable and plausible by participants. Together, we hypothesized that therapist-led VRET in CIVE may support engagement with height exposure by increasing guidance, attunement, and moment-to-moment adjustment, which may be reflected in greater behavioral approach, changes in self-reported anxiety, and physiological responses across sessions.

Given the exploratory nature of therapist-led VRET in a CIVE and the need to examine individual change processes, a multiple-baseline single-case experimental design (SCED) was selected. This design was appropriate because therapist-led VRET in a CIVE represents an early-stage, resource-intensive intervention format in which detailed within-participant change is central to interpretation. Rather than estimating population-level efficacy, the aim was to examine whether behavioral, subjective, and physiological changes emerged within participants across repeated exposure sessions and whether similar patterns could be observed across cases after staggered introduction of the intervention. SCED enables rigorous evaluation under small-sample conditions by relying on repeated measurement, prospective phase structuring, and within-case experimental control72. The multiple-baseline framework further supports examination of functional relations between the intervention and outcomes by introducing the intervention at different time points across participants, thereby helping to demonstrate replicable individual-level change and build an empirical basis for subsequent larger-scale controlled studies.

In this design, several data sequences were collected, and the timing of the intervention was systematically staggered across participants. This staggered implementation enabled both within-series and between-series comparisons, strengthening causal inference. We implemented repeated instances of the same phase change, introduced at different time points for each participant, with the duration and timing of phases varying across individuals.

Methods

Study design

In this SCED study, we examined how self-reported anxiety and HR change across baseline (B), intervention (I), and follow-up (F) phases when the therapist leads the therapy inside the CIVE.

To complement the quantitative data obtained through the SCED methodology, brief supplementary semi-structured interviews were conducted with each participant after their sessions. These interviews provided qualitative insights into participants’ subjective experiences, attitudes, and perceptions of the therapeutic process.

Participants

In total, eight individuals responded to the recruitment advertisement. Of these, eight completed the initial screening questionnaire, including the Heights Interpretation Questionnaire (HIQ). Six individuals met the predefined eligibility criteria. Three individuals were excluded because they stopped responding. Three eligible participants were ultimately enrolled and completed all study phases.

The sample consisted of one male (35 years) and two females (33 and 27 years). All participants had completed university education and were employed at the time of the study. Participants were recruited from the general population and did not constitute a clinical sample. No structured clinical interview was conducted; therefore, participants were not formally diagnosed with acrophobia. All met predefined inclusion criteria designed to ensure the relevance and integrity of the findings. Every participant reported fear of heights and scored above 55 on the HIQ, indicating elevated height-related fear and threat interpretation. None had previously received psychotherapy for acrophobia, and none had prior experience with VR applications simulating height-related situations.

Participants were approached through social media (Facebook) and invited to complete the HIQ screening questionnaire. Eligible participants then attended a brief online psychoeducation session focused on fear of heights, avoidance and safety behaviors, the anxiety vicious circle, the rationale of exposure therapy, and the procedure of the VR sessions (e.g., headset use and questionnaire administration).

The sample size was determined in accordance with the logic of multiple-baseline SCED, where intervention effects are evaluated through repeated measurement, within-participant change, and replication across cases rather than through group-level statistical power. The inclusion of three participants allowed staggered introduction of the intervention across cases and enabled preliminary examination of individual response patterns. Upon completion of all sessions, each participant received 1,000 CZK in financial compensation.

Because the intervention consisted of repeated, therapist-guided sessions that closely followed real CBT practice as it was consulted with an established CBT expert in the field, the implementation was both time- and resource-intensive.

Measures and outcomes

All standardized questionnaires were administered in Czech translations. As validated Czech versions with established reliability and validity indices were not publicly available for these instruments, the questionnaires were translated by the authors from the original English versions. The translations were subsequently reviewed by a native English speaker for semantic clarity and conceptual equivalence with the original items73,74. This review should not be interpreted as formal translation validation or cross-cultural adaptation. No formal back-translation, cognitive interviewing, pilot testing, or psychometric validation procedure was conducted. Given the very small SCED sample, internal consistency and item-level functioning could not be meaningfully evaluated in the present study.

Heights interpretation questionnaire

Heights Interpretation Questionnaire (HIQ41) is a self-report measure assessing threat-related interpretations of height situations. In the present study, it was used as a screening measure to identify participants with elevated height-related fear and threat interpretation. Although the HIQ is not a diagnostic instrument for acrophobia, previous research has shown that it predicts fear, anxiety, and avoidance in response to height-related situations. Participants were included if they scored above the predefined threshold of 55 points41,45.

State-trait anxiety inventory

State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI75) is a widely used self-report measure of anxiety, comprising separate scales for state anxiety and trait anxiety. The present study used the state anxiety scale, STAI-Y1, to assess immediate state anxiety before and after each VR exposure session. The STAI-Y1 has been widely used in anxiety and VR exposure research as a measure of momentary anxiety76. This choice was informed by previous VR studies that used the STAI-Y1 to measure state anxiety in similar contexts5,77,78.

Subjective units of distress scale

Subjective Units of Distress Scale79 (SUDS) is a brief self-report rating of momentary subjective distress during exposure. In the present study, SUDS ratings were used to capture within-session changes in fear intensity during the VR elevator task and were recorded every five floors. Usually, this self-report scale ranges from 0 (no distress) to 10 (maximum distress), allowing participants to rate their distress levels during the simulated elevator experience. A simplified 0–10 format was used to facilitate rapid distress ratings, as frequent assessment during VR exposure needed to be brief, intuitive, and minimally disruptive to presence and task engagement, while still providing ordinal estimates of subjective anxiety intensity. SUDS scores were treated as ordinal indicators of momentary distress.

Maximum floor reached

Maximum floor reached was included in this section as a primary behavioral outcome variable rather than as an independent standardized measurement instrument. It was operationalized as the highest floor reached during each VR exposure session and served as a session-level index of behavioral approach toward the height-related stimulus. Conceptually, this measure can be understood as a VR-adapted Behavioral Avoidance Test (BAT), in which greater height progression reflects reduced avoidance and greater willingness to approach the feared situation80. Unlike a traditional in vivo BAT, this outcome was assessed within the standardized virtual elevator environment, allowing repeated measurement across sessions under controlled conditions.

Heart rate measurement

HR was monitored using a Vernier Go Wireless (Go Wireless® Exercise Heart Rate, n.d.), a device commonly used for HR recording in exercise and psychophysiological research68,81. It is a non-invasive, precise device (sampling frequency: 1000 Hz) that is safe, reliable, and easy to deploy. The sensor connects via Bluetooth and provides stable, high-resolution recordings suitable for detailed physiological analysis. Additionally, data regarding HR is sent directly to our VR application, enabling therapists to monitor it in real-time while being in the virtual environment alongside the participant. HR was continuously monitored across all phases. HR data were available to the research team during both baseline and intervention sessions; however, their role differed by phase. During the baseline and follow-up phases, HR was observed externally on a monitor only and was not used to guide the exposure. During the intervention phase, HR was available in real time to the therapist and was incorporated into the therapeutic process to help adjust the pacing and intensity of exposure. Participants did not see their HR data in real time in either phase. This integration allowed therapists to assess the participant’s physiological response to VR therapy sessions in real time, facilitating a more informed and therapeutic process.

Simulator sickness questionnaire

Simulator Sickness Questionnaire82 (SSQ) is a standardized self-report measure of symptoms associated with simulator or VR exposure, including nausea, oculomotor discomfort, and disorientation. In the present study, the SSQ was administered after each session to monitor simulator sickness and tolerability of the VR environment. Because several SSQ symptoms overlap with anxiety-related bodily sensations, SSQ findings were interpreted cautiously and primarily as indicators of VR tolerability rather than as independent clinical outcomes.

Credibility/expectancy questionnaire

Credibility/Expectancy Questionnaire83 (CEQ) is a self-report measure assessing treatment credibility and expectancy of improvement. In the present study, it was used as a secondary acceptability-related outcome to assess whether participants perceived the VR fear-of-heights intervention as plausible and potentially helpful. CEQ scores were reported descriptively using raw credibility and expectancy scores.

Apparatus

Participants received visual stimuli using Meta Quest 3. Our own CIVE app, developed in cooperation with the Faculty of Informatics, Masaryk University, and Brno University of Technology, Czech Republic, was used to represent the virtual height environment. The VR application was developed in Unity and presented through an HMD (Meta Quest 3).

Our CIVE app can be defined as a shared immersive virtual space in which therapist and participant can jointly perceive, navigate, and interact with heights exposure-relevant content. In this app, users find themselves in an elevator in the city center, near a tall building. Initially, the user finds themselves on the lowest floor (ground level) in a glass elevator, which offers a view of the street outside. The elevator is used to change the vertical level and features a control panel with “START”, “EMERGENCY STOP”, and “BACK” buttons, all positioned on the left side (see Fig. 1). Our environment simulates the experience of ascending or descending at least 30 floors, with the possibility to stop on any floor; by default, however, the elevator automatically paused every five floors.

The virtual environment used in the study.

Biofeedback is integrated into the virtual environment, enhancing immersive experience by incorporating physiological responses. The HR panel is visible only to the therapist and is positioned above the participant’s avatar (see Fig. 2). It displays the participant’s HR in real time during the session. The therapist and participant shared the same virtual elevator environment and could see each other’s avatars, but the interface was role-specific. The participant saw the elevator, control panel, surrounding height environment, and therapist avatar. The therapist saw the same shared environment, together with an additional HR display positioned above the participant’s avatar. This HR panel was visible only to the therapist and displayed the participant’s current HR in real time. The participant did not see their HR value during the session. Thus, HR feedback functioned as therapist-side information to support clinical monitoring and pacing, rather than as direct biofeedback to the participant.

Therapist-side view of the participant avatar with live HR information displayed above the avatar. The HR panel was visible only to the therapist and was not shown in the participant interface.

Participants were represented in the virtual environment by a simple first-person humanoid avatar reflecting head and hand movements, while the therapist used a separate synchronized avatar during collaborative sessions. From the participant’s first-person perspective, self-representation was limited to tracked hand/controller representations and head movement was reflected through the participant’s viewpoint. The participant did not view a full-body self-avatar from a third-person perspective. The therapist, however, could see the participant’s avatar, including head and hand movements, within the shared virtual environment. Similarly, the participant could see the therapist’s avatar in the CIVE. Thus, the avatar system primarily supported mutual visibility and real-time social interaction within the shared environment rather than a fully embodied self-avatar experience.

In the I sessions, both therapist and participant wore separate Meta Quest 3 HMDs and entered the same synchronized CIVE, where they were represented by avatars. Communication occurred verbally in real time while both were wearing the HMDs, supported by the headset microphone/audio system. Thus, therapist and participant shared both the physical laboratory space and the virtual environment.

During B and F sessions, the therapist and participant were also physically located in the same laboratory room; however, the therapist did not enter the virtual environment and was not represented by an avatar. In these phases, the participant completed the VR exposure independently, while the therapist remained outside the CIVE and did not provide therapeutic guidance.

Procedure

The complete course of the experiment is illustrated in Fig. 3.

Overview of the entire experimental procedure.

Upon first arrival at the experimental laboratory session, participants provided basic demographic information, including age, gender, education, and occupational status, before completing the first questionnaire battery. Before the first exposure, participants received a brief equipment induction and VR familiarization. The researcher explained how to wear and adjust the HMD, how to use the hand controllers, how to communicate during the session, and how to stop the exposure if needed. Participants were also given a short opportunity to orient themselves in the virtual environment and become familiar with the elevator controls before the exposure task began. Because none of the participants had prior experience with height-related VR applications, possible novelty effects during early baseline sessions were considered when interpreting baseline variability.

At the beginning of each session, participants completed the STAI-Y1 as a pre-exposure measure of state anxiety. They were then instructed to wear the Vernier Go Wireless device, and HR recording commenced with a standardized three-minute seated baseline period before each exposure session (while participants were seated). During this time, we engaged in a brief conversation about their current mood. Light neutral conversation was permitted during this period to maintain a naturalistic pre-session context and help participants acclimatize to the setting before exposure. Afterwards, participants put on the HMD.

Each intervention session followed the same general structure: brief pre-session check-in, preparation for exposure, entry into the CIVE, graded height exposure, post-exposure questionnaire completion, and a brief post-session reflection. Therapist guidance occurred only during the intervention phase. No formal treatment fidelity instrument was used to assess therapist adherence or consistency of intervention delivery. This statement refers to clinical intervention fidelity rather than the technical fidelity or realism of the VR system. Treatment consistency was supported through use of the same therapist across sessions, adherence to a structured exposure procedure, and supervision-based consultation with a CBT expert.

The length of the B phase varied across participants to strengthen causal inference through staggered introduction of the I. One participant completed three B sessions, one completed four sessions, and one completed five sessions, each spaced approximately two days apart. This closely spaced format is consistent with prior VRET studies for acrophobia, in which sessions were delivered multiple times per week or across a brief treatment window45,48. It also aligns with exposure-learning models suggesting that repeated exposure across sessions can support inhibitory learning and reduce opportunities for renewed avoidance between sessions21.

All of the participants started with B phase. During the B phase, participants completed individual, self-led exposure within the VR height environment. This allowed us to observe participants’ responses to the VR height stimulus without therapist guidance inside the virtual environment. However, because all participants had already received psychoeducation about fear of heights, avoidance, safety behaviors, and the rationale for exposure therapy, baseline responses should not be interpreted as entirely spontaneous or unaffected by prior therapeutic information.

In the B phase, participants were instructed to enter the elevator in the virtual environment and ascend as high as they felt able. Before starting, participants were informed that they would remain in the VR environment for ten minutes. Participants experienced our virtual environment using an HMD. Elevator movement during sessions was controlled by participants. Although HR was monitored continuously and could be observed externally by the researcher on a monitor, it was not used to guide or modify the exposure, and the researcher did not intervene in the session.

After the B phase, all participants entered the I phase, consisting of repeated therapist-guided VRET sessions delivered within the CIVE. The duration of this phase varied depending on the length of each participant’s B but generally spanned from the second to the fourth week of the study. For I sessions, participants were briefed on what to expect and received therapeutic guidance from a CBT therapist. They were informed that communication would occur throughout the exposure and that the aim was to work with their perceived fear. This pre-exposure explanation was essential for maintaining therapeutic standards. During I sessions, both the participant and therapist were represented in the same virtual environment, a city-center scene with a glass elevator. They were also physically located in the same laboratory room. The participant experienced the virtual environment using an HMD, while the therapist also entered the shared virtual environment using a separate HMD. Therapist and participant were represented by avatars inside the CIVE and communicated verbally throughout the session in real time. Elevator movement during intervention sessions was controlled by the participant. Participants could decide whether to continue upward exposure or stop the session, while the therapist could suggest pacing adjustments based on observed distress and physiological responses.

The instructions were identical to those in the B phase; however, in I sessions, both the therapist and the participant were together in the CIVE, and the entire session consisted of guided VRET. VRET sessions were delivered by the same therapist, a psychologist enrolled in long-term CBT training accredited by the Ministry of Health of the Czech Republic. The training includes supervised practice, regular individual supervision sessions, and supervision groups. Treatment followed a structured exposure protocol based on established CBT principles, including graded exposure, reduction of avoidance and safety behaviors, support for anxiety tolerance, and post-session reflection. Fidelity was supported through protocol adherence and supervision-based consultation. For example, the therapist used strategies to support tolerance of anxiety by explaining its natural rise and fall (habituation), emphasizing that anxiety is temporary and diminishes with continued exposure. In this phase, HR data were available to the therapist in real time and were incorporated into the therapeutic process alongside direct observation, allowing the pacing and intensity of exposure to be adjusted dynamically as the participant’s state changed. HR feedback was used as an informal clinical aid rather than as a standardized algorithmic decision rule. The therapist considered HR together with the participant’s verbal reports, observed behavior, and visible signs of distress. In practice, elevated or persistently increasing HR could prompt the therapist to check in verbally, suggest pausing at the current floor, encourage grounding or breathing, or slow the pace of ascent. Conversely, when HR stabilized together with reduced reported distress, the therapist could support continued exposure or suggest gradual progression to the next floor. Final decisions about continuing, pausing, or descending remained with the participant.

Following the I, three self-guided F sessions were conducted for each participant within a single week, three weeks later. These F sessions served to evaluate the persistence of treatment effects over time. The F phase was procedurally analogous to the B phase. Participants completed the VR exposure sessions independently without therapist guidance inside the virtual environment (the therapist was physically co-located in the same room as the participant). No real-time therapeutic support, pacing recommendations, grounding interventions, or physiological feedback integration were provided during F sessions. Participants navigated the virtual environment and controlled elevator movement independently according to their own decisions and tolerance.

At the end of each session, participants removed the HMD and completed the post-exposure questionnaires (STAI-Y1-b, CEQ, SSQ). Researchers simultaneously monitored HR for an additional three minutes. During all sessions, SUDS ratings were verbally obtained at predefined intervals, manually recorded by the researcher, and entered into the data table.

Brief supplementary semi-structured interviews were conducted after each VR session by the therapist. The interviews lasted approximately 10 min and focused on participants’ current emotional state, perceived coping strategies, subjective experience of the exposure, vicious circles, perceived helpful or difficult elements of the session, and any changes noticed since the previous session. Responses were recorded as written session notes and used to contextualize the quantitative findings rather than as a formal qualitative analysis.

Ethics

The study adhered to all relevant ethical standards and was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Masaryk University, Brno, Czech Republic (project number EKV-2023-013-E5). This study does not constitute a clinical trial as defined by the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform. The study was conducted on a non-clinical sample with self-reported fear of heights using a single-case experimental design. Therefore, prospective trial registration was not required. All procedures followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection began. Participants were explicitly informed that they could withdraw from the study at any point up until the completion of data collection, after which the identifying key linking personal information to research data was permanently deleted. Withdrawal carried no negative consequences and did not affect the promised remuneration. All aspects of the study, including its aims, procedures, and data-processing methods, were explained in detail before participation. The anxiety elicited during VR exposure did not produce any long-term or irreversible changes in HR or related physiological parameters.

Data analysis

Each participant was assigned a neutral identifier (P1, P2, and P3) for data analysis and reporting purposes; original study codes were used only for internal data management. Sessions were coded into B, I, and F phases according to the prespecified study schedule (staggered baselines of 3, 4, and 5 sessions; intervention phases matched the length of each participant’s baseline; 3 follow-ups). This staggered multiple-baseline structure supports replication logic and strengthens causal inference in single-case research72,84. All analyses and figures were produced in Python.

Primary outcomes were maximum floor reached, state anxiety (STAI-Y1), and SUDS ratings. STAI-Y1 was collected pre-exposure (STAI-Y1-a) and post-exposure (STAI-Y1-b) for each session; ΔSTAI was computed as STAI-Y1-b − STAI-Y1-a (negative values indicate pre-to-post reduction). SUDS were recorded every five floors. Maximum floor reached reflected the highest floor attained within a session and served as a behavioral index of approach toward the feared stimulus. Despite its discrete format, floor reached provides an interpretable ordered index of behavioral approach that is suitable for descriptive within-case trend analysis in SCED. Regression analyses were therefore used descriptively to summarize phase-related patterns rather than to imply strict continuous measurement. Because ratings above the highest reached floor are structurally unavailable, SUDS were summarized per session as mean SUDS (across reached floors). SSQ and CEQ were analyzed descriptively as secondary acceptability-related outcomes. For each participant and phase (B, I, F), means, SDs, and n were calculated for floor reached, STAI-Y1-a, STAI-Y1-b, ΔSTAI, and mean SUDS. Primary inference was based on visual inspection of time-series plots with clearly marked phase transitions. We evaluated level, trend, variability, immediacy of effect, overlap, and consistency across participants. Phase stability was summarized using a commonly applied SCED visual-analysis criterion, defined as at least 80% of observations falling within ± 15% of the phase median, which provides an estimate of within-phase variability and trend stability prior to phase comparisons85.

Procedures for data inspection, preprocessing, synchronization, and handling of missing or low-quality observations were performed prior to physiological analyses. Analyses were conducted on session-level HR recordings without additional filtering beyond device preprocessing. HR recordings were visually inspected prior to analysis for major signal loss, recording interruptions, or obvious artifacts. HR time-series data were synchronized with elevator-height data before cross-correlation analyses. Short transient irregularities were retained unless they substantially impaired interpretation of the session-level physiological profile. No sessions were excluded due to HR recording quality. Questionnaire data were complete across all sessions; therefore, no imputation procedures were required.

Quantification of physiological reactivity

To explore changes in physiological reactivity across sessions, HR time-series data were synchronized with elevator-height data before cross-correlation analyses. Within-session cross-correlations between elevator height and HR were then calculated as an exploratory index of coupling between exposure intensity and cardiovascular response. The primary reactivity metric was the maximum cross-correlation coefficient (rmax) within a − 10 to + 30 s time-lag window, allowing for possible physiological delay between height changes and cardiac responses. Session-level rmax values were then summarized across phases.

Tau-U, a non-parametric single-case effect index combining non-overlap and trend86, was used for phase comparisons. In the present study, Tau-U was applied exploratorily to estimate phase-related directional changes in HR–height coupling. Because this analysis was not specified as a primary confirmatory outcome and represents a non-standard application of Tau-U, the findings should be interpreted cautiously as hypothesis-generating rather than as confirmatory evidence of physiological adaptation. Non-overlap effect sizes.

To quantify phase differences, we computed Non-overlap of All Pairs (NAP) and Percent of Non-overlapping Data (PND) for B vs I and B vs F. NAP is the proportion of pairwise comparisons in which an intervention data point exceeds a baseline data point; ties receive half weight87. PND is the percentage of intervention observations that exceed the most extreme baseline observation when an increase is desirable. NAP compared all baseline–treatment pairs (ties were set to 0.5), using the favorable direction (higher floor reached; lower ΔSTAI). PND reflected the proportion of treatment points exceeding the most extreme baseline value in the favorable direction. Effect sizes were computed using established single-case effect-size calculators to ensure reproducibility (https://jepusto.shinyapps.io/SCD-effect-sizes).

Piecewise regression (level and slope change)

Within each participant, segmented regression estimated the immediate level change at intervention onset (β₂) and the slope change during the intervention (β₃), using a session index, an intervention indicator, and a time-after-intervention term. Models fit for floor reached and ΔSTAI. Although maximum floor reached was recorded in discrete five-floor increments, it was treated as an approximately ordered repeated-measures variable for descriptive trend estimation. Regression analyses were used to summarize phase-related change rather than to imply strict interval-level measurement. Findings were interpreted alongside visual inspection and robust summaries (median/IQR).

Multilevel modelling

Exploratory linear mixed-effects models were fitted to descriptively summarize phase-related trends across repeated observations. Their purpose was to provide a compact descriptive aggregation of coefficient direction and magnitude and to examine whether these model-based trends were broadly consistent with the visual and within-participant SCED analyses. The models were not intended to test intervention effectiveness, estimate stable between-person variance, or provide generalizable population-level inference. Because the study included only three participant-level clusters, these models were not considered suitable for inferential interpretation and were used solely as descriptive summaries of the observed data structure. Primary interpretation was based on SCED-appropriate analyses, including visual inspection, within-participant phase comparisons, descriptive statistics, segmented regression, and non-overlap indices.

For exploratory descriptive purposes, models included participant-specific intercepts and phase indicators for I and F, with B as the reference phase. Outcomes included floor reached, mean SUDS, STAI-Y1-a, STAI-Y1-b, SSQ, and CEQ. Coefficient estimates were examined only as descriptive indicators of direction and magnitude of phase-related trends across observations. Standard errors and p-values were not interpreted, and no confirmatory conclusions were based on these models (https://jepusto.shinyapps.io/scdhlm).

Post-session interviews

Brief semi-structured interview notes were reviewed descriptively and used only as supplementary contextual data. Written session notes were reviewed after data collection and used to contextualize quantitative findings, identify recurring patterns in behavioral and emotional responses, illustrate participant experiences, and help interpret phase-related changes. No formal qualitative coding or thematic analysis was conducted.

Statistical assumptions and interpretive constraints

Piecewise regression was used descriptively to estimate changes in level and slope across phases and assumes approximately linear within-phase trends and independent residuals. Linear mixed-effects models assume approximately normally distributed residuals and random effects; however, given the very small number of participants, these models were treated only as exploratory summaries and not as generalizable population-level estimates. Tau-U was used as a non-parametric single-case effect index suited to small repeated-measures datasets, although its interpretation remains dependent on phase stability, sufficient observations, and the presence of baseline trend. Across analyses, results were interpreted alongside visual inspection and descriptive phase-level summaries rather than as standalone confirmatory tests.

Baseline stability varied across participants and outcomes, and some baseline trends were already present before the I phase. Therefore, phase-related changes were interpreted cautiously and only considered suggestive when they were accompanied by visible level change, reduced overlap, and replication across participants.

Non-overlap effect sizes

To quantify phase differences, non-overlap indices were computed. The nonoverlap of all pairs (NAP) compares every baseline observation with every treatment observation and expresses the proportion of favorable comparisons. Values near 1 (100%) indicate complete non-overlap (positive effect), values around 0.5 suggest no effect, and values near 0 indicate the treatment produced worse outcomes.

B vs. I: For floor reached, NAP values were 0.28 for participant P1 (weak effect), 0.94 for participant P2 (stronger effect), and 0.96 for participant P3 (stronger effect). The percentage of non-overlapping data (PND) corroborated these findings: 0% of intervention scores exceeded the highest B score for participant P1, whereas 75% and 80% of I scores did so for participants P2 and P3, respectively. For ΔSTAI (improvement defined as a decrease), NAP values were 0.00 (participant P1), 0.91 (participant P2), and 0.96 (participant P3), indicating that anxiety increased for participant P1 and decreased substantially for the other two participants.

B vs. F: Comparing B with F yielded NAP values of 1.00 for floor reached across all participants, reflecting complete non-overlap; every F session exceeded the maximum B session. For ΔSTAI, NAP values were 0.72 (participant P1), 0.96 (participant P2), and 0.93 (participant P3), demonstrating sustained or further anxiety reductions at F. PND was also 100% for all participants, indicating that F performance surpassed B in every session.

Multilevel modelling

Descriptively, model coefficients followed the same general direction as the participant-level SCED summaries. Estimated phase coefficients were positive for maximum floor reached during both the I and F phases, and negative for SUDS, STAI-Y1-a, STAI-Y1-b, and SSQ, indicating lower average distress, state anxiety, and simulator sickness scores relative to B. CEQ coefficients were positive during I and F, suggesting higher credibility and expectancy scores relative to B. These patterns were interpreted only as descriptive consistency checks alongside the primary SCED analyses.

Trend and level changes

Piecewise linear regression models estimated immediate level changes (β₂) and slope changes (β₃) associated with the onset of the I. For the floor reached, participant P1 exhibited a significant negative level change (β₂≈− 7 floors), consistent with the observed drop during the I, followed by a positive slope indicating gradual improvement. Participants P2 and P3 showed positive level changes (β₂≈ + 1–2 floors) and positive slope changes, suggesting immediate and sustained increases in height tolerance. Regression models for ΔSTAI mirrored these patterns: participant P1’s β₂ was positive (indicative of increased anxiety), whereas participants P2 and P3 had negative β₂ values, reflecting decreased anxiety during the I. R2 values ranged from 0.49 to 0.98; however, given the very small number of observations per participant, these values should not be interpreted as evidence of robust model fit.

Secondary outcomes

SSQ scores varied across sessions, with some elevated values observed, but no consistent phase-related increase was apparent. Range for SSQ was ≈4–67, with no systematic increases during the I, suggesting that the VR system was well tolerated.

CEQ scores are reported as raw credibility and expectancy scores rather than z-scores. Descriptively, CEQ scores did not show a clear participant-level phase pattern, although exploratory mixed-model estimates suggested higher CEQ scores during I and F.

These findings suggest that the VR system was generally perceived as credible and was generally tolerated, with no systematic increase in cybersickness during the intervention phase.

Therapist observations and post-session supplementary interviews

P1

Across sessions, P1 initially entered VR in a positive mood but reported fear of height exposure and surprise at the realism of the VR environment, reporting marked sweating and an urge to escape when height cues intensified. Early sessions were characterized by sweating, dizziness, and repeated urges to quickly descend when anxiety intensified, particularly when looking downward or during elevator movement, as P1 reported. During B sessions, P1 often ascended rapidly and then returned to lower floors shortly after anxiety increased and reported having difficulties with remaining at higher levels for prolonged periods. Catastrophic thoughts related to falling were frequently reported.

During I sessions, P1 increasingly adopted a gradual floor-by-floor pacing approach and repeatedly reported that breathing exercises and grounding through stepping or focusing on contact with the floor helped maintain engagement with the exposure task. P3 described therapist guidance within the shared VR environment as helpful and supportive during moments of distress. Compared with B sessions, I and F sessions appeared to involve longer stays at higher floors and more controlled upward progression.

During F sessions, P1 reported greater comfort at heights and improved ability to regulate anxiety using breathing and grounding techniques, even when anxiety increased at higher floors. In the closing interview, the participant described the most notable change as an increased ability to tolerate discomfort and remain in the situation longer before deciding to descend.

P2

During B sessions, P2 frequently reported strong urges to descend to lower floors and often returned to the basement level for prolonged recovery periods. Height exposure was consistently accompanied by prominent gastrointestinal discomfort, including stomach pain and increased salivation, which the participant described as the primary limiting factor during exposure. Although mood and sleep were generally reported as stable, skepticism regarding possible improvement remained present during early sessions.

Across I sessions, grounding strategies and therapist guidance inside the shared VR environment were repeatedly described as helpful for reducing distress and increasing perceived control during exposure. Over time, P2 reported gradual reduction in gastrointestinal discomfort and reduced anxiety regarding progression to higher floors. Later sessions involved more deliberate self-regulation strategies, including breathing exercises and attentional focus on bodily sensations.

During F sessions, some gastrointestinal discomfort re-emerged after the time gap between sessions; however, the participant continued to report greater confidence and improved tolerance of height exposure compared with baseline. By the final F session, abdominal discomfort was described as relatively mild.

P3

At the outset, P3 reported pronounced anxiety related to the upcoming VR exposure, including observable hyperventilation before entering the virtual environment. During the early sessions, distress remained prominent despite some familiarization with the VR setting, and fatigue was repeatedly mentioned. P3 described a persistent sense of insufficient support (“missing support”), accompanied by somatic symptoms such as a “lump in the throat,” trembling, shakiness, and feelings of instability in the legs. Concerns about lack of progress also became increasingly apparent and culminated in one session in which P3 chose not to ascend at all.

From the mid-I sessions onward, P3 appeared willing to continue the exposure despite ongoing anxiety and sweating. Several coping strategies were repeatedly described as helpful, particularly grounding through stepping and maintaining contact with the floor. Box breathing, brief eye-closing, looking around the environment, and focusing attention on the therapist avatar were also reported as supportive during periods of increased distress. Therapist guidance within the shared VR environment was described by P3 as helpful and supportive and was associated with greater willingness to continue upward exposure.

Over time, thoughts resembling “I can’t do this” appeared less frequently in the therapist notes and participant reports, while expressions of increased confidence in coping became more common. By the later I and F sessions, P3 reported quicker self-calming during transitions between floors, reduced sweating, and greater confidence in continuing the exposure independently, even without therapist guidance inside the CIVE. P3 also noted that previously practiced coping techniques remained useful during the follow-up phase.

Discussion

This multiple-baseline single-case experimental study investigated how therapist-led VRET delivered in a CIVE affected individuals with fear of heights. By comparing self-led baseline sessions with therapist-guided intervention and follow-up sessions, we examined behavioral (maximum height reached), emotional (state anxiety and distress), and physiological (HR) responses.

Our findings are broadly consistent with previous evidence suggesting that VRET can support fear reduction in some individuals with specific phobias7,24. Baseline sessions, in which three participants explored the height environment alone (self-led exposure), provided stable measures for participants P1 and P2 but were unstable for P3, underscoring the variability often observed in SCED research64. Such variability may reflect individual differences in fear responding and highlights the importance of tailoring exposure intensity and duration10.

Two participants (P2 and P3) displayed immediate increases in maximum floor reached and reductions in distress during the intervention, while the third participant (P1) showed delayed gains. These patterns mirror findings from previous VRET studies, in which some individuals require multiple sessions for fear reduction11,15,37,44. Descriptive participant-level analyses suggested improvements across phases. Participant notes and comments suggested that sharing the virtual space with a therapist was experienced as supportive by some participants and may help contextualize their willingness to continue exposure. The observed behavioral changes may reflect several possible exposure-related processes, but the present design does not allow conclusions regarding specific mechanisms9. Therapist-guided sessions may facilitate such learning by providing real-time encouragement, modeling approach behaviors, and prompting grounding and breathing techniques. These strategies were not used as safety behaviors but to maintain engagement with the feared stimulus, consistent with recommendations for enhancing inhibitory learning42.

P1’s temporary decline during the intervention phase may partly reflect an underestimation of the emotional realism of the VR environment. Supplementary interview data provide context for P1’s temporary decline: P1 described the height simulation as stronger and more somatically activating than expected, which may have contributed to reduced willingness to ascend higher during early intervention sessions. This highlights the need for more detailed pre-exposure preparation, including explicit discussion of the potential intensity, realism, and bodily impact of VR exposure.

Therapist-guided sessions differed from baseline across several active components, including therapist involvement, real-time guidance, and physiological feedback, and the relative contribution of these elements cannot be isolated in the present design. The therapist’s presence in the virtual environment may have contributed to these effects. Consistent with research showing that social support and perceived presence can reduce fear responses in VR5,26, our results suggest greater tolerance of height exposure and lower anxiety during therapist-led sessions relative to baseline. Participant reports suggested that therapist involvement was perceived as supportive during exposure, which is also shown in the existing literature14,15. However, these reports were descriptive and were not analyzed using a formal qualitative framework. These findings suggest that therapist involvement can remain relevant in technology-mediated exposure settings, although the present study did not directly examine therapeutic alliance. The CIVE allowed therapists to join the virtual scene, model approach behaviors, and adjust exposure intensity dynamically based on participants’ verbal and physiological cues. Supplementary participant notes are consistent with this interpretation, but should be treated as contextual rather than confirmatory evidence. Therapist-supported coping strategies, such as grounding and box breathing, were repeatedly mentioned as helpful. Although grounding, box-breathing, and similar strategies may function as safety behaviors if used to suppress anxiety, in the present study they were used only to help participants remain engaged with the feared stimulus and prevent premature escape. Evidence suggests that safety behaviors do not necessarily interfere with exposure outcomes, and some authors have argued for their judicious use when they support treatment tolerability and continued engagement with feared stimuli88,89.

Physiological data provide further nuance. Because HR–height coupling was explored post hoc rather than specified as a primary theoretical construct, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Real-time HR monitoring enabled the therapist to titrate exposure intensity and offered feedback on arousal. Cross-correlation analyses suggested heterogeneous patterns of HR–height coupling across participants. P2 showed a statistically significant reduction in coupling between elevator height and HR, whereas P3 showed a similar but non-significant trend. In contrast, P1 showed a Tau-U coefficient in the opposite direction, suggesting increased rather than reduced HR–height coupling during the intervention phase. Although reduced stimulus–response coupling could theoretically indicate changes in how physiological responses track threat cues during exposure, this interpretation would require confirmation in future research using larger samples and broader autonomic measures. These findings could therefore be interpreted as exploratory indicators of possible changes in physiological reactivity, which could be consistent with findings that decoupling can indicate habituation and inhibitory learning21,39. Some participants also reported reduced sweating and quicker self-stabilization in later sessions. Reduced coupling across sessions could reflect altered physiological reactivity to the height stimulus.

Notably, cybersickness remained low across sessions, suggesting feasibility and acceptability of the CIVE setup82. The CEQ findings also suggest that our therapist-led CIVE intervention was perceived as credible and acceptable.

The integration of physiological feedback into the VR environment further enhances therapists’ ability to deliver individualized support, consistent with proposals that biofeedback enriches exposure interventions64,65. Moreover, the collaborative nature of the environment may support new types of therapeutic interaction, such as modelling approach behaviors and providing immediate reinforcement, which are difficult to achieve when therapists are restricted to external observation.

The observed symptom reductions should be interpreted as the possible result of a multicomponent therapeutic approach rather than the effect of therapist presence alone. The intervention combined several active elements: repeated exposure to height-related stimuli in an immersive VR environment, therapist-guided support during exposure, real-time adjustment of task difficulty, and the integration of HR monitoring into the therapeutic process. Improvements observed in P2 and P3, and delayed gains in P1, may therefore reflect the combined contribution of exposure learning, the controlled and repeatable VR setting, therapist guidance, and physiological feedback.

In general, the present findings should be interpreted cautiously. Given the very small sample size and variability across participants, the study should be considered exploratory. Although two participants showed improvements in maximum floor reached, subjective distress, or physiological indicators during the intervention phase, these effects were not uniform across cases or outcomes. The results suggest that therapist-guided VRET in a CIVE may be beneficial for some individuals and warrants further investigation in larger controlled studies. Observed improvements should be interpreted relative to the self-guided baseline phase rather than as evidence of absolute treatment efficacy.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the SCED design and the very small sample size limit causal inference and external validity. Findings from the present study should therefore be considered exploratory and cannot be generalized beyond the observed cases, even though SCEDs are valuable for early-stage intervention development and within-participant analyses89,90.

Second, participants were recruited from the general population (non-clinical sample) and did not undergo a structured clinical diagnostic interview. Therefore, findings may be most appropriately interpreted as applying to individuals with fear of heights or elevated acrophobic symptoms rather than clinically diagnosed acrophobia.

Third, the experimental design does not allow clear separation of intervention effects. All participants completed the intervention phase only after the baseline phase, meaning that therapist-guided sessions were always preceded by unguided exposure. Observed changes therefore cannot be attributed solely to therapist guidance, as they may also partly reflect prior exposure, habituation, familiarization, or practice effects. In addition, the intervention combined several active elements, including repeated exposure to height-related stimuli in an immersive VR environment, therapist-guided support, real-time task adjustment, and the integration of HR monitoring into the therapeutic process. The relative contribution of these components cannot be disentangled in the present design. Similarly, although the therapist’s guidance may have been helpful, this design cannot distinguish its effects from those of general therapist support.

Fourth, baseline stability was not fully consistent across participants. Staggered baseline lengths were relatively short, particularly for P3, reducing baseline stability and increasing susceptibility to maturation effects. Several baseline series also showed variability or trend before the intervention phase was introduced, which reduces confidence that subsequent changes can be attributed solely to the intervention.

Several additional methodological limitations should also be noted. Participants knew they were taking part in an experimental study, which may have influenced expectancy and engagement. Because participants had no prior experience with height-related VR applications, novelty and familiarization effects may have influenced early baseline responses. A further limitation is that all participants received psychoeducation before baseline data collection. This may have influenced expectations, coping attempts, or willingness to approach height stimuli during baseline sessions. Therefore, the baseline phase should be interpreted as unguided exposure after psychoeducation rather than as a fully uncontaminated measure of spontaneous fear responding. The avatar implementation was relatively simple and was not designed to systematically manipulate or assess embodiment, self-perception, or avatar realism; therefore, conclusions regarding embodiment-related mechanisms cannot be drawn.

The study also relied partly on self-report measures, which may be susceptible to response bias, although physiological data provided an additional source of information. Some questionnaire measures relied on translated versions without independent validation in Czech/Slovak samples, which may affect measurement reliability and comparability with previous studies. Because measures were administered using non-validated Czech translations, questionnaire findings should be interpreted primarily as repeated within-person indicators rather than precise psychometric estimates. Such measurement constraints are not uncommon in exploratory SCED pilot studies; however, they nevertheless limit interpretability and generalizability of questionnaire-based findings. Therapeutic alliance was not formally measured, therapist perceptions were not systematically collected, and the intervention was delivered by a single therapist; therefore, no conclusions can be drawn about alliance processes or therapist-level effects.

Physiological conclusions were based primarily on HR indices rather than broader autonomic measures such as HRV, and baseline HR estimates may have been influenced by conversational interaction prior to sessions. In addition, although HR was monitored across all phases, only during the intervention phase was this information actively integrated into therapeutic guidance. Therefore, the intervention effect likely reflects a combination of therapist-guided VRET and the use of real-time physiological information, rather than therapist guidance alone.

Follow-up sessions were compressed into a single week, which limits conclusions regarding longer-term maintenance. Future studies should include more extended and temporally distributed follow-up assessments.

Another limitation concerns therapist observations, which were recorded by the treating therapist and therefore represent a non-independent, potentially biased data source. As these observations were not formally coded or independently verified, they should be interpreted as contextual rather than objective measures.

Finally, therapist presence in VR may increase cognitive and technical demands, as the therapist must guide exposure, monitor participant responses, manage the VR environment, and respond to technical issues simultaneously.

Despite these limitations, this exploratory study contributes to an emerging body of work showing that therapist-guided VRET in shared virtual environments (such as CIVEs) may enhance engagement, support inhibitory learning, and promote both behavioral and physiological adaptation for individuals with fear of heights.

Future research should compare therapist-led CIVE interventions with traditional therapist-outside formats and fully automated programs, while also including measures of therapeutic alliance and engagement. A promising direction would be to explore therapist-led CIVE-based exposure combined with serious-game elements and biofeedback. Serious-game components may increase engagement, motivation, and repeated practice, while biofeedback can make physiological arousal more visible and usable during exposure89,90. Additionally, exploring other physiological markers (e.g., skin conductance, respiration) and their real-time integration could further optimize exposure titration. Extending this approach to other anxiety and phobia-spectrum disorders and group formats may broaden the utility of immersive collaborative therapy. The present findings suggest that therapist-guided VRET in a shared virtual environment may represent a feasible and potentially useful direction for further investigation, but larger controlled studies are needed before conclusions regarding effectiveness can be drawn.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this exploratory SCED study suggests that therapist-guided VRET delivered within a CIVE may be associated with greater exposure progress and lower subjective distress for some individuals with fear of heights when compared with the self-guided baseline phase. The intervention combined several active components, including repeated VR exposure, therapist support, shared interaction within the CIVE, and physiological monitoring. Descriptive participant-level analyses suggested greater tolerance of height exposure and lower subjective distress during therapist-guided sessions in some participants, with follow-up data indicating possible maintenance of some gains. Exploratory HR–height coupling analyses showed participant-specific patterns and were not statistically significant across all participants. However, HR responses tended to become less tightly coupled to height exposure in two participants. The present findings complement existing evidence suggesting that VRET may support fear reduction in some individuals. Together, our findings indicate that multicomponent therapist-guided VRET delivered in a shared virtual environment such as a CIVE may represent a feasible and potentially useful direction for further investigation in fear-of-heights treatment. However, given the limited sample size, non-clinical population, multicomponent intervention design, and heterogeneous responses, larger controlled studies are needed before conclusions regarding effectiveness can be drawn.
