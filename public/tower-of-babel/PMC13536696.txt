The role of problem-solving and decision-making skills in linking critical thinking dispositions to perceptions of success
Authors: Ahmet Oğuz Akçay, Engin Karahan, Ahmet Kara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs educational priorities increasingly emphasize higher-order cognitive skills over rote memorization, it has become essential to clarify how these competencies interact in young learners. To address this, the current study examines how primary school students’ problem-solving and decision-making skills mediate the relationship between their critical thinking dispositions and self-perceptions of success.MethodThe sample consisted of 285 third- and fourth-grade students in Türkiye, aged 8 to 10 years, who were selected based on their enrollment status, absence of psychiatric diagnoses, and voluntary participation. Data were collected in person using paper-based surveys. Four instruments were administered: the Critical Thinking Dispositions Scale, the Problem-Solving Inventory for Children, the Decision-Making Skills Scale, and the Perception of Success Scale. Direct and indirect relationships between variables were assessed using path analysis. Bootstrapping procedures determined the statistical significance of the mediating effects.ResultsThe indirect effects of decision-making and problem-solving skills on the relationship between critical thinking disposition and perception of success were statistically significant [β = .20, SE = .05 (95% CI = .095, .308)]. The results indicate that students with advanced critical thinking, problem-solving, and decision-making skills are more likely to perceive themselves as academically successful.ConclusionThe study confirms that critical thinking, problem-solving, and decision-making are interconnected competencies that significantly predict students’ perceptions of success. These results support educational initiatives that prioritize higher-order cognitive abilities over basic knowledge retention. Ultimately, educators are encouraged to implement comprehensive curricula that simultaneously develop these skills to prepare students for real-world challenges.

Introduction

In today’s rapidly changing world, the skills that are essential for success are constantly evolving. Advances in information and communication technology are profoundly influencing social, economic, and political structures. The ongoing development of education systems requires aligning education with current transformations. Education should equip students with competencies aligned with these transformations, as students are the primary constituents of these systems. Acquiring advanced skills is essential for individuals to adapt to these changes effectively. These skills include technological proficiency, effective decision-making, information selection, analysis, utilization, and integration into various aspects of daily life, encompassing social and professional domains [9].

There has been a longstanding and robust critique of the pedagogical methods employed in education. Both Clement [22] and Norman [68] posited that it is more advantageous to focus on instructing students in the art of thinking and learning rather than merely imparting specific content knowledge and expecting them to absorb it. Ellerton [31] also emphasized that thinking skills function as catalysts apart from content knowledge, and emphasizing these processes offers a significant edge in transferring knowledge to new situations. According to several scholars [58, 80, 103], there is a prevailing argument that the present educational inclination towards standardizing curricula and prioritizing test scores has a detrimental impact on educators’ capacity to foster higher-order thinking skills within the classroom.

The ongoing inconsistency in research findings on the most effective instructional methods for developing critical thinking requires updated studies to improve and modernize our understanding of teaching [1]. This need is highlighted by the lack of formal recognition, which often leads to “vague application” in which implementation depends on individual interpretation without standardized guidance [4]. Consequently, while traditional didactic methods focusing on rote memorization fail to cultivate critical thinking, inquiry-based approaches actively engage students in the higher-order thinking skills necessary for cognitive development [88]. Indeed, recent empirical evidence demonstrates that inquiry-based learning significantly outperforms conventional teaching, which frequently fails to produce statistically significant progress in developing students’ critical thinking dispositions [8]. To bridge this gap, scholars advocate for innovative pedagogical practices and learner-centered methods that explicitly integrate content with activities to foster critical thinking skills [70]. However, practitioners still encounter significant methodological challenges in selecting and designing effective techniques [91]. Despite valuing critical thinking, many teachers’ practices remain incoherent and traditional because they lack specific training in transitioning from delivering declarative knowledge to facilitating procedural inquiry [60].

Literature review

Contemporary education increasingly emphasizes cultivating self-reliant individuals capable of addressing problems from diverse perspectives. In this context, cultivating individuals who can effectively access information, solve problems, exhibit diverse perspectives, engage in critical thinking, and reflect metacognitively is of utmost significance. Critical thinking, a cognitive skill, is particularly prominent among the various skills under consideration. Various definitions of critical thinking exist, with the American Psychological Association [5] defining it as a form of purposeful thinking focused on problem-solving. In this process, individuals evaluate ideas or potential solutions to identify any errors or limitations they may possess.

Despite debate over whether critical thinking is a general skill, a domain-specific ability, or a moral trait, the Delphi Report’s consensus definition describes it as a “purposeful, self-regulatory judgment” [35]. Although analysis, evaluation, and inference are the core cognitive components of critical thinking [28], these skills need to be combined with a personal disposition, or an innate tendency to be open-minded and systematic [32, 97]. Therefore, the ability to think critically is inherently linked to the willingness to apply those skills in practice [56]. Bloom’s Taxonomy [17] and its revision [7] provide the operational vocabulary for defining how critical thinking is performed. While Bloom’s lower levels (knowledge/remembering and comprehension/understanding) are viewed as prerequisites, the upper levels are often considered synonymous with critical thinking itself [26].

Building on this, it is worth noting that critical thinking encompasses two distinct components, namely critical thinking skills and critical thinking dispositions, as highlighted by Duru et al. [27]. According to Facione [34], the various subcomponents of critical thinking skills include analyzing, interpreting, self-regulating, inferring, explaining, and evaluating. In contrast, critical thinking dispositions refer to an individual’s inherent inclination to engage in critical thinking, such as questioning, evaluating alternative perspectives, and actively seeking supporting evidence [35]. Consequently, individuals must cultivate critical thinking dispositions within this framework. According to Kayman (2022), it is imperative for individuals with critical thinking skills to cultivate a critical thinking disposition, thus enabling them to apply this skill effectively in appropriate contexts. Upon examination of the literature, it becomes evident that studies on critical thinking disposition focus on various age groups. Several studies have been conducted involving various groups, including teachers [29, 53], academicians [47], undergraduate students [25], and middle and high school students [21, 48].

Much like critical thinking, decision-making is a crucial, often unconscious, cognitive process that significantly impacts daily life. According to Baysal [13], decision-making involves identifying and delineating options, establishing relevant criteria, and ultimately selecting the most suitable option by applying these criteria. The ability to make decisions is closely linked to various skills, including problem-solving, research and analysis, critical thinking, and creative thinking. These skills are actively employed during decision-making [62]. According to Phillips et al. [74], there is a correlation between problem-solving skills and the decision-making styles individuals employ. Furthermore, a study by Cenkseven and Önder [20] found that individuals who possess strong decision-making abilities and are satisfied with their decisions tend to exhibit higher levels of life satisfaction. Decision-making involves selecting an option and assuming accountability for the chosen action with outcomes directly impacting the individuals.

Alongside decision-making, there is a growing demand for exceptional problem-solving abilities [79]. Problem-solving is the cognitive process an individual undertakes to resolve a complex situation or overcome a difficulty encountered for the first time [16]. The scope of problem-solving should not be limited solely to mathematical problems [40], but should also encompass the cognitive processes employed in addressing challenges encountered in everyday situations [54]. According to the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), problem-solving competence is an individual’s ability to employ cognitive processes to comprehend and resolve problems that lack an immediately apparent solution method [69]. Problem-solving is considered a cognitive process of high-level thinking [37]. Bransford and Stein [19] argue that critical and creative thinking skills are fundamental to problem-solving. Educational programs emphasize cultivating problem-solving skills, as this multifaceted competency encompasses cognitive abilities such as critical thinking, decision-making, and reasoning. The acquisition of problem-solving skills is widely recognized as a crucial competency for today’s children, as it is essential for their success in various educational and non-educational settings [76].

Relationships among higher-order thinking skills

Previous studies investigating the relationship between decision-making styles and problem-solving skills have consistently yielded similar findings, suggesting that decision-making plays a crucial role in predicting an individual’s problem-solving abilities [14, 105]. Snyder and Snyder [88] argue that a similar relationship exists between critical thinking and problem-solving abilities.

Success can be defined as the attainment of a desired objective within a predetermined timeframe [83]. While hundreds of distinct variables exert an influence on student achievement [98], one of the foremost factors to consider is the student’s own perception of success [49]. While specific determinants of academic achievement can be attributed to individual students, others are influenced by external factors such as familial and environmental engagement [38]. The formation of students’ perceptions of success is influenced by their life experiences [102] and personal factors such as socioeconomic status, beliefs, values, and cultural background [77, 92]. In the present context, it can be posited that perceptions of success will be influenced by advanced cognitive abilities, such as critical thinking, decision-making, and problem-solving.

Learning involves acquiring knowledge in various domains, including cognitive, emotional, and psychomotor components. Expertise is cultivated through the amalgamation of ability, knowledge, and experience [18]. Because contemporary education systems have shifted from prioritizing isolated basic knowledge to real-world application, there is a notable emphasis on cultivating higher-order thinking skills [71]. The United Nations Educational, Scientific, and Cultural Organization [96] has underscored the need for educational systems to prioritize problem-solving, critical thinking, and decision-making. Hence, individuals’ perception of success is intricately linked to cognitive processes that involve problem-solving (the act of addressing a recognized obstacle), decision-making (the process of selecting the most optimal alternative), and critical thinking (the ability to comprehend a specific meaning) [75].

Research hypotheses and theoretical background

Primary schools play a crucial role in fostering fundamental concepts and skills [41]. Hence, it is imperative to equip primary school pupils with problem-solving, critical thinking, and decision-making abilities to successfully attain their learning objectives and navigate future complex challenges [43, 51, 78, 106]. At this point, it is essential to distinguish between critical thinking dispositions and skills. While critical thinking skills refer to the cognitive proficiency and technical ability to analyze and evaluate information, critical thinking dispositions represent the consistent internal motivation, spirit, and habitual tendency to employ those skills [36]. This study specifically focuses on dispositions as the foundational person factor that drives subsequent behavioral manifestations.

Problem-solving is commonly regarded as the initial phase of higher-order thinking (Alkhatib, 2019). Individuals must possess advanced critical thinking skills to effectively solve problems through critical reflection [63]. Hence, individuals with strong critical thinking skills and dispositions are apt to exhibit proficiency in problem-solving [71, 88] equipping them to effectively address social, scientific, and practical challenges [86].

This interconnected process is best understood through the lens of Bandura’s [11] Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), which posits that personal, behavioral, and environmental factors continuously interact (triadic reciprocal determinism). Within SCT, problem-solving and decision-making are viewed as closely related self-regulatory behaviors. SCT suggests that a single dispositional factor, such as an individual’s critical thinking disposition (a personal-cognitive characteristic), can activate multiple regulatory responses simultaneously. This provides a clear rationale for using a parallel mediation model. As students utilize their critical thinking dispositions to develop more effective problem-solving and decision-making strategies, these mastery experiences boost their self-efficacy and outcome expectations [10]. Consequently, these behavioral manifestations directly enhance the students’ ultimate environmental and motivational outcome: their perception of success. Therefore:

Primary education in Türkiye covers grades 1 to 4 and is delivered predominantly through public schools governed by a centralized curriculum. Within this system, the curriculum aims to provide students with foundational knowledge while also fostering twenty-first century skills such as collaboration, critical thinking, creativity, and communication [15]. However, recent studies indicate that while learning and innovation skills are emphasized, competencies related to information, media, and technology remain underrepresented [104]. By situating this study in a Turkish primary school context, we aim to contribute to the international literature by highlighting both the strengths and the gaps of the current curriculum in fostering twenty-first century skills.

This study aims to investigate the effect of critical thinking disposition on perceptions of success through problem-solving and decision-making abilities. This process can be understood in the context of the Social Cognitive Theory model, which depicts the interaction of personal-cognitive, behavioral, and environmental factors. Methods

Procedure and research group

This study was conducted with 3rd and 4th-grade students attending primary school, which constitutes the first stage of compulsory education in Türkiye. Primary school education in Türkiye lasts four years, with students generally starting at the age of six. Typically, students are 8–9 years old by the conclusion of the 3rd grade and 9–10 years old by the end of the 4th grade. This age group was selected primarily because essential cognitive and self-regulatory structures, including critical thinking dispositions, problem-solving and decision-making skills, and perceptions of success, typically start to develop during this specific developmental stage. Furthermore, the fact that students at these grade levels have achieved a level of reading comprehension that enables them to answer self-report-based assessment tools reliably also supports this choice. Beyond these developmental considerations, it is also important to situate the study within the broader structure and priorities of the Turkish primary education system. In Türkiye, primary education covers grades 1 to 4 and is provided predominantly by public schools. The curriculum is centrally determined by the Ministry of National Education, and recent reforms have emphasized the development of 21st-century skills such as problem-solving and critical thinking. By focusing on fourth-grade students in a public school in the northwestern region of the country, this study contributes to the growing need for context-specific evidence on mediating effects in early education. Out of the total sample size of 285 third (age 8–9 years) and fourth (age 9–10 years) grade primary school students included in the study, 147 individuals (51.6%) identified as female, 133 individuals (46.7%) identified as male, and five individuals (1.8%) did not provide information regarding their gender. Criterion sampling was used to determine which individuals would be included in the study. The inclusion criteria for this study included individuals who met the following conditions: being enrolled as a primary school student in Türkiye, having no documented psychiatric diagnoses, and expressing a willingness to participate voluntarily.

The data collection process began with obtaining the necessary permissions from the relevant ethics committee. Following this, contact was made with primary schools affiliated with the Ministry of National Education and their administrations. The purpose and process of the research were explained to the students in language appropriate to their age, and it was emphasized that participation was entirely voluntary. Informed consent was also obtained from the students and their parents. After obtaining consent, the scales were administered in the classroom under the supervision of the researchers during class hours. Data were collected through face-to-face interactions with volunteer students using a traditional paper-and-pencil questionnaire format.

Power analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics to assess the statistical robustness and sensitivity of the study. For all tested hypotheses (H1–H6), statistical power was calculated based on a sample size of N = 285 and a significance level of α = 0.05. The analysis indicated that, for observed effect sizes ranging from partial correlations of 0.31 to 0.64, statistical power reached the maximum value of 1.000. For the multiple mediation model (H6) with three predictors, power also remained at 1.000 for the total effect of 0.50. These findings indicate that the sample size is sufficient to detect the hypothesized effects with high precision, surpassing the conventional threshold of 0.80 and minimizing the risk of Type II errors [23].

Measures

In this study, the face validity of the items in the Critical Thinking Dispositions Scale, Problem- Solving Inventory for Children, Decision-Making Skills Scale, and Perception of Success Scale was examined to determine whether they accurately represented the intended constructs. For this purpose, the conceptual consistency, linguistic clarity, and compatibility of the scale items with the intended construct were evaluated by field experts. Based on field experts’ evaluations, the necessary revisions were made to ensure the scales’ face validity. This process aligns with Allen et al. [3], who emphasized that it constitutes proof of the face validity of the measurement instruments.

One of the main reasons for selecting the “Critical Thinking Dispositions Scale”, “Problem-Solving Inventory for Children”, “Decision-Making Skills Scale”, and “Perception of Success Scale” in this study is that psychometric evidence exists indicating their validity and reliability. Secondly, these scales are appropriate to the developmental characteristics of primary school students. Thirdly, these measurement tools evaluate the constructs under consideration holistically. Finally, they demonstrate a higher fit with the theoretical framework of the model constructed in this research.

Critical Thinking Dispositions Scale (CTDS): CTDS was developed by Uluçınar and Akar [95], who subsequently conducted validity and reliability assessments. CTDS comprises 18 items organized into four distinct dimensions. These dimensions have been classified as Skepticism, Curiosity, Open-mindedness, and Biasness. Item examples of CTDS: “When I hear a friend express ideas that differ from mine, I try to understand their perspective by putting myself in their shoes” and “My friends, family and teacher say that I am a curious person.” In the present study, critical thinking dispositions were incorporated into the path analysis model by treating them as observed variables and using them as a measure of the total scale scores. The confirmatory factor analysis results of the CTDS indicate that the goodness of fit values, namely RMSEA = 0.03, CFI = 0.94, IFI = 0.94, and TLI = 0.94, are deemed to be within an acceptable range [95]. According to Uluçınar and Akar [95], Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient for the entire CTDS was 0.74. Problem-Solving Inventory for Children (PSIC): PSIC was created by Serin et al. [84], who subsequently performed analyses to assess its validity and reliability. PSIC comprises 24 items organized into three distinct dimensions. Item examples of PSIC: “Instead of running away from my problems, I try to face and solve them”, “I try to stay calm when I encounter problems.” In this research, the problem-solving skills variable was operationalized as an observed construct and incorporated into the model using path analysis, including the total scale scores. According to the findings of Serin et al. [84], the exploratory factor analysis revealed that the total explained variance for PSIC was 42.26%. Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient for the entire PSIC was 0.80 [84]. The present study found that Cronbach’s alpha for the entire PSIC was 0.79, as indicated in Table 1.

Decision-Making Skills Scale (DMSS): DMSS was formulated by Sever and Ersoy [85]. Furthermore, investigations were carried out to assess the validity and reliability of the data. The DMSS scale comprises 15 items that collectively measure a single dimension. Item examples of DMSS: “I consider the positive and negative aspects of the options when making a decision”, “I think about how to implement my decision.” The percentage of variance accounted for in the exploratory factor analysis of the DMSS was 40.07%. Furthermore, the study conducted a confirmatory factor analysis to assess the goodness of fit indices of DMSS, which were within acceptable ranges (RMSEA = 0.07,CFI = 0.97; NFI = 0.93; NNFI = 0.96) [85]. The reliability coefficient for the entire DMSS, measured by Cronbach’s alpha, was 0.89 [85]. The Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient for the entire DMSS was 0.86, as shown in Table 1.

Perception of Success Scale (PSS): PSS was developed by Kara and Şahin [49], who subsequently conducted validity and reliability assessments. PSS consists of a total of 24 items that are organized into three distinct dimensions. Item examples of PSS: “Success is about being willing to learn.”, “Success is knowing your responsibilities.” The study employed a path analysis model in which the observed variable of success perception was used, and measurement was conducted on the total scale scores. The exploratory factor analysis conducted by the PSS revealed that the total explained variance amounted to 45.20% [49]. Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient for the entire PSS, yielding a value of 0.86 [49]. The present study ascertained the Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient of the complete PSS to be 0.92, as indicated in Table 1.

Data analysis

The present study initially examined normality (kurtosis, skewness) and multicollinearity (VIF, tolerance, conditional index, and bivariate correlations). This analysis was conducted as part of the preliminary analysis phase, drawing upon the works of Kline [52] and Tabachnick and Fidell [90]. Upon examining the underlying assumptions, the path analysis methodology was employed to unveil the causal relationships among the observed variables [52]. The path model was evaluated using several goodness-of-fit indices, including χ2/df, NFI, CFI, GFI, AGFI, and RMSEA [81]. Furthermore, the mediation analysis followed the procedural guidelines outlined by Baron and Kenny [12]. The researchers employed bootstrapping analysis to examine the statistical significance of indirect effects, conducting 1000 resampling iterations. Furthermore, the determination of lower and upper-bound confidence intervals was conducted as described by Hayes [42]. According to Shrout and Bolger [87], if the confidence intervals do not include zero, the indirect effects are statistically significant.

Findings

Preliminary analysis

The initial analysis phase was conducted as the first step in this investigation. In this study, the researchers first assessed the normality assumption and then examined multicollinearity. Within the context of the normality assumption, the current study exhibited skewness values ranging from −0.47 to 0.19 and kurtosis values ranging from 0.14 to 3.98 (see Table 1). The findings of this study lead to the conclusion that, with the exception of the kurtosis value of the observed variable related to problem-solving skills, all other values are within the accepted range of normality [90].

Secondly, this analysis’s VIF, tolerance, and conditional index values for multicollinearity fell within the ranges of 1.11–1.79, 0.55–0.89, and 1.00–20.62, respectively. Table 1 also displays the observed bivariate correlation coefficients, which ranged from r = 0.25 to r = 0.64. These results corroborate the absence of multicollinearity in the current investigation [52].

The model, from critical thinking disposition to perception of success, was tested in the second stage, with decision-making and problem-solving skills included as mediating variables. Bootstrapping analysis (Significance of Indirect Effects)

The mediation test revealed that problem-solving and decision-making skills partially parallel multiple mediating the relationship between critical thinking dispositions and primary school students’ perceptions of success. However, bootstrapping analysis was used to determine whether these partial mediation effects were statistically significant. In this case, 1000 resamplings were performed, and lower and upper-bound confidence intervals were calculated [42].

Discussion

Modern education systems have shifted their focus from basic knowledge retention to developing higher-order cognitive skills, including critical thinking, problem-solving, and decision-making [71, 82]. Cultivating these competencies is increasingly emphasized in educational policy and practice as instrumental for achieving academic objectives across diverse disciplines [33]. This study examined the mediating effects of problem-solving and decision-making skills on the association between critical thinking dispositions and perceptions of success among primary school students. The data analysis provided empirical support for the proposed hypotheses, though the strength and consistency of effects varied across measures. Bandura’s [11] Social Cognitive Theory offers a framework for understanding this finding. According to the theory, personal factors, behavioral effects, and interaction effects all contribute to the formation of individual personality. In the context of this study, critical thinking states are personal factors that involve both academic and methodological concerns. These states influence how people approach problems and learning, which in turn affects their decision-making and problem-solving behaviors [10]. Such behaviors are central to Social Cognitive Theory and help to develop academic success at the national level. The perception of academic success is based on self-efficacy, which is defined as a person’s belief in their ability to achieve specific goals within a given domain. Higher-order cognitive abilities, including critical thinking, problem-solving, and decision-making, are increasingly recognized as essential drivers of perceived personal success [2, 46],therefore, this study emphasizes a comprehensive approach that leverages the deep interconnectedness among these four dimensions.

The study’s findings indicate a significant positive relationship between critical thinking and decision-making skills. The significance of critical thinking in facilitating effective decision-making arises from its essential role in the process of logical reasoning and the formation of informed assumptions by individuals [45, 50, 72]. Therefore, it is imperative to offer students opportunities to enhance their critical thinking and decision-making skills in both academic and social contexts, which are directly applicable to real-life scenarios [30]. Given the complementary nature of these two competencies, providing students with real-life problem-solving experiences has significant potential to improve these skills exponentially.

Besides having critical thinking skills, individuals in this study also demonstrated a correlation between their decision-making and problem-solving abilities. Previous studies investigating the relationship between decision-making styles and problem-solving skills have consistently yielded similar findings, suggesting that decision-making plays a crucial role in predicting an individual’s problem-solving abilities [14, 105]. Snyder and Snyder [88] argue that a similar relationship exists between critical thinking and problem-solving abilities. The findings indicate a significant positive relationship between critical thinking and problem-solving skills. Critical thinking is a cognitive process characterized by meticulous, exacting reasoning employed in problem-solving [64]. Hence, applying critical thinking skills is integral throughout the various problem-solving phases.

Moreover, individuals with advanced critical thinking skills and inclinations are more likely to excel in problem-solving [71]. Researchers highlighted the correlation between possessing a strong critical-thinking disposition and enhanced problem-solving abilities [94, 99, 101]. The present study aligns with the existing literature by establishing a correlation between primary school students’ critical thinking abilities and their subsequent problem-solving aptitude. Hence, this research emphasizes the importance of employing critical thinking and decision-making methodologies to develop problem-solving skills, aligning with the central objectives of contemporary educational reforms.

The research results show a significant, positive relationship between critical thinking and perceptions of success. Researchers mentioned that there is a relationship between students’ critical thinking skills and their academic achievement [61, 100]. Furthermore, critical thinking skills have the potential to enhance students’ cognitive abilities, enabling them to engage in more effective thinking processes, form sound judgments, draw logical conclusions, and improve overall problem-solving proficiency [93]. The study by Amin et al. [6] found a positive correlation between students’ critical thinking abilities and their academic performance. Numerous studies in the literature have concluded that a significant correlation exists between critical thinking skills or dispositions and academic achievement [24, 39]. Li et al, [59] highlighted the importance of critical thinking for students’ academic success.

The data analysis shows a significant, positive relationship among problem-solving skills, decision-making, and the perception of success. The impact of problem-solving and decision-making skills on student perception of success has been recognized as crucial, as highlighted by Heppner and Peterson (1982) as cited in Kuh et al., [57]. This is particularly relevant when considering the goals outlined in the school curriculum (Ministry of National Education [MoNE], [66, 67]). Hence, it is reasonable to assert that the suppositions positing a correlation between enhanced higher-order thinking skills and success in educational and professional contexts are justifiable.

The findings indicate a statistically significant, positive correlation between individuals’ decision-making abilities and their perceptions of success. According to Şeker and Yeşilyaprak [89], there is a strong correlation between individuals who perceive themselves as highly successful and those who possess high vocational decision-making competencies. Furthermore, Hemmingsen [44] found a positive correlation between higher academic achievement and greater vocational decision-making competence among students. Students who aspire to succeed demonstrate a heightened sense of responsibility, actively engage in problem-solving by gathering relevant information, and approach decision-making with curiosity and sensitivity as they contemplate various possibilities for their future endeavors.

The current study found evidence of an indirect effect, demonstrating that problem-solving and decision-making skills partially mediate the association between critical thinking skills and the perception of success among primary school students. It also indicates a significant relationship between critical thinking and perception of success, mediated by problem-solving and decision-making skills. Ultimately, these findings highlight the highly intertwined relationship among these core cognitive skills. Understanding this pathway is particularly significant today, as cultivating a critical thinking disposition is necessary to address the younger generation’s lack of awareness regarding potential future challenges [54].

When analyzed through the lens of the tripartite reciprocal determinism principle in Social Cognitive Theory [11], the research findings demonstrate a complex interplay among individual cognitive factors, behavioral outcomes (e.g., problem-solving), and self-perception, specifically the perception of success. According to the theoretical framework, students’ self-efficacy beliefs serve as a primary motivator influencing their behavior. The observed discrepancy between perceived success and actual performance in this study highlights Bandura’s [10] emphasis on the importance of self-regulation. Discrepancies often exist between students’ perceptions of success and their actual academic proficiency. From a practical standpoint, these findings suggest that classroom instruction must shift from a focus on correct answers to metacognitive reflection. To bridge the gap between perceived and actual success, teachers should incorporate think-aloud protocols and reflection journals, requiring students to justify their decision-making steps during problem-solving. By encouraging students to evaluate how they reached a solution, rather than just what the solution is, educators can mitigate the illusion of knowing. Such interventions align students’ outcome expectations with their true capabilities, fostering the healthy self-regulation and mastery experiences emphasized by Social Cognitive Theory [10].

Recent reform documents [73, 96] within education emphasize the significance of higher-order cognitive abilities, such as critical thinking, decision-making, and problem-solving, as crucial factors for success in contemporary society. This research elucidates the correlation between these specific skills and students’ perceptions of success, indicating that possessing these skills leads students to perceive themselves as successful in academic settings. Hence, it offers a significant perspective on the rationale behind cultivating students’ proficiency in these essential skills.

Study limitations, recommendations, and directions for future research

This study was conducted with a sample of primary school students from a single geographical region, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. A primary limitation of this study is its reliance on self-report scales and intuitive assessment methods. Primary school students often use mental shortcuts when evaluating their own thinking, which increases the risk of the “illusion of explanatory depth” described earlier. As a result, students’ self-assessments of critical thinking or problem-solving skills tend to reflect their confidence rather than their actual abilities. Additionally, since the research used a cross-sectional correlational survey, the identified mediating roles should be viewed as statistical associations rather than proof of causality.

This study observed that students frequently overestimate their conceptual understanding, a phenomenon known as the “illusion of knowing” or the “illusion of explanatory depth” [65]. Without consistent cognitive monitoring, students may fail to notice errors and develop an inflated sense of self-efficacy that does not align with their actual performance [55].

While this study is based on Social Cognitive Theory, it focuses almost exclusively on the internal cognitive and behavioral processes of students, and environmental factors such as the socio-economic context of schools, or the teaching approaches used in classrooms were not included in the analysis; future research could integrate these environmental factors to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how students form perceptions of success. Additionally, although anonymity was stressed during the data collection process, the use of self-report measures with primary school students may introduce social desirability bias; that is, students may respond in ways they think their teachers or the researchers expect.

Future research can adopt mixed-method designs that include direct observation of students’ performance in applying these skills and use achievement tests. Longitudinal studies are also recommended to explore how the mediating effect of critical thinking tendencies on problem-solving develops as students’ progress through educational stages and as intuitive self-assessments become more rational. Additionally, given ongoing technological advancements, comparative analyses of students’ problem-solving strategies when interacting with artificial intelligence tools, along with their levels of illusion of knowledge during these interactions, would contribute valuable insights to the literature.
