Integrating inflammation, insulin resistance, and visceral adiposity: the C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose-Chinese visceral adiposity (CTI–CVAI) index is associated with cardiovascular disease risk across CKM stages 0–3 in a nationwide prospective cohort
Authors: Qian Wang, Jia Li, Hao Qiao, Yuelang Zhang, Ying Xu, Shuqun Zhang, Guihua Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly-stage (0–3) cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome populations require urgent cardiovascular disease (CVD) prevention. Chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and visceral adiposity are three core interactive CVD pathogenic pathways, while current biomarkers fail to integrate all three. This study aimed to explore the association of the novel three-dimensional composite index CTI–CVAI with incident CVD and its predictive value in Chinese adults with CKM stages 0–3.MethodsThis nationwide prospective cohort study used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS, 2011–2020), including 6728 participants aged ≥ 45 years with CKM stages 0–3. CTI–CVAI was calculated by combining the C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose index (CTI) and Chinese visceral adiposity index (CVAI). Cox regression, restricted cubic spline, survival analysis, ROC curves, and reclassification analysis were used to evaluate its value. Subgroup and multiple sensitivity analyses, including competing-risks models, were conducted to assess the robustness of the results.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 9 years, the overall CVD incidence was 23.83%. CTI–CVAI elevation showed a linear, dose-dependent correlation with increased CVD risk (P < 0.001). After full confounder adjustment, per 1-SD CTI–CVAI increment increased CVD risk by 32% (HR = 1.32, 95% CI 1.26–1.39, P < 0.001), and the highest quartile exhibited 2.07-fold higher risk than the lowest (HR = 2.07, 95% CI 1.77–2.42, P < 0.001). Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a linear association between CTI–CVAI and incident CVD (P for non-linearity > 0.05). CTI–CVAI demonstrated improved risk reclassification (NRI = 0.0566, P < 0.001) compared with single biomarkers. The association was stable across most subgroups. All sensitivity analyses yielded reliable, robust results.ConclusionsCTI–CVAI is independently and linearly associated with incident CVD risk in Chinese adults with CKM stage 0–3. Background

With the aging population, chronic diseases, including metabolic syndrome (MetS), cardiovascular disease (CVD), and chronic kidney disease (CKD), have evolved into urgent global health challenges [1, 2]. Given the intricate interplay among these diseases, the American Heart Association (AHA) has recently introduced the concept of cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome [3], a framework that emphasizes the bidirectional, synergistic interactions among metabolic dysfunction, CKD, and the cardiovascular system. These interactions collectively increase the risk of adverse cardiovascular events and multiple organ dysfunction, and increasing evidence confirms this relationship, making CKM syndrome a research focus in modern clinical and epidemiological practice [4, 5]. It is estimated that between 2015 and 2020, 25% of American adults suffered from chronic kidney disease [6]. Numerous epidemiological studies suggest that as CKM progresses from stage 0 to stage 3, the absolute risk of cardiovascular disease increases [7–9]. In addition, the high medical expenses caused by CKM are mainly attributed to cardiovascular disease [10]. The AHA stresses that research involving the CKM 0–3 group should prioritize preventing CVD events [9]. Therefore, identifying modifiable cardiovascular disease risk factors in patients with CKM syndrome stages 0–3 is an urgent clinical task.

Chronic systemic inflammation, insulin resistance (IR), and visceral adiposity represent three interrelated and independent risk factors for cardiovascular disease (CVD), whose synergistic pathological crosstalk accelerates the onset and progression of atherogenesis and cardiovascular adverse events, and together constitute the core pathophysiological basis of cardiometabolic risk [11–13]. As a classic and sensitive biomarker of low-grade systemic inflammation, C-reactive protein (CRP) not only directly reflects the inflammatory state but also exerts pro-atherosclerotic effects by mediating vascular endothelial dysfunction and lipid metabolism disorders and has been widely validated as an independent predictor of incident CVD [11]. IR, a key feature of metabolic dysfunction, disrupts glucose and lipid homeostasis, and further exacerbates vascular injury and inflammatory activation, thus driving the development of CVD and its metabolic comorbidities; the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, a low-cost and easy-to-obtain surrogate marker for IR based on routine fasting laboratory measurements, has been consistently associated with elevated risks of CVD, stroke and other cardiometabolic diseases [14, 15]. Visceral fat, a metabolically and immunologically active adipose depot distinct from subcutaneous fat, is a major source of pro-inflammatory cytokines and free fatty acids, and its abnormal accumulation aggravates IR and vascular damage [16]. The Chinese Visceral Adipose Index (CVAI) is an index for accurate quantification of visceral fat and CVD risk stratification in the Chinese population. It has shown better predictive performance for cardiovascular outcomes than traditional anthropometric indicators, such as body mass index (BMI), which cannot distinguish between fat distribution [17]. Furthermore, composite biomarkers exhibit stronger correlations with CVD than single indicators [18, 19]. The TyG-CVAI combination has been validated for CVD risk assessment [18], while the CRP-TyG (CTI) index integrates inflammation and IR to outperform single CRP or TyG measurements in cardiometabolic risk stratification [19]. However, the association between the multi-dimensional CTI–CVAI composite index, which incorporates inflammation, IR, and visceral adiposity, and CVD risk remains inadequately elucidated.

Thus, this study aims to use the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHRLS) data to explore the association between CTI–CVAI and cardiovascular disease risk in a population of CKM syndrome stage 0–3 individuals for early identification of high-risk individuals. This study is expected to provide early cardiovascular disease prevention and intervention strategies for individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3 to reduce the burden of CKM disease.

Methods

Data source and study population

Our analyses drew on data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationwide cohort investigation focusing on aging-related health and socioeconomic issues in China. The baseline survey (Wave 1) was launched during 2011–2012, followed by four consecutive follow-up waves in 2013 (Wave 2), 2015 (Wave 3), 2018 (Wave 4), and 2020 (Wave 5), with study participants followed up at 2–3 year intervals. The CHARLS sampling framework covered 150 county-level administrative units and 450 village-level clusters across China, enrolling community-dwelling individuals aged 45 years and older. All study participants provided voluntary, written informed consent prior to data collection, and the entire study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University [20].

For the present analysis, we integrated baseline information and all available follow-up data through 2020 from the initial 17,705 baseline participants, with the following exclusion criteria applied to refine the study cohort: ① classification as CKM stage 4 or lack of CKM staging data (N = 6643); ② age under 45 years (N = 199); ③ pre-existing cardiovascular disease (CVD) before 2012 or unknown baseline CVD status (N = 99); ④ missing key baseline data including age, gender, anthropometric indicators (waist circumference [WC], body mass index [BMI], and blood pressure), and lifestyle information (N = 1357); ⑤ absence of fasting blood samples or laboratory measurements (triglycerides [TG], fasting blood glucose [FBG], high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C], C-reactive protein [CRP]) (N = 2496); ⑥ no available follow-up outcome data (N = 183). After these exclusions (N = 10,977), 6728 participants were retained for the final analysis. The detailed screening flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. Baseline characteristics of included (N = 6728) and excluded (N = 10,977) participants are presented in Table S1. Excluded individuals had a slightly higher prevalence of chronic diseases and lower educational attainment. Nevertheless, most standardized mean differences (SMDs) were below 0.20, indicating only minor imbalance, and core confounders, including age, sex, and blood pressure, remained comparable.

Definition of CKM, exposure, and outcome

CKM staging (stages 0 to 4) was categorized in accordance with the AHA Presidential Advisory [3]. In brief, stage 0 denotes the absence of any CKM-associated conditions, while stage 1 is characterized by early metabolic abnormalities, including excessive adiposity and/or dyslipidemia. Stage 2 encompasses confirmed metabolic disorders (e.g., metabolic syndrome, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, hypertriglyceridemia) and/or chronic kidney disease, and stage 3 is defined as subclinical cardiovascular disease or very high-risk CKD (G4–G5) [3]. For CKM staging, very high-risk CKD and an elevated 10-year predicted CVD risk (assessed via the Framingham Risk Score) were used as surrogates for subclinical CVD [3, 21]. The complete diagnostic criteria for each CKM stage are presented in Table S2.

We collected data on age, gender, TG, FBG, WC, BMI, CRP, and HDL-C from the first wave. Using the previously established methods [22, 23], we calculated the following indicators: (1) CTI [15] = 0.412 × ln(CRP [mg/L]) + ln(TG [mg/dL] × FPG [mg/dL])/2; (2) Chinese man: CVAI = − 267.93 + 0.68 × age + 0.03 × BMI + 4.00 × WC + 22.00 × log10 (TG) − 16.32 × HDL-C; Chinese woman: CVAI = − 187.32 + 1.71 × age + 4.23 × BMI + 1.12 × WC + 39.76 × log10 (TG) − 11.66 × HDL-C. (3) CTI–CVAI = CTI × CVAI.

The primary study endpoint was the development of new-onset CVD over the follow-up period (Waves 2 to 5). Information pertaining to participants’ cardiovascular disease history was collected using standardized questionnaires administered by trained interviewers. In line with previous research utilizing CHARLS data, incident CVD was defined as a self-reported physician diagnosis of heart disease and/or stroke [24, 25]. For the classification of heart disease, participants were asked to respond to the following question: “Has your physician ever informed you that you had a heart attack, angina, Coronary Artery Disease, HF, or other heart-related problems?” Stroke was determined based on participants’ answers to the query: “Has your doctor ever told you that you have been diagnosed with a stroke?”.

Covariates

Baseline covariates were collected via structured questionnaires, physical examinations and laboratory assays, with the study variables categorized into four main groups: (1) Sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics, including sex, age, educational attainment (below high school vs. high school and above), marital status (married/cohabiting vs. other statuses), smoking status (former, current or never smoker) and alcohol consumption (yes or no); Physical activity was assessed using a modified version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). In this study, according to previous literature in similar Chinese cohorts, we divide physical activity into: (1) zero type = 0, (2) Low type: < 600, (3) Moderate type 2: 600–3000, and (4) High type: > 3000 [26]. (2) Anthropometric indices and blood pressure parameters, including height, weight, waist circumference, body mass index (BMI), and resting systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP, DBP); (3) Clinical history and medication use, including self-reported physician-diagnosed hypertension and diabetes, as well as the current use of antihypertensive and glucose-lowering medications; (4) Laboratory biomarkers, including C-reactive protein (CRP), fasting blood glucose (FBG), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), uric acid (UA), creatinine (CREA), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN). Hypertension was diagnosed if any of the following criteria were met: SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, current use of antihypertensive agents, or a self-reported history of hypertension. Diabetes was defined as FBG ≥ 126 mg/dL, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%, current use of antidiabetic medications, or a self-reported physician diagnosis of diabetes.

Assessment of potential confounders

To mitigate overadjustment bias related to the composite nature of CTI–CVAI, we selected covariates based on a causal framework using a directed acyclic graph (DAG) constructed in DAGitty (dagitty.net) to identify the minimally sufficient confounder set for estimating the total effect of CTI–CVAI on incident CVD [27, 28] (Fig. 2). Initial variables comprised age, sex, BMI, WC, SBP, DBP, drinking, education, marital status, physical activity, smoking, FBG, CRP, TG, HDL-C, BUN, CREA, CKM stage, and UA (Fig. 2A). As age, BMI, WC, FBG, CRP, TG, and HDL-C are intrinsic components of CTI–CVAI, they were excluded from adjustment to avoid over-adjustment bias. The CKM stage was conceptualized as a mediator along the causal pathway from CTI–CVAI to incident CVD and was therefore not adjusted for to retain the total effect of the exposure. The final minimal adjustment set included sex, smoking status, alcohol drinking, education, marital status, physical activity, SBP, DBP, BUN, CREA, and UA, which blocked all backdoor paths and minimized confounding for the association between CTI–CVAI and incident CVD (Fig. 2B).

Statistics

The normality of continuous variables was assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test and distribution plots. Since most continuous variables were non-normally distributed, data were expressed as median [interquartile range (IQR)] for continuous variables and counts and percentages [n (%)] for categorical variables. Participants were divided into four groups based on CTI–CVAI quartiles for descriptive analyses. Between-group differences were compared using the Pearson’s χ2 test for categorical variables and the Kruskal–Wallis H test for non-normal continuous variables. Missing patterns of covariates across the 6728 included participants are presented in Table S3. Multiple imputation was performed with the “mice” package in R to address missing covariate data.

Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to visualize the cumulative incidence of CVD across CTI–CVAI quartiles, with intergroup comparisons conducted via the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to estimate the associations of CTI, CVAI, and CTI–CVAI with incident CVD, and results were expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All exposure variables were standardized to z-scores to enable direct comparison of effect sizes, with HRs interpreted as the risk change per 1-standard deviation (SD) increase in the exposure. Three hierarchical regression models were constructed: Model 1 showed the crude association between CTI–CVAI and new-onset CVD; Model 2 was a conventional partially adjusted model for demographic and lifestyle factors (sex, smoking status, alcohol drinking, educational level, marital status, physical activity); Model 3 was the fully adjusted model based on the DAG-derived minimal sufficient adjustment set, further adding SBP, DBP, BUN, CREA, and UA. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. To explore potential non-linear associations between CTI–CVAI and incident CVD, restricted cubic spline (RCS) functions were incorporated into Cox proportional hazards models. We tested the linearity of the association between CTI–CVAI and CVD risk using a restricted cubic spline (RCS) with four knots and plotted the corresponding log hazard ratios (log HRs). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC) were used to assess the discriminative ability of CTI–CVAI for incident CVD, with the De’Long test used for AUC comparisons. Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) were calculated to estimate their incremental predictive value over Model 3.

Subgroup and interaction analyses were carried out across the following stratification factors: sex, CKM stage, drink status, smoking status, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, and age groups (< 60 years vs. ≥ 60 years). All subgroup models were adjusted for the same covariate set as Model 3, and the stratification variable was excluded from the adjustment to reduce the risk of overadjustment. Robustness of the primary findings was verified through a series of sensitivity analyses, including: (1) reanalysis of associations using logistic regression models; (2) repeated RCS analyses with a three-knot setup; (3) calculation of E-values to estimate the minimum magnitude of an unmeasured confounder required to nullify the observed associations; and (4) given that hypertension, dyslipidaemia, and diabetes are well-established CVD risk factors, we further excluded participants with these baseline comorbidities to mitigate potential confounding effects; (5) Considering the potential competing risk of non‑cardiovascular death in this middle‑aged and elderly cohort, a Fine‑Gray subdistribution hazard model was performed to re‑estimate the association between CTI‑CVAI and incident CVD. Non‑CVD death was treated as a competing event, and subdistribution hazard ratios (SHR) and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. (6) To avoid bias caused by sample exclusion, missing covariates were imputed using the MICE algorithm. Five imputed datasets were generated under the MAR assumption, with all relevant variables included. Hazard ratios and 95% CIs were pooled based on Rubin’s rules. (7) To validate the multiplicative definition of CTI‑CVAI, we compared five Cox proportional hazards models (all adjusted for the same covariates as in Model 3): CTI alone, CVAI alone, an additive model (CTI + CVAI), an interaction model (CTI + CVAI + CTI × CVAI), and the multiplicative product (CTI × CVAI) as a single term. Model performance was assessed using AIC and Harrell’s C‑index. All statistical analyses were implemented in R software (version 4.1.2), and a two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

This study comprised 6728 participants, including 3215 males (47.8%) and 3513 females (52.2%), with a median age of 58 (52, 65) years. CVD risk factors were analyzed across subgroups stratified by baseline CTI–CVAI quartiles. Across ascending CTI–CVAI quartiles, we observed significant increases in age, GLU, TC, TG, CRP, HbA1c, CTI, CVAI, SBP, DBP, BMI, and WC, as well as progressively higher prevalence of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes mellitus. Conversely, HDL-C levels decreased gradually with increasing CTI–CVAI quartiles (Table 1).

Association between CTI–CVAI and incident CVD among individuals with CKM stages 0–3

Over a 9-year follow-up period, incident CVD was reported in 1603 participants, corresponding to an overall incidence of 23.83%. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed a stepwise increase in cumulative CVD incidence across ascending CTI–CVAI quartiles (Fig. 3; log-rank P < 0.001), corroborating a dose-dependent association between baseline CTI–CVAI levels and the subsequent risk of CVD events.

Kaplan–Meier curves for cumulative CVD incidence according to CTI–CVAI quartiles

Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were conducted to explore the association between CTI–CVAI and incident CVD, with all outcomes summarized in Table 2. In the unadjusted Model 1, a 1-SD increase in CTI–CVAI was associated with a 36% increase in incident CVD risk (HR = 1.36, 95% CI 1.29–1.42; P < 0.001). After full adjustment for confounding factors in Model 3, this prominent positive association remained stable (HR = 1.32, 95% CI 1.26–1.39; P < 0.001). Quartile stratification verified a distinct dose–response relationship between CTI–CVAI and incident CVD across all regression models (all P for trend < 0.001). In the fully adjusted model, participants in the highest CTI–CVAI quartile showed higher CVD-related risk than those in the lowest quartile, with an adjusted HR of 2.07 (95% CI 1.77–2.42; P < 0.001), suggesting that CTI–CVAI can effectively differentiate populations with varying degrees of CVD-related risk. Compared with its two constituent indicators, CTI and CVAI, CTI–CVAI showed equivalent correlation with CVAI in continuous SD correlation analysis and demonstrated better efficacy in distinguishing graded risk differences across quartile subgroups. In general, the novel CTI–CVAI shows stable associations with incident CVD and favorable stratified correlations, serving as a valid indicator of CVD risk status in this population.

Discriminative performance of the CTI–CVAI index for CVD in CKM syndrome stage 0–3

ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminative performance of CTI–CVAI, CVAI, and CTI for incident CVD among individuals with CKM stage 0–3. The AUC value of CTI–CVAI was 0.5965 (95% CI 0.5806–0.6123), marginally higher than 0.5961 (95% CI 0.5803–0.6120) of CVAI (Fig. 5). Both CTI–CVAI and CVAI yielded higher AUCs than CTI (0.5630, 95% CI 0.5471–0.5790). Pairwise DeLong tests indicated that CTI–CVAI and CVAI had comparable discriminative performance, with no significant difference between the two indicators. By contrast, both CTI–CVAI and CVAI exhibited significantly higher discriminative performance than CTI (all P < 0.05). Based on the fully adjusted Model 3, reclassification analysis showed that adding CTI–CVAI to the baseline model resulted in a significant net reclassification improvement (NRI = 0.0566, P < 0.001), while the IDI was not significant (P = 0.784) (Table S4). Collectively, these findings suggest that CTI‑CVAI may serve as a modest reclassification tool in population‑based screening and has modest discriminative ability for incident CVD.

Subgroup and interaction analyses

Subgroup analyses were performed to examine the consistency of the association between CTI–CVAI and incident CVD risk across demographic, lifestyle, and clinical strata (Fig. 6). Higher CTI–CVAI was significantly associated with elevated CVD risk in all subgroups of sex, age, drinking status, smoking status, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidaemia (all P < 0.05). For CKM stage subgroups, a significant positive association was observed only in participants with CKM stage 2 (P < 0.001), whereas no significant association was observed in CKM stage 1 or 3 (all P > 0.05). Interaction tests showed no significant heterogeneity across all stratifying variables (all P for interaction > 0.05), including sex (P = 0.270), age (P = 0.262), drinking status (P = 0.376), smoking status (P = 0.110), hypertension (P = 0.609), diabetes mellitus (P = 0.075), dyslipidaemia (P = 0.209), and CKM stage (P = 0.904). Overall, the association between CTI–CVAI and CVD risk was generally consistent across most subgroups.

Sensitivity analyses

We performed multiple sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our primary results. First, re-analyses using binary logistic regression instead of Cox proportional hazards models yielded consistent effect directions (Table S5). Second, restricted cubic spline models with three knots yielded similar findings and confirmed a linear relationship between CTI–CVAI and CVD risk (Figure S1). Third, the E-value of 2.55 for CTI–CVAI (derived from fully adjusted Model 3) suggested that extremely strong unmeasured confounders would be required to eliminate the observed association (Table S6). We further excluded participants with baseline hypertension, dyslipidaemia, or diabetes to examine the stability of the CTI–CVAI–CVD association (Tables S7–S9). CVD risk increased progressively across higher CTI–CVAI quartiles in all subgroups. Specifically, the highest quartile was associated with substantially elevated CVD risk, with adjusted HRs of 1.521 (non-hypertensive), 1.526 (non-dyslipidaemic), and 1.506 (non-diabetic).

To address potential competing risks, we fitted a Fine-Gray model to account for non-cardiovascular mortality. After adjusting for competing events, elevated CTI–CVAI remained significantly linked to higher CVD risk in a clear dose–response manner (Q4 vs Q1: SHR = 2.02, 95% CI 1.74–2.35, P < 0.001; Table S10). This pattern was consistent with the main Cox regression results, demonstrating that competing risks did not bias our conclusions. Given minor discrepancies in inclusion and exclusion criteria (Table S1), we supplemented and finalized the full dataset. Table S11 summarises the count and proportion of missing data for CTI–CVAI across the CHARLS cohort (N = 17,705). Multiple imputation via MICE confirmed a stable association between CTI–CVAI and incident CVD. In the fully adjusted model, each SD increase in CTI–CVAI was associated with an HR of 1.24 (95% CI 1.20–1.28, P < 0.001). Quartile analyses showed a stepwise rise in CVD risk: Q2 HR = 1.19, Q3 HR = 1.44, Q4 HR = 1.76 (all P < 0.001). The trend test remained statistically significant across all models, consistent with our primary analyses (Table S12). Lastly, we evaluated the performance of CTI–CVAI by comparing five fully adjusted Cox models: CTI alone, CVAI alone, additive model, interaction model, and multiplicative CTI × CVAI. All combined models achieved better model fit and discriminative ability than individual indices. The multiplicative CTI–CVAI had AIC and C-index values similar to those of the additive model, whereas adding the interaction term did not further improve discriminative performance (Table S13). Overall, these multiple sensitivity analyses solidify the reliability and validity of our findings.

Discussion

The American Heart Association (AHA) has identified individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3 as a key group for early cardiovascular risk stratification and intervention, and has stressed the need to detect latent metabolic abnormalities before overt organ damage occurs [3]. Using a large, nationally representative cohort of Chinese adults with CKM syndrome stages 0–3, we demonstrated that higher baseline CTI–CVAI was significantly and linearly associated with elevated incident CVD risk over the 9-year follow-up. This association remained robust after extensive confounder adjustment and was further validated by multiple sensitivity analyses and subgroup analyses. Collectively, our findings support that the novel CTI–CVAI-based index serves as an accessible and reliable biomarker for identifying CVD risk among individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3.

Our findings align with existing literature on CVD biomarkers and further expand current research evidence. Components of CTI and CVAI, including insulin resistance (TyG) [29], central obesity (CVAI) [17, 30], and systemic inflammation (CRP) [31–35], have been well documented to be independently associated with CVD. A previous study reported that concurrent increases in CVAI and CRP elevate the risk of cardiometabolic diseases [36]. Accordingly, researchers have constructed composite indices combining TyG with inflammatory or adiposity markers, and multiple studies have validated that CTI and TyG-CVAI are superior to single biomarkers for predicting incident CVD [18, 37, 38]. Even so, conventional composite tools such as CTI, TyG-CVAI, and AIP-CVAI only encompass two of the three core pathogenic pathways of CVD, including inflammation, insulin resistance, and central obesity, which restricts their capacity to fully characterize disease-related pathophysiology. Our analyses confirmed strong, linear, and independent associations between baseline CTI–CVAI and incident CVD. This novel index showed more evident graded risk relationships than single biomarkers and yielded significant improvements in risk reclassification as validated by NRI. By capturing the synergistic interactions across all three pathogenic pathways, CTI–CVAI comprehensively reflects the complex pathophysiology of CVD and holds promise as a practical biomarker for population-based CVD risk assessment and early intervention.

Accumulating evidence demonstrates tight biological interactions between visceral adiposity, glucolipid metabolism, and inflammation. First, insulin resistance represents the key alteration in glucolipid metabolism. As the core part of CTI, it links metabolic abnormalities to CVD through multiple pathological mechanisms [31]. IR contributes to endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and chronic low-grade inflammation [39–41]. Second, visceral adiposity disturbs overall cardiometabolic homeostasis. Visceral adipose tissue is metabolically active and functions as an endocrine organ, secreting various adipokines, inflammatory mediators, and free fatty acids, thereby amplifying systemic inflammation and worsening IR [17, 30]. Third, inflammation further exacerbates cardiovascular pathological changes. It is increasingly recognized as a key driver of cardiovascular disease [11, 42]. Elevated inflammatory activity contributes to endothelial activation, vascular remodeling and dysregulated glucolipid metabolism [11, 43]. Given the close crosstalk among these pathways, visceral adiposity triggers inflammation and insulin resistance via pro-inflammatory cytokines and free fatty acids; in turn, inflammation impairs endothelial function, creating a vicious cycle [44]. Supported by these intertwined biological mechanisms, the novel index CTI–CVAI effectively identifies CVD-associated cardiometabolic abnormalities and exhibits superior risk association.

Subgroup analyses confirmed that the association between CTI–CVAI and incident CVD was consistently stable across all demographic, lifestyle, and comorbidity strata, with no significant effect modification detected. Notably, stratified by CKM stage, the association was statistically significant only in stage 2, with null findings in stages 1 and 3. Importantly, no significant heterogeneity was observed across stages, indicating that these differences in statistical significance do not reflect true effect modification. These null findings likely stem from mild pathophysiology and low baseline risk in early CKM, and from the extremely small sample size and the dominant irreversible organ damage in advanced stage 3, which overshadows the prognostic value of metabolic indices. Clinically, CTI–CVAI is most useful for risk stratification in CKM stage 2, a critical therapeutic window where metabolic abnormalities actively drive progression and targeted interventions yield the greatest benefit.

These findings have several potential clinical and public health implications. Firstly, CTI–CVAI provides incremental value over conventional cardiovascular risk assessment, with a significant net reclassification improvement (NRI = 0.0566, P < 0.001) that identifies individuals at high risk who are missed by routine biomarkers. Secondly, CTI–CVAI is highly practical for routine clinical use, as all required parameters are obtained from standard physical examinations without additional costly or invasive testing, making it cost-effective across all care settings. Furthermore, the linear relationship between CTI–CVAI and CVD risk suggests that early identification of individuals with elevated CTI–CVAI levels may facilitate timely lifestyle or pharmacological interventions to reduce CVD risk.

This study has several strengths. First, the use of a nationwide prospective cohort with a large sample size enhances the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Second, the prospective design reduces the risk of reverse causality. Third, we comprehensively evaluated the association between CTI–CVAI and incident CVD using multiple analytical approaches, including multivariable Cox regression models, restricted cubic spline analyses, and subgroup analyses. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, because the study is observational, causal relationships cannot be definitively established. Second, residual confounding from unmeasured variables may still exist despite extensive adjustments. Third, CTI–CVAI was calculated using baseline measurements, and potential changes in these biomarkers over time were not captured. Fourth, the included sample had a healthier baseline profile, which may cause mild selection bias and underestimate the true association. Finally, external validation in other populations is needed to confirm the generalizability of our findings. Despite these limitations, multiple sensitivity analyses verified the robust association, and our results remain clinically valuable.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this nationwide prospective cohort study demonstrates that the composite CTI–CVAI is independently associated with an increased risk of incident cardiovascular disease across CKM syndrome stages 0–3. These findings highlight the potential value of integrating inflammatory, metabolic, and adiposity-related markers into a single composite index for cardiometabolic risk assessment.
