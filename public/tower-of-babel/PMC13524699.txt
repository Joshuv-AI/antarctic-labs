Paraspinal muscle CT measurements in predicting subsequent fractures after percutaneous kyphoplasty in elderly women
Authors: Jian Geng, Yandong Liu, Wenshuang Zhang, Yi Yuan, Fayan Liu, Dongfeng Xu, Bo Hu, Qingyu Zhang, Chunhua Mai, Xiaoguang Cheng, Ling Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubsequent fractures are a common complication after percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) in elderly women with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCF). Although bone mineral density (BMD) is a known predictor, its limitations highlight the need for additional prognostic markers.PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the predictive value of lumbar paraspinal muscle parameters measured by preoperative computed tomography (CT) for subsequent fractures after PKP, and to assess whether incorporating these muscle parameters improves risk stratification beyond BMD alone.MethodsThis prospective longitudinal study enrolled 185 elderly female patients (mean age 70.9 ± 8.6 years) with OVCF who underwent PKP. Preoperative quantitative CT (QCT) was used to measure muscle parameters—including density (Hounsfield units [HU]) and functional cross-sectional area (FCSA) of the extensor (erector spinae and multifidus) and flexor (psoas major) muscles at the L4 level—as well as volumetric BMD (vBMD) and vertebral HU. Incident subsequent fractures were defined according to the semiquantitative Genant classification during a mean follow-up of 4.7 years. Cox proportional hazards models, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were employed to assess predictive performance.ResultsDuring follow-up, 56 patients (30.3%) developed incident fractures. In multivariable analysis adjusted for age, BMI, fracture history, and baseline vertebral fracture status, the extensor-to-flexor density ratio remained an independent protective factor for any incident fracture (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.29 per SD, 95% Confidence interval [CI]: 0.11–0.79), while vBMD showed the strongest protective effect for vertebral-specific fractures (HR = 0.60 per SD, 95% CI: 0.41–0.88). Incorporating muscle parameters into a baseline BMD model significantly improved predictive performance for both fracture endpoints (IDI = 0.067 for any fracture, p = .036; IDI = 0.09 for vertebral fracture, p = .028).ConclusionsCT-based measurements of paraspinal muscles, particularly the extensor-to-flexor density ratio, are independent predictors of subsequent fractures after PKP in elderly women. Combining muscle parameters with vBMD enhances risk stratification, underscoring the clinical value of assessing the “muscle-bone unit” in postoperative management. These findings suggest that postoperative rehabilitation strategies should prioritize strengthening of the spinal extensor musculature to mitigate fracture riskKeywords: Osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture, Percutaneous kyphoplasty, Paraspinal muscles, Muscle density, Subsequent fracture, Quantitative computed tomography

Introduction

Osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCF), also referred to as domino osteoporotic vertebral fracture (DOVF), are the most common type of osteoporotic fracture [1,2]. The term “domino” captures the cascading nature of this condition: an initial vertebral fracture shifts spinal biomechanics, increasing load on adjacent vertebrae and creating a vicious cycle that predisposes further collapse [3]. They are frequently overlooked yet can cause residual back pain (RBP), and significantly impair quality of life [1,2]. The occurrence of an OVCF significantly influences probability of a subsequent fracture [4]. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, approximately 5.37 million people (95% confidence interval [CI]: 4.70–6.20 million) experienced vertebral fractures worldwide in 2021, with an age-standardized prevalence of 65 per 100,000 [5,6]. The global annual incidence of OVCF is estimated at ∼1.4 million cases, predominantly affecting postmenopausal women aged ≥65 years [7]. Driven by the rapid aging of its population, China is facing a prominently increasing incidence of OVCF [8,9], which has led to a severe burden [2,[9], [10], [11]]. While percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) is widely adopted as a first-line treatment for its minimal trauma and positive impact on pain and quality of life, postoperative subsequent fractures remain a key drawback [12], frequently causing RBP, progressive spinal deformity, and reduced quality of life [[13], [14], [15]]. Consequently, predicting and preventing these subsequent fractures presents a significant and ongoing research challenge.

Bone mineral density (BMD) is closely linked to fracture risk and declining bone strength, with low bone mass regarded as a key determinant of both osteoporotic and postoperative vertebral fractures [12,[16], [17], [18]]. Nevertheless, the occurrence of fragility fractures in patients with normal or nonosteoporotic BMD on dual X-ray energy absorptiometry (DXA) challenges this paradigm [19]. Low muscle mass and strength have also been associated with decreased bone mass and strength [20,21]. Consequently, a growing body of evidences now underscores advancing imaging (CT/MRI) and the muscle-bone unit as an integrated whole, positing that muscle parameters (both quantity and quality) are crucial factors in fracture prevention [[22], [23], [24], [25], [26]].

Several factors predict postoperative subsequent fractures, including female gender, older age, paraspinal muscle fat infiltration rate (FIR) and muscle mass, cement leakage, intravertebral vacuum cleft (IVC), and lower BMD [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. Currently, most studies focusing on muscle quantity assessment using MRI primarily measure the cross-sectional area (CSA), while muscle quality evaluation is mainly based on the FIR [31]. However, CT attenuation values also serve as a quantitative indicator of muscle quality, partly reflecting the intramyocellular fat content [32]. Furthermore, no studies have compared the predictive value of the spinal extensors (including the erector spinae and multifidus muscles) versus the flexors (the psoas major muscle) in this context. In addition, few studies have employed CT for simultaneous assessment of both muscle and bone parameters in research on subsequent vertebral fractures following percutaneous cement augmentation [33].

The objectives of our study are (1) to investigate the predictive value of lumbar muscle parameters measured by CT scans for incident fractures following PKP; (2) to compare the differences between lumbar extensor and flexor muscle parameters in predicting subsequent fractures; and (3) to assess the improvement in predictive performance contributed by incorporating muscle parameters.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This prospective study received approval from the Institutional Review Board. It enrolled elderly female patients with OVCF to evaluate outcomes following PKP. Eligible participants were identified from the emergency department with lower back pain after mild trauma and an OVCF diagnosis confirmed by emergency preoperative CT (pCT). A thorough medical history review and lumbar MRI were performed to verify acute fracture etiology before admission to the spinal surgery department for PKP. All participants provided informed consent.

Between March 2013 and June 2015, 185 patients with lumbar vertebral fractures (mean age 70.9 ± 8.6 years) were enrolled and followed for a mean of 4.7 years. Follow-up assessments for subsequent fractures were conducted by orthopedic specialists via telephone interviews, medical record review, or imaging analysis. Subsequent vertebral fractures were confirmed by follow-up CT or MRI imaging. A subsequent vertebral fracture was defined as a new fracture in a previously intact vertebra or a worsening fracture in a consolidated vertebra, requiring an increase of at least one grade according to the semiquantitative Genant classification [34]. The follow-up process and outcomes were blinded to the observers performing all subsequent BMD and HU measurements.

Inclusion criteria comprised patients aged over 50 who underwent spinal CT with a QCT phantom within 48 hours postfracture. Exclusion criteria included: fractures due to severe trauma (eg, motor vehicle accidents, assault); patients in whom all vertebrae throughout the CT scanning field were fractured, precluding accurate measurement of vertebral BMD (fractured vertebrae cannot provide reliable BMD values; at least one measurable vertebra was required); and a history of prior fracture surgery. Regarding fall-related fractures, cases with a fall height less than the patient's own height were uniformly considered minor trauma [35].

Baseline data collection

Prior to CT scanning, the following baseline data were recorded: age (years), sex, height (cm), weight (kg), and body mass index (BMI, kg/m2). BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. After scanning, any fractured vertebrae were found and documented.

CT acquisition

The preoperative lumbar scan was performed on either a uCT-780 scanner (United Imaging Healthcare) or a Toshiba CT scanner (Aquilion PRIME, Toshiba). A Mindways model 3 synchronous calibration phantom (Mindways Software Inc.) was included in all scans. Scans were acquired in the supine position from the top of the 11th thoracic vertebra (T11) to the fourth sacral (S4). The spine was kept parallel to the long axis of the calibration phantom, and minimal air gaps existed between the phantom and the volunteer. The scanning parameters were as follows: 120 kV, 187 mAs, field of view 50 cm, 1 mm slice thickness, and reconstruction matrix 512 × 512. Other methodological details have been described previously [36].

Paraspinal muscle and psoas muscle measurements

This study assessed muscle density and functional cross-sectional area (FCSA) for the back extensors (erector spinae and multifidus) and the flexor (psoas major) at the midvertebral level of the fourth lumbar vertebra (L4) (Fig. 1C). The analysis was performed on selected CT sections using OsiriX software (Lite version 10.0.2, Pixmeo). Following established methodology [37], muscle regions were segmented within a Hounsfield unit (HU) threshold range of −30 to 150 HU. Manual contour delineation combined with this thresholding enabled the software to automatically differentiate muscle tissue from intermuscular adipose tissue, subsequently calculating the muscle FCSA (in cm²) and muscle density (represented by the average CT attenuation in HU). The derived parameters for final analysis included: extensor muscle density (EMD), flexor muscle density (FMD), extensor FCSA (EFCSA), flexor FCSA (FFCSA), along with the respective extensor-to-flexor ratios for both density and FCSA.

All measurements were conducted independently by 2 radiologists, each with over 5 years of experience. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated to assess both intra and interobserver reproducibility. The ICCs for intraobserver reliability were 0.990 for EMD, 0.942 for FMD, 0.935 for EFCSA, and 0.956 for FFCSA, indicating good to excellent reproducibility. interobserver reliability was similarly high, with ICCs of 0.932 for EMD, 0.921 for FMD, 0.914 for EFCSA, and 0.942 for FFCSA.

QCT volumetric BMD measurements

After scanning, the CT DICOM images were transferred to the QCT workstation for further analysis with the QCT Pro 5.0.3 software (Mindways Inc.). vBMD was measured within a specific region of interest, which was defined as the oval-shaped areas containing the largest areas of the trabecular bone in the midplane of each vertebral body, not including the cortical bone or basivertebral vein. For most of the subjects, the Trab.vBMD values (mg/cm3) of L1–L2 were recorded and analyzed, respectively, and the average was calculated. If the L1 or L2 vertebral bodies were fractured, they were replaced by an adjacent nonfractured vertebral body. The placement of ROIs for BMD measurements at T12 and L2 is illustrated in Fig. 1D, E. All subjects must have at least one vertebra available for BMD measurement; if all vertebrae are uniformly compressed or wedged, then the patient was excluded. The ICCs for intraobserver reliability and interobserver reliability were 0.995 and 0.99 for vertebral vBMD, respectively.

CT Hounsfield unit measurements in vertebrae

Vertebral Hounsfield Unit (HU) measurements were performed using 3D Slicer software (version 5.0.3; http://www.slicer.org). The semiautomated analysis tools in 3D Slicer were used to define regions of interest (ROIs) within the midvertebral plane of each measured vertebra (Fig. 1B). To ensure that the vertebral bodies assessed by HU measurements were the same as those evaluated for bone mineral density (BMD) using the QCT Pro software, HU values and vBMD were measured synchronously on identical vertebral bodies. Subsequently, the software calculated the average CT attenuation (ie, the mean HU value) within these ROIs. The ICCs for intraobserver reliability and interobserver reliability were 0.955 and 0.921 for vertebral HU, respectively.

Statistics

Statistical analyses used SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp.) and GraphPad Prism 8.0. Data are presented as mean ± SD for continuous variables and n (%) for categorical variables. Group differences in baseline characteristics were compared using independent t-tests (continuous) or Chi-square tests (categorical). Cox proportional hazards models assessed associations between muscle/bone parameters (per SD increase) and fracture risk. The proportional hazards assumption for all Cox regression models was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Results are reported as HRs with 95% CIs. Multivariable models were adjusted sequentially: Model 1 for age and BMI; Model 2 additionally for fracture history; Model 3 further for baseline vertebral fracture status. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis with log-rank testing and model improvement analysis using IDI and NRI were performed. A 2-sided p < .05 was significant.

Results

Study population characteristics

A total of 185 subjects were included in the analysis. During a mean follow-up of 4.7 ± 2.4 years, 56 patients (30.3%) developed incident fractures. The baseline characteristics of the cohort, stratified by incident fracture status, are presented in Table 1. There were no significant differences between the fracture and nonfracture groups in terms of weight, height, body mass index (BMI), age, years of follow-up, or history of previous fractures (all p > .05). However, the prevalence of multiple vertebral fractures at baseline was significantly higher in the incident fracture group (58.9% vs. 34.9%, p = .007).

Muscle and bone parameters

Quantitative CT measurements of muscle and bone parameters are summarized in Table 2. Compared to patients without incident fractures, those who developed any incident fracture had significantly lower CT attenuation and functional cross-sectional area (FCSA) of the extensor muscles (CT value: 19.7 ± 10.0 vs. 23.7 ± 9.7 HU, p = .011; FCSA: 27.2 ± 8.7 vs. 30.9 ± 7.6 cm², p = .004), lower FCSA of the flexor muscle (14.3 ± 4.1 vs. 15.8 ± 4.9 cm², p = .038), and a lower extensor-to-flexor density ratio (0.6 ± 0.3 vs. 0.7 ± 0.2, p = .013). When specifically analyzing incident vertebral fractures, these differences remained significant for paraspinal muscle CT value, FCSA, and the CT value ratio (all p < .05). Most notably, quantitative CT bone mineral density (QCT BMD) and vertebral body CT attenuation were substantially lower in both incident fracture groups compared to the nonfracture group (eg, for any fracture: QCT BMD: 36.7 ± 25.3 vs. 53.9 ± 28.5 mg/cm³, p < .001; vertebral CT value: 54.9 ± 32.4 vs. 74.9 ± 37.1 HU, p < .001).

Univariate and multivariable predictors of fracture

In univariate analysis (Figs. 2 and 4), several muscle and bone parameters showed significant associations with the risk of incident fractures. Specifically, for any incident fracture, each standard deviation (SD) increase in EMD (HR = 0.75 per SD, 95% CI: 0.57–0.98), EFCSA (HR = 0.68 per SD, 95% CI: 0.52–0.90), extensor-to-flexor ratios for density (HR = 0.32 per SD, 95% CI: 0.12–0.82), QCT BMD (HR = 0.67 per SD, 95% CI: 0.50–0.89), and vertebral CT value (HR = 0.68 per SD, 95% CI: 0.51–0.90) was associated with a significantly decreased risk (all p < .05). For incident vertebral fractures, similar significant protective associations were observed for increases in these parameters, with QCT BMD (HR = 0.55 per SD, 95% CI: 0.38–0.78) and vertebral CT value (HR = 0.58 per SD, 95% CI: 0.41–0.81) showing the lowest hazard ratios, indicating the strongest protective effects.

In multivariable Cox models, sequential adjustments were made for age and BMI, history of fracture, and baseline vertebral fracture status (Figs. 3 and 5). For any incident fracture, in the fully adjusted model (Model 3), an increase in the extensor-to-flexor ratios for density (HU ratio) remained an independent protective factor, with a hazard ratio (HR = 0.29 per SD, 95% CI: 0.11–0.79) that indicated a stronger protective effect than that of QCT BMD (HR = 0.70 per SD, 95% CI: 0.53–0.96). Conversely, for incident vertebral fractures, an increase in QCT BMD consistently demonstrated the strongest protective effect in the multivariable models (HR = 0.60 per SD, 95% CI: 0.41–0.88 in Model 3), with a hazard ratio significantly lower than that for any muscle parameter, underscoring the central protective role of higher bone density against vertebral-specific fractures. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals (all p > .05).

Survival analysis

Log-rank tests provided further evidence for the predictive utility of these parameters (Table 3). The extensor-to-flexor ratios for density (χ² = 3.970, p = .046) and the FCSA ratio (χ² = 4.081, p = .043) were significant predictors for any incident fracture. For vertebral fractures, QCT BMD (χ² = 15.379, p < .001) and vertebral CT value (χ² = 9.717, p = .002) showed the strongest associations.

Model improvement with muscle parameters

Despite the superior performance of BMD in predicting vertebral fractures, incorporating muscle parameters into a baseline model containing BMD significantly improved overall prediction performance for both fracture endpoints (Table 4). For any incident fracture, the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) was 0.067 (95% CI: 0.002–0.176, p = .036), and the continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) was 0.48 (95% CI: 0.081–0.697, p = .028). For vertebral fractures, the IDI was 0.09 (95% CI: 0.003–0.283, p = .028).

Discussion

This prospective longitudinal study with a mean follow-up of 4.7 years investigated the role of CT-derived paraspinal muscle parameters in predicting subsequent fractures in elderly women after PKP. Our primary finding is that the quality and quantity of these muscles, particularly the balance between spinal extensors and flexors, are significant and independent predictors of subsequent fractures. Notably, the extensor-to-flexor density ratio emerged as the strongest independent predictor for any subsequent fracture, surpassing even BMD in the fully adjusted model. Furthermore, integrating these muscle parameters with traditional BMD measurements significantly improved the accuracy of fracture risk stratification. These results underscore the clinical importance of evaluating the “muscle-bone unit” in the post-PKP setting.

Our results align with and expand upon the growing body of evidence implicating muscle health in fracture risk. The observed 30.3% incidence of subsequent fractures is consistent with prior reports [38]. More importantly, our study corroborates research linking sarcopenia and reduced paraspinal muscle mass to vertebral refracture [27,29,31,33,39].

However, we go beyond simple measures of muscle size by demonstrating that muscle quality (density) and the functional balance between muscle groups (the extensor-to-flexor ratio) are critical determinants. This supports the concept of the “muscle-bone unit” [40], where the mechanical and metabolic interplay between these tissues synergistically influences skeletal integrity. The predictive value of the extensor-to-flexor ratio, in particular, suggests that an imbalance, likely reflecting disproportionate extensor muscle degradation, may be a more sensitive marker of spinal instability and fracture vulnerability than the assessment of either muscle group in isolation. This finding is in line with Shah et al. [26], who noted the importance of the cross-sectional area ratio in MRI scans [26], and provides a more detailed, CT-based quantification of this phenomenon.

The biological and mechanical rationale for our findings is compelling. The extensor muscles (erector spinae and multifidus) are the primary dynamic stabilizers of the spine, counteracting the anterior flexion forces that predispose the vertebral bodies to compression fractures. Their degradation, indicated by lower density (reflecting fatty infiltration) and reduced FCSA, directly compromises this protective mechanism, increasing the load on the anterior spinal column [41]. The flexor (psoas) muscle, while important for hip flexion, plays a lesser role in directly maintaining sagittal balance. Therefore, a decline in the extensor-to-flexor ratio signifies a specific loss of the primary spinal defense against compression, making subsequent fractures more likely. This anatomical and functional distinction explains why our composite ratio outperformed individual muscle parameters and, for any fracture, even BMD.

Interestingly, while the muscle ratio was superior for predicting any fracture, QCT BMD remained the single strongest predictor for subsequent vertebral fractures specifically. This nuanced finding suggests that bone strength is paramount for the integrity of the vertebral body itself, while overall musculoskeletal health, reflected in the muscle balance, is a broader determinant of fragility fractures throughout the skeleton. Nevertheless, the significant improvement in predictive models when muscle parameters were added to BMD (with IDI values of 0.067 and 0.09) demonstrates that these factors provide complementary information. BMD captures the strength of the bone “scaffold,” while muscle parameters assess the dynamic forces and loads applied to it. This synergistic effect highlights the inadequacy of relying on BMD alone and reinforces the value of a comprehensive “muscle-bone” assessment.

This study also highlights the practical advantages of using routine preoperative CT scans for this purpose. Unlike DXA, which can be artifactually elevated by degenerative changes and provides only areal BMD, QCT offers true volumetric BMD of the metabolically active trabecular bone. Simultaneously, the same CT dataset allows for precise, threshold-based quantification of paraspinal muscle mass and quality [42]. This “opportunistic” use of existing CT scans—without additional scan, contrast, or radiation—enables quantification of paraspinal muscle parameters, providing prognostic information that can be reported alongside BMD to facilitate an integrated bone–muscle assessment without prolonged hospital stay or increased costs. The superior predictive performance of QCT BMD over vertebral HU values in our analysis further supports the use of quantitative techniques for accurate bone mass assessment. For patients with low extensor density or an unfavorable extensor-to-flexor ratio, actionable strategies include tailored rehabilitation to strengthen spinal extensors, earlier escalation of antiosteoporotic therapy, and more vigilant surveillance. Although prospective trials are still needed to validate whether risk stratification improves clinical outcomes, the identification of high-risk factors and patients for subsequent fractures already facilitates personalized follow-up and the prioritization of preventive interventions.

Despite these strengths, our study has several limitations. First, as a single-center study with a modest sample size (n = 185), we were unable to perform subgroup analyses by fracture location (adjacent-level vs. prior-level worsening). Larger, more diverse populations are needed to validate our findings and explore potential differential predictors for distinct fracture patterns. Second, we did not account for all potential confounders, such as detailed medication histories (eg, antiosteoporotic drugs and corticosteroids), nutritional status, or physical activity levels, which could influence both muscle and bone health. Third, although we adjusted for baseline fracture status, we lacked detailed data on antiosteoporotic treatment regimens during follow-up, which may have affected subsequent fracture risk. Future multicenter prospective studies should incorporate these variables to build more robust predictive models and establish causality.

In conclusion, this study provides strong evidence that CT-derived measurements of the paraspinal muscles, particularly the extensor-to-flexor density ratio, are independent and powerful predictors of subsequent fractures in elderly women following PKP. The integration of these muscle parameters with QCT BMD significantly enhances risk stratification, validating the clinical assessment of the “muscle-bone unit.” Our findings suggest that postoperative management and rehabilitation strategies should prioritize not only bone health but also the preservation and strengthening of the spinal extensor musculature to mitigate fracture risk and improve long-term outcomes in this vulnerable population.
