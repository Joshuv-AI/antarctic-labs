Rectangles, triangles and Schrödinger waves
Jonathan Bennett, Vjekoslav Kovač, Shohei Nakamura, Itamar Oliveira

Abstract
. Can a finite set of lattice points determine many rectangles and few isosceles triangles? This turns out to be a surprisingly interesting question in combinatorial geometry that we answer using basic analytic number theory combined with a finite-field construction. The result is useful because it gives obstructions to Mizohata–Takeuchi-type estimates in the setting of the paraboloid. Specifically, we establish transference between Euclidean and periodic weighted L 2 \mathrm{L}^{2} estimates for solutions to the Schrödinger equation, and then relate the failure of the latter to quantities tied to combinatorial problems, such as the one above. By completing this programme we give new explicit combinatorial counterexamples to the paraboloid case of the Mizohata–Takeuchi conjecture, which was recently shown to be false by Cairo for curved hypersurfaces.

1.
Introduction

Numerous problems in combinatorial geometry concern rectangles or triangles determined by points in the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. We begin with a simple-looking question of this type involving both rectangles and triangles, and then go on to reveal a surprising connection with the behaviour of solutions to the Schrödinger equation. Readers with a combinatorial inclination may also find the question of independent interest.

Configuration counts already have well-established links with problems in harmonic analysis and dispersive PDE, some of the most prominent examples being:
counts of lattice solutions arising from moments of exponential sums
[
8
]
,
counts of polynomial Diophantine relations associated with periodic KdV moments
[
9
]
,
counts of repeated angles, parallelograms or rectangles to prove extension estimates for finite-field paraboloids
[
43
,
29
,
32
]

and counts of parallelograms in frequency sets to prove Strichartz estimates for periodic Schrödinger equations
[
27
,
33
]
. However, the appearance of triangles from such problems in harmonic analysis seems to be much more unusual.
We will need to show that rectangles inside a subset of the grid can greatly outnumber isosceles triangles, and such (direct) comparative counts of geometric configurations also appear to be relatively uncommon in the literature. Certain comparative counts are common in additive combinatorics, where counts of arithmetic progressions (and other configurations) in a set are controlled by the Gowers uniformity norms, which themselves count parallelotopes; see
[
51
,
50
]
. One may also
compare counts of parallelotopes of different dimensions using the log-convexity of Gowers norms
[
44
,
34
,
6
]
.

We ask whether the number of rectangles in a finite set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is necessarily controlled by the number of isosceles triangles in
A
.

Question 1.1
.

Is there a constant
M>0
such that

(1.1)

\#\Box(A)\leqslant M\#\triangle(A)

holds for every finite set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
? A similar question can be asked about the weaker “pinned” inequality

(1.2)

\#\Box(A)\leqslant M\,\#A\,\max_{k\in A}\#\triangle^{k}(A).

Inequalities (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) should be compared with the estimate

(1.3)

\#\Box(A)\lesssim(\#A)^{2}\log\#A

of Pach and Sharir
[
40
]
, which is best possible if the right-hand side depends only on the cardinality of
A
. Pach and Sharir also considered bounds for
\#\triangle(A)
, proving that

(1.4)

\#\triangle(A)\lesssim(\#A)^{7/3},

and this has since been improved by Pach and Tardos
[
41
]
. Both (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) hold for arbitrary finite subsets of the plane, not just subsets of the integer lattice. For context, upper bounds on
\#\triangle(A)
quickly imply distance-set estimates (see
[
40
,
41
,
51
]
), suggesting that
\#\triangle(A)
may also satisfy a near-quadratic bound similar to (
1.3
).

Both parts of Question
1.1
have negative answers. We quantify their failure in two natural finitary ways. Define

\mathfrak{R}(N):=\max_{\emptyset\neq A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}\cap[-N,N]^{2}}\frac{\#\Box(A)}{\#\triangle(A)},\quad\mathfrak{R}^{\mathrm{pin}}(N):=\max_{\emptyset\neq A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}\cap[-N,N]^{2}}\frac{\#\Box(A)}{\#A\,\max_{k\in A}\limits\#\triangle^{k}(A)},

and

\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}(n):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}\\
\#A=n\end{subarray}}\frac{\#\Box(A)}{\#\triangle(A)},\quad\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}^{\mathrm{pin}}(n):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}\\
\#A=n\end{subarray}}\frac{\#\Box(A)}{\#A\,\max_{k\in A}\limits\#\triangle^{k}(A)}.

In words,
\mathfrak{R}(N)
and
\mathfrak{R}^{\mathrm{pin}}(N)
measure the blowup of the best constants in (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) as
A
ranges over subsets of a bounded part of the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\cap[-N,N]^{2}
, while
\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}(n)
and
\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}^{\mathrm{pin}}(n)
measure the blowup as
A
varies over all
n
-element subsets of the lattice.

Here is our main combinatorial result.

Theorem 1.2
.

There is a constant
c>0
such that, for all sufficiently large
N
,

\mathfrak{R}(N)\geqslant\mathfrak{R}^{\mathrm{pin}}(N)\geqslant(\log N)^{c/\log\log\log N},

and, for all sufficiently large positive integers
n
,

\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}(n)\geqslant\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}^{\mathrm{pin}}(n)\geqslant(\log n)^{c/\log\log\log n}.

The lower estimates from Theorem
1.2
are close to optimal because they are complemented by easy upper bounds. Namely, combining (
1.3
) with the trivial observation
\#\triangle(A)\geqslant(\#A)^{2}
, we obtain

\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}^{\mathrm{pin}}(n)\leqslant\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}(n)\lesssim\log n.

Applying the same to a subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}\cap[-N,N]^{2}
, which necessarily has cardinality
\#A\lesssim N^{2}
, gives

\mathfrak{R}^{\mathrm{pin}}(N)\leqslant\mathfrak{R}(N)\lesssim\log N.

The construction of the sets that achieve the bounds stated in Theorem
1.2
was found by OpenAI’s ChatGPT 5.5 Pro; for details see the section declaring the AI usage. The authors ran it numerous times shortly after the announcement of the breakthrough by an internal OpenAI model on the Erdős unit distance conjecture
[
39
,
1
]
. Even though the two problems are very different, there is a certain similarity between the proofs, both in the local-to-global transference from simpler fields to the reals, and in the use of certain sieving when counting occurrences of (un)desired patterns. This vague analogy is even more apparent in relation to subsequent papers
[
7
,
42
,
17
]
enabled by the aforementioned breakthrough, which appeared after our initial discovery, while we were writing up the results. Related local-to-global arguments may be found elsewhere in harmonic analysis, with
[
28
]
being a natural example to mention.

1.2.
Strichartz estimates for the Schrödinger equation

Now we turn to the analytic side of the paper.

The classical
Strichartz estimates
for a solution
u\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
of the
free Schrödinger equation

2\pi i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u(x,t)=\Delta_{x}u(x,t);\quad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R},

with initial data
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are space-time norm inequalities of the form

(1.5)

\|u(x,t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}_{t}\mathrm{L}^{p}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

The time-integrability captured by such estimates is a reflection of the dispersive nature of the Schrödinger equation. These inequalities and their variants have had many applications, notably in the well-posedness theory of nonlinear Schrödinger equations; see for example
[
49
]
.
The Lebesgue exponents
p,q
for which (
1.5
) holds have been known for some time, beginning with the original work of Strichartz
[
46
]
, and culminating in
[
30
]
. From the point of view of harmonic analysis, these are oscillatory integral estimates arising from the validity of the explicit formula

(1.6)

u(x,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{u_{0}}(\xi)e^{2\pi i(x\cdot\xi+t|\xi|^{2})}\,\mathrm{d}\xi

for suitable initial data
u_{0}
.

It is also natural to consider Strichartz inequalities in periodic settings. For a function
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, where
\mathbb{T}^{d}
denotes the
d
-dimensional torus, the Strichartz estimates for the
periodic Schrödinger equation

2\pi i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u(x,t)=\Delta_{x}u(x,t);\quad(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T},

take a similar form

(1.7)

\|u(x,t)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}_{t}\mathrm{L}^{p}_{x}(\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

However, these are now exponential sum estimates, with
the solution
u
given by the formula

(1.8)

u(x,t)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\widehat{u_{0}}(k)e^{2\pi i(x\cdot k+t|k|^{2})},\quad(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}

for suitable initial data
u_{0}
.
The family of inequalities (
1.7
) appears to be considerably more subtle than its Euclidean counterpart (
1.5
), and the full range of admissible exponents
p,q
remains to be understood.
Much is known, however, beginning with
[
8
]
and culminating recently in
[
10
]
in the pure-norm (
p=q
) case; see
[
20
,
38
]
.
Characterisation of the mixed-norm (
p\neq q
) case is still open. In this direction, we mention
[
11
]
, in which they established (
1.7
) for a wide range of
p,q
by allowing a certain loss of regularity. In
[
31
]
, motivated by a problem in nonlinear dispersive PDEs, the authors proposed the question of the validity of (
1.7
) at the Keel–Tao endpoint with an
\varepsilon
-loss of regularity; as far as we know, this remains open as well.
Related to this problem, it was recently observed in
[
18
]
that the corresponding mixed-norm decoupling estimates turn out to be false.
As may therefore be expected, the admissible exponents in (
1.5
) and (
1.7
) are well known to be quite different, reflecting common distinctions between oscillatory integrals and exponential sums, the latter relating to aspects of combinatorial geometry and number theory. In particular, it is not in general possible to pass between the continuous and discrete inequalities (
1.5
) and (
1.7
) for given exponents
p,q
.

1.3.
Weighted Strichartz estimates on
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}

In weighted
\mathrm{L}^{2}
settings, however, such distinctions may be less apparent.
There is a considerable literature on weighted Strichartz estimates of various forms, often involving specific classes of weights; see, for example,
[
2
]
and the references there. In certain situations, weighted Strichartz estimates are referred to as
Morawetz estimates
, having their origins in
[
36
]
; see
[
49
]
.
Here we are interested in
\mathrm{L}^{2}
quantities of the form

(1.9)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
w
is an arbitrary weight, by which we mean a nonnegative locally integrable function. There is a growing literature on
\mathrm{L}^{2}
estimates for such expressions, partly due to their relation with problems in geometric measure theory; see, for example,
[
22
]
and the references there.

One way to generate conjectural estimates for the general weighted norm (
1.9
) is to first reformulate it as a
phase-space integral
, as recently described in
[
21
,
3
]
. The starting point is the
Wigner transform

W\colon\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
given by

(1.10)

W(g_{1},g_{2})(x,v):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g_{1}\Bigl(x+\frac{y}{2}\Bigr)\overline{g_{2}\Bigl(x-\frac{y}{2}\Bigr)}e^{-2\pi iv\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}y;\quad(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

and the well-known observation
[
53
]
that the function

f(x,v,t):=W\bigl(u(\cdot,t),u(\cdot,t)\bigr)(x,v);\quad(x,v,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}

satisfies the
kinetic transport equation

(1.11)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f(x,v,t)=2v\cdot\nabla_{x}f(x,v,t),

meaning that

W(u(\cdot,t),u(\cdot,t))(x,v)=W(u_{0},u_{0})(x+2tv,v).

It then follows by an application of the classical spatial marginal property

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}W(g,g)(x,v)\,\mathrm{d}v=|g(x)|^{2}

that

(1.12)

|u(x,t)|^{2}=\rho W(u_{0},u_{0})(x,t),

where the operator
\rho
, acting on a suitable phase-space function
f_{0}
, is given by

\rho f_{0}(x,t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{0}(x+2tv,v)\,\mathrm{d}v.

By duality this leads to the desired phase-space integral representation

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}W(u_{0},u_{0})(x,v)\rho^{\ast}w(x,v)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v,

where we identify
the operator

\rho^{\ast}w(x,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}w(x-2tv,t)\,\mathrm{d}t

as a certain
parametrised space-time X-ray transform
: for each
x,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
it computes an integral of the weight
w
along the space-time line through
(x,0)
in the direction
(-2v,1)
.
Finally, noting the frequency marginal property

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}W(g,g)(x,v)\,\mathrm{d}x=|\widehat{g}(v)|^{2},

one is then tentatively led to the following conjectures.

Claim

(Parabolic Stein-type estimate, motivated by
[
45
]
)
.

For each
d\geqslant 1
there is a constant
C
such that

(1.13)

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{u_{0}}(v)|^{2}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho^{\ast}w(x,v)\,\mathrm{d}v

for all nonnegative weights
w
and all
u_{0}
with compact Fourier support.

Claim

(Parabolic Mizohata–Takeuchi estimate, motivated by
[
47
,
48
,
35
,
2
]
)
.

For each
d\geqslant 1
there is a constant
C
such that

(1.14)

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant C\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
v\in\operatorname{supp}\widehat{u_{0}}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\rho^{\ast}w(x,v)\ \|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

for all
w
and
u_{0}
as before.

The reader may have noticed that the time localisations on the left-hand sides of (
1.13
) and (
1.14
) do not feature in our Wigner-function heuristics. However, an elementary scaling and limiting argument reveals that (
1.13
) and (
1.14
) are equivalent to the fully global and scale-invariant inequalities

(1.15)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{u_{0}}(v)|^{2}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho^{\ast}w(x,v)\,\mathrm{d}v,

and

(1.16)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant C\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
v\in\operatorname{supp}\widehat{u_{0}}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\rho^{\ast}w(x,v)\ \|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

respectively.
Indeed, if one wishes one may also spatially localise the left-hand sides of (
1.13
) and (
1.14
), replacing
[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
[0,1]^{d+1}
, without penalty; one simply applies a parabolic scaling to such an estimate to deduce that

\int_{[0,M]^{d}\times[0,M^{2}]}|u(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{u_{0}}(v)|^{2}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho^{\ast}w_{M}(x,Mv)\,\mathrm{d}v,

uniformly in
M\geqslant 1
, where
w_{M}(x,t)=M^{2}w(Mx,M^{2}t)
, and notices that
\rho^{\ast}w_{M}(x,Mv)=\rho^{\ast}w(Mx,v)
.
Alternatively, it is straightforward to observe that one may harmlessly impose a frequency localisation in the global inequalities (
1.15
) and (
1.16
), restricting attention to initial data
u_{0}
with Fourier support in the unit ball.

Clearly, (
1.14
) is weaker than (
1.13
).
These classical conjectures have often been formulated by replacing the underlying paraboloid
\{(\xi,|\xi|^{2}):\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
either with a more general hypersurface, or simply with the sphere. We refer to Sections 1 and 2 of
[
3
]
for some discussion on the origins of these problems and how the specific inequalities (
1.13
) and (
1.14
) fit into this wider geometric framework. We note that one may pass back and forth between these parabolic forms and the more familiar spherical forms using parabolic scaling and tensoring arguments; see
[
25
]
.

Very recently, these two claims were shown to be false by Cairo, who constructed a counterexample to the Mizohata–Takeuchi conjecture for every non-flat
\mathrm{C}^{2}
hypersurface
[
12
]
. (We note that there is nonetheless some evidence in their favour; see for example
[
37
,
3
]
and the references there.)
It is then natural to seek to quantify the failure of these estimates.
For
N\geqslant 1
, define
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(N)
to be the least constant
C
in (
1.13
) under the additional hypothesis

\operatorname{supp}\widehat{u_{0}}\subseteq[-N,N]^{d},

and define
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(N)
similarly using (
1.14
). Thus, the fact that estimates (
1.13
) and (
1.14
) do not hold can be restated as

\sup_{N}\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(N)=\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{N}\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(N)=\infty.

Cairo’s counterexample
[
12
]
actually gives
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(N)\geqslant\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(N)\gtrsim\log N
, while Cairo and Zhang
[
13
]
(also see
[
24
]
) later obtained counterexamples with a power loss,
N^{d/(d+k)-\varepsilon}
, for certain families of
\mathrm{C}^{k}
convex hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
; we refer to
[
14
]
for earlier indications of the relevance of combinatorial geometry to Mizohata–Takeuchi-type inequalities.
Complementary upper bounds of order
N^{d/(d+2)+\varepsilon}
for all strictly convex
\mathrm{C}^{2}
hypersurfaces were provided earlier by the work of Carbery, Iliopoulou and Wang
[
15
]
; see also Guth
[
26
]
.

1.4.
Weighted Strichartz estimates on
\mathbb{T}^{d+1}

The phase-space point of view developed in
[
3
]
remains relevant, and recent exponential-sum work such as
[
52
]
suggests that periodic and arithmetic models may deserve separate attention.

In order to formulate our periodic conjectures, we mimic the heuristics from the previous section, beginning with a Wigner transform on the torus,

W_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(g_{1},g_{2})(x,\kappa):=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}g_{1}(x+y)\overline{g_{2}(x-y)}e^{-2\pi i\kappa\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}y;\quad(x,\kappa)\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

We note that our normalisation of
W_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}
is slightly different from (
1.10
), since the factors
1/2
are missing from the input functions.
We do this “oversampling” in order to avoid an inconvenient aliasing phenomenon (see
[
16
]
) that would otherwise arise in the frequency marginal, interfering with the desired periodic analogue of the phase-space representation (
1.12
).

As in the Euclidean setting, for a periodic Schrödinger solution
(
1.8
), the function

f(x,\kappa,t):=W_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\bigl(u(\cdot,t),u(\cdot,t)\bigr)(x,\kappa);\quad(x,\kappa,t)\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}

satisfies a kinetic transport equation: this time the discrete-velocity equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f(x,\kappa,t)=\kappa\cdot\nabla_{x}f(x,\kappa,t).

Again, we note a difference between this equation and its Euclidean counterpart (
1.11
), arising from the slight difference in the definitions of the Euclidean and periodic Wigner distributions.
From the differential equation, or directly, we obtain the following identity: if
u
is the periodic Schrödinger solution (
1.8
), then

W_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(u(\cdot,t),u(\cdot,t))(x,\kappa)=W_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(u_{0},u_{0})(x+t\kappa,\kappa).

Using the marginal property

\sum_{\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}W_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(g,g)(x,\kappa)=|g(x)|^{2}

and Fubini’s theorem,
this leads to the phase-space representation

(1.17)

|u(x,t)|^{2}=\widetilde{\rho}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}W_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(u_{0},u_{0})(x,t),

where
\widetilde{\rho}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
is the discrete phase-space operator

\widetilde{\rho}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}f_{0}(x,t):=\sum_{\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}f_{0}(x+t\kappa,\kappa).

Observe that the adjoint of
\widetilde{\rho}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, defined on
1
-periodic space-time functions
w
by

\widetilde{\rho}^{\ast}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}w(x,\kappa)=\int_{0}^{1}w(x-t\kappa,t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

is now a (normalised) geodesic X-ray transform on the space-time torus
\mathbb{T}^{d+1}
with integer velocities.
In particular, for a weight function
w\geqslant 0
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}
, by (
1.17
) we have the phase-space integral representation

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|u(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}W_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(u_{0},u_{0})(x,\kappa)\widetilde{\rho}^{\ast}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}w(x,\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}x.

This representation is the periodic analogue of the Euclidean phase-space identity mentioned in the previous subsection and used in
[
3
]
.
Noting the other marginal property,

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}W_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(g,g)(x,\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}x=\begin{cases}|\widehat{g}(\kappa/2)|^{2}&\text{if }\kappa\in 2\mathbb{Z}^{d},\\
0&\text{if }\kappa\not\in 2\mathbb{Z}^{d},\end{cases}

one might tentatively formulate
periodic
(or
discrete
)
Stein
and
Mizohata–Takeuchi conjectures
as follows.
Denote

1.6.
Summary, notation and paper organisation

•

For every prime

\displaystyle\#B_{p}

\displaystyle=p+1,

(2.4)

\displaystyle=4(p-1)p(p+1),

\displaystyle=3(p-1)p(p+1),

Proof.

We first count the points of

. Since
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, the element
-1
is not a square in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
(i.e., it is a quadratic non-residue), so

for every

. For each of these parametrised points

. Conversely, if

and

, then, with

,

The remaining case

. This verifies (
2.3
).

Let us count

. Denote

. For

The vectors

x
to

. Then

\displaystyle\#B_{q}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\in P}(p+1),

\displaystyle\#\Box_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\in P}4(p-1)p(p+1),

\displaystyle\#\triangle_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\in P}3(p-1)p(p+1),

and, for every
k\in B_{q}
,

\#\triangle^{k}_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})=\prod_{p\in P}3(p-1)p.

Consequently,

\frac{\#\Box_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})}{\#\triangle_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})}=\Bigl(\frac{4}{3}\Bigr)^{\#P},\quad\frac{\#\Box_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})}{\#B_{q}\,\max_{k\in B_{q}}\#\triangle^{k}_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})}=\Bigl(\frac{4}{3}\Bigr)^{\#P}.

Proof.

Since the primes in
P
are pairwise coprime, the Chinese
remainder theorem gives a ring isomorphism

\pi\colon\mathbb{Z}_{q}\to\prod_{p\in P}\mathbb{F}_{p},\quad\alpha\mapsto(\alpha\!\!\!\!\mod p)_{p\in P}.

This isomorphism also maps the inverse of
2
in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
to the tuple of inverses of
2
in each
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
We also extend
\pi
to the Cartesian powers of
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
as

\displaystyle\pi\colon\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}\to\prod_{p\in P}\mathbb{F}_{p}^{m}\cong\Bigl(\prod_{p\in P}\mathbb{F}_{p}\Bigr)^{m},

\displaystyle\pi(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}):=\bigl((\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})\!\!\!\!\mod p\bigr)_{p\in P}\equiv\big(\pi(\alpha_{1}),\ldots,\pi(\alpha_{m})\big)

for every positive integer
m
.
These maps are still bijective. In particular,
\pi
maps
W_{q}
onto

\pi(W_{q})=\prod_{p\in P}\bigl(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}\bigr),

preserves the dot product,

\pi(x\cdot y)=\pi(x)\cdot\pi(y);\quad x,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{2},

and commutes with
J
,

\pi(Jx)=J\pi(x);\quad x\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{2}.

By our definitions,

\displaystyle\pi(B_{q})

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\in P}B_{p},

\displaystyle\pi\bigl(\Box_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\in P}\Box_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}(B_{p}),

\displaystyle\pi\bigl(\triangle_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\in P}\triangle_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}(B_{p}),

and

\pi\bigl(\triangle^{k}_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})\bigr)=\prod_{p\in P}\triangle^{k_{p}}_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}(B_{p})

for every
k\in B_{q}
such that
\pi(k)=(k_{p})_{p\in P}
.
Computing the cardinalities of these four Cartesian products using Lemma
2.2
immediately proves all four claimed identities.
∎

2.2.
Lattice lifting

A vector
w=(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is called
primitive
if
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are coprime. The following lemma converts modular “orthogonality” into exact lattice orthogonality. We state both the unpinned and pinned counts, and keep the dependence on
q
explicit, so that the later proofs can refer to a single uniform counting result.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
q
be an odd square-free positive integer and put

\eta_{q}:=\frac{8}{q^{2}}\prod_{p\nmid q}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\Bigr)>0,

where the product is over primes
p
not dividing
q
.

(i)
Let

E\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{2}\times W_{q}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}
. For
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0
, let
\mathcal{M}_{E}^{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}(N)
be the number of quadruples
(x,w,r,s)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\times\mathbb{Z}^{2}\times\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
such that

\displaystyle w\text{ is primitive},\quad(r,s)\neq(0,0),\quad\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant\alpha N,

\displaystyle\lVert rw\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant\beta N,\quad\lVert sw\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant\gamma N,\quad(x,w,r,s)\bmod q\in E.

Then, for
N\geqslant q
, uniformly in
E
,

(2.9)

\mathcal{M}_{E}^{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}(N)=\frac{16\eta_{q}}{q^{4}}\alpha^{2}\beta\gamma\,\#E\ N^{4}\log N+O_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\!\left(\frac{\#E}{q^{4}}N^{4}\right).

(ii)
Let
F\subseteq W_{q}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}
. For
\beta,\gamma>0
, let
\mathcal{N}_{F}^{\beta,\gamma}(N)
be the number of triples
(w,r,s)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\times\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
such that

w\text{ is primitive},\quad(r,s)\neq(0,0),\quad\lVert rw\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant\beta N,\quad\lVert sw\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant\gamma N,\quad(w,r,s)\bmod q\in F.

Then, for
N\geqslant q
, uniformly in
F
,

(2.10)

\mathcal{N}_{F}^{\beta,\gamma}(N)=\frac{4\eta_{q}}{q^{2}}\beta\gamma\,\#F\ N^{2}\log N+O_{\beta,\gamma}\left(\frac{\#F}{q^{2}}N^{2}\right).

Moreover,

(2.11)

\#A_{N}(q)=\frac{4\#B_{q}}{q^{2}}N^{2}+O\biggl(\frac{\#B_{q}}{q}N\biggr).

Proof.

We first carry out the calculation for one residue class

e=(x_{0},w_{0},r_{0},s_{0})\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{2}\times W_{q}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}
. The number of choices for
x
is

\frac{4\alpha^{2}}{q^{2}}N^{2}+O_{\alpha}\left(\frac{N}{q}\right),

where we used
N\geqslant q
. For
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}
, write

\mathcal{A}_{a}(Y):=\#\{n\in\mathbb{Z}\,:\,n\equiv a\!\!\!\pmod{q},\ |n|\leqslant Y\}=\frac{2Y}{q}+O(1),

which holds uniformly in
a
and
Y\geqslant 0
. Since
(r,s)\neq(0,0)
, every contributing
w
has
\lVert w\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant CN
, where
C:=\max\{\beta,\gamma\}
. For a fixed primitive
w\equiv w_{0}\pmod{q}
, the choices for
(r,s)
are counted by

\mathcal{A}_{r_{0}}\!\Bigl(\frac{\beta N}{\lVert w\rVert_{\infty}}\Bigr)\mathcal{A}_{s_{0}}\!\Bigl(\frac{\gamma N}{\lVert w\rVert_{\infty}}\Bigr),

with the forbidden pair
(r,s)=(0,0)
subtracted when
r_{0}=s_{0}=0
. Also

\mathcal{A}_{r_{0}}(Y)\mathcal{A}_{s_{0}}(Z)=\frac{4YZ}{q^{2}}+O\left(\frac{Y}{q}\right)+O\left(\frac{Z}{q}\right)+O(1).

We now check the error terms in this summation. Choose a representative
\widetilde{w}_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
of
w_{0}
with
\lVert\widetilde{w}_{0}\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant q/2
. Every
w\equiv w_{0}\pmod{q}
is of the form
\widetilde{w}_{0}+qz
. Hence, for
N\geqslant q
,

\#\{w\,:\,0<\lVert w\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant CN,\ w\equiv w_{0}\!\!\!\pmod{q}\}=O_{\beta,\gamma}\left(\frac{N^{2}}{q^{2}}\right).

Thus the forbidden pair
(r,s)=(0,0)
, when it occurs, and the summed contribution of the
O(1)
term in the product formula are both
O_{\beta,\gamma}(N^{2}/q^{2})
. Also, separating the possible term
z=0
, and for a constant
C^{\prime}
depending only on
C
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\lVert w\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant CN\\
w\equiv w_{0}\!\!\!\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\lVert w\rVert_{\infty}}\lesssim_{\beta,\gamma}1+\frac{1}{q}\sum_{0<\lVert z\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant C^{\prime}(N/q+1)}\frac{1}{\lVert z\rVert_{\infty}}\lesssim_{\beta,\gamma}1+\frac{N}{q^{2}}.

Therefore the terms
O(Y/q)+O(Z/q)
contribute

O_{\beta,\gamma}\biggl(\frac{N}{q}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\lVert w\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant CN\\
w\equiv w_{0}\!\!\!\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\lVert w\rVert_{\infty}}\biggr)=O_{\beta,\gamma}\left(\frac{N}{q}+\frac{N^{2}}{q^{3}}\right)=O_{\beta,\gamma}\left(\frac{N^{2}}{q^{2}}\right),

where the last step uses
N\geqslant q
.
Thus the
(w,r,s)
count in one residue class is

(2.12)

\frac{4\beta\gamma N^{2}}{q^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\lVert w\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant CN\\
w\equiv w_{0}\!\!\!\pmod{q}\\
w\text{ primitive}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\lVert w\rVert_{\infty}^{2}}+O_{\beta,\gamma}\left(\frac{N^{2}}{q^{2}}\right).

It remains to evaluate the primitive sum. Since
w_{0}\in W_{q}
, every common divisor
d
of the two coordinates of a vector
w\equiv w_{0}\pmod{q}
is coprime to
q
, and therefore
d^{-1}
exists in
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
. By the Möbius inversion formula we have

\mathbbm{1}_{w\,\mathrm{primitive}}=\sum_{d\mid\mathrm{gcd}(w_{1},w_{2})}\mu(d),

so that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\lVert w\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant CN\\
w\equiv w_{0}\!\!\!\pmod{q}\\
w\text{ primitive}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\lVert w\rVert_{\infty}^{2}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\lVert w\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant CN\\
w\equiv w_{0}\!\!\!\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\sum_{d\mid\mathrm{gcd}(w_{1},w_{2})}\frac{\mu(d)}{\lVert w\rVert_{\infty}^{2}}.

Note that the latter sum is over all (not necessarily primitive)
w
divisible by
d
. By interchanging the sums and performing the substitution
w=dz
, we obtain

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\geqslant 1\\
d,q\text{ coprime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\lVert z\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant CN/d\\
z\equiv d^{-1}w_{0}\!\!\!\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\lVert z\rVert_{\infty}^{2}}.

For any residue class
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{2}
and
T\geqslant 0
,

\#\{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\,:\,0<\lVert z\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant T,\ z\equiv a\!\!\!\pmod{q}\}=\frac{4T^{2}}{q^{2}}+O\left(\frac{T}{q}\right)+O(1),

uniformly in
a
and
q
. Partial summation therefore gives, uniformly in
a
and
q
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\lVert z\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant X\\
z\equiv a\!\!\!\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\lVert z\rVert_{\infty}^{2}}=\frac{8}{q^{2}}\log X+O(1)

for
X\geqslant 2
: the
O(T/q)+O(1)
error contributes

O\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)+O(1)+O\left(\frac{1}{qX}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{X^{2}}\right)=O(1).

If
CN<2
, then the primitive sum and
\eta_{q}\log N
are both
O_{\beta,\gamma}(1)
, so the desired estimate for the primitive sum is immediate. We may therefore assume
CN\geqslant 2
. Put
D=\lfloor CN/2\rfloor
and split the sum in
d
at the number
D
.
In the range
d\leqslant D
we have
CN/d\geqslant 2
, so this part of the sum contributes

\frac{8}{q^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\leqslant D\\
d,q\text{ coprime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^{2}}(\log N+\log C-\log d)+O_{\beta,\gamma}(1).

The terms involving
\log C
,
\log d
and the displayed error are
O_{\beta,\gamma}(1)
, since
\sum_{d\geqslant 1}(1+\log d)/d^{2}<\infty
. Extending the truncated coefficient of
\log N
to the infinite Euler product costs

O_{\beta,\gamma}\biggl(\frac{\log N}{q^{2}}\sum_{d>D}\frac{1}{d^{2}}\biggr)=O_{\beta,\gamma}(1),

and the range
d>D
contributes only
O_{\beta,\gamma}(1)
. Therefore

(2.13)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\lVert w\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant CN\\
w\equiv w_{0}\!\!\!\pmod{q}\\
w\text{ primitive}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\lVert w\rVert_{\infty}^{2}}=\eta_{q}\log N+O_{\beta,\gamma}(1).

Combining (
2.12
) and (
2.13
) gives

\frac{4\eta_{q}}{q^{2}}\beta\gamma N^{2}\log N+O_{\beta,\gamma}(N^{2}/q^{2})

for the
(w,r,s)
count in one residue class
(w_{0},r_{0},s_{0})
. Summing over the residue classes in
F
proves (
2.10
).

Multiplying the one-residue-class
(w,r,s)
count by the
x
count gives the stated main term. The product of the main
x
term with the
(w,r,s)
error is
O_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}(N^{4}/q^{4})
, while the product of the
x
error with the
(w,r,s)
main term is

O_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\!\left(\frac{\eta_{q}N^{3}\log N}{q^{3}}\right)=O_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\!\left(\frac{N^{4}}{q^{4}}\right),

since
\eta_{q}\leqslant 8/q^{2}
and
\log N\leqslant Nq
. The remaining product of errors is also
O_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}(N^{4}/q^{4})
because
N\geqslant q
. Thus the one-residue-class count is

\frac{16\eta_{q}}{q^{4}}\alpha^{2}\beta\gamma N^{4}\log N+O_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\!\left(\frac{N^{4}}{q^{4}}\right).

Summing over
e\in E
proves (
2.9
).

Finally, for each residue class
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{2}
,

\#\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\,:\,\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant N,\ x\equiv a\!\!\!\pmod{q}\}=\frac{4N^{2}}{q^{2}}+O\left(\frac{N}{q}\right),

because
N\geqslant q
. Summing over
a\in B_{q}
proves (
2.11
).
∎

2.3.
The asymptotic lower bounds

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

Fix
P
,
q
and
B_{q}
as in the proposition statement.
We shall choose
C_{0}\geqslant 1
at the end. Throughout we also use the elementary lower bound

(2.14)

\eta_{q}=\frac{8}{q^{2}}\prod_{p\nmid q}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)\geqslant\frac{8}{\zeta(2)q^{2}}.

We first lift rectangles. Take a tuple counted by

\mathcal{M}_{\Box_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})}^{1/4,1/4,1/4}(N)
. The residue condition says that

x,\quad x+rw,\quad x+sJw,\quad x+rw+sJw

all have residues in
B_{q}
. The size restrictions give

\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant\frac{N}{4},\quad\lVert rw\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant\frac{N}{4},\quad\lVert sJw\rVert_{\infty}=\lVert sw\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant\frac{N}{4},

and hence all four displayed points lie in
[-N,N]^{2}
. They therefore lie in
A_{N}(q)
. Moreover,

(x+rw)+(x+sJw)=x+(x+rw+sJw),\quad(rw)\cdot(sJw)=0,

so these four points form an ordered rectangle in the sense of the definition of
\Box(A_{N}(q))
. Since
(r,s)\neq(0,0)
and
w
is primitive, the two side vectors are not both zero. Conversely, a fixed ordered rectangle arising in this way has at most two primitive-direction representations of the form
rw
and
sJw
, namely those corresponding to
w
and
-w
: if
rw\neq 0
, then
w
is the primitive direction of
rw
up to sign, while if
rw=0
then
sJw\neq 0
and
w
is determined up to sign by
sJw
. Hence Lemma
2.4
, with
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(1/4,1/4,1/4)
, gives the uniform lower bound

\#\Box(A_{N}(q))\geqslant\frac{\eta_{q}}{32q^{4}}\#\Box_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})N^{4}\log N-O\left(\frac{\#\Box_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})}{q^{4}}N^{4}\right).

Using (
2.14
), this can also be written as

(2.15)

\#\Box(A_{N}(q))\geqslant\frac{\eta_{q}}{32q^{4}}\#\Box_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})N^{4}\log N\left(1-O\Bigl(\frac{q^{2}}{\log N}\Bigr)\right).

We next control the unpinned triangle count. Let

(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})\in\triangle(A_{N}(q))
and put

d=k_{2}-k_{1},\quad h=2k_{3}-k_{1}-k_{2}.

Then
d\cdot h=0
. The case
d=h=0
forces
k_{1}=k_{2}=k_{3}
and contributes at most
\#A_{N}(q)\leqslant(2N+1)^{2}
triples. In every other case there is a primitive vector
w\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and integers
r,s
, unique up to simultaneous sign change, such that

d=rw,\quad h=sJw.

Indeed, if
d\neq 0
we take
w
to be the primitive vector in the direction of
d
; since
h
is an integer vector perpendicular to the primitive vector
w
, it is an integer multiple of
Jw
. The case
d=0
and
h\neq 0
is the same argument applied to
h
and
Jw
. Thus the possibilities
d=0
and
h=0
are included by allowing
r=0
and
s=0
, respectively. The norm bounds are

\lVert k_{1}\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant N,\quad\lVert rw\rVert_{\infty}=\lVert k_{2}-k_{1}\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant 2N,\quad\lVert sw\rVert_{\infty}=\lVert h\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant 4N.

Since
w
is primitive,
w\bmod q\in W_{q}
. Also, modulo
q
,

k_{1},\quad k_{1}+rw=k_{2},\quad k_{1}+\frac{rw+sJw}{2}=k_{3}

all lie in
B_{q}
. Hence the two choices
(w,r,s)
and
(-w,-r,-s)
give two tuples counted by

\mathcal{M}_{\triangle_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})}^{1,2,4}(N)
. The modular count may include additional tuples for which
rw+sJw
is not divisible by
2
in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, but that only enlarges the count. Therefore

\#\triangle(A_{N}(q))\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{M}_{\triangle_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})}^{1,2,4}(N)+O(N^{2}).

Applying Lemma
2.4
with
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(1,2,4)
yields

\#\triangle(A_{N}(q))\leqslant\frac{64\eta_{q}}{q^{4}}\#\triangle_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})N^{4}\log N+O\left(\frac{\#\triangle_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})}{q^{4}}N^{4}\right)+O(N^{2}).

The first error term is the main term times
O(q^{2}/\log N)
, by (
2.14
). The degenerate contribution
O(N^{2})
is absorbed by the same relative error: Lemma
2.3
gives
\#\triangle_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})\gtrsim q^{3}
, and so, using (
2.14
) and
N\geqslant q
,

N^{2}=O\left(\frac{\eta_{q}}{q^{4}}\#\triangle_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})N^{4}\log N\cdot\frac{q^{2}}{\log N}\right).

Thus

(2.16)

\#\triangle(A_{N}(q))\leqslant\frac{64\eta_{q}}{q^{4}}\#\triangle_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})N^{4}\log N\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{q^{2}}{\log N}\Bigr)\right).

It remains to estimate the pinned triangle count. Fix
k\in A_{N}(q)
and put
k_{0}=k\bmod q\in B_{q}
. If
(k_{1},k_{2},k)\in\triangle^{k}(A_{N}(q))
, set

d=k_{2}-k_{1},\quad h=2k-k_{1}-k_{2}.

The case
d=h=0
contributes only the pair
k_{1}=k_{2}=k
. In every other case the same primitive-direction argument gives
w\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
primitive and
r,s\in\mathbb{Z}
, unique up to simultaneous sign change, such that

d=rw,\quad h=sJw,\quad\lVert rw\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant 2N,\quad\lVert sw\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant 4N.

Modulo
q
the two base points can be recovered from the apex by

k_{1}=k-\frac{rw+sJw}{2},\quad k_{2}=k+\frac{rw-sJw}{2},

and they lie in
B_{q}
. Thus, again allowing the harmless overcount from parity, we have

\#\triangle^{k}(A_{N}(q))\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{N}_{\triangle^{k_{0}}_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})}^{2,4}(N)+1.

Lemma
2.4
, now with
(\beta,\gamma)=(2,4)
, gives, uniformly in
k\in A_{N}(q)
,

\#\triangle^{k}(A_{N}(q))\leqslant\frac{16\eta_{q}}{q^{2}}\#\triangle^{k_{0}}_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})N^{2}\log N+O\left(\frac{\#\triangle^{k_{0}}_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})}{q^{2}}N^{2}\right)+1.

The displayed error term is the main term times
O(q^{2}/\log N)
, by (
2.14
). The final
+1
is also absorbed uniformly: Lemma
2.3
gives
\max_{b\in B_{q}}\#\triangle^{b}_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})\gtrsim q^{2}
, and hence, using (
2.14
) and
N\geqslant q
,

1=O\left(\frac{\eta_{q}}{q^{2}}\max_{b\in B_{q}}\#\triangle^{b}_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})N^{2}\log N\cdot\frac{q^{2}}{\log N}\right).

Consequently, again uniformly in
k\in A_{N}(q)
,

(2.17)

\max_{k\in A_{N}(q)}\#\triangle^{k}(A_{N}(q))\leqslant\frac{16\eta_{q}}{q^{2}}\max_{b\in B_{q}}\#\triangle^{b}_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})N^{2}\log N\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{q^{2}}{\log N}\Bigr)\right).

Finally, Lemma
2.4
also gives

(2.18)

\#A_{N}(q)=\frac{4\#B_{q}}{q^{2}}N^{2}+O\left(\frac{\#B_{q}}{q}N\right)=\frac{4\#B_{q}}{q^{2}}N^{2}\left(1+O\Bigl(\frac{q}{N}\Bigr)\right).

Choosing
C_{0}
sufficiently large in (
2.1
) makes the relative error terms
O(q^{2}/\log N)
and
O(q/N)
arbitrarily small, so that they can be absorbed into the corresponding dominant terms.
Combining the preceding estimates, we obtain

\mathfrak{Q}(A_{N}(q))\gtrsim\frac{\#\Box_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})}{\#\triangle_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})},

and

\mathfrak{Q}^{\mathrm{pin}}(A_{N}(q))\gtrsim\frac{\#\Box_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})}{\#B_{q}\,\max_{b\in B_{q}}\#\triangle^{b}_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})}.

Applying Lemma
2.3
proves (
2.2
).
∎

We can now finalise the proof of our main combinatorial result.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Bounds for
\mathfrak{R}(N)
and
\mathfrak{R}^{\mathrm{pin}}(N)
are now almost immediate.
Let
p_{1}<p_{2}<\cdots
be the first
m
primes congruent to
3
modulo
4
, and put
q_{m}=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{m}
. By the prime number theorem in arithmetic progressions
[
19
, §20–22]
,
[
23
, §7.5]
, there is an absolute constant
C_{1}
such that

q_{m}\leqslant\exp(C_{1}m\log m)

for
m\geqslant 2
.
Choosing

m(N):=\left\lfloor c_{1}\frac{\log\log N}{\log\log\log N}\right\rfloor,

where
c_{1}>0
is a sufficiently small absolute constant, gives
q_{m}^{2}\leqslant(\log N)^{\theta}
with
\theta<1
. Then, for all sufficiently large
N
, the modulus
q_{m(N)}
satisfies
N\geqslant\exp(C_{0}q_{m(N)}^{2})
, where
C_{0}
is the constant from Proposition
2.1
.
Applying that proposition with
q=q_{m(N)}
gives

\mathfrak{R}(N),\ \mathfrak{R}^{\mathrm{pin}}(N)\gtrsim\Bigl(\frac{4}{3}\Bigr)^{m(N)},

which is the claimed bound.

Now we turn to the bounds for
\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}(n)
and
\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}^{\mathrm{pin}}(n)
.
Again let
q=q_{m}=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{m}
be the product of the first
m
primes congruent to
3
modulo
4
. There is an absolute constant
C_{2}
such that, whenever

(2.19)

n\geqslant e^{C_{2}q^{2}},

there is an
n
-point set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
satisfying

(2.20)

\mathfrak{Q}(A),\ \mathfrak{Q}^{\mathrm{pin}}(A)\gtrsim\Bigl(\frac{4}{3}\Bigr)^{m}.

Indeed, choose a nonnegative integer
N
maximal subject to

\#A_{N}(q)\leqslant n
, and then choose an intermediate set

A_{N}(q)\subseteq A\subseteq A_{N+1}(q),\quad\#A=n.

The individual configuration counts are monotone under inclusion. Thus

\mathfrak{Q}(A)\geqslant\frac{\#\Box(A_{N}(q))}{\#\triangle(A_{N+1}(q))},

and

\mathfrak{Q}^{\mathrm{pin}}(A)\geqslant\frac{\#\Box(A_{N}(q))}{\#A_{N+1}(q)\,\max_{k\in A_{N+1}(q)}\limits\#\triangle^{k}(A_{N+1}(q))}.

We now spell out the uniform estimates needed for these two right-hand sides. The estimates (
2.15
), (
2.16
), (
2.17
) and (
2.18
), applied with
M
in place of
N
, imply the following: after increasing an absolute constant
C
if necessary, whenever
M\geqslant\exp(Cq^{2})
,

\displaystyle\#\Box(A_{M}(q))

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\eta_{q}}{64q^{4}}\#\Box_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})M^{4}\log M,

\displaystyle\#\triangle(A_{M}(q))

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{128\eta_{q}}{q^{4}}\#\triangle_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})M^{4}\log M,

\displaystyle\max_{k\in A_{M}(q)}\#\triangle^{k}(A_{M}(q))

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{32\eta_{q}}{q^{2}}\max_{b\in B_{q}}\#\triangle^{b}_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})M^{2}\log M,

\displaystyle\#A_{M}(q)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{8\#B_{q}}{q^{2}}M^{2}.

The maximality of
N
gives
n\leqslant\#A_{N+1}(q)\leqslant(2N+3)^{2}
, hence
N\geqslant\sqrt{n}/4
for all large
n
. After increasing
C_{2}
, condition (
2.19
) ensures
N\geqslant\exp(Cq^{2})
, and the same bound with
N+1
in place of
N
. Applying the displayed estimates with
M=N
in the numerator and with
M=N+1
in the denominator gives

\mathfrak{Q}(A)\gtrsim\frac{\#\Box_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})N^{4}\log N}{\#\triangle_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})(N+1)^{4}\log(N+1)}\gtrsim\frac{\#\Box_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})}{\#\triangle_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})}=\Bigl(\frac{4}{3}\Bigr)^{m},

and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Q}^{\mathrm{pin}}(A)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\frac{\#\Box_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})N^{4}\log N}{\#B_{q}(N+1)^{2}\max_{b\in B_{q}}\#\triangle^{b}_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})(N+1)^{2}\log(N+1)}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\frac{\#\Box_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})}{\#B_{q}\,\max_{b\in B_{q}}\#\triangle^{b}_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(B_{q})}=\Bigl(\frac{4}{3}\Bigr)^{m}.

This proves (
2.20
).

Now choose

m(n):=\left\lfloor c_{2}\frac{\log\log n}{\log\log\log n}\right\rfloor

with
c_{2}>0
sufficiently small. Then,
n\geqslant\exp(C_{2}q_{m(n)}^{2})
holds for all sufficiently large
n
.
Applying (
2.20
) with
q=q_{m(n)}
gives

\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}(n),\ \widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}^{\mathrm{pin}}(n)\gtrsim\Bigl(\frac{4}{3}\Bigr)^{m(n)},

as desired.
∎

3.
Equivalence of the Euclidean and periodic formulations

This section proves the quantitative Euclidean-to-periodic transference stated in Theorem
1.3
. We also record a converse argument since it clarifies the close relationship of the periodic Stein-type and Mizohata–Takeuchi inequalities to their Euclidean counterparts.

Throughout this section,
d\geqslant 1
is fixed. In the Euclidean estimates, “suitable” initial data means that
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
has compactly supported Fourier transform, so that the representation (
1.6
) is absolutely convergent. In the periodic estimates, suitable data are trigonometric polynomials, equivalently
u_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
with finite Fourier support, so that (
1.8
) is a finite sum. We identify functions on
\mathbb{T}^{n}
with their
1
-periodic representatives on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whenever this causes no ambiguity.

For a finite set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and coefficients
a=(a_{k})_{k\in A}
, it will be convenient to write

E_{A}a(x,t):=\sum_{k\in A}a_{k}e^{2\pi i(x\cdot k+t|k|^{2})},

noting that
E_{A}a
is the general solution to the periodic Schrödinger equation for the class of initial data under consideration.
We will use the following elementary reduction several times. For fixed
A
, it is enough to prove the periodic estimates for continuous weights. Indeed, let
P_{\varepsilon}
be a nonnegative smooth approximate identity on
\mathbb{T}^{d+1}
. Since
E_{A}a
is bounded,
P_{\varepsilon}\ast w\to w
in
\mathrm{L}^{1}
implies convergence of the common left-hand side of the forthcoming (
3.1
) and (
3.2
). On the other hand,

\rho_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}^{\ast}(P_{\varepsilon}\ast w)(x,k)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d+1}}P_{\varepsilon}(y,s)\rho_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}^{\ast}w(x-y-2sk,k)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}s,

and hence

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}^{\ast}(P_{\varepsilon}\ast w)(x,k)\leqslant\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}^{\ast}w(x,k).

We note in passing that one may avoid approximating on the left-hand side here if one instead uses the local constancy property of
E_{A}a
that comes from its finite Fourier support. Local constancy is routinely used in this way in weighted extension theory; see
[
15
]
, for example.

3.1.
From Euclidean to periodic

Our first goal is to prove the direction claimed in Theorem
1.3
. The proof is a somewhat standard wave-packet “thickening” argument.

Fix
N\geqslant 1
and a finite set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap[-N,N]^{d}
. We first prove

(3.1)

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|E_{A}a(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(N+1)\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\\
k\in A\end{subarray}}\rho^{\ast}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}w(x,k)\sum_{k\in A}|a_{k}|^{2},

for every nonnegative weight
w
on
\mathbb{T}^{d+1}
. We then prove the corresponding Stein-type estimate

(3.2)

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|E_{A}a(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(N+1)\sum_{k\in A}|a_{k}|^{2}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho^{\ast}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}w(x,k).

Since
E_{A}a
is the periodic Schrödinger solution with initial data
u_{0}(x)=\sum_{k\in A}a_{k}e^{2\pi ix\cdot k}
and
\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=\|a\|_{\ell^{2}(A)}
, inequalities (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) imply (
1.22
) and (
1.21
), respectively, after taking the least admissible constants.

By the reduction above we may suppose that
w
is continuous. Define

X_{w}(v):=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{1}w(x-2tv,t)\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Because
w
is continuous and periodic in
x
, the function
X_{w}
is uniformly continuous on bounded subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; indeed,

|X_{w}(v_{1})-X_{w}(v_{2})|\leqslant\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{1}|w(x-2tv_{1},t)-w(x-2tv_{2},t)|\,\mathrm{d}t.

Choose a Schwartz function
\varphi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\widehat{\varphi}
is compactly supported and
\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=1
. For
L\geqslant 1
, put
\varphi_{L}(x)=\varphi(L^{-1}x)
, so that
L^{-d}|\widehat{\varphi_{L}}|^{2}
is an approximate identity at the origin. For
L
sufficiently large, the sets
k+\operatorname{supp}\widehat{\varphi_{L}}
,
k\in A
, are pairwise disjoint and are contained in
[-N-1,N+1]^{d}
. Define

F_{L}(\xi):=\sum_{k\in A}a_{k}\widehat{\varphi_{L}}(\xi-k)

and let

u_{L}(x,t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F_{L}(\xi)e^{2\pi i(x\cdot\xi+t|\xi|^{2})}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

We apply the Euclidean estimates to
u_{L}
with the Euclidean weight equal to
w(x,t)\mathbbm{1}_{[0,1]}(t)
.

First,

\displaystyle L^{-d}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{L}(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}L^{-d}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|u_{L}(x+j,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

For fixed
(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}^{d+1}
, the quantity
u_{L}(x+j,t)
is the
j
-th Fourier coefficient, in the variable
\eta\in[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]^{d}
, of

\eta\mapsto\sum_{k\in A}a_{k}e^{2\pi i(x\cdot(\eta+k)+t|\eta+k|^{2})}\widehat{\varphi_{L}}(\eta),

provided
L
is large enough that
\operatorname{supp}\widehat{\varphi_{L}}\subseteq[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]^{d}
. By Parseval’s identity,

\displaystyle L^{-d}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|u_{L}(x+j,t)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]^{d}}\left|\sum_{k\in A}a_{k}e^{2\pi i(x\cdot(\eta+k)+t|\eta+k|^{2})}\right|^{2}L^{-d}|\widehat{\varphi_{L}}(\eta)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\eta.

Since
L^{-d}|\widehat{\varphi_{L}}|^{2}
is an approximate identity of mass
1
, this converges uniformly in
(x,t)
to
|E_{A}a(x,t)|^{2}
. Hence

(3.3)

L^{-d}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{L}(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\longrightarrow\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|E_{A}a(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

Also, by Plancherel’s theorem and the disjointness of the translated supports,

(3.4)

L^{-d}\|u_{L}(\cdot,0)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=L^{-d}\|F_{L}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=\sum_{k\in A}|a_{k}|^{2}.

Finally, if

A_{L}:=\bigcup_{k\in A}(k+\operatorname{supp}\widehat{\varphi_{L}}),

then the uniform continuity of
X_{w}
gives

(3.5)

\sup_{v\in A_{L}}X_{w}(v)\longrightarrow\max_{k\in A}X_{w}(k)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\\
k\in A\end{subarray}}\rho^{\ast}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}w(x,k).

Applying the Euclidean Mizohata–Takeuchi inequality with constant
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(N+1)
gives

L^{-d}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{L}(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(N+1)\sup_{v\in A_{L}}X_{w}(v)L^{-d}\|u_{L}(\cdot,0)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}.

Letting
L\to\infty
and using (
3.3
), (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) proves (
3.1
).

For the Stein-type estimate, applying (
1.13
) with constant
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(N+1)
gives

L^{-d}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{L}(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(N+1)L^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|F_{L}(v)|^{2}X_{w}(v)\,\mathrm{d}v.

The right-hand side converges to

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(N+1)\sum_{k\in A}|a_{k}|^{2}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho^{\ast}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}w(x,k),

because the translated supports of
\widehat{\varphi_{L}}
are disjoint and
X_{w}
is uniformly continuous near
A
. Together with (
3.3
), this proves (
3.2
) and completes the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

3.2.
From periodic to Euclidean

Suppose that the periodic Stein-type and Mizohata–Takeuchi inequalities (
1.18
) and (
1.19
) hold, or equivalently that (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) hold with a constant
C
in place of
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(N+1)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(N+1)
, respectively. Here we show that the global Euclidean Stein-type and Mizohata–Takeuchi inequalities (
1.15
) and (
1.16
) may be recovered. Since these are known to be false (disproved by Cairo in
[
12
]
), this provides an alternative way to conclude the failure of the global periodic estimates (
1.18
) and (
1.19
). As we shall see, our argument relies heavily on scaling and approximation, meaning that we are unable to conclude a reverse form of the finitary (band-limited) Theorem
1.3
.

We first record the scaling of the periodic estimates. For
T>0
, let

Q_{T}:=[-T/2,T/2]^{d}\times[-T^{2}/2,T^{2}/2].

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be finite, and let
w
be a nonnegative function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
that is periodic with spatial period
T
in each coordinate and temporal period
T^{2}
. If the finite-frequency periodic Mizohata–Takeuchi estimate holds with constant
C
, then

\displaystyle\int_{Q_{T}}

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{1}{T^{d}}\sum_{\xi\in T^{-1}A}a_{\xi}e^{2\pi i(x\cdot\xi+t|\xi|^{2})}\bigg|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

(3.6)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
\xi\in T^{-1}A\end{subarray}}\int_{-T^{2}/2}^{T^{2}/2}w(x-2t\xi,t)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\frac{1}{T^{d}}\sum_{\xi\in T^{-1}A}|a_{\xi}|^{2}.

Similarly, if the finite-frequency periodic Stein-type estimate holds with constant
C
, then

\displaystyle\int_{Q_{T}}

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{1}{T^{d}}\sum_{\xi\in T^{-1}A}a_{\xi}e^{2\pi i(x\cdot\xi+t|\xi|^{2})}\bigg|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

(3.7)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C}{T^{d}}\sum_{\xi\in T^{-1}A}|a_{\xi}|^{2}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{-T^{2}/2}^{T^{2}/2}w(x-2t\xi,t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Proof.

We prove (
3.1
); the proof of (
3.1
) is the same with the supremum taken outside the sum. Define

w_{T}(y,s):=T^{2}w(Ty,T^{2}s),

which is
1
-periodic in
(y,s)
. Changing variables
x=Ty
,
t=T^{2}s
and writing
m=T\xi
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{Q_{T}}\bigg|\frac{1}{T^{d}}\sum_{\xi\in T^{-1}A}a_{\xi}e^{2\pi i(x\cdot\xi+t|\xi|^{2})}\bigg|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\bigg|\sum_{m\in A}a_{m/T}e^{2\pi i(y\cdot m+s|m|^{2})}\bigg|^{2}w_{T}(y,s)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}s.

Applying the finite-frequency periodic Stein-type estimate with constant
C
to the last integral gives

\frac{C}{T^{d}}\sum_{m\in A}|a_{m/T}|^{2}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}w_{T}(y-2sm,s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

The last X-ray transform scales as

\sup_{y\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}w_{T}(y-2sm,s)\,\mathrm{d}s=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{-T^{2}/2}^{T^{2}/2}w(x-2t(m/T),t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

where the interval of integration may be centred because the integrand is
1
-periodic in
s
. This proves (
3.1
).
∎

We now pass to the Euclidean limit.
Evidently it suffices to establish the manifestly stronger global inequalities (
1.15
) and (
1.16
) from their periodic counterparts (
1.18
) and (
1.19
). By the parabolic scale invariance of (
1.15
) and (
1.16
) it suffices to consider initial data with
\operatorname{supp}\widehat{u_{0}}\subseteq B(0,1)
.
We may also restrict attention to continuous compactly supported (Euclidean) weights
w
, as the general case then follows by standard approximation arguments.

Next we observe that we may further assume that
\widehat{u_{0}}
is smooth. If
\widehat{u_{n,0}}\to\widehat{u_{0}}
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}
with
\operatorname{supp}\widehat{u_{n,0}}\subseteq B(0,1)
for all
n
, and

u_{n}(x,t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{u_{n,0}}(\xi)e^{2\pi i(x\cdot\xi+t|\xi|^{2})}\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

then
u_{n}\to u
uniformly, and so

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{n}(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\longrightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

by the assumed regularity of
w
.
Similarly, the right-hand side of (
1.15
) also converges suitably by the uniform continuity of the map

(3.8)

X_{w}(v):=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}w(x-2tv,t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

recalling that
w
is continuous and compactly supported. For the right-hand side of (
1.16
) we make the additional assumption, as we may, that the support of
\widehat{u_{n,0}}
is decreasing to that of
\widehat{u_{0}}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, so that

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
v\in\operatorname{supp}\widehat{u_{n,0}}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\rho^{\ast}w(x,v)\rightarrow\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
v\in\operatorname{supp}\widehat{u_{0}}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\rho^{\ast}w(x,v),

again by the uniform continuity of
X_{w}
.
We may therefore assume that
\widehat{u_{0}}
is supported in
B(0,1)
and smooth.

Fix a continuous nonnegative
w
supported in
[-R,R]^{d+1}
, for some
R>0
.
For
T>0
define the full space-time periodisation

w^{T}(x,t):=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}w(x+Tj,t+T^{2}l).

For
T
sufficiently large,
w^{T}=w
on
Q_{T}
. Moreover, if
v\in B(0,1)\cap T^{-1}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, then the compatibility of the velocity
v=m/T
with the periods
T
and
T^{2}
, together with the support of
w
, gives

(3.9)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{-T^{2}/2}^{T^{2}/2}w^{T}(x-2tv,t)\,\mathrm{d}t=X_{w}(v)

for all sufficiently large
T
, uniformly in such
v
.
This follows from the support hypothesis on
w
, which means that the sum

\int_{-T^{2}/2}^{T^{2}/2}w^{T}(x-2tv,t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{-R}^{R}w^{T}(x-2tv,t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\int_{-R}^{R}w(x+Tj-2tv,t)\,\mathrm{d}t

has at most one nonzero term for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
|v|\leqslant 1
and
T
sufficiently large compared with
R
. This reduces (
3.9
) to the definition (
3.8
).

The Riemann sums

U_{T}(x,t):=\frac{1}{T^{d}}\sum_{\xi\in T^{-1}\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\widehat{u_{0}}(\xi)e^{2\pi i(x\cdot\xi+t|\xi|^{2})}

are finite and converge uniformly to

u(x,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{u_{0}}(\xi)e^{2\pi i(x\cdot\xi+t|\xi|^{2})}\,\mathrm{d}\xi

on the compact support of
w
. Hence

(3.10)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{Q_{T}}|U_{T}(x,t)|^{2}w^{T}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

Applying (
3.1
) to the right-hand side and using (
3.9
), we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leqslant C\limsup_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T^{d}}\sum_{\xi\in T^{-1}\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\widehat{u_{0}}(\xi)|^{2}X_{w}(\xi)

\displaystyle=C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{u_{0}}(v)|^{2}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}w(x-2tv,t)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}v,

establishing (
1.15
).

The Mizohata–Takeuchi estimate (
1.16
) follows in a similar way from (
3.1
). Indeed,

\frac{1}{T^{d}}\sum_{\xi\in T^{-1}\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\widehat{u_{0}}(\xi)|^{2}\longrightarrow\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2},

and, by the continuity of
X_{w}
on
B(0,1)
,

\sup_{\xi\in T^{-1}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap\,\operatorname{supp}\widehat{u_{0}}}X_{w}(\xi)\longrightarrow\sup_{v\in\operatorname{supp}\widehat{u_{0}}}X_{w}(v).

The inequality (
1.16
) now follows
from (
3.1
) applied to (
3.10
).

4.
Periodic constants from configuration lower bounds

We now prove Theorem
1.4
.
Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be a nonempty and finite set of frequencies.
Then

u(x,t)=u_{A}(x,t):=\sum_{k\in A}e^{2\pi i(x\cdot k+t|k|^{2})}

is the solution (
1.8
) of the periodic Schrödinger equation with the initial data

u_{0}(x)=u_{A}(x,0)=\sum_{k\in A}e^{2\pi ix\cdot k},

which clearly has Fourier coefficients

\widehat{u_{0}}(k)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }k\in A,\\
0&\text{if }k\not\in A.\end{cases}

Lemma 4.1
.

For a finite
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,

(4.1)

\|u_{A}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{T})}^{4}=\#\Box(A).

Moreover, for every
k\in A
,

(4.2)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}}\rho^{\ast}_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(|u_{A}|^{2})(x,k)=\#\triangle^{k}(A).

Proof.

Expanding the fourth power and integrating,

\displaystyle\|u_{A}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{T})}^{4}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\in A}

\displaystyle e^{2\pi i(x\cdot k_{1}+t|k_{1}|^{2})}e^{-2\pi i(x\cdot k_{2}+t|k_{2}|^{2})}

\displaystyle e^{2\pi i(x\cdot k_{3}+t|k_{3}|^{2})}e^{-2\pi i(x\cdot k_{4}+t|k_{4}|^{2})}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t,

gives precisely the number of quadruples
(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})
in
A^{4}
satisfying

k_{1}+k_{3}=k_{2}+k_{4},\quad|k_{1}|^{2}+|k_{3}|^{2}=|k_{2}|^{2}+|k_{4}|^{2}.

Writing
k_{3}=k_{2}+k_{4}-k_{1}
, the second condition becomes

(k_{2}-k_{1})\cdot(k_{4}-k_{1})=0.

This identifies these quadruples with the ordered rectangles in
\Box(A)
, proving (
4.1
).

For the second part, observe

\displaystyle\rho^{\ast}_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(|u_{A}|^{2})(x,k)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}\in A}e^{2\pi i((x-2tk)\cdot k_{1}+t|k_{1}|^{2})}e^{-2\pi i((x-2tk)\cdot k_{2}+t|k_{2}|^{2})}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2}\in A\\
|k_{1}|^{2}-|k_{2}|^{2}-2k\cdot(k_{1}-k_{2})=0\end{subarray}}e^{2\pi ix\cdot(k_{1}-k_{2})}.

The last sum is real-valued; it is bounded by the total number of surviving pairs
(k_{1},k_{2})
and equality is attained at
x=0
. The summing condition can be rewritten as

(k_{1}-k_{2})\cdot(k_{1}+k_{2}-2k)=0.

This proves (
4.2
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

We take
u=u_{A}
and
u_{0}
as before, and choose the weight

(4.3)

w=|u_{A}|^{2}.

The periodic Stein-type and Mizohata–Takeuchi estimates (
1.18
) and (
1.19
) applied to
u=u_{A}
then read

(4.4)

\|u_{A}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{T})}^{4}\leqslant C\sum_{k\in A}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}}\rho^{\ast}_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(|u_{A}|^{2})(x,k)

and

(4.5)

\|u_{A}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{T})}^{4}\leqslant C\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}\\
k\in A\end{subarray}}\rho^{\ast}_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(|u_{A}|^{2})(x,k)\,\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{2}.

Applying Lemma
4.1
, observing
\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{2}=\#A
and varying

4.5

\displaystyle\#\Box(A)\leqslant\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(N)\,\#A\,\max_{k\in A}\#\triangle^{k}(A)

\displaystyle\quad\text{if }A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}\cap[-N,N]^{2},

\displaystyle\#\Box(A)\leqslant\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(n)\,\#A\,\max_{k\in A}\#\triangle^{k}(A)

\displaystyle\quad\text{if }A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2},\ \#A=n.

Dividing by the quantities on the right-hand sides that depend on

1.24
).
Similarly, inequality (

\sum_{k\in A}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}}\rho^{\ast}_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(|u_{A}|^{2})(x,k)=\#\triangle(A),

implies

\displaystyle\#\Box(A)\leqslant\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(N)\,\#\triangle(A)

\displaystyle\quad\text{if }A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}\cap[-N,N]^{2},

\displaystyle\#\Box(A)\leqslant\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(n)\,\#\triangle(A)

\displaystyle\quad\text{if }A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2},\ \#A=n.

These two estimates immediately establish (
1.23
).
∎

This section proves Theorem

The one-dimensional weight can be built from subset sums of the space-time parabola points

In the formulation where the constants are expressed in terms of

After proving this lower bound, we record a trivial but useful converse, as the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the average of the X-ray transform easily give the matching upper bound in (

Construction of the higher-dimensional weight

Fix

We also write
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})
and
\mathbf{1}=(1,\ldots,1)
.
Define

M_{l}:=m_{1}+\cdots+m_{l-1}

for
l=1,\ldots,d
, with the convention
M_{1}=0
. The frequency set in our example will be the discrete Cartesian product

A_{\mathbf{m}}:=\bigl\{(2^{M_{1}+j_{1}},2^{M_{2}+j_{2}},\ldots,2^{M_{d}+j_{d}})\,:\,j_{l}\in\{1,2,\ldots,m_{l}\}\text{ for every }l=1,\ldots,d\bigr\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

Note that

\#A_{\mathbf{m}}=m_{1}\cdots m_{d}\quad\text{and}\quad A_{\mathbf{m}}\subseteq[0,2^{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{d}}]^{d}.

Consider the solution (
1.8
) of the periodic Schrödinger equation given by the formula

(5.1)

u(x,t)=u_{\mathbf{m}}(x,t):=\sum_{k\in A_{\mathbf{m}}}e^{2\pi i(k\cdot x+|k|^{2}t)}=\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{m_{1}}\cdots\sum_{j_{d}=1}^{m_{d}}e^{2\pi i\sum_{l=1}^{d}(2^{M_{l}+j_{l}}x_{l}+4^{M_{l}+j_{l}}t)}.

Then
u_{0}(x)=u_{\mathbf{m}}(x,0)
has frequency support precisely
A_{\mathbf{m}}
and

\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}=\#A_{\mathbf{m}}=m_{1}\cdots m_{d}.

For each
l=1,2,\ldots,d
and every subset
J\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,m_{l}\}
put

X_{l,J}:=\sum_{j\in J}2^{M_{l}+j},\quad Y_{l,J}:=\sum_{j\in J}4^{M_{l}+j};

see Figure

If
\mathbf{J}=(J_{1},\ldots,J_{d})

J_{l}\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,m_{l}\}
, define

X_{\mathbf{J}}:=(X_{1,J_{1}},\ldots,X_{d,J_{d}})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

Let us first define an auxiliary
1

f_{\mathbf{m}}(x,t):=\sum_{\mathbf{J}\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{r}}}e^{2\pi i(X_{\mathbf{J}}\cdot x+t\sum_{l=1}^{d}Y_{l,J_{l}})}=\prod_{l=1}^{d}\Bigl(\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}_{l,r_{l}}}e^{2\pi i(X_{l,J}x_{l}+Y_{l,J}t)}\Bigr),

and then define the desired weight as

(5.2)

w(x,t)=w_{\mathbf{m}}(x,t):=|f_{\mathbf{m}}(x,t)|^{2}.

Lemma 5.1
.

For every
(x,k)\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\times A_{\mathbf{m}}
we have

(5.3)

\rho^{\ast}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}w_{\mathbf{m}}(x,k)=\prod_{l=1}^{d}\binom{m_{l}}{r_{l}}.

Also,

(5.4)

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|u_{\mathbf{m}}(x,t)|^{2}w_{\mathbf{m}}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\geqslant\prod_{l=1}^{d}(r_{l}+1)^{2}\binom{m_{l}}{r_{l}+1}.

Proof.

We first prove (
5.3
). Take

k=\bigl(2^{M_{1}+q_{1}},\ldots,2^{M_{d}+q_{d}}\bigr)\in A_{\mathbf{m}}

determined by some
q_{l}\in\{1,2,\ldots,m_{l}\}
;
l=1,\ldots,d
. Expanding
|f_{\mathbf{m}}(x,t)|^{2}
, we obtain the sum

w_{\mathbf{m}}(x,t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{J}=(J_{1},\ldots,J_{d})\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{r}}\\
\mathbf{J}^{\prime}=(J^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,J^{\prime}_{d})\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{r}}\end{subarray}}e^{2\pi i\left((X_{\mathbf{J}}-X_{\mathbf{J}^{\prime}})\cdot x+t\sum_{l=1}^{d}(Y_{l,J_{l}}-Y_{l,J^{\prime}_{l}})\right)},

so that

(5.5)

\rho^{\ast}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}w_{\mathbf{m}}(x,k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{J}=(J_{1},\ldots,J_{d})\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{r}}\\
\mathbf{J}^{\prime}=(J^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,J^{\prime}_{d})\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{r}}\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{1}e^{2\pi i\left((X_{\mathbf{J}}-X_{\mathbf{J}^{\prime}})\cdot(x-2tk)+t\sum_{l=1}^{d}(Y_{l,J_{l}}-Y_{l,J^{\prime}_{l}})\right)}\,\mathrm{d}t.

After we perform the integration in
t
, we conclude that the summand depending on
\mathbf{J}
and
\mathbf{J}^{\prime}
is zero unless

(5.6)

\sum_{l=1}^{d}\bigl(Y_{l,J_{l}}-Y_{l,J^{\prime}_{l}}-2^{M_{l}+q_{l}+1}(X_{l,J_{l}}-X_{l,J^{\prime}_{l}})\bigr)=0.

We claim that this cannot happen when
\mathbf{J}\neq\mathbf{J}^{\prime}
. After this is proved, we will know that only diagonal pairs in (
5.5
) survive, i.e.,

\rho^{\ast}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}w_{\mathbf{m}}(x,k)=\#\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{r}}
,
which will establish (
5.3
).

Fix
\mathbf{J},\mathbf{J}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{r}}
and define

c_{l,j}:=\mathbbm{1}_{J_{l}}(j)-\mathbbm{1}_{J^{\prime}_{l}}(j)\in\{-1,0,1\},\quad l=1,2,\ldots,d,\quad j=1,2,\ldots,m_{l}.

Rewriting (

(5.7)

\sum_{l=1}^{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j\leqslant m_{l}\\
j\neq q_{l}+1\end{subarray}}c_{l,j}\,2^{2M_{l}}\bigl(2^{2j}-2^{j+q_{l}+1}\bigr)=0.

Since
\#J_{l}=\#J^{\prime}_{l}=r_{l}
, we have

(5.8)

\sum_{j=1}^{m_{l}}c_{l,j}=0\quad\text{for every }l=1,2,\ldots,d.

We claim that
c_{l,j}=0
for every
l
and
j
. The integer exponent of the largest power of
2
dividing the number
2^{2M_{l}}(2^{2j}-2^{j+q_{l}+1})
, i.e., its
2

\begin{cases}2M_{l}+2j&\text{if }j\leqslant q_{l},\\
2M_{l}+j+q_{l}+1&\text{if }j\geqslant q_{l}+2.\end{cases}

Observe that all these numbers are mutually different, over all choices of indices
l
and
j
appearing in (
5.7
). Consequently, if the sum (
5.7
) had at least one nonzero term, then we could divide it by the largest power of
2
possible. By what we just proved, this would give a zero sum with precisely one odd term, which would be a contradiction.
Hence
c_{l,j}=0
whenever
j\neq q_{l}+1
. For each fixed
l

5.8
) forces this coefficient to vanish as well.

\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{J}^{\prime}
and, as we have already said, this completes the proof of (
5.3
).

It remains to prove (
5.4

u_{\mathbf{m}}f_{\mathbf{m}}
gives

u_{\mathbf{m}}(x,t)f_{\mathbf{m}}(x,t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j_{1}\leqslant m_{1}\\
\cdots\\
1\leqslant j_{d}\leqslant m_{d}\\
(I_{1},\ldots,I_{d})\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{r}}\end{subarray}}e^{2\pi i\sum_{l=1}^{d}\left((2^{M_{l}+j_{l}}+X_{l,I_{l}})x_{l}+(4^{M_{l}+j_{l}}+Y_{l,I_{l}})t\right)}.

For given
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{d})
and
(I_{1},\ldots,I_{d})
such that
j_{l}\not\in I_{l}
for every
l
, the space-time frequency of the above summand is precisely

\biggl(X_{\mathbf{J}},\sum_{l=1}^{d}Y_{l,J_{l}}\biggr),\quad J_{l}:=I_{l}\cup\{j_{l}\}\in\mathcal{J}_{l,r_{l}+1}.

Conversely, fix
\mathbf{J}\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{1}}
. For each
l
, choose
j_{l}\in J_{l}
and set
I_{l}=J_{l}\setminus\{j_{l}\}
. This gives exactly
\prod_{l=1}^{d}(r_{l}+1)
representations of the same space-time frequency. Distinct choices of
\mathbf{J}
give distinct spatial frequencies
X_{\mathbf{J}}
, since each coordinate
X_{l,J_{l}}=\sum_{j\in J_{l}}2^{M_{l}+j}
determines
J_{l}
by uniqueness of binary expansion. Therefore, the Fourier coefficient of
u_{\mathbf{m}}f_{\mathbf{m}}
at each of these
\prod_{l=1}^{d}\binom{m_{l}}{r_{l}+1}

\prod_{l=1}^{d}(r_{l}+1)
, so orthogonality on
\mathbb{T}^{d+1}
gives

\|u_{\mathbf{m}}f_{\mathbf{m}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d+1})}^{2}\geqslant\prod_{l=1}^{d}(r_{l}+1)^{2}\binom{m_{l}}{r_{l}+1}.

Since
w_{\mathbf{m}}=|f_{\mathbf{m}}|^{2}
, this is precisely (
5.4
).

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Choose the solution and the weight respectively as (
5.1
) and (
5.2
).
By Lemma
5.1
, both the Stein-type and the Mizohata–Takeuchi right-hand sides are equal to

\#A_{\mathbf{m}}\prod_{l=1}^{d}\binom{m_{l}}{r_{l}}=\prod_{l=1}^{d}m_{l}\binom{m_{l}}{r_{l}}.

Also, the left-hand sides can be bounded from below using (
5.4
). Hence, both constants
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(2^{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{d}})
and
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(2^{m_{1}+\cdots+m_{d}})
are bounded from below by

(5.9)

\prod_{l=1}^{d}\frac{(r_{l}+1)^{2}\binom{m_{l}}{r_{l}+1}}{m_{l}\binom{m_{l}}{r_{l}}}=\prod_{l=1}^{d}\frac{(r_{l}+1)(m_{l}-r_{l})}{m_{l}}.

For the statement on the constants localised to
[-N,N]^{d}
, take the “isotropic” choice
m_{1}=\cdots=m_{d}=m\geqslant 2
and recall that
r_{l}=\lfloor m/2\rfloor
. Then
A_{\mathbf{m}}\subseteq[0,2^{dm}]^{d}
and (
5.9
) gives

\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(2^{dm}),\ \mathfrak{S}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(2^{dm})\geqslant\biggl(\frac{(r+1)(m-r)}{m}\biggr)^{d}\gtrsim\Bigl(\frac{m}{4}\Bigr)^{d}\gtrsim_{d}m^{d}.

Choosing
m=\lfloor(\log_{2}N)/d\rfloor
for sufficiently large
N
proves (
1.26
).

For the lower bound in (
1.27
), take the “anisotropic” choice
\mathbf{m}=(n,1,\ldots,1)
, so that
\#A_{\mathbf{m}}=n
. In (
5.9
), all factors except the first are equal to
1
, while the first factor is
\frac{(r+1)(n-r)}{n}
, where
r=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
.
Thus, for every
n\geqslant 2
,

\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(n),\ \widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(n)\geqslant\frac{(r+1)(n-r)}{n}\gtrsim n.

This proves the lower bounds in (
1.27
).

Now we turn to the upper bounds. Because of (
1.20
), it only remains to prove the Stein-type upper bound.
Let

u(x,t)=\sum_{k\in A}a_{k}e^{2\pi i(x\cdot k+t|k|^{2})}

be the solution (
1.8
) for a given trigonometric polynomial

u_{0}(x)=\sum_{k\in A}a_{k}e^{2\pi ix\cdot k},\quad A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d},\ \#A=n,\ a_{k}\in\mathbb{C}.

We estimate pointwise on
\mathbb{T}^{d+1}
using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

|u(x,t)|^{2}\leqslant\Bigl(\sum_{k\in A}|a_{k}|\Bigr)^{2}\leqslant n\sum_{k\in A}|a_{k}|^{2}=n\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}.

Thus,

(5.10)

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|u(x,t)|^{2}w(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant n\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d+1}}w

for every nonnegative weight
w
. On the other hand, for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, translation-invariance gives

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho^{\ast}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}w(x,k)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}w(x-2tk,t)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d+1}}w.

Consequently,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho^{\ast}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}w(x,k)\geqslant\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d+1}}w

for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Summing this inequality with weights
|a_{k}|^{2}
over
k\in A
gives

(5.11)

\sum_{k\in A}|a_{k}|^{2}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho^{\ast}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}w(x,k)\geqslant\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d+1}}w.

Combining (
5.10
) and (
5.11
) we obtain

\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(n)\leqslant\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(n)\leqslant n.\qed

5.2.
A comment on flat rectangles and triangles

The one-dimensional construction considered above uses a weight that could not have come from the mere counting of geometric configurations.
Indeed, if
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
is finite, then the one-dimensional resonance equations

k_{1}+k_{3}=k_{2}+k_{4},\quad k_{1}^{2}+k_{3}^{2}=k_{2}^{2}+k_{4}^{2}

force
\{k_{1},k_{3}\}=\{k_{2},k_{4}\}
, and hence

\|u_{A}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{4}=2(\#A)^{2}-\#A.

For
k\in A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, define
\triangle^{k}(A)
on
\mathbb{Z}
in the same way as we did on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
Degenerate isosceles triangles give
\#\triangle^{k}(A)\geqslant\#A
for every
k\in A
. Thus the analogous “rectangle–triangle” ratios coming from weights of the form (
4.3
) remain bounded by
2
:

\frac{\|u_{A}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{4}}{\sum_{k\in A}\#\triangle^{k}(A)}\leqslant 2,\quad\frac{\|u_{A}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{4}}{\#A\,\max_{k\in A}\limits\#\triangle^{k}(A)}\leqslant 2

and thus cannot be used to construct counterexamples to (
1.18
) and (
1.19
).
