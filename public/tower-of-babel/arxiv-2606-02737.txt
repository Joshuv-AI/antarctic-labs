Attention Calibration for Position-Fair Dense Information Retrieval
Andrianos Michail Elias Schuhmacher Juri Opitz Simon Clematide Rico Sennrich Department of Computational LinguisticsUniversity of Zurich andrianos.michail@cl.uzh.ch

Abstract
Dense retrieval models exhibit positional bias: retrieval effectiveness degrades when relevant information appears later in a passage ( 6 ) . We ask whether this bias can be reduced at inference time, without retraining and without sacrificing overall retrieval effectiveness. To this end, we adapt inference-time attention calibration ( 5 ) to downstream retrieval and extend it with a strength coefficient λ \lambda that interpolates between the original and fully calibrated attention distributions. Across three embedding models on SQuAD-PosQ and FineWeb-PosQ, we examine how basket size, calibrated layer set, and strength affect the trade-off between positional fairness and retrieval effectiveness, finding that partial calibration frequently outperforms full calibration. A single configuration ( 𝔅 = 128 \mathfrak{B}=128 , λ = 0.5 \lambda=0.5 , 50% layer depth) improves the harmonic mean of nDCG@10 across positional groups on FineWeb-PosQ for all three models without per-model tuning, and applies to both <s> -pooled and last-token-pooled architectures. This default configuration transfers without modification to PosIR, which spans 10 languages and 31 domains, reducing the Position Sensitivity Index in all 16 length-quartile × \times model × \times retrieval-setting combinations, while preserving or improving aggregate nDCG@10. We release our extended codebase at github.com/impresso/fair-sentence-transformers

Introduction

Dense retrieval models are central to modern information retrieval systems, mapping queries and documents into a shared embedding space to enable efficient semantic matching.
However, recent work by
6
reports that such models exhibit
positional bias
: retrieval effectiveness degrades when relevant information appears later in a passage.
Using two position-aware benchmarks, they show that dense embedding models are sensitive to answer position.

5
investigate positional imbalance at the representation level and propose an inference-time attention calibration method that redistributes attention mass more evenly across document segments.
While this approach reduces positional skew in representation profiles, its effect on downstream retrieval has not been examined, and their method offers no control over the strength of the redistribution.

In this work, we extend attention calibration with a tunable strength coefficient
\lambda
and study when it mitigates positional bias in dense retrieval without sacrificing retrieval effectiveness. Figure
1
illustrates the calibration procedure and our strength coefficient.
We characterize the effects of the calibration parameters—basket size
\mathfrak{B}
, calibrated layer set
\mathfrak{L}^{C}
, and strength
\lambda
—on the trade-off between positional consistency and retrieval quality.
We use this analysis to identify a model-agnostic default configuration that applies across embedding models with different pooling mechanisms (
<s>
-pooling and last-token pooling).
We then validate this configuration at scale on the Position Aware IR Benchmark (PosIR), covering 10 languages, 31 domains, and two embedding models in both multilingual and cross-lingual retrieval settings.

We make the following contributions:

•

Calibration strength coefficient:

We extend the attention calibration method of
5
with a strength coefficient
\lambda\in[0,1]
that linearly interpolates between the original and calibrated attention distributions, enabling controlled exploration of the trade-off between positional redistribution and retrieval effectiveness. We also test whether attention calibration is specific to
<s>
-pooled models, where early-token over-attention may be expected from the pooling-token position, and show that the same intervention applies to last-token-pooled models.

•

Systematic study of calibration parameters:

We analyze how basket size
\mathfrak{B}
, calibrated layer set
\mathfrak{L}^{C}
, and strength
\lambda
jointly control the balance between positional consistency and retrieval effectiveness, identifying cases where calibration improves or degrades downstream performance.

•

Model-agnostic default configuration:

We identify a modest partial-calibration setting (
\lambda=0.5
, 50% of layers,
\mathfrak{B}=128
) that improves the harmonic mean of nDCG@10 across positional groups and reduces positional sensitivity on FineWeb-PosQ across all three evaluated embedding models without per-model tuning.

•

Large-scale generalization evidence:

We validate the identified configuration on PosIR across 10 languages, 31 domains, and two embedding models, showing consistent reductions in positional sensitivity with preserved or improved retrieval effectiveness in both multilingual and cross-lingual (x
\rightarrow
en) settings.

2
Related Work

Position Bias in Dense Retrieval.

6
introduced two position-aware evaluation sets to study how the location of relevant information within a passage affects dense retrieval effectiveness.

SQuAD-PosQ
repurposes the SQuAD v2 test set by removing unanswerable questions, yielding 92,749 examples. Retrieval is performed over the full corpus and questions are grouped by the character position where the answer begins in the reference passage: [0–100], [100–200], [200–300], [300–400], [400–500], and [500–3,120]. nDCG@10 decreases as the answer position moves later in the passage.

FineWeb-PosQ
addresses the short average passage length of SQuAD (
\approx
117 words) and potential training data leakage. The authors sample 13,902 passages of 500–1,024 words from FineWeb-edu, divide each into beginning, middle, and end segments, and generate section-specific questions with GPT-4o-mini, resulting in 25,775 questions. Retrieval performance again degrades for the middle and end segments.

Position Aware IR Benchmark

(
7
)
extends this line of work to multilingual and cross-lingual retrieval. It comprises 310 datasets spanning 10 languages and 31 domains, with relevance tied to precise reference spans. Its central methodological feature is a length-controlled bucketing strategy that groups queries by the length of the positive document (Q1: 512, Q2: 1024, Q3: 1536, Q4: 2048 tokens) and analyzes positional effects within each bucket, isolating position bias from length-induced degradation. Results across ten state-of-the-art embedding models show that position bias is pervasive and intensifies with document length.

Attention Calibration

5
analyze how information from different document positions contributes to the final embedding representation.
Using a permutation-based evaluation framework, they show that multilingual encoder-based embedding models exhibit systematic positional imbalance: early segments are over-represented in the document embedding, while later segments are progressively less reflected.
They attribute this effect to front-loaded attention distributions of the pooling token, whose query assigns disproportionate attention mass to early key tokens across transformer layers.
However, their analysis focuses on representation profiles rather than downstream retrieval, and it remains open whether the same attention-based mechanism applies across pooling architectures.

To mitigate this imbalance, they propose an inference-time attention calibration that redistributes the pooling token’s attention more evenly across document positions. The method partitions the key sequence into contiguous baskets of size
\mathfrak{B}
, isolating the document representation token (e.g.,
<s>
or
</s>
) in its own basket to avoid redistributing special-token attention across content tokens, and enforces equal total attention mass per basket while preserving relative attention weights within each basket. For a token at position
k
assigned to basket
b(k)
, the calibrated attention weight is:

a^{\mathrm{cal}}_{k}=\frac{a_{k}}{\sum_{j\in b(k)}a_{j}}\cdot\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{baskets}}},

where
a_{k}
is the original post-softmax attention weight and
n_{\mathrm{baskets}}
is the number of baskets. Calibration is applied only to the pooling token’s query row in selected transformer layers and requires no retraining. While this method was shown to flatten representation profiles in a controlled permutation setting, its effect on downstream retrieval has not been examined.

3
Method and Experimental Setup

We study attention calibration as an inference-time mitigation for position bias in dense retrieval. The remainder of this section introduces the strength coefficient
\lambda
that controls how aggressively calibration redistributes attention, the configurations we examine, the embedding models used in our experiments, the evaluation metrics, and a position-agnostic baseline. We use SQuAD-PosQ and FineWeb-PosQ to characterize the effects of calibration parameters and to identify a default configuration; we then use PosIR as a large-scale generalization test of that configuration.

3.1
Calibration Strength Coefficient

We extend the attention calibration method of
5
with a strength coefficient
\lambda\in[0,1]
that linearly interpolates between the original and calibrated attention distributions of the pooling token. The resulting attention weight for key position
k
is:

\hat{a}_{k}=\lambda\cdot a^{\mathrm{cal}}_{k}+(1-\lambda)\cdot a_{k},

where
\lambda=0
recovers the original model and
\lambda=1
applies full calibration as in the original formulation. The coefficient enables controlled exploration of the trade-off between positional redistribution and retrieval effectiveness, rather than committing to a single fixed level of redistribution. Implementation details are provided in Appendix
A
. We calibrate passage/document encodings only; query encodings are left unchanged.

3.2
Examined Calibration Configurations

We evaluate attention calibration across a small set of configurations to characterize the directional effects of each parameter, rather than to optimize for a specific benchmark. Our choices follow the configurations examined by
5
in the context of representational fairness, where last-layer calibration was shown to already reduce positional bias and calibrating the last 50% of layers produced a fairly flat representation profile across positions.

3.3
Models

We evaluate three embedding-based retrieval models covering both common pooling architectures: gte-multilingual-base
(
8
)
and bge-m3-dense
(
1
)
use
<s>
-pooling, and Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B
(
9
)
uses last-token pooling.
This distinction is important because the pooling token determines which attention query is calibrated: the first-token query for
<s>
-pooled models and the final-token query for last-token-pooled models.

Since positional attention patterns can depend on architectural choice, we treat cross-pooling transfer as an empirical question rather than assuming that calibration behaves identically across models.

3.4
Evaluation Metrics

We report retrieval performance using nDCG@10 (Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain at rank 10)
(
3
)
with binary relevance labels, following
6
.

To summarize performance across positional groups, we report the harmonic mean (
Harm
):

\text{Harm}=\frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\text{nDCG@10}_{i}}},

where
n
is the number of positional groups and
\text{nDCG@10}_{i}
is the score for group
i
.
The harmonic mean penalizes low-performing groups more strongly than the arithmetic mean and therefore reflects both overall retrieval effectiveness and sensitivity to positional disparities.
We therefore use
Harm
as the primary summary metric for the PosQ experiments.

We additionally report the Position Sensitivity Index (PSI) introduced by
6
, which captures the relative gap between the best- and worst-performing positional groups.
PSI is useful for diagnosing positional spread, but reductions in PSI are only desirable when retrieval effectiveness is preserved. We therefore interpret PSI together with
Harm
or aggregate nDCG@10 rather than as a standalone success criterion.

3.5
Segment Embed Average Baseline

As an additional position-agnostic baseline, we evaluate
Segment Embed Average (SEA)
, which constructs passage embeddings by segmenting each passage with semantic chunking
(
4
)
, embedding each chunk separately, and averaging the resulting representations.
This baseline tests whether a simple segmentation-and-aggregation strategy can reduce positional dependence without modifying model internals.
It is not expected to preserve all information available to the original full-passage embedding, so we use it mainly to contextualize the trade-off between positional spread and retrieval effectiveness.

SQuAD-PosQ

On SQuAD-PosQ, attention calibration produces limited benefit because most passages are too short for basket-level redistribution to meaningfully separate early and late content.
As illustrated in Figure
2
(a), only the last positional bin (500+ characters, corresponding to
\approx
125+ subtokens) extends beyond the first basket at
\mathfrak{B}=128
, while larger basket sizes place most or all of the passage within a single content basket.
Accordingly, improvements are only concentrated in the 500+ character bin for all three models—e.g., bge-m3-dense improves from 74.61 to 77.85 with 50% layer calibration at
\mathfrak{B}=128
,
\lambda=0.5
—but these are offset by decreases in earlier bins, leaving harmonic means close to the off-the-shelf model.

FineWeb-PosQ

On FineWeb-PosQ, where passages are roughly ten times longer, calibration produces consistent harmonic-mean improvements across all three models (Table
1
, right).
We observe a clear trade-off: calibration usually reduces nDCG@10 for the beginning group while improving the middle and end groups, reflecting the intended redistribution of attention away from early positions.
This trade-off results in net harmonic mean gains across multiple configurations.
For gte-multilingual-base, the best configuration improves the harmonic mean from 78.66 to 79.81; for bge-m3-dense, from 79.10 to 80.34; and for Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B, from 76.49 to 79.94.
For Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B, the same setting reduces the gap between the begin and end groups: begin performance decreases from 88.54 to 83.46, while end performance increases from 65.61 to 75.63.

Effects of basket size

On SQuAD-PosQ, larger basket sizes (
\mathfrak{B}\geq 256
) place most or all content tokens within a single basket, as shown in Figure
2
(a), and therefore produce results nearly identical to the uncalibrated baseline.
On FineWeb-PosQ, where passages are long enough for all basket sizes to create multiple content baskets, a consistent pattern appears: smaller baskets yield higher harmonic mean performance. For example, bge-m3-dense with 50% layer calibration at
\lambda=0.5
achieves a harmonic mean of 80.34 at
\mathfrak{B}=128
, 80.15 at
\mathfrak{B}=256
, and 79.40 at
\mathfrak{B}=512
.
As shown in Figure
2
(b),
\mathfrak{B}=128
produces six baskets over a typical FineWeb-PosQ passage, allowing finer-grained redistribution of attention than
\mathfrak{B}=512
.

Interaction between calibration strength and layer depth

Calibration strength
\lambda
and layer depth
\mathfrak{L}^{C}
jointly control how strongly the pooling-token attention distribution is modified. At
\lambda=0.5
, early-position performance is partly preserved while later positions improve moderately.
At
\lambda=1.0
, later positions often improve further, but early positions degrade more strongly.
Calibrating more layers amplifies this effect: last-layer calibration at
\lambda=1.0
moves in the same direction as 50% layer calibration at
\lambda=0.5
, but to a lesser extent.

The strongest intervention, 50% layer calibration at
\lambda=1.0
, illustrates the cost of over-calibration. This configuration produces the flattest representation profile in
5
, but it degrades retrieval for the two
<s>
-pooled models on FineWeb-PosQ: the harmonic mean drops by 1.03 points for gte-multilingual-base (78.66
\rightarrow
77.63) and 1.89 points for bge-m3-dense (79.10
\rightarrow

4.1
Ablation: Calibration Strength

To understand how calibration strength affects retrieval behavior—and where redistribution begins to hurt rather than help—we conduct a fine-grained ablation over
\lambda\in[0.05,1.0]
in increments of 0.05, with basket size fixed at
\mathfrak{B}=128
. Figure
3
shows the change in nDCG@10 on FineWeb-PosQ relative to the uncalibrated baseline for each positional group, comparing 50% layer calibration with last-layer calibration.

As shown in Figure
3
, increasing
\lambda
generally improves nDCG@10 for the end group and degrades the begin group across all three models, with the middle group following a moderate upward trend.
The harmonic mean initially increases as gains in the middle and end groups outweigh the begin-group losses, then declines at higher
\lambda
values as the cost to the begin group becomes too large. This pattern shows that attention calibration improves retrieval for later positions, but that stronger redistribution is not always better for overall performance.
The depth of calibration controls how quickly this trade-off appears: with 50% layer calibration, the curves are steeper and the harmonic mean peaks at lower
\lambda
values, while last-layer calibration produces smaller changes across the full range of
\lambda
.
This difference is strongest for Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B, where 50% layer calibration at
\lambda=1.0
yields end-group gains exceeding +10 nDCG@10 points but begin-group losses beyond
-
5 points, while last-layer calibration keeps all changes within a narrower range.
Across configurations, the harmonic mean peaks in the
\lambda\approx 0.3
–
0.5
region, consistent with the default identified previously.
This indicates that partial calibration—which preserves part of the original attention distribution—provides a better retrieval trade-off than full calibration, even though full calibration produces more positionally uniform representations.

4.2
Robust Default Configuration

Across the parameter analysis and the fine-grained
\lambda
sweep, a single configuration provides a robust trade-off without per-model tuning:
\mathfrak{B}=128
,
\lambda=0.5
, with 50% layer calibration.
At this setting, all three models reach their best or near-best harmonic mean on FineWeb-PosQ, the harmonic mean curves in Figure
3
sit near their peaks, and the configuration applies uniformly across both
<s>
-pooled and last-token-pooled models.
We use this default in the large-scale validation that follows.

5
Position Aware IR Benchmark

To test whether the default configuration identified above generalizes beyond the parameter exploration, we evaluate it on PosIR
(
7
)
, which spans 10 languages, 31 domains, and four document-length quartiles (Q1: 512 tokens to Q4: 2048 tokens). PosIR’s length-controlled bucketing isolates position bias from length-induced degradation, making it well-suited to measuring whether calibration reduces positional sensitivity across regimes that differ substantially from those used to identify the default.
Due to the computational cost of PosIR, we evaluate two of the three models from our parameter exploration: gte-multilingual-base and bge-m3-dense. These calibrated PosIR runs required over 2,750 GPU-hours in total; detailed compute estimates are reported in Appendix
B
.
We evaluate both models in the multilingual (x
\rightarrow
x) and cross-lingual (x
\rightarrow
en) settings, applying calibration with
\mathfrak{B}=128
,
\lambda=0.5
, 50% layer depth at indexing time. Following PosIR’s protocol, results are weighted-averaged across the 31 domains within each language and then macro-averaged across the 10 languages. Per-language results are reported in Appendix (Tables
4
,
5
).

The PosIR results are not directly identical in form to the PosQ results above. In SQuAD-PosQ and FineWeb-PosQ, positional groups are defined by the location of the answer or reference span within a passage, and we report nDCG@10 separately for these groups. In PosIR, following the benchmark protocol, aggregate nDCG@10 is reported across all queries, while PSI is computed within document-length quartiles from position-conditioned performance. We therefore use PosIR primarily as a generalization test for positional sensitivity reduction, with aggregate nDCG@10 serving as an effectiveness check.

PSI is reduced consistently

Table
2
shows that calibration reduces PSI in all 16 length-quartile
\times
model
\times
retrieval-setting combinations, without exception.
The reductions are generally larger for longer documents: in the multilingual setting, PSI for gte-multilingual-base drops by 0.05 at Q1 (512 tokens) and by 0.10 at Q4 (2048 tokens); for bge-m3-dense, reductions are roughly stable at 0.10–0.12 across all quartiles.
The cross-lingual setting follows the same pattern, with reductions reaching 0.14 for bge-m3-dense at Q4.
Overall, the largest absolute reductions occur in the longer quartiles, where positional sensitivity is also more pronounced in the uncalibrated baselines.

Aggregate retrieval effectiveness is largely preserved

Aggregate nDCG@10 is preserved or slightly improved across both retrieval settings. In the multilingual setting, both models gain (gte-multilingual-base: +2.41%, bge-m3-dense: +0.82%). In the cross-lingual setting, bge-m3-dense gains +0.27% while gte-multilingual-base changes by
-
0.06%, which is essentially unchanged. The per-language breakdown (Appendix Tables
4
,
5
) shows that calibration tends to improve nDCG@10 most for the shortest quartile (Q1) and to slightly reduce it for longer quartiles, while PSI improves throughout. The Segment Embed Average method shows this is not a given: the baseline reduces PSI only by sacrificing effectiveness, lowering aggregate nDCG@10 by 4.50–10.44 points (e.g.,
-
10.44 for bge-m3-dense in the Multilingual setting). Calibration achieves comparable PSI reductions while keeping nDCG@10 flat or positive.

Takeaway

The default configuration, identified on two English-only datasets, transfers without modification to PosIR, consistently reducing PSI across 10 languages in both multilingual and cross-lingual retrieval. Unlike SEA, which pays a substantial effectiveness cost for comparable PSI reductions, attention calibration maintains or improves aggregate nDCG@10—positional fairness and retrieval effectiveness can jointly improve.

6
Conclusions

We studied inference-time attention calibration as a mitigation for positional bias in dense retrieval.
We extended the method of
5
with a strength coefficient
\lambda\in[0,1]
that interpolates between the original and fully calibrated attention distribution. This extension enables a systematic study of how basket size
\mathfrak{B}
, calibrated layer set
\mathfrak{L}^{C}
, and strength
\lambda
trade off positional fairness against retrieval effectiveness.

We find that partial calibration provides a better retrieval trade-off than full calibration: across three embedding models, the harmonic mean of nDCG@10 on FineWeb-PosQ peaks at moderate strength (
\lambda\approx 0.3
–
0.5
), while stronger settings improve late-position performance at the cost of begin-position performance. A single configuration—
\mathfrak{B}=128
,
\lambda=0.5
, 50% layer depth—achieves the best or near-best harmonic mean for all three models on FineWeb-PosQ without per-model tuning, and applies uniformly across
<s>
-pooled and last-token-pooled architectures.

This default transfers cleanly to PosIR: applied without modification, it reduces PSI in all 16 length-quartile
\times
model
\times
retrieval-setting combinations while preserving or improving aggregate nDCG@10. That this outcome is not a given is shown by the position-agnostic SEA baseline, which reaches comparable PSI reductions only at a substantial effectiveness cost. The configuration therefore offers a simple inference-time intervention for dense retrieval systems, and its transfer from a small English-only parameter exploration to PosIR’s multilingual, cross-lingual, and broad-domain coverage suggests that the calibration-strength trade-off characterized here is not specific to the datasets used for parameter analysis.

Limitations

Scope is restricted to dense retrieval

This work focuses exclusively on dense embedding-based retrieval. Lexical retrieval methods, cross-encoder rerankers, and hybrid approaches are out of scope.

Limited search space for calibration parameters

The parameter grid covers three basket sizes, two layer-set choices, and two strength values, refined for
\lambda
through a fine-grained sweep on FineWeb-PosQ. The identified default is the strongest-performing configuration in this grid and an intuitive choice given the ablation, but it is not the result of a global joint optimization over
\mathfrak{B}
,
\mathfrak{L}^{C}
, and
\lambda
, which would be prohibitively expensive.

Exact mechanism of partial calibration

We show how calibration affects retrieval performance but do not provide a mechanistic explanation for why partial calibration gives a better retrieval trade-off than full calibration. In particular, we do not analyze how basket-level redistribution interacts with learned attention patterns, pooling strategy, or positional encoding. This remains an open question.

More calibration variants remain unexplored

The attention calibration method studied here redistributes the pooling token’s attention through basket-level equalization. Other inference-time variants—for example, preserving a fixed share of the pooling token’s original self-attention or varying the target mass assigned to special-token baskets—may yield different trade-offs and are a promising direction for future work.

Appendix A
Implementation Details

The attention calibration is implemented via
nnsight

(
2
)
, an interpretability library that provides hooks into PyTorch modules during the forward pass. By using the
attention-implementation = "eager"
, we intercept the post-softmax attention weight tensors
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times H\times Q\times K}
at specified layers and apply basket-level equalization in-place before the forward pass continues. This operates entirely at inference time with no gradient computation.

The calibration modifies only the query row corresponding to the pooling token, leaving all other query rows unchanged. For
<s>
-pooled models (also called
<CLS>
-pooled models), this is the first token’s query row; for last-token-pooled models, this is the final token’s query row
</s>
(also called <EOS>). The
<s>
and
</s>
tokens can optionally be isolated into their own baskets to prevent special-token attention from being redistributed across content baskets. Table
3
summarizes the default calibration configuration for each validated model, alongside the generic defaults for each pooling architecture.
