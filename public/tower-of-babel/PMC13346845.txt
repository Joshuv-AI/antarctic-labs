Effects of surface treatment and screw access hole filling method on the retention of CAD-CAM milled PMMA restoration
Authors: Ha-Eun Choi, Yoon-Hyuk Huh, Chan-Jin Park, Lee-Ra Cho, Kyung-Ho Ko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSETo evaluate the effects of titanium abutment surface treatment, screw access hole (SAH) filling method, and restoration internal surface treatment on the retention of CAD-CAM milled PMMA restorations.MATERIALS AND METHODSThe study was conducted in two phases. First, the effects of abutment airborne particle abrasion (APA) and SAH filling method (full, partial) on retention were evaluated (n = 19). Second, the effect of PMMA internal surface treatment (no treatment, MDP-containing primer, APA) was evaluated (n = 19). All specimens were cemented with a non–eugenol temporary cement. Thermomechanical aging was performed prior to pull-out test. Surface characteristics were analyzed using scanning electron microscope (SEM) and atomic force microscope (AFM). Statistical analysis included one-way and two-way ANOVA, Tukey’s HSD test, multiple regression, and chi-square test (α = .05).RESULTSAbutment APA significantly increased retention compared with untreated abutments (mean difference, 33.71 N; 95% CI, 21.34 to 46.08; P < .001), and partial SAH filling produced higher retention than full filling (mean difference, 13.54 N; 95% CI, 1.17 to 25.91; P = .033). PMMA internal surface treatment significantly affected retention, with MDP-containing primer treated PMMA showing the lowest values, whereas APA resulted in the highest (P < .05). SEM and AFM revealed smoothing of the PMMA intaglio surface after MDP-containing primer application and pronounced microroughness after APA.CONCLUSIONWithin the limitations of this in vitro study, abutment APA may improve the retention of CAD-CAM milled PMMA restorations under the tested conditions, and partial SAH filling may provide an additional retention. In contrast, application of an MDP-containing primer to the PMMA internal surface may reduce retention.Keywords: Implant interim restoration, Surface treatment, Screw access hole, Retention

INTRODUCTION

The scope of the interim restorations extends beyond the short-term masticatory and esthetic restoration, serving as a critical diagnostic process that helps determine the patient’s functional and esthetic needs.1 Moreover, interim restorations can be utilized to evaluate the vertical dimension,2 occlusal scheme,3 and any supplementary esthetic treatments necessary prior to the implementation of the final restoration.4 In such cases, the implementation of long-term interim restorations is imperative to ensure precise evaluation.1,5

CAD-CAM has had a significant impact on the qualitative improvement of interim restorations.6 Because CAD-CAM restorations are designed to be machined irrespective of the material, they enable a seamless transition between the interim and definitive restoration morphology.7 In addition, milled PMMA exhibits superior wear resistance, fracture strength, and dimensional stability compared with conventional interim restorations.8,9

Nevertheless, PMMA restorations pose significant challenges in their application as long-term interim restorations, primarily due to their proclivity for dislodgment.10,11,12,13 Because interim restorations must be retrievable for definitive restoration, it is difficult to control the retention force.14 However, the research on the retention of interim restorations is limited,15,16 particularly regarding the long-term clinical performance of various surface conditioning methods.

Several studies have investigated airborne-particle abrasion (APA) of titanium implant abutments, demonstrating that this surface treatment can increase surface energy, improve wettability, and promote micromechanical interlocking, thereby enhancing retention.17,18,19,20,21 However, most of these studies have focused on definitive restoration and resin cements.17,18,19,20 And evidence regarding the behavior of temporary cements as non-adhesive luting materials on APA-treated titanium surfaces remains scarce.21 Some studies have examined how modifications of the screw access hole (SAH) influence the retention of implant-supported restorations.22,23,24,25,26,27,28,29,30 However, these studies that concurrently evaluate the effects of abutment surface treatment and SAH filling method, and compare the magnitude of their individual contributions, are limited.

Previous studies have indicated that the use of functional primers can improve the retention of PMMA-based restorations when bonded with resin cement.28,29,30,31,32 This effect is largely attributed to functional monomers such as 10-methacryloyloxydecyl dihydrogen phosphate (10-MDP), which can form stable chemical bonds with hydroxyapatite and metal oxides in adhesive resin cement systems. However, such bonding mechanisms are substrate- and cement-dependent, and their effectiveness under temporary cementation conditions remains unclear. In particular, when CAD-CAM milled PMMA restorations are cemented with non-eugenol temporary cements that rely primarily on micromechanical retention rather than chemical bonding, the contribution of MDP-containing primers may differ from that observed in adhesive systems. Although several studies have evaluated the effect of APA on the internal surfaces of PMMA when bonded with resin cements, it remains unclear when PMMA restorations are cemented with non-eugenol temporary cements.33,34,35

Therefore, the present in vitro study was designed to evaluate the retention of PMMA implant restoration cemented with a non-eugenol temporary cement, focusing on: the combined effects and relative contributions of different titanium abutment surface treatments and SAH filling methods, and the effects of internal surface treatments of PMMA restorations, including an MDP-containing primer and airborne-particle abrasion. The following null hypotheses were tested: first, titanium abutment surface treatment and SAH filling method do not significantly affect the retention of PMMA implant restorations cemented with a non-eugenol temporary cement. Second, internal surface treatment of PMMA restorations does not significantly affect the retention when cemented with a non-eugenol temporary cement.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

To fabricate PMMA restoration specimens representing a maxillary first molar, a titanium abutment (BGSTA5430TH; Osstem Implant, Seoul, Korea) was connected to an implant analog (GSTLA400; Osstem Implant). An intraoral scanner (Primescan; Dentsply Sirona, Bensheim, Germany) was used to digitize the abutment. The scanned file was saved in standard tessellation language (STL) file format. A CAD-CAM software (Exocad; Exocad GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany) was used to design a PMMA restoration on the abutment. The dimensions of the restoration were an axial wall thickness of 1 mm and an occlusal thickness of 1.5 mm. The cement space was set to 50 µm from the margin up to a height of 2 mm and 100 µm above the margin. The designed restorations were milled from PMMA blocks (Real Fit PMMA Block; HASEM, Daegu, Korea) using a milling machine (Rainbow™ Mill; Dentium Co., Suwon, Korea).

The study was structured to evaluate three clinically relevant factors affecting the retention of CAD-CAM milled PMMA implant restorations: abutment surface treatment, SAH filling method, and restoration internal surface treatment. First, the study used a 2 × 2 factorial design with 4 combinations, including 2 abutment surface treatments (no treatment or APA) and 2 SAH filling methods (full or partial filling) using restorations fabricated without internal surface treatment. A separate set of specimens was prepared to isolate the effect of PMMA restoration internal surface treatment (no treatment, MDP-containing primer application, and APA), while controlling the abutment condition constant (untreated abutment with full SAH filling). Across all experiments, the shared control condition consisted of an untreated abutment with full SAH filling and an untreated PMMA restoration. Study group allocation is listed in Table 1.

In group 1 and 2, the abutment was subjected to APA with 50 µm Al2O3 at 2.5 bar, 10 mm distance, for 10 s limited to 1 mm above the finish line to minimize the risk of margin distortion. In partial SAH filling group, PTFE tape (3M PTFE Thread Seal Tape; 3M, St. Paul, MN, USA) was filled up to 2 mm below the occlusal surface, leaving the upper 2 mm void; and in full filling group, the remaining 2 mm was completely filled with a flowable composite resin (Filtek™ Supreme Flowable Restorative; 3M Oral Care, St. Paul, MN, USA) and photopolymerized for 20 s at 1,200 mW/cm2 according to manufacturer instructions (Fig. 1). In group 5, the MDP-containing primer (G-Multi PRIMER; GC Corp., Tokyo, Japan) was applied to the restoration intaglio surface for 20 s and dried. In group 6, the internal surface of the restoration was airborne-abraded with 50 µm Al2O3 at 2.5 bar, 10 mm distance, for 10 s. After APA, surfaces were ultrasonically cleaned in distilled water for 5 min to remove residual particles. A non-eugenol temporary cement (RelyX™ Temp NE; 3M Oral Care, St. Paul, MN, USA) was used under a 10 N static load for 5 min to ensure complete seating and excess cement was removed. All the specimens were subjected to thermomechanical aging using a mastication simulator (TW-T1000; Taewon Tech, Busan, Korea). Each specimen was fixed in a jig so that the analog was mounted on the lower table and the indenter contacted the inner slope of the buccal cusp of the maxillary first molar. A load of 50 N was repeatedly applied 100,000 times at a frequency of 2 Hz, corresponding to a clinical period of approximately 0.5 years, at 5℃ and 55℃ with a 30 s dwell time. No specimen exhibited loosening or fracture during the aging process.

Schematic diagram of screw access hole filling methods. (A) Partial filling: upper 2 mm left void. (B) Full filling: upper 2 mm filled with resin.

The retention of each specimen was evaluated by a pull-out test, in which the dislodging force (N) required to remove the restoration from the abutment along the path of insertion was measured with a universal testing machine (Model 5982; Instron, Norwood, MA, USA). Specimens were mounted on a custom metal jig designed to align the specimen with the loading axis of the machine (Fig. 2). The maximum load value (N) at the moment of restoration separation from the abutment was recorded at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/s.

Pull–out test setup. (A) Customized metal jig for crown fixation. (B) Specimen loaded on universal testing machine.

After the pull-out test, failure patterns of dislodged specimens were classified based on cement residue location as Pattern 1 (≥ 90% on abutment surface), Pattern 2 (10 – 90% on both the abutment and PMMA restoration), and Pattern 3 (≥ 90% on the PMMA restoration internal surface) (Fig. 3). All specimens were independently evaluated by two dentists in a blinded manner, achieving a Cohen κ of 0.84 (95% CI, 0.76 – 0.92), indicating “almost perfect” reliability during preliminary calibration. In cases of disagreement, the final failure pattern was determined by consensus through joint re-evaluation.

Failure mode classification according to cement remnant location. (A) Pattern 1 (≥ 90% cement on abutment). (B) Pattern 2 (10 – 90% on both the abutment and PMMA restoration). (C) Pattern 3 (≥ 90% cement on PMMA restoration internal surface).

After surface treatment, representative specimens were prepared for scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Quanta FEG 250; FEI Co., Hillsboro, OR, USA) and atomic force microscopy (ezAFM; NanoMagnetic Instruments, Oxford, UK) analyses to characterize the surface morphology. For SEM observation, specimens were mounted on aluminum stubs with carbon adhesive tape and sputter-coated with a 10 nm gold-palladium (Au-Pd) layer (E-1045; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). SEM images were obtained at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV and magnifications of × 200 (S-4800; Hitachi High-Tech Corp., Tokyo, Japan). AFM analysis was conducted using an atomic force microscope in non-contact mode over a 60 × 60 µm scan area to generate 3D topographic maps. Surface roughness parameters (Ra) were obtained using software (XEI software; Park Systems, Suwon, Korea) under controlled environmental conditions (25℃, 40% relative humidity). Three representative areas per group were scanned, and the mean ± standard deviation was calculated for descriptive comparison.

All fabrication and testing procedures—including digital scanning, CAD design, CAM milling, surface treatment, cementation, thermomechanical aging, and retention testing—were performed by a single calibrated operator with clinical and laboratory experience in implant prosthodontics to ensure procedural consistency and minimize operator-related variability. Power analysis (G*Power v3.1.9.6; Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany; effect size f = 0.40, α = 0.05, power = 0.80, two-tailed ANOVA) indicated a minimum sample size of 108, and 19 specimens per group (N = 114) were prepared according to pilot data.

Statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics, v25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Two-way ANOVA and multiple regression analysis were used to evaluate the main and interaction effects of abutment surface treatment and SAH filling method, with Tukey’s HSD test for pairwise comparisons among the four 2×2 combinations. Failure-mode distributions were compared with χ2 test, and the significance level was set at α = .05. For internal surface treatment factors, normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test and homogeneity was assessed using Levene’s test. Welch one-way ANOVA followed by the Games-Howell post hoc comparison was applied when assumptions were violated.

In Groups 4, 5 and 6, which evaluated the effect of the restoration internal surface treatment, the MDP-containing primer group showed the lowest retention (32.6 ± 10.7 N), followed by the untreated control group (43.2 ± 19.7 N), whereas the APA-treated group exhibited the highest retention (52.0 ± 18.9 N). Welch one-way ANOVA followed by Games-Howell post hoc comparisons indicated that the MDP-containing primer group had significantly lower retention than both the control and APA groups.

Failure mode analysis demonstrated distinct patterns between groups (Table 4); however, these results should be interpreted with caution due to low or zero counts in some categories. Pattern 3 occurred in 100% of specimens in Group 3, 4, 5 and 6, all of which lacked abutment surface treatment. In contrast, APA-treated abutments (Group 1, 2) showed predominantly Pattern 1 failures, indicating cohesive cement remnants on the abutment surface. Partial SAH filling (Group 1) exhibited a higher proportion of Pattern 1 than full filling. However, because some categories included low or zero counts, the assumptions of the Pearson chi-square test may not have been fully satisfied, and the results should be interpreted with caution.

Surface morphology analysis supported these mechanical findings (Table 5, Fig. 5). For restoration internal surface treatments, SEM revealed a smooth intaglio surface for the MDP-containing primer group, partially irregular surfaces for the control group, and pronounced micro-irregularities for the APA group. AFM confirmed significantly higher roughness in the APA restorations (Ra = 280.0 ± 24.6 nm) compared with the control (57.3 ± 5.2 nm) and MDP-containing primer groups (43.9 ± 6.8 nm). For abutment surfaces, untreated abutments showed smooth surfaces on SEM, whereas APA-treated abutments exhibited distinct pits and scratch patterns. AFM measurements were in line with these observations, with APA-treated abutments presenting higher roughness (122.3 ± 13.3 nm) than untreated abutments (23.5 ± 3.4 nm).

DISCUSSION

The APA of the titanium abutment and the SAH filling method may influence the retention under the experimental conditions of this study. Therefore, the first null hypothesis was rejected. The increased retention with the APA is consistent with previous studies that reported a positive effect of APA by using shear or tensile testing.36,37,38,39,40 SEM and AFM analyses revealed a roughened surface on APA-treated abutments, in agreement with previous studies demonstrating that air abrasion enhances surface roughness and increases surface energy, thereby promoting cement flow into microretentions.17,18,19,20,21 The predominance of cohesive cement failures (Pattern 1) in the APA-treated abutment groups further supports that surface roughening of the abutment improves interfacial integrity and overall retention.

The SAH filling method also affected retention in this study. Partial filling, in which a 2 mm coronal void is intentionally preserved, produced approximately 19% higher retention compared with full filling. This configuration may function as a vent space that reduces hydrostatic pressure during cementation and promotes more uniform cement flow, while simultaneously increasing the axial wall engagement area available for micromechanical retention. These findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that variations in SAH geometry or unfilled volume can influence cement extrusion patterns and the retention of implant supported restorations.26,27,28,29 Although the magnitude of retention improvement from partial filling was moderate compared with abutment surface treatment, this approach may still have potential clinical value under specific conditions, such as limited abutment height, reduced interocclusal space, or compromised mechanical form. Regression analysis suggested that abutment APA accounted for a larger proportion of the explained variance in retention compared with SAH filling method, while the lack of interaction between factors indicates that SAH filling may serve as an adjunctive strategy for improving retention.

The second null hypothesis was rejected because significant differences were identified between the internal surface treatments. The reduced retention observed in the MDP-containing primer group can be explained by the combined effects of substrate dependent chemical compatibility and micromechanical retention mechanisms. 10-MDP is a functional phosphate monomer primarily designed to chemically bond with metallic oxides through stable M-O-P linkages.41 However, this bonding mechanism is dependent on oxide-rich inorganic substrates and is not directly applicable to PMMA, which is a polymeric material lacking such reactive sites.16 Therefore, under the present conditions, using a non-eugenol temporary cement that relies primarily on micromechanical interlocking without chemical bonding, the application of an MDP-containing primer is unlikely to enhance retention.

Contrary to this expectation, the MDP-containing primer group exhibited the lowest retention among all groups, suggesting that the MDP-containing layer on a chemically incompatible PMMA surface may act as a relatively weak interfacial film that could interfere with optimal wetting or micromechanical interlocking of the temporary cement. The high ethanol content (up to 90%)42 of the MDP-containing primer can swell the PMMA substrate, because the solubility parameter of ethanol (12.92 (cal/cm3)1/2) is very close to that of PMMA (8.9 – 12.7 (cal/cm3)1/2),43 leading to softening and superficial swelled semi-interpenetrating polymer network polymer matrix.44 While such swelling may be beneficial for chemical coupling in resin luting systems,43,44,45 it may result in a mechanically weaker and less compatible interface under temporary cementation conditions. These interpretations are consistent with the SEM and AFM findings of the present study, which demonstrated a smoother and more homogeneous surface morphology with decreased roughness after primer application (Fig. 5). Such surface smoothing can reduce the number and accessibility of microretentive features available for cement penetration, thereby limiting effective micromechanical interlocking. Consequently, the primer-treated surface may act as a relatively weak or lubricating interfacial layer, which could contribute to reduced retention.

In contrast, APA substantially increased the surface roughness of both the PMMA intaglio surface and titanium abutments, leading to higher retention values than those observed with untreated surfaces. Previous studies have demonstrated that controlled alumina abrasion generates micro-irregularities that enhance mechanical interlocking between temporary cement and polymeric or metallic substrates when appropriate parameters are used.46 However, the increase in retention was not directly proportional to the increase in surface roughness. This may be explained by the influence of surface irregularities on cement flow and film thickness, which can influence retention.47 In the present study, excessive micro-irregularities may disrupt cement flow and limit the potential gain in retention. These findings also suggest that the optimal parameters for APA may be material dependent, as polymeric substrates such as PMMA may require more conservative abrasion conditions than metallic substrates. Clinically, this suggests that PMMA intaglio APA should be applied cautiously with careful control of treatment parameter.

Limitations of this investigation include the restricted range of PMMA materials, the specific MDP-containing primer, and the non-eugenol temporary cement used, as well as the use of a relatively high crosshead speed, which was selected to reduce the influence of time-dependent viscoelastic deformation of the temporary cement.48,49,50 Moreover, because the behavior of 10-MDP is likely to depend on the chemical environment created by different cements, further studies with various cement systems are required to clarify specific effects on interim restoration retention. Within these limitations, the present findings indicate that several surface treatments and SAH filling methods may be combined to adjust retention under the specific conditions evaluated in this study.

CONCLUSION

This study evaluated how abutment surface treatment, SAH filling method, and restoration internal surface treatment affect the retention of PMMA implant restorations cemented with non-eugenol temporary cement. Within the limitations of this in vitro study, abutment APA showed the greatest effect on retention among evaluated conditions, and partial SAH filling provided additional retention. In contrast, application of an MDP-containing primer to the PMMA restoration intaglio surface was associated with reduced retention under the tested conditions. Further studies are required to confirm these findings under different material combinations and clinical conditions.
