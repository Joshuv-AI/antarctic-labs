Prevalence of preoperative sarcopenia according to EWGSOP2 in patients with colorectal cancer and its impact on surgical outcomes: a prospective observational study
Authors: Mustafa Yılmaz, Mahsum Barçin, Simge Tuna, Hasan Basri Koç, Meriş Esra Bozkurt, Gülnaz Aslan, Cumhur Özcan, Hilmi Bozkurt, Tahsin Çolak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcopenia may adversely affect postoperative outcomes, but most studies in colorectal cancer (CRC) have relied solely on computed tomography (CT)-derived muscle mass. We evaluated the association between a comprehensive assessment based on the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People 2 (EWGSOP2) criteria and short-term postoperative outcomes after curative CRC surgery.MethodsIn this prospective observational study, 70 consecutive patients who underwent curative CRC resection between July 2024 and July 2025 were assessed for muscle strength (handgrip strength), physical performance (4-m gait speed), and muscle mass (L3 CT skeletal muscle index). According to EWGSOP2 criteria, patients were classified as non-sarcopenic or into the hierarchical cumulative stages of probable, confirmed, and severe sarcopenia. Postoperative outcomes included major complications and length of hospital stay.ResultsOf the 70 patients, 41 (58.6%) were non-sarcopenic, whereas 29 (41.4%) had at least probable sarcopenia; within this hierarchical group, 27 (38.6%) had confirmed sarcopenia and 18 (25.7%) had severe sarcopenia. Patients with at least probable sarcopenia were older and had lower body mass index and serum albumin levels (all p < 0.01). Across the hierarchical EWGSOP2 stages, the frequency of major postoperative complications increased compared with non-sarcopenic patients (probable 34.4%, confirmed 37.0%, severe 55.5% vs. 9.7%, respectively; p < 0.01), and hospital stay was significantly longer (9.3–10.6 vs. 6.1 days; p < 0.001). Tumor characteristics did not differ significantly between groups. In exploratory multivariable analysis, at least probable sarcopenia was associated with major postoperative complications (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 11.75, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.27–108.82; p = 0.030) and prolonged hospital stay (aOR 8.33, 95% CI 1.01–68.38; p = 0.049), although these estimates should be interpreted cautiously because of the small sample size and wide confidence intervals.ConclusionEWGSOP2-based sarcopenia assessment was associated with worse short-term postoperative outcomes after curative CRC resection. Given the limited sample size and wide confidence intervals, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and require external validation in larger prospective cohorts before clinical implementation.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06698289.

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer worldwide, with more than 1.9 million new cases and 904,000 deaths in 2022 [1–3]. CRC surgery is associated with high complication rates, creating a significant economic and social burden [4–6]. Therefore, predicting postoperative complications is of paramount importance in clinical practice.

Sarcopenia is a progressive and generalized skeletal muscle disorder that increases the risk of falls, fractures, physical disability, and mortality [7]. The European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People 2 (EWGSOP2) and the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) define sarcopenia based on low muscle strength, low muscle mass, and impaired physical performance. Probable sarcopenia is identified when low muscle strength is detected. Confirmed sarcopenia is diagnosed when low muscle mass is present in addition to low muscle strength. Severe sarcopenia is diagnosed when low physical performance is also present [7, 8].

In patients undergoing colorectal surgery, sarcopenia has increasingly been recognized as a clinically relevant marker of reduced physiological reserve and adverse postoperative outcomes. Recent studies and meta-analyses have reported associations between preoperative sarcopenia and increased postoperative morbidity, mortality, and prolonged hospital stay after colorectal cancer surgery [9–13]. However, much of the available evidence has relied on imaging-derived parameters, such as the skeletal muscle index (SMI) measured on computed tomography (CT) or the psoas muscle index, which may not fully capture the multidimensional nature of sarcopenia [10, 11, 14–16]. In particular, functional components such as muscle strength and physical performance, which are central to EWGSOP2-based assessment, are often underrepresented in surgical oncology studies.

Therefore, this prospective observational study aimed to determine the prevalence of preoperative sarcopenia according to EWGSOP2 criteria and to evaluate its association with short-term postoperative outcomes after curative colorectal cancer surgery.

Methods

Study design and participants

Between July 2024 and July 2025, consecutive patients who underwent surgical resection for CRC at the Department of General Surgery of a tertiary university hospital were enrolled in this prospective study. Inclusion criteria were: age ≥ 18 years; American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status class III or lower; elective surgery planned with curative intent for colorectal malignancy; availability of a preoperative abdominal CT scan obtained within 1 month before surgery; and provision of written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: stage IV disease (distant metastasis); prior oncologic treatment, including neoadjuvant therapy; palliative surgery; physical impairment precluding muscle strength or physical performance testing; being bedridden or having severe mobility restrictions; and refusal to participate. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (Decision No: 2024/932). The study was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov before patient enrollment (Identifier: NCT06698289). The primary outcomes and main analysis plan were prespecified in the study protocol before data collection.

Data collection

All data were collected prospectively and stored in a digital database. Preoperative variables recorded for each patient included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), ASA physical status class, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), unintentional weight loss > 10% within the previous 3 months, serum albumin level (hypoalbuminemia defined as < 35 g/L), hemoglobin level (anemia defined as < 13 g/dL for men and < 12 g/dL for women), and tumor location. Perioperative and postoperative variables included surgical approach (laparoscopic vs. open), operative time, length of hospital stay, and pathological findings. Postoperative complications were graded according to the Clavien–Dindo classification. Major postoperative complications were defined as Clavien–Dindo grade IIIB–V, corresponding to complications requiring intervention under general anesthesia, life-threatening complications requiring intensive care management, or death. Grade IIIA complications were reported separately but were not included in the primary major complication definition because they involve interventions without general anesthesia.

Sarcopenia assessment

Preoperative sarcopenia risk was additionally screened using the SARC-F (Strength, Assistance with walking, Rise from a chair, Climb stairs, and Falls) questionnaire, a previously published five-item screening tool assessing strength, assistance with walking, rising from a chair, climbing stairs, and falls [17]. Each item is scored from 0 to 2, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 10, with scores ≥ 4 indicating high risk for sarcopenia. In the present study, SARC-F was recorded as an adjunct screening parameter and was not used as a diagnostic component of EWGSOP2 classification, which was based on handgrip strength, skeletal muscle index, and gait speed.

Sarcopenia was diagnosed according to the EWGSOP2 consensus criteria [7].

Muscle strength was assessed by handgrip strength testing of the dominant hand using a handheld hydraulic dynamometer (EH101; CAMRY, Guangdong, China), and values were recorded in kilograms (kg). Low muscle strength was defined as < 16 kg for women and < 27 kg for men [18].

Physical performance was assessed using the 4-meter usual gait speed test. Patients were instructed to walk a straight 4-meter course at their usual pace. Timing started at the first foot movement and stopped when the foot completely crossed the finish line. Three trials were performed, and gait speed was calculated in meters per second (m/s). According to EWGSOP2, gait speed ≤ 0.8 m/s indicated low physical performance; the fastest value from the three trials was used for analyses [7, 19].

Skeletal muscle mass evaluation was performed by a single radiologist using a standardized protocol. The radiologist was blinded to postoperative clinical outcomes during CT-based body composition analysis. Preoperative CT images were analyzed using dedicated body composition software. A single axial image at the level of the third lumbar vertebra (L3) was selected as the reference plane. Skeletal muscle area measurements included the psoas, paraspinal, and abdominal wall muscle groups. Semi-automated segmentation was performed with manual correction when necessary.

Skeletal muscle tissue was identified using attenuation thresholds ranging from − 29 to + 150 Hounsfield units (HU). The skeletal muscle index (SMI) was calculated by dividing skeletal muscle area (cm²) by height squared (m²). Low muscle mass was defined as an SMI < 52.4 cm²/m² for men and < 38.5 cm²/m² for women. These cut-off values were selected because they are widely used in oncologic body composition studies and allow comparability with previous colorectal cancer literature [20].

According to EWGSOP2, patients were classified as non-sarcopenic, probable sarcopenia, confirmed sarcopenia, or severe sarcopenia. Probable sarcopenia was defined by low muscle strength, confirmed sarcopenia by low muscle strength plus low muscle mass, and severe sarcopenia by the presence of confirmed sarcopenia together with reduced physical performance. Because these categories are hierarchical and cumulative by definition, confirmed sarcopenia represents a subset of probable sarcopenia, and severe sarcopenia represents a subset of confirmed sarcopenia [7].

Therefore, probable, confirmed, and severe sarcopenia were not considered mutually exclusive diagnostic groups. For the main inferential analyses, patients were dichotomized as non-sarcopenic or as having at least probable sarcopenia. This approach was selected to avoid statistically inappropriate comparisons between overlapping EWGSOP2 categories and to preserve interpretability in a relatively small cohort. Confirmed and severe sarcopenia were additionally presented descriptively to demonstrate the progressive burden of sarcopenia severity.

Patient follow-up

During the follow-up period after discharge, outpatient clinic visits were scheduled within the first two weeks, and conducted by the surgeon who performed the surgery.

Additionally, all patients were monitored through weekly telephone calls for 30 days after discharge. This follow-up method allowed for the assessment of the patients’ general health status, and hospitalization was recommended in cases where complications were suspected. Patients who developed complications after discharge were clinically evaluated upon hospitalization and received appropriate treatment.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical variables were summarized as number (n) and percentage (%).

For the primary comparative analyses, patients were grouped as non-sarcopenic or as having at least probable sarcopenia. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using the independent samples t test or the Mann-Whitney U test, depending on data distribution.

Because EWGSOP2 stages are hierarchical and cumulative rather than mutually exclusive, probable, confirmed, and severe sarcopenia were not treated as independent exposure groups in the primary inferential analyses. Confirmed sarcopenia represents a subgroup of patients with at least probable sarcopenia who also have low muscle mass, whereas severe sarcopenia represents a subgroup of confirmed sarcopenia with additional impairment in physical performance. Therefore, the main comparative and regression analyses were performed by dichotomizing patients as non-sarcopenic versus at least probable sarcopenia. This approach was chosen to avoid statistically inappropriate comparisons between overlapping categories and to preserve model interpretability in a relatively small cohort. The hierarchical EWGSOP2 stages were presented descriptively to illustrate the progressive burden of sarcopenia severity.

Accordingly, p values in the main tables refer to comparisons between non-sarcopenic patients and patients with at least probable sarcopenia, not to comparisons among mutually exclusive EWGSOP2 stages.

In addition, logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association between at least probable sarcopenia—defined as probable, confirmed, or severe sarcopenia according to EWGSOP2—and major postoperative complications and prolonged hospital stay. Unadjusted odds ratios were first calculated. Length of hospital stay was evaluated as a continuous postoperative outcome in the descriptive analyses.

For logistic regression, prolonged hospital stay was defined as hospitalization lasting more than 6 days and was considered an exploratory secondary outcome. This threshold was used to distinguish patients with delayed discharge relative to the early postoperative course of the non-sarcopenic group; however, because length of stay may be influenced by institutional discharge practices and non-clinical factors, this outcome was interpreted cautiously. The regression models were adjusted for age, sex, ASA physical status class, BMI, Charlson Comorbidity Index, serum albumin level, weight loss within the previous 3 months, and surgical approach (open vs. laparoscopic). BMI and serum albumin were included as clinically relevant covariates because of their known associations with nutritional status and postoperative outcomes; however, their potential role as markers within the nutritional/frailty pathway was considered when interpreting the adjusted estimates. These covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and were entered simultaneously into the model; no stepwise variable selection was performed. Given the relatively small number of outcome events in relation to the number of covariates, the adjusted models were considered at risk of overfitting and were used only to explore covariate-adjusted associations. Therefore, the adjusted estimates were interpreted cautiously and were not considered evidence of independent effects.

Results

Patient distribution according to sarcopenia status

A total of 70 patients were included in the study (mean age, 64.8 ± 13.2 years; 57.1% male). According to EWGSOP2 criteria, 41 patients (58.6%) were classified as non-sarcopenic, whereas 29 patients (41.4%) had at least probable sarcopenia. Within this hierarchical cumulative group, 27 patients (38.6%) fulfilled the criteria for confirmed sarcopenia and 18 patients (25.7%) fulfilled the criteria for severe sarcopenia. Because confirmed sarcopenia is nested within probable sarcopenia and severe sarcopenia is nested within confirmed sarcopenia, these categories are not mutually exclusive.

Therefore, inferential comparisons were limited to non-sarcopenic patients versus patients with at least probable sarcopenia, whereas confirmed and severe sarcopenia were presented descriptively to show increasing sarcopenia severity (Fig. 1).

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Compared with non-sarcopenic patients, those with at least probable sarcopenia were older and had lower body mass index, lower serum albumin levels, and higher Charlson Comorbidity Index scores. Overall, 53 patients (75.7%) had colon cancer and 17 patients (24.3%) had rectal cancer. Tumor location did not differ significantly between non-sarcopenic patients and those with at least probable sarcopenia (p = 0.778). No significant differences were observed with respect to sex, hemoglobin level, ASA physical status class, surgical approach, or operative time (Table 1).

Sarcopenia assessment parameters

Compared with non-sarcopenic patients, patients with at least probable sarcopenia had significantly higher SARC-F scores and significantly lower handgrip strength, skeletal muscle index, and gait speed. Confirmed and severe sarcopenia were not analyzed as independent comparison groups because they represent hierarchical subgroups within at least probable sarcopenia. Descriptively, handgrip strength, skeletal muscle index, and gait speed showed progressive deterioration across the EWGSOP2 severity spectrum (Table 2).

Postoperative outcomes and pathological characteristics

Length of hospital stay, evaluated as a continuous variable, was longer among patients with sarcopenia. The mean length of stay was 6.1 ± 1.9 days in non-sarcopenic patients and ranged from 9.3 to 10.6 days across the hierarchical EWGSOP2 stages. Major postoperative complications, defined as Clavien–Dindo grade IIIB–V, were observed in 9.7% of non-sarcopenic patients and increased descriptively across the cumulative sarcopenia stages, reaching 34.4% in probable sarcopenia, 37.0% in confirmed sarcopenia, and 55.5% in severe sarcopenia. Grade IIIA and grade V events were also reported separately in Table 3 to allow transparent interpretation of the complication severity distribution. Because these stages are overlapping hierarchical categories, this pattern should be interpreted descriptively rather than as a comparison between independent groups. No statistically significant differences were observed in tumor size, T stage, N stage, lymphovascular invasion, perineural invasion, or microsatellite instability status (Table 3). Postoperative complications were classified and analyzed according to Clavien–Dindo severity grades. However, detailed complication subtypes, including anastomotic leakage, surgical site infection, ileus, intra-abdominal abscess, pulmonary complications, urinary complications, and reoperation, were not systematically recorded in a standardized format; therefore, subtype-specific descriptive summaries, subgroup analyses, and regression analyses could not be performed.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis

In unadjusted logistic regression analysis, at least probable sarcopenia was associated with higher odds of major postoperative complications (OR, 4.87; 95% CI, 1.35–17.58; p = 0.015). An exploratory adjusted logistic regression model was then fitted to estimate covariate-adjusted associations with postoperative outcomes. In this model, at least probable sarcopenia showed an association with major postoperative complications (aOR, 11.75; 95% CI, 1.27–108.82; p = 0.030) and prolonged hospital stay (aOR, 8.33; 95% CI, 1.01–68.38; p = 0.049). A higher ASA physical status class also showed an association with prolonged hospital stay (aOR, 4.22; 95% CI, 1.25–14.25; p = 0.020).

However, because the number of outcome events was limited relative to the number of covariates and the confidence intervals were wide, these estimates should not be interpreted as precise effect estimates or as evidence of independent effects. Therefore, the adjusted regression findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory (Table 4).

Discussion

Study originality and importance of EWGSOP2 criteria

This study adds to the limited prospective evidence evaluating sarcopenia in patients undergoing CRC surgery using a multidimensional EWGSOP2 framework that incorporates muscle strength and physical performance in addition to muscle mass. In many previous studies, sarcopenia has been assessed solely using CT-derived indices such as the SMI, which may overlook functional impairment. According to EWGSOP2, low muscle strength indicates probable sarcopenia, the diagnosis is confirmed by the presence of low muscle mass, and severity is determined by low physical performance [7]. Using this comprehensive approach, our study provides a novel evaluation of EWGSOP2-defined sarcopenia and its association with postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing curative CRC resection.

Sarcopenia prevalence: triple assessment vs. CT-based approach

In our study, the prevalence of EWGSOP2-confirmed sarcopenia was 38.6%, and severe sarcopenia was identified in 25.7% of patients. When the same cohort was evaluated using CT-derived skeletal muscle index (SMI) alone, the sarcopenia prevalence increased to 57.1%. Similar patterns have been reported in the literature. Confirmed and severe sarcopenia prevalences of 19.4% and 9.7%, respectively, were reported using a multidimensional (triple) assessment; however, when SMI alone was used, the prevalence increased to 48.4% [21]. Likewise, a sarcopenia prevalence of 12.0% was reported using EWGSOP-based multidimensional assessment, whereas the proportion of patients with low muscle mass alone was 14.8% [18]. Collectively, these findings suggest that CT-based muscle-mass assessments may yield higher prevalence estimates than EWGSOP2-based multidimensional definitions, and that incorporating functional measures refines patient classification. In a prospective study that included only patients with malnutrition, sarcopenia prevalence was 46.7% [11]. Reported prevalences in retrospective studies using CT/SMI vary widely, ranging from 25% to 49.5%, 60.3%, and 68.2% [22–25]. In a meta-analysis pooling 20 studies, the overall sarcopenia prevalence was estimated as 34% [26]. Overall, our prevalence estimates using both CT-based SMI and EWGSOP2-based multidimensional assessment are consistent with prior reports.

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics

In our study, the mean age was 64.8 years, and 57.1% of patients were male, which is broadly consistent with the demographic profile reported in the CRC literature.

Sarcopenic patients had significantly lower BMI and albumin levels than non-sarcopenic patients. Weight loss ≥ 10% in the last 3 months was observed in 41.4% of the overall cohort and in approximately half of the sarcopenia groups, without a statistically significant difference between non-sarcopenic patients and those with at least probable sarcopenia (p = 0.417). Similar associations have been reported previously, with multiple studies showing that sarcopenia is linked to lower BMI and more frequent hypoalbuminemia/hypoproteinemia [18, 21–24]. Collectively, these findings suggest that sarcopenia reflects a more frail patient phenotype and may be relevant to preoperative risk evaluation.

Postoperative outcomes and complications

Many studies have reported an association between sarcopenia and increased postoperative morbidity after CRC surgery, although reported complication rates vary according to sarcopenia definition, patient population, operative approach, and outcome definitions. Huang et al. reported an overall postoperative complication rate of 58.8% among sarcopenic patients using a multidimensional definition incorporating muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical performance [18]. Nakanishi et al., using CT-based muscle mass assessment, reported an association between sarcopenia and postoperative complications, with an overall complication rate of 45.6% among sarcopenic patients [24]. More recently, Su et al. reported a postoperative complication rate of 46.8% in patients with CT-derived sarcopenia undergoing laparoscopic radical resection for non-metastatic CRC [11]. In addition, Li et al. demonstrated in a prospective cohort that sarcopenia was associated with postoperative complications and survival among CRC patients with GLIM-defined malnutrition [12]. An updated meta-analysis by Keshavjee et al. also supported the association between preoperative sarcopenia and poorer short-term postoperative outcomes after curative CRC surgery [13].

In the present study, major postoperative complications increased across the hierarchical EWGSOP2 spectrum, from 9.7% in non-sarcopenic patients to 34.4% in probable sarcopenia, 37.0% in confirmed sarcopenia, and 55.5% in severe sarcopenia.

Although direct comparison between studies is limited by differences in sarcopenia criteria and complication definitions, our findings are broadly consistent with previous reports showing a higher postoperative risk among sarcopenic patients [11, 12, 18, 24]. However, given the limited sample size, low number of outcome events relative to the number of covariates, and wide confidence intervals in the adjusted analyses, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of independent effects.

Sarcopenia was also associated with longer hospitalization in our cohort. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting prolonged postoperative length of stay among sarcopenic CRC patients [11, 12, 18]. Huang et al. and Su et al. both reported significantly longer hospital stay in sarcopenic patients, supporting the concept that reduced muscle reserve may adversely affect early postoperative recovery [11, 18].

However, length of stay is influenced not only by clinical recovery but also by institutional discharge protocols, surgeon preference, social factors, and perioperative care pathways. Therefore, the association between EWGSOP2-defined sarcopenia and prolonged hospitalization in our study should be interpreted as supportive but not definitive evidence of impaired postoperative recovery.

Functional parameters in sarcopenia assessment

Prospective studies suggest that multidimensional definitions of sarcopenia incorporating muscle strength and physical performance, in addition to muscle mass, may be associated with postoperative outcomes in CRC surgery. It has been reported that a multidimensional sarcopenia definition incorporating low muscle mass, low muscle strength, and impaired physical performance was associated with postoperative complications after CRC surgery [18]. Similarly, functional parameters have been evaluated as part of preoperative sarcopenia assessment in patients with CRC [21].

Supporting the importance of nutritional and functional reserve, sarcopenia was associated with postoperative complications and survival in a prospective CRC cohort [12].

Consistent with these findings, our study observed that patients with at least probable sarcopenia had lower handgrip strength, lower SMI, slower gait speed, and higher postoperative morbidity. Nevertheless, because we did not perform a formal head-to-head comparison between SMI alone and the full EWGSOP2 definition, our findings should be interpreted as showing an association between multidimensional sarcopenia assessment and postoperative outcomes rather than demonstrating superiority over CT-based assessment alone. This cautious interpretation is also important given the limited sample size and exploratory nature of the multivariable findings. Because the primary exposure variable was defined as at least probable sarcopenia, it included patients with low muscle strength even before the additional confirmation of low muscle mass or impaired physical performance. Therefore, this variable should be interpreted not only as a marker of established multidimensional sarcopenia, but also as a broader indicator of reduced functional reserve, dynapenia, and frailty-related vulnerability. Because at least probable sarcopenia includes patients with low muscle strength alone as well as patients with confirmed or severe sarcopenia, the observed associations cannot determine whether postoperative risk was driven by sarcopenia itself, impaired muscle strength, or their combined effect.

Relationship between sarcopenia and pathological parameters

From a pathological perspective, we found no statistically significant differences between sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic patients regarding T stage, N stage, LVI, PNI, MSI status, or tumor size. This finding suggests that sarcopenia reflects the patient’s systemic physiological reserve rather than tumor anatomical or pathological features.

Consistent with this, prior studies have reported that sarcopenia is not necessarily linked to tumor stage, size, or invasion characteristics. Sarcopenia was reported to be independent of TN stage and tumor location [24]. Similarly, no significant association was found between sarcopenia and tumor stage in a CRC cohort [12]. Overall, the available evidence indicates that associations between sarcopenia and pathological tumor parameters are limited and may vary across cohorts and definitions.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study is its prospective design and multidimensional sarcopenia assessment based on EWGSOP2 criteria. However, the small sample size and limited number of major complications may have reduced model stability; therefore, the regression findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Because EWGSOP2 stages are hierarchical, at least probable sarcopenia was used as the primary exposure, while confirmed and severe sarcopenia were reported descriptively. In addition, because the primary exposure was defined as at least probable sarcopenia, which combines patients with low muscle strength alone and those with confirmed or severe sarcopenia, the relative contribution of low muscle strength versus established multidimensional sarcopenia to postoperative outcomes could not be determined.

Generalizability may be limited by the single-center design, colon-predominant cohort, exclusion of patients receiving neoadjuvant treatment or unable to complete functional tests, lack of Turkish population-specific SMI cut-offs, absence of CT measurement reliability analyses, and the lack of systematically recorded postoperative complication subtypes. Residual confounding remains possible because inflammatory status, GLIM-defined malnutrition, and formal performance status were not systematically assessed; therefore, the observed associations should not be interpreted as causal. In addition, length of hospital stay may be influenced by institutional practices, the Clavien–Dindo IIIB–V threshold may limit comparability, and adjustment for BMI and albumin may have introduced potential overadjustment.

Conclusions

Preoperative sarcopenia defined according to EWGSOP2 criteria was associated with worse short-term postoperative outcomes after curative colorectal cancer surgery in this colon-predominant cohort. However, because of the limited sample size, low number of outcome events, and wide confidence intervals, these findings should be considered exploratory and require external validation in larger multicenter cohorts with balanced representation of colon and rectal cancer patients before clinical implementation.
