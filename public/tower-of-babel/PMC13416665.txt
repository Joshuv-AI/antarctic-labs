Associations Between the Vitamin D Levels and Urinary Albumin-Creatinine Ratio in Adults With Hypertension
Authors: Youxue Zhang, Xuexun Chen, Xiangming Wang, Min Guo, Shujuan Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rationale & ObjectiveHypertension is a major risk factor for cardiovascular and kidney diseases. Although an elevated urinary albumin-creatinine ratio (UACR)—even within the normal range—predicts increased cardiovascular risk, and vitamin D level is associated with UACR in general populations, this relationship remains unclear in individuals with hypertension.Study DesignThis study employed a cross-sectional design using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, 1999-2018).Setting & ParticipantsThe study involved a cohort of 37,554 individuals, comprising 19,802 patients with hypertension and 17,752 nonhypertensive individuals from the United States, with a mean age of 46.7 ± 16.8 years.ExposureSerum vitamin D levels.OutcomeUACR.Analytical ApproachSurvey-weighted multivariable regression models were applied to assess the relationship between vitamin D level and log2-transformed UACR. Restricted cubic splines, subgroup analyses, and sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the nature and robustness of the association.ResultsIn patients with hypertension, the inverse association between vitamin D level and UACR was more pronounced than in nonhypertensive individuals (P for interaction = 0.03). Vitamin D deficiency was associated with 17.3% higher geometric mean UACR (β = 0.23) than insufficiency, whereas sufficient levels showed no significant difference (P = 0.51). A threshold effect was identified at approximately 60 nmol/L, with levels below this point showing a significant negative correlation (β = −0.14, equivalent to a 9.3% reduction in UACR, P < 0.001). These consistent findings were corroborated by subgroup and sensitivity analyses.LimitationsThe observational design precludes causal inferences, and residual confounding may persist despite adjustments. Generalizability may be limited, because data were derived from a single US cohort, and both vitamin D level and UACR relied on single measurements.ConclusionsThere is an L-shaped inverse association between serum vitamin D levels and UACR among US adults with hypertension.

Long-standing hypertension can lead to systemic vascular diseases, including coronary heart disease, stroke, and chronic kidney failure. It has significantly contributed to increased global mortality.1, 2,1, 2 The urinary albumin-creatinine ratio (UACR) is strongly associated with the risk of myocardial injury,3,4 renal outcomes, and mortality among individuals with hypertension.5 Even within the normal range, elevated UACR increases overall and cardiovascular-related mortality in patients with hypertension.6, 7, 8 Moreover, reducing UACR has been shown to confer significant cardiac and renal benefits.9,10 Vitamin D is closely associated with primary hypertension, with low levels of vitamin D correlating with an increased incidence of hypertension, particularly elevated diastolic blood pressure.11 Vitamin D supplementation has been shown to effectively lower blood pressure in patients with hypertension.12 Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated a negative correlation between vitamin D concentrations and UACR in patients with diabetes.13 However, it remains unclear whether vitamin D concentrations exhibit a similar association with UACR in patients with hypertension, or if vitamin D supplementation could effectively reduce UACR in this population. This study aimed to investigate the influence of hypertension status on the relationship between vitamin D levels and UACR through a secondary analysis of the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database. Additionally, it examined the association between serum vitamin D levels and UACR within a cohort of patients with hypertension.

Methods

Study Participants

The NHANES was conducted in the United States by using a sampling approach that includes the random selection of individuals from the overall population. Comprehensive technical details regarding the sample design can be found on the NHANES Survey Methods and Analytic Guidelines page.14,15 Approval for the study protocol was granted by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board. Before the commencement of data collection and NHANES health examinations, informed consent was secured from the qualifying participants. All authors verified that the methods were implemented in alignment with the applicable NHANES Analytic Guidelines.16 Data collection took place on an ongoing basis and was disseminated in biennial intervals. The data were analyzed based on instructions designed to improve epidemiologic tracking tests. Our analysis encompassed 10 cycles from 1999-2018. Initially, participants with missing vitamin D or UACR data were excluded, resulting in a sample of 65,424 individuals. Subsequently, we removed 20,149 participants under the age of 18 years and 7,721 individuals lacking complete covariate data. This process yielded a final cohort of 37,554 individuals, which included 19,802 patients with hypertension and 17,752 nonhypertensive individuals (Fig 1).Figure 1Flowchart of sample selection from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 1999-2018. Exposure and Outcome Variables

UACR was the dependent variable selected for this study. To evaluate UACR, midstream random urine samples were collected, with a preference for morning specimens. The samples were transported under refrigerated conditions at 2-8°C and were analyzed or frozen within 24 hours. UACR is a critical measure in nephrology and is computed by dividing the concentration of urinary albumin in milligrams (mg) by the concentration of urinary creatinine in grams (g). Both data points were directly obtained from the NHANES database. The present analysis focused on the independent variables related to serum vitamin D levels, which includes the total amounts of 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 and 25-hydroxyvitamin D2. The serum concentrations of both 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 and 25-hydroxyvitamin D2 were quantified in NHANES using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry method.17 Participants’ vitamin D levels were categorized following the criteria set by the Endocrine Society: deficient (<50 nmol/L), insufficient (≥50 to <75 nmol/L), and sufficient (≥75 nmol/L).18 The identification of hypertension was made based on antihypertensive drug usage, individual accounts of hypertension, or blood pressure measurements of at least 130 mm Hg for systolic or at least 80 mm Hg for diastolic, as per the guidelines established by the American Heart Association in 2017.19

Covariables

This study examined various potential confounding factors that might affect the connection between vitamin D levels and UACR. The criteria for determining which variables to control were as follows: (1) including variables acknowledged as confounders according to existing research or professional expertise; and (2) selecting variables that would result in at least 10% variation in the matched β when included in the model. The covariables considered included sex, age, race/ethnicity, alcohol consumption, smoking status, physical activity, body mass index (BMI), and laboratory measurements such as glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), serum albumin, serum calcium, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Because of the strong association between diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and UACR, both conditions were included in the adjustment.

We conducted multicollinearity diagnostics for all covariates by calculating the variance inflation factor. The variance inflation factor values for all variables were <2.5, indicating that collinearity issues did not compromise the stability of the model results.

Sex, age, race/ethnicity, BMI, and laboratory parameters such as HbA1c, serum albumin, and serum calcium were directly retrieved from the NHANES database. Smoking status was categorized as never, former, or current according to established guidelines.20 Concerning alcohol intake, individuals were classified as drinkers if they had consumed a minimum of 12 alcoholic beverages within a year at any time during their lifetime.21 To assess the levels of physical activity among participants, the study computed the weekly metabolic equivalent of task (MET)-minutes. Following the Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, these MET-minutes were classified into 3 distinct categories. Individuals were labeled as inactive if their MET-minutes amounted to zero per week. Those who achieved between 1 and 599 MET-minutes per week were deemed insufficiently active. Lastly, individuals who exceeded 600 MET-minutes per week were considered sufficiently active.22 The Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) formula was used to determine the eGFR, offering a reliable estimate of kidney function. Diabetes diagnosis in participants adhered to guidelines established by the American Diabetes Association, which encompass various criteria including self-reported cases, ongoing medication use for diabetes management, fasting glucose measurements ≥126 mg/dL, or HbA1c measurements ≥6.5%.23

Confirmation of cardiovascular disease among participants was facilitated through self-reporting during structured interviews that evaluated their medical history. During these interviews, individuals were queried about whether a health care professional had ever diagnosed them with any cardiovascular conditions. Any affirmative answer was interpreted as an affirmation of a cardiovascular disease diagnosis.24

Statistical Analysis

A comprehensive analysis of NHANES data requires the application of sampling weights, data clustering, and stratification methods to provide accurately representative estimates.16 In this study, the protocols for NHANES analysis were followed, applying weights for the years 1999-2002 set at one-fifth of the 4-year Mobile Examination Center weight. For data from 2003-2018, the weights were calculated using a formula of one-tenth of the weight assigned to the two-year Mobile Examination Center examination. In the analyses, sampling weights, primary sampling units, and stratification variables were concurrently applied to both descriptive and inferential statistics. For descriptive statistics, weighted calculations guaranteed the representativeness of means and proportions. In weighted linear regression, by specifying all 3 design elements, the resulting regression coefficients, standard errors, and P values were adjusted for the complex sampling design, thereby ensuring the validity of statistical inference.

Continuous variables are summarized as means with standard deviations, while categorical variables are presented as unweighted frequencies paired with weighted percentages. For continuous variables, group comparisons were performed using weighted linear regression within the complex survey design framework. Variables that deviated from a normal distribution were log-transformed before analysis to better meet model assumptions. Categorical data analysis was conducted using Rao–Scott χ2 tests.

The independent association between vitamin D levels and UACR was analyzed using multivariable regression analysis with various models. No covariates were included in model 1. Model 2 accounted for variables including sex, age, and race/ethnicity. Meanwhile, model 3 incorporated all covariates from model 2, in addition to factors such as smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, BMI, HbA1c, serum albumin, serum calcium, eGFR, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease.

Given its right-skewed distribution, UACR was log2-transformed for all models. Vitamin D was analyzed both as a continuous variable, scaled in increments of 10 nmol/L for clinical interpretability, and as a categorical variable. When treated categorically, the P value for trend was calculated by modeling the categorical variable as a continuous term. To investigate nonlinearity, the continuous relationship was modeled using weighted restricted cubic splines with 4 knots, and the statistical significance of the nonlinear component was assessed using the likelihood ratio test, reported as the P for nonlinearity.

Furthermore, stratified analyses were conducted by sex, age, race/ethnicity, physical activity, BMI, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease. Effect modification was assessed by including interaction terms (eg, vitamin D × stratum) in the regression models, with significance tested via the likelihood ratio test (reported as the P for interaction). The complex survey design was incorporated into all regression and spline models to ensure valid inference. The determination of the sample size in this study was based solely on the existing data rather than any a priori statistical power calculations.

R software (version 4.2.1), R survey package (version 4.1-1), and free statistical software (version 2.1.1; Beijing Free Clinical Medical Technology Co, Ltd) were used for data analysis. Two-sided P values <0.05 were defined as statistically significant.

Association Between Hypertensive Status and the Relationship of Vitamin D Levels With UACR

Figure S1 shows the association between vitamin D levels and UACR across different hypertensive states. The subgroup analysis indicated that, regardless of hypertensive status, individuals with vitamin D deficiency exhibited significantly elevated UACR levels compared with those with vitamin D insufficiency. Notably, this difference was more pronounced in the hypertensive cohort (P for interaction = 0.03). Furthermore, no significant differences were observed between the vitamin D sufficiency and insufficiency groups in either cohort.

Factors Associated With UACR in Participants With Hypertension

The outcomes of the univariate regression analysis indicated a relationship between UACR and various demographic and clinical factors (Table S1). These variables comprised age, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, marital status, alcohol consumption, BMI, HbA1c, total cholesterol, serum albumin, fasting blood glucose, eGFR, serum calcium, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases. When vitamin D was treated as a continuous variable, a 10 nmol/L increase in vitamin D concentration was associated with a mean decrease of −0.1 in log2(UACR), which corresponds to a 7.0% reduction in UACR after transformation (P = 0.078). Furthermore, a more in-depth analysis was performed by categorizing vitamin D into groups. This investigation indicated that participants in the vitamin D deficient group had significantly higher log2(UACR) levels compared with those in the insufficient group (β, 0.35; 95% confidence interval, 0.29-0.41; P < 0.001). This corresponds to a 27.4% higher geometric mean UACR. In contrast, no notable difference in UACR was detected between the vitamin D sufficient group and the insufficient group.

Associations Between Vitamin D and UACR in Individuals With Hypertension

In the multivariable model treating vitamin D level as a continuous variable, a significant inverse linear association with log2(UACR) was observed (β = −0.02 per 10 nmol/L, P < 0.01). When modeled categorically, a nonlinear pattern emerged: compared with the insufficient group, the deficient group had significantly higher log2(UACR) (β, 0.23: 95% confidence interval, 0.16-0.30), which corresponds to a 17.3% higher geometric mean UACR (95% confidence interval, 11.6%-23.1%). In contrast, the sufficient group did not significantly differ from the insufficient group (β, −0.02; P = 0.51) (see Table 2). The linear trend test yielded a nonsignificant result (P for trend = 0.183), suggesting a nonlinear relationship, which aligns with the findings from the subsequent nonlinear analysis. Weighted curve fitting, adjusted for all variables, indicated an L-shaped relationship between vitamin D levels and UACR (Fig 2). Discussion

This study provides insights into the exploration of the relationship between vitamin D levels and UACR among patients with hypertension at a national level. Using a cross-sectional design with a large-scale sample, the findings indicated in the setting of hypertension, there is an amplified L-shaped association between vitamin D concentrations and UACR. A threshold of 59.5 nmol/L was identified, below which vitamin D levels were significantly negatively correlated with UACR. The observed associations remained consistent across subgroup analyses that took into account sex, age, BMI, physical activity, eGFR, and comorbid conditions. Sensitivity analyses further demonstrated the robustness of these findings.

Microalbuminuria serves as a crucial marker of vascular endothelial dysfunction and hypertensive target organ damage.25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30 Importantly, UACR predicts mortality risk in a continuous manner, with elevations observed even within the normal range (15-30 mg/g) being associated with significantly increased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality.31,32 Furthermore, vitamin D deficiency has been linked to higher levels of microalbuminuria in the general population, independently of eGFR,33, 34, 35 and is associated with elevated UACR in chronic kidney disease.36

The inverse association between vitamin D concentration and UACR may be explored through several hypothesized pathways. Experimental studies have demonstrated that vitamin D suppresses the renin-angiotensin system,37, 38, 39, 40 and vitamin D deficiency has been linked to elevated angiotensin II, which has been implicated in the pathogenesis of proteinuria and renal injury.39 In addition, inflammation has been suggested as a potential link, as vitamin D deficiency is associated with increased levels of inflammatory markers in kidney disease.41, 42, 43 These pathways remain speculative in the context of our observational study and are discussed only as potential hypotheses that could inform future research, rather than as established mechanisms underlying the vitamin D–UACR association.

Earlier research has demonstrated a negative correlation between levels of vitamin D and UACR in individuals with diabetes.13,44 Consistent with these findings, we observed a similar association in patients with hypertension, independent of eGFR, diabetes, or cardiovascular disease. Although diabetes is a confirmed risk factor for elevated UACR, a key finding of our interaction analysis is that diabetic status did not significantly modify the association between vitamin D levels and UACR in hypertensive individuals (P for interaction = 0.482; Fig 3). This finding suggests that the relationship between vitamin D deficiency and higher albuminuria in hypertension may be independent of diabetic status. The persistent L-shaped association across diabetes subgroups is consistent with the involvement of more generalized pathways that are common to both hypertension and diabetic renal injury. Therefore, our data indicate that vitamin D status is associated with albuminuria in patients with hypertension, irrespective of the concomitant presence of diabetes. We identified a threshold of 59.5 nmol/L, below which vitamin D levels correlated negatively with UACR, indicating that lower vitamin D is associated with higher UACR in hypertension.

Although vitamin D has been shown to protect podocytes and reduce proteinuria,45,46 and its supplementation has been associated with reduced mortality in patients with hypertension in some observational research,47 interventions have not consistently lowered UACR, particularly in patients with diabetes.48 Of note, these inconsistent findings highlight that the observed association between vitamin D level and UACR does not imply causality. Several alternative explanations should be considered. First, reverse causation is plausible: elevated UACR may indicate early renal impairment, which could reduce vitamin D synthesis and increase urinary loss of vitamin D-binding protein,49 leading to lower measured vitamin D levels. Second, unmeasured confounding factors—such as physical inactivity, limited sunlight exposure, or overall poor health status—may independently influence both vitamin D levels and UACR.50,51 Although we adjusted for several factors, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Taken together, these considerations underscore that the observed vitamin D–UACR association may reflect underlying disease processes or shared risk factors and should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct protective effect of vitamin D on kidney function.

Research findings indicate that among patients with hypertension, the L-shaped association between vitamin D concentrations and UACR is more pronounced than that observed in nonhypertensive individuals. Specifically, UACR was negatively correlated with vitamin D levels when these levels were <59.5 nmol/L. This association is based on data obtained from the NHANES (1999-2018). A thorough examination of possible biases and confounding factors, along with a rigorous sampling design based on a population, supports the reliability of these results.

This study has limitations inherent to its observational cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inference. Although adjustments for known confounders were conducted comprehensively and subgroup analyses were performed on identified variables, residual confounding effects persist. Furthermore, the reliance on a single measurement for both serum vitamin D and UACR may not fully capture long-term exposure or intraindividual variability. Additionally, data from US cohorts underscore the necessity of conducting research in varied populations to confirm our results. However, the reliability of the results from this study is bolstered by a robust study design and extensive nationwide support.

In conclusion, this cross-sectional study indicates that in individuals with hypertension, lower vitamin D levels are associated with higher UACR, with this association characterized by a threshold effect. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses further supported these observed associations.
