Brain Abscess Revealing an Undiagnosed Atrioventricular Septal Defect With a Common Atrium in a Young Adult
Authors: Evans Nii Ayitey MacCready, Ama Owusuaa Kwakye, Kelvin Denanyoh, Hilda Kwapong, Isaac Nene Keteku, Jochebed Ansah, Werman Reve Robles
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBrain abscess is an uncommon but life‐threatening intracranial infection that most often arises from contiguous spread but may also occur through hematogenous dissemination in patients with congenital heart disease. This is usually associated with defects that permit right‐to‐left shunting of septic emboli. Its occurrence in association with atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD) is rarely reported, particularly as the initial presentation in adulthood. We report an 18‐year‐old male who presented with a generalized tonic–clonic seizure preceded by a 4‐day history of frontal headache. Examination revealed digital clubbing, conjunctival plethora, and a pansystolic murmur. Neuroimaging demonstrated a right frontal lobe abscess. Cardiac evaluation identified atrial flutter and a previously undiagnosed AVSD with a large atrial component resulting in a common atrium and a ventricular component with predominantly left‐to‐right shunting, without echocardiographic evidence of pulmonary hypertension. The patient recovered fully with complete radiological resolution after surgical drainage of the abscess, and remained clinically stable at 6‐month follow‐up. In patients with AVSD and a common atrium, the absence of atrial septation due to the large atrial component permits mixing of systemic and pulmonary venous blood that may result in chronic hypoxemia. This intracardiac communication may also allow septic material to bypass pulmonary clearance. Brain abscess may therefore represent the initial manifestation of AVSD with a common atrium, highlighting the importance of careful cardiovascular examination and timely echocardiographic evaluation in patients presenting with a brain abscess of unclear origin.Keywords: atrioventricular septal defect, brain abscess, common atrium, congenital heart disease

Introduction

Brain abscess is a serious and potentially life‐threatening intracranial infection that results from contiguous spread from otogenic, paranasal sinus, or dental sources in approximately half of cases [1]. Less commonly, it occurs via hematogenous dissemination, particularly in patients with congenital heart disease (CHD), where intracardiac shunts allow septic emboli to bypass pulmonary filtration and seed the cerebral circulation [1, 2, 3]. Congenital heart defects with right‐to‐left shunting, such as tetralogy of Fallot and septal defects with Eisenmenger physiology, are most commonly implicated, as chronic hypoxemia, polycythemia, and impaired pulmonary clearance of bacteria increase susceptibility to cerebral infection [4].

Atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD) is a congenital cardiac anomaly characterized by a common atrioventricular junction with varying degrees of deficiency of the atrioventricular septal structures and accounts for approximately 4%–7% of congenital heart defects [5, 6]. Complete or near‐complete absence of the atrial septum may occur, resulting in a common atrium [5]. Atrioventricular septal defects are often diagnosed early in life due to their association with Down syndrome, characteristic murmurs, and early heart failure [5]. Neurological complications such as brain abscesses are rarely reported in association with AVSD, with the literature limited to isolated case reports, often in the context of complex congenital cardiac anomalies [7, 8, 9, 10].

We present the case of a young adult male without syndromic features who developed a brain abscess as the initial presentation of an unrecognized AVSD with a common atrium, underscoring the importance of considering underlying congenital heart disease in patients presenting with brain abscess of unclear origin.

Case History and Examination

An 18‐year‐old male with no known chronic medical conditions was brought to our hospital after experiencing a generalized tonic–clonic seizure at school. The seizure lasted approximately 5 min and resolved spontaneously. There was no preceding aura, tongue biting, or urinary or fecal incontinence, but he experienced post‐ictal confusion for about 30 min.

He reported a 4‐day history of progressively worsening, severe, constant headache localized to the right frontal and periorbital region. The headache had no clear exacerbating factors and was partially relieved by over‐the‐counter analgesics. He denied fever, prior seizures, head trauma, ear or sinus infections, dental disease, or recent systemic illness.

Further history revealed mild exertional dyspnea since childhood, particularly during running or playing football, which improved with rest. There was no history of syncope, chest pain, palpitations, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, or peripheral edema. He had no previous cardiac diagnosis or surgical history. Birth and developmental history were unremarkable. He did not smoke, consume alcohol, or use recreational drugs.

On examination, he was afebrile with a temperature of 36.8°C and an oxygen saturation of 94% on room air at rest. He was bradycardic with a heart rate of 50 beats per minute, and had a blood pressure of 108/68 mmHg. There was no cyanosis, but conjunctival plethora and stage 3 digital clubbing of both fingers and toes were noted.

Precordial examination revealed a grade 3/6 pansystolic murmur best heard at the lower left sternal border, with a fixed split second heart sound. Jugular venous pressure was not elevated, and there was no peripheral edema. Neurological examination was unremarkable, with intact cranial nerves and normal motor and sensory findings. Other system examinations, including ear, nose, throat, and dental examinations, were normal.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Differential diagnoses considered included brain abscess and other intracranial space‐occupying lesions such as neoplasm and tuberculoma, given the subacute presentation of headache followed by a seizure. A primary seizure disorder and intracranial hemorrhage were also considered. The presence of digital clubbing, conjunctival plethora, a fixed split second heart sound, and pansystolic murmur also raised suspicion of an underlying congenital heart disease, prompting further cardiac evaluation.

Full blood count showed marked erythrocytosis, with hemoglobin of 21.4 g/dL and hematocrit 63.5%, suggestive of chronic hypoxemia. White blood cell and platelet counts were normal but C‐reactive protein was elevated. Renal and liver function tests, and electrolytes were within normal limits.

Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the brain demonstrated a 1.9 × 1.2 × 1.9 cm ring‐enhancing lesion in the right frontal lobe with surrounding vasogenic edema and no significant mass effect, consistent with a brain abscess. Electrocardiography revealed atrial flutter with a slow ventricular response of 52 beats per minute, as well as left axis deviation, left ventricular hypertrophy, and T wave inversion in V1–V3, as seen in Figure 1. He remained hemodynamically stable.

Chest radiography showed cardiomegaly with prominent central pulmonary arteries and increased pulmonary vascular markings, suggestive of chronic pulmonary overcirculation, as shown in Figure 2.

Chest radiograph demonstrating cardiomegaly and prominent pulmonary vascular markings.

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed findings consistent with AVSD with a common atrium. These included a common atrioventricular junction with separate right and left atrioventricular valve orifices, a large atrial component of the AVSD resulting in a common atrium due to the absence of the atrial septum with blood flow into both ventricles, and a small ventricular component of the AVSD (Figure 3A,B). The ventricular component demonstrated predominantly left‐to‐right shunting (Figure 3C). There was no echocardiographic evidence of pulmonary hypertension, supported by trace right atrioventricular valve regurgitation and an inferior vena cava diameter of 1.3 cm with normal respiratory variability, as shown in Figure 3D. Left atrioventricular valve regurgitation with a maximum velocity of 3.9 m/s was noted. Biventricular systolic function was preserved with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 55%. No vegetations or other features suggestive of infective endocarditis were identified.

A diagnosis of right frontal lobe abscess in the setting of previously undiagnosed AVSD with a common atrium was made. Blood cultures were negative and no extracranial source of infection was identified. He was commenced on intravenous ceftriaxone and metronidazole, together with levetiracetam for seizure prophylaxis. Repeat CT imaging performed after 12 days of antibiotic therapy demonstrated enlargement of the abscess to 3.5 × 3.0 × 3.3 cm, without midline shift, as seen in Figure 4A. Burr‐hole drainage was therefore performed, and 30 mL of purulent material was aspirated. Culture of the abscess yielded no growth, probably due to prior antibiotic therapy, with Streptococcus species and anaerobes being the most commonly implicated organisms. Rivaroxaban was initiated after weighing the bleeding risk because of atrial flutter and thromboembolic risk in line with current guidelines.

(A) Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography of the brain showing a ring‐enhancing lesion (yellow arrow) in the right frontal lobe with surrounding vasogenic edema, consistent with a brain abscess. (B) Follow‐up contrast‐enhanced computed tomography of the brain 6 weeks after surgery depicting complete resolution of the abscess.

Conclusions and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

He was discharged 12 days after surgery on oral antibiotics, levetiracetam, and oral anticoagulation. Follow‐up CT imaging at 6 weeks demonstrated complete resolution of the abscess (Figure 4B). At the 6‐month review, he remained asymptomatic, with no further seizures or neurological deficits, and continued cardiology and cardiothoracic surgery follow‐up.

Discussion

Brain abscess secondary to congenital heart disease is classically associated with cyanotic lesions and established right‐to‐left shunting [2]. In such settings, chronic hypoxemia, polycythemia, and impaired pulmonary bacterial clearance predispose patients to septic embolization and subsequent cerebral infection [2, 4]. Intracardiac right‐to‐left shunting may allow pathogens to bypass pulmonary filtration and enter the cerebral circulation directly [3]. Reports of brain abscess in patients with AVSD are scarce and largely limited to isolated case reports. One report described brain abscess as the initial manifestation of a congenital cardiac anomaly reported as a primum atrial septal defect with a cleft mitral valve [7], which would be considered an AVSD with an isolated atrial component and a left atrioventricular valve zone of apposition abnormality using contemporary nomenclature [6]. Another report described brain abscess in a patient with complex congenital heart disease including complete atrioventricular septal defect and single ventricle physiology [8]. In addition, brain abscess has been reported in a child with a common atrium, a common atrioventricular valve, and associated structural abnormalities, in the setting of cyanotic congenital heart disease with pulmonary hypertension [9]. Furthermore, brain abscess has been reported in a patient with complete atrioventricular septal defect associated with tetralogy of Fallot, highlighting the role of cyanotic physiology in predisposing to cerebral infection [10].

This case illustrates AVSD with a common atrium in a young adult in whom brain abscess was the initial presentation. The small ventricular component and the absence of echocardiographic evidence of pulmonary hypertension may have limited early hemodynamic compromise and delayed the development of heart failure [5, 11]. These factors may have contributed to survival into early adulthood without previous recognition of the congenital defect.

The common atrium resulting from the large atrial component of the AVSD allows direct communication between systemic and pulmonary venous blood, resulting in intracardiac mixing and providing a potential pathway for right‐to‐left shunting that may lead to reduced systemic arterial oxygen saturation [12, 13]. This may explain the secondary polycythemia and digital clubbing observed in our patient. Importantly, this intracardiac communication may allow septic material to bypass pulmonary clearance, predisposing to cerebral infection [3].

Atrial flutter, as observed in this patient, may occur in adolescents and adults with congenital heart disease and is associated with underlying structural abnormalities and atrial remodeling. It reflects advanced structural changes and may confer thromboembolic risk, warranting the consideration of anticoagulation in accordance with current guidelines [11, 14].

Management of brain abscess in the setting of congenital heart disease requires a multidisciplinary approach. While initial medical therapy may be appropriate, surgical drainage is indicated when there is failure of medical management or radiological progression, as occurred in this case [1]. Following resolution of the intracranial infection, long‐term management of the underlying cardiac defect is essential to reduce the risk of recurrence and other complications, in accordance with European Society of Cardiology adult congenital heart disease guidelines [11].

This case contributes to the limited literature on brain abscess in atrioventricular septal defects and highlights that cerebral infection may occur in patients with AVSD and a common atrium even in the absence of pulmonary hypertension and Eisenmenger physiology. Although limited by the inherent constraints of a single case, it emphasizes a clinically important presentation of atrioventricular septal defect.
