Detecting Distributional Differences in Spatially Correlated Multivariate Data via Kernel-Smoothed Rank-Based Empirical Copula Tests
Marco Mandap

Abstract
Comparing multivariate yield quality distributions across spatially referenced agricultural fields is complicated by two pervasive features: non-normality and spatial autocorrelation. Classical procedures such as ANOVA, MANOVA, and standard rank tests assume independence and therefore exhibit severe Type I error inflation when spatial dependence is present. We propose a nonparametric spatial Cramer–von Mises–type test based on kernel-smoothed empirical copula processes constructed from pooled componentwise ranks. Spatial kernel weights account explicitly for local dependence, while the rank transformation removes sensitivity to marginal distributional form. Under fixed-domain infill asymptotics and polynomial α \alpha -mixing conditions, we establish weak convergence of the smoothed empirical copula process to a mean-zero Gaussian limit and show that the resulting quadratic test statistic converges to a weighted sum of chi-squared random variables restricted to the ( K − 1 ) (K-1) -dimensional contrast subspace. Practical inference is obtained through a Satterthwaite approximation calibrated using the exact discrete spatial covariance operator under a Gaussian copula model. Monte Carlo experiments with bivariate log-normal spatial data demonstrate that the proposed test maintains nominal size across varying strengths of spatial dependence, in contrast to classical parametric and non-spatial rank-based methods, which become severely anti-conservative. The procedure provides a theoretically justified and computationally tractable framework for comparing multivariate spatial yield distributions in precision agriculture and related applied settings. Keywords: Spatial Autocorrelation, Rank-Based Tests, Kernel Smoothing, Alpha-Mixing, Precision Agriculture, Satterthwaite Approximation.



1
Introduction

Rigorous comparison of yield-quality distributions across agricultural fields or management zones is central to evidence-based decision-making in precision agriculture. Evaluating fertilizers, cultivars, irrigation regimes, or soil amendments requires determining whether observed differences in outcomes such as protein content, moisture, or nutrient density reflect genuine distributional changes rather than sampling variability. In practice, however, yield data are spatially indexed and rarely satisfy the independence and normality assumptions underlying classical inference procedures. Applying standard parametric tests without accounting for spatial structure risks misleading conclusions.

1.1
Challenges of Spatial Yield Data

Two structural features complicate inference for spatial yield measurements. First, non-normality is common. Empirical distributions of yield-quality metrics are often skewed, heavy-tailed, or multimodal due to localized environmental heterogeneity, including drainage patterns, pest infestations, soil variability, and microclimatic differences. Mean-based parametric procedures are sensitive to such departures from Gaussian assumptions, particularly in moderate sample sizes.

Second, spatial autocorrelation induces dependence among nearby observations. Measurements collected in close proximity tend to be more similar than those separated by larger distances. This spatial dependence effectively reduces the amount of independent information in the dataset. When ignored, it leads to underestimated variances, artificially narrow confidence intervals, and inflated Type I error rates. Empirical and theoretical work in spatial statistics and econometrics has documented that classical variance estimators can perform poorly under spatial dependence, motivating the development of spatially robust procedures
[
1
,
10
]
.

Kernel-based smoothing methods have long been used to model spatially structured phenomena by replacing discrete observations with locally weighted averages that reflect spatial proximity
[
7
,
9
]
. While such approaches are typically employed for estimation and mapping, they also provide a natural mechanism for constructing spatially informed test statistics. At the same time, rank-based procedures offer robustness to skewness and heavy tails by relying on order information rather than moment assumptions
[
3
]
. Integrating these two ideas suggests a framework for distributional comparison that is both robust to non-normality and explicitly attentive to spatial geometry.

1.2
Limitations of Classical Tests Under Spatial Dependence

Standard inferential tools such as the
t
-test, ANOVA, and MANOVA assume independent observations and often approximate normality. When applied to spatially correlated yield data, these methods typically underestimate sampling variability. In agricultural applications, this can produce spurious declarations of statistically significant differences between fields or management zones. Even nonparametric rank tests, while robust to non-normality, generally retain an implicit independence assumption and therefore remain vulnerable to spatial variance inflation.

Spatially robust covariance estimators

and heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) methods address some aspects of dependence
[
1
]
. However, they are usually formulated for mean-based statistics rather than full distributional comparison. Geostatistical approaches such as kriging focus primarily on prediction and spatial interpolation rather than formal hypothesis testing of equality of distributions
[
7
]
. Consequently, there remains a methodological gap for tests that (i) compare entire yield-quality distributions, (ii) remain robust to heavy tails and outliers, and (iii) accommodate spatial dependence in a theoretically principled manner.

1.3
A Rank-Based Spatial Testing Framework

We propose a spatial Cramer–von Mises–type testing procedure built on kernel-smoothed empirical distribution functions (EDFs) constructed from pooled ranks. The approach combines three elements.

First, rank transformation reduces sensitivity to skewness and extreme observations by replacing raw measurements with their relative ordering within the pooled sample. This yields a distribution-free representation under the null hypothesis and avoids reliance on moment conditions.

Second, spatial kernel smoothing incorporates the geometry of observation locations. By weighting observations according to spatial proximity to a reference location, the resulting smoothed EDF reflects local spatial structure while preserving distributional information. The effective sample size is determined by the kernel weights, providing a transparent link between spatial dependence and variance inflation.

Third, under
\alpha
-mixing (strong mixing) conditions that quantify the decay of spatial dependence, we establish that the normalized contrast process converges weakly to a mean-zero Gaussian limit. The associated quadratic test statistic converges in distribution to a weighted sum of chi-squared random variables, with eigenvalues determined by the spatial covariance operator induced by the kernel and the dependence structure. This limiting form enables direct p-value computation without intensive resampling.

For practical implementation, we approximate the weighted chi-squared limit using a Satterthwaite-type moment-matching approach. The required eigenvalues are obtained from an exact discrete covariance operator evaluated on a finite grid, yielding a computationally tractable procedure suitable for routine agronomic datasets.

1.4
Scope and Contributions

The proposed framework targets comparison of yield-quality distributions across multiple fields or management zones and extends naturally to multivariate outcomes. By focusing on distributional equality rather than mean differences alone, the method complements existing geostatistical smoothing techniques that emphasize prediction or interpolation. Theoretical guarantees under spatial mixing conditions provide a rigorous foundation, while simulation studies demonstrate that the procedure maintains nominal size in settings where classical parametric and non-spatial rank-based tests exhibit severe size distortions.

Together, these elements form a coherent and practically implementable approach to distributional inference for spatially dependent agricultural data.

2
Methodology

2.1
Statistical Model and Notation

Let there be
K
fields indexed by
k=1,\dots,K
. For field
k
, observations are collected on a regular lattice
\mathcal{L}_{n,k}=\{s_{k1},\dots,s_{kn_{k}}\}\subset\mathcal{D}_{k}
with mesh
\Delta_{n}\rightarrow 0
. Thus
n_{k}\rightarrow\infty
and
\mathcal{D}_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is fixed and bounded.

The yield quality observed at location
s_{kj}
is
X_{k,s_{kj}}
. Within each field,
\{X_{k,s}:s\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\}
is assumed strictly stationary and isotropic with marginal CDF
F_{k}
.

The hypothesis of interest is:

H_{0}:F_{1}(x)=F_{2}(x)=\dots=F_{K}(x)=F(x)\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}

Spatial dependence.
For each field
k
, let
\alpha_{k}(r)
be the
\alpha
-mixing coefficient. We assume polynomial decay:

Assumption 1

(
\alpha
-mixing decay)
.

There exist constants
C>0
,
\theta>4
such that
\alpha_{k}(r)\leq Cr^{-\theta}
for all
r\geq 0
and all
k
.

This rate implies absolute integrability of the relevant covariance functions via Davydov’s inequality, ensuring central limit behavior for local (kernel-weighted) averages.

2.2
Kernel-Smoothed Empirical Distribution Function

Fix an interior reference location
s_{0}
. Let
K:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be bounded, symmetric, Lipschitz, compactly supported, and satisfy
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K(u)du=1
. For a fixed bandwidth
h>0
, define
K_{h}(u)=h^{-2}K(u/h)
and the smoothed EDF:

\hat{F}_{k,h}(x;s_{0})=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}K_{h}(s_{kj}-s_{0})\mathbf{1}\{X_{k,s_{kj}}\leq x\}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}K_{h}(s_{kj}-s_{0})}

Because the design is deterministic, the effective local sample size is determined by the normalized weights
W_{j}=K_{h}(s_{kj}-s_{0})/\sum K_{h}(s_{kj}-s_{0})
, giving an exact finite-sample effective size of:

m_{n,k}:=\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}W_{j}^{2}}

Under a regular lattice and regular kernel,
m_{n,k}\asymp h^{2}/\Delta_{n}^{2}
, so this scaling aligns with the infill asymptotic regime. Accordingly, we work with the normalized empirical process:

\tilde{\mathbb{G}}_{n,k}(x):=\sqrt{m_{n,k}}(\hat{F}_{k,h}(x;s_{0})-F(x))

2.3
Contrast Processes and Test Statistic

Let
m_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}m_{n,k}
. Define the pooled smoothed EDF using effective sample size weights:

\hat{F}_{h}(x;s_{0}):=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{m_{n,k}}{m_{n}}\hat{F}_{k,h}(x;s_{0})

The natural scaling for differences is
\sqrt{m_{n}}
. Define the contrast processes:

\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{n,k}(x):=\sqrt{m_{n}}(\hat{F}_{k,h}(x;s_{0})-\hat{F}_{h}(x;s_{0}))

We use an
L_{2}(F)
-type quadratic form test statistic, evaluated over
M
discrete thresholds. In practice, this is computed as a weighted Riemann sum over intervals
\Delta\hat{F}_{h}(x_{i})
. To connect with the discrete implementation, we define a discretized inner product on the grid
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{M}\}
with weights
w_{i}
approximating
dF
(e.g.,
w_{i}=\Delta\hat{F}_{h}(x_{i})
). Then

T_{n}:=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i=1}^{M}[\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{n,k}(x_{i})]^{2}w_{i}.

This corresponds to the squared norm of the
K
-variate contrast vector in the discretized space, with the factor
1/K
ensuring an average over fields. The asymptotic eigenvalues
\lambda_{m}
are then scaled appropriately when matching the Satterthwaite approximation to the discretized statistic.

2.4
Practical Inference via the Satterthwaite Approximation

Because
\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_{n,k}
is a linear transformation of the fieldwise processes, its limiting covariance operator is obtained by applying the same linear transformation at the Gaussian-process limit level.
T_{n}
converges to a weighted sum of chi-squared variables with
K-1
degrees of freedom for each eigen-component (reflecting the contrast subspace). Specifically,

T_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}\chi^{2}_{K-1,m},

where
\{\lambda_{m}\}
are the nonzero eigenvalues of the covariance operator restricted to the contrast subspace, and
\chi^{2}_{K-1,m}
are independent chi-square variables with
K-1
degrees of freedom.

We approximate this mixture by a single
a\chi^{2}_{\nu}
(Satterthwaite) by matching the first two moments using estimated eigenvalues:

a=\frac{\sum\lambda_{m}^{2}}{\sum\lambda_{m}},\quad\nu=\frac{(\sum\lambda_{m})^{2}}{\sum\lambda_{m}^{2}}.

2.5
Multivariate Extension

The framework naturally extends to comparing joint probability distributions of
p
distinct agricultural characteristics (e.g., simultaneously analyzing protein content and test weight). For a
p
-dimensional quality vector
\mathbf{X}_{k,s}=(X^{(1)},X^{(2)},\dots,X^{(p)})
, the smoothed EDF evaluates the joint indicator function
\mathbf{1}\{\mathbf{X}_{k,s_{kj}}\leq\mathbf{x}\}
over a multivariate grid
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

Under a Gaussian copula model for the dependence structure, the covariance between indicators at two spatial locations
\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{t}
involves a
4p
-dimensional multivariate normal probability (because we consider both variables at both sites). For
p=2
this reduces to a 4-dimensional calculation, which our code implements. The test statistic averages over an
M^{p}
grid, and the same weighted chi-square asymptotics hold.

3
Mathematical Framework and Asymptotic Theory

For the theoretical development we specialize to
d=2
dimensions throughout. All integrals are over
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Lemma 1

(Asymptotic covariance under fixed-bandwidth infill)
.

Assume
H_{0}
so that
F_{k}\equiv F
and assume the field
\{X_{k,s}\}
is strictly stationary and
\alpha
-mixing with
\alpha(r)\leq Cr^{-\theta}
for some
\theta>2(2+\delta)/\delta
and some
\delta>0
. For fixed
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(\tilde{\mathbb{G}}_{n,k}(x),\tilde{\mathbb{G}}_{n,k}(y)\bigr)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\gamma_{k}(t;x,y)\psi_{h}(t)dt

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}(t;x,y)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(\mathbf{1}\{X_{k,0}\leq x\},\mathbf{1}\{X_{k,t}\leq y\}\bigr),

\displaystyle\psi_{h}(t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K_{h}(u)K_{h}(u-t)du.

The integral converges absolutely.

Theorem 1

(Weak convergence of
\tilde{\mathbb{G}}_{n,k}
)
.

Under Assumption
1
,
\tilde{\mathbb{G}}_{n,k}\Rightarrow\mathbb{G}_{k}
in
\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, where
\mathbb{G}_{k}
is a mean-zero Gaussian process with covariance
\Gamma_{k}(x,y)
given by Lemma
1
. If fields are observed on independent domains, then
(\mathbb{G}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{G}_{K})
are independent.

Theorem 2

(Asymptotic null distribution)
.

Under
H_{0}
and Assumption
1
, the test statistic converges in distribution to a weighted sum of independent chi-square random variables with
K-1
degrees of freedom:

T_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}\chi_{K-1,m}^{2},

where
\{\lambda_{m}\}
are the nonzero eigenvalues of the covariance operator restricted to the
(K-1)
-dimensional contrast subspace.

5
Conclusion

This study validates a non-parametric spatial CvM testing framework for agricultural yield data. By combining kernel-smoothed EDFs with an exact discrete evaluation of the
\alpha
-mixing spatial covariance, we offer a rigorously calibrated alternative to standard ANOVA and MANOVA tests, completely neutralizing the severe Type I error inflation inherently caused by spatial autocorrelation.

Appendix A
Proof of Tightness (Detailed)

We prove tightness of
\{\tilde{\mathbb{G}}_{n,k}\}
in
\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
under the fixed-bandwidth infill design.

From the denominator expansion and the definition of
\hat{F}_{k,h}
we obtain the linear form:

\tilde{\mathbb{G}}_{n,k}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}a_{n,j}\xi_{kj}(x)+r_{n}(x),\quad\xi_{kj}(x):=\mathbf{1}\{X_{k,s_{kj}}\leq x\}-F(x)

where the deterministic weights are
a_{n,j}:=\sqrt{m_{n,k}}W_{j}
.

For
x<y
, define the centered increment variable
\eta_{kj}(x,y):=\mathbf{1}\{X_{k,s_{kj}}\in(x,y]\}-(F(y)-F(x))
. Assume
F
is Lipschitz. By Davydov’s inequality, for
\delta>0
:

|\operatorname{Cov}(\eta_{kj}(x,y),\eta_{kl}(x,y))|\leq C\alpha(\|s_{kj}-s_{kl}\|)^{\delta/(2+\delta)}|y-x|^{2/(2+\delta)}

Therefore, the second moment of the increment is bounded by:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}a_{n,j}\eta_{kj}(x,y)\right)^{2}\right]\leq C|y-x|^{2/(2+\delta)}\sum_{j,l=1}^{n_{k}}|a_{n,j}a_{n,l}|\alpha(\|s_{kj}-s_{kl}\|)^{\delta/(2+\delta)}

Because
K_{h}
is bounded and compactly supported, and utilizing the spatial mixing rate, the double sum over the lattice is uniformly bounded. There exists
\gamma:=2/(2+\delta)>0
such that
\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{\mathbb{G}}_{n,k}(y)-\tilde{\mathbb{G}}_{n,k}(x))^{2}]\leq C|y-x|^{\gamma}+o(1)
. The increment moment bound, together with finite-dimensional convergence, implies tightness of
\{\tilde{\mathbb{G}}_{n,k}\}
in
\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
by a standard moment criterion for tightness.
