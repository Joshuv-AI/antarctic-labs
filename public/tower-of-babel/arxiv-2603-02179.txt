Link between bipartite and general unicellular toroidal maps via slit–slide–sew bijections
Jérémie Bettinelli, Dimitri Korkotashvili

Abstract
We relate general maps to bipartite maps through a bijection of type slit–slide–sew. We provide an involution on arbitrary genus maps with even degree faces. This allow a full interpretation of the relation between general and bipartite maps, in the case of genus 1 1 maps with a unique face. The main tool is the use of rotations along well-chosen specific loops. Once a noncontractible simple loop is given, one slit along it, slide one notch, and sew back. This mildly modifies the structure of the map along the loop, changing the parity of the length of other loops crossing it. In the case of unicellular toroidal maps, the simple structure of noncontractible loops makes it possible to fully relate general maps to bipartite maps.

Introduction

In the last decade, so-called
slit-slide-sew bijections
have been introduced in
[
Bet14
]
in order to explain combinatorial identities on forests and on plane quadrangulations with a boundary. They were then developed to broader of similar contexts: plane bipartite and quasibapartite maps
[

]
, quadratic formulas for plane maps derived from the KP hierarchy
[
Lou19
]
, plane tight maps
[

[

]

[

[

]
.

These bijections interpret combinatorial identities on several classes of (possibly decorated) maps, which may usually be derived from known counting results. They however provide alternate proofs to those formulas and, usually, allow to recover them from simple to obtain initial conditions.

Planarity plays a crucial role in all the aforementioned works. In the present paper, we consider maps of positive genus, mainly toroidal maps. We will also restrict our attention in due course to unicellular maps, that is, maps with a unique face. Although the structure of these maps is simpler, they are, from a combinatorial point of view, quite rich, and have attracted a lot of attention in the past
[

Main involution on general maps with a distinguished loop

At this stage, we work with general maps of any positive genus
g
. We denote by
\mathcal{M}^{g}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\hskip-0.6pt=\bigcup_{n}\prescript{2\mathbb{N}\!}{n}{\mathcal{M}}^{g}
the set of maps of genus
g
with even face degrees. For a tuple
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})
of positive even integers, we denote by
\mathcal{M}^{g}(\bm{a})
the set of maps of genus
g
and type
\bm{a}
, that is, whose faces are labeled
f_{1}
, …,
f_{r}
and such that
(\deg(f_{1}),\dots,\deg(f_{r}))=\bm{a}
.

2.1
Preliminary definitions

Paths and loops.

A
path
is a finite sequence
\bm{\wp}=(e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{k})
of half-edges such that
e_{i}^{+}=e_{i+1}^{-}
, for
1\leq i\leq k-1
. Its
length
is the integer
k
, which we denote by
[\bm{\wp}]\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\hskip-0.6pt=k
. The
reverse
of
\bm{\wp}
is the path
\bar{\bm{\wp}}=(\bar{e}_{k},\dots,\bar{e}_{2},\bar{e}_{1})
, where
\bar{e}_{i}
is the reverse of
e_{i}
, that is, the half-edge corresponding to the same edge, with reverse orientation.

We say that
\bm{\wp}

links
the vertices
e_{1}^{-}
to
e_{k}^{+}
. A path is called
simple
if the vertices it visits are all distinct. If the paths
\bm{\wp}_{1}=(e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{k})
and
\bm{\wp}_{2}=(e_{k+1},e_{k+2},\dots,e_{m})
satisfy
e_{k}^{+}=e_{k+1}^{-}
, we denote their
concatenation
by
\bm{\wp}_{1}\bullet\bm{\wp}_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\hskip-0.6pt=(e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{m})
.

A
loop
is a path
\bm{\gamma}=(e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{k})
linking some vertex to itself, that is, such that
e_{1}^{-}=e_{k}^{+}
. The loop
\bm{\gamma}=(e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{k})
is
simple
if the path
(e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{k-1})
is simple; in other words, all the vertices it visits are visited exactly once, except the origin and end, which is visited exactly twice.

Through the embedding of the graph in the definition of a map, any half-edge
e
is given with a continuous mapping
s\in[0,1]\mapsto e(s)
with
e(0)=e^{-}
and
e(1)=e^{+}
. A loop
\bm{\gamma}=(e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{k})
is
contractible
(resp.
noncontractible
), if the curve
t\in(0,k]\mapsto e_{\lceil t\rceil}(t+1-\lceil t\rceil)
associated with it is homotopic (resp. nonhomotopic) to a single point.

Slit-slide-sew operation.

The techniques of so-called
slit-slide-sew
have been used – exclusively in the planar setting – in order to mildly transform maps
[
Bet14
,
Lou19
,
Bet20
,
BGM24
,
Sch25
]
, or extensions thereof
[
BK26
,
BFL24
]
, by sliding along a well-chosen path.

The general idea is as follows. Let us assume that we are given a simple path
\bm{\wp}=(e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{k})
in some map
\mathbf{m}
. We may slit the surface into which the map is embedded along this path. In the map
\mathbf{m}
, this doubles the path
\bm{\wp}
, making up two copies: one to its left,
\bm{\wp}^{\ell}=(e^{\ell}_{1},e^{\ell}_{2},\dots,e^{\ell}_{k})
, and one to its right,
\bm{\wp}^{r}=(e^{r}_{1},e^{r}_{2},\dots,e^{r}_{k})
. The way each extremity of the path is treated has to be made precise: we may keep
\bm{\wp}^{\ell}
and
\bm{\wp}^{r}
connected at the extremal vertex, or disconnect them by sliding inside some incident corner.

We then sew back
\bm{\wp}^{\ell}
onto
\bm{\wp}^{r}
but only after sliding by one unit, in the sense that we match
e^{\ell}_{i}
with
e^{r}_{i\pm 1}
. For further reference, we denote by
e^{\ell}_{i}\,{\Join}\,e^{r}_{i\pm 1}
the edge resulting of this gluing. Again, what to do exactly at the extremities of the paths has to be set. This toy procedure possibly affects only up to two faces, those into which the extremities of the paths
\bm{\wp}^{\ell}
and
\bm{\wp}^{r}
have been possibly disconnected. The rest of the embedding, as well as the genus, remains unchanged.

Conceptually, this operation defines a function that maps the original map to a new map, whose parameters have been mildly changed. To promote this construction to a
bijection
, the construction has to be revertible: from the transformed map, one should be able to recover the image of the sliding path
\bm{\wp}
in order to slide backwards. This necessitates the introduction of a canonical notion of sliding path, which can be tracked down in the resulting map.

2.2
Involution

We present here the involution at the heart of our processes. It works on general maps, possibly with prescribed face degrees, but who carry a distinguished noncontractible simple loop. It can be thought of as an
R or R’ move
on a genus
g
equivalent of a
Rubik’s cube
. Basically, we will rotate, in one direction or another, by one notch along the distinguished loop. See Figure
2
.

Let
\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{M}^{g}
, let
\bm{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{k})
be a noncontractible simple loop in
\mathbf{m}
, and let
\varepsilon\in\{+,-\}
.

1. Slit

W
e cut the surface along
\bm{\gamma}
and obtain a surface with two boundary components, one carrying the left copy
\bm{\gamma}^{\ell}=(\gamma^{\ell}_{1},\dots,\gamma^{\ell}_{k})
of
\bm{\gamma}
, and one carrying its right copy
\bm{\gamma}^{r}=(\gamma^{r}_{1},\dots,\gamma^{r}_{k})
.

2. Slide and Sew

W
e sew back
\bm{\gamma}^{\ell}
onto
\bm{\gamma}^{r}
after sliding one notch: for
1\leq i\leq k
, we identify
\gamma^{r}_{i}
with
\gamma^{\ell}_{i\pm 1}
, where the sign
\pm
is given by
\varepsilon
, and
i\pm 1
is understood modulo
k
.

3. Output

Let
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}
be the resulting map, let
\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}=(\gamma^{r}_{1}\,{\Join}\,\gamma^{\ell}_{1\pm 1},\dots,\gamma^{r}_{k}\,{\Join}\,\gamma^{\ell}_{k\pm 1})
be the image of
\bm{\gamma}
in
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}
, and let
\tilde{\varepsilon}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\hskip-0.6pt=-
if
\varepsilon=+
or
\tilde{\varepsilon}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\hskip-0.6pt=+
if
\varepsilon=-
. The output of the construction is
\Phi(\mathbf{m},\bm{\gamma},\varepsilon)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\hskip-0.6pt=(\tilde{\mathbf{m}},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}},\tilde{\varepsilon})
.

Figure 2:
Main involution. The origin of the loops
\bm{\lambda}
and
\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}
are indicated with purple vertices. When

, we “screw” the map one notch along the distinguished loop. We “unscrew” one notch along the loop if
\varepsilon=-
.

If, furthermore, the faces of
\mathbf{m}
are labeled, those of
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}
naturally inherit the labeling from that of
\mathbf{m}
. In what follows, applying
\Phi
to
\mathbf{m}
and
\bm{\gamma}
might be referred to as
rotating

\mathbf{m}
along
\bm{\gamma}
, and the loop
\bm{\gamma}
will be called the
sliding loop
.

Observe that the action of the
k
-th iterate
\Phi^{k}
is trivial on loops of length
k
since its merely consists in a
Dehn twist
for these maps:
\Phi^{[\bm{\gamma}]}(\mathbf{m},\bm{\gamma},\varepsilon)=(\mathbf{m},\bm{\gamma},\pm)
. In particular,
\Phi
bears no effect on maps whose distinguished loop has length
1
.

Proposition 2
.

The mapping
\Phi
is an involution on the set of triples consisting of a map in
\mathcal{M}^{g}
, a distinguished noncontractible simple loop, and a sign in
\{+,-\}
.

It preserves the number of edges of the map, as well as its type when the faces are labeled.

Proof.

During the
Slit
step, the map
\mathbf{m}
gains
k
edges,
2
faces and sees its genus decrease by
1
. In the
Slide and Sew
step, it then loses
k
edges,
2
faces and sees its genus increase by
1
. So the resulting map
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathcal{M}^{g}
and
|E(\tilde{\mathbf{m}})|=|E(\mathbf{m})|
. Furthermore, the faces are not affected by the process so the type is preserved when the faces are labeled.

The underlying surface and the embedding of
\bm{\gamma}
are also preserved by
\Phi
. So
\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}
is a noncontractible simple loop of
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}
. Let us assume, without loss of generality, that
\varepsilon=+
. In the application of
\Phi
to
(\tilde{\mathbf{m}},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}},\tilde{\varepsilon})
, the loop
\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}
is slit into its left copy
\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{\ell}=(\tilde{\gamma}^{\ell}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\gamma}^{\ell}_{k})
and its right copy
\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{r}=(\tilde{\gamma}^{r}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\gamma}^{r}_{k})
, where

\tilde{\gamma}^{\ell}_{i}=\gamma^{\ell}_{i+1}
and
\tilde{\gamma}^{r}_{i}=\gamma^{r}_{i}
, and then sewn back by identifying
\tilde{\gamma}^{r}_{i}
with
\tilde{\gamma}^{\ell}_{i-1}
, that is,
\gamma^{r}_{i}
with
\gamma^{\ell}_{i}
. We thus recover
\mathbf{m}
, and the distinguished loop is then
(\gamma^{r}_{1}\,{\Join}\,\gamma^{\ell}_{1},\dots,\gamma^{r}_{k}\,{\Join}\,\gamma^{\ell}_{k})=\bm{\gamma}
. Finally, the third coordinate is the opposite of
\tilde{\varepsilon}
, that is,
\varepsilon
. As a result,
\Phi(\tilde{\mathbf{m}},\tilde{\bm{\gamma}},\tilde{\varepsilon})=(\mathbf{m},\bm{\gamma},\varepsilon)
, as desired.
∎

does not affect the sliding loop itself but creates an “offset” for all the paths that traverse it, as is illustrated on Figure

\mathbf{p}\bullet\tilde{\bm{\wp}}\bullet\mathbf{p}^{\prime}

is as follows.

\diamond

If

We may similarly canonically define the image of paths that cross
\bm{\gamma}
from left to right or use half-edges of

Remark 1
.

Beware that the way we defined here the image of a path crossing the sliding loop is not the only possibility. We could indeed use other parts of the sliding loop for example. Furthermore, the image of a simple path may no longer be simple. If we rotate along a length
1

2.3
Rightmost loop

In order to interpret the desired equations (
1.2
) and (
1.3
), we will apply the involution
\Phi
to the desired maps, while canonically defining the distinguished loop along which to rotate. This has to be done in such a way that the image of the chosen loop is recoverable in the resulting map, in order to invert the process.

In this subsection, we will define a first loop and see that everything goes smoothly when rotating along it, even for general maps of any positive genus

Rightmost noncontractible simple loop.

L

\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{M}^{g}
be a map. We consider the set
\mathcal{S}
of all paths of the form

\mathbf{p}

\bm{\gamma}
is a noncontractible simple loop that intersects

\bm{\wp}
of

, which might be constructed iteratively as follows.

\diamond

Starting from the root and turning counterclockwise around the root vertex, we select the first half-edge
\wp_{1}
that belongs to an element of
\mathcal{S}
.

\diamond

Assuming the beginning of the path

has been constructed:

–

, we stop and set the rightmost path as
\bm{\wp}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\hskip-0.6pt=(\wp_{1},\dots,\wp_{i})

–

otherwise, we let
\wp_{i+1}
be the first half-edge after
\wp_{i}
, turning counterclockwise around the vertex
\wp_{i}^{+}
, that is such that at least one element of
\mathcal{S}
has prefix

Finally, the

is defined as the noncontractible simple loop
\bm{\gamma}
of the decomposition

Rotating along
\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{m}}

Let us see what happens when we apply the involution
\Phi
to the rightmost loop
\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{m}}

Theorem 3
.

Let
g\geq 1
be fixed. Let us define
\Psi^{\varepsilon}\colon\mathcal{M}^{g}\to\mathcal{M}^{g}
by letting
\Psi^{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{m})
be the first coordinate of
\Phi(\mathbf{m},\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{m}},\varepsilon)
. Then
\Psi^{+}
and
\Psi^{-}
are inverse bijections. Furthermore,

(
i
)

they preserve the number of edges of the map;

(
ii
)

they preserve the type of the map when the faces are labeled.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{M}^{g}
and
(\tilde{\mathbf{m}},\tilde{\bm{\lambda}},\tilde{\varepsilon})=\Phi(\mathbf{m},\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{m}},\varepsilon)
. Since
\Phi
is an involution preserving number of edges and types (by Proposition
2
), it suffices to show that the rightmost loop of
\mathbf{m}
is mapped to the rightmost loop of
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}
, that is,
\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}=\bm{\lambda}_{\tilde{\mathbf{m}}}
.

Recalling the construction from the
previous paragraph
, let
\mathcal{S}
be the set defined there for the map
\mathbf{m}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
be the equivalent set defined for
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}
. In
\mathbf{m}
, let also
\mathbf{p}
be the path appearing there, so that
\mathbf{p}\bullet\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{m}}
is the rightmost path of
\mathcal{S}
. Since
\mathbf{p}
only intersects
\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{m}}
at its end and reaches it from the right, it is mapped through
\Phi
in
\tilde{\mathbf{m}}
into a simple path
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}
starting from the root vertex, ending at the origin of
\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}
, without intersecting with it otherwise. As a result,
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}\bullet\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
.

In order to conclude, it remains to see that it is the rightmost path of
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
. Arguing by contradiction, let us assume otherwise and let
\tilde{\bm{\wp}}\neq\tilde{\mathbf{p}}\bullet\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}
be the rightmost element of
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
. Then
\tilde{\bm{\wp}}
starts with a (possibly null) number of half-edges from

\tilde{\mathbf{p}}\bullet\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}

\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}
afterward, then this path is completely preserved, when applying the reverse mapping

3
Classification of unicellular toroidal maps

From now on, we restrict our attention to unicellular toroidal maps. In order to derive (
1.2
), we will need to rotate along other loops. The very rough idea is that we can rotate along two loops, changing the parity of the one along which we
do not
rotate. Up to a side factor (the planar term), there are thus 4 ways to go from bipartite to nonbipartite. We will see that it is actually a bit more complicated.

The simpler structure of unicellular toroidal maps will allow us to properly define loops in such a way that rotations along them will be tractable. However, in contrast with the first loop
\bm{\lambda}_{\mathbf{m}}
, the other loops might not be preserved through the involution. As a result, we have to classify more thoroughly the maps.

1

2

3

2
