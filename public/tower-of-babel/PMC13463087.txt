A CFD assessment of Weber number impacts on dropwise condensation heat transfer for an individual droplet
Authors: Loghman Mohammadpour, Hesam Moghadasi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Due to the high importance of Dropwise Condensation (DWC) in industry fields, it has attracted significant attention to itself during the last decades. Numerous researchers have been doing much effort to study the ways that may contribute to an amelioration in heat transfer rate in DWC. In this assessment, a computational fluid dynamics (CFD) analysis executed to assess the influence of the Weber number (We) on the heat transfer between a liquid droplet and a flat solid surface. In this regard, to simulate the droplet’s morphology, Surface Evolver (SE) was employed, and governing equations were processed through the use of the finite volume method (FVM) as well. Additionally, ANSYS FLUENT, a CFD solver, was utilized to calculate the average heat flux for diverse We numbers, incorporating the influence of critical parameters such as saturation temperature, droplet size, and fluid properties. The results were also validated against available data and it showed acceptable agreement. The findings of this study demonstrate that increasing the We number from 0.1 to 0.4 for a water droplet with a volume of 2 μ l and a contact angle of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$90^\circ$$\end{document} leads to a 38.43% enhancement in average heat flux, indicating a significant improvement in thermal performance.

Introduction

Condensation is defined as the process in which vapor transforms into liquid on a solid surface when the surface temperature is lower than that of the adjacent vapor1–4. The significance of condensation is evident across numerous industrial applications, including power generation5,6, chemical processing7, refrigeration8, and water harvesting9,10. Surface condensation is generally categorized into two types: dropwise condensation (DWC) and filmwise condensation (FWC)11. In FWC, vapor condenses into a continuous liquid film, whereas in DWC, vapor condenses into discrete liquid droplets12. The heat transfer rate in DWC is considerably higher than in FWC13–15 which explains the greater research attention devoted to DWC. Moreover, DWC is more likely to occur on surfaces with reduced surface energy16–19.

Various parameters including contact angle, droplet volume, liquid type, and surface material play critical roles in determining heat transfer during DWC20–24. Abbatecola et al.25 analyzed the thermal behavior of DWC with steam at sub-atmospheric saturation pressures, highlighting the influence of contact angle. They observed that increasing the contact angle reduces the amount of heat transferred. Mohammadpour et al.26 numerically investigated the effect of droplet volume on heat transfer for droplets on tilted surfaces. Their results showed that for larger droplets (>12 μ l), increasing the inclination angle enhances heat transfer, whereas for smaller droplets (<4 μ l), this effect is negligible.

Another important aspect requiring further exploration is the role of dimensionless numbers in DWC. Baghel et al.27 numerically studied the impact of Marangoni convection on heat transfer rate for a liquid droplet underneath a surface. They scrutinized the impacts of Reynolds and Prandtl numbers (Re and Pr) concluding that higher Re number values increase average heat flux, while lower Pr number values yield greater heat flux magnitudes. Regarding the Nusselt number (Nu) influences, based on Ref.28, increasing Nu number enhances the heat transfer coefficient, thereby amplifying heat transfer during DWC. The Weber number (We), is also significant, as it governs droplet shape and consequently affects overall heat transfer. Specifically, We determines whether a condensed droplet spreads across the surface or retains its initial shape29,30. The magnitude of the We number can be obtained from the Eq. (1):1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$We=\frac{\rho {u}^{2}L}{\sigma }$$\end{document}

Equation (1), quantifies the relationship between inertial and surface tension forces where ρ, is the liquid droplet density, u stands for the flow velocity inside the droplet, L signifies the droplet height, and σ denotes the droplet surface tension.

Khandekar et al.31 examined droplet dynamics with consideration of the We number. They demonstrated that as the We number increases, the liquid droplet spreads more extensively on the surface due to the corresponding rise in droplet kinetic energy. Chakraborty et al.32, employed a machine learning-based approach to evaluate droplet size distributions produced during various fragmentation processes across different We numbers. Their findings indicated that when airstreams are vertical, droplet fragmentation occurs at lower We numbers compared to horizontal airstreams. Different reports33,34 have investigated droplet spreading behavior on wettable surfaces for We numbers approaching zero. They concluded that for We < 0.5, the spreading effect can be safely neglected, as the droplet does not significantly spread on the surface within this range.

A review of the available literature reveals that most studies have focused on the spreading behavior of liquid droplets across different ranges of We numbers. However, the specific impact of the We number on heat transfer during DWC has not been comprehensively addressed. In the present research, a computational fluid dynamics (CFD) approach was developed to assess the influence of the We number on the heat transfer rate of individual droplets with varying volumes during DWC on a flat, horizontal surface. To simulate droplet geometry on the solid surface, the open-source software Surface Evolver (SE) was employed. Additionally, ANSYS FLUENT was utilized as the CFD solver to carry out the numerical analysis.

Physical model

Geometric characteristics

For the purpose of this study, an individual liquid droplet was numerically analyzed on a solid surface with a fixed contact angle of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$90^\circ$$\end{document}. As previously discussed, droplets typically interact with their neighboring droplets and create a liquid film over time. However, in this study the single droplet upon its creation while it hasn’t colloid with the other droplets has been investigated. This scenario is quite common in reality and hence the results can be utilized for diverse purposes. Various simulation methods have been proposed in the literature35,36; however, in the present research, Surface Evolver (SE), an open-source software, was employed in order to simulate the 3D shape of the droplet. SE36, is a freeware tool designed to model liquid and fluid interfaces shaped by surface tension and other energetic contributions. It represents surfaces as triangular meshes and applies gradient descent to evolve toward minimal energy configurations, incorporating effects such as surface tension, gravity, contact angles, volume constraints, curvature, and custom energy terms. To obtain the results of the numerical assessments, ANSYS FLUENT, a CFD software, was employed. Within this framework, the second-order upwind scheme was applied to discretize and solve the Navier–Stokes equations, ensuring improved accuracy in capturing flow gradients. In addition, the semi-implicit method for pressure-linked equations (SIMPLE) algorithm was utilized to couple the pressure and velocity fields, thereby maintaining numerical stability and consistency in the solution process.

Result and discussion

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the influence of low We numbers on the heat transfer rate during DWC process for an individual liquid droplet on a flat solid surface. While the Weber number has been extensively investigated, additional parameters including droplet volume, liquid type, and saturation temperature were also examined to determine their effects on the We number and, consequently, on the heat transfer rate. These factors play a critical role in defining the interfacial dynamics and thermal characteristics of the DWC process. Furthermore, understanding the combined effects of these variables provides valuable insights into optimizing condensation efficiency in practical applications.

In DWC processes, the We number influences two primary factors: the spreading of the liquid droplet on the solid surface and variations in kinetic energy. As previously noted, for We numbers below 0.5, the spreading impact can be considered negligible. Accordingly, this study focused on We numbers of 0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.4 to assess how the heat transfer rate varies when droplet spreading does not occur, albeit with relatively low We number values. The results clearly show that for all three liquids, increasing the We number leads to an augmentation in average heat flux. This behavior can be attributed to the generation of internal fluid flow within the droplet once the We number exceeds zero. As the We number increases, the velocity of this internal flow intensifies, thereby raising the droplet’s kinetic energy and enhancing heat transfer. Furthermore, water droplets exhibit higher heat release during DWC compared to ethanol and R134a droplets. This difference is explained by the higher latent heat of vaporization (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${h}_{lv}$$\end{document}) of water, which, according to Eq. The data in Fig. 6 also indicate that although average heat flux increases with rising We numbers, the rate of improvement diminishes at higher values. For instance, in Fig. 6 (A) at V=2 μ l, increasing the We number from 0.1 to 0.2 yields a 17.24% rise in average heat flux. The corresponding increases for We numbers from 0.2 to 0.3 and 0.3 to 0.4 are 10.2% and 7.15%, respectively. Overall, the total increase in average heat flux from We = 0.1 to We = 0.4 at V=2 μ l is 38.43%. A similar percentage increase is observed at V=32 μ l. Thereby, it can be concluded that while average heat flux yields higher for smaller droplets but the change caused by We number increase in average heat flux is independent of any particular volume. Under the same conditions, ethanol (Fig. 6 (B)) and R134a (Fig. 6 (C)) droplets exhibit total increases of 30.35% and 21.45%, respectively. These findings confirm that water not only produces a greater average heat flux compared to ethanol and R134a, but also demonstrates a more pronounced sensitivity of heat transfer rate to variations in We number. This can be traced back to the higher specific heat that water possess compared to the other two fluids.

Conclusions

In this work, a numerical investigation was conducted to evaluate the influence of the Weber number (We) on the heat transfer rate during the condensation of a liquid droplet on a flat solid surface. Several additional parameters containing saturation temperature, liquid type, and droplet volume were scrutinized to provide a more comprehensive understanding of their combined effects. To ensure accurate droplet shape representation, Surface Evolver was employed, while the computational results were obtained using ANSYS FLUENT, a commercial CFD software based on the finite volume method (FVM). The findings revealed that, at a constant contact angle and droplet volume, increasing the We number enhances the average heat flux. For instance, for a water droplet with V=2 μ l and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta =90^\circ$$\end{document}, increasing the We number from 0.1 to 0.4 results in a 38.43% rise in average heat flux. Additionally, although saturation temperature is directly correlated with average heat flux, the influence of the We number diminishes for larger droplets as saturation temperature increases. Moreover, a higher We number induces greater nonlinearity within the droplet, leading to symmetry disruption and an increase in local wall shear stress at the droplet’s base on the solid surface.
