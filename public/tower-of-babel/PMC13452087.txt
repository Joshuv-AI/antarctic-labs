Profiling Protein‐Peptide Interactions by Yeast Surface Display
Authors: Joseph D Hurley, Andrew C Kruse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein‐peptide interactions are central to many biological processes and form the basis for many techniques in basic research and therapeutic discovery. A common research challenge is to identify specific peptide ligands or binding motifs where no initial hit is known. Here we describe a protocol for discovering protein‐binding peptides using a very large, target‐agnostic yeast surface display library containing approximately 6.1 × 109 unique clones and providing broad coverage of short peptide sequence space. To facilitate its use, this library has been made commercially available at low cost. The described workflow entails thawing and expanding the library, validating target protein reagents, enriching target‐binding cells through iterative rounds of cell sorting, monitoring enrichment by analytical flow cytometry, and analyzing selected pools by next‐generation sequencing. We emphasize practical considerations that affect selection outcomes, including target labeling strategy, appropriate counterselections, and cell sorting parameters. A selection campaign using these protocols can be completed in a matter of weeks, and will typically result in the identification of thousands of candidate target‐binding peptide sequences. These protocols are broadly applicable to many diverse protein targets, provided that the target can be purified and labeled in a suitable format. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Protein‐peptide interactions underlie many biological processes, including antibody recognition, peptide hormone receptor signaling, and protein degradation, among many others. Peptides are also widely used as affinity tags, chemical probes, and therapeutics. Despite peptides’ importance in research and medicine, de novo discovery of peptide ligands remains challenging and resource intensive.

Common approaches for peptide discovery are typically limited by one of two problems: either the screen is focused around an initial hit and therefore requires prior knowledge of a peptide ligand and samples only a small region of sequence space, or the screen samples peptide space broadly but shallowly, yielding isolated hits without providing insight into the properties required for target binding. As examples of the first issue, focused mutational scans and structure‐guided libraries are useful when an initial binding sequence is already known, but they do not broadly sample sequence space (Pires et al., 2001; Randall et al., 2024; Skelton et al., 2003). Conversely, genetically encoded display technologies such as phage or mRNA display can achieve very high theoretical diversities, but in practice these libraries often have modest sizes and are incompatible with fluorescence‐activated cell sorting (FACS)‐based selection strategies (Cortese et al., 1996; Szardenings, 2003; Yang et al., 2008). As a result, there remains a need for practical peptide discovery platforms that combine broad sequence coverage with flexible selection methods.

The methods described here utilize a very high‐diversity (∼6 × 109 unique clones) yeast surface display library in which peptides are presented as C‐terminal fusions of the Aga2 protein on the surface of Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Hurley et al., 2025). Library diversity is introduced via eight consecutive degenerate NNK codons, resulting in predominantly eight‐residue peptides, while also sampling shorter peptides through the introduction of stop codons. This design provides broad coverage of short‐peptide sequence space (∼23% of all possible peptides of eight amino acids and fewer) without requiring impractically large culture volumes (Fig. 1). Because of its high diversity, this library enables the de novo discovery of peptide ligands without requiring any prior knowledge. Additionally, by covering such a large proportion of sequence space, the library provides insights into motifs involved in binding interactions and can allow identification of which residues are essential versus those that are modifiable.

Because our library was designed to be fully randomized and contain a very high diversity of peptides, it is suitable for screening diverse protein targets. We have previously demonstrated its utility in profiling antibody‐epitope interactions and E3 ubiquitin ligase degron specificity. In each of these cases, we recovered expected sequence motifs and defined tolerated substitutions and truncations of these targets’ best‐studied peptide ligands. We have also demonstrated the library's compatibility with more challenging targets, such as membrane proteins in detergent micelles, by discovering novel ligands against the bacterial enzyme RodA‐PBP2. We anticipate that our library and selection strategy will be compatible with a wide array of additional targets, as we have observed with other yeast surface display libraries (McMahon et al., 2018).

This library and the associated methods provide several advantages over other approaches to peptide discovery. Chiefly, the library's large scale enables the screening of a large proportion of all possible short linear peptides in a single experiment, while its unbiased design allows it to be used across diverse protein targets. Additionally, the yeast‐based selection protocol presented here can be performed in a matter of weeks, at low cost, and using common laboratory equipment (Boder & Wittrup, 1997, 1998; Chao et al., 2006; Lopez‐Morales et al., 2023). Lastly, it is possible to screen for properties beyond affinity (e.g., binding to an off‐target; stickiness) using multi‐color FACS sorting strategies (Harvey et al., 2022). The primary limitation of this approach is that certain types of peptides are excluded from the library design: for example, longer peptides that require secondary structure, peptides incorporating noncanonical amino acids, and peptides requiring a free N‐terminus for binding.

The following detailed, step‐by‐step protocols are intended to facilitate high‐throughput peptide discovery campaigns using our previously described yeast surface display library. The Basic Protocol describes the general selection workflow using multiple iterative rounds of magnetic‐activated cell sorting (MACS) and FACS (Bonner et al., 1972; Miltenyi et al., 1990). Support Protocol 1 describes how to thaw, expand, and cryopreserve working aliquots of the peptide library, and how to cryopreserve selected pools from later rounds. Support Protocol 2 describes how to prepare and validate target protein reagents before beginning a campaign, with an emphasis on labeling via chemical biotinylation. Support Protocol 3 describes how to perform analytical flow cytometry on the naïve library, post‐selection pools, or individual clones to evaluate target protein binding. Support Protocol 4 describes how to extract plasmid DNA from yeast pools or individual clones and how to prepare next‐generation sequencing (NGS) libraries for sequencing.

STRATEGIC PLANNING

Before starting a selection, consideration should be given to the preparation and labeling of the target protein. A typical selection campaign will require ∼20 nmol of high‐quality, pure, suitably labeled protein. Depending on the objectives of the selection, it may also be necessary to prepare various negative controls or anti‐targets that you wish to counterscreen against. For example, it is typically desirable to counterscreen against any secondary reagents, and it may also be advantageous to counterscreen against mutants of the desired binding site or a similar protein to ensure target specificity. We have achieved the most reliable results by labeling the target protein with biotin and using streptavidin or anti‐biotin antibodies as secondary reagents. Biotinylation can be achieved chemically (via coupling with biotin‐NHS or biotin‐maleimide reagents) or enzymatically (via labeling of an AviTag with the biotin ligase BirA). This approach also permits the use of a single batch of target protein but multiple different secondary reagents to reduce the likelihood of enriching for direct secondary reagent binders. As alternatives to biotinylation, target can be labeled directly with a fluorophore (e.g., via coupling with fluorophore‐NHS ester) or through the use of fluorescent antibodies that bind an epitope tag (e.g., FLAG or ALFA tags). Note that the latter approach presents a high risk of selecting direct secondary reagent binders, and careful counterselections must be performed to avoid this.

Next, the protein buffer and yeast selection buffers should be chosen. Most common protein buffers are compatible with yeast sorting, with the exception of those containing reducing agents. Because two disulfide bonds stabilize the Aga1‐Aga2 protein complex that tethers the peptides to the yeast cell surface, the presence of reducing agents will result in loss of the Aga2‐peptide fusion protein from the yeast cells. Typically, the yeast selection buffer is composed of the protein storage buffer supplemented with 0.1% bovine serum albumin to reduce nonspecific binding and 5 mM maltose to prevent cell adhesion and flocculation. When using less common buffer components, it can be useful to perform a simple flow cytometry experiment with a known interacting peptide‐target pair (e.g., Rho1D4) to ensure that the component(s) are compatible with yeast surface display selections.

The next consideration is how deeply to initially sample the library. For general ligand‐discovery campaigns, sampling 1010 cells is a useful default. If the expected recognition element is very short (up to perhaps six amino acids) and therefore highly represented, a smaller starting population can be adequate. In such cases, 107 cells may be a useful starting point, as this sampling depth permits skipping MACS altogether and proceeding directly to FACS sorting.

This raises a more general consideration, which is when to use MACS versus FACS sorting. MACS is most effective in early rounds, when the population is far too large to sort efficiently by FACS and the library must be depleted of nonbinders. MACS can also be effective later in selections as a negative selection step—the LD columns in particular are very effective for removal of secondary reagent binders. However, it is generally preferable to switch to FACS as soon as the library size is small enough to make this practical (i.e., to sort ∼10 × the number of cells collected in the previous MACS round). This will often be when the previous‐round library size is 106‐107 cells. FACS allows much more transparency into the sorting process and enables sorting on the basis of target binding relative to peptide expression, rather than simply overall target binding. FACS can also allow much more complex Boolean sorting strategies, for example simultaneous binding to multiple reagents, or binding one reagent and not another. In our laboratory, most campaigns comprise one MACS round followed by two or more FACS rounds.

A closely related consideration is choosing the appropriate selection stringency for each round. If the stringency is too low, the selection campaign may require more rounds than necessary, wasting time and experimental resources. Unnecessary low‐stringency selection rounds also allow nonbinding or weakly binding clones to persist, increasing the potential for genetic drift and amplification biases to distort library composition, especially during outgrowth steps when the library is not under intentional selection pressure. If the stringency is too high, desirable clones may be eliminated by chance. The primary tool for adjusting selection pressure is target protein concentration, and this should be adjusted empirically after every round. Early rounds will typically use a high concentration—1 µM is often a good starting point—and the concentration will decrease in later rounds. As a guideline, we recommend choosing a next‐round target concentration such that 10‐20% of previous‐round cells bind by analytical flow cytometry. This range reflects empirical experience across many selection campaigns in our laboratory, and is intended as a practical guideline rather than a strict rule. In early rounds, this level of binding may not be achievable, in which case sorting at the initial high concentration is recommended until nonbinders have been sufficiently depleted.

The second tool for adjusting selection pressure is the drawing of FACS sorting gates. The first FACS round should usually use a permissive gate that captures most peptide‐expressing cells with binding above background (i.e., unstained cells), whereas later rounds should use more stringent gates that prioritize high binding relative to expression (Fig. 2). A useful rule of thumb for later‐round gate drawing is to draw a gate off the expression vs. binding diagonal (i.e., to capture clones with a high level of binding with respect to expression) such that 0.2‐2% of cells fall within the gate. Again, this range is based on empirical experience from many selection campaigns in our laboratory, and is provided as an approximate guideline rather than a strict range.

A final consideration is deciding what a successful outcome would look like given your objectives. This determines how many selection rounds to perform and when to start screening individual clones. If the goal is simply to obtain a single peptide that binds at or above a pre‐specified affinity, it may be appropriate to start screening individual clones when >20% of the previous‐round population binds at this concentration. By screening 24 colonies from such a population, you would have a ∼99.5% chance of obtaining such a clone. However, if your goal is to obtain many such peptides, or profile the entire ensemble of peptides binding at a given concentration, it can be more suitable to continue selections until 70‐90% of clones exhibit binding. Alternatively, achieving your objective may require a clone with multiple properties, such as binding two different targets. In each of these cases, deciding when to end the campaign is a matter of judging what proportion of the selected library exhibits the desired properties, and therefore whether you are likely to sample such clones from individual colony screening.

When planning yeast display experiments, it is useful to keep in mind the timelines of yeast culture (see Time Considerations). Typically, an experiment will begin with thawing a frozen aliquot of yeast and growing the culture for 24‐48 hr in glucose medium. Then, the culture will be induced by transferring it to galactose medium and growing it for 24‐72 hr (peak expression is usually seen at 48 hr). After sorting, cultures are recovered until medium is saturated (24 hr for MACS; 48 hr for FACS). At this point cultures may be expanded to a larger volume for freezing (24‐48 hr). Cultures will typically be frozen as DMSO stocks, and can be induced in parallel if you are proceeding directly to the next round.

COMMENTARY

Critical Parameters

Target protein preparation is often the most important determinant of selection success because impure, aggregated, or poorly labeled protein can enrich for nonspecific binders. We recommend preparing at least 20 nmol of purified, monodisperse, suitably labeled target reagent before beginning a campaign, and validating it as described in Support Protocol 2. Biotinylated targets with streptavidin or anti‐biotin secondary reagents have been most reliable in our hands. In any case, the labeling strategy should be chosen so that target quality (including, if applicable, conformation, activity, and accessible binding site) is preserved.

Initial library sampling depth should be matched to the question being asked. For general discovery campaigns, we recommend starting with 1010 cells, as described in Strategic Planning and the Basic Protocol. If the expected motif is very short and likely to be well represented, a smaller starting population may be adequate and simplifies early rounds of selection. If near‐complete library coverage is important, the first round should be scaled up approximately three‐fold and should be performed with three MACS columns in parallel.

The initial (i.e., round 1) target concentration creates a selection bottleneck and determines the extent to which weak binders are retained for subsequent steps. We generally recommend beginning with a relatively high concentration, often 1 µM, unless prior binding data suggest a different range. If positive controls are available, analytical staining (as in Support Protocol 3) can be used to guide concentration choice.

MACS is a useful technique for debulking when the population is too large to sort by FACS, but permits little control over selection stringency and provides little information about which clones were retained. We typically perform one round of MACS and then move to FACS if practical. If the initial MACS round yields a population larger than ∼107 cells, it may be necessary to perform a second MACS round with a lower concentration of target protein before proceeding to FACS sorting.

In addition to positive selection against a high‐quality target protein, suitable negative selection steps are critical because peptides can bind beads, streptavidin, antibodies, fluorophores, detergent micelles, or other assay components rather than the intended target. At a minimum, include depletion steps against the secondary reagents and beads used for MACS enrichment, as described in the Basic Protocol. For targets with close homologs, mutant binding sites, detergent requirements, or complex labeling schemes, additional counterscreens should be considered to enrich for the desired specificity. During selections, it is worth keeping in mind the high‐throughput screening tautology “you get what you select for”: consider possible ways in which the selection may be “cheating” to yield peptides with high apparent binding but not in the intended manner.

Selection pressure should be increased gradually. Selection pressure controls how quickly the population converges, and either extreme can be harmful: overly weak selection wastes time and can introduce population drift through excessive culture steps, whereas overly stringent selection can eliminate desired clones by chance. We recommend increasing stringency gradually by lowering the target concentration and/or tightening FACS gates in each successive round. Performing analytical flow cytometry between rounds, described in Support Protocol 3, is useful to guide these changes. A useful rule of thumb is to choose a concentration at which ∼10‐20% of the previous round binds.

The shape of a FACS gate largely determines the stringency of the selection round, and we recommend using both peptide expression and target binding to draw gates. In the first FACS round, we recommend a permissive gate that captures most expression‐positive, binding‐positive cells. In later rounds, use a more stringent gate that contains cells with high target binding relative to expression. As discussed in Strategic Planning and shown in Figure 2, later gates often fall below the expression‐binding diagonal and contain ∼0.2‐2% of cells.

When to end a given selection campaign should be based on the goal of the campaign, not simply the number of rounds completed. If the goal is simply to obtain one peptide that binds above a specified affinity threshold, it may be reasonable to begin screening individual clones once 10‐20% of the pool binds under the desired condition. However, if the goal is motif discovery or recovery of many distinct binders, continuing until 70‐90% of the population binds, while monitoring diversity by NGS as described in Support Protocol 4, is likely to be more informative.

The method(s) of individual clone testing should also match the goal of the selection campaign. Random colony picking can often result in high‐affinity binders. NGS‐guided clone testing is useful for validating highly abundant clones and representative motifs that, by chance, may not be tested through colony picking. We often take both approaches in parallel, again informed by the goals of the selection. In any case, peptides of interest should be synthesized and tested in an orthogonal assay outside the context of yeast surface display to validate binding.

Statistical Analysis

Flow cytometry data can be summarized either as the median fluorescence intensity (MFI) of expression‐positive cells or as the fraction of expression‐positive cells that bind above background. We typically use the latter, as it is more sensitive to small increases in binding. For example, if 5% of cells bind above background, this is a meaningful improvement over the naïve library but generally will not result in a large increase in MFI.

For sequencing data, rarefaction‐extrapolation analysis and Chao1 estimators can be used to monitor diversity reduction across rounds (Chao, 1984; Chao et al., 2014). Sequence logo visualization can be useful for evaluating convergence on one of more motifs (see Support Protocol 4 and Internet Resources).

Understanding Results

Successful campaigns are characterized by two concurrent trends: progressive enrichment of target binding, alongside progressive reduction in library diversity. Enrichment of target binding is typically the most important success criterion, with diversity being a secondary or irrelevant concern. It is also advisable to track binding to one or more off‐target sites to ensure enrichment is specific to the desired target. Target binding can be assessed by analytical flow cytometry (see Support Protocol 3), and it can be defined precisely as the fraction of expression‐positive cells that bind at a level above an unstained control. In a successful campaign, the fraction of cells binding the target at a concentration of ∼1 µM will increase from 0‐1% in the naïve library to 40‐100% after several rounds of selection (Fig. 3). Simultaneously, library diversity will decrease substantially, from a starting diversity of ∼6 × 109 to a final diversity of ∼103‐104. This reduction in library diversity can be best assessed by NGS sequencing and diversity analysis, as described in Support Protocol 4. Additionally, successful campaigns often show the emergence of recognizable motifs in the NGS data, which can be observed by inspection of the highest‐abundance sequences or by the visualization of sequence logos. It can be informative to test consensus peptides, in addition to randomly picked clones and high‐abundance clones, as these sequences can confer very high affinities despite not being enriched throughout the selection (or even necessarily present in the naïve library).

Selections can also fail in several common ways. Some targets appear incapable of binding any short linear peptides in this display format in the relevant concentration regime, and thus selection campaigns yield little or no increase in binding across successive rounds. In such cases, if a positive control is known, it can be informative to generate the positive control yeast display construct and perform a simple titration experiment to establish an on‐yeast apparent K D value. In some cases, this value will be too high for a selection campaign to be feasible. The other most common mode of failure is, as discussed previously, the inadvertent selection of peptide binders to a secondary reagent, protein tags, beads, fluorophores, detergent micelles, or buffer components rather than the intended target. This can often be mitigated through careful counterselection against these reagents. The final common failure mode is one in which the selection pressure is increased too quickly, bottlenecking the selection and generating only a few binding clones. This can be diagnosed by the diversity analyses described previously, and can generally be reversed by restarting the selection from a previously frozen aliquot of a prior round.

Time Considerations

Expanding and freezing aliquots of the library requires ∼3 days, including 48 hr of growth. In terms of active time, it requires 15 min on day 1 and 1‐4 hr on day 3.

A single MACS round typically requires 5 total days: 1 day for growth in glucose medium, 2 days for induction in galactose medium, 1 day for magnetic selection, and 1 day for recovery. In terms of active time, it requires 15 min on day 1, 15 min on day 2, 4‐8 hr on day 4, and 15 min on day 5. Preparation of frozen stocks adds ∼2 additional days but can overlap with other work (15 min active time on day 5, 1 hr on day 7).

A single FACS round typically requires 6 days: 1 day for growth, 2 days for induction, 1 day for staining and sorting, and 2 days for recovery. In terms of active time, it requires 15 min on day 1, day 2, and day 6, and 4‐8 hr on day 4. Again, preparing frozen stocks adds 2 days total time (15 min active time on day 6, 1 hr on day 8).

A complete selection campaign generally requires 3‐8 weeks depending on the number of rounds performed. In general, if progressing from round to round as quickly as possible, it takes ∼8 days per selection round. Sequencing, isolation and testing of individual clones, and orthogonal validation, typically require at least 2‐4 additional weeks.
