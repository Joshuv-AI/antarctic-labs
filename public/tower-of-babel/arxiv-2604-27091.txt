Measurement of the top quark pair production cross section in PbPb \mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{Pb} collisions at \sqrtsNN = 5.36 ​ \TeV \sqrtsNN=5.36\TeV


Abstract
The inclusive cross section for top quark pair ( \ttbar ) production in lead-lead ( PbPb \mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{Pb} ) collisions is reported for the first time at a center-of-mass energy per nucleon pair of 5.36 \TeV . The analysis uses data corresponding to an integrated luminosity of 1.58 \nbinv collected by the CMS experiment at the CERN LHC in 2023. The \ttbar production cross section, σ \ttbar = 3.42 − 0.51 + 0.54 ​ \stat − 0.43 + 0.50 ​ \syst ​ \unit ​ μ ​ b \sigma_{\ttbar}=3.42^{+0.54}_{-0.51}\stat^{+0.50}_{-0.43}\syst\unit{\mu b} , is measured in dilepton final states using a fit to a multivariate discriminator that combines the decay electron and muon kinematic properties with the multiplicity of bottom quark jets. The result is consistent with perturbative quantum chromodynamics calculations at next-to-next-to-leading order (NNLO) accuracy employing several nuclear parton distribution functions. In addition, the Drell–Yan production cross section ( σ DY \sigma_{\text{DY}} ) for dilepton masses above 10 \GeV and the ratio of \ttbar to DY cross sections ( R \ttbar / DY R_{\ttbar/\mathrm{DY}} ) are found to be compatible with the NNLO predictions. The observables σ \ttbar \sigma_{\ttbar} , σ DY \sigma_{\text{DY}} , and R \ttbar / DY R_{\ttbar/\mathrm{DY}} are measured separately for central and semicentral PbPb \mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{Pb} collisions to investigate for the first time the dependence of top quark production on the collision impact parameter.

0.1
Introduction

Extensive studies of the cross section and properties of top quark-antiquark pairs (
\ttbar
) produced in
proton-proton (
\Pp\Pp
) collisions at the CERN LHC
[
46
,
9
]
have improved our knowledge
of key fundamental parameters of the standard model, such as the top quark mass (
m_{\PQt}
),
the strong coupling constant (
\alpS
), and the Yukawa coupling of the top quark to the Higgs boson, through comparisons
of the data with high-accuracy perturbative quantum chromodynamics (pQCD) predictions. These measurements
have also contributed to determining the parton distribution functions (PDFs) of the proton with reduced
uncertainties. In heavy ion collisions, the production of top quarks has long been proposed as a powerful
probe of the nuclear gluon density at high virtualities
[
51
]
, as encoded in nuclear
PDFs (nPDFs). It has also been proposed as a useful means to study final-state effects on top quark decay products as they
traverse the produced quark-gluon plasma (QGP), where they may experience parton energy loss in the medium (“jet quenching”)
[
51
,
7
]
.

Because the top quark decays almost exclusively as
\PQt\to\PW\PQb

[
61
]
, a
\ttbar
event contains two
\PQb
jets and the decay products of the two
\PW
bosons. Experimentally,
\ttbar
events are classified according to the
\PW
boson decay modes.
About 46% of
\ttbar
events are fully hadronic, but these are difficult to isolate from quantum chromodynamics (QCD) multijet backgrounds, which have much larger cross sections. Events in which only one
\PW
boson decays leptonically account for roughly 45% of the total; however, their final states contain multiple jets and are subject to systematic uncertainties from jet energy calibration, quark flavor tagging, and combinatorial ambiguity. The dilepton decay mode,
\ttbar\to(\PWp\PQb)(\PWm\PAQb)\to(\Pellp\HepParticle{\PGn}{\Pell}{}\PQb)(\Pellm\HepAntiParticle{\PGn}{\Pell}{}\PAQb)
, provides the cleanest experimental signature, even though its branching fraction is about 4% when restricting
\Pell
to electrons and muons. This channel yields a pure (
{>}95\%
) sample with relatively loose lepton selection requirements. In
\Pp\Pp
collisions, the single lepton
[
36
]
and dilepton
[
31
]
channels provide the most precise measurements of the
\ttbar
production cross section. Among all channels, the
\Pemp\PGmpm
final state typically provides the best sensitivity. Owing to its higher purity and nearly twice the event yields of the same flavor (
\Pep\Pem
or
\PGmp\PGmm
) channels, the
\Pemp\PGmpm
final state is used to measure the
\ttbar
production cross section with lower uncertainties. In the heavy ion environment, the presence of a very large hadronic background makes it more challenging to measure the lepton
\,+\,
jets decay final states. For these reasons, the dilepton final state, including events with tau leptons that decay into an electron or a muon, is commonly adopted to measure the
\ttbar
production cross sections.

The production of top quark pairs has been measured in proton-lead collisions at a nucleon-nucleon (
\PN\PN
) center-of-mass energy of
\sqrtsNN=8.16\TeV

[
27
,
8
]
and in lead-lead (
\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{Pb}
) collisions at
\sqrtsNN=5.02\TeV

[
32
,
10
]
. The early
\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{Pb}
collision measurements used data corresponding to 1.7–1.9
\nbinv
of integrated luminosity. They have proved useful to establish the baseline observables and selection criteria in the dilepton channel, confirming the feasibility of top quark measurements in events with very large particle multiplicities. However, the ability of these studies to constrain nPDFs or the properties of QGP is limited by the poor statistical precision of the datasets analyzed.

This paper reports the first measurement of the inclusive production cross section of
\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{Pb}\to\ttbar
at
\sqrtsNN=5.36\TeV
, utilizing data corresponding to an integrated luminosity of 1.58
\nbinv
collected in 2023. The analysis uses advanced algorithms to select leptons and identify jets stemming from the fragmentation and hadronization of
\PQb
quarks (
\PQb
jets). These improved techniques combine new variables used to characterize the isolation and prompt production of leptons from
\ttbar
events, and make use of new discriminants based on machine learning to identify the decay leptons, as well as the
\PQb
jets. By incorporating these novel algorithms and performing a combined fit to a multivariate
\ttbar
discriminant across several event categories, the analysis improves upon previous results, reducing the uncertainty in the
\sigma(\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{Pb}\to\ttbar)
cross section and enabling the first observation of this process in heavy ion collisions by the CMS experiment.

The paper is structured as follows. The experimental apparatus is presented in Section
0.2
, and the simulation and event selections are described in Sections
0.3
and
0.4
, respectively. The methods used to control the backgrounds and to extract the signal are described in Sections
0.5
and
0.6
, respectively. The results are presented in Section
0.7
, and summarized in Section
0.8
. Tabulated results of the analysis can be found in HEPData
[
1
]
.

0.2
The CMS detector

The CMS apparatus
[
24
,
38
]
is a multipurpose, nearly hermetic detector, designed to trigger
on
[
34
,
29
,
40
]
and identify electrons, muons, photons, and (charged and neutral)
hadrons
[
35
,
30
,
25
]
. Its central feature is a superconducting solenoid of 6
\unit
m
internal diameter, providing a magnetic field of 3.8
\unit
T. Within the solenoid volume are a silicon pixel and strip tracker,
a lead tungstate crystal electromagnetic calorimeter (ECAL), and a brass and scintillator hadron calorimeter (HCAL),
each composed of a barrel and two endcap sections.
Muons are reconstructed using gas-ionization detectors embedded in the steel flux-return yoke outside the solenoid.
Forward calorimeters extend the pseudorapidity (
\eta
) coverage provided by the barrel and endcap detectors.
Two forward hadron (HF) calorimeters use steel as an absorber and quartz fibers as the sensitive material. They are located 11.2
\unit
m from the interaction region, one on each end, and together they provide coverage in the range
3.0<\abs{\eta}<5.2
.
They also serve as luminosity monitors. In addition, two zero-degree calorimeters (ZDCs), made of quartz fibers and plates
embedded in tungsten absorbers, are used to detect neutrons (
\Pn
) from nuclear breakup events in the range
\abs{\eta}>8.3

[
23
]
.
More detailed descriptions of the CMS detector, together with a definition of the coordinate system used and the
relevant kinematic variables, can be found in Refs.
[
24
,
38
]
.

Events of interest are selected using a two-tiered trigger system. The first level, composed of custom hardware processors,
uses information from the calorimeters and muon detectors to select events at a rate of up to 70
\unit
kHz within a
fixed latency of 4
\mus

[
34
]
. The second level, known as the high-level trigger (HLT), consists of a farm of
processors running a version of the full event reconstruction software optimized for fast processing, and reduces the
event rate down to 50
\unit
kHz before data storage
[
29
,
40
]
. The quoted values have been optimized for
\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{Pb}
collisions.

A particle-flow (PF) algorithm
[
28
]
aims to reconstruct and identify each individual particle in an event,
with an optimized combination of information from the various elements of the CMS detector. The energy of photons is
obtained from the ECAL measurement. The energy of an electron is determined from a combination of the electron momentum
at the primary interaction vertex as determined by the tracker, the energy of the corresponding ECAL cluster, and the
energy sum of all bremsstrahlung photons spatially compatible with originating from the electron track.
The energy of a muon is obtained from the curvature of the corresponding track. The energy of a charged hadron is
determined from a combination of the momentum measured in the tracker and the matching ECAL and HCAL energy deposits,
corrected for the response function of the calorimeters to hadronic showers. Finally, the energy of neutral hadrons is
obtained from the corresponding corrected ECAL and HCAL energies.

0.3
Monte Carlo simulations

Monte Carlo (MC) simulations are used to estimate the signal acceptance and efficiency, as well as to validate the background estimation
methods. The simulations are performed using matrix element (ME) generators based on pQCD calculations at next-to-leading order (NLO) accuracy, emulating a mixture of
\Pp\Pp
,
\Pp
\Pn
, and
\Pn
\Pn
interactions in proportions corresponding to their relative abundances in
lead nuclei (mass number
A=208
). The ME generators employ the EPPS21_nlo nPDFs
[
53
]
, which use the CT18ANLO PDF
[
57
]
as the proton parton density baseline. The generated events are then interfaced with the
\PYTHIA
8.306
[
65
]
parton shower (with the CP5 tune
[
33
]
) and are embedded in inelastic
\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{Pb}
events simulated with the
\HYDJET

[
58
]
MC generator. The
\HYDJET
model reproduces the effects of the underlying event (UE) measured in
\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{Pb}
data.
In doing so it does not alter the hard-scattering process simulated at the ME level, nor the decay products of the generated particles. To account for possible medium effects affecting the simulated signal and background final states, methods based on data are employed and described in more detail at the end of Section
0.4
. The
\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{Pb}
collision “centrality” (
\ie
, the degree of overlap of the two
\mathrm{Pb}
ions) is determined from the sum of the transverse energy (
\sum\ET
, where
\ET=E\cosh\eta
), deposited in both HF calorimeters and corrected with an event-by-event weighting procedure based on the average number of
\PN\PN
collisions in each centrality interval as obtained with a Glauber model
[
52
]
. After this weighting, residual differences between the centrality distribution measured in data and predicted in simulation are corrected for based on a control sample of
\PZ\to\PGmp\PGmm
events. All the MC samples include a simulation of the full detector response implemented in
\GEANTfour

[
4
]
with realistic alignment and calibration, tuned to data.

The MC
\ttbar
signal samples are generated at NLO accuracy with
\MGvATNLO
(v2.9.18)
[
6
]
and
\POWHEG
v2
[
55
]
. In the former case, the event generation includes up to two extra partons at the ME level, and the merging with the parton shower is performed via the FxFx procedure
[
54
]
. In the
\POWHEG
case, only up to one real parton emission is generated, with the remainder produced by the parton shower. The cross sections of both simulated
\ttbar
samples are scaled up to match the next-to-next-to-leading order (NNLO) pQCD predictions, including soft-gluon resummation at next-to-next-to-leading logarithmic (NNLL) accuracy, obtained with the
\TOPpp
program
[
49
]
. In the calculation a top quark mass of
m_{\PQt}=172.5\GeV
is used, the nominal renormalization and factorization scales are set to
\mu_{\text{R}}=\mu_{\text{F}}=m_{\PQt}
, and the QCD strong coupling is set to
\alpS(m_{\PZ})=0.118
. Uncertainties of
{\pm}1.0\GeV
and
{\pm}0.001
are assumed for the
m_{\PQt}
and
\alpS(m_{\PZ})
values
[
61
]
, respectively. The predicted NNLO
\,+\,
NNLL inclusive cross section is
\sigma_{\text{th}}(\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{Pb}\to\ttbar)=3.69^{+0.71}_{-0.48}\unit{\mu b}
, where the uncertainty is the quadratic sum of theoretical scale, parametric (from
m_{\PQt}
and
\alpS
), and EPPS21_nlo nPDF uncertainties.

The characteristic signature of the
\ttbar
events selected in this analysis is the presence of two leptons with large transverse momentum (
\pt
) originating from the
\PWp\PWm
decays. Consequently, any process that produces a similar dilepton final state can mimic the signal and constitutes a background to the measurement. The simulated background samples include Drell–Yan (
\mathrm{DY}
,
\PZ/\Pgg^{*}\to\Pep\Pem/\PGmp\PGmm/\PGtp\PGtm
),
\PW+\text{jets}
, single top quark production, diboson processes, and multijet events containing nonprompt leptons, mostly from heavy-quark hadron decays. The inclusive DY process
\Pp\Pp\to\PZ+\PX
, including up to two recoiling jets via the FxFx merging procedure, is simulated with
\MGvATNLO
separately in the low-mass (10–50
\GeV
) and high-mass (
{>}50\GeV
) regions, with predicted cross sections of 287 and
83\unit{\mu b}
, respectively. The same generator is used for the
\PW+\text{jets}
sample, with a cross section of 899
\unit
\mu
b. Single top quark production in association with a
\PW
boson or via the
t
channel is simulated with
\POWHEG

[
64
]
, with corresponding cross sections of 0.272 and
1.27\unit{\mu b}
. Diboson events, in particular
\PW\PW
, are generated with
\POWHEG
including all final states with at least one lepton. The
\PW\PW
contribution is normalized to a cross section of
0.613\unit{\mu b}
, as predicted by
\POWHEG
. Finally, multijet processes with at least one muon or electron are simulated with
\PYTHIA
. The multijet samples are included in the training of the lepton isolation discriminators, but are not used to estimate any of the backgrounds.

0.4
Event selection

Candidate events are first selected based on whether they pass the minimum-bias or the single-muon HLT triggers. The minimum-bias trigger requires a coincident signal in both HF calorimeters and the presence of at least one neutron in one of the ZDCs, whereas the single-muon trigger requires a muon reconstructed at the HLT with
\pt>7\GeV
. The stability of these triggers is verified throughout the data-taking period, and their efficiency is extracted directly from the data. Offline, collision events are further required to have at least one interaction vertex reconstructed with two or more tracks within a distance of 25
\cm
from the nominal collision point along the beam axis and 2
\cm
in the transverse plane. In addition, at least two towers (arrays of channels) on each side of the HF calorimeter must have a total reconstructed energy larger than 4
\GeV
.

Events containing at least two charged leptons—where “lepton” (denoted by
\Pell
) refers exclusively to electrons and muons hereafter—are selected, with additional criteria applied to target dilepton decays of the two
\PW
bosons from
\ttbar
production. Lepton candidates must have
\pt>20\GeV
, originate promptly from the primary collision vertex, and be isolated from other particles in the event. Electrons are required to lie within the ECAL barrel or endcap fiducial regions (
\abs{\eta}\in[0,1.444]\cup[1.566,2.5]
), and muons are selected within
\abs{\eta}<2.4
. Candidates passing these kinematic and fiducial selections are further considered in the analysis if they satisfy two distinct sets of identification and isolation criteria. Decoupling these two requirements enables the isolation variable to define control regions (CRs), which are used to estimate the nonprompt-lepton background and extrapolate it to the signal region.

Electron identification relies on a dedicated boosted decision tree (BDT) discriminant that combines several observables, including the shower shape and position of the ECAL cluster, its matching to the associated reconstructed track, and the energy deposits in the HCAL towers subtended by the ECAL cluster. The BDT is trained on QCD multijet and
\ttbar
simulations using
XGBOOST

[
22
]
and optimized with a grid-search cross-validation procedure
[
62
]
. An electron candidate is considered to pass the identification requirement if the BDT discriminant exceeds a centrality-dependent threshold corresponding to a 95% efficiency and an expected misidentification rate below 4%. This procedure ensures a selection efficiency independent of the collision centrality. The contamination from misidentified electron candidates is significantly reduced with respect to the rectangular-based identification criteria used in our previous study
[
32
]
. Muon identification is based on a set of quality requirements applied to the global track fit, which combines information from the inner tracker and the muon system. The selection follows the standard “tight” criterion described in Ref.
[
30
]
.

For the isolation criteria, a common procedure applicable to both electrons and muons has been further developed, making use of dedicated BDTs trained separately for each lepton type. These BDTs combine different isolation sums of PF candidate energies within cones of radius
R=\sqrt{\smash[b]{\left(\Delta\eta\right)^{2}+\left(\Delta\phi\right)^{2}}}=0.3
around the lepton direction. These isolation sums are computed using the “soft killer” method
[
20
]
with thresholds estimated in binned ranges of detector pseudorapidity, namely
[-2.5,\,-2,\,-0.8,\,0.8,\,2,\,2.5]
. This procedure effectively mitigates the dependence of the isolation sums on the
\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{Pb}
event activity and enables a robust multivariate discrimination, less reliant on the simulation model used. In addition, the isolation BDT makes use of the three-dimensional impact parameter significance of each lepton. This variable is largely uncorrelated with the isolation sums and provides good discrimination against leptons from heavy-quark hadron decays. As for the electron identification BDT case, the independence of the working point on centrality ensures that the isolation requirement has a constant efficiency of 90%.

In case more than two leptons pass the selections above, priority is given to muons over electrons. The efficiencies associated with the trigger, identification, and isolation selections are determined using the tag-and-probe method based on
\PZ\to\Pellp\Pellm
events in data
[
35
,
30
]
. To improve the identification of the signal, the selected events are further categorized as follows:

•

“SF/DF” labels indicate whether the leptons have the same (
\Pep\Pem
or
\PGmp\PGmm
) or different (
\Pemp\PGmpm
) flavors. For SF final states, it is required that the acoplanarity
A_{\phi}=1-\abs{\Delta\phi(\Pellp\Pellm)}/\pi
is larger than 0.01 to remove contributions from elastic two-photon production (
\PGg\PGg\to\Pellp\Pellm
)
[
44
]
.

•

“SS/OS” labels indicate whether the leptons have the same or opposite electric-charge signs.

•

“on-Z/off-Z” labels indicate whether the invariant mass of the SF OS pair is
\abs{m(\Pell\Pell)-91\GeV}<15\GeV
or
\abs{m(\Pell\Pell)-91\GeV}>15\GeV
. Events with SF OS dilepton invariant mass
m(\Pell\Pell)<20\GeV
are excluded to remove background contributions from quarkonia decays and the low-mass DY process.

To improve the discrimination from the main backgrounds, three additional BDT classifiers are trained for the
\Pep\Pem
,
\Pemp\PGmpm
, and
\PGmp\PGmm
final states. The BDTs make use of the following lepton-based variables:

1.

the
\pt
of the leading (
\ie
, highest
\pt
) and subleading leptons:
\pt(\Pell_{1})
,
\pt(\Pell_{2})
;

2.

the asymmetry of the leptons’
\pt
:
[\pt(\Pell_{1})-\pt(\Pell_{2})]/[\pt(\Pell_{1})+\pt(\Pell_{2})]
;

3.

the
\pt
and mass of the dilepton system:
\pt(\Pell\Pell)
,
m(\Pell\Pell)
;

4.

the sphericity of the dilepton system:
\pt(\Pell\Pell)/[\pt(\Pell_{1})+\pt(\Pell_{2})]
;

5.

the absolute pseudorapidity of the dilepton system:
\abs{\eta(\Pell\Pell)}
;

6.

the sum of the lepton pseudorapidities:
\sum\abs{\eta_{\Pell}}
; and

7.

the acoplanarity
A_{\phi}
of the dilepton system.

Among these,
\pt(\Pell\Pell)
and
m(\Pell\Pell)
are found to be the most discriminating variables for the SF channels, whereas
\pt(\Pell_{2})
,
\sum\abs{\eta_{\Pell}}
, and
\pt(\Pell\Pell)
are the most relevant observables in the DF final state. The relative importance of all input features has been quantified using a
SHAP

[
63

\ttbar

[

For each event, hadronic jets are clustered from the PF candidates using the anti-
\kt
algorithm
[
18
,
19
]
with a distance parameter of 0.3. Jet energy corrections and
\PQb
quark identification are performed with a dedicated multivariate algorithm called “unified particle transformer” (
UParT
)
[
37
]
, explicitly trained for heavy ion collisions.
A detailed description of this algorithm for heavy-flavor quark identification can be found in Refs.
[
39
,
45
]
. Jets with
\pt>30\GeV
,
\abs{\eta}<2.4
, and separated from the lepton by a distance
\Delta R(\Pell,j)>0.4
are accepted in the analysis. Three working points are defined for the identification of
\PQb
jets: loose, intermediate, and tight; corresponding to
\PQb
quark tagging efficiencies of 90, 80, and 70%, respectively. The intermediate working point is chosen as the default one in the analysis. These working points are parameterized such that the efficiency in simulation is constant as a function of collision centrality. The use of
UParT
reduces the misidentification rate by more than an order of magnitude relative to the
\PQb
jet identification algorithm employed during Run 2
[
32
]
. This improvement stems from the explicit exploitation of the jet PF constituent information within a transformer architecture, in contrast to the Run-2 approach that relied primarily on higher-level variables. In addition, the resulting jet energy scale correction becomes largely independent of the collision centrality (ignoring potential “jet quenching” effects) and remains robust with respect to the hadronization model, while retaining a residual dependence on the jet flavor.

\PZ

\pt(\PZ)>30\GeV

\abs{\Delta\phi(\PZ,\text{jet})}>2\pi/3

events, the
UParT
efficiencies have been measured separately for UE and jets recoiling against the
\PZ
boson. In the regime considered in the analysis,
UParT
is fully efficient for recoil jets, while being inefficient for UE jets with
\pt<70\GeV
. Data-to-MC scale factors for these efficiencies are parameterized as functions of the collision centrality and the uncorrected jet
\pt
, and are applied to the baseline simulation as event weights to more accurately reproduce the
UParT
performance observed in data. The scale factors account for up to 20% differences between data and MC simulations, being larger for UE jets and in the 0–10% centrality bin. In the analysis, the simulated jet
\pt
is additionally corrected using the measured
\langle x_{\mathrm{j}}\rangle
profile. The uncertainties associated with this procedure range from 2 to 20%, depending on the centrality and
\pt
bins and jet origin.

The adopted procedure is expected to provide a consistent description of the jets in data; any inconsistency would manifest as significant discrepancies in the number of reconstructed inclusive and/or bottom-quark jets. We therefore validate the approach by fitting the jet and
\PQb
jet multiplicities in
\PZ
boson events. The results of these fits are displayed in Fig.
5
for central (left) and semicentral (right)
\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{Pb}
collisions, and show a very good description of the inclusive jet (upper) and
\PQb
jet (lower) multiplicities.

0.5
Background estimation

After the selection of dilepton pairs as described in Section
0.4
, two main sources of background remain:

\mathrm{DY}
production, which enters into the SF categories, and nonprompt leptons, which contribute mainly to the DF category.

The normalization of the DY background is left to float freely in the signal extraction fit described in Section
0.6
.
Prior to the fit, the counting of on-Z events shows good agreement between data and expectations within the uncertainties in the
integrated luminosity and theoretical cross section. The data/theory ratios amount to
1.08\pm 0.04\stat
for the
\Pep\Pem
and
1.09\pm 0.04\stat
for the
\PGmp\PGmm
channels. As illustrated in Section
0.6
, good agreement is found between the measurements and the theoretical predictions in the BDT regions where
\mathrm{DY}
production is expected to dominate.

The second main source of background is nonprompt and misidentified leptons from multijet QCD events. Given the large cross section for the production of heavy-flavor jets, the probability for these processes to mimic a
\ttbar
dilepton event is not necessarily negligible for the working points used for identification and isolation of the lepton candidates. However, when pairing two leptons in the event, their properties are expected to be mostly uncorrelated with each other, particularly if the leptons had their origin in different partonic interactions.
This background is estimated based on an event-mixing technique which pairs leptons from different events as if they were produced in a single
\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{Pb}
collision.

The selection of events to be mixed is performed by identifying the nearest neighbors in the three-dimensional space defined by the
centrality,
\pt(\Pell\Pell)
, and
m(\Pell\Pell)
bins. The distances between events are evaluated using a
k
-D tree algorithm,
which efficiently partitions the phase space in these variables and retrieves the closest matches
[
15
,
16
]
.
For each event, four alternative dilepton candidates are constructed in this way, and the distribution of their kinematic
variables is used to obtain an alternative estimate of the nonprompt (combinatorial) background contribution.
The final estimate is an average of the four with the normalization assigned based on the number of SS events selected
in each channel. This technique significantly improves the statistical uncertainty associated with this background. Using simulated events, the accuracy of this method has been tested and ascribed an intrinsic uncertainty of 20–40%, depending on the final state.

Finally, other less relevant backgrounds include single top quark and diboson (notably
\PW\PW
) processes. These backgrounds are irreducible and estimated from the corresponding MC simulations described in Section
0.3
with the relevant data-to-MC corrections
applied to them.

0.6
Signal extraction

The
\ttbar
signal strength
\mu=\sigma_{\text{meas}}/\sigma_{\text{th}}
(where
\sigma_{\text{meas}}
and
\sigma_{\text{th}}
are the measured and theoretical cross sections, respectively) is extracted from a fit to the BDT output of the
\Pep\Pem
,
\PGmp\PGmm
(SF/off-Z/OS), and
\Pemp\PGmpm
(DF/OS) channels, analyzed in events with zero, one, or at least two
\PQb
-tagged jets. Two additional categories (
\PZ\to\Pep\Pem
and
\PZ\to\PGmp\PGmm
in the on-Z/OS class) are added to the fit to control the normalization of the
\mathrm{DY}
background. In each category, the fit is performed to the BDT output in events with zero, one, or at least two
\PQb
-tagged jets. The impact of the systematic uncertainties on the signal and background expectations, detailed below, is modeled through a set of dedicated nuisance parameters
[
47
]
. The fitted BDT distributions are displayed in Fig.
6
.
In addition to the signal strength and the corresponding breakdown of statistical and systematic uncertainties, the significance of the
\ttbar
process is also extracted. The statistical analysis is performed using the
Combine
tool
[
41
]
based on the
RooFit

[
66
]
and
RooStats

[
59
]
frameworks. The best-fit signal strength and its approximate 68% confidence level (
\CL
) interval are extracted following the procedure outlined in Section 3.2 of
[
26
]
, while the significance (
Z
score) is evaluated using an asymptotic approximation and validated with pseudo-experiments
[
50
]
. The experimental uncertainties can affect the modeling of both signal and backgrounds in the fit.
They can be broadly grouped into those associated with the selection and calibration of physics objects,
and those arising from the background estimation, as discussed below.

The efficiency of the trigger and lepton selections is corrected for the differences between data and simulation using scale factors
derived from events in the
\PZ\to\Pellp\Pellm
CRs. The uncertainties associated with these scale factors are propagated to
the analysis by varying the event weights used in the simulation, resulting in different predictions for the cross sections for the
signal, single top, and diboson processes. The difference between these alternative predictions and the nominal one is parameterized as a function of several nuisance parameters depending on the type of lepton and the associated efficiency (trigger, identification, or
isolation).

The impact of jet reconstruction inefficiencies in regions where CMS subdetectors suffer from aging or malfunctioning components is evaluated. These areas are mapped in the
\eta
–
\phi
plane, and simulated jets with hadronic activity inside them are removed from the event. The resulting differences in the BDT shapes, relative to the nominal simulation, are assigned as a systematic uncertainty.
Additional sources of jet selection inefficiency may arise from the
UParT
application, as discussed in Section
0.4
. To assess this effect, the data-to-simulation scale factors associated with the
UParT
efficiency are independently varied for UE jets and for jets matched to the hard process in the simulation. The corresponding variations in the BDT distributions are taken as a systematic uncertainty.

The impact of the jet energy scale uncertainty on the final observables is broken down into four main contributions: parton type, hadronization, process dependence, and uncertainty in the residual scale. The parton type uncertainty is estimated from simulation based on the different responses of
\PQb
, and light-quark or gluon jets with respect to the mixed-flavor response. The hadronization uncertainty is evaluated from the difference in detector response obtained when using
\PYTHIA
versus
\HERWIG
7
[
11
,
14
]
as parton shower and hadronization models. The process-dependent uncertainties result from the fact that
UParT
has been trained using
\ttbar
events, and it may thereby exhibit residual response differences when applied to other final states with different jet kinematics. This effect is evaluated from the jet response in the
\PZ+\text{jets}
MC sample. Finally, the residual scale uncertainty is subdivided into two sources: jet quenching and extrapolation to the vacuum or peripheral collision regime. Both are estimated from the average
x_{\mathrm{j}}
ratio measured in data over the MC prediction (Fig.
4
). The deviation from unity when extrapolating the relative response to peripheral events is considered as a
{\approx}5\%
systematic uncertainty. The systematic uncertainty associated with jet quenching is evaluated by comparing the final observables with and without applying the parameterized energy loss shown in Fig.
4
on a jet-by-jet basis in the simulation. This procedure leads to a substantial loss of efficiency for reconstructing jets with
\pt>30\GeV
, thereby affecting the jet multiplicity in the
\PQb
-tagged analysis. Finally, the impact of
\PQb
tagging on the predicted distributions shown in Fig.
6
is assessed by varying the
\PQb
tagging working point,
\ie
using loose or tight instead of intermediate, such that the signal efficiency changes by
{\pm}10\%
. These variations correspond to an expected change of the misidentification rate by more than a factor of two
[
39
]
.

Beyond the uncertainties affecting the reconstruction of physics objects, the normalization of the event yields is impacted by a 6.4%
uncertainty in the total
\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{Pb}
integrated luminosity obtained from a preliminary analysis of van der Meer scan data collected during the
\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{Pb}
run, following the same methodology of Ref.
[
43
]
.

All simulated signal and background samples are affected by the experimental uncertainties described above. In the signal extraction fit, these uncertainties are treated as fully correlated between signal and background processes, with the exception of the
\PQb
tagging uncertainties. The latter are kept uncorrelated to reflect the different parton type and kinematics between the on-
\PZ
CR and the
\ttbar
signal region. Theoretical uncertainties from missing higher-order corrections in the ME predictions are evaluated by varying the
\mu_{\text{F}}
and
\mu_{\text{R}}
scales within factors of two (excluding variations in opposite directions). The nuisance parameters associated with these scale uncertainties are treated as uncorrelated between the background and signal samples. The normalization of the dominant DY background is left unconstrained in the fit, but an additional model uncertainty is introduced from the difference between the shape of the
\POWHEG
and
\MGvATNLO
predictions. For the nonprompt-lepton background, uncertainties arise from its normalization (20–40%), and from differences in shape between the event-mixing method and the SS and nonisolated sidebands. An uncertainty of 30% in the absolute normalization of other backgrounds (single top and
\PW
\PW
) has been included in the fit.
Statistical uncertainties in the simulated background differential cross sections are also incorporated via a Barlow–Beeston “lite” treatment
[
13
]
.

Finally, the signal MC samples include two classes of modeling uncertainties. First, differences between the signal BDT shapes predicted by
\POWHEG
and
\MGvATNLO
are included as a ME generator uncertainty. Although this variation is nominally one-sided, a symmetrized uncertainty is used in the statistical analysis. Additional modeling uncertainties are introduced by varying the top quark mass by
{\pm}1\GeV
(implemented through generator-level reweighting using relativistic Breit–Wigner ratios) and by modifying the top quark
\pt
spectrum through an empirical reweighting that reproduces the softer
\pt
distribution observed in the
\Pp\Pp
data
[
42
]
. Second, uncertainties from the parton densities are evaluated by reweighting the
\ttbar
events using all eigenvectors of the EPPS21_nlo set—a total of 106 variations—and propagating the resulting changes in the BDT output. The final nPDF plus
\alpS
uncertainties are determined following the PDF4LHC prescription
[
17
]
.

0.8
Summary

The first measurement of the inclusive production of top quark pairs (
\ttbar
) in lead-lead (
\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{Pb}
) collisions at a nucleon-nucleon center-of-mass energy of
\sqrtsNN=5.36\TeV
has been presented, extending earlier studies performed at lower collision energies. The analysis is based on dilepton events selected from the 2023 dataset recorded by the CMS experiment, corresponding to an integrated luminosity of 1.58
\nbinv
. Several improvements have been implemented—including enhanced electron identification and lepton isolation, refined heavy-flavor jet identification, and a dedicated calibration of the
\PQb
jet energy scale—which collectively provide a substantial gain in precision compared with previous analyses.

The cross sections
\sigma_{\ttbar}=3.42^{+0.54}_{-0.51}\stat^{+0.50}_{-0.43}\syst\unit{\mu b}
, and
\sigma_{\text{DY}}=397\pm 3\stat^{+27}_{-25}\syst\unit{\mu b}
for the Drell–Yan process, along with their ratio
R_{\ttbar/\mathrm{DY}}=0.0086^{+0.0014}_{-0.0013}\stat^{+0.0011}_{-0.0010}\syst
, have been measured. The
\sigma_{\ttbar}
and
R_{\ttbar/\mathrm{DY}}
measurements have total uncertainties of
{\approx}20\%
and are consistent with perturbative quantum chromodynamics calculations at next-to-next-to-leading-order accuracy obtained using different nuclear parton distribution functions. Results for central and semicentral
\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{Pb}
collisions are also reported for the first time to examine the dependence of the top quark yields on the collision impact parameter. Combined with the higher collision energy and the larger data samples anticipated for the remainder of Run 3, these developments firmly establish top quark production as a future probe of initial- and final-state effects in heavy ions.
