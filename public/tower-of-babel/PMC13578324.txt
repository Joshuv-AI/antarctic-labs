HB reduces inflammation in HUVECs via the Nrf2/HO-1 axis by inhabiting apoptosis and oxidative stress
Authors: Xiaoyu Zhang, Chunlin Cong, Hui Cao, Lina Zhu, Na Cheng, Shuang Wu, Peina Geng, Lili Cao, Rui Chen, Zhaoxiu Liu, Peng Wu, Linyu Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Existing research data indicate that Hemoglobin (HB) can ameliorate oxidative stress, fibrosis, and inflammatory responses. Given that traditional cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases are associated with chronic vascular injury and oxidative stress, this study aimed to investigate the effect of HB on vascular inflammation and explore its potential for vascular protection. The study employed the CCK-8 assay, Western blot, immunofluorescence, PCR, as well as assessments of reactive oxygen species (ROS), malondialdehyde (MDA), Caspase-3 activity, and flow cytometry. Apoptosis was detected by flow cytometry using Annexin V-FITC staining, and intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were measured using the fluorescent probe DCFDA-DA. HB significantly reduced the expression of inflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑6, IL‑1β) in H2O2‑stimulated HUVECs, an effect associated with attenuation of oxidative stress‑induced inflammatory responses, and reduced levels of MDA, ROS, and vascular cell apoptosis. These effects were mediated through enhancing Nrf2/HO‑1 pathway expression and function. When the Nrf2/HO‑1 pathway was inhibited, the suppressive effects of HB on inflammatory markers and vascular oxidative stress were markedly attenuated. These findings suggest that HB enhances Nrf2/HO‑1 expression and function and that this pathway contributes to the protective effects of HB to reduce inflammatory cytokine release, oxidative stress, and apoptosis in H2O2-stimulated HUVECs. HB’s anti‑inflammatory effects are secondary to the attenuation of oxidative stress. These findings extend the known antioxidant properties of HB to the vascular endothelium, supporting further investigation in the context of oxidative stress‑induced endothelial dysfunction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58161-2.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases such as atherosclerosis and thrombosis remain leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide1–3. Exposure to H2O2 induces oxidative stress, which in turn triggers an inflammatory response characterized by upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines. In the present study, this is referred to as an oxidative stress–induced inflammatory response rather than a direct inflammatory stimulus.A critical early event in their pathogenesis is vascular endothelial dysfunction, characterized by increased oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and heightened susceptibility to apoptosis4–8. Upon activation or injury under various pathological stimuli, the vascular endothelium promotes the expression of adhesion molecules and pro-inflammatory cytokines, accelerates leukocyte recruitment, and shifts the hemostatic balance toward a prothrombotic state9–12. Among the multiple molecular pathways involved in maintaining vascular endothelial homeostasis, the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)/heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) axis has been identified as a central regulatory mechanism, serving as a pivotal defensive hub for cells against oxidative and inflammatory damage13. This pathway senses changes in the intracellular redox state and coordinates the expression of a series of protective genes, thereby maintaining endothelial integrity, regulating vascular tone, and inhibiting the shift toward pro-inflammatory and pro-thrombotic phenotypes. Its activation not only directly enhances cellular antioxidant capacity but also exerts broad anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects through downstream products such as carbon monoxide and bilirubin14–16. Consequently, it is regarded as a key molecular nexus linking oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and endothelial dysfunction, and has emerged as an important target for the prevention and treatment of vascular diseases17–19.

Nrf2 is a crucial transcription factor. Under normal physiological conditions, its activity is tightly regulated: Nrf2 specifically binds to its inhibitory protein, Keap1, in the cytoplasm via its Neh2 domain20. Keap1, functioning as an adaptor for an E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, mediates the ubiquitination of Nrf2, leading to its rapid degradation by the proteasome21,22. This process maintains Nrf2 activity at a low basal level. This regulatory mechanism forms the core of the classic “Keap1-Nrf2-ARE” signaling pathway, ensuring that the Nrf2-mediated adaptive response is effectively activated only when the cell is exposed to stimuli such as oxidative stress23,24. When cells are exposed to stimuli such as oxidative stress or electrophilic compounds, the activation mechanism of Nrf2 is initiated25. Specifically, these inducers can induce covalent modifications of key cysteine residues on the Keap1 protein, thereby disrupting the stability of the Keap1-mediated ubiquitination degradation complex and promoting the dissociation of Nrf2 from its inhibitory binding26,27. The liberated Nrf2 subsequently undergoes nuclear translocation. Upon entering the nucleus, it forms a heterodimer with small Maf proteins. This complex specifically recognizes and binds to the antioxidant response elements located in the promoter regions of target genes, thereby efficiently initiating the transcriptional program of a comprehensive set of cytoprotective genes, including HO-1, NQO1, and GCLC. The products of these genes act synergistically to enhance the cell’s detoxification, antioxidant, and adaptive capacities, constituting a core defense system for cells against environmental stress28,29. HO-1 is a key effector gene downstream of the Nrf2 signaling pathway. It catalyzes the first step of heme degradation, breaking it down into carbon monoxide, biliverdin, and free iron. Biliverdin is then rapidly converted to bilirubin by the enzyme biliverdin reductase30,31. Each of these metabolites possesses important biological functions: carbon monoxide exerts cytoprotective effects with anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic properties; biliverdin and bilirubin are potent endogenous antioxidants; and the released free iron induces the expression of ferritin, which stores iron ions in a safe form, preventing them from participating in the catalysis of free radical generation32. Therefore, through the multiple mechanisms mediated by its catalytic byproducts, HO-1 enhances cellular antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cytoprotective capacities. It plays a crucial protective role in the pathophysiology of various oxidative stress-related diseases and represents an important therapeutic target33,34. Therefore, pharmacological activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway represents a promising therapeutic strategy for protecting endothelial cells and retarding the progression of vascular diseases35–37.

Heterophyllin B (HB) is a natural cyclic peptide compound isolated from medicinal plants. Studies have reported that it exhibits various biological activities, primarily including anti-fibrotic and antioxidant effects38. In terms of anti-fibrotic activity, HB may inhibit excessive activation of fibroblasts and abnormal deposition of extracellular matrix through the regulation of signaling pathways such as TGF-β/Smad. Regarding its antioxidant properties, it can enhance the endogenous antioxidant defense system within cells, such as by activating pathways like Nrf2/HO-1, thereby mitigating oxidative stress-induced cellular damage39,40. These characteristics endow HB with potential therapeutic value in disease models such as liver fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and conditions associated with oxidative damage. Currently, it is being investigated as a lead compound for further structural optimization and mechanistic studies41,42. However, its direct role in modulating vascular endothelial inflammation and the underlying mechanisms, particularly its interaction with the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway, remain to be fully elucidated. Given the established link between endothelial oxidative stress, inflammation, and the development of cardiovascular complications, we postulate that HB may exert vasculoprotective effects through the activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway.

While HB has shown antioxidant effects in other cell types, its direct role in modulating vascular endothelial inflammation and the underlying mechanisms remain unexplored. This study aimed to investigate whether HB protects HUVECs against H2O2-induced injury and whether this protection requires Nrf2/HO-1 signaling. We focused on assessing the impact of HB on the following key pathological processes: the production of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), oxidative stress markers (ROS and MDA), and cellular apoptosis. Furthermore, we employed gene silencing techniques to further elucidate the mechanistic role of the Nrf2/HO-1 axis in these effects. Our findings suggest that HB attenuates endothelial inflammation, oxidative damage, and apoptosis primarily through activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, thereby suggesting that HB warrants further investigation as a candidate for protecting against endothelial dysfunction.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were supplied by Jennio (Guangzhou, China). The cells were maintained in an incubator at 37 °C with a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (PM150110, Pricella) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 100 units/mL penicillin. HB (Cat. No. HY-N1476, MCE, USA) was purchased from Nantong Qixiang Biological Co., Ltd., dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and then diluted with cell culture medium to achieve the desired final treatment concentration before being used in the experiment. In each petri dish, we selected three different fields of view for microscopic observation, and performed three independent counts of the number of cells in each field of view, aiming to assess the stability and reliability of the cell growth rate. We conducted three separate cell culture experiments, each utilizing different batches of culture media and cells, to ensure the stability and reproducibility of the experimental results.(Notes: 1.HUVECs were used at passages 3–8 for all experiments. Cells beyond passage 8 were discarded to ensure phenotypic stability and reproducible responses.2.DMSO concentration༚The final concentration of DMSO in all treatment groups was 0.1% (v/v), which is a commonly used non‑toxic concentration for in vitro cell experiments and did not affect cell viability or experimental results.The vehicle alone exerted no significant effect on any of the measured parameters in this study.3.Vehicle‑only controlA vehicle‑only control group (0.1% DMSO alone) was included in all experiments. Cells in this group were treated with the same volume of solvent (0.1% DMSO in culture medium) without HB, and all procedures and detection indicators were consistent with other groups. The vehicle control was used as the baseline reference in statistical analysis.)

Cell viability measurement by CCK-8 Kit

For the CCK-8 study, HUVEC cells were inoculated into 96-well plates at a cell density of 5 × 103 cells per well, and 100 µL of medium was added to each well. After 24 h of culture, HB was added to the medium to achieve the final concentrations (0, 25, 50, 75, 100 µM HB). The cells were then cultured for an additional 6–8 h. Subsequently, 100 µM H2O2 was added to each well and incubated for 24 h. A preliminary dose‑response experiment was performed over a range of 10–200 µM H2O2, and 100 µM was selected because it induced sub‑lethal oxidative stress with approximately 70% cell viability. Following this, 200 µL of CCK-8 solution (Cat. No. C6005, Syncyme) was added to each well and incubated for another 2 h at 37 °C. Optical density (OD) values were read at 450 nm using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader.

Western blot analysis

Cell lysates were prepared using an immunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA) containing 0.01% protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The cells were incubated at 4 °C for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000xg at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was collected, and the loading buffer equivalent to 1/4 of the total volume was added. The samples were then heated at 105 °C for 10 min. Protein samples were resolved using 10% SDS-PAGE at 300 mA for 90 min. The proteins were subsequently transferred to a PVDF membrane and blocked in a blocking solution (5% skim milk dissolved in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween 20) for 2–3 h. Following this, the primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase was incubated at room temperature for 2 h. Chemiluminescence signals were detected using the ECL method (Bio-Rad). The primary antibodies used included Nrf2 (NO.A0674, 1:1000), HO-1 (10701-1-AP, 1:1000), TNF-α (10602-R101-A, 1:1000), IL-1β (50101-T48, 1:1000), and IL-6 (HY-P80723, 1:1000).

Intracellular ROS production assay

The fluorescence probe DCFDA-DA was used to determine intracellular ROS production. Simply put, the fused HUVECs were pretreated in a 24-well culture plate for 30 min. After removal from the plate, the HUVECs were incubated with 20 µM DCFDA for 6 h and then stimulated with H2O2. The fluorescence intensity was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader and observed under a fluorescence microscope. Additionally, analysis was performed using flow cytometry (SN: BF29333, BECKMAN) and FlowJo software.

Knockdown of Nrf2 expression using CRISPR/Cas9 with specific sgRNA

Nrf2 was knocked down using true CRISPR/Cas9 system (not siRNA). HUVECs were transfected with 50 nM Nrf2‑targeting sgRNA and Cas9 nuclease using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen).The sgRNA sequences were: sg‑Nrf2‑2: 5′-GCAGTCACAACGAGGACAGG-3′;sg‑NC (negative control): 5′-TTTCTATGCATAGCTGACGT-3′.After 48 h transfection, 2 µg/mL puromycin was added for 72 h to select stable cells. Knockdown efficiency was verified by Western blot in three independent experiments, and representative data are shown in Fig. 5A.

HO‑1 inhibition by ZnPP

To inhibit HO‑1 activity, cells were pretreated with 10 µM zinc protoporphyrin IX (ZnPP, Sigma‑Aldrich, Cat. No. Z2251) for 2 h before HB and H2O2 administration. The concentration was selected based on previous reports and preliminary cytotoxicity tests.ZnPP alone did not affect basal cell viability, ROS levels, or apoptosis (data not shown).

Apoptosis analysis

The Annexin V-FITC apoptosis detection kit (Vazyme, A211) was used to detect the apoptosis rate. After treatment under specified conditions, the cells were treated with trypsin and harvested. Cells were harvested by trypsinization and resuspended in 195 µl of Annexin V binding buffer. Then, 5 µl of Annexin V-FITC and 10 µl of propidium iodide were added, and the cells were incubated in the dark for 20 min at room temperature. Stained cells were analyzed by flow cytometry within 1 h.(SN: BF29333, BECKMAN).

qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA from HUVECs under different conditions was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Sigma), and cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription using a one-step miRNA cDNA synthesis kit (R323, Vazyme). Then, real-time PCR was used to detect the expression levels of Nrf2, HO-1, and the transcription levels of inflammatory molecules TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq II kit. All procedures were carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions. GAPDH mRNA expression was used for normalization, and the gene transcript levels were normalized using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) formula.

Measurement of malondialdehyde (MDA) content

Following experimental treatment, the contents of MDA were measured using the commercial MDA detection kit (Biyuntian, S0021S). For MDA measurement, the reaction was performed based on the measurement of red color produced during the reaction to thiobarbituric acid (TBA) with MDA, and the absorbance of 532 nm was determined.

Determination of Caspase-3 activity

The cells under different stimuli were trypsinized and then lysed on ice for 15 min with cell lysis buffer (100 mM sodium chloride, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, 2 mM sodium vanadate, and protease inhibitor cocktail). After centrifugation at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was quantified using the BCA method. Then, 50 µg of protein was incubated with 10 µl of substrate Ac-DEVD-pNA (2 mM) at 37 °C for 2 h. Caspase-3 activity was measured at 405 nm using a microplate reader.

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent biological replicates (n = 3) (technical replicates were performed within each biological replicate, unless otherwise indicated). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 5.0a (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA).)

Results

HB does not cause damage to endothelial cells and can partially reverse the damaging effect of H2O2 on endothelial cells

As shown in Fig. 1A, we initially investigated the potential toxic effects of HB on HUVEC cells and discovered that HB treatment did not affect the viability of HUVECs at concentrations ranging from 25 to 100 µM. Notably, HB antagonized the detrimental effects of H2O2 on cell viability in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1B). Given the minimal difference between 50 µM and 75 µM, we selected concentrations of 25, 50, and 100 µM for further studies. Additionally, HB could significantly reverse the apoptosis of HUVEC cells induced by H2O2 (Fig. 1C-D). Simultaneously, H2O2-enhanced caspase-3 activity was also reduced following HB pretreatment (Fig. 1E).

HB has the capability to diminish the production of ROS and MDA that were provoked by H2O2

ROS is widely regarded as an indicator of cellular oxidative stress status. As a result, the fluorescence derived from DCFH-DA probes was measured to determine intracellular ROS accumulation. The results indicate that H2O2 significantly increased the ROS level compared with the control group, and that HB treatment significantly reduced the ROS level in a concentration-dependent manner compared with the group treated with H2O2 only (Fig. 2A-B). We also examined the effects of HB on ROS using DCF by enzymography (Fig. 2C) and by fluorescence flow cytometry (Fig. 2D). Additionally, HB treatment also ameliorated the adverse effect of H2O2 on MDA production, a marker of enzymatic antioxidant state(Fig. 2E). These results suggest that HB can attenuate H2O2-induced ROS production and oxidative stress in HUVECs.

HB reduces the expression of inflammatory cytokines upregulated secondary to H2O2‑induced oxidative stress

TNF-α, IL-1βand IL-6 are inflammation-related factors, and their increased expression can promote the death of endothelial cells and the expression of thrombus-related molecules. Compared with the control group, H2O2 significantly increased the protein levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 (P < 0.05). In contrast, when compared with the H2O2 group only (P < 0.05), the protein expressions of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 were significantly decreased after HB pretreatment (Fig. 3).

HB supplementation increases the expression the Nrf2-HO-1 signaling pathway in HUVECs exposed to H2O2

Activation of the Nrf2-HO-1 signaling pathway has an extraordinary impact on the response to adverse stimuli, including oxidative stress damage, and plays an prominent role in thrombosis. Therefore, we investigated the possible effect of HB on this pathway. As shown in Fig. 4A and B, exposure to H2O2 induced Nrf2 protein expression, and HO-1 protein levels were also significantly upregulated (Figs. 4A-C). However, HB preconditioning significantly enhanced the transcription and expression of Nrf2 and HO-1 in HUVECs after exposure to H2O2 (Fig. 4D).

Blockade of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway abolishes the protective effect of HB against H2O2-induced apoptosis

To further elucidate the correlation between Nrf2-HO-1 activation and HB-mediated protective effects on HUVECs, we silenced Nrf2 expression and inhibited downstream HO-1 expression by transfecting with Nrf2 sgRNA (Fig. 5A). Functional analysis confirmed that, under H2O2 conditions, silencing Nrf2 reversed the protective effect of HB on cell viability (Fig. 5B). Meanwhile, H2O2-induced caspase-3 activation and cell apoptosis were inhibited by HB pretreatment, while silencing of Nrf2 reversed these changes (Fig. 5C-E).

Transfection with sg-Nrf2 weakened HB’s protective effect against H2O2-induced inflammatory molecule expression

Upon transfection with sg-Nrf2, the protective shield of HB cells against the upregulation of inflammatory molecules such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 triggered by H2O2 exposure was significantly compromised. The silencing of Nrf2, a vital antioxidant and anti-inflammatory regulator, disrupted the normal defensive mechanisms that HB cells employ to counter the adverse effects of oxidative stress, leading to an exacerbated inflammatory response. This outcome highlights the pivotal role of Nrf2 in preserving cellular homeostasis and mitigating the harmful consequences of H2O2-induced inflammation (Fig. 7A-D).

Discussion

This study confirms that the active compound HB significantly ameliorates inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis in hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)-induced injured human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) by activating the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway43,44. The protective effects of HB are reflected in its ability to restore cell viability, attenuate the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and malondialdehyde (MDA), suppress the expression of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), and inhibit caspase-3 activation and cellular apoptosis. Notably, these beneficial effects were largely abrogated upon silencing of Nrf2 or inhibition of the Nrf2/HO-1 axis, underscoring the central role of this pathway in mediating the actions of HB.

Our findings align with the growing body of evidence indicating that activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway constitutes a critical adaptive mechanism for endothelial cells to counteract oxidative and inflammatory injury. Under physiological conditions, Nrf2 is sequestered in the cytoplasm bound to Keap1; however, under oxidative stress, it translocates into the nucleus, where it promotes the transcription of antioxidant genes, including HO-1. Heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) is a key downstream effector molecule in the cellular defense system. By catalyzing the degradation of heme, HO-1 not only eliminates free heme, which exhibits pro‑oxidant and pro‑inflammatory effects, but also generates biologically active metabolites such as carbon monoxide (CO) and bilirubin. Among these, CO exerts anti‑inflammatory and anti‑apoptotic functions by modulating signaling pathways, while bilirubin serves as a potent endogenous antioxidant. Through these mechanisms, HO-1 integratively mediates multifaceted cytoprotective effects—anti‑inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti‑apoptotic—thereby playing a central role in maintaining tissue homeostasis45–47. In the present study, HB enhanced both the expression and transcriptional activity of Nrf2 and HO-1 in H2O2-stimulated HUVECs, correlating with a reduction in oxidative markers and inflammatory mediators. This suggests that HB may function as an Nrf2 activator, thereby bolstering the cellular defense capacity against external insults.Although our data demonstrate that HB upregulates Nrf2 and its downstream target HO-1, and that Nrf2 is functionally required for HB’s protective effects, direct visualization of Nrf2 nuclear translocation (e.g., by immunofluorescence or nuclear fractionation) would provide more definitive evidence of Nrf2 activation. Future studies incorporating such approaches will further elucidate the precise mechanism.

Prior studies have reported that HB exhibits anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties in other models, such as those involving fibrosis and metabolic disorders44,48–50. However, its role in vascular endothelial protection, particularly within the context of inflammation-associated endothelial dysfunction, remains underexplored. Our work addresses this gap by providing mechanistic evidence that HB modulates endothelial inflammation through the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway. The observed reduction in TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 is particularly significant, as these cytokines are key drivers of endothelial activation, leukocyte recruitment, and thrombus formation—processes central to the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis and thrombosis.We acknowledge that H2O2 is primarily an inducer of oxidative stress, not a classical direct inflammatory stimulus such as TNF‑α or LPS. The increased expression of TNF‑α, IL‑1β, and IL‑6 observed in our study is likely secondary to oxidative damage, representing an oxidative stress‑induced inflammatory response. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as evidence that HB attenuates the inflammatory consequences of oxidative stress, rather than demonstrating a direct anti‑inflammatory effect against classical inflammatory agonists. Future studies using TNF‑α or LPS would be needed to assess direct anti‑inflammatory actions of HB.

Furthermore, functional loss-of-function experiments confirmed the functional relevance of Nrf2/HO-1 activation. Silencing Nrf2 not only reversed the HB-mediated reductions in ROS and inflammatory cytokines but also restored the H2O2-induced apoptosis and caspase-3 activation. These results indicate that the protective effects of HB are largely dependent on an intact Nrf2/HO-1 pathway. They also highlight the potential risk posed by impaired Nrf2 signaling—as observed in aging, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases—in the development of endothelial dysfunction.

However, certain limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the investigation was conducted entirely in vitro using HUVECs, which may not fully replicate the complexity of the vascular microenvironment in vivo. Second, although HB showed no cytotoxicity at the tested concentrations, its pharmacokinetics, bioavailability, and long-term safety in physiological systems remain to be evaluated. Third, while we focused on the Nrf2/HO-1 axis, other signaling pathways such as NF-κB, MAPK, or PI3K/Akt may also be involved in the actions of HB and warrant further investigation. Fourth, direct evidence of Nrf2 nuclear translocation was not provided in this study; therefore, we describe our results as enhanced Nrf2/HO-1 pathway expression and function rather than direct activation. Fifth, the anti-inflammatory effects observed in this study are secondary to the attenuation of oxidative stress induced by H2O2, rather than direct anti-inflammatory activity against classical inflammatory stimuli. Sixth, a full dose-response curve for H2O2 was not presented in the main manuscript, although preliminary dose-finding data were used to determine the optimal concentration. Seventh, vehicle control (0.1% DMSO alone) and ZnPP alone control data were not shown in figures for brevity, but both were verified to have no significant effects on cell viability, oxidative stress, inflammation, or apoptosis.

We acknowledge that the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway is a well-established cytoprotective mechanism, and that HB’s activation of this pathway in endothelial cells represents an extension of its known antioxidant properties. However, the genetic evidence presented here establishes Nrf2 as a functionally necessary mediator of HB’s effects in the vascular endothelium—a cell type with direct relevance to cardiovascular disease pathogenesis. Future studies in animal models will be needed to determine whether these in vitro findings translate to in vivo vascular protection.

In summary, these findings suggest that HB protects vascular endothelial cells from H2O2-induced inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis by enhancing Nrf2/HO-1 pathway expression and function. These results also indicate that HB’s anti-inflammatory effects are secondary to the attenuation of oxidative stress rather than direct anti-inflammatory activity. These findings extend the known antioxidant properties of HB to the vascular endothelium and support further preclinical investigation, including animal studies, to evaluate its potential for preserving endothelial function under oxidative stress conditions (Fig. 8).
