Synthesis, biological evaluation and in silico studies of novel propargyl-tethered isatin hydrazones as monoamine oxidase inhibitors for Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Saranya Kattil Parmbil, Hee Jung Kim, Jaeyeop Lim, Mohamed A Abdelgawad, Mohamed Sadek Abdel-Bakky, Mohammed M Ghoneim, Harish Chandra Vishwakarma, Sunil Kumar, Sandeep Kumar, Hoon Kim, Bijo Mathew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson’s disease are closely associated with dysregulated activity of monoamine oxidase-B (MAO-B), which leads to dopamine depletion and oxidative stress. Despite the availability of numerous monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibitors on the market, their irreversibility and associated side effects necessitate the development of more effective and reversible MAO-B inhibitors. In this study, a series of twenty-one indole-based derivatives (PSH1-PSH21), collectively designated as PSH, was synthesised and evaluated for inhibitory activity against MAO isoforms. Most synthesised compounds showed higher inhibitory activity toward MAO-B than MAO-A, indicating selectivity for MAO-B. Among the PSH derivatives, PSH18 exhibited the most potent MAO-B inhibitory activity (IC50 = 0.95 ± 0.02 µM), followed by PSH6 (IC50 = 1.79 ± 0.40 µM) and PSH2 (IC50 = 1.96 ± 0.08 µM). Compound PSH18 showed the highest selectivity index value of 42.11, followed by PSH6 (22.35) and PSH2 (20.41). Additionally, PSH18 was confirmed to be a competitive and reversible inhibitor of MAO-B with an inhibition constant value of 0.89 ± 0.035 µM. Notably, PSH18 exhibited good permeability across the blood–brain barrier in parallel artificial membrane permeability assay experiments, along with acceptable absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity parameters predicted through in silico modelling, suggesting its potential as a central nervous system-targeted molecule. Molecular docking and a 200 ns molecular dynamics simulation demonstrated stable binding of the ligand to the MAO-B active-site pocket, driven by hydrophobic and π–π stacking interactions with key amino acid residues lining the aromatic cage. Moreover, the calculated binding energy indicated strong ligand–protein interactions. Overall, these results indicate that the PSH scaffold could serve as a promising lead structure for the development of potent and selective MAO-B inhibitors. Introduction

Neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson’s disease (PD) represent a major global health challenge and are mainly characterised by the gradual and selective loss of neurons in brain regions responsible for motor and cognitive function. The neuronal loss observed in these disorders is frequently associated with oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and imbalance of neurotransmitter systems. Among the many factors involved in the neurodegenerative process, monoamine oxidases (MAOs) play a critical role because they catalyse the oxidative deamination of neurotransmitters and generate oxidative stress, thereby increasing neuronal susceptibility to damage1–3.

MAO refers to flavin-containing enzymes responsible for the oxidative deamination of monoamines such as dopamine, serotonin, and norepinephrine. The hydrogen peroxide and reactive oxygen species produced during this enzymatic reaction generate oxidative stress and contribute to neuronal cell death. Although two isoforms of MAO (monoamine oxidase-A [MAO-A] and monoamine oxidase-B [MAO-B]) share approximately 70% structural similarity, they differ significantly in tissue distribution, inhibitor sensitivity, and substrate specificity. Increased MAO-B activity has been associated with dopamine depletion and oxidative stress in PD. In contrast, MAO-A plays a key role in regulating serotonergic and noradrenergic transmission in the central nervous system (CNS)4–7.

Therapeutically, MAO-B inhibition has been shown to be effective in managing Parkinson’s symptoms by increasing dopamine availability and mitigating oxidative stress. However, most clinically available MAO inhibitors, including clorgyline, rasagiline, and selegiline, act irreversibly, which may raise safety concerns. Therefore, there is a critical need for novel, selective, and reversible MAO inhibitors with improved therapeutic value. Reversible inhibitors such as safinamide have demonstrated improved safety profiles but remain limited in availability8–10.

Over the years, a wide range of aryl and heterocyclic scaffolds, including pyrazolines, isatin, chromones, chalcones, coumarins, thiazoles, and xanthines, has been explored for the development of MAO inhibitors3–9. Among these, isatin has gained particular attention because of its endogenous origin and broad pharmacological properties. Structurally, isatin contains a nitrogen atom at the N-1 position and two carbonyl groups at the C-2 and C-3 positions. Due to its ability to occupy the enzyme substrate cavity, it exhibits reversible MAO inhibitory activity with remarkable selectivity toward MAO-B in the nanomolar range. However, its affinity and potency are limited by unfavorable interactions of the polar C-3 carbonyl group within the hydrophobic binding pocket10–16. Recently, our group performed several rational modifications of this region by incorporating acyl hydrazone, cinnamyl, benzyloxybenzene, and phenyl substituents, which provided a promising strategy for enhancing both activity and selectivity11–16. Numerous studies have demonstrated that propargylamine is a promising pharmacophore for the development of MAO-B inhibitors due to its ability to form irreversible covalent interactions with the flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) cofactor present in the active site of MAO-B, as observed in clinically used drugs such as rasagiline and selegiline. This interaction contributes to potent and selective MAO-B inhibition17,18.

The present study focuses on the design of a new class of isatin-based MAO inhibitors. For this purpose, a propargyl group was introduced at the N-1 position of substituted isatin, followed by the incorporation of various hydrazone moieties at the C-3 position. The objective of this study was to synthesize twenty-one propargyl-based isatin hydrazones and evaluate their in vitro MAO inhibitory activity. The lead molecule was further assessed for cytotoxic effects in normal cell lines. It was hypothesized that these combined structural features would improve isoform selectivity, reduce off-target effects, and enhance reversible inhibition. Through this integrated approach, the present study aims to contribute to the development of safer, more effective, and clinically relevant therapeutic candidates for the management of neurodegenerative diseases.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

For the synthesis of propargyl-tethered isatin derivatives, isatin, 5-bromoisatin, 5-fluoroisatin, 5-nitroisatin, 5-methoxyisatin, and propargyl bromide were purchased from TCI Chemicals (Chennai, Tamil Nadu, India). 5-Chloroisatin was obtained from Spectrochem (Mumbai, Maharashtra, India). Hydrazine hydrate was procured from Merck Life Science (Mumbai, Maharashtra, India). Phenylhydrazine was acquired from Nice Chemicals (Cochin, Kerala, India). 5-Methylisatin and 4-phenylthiosemicarbazide were procured from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). All compounds were of analytical grade and were used without further purification.

Synthesis

Propargyl-tethered isatin derivatives were synthesized via a two-step protocol. Initially, isatin derivatives (1.0 equiv) underwent N-alkylation with 1.5 equivalents of propargyl bromide in the presence of potassium carbonate, using N, N-dimethylformamide as the solvent. The reaction mixture was stirred at ambient temperature for 16–20 h, and the progress of the reaction was monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) using hexane: ethyl acetate (1:1, v/v) as the mobile phase. After completion, the reaction mixture was poured into water to dissolve inorganic salts, followed by extraction with ethyl acetate. The organic layer was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, concentrated under reduced pressure, and the crude product was purified by recrystallization from ethanol to yield the corresponding propargylated isatin intermediates19.

In the second step, the propargylated isatin intermediates were condensed with appropriate hydrazine derivatives in methanol in the presence of a catalytic amount of glacial acetic acid. Reactions involving hydrazine hydrate and phenylhydrazine were carried out in a Monowave reactor under microwave irradiation at 100 °C for 10 min, whereas reactions with 4-phenylthiosemicarbazide were conducted under reflux for 8–10 h. The progress of the reaction was monitored by TLC using hexane/ethyl acetate (4:1, v/v) as the mobile phase for hydrazine hydrate and phenylhydrazine derivatives, and hexane/ethyl acetate (2:1, v/v) for thiosemicarbazide derivatives. All final compounds were purified by flash column chromatography, followed by recrystallization from ethanol20.

Enzyme inhibition assay

Chemicals and Enzymes

All enzymes and chemicals used in this study were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), except for pargyline, which was obtained from BioAssay Systems (Hayward, CA, USA).

Enzyme study

For the MAO activity assay, recombinant human MAO-A and MAO-B were used with kynuramine (0.06 mM) and benzylamine (0.3 mM) as substrates, respectively. The enzymatic activities of MAO-A and MAO-B were monitored at 316 and 250 nm, respectively, for 45 min, as described previously21, using 500 µL reaction mixtures in cuvettes or 200 µL reaction mixtures in a 96-well microplate reader (VARIOSKAN LUX; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with UV plates (Corning Incorporated, Kennebunk, ME, USA). All synthesized compounds were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at a stock concentration of 20 mM and subsequently diluted to the desired concentrations before use. Inhibition assays were conducted by measuring the residual enzyme activity after the addition of the inhibitor relative to a control containing 1 µL of DMSO. The concentrations at which residual activity reached approximately 50% were determined, and the IC50 values were calculated. The selectivity index (SI) values were calculated as the ratio of IC50 for MAO-A to IC50 for MAO-B, as described previously21. Toloxatone, clorgyline, safinamide, and pargyline were used as reference compounds.

Kinetic analysis

Kinetic analysis of MAO-B was conducted using PSH18, the most potent inhibitor in the PSH series. Enzyme kinetics were analyzed at five substrate concentrations ranging from 0.0375 µM to 0.6 µM benzylamine using cuvettes, as described previously21. The assay was performed by comparing three inhibitor concentrations corresponding to approximately 0.5×, 1×, and 2× the IC50 (i.e., 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 µM PSH18) with the control. The inhibition pattern was analyzed using a Lineweaver–Burk (LB) plot, and the Ki values were calculated from secondary plots generated at the three inhibitor concentrations22.

Reversibility test using dilution assay for MAO-B inhibition by PSH18

The time dependency of MAO-B inhibition by PSH18 and recovery from inhibition were examined as described previously, with slight modifications23. After preincubation of PSH18 with MAO-B for 0, 2, 5, 10, 15, or 30 min at room temperature, residual activity was determined by the addition of 0.3 mM benzylamine. The compound was used at concentrations twofold higher than its IC50 value. For the dilution assay, excess PSH18 (100× IC50) was incubated with MAO-B for 10 min and then diluted 100-fold into the reaction mixture. Residual activity was compared with that under the undiluted condition (i.e., 1× IC50). Safinamide and pargyline were used as reference reversible and irreversible MAO-B inhibitors, respectively, following the same procedure as that used for PSH18.

Parallel artificial membrane permeability assay (PAMPA)

Efficient drug delivery to the CNS requires effective transport across the blood–brain barrier (BBB). Compounds with permeability coefficient (Pe) values greater than 4.0 × 10− 6 cm/s are considered to have a high likelihood of brain penetration, whereas those exhibiting Pe values below 2.0 × 10− 6 cm/s are categorized as having poor BBB permeability24.

In this study, the BBB permeability of PSH18 was evaluated using the PAMPA-BBB model. The ability of the compound to cross the BBB was assessed by determining its Pe. PSH18 demonstrated a Pe value of 5.89 × 10− 6 cm/s, indicating significant BBB permeability.

Computational studies

The absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) prediction

The ADME characteristics of potential drug candidates are evaluated during the drug discovery process to achieve optimal pharmacokinetic behaviour. In the present study, pharmacokinetic properties were predicted in silico using web-based platforms, including SwissADME (http://www.swissadme.ch/) and pkCSM (http://biosig.unimelb.edu.au/pkcsm/)25,26.

Density functional theory (DFT)

DFT calculations were performed to investigate the electronic structure and reactivity of the lead compound, PSH18, using the Jaguar module of the Schrödinger suite. The molecular geometry was fully optimised using the B3LYP exchange correlation functional in combination with the 6-31G basis set. All DFT calculations were conducted in the gas phase to evaluate the intrinsic electronic properties of the molecule without solvent-induced perturbations. The optimized structure was then used to calculate frontier molecular orbitals (highest occupied molecular orbital [HOMO] and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital [LUMO]) and the dipole moment. In addition, electrostatic potential (ESP) surfaces were generated and mapped onto the electron density isosurface to visualize charge distribution across the molecular framework27–30.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking analysis of the lead compound PSH18 was carried out using the Schrödinger molecular modeling suite31. The crystallographic structure of human MAO-B (PDB ID: 2V5Z) was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank32. The protein was prepared using the Protein Preparation Wizard, which included energy minimization, preprocessing, and optimization of protonation states. Ligand structures were generated and optimized using the LigPrep module. The docking grid was automatically defined around the co-crystallized ligand present in the active site. Docking simulations were performed using the Extra Precision mode with the OPLS4 force field to ensure accurate scoring and pose prediction33,34.

Molecular dynamics (MD)

MD simulations were performed using the Desmond simulation engine integrated within the Schrödinger LLC software suite31. The protein–ligand complex (MAO-B in complex with PSH18) was constructed using the Desmond System Builder and solvated in an explicit aqueous environment. Simulations were conducted under physiological conditions for a total duration of 200 ns, with the temperature maintained at 300 K and the pressure at 1.01325 bar33,34.

Molecular Mechanics Generalized Born Surface Area (MM-GBSA)

The binding free energy of the protein–ligand complex was calculated using the MM-GBSA method implemented in the Prime module of the Schrödinger suite. Binding free energy calculations were performed by extracting representative frames at different time points (0–200 ns) from the trajectory generated during the MD simulation. The binding free energy was calculated using the following equation:

ΔGbind = ΔGcomplex − (ΔGprotein + ΔGligand).

where ∆Gbind represents the binding free energy, and ΔGcomplex, ΔGprotein, and ΔGligand correspond to the free energies of the protein–ligand complex, the isolated protein, and the ligand, respectively.

The total binding free energy was further decomposed into energetic components, including hydrogen bonding, lipophilic, and van der Waals interactions. A negative binding free energy value indicates favorable binding affinity35,36.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

The PCA approach was applied to explore the essential dynamics and dominant modes of motion in the protein-ligand interaction model during the MD simulations. The covariance matrix of atomic displacements (commonly Cα atoms) was calculated and subsequently diagonalized to derive eigenvectors and their corresponding eigenvalues, which provide information about the direction and magnitude of collective motions, respectively. The first two eigenvectors correspond to the largest variances in the data set (PC1 and PC2) and were used to project the trajectories onto a two-dimensional space. This analysis enables the identification of critical conformational states and provides insight into the dynamic characteristics of the system37. The resulting PCA data were used to construct the free energy landscape (FEL).

Free Energy Landscape (FEL)

FEL analysis was performed to examine the conformational stability and free energy profile of the protein-ligand complex during the MD simulation. The FEL was constructed using the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) derived from PCA, which describe the dominant modes of collective motion in the system. The free energy landscapes were presented as both two-dimensional contour plots and three-dimensional surface plots, where low energy regions represent stable conformations of the system. This technique provides a deep understanding of the conformational space of the system and identifies the most energetically favourable states38.

The dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM)

DCCM analysis has been done to assess motion correlations among different protein residues during the MD simulation. The correlations were evaluated based on the cross-correlation coefficients (Cij) between the displacement vectors of the Cα atoms of residues i and j. The correlation coefficient values range from + 1 to − 1, where positive values indicate correlated motion (movement in the same direction), negative values indicate anti-correlated motion (movement in opposite directions), and values close to zero indicate independent motion of residues39,40.

Results and discussion

Synthesis

A series of propargyl-substituted isatin hydrazone derivatives (PSH1–PSH21) was successfully synthesized via a two-step synthetic strategy, as outlined in Scheme 1. In the first step, substituted isatin derivatives bearing electron-donating and electron-withdrawing groups were selectively N-propargylated to afford the corresponding propargylated isatin intermediates (PS1–PS7). These intermediates were obtained in good yields and served as key precursors for further functionalization. In the second step, the propargylated isatin intermediates were condensed with hydrazine hydrate, phenylhydrazine, or 4-phenylthiosemicarbazide in methanol in the presence of a catalytic amount of glacial acetic acid to furnish the target hydrazone and thiosemicarbazone derivatives. Reactions involving hydrazine hydrate and phenylhydrazine proceeded efficiently under microwave irradiation, whereas thiosemicarbazone formation required conventional reflux conditions.

Synthesis of propargyl-substituted isatin hydrazone derivatives (PSH1–PSH21).

The formation of the hydrazone linkage was confirmed by spectroscopic analysis. In the 1H NMR spectra of the hydrazine hydrate series (PSH1–PSH7), the CH2 and CH protons of the propargyl moiety appeared in the shielded region between 3.28 and 4.61 ppm. The protons corresponding to the NH2 group were observed in the downfield region at 10.05–10.62 ppm. The aromatic protons of isatin appeared in the range of 7.15–7.45 ppm. In the 1H NMR spectra of the phenylhydrazine series (PSH8–PSH14), the CH2 and CH protons of the propargyl moiety were observed in the shielded region between 3.34 and 4.68 ppm. The NH proton appeared in the deshielded range of 12.61–12.67 ppm. The aromatic protons of the two phenyl rings were observed in the range of 7.14–7.85 ppm. Similarly, the 1H NMR spectra of the phenylthiosemicarbazide derivatives (PSH15–PSH21) showed CH2 and CH protons of the propargyl moiety in the shielded region of 2.31–4.58 ppm. The NH proton appeared in the deshielded range of 12.72–12.85 ppm, while the additional NH proton of the thiosemicarbazide group appeared in the range of 9.45–9.52 ppm. The aromatic protons of the two phenyl rings appeared in the range of 7.03–7.80 ppm. In the 13C NMR spectra of the synthesized compounds (PSH1–PSH21), the carbonyl carbon of isatin appeared in the range of 159.61–160.84 ppm. The propargyl carbons were detected between 73.43 and 75.61 ppm, while the corresponding methylene carbon appeared in the region of 20.59–28.16 ppm. The aromatic carbons of isatin and the phenyl rings appeared between 108.53 and 142.72 ppm. In addition, the thiocarbonyl (C = S) carbon of the thiosemicarbazide derivatives (PSH15–PSH21) appeared in the downfield region at 174.71–175.94 ppm. High-resolution mass spectrometry further confirmed that the molecular weights of all synthesized compounds were consistent with their proposed structures.

Kinetic analysis

A LB plot was constructed to evaluate the inhibition of MAO-B by PSH18. The plots were generated using five different substrate concentrations (0.0375–0.6 µM benzylamine) in the absence and presence of increasing concentrations of PSH18 (0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 µM). In the kinetic analysis, the lines corresponding to PSH18 intersected at the y-axis in the LB plot (Fig. 1A). This kinetic behavior is characteristic of competitive inhibition, suggesting that PSH18 competes with the substrate for binding at the active site of MAO-B. To further confirm the inhibition pattern, secondary plots of the slopes of the LB plots versus inhibitor concentration were constructed. The secondary plot exhibited a linear relationship, from which the inhibition constant (Ki) was determined to be 0.89 ± 0.035 µM (Fig. 1B). The low Ki value indicates a strong binding affinity of PSH18 toward MAO-B.

Reversibility test by dilution assay

MAO-B activity in the presence of PSH18 remained nearly unchanged after preincubation for up to 30 min and was comparable to the untreated control (Fig. 2A). This finding indicates that PSH18 does not inhibit MAO-B in a time-dependent manner. In the reversibility test using the dilution assay, a 100-fold dilution of a high-concentration PSH18 mixture resulted in 41.35% residual activity relative to the control, which was comparable to that observed under the undiluted condition (55.35%) (Fig. 2B). These findings demonstrate that PSH18 acts as a reversible MAO-B inhibitor, similar to the reference compound safinamide (53.97% to 26.24%), whereas pargyline showed a marked reduction in residual activity (53.92% to 0.32%), consistent with irreversible MAO-B inhibition.

Structure–activity relationship (SAR)

Subgroup PSH1–PSH7 shares an identical core structure with different substituents attached at the C-5 position of the phenyl ring. In this subseries, a hydrazine moiety is linked to the C-3 position of the isatin scaffold through an imine linkage. Among these compounds, PSH2 (–Br) and PSH6 (–NO2) exhibited moderate inhibitory activity against MAO-B. Similarly, the PSH8–PSH14 subseries possesses a common structural framework in which a phenylhydrazine moiety is connected to the C-3 position of the isatin ring. Although these compounds differ in the substituents at the C-5 position of the phenyl ring, none of them displayed notable inhibitory activity against MAO-B. The PSH15-PSH21 subseries also shares an identical backbone, except that a phenylthiosemicarbazide moiety is attached at the C-3 position of the isatin scaffold. Most compounds in this group exhibited strong inhibitory activity toward MAO-B. Notably, PSH18, containing fluorine at the C-5 position of the phenyl ring and a phenylthiosemicarbazide group at C-3 of the isatin scaffold, showed the highest inhibitory activity. The relative order of activity was F > Cl ≈ OCH3 > CH3 > H ≈ Br ≈ NO2. Overall, PSH18 exhibited the most potent inhibitory activity among all PSH derivatives. DFT

DFT calculations were carried out to understand the electronic characteristics, stability, and reactivity of the lead compound PSH18. The optimised geometry obtained from Jaguar calculations corresponded to a minimum energy structure, which indicates the conformational stability of the molecule and validates its suitability for electronic property analysis.

Frontier molecular orbital analysis revealed distinct localisation patterns for the HOMO and LUMO orbitals (Fig. 4A). The HOMO was predominantly localised over the thiosemicarbazide/hydrazone linker region, with a major contribution from the nitrogen and sulfur atoms, indicating that this fragment acts as the principal electron-donating site of the molecule. While, the LUMO was mainly distributed over the isatin scaffold and carbonyl containing aromatic framework, which indicates that these regions as favorable electron-accepting sites. This spatial separation of the HOMO and LUMO across different molecular fragments suggests efficient intramolecular charge transfer, which may contribute to stabilization of ligand-protein interactions during binding29,30. For PSH18, the HOMO energy was − 0.2126 Ha, whereas the LUMO energy was − 0.0945 Ha.

The propargyl chain of PSH18 extended into the hydrophobic channel of the protein cavity, which also stabilises the ligand-protein complex.

Molecular dynamics

The stability of the PSH18–MAO-B complex was evaluated using a 200 ns MD simulation under NPT ensemble conditions at 300 K. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) analysis (Fig. 6A) indicated that the Cα atoms of the protein and the ligand remained within acceptable limits throughout the simulation. The RMSD of the protein increased during the initial phase of the simulation (approximately 0–20 ns) and subsequently stabilized between 1.5 and 4 Å. A slight increase was observed during the mid-simulation period (approximately 60–90 ns); however, no significant drift was detected. In contrast, the RMSD of the ligand (Lig fit Prot) remained relatively stable during the post-equilibration period, without sudden deviations, indicating that PSH18 remained bound within the catalytic cavity.

Further evaluation of protein backbone flexibility was performed using root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis (Fig. 6B). The majority of residues fluctuated below 1.8 Å, hich indicates structural rigidity. Greater fluctuations were observed in the terminal regions, consistent with typical protein dynamic behavior. In particular, residues located around the active site showed minimal flexibility, pointing out that the ligand binding stabilised the catalytic cavity.

As shown in the interaction histogram (Fig. 6D), hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic contacts, π-π stacking, and water-bridge interactions contributed substantially to the stability of the PSH18-MAO-B complex. Several key amino acid residues exhibited sustained interactions with PSH18 throughout the 200 ns simulation. PSH18 interacted with 22 amino acid residues, including Tyr60, Leu88, Phe99, Phe103, Pro104, Trp119, Leu164, Phe168, Leu171, Cys172, Tyr188, Ile198, Ile199, Ser100, Thr101, Gln206, Ile316, Tyr326, Phe343, Tyr398, and Tyr435. Tyr326 displayed a combination of hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and water-bridge formation, with a cumulative interaction fraction approaching unity, indicating persistent engagement throughout the trajectory. Strong and continuous hydrophobic contributions from Tyr398 and Tyr435 further support stabilization within the aromatic cage region of MAO-B.

The ligand-protein contact diagram (Fig. 6C) shows that PSH18 forms a strong hydrogen bond interaction (83%). The aromatic ring participates in π–π stacking with Tyr398 (67%), contributing to stabilization within the MAO-B aromatic cage. Additional hydrophobic stabilization is provided by Leu171, Tyr435, and Phe343.

Principle component analysis

PCA was performed to check the conformational dynamics of the protein-ligand complex during the MD simulation. Projection of the trajectory onto the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) displayed the conformational states sampled throughout the simulation. As shown in the PCA plot (Fig. 7), clear clusters of conformations were observed in confined regions of conformational space. The presence of tight clusters indicates that the system sampled a limited number of energetically favorable conformations with minimal structural fluctuations. The mostly smaller dispersion along PC1 compared with PC2 suggests that the dominant motion was restricted primarily to one principal direction, but secondary motions were less pronounced. This behavior reflects a balance between structural stability and intrinsic flexibility, which allows limited conformational movement while preserving overall protein integrity. Also, the absence of widely scattered conformations supports the structural stability of the complex throughout the simulation, with no evidence of major conformational instability. The complex remained within a confined conformational space during the entire simulation period.

Principal component analysis (PCA) projection of the protein–ligand complex along the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) obtained from molecular dynamics simulation. The clustered distribution of conformations indicates restricted conformational space and stable dynamic behavior of the system.

These findings suggest that binding of PSH18 exerted minimal perturbation on the global conformational state of MAO-B and contributed to maintaining the protein within a restricted conformational range. This observation indicates that ligand binding does not induce significant structural alterations but rather stabilizes the protein within a preferred conformational ensemble.

Free Energy Landscape analysis

FEL of the protein–ligand complex was constructed based on the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2). Two-dimensional contour plots (Fig. 8A) and three-dimensional surface plots (Fig. 8B) were used as complementary representations to analyze the FEL of the complex. The two-dimensional FEL plot revealed several low-energy states (represented by deep blue regions), corresponding to the most stable conformations of the complex. These states were mostly concentrated in specific regions of the plot, showing that the protein sampled a limited number of conformational states rather than randomly exploring the entire conformational space. The three-dimensional FEL also showed the presence of distinct energy wells separated by energy barriers, pointing to regulated transitions between stable conformational states. This pattern indicates that the protein-ligand complex undergoes minor but major conformational adjustments while maintaining overall structural stability. The presence of multiple energy minima nearby suggests that the complex retains a degree of flexibility within a stable energetic framework, which is important for binding adaptability. In addition, the absence of highly scattered high energy regions indicates that no major destabilization occurred during the simulation. These observations are consistent with the PCA results, which showed confinement of the conformational space to defined clusters.

Dynamic Cross-Correlation Map

DCCM analysis was performed on the protein-ligand complex to see coordinated and anti-coordinated motions among protein residues during the simulation. The correlation map outlines the dynamic communication between residues within the protein structure. The DCCM plot (Fig. 9) showed distinct regions of positive correlation (blue regions) and negative correlation (red regions). Positive correlation indicates coordinated movement of residue pairs in the same direction, while negative correlation reflects anti-correlated motion in opposite directions. The major diagonal band of positive correlation corresponds to the self-correlation of individual residues. Additionally, several off-diagonal regions showed high positive correlation values, proving that residues located distantly in sequence space undergo coordinated motions, thereby contributing to structural stability and long-range communication within the protein. But, anti-correlated regions indicate compensatory movements that help maintain structural equilibrium. The absence of highly scattered or chaotic correlation patterns shows that ligand binding does not induce abnormal dynamic perturbations in the protein structure. Overall, the DCCM analysis showed cooperative residue motions and preserved dynamic integrity of the protein-ligand complex.

Dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) of residue motions for the protein-ligand complex obtained from molecular dynamics simulation. The colour scale ranges from − 1 to + 1, where blue indicates positively correlated motions, red indicates anti-correlated motions, and white represents uncorrelated movements, following coordinated dynamic behavior within the protein structure.

Conclusion

In this study, the PSH series was synthesized and evaluated for inhibitory activity against MAO-A and MAO-B. Most compounds belonging to the three-subseries exhibited selective inhibition toward MAO-B compared with MAO-A. Among them, PSH18 was identified as the most potent MAO-B inhibitor (IC50 = 0.95 ± 0.02 µM) with high selectivity. PSH6 and PSH2 also demonstrated notable MAO-B inhibitory activity. Kinetic analysis revealed that PSH18 acts as a competitive MAO-B inhibitor. Additionally, the dilution assay confirmed that PSH18 is a reversible inhibitor of MAO-B. Besides its favourable kinetic profile, PSH18 demonstrated significant BBB permeability and acceptable ADMET characteristics, supporting its potential as a CNS active compound. Molecular docking and MD simulations, together with MM-GBSA analysis, indicated that PSH18 forms a stable complex within the MAO-B binding pocket, primarily driven by hydrophobic and aromatic interactions. Interestingly, incorporation of the propargyl functional group in combination with the hydrazone moiety of the isatin scaffold appears to enhance selectivity, potency, and reversibility. Altogether, these findings suggest that PSH derivatives represent promising scaffolds for the development of potent and selective MAO-B inhibitors as potential therapeutic agents for PD and Alzheimer’s disease.
