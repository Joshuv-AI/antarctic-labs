Beyond a Single Direction: Chain-of-Thought Disrupts Simple Steering of Refusal
Kia-Jüng Yang, Dominik Meier, Jiachen Zhao, Terry Ruas, Bela Gipp

Abstract
Large reasoning models (LRMs) generate chain-of-thought (CoT) traces before producing final outputs, introducing a dynamic internal state that may complicate control mechanisms such as refusal. Unlike instruction-tuned LLMs, where refusal is mediated by a single directional subspace, refusal in large reasoning models (LRMs) additionally depends on the CoT. In DeepSeek-R1-Distill-LLaMA-8B, activation steering reverses refusal in only 39% of cases when the CoT is kept fixed, but removing the CoT entirely increases this to 70%, indicating that the CoT actively reinforces refusal. In a two-stage intervention where the model regenerates its CoT under activation steering, refusal is reversed in 94% of cases, while the resulting CoT alone retains 48% of this effect even after steering is removed. This suggests that the CoT can carry and reconstruct the compliance signal independently. These findings indicate that refusal in LRMs is jointly encoded in residual stream activations and CoT. This joint activation makes LRM more robust against activation-level interventions alone, but exposes CoT to a possible alternative surface attack.

Related Work

Refusal Mechanism in LLM.
Recent studies characterize refusal as a low-rank phenomenon mediated by a single ”refusal direction” in the residual stream
(
1
)
.
This mechanism operates independently of the model’s ability to recognize harm, as refusal and harmfulness are encoded separately in the activation space
(
16
)
.
Furthermore, these safety circuits are often anchored within template tokens rather than the instruction semantics
(
7
)
.
These works provide a detailed characterization of refusal in LLMs.
However, it remains unclear whether the same mechanisms hold in LRMs, where the model generates an intermediate CoT before producing a final response.
We investigate this by examining how activation-level refusal signals interact with the presence and generation of the CoT in LRMs.

Adversarial Attacks in LRM.
The explicit reasoning trace introduces novel attack surfaces.
Fabricated reasoning paths can trick models into treating safety checks as already passed
(
5
)
, and prepending long benign reasoning dilutes refusal signals before harmful instructions
(
17
)
.
Beyond prompt-level attacks, steering or ablating a caution direction specifically during CoT generation significantly increases compliance
(
15
)
.
These results show that manipulating the CoT, whether through prompt-level forgery or activation-level steering, is sufficient to override refusal.
However, they treat the CoT primarily as a passive target for intervention, without examining how it interacts with activation level signals or to what extent it can independently maintain or reconstruct refusal. We address this by systematically isolating the individual and joint contributions of residual stream activations and CoT generation to the refusal signal.

3.2
Activation Steering via Refusal Direction

Difference-in-Means.
To extract the refusal direction, we employ a difference-in-means approach
(
1
;
2
)

over the model’s residual stream activations.

Let
D_{\text{harmful}}^{(\text{train})}
denote a set of harmful instructions that the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B model refuses, and
D_{\text{harmless}}^{(\text{train})}
a set of harmless instructions. For each instruction
t
, we extract the residual stream activation
x^{(l)}(t)
at layer
l\in\{1,\dots,L\}
, at the final token position of the end-of-instruction (EOI) or end-of-think (EOT) tokens. The mean activation for each set is
computed as:

\displaystyle\mu^{(l)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|D_{\text{harmful}}^{(\text{train})}|}\sum_{t\,\in\,D_{\text{harmful}}^{(\text{train})}}x^{(l)}(t),

(1)

\displaystyle\nu^{(l)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|D_{\text{harmless}}^{(\text{train})}|}\sum_{t\,\in\,D_{\text{harmless}}^{(\text{train})}}x^{(l)}(t).

(2)

The refusal direction is then defined as the difference between these two means:

r^{(l)}=\mu^{(l)}-\nu^{(l)},

(3)

where
\mu^{(l)}
and
\nu^{(l)}
denote the mean residual stream activations over harmful and harmless instructions, respectively. This direction captures the dominant shift in representation space between refused and complied behaviors.

Activation Addition.
To steer model behavior toward or away from refusal, we apply activation addition along the extracted direction
r^{(l)}
.
Concretely, adding
r^{(l)}
to the residual stream shifts activations toward the mean harmful representation, thereby inducing refusal:

x^{(l)\prime}\leftarrow x^{(l)}+\alpha\cdot r^{(l)}.

(4)

While the magnitude of
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
determines the strength of the steering, its sign controls the direction.
While positive values shift activations toward the mean harmful representation and promote refusal, negative values steer them toward the mean harmless representation and encourage compliance.
The intervention is applied at layer
l
across all token positions.

4.4
Persistence of CoT Signals.

Persistence of CoT Signals.

To isolate whether the high compliance in
Section

4.3
is driven by the compliant CoT alone or requires both steering and a compliant CoT, we remove steering at inference time and replace the original CoT with one generated under steering.
In the absence of steering, compliance drops to 48%, compared to 94% when both steering and CoT regeneration are applied, indicating that the CoT encodes a partial compliance signal that persists independently of the steering intervention.
However, the substantial drop confirms that the CoT alone cannot fully suppress the refusal signal, as part of it remains encoded in the activations and can only be reduced by additional steering intervention.
These results show that the CoT is an active component of the refusal mechanism, capable of either reinforcing refusal or amplifying compliance depending on its content.

5
Discussion

Our results reveal that the refusal signal in LRMs is not solely encoded at template tokens, but is jointly maintained by residual stream activations and the CoT.
Steering alone yields only 39–43% compliance.
Yet, combining steering with a compliant CoT reaches 94%, highlighting the CoT as an active contributor to the refusal mechanism.
Two findings reinforce the joint encoding of the refusal signal. First, suppressing the CoT raises compliance to 70%, showing that the original reasoning trace actively reconstructs the refusal signal. Secondly, reusing a compliant CoT without steering retains 48% compliance, showing that the trace independently carries a partial compliance signal.
Unlike LLMs, where refusal is mediated by a single directional subspace
(
1
)
, refusal in LRMs is distributed across activations and CoT.
This distribution makes LRM more resistant to activation-level interventions, but introduces the CoT as an additional attack surface.
Our results suggest that detecting a refusal signal in the activations and suppressing CoT could serve as an effective defense mechanism, preventing the CoT from being exploited to override or reconstruct the compliance signal.

6
Limitations

Qualitative Analysis and Faithfulness of Chain-of-Thought.
A qualitative inspection reveals systematic differences in reasoning, such as unsteered CoT emphasizes ethical justifications while steered traces omit them. We did not verify if these patterns hold across all samples. Crucially, our analysis does not establish whether these generated CoT are ”faithful” or causally responsible for the final model behavior. Although we observe a correlation between ethical reasoning and refusal, we have not tested whether removing these specific reasoning steps would increase compliance. Determining the causal impact of these patterns remains an important direction for future work.

Single-model Evaluation.

All experiments are conducted on a single model (DeepSeek-R1-LLaMA-8B). While this allows for a controlled analysis, it remains unclear whether the observed effects generalize to other chain-of-thought models. Evaluating across a broader set of models is necessary to assess the robustness of our findings. Proprietary models do not make the reasoning available in unaltered form, making evaluation there impossible.

Refusal Classification

To ensure reliable evaluation of refusal behavior, we compare two approaches for automatically classifying model responses as either refused or complied.

We construct a dataset of 50 harmful instructions and manually annotate each corresponding response with a binary label (refusal vs. compliance). These annotations serve as ground truth for evaluating automatic classification methods.

We consider the following approaches:

•

Phrase-based heuristic.
Responses are classified as refusals if they contain predefined phrases (e.g., “I’m sorry”, “I cannot help with that”,…).

•

Model-based classifier (Llama-3.2-8B-Guard).
A dedicated safety model is used to predict refusal behavior.
