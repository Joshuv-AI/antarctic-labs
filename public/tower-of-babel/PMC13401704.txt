The Persian Version of the Social Participation Questionnaire: Translation, Cross‐Cultural Adaptation, and Psychometric Evaluation Using Categorical Principal Component Analysis (CATPCA): A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Neda Ahmadzadeh Tori, Mina Galeshi, Hamid Sharif‐Nia, Saeed Ghoorchaiy, Safieh Faghani, Zahed Rezaei, Aram Tirgar, Dariush Moslemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSocial participation is recognized as an important determinant of public health and has been associated with cognitive function, quality of life, well‐being, and reduced mortality. This study aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and evaluate the psychometric properties of the Persian version of the Social Participation Questionnaire (SPQ‐P).MethodsThis cross‐sectional psychometric validation study was conducted from February to June 2024 in Babol, Iran. The SPQ was translated and culturally adapted into Persian following standard procedures. Categorical Principal Component Analysis (CATPCA) was used to assess construct validity, while internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha coefficients.ResultsA total of 500 participants with a mean age of 31.34 ± 6.66 years were included. CATPCA identified a two‐component structure explaining 68.4% of the total variance (95% CI: 62.1–68.5%). The first component (42.1% of the variance) demonstrated strong loadings (> 0.70) for Q2, Q3, Q8, and Q9, representing General Social Engagement. The second component (23.2% of the variance) showed high loadings for Q4, Q5, and Q10, representing Context‐Specific Participation. Cluster analysis identified three distinct participation profiles. Internal consistency was acceptable for both components (Cronbach's α = 0.76 [95% CI: 0.72–0.80] and 0.68 [95% CI: 0.63–0.73], respectively, and discriminant validity was supported by low cross‐loadings (< 0.30).ConclusionThe SPQ‐P demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability for assessing social participation among Persian‐speaking populations. The structure of the identified components confirms the multidimensional nature of social participation, especially in the two dimensions of “general social participation” and “context‐dependent participation”. In addition, cluster analysis revealed distinct participation profiles, including highly engaged, context‐specialized, and minimally involved groups, highlighting the questionnaire's ability to discriminate between different patterns of social participation. These findings support the applicability of the SPQ‐P as a useful instrument for public health research and population‐based assessments.

Background

Social participation, increasingly recognized as a modifiable and critical determinant of health, goes beyond its traditional understanding as simply social interaction [1]. There is a growing body of research demonstrating an important relationship between social participation and cognitive function, well‐being, quality of life, mortality, and morbidity [2]. Promoting social participation is a key policy recommendation of the WHO, particularly in response to the challenges of an aging population [3]. However, the importance of social participation extends far beyond geriatric populations. It is now recognized as a critical determinant of health and well‐being across the entire lifespan, from adolescence through to older adulthood [4, 5].

The concept of social participation used in medical research has been adopted from the fields of geriatrics, disability research, and rehabilitation [5]. These concepts assume that individual diseases, symptoms, or aging processes alter or even limit an individual's ability to participate in social activities. Conversely, social participation is generally associated with positive health outcomes. While the benefits of social participation are particularly pronounced in later life—where it can mitigate age‐related declines in health and function [6] —its protective and promotive effects are equally vital for younger and middle‐aged adults. For these groups, robust social participation is associated with better mental health outcomes, lower allostatic load, and the prevention of chronic disease onset [7]. From a lifespan developmental perspective, social participation is a dynamic process that evolves with an individual's changing life circumstances, roles, and needs [8].

Activity theory posits that successful aging requires maintaining the interests and social interactions of mid‐adulthood [9]. Extending this logic, social integration theory suggests that individuals of all ages navigate role changes to maintain social capital and enhance quality of life [10, 11, 12]. A cross‐sectional study examined the association between the type, frequency, and independence of social participation with physical and mental health; results showed that only independent and appropriately frequent (i.e., weekly or monthly, rather than annually) social participation positively impacts mental health [13]. The optimal effect of participation type and frequency on loneliness may relate to individual characteristics such as age, socioeconomic status, and personality traits [14].

Social participation, defined as engagement in activities fostering interaction within a community or society, remains relevant even during pandemics. While encompassing a broad range of interactions, some researchers narrow its scope to productive, social, and recreational activities [6]. Furthermore, the main dimensions of most definitions of social participation are activities and social interaction [2].

To our knowledge, numerous instruments measure or monitor individuals' social participation, such as the CPQ [15], the I‐CPQ [16], the FAI [17], the WHO DAS [18], the LIFE‐H [19], and the SPS of the Chinese Adult Social Participation Assessment Questionnaire [20]. Some instruments were developed for elderly populations and others for children, making them unsuitable for other healthy populations. Other instruments are more sensitive to patient populations, especially those with specific diseases [21, 22]. Social functioning refers to an individual's ability to perform social activities such as personal care and occupational tasks, while social participation refers to the level to which an individual participates in social activities [2].

Few instruments can be applied to a wide range of populations, and those that exist are culturally dependent. For example, the Social Participation Questionnaire for the Elderly was developed in Iran [23]. Although similarities exist in individuals' social activities, cultural differences require different activity patterns. However, this scale primarily assesses social participation in elderly groups. Individual activities are determined by life habits and preferences, and the types of activities are numerous, making social activities endless. The WHO policy proposal addressing population aging challenges specifically states that social participation is crucial for maintaining the health of the general population as well as improving functioning and social ties [2]. Given the absence of a culturally relevant instrument for assessing social participation in Iran across the adult lifespan, this study aims to validate and adapt the SPQ for the Iranian context. The SPQ was selected because its broad conceptualization of social participation—encompassing productive, social, and recreational activities—is not restricted to any single age group [6]. Recognizing the influence of sociocultural factors, the research focused on the translation and psychometric evaluation of the SPQ to ensure its suitability and accuracy for the general adult Iranian population (aged 18 and over). Validating this tool for a broad adult demographic is essential for future epidemiological and public health research aimed at understanding the determinants and outcomes of social participation at every stage of adulthood in Iran. This is particularly crucial given potential cultural differences in interpreting questionnaire items and the current lack of established reliability and validity for a Persian version of the SPQ (SPQ‐P).

Methods

Study Design and Setting

The present study is methodological research. This study was a validation study that consisted of translation, cultural adaptation, and evaluation of psychometric properties (face, content and construct validity) of the instrument. Data were collected using a convenience sampling method from February 2025 to June 2025 in Babol, Iran.

Participants (Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria)

The study population consisted of individuals aged 18 years and above residing in Babol. Inclusion criteria included being 18 years and above and willing to participate. Exclusion criteria included having cognitive impairments or serious underlying diseases.

This study employed a sample of 365 individuals, guided by Munro's [24] recommendations for construct validity, suggesting 5–10 participants per question. The need for two distinct samples to assess construct validity necessitated this number, gathered via convenience sampling. Participants received comprehensive explanations of the study's aims before completing the questionnaire, collected directly by the researcher. The researcher's presence ensured immediate clarification of uncertainties, minimized misunderstandings, maintained high response rates, and facilitated prompt collection of completed questionnaires, mitigating missing data. Emphasis on voluntary participation and researcher oversight enhanced the reliability and validity of the collected data.

Original Questionnaire

The SPQ was developed in China; its initial items were developed from a theoretical framework, literature review, and expert Delphi consultation. Item analysis, EFA, CFA, criterion validity, construct reliability, and internal consistency reliability were conducted to investigate the psychometric properties of the SPQ, which is an 11‐item instrument including eleven different types of social activities. These items are grouped into three dimensions: daily living activities, sports and leisure activities, and community service activities. The SPQ has acceptable validity and reliability for assessing individuals' social participation and can be used in clinical and population‐based research. The total SPQ score ranges from 0 to 99, with higher scores indicating higher social participation. Meanwhile, the SPQ can also calculate scores for frequency of social participation (range: 0–33) and level of interaction with others (range: 1–33). It is a valid and reliable tool across settings, enabling support and assessment of disease prevention, health promotion, rehabilitation, and continuity of care [2].

Translation Procedure

Following receipt of written consent from the scale designer, the researcher proceeded with the translation process using a forward‐backward translation methodology [2]. Initially, two translators proficient in both Persian and English independently translated the questionnaire into Persian. These translations were then synthesized to create a consolidated version, with the most suitable translation chosen for each item. Subsequently, the Persian questionnaire was back‐translated into English by two independent agetors unfamiliar with the original questionnaire. After review, correction, revision, and merging of these back‐translations, the resulting version was submitted to the original questionnaire designer, who confirmed semantic equivalence.

Evaluation of Psychometric

The psychometric properties of the instrument were evaluated for validity and reliability. Face, content, and construct validity were assessed. Reliability was determined using Cronbach's alpha coefficient, average inter‐item correlation (AIC), McDonald's omega, composite validity, and maximum reliability H.

Face Validity

Face validity, a crucial aspect of scale development, was rigorously evaluated through both qualitative and quantitative methods in a sample of fifteen individuals. The qualitative assessment involved surveying participants on eleven items, followed by inquiries regarding the difficulty, relevance, and ambiguity of each question. Feedback obtained from these surveys led to revisions of the items by the research team; however, no items were deemed necessary to remove. Subsequently, quantitative face validity was established by calculating the impact score of each item, derived from the formula: impact score = frequency (%) × importance. Participants rated the importance of each item on a five‐point Likert scale, ranging from “not important” to “very important.” The impact score was calculated by multiplying the frequency of participants who rated an item with a score of 4 or 5 by the mean importance score of that item. An impact score exceeding 1.5 was the threshold for considering an item acceptable, demonstrating a robust level of perceived relevance and importance. This dual approach ensured a comprehensive evaluation of face validity, strengthening the scale's credibility and appropriateness for its intended purpose [25, 26].

Content Validity

Content validity was rigorously assessed through both qualitative and quantitative methods. Initially, qualitative insights were gathered via interviews with fifteen subject matter experts, including health professionals, psychologists, sociologists, and physicians. These interviews focused on refining the instrument's grammar, phrasing, item placement, and scoring accuracy [27]. Subsequently, a quantitative content validity assessment was performed, calculating the CVR and CVI for individual items. Experts rated item importance on a three‐point scale, and CVR was calculated using the formula CVR = (ne – [N/2])/(N/2). A minimum acceptable CVR of 0.56 was applied, based on the Lawshe table for a panel of twelve experts. CVI was determined by evaluating item relevance on a four‐point scale. Items achieving a CVI above 0.79 were deemed acceptable, those between 0.70 and 0.79 warranted revision, and those below 0.70 were rejected [28].

The S‐CVI and S‐CVR were then calculated by averaging the CVI and CVR values, respectively. An S‐CVI exceeding 0.9 was considered satisfactory. Finally, a modified kappa statistic (K) was employed to evaluate inter‐rater agreement among the expert panel, with items exhibiting a K of 0.7 or higher considered adequate. This multi‐faceted approach ensured a comprehensive evaluation of the scale's content validity [29].

Reliability

Cronbach's alpha, McDonald's omega coefficient, CR, and MaxR were calculated to gauge internal consistency and construct reliability [14]. If the scale's Cronbach's alpha was more than 0.7 and CR and MaxR were more than 0.7, it was deemed to have good internal consistency and construct reliability [27].

Statistical Analysis

We employed CATPCA to examine the underlying structure of the ordinal survey data (11 items, Q1–Q11) using IBM SPSS 28 with the CATPCA module. This optimal scaling technique is particularly suited for Likert‐type variables as it transforms categorical data into continuous dimensions while preserving their inherent relationships [30]. Prior to analysis, we confirmed the absence of missing values across all 500 cases and specified all variables as ordinal, given their 0–3 response scales. Principal normalization was applied to maintain component interpretability.

Component extraction followed Kaiser's criterion (eigenvalue > 1), supported by scree plot inspection, which clearly indicated a two‐component solution. Varimax rotation was then applied to maximize interpretability, with item loadings ≥ 0.50 considered substantively meaningful [31]. To further validate the component structure, we computed Cronbach's α for internal consistency and performed K‐means clustering (k = 3) on component scores using squared Euclidean distance, with cluster stability verified through 10 random initializations. All analyses were cross‐validated using R's FactoMineR package, with assumptions of linearity and variance homogeneity confirmed via residual diagnostics.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

The final sample consisted of 500 participants (mean age = 31.34 ± 6.66 years; 95% CI: 30.76 to 31.92; range: 18–50 years). Among them, 329 (65.8%) were female and 171 (34.2%) were male. Regarding marital status, 274 participants (54.8%) were single, and 206 (41.2%) were married. The majority of participants were literate (316 participants, 63.2%). Income levels were categorized as low (below basic needs; n = 188, 37.6%), moderate (meeting basic needs; n = 170, 34.0%), and adequate (above basic needs; n = 142, 28.4%). A history of chronic illness was reported by 73 participants (14.6%).

Rationale for CATPCA

Categorical Principal Component Analysis (CATPCA) was employed to examine the underlying structure of the ordinal survey data (11 items, Q1–Q11) using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 28) with the CATPCA module. This optimal scaling technique was preferred over traditional PCA or Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) due to its ability to handle ordinal data (Likert‐type 0–3 responses) and non‐linear relationships through optimal scaling, without assuming multivariate normality [30, 32]. Prior to analysis, we confirmed the absence of missing values across all 500 cases and specified all variables as ordinal. Principal normalization was applied to maintain component interpretability. Component extraction followed Kaiser's criterion (eigenvalue > 1), supported by scree plot inspection, which clearly indicated a two‐component solution. Varimax rotation was then applied to maximize interpretability, with item loadings ≥ 0.40 considered substantively meaningful [33]. All analyses were cross‐validated using R's FactoMineR package.

Dimensional Structure

The CATPCA revealed a stable two‐component structure explaining 68.4% of the total variance. The first component (Eigenvalue = 5.21) accounted for 47.4% of the variance, while the second component (Eigenvalue = 2.31) explained an additional 21.0%. While Component 1 met all convergent validity criteria, Component 2's AVE of 0.48 fell slightly below the 0.50 benchmark. However, as noted below, its composite reliability (CR = 0.74) supports its retention. Although the AVE for Component 2 was slightly below the recommended threshold, its CR remained acceptable (CR = 0.74). According to psychometric recommendations, convergent validity may still be considered adequate when AVE is marginally below 0.50, but CR exceeds 0.70, particularly when the items are theoretically coherent and conceptually representative of the construct. Therefore, given the satisfactory composite reliability and the strong theoretical relevance of the items, Component 2 was retained in the final model.

Context‐Specialized 40 out of 500 participants (28.0%): High PC2, low PC1.

Minimally Involved 200 out of 500 participants (40.0%): Low scores on both components.

Thus, the structure of the identified components confirms the multidimensional nature of social participation, especially in the two dimensions of “general social participation” and “context‐dependent participation”. In addition, cluster analysis revealed distinct participation profiles, including highly engaged, context‐specialized, and minimally involved groups.

Discussion

The results of the CATPCA showed that the structure of social participation in the data under study can be separated into two distinct and meaningful dimensions. These two components, named “general social participation” and “context‐dependent participation,” respectively, explain more than 65% of the total variance, indicating the strength and adequacy of the extracted model.

The first component, which had high loadings on questions related to broad and general participation (42.1% of the variance), indicates the extent to which individuals generally interact with their social environment. Individuals with high scores on this component tend to attend gatherings, participate in social activities, and have ongoing contact with different groups. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have defined social participation as a comprehensive and multifaceted behavior. For example, in the study by Amagasa et al., social participation was classified using factor analysis into two types: community involvement (volunteer activities, social events, senior clubs) and individual relationships (friendship, relationships with family and friends, hobbies) [1]. Also, in the study by Stevelink et al. (2013), which investigated the psychometric properties of the Social Participation Scale among people with different disability conditions in eastern Nepal, the results of exploratory analysis also showed a two‐factor structure of “work‐related participation” and “general participation” [2]. Furthermore, in a study by Mélanie Levasseur et al. (2022), aiming to provide an interdisciplinary consensual definition of social participation, social participation was defined as an individual's participation in activities that provide interaction with others in social life and in important shared spaces, which evolves with the time and resources available, based on the social context and what individuals want and find meaningful [3].

The second component, which refers to specific partnerships dependent on specific conditions or contexts (23.2% of variance), shows that people in certain situations or contexts exhibit different collaborative behaviors. These people do not participate much in public communities but show more social interactions in specific meetings or events. The second component corresponds to findings of other research that highlights the role of specific conditions and contexts in participatory behaviors. For example, the study by Bereket Roba Gamo et al. (2022) showed that community capacity, respondents' social status, skills in assessing community problems, sense of belonging to the community, and amount of social networking affect individuals' social participation [34]. Also, in the qualitative study of Mohammadi Shahbalaghi et al. (2021) on the elderly, the three main classes of the elderly, family participation, and community capacity for the social participation of the elderly were recognized as underlying factors for promoting and developing social participation of the elderly. In other words, social participation of the elderly in these specific areas increases [4]. In addition, another study examining social factors affecting citizenship in urban management in Iran emphasized the importance of targeted participation, which aligns with our results [5].

This emphasis on the importance of context in social participation reflects the complexity of participatory behaviors, which cannot be analyzed in terms of a single, simple dimension. These two orthogonal dimensions highlight the importance of considering the diversity and multidimensionality of social participation. A recent meta‐analysis found that considering the multidimensionality of social participation leads to a stronger association between social participation and health [6]. In Douglas et al.‘s (2017) study, three concepts of social participation (i.e., social connections, informal social participation, and volunteering) were defined, all of which contribute to the association between social participation and health through shared mechanisms of social support and social cohesion with the wider community. It has also been emphasized that using an instrument that can address these separate concepts will help health services researchers determine the relative impact of each form of participation on health [7].

Also, Hiroyuki Hashidate et al., in their study, since there are many factors associated with social participation and there are many restrictions on social participation evaluation, suggested that social participation evaluation should be based on the cultural and social characteristics of each society [8]. Cluster analysis identified three distinct profiles from participants, including high participation groups, context‐focused participation, and minimal participation. This classification can be used as a basis for designing targeted interventions and social policy making. For example, for the group with minimal participation, incentive programs and participatory training can be effective, while for the context‐focused group, enhancing participation opportunities in specific fields is important.

Sampling from a particular society and the lack of examination of further contextual factors such as demographic variables may limit the generalization of the results. Also, data were collected based on self‐report, which may be affected by respondents' bias. It is suggested that in future research, in addition to examining the dimensions of social participation in more diverse samples, the factors affecting participation changes over time should also be studied. Also, combining qualitative and quantitative methods can help a deeper and more comprehensive understanding of the dimensions of social participation in tool design.

Limitations and Future Directions

“Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, because the sample was drawn from a specific population without controlling for broader demographic variables, the generalizability of our findings may be limited. Second, the reliance on self‐reported data introduces the potential for response bias. To address these limitations, future research should evaluate the dimensions of social participation across more diverse cohorts and employ longitudinal designs to examine how these participation patterns change over time. Finally, integrating qualitative and quantitative approaches (mixed methods) in future tool design would facilitate a deeper, more comprehensive understanding of social participation.”

Conclusions

This study shows that social participation is a multidimensional phenomenon and that various factors affect it, and that social, cultural, and individual contexts should be carefully considered in analysis and policy‐making related to it. An examination of the components shows that the questionnaire tool and the selected analysis model have appropriate reliability and validity. Also, the use of the CATPCA method, due to its compatibility with Likert ordinal data, has preserved the inherent structure of the data and provided reliable results. However, revalidation is necessary in each new cultural environment in which this tool is to be used.
