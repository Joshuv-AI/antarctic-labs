Item comprehension and content coverage of patient-reported outcome measures for long COVID: a qualitative study
Authors: Christina K Zigler, Charis Yousefian, Rikki Mangrum, Maliyah Faulstich, Sean M O’Brien, Christine Maughan, Marta Cerda, Lucinda Bateman, Cyndya A Shibao, Barry Make, Leonard A Jason, Kevin P Weinfurt, Kanecia O Zimmerman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeFit-for-purpose clinical outcome assessments are essential to inform medical product development, but outcome measures for Long COVID, a new and devastating phenomenon, have not yet been established. The objective of this study was to evaluate item comprehension and content coverage of four selected patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) for use in a Long COVID clinical trial platform.MethodsA noninterventional, cross-sectional qualitative cognitive debriefing study of English-speaking adults with Long COVID was conducted, with interviews taking place virtually between August and November 2024. Recruitment was managed by a research firm. The main outcomes of the study were participant comprehension and relevance of the selected PROMs, including symptoms and functioning as measured by modified versions of the DePaul Symptom Questionnaire–Post-Exertional Malaise, Everyday Cognition Scale 2 questionnaire, Composite Autonomic Symptom Score-31, and Orthostatic Hypotension Questionnaire.ResultsParticipants were able to comprehend most items and indicated they were relevant to their experiences with Long COVID. Participants identified potential enhancements to the measures such as simplifying instructions, modifying item wording, and reducing PROM length. Interviewers observed cognitive burden during some of the interviews.ConclusionsBased on the overall support for comprehension and relevance of items within each measure, use of these measures within the Long COVID clinical trial platform was generally supported. Future work, including qualitative and quantitative studies, could improve the experience of PROM data collection in this context. Clinical trial protocols for Long COVID should carefully consider burden on participants when collecting PROMs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-026-04340-7.Keywords: Long COVID, Patient-reported outcome measures, Qualitative research, Item comprehension, Content coverage

Introduction

Eighteen percent of US adults have had acute COVID-19 disease followed by symptoms that lasted ≥ 3 months [1], meeting the current definition for post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 or Long COVID [2, 3]. Data from the National Center for Health Statistics (September, 2024) indicate that over 5% of adults in the US may currently be living with Long COVID, although estimates in some states are as high as 10.4% (Mississippi, 95% CI 6.7%–15.1%) [1]. Hundreds of symptoms are associated with Long COVID, including respiratory, nervous system, mental health, cardiovascular, and gastrointestinal [4]; almost every organ system can be affected [5]. Some individuals experience autonomic dysfunction (orthostatic intolerance symptoms such as dizziness, orthostatic tachycardia, inappropriate sinus tachycardia, or syncope), exercise intolerance (exhaustion or fatigue even from minimal exertion), postexertional malaise (worsening of both symptoms and function following even minor physical or mental exertion), or cognitive dysfunction (brain fog, memory issues, difficulty thinking or concentrating) [4, 6]. These symptoms can result in substantial, persistent distress and impairment of functioning [7] that can be “devastating” [8]. Despite the established negative impacts, there are still no approved treatments for Long COVID.

Clinical trials represent the gold standard for evaluating therapeutic options for conditions like Long COVID, and fit-for-purpose measures of patients’ health and well-being are needed to inform medical product development and regulatory decision making [9–11]. Accordingly, the Researching COVID to Enhance Recovery Clinical Trials (RECOVER-CT) initiative was created by the National Institutes of Health to evaluate interventions for treatment of Long COVID across a number of platform protocols. Evaluation of effective therapeutic options in Long COVID is complicated by the differential presentation in symptoms from one individual to another [12], which makes the measurement of treatment effects challenging. Biomarkers for Long COVID would be a solution to, this issue, but research is still in its infancy [13, 14]. Thus, the RECOVER-CT initiative prioritized direct self-report from participants, which in turn created a need for psychometric evidence supporting outcome assessments.

To address this need, the RECOVER-CT initiative selected a set of existing patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) for use as clinical trial endpoints for the adult Long COVID population. Concepts of interest were chosen based on a list of specific symptoms that were experienced by patients and that were targeted by the interventions in the first round of platform trials. A literature review was conducted, selecting a shortlist of existing measures that had evidence supporting validity in similar patient populations or had published studies utilizing the measure with patients diagnosed with Long COVID (Table 1) [15–20]. Since Long COVID is an emergent health condition, the selected PROMs were not previously qualitatively evaluated in the adult Long COVID population, leaving a gap in evidence supporting these measures as fit for purpose. Thus, in parallel to the phase 2 clinical trials, the RECOVER-CT initiative also began evaluating the PROMs. Our objective was to evaluate the item comprehension and content coverage (also known as content validity) [21] of the selected PROMs through descriptive and formative cognitive debriefing interviews with adults with Long COVID.

Methods

Study design and measures

This study was a noninterventional, cross-sectional qualitative analysis of responses from adults who self-reported a diagnosis of Long COVID. The selected PROMs included the DePaul Symptom Questionnaire–Post-Exertional Malaise (DSQ-PEM) [15, 22], Everyday Cognition Scale 2 (ECog2) questionnaire [17, 23], Composite Autonomic Symptom Score-31 (COMPASS-31) measure [19, 24], and Orthostatic Hypotension Questionnaire (OHQ) [20] (see Table 1). These PROMs were selected pragmatically for RECOVER-CT protocols designed to target these specific symptom domains and were not intended to be considered a universal Long COVID outcome set. Other items and anchor measures were also included in the cognitive interviews (results not reported in this paper). PROMs were modified, when necessary, to align with clinical trial designs. For example, recall periods were standardized to “the past 7 days” (the PROM modifications are listed in Table 1).

Study participants

Study recruitment, screening, enrollment, scheduling, and compensation of participants were managed by a third-party professional research recruiting firm (Global Perspectives, an IQVIA business). All participants provided informed consent and were compensated for their participation. The Duke University Health System institutional review board approved the study.

Eligible participants were English-speaking adults in the US who reported moderate to severe autonomic dysfunction, exercise intolerance, or cognitive symptoms in the previous 7 days related to Long COVID. Long COVID status was based on self-report responses to a series of screening questions for the RECOVER-VITAL (Viral Persistence and Reactivation, and Immune Dysregulation) trial [6]. Exclusion criteria included one or more clinically significant diseases that, in the opinion of the investigator, might interfere with study objectives (e.g., heart disease) or compromise the ability to give informed consent (e.g., dementia) and current or recent substance abuse as indicated by a score of ≥ 2 points on the CAGE Adapted to Include Drugs scale [25]. Individuals who rated all their symptoms as none/mild or severe were excluded in order to purposively select individuals who were likely to report a range of varied experiences as those with uniformly mild or uniformly severe symptoms may have been less likely to be able to describe much variation in their symptom experience (it should be noted that no individuals reported uniformly severe symptoms during screening).

Specific recruitment targets, based on prevalence data [3] and feedback from the study team, were established to ensure heterogeneity of participants across age, sex, race, and ethnicity. The screening questions were used to assign participants to a symptom cluster (i.e., autonomic dysfunction, cognitive dysfunction, exercise intolerance). Although PROMs were intended to be evaluated as a set, the total number of items across all the selected PROMs was too large to complete in a 90-min interview. Symptom cluster assignment determined which PROMs would be reviewed during debriefing so that at least 8 participants [26] would review each measure.

Data collection

Cognitive debriefing methodology evaluates common sources of response error in surveys, including problems with instrument comprehension and interpretation. Using these methods, the study team conducted interviews using a semi-structured interview guide. The interview objectives were to gather evidence of the comprehension/relevance of items, appropriateness of the recall period, and content coverage.

Methods for data collection and analysis adhered to best practices for establishment of content validity evidence within medical product evaluation [9, 10, 15, 27]. Interviews were conducted via the Microsoft Teams web-conferencing system and were professionally transcribed by Opal Transcription. All transcripts in their entirety underwent dual deidentification review.

Qualitative analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study sample in the aggregate. Analysts recorded analytic judgments of whether each participant understood the items and response options, and recorded information about any difficulties with comprehension or response selection as well as participant comments on the relevance of PROM content and overall feedback on the PROMs (e.g., suggestions for wording, missing concepts, or perceived redundancies). Supportive transcript quotations for each analytic judgment were also recorded in the data file. Cognitive debriefing data were tabulated in an item tracking matrix. Adequate support for item comprehension and relevance was set at ≥ 75% of participants per item. For content coverage, the team tabulated symptoms related to the concept of interest targeted by the PROM under discussion, as well as additional symptoms mentioned by participants.

Results

Interviews were conducted in August–November 2024. Sixty-nine individuals were screened (8 did not meet eligibility criteria) for a final sample of 30 participants of varied age, sex, race, ethnic background, educational background, and number of self-reported COVID infections (Table 2). Symptom cluster assignments were made such that each cluster included 10 participants. However, all participants endorsed symptoms in all 3 symptom clusters by at least rating one symptom as “mild.” Additionally, 20 participants were eligible for more than one cluster. At least 8 participants reviewed each measure (Table 3), with the most frequently reviewed measure being the DSQ-PEM (n = 13).

Item comprehension

Across the DSQ-PEM, ECog2, COMPASS-31, and OHQ, participants understood the items (Table 3; Tables S1–S4 in the Supplementary Appendix). For the ECog2, one question (memory item 5, “Avoiding telling stories or asking questions multiple times”) was misunderstood by half of participants (4/8). For example, one participant (P39) was unsure what “telling stories” referred to, stating: “I guess I’m just asking like what is it specifically referring to when it says the ‘telling stories’… I was just kind of confused on the specifics behind it.” There were no major issues with comprehension reported across the other items.

Although there was support for the understanding or comprehension of items, some participants did mention preferences and suggestions for updated item wording. For example, 5 of 8 participants expressed dislike of the term “goofy” in COMPASS-31 Item 1 (“In the past 7 days, have you ever felt faint, dizzy, ‘goofy,’ or had difficulty thinking…?”). As one participant (P20) noted, “I mean, for the most part, they’re all clear except for the ‘goofy’ one…. I wouldn’t explain myself ever being the way you think about goofy.” Likewise, half of the participants reviewing the OHQ were unsure if “head discomfort” in item 6 was related to a headache (e.g., P37: “…if they are interested in more specific symptoms whether it’s headache or it’s muscle pain in the head and neck area, they might want to clarify or break it down because that will also change my answer.” See Tables S5–S8 in the Supplementary Appendix for more detailed descriptions of participant-reported suggestions across measures.

Relevance

When asked about the relevance of concepts, participants discussed the impact that Long COVID had on their everyday lives (Table 3). As one participant (P16) shared during review of the ECog2, “When you have the long-term issues of memory, everything is important to address because all of our lifestyles are different where memory is significant to our lives. So, just because going grocery shopping may not be important for someone else, it’s still a day-to-day task that affects our everyday lives … all of these things, all of these questions asked, affect us daily, multiple times a day, and it affects people connected close to us.”

All individual items on the DSQ-PEM (Table S9 in the Supplementary Appendix) and the OHQ (Table S4 in the Supplementary Appendix) and all but one of the items on the ECog2 (Table S10 in the Supplementary Appendix) were endorsed as relevant by ≥ 75% of the participants (Table 3). For the COMPASS-31, there were specific items that < 75% of participants endorsed as relevant to their experiences: Specifically, vomiting (item 14), constipation (item 20), and bladder function (item 24) were reported as not relevant or important by 3 (38%) participants, while difficulty passing urine (item 25) and difficulty completely emptying their bladder (26) were reported as not relevant or important by 5 (63%) participants.

Recall period/stability of symptoms

Most participants reported that a 7-day recall period was appropriate in relation to how they think of their symptoms of Long COVID over time. Asked about any issues with remembering symptoms over 7 days, one participant (P02) reviewing the OHQ replied, “No. Very crystal clear what happened.” A few individuals indicated issues with the recall period when discussing the COMPASS-31 (n = 1), DSQ-PEM (n = 3), or ECog2 (n = 3). One participant (P05, during a DSQ-PEM discussion) stated that they answered the questions based on their typical symptom experience because it was too challenging to recall what had happened only in the past 7 days: “I do have kind of a time blindness that I didn’t experience before where you can say something is one week and I think it could be the last month.” Another participant (P11, DSQ-PEM discussion) noted that because of how quickly things can change, a 24-h recall period would be more appropriate: “Because [for] people with Long COVID, 24 h can mean a world of difference, honestly, depending on if you’ve gotten any rest.”

There were a few participants who could recall their symptoms adequately over the specified recall period but expressed a preference for a longer time frame. For example, one participant (P02) shared, “I think it could be a little longer … 2 weeks would give you a little bit more leeway, go with some more symptoms” (during an OHQ discussion).

Content coverage

During each measure discussion, some participants identified associated symptoms they felt were important but missing from the PROM (Fig. 1). These included difficulty breathing when discussing the DSQ-PEM and fatigue when discussing the COMPASS-31. Other additional symptoms that were not directly related to the concepts targeted by the specific PROM under discussion included respiratory issues and loss of taste and smell (Fig. 1). While not included on the PROM being discussed, some of the additional symptoms were included in one of the other three PROMs (for example, fatigue is measured within the OHQ) and others were included in the RECOVER clinical trials data collection battery (those included in data collection are marked with asterisk in Fig. 1).

Other findings

Participants suggested simplifying and shortening the PROMs where possible. For example, one said, “It’s almost way too repetitive to where, honestly, people with Long COVID, a lot of us have other underlying health conditions to where it just exacerbates them to … the simpler the better, if that makes sense” (P11, COMPASS-31 discussion). A few participants said that people with Long COVID, especially those with cognitive symptoms, may find it exhausting to complete a long, complicated questionnaire. Throughout data collection, interviewers also observed that some participants exhibited signs of cognitive strain, including becoming visibly tired, having to reread items, and demonstrating difficulty expressing their ideas.

Discussion

The cognitive debriefing results demonstrate that most study participants understood the items included in the PROMs they reviewed and found the concepts included in the measures relevant to their experiences with Long COVID. Participants were generally able to rate their experiences for each of the measures using the provided response scales and recall periods. These results support the overall content of the modified DSQ-PEM, ECog2, COMPASS-31, and OHQ within the RECOVER Long COVID clinical trial platform. This is important and timely—these measures are currently being used in a number of Long COVID research and clinical settings [6]. For example, since initiation of this study, the DSQ-PEM has been utilized to track intensity of Long COVID symptoms over time [28] as well as to evaluate the efficacy of interventions [29, 30].

Participants raised issues with terminology for only a limited number of items. These issues were infrequent and largely did not affect those participants’ ability to respond to the items, underscoring the overall robust validity of the content of these PROMs. However, these findings may provide direction to enhance comprehension, reduce participant burden, or improve the reliability or precision of scores. As such, recommendations are included in Table 4. For example, based on suggestions from multiple participants (see Table S5), we recommend combining the three bladder items on COMPASS-31 into one item and revision of the term “goofy” within the COMPASS-31.

There was also support for content coverage, especially for the set of PROMs. For example, a participant identified cognitive symptoms as important during the discussion of the COMPASS-31; cognitive symptoms are captured by the ECog2. For clinical trials, along with a set of established multi-item PROMs, we recommend collecting additional single-item frequency or intensity scales to capture other important symptoms such as loss of taste and smell, without adding too much burden on participants. A set of single-item scales written using US Food and Drug Administration COVID-19 guidance [31] is currently a part of all the RECOVER-CT trials [6].

Interviewers observed that there were participants in this sample becoming visibly tired, having to reread items, or demonstrating difficulty expressing their ideas. This is unsurprising based on the cognitive symptoms and fatigue reported in patients with Long COVID [4, 32], the inclusion criteria for this study that allowed patients to fall within multiple symptom “clusters’, and the general overlap in symptom profiles for individuals with myalgic encephalomyelitis/ chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) and Long COVID [33]. We recommend that researchers carefully balance the measurement burden imposed by a larger number of PROMs with the need for capturing multiple symptoms relevant to patients with Long COVID. The selection of a concise set of “core” concepts or items that could be used as indicators of change due to treatment [34] could be a useful solution. Future work might also need to consider differences in measurement properties across phenotypic subgroups of individuals with Long COVID.

Another important consideration for clinical trial planning is the relationship between different PROM administration schedules and recall periods. In this study, most participants reported being able to adequately respond to the 7-day recall period, and participants mentioned the fluctuations of their symptoms over the course of a day, week, or even months. The choice of optimal recall periods can be complex within a trial that includes multiple heterogeneous symptoms [35–38], as in this case. Additional work could be done to formally evaluate the optimal recall period and data collection schedule for different symptoms within Long COVID clinical trials.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. Although the study met all of the a priori purposive sampling goals set by our team, a larger study might produce additional insights. There was an overrepresentation of individuals with a bachelor’s degree or higher (70%) in the sample. The sample was also limited to English-speaking participants from the US. Future studies should be done, as the results might not be transferable to individuals with lower health literacy, greater cognitive impairment, or non-English speaking populations.. Across interviews, there was occasional inconsistency in data capture, meaning that some items were not debriefed in every interview. The limitations of this were mitigated by the assignment of PROMs across clusters to aim for discussion by at least 8 participants, aligned with recommended sample sizes. There also was a high level of general content duplication among PROMs, meaning that information about relevance and recall is likely cross-cutting. Further, this study primarily supports item comprehension and content coverage. Although an important consideration on its own, the determination that a measure is ultimately valid is tied to the overall body of evidence supporting the measure in a specific use case [9, 39]. This study was also never meant to establish a universal Long COVID core outcome set. For RECOVER-CT, additional work is planned to further support interpretation of changes in the selected PROM scores during ongoing trials, including utilizing blinded trial data to estimate meaningful score regions [11] for patients with Long COVID. Relatedly, the layering of evidence, including the original development work, is critical to support a validity argument for a PROM in a regulatory setting [9], typically done on a measure-by-measure basis.

Conclusions

Overall, these results support the use of this set of modified PROMs in the phase 2 RECOVER-CT trials. These findings also indicate that additional steps could be taken to improve these PROMs for future clinical trials (i.e., simplifying instructions and reducing the number of items). We support conducting additional qualitative and quantitative work on PROMs in Long COVID as well as future establishment of a Long COVID core outcome set. Having high-quality PROMs deemed fit for purpose in trials for Long COVID is critical for identifying treatments that will allow the millions of adults who currently have Long COVID to recover.
