Atomically confined insertion for 2D strain and polarization engineered GaN electronics
Authors: Yuanhong Shi, Zilong Dong, Jiangwen Wang, Di Guo, Wanpeng Li, Wei Sha, Zekun Li, Zhen Yang, Jizhou Zhang, Jitao Liu, Te Xu, Weiguo Hu, Zhong Lin Wang, Junyi Zhai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGallium nitride semiconductors are essential for advanced electronics, but realizing their potential requires robust normally-off devices. The P-GaN gate high-electron-mobility transistor is the dominant architecture, yet its threshold voltage is restricted to less than 2 volts by the low activation efficiency of magnesium acceptors. Here, we demonstrate atomically confined insertion to overcome this bottleneck. This technique creates self-terminating, two-dimensional magnesium layers within a complex heterostructure, inducing localized strain and polarity inversion. The resulting atomic-scale polarization fields increase the average effective hole concentration several-fold. When integrated into a P-GaN gate high-electron-mobility transistor, atomically confined insertion boosts the threshold voltage from 1.5 to 4.3 volts while mitigating the degradation in transconductance and output current typical of conventional methods. Furthermore, this approach substantially suppresses the current collapse effect via an efficient vertical hole injection mechanism. This work establishes atomic-scale field engineering as a viable axis for performance control and optimization in semiconductor devices.Subject terms: Electronic devices, Nanofabrication and nanopatterning

Introduction

The intrinsic material properties of the wide-bandgap semiconductor gallium nitride (GaN), such as its wide bandgap, high breakdown field, and high electron saturation velocity, have established it as a cornerstone for modern electronics1,2. GaN-based high-electron-mobility transistors (HEMTs) in particular are advancing power systems, enabling high efficiency and power density in demanding applications ranging from data centers and 5 G/6 G communications to electric vehicles and aerospace systems3–5. To fully harness the potential of these HEMT devices, achieving reliable normally-off (enhancement-mode) operation is paramount for system safety and simplified driver design6,7. Among a host of strategies, the p-GaN gate architecture has emerged as an effective and commercially viable solution, as it can achieve a stable, positive threshold voltage (Vth) without etching or damaging the critical AlGaN/GaN heterointerface8–10.

Nevertheless, the full potential of this architecture is limited by one of the most persistent challenges in semiconductor science: the notoriously inefficient p-type doping of GaN11. This architecture’s operation relies on the p-type GaN layer to deplete the two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG) in the channel beneath it, thus achieving normally-off functionality12. While the achievable hole concentration from magnesium (Mg) doping is generally sufficient for optoelectronic devices, it remains a bottleneck for power electronics. The low activation efficiency of Mg acceptors (~1%) restricts the achievable Vth in p-GaN HEMTs to below +2 V. This narrow threshold window creates an insufficient noise margin that leads to a high risk of spurious turn-on in harsh environments13–15. Although further engineering methods specific to this architecture, such as p-GaN bridges or gate passivation, can increase Vth, they invariably suffer from performance trade-offs, typically degrading both transconductance and output current16–19. Thus, strategies to overcome these limitations are highly desired.

Beyond conventional methods, engineering the internal polarization fields of III-nitrides offers a potent alternative for carrier density manipulation. This concept has since seen significant evolution and refinement20,21. Simon et al. established the foundational principle of polarization-induced doping, demonstrating that the strong internal fields in compositionally graded AlGaN could efficiently ionize deep-level Mg acceptors, thereby generating a three-dimensional hole gas22. This was later advanced by Chaudhuri et al., who demonstrated the existence of a high-density, two-dimensional hole gas (2DHG) at an abrupt, undoped GaN/AlN heterointerface23. More recently, an additional physical pathway emerged as Wang et al. reported the spontaneous intercalation of 2D Mg sheets into bulk GaN, creating superlattices with massive strain and local polarity transitions24. However, translating these bulk material phenomena into functional semiconductor devices, particularly complex heterostructures, presents fundamental challenges. Unlike bulk crystals, heterostructures (such as AlGaN/GaN) rely on atomically sharp interfaces to sustain 2DEGs. Conventional global intercalation or strain engineering methods often lack spatial selectivity and depth control, leading to the degradation of these critical active interfaces or the introduction of widespread defects25,26. Consequently, a strategy that achieves atomically confined insertion—localized precisely where needed (e.g., the gate region) while self-terminating to protect the underlying channel—is essential to harness these quantum structural effects without compromising device integrity. Building upon these insights, the possibility of achieving controlled atomic layer embedding within a complex heterostructure for device applications, and furthermore, the functional application of this intercalation physics as a tool for targeted device performance engineering, present important areas for investigation.

In this work, we introduce a technique—termed Atomically Confined Insertion (ACI)—that harnesses such atomic-level interactions as a tool for device optimization. By integrating a controlled intercalation process into the gate region of a p-GaN HEMT, we demonstrate the formation of self-terminating, 2D intercalated Mg layers within the p-GaN layer of the complex HEMT heterostructure. Our microstructural analyses reveal that this insertion process concurrently induces substantial localized compressive strain (exceeding −12% along the c-axis) and a local inversion of crystal polarity. We propose a mechanism whereby these dramatic local strain and polarity structural changes generate strong, localized polarization fields, which in turn boost the average effective hole concentration. Leveraging this, by integrating the ACI technique into the p-GaN gate, we engineer a substantial Vth increase in the device, from a 1.5 V baseline to 4.3 V. Notably, this approach alleviates the reductions in transconductance and output current that frequently accompany conventional threshold-shifting methods. Furthermore, the 2D Mg intercalation enhances vertical hole mobility and facilitates highly efficient hole injection from the p-GaN gate, which substantially suppresses current collapse and improves the overall dynamic performance of the device. This study validates ACI as a viable strategy, establishing atomic-scale field engineering as an effective approach for performance control in advanced semiconductor devices.

Results

Structural characterization of the self-terminating interstitial layers

The Atomically Confined Insertion (ACI) process was implemented by annealing a metallic Mg film deposited onto a p-GaN/AlGaN/GaN HEMT wafer (see Methods for details). The pristine heterostructure exhibits high-quality epitaxy with sharp interfaces, as confirmed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 1a). After the ACI treatment, significant microstructural evolution occurs selectively within the p-GaN layer, where distinct inverted pyramid-like regions form (Fig. 1b). Crucially, this insertion process demonstrates a self-terminating behavior. The propagation of the inserted layers halts abruptly at the p-GaN/AlGaN heterointerface, leaving the AlGaN barrier and the underlying 2DEG channel structurally intact (Fig. 1c). This self-termination is further corroborated by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping at the interface region (Supplementary Fig. 1), which clearly shows the Mg signal confined to the top P-GaN layer. This intrinsic self-stopping characteristic distinguishes the process from phenomena in bulk materials and renders the technique compatible with sensitive device architectures. This self-termination can be attributed to an abrupt change in the host material’s physicochemical environment at the heterointerface. The spontaneous intercalation of Mg into GaN is understood to rely on a delicate balance of factors, including the near-perfect lattice match between hexagonal Mg and GaN and specific Mg diffusion kinetics. The introduction of aluminum to form the AlGaN ternary alloy fundamentally alters this balance. Changes in lattice parameters disrupt the favorable registry, while the stronger Al-N bond energy imposes a thermodynamic penalty for atomic rearrangement27,28. Consequently, the AlGaN layer presents a significantly higher energy barrier for Mg diffusion and intercalation, effectively acting as an intrinsic, atomically sharp stop-layer. To rigorously validate this hypothesis, we performed first-principles calculations to evaluate the energetics of Mg incorporation and diffusion within the different host materials (Supplementary Table 1). The calculation results reveal that the dissolution energy of interstitial Mg in Al0.2Ga0.8N is 20.59 eV, which is an order of magnitude higher than in GaN (1.06 eV) (Supplementary Fig. 2, and Supplementary Note 1). Furthermore, the diffusion barrier for Mg migration in the Al0.2Ga0.8N lattice (3.0 eV) is found to be significantly higher than that in GaN (1.85 eV). This substantial thermodynamic and kinetic penalty effectively creates an atomic-scale stop-layer, strictly confining the atomistic co-diffusion process to the p-GaN region.

To resolve the atomic structure, high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) was employed. Within the pyramid regions, the ACI process creates what is effectively a localized, quasi-superlattice structure composed of alternating bright GaN slabs and dark, atomically thin Mg-rich layers (Fig. 1d). These inserted atomic sheets are separated by approximately 5–8 monolayers of GaN and exhibit considerable lateral dimensions, often exceeding 100 nm (Fig. 1e). Atomic-resolution integrated differential phase contrast (iDPC)-STEM imaging provides clear evidence that these are single monolayers of Mg atoms occupying interstitial sites within the host GaN’s hexagonal framework (Fig. 1f). This interstitial placement establishes an ABCAB stacking sequence with the adjacent hexagonal GaN layers, which has been theoretically identified as the most energetically stable configuration for Mg intercalation29. Furthermore, The GaN monolayer immediately below the Mg sheet tends to adopt N-polarity, while the monolayer above retains the original Ga-polarity24. A clear inversion domain boundary is observed within the GaN slab confined between two Mg sheets, marking the gradual transition from N-polar back to Ga-polar. This structure therefore creates periodic polarity inversions and associated polarization gradients throughout the quasi-superlattice region (Supplementary Fig. 3). To further elucidate the driving force behind the associated local polarity inversion, we performed DFT calculations comparing the energetics of non-inverted and inverted structures (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Note 2). The calculations demonstrate that the polarity-inverted configuration (N-polar) is energetically favored by 10.25 eV compared to the non-inverted case, indicating that the polarity inversion following Mg intercalation is a thermodynamically spontaneous process. Electronic structure analysis confirms that the fundamental driving force for this lattice reconstruction is the strong Coulombic repulsion between the interstitial Mg and the nearest-neighbor Ga cations (binding energy penalty of +5.24 eV), which is effectively relieved by the polarity inversion. The chemical identity of these features is confirmed by EDS. Elemental maps of Ga, N, and Mg, in conjunction with the reference HAADF-STEM image, verify that the intercalated sheets are predominantly composed of Mg (Fig. 1g). Furthermore, a line scan profile across these layers reveals sharp Mg intensity peaks that correlate precisely with the dark bands observed in the HAADF-STEM imaging (Fig. 1h).

Quantifying localized strain via multi-scale analysis

The formation of ACI structures introduces significant localized strain, first visualized using geometric phase analysis (GPA) on HAADF-STEM images (Fig. 2a). The strain maps, referenced to an undistorted GaN region, reveal substantial out-of-plane compressive strain (εyy) and in-plane tensile strain (εxx) within the GaN layers confined between Mg sheets. Notably, the shear strain (εxy) is negligible, indicating the deformation is primarily uniaxial or biaxial, preserving the hexagonal lattice symmetry30.

To quantify this strain, atomic column positions were determined from high-resolution HAADF-STEM images. As illustrated in Fig. 2b, this involved fitting the Ga sublattice positions to 2D Gaussian functions, comparing a strained region (green box) with a nearby pristine reference region (yellow box). This analysis yields average lattice parameters of a ≈ 3.18 Å and c ≈ 5.19 Å for the reference region, consistent with bulk GaN31. In contrast, the GaN confined between Mg layers exhibits an altered structure: the in-plane lattice constant a expands to 3.29 Å, while the out-of-plane constant c compresses to 4.55 Å (Fig. 2c). These changes correspond to an out-of-plane compressive strain (εc) of −12.33% and a large in-plane tensile strain (εa) of +3.46%. It should be noted that local strain values exhibit considerable variation, with peak compressive strains in some areas exceeding -18% (Supplementary Fig. 5). The ability to sustain such high elastic strain is attributed to the specific interstitial nature of the ACI structure. Unlike conventional substitutional intercalation, this process preserves the continuity of the GaN atomic planes across the inserted Mg layers (schematically shown in the inset of Fig. 2c), which imposes a strong uniaxial compression on the adjacent GaN slabs32,33.

High-resolution X-ray diffraction (HRXRD) provided macroscopic confirmation. The symmetric 2θ/ω scan of the GaN (0002) reflection exhibits a distinct shift to a higher angle after the ACI process, indicating a reduction in the average c-lattice parameter across the sample (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, the reciprocal space map (RSM) around the GaN (0002) reflection (Fig. 2e) is elongated along the Qz direction, confirming the presence of a strain gradient throughout the layer. A slight diffusion of the diffraction spot along the Qx direction is also observed, potentially indicating minor variations in crystallographic tilt or lateral coherence length introduced by the ACI structures34,35. This contrasts with the more compact diffraction spot observed in the pre-treatment sample (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Despite this substantial induced strain, the crystalline quality remains high, as evidenced by the negligible change in the rocking curve’s full width at half maximum (FWHM) (Supplementary Fig. 6b, c). This is likely aided by the close lattice matching between hexagonal Mg and GaN, which minimizes the generation of mismatch-induced defects such as threading dislocations28.

Mechanism of polarization-induced carrier enhancement

Building on the structural analysis, which revealed that ACI induces substantial localized strain and periodic polarity inversions, we find that this combination provides an effective pathway to enhance p-type conductivity via polarization effects. This proposed mechanism is schematically depicted in Fig. 3a, which illustrates the atomic model of an interstitial Mg sheet, the resulting gradients in polarization, and the generation of net bound charges and a strong internal electric field. Specifically, the local polarity inversion creates a sharp gradient in the spontaneous polarization (PSP), while the concurrent large compressive strain induces a substantial piezoelectric polarization (PPE). The gradient of the total polarization (P=PSP+PPE), according to Gauss’s law (ρπ=−∇⋅P), generates significant net-bound volume charges (ρπ). These charges establish strong internal electric fields (potentially on the order of MV cm-1), which are strong enough to ionize deep-level Mg acceptors through field-assisted emission (e.g., the Poole-Frenkel effect)22,36,37. This provides a non-thermal pathway for hole generation, boosting the free hole concentration beyond the limits of conventional thermal activation.

We experimentally validated this enhanced hole concentration through temperature-dependent Hall effect measurements (Fig. 3b). For this analysis, a dedicated 200-nm-thick p-GaN layer on a sapphire substrate was used, with an underlying high-resistivity buffer to ensure accurate measurement of the p-GaN layer itself (Fig. 3b, inset). Compared to the as-grown control sample, the ACI-processed p-GaN exhibits a more than twofold increase in room-temperature average hole concentration. Notably, the temperature dependence of the hole concentration in the ACI sample is significantly weaker. This reduced carrier freeze-out is a hallmark signature of a non-thermal, field-driven ionization mechanism, aligning with our proposed model. Given that Hall measurements provide a spatially averaged value, the local hole concentration enhancement within the ACI regions is expected to be considerably higher.

Beyond the polarization-induced doping effect that enhances hole concentration, the ACI structure inherently introduces significant lattice strain. Recent theoretical studies have substantiated that the substantial anisotropic compressive strain within such superlattices fundamentally modifies the valence band structure. Specifically, this strain lifts the low-effective-mass split-off (SO) band to the valence band maximum, theoretically creating a pathway for high-mobility vertical transport24,38. To spatially resolve this enhancement and decouple the contributions of carrier concentration and mobility, we performed cross-sectional Scanning Spreading Resistance Microscopy (SSRM) measurements (Supplementary Fig. 7). The results reveal that the local vertical conductivity in the ACI regions is over five orders of magnitude higher than that in the reference p-GaN. Given that the Hall-measured increase in average hole concentration is insufficient to account for this disproportionate surge, the SSRM data provides clear experimental evidence that the ACI structure implies a simultaneous, substantial enhancement in vertical hole mobility.

The demonstrated ability of the ACI technique to substantially boost the effective hole concentration presents a viable strategy for overcoming the threshold voltage limitations in p-GaN gate HEMTs. To theoretically quantify this potential, Technology Computer-Aided Design (TCAD) simulations were performed (see Supplementary Fig. 8 for simulation details). The simulations modeled a p-GaN gate HEMT where the effective hole concentration (p) in the p-GaN layer was varied from 1 × 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ to 1 × 10¹⁸ cm⁻³. This range was chosen to represent technologically relevant p-GaN doping levels, encompassing the typical concentration of the as-grown wafer. The simulated conduction band diagrams under the gate (Fig. 3c) clearly show that a higher hole concentration more effectively depletes the 2DEG channel, raising the potential barrier39. This enhanced depletion capability translates directly to a positive shift in the device’s threshold voltage, as seen in the simulated transfer characteristics (Fig. 3d). The extracted Vth shows a clear and substantial increase from 1.9 V to 5.6 V across the simulated range (Fig. 3e). These simulation results strongly suggest that integrating the ACI process into a p-GaN gate should yield devices with significantly improved threshold voltages, providing a clear motivation for the device fabrication and characterization presented next.

High threshold voltage HEMTs enabled by ACI gate engineering

To experimentally validate the effectiveness of the ACI technique for threshold voltage enhancement, we fabricated p-GaN gate HEMTs incorporating this process (ACI-HEMTs) alongside conventional reference devices (C-HEMTs). The schematic structure of the ACI-HEMT, highlighting the integration of the 2D intercalated Mg structures within the p-GaN gate layer, is depicted in Fig. 4a. The fabrication process flow is outlined in Fig. 4b. Compared to the standard HEMT process, the ACI integration requires only the addition of a metallic Mg deposition step, followed by thermal annealing and subsequent acid cleaning to remove residues. All steps are compatible with standard fabrication lines. Moreover, the deposited Mg film can serve as a self-aligned etch mask for patterning the p-GaN gate. This approach can potentially mitigate lithographic misalignment issues, where Mg might otherwise inadvertently diffuse into device access regions during annealing and create undesirable interface defects.

The direct current (DC) transfer characteristics reveal the primary benefit of our approach (Fig. 4c, d). The reference C-HEMT exhibits a Vth (defined at Id = 1 mA mm−1) of +1.5 V. In contrast, the ACI-HEMT demonstrates a substantially increased Vth of +4.3 V. This significant positive shift is directly attributed to the enhanced effective hole concentration in the ACI-treated p-GaN. The higher hole density elevates the conduction band under the gate, raising the channel potential further from the Fermi level and thus requiring a higher positive gate voltage to initiate conduction. Notably, this improvement is achieved while maintaining robust gate control over the channel. This contrasts favorably with many gate passivation techniques, where inserting a dielectric layer, while boosting Vth, often leads to an unavoidable reduction in gm due to voltage division effects18,40. The logarithmic plot of drain current (Id) and gate leakage current (Ig) versus Vgs (Fig. 4e) confirms both devices achieve a high ON/OFF current ratio of approximately 10⁸, with no significant degradation in gate leakage performance.

The output characteristics (Id -Vds) further demonstrate the effects of the ACI technique (Fig. 4f, g). The ACI-HEMT exhibits enhanced output performance compared to the C-HEMT, with the maximum drain current density (Id,max) increasing from 86 mA mm−1 to 113 mA mm−1. However, the ACI formation process involves an additional high-temperature annealing step (680 °C for 10 min). This thermal process introduces additional trap states within the access regions flanking the p-GaN gate island or in the buffer layer. These traps can capture electrons to induce a virtual gate effect, resulting in a slightly higher on-resistance (Ron) for the ACI-HEMT (29.13 Ω·mm) compared to the C-HEMT (23.15 Ω·mm). Despite this elevated Ron, the ACI-HEMT exhibits a higher saturation output current, which is attributed to the improved conductivity modulation (GIT) effect enabled by the ACI structure41. Statistical analysis of Vth from multiple devices across different fabrication batches is summarized in Fig. 4h, alongside the range predicted by TCAD simulations. While the Vth for C-HEMTs varies between 1.4 V and 2.3 V, all fabricated ACI-HEMTs show a substantial increase in Vth, albeit with a relatively larger distribution. This spread is tentatively attributed to the inherent non-uniformity in the spatial distribution and density of the self-assembled ACI structures within the p-GaN layer.

To further evaluate the device’s robustness under electrical stress, breakdown characteristics were systematically investigated. Figure 5a compares the forward gate breakdown characteristics (Vds=0 V). The C-HEMT exhibits breakdown around Vgs=+10 V, whereas the ACI-HEMT shows an improved forward gate breakdown voltage (VGBD, defined at Ig=1 mA mm−1) of +12.7 V. This enhancement is explained by the device physics: in p-GaN gate HEMTs, forward breakdown is often attributed to channel electrons gaining sufficient energy to overcome the AlGaN barrier and trigger impact ionization within the depletion region near the gate metal/p-GaN interface42. The higher hole concentration in the ACI-HEMT likely raises the AlGaN conduction band edge further relative to the channel, creating a higher barrier for electron injection and thus increasing VGBD. In terms of off-state breakdown characteristics (Fig. 5b), the breakdown voltage (VBD) increases from 688 V for the C-HEMT to 750 V for the ACI-HEMT. This marginal improvement is, in itself, a significant finding. It strongly suggests that the ACI structures, due to their self-limiting formation terminating above the AlGaN/GaN interface, do not introduce significant crystalline defects. Conversely, it is plausible that the enhanced lateral depletion from the higher hole concentration leads to a more uniform electric field distribution near the gate edge, contributing to the modest VBD increase43.

Beyond static characteristics, p-GaN gate structures can exhibit a beneficial conductivity modulation effect, often referred to as the Gate Injection Transistor (GIT) effect, which suppresses current collapse by neutralizing trapped electrons via hole injection41,44. As the ACI process creates structures that have been associated with enhanced out-of-plane hole mobility, it could potentially amplify this advantageous GIT effect38. Strong evidence for the enhanced p-GaN conductivity is presented in Fig. 5c, which shows the contact characteristics between Ni/Au electrodes and p-GaN layers with and without ACI treatment. The ACI-treated sample exhibits a clear transition from non-linear to linear (Ohmic) behavior with over an order of magnitude increase in current level, confirming improved conductivity and contact properties. To evaluate the impact of this on dynamic performance, pulsed Id −Vds measurements were conducted, starting from a quiescent bias point of Vgs0=0 V, Vds0=0 V (see Supplementary Fig. 9a for waveforms).

As shown in Fig. 5d, e under a 500 μs pulse width, despite showing more severe degradation in the linear region, the ACI-HEMT exhibits improved collapse suppression capability in the saturation region (high Vds), with a saturation current degradation rate of only 26%, outperforming the 32% observed in the C-HEMT. This characteristic contrast—higher degradation in the linear region versus reduced collapse in the saturation region—provides clear evidence for the voltage-dependent hole injection mechanism of the ACI structure. In the linear region (Vds ≈ 1 V) during pulsed testing, the internal lateral electric field is weak. Consequently, holes injected from the Ohmic gate cannot sufficiently diffuse into the access regions to neutralize electrons trapped in defects. Therefore, device performance at this stage is primarily dominated by the high density of trap states induced by the thermal process, leading to the observed more pronounced on-resistance degradation in the ACI-HEMT compared to the C-HEMT. Conversely, in the saturation region (high Vds), the strong lateral electric field facilitates the efficient drift of holes injected from the ACI gate toward the source-to-gate access region. These holes effectively neutralize trapped electrons to suppress the virtual gate effect, generating more effective conductivity modulation. More importantly, under high-frequency dynamic stress, the ACI structure benefits from its enhanced vertical hole mobility. This characteristic enables a rapid vertical redistribution of holes, establishing an effective potential shield that protects the underlying 2DEG channel from the influence of parasitic trap charges. Through this combination of source-side lateral neutralization and rapid vertical potential shielding, the trap-induced conductivity degradation is effectively suppressed. Thus, current collapse in the ACI-HEMT is mitigated compared to the C-HEMT in the saturation regime. This improvement is further pronounced when tested with a wider 1 ms pulse (Supplementary Fig. 9b, c). This result confirms that ACI-enhanced vertical hole transport is effective in suppressing dynamic failure45.

Finally, Fig. 5f benchmarks the performance of our ACI-HEMT against previously published results for p-GaN gate HEMTs employing various Vth engineering techniques. The ACI-HEMT achieves a high threshold voltage while simultaneously preserving, and even slightly enhancing, the transconductance and saturation current. This demonstrates its potential for advancing normally-off GaN power transistors, and furthermore, as the ACI technique modifies the fundamental electronic properties of GaN via engineered local fields, it offers a versatile strategy for performance optimization in a wide range of other GaN-based electronic and optoelectronic devices.

Discussion

In this work, we have established the atomic-scale field engineering as a viable design axis in GaN electronics by introducing and demonstrating a platform we term Atomically Confined Insertion (ACI). We have shown that this process induces the formation of an interface-self-terminating, 2D Mg intercalation quasi-superlattice, which is selectively confined within the p-GaN layer of the HEMT structure. This resulting structure generates substantial elastic strain (exceeding −12%) and local polarity inversions, which in turn create strong internal polarization fields. These engineered fields substantially enhance the p-type conductivity, boosting the average hole concentration by more than twofold. Leveraging this, we have fabricated enhancement-mode HEMTs with a threshold voltage of +4.3 V—a near threefold increase from the baseline—while circumventing the inherent trade-offs that typically compromise transconductance and output current in traditional architectures. Furthermore, dynamic performance is improved through a reduction in current collapse, attributable to a more efficient gate injection effect. This result directly addresses the critical need for robust, high-threshold normally-off GaN power transistors.

Looking forward, the implications of the ACI platform extend far beyond the lateral power devices demonstrated here. The ability to fundamentally enhance p-type conductivity could prove highly beneficial for a wide range of other III-nitride devices where hole transport or injection is a primary bottleneck, such as high-efficiency light-emitting diodes (LEDs), laser diodes (LDs), and vertical power transistors. Moreover, the ACI process itself provides an accessible platform to study the fundamental physics of semiconductors under severe lattice deformation, potentially revealing distinct electronic, phononic, and optical properties. Despite these promising prospects, the current ACI prototype still faces certain practical limitations. Specifically, the additional high-temperature annealing and acid treatment steps involved in this process may introduce interfacial defect states, leading to a slight increase in device on-resistance. Furthermore, given that the current ACI platform is implemented within a GIT architecture, it inherently exhibits higher forward gate current and power consumption compared to Schottky-gate counterparts. Additionally, the reliance on a spontaneous nucleation mechanism for the current intercalation process currently precludes full spatial uniformity in the morphology, depth, and distribution of the Mg-intercalated structures. This non-uniformity contributes to a broader threshold voltage distribution and the subthreshold current tailing. While this work validates the compatibility of the process with industrial fabrication, suppressing these non-idealities to achieve precise control over ACI structural uniformity, minimizing gate-drive power dissipation, and comprehensively evaluating long-term device reliability are the critical next steps for technological maturation. To advance from the current spontaneous nucleation to precise structural control, promising avenues include lithographically guided nucleation, surface energy engineering, and advanced in-situ epitaxial growth techniques. Implementing these strategies to leverage the thermodynamic stability of the Mg-intercalated phase could enable the synthesis of uniform, wafer-scale superlattices, ensuring the consistency and scalability required for diverse optoelectronic and power applications. In essence, Atomically Confined Insertion represents a conceptual advance, demonstrating that deliberate, atomic-scale structural engineering can expand the accessible regimes of material properties and device performance, providing a versatile strategy for the development of advanced semiconductor technologies.

Methods

Epitaxial materials

The GaN HEMT heterostructures were grown via metal-organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD) on 4-inch Si (111) substrates. The epitaxial stack consisted of, in sequence: an AlN nucleation layer, a 3.5 µm high-resistivity C-doped GaN/AlGaN graded buffer, a 300 nm unintentionally doped (UID) GaN channel, a 0.6 nm AlN spacer, a 15 nm Al₀.₂Ga₀.₈N barrier, and a 70 nm p-type GaN cap layer with a nominal Mg doping concentration of ~3 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³. For Hall effect measurements, dedicated samples were prepared with a 200-nm-thick p-GaN layer grown on a c-plane sapphire substrate, which included a 2 µm high-resistivity carbon-doped GaN (HR GaN:C) buffer for electrical isolation. The samples utilized for SSRM analysis consisted of a 500-nm-thick p-GaN layer grown on a Si (111) substrate with a 4-µm-thick buffer layer.

ACI formation process

The ACI structure was formed by first depositing a 50-nm-thick metallic Mg film (99.99% purity) onto the p-GaN surface via magnetron sputtering (YNS-600). The samples were then annealed via rapid thermal annealing (RTP-3S01, JouleYacht, Wuhan China) at 680 °C for 10 min under a N₂ ambient at atmospheric pressure. After annealing, residual surface material was removed through a multi-step acid cleaning procedure involving sequential immersions in: (i) boiling aqua regia (HCl:HNO₃ = 3:1) for 15 min; (ii) diluted hydrofluoric acid (HF:H₂O = 1:9) for 15 min; and (iii) an acid solution (HCl:H₂O₂:H₂O = 1:1:4) at 80 °C for 10 min. Samples were rinsed with deionized water and dried with N₂ between each step.

Device fabrication

Both C-HEMTs and ACI-HEMTs were co-fabricated on the same wafer for direct comparison. The process began with mesa isolation etching down to the buffer layer using a Cl₂/BCl₃-based inductively coupled plasma reactive ion etching (ICP-RIE) process. For the ACI-HEMTs, a 70 nm Mg film was then deposited by sputtering onto the photolithographically defined gate region and served as a self-aligned hard mask for the subsequent ICP-RIE of the p-GaN layer outside the gate footprint. After the p-GaN etch, the ACI formation annealing (680 °C, 10 min, RTA, N₂) was performed, followed by the acid cleaning procedure to remove the remaining Mg mask. For C-HEMTs, the ACI formation annealing step was omitted. Subsequently, Ti/Al/Ni/Au (22/140/55/80 nm) source/drain Ohmic contacts were formed by e-beam evaporation and annealed at 850 °C for 30 s in N₂. A 200 nm SiNₓ passivation layer was then deposited via plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD), followed by the opening of gate windows. Finally, the Ni/Au (50/150 nm) gate electrode and Ti/Au pad metallization were deposited. All devices featured identical dimensions: LG = 4 µm, LGS = 4 µm, LGD = 11 µm, and WG = 100 µm.

For Hall effect measurements, samples grown on sapphire substrates were diced into 1.5 mm × 1.5 mm squares. Van der Pauw patterned Ni (20 nm) / Au (150 nm) electrodes were formed using standard photolithography, lift-off, and electron-beam evaporation, followed by annealing at 525 °C for 5 min in an O₂ ambient to minimize contact resistance.

Materials characterization

Cross-sectional TEM/STEM samples were prepared using a focused ion beam (FIB, FEI Helios 600i). A standard lift-out method was employed, with a protective Pt layer deposited prior to milling. Bright-field TEM was conducted on an FEI Tecnai G2 F20 (200 kV). HAADF-, iDPC-, and ABF-STEM imaging were conducted on a double Cs-corrected scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM, FEI Spectra 300) operated at 300 kV. To mitigate artifacts from sample drift during acquisition, high-resolution HAADF images were produced by recording and averaging a rapid series of frames that were computationally aligned using a cross-correlation algorithm. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) for elemental mapping was performed using the integrated Super X 4 SDD detector system. Geometric Phase Analysis (GPA) was carried out on atomic-resolution HAADF-STEM images using the GPA Phase 2.0 plugin within Gatan Microscopy Suite (GMS), utilizing undistorted GaN regions as the reference lattice46. Quantitative strain analysis based on atomic column positions was executed using the StatSTEM MATLAB script, which fits 2D Gaussian functions to the Ga sublattice positions derived from atomic-resolution HAADF-STEM images47. SSRM measurements

To enable direct side-by-side comparison, periodic Mg films were deposited and patterned on the p-GaN surface using photolithography, followed by the standard annealing and cleaning procedures. This process created alternating regions with Mg intercalation (ACI) and without Mg intercalation (reference p-GaN). Ohmic contacts were formed on the sample surface by depositing Ni/Au stacks followed by annealing in an oxygen atmosphere. Cross-sectional samples were prepared by precision mechanical polishing, followed by Ar ion beam milling to remove the damage layer and obtain a clean surface for measurement. SSRM measurements, along with simultaneous Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) topography, were conducted using a scanning probe microscope (JEOL JSTM-4200A) equipped with an SSRM module. A conductive diamond-coated silicon probe was used for the scans. A bias voltage was applied between the top surface electrode and the conductive probe on the cross-section to establish the vertical current path. To eliminate surface contamination and moisture adsorption, and to ensure probe stability, all measurements were performed in a high-vacuum chamber with a base pressure of 1×10-5 Torr.

Electrical characterization

All DC electrical characteristics (transfer, output, gate leakage, and breakdown) were measured at room temperature using Keysight B1500A and B1505A semiconductor parameter analyzers. The threshold voltage (Vth) was defined as the gate voltage (Vgs) at a drain current (Id) of 1 mA mm-1 with Vds =8 V. Pulsed Id-Vds measurements to evaluate current collapse were performed using the pulse measurement units integrated within the B1500A. A double-pulse technique was employed with pulse widths ranging from 500 µs to 1 ms and a pulse period of 5 ms, stepping from a quiescent bias point (Vgs0 =0 V, Vds0 =0 V). Current collapse was quantified as the percentage reduction in pulsed drain current relative to the DC drain current at a specified bias point (e.g., high Vds). Gate contact characteristics (Fig. 5c) were assessed using transmission line model (TLM) structures fabricated on p-GaN layers with and without ACI treatment.

TCAD simulation methodology

Device simulations were conducted using the Technology Computer-Aided Design (TCAD) software suite. The simulated device structure (Supplementary Fig. 8) replicated the fabricated devices. Standard physical models for III-nitrides were employed, including drift-diffusion transport, field-dependent mobility, Shockley-Read-Hall (SRH) recombination, spontaneous and piezoelectric polarization, and incomplete ionization of Mg acceptors. A finite element mesh was adaptively refined near heterointerfaces and the gate region to ensure numerical accuracy.
