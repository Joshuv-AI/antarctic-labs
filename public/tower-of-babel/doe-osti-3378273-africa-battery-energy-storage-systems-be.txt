 1. Policy and Legal Framework

Before reviewing the estimate, ensure:

• There is policy clarity on unsolicited proposals (e.g., are they allowed? under what
  conditions?).

• Clear evaluation criteria and process should be established, including how public
  interest and cost-effectiveness will be judged.

• Ensure the proposal adheres to national energy laws, procurement regulations, and
  environmental standards.

Source: World Bank Group/PPIAF 2017

                                                                         National Laboratory of the Rockies | 4

 2. Cost Breakdown Analysis

Break down the submission into key cost components:

• Capital expenditure (CAPEX): Battery modules, inverters, balance of system, civil works,
  interconnection costs, land acquisition.
• Operating expenses (OPEX): Maintenance, staff, degradation replacement, insurance.
• Financing costs: Debt service, interest rate assumptions, return on equity.
• Revenue assumptions: Market participation (if allowed/exists), ancillary services pricing (if exists),
  capacity payments.

Financial assessment tools can support financial modeling and cost assessment.

• System Advisor Model (SAM) – NLR's free tool for modeling battery storage costs and performance
• ATB (Annual Technology Baseline) – NLR's interactive tool for capital costs by size, duration, and
  assumptions

Source: IRENA 2020
                                                                                        National Laboratory of the Rockies | 5

 3. Benchmarking and Validation
Benchmark costs locally and internationally

• International Benchmarks: Compare the proposed costs with international standards and similar projects in
  comparable economies. Including:
 • NLR Annual Technology Baseline (ATB)
 • Lazard Levelized Cost of Storage (LCOS)
 • IRENA cost reports
 • Recent procurements in comparable economies
• Local Context Adjustments: Adjust benchmarks for local factors such as import duties, labor costs, and
  infrastructure availability. Including:
 • Import duties and VAT
 • Logistics and shipping
 • Labor costs
 • Currency risk and financing environment
 • Infrastructure constraints
• Independent Verification: Engage third-party experts to validate the technical and financial assumptions.
• Adjust for local market risks, logistics costs, and import duties.
• Consult neighboring countries’ experiences with similar IPPs or storage projects.

 Source: IRENA 2020                                                                         National Laboratory of the Rockies | 6

 4. Financial Sensitivity and Risk Analysis
Assess Financial Assumptions
• Battery lifespan and degradation rates: Should reflect realistic usage profiles.
• Revenue projections: Are they based on credible market forecasts or over-optimistic assumptions?

Require Sensitivity and Risk Analysis
• Ask the IPP to submit:
 • Sensitivity analysis of the impact of key variables (e.g., battery prices, interest rates) on project
 viability.
 • Risk mitigation strategies to evaluate proposed measures to address potential risks, including
 technological, financial, and operational uncertainties.
 • Ownership/transfer clauses and residual value handling.
 • Decommissioning and disposal plans. Decommissioning bonds or financial security mechanisms
 indexed to inflation ensure adequate funds at end of life.

Source: FasterCapital 2025
                                                                                         National Laboratory of the Rockies | 7

 5. Public Interest, Consultation, and Transparency
• Evaluate if the proposed cost is affordable for end-users or utilities.

• Assess the BESS's role in supporting grid reliability, renewable integration, or peak shaving —
  i.e., does it solve a real system need? (Ideally, reverse the process, e.g., identify system need
  then determine best solution then accept or tender proposals)

• Assess long-term fiscal commitments and contingent liabilities for the government.

• Stakeholder Engagement: Conduct consultations with relevant stakeholders, including
  utilities, consumers, industry, and environmental groups.

• Transparency Measures: Ensure that the evaluation process is transparent and that non-
  confidential information is publicly accessible.

 Source: World Bank Group/PPIAF 2017

Phase 3: Analyze the system value of electricity storage compared to alternative flexibility options

Goal in this phase: electricity storage is valued both for its effectiveness in providing identified
services and its economic attractiveness compared to alternative options.

• Step I:
 • To identify optimal investments, electricity storage is considered together with alternative
 technologies (such as energy efficiency, demand response, new transmission, grid
 enhancing technologies, and peaking power plants) in a least-cost capacity expansion
 optimization.
 • Production cost simulations are needed for an accurate estimation of the value and optimal
 amount of storage, as well as the costs of storage to the system over the period of study
 (one or multiple years).

Source: IRENA 2020                                                                National Laboratory of the Rockies | 12

• Step II:
 • Step I above is repeated with storage removed from the available options (the
 alternative technologies mentioned in Step I are included). The capacity expansion analysis
 will provide a set of alternative solutions to serve system needs. Production cost simulation
 is used to estimate operational costs accurately.

• Step III:
 • A comparison of total system costs from Steps I and II gives the total system value of storage
 over the period of study. Phase 3 will only deploy electricity storage if this is beneficial for
 the system; the difference compared with the “no new storage” scenario is deemed to be
 the benefit of adding electricity storage to the system.

Source: IRENA 2020                                                              National Laboratory of the Rockies | 13

Phase 4: Simulate storage operation and stacking of revenues

• In this phase, the revenue that the storage project receives is maximized by combining the various
  services the project can provide. In reality, if there is a liquid wholesale energy market, a large
  electricity storage asset has the potential to affect market prices.

• This phase assesses the revenue streams that electricity storage can bring to a project owner under
  specific market settings.

• A wide range of project-level benefits and costs of electricity storage depend on the market and
  regulatory environment surrounding the system of interest.
 • For this type of exercise, dispatch simulation software (i.e., production cost model) is used.
 • However, the objective of the analysis is different from the previous step. Instead of minimizing
 total system costs, the model rather maximizes profits of a specific electricity storage project
 within the overall optimal storage portfolio resulting from Phase 3.

  Source: IRENA 2020
                                                                                        National Laboratory of the Rockies | 14

Phase 5: Assess the viability of storage projects: System Value vs. Monetizable Revenues

• Enough revenue streams for project owners are needed in order for system benefits to be realized.
  Some benefits cannot be monetized or are not attainable.

• Comparing calculated system benefits from Phase 3 with the project owner’s potential revenue streams
  for the set of services optimized in Phase 4 can be instructive. Electricity prices derived from the
  simulations in Phase 3 are used to calculate the revenue streams.

• The output of this final phase is a project-level cost and benefit analysis. Cost refers to the cost of
  building and operating a storage project under a specified case. Benefits refer to both system level (non-
  monetizable) and project level (monetizable) benefits

• If the total benefits exceed costs, but monetized benefits are less than costs, it means that the project
  developers do not have enough economic incentive to build this project. In this case, policy intervention
  or grant funding would be needed to enhance the overall social good.

Cost Recovery through Tariffs

 Introduction to Tariff
  • What is a tariff?
 ▪ A tariff is a pricing structure determined by a utility or regulatory body.
 ▪ A tariff structure determines the rate at which electricity service will be sold per unit (e.g., per
 kWh or per kW-month) for end-user customers or bought per unit from electricity suppliers.
 Storage needs both.
 ▪ A tariff needs to ultimately lead to enough revenue for the supplier to pay back the investment
 costs.

  • Utilities: a system operator, vertically-integrated utility, or distribution company pays for the Battery
 Energy Storage System (BESS) equipment or service
 ▪ A utility tariff may determine:
 ▪ how the BESS investment is reimbursed
 ▪ the rate the BESS pays for charging power
 ▪ the rate the BESS is paid for discharge
 ▪ some combination

Source: Jackson 2024

 Energy Storage Payment/Revenue Structures
• Arbitrage: energy arbitrage consists of purchasing electricity (charging) when energy prices are low and
  reselling it (discharging) when energy prices are high, hence generating a profit from the price difference.

• Energy storage tariff when defined: the tariff incorporates the costs of BESS and passes it down to end-users or
  utilities. It can be in the form of:

 • Capacity payments (Fixed payment): a capacity payment is made by the user of an energy asset to the
 owner of the asset for the right to utilize the asset’s capacity.
 • Tolling: a rental agreement in which the owner 'operates' the asset and the utility pays rent and
 supplies fuel, or ‘charge,’ for electricity. Need to specify the operation parameters.
 • Ancillary services payment: a fee for frequency regulation, operating reserves products, voltage control
 (reactive power), and/or black start.
 • Non-wires alternative (NWA) payment: a payment for BESS (or other DER and microgrid technologies)
 valued based on BESS precluding a replacement of more costly infrastructure investments (and thus a
 contribution to BESS capital cost).
