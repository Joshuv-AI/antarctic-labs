Association Between Estimated Glucose Disposal Rate and Biological Aging Among U.S. Adults: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Mengjie Zhao, Jing Cao, Mengxuan Li, Jiwei Zhang, Wantong Zhang, Qiuyan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsBiomarkers of biological aging, including homeostatic dysregulation (HD), Klemera–Doubal method biological age (KDM‐BA), and phenotypic age (PA), provide multidimensional quantification of the aging process. Although insulin resistance correlates with accelerated aging, the connection between biological aging and the estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR)—a proxy indicator of insulin sensitivity—has yet to be systematically examined.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study analyzed 15,048 U.S. adults from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, 2003–2018). eGDR was calculated as: 21.158 − (0.09 × waist circumference) − (3.407 × hypertension) − (0.551 × HbA1c). To evaluate relationships between eGDR and markers of biological aging, weighted multivariable linear and logistic regression models were employed, supplemented by trend analyses. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) modeled nonlinear relationships. Subgroup analyses evaluated effect modifications by demographic/clinical factors.ResultsFollowing comprehensive adjustment, a 1‐unit elevation in eGDR demonstrated significant correlations with a 1.88‐year decline in KDM‐BA (β = −1.88, 95%CI: −2.01, −1.76), a reduction of 0.60 years in PA (β = −0.60, 95%CI: −0.67, −0.54), and a 16% decrease in HD (β = −0.16, 95%CI: −0.17, −0.15). Relative to the lowest tertile (T1), individuals in the highest eGDR tertile (T3) demonstrated an 87% decrease in the likelihood of KDM‐BA acceleration (OR = 0.13, 95%CI: 0.10, 0.15) and a 60% reduction in the probability of PA acceleration (OR = 0.40, 95%CI: 0.34, 0.48). The RCS modeling identified a nonlinear inverse association linking eGDR levels to biological aging. Across subgroups, higher eGDR consistently correlated with younger biological age, even when accounting for notable interaction effects.ConclusionIn this large cross‐sectional analysis of US adults, higher eGDR levels were associated with younger biological age and lower odds of accelerated aging. Higher eGDR levels were associated with younger biological age and reduced risks of accelerated aging.Keywords: biological age, estimated glucose disposal rate, homeostatic dysregulation, NHANES, phenotypic age

Introduction

Senescence, characterized by the progressive deterioration of functional stability in diverse physiological systems [1], manifests as increased susceptibility to age‐related diseases and mortality [2]. Conventional chronological age (CA) often fails to capture individual variations in aging trajectories, prompting the development of quantitative biomarkers such as phenotypic age (PA) and Klemera–Doubal method biological age (KDM‐BA) [3, 4]. These metrics integrate clinical biomarkers to quantify discrepancies between biological and CA, serving as robust predictors of morbidity and mortality [5, 6].

Insulin resistance (IR), recognized as a fundamental pathological hallmark of metabolic syndrome, is linked to accelerated aging through mechanisms including chronic inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cellular senescence [7]. The estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR)—a validated, noninvasive clinical surrogate for IR—calculated using waist circumference (WC), hypertension status, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels [8]. Critically, this composite formula integrates domains of central adiposity, vascular health, and chronic glycemic control, thereby capturing multisystem metabolic dysregulation. Recent studies indicate that eGDR has shown significantly better discrimination for cardiovascular disease risk (AUC = 0.683) than HOMA‐IR (AUC = 0.543) [9]. Similarly, while lipid‐based indices like the triglyceride‐glucose (TyG) index are informative, they do not incorporate metrics of adiposity distribution or hypertension. We hypothesize that the multidimensional nature of eGDR may therefore provide a more holistic proxy for the body‐wide physiological impairment that characterizes accelerated biological aging. Supporting this notion, studies have identified associations between other IR surrogates (e.g., TyG index) and biological aging [10, 11]; however, the specific relationship between eGDR and integrative biomarkers of biological aging in the general population remains unexamined.

To address this gap, we employed the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), leveraging its demographically diverse sampling framework and extensive biomarker repositories. This study aimed to investigate the cross‐sectional associations between eGDR and validated senescence metrics (PA, KDM‐BA, and homeostatic dysregulation [HD]). Our findings could elucidate how a composite metabolic marker relates to system‐level aging, potentially informing targeted interventions to mitigate metabolically driven aging processes.

Materials and Methods

Study Participants

The NHANES, a demographically diverse population‐based survey administered by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), is a core component of the United States' public health monitoring framework. It provides benchmark assessments of community health through systematic data collection. The study utilizes a multiphase layered probabilistic sampling methodology to assemble a demographically balanced cohort, integrating three core data collection modules: standardized questionnaires, clinical physiological examinations, and laboratory analyses of biospecimens. The research protocol received formal authorization from the NCHS Institutional Review Board and was implemented in full compliance with ethical standards governing biomedical investigations. Written informed consent documentation was obtained from all enrolled individuals. The study strictly adhered to the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting standards to maintain methodological robustness. Study documentation and raw data sets are publicly accessible via the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) portal.

This analysis utilized data from NHANES cycles spanning 2003–2018, encompassing an initial sample of 80,312 participants. We included adults aged ≥ 20 years (n = 44,790) and implemented a three‐tier quality control protocol to refine the cohort. First, 6019 participants were excluded due to missing parameters required for calculating the eGDR, including WC, hypertension status, and HbA1c levels. Next, 20,947 participants were excluded for incomplete serum biomarker data necessary to compute the KDM‐BA, PA, and HD, which collectively required 20 validated biomarkers. Finally, an additional 2776 participants were excluded because of missing covariate data. After these sequential exclusions, the final analytical cohort comprised 15,048 eligible participants (Figure 1). We compared key characteristics between participants included in the final analysis (n = 15,048) and those excluded due to missing data (n = 65,264). Excluded participants were slightly younger (mean age 33.19 years vs. 47.56 years), had a higher proportion of females (51.5% vs. 50.05%), and exhibited a lower prevalence of diabetes (7.75% vs. 12.90%) (p < 0.05). As participants under 20 years of age were excluded from this study, the proportion of older individuals and those with impaired glucose metabolism within the analyzed cohort was slightly higher.

Calculation of eGDR

The eGDR was employed to assess IR, serving as a validated non‐invasive surrogate marker. The eGDR formula was initially derived and validated within a type 1 diabetes cohort to estimate insulin‐mediated glucose uptake [8]. This multi‐parameter model integrates three key physiological dimensions associated with IR: anthropometric indicators (WC), vascular health markers (hypertensive status), and glycemic control indicators (HbA1c). The mathematical model is expressed as: eGDR (mg·kg−1·min−1) = 21.158 − (0.090 × WC) − (3.407 × hypertension status) − (0.551 × HbA1c) where WC represents waist circumference in centimeters, and hypertension status is a binary variable (coded as 1 for diagnosed hypertension and 0 for no hypertension history) [12]. Hypertension diagnosis is based on systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, physician diagnosis, or current antihypertensive medication use.

Given that the components of eGDR (central obesity, blood pressure, chronic hyperglycemia) are all recognized clinical markers of IR and metabolic syndrome, its application has been extended and validated across broader clinical settings. Subsequent studies have employed eGDR as a composite indicator of IR and a predictor of cardiovascular complications not only in populations with type 1 and type 2 diabetes, but also in non‐diabetic individuals [13, 14, 15]. We therefore adopt eGDR as a practical composite metric to capture the spectrum of insulin sensitivity within general population samples.

Construction of Biological Aging Indices

Biological aging was quantified through a multidimensional modeling framework integrating 13 serum biomarkers spanning key physiological systems: metabolic regulation (albumin, total cholesterol [TC]), cellular function (lymphocyte percentage, white blood cell count), oxidative stress (uric acid), and organ function (creatinine, blood urea nitrogen) (Supporting Information S1: Table S1). To overcome the limitation of lacking high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein (hs‐CRP) measurement data in the 2011–2018 NHANES cycle [16], This study employed a validated CRP‐free biological age algorithm. Previous validation studies demonstrate that the CRP‐free model exhibits high predictive consistency with the original CRP‐inclusive model, while maintaining significant associations with mortality and age‐related diseases [17]. Furthermore, other research confirms strong correlations between ageing acceleration outcomes calculated using different biomarker sets (with or without CRP) [18].

Three indices were constructed: the KDM‐BA, derived from seven core biomarkers including HbA1c, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, alkaline phosphatase, albumin, systolic blood pressure (SBP), and TC; PA, modeled using a Cox proportional hazards framework incorporating CA and eight physiological parameters (mean corpuscular volume, red cell distribution width, lymphocyte percentage, glucose, albumin, alkaline phosphatase, creatinine and white blood cell count); and HD, calculated as the Mahalanobis distance [19] between an individual's biomarker profile and a healthy reference cohort (aged 20–30 years) from NHANES Phase III.

Although CRP is an effective inflammatory marker reflecting ageing, its absence is unlikely to affect the relative associations within our primary analytical focus. Any systemic measurement error should be non‐differentially distributed across exposure groups. Furthermore, the consistency of results across three independent biological ageing indicators (KDM‐BA, PA, and HD) further validates the robustness of our study conclusions regarding biomarker combination selection.

Accelerated biological ageing is primarily defined as a binary outcome: biological age (KDM‐BA or PA) exceeding CA. This represents a conventional and clinically interpretable threshold widely adopted in the literature [17, 20].

Details of the calculation methods for KDM‐BA, PA, and HD are provided in Supporting Information S1. All indices were computed using the BioAge package (https://github.com/dayoonkwon/BioAge) in R software (version 4.4.2).

Covariate Assessment

Covariates were selected based on established biological knowledge and prior literature linking eGDR, metabolic health, and ageing [21]. Trained investigators collected sociodemographic and health‐related data through standardized questionnaires. Sociodemographic variables encompassed three domains: (1) demographic characteristics (age, sex, race/ethnicity); (2) socioeconomic indicators (education level, marital status, and poverty‐to‐income ratio [PIR]); and (3) health behaviors and clinical profiles (alcohol use history, serum cotinine levels [a biomarker of nicotine exposure], diabetes (DM), hyperlipidemia, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and medication use [glucose‐lowering, antihypertensive, and lipid‐lowering agents]). Serum cotinine levels were categorized as < 1 and ≥ 1 ng/mL. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated according to the World Health Organization (WHO) definition (kg/m2). Diabetes was defined as a physician's diagnosis, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or use of diabetes medication or insulin. Hyperlipidemia was defined as a physician diagnosis, TC ≥ 240 mg/dL, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥ 160 mg/dL, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol < 40 mg/dL, triglycerides ≥ 200 mg/dL, or use of lipid‐lowering medication. CVD encompasses congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, angina pectoris, and stroke. Core demographic characteristics (age, sex, race/ethnicity) and socioeconomic status indicators (education level, marital status, and PIR) were treated as confounding factors. Key health behaviors (alcohol use history and serum cotinine levels), clinical conditions (DM, hyperlipidemia, CVD), and associated medications may represent both consequences of IR and contributors to biological ageing, thus occupying causal pathways. Adjusting for these variables may introduce estimation bias by eliminating genuine effects. Consequently, they were excluded from the primary model, though their mechanisms and effect‐modifying roles were explored via sensitivity analyses and subgroup analyses.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data were presented as weighted averages (95% confidence intervals [CI]), while categorical data were expressed through adjusted frequency distributions (%) accounting for sample weighting. To assess inter‐group differences in the eGDR cohort, we employed analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare weighted means for continuous variables and the chi‐squared test to compare weighted percentages for categorical variables. Weighted multivariable linear and logistic regression models were employed to estimate β coefficients or odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CI for associations between eGDR and biological aging indices (HD, KDM‐BA, PA, and KDM‐BA/PA accelerations). We constructed three regression models: Model 1 adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 further adjusted for race/ethnicity, education, marital status, PIR, and BMI; Model 3 additionally adjusted for cotinine, alcohol use, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease history, antihypertensive medications, lipid‐lowering medications, and glucose‐lowering medications. Model 3 served as a sensitivity analysis to assess the stability of estimates after adjusting for a broader range of potential confounders. To evaluate potential non‐linear exposure‐response patterns linking eGDR to senescence‐related outcomes, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models incorporating four knot points (at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles) were implemented, with covariate adjustments based on Model 3 specifications. Non‐linearity was formally tested using likelihood ratio tests. Subgroup analyses categorized participants according to age strata, sex, racial/ethnic background, educational attainment, marital status, PIR, BMI, cotinine concentrations, alcohol intake, hyperlipidemia, DM, CVD, antihypertensive, lipid‐modifying, and glucose‐regulating agents, applying covariate adjustments aligned with Model 3 parameters. All statistical examinations were performed using R software (version 4.4.2), with a two‐tailed p < 0.05 threshold applied to define statistical significance.

In the overall study population, KDM‐BA exhibited a weighted mean of 43.92 years (95%CI: 43.47, 44.38), while PA demonstrated a mean value of 45.19 years (95%CI: 44.65, 45.73). For HD, the weighted average was 1.17 (95%CI: 1.15, 1.18). Relationship of eGDR With Biological Aging

Following covariate adjustment in Model 2, every 1‐unit increment in eGDR demonstrated a 1.88‐year decrease in KDM‐BA (β = −1.88, 95%CI: −2.01, −1.76) (Table 2). Similarly, a per‐unit elevation in eGDR demonstrated a 0.60‐year reduction in PA (β = −0.60, 95%CI: −0.67, −0.54) (Table 2). Additionally, each 1‐unit elevation in eGDR correlated with a 0.16‐unit reduction in HD (β = −0.16, 95%CI: −0.17, −0.15) (Table 2). Relative to the lowest eGDR tertile (T1), individuals in the highest tertile (T3) had an OR of 0.13 (95%CI: 0.10, 0.15) for KDM‐BA acceleration and an OR of 0.40 (95%CI: 0.34, 0.48) for PA acceleration (Table 3). The RCS analytical approach additionally uncovered an inverse nonlinear correlation linking eGDR to biological aging (p for nonlinear < 0.001) (Figure 2). All curves demonstrate an effect threshold at eGDR = 7.95: within the eGDR < 7.95 range, the β value for the outcome measure decreases rapidly with increasing eGDR, indicating a strong protective effect. When eGDR > 7.95, the curve's slope flattens and approaches a plateau, signifying a marked attenuation of the protective effect.

Stratified Examination of eGDR and Biological Aging

Subgroup analyses revealed significant interactions between eGDR and age, sex, race/ethnicity, BMI, DM, as well as the utilization of cholesterol‐reducing or blood sugar‐regulating medications (p for interaction < 0.05) (Figure 3 and Supporting Information S1: Figure S1). For age subgroups, the inverse association between eGDR and all three biological aging indices was consistently stronger in younger adults (age < 60 years) than in older adults (age ≥ 60 years). Specifically, for KDM‐BA, each 1‐unit increment in eGDR was associated with a 3.39‐year reduction in younger adults (β = −3.39, 95%CI: −3.53, −3.24) versus a 2.08‐year reduction in older adults (β = −2.08, 95%CI: −2.28, −1.88). Similarly, for PA, eGDR was associated with a 2.31‐year reduction in younger adults (β = −2.31, 95%CI: −2.43, −2.18) versus a 0.51‐year reduction in older adults (β = −0.51, 95%CI: −0.68, −0.35). For HD, eGDR was associated with a 0.17‐unit reduction in younger adults (β = −0.17, 95%CI: −0.18, −0.16) versus a 0.14‐unit reduction in older adults (β = −0.14, 95%CI: −0.16, −0.13).

Sensitivity Analysis

To assess the robustness of the association between eGDR and biological age, we conducted a sensitivity analysis. After comprehensive adjustment for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, marital status, PIR, BMI, cotinine, alcohol use, hyperlipidemia, DM, CVD, antihypertensive medications, lipid‐lowering medications, and glucose‐lowering medications (Model 3), the association between eGDR and biological age remained statistically significant (Supporting Information S1: Tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

This nationally representative observational study identifies a consistent independent association between higher eGDR and lower biological age acceleration, as assessed by HD, KDM‐BA, and PA across three distinct biological aging indices. Following multivariable adjustment, each 1‐unit increase in eGDR was associated with a 1.88‐year lower KDM‐BA, a 0.40‐year lower PA, and a 0.16‐unit lower HD. Additionally, participants in the highest eGDR tertile (T3) exhibited the strongest association with lower levels of biological aging overall. These findings extend prior observational evidence linking IR to epigenetic aging, and establish eGDR as a robust predictor of multisystem physiological aging in the general adult population.

While higher eGDR was consistently associated with younger biological age across all three indices, the effect sizes differed meaningfully. The association was strongest for KDM‐BA (β = −1.88 years per unit), intermediate for PA (β = −0.40 years), and most modest for Homeostatic Dysregulation (β = −0.16 units). These differences align with the indices' designs: KDM‐BA measures deviation from an ideal age‐specific physiology, PA reflects mortality risk, and HD quantifies multivariate distance from a healthy reference independent of age. Thus, eGDR shows the closest link to metrics that directly gauge the pace of physiological aging.

As a noninvasive surrogate marker of insulin sensitivity, eGDR integrates hypertension status, WC, and HbA1c [12]. IR serves as a crucial factor in metabolic and aging‐related conditions [22, 23, 24, 25, 26], and a decrease in eGDR has been associated with heightened risks of CVD, stroke, metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease, and mortality [27, 28, 29, 30], especially in people with metabolic syndrome or DM [31, 32]. Recent research [21] has found that lower eGDR levels may be associated with both accelerated ageing and increased mortality risk in patients with cardiovascular‐renal metabolic syndrome, with accelerated ageing potentially mediating the relationship between eGDR and mortality. Moreover, a cross‐sectional study from China similarly confirmed [33] that there exists a significant inverse correlation between eGDR in middle‐aged and older adults and accelerated ageing. Our findings—demonstrating an inverse association between eGDR and biological aging—extend this evidence by directly linking insulin sensitivity to integrative metrics of organismal aging. Previous research using NHANES data has demonstrated that a 1‐unit increase in the TyG index is associated with a 1.64‐year increase in biological age [10]. In the present study, we observed that a 1‐unit increase in eGDR was associated with a 1.88‐year decrease in KDM‐BA. The stronger magnitude of association per unit change may be attributed to the broader physiological scope of eGDR, which incorporates domains of adiposity distribution (WC) and vascular health (hypertension) beyond glycemic and lipid control. This suggests that the multisystem dysregulation summarized by eGDR might be a more robust correlate of the organism‐wide aging process than indices reflecting a narrower metabolic spectrum.

The observed association may be explained through several plausible, interconnected mechanisms involving the individual components of eGDR and core aging pathways. Mechanistically, eGDR components directly influence hallmarks of aging: abdominal obesity promotes inflammation via adipose tissue macrophage infiltration [34, 35]; chronic hyperglycemia accelerates telomere attrition through reactive oxygen species [36, 37]; and hypertension induces vascular endothelial senescence via mechanical stress [38]. Furthermore, insulin signaling is a known modulator of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathways—key controllers of cellular senescence and autophagy [39]. We hypothesize that the confluence of these component‐specific effects, potentially mediated through shared pathways like mTOR/AMPK, could drive the accelerated biological aging captured by KDM‐BA and PA in our study. This mechanistic model, particularly regarding the roles of epigenetic aging and cellular senescence, remains speculative and warrants direct investigation in future longitudinal and experimental studies.

Subgroup analyses revealed clinically meaningful heterogeneity: the inverse association between higher eGDR and lower biological age was more pronounced in younger adults (< 60 years). This pattern of a stronger association in younger individuals is consistent with the concept of “metabolic memory” derived from prior research [40]. That theory posits that early‐life metabolic status, such as insulin sensitivity, may have lasting programming effects on the trajectory of age‐related damage accumulation. Conversely, attenuated eGDR effects in obese subgroups (BMI ≥ 30) may reflect senescence driven by ectopic lipid deposition and adipokine dysregulation [41], necessitating adjunctive weight management strategies. Despite these interactions, the persistent inverse association across all subgroups underscores the robustness of eGDR as a biomarker of aging, even in high‐risk populations. This parallels recent trials showing that intensive glycemic and lipid control delays biological aging [10].

Certain variables (such as HbA1c and SBP) were included in both eGDR and KDM‐BA calculations, potentially leading to mathematical coupling and collinearity, thereby affecting the estimation of association strength. To mitigate this issue, we employed multiple strategies: utilizing three independent ageing indices (KDM‐BA, PA, and HD), with the latter two excluding the aforementioned overlapping variables; and conducting sensitivity analyses incorporating comprehensive covariate adjustments. Results demonstrate that the negative association between eGDR and ageing remains consistent across all three indices, even when using indices excluding overlapping variables. This cross‐index consistency indicates that mathematical coupling did not substantially alter core conclusions, although residual bias from unmeasured overlapping pathways cannot be entirely ruled out.

These findings extend prior work on eGDR's metabolic implications by establishing its link to biological aging. Clinically, eGDR—calculated using routine parameters (hypertension, WC, HbA1c)—offers a practical tool for personalized aging risk stratification. Future studies should investigate whether eGDR‐guided interventions (e.g., lifestyle modification, GLP‐1 agonists) mitigate biological aging, particularly in individuals with early metabolic dysfunction.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths include the nationally representative data and methodological rigor through the concurrent use of HD, KDM‐BA, and PA. The observed 1.88‐year reduction in KDM‐BA per eGDR unit aligns with experimental models where insulin sensitizers (e.g., metformin) reduce senescent cell burden [42]. This study has several important limitations that warrant careful consideration during interpretation. Firstly, the cross‐sectional design precludes establishing the causal direction between insulin sensitivity and biological ageing. Secondly, the substantial exclusion of samples to obtain complete data may introduce selection bias, limiting the representativeness of the analyzed cohort. Thirdly, the overlap in indicators and physiological associations between eGDR and KDM‐BA may amplify statistical correlations, although their association with independent indicators PA and HD provides partial corroboration. Fourthly, despite adjusting for multiple covariates, residual confounders from unmeasured factors such as diet, physical activity, and genetics may persist. Finally, analyses based on complete data may further limit the generalizability of findings to broader populations.

Conclusion

Higher eGDR levels correlate with younger biological age and reduced risks of accelerated aging. These findings underscore eGDR's promise as a straightforward yet potent biomarker to pinpoint individuals with a higher likelihood of age‐related conditions and to steer precise interventions for healthier aging.
