Myeloid Cdc42 deficiency-mediated macrophage pyroptosis exacerbates diabetic cardiomyopathy in type 1 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Li-Xin Liu, Jing He, Feng Zhang, Xin-Qiao Li, Xin-Yu Xiao, Xiao-Ying Zhou, Qi-Kun Yang, Ming-Hui Wu, Xu-Hui Qiao, Bing-Qian Lu, Zhe-Yu Pang, Hong-Wei Sun, Zhao-Xia Chen, Ying-Jie HongShu, Hong-Bo Xin, Ke-Yu Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) is one of the severe complications in type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) patients with impaired cardiac function and faster progression to heart failure due to systolic malfunction. It has been demonstrated that macrophage-mediated chronic inflammation is closely associated with DCM. Cell division cycle 42 (Cdc42) plays a crucial role in regulating the polarization, migration and phagocytosis of macrophages, however, the underlying mechanism of Cdc42 in DCM remains to be elucidated.MethodsMouse DCM models with T1DM were generated using myeloid-specific Cdc42-knockout (Cdc42Mye) and Cdc42Flox/Flox (Cdc42Flox) male mice at 10–12 weeks old by injection of streptozotocin (STZ, 50 mg/kg/day) for 6 continuous days and then following the observation of 16 weeks. Cardiac functions were assessed by echocardiography in vivo, and cardiac morphological and histopathological alterations were evaluated by hematoxylin and eosin (HE), Masson’s trichrome, Picrosirius red, and immunohistochemistry staining, respectively. The infiltration of myeloid macrophages was examined by multiplex immunofluorescence tyramide signal amplification (TSA) assay or Rosa mTmG fluorescent myeloid tracking reporter in mice. Macrophage pyroptosis, cardiomyocyte damage and myofibroblast activation were evaluated by Western blot and immunofluorescence analysis. Additionally, single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data analysis was performed to explore macrophage-mediated signaling in DCM using publicly available GSE datasets from STZ-induced T1DM mice.ResultsMyeloid Cdc42 deletion exacerbated T1DM-induced cardiac dysfunctions and histopathological changes including cardiac fibrosis, and promoted T1DM-induced macrophage infiltration and M1 polarization of macrophages, and facilitated T1DM-induced pyroptosis by activating NLRP3 inflammasome in the hearts in mice. Notably, there were no significant differences between Cdc42Mye and Cdc42Flox mice under normal condition. In addition, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of scRNA-seq data indicated that low Cdc42 expression in macrophages was positively associated with the NF-κB signaling pathway in DCM. Mechanistically, we demonstrated that Cdc42 deficiency or inhibition aggravated T1DM-induced cardiomyocyte injury and cardiac fibrosis by activating ERK1/2-NF-κB-interleukin-1β signaling pathway-mediated macrophage pyroptosis.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that myeloid Cdc42 deficiency or inhibition aggravates T1DM-induced cardiomyocyte injury and cardiac fibrosis of DCM by promoting macrophage pyroptosis and inflammatory responses, which might provide a novel therapeutic target for immunomodulatory intervention in DCM of type 1 diabetes mellitus.Graphical abstract Myeloid-specific deletion of Cdc42 exacerbates DCM in T1DM via promoting macrophage pyroptosis by enhancing ERK1/2/NF-κB-NLRP3-IL-1β axis, and aggravates cardiac damage, inflammation, and fibrosis. What is currently known about this topic?

Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) is characterized by hyperglycemia due to insufficient insulin production. Currently, its prevalence and burden are substantial and growing since there are later-onset cases with the median age of 39 and more patients with T1DM surviving since a young age. Diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a common and severe complication defined by impaired cardiac function and is closely associated with faster progression to heart failure due to systolic malfunction in T1DM patients. However, its pathogenic mechanisms remain obscure.

What is the key research question?

How does Cdc42 modulate macrophage function to mediate diabetic cardiomyopathy in T1DM?

What is new?

This study defines Cdc42 as an important regulator of macrophage pyroptosis in DCM in T1DM. Myeloid-specific Cdc42 deficiency accelerates T1DM-induced cardiomyocyte injury and cardiac fibrosis by activating ERK1/2-NF-κB signaling-mediated macrophage pyroptosis, in which proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β released from macrophage pyroptosis play a pivotal role in the crosstalk between macrophages and cardiomyocytes and lead to severe cardiomyocytic damage and fibroblast activation in DCM.

How might this study influence clinical practice?

Targeting Cdc42-modulated macrophage pyroptosis in DCM may represent a novel therapeutic approach to coordinate macrophage-cardiomyocyte crosstalk and prevent cardiac dysfunction in T1DM.

Introduction

Diabetes threatens around 10% of the worldwide population and poses a serious health burden and is often characterized by chronic inflammation and metabolic disturbance with hyperglycemia due to insulin insufficiency, or insulin resistance, among which type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) with severe insulin deficiency comprises approximately 2–5% of cases in diabetes [1]. Currently, the prevalence and burden of T1DM are substantial and increasing rapidly due to vast cases of later-onset (with median age of onset 39 years) and more patients surviving since a young age [2]. Diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) is one of the major cardiovascular complications in diabetic patients with high morbidity and mortality, in which there are more than twice the risk in developing heart failure (HF) in patients with T1DM than people without diabetes [3–6].

Diverse forms of cell death may be involved in DCM, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, autosis, ferroptosis, and necrosis [7], and among these, pyroptosis acts as a key driving factor in the genesis and progression of DCM as well as in many chronic diseases [8]. Pyroptosis is a type of highly inflammatory form of programmed cell death and occurs primarily in immune cells such as macrophages and dendritic cells. It is often triggered by NLR family pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasomes via recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [9–11]. NLRP3 inflammasome activation subsequently converts pro-Caspase 1 into its active mature form, which in turn mediates the cleavage of gasdermin D (GSDMD) to generate its pore-forming N-terminal fragment (GSDMD-N) and processes pro-interleukin-1β (pro-IL-1β) into mature IL-1β, thereby driving pyroptotic cell death and downstream inflammation [12]. Macrophages, the first responders in immunosurveillance, play critical roles in maintaining tissue homeostasis [13], and they are highly adaptable and plastic with inflammatory or anti-inflammatory properties in responding to various stimuli [14]. It has been reported that macrophage pyroptosis contributes to the occurrence and progression of DCM with a close association between inflammation and high glucose levels in diabetic organisms [8, 15], but the underlying mechanisms of macrophage-mediated pyroptosis in diabetic cardiomyopathy, especially in T1DM, still remain poorly understood.

Cell division cycle 42 (Cdc42), a small GTPase of the Rho family, serves as a molecular switch for transitioning between inactive GDP-bound and active GTP-bound states to regulate a variety of cellular processes [16]. Martinon group has reported that Cdc42 acts as an essential regulator of PYRIN inflammasome activation in PYRIN inflammasome assembly via its GTPase-independent activity to defend against invading pathogens [17, 18]. Our previous work revealed that myeloid-specific deletion of Cdc42 favors M2 macrophage polarization in acute liver ischemia reperfusion (IR) and alleviates IR injury [19]. These data suggest that Cdc42 might play a role in the progression of DCM via modulating macrophage function in diabetes.

Here, we demonstrated that myeloid Cdc42 deficiency exacerbates cardiomyocytic injury and cardiac fibrosis in diabetic cardiomyopathy via activating ERK1/2-NF-κB signaling-mediated macrophage pyroptosis in T1DM. Our findings highlight that Cdc42 might serve as a potential therapeutic target in mitigating diabetic cardiomyopathy, especially in T1DM, and offer new insights into its pathogenic mechanisms.

Methods

Animals

Myeloid-specific Cdc42-deficient (Cdc42Mye and Cdc42MyeROSAmTmG), Cdc42Flox and ROSAmTmGLyz mice were obtained as described previously [19]. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with China Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanchang University. Mice were maintained in individual ventilated cages with irradiated standard rodent diet (XTI01FZ-010, Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bio-engineering Co, Ltd, Nanjing, China) and autoclaved domestic water ad libitum at 20 ℃ − 25 ℃, humidity of 30% to 70% and a 12/12-hour light/dark cycle (7:00AM-7:00PM) in SPF transgenic facility, Institute of Translational Medicine, Nanchang University, China. Type 1 diabetic modeling was established in mice as previously described with minor modification [20]. In brief, male mice at 10-12-week-old were used and administrated intraperitoneally with streptozotocin (STZ, Sigma, S0130) at 50 mg/kg/day for 6 consecutive days, in which STZ was prepared in 0.1 mol/L sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.5, sterilized) and injected within 15 min, and control group received an equal volume of sodium citrate buffer about 100 µl/10 g body weight. The levels of fasting blood glucose (FBG) were examined at two weeks post STZ administration with tail-snipping blood by Sinocare GA-3 glucose meter. The mice with FBG ≥ 13.3 mM were considered diabetic. Subsequently, all the experimental mice were raised for another 14 weeks, and blood glucose and body weight were checked biweekly.

Echocardiography

As previously described [21], cardiac function was assessed with Vevo 3100 echocardiography system (Visual Sonics, Toronto, Ontario, Canada) in mice after 16 weeks’ STZ modeling. After being anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane, the mouse was placed in a supine position on a temperature-controlled animal platform. Two-dimensional long-axis and short-axis views were acquired with an MX400 high-frequency probe (18–38 MHz). Cardiovascular parameters including left ventricular internal dimensions, wall motion, ejection fraction, and fractional shortening were determined to quantify cardiac function. All the echocardiographic images were analyzed using VevoLab-3.2.6 software.

Glucose tolerance tests

Glucose tolerance tests (GTT) were performed in mice two weeks prior to sample collection. Blood glucose levels were measured at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min in mice after 16 h fasting following intraperitoneal injection of 2.0 g/kg glucose (Sigma, G6152).

Heart tissue staining

Heart tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. After dewaxing and rehydration, tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E, Servicebio, G1076). Additional paraffin sections were stained with Picrosirius red (Solarbio, G1473) and Masson’s Trichrome Stain (Leagene, DC0033) to assess myocardial fibrosis. The photo images were acquired under light microscopy to assess structural and morphological alterations.

For immunohistochemistry, sections were deparaffinized, hydrated and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval (0.01 M sodium citrate buffer, pH = 6.0). Slides were blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min and in 3% BSA for 30 min, then incubated with iNOS (Proteintech, 22226-1-AP, 1:200) and α-SMA (CST, 19245, 1:400) antibodies overnight at 4 ℃. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibodies were then applied for 1 h at 37 ℃. Immunoreactivity was detected by diaminobenzidine (DAB-2031, MXB Biotechnologies, China). Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, and the positive area was analyzed by ImageJ-win64 software.

TSA multiplex fluorescent staining

Tyramide Signal Amplification (TSA) staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Ai Fang biological, AFIHC025). Briefly, endogenous peroxidases in cells or tissue slides were blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and nonspecific binding was blocked with antibody diluents. Target proteins were first recognized by corresponding primary antibody and followed by HRP-labeled secondary antibody. Then, the tissue spatial fluorescent signals were revealed and amplified by HRP-mediated reaction with tyramide derivative dyes. Up to four antibodies were applied per slide, and critical stripping steps were rigorously performed between cycles to remove the primary-secondary complex. Finally, nuclei were labeled with DAPI (Servicebio, G1407) and the images were acquired using a ZEISS LSM 800 confocal microscope (version ZEN 3.3 software). The primary and secondary antibodies used in this experiment are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Immunofluorescence staining of pancreatic tissue

Pancreatic tissues were isolated and fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by routine dehydration and paraffin embedding. Paraffin Sect. (5 μm) were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed using sodium citrate buffer. The sections were incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min at room temperature to block endogenous peroxidase activity and reduce tissue autofluorescence. After blocking with 3% BSA to eliminate non-specific binding, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Insulin (Proteintech, 66198-1-Ig, 1:100) and Glucagon (Affinity, AF0166, 1:100). After washing with 0.2% Tween-20 in PBS, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 and 594 conjugated secondary antibodies ((Proteintech, SA00013-2 and SA00013-3, 1:500) for 1 h at room temperature, counterstained with DAPI, and observed under Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope.

Western blotting

Total proteins were extracted from tissues or cultured cells using RIPA lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 140 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate) supplemented with a protease (MCE, HY-K0010) and phosphatase (MCE, HY-K0023) inhibitor cocktail. The protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Sharebio, SB-WB013). Equal amounts of protein (typically 20–30 µg) were separated by 8–12% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (GE Healthcare). The NC membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma, B2064) or non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.2% Tween-20 (TBST) for 2 h at room temperature to prevent non-specific binding, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The protein signals were amplified with Goat anti-Rabbit/Mouse IgG (H + L) secondary antibody, HRP for 2 h at room temperature and detected and analyzed using the ChemiDoc™ XRS+ imaging system and Image Lab 6.0.1 or Image J-win64 software. The primary and secondary antibodies used in this experiment are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Single-cell RNA-seq (ScRNA-seq) data processing and analysis

To explore macrophage heterogeneity and Cdc42-associated signaling alterations in diabetic cardiomyopathy, two publicly available mouse cardiac scRNA-seq datasets (GSE213337 [22], one normal and one diabetic heart samples and GSE244475 [23], three normal and four diabetic samples) were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Quality control included median absolute deviation (MAD) thresholds (± 3 MAD on log10 transformed nFeature_RNA and nCount_RNA), a mitochondrial gene proportion < 20%, doublet removal with scDblFinder, and exclusion of genes detected in fewer than three cells. For the GSE244475 dataset, which encompasses multiple organs (heart, liver, kidney, and spleen), hashtag oligonucleotide (HTO) signals were separated from RNA features, normalized by centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation, and demultiplexed with HTODemux (positive.quantile = 0.95). Only cells assigned to the heart hashtag were retained for further analysis. Data were normalized using SCTransform, reduced by principal component analysis (PCA), and integrated via Seurat-based canonical correlation analysis (CCA) to correct batch effects. Shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graph construction, clustering (resolution = 0.8), and UMAP visualization were performed. Cell types were annotated based on canonical marker genes. Cell–cell communication analysis was performed with CellChat to identify significant ligand–receptor interactions. For macrophage subset analysis, macrophages were extracted and stratified into Cdc42-high and Cdc42-low groups according to normalized Cdc42 expression levels. Differential expression analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Genes were ranked by log2 fold change and subjected to gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using the clusterProfiler package. Genes with adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > log2(1.5) were considered significantly differentially expressed and were further analyzed by Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from cultured cells using Trizol reagent (TIANGEN, DP424) and subsequently reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (AG, AG11728). The mRNA levels were then quantified by real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) using the 2×SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Low Rox) (Servicebio, G3321-05) on a QuantStudio™ Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad). Relative gene expressions were calculated by the 2ΔΔCt method with GAPDH normalization. Primer sequences were listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Levels of glycated hemoglobin (GHb, Jianglaibio, JL20567) and B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP, Jianglaibio, JL12884) were measured in mouse serum using commercial ELISA kits, following the manufacturers’ instructions. For cardiac inflammatory cytokines, interleukin-6 (IL-6, Jianglaibio, JL20268), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α, Jianglaibio, JL10484), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β, Jianglaibio, JL18442) were quantified in whole-heart tissue homogenates with the corresponding ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Briefly, standards and appropriately diluted samples were added to antibody-precoated 96-well plates and incubated, followed by incubation with the horseradish peroxidase-conjugated detection antibody and chromogenic substrate. The reaction was stopped with acidic stop solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. All samples were assayed in duplicate, with at least 3–5 biological samples per group. Analyte concentrations were calculated from the respective standard curves generated by regression analysis.

Cell culture

H9c2 rat cardiomyoblasts (ATCC, CRL-1446TM) and NIH/3T3 fibroblasts (ATCC, CRL-1658) were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cell-Box, AUS-01 S-02-S) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Solarbio, P1400) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO₂. For experimental treatments, cells were seeded around 3–5 × 104/cm2 and allowed to adhere for 24 h prior to intervention.

Isolation and culture of peritoneal macrophage

To collect peritoneal myeloid macrophages, 3% thioglycollate broth (BD, 211716) was injected into the peritoneal cavity of mice for increasing monocyte migration into the peritoneum. After 72 h, the cells were harvested from peritoneal lavage with ice-cold sterile PBS and centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min; then the cell pellets were resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 5% FBS. Cells were plated at 3–5 × 104/cm2 and allowed to adhere for 3 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Non-adherent cells were removed by gentle washing with PBS, and the adherent population was utilized as peritoneal macrophages for subsequent experiments. High glucose (HG, at 33.3 mM) plus lipopolysaccharides (LPS, Sigma, L2880, at 100 ng/mL) were used to mimic hyperglycemia conditions in vitro and Cdc42 specific inhibitor ML141 (MCE, HY-12755) or ERK kinase inhibitor (U0126, MCE, 109511-58-2) were used at 10 µM, respectively. To explore the interaction between macrophages and cardiomyocytes or fibroblasts, the conditioned medium from MPMs or H9c2 cells was collected under HG/LPS stimulation and used reciprocally. In the meantime, interleukin-1 receptor (IL-1R) antagonist Anakinra (MCE, HY-108841) was used to pretreat cardiomyocytes at 100 nM for 30 min. In addition, MPMs were stimulated with 100 ng/mL recombinant HMGB1 (MCE, HY-P700182AF) for 24 h, followed by immunofluorescence and qPCR detection.

Cell death assay

H9c2 cells were treated with conditioned medium from MPMs in 48-well plate for 24 h. Then, Hoechst 33342/Propidium iodide (PI) mixture (Solarbio, CA1120) was added to the well and incubated for 20–30 min at 4 °C in the dark according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cell apoptosis and necrosis were assessed under Olympus BX63 fluorescence microscopy.

Statistical analysis

The data were presented as the mean ± standard deviations (SD). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software. Unpaired Student’s t-test was used between two groups. For three or more groups, data were assessed by two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test and a p value of < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Myeloid Cdc42 deficiency exacerbated cardiac dysfunction and cardiac fibrosis in STZ-induced T1DM mice

To investigate the role of Cdc42 in diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM), the mouse models of type 1 diabetic mellitus (T1DM) were generated by injection of streptozotocin (STZ) using myeloid-specific Cdc42 knockout mice (Cdc42Mye) and Cdc42Flox mice with C57BL/6 genetic background (Fig. 1A). The deletion of myeloid Cdc42 was confirmed at both the protein and mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. S1A–F). Our results showed that STZ-induced T1DM mice had lower body weight (Fig. 1B), elevation of blood glucose (Fig. 1C) and glycated hemoglobin (GHb) (Fig. 1D), as well as the impaired glucose tolerance (GTT, Fig. 1E and F) and reduction of pancreatic beta cells (Fig. 1G) post STZ administration for 16 weeks compared with control mice, but there were no significant differences between Cdc42Mye and Cdc42Flox T1DM mice. However, myeloid Cdc42 deficiency significantly aggravated cardiac dysfunctions, evidenced by the decreased left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular fraction shortening (LVFS), and the increased systolic left ventricular internal dimension (diameter) and volume (Fig. 1H–L), and remarkably elevated serum BNP (Fig. 1M–O), which is one of the biomarkers of heart failure [24, 25], in Cdc42Mye T1DM mice compared with Cdc42Flox T1DM mice. In addition, HE staining showed that myeloid Cdc42 deletion remarkably accelerated T1DM-induced disarray of myocardial structure (Fig. 2C) although the ratios of heart weight-to-tibia length (HW/TL) were similar between Cdc42Mye and Cdc42Flox T1DM mice (Fig. 2A and B). Masson’s trichrome and Picrosirius red staining showed that myeloid Cdc42 deletion remarkably increased T1DM-induced cardiac fibrosis including perivascular fibrosis in Cdc42Mye mice compared with Cdc42Flox mice (Fig. 2C–E, Supplementary Fig. S2A and B). Furthermore, myeloid Cdc42 deficiency markedly increased T1DM-induced elevated expressions of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) in hearts of Cdc42Mye mice compared with Cdc42Flox mice (Fig. 2C and F–H). Notably, there were no significant differences in all the above parameters including cardiac functions between Cdc42Mye and Cdc42Flox mice under normal condition. These results indicated that myeloid Cdc42 deficiency promoted T1DM-induced cardiac dysfunctions and cardiac fibrosis to aggravate DCM in mice.

Myeloid Cdc42 deficiency promoted macrophage infiltration and M1 polarization in hearts of T1DM mice

It has been reported that macrophages participate in the pathogenesis and progression of DCM [26]. To determine the role of myeloid Cdc42 in DCM, myeloid lineage was traced and evaluated with the fluorescent myeloid-derived macrophages in hearts using Rosa mTmG/LyzCre reporter mice (mTmG). As shown in Fig. 3A, fluorescent myeloid-derived GFP positive cells were remarkably increased in the hearts of Cdc42MyeROSAmTmG (Cdc42 ROSA Lyz) T1DM mice compared with ROSAmTmG (Rosa Lyz) T1DM mice. Meanwhile, a multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) analysis based on tyramide signal amplification method was performed with primary antibodies of macrophage markers including M1 polarization markers such as CD11b, iNOS and CD86 or M2 polarization markers such as CD11b, CD163 and CD206. The results showed that the ratio of co-localization of iNOS (Fig. 3B and C) or CD86 (Fig. 3F and G) to CD11b was significantly increased in cardiac tissues in Cdc42Mye T1DM mice, whereas there was no obvious difference in the ratio of colocalization of CD163 (Fig. 3B and D) or CD206 (Fig. 3F and H) with CD11b in the cardiac tissues between Cdc42Mye and Cdc42Flox T1DM mice. In addition, myeloid Cdc42 deletion markedly increased the ratio of co-localized iNOS/CD163, CD86/CD206 or SPP1/CD86 in the hearts of T1DM mice (Fig. 3E and I, Supplementary Fig. S3A and B). Furthermore, our immunohistochemical staining and western blot results showed that myeloid Cdc42 deletion remarkably increased iNOS expression in cardiac tissues of the Cdc42Mye T1DM mice compared with that of Cdc42Flox T1DM mice (Supplementary Fig. S3C–F). These results indicated that infiltrated macrophages were primarily M1 type of macrophages which might participate in the inflammatory and profibrotic responses in hearts, and myeloid Cdc42 deletion promoted the infiltration of macrophages and M1 polarization in DCM during T1DM.

To determine the role of M1 macrophage-mediated proinflammatory responses in DCM, we further examined the expressions of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1ꞵ in hearts of T1DM mice. Pyroptosis is an inflammatory programmed cell death process often occurring in immune cells with the involvement of NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, in which the cleaved active N-terminal fragment (GSDMD-N) forms membrane pores to facilitate the secretion of key pro-inflammatory mediators such as cleaved-IL-1β and IL-18 to trigger pyroptosis [30]. More studies indicated that pyroptosis plays a critical role in the progression of DCM [8, 31]. To explore the effects of macrophage Cdc42 on pyroptosis, we examined a set of pyroptosis-related biomarkers in the heart tissues. The results showed that the expressions of the pyroptosis related proteins such as NLRP3, Caspase 1 (p25), IL-1ꞵ (p17), AIM2, ASC, GSDMD-N and IL-1ꞵ (p31) were significantly upregulated in cardiac tissues of Cdc42Mye T1DM mice compared with those of Cdc42Flox T1DM mice (Fig. 5A–J). Consistent with these results, immunofluorescent staining also showed that myeloid Cdc42 deletion exacerbated T1DM-induced the increases of NLRP3/GSDMD-N expressions in Cdc42Mye mice compared with those of Cdc42Flox mice (Fig. 5K-M).

In addition, we performed integrative analysis of publicly available mouse cardiac single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets (GSE213337 and GSE244475). After merging and quality control, 18, 115 high-quality cells and 18, 425 genes were retained for downstream analysis. UMAP visualization revealed a prominentclear batch effect between the two datasets (Fig. 7A), which was effectively corrected using a canonical correlation analysis (CCA)-based integration strategy, resulting in well-mixed cell populations (Fig. 7B). Cell types were manually annotated based on canonical marker genes, including cardiomyocytes (Tnnt2, Myh6, Actc1), fibroblasts (Col1a1, Col1a2, Pdgfra), and macrophages (Adgre1, Cd68, Csf1r) (Fig. 7C and D). Collectively, these results support the notion that cardiomyocyte–macrophage crosstalk in DCM is dominated by inflammatory, chemotactic, and extracellular matrix-associated signaling pathways, which may contribute to chronic inflammation and adverse cardiac remodeling.

To explore the potential role of Cdc42 in diabetic macrophages, we stratified macrophages into Cdc42-high and Cdc42-low groups based on mean expression. Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes between the two groups revealed predominant involvement in immune and inflammatory processes. Myeloid Cdc42 deficiency aggravated macrophage pyroptosis and cardiomyocyte damage via ERK1/2-NF-ҡB signaling under HG/LPS stimulation

Given that the above bioinformatics analyses indicated a close association of Cdc42 with cytokine regulation and NF-κB signaling, and considering that NF-κB is a key transcriptional regulator of NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis [33], we next investigated whether Cdc42 deficiency affects macrophage pyroptosis through related signaling pathways under a high glucose inflammatory environment. We applied high glucose (HG, at 33.3 mM) plus lipopolysaccharides (LPS, at 100 ng/mL) to mimic the hyperglycemia-inflammatory microenvironment in vitro. The results showed that myeloid Cdc42 deficiency markedly enhanced the expressions of ERK1/2 phosphorylation, NLRP3, IL-1ꞵ and iNOS in macrophages under HG/LPS stimulation, although high glucose alone had no effect on ERK1/2 activation or pyroptosis-related protein expression (Supplementary Fig. 6A–E). In addition, the expressions of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α were also significantly upregulated in Cdc42-deficient macrophages with HG/LPS stimulation (Supplementary Fig. S6F–H). These results suggested that myeloid Cdc42 deficiency might aggravate macrophage pyroptosis via ERK1/2 signaling under HG/LPS conditions.

NLRP3 inflammasome and its mediated pyroptosis are the key driving force in diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) [15]. To further clarify the role of Cdc42 in ERK1/2 signaling-mediated activation of NLRP3 inflammasome/pyroptosis, U0126, an ERK kinase inhibitor, was used in the following experiments. Mouse peritoneal macrophages (MPMs) were preincubated with 10 µM U0126 for 1 h, followed by HG/LPS stimulation for 24 h. As shown in Fig. 8A–H, HG/LPS-mediated NLRP3 inflammasome/pyroptosis was significantly suppressed by U0126 in Cdc42Mye MPMs, as evidenced by reduced expressions of p-ERK1/2, p-NF-ҡB, NLRP3, GSDMD (p53/30) and IL-1ꞵ (p35/17). In addition, the similar results were confirmed by the immunofluorescence analysis in Cdc42Mye MPMs (Fig. 8I–M). Furthermore, HG/LPS-induced upregulated expression of iNOS, a key pro-inflammatory mediator, was also remarkably suppressed by U0126 in Cdc42Mye MPMs (Supplementary Fig. S6I–L). Subsequently, we conducted the experiments in H9c2 cells in which they were stimulated with conditioned medium (CM) derived from mouse peritoneal macrophages (MPMs) to evaluate whether there were reciprocal effects from MPMs to cardiomyocytes. The results showed that blocking macrophage pyroptosis by the ERK kinase inhibitor, U0126, significantly reduced the detrimental effects from Cdc42Mye MPMs’ CM on cardiomyocytes and attenuated cardiomyocytic damage in H9c2 cells (Fig. 8N and O). Taken together, these results indicated that myeloid Cdc42 deficiency promoted HG/LPS-induced macrophage pyroptosis and M1 polarization via activating ERK1/2-NF-ҡB signaling pathway, thereby aggravating cardiac injury in DCM.

Deficiency or inhibition of myeloid Cdc42 exacerbated macrophage pyroptosis-mediated cardiomyocyte damage and fibroblast activation under HG/LPS stimulation

Pyroptosis-mediated inflammatory responses are the triggers of persistent myocardial damage and cardiac dysfunction in DCM progression [34]. As we demonstrated in the present study, macrophages were the source of pyroptosis in DCM in T1DM. To further determine the role of Cdc42 in macrophage pyroptosis, the co-culture systems were set up to assess the interaction between macrophages and cardiomyocytes (H9c2) or macrophages and fibroblasts (3T3), in which conditioned medium (CM) was collected from either cardiomyocytes or macrophages and used reciprocally in culture (Fig. 9A and H). As depicted in Fig. 9A, the conditioned medium was collected from cardiomyocytes stimulated with HG/LPS and used for MPMs’ culture system, and the results showed that myeloid Cdc42 deficiency significantly enhanced macrophage pyroptosis as evidenced by elevated expressions of NLRP3, GSDMD-N, IL-1β (p17) and cleaved-Caspase 1 (Fig. 9B–G), indicating that myeloid Cdc42 deficiency promoted macrophage pyroptosis in response to HG/LPS-induced cardiomyocyte damage. On the other hand, the conditioned medium from Cdc42Mye macrophages under HG/LPS stimulation significantly exacerbated H9c2 cell injury, as evidenced by an increased proportion of propidium iodide (PI)-positive cells. Moreover, pretreating H9c2 cells with Anakinra (Ana), an IL-1R antagonist, was able to relieve cardiomyocyte damage effects from macrophage’s extracellular secretions (Fig. 9I and J). It is well known that pyroptosis, a highly inflammatory form of programmed cell death during immune responses, is often initiated when pattern‑recognition receptors in immune cells sense PAMPs and DAMPs, thereby amplifying innate immune activation and causing progressive cellular and tissue injury [35]. To identify which DAMPs might drive NLRP3 inflammasome activation in DCM, we examined the expressions of canonical DAMPs such as HMGB1 and HSP70 in cardiac tissues from T1DM mice. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S7A–C, myeloid Cdc42 deficiency markedly increased cardiac HMGB1 levels in T1DM mice, whereas HSP70 expression was not significantly altered. Next, we further detected the extracellular HMGB1 levels in the conditioned medium of cardiomyocytes after HG/LPS stimulation in vitro. The results showed that cardiomyocytic HMGB1 was prominently secreted into the extracellular space after thoroughly washing away residual HG/LPS (Supplementary Fig. S8A and B). To determine the susceptibility of MPMs to HMGB1-mediated DAMP signaling, MPMs were challenged with high glucose combined with recombinant HMGB1 (HG/rHMGB1). Under this intervention, myeloid Cdc42 deficiency markedly augmented macrophage pyroptosis, accompanied by significant upregulation of NLRP3, cleaved Caspase-1, and GSDMD-N (Supplementary Fig. S8C–H). Finally, treatment of H9c2 cardiomyocytes and 3T3 fibroblasts with conditioned medium collected from HG/rHMGB1-stimulated Cdc42Mye macrophages further aggravated H9c2 cell necrosis (Supplementary Fig. S8I and J) and promoted the activation of 3T3 myofibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. S8K and L).

Discussion

Diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) is the most common and critical complication in diabetes with a high morbidity and mortality due to chronic hyperglycemia and inflammatory immune responses, especially in patients with T1DM [3–6]. T1DM is characterized by hyperglycemia when pancreatic β-cells do not produce enough insulin, and is becoming global epidemic due to its late-onset cases with a median age of 39 years and more patients surviving since a young age [2]. However, the mechanism of DCM in T1DM remains obscure, since most DCM-related research to date has been conducted in T2DM, and there are distinct cardiac remodeling processes and pathogenic mechanisms involved in DCM progression between T1DM and T2DM [36–38]. In the present study, we observed that myeloid Cdc42 deficiency significantly exacerbated cardiac dysfunction, elevated inflammatory responses and fibrosis in heart tissues in STZ-induced T1DM mice. Importantly, myeloid-specific Cdc42 deficiency aggravated macrophage pyroptosis, promoted the infiltration of monocytes/macrophages into the myocardium, enhanced cardiac inflammatory responses, cardiomyocyte damage and fibroblast activation in the progression of DCM in T1DM.

Cell division cycle 42 (Cdc42) serves as a signaling switch to participate in many physiological and pathophysiological processes, and its alteration might contribute to the pathogenesis of various diseases, such as cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and cardiovascular diseases [39]. Cdc42 plays a pivotal role in regulating macrophage functions, including the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the modulation of phagocytic activity [40]. Martinon group reported that Cdc42 acts as an essential regulator of PYRIN inflammasome activation in both upstream and downstream of PYRIN inflammasome assembly via GTPase-independent activity in U937 cells in vitro [18]. It has been reported that pyroptosis, a proinflammatory programmed cell death, is tightly associated with the occurrence and development of DCM [41]. Macrophage pyroptosis plays a critical role in the occurrence and progression of DCM via modulating inflammatory responses in diabetic organisms [8, 15]. However, the underlying regulatory mechanism of macrophage pyroptosis in DCM still remains unclear. There are two activation pathways for pyroptosis: the canonical pathway, which depends on caspase-1, and the noncanonical pathway, which depends on caspase-4/5/11 activation [42, 43]. The canonical pyroptosis pathway is initiated when inflammasomes recognize endogenous and exogenous damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs and PAMPs), leading to the activation of pro-caspase 1 into its mature form, caspase-1 [44]. Caspase-1 modulates NF-κB signaling and facilitates the cleavage of pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their active forms [45]. Additionally, caspase-1 cleaves gasdermin D (GSDMD) to generate N-terminal (GSDMD-N) and C-terminal fragments, in which GSDMD-N oligomerizes on the plasma membrane, forming pores that enable the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18, resulting in cell swelling, increasing membrane permeability and eventually cell death [45–47]. NF-κB family members are known to be involved in the process of inflammatory cytokine release [48]. The degradation of IҡBα facilitates nuclear translocation of p65, initiating transcription of target genes [49], promoting M1 polarization and pyroptosis [50], which eventually results in the release of a large number of mature inflammatory cytokines. Studies demonstrated that hyper-phosphorylation of MAPKs (JNK, p38, and ERK1/2) finally activated the transcription factor NF-ҡB and the follow-up inflammatory reactions [51]. In this study, we observed that myeloid Cdc42 deficiency significantly aggravated T1DM-induced cardiac dysfunction, cardiomyocyte injury, and myeloid macrophage infiltration in Cdc42Mye mice compared with Cdc42Flox mice although there were similar alterations in fasting blood glucose, glycated hemoglobin and pancreatic islet damage in these mice treated with STZ. Moreover, our study highlighted that myeloid Cdc42 deficiency enhanced T1DM-induced phosphorylation of ERK1/2-NF-κB and pyroptosis in diabetic hearts.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enables us to characterize and identify distinct cellular subpopulations in the heart, thereby deepening our understanding of the pathophysiological complexity of DCM [52]. In this study, we analyzed two publicly available scRNA‑seq datasets of STZ‑induced DCM in mice. Ligand–receptor interaction analysis revealed extensive intercellular communication between cardiomyocytes and macrophages, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further demonstrated that the NF‑κB signaling pathway was significantly activated in Cdc42‑low macrophages, supporting an association between reduced Cdc42 expression and the enhanced inflammatory activation observed in our T1DM model. High mobility group box 1 protein (HMGB1) is an important mediator in inflammatory processes. Under high glucose stress, damaged cardiomyocytes robustly release HMGB1 as a key damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP). Extracellular HMGB1 further amplifies inflammatory and pyroptotic signaling by activating surface receptors on macrophages, serving as a core upstream mediator that orchestrates the crosstalk between myocardial injury and abnormal macrophage activation [53]. Cardiomyocytes are the parenchyma cells responsible for contraction-relaxation function in heart and recruiting signals for macrophages in response to various stresses such as hyperglycemia, overloading stretch, neurohumoral overactivation and pathogen infection. Long-term hyperglycemia causes glucotoxicity which induces the injury of cardiac tissue through the excessive activation of immune system and inflammatory cascades and leads to cardiac remodeling, even heart failure [38, 54, 55]. We have observed elevated HMGB1 levels in DCM, especially in myeloid Cdc42-deficient T1DM mice. Among the immune cells, myeloid macrophages are promptly recruited into the heart in response to various stresses in cardiac remodeling [56]. In the present study, we revealed that myeloid Cdc42 deficiency predominantly elevated M1 macrophage polarization and inflammatory responses. We further demonstrated that absence or inhibition of myeloid Cdc42 exacerbated macrophage pyroptosis, promoted inflammatory cytokine release and amplified the inflammatory response upon exposure to HG/LPS-induced cardiomyocyte injury. Mechanistically, Cdc42 plays a critical role in myeloid macrophages by shaping microenvironmental sensing and the immune response, and Cdc42 deficiency promotes activation of the ERK1/2–NF-κB signaling pathway and enhances the NLRP3/Caspase‑1/GSDMD/IL‑1β signaling axis in macrophages following cardiomyocytic injury under hyperglycemia and inflammatory conditions. Furthermore, the released extracellular cytokines including IL6, IL-1β, and TNF-α from Cdc42Mye macrophages might further strengthen cardiomyocyte damage and fibroblast activation, which are consistent with the cardiomyopathy (DCM) phenotypes observed in vivo STZ-induced T1DM model. Interestingly, pretreating cardiomyocytes with IL-1R antagonist, Anakinra, partially prevented cardiomyocyte damage from extracellular components (possibly IL-1β) derived from Cdc42Mye macrophages due to macrophage pyroptosis under HG/LPS stimulation.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Due to the lack of accessible clinical sample cohorts, the clinical relevance between myeloid Cdc42 expression and cardiac function in diabetes patients remains to be confirmed. In addition, our study focused mainly on the role of myeloid Cdc42 in diabetic cardiomyopathy in T1DM (T1DM-DCM) and it might not be directly applicable to diabetic cardiomyopathy in T2DM (T2DM-DCM), since there are distinct structural, functional, and molecular characteristics in DCM between T1DM and T2DM, even though T2DM-DCM has already been widely studied in clinical and experimental research. In addition, it has been reported that macrophages with high SPP1 expression facilitate crosstalk with tumor cells or fibroblasts and promote tumor progression or adverse tissue remodeling via paracrine signaling, thereby accelerating collagen deposition [57, 58]. We observed an increased population of SPP1⁺/CD86⁺ macrophages in cardiac tissues in T1DM, especially in myeloid Cdc42-deficient mice. However, whether and how this macrophage subset contributes to the link between cardiac inflammation and remodeling in DCM remains unclear, and future studies are needed to confirm and validate the existence and role of SPP1⁺ macrophages in DCM.

Conclusions

In summary, our study demonstrated that myeloid Cdc42 deficiency exacerbated T1DM-induced cardiomyocyte injury and cardiac fibrosis by activating ERK1/2–NF‑κB signaling-mediated M1 polarization and pyroptosis of macrophages, leading to an amplified inflammatory response in the progression of DCM. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the molecular pathogenesis of DCM but also highlight Cdc42 as a potential therapeutic target to mitigate inflammation-driven cardiac damage in type 1 diabetes mellitus.
