Omphalocele-exstrophy-imperforate anus-spinal defect syndrome in a monozygotic twin pregnancy: a case report and literature review
Authors: Qing Hu, Hua Liao, Zhaomin Zeng, Hongyan Liu, Haiyan Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Omphalocele, Exstrophy, Imperforate Anus, and Spinal Defects Complex (OEIS complex) is a rare birth defect with a prevalence of 1 in 200,000–400000 pregnancies and 1 in 82,000–200000 live births. While cases in female monozygotic twins have been reported, the perinatal outcomes of the unaffected co-twin remain insufficiently addressed.Case presentationWe report a spontaneously conceived monochorionic monoamniotic twin pregnancy where Twin B was diagnosed with OEIS. Prenatal ultrasound at 12 + 4 weeks identified an abdominal cystic mass in Twin B, which enlarged progressively until 20 + 4 weeks (7.0 × 4.7 × 6.2 cm) and then decreased (3.3 × 2.5 × 2.0 cm) at 22 weeks, suggesting cloacal membrane rupture. Further evaluations confirmed OEIS, single umbilical artery, and sacrococcygeal spinal defect in Twin B. Background

The condition known as the OEIS complex, which includes omphalocele, exstrophy, imperforate anus, and spinal defects, is a rare birth defect within the exstrophy-epispadias complex [1, 2]. It was first documented by Littre in 1709 [3, 4]. The prevalence of this complex is estimated to be around 1 in 200,000 to 400,000 pregnancies [1, 5–7], with a range of 1 in 82,000 to 1 in 200,000 live births [2]. The development of the OEIS complex in the first trimester of pregnancy is believed to involve a sequence of events, including the absence of cloacal septation, failure of cloacal membrane breakdown, and abnormalities in the lumbosacral somite [8]. The exact cause of the OEIS complex is still unknown, but maternal influenza infection during the first trimester, monozygotic twins, IVF pregnancy, and being female have been identified as potential risk factors [2, 6, 8–11]. Female monozygotic twins have even been associated with a risk of recurrence in subsequent pregnancies [2]. According to a study, the presence of a single umbilical artery is associated with 30%−50% of cases of OEIS syndrome, though it is not established as a direct causal factor [12].

Prenatal features of OEIS complex are characterized by a constellation of ultrasonic findings, including: (1) large midline infraumbilical anterior abdominal wall defect with viscus bulging; (2) absent or non-visualized urinary bladder; (3) lumbosacral spinal anomalies (e.g., meningomyelocele, sacrococcygeal dysplasia); (4) ambiguous or absent external genitalia; and (5) occasional single umbilical artery (SUA) [13–15]. Serial ultrasound monitoring may also detect dynamic changes, such as enlargement and subsequent reduction of abdominal cystic masses (suggestive of cloacal membrane rupture), which aids in differential diagnosis from gastroschisis or amniotic band sequence [16].

In this case, we observed a discordant case of OEIS complex in a monozygotic female twin pregnancy with a single artery in the umbilical cord. We performed selective feticide and conducted an extensive literature review in English and Chinese to explore the postnatal outcomes of the normal co-twin. The literature review was conducted using the following databases: PubMed, Web of Science, CNKI (China National Knowledge Infrastructure), and WanFang Data—covering both English and Chinese literature. The search timeframe was from January 1980 to December 2023 (to capture all relevant twin pregnancy cases of OEIS with long-term follow-up data).

Inclusion criteria: (1) Studies reporting OEIS complex in twin/multiple pregnancies; (2) Case reports, case series, or retrospective cohort studies with explicit prenatal/delivery/postnatal data; (3) Studies providing information on co-twin outcomes, prenatal features, or genetic findings; (4) Full-text articles available in English or Chinese.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Studies on OEIS in singleton pregnancies (without twin/multiple pregnancy context); (2) Abstract-only publications, conference proceedings, or reviews lacking original data; (3) Duplicate reports of the same case; (4) Studies with insufficient clinical details (e.g., no clear diagnosis of OEIS or co-twin outcome data).

After applying these criteria, 19 eligible studies (18 case reports, 1 case series) were included for qualitative analysis. The list of keywords included “omphalocele-exstrophy-imperforate anus-spinal defect syndrome”, “OEIS syndrome”, “OEIS complex”, “twin pregnancy”, and “multiple pregnancy”. Written informed consent was obtained from the couple before the procedure and manuscript publication. The treatment procedure followed ethical principles, all data were collected from chart reviews, and approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board in our hospital.

Case presentation

The patient in this case was a 33-year-old woman with a spontaneously conceived monochorionic monoamniotic twin pregnancy. The couple had no history of previous pregnancies (this was their first pregnancy), no reported smoking habits, alcohol consumption, or substance abuse. The patient denied taking any regular medications, over-the-counter drugs, or herbal supplements during the pregnancy. She had no personal or family history of hereditary diseases, congenital malformations, or OEIS syndrome. The patient's serology was negative for HIV, VDRL, HBsAg, and she did not have diabetes mellitus.

An ultrasonography performed at 12 + 4 weeks’ gestation demonstrated a monochorionic monoamniotic twin pregnancy and twin B with an abdominal cystic mass of 2.8 * 2.5 * 2.5 cm, which was protruding to the amnion cavity (Fig. 1).At 15 + 3 weeks’ gestation, ultrasound showed that the abdominal cystic mass in twin B enlarged as 5.8*3.1*5.4 cm. Additionally, there was an echoless area in the pelvis measuring 0.7*0.5*0.6 cm, single umbilical artery, abnormal physiological curvature of the sacrococcygeal segment of the spine, and the co-twin (twin A) showed no abnormalities (Fig. 2). The pregnant woman and her partner were consulted extensively by the multidisciplinary team and the amniocentesis was performed. The result of the chromosome high-throughput sequencing analysis revealed a normal karyotype, with no pathogenic or likely pathogenic copy number variations.

Throughout gestation, biometric parameters of both twins were appropriate for their gestational age. The abdominal cystic mass in Twin B decreased continuously on sequential ultrasound examinations. A dramatic shrinkage of the cyst was observed at 22 weeks (Fig. 3), accompanied by poor tension and irregular shape, highly suggestive of cloacal membrane rupture.

Twin B was complicated with single umbilical artery and sacrococcygeal spinal dysplasia (Fig.4). Subsequent assessments at 28 weeks demonstrated absent fetal urinary bladder, abnormal perineal soft tissue echoes, loss of typical genital and anorectal ultrasonic features, as well as malaligned sacrococcygeal vertebrae and invisible conus medullaris. The cyst remained small on the final ultrasound at 33 weeks.

Ultrasonography images of twin B with a spinal defect at 28 weeks

Also, at 23 + 6 weeks, fetal echocardiography revealed a small amount of tricuspid regurgitation in Twin B, with normal cardiac structure and function; Twin A’s echocardiogram was unremarkable. The couple was extensively consulted by the multidisciplinary team (obstetricians, neonatologists, pediatric surgeons, and geneticists) at multiple time points to discuss the potential risks (e.g., preterm labor, twin-twin transfusion syndrome, co-twin morbidity) and outcomes of the pregnancy. Both twins showed appropriate gestational growth throughout pregnancy. After 22 weeks, serial ultrasounds revealed gradual shrinkage of Twin B’s abdominal cyst, accompanied by progressive multiple structural anomalies including cloacal membrane rupture, single umbilical artery, spinal dysplasia, and abnormal perineal and anorectal morphology. The affected twin remained stable during the second and early third trimesters with no acute threats to the co-twin, so earlier selective feticide was deferred. This avoided early iatrogenic preterm delivery, which carries far higher risks of organ immaturity, severe respiratory distress, intracranial hemorrhage and neonatal mortality than late preterm birth. By 33 + 6 weeks, progressive malformations of Twin B created growing intrauterine risks for the healthy co-twin, precluding further expectant management. Delivery of both fetuses with perinatal palliative care for the anomalous twin was suboptimal. While 33 + 6 weeks is late preterm, it affords markedly better organ maturity and neonatal prognosis than earlier intervention. Additionally, postnatal palliative care would impose considerable long-term financial strain and persistent psychological stress, including parental anxiety and prolonged grief. Therefore, we and the couple decided to performed concurrent selective feticide and cesarean section at 33 + 6 weeks to eliminate intrauterine hazards, optimize the healthy twin’s prognosis, and reduce familial psychological and economic burdens.

Intrafetal laser therapy was performed under ultrasound guidance. The18-gauge needle (Hakko Co., Ltd., Japan) was introduced into the abnormal fetus. A 400-μm laser fiber was then passed through the needle and placed close to the pelvic vessels. Intrafetal laser therapy was performed using a NdYAG-laser source (Dornier MedTech, Wessling, Germany) in 5-s bursts at 10 W initial power, which was then doubled in steps to a maximum level of 35 W. Cesarean section was performed after selective feticide. The OEIS fetus was weighed 1769 g, with no external genitalia and anus, and presented with a bulging of 3 × 4 cm at the umbilical location. The parents refused to do the autopsy. The healthy female baby weighing 2020 g was delivered, with Apgar scores of 8 and 9 at the first and fifth minute, respectively. Images of the monochorionic-monoamniotic placenta and the OEIS fetus are shown in Fig. 5. The baby was followed up, and now at one year and 6 months old, is in good health.

Discussion and conclusions

The OEIS complex is a rare condition that affects 1 in every 200,000 to 400,000 pregnancies [1, 5–7, 17], with 10–30% of cases occurring in twin pregnancies [10]. Research suggests that during early development, the urinary, genital, and gastrointestinal tracts merge into a common chamber called the cloaca. Around the fourth week of development, a layer of tissue called the cloacal membrane forms between the endoderm and ectoderm. If this layer does not properly develop, it can lead to poor abdominal wall development and potential rupture [13], and the membrane typically ruptures around 8 weeks in typical cases [3]. Notably, our literature review of 20 twin pregnancy cases found only two reports of cloacal membrane rupture at 22–24 weeks [3, 18], whereas our case showed rupture at 20 weeks—an earlier timing that enriches the spectrum of prenatal OEIS manifestations.

The diagnosis of OEIS is still not universally standardized, but there are three main ultrasonic features associated with it: a large midline infraumbilical anterior abdominal wall defect, a large lumbosacral meningomyelocele, and a failure to visualize the urinary bladder [14]. Other criteria for prenatal diagnosis of OEIS include the failure to visualize the urinary bladder, a large midline infraumbilical anterior abdominal wall defect with or without lumbosacral myelomeningocele [13]. Herein, an abdominal wall defect with viscus bulging, closed spinal bifida, absent bladder, and ambiguous external genitalia on ultrasound images indicated a suspicion of OEIS complex [15]. It is important to differentiate it from gastroschisis, amniotic band sequence, and limb-body wall complex [16].

Ultrasound is the primary modality for prenatal detection of OEIS complex, with a variable detection rate of 40%–70% [14, 19]. Early detection (first trimester) is rare but possible with identification of abdominal cystic masses or increased nuchal translucency [5]. The detection rate improves significantly in the second trimester (18–24 weeks) with the maturation of characteristic findings (e.g., abdominal wall defect, absent bladder, spinal anomalies). Serial ultrasound is critical for confirming the diagnosis, as dynamic changes (e.g., cloacal membrane rupture leading to cyst size reduction) may not be evident on a single scan.

Prenatal ultrasonic differentiation of OEIS complex remains challenging due to its variable phenotypic presentation and overlapping imaging features with other fetal pelvic and abdominal wall malformations. The primary differential diagnoses include fetal megacystis, isolated cloacal malformation, amniotic band syndrome, and isolated sacrococcygeal spinal dysplasia. Differential diagnosis from mimickers such as gastroschisis and limb–body wall complex further relies on the comprehensive integration of multiple ultrasonic features and clinical context. Notably, specific prenatal ultrasound findings are strongly correlated with adverse postnatal outcomes. Large abdominal wall defects exceeding 5 cm, concurrent spinal dysraphism with neural tissue involvement, and persistent non‑visualization of the fetal bladder are well‑recognized high‑risk features associated with complex postnatal management and poor prognosis [13, 20, 21]. Nevertheless, current evidence is insufficient to validate these ultrasonic markers as definitive prognostic predictors, emphasizing the necessity of further large‑scale cohort studies to refine risk stratification for affected fetuses.

Up to now, genetic reports about OEIS were mostly based on sporadic cases. Chromosomal anomalies like 9q34.1-qter deletion [22], 3q12.2-3q13.2 deletion [23], trisomy 18, 1p36 deletion [10] and homeobox genes such as HLXB9 [24] have been shown in published papers. However, our reported case did not present with any chromosomal anomalies. Genetic associations with OEIS complex remain heterogeneous and mostly reported in sporadic cases. Documented chromosomal anomalies include 9q34.1-qter deletion, 3q12.2-3q13.2 deletion, 1p36 deletion, and trisomy 18 [10, 22, 23]. Among candidate genes, variants in homeobox gene HLXB9 have been implicated in a subset of cases [24]. However, in twin pregnancies with OEIS (including our case and reviewed twin cases), chromosomal analyses were normal, suggesting that non-chromosomal factors (e.g., early blastogenesis disturbances, environmental triggers) may play a more prominent role in twin-related OEIS [2, 25]. Routine prenatal genetic testing (e.g., chromosomal microarray, whole-exome sequencing) is recommended for fetuses suspected of OEIS, though negative results do not exclude the diagnosis.

However, it is important to note that the complex is usually discordant between the two fetuses [10, 25, 26]. Our case aligns with this finding, supporting the idea that OEIS may be a result of early disturbances in blastogenesis (2). Our case is also consistent with previous cases, as 10 of them were male (all monozygotic twins) and 10 were female (7 of them monozygotic twins). The lack of a significant differe(nce in sex distribution in our study could be attributed to its rarity and the fact that they were twin pregnancies.

In our study, we analyzed published papers on twin pregnancies. The results, shown in Table 1, indicate that the discordant co-twin has a higher chance of survival and only a few cases of survival were reported [8, 14, 16, 18, 19, 25, 27, 28, 33, 37]. It is interesting to note that Kapur et al. [37] reported a case of conjoined twin pregnancy where the co-twin not only survived but also had a successful postoperative outcome, requiring treatment only for mild talipes equinovarus. Another case reported by Ben-Neriah et al. [28] had dichorionic, diamniotic fetuses with a grossly normal co-twin. Meizner et al. Thanks to advancements in neonatal care and surgical techniques, the survival rates for cases of OEIS can reach up to 100% [20]. It is recommended to perform surgical management on newborns within 48–72 h after birth [13]. The treatment for OEIS newborns typically involves multiple staged reconstruction procedures, including colostomy, anoplasty, stoma closure, bladder closure, surgeries specific to the individual's sex and so on. This raises a number of clinical questions, such as gender assignment, timing of bladder closure, bladder function, and fertility [38]. It has been reported that delaying the first-stage exstrophy repair (2 days of life) to allow for physiologic optimization is safe, but it results in longer hospital stays and the use of parenteral nutrition [39]. Despite these advancements, the prognosis for OEIS remains poor.

Given the rarity of OEIS in twin pregnancies, standardized management guidelines are lacking. Based on our case experience and literature review, we propose the following clinical management considerations:

Prenatal monitoring schedule: For fetuses suspected of OEIS, serial ultrasound should be performed every 2–3 weeks from the second trimester onwards. This interval allows for timely detection of dynamic changes (e.g., cyst enlargement/rupture, progression of spinal defects) and monitoring of co-twin growth (to rule out intrauterine growth restriction or twin-twin transfusion syndrome in monochorionic twins). Doppler ultrasound of umbilical and middle cerebral arteries should be included in each follow-up to assess fetal well-being.

Indications for invasive prenatal testing: Invasive testing (amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling) is recommended for all fetuses with suspected OEIS, regardless of maternal age or first-trimester screening results. This is to rule out chromosomal anomalies (e.g., 1p36 deletion, trisomy 18) and copy number variations that may impact prognosis and management.

Multidisciplinary consultation timeline: Genetic consultation should be offered within 1–2 weeks of suspected OEIS diagnosis to counsel on etiology, recurrence risk, and testing options. Pediatric surgical consultation (prenatally, if available) or immediate postnatal consultation is essential to plan for staged reconstructive procedures. Obstetric consultation with a high-risk pregnancy specialist is critical to discuss delivery timing, mode (cesarean section is preferred for fetuses with large abdominal wall defects to avoid trauma), and the role of selective feticide in discordant twins.

Considerations for selective feticide: While our review of 21 cases(including our case)showed favorable co-twin outcomes, the small sample size limits definitive recommendations. Selective feticide may be considered in discordant monochorionic twin pregnancies after thorough multidisciplinary counseling, weighing the risks (e.g., preterm labor, co-twin morbidity) and benefits (e.g., reduced maternal physiological stress, optimized resource allocation for the healthy co-twin). This decision should be individualized based on gestational age, co-twin status, and parental preferences.

Future research should focus on large-scale multicenter studies to validate ultrasound markers of poor outcome, clarify genetic etiologies in twin-related OEIS, and establish evidence-based management guidelines for discordant twin pregnancies. Longitudinal follow-up of OEIS survivors and their co-twins is also needed to assess long-term developmental, urological, and gastrointestinal outcomes.

In summary, our study found that OEIS syndrome is more commonly observed in monozygotic twins. The distinction in occurrence of OEIS syndrome between males and females was not a significant focus in our research, likely due to the rarity of the syndrome and the fact that our study specifically examined twin pregnancies. Regarding the prognosis for the co-twin, once it was confirmed that there was a difference in development between the twins, the co-twin had a higher likelihood of survival with fewer malformations. Additionally, in cases of discordant twin pregnancies, selective feticide in discordant twin pregnancies should be considered as an individualized option following comprehensive multidisciplinary counseling.
