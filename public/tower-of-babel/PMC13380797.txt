From giant components to communities: community-level insights for prioritizing interventions within large clusters of HIV-1 transmission networks
Authors: Huanchang Yan, Hao Wu, Jiahang Wang, Shunming Li, Yefei Luo, Lingxuan Lai, Jingyang Hu, Zhenming Tian, Yuanli Rao, Jing Gu, Shixing Tang, Yuantao Hao, Zhigang Han, Yu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLarge clusters in HIV-1 molecular networks contain a substantial proportion of people living with HIV and dominate local epidemics; however, their large size and complex structure pose a challenge for effective public health interventions. We developed an analytical framework to partition the large cluster into small groups for precise intervention. In the HIV-1 CRF07_BC molecular transmission network in Guangzhou, China (2008-2020), a giant component (681 members) was partitioned into 34 communities with dense internal and sparse external links. All 378 inter-community links involved high-centrality members from Community 1 (P < 0.001) and phylogenetic analysis identified Community 1 as the most likely ancestral source of the giant component (marginal probability = 0.989). Exponential random graph models (ERGMs) revealed significant homophily effect among members with specific characteristics in the giant component and its large communities, highlighting potential outbreaks within specific subgroups. Partitioning giant components into communities may be a promising approach to help develop community-level interventions and could improve the effectiveness of interventions targeted at large clusters.Keywords: HIV, Molecular surveillance, Molecular epidemiology, Molecular transmission network, Community detection, Exponential random graph model

Introduction

Molecular transmission networks of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) provide critical insights into HIV transmission dynamics and public health interventions (Schuster et al., 2025); however, not all clusters contribute equally to the epidemic (Novitsky et al., 2023). Large clusters, which comprise a substantial proportion of people with HIV (PWH), were frequently detected in networks derived from long-term molecular surveillance data (Alexiev et al., 2021; Cheng et al., 2022; Labarile et al., 2022; M. Liu, Wang, et al., 2023; Otani et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022). In network science, these large clusters correspond to giant components where any node can be reached from another via connected paths (Janson et al., 1993). Such structures dominate the HIV transmission networks and play a central role in sustaining local epidemics (Di Lauro et al., 2025; Minna et al., 2025; Patiño-Galindo et al., 2018; Zsichla et al., 2025). They are often associated with super-spreaders, recent infections, and highly transmissible viral strains (Brenner et al., 2021; Li et al., 2020; Novitsky et al., 2011, 2021), making them essential for understanding and controlling HIV transmission (Brenner et al., 2017).

However, the large size of giant components poses a challenge for public health interventions. Prioritizing these clusters has been shown to yield outcomes similar to targeting random clusters (Wertheim et al., 2018), resulting in poor cost-effectiveness similar to prioritizing the entire population (Chato et al., 2020). Since not all members within a giant component contribute equally to viral transmission, partitioning the component into smaller but more informative subgroups may inform precise interventions. Community detection methods, which identify subgroups with dense internal and sparse external links (Newman, 2004), offer a promising solution to this challenge (Clipman et al., 2022; M. Liu, Wang, et al., 2023). Furthermore, homophily - the tendency for individuals with similar characteristics to cluster - may shape the viral transmission within the communities (DEV, 2016; Solomon et al., 2019), providing important insights for outbreak detection among specific subgroups and designing community-level interventions (DeGruttola et al., 2023).

In China, CRF07_BC is one of the most prevalent HIV-1 circulating recombinant forms, accounting for approximately 40% of infections (X. Liu, Wang, et al., 2023), and tends to form large clusters among men who have sex with men (Minna et al., 2025; Pang et al., 2023). Guangzhou, one of the most populous megacities in South China, has been experiencing a CRF07_BC epidemic, with a growing giant component detected through long-term molecular HIV surveillance (Yan et al., 2024). This study aimed to detect communities and characterize intra-community transmissions in the CRF07_BC giant component in Guangzhou, offering a novel analytical framework to enhance precise and effective interventions targeting giant components.

Materials and methods

CRF07_BC sequences collection

HIV-1 pol gene sequences (HXB2: 2253-3821 nt, 1568bp) were collected from PWH newly diagnosed in Guangzhou, China, between 2008 and 2020. Sample and social-demographic information collection, gene amplification and sequencing, and genotyping were performed according to the previous pipeline (Yan et al., 2024). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of School of Public Health, Sun Yat-sen University (No. 2023073). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and data were anonymized for analysis. Only PWH with CRF07_BC infection were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

The sample flowchart of this study. PWH, people with HIV.

Molecular transmission network inference

The molecular network of CRF07_BC was constructed using HIV-TRACE (TRAnsmission Cluster Engine) (Kosakovsky Pond et al., 2018). Sequences were aligned by MAFFT v7 and pairwise genetic distances (GD) were calculated based on the Tamura-Nei 93 model (Katoh et al., 2002), while ambiguous bases in the sequence alignment were resolved. The optimal threshold of 0.008 substitutions/site was adopted from our previous study, which determined this threshold as the minimum value that maximized the number of detected clusters through a sensitivity analysis across thresholds ranging from 0.001 to 0.020 substitutions per site (step size: 0.001) (Yan et al., 2024). Each PWH was represented as a node, while each potential transmission between PWH was inferred when the pairwise GD was less than or equal to the threshold and represented as a link. Clusters were defined as components containing at least two genetically linked PWH. The network was visualized using Cytoscape 3.7.0 (Shannon et al., 2003). The largest cluster containing the most nodes was identified as the giant component in subsequent analysis. A permutation test on giant component size was conducted using 1000 random networks with the same node count and degree distribution as the observed network.

Ancestral reconstruction among communities

A maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed using IQ-TREE 1.6.12 (Nguyen et al., 2014). To reduce long-branch attraction, representative sequences for each community were generated using the CD-HIT (Cluster Database at High Identity with Tolerance) Suite with an identity cut-off of 0.99 (Li & Godzik, 2006). The optimal nucleotide substitution model (TVM + F + I + G4) was selected using ModelFinder based on the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) (Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017). Node support in the ML phylogeny was assessed using an ultrafast bootstrap with 1000 replicates and a Shimodaira-Hasegawa approximate likelihood-ratio test (SH-aLRT) with 1000 replicates (Minh et al., 2013).

To infer the ancestral community of the giant component, the state of each internal node was reconstructed by PastML (Phylogenetic Ancestral State Reconstruction with Maximum Likelihood) based on the marginal posterior probabilities approximation (MPPA) method and the F81 substitution model (Ishikawa et al., 2019). The phylogeny along with the inferred ancestral states, was visualized using ggtree R package (Yu et al., 2017). To assess the robustness, sensitivity analysis was conducted of ancestral reconstructions.

Due to the non-normal distribution of centrality metrics, we used Mann-Whitney U tests for all comparisons between bridging nodes and non-bridging nodes. To address the imbalance between groups, we conducted two additional analyses to enhance robustness: (1) 1000 iterations of 1:1 random matching between cross-nodes and non-cross nodes, and (2) bootstrap resampling with 2000 iterations to compute 95% confidence intervals for median differences.

Exponential random graph models

In a molecular transmission network, the primary unit of interest is a potential transmission link between two individuals, whose characteristics may influence the probability of transmission (Nelson et al., 2020). To assess factors associated with potential transmission links within communities, exponential random graph models (ERGMs) were employed to model both main effects and homophily effects of PWH characteristics on HIV transmission (Mattie et al., 2025). The binary outcome was defined as the presence (1) or absence (0) of a link between two members of the same community. ERGMs were applied to all communities with ≥20 members.

Results

The giant component in the CRF07_BC molecular network

Between 2008 and 2020, 8395 HIV-1 pol gene sequences were collected through molecular HIV surveillance in Guangzhou, among which 3337 were identified as CRF07_BC strains (Fig. 1). Using a pairwise GD threshold of 0.008 substitutions/site, 48.5% (1619/3337) of these CRF07_BC sequences formed 253 molecular clusters with 5308 potential transmission links (Fig. 2A). The median cluster size was 2.0 (interquartile range [IQR]: 2.0-4.0). The largest cluster (Cluster 1), comprising 681 nodes and 3376 links, was identified as the giant component due to its size, more than 20 times larger than the second-largest cluster (n = 32). The permutation test revealed that the observed size of giant component was significantly different from those expected by chances (P < 0.001), indicating that the component did not arise randomly.

Strong community structure in the giant component

Using the fast greedy algorithm, 34 communities were identified within the giant component, yielding a modularity of 0.620 (P < 0.001) and indicating a strong community structure (Fig. 2B). Community sizes ranged from 2 to 235 members (median = 7.0; IQR: 3.0-17.5), with 23.5% (8/34) consisting of only 2 members, 41.2% (14/34) comprising 3-9 members, 11.8% (4/34) having 10-19 members, and 23.5% (8/34) consisting of 20 or more members. Additionally, 33 PWH joined the giant component in 2020 but were unevenly distributed in communities. The 5 largest communities - Community 1 (n = 235), Community 3 (n = 71), Community 12 (n = 63), Community 2 (n = 58) and Community 5 (n = 33) - gained 13, 3, 0, 1 and 2 new members, respectively (Table S1).

In the giant component, 88.8% (2998) of the 3376 links detected were intra-community (Fig. 2B), while community density varied widely (Table S1). Community 30 had a density of 0.942, making it nearly fully connected, indicating fast viral transmission within the community. The largest community, Community 1, exhibited the lowest density (0.044). All communities showed higher density than the overall giant component (0.015), suggesting that PWH were closely connected within their respective communities.

Inter-community transmission from community 1 to the other communities

All 378 inter-community links exclusively connected Community 1 to the other 33 communities (Fig. 2B), among which the majority connected to Community 3 (88, 23.3%), Community 2 (57, 15.1%), Community 12 (50, 13.2%) and Community 5 (41, 10.8%) (Fig. 2C). 40 bridging members in Community 1 were involved in these inter-community transmission links, and each was linked to 1 (n = 23), 2-3 (n = 10), 4-6 (n = 4), or >10 communities (n = 3) (Fig. 3A).

Centrality analysis revealed that bridging members occupied central positions in the viral transmission network of Community 1 (Fig. 3B). Degree was significantly higher among bridging members than non-bridging members (median: 23.0 vs 3.0, P < 0.001), indicating that bridging members were much more connected within Community 1. ASPL was shorter for bridging members than for non-bridging ones (median: 2.1 vs 2.4, P < 0.001), suggesting that bridging members could facilitate faster viral transmission within the community. Additionally, stress centrality was remarkably higher among bridging members (median: 1650.0 vs 0.0, P < 0.001), underscoring the critical role of bridging members in maintaining transmission pathways in Community 1. Consistent results were observed across the matching and permutation tests (all P < 0.001).

Furthermore, phylogenetic analysis identified Community 1 as the most likely ancestral source of the giant component, with a marginal probability of 0.989 (sensitivity analysis: >0.999) (Fig. 3C; Fig. S1). As expected, members of the same community generally clustered together in the phylogenetic tree. This phylogenetic pattern provided independent support for the evolutionary relationships within communities, suggesting that the community partition based on network topology captured meaningful viral lineage structure. Inter-community transitions from Community 1 to the other large communities were observed, suggesting Community 1 was the hub in viral transmission within the giant component.

Diverse demographic and epidemiological profiles across communities

Of the 681 PWH in the giant component, the majority were male (671, 98.5%) and men who have sex with men (MSM, 544, 79.9%) (Table S1). Most of the members reported 1-4 close contacts (393, 59.5%), diagnosed at a young age (18-29 years: 313, 46.0%) and had a junior or senior high school education (356, 53.1%). Approximately one-third resided in the central area of Guangzhou (215, 32.5%) or outside Guangzhou (214, 32.4%). Most were diagnosed at the HIV infection stage (431, 68.5%) and between 2012 and 2016 (309, 45.4%), with the majority remaining under treatment (513, 88.9%).

Eight large communities showed distinct epidemiological characteristics (Table S1; Fig. S2). The infection route in these large communities was typically MSM (71.8%-92.1%). However, Community 3 (26.8%) and Community 13 (26.1%) had more than one-quarter of members infected through heterosexual transmission (HET). Notably, Community 13 was dominated by those diagnosed at the age of 40-59 (13, 56.5%) and the AIDS stage (13, 59.1%), while the others were dominated by young members (e.g., Community 30: 71.4% aged 18-29 years).

In terms of diagnosis year, approximately one-third of the members in Community 1 (69, 29.4%), Community 3 (23, 32.4%), Community 5 (10, 30.3%) and Community 13 (8, 34.8%) were diagnosed between 2017 and 2020. Community 12 (6, 9.5%) and Community 15 (4, 18.2%) had a low proportion of members diagnosed during this period, and no new members joined them in 2020, suggesting a stable structure. In contrast, Community 30 was predominantly composed of members diagnosed between 2017 and 2020 (15, 53.6%).

Determinants of intra-community transmission

Univariate ERGM analysis was performed to identify main effect and homophily effects of potential factors on HIV-1 transmission within the giant component and its large communities (Fig. S3, Table S2). In the giant component, significant positive homophily effects were observed among members aged 40-49 years (odds ratio [OR] = 1.552, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.284-1.876), those living in the same area of Guangzhou (central area: OR = 1.278, 95% CI: 1.147-1.424; suburban area: OR = 1.451, 95% CI: 1.283-1.641; outer suburban area: OR = 1.959, 95% CI: 1.443-2.659), MSM (OR = 1.245, 95% CI: 1.155-1.343), those reporting 5∼9 closed contacts (OR = 1.762, 95% CI: 1.544-2.011), those under treatment (OR = 1.262, 95% CI: 1.175-1.355) or lost to follow-up (OR = 1.994, 95% CI: 1.190-3.339), and those diagnosed before 2017 (2008-2011: OR = 1.304, 95% CI: 1.126-1.510; 2012-2016: OR = 1.652, 95% CI: 1.531-1.783). Homophily effects also varied across the communities. For instance, PWH aged 18-29 years in Community 2 (OR = 2.297, 95% CI: 1.622-3.251) and Community 12 (OR = 1.427, 95% CI: 1.083-1.880), as well as 50-59 years in Community 3 (OR = 10.567, 95% CI: 1.926-57.976) tended to link with members of the same age group, whereas no significant positive effect of age were observed in the other communities.

Terms with P ≤ 0.1 from the univariate models were subsequently entered into multivariate models (Fig. S4, Table S3). However, due to the inclusion of main effects and limited sample sizes, the homophily terms yielded non-finite estimates and were thereby removed from the final multivariate models. Nevertheless, different factors were found to be significantly associated with transmission links across communities, as detailed below.

In the giant component, PWH aged 30-49 years (30-39: OR = 1.093, 95% CI:1.032-1.157; 40-49: OR = 1.520, 95% CI: 1.419-1.627) were more likely to form transmission links than the youngest age group (18-29 years), while those aged 50-59 years were less likely (OR = 0.653, 95% CI: 0.565-0.755; Table S3). Compared to the youngest group, PWH in older group were less likely to form intra-community transmission links in Community 2 (30-39: OR = 0.673, 95% CI: 0.512-0.884; 50-59: OR = 0.317, 95% CI: 0.151-0.663; Fig. 4B), Community 12 (30-39: OR = 0.497, 95% CI: 0.387-0.638; 40-49: OR = 0.278, 95% CI: 0.178-0.435; 50-59: OR = 0.320, 95% CI: 0.138-0.738; Fig. 4E), Community 13 (30-39: OR = 0.236, 95% CI: 0.092-0.603; 40-49: OR = 0.082, 95% CI: 0.028-0.240; Fig. 4F) and Community 30 (30-39: OR = 0.055, 95% CI: 0.011-0.266; Fig. 4H). Conversely, the 40-49 age group tended to form links in Community 3 (OR = 1.743, 95% CI: 1.229-2.473). Additionally, a strong homophily effect was observed among the 50-59 age group in Community 3 (OR = 10.251, 95% CI: 1.284-81.822), indicating a preference for forming links within the same age group. Low education levels were positively associated with transmission in Community 1 (OR = 3.749, 95% CI: 3.120-4.503; Fig. 4A) and Community 5 (OR = 6.873, 95% CI: 1.978-23.884; Fig. 4D), but negatively associated in the giant component (OR = 0.604, 95% CI: 0.509-0.715), Community 3 (OR = 0.431, 95% CI: 0.272-0.684; Fig. 4C) and Community 12 (OR = 0.127, 95% CI: 0.029-0.563; Fig. 4E). No significant homophily effect was observed with respect to education level.

Furthermore, spatial patterns in viral transmission varied across the communities (Fig. 4; Table S3). The multivariate ERGM in the giant component revealed that PWH residing the suburban (OR = 1.083, 95% CI: 1.016-1.154) and outer suburban (OR = 1.614, 95% CI: 1.480-1.760) area were more likely to be involved in viral transmission than those in the central area while those outside Guangzhou (OR = 0.812, 95% CI: 0.759-0.869) were less likely. In Community 1, members residing in the suburban area were more likely to form transmission links than those living in the central area (OR = 1.345, 95% CI: 1.200-1.506; Fig. 4A). Members in the outer suburban area in Community 2 (OR = 0.201, 95% CI: 0.097-0.416; Fig. 4B), Community 3 (OR = 0.603, 95% CI: 0.439-0.827; Fig. 4C) and Community 13 (OR = 0.064, 95% CI: 0.015-0.279; Fig. 4F) were less likely to form links, whereas the opposite pattern was observed in Community 5 (OR = 5.549, 95% CI: 2.308-13.343; Fig. 4D). PWH residing outside of Guangzhou were significantly less likely to form links in 5 communities, including Community 1 (OR = 0.826, 95% CI: 0.736-0.927), Community 2 (OR = 0.493, 95% CI: 0.370-0.657), Community 3 (OR = 0.486, 95% CI: 0.379-0.624; Fig. 4C), Community 12 (OR = 0.427, 95% CI: 0.320-0.570; Fig. 4E), Community 13 (OR = 0.231, 95% CI: 0.113-0.471), Community 15 (OR = 0.336, 95% CI: 0.168-0.672) and Community 30 (OR = 0.166, 95% CI: 0.038-0.729).

In addition, HIV-1 infection routes were significant factors in some communities. Injecting drug users (IDU) were more likely to form links than those HET in Community 2 (OR = 10.165, 95% CI: 3.702-27.916; Fig. 4B; Table S3) and Community 3 (OR = 21.172, 95% CI: 9.621-46.591; Fig. 4C). In Community 5, members who did not disclose their contacts were more likely to form links (OR = 2.171, 95% CI: 1.222-3.857; Fig. 4D) while those disclosed 5∼9 (OR = 0.317, 95% CI: 0.146-0.687) or ≥10 (OR = 0.066, 95% CI: 0.016-0.264) closed contacts were less likely to be involved in viral transmission.

Regarding HIV status at first diagnosis, members diagnosed at the HIV infection stage were less likely to form intra-community links in the giant component (OR = 0.941, 95% CI: 0.889-0.995; Table S3) and Community 30 (OR = 0.267, 95% CI: 0.079-0.905; Fig. 4H). PWH lost to follow-up were more likely to be involved in transmission than those in treatment in the giant component (OR = 1.730, 95% CI: 1.395-2.146), but less likely in Community 12 (OR = 0.314, 95% CI: 0.126-0.781; Fig. 4E) and Community 30 (OR = 0.023, 95% CI: 0.004-0.140; Fig. 4H). Significant homophily effect was observed among PWH under treatment in the giant component (OR = 1.283, 95% CI: 1.021-1.613). Furthermore, members diagnosed between 2017 and 2020 were less likely to form intra-community links in the giant component (OR = 0.659, 95% CI: 0.595-0.730) and several communities, including Community 1 (OR = 0.561, 95% CI: 0.470-0.668; Fig. 4A), Community 3 (OR = 0.385, 95% CI: 0.266-0.558; Fig. 4C), Community 12 (OR = 0.261, 95% CI: 0.160-0.423), Community 13 (OR = 0.126, 95% CI: 0.031-0.517), and Community 15 (OR = 0.271, 95% CI: 0.120-0.613; Fig. 4G). Positive homophily effect was observed among PWH diagnosed between 2012 and 2016 (OR = 1.231, 95% CI: 1.053-1.438), indicating that these members drove the viral transmission in the giant component. Negative homophily effect was observed among members diagnosed between 2017 and 2020 in Community 1 (OR = 0.619, 95% CI: 0.391-0.979), suggesting that the epidemic after 2016 was likely sustained by secondary infections related with those infected before 2016, rather than ongoing transmission within the newly diagnosed population.

Discussion

This study proposes an analytic framework that integrates community detection and ERGMs into HIV molecular network toolkits, offering valuable insights for precise prevention targeting communities within large HIV molecular clusters. Applied to the CRF07_BC molecular network in Guangzhou from 2008 to 2020, this approach partitioned the giant component into 34 communities, identifying Community 1 as the hub. Furthermore, significant differences were observed between communities by demographic factors while homophily effects were detected among members, underscoring targeted prevention efforts aimed at specific risk groups within communities.

This study provided a novel extension of community detection for molecular network analysis, enabling the identification of more informative subsets within large clusters for public health interventions. Recently, Liu et al. discussed the potential of community detection methods in the context of HIV molecular networks, and highlighted the challenges associated with precise prevention targeting within giant components (M. Liu, Wang, et al., 2023). In our study, the modularity of 0.620 demonstrated strong community structure within the CRF07_BC giant component in Guangzhou, highlighting the feasibility of partitioning the giant component into smaller but more informative groups for precise intervention. Communities were defined by dense intra-community connections but sparse inter-community links (Newman, 2004). However, we found significant differences between communities and homophily among community members (Fig. 4). Furthermore, the uneven distribution of new members across communities (Fig. 2B) signalled their unequal contributions to transmission, underscoring that communities - rather than the entire molecular cluster - should be the primary unit for targeted interventions, such as enhanced case tracing and expanded testing and counselling services.

Community 1 served as the hub of the giant component. Although the proportion of inter-community links was relatively small, their distribution was particularly striking. All inter-community links involved a member in Community 1 (Fig. 2B; Fig. 2C), indicating its central position within the giant component. This stands in contrast to other HIV-related social networks, which often exhibit numerous connections between different communities (Billock et al., 2020; Clipman et al., 2022). The explanation may lie in the construction of molecular networks based on viral genetic similarity, which is a product of viral evolution (CDC, 2018). Furthermore, phylogenetic analysis revealed that Community 1 was the ancestral source of the viral strains (Fig. 3C; Fig. S1), indicating viral evolution as a plausible mechanism for the emergence of communities. In addition, bridging members occupied central positions and more than half of them were connected to more than one community by multiple links. This structural centrality is associated with their role in connecting different communities, while playing a critical role in intra-community transmission. Therefore, we recommend implementing enhanced surveillance of newly diagnosed PWH who had links to members in Community 1, particularly to those 17 bridging members with multiple inter-community links.

In infectious disease transmission networks, the key unit is the potential transmission link rather than the node (Nelson et al., 2020). Characterizing transmission patterns requires a shift in focus from nodes to links, as analyzing homophily within potential transmission links can provide early signals of outbreak risk (DeGruttola et al., 2023). While previous studies have primarily used clustering as the outcome variable in generalized linear regression models to investigate transmission drivers (Liu et al., 2024; Paraskevis et al., 2019; Ragonnet-Cronin et al., 2018, 2019; Yuan et al., 2022), we employed ERGMs to characterize the intra-community links and found significant differences between communities by demographic factors and homophily among community members. In Community 3, although nearly 40% of its members were aged 30-39, this group showed no high likelihood of forming potential transmission links compared to the youngest members in both the univariate and multivariate ERGMs. A particularly strong homophily effect with large confidence interval among those aged 50-59 years indicated a potential tendency for within-group transmission links, suggesting a potential outbreak within the group. In contrast, Community 13 had the lowest proportion of young PWH (17.4%) and consisted primarily of those aged 40-49 (43.5%). Here, a significant disassortative mixing pattern was observed in the 40–49 age group (OR = 0.473, 95% CI: 0.230-0.973) in the univariate ERGM, suggesting a higher probability of cross-age transmission. This pattern underscores that PWH aged 40-49 years serve as a key population for intergenerational transmission within this community. Thus, enhanced contact tracing efforts should prioritize eliciting intergenerational close contact history for this age group.

Several limitations are acknowledged in this study. First, the term “community” may be misleading in the context of molecular transmission networks, as it conventionally denotes vital sources of support, information, and advocacy for PWH. Although community detection methods were originally developed for social network analysis, links within the community reflect viral genetic similarity rather than social relationships. Additionally, modularity-based algorithms are subject to a resolution limit. Thus, we applied Infomap algorithm and found a finer community partition, which preserved the central community structure (data not shown). Second, links in molecular networks do not represent direct contact between PWH; instead, they indicate the speed of viral spread within the population (Ragonnet-Cronin et al., 2018). Consequently, modelling the probability of forming links can still offer valuable insights for guiding targeted prevention strategies. Third, our ERGM analysis focused on nodal attribute effects to identify factors associated with linkage formation, rather than to fully capture higher-order network structures (Nelson et al., 2020). Thus, some discrepancies in structural goodness-of-fit and large confidence intervals due to limited sample size were expected but did not affect our inferences regarding nodal attribute effects, which remain the primary focus. Fourth, the pairwise GD threshold is a critical parameter in network reconstruction. However, sensitivity analysis using 0.005 and 0.015 substitutions/site yielded results comparable to our primary findings (data not shown), supporting the robustness of our conclusions based on the threshold of 0.008 substitutions/site. Lastly, since our study proposed a novel strategy to facilitate public health interventions targeted at the large clusters, the effectiveness of this approach in improving intervention outcomes requires future simulation studies and comparative analyses.

Conclusion

By integrating community detection and ERGM methods, our study provides a refined framework for partitioning large HIV molecular clusters into communities and characterizing intra-community transmission patterns. The findings from the largest CRF07_BC cluster in Guangzhou highlight shifting intervention focus to the community level may be a promising approach to guide precise prevention strategies.
