In-Context Learning for Data-Driven Censored Inventory Control
Sohom Mukherjee Julius-Maximilians-Universität Würzburg sohom.mukherjee@uni-wuerzburg.de Anh-Duy Pham Julius-Maximilians-Universität Würzburg anh-duy.pham@uni-wuerzburg.de Richard Pibernik Julius-Maximilians-Universität Würzburg Zaragoza Logistics Center richard.pibernik@uni-wuerzburg.de Yunbei Xu National University of Singapore yunbei@nus.edu.sg

Abstract




Abstract

We study inventory control with decision-dependent censoring. While this class encompasses a wide range of operational decision problems with continuous action spaces, we focus on the canonical example of the censored or repeated newsvendor (R-NV). Each period, the decision-maker chooses an order quantity and observes the sales, so the demand is only partially observed. Two complementary schools of work have been popular in this domain, but suffer from their own limitations: parametric Thompson sampling (might fail under prior mismatch), and offline imputation (might fail to transfer to online environments). Based on the recent line of work on the predictive view of decision making, we take a fresh look at combining the two approaches above, by taking oracle actions on learned completions of the latent demand. We propose in-context generative posterior sampling (ICGPS) that makes this possible via modern generative models capable of offline meta-training and online in-context autoregressive generation. Theoretically, we show that the Bayesian regret of the deployed ICGPS policy using the learned completion kernel is bounded by the Bayesian regret of the TS benchmark with an ideal completion kernel plus a deployment penalty that scales as
\sqrt{T}
times the square root of the completion mismatch. This provides a template where one can plug in the Bayesian regret of TS for any known operational problem. In particular, for the R-NV problem, we derive a sublinear Bayesian regret by showing that censored feedback can be reduced to a bandit convex optimization feedback. Moreover, the completion mismatch is controlled by the offline predictive mismatch, i.e., offline quality translates to online performance, under reasonable assumptions. We instantiate ICGPS in practice by proposing a novel ChronosFlow architecture that combines a frozen time-series transformer backbone with a trainable conditional normalizing-flow head for fast conditional sampling. ChronosFlow-ICGPS performs at par with TS and outperforms Myopic and UCB-style baselines for benchmark experiments, and shows robustness against prior mismatch and distribution shift. ChronosFlow-ICGPS also performs well for the real-world SuperStore dataset, especially under heavy censoring.

Keywords:
censored newsvendor; generative posterior sampling; in-context learning; offline-to-online learning

Introduction

Many operational decision problems involve decision-dependent uncertainty: the decisions we take determine what we get to observe. A canonical example is retail inventory under stockouts, where we observe sales but not demand whenever inventory is insufficient. This
right-censoring
creates an exploration–exploitation tension: ordering conservatively reduces immediate holding costs but also increases censoring, making future learning harder. The aforementioned problem, usually referred to as the repeated or censored newsvendor, has therefore become a central testbed for understanding learning under censored feedback.
While Bayesian methods, dating back to the popular posterior sampling approach introduced by Thompson
38
, have proven to be powerful in solving this problem
44
, the bottleneck of using such methods in modern practice remains the choice of the prior distribution
42
. On the other hand, imputation methods that perform some form of demand de-censoring are confined mainly to offline settings and fail to provide online guarantees
11
.

To overcome this challenge, we adopt a missing-data view of uncertainty in sequential decision-making.
Rather than viewing learning as estimating parameters, we treat the unobserved components of the trajectory as
missing outcomes
that can be
completed
in a way that is consistent with the feedback mechanism.
If one could sample a completion from the true posterior, then posterior sampling would act by drawing a plausible complete
trajectory and choosing the corresponding oracle action. Recent work has shown that this perspective can be made algorithmic by replacing explicit posteriors with modern generative models that can sample missing data
in-context
, i.e., only by conditioning on examples provided in the context, and no re-training
(
10
;
43
)
. However, such work has been confined to finite discrete action spaces and uncensored feedback, limiting their application to operational problems with decision-dependent uncertainty.

In this paper, we generalize this work to solve a broad class of operational sequential decision problems where feedback is decision-dependent (censoring) and the decision (action) space is continuous and scalar.
Our goal is online learning with performance guarantees in continuous action spaces: we seek sublinear regret against the
Bayes-optimal benchmark induced by the data-generating model. We propose in-context
Generative Posterior Sampling (GPS)
for the aforementioned class of operational problems. Offline, we learn a conditional generative model that can
complete
the latent trajectory given the observed
history (including censoring indicators) by autoregressive sampling.
Online, at each time, GPS draws a completion and takes the oracle decision. In particular, for the repeated newsvendor, the oracle is the critical-fractile map, and the completion model generates demand trajectories consistent with right-censored sales data.

To make in-context GPS (ICGPS) practical at scale, we instantiate the completion model using a modular architecture,
ChronosFlow
. ChronosFlow combines a pretrained probabilistic time-series backbone (Chronos-2)
(
2
)
, which is a successor of Chronos
(
3
)
, with a lightweight
conditional flow head that supports fast conditional sampling and exact enforcement of censoring constraints. Crucially, ChronosFlow is trained
offline
and then
frozen
at deployment: online learning is performed entirely through in-context conditioning on the growing history, avoiding expensive online gradient updates while still producing posterior-sampling-like exploration.

Contributions

Methodology.
Our first contribution is a methodological one whereby we extend the ICGPS framework proposed in
10
;
43
to continuous action spaces and censored feedback. Continuous actions are handled in theory by proving an equivalence between function and outcome posterior sampling, and in practice using a generative architecture with a conditional flow head for sampling continuous distributions. Decision-dependent censoring makes the generative step nontrivial, since the completions are not arbitrary and must satisfy hard
consistency constraints
induced by the censoring map. This necessitates using a censored negative log likelihood loss in the offline training, and constraint-aware sampling in the online phase. In particular, we derive the censoring-consistent autoregressive factorization and a tail-conditioning procedure that guarantees generated completions satisfy the censoring constraints, leading to the online Algorithm

Related Work

Posterior sampling and Bayesian regret.

Thompson sampling (TS), the most common form of posterior sampling, dates back to
38
and has become a central algorithmic paradigm for exploration in Bayesian bandits.
A large body of theoretical literature establishes sublinear regret guarantees in increasingly rich stochastic models, beginning with
logarithmic regret for classical multi-armed bandits
(
1
)
, and extending to general Bayesian regret
bounds via information-theoretic and posterior-variance arguments
(
31
;
30
)
.
Our work shares the same Bayesian regret objective, but the uncertainty we address is induced by
censored feedback
. We implement posterior sampling by
generating missing outcomes
consistent with the censoring mechanism, and our regret bounds separate an ideal Thompson-sampling term from a learned-model
deployment penalty
.

Bandit convex optimization and continuous-action bandits.

Bandit convex optimization (BCO) studies online optimization of an unknown convex loss function using only bandit
evaluations.
Foundational work introduced gradient-estimation approaches for bandit convex optimization
(
13
)
,
and continuum-armed bandits more broadly
(
25
)
.
In one dimension, the minimax regret improves to
\widetilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{T})
under convexity and boundedness

(
9
)
, and recent work proves that Thompson sampling itself attains
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{T})

Bayesian regret for BCO with scalar actions
(
4
)
. We leverage this result to obtain the Bayesian regret bound for TS on the R-NV problem, via a reduction of the censored feedback to the BCO feedback.

Posterior sampling via generation.

A recent line of work reinterprets posterior sampling as
sampling missing data
and then optimizing an oracle
decision rule on the completed dataset, enabling posterior sampling to be implemented by a generative model rather than an explicit posterior
(
10
;
43
)
.
This aligns with the broader
prediction-centric
view of uncertainty quantification
(
14
;
33
;
34
)
and decision-making
(
41
)
.
Our framework builds directly on the missing-data perspective, but extends it in two directions needed for operational
decision-making: (i) we connect completion sampling to continuous-action convex regret guarantees via BCO, and (ii) we
develop censoring-consistent sampling online, and censored-likelihood training objectives offline, which are tailored to the R-NV problem.

Learning the newsvendor under censored demand.

Learning under stockouts has a long history in operations and revenue management.
Early distribution-free approaches for censored newsvendor-type problems include
16
,
while
19
provides a nonparametric asymptotic analysis under censored demand.
A prominent estimator-based approach uses Kaplan–Meier methods to correct censoring and yields adaptive inventory control
policies with performance guarantees
(
21
;
18
)
.
Recent work sharpens the exploration–exploitation perspective and quantifies when active exploration is essential

(
7
)
, while data-driven formulations highlight identifiability barriers under severe censoring

(
17
)
.
In contrast, we treat the censored newsvendor as an instantiation of a general missing-data posterior-sampling framework:
our algorithm explores by sampling
feasible demand completions
.

Data-driven approaches for censored newsvendor.

A growing literature develops offline procedures to correct censoring and to improve order decisions in the R-NV using robust or data-driven approaches.
Examples include data-driven DRO corrections for censored demand
(
35
)
, iterative maximum-likelihood
procedures tailored to censored observations
(
11
)
, and target-oriented data-driven policies

(
40
)
.
These works focus primarily on
offline
decision quality and robustness, whereas our focus is
sequential

decision-making: we integrate censoring-consistent modeling and sampling into a posterior-sampling-style online policy and
provide regret guarantees that explicitly quantify the cost of using a learned completion model.

Meta-learning in bandits.

Many operational settings exhibit repeated learning across related tasks (e.g., products, regions, seasons), motivating
transfer and meta-learning approaches.
In revenue management,
6
studies meta dynamic pricing and shows how learning a shared prior
across experiments can reduce regret; more broadly, meta-learning with bandit feedback has been studied in adversarial and
stochastic formulations
(
26
;
22
)
.
Our empirical design similarly evaluates offline pretraining across heterogeneous demand distributions, but our algorithmic
mechanism is different: we transfer via an offline-trained
completion model
that is used online purely through
in-context conditioning.

Background

This section introduces the repeated newsvendor (R-NV) problem along with other problems belonging to the same class of decision-making problems. This is followed by a background on generative posterior sampling (GPS) and in-context GPS (ICGPS) via autoregressive completion. The section ends with a generic analysis of ICGPS for the R-NV problem.

2.1
Inventory control with censored demand

We begin by describing the problem setup for inventory control with right-censored demand (repeated newsvendor), which will serve as a running example of an operational sequential decision-making problem with continuous actions and decision-dependent uncertainty. A general template for the aforementioned class of operational decision problems, that can be solved using our proposed method, is provided in Appendix
A.1
. In particular, Table
A.1
lists additional instantiations of the same class, including booking-limit (capacity controls) in revenue management
37
;
27
, budget pacing under spend caps in digital advertising
5
;
12
, and posted-price mechanisms
24
;
28
.

Interaction and censoring.

Fix a horizon
T
, and let the
action
space be
\mathcal{X}:=[0,B]
and the
demand
space be
\mathcal{D}:=[0,B]
. At each period
t=1,\dots,T
, the decision-maker chooses an order quantity
X_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
. The environment then realizes a latent demand
D_{t}\in\mathcal{D}
, whose random trajectory is given by
D_{1:T}:=(D_{1},\dots,D_{T})
. Under lost sales, the decision-maker observes only the right-censored feedback, which is, in this case, the sales and a stockout indicator

S_{t}:=\min\{D_{t},X_{t}\},\qquad C_{t}:=\mathbf{1}\{D_{t}\leq X_{t}\},\qquad O_{t}:=(S_{t},C_{t})=:\psi(X_{t},D_{t}).

The history at time
t
is
H_{t}:=\bigl((X_{s},O_{s})\bigr)_{s=1}^{t}\in(X\times\mathcal{O})^{t},H_{0}:=\emptyset,

and a (possibly randomized) policy
\pi
maps
H_{t-1}
to a distribution over
X
, from which
X_{t}
is drawn. The per round loss is a known measurable function, given by the classical newsvendor cost
\ell(x,d)=h(x-d)_{+}+b(d-x)_{+}
, where
h>0
is the unit overage cost,
b>0
is the unit underage cost, and we denote
(a)_{+}:=\max\{a,0\}
.

Bayesian environment.

The unknown environment is captured by a distribution
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
over complete trajectories
D_{1:T}
.
All expectations below are with respect to
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
and the policy randomness, unless stated otherwise. Let
x^{\star}\in\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f^{\star}(x)
be an optimal action, and define the
Bayes risk function

f^{\star}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}}\bigl[\ell(x,D_{t})\bigr]
. Let us denote the per-round regret as
r_{t}:=f^{\star}(X_{t})-f^{\star}(x^{\star})
. The performance criterion is given by the
cumulative
Bayesian regret, which is measured as

\mathrm{BayesReg}_{T}(\pi;\mathbb{P}^{\star}):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi}}\Bigl[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(f^{\star}(X_{t})-f^{\star}(x^{\star})\bigr)\Bigr].

Oracle map

We denote the critical fractile by
\gamma:=\frac{b}{b+h}\in(0,1)
. Let
d_{1:T}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{T})\in[0,B]^{T}
be a completed demand trajectory. Define the empirical risk
\widehat{f}_{d_{1:T}}(x):=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(x,d_{t})
. We define the oracle action
\hat{x}(\cdot)
by empirical risk minimizer:
\hat{x}(d_{1:T})\in\arg\min_{x\in[0,B]}\ \widehat{f}_{d_{1:T}}(x)
. Define the empirical CDF
\widehat{F}_{T}(x):=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{d_{t}\leq x\}
, and its left limit
\widehat{F}_{T}(x^{-}):=\lim_{y\uparrow x}\widehat{F}_{T}(y)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{d_{t}<x\}
. In particular, for the repeated newsvendor problem, the oracle equals the
left empirical
\gamma
-quantile

\hat{x}(d_{1:T})=\inf\{x:\widehat{F}_{T}(x)\geq\gamma\}
.

2.2
Generative posterior sampling

Generative posterior sampling (GPS) is based on the missing data view of uncertainty put forward by the literature on predictive Bayes
(
33
;
34
;
10
;
43
)
. The key intuition is that
uncertainty arises because we have not yet observed a complete dataset of outcomes.
If we had the complete dataset, we could fit an oracle decision rule and act without uncertainty. In our setting, the
complete dataset
is the full trajectory
D_{1:T}
.

A natural oracle action given
D_{1:T}
, for the purpose of illustration, is the empirical risk minimizer
\hat{x}(D_{1:T})\in\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\;\hat{f}_{D_{1:T}}(x)
, where
\hat{f}_{D_{1:T}}(x):=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(x,D_{t})
. At decision time
t
, the learner has only a partial history
H_{t-1}
. Under the Bayesian model
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
, this induces a posterior distribution over the
complete
trajectory:

\mathbb{P}^{\star}(\,\cdot\mid H_{t-1}).

A generative posterior-sampling strategy (Algorithm
1
) draws a plausible completion

\widetilde{D}_{1:T}^{(t)}\sim\mathbb{P}^{\star}(\cdot\mid H_{t-1})
and acts as if it were true by selecting

X_{t}=\hat{x}(\widetilde{D}_{1:T}^{(t)}).

(1)

Lemma 1

(Probability matching)
.

Conditioned on
H_{t-1}
, the action
X_{t}
in (
1
) is distributed as
the posterior distribution of the oracle action
\hat{x}(D_{1:T})
, i.e., we have:

\Law(X_{t}\mid H_{t-1})=\Law\bigl(\hat{x}(D_{1:T})\mid H_{t-1}\bigr).

Proof.

Since
\widetilde{D}_{1:T}^{(t)}\mid H_{t-1}\sim\mathbb{P}^{\star}(\cdot\mid H_{t-1})
by construction, and

X_{t}
is a measurable function of
\widetilde{D}_{1:T}^{(t)}
, the claim follows by the
pushforward measure identity: for any measurable
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
,

\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in A\mid H_{t-1})=\mathbb{P}\bigl(\hat{x}(\widetilde{D}_{1:T}^{(t)})\in A\mid H_{t-1}\bigr)=\mathbb{P}\bigl(\hat{x}(D_{1:T})\in A\mid H_{t-1}\bigr).

Deployment penalty via completion kernel mismatch

In this Section, we quantify how much extra Bayesian regret is incurred when in-context GPS is deployed with a learned completion model instead of the ideal completion kernel. We denote by
H_{t-1}
the
history up to time
t-1
. For any policy
\pi
, let
\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi}
denote the induced law of the interaction history

H_{T}=((X_{t},O_{t}))_{t=1}^{T}
when
\pi
is run in the true environment
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
. We compare the
ideal
policy
\pi_{\star}:=\pi_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}}
, which uses the
true
completion kernel
\mathbb{P}^{\star}(\cdot\mid H_{t-1})
, and the
deployed
policy
\pi_{\theta}:=\pi_{p_{\theta}}
, which uses a learned kernel
p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid H_{t-1})
.
At time
t
, the
true
completion kernel is the conditional law

Q_{t}^{\star}(\,\cdot\,|h):=\Law_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}}\!\bigl(D_{1:T}\in\cdot\,\big|\,H_{t-1}=h\bigr),

and a learned completion model with parameter
\theta
induces a kernel on the same space given by
Q_{t,\theta}(\,\cdot\mid h):=\mathrm{Law}_{\theta}\bigl(\widetilde{D}_{1:T}^{(t)}\mid H_{t-1}=h\bigr),
.

Definition 1

(On-policy completion mismatch)
.

The cumulative
completion mismatch
of
Q_{t,\theta}
relative to
Q_{t}^{\star}
, measured along the
deployment trajectory of
\pi_{\theta}
, is given by the following expression

\Delta_{T}^{\mathrm{comp}}(\theta;\pi_{\theta})\ :=\ \sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{H_{t-1}\sim\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\theta}}}\Bigl[\KL\!\bigl(Q_{t}^{\star}(\cdot|H_{t-1})\,\|\,Q_{t,\theta}(\cdot|H_{t-1})\bigr)\Bigr].

Theorem 1

(Deployment penalty bound under
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
)
.

Let
\pi_{\star}=\pi_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}}
be the ideal GPS policy that uses the true completion kernel, and
let
\pi_{\theta}=\pi_{p_{\theta}}
be the deployed GPS policy using the learned kernel. Assume there exists
V_{\mathrm{comp}}\in[1,\infty)
such that for all
t\in[T]
and all histories
h\in\mathcal{H}_{t-1}
,

Q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)\ll Q^{\star}_{t}(\cdot\mid h)
and

\sup_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{U}}}\frac{Q_{t,\theta}(F\mid h)}{Q^{\star}_{t}(F\mid h)}\;\leq\;V_{\mathrm{comp}}
.
Moreover, assume that the per-round regret
r_{t}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}}[r_{t}\mid H_{t-1}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}}[r_{t}]

\mathbb{P}^{\star}
-a.s..
Then

\mathrm{BayesReg}_{T}(\pi_{\theta};\mathbb{P}^{\star})\leq\underbrace{\mathrm{BayesReg}_{T}(\pi_{\star};\mathbb{P}^{\star})}_{\text{Term I: TS Bayes Regret}}+\underbrace{B^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{T\left(2+\log V_{\mathrm{comp}}\right)}{2}\,\Delta^{\mathrm{comp}}_{T}(\theta;\pi_{\theta})}}_{\text{Term II: Deployment Penalty}}\,,

(2)

where
B^{\prime}
denotes the range length of the per round regret
r_{t}
, i.e, we have
\sup r_{t}-\inf r_{t}\leq B^{\prime}
a.s.

Proof Sketch.

We provide a proof sketch here, with the main technical components, and refer the reader to
Appendix
C.2
for the full proof. Let
P\coloneqq\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\theta}}
and
Q\coloneqq\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\star}}
. We denote the per-round regret as
r_{t}:=f^{\star}(X_{t})-\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f^{\star}(x)
, and note that
r_{t}
has range length
B^{\prime}
. The cumulative regret is denoted as
R_{T}:=\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}
, and the cumulative Bayesian regret for a policy
\pi
under environment
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
is denoted by
\mathrm{BayesReg}_{T}(\pi;\mathbb{P}^{\star}):=\mathbb{E}_{P_{\pi_{\star}}}\!\big[R_{T}\big]
. The proof proceeds by first deriving a bound on
\mathbb{E}_{P}[R_{T}]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[R_{T}].
First, we perform a change of measure by applying the Donsker–Varadhan variational formula (Lemma
5
) with
f=\lambda R_{T}
. Second, we apply a Hoeffding-type MGF bound for bounded adapted sums under
Q
(Lemma
12
), and third, optimize over
\lambda
to get
E_{P}[R_{T}]-E_{Q}[R_{T}]\leq B^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{T}{2}\,\KL(P\|Q)}
. Now, we note that Lemma
13
relates the trajectory divergence to the causal action mismatch as
\KL(\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\theta}}\|\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\star}})=\Delta^{\mathrm{act}}_{T}(\theta)
. Moreover, Lemmas
14
–
15
bound the causal action mismatch by the reverse
completion mismatch, and then relate the reverse completion mismatch to the forward completion mismatch:

\Delta^{\mathrm{act}}_{T}(\theta)\leq\Delta^{\mathrm{comp}}_{T,\mathrm{rev}}(\theta;\pi_{\theta})\leq\bigl(2+\log V_{\mathrm{comp}}\bigr)\,\Delta^{\mathrm{comp}}_{T}(\theta;\pi_{\theta})
. This is done via the symmetry of the squared Hellinger distance, under Assumption
3
. Combining this with the third step and using
E_{P}[R_{T}]=\mathrm{BayesReg}_{T}(\pi_{\theta};\mathbb{P}^{\star})
,

E_{Q}[R_{T}]=\mathrm{BayesReg}_{T}(\pi_{\star};\mathbb{P}^{\star})
yields the claimed result.

Remark 1

(Interpretation of Theorem
1
)
.

This result shows that the additional price of using a learned completion model instead of the ideal posterior-sampling benchmark
\pi_{\star}
, under the true environment, is a deployment penalty. This deployment penalty scales as
\sqrt{T}
times the square root of the on-policy completion mismatch. Importantly, note that in our theory,
both
the deployed and the benchmark regret are defined under the
same
true environment
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
. We depart from prior work
(
43
)
in this regard, whose benchmark is evaluated on the deployed history, i.e., counterfactual Bayesian regret. The rationale behind choosing this presentation is to have the benchmark regret equivalent to the TS on-policy regret. This provides a template to directly import any existing Bayesian regret results, thereby making our theory useful for application to a wide range of operational problems with known theoretical results. The overlap constant
V_{\mathrm{comp}}
makes explicit that severe support mismatch between
Q_{t,\theta}
and
Q_{t}^{\star}
can amplify this penalty.

to the particular case of the R-NV, by tailoring Algorithm
1
to the particular requirements posed by the R-NV problem. We begin by listing two problem-specific obstacles that our method and analysis aim to address:

Obstacle 1: The per-round loss is continuous and not observable.
The per-round R-NV loss is convex and continuous, but not observable, and in general also not bounded. While the latter issue can be overcome by assuming a known bound
B>0
such that
D_{t}\in[0,B]
almost surely, the observability issue needs more careful analysis. We show that the R-NV admits a reduction to the feedback structure of bandit convex optimization (BCO) problem, and provide Bayesian regret guarantees using results from the BCO literature
4
.

Obstacle 2: Right-censoring creates challenges for training and sampling
. One must note that in the R-NV problem, the missing part of the data is not just unobserved future outcomes, but also the latent censored demand. This impacts
both
the offline training objective and the online sampling routine:

•

Offline:
the appropriate likelihood is the likelihood of censored observations, not of raw demands.
This aligns with survival analysis: uncensored observations contribute a density term, whereas censored
observations contribute a survival term
(
21
)
.

•

Online:
autoregressive generation must respect the censoring constraints imposed by the observed
history; i.e., when
C_{t}=0
the completion must satisfy
\widetilde{D}_{t}>X_{t}
.

Recent work on the censored newsvendor
(
17
)
shows that the aforementioned issues are significant, and censoring can create fundamental information loss and identifiability barriers unless sufficient coverage (large enough historical
orders) is present. To fix notation for later subsections, we record the censored likelihood identity. Lemma
2
is the basic building block for the offline censoring-aware
negative log-likelihood used to train the completion model in Section
3.2
,
and for the constraint-aware sampling procedure used online in Section
3.3
.

Lemma 2

(One-step censored likelihood)
.

Fix a history
h
and action
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
Let
D
be a demand random variable with conditional distribution (given
H_{t-1}=h
) admitting CDF
F(\cdot\mid h)
.
Assume
F(\cdot\mid h)
is absolutely continuous with density
f(\cdot\mid h)
on
(0,B)
.
Define the right-censored observation

O=(S,C)=(\min\{D,x\},\mathbf{1}\{D\leq x\}).

Then the conditional density/mass of
O
given
(h,x)
is

p_{O}(s,c\mid h,x)=\begin{cases}f(s\mid h),&c=1,\;s<x,\\[2.0pt]
1-F(x\mid h),&c=0,\;s=x,\\[2.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

On the event
\{C=1\}
we have
D\leq x
and
S=D
, so for
s<x
,

\mathbb{P}(S\in ds,C=1\mid h,x)=\mathbb{P}(D\in ds\mid h)=f(s\mid h)\,ds.

On the event
\{C=0\}
we have
D>x
and
S=x
, so

\mathbb{P}(S=x,C=0\mid h,x)=\mathbb{P}(D>x\mid h)=1-F(x\mid h).

All other
(s,c)
pairs are impossible by definition of
(S,C)
.
∎

3.1
In-context learning for R-NV

In-context learning in modern generative models, including transformer-based architectures, involves two main steps: the first being offline meta-training, and the second being online in-context adaptation. Central to both these steps is the idea of tasks: episodes with their own demand law. The meta-training over tasks and in-context conditioning within a task is precisely the operational viewpoint in the missing-data Thompson sampling literature
(
10
;
43
)
.

Task.

Let us denote by
\mathbb{P}
the law of a latent task variable, with prior distribution
\xi
.
Conditional on
\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}^{\star}
, the demand sequence
(D_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
is generated from a (possibly unknown)
task-specific law
D_{1},\dots,D_{T}\ \overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathbb{P}^{\star}
, where
\mathbb{P}^{\star}\sim\xi
. The conditional i.i.d. assumption is not necessary and is used only to obtain explicit likelihood factorizations later. Similar to before, the latent outcome at time
t
is
D_{t}
, and the complete trajectory is
D_{1:T}
. The learner observes neither
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
nor
D_{t}
, but only
O_{t}=\psi(X_{t},D_{t})
.

Bayes risk within a task and Bayes regret.

For a realized task
\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}^{\star}
, define the (task-conditional) Bayes risk
f_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}}\!\left[\ell(x,D_{t})\right]
, where the RHS does not depend on
t
in the conditionally i.i.d. (or stationary) case.
Let
x_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}}^{\star}\in\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}}(x)
. Omitting the subscripts for brevity, the Bayesian regret is the expectation over the prior on
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
and all randomness of the interaction:

\mathrm{BayesReg}_{T}(\pi;\mathbb{P}^{\star}):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(f(X_{t})-f(x^{\star})\bigr)\right]
, similar to the definition in Section
2.1
.

Offline meta-training and Online in-context adaptation.

Offline, the learner is given a dataset consisting of
N
historical tasks (episodes)

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{off}}:=\bigl\{H^{(i)}_{T_{i}}\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},H^{(i)}_{T_{i}}=\bigl((X^{(i)}_{t},O^{(i)}_{t})\bigr)_{t=1}^{T_{i}},

where each episode is generated by first sampling
\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{i}\sim\xi
and then generating demands

D^{(i)}_{1:T_{i}}\sim\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{i}
and interacting under some (possibly unknown)
behavior policy

that selects
X^{(i)}_{t}
and reveals only
O^{(i)}_{t}=\psi(X^{(i)}_{t},D^{(i)}_{t})
.
We do
not
assume the behavior policy is optimal or even stationary; it is part of the offline data
collection mechanism. This offline dataset is used to
meta-learn
a completion model (Section
3.2
). Online, the learner is evaluated on a fresh task
\mathbb{P}^{\star}\sim\xi
and adapts by conditioning on the
within-task history
H_{t-1}
(no online weight updates). This is exactly the
in-context
usage
of the learned completion model described abstractly in Section
2.3
.

3.2
Offline meta-learning: training completion model under censored demand

This subsection specifies the
offline
learning problem used to fit the completion model

p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h)
required by generative posterior sampling. The key issue is that the offline data are
right-censored
:
we observe
(X_{t},O_{t})
with
O_{t}=(S_{t},C_{t})
but generally do not observe the latent demand
D_{t}

on stockout rounds (refer Obstacle 2 above). The goal of this section is to provide a
trainable
conditional model of demand given a within-task history, which can then be used online to sample completions.

In-context training via random prefixes.

Recall the task variable
\mathbb{P}^{\star}\sim\xi
. Offline we observe
N
independent
episodes
(tasks)
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{off}}:=\bigl\{(H^{(i)}_{T_{i}})\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N}
, as defined before. To reflect how the completion model will be used online (conditioning on a within-task prefix
H_{t-1}
),
we view each episode as supplying many training pairs

\bigl(\text{prompt }H^{(i)}_{t-1},\ \text{next observation }O^{(i)}_{t}\bigr),\qquad t=1,\dots,T_{i}.

Model class: conditional law of demand

We posit a parametric family
\{p_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
of conditional laws for
D_{t}
given history.
Formally, for each
\theta\in\Theta
and each history value
h_{t-1}
, let
p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h_{t-1})
be a probability measure on
[0,B]
, with associated conditional CDF
F_{\theta}(x\mid h_{t-1}):=p_{\theta}(D_{t}\leq x\mid h_{t-1})
and (when it exists) density
f_{\theta}(x\mid h_{t-1}):=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}F_{\theta}(x\mid h_{t-1})
.
We allow
p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h)
to depend on the full observed history
h
(including past actions),
which is natural for sequence models; this dependence is crucial for in-context adaptation.

Remark 2

(Autoregressive sequence modeling viewpoint)
.

One can implement
p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h_{t-1})
by an autoregressive sequence model trained by next-token
prediction (e.g., a Transformer), where the input tokens encode the prompt
h_{t-1}
and the output head
parameterizes a distribution over
D_{t}
.
For the theory below, we only need that the model outputs a valid CDF
F_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h)
(and possibly a
density
f_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h)
).

3.2.1
Censoring-aware observed-data likelihood

The offline dataset does not reveal
D_{t}
when
C_{t}=0
(stockout). Thus, we cannot train
p_{\theta}

by direct demand log-likelihood. Instead, we maximize the likelihood of the
censored observation

O_{t}=(S_{t},C_{t})
induced by
p_{\theta}
. Fix a history
h_{t-1}
and action
x\in[0,B]
.
Under a conditional law
p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h_{t-1})
for
D_{t}
, define the induced conditional law for

O_{t}=(S_{t},C_{t})
by the pushforward through the censoring map

\psi(x,D)=(\min\{D,x\},\mathbf{1}\{D\leq x\}).

When
p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h_{t-1})
has density
f_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h_{t-1})
, Lemma
2

specializes to:

p_{\theta,\mathrm{obs}}(s,c\mid h_{t-1},x)=\begin{cases}f_{\theta}(s\mid h_{t-1}),&c=1,\;s<x,\\[2.0pt]
1-F_{\theta}(x\mid h_{t-1}),&c=0,\;s=x,\\[2.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(3)

This is the standard right-censoring likelihood: uncensored observations contribute a density term and
censored observations contribute a survival term
(
21
;
20
;
23
)
. The oracle requires completed demands, but censored observations only reveal whether
D_{t}
lies above or below
X_{t}
and, if below, its exact value. Training on the induced observation likelihood (
3
) is therefore the

maximum-likelihood
way to learn the conditional demand law from censored data. Define the one-step predictive log-loss at time
t
as

\ell^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\theta}\bigl(O_{t}\mid H_{t-1},X_{t}\bigr):=-\log p_{\theta,\mathrm{obs}}\bigl(O_{t}\mid H_{t-1},X_{t}\bigr),

(4)

with
p_{\theta,\mathrm{obs}}
given by (
3
). Writing
O_{t}=(S_{t},C_{t})
, we obtain the censoring-aware negative log-likelihood

\ell^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\theta}(O_{t}\mid H_{t-1},X_{t})=\begin{cases}-\log f_{\theta}(S_{t}\mid H_{t-1}),&C_{t}=1\ \ (\text{uncensored}),\\[2.0pt]
-\log\bigl(1-F_{\theta}(X_{t}\mid H_{t-1})\bigr),&C_{t}=0\ \ (\text{censored}).\end{cases}

Population and empirical objective

We now formalize what the offline objective estimates at the population level.
Fix any (possibly stochastic, history-dependent) behavior policy used to collect offline data.
Let
\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi}
denote the induced law of offline episodes under
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
and that behavior policy.
Define the population censored predictive risk

\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\theta}\bigl(O_{t}\mid H_{t-1},X_{t}\bigr)\right],

(5)

(where
T
is the episode length; for variable-length episodes use
\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}
and average over
i
).
Let
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\star)
denote the same quantity evaluated at the true induced observation kernel

\mathbb{P}^{\star}(O_{t}\in\cdot\mid H_{t-1},X_{t})
.
Given offline episodes
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{off}}=\{H^{(i)}_{T_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, we minimize the empirical version of
(
5
):

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{\mathrm{obs}}_{N}(\theta):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}\ell^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\theta}\!\Bigl(O^{(i)}_{t}\ \big|\ H^{(i)}_{t-1},X^{(i)}_{t}\Bigr).

(6)

A minimizer
\hat{\theta}\in\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{\mathrm{obs}}_{N}(\theta)
defines a learned
completion model
p_{\hat{\theta}}(\cdot\mid h)
.
A practical implementation samples random prefixes (prompts)
H^{(i)}_{t-1}
from offline episodes and trains the
model to predict the next censored observation
O^{(i)}_{t}
. The objective is exactly the stochastic optimization
of (
6
). The model is trained to perform conditional prediction given a prompt,
and at deployment time it adapts by conditioning on the online prompt rather than by updating parameters.

3.3
Online algorithm: in-context GPS for R-NV via autoregressive sampling

This subsection specializes ICGPS (Algorithm
1
) to the repeated newsvendor with right-censored demand. The primary additional ingredient beyond Section
2
is that
the completion sampler must respect censoring constraints induced by the observed history. Throughout, we work with the bounded loss
\ell
on
\mathcal{X}=[0,B]
and the oracle map
\hat{x}(\cdot)
which is the empirical
\gamma
-quantile.

Completion variables and censoring constraints.

Recall that the latent outcome is the demand
D_{t}\in[0,B]
, and the observation is
O_{t}=(S_{t},C_{t})=\psi(X_{t},D_{t})=\bigl(\min\{D_{t},X_{t}\},\ \mathbf{1}\{D_{t}\leq X_{t}\}\bigr)
. We treat
D_{t}
as the completion variable. The history at time
t
is
H_{t}=\bigl((X_{s},O_{s})\bigr)_{s=1}^{t}
. Fix a realized history value
h_{t-1}=\bigl((x_{s},(s_{s},c_{s}))\bigr)_{s=1}^{t-1}
.
Any completed demand trajectory
D_{1:T}=(D_{1},\dots,D_{T})\in[0,B]^{T}
is

consistent
with
h_{t-1}
if for each
s<t
:

\begin{cases}D_{s}=s_{s},&\text{if }c_{s}=1\quad(\text{no stockout}),\\
D_{s}\in(x_{s},B],&\text{if }c_{s}=0\quad(\text{stockout}).\end{cases}

(7)

3.3.1
Learned completion kernel and constrained sampling

Section
3.2
defines a learned conditional model
p_{\theta}
for demand.
Online GPS requires sampling a
completion
from a conditional law over entire trajectories:

\widetilde{D}_{1:T}^{(t)}\sim p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid H_{t-1})
. In practice,
p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid H_{t-1})
is implemented by autoregressive generation conditioned on the prompt

H_{t-1}
(
10
;
43
), with additional conditioning that enforce the hard
constraints (
7
).

Implementation via autoregressive factorization.

We assume that, for each prompt
h_{t-1}
, the model admits an implementable factorization of the form

p_{\theta}(D_{1:T}\mid h_{t-1})=\prod_{s=1}^{T}p_{\theta}\!\left(D_{s}\,\middle|\,D_{1:s-1},\,h_{t-1}\right),

(8)

where each one-step conditional is samplable. This is exactly the interface provided by autoregressive sequence models: the prefix
D_{1:s-1}
and the prompt
h_{t-1}
are treated as “context tokens,” and the model outputs a distribution for the next value. When
s<t
and
c_{s}=1
, the constraint
D_{s}=s_{s}
is deterministic. When
s<t
and
c_{s}=0
with
x_{s}<B
, we
must sample
D_{s}
conditional on the event
D_{s}>x_{s}
. The next lemma gives the exact conditional law. Lemma
3
yields an exact,
constraint-aware
sampling routine: sample
D_{s}
from the one-step conditional (
8
) when unconstrained, and sample from its tail conditional law (
9
) when the history indicates a stockout.

Lemma 3

(Tail conditioning for demand)
.

Fix a prompt
h_{t-1}
and a prefix
D_{1:s-1}
.
Let
\Pi(\cdot)
denote the conditional law

\Pi(\cdot)=p_{\theta}(D_{s}\in\cdot\mid D_{1:s-1}=D_{1:s-1},\,h_{t-1})

on
[0,B]
, and write its CDF as
F(z)=\Pi([0,z])
.
Fix a threshold
x\in[0,B)
such that
\Pi(D_{s}>x)>0
.
Then the conditional law of
D_{s}
given
\{D_{s}>x\}
is supported on
(x,B]
and satisfies

\Pi(D_{s}\leq z\mid D_{s}>x)=\frac{F(z)-F(x)}{1-F(x)},\qquad z\in[x,B].

(9)

If
\Pi
admits a density
f
on
(0,B)
, then the conditional density on
(x,B)
is

f^{\mathrm{tail}}(z)=\frac{f(z)}{1-F(x)}\ \mathbf{1}\{z>x\}.

(10)

Proof.

For
z\in[x,B]
,

\Pi(D_{s}\leq z\mid D_{s}>x)=\frac{\Pi(x<D_{s}\leq z)}{\Pi(D_{s}>x)}=\frac{\Pi(D_{s}\leq z)-\Pi(D_{s}\leq x)}{1-\Pi(D_{s}\leq x)}=\frac{F(z)-F(x)}{1-F(x)}.

The density form follows by differentiating (
9
) on
(x,B)
.
∎

ICGPS Algorithm.

We can now state the online policy. At each round
t
, the algorithm: (i) samples a completion consistent with the observed censoring pattern, (ii) computes the oracle action (empirical
\gamma
-quantile) for that completion, and (iii) observes a new censored datum and appends it to the prompt. Algorithm
2
performs
no
online updates to
\theta
. All adaptation to the new (unknown) task is through conditioning on the prompt
H_{t-1}
(in-context adaptation). If the one-step conditional in (
8
) provides an invertible CDF
F_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\cdot)
, then tail sampling can be done by inverse transform: draw
U\sim\mathrm{Unif}(F_{\theta}(X_{s}\mid\cdot),1)
and set
\widetilde{D}_{s}^{(t)}=F_{\theta}^{-1}(U\mid\cdot)
. Otherwise, one may use rejection sampling (sample from
p_{\theta}
until
>X_{s}
) or any exact sampler for distributions. Our proposed architecture in the experimental section supports the former.

3.4
Theoretical analysis of ICGPS for repeated newsvendor

The Bayesian regret of the deployed ICGPS policy for the R-NV problem (Algorithm
2
) admits the decomposition into the two terms, put forward by Theorem
1
. In this section, we tailor this decomposition given by Equation (
2
) to the specific feedback structure of the R-NV. In §
3.4.1
, we upper bound the first term by analyzing the Bayesian regret of Thompson sampling for the R-NV problem. This involves reducing our censored feedback structure to the feedback structure of bandit convex optimization. In §
3.4.2
we upper bound the second term (completion mismatch) by the
observable
predictive objective used to train the completion model from censored data. This involves deriving assumptions on coverage and self-contraction (a form of harmonic decay) to account for the information loss due to censoring. In §
3.4.3
we combine the previous two bounds to obtain the final regret guarantee for ICGPS in the repeated newsvendor setting.

3.4.1
Term I: Bayesian regret of Thompson sampling for repeated newsvendor

We recall that
\xi
denotes the Bayesian prior over
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
, and that
\xi(\cdot\mid H_{t-1})
is the posterior given history. The oracle policy
\pi^{\star}=\pi_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}}
implements Thompson sampling by drawing a posterior sample
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{t}\sim\xi(\cdot\mid H_{t-1})
and then playing

X_{t}\in\arg\min_{x\in[0,B]}f_{\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{t}}(x)
. Equivalently (Appendix
A.2
), one may view TS as sampling a
random function

f^{(t)}\sim\Law(f^{\star}\mid H_{t-1})
, and playing its minimizer: this function-sampling perspective provides a natural interface for continuous-action problems, and allows us to borrow ideas from the literature on bandit convex optimization.

Theorem 2

(Bayesian regret of TS on R-NV)
.

Assume the demand is bounded:
D_{t}\in[0,B]
almost surely for all
t
. Let
\pi_{\star}=\pi_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}}
be the ideal GPS policy that uses the true completion kernel, i.e.,
\pi_{\star}
implements Thompson Sampling for the R-NV problem. Then

\mathrm{BayesReg}_{T}(\pi_{\star};\mathbb{P}^{\star})\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})\,,

(11)

where the notation
\widetilde{O}(\cdot)
is used to hide polylogarithmic factors (and constants depending on
B,h,b
).

Proof Sketch.

We provide a proof sketch here, and refer the reader to Appendix
C.3
for the full proof. The proof hinges on the fact that the R-NV problem admits a derived bandit feedback that is equivalent to the feedback in bandit convex optimization (BCO). We introduce a risk-equivalent convex objective
g_{\mathbb{P}}
(Lemma
16
) such that
f_{\mathbb{P}}(\cdot)
and
g_{\mathbb{P}}(\cdot)
yield identical regret, so it suffices to bound

\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{t\leq T}\big(g_{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t})-g_{\mathbb{P}}(x^{\star}_{\mathbb{P}})\big)\big]
. We construct an
unbiased
(Lemma
18
) and
observable
(Lemma
19
) feedback for
g_{\mathbb{P}}
given by
Y_{t}:=(h+b)X_{t}Z_{t}-bX_{t}
. This is done by augmenting the learner with auxiliary randomness
U_{t}\sim\mathrm{Unif}([0,X_{t}])
drawn independently each round, and defining the latent indicator
Z_{t}:=\mathbf{1}\{D_{t}\leq U_{t}\}
. Finally, showing the required regularity (convexity, Lipschitzness and boundedness) using Lemma
20
, and scaling gives a valid BCO instance with scalar actions, yielding the
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
regret bound from
(
4
, Theorem 4)
.

Remark 3

(Interpretation of Theorem
2
)
.

This theorem shows that the Thompson sampling algorithm for the R-NV problem is able to achieve sublinear Bayesian regret, despite censored feedback. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first proof applying the information ratio analysis
31
to obtain the Bayesian regret of TS for the R-NV problem. The key technical novelties include deriving the computable unbiased feedback structure and recognizing the equivalence of function and outcome sampling via Lemma
4
.

3.4.2
Term II: Linking deployment penalty to predictive objective

The second term in Theorem
1
is governed by the on-policy completion mismatch

\Delta^{\mathrm{comp}}_{T}(\theta;\pi_{\theta})
, which is not directly observable under right-censoring. In this subsection, we show that for R-NV, the completion mismatch is controlled by the censored predictive objective.
Let us denote the observed-data kernels, i.e., the conditional laws of
O_{t}
given
(H_{t-1},X_{t})=(h,x)
under the true model and under
\theta
, as
P^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,x}
and
Q^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,x}
, respectively. Alternatively, they can also be defined as pushforwards through
\psi
:

P^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,x}\;:=\;\bigl(q_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid h)\bigr)\circ\psi(x,\cdot)^{-1},Q^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,x}\;:=\;\bigl(q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)\bigr)\circ\psi(x,\cdot)^{-1}
. We measure
censoring-aware predictive mismatch
along a policy
\pi
by the KL mismatch between observed-data kernels as below

\Delta^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\theta;\pi)\;:=\;\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{(H_{t-1},X_{t})\sim\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi}}\!\left[\KL\!\Bigl(P^{\mathrm{obs}}_{H_{t-1},X_{t}}\,\Big\|\,Q^{\mathrm{obs}}_{H_{t-1},X_{t}}\Bigr)\right].

(12)

Equivalently, if
p_{\theta,\mathrm{obs}}(\cdot\mid h,x)
denotes the model likelihood of
O_{t}
induced by
q_{t,\theta}

and
\psi
, then
\Delta^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\theta;\pi)
coincides with the population excess censored log-loss, due to Lemma
21
(Appendix
C.4.2
). Therefore, defining
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\theta;\pi
and
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\star;\pi)
according to (
5
), we have

\Delta^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\theta;\pi)\;=\;\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\theta;\pi)-\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\star;\pi).

(13)

Because censoring discards information about the demand tail,
\Delta^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\theta;\pi)
does not, in general,
control
\Delta_{T}^{\mathrm{comp}}(\theta;\pi_{\theta})
without further assumptions. In the following, we provide a minimal
coverage
condition, in line with the identifiability barriers highlighted in the censored newsvendor literature
(
17
)
. Moreover, we also need an assumption on harmonic decay along the AR pseudo-generation. This is given by a relation between the one-step divergence
d(h)
and
s-
th conditional divergence
\delta_{s}(h)
, between completion kernels
Q_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid h)
and
Q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)
, respectively. The formal definitions of these two quantities, along with further details about implications of this assumption can be found in Appendix
C.4.1
.

Theorem 3

(Relation between deployment penalty and censoring-aware predictive mismatch)
.

Consider the deployed policy
\pi_{\theta}
in the true environment
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
.
Assume that, for
\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\theta}}
-a.e. history
h
and all
t
the following hold:

1.

Max-order coverage:
\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\theta}}(X_{t}=B\mid H_{t-1}=h)\geq\eta
, for some
\eta>0
; and

2.

Self-contraction along AR trajectory:
\delta_{s}(h)\leq(c_{\mathrm{sc}}/s)\,d(h)
, for all
s\geq 2
.

Then the on-policy completion mismatch is upper bounded by the observed censoring-aware predictive mismatch:

\Delta_{T}^{\mathrm{comp}}(\theta;\pi_{\theta})\;\leq\;\frac{1+c_{sc}\log T}{\eta}\,\Delta_{T}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\theta;\pi_{\theta}).

(14)

Proof Sketch.

We provide a proof sketch here, with the main technical components, and refer the reader to Appendix
C.4
for the full proof. Using a KL chain-rule decomposition for AR-completion kernels, we show that for each

\KL\!\Bigl(Q_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid h)\,\big\|\,Q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)\Bigr)\leq\Bigl(1+c_{\mathrm{sc}}\log T\Bigr)\,d(h),

which is summarized as Lemma

. When

, the censoring map is (a.s.) information-preserving under (i), so the latent divergence
d(h)

can be upper bounded by the observed-data KL averaged over the deployed action distribution.
The key inequality is Lemma

d(h)\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\,\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta,t}(\cdot\mid h)}\!\left[\KL\!\Bigl(P^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,X_{t}}\,\big\|\,Q^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,X_{t}}\Bigr)\right].

Take expectations over
H_{t-1}\sim\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\theta}}
, sum over
t\leq T
, and recognize the right-hand side
as
\Delta^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\theta;\pi_{\theta})
via (

).
Finally, we can use the excess-log-loss identity (

) (Lemma
21
) to connect

\Delta^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}
to the observable censored predictive objective.

Remark 4

(Interpretation of Theorem
3
)
.

This theorem operationalizes the deployment-penalty of Theorem
1
by relating it to the observed mismatch via two key assumptions.
The two assumptions have clear roles: the

factor is an identifiability price: without a persistent probability of the de-censoring action
X_{t}=B

the tail of the demand distribution is not statistically constrained by the data, and observed predictive fit need not imply latent fit. On the other hand, the logarithmic factor arises from controlling error propagation along the AR pseudo-generation through
self-contraction. We need the latter assumption, while
43
do not, since our theoretical statement with on-policy TS Bayesian regret in the first term requires us to compare two different trajectory laws under
\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\theta}}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\star}}
, respectively.

Experimental Evaluation

Our experimental evaluation addresses four empirical questions aligned to our methodological and theoretical contributions in Sections
2
–
3
: (Q1) As a first sanity check for our proposed ICGPS, we check whether it matches Thompson sampling with correctly specified prior under i.i.d. demand?
(Q2) Secondly, we investigate whether offline quality translates into online regret, i.e., does improving the completion model reduce regret in a fixed environment?
(Q3) Thirdly, does multi-task pretraining improve robustness and transfer learning capability under distribution shift and prior mismatch?
(Q4) Finally, for real-world data, does in-context GPS improve performance on non-i.i.d. demand streams with temporal structure? We begin this section by describing our experimental setup, followed by our proposed ChronosFlow architecture for implementing the ICGPS algorithm in practice. Finally, we present the experiments to answer the above questions, and our inferences.

4.2
Implementation of ICGPS for R-NV

We propose the ChronosFlow-ICGPS architecture (Figure
2
) to instantiate the learned completion kernel in Algorithm
2
. The goal is to produce
fast conditional samples of the latent demand process while enforcing censoring constraints induced by the
censoring map, via offline meta-training and online in-context inference. The architecture consists of three main components: (a) conditioning context vector layer, (b) conditional normalizing flow (CNF) head, and (c) ICGPS sampler. We explain the main highlights here, and defer some of the details for Appendix
D.1
.

•

(a) Conditioning context vector layer
. This layer is used to produce the context vector
\overline{H}_{t-1}=\omega(H_{t-1})
during both offline meta-training and online sampling. It consists of a Chronos backbone that produces an in-context quantile grid at levels
\{0.05,0.10,\ldots,0.95\}
, and a Kaplan–Meier (KM) module that maintains censoring-consistent quantiles on the same grid. The final context vector is obtained by concatenating the above Chronos quantiles and KM quantiles to a simple
summary statistic of the historical data.
During offline meta-training, the Chronos backbone can be either frozen (real-data experiments) or fine-tuned (i.i.d. experiments). During online sampling, this layer is always frozen.

•

(b) Conditional normalizing flow head.
The CNF head consists of a light hypernetwork that maps the above context vector to the parameters of a one-dimensional strictly monotone conditional normalizing flow, finally outputting the induced CDF
F_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\overline{H}_{t-1})
and inverse CDF
F^{-1}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\overline{H}_{t-1})
. It is optimized during offline training via the censoring-aware negative log-likelihood objective (
4
), and frozen during online sampling.

•

(c) ICGPS sampler.

Online sampling is performed according to the description in §
3.3
, and Algorithm
2
. In case of censoring, we sample from the conditional tail
p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\overline{h}_{s},\,D_{s}>\overline{H}_{s})
using exact inverse-CDF tail-conditioning
U\sim\mathrm{Unif}\!\big(F_{\theta}(\overline{H}_{s}\mid\overline{h}_{s}),1\big),\widetilde{D}_{s}:=Q_{\theta}(U\mid\overline{h}_{s}).
At each time
t
, we draw
M
independent completions

\{\widetilde{D}^{(m)}_{1:T}\}_{m=1}^{M}
from the constrained decoder, compute the corresponding newsvendor
oracle action
\widehat{x}(\widetilde{D}^{(m)}_{1:T})
, and play a robust
aggregate (median over
m
) as
X_{t}
. This aggregation stabilizes decisions against occasional
outlier completions. We use a short warm-up of
T_{0}=3
rounds (e.g.,
ordering
B
) before switching to the rollout-based policy.

5
Conclusion

This work studies sequential inventory control under
decision-dependent right censoring
in the repeated newsvendor (R-NV): the order quantity determines what is observed, and stockouts censor demand so the learner observes only sales. To overcome the limitations of existing approaches, including choice of priors and inability to transfer to online settings, we propose the in-context generative posterior sampling methodology, which consists of offline meta-training and online in-context sampling, both respecting the censored feedback structure.
On the theoretical front, our analysis provides a regret decomposition consisting of (i) the regret of an ideal TS benchmark that uses the true completion kernel, and (ii) a deployment penalty bounded by a completion-mismatch quantity. We show that in the R-NV setting, the TS benchmark admits sublinear Bayesian regret via a reduction to a derived bandit convex optimization (BCO) feedback, while the completion-mismatch term links online performance to offline predictive fit of the completion model. Decision-dependent censoring can limit information about tail demand and create identifiability challenges in the offline-to-online transfer, without additional structural assumptions, which we derive precisely for ICGPS applied to R-NV problem. Empirically, the ChronosFlow-ICGPS architecture is robust to model and prior misspecification under censoring. On the SuperStore real-world data benchmark under heavy censoring, ChronosFlow-ICGPS achieves strong performance, especially for the
Meta
algorithm variant. A natural extension is a
contextual
version of R-NV, to include features like weather, price, text, etc., along the lines of
17
;
43
. A second direction is tackling
non-stationarity
in demands
8
using recent advances in non-stationary BCO
45
.

General template for operational problems with decision-dependent uncertainty

This appendix presents a generic template for sequential decision problems with scalar actions used implicitly throughout the main text, and provides a mapping for several operational examples.
Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}
be a compact interval (the action space).
A policy
\pi
sequentially chooses actions
X_{t}\in X
for
t=1,\dots,T
based on past observations.
We assume there exists an underlying outcome space
\mathcal{U}
and a random trajectory

U_{1:T}:=(U_{1},\dots,U_{T})\in\mathcal{U}^{T}
such that the per-round loss is a known measurable function

\ell:X\times\mathcal{U}\to[0,1]
, and the learner observes a (possibly censored/coarsened) feedback signal

O_{t}=\psi(X_{t},U_{t})

for a known measurable map
\psi:X\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{O}
.
The history at time
t
is

H_{t}:=\bigl((X_{s},O_{s})\bigr)_{s=1}^{t}\in(X\times\mathcal{O})^{t},H_{0}:=\emptyset,

and a (possibly randomized) policy maps
H_{t-1}
to a distribution over
X
.
Define the Bayes risk

f^{\star}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{P^{\star}}\!\left[\ell(x,U_{t})\right],

where in the stationary case the RHS does not depend on
t
.
The unknown environment is captured by a distribution
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
over complete trajectories
U_{1:T}
. Table
A.1
provides additional examples of operational problems that admit decision-dependent uncertainty due to censored feedback, and can therefore possibly inherit, with minor modifications, the method and theory presented in this work for the repeated newsvendor example.

A.2
Function-valued uncertainty in continuous action problems

For problems with finite action sets, posterior sampling is typically described as sampling a latent parameter or a finite vector of missing potential outcomes, and then choosing the best action under that sampled instance. When
\mathcal{X}
is a continuum, it is typically useful to view the unknown object as an

entire loss (or risk) function
over
\mathcal{X}
and to sample this function from its posterior.

Function-valued latent object.

Let us assume that the Bayes risk
f^{\star}:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
is convex and
L
-Lipschitz on the
compact interval
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}
, hence continuous. It is therefore natural to regard
f^{\star}
as a random
element of the Polish space
(C(\mathcal{X}),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra.
In a Bayesian model, randomness in
f^{\star}
arises from randomness in the underlying environment
(e.g., an unknown demand model), and conditioning on the observed history
H_{t-1}
induces a posterior
distribution
\Law(f^{\star}\mid H_{t-1})
over functions.

Posterior sampling in function space.

Fix any measurable tie-breaking rule that selects a minimizer from a nonempty set
(e.g., the smallest minimizer).
2
2

2

Measurable selection issues can be bypassed by a fixed
tie-breaking rule, or by working with a vanishing strongly convex regularizer so that the minimizer is unique;
both options are standard in continuous-action analyses.

Then posterior sampling can be written as

\widetilde{f}^{(t)}\sim\Law(f^{\star}\mid H_{t-1}),\qquad X_{t}\in\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\widetilde{f}^{(t)}(x).

(15)

This is essentially Thompson sampling in
bandit convex optimization (BCO)
: the learner is optimizing an unknown convex function over a continuum using only bandit feedback. In 1D, Thompson sampling enjoys
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
Bayesian regret in BCO under
convexity, boundedness, and a mild Lipschitz condition
(
4
)
.

Equivalence of function and outcome sampling.

The missing-data formulation of Section
2.2
samples a completion

\widetilde{U}_{1:T}^{(t)}\sim\mathbb{P}^{\star}(\cdot\mid H_{t-1})
and then applies an oracle map, e.g., the ERM oracle. The function-valued viewpoint is simply the pushforward of this posterior through the map that associates a completion to a risk function. To make this explicit, define the (random) empirical loss function associated with a complete trajectory

u_{1:T}\in\mathcal{U}^{T}
:
\widehat{f}_{u_{1:T}}(x):=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T}\ell(x,u_{s}),
where
x\in\mathcal{X}.

Let
\Phi:\mathcal{U}^{T}\to C(\mathcal{X})
denote the measurable map
\Phi(u_{1:T})=\widehat{f}_{u_{1:T}}(\cdot)
.
Then the posterior distribution of
\widehat{f}_{U_{1:T}}
given history is the pushforward measure

\Law(\widehat{f}_{U_{1:T}}\mid H_{t-1})=\Law(\Phi(U_{1:T})\mid H_{t-1})
.

Lemma 4

(Outcome-sampling and function-sampling induce the same action law)
.

Let
\hat{x}(u_{1:T})\in\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\widehat{f}_{u_{1:T}}(x)
be an oracle action computed from a completed
trajectory, using a fixed measurable tie-break rule.
Let
\widetilde{U}_{1:T}^{(t)}\sim\mathbb{P}^{\star}(\cdot\mid H_{t-1})
and set

X_{t}:=\hat{x}(\widetilde{U}_{1:T}^{(t)})
.
Alternatively, sample
\widetilde{f}^{(t)}\sim\Law(\widehat{f}_{U_{1:T}}\mid H_{t-1})
and set

\widetilde{X}_{t}\in\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\widetilde{f}^{(t)}(x)
(with the same tie-break rule).
Then

\Law(X_{t}\mid H_{t-1})=\Law(\widetilde{X}_{t}\mid H_{t-1}).

Proof.

By definition,
\widetilde{f}^{(t)}=\Phi(\widetilde{U}_{1:T}^{(t)})
in distribution conditional on
H_{t-1}
.
Since
\hat{x}(\cdot)
is a measurable function of
\Phi(\cdot)
(it depends on
u_{1:T}
only through

\widehat{f}_{u_{1:T}}
and the tie-break rule), we have

X_{t}=\hat{x}(\widetilde{U}_{1:T}^{(t)})=\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\Phi(\widetilde{U}_{1:T}^{(t)})(x).

Thus
X_{t}
is the pushforward of
\widetilde{U}_{1:T}^{(t)}
under the measurable map

u_{1:T}\mapsto\argmin_{x}\Phi(u_{1:T})(x)
, which is exactly the same pushforward used to define
\widetilde{X}_{t}
.
∎

Lemma
4
shows that
sampling missing outcomes and optimizing
and

sampling a function and optimizing
are mathematically the same operation: the latter is just the
pushforward of the former through a map from completed data to a loss function.
This equivalence is the continuous-action analogue of probability matching
(Lemma
1
).

Remark 5

(Non-stationary environments)
.

The function-valued viewpoint does
not
inherently require stationarity; what stationarity buys is a
time-invariant comparator
and a
single
latent risk function. If the data-generating process is non-stationary, the relevant object becomes a sequence

f^{\star}_{1},f^{\star}_{2},\dots
, where, for example,
f_{t}^{\star}(x):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\ell(x,U_{t})\right]
may vary with
t
. One then typically studies
dynamic regret
(tracking a changing benchmark) or imposes structure such as piecewise stationarity or bounded variation. Notably, even in the fully adversarial 1D BCO model—where an arbitrary sequence of convex loss functions is chosen—the minimax regret remains
\widetilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{T})
; the proof reduces the game to a Bayesian formulation and solves it
using a variant of Thompson sampling
(
9
)
. This indicates that stationarity is not necessary for
sublinear regret
in principle
. In our paper, we adopt the stationary formulation because it matches the operational setting (a fixed environment generating repeated instances). If drift is expected, a natural extension is to encode time or seasonality as context
43
or to use windowed conditioning in the generative model.

Censored NV problem.

We consider a horizon
T\in\mathbb{N}
with rounds indexed by
t\in[T]:=\{1,\dots,T\}
. Let the
action
space be
\mathcal{X}:=[0,B]
and the
demand

\mathcal{D}:=[0,B]
.
The latent demand is
D_{t}\in\mathcal{D}
. The decision (order quantity) is
X_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
. Given
(X_{t},D_{t})

S_{t}:=\min\{D_{t},X_{t}\}\in[0,B],C_{t}:=\mathbf{1}\{D_{t}\leq X_{t}\}\in\{0,1\},O_{t}:=(S_{t},C_{t})\in\mathcal{O}:=[0,B]\times\{0,1\}.

Equivalently,
O_{t}=\psi(X_{t},D_{t})
where
\psi(x,d)=(\min\{d,x\},\mathbf{1}\{d\leq x\})
.
We reserve the letter
C
exclusively for the censoring indicator (no other object uses
C
). Define
Z_{t}:=(X_{t},O_{t})
and the history (interaction trajectory) up to time
t
as

H_{t}:=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{t})=\bigl((X_{s},O_{s})\bigr)_{s=1}^{t},H_{0}:=\varnothing.

We write realizations as
h_{t}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{t})
, and use the shorthand
X_{1:t}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{t})
,
O_{1:t}=(O_{1},\dots,O_{t})
, etc.

Policies and induced laws.

For each
t
, the environment is specified by a Markov kernel

M^{\star}_{t}(\cdot\mid h_{t-1},x_{t})\in\Delta(\mathcal{O}).

A (possibly randomized, history-dependent) policy is
\pi=(\pi_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
where each

\pi_{t}(\cdot\mid h_{t-1})
is a probability measure on
\mathcal{X}
.
Let
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
denote the true environment (Bayesian model) governing latent demands.
Let
P_{\pi}^{\star}
denote the probability law of the entire interaction history
H_{T}

generated by
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
when actions are chosen according to
\pi
and observations are produced
via the censoring map
\psi
. The policy
\pi
is given by action kernels

K^{\pi}_{t}(\cdot\mid h_{t-1})\in\Delta(\mathcal{X}),t=1,\dots,T,

and induces a trajectory law
\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi}
on
Z_{1:T}=(X_{1},O_{1},\dots,X_{T},O_{T})
through

X_{t}\mid H_{t-1}\sim K^{\pi}_{t}(\cdot\mid H_{t-1})
, and

O_{t}\mid(H_{t-1},X_{t})\sim M^{\star}_{t}(\cdot\mid H_{t-1},X_{t}).

Completion kernels (trajectory-level conditional laws).

At time
t
, given a realized history
h_{t-1}
, define the
true completion kernel

as the conditional law of a full latent trajectory:

Q_{t}^{\star}(\,\cdot\mid h_{t-1}):=\mathrm{Law}\bigl(D_{1:T}\mid H_{t-1}=h_{t-1}\bigr),\text{a probability measure on }\mathcal{D}^{T}
. Let
p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h_{t-1})
be the learned generative model used to sample completions. Because observed right-censoring imposes hard feasibility constraints (e.g. if
C_{s}=1
then
D_{s}=S_{s}
, and if
C_{s}=0
then
D_{s}>X_{s}
for
s<t
), we define the

learned completion kernel
as the conditional law actually used online after enforcing

Q_{t,\theta}(\,\cdot\mid h_{t-1}):=\mathrm{Law}_{\theta}\bigl(\widetilde{D}_{1:T}^{(t)}\mid H_{t-1}=h_{t-1}\bigr),

where
\widetilde{D}_{1:T}^{(t)}\in\mathcal{D}^{T}
denotes the completed trajectory sampled at time
t
. The
true one-step completion kernel
is the conditional law of the next demand:

q_{t}^{\star}(\,\cdot\mid h_{t-1}):=\mathrm{Law}\!\left(D_{t}\,\middle|\,H_{t-1}=h_{t-1}\right),\text{a probability measure on }\mathcal{D}=[0,B]
. The
learned one-step completion kernel
is

q_{t,\theta}(\,\cdot\mid h_{t-1}):=\mathrm{Law}_{\theta}\!\left(\widetilde{D}_{t}^{(t)}\,\middle|\,H_{t-1}=h_{t-1}\right)
, where
\widetilde{D}_{t}^{(t)}
is the time-
t
coordinate of the completion sampled online at round
t
.

Induced action kernels and observed kernels.

Let
\varphi_{t}:\mathcal{D}^{T}\times\mathcal{H}_{t-1}\to\mathcal{X}
be a measurable oracle map
that converts a completion into an action (possibly depending on the history).
In the repeated newsvendor, the canonical choice is empirical-risk minimization on the completion:

\hat{x}(D_{1:T})\in\arg\min_{x\in[0,B]}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T}\ell(x,D_{s}),\text{and we can take }\varphi_{t}(D_{1:T},h_{t-1})=\hat{x}(D_{1:T}).

Define the induced
action kernels
(conditional laws of
X_{t}
given history) via pushforward:

K_{t}^{\star}(\,\cdot\mid h_{t-1}):=(\varphi_{t}(\cdot,h_{t-1}))_{\#}Q_{t}^{\star}(\,\cdot\mid h_{t-1}),K_{t,\theta}(\,\cdot\mid h_{t-1}):=(\varphi_{t}(\cdot,h_{t-1}))_{\#}Q_{t,\theta}(\,\cdot\mid h_{t-1}),
where the pushforward is defined by
(f_{\#}\mu)(A)=\mu(f^{-1}(A))
for measurable
A
. Moreover, let
P^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,x}
and
Q^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,x}
denote the conditional laws of
O_{t}

given
(H_{t-1},X_{t})=(h,x)
under the true environment and the learned model, respectively.

Mismatch functionals.

We use three KL-based mismatch functionals that are defined as follows:

1.

On-policy completion mismatch:

\Delta^{\mathrm{comp}}_{T}(\theta;\pi_{\theta}):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{H_{t-1}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\pi_{\theta}}^{\star}}\!\left[\mathrm{KL}\!\left(Q_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid H_{t-1})\,\big\|\,Q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid H_{t-1})\right)\right].

2.

Causal action mismatch:

\Delta_{T}^{\mathrm{act}}(\theta):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{H_{t-1}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\pi_{\theta}}^{\star}}\!\left[\mathrm{KL}\!\left(K_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid H_{t-1})\,\big\|\,K_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid H_{t-1})\right)\right].

3.

On-policy observed mismatch:

\Delta^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\theta;\pi_{\theta}):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{(H_{t-1},X_{t})\sim\mathbb{P}_{\pi_{\theta}}^{\star}}\!\left[\mathrm{KL}\!\left(P^{\mathrm{obs}}_{H_{t-1},X_{t}}\,\big\|\,Q^{\mathrm{obs}}_{H_{t-1},X_{t}}\right)\right].

\mathrm{KL}(P\|Q)=\sup_{f}\{\mathbb{E}_{P}[f]-\log\mathbb{E}_{Q}[e^{f}]\}

\mathbb{E}_{P}[f]\;\leq\;\mathrm{KL}(P\|Q)+\log\mathbb{E}_{Q}[e^{f}].

Q

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

\mathbb{E}_{Q}\!\left[\exp\!\Big(\lambda\big(X-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\big)\Big)\,\Big|\,\mathcal{G}\right]\;\leq\;\exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}(b-a)^{2}}{8}\right)\qquad Q\text{-a.s.}

C.2.1
Assumptions

Assumption 1

(Bounded increments)
.

Let
Q\coloneqq\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\star}}
. There exist constants
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
B^{\prime}>0
such that,
Q
-almost surely, for all

t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, the per-round regret

r_{t}\in[a,a+B^{\prime}].

Equivalently, the range length satisfies
\sup r_{t}-\inf r_{t}\leq B^{\prime}
a.s.

Assumption 2

(Deterministic predictable drift under
Q
)
.

Let
Q\coloneqq\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\star}}
.
For each
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, the conditional mean

\mu_{t}^{Q}:=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[r_{t}\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1}]

is
Q
-almost surely deterministic. In other words, there exists a constant
m_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\mu_{t}^{Q}=m_{t}

Q
-a.s. Equivalently, we can also say
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[r_{t}\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1}]=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[r_{t}]

Q
-a.s.

Assumption 3

(Bounded likelihood ratio)
.

There exists
V_{\mathrm{comp}}\in[1,\infty)
such that for all
t
and all histories
h\in\mathcal{H}_{t-1}
,

Q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)\ll Q^{\star}_{t}(\cdot\mid h)
and

\sup_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{U}}}\frac{Q_{t,\theta}(F\mid h)}{Q^{\star}_{t}(F\mid h)}\;\leq\;V_{\mathrm{comp}}.

Discussion.

Assumption
2
states that, under the benchmark measure
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
, the one-step

predictable drift
of the adapted sequence is
history-independent
:
for each
t
, the conditional mean
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[r_{t}\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1}]
is (almost surely) a
deterministic constant
m_{t}
. Intuitively, this rules out shocks in the filtration that could simultaneously shift many
future increments through their conditional expectations. Practically, such an assumption is natural whenever
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
describes a benchmark
data-generating process that is exogenous to the learner’s history, e.g., the environment noise is i.i.d. under
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
, or more generally, if the benchmark makes the conditional expectation time-dependent but history-independent.

Assumption
3
is a uniform support or overlap requirement between the learned completion kernel and the true completion kernel. Intuitively, this rules out missing mass and prevents extreme underestimation of events that can occur under
Q_{t}^{\star}
. In real scenarios, this is plausible when the model class is constrained to have the same effective support as the true completion process. The main implication for our proof is that this boundedness makes a change-of-measure possible by converting the
reverse
KL terms that arise naturally under deployment into
forward
KL terms that we can control via the on-policy completion mismatch, incurring only a constant logarithmic factor.

Lemma 12

(MGF bound for
R_{T}
)
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\log\mathbb{E}_{Q}\!\left[e^{\lambda R_{T}}\right]\leq\lambda\,\mathbb{E}_{Q}[R_{T}]\;+\;\frac{\lambda^{2}T(B^{\prime})^{2}}{8}.

Proof.

By Assumption
2
, define the deterministic constants

m_{t}:=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[r_{t}\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1}]
. Also define

d_{t}:=r_{t}-m_{t}.

Then
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[d_{t}\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1}]=0

Q
-a.s.
By Assumption
1
,
r_{t}\in[a,a+B^{\prime}]
a.s., hence
d_{t}
lies in an interval
of length
B^{\prime}
a.s. Therefore Lemma
7
, with
c=B^{\prime}
, gives

\log\mathbb{E}_{Q}\!\left[\exp\!\Big(\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}\Big)\right]\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}T(B^{\prime})^{2}}{8}.

Since
\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}=R_{T}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}m_{t}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[e^{\lambda R_{T}}]=e^{\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T}m_{t}}\;\mathbb{E}_{Q}\!\left[\exp\!\Big(\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}\Big)\right],

and taking logs yields

\log\mathbb{E}_{Q}[e^{\lambda R_{T}}]=\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T}m_{t}+\log\mathbb{E}_{Q}\!\left[\exp\!\Big(\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}\Big)\right]\leq\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T}m_{t}+\frac{\lambda^{2}T(B^{\prime})^{2}}{8}.

Finally, because each
m_{t}
is deterministic,

\sum_{t=1}^{T}m_{t}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{Q}[r_{t}\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1}]=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{Q}[r_{t}]=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[R_{T}],

where we used the tower property
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\mathbb{E}_{Q}[r_{t}\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1}]]=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[r_{t}]
and the

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[r_{t}\mid\mathcal{H}_{t-1}]=m_{t}
is constant.
Substituting completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 13

(Trajectory KL equals causal action mismatch)
.

Let
\pi_{\theta}
and
\pi_{\star}
be two policies interacting with the
same
environment kernels

\{M^{\star}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
.
Then we have the following equality below

\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\theta}}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\star}}\right)=\Delta_{T}^{\mathrm{act}}(\theta).

Proof.

Under
\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\theta}}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\star}}
, the joint density or kernel factorization over

is, informally,

\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi}(dz_{1:T})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}K^{\pi}_{t}(dx_{t}\mid h_{t-1})\,M^{\star}_{t}(do_{t}\mid h_{t-1},x_{t}),

where
z_{t}=(x_{t},o_{t})
and
h_{t-1}=z_{1:t-1}
.
Since
M^{\star}_{t}
is identical under both policies, the Radon–Nikodym derivative

is the product of the action-kernel ratios only, hence by the

\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\theta}}\,\middle\|\,\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\star}}\right)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\theta}}}\!\left[\mathrm{KL}\!\Big(K^{\pi_{\theta}}_{t}(\cdot\mid H_{t-1})\,\Big\|\,K^{\pi_{\star}}_{t}(\cdot\mid H_{t-1})\Big)\right].

Finally we can substitute
K^{\pi_{\theta}}_{t}=K_{t,\theta}
and
K^{\pi_{\star}}_{t}=K^{\star}_{t}
to obtain the desired result of the Lemma.
∎

(Causal action mismatch bounded by reverse completion mismatch)

\Delta_{T}^{\mathrm{act}}(\theta)\;\leq\;\Delta^{\mathrm{comp}}_{T,\,\mathrm{rev}}(\theta;\pi_{\theta}).

Proof.

Fix

H_{t-1}=h
.
By construction,
K^{\star}_{t}(\cdot\mid h)
and
K_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)
are pushforwards of

Q^{\star}_{t}(\cdot\mid h)
and

Thus the data-processing inequality for KL divergence (Lemma

\mathrm{KL}\!\Big(K_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)\,\Big\|\,K^{\star}_{t}(\cdot\mid h)\Big)\;\leq\;\mathrm{KL}\!\Big(Q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)\,\Big\|\,Q^{\star}_{t}(\cdot\mid h)\Big).

Taking expectation
\mathbb{E}_{H_{t-1}\sim\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\theta}}}
and summing over

Lemma 15

(Reverse completion mismatch is controlled by forward completion mismatch)
.

Under Assumption
3
,

\Delta^{\mathrm{comp}}_{T,\,\mathrm{rev}}(\theta;\pi_{\theta})\;\leq\;(2+\log V_{\mathrm{comp}})\,\Delta^{\mathrm{comp}}_{T}(\theta;\pi_{\theta}).

Proof.

Fix

and condition on
H_{t-1}=h
.
Let

and

Lemma 16

(Risk-equivalent objective
g_{\mathbb{P}}
)
.

For any
\mathbb{P}
, define

g_{\mathbb{P}}(x):=(h+b)\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\big[(x-D)^{+}]-bx
, where
x\in[0,B].
Then,
f_{\mathbb{P}}(x)\;=\;g_{\mathbb{P}}(x)+b\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[D]
, for all
x\in[0,B]
.
Consequently, for any policy
\pi
, the Bayesian regret can be analyzed using
g_{\mathbb{P}}
instead of
f_{\mathbb{P}}
, since

\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(f_{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t})-f_{\mathbb{P}}(x_{\mathbb{P}}^{\star})\big)\Big]\;=\;\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(g_{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t})-g_{\mathbb{P}}(x_{\mathbb{P}}^{\star})\big)\Big].

Proof.

Using the identity
(d-x)^{+}=(d-x)+(x-d)^{+}
, valid for all
x,d\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\ell(x,d)

\displaystyle=h(x-d)^{+}+b\big((d-x)+(x-d)^{+}\big)

\displaystyle=(h+b)(x-d)^{+}+b(d-x).

Taking conditional expectation given
\xi=\mathbb{P}
yields the following

\displaystyle f_{\mathbb{P}}(x)

\displaystyle=(h+b)\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\big[(x-D)^{+}\big]+b\big(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[D]-x\big)

\displaystyle=g_{\mathbb{P}}(x)+b\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[D].

The above proves the first claim of the Lemma. Recognizing that the additive term
b\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[D]
does not depend on
x
, hence cancels in regret and does not change minimizers, gives the final equality of the Bayesian regret terms.
∎

Lemma 17

(Randomization identity)
.

Fix
x\in[0,B]
. Let
U\sim\mathrm{Unif}([0,x])
be a random variable independent of
D
, with the convention that if
x=0
then
U\equiv 0
. Also define
Z:=\mathbf{1}\{D\leq U\}
. Then

\mathbb{E}[xZ]\;=\;\mathbb{E}[(x-D)^{+}].

Proof.

The claim is immediate for
x=0
. So, let us fix
x>0
. Conditioning on
D
, for any realization
D=d
we have

\mathbb{E}[Z\mid D=d]=\mathbb{P}(U\geq d\mid D=d)=\frac{(x-d)^{+}}{x},

since
U
is uniform on
[0,x]
.
Therefore, we have

\mathbb{E}[xZ\mid D]=x\,\mathbb{E}[Z\mid D]=(x-D)^{+},
where the first equality is due to the fact that
x
is deterministic here, and can therefore be taken outside the expectation. Finally, taking expectation again and applying the tower rule of conditional expectations gives
\mathbb{E}[xZ]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[xZ\mid D]]=\mathbb{E}[(x-D)^{+}]
.
∎

Lemma 18

(Observable unbiased feedback for
g_{\mathbb{P}}
)
.

Fix
x\in[0,B]
and define

as in Lemma
17
. Let us define a one-step bandit feedback as

\mathbb{P}

From the definition of

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[Y]

\displaystyle=(h+b)\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[xZ]-bx

\displaystyle=(h+b)\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[(x-D)^{+}]-bx

\displaystyle=g_{\mathbb{P}}(x),

where in the second step we used Lemma
17
.
∎

Lemma 19

(Computability of

. Let

Lemma 20

(Convexity, Lipschitzness, and bounded feedback)
.

For every
\mathbb{P}
, the function
g_{\mathbb{P}}:[0,B]\to\mathbb{R}
is convex and

L
-Lipschitz with
L:=\max\{h,b\}
.
Moreover, for any

satisfies the uniform bounds

Define
\Phi_{\mathbb{P}}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[(x-D)^{+}]
.
For each fixed

, the map

is convex, and expectation preserves convexity,

\Phi_{\theta}
is convex; hence
g_{\mathbb{P}}(x)=(h+b)\Phi_{\theta}(x)-bx

To obtain Lipschitzness, note that for each

lies in

Proof of the Theorem

Theorem

(Bayesian regret of TS for the repeated newsvendor)
.

Assume demands satisfy
D_{t}\in[0,B]
almost surely. Let
\pi^{\star}
be the Thompson sampling policy that
samples
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{t}
from the posterior of
\xi
given
H_{t-1}
and plays

X_{t}\in\arg\min_{x\in[0,B]}f_{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{t}}(x)
.
Then

\mathrm{BayesReg}_{T}(\pi^{\star})\;\leq\;\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T}),

where
\tilde{O}(\cdot)
suppresses polylogarithmic factors and constants depending on
h,b,B
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

By Lemma
16
, it suffices to bound

\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}(g_{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t})-g_{\mathbb{P}}(x_{\mathbb{P}}^{\star}))\big]
in order to bound
\mathrm{BayesReg}_{T}(\pi^{\star})
. The proof works by first showing that the R-NV admits the same feedback structure as bandit convex optimization (BCO) in
4
, and then using their Bayesian regret bound for Thompson Sampling in the case of one-dimensional actions
(
4
, Theorem 4)
.

The first step involves reducing the repeated newsvendor problem to a one-dimensional bandit convex optimization instance. Fix any round
t
. After choosing
X_{t}\in[0,B]
and observing censored feedback
O_{t}=(S_{t},C_{t})
,
sample
U_{t}\sim\mathrm{Unif}([0,X_{t}])
(degenerate at 0 if
X_{t}=0
), and compute
Z_{t}
from
(O_{t},U_{t})

as in Lemma
19
. Define the scalar feedback

Y_{t}:=(h+b)\,X_{t}Z_{t}-bX_{t}.

Then Lemma
18
ensures that conditional on
\mathbb{P}
and
X_{t}
,

\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}\mid\mathbb{P},X_{t}]=g_{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t}),

and Lemma
20
gives that
g_{\mathbb{P}}
is convex,
L
-Lipschitz, and
Y_{t}\in[-bB,hB]
.

The second step involves, normalization to the canonical BCO class. Define the rescaled action
\bar{X}_{t}:=X_{t}/B\in[0,1]
and the normalized loss feedback

\bar{Y}_{t}:=\frac{Y_{t}+bB}{(h+b)B}\in[0,1].

For each
\mathbb{P}
, define the normalized convex loss

\bar{g}_{\mathbb{P}}(u):=\frac{g_{\mathbb{P}}(Bu)+bB}{(h+b)B},u\in[0,1].

Then
\tilde{g}_{\mathbb{P}}
is convex, takes values in
[0,1]
, and is
1
-Lipschitz on
[0,1]

(because any subgradient of
g_{\mathbb{P}}
lies in
[-b,h]
, hence any subgradient of
\tilde{g}_{\mathbb{P}}

lies in
\big[-\frac{b}{h+b},\frac{h}{h+b}\big]\subseteq[-1,1])
.
Moreover,

\mathbb{E}[\bar{Y}_{t}\mid\mathbb{P}=\theta,\bar{X}_{t}=u]=\bar{g}_{\mathbb{P}}(u).

The third step is to recognize the equivalence between TS for outcome and function sampling, that has been detailed in Appendix
A.2
. The prior on
\mathbb{P}
induces a prior on the (random) convex function
\tilde{g}_{\mathbb{P}}
.
The TS policy
\pi^{\star}
samples
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{t}
from the posterior and plays a minimizer of

f_{\tilde{\theta}_{t}}
, equivalently a minimizer of
g_{\tilde{\theta}_{t}}
by Lemma
16
,
and equivalently a minimizer of
\tilde{g}_{\tilde{\theta}_{t}}
after the above scaling.
Therefore,
\pi^{\star}
is precisely Thompson sampling for the normalized one-dimensional BCO instance with convex 1-Lipschitz losses in
[0,1]
and bandit feedback
\bar{Y}_{t}
.

Finally, according to
(
4
, Theorem 4)
, Thompson sampling in one-dimensional bandit convex optimisation has Bayesian regret

under convexity, boundedness, and a Lipschitz assumption. Applying this to the normalized instance yields

\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(\tilde{g}_{\mathbb{P}}(\bar{X}_{t})-\tilde{g}_{\mathbb{P}}(\tilde{x}_{\mathbb{P}}^{\star})\big)\Big]\;\leq\;\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T}),
, where
\tilde{x}_{\mathbb{P}}^{\star}\in\arg\min_{u\in[0,1]}\bar{g}_{\mathbb{P}}(u)
corresponds to
x_{\mathbb{P}}^{\star}
under scaling.
Undoing the normalization from above gives

\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(g_{\mathbb{P}}(X_{t})-g_{\mathbb{P}}(x_{\mathbb{P}}^{\star})\big)\Big]\;\leq\;(h+b)B\cdot\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})\;=\;\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T}),

absorbing
(h+b)B
into the
\tilde{O}(\cdot)
notation.
Lastly, Lemma

transfers this bound back to

∎

Assumption 4

(Max-order coverage)
.

There exists

Assumption

h\in H_{t-1}
,
the policy selects the maximal order
X_{t}=B
with probability at least
\eta>0
. In our censored setting,
the event
\{X_{t}=B\}
yields fully informative observations because the censoring map does not
truncate demand at the boundary, so a nonvanishing fraction of rounds directly captures the latent demand
mechanism. This mirrors the observable boundary phenomenon in

Assumption 5

(Self-contraction along AR pseudo-generation)

There exists
c_{\mathrm{sc}}\in(0,\infty)
such that for every history value
h

s\geq 2

\delta_{s}(h)\leq\frac{c_{\mathrm{sc}}}{s}\,d(h),

\delta_{s}(h)

s

defined in (
22
) below.

Discussion

Assumption
5
is a non-accumulation condition for autoregressive (AR) pseudo-generation in the completion model. The assumption requires a harmonic decay, meaning that errors in later AR conditionals are controlled by (and shrink relative to) the first-step mismatch, rather than compounding linearly with rollout length. Intuitively, this captures a self-correcting regime: as pseudo-history grows, conditioning becomes more informative, and the AR process becomes increasingly stable to small perturbations. The key implication for our proof is that this assumption yields a sharp control of the full completion KL by the one-step mismatch up to a mild
\log T

Lemma 21

(Observed KL mismatch equals excess observed log-loss)
.

We have the identity

\Delta^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\theta;\pi)=\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\theta;\pi)-\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\star;\pi)\;\;\geq\;0.

Proof.

Fix
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
and condition on
(H_{t-1},X_{t})=(h,x)

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\!\big(P^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,x}\,\|\,Q^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,x}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\log\!\left(\frac{p^{\star}_{\mathrm{obs}}(O_{t}\mid h,x)}{p_{\theta,\mathrm{obs}}(O_{t}\mid h,x)}\right)\,\Big|\,H_{t-1}=h,X_{t}=x\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[-\log p_{\theta,\mathrm{obs}}(O_{t}\mid h,x)+\log p^{\star}_{\mathrm{obs}}(O_{t}\mid h,x)\,\Big|\,H_{t-1}=h,X_{t}=x\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\ell^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\theta}(O_{t}\mid h,x)-\ell^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\star}(O_{t}\mid h,x)\,\Big|\,H_{t-1}=h,X_{t}=x\right].

Now take expectation over
(H_{t-1},X_{t})\sim\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi}
and apply the tower property to obtain the following

\mathbb{E}_{(H_{t-1},X_{t})\sim\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi}}\Big[\mathrm{KL}\!\big(P^{\mathrm{obs}}_{H_{t-1},X_{t}}\,\|\,Q^{\mathrm{obs}}_{H_{t-1},X_{t}}\big)\Big]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi}}\!\big[\ell^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\theta}(O_{t}\mid H_{t-1},X_{t})-\ell^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\star}(O_{t}\mid H_{t-1},X_{t})\big].

Finally, summing over
t=1,\dots,T
and using linearity of expectation yields the desired identity

\Delta^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\theta;\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi}}\!\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\theta}(O_{t}\mid H_{t-1},X_{t})\Big]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi}}\!\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\star}(O_{t}\mid H_{t-1},X_{t})\Big]=\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\theta;\pi)-\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\star;\pi).

Moreover,
\Delta^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\theta;\pi)\geq 0
holds because KL divergence is always nonnegative due to Gibbs’ inequality.
∎

Lemma 22

(Invertible censoring at
x=B
preserves KL)
.

Assume that for all
t
,
D_{t}\in[0,B]
almost surely under the true environment. Fix
h
and let
q_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid h),q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)
be probability measures on
[0,B]
.
Then

\mathrm{KL}(q_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid h)\|q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h))\;=\;\mathrm{KL}\big(P^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,B}\,\|\,Q^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,B}\big).

Proof.

First, we recall that for any action
x\in[0,B]
, the induced conditional law of the censored observation
O=\psi(x,D)
is defined by pushforward:

P^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,x}:=q_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid h)\circ(\psi(x,\cdot))^{-1},Q^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,x}:=q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)\circ(\psi(x,\cdot))^{-1}
. Under the bounded demand assumption, for
x=B
we have
\psi(B,d)=(d,1)
for all
d\in[0,B]
.
Define the measurable bijection
\phi:[0,B]\to O_{B}:=[0,B]\times\{1\}
by
\phi(d)=(d,1)
, whose measurable inverse is

\phi^{-1}(s,1)=s
.
By construction,
P^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,B}=q_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid h)\circ\phi^{-1}
and
Q^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,B}=q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)\circ\phi^{-1}
.

Let
R:=\frac{dq_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid h)}{dq_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)}
be the Radon–Nikodym derivative (with the convention
\mathrm{KL}(q_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid h)\|q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h))=+\infty
if
q_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid h)\not\ll q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)
).
Then
P^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,B}\ll Q^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,B}
and

\frac{dP^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,B}}{dQ^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,B}}(o)=R(\phi^{-1}(o))

for
o\in O_{B}
. Therefore, by change of variables under the pushforward,

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\big(P^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,B}\,\|\,Q^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,B}\big)

\displaystyle=\int_{O_{B}}\log\!\Big(\frac{dP^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,B}}{dQ^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,B}}(o)\Big)\,dP^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,B}(o)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,B]}\log(R(d))\,dq_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid h)(d)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{KL}(q_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid h)\|q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)).

Informally, when
X_{t}=B
, the right-censoring map reveals the demand exactly, so the observed KL mismatch at B equals the latent one-step completion mismatch. The above is a formal proof for this simple intuition.
∎

Lemma 23

(Coverage turns observed mismatch into completion mismatch)
.

Assume for all
t
,
D_{t}\in[0,B]
almost surely under the true environment. Moreover, also assume Assumption
4
. Let
\mu(\cdot\mid h)
be any action distribution on
[0,B]
satisfying
\mu(\{B\}\mid h)\geq\eta
.
Then

\mathrm{KL}(q_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid h)\|q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h))\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\eta}\;\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu(\cdot\mid h)}\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(P^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,X}\,\|\,Q^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,X}\big)\Big].

(20)

Proof.

Define the nonnegative function
m(x):=\mathrm{KL}\big(P^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,x}\,\|\,Q^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,x}\big)\geq 0
.
By Lemma
22
,
m(B)=\mathrm{KL}(q_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid h)\|q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h))
.
Therefore, using these two results we get the foll

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu(\cdot\mid h)}[m(X)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[m(X)\mathbf{1}\{X=B\}]+\mathbb{E}[m(X)\mathbf{1}\{X\neq B\}]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}[m(X)\mathbf{1}\{X=B\}]

\displaystyle=m(B)\,\mu(\{B\}\mid h)

\displaystyle\geq\eta\,\mathrm{KL}(q_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid h)\|q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)),

where the first equality is due to the non-negativity of KL divergence or Gibbs’ inequality. Finally, we can rearrange to obtain (
20
), which is the desired result of this Lemma.
∎

Lemma 24

(KL decomposition for AR-completion kernels)
.

Fix
(t,h)
and suppose the (true and learned) completion kernels admit autoregressive factorizations

Q^{\star}_{t}(d\tilde{d}_{1:T}\mid h)=q^{\star}_{t,1}(d\tilde{d}_{1}\mid h)\prod_{s=2}^{T}q^{\star}_{t,s}(d\tilde{d}_{s}\mid h,\tilde{d}_{1:s-1}),

Q_{t,\theta}(d\tilde{d}_{1:T}\mid h)=q_{t,1,\theta}(d\tilde{d}_{1}\mid h)\prod_{s=2}^{T}q_{t,s,\theta}(d\tilde{d}_{s}\mid h,\tilde{d}_{1:s-1}),

where the product is the usual iterated kernel product construction.
Define

d(h):=\mathrm{KL}\big(q^{\star}_{t,1}(\cdot\mid h)\,\|\,q_{t,1,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)\big),

(21)

and for
s\geq 2
,

\delta_{s}(h):=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{D}_{1:s-1}\sim Q^{\star}_{t}(\cdot\mid h)}\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(q^{\star}_{t,s}(\cdot\mid h,\tilde{D}_{1:s-1})\,\|\,q_{t,s,\theta}(\cdot\mid h,\tilde{D}_{1:s-1})\big)\Big].

(22)

Then

\mathrm{KL}\big(Q^{\star}_{t}(\cdot\mid h)\,\|\,Q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)\big)=d(h)+\sum_{s=2}^{T}\delta_{s}(h).

Proof.

First we apply the two variable chain rule for KL (Lemma
11
) with
(X,Y)=(\tilde{D}_{1:T-1},\tilde{D}_{T})
. Then we recurse on the first term

\mathrm{KL}(\mathrm{Law}(\tilde{D}_{1:T-1})\|\mathrm{Law}_{\theta}(\tilde{D}_{1:T-1}))
, splitting off
\tilde{D}_{T-1}
, etc.
After
T-1
steps this yields the stated decomposition, with the first marginal term equal to
d(h)
and the summation of the remaining terms equal to
\delta_{s}(h)
by definition.
∎

Lemma 25

(Self-contraction implies completion KL is controlled by one-step KL)
.

Assume Assumption
5
. Then for every
(t,h)
,

\mathrm{KL}\big(Q^{\star}_{t}(\cdot\mid h)\,\|\,Q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)\big)\;\leq\;\bigl(1+c_{\mathrm{sc}}\log T\bigr)\,d(h).

Proof.

Using Lemma
24
followed by Assumption
5
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\big(Q^{\star}_{t}(\cdot\mid h)\,\|\,Q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)\big)

\displaystyle=d(h)+\sum_{s=2}^{T}\delta_{s}(h)

\displaystyle\leq d(h)+\sum_{s=2}^{T}\frac{c_{\mathrm{sc}}}{s}\,d(h)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(1+c_{\mathrm{sc}}\sum_{s=2}^{T}\frac{1}{s}\Bigr)d(h).

Now, using
\sum_{s=2}^{T}\frac{1}{s}\leq\log T
to bound the last expression gives the claim of this Lemma.
∎

C.4.3
Proof of the Theorem

Theorem

(Relation between completion mismatch and censoring-aware predictive objective)
.

Assume bounded demand (
D_{t}\in[0,B]
a.s.), max-order coverage (Assumption
4
),
and self-contraction (Assumption
5
). Then

\Delta^{\mathrm{comp}}_{T}(\theta;\pi_{\theta})\;\leq\;\frac{1+c_{\mathrm{sc}}\log T}{\eta}\;\Delta^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\theta;\pi_{\theta}).

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Fix
t
and condition on
H_{t-1}=h
. The first step is to apply Lemma
25
and obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\big(Q^{\star}_{t}(\cdot\mid h)\,\|\,Q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)\big)\leq(1+c_{\mathrm{sc}}\log T)\,d(h).

(23)

The second step is to recognize that
d(h)=\mathrm{KL}(q_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid h)\|q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h))
, due to the following reasoning. The first-step AR marginal
q^{\star}_{t,1}(\cdot\mid h)
is (by construction of the pseudo-generation order)
the conditional law of the current-period latent demand
D_{t}
given
h
, i.e. it equals
q_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid h)
, and similarly

q_{t,1,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)=q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)
.

In the third step, letting
\mu(\cdot\mid h)
be the deployed action distribution at
h
(i.e.
X_{t}\mid H_{t-1}=h\sim\mu(\cdot\mid h)
), yields the following using Lemma
23

\displaystyle d(h)=\mathrm{KL}(q_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid h)\|q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h))\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\;\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}\sim\mu(\cdot\mid h)}\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(P^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,X_{t}}\,\|\,Q^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,X_{t}}\big)\Big].

(24)

Combining the (
23
) and (
24
) above we obtain,

\mathrm{KL}\big(Q^{\star}_{t}(\cdot\mid h)\,\|\,Q_{t,\theta}(\cdot\mid h)\big)\leq\frac{1+c_{\mathrm{sc}}\log T}{\eta}\;\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}\sim\mu(\cdot\mid h)}\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(P^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,X_{t}}\,\|\,Q^{\mathrm{obs}}_{h,X_{t}}\big)\Big].

Finally, taking expectation over
H_{t-1}
under
\mathbb{P}^{\star}_{\pi_{\theta}}
and summing over
t=1,\dots,T

\Delta^{\mathrm{comp}}_{T}(\theta;\pi_{\theta})\leq\frac{1+c_{\mathrm{sc}}\log T}{\eta}\;\Delta^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\theta;\pi_{\theta}),

as claimed. Moreover, Lemma
21
connects the definition of
\Delta^{\mathrm{obs}}_{T}(\theta;\pi_{\theta})
in the manuscript (based on excess observed log-loss) to the definition using observed KL mismatch, completing our claim in the Theorem.
∎

Appendix D
Experimental Details

D.1
ChronosFlow-ICGPS Architecture

In this Appendix, we provide some further details regarding the ChronosFlow-ICGPS architecture. Similar to the main paper, we study the architecture under three headings: (a) conditioning context vector layer, (b) conditional normalizing flow (CNF) head, and (c) ICGPS sampler, the first two of which we elaborate below, along with details about training.

Conditioning context vector layer

We start by describing the prompt structure for the Chronos-2 backbone. Note that Chronos-2 expects a time-series prompt. We encode
H_{t-1}
using a demand-proxy series and optional auxiliary channels. The demand-proxy series is given by

\widetilde{Z}_{s}:=S_{s}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{C_{s}=1\}+X_{s}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{C_{s}=0\},s<t,

i.e., revealed demand when uncensored and the known lower bound when censored. We provide Chronos with
(Z_{s})_{s<t}
as the main series, and optionally
(X_{s})_{s<t}
and
(C_{s})_{s<t}
as covariates.

From this prompt, Chronos-2 returns predictive quantiles
\bigl\{q^{\mathrm{Chr}}_{\alpha}(t)\bigr\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}
on a fixed grid

\mathcal{J}:=\{0.05,0.10,\dots,0.95\}
. Secondly, we maintain a KM product-limit estimator treating
C_{s}=1
as events at
S_{s}
and
C_{s}=0
as right-censoring
at
X_{s}
. Writing the KM survival estimate as
\widehat{S}_{t-1}(\cdot)
, we obtain the KM quantiles as
\bigl\{q^{\mathrm{KM}}_{\alpha}(t)\bigr\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}},

where
q^{\mathrm{KM}}_{\alpha}(t):=\inf\{z\in[0,B]:1-\widehat{S}_{t-1}(z)\geq\alpha\}
. Thirdly, the summary statistics are obtained as

\mathrm{stats}(H_{t-1}):=\Big(X_{t-1},S_{t-1},\ \widehat{\mu}(\widetilde{Z})_{t-1},\widehat{\mathrm{sd}}(\widetilde{Z})_{t-1},\ \widehat{\rho}_{t-1},\ t-1\Big),

where
\widehat{\rho}_{t-1}:=\frac{1}{t-1}\sum_{s<t}\mathbf{1}\{C_{s}=0\}
is the severity indicator of censoring. The conditioning vector is defined as concatenation of above

\overline{H}_{t}:=\omega(H_{t-1}):=\Big(\{q^{\mathrm{Chr}}_{\alpha}(t)\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}},\{q^{\mathrm{KM}}_{\alpha}(t)\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}},\mathrm{stats}(H_{t-1})\Big)\in\mathbb{R}^{44}
.

Conditional normalizing flow head.

We model demand via a monotone flow

D_{t}=T_{\theta}(\widetilde{Z};\overline{h}_{t}),

where
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
T_{\theta}(\cdot;\overline{h})
is strictly increasing. We use a piecewise-linear map on latent support

[-L,L]
(linear tails beyond) with
K
bins: an MLP hypernetwork maps
\overline{h}_{t}
to positive bin widths/slopes via

softplus
. The induced CDF and inverse-CDF are given by

F_{\theta}(d\mid\overline{h}_{t})=\Phi\!\bigl(T_{\theta}^{-1}(d;\overline{h}_{t})\bigr),
and

Q_{\theta}(u\mid\overline{h}_{t})=T_{\theta}\!\bigl(\Phi^{-1}(u);\overline{h}_{t}\bigr)
, respectively.

Training details.

We use: AdamW optimizer with learning rate on the order of
10^{-4}
when fine-tuning the Chronos backbone (when enabled for i.i.d. experiments), AdamW optimizer with learning rate on the order of
10^{-3}
for CNF training, minibatch sizes ranging from 128 (Chronos fine-tuning) to 2048 (CNF training), and optional global-norm gradient clipping for stability. We select the best checkpoint by validation censored-NLL.

D.2
Experiment
4.3.4
on real-world dataset

This appendix records the dataset-to-episode mapping and evaluation protocol used in
Experiment
4.3.4
. We follow
17
for preprocessing,
splits, and the
\lambda
-controlled censoring procedure to enable direct comparison.

Dataset construction

We map a transactional retail dataset, called SuperStore
(
32
)
, into
episodes
(selling seasons), where each episode is an ordered sequence of periods. We treat each (product, season/store) unit as an episode. Within an episode, records are ordered chronologically to form the sequential history
H_{t-1}=\{(X_{s},O_{s})\}_{s\leq t-1}
required by
Algorithm
2
. We apply the same filtering and preprocessing choices as

17
(e.g., removing incomplete seasons and using their normalization/field
handling) so that the resulting benchmark instances match their setting.
We evaluate across the censoring-control values
\lambda
defined in

17
; smaller
\lambda
induces heavier censoring. For each
\lambda
, we run
the same season-based evaluation described below.
We meta-train on historical seasons and evaluate online on held-out seasons using the split
convention of
17
. Online evaluation treats each held-out season as a fresh
episode: the policy selects an order each period and updates only through the evolving censored
history
H_{t}
.

Algorithm variants.

We report two ChronosFlow-ICGPS variants under an identical online wrapper and evaluation protocol:
(i)
Native
, whose completion kernel is trained only on the target dataset’s historical seasons,
and (ii)
Meta
, whose completion kernel is pretrained on the other real datasets and then
deployed on the target dataset under the same season-based protocol. The purpose is to isolate
whether cross-dataset pretraining improves robustness.

Results and discussion

Tables
1
,
D.1
–
D.2
report performance across product categories and

\lambda
values, compared to baselines SAA (sample average approximation), KM (Kaplan-Meier), and RCN
(
17
)
. The observations are summarized below

•

Heavy censoring (
\lambda\leq 3
): strongest gains.

Under severe censoring, demand is weakly observed early in the season, making cold-start learning
difficult for methods that rely on effectively uncensored demand estimates. ChronosFlow-ICGPS
(
Meta
) achieves its largest improvements in this regime; for example, on
Furniture
at

\lambda=1
,
Meta
achieves
4.1
versus RCN
19.8
and SAA
16.0
, and on

Technology
at
\lambda=3
,
Meta
achieves
4.2
(best).

•

As censoring relaxes: gaps narrow, but not uniformly.

As
\lambda
increases and more periods effectively reveal demand, performance differences often
shrink. The stabilization point is category-dependent (e.g., some categories tighten earlier than
others), reflecting heterogeneous demand dynamics and noise under the same protocol.

•

Meta vs. Native: cross-dataset pretraining improves stability.

Across
\lambda
,
Meta
is typically more stable than
Native
. A representative
instance is
Office Supplies
at
\lambda=15
, where
Native
degrades to
18.2
while

Meta
remains at
12.8
. This suggests cross-dataset pretraining helps
both during heavy censoring and by regularizing adaptation when censoring is light.

•

Conclusion.

The real-data benchmark supports that completion-based in-context GPS transfers beyond i.i.d. synthetic
demand to real seasonal episodes with heterogeneous censoring, with the clearest benefits under
severe censoring and competitive performance elsewhere.
