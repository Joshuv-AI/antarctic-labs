Understanding the costs and cost-efficiencies of preventing stunting combining standard of care, nutrition supplementation and cash transfer interventions in Southern Angola: an analysis of implementation data from the MuCCUA cluster randomised trial
Authors: Rocio Martin-Cañavate, Chloe Puett, Elena Trigo, Maria de Lourdes Faria, Ana Silva Gerardo, Paula Cristina Gonçalves, Eva Iráizoz, Israel Molina, Estefania Custodio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTo advance stunting reduction efforts, robust cost evidence on multisectoral interventions is essential for optimising resources and achieving outcomes efficiently. This study objective was to estimate the cost and cost-efficiency of three multisector interventions that combined standard of care (SOC), SOC plus cash transfers (SOC+CT) and SOC plus nutritional supplementation (SOC+NS) with small quantity lipid-based nutrient supplements (SQ-LNS) complemented with a family food ration (FFR) to prevent stunting in Southern Angola. Cost-effectiveness will be estimated in a future study.MethodsInterventions were nested within the Mother and Child Chronic Undernutrition in Angola (MuCCUA) trial and costs estimated after 1 year of implementation. Using a mixed-methods approach, we estimated financial and economic costs from institutional and societal perspectives. Activity-based costing and the ingredients approach were used to capture and calculate total institutional costs and opportunity costs incurred by participants. Cost-efficiency of each intervention was calculated as cost per participant reached. Costs were adjusted for inflation and reported in 2024 USD.ResultsThrough the first 12-month implementation period, MuCCUA interventions covered 1423 pregnant women and their children. Total institutional costs by intervention arms were: US$190 408 for SOC (n=463), US$414 946 for SOC+CT (n=479) and US$515 799 for SOC+NS (n=481). Total monthly costs per participant reached were US$12.85, US$27.07 and US$33.51, respectively, for SOC, SOC+CT and SOC+NS (societal costs: US$13.48, US$28.26, US$34.56). Personnel (73%) and staff training (24%) drove most costs in SOC, whereas supplies—cash transfers (32%) in SOC+CT and SQ-LNS with FFR (35%) in SOC+NS—were the largest expenses.ConclusionIntervention costs were higher in the SOC+CT and SOC+NS arms driven by inputs and recurrent distributions of cash transfers and nutrition supplies and lower in the SOC driven by personnel and training. Cost-efficiency could be increased by increasing the implementation period, so that start-up costs are spread over a long period of time. Disaggregated costing enhances understanding of resource use in complex programmes, supporting operational planning and budgeting, and will inform forthcoming cost-effectiveness analyses once outcome data become available.Trial registration numberNCT05571280.Keywords: Health economics, Nutrition, Africa South of the Sahara, Health policy

Introduction

Stunting, defined as low height-for-age, is a largely irreversible outcome originating in utero and early childhood due to inadequate maternal health and nutrition, suboptimal infant and young child feeding practices, and repeated infections.1 Although global stunting prevalence declined from 40.2% in 1990 to 22.3% in 2022,2 rates in Africa remain high at 30%, despite notable improvements.3 Stunting before age two is linked to long-term cognitive and educational deficits, increased non-communicable disease risk and reduced labour productivity and income earning potential in adulthood.4 Interventions during the first 1000 days have proven to be more effective in preventing stunting, especially when implemented through a multisectoral approach combining nutrition-specific and nutrition-sensitive actions.5 Evidence on effective interventions to prevent stunting continues to accumulate, though evidence on multisectoral approaches remains limited. Nutrition-specific interventions, such as small-quantity lipid-based nutrient supplements (SQ-LNS), have shown positive impacts in preventing stunting,6 while cash transfer interventions, especially those paired with food or healthcare, have shown to be more effective for stunting prevention than cash alone.7 Additionally, health promotion and behaviour change interventions have proven to be effective drivers of stunting prevention in sub-Saharan Africa,8 especially when thoughtfully integrated with nutrition-specific and nutrition-sensitive elements.9

Despite evidence on effectiveness, rigorous data on costs and cost-efficiency of these multisectoral programmes to prevent stunting remains limited.10 Such information is essential for programme implementers, local authorities and policymakers to inform operational planning, choice of delivery model, budgeting, efficient allocation of resources and prioritisation of investments, particularly in rural regions of low- and middle-income countries where undernutrition is more prevalent and implementation costs are challenging to assess.11 Many nutrition and public health strategies remain unsustainable due to reliance on limited local resources.12 Economic evaluations can identify potential cost savings by improving delivery efficiency or reducing the prices of major cost drivers.10 Therefore, comprehensive cost analyses of multisectoral interventions, including opportunity costs to participants, are needed in areas with high stunting rates.13 Such implementation cost evidence is crucial to guide budgeting, support the scale-up of interventions and strengthen the case for cross-sectoral collaboration between traditionally distinct sectors, all critical for long-term programme sustainability.14

Stunting remains a significant public health problem in Angola, with a national prevalence of 38% and high rates in the Southern provinces of Huila and Cunene, at 49.9% and 37.2%, respectively.15 In these provinces, the Crescer project implemented three multisectoral interventions, which will be evaluated by the Mother and Child Chronic Undernutrition in Angola (MuCCUA) trial. This community-based trial aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of three maternal and child nutrition interventions combining standard of care (SOC) (health promotion and pharmacological prevention), nutritional supplementation consisting of SQ-LNS complemented with a family food ration (FFR) and unconditional cash transfers in preventing stunting. It provided a unique perspective on the study of chronic malnutrition by simultaneously comparing nutrition-specific with nutrition-sensitive interventions within the same trial and populations, which has only been done in very few studies in Pakistan16 and Mali17 to our knowledge. Here, we report on the first part of the economic evaluation of the MuCCUA trial interventions.18

The present study aims to report and better understand the costs of the MuCCUA trial interventions during the first year of implementation in Southern Angola. The specific objectives of this economic evaluation are: (1) to estimate total programme costs and cost-efficiency (costs per participating mother–child pair) of implementing the three interventions; (2) to estimate societal costs of the interventions for participants to identify unintended participant costs and inform the design of more efficient and accessible interventions; (3) to present cost shares disaggregated by input, activity and stage; (4) to explore cost drivers to optimise interventions operating under resource constraints.

Once effectiveness data becomes available from the MuCCUA trial, costs will be combined with programme outcomes for a full economic evaluation including cost-effectiveness analyses.

Methods

MuCCUA trial study design

The MuCCUA trial was a community-based three arm, cluster randomised trial set in four communes (Jamba, Libongue, Mupa and Otchinjau) of the Southern provinces of Huila and Cunene in Angola. The trial followed mother–child pairs from pregnancy through 2 years of age of the newborn child from October 2022 to October 2025. Informed community and individual-level consent were obtained to participate in the trial and surveys. Detailed descriptions of the MuCCUA trial interventions and study design are presented elsewhere.19

Study setting

Both Huila and Cunene provinces are predominantly rural, with estimated populations of 3.2 million and 1.3 million, respectively.20 According to the latest official data, the prevalence of stunting among children under five is estimated at 50% in selected areas of Huila province and 37% in Cunene province, while the prevalence of acute malnutrition is approximately 11% in both provinces.15

Interventions and study participants

The MuCCUA study population were pregnant women 16 years of age or older and their newborn children residing in the four communes, who were target recipients of one of three arms: SOC, SOC+unconditional cash transfer (SOC+CT) or SOC+nutrition supplementation (SOC+NS). A total of 36 clusters were randomised to enrol 1440 pregnant women who are being followed through the first 2 years of their children’s lives. Interventions were implemented from October 2022 to October 2025 by the Institute of Local Development-Social Support Fund of Angola (FAS) and Vall d’Hebron Institute of Research (VHIR), with technical support from Instituto de Salud Carlos III (ISCIII), Universidade Mandume Ya Ndumefayo (UMN) and Action Against Hunger (ACF). The SOC intervention was delivered at community level, to all residents of the clusters not only study participants, whereas the supplies in the SOC+CT and SOC+NS interventions were delivered at the individual level to participant women. The three MuCCUA interventions have been described elsewhere,18 19 and are concisely described in figure 1.

Standard of care arm

The SOC arm comprised two different components, health and nutrition promotion activities delivered by Community and Health Development Agents (ADECOS) and preventive pharmacological activities. It involved recruiting ADECOS and carrying out activities outlined in Angola’s National Development Plan21 and Multisectoral Strategic Plan on Nutrition.22 These activities were aligned with WHO recommendations on maternal and child health and follow the guidelines established in the National Policy for ADECOS. As this policy had not yet been fully operationalised in the study areas at the time of the trial, recruitment and training of ADECOS were required to enable the implementation of these SOC activities in line with national guidelines. A total of 209 (110 male/99 female) ADECOS were recruited, trained and active in the four communes since July 2022. SOC activities can be grouped in two blocks:

Health promotion community activities delivered by ADECOS to improve accessibility to primary healthcare and community awareness in nutrition, water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) and health-related good practices. ADECOS received an initial 21-day training and fortnightly trainings to implement community sensitisation sessions every 15 days and biweekly home visits to families in their communities.

Health promotion community activities delivered by ADECOS to improve accessibility to primary healthcare and community awareness in nutrition, water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) and health-related good practices. ADECOS received an initial 21-day training and fortnightly trainings to implement community sensitisation sessions every 15 days and biweekly home visits to families in their communities.

Provision of preventative pharmacological inputs including: monthly malaria prophylaxis with sulfadoxine–pyrimethamine for pregnant women from the 13th week of pregnancy, deworming with single-dose albendazole for pregnant women from the second trimester and semiannually for children aged 12–23 months and semiannual vitamin A supplementation for children 6–23 months.

Standard of care + cash transfer arm

The second arm received SOC+CT. The amounts distributed are 10 855 AOA/month (US$25.9, October 2022) for households with three or fewer family members and 13 855 AOA/month (US$33.1, October 2022) for households with four or more family members. The amount was directly delivered to study participants at designated distribution points.

Standard of care + nutritional supplementation arm

The third arm received SOC+NS with SQ-LNS and a FFR. Nutritional supplementation included a daily individual ration of SQ-LNS for pregnant women (1 sachet of 20 g of Enov'Mum+) and for children 6–23 months (1 sachet of 20 g of Enov'Nutributter+), both produced and packaged by Nutriset in France. A complementary FFR consisting of a basket of locally-produced staple foods was distributed at designated distribution points to supplement the usual diet (providing 300 kcal per person/day).

Evaluating costs

Our costing study adopted a societal perspective, considering costs from both implementing institutions and intervention participants.23 We used the Strengthening Economic Evaluation for Multisectoral Strategies for Nutrition (SEEMS-Nutrition) costing framework that provides standardised protocols, data collection tools and guidance for allocating costs.24 A mixed-methods approach combining financial and economic costs was used to measure and allocate costs for programme activities and inputs. Financial costs, defined as direct monetary expenditures, were estimated using the activity-based costing method, using project’s accounting records and staff time allocation. The ingredients approach was used to estimate economic costs, which include costs not included in accounting records and the direct and indirect opportunity costs incurred by beneficiary households during programme participation, collected via key informant interviews, focus group discussions (FGDs) and structured questionnaires.25 The distribution of cash transfers (SOC+CT arm) and FFR (SOC+NS arm) was implemented by external service providers, who charged a fixed percentage of the total value distributed, included in the final input cost. Triangulating input costs required balancing purchase invoices against beneficiary distribution lists. A bottom-up costing approach was used to calculate input costs (cash transfer, FFR, SQ-LNS and pharmacological supplies delivered to health posts). SQ-LNS supplements were delivered fortnightly to beneficiaries through ADECOS. A bottom-up approach was used to estimate total quantities delivered by mother–child pair and the price per sachet was calculated using purchase invoices. The full costing methodology is presented in the economic evaluation protocol.18 Project records and population census data were used to determine the number of beneficiaries reached.

Data collection

All cost data were collected prospectively from the MuCCUA trial launch in October 2022 until 1 year of full implementation in February 2024 and divided into start-up and recurrent costs. The cost analysis captures the total costs of the MuCCUA trial interventions and the incremental costs of adding another component to the SOC. Data were collected from the period of February 2021–February 2024, comprising a 22-month start-up period (February 2021–September 2022) and a 12-month full implementation phase with the total sample of the trial (March 2023–February 2024). Due to logistical and bureaucratic challenges that delayed enrolment, the recruitment of participants lasted from October 2022 to February 2023, with the full sample of 1423 participants being completed by March 2023. Consequently, this cost analysis of the first year of implementation covers the period from March 2023 to February 2024. This time frame was selected to reflect the costs of implementation under standard operating conditions, aiming to simulate what costs would be in a routine delivery context, without the delays, inefficiencies and disruptions associated with the trial setup and the COVID-19 context.

Most of the cost data were extracted from Crescer accounting ledgers, and other sources were used to triangulate and prorate it. We collected data to evaluate participants’ opportunity costs as well as their perceptions of the programme. A total of 11 interviews with participants were performed, as well as 21 FGDs, 7 by intervention arm including 8–10 participants with a total of 186 participants. Additionally, 44 interviews were conducted with key informants from implementing organisations including ADECOS (n=31), VHIR staff (n=2), FAS coordination (n=2) and finance (n=3), government officials such as municipal and provincial supervisors (n=7). Data obtained from interviews was used to estimate averages and ranges of project-related implementation time use, including staff-reported proportions of time allocated to specific programme activities which were used in activity cost allocation for the activity-based costing methodology.

Data analysis

We analysed expenditure and monitoring data obtained from routine programme accounting and reporting systems and combined these with primary data collected through interviews for the economic analysis. All data collected were entered monthly into Microsoft Excel worksheets.

An activity-based costing method was applied to allocate and estimate financial costs per major programme activity determined within each of the three interventions, using accounting records and staff time allocation sheets. Data from administrative financial records was assigned cost category codes according to implementation phase (start-up, recurrent), input type (personnel, supplies, per diems, transportation and overhead) and activity type.

An ingredients approach to estimate costs was used where appropriate ledger data was unavailable or insufficiently detailed. Staff costs were allocated to intervention activities and components using data from staff time use interviews and programme documentation. A proportional allocation approach was used to distribute non-staff joint costs, such as transportation, fuel and overhead costs, across the intervention components. These shared costs were proportionally allocated to each intervention based on factors like the relative size of each programme and the level of staff effort needed for implementation. Microcosting data on beneficiaries’ travel time, transport costs and out-of-pocket expenditure, collected through FGDs and interviews, were summarised and analysed in Excel. Time allocated to research activities was estimated and excluded to ensure that the analysis exclusively reflected the time dedicated to implementing the interventions.

Fuel prices reflected actual payments from 2021 to 2024. Average round trips in kilometres were calculated using driving logs and routes. Car usage and fuel information was estimated and discussed with the project logistician.

Capital costs were annualised based on standard tables using an estimated useful life of 3 years for computers and 5 years for cars and other equipment, discounted at a rate of 3%.26 We included the contributions of international technical partners (ACF) and research institutions (ISCIII and UMN) related to the development and support of the interventions’ implementation, while excluding research-related costs.

We calculated total costs of the interventions. Costs incurred in Angola kwanzas or Euros were adjusted for inflation and converted to 2024 US dollars (USD) using monthly average exchange rates due to fluctuations.27

Cost analysis

This analysis follows the published economic evaluation protocol.18 It reports the costing and cost-efficiency components based on the first year of implementation; while the full economic evaluation including cost-effectiveness analysis will be conducted once effectiveness data become available.

Cost shares were broken down by intervention arm, input type, activity and stage (start-up and recurrent). Description of activities and inputs are included in online supplemental table 1. We selected an institutional perspective as the base case to capture the potential budgetary impact of adopting the intervention. The societal perspective presents the full costs of implementing the interventions, including those borne by participants (also called opportunity costs).

Household costs

Beneficiaries participated in SOC activities such as home-visits, community sensitisation sessions and health posts attendance for preventive pharmacological inputs. In addition, SOC+CT and SOC+NS beneficiaries attended quarterly distributions to receive the cash transfers and the FFR respectively. We divided household-costs into: (1) direct out-of-pocket costs related to transportation (round-trip travel to and from health post and distribution points) and (2) indirect opportunity-cost of time spent travelling and waiting at health post and distribution points. Descriptive analyses presented median transportation costs; median travel and waiting times to distribution points, community sensitisation sessions and health posts. We estimated the median daily wage in the community at 1500 AOA per day (US$1.91), assuming a 5-hour working day, as most livelihoods were agricultural.

Cost-efficiency analysis

Total costs were estimated for each of the three MuCCUA trial intervention arms: SOC, SOC+CT and SOC+NS. The cost-efficiency of the interventions was assessed by integrating activity-specific cost data with routine programme output data, enabling the calculation of cost per participant reached. Number of total participant women and their newborn children engaged in the intervention were recorded by programme staff and obtained from project records. Ratios of cost per mother–child pair reached were calculated by dividing the total estimated intervention cost from both an institutional and a societal perspective by the respective numbers of participants reached. Metrics included total cost per participant, monthly cost per participant (assuming a 32-month programme duration including start-up period) and per capita costs from a broader community perspective for the SOC intervention that is provided at community level. Total cost transfer ratios (TCTRs) were calculated for the CT component, which is the total cost to transfer one monetary unit to a beneficiary, including the value of the transfer itself, from a societal and institutional perspective.

Patient and public involvement

The MuCCUA trial was designed and implemented in close collaboration with government institutions, implementing and research partners and participants. Community leaders (‘sobas’) and members were engaged early in the research process through discussions on anticipated outcomes and proposed interventions. Sobas, communities and ADECOS participated in the cluster randomisation process. Recruitment methods were informed by discussions with supervisors and ADECOS. All women from selected randomised communities were identified and informed about the study by ADECOS. Findings will be shared with participants and communities through dedicated workshops at the study’s conclusion.

Results

During the first 12 months of the implementation period, the MuCCUA trial interventions reached a total of 1423 pregnant women and their newborns, with participants distributed across the study arms as follows: SOC (n=463), SOC+CT (n=479) and SOC+NS (n=481).

Institutional costs

In the observed scenario, the total institutional cost of the SOC, SOC+CT and SOC+NS intervention arms were estimated at US$190 408, US$414 946 and US$515 799, respectively. A summary of interventions costs is provided in table 1. Start-up costs represented a higher share of total costs in the SOC arm (13%), due to more staff time being spent preparing materials and selecting, hiring and training ADECOS and their supervisors, and a lower share in SOC+NS arm (6.7%) and SOC+CT arm (8.3%) due to substantial recurrent costs related to cash transfers and nutritional supplies distributions.

Institutional costs by input

When analysing costs by input type, personnel costs represented the largest share of total costs across all interventions (table 1 and figure 2). In the SOC arm, personnel accounted for 73% of total costs, driven by expenditures for national staff (51%) and international staff (22%). In contrast, the relative share of personnel costs decreased in the SOC+CT and SOC+NS arms to 53% and 52%, respectively. Notably, international staff costs were higher in SOC+CT and SOC+NS, reflecting that implementation of these interventions were allocated to international partners and the additional time they had to dedicate to procurement activities, particularly the acquisition of supplements and transfers contracts.

Distribution of inputs costs by intervention arm. SOC, standard of care; SOC+CT, standard of care plus cash transfer; SOC+NS, standard of care plus nutrition supplementation.

In the SOC+NS arm, supplies were the second-largest category, accounting for 37% of total costs, primarily driven by the procurement of SQ-LNS supplements, which accounted for 11% of total costs, and FFR which comprised 20%. In the SOC+CT arm, cash transfers delivered to beneficiaries accounted for 35% of total costs. In contrast, supplies accounted for a smaller share in the SOC arm, 17% of total costs, including expenses for training materials, refreshments and accommodation.

Other cost categories showed smaller contributions to the total costs. Per diems accounted for between 2% in SOC+CT and SOC+NS and 3% in SOC of costs across interventions. Transportation costs ranged from 4% in SOC to 7% and 6% in SOC+CT and SOC+NS, respectively, reflecting additional logistical demands of these interventions that had field distributions.

Institutional costs by activity

When costs were categorised by programmatic activity (table 1 and figure 3), procurement emerged as the single largest cost driver in both SOC+CT (32%) and SOC+NS (35%), primarily because this category includes the cost of all intervention-related supplies, such as nutritional products and transfer modalities. In the SOC+NS arm, SQ-LNS accounted for 30% of procurement-related costs, FFR for 58% and all other procurement-related costs, including staff time, for the remaining 12%. In the SOC+CT arm, the cash transfers represented 83% of total procurement-related costs. Planning and distributing these inputs took considerably more resources than the distribution of pharmacological supplies or health promotion activities in the SOC intervention, due to more staff time being required. In contrast, procurement represented only 6% of SOC’s total cost, as the standard intervention relied less on material inputs.

Distribution of activity costs by intervention arm. ADECOS, Community and Health Development Agents; SOC, standard of care; SOC+CT, standard of care plus cash transfer; SOC+NS, standard of care plus nutrition supplementation.

In all arms, training of ADECOS and home visits for household counselling remained prominent cost components. Training activities accounted for 24% of SOC costs, and while their relative share decreased in SOC+CT (12%) and SOC+NS (10%), the absolute investment increased due to the broader training scope.

Many activities common across the three interventions required similar levels of resources. Planning and microplanning ranged from 8% (SOC) to 9% (SOC+CT). Awareness-raising and sensitisation accounted for 11% in SOC while it was lower in both SOC+CT and SOC+NS.

Societal costs

The estimated total societal costs for participating households were US$9374 (SOC), US$18 231 (SOC+CT) and US$16 142 (SOC+NS), representing 4.7%, 4.2% and 3.0% of the total costs in each respective intervention arm (table 2). The total household cost per participating woman over the 12 month implementation period was US$20.2 in SOC arm, US$39.4 in SOC+CT arm and US$34.9 in SOC+NS arm—equivalent to approximately 10, 23 and 24 days of work, respectively. Women attending distribution points in SOC+CT and SOC+NS arms incurred higher costs, primarily due to high direct costs related to travel expenses compared with women in the SOC arm. However, indirect opportunity costs from time spent travelling, waiting and attending activities far exceeded direct costs, highlighting the importance of valuing participants’ time involvement.

Cost-efficiency

Cost-efficiency results are presented in table 2. From an institutional perspective, the cost per participant was lowest in the SOC arm, totalling US$411.25 per year of implementation, being the least expensive, most cost-efficient intervention. This reflects the relatively lean structure of the intervention, delivered through ADECOS without additional inputs. The SOC+CT arm showed an intermediate cost per participant of US$866.28. Although the SOC+NS arm demonstrated the highest cost per participant at US$1072.35, more than double that of SOC, it included not only SQ-LNS but an FFR. If only the SQ-LNS component is considered, the estimated cost would be substantially lower (approximately US$854), closer to that of the SOC+CT arm. When programme costs were annualised over the 32 month implementation period, the monthly programme cost per participant was estimated at US$12.85 in the SOC arm, US$27.07 in SOC+CT and US$33.51 in SOC+NS. These differences reflect the higher operational and procurement expenditures required to deliver cash transfers, FFR and SQ-LNS, respectively. TCTRs for the cash transfer component, which is the total cost to transfer one monetary unit to a beneficiary, was estimated at US$2.04 (online supplemental table 2). From a population-level perspective, the SOC intervention, which was designed and delivered to the whole community rather than only trial participants as with the SOC+CT and SOC+NS arms, had a monthly cost per person in the community of US$0.34 and a monthly cost per woman in the community of US$0.66. Total cost efficiency from a societal perspective, per mother–child pair participant was US$13.48 in SOC, US$28.26 in SOC+CT and US$34.56 in SOC+NS during the first year of implementation.

Discussion

This study adds to the body of economic evidence on multisectoral nutrition interventions by assessing the total cost of combining nutrition-specific and nutrition-sensitive interventions in rural Angola including a detailed economic analysis of the SOC. The SOC intervention was the least expensive and most cost-efficient per participant per year (US$431.49), because it did not include the additional components integrated into the SOC+CT and SOC+NS interventions. These enhanced interventions, while potentially offering added benefits, incurred higher per-participant costs of US$904.34 and US$1,105.91, respectively, due to the inclusion of supplementary inputs. The current analysis offers a comprehensive costing and cost-efficiency analysis that will complement the forthcoming cost-effectiveness analysis from the MuCCUA trial, as each of the three intervention arms offers a distinct breadth and range of benefits to participants. Broader literature on complex public health interventions emphasises the importance of balancing added implementation costs and complexity with the potential for greater impact and long-term benefits when analysing costs in such trials.28 29 This evidence is crucial for programme managers, implementers, national governments and international organisations to guide budgeting, resource allocation, choice of delivery modalities and operational planning, particularly in rural and remote settings such as Angola where logistical constraints can significantly affect programme resource use and performance. The SOC+CT arm demonstrated slightly greater cost-efficiency than the SOC+NS, suggesting cash transfers may offer a more efficient approach from an administrative perspective than delivering SQ-LNS plus an FFR in rural remote areas. However, this difference is driven by intervention design; when only the SQ-LNS component is considered, without the FFR, the estimated cost-efficiency is slightly higher than that of the cash transfer arm.

The start-up costs reflect the absence of pre-existing infrastructure in Angola to support implementation by international partners, requiring considerable time to set up offices and operational systems, especially as international staff arrived during the post-COVID-19 period, which slowed all processes. Nonetheless, with longer implementation periods, start-up costs are diluted across total programme costs.30 Our results show that start-up costs represented a higher share of total costs in the SOC arm compared with the other two arms, due to considerable staff time spent preparing materials, selecting eligible communities and beneficiaries, and hiring and training ADECOS and their supervisors. In contrast, the SOC+CT and SOC+NS arms include substantial recurrent costs related to cash transfers and nutritional supplies, which reduce the relative contribution of start-up costs.

Personnel consistently remained the primary cost driver across all MuCCUA interventions, accounting for slightly more than one-half of total costs, though their proportional share declines in interventions with added components. Other costing studies of multisectoral nutrition interventions also found personnel to be a main cost driver, accounting for 68% of total costs in Bangladesh,31 31% in Niger32 and 60% in India.33 Regarding supplies, our results show that adding SQ-LNS and FFR or cash transfers to SOC substantially increases total and recurrent costs, mainly due to the ongoing delivery of supplies, their prices and associated logistics. These interventions shift cost distributions, with greater shares allocated to supplies and transportation. In the MuCCUA trial, procurement costs made up 38% of total SOC+NS costs and 35% of SOC+CT costs. This cost composition is in line with the PROCOMIDA programme in Guatemala and Tubaramure in Burundi where distributing the food rations and supplements was the most expensive programme activity, accounting for 19% and 30% of total costs, respectively.30 In addition, interventions that include nutrient supplements like SQ-LNS often face challenges related to supply chain disruption, which happened in the present study and was also reported in studies in Bangladesh,34 Honduras and Somalia35 that also reached rural remote areas. In our programme, these challenges required staff to spend more time managing logistics and delivery in the SOC+NS arm.

Overall, few costing studies assess stunting-reduction packages that include cash transfers and nutritional supplements with health and nutrition promotion activities; and wide variation in transfer amounts, supplement dosages and the content and delivery of behaviour change communication further constrains comparability across studies. A similar study in Pakistan36 compared medium-quantity lipid-based nutrient supplement, social and behaviour change communication (SBBC) and existing unconditional cash transfers programme for children 6–23 months of age. They found that the major cost driver for SBCC was training costs, accounting for 34% of the total cost, comparable to our SOC intervention, where ADECOS training represented 24% of total costs.

Regarding cash transfer interventions, the REFANI study in Pakistan assessed the cost-efficiency of three cash transfer modalities for nutrition outcomes from a societal perspective, reporting total cost per beneficiary ranging from US$193 to US$284 over 6 months.37 In contrast, our study estimated a substantially higher cost for the SOC+CT intervention (US$904 per participant for a 12-month period) as compared with REFANI, reflecting the longer intervention duration and the inclusion of the SOC component costs. However, when we calculated the incremental cost of the CT component alone, the estimate was US$455 per participant, aligning with REFANI’s costs if the project lasted 12 months.

In relation to the nutrition supplementation arm, the SOC+NS was the most expensive of the three interventions, costing US$1072 per mother–child pair over 1 year, primarily driven by the higher cost of provision of SQ-LNS and FFR. Similar results were found in the PROCOMIDA programme where the total combined cost of the full FFR and individual LNS was US$1090 per beneficiary during the first 1000 days. Although this intervention had a longer duration, delivering programmes for longer periods of time can reduce cost per beneficiary.30 Another study in Chad found that adding a ready-to-use lipid-based supplement (RUSF) to a 5-month general food distribution increased programme costs by 23%, or €374 (US$426) per child, bringing the total to €1102 (US$1255) per child.38 The current global debate, led by the World Food Programme and the United Nations, centres on whether traditional in-kind food rations remain the most appropriate delivery modality in humanitarian settings, or whether cash or vouchers should be increasingly used, with the recognition that the choice must depend on contextual factors.39 We examined the potential cost implications of removing the FFR from the SOC+NS intervention and estimated that the total cost would decrease to US$854. Even though this could be a source of cost savings, studies have shown that adherence to SQ-LNS and resulting effectiveness of the intervention increases when complemented by FFR, especially in food-insecure settings.40 Despite the management and logistical challenges associated with food-based programmes, these inputs continue to play an important role in maintaining food security, protecting livelihoods and supporting nutritional status of vulnerable populations especially in contexts of food insecurity.37 We also estimated the incremental cost of adding the NS component to the SOC intervention at US$661 per participant. However, this unit cost would still be higher than other consulted studies that do not include the FFR. A study in Burkina Faso found that RUSF without any additional intervention component had a cost of US$245 per child over 18 months41 and another study in Niger found an average cost per mother–child pair of US$257 per year of adding SQ-LNS to the national 1000 Days Programme.32

Household costs increased with intervention complexity. Many SOC+NS and SOC+CT beneficiaries had to travel long distances to collect supplies, often on foot, which for some required nearly an entire day. Centralising cash and FFR distributions for groups of villages lowered operational costs for implementing organisations, but shifted costs onto participants ultimately reducing the real value of the cash or foods received by beneficiaries. Similarly, the SNACK study in Mali and the REFANI in Pakistan found that implementation constraints due to remoteness and inaccessibility may have undermined the value of the cash delivered,37 42 and recommended adding a transportation allowance to cash transfers for recipients who must travel outside their village to collect them, to ensure they receive the full intended benefit.37 Additionally, the intensive nature of SOC activities, like weekly home visits and bimonthly community sessions, substantially increased women’s time commitments. Studies in Bangladesh31 and India33 found that the opportunity costs of programmes including behaviour change activities are high, particularly due to the significant time burden placed on participating women. This highlights the importance of incorporating a societal perspective in economic evaluations to ensure future programme sustainability.

Cost-efficiency estimates reflect programme intensity and help contextualise investment requirements for potential scale-up, which is particularly relevant given the Angolan’s government’s interest in the cost of implementing the SOC intervention in rural areas and the incremental resources needed for enhanced nutrition support. In the SOC arm, the monthly cost per person in the community was US$0.34 and the monthly cost per woman was US$0.66. These per capita estimates highlight the resources needed to scale community-based platforms to entire rural populations as recommended in other studies in low-resource settings.31 Recent studies in these types of contexts have found that integrating community health workers in maternal and child health and nutrition interventions was cost-saving and improved facility-based care-seeking and nutrition outcomes in mothers and children.43 Although additional training and supervision increase costs, as was also seen in Mali, scaling-up community health worker coverage in rural areas with limited healthcare access remains essential for improving maternal and child nutrition, especially through home visits.44

Strengths and limitations

The study findings have limitations to consider. Concerning SQ-LNS provision, global supply chain problems created difficulties in the implementation of the intervention, increasing costs and staff time dedication. Additionally, sensitivity around salary disclosures from government and non-project-funded staff complicated data collection. We relied on salary estimates from interviews and cross-checked information with other stakeholders. However, early engagement with project staff and key stakeholders provided valuable insights into expenditure rationales. Cost data were collected prospectively from the start of programme implementation, and all programme staff were interviewed during the real-time implementation period enabling a more precise analysis. Furthermore, analysing costs from both institutional and societal perspectives captures beneficiary-incurred costs, providing a more complete understanding of economic impacts that could affect intervention success. Finally, this is one of the few studies that simultaneously evaluates implementation costs of nutrition-specific and nutrition-sensitive interventions within the same rural Angolan population. Moreover, one particular strength of this study is that the same consortium conducted the interventions implementation, the economic evaluation and research activities, thus allowing comprehensive access to all internal cost data.

Conclusion

This study provides a detailed economic evaluation of a SOC programme and two enhanced nutrition-sensitive and nutrition-specific interventions in Angola, highlighting the cost structures and key cost drivers of each implementation model. The SOC intervention was the least expensive, as it did not include the additional components of the cash transfer and nutrition interventions that offer potentially added benefits to participants.

The SOC+CT and SOC+NS intervention arms incurred higher costs due to the inclusion of additional inputs and recurrent distributions, particularly the distribution of cash transfers and nutritional supplies. In the SOC+NS arm, costs were especially driven by the combined provision SQ-LNS and an FFR. While the SOC+CT arm showed slightly greater cost-efficiency per mother–child pair, this reflects differences in intervention design, as the SOC+NS intervention costs were highly impacted by the FFR costs which are included in SQ-LNS supplementary interventions in many cases. However, cost-efficiency alone does not capture the value for money of each model, which must be considered in relation to health and nutrition outcomes. Disaggregated costing provides valuable insight into how resources are allocated across programme components, supporting operational planning, budgeting and the design of more efficient delivery models in similar settings.

This analysis will inform forthcoming cost-effectiveness analyses once the MuCCUA trial is finished that will assess the comparative impacts of these interventions on child stunting with the costs to generate comprehensive evidence on its cost-effectiveness and fully inform programmatic decision-making.
