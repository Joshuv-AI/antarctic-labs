Enhanced cytotoxic activity and pH-responsive drug delivery of quinalizarin-conjugated gold nanoparticles in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Yasir A. Taha, Ammar I. Ibrahim, Namarig A. Mohammed, Jamila M. Mahgoub, Hany M. El-Wahsh, Sabry A. El-Naggar, Kadry M. Sadek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Casein kinase 2 (CK2) is a key regulator of cancer cell survival and proliferation, making it an attractive therapeutic target. Quinalizarin is a potent CK2 inhibitor; however, its clinical application is limited by suboptimal delivery and bioavailability. This study aimed to develop and characterize a quinalizarin–gold nanoparticle nanocomplex (QGNPs) and evaluate its potential to enhance anticancer activity in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). QGNPs were synthesized using sulfhydryl calcium acetate as a linking agent. The nanocomplex was characterized by UV–Vis spectroscopy, dynamic light scattering, zeta potential analysis, and transmission electron microscopy. Drug loading and release profiles were assessed using UV–Vis spectroscopy. In vitro cytotoxicity was evaluated in A549 lung cancer cells using MTT assay, and cellular uptake was examined by confocal laser scanning microscopy. QGNPs demonstrated successful conjugation, with an increase in particle size from ~ 15 to 85.18 ± 8.25 nm and a zeta potential shift to − 19.59 ± 5.62 mV. The nanocomplex exhibited pH-responsive drug release, with significantly higher quinalizarin release under acidic conditions (pH 5.0) compared to physiological pH (7.4). In vitro studies showed that QGNPs significantly enhanced cytotoxic activity compared to free quinalizarin, with lower IC₅₀ values. Confocal microscopy confirmed efficient intracellular uptake of QGNPs in A549 cells. QGNPs represent a promising nanocarrier system for improving the delivery and anticancer efficacy of quinalizarin in NSCLC. The enhanced cytotoxicity and pH-responsive release suggest potential for targeted cancer therapy. However, further studies are required to elucidate underlying mechanisms and to evaluate radiosensitization and in vivo therapeutic efficacy

Introduction

Casein kinase 2 (CK2) is a constitutively active serine/threonine protein kinase located in both the cytoplasm and nucleus of cells, with the ability to phosphorylate hundreds of substrates1. The heterotetrameric configurations (α₂β₂, αα′β₂, and α′₂β₂) consist of two regulatory subunits (28 kDa β) and two catalytic subunits (42 kDa α and 38 kDa α′)2. CK2 plays critical roles in both normal cellular processes and pathological conditions, including cell proliferation and apoptosis3. Previous studies have demonstrated that elevated CK2 activity is associated with tumor progression and survival4. Furthermore, CK2 exerts anti-apoptotic effects through phosphorylation of multiple downstream targets, and its inhibition has been shown to suppress tumor growth and promote apoptosis5, highlighting its importance as a therapeutic target in cancer treatment.

Several CK2 inhibitors have been developed, most of which are classified as ATP-competitive (class I) kinase inhibitors targeting the ATP-binding site. These include pyrazolotriazines, anthraquinones, flavonoids, coumarins, and benzimidazoles1. Quinalizarin is a potent, selective, and cell-permeable CK2 inhibitor, with an IC₅₀ of 110 nM and a Ki of approximately 50 nM. It has been shown to induce apoptosis in various cancer cell types6. In addition, previous studies have suggested that quinalizarin may enhance the sensitivity of certain non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells to radiation, potentially through suppression of STAT3 signaling pathways7,8. Quinalizarin has also been reported to modulate SHP-1 expression, which may contribute to altered cellular responses in cancer models9, further supporting its potential as a multifunctional anticancer agent and therapeutic candidate.

Nanoparticles (NPs) have attracted significant attention in cancer therapy due to their unique physicochemical properties and ability to enhance drug delivery, including improved bioavailability, targeted delivery, and controlled release profiles. Their small size influences cellular uptake, intracellular trafficking, and biological interactions10, facilitating efficient penetration into tumor tissues and cellular compartments. Exposure to nanoparticles can affect multiple cellular processes, including oxidative stress, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, DNA damage, cell cycle dysregulation, and apoptosis11–14. Among these, gold nanoparticles (GNPs) are particularly promising due to their biocompatibility, ease of functionalization, and favorable physicochemical characteristics15, making them suitable platforms for anticancer drug delivery and nanomedicine applications.

The use of GNPs as drug delivery systems offers a potential strategy to improve the intracellular delivery and therapeutic efficacy of anticancer agents such as CK2 inhibitors16,17. While GNPs have been extensively investigated in oncology, their conjugation with quinalizarin remains limited in the current literature. Therefore, this study aims to develop and characterize a novel quinalizarin–gold nanoparticle nanocomplex (QGNPs) and evaluate its potential to enhance anticancer activity in vitro.

Although both quinalizarin and GNPs have been individually associated with radiosensitizing properties, the present study focuses primarily on their combined cytotoxic and drug delivery effects, as no direct irradiation experiments were performed, and therefore no definitive conclusions regarding radiosensitization can be drawn from the current findings.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Tetrachloroaurate (HAuCl₄·3H₂O) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemie GmbH (Germany). Quinalizarin was obtained from Merck GmbH (Germany). Calcium thioglycolate trihydrate and sodium citrate were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Dimethylthiazolyl-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) was purchased from Promega (Germany). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was obtained from PAA Laboratories (Germany), and Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) was supplied by Biochrom GmbH (Germany). All other chemicals and solvents were of analytical grade and used without further purification.

Instrumentation

Ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectra were recorded using a spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, Netherlands). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements were performed using a Malvern instrument (UK). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis was carried out using a JEOL microscope (Germany). Carbon-coated copper grids were obtained from Plano GmbH (Germany). Cell viability was assessed using an Infinite M200 plate reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Switzerland). Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was performed using an LSM510 microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany). Standard 96-well plates (Opaque GmbH & Co. KG, Germany) were used. A pre-swelled dialysis membrane (molecular weight cut-off 3.5 kDa) was obtained from Fisher Scientific Inc. (USA).

Nanoparticles preparation

Preparation of gold nanoparticles (GNPs)

Gold nanoparticles were synthesized using the Turkevich method, which produces monodisperse spherical particles with sizes typically ranging from 13 to 20 nm. Trisodium citrate dihydrate was used as both a reducing agent and stabilizer in this redox reaction. Briefly, 10 mg of tetrachloroaurate (HAuCl₄·3H₂O) was dissolved in 50 mL of ultrapure deionized water and heated to boiling under continuous stirring. Subsequently, 1 mL of an aqueous solution of sodium citrate (37.34 mg/mL) was rapidly added to the boiling solution, leading to the reduction of Au3⁺ to Au⁰. The reaction mixture was maintained under boiling conditions for 10 min with constant stirring. The formation of gold nanoparticles was confirmed by a characteristic color change from pale yellow to colorless, followed by pink and finally deep red. The resulting colloidal gold solution, containing nearly spherical nanoparticles with an average diameter of approximately 15 nm, was cooled to room temperature and stored at 4 °C for further use.

The concentration of synthesized gold nanoparticles was estimated based on the initial gold precursor concentration; however, precise quantification of gold content was not performed in this study.

Preparation of quinalizarin–gold nanocomplex (QGNPs)

Sulfhydryl calcium acetate was used as a linking bridge to conjugate quinalizarin with gold nanoparticles (GNPs). Briefly, 55.55 mg of quinalizarin was dissolved in 10 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a stock solution with a concentration of 20,000 μM.

Subsequently, 320 μL of the quinalizarin stock solution was added dropwise to 21.109 mL of sulfhydryl calcium acetate solution (567 μM) under continuous stirring at 400 rpm, resulting in the formation of a dark blue quinalizarin–sulfhydryl calcium acetate complex.

Thereafter, 6.2 mL of the previously prepared colloidal GNPs (100 μg/mL) was added to the above mixture. The color of the solution changed from dark blue to orange-red, indicating successful formation of the quinalizarin–gold nanocomplex (QGNPs).

The resulting mixture was centrifuged at 11,572 × g for 10 min to remove unbound free GNPs. The obtained pellet was resuspended in Milli-Q water to a final volume of 32 mL.

UV/Vis spectroscopy quantification of quinalizarin loaded in QGNPs

According to the Beer–Lambert law, a linear relationship exists between the absorbance at 289 nm (λmax of quinalizarin) and its concentration in solution. Based on this principle, the amount of quinalizarin loaded onto QGNPs was estimated using UV/Vis spectroscopy.

A series of standard quinalizarin solutions in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were prepared at different concentrations (200 μM, 150 μM, 100 μM, 50 μM, 25 μM, and 12.5 μM). The absorbance of each solution at 289 nm was measured to construct a calibration curve by plotting absorbance against concentration.

In addition, a control set of quinalizarin solutions with the same nominal concentrations was prepared in water under continuous stirring, taking into account the limited solubility of quinalizarin in aqueous media.

The concentration of quinalizarin in the QGNPs formulation was estimated by comparing the measured absorbance values with the standard calibration curve.

It should be noted that this method provides an indirect estimation of drug loading, and potential interference from nanoparticle scattering or incomplete solubilization of quinalizarin may affect the accuracy of the measurements.

Measurement of particle size and zeta potential

The mean particle size (hydrodynamic diameter, nm) and zeta potential (mV) of the nanoparticles were measured using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, UK). Particle size was determined based on dynamic light scattering (DLS), which analyzes fluctuations in scattered light intensity resulting from the Brownian motion of particles in a diluted suspension. Zeta potential measurements were performed using laser Doppler velocimetry.

All samples were appropriately diluted with deionized water prior to analysis to avoid multiple scattering effects. Measurements were conducted at 25 °C, and each sample was analyzed in triplicate using the Smoluchowski model.

The reported values represent the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent measurements.

Particle morphology characterization

The morphology of the nanoparticles was examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). TEM analysis was performed using a JEOL JEM-2010 instrument (JEOL GmbH, Eching, Germany) operated at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV.

For sample preparation, 12 μL of a purified nanoparticle suspension was deposited onto 400-mesh carbon-coated copper grids (S160-4, Plano GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). The samples were allowed to dry at room temperature until complete evaporation of the solvent prior to imaging.

Determination of drug complexation efficiency

The drug loading capacity of the nanoparticles was evaluated in terms of complexation efficiency (CE, %) and drug loading (DL, %). CE (%) represents the percentage of drug successfully associated with the nanoparticles relative to the total amount of drug used, while DL (%) represents the proportion of drug relative to the total nanoparticle mass.

After preparation of QGNPs, unbound quinalizarin and free GNPs were separated by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C using an Eppendorf Centrifuge 5804 R. The supernatant was carefully removed, and the resulting pellet was washed and re-dispersed in Milli-Q water. This washing process was repeated twice to ensure removal of unbound drug and excess nanoparticles.

The final pellet was re-dispersed in Milli-Q water and transferred to a 1 cm quartz cuvette for UV/Vis spectroscopic analysis. The absorbance was measured at 289 nm to determine the amount of quinalizarin associated with the nanoparticles.

The quantification of quinalizarin was based on UV/Vis absorbance measurements, and no independent analytical technique (e.g., HPLC) was used to validate drug content, which may affect the accuracy of the calculated values.

In vitro drug release study

To simulate physiological and intracellular conditions, the release profile of quinalizarin from QGNPs was evaluated at two different pH values (7.4 and 5.0).

Briefly, 20 mL of the prepared QGNPs suspension was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was re-dispersed in RPMI high-glucose medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) without phenol red to a final volume of 10 mL.

The suspension was then transferred into a pre-swelled dialysis bag (molecular weight cut-off 3.5 kDa) and immersed in 90 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 0.01 mol/L, pH 7.4) at 37 °C under continuous shaking.

At predetermined time intervals (0, 2, 4, 8, 24, 48, and 72 h), 1 mL aliquots of the release medium were collected and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS to maintain sink conditions. The amount of released quinalizarin was determined by measuring the absorbance at 289 nm using UV/Vis spectroscopy.

The release study under acidic conditions (pH 5.0) was performed using the same protocol, to more closely mimic the tumor microenvironment and intracellular endosomal/lysosomal compartments where enhanced drug release is expected.

The cumulative drug release percentage was calculated based on the standard calibration curve of quinalizarin, allowing quantitative assessment of release kinetics over time. However, the release mechanism was not mathematically modeled, and potential interactions between nanoparticles and serum components may influence the release behavior, which represents a limitation and suggests the need for further kinetic modeling (e.g., Higuchi or Korsmeyer–Peppas models) and controlled release studies in future investigations.

Study of cellular uptake

The cellular uptake of QGNPs was investigated using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM; Zeiss LSM510 META system, Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany), to visualize intracellular localization and distribution of the nanocomplex. A549 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 3.0 × 105 cells per well and allowed to attach for 48 h.

Following incubation, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing QGNPs at different concentrations (12.5 μM, 25 μM, and 50 μM). Cells were incubated for 72 h at 37 °C under standard culture conditions, to ensure sufficient exposure time for nanoparticle internalization.

After incubation, cellular uptake was analyzed using CLSM with multiphoton excitation at 780 nm. Images were acquired using a 63 × /1.2 NA water immersion objective lens at a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels. Z-stack images were obtained at intervals of 1.0 μm to evaluate nanoparticle distribution within the cells, allowing three-dimensional assessment of intracellular localization.

Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software. A threshold value of 25 was applied to eliminate background noise, allowing selective visualization of QGNPs, thereby improving signal specificity. The “Analyze Particles” function was used to quantify the number and total area of detected nanoparticle signals within the image stacks, providing semi-quantitative evaluation of cellular uptake efficiency.

No human or animal subjects were involved in this study; therefore, ethical approval and informed consent were not required.

Assay for cell viability

The in vitro cytotoxic activity of QGNPs and free quinalizarin was evaluated using the standard MTT assay in A549 human lung cancer cells. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and allowed to attach for 24 h prior to treatment.

Cells were then treated with a range of concentrations of QGNPs and free quinalizarin prepared in complete culture medium. The concentrations were selected based on preliminary dose–response experiments to cover a wide range of cytotoxic effects. The amount of quinalizarin in the QGNPs formulation was adjusted to be equivalent to that of the free drug to allow direct comparison of cytotoxicity.

Control groups included untreated cells (negative control), cells treated with 1% Triton X-100 (positive control), and cells treated with GNPs alone to evaluate the effect of the nanoparticle carrier.

After 72 h of incubation at 37 °C, 20 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well, followed by incubation for an additional 4 h. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved by adding 100 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to each well.

The absorbance was measured at 570 nm using an Infinite M200 plate reader. Dose–response curves were generated, and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) values were calculated using nonlinear regression analysis.

It should be noted that the cytotoxicity evaluation was performed using a single cell line (A549), which may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, no mechanistic assays (e.g., ROS generation, apoptosis markers, or DNA damage analysis) were conducted to further elucidate the underlying mechanisms of cytotoxicity.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Multiple comparisons between treatment groups and the control group were conducted using Dunnett’s test. A p value of less than 0.05 or 0.01 was considered statistically significant. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments.

Results

UV/Vis characterization of QGNPs

The formation and characterization of QGNPs were confirmed using UV/Vis spectroscopy. A linear correlation between the absorbance at 289 nm and quinalizarin concentration was established according to the Beer–Lambert law, demonstrating reliable quantitative analysis of the drug. The calibration curve was generated using quinalizarin solutions at concentrations ranging from 12.5 μM to 200 μM in DMSO, yielding a high correlation coefficient (R2 > 0.99), indicating excellent linearity and analytical accuracy.

The loading of quinalizarin onto GNPs was evaluated by comparing the UV/Vis spectra of different components, including calcium thioglycolate trihydrate (a), quinalizarin (b), GNPs (c), quinalizarin + calcium thioglycolate (d), and QGNPs (e). Except for solutions (a) and (c), all other samples (b, d, and e) exhibited a characteristic absorption peak at 289 nm, confirming the presence of quinalizarin, and supporting successful incorporation within the nanocomplex.

Additionally, a shift in the secondary absorption peaks from 447 to 556 nm was observed in the QGNPs spectrum, indicating surface plasmon resonance modification due to drug–nanoparticle interaction, suggesting successful interaction between quinalizarin and the gold nanoparticle surface (Figs. 1 and 2). Morphology of nanoparticles

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed that GNPs appeared as well-dispersed spherical particles with an average size of approximately 15 nm. In contrast, QGNPs exhibited a larger, aggregated polyelectrolyte-like structure with multiple GNPs embedded within the matrix. This structural organization supports the formation of a nanocomplex rather than simple surface adsorption.

The release profile was consistent with a diffusion-controlled mechanism and showed agreement with the Higuchi model, supporting controlled drug diffusion from the nanoparticle matrix. The enhanced release at acidic pH suggests potential relevance to tumor microenvironment or intracellular lysosomal conditions; however, further mechanistic modeling is required to fully characterize the release kinetics, including validation using additional kinetic models (e.g., Korsmeyer–Peppas) and extended time-point analysis.

At the lowest concentration (12.5 μM), a more pronounced difference in cytotoxicity between QGNPs and free quinalizarin was observed. At higher concentrations (25 μM and 50 μM), QGNPs continued to show greater cytotoxicity than free quinalizarin; however, the magnitude of this difference was reduced. To further investigate the cellular internalization of QGNPs, a cellular uptake study was performed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). A549 cells were incubated with different concentrations of QGNPs for 72 h and subsequently imaged.

Bright signals corresponding to QGNPs were observed throughout multiple layers of the 3D Z-stack images, indicating intracellular localization rather than surface adsorption. The intensity and number of detected signals increased with increasing nanoparticle concentration, suggesting concentration-dependent cellular uptake.

Although these observations support effective internalization of QGNPs, the analysis remains qualitative, and no direct quantitative validation method (e.g., flow cytometry or elemental analysis) was used to confirm intracellular nanoparticle concentration.

Discussion

Several therapeutic modalities are currently used in cancer management, including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, and gene therapy18. Considerable efforts have been directed toward improving treatment efficacy, particularly through enhancing drug delivery and cellular targeting. Nanoparticles, especially gold nanoparticles (GNPs), have attracted increasing attention due to their unique physicochemical properties and their potential to improve therapeutic outcomes, particularly through enhanced cellular uptake, controlled drug release, and improved bioavailability.

Previous studies have suggested that both GNPs and CK2 inhibitors may influence cancer cell response to treatment, including potential modulation of radiation sensitivity7,8,19. However, in the present study, no irradiation experiments were performed; therefore, the findings should be interpreted in the context of enhanced cytotoxicity and drug delivery rather than confirmed radiosensitization, clearly distinguishing the scope of this work from radiation-based therapeutic investigations.

In this study, a quinalizarin–gold nanoparticle complex (QGNPs) was successfully synthesized using sulfhydryl calcium acetate as a linking agent. The presence of sulfhydryl groups facilitated the interaction between quinalizarin and the gold nanoparticle surface through coordination bonding, resulting in stable nanocomplex formation. The physicochemical characterization demonstrated clear differences between GNPs and QGNPs, confirming successful nanocomplex formation, as evidenced by changes in size, surface charge, and optical properties.

Transmission electron microscopy revealed that GNPs were spherical with an average size of approximately 15 nm, whereas QGNPs formed a larger polyelectrolyte-like structure with embedded nanoparticles, indicating successful structural integration of the drug within the nanoparticle system. Dynamic light scattering analysis showed a significant increase in particle size following conjugation, along with an increase in the polydispersity index (PDI), suggesting increased structural complexity and heterogeneity after drug loading.

This increase in size and heterogeneity may be attributed to aggregation and complex formation during the conjugation process, which could influence cellular uptake and biological behavior.

The stability of nanoparticles in suspension and their initial interaction with the cell membrane are influenced by the zeta potential, which is largely determined by surface charge, governing colloidal stability and nanoparticle–cell membrane interactions. Particle size also plays a critical role in cellular uptake, as it affects the rate of endocytosis following membrane adsorption, with nanoscale dimensions favoring efficient internalization.

The observed zeta potential values indicate moderate colloidal stability of both GNPs and QGNPs. The increased negative surface charge of QGNPs may be attributed to the presence of calcium thioglycolate, which contributes to surface functionalization, enhancing nanoparticle dispersion and interaction with the surrounding medium. However, the relatively moderate magnitude of zeta potential suggests that electrostatic stabilization alone may not be sufficient to prevent aggregation under all conditions, highlighting the potential need for additional steric stabilization strategies.

UV/Vis spectroscopy demonstrated a characteristic absorption peak at 289 nm in quinalizarin-containing samples, confirming the presence of the drug within the nanocomplex, and supporting successful drug incorporation. In addition, the shift in secondary peaks from 447 to 556 nm suggests interaction between quinalizarin and the gold nanoparticle surface, likely reflecting changes in surface plasmon resonance properties. However, these spectral findings should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive evidence of binding, as UV/Vis analysis alone cannot fully confirm structural conjugation, necessitating further validation using complementary techniques such as FT-IR, XPS, or NMR.

The drug release profile revealed a sustained release at physiological pH (7.4) and an enhanced release under acidic conditions (pH 5.0). The initial burst release observed during the first two hours at pH 5.0 may be attributed to surface-associated drug molecules, followed by a more controlled release phase. This pH-dependent behavior suggests potential relevance to the tumor microenvironment and intracellular compartments such as lysosomes.

Similar pH-responsive gold nanoparticle systems have been reported in the literature. For example, Kumar et al.20 developed doxorubicin-loaded, peptide-functionalized gold nanoparticles that demonstrated enhanced cytotoxicity compared with the free drug in multiple cancer cell lines. These findings support the concept that nanoparticle-based delivery systems can improve drug efficacy; however, differences in drug type, nanoparticle design, and experimental conditions should be considered when making comparisons.

The data demonstrated that QGNPs exhibited enhanced cytotoxic activity compared with free quinalizarin across the tested concentration range, indicating improved therapeutic efficacy likely mediated by nanoparticle-assisted delivery. However, unlike quinalizarin, the cytotoxic effect of QGNPs did not show a clear dose-dependent pattern, as no proportional increase in cytotoxicity was observed with increasing concentration. The more pronounced difference at lower concentrations, compared with higher doses, may be attributed to saturation of cellular uptake pathways or limited intracellular processing capacity of nanoparticles, suggesting a threshold effect in nanoparticle-mediated drug delivery.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy further demonstrated the internalization of QGNPs in A549 lung cancer cells. Bright intracellular signals were observed across multiple layers of the 3D Z-stack images, indicating that the nanoparticles were internalized rather than merely adsorbed onto the cell membrane, confirming effective cellular penetration of the nanocomplex. The intensity and distribution of these signals increased with increasing nanoparticle concentration, suggesting concentration-dependent uptake, supporting enhanced intracellular accumulation at higher exposure levels.

However, the uptake analysis was based on qualitative imaging, and no direct quantitative techniques were employed to measure intracellular nanoparticle concentration, which limits precise correlation between uptake efficiency and biological effect. Therefore, while a relationship between cellular uptake and cytotoxicity is suggested, it cannot be conclusively established in the present study, and should be validated using quantitative methods such as ICP-MS or flow cytometry.

Overall, the findings of this study indicate that QGNPs may enhance the cytotoxic activity of quinalizarin in A549 lung cancer cells, potentially through improved cellular delivery and intracellular accumulation. However, the absence of mechanistic studies (e.g., ROS generation, apoptosis signaling, or CK2 pathway analysis) limits the ability to fully explain the observed effects, highlighting the need for further molecular investigations to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of action.

Importantly, although both gold nanoparticles and quinalizarin have been previously associated with radiosensitizing properties, the current study did not include irradiation experiments, and therefore does not provide direct evidence of radiosensitization. Therefore, any implication of radiosensitization remains speculative and requires further validation through dedicated radiation-based assays and in vivo studies, including clonogenic survival assays and tumor response models to confirm potential synergistic effects with radiotherapy.

Conclusion

QGNPs were successfully developed as a novel nanocarrier system with confirmed conjugation, pH-responsive release, and enhanced cytotoxicity compared to free quinalizarin. The results suggest improved drug delivery and cellular uptake. However, low drug loading, lack of mechanistic studies, and absence of irradiation experiments remain key limitations. Further studies are required to validate their full therapeutic and radiosensitization potential.
