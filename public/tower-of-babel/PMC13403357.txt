Differential linguistic patterns across Rorschach, TAT, and SCT in individuals with PTSD
Authors: Hongsin Ahn, Ji Sun Hong, So Hee Lee, Doug Hyun Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: While prior studies have examined linguistic markers of PTSD using everyday discourse or autobiographical narratives, comparative studies identifying specific markers across different projective personality tests remain limited.Objective: To investigate whether language features in projective personality tests differentiate individuals with PTSD from non-PTSD controls and assess their predictive value for symptom severity.Method: Verbatim transcripts from 283 participants (183 PTSD, 100 controls) across the Rorschach, TAT, and SCT were analysed using NLP across six categories: negative emotion, positive emotion, insight, causation, body-related, and self-focus. ANCOVAs with Bonferroni correction compared linguistic indicators between groups controlling for age and sex. Hierarchical regression analyses assessed predictive validity for symptom severity.Results: Following Bonferroni correction (α = .0028), four indicators showed significant group differences (ηp² = .036–.092): Rorschach Negative Emotion, SCT Positive Emotion, SCT Insight, and TAT Negative Emotion. In the PTSD group, TAT Negative Emotion was the strongest cross-symptom predictor of PTSD, depressive, and anxiety severity. Reduced Rorschach Body-related language and elevated SCT Insight were associated with greater symptom burden. TAT Positive Emotion negatively predicted symptom severity. In the non-PTSD group, SCT Self-Focus was the strongest predictor of PTSD-related severity, and SCT Positive Emotion negatively predicted anxiety. A sensitivity analysis revealed that SCT Positive Emotion and TAT Negative Emotion did not replicate under a more conservative Core Lexicon and should be treated as exploratory; only Rorschach Negative Emotion and SCT Insight were robust across lexicon configurations.Conclusions: Individuals meeting PTSD criteria showed reduced somatic language and elevated negative emotional expression, while self-referential processing and positive emotional expression emerged as salient markers in non-clinical individuals, though these differences should be interpreted cautiously given substantial demographic imbalances. These preliminary findings suggest that projective linguistic analysis may have potential as a supplementary approach in trauma-related research, pending external validation and replication in independent datasets.KEYWORDS: Post-Traumatic stress disorder, projective personality tests, natural language processing, linguistic markers, language features

Introduction

Trauma is a common human experience, with most individuals encountering one or more traumatic events during their lifetime (Bonanno, 2004; Fink & Galea, 2015; Peters et al., 2021). Such exposure increases the risk of acute mental disorders and can have long-lasting effects on mental health (Fink & Galea, 2015). Evidence further shows that trauma exposure contributes to a wide range of psychological issues (Goldsmith et al., 2013), even in the absence of a formal post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) diagnosis (Creamer et al., 2005). Some individuals experience symptoms such as flashbacks, intrusive thoughts, or sleep problems without meeting the full diagnostic criteria for PTSD (Luoni et al., 2018). In addition to PTSD-related symptoms, trauma may be associated with somatic complaints, mood changes, cognitive impairments, interpersonal difficulties, and dissociative symptoms (Auxéméry, 2018). Individual trauma-related effects and variability in distress are more informative predictors of psychological functioning than the presence or absence of a formal PTSD diagnosis (Brewin et al., 2000). Thus, understanding the psychological effects of trauma requires consideration of the full spectrum of trauma responses rather than PTSD alone as a discrete diagnosis.

Language patterns convey meaningful information about psychological processes, including emotional regulation, cognitive processing, and identity reconstruction (Tausczik & Pennebaker, 2010). Trauma narratives frequently incorporate sensory and affective details that reflect the emotional and cognitive engagement of an individual with the traumatic experience (Jaeger et al., 2014). Prior studies show that the way individuals describe trauma is closely associated with their mental state, particularly the severity of PTSD symptoms (Booker et al., 2018; Luno et al., 2013). While a significant body of literature, including meta-analyses and studies using Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC), has compared linguistic patterns between PTSD and control groups (Knoff et al., 2024; Tausczik & Pennebaker, 2010; Yu et al., 2025), these studies have predominantly focused on everyday discourse or autobiographical trauma narratives. The present study extends this line of inquiry by analysing linguistic responses to the ambiguous stimuli presented in projective personality tests. Projective personality tests such as the Rorschach Inkblot Test, TAT, and SCT are theoretically designed to elicit responses that may differ from those obtained during explicit trauma recall, though whether they provide empirical advantages over standard narrative paradigms in capturing trauma-related linguistic patterns remains to be demonstrated (Orji et al., 2025; Santillo et al., 2025). Although projective tests have faced criticism in contemporary clinical psychology due to concerns over subjective scoring and inter-rater reliability (Lilienfeld et al., 2000; Mihura et al., 2013), we seek to address these limitations by integrating objective Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques. This methodology aims to provide a more standardized and rigorous framework for evaluating the nuanced emotional and cognitive schemas of individuals with PTSD. Furthermore, the concurrent use of three structurally distinct instruments – spanning perceptual (Rorschach), narrative (TAT), and sentence-level (SCT) task demands – enables a more comprehensive mapping of trauma-related linguistic expression across different levels of task structure and cognitive demand than would be possible with a single narrative paradigm.

This study employed NLP to analyse verbatim transcripts from projective personality tests across six linguistically defined categories with established relevance to PTSD psychopathology (Kleim et al., 2018; Knoff et al., 2024; Wisco et al., 2015; Yu et al., 2025): (1) negative emotion, (2) positive emotion, (3) insight, (4) causation, (5) body-related, and (6) self-focus words. Emotional categories, including negative and positive emotion, were designed to capture the full spectrum of emotional expression. Cognitive categories, such as insight and causation, assessed narrative integration and cognitive processing. The body category indexed somatic references, while self-focus identified first-person singular pronouns. Although PTSD research traditionally emphasizes negative emotion in narratives, positive emotion also serves as an important indicator of psychological resilience or emotional numbing, a core PTSD symptom (Knoff et al., 2024). Therefore, by including both emotional valences, this study aims to determine whether emotional processing patterns could differentiate the PTSD group and control groups and reflect variation in symptom severity. Cognitive processing was similarly evaluated to assess narrative integration, given that greater PTSD severity is often associated with difficulty organizing traumatic memories into a coherent structure (Kleim et al., 2018). To assess self-referential processing, self-focus words, primarily first-person singular pronouns, were identified. In trauma contexts, elevated self-focus may reflect difficulty distancing from the traumatic experience, suggesting emotional overwhelm (Wisco et al., 2015). Given that PTSD involves prominent somatic re-experiencing and body-image disturbances, analysis of body-related linguistic markers may provide additional insights into the somatic preoccupation of trauma survivors (Yu et al., 2025).

Computational linguistic analysis enables an objective and systematic examination of how trauma is encoded in language. Since projective personality tests have different task demands and levels of psychological projection, we sought to determine how these linguistic markers vary across tests. We developed three hypotheses: (1) PTSD and control groups will exhibit distinct linguistic profiles across the six categories; (2) the utility of specific linguistic markers in predicting symptom severity will differ by test type; and (3) emotional, cognitive, and somatic language categories will play differential roles in distinguishing clinical pathology and trauma-related distress in non-clinical individuals. By integrating NLP with projective assessments, this study aims to examine how the language features varied with PTSD severity and diagnostic status to identify markers distinguishing the PTSD and control groups.

Method

Participants

The study protocol was approved by the appropriate Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 2507-255-114). Overall, 283 participants who were native Korean speakers aged 18 years or older were recruited through online and print advertisements and outpatient clinics. Data were collected from August 22 to November 30, 2025. After providing written informed consent, participants completed the projective tests and self-report measures, which typically lasted 2 h.

Participants were categorized into two groups based on PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) scores and clinical history:

PTSD Group (n = 183): Participants met DSM-5 Criterion A for trauma exposure on the Life Events Checklist (LEC-5) and were either clinically diagnosed with PTSD or scored ≥33 on the PCL-5, indicating clinically significant symptoms. This group included 166 females (90.7%) and 17 males (9.3%), with a mean age of 51.24 years (SD = 12.38, range: 19–84).

PTSD Group (n = 183): Participants met DSM-5 Criterion A for trauma exposure on the Life Events Checklist (LEC-5) and were either clinically diagnosed with PTSD or scored ≥33 on the PCL-5, indicating clinically significant symptoms. This group included 166 females (90.7%) and 17 males (9.3%), with a mean age of 51.24 years (SD = 12.38, range: 19–84).

Control Group (n = 100): Participants scored below 33 on the PCL-5 and reported no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. This group included 66 females (66.0%) and 34 males (34.0%), with a mean age of 32.85 years (SD = 14.24, range: 19–68).

Control Group (n = 100): Participants scored below 33 on the PCL-5 and reported no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. This group included 66 females (66.0%) and 34 males (34.0%), with a mean age of 32.85 years (SD = 14.24, range: 19–68).

The observed imbalances in age and sex distribution between the groups reflect the higher prevalence of PTSD among middle-aged females in the clinical setting from which the participants were drawn (Kessler et al., 2017). To mitigate the potential confounding effects of these demographic discrepancies, age and sex were included as covariates in subsequent statistical analyses.

Measures

Projective Personality Test

Projective Personality Test

Three projective tests were administered by four trained clinical psychologists who are native Korean speakers to collect narrative linguistic data. Responses were captured through real-time verbatim manual transcription. To standardize the transcription process and minimize transcriber variability, all four psychologists completed a joint training session prior to data collection, during which transcription protocols were reviewed and practiced using standardized example responses. Psychologists were instructed to record all vocalizations precisely as spoken, including incomplete sentences, fillers, and repetitions, without interpretation or paraphrasing. Although the tests followed standardized clinical protocols, this study focused on the verbatim linguistic content for NLP rather than traditional scoring or clinical interpretation. This provided the raw data for the subsequent Korean-specific NLP analysis. Following transcription, a random sample of responses (approximately 10%) was reviewed by a second psychologist to check for omissions or errors, and any discrepancies were resolved through consensus. Although formal inter-rater reliability coefficients for transcription were not calculated, these procedural safeguards were implemented to ensure the consistency and accuracy of the transcribed data.

Rorschach Inkblot Test

Rorschach Inkblot Test

The Rorschach Inkblot Test uses 10 standardized inkblot cards, and examinees provide responses to each inkblot (Mihura et al., 2013). In this study, we focused on the initial response and the inquiry phases, transcribed verbatim to capture the full linguistic profile of participants rather than formal Comprehensive System scoring.

Thematic Apperception Test

Thematic Apperception Test

The TAT presents ambiguous social scenes, and examinees create a story for each card (Miller, 2015). In this study, we analysed the linguistic properties and thematic content of the narratives rather than clinically scoring needs or presses.

Sentence Completion Test

Sentence Completion Test

The SCT presents sentence fragments that examinees complete to form full sentences, providing insights into psychological functioning (Rogers et al., 2003).

2.Clinical symptom scales

Clinical symptom scales

All psychometric assessments and projective tests were conducted in Korean. To ensure linguistic and cultural equivalence, we utilized standardized Korean-validated versions of scales.

PTSD Checklist for DSM-5

PTSD Checklist for DSM-5

The PCL-5 is a 20-item self-report scale assessing PTSD symptom severity over the past month. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0–4), with higher scores indicating greater severity. In this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.963, indicating excellent internal consistency.

Life Events Checklist for DSM-5

Life Events Checklist for DSM-5

The LEC-5 screens for lifetime exposure to potentially traumatic events, covering 17 event types (e.g. natural disasters, physical/sexual assault, or life-threatening accidents) to determine whether participants meet the DSM-5 Criterion A for PTSD.

Comorbidity Control Measures

Comorbidity Control Measures

Depression and anxiety were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), respectively, to control for comorbid symptom effects. Both scales use a 4-point Likert scale and have well-established clinical validity. The Cronbach’s α in this sample was 0.941 for the PHQ-9 and 0.959 for the GAD-7, indicating excellent internal consistency.

Linguistic data processing

To examine language features in the PTSD and control groups, we applied quantitative linguistic analysis using NLP performed in Python (version 3.14.2; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) within the Anaconda environment via Jupyter Notebook (version 7.2.2.). Automated frequency analysis identified the top 100 terms for each projective test. This frequency analysis served as a post-hoc verification step to confirm that theoretically derived lexicon categories captured the most contextually salient vocabulary within each projective modality, rather than as the primary basis for lexicon construction.

While LIWC (Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count) is widely utilized for automated text analysis, its application to the Korean language presents significant challenges. First, translated versions of LIWC across languages differ substantially in lexicon size and cross-linguistic comparability, limiting their direct applicability in non-English clinical contexts (Kučera & Mehl, 2022). Second, the agglutinative nature of Korean, in which complex morphological structures and particles produce virtually unbounded word-form variations, fundamentally confounds simple word-matching algorithms designed for analytic languages such as English (Sung & Shin, 2025). To address these linguistic complexities, we employed a more nuanced NLP approach that incorporates a Korean morphological analyzer and a specialized Clinical Linguistic Lexicon developed specifically for this study. This method ensures higher precision in capturing clinically relevant semantic units that standard translated versions of LIWC might overlook (Kučera & Mehl, 2022).

Data screening and inclusion criteria

Data screening and inclusion criteria

Specific inclusion criteria were applied based on narrative length and completion rates to ensure the reliability and representativeness of the linguistic data. For the Rorschach and TAT, participants who produced a total of 150 words or more were included to ensure sufficient lexical density. For the SCT, participants who completed at least 80% of the stimulus stems were included to maintain narrative consistency. These thresholds were established to avoid the skewing of word frequencies in excessively brief narratives. No participants were excluded based on these criteria.

2.Text preprocessing and tokenization

Text preprocessing and tokenization

The text data were preprocessed using KoNLPy (v0.6.0), a Python-based Korean NLP library, with the Open Korean Text (Okt) analyzer. Stemming and normalization were applied to standardize the diverse conjugated forms of Korean verbs and adjectives. For example, lexical variants such as ‘went’ or ‘going’ were consolidated into their base form, ‘go,’ thereby enhancing the matching accuracy with the sentiment lexicons and custom dictionaries. In addition, to accommodate the agglutinative characteristics of the Korean language, relevant verbal suffixes and particles were incorporated.

To ensure the accuracy of the linguistic analysis, a rigorous data-cleaning procedure was performed. In the SCT, the fixed stimulus stems (e.g. ‘When something strange happens to me,’ ‘My mother is’) were programmatically removed using a custom Python script to isolate the unique responses of the participants. Furthermore, Korean stop words with minimal emotional or cognitive relevance – such as light verbs (e.g. ‘be,’ ‘do’), existential verbs (e.g. ‘exist’), and general nouns (e.g. ‘thing,’ ‘person,’ ‘appearance’) – were excluded. Non-lexical elements, including special characters (e.g. ‘/’), filler words (e.g. ‘um,’ ‘well’), and system-generated labels (e.g. ‘no data’), were also removed.

3.Frequency analysis for the clinical linguistic Lexicon refinement

Frequency analysis for the clinical linguistic Lexicon refinement

Following preprocessing, an initial frequency analysis was performed to identify the most frequently occurring terms within each of the three projective tests. The 100 highest-frequency words were extracted separately for each participant group across all three tests. This analysis served a verificatory rather than generative function: the resulting high-frequency terms were cross-referenced against the theoretically pre-specified lexicon categories to confirm coverage adequacy and to identify any highly recurrent, projective test-specific expressions not captured by existing resources (e.g. KNU Korean Sentiment Lexicon or prior literature). Where such terms were identified, their inclusion was adjudicated by the four clinical psychologists based on established theoretical criteria, not solely on frequency. This procedure ensured ecological validity without introducing circularity into the lexicon construction process.

4.Development of the clinical linguistic lexicon

Development of the clinical linguistic lexicon

To analyse the narrative responses, a specialized linguistic lexicon was developed using a systematic three-step process. First, six linguistic dimensions – negative emotion, positive emotion, insight, causation, body-related, and self-focus – were defined based on their theoretical relevance to PTSD psychopathology (Kleim et al., 2018; Knoff et al., 2024; Wisco et al., 2015; Yu et al., 2025), as described in the Introduction.

Second, to enhance the ecological validity of the lexicon within projective test contexts, the theoretically derived word lists were cross-referenced with the high-frequency vocabulary observed in participant responses. For the emotional categories (negative and positive emotion), a comprehensive word list was established primarily through the KNU Korean Sentiment Lexicon, a validated open-source resource developed from the Standard Korean Language Dictionary using a Bi-LSTM deep learning model (Park et al., 2019), supplemented by terms identified in prior PTSD linguistic research. For the four domain-specific categories (causation, insight, body-related, and self-focus), initial word candidates were identified from prior empirical and theoretical literature (Kleim et al., 2018; Miragoli et al., 2019; Pennebaker et al., 2003; Procaccia & Castiglioni, 2022; Yu et al., 2025). The frequency analysis was then used to verify that these theory-driven entries were indeed represented in participants’ actual language use, and to supplement the lists with projective test-specific expressions that met the pre-established theoretical criteria. All supplementary terms were reviewed and approved by the clinical psychologist panel prior to inclusion. This approach ensured that lexicon construction remained grounded in existing theory while capturing the contextual specificity of the assessment instruments used.

Finally, to ensure content validity, the consolidation process included a thorough validation by four trained clinical psychologists. They independently categorized a random sample of the lexicon (20% of the total terms) to empirically evaluate the reliability of this process. Inter-rater agreement was calculated using Fleiss’ Kappa to account for multiple raters, yielding a coefficient of 0.82, which indicates substantial agreement. Furthermore, the convergent validity of the clinical lexicon was supported by its integration of the KNU Korean Sentiment Lexicon, aligning our data-driven categories with an established linguistic resource. The finalized lexicon was applied uniformly to the PTSD and control groups to ensure a consistent and objective comparison of linguistic patterns.

6.Sensitivity analysis for Lexicon robustness

Sensitivity analysis for Lexicon robustness

To address potential concerns regarding methodological circularity and the structural asymmetry of the lexicon, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using a theoretically driven ‘Core Lexicon.’ Unlike the extended lexicon that incorporated data-driven terms and large-scale external dictionaries (KNU), the Core Lexicon was restricted to a priori defined clinical markers grounded in established literature: (1) Negative/Positive Emotion, established as core linguistic indicators of PTSD-related affective dysregulation in prior research (Kleim et al., 2018; Knoff et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025) (2) Insight and Causation representing cognitive processing of trauma(Kleim et al., 2018) (3) Body-related terms associated with somatic experiencing of trauma(Yu et al., 2025) and (4) Self-focus linked to ruminative attention (Brockmeyer et al., 2015; Kleim et al., 2018). By re-analysing the data with these constrained sets, we aimed to verify whether the observed group differences remained robust independent of the lexicon configuration. Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0 (International Business Machines Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < .05 (two-tailed). The analytical procedures were as follows: First, descriptive statistics and independent-samples t-tests were conducted to assess differences in linguistic indicators and psychological symptom levels between the PTSD and control groups. Second, ANCOVA analysis with age and gender as covariates to examine the linguistic differences between the PTSD group and the control group. To control for Type I error rate inflation due to multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied across all 18 comparisons (3 projective tests × 6 linguistic categories), setting the significance threshold at α = .0028 (.05/18). Third, hierarchical multiple regression analysis (Enter method) was performed to examine the unique predictive contribution of the linguistic indicators from the projective tests. In Step 1, demographic variables (sex, age) were entered as covariates. In Step 2, all 18 linguistic markers were simultaneously entered using the Enter method. The incremental contribution of linguistic variables (ΔR²) and its statistical significance (ΔF) are reported alongside overall model fit statistics.

Group differences in demographical and clinical characteristics

Significant differences were observed between the PTSD and control groups across all measured demographic and clinical variables (Supplementary Table 1).

Demographics

Demographics

The PTSD group was significantly older (M = 51.24, SD = 12.38) than the control group (M = 32.85, SD = 14.24; t = 10.86, p < .001). Additionally, the proportion of female participants was significantly higher in the PTSD group (90.7%) than in the control group (66.0%).

2.Clinical variables

Clinical variables

Differences in PTSD symptoms were evaluated using PCL-5 scores. The PTSD group reported significantly greater symptom severity (M = 54.83, SD = 13.26) than the control group (M = 9.81, SD = 11.74; t = 29.44, p < .001). The effect size was extremely large (Cohen’s d = 3.53), indicating a clear clinical separation between the two groups.

Differences in depressive symptoms were evaluated using PHQ-9 scores. The PTSD group reported significantly greater symptom severity (M = 18.47, SD = 7.74) than the control group (M = 3.38, SD = 4.06; t = 21.51, p < .001). The effect size was large (Cohen’s d = 2.26), indicating a distinct clinical separation between the two groups.

Differences in anxiety symptoms were evaluated using GAD-7 scores. The PTSD group reported significantly greater symptom severity (M = 13.12, SD = 6.36) than the control group (M = 1.82, SD = 2.86; t = 20.54, p < .001). The effect size was large (Cohen’s d = 2.10), indicating a distinct clinical separation between the two groups.

2.Sentence completion test (SCT)

Sentence completion test (SCT)

Two indicators survived Bonferroni correction in the SCT. The control group (Madj = 12.98) showed a significantly higher frequency of Positive Emotion than the PTSD group (Madj = 11.09; F = 10.904, p = .001, ηp² = .038). Additionally, Insight was significantly higher in the PTSD group (Madj = 4.80) than in the control group (Madj = 3.05; F = 10.487, p = .001, ηp² = .036). Although Body-related language also showed a higher frequency in the PTSD group at the uncorrected level (F = 7.218, p = .008, ηp² = .025), this finding did not survive Bonferroni correction and should be interpreted as exploratory.

3.Thematic apperception test (TAT)

Thematic apperception test (TAT)

Following Bonferroni correction, the PTSD group demonstrated a significantly higher frequency of Negative Emotion (Madj = 16.25) compared to the control group (Madj = 14.36; F = 14.699, p < .001, ηp² = .050). Although Insight also showed a higher frequency in the PTSD group at the uncorrected level (F = 8.250, p = .004, ηp² = .029), this result did not meet the Bonferroni-corrected threshold and should therefore be regarded as an exploratory finding.

Sensitivity analysis result: robustness of the clinical linguistic Lexicon

To assess the robustness of the primary findings against potential lexicon configuration effects, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using a theoretically constrained Core Lexicon. For the emotional categories, the Core Lexicon excluded the large-scale KNU Korean Sentiment Lexicon, retaining only the theory-driven terms derived from prior PTSD linguistic research (negative emotion: 35 words; positive emotion: 30 words). The four domain-specific categories (causation, insight, body-related, and self-focus) were kept identical to the primary lexicon, as these had been constructed exclusively from prior empirical and theoretical literature without reliance on any external large-scale dictionary. This configuration allowed for a direct test of whether the emotional category findings were dependent on the breadth of the KNU-derived vocabulary, while controlling for any potential influence of the domain-specific word lists. Results are presented in Supplementary Table 3.

Applying the Bonferroni-corrected threshold (α = .0028), two indicators demonstrated consistent significance across both the full and Core Lexicon analyses: Rorschach Negative Emotion (F = 10.885, p = .001, ηp² = .038) and SCT Insight (F = 10.487, p = .001, ηp² = .036). This convergence indicates that group differences in these indicators are robust and independent of lexicon size or composition. However, two indicators that were significant in the primary analysis – SCT Positive Emotion and TAT Negative Emotion – did not reach significance in the Core Lexicon analysis (p = .080 and p = .542, respectively). This divergence is attributable to the substantially reduced emotional vocabulary in the Core Lexicon (30 and 35 words, respectively, compared to 3,380 and 6,756 in the full lexicon), which limits the sensitivity of emotional category detection. These findings suggest that the group differences in emotional language categories are partly dependent on the breadth of lexicon coverage rather than reflecting purely clinical signal. Accordingly, results pertaining to SCT Positive Emotion and TAT Negative Emotion should be interpreted with greater caution and are discussed as potentially lexicon-sensitive findings in the limitations section.

Taken together, the sensitivity analysis provides partial support for the robustness of the primary findings. Non-emotional cognitive markers, particularly SCT Insight, demonstrated stability across lexicon configurations, while emotional category results showed sensitivity to lexicon breadth. This pattern underscores the importance of distinguishing between lexicon-robust and lexicon-sensitive findings when interpreting the clinical implications of the present study.

Discussion

Summary of findings

This study identified distinct linguistic patterns associated with PTSD and examined how these patterns differ across various projective personality tests. To achieve this, a specialized Clinical Linguistic Lexicon was developed by integrating standardized sentiment resources with theoretically grounded category definitions, supplemented by frequency-based verification against participant narratives to ensure ecological validity. Regarding the methodological framework, we opted for a lexicon-based frequency analysis rather than black-box deep learning models to prioritize clinical interpretability, given that deep learning approaches in mental health NLP have been noted for their limited transparency and clinical applicability (Quillivic et al., 2024). In the context of Korean computational linguistics, where high-performance tools for clinical narratives are still evolving (Kučera & Mehl, 2022; Sung & Shin, 2025), our study provides a bridge between traditional manual coding and automated analysis. This approach was intended to provide comprehensive coverage of emotional expressions while maintaining theoretically grounded, interpretable category structures that capture the nuanced linguistic responses of individuals with PTSD.

The present lexicon-based approach shares conceptual similarities with widely used LIWC-based studies, which consistently report elevated negative and reduced positive emotion in PTSD populations (Knoff et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025) – a pattern broadly aligning with our findings for the TAT, Rorschach, and SCT. However, unlike LIWC’s general-purpose English dictionary, our Clinical Linguistic Lexicon necessitated a substantially larger Korean-specific emotion vocabulary to account for the highly agglutinative nature of Korean morphology. Furthermore, by contextualizing these markers within projective responses rather than typical autobiographical narratives, this study suggests that differences in emotional expression may vary according to the structure of the psychological task, although the present research does not allow definitive conclusions regarding modality-specific effects.

To ensure the robustness of our findings and address potential demographic confounding, we employed a rigorous statistical framework. ANCOVA with age and gender as covariates was conducted across 18 linguistic comparisons, with Bonferroni correction applied to control for Type I error inflation (adjusted α = .0028). Four indicators survived Bonferroni correction in the primary analysis. However, the sensitivity analysis demonstrated that only two of these – Rorschach Negative Emotion and SCT Insight – were robust across lexicon configurations. SCT Positive Emotion and TAT Negative Emotion should therefore be considered exploratory findings pending replication with independent lexicons. It should further be acknowledged that, given the substantial age and sex imbalances between groups, residual confounding cannot be excluded even after statistical adjustment, and the group differences observed in the primary analysis may not fully reflect PTSD-specific linguistic characteristics. Additional indicators that were significant at the uncorrected threshold are reported descriptively and should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis using a theoretically constrained Core Lexicon – excluding the large-scale KNU sentiment dictionary – partially supported the robustness of primary findings.

Individual predictors showed small-to-medium standardized effects (β range:.162–.353 across significant predictors), consistent with the modest but statistically meaningful contributions expected from linguistic markers in complex clinical contexts. The incremental contribution of linguistic variables beyond demographic covariates was substantial in most models. In the PTSD group, linguistic variables accounted for an additional 16.7% to 21.4% of variance in symptom severity (ΔR² = .167–.214) after controlling for age and gender, with all three models reaching statistical significance (ΔF p-values ranging from .006 to .021). Notably, Step 1 demographic variables alone were non-significant predictors of depressive and anxiety symptoms (PHQ-9: p = .101; GAD-7: p = .056), indicating that the explanatory power of the final models was driven primarily by linguistic markers rather than demographic factors. In the non-PTSD group, linguistic variables similarly produced large incremental contributions for PCL-5 and GAD-7 (ΔR² = .331 and .353, respectively; both p < .01) though these estimates should be interpreted with caution given the absence of cross-validation procedures and the relatively large predictor-to-sample ratio in this group. However, the linguistic model did not significantly predict depressive symptoms in the non-PTSD group (ΔR² = .173, ΔF p = .510), suggesting that projective linguistic indicators may have limited sensitivity for capturing low-level depressive symptoms in non-clinical populations.

It is important to note that the present study does not directly demonstrate that projective tests provide superior access to latent psychological processes compared with autobiographical narrative tasks, and the interpretation of modality-dependent effects as reflecting differential levels of conscious filtering remains speculative and is not empirically verified by the present data. Nonetheless, the consistent incremental validity of linguistic markers across most models provides preliminary support for the potential utility of projective linguistic analysis as a supplementary research approach in trauma assessment.

Interpretation of results

Modality-Dependent emotional expression

Modality-Dependent emotional expression

It should be noted that, given the substantial demographic differences between groups, the following interpretations should be considered preliminary and interpreted with caution, as residual confounding effects of age and sex on language use cannot be fully ruled out. Consistent with the study hypotheses, emotional, cognitive, and somatic linguistic markers demonstrated differential patterns across groups and assessment modalities, with their clinical relevance varying systematically as a function of both task structure and symptom severity. One notable finding of this study is the contrasting pattern of negative emotional expression across different projective tests. Although the PTSD group showed higher negative emotion in the Rorschach and TAT, the direction of group differences in SCT Negative Emotion was reversed – with the PTSD group showing numerically lower scores than the control group – although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = .272). These contrasting patterns are consistent with the interpretation that test structure influences the degree to which emotional content is expressed. As a semi-structured task that provides verbal stems as prompts (Holaday et al., 2000), the SCT permits greater conscious control, increasing the possibility of deliberate emotional regulation or reduced spontaneous emotional expression. In contrast, the ambiguous stimuli of the Rorschach and TAT (Lilienfeld et al., 2000) may provide less opportunity for deliberate response monitoring, potentially eliciting linguistic responses that are less subject to deliberate self-monitoring compared with more structured tasks (Mihura et al., 2013; Orji et al., 2025). These observations might be consistent with neurobiological models suggesting that ambiguous visual stimuli may engage subcortical limbic regions through amygdala hyper-responsiveness (Giromini et al., 2017), whereas the structured verbal nature of the SCT may promote processes akin to ‘affect labeling’ – the act of putting emotions into words – which has been shown to activate prefrontal regions and attenuate amygdala activity (Torre & Lieberman, 2018).

For clarity of interpretation, the following discussion distinguishes between lexicon-robust findings – those that replicated across both the full and Core Lexicon analyses (Rorschach Negative Emotion and SCT Insight) – and lexicon-sensitive findings that should be treated as exploratory (SCT Positive Emotion and TAT Negative Emotion). Regression-based findings are discussed separately, as these reflect within-group individual differences rather than group-level mean comparisons and are not directly subject to the same lexicon sensitivity concerns.

Importantly, the observed cross-modal differences should not be interpreted as direct evidence of modality-specific psychological processes. These inconsistencies may equally reflect measurement instability or limited construct validity across the three projective instruments. The absence of convergent findings across instruments – with negative emotion significant in Rorschach and TAT but not SCT, and positive emotion significant in SCT but not Rorschach or TAT – raises substantive questions regarding whether the linguistic categories as operationalized in this study capture stable constructs across different task formats. Accordingly, construct validity should be considered at least as plausible an explanation as modality-specific effects. Furthermore, the group difference in SCT Positive Emotion – while significant in the primary ANCOVA – was not replicated in the Core Lexicon sensitivity analysis (p = .080), suggesting that this result may also depend on lexicon construction rather than reflecting a stable clinical phenomenon. Therefore, the present findings should be regarded as preliminary and should not be interpreted as definitive evidence for modality-specific psychological processes until convergent validity across projective tasks has been established.

Importantly, while SCT Negative Emotion did not differentiate the groups at the level of mean comparison, it emerged as a significant positive predictor of PTSD symptom severity (β = .184, p = .016), depressive symptoms (β = .243, p = .001), and anxiety symptoms (β = .188, p = .016) within the PTSD group in regression analyses. This dissociation between group-level and individual-level effects suggests that the SCT may be less sensitive to broad diagnostic differences but more attuned to capturing within-group variation in symptom severity, functioning less as a marker of PTSD diagnosis and more as an index of symptomatic burden within clinical populations.

2.Cognitive processing and insight language

Cognitive processing and insight language

SCT Insight demonstrated robust group differences across both the primary and Core Lexicon analyses, with the PTSD group consistently using more insight-related language than the control group. This pattern may reflect heightened cognitive engagement with trauma-related material in the context of a structured task. Given that the SCT elicits explicit self-referential responses, the elevated use of insight language – terms reflecting awareness, realization, and understanding – may indicate active attempts at meaning-making or narrative integration in individuals with PTSD (Ehlers & Clark, 2000). Furthermore, SCT Insight was a significant positive predictor of depressive symptom severity (β = .162, p = .036), suggesting that greater cognitive engagement with distressing experiences may simultaneously reflect and amplify depressive affect. This finding is consistent with theories of maladaptive rumination, in which insight-oriented self-reflection may co-occur with sustained negative affect rather than facilitating emotional resolution (Watkins, 2004). Taken together, these findings suggest that the SCT may be particularly sensitive to the cognitive processing characteristic of PTSD – one in which elevated cognitive engagement may co-occur with, rather than resolve, affective distress.

3.Distinct patterns of somatic and negative emotional language use

Distinct patterns of somatic and negative emotional language use

Regression analyses revealed that PTSD symptom severity was associated with distinct patterns of somatic, and negative language use across projective modalities. Regarding the Rorschach, lower body-related language frequency was associated with higher PCL-5 scores (β = −.180, p = .017). Although trauma is inherently somatic, individuals with severe symptoms may experience physical numbness as a pronounced dissociative feature of PTSD (Lay et al., 2025), such that reduced somatic language may reflect difficulty accessing or articulating bodily experiences in the context of severe PTSD (Frewen et al., 2008). However, the possibility that this finding reflects a floor effect attributable to the Rorschach's perceptual demand characteristics, rather than a substantive psychological mechanism, should be considered.

Because the group-level TAT Negative Emotion finding was not replicated in the Core Lexicon sensitivity analysis, the following interpretation should be regarded as exploratory. In the TAT, higher negative emotion language significantly predicted not only PTSD severity (β = .312, p < .001) but also depressive symptoms (β = .186, p = .025) and anxiety symptoms (β = .184, p = .031), indicating that the intrusion of negative affect into social narrative reconstruction is associated not only with core PTSD symptomatology but also with co-occurring depressive and anxiety symptom severity within the PTSD group (Ehring & Quack, 2010; Goldsmith et al., 2013). This cross-symptom predictive pattern suggests that TAT negative emotion language may be associated with broad affective difficulties that cut across multiple symptom domains (Ehring & Quack, 2010), rather than being specific to any single symptom domain. It should be noted that the group-level difference in TAT Negative Emotion was not replicated in the Core Lexicon sensitivity analysis (p = .542), suggesting that the ANCOVA finding may be sensitive to lexicon breadth. Accordingly, the following interpretation should be considered exploratory. The regression finding, reflecting within-group individual differences, may therefore provide a potentially informative index of the clinical relevance of TAT negative emotion language.

4.Protective role of positive emotional expression

Protective role of positive emotional expression

TAT Positive Emotion emerged as a significant negative predictor of both PTSD severity (β = −.223, p = .013) and anxiety symptoms (β = −.190, p = .037). This suggests that the capacity to incorporate positive emotional language into social narrative reconstruction may serve a protective function, potentially reflecting preserved emotional flexibility or resilience (Fredrickson et al., 2003; Knoff et al., 2024). Additionally, Rorschach Positive Emotion was a significant positive predictor of anxiety symptoms (β = .193, p = .036). This counterintuitive finding may reflect hypervigilant processing of stimuli in the context of chronic anxiety, whereby individuals with elevated anxiety may be particularly attuned to detecting and elaborating upon perceptual features in the ambiguous inkblots, potentially as a compensatory regulatory strategy (Fani et al., 2012; Kimble et al., 2014). The contrasting roles of positive emotion language across modalities – protective in the TAT yet associated with heightened anxiety in the Rorschach – further underscore the modality-dependent nature of linguistic markers in trauma assessment.

This protective pattern extended to the non-PTSD group, in which SCT Positive Emotion was a significant negative predictor of anxiety symptoms in the non-PTSD group (β = −.248, p = .046). Given that this finding was not replicated in the Core Lexicon sensitivity analysis, the following interpretation should be considered exploratory, indicating that greater use of positive emotional language in structured task contexts was associated with lower anxiety. This finding parallels the protective role of TAT Positive Emotion observed in the PTSD group, suggesting that positive emotional expression may serve as a cross-contextual linguistic indicator associated with lower symptom burden across both clinical and non-clinical populations (Tugade & Fredrickson, 2004). Together, these findings highlight the potential utility of positive emotional language as a cross-contextual indicator of psychological well-being, while also underscoring the importance of considering task modality when interpreting its clinical significance.

5.Patterns of self-Referential processing

Patterns of self-Referential processing

In the non-PTSD group, SCT Self-Focus was the strongest predictor of PTSD-related symptom severity (β = .317, p = .007), alongside Age. In individuals without clinical PTSD, self-focus may reflect a meaning-making coping strategy in response to stressful life experiences, even if temporarily linked to increased distress (Mor & Winquist, 2002). While excessive self-focus language may signal defensive stress-related processes (Watkins, 2004), its absence as a significant predictor in the PTSD group may reflect avoidance of self-disclosure to shield against negative self-concepts and overwhelming trauma-related emotions (Brewin & Holmes, 2003).

This pattern was further supported by the finding that Rorschach Self-Focus also significantly predicted anxiety symptoms in the non-PTSD group (β = .219, p = .028). The convergence of self-focus language as a symptom predictor across both structured (SCT) and unstructured (Rorschach) tasks in the non-PTSD group suggests that self-referential processing operates as a transmodal marker of distress in non-clinical individuals (Mor & Winquist, 2002), independent of the degree of task structure. These findings collectively suggest that the role of self-focused language varies with trauma severity, shifting from a marker of active cognitive processing in non-clinical individuals to a potentially avoidant pattern in clinical PTSD (Brewin & Holmes, 2003; Mor & Winquist, 2002).

Limitations

Despite these significant findings, this study has several limitations. First, the participant pool was predominantly female, and substantial age differences existed between groups. Although sex and age were statistically controlled in all primary analyses, the magnitude of these demographic imbalances may introduce residual confounding that statistical adjustment alone cannot fully eliminate. Additionally, unequal group sizes may have influenced statistical power, as reflected in the non-significant PHQ-9 model in the non-PTSD group. Future research should employ more balanced sampling strategies and larger sample sizes to strengthen the generalizability of findings.

Second, formal inter-rater reliability coefficients for the transcription process were not calculated. Although procedural safeguards were implemented – including a joint training session, standardized transcription protocols, and consensus-based review of a randomly selected subset of transcripts – the absence of quantified transcription reliability metrics represents a methodological limitation. Future studies should report transcription reliability indices, such as Cohen's Kappa or word error rate.

Third, although the present study sought to address the psychometric limitations of projective techniques by applying objective NLP-based analysis, the assumption that projective stimuli elicit less consciously mediated responses than other narrative tasks remains empirically unverified (Lilienfeld et al., 2000). The divergent linguistic patterns observed across the three tests may reflect measurement instability in addition to genuine modality effects, and the convergent validity of projective linguistic markers across instruments has yet to be established (Mihura et al., 2013).

Additionally, the relatively large number of predictors (18 linguistic variables) entered into the regression models for the non-PTSD group (n = 100) raises concerns about the stability of the reported predictive effects. The absence of cross-validation, bootstrap validation, or shrinkage procedures means that the reported ΔR² values may be optimistic estimates. These regression findings, particularly in the non-PTSD group, should therefore be interpreted as preliminary and requiring replication in larger, independently validated samples.

Finally, the Rorschach is primarily designed to elicit perceptual rather than somatic responses (Mihura et al., 2013), and the observed negative association between body-related language and PTSD severity may partly reflect a floor effect attributable to the task's demand characteristics rather than a substantive pattern of somatic numbing. Supporting this concern, the adjusted group means for body-related language were closely comparable (Madj = 6.860 for the PTSD group vs. Madj = 7.483 for the control group), and the group difference did not survive Bonferroni correction (p = .231), suggesting that the range of this variable may have been inherently restricted by the nature of the task. These considerations suggest that the present findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution pending replication with methodologically refined designs.

Future directions

Building on these findings, several directions for future research are warranted. First, previous research suggests that linguistic expression varies with trauma type; for example, interpersonal trauma often evokes complex shame-based emotions (Brewin et al., 2000; Herman, 1992). Future research should categorize trauma types to identify linguistic features specific to each form of exposure.

Second, the specialized morphological features of the Korean language limited the use of established English-based tools, including Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count software (Kučera & Mehl, 2022; Sung & Shin, 2025). While the custom NLP pipeline developed in this study enabled tailored analysis, future research should standardize Korean trauma-related lexicons to ensure replicability across studies.

Third, the sensitivity analysis revealed that group differences in emotional language categories – specifically SCT Positive Emotion and TAT Negative Emotion – were sensitive to lexicon breadth. Future research should validate these findings using alternative emotional lexicons or independent corpora to establish the robustness of emotional category markers.

Finally, future research should directly compare linguistic profiles derived from projective tests with those from autobiographical narrative tasks to clarify the unique contribution of each paradigm to PTSD assessment (Knoff et al., 2024). Cross-modal convergent validity and test-retest reliability of projective linguistic markers should also be systematically examined, and body-related language findings should be replicated using instruments with greater somatic demand characteristics (Frewen et al., 2008).

Conclusions

This study examined linguistic patterns in PTSD and non-PTSD individuals using projective tests combined with NLP analysis, identifying several significant group differences and symptom predictors across assessment modalities. The findings support the view that trauma-related psychological distress is reflected in verbal expression and suggest that computational linguistic analysis may facilitate more objective examination of verbal responses elicited by projective tests.

Across modalities, the findings were consistent with a possible modality-dependent pattern of emotional expression, with PTSD-related negative affect more frequently observed in the Rorschach and TAT than in the more structured SCT. However, these cross-modal differences should be interpreted cautiously because they may also reflect measurement instability or incomplete construct validity across the projective instruments. Within the PTSD group, linguistic markers reflecting elevated negative emotional expression – particularly TAT Negative Emotion – emerged as cross-symptom predictors of PTSD, depressive, and anxiety severity, while reduced body-related language in the Rorschach and elevated SCT Insight were associated with greater symptom burden. The capacity for positive emotional expression, especially in the TAT, was consistently associated with lower symptom severity, suggesting a potential protective role of positive linguistic expression in trauma contexts (Fredrickson et al., 2003; Tugade & Fredrickson, 2004). In the non-PTSD group, self-focus language across both structured and unstructured tasks predicted distress levels, indicating that self-referential processing may function as a transmodal marker of psychological burden in non-clinical individuals (Brockmeyer et al., 2015; Mor & Winquist, 2002).

These findings should be interpreted considering several important caveats. Bonferroni correction reduced the number of statistically robust group differences, and sensitivity analyses indicated that emotional category findings were partly dependent on lexicon breadth. The divergent patterns across instruments may also reflect measurement instability in addition to genuine modality effects. Accordingly, the present findings are best understood as preliminary evidence suggesting that projective linguistic analysis may warrant further investigation as a supplementary research tool, pending external validation against independent datasets and predictive testing in clinical contexts (Mihura et al., 2013), rather than as definitive conclusions about the mechanisms of PTSD-related language. By combining projective assessments with computational linguistic methods, this study contributes to the growing literature on linguistic markers of psychological trauma and offers a foundation for developing more objective, language-based approaches to trauma evaluation in clinical and research settings (Yu et al., 2025).
