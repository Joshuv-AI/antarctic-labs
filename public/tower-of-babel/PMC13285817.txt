Local global feature enhanced transformer with attention pruning for precise medical image segmentation
Authors: Ning Xu, Qun Song, Hongxu Yin, Cheng Qian, Liuyang Yang, Fei Lu, Wenhui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWhile Vision Transformer (ViT)-based methods excel at global modeling for medical image segmentation, optimizing them requires capturing fine-grained local details and mitigating attention redundancy. To address these issues, we propose a hybrid Transformer network, named Local Feature Enhanced AgentTopk UNet (LFEAT-UNet). LFEAT-UNet features three key designs: a Local Feature Enhancement (LFE) module to better represent detailed structure information, a Token Selection and Filtering (TSF) mechanism to dynamically prune background tokens, and a Cascaded Multi-scale Patch Perception (CMSPP) module to improve feature fusion. We conducted extensive experiments on Synapse, Automated Cardiac Diagnosis Challenge (ACDC), and a large-scale private adrenal nodule dataset. Our method achieves Dice scores of 83.12% on Synapse and 92.20% on ACDC. The superior results on the challenging private dataset further validate the model’s strong robustness. The proposed LFEAT-UNet provides an effective, sparsity-aware solution for medical image segmentation, excelling in delineating complex clinical targets, and shows significant potential for clinical applications.Subject areas: Health sciences, Health informatics, Artificial intelligence, Machine learning

Introduction

Medical image segmentation, aimed at accurately delineating anatomical structures and lesions from medical scans, is a critical component in modern clinical workflows.1,2 It plays a pivotal role in computer-aided diagnosis, treatment planning, and disease progression monitoring. High-quality segmentation can significantly improve the accuracy of quantitative analysis and support physicians in making more informed decisions. However, the task is often challenging due to the high variability in the shape, scale, and location of biological structures, as well as disturbances from imaging noise, blurred boundaries, and artifacts.3

In recent years, deep learning, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), has become the mainstream approach for medical image segmentation. Architectures like U-Net,4 with its symmetric encoder-decoder structure and skip connections, have achieved remarkable success by effectively fusing low-level and high-level semantic information. Building upon this, numerous U-Net variants5,6,7,8,9 have introduced various modifications to enhance feature extraction. For instance, UNet++5 introduced nested and dense skip connections to improve feature fusion, while ResUNet++8 incorporated residual blocks to ease the training of deeper networks. Others, like Attention U-Net,7 integrated attention gates into the skip connections to help the model focus on salient regions and suppress irrelevant information. More recently, large-kernel CNNs, such as ConvNeXt,10 have demonstrated the efficacy of expanding effective receptive fields.

As a result, convolution-based methods still suffer from inherent limitations in modeling global context and long-range dependencies due to their restricted receptive fields. Recent works explicitly highlight this limitation: for example, MCANet11 reports that pure convolution-based models inherently lack long-range interaction capability, while Transformers struggle to capture positional bias on small medical datasets. Likewise, CTH-Net12 notes that CNN-based models alone often fail when lesion boundaries are fuzzy or contrast is low, motivating the introduction of Transformer components for stronger global reasoning. To alleviate these limitations without introducing explicit attention mechanisms, SUNeXt13 adopts shifted large-kernel convolution combined with a grouped feature fusion strategy. By expanding the effective receptive field, it mitigates, to some extent, the shortcomings of traditional convolutions in modeling long-range dependencies. Nevertheless, its reliance on fixed large kernels may still limit the network’s ability to adaptively focus on the most informative regions, leaving room for further improvements in noise-resistant and adaptive global feature modeling. This limitation has motivated the adoption of Transformers in the medical domain.

To overcome the limitations of CNNs, Vision Transformers (ViT)14 have been introduced to the field of computer vision. By leveraging the self-attention mechanism, ViTs can model global context and capture long-range dependencies across the Vites entire image.15 This has led to the development of a powerful series of models such as Swin Transformer,16 SegFormer,17 and Pyramid Vision Transformer (PVT).18 In medical image segmentation, there are also related works such as TransUNet19 and SwinUNet,20 which incorporated Transformer mechanisms to achieve excellent results. However, despite their success, standard Transformer-based methods face two critical issues in the medical domain. First, they often struggle to capture fine-grained local features,21 such as sharp boundaries and small anatomical details, which is a strength of CNNs. Second, even with hierarchical designs, representational redundancy remains high because medical images often contain large areas of uninformative background (for example, air in computed tomography [CT] scans), which are processed with the same attention capacity as the regions of interest (ROIs). This not only dilutes the attention weights allocated to critical regions but, more importantly, introduces severe background noise that degrades the feature representation of the target lesions.

Addressing the lack of locality remains a primary research focus. For instance, H2Former22 incorporates hierarchical hybrid blocks that constrain attention to structured local neighborhoods, CiT-Net23 embeds convolutional feature extractors within Transformer layers to maintain spatial continuity, and MCANet11 refines attention computation through multi-scale cross-axis interactions to better model elongated or boundary-sensitive anatomical patterns. These approaches demonstrate that enhancing locality inside the Transformer is essential for medical image segmentation. However, most existing mechanisms remain tightly coupled to fixed receptive fields, operate in isolated stages, or only modify attention computation without addressing the broader information flow across scales. As a result, Transformers still struggle to jointly preserve fine-grained structures and global context, particularly when boundary information is attenuated through deep encoding.

On the other hand, to improve global context aggregation process of Transformers, refined attention mechanisms and alternative architectures have emerged. Models such as the Attention Free Transformer (AFT) 22 and Agent Attention23 aim to streamline the aggregation of global information. Agent Attention, in particular, employs a small set of agent tokens to broadcast global context effectively. However, directly applying Agent Attention to medical imaging is suboptimal. Standard Agent Attention indiscriminately aggregates information from all tokens, including dominant background noise. This not only fails to leverage the inherent sparsity of medical scans but also introduces severe noise contamination into the feature representation of target organs. More recently, State Space Models (SSMs) like U-Mamba24 and VM-UNet25 have gained prominence for their linear complexity and ability to model long sequences. However, existing SSM-based segmentation models typically flatten 2D feature maps into 1D sequences. This flattening process weakens explicit 2D spatial inductive biases, making the preservation of intricate 2D boundaries challenging.

Beyond attention level improvements, hybrid CNN-Transformer architectures have emerged to balance global modeling and local detail preservation. Models such as H2Former,22 CiT-Net,23 and MCANet11 leverage convolutional biases alongside attention mechanisms to better capture anatomical structures. Yet, their encoder-decoder fusion remains largely dependent on conventional convolutions or simple skip connections, which insufficiently align multi-scale semantics and cannot explicitly recover high-frequency boundary details lost during downsampling. Furthermore, these hybrids typically operate in the spatial domain without leveraging frequency domain cues that are crucial for restoring sharp structural transitions. Meanwhile, foundation models such as Segment Anything Model (SAM)26 and MedSAM27 provide strong zero-shot segmentation capabilities. However, they generally lack pixel-level precision for specific medical tasks without extensive fine-tuning and remain prompt dependent. Moreover, they may struggle in low contrast or weak boundary scenarios, limiting their applicability to fully automatic clinical workflows.

To specifically mitigate the aforementioned background interference in global modeling, various sparsification and pruning strategies have been developed in Transformers and self-attention networks to enhance feature selectivity. Broadly, these approaches can be categorized based on their granularity and effect. Attention sparsification primarily aims to suppress the influence of irrelevant tokens during context aggregation. For instance, sparse mapping mechanisms like Sparsemax28 replace the standard Softmax to force low-scoring attention weights to exactly zero, thereby enhancing the model’s selectivity for critical features and preventing noisy tokens from contaminating the representation. Similarly, non-local modeling has been combined with sparse attention29 to capture long-range self-similarities in tasks such as image super-resolution. However, a key limitation of these soft-sparsification methods is that all tokens continue to participate in the query-key interactions, meaning background noise can still implicitly affect the attention distribution.

In contrast to soft sparsification, explicit token selection or pruning strategies actively identify and isolate uninformative tokens, thereby purifying the sequence representation. Dynamic token pruning30 has been applied to dense prediction tasks, utilizing lightweight prediction modules to eliminate unimportant tokens at intermediate layers, forcing the network to dedicate its representational capacity solely to salient regions. Alternatively, token merging strategies based on bipartite matching31 have been proposed to fuse similar tokens, reducing representation redundancy. While these token-level reduction methods offer superior noise suppression, aggressive selection may lead to the loss of fine structural details or weak boundaries if not carefully designed.

Beyond token-level operations, structured optimization focuses on refining feature channels. Attention-driven mechanisms have been introduced to tailor channel importance for dense prediction tasks.32 For example, visual saliency-guided channel pruning33 retains channels most responsive to salient regions, effectively filtering out domain-specific noise. Furthermore, recent advancements have integrated location-aware mechanisms that combine semantic saliency with spatial distribution priors to perform non-uniform pruning.34 These saliency and location-guided strategies align representation decisions with the semantic importance of anatomical structures.

Overall, existing literature illustrates a clear trajectory from soft attention sparsification to explicit token selection. These methods highlight the critical importance of isolating target features from complex backgrounds. Drawing inspiration from these advancements, our work specifically integrates a token selection and filtering strategy tailored for medical image segmentation, aiming to effectively suppress severe background noise while maintaining robust global context modeling for the precise recovery of anatomical details.

To address these gaps comprehensively, specifically the noise interference in standard global attention and the loss of high-frequency details in global modeling, we propose a framework: Local Feature Enhanced AgentTopk UNet (LFEAT-UNet). Unlike standard Agent Attention, we introduce a Token Selection and Filtering (TSF) mechanism to filter background tokens and suppress noise interference, solving the noise issue inherent in global context aggregation by strictly allocating representational capacity to the ROIs. Unlike standard hybrid models, we design a Local Feature Enhancement (LFE) module that utilizes frequency domain differences to explicitly capture and restore high-frequency boundary details. By dynamically filtering low-response tokens based on attention scores, TSF adapts to the sparsity characteristics of medical images by suppressing background interference. By concentrating attention explicitly on informative regions, it prevents background noise propagation and yields highly robust feature representations, particularly for boundary-ambiguous targets.

Results

In this section, we conduct a series of experiments to comprehensively evaluate the performance of our proposed LFEAT-UNet. We compare our method with several representative and state-of-the-art segmentation models on public and private datasets. To ensure a rigorous evaluation across different architectural paradigms, these models include CNN-based and Transformer-based models (e.g., TransUNet, SwinUNet, MISSFormer, and DAE-Former), recent hybrid architectures (e.g., H2Former and CiT-Net), SSM-based models (e.g., U-Mamba and VM-UNet), and a large-scale foundation model (MedSAM). Finally, we perform detailed ablation studies and hyperparameter sensitivity analyses to validate the effectiveness of each component of our model.

Results on Synapse dataset

We first evaluate our model on the Synapse multi-organ CT segmentation task. The quantitative results are presented in Table 1. Our model achieves an average Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) of 83.12% and an average HD95 of 14.68 mm, ranking among the best-performing methods in terms of boundary accuracy and demonstrating strong overall segmentation accuracy and superior boundary precision. Notably, compared to the representative Transformer-based models, our LFEAT-UNet surpasses TransUNet and SwinUNet by 5.64% and 3.99% in average DSC, respectively. This demonstrates the effectiveness of our proposed modules in capturing both global and local features. Among all competing methods, U-Mamba attains the highest average DSC (85.18%), reflecting its strong capability in capturing global contextual information via state space modeling. However, LFEAT-UNet achieves a lower HD95, indicating more accurate and stable boundary predictions, particularly for organs with complex shapes and ambiguous boundaries. This observation highlights a trade-off between maximizing region-wise overlap (DSC) and ensuring precise boundary localization, where LFEAT-UNet places stronger emphasis on boundary-aware feature refinement. In addition, foundation-model-based approaches such as MedSAM achieve competitive performance on several large organs but exhibit relatively higher HD95 values, suggesting less consistent boundary accuracy in fully automatic segmentation settings.

Results on private adrenal nodule dataset

To evaluate the robustness of our model in a more challenging, real-world clinical scenario, we tested it on our private adrenal nodule dataset. The results are summarized in Table 3. Our LFEAT-UNet achieves the best performance on this challenging task, obtaining the best scores in both Intersection over Union (IoU) (93.03%) and DSC (96.38%), while also achieving the lowest HD95 value (6.82 mm) among all compared methods including recent hybrid and SSM-based models such as H2Former, CiT-Net, VM-UNet, U-Mamba, MCANet, and MedSAM. The visual comparisons in Figure 3 show that our proposed method produces more accurate segmentation results in segmenting small targets and handling regions with boundary uncertainties. This robust performance on a large-scale, multi-center dataset highlights the practical value and clinical potential of our model.

Ablation studies and hyperparameter sensitivity

To validate the effectiveness of our proposed modules and architectural choices, we conducted a series of ablation studies on the Synapse dataset. We systematically analyzed the contribution of each core component (CMSPP, TSF, and LFE) and the internal design of the CMSPP module. Furthermore, to examine the sensitivity of key hyperparameters and architectural settings, we investigated the impact of the number of skip connections and the number of agent tokens on segmentation performance. Finally, we explored the optimal setting for the weighting factors of the loss function to maximize segmentation accuracy.

Effectiveness of core modules

We first investigated the individual contributions of our three main proposed modules: the CMSPP module, the TSF mechanism, and the LFE module. We started with a baseline model that only adopted the Agent attention, and progressively added each component. The results are summarized in Table 6.

Analysis of the CMSPP module

We conducted further experiments to analyze the internal components of our CMSPP module and the optimal number of skip connections. In these experiments, the network was built upon the baseline architecture augmented only with the CMSPP module, while the TSF and LFE modules were not included, in order to isolate the impact of CMSPP-related design choices.

The CMSPP module contains two key sub-modules: the Cascaded Convolution Module (CCM) and the Multi-scale Patch Perception Module (MSPPM). To validate their individual contributions, we ablated each one from the full CMSPP design. As shown in Table 7, removing either component leads to a drop in performance. Specifically, removing the MSPPM results in a more significant DSC decrease of 0.78% compared to removing the CCM (0.17% drop).

From a boundary-sensitive perspective, however, the HD95 metric exhibits a different trend. When only CCM is introduced (without MSPPM), HD95 slightly increases (20.08 mm → 21.56 mm), despite the improvement in DSC. This phenomenon suggests that while CCM enhances semantic consistency through cascaded convolutional refinement, it may introduce slight boundary smoothing when used alone, which affects extreme surface deviations measured by HD95.

In contrast, MSPPM explicitly models multi-scale contextual relationships, which helps stabilize boundary geometry and suppress localized contour outliers. When both CCM and MSPPM are combined, HD95 decreases to 20.32 mm, indicating that the two branches complement each other in improving boundary precision rather than functioning optimally in isolation.

From a computational perspective, removing the MSPPM reduces the model size from 30.65 M to 29.60 M parameters and the computational cost from 11.39 G to 11.06 G FLOPs, indicating a relatively mild reduction in complexity. This suggests that both components are beneficial, and their complementary nature in capturing deep semantics and multi-scale context is crucial for the module’s overall effectiveness.

Effect of agent token numbers

To further investigate the impact of Agent Token numbers on model performance, we conducted ablation experiments on the Synapse dataset by varying the number of Agent Tokens.

As shown in Table 9, the model achieves optimal segmentation performance when the number of Agent Tokens is set to 49, with DSC of 83.12% and HD95 of 14.68 mm. Using more tokens (64) does not improve performance, suggesting that excessive tokens may introduce redundancy and noise. Conversely, using fewer tokens (36 or 25) leads to performance degradation, indicating insufficient capacity to capture the necessary global context. The optimal setting of 49 tokens (7 × 7 spatial arrangement) provides a good balance between feature granularity and representational capacity.

Effect of loss function weights

To investigate the impact of loss function weights on segmentation accuracy, we conducted ablation experiments with different combinations of α and β weights in the combined loss function Ltotal=αLDice+βLCe on the Synapse dataset.

As shown in Table 10, using only Dice loss ([1,0]) or only Cross-Entropy loss ([0,1]) yields suboptimal results. Optimal performance is achieved when [α, β] = [0.4, 0.6], with DSC of 83.12% and HD95 of 14.68 mm. This suggests that slightly emphasizing Cross-Entropy loss helps the model better learn pixel-wise classification, while Dice loss ensures region-level consistency.

Discussion

In this study, we developed the LFEAT-UNet to address critical challenges in medical image segmentation: the lack of fine-grained local details in global modeling and the severe interference from uninformative background noise. Through extensive evaluation across public benchmarks and a large-scale multi-center private clinical dataset, LFEAT-UNet demonstrated highly competitive performance, excelling particularly in precise boundary delineation and background noise suppression.

The success of our architecture is driven by the synergistic integration of three core mechanisms. First, the TSF mechanism explicitly addresses the representational redundancy inherent in standard Agent Attention. By dynamically filtering low-response background tokens, it forces the network to allocate representational capacity exclusively to salient regions, significantly boosting robustness against noise. Second, the LFE module acts as a critical counterbalance to the Transformer’s loss of high-frequency spatial details. By decoupling structural and residual information in the frequency domain, it explicitly restores complex boundaries. Finally, the CMSPP module ensures that these refined local and global semantics are properly aligned across the encoder-decoder hierarchy, mitigating the semantic gap that plagues conventional skip connections.

As observed in Table 12, no statistically significant differences were observed between LFEAT-UNet and several other state-of-the-art methods on the Synapse dataset. We report these findings to maintain rigorous academic transparency and to avoid cherry-picking favorable metrics. As performance on established public benchmarks like Synapse has become highly saturated, achieving statistically significant improvements over top-tier models is increasingly difficult. These non-significant results serve to confirm that LFEAT-UNet successfully reaches this performance ceiling, operating on par with current SOTA architectures.

However, it is crucial to note that the true advantage of LFEAT-UNet lies in its application to highly complex and non-saturated clinical scenarios, such as our large-scale private adrenal nodule dataset shown in Table 3. In environments characterized by severe background adhesion and irregular boundaries, our boundary-aware and noise-suppression designs yield more pronounced and consistent performance gains. Thus, the proposed architecture provides a highly robust solution tailored for challenging medical image segmentation tasks.

In conclusion, LFEAT-UNet provides an effective, sparsity-aware approach that simultaneously models global dependencies and preserves intricate boundaries. Its strong generalization on real-world multi-center data highlights its significant potential for integration into fully automatic clinical workflows and computer-aided diagnosis systems.

Limitations of the study

While LFEAT-UNet demonstrates competitive performance on multiple medical image segmentation benchmarks, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the experimental evaluation is limited to three datasets. Validation on additional and more diverse medical imaging datasets would be beneficial to further assess the generalizability of the proposed method. Moreover, the current implementation focuses on 2D slice-based segmentation. Extending the framework to 3D volumetric segmentation, which could better capture spatial continuity and inter-slice contextual information, remains an important direction for future work.

Additionally, regarding the private adrenal nodule dataset, strict data de-identification protocols mandated by the Institutional Review Board of Yunnan Cancer Hospital precluded access to demographic metadata, such as the age, sex, and gender of the participants. Consequently, we were unable to evaluate the potential influence or association of these demographic factors on the segmentation performance. This limits our ability to fully assess the model’s generalizability across specific patient subpopulations. Future prospective studies incorporating ethically approved demographic tracking are necessary to validate algorithmic fairness and robust clinical applicability.

Finally, from a computational perspective, while the TSF module effectively filters background noise without adding learnable parameters, its current implementation relies on dynamic top-k selection and sorting operations to estimate the adaptive threshold. Although these operations are executed entirely on the graphics processing unit (GPU), they introduce data-dependent control flows. Specifically, using the selected indices for subsequent advanced indexing forces CUDA synchronization, as the scheduler must wait for the exact indices before fetching data, disrupting the high parallelism typical of dense networks. Furthermore, unlike matrix multiplications, sorting operations are notoriously memory-bandwidth intensive on GPUs, often leaving compute units idle. These hardware-level execution bottlenecks offset some of the theoretical inference speed advantages gained from token sparsity. Future work will focus on developing optimized, custom CUDA kernels or hardware-friendly approximations for the TSF mechanism to mitigate these latency overheads.

Experimental model and study participant details

To validate the effectiveness and generalization ability of our proposed model, we conducted experiments on two widely used public benchmarks and a large-scale private clinical dataset.

The Synapse Multi-organ Segmentation Dataset contains 30 abdominal CT scans with 3,779 axial contrast-enhanced CT images. Following the standard protocol, we use 18 cases for training and 12 cases for testing. The segmentation task involves 8 abdominal organs: aorta, gallbladder, left kidney, right kidney, liver, pancreas, spleen, and stomach.

The Automated Cardiac Diagnosis Challenge (ACDC) dataset consists of cardiac MRI scans from 100 patients. The dataset is divided into 70 samples for training, 10 for validation, and 20 for testing. The objective is to segment three structures: the left ventricle (LV), the right ventricle (RV), and the myocardium (MYO).

The Private Adrenal Nodule CT Segmentation dataset comprises 14,006 CT images collected from three independent medical centers, covering six common types of adrenal nodules. To ensure methodological consistency and reduce inter-class heterogeneity across different adrenal nodule subtypes, we selected pheochromocytoma, a clinically important functional adrenal tumor, for this study. The final dataset consists of 3,118 CT images of pheochromocytoma cases. Adrenal nodules are common incidental findings in clinical practice. Accurate segmentation of adrenal nodules is crucial for differential diagnosis and treatment planning. Among the various types of adrenal nodules, pheochromocytoma is a tumor of significant clinical importance due to its potential to secrete catecholamines and cause life-threatening hypertension,40 making it an ideal candidate for developing and validating automated segmentation methods.

This private adrenal nodule dataset utilized retrospective patient medical data. All experimental protocols involving human data were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Yunnan Cancer Hospital (Approval Number: KYLX2024-146). Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived. To strictly protect patient privacy and comply with the regulatory requirements of the Ethics Committee, all medical data were fully de-identified prior to analysis. Due to these strict de-identification protocols, specific demographic information, including the sex, gender, and age of the study participants, was stripped from the records and is therefore not reported.

All ground truth annotations were performed by three experienced radiologists following a rigorous consensus-based annotation protocol. One radiologist initially performed the segmentation, after which the remaining two radiologists independently reviewed each annotation. In cases of disagreement, a final decision was reached through joint discussion among all three radiologists, ensuring annotation accuracy and consistency.

The segmentation task on the private dataset is formulated as a binary classification problem, where the goal is to segment pheochromocytoma regions from the background. We employ BCE Loss as the training objective for this dataset. The dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets with a ratio of 7:1:2.

For the Synapse and ACDC datasets, we use two standard metrics to evaluate model performance: the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) and the 95% Hausdorff Distance (HD95). The DSC measures the overlap between the predicted segmentation and the ground truth, while the HD95 evaluates the distance between their boundaries. For our binary-class private dataset, we employ a more comprehensive set of four metrics: DSC, Intersection over Union (IoU), Sensitivity (SEN), and HD95.

Method details

Overall architecture

The overall architecture of our LFEAT-UNet is illustrated in Figure 4. The model adopts a symmetric encoder-decoder structure, which is a proven paradigm for image segmentation tasks. Given an input image F∈RH×W×C, where H, W, and C represent the height, width, and number of channels, respectively, the network produces a segmentation map Y∈RH×W×N, where N is the number of target classes.

Overall architecture of LFEAT-UNet

The model adopts a U-shaped encoder-decoder structure with a bottleneck. Encoder features at multiple scales are forwarded to the decoder via skip pathways enhanced by CMSPP modules, while AgentTopk blocks are employed to facilitate robust global modeling.

The encoder path is designed to extract hierarchical feature representations. Initially, the input image is processed by a Patch Embedding layer, which divides the image into patches and maps them into a high-dimensional feature space. The resulting feature map is then fed into a sequence of three stages. Each stage consists of several stacked AgentTopk Blocks followed by a Patch Merging layer. The AgentTopk Blocks are responsible for learning complex semantic features, while the Patch Merging layer performs downsampling, reducing the spatial resolution by half and doubling the number of channels.

The bottleneck, which connects the encoder and decoder, consists of two consecutive AgentTopk Blocks to further process and refine the deepest features.

The decoder path aims to progressively recover the spatial details and generate the final segmentation map. It also comprises three stages, each beginning with a Patch Expanding layer that performs upsampling to restore the feature map’s resolution. The upsampled features are then fused with the corresponding multi-scale features from the encoder path via our proposed CMSPP skip-connection module. This fused feature map is then processed by a series of AgentTopk Blocks. Finally, a linear projection layer with a 1 × 1 convolution is applied to the full-resolution feature map from the last decoder stage to produce the final segmentation output Y.

AgentTopk block

The AgentTopk Block is the core computational unit of our LFEAT-UNet. As shown in Figure 5, its central design philosophy is to model local and global dependencies at different granularities through a parallel structure. This allows the network to comprehensively understand the input data and enhances its ability to process complex information.

Local feature enhancement (LFE) module

While Transformers excel at modeling global, low-frequency information, they often neglect local, high-frequency details such as edges and textures, which are critical for precise segmentation. To address this, we propose the LFE module, illustrated in Figure 6. This module is designed to strengthen local feature modeling through hierarchical convolutional operations, without introducing an explicit attention mechanism.

The detailed architecture of the LFE module

The LFE module explicitly decouples low-frequency structures and high-frequency details using two complementary streams, providing a simple yet interpretable alternative to attention-based local enhancement. This design allows the network to selectively strengthen structural features while capturing fine-grained residual details, thereby improving segmentation accuracy for complex boundaries and textures.

Given an input feature map F∈RC×H×W, it first passes through a downsampling operation with a Conv to extract low-frequency information, resulting in a feature map F1∈RC×H/2×W/2. This map is then upsampled back to the original size using bilinear interpolation to create a low-frequency guidance feature, F2∈RC×H×W, which primarily captures smooth structural information and local contextual relationships.

The low-frequency guidance F2 not only captures smooth structural information but also serves as a reference for selective enhancement and residual computation, ensuring effective separation of structural and detail information.

Next, the feature F is decomposed into two complementary information streams. The first stream models regions consistent with the guidance feature via element-wise multiplication, resulting in the feature x1. This operation enhances areas aligned with the low-frequency structural information while suppressing background or weak-response regions, thereby effectively highlighting the main contours. The second stream models structural differences via element-wise subtraction, resulting in the feature x2, which serves as a high-frequency residual encoding that explicitly captures the discrepancy between the input features and their low-frequency approximation, thereby highlighting fine-grained details such as boundaries, edges, and textures. Compared with addition or concatenation, this dual-stream design provides a clear algorithmic rationale for decoupling structural and textural information without relying on attention mechanisms, allowing the network to effectively capture both low-frequency structures and high-frequency details. Importance-scored AgentTopk attention

Standard self-attention mechanisms often generate dense and redundant attention maps, leading to unnecessary computations. Although Agent Attention41 optimizes global context aggregation by using a set of agent tokens, its attention maps remain dense. To introduce explicit sparsity and focus the model on critical regions, we propose the TSF mechanism within our AgentTopk Attention module, shown in Figure 7.

The architecture of our proposed AgentTopk Attention with the TSF mechanism

While maintaining the original logical structure of the Agent Attention mechanism, we propose the TSF module, which is placed immediately after the Softmax layer to further refine the attention map and effectively suppress the noise and redundancy introduced by the Softmax operation. Unlike approaches that rely on the dense distribution of Softmax, TSF dynamically estimates an adaptive threshold τ from the top-k high-response feature tokens and leverages this threshold to suppress low-confidence values, thereby producing a more discriminative and sparse attention representation. The overall workflow of the TSF module is illustrated in Figure 8.

Schematic illustration of the TSF module

Specifically, after the Softmax operation, the feature values are normalized to the range of [0, 1], forming the corresponding feature set N. Considering the inherent sparsity of medical feature spaces, many tokens contribute minimally to the target semantic information; thus, processing the entire sequence may adversely affect the discriminability of the attention map. To address this, we introduce a top-k strategy to select the k highest-response tokens from the normalized set, thereby focusing on the most informative features and ensuring that they preferentially participate in the subsequent adaptive threshold estimation.

What needs to be pointed out is that when the sequence length N is greater than k, the top-k operator is applied to retain only the k highest-response tokens; otherwise, if N ≤ k, all N tokens are preserved for subsequent processing. This design avoids imposing unnecessary sparsity on small-scale feature spaces, while ensuring a discriminative capability in high-dimensional scenarios.

Following this strategy, the selected k features constitute the set Nk. These elements are then sorted in descending order to facilitate the adaptive thresholding procedure. This sorting helps prioritize the most informative features for the threshold estimation, ensuring that only the most relevant features contribute to the final sparse representation.

In U-Net4 like architectures, skip connections are crucial for combining low-level spatial details from the encoder with high-level semantic information from the decoder. Implementation details

All experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU with 24 GB of memory, running on an Ubuntu 22.04 operating system. The proposed network was implemented using the PyTorch 2.1.0 framework and Python 3.8.10. To prevent overfitting and improve model robustness, we applied standard data augmentation techniques during training, including random flipping and random rotation. The training strategies were tailored for each dataset as follows.

For the Synapse dataset, the input images were resized to a resolution of 224 × 224. We trained the model for 400 epochs with a batch size of 24. We used the SGD optimizer with a momentum of 0.9 and a weight decay of 0.0001. The initial learning rate was set to 0.05.

For the ACDC dataset, the input images were also resized to 224 × 224. The model was trained for 400 epochs with a batch size of 24. For this task, we used the AdamW optimizer with β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999 and a weight decay of 0.0001. The initial learning rate was set to 0.001.

For the private dataset, the input images were resized to 512 × 512. The model was trained for 150 epochs with a batch size of 4. We used the SGD optimizer with a momentum of 0.9 and an initial learning rate of 0.001.

All convolutional and linear layers were initialized using truncated normal distribution with a standard deviation of 0.02. Bias terms are initialized to zero, and LayerNorm parameters are initialized with weight = 1.0 and bias = =0. The network is trained from scratch without using any pre-trained weights.

For all experiments, the learning rate followed a polynomial decay strategy, starting from the initial learning rate and gradually decreasing throughout the training process.
