1. Description of the Decarbonized Industrial Process
1.1. Introduction and State of the Art
Ethylene (C₂H₄) is one of the most important building blocks in the global chemical industry.
As the simplest olefin, it serves as a key feedstock in the production of a wide range of
chemicals and plastics. Major derivatives include polyethylene (PE), ethylene oxide, ethylene
dichloride (a precursor to PVC), and styrene. Due to its versatility and reactivity, ethylene is
foundational to the manufacture of consumer goods, packaging materials, automotive
components, and construction products1.

Global ethylene production exceeds 200 million metric tons per year, making it the most
produced organic chemical by volume. The state-of-the-art method for ethylene production is
steam cracking—a high-temperature, thermal process that breaks down hydrocarbon
feedstocks such as ethane (C2H6), propane (C3H8), naphtha, or gas oil. In this method, the
feedstock is mixed with steam and rapidly heated to temperatures above 750°C in a cracking
furnace. The intense heat breaks apart hydrocarbon molecules into smaller fragments,
primarily ethylene. The reaction products are then rapidly quenched to prevent secondary
reactions and undergo compression, acid gas removal, drying, and cryogenic separation to
isolate ethylene and other valuable by-products like propylene, hydrogen, and butadiene2,3.

In the chemical and petrochemical sector, the energy-intensive steam cracking process
accounts for 40% of overall energy consumption4. Among steam cracking options, ethane-
based cracking is particularly prevalent in North America due to the availability of low-cost
shale gas, offering higher yields and lower carbon intensities compared to naphtha-based
routes used in Asia and Europe5. However, this method remains highly energy- and carbon-
intensive, with ethane steam cracking typically requiring 17–21 GJ/t of specific energy for
ethylene and 16–19 GJ/t of HVCs2. Thermodynamic limitations constrain single-pass
ethylene yields from ethane cracking to roughly 50% at high temperatures (up to 1100 °C on
the furnace side)6. Additionally, despite adding steam as a diluent, steam cracking’s proclivity
for coke formation requires periodic shutdowns of cracker furnaces for burnout, further
limiting ethylene output. Despite these shortcomings, steam cracking technology has matured

 8

to a point where technical constraints make increasing yield and product selectivity
challenging.

In response to growing environmental concerns, alternative olefin production pathways such
as oxidative dehydrogenation (ODH) and methane coupling are being investigated to reduce
energy consumption and greenhouse gas emission. These emerging technologies aim to
support global decarbonization goals while maintaining economic competitiveness7.
However, these technologies face various challenges as detailed in both Section 2.10 of the
proposal narrative and a highly cited perspective article by the PI4. The chemical looping
oxidative dehydrogenation (CL-ODH) of ethane was developed to address these limitations,
and it represents a highly promising approach.

1.2. Conventional and Chemical Looping Oxidative Dehydrogenation
In conventional ODH, ethane is partially oxidized to ethylene in the presence of co-fed oxygen
over a catalyst at moderate temperatures (400–600°C). From a purely thermodynamic
standpoint, this process represents an improvement relative to steam cracking, as it is
exothermic and not subject to equilibrium limitations. However, its practical appeal is limited.
On-site production of the high-purity oxygen co-feed requires costly air separation units, and
co-injection of hydrocarbons and oxygen at high temperatures presents a combustion hazard.

To overcome these issues, CL-ODH employs metal oxide oxygen carriers to transfer oxygen
internally in a cyclic, two-step redox loop, eliminating the need to introduce gaseous oxygen
directly8,9. These catalysts are typically tunable metal oxides with excellent solid state
conductivity to minimize diffusion limitations10,11.

Figure 1 shows the schematic of the ethane CL-ODH process and its key reactions. In the
reducing reactor, the metal oxide carriers act as redox catalysts, oxidatively dehydrogenating
C2H6 into C2H4 and water by donating oxygen from its lattice structure. Following their
reduction, the metal oxides particles are transported to a regeneration reactor where they
exothermically re-oxidize under air flow12. In transporting oxygen from the regenerator to the
reactor, the carrier precludes direct interaction between the feedstock and the gaseous

 9

oxidizer, thereby improving process safety and efficiency while also enhancing ethylene
selectivity4. Additionally, heat generation in the regeneration step can enable autothermal
functionality with fine-tuning of process operating and design parameters.

Figure 1. Schematic of the proposed ethane CL-ODH process and the key reactions. Dashed
line represents heat carried by the redox catalyst (denoted by MeOx when oxidized and
MeOx-1 when reduced).

The PI's research group was the first to propose the CL-ODH concept and holds four issued
patents covering a broad range of redox catalyst formulations and generalized redox catalyst
design principles. The current study presents a detailed techno-economic and environmental
assessment of our patented CL-ODH technology for sustainable ethylene production13.

2. CL-ODH Conceptual Design
The simplified block flow diagram and reactor schematic in Figure 2-Figure 3 depict the
conceptual process and reactor designs of the proposed CL-ODH technology, respectively.

 10

Figure 2. Simplified BFD of the proposed CL-ODH technology with each major subsystem
identified: (1) heat recovery and preheating; (2) catalyst regeneration reactor; (3) ethane ODH
reactor; (4) product gas quench; (5) product gas compression, acid gas removal, and
refrigeration; (6) cryogenic distillation; and (7) power generation.

Immediately downstream of the ODH reactor, transfer line heat exchangers and quench
towers commonplace in commercial cracking facilities recover heat from the ethylene-rich
product gas. Following cooling and water vapor removal, the gas is compressed and processed
through an acid gas removal system prior to refrigeration. A sequence of cryogenic distillation
towers then fractionates the cracked gas mixture into ethylene, C3+ olefins (e.g., propylene,
butadiene), and fuel gas components (CH₄, H₂, CO). A pressure-swing adsorption (PSA)
system recovers a portion of unreacted H₂ for tail-end acetylene hydrogenation. Our ASPEN
Plus® model and conceptual framework are informed by robust experimental data and

 11

incorporate industry-standard compressor and separation technologies. Circulating fluidized
bed (CFB) reactor dynamics are also substantiated by the PI’s prior research.

Figure 3. Representative schematic for a commercial-scale ethane CL-ODH CFB reactor
system.

Figure 3 illustrates a dual-reactor CFB configuration comprising a reducer and an oxidizer,
interconnected for continuous oxygen carrier circulation. In the reducer, ethane reacts with
the lattice oxygen of a solid oxygen carrier at 800–950°C, producing ethylene and water while
reducing the carrier. The depleted carrier is separated via cyclones and transferred to the
regenerator, where it is re-oxidized by air and releases heat that sustains the endothermic ODH
reaction. The re-oxidized carrier returns to the reducer, ensuring autothermal operation. High
gas velocities fluidize the carriers, enhancing gas-solid contact and heat transfer, while
cyclones and transfer lines maintain stable solid circulation,

 12

As evidenced in the following TEA, LCA, and in our cited peer-reviewed publications, CL-
ODH offers substantial reductions in energy consumption, CO₂ output, and operational costs.

3. Experimental Results

3.1. Na2WO4-promoted Mg6MnO8 as an Ethane CL-ODH Catalyst

In our previous study, Mg6MnO8 promoted with Na and W was investigated as a CL-ODH
catalyst10,14. Several Na:W molar ratios were used in impregnating Mg6MnO8 with sodium
tungstate dihydrate, or sodium nitrate and ammonium metatungstate. For the CL-ODH
testing, 500 mg of each catalyst (425-850 µm) was fixed at the bottom of a quartz U-tube by
quartz wool and Al2O3 grit on either side. The catalysts were subjected to redox cycles with a
reduction step consisting of 80% C2H6/Ar (5 mL C2H6 total) and a reoxidation step using 17%
O2/Ar. The product gases from the reduction half-cycle were collected in a gas sampling bag
and analyzed using gas chromatography (GC). The catalysts were tested using a gas hourly
space velocity (GHSV) of 4500 h-1 at 800°C, 825°C, and 850°C. The CL-ODH results using
these catalysts are summarized in Figure 4.

 13

Figure 4. CL-ODH reaction data for Mg6MnO8 catalysts promoted with Na and W at GHSV
= 4500 h-1 and T = 800°C (green), 825°C (blue), and 850°C (red); (a) ethylene selectivity vs
ethane conversion and (b) COx selectivity vs. ethane conversion10.

Promotion of the redox catalysts resulted in improved ethylene yield, with H2 conversion
exceeding 70%. The catalyst promoted with Na2WO4 resulted in a maximum ethylene yield
of 62.17%, with a COx selectivity of 8.3% at 850°C. From further characterization, it was
deduced that the Na2WO4 functions as a promoter by forming a molten salt shell which
transports lattice oxygen from the Mg6MnO8 core to selectively combust H2 at the gas-molten
salt interface. This catalyst formulation represented an improvement in single-pass ethylene
yield and consequently a promising design strategy for CL-ODH catalysts.

 14

3.2. Advanced NaMgW CL-ODH Catalyst Formulations

In a follow-up study, Na4Mg(WO4)3 (abbreviated hereafter as NaMgW) on Mg6MnO8 was
investigated as a catalytic promoter for ethane CL-ODH and demonstrated markedly reduced
COx selectivity relative to promotion of the same support with Na2WO415. During Phase I, a
secondary promoter phase was physically mixed with NaMgW-Mg6MnO8 to further enhance
C3-5 olefin yield, and two sets of additional experiments were conducted using this advanced
catalyst material. In the first set of CL-ODH experiments, 500 mg of 20 wt.% NaMgW on
Mg6MnO8 was loaded into a quartz U-tube, secured with quartz wool, and packed with Al₂O₃
grit on both ends. No secondary promoter phase was included. In the second set, 250 mg of
the novel secondary promoter was physically mixed with NaMgW-Mg6MnO8 and packed
similarly. In both groupings, each material was pelletized and sieved to a size range of 425–
850 µm. During each reduction half-cycle, a total of 5 mL of C₂H₆ (as 80% C₂H₆ in Ar) was
introduced at 800°C, 825°C, and 850°C. The product gases were collected in a gas sampling
bag and analyzed via GC. Both catalyst formulations were tested at a normalized GHSV of
4500 h⁻¹. The mixed bed (i.e., NaMgW-Mg6MnO8 + secondary promoter) was also
subsequently evaluated at 3600 h⁻¹.

4.1.3. CO2 Capture and Compression (CCC)
In the steam cracking processes, CO2 emissions originate from two major flue gas streams
(Figure 6-Figure 7 and Table 8a for molar ratio, Table 8b for molar flow rate, and Table 8c
for mass flow rate). The first is produced by the combustion of natural gas in the furnace,
which provides the heat required for the highly endothermic cracking reactions. The second
arises from the power generation unit, where fuel gases—primarily methane (CH4), H2, and
CO—are combusted to generate electricity for operating downstream units such as
compressors, refrigeration systems, and other separation equipment. Compositions of gaseous
streams input to amine CCC units are shown in Table 8a-c. Note, the cracking product stream
is cleaned via a relatively small caustic wash with NaOH unit to produce impurities/acid gas,
which has a very low concentration (ppm-scale) of CO2, and is therefore not captured.

In the SOTA + CCC configuration, the overall process remains largely the same as in the base
SOTA system, in which CO2 emissions originate from two primary flue gas streams (Figure
7 and Table 8a-c). However, an additional amount of CH4 is combusted in power generation

 23

to meet the increased energy demand associated with operating the CO2 capture units. The
additional methane combustion increases CO2 concentration in flue gas moderately. The CO2
concentration in the cracking furnace flue gas (9.5 mol%) falls within the typical range of 7–
10 mol% observed in steam cracking processes under lean combustion conditions with CH4
being the only fuel component16. The CO2 concentration in the power generation flue gas (4.0
mol%) is expected given the substantial amount of H2 in the fuel gas mixture.

In the ODH + RE + CCC configuration, the CO2 stream exhibits the same characteristics as
the primary stream in the ODH + CCC system (Figure 9 and Table 8a-c)—originating from
the ODH reactor with a CO2 concentration of approximately 3.5 mol% and containing a

ethylene, methane, acetylene, propylene, 1,3-butadiene, benzene, toluene, and other light
hydrocarbons, along with carbon monoxide and hydrogen. In this configuration, the energy
required for downstream operations—such as CO2 compression, separation, and
refrigeration—is supplied entirely by renewable electricity. As a result, the secondary
combustion-based CO2 stream, typically associated with the burning of fuel gas and
supplemental fossil fuels, is eliminated. This approach not only lowers total CO2 emissions
but also enhances the overall carbon intensity and sustainability of the process by avoiding
additional combustion-related emissions.

A CO2 capture efficiency of 92% was used for all process configurations evaluated in this
study. This value aligns closely with the 90% capture efficiency commonly adopted in U.S.
Department of Energy National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL) baseline reports for
post-combustion CO2 capture systems17. The selected 92% reflects a slightly more optimistic

yet realistic performance target for advanced capture technologies like amine-based
absorption and is within the range typically considered feasible for commercial-scale
deployment. This assumption ensures consistency with widely accepted benchmarking
studies and facilitates meaningful comparisons across technology scenarios17,18 . With this
capture efficiency, the overall CO2 reduction is represented as 95% (based on the SOTA
benchmark) for the CL-ODH+CCC process. In the ODH + RE + CCC configuration—where
renewable electricity replaces fossil fuel combustion for downstream energy needs—the total
CO2 reduction increases further, reaching up to 98% reduction from SOTA.

for over 70% of the total energy process, making furnace heating the single largest consumer

To ensure results were representative of commercial-scale operations, a refinery capacity of

calculated ethane cost at 10% above methane. For CL-ODH configurations using electricity,
we applied renewable electricity price of $0.117/kWh, which reflects average rates in wind-
and solar-rich regions34. The hydrogen selling price was estimated as twice the methane price,
consistent with market expectations for blue or green hydrogen35. Additional product
revenues were modeled using selling prices of $900/t for propylene, $1,200/t for 1,3-
butadiene, and $1,000/t for other high-value hydrocarbons. CL-ODH utilizes an oxygen
carrier catalyst, modeled with a unit cost of $8,000/t, a catalyst mass of 400 tonnes, and an
attrition rate of 0.28 wt%/day.

Capital process contingencies were applied selectively based on the technology readiness
level of each section: A 30% process contingency was applied to the CL-ODH reactor system,

early-stage processes. A 10% contingency was applied to the cracking reactor, which is based
on commercially mature technology. A 10% contingency was applied to the CO2 capture and

integration. A standard 10% contingency was also applied to the remaining sections—

 39

including pre-heating, heat recovery, compression, refrigeration, and separation, which are all

The owner’s costs are also included in the reported capital cost estimates, consistent with the
methodology outlined in the NETL’s Quality Guidelines for Energy System Studies (QGESS)
documents, specifically the Cost Estimation Methodology for NETL Assessments of Power
Plant Performance (February 2021)30. According to QGESS guidance, the following owner’s
cost components were considered and included as part of the Total Overnight Cost (TOC):

  Preproduction costs (e.g., 6 months of operating labor, consumables, and waste
 handling)
  Inventory capital (e.g., spare parts, 60-day supply of non-fuel consumables)
  Land cost
  Financing costs (estimated at ~2.7% of TPC)
  Other owner’s costs (estimated at 15% of TPC), covering permitting, legal, owner’s
 engineering, and startup contingencies.

In total, a composite owner’s cost factor of approximately 20% of TPC was applied in the
analysis, in alignment with NETL’s recommended approach. These costs are reflected in the
TOC and support consistency with NETL-style TEA methodology.

4.2.1.2. Operating Expenditure (OPEX) Estimation

For OPEX estimation, costs related to solvent consumption, water usage, and waste handling
were calculated using data from the NETL Natural Gas Combined Cycle (NGCC) CO2
Capture report17. The energy demand associated with CO2 drying and compression was met
by electricity generated onsite via methane combustion, except for the renewable energy case.
Equations derived from two NETL studies36 were used to estimate energy demand for CO2
drying and compression (also the same equations that were used in the DOE Getting to
Neutral report37). Furthermore, labor costs were included in the annual fixed operating costs.
The fixed cost estimates account for the salaries of essential plant personnel, including plant
manager, plant engineers, maintenance supervisors, lab managers, shift supervisors, lab

 40

technicians, maintenance technicians, shift operators, yard employees, and clerks &
secretaries. A 60% overhead was applied to the total salary cost to account for benefits,
administrative expenses, and other indirect costs. In addition to labor, fixed operating costs
also include maintenance expenses estimated at 2% of the total direct capital cost, and
insurance costs estimated at 3% of the total direct capital cost. These assumptions are
consistent with standard industry practices and applied across all cases to ensure even
comparison.

4.2.1.3. Air Ingress Handling and Impact on TEA

In both steam cracking and CL-ODH processes, air ingress can affect energy efficiency,
system performance, and product selectivity. According to NETL guidelines, air ingress in
high-temperature systems like steam crackers is typically 0.5–2% of the total process gas
flow. This introduces oxygen and nitrogen, which dilute reactants, increase fuel consumption,
and promote coke formation. In the CL-ODH process, air ingresses a concern due to the need
to maintain a reducing environment. NETL recommend limiting ingress to below 1% to
prevent unintended hydrocarbon oxidation. In both cases, a small fraction of products (up to
~1%) may be lost. Based on our techno-economic sensitivity analysis air ingress is a relatively
minor contributor to overall levelized cost, and thus a detailed discussion was not included in
the TEA report. Standard air ingress control measures such as maintaining positive pressure,
nitrogen purging, and LDAR programs, will be implemented in the full TEA.

4.3. Preliminary Life Cycle Analysis

The preliminary LCA presents gate-to-gate and cradle-to-gate CO2 emissions for four
ethylene production (1 Mt ethylene per year) pathways. The analysis demonstrated superior
environmental performance of the CL-ODH technology compared to the SOTA ethane steam
cracking process. For the gate-to-gate LCA, the conventional SOTA process emits 0.498 tCO2
per tonne of ethylene with no CCC. By incorporating carbon capture and compression,
SOTA+CCC reduces net emissions to 0.049 tCO2 per tonne of ethylene, achieving a 90%
reduction relative to the SOTA baseline. The CL-ODH+CCC pathway further lowers net CO2
emissions to 0.0256 tCO2 per tonne of ethylene (95% reduction), while the integration of
renewable electricity in the CL-ODH+RE+CCC system results in a 98% reduction relative to
SOTA. These results meet the DOE’s minimum emissions reduction threshold (>95% CO2
emissions reduction relative to SOTA) and highlight the transformational potential of CL-
ODH in achieving near-zero emission ethylene production.

4.3.1. Goal and Scope

The goal of this preliminary LCA is to evaluate the potential environmental impacts associated
with the deployment of an oxygen-based decarbonized industrial process, specifically
focusing on chemical looping and/or oxy-combustion technologies. This assessment aims to
identify environmental hotspots, quantify energy and carbon footprints, and provide early-
stage sustainability insights that inform technology optimization, design decisions, and
research prioritization. The LCA also serves as a comparative benchmark against conventional
industrial practices, supporting the U.S. DOE’s mission to advance economically viable and
low-carbon process alternatives. This analysis will guide future development by aligning
environmental performance with techno-economic feasibility, ultimately facilitating the
transition toward net-zero industrial operations.

The scope of this preliminary LCA includes a cradle-to-gate boundary encompassing
feedstock acquisition, oxygen-based process operations, and product separation units. All
major subsystems of the proposed decarbonized process will be evaluated, including material

 55

and energy inputs, reaction kinetics, separation technologies, and utility demands. Key
performance indicators will be estimated using available Phase 1 data, such as mass and
energy balances, heating and cooling duties, and electricity requirements. The functional unit
is defined as the production of one metric ton of target chemical (e.g., ethylene), allowing for
consistent environmental performance comparisons across technological alternatives. The
assessment adopts a process-based modeling approach using standard LCA methodologies,
with results intended for internal guidance and to fulfill DOE Phase 2 application
requirements.

4.3.1.1. Functional Unit of the Study
The functional unit of this preliminary LCA is defined as the production of one metric tonne
of ethylene via the proposed oxygen-based decarbonized industrial process. This unit provides
a consistent basis for quantifying environmental inputs and outputs, enabling comparative
evaluations against conventional ethane steam cracking. All material and energy flows,
emissions, and process efficiencies are normalized to this reference quantity to ensure
consistency and reproducibility in the assessment. This functional unit is particularly suited
to support early-stage design decisions and aligns with the DOE’s requirement to assess the
sustainability of innovative low-carbon technologies at a system level.

4.3.1.2. System Boundary

The system boundary for this preliminary LCA adopts a cradle-to-gate and gate-to-gate
approach, encompassing all major stages from feedstock acquisition to the final separation of
ethylene. It includes upstream processes such as raw material extraction, core process
operations like chemical looping, and downstream utilities including heating, cooling, and
electricity usage in product separation. The analysis covers key unit operations based on
available Phase 1 design and experimental data, such as reactors, heat exchangers,
compressors, and separators. Infrastructure construction, plant decommissioning, and
downstream product use are excluded at this stage due to data limitations and the preliminary
nature of the study. This boundary ensures the assessment captures all relevant environmental

 56

impacts associated with the ethylene production process up to the plant gate, consistent with
DOE reporting requirements.

4.3.1.3. Carbon Dioxide Source and Utilization

In the proposed ODH process, carbon dioxide is primarily generated from the power
generation block, which combusts natural gas and fuel gas byproducts from the ethane
conversion to satisfy the parasitic power requirement. In addition, CO2 is a by-product during
the partial oxidation of ethane within the chemical looping system. The oxygen carrier
facilitates selective oxidative dehydrogenation of ethane to ethylene, minimizing complete
combustion but still producing a small amount of CO2. In systems equipped with carbon
capture and compression, the process is designed to separate the CO2, which can be either
permanently sequestered in geological formations or directed toward utilization pathways.
The LCA includes direct CO2 emissions and captured CO2 flows, supporting an accurate
evaluation of net carbon impacts and alignment with U.S. DOE decarbonization targets.

4.3.1.4. Technology Representativeness

The technology assessed in this preliminary LCA represents an emerging oxygen-based
decarbonization pathway, specifically utilizing CL-ODH for ethylene production. The
performance data are obtained from laboratory-scale experiments performed both before and
during Phase I. The system design is developed through process modeling conducted during
Phase 1 of the project. As such, representativeness reflects a pre-commercial, pilot-scale
configuration, incorporating realistic engineering assumptions and industry-relevant
operating conditions. Aside from the CL-ODH reactor system, which is the key innovative
component, all other unit operations in the ethylene production process use proven
technologies consistent with those in state-of-the-art ethane crackers. The amine-based CO2
capture system is based on the NETL report titled Methodology for Estimating Performance
and Cost of Natural Gas Combined Cycle Plants with Carbon Capture17. While full-scale
deployment of the CL-ODH reactor system is not yet realized, the technology configuration
is designed to simulate future commercial operation scenarios with integrated carbon capture.
This preliminary analysis serves to evaluate potential environmental benefits and identify key

 57

impact drivers prior to scale-up, aligning with DOE's intent to support early-stage, high-
impact decarbonization technologies.

4.3.1.5. Geographical Representativeness

The geographical representativeness of this preliminary LCA is based on deployment within
the United States, reflecting the regional energy mix, industrial infrastructure, and regulatory
landscape relevant to U.S. DOE-funded projects. The assessment assumes access to U.S.
average grid electricity and domestic sources of ethane feedstock, which is consistent with
shale gas availability in regions such as the Gulf Coast. Emission factors, utility profiles, and
background life cycle inventories are to maintain consistency and relevance. While site-
specific variables such as transport distances or localized energy tariffs are not yet
incorporated, the results provide a representative snapshot for national-scale implementation
and policy evaluation.

4.3.1.6. Temporal Representativeness

The temporal representativeness of this preliminary LCA corresponds to a near-future
deployment horizon, with environmental performance modeled using data representative of
current (2023–2025) technology assumptions and market conditions. Process parameters are
based on experimental results and simulation outputs developed during Phase 1, which reflect
the present state of system development and engineering design. Background data sources
such as emission factors, grid electricity mixes, and material inventories are aligned with the
most recent publicly available U.S. datasets. Although long-term projections, policy shifts,
and energy system transformations (e.g., increased renewable penetration) are not explicitly
modeled, the assessment provides a relevant baseline for evaluating the technology’s current
sustainability potential and its readiness for scale-up in the next 5–10 years.

4.3.1.7. Life Cycle Impact Assessment Methods for Results Interpretation

The Life Cycle Impact Assessment (LCIA) calculations are performed using life cycle
inventory data normalized to the functional unit (1 metric ton of ethylene). The selected
impact methods provide a balanced framework to support early-stage environmental

 58

benchmarking of low-carbon ethylene production technologies under DOE’s clean energy
transition goals.

4.3.1.8. Completeness Requirements

The completeness of this preliminary LCA is evaluated in terms of system boundary coverage,
data availability, and flow accounting. The study includes all major cradle-to-gate stages
relevant to ethylene production via oxygen-based decarbonized technologies, covering
feedstock acquisition, oxygen carrier usage, core reaction systems (e.g., CL-ODH), utility
consumption, and product separation. While downstream use, infrastructure, and
decommissioning are excluded due to early-stage development, the included processes
capture the primary environmental impacts. Data completeness is ensured through a
combination of Phase 1 experimental results, process simulations (e.g., Aspen Plus®), and
secondary datasets from GREET, eGRID, and U.S. Life Cycle Inventory (LCI) databases.
Engineering assumptions and proxies are used where necessary, with clear documentation.
Material and energy flows are tracked relative to the defined functional unit, ensuring
sufficient coverage to support robust, comparative environmental analysis aligned with DOE
objectives.

4.3.1.9. Sensitivity and Uncertainty Analysis

Sensitivity analysis is conducted to evaluate the influence of key input parameters and
modeling assumptions on the overall environmental performance of the proposed oxygen-
based ethylene production system. Given the early-stage nature of the technology and reliance
on modeled data, parameters such as ethane conversion efficiency, oxygen carrier
regeneration rates, utility demands, and CO2 capture efficiency are varied within plausible
bounds to assess their impact on life cycle greenhouse gas emissions and energy use. The
analysis helps identify the most influential variables of driving system performance, prioritize
areas for process optimization, and understand the robustness of results under uncertainty.
This approach supports decision-making during early technology development and aligns
with the U.S. Department of Energy’s requirement to characterize system-level variability in
performance assessments.

4.3.2.9. Results of Sensitivity Check

In our LCA, we evaluated two boundary conditions: (1) cradle-to-gate and (2) gate-to-gate.
The gate-to-gate case was subject to a DOE requirement of at least 95% greenhouse gas
(GHG) reduction relative to the conventional baseline. During our preliminary analysis, we
found that variations in process parameters in this case often reduced GHG performance
below the required 95% threshold. As a result, we held gate-to-gate parameters constant to
ensure compliance with program requirements.

In contrast, the cradle-to-gate case was conducted for informational purposes only, without a
mandated emissions threshold. This allowed us to explore the effects of varying upstream
parameters—particularly feedstock production and natural gas supply chains—on net GHG
emissions. These upstream parameters were more influential in the cradle-to-gate context,
largely due to the high global warming potential of methane leakage, which dominates
sensitivity in this portion of the system. Moving forward, we agree that including gate-to-gate
parameters in the sensitivity analysis would be valuable and plan to incorporate them in future
work.

Our comprehensive TEA and LCA, combined with experimental findings, confirm that the

  At least a 15% cost reduction relative to retrofitted SOTA processes with carbon

economically viable and environmentally sustainable, offering a promising alternative to
legacy ethylene manufacturing methods.

5. Technology Maturation Plan (TMP)
5.1. Introduction

5.1.1. Purpose of the Project
The project aims to demonstrate our patented CL-ODH technology for sustainable and cost-
effective ethylene production12,13,47,48. Briefly speaking, the CL-ODH process uses a
bifunctional, catalytically active oxygen carrier, also known as a redox catalyst (RC), to
integrate ethane ODH with redox based oxygen separation, rendering an autothermal ethylene
production process with a high olefin yield and a decreased downstream separation load. Our
extensive experimental data, techno-economic analysis (TEA), and life cycle analysis (LCA)
indicate that, even without renewable energy input or carbon sequestration, the process
intensification offered by CL-ODH would enable more than 80% energy saving compared to
state-of-the-art (SOTA) steam cracking process and up to an 87% reduction in CO2 emission
through process improvements alone (i.e. without carbon capture or renewable energy
inputs).32,49 Switching to renewable energy for the separation block in CL-ODH would lead
to >95% emissions reduction along with >20% cost savings, proving a clear pathway for net
zero ethylene.

The overall objective of this Phase I project is to validate the environmental and economic
attractiveness of the chemical looping–oxidative dehydrogenation (CL-ODH) technology via
detailed techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life cycle analysis (LCA). The TEA and LCA
work is primarily built upon extensive experimental data obtained from a robust redox catalyst
(1,400+ cycles in a fluidized bed) and a circulating fluidized bed design based on previous
cold model studies. During Phase I, we are validating the performance of a new generation of
redox catalysts, which will further improve product selectivity and determine their impact on
the process economics and LCA emissions. The target is to achieve >95% CO2 emission
reduction when compared to state-of-the-art (SOTA) thermal cracking technology while
reducing the cost of ethylene by >20%, per the FOA. We are also developing a detailed plan
for Phase II, which will involve long-term experimental validation of the CL-ODH technology
to ready it for pilot-scale demonstration.

after the compression step. Depending on the process configuration, additional separation

been elaborated in the TEA report to further reduce the CO2 emission from CL-ODH. Our
conceptual design and ASPEN Plus® model are supported by extensive experimental data
(Figure 15-Figure 17) and have fully considered the compressor and separator designs and
configurations used in commercial cracking and gas separation plants.

 3. Further validate the CL-ODH technology in a sub-pilot scale circulating fluidized bed

 4. Demonstration of the advanced catalyst in the CFB for 100+ hours;
 5. Detailed LCA and TEA validating the ability to achieve ≥20% cost reduction and
 ≥97% carbon emission reduction;
 6. Perform market analysis and a detailed design of a pre-commercial demo unit to ready
 the technology for post-Phase II scale up and commercialization.

Through such experimental activities, we will not only validate the CL-ODH performance
under an operating mode similar to the anticipated commercial-scale reactor but also obtain
important data about the redox catalyst performance that would otherwise be difficult to
obtain in typical academic lab research. For instance, the attrition resistance of the redox

catalyst at high solids circulation rates and velocities in risers and cyclones will be determined.
Completing Phase II work will therefore advance the technology to TRL 5.

completion of the Phase II project, we anticipate collaborating with SABIC to carry out the

adequate technical data and experience to move the technology towards commercial
demonstration (~50,000 tons/year).

5.3.1. Definition of TRA System

NETL’s interpretation (Section 6.3.2) of the DOE TRL definitions (Section 6.3.3) is based on
a view of technology maturation in which “components” are integrated into a “system” that
is being assessed for its technology readiness. To clearly and consistently apply the DOE
TRL definitions, one must first precisely identify what “system” is being assessed, defined
herein as the “Technology Readiness Assessment (TRA) System.” Since most technologies
can be viewed as subsystems within larger systems, multiple choices are available for defining
the TRA System. However, note that the choice of the “level” of the TRA System affects
how TRLs are assessed:

  A TRL 3 is achieved for the specified TRA System when analytical performance
 predictions for each of the TRA System’s critical1 components have been validated in
 separate experiments (i.e., without integration across components). Accordingly, the
 table in Section 6.3.2 shows the required scope of TRL 3 as “single component” and
 the required integration of TRL 3 as “none.”

1
  A component or subsystem of a TRA System is considered critical if it is new, novel, and necessary for the
TRA System to meet its anticipated operational performance requirements or poses major cost, schedule, or
performance risk during design or demonstration. Note that a component that is fully mature and non-critical
for an established application or operational environment may be considered critical if it is incorporated into a
new application or operational environment.

 85

  A TRL 4 or 5 is achieved for a given TRA System when the targeted performance
 requirements for each of its critical, multi-component subsystems (or the entire TRA
 system) have been validated in a laboratory environment (TRL 4) or relevant
 environment (TRL 5) with integration of some or all components.
  Achieving TRLs 6 to 9 requires testing of the entire, fully integrated, TRL system.

To further clarify, consider, for example, a fuel cell stack. Its critical components are multiple,
identical fuel cells. In turn, the critical components of each fuel cell are an anode, cathode and
electrolyte. If one wished to assess the technology readiness of the fuel cell stack, the TRA
System would be defined as an integrated system of multiple fuel cell subsystems, and a TRL
6 could only be achieved by successfully testing an entire stack of integrated fuel cells.
However, if one instead wished to assess the technology readiness of only the fuel cell, the
TRA System would be defined as an integrated system of cathode, anode and electrolyte
components, and a TRL 6 could be achieved by successfully testing just a single, integrated
fuel cell. In both cases, achievement of TRL 6 could be claimed, but only in the context of
the properly specified TRA System.

5.3.4. NETL Systems Analysis Best Practices
NETL has developed Systems Analysis Best Practices (SABP) as an accompaniment to the
DOE Technology Readiness Level (TRL) definitions. The SABP serve as a guide for the
Principal Investigator/researcher to inform on the level of systems and economic analysis
rigor appropriate at each TRL.

System and economic analyses are an essential component of research and development
(R&D). They are used to determine appropriate experimental conditions, inform R&D targets
and technology maturation plans, assess R&D progress, and estimate the benefits of
successful technology development in commercial applications.

Systems analysis is the analytic process used to evaluate the behavior and performance of
processes, equipment, subsystems, and systems. Such analyses serve to characterize the
relationships between independent (e.g., design parameters and configurations, material
properties, etc.) and dependent variables (e.g., thermodynamic state points, output, etc.)
through the creation of models representative of the envisioned process, equipment,
subsystem, or system. These analyses are used to determine the important variables (i.e.,
performance attributes) and the associated targets (i.e., performance requirements) that must
be achieved through R&D and testing to realize commercial and/or program goals.

The performance requirements are selected such that the equipment, subsystem, or system
meets the envisioned objectives in the target commercial application. The target commercial

 92

application refers to one specific use for the advanced technology, at full commercial scale.
A project may include more than one target commercial application. For example:

 1. Technologies that reduce the cost of gasification may be useful for both liquid fuels
 and power production.
 2. Technologies that may be useful to monitor CO2 storage in more than one type of
 storage site.

The modeling and simulation effort may use one or more of a variety of tools, such as Excel,
MATLAB, Aspen Plus, Aspen Plus Dynamics, Thermoflow, CHEMCAD, etc., depending
upon suitability to the specific processes, the scope of the development effort, and the stage
of development.

An integral part of systems analysis is economic analysis - the process of estimating and
assigning costs to equipment, subsystems, and systems corresponding to models of and
specifications for the commercial embodiment of the technology. Such analyses include the
estimation of capital costs, as well as operating and maintenance costs. Component service
life and corresponding replacement costs are often a crucial aspect of these analyses. See
Performing a Techno-economic Analysis for Power Generation Plants, DOE/NETL-
2015/1726, July 2015, for further guidance.

As a technology matures, the systems analyses are frequently updated and are expected to
increase in fidelity and complexity commensurate with the available technical understanding,
experimental data, and overall level of effort (cost of R&D). The results are used to inform
the next stage of development and provide specific experimental and analysis success criteria
(the performance requirements).

As a general principle, the performance requirements that may be appropriately tested at a
particular TRL must be substantially met, thereby supporting the feasibility of commercial
success/goal achievement, prior to proceeding to the subsequent TRL. Note that, as with the
TRL descriptions, these SABP are “gate-in;” that is, prerequisites to achieving the associated
TRL.

 93

NETL supports a wide range of RD&D projects, from small, short-duration materials
development and property characterization projects up to large-scale power plant
demonstrations. The nature and complexity of the technology under development and the
scope of the project must be considered when applying the SABP – they may not be strictly
applicable as written to every project. For example, it is an unreasonable expectation for a
project developing a sensor, or fuel cell cathode, or thermal boundary coating for a turbine
airfoil to perform a full-scale power plant simulation to determine the performance
requirements of the specific technology while pursuing TRL 4. However, the project must
explicitly tie the quantitative goals/objectives for the technology to referenced system studies
as well as relevant industry and/or market requirements in such a manner that their pedigree
is readily traceable. On the other hand, a project endeavoring to develop a full system concept
incorporating novel components and process integration is expected to perform more robust,
extensive analyses.

hazardous, affecting disposal options. The Toxic Substances Control Act may require
additional reporting if novel chemicals or modified catalysts are introduced. SARA Title III,
under EPCRA, will mandate emergency planning and spill reporting for certain hazardous
substances.

We would have to do a site-specific regulatory requirement list (particularly state-level
variations), conduct a detailed hazard assessment for spent catalysts, and evaluate waste
minimization strategies such as catalyst regeneration and metal bioaccumulation. Compliance
with air emission thresholds is something commonly dealt with by gas services companies.

In Phase II, we will work with project partner SABIC to develop a more detailed plan for
compliance review.

The compounds of interest for potential water and air emissions (if not properly mitigated)
are inherent to natural gas processing, and the amounts produced are unlikely to be

significantly reduced through engineering controls aside from potential improvements in
overall process efficiency.

Likewise, solid waste from the catalyst can be mitigated by an increase in catalyst life and/or
productivity. Metal compounds are inherent to the catalyst selectivity. While further
optimization could lower the loading, significant reductions are unlikely. No lower-hazard
alternatives have demonstrated comparable activity or selectivity. Future work may evaluate
doping strategies or support modifications to reduce hazardous component concentrations
without loss of function.

6) Precautions for safe handling and conditions for safe storage shall be identified,
 including any incompatibilities with other materials that may be used in the process.
 Waste treatment and offsite disposal options shall be examined. Accidental release
 measures shall also be discussed.

An examination of the potential waste has identified that existing safe handling, mitigation,
and disposal practices exist for many of the identified hazards within the context of gas
processing. The solid spent catalysts were identified as novel and therefore determined to be
of most concern due to their relative novelty. While conventional personal protective
equipment and solid hazardous waste disposal procedures should be adequate, these impacts
will be studied further in Phase II. Suggested practices as they relate to the proposed fluidized
bed system are detailed as follows:

 Safe Handling
 Catalyst should be handled in enclosed systems or under local exhaust ventilation (LEV)
 to minimize worker exposure and environmental release of dust. Personnel should wear
 gloves, safety goggles, and respiratory protection as appropriate. Avoid direct contact with
 acids or moisture, which may induce unwanted reactivity or corrosion.

 Storage Conditions

 103

Store catalyst materials in sealed containers in a cool, dry, well-ventilated area. Prevent
exposure to high humidity and segregate from incompatible chemicals such as strong
acids and oxidizers. Secondary containment is recommended for bulk catalyst storage.

Incompatibilities
Incompatible with strong acids (e.g., HCl, HNO₃), which can lead to the release of toxic
gases or dissolved metal species. Avoid storage near ammonium salts. We note that the
plant will handle neither strong acid nor ammonium salts. Moisture may cause partial
hydrolysis or leaching of Li, Na, and B compounds.

Waste Treatment and Disposal
Spent catalyst must be characterized for hazardous constituents using TCLP protocols.
Reuse or regeneration is preferred; otherwise, disposal must follow RCRA regulations for
hazardous (Subtitle C) or non-hazardous (Subtitle D) solid waste. Water treatment
systems must remove trace metals and neutralize pH before any permitted discharge.
Baghouses and water treatment residues must be regularly cleaned and managed as
regulated waste.

Accidental Release Measures
Dry catalyst spills should be contained and cleaned using HEPA-filtered vacuums or wet
sweeping to avoid dust dispersion. Liquid-phase releases (e.g., catalyst slurries) should be
contained with inert absorbents, then disposed of in chemical waste containers. Spills must
be prevented from entering drains or waterways; reportable quantities must be disclosed
per CERCLA rules. Emergency responders must use appropriate PPE and follow the
facility's spill response protocol.

7. Technology Gap Analysis

1) Brief review of the process under investigation. This should include the overall
 configuration of the process and a summary of the potential advantages of the process in
 terms of Performance, Cost, Emissions, Market, and Safety Metrics.

The process under investigation is a chemical looping scheme that uses a bifunctional,
catalytically active oxygen carrier (i.e., a redox catalyst) composed of Na/Mg/Mn/W (and
potentially Li and/or B) to oxidatively dehydrogenate C2H6 into ethylene. Circumventing
both the thermodynamic limitations of SOTA steam cracking and the safety and practicality
concerns surrounding oxygen co-injection under conventional ODH, ethane CL-ODH
represents a promising route for reducing the carbon intensity of ethylene. The process’
energy savings relative to steam cracking stem from i) higher per-pass yield, (ii) reduced
product gas volume for compression via H2 combustion, (iii) avoidance of steam usage, (iv)
the exothermic ODH reaction combined with lower operating temperature, and (iv) advanced
energy integration.

Figure 1. Simplified schematic of the proposed ethane CL-ODH concept and key benefits
relative to steam cracking and conventional ODH.

Although a commercial-scale chemical looping process has yet to be developed, the novelty
of the proposed process arises largely from its redox carrier: Na4Mg(WO4)3-promoted
Mg6MnO8. Previous works from our group have identified Na2WO4-promoted Mg6MnO8 as
catalytically active for ethane CL-ODH10,14. In our most recent study, Na4Mg(WO4)3-

 105

promoted Mg6MnO8 (referred to as NaMgW) demonstrated further improvements to activity,
C2H4 and C3+ yields, H2 conversion, and COx suppression relative to its monotungstate
precursor15. Jet cup testing by PSRI has also highlighted the base material’s robustness, with
its attrition resistance notably surpassing that of the SOTA industry FCC catalyst. The
mechanical durability, high catalytic activity, widespread availability of its raw materials, and
low toxicity of the NaMgW material make it well-suited for deployment in a commercial
ethane CL-ODH fluidized bed.

4) For commercially available equipment, the potential vendors should be identified. A
 description of how the equipment offered by the vendor fits the requirements of the process
 should also be included.

Several vendors globally specialize in commercial-scale fluidized bed reactor systems and
catalyst synthesis infrastructure that are directly applicable to the high-temperature, solids-
circulating requirements of the proposed chemical looping oxidative dehydrogenation (CL-
ODH) process. These vendors offer solutions for either the reactor hardware, the catalyst, or
both.

Regarding reactor vendors, our group recently acquired a sub-pilot scale (~25 kg/day)
circulating fluidized bed reactor from SEIN Technologies. While originally designed for
biomass-based chemical looping, this unit is designed to operate at the CL-ODH operating
temperature range (800 – 950°C) and with compatible fluidization regimes and solids
circulation rate ranges that would support an ethylene production rate of 25 – 50 kg/day. These
features make it well-suited for retrofit to ethane CL-ODH service, especially given the
similar gas-solid contacting and redox cycling requirements. SEIN’s modular design supports
experimental campaigns under oxidative and reducing environments typical of ethane partial

 113

oxidation. For future scale-up, several vendors offer commercially available large-scale CFB
reactor systems, including Metso, Hatch, and Heyl Patterson among many others.

Catalyst manufacturing and supply will be outsourced to experienced toll manufacturers with
spray drying capabilities, such as Johnson Matthey, Clariant, PIDC, or Evonik. Spray drying
is a mature and scalable method, widely used for manufacturing catalysts in FCC, automotive,
and syngas industries. It enables the creation of spherical, flowable particles with narrow
particle size distributions, which ensures uniform fluidization and gas-solid contact. For the
proposed CL-ODH system, this manufacturing approach supports rapid deployment at both
pilot and commercial scales. The listed suppliers can also tune catalyst performance and
durability as needed by tailoring particle porosity, morphology, and formulation, in addition
to providing quality control to ensure batch reproducibility.
