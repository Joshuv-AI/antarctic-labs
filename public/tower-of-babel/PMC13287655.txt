Trends in circadian rhythms research in fungi since the millenium
Authors: Jay C Dunlap
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFoundational work in Neurospora set the stage for recent enormous gains in understanding fungal clocks, revealing in depth their regulatory architecture which parallels that of animal clocks. Mechanistic descriptions of photoreception and light-resetting and a complete inventory of core components accompanied quantal leaps in understanding how components work together to build a compensated clock. These advances are contributing to understanding clocks in myriad other fungi including several of agricultural importance.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Neuroscience

Introduction

Before 2000, circadian research on fungi was almost exclusively done on Neurospora crassa where it existed primarily as a proving ground for ideas that could be applied to animal clocks (e.g. ref. 1). In this regard the system might be said to have succeeded spectacularly. Unlike the case of plants and cyanobacteria, fungal and animal clocks turned out to be based on nearly identical regulatory architectures (Fig. 1)2–4. Indeed, work on Neurospora provided a number of precedents including understanding circadian output as clock-controlled genes (ccgs)5, the core clock as a single step transcriptional negative feedback loop6 involving a heterodimer of proteins interacting via PAS domains as a positive element7, and the means by which light8,9) and temperature10 reset the core clock. By the turn of the millennium most of the basic elements of the core feedback loop had been described11 and research was turning towards understanding the mechanisms by which individual components interacted to keep time, and the pathways through which this timing information was propagated to the cell/syncytium as a whole. Unanticipated at that time was the wave of research that built upon the Neurospora canon to understand the ways in which circadian systems contribute to the biologies of various other species in the fungal kingdom.

An apologia is perhaps warranted at the outset. Since 2000, PubMed cites roughly 500 papers under “Neurospora AND circadian” and yet the strictures for this survey piece request no more than 60 citations. While this limit has been pushed up a great deal thanks to a cooperative editor, necessarily, a number of high-quality works have remained uncited. I’ve attempted to subset the 500+ published works into themes wherein I’ll cite seminal or watershed papers, relying on reviews to direct readers to greater depth. Beyond this, I’ve strived to be comprehensible at the expense of being less than comprehensive.

Circadian Input

Although the Neurospora clock can be reset by both light and temperature cues (reviewed in ref. 12), nearly all work has focused on photoresponses wherein a wealth of structural and mechanistic detail is known. Genes encoding multiple photoreceptors have been described, including a cryptochrome, opsin, and multiple phytochromes13,14, but the chromophore-binding blue light photoreceptor encoded by WC-115,16), is the most important of these with other photoreceptors acting generally to modulate responses originating with WC-1. WC-1 exists as a heterodimer with WC-2 (the White Collar Complex or WCC) in the dark and undergoes a quaternary change to a dimer of dimers upon light exposure15, allowing it to bind DNA at light response elements within promoters using the Zn-fingers of only WC-217 as explained more fully below. WC-1 uses FAD as a prosthetic group to detect light15,16); FAD is held within the LOV domain, a conserved protein domain common to many blue light photoreceptors found in plants and fungi18.

Most of what is known mechanistically regarding photoreception derives from the study of the VVD protein which is basically just a LOV domain and whose interaction with WC-1 is the basis for photoadaptation, the capacity of the WCC photoreceptor to retain responsivity to incrementally increasing levels of light rather than just being an on/off switch19,20. VVD normally exists as a monomer in the dark but is under control of the WCC so it is induced by blue light. Light exposure also leads to a change in the structure of the bound chromophore FAD, which elicits a change in VVD protein structure that is propagated to the surface of the protein causing release of an α-helix, thereby exposing a protein-protein interaction domain21,22. This interaction domain enables VVD dimerization, as well as allowing VVD monomers to interact with the light-activated LOV domain of WC-1, which is thought to act similarly; VVD interaction with WC-1 interferes with the quaternary structure change in the WCC elicited by light, potentially competing with the formation of WC-1 dimers, thereby attenuating WCC activity and leading to photoadaptation19,23.

A potentially interesting thread has emerged recently with connections between light-induced and WC-1-mediated ROS signaling and temperature perception, or phytochrome-mediated light and temperature perception wherein WC-1 or PHY or its orthologs act as both light and temperature receptors24–26. As this signaling has been described by orthologous proteins in plants it may be phylogenetically conserved.

The core circadian oscillator

A strength of research on Neurospora has been the sustained focus on delving ever deeper into the mechanism of the circadian oscillator which shows strong conservation of its regulatory architecture with animal clocks (Fig. 1). As a result, work on Neurospora has revealed some widely conserved features. As noted at the outset, most of the components of this oscillator were identified in the 1980s-90s, but how they work together to keep time represents a sustained thread of research. These can be grouped into several categories that are considered separately.

Novel clock genes/ structure function of clock proteins

Structure/function studies of clock components represent a steady thread through the past quarter century (Fig. 2). The structure of the frq gene and its products has continued to draw attention since 1997, when multiple isoforms of FRQ whose expression reflected ambient temperature, were first described27. The molecular basis for this is now understood as deriving from temperature-modulated alternative splicing and use of multiple promoters28,29), but the functional differences between the two isoforms continue to draw attention. Both forms support temperature compensated rhythms30, although differing in phosphorylation and stability31. In addition to the coding transcript, frq encodes an antisense transcript (qrf;32. qrf is regulated by both light and the clock and likely acts in cis; however, despite considerable effort, it remains unclear whether or how it influences rhythmicity33–35. Antisense transcription has also been described as a regulatory mechanism of the mammalian Per gene36.

Structurally, FRQ contains several functional domains: a coiled-coil domain that mediates protein–protein interactions37; a PEST domain implicated in protein stability38; and multiple dispersed acidic clusters that contribute to interactions with the WCC39. The C-terminal region of FRQ harbors the FRQ–FRH interaction domain (FFD)40,41, and is also required for interaction with the WCC (42; see also evidence that the truncated protein arising from the frq9 allele fails to interact with the WCC). In addition, FRQ interacts with CK1a via two domains (FCD1 and FCD2)43,44, and contains several nuclear localization signals as well as an imprecisely mapped nuclear export signal42,45. The question of nuclear import remains somewhat clouded, however, as mutants with all three NLS motifs disrupted retain some, albeit altered, rhythmicity (Fig. 2).

Similarly, long and short versions of WC-146 and WC-247 are expressed from multiple promoters both upstream of and internal to the ORFs. Neurospora WC-1 and WC-2 interact via PAS domains to make the WCC48,49 and in addition WC-1 contains the N-terminal light-responsive LOV domain as noted above. WC-2 has few identified domains besides the ZnF and flanking basic regions required for DNA binding17. NLSs have yet to be identified for either WC-1 or WC-217, but genetic analysis of their nuclear import shows that import of WC-1 requires Impα, whereas import of WC-2 does not50. WC-1 has an N-terminal transcriptional transactivation domain and also recruits SWI/SNF via an N-terminal region to help it remodel chromatin51. It binds to DNA via ZnF and also a DBD region (named for being defective in binding DNA), a basic region proximal to the DNA-binding zinc finger (ZnF) that plays multiple essential roles assisting in DNA binding and mediating interactions with the FFC17. Due to light-induced differences in the WCC quaternary structure15, DNA binding for light-induction by the WCC requires only the WC-2 ZnF, whereas DNA binding for circadian functions requires the ZnF of both WC-1 and WC-2 as well as the DBD motif (Fig. 2).

A significant advance occurred in 2005 with the identification of FRH (FRQ-Interacting RNA Helicase) as a stoichiometric partner with FRQ in the Negative Arm complex40. In terms of core clock proteins themselves, FRH displays the domains typical for an RNA helicase but in addition bears an N-terminal Intrinsically Disordered Region (IDR) that mediates interactions with FRQ52,53 (Fig. 2). FRH performs essential functions in the cell and was initially and quite reasonably assumed to act as an RNA helicase in the exosome as does its yeast ortholog. However, an opportunity to re-evaluate this arose when a novel allele of frh arose in a genetic screen; frhR806H preserved vital functions but failed to support its role in the clock54. The existence of frhR806H thus provided the opportunity to ask whether FRH was in fact acting catalytically as an RNA helicase, and the surprising answer was that it was not: FRH instead supplies a support role to stabilize FRQ53 in a manner similar to the role of TIM in the Drosophila clock. Closer examination of FRQ structure revealed what was hiding in plain sight, that much of FRQ (and indeed much of Drosophila and mammalian PER also) is comprised of IDRs, a fact “rediscovered” several times over the last decade. The Intrinsically Disordered nature of the Negative Arm elements, and of FRQ in particular, has given rise to an entirely new line of research where the role of such IDRs in circadian regulation is being elucidated (e.g. refs. 55–57); reviewed in refs. 58–60).

Overlapping in time with the emergence of the importance IDRs came a report on the role of codon bias in structure/function of FRQ which shows extreme bias towards rare codons61. Remarkably, changes in the gene substituting common for rare codons causes arrhythmicity despite increased FRQ expression, thus signaling a connection between rate of translation and structure61. Like the study of IDRs, this observation has given rise to a new line of research now expanded well beyond Neurospora and clocks (e.g. refs. 62,63).

Posttranslational modifications (Fig. 2)

A major theme in examination of the core circadian oscillator in the past quarter century has been the description and elucidation of the importance of posttranslational modifications of clock components. Although acetylation and methylation of FRQ have been recently reported64, far and away the most effort has been spent documenting phosphorylations of FRQ and the WCC and understanding the roles of these modifications in the clock.

Kinases and phosphatases

FRQ phosphorylation was noted early65; FRQ is now known to be phosphorylated by CK138, CK266, CAMK-167, PRD-468 and dephosphorylated by PP1, PP2a69 although the importance of PKA and PP2a in this regard has been questioned70.

WCC is phosphorylated by CK1, CK243,71, GSK72 and dephosphorylated by PP473 and likely by PP2A and also by CSP-674, the last of which provides a cautionary note in making clear that it is specific phosphorylations rather than the overall level of phosphorylation that is important.

Roles for phosphorylation

FRQ is phosphorylated at over 100 sites75,76 and these appear within the protein in a highly choreographed time-of-day specific manner76. Despite decades of study, there remains much that is poorly understood about the roles of FRQ phosphorylation in the clock cycle. Two general ideas have emerged. One overarching idea for interpreting FRQ phosphorylation was in terms of FRQ stability, a concept that matches the interpretation of phosphorylation of Negative Arm components in animal clocks. Indeed, prior to 2015, virtually all FRQ phosphorylation was interpreted as influencing the rate of FRQ turnover (e.g. refs. 38,41,65,75,77). A major turning point for this concept came in 2015 with the surprising discovery that the turnover rate of FRQ per se is not linked to period determination. When FRQ degradation becomes excessively slow, its inactivation ensures that the clock remains functional78. Thus, degradation and inactivation may represent redundant mechanisms that act in parallel. This finding lent strength to the other way of looking at FRQ phosphorylation which stems from the realization that most of FRQ is an IDR. The longest stretch of non-phosphorylated residues is only 127 amino acids76 suggesting that phosphorylation is likely to engender structural and surface charge changes that will impact tertiary structure as well as interacting partners (e.g. refs. 42,44,56–58,71,79).

WCC

The importance of WCC phosphorylation stems from two landmark papers which interpreted WCC phosphorylation as the means through which their activity was depressed as a part of closing the feedback loop43,80. While this basic concept remains, more recent data have provided more detail and some corrections. Phosphorylations occur on both WC-1 and WC-2, but interestingly only a few sites must be phosphorylated to effect repression70–whereas there are over 80 phosphosites on WC-1 and numerous on WC-2, modification of just six on WC-1 (serines 971, 988, 990, 992, 994, and 995) near to the WC-1 ZnF and a handful on WC-2 are sufficient for circadian repression; these are executed by CK1 which is brought there by FRQ70. This leaves still unknown the function(s) of the many additional phosphosites, although it is clear that WC-1 can be heavily phosphorylated but retain its role in keeping time74. Interestingly, most of the cellular pool of WCC remains continually phosphorylated and repressed and relief from repression happens when a small subset of this pool is transiently dephosphorylated allowing it to drive new frq expression81. Indeed, the clock can run for several days with no new synthesis of either WC-1 or WC-281, emphasizing the primacy of posttranslational modifications to circadian timekeeping.

Compensation

Temperature and nutritional compensation (TC and NC), the preservation of period length despite changes in these ambient conditions, are defining characteristics of circadian rhythms and represent research foci that have received steadily more attention over the past two decades (recently reviewed in ref. 82. Despite being defining properties of circadian rhythms they have received relatively little attention because they are so difficult to study, requiring as they do repeated estimates of period length across a range of conditions. Of the two, TC is the better studied and is known to involve the action of kinases CK1 and CK2 at the least. As noted above, prior to Larrondo et al. (2015), every change in period length was interpreted as reflecting a change in FRQ stability, and compensation was no different (e.g. ref. 77 but after 2015 alternative explanations were required. The first molecular entrée to TC in Neurospora came when the overcompensated prd-4 mutant was identified as a constitutively active checkpoint kinase 2 (CHK2)68, and the role of kinases was emphasized when two classical clock mutants affecting compensation, prd-3 and chr, were shown to encode respectively the catalytic and regulatory subunits of CK283. The first mutant known to affect TC was the Neurospora frq7 allele84 followed by the mouse tau gene that encodes CK185, and the role of CK1 in Neurospora TC was then subsequently confirmed86. While different clock protein targets of kinases might affect TC, only FRQ has been identified so far87, and a systematic screen of Neurospora kinase knockouts revealed no genes beyond those above as influencing TC88. Interestingly, a very recent systematic screen of phosphatase knockouts/knockdowns has revealed that knockouts of pph-4 have short periods but are overcompensated, providing a new avenue of approach to the problem89. Now that FRQ turnover rates are no longer viewed as relevant to period length determination78, TC is modeled in several ways, including as a balance between clock-relevant steps having positive and negative temperature coefficients, or requiring activities that are inherently non-temperature sensitive82. A concrete model predicts that period length and TC are determined by the strength of the FRQ-CK1 interaction86. However, a recent detailed genetic analysis of CK1 and CK2 has revealed ck-1a mutants where period length is lengthened or shortened without affecting TC. At 20 °C CK1 dominates period with seemingly no requirement for CK2, whereas at 30 °C CK2 dominates with seemingly little requirement for CK190.

Mutations affecting NC are not correlated with those affecting TC and have appeared sporadically over the past two decades. The daytime repressor CSP-191 and RCO-1 co-repressor92, PRD-1 RNA helicase93, ras-2 and a RAS GEF94,95 have all been tied to nutritional/metabolic compensation, and an aspect of nutritional stress, glucose starvation, results in changes in expression of many clock proteins96. It was only recently that a systematic targeted screen for NC mutants has been undertaken, and it revealed conserved roles for gene expression and mRNA stability97 in NC.

Chromatin and chromatin modification

A small body of work peaking within the last decade has described rhythms in chromatin structure at core clock gene loci as well as the proteins that bring about these changes (reviewed in ref. 98. Generally, these proteins could best be described as helpful but not essential for rhythmicity, playing roles in regulating nucleosome occupancy to permit transcription factor binding, or to partially limit binding, to appropriate times51,99–104; that is, the phenotypes involve clock genes like frq but are not limited to the clock and may reflect a much wider assortment of genes than only those involved in timekeeping. In some cases, the phenotypes of knockouts of such chromatin modifiers may be further complicated by nutrient conditions (e.g. ref. 92 vs ref. 105). A potentially interesting subset of these has highlighted genes whose products act in complexes to affect the rate of transcriptional elongation rather than simply affecting chromatin accessibility (e.g., the NuA4 histone acetylation complex106 or PAF1 complex107).

Circadian output

There has been sustained interest in cataloging genes regulated by the clock in as much as these highlight the global influence of circadian regulation (reviewed in ref. 2. Often this is “cherry picking” analysis of individual genes (too numerous to mention), but they serve to highlight pathways such as MAP kinase signaling108, metabolism109 or stress responses110 and have elucidated some more global regulators (e.g. ref. 111. Inevitably, ‘omics approaches have been applied to this providing global lists of ccgs112,113 and proteins114, with this noteworthy observation that while about a quarter of genes are clock-regulated, >40% of these do not arise from clock-regulated transcripts but rather that these protein rhythms arise from oscillations in translational rates (see below also).

FRQ-less oscillators

Oscillators that do not require FRQ or WCC but that can control conidiation rhythms (FRQ-Less Oscillators or FLOs) have been noted for decades, and understanding their potential interaction with the circadian system represents a persistent thread in research115. One thread derived from work showing that lipid deficiencies exposed rhythms whose period depended on supplementation and could even restore rhythmicity (albeit not circadian rhythmicity) to strains lacking frq, wc-1 or wc-2 (e.g. ref. 116); however, subsequent work revealed that the lipid deficiencies simply allowed a FLO to assume control of conidiation without affecting the underlying (and now masked) circadian oscillation117. This explanation may explain many FLOs involving lipid metabolism. Genetic screens uncovered genes impacting the choline-deficiency induced oscillation118–120 and knockouts of two of these (vta and gtr-2), which encode orthologs of genes involved in TOR signaling, lead to dampening of FRQ protein rhythms; this led to interest in a possible connection between circadian rhythms and TOR signaling (e.g. ref. 121. However, deletions of these genes retain circadian rhythms in WCC activity with phases and period lengths close to wild type122. Additional FLOs have also been described but are less well studied (e.g. refs. 123,124).

Emerging themes

Circadian control of translation

Consistent with the observation that, while a quarter of expressed proteins are clock regulated >40% of these do not arise from clock-regulated transcripts114, a theme that has emerged in the past decade centers on circadian control of translation. Rhythms in MAPK pathways lead to rhythmic phosphorylation and inactivation of translation elongation factor eEF2 and decreased translational activity in the late subjective morning125. Further work revealed rhythms in translation initiation via rhythmic inhibitory phosphorylation of eIF2α and reduced translation during the day126. Some tRNA synthases are clock-controlled leading to rhythmic interactions among components within ribosomes. Most excitingly, recent work has revealed rhythmic changes in ribosomal subunits which lead to cycles in translation and termination127. These studies highlight the tractability of Neurospora in combining classic and molecular genetics to dissect a universal process.

Cell biology

Another emerging theme in Neurospora circadian research uses the expanding array of modern cell biology tools. Early studies were limited by brightness of fluors to abundant proteins (e.g. ref. 128) and to fluors not sensitive to wavelengths that would reset the clock but that were also often limited in their brightness (e.g. ref. 129). There has been some recent use of heterologous systems to overcome these issues, such as U2OS cells in which Neurospora proteins are expressed, and accepting the inherent limitations of expression in non-native systems, these may allow insights. However, more modern tools have gradually been acquired and applied to Neurospora130,131, including the use of microfluidics and light-blind but rhythmic strains that allow application of the full spectrum of fluors, so that now the clock cycle can be fully imaged in normal developing live cells50,132. This opens the system to full exploitation of modern cell biological approaches revealing, for instance, that FRQ oscillations are synchronized among nuclei via internuclear protein exchange, and that within nuclei there exist distinct highly dynamic WCC and FRQ nuclear bodies132. Further exploitation of these tools will show how subcellular organization and internuclear communication coordinate timing in a multinucleated syncytium.

Novel systems

A final theme that I have found especially gratifying is the emergence of a subfield describing rhythmic behavior in other fungi. In these organisms, more than fifteen to date (reviewed in ref. 2), the core circadian clock as described in Neurospora has been shown to be evolutionarily conserved within a broad range of fungi centered on the Sordariomycetes, but ranging some distance beyond, in all including members from at least 6 classes within the phylum Ascomycota. A common use of the clock is to regulate pathogenicity in plant or insect pathogens as seen with Fusarium oxysporum133 or in Botrytis cinerea where the fungal clock plays the main role in defining the outcome of the Arabidopsis-Botrytis interaction134. Alternatively, the fungal clock can be seen regulating growth and development in a manner like Neurospora as with hyphal melanization in Cercospora kikuchii135 or with conidiation and mycoparasitism in Trichoderma atroviride136. The clock even appears to impact the timing of host death in zombie insect models such as Entomophthora muscae137 and Ophiocordyceps kimflemingiae138. Further development of the rich biology in this approach will be a joy to watch.
