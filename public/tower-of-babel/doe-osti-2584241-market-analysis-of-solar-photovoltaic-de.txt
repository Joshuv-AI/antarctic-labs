Executive Summary
This report explores the magnitude of the potential buildout of new solar photovoltaic (PV)
development on Bureau of Land Management (BLM) land in Arizona based on market
conditions in 2023.

The market demand for solar projects built in Arizona could be robust over both the near and
medium terms. Based on their respective integrated resource plans (IRPs), Arizona’s four largest
utilities – accounting for 86% of the state’s retail electricity sales – plan on procuring between
4,900 and 6,200 megawatts (MW) of solar capacity plus an additional 2,000 MW of capacity
from an undetermined mix of PV and wind through 2035 (TEP 2020; APS 2020; UNSE 2020;
SRP n.d.a.). 1 PV and wind procurements are expected to rise through 2035 as Arizona utilities
revise IRPs to meet increasing renewable energy demand (APS 2023; TEP n.d.; UNSE n.d.a.).

Approximately 20% of the electricity generated in Arizona is exported, mostly to California.
Three new high-voltage transmission lines—Ten West, Southline, and SunZia—are scheduled to
be in service by 2030 and will further increase electricity import/export capacity. It is unclear
what impact this increased import/export capacity will have on the ultimate buildout of PV in
Arizona on BLM land, but regional demand from other nearby states is expected to affect
Arizona’s intrastate market demand.

Proximity to transmission is a key consideration in PV project siting. In Arizona, extensive tracts
of BLM-managed land lie along existing and planned transmission lines, mostly in the
southeastern part of the state.

Arizona could see extensive PV deployment over the next 10–15 years and beyond. BLM’s
control of prime real estate for siting of PV projects indicates a significant, but difficult to
quantify, portion of this deployment is likely to occur on BLM land. See Figure ES-1 for a map
comparing BLM land, solar projects, and transmission lines in Arizona.

1 Introduction
This report examines, based on market conditions in 2021 through 2023, the potential buildout
and commercial viability of new solar development on land managed by the Bureau of Land
Management (BLM) in Arizona. The BLM currently has 18 solar energy zones (SEZs),
including 3 in Arizona, where land has undergone preliminary reviews and is suitable for
expedited environmental review and permitting for solar energy projects. The BLM also
administers variance areas outside SEZs and may consider right-of-way applications for utility-
scale solar development on these lands. Within variance areas in Arizona, the BLM has further
identified Renewable Energy Development Area lands likely well suited to solar or wind
development. 2 The purpose of this analysis is to explore new solar development potential on
BLM land in Arizona by 2035. 3

The analysis includes three parts, each assessing one of the following:

 • Demand: Who are the load-serving utilities (or other potential energy purchasers)
 investing in solar development in Arizona by 2035? Have they indicated any desire for
 procuring new solar energy generation, either by direct ownership or via power purchase
 agreements (PPAs)?
 • Transmission: Is it possible to get energy from BLM land to market via existing or
 planned transmission? Where are projects currently planned on BLM land located in
 relation to transmission corridors?
 • Project cost: Is it likely solar projects on BLM lands could provide energy to load-
 serving utilities at a lower or equal cost than the utilities could procure from energy
 projects not located on BLM managed land?
Figure 1 illustrates how these three factors affect the likelihood BLM land will stimulate
commercial interest among solar project developers. If there is no proximate market appetite for
a project on BLM land, if there is no way to get the energy to market, or if project costs on BLM
land are not lower than potential costs in other locations, developer demand for BLM land may
be weak, and a competitive auction for the BLM land may draw little interest. If all three factors
are positive there may be more predictable developer interest.

1.1 Characteristics of Bureau of Land Management Land in Arizona
The BLM manages 12.1 million acres of public land and 17.5 million subsurface acres in
Arizona (BLM 2025). The BLM administers different classifications of land a developer may
consider for solar development in Arizona. Three of the BLM’s 18 SEZs are in Arizona 4, and all
three are in the southwestern portion of the state: the Brenda SEZ, the Gillespie SEZ, and the
Agua Caliente SEZ (BLM 2022a). The BLM also identified specific variance areas where solar
development on BLM-administered land may be considered (BLM 2020). In addition, the BLM

1.2 Need for Future Updates
Market conditions change over time. Therefore, although the findings of this study are valid for
the short term, they should be revisited as circumstances change. Utility company administrators
typically account for future load growth, plant retirements, and regulatory requirements when
determining their future procurement needs. This study reflects the current outlooks of the
relevant utilities. All the load-serving utilities that could purchase solar power generated on BLM
land in Arizona maintain integrated resource plans (IRPs). Any change in market conditions that
would affect a utility’s procurement plans would be reflected in the utility’s IRP. For example, at
the time this report was initially being drafted in 2023, several of the utilities discussed in
Section 2 planned to submit their latest IRPs to the Arizona Corporation Commission (ACC) in
late 2023 to include the utilities’ most recent forecasts for the coming decades. 5

2 Demand for New Solar Energy Projects
Several factors suggest the largest utilities in Arizona may have increased demand for solar
energy as part of their energy mixes through the coming decades. Although Arizona lacks the
more aggressive statewide renewable energy goals found in other states, Arizona’s largest
utilities have adopted voluntary plans to increase their use of renewable resources beyond the
state mandate. The ACC’s Renewable Energy Standard and Tariff requires 15% of regulated
utilities’ energy come from renewable sources by 2025 (ACC n.d.a.). The ACC has noted the
state’s largest utilities are voluntarily shifting towards renewable energy resources because they
are cost competitive and reliable (ACC 2022). Four of Arizona’s largest electric utilities—TEP,
APS, UNSE, and SRP (which together accounted for 86% of Arizona’s electricity retail sales in
2021) 6—have committed to surpassing that mandate by switching a larger proportion of their
key power sources from fossil fuels to renewable options (U.S. EIA Form 861 database (2021
data for retail sales, average rates); 2021 Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) Form
1 data (2022); EIA 2022d). As discussed in Table 2 and in the rest of this section, TEP
announced it plans to provide more than 70% of its electricity from zero-carbon resources by
2035, which includes retiring the Springerville Generating Station by 2032. Arizona Public
Service plans to retire all its coal generation by 2031 and supply 100% of its electricity from
renewable resources by 2050. Salt River Project plans to retire its various coal plants between
2027 and 2032 and reduce its carbon emissions by 65% of its 2005 levels by 2035 and by 90% of
its 2005 levels by 2050. As discussed later, the capacity from these coal plants must be replaced
by another source, such as solar. Table 3 is a list of anticipated coal plant retirements. UNSE also
announced in its 2020 resource plan it aims to supply half of its energy to retail energy customers
from renewable resources by 2035 (UNSE 2020). Based on their respective IRPs, Arizona’s four
largest utilities plan to procure a combined total of between 6,935 and 8,199 MW of PV and
wind capacity through 2035 (TEP 2020; APS 2020; UNSE 2020; SRP n.d.a.). 7 It is likely these
values could rise in the next several years as utilities seek to meet their customers’ energy
demand with increasingly cleaner resource mixes, including additional solar (APS 2023; TEP
n.d.; UNSE n.d.).

In addition, the Clean Electricity Investment Tax Credit and the Production Tax Credit available
to certain facilities as of January 1, 2025, may also encourage solar development in Arizona
through 2034 (EIA 2022a).

Arizona is typically an exporter of electricity producing more than required to meet in-state
consumer demand and exporting excess supply to out-of-state consumers. In 2020 more than
one-fifth of the electricity generated in Arizona was exported outside the state (EIA 2022a). For
example, several California utilities 8 are part owners of the Palo Verde Nuclear Generating
Station in southwestern Arizona, which supplied California with approximately 34% of its
nuclear electricity in 2021 (nuclear made up about 10% of California’s total electricity supply
that year) (NRC 2022; EIA 2022b ). According to the U.S. Energy Information Administration,
Arizona also has the second-best solar energy potential in the country after Nevada and ranks
fifth in the country for solar electricity generation. Although this report focuses solely on
Arizona’s intrastate demand for solar energy, regional demand for solar energy could also have a
role in increasing demand on Arizona’s solar resources (EIA 2022a).

2.1 Tucson Electric Power
On June 26, 2020, TEP submitted its 2020 IRP to the ACC. When TEP prepared its 2020 IRP, it
included an assessment of future needs for additional generation capacity on the system (load
and resource balance [L&R]). TEP uses the L&R analysis as a starting point to determine
whether it needs additional generation capacity for system reliability by forecasting annual loads
and comparing those to the utility’s available resources. TEP determines whether it needs
additional generation capacity for system reliability by forecasting whether it can maintain its
planning reserve margin requirement across its 15-year planning period (TEP 2020).

TEP announced plans to provide more than 70% of its power from renewable resources by 2035,
and as part of that goal, plans to retire all its coal units by the year 2032. TEP closed its single
San Juan Generating Station unit in July 2022 and plans to close its two Four Corners Power
Plant units by 2031 (TEP 2022a; TEP 2020.). TEP is also planning to retire its two Springerville
Generating Station units by 2027 and 2032, respectively, and to offset those two units’ output
with a mix of new wind, solar, and storage resources (TEP 2020).

Table 4 shows TEP’s L&R forecast for the planning period, including the capacity of its existing
resources compared with the total resources needed to meet its planning reserve margin
requirement over the 15-year planning period.

Between 2024 and 2035, TEP plans to add 2,000 MW of wind and solar resources and 1,400
MW of storage capacity to offset coal and gas retirements planned for that time period (TEP
2020). In the preferred portfolio outlined in its 2020 IRP, TEP analyzed trends and projections
for technology costs, tax incentive policies, and solar insolation values in southern Arizona and
identified utility-scale PV single-axis tracking solar as the least costly resource. TEP also
identified higher-capacity factor wind resources in eastern New Mexico as the next least costly
option. TEP also noted it would continue to track factors affecting the cost-effectiveness of
renewable energy resource options and update its plans accordingly (TEP 2020).

2.2 Arizona Public Service Company
On June 26, 2020, Arizona Public Service submitted its 2020 IRP to the ACC. When APS
prepared its 2020 IRP, it included an assessment of future needs for additional generation
capacity on the system (L&R). APS uses the L&R analysis as a starting point to determine
whether it needs additional generation capacity for system reliability by forecasting annual loads
and comparing those to the utility’s available resources. APS determines whether it needs
additional generation capacity for system reliability by forecasting whether it can maintain its
planning reserve margin requirement across its 15-year planning period. Arizona Public Service
noted economic and population growth, along with the resulting increase in residential customers
and commercial and industrial floor space, will be a main driver of demand increases in the near

 9

term. Additionally, load growth from new data centers and the addition of electric vehicles over
time will also contribute to increased demand (APS 2020).

Arizona Public Service plans to supply 100% of its electricity from renewable resources by 2050
(ACC 2022). Arizona Public Service is planning to decommission the remaining two units of the
coal-fired Cholla Power Plant by 2025 (two of the four units have already come offline) and
transition the coal-fired Four Corners Power Plant to seasonal operation status in 2023 and retire
it completely by 2031 (APS 2021). As an interim goal, APS aims to achieve an energy mix that
includes 65% clean energy and serving 45% of its customers’ electricity needs with renewable
energy resources by 2030 (APS 2020). 9 To meet these goals, APS intends to continue relying on
the Palo Verde Nuclear Generating Station, expanding its renewable energy portfolio
significantly over the next decade, and mixing solar, wind, and energy storage technologies into
its energy portfolio (APS 2020).

For its 2020 IRP, APS developed three portfolios that take increasingly ambitious approaches to
meeting its clean energy goals:

 • Bridge Portfolio, which combines renewable plus storage resources with plans to build
 hydrogen-ready, gas-fired generation and to use the current regional fleet of merchant gas
 generators while relying on natural gas as a bridge fuel to provide APS with reliability
 and affordability while it moves toward a 100% clean portfolio
 • Shift Portfolio, which begins similarly to the Bridge Portfolio but does not call for new
 natural gas generation, moving APS away from natural gas more quickly, and instead
 calls for the temporary purchase of additional regional merchant gas generation under
 PPAs to provide reliability and affordability to APS’s system while emergent
 technologies are given time to mature and become integrated into APS’s system
 • Accelerate Portfolio, which calls for significant renewable energy and energy storage
 procurement to offset the fact this portfolio does not allow for any new natural gas
 procurement (i.e., neither through new build nor through PPAs) and would allow APS to
 achieve its clean energy goals more quickly than the other two proposed portfolios.
Because all three portfolios share the same commitments and actions in the near-term Action
Plan window (2020–2024), APS declined to commit to any one of the suggested portfolios in
order to remain flexible in selecting the long-term path best suited to APS’s customers and
needs. APS will use the L&R analyses to support future supply and demand-side resource
additions. The L&R analyses inform both near-term decisions made during the utility’s Action
Plan window (2020–2024) and general long-term strategy to be adjusted and updated as needed
in future IRPs. APS considered grid-scale solar, rooftop solar, wind, energy storage, and natural
gas in its assessment of 2020–2024 generation resource options. In the longer term, APS plans to
consider new and emerging technologies (APS 2020). 10

3 Transmission Capability
The transmission line corridors, BLM land with currently planned projects, and existing solar
projects in Figure 2 and Table 2 provide insight into potential future renewable project
development on BLM lands in Arizona. As seen in Figure 2 and Table 2, most of the capacity for
both existing projects and in current BLM applications is within 5 miles of an existing
transmission line. Most proposed projects on BLM land are in the western half of Arizona, near
high-voltage interstate transmission lines connecting load centers in Arizona with California and
Nevada (Most existing projects are clustered near or between the Phoenix and Tucson metro
regions.)

Over the next 5 years, three new interstate transmission projects with significant capacity are
also planned to come online in Arizona. 18 These lines will likely affect renewable resource
development both in and outside of Arizona, while also increasing system capacity and
reliability. The Ten West Project (500 kilovolt [kV], 3,200 MW) officially became operational in
June 2024. Ten West follows the existing transmission line connecting the Tonopah/Palo Verde
area to a Southern California Edison substation just across the California border in Blythe, with a
goal of improving interstate transmission capacity. Multiple projects on BLM land have been
proposed along this corridor.

The other two transmission projects will follow similar paths into Arizona from southern New
Mexico. The Southline Project (345 kV, 1,000 MW) is planned to come online in 2027 and will
provide bidirectional flows to improve system reliability between Las Cruces, New Mexico, and
the Phoenix/Tucson regions. In contrast, the SunZia Project is planned primarily to export
renewable generation from New Mexico to western markets in Arizona and California. The first
phase of SunZia (525 kV DC, 3,000 MW) is also planned for 2025 in-service, and its
transmission capacity is already entirely dedicated to a 3,000 MW wind development in central
New Mexico. The second phase of the SunZia Project (500 kV AC, 1,500 MW) broke ground in
September 2023 and targets 2026 for commercial operations (Pattern 2022a). Both phases of the
SunZia Project were permitted simultaneously. The SunZia lines will start in central New
Mexico and terminate at the Pinal Central substation between Tucson and Phoenix. These lines
will cross BLM land in the eastern part of Arizona.

Most projects currently planned on BLM land are in the western part of Arizona (see Figures 1

transmission corridors connecting the Palo Verde Nuclear Generating Station with southern
California: the northern corridor to the Colorado River and ultimately Devers substations, or the
south corridor to Yuma and the Imperial Valley. (A smaller number of planned solar projects are
closer to the Nevada border, near Lake Mead/Hoover Dam.) Most of the existing transmission

3.2 Planned New Interstate Transmission Lines
The three previously described new transmission projects were all originally proposed in the mid
to late 2000s. The Ten West and Southline projects were both planned to facilitate greater
bidirectional interstate transmission capacity and improve reliability. The Southline and SunZia
projects follow similar corridors from southern New Mexico to the Tucson and Phoenix metro
regions. However, SunZia is planned more for long-distance, single-direction export of
renewable generation from New Mexico into Arizona and California. The large amount of solar
generation capacity built in California may also lead to some amount of solar curtailment or
other economic impacts in Arizona through the Western Energy Imbalance Market (Hurlbut et
al. 2023; O’Shaughnessy et al. 2020), potentially facilitated by the increased interstate capacity
from the Ten West project. All these factors may impact the development of new renewable
resources on BLM land in Arizona.

3.2.1 Ten West
The Ten West transmission line will provide an additional 3,200 MW of new transmission
capacity between the APS and Southern California Edison systems. In 2014 the California
Independent System Operator identified a need for more high-voltage transmission capacity
between the two states, both to increase reliability and efficiency of the California and other
western grids and to facilitate increased renewable generation development in both Arizona and
California (BLM 2019). Based on these needs, the Ten West line is likely to operate
bidirectionally.

A precursor project to the current Ten West transmission project (then known as the Devers-Palo
Verde 2 line) was originally proposed in the early 2000s. The originally proposed Devers-Palo
Verde 2 line was planned to stretch from the Harquahala Generating Substation west of Tonopah
to the Devers substation near Palm Springs, California. Although the proposal was approved by
the California Public Utilities Commission, the ACC rejected it, citing concerns about exporting
large volumes of generation to California. The California sections were ultimately built over the
next few years, terminating at the Colorado River Substation (then known as Midpoint) in Blythe
at the California/Arizona border (Aspen Environmental Group n.d.).

The Arizona Ten West project was reproposed a decade later following recommendation by the
California Independent System Operator and was approved by the ACC in March 2020.
Similarly, the BLM Record of Decision authorizing the Ten West project was issued in
November 2019, and BLM approved construction in July 2022. Construction of the Ten West
line began in January 2023, and the line officially became operational in June 2024 (Lotus
Infrastructure Partners 2024). The new Ten West transmission proposal connects into this same
Colorado River substation at the border with its eastern terminus at the Delaney substation near
Tonopah. The Ten West line is approximately 125 miles long. The line will parallel Interstate 10

3.2.2 Southline
The Southline project is also expected to operate bidirectionally between Las Cruces, New
Mexico, and the Tucson/Phoenix region (Southline Transmission Project 2017). It is expected to
provide approximately 1,000 MW of capacity. The project consists of two distinct sections, as
seen in Figure 4:

 • A new 345-kV double-circuit line connecting existing substations at Afton (near Las
 Cruces) and Apache (eastern Arizona)
 • An upgrade of existing Western Area Power Administration 115-kV lines between
 existing substations at Apache and Saguaro/Tortolita (between Tucson and Phoenix),
 with the lines upgraded to double-circuit 230 kV. In 2021 TEP purchased the
 development rights for the line segment between the Vail and Tortolita substations (see
 Figure 4).
Initial planning for Southline began in 2008. Planning studies started by defining the line’s
endpoints (load centers) and focused on bidirectional flows to improve reliability and facilitate
renewable energy development along its path. Southline is planned to connect with up to 14
existing substations, and 85% of the line follows existing corridors (Southline Transmission
Project 2017; Southwest Area Transmission 2022). As of June 2024, the project is fully
permitted with the National Environmental Policy Act, and New Mexico and Arizona state
approvals are secured (Southline Transmission Project 2024). Construction is anticipated to
begin in 2025, with the first phase possibly operational by 2028 (Southline Transmission Project
2024).

3.3 Key Takeaways
Key takeaways related to transmission availability for development of renewable generation
projects on BLM land in Arizona include the following:

 • Most of the currently planned projects on BLM land are in western Arizona and are
 within 5 miles of existing high-voltage transmission lines connecting load centers in
 Arizona with California and Nevada. Additional planned in-state transmission upgrade
 projects may improve the interconnection between these lines and the planned interstate
 transmission projects.
 • Three key high-voltage interstate transmission line projects are expected to come online
 over the next 5 years. Although two of these projects (Ten West, Southline) were
 designed to facilitate greater renewable generation development within Arizona, all three
 projects will also increase the availability of imports from out-of-state resources, which
 may also impact generation curtailment or other project economics of in-state projects.
 • The planned Ten West line will follow an existing line between the Palo Verde area and
 California. This corridor already has numerous planned solar projects on BLM land. The
 other planned lines from New Mexico will cross BLM land in eastern Arizona, although
 currently only one solar project is planned there.

4 Findings
This market assessment provides insight into commercial viability of solar development on BLM
land in Arizona. We offer the following observations based on the findings of our analysis:

 1. Load-serving utilities in Arizona anticipate future demand for more renewable resources
 because of voluntary clean energy goals and the expected cost of new solar projects sited
 in the region is less than the utilities’ current cost of electricity. Based upon their
 respective IRPs, Arizona’s four largest utilities plan to procure an approximate combined
 total of between 6,900 and 8,200 MW of PV and wind capacity through 2035. BLM’s
 control of prime real estate for siting of PV projects indicates a significant, but very
 difficult to quantify, portion of this deployment is likely to occur on BLM land.
 2. Most planned projects on BLM land lie along existing high-voltage transmission
 corridors in western Arizona. These corridors link load centers in Arizona with California
 and Nevada.
 3. A significant increase in interstate transmission capacity is expected later this decade,
 including from the Ten West project (Palo Verde to California) and Southline and SunZia
 projects (New Mexico to Phoenix and Tucson). Multiple projects on BLM land are
 already planned along the Ten West corridor. Greater transmission access to these out-of-
 state markets may impact the total quantity of PV deployed on BLM land in Arizona.
In summary, the market demand for a solar project built on BLM lands in Arizona could be
robust. Lands in western Arizona likely have good transmission access, but interstate market
dynamics may play an uncertain role in the relative economics of, and demand for, the future
projects.
