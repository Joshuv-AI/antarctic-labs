Ultrasound phacoemulsification: physical mechanisms, cavitation, and thermal effects
Authors: Thomas Graf, Thomas Gisler, Silvio Emmenegger, Samuel Tanner, Rupert Menapace, Silvio Di Nardo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The study shows that phacoemulsification operates through mechanical jackhammer action. Cavitation did not contribute to lens fragmentation, and ultrasound-related heat and pressure remained clinically insignificant.

Currently, 2 main technologies are available for the workup of cataractous lenses: ultrasound-based phacoemulsification cataract surgery (PCS) and femtolaser-assisted cataract surgery (FLACS). Since the introduction of FLACS in 2011, numerous randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews have compared the safety and effectiveness of the 2 technologies.1–6 Considering current knowledge, the American Academy of Ophthalmology concluded that FLACS and PCS have similar excellent safety and refractive outcomes, but that FLACS is less cost-effective.7 The ESCRS published a similar position in a draft guideline.8 Thus, from a clinical perspective, ultrasound-based PCS is a well-proven and safe technology that enables surgeons to achieve optimal clinical results. Ultrasound remains the most important and widely used technology for cataract removal. Therefore, it is important to continuously improve the safety and effectiveness of this method.

The interaction between ultrasound and proteins is mediated by 6 physicochemical effects: (1) ultrasound pressure, (2) cavitation, (3) acoustic streaming, (4) temperature, (5) free radicals, and (6) shear forces.9 The aim of this study was to analyze these physicochemical effects through numerical simulation and experiments using a state-of-the-art phacoemulsification system.

The results concerning free radical generation have been published elsewhere, and experiments have demonstrated that free radicals are generated during the delivery of ultrasound power.10 Vapor bubbles in the fluid surrounding the PCS tip enhance the generation of free radicals, whereas running fluidics and emulsification processes reduce the production of free radicals. In a clinically desired situation, the PCS tip is occluded by the lens, and only as much ultrasound energy as needed is applied; in this situation, a small peak in the electron paramagnetic resonance spectrum is visible. The corresponding number of free radicals is below the limit of accurate quantification and can be considered negligible. The authors concluded that causes other than ultrasound are responsible for the suggested oxidative stress during cataract surgery.

In this study, 4 ultrasound-specific, safety-relevant processes were investigated through technical analysis: propagation of ultrasound waves, formation and dynamics of cavitation bubbles, generation of acoustic streaming, and temperature increase in the anterior chamber. To date, no systematic assessment of all relevant ultrasound-induced risks at the engineering level has been published.

METHODS

Numerical Simulations

Numerical simulations were performed using COMSOL Multiphysics (v. 6.1 and 6.2., COMSOL AB) using the finite element method. The coupled wave propagation in both water and solid materials was computed using frequency-domain studies. The elastic modulus, Poisson ratio, and mass density of titanium (grade 5) for the PCS tip and handpiece, as well as those of the elastomer (Elastosil LR 3003/85) for the sleeve, were defined as material properties in the simulation. Realistic viscoelastic models and material properties for ocular tissues were taken from the literature and implemented in the simulation, including distinct internal damping models for the cornea, lens, and vitreous.11–13 The fluid in the anterior chamber of the eye was modeled as water.

The longitudinal oscillation of the tip was driven by the prescribed displacement of the piezoelectric package, operating at 27 kHz. The simulated motion of the tip and shaft agreed within 5 μm with measurements obtained using a laser Doppler vibrometer (Polytec; Controller: OFV-3320, Sensor: LSV-065-306F), with a maximum tip amplitude of 50 μm.

Experimental Methods

Phacoemulsification Apparatus With Handpiece

Phacoemulsification was performed using 2 surgical platforms, CataRhex 3 and OS4 (both manufactured by Oertli Instrumente AG). The procedure used the easyPhaco handpiece (Oertli), which incorporated 6 piezoelectric ceramic rings. A variety of phacoemulsification tips (easyTip, Oertli) were used, including CO-MICS tips (1.6 mm, angled at 30 degrees and 53 degrees) and standard tips (2.2 mm and 2.8 mm, angled at 15 degrees, 30 degrees, and 40 degrees). The handpiece was operated in the continuous ultrasound mode.

Pressure Chamber and High-Speed Camera

To suppress the formation of cavitation bubbles in front of the moving PCS tip, a pressure chamber was constructed, as shown in Figure 1. This design concept was inspired by the work of Zacharias.14,15 A feedthrough for the PCS handpiece was incorporated, allowing phacoemulsification operation inside the chamber at a maximum ambient pressure of 4 bar. Through glass windows in the pressure chamber, the formation and dissipation of cavitation bubbles could be observed using a high-speed camera (Motion Pro X4, Redlake).

Phacoemulsification Efficiency Measurement

The emulsification duration was measured at ambient pressures of 1 bar and 4 bar for the following ultrasound power settings: 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100%. An EasyTip 2.2 mm 40 degrees phacoemulsification tip attached to an OS4 unit operating in the continuous mode served as the emulsification instrument. On the occurrence of occlusion, timing commenced; measurement ceased when a visible fluid jet emerged from the opposite face of the test samples. At least 9 replicates were performed for each condition. The test samples comprised 1 cm3 cubes of Sbrinz cheese (aged 22 months) and hardened pig lenses (4% formalin for 2 hours).16,17

Hard Sbrinz cheese was selected to enable controlled evaluation of contact-driven fragmentation mechanisms while minimizing biological variability. Compared with the human crystalline lens, which exhibits relatively low stiffness in the kPa range and pronounced viscoelastic behavior, hard cheeses exhibit Young moduli in the range of approximately 0.2 to 1.7 MPa.18,19

To confirm the findings regarding the emulsification mechanism, additional experiments were performed using formalin-hardened porcine lenses. Such lenses have been used as an experimental cataract model in phacoemulsification studies, and previous reports indicate that formalin treatment can produce the lens material whose density and emulsification behavior resemble that of human cataractous nuclei.16,20,21

Temperature Measurement in a Cuvette Setup

The heat generation in water due to the ultrasonic motion of the PCS tip was investigated using the experimental setup shown in Figure 2. The PCS tip was immersed in thermally insulated cuvettes containing either 4 g or 64 g of water. Water temperature was monitored using a thermistor probe (Fluke, 5640 series).

Infrared Camera

The heat generation of the PCS handpiece was investigated using an infrared camera (Pyroview, Infrared Systems). The casing of the PCS handpiece was removed to allow for a direct view of the heating of the piezoelectric components and wiring. The metal components were blackened to ensure accurate temperature measurements.

RESULTS

Simulation Results of Ultrasound and Cavitation

Oscillation of the PCS tip and metal shaft of the handpiece generates ultrasound waves in the fluid of the anterior chamber, as well as in the irrigation and aspiration channels within the handpiece. Figure 3 illustrates the simulated acoustic pressure amplitudes in the central plane through the anterior chamber of the eye. Acoustic pressures of up to 5 bar are observed directly in front of the PCS tip and within the distal portion. Acoustic pressure exhibits rapid attenuation in the corneal direction, reaching 0.1 bar at 3 mm from the distal center of the tip. The acoustic pressure of the fluid within the anterior chamber induces displacements of the viscoelastic tissues of the eye. These displacements remained well below 2 μm across all ocular structures, particularly in the corneal region.

Experimental Results

Observation of Cavitation Vapor Bubbles

To rule out the potential formation of air bubbles, we used boiled and distilled water in our experiments. Where the simulated acoustic pressure amplitude locally exceeds 1 bar, cavitation, that is, the formation of vapor bubbles during the negative half-cycle, becomes possible (Figure 3). Figure 4A shows a high-speed photograph of the PCS tip shaft, revealing cavitation bubbles formed at locations predicted by the simulations (not visible in the section shown in Figure 3). The graph of Figure 4B shows the observed bubble area within the section shown in Figure 4A as a function of the observational time. The measured bubble area is best fitted by a sine curve at 27.1 kHz, indicating that the bubbles pulsate at the tip's expected oscillation frequency and that cavitation occurs where predicted. The number of vapor bubbles was significantly lower than anticipated, given the extensive regions of high ultrasound pressure in the simulations. This discrepancy may be due to the lack of nucleation sites in the pure water. We observed cavitation at the shaft and tip of the PCS handpiece but never off the handpiece parts. This finding was confirmed by photographs published in the literature.14,22 The formation of cavitation bubbles at the front of the tip and inside it supports the hypothesis that phacoemulsification may occur through cavitation-bubble implosions. Whether the observed cavitation bubbles at the distal end of the tip and inside the tip actually contribute to the emulsification of the cataractous lens is clarified in the next chapter. The finite element method simulations in Figure 3 show that the acoustic pressure is the highest within the aspiration channel of the PCS tip, making formation of cavitation bubbles most likely in this region.

Phacoemulsification Efficiency

Subsequently, the phacoemulsification process was investigated within a high-pressure chamber. The tip was brought into contact with a model tissue consisting of cubic samples (1 cm3) of hard Sbrinz cheese, and phacoemulsification was initiated. The cheese cubes exhibited consistent material properties, which allowed reproducible experimental conditions across different tests. The emulsification efficiency was evaluated by measuring the time tp required for the PCS tip to completely pierce 1 cm through the model tissue. Repeated experiments showed that the emulsification efficiency did not decrease at ambient pressures above 3 bar, where cavitation in front of or within the tip was suppressed.

The same experiments were also performed on porcine eye lenses that had been hardened by immersion for 2 hours in 4% formalin. In these specimens, phacoemulsification efficiency did not decrease with increasing ambient pressure.

In additional experiments, glycerin was used as the working fluid instead of water. Glycerin has a much larger critical radius than water, beyond which vapor pressure overcomes surface tension allowing a vapor bubble to grow freely. As a result, cavitation bubble formation in glycerin is absent. Nevertheless, phacoemulsification of the model tissue with glycerin was still effective, with a piercing time of approximately tp = 6 seconds.

In a third experiment, a plant leaf was used to investigate the interaction between cavitation bubbles and organic tissue. As shown in Figure 6A, the tip of the leaf was in direct contact with the conical section of the PCS tip. After 120 seconds of phacoemulsification in water, the leaf showed clear signs of wear attributable to mechanical friction with the metal parts of the tip shaft. By contrast, as shown in Figure 6B, the leaf was positioned without contact with the PCS tip. After 120 seconds of phacoemulsification tip oscillation in water, no visible damage to the leaf was observed despite the high likelihood of cavitation implosions occurring near the tissue.

All of the above experimental observations support the conclusion that cavitation is not the primary mechanism driving the emulsification of organic materials during PCS.

Acoustic Streaming by Tip Oscillation

High-frequency oscillation of the PCS tip generates acoustic streaming, which primarily occurs when the sleeve is removed, exposing the vertical surface where the tip connects to the handpiece (see the marked area in Figure 4A). Video analysis of the tip oscillation in water estimated the resulting streaming velocity of cavitation bubbles to be approximately 0.1 m/s. By comparison, this is lower than the irrigation flow velocity observed from the sleeve's infusion ports and 2 orders of magnitude lower than the tip's peak oscillation velocity of 8.5 m/s.15 The sleeve effectively suppresses acoustic streaming into the anterior chamber during phacoemulsification. Weaker streaming is also observed at the distal end of the tip. When aspiration is turned off, this streaming pushes the particles in front of the tip away from it. Given the small oscillating vertical surface, the distal end of the tip transfers minimal momentum to the fluid, resulting in limited ultrasound-driven streaming. This opposing flow is effectively overcome by aspiration, which is essential for stabilizing the lens fragments and ensuring efficient emulsification.

Heat Generation in the Anterior Chamber of the Eye

The heat generated by the oscillatory motion of the PCS tip within the anterior chamber of the eye could be estimated analytically. Tip and shaft were modeled as a cylinder with an outer diameter of D = 0.74 mm and length L = 2 cm. The surrounding water is assumed to follow the oscillatory motion of the cylinder surface, with its velocity decaying exponentially with the radial distance y from the cylinder surface, as described byv(y)=v0·e−yδvHere, δv=ηfπϱ=3 μm is the characteristic boundary layer thickness, and v0=vRMS=A·2πf=6ms represents the root-mean-square velocity of oscillation. The viscosity and density of water at 36°C are taken as η=7×10−4Pa·s and ϱ=1000kgm3, respectively. A simplified COMSOL Multiphysics model, coupling the heat transfer and solid mechanics modules, was used to verify the exponential velocity profile and boundary-layer thickness. A no-slip condition coupled an oscillating cylinder (27 kHz) to the surrounding water, and the analytical and numerical velocity profiles matched perfectly. The viscous friction force exerted on the cylinder was calculated as follows:Ffr=η·LDπ·dvdy|y=0=ηLDπv0δv=6.5×10−2N

The corresponding heating power due to this frictional force isP=Ffrv0=0.4 W

Accounting for both the inner and outer surfaces of the tip shaft and considering that only the distal portion of the tip is immersed in the ocular fluid, the total heating power generated by the oscillating PCS tip in water remains well below 1 W.

The heat input into the anterior chamber of the eye was replicated using controlled experiments. In these experiments, the PCS tip was immersed to varying depths in thermally insulated cuvettes containing either 4 mL or 64 mL of water (Figure 2). The water temperature inside the cuvettes was recorded over a 2-minute interval under continuous application of maximum phacoemulsification power. The time-dependent temperature rise T(t) was observed to follow an exponential behavior:T=ΔT·(1−e−tτ)where ΔT and τ are the fitting parameters derived from the experimental data. From this, the total heating power P delivered by the PCS tip to the water was calculated usingP=CWmWΔTτHere, CW=4.2Jg·K is the specific heat capacity of water, mW is the mass of the water, and ΔTτ=dTdt|t=0 represents the initial rate of temperature increase.

Across multiple measurements, the estimated heat input is approximately 4 W. This value is an order of magnitude higher than the estimate based solely on the viscous friction between the oscillating tip and surrounding fluid. The discrepancy may be attributed to the friction between the tip and sleeve, and more significantly to heat conduction from the piezo actuator inside the PCS handpiece.

When extrapolated to the entire eye, which contains approximately 6 g of aqueous fluid, a heating power of 4 W corresponds to an initial temperature rise ofdTdt=0.16°Cs

Notably, this temperature increase was observed and calculated under thermally insulated stagnant fluid conditions.

In 1 experimental series reported in the literature, the typical mean overall effective phacotime is 10.7 seconds for low fluidic settings and 7.4 seconds for high fluidic settings.23 This corresponds to a hypothetical maximum temperature rise of 1.7°C. During the procedure, periods of ultrasound delivery alternate with phases of fragment removal, and the effective temperature rise is considerably lower than the hypothetical temperature rise because of the cooling effect of fluidics.

Thermal imaging using an infrared camera revealed that the temperature of the 6 piezoelectric rings increased by several degrees within the first few seconds of the activation. In addition to the piezoelectric elements, the piezo driver circuit contributes to heat generation. This heat is transferred through the irrigation fluid into the anterior chamber of the eye but is removed during periods of an unoccluded tip through aspiration through the handpiece. The volume of fluid transported (36 mL/min) corresponds to a thermal transport capacity of 150 J/(K·min), which is sufficient to outbalance any clinically significant temperature increase.

DISCUSSION

Numerical simulations and experimental observations confirmed that cavitation bubbles formed only at the PCS tip and shaft, restricted to zones of high acoustic pressure. Cavitation in front of the tip at ambient pressure ≥3 bar was suppressed. Under the present experimental conditions, fragmentation of the hard cheese substrate occurred with unchanged efficiency despite suppression of cavitation, whereas cavitation alone—when direct tip contact was prevented—did not produce detectable erosion or structural damage. These findings indicate that cavitation is neither necessary nor sufficient to induce measurable material disintegration in this mechanically stringent test model. By contrast, direct impulsive tip–material interaction (jackhammer effect) was sufficient to produce reproducible fragmentation. Given that the crystalline lens exhibits higher viscoelastic dissipation and lower stiffness than hard cheese, a cavitation-dominated erosion mechanism would be even less likely to account for efficient lens disintegration under comparable conditions. Therefore, our data support a predominantly contact-driven fragmentation mechanism (jackhammer) for cataract nuclei within the investigated operating regime.

In glycerin, which strongly inhibits cavitation, phacoemulsification was still effective, albeit approximately 50% slower, partly due to reduced occlusion efficiency. This further supports a mechanical rather than cavitation-based emulsification process.

Experiments with plant tissue confirmed that cavitation alone, in the absence of direct mechanical contact, caused no observable damage. Tissue wear was only evident when physical contact with oscillating metal surfaces occurred.

Therefore, simulations and experiments support the following mechanism of phacoemulsification: Lens fragments are stabilized at the distal end of the PCS tip by the aspiration vacuum and their own inertia, allowing mechanical disintegration by the longitudinally oscillating beveled tip (jackhammer effect). The fragments are then drawn into the aspiration channel, where high-frequency wall motion and fragment inertia prevent clogging and promote further disintegration through frictional interaction with the channel walls. The formation of cavitation bubbles is a secondary effect, which is not essential for the emulsification mechanism.

Acoustic pressures from PCS peaked at 5 bar near the tip and declined to 0.1 bar at the inner surface of the cornea, producing tissue displacements below 2 μm—significantly lower than those caused by manual surgical handling.

Heat generated by PCS tip oscillation was analytically and experimentally determined to be <4 W, leading to an estimated temperature increase of <0.2°C/s in the anterior chamber. Typically, the temperature increase is below 2°C during phacoemulsification. By comparison, the temperature rise of the cornea during excimer laser refractive surgery has been measured using 2 different surgical platforms and is 7.2°C and 4.1°C, respectively.24 Thermal damage to the cornea is expected after exposure of 45°C for 45 minutes.25 In another study, a thermal impact of 40°C for 10 seconds did not cause histological changes in the cornea.26 Thus, thermal damage due to the thermal impact of the ultrasound can be excluded.

Ultrasound-induced acoustic streaming is effectively suppressed by the sleeve and by the aspiration flow when active. Consequently, it does not impair lens fragmentation efficiency or pose a risk to the ocular tissue.

Several previous investigations have demonstrated the formation of cavitation bubbles during phacoemulsification.14,22,27,28 Using a similar hyperbaric system to suppress cavitation, Zacharias (2008) concluded that cavitation plays no role in phacoemulsification.14

By contrast, a recent study describes penetration experiments performed in an overpressure chamber.28 In that setup, penetration of the phacoemulsification tip into a synthetic test material ceased, when cavitation was suppressed at 6 bar. The author concluded that inertial cavitation represents the predominant mechanism of soft tissue erosion and emulsification.

At first glance, these findings seem contradictory to our results. However, several essential differences in experimental design may explain the discrepancy.

First, different test materials were used. Our experiments used cheese and hardened porcine nuclei, whereas the overpressure study used a homogeneous polymer based on ethylene glycol phenyl ether acrylate. These materials differ substantially in microstructure, viscoelastic properties, water content, and fracture behavior, all of which may influence the relative contribution of cavitation-induced erosion vs direct mechanical fragmentation.

Second, different phacoemulsification systems were applied (Oertli system, 28 kHz, vs a modified dental descaler with a 30 degrees bent tip at 27 kHz). Tip geometry and the design of the handpiece significantly affect acoustic field distribution, shear stresses, and cavitation localization.

Third, the experiments were conducted at markedly different amplitude settings (100% vs 30%), which may alter the cavitation regime and the balance between mechanical oscillatory stress and inertial cavitation effects.

Fourth, continuous ultrasound was used in our experiments, whereas the overpressure study used pulsed ultrasound. Pulsed excitation may alter cavitation dynamics, bubble replenishment, and energy partitioning between low-frequency oscillation and transient cavitation events.

Taken together, these methodological differences may shift the relative dominance of inertial cavitation vs direct mechanical fragmentation.

This study has 2 main limitations. First, the experiments were performed in vitro using model substrates rather than human cataract lenses. Although additional experiments with formalin-hardened porcine lenses were conducted to increase biological relevance, these models cannot fully replicate the structural and biomechanical complexity of human cataracts. Second, the study evaluated only 1 phacoemulsification platform and a limited range of operating parameters, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings.

In addition to this study, variables such as tip geometry, or modification of the ultrasound mode may influence the relative contribution of cavitation and mechanical fragmentation and could be explored in future studies.

In conclusion, this study provides strong evidence that ultrasound phacoemulsification using a PCS handpiece functions primarily—if not exclusively—through a mechanical jackhammer mechanism and not by cavitation. Both simulation and experimental data confirm that the system does not pose a thermal or mechanical risk to sensitive ocular tissues, including the cornea.
