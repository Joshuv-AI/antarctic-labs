Abstract

Wildfires in boreal ecosystems represent a globally significant ecological process that is expected
to be sensitive to changing climate as well as forest management strategies, but is currently
insufficiently understood and represented in models. We sought to determine whether linking
belowground microbial community composition, size, and activity to aboveground properties of
burn severity and plant community composition (upland jack pine and upland spruce) would allow
us to better model post-fire soil CO2 fluxes using the Carbon, Organisms, Rhizosphere and
Protection in the Soil Environment (CORPSE) model. We used an integrated modelling-
experimental approach, with mechanistic laboratory experiments designed to inform models.

Key highlights of outcomes supported by this grant included training two early-career scientists and
an early-career PI, publication of two peer-reviewed papers, with two more pending, over twenty
scientific presentations, collaborations between US government scientists and academic
researchers that empowered innovative scientific approaches, and enhanced theoretical and
predictive understanding of post-fire microbial community dynamics and fire-induced changes to
microbial functioning.

Our findings indicate that C storage in boreal forests following wildfires will be driven by the
combination of C losses during the fire itself, fire-induced changes to the soil C pool that modulate
post-fire respiration rates, and shifts in microbial community function, all of which are dependent
to some degree on fire severity. Moving forward, more work is needed to expand this work beyond
fire severity to other changes in predicted future fire regimes in the boreal forest, such as decreased
fire return intervals and larger fire size.

Background and Project Overview

Boreal forests hold between 370-1720 Pg carbon (C) above- and belowground, making them a major
stock of C globally. In North American boreal ecosystems, wildfire is the primary stand-replacing
disturbance, and fires are projected to increase in frequency and severity in the future. Fires have
direct effects on C stocks due to immediate losses during combustion, but also have indirect
effects, through the modification of soil properties and remaining soil C, the response of vegetation
communities, and changes to soil microbial communities and their activities. Changes to fire
frequency and severity could affect each of these processes, and changes to fire severity or
frequency have, indeed, been associated with changes in soil C storage. However, data quantifying
these effects are sparse, and our understanding of the mechanisms driving them remains limited.

Recent advances in Earth system model (ESM) representations of soil C cycling, such as the
Carbon, Organisms, Rhizosphere and Protection in the Soil Environment (CORPSE) model, have
included an emphasis on explicitly representing the system in an increasingly mechanistically
accurate way. In particular, this has included: representing both physically protected and
unprotected C; distinguishing between rhizosphere and bulk soil; representing microbes as dead or
living, or as different functional types, among other developments. Because of their mechanistic
nature, these models may be better prepared to represent the effects of wildfires on soil C
dynamics than most current ESMs. Standard ESMs generally do not represent complex feedbacks
between fire, vegetation, soils, microbes, and climate. Rather, they usually represent fires only in
terms of their frequency and impacts on vegetation biomass pools, and predict that the above-
ground community follows a well- known trajectory over time, with no new steady states other than
those functionally similar to the previous state.

We addressed these challenges through our scientific objectives, which follow below along with our
original hypotheses and actual results:

• Objective 1. Quantify changes in C fluxes, microbial biomass, and microbial community
composition, after simulated fires in intact soil cores across a burn severity gradient

Original Hypothesis 1. Increasing burn severity will shift the microbial community toward fast-
growing recolonizing microbes. This will result in decreased community-level carbon use efficiency
and also be reflected in an increase in community-normalized mean 16S copy number. If the
microbial community shifts as hypothesized, this may result in changes in C flux rates (increase in
CO2 per unit soil C).

Actual Results for O1: Most of our findings for this objective were very on target with our original
hypotheses. Increasing burn severity did shift the microbial community toward fast-growing
microbes. This also did result in decreased community-level carbon use efficiency, and was
reflected in an increase in community-normalized mean 16S copy number. The surprisingly strong
correlation between these last two variables is of particular interest for potential future model
parameterization. Burning also did result in changes in C flux rates, but, counter to our predictions,
it actually caused a decrease in respiration per gram total (post-burn) C. This was driven by fire-

induced changes in the soil C pool, and indicates that soil C lost during a burn may be partially
offset by burn-induced decreases in respiration rates.

• Objective 2. Develop and evaluate adaptations to the Carbon, Organisms, Rhizosphere and
Protection in the Soil Environment (CORPSE) model to better represent post-fire SOC dynamics
across a burn severity gradient using data from Objective 1 across a hierarchy of model complexity:
(1) no explicit microbial representation; (2) community-level microbial traits characterized by single
scalable parameters – maximum growth rate, CUE, and total microbial biomass; (3) microbes
characterized as an assemblage of multiple functional groups with different traits constrained by
the observed experimental data across the microbial community.

Original Hypothesis 2. We hypothesize that soil C flux rates will be best predicted by the model
where microbes are characterized as multiple functional groups.

Actual Results for O2: For this objective, we first added a pyrogenic (fire-affected) carbon pool to
the original CORPSE, and then expanded that version to create a new version of CORPSE –
CORPSE-Multi Microbial Groups (CORPSE-MMG) – which includes (as of now) two functional
groups – slow- and fast-growing microbes. We parameterized both models based on CO2 flux data
from our soil incubation and our lab characterization of microbial community composition and
functioning, including measurements of carbon use efficiency. Counter to our hypothesis, we found
that the incorporation of microbial groups did not improve model performance (R2 values for the
two models were usually within 0.001 of each other). In addition to the two CORPSE models, we
fitted simple two-pool exponential decay models to each CO2 flux dataset, which offered very good
fits to respiration data for individual soil cores (R 2>0.998); however, we have not yet tried fitting a
general model for one set of soil cores and testing it on the second set, and then assessing the
goodness of fit, as a rough way of comparing the strength of the model to the microbially-explicit
models. As the modelling and its results are in the final paper we are currently preparing for
publication, we will plan to explore this approach as we work toward paper submission.

• Objective 3. Test the capacity of the adapted CORPSE model (Objective 2) to predict CO 2 flux rates
in burned cores from new sites

Original Hypothesis 3. The most microbially-informed (high-complexity) CORPSE model, which will
divide the measured microbial biomass pool into multiple functional groups, and parameterize
those groups using 16S copy number and CUE measurements, will better predict soil C fluxes
across a gradient of burn severity. We expect that this effect will be greatest for sites where the
majority of the microbial community is also present in the dataset generated from the soil cores in
Objective 1. Where sites lie outside this range, the mid-complexity model, which is parameterized
with the community-weighted mean 16S copy number and CUE, will perform better.

Actual Results for O3: We have not yet tested whether community correspondence to communities
from the soil cores for the dataset used to parameterize the model relates to how well the modified
CORPSE model performs in predicting CO2 cycling. Again, as the modelling and its results are in the

final paper we are currently preparing for publication, we will plan to look into this as we work
toward paper submission.

In addition to addressing our central scientific objectives, as outlined above, this project supported
research that formed the core of a PhD student’s dissertation (Dr. Dana B. Johnson). A brief
summary of her dissertation and its key findings follows.

First, working with intact soil cores, we investigated the effect of fire and fire-induced changes in
soil properties on post-fire soil respiration and explored the implications of reduced post-fire
respiration rates on long term soil C stocks. Burning caused a decrease in respiration per gram total
(post-burn) C driven by fire-induced changes in the soil C pool, indicating that soil C lost during a
burn may be partially offset by burn-induced decreases in respiration rates. Next, we used metrics
of resistance and resilience to explore the effects of burning on soil microbial community
composition. Microbial community resistance and resilience to burning varied across soil types
and between fungi vs. bacteria, highlighting the complexity of burn effects on microbial
communities. In addition, we explored how burning and varying burn duration impact microbial
community function as measured by changes in substrate-specific carbon use efficiency (CUE) n
boreal forest soils. Burning caused large decreases in glucose-specific CUE primarily driven by
decreases in microbial growth. The effect of burning on the CUE of ground pine roots was smaller,
which suggests that the effects of fire on community-level carbon use efficiency will depend on the
availability of various C substrates. Finally, we used the CORPSE model to investigate the role of
microbial functional groups in modulating post-fire soil respiration. We expanded the CORPSE
model by splitting the microbial biomass pool into two discrete microbial functional groups – fast-
growing and slow-growing taxa – each with unique decomposition abilities – and added a pyrogenic
C pool. While adding multiple microbial functional groups did not improve model agreement with
laboratory and field data, the two-functional pool model allowed us to observe temporal changes in
average community carbon use efficiency. Variation in microbial carbon use efficiency may be an
important part in linking post-fire microbial community recovery to the fate of post-fire soil C stocks
and fluxes.

Together, this research has helped advance understanding of Earth’s biogeochemical systems and
climate while developing insights into terrestrial cycling of C, and their feedbacks with Earth’s
climate system, thus strengthening scientific knowledge in ecology, biology, and biogeochemistry.

This project was a neat example of how collaborations between government and academic

with Dr. Sulman (Co-I), at Oak Ridge National Lab (ORNL). We worked closely together throughout

and expanded his original CORPSE biogeochemical model, to represent post-fire microbial

the formal collaboration between project investigators, we also worked closely with Dr. Kara

to ensure our fire simulations were tethered to ecologically realistic conditions, and were also
highly replicable. This collaboration offered a second perspective to our trainee on US government
science while also improving the science.

Traits-based Framework for Microbial Response to Wildfire – Field-Lab-Model Integrations

Our unusual and innovative approach of empirically identifying microbial traits through laboratory
experiments, and then pairing them with field data, resulted in a high-impact paper [Table 2, #2]
published in Nature Ecology and Evolution. Published in September of 2023, this paper has been
accessed 8,435 times via the journal website, and has already been cited 44 times. The initial lab
data collection was the result of Dana’s MS project, while the extended analysis, expansion, and
writing of the paper reached into her time as a PhD student, supported by this grant. Her
subsequent PhD research expanded on our initial approach, collecting and burning more soil cores,
and then measuring CO2 fluxes [Table 2, #1 - another highly-cited paper, currently accessed 6,909
times via the journal website and cited 20 times since its publication in 2024], and also conducting
very technical and labour-intensive carbon use efficiency measurements of post-fire communities.
What really extended the impact of this work, though, was the direct incorporation of all these
results in informing and parameterizing the CORPSE model. The strong interlinkages between all
aspects of Dr. Johnson’s PhD are nicely described in the Conclusions section of her dissertation,
which we attach as an appendix. One particularly intriguing finding is that the lab-measured carbon
use efficiency (CUE) is strongly correlated with weighted mean estimated 16S rRNA gene copy
number. CUE is very labour intensive and difficult to empirically measure, but is a key control of
many or perhaps even most biogeochemical carbon cycling models, whereas the latter metric is
readily derived from microbial community sequencing data, which is increasingly available. Should

this correlation hold, it could be highly influential in helping parameterize future biogeochemical
models across scales.

Summary

Our research directly addressed the ESS program’s goals of examining complex ecological and
hydro-biogeochemical processes within terrestrial systems to understand inherent and emergent
properties of changes to Earth and environmental systems, by improving understanding of
mechanisms and processes controlling the impacts of wildfire on microbial communities and
microbially-driven organic matter cycling in soils. Our research advanced the ESS goals in
terrestrial ecology by improving the representation of terrestrial ecosystem processes in Earth
system models, thereby enhancing the robustness of model projections and providing the scientific
foundation for solutions to the DOE’s most pressing science, energy, and national security
challenges.

The research conducted during the project has improved our understanding of the interactions and
feedbacks among aboveground wildfires and plant communities on belowground microbes and
soils in wildfire‐affected ecosystems. We have leveraged this understanding to improve
conceptualization and mechanistic representation of the effects of wildfires (“hot moments” in
both senses of the term) on the assimilation, stabilization, and mineralization of soil organic matter
stocks. By targeting two different boreal vegetation communities and a range of burn severities, we
achieved a new and improved predictive understanding of the microbial traits that impact critical
biogeochemical transformations across scales under a changing environment and Earth system.
Through hypothesis‐driven observations, model‐informed experimental laboratory and
manipulations, field studies, and C flux modelling, we have improved understanding of and helped
to explain the mechanisms and processes controlling the impacts of wildfire on organic matter
cycling in the boreal forests, and enhanced our ability to represent them in models. Together, our
findings will be relevant for ecosystem management, post-fire recovery, Earth system science and
modelling, and fundamental microbial genomics, ecology, and biogeochemistry.

 Chapter 6. Conclusion

As part of my research on wildfire and soils, I have spent many hours driving through boreal

forests. The drive to Wood Buffalo National Park is an exercise in patience as kilometer after

kilometer of flat forested land rolls by for hours on end. However, viewed from the vantage point

of a helicopter – a wonderful experience for which I am indebted to Parks Canada – a mosaic of

burned and unburned forest emerges, and one can begin to see the role that fire plays in shaping

these forests (Figure 1).

Figure 1. Aerial view of Wood Buffalo National Park one year after the 2023 Wood Buffalo Complex
Fire.
Fire has been part of these ecosystems for thousands of years, but the arrival of European settlers

to North America caused a shift in the relationship between humans and fire in the boreal forest,

temporarily obscuring the importance of fire in shaping these forests. Today there is some

difficulty in disentangling how fire drives ecosystem succession in the boreal forest from the

 178

compounding effects of climate change on both fire and organisms of the forests. In the field of

soil microbial ecology, we are left working to simultaneously understand the role of soil

microbes in boreal forest biogeochemical cycles, how fire-induced shifts in the soil environment

and microbial community composition affect biogeochemical cycling, and how shifting wildfire

regimes under novel climate conditions affect these relationships.

Measurements of microbial community composition and function in the immediate aftermath of

fire are relatively limited due to inherent difficulties in planning field sampling campaigns

around such an unpredictable event as wildfire. Unsurprisingly, the majority of soil

measurements collected after wildfires are from greater than 1 year post-burn and miss trends in

soil biogeochemical cycling that play out in the weeks to months after a fire. The primary goal of

my research has been to address this gap in post-fire soil science by asking what happens to the

soil environment and soil microbial communities in the immediate aftermath of fire and how

these dynamics shape post-fire soil C cycling.

The difficulty of isolating the effects of fire on any single part of the soil makes it challenging to

disentangle the effect of microbes on post-fire soil C cycling. Soil C cycling is shaped by a

combination of organic matter composition and availability, microbial community function, and

local environment conditions. Fire causes immediate changes to soil, such as raising pH,

volatilizing organic matter, decreasing C:N ratios, and killing soil microbes, which all potentially

influence soil microbial community composition over longer timescales of days to weeks. Thus,

understanding how fire alters soil C cycling requires piecing together the interactions between

burn-induced changes in organic matter composition, microbial mortality, and changes in

microbial community composition driven by changes in the soil environment.

 179

To circumvent the difficulties associated with sampling soil after wildfires, I used a mass loss

calorimeter in controlled laboratory settings to simulate the heat exposures characteristic of

boreal crown fires on intact soil cores collected from the Canadian boreal forest. The use of

laboratory burns has the advantage of dosing all soil cores with a measured heat flux as well as

allowing immediate post-fire soil sampling.

Using post-burn incubations of intact soil cores, we were able to observe an immediate and

temporary increase in soil respiration (per g post-burn soil C) that quickly recedes leaving soil

respiration rates lower in burned soils compared to unburned controls (Chapter 2). Based on

previous work documenting fire-induced plant and microbial mortality, we suggest that the initial

increase in soil respiration is likely driven by an increase in necromass from fire killing both

plants and microbes. Although microbes are undoubtedly killed in the fire (as evidenced by the

complete combustion of habitat at the soil surface), soil proves a good insulator against the heat

of fire and a robust albeit altered microbial community survives beneath the soil surface and is

able to take advantage of burn-created necromass. As evidence of this, we point to the increase in

16S rRNA gene copy number (a proxy for fast growth) of microbial communities and an increase

in the relative abundance of fungal saprotrophs 2 days post-burn.

Fits of a two-pool exponential decay model to measurements of soil respiration over a 70-day

incubation show a decrease in the fractional size of the fast-cycling C pool following fire,

suggesting that this potential pool of fire-created necromass is relatively small. Once depleted,

soil microbes must turn to other C sources that are less accessible and/or less metabolically rich

such as pyrogenic organic matter, resulting in a decrease in respiration rates, which persist in

burned soils.

 180

The continued elevation of 16S rRNA gene copy number in burned soils relative to unburned

control soils suggests that fast-growing taxa thrive in burned soil for months (Chapter 3). Fast

growth, however, comes at the cost of efficiency. The increase in 16S rRNA gene copy number

in post-burn soils corresponds to a decrease in glucose-specific C use efficiency (CUE) in O

horizon soil (Chapter 4). Decreased CUE can be driven by less C from a given substrate

converted to microbial biomass and/or more of the C lost from the soil as CO2. In this case, the

decrease in glucose-specific CUE in burned soils is primarily driven by decreases in microbial

biomass; burning had only a small impact on the respiration of C from glucose. In summary, in

burned soils, smaller, faster-growing microbial communities are respiring as much CO2 per g

available C as larger microbial communities in unburned soil. However, burning has no observed

effect on the CUE of pine roots, a more complex C source than glucose, which underscores the

complexity of microbial controls on post-burn C cycling. This is further reinforced by our

analyses of microbial community resistance and resilience to burning. The effects of burning on

microbial community resistance and resilience vary across soil type and between bacteria vs.

fungi. Low resilience of microbial communities to burning suggests that the recovery of

microbial communities to their pre-burn state likely takes years. Indeed, the burn-induced

enrichment of putative saprotrophic fungi and fast-growing bacteria persists for months,

evidence that burning and burn-induced changes in the soil environment have a lasting impact on

microbial communities.

Delving into the minutia of microbial biomass, community composition, and soil respiration in

the days to weeks after burning of intact soil cores can help us begin to understand how soil

function may be affected by wildfire. But the conclusions we can draw from these types of

laboratory experiments are limited in scope. For example, CUE measurements were collected for

 181

a single timepoint post-burn and sequencing data was collected for a total of four timepoints

extending to 70 days post-burn.

To expand the spatial and temporal scale of this work, we need to turn to another important tool

in the ecologist’s toolkit – modelling. By integrating laboratory data of post-fire soil respiration

and microbial community measurements into microbially-explicit soil C models, we can begin to

disentangle the drivers of post-fire soil C cycling (Chapter 5). The Carbon, Organisms,

Rhizosphere, and Protection in the Soil Environment (CORPSE) model represents microbes as

active drivers of the decomposition process, relying on CUE, maximum enzymatic

decomposition rates, and microbial biomass C pool size, and thus, serves as a good test for using

our laboratory measurements to model post-fire soil C fluxes.

To test the importance of microbial function on post-fire soil C cycling, we integrated two unique

microbial functional groups – fast growing taxa and slow growing taxa – into CORPSE. Our

post-fire soil C cycling simulations done using CORPSE suggest that C chemistry is a bigger

constraint on soil CO2 emissions than fire-induced shifts in microbial function in the first year

after a burn.

In Chapter 2, we observed that the extent to which depressed respiration in post-burn soils could

offset the large loss of soil C during a fire depends on the persistence of depressed respiration

rates in burned soils, i.e., how long it takes for the soil microbial community to recover to pre-

burn function. CORPSE offers one possible method by which to estimate the duration of

depressed respiration rates post-fire. However, integrating multiple microbial functional groups

into CORPSE did not improve model performance relative to laboratory observations of post-fire

soil CO2 fluxes. While it is possible that soil C cycling is not constrained by microbial function

 182

(or not by fast- vs. slow-growing functional groups), more data for parameterization and

calibration of these two functional groups is needed.

Specifically, better measurements of fire effects on CUE and fractional size of the total

community for these two functional groups would enable us to more accurately parameterize the

microbial component of CORPSE. Many soil C cycling models rely on CUE as a central

parameter controlling microbial decomposition of available substrates, which calls attention to

the importance of collecting experimental measurements of soil microbial function in order to

best inform existing soil C models. Our results highlight the influence of environmental factors

on soil CUE and support previous calls for a more nuanced representation of CUE in soil C

models, which may be made possible by using weighted mean predicted 16S rRNA gene copy

numbers as a predictor of CUE.

Additionally, there are numerous factors that may affect post-fire soil microbial function and

recovery that could not be captured in our laboratory burn experiment and are not currently

accounted for in our use of CORPSE, such as pyrogenic organic matter production, revegetation,

and unburned refugia serving as a source for microbial recolonization of burned soil. For

example, revegetation of the burned environment occurs in the months to decades after a wildfire

and is affected by seed availability, soil bed conditions, and local climate conditions.

Undoubtedly the return of vegetation to burned environments has a large effect on belowground

processes, but these relationships are difficult to disentangle and play out over longer timescales

than were considered in our laboratory experiments. Eventually, the integration of aboveground

vegetation and belowground microbial dynamics will be needed to advance soil C models.

Additionally, there is a high degree of heterogeneity in soil conditions within a forest.

Hummocks dot the forest floor, downed wood creates potential fire refugia, and soil moisture

 183

varies on a decimeter scale. This soil heterogeneity reinforces the heterogeneity of fire resulting

in a patchwork of burned and unburned soil on a decimeter to meter scale. Unburned areas may

serve as a source of soil microbes for burned areas allowing the recolonization of burned soil by

fire-sensitive taxa. There has been little work done to date on the role of unburned refugia in

structuring post-fire soil communities, and this remains an area of interest.

In summary, there are numerous ways in which fire may affect microbial communities, CUE, and

soil properties that are not explored here and may be important for understanding post-fire C

cycling. The persistence of fire effects on microbial community composition and function

observed in the CORPSE simulations may be important in understanding longer term post-fire

soil C dynamics in the field. A combination of field studies, lab work, and model development is

needed to understand both the timeline of and the mechanisms responsible for post-fire recovery

 184

of microbial community composition and function in order to better predict how shifting wildfire

regimes will impact the globally significant C stocks held in boreal forest soils.

Where does this leave us? Boreal forests have stood since time immemorial, quietly accruing C

(Figure 3) and hosting a diversity of plants, animals, and microbes. Wildfire has served as a force

of destruction and renewal for these forests for thousands of years by removing dead standing

wood and creating space for new seedlings to grow. Increasing global temperatures threaten to

upend this paradigm by shifting wildfire regimes toward hotter, larger fires that overcome the

capacity of forest recovery. The microbes living belowground are no exception to these trends –

higher severity fires will likely drive larger shifts in community composition and function and

require more time for the communities to recover to their pre-burn function if indeed recovery is

possible. However, in the aftermath of wildfire, these forests also emit less C in the form of soil

CO2 emissions than in their pre-burn state. The period of reduced soil CO2 emissions presents an

opportunity to offset some of the C released via combustion during the fire itself. The extent of

the offset depends on the time it takes for the forest soil to recover to its pre-burn soil CO2

emissions, which in turn depends on factors such as temperature, moisture, revegetation rates,

and the resiliency of the microbial community.
