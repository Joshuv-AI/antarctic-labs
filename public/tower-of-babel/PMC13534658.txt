Acute ischemic stroke and intracerebral hemorrhage are two distinct diseases in immunology
Authors: Yang Xu, Yanfei Qin, Yiyi Peng, Jiajin Chen, Yuqiong Li, Biqiong Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute ischemic stroke (AIS) and intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) are both cerebrovascular accidents where clinical management remains predominantly symptomatic, with few immunologically-targeted approaches. This cross-sectional study compared AIS (n = 73), ICH (n = 28), and controls (n = 31) in terms of demographic, clinical, laboratory, and immunological parameters, including regulatory T cells (Tregs), effector regulatory T cells (eTregs), CD4+ T cells, and cytokines. ICH patients showed lower total cholesterol (TCHO), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C) than both controls and AIS patients, whereas AIS patients had reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) versus controls. Both stroke groups exhibited higher proportions of Tregs and eTregs versus controls. Correlation analyses revealed divergent immune networks: in AIS, the percentages of Tregs and eTregs positively correlated with interleukin-10 (IL-10), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-2 receptor (IL-2R), whereas in ICH, these percentages negatively correlated with tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). We conclude that AIS features ‘compensatory immune regulation’, while ICH is characterized by ‘uncontrolled innate immune inflammation’, indicating two distinct immunological diseases with implications for subtype-specific therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57629-5.Keywords: Acute ischemic stroke, Intracerebral hemorrhage, T lymphocyte subsets, T regulatory cells, CytokinesSubject terms: Diseases, Immunology, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Stroke is a central nervous system (CNS) dysfunction caused by cerebral ischemia or hemorrhage, characterized by high incidence, disability, mortality, and recurrence rates, and has become the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of disability worldwide1. Cerebrovascular accidents include AIS, ICH, and subarachnoid hemorrhage, with AIS accounting for nearly 87% of cases2. Both AIS and ICH share common pathophysiological characteristics: an inability to meet the metabolic demands of the CNS, leading to brain tissue damage and neuronal cell death3. In AIS, this metabolic dysfunction primarily stems from impaired cerebral blood flow (CBF) due to vascular occlusion or systemic circulatory insufficiency4. In ICH, metabolic disruption is a secondary consequence of vascular rupture and subsequent blood extravasation4.

Inflammatory processes are integral to the pathophysiology of stroke, significantly contributing to both the primary injury and subsequent secondary brain damage5. In the context of AIS, neuroinflammatory responses are rapidly initiated within minutes of cerebral ischemia and continue through multiple phases over several days6. Similarly, in ICH, neuroinflammation is triggered by the presence of blood breakdown products within the brain parenchyma7. Both subtypes of stroke exhibit common neuroinflammatory mechanisms, which include: (1) the rapid activation of resident immune cells, such as microglia and astrocytes, (2) the recruitment and infiltration of peripheral immune cells, and (3) the subsequent release of pro-inflammatory mediators, including cytokines, chemokines, and reactive oxygen species8,9.

Inflammatory cascades exert dual effects: they aggravate secondary injury via blood-brain barrier disruption, neuronal apoptosis, impaired neuroplasticity, and cerebral edema5; yet they also facilitate neuroprotection through debris clearance, tissue remodeling, and neural repair5,8. T cells have been extensively studied for their dual role in regulating immunity and inflammation following stroke10. Among them, Tregs serve as a critical subpopulation of immune-regulating cells, playing a significant role in modulating stroke-induced inflammatory injury and subsequent immunosuppression11,12. Additionally, Treg cells exert distinct functions at different stages of ischemic stroke (IS)11.The complex and temporally dynamic nature of neuroinflammatory responses in AIS and ICH presents both significant challenges and opportunities for therapeutic intervention. Despite this, systematic investigations into the distinct immune profiles associated with AIS and ICH are notably limited, with few comparative analyses conducted between the two conditions. This critical knowledge gap highlights the need for a comprehensive exploration of immune responses in these contexts, which is essential for identifying actionable biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

This cross-sectional study comprehensively analyses peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets (including CD4+ T cells, Tregs, and eTregs), cytokine profiles, clinical baseline indicators such as blood glucose and lipid levels, and systemic inflammation indices to delineate the immune and inflammatory characteristics of AIS and ICH, provide immunological support for clinical treatment.

Methods

Type of study and included patients

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted in accordance with STROBE guidelines and approved by the Ethics Committee of Hunan Second People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2021K034). All participants or their legal guardians provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, and the study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. A total of 101 stroke patients (73 with AIS and 28 with ICH) and 31 age- and sex-matched healthy controls were recruited from the Second People’s Hospital of Hunan Province. The sample size was determined based on a power analysis using previous literature, with an alpha of 0.05 and power of 0.8, to detect a clinically significant difference in Treg cell levels. To minimize selection bias, participants were consecutively recruited. Measurement bias was reduced by using standardized protocols and blinding the laboratory personnel to group allocation where possible.

Inclusion criteria included: (1) diagnosis of AIS or ICH confirmed by CT or MRI, fulfilling World Health Organization diagnostic criteria; (2) AIS subtypes classified according to the 2023 Chinese clinical guidelines for acute ischemic stroke management; (3) time from symptom onset (or last known normal time for wake-up strokes) to hospital admission within 24 h; (4) age ≥ 18 years; (5) ability to provide informed consent.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) severe comorbidities (cardiac, hepatic, renal, or autoimmune diseases); (2) active malignancy; (3) pre-existing immunological or hematological disorders; (4) use of immunomodulatory agents within one month prior to enrollment; (5) history of severe dementia or psychiatric conditions impairing consent capacity; (6) contraindications to CT/MRI; (7) pregnancy or lactation; and (8) incomplete clinical data; (9) Confirmed active infection at admission, including aspiration pneumonia, urinary tract infection, sepsis, or other documented infections, based on clinical, laboratory, and imaging evidence.

Healthy controls were required to: (1) be aged ≥ 18 years; (2) exhibit normal hepatic and renal function, coagulation profiles, and complete blood counts; (3) show no evidence of active infection or systemic inflammation upon comprehensive evaluation; and (4) have no history of major chronic diseases, including diabetes mellitus or malignancy.

Stroke patients were further stratified by severity into mild (NIHSS score ≤ 10 or GCS score 9–15) and moderate-to-severe (NIHSS score 11–42 or GCS score ≤ 8) subgroups.

Specimen collection

Peripheral blood samples were collected from all participants by drawing approximately 6–8 mL of venous blood. All samples were drawn immediately upon admission, within 2 h of emergency department arrival, and before any surgical intervention or antibiotic therapy. The sample was distributed into one EDTA-coated tube for complete blood count analysis and Treg cell immunophenotyping, and one serum separator tube for biochemical and cytokine analysis. The serum separator tube was clotted for 30 min and then centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min to isolate serum. All analyses, including complete blood count, Treg cell detection, biochemical measurements, and cytokine quantification, were completed within 2 h of sample collection.

Routine clinical laboratory tests

Reagents for routine assays, including those for blood glucose, blood lipids, and complete blood count analysis, were supplied by Mindray Bio-medical Electronics Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China). Measurements were performed on a Mindray SAL9000 fully automatic biochemical analyzer and a Mindray BC7500 fully automatic hematology analyzer. The biochemical parameters measured were glucose (GLU), homocysteine (HCY), TCHO, HDL-C, LDL-C, non-HDL-C, triglycerides (TG), and uric acid (UA). The complete blood count parameters included platelet count (PLT), monocyte count (MONO), lymphocyte count (LYMPH), and neutrophil count (NEUT).

Analysis of Treg cells in peripheral blood

Utilizing precision micropipettes, 25 µL fluorescently labeled antibody cocktail (CD4 [cat. no. UB105441], CD25 [cat. no. UB112421], CD127 [cat. no. UB113451], CD45RO [cat. no. UB130411], CD45RA [cat. no. UB129481]; 1:200 dilution; UB Biotechnology Co., Ltd) was carefully dispensed into each flow cytometry tube. Briefly, 50 µL of whole blood was incubated with 25 µL of fluorescently labeled antibody cocktail for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Then, 450 µL of NH Lysis Solution was added to lyse red blood cells, followed by another 15-minute incubation in the dark. After staining, samples were analyzed on a DiagCyto 6C2L flow cytometer (UBBio technology), and data were processed using NovoExpress software (UBBio technology). Treg cells were defined as CD4+CD25+CD127− populations, and subsets were further characterized based on CD45RA and CD45RO expression patterns. The gating strategy is shown in Fig. 3A.

Correlation analysis between inflammatory cytokines and Treg level. (A) Heatmap of Spearman’s correlation between inflammatory cytokines and Treg level in AIS patients. (B) Heatmap of Spearman’s correlation between inflammatory cytokines and Treg level in ICH patients.

Analysis of cytokine levels in peripheral blood

The frozen serum samples were carefully thawed. Quantification of IL-10 (cat. no. 310041), IL-1β (cat. no. 309031), IL-2R (cat. no. 409021), IL-6 (cat. no. 404021), IL-8 (cat. no. 310021), and TNFα (cat. no. 312031) was performed using a fully automated chemiluminescence immunoassay analyzer (LA3000, Sichuan Orienter Bioengineering Co., Ltd, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. All samples and standards were measured in triplicate. The analyzer automatically calculated cytokine concentrations based on established standard curves.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed utilizing SPSS 22.0 software (IBM SPSS, Armonk, NY, USA), whereas GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) was utilized for data visualization. Quantitative and qualitative variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x ± SD), median and range, frequency (%), and 95% confidence interval (95% CI). Normality was assessed via the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using the Levene test. Associations between qualitative variables were examined employing the chi-square test. Comparisons of means across the three diagnostic criteria were conducted using one-way ANOVA or the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Missing data were minimal (< 5%) and handled using complete case analysis. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the impact of missingness. Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding outliers or using alternative statistical models to verify the robustness of findings. For Kruskal‑Wallis tests, effect sizes (η2) were calculated as H/(N‑1), where H is the Kruskal‑Wallis statistic and N is the total number of observations. Effect sizes are reported in Tables 2, 3 and 4, with thresholds of 0.01 (small), 0.06 (medium), and 0.14 (large).

Results

Clinical patient case enrollment process

A total of 312 patients and 50 healthy participants were initially assessed for eligibility. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 101 stroke patients (73 with AIS and 28 with ICH) and 31 healthy controls were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

STROBE flow diagram.

Changes in clinical data and routine laboratory indicators at admission for AIS and ICH patients

Table 1 summarizes the baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics of the participants. Compared to the ICH group, the AIS group had a significantly higher prevalence of fatty liver and smoking history. Systolic blood pressure was significantly higher in both patient groups than in controls, with the ICH group showing the highest values. Regarding hematological profiles, platelet counts were elevated in the ICH group versus controls. Monocyte counts were higher in both AIS and ICH groups compared to controls. Lymphocyte counts were lower in AIS patients than in controls. Neutrophil counts were significantly higher in both patient groups than in controls, with the ICH group exhibiting higher counts than the AIS group. Consequently, all calculated systemic inflammatory indices were significantly elevated, and the lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR) was significantly reduced in both patient groups compared to controls. Glucose levels were higher in both AIS and ICH groups than in controls. Lipid profiles differed among groups: AIS patients had lower HDL-C than controls, while ICH patients had lower TCHO, LDL-C, and non-HDL-C than both controls and AIS patients (Table 1).

Level of CD4+ T cells, Tregs, and eTregs in AIS patients with different NIHSS scores

In AIS patients, the moderate-to-severe group exhibited a significantly lower count of CD4+ T cells compared to the control or mild groups (p = 0.011 and p = 0.016, respectively), although no significant differences was observed in the absolute counts of Tregs or eTregs, however, the proportions of Tregs and eTregs in the mild group and moderate-to-severe group were notably higher than those in the healthy control group (p = 0.030, p = 0.012; p = 0.012, p = 0.013) (Table 3 and Fig. 2D).

Level of CD4+ T cells, Tregs, and eTregs in ICH patients with different GCS scores

There were no significant differences observed between the groups in terms of Tregs and eTregs, however, the mild group showed a significantly lower count of CD4+ T cells compared to the control group (p = 0.030), at the same time, an increase in the number of Tregs and eTregs can be observed, although there is no statistical difference (Table 4 and Fig. 2E).

Comparison of cytokine levels among control, AIS, and ICH groups

As shown in Supplementary Table S1, both AIS and ICH patients had significantly higher IL‑6 levels than healthy controls (both p < 0.001), and ICH patients had significantly higher IL‑6 levels than AIS patients (p < 0.001). Regarding IL‑8, both patient groups showed significantly higher levels than controls (both p < 0.001). In addition, ICH patients also had numerically higher IL‑8 levels than AIS patients, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. ICH patients exhibited significantly higher IL‑10 levels than AIS patients (p < 0.001). AIS patients had significantly higher IL‑2R levels than controls (p < 0.001). No significant differences among the three groups were found for IL‑1β (p = 0.162) or TNF‑α (p = 0.137) (Supplementary Table S1).

Correlation analysis between cytokines and Treg levels

The results of the correlation analysis showed that in the AIS patient group, CD4+ lymphocyte counts, Tregs, and eTregs were all negatively correlated with IL-6 (r = − 0.3507, r = -0.2930, r = − 0.2977), while Treg% and eTreg% were positively correlated with IL-10 (r = 0.3677, r = 0.4045), IL-2R (r = 0.2853, r = 0.3300), and IL-1β (r = 0.2894, r = 0.2605) (Fig. 3A). In the ICH patient group, CD4+ lymphocyte counts were positively correlated with IL-2R and TNF-α (r = 0.3449, r = 0.4567), while Treg% and eTreg% were negatively correlated with TNF-α (r = − 0.3273, r = − 0.3697) (Fig. 3B).

Discussion

Immune and inflammatory responses are pivotal factors that not only exacerbate tissue damage but also influence the recovery and long-term prognosis of stroke patients13. Previous studies have highlighted the critical role of the immune system in stroke pathology, particularly the involvement of Tregs in modulating neuroinflammatory responses and promoting tissue repair14. In the context of stroke, the immune system undergoes complex changes, including the recruitment and activation of various immune cell types, which contribute to both the primary injury and subsequent secondary brain damage15. This study focuses on the characteristics of immune regulation in AIS and ICH, revealing potential differences in disease development and treatment mechanisms.

During the occurrence of AIS, reduced cerebral blood flow leads to neuronal necrosis, releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and triggering local inflammatory responses. Neutrophils, as the first wave of infiltrating cells, produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), nitric oxide (NO), and pro-inflammatory cytokines, which damage the blood-brain barrier and exacerbate brain injury. Meanwhile, inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and TNF-α induce endothelial cells to express adhesion molecules, facilitating neutrophil adhesion and transmigration across the blood vessel wall16.

The baseline characteristics of our cohorts revealed distinct clinical profiles. The prevalence of fatty liver and smoking history was significantly higher in the AIS group than in the ICH group, aligning with known vascular risk factor profiles for atherosclerotic diseases17. Consistent with these profiles, our lipid analysis showed divergent patterns. AIS patients exhibited significantly lower HDL-C levels compared to healthy controls, a well-established risk factor for atherosclerosis and ischemic stroke18. In contrast, ICH patients presented with markedly lower levels of TCHO, LDL-C, and non-HDL-C compared to both controls and AIS patients. This pattern of hypocholesterolemia in ICH has been reported previously19, and this may reflect multiple underlying mechanisms. Several hypotheses may explain the phenomenon of hypolipidemia observed in patients with cerebral hemorrhage. First, cholesterol is a vital component of cell membranes, contributing to stability and fluidity. Chronically low cholesterol levels might theoretically reduce vascular endothelial membrane elasticity and increase fragility, potentially predisposing vessels to rupture—a mechanism that has been proposed in some epidemiological studies linking low cholesterol to hemorrhagic stroke risk20. Second, cholesterol serves as a substrate for steroid hormone synthesis. The significantly higher admission blood glucose levels observed in our ICH patients suggest a more intense acute stress response compared to AIS patients. Under such conditions, a surge in glucocorticoid synthesis could consume substantial cholesterol, leading to an acute depression in serum cholesterol levels21.

NLR, LMR, SII, and SIRI all indicate that inflammatory levels in the ICH patient group are significantly higher than in the AIS patient group. Although the proportion of patients with a history of diabetes in the ICH group was significantly lower than that in the AIS group, their blood glucose levels at admission were markedly higher than those in the AIS group, indicating a more pronounced stress response. A caveat regarding stress hyperglycemia and other complications is necessary. Stress‑induced hyperglycemia can independently activate systemic inflammation by promoting oxidative stress and pro‑inflammatory cytokine release. Therefore, some of the heightened inflammatory profile observed in ICH could be partially attributable to this stress response rather than exclusively to the hemorrhagic lesion itself. In addition, although patients with confirmed active infection at admission were excluded, subclinical infections or early post‑admission complications cannot be completely ruled out as potential confounders. Future studies should include measurements of stress hormones and systematic documentation of in‑hospital infections to further disentangle these effects.

These findings underscore that AIS and ICH may arise from and be associated with fundamentally different vascular pathophysiology. AIS is often the culmination of chronic, lipid-driven inflammatory atherosclerosis, leading to vascular occlusion22. Our data support this, showing a profile of low HDL-C. Conversely, ICH may involve vessels compromised by different processes, where altered lipid metabolism (reflected here as global hypocholesterolemia) might interact with acute hemodynamic stress or vascular fragility. Accordingly, the inflammatory and immune responses—including the role and regulation of Tregs—are likely to be context-dependent and differ substantially between these two stroke subtypes, as our subsequent data on immune cell correlations suggest.

Treg cells, originating from CD4+ T cells and constituting approximately 5–10% of peripheral CD4+ T cells, are a specialized subset with potent immunosuppressive functions23. Integral to immune regulation, Tregs mediate their suppressive effects on effector T cells and other immune cells through the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines, including IL-10 and TGF-β14. Studies indicate that ischemic injury recruits and activates Tregs to lesion sites, modulating immune responses and promoting tissue repair14. The regulatory role of Treg cells has attracted considerable research interest in recent decades. The research team led by Li-Peiying administered purified Tregs intravenously at a delayed time point in mouse models of cerebral ischemia. This intervention increased the number of peripheral Tregs. They discovered that Treg cells protect the brain from ischemia/reperfusion injury by attenuating the release of matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9) from neutrophils and maintaining the integrity of the blood-brain barrier. This mechanism significantly reduces cerebral infarction size and improves the recovery of neurological function24. However, whether they exert beneficial or detrimental effects on stroke outcomes remains controversial. Furthermore, Treg cells demonstrate distinct functions during different phases of stroke. Therefore, elucidating how Treg cells modulate stroke -induced immune responses is critically important11.

Our study found that the neutrophil counts in both disease groups were significantly higher than those in the healthy group. Notably, the neutrophil count in the ICH group was markedly higher than that in the AIS group. As previously mentioned, neutrophils, as early responders, play a pivotal role in the inflammatory cascade following stroke through their infiltration and activation. In addition, absolute Treg and eTreg counts were numerically but not significantly lower in both AIS and ICH patients compared to healthy controls, with the lowest values observed in the AIS group. Despite the absence of statistically significant differences in absolute counts, AIS patients displayed significantly higher relative percentages of Tregs and eTregs within the CD4⁺ T‑cell compartment. This pattern may be attributable, at least in part, to reduced total CD4⁺ T‑cell counts in AIS patients rather than genuine Treg expansion. Nevertheless, the elevated Treg proportion could still represent a relative compensatory adjustment within the CD4⁺ subset, potentially preserving regulatory function, in which the immune system adjusts the proportion of regulatory T cells to help modulate inflammatory responses. In moderate‑to‑severe AIS cases, Treg and eTreg percentages were highest among all groups. Given the significantly reduced absolute CD4⁺ T‑cell count in this subgroup, this finding should be interpreted as a relative increase rather than an absolute expansion. In contrast, moderate‑to‑severe ICH patients did not show a similar elevation in Treg or eTreg percentages compared to mild cases or healthy individuals, suggesting a potential imbalance in immune regulation. Taken together, these findings suggest enhanced relative immune regulation in AIS and possible dysregulation in ICH, although causality cannot be inferred from this cross‑sectional design. These findings align with prior research indicating that severe hemorrhagic stroke patients may experience immunosuppression and metabolic decline due to elevated stress responses12,25.

In AIS patients, Treg counts and eTreg counts did not show a positive correlation with IL-10, whereas Treg percentage and eTreg percentage were positively correlated with IL-10. This might suggest a relative increase in Treg proportion despite the absence of absolute Treg expansion, although the contribution of reduced total CD4⁺ T‑cell counts cannot be excluded. In ICH patients, no positive correlation with IL-10 was observed for Treg counts or Treg percentage; instead, Treg percentage and eTreg percentage showed a negative correlation trend with IL-10. These correlational findings are exploratory and do not imply causation. They generate the hypothesis that ICH may be associated with impaired Treg‑related regulation, but this requires validation in future mechanistic studies.

Our study delineates distinct immunomodulatory pathways in AIS and ICH (Fig. 4). AIS pathway: Characterized by ‘compensatory immune regulation’. The ischemic environment activates specific pathways, promoting the expansion of Tregs in an attempt to suppress inflammation and protect neurons. ICH pathway: Characterized by ‘uncontrolled innate immune inflammation’. Hemorrhage triggers a robust response dominated by neutrophils and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α), concurrently suppressing Treg regulatory function. This induces a vicious cycle of inflammation, exacerbating tissue injury. As directly demonstrated in Supplementary Table S1, ICH patients had significantly higher IL‑6 levels than both AIS patients and healthy controls, supporting an exaggerated innate inflammatory response. Notably, serum cytokine profiling further supported distinct inflammatory signatures between AIS and ICH. ICH was characterized by markedly elevated IL‑6 levels, whereas AIS showed a specific pattern featured by increased IL‑2R levels.

Differential immune regulation mechanisms in AIS and ICH. Left panel: Ischemia induces ATP, HMGB1 and mtROS release, activating dendritic cells (DCs) to secrete TGF-β and IL-2, which promote CD4+ T cell differentiation into Tregs. Inflammatory cytokines further drive Treg conversion to eTregs, elevating Treg and eTreg proportions. Right panel: Hemorrhage releases heme and iron, inducing neutrophil infiltration and IL-6 production. This suppresses Treg differentiation and promotes Th17 polarization. TNF-α induces eTreg apoptosis, causing dysregulated immune suppression.

In AIS, both Treg and eTreg cell populations showed significant positive correlations with IL-2 receptor expression levels. The ischemic milieu fosters Treg expansion through TGF-β / IL-2 signaling, correlating with higher Treg and eTreg cell populations—a potential adaptive mechanism to mitigate inflammation. Conversely, ICH exhibits exacerbated neutrophil recruitment and IL-6-driven suppression of Tregs, aligning with its stronger innate immune response. Notably, ICH patients exhibited a positive association between CD4+ lymphocyte counts and TNF-α levels. These observations suggest that AIS and ICH may influence disease progression through distinct immunomodulatory pathways: in AIS, Treg activation likely exerts neuroprotective effects through anti-inflammatory factors like IL-10, whereas in ICH, IL-6-mediated immune suppression coupled with TNF-α-associated alterations in CD4+ lymphocyte may exacerbate neuroinflammatory damage. However, as with all correlational findings in this cross‑sectional study, these associations do not establish causality. The proposed mechanisms should be interpreted as hypothesis‑generating and require validation in future mechanistic studies.

This study retains several limitations requiring refinement in future investigations: (1) Due to the low incidence and age of onset in ICH patients, case numbers and age distribution across the three groups were not matched; (2) As most patients were bedridden, body mass index (BMI) data could not be obtained for all subjects, precluding analysis incorporating obesity factors; (3) No dynamic observation was conducted; (4) The complexity of clinical conditions and treatment factors exerted some interference on outcomes, though these could not be fully controlled; (5) The sample size of the ICH group was substantially smaller than that of the AIS group and the control group. This imbalance reduces statistical power, particularly for subgroup analyses stratified by severity. Therefore, non‑significant findings in these subgroups should be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect Type II error rather than a true absence of biological differences.

Current treatment strategies predominantly emphasize acute interventions, such as intravenous tissue plasminogen activator thrombolysis for AIS and surgical decompression for ICH26,27. Nevertheless, these therapeutic approaches have limitations and frequently overlook the critical role of immune regulation in stroke recovery. Immune cells, particularly Tregs, are instrumental in modulating inflammatory responses and reducing brain tissue damage. In animal models of ICH, enhancing IL-10 secretion through Treg stimulation has been shown to alleviate ICH-induced cerebral damage28. This study comprehensively analyzed the relationships among Treg cells, neutrophils, and cytokines, revealing the complexity and diversity of immune regulation in AIS and ICH. These findings not only enrich our understanding of the immunopathological mechanisms underlying stroke but also provide new directions and insights for future research and clinical treatment.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study reveals differences between AIS and ICH in both metabolic and immunological profiles. Patients in the AIS and ICH groups exhibited distinct lipid metabolism profiles, with low serum TCHO, LDL-C, and non-HDL-C levels characterising ICH patients, whereas low serum HDL-C levels were characteristic of the AIS group. Immunologically, Treg% and eTreg% serve as predictive indicators of AIS severity; the magnitude of cytokine elevation acts as a predictive indicator of ICH severity. AIS exhibited a compensatory rise in Treg and eTreg proportions, which correlated positively with anti-inflammatory IL-10 levels. In contrast, ICH displayed a stronger innate inflammatory response, with Treg proportions inversely associated with pro-inflammatory TNF-α. These results indicate that AIS involves adaptive immunoregulatory compensation, whereas ICH is characterized by unabated innate inflammation potentially suppressing regulatory T cell activity. The detailed mechanisms warrant further investigation.

Compared with ICH, the relationship between the immune regulatory system and inflammatory mediators in patients with AIS is more complex, potentially involving a compensatory mechanism that counteracts inflammation. Conversely, ICH patients exhibit a more direct antagonistic relationship between inflammatory responses and immunosuppressive functions. This underscores the necessity for differentiated immune strategies tailored to strokes arising from distinct aetiologies.
