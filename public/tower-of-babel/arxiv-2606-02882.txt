Optimal stability of Pál’s isominwidth inequality for ball convex bodies in planes of constant curvature
Ferenc Fodor, Ádám Sagmeister

Abstract
. Pál’s isominwidth inequality (1921) answered the Kakeya needle problem (1917) for convex sets. It states that among convex bodies of fixed minimum width w w in the Euclidean plane, the regular triangle has minimal area. The isominwidth inequality was generalized to the 2 2 -dimensional sphere by Bezdek and Blekherman [ 4 ] and Freyer and Sagmeister [ 14 ] . Interestingly, in hyperbolic space, no minimizer exists, as shown by Böröczky, Freyer and Sagmeister [ 9 ] . The stability of the Euclidean Pál inequality with respect to the Hausdorff metric and the symmetric difference metric was proved by Lucardesi and Zucco [ 20 ] . Fodor, Robock and Sagmeister [ 12 ] proved r r -ball convex analogs of the isominwidth inequality in all three constant curvature planes connecting Pál’s theorem with the Blaschke–Lebesgue inequality. In this paper, we prove optimal stability versions of this statement with respect to the Hausdorff distance and the symmetric difference metric in all three constant curvature planes.

1.
Introduction

The concept of width plays an important role in convex geometry. The width of a convex body
K
(compact convex set whose interior is nonempty) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
in a given direction
u
is the distance between its two supporting hyperplanes orthogonal to
u
. The body
K
has constant width if its width is the same in every direction. The theory of bodies of constant width is a rich topic whose results have a wide range of applications; for more information, we refer to the book by Martini, Montejano and Oliveros
[
21
]
. One of the most well-known results about bodies of constant width is the Blaschke–Lebesgue theorem (Blaschke
[
7
]
, Lebesgue
[
18
]
), which states that the Reuleaux triangle has minimal area among plane convex bodies of fixed constant width
w>0
. The Blaschke–Lebesgue theorem was extended to the
2
-dimensional sphere
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
by Araújo
[
3
]
and Leichtweiss
[
19
]
under some smoothness conditions on the boundary. More recently, K. Bezdek
[
5
]
gave a proof in the general case when
w<\frac{\pi}{2}
, and Freyer, Sagmeister
[
14
]
proved the inequality when
w\geq\frac{\pi}{2}
. In
[
14
]
, the equality case was also characterized.
Böröczky and Sagmeister
[
10
]
proved the Blaschke–Lebesgue theorem on the hyperbolic plane
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
.
In the hyperbolic plane the Reuleaux triangle is the area minimizer. In the sphere, the Reuleaux triangle still has a minimal area if the minimal width is less than
\frac{\pi}{2}
.
However, if the minimal width exceeds
\frac{\pi}{2}
, then the minimizer is the polar of a Reuleaux triangle. We note that the Blaschke–Lebesgue problem is open in higher dimensions. Campi, Colesanti and Gronchi
[
11
]
proved that, among
3
-dimensional rotationally symmetric convex bodies of constant width, the body that one obtains by rotating a Reuleaux triangle about one of its axes of symmetry minimizes the volume.
Anciaux and Guilfoyle
[
2
]
proved some necessary conditions on the boundary of extremal bodies in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
with respect to the Blaschke–Lebesgue problem. Anciaux and Georgiou
[
1
]
have recently established that, among bodies of revolution in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
with constant width, the ratio of the volume to the cube of the width is minimal for the rotated copy of a Reuleaux triangle. We refer for further references and for an overview of problems of similar type in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
to
[
11
]
.

In 1921, Pál
[
22
]
proved a theorem using the minimal width that is similar in taste to the Blaschke–Lebesgue theorem. The statement is often referred to as the isominwidth theorem and states that the regular triangle has minimal area among plane convex bodies of a given minimal width
w>0
. The original motivation for Pál’s result was to answer the Kakeya needle problem (1917) for convex sets for which the isominwidth theorem provides the solution.
Although no higher dimensional analog of the isominwidth theorem is known, it was extended to the
2
-dimensional sphere. Similarly to the Blaschke–Lebesgue theorem, the minimizer is the Reuleaux triangle if the minimal width is less than
\pi/2
(K. Bezdek and Blekherman
[
4
]
). If the minimal width exceeds
\pi/2
, then the minimizer is the polar of a Reuleaux triangle (Freyer, Sagmeister
[
14
]
). Interestingly, no minimizer exists in hyperbolic space, as shown by Böröczky, Freyer and Sagmeister
[
9
]
. However, in
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
, if the problem is restricted to
horocyclically convex
bodies (also called
horoconvex
or
h-convex
, that is, the intersection of horoballs, which are Euclidean balls touching the unit ball in the Poincaré ball model), then the unique minimizer of the area with respect to the width introduced by Lassak
[
17
]
is the regular horocyclic triangle; see Böröczky, Freyer, Sagmeister
[
9
]
. Pál’s problem is also open in higher dimensions. The only
3
-dimensional result that we are aware of is due to Campi, Colesanti, Gronchi
[
11
]
, who proved that among rotationally symmetric convex bodies in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
whose minimal width is fixed, the rotation of the regular triangle about one of its axes of symmetry minimizes the volume.

Since the unique extremizer is known both in the (planar) Blaschke–Lebesgue and Pál theorems, the natural question of stability arises. An essentially optimal stability version of the Blaschke–Lebesgue inequality with respect to the Hausdorff metric was proved by Böröczky and Sagmeister
[
10
]
in all three constant curvature planes. The stability of the Euclidean Pál inequality with respect to the Hausdorff metric and the symmetric difference metric was proved by Lucardesi and Zucco
[
20
]
. Their results are not only of optimal order, but they have also determined the involved constants. The stability of the horocyclically convex case of Pál’s inequality in
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
was proved by Böröczky, Freyer, Sagmeister
[
9
]
, although with a non-optimal order of magnitude. Freyer and Sagmeister
[
14
]
obtained stability versions of the spherical Pál inequality both in the case when
w\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
and
w>\frac{\pi}{2}
. While the order is optimal in the first case, it is not optimal in the second case.

A further way to generalize the above statements is to consider the so-called
r
-ball convex bodies. A convex body is
r
-ball convex if it is the intersection of closed balls of radius
r
. The concept of
r
-ball convexity provides a unified framework for the Blaschke–Lebesgue and Pál problems. For a fixed minimal width
w
, let
r\geq w
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
, and let
w\leq r<\frac{\pi}{2}
in
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
. In
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, the case
r=w
provides the Blaschke–Lebesgue inequality, and in the limit as
r\to\infty
we obtain Pál’s problem. In
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, the limit case
r\to\frac{\pi}{2}
is the spherical version of the Pál-inequality. In
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
, the horocyclic Pál inequality is obtained in the limit as
r\to\infty
. The
r
-ball convex version of Pál’s isominwidth inequality was proved by M. Bezdek
[
6
]
for disk polygons (intersection of a finite number of equal radius closed circular disks) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. The general version in all three constant curvature planes was proved by Fodor, Robock and Sagmeister
[
12
]
. The precise formulation is provided in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
12
]
)
.

Let
K
be an
r
-ball convex body in
\mathcal{M}^{2}
where
\mathcal{M}^{2}
stands for one of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
or
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
. Let
w
denote the minimal width of
K
, and let
w\leq r
(if
\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, we also assume
r<\frac{\pi}{2}
). Then, we have

\area\left(K\right)\geq\area\left(T_{w,r}\right),

where
T_{w,r}
is the
r
-ball convex hull of a regular triangle with the same minimal width as
K
. We have equality if and only if
K
is congruent to
T_{w,r}
.

In this paper, we prove optimal stability versions of Theorem
1.1
with respect to the Hausdorff distance
\delta(\cdot,\cdot)
and the symmetric difference
\Delta(\cdot,\cdot)
; for precise definitions, see (
1
) and (
2
). Our main result is the following stability statement.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
K
be an
r
-ball convex body in
\mathcal{M}^{2}
of minimal width
w
, and let
w\leq r
(in the case
\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{S}}^{2}
we also assume
r<\frac{\pi}{2}
). Assume

\area\left(K\right)\leq\area\left(T_{w,r}\right)+\varepsilon

for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\overline{\varepsilon})
where
\overline{\varepsilon}
is sufficiently small. Then, there are a positive constant
c_{\delta}
and an isometry
\phi\colon\mathcal{M}^{2}\to\mathcal{M}^{2}
such that

\delta(K,\phi T_{w,r})\leq c_{\delta}\varepsilon.

Based on Gromer’s ideas
[
15
]
, we prove (in Section
4
) the following estimates that connect the Hausdorff distance and the symmetric difference on
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
and
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
.

Lemma 1.3
.

Let
K
and
L
be convex bodies in
\mathcal{M}^{2}
. Let
D=\max\{\diam K,\diam L\}
. If
\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, then assume also that
K\cup L
is contained in an open hemisphere. Then there exists an
\tilde{\varepsilon}>0
such that if
\delta(K,L)<\tilde{\varepsilon}
, then

\Delta(K,L)\leq c_{G}\cdot\delta(K,L)
for some constant
c_{G}
that depends only on
D
.

Lemma
1.3
yields the following corollary.

Theorem 1.4
.

Under the same hypotheses as in Theorem
1.2
, it holds that
there are a positive constant
c_{\Delta}
and an isometry
\phi\colon\mathcal{M}^{2}\to\mathcal{M}^{2}
such that

\Delta(K,\phi T_{w,r})\leq c_{\Delta}\varepsilon.

We note that the arguments leading to Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
also work in the limit cases, that is, when
r\to\frac{\pi}{2}
on
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
and when
r\to\infty
in
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
. Thus, Theorem 1.2 of Freyer and Sagmeister
[
14
]
and Lemma
1.3
yield the following stability statement for the symmetric difference on
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, which is the spherical analog of the corresponding statement of Lucardesi and Zucco
[
20
]
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
T_{w}\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
denote a regular triangle of width
0<w\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
. It holds that there are a positive constant
c_{\Delta}
and an isometry
\phi\colon{\mathbb{S}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
such that

\Delta(K,\phi T_{w})\leq c_{\Delta}\varepsilon

for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\overline{\varepsilon})
and all convex bodies
K\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
with
\area(K)\leq\area(T_{w})+\varepsilon
.

We also note that in the limit case
r\to\infty
in
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
, one obtains the horocyclically convex Pál inequality, thus, Theorem
1.1

improves on the orders of magnitude of the previously non-optimal stability result of Böröczky, Freyer, Sagmeister
[
9
]
, and we also obtain a stability statement for the symmetric difference.

Theorem 1.6
.

For
w>0
, let
T_{w}
denote the regular horocyclic triangle of minimal width
w
. If
K\subset{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
is a horocyclically convex body of minimal width
w
and
\area(K)\leq\area(T_{w})+\varepsilon
for
\varepsilon\in(0,\overline{\varepsilon})
, then there exists an isometry
\phi\colon{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
such that

\delta(K,\phi T_{w})\leq c_{\delta}\varepsilon

and

\Delta(K,\phi T_{w})\leq c_{\Delta}\varepsilon

for some positive constants
c_{\delta}
and
c\Delta
.

Finally, we note that optimality holds in the limit cases as well.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We gather some necessary tools and information in Section
2
. Theorem
1.2
is proved in Section
3
. We prove Lemma
1.3
in Section
4
that directly yields Theorem
1.4
. Finally, the optimality of Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
is verified in Section
5
.

2.
Preliminaries

We use the notation
\mathcal{M}^{n}
for an
n
-dimensional space of constant curvature with the geodesic metric
d
. Although many of the concepts used in the paper depend on which constant curvature space is considered, we simplify the notation by suppressing this dependence if it is clear from the context. We only indicate the underlying space if the clarity of exposition requires it. This rule is applied to all the quantities that appear throughout the paper.

For a pair of points
x,y\in\mathcal{M}^{n}
, the unique geodesic segment with endpoints
x
and
y
is denoted as
[x,y]
(where we also assume that
x
and
y
are not antipodal if
\mathcal{M}^{n}={\mathbb{S}}^{n}
). A set
K\subset\mathcal{M}^{n}
is
convex
if (in the case
\mathcal{M}^{n}={\mathbb{S}}^{n}
, we assume that
K
is contained in an open hemisphere and)
[x,y]\subseteq K
for all
x,y\in K
. We say that
K\subset\mathcal{M}^{n}
is a
convex body
if it is convex, compact, and has a non-empty interior.

The distance of a point
x
and a compact set
A
in
\mathcal{M}^{n}
is

d(x,A)=\inf_{a\in A}d(x,a).

The Hausdorff distance of non-empty compact sets
A,B\subset\mathcal{M}^{n}
is defined as

(1)

\delta(A,B)=\max\{\sup_{a\in A}d(a,B),\sup_{b\in B}d(b,A)\}.

For properties of Hausdorff-distance, we refer to
[
29
]
. For
\lambda>0
(we assume that
\lambda<\pi/2
if
\mathcal{M}^{n}={\mathbb{S}}^{n}
), the radius
\lambda
parallel domain of a set
A
is

A^{(\lambda)}=\{x\in\mathcal{M}^{n}\colon d(x,A)\leq\lambda\}.

Then for compact sets
A
and
B
,

\delta(A,B)=\min\{\lambda\geq 0\colon A\subset B^{(\lambda)}\text{ and }B\subset A^{(\lambda)}\}.

We use
\vol_{n}(\cdot)
to denote the volume (Lebesgue measure) of (measurable) sets in
\mathcal{M}^{n}
. If
n=2
, then
\area(\cdot)
is often used for the area instead of
\vol_{2}(\cdot)
. For
A,B\subset\mathcal{M}^{n}
, the symmetric difference distance is defined as

(2)

\Delta(A,B)=\vol_{n}(A\cup B)-\vol_{n}(A\cap B).

Groemer proved bounds between the Hausdorff metric and the symmetric difference metric in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
; see
[
15
]
*(1)–(4) on p. 108. For our purposes, the following inequality is important (this is (1) in
[
15
]
): Let
K
and
L
be compact convex sets in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with
K\cap L\neq\emptyset
, and let
D=\max\{\diam K,\diam L\}
, where
\diam
denotes the diameter of a set. Then

(3)

\Delta(K,L)\leq c_{G}\delta(K,L),

with
c_{G}=(2\kappa_{n}/(2^{1/n}-1))(D/2)^{n-1}
, where
\kappa_{n}
is the volume of a unit ball of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

We are unaware of
n
-dimensional spherical and hyperbolic analogs of this inequality (and, in general, those in
[
15
]
). However, we prove and use the analogs of (
3
) stated in Lemma
4.3
for
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
and
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
.

The notion of width depends on the space.
The
width
of a convex body
K
with respect to a supporting hyperplane
H
, denoted by
w(K,H)
, is the geodesic distance of
H
and a farthest parallel or ultraparallel supporting hyperplane to
K
if
\mathcal{M}^{n}={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
or
\mathcal{M}^{n}={\mathbb{H}}^{n}
. In
{\mathbb{S}}^{n}
,
w(K,H)
is defined with lunes. A
lune
is the intersection of two different hemispheres with non-antipodal centers. The
corner
of a lune is the intersection of its two bounding hyperplanes. A lune
L

supports
a convex body
K
if
K\subset L
and
K
intersects both hyperplanes bounding
L
outside its corner. The
breadth
of the lune
L
is the angle of the two hyperplanes bounding
L
, and
w(K,H)
is the minimum breadth of all supporting lunes to
K
such that one of their bounding hyperplanes is
H
. In all cases, the width function is continuous in
K
with respect to the Hausdorff metric and in
H
with respect to the topology of the sphere; for a detailed discussion of the hyperbolic case, see Böröczky, Csépai and Sagmeister
[
8
]
*Theorem 1.1 and Lassak
[
16
]
for the spherical case. We note that the hyperbolic width functions studied in
[
8
]
are different from the one we are using in this paper, however, this notion of width coincides with the so-called “extended Leichtweiss width” introduced in
[
8
]
restricted to supporting hyperplanes (see Lassak
[
17
]
*Proposition 2). For a fixed convex body
K
, the maximum of the width function among all supporting hyperplanes is the diameter of
K
, and the minimum width of
K
, which is denoted by
w(K)
, is monotone with respect to containment (see
[
16
]
and
[
17
]
).

In this paper, we consider
r
-ball convex bodies. We use the notation
B(x,r)
for the closed ball of radius
r
centered at
x
with respect to the geodesic metric in
\mathcal{M}^{n}
. In
{\mathbb{S}}^{n}
,
B(x,r)
is convex if and only if
r<\tfrac{\pi}{2}
. We say that
K\subset\mathcal{M}^{n}
is
r
-ball convex
if it is equal to the intersection of all closed balls of radius
r
in which it is contained, and if
r<\tfrac{\pi}{2}
if
\mathcal{M}^{n}={\mathbb{S}}^{n}
. Unless
K
is the empty set or a singleton, the interior of an
r
-ball convex
K
is non-empty, so
r
-ball convex sets with at least two points are convex bodies. The smallest
r
-ball convex set containing a subset
X
of some
r
-ball is called the
r
-ball convex hull
of
X
. If
n=2
, we use the term
r
-disk
for
B(x,r)
.
The intersections of finitely many
r
-disks in
\mathcal{M}^{2}
are called
r
-disk polygons
. It is not difficult to see that the boundary of an
r
-disk polygon
P
is the union of a finite number of circular arcs of radius
r
and, unless
P
is a disk of radius
r
, it has an equal number of arcs, called
sides
, and non-smooth points, called
vertices
. We introduce the notation
T_{w,r}
as the
r
-disk triangle with minimal width
w
where the centers of the three defining
r
-disks are the vertices of a regular triangle in
\mathcal{M}^{2}
. With the assumption
0<w\leq r
,
T_{w,r}
is unique up to isometry, and its minimal width is attained by the supporting line at the midpoints of the sides (see Fodor, Robock, Sagmeister
[
12
]
).

For a convex body
K\subset\mathcal{M}^{n}
, let

\varrho(K)=\max\{R>0\colon B(x,R)\subseteq K\text{ for some }x\in K\}.

We call
\varrho(K)
the
inradius
of
K
and a ball
B(x,\varrho(K))\subseteq K
an
inscribed ball
(or an
incircle
if
n=2
) of
K
. Inscribed balls of convex bodies are not unique in general, however, they are if
\mathcal{M}^{n}={\mathbb{S}}^{n}
, as well as for horocyclically convex bodies in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
(see Böröczky, Freyer, Sagmeister
[
9
]
) and ball-convex bodies in any space of constant curvature
[
12
]
.

We will call the domain bounded by a concave circular arc and two convex circular
r
-arcs touching the circle with the concave arc of the domain on its boundary a
cap
. The
apex
of a cap is the intersection point of the two convex
r
-arcs of the cap. A
cap-domain
is the union of a circle and finitely many caps (at least one). We have the following lemma from Fodor, Robock, Sagmeister
[
12
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
K\subset\mathcal{M}^{2}
be an
r
-ball convex body of minimal width
w
where
0<w\leq r
and if
\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, then
r<\frac{\pi}{2}
. Let
B=B(p,\varrho)
be the incircle of
K
with
\varrho<\frac{w}{2}
. Then, there is an
r
-ball convex cap-domain
B\cup C_{1}\cup C_{2}\cup C_{3}
contained in
K
such that
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}
are non-overlapping with apexes
q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}
with
d(p,q_{i})=w-\varrho
.

We also have the following formula for the inradius of
r
-ball convex regular triangles of width
w

[
12
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
T_{w,r}\subset\mathcal{M}^{2}
be an
r
-ball convex regular triangle of minimal width
w
where
0<w\leq r
and if
\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, we also assume
r<\frac{\pi}{2}
, and let
\varrho_{0}
be the inradius of
T_{w,r}
. Then

(4)

\varrho_{0}=\begin{cases}\frac{r+w-\sqrt{\tfrac{4r^{2}-(r-w)^{2}}{3}}}{2},&\text{if }\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{R}}^{2},\\
\frac{r+w-\arcosh\tfrac{4\cosh r-\cosh(r-w)}{3}}{2},&\text{if }\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{H}}^{2},\\
\frac{r+w-\arccos\tfrac{4\cos r-\cos(r-w)}{3}}{2},&\text{if }\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{S}}^{2}.\end{cases}

For
r
-ball convex bodies of minimal width
w
in
\mathcal{M}^{2}
, we have the following bound on the inradius (see Fodor, Robock, Sagmeister
[
12
]
).

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
K\subset\mathcal{M}^{2}
be an
r
-ball convex body of minimal width
w
where
0<w\leq r
and if
\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, we also assume
r<\frac{\pi}{2}
. Let
T_{w,r}\subset\mathcal{M}^{2}
denote an
r
-ball convex regular triangle of minimal width
w
. Then

\varrho\left(K\right)\geq\varrho\left(T_{w,r}\right)

with equality if and only if
K
and
T_{w,r}
are congruent.

For our main result, we will need the area formula for triangles in
\mathcal{M}^{2}
using two sides and the enclosed angle. In
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
and in
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
this is less known, so we cite it below. For the proof of the hyperbolic version, visit the PhD thesis of Frenkel
[
13
]
, while the proof of the spherical formula can be found in the PhD thesis of Sagmeister
[
23
]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
T\subset\mathcal{M}^{2}
be a triangle of side lengths
a,b,c
and opposite angles
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
. Then for the area of
T
we have the following equations depending on
\mathcal{M}^{2}
:

\cot\left(\frac{\area\left(T\right)}{2}\right)=\frac{\coth\frac{a}{2}\coth\frac{b}{2}-\cos\gamma}{\sin\gamma}\text{ if }\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{H}}^{2},

\cot\left(\frac{\area\left(T\right)}{2}\right)=\frac{\cot\frac{a}{2}\cot\frac{b}{2}+\cos\gamma}{\sin\gamma}\text{ if }\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{S}}^{2}.

We also need the following area formula for circular segments in
\mathcal{M}^{2}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
t\in(0,2r)
. Then, the area of a circular segment
S_{0}(t)
of an
r
-disk with chord length
t
is

\area(S_{0}(t))=\begin{cases}\frac{r^{2}}{2}(\gamma(t)-\sin\gamma(t)),&\text{if }\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{R}}^{2},\\
(\cosh r-1)\gamma(t)-2\arccot\frac{\coth^{2}\frac{r}{2}-\cos\gamma(t)}{\sin\gamma(t)},&\text{if }\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{H}}^{2},\\
(1-\cos r)\gamma(t)-2\arccot\frac{\cot^{2}\frac{r}{2}+\cos\gamma(t)}{\sin\gamma(t)},&\text{if }\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{S}}^{2}.\end{cases}

Lemma 3.3

4.
Stability with respect to the symmetric difference metric

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.4
. We note that for
\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, the limit case as

of the statement of the theorem was shown by Lucardesi and Zucco
[

]
. The general statement in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
follows directly from Theorem
1.2
and Groemer’s inequality (
3
). For the hyperbolic and spherical case, we use a similar argument to Groemer’s
[
15
]
. First, we need the following non-Euclidean planar Steiner formulas (see, e.g., Santaló
[
25
]
). For more details on the spherical Steiner formula, see Schneider and Weil
[

]
*Section 6.5.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
K
be a convex body in
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
or in

. For a positive number

, the area of the parallel body

of

with radius

(where in

, we also assume that

) is

\area(K^{(\lambda)})=\begin{cases}\cosh\lambda\area(K)+\sinh\lambda\per(K)+2\pi(\cosh\lambda-1),&\text{if }\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{H}}^{2},\\
\cos\lambda\area(K)+\sin\lambda\per(K)+2\pi(1-\cos\lambda),&\text{if }\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{S}}^{2}.\end{cases}

We also use the following.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
K\subset\mathcal{M}^{2}
be a convex body of diameter at most

. Then

(9)

\per(K)\leq\begin{cases}\per\left(B\big(z,\frac{D}{2}\big)\right),&\text{if }\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{R}}^{2}\text{ or }\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{H}}^{2},\\
\per(T),&\text{if }\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{S}}^{2},\end{cases}

with equality if and only if
K
is congruent to

if
\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{R}}^{2}
or
\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{H}}^{2}
, and

is congruent to
T
if
\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, where

denotes a regular spherical triangle of diameter (height)
D
if

and a Reuleaux triangle of diameter

if

.

Proof.

By the monotonicity of the perimeter, it is clear that if a convex body
K
has a maximal perimeter among convex bodies of diameter at most
D
, then
\diam K=D
.

Case 1
.

\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{H}}^{2}
.

Let
K^{\prime}
be a completion of
K
(i.e., a convex set
K^{\prime}\supset K
such that
\diam K^{\prime}=\diam K
and
\diam K^{\prime}\cup\{y\}>\diam K^{\prime}
for any point

). Then
\per(K^{\prime})\geq\per(K)
by the monotonicity of the perimeter.
It is known that complete bodies and bodies of constant width are the same in hyperbolic space (see Böröczky, Csépai and Sagmeister
[
8
]
), therefore
K^{\prime}
has constant width
D
. The hyperbolic version of Barbier’s theorem (see, for example, Santaló
[
24
]
*(7.3) on p. 411) states that

(10)

\per(K^{\prime})=(2\pi+\area(K^{\prime}))\tanh\left(\frac{D}{2}\right).

We note that Santaló
[
24
]
uses a different width function for the hyperbolic version of Barbier’s theorem, but for complete bodies both width functions have constant value that is equal to the diameter of the body (see Böröczky, Csépai and Sagmeister
[
8
]
). Thus, by (
10
) the perimeter of
K^{\prime}
is maximal if and only if the area of
K
is maximal.
According to the hyperbolic isodiametric inequality (see Schmidt
[
26
,
27
]
), the area of a convex set of diameter
D
in
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
is maximal precisely when the set is a ball of radius
D/2
. Combining this with (

), we get the statement of the lemma.

Case 2
.

\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{S}}^{2}
.

It is known (see Bezdek and Blekherman
[
4
]
) that the polar
K^{\circ}
of a spherical convex body

with diameter

has minimal width
\pi-D
. Furthermore,
\per(K)=2\pi-\area(K^{\circ})
. Therefore,
K
has maximal perimeter with a fixed diameter

if and only if

has minimal area with a fixed minimal width

. Hence,
K^{\circ}
is a regular triangle of minimal width
\pi-D
if
\pi-D\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
, and
K^{\circ}
is the polar of a Reuleaux triangle otherwise (see Freyer and Sagmeister
[
14
]
). This concludes the proof.
∎

We have the following inequality between the Hausdorff metric and the symmetric difference metric.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
K

\mathcal{M}^{2}
. Let

. Then

\Delta_{{\mathbb{H}}^{2}}(K,L)<4\pi\Big(\cosh\frac{D}{2}(\cosh\delta(K,L)-1)+\sinh\frac{D}{2}\sinh\delta(K,L)\Big)

and

(12)

\Delta_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}(K,L)<\begin{cases}4\pi(1-\cos\delta(K,L))+12\sin\delta(K,L)\sin D\arcsin\frac{1}{2\cos\frac{D}{2}},&\text{if }D<\frac{\pi}{2},\\
4\pi(1-\cos\delta(K,L))+12\sin\delta(K,L)\arcsin\frac{-\cos D+\sqrt{\cos^{2}D+8}}{4},&\text{if }\frac{\pi}{2}\leq D<\pi.\end{cases}

Proof.

We note that
\Delta_{\mathcal{M}^{2}}(K,L)=\area(K\cup L)-\area(K\cap L)=(\area(K)-\area(K\cap L))+(\area(L)-\area(K\cap L))
. We bound
\area(L)-\area(K\cap L)
from above.

(13)

\area(L)-\area(K\cap L)=\area(K\cup L)-\area(K)\leq\area(K^{(\delta(K,L))})-\area(K).

The rest of the proof is divided into two cases depending on
\mathcal{M}^{2}
.

Case 1
.

\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{H}}^{2}
.

According to the hyperbolic Steiner formula
4.1
,

(14)

\area(K^{(\delta(K,L))})-\area(K)=(\cosh\delta(K,L)-1)(\area(K)+2\pi)+\sinh\delta(K,L)\per(K).

Since
\area(K)\leq\area\left(B\big(0,\frac{D}{2}\big)\right)=2\pi\big(\cosh\frac{D}{2}-1\big)
from the hyperbolic isodiametric inequality and
\per(K)\leq\per\left(B\big(0,\frac{D}{2}\big)\right)=2\pi\sinh\frac{D}{2}
from Lemma
4.2
, we obtain

(15)

\area(L)-\area(K\cap L)\leq 2\pi\Big(\cosh\frac{D}{2}(\cosh\delta(K,L)-1)+\sinh\frac{D}{2}\sinh\delta(K,L)\Big)

from (
13
). We can give the same bound for
\area(K)-\area(K\cap L)
, and hence

(16)

\Delta(K,L)\leq 4\pi\Big(\cosh\frac{D}{2}(\cosh\delta(K,L)-1)+\sinh\frac{D}{2}\sinh\delta(K,L)\Big).

Equality could hold only if
K
and
L
are both circles of radius
\frac{D}{2}
, but then
\area(K\cup L)
and
\area\left(K^{(\delta(K,L))}\right)
can be equal only if
K=L
.

Case 2
.

\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{S}}^{2}
.

From the spherical Steiner formula
4.1
, we get

(17)

\area\left(K^{(\delta(K,L))}\right)-\area(K)=(1-\cos\delta(K,L))(2\pi-\area(K))+\sin\delta(K,L)\per(K).

Again, we use Lemma
4.2
to bound
\per(K)
from above. Let
T
be a regular triangle of diameter (height)
D
if
D\geq\frac{\pi}{2}
, and a Reuleaux triangle of diameter
D
if
D<\frac{\pi}{2}
. Then

(18)

\per(K)\leq\per(T)=\begin{cases}6\sin D\arcsin\frac{1}{2\cos\frac{D}{2}},&\text{if }D<\frac{\pi}{2},\\
6\arcsin\frac{-\cos D+\sqrt{\cos^{2}D+8}}{4},&\text{if }\frac{\pi}{2}\leq D<\pi.\end{cases}

Thus,

(19)

\area(L)-\area(K\cap L)<2\pi(1-\cos\delta(K,L))+6\sin\delta(K,L)\sin D\arcsin\frac{1}{2\cos\frac{D}{2}}

if
D<\frac{\pi}{2}
, and

(20)

\area(L)-\area(K\cap L)<2\pi(1-\cos\delta(K,L))+6\sin\delta(K,L)\arcsin\frac{-\cos D+\sqrt{\cos^{2}D+8}}{4}

if
\frac{\pi}{2}\leq D<\pi
. Using the same estimates for
\area(K)-\area(K\cap L)
, we obtain the bounds

(21)

\Delta(K,L)<\begin{cases}4\pi(1-\cos\delta(K,L))+12\sin\delta(K,L)\sin D\arcsin\frac{1}{2\cos\frac{D}{2}},&\text{if }D<\frac{\pi}{2},\\
4\pi(1-\cos\delta(K,L))+12\sin\delta(K,L)\arcsin\frac{-\cos D+\sqrt{\cos^{2}D+8}}{4},&\text{if }\frac{\pi}{2}\leq D<\pi.\end{cases}

∎

Using Taylor expansions in the estimates of Lemma
4.3
for the trigonometric and hyperbolic functions in terms of
\delta(K,L)
, we obtain the statements of Lemma
1.3
.

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
K
be an
r
-ball convex body in
\mathcal{M}^{2}
with
\area(K)=\area(T_{w,r})+\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon\in(0,\overline{\varepsilon})
. Note that the
r
-ball convexity of
K
implies that
\diam K\leq 2r
. If
\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, then the theorem is a direct consequence of Theorem
1.2
and (
3
). For
\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{H}}^{2}
and
\mathcal{M}^{2}={\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, the result follows from Theorem
1.2
and Lemma
1.3
.
∎

Proof.
