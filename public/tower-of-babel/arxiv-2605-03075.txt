Refining Compositional Diffusion for Reliable Long-Horizon Planning
Kyowoon Lee, Yunhao Luo, Anh Tong, Jaesik Choi

Abstract
Compositional diffusion planning generates long-horizon trajectories by stitching together overlapping short-horizon segments through score composition. However, when local plan distributions are multimodal, existing compositional methods suffer from mode-averaging, where averaging incompatible local modes leads to plans that are neither locally feasible nor globally coherent. We propose Refining Compositional Diffusion (RCD), a training-free guidance method that steers compositional sampling toward high-density, globally coherent plans. RCD leverages the self-reconstruction error of a pretrained diffusion model as a proxy for the log-density of composed plans, combined with an overlap consistency term that enforces consistency at segment boundaries. We show that the combined guidance concentrates sampling on high-density plans that mitigate mode-averaging. Experiments on challenging long-horizon tasks from OGBench, including locomotion, object manipulation, and pixel-based observations, demonstrate that RCD consistently outperforms existing methods. Project website at https://refining-compositional-diffusion.github.io/ .

Introduction

Planning plays a fundamental role in sequential decision-making, enabling agents to reason about future consequences before committing to actions. Classical approaches such as Model Predictive Control
[
72
]
and Monte Carlo tree search
[
67
,
68
,
40
]
achieve strong performance when accurate dynamics models are available, yet constructing such models for high-dimensional, continuous systems remains a significant challenge
[
31
]
.
Diffusion probabilistic models have recently emerged as a compelling alternative for trajectory planning, learning to sample directly from the distribution of feasible plans and incorporating task-specific guidance through auxiliary gradient signals
[
31
,
3
,
45
]
. However, the effectiveness of a diffusion planner is inherently constrained by the horizon of available training data. Collecting long-horizon demonstrations covering all possible start-to-goal combinations is prohibitively expensive, motivating
compositional
approaches that assemble long plans from short, reusable segments
[
52
,
81
,
48
,
51
]
.

Compositional approaches address this limitation by decomposing a long trajectory into overlapping local segments, each modeled by a single short-horizon diffusion model, and composing their score functions at inference time to sample from an approximation of the joint distribution
[
18
,
17
,
81
]
. This strategy is data efficient and modular, allowing a model trained on short data to plan over arbitrarily long horizons. GSC
[
52
]
pioneered this for trajectory planning by chaining short-horizon diffusion trajectories via score composition. In practice, this composition averages the noise predictions of adjacent segments at every denoising step on their shared overlap regions. CompDiffuser
[
48
]
additionally introduced a bidirectional conditioning mechanism that propagates information between adjacent chunks during denoising. However, when the local plan distribution is
multimodal
, this averaging-based composition suffers from
mode-averaging

[
51
]
. Adjacent segments independently commit to incompatible modes, and their averaged scores produce composed trajectories that lie in low-density regions of the true distribution. While CDGS
[
51
]
mitigates this issue through population-based search and DDIM-inversion-based pruning
[
69
]
, it incurs substantial inference-time overhead because many candidate trajectories must be repeatedly resampled, ranked, and filtered during the denoising process. More importantly, it improves final outcomes primarily through post hoc candidate selection, rather than by directly steering the denoising process toward more coherent plans.

In this paper, we propose
R
efining
C
ompositional
D
iffusion (
RCD
), a training-free guidance method that steers compositional sampling toward high-density regions of the local plan distributions while maintaining consistency across overlapping boundaries. We first show that the
self-reconstruction error
, the discrepancy between a predicted clean sample and its reconstruction after partial renoising, provides a differentiable proxy for the local log-density. High-density samples reconstruct faithfully, whereas off-manifold trajectories, including mode-averaging artifacts, exhibit large reconstruction error. We then introduce an
overlap consistency
term that penalizes score disagreement between adjacent segments at shared boundaries. Computed from pretrained local models alone, the combined signal directly steers denoising toward a tilted distribution that concentrates on high-density, globally coherent plans, mitigating mode-averaging without the population-based resampling and ranking overhead of prior search-based approaches, making RCD a plug-and-play component universally applicable to diffusion models.

Our main contributions are as follows:

(1)
We analyze the self-reconstruction error as a density proxy for composed trajectories and show its formal connection to the diffusion Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO), complemented by an overlap consistency term that measures score disagreement at segment boundaries.

(2)
We propose
Refining Compositional Diffusion
(RCD), a training-free guidance method that combines these signals to steer compositional sampling toward high-density, globally coherent plans using only a pretrained local diffusion model.

(3)
We evaluate RCD on OGBench long-horizon tasks spanning locomotion, object manipulation, and pixel-based observations, where it consistently improves success rates over prior compositional methods while running an order of magnitude faster than search-based alternatives, all without additional training or architectural changes to the local diffusion model.

Background

2.1
Planning with Diffusion Models

Diffusion probabilistic models
[
29
,
71
]
learn a data distribution by defining a forward process that gradually adds Gaussian noise and a reverse process that recovers the clean data.
Given a clean trajectory
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(0)}\sim p(\boldsymbol{\tau})
, the forward process produces a sequence of increasingly noisy versions
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(T)}
via

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)}=\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\,\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(0)}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\quad\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}),

(1)

where
\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}>\cdots>\alpha_{T}\approx 0
is a monotonically decreasing noise schedule.
The reverse process recovers
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(0)}
by learning a noise-prediction network
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)},t)
trained to minimize
\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)},t)\|^{2}]
.
Sampling proceeds iteratively from
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(T)}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t-1)}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)},t)+\sigma_{t}\,\mathbf{z},\quad\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}),

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\theta}
is the predicted mean derived from
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}
and
\sigma_{t}
is the reverse-process standard deviation.
Diffuser
[
31
]
applies this framework to trajectory planning by training a diffusion model over trajectories
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\mathbf{s}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots)
from offline data.
Task-specific objectives such as goal-reaching or return maximization are incorporated via gradient-based guidance at inference time
[
13
]
.

2.2
Compositional Diffusion via Factor Graphs

Consider a global plan
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}
decomposed into
M
overlapping local segments (factors)
y_{j}
, where each variable
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
represents a state or state-action pair and adjacent segments
y_{j}
and
y_{j+1}
share boundary variables in the overlap
y_{j}\cap y_{j+1}
.
Following the Bethe approximation
[
78
,
51
]
, the global distribution factorizes as:

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\tau})=\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{M}p(y_{j})}{\prod_{i=1}^{N}p(x_{i})^{d_{i}-1}},

(3)

where
d_{i}
is the degree of variable
x_{i}
in the factor graph (
d_{i}=2
for overlap variables,
d_{i}=1
otherwise).
Taking log-gradients yields the compositional score:

\displaystyle\nabla\log p(\boldsymbol{\tau})=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\nabla\log p(y_{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-d_{i})\nabla\log p(x_{i}).

(4)

In practice, each
p(y_{j})
is represented by a shared diffusion model conditioned on the noisy states of its neighbors,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(y_{j}^{(t)},t\mid y_{j-1}^{(t)},y_{j+1}^{(t)})

[
48
]
. For notational simplicity, we write
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(y_{j}^{(t)},t)
throughout, with the neighbor conditioning left implicit. The global score is computed by averaging noise predictions in overlapping regions
[
52
,
81
,
51
]
.
Bethe marginal consistency requires
p_{y_{j}}(x_{i})=p_{y_{j+1}}(x_{i})
for all shared variables
x_{i}\in y_{j}\cap y_{j+1}
.
When local distributions are multimodal, score averaging blends incompatible modes, pushing samples toward low-density inter-modal regions. This problem worsens with the number of composed segments.

2.3
Tweedie’s Formula for Denoising

When samples are perturbed by Gaussian noise
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\tau},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})
, Tweedie’s formula
[
64
]
provides the Bayes-optimal denoised estimate
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\tau}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}]=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}+\sigma^{2}\nabla_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\log p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}})
.
Applying this to the diffusion forward process in
Equation

1
, we obtain the posterior mean
[
11
]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(0)}|\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)}]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)}+(1-\alpha_{t})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)}}\log p(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)})\bigr)\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)}-\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)},t)\bigr),

(5)

where the score function
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
is approximated by
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)}}\log p(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)})\approx-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)},t)/\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}
.
The right-hand side of
Equation

5
defines the
Tweedie denoised estimate

\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}^{(t)}
, which serves as a one-step prediction of the clean trajectory from a noisy sample at timestep
t
.

3.1
Mode-Averaging Problem in Compositional Diffusion

Compositional score averaging as described in
Section

2.2
provides a reasonable approximation when the Bethe-style factorization is accurate and the composed score field faithfully represents the intended global distribution. In practice, however, both are only approximate, and the resulting artifacts are especially severe when
p(y)
is multimodal, where incompatible local modes can be averaged together. Consider two adjacent segments
y_{k}
and
y_{k+1}
sharing overlap variables.
If
p(y)
is bimodal, for instance the overlap variables can take values near
+1
or
-1
with equal probability, then
y_{k}
and
y_{k+1}
may independently commit to different modes.
When the scores from the two segments point in opposite directions, their average nearly cancels, leaving the overlap variables stranded in a low-density region between modes (near
0
).
The composed trajectory thus contains transitions that are individually implausible under any local model, leading to plan failure (
Figure

1(b)
). The probability of mode mismatch grows with the number of segments
M
.
Under idealized independent mode selection, the probability that all
M{-}1
adjacent pairs agree on the same mode decays exponentially with
M
. In practice, iterative denoising introduces correlations that slow the decay, but the qualitative trend persists and the valid rate drops sharply as
M
grows (
Figure

1(d)
).
Figure

2
illustrates how RCD addresses this problem.

3.2
Self-Reconstruction Error as a Density Proxy

The core observation of RCD is that a pretrained diffusion model already provides an intrinsic density signal. Samples in high-density regions are faithfully reconstructed through a noise-denoise cycle, while mode-averaged samples in low-density regions are not. This
self-reconstruction error
serves as a training-free density proxy for compositional guidance. Given a candidate clean trajectory
\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}
obtained via the Tweedie estimate in
Equation

5
, we assess its quality by measuring how well it can be
self-reconstructed
through the local models.

Definition 1

(Self-Reconstruction Error)
.

Given a candidate trajectory
\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}
, a shared diffusion model
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}
, and a probe noise level
s\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
, the self-reconstruction error is

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{recon}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0};\,s)=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\!\bigl[\,\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}^{\mathrm{rec}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0},\boldsymbol{\epsilon},s)\|^{2}\,\bigr],

(6)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}^{\mathrm{rec}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0},\boldsymbol{\epsilon},s)
is the composed Tweedie reconstruction obtained from the noised sample
\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{s}=\sqrt{\alpha_{s}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}+\sqrt{1{-}\alpha_{s}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
: each local segment
\hat{y}_{j,s}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{s}[j]
is independently denoised via
\hat{y}_{j,0}=(\hat{y}_{j,s}-\sqrt{1{-}\alpha_{s}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\hat{y}_{j,s},s))/\sqrt{\alpha_{s}}
, and the per-segment estimates are merged by the Bethe composition rule in
Equation

3
.

The probe timestep
s
controls the perturbation magnitude. In practice, we approximate the expectation with a single Monte Carlo sample. Intuitively, when
\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}
lies near a mode of
p(\boldsymbol{\tau})
, the score network
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}
accurately predicts the injected noise at level
s
, yielding a small reconstruction error.
Conversely, when
\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}
falls between modes, as occurs under mode-averaging, the score prediction is biased toward a neighboring mode rather than recovering the original inter-modal input, resulting in large reconstruction error.
The following proposition formalizes this connection.

Proposition 1

(Reconstruction Error as Density Proxy)
.

Consider the self-reconstruction error
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{recon}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0};s)
with squared-error distance.
Assume that each local denoiser
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}
is trained via the standard DDPM objective.
Then the weighted sum over probe timesteps satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{T}\frac{\alpha_{s}}{1-\alpha_{s}}\,\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{recon}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0};\,s)=\sum_{s=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\!\left[\,\bigl\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{\theta}\bigl(\sqrt{\alpha_{s}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}+\sqrt{1{-}\alpha_{s}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\,s\bigr)\bigr\|_{2}^{2}\,\right]\geq-\log p_{\theta}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}),

(7)

where
\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{\theta}
is the composed noise prediction and
p_{\theta}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0})
is the marginal likelihood.

The proof can be found in
Section

D.1
. Since
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{recon}}
at a single probe timestep
s
is one non-negative term of this upper bound on
-\log p_{\theta}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0})
, minimizing it is directionally aligned with maximizing the composed log-density, providing a principled justification for using
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{recon}}
as a guidance objective.

3.3
Overlap Consistency

The self-reconstruction error captures overall fidelity but may miss
local inconsistencies
at segment boundaries.
Two adjacent segments may each reconstruct well individually while predicting different values for their shared variables
y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}
.
We introduce the overlap consistency term to detect and penalize such disagreements.

Definition 2

(Overlap Consistency)
.

Let
\hat{y}_{1,0},\ldots,\hat{y}_{M,0}
be the per-segment Tweedie estimates from the reconstruction step of
Definition

1
.
The overlap consistency is

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{ov}}(\hat{y}_{1:M}):=\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_{k=1}^{M-1}\bigl\|\hat{y}_{k,0}\big|_{y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}}-\hat{y}_{k+1,0}\big|_{y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}}\bigr\|_{2}^{2},

(8)

where
\hat{y}_{j,0}\big|_{y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}}
denotes the Tweedie estimate of segment
j
restricted to the variables shared between segments
k
and
k{+}1
.

The overlap consistency is zero when all segments agree perfectly in their shared regions, a necessary condition for the Bethe approximation to be self-consistent, and grows when independent denoising pushes adjacent segments toward incompatible modes.

Proposition 2

(Overlap Consistency Measures Score Disagreement)
.

Let
\hat{y}_{k,0}\big|_{y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}}
and
\hat{y}_{k+1,0}\big|_{y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}}
be the per-segment Tweedie estimates restricted to the overlap region
y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}
.
Since both segments receive the same noisy input in the overlap (extracted from
\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{s}
), the overlap mismatch decomposes as

\displaystyle\bigl\|\hat{y}_{k,0}\big|_{y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}}-\hat{y}_{k+1,0}\big|_{y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}}\bigr\|^{2}=\frac{(1{-}\alpha_{s})^{2}}{\alpha_{s}}\,\bigl\|\mathbf{s}_{\theta}^{(k)}-\mathbf{s}_{\theta}^{(k+1)}\bigr\|^{2}\big|_{y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}},

(9)

where
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}^{(j)}:=-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{(j)}/\sqrt{1{-}\alpha_{s}}
is the score from segment
j
.

The proof can be found in
Section

D.2
.
Proposition

2
reveals that the overlap consistency measures the
score disagreement
between adjacent segments.
Bethe marginal consistency requires that both segments produce identical scores in their shared region, so
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}^{(k)}\neq\mathbf{s}_{\theta}^{(k+1)}
signals a violation.
When they disagree, the overlap variables are pulled in different directions, a hallmark of mode-averaging.
Minimizing
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{ov}}
thus encourages the marginal consistency implicit in the Bethe approximation, providing complementary information to the global reconstruction error.

3.4
RCD Guidance for Compositional Diffusion

Combining the self-reconstruction error and overlap consistency, we define the RCD guidance objective:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{RCD}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0};\,s):=\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{recon}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0};\,s)+\lambda_{\mathrm{ov}}\,\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{ov}}(\hat{y}_{1:M}),

(10)

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{ov}}\geq 0
weights the overlap consistency.
Following the training-free guidance framework (
Appendix

C
), RCD treats
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{RCD}}
as the loss function and applies its gradient to steer the reverse process. At each denoising step
t
, we first obtain the Tweedie estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}^{(t)}
from the current noisy sample
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)}
via
Equation

5
, then compute the guidance gradient with respect to
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)}
through the Tweedie estimate:

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}^{(t)}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{RCD}}\!\bigl(\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}^{(t)};\,s\bigr).

(11)

The gradient is normalized and applied as a correction to the standard reverse step
[
66
]
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t-1)}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)},t)+\sigma_{t}\,\mathbf{z}-w\,\sigma_{t}^{2}\,\tilde{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)},\quad\tilde{\mathbf{g}}^{(t)}=\frac{\mathbf{g}^{(t)}}{\|\mathbf{g}^{(t)}\|_{\infty}+\delta},

(12)

where
w>0
is the guidance weight and
\delta>0
ensures numerical stability. The resulting guided reverse process targets a tilted distribution
\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\propto p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\cdot\exp(-w\cdot\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{RCD}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}))
that concentrates on high-density, globally coherent plans, with the guidance weight
w
controlling the sharpness of this concentration (see
Proposition

3
in
Section

D.3
for the formal statement and proof).
The full procedure is summarized as pseudo-code in
Algorithm

1
, with further practical details provided in
Appendix

E
.

Related Work

Planning with Diffusion Models.

Diffusion probabilistic models
[
29
,
71
]
have emerged as a powerful framework for trajectory planning in reinforcement learning.
Diffuser
[
31
]
pioneered this direction by training an unconditional diffusion model on offline trajectories and guiding it toward high-return regions via a learned value function.
Decision Diffuser
[
3
]
introduced classifier-free guidance, conditioning directly on reward or constraint signals, while AdaptDiffuser
[
45
]
progressively fine-tuned the model with synthetic high-quality data.
Subsequent work extended diffusion planning to hierarchical settings
[
8
,
43
]
, multi-agent coordination
[
85
]
, latent trajectory spaces
[
76
]
, and inference-time tree search over diffusion rollouts
[
79
,
80
]
.
Monolithic diffusion planners, however, are limited by the training data horizon, motivating
compositional
approaches that stitch short segments at inference time.
GSC
[
52
]
chains skills via score composition, CompDiffuser
[
48
]
introduces bidirectional neighbor conditioning for smoother stitching, and CDGS
[
51
]
combines compositional diffusion with population-based guided search to mitigate mode-averaging.
A complementary line of work improves the reliability of plans produced by a diffusion planner.
LoMAP
[
37
]
projects intermediate diffusion samples onto a local approximation of the data manifold to suppress off-manifold artifacts, while RGG
[
39
]
detects infeasible plans via a separately trained, time-dependent classifier and resamples them, which requires additional training data.
RCD shares the goal of refining diffusion-generated plans but targets the compositional setting, where mode-averaging is the dominant failure mode.
Unlike RGG, which trains a time-dependent classifier on additional labeled data, RCD is fully training-free and uses only the pretrained local denoiser as an intrinsic density proxy.

Trajectory Stitching.

The ability to compose novel long-horizon behaviors from short trajectory fragments is a long-standing challenge in offline RL.
One broad category of approaches relies on offline data augmentation, where short or reward-suboptimal trajectories are stitched or synthesized and a downstream policy is retrained on the enriched data, spanning generative augmentation
[
41
,
30
,
36
,
38
,
9
]
, model-based stitching
[
6
,
28
,
84
]
, and clustering-based augmentation
[
25
]
.
RCD instead follows the
inference-time
compositional diffusion line of CompDiffuser
[
48
]
and CDGS
[
51
]
, which compose local diffusion models directly at sampling time and avoid regenerating the dataset.

6
Conclusion

We presented Refining Compositional Diffusion (RCD), a training-free guidance method that addresses the mode-averaging problem in compositional diffusion planning.
By leveraging the self-reconstruction error of a pretrained diffusion model as a density proxy and combining it with an overlap consistency term that penalizes score disagreement at segment boundaries, RCD steers compositional sampling toward high-density, globally coherent plans without additional training.
We established theoretical connections between the reconstruction error and the composed model’s evidence lower bound, and showed that the RCD-guided reverse process targets a tilted distribution concentrating on globally coherent plans.
Empirically, RCD achieves consistent improvements over existing compositional methods across OGBench tasks spanning locomotion, object manipulation, and pixel-based observations, with particularly pronounced gains on the most challenging long-horizon tasks where mode-averaging is most severe.

Limitations.

The current formulation operates within the Bethe factor graph framework of
[
78
,
51
]
, which assumes chain-structured overlapping segments. Extending RCD guidance to other compositional structures, such as hierarchical or temporal abstractions, or factor graphs with loops, is an interesting future direction. Another practical aspect, shared with prior compositional planners such as CompDiffuser
[
48
]
and CDGS
[
51
]
, is that the number of local segments used to determine the total horizon is pre-specified at inference time. Adaptively choosing it is another promising direction, for instance via tree-search-style horizon expansion
[
79
]
or by monitoring the self-reconstruction error along candidate horizons.

Impact Statement.

This paper advances compositional diffusion planning by introducing a training-free guidance method that refines long-horizon plans toward high-density, globally coherent trajectories. While we do not identify direct negative societal impacts stemming from this research, practitioners deploying diffusion-based planners in real-world decision-making systems are encouraged to carefully assess safety and reliability prior to deployment, particularly in settings where unsafe actions could carry significant consequences.

Diffusion and Flow Models for Offline Decision Making.

Beyond trajectory planning, diffusion and flow models have been widely adopted as policy and value classes in offline RL and imitation learning. Diffusion Policy
[
10
]
and Latent Diffusion Planning
[
76
]
train diffusion- (or latent-diffusion-) based visuomotor policies, and Ada-Diffuser
[
21
]
extends this line with a causal diffusion framework that identifies latent dynamics from short temporal observation blocks and applies to both planning and policy learning. Diffusion Q-Learning
[
75
]
and more recent flow-matching-based variants such as FQL
[
60
]
, DEAS
[
34
]
, MAC
[
55
]
, and DQC
[
42
]
combine diffusion/flow policy classes with Q-learning. floq
[
1
]
instead applies flow matching on the critic side. On a different axis, training-time data augmentations such as Proximal Action Replacement
[
14
]
progressively replace low-value actions in the offline buffer with high-value ones from a stable actor to lift the performance ceiling of behavior-cloning-regularized actor-critic methods. A complementary line treats guidance itself as a policy-improvement step
[
23
,
30
]
, including Prior-Guided Diffusion Planning
[
33
]
, which replaces the standard Gaussian noise prior of a behavior-cloned diffusion planner with a state-conditioned learnable prior concentrated on high-value trajectories. Sequence-level diffusion models have also been extended along the time axis: Diffusion Forcing
[
7
]
unifies next-token prediction with full-sequence denoising, FloodDiffusion
[
5
]
streams motion generation online, and DiffuserLite
[
15
]
employs a coarse-to-fine planning refinement process to accelerate inference. Structured variants of diffusion planners include Hierarchical Diffusion for Offline Decision Making
[
43
]
, which uses a two-level trajectory-level diffusion with classifier-free guidance, and Potential-Based Diffusion Motion Planning
[
49
]
, which parametrizes motion-planning potentials with diffusion models and composes constraints by summing the learned potentials, and HDFlow
[
53
]
, which couples a high-level diffusion planner over latent subgoals with a low-level rectified-flow trajectory generator for long-horizon robotic assembly. Diffusion planners have further been deployed across domains: Diffusion-Based Planning for Autonomous Driving
[
83
]
brings related ideas to driving-policy generation, and multi-robot motion planning methods
[
65
,
44
]
compose single-robot diffusion planners under collision and kinematic constraints. An empirical study of design principles by
46
systematically evaluates over
6{,}000
diffusion-planner configurations for offline RL,
12
identifies shift equivariance and local receptive fields as the architectural ingredients that enable diffusion planners to compose diverse trajectories from short BC-trained segments, and CleanDiffuser
[
16
]
provides a modular library that unifies many of the above components. A broader set of inference-time methods such as Inference-Time Policy Steering
[
74
]
and DriftLite
[
63

modify a pretrained diffusion model at test time without additional training, but target settings orthogonal to compositional long-horizon planning.

[

learn geometry-aware state embeddings tailored for long-horizon value estimation or temporal-distance prediction. Horizon-reduction strategies explicitly shrink the effective planning horizon and shift reasoning to a hierarchical or graph-based representation: SHARSA
[

Along the data-centric axis, trajectory-stitching methods synthesize longer trajectories

Inference-Time Search and Plan Refinement for Diffusion Planners.

A recent line of work explores explicit search or population-based selection over diffusion-generated plans. Monte Carlo Tree Diffusion
[
79
]
and its faster variant
[
80
]
perform MCTS over denoising trajectories, and Tree-Guided Diffusion Planner
[
32
]
balances exploration and exploitation through a bi-level tree-search sampling procedure. Within the compositional diffusion setting, CDGS
[
51
]
augments segment-level score composition with population-based guided search and plan pruning. Plan-refinement methods instead detect or project infeasible trajectories: RGG
[
39
]
trains a time-dependent classifier to flag infeasible plans, LoMAP
[
37
]
projects intermediate samples onto a local approximation of the data manifold, TAT
[
22
]
aggregates multiple diffusion-generated trajectories into a dynamic tree and filters unreliable predictions via weighted majority voting over shared states, SAGE
[
47
]
re-ranks sampled plans at inference time using a JEPA-based latent prediction error as a feasibility energy combined with value estimates, and AdaptDiffuser
[
45
]
generates synthetic trajectories via reward-gradient guidance, filters them with a discriminator, and fine-tunes the diffusion planner on the filtered high-quality samples in a self-evolving loop. In contrast, RCD uses the pretrained diffusion model’s own self-reconstruction error as an intrinsic density proxy, directly at the composed-trajectory level where mode-averaging originates, and requires no auxiliary classifier, manifold estimator, or search tree. Compositional Visual Planning
[
82
]
addresses long-horizon video planning by enforcing boundary residual constraints across overlapping chunks via message passing. RCD instead introduces a self-reconstruction density signal with a formal ELBO connection that explicitly targets mode-averaging, and applies gradient guidance on a single composed trajectory rather than residual-based message passing.

Appendix C
Training-free Diffusion Guidance

Training-free guidance methods steer diffusion sampling toward a target condition
y
without retraining the model.
Starting from classifier guidance
[
13
]
, which modifies the score via
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)}}\log p(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)}|y)=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)}}\log p(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)})+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)}}\log p(y|\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)})
, recent methods avoid the need for a time-dependent classifier by operating directly on the Tweedie estimate.
Given a differentiable loss function
\mathcal{L}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}^{(t)})
defined on the clean-data support, the likelihood term is approximated as
[
11
,
70
]
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)}}\log p(y|\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)})\approx-\gamma\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)}}\mathcal{L}\!\bigl(\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}^{(t)}\bigr),

(13)

where
\gamma>0
is a guidance scale and the gradient is taken with respect to
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{(t)}
through the Tweedie estimate.
This approach requires only a pretrained diffusion model and a differentiable objective, making it applicable to a wide range of tasks without additional training
[
77
,
27
]
.

Proofs

D.1
Proof of
Proposition

1
(Reconstruction Error as Density Proxy)

We provide the full derivation of the ELBO connection.

Per-segment ELBO.

For a single segment
y_{j}
with clean sample
y_{j}^{(0)}
, the per-segment reconstruction error at probe timestep
s
is:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\!\left[\|y_{j}^{(0)}-\hat{y}_{j,0}^{(s)}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\!\left[\left\|y_{j}^{(0)}-\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_{s}}\,y_{j}^{(0)}+\sqrt{1{-}\alpha_{s}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\sqrt{1{-}\alpha_{s}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(y_{j}^{(s)},s)}{\sqrt{\alpha_{s}}}\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\alpha_{s}}{\alpha_{s}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\!\left[\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(y_{j}^{(s)},s)\|^{2}\right].

(14)

Multiplying both sides by
\alpha_{s}/(1{-}\alpha_{s})
and summing over
s=1,\ldots,T
:

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{T}\frac{\alpha_{s}}{1{-}\alpha_{s}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\!\left[\|y_{j}^{(0)}-\hat{y}_{j,0}^{(s)}\|^{2}\right]=\sum_{s=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\!\left[\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(y_{j}^{(s)},s)\|^{2}\right].

(15)

The right-hand side is the negative ELBO for the local diffusion model
[
29
]
, providing an upper bound on
-\log p_{\theta}(y_{j}^{(0)})
.

Composed trajectory.

Let
\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{\theta}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{s},s)
denote the
composed noise prediction
: equal to
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{(j)}
on non-overlap variables
x_{i}\in y_{j}
and to
\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{(k)}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{(k+1)}\bigr)
on overlap variables
x_{i}\in y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}
.
By the definition of the composed Tweedie reconstruction
\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}^{\mathrm{rec}}
in
Definition

1
, a direct algebraic manipulation with
\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{s}=\sqrt{\alpha_{s}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}+\sqrt{1{-}\alpha_{s}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
yields

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}^{\mathrm{rec}}=-\sqrt{\tfrac{1-\alpha_{s}}{\alpha_{s}}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{\theta}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{s},s)\bigr).

(16)

Squaring and taking the expectation over
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{s}}{1{-}\alpha_{s}}\,\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{recon}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0};\,s)=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\!\left[\bigl\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{\theta}\bigl(\sqrt{\alpha_{s}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0}+\sqrt{1{-}\alpha_{s}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\,s\bigr)\bigr\|^{2}\right].

(17)

Summing over
s=1,\ldots,T
, the right-hand side is the DDPM simple loss
L_{\mathrm{simple}}
for the composed denoiser
\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{\theta}
.
By the standard variational argument
[
29
]
,
L_{\mathrm{simple}}
upper-bounds
-\log p_{\theta}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{0})
up to a timestep re-weighting, where
p_{\theta}
is the distribution implicitly defined by the composed denoiser
\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{\theta}
.
This establishes the inequality in
Equation

7
. ∎

D.2
Proof of
Proposition

2
(Overlap Consistency Measures Score Disagreement)

Both segments
k
and
k{+}1
receive the same noisy trajectory
\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{s}
in the overlap region
y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}
.
Let
z:=\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{s}\big|_{y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}}
be the common noisy overlap.
The Tweedie estimates from each segment in the overlap are:

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{k,0}\big|_{y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{z-\sqrt{1{-}\alpha_{s}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{(k)}(y_{k}^{(s)},s)}{\sqrt{\alpha_{s}}},

(18)

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{k+1,0}\big|_{y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{z-\sqrt{1{-}\alpha_{s}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{(k+1)}(y_{k+1}^{(s)},s)}{\sqrt{\alpha_{s}}},

(19)

where
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{(j)}(y_{j}^{(s)},s)
is the noise prediction from segment
j
evaluated on its full noisy input
y_{j}^{(s)}
.
Taking the difference:

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{k,0}\big|_{y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}}-\hat{y}_{k+1,0}\big|_{y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}}=\frac{\sqrt{1{-}\alpha_{s}}}{\sqrt{\alpha_{s}}}\,\bigl(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{(k+1)}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{(k)}\bigr)\big|_{y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}}.

(20)

Squaring yields:

\displaystyle\|\hat{y}_{k,0}-\hat{y}_{k+1,0}\|^{2}\big|_{y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}}=\frac{1{-}\alpha_{s}}{\alpha_{s}}\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{(k+1)}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{(k)}\|^{2}\big|_{y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}}.

(21)

Substituting the score identity
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{(j)}=-\sqrt{1{-}\alpha_{s}}\,\mathbf{s}_{\theta}^{(j)}
from
Section

2.3
yields
Equation

9
. ∎

Connection to marginal consistency.

In the Bethe approximation, marginal consistency requires
p_{k}(x_{i})=p_{k+1}(x_{i})
for all
x_{i}\in y_{k}\cap y_{k+1}
.
Taking gradients:
\nabla_{x_{i}}\log p_{k}(x_{i})=\nabla_{x_{i}}\log p_{k+1}(x_{i})
, i.e., scores from both segments must agree at overlap variables.

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{ov}}
is a relaxation of this hard constraint, penalizing violations via the squared score difference.

D.3
Proof of
Proposition

3
(Guided Distribution)

Proposition 3

(Guided Distribution)
.

Let
p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
denote the composed distribution obtained via score averaging.
The RCD-guided reverse process targets the modified distribution

\displaystyle\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\propto p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\cdot\exp\bigl(-w\cdot\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{RCD}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\bigr),

(22)

which up-weights high-density, globally coherent plans and down-weights mode-averaged plans.

Proof.

Consider the reverse-time SDE for the composed distribution
p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\tau})

[
71
]
:

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tau}=\bigl[f(\boldsymbol{\tau},t)-g(t)^{2}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\bigr]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{\boldsymbol{w}},

(23)

where
f
and
g
are the drift and diffusion coefficients and
\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}
is a reverse-time Wiener process.

The RCD guidance modifies the score by adding
-w\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{RCD}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
:

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tau}=\bigl[f(\boldsymbol{\tau},t)-g(t)^{2}\bigl(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{\tau})-w\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{RCD}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\bigr)\bigr]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}.

(24)

This is the reverse SDE for the modified distribution:

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\propto p_{t}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\cdot\exp\bigl(-w\cdot\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{RCD}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\bigr),

(25)

since
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\log\tilde{p}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\tau})=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\log p_{t}(\boldsymbol{\tau})-w\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{RCD}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
.

At
t=0
, the marginal is
\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\propto p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\cdot\exp(-w\cdot\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{RCD}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}))
.
Since
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{RCD}}\geq 0
with equality for perfectly reconstructed, globally coherent plans, the exponential factor
\exp(-w\,\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{RCD}})
up-weights plans with low reconstruction error and high overlap consistency, while down-weighting mode-averaged plans.
∎

s
) and one backward pass for the gradient.
The expectation in
Equation

6
is approximated with a single Monte Carlo noise sample.
The complete procedure is summarized in
Algorithm

1
.

F.4
Evaluation Protocol

We follow the OGBench evaluation protocol
[
56
]
.
Each environment specifies 5 test-time start-goal pairs.
For each pair, we run 20 episodes and report the binary success rate (reaching within a threshold distance of the goal).
Results are averaged over 5 random seeds, and we report mean
\pm
standard deviation.
For replanning experiments, we adopt the adaptive replanning strategy proposed by CompDiffuser
[
48
]
: replanning is triggered when the agent deviates from its current subgoal beyond a threshold distance, and a receding scheme progressively reduces the number of composed segments based on how much of the current plan has been executed to encourage faster convergence toward the goal.

F.5
Compute Resources

All experiments, including training of the local diffusion models and all evaluations are conducted on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU.

F.6
Low-Level Controllers

The diffusion planner generates state-space trajectories, and a separate low-level controller converts these into executable actions.

Locomotion and AntSoccer.
Following CompDiffuser
[
48
]
, we train an MLP-based inverse dynamics model that takes consecutive states
(s_{t},s_{t+1})
as input and predicts the action
a_{t}
. The same model architecture and training procedure from CompDiffuser is used.

Cube Manipulation.
We use a DQL-based value-learning policy
[
75
]
that takes the current state and a planned subgoal as input and outputs actions. The DQL policy is trained on the same offline dataset used for the diffusion planner.

Visual AntMaze.
We train an inverse dynamics model in the VAE latent space that maps consecutive latent states
(z_{t},z_{t+1})
to actions, following a similar architecture to the state-based inverse dynamics but operating on 16-dimensional latent representations.
