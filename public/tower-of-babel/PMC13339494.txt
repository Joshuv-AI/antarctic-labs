Patient‐Centric First‐in‐Human Dose Selection: An Ex Vivo Minimal Anticipated Biological Effect Level Approach for T‐Cell Engagers in Multiple Myeloma
Authors: Thomas E. Kraft, Estelle Marrer‐Berger, Sara Belli, Helene Haegel, Anneliese Schneider, Anna Maria Giusti, Michael Bscheider, Wolfgang Jacob, Martin Weisser, Laura Blanco, Nicolas Frances, Eva Rossmann, Guido Steiner, Aintzane Zabaleta Azpiroz, Bruno Paiva, Ludovic Martinet, Tanja Fauti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The minimal anticipated biological effect level (MABEL) approach to first‐in‐human (FIH) dose calculation can lead to long dose‐escalation periods before observing clinical activity. We used a novel ex vivo MABEL approach to calculate the FIH starting dose for forimtamig, a T‐cell bispecific antibody under investigation for the treatment of relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM). We used in vitro and ex vivo MABEL approaches to calculate the FIH dose for a Phase 1 trial (NCT04557150) in patients with RRMM. In vitro assays used the OPM‐2 MM cell line and peripheral blood mononucleocytes from healthy donors and patients with MM. Ex vivo assays used fresh bone marrow aspirates from MM patient donors. Forimtamig increased tumor cell lysis, cytokine release, and T‐cell activation in vitro and ex vivo. Tumor cell lysis was considered the most relevant assay for dose calculation. EC50 for LDH release (in vitro: mean 0.6 pM; ex vivo: 9 pM for the most sensitive patient) led to calculated FIH starting doses of 0.4 and 6 μg, respectively. FIH starting dose was 6 μg. Clinical activity was observed in the second dosing cohort (18 and 54 μg). The third dosing cohort (54 and 162 μg) showed durable objective responses. Cytokine release syndrome was Grade 1 at the starting dose and absent in the second dosing cohort. Our ex vivo MABEL approach was accepted by regulatory authorities and yielded a tolerated forimtamig FIH starting dose which demonstrated clinical activity in the second dosing cohort. We encourage similar innovative patient‐centric approaches to FIH dose calculation.

Similar patient‐centric approaches to FIH dose calculation for T‐cell engagers could reduce the number of vulnerable patients receiving sub‐therapeutic doses in Phase 1 trials. We suggest testing similar strategies wherever possible to maximize potential benefit while minimizing safety risks.

Phase 1 trials are the first stage in clinical drug development, where new treatments or combinations are tested in humans. 1 Following extensive nonclinical in vitro and in vivo studies, these trials assess safety and clinical activity, paving the way towards approval and patient benefits. Participants in Phase 1 oncology trials often face urgent needs, having exhausted other therapy options. 1 In particular, individuals with relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM) are typically aged over 60 years and have undergone numerous prior lines of therapy. 2 , 3 , 4 Median overall survival for patients with triple‐class refractory MM can be less than 10 months. 5 These patients therefore need treatments that are well tolerated but also efficacious. It is of vital importance that Phase 1 trials are designed ethically, to maximize potential for disease control, while limiting risks of treatment‐related adverse events (AEs). 1 The pivotal basis for initiation of Phase 1 clinical trials is the definition of the first‐in‐human (FIH) starting dose.

Calculation of the FIH dose of immunomodulatory drugs is complex, with immunostimulators such as T‐cell engagers being extremely potent and having risks for complications such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS) that necessitate low starting doses. 6 , 7 Experience of the TGN1412 FIH Phase 1 trial led to adoption of the minimal anticipated biological effect level (MABEL) approach to calculating a safe starting dose for immunomodulators. 6 , 8 , 9 The minimal biological effect of T‐cell engagers may be evaluated in a variety of in vitro assays, such as for T‐cell activation, cytokine release and cytotoxicity. 10 , 11 These assays typically involve incubating the study drug with tumor cell lines and effector cells from healthy donors, rather than tissue originating from patients with the target disease, and with a higher ratio of effector to tumor cells (E:T) than is seen in vivo 10 , 12 Furthermore, differences in target expression may pose challenges in translation to human relevant doses. 10 , 13 Patient‐derived cells are biologically more relevant than cell lines and better represent inter‐patient variability, but can be difficult to source. 12

For early T‐cell engagers, the EC20 from the most sensitive assay or model was often used to select the FIH dose. 10 , 12 However, this classic MABEL approach often leads to a conservative first dose and consequently long dose‐escalation periods at sub‐therapeutic levels to reach an efficacious dose, neither of which adds benefit to the vulnerable participants of Phase 1 clinical trials. 1 , 6 , 10 , 11

While there is growing recognition of the limitations of classic MABEL and the potential for modified approaches to FIH dosing, 6 , 10 , 12 little first‐hand experience of successful alternative strategies has been published. Zhou et al. used translational PK/PD modeling with MABEL to calculate an FIH dose for a bispecific T‐cell engager and reported safety observations from the first two doses. 14 However, the pharmacological activity of the starting dose was not published, and the trial has been terminated. 15 A PK/PD model with an endpoint of cytokine release in cynomolgus monkeys was used to calculate the FIH dose of glofitamab 16 ; in the subsequent Phase 1 trial, significant clinical activity was observed at doses much higher than the starting dose in the first patient. 17 Ji et al. used a model‐based approach to determine the minimum effective level and the resulting FIH dose of a monoclonal antibody, but the initial efficacy data showed only modest clinical benefit. 18 , 19 A cytotoxicity assay using frozen patient samples was used to inform the Phase 1 dose of talquetamab, but the calculation of the FIH dose is not disclosed. 20 , 21 Other descriptions of modified MABEL approaches similarly do not provide the full picture of safety and efficacy in the first patients. 10 , 22

Forimtamig (RO7425781) is a T‐cell bispecific antibody with a 2 + 1 format targeting GPRC5D (present on MM tumor cells) and CD3 (present on T cells). 23 Following promising characterization of forimtamig in humanized mouse models for efficacy, as well as the nonclinical assessment of safety risk, we began planning for a Phase 1 clinical trial in patients with RRMM. We employed a novel ex vivo MABEL approach to optimize the FIH starting dose of forimtamig, with the goal of maximizing patient benefit and minimizing the safety risk. The approach was accepted by the health authorities, with no additional safety factors applied. We report our innovative approach, together with efficacy and safety results in the first patients, to share our learnings and encourage others to consider a similarly patient‐centric strategy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell preparation

The OPM‐2 MM cell line (Immunosafety laboratory, Roche Innovation Center Basel, Switzerland; Cellosaurus CVCL_1625) was used for the in vitro assays, with frozen vials of peripheral blood mononucleocytes (PBMCs) from healthy blood donors (Roche's pool of donors) and three individuals with MM (Discovery Life Sciences, USA). Two of the individuals had newly diagnosed MM, and the third had stable disease that had previously been treated with lenalidomide. PBMC suspension was added to target cells to obtain an E:T ratio of 5:1.

Fresh bone marrow aspirate (BMA) samples for the ex vivo analysis were collected from MM patient donors (independent of the Phase 1 forimtamig study) at the Institut Universitaire du Cancer de Toulouse‐Oncopole (IUCTO, Toulouse) as previously described 23 and the Clínica Universidad de Navarra. All MM patient donors provided written informed consent before collection, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the local ethics committees. Collection at IUCTO was approved by the French Committee for the Protection of Persons (CPP; DC‐2012‐1,654) and local IUCT‐Oncopole review boards, and at Clínica Universidad de Navarra by the local ethics committee (2017.22).

See Supplementary Methods for further information on cell preparation and setup of in vitro assays.

In vitro assays

To assess tumor cell lysis, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity release was measured using a Cytotoxicity Detection Kit (Roche Applied Science #1644793) following the manufacturer's instructions and a Cytation 5 Cell imaging Multi‐Mode Reader (BioTek). An optimal development time of 20 minutes was used.

Cytokine and chemokine concentrations were determined from each triplicate well by multiplex immunoassay using a Human Cytokine chemiluminescent assay kit (Aushon, 5‐Plex Array, Cat#101‐269‐1‐AB) and the Cirascan plate reader (Aushon/Quanterix #AS0077) with the Cirasoft Multiplex Immunoassay Analysis Software.

Analysis of T‐cell activation was performed on a BD Fortessa flow cytometer and using the FlowJo software. After exclusion of dead cells, selection of singlets and gating of the CD45+ immune cell population, the CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes were gated, and the percentages of CD25+ and CD69+ activated cells were measured among each population.

Ex vivo analysis

To evaluate the functional response of resident immune effector cells and MM plasma cell lysis, tumor cell lysis and T‐cell activation as well as cytokine assays were performed using BMA from MM patient donors independent of the forimtamig clinical trial. Two distinct protocols were employed in different laboratories, differing primarily in sample preparation steps while following a similar workflow for treatment and analysis (see Supplementary Methods ).

Both protocols assessed immune effector cell activation and MM plasma cell lysis by measuring the expression of key markers, including CD38, BCMA, CS1, FcRH5, and GPRC5D on MM cells, as well as CD4 and CD8 on T cells. Viability and activation markers (e.g., CD69) were also analyzed. Target expression and T‐cell infiltration were determined on pre‐treatment samples.

Cytokine release upon T‐cell activation was measured by cytometric bead array technology following the manufacturer's instructions (see Supplementary Methods ).

Phase 1 study design

This was an open‐label, non‐randomized, dose escalation, multicenter Phase 1 study (NCT04557150) in patients with RRMM, investigating the safety, PK, PD, and clinical activity of forimtamig. 24 Local ethics committee approval was obtained, and all trial patients provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. Further details of Study NCT04557150 are in the Supplementary Methods and have been presented. 25 , 26 The dosing schedule is shown in Figure S1 .

Data and statistical analysis

To determine EC50 values for in vitro tumor cell lysis, a 4‐parameter logistic regression model was fitted to the sigmoidal dose–response curves of LDH release for individual donors using XLfit 5.5.0 (IDBS). The model used was of the form f(x) = A+(B‐A)/(1 + (C/x)^D). All parameters were fitted freely (i.e., no given constraints), but a few obviously outlying data points were excluded by specifying a knockout criterion in the software (prediction interval based, constant = 0.2). This improved quality of the fit and estimated EC50 parameters. Algebraic mean and standard deviation were used to summarize EC50 values across the cohort, in line with a sufficiently symmetric and close to normal distribution of values.

EC50 values for in vitro cytokine release and in vitro T‐cell activation were calculated using a 3‐parameter nonlinear regression model, applied using GraphPadPrism version 10.5.0.

Data from the ex vivo tumor cell lysis, T‐cell activation, and cytokine release assays were analyzed using FlowJo v10 software and the Infinicyt v2.0 software (Cytognos SL, Spain). Data sets were transferred to GraphPad Prism 8 for statistical analysis. For analysis of tumor cell lysis, a nonlinear regression fit (log(agonist) vs. response) with a hill slope fixed to −1 analysis was applied to the values to produce a dose–response curve fit and EC50 values. Cytokine concentration values were plotted in XY table format. Each cytokine concentration value was normalized to the “no antibody” control.

The IV FIH dose was calculated assuming a typical plasma volume of 3.5 L for a 70‐kg body weight individual and a forimtamig molecular weight of 193 kDa, using the formula: mass = molar concentration × volume × molecular weight. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the safety and anti‐tumor activity of forimtamig in the Phase 1 study.

Ex vivo MABEL assays

Similar to the in vitro assays, incubation of forimtamig with BMA samples from MM patient donors (independent of the clinical trial) led to tumor cell lysis, T‐cell activation, and cytokine release (Table S3 ).

Forimtamig‐induced depletion of MM cells in 9 of 12 assessed BMA samples (Figure 1 a,b ). The mean EC50 for tumor cell lysis was 52.3 pM. Forimtamig also induced dose‐dependent activation of tumor‐infiltrating T cells (Figure 1 c–e ), with EC50 values ranging from 0.4 to 45 pM for CD69+ T cells. Finally, forimtamig‐induced IL‐6 levels of 12.7 pM (< 300 pg/mL) following incubation with BMA samples from individuals with MM, up to the highest tested dose of 7 nM (Figure 1 f ).

FIH dose calculation

Tumor cell lysis was considered the most relevant readout for FIH starting dose calculation, for both in vitro and ex vivo MABEL approaches. It offers high therapeutic relevance as it reflects the direct, desired mechanism of action and provides the best evidence of potential anti‐tumor activity. The mean EC50 for LDH release from the in vitro assay was 0.6 pM, resulting in an FIH starting dose of 0.4 μg. The EC50 of the subsequent IL‐6 release in the in vitro killing assay (7.06 pM) was well above the EC50 of LDH release, suggesting limited cytokine release.

Cytokine release assays performed in whole blood from healthy donors showed that forimtamig did not induce release of IL‐6 or other cytokines at concentrations up to 0.5 nM, similar to the low‐risk compound cetuximab (data not shown). This concentration was much higher than the EC50 of IL‐6 release measured in the in vitro killing assay (7.06 pM). Target‐independent cytokine release was therefore not expected to add to the target‐dependent cytokine release at the calculated FIH dose.

In the ex vivo assay, we observed a large variability in forimtamig EC50 values between MM patient donor samples from the ex vivo tumor cell lysis assessment, with a geometric mean of 52 pM and an EC50 from the most sensitive MM patient donor of 9 pM. Weighing different options, we decided on the approach with the lowest safety risk and calculated an FIH starting dose based on the EC50 of the most sensitive MM patient donor sample, resulting in a flat forimtamig dose of 6 μg. IL‐6 release detected in the BMA samples as a key indicator of cytokine release was in a low range (0.62–5.92 pM at 7 pM), with an expected low risk for CRS in clinical trial participants at the FIH dose.

DISCUSSION

Our ex vivo MABEL approach to calculating the FIH starting dose for forimtamig led to a dose 15 times higher than a classic, in vitro MABEL approach. PD‐active and clinically efficacious doses were rapidly reached: the second target dose in the second clinical trial participant tested (54 μg) was clinically active and the third target dose (162 μg) led to an objective response in the fourth participant tested. Despite the higher FIH dose, forimtamig was well tolerated in the first participant dosed (solely Grade 1 CRS), as well as in the following four participants at increasing dose levels, with none experiencing greater than Grade 2 CRS events in the first treatment cycle.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first description of an ex vivo MABEL approach leading to a well‐tolerated starting dose that enabled a rapid escalation to clinically active dose levels – resulting in clinical activity in the second dose cohort and deep and durable responses (VGPR and sCR) in the third dose cohort. Other reports of modified MABEL approaches focus on the methodology and do not share efficacy data, 14 or show limited efficacy at initial doses. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Ex vivo assays using frozen MM patient donor samples were part of the pre‐clinical development of talquetamab, another GPRC5D–CD3 bispecific antibody, but do not appear to form the basis of the FIH dose calculation. 20 , 21 In contrast, our ex vivo MABEL approach yielded an FIH dose that was well tolerated, as well as reaching an efficacious dose after treating only four patients.

Together with the aim to optimize patient outcomes in the Phase 1 trial, three practical factors led us to our ex vivo MABEL approach utilizing untouched BMA samples from individuals with MM with resident, autologous T cells. First, GPRC5D has a restricted expression profile in normal tissues and cells, limited to normal plasma cells, hair follicles and filiform papillae in the tongue. 29 , 30 Second, MM has features not easily replicated with cell lines, such as clonal heterogeneity, inter‐patient variability and the immunosuppressive bone marrow microenvironment. 31 , 32 , 33 For example, the bone marrow in MM may be immunosuppressive with reduced T‐cell activation, 31 , 32 meaning that the fixed E:T ratio of in vitro assays may not accurately represent the human disease. By contrast, our ex vivo assays used patient‐relevant ratios, likely reflecting the range of activity and subsequent cytokine release that could be expected in a clinical setting. Finally, nonclinical safety assessments did not identify other risks beyond CRS at the FIH dose (data not shown). Cytokine release as a safety readout was also a component of the ex vivo approach.

Treatment with forimtamig resulted in dose‐dependent increases in three measures of biological effect: tumor cell lysis, cytokine release, and T‐cell activation. The most sensitive parameters for the activity of forimtamig in both in vitro and ex vivo assays were tumor cell lysis and induction of CD69 expression as a marker for activation on activated CD8+ T cells, reflecting the T‐cell bispecific antibody mode of action.

In selecting a relevant concentration to inform the FIH dose, our approach aimed to balance the potential for early therapeutic activity with paramount patient safety. We prioritized physiological relevance by using an ex vivo model with MM patient donor‐derived tumor cells and autologous T cells with the inherent E:T ratio. While conventional MABEL approaches often utilize the most sensitive upstream readouts (e.g., EC20 of tumor cell lysis, cytokine release or T‐cell activation), 13 we based our primary calculation on the EC50 for tumor cell lysis, as it represents the direct therapeutic endpoint. The potential risk of using a less conservative EC50 metric was balanced by anchoring the calculation to the response of the most sensitive MM patient donor rather than the geometric mean of the population. Our ex vivo MABEL FIH dose was calculated to be 15 times higher than the classic approach (defined here as the inter‐donor geomean EC50 of the most sensitive readout, tumor cell lysis), similar to another modified MABEL approach for a T‐cell engager which gave a starting dose 10‐fold higher than classic MABEL. 14 Importantly, we confirmed that the concentration derived from this method elicited low IL‐6 release as a key indicator for cytokine release in our ex vivo system, providing additional support for a low anticipated risk of CRS at the proposed FIH dose.

To minimize the severity and impact of potential CRS events, we utilized step‐up dosing and premedication in the Phase 1 trial, as recommended by the European Myeloma Network. 7 Neither of the clinical trial participants in the first two cohorts experienced greater than Grade 1 CRS during the first treatment cycle.

Limitations of the ex vivo MABEL approach include difficulties obtaining suitable and sufficient fresh primary samples to maintain viability of resident immune cells. This requires expert laboratories to be close to treatment facilities, which is not always the case. The size constraint of 9 fresh primary samples used for the ex vivo MABEL approach was a consequence of the unpredictable nature of fresh sample availability from MM patient donors. While using frozen/thawed samples may be an alternative, it can be challenging to source sufficient samples due to the poor viability of MM cells after thawing. The thawing process also alters the cellular composition of the samples, potentially impacting the results. Due to the scarcity of fresh patient material, a direct functional head‐to‐head comparison between fresh and cryopreserved samples from the same patient timepoint could not be performed. Therefore, while frozen samples may prove useful for exploratory screening or endpoint measurement in future work, our reliance on fresh BM samples was necessary to ensure maximum scientific rigor and relevance of the EC50 calculation that defined the FIH starting dose.

Coupled with considerable inter‐MM patient donor heterogeneity, our small number of BMA samples yielded a wide range of results, as seen in the tumor cell lysis assay. Our recommendation is to ideally source at least 15 or even 30 MM patient donor samples to mitigate this effect. Based on a simulation approach, using n ≥ 15 allows specification of a lower limit of EC50 values which is expected to be undercut for at most 20% of the population with 95% statistical confidence. Using n ≥ 30 would limit this risk to 10% of the population if that level is required (see Supplementary Methods ).

If access to additional, high‐quality MM patient donor‐derived samples with high cell viability were not a limiting factor, we would have assessed IL‐6 release over time to ensure the peak level was used in FIH dose selection instead of value from a single timepoint. Investigation of the immune cell composition relative to tumor cells could also have provided further context to the E:T ratio.

Strengths of the ex vivo MABEL approach include a more physiological reflection of the in vivo microenvironment and the possibility of more patients in Phase 1 trials receiving a dose that is clinically active as well as safe. Developing a new approach in drug development is risky; we share our learnings (Box 1 ) to support others in taking a similarly patient‐centric approach to achieve the best results for clinical trial participants.

To conclude, we used a novel ex vivo MABEL approach to calculate a tolerated but also sufficiently high, FIH dose for the Phase 1 clinical trial of forimtamig in RRMM, allowing clinically active doses to be reached more quickly than with a standard MABEL approach. With more innovation we can work to minimize sub‐therapeutic doses in Phase 1 clinical trials, thereby ensuring that participants have a higher likelihood of receiving effective therapy while maintaining safety.
