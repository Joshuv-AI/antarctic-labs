Sex-based differences in tolerability of developmental antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) in non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC)
Authors: C Parisi, M Banini, F Mantuano, M Aldea, P Abdayem, L Derosa, M Frelaut, P Lavaud, A Robert, S Simon, A Gazzah, A Hollebecque, C Massard, B Besse, J Remon, A Wagner, B Pistilli, I Vaz-Luis, D Planchard, F Barlesi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSex-based differences in treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) have been described across several anticancer therapies. However, sex-disaggregated safety data for antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) in non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is lacking.Patients and methodsWe conducted a monocentric cohort study including patients with advanced NSCLC treated with developmental ADCs within phase I-II clinical trials at Gustave Roussy (2018-2023). Clinically significant TRAEs (grade ≥2, Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0), dose modifications, hospitalisations, and survival outcomes were analysed according to sex using univariate and multivariate analysis.ResultsOur population included 132 patients [women, 64 (48.5%); men, 68 (51.5%)] receiving ADCs targeting TROP2 (32.6%), ITGB6 (31.8%), HER2 (13.6%), CEACAM5 (13.6%), HER3 (5.4%), c-MET (2.3%), and Nectin-4 (0.7%). Overall, 100 (76%) and 39 (29%) patients experienced grade ≥2 and grade ≥3 TRAEs, respectively. Women had a higher incidence of grade ≥2 TRAEs than men [82.8% versus 69.1%; adjusted odds ratio (OR) 2.8, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.03-7.84, P = 0.04)] and a higher median number of TRAEs (2 versus 1, P = 0.04). Metabolic TRAEs (anorexia, weight loss, ion and lipid imbalances) were significantly more frequent in women (29.7% versus 10.3%; OR 3.68, 95% CI 1.42-9.49, P = 0.009), as were dose reductions (37.5% versus 19.1%; OR 3.35, 95% CI 1.24-9.01, P = 0.02).ConclusionsWomen treated with developmental ADCs for advanced NSCLC experience a higher burden of clinically relevant TRAEs. Our data suggest that sex may represent a key variable to integrate into safety reporting and monitoring strategies for developmental ADCs in NSCLC.Key words: adverse events, AEs, antibody-drug conjugates, ADCs, clinical trials, non-small-cell lung cancer, NSCLC, sex, gender

Introduction

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) represent a novel therapeutic class in lung cancer, combining target specificity with potent cytotoxic payload. By exploiting the overexpression of tumour-associated antigens on cancer cells, ADCs enable more precise tumour targeting and potentially reduce off-target effects compared with conventional chemotherapy.1 Patient- and therapy-related factors such as performance status (PS), age, organ function, comorbidities, and prior treatment history are commonly considered when selecting candidates for ADCs in clinical trials, as they may influence the risk of treatment-related toxicity.2

Nevertheless, even with careful patient selection and advanced drug design, clinically relevant toxicities remain frequent and may limit treatment delivery.3 Although adverse events (AEs) are often attributed to payload-related mechanisms, both the antibody component and the linker stability contribute to organ-specific treatment-related AEs (TRAEs). Management of common ADC-associated toxicities, including pneumonitis, nausea, and myelosuppression, relies on baseline assessment, proactive monitoring, dose modification, and multidisciplinary supportive care.3 As ADCs are increasingly studied in non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), identifying patient-related factors influencing ADC tolerability is crucial.

Sex- and gender-sensitive medicine is an emerging field that acknowledges the role of biological sex differences and gender-related factors in shaping health outcomes.4 However, sex-disaggregated safety data for developmental ADCs are largely lacking, as most pivotal trials are not designed to explore such differences.

This study aimed to investigate sex-related differences in tolerability among patients with advanced NSCLC treated with developmental ADCs in early-phase clinical trials. Based on the results of a French Data Health System study with novel targeted therapies5 and remote patient monitoring of symptoms using electronic patient-reported outcomes across Belgium and France,6 we hypothesised that, when treated with ADCs, women were more likely to experience moderate (grade ≥2) and severe (grade ≥3) AEs than men.

Patients and methods

Study design

This was a monocentric, retrospective cohort study conducted at a single academic cancer centre (Gustave Roussy, Villejuif, France). The study included patients with advanced NSCLC treated within phase I or II clinical trials evaluating ADCs between January 2018 and January 2023.

All clinical trials included in the present analysis were approved by local institutional review boards, and all patients provided written informed consent before trial participation.

Study population

The study population included 132 patients experiencing 296 AEs. Eligible patients were aged ≥18 years, had a histologically confirmed diagnosis of stage IV NSCLC according to eighth edition of American Joint Committee on Cancer/Union for International Cancer Control tumour–node–metastasis classification, and had received at least one cycle of an ADC administered as monotherapy. Clinical, treatment-related, and toxicity data were extracted from electronic case report forms and source medical records. TRAEs were defined as AEs considered by the investigator to be at least possibly related to ADC treatment and were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0.

Objectives

The primary objective was to evaluate sex-related differences in the incidence of clinically relevant TRAEs, defined as grade ≥2 and grade ≥3 events, occurring during ADC treatment.

Secondary objectives included the assessment of sex-related differences in number of TRAEs per patient, dose reductions and treatment discontinuations, TRAE-related hospitalisation rates and duration, and survival outcomes. Median progression-free survival (mPFS) was defined as the time (in full months) from ADC initiation to the first documented radiological or clinical disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first, while median overall survival (mOS) was defined as the time from ADC initiation to death from any cause. Patients without an event were censored at the date of last clinical follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to outline patients’ characteristics, treatment exposure, and toxicity outcomes. Continuous variables were reported as medians with ranges or 95% confidence intervals (CIs), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Statistical methods used to analyse clinical factors balanced between the two sexes included the chi-square test, Fisher’s exact two-tailed test, or Mann–Whitney U test for categorical variables and Wilcoxon rank-sum and median tests for continuous variables. Univariate analysis (UVA) was conducted to explore associations between sex and toxicity outcomes. Multivariate analysis (MVA) was carried out using logistic regression models to estimate adjusted odds ratio (OR) and 95% CI for the association between sex and TRAEs. Clinically relevant covariates and variables with P < 0.10 in UVA were included in multivariate models. These covariates included age, smoking status, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) PS, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), number of metastatic sites, presence of liver or brain metastases, number of prior systemic treatment lines, and prior immunotherapy or thoracic radiotherapy.

Median regression UVA and MVA, using the same model, were additionally applied to evaluate potential sex-related differences in treatment exposure and toxicity burden. Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and comparisons were conducted using Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for relevant clinical factors.

Subgroup analysis was carried out according to ADC target and payload type [topoisomerase 1 inhibitor (Topo1i) or microtubule inhibitor (monomethyl auristatin E, MMAE)], although it was limited by the relative sample size. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were carried out using StataNow v19.5 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Results

Study population and treatment exposure

Among 200 patients screened, 132 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis [64 (48.5%) women and 68 (51.5%) men] accounting for a total of 296 TRAEs (Figure 1). The median age was 60 years (range 26-86), with most patients (67.4%) presenting an ECOG PS 1 and a median CCI of 2 (range 0-6). Lung adenocarcinoma was the main histology, and 67.4% of patients harboured an oncogenic driver alteration. All patients had metastatic disease at enrolment with a median of three metastatic sites (range 1-6) and were heavily pretreated [median of two prior systemic therapy lines (range 1-9)]. Approximately 51% of patients had received chemotherapy as last treatment, whereas 36% and 13% received immunotherapy and targeted therapy, respectively. ADCs targeted TROP2 (32.6%), ITGB6 (31.8%), HER2 (13.6%), CEACAM5 (13.6%), HER3 (5.4%), c-MET (2.3%), and Nectin-4 (0.7%). Payloads were equally distributed between Topo1i and MMAE.

Toxicity outcomes

After a median follow-up of 12 months (range 1-79), the median duration of ADC treatment was 101.5 days (95% CI 71.2-140.1), with no significant difference between women and men in both UVA and MVA (P = 0.39). Overall, rate of grade ≥2 TRAEs was 76%, with 29% of patients experiencing a grade ≥3 TRAE and a median number of TRAEs per patient of 2. A total of 22 (16.6%) patients discontinued treatment because of TRAEs. Fifty-three (82.8%) women and 47 (69.1%) men developed grade ≥2 TRAEs, with an OR in women of 2.8 (95% CI 1.03-7.84, P = 0.04) after adjustment for confounding factors at MVA. A trend towards a higher frequency of severe (grade ≥3) TRAEs was also observed in women than in men (37.5% versus 22%; P = 0.06), with a statistically significant higher median number of TRAEs per patient in women (2 versus 1; P = 0.04). Gastrointestinal (GI) were the most common TRAEs overall (32.6%), with a nonsignificant trend towards higher rates in women (40.6% versus 25.0%; P = 0.08).

Metabolic TRAEs, namely anorexia, weight loss, and ion and lipid imbalances, were significantly more frequent among women than men (29.7% versus 10.3%; OR 3.68, 95% CI 1.42-9.49, P = 0.009).

Women were also more likely to require ADC dose reductions compared with men (37.5% versus 19.1%; OR 3.35, 95% CI 1.24-9.01, P = 0.02). No significant sex-based differences were observed in TRAE duration, resolution rates, hospitalisation rates, or treatment discontinuation rate, although a trend towards longer TRAE-related hospitalisations was noted among women (median 11 versus 5 days; P = 0.15). Subgroup analysis

When looking at different ADC class according to tumour-associated antigens, men treated with anti-TROP2 (n = 24) showed a higher incidence of interstitial lung disease (ILD) (37.5% versus 10.5%; P = 0.04), although this association was not confirmed at MVA (P = 0.22). Conversely, women treated with anti-TROP2 (n = 19) experienced higher rates of metabolic TRAEs (31.6% versus 8.3%; P = 0.05), with a similar nonsignificant trend after adjustment (P = 0.08). Women (n = 20) experienced a trend towards higher rates of GI AEs (25% versus 4.5%; P = 0.06) with anti-ITGB6 compared with men (n = 22).

When stratified by payload type, women receiving Topo1i (n = 34) exhibited higher rates of metabolic TRAEs (35.3% versus 11.8%; P = 0.051) and a significant increased incidence of grade ≥3 TRAEs compared with men (55.9% versus 26.5%; OR 4.26, 95% CI 1.08-16.83), confirmed at both UVA (P = 0.01) and MVA (P = 0.04). In addition, we highlighted a trend towards higher fatigue in women than in men (61.8% versus 38.2%; P = 0.051). Significant ORs are plotted in Figure 2.

Survival outcomes

mPFS was 4 months (range 1-79) and mOS was 12 months (range 1-79), with no significant differences between women and men after adjustment for key clinical confounders (P = 0.30 and P = 0.9, respectively).

Discussion

Sex disparities can affect multiple aspects of cancer care, including treatment tolerability.7,8 In this regard, sex-related disparities in the experience of side-effects from developmental ADCs in NSCLC have not been reported.3,9,10

Our study provides the first dedicated analysis of sex-based tolerability profile in patients with advanced NSCLC receiving developmental ADCs. Using high-quality toxicity data derived from early-phase clinical trials, we observed that sex may represent a key factor affecting ADC tolerability in NSCLC.

In our series, women experienced a significantly higher burden of clinically relevant TRAEs compared with men, including higher rates of grade ≥2 AEs, a greater number of TRAEs per patient, and nearly doubled rates of dose reductions. These findings persisted after adjustment for key clinical confounders. Notably, metabolic TRAEs emerged as a distinctive and significantly more frequent toxicity category among women, together with a trend towards increased GI- and fatigue-related AEs. This observation is particularly relevant, as metabolic AEs are often underreported yet may significantly impact treatment adherence and nutritional status.

Potential mechanisms may involve sex-related variations in body composition, hormonal background, and pharmacokinetics affecting ADC distribution and metabolism. Specifically, differences in hepatic enzyme activity and renal clearance may influence exposure to both the monoclonal antibody component and the cytotoxic payload. Moreover, immune and inflammatory responses, known to differ by sex, might modulate susceptibility to certain ADC-related toxicities.11

Interestingly, women treated with Topo1i-based ADCs showed higher rates of severe (grade ≥3) toxicity, suggesting a potential interaction between sex and payload-specific exposure. There is evidence that patient’s sex can influence the pharmacokinetics of anticancer agents, and thereby their safety. Significant sex differences (≥20%) in pharmacokinetic parameters are reported with topotecan, for example.12

Although it has been hypothesised that Topo1i-based ADCs may contribute to different side-effects experiences in women compared with men, current evidence from irinotecan and topotecan studies does not consistently demonstrate a reproducible sex-specific toxicity pattern beyond the increased susceptibility of women to chemotherapy-related AEs in general. Importantly, the inclusion of multiple ADCs with heterogeneous targets, linkers, and cytotoxic payloads may have significantly weakened the power of any payload-specific statistical analysis, confounding sex-based toxicity associations at that level.

Therefore, whether the observed signal in our cohort is payload-specific remains speculative and requires validation in larger, dedicated datasets.

Conversely, men in our study appeared to have a slightly higher incidence of ILD, consistent with prior reports with other treatment regimens,13 although this trend did not reach statistical significance.

Overall, our findings are consistent with growing evidence across oncology that women are more prone to treatment-related toxicity than men, including chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapies.14, 15, 16 However, a recent meta-analysis of oncology clinical trials found that fewer than 1% report curated sex-specific toxicity comparisons.8

Identifying sex-specific patterns of toxicity in patients receiving developmental therapeutics including ADCs could support the empowerment of personalised care pathways, improve short- and long-term toxicity management, and ultimately enhance quality of life (QoL) in lung cancer patients. Thus, integrating sex considerations into the design of early-phase clinical trials could improve patient stratification and risk mitigation. Early recognition of sex-related differences in ADC tolerability could enhance compliance, reduce disparities in treatment exposure, and support sex-informed personalised care pathways in clinical practice. In this regard, real-world evidence derived from postmarketing surveillance and digital health tools such as remote symptom monitoring17 may help to provide more granular insights into sex-specific tolerability.

Our study has several strengths, including rigorous toxicity documentation inherent to clinical trial settings and balanced sex representation. However, some limitations should be acknowledged, including the retrospective and monocentric design, the limited sample size, and the heterogeneity of ADC compounds, which may limit the interpretability of our findings, particularly in subgroup analyses.

In addition, due to the lack of detailed exposure data, a potential exposure-toxicity relationship cannot be fully excluded. However, MVA did not identify significant differences between sexes in treatment duration, survival outcomes, or baseline clinical characteristics, thereby reducing the likelihood that these factors fully explain the observed associations. Although women had a numerically longer duration of ADC treatment, this difference was not statistically significant and was not associated with improved survival outcomes. mPFS and mOS were comparable between sexes, suggesting that prolonged exposure alone does not account for the higher toxicity burden in women. Furthermore, baseline characteristics reflecting clinical condition and disease burden were well balanced between groups, arguing against worse baseline status in female patients. Given these limitations, our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Conclusion

In this monocentric cohort study, women treated with developmental ADCs for advanced NSCLC experienced a higher burden of clinically relevant TRAEs and more frequent dose reductions.

Our data suggest that biological sex may represent a clinically relevant variable to consider in safety reporting and monitoring strategies for ADCs in NSCLC. Integrating sex-based considerations into ADC development and clinical evaluation may ultimately enhance treatment tolerability, adherence, and QoL for patients with NSCLC.

Future investigations are warranted to validate these findings and should incorporate pharmacokinetic, pharmacogenomic, as well as hormonal factors to better elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying sex-related differences in toxicity outcomes with developmental ADCs.
