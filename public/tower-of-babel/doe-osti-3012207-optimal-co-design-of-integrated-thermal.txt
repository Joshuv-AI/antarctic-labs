 ABSTRACT

 This project advances a unified, open-source framework for the optimal co-design of
thermal, electrical, and control systems in grid-interactive efficient districts (GEDs). As
communities integrate growing levels of distributed energy resources, traditional approaches
that model thermal and electrical networks independently lead to reduced efficiency, limited
flexibility, and missed opportunities for coordinated operation. To address these challenges,
the research team developed a comprehensive suite of physics-based models, control algo-
rithms, and software tools that enable holistic simulation, optimization, and demonstration
of district-scale energy systems.
 The project delivers several major contributions: (1) new open-source microgrid and
inverter models based on the Western Electricity Coordinating Council’s specifications, val-
idated through both industry-standard simulation tools and power-hardware-in-the-Loop ex-
periments; (2) coupled thermal–electrical component and system models, including induc-
tion machines, HVAC equipment, and combined heat-and-power systems; (3) advanced op-
timization and reinforcement-learning–based control strategies for microgrids and district
energy plants; and (4) seamless integration of these models and algorithms into the Na-
tional Laboratory of the Rockies’ URBANopt platform for district-scale design and analysis.
The full workflow supports automated translation from GIS-based district representations to
high-fidelity Modelica system models, enabling robust evaluation of alternative configura-
tions and operational strategies.
 Two real-world district energy systems—at the University of Colorado Boulder and the
University of Texas at Austin—were modeled, calibrated, and studied using the developed
framework. Demonstrated benefits include improved operational efficiency, enhanced re-
silience, and energy savings meeting 25% under optimized control strategies. Collectively,
the project establishes an open, extensible foundation for designing next-generation grid-
interactive districts and supports the widespread deployment of efficient, flexible, and re-
silient community energy systems.

 CHAPTER 1

 INTRODUCTION

1.1 Motivation

Communities in the United States are increasingly seeking energy systems that are efficient,
resilient, and capable of supporting growing amounts of local energy generation. District
energy and microgrid systems offer a promising path toward these goals, but their design
and operation are complex. Traditionally, thermal and electrical networks have been planned
and optimized separately, leading to inefficiencies, missed opportunities for coordinated op-
eration, and slower adoption of emerging technologies. As energy sources such as solar
photovoltaic (PV) systems and wind generation become more prevalent, the need for holistic
evaluation tools continues to increase. These tools must support informed decision-making
from early design to operation.

The grid-interactive efficient district (GED) initiative responds to this need by advancing an
integrated approach to modeling, simulation, and optimization of coupled thermal-electric
energy systems. Building on open-source tools and enabling multi-domain analysis, the
project seeks to support communities, designers, and operators in creating more efficient,
flexible, and resilient district energy systems.

1.2 Project Overview

Coordinating the design of thermal, electrical and control networks is essential for the ad-
vancement of GED energy systems, particularly as communities increasingly depend on re-
newable energy resources and seek greater resilience through district energy networks and
microgrids. Achieving these goals requires modeling tools that can represent the dynamic

 1

interactions between energy domains while supporting rigorous design, analysis, and opti-
mization.

This project addresses this need by developing an open-source holistic modeling and opti-
mization platform for the integrated design and retrofit of GED energy systems. Built on
established open-source environments—including the National Laboratory of the Rockies
(NLR) URBANopt platform, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL)’s Modelica
Buildings Library (MBL), Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute (RPI)’s OpenIPSL Library, and
the Department of Energy (DOE)’s Spawn of EnergyPlus—the platform unifies GIS-based
district representations, physics-based thermal and electrical models, and coordinated con-
trol strategies into a single scalable workflow. The effort also leverages previous DOE funded
work on district energy optimization and combined heat and power (CHP) systems.

Through this integrative approach, the project delivers five key contributions:

 1. Seamless integration between URBANopt and Modelica enables multi-domain dis-
 trict design, from geographic representations to high-fidelity physical models.

 2. Computationally efficient lumped models support the exploration and optimization
 of early-stage design of heterogeneous district systems.

 3. A unified multi-domain modeling methodology reduces information-transfer gaps
 between design teams and enables early detection of system-level inconsistencies.

 4. An open-source industry-ready framework for modeling, simulation, and optimiza-
 tion supports research, planning, and commercial applications.

 5. An enhanced iterative co-design workflow integrates system configuration, controls,
 and performance evaluation across thermal and electrical domains.

The project produced a publicly available modeling platform that supports the optimal design
and retrofit of GED energy systems and microgrids by integrating the thermal, electrical, and

 2

control domains. Coupled models were developed, simulated and validated using virtual mi-
crogrids and district-energy testbeds. Two real-world community energy systems were also
modeled and calibrated using measured data, allowing detailed analysis of efficiency, oper-
ational improvements, and resilience. The optimized strategies demonstrated performance
gains exceeding 25% in both total energy efficiency of the system and community resilience.

1.3 Report Structure

The remainder of this report is organized to reflect the development, integration, and ap-
plication of the modeling platform. Chapter 2 presents the open-source microgrid models
developed in Modelica, including component-level representations, subsystem formulations,
and validation efforts. Chapter 3 describes the integration of electrical and thermal-fluid
systems for district energy applications, detailing component development, coupled cycle
modeling, and associated validation activities. Chapter 4 outlines the development of opti-
mal algorithms that support co-design, coordinated control, and multi-domain optimization.
Chapter 5 explains how these models and algorithms are integrated into the URBANopt plat-
form to enable district-scale workflows. Chapter 6 demonstrates the application of the mod-
eling framework in two real-world district energy testbeds, covering thermal and electrical
subsystems. Finally, Chapter 7 summarizes the results of the project, open-source releases,
commercialization and outreach efforts, and directions for future work.

 CHAPTER 2

 CREATION OF OPEN-SOURCE MICROGRID MODELS IN MODELICA

2.1 Introduction

The electric power grid is undergoing a profound transition driven by the rapid growth of
inverter-based distributed energy resources (DER), increasing electrification, and heightened
expectations for resiliency in the face of disturbances and extreme events. Traditional power
systems were built around large synchronous machines whose dynamics, inertial characteris-
tics and control behaviors are well understood; in contrast, today’s grids depend increasingly
on fast, power-electronics–interfaced generation whose internal control loops, limited fault
current contributions, and sensitivity to grid conditions introduce new modeling and oper-
ational challenges. These emerging complexities have intensified the need for modeling
frameworks capable of representing cyber-physical energy systems with sufficient fidelity to
capture interactions across electrical, mechanical, thermal, and control domains. Because ex-
perimentation on real grids is constrained by reliability requirements and potential financial
risks, simulation has become a primary mechanism for evaluating new architectures, vali-
dating DER controls, and assessing microgrid performance under a wide range of operating
scenarios.

In this context, the Modelica modeling language and the OpenIPSL library are significant for
developing transparent, modular, and physics-based representations of modern power sys-
tems. Their equation-based formulation and object-oriented structure allow users to capture
device-level behaviors, embed manufacturer-agnostic control logic, and extend models for
customized applications. This chapter leverages these capabilities to develop a comprehen-
sive suite of dynamic models for renewable energy sources (RES), multi-domain electrical
loads, and complete microgrid systems. Beginning with the implementation of generic re-

newable energy models, such as PV, wind, and battery energy storage systems (BESS), this
work demonstrates how Western Electricity Coordinating Council (WECC)-based control
structures can be reproduced in the phasor domain and validated against industry-standard
tools. The work further extends into detailed induction motor and variable-speed drive mod-
els that represent coupled electromechanical and thermo-fluid behavior, enabling richer anal-
ysis of load dynamics within microgrids. With these component-level models established,
the thesis proceeds to construct full microgrid representations, including a realistic university
campus system, and uses them to evaluate both autonomous operation and transitions such
as islanding and re-synchronization.

A central contribution of this work is the integration of these Modelica-based models with
advanced control strategies. Specifically, microgrid models are used as platforms to im-
plement and test Model Predictive Control architectures to improve operational resiliency,
manage disturbances, and coordinate DER under normal and abnormal conditions. By cou-
pling predictive control with high-fidelity dynamic models, this research demonstrates how
microgrids can maintain voltage and frequency stability, adhere to operating constraints, and
achieve smooth reconnection to the main grid. The overall body of work illustrates the impor-
tance of open-source physics-based modeling in supporting the evolving needs of DER-rich
power systems and highlights the value of Modelica and OpenIPSL as tools for both research
and practical microgrid control development.

2.2 Modeling and Validation of Renewable Energy

With the goal of unambiguous modeling and providing model portability [2], an implemen-
tation of renewable energy source models based on industry grade references is carried out
in OpenIPSL [3], an open-source Modelica-based library used for power system dynamic
modeling and simulation. The Modelica language offers an open access standardized speci-
fication [4], which enables model portability to multiple tools supporting the language spec-
ification.

2.2.1 Renewable Energy Source Models

The models described in this section are the so-called “second generation” generic energy
models from the WECC. A detailed description of the models can be found in the Battery
Storage Dynamic Modeling Guideline from the WECC [5] and the Solar PV Power Plant
Modeling Guideline [6], as well as other sources, including software manuals [7, 8] and pa-
pers [9]. These models are suitable for electrical transient stability dynamic studies, allowing
for a phasor representation of generic renewable energy models in electrical power systems.
The practicality of using the phasor domain instead of waveform representation is that, by
assuming a time-invariant frequency, the set of differential equations that describes the states
can now be characterized by algebraic equations [10]. This reduces the number of ultra-fast
dynamic states that would otherwise need to be initialized and included in the set of dif-
ferential equations of the model, significantly increasing its complexity and, consequently,
impacting simulation run time.

 Figure 2.1: WECC Block Diagram of the RES Modules

The implementation of the WECC generic renewable models was performed using a care-
fully designed structure that facilitates the substitution of specific components within the
model to accommodate different representations of RES. The second-generation renewable
energy models consist of three key functional blocks, illustrated in Figure 2.1:

 • The “REGC_" module family, short for the renewable generator/converter model, rep-
 resents the current injection model for various DERs such as PV systems, batteries,
 and wind turbines.

 6

 • The “REEC_" module family, which stands for renewable electrical controller, embod-
 ies the electrical controls of electrical inverters. It operates in a closed-loop connection
 with the “REGC_" module, adjusting the active and reactive power based on the output
 voltage and the power from the “REGC_" module.

 • The “REPC_" module family, or renewable plant controller, defines the control scheme
 for a group of renewable sources. Similarly to the electrical controller, it encompasses
 control functions for active and reactive power, voltage control, and adds frequency
 control for active power regulation.

The new renewable models were implemented to expand the portfolio of existing Modelica
models for power system dynamic simulation in OpenIPSL [3].

2.2.2 REGCA, REECB, and REPCA Modelica Implementation

The REGCA module, shown in Figure 2.2, is a generic current source model with current
output based on the active and reactive power values of the renewable generator model and
its voltage magnitude and angle. The renewable generator/converter model has two input
signals: the real and imaginary current component commands (Ipcmd and Iqcmd), which is
determined via the electrical controller model and two output signals. The singals are the
real and imaginary current components (Ip and Iq) injected into the system. The REGCA
generator/inverter model has the capability of adjusting the active and reactive current com-
ponents of the model when its terminal voltage reaches the limit. For instance, when the
terminal voltage of the generator exceeds a maximum value, defined by the constant block
Vo_limit, the model decreases the reactive current value that is injected into the system and,
consequently, reduces the voltage magnitude. The min_limiter block in the blue hatched area
from Figure 2.2 is a limiter block with a specified minimum and infinite maximum limits.
While the generator terminal voltage (V t) is lower than a maximum value set by the user (k
= Volim at the Vo_limit component), the difference between these two values is negative and
the output of the min_limiter block is zero.

The REECB component has five different input signals, as shown in Figure 2.3. In particu-

2.2.4 New Generic Renewable Energy Source Models - REGCB and REECD Imple-
 mentation

Building on earlier implementations of the WECC renewable energy models in OpenIPSL,
this section incorporates the updated REGCB and REECD components introduced in the
second-generation RES framework, providing improved numerical stability and more accu-
rate inverter behavior under weak-grid and low-voltage conditions. The REGCB generator/-
converter model replaces the current-source formulation of REGC_A with a voltage-source

 13

interface, addressing performance issues in low short-circuit ratio environments. As shown
in Figure 2.7, injected dq-axis currents are computed from the converter’s internal voltage
and impedance, enabling more realistic dynamic behavior during disturbances. REGCB also
introduces enhanced current ramp-rate limits, updated prioritization between active and re-
active current, and algebraic current-magnitude limiting to improve robustness across a wide
range of operating conditions.

The REECD electrical controller extends the earlier REEC_A/B/C models by incorporating
expanded voltage-dependent limit (VDL) tables and updated current-limiting logic. Fig-
ure 2.8 illustrates the Modelica implementation, where REECD processes power references
and system measurements to generate active and reactive current commands. Its refined
limit handling—including consistent Iqmin behavior, configurable active-power absorption
through the Ke parameter, and a local current-compensation block—allows for improved
representation of inverter dynamics during faults and low-voltage events.

Together, the REGCB and REECD models strengthen the OpenIPSL renewable energy mod-
eling framework by providing greater stability, enabling more reliable simulation and analy-
sis of inverter-based resources in modern power systems.

The updated REECD controller is evaluated against the previous REEC_B implementation

2.7 Multimachine Power System Model for Stability and Control Design Analysis
 using Modelica and the OpenIPSL

2.7.1 Introduction

Modern power systems exhibit a complex architecture that requires the use of both physics-
based models for sophisticated control system designs. The design of robust control systems
is crucial to ensure reliable grid operation, which facilitates the management of complex
power system dynamics. A significant aspect of this involves conducting stability analysis
and tuning of Power System Stabilizers (PSS), which are essential to damp electromechani-
cal oscillations that can adversely affect system stability [21]. To address this need of devel-
oping models for control design and analysis, Modelica, in conjunction with the OpenIPSL
library, has been used effectively to create a detailed University Campus Microgrid model,
demonstrating its effectiveness in linear analysis, which is often challenging with traditional

 28

power system analysis tools [22].

This section explores the capabilities of Modelica [23], and the Dymola tool [24], to de-
velop power system models that are suitable for control system design and analysis. The
model developed here emerges from the power system literature [25], which can be used to
address intra-plant and inter-area oscillations when considering a power plant with multi-
ple machines. The developed can be further explored to create a more detailed design for
specific control tasks beyond those for which it was originally developed [21].

2.7.2 System Modeling and Refactoring for Linearization

The multimachine power system model in this work is built using Modelica’s object-oriented,
equation-based framework together with component models from the OpenIPSL library. The
implementation follows the standard Three-Machine Infinite Bus (ThreeMIB) benchmark
system [26], which provides the structural layout and parameters for the generators, net-
work, loads, and infinite bus. Each subsystem is represented as an independent, reusable
component assembled through Modelica’s graphical interface, which automatically gener-
ates the underlying connection equations. The network—comprising busses, transmission
lines, transformers, and loads—is constructed as a partial model extending OpenIPSL’s
pfComponent structure, allowing the system to be initialized directly from a power-flow
solution. The three generation units are then incorporated as modular subsystems, each
consisting of a synchronous machine, excitation system, and PSS, enabling an accurate rep-
resentation of the system’s dynamic behavior within a clear and reusable modeling structure.

2.8 Integrating IEEE/CIGRE DLL “Real Code” Models into Modelica Tools

2.8.1 Introduction

Vendors of power system simulation tools are investigating the incorporation of actual con-
troller code into specialized simulation environments. To facilitate this, Institute of Electrical
and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) and Conseil International des Grands Réseaux Électriques
(CIGRE) have collaboratively created the IEEE/CIGRE DLL Modeling Standard (ICDMS).
However, adoption by simulation tool providers has been minimal because ’real code’ mod-

 32

els per the ICDMS must be provided as Dynamic Link Libraries (DLLs) by equipment ven-
dors. Thus, to support the standard, tools need to support a standard-specific interface and
provide additional functions to execute the models.

During this budget period, I developed a method for integrating ‘real controller code’ models
(RCMs) built according to the IEEE/CIGRE DLL Modeling Standard into Modelica-based
tools. This was achieved by linking precompiled C code to Modelica models and using
components from the OpenIPSL library. The approach was demonstrated with an RCM of a
simplified silicon-controlled rectifier excitation system (SCRX).

2.8.2 IEEE/CIGRE DLL Modeling Standard Workflow

To explain how the ICDMS functions, Figure 2.25 shows the simulation workflow. It starts
with “Allocate Memory”, where memory for inputs, outputs, and parameters is allocated.
Model Initialization then sets initial conditions and parameters. The Update Input step reads
the current input values, followed by Run Calculation, where the model computes the output
based on input values and parameters. Finally, Update Output writes the results to the out-
put variables, completing one simulation cycle. This workflow repeats, allowing dynamic
simulation of the controller’s behavior.

Figure 2.25: Model Structure and Simulation Workflow According to the IEEE/CIGRE DLL
Modeling Standard

The implementation of this workflow follows the ICDMS by defining a clear structure for in-

 33

put signals, output signals, and parameters using standardized data types and units. Standard-
ized functions such as Model_GetInfo, Model_CheckParameters, and Model_Initialize
ensure proper initialization and parameter validation. The Model_Outputs function per-
forms the main computational tasks, adhering to the fixed time step approach common in
real-world controller firmware.

2.8.3 SCRX Excitation Control System Implementation

The SCRX excitation model is a simplified control system designed to regulate the field volt-
age of a synchronous generator, thereby maintaining the machine’s alternating current (AC)
voltage at a specified reference set-point. Figure 2.25 illustrates the overall structure of the
excitation control system. The excitation control system consists of three main components:

 • Synchronous Machine Regulator: Generates control signals based on the reference
 voltage and feedback signals from the synchronous machine. It takes these inputs to
 produce an appropriate control command for the exciter.

 • Exciter: Modulates the field voltage of the synchronous machine in response to the
 control commands from the regulator. It serves as an intermediary that translates the
 regulator’s signals into field winding voltage adjustments.

 • Synchronous Machine: The plant of this system that converts rotational mechanical
 energy into electrical power. It responds to the field voltage adjustments made by the
 exciter and influences the voltage and stability of the broader power system.

The SCRX model presents a detailed block diagram and standardized modeling approach, as
shown in Figure 2.25. This representation is generally adopted to demonstrate how control
of the field voltage of synchronous generators is achieved. Integrating this model into power
system simulations allows for extensive testing and validation, ensuring optimal performance
under various operational conditions.

The parameters required by the SCRX controller include time constants (TAdTB, TB, TE),

 34

controller gain (K), voltage limits (EMin, EMax), power source selection switch (CSwitch),
and field resistance ratio (RCdRFD). These parameters are essential for configuring the
controller to operate within the desired specifications and to ensure compatibility with the
ICDMS.

Figure 2.26 shows the simulation results with the SCRX controller implemented as an RCM

the real code SCRX controller, implemented as a DLL following the ICDMS, performs sim-

2.9 Summary

This chapter addresses the growing need for accurate, modular and physics-based modeling
tools as modern power systems transition toward inverter-based renewable generation and in-
creasingly complex operational conditions. It presents the implementation and evaluation of
advanced modeling approaches for RES and multi-machine power systems using Modelica
and the OpenIPSL library. The second-generation WECC renewable energy source models
are implemented and described, highlighting their structure and suitability for phasor-domain
dynamic studies. A comparative analysis between a real inverter and the WECC generic
models is conducted, demonstrating both the validity of the WECC framework and the need
for targeted improvements. Several enhancements, such as solar irradiance-to-power func-
tionality, voltage ride-through capability, and modifications to the internal control loops, are
developed to improve the reproducibility of real inverter behavior. The chapter also develops
a multi-machine power system model using Modelica’s object-oriented framework together
with OpenIPSL components. This model is refactored into a variant ready for linearization,
enabling automatic derivation of state-space representations for studies oriented to small-
signals and control. In addition, a preliminary workflow is introduced for integrating IEEE/-
CIGRE DLL “real code” controller models into the Modelica environment, demonstrating a
method for linking compiled controller code with OpenIPSL system models. Collectively,
these contributions establish a foundation for high-fidelity inverter modeling, system lin-
earization, and controller integration within an open-source modeling framework.

 CHAPTER 3

 COUPLING OF ELECTRICAL AND THERMAL-FLUIDS SYSTEMS IN
 DISTRICT ENERGY SYSTEMS

3.1 Introduction

Distributed energy systems (DES) are increasingly recognized as critical infrastructures for
achieving national goals related to electrification and improved energy system resilience. As
these systems evolve, the boundaries between electrical networks and thermal-fluid networks
are gradually blurred. Electrically driven heating and cooling technologies, distributed gen-
eration, and advanced control architectures now create strong interdependencies between
power system behavior and the thermodynamic and hydraulic characteristics of district heat-
ing and cooling networks. Understanding and managing these interactions is essential for im-
proving system performance and enabling new operational capabilities across multi-energy
infrastructures.

However, most existing DES modeling frameworks still treat electrical and thermal-fluid
subsystems as loosely coupled or independent. Such simplifications are no longer adequate.
Components such as induction machines (IM), pumps, heat pumps, chillers, and Combined
Heat and Power (CHP) units introduce dynamic multi-physics relationships that must be rep-
resented with higher fidelity to accurately capture system behavior. Induction machines and
pumps establish direct electromechanical–hydraulic coupling, while heat pumps and chillers
connect electricity consumption with thermodynamic cycles that are highly sensitive to tem-
perature and flow conditions. CHP units further integrate power and heat generation through
efficiency trade-offs and operating constraints that depend simultaneously on electrical and
thermal demands. The absence of detailed, integrated models limits the ability to evalu-
ate operational flexibility, assess resilience under disturbances, quantify the impacts of load

shifting and electrification, or design coordinated control strategies. As DES increasingly in-
corporates renewable energy, thermal storage, and advanced supervisory controls, the need
for unified modeling approaches becomes even more urgent.

This chapter presents the motivation, framework, and development of enhanced component-
level and system-level models that explicitly represent the coupling between the electrical
and thermal-fluid domains. By introducing expanded models for IM, pumps, heat pumps,
chillers, and CHP units, this chapter establishes the foundation for holistic multi-energy
simulation, co-optimization, and control—capabilities that are essential for next-generation
district energy systems.

3.2 Coupled Thermal Electrical Components Models

3.2.1 Induction Machine

Introduction

Initially predominant in industrial settings, IM are now integral to various applications. For
example, IM are increasingly employed in heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC)
systems, elevators, and other building services that require efficient power management.
Integration into building management systems is vital to optimizing energy use, minimizing
operational costs, and improving overall building performance. This integration not only
contributes to energy efficiency but also plays a role in the comfort and safety of building
occupants.

This reliance on IM within the building sector is a crucial step towards their strategic role
in the larger context of grid-interactive applications. The concept of integration building-to-
grid (B2G) represents a transformative approach to energy management, allowing buildings
to change from passive energy consumers to dynamic and interactive energy grid compo-
nents. In this paradigm, buildings actively respond to grid conditions, such as voltage drops,
by adapting their energy consumption and operational performance. Induction machines are
pivotal in this context, ensuring efficient energy transfer and maintaining operational stability

 38

under varying grid conditions. Their ability to maintain performance during voltage fluctua-
tions is critical for grid resilience, allowing buildings to support grid stability and contribute
to a more flexible and robust energy infrastructure.

The complexity and dynamic nature of B2G systems require accurate and efficient modeling
of IM. Modeling is crucial as it enables the prediction and optimization of machine perfor-
mance in dynamic scenarios, ensuring that systems can efficiently respond to varying de-
mands from both buildings and the grid. A comprehensive model also improves understand-
ing of the complex interactions between machines, building systems, and the grid, facilitat-
ing the development of energy-efficient and sustainable strategies. Additionally, modeling
allows simulation of scenarios, supporting informed decision-making in building design and
grid integration. Developing a model that accurately represents the complex dynamics of
IM in B2G systems presents several challenges. B2G applications care about variables such
as terminal voltage, current, PF, and power consumption. The building and energy industry
practice requires fast computing speed, such as completing an annual simulation within a
few minutes.

Several existing models provide steady or quasi-transient state analysis that considers the
operating conditions and responses to resemble steady state to avoid using complex calcu-
lations [27]. However, this steady-state assumption is only suitable for mechanical devices,
such as chillers and cooling towers, which have response times of minutes to hours. The
slow response time is not valid for electrical equipment such as electric motors. In such ap-
plications, the steady-state models fail to consider the dynamic nature of voltage, current, PF,
and dynamic responses to slight changes in the set point since these factors tend to change
in periods of milliseconds to seconds.

There is a gap in the existing models that can achieve both high accuracy and computational
efficiency for IM in B2G applications. We propose a new Computationally Efficient and
Accurate Induction Machine (CEAIM) model by combining circuit theory with parametric
methods that can be coupled with mechanical devices like pumps, chillers, and heat pumps
to simulate multi-domain models. This approach bypasses the direct simulation of power

 39

electronic transitions by substituting them with sophisticated mathematical approximations,
since it considers the interaction of current and voltage through the basic circuit elements,
such as resistors, inductors, and capacitors, using circuit theory and having predefined circuit
parameters from the product database, thereby optimizing computational efficiency.

3.3 Combined Heat and Power System Model Development

CHP systems generate on-site electricity and reuse waste heat to supply heating or cooling
demand with steam. Therefore, CHP systems are efficient, achieving overall efficiencies
between 65% and 80%. They are 30% to 70% more efficient than traditional separate on-
site heat generation and grid power [32]. In large-scale district energy applications, the new
CHP model must satisfy two key requirements. First, the model should be physics-based and
capable of capturing the dynamic behavior of thermodynamic variables, including mass flow
and enthalpy variations in liquid water and saturated steam, while also calculating power
generation. Second, it must remain computationally efficient by avoiding discontinuities and
reducing the presence of nonlinear equation systems, ensuring stable and fast simulations.

To meet the requirements, two main approaches are employed to increase the simulation
speed while maintaining acceptable accuracy in district energy applications. The first ap-

 53

proach is the split-medium [33] method. Separates the water and steam mediums into two
different medium models, avoiding numerical discontinuities and nonlinearities when simu-
lating phase changes from water to steam. The model using this approach is available in the
Modelica Buildings Library [31]. The second approach uses empirical correlation functions
or look-up tables to compute essential physical variables and thermal efficiencies, captur-
ing thermodynamic properties and energy conversion processes. This eliminates the need to
calculate the nonlinear thermodynamic properties of superheated steam, thereby enhancing
the simulation speed. The application of these two approaches leads to differences between
the simplified CHP model and the actual physical system. Thus, the following sections are
arranged as follows: 1) description of the CHP physical system, 2) implementation of the
simplified CHP model using Modelica, and 3) model validation.

3.3.1 Description of Physical System

In district energy applications, CHP combined-cycle plants, illustrated in Figure 3.16, op-
timize fuel utilization. The combined-cycle system can be divided into topping cycle and
bottoming cycle subsystems, and the details are described as follows.

3.3.1.1 Topping Cycle Subsystem

In the topping cycle, the gas turbine transforms a fraction of the fuel energy into mechan-
ical energy, driving a generator to produce electricity. In this process, assuming negligible
mechanical and thermal losses, the fuel combustion energy equals the sum of generated elec-
tricity and the exhaust gas heat flow. The energy balance is expressed as:

 Q̇fuel = PGTG + Q̇exhaust , (3.23)

where Q̇fuel represents the energy from the combustion of fuel in the gas turbine, PGTG is the
generation of electricity, and Q̇exhaust denotes the heat from the exhaust gas. The combustion
energy Q̇fuel is determined by the mass flow rate of the fuel, ṁfuel , and its lower heating value
(LHV):
 Q̇fuel = ṁfuel LHVfuel . (3.24)

In detail, the portion of the fuel combustion energy, ηGTG , is used to generate electricity.
The remaining portion of energy, 1 − ηGTG , is supplied to the bottoming cycle as exhaust
gas. These equations are:
 PGTG = Q̇fuel ηGTG and (3.25)

3.3.1.2 Bottoming Cycle Subsystem

In the bottoming cycle, the heat recovery steam generator (HRSG) uses the exhaust gas
energy, Q̇exhaust , to heat the feedwater and then produces superheated steam, which then
drives the steam turbine shaft to generate additional electric power PSTG . Finally, the exhaust
steam from the steam turbine is desuperheated from the lower-pressure superheated steam
to saturated steam Q̇steam,sat , including energy losses Q̇steam,loss during this desuperheating
process. Therefore, the energy balance in the bottoming cycle subsystem is presented as:

 Q̇exhaust + Q̇water = Q̇exhaust,loss + Q̇steam,loss + Q̇steam,sat + PSTG , (3.27)

where Q̇water is the energy flow of the feedwater, Q̇exhaust,loss denotes the heat losses in the
exhaust gas stack, Q̇steam,loss represents the losses in steam energy during the conversion
from superheated steam to saturated steam, and PSTG means the electric power production of
the steam turbine generator. The steam turbine electricity is calculated as:

 PSTG = Q̇exhaust ηHRSG ηSTG , (3.28)

where ηHRSG is the effectiveness of the HRSG and ηSTG denotes the efficiency of the steam
turbine generator. The thermal efficiencies of HRSG, steam turbine generator, and gas tur-

CHP system. The following section details the correlation functions used to calculate these

3.4 Summary

This chapter highlighted the growing importance of accurately representing the coupling
between electrical and thermal-fluid systems in modern district energy networks. As electri-
cally driven thermal technologies and multi-energy assets become more prevalent, simplified
or domain-separated modeling approaches are no longer sufficient to capture the complex in-
teractions that influence system performance and stability. By introducing enhanced models
for IM, pumps, heat pumps, chillers, and CHP units, the chapter provides a unified frame-
work for analyzing the electromechanical, thermodynamic, and hydraulic relationships that
govern district energy systems. These models offer improved fidelity, enabling a more realis-
tic simulation of component behavior, system-level dynamics, and cross-domain operational
constraints.

The integrated modeling approach presented here supports more accurate assessments of
flexibility, efficiency, and resilience across district energy networks. It also lays the founda-
tion for advanced analysis and control strategies that leverage the intrinsic coupling between
electrical and thermal-fluid subsystems. As district energy systems continue to evolve toward
more interconnected and responsive architectures, the ability to model these interactions with
high resolution will remain essential for effective planning, optimization, and operation.

 CHAPTER 4

 DEVELOPMENT OF OPTIMAL ALGORITHMS

4.1 Introduction

Enhancing grid resilience through the integration of DERs with microgrids is essential due
to the varied nature of DERs. This chapter explores the optimal combined operation of these
energy sources within microgrids. It presents the design, implementation, and validation
of a model predictive control (MPC)-based secondary control scheme to address two key
challenges: optimal islanded operation and optimal re-synchronization of a microgrid. The
MPC algorithm dynamically adjusts input signals, or manipulated variables, for each DER,
including a gas turbine, an aggregate PV unit, and an electrical BESS unit.

For optimal islanded operation, the MPC-based secondary controller promptly upholds mi-
crogrid functionality after an islanding event, balancing power and ensuring system relia-
bility. For optimal re-synchronization, the controller adjusts the manipulated variables to
synchronize voltage and angle with the point of common coupling of the system. This single
MPC-driven control system effectively guides the microgrid through different operational
stages by updating the MPC’s target reference.

4.2 Microgrid Control Basic Concepts

We first discuss some background concepts necessary to understand the overall optimization
framework specific to microgrids and DERs.

 67

4.2.1 Microgrid Control

When a microgrid is disconnected from the main grid (islanded mode), the microgrid energy
management system (EMS) must maintain the isolated microgrid operationally, adhering to
the system performance requirements. For medium level grids, which is the case of the
microgrid in this work (ranging from 1 kV to 35 kV), the power quality requirements that
the microgrid must abide by are:

 • Voltage - Rapid voltage changes in the grid, mainly caused by load variations in the
 network, must be within a 5% margin of the nominal voltage level.

 • Frequency - For systems with distributed energy resources, which is the case for mi-
 crogrids, the frequency must be within a tight frequency band of ± 0.5 Hz from the
 nominal frequency of the system. This condition will be enforced during the pre-,
 during-, and post-islanding phases of the microgrid.

The quality criteria defined above are found in the power system energy quality standards
IEEE Std 1547.4-2011 [37] and Standard EN 50160 [38].

4.2.2 Microgrid Re-synchronization

When the microgrid undergoes re-synchronization, the EMS tracks the main grid side volt-
age phasor at the point of common coupling between the microgrid and the utility grid. A
successful re-synchronization achieves minimal power oscillations in both systems, and it
is conducted by measuring the voltage phasor at the utility bus side. A smooth connection
is guaranteed when the voltages in both busses are similar in magnitude and phase [39].
Furthermore, the frequency deviation between the two systems must be as small as pos-
sible [39, 40]. Additionally, proper re-synchronization also reduces unnecessary torsional
stress on the synchronous generator rotor. Elevated torsional stress in rotors can reduce the
expected lifetime of the equipment, resulting in the loss of millions of dollars. Thus, the
maximum acceptable variation in a generator’s power injection at the re-synchronization

 68

instant (for the case of steam turbines) is 50% of its rated active power [41].

4.3 Microgrid Design

Here, we introduce a method for optimizing resource placement in distribution networks
based on the multi-armed bandit machine learning (ML) algorithm. Unlike reinforcement
learning (RL) k-armed bandits operate in stateless environments and settle on a single strat-
egy regardless of the time in which an action is implemented [42]. We consider the “action”
to be the placement of a resource, which should be consistent over time to indicate a physical
permanent installation. This is in contrast to typical uses of RL, which are for operational
tasks— for example, controlling a robot to avoid falling while walking across rough terrain,
training autonomous vehicles, and the like. This use of RL is less commonly seen, but it
allows us to solve a challenging optimization problem by “learning” what the optimal design
should be.

Using the given load profiles and a network topology, the resulting ML agent can place
distributed energy resources throughout a network during the planning phase. The proposed
ML agent can be used to determine where resources should be placed using approximate
load profiles at various points in the network during the design phase or as part of a retrofit,
especially where the historical load profile has already been observed. ML is useful even in
scenarios where the availability of allowable actions is limited or the number of resources is
constrained, as shown in the remainder of this section. Additionally, a system does not need
to have a set of analytical equations to be optimized, in contrast to conventional optimization.
The model of the system could be as abstract as a black box, and as long as outputs can be
observed, the ML agent can learn optimal placements.

prediction function (i.e., the ML agent only uses information it has already observed instead
of trying new combinations to gain more knowledge).

We compare the “Selected” node case with the “Baseline,” or the original IEEE 13-bus net-
work design, which, while the network does not necessarily have “optimal” resource place-
ments, presumably has relatively realistically placed ones. In contrast to the other checkpoint
tests, both cases have a fixed resource placement at every timestep. In this way, these two
cases are the only truly feasible solutions because the checkpoint voltage profiles represent a

 73

scenario where the resources may be mobilized over time. Figure 4.1 shows the underlying
“top” picks for resource placement at each checkpoint, as well as the actual distribution of
exploration in the darker shaded region.

We effectively terminate the training period of the ML agent at 200k training steps, where
we can see in the extended training period shown in Figure 4.2 that the ML agent tested on
the training dataset and a testing dataset converges in performance. The sharp divergence in
performance after this cutoff indicates that the ML agent overfits the training dataset at the
cost of poor performance on the testing dataset.

4.4.1 System Model Blueprint

Figure 4.4 illustrates the configuration of the microgrid system and its connection to the
main grid. The microgrid includes a 10 MW combustion-based synchronous generator with

primary-level control, a PV plant, and BESS units with active/reactive power controls. The
renewable sources are rated at 5 MW. The utility grid model consists of a power distri-
bution system, a hydro power plant, and associated infrastructure. In this setup, the syn-

 76

chronous generator dictates the voltage and frequency within the microgrid, while the re-
newable DERs operate as “grid-following" components. This system model aims to examine
microgrid interaction with nearby energy sources and simulate both joint operation during
re-synchronization and isolated operation when the microgrid operates independently due to
circuit breaker tripping.

MPC optimization algorithms generate updated system input values that steer the system
towards a user-defined goal. Thus, for secondary control and re-synchronization purposes,
the idea is to use MPC to update the set point values for specific variables within each of the
DER’s primary control loops. For the microgrid model studied in this work, there are five
input variables (which dictate the reference values in each DER source), characterized by
the variables u1 through u5 . These set point values are herein referred to as the “manipulated
variables” that the MPC algorithm will provide to steer the microgrid toward a desired tar-
get. Rather than updating the reference value itself, the manipulated variables u1 through u5
are added to the pre-islanded reference value, which originates from a power flow solution.
This was implemented to facilitate the simulation procedure. For the synchronous genera-
tion unit, the manipulated variables are mechanical power reference and excitation system
voltage reference. Since PV systems are non-dispatchable units, typically set to maximum
active power injection using a maximum power point tracking algorithm, this leaves reactive
power control as the only manipulated variable. BESS, on the other hand, is dispatchable;
therefore, the manipulated variables are active and reactive power references. The microgrid
is interconnected to the utility grid through a single feeder transmission line, represented by
the Distribution Line 3 and its point of common coupling (PCC), which is equipped with a
circuit breaker. Both the microgrid and the utility grid have a load that represents an aggre-
gate of electrical loads. Load 1 is the aggregate load of a passive distribution network, and
Load 2 is the aggregate load within the microgrid. In order to test the effectiveness of the
MPC controller, the authors added stochastic features to Load 2 as well as irradiance for the
PV unit. More information is presented in the following sections.

4.4.3 Resilience of Microgrid

At time t = 1.0, the line connecting the Utility Grid to the Microgrid in Fig. 4.5 is opened,
causing the microgrid to operate in islanded mode. Figure 4.6 shows the real (P) and reactive
(Q) power from the battery over time (left-hand plots) as well as the state of charge over time
(right hand plots). In islanded operation mode, the MPC solution changes the references
of G2 (for torque and voltage), PV (for reactive power) and BESS (for P and Q) to control
the frequency, voltage and supply the load reaching steady state at t = 6.02 seconds. When
the microgrid has been controlled and it is ready to resynchonized, the MPC resynchronizes
to the grid at approximately t = 80.1 seconds. The MPC is initially configured to cease to
operate after the resynchronization process has been completed, after approximately t = 120
seconds; that is, the set-points for each of the sources are no longer updated by the MPC.
This means the MPC solution can assist the microgrid to withstand external grid disturbances
in autonomous mode during major grid transients, guaranteeing resilience. More detailed
analysis of the islanding and resychronization process can be found in [15].

To understand the resiliency implications after resynchronization and how these can be

 79

address through optimization, we present results for two scenarios of operation after re-
synchronization. Again, Figure 4.6 shows the real (P) and reactive (Q) power from the bat-
tery over time (left-hand plots) as well as the state of charge over time (right hand plots). The
top two subplots illustrate a “baseline” case, and the bottom two are the “optimized” case
(note the different x-axes). Without full modeling of the control system, the battery drains
itself immediately to its minimum allowable charge (20%) after the disconnection event, pro-
viding power for about 1.8 hours. With intelligent control, the MPC optimization algorithm
better utilizes all the resources in the microgrid to allow the battery to charge from sources
like the 5 MW PV generators, supplying power to the microgrid for nearly 4 hours. The
battery and on-site generation can be sized up even further for increased resiliency benefits,
although practically, cost would come into consideration in the optimization.

 DEMONSTRATION AT TWO REAL-WORLD TESTBEDS

6.1 Introduction

District energy systems are critical infrastructure for universities, cities, and large campuses,
providing reliable heating, cooling, and electricity across many interconnected buildings.
Their scale and complexity make them ideal environments for demonstrating advanced mod-
eling, optimization, and control technologies. This chapter presents how the project’s inte-
grated modeling framework was applied to two real-world testbeds—the University of Col-
orado Boulder (CU Boulder) and the University of Texas at Austin (UT Austin)—to eval-
uate system performance, identify retrofit opportunities, and validate new control and opti-
mization strategies. These testbeds represent two distinct but complementary challenges: a
multi-plant district cooling system, an extensive steam-based district heating system, and a
high-efficiency, multi-station cooling network with thermal energy storage.

At both campuses, high-fidelity physics-based models were developed using Modelica lan-
guage to capture the behavior of chillers, boilers, heat exchangers, cooling towers, pumps,
distribution networks, and building loads. Using real operational data such as flow rates,
temperatures, pressures, and equipment performance curves, these models were calibrated
to represent actual system behavior with high accuracy. Using these validated models, we
evaluated a range of operational improvements, such as waste heat recovery, reduction of
pressure-setpoints, and advanced rule- or model-based control retrofits. These analyzes
demonstrate how dynamic simulation enables the discovery of energy-saving opportunities
that are often difficult to identify or test directly in a live campus environment.

Together, these two testbeds demonstrate the power of using integrated thermal–electrical

 94

modeling and control analysis to improve district system efficiency, reduce energy costs, and
support the adoption of DER technologies. This chapter highlights the methodologies used,
key results, and performance insights obtained on both campuses.

6.2 District Cooling System

6.2.1 University of Colorado Boulder District Energy Plants

The CU Boulder campus has two district energy plants. The West District Energy Plant
(WDEP) and the East District Energy Plant (EDEP) are interconnected to serve the campus
thermal load via ∼ 15 miles of steam and chilled water (CHW) distribution piping on the
main campus. WDEP consists of 31 MW CHP, two conventional steam boilers (130,000
lb/hr each), two chillers (1,625 tons each), and the required pumping and distribution sys-
tems; EDEP has two chillers (1,625 tons each) serving the main campus.

The centralized supply of thermal energy via the district energy system serves most of the
main campus buildings (∼ 70%), and a few decentralized chillers and boilers are set up at re-
mote buildings. The central supply facilitates efficient operations and maintenance, deliver-
ing stable and reliable base load heating and cooling year-round. This efficient performance
may not be possible to the same degree via decentralized approaches. The process control
systems are integrated between plants under one control system hierarchy using a dedicated
redundant fiber optic network.

6.2.1.1 Testbed Validation

We collected technical information such as schematics, equipment parameters, control se-
quences, and operational data, including mass flow, pressure, and temperature measure-
ments. Operational data used for calibrating the component models were selected based
on accuracy and sampling frequency (15-minute intervals).

Model Development: The Modelica Language was used to create and simulate all models

6.2.1.2 CDW Setpoint Optimization

To reduce energy use, we optimized the CDW supply temperature and the CDW mass flow in
the WDEP. CDW was optimized by applying a single constant CDW supply setpoint value
for the entire simulation (constant), optimizing constant CDW supply setpoints for given
timeframes (scheduled), optimizing a constant approach temperature (Tapp) as the CDW
supply temperature setpoint, and optimizing an approach temperature-based setpoint for a
given timeframe (scheduled Tapp). In each of these scenarios, we ran trials with and without
optimizing the CDW mass flow. We found that varying the CDW supply temperature setpoint
based on wetbulb temperature saved the most energy, but simply changing the CDW supply
temperature to stay above the wetbulb temperature can save up to 12% of energy. We also
found that reducing mass flow consistently reduced energy use by up to another 3%.

While obtaining results, we realized that the chiller models used in the WDEP assume that
the chiller refrigerant flow, as well as the condenser and CHW loops, is a constant flow,
which is not the case for the WDEP. A newer chiller model accounts for variable flow by
considering the CDW return temperature instead of the supply temperature in the perfor-
mance curve. However, this chiller model causes simulation crashes when implemented in
the WDEP model. In the interest of time, we are attempting to quantify the chiller power
input difference between the original and new chiller models to determine for which CDW
setpoint values and mass flows the models are comparable, instead of redeveloping the model
with the newer chiller components. For now, all results presented will only include scenarios
where the mass flow is kept at a nominal value.

Discussion: After successfully simulating the entire virtual WDEP testbed, setpoint op-
timization using Dymola Optimization Library was conducted. Energy was minimized by
tuning the CDWST setpoint and/or the CDW mass flow. The CDWST setpoint was tuned
using four different methods: a constant value, a constant value changed monthly, a constant
approach temperature, and a constant approach temperature changed monthly. The CDW
mass flow was tuned by changing the CDW pump input signal. As expected, optimization

 105

results improved as optimized timeframes became more granular, and approach temperature-
based methods outperformed constant setpoint methods.

Because the chiller model used was designed for constant flow scenarios, the trials optimiz-
ing mass flow require more analysis before being presented as more than preliminary results.
The highest energy savings for constant CDWST and approach temperature-based methods,
respectively, were 11.0% and 15.9% when optimized at monthly timeframes. More savings
are likely if optimizing at more granular timescales, such as weeks or days.

The largest reason so much energy was saved in each scenario was that the WDEP controls
originally set the setpoint at a temperature lower than or equal to the wetbulb temperature
for much of the year. As a result, the cooling towers expended large amounts of power to
attempt to meet those setpoints, which could not physically be achieved through evaporative
cooling. The consequence of this low setpoint can be seen in Figure 6.3. In Figure 6.3,
the cooling towers in scenarios that do not raise the CDWST operate at maximum speed for
the majority of the investigated time period, whereas the cooling towers in the optimized
CDWST scenarios can consistently drop well below maximum power input. Note that two
cooling towers are assigned to each chiller, and the maximum power input of each cooling
tower is around 7500 kW.

6.2.1.3 Guideline 36

Overview: In the next analysis for the district cooling system case study, we compared
our setpoint optimization results against the ASHRAE G36-2021, a well-known rule-based
control sequence recognized for its high performing control sequence. We conducted this
analysis to evaluate G36 in a district cooling system, which had not been done in prior liter-
ature. To conduct this experiment, we improved our initial district system model, integrated
G36 controls, and compared the energy use of each control sequence using four representa-
tive load timeframes.

G36 Controls Implementation: At its core, G36 follows the same control structure as the
CU Boulder default controls. It loosely bases CT, CHW pump, and CDW pump staging on
chiller staging (although cooling tower and CHW pump staging are more accurately based on
flow). It incorporates CDW and CHW bypass controls to prevent freezing and chiller dam-

 107

age. CHW pump speed is based on differential pressure control. Unlike the default controls,
however, G36 incorporates more sophisticated, responsive logic to adjust cooling plant oper-
ation according to CHW needs, thereby theoretically improving overall efficiency. Examples
of this include the CHWST, differential pressure, and CDWST resets. The research question
arising from these more sophisticated controls is whether they make the chiller plant more
energy efficient than optimized setpoints.

Control Sequence Testing: All G36 controls underwent a systematic testing procedure
meant to ensure robustness, replicability, and easy referencing back to the original guide-
line. This method is outlined in Figure 6.4 below. Centralized documentation on each main
control sequence, the individual control actions within each sequence, the G36 sections,
Modelica models and simulation examples corresponding to each action, and whether said
models passed testing is kept with the model repository. Modelica models are named ac-
cording to their control sequence, and simulation examples are named according to the G36
section they reference. Results are plotted automatically with the simulation examples to
facilitate control testing replicability and understanding.

Discussion: Our preliminary results show that the proposed control (G36) case used the
most energy of all three investigated control sequences, and that a fault in the control logic
caused it to get stuck in suboptimal CDWST setpoints, leading to higher energy use than
the default and optimized controls. As shown by the optimized case, increasing CDWST
setpoints during the summer months and decreasing during the swing seasons allowed effec-
tive evaporative cooling without overrunning the cooling tower fans. In all but the med-high
case, this led to the optimal case using less cooling tower energy than the baseline with very
similar pump and chiller input power. In the med-high case, the optimized setpoints lead to
basically equivalent energy use, which is likely because optimizing a given timeframe does
not guaranty better performance for every sub-timeframe within it. Therefore, these results
show that the proposed controls consistently met building loads but used more energy than
the default and optimized cases in the medium-low, medium-high, and high load cases. De-
spite the higher energy use, all of these results are reasonable and therefore suggest that G36
can be applied to district cooling systems. It should be noted that total energy savings depend
on the frequency of each load range occurring throughout the year, so analyzes focusing on

 120

an annual timeframe are therefore needed. The high energy use of G36 in this case study is
likely primarily due to the high setpoints of CDWST that subuse CT and overuse chillers,
but might also be due to differences between district cooling systems and the operation of
the individual system, three examples of which are presented here.

Firstly, the current implementation of G36 relies heavily on trim and respond-like controls
that modify plant behavior every few minutes. However, district cooling system pipe net-
works are large, which could require longer lag times to be considered in controls. District
cooling system equipment might also be oversized compared to individual systems, which
could affect trim-and-respond logic setpoints. Secondly, the current CDWST setpoint con-
trol tends to shift the cooling load from the chiller to the cooling towers It is possible that
district cooling system equipment is sized differently than individual systems in ways that
change the optimality of when and to which equipment to shift the cooling load. Thirdly,
there were no explicit directions on how to generate CHW and plant reset signals for district
cooling systems in G36. To accommodate multiple buildings in the district cooling system,
our implementation takes the most extreme value of all buildings as the proxy for a single
zone signal in an individual system, which might be a suboptimal method to generate reset
signals.

Conclusions: The main goals of this work were to conduct a study evaluating rule-based
control strategies for district cooling systems using dynamic, equation-based modeling tools.
The main questions investigated through this work are: how equation-based modeling can
be applied to optimize the operation of large district cooling systems; what the impact of
different rule-based control strategies is on district cooling system operation; and whether
the well-known rule-based G36 control is worth overhauling an existing control sequence,
or, put another way, whether it uses less energy than a simple control retrofit.

Through this work, we demonstrated the usefulness of dynamic equation-based models for
control analysis through the development of a calibrated chiller plant and district network
of a real-world cooling system, coupled with two main control sequences, one of which has
been optimized for monthly setpoints for detailed control evaluation. We first developed and

 121

calibrated the equipment of a high-fidelity chiller plant using manufacturer and operational
data from CU Boulder. We then analyzed real-world district network data from CU Boulder,
such as monthly metered building cooling data, district network configuration, and chiller
plant schematics, to create a corresponding district network model with a reasonable remote
building pressure drop and multiple parallel building clusters that emulated the CU Boulder
district. In addition to developing the physical plant model, we also developed Modelica
models for two separate district cooling system control sequences. First, we implemented
default controls in Modelica using a combination of operational data and control sequences
provided by the operators and optimized its CDWST setpoint on a monthly basis, using the
pattern search optimization method in the Dymola Optimization Library. Then we developed
and tested Modelica models for fifteen G36 control sequences related to the district cooling
system as a proposed rule-based control.

We then show the impact of controls on district cooling system energy use first through
setpoint optimization and then through an analysis that compares the district cooling system
with the default, optimized, and proposed controls. When simulating the chiller plant with a
simplified district network over a 9.5 month period, we found that optimized controls saved
more than 11% of total energy compared to default controls. For the default, optimized,
and proposed control analyzes, we simulated the district cooling model with each control
sequence for four representative load cases spanning the full range of loads experienced by
the plant and found significant energy differences between each control sequence.

We found that G36 control sequences do not save energy compared to the default and opti-
mized control sequences for this case study. There are two possible reasons. First, G36 is
intended for large buildings, not district cooling systems. Thus, our implementation may not
reflect the intention of the G36 well. Second, there are potential faults or improvements in the
control sequence. This latter hypothesis needs further evaluation, which will be conducted
in future studies.

This work is one of the first studies analyzing G36 hydronic control sequences’ energy per-
formance in district cooling systems. More work is needed to quantify energy savings and

 122

understand how G36 can be modified to better serve district cooling systems. Future work
can include quantifying energy use over an annual timeframe, modifying G36 chiller plant
controls to consider the unique aspects of district cooling systems (such as equipment sizing,
lag in pipe networks, and signal generation), and investigating different climates and plant
configurations.

6.2.2.5 Optimal Condenser Flowrate Simulations in Modelica

The validated model of the UT Austin cooling system was used to identify optimal condenser
loop flow rates to minimize energy use. An aspect of district cooling system control that
has been studied by various authors in the past without producing consistent results is the
varying flow of the condenser-cooling tower loop. If condenser loop flow is reduced by
properly designed variable speed pumps, it will save pump power by following the pump
affinity laws (P-V³). However, the reduction in flow will cause larger differences in the inlet-

 128

outlet temperature of the condenser, resulting in higher average CDW temperatures; this
increases the work done by the compressor, thereby increasing the electricity consumption
of the chiller. At some optimal flow rate, the combined electrical consumption of the chiller,
pumps, and cooling tower will have a minimum value.

To isolate the effect of varying condenser loop flow, cooling tower control was consistently
maintained between trials while condenser loop flow was varied across a wide range of real-
istic values (3.0 GPM/Ton to 1.0 GPM/Ton) in increments of 0.1 GPM/Ton. For this study,
the cooling tower fan control at UT Austin was taken from nominal tower design conditions
and verified using measured data. It was found that the cooling tower leaving water setpoint
is set to an approach temperature (difference between ambient wet bulb temperature and
leaving cooling tower water temperature) of approximately 3°C or 5.5°F.

Controlling cooling tower leaving water setpoints using an approach temperature is a com-
mon method of control [55]. This method has also been used in previous case studies mod-
eling cooling towers [56]. Typical cooling tower control maintains a constant approach tem-
perature based on nominal design conditions, and an approach temperature of approximately
3°C (5.5°F) is common in modern cooling towers and has been used in previous studies [57].
Annual simulations were conducted with real data and the flow rate that produced the min-
imum electrical consumption was identified for each hour of the year. Figure 6.16 displays
the simulation results testing across a range of constant flow rates for an annual simulation
using station 6 loadings as input. Data are normalized to the minimum annual consumption
at 2.4 GPM/Ton (0.043 kg/s/kW). Figure 6.17 displays results collecting all of the hourly in-
stances in which a particular flow rate led to the lowest overall system electrical consumption
while testing station 6 loadings as inputs.

Model Development: The integrated district heating system model is developed using the
German method of network aggregation [58], which simplifies intricate networks by consoli-
dating numerous nodes (consumers) with similar demand patterns or geographical proximity
into fewer representative nodes. This approach, illustrated in Figure 6.21, aggregates ther-
mal demands (building clusters) and recalculates equivalent thermal properties and hydraulic
resistances of the distribution network, reducing computational complexity when modeling
extensive thermal grids. The demand duration curve for the nine clusters of aggregated build-
ings during the peak season, winter, and summer is depicted in Figure 6.22. These hourly
heating demand profiles, obtained from SCADA systems, serve as inputs for the ETS model,
which is supplied by the district plants, accounting for network steam losses. Derived equiv-
alent steam distribution network sizing with aggregated building loads and pressure-reducing
valve (PRV) setpoints is detailed in Table 6.11. The simplified model has comparable per-
formance to a detailed model, which we have evaluated by comparing our model results to a
steady-state engineering tool called the Pipe-Flo advantage v18.1 in the successive sections
(developed and used by the engineering consultants of CU Boulder facility). Consequently,
the aggregated DES model facilitates streamlined analysis and optimization of operational
strategies, such as supply pressure control and load management across multiple plants.

6.3.1.2 Retrofit Approaches

Figure 6.26: Retrofit Explorations to Meet the Energy Efficiency Goals Broadly Classified as
(a) System Retrofit (b) Control Retrofit, Along with the Systems Considered for Evaluation

CU Boulder aging steam systems commonly experience energy losses during the generation,
distribution, and consumption phases, leading to inefficiencies and increased operational ex-
penses. In response, we explored two system retrofit opportunities (see Figure 6.26). First,
we identified the waste heat lost in the system and investigated methods for its recovery.
Second, we conducted a parametric study on operational pressure setpoint reduction, which
resulted in lower network losses. Furthermore, CU Boulder systems typically rely on con-
ventional control methods based on operator experience or simplistic rule-based algorithms.
Currently, operators are responsible for maintaining steam system pressure. This requires
central plants to have sufficient boiler capacity available and operating to meet system de-
mand at all times. Additionally, multiple boilers are operated at capacity levels that allow
for immediate ramp-up if the largest boiler in operation fails, ensuring that system pressure
is maintained. Consequently, different boilers are online and operating at various capacities
based on demand and available capacity, all managed by operators working 12-hour shifts
each day. While the CHP system is typically operated as a peaker plant to serve utility
needs, there is no defined sequence of operations. These traditional approaches often fail
to optimize system performance or quickly and dynamically respond to shifts in demand
and weather conditions. In response, we explored a model-based multi-central plant co-
ordinated control approach for the district heating system and a model-free reinforcement

 142

learning-based approach for optimizing the operational cost for the district heating + CHP
systems (refer Figure 6.26). The following subsections discuss the results of these retrofit
explorations in detail.

Waste Heat Recovery: In this step, we explore two waste heat recovery opportunities.
First, exhaust heat recovery through (non-)condensing economizers integrated with boilers
and combined heat and power systems. Second, flash steam recovery from high-temperature
condensate from the district distribution network (see Figure 6.27). Comparative analyzes
against the baseline system reveal substantial heat recovery of 38% through heat recovery
steam generators, along with 5.2-7.8% fuel reductions through exhaust economizers (see
Figure 6.28). The results indicate that condensing economizers are impractical for districts
with high condensate returns at a saturated state (see Figure 6.28 (d)), while they can be
beneficial for districts that use ambient temperature feedwater. In addition, a fuel savings of
1.8% is achieved by recovering 9.2% of flash steam energy from the net energy condensate
losses.

6.3.1.4 Operational Cost Optimization - Model-free Approach

Model-free control methodologies may be a viable alternative to the challenges faced by
model-based methods in terms of adaptability, computational resources, handling multi-
objectives, etc. As a response, this task explored a RL supervisory control to minimize the
operational costs of the CU Boulder campus DES. To enhance overall system efficiency, we
utilize resource flexibility to improve DES operations by responding to fluctuations in utility
prices. In this section, we demonstrate the toolchain, develop the virtual testbed, engineer a
suitable RL reward, and learn from challenges.

Reinforcement Learning: The RL agent, represented by a machine learning algorithm,
takes actions in the environment (DES virtual testbed), and receives rewards or penalties as
feedback. The objective of the agent is to continuously revise its strategy over time to maxi-
mize cumulative rewards. In brief, RL is broadly categorized into value-based methods (e.g.,
Q-learning, Deep Q Network), estimating expected rewards for actions, and policy-based
methods (e.g., deep deterministic policy gradient, actor-critic, proximal policy optimiza-
tion), directly determining optimal decision-making policies. For this work, we use proximal
policy optimization (PPO), a modification of the actor-critic framework that addresses chal-
lenges related to stability and sample efficiency in training policies using Kullback-Leibler

6.3.1.5 Aggregated Efficiency, Economic, and Environmental Impacts

To quantify the broader environmental benefits and evaluate the system against total effi-
ciency targets, the operational fuel savings from the pressure-setpoint and multi-plant coor-
dination strategies were translated into source energy reductions and carbon dioxide equiv-
alent (CO2 e) emissions avoided. The technical analyses in the preceding sections quantified
savings in terms of site fuel consumption (ṁ f uel ); these values were converted to primary en-
ergy impacts using standard natural gas conversion factors, specifically a Source-Site Ratio
of 1.05 and an Emission Factor of approximately 53.06 kg CO2 e/MMBtu.

The feasible reduction of steam operational pressure from 9 bar to 6 bar yielded a 4.66%
reduction in site natural gas consumption. Given the direct linear relationship between
fuel combustion and emissions, this translates to a proportional 4.66% reduction in total
source energy and operational carbon intensity for the district. Furthermore, the implemen-
tation of the optimized multi-plant load modulation strategy shifts heating loads to boilers
with higher part-load efficiencies—specifically prioritizing Plant 2 boilers, which operate at
nearly 90% efficiency, over the less efficient Plant 1 units. This improved dispatching results
in a weighted average source energy reduction of 13.1% to 15.3% across the heating season,
significantly lowering the primary fuel input required per unit of delivered heat.

 160

Complementing these efficiency-focused strategies, a model-free Reinforcement Learning
(RL) supervisory control was evaluated to optimize operational costs and grid interaction.
Utilizing a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agent, this approach demonstrated a 32.3%
reduction in net operational costs and a 13.2% reduction in peak electrical demand over
a representative testing period. By dynamically managing CHP generation in response to
utility signals, the RL controller effectively transforms the district into a grid-interactive
asset, offering significant demand flexibility alongside thermal efficiency.

Aggregating these measures demonstrates that the proposed unified modeling and control
framework achieves substantial gains in total system efficiency. Combining the pressure-
setpoint reduction with multi-plant coordinated control yields a cumulative operational effi-
ciency improvement of approximately 17.8% to 20.0%. When these operational strategies
are deployed alongside the complementary waste heat recovery retrofits identified in Section
6.3.1.2, which contribute an additional 5.2% to 7.8% reduction, the total system efficiency
improvement reaches 23% to 27.8%. This combined implementation validates the capability
to achieve a total system efficiency improvement exceeding 25% from source to delivered
energy, significantly reducing the carbon footprint of the district heating network.

6.3.2.1 UT Austin CHP and District Heating System

This section describes the development of CHP plant and district heating models at UT
Austin. CHP system models were optimized to reduce annual energy use, and were mod-
eled with new hypothetical renewable generation from wind and solar to reduce emissions.
Additionally, retrofit options were proposed to further reduce energy use. UT Austin’s main
campus utilizes an islanded microgrid CHP system with district heating to provide its over
160 buildings with all of their utility needs. The CHP system at UT Austin is not able to
sell excess electricity to the surrounding grid because UT Austin is not a registered Texas
electric energy power producer, and it does not follow regulations defined by The Electric
Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT). UT also does not purchase electricity from the local

 161

utility.

The UT campus utility system is closely monitored and records detailed, high frequency
data for power generation, heating, and cooling at the district level, as well as consumption
in individual buildings. Due to the availability of data, the campus is uniquely suited for the
development of accurate, validated energy system models to be used for testing and opti-
mization. Reduced order models of the campus CHP system were developed and validated
independently using measurement data provided by the UT Austin utilities office. The mod-
els were then integrated into a single optimization problem with the objective function set to
minimize campus energy use, or emissions. Then, wind and solar generation were modeled
and integrated into the existing system model to be tested at increasing nominal capacities.
Later, electric battery storage systems were also included to further reduce emissions and
district heating retrofit options were considered.

From the schematic in Figure 6.43 a mass and energy balance was conducted across each

of the campus power demand, some minimum production from the CHP was still required.
However, the developed optimization model would optimally control CHP components and
reduce them to minimal loadings to maximize renewable penetration as required.

6.3.2.2 Model Validation

Next, the model was validated with real operating data to ensure accuracy. For the purposes
of validation, ER and PRR, and the two campus load constraints P_E_C and Q_S_C, were set
equal to real values extracted from the campus data system for two 24-hour periods. Then,
the calculated outputs for Q_G_GT and Q_G_SB were compared to real measured values

 165

to determine the model’s accuracy. The system model was validated using hourly data from
the two days November 18, 2022, and March 13, 2023. In addition to validating the system
it was useful to then use an optimization function within MATLAB to minimize the natural
gas burned (minimizing energy use and emissions) during these two test days and determine
if the campus system was operated in an optimal fashion. For this calculation, ER and PRR
were no longer set to real values, but were optimally determined by the optimization algo-
rithm to minimize natural gas use, while delivering the required steam and electrical demand
to campus. The CHP model had a CVRMSE of 2.7% and 3.4% and an MBE of 1.2% and
3.4% respectively for each day. The optimization results showed that 3-3.5% savings were
available from optimizing the performance of the CHP components. The baseline annual
energy use was 1,093,000 MWh in natural gas.

6.3.2.3 Integration of Renewables and Storage Systems

Typical meteorological year data for both wind speed and solar irradiance in west Texas was
collected from the NRL National Solar Radiation Data Base (NSRDB) in hourly increments.
For the purposes of the solar generation model, it was assumed that the panels were tilted at
an angle of 20° and were 18% efficient. The total irradiance to the tilted PV panels (W/m²)
was calculated based on measured diffuse horizontal radiation (DHR) and direct normal
irradiation (DNI) for the location and solar angles.

For simulations, a year of hourly data for was normalized, with the maximum irradiance seen
during the year set to 1. All other hourly values throughout the year then ranged from 0 to 1.
It was then assumed that a nominal installation of 1 MW of solar in m² would produce 1 MW
of electricity during an hour when irradiance was equal to 1, and would produce a fraction
of the 1 MW nominal capacity ranging from 0 to 1 for all other hours. Later, testing was
conducted to increase the nominal installations of solar generation capacity in increments of
10 MW.

Similar modeling was conducted for wind generation. The turbine used for simulation was
the Goldwind 140-3.4MW PMDD smart wind turbine. The tower of the turbine was 100m

 166

high, it had a rated power of 3.4MW, a cut in wind speed of 2.5 m/s (speed required to gen-
erate power) and a cut out wind speed of 20 m/s (maximum speed before shutdown). Below
the cut-in speed of 2.5 m/s, and above the cut-out speed of 25 m/s, no power is generated.
Simulations testing for the integration of wind power into the campus optimization problem
was conducted in increments of 2 wind turbines up to 60 wind turbines, or approximately
6.8 nominal MW to 204 nominal MW. Similar to the solar calculation, the output from one
wind turbine was multiplied by the number of wind turbines used in any given simulation.

Solar and wind generation were tested separately, and were input into the model during
an annual simulation to determine how increasing nominal installations of both wind and
solar could reduce campus energy and emissions use. After initial simulations, additional
simulations were conducted with electric battery storage to increase renewable penetration
and further reduce campus energy use.

6.3.2.5 Proposed Conversion of Steam District Heating to Hot Water

The UT campus’ aging steam district heating network is nearing the end of its lifetime, and
a conversion of the existing steam distribution system to hot water is under consideration. In
recent years, other universities with similar campus steam district heating systems have made
substantial investments to replace them with hot water heating distribution systems. This
occurred at Stanford University, Cornell University, Dartmouth College, and the University
of British Columbia and others. Converting high temperature steam distribution systems can
result in significant energy savings from heat loss, significantly lower maintenance costs,

 170

integration with renewable heating sources, and the use of hot water TES to better satisfy
CHP loads by decoupling heating and electrical demand. Additionally, despite being located
in a hotter climate, there are still several months of the year where significant energy is
required for heating the entire UT campus, and these loads generally peak overnight, when
electrical demand is at a minimum. It is possible that a new hot water TES in a new hot water
district heating system could provide worthwhile fuel and emissions savings to the campus
CHP by decoupling heating and electrical demand, improving CHP efficiency, and reducing
supplementary steam boiler use.

The integrated campus optimization model was modified to include new constraints for a
proposed hot water storage system. It was assumed that CHP components would continue to
be used to produce steam, but instead of sending the high pressure high temperature steam
to campus for heating, a steam to hot water heat exchanger at the central plant would be
used to generate hot water from the CHP (a strategy that has been used for other campus
conversions). The hot water system would operate in parallel with a hot water storage tank
to meet the campus’ heating needs. The proposed system retrofit offers source energy re-
ductions from (i) significantly reduced energy losses from a district hot water distribution
system compared to high pressure high temperature steam and (ii) decoupling of electrical
and thermal loads and reduction in CHP steam boiler sizing from optimally controlled hot
water TES.

A conversion of the steam district heating system to hot water at Stanford reduced energy
losses by more than 20% [60]. In another campus project converting a steam district heating
system to hot water, engineers claimed that the amount of energy lost in steam distribution
is 15-20%, compared to 3-5% for hot water systems. The operational experience of the
engineers demonstrated that a retrofit of steam based systems to hot water offered efficiency
improvements ranging from 25 to 40% [61]. Similar work at Cornell replacing steam district
heating with hot water heating estimated that the steam network resulted in a 20% loss of
efficiency, compared to a 5% loss in efficiency when delivering heating with a hot water
system [62]. The previously referenced projects involving the retrofitting of district steam
heating systems are consistent in stating that a new hot water district heating system will

 171

improve efficiency by at least 15%. In modeling the proposed hot water district heating
system operating alongside the existing CHP plant, it was assumed efficiency would improve
15% due to reductions in losses. Additionally, simulations were conducted modeling the
proposed system with a hot water TES to decouple thermal and electrical loads.

Annual simulations were conducted with new constraints for optimal operation of a 200
MWh hot water TES. It was found that the hot water TES significantly reduced use of the
CHP steam boiler. Figure 6.46 displays daily results for the steam boiler during a single
winter month of testing. In Figure 6.46A daily savings in SB fuel use while using hot water
TES were compared to the baseline optimal system operation without hot water TES. In
Figure 6.46B the daily number of hours the SB spent at its minimum loading is displayed.
In several days of the month in Figure 6.46A SB fuel savings was reduced by 25% or more.
In Figure 6.46B there were only several days of the month when the SB was not at minimum
loading for more than four hours when using TES.

Figure 6.46: UT Austin CHP Steam Boiler Savings and Operation Reduction Due to Pro-

As mentioned in the methodology section, at UT all CHP system components maintain oper-
ation at all times to ensure reliability. Components only ramp down to an allowed minimum
of approximately 20%. During hours where the SB is not needed, it prevents the CHP system
from operating at maximum efficiency and can result in significant fuel costs as it idles at

 172

its minimum loading of 20%. While eliminating the SB is not an option as its operation is
needed to ensure reliability, it is apparent that the addition of the hot water TES deemed the
high-capacity SB somewhat redundant. If the SB were replaced with one of a lower capac-
ity, its operating hours spent at minimum loading would require less fuel use. In this case
additional savings would be realized, not directly from the optimal shifting of loads, but by
the TES enabling a successful system retrofit that improves efficiency.

To evaluate the impact of a hot water TES combined with a smaller SB, additional simu-
lations were conducted gradually reducing the capacity of the SB. It was found that a new
SB with as low as 50% of the original capacity would still result in all loads being met by
the CHP system during the course of the year tested. The combination of reducing the CHP
SB capacity by 50% (thereby reducing the amount of energy it consumed during periods of
idling) and optimally controlling the hot water TES, led to a source energy reduction of 2.7%
annually. Overall, the optimal control of CHP components and chilled and hot water storage
systems, along with a district heating system retrofit and steam boiler replacement, were able
to reduce campus energy use from 1,093,000 MWh to 834,000 MWh, or 23.7%. Combined
with the 1.6% savings from the chilled water system optimal condenser flow, total savings
were 25.3%.

6.4.2.1 UT Microgrid Resilience

The UT campus system as it exists can operate indefinitely without any assistance from the
utility grid as it continuously operates in an islanded status. The UT system operated without
interruption when the larger state grid system experienced an outage during winter storm
Uri. However, the campus continues to expand and extreme heat has resulted in increasing
cooling loads. Improved efficiency and reduced electrical peaks in the islanded system can
further improve reliability ensuring continuous standalone operation in the future.

Optimization results demonstrated that improved TES control could be used to provide af-
ternoon peak shaving during the summer months, thereby eliminating the use of less ef-
ficient peaking steam turbines and further reducing energy used on campus. When loads
approach 60 MW or above, additional supplemental steam turbines can be required to meet
demand. The overall system efficiency decreases as insufficient waste heat is available to
provide steam to the steam turbines, and more fuel is consumed in steam boilers to feed
them. Reducing demand and shifting additional chiller operation to overnight in more favor-

 177

able operating conditions can provide daily electrical peak reductions of 5-10%. The older,
less efficient peaking turbines are also less reliable, and eliminating their use by reducing
electrical peaks will allow the campus islanded microgrid system to continue operating in-
definitely while disconnected from the surrounding utility grid. Additionally, in testing with
batteries, a battery size of 200 MWh was able to increase renewable penetration and pro-
vide adequate power to the campus for several hours during a potential loss of generation,
increasing resiliency.

The better management of peak loads and improved efficiency enables the islanded microgrid
system to operate continuously using the same CHP components and microgrid infrastruc-
ture despite campus expansion and increasing loads. The 5-10% reduction in peak loads and
hours of operation available from battery systems ensures that campus electrical service will
not be interrupted due to peak cooling loads from extreme summer heat in the future.

 PROJECT OUTCOMES AND FUTURE WORK

7.1 Summary and Conclusions

This project successfully developed and demonstrated a unified, open-source framework for
the optimal co-design of Grid-interactive Efficient District (GED) energy systems. The
project addressed the critical challenge of coordinating distributed energy resources with
district-scale heating and cooling networks. The resulting ecosystem of tools bridges the gap
between traditional power system analysis and building energy modeling, enabling engineers
to design systems that are not only energy-efficient but also resilient and responsive to grid
dynamics.

A foundational achievement of this work was the advancement of high-fidelity microgrid
modeling capabilities. The project delivered a suite of "second-generation" renewable energy
source models, including photovoltaics, wind turbines, and battery energy storage systems,
all compliant with WECC specifications. These models were rigorously validated against
industry-standard tools and hardware-in-the-loop experiments to ensure they accurately cap-
ture the rapid dynamics of modern inverter-based grids. To complement this, physics-based
models for induction machines, pumps, chillers, and CHP units were developed to capture
multi-physics interactions often ignored in conventional software. Innovations such as the
"Computationally Efficient and Accurate Induction Machine" model and simplified CHP
formulations demonstrated computational speed-ups of over 100 times compared to existing
libraries while maintaining high accuracy, making annual simulations of complex districts
computationally feasible.

Building on this modeling foundation, the project implemented advanced optimization and

 180

control frameworks to enhance system resilience. A Model Predictive Control approach was
developed to manage critical microgrid operations, including optimal islanded operation and
optimal re-synchronization. This single-controller architecture successfully coordinated di-
verse distributed energy resources to maintain voltage and frequency stability during grid
disturbances. Through this intelligent control, the optimization algorithm was shown to bet-
ter utilize all microgrid resources, such as allowing the battery to charge from photovoltaic
generators during a disconnection event, which effectively extended the battery’s power sup-
ply from roughly 1.8 hours to nearly 4 hours.

To ensure the project’s advanced modeling capabilities are accessible to practitioners, the mi-
crogrid models were successfully integrated into the URBANopt platform via the GeoJSON-
to-Modelica Translator (GMT). This automated workflow allows users to seamlessly trans-
late GIS-based district data into simulatable Modelica models, significantly streamlining the
design process.The research team achieved this by expanding the standard GeoJSON format
to capture essential electrical network parameters alongside traditional district thermal data.
Using a newly developed web-based graphical interface, engineers can easily map distributed
energy resources—such as photovoltaics, wind turbines, and batteries—directly onto a dis-
trict layout. The GMT then utilizes a template-based approach to automatically generate
the complex, coupled thermal-electrical models. This toolchain proved highly effective and
scalable, successfully automating the creation of the comprehensive models used to evaluate
both the CU Boulder and UT Austin testbeds.

The practical impact of this framework was validated through extensive demonstrations at
the University of Colorado Boulder and the University of Texas at Austin. At the CU Boul-
der testbed, optimizing condenser water setpoints yielded cooling energy savings between
11% and 16%, while pressure setpoint reductions in the steam network resulted in a 4.66%
reduction in fuel consumption. When combined with multi-plant coordination and waste
heat recovery strategies, the analysis demonstrated a potential total system efficiency im-
provement of approximately 27.8%. Similarly, at the UT Austin testbed, the integration of
significant wind generation with thermal and battery energy storage was shown to reduce
the campus carbon intensity by 42. Furthermore, resiliency was a critical focus at the UT

 181

Austin campus, which operates an islanded microgrid system capable of running indefinitely
without the utility grid—a capability proven when the system operated without interruption
during Winter Storm Uri. However, as campus loads approach 60 MW during extreme heat
events, the system typically requires less efficient and less reliable supplemental peaking
steam turbines. By using optimal control of thermal energy storage to reduce peak electrical
demand by 5% to 10%, the campus can eliminate reliance on these peaking turbines. Addi-
tionally, testing demonstrated that a 200 MWh battery system can further increase renewable
penetration and provide adequate power to the campus for several hours during a potential
loss of generation. Together, these strategies ensure that electrical service will not be in-
terrupted and secure continuous standalone operation as the campus expands. Finally, the
conversion of the steam district heating system to hot water, coupled with optimal storage
control, also identified a pathway to reduce source energy use by 23.7%.

Collectively, these outcomes confirm that integrated thermal-electrical modeling is essential
for unlocking deep efficiency and resilience in district energy systems. The open-source tools
and methodologies established by this project provide a robust foundation for the widespread
deployment of next-generation, grid-interactive communities that can meet the dual chal-
lenges of decarbonization and energy security.

7.3 Commercialization and Outreach

Commercialization and outreach efforts during the project focused on preparing the GED
platform for real-world adoption while engaging organizations and stakeholders who would
benefit most from advanced district energy modeling. A comprehensive commercialization
plan was developed to define the market opportunity, including the relevance of GED for
universities, hospitals, municipalities, engineering firms, and developers. The plan identified
key market drivers, including rising energy costs, new regulatory requirements, and the grow-
ing adoption of digital twin technologies. They clearly outlined the value proposition of GED
for system operators, designers, and developers. It also highlighted the platform’s holis-
tic thermal-electric modeling capabilities, dynamic digital-twin features, and user-friendly
Modelica templates as the main differentiators in the district energy market.

As part of the outreach effort, the project publicly shared the district energy and microgrid
modeling tools developed under this initiative. Multiple open-source releases—across the
URBANopt-Modelica workflow, the Modelica Buildings Library, and the OpenIPSL library
were made available to broaden access to the modeling framework. These releases enabled
researchers, practitioners, and community partners to explore the models, evaluate their ca-
pabilities, and apply them to district-energy planning and analysis.

Industry engagement was an essential component of the commercialization pathway. The
team met with representatives from the Emergent Group/Urban Concepts to discuss the
growing interest in community-grade modeling and simulation tools. Their feedback high-
lighted the value of a holistic district-level modeling platform that can support rough-order-
of-magnitude cost estimates for community thermal and electrical systems. These conversa-
tions helped refine the platform’s value proposition and identified several potential next steps,
including developing a pitch deck for funders, selecting a commercial pilot to demonstrate
the platform, and exploring partnerships with local utilities.

The project also maintained strong stakeholder engagement through multiple Technical Ad-
visory Group meetings. These meetings provided opportunities to share progress and collect

 184

feedback from operators, consultants, and engineering firms. This input helped ensure that
the GED platform aligned with real-world district energy needs. Additional outreach through
industry associations helped raise awareness and connect the project with practitioners and
potential adopters.
