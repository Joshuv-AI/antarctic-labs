Delayed Salvage Radiation Therapy Guided by Contrast-Enhanced Local and Whole-Body Diffusion-Weighted Magnetic Resonance Imaging During Prostate-Specific-Antigen-Based Surveillance After Radical Prostatectomy: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Tadashi Matsumoto, Shoji Morishita, Tomohiko Matsuyama, Kazuhiro Katahira, Yasuyuki Hamada, Kazumasa Hara, Shinsuke Hamada, Chikako Awazu, Takahiro Otsuka, Shigeo Yamada, Hirohito Otsuka, Takahiro Watakabe, Yoshiyuki Fukugawa, Natsuo Oya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeBiochemical recurrence (BCR) after radical prostatectomy (RP) is common. Although early prostate-specific-antigen (PSA)–triggered salvage radiation therapy (SRT) improves outcomes, PSA elevation alone cannot reliably distinguish local recurrence from occult metastatic disease, leading to overtreatment. This study retrospectively evaluated long-term outcomes of a PSA-based surveillance strategy, wherein delayed SRT was guided by contrast-enhanced local and whole-body diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and initiated only after radiologic confirmation of macroscopic local recurrence (MLR).Methods and MaterialsBetween January 2015 and June 2023, 226 patients with MRI-detected MLR in the prostate bed were identified during PSA-based surveillance incorporating serial PSA measurements and repeat contrast-enhanced pelvic MRI combined with whole-body diffusion-weighted imaging after RP. SRT was intentionally deferred and initiated only after imaging-confirmed local recurrence with exclusion of distant metastases. Ninety patients who underwent SRT and had ≥6 months of follow-up were included in the final analysis. Radiation therapy was delivered at a total dose of 64 to 68 Gy. The primary endpoint was BCR-free survival. Secondary endpoints included local-recurrence-free survival, distant-metastasis–free survival, overall survival, patterns of failure, and late toxicity.ResultsThe median follow-up was 67 months, and the median PSA level at SRT initiation was 0.98 ng/mL. The estimated 5-year BCR-free survival and local-recurrence-free survival rates were 84.8% and 91.3%, respectively. The 5-year distant metastasis–free survival and overall survival rates were 94.1% and 97.3%, respectively. Disease recurrence occurred in 14 patients (15.6%), predominantly as local recurrence after SRT. Two patients exhibited late grade ≥3 gastrointestinal toxicity. However, no grade ≥3 genitourinary toxicity was reported.ConclusionsThe PSA-based surveillance strategy, incorporating serial MRI, facilitated the detection of localized recurrence and allowed delayed initiation of SRT in patients with imaging-confirmed MLR following RP. This approach achieved durable disease control with acceptable toxicity in the studied cohort. However, the broader clinical utility of this imaging-based strategy warrants further prospective evaluation.

Introduction

Despite advances in surgical techniques and perioperative management, biochemical recurrence (BCR) occurs in approximately 20% to 30% of patients within 10 years after radical prostatectomy (RP).1 Early salvage radiation therapy (SRT) at low prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels has been widely recommended for patients with BCR and is associated with improved oncologic outcomes.2 Consequently, PSA-triggered early SRT is commonly adopted in clinical practice for postprostatectomy recurrence.

However, prospective trials, including GETUG-AFU 16, have not consistently demonstrated a definitive overall survival (OS) benefit of early SRT,3 and early intervention has also been reported to increase the risk of adverse functional outcomes.4 One potential explanation for these findings is that PSA elevation alone does not reliably distinguish isolated local recurrence from occult extraprostatic disease. Consequently, SRT initiation based solely on increasing PSA levels may lead to the delivery of local therapy in patients who already harbor distant metastases, potentially resulting in overtreatment and limited oncologic benefit.

This limitation has been highlighted by the introduction of prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography (PSMA PET), demonstrating that a clinically meaningful proportion of patients with BCR have metastatic disease at the time of PSA recurrence. According to PSMA PET–based studies, approximately 12% to 37% of patients with BCR after RP have distant metastases.5,6 However, despite its diagnostic advantages, access to PSMA PET remains variable across institutions and geographic regions, limiting its universal implementation.7

By contrast, multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is widely available and routinely incorporated into posttreatment evaluation and surveillance following RP.8 According to previous studies, MRI, particularly when combined with whole-body diffusion-weighted imaging (WBDWI), demonstrates high diagnostic performance for detecting local recurrence and clinically relevant metastatic disease, with performance approaching that of PSMA PET in selected settings.9 Moreover, MRI can be longitudinally repeated during PSA-based surveillance with relative ease.

Against this background, the authors’ institution has implemented a PSA-based surveillance approach incorporating repeat MRI, wherein SRT is deferred until macroscopic local recurrence (MLR) is radiologically confirmed and distant metastatic disease is excluded. The present study aimed to evaluate the oncologic outcomes of SRT delivered to patients with MRI-confirmed MLR identified within this surveillance framework.

Methods and Materials

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective single-institution observational study evaluated patients who developed BCR after RP between January 2015 and June 2023. These patients were monitored with serial PSA measurements and repeat MRI. During PSA-based surveillance incorporating serial MRI, MLR was eventually detected in 226 patients without evidence of distant metastases. The patient selection process is summarized in Figure 1. Of these, 133 patients underwent intentionally delayed SRT initiated after MRI confirmation of local recurrence. The remaining patients did not receive SRT: 69 were treated with salvage androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), 21 received treatment at outside institutions, and 3 were managed with observation alone. Detailed treatment information for patients treated externally was unavailable. Among the 133 patients who underwent SRT, 90 with a minimum follow-up of 6 months were included in the final analysis, whereas 43 were excluded because of insufficient follow-up (<6 months).

Clinical, pathologic, imaging, and treatment-related data were retrospectively collected in collaboration with a partner institution. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of Kumamoto Chuo Hospital (approval number: 202509-4), which waived the requirement for individual participant consent because of the retrospective study design.

MRI timing and imaging protocol

Contrast-enhanced pelvic MRI combined with WBDWI was performed following BCR, which was defined as a PSA level of ≥0.2 ng/mL confirmed by a second consecutive measurement, or PSA re-elevation without prior decline to <0.2 ng/mL. In patients without evidence of MLR on initial imaging, PSA levels were measured every 3 months, and follow-up MRI was performed either at PSA progression or 1 year after initial MRI, whichever occurred first, at the discretion of the treating physicians.

The MRI protocol included the following: T2-weighted turbo spin-echo imaging, T2-weighted volume turbo spin-echo imaging, diffusion-weighted imaging, including a high b-value (b = 2000 seconds/mm²), dynamic contrast-enhanced imaging, and 3-dimensional contrast-enhanced sequences. Local recurrence was defined as a lesion exhibiting low signal intensity on T2-weighted images, restricted diffusion, and early contrast enhancement.

WBDWI was acquired from the skull base to the mid-thigh using a multistation free-breathing technique, with b-values of 0 and 1000 seconds/mm². Apparent diffusion coefficient maps were generated automatically. Distant metastases were defined as focal lesions exhibiting high signal intensity on high-b-value images with corresponding low apparent diffusion coefficient values, supported by findings on T1- and T2-weighted images and/or chemical shift imaging.

Experienced radiologists interpreted all imaging studies in conjunction with radiation oncologists.

Radiation therapy

All patients were treated with 3-dimensional conformal radiation therapy (3D-CRT) using a 5-field technique. The prescribed dose was 64 Gy in 32 fractions in 14 patients and 68 Gy in 34 fractions in 76 patients.

Definitions

Consistent with the definition adopted in the GETUG-AFU 16 trial, biochemical progression after SRT was defined as a PSA increase of ≥0.5 ng/mL above the posttreatment PSA nadir, confirmed by a subsequent PSA measurement.3 BCR-free survival (bRFS) was defined as the interval from SRT initiation to biochemical progression or last follow-up. Local recurrence–free survival (lRFS) was defined as the interval from SRT initiation to imaging-confirmed local recurrence within the prostate bed or last follow-up. lRFS included pelvic nodal recurrence only when the involved nodes were within the irradiated field. Distant metastasis–free survival (DMFS) was defined as the interval from SRT initiation to the imaging-based diagnosis of distant metastasis or last follow-up. OS was defined as the interval from SRT initiation to death from any cause or last follow-up.

The patterns of failure were classified as isolated biochemical, local (prostate bed), regional (pelvic lymph nodes), or distant recurrence based on follow-up imaging. Late grade ≥3 gastrointestinal (GI) and genitourinary (GU) toxicities were assessed according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0.

ADT

The use of ADT before or at the time of SRT was heterogeneous and was determined at the discretion of treating physicians. Concurrent ADT was defined as ADT administered during the course of radiation therapy, irrespective of whether ADT had been initiated prior to SRT. Consequently, 50 patients (55.6%) received peri-SRT ADT, whereas 40 patients (44.4%) did not receive ADT. Among these 50 patients, 23 received concurrent ADT, whereas 27 received nonconcurrent ADT. By contrast, ADT was uniformly initiated in all patients after secondary disease progression following SRT in accordance with institutional policy.

Endpoints and follow-up

The primary endpoint of this study was bRFS. Meanwhile, secondary endpoints included lRFS, DMFS, OS, patterns of failure, and late toxicity. The median follow-up duration was 67 months.

Statistical analysis

The Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate bRFS, lRFS, DMFS, and OS. Univariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to explore potential clinicopathological predictors of bRFS, lRFS, and DMFS, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs were estimated. Concurrent ADT, rather than ADT use itself, was included in the univariable analyses because ADT was uniformly initiated in all patients after secondary disease progression according to institutional policy and, therefore, did not represent an independent baseline variable. Considering the limited number of events, multivariable analyses were not performed to avoid overfitting of the model and unstable estimates.

All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Easy R; developed by Yoshinobu Kanda, Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R, which is a modified version of R Commander.10 Statistical significance was considered at a 2-sided P value < .05.

Results

Patient characteristics

Ninety patients were included in the analysis. The median follow-up duration was 67 months (IQR, 53-88 months). The median age at SRT initiation was 73.5 years (IQR, 69.0-78.0 years).

The median PSA levels at RP and SRT initiation were 8.69 ng/mL (IQR, 5.66-13.15 ng/mL) and 0.98 ng/mL (IQR, 0.50-1.39 ng/mL), respectively.

Oncologic outcomes

The estimated bRFS rate was 84.8% at 5 years with a median follow-up duration of 67 months. The corresponding lRFS rate was 91.3% at 5 years. The DMFS and OS rates were 94.1% and 97.3% at 5 years, respectively (Fig. 2), with only 1 prostate-cancer-related death observed.

Late toxicity

Two patients exhibited late grade ≥ 3 GI toxicity, both developing rectal bleeding requiring argon plasma coagulation and blood transfusion. However, no grade ≥3 GU toxicity was observed during the follow-up period.

Discussion

This retrospective single-institution study evaluated the long-term oncologic outcomes of SRT delivered for MRI-confirmed MLR detected during PSA-based surveillance after RP. The observed outcomes (eg, favorable biochemical control, low incidence of distant metastasis, and acceptable late toxicity profile) suggest that deferring SRT until radiologic confirmation of local recurrence may be a feasible management strategy in carefully selected patients.

Currently, PSA-triggered early SRT at low PSA levels is regarded as an effective standard management strategy for BCR after RP. This approach is supported by randomized trials, including RADICALS-RT, RAVES, and GETUG-AFU 17,11, 12, 13 which have reported 5-year biochemical control rates of approximately 87% to 90% in the salvage arms and form the basis of current guideline recommendations favoring early intervention.

However, despite the established efficacy of early intervention, the oncologic outcomes of SRT have generally been inferior to those observed in patients treated for BCR alone once MLR is present. Dirix et al14 reported a 2-year biochemical progression–free survival rate of 63% in patients with macroscopic recurrence compared with 81% in those without visible disease, highlighting the adverse prognostic impact of macroscopic local failure.

Compared with these observations, PSMA PET–guided approaches have shown favorable biochemical outcomes among patients with imaging-confirmed MLR. In a representative PSMA PET–guided cohort restricted to such patients, Tamihardja et al15 reported a 3-year biochemical progression–free survival rate of 89.1%. This oncologic outcome appears more favorable than those reported for imaging-based strategies that do not incorporate PSMA PET (5-year biochemical progression–free survival rates of 44%-75%).16,17 These findings have raised concerns regarding the oncologic adequacy of delayed SRT initiated at the stage of MLR when PSMA PET is not incorporated into staging.

In this context, the oncologic outcomes observed in the present study are of interest. The present cohort achieved a 5-year bRFS rate of 84.8% despite initiating SRT at relatively higher PSA levels, in the presence of macroscopic disease, and without the use of PSMA PET. This favorable outcome is more plausibly attributable to patient selection through PSA-based surveillance combined with serial imaging, which enabled the exclusion of patients who developed metastatic progression prior to SRT, rather than reflecting superior intrinsic local treatment efficacy. In this regard, the advantage of PSMA PET–guided strategies is also thought to primarily reflect improved patient selection rather than intrinsic differences in local treatment efficacy because PSMA PET allows the sensitive detection of microscopic nodal and distant disease and facilitates the exclusion of patients with occult metastatic involvement at the time of treatment.5,6

This degree of patient selection may be attributed to 2 complementary factors and could partly explain why the oncologic outcomes observed in this cohort were comparable to those reported in PSMA-PET–guided series. One is the ability to perform serial imaging, which represents an advantage not inherently provided by single-time-point PSMA-PET-guided staging. PSMA PET is typically applied as a single-time-point cross-sectional examination to guide treatment decisions. However, longitudinal repetition is often constrained in routine clinical practice through considerations of radiation exposure, cost, and limited tracer and scanner availability.18 On the other hand, serial MRI-based imaging enables longitudinal assessment of disease dynamics guided by PSA levels, enabling MLR to be identified at a more treatable stage of disease evolution and facilitating timely initiation of SRT before extensive local progression occurs.

The second factor is the diagnostic complementarity of contrast-enhanced multiparametric MRI and WBDWI. Contrast-enhanced MRI demonstrates high sensitivity for small-volume local recurrence within the prostate bed, potentially exceeding that of PSMA PET in selected settings.19 Although PSMA PET generally offers superior sensitivity for small-volume nodal disease, equivocal nodal findings on whole-body MRI can often be clarified through longitudinal assessment, which is consistent with the surveillance-based strategy employed in the present study. Regarding distant metastases, previous prospective data suggest a broadly comparable diagnostic performance between PSMA PET and whole-body MRI, with possible advantages of MRI for detecting early bone marrow involvement.20

Taken together, PSA-based surveillance combined with serial imaging, along with the diagnostic complementarity of local and whole-body MRI, may partly explain why the oncologic outcomes observed in this cohort were comparable to those reported in PSMA PET–guided series, despite the absence of routine PSMA PET imaging.

The observed patterns of failure lend further support to this interpretation. In the present study, local control was comparable to that reported in a previous series evaluating SRT for MLR after RP. In the Prostatectomy Bed Image-guided Dose-escalated Salvage Radiotherapy study by Benziane et al,16 13 out of 363 patients experienced in-field local relapse after SRT for MLR, corresponding to a 5-year local progression–free survival rate of 89%. In their study, radiation dose escalation was intentionally implemented, and escalation to a total dose of ≥72 Gy was associated with improved progression-free survival. However, the benefit of radiation dose escalation remains controversial as other studies—including prospective analyses in patients treated for BCR—have suggested that the dose–response relationship for postprostatectomy SRT may plateau at approximately 70 Gy, with no consistent additional benefit observed beyond this threshold.21,22 In the present cohort, all patients with MLR after RP were treated with a total dose of 68 Gy or less. However, durable local control was achieved. This finding suggests that earlier MRI-guided therapeutic intervention may mitigate the need for routine dose escalation in appropriately selected patients.

Regarding distant metastatic recurrence, PSMA PET–guided SRT has been associated with excellent metastasis-free outcomes, with 3-year metastasis-free survival rates exceeding 95%. By contrast, earlier MRI-guided series have reported lower long-term metastasis-free survival. For example, Lee et al23 reported a 7-year DMFS rate of 83.6%. In this study, the DMFS rate was more closely aligned with those reported in PSMA PET–guided cohorts than with previously published MRI-based series, which may support the proposed treatment paradigm. OS remained high, with a 5-year OS rate of 97.3%. Although the limited number of death events and the retrospective design preclude definitive conclusions regarding survival benefit, no adverse impact on OS was observed in this cohort.

According to exploratory univariable analyses, advanced pathologic T stage (>T2c) was significantly associated with an increased risk of local recurrence. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution and regarded as hypothesis-generating considering the limited number of events. In the present study, concurrent ADT was not associated with improved bRFS, lRFS, or DMFS. In contrast, the GETUG-AFU 16 trial demonstrated that short-term ADT combined with prostate bed radiation therapy (66 Gy) improved progression-free survival, biochemical control, and metastasis-free survival in patients with biochemical recurrence after radical prostatectomy and PSA levels ranging from 0.2 to <2.0 μg/L.3 This discrepancy may be partly attributable to the heterogeneous, nonprotocolized administration of ADT in the present cohort, in contrast to the standardized concurrent ADT protocol used in the GETUG-AFU 16 trial.

Delayed SRT was also associated with an acceptable late toxicity profile, with grade ≥ 3 GI toxicity observed in only a small proportion of patients and no grade ≥ 3 GU toxicity. However, these toxicity outcomes may not be directly generalizable to contemporary intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT)–based practice, as all patients in the present study were treated using a 5-field 3D-CRT technique. Although IMRT has been reported to reduce radiation exposure to surrounding organs at risk and decrease GI and GU toxicities in the postprostatectomy setting, no consistent improvement in oncological outcomes, including biochemical control, has been demonstrated compared with 3D-CRT,24,25 and this should be considered when interpreting the present findings.

This study has several important limitations. First, the retrospective single-institution design introduces an inherent selection bias. The favorable oncological outcomes observed in this study may be attributable to the PSA-based surveillance strategy implemented before the identification of the 226 patients with MLR, potentially enriching the cohort for patients with more indolent disease biology. However, because data for the entire cohort of patients with biochemical recurrence after RP were not available, the effectiveness of this treatment strategy could not be validated against an early treatment intervention approach. Second, the heterogeneous use of ADT is a significant confounding factor. Peri-SRT ADT was administered at the discretion of the treating physicians, and its timing and duration were not standardized. This variability may have influenced the outcomes of biochemical control. Third, the limited number of events precluded the use of multivariable analyses. Finally, all patients were treated using 3D-CRT, which may limit the generalizability of these findings to contemporary IMRT-based practice. Accordingly, the generalizability of these findings to broader postprostatectomy populations should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

The PSA-based surveillance strategy incorporating contrast-enhanced local and whole-body diffusion-weighted MRI enabled the identification of localized recurrence and the delivery of delayed MRI-guided SRT in selected patients with imaging-confirmed MLR after RP, with encouraging long-term oncological outcomes and acceptable late toxicity. However, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and require prospective validation because of the retrospective design and lack of comparative data.
