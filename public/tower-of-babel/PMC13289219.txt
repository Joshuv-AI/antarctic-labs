What factors influence enrolment in Nepal’s National Health Insurance Program? A community-based cross-sectional study using the Health Belief Model
Authors: Anil Paudel, Richa Acharya, Pratik Khanal, Aaisha Pandey, Naveen Shrestha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDespite government efforts to improve healthcare access through the National Health Insurance Program (NHIP) in Nepal, enrolment rates remain suboptimal. Understanding the behavioural, psychological and social drivers of enrolment is necessary to inform targeted intervention. This study assessed factors influencing NHIP enrolment using the Health Belief Model (HBM).MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 360 randomly selected households in Panini Rural Municipality of Arghakhanchi district from July to August 2022. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using an HBM-guided semi-structured questionnaire via KoboToolbox. Logistic regression analysis was performed using SPSS 22 to identify facilitators and barriers.ResultsPerceived susceptibility and severity of illness significantly influenced enrolment. Households perceiving poor family health had higher odds of enrolment (AOR 4.06, 95% CI 1.00 to 16.41), as did those with recent illness experience (AOR 2.51, 95% CI 1.09 to 5.75). Good knowledge about NHIP strongly predicted enrolment (AOR 6.61, 95% CI 3.09 to 14.13), along with perceived affordability of premium payments (AOR 4.51, 95% CI 1.12 to 18.19). Social cues were powerful motivators; peer enrolment (AOR 4.00, 95% CI 1.86 to 8.60), neighbour encouragement (AOR 7.95, 95% CI 3.63 to 17.39) and family agreement (AOR 4.72, 95% CI 1.56 to 14.34) markedly increased the likelihood of enrolment. Perceived benefits, however, were not significant predictors. Among the 180 non-enrolled households, common barriers included inappropriate premium collection timing (53.9%), belief that insurance was unnecessary (43.9%), limited service points (29.4%) and referral card requirements (19.4%).ConclusionsThese findings emphasise the need for targeted interventions that improve awareness by leveraging social networks. Affordability and referral card requirements were reported as barriers to enrolment underscoring a need for further exploration of flexible payment mechanisms and referral processes.

Background

Globally, about 11.7% of the population incurs catastrophic healthcare costs, and nearly 100 million people are pushed into poverty each year due to out-of-pocket (OOP) health spending.1 2 A significant majority—over 90%—of these catastrophic healthcare events occur in resource-limited settings.3 Asia is particularly affected, being the region where the highest number of individuals experiencing catastrophic payments reside.4 In low-income and middle-income countries like Nepal, where the healthcare system has long suffered from underfunding, a significant portion of health financing relies on OOP payments. This heavy dependence on OOP spending often limits access to essential healthcare services and exposes households to financial hardship or impoverishment due to catastrophic health expenditures.5 According to the Global Monitoring Report on Financial Protection 2021, approximately 10% of households in Nepal incurred catastrophic health spending, underscoring the urgent need for stronger financial protection mechanisms.6

Health insurance is recognised as a sustainable financial strategy for healthcare system reform, as it reduces financial barriers and promotes greater participation in decision-making among enrolled members.7 The Government of Nepal introduced the National Health Insurance Program (NHIP) as a pilot programme in Kailali, Baglung and Ilam districts in 2016. The programme has now been scaled up to cover all 77 districts of Nepal,8 in alignment with the constitutional mandate that recognises health as a fundamental right and ensures that no one is denied access to essential healthcare services.9 Additionally, the Government of Nepal remains committed to achieving Universal Health Coverage, one of the targets of Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 3.8).10

In Nepal, the current provision (during the time of manuscript preparation, exchange rate was 1 US$=NRs. 132.57) for health insurance requires an annual premium of NRs. 3500 ($26.40) for enrolment, which provides coverage up to NRs. 100 000 ($754). For each additional family member, an extra premium of NRs. 700 ($5.30) is required, providing additional coverage of NRs. 20 000 ($150.80), with a maximum coverage ceiling of NRs. 200 000 ($1508.30). The government provides full subsidy to ultra-poor households, senior citizens aged 70 and above, and households having individuals with severe disabilities, and patients suffering from specific chronic conditions, including leprosy, multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) and HIV/AIDS. Female community health volunteers (FCHVs) receive 50% subsidy.11 12

However, challenges persist with low coverage and high dropout. According to the 2022 Nepal Demographic Health Survey, only 10% of the population aged 15–49 is enrolled in the NHIP.13 Since this age group is generally more educated, aware, independent and socially and economically active compared with other age groups, it is likely that the overall health insurance enrolment rate among the general population is even lower than 10%.14 The dropout rate of the NHIP varies by districts, ranging from 15% to 96%.15 The reasons, as identified by existing studies, include lack of awareness about NHIP scheme, high premium rate, inappropriate timing of enrolment, difficult process of accessing services, limited service point, poor quality of healthcare services, unavailability of drugs and rude behaviour of health service providers.712 1520

Despite the nationwide expansion of the NHIP, the Government of Nepal has not achieved the expected level of coverage. To address this gap, it is crucial to generate evidence on the key facilitators and barriers influencing enrolment and to design appropriate interventions based on these insights. In Nepal, limited studies have explored the psychological, behavioural and social factors affecting enrolment in health insurance. Very few published studies used HBM model and those used were more concentrated on perceived severity and susceptibility.21 This study used all components of HBM, originally developed by Hochbaum in 1958, as a guiding framework. The core premise of HBM is that individuals are more likely to engage in preventive health behaviours if they perceive themselves to be at risk of illness (perceived susceptibility), believe the consequences of the illness would be severe (perceived severity), trust that the preventive action will be beneficial (perceived benefits) and view the action as affordable and feasible (perceived barriers).22 While other behavioural theories such as the Theory of Planned Behavior or Social Cognitive Theory could also be relevant, HBM was selected because of its particular emphasis on perceived barriers and cues to action—constructs that closely align with the objective of this study. In Nepal’s context, it is increasingly important to complement structural interventions with behavioural approaches that consider psychological and cognitive drivers of health behaviour. Therefore, this study uses HBM to explore behavioural and psychological dimensions of health insurance enrolment, with a particular focus on whether perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, barriers and cues to action influence individuals’ attitudes and decisions to enrol in the NHIP.

Methods

Study design and setting

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from 12 July 2022 to 5 August 2022 in Panini Rural Municipality of Arghakhanchi district of Nepal. Arghakhanchi, one of the hilly districts of Lumbini province, covers an area of 1193 km² and has a population of 177 200 according to the national census of 2021.23 The district is administratively divided into six local governments, comprising three municipalities and three rural municipalities. Most previous studies examining enrolment in Nepal’s NHIP have been conducted in urban or semi-urban municipalities, whereas evidence from rural settings remains limited. Therefore, Panini Rural Municipality was purposively selected considering it appropriate for this study. Panini Rural Municipality has eight wards—the lowest level of administrative unit in Nepal and has a population of 22 330.23 A family-based NHIP was implemented in the district from 2018/2019.

Study participants

Participants for this study were drawn from the general population of Panini Rural Municipality and the unit of analysis was household. In instances where the head of the household (HH) was not present at the time of data collection, senior members of the household were interviewed as respondents. Individuals under the age of 18 were not included in the study. Furthermore, households that received government subsidies (such as ultra-poor families, the elderly, FCHVs and patients living with HIV/AIDS, MDR-TB, leprosy or disabilities) to participate in the NHIP were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

The sample size was determined using Cochran’s formula24 at 95% level of confidence and 5% margin of error. The proportion of enrolment was 38.75%, which was calculated by dividing the total number of insured people (68 678)25 by the total population of the Arghakhanchi district (177 200)23 at the time of the study.

The study site’s population size, 22 330, was also known. Thus, a further calculation of the sample size applying population adjustment resulted in a final sample size of 360 for this study. To ensure a balanced representation, the sample was evenly split between enrolled and non-enrolled households, with 180 participants in each category using a 1:1 ratio.

Out of the eight wards in the Panini Rural Municipality, three wards (3, 7 and 8) were randomly selected using a lottery method. Samples were then drawn proportionally based on the number of households enrolled in each selected ward. Enrolled households were selected using simple random sampling technique, with the household list obtained from the Health Insurance Board in Arghakhanchi. The sampling process used a lottery number generator available on Google to ensure randomness. For identification of enrolled household, help was taken from enrolment assistants, FCHVs and local leaders. Non-enrolled households were identified as neighbouring households in any direction from the enrolled households.

Study variables

The dependent variable in this study was enrolment in the NHIP. Grounded in HBM,26 all key components of the model were included as independent variables. These comprised modifying factors (such as sociodemographic characteristics), perceived susceptibility and severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers and cues to action. For detailed conceptual framework of the study variables, see online supplemental figure S1.

Data collection tools and techniques

The study tool was meticulously developed through an extensive literature review212731 and expert consultation (see online supplemental file 1, questionnaire). A digital questionnaire was created using the KoboToolbox, an Android application, and data were collected via mobile devices. The questionnaire was pretested with 36 (10% of sample size) households other than study wards, to identify and rectify any errors before the main study commenced. Data collection involved face-to-face interviews with participants, where probing was employed to clarify questions and ensure accurate data capture. Data were collected by the principal investigator ‘AP’ himself.

Data management and analysis

Each day after returning from the field, the collected data were meticulously reviewed for completeness. Several measures were taken to minimise technical issues during data collection using KoboToolbox. Data collector was well trained and already familiar with the KoboToolbox platform prior to fieldwork, which helped reduce user-related errors. Data were collected in offline mode and later synchronised once internet connectivity was available, minimising disruptions due to poor network access. In addition, the questionnaire was programmed with built-in skip logic and validation constraints to reduce entry errors and ensure data consistency. To minimise the risk of data loss, the data were daily transferred from the mobile application to the corresponding KoboToolbox profile. Subsequently, the data were imported and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, V.22. Descriptive statistical measures such as frequencies, percentages, mean and SD were used to summarise results. The wealth index was calculated using the Household International Wealth Index (IWI) Excel tool.32 Knowledge level was assessed using six specific questions related to the premium amount and benefits of the NHIP. Respondents who answered at least four questions correctly were categorised as having good knowledge.

To examine associations between dependent and independent variables, Pearson’s χ2 test and logistic regression were used. Variables with a p value of <0.1 in the bivariate analysis were included in the logistic regression analysis, consistent with approaches used in previous health insurance study.33 Model I incorporated all significant sociodemographic variables identified in the bivariable analysis, while Model II included additional variables meeting the same criteria from the constructs of the HBM. A multicollinearity test, using the variance inflation factor (VIF), confirmed that no variables were highly correlated (all variables had VIF <5). Variables with a p value of <0.05 at a 95% CI in the multivariable logistic regression model were considered to have a significant association with the outcome variable.

To further explore the role of socioeconomic status, a stratified sensitivity analysis was conducted. The wealth index (IWI) was recategorised into two groups (low vs middle/high SES) to ensure adequate sample size within strata and improve model stability. Separate logistic regression models were then fitted within each stratum using the same set of independent variables.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics and enrolment in health insurance

A total of 360 households participated in this study, of which two-thirds (66.4%) of the households were headed by males. The majority (74.7%) belonged to a privileged ethnic group, and 90.3% of the households followed Hinduism. Foreign employment was the primary source of income for 28.9% of households, followed by business (24.7%), agriculture (19.7%), daily wages (9.7%) and other sources. Most household heads (36.9%) had an education level below secondary school, and 18.6% of families had no members with a secondary-level education. More than half (51.1%) of the families were extended, and 60.3% had five or fewer members. The distribution of households by wealth index was nearly even, with the upper middle class representing the largest group at 22.8% (table 1).

Perceived benefit and enrolment in health insurance

While examining the association between the components of perceived benefit and enrolment in health insurance, a significant association was observed with the statement ‘it helps in medical emergencies’, and enrolment in health insurance (p=0.031). All other variables related to perceived benefits (NHIP benefit packages are adequate to meet health needs, NHIP helps to reduce financial burden, quality of services, provider makes good diagnosis and helps in seeking early treatment) were not significantly associated with the enrolment in health insurance (see online supplemental table S1).

Perceived barriers and enrolment in health insurance

When examining the association between perceived barriers and health insurance enrolment, knowledge about health insurance (p<0.001), behaviour of service providers (p<0.001), trust in NHIP (p=0.004) and affordability of premiums (p<0.001) were significantly associated with enrolment. Availability of health workers during visits to health facilities, distance to the first contact point and the process of using services showed no significant association with NHIP enrolment (table 3).

Those who perceived their family’s health status as poor had four times higher odds of enrolling in health insurance (AOR 4.06, 95% CI 1.00 to 16.41) compared with those who perceived their family’s health status as good. Similarly, families with a history of illness in the last 6 months had more than two times higher odds of enrolling (AOR 2.51, 95% CI 1.09 to 5.75) compared with families without such illness experience.

Knowledge about health insurance emerged as a significant factor, with individuals possessing good knowledge having more than six times higher odds of enrolling (AOR 6.61, 95% CI 3.09 to 14.13) compared with those with poor knowledge. Affordability of the premium was another concern, as households that found the premium affordable had more than four times higher odds of enrolling (AOR 4.51, 95% CI 1.12 to 18.19) compared with those who considered it unaffordable.

Peer influence had a significant impact on NHIP enrolment. Households with neighbours who were already enrolled had four times higher odds of enrolling themselves (AOR 4.00, 95% CI 1.86 to 8.60) compared with those without such neighbours. Additionally, households that were requested by neighbours to enrol had nearly eight times higher odds of enrolling (AOR 7.95, 95% CI 3.63 to 17.39) compared with households that did not receive such requests. Households where family members agreed to enrol had almost five times higher odds of enrolling (AOR 4.72, 95% CI 1.56 to 14.34) compared with households where family members did not agree to NHIP enrolment.

The diminished significance of IWI in the adjusted model prompted further stratified sensitivity analysis to better understand the role of socioeconomic status. The findings suggest that enrolment decisions are influenced through different mechanisms across socioeconomic groups. Among lower SES households, HBM variables such as knowledge, perceived family health status and social influence played a more prominent role. This indicates that behavioural and perceptual factors are critical drivers of enrolment in economically disadvantaged populations. In contrast, among middle and higher SES households, enabling factors such as affordability and family support, along with social influence, were more strongly associated with enrolment. This suggests that while awareness and perception remain important, structural and economic facilitators become more influential in relatively better-off groups (see online supplemental table S2). Further analysis was conducted using the original Bloom’s cut-off (≥80%) to classify respondents as having good knowledge. The analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the knowledge–enrolment association and did not involve re-estimation of the full adjusted regression models. Using this stricter threshold, respondents with good knowledge had significantly higher odds of enrolment (OR 2.92, 95% CI 1.59 to 5.36; p=0.001), consistent with the primary findings, although the magnitude of association was somewhat reduced (see online supplemental table S3).

Reasons for non-enrolment in health insurance

Among the 180 households that did not enrol in health insurance, the most common reasons for not enrolling were inappropriate timing of premium collection (53.9%), followed by not being necessary (43.9%), limited service points (29.4%), a mandatory referral card from the first contact point to visit other health facilities (19.4%), an inadequate number of enrolment assistants to follow-up and inform people about enrolment and renewal (12.8%), and a long queue at the hospital (11.7%) (see online supplemental figure S2).

Discussion

This study explored the application of the HBM in understanding the factors influencing individuals’ decisions to enrol in Nepal’s NHIP. Perceived susceptibility, perceived severity of diseases and cues to action emerged as significant drivers for enrolment, highlighting the role of health-related concerns and external prompts in driving decisions. Interestingly, the perceived benefits of the insurance programme did not significantly influence enrolment behaviour. Conversely, critical barriers such as limited knowledge about the programme and the affordability of insurance premiums—categorised under perceived barriers—were found to hinder the enrolment. These findings underscore the complex interplay of cognitive and structural factors in shaping health insurance enrolment in Nepal.

These findings highlight that individual health perceptions and social cues—rather than purely structural or demographic factors—play a central role in health insurance uptake. For example, individuals who perceived their family’s health to be in poor condition who had experienced illness in the past 6 months were significantly more likely to enrol, consistent with studies from Ethiopia showing 3–6 times increased odds of enrolment,31 34 35 Ghana36 37 and other LMICs, including Nepal.38 39 The decision to enrol in health insurance tends to increase with perceived susceptibility to health issues or diseases, a conclusion corroborated by additional research suggesting that perceived health risk is a major motivator in the decision to enrol and use the health services.21 40 This finding aligns with the HBM, which posits that individuals who believe they are at higher risk of illness are more likely to take preventive measures, such as enrolling in health insurance.

Social influences—categorised as cues to action—were another important predictor. Households were more likely to enrol if peers or relatives had enrolled or requested to do so. This reflects a broader pattern seen in behavioural science where individuals often look to others in their community to guide decisions, especially when navigating new or unfamiliar systems like NHIP. Studies from Nepal and East Africa have similarly found that community endorsement and peer behaviours are powerful cues that encourage insurance enrolment.21 41 These social influences act as informal channels of trust and assurance, which can be particularly effective in settings where institutional trust is limited.

On the contrary, perceived benefits, while theoretically important, did not emerge as a key driver in this study. This contradicts prior research suggesting that perceived financial security during emergencies, and medical needs and improved service quality are strong motivators.42 43 This could indicate that while people value the theoretical benefits of NHIP, these are often overshadowed by practical barriers such as premium affordability and limited awareness of the programme. It is possible that in the Nepalese context, potential benefits are not clearly communicated or not consistently realised in practice, reducing their impact on decision-making. However, recent policy changes by the Health Insurance Board Nepal, including the revision of the benefit package to a ceiling of NRs. 25 000 for outpatient services for a family of five,44 may further contribute to confusion or misperceptions if not effectively communicated. Such changes could influence public trust and perceived value of the programme, thereby affecting enrolment decisions.

We found knowledge as a powerful facilitator of enrolment in NHIP as participants with good knowledge of NHIP had six times higher odds to enrol. This finding is supported by a meta-analysis from LMICs45 and other studies,3442 4648 which also confirmed the role of knowledge in health insurance enrolment. Knowledge equips individuals to make informed decisions and reduces uncertainty or misconceptions about the programme. In contrast, misinformation or lack of familiarity can lead to mistrust or disinterest, even among those who might benefit most from NHIP. Affordability of premiums emerged as another key barrier to enrolment, with households who found the premium unaffordable being significantly less likely to enrol—confirming a trend reported across studies in Nepal (AOR 4.674, 2.156 to 10.132),28 Ethiopia (AOR 2.30, 1.23 to 4.26)31 and other low-resource settings.45 49

Although all constructs of the HBM showed significant associations, not all predicted enrolment in NHIP. Perceived susceptibility and cues to action were influential in the decision to enrol, while perceived benefits did not significantly impact enrolment decisions. Although perceived barriers were associated with enrolment, they did not have a strong influence, with knowledge and affordability emerging as the primary barriers rather than other perceived obstacles. This finding contrasts with another study on injury prevention programmes among high school students, where perceived benefits and perceived severity were the most influential factors, followed by perceived susceptibility and cues to action.50

The study has notable strengths. By applying the HBM, it contributes valuable behavioural and psychological insights to the limited body of literature on NHIP enrolment in Nepal. The use of a theory-driven framework to identify both cognitive and contextual factors provides a structured understanding of the decision-making process, which can inform more targeted policy interventions.

At the same time, this study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively small, and data were collected from only one municipality, which limits the generalisability of the findings to broader geographic or demographic contexts in Nepal. Second, the use of a purely quantitative approach constrained the ability to gain deeper, contextual insights into the complex motivations, perceptions and lived experiences that influence NHIP enrolment. Third, the study focused exclusively on the demand side—individual and household-level factors—while neglecting supply-side determinants such as health service availability, provider attitudes and administrative challenges within the NHIP system. Fourth, although the study employed a cross-sectional design, the sample was equally allocated between enrolled and non-enrolled households to facilitate comparison of factors associated with enrolment. As a result, the proportion of enrolled households in this study does not represent the true population distribution. The study is therefore more appropriate for identifying factors associated with enrolment than for estimating enrolment prevalence. Furthermore, non-enrolled households were selected from the same communities as enrolled households. As a result, both groups may have shared exposure to NHIP information and community-level social influences. This may have reduced differences between groups and could have influenced findings related to cues to action and social influence. While bivariable p value screening was used as an initial step to identify candidate variables for inclusion in the multivariable model, we acknowledge that this approach alone may not be sufficient in a theory-guided study. Given these limitations, future studies should consider adopting qualitative or mixed methods design to explore both user and provider perspectives in greater depth. These approaches can uncover nuanced barriers and facilitators that are not easily captured through structured surveys alone.

Conclusion

This study sheds important light on the complex factors influencing enrolment in NHIP programmes, revealing that while all constructs of HBM are associated with enrolment decisions, not all are equally predictive. Key drivers of enrolment include perceived susceptibility to health issues, such as poor family health status and experience of illness within the previous 6 months, and cues to action like the influence of social networks, such as peers, neighbours and family members. Interestingly, perceived benefits, often considered a critical factor in the Health Belief Model, did not significantly influence enrolment in this context. Instead, practical barriers like limited knowledge of the programme and concerns about premium affordability emerged as decisive obstacles underscoring the importance of addressing these issues in NHIP programme design.

Implications for policy and practice

The study findings underscore the importance of targeted interventions that boost awareness, enhance affordability and harness social influence to increase NHIP enrolment. Given the identified challenges related to affordability and timing of premium payments, policymakers may consider exploring flexible payment mechanisms, such as income-based contributions or instalment options, as potential strategies to improve accessibility. Policymakers should focus on making insurance programmes more adoptive considering flexibility in service provision such as removing mandatory referral cards from first contact point to facilitate easier access to NHIP services. It is also recommended to empanel local health facilities to provide health insurance services, as this may improve people’s perception of the accessibility and usability of healthcare services under health insurance. Furthermore, leveraging the power of social networks, community leaders and peer influences can create a ripple effect that encourages widespread participation in NHIP programmes.
