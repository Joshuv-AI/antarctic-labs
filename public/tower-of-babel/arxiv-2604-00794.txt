Glassy Arrest Behind the Apparent Second Liquid in Water
Florian Pabst, Ali Hassanali

Abstract
The origin of water’s anomalous behavior remains a central open problem in the physical sciences and is often attributed to a liquid–liquid transition (LLT) between high- and low-density liquid states deep in the supercooled regime. Experimental access to this region has been challenging due to rapid crystallization, leaving atomistic simulations as a major source of supporting evidence. Using extensive machine-learning–accelerated first-principles simulations in direct comparison with spectroscopic, structural, and dynamical experimental measurements, we show that features commonly interpreted as signatures of two-liquid behavior emerge at the onset of dynamical arrest. Specifically, we find that two-state fluctuations previously associated with an LLT reflect a transformation from a high-density liquid to a kinetically arrested low-density glass. By mapping equilibrium dynamics across pressure and temperature, our results suggest a reassessment of water’s metastable landscape, in which apparent two-state behavior may reflect a relatively high glass-transition temperature of ambient-pressure low-density water, 189 ± \pm 8 K—remarkably close to the temperature previously associated with the LLT.



Water defies the expectations of a simple liquid. Upon cooling, it expands rather than contracts, while its compressibility and heat capacity exhibit pronounced extrema. These anomalies have long hinted at an underlying organizing principle that remains unresolved
[
46
,
26
]
.
A leading hypothesis attributes them to fluctuations associated with a hidden liquid–liquid critical point (LLCP), buried below the freezing line at elevated pressure. Support for this view emerged from the observation of a first-order–like transition between two amorphous states in experiments
[
33
]
, and later between two liquid states in simulations
[
39
]
, giving rise to the liquid–liquid transition scenario.

Experimental access to the LLCP has proven extremely challenging because it lies within the crystallization-prone
no-man’s-land
. Only very recent experiments have managed to penetrate this regime, probing various physical quantities on ultra-short time scales before ice formation intervenes
[
26
,
25
,
50
,
54
]
. As a consequence, much of the support for the LLCP hypothesis has relied on information coming from molecular dynamics simulations.

Very recently, atomistic simulations using machine-learning based potentials trained on first-principles data
[
18
,
41
]
seem to confirm the original hypothesis of the existence of an LLT that were based on the ST2 potential
[
39
]
, while studies based on the classical point-charge model TIP4P/2005 have produced conflicting conclusions on the presence of an LLT
[
12
,
23
,
35

. Although these simulations have pushed the boundary to microsecond timescales, comparable to the ultrafast experiments that briefly access deeply supercooled water before crystallization intervenes, equilibration at these low temperatures becomes exceedingly challenging due to the very long structural relaxation times. At these conditions, the dynamics becomes glassy and the inference of thermodynamic quantities from simulations becomes difficult. This challenge also translates to experiments deriving thermodynamic support for the presence of an LLCP
[
54
]
, since they involve measurements limited to the microsecond timescales before the onset of crystallization.

In fact, one of the open questions in the physics of supercooled water is the exact location of its glass transition temperature
[
2
]
. The commonly cited value of 136 K hinges on experiments of hyper-quenched and vapor-deposited samples whose relationship to slowly-supercooled pure water is uncertain. These measurements show only a faint calorimetric signal at this temperature, small enough to be interpreted as a ”shadow” transition
[
55
,
20
]
, suggesting that the true liquid–glass transformation may occur at substantially higher temperatures. While moderately concentrated salt solutions often exhibit a

around 136 K, recent experiments using minimal solute content find a markedly elevated

[
30

near 190 K
[
58
,
52

T_{g}
between 170 and 200 K
[
32
]
. Temperature dependent electron diffraction experiments cooling from the liquid have shown that the structure does not evolve further below 200 K, placing the glass transition at this temperature for the cooling time of
15

\mu

[

of slow-cooled bulk water. Most importantly, if water’s

indeed lies at the upper end of the range suggested by the aforementioned experiments, it would be very close to the LLT temperature found in the most realistic ab initio–based water models based both on DFT
[
57

and MB-pol
[

, corrected for their melting point mismatch with respect to experiment.

These observations raise an intriguing possibility: if deeply supercooled water becomes glassy around the temperature of the LLCP, could signatures often attributed to a liquid–liquid transition also be consistent with a transition between a fluid high-density liquid (HDL) and a kinetically arrested low-density glass? To tackle this question, we explore how the equilibrium dynamics of deeply supercooled water evolve as a function of temperature and pressure, comparing state-of-the-art machine-learning–accelerated first-principles simulations with experimental measurements in regimes where crystallization can be suppressed. This allows us to identify the conditions under which liquid water remains ergodic and when it instead becomes kinetically arrested—distinguishing genuine liquid behavior from glassy non-equilibrium states. By establishing the location of the glass-transition boundary relative to the proposed liquid–liquid transition, we suggest that signatures attributed to an LLT may not necessarily reflect an equilibrium transformation between two liquids, but could instead be consistent with a crossover between a liquid and a glass.

A Pressure or Temperature Driven Glass Transition

Identifying whether the fluctuations in supercooled water shown earlier, reflect a glass transition requires directly interrogating the kinetics of glassy dynamics. To this end, we design simulations that independently induce vitrification through changes in temperature or pressure, motivated by two experimental protocols that have been used to infer the existence of distinct liquid phases. The first is inspired by X-ray scattering experiments that track the depressurization dynamics of high-density liquid water following laser-induced heating of high-density amorphous ice (HDA)
[
25
]
, while the second mirrors calorimetric measurements upon cooling. For all the ensuing analysis, unless stated otherwise, we report results coming from extensive simulations with the DNN@SCAN potential using 512 water molecules.

A Glass Transition Temperature in the Range
\sim
181–197 K

The preceding analysis points to a glass transition significantly above the commonly accepted value of 136 K. \tau=\tau_{0}\exp\!\left[\frac{D}{T-T_{0}}\right],

(2)

which captures the super-Arrhenius slowing down of supercooled liquids approaching the glass transition

We extract the relaxation time
\tau
by probing the structural relaxation of the liquid through its total electric dipole. This choice is motivated by two considerations. First, dipole fluctuations can be directly compared with dielectric spectroscopy, the most widely used experimental probe of supercooled-liquid dynamics. Dielectric spectroscopy spans an exceptional frequency range—from terahertz to microhertz—thereby covering the entire crossover from liquid to glassy behavior. Importantly, dielectric spectroscopy measurements for water under nanoconfinement and in salt solutions—two strategies that suppress crystallization—are already available as a function of supercooling, enabling direct comparison with our simulations, as discussed below. Second, the dominant peak in water’s dielectric loss spectrum reflects collective orientational rearrangements of the hydrogen-bond network. This process occurs on longer timescales than translational diffusion over intermolecular distances
[
4

and therefore sets the longest relaxation timescale required for full equilibration of the liquid. For other transport properties like viscosity or diffusion coefficient see Fig.

Discussion and Conclusions

The hypothesis of a liquid–liquid transition (LLT) has shaped modern understanding of water’s anomalies. Yet direct experimental proof remains elusive, leaving molecular simulations as a primary source of supporting evidence. By combining extensive machine-learning–accelerated first-principles simulations with direct comparison to multiple experimental measurements, we identify a common constraint affecting both approaches: in the deeply supercooled regime near the proposed LLT, structural relaxation slows dramatically with small reductions in pressure or temperature. Systems commonly interpreted as liquid may therefore reside in a kinetically arrested glassy state, biasing inferred thermodynamic properties. We propose that many signatures attributed to a liquid–liquid transition can emerge at the onset of dynamical arrest, where equilibration fails and glassy behavior mimics liquid–liquid coexistence.

This proximity between vitrification and the putative LLT motivates a re-examination of prior interpretations. In simulations, fluctuations between high- and low-density states appear to be associated with trapping in low-energy configurations with vanishing molecular mobility, consistent with transitions between a diffusive high-density liquid and a dynamically arrested low-density glass. Mobilities in the arrested state for all three models in Fig. 1—including DNN@MB-pol, currently the most accurate model for neutral bulk water—fall within the range reported for water in low-density amorphous (LDA) ice
[
19
]
. Free-energy estimates may be sensitive to equilibration in regions of low mobility, where incomplete sampling can bias the apparent stability of the low-density state and give rise to an apparent coexistence between two liquid phases. Similarly, abrupt experimental signatures may reflect observations on timescales too short for full equilibration, consistent with the formation of an amorphous low-density state rather than a distinct second liquid. Apparent LLT or Widom-line signatures (i.e., maxima in thermodynamic response functions) may therefore emerge near the boundary between ergodic and non-ergodic behavior, where rapidly increasing relaxation times hinder equilibration.

To further elucidate these issues, we construct a dynamical map of the supercooled phase diagram for the DNN@SCAN model by extracting isochronals, i.e., lines of constant structural relaxation time
\tau
(Fig.
4
). These are obtained by fitting the Vogel–Fulcher–Tammann equation to
\tau(T)
at different pressures and determining temperatures where
\tau
reaches specified values. Filled circles denote data on time scales covered by the simulations, while open circles indicate VFT extrapolations (see Fig.
3
). Also shown are the melting line
[
57
]
and the first-order–like transition between low-density amorphous (LDA) and high-density amorphous (HDA) ice
[
48
]
.

Near 4000 bar, water transitions from anomalous behavior—characterized by negative slopes of both the melting line and the isochronals—to simple liquid behavior at higher pressures. At nearly the same pressure, the first-order–like LDA–HDA glass-glass transition occurs. Figure
4
also shows the temperature at which the pressure sensitivity of the relaxation time is maximal,

T^{\rm max}_{\nabla_{P}\tau}=\max\left(\frac{{\rm d}\log\tau}{{\rm d}P}\right)
. Along this line,
\tau
varies by roughly eleven orders of magnitude over about 2000 bar. Strikingly, this temperature (193.5 K) coincides with the regime where fluctuations between the two states in Fig.
1
appear and lies close to the proposed LLT. Because
\tau
and density vary extremely steeply with pressure in this region, fluctuations in pressure or density in small simulation boxes can transiently push the system across the ergodic–non-ergodic boundary, where dynamical arrest generates apparent two-state behavior. Consistently, such two-state fluctuations appear only in small systems, whereas larger simulations
[
31
,
51
]
or different barostat settings (see SM Fig.
S1
) do not show this behavior. The same mechanism could allow non-equilibrated states to enter free-energy calculations, artificially altering the free energy of the lower-density state.

The pressure dependence of water’s dynamics under supercooling thus reveals a complex landscape of structural relaxation. At higher pressures the dynamics resembles that of concentrated aqueous solutions, with a glass-transition temperature near 136 K, consistent with previous reports. At ambient pressure, however, relaxation slows much more dramatically and extrapolates to a surprisingly high glass-transition temperature,
T|_{\tau=100\mathrm{s}}=189\pm 8
K, in the DNN@SCAN and DNN@MB-pol models as well as TIP4P/2005. This value agrees with recent experimental studies of confined water
[
32
]
, rapidly cooled water
[
28
]
, and salt mixtures that minimally perturb water’s structure
[
58
,
52
]
. Also, experimental diffusion coefficients, inferred from crystal growth rates, show a clear kink in their temperature dependence around 189 K
[
53
]
. This feature can be interpreted as the transition from crystallization advancing via structural relaxation at higher temperatures to proceeding via secondary (
\beta
) relaxation below
T_{g}
(see SM, Fig.
S7
for more details). These experiments are therefore consistent with probing the onset of vitrification, supporting a scenario in which dynamical arrest can be regarded the dominant organizing feature of deeply supercooled water.

Our results suggest that an abrupt, first-order–like transition between high- and low-density states can emerge when at least one of these states becomes dynamically arrested. Under equilibrium conditions, by contrast, the density varies smoothly with pressure and temperature, as the system remains able to adapt to the imposed state point. Although we cannot exclude the existence of a liquid–liquid transition (LLT) in regions inaccessible to current simulations, our perspective is consistent with earlier suggestions that a relatively high glass-transition temperature could obviate the need for an LLT to explain water’s anomalies
[
42
]
, as well as with models in which a liquid–liquid critical point lies below the Kauzmann temperature
[
43
]
.

Our results are also consistent with recent coexistence simulations, in which a low-mobility, low-density phase is brought into contact with a high-mobility, high-density phase
[
51
]
. In these simulations, the interface persists for tens of nanoseconds—timescales comparable to those associated with structural relaxation and aging toward lower-energy states in deeply supercooled systems (Fig.
2
). Similar front-like melting dynamics have been reported in glassy systems
[
17
]
, and model glass formers can exhibit first-order–like coexistence between ordered glassy and disordered liquid phases
[
8
]
.

Taken together, our results indicate that a crossover from an high density liquid to a glass may rationalize the origins of the apparent two-state behavior. In this regard, these findings hint at the possibility that the singularity-free scenarios for water’s anomalies
[
40
]
, in which anomalous thermodynamic behavior arises from the continuous evolution of local structure rather than from a hidden critical point, should be reconsidered. Indeed, several models reproduce many of water’s anomalies in the absence of a liquid–liquid critical point, including the coarse-grained mW model
[
21
]
. Signatures of a Widom line—potentially arising either from nonequilibrium effects or from an LLT—are also expected under equilibrium conditions within the singularity-free scenario for substances such as water, which expand upon cooling
[
40
]
. More broadly, the phenomenology attributed to a liquid–liquid transition appears to emerge naturally from the rapid onset of dynamical arrest. From this perspective, the longstanding debate over supercooled water may reflect not a critical point, but the boundary between liquid and glass, which gives rise to phenomena that mimic a liquid–liquid transition.

are deduced, great care is taken to equilibrate the system prior to production runs, as follows. First, NPT simulations are performed, starting from the final configuration of the preceding higher-temperature run, for at least
10\tau
with a time step of 0.5 fs. The density obtained at the end of this simulation is then used for a subsequent NVT run, again with a 0.5 fs time step. After at least
10\tau
, eight independent configurations, separated by at least
1\tau
, are extracted and used as starting points for eight production runs in the NVE ensemble, with a time step of 0.25 fs and a duration of at least
10\tau
.

Simulations with the TIP4P/2005 model are performed in GROMACS on systems of 1000 water molecules, using the same settings as in the study reporting two-state fluctuations
[
12
]
. The same NPT–NVT–NVE protocol described above is employed, but with a time step four times larger. At the lowest temperatures (220 K and below), the temperature could not be maintained within acceptable limits during the very long NVE runs. Therefore, in these cases, production runs are performed in the NVT ensemble using the v-rescale thermostat
[
7
]
.

Dielectric spectra (imaginary part of the dielectric function) are calculated from the total electric dipole moment of the simulation box, obtained using a second neural network trained to predict molecular dipole moments
[
31
]
, via the equation

\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(\omega)=\frac{\omega}{3V\varepsilon_{0}k_{B}T}\int\text{d}t\exp(-i\omega t)\left<\mathbf{M}(t)\mathbf{M}(0)\right>,

(S1)

where
V
is the box volume,
\mathbf{M}
is the total dipole moment of the system (i.e., the sum over all molecular dipole moments),
T
is the simulation temperature, and
\epsilon_{0}
and
k_{B}
are the vacuum permittivity and Boltzmann constant, respectively. This method has been shown to yield excellent agreement with the experimental dielectric spectrum of ambient water; further details can be found in Ref.
[
37
]
.

2.1
Influence of the barostat on two-state fluctuations

In the main text, we showed that the two-state fluctuations reported in the literature, which were previously assigned to a liquid–liquid transition, instead reflect intermittent trapping in a kinetically arrested, low-energy glassy state that is not accessible in equilibrium at the corresponding state point.
Since large pressure fluctuations in the small simulation box (192 molecules) are presumed to facilitate trapping in an energy state corresponding to lower pressures, we investigated how different barostat settings influence the observed two-state fluctuation behavior. To this end, we employed the SCAN@DNN model with identical settings and procedures (3200 bar, initialized in the high-density regime) as used in the literature
[
18
]
to detect the purported liquid–liquid fluctuations. In addition, we performed a second simulation in which all parameters were kept identical, except that the damping constants of both the barostat and thermostat were increased by a factor of 10.
Both simulations were run for 1
\mu
s, i.e., twice as long as in the original work. The potential energy, density, and time-resolved mean square displacement are shown in Fig.
S1
, with the original settings on the left-hand side and the modified settings on the right-hand side. While the simulation performed with the original settings again exhibits trapping in a low-energy, low-density kinetically arrested state for a significant fraction of the trajectory (here,
\sim 0.5~\mu
s), in agreement with Fig.
1
, the behavior is markedly different for the modified settings. In this case, no long-lived trapping in the low-energy state is observed; instead, only very short excursions occur, with the longest appearing at around 0.75
\mu
s and lasting for approximately
\sim 50
ns.
These results demonstrate that the previously reported two-state fluctuations are highly sensitive to barostat parameters and may therefore not be indicative of an underlying equilibrium liquid–liquid transition, but instead from non-equilibrium trapping effects.

4.4
Relation of
T_{g}
to other characteristic temperatures.

Diffusion coefficients extracted from crystal growth measurements have been used to support the notion of a glass transition in water around 136 K, since significant crystal growth (and thus molecular mobility) has been observed in this temperature regime
[
53
]
. The temperature dependence of the inferred diffusion coefficients follows Arrhenius behavior below
\sim 150
K and crosses over to a much stronger temperature dependence above
\sim
180 K
[
53
]
. Such an apparent change in slope is commonly observed when secondary (
\beta
) relaxation processes, which typically exhibit Arrhenius behavior, separate from the structural (
\alpha
) relaxation at
T_{g}
.

Indeed, it is well established for various liquids that crystallization can proceed via molecular mechanisms distinct from the structural relaxation, allowing crystallization to occur even in the glassy state
[
34
]
. To test this scenario, we compare the experimental diffusion coefficients from Ref.
[
53
]
, inferred from crystallization rates, with the diffusion coefficients obtained from our simulations in Fig.
S7
. The shaded area denotes the range of structural relaxation times
\tau
from the different models, as shown in Fig.
3
C. The average glass transition temperature
T_{g}=189
K determined in the main text is indicated by a vertical line.

The experimental diffusion coefficients below 189 K are well described by an Arrhenius fit. Most importantly, above 189 K, the experimental data deviate from this Arrhenius behavior, reflecting the pronounced change in temperature dependence discussed above. Thus, the experimental diffusion coefficients are fully consistent with a glass transition around
\sim 189
K, with crystallization below
T_{g}
proceeding via secondary relaxation processes rather than structural relaxation. We note that a similar scenario has been proposed for water under confinement
[
32
]
, but also that the
\beta
-relaxation scenario at the lowest temperatures is challenged by diffusivity measurements on layered H
2
O-D
2
O films
[
27
]
.

Lastly, we compare our simulated value of
T_{g}
with empirical relationships for glass transition temperatures. While these relations are not definitive, they provide useful context. In the main text, we already discussed the coincidence between the experimental estimate of the Kauzmann temperature and our
T_{0}
value obtained from the VFT fit, a correspondence observed in many glass-forming liquids. Furthermore, many liquids follow the so-called two-thirds rule, according to which the glass transition temperature is approximately two-thirds of the melting temperature
[
22
]
. For water, this yields
\frac{2}{3}\cdot 273~\mathrm{K}=182~\mathrm{K}
, which lies within the uncertainty of our estimate
T_{g}=189\pm 8~\mathrm{K}
.

In addition, for many systems, fitting correlation times with a power law as in mode-coupling theory yields a divergence temperature
T_{c}
that is approximately
1.2\,T_{g}

[
11
]
. For water, using the commonly cited value
T_{c}=228~\mathrm{K}
leads to
T_{g}=228~\mathrm{K}/1.2=190~\mathrm{K}
, again in excellent agreement with our estimate.

Taken together, these observations indicate that, from the perspective of glass-forming liquids, water at ambient pressure does not exhibit anomalous behavior but rather conforms to established empirical trends, displaying VFT-like structural relaxation and the emergence of secondary relaxation processes at the glass transition temperature.
