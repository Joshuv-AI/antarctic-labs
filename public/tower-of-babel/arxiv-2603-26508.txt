Semirings of formal sums and injective partial transformations
Maximilien Gadouleau, Marianne Johnson

Abstract
The semiring of discrete dynamical systems is a simple algebraic model for modularity in deterministic systems. The objects of the semiring are finite transformations (viewed as directed graphs and regarded up to isomorphism), the sum of two transformations corresponds to applying them independently on distinct sets, and the product corresponds to applying both transformations in parallel. In this paper, we extend this semiring to include partial transformations; the sum and product are natural generalisations. Each (partial) transformation can be viewed as a sum (over ℕ \mathbb{N} ) of connected (partial) transformations. We generalise this idea by working in semirings of formal sums over any semiring 𝕊 \mathbb{S} . Here we consider the case where 𝕊 = 𝔽 2 \mathbb{S}=\mathbb{F}_{2} , the binary field, and we focus on injective partial transformations, i.e. sums of chains and cycles. While no efficient algorithm for the division problem for sums of cycles in the original semiring of discrete dynamical systems is known, we give a concise characterisation of all the solutions of the division problem for sums of cycles over 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} . We then extend this characterisation to dividing any injective partial transformations, i.e. sums of chains and cycles over 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} .

The semiring of transformations

Many large systems, both abstract and in real life, are modular, i.e. they are built by combining smaller modules that perform individual tasks. Some examples include Boolean networks

The semiring of transformations was first proposed in 2018 by researchers in Cellular Automata

It has appeared under the name of semiring of Discrete Dynamical Systems, Finite Dynamical Systems, or functional digraphs; we shall refer to those objects as transformations.
The objects of the semiring are finite transformations (functions from a finite set to itself) up to graph isomorphism. Those objects are equipped with two operations: the sum corresponds to two systems having disjoint dynamics, while the product corresponds to the parallel composition of the two dynamics.

The proposed model aims to be very broad by remaining simple: the modelled system has a finite number of possible states, for which we do not know the semantics, and is represented by a deterministic evolution. The two operations represent different ways of combining those systems. While (full) transformations assume that every state has a defined successor, some states may lead to undefined characteristics, or crashing the whole system entirely; such states without successors are encapsulated in a partial transformation instead. This importantly also applies to the case where only partial information on the transitions are available. As such, in this paper we extend the semiring of transformations to the semiring of partial transformations; the sum and product are defined naturally in this case.

1.2
Contributions and outline

A partial transformation of a set
X
is a mapping
f:Y\to X
, where
Y\subseteq X
. From
f
one can define the directed graph
D_{f}
with vertex set
V(D_{f})=X
and arc set
E(D_{f})=\{(y,f(y)):y\in Y\}
. In this digraph, every vertex has out-degree at most one; conversely, any digraph where every vertex has out-degree at most one can be constructed that way. We say that two partial transformations
f
and
f^{\prime}
of
X
and
X^{\prime}
are equivalent if
D_{f}
and
D_{f^{\prime}}
are isomorphic. We then consider classes of equivalence of partial transformations. The sum of two (equivalence classes of) partial transformations
f:Y\to X
and
f^{\prime}:Y^{\prime}\to X^{\prime}
with
X
and
X^{\prime}
disjoint is their disjoint union:
f+f^{\prime}:Y\cup Y^{\prime}\to X\cup X^{\prime}
with
(f+f^{\prime})(y)=f(y)
for all
y\in Y
and
(f+f^{\prime})(y^{\prime})=f^{\prime}(y^{\prime})
for all
y^{\prime}\in Y^{\prime}
. Their product is given by the direct product of digraphs:
ff^{\prime}:Y\times Y^{\prime}\to X\times X^{\prime}
with
(ff^{\prime})(y,y^{\prime})=(f(y),f(y^{\prime}))
for all
(y,y^{\prime})\in Y\times Y^{\prime}
. It is clear that those operations are well-defined, since sum and product preserve the equivalence of partial transformations.

Our paper makes two main qualitative contributions. The first qualitative contribution is to consider partial transformations. We motivate this generalisation in two ways. Firstly, in many systems, some configurations lead to an undefined state, or a failure of some kind. As such, all states may not always have a successor, and the system is represented by a partial transformation. Secondly, in system biology, one learns the system through discovering some of the transitions in a successive manner, and some transitions may be impractical to actually discover. As such, the current knowledge of the system must be described by a partial transformation. Partial transformations form a semiring, by naturally extending the definitions of sum and product given for transformations.

The second main qualitative contribution is to introduce torsion to the semiring of partial transformations, by considering formal sums over a finite semiring
\mathbb{S}
. This immediately brings additive torsion, but can also bring multiplicative torsion as well. In the original semiring of transformations, elements have no torsion, since the vertex sets of
a,a^{2},a^{3},\dots
all have distinct sizes; similarly
a,2a,3a
, and so on are all distinct. For instance, the cycle
C_{d}
satisfies
C_{d}^{2}=dC_{d}
, and hence
C_{d}^{k}=d^{k-1}C_{d}
for all
k\geq 1
. On the other hand, if for instance
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{F}_{2}
is the binary field, then we count sums of cycles “modulo
2
”, e.g. the permutation
C_{3}+4C_{4}+2C_{5}+7C_{8}
becomes
C_{3}+C_{8}
. In this semiring, we have
C_{d}^{2}=0
if
d
is even and
C_{d}^{2}=C_{d}
if
d
is odd; and moreover,
C_{d}+C_{d}=0
. These relations not only greatly simplify calculations, but also yield a rich algebraic structure which can be exploited (see Section
3
).

In this paper we consider the division problem (given
a,b\in S
determine whether there exists a solution to
ax=b
) in certain commutative semirings, which is equivalent to studying (any one of) Green’s relations in the underlying multiplicative semigroup. Particularly we consider the subsemiring of equivalence classes of injective partial transformations with finite support, and each such graph can be decomposed into cycles and chains. More concretely, for each positive integer
d
, let
L_{d}=d\to(d-1)\to\dots\to 1
be the
chain
of length
d
and as before
C_{d}=d\to d-1\to\dots\to 1\to d
be the
cycle
of length
d
. We denote the set of all finite chains as
L
and the set of all finite cycles as
C
. Our main technical contributions centre around the division problem for sums of cycles, when counting modulo
2
, i.e. working in
\mathbb{S}C
, where
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{F}_{2}
and
C=\{C_{d}:d\geq 1\}
is the set of all cycles. We show in Theorem
3.9
that the solutions to
ax=b
when counting modulo
2
can be expressed in terms of intervals in a Boolean algebra, and in particular deciding whether
ax=b
has a solution can be done efficiently. We extend this result in two ways. Firstly, reflecting on the work in
[
24
]
on injective polynomials, we classify the injective univariate polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{2}C
and show that solving equations for those polynomials is straightforward in Theorem
3.21
. Secondly, we solve the division problem for injective partial transformations, i.e. in
\mathbb{F}_{2}(L\cup C)
, in Theorem
4.2
.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Firstly, we introduce the general framework of semirings of formal sums in Section
2.1
, and we focus on the important case of formal sums of elements of a semilattice over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
in Section
2.2
. Secondly, we carry out a thorough investigation of the semiring
\mathbb{F}_{2}C
of formal sums of cycles modulo
2
in Section
3
: we give some general basic results in Section
3.1
; we characterise the Boolean algebra of idempotents in Section
3.2
; we provide a characterisation of the solutions to the division equation
ax=b
in Section
3.3
; we characterise the (extended) Green’s relations in that semigroup in Section
3.4
; we classify units, regular elements and more in Section
3.5
; we classify injective univariate polynomials in Section
3.6
; and we study the intersection of principal ideals in Section
3.7
. Finally, we consider the division problem in the semiring of formal sums of injective partial transformations in Section
4

4.1
and then the general case in Section
4.2
. The paper ends in the Conclusion Section
5

2
Semirings of formal sums

2.1
General case

A commutative semiring is a structure
(\mathbb{S},+,\times,0,1)
, where
(\mathbb{S},+)
is a commutative monoid with identity
0
,
(\mathbb{S},\times)
is a commutative monoid with identity
1
, multiplication distributes over addition, i.e.
a\times(b+c)=(a\times b)+(a\times c)
for all
a,b,c\in\mathbb{S}
, and
0
is a multiplicative zero, i.e.
0\times a=a\times 0=0
for all
a\in\mathbb{S}
. Since all semirings considered in this paper are commutative, we will frequently say simply ‘semiring’. As usual, when the context is clear, we shall write
ab
in place of
a\times b
. We denote the semiring of natural numbers with respect to the usual arithmetic operations by
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,\dots,\}
.

Let
G
be a set and
\mathbb{S}
be a semiring. Let
\mathbb{S}G
be the set of formal sums of elements in
G
with coefficients in
\mathbb{S}
and finite support, i.e.

\mathbb{S}G=\left\{\sum_{j\in G}s_{j}j:s_{j}\in\mathbb{S},\text{ finitely many non-zero }s_{j}\right\}.

For
s\in\mathbb{S}G
and
j\in G
we will write
s_{j}
to denote the coefficient of
j
in
s
. Notice that there is an obvious left action of
\mathbb{S}
on
\mathbb{S}G
determined by
(as)_{j}=as_{j}
for all
a\in\mathbb{S},s\in\mathbb{S}G,j\in G
, and a point-wise addition on
\mathbb{S}
determined by
(s+t)_{j}=s_{j}+t_{j}
for all
s,t\in\mathbb{S}G
and all
j\in G
. It is clear that this operation is associative and commutative. For
g\in G
we shall also (by a slight abuse of notation) write simply
g
for the element
1g
of
\mathbb{S}G
. Let us also write
0
for the empty sum, which is the additive identity element. In order to specify a semiring structure on
\mathbb{S}G
, it suffices to specify a function of the form
G\times G\rightarrow\mathbb{S}G
obeying certain natural conditions.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\mathbb{S}
be a commutative semiring and suppose that
G
is a set endowed with an operation
\cdot:G\times G\to\mathbb{S}G
, satisfying the following three conditions:

1.

for all
g,h\in G
,
g\cdot h=h\cdot g
;

2.

there exists
e\in G
such that
e\cdot g=g\cdot e=g\in\mathbb{S}G
for all
g\in G
;

3.

for all
g,h,i,k\in G

\sum_{j\in G}(g\cdot h)_{j}(j\cdot i)_{k}=\sum_{j\in G}(h\cdot i)_{j}(j\cdot g)_{k}.

Then
\mathbb{S}G
is a semiring with respect to point-wise addition and multiplication given by:

\sum_{g\in G}s_{g}g\cdot\sum_{h\in G}t_{h}h=\sum_{g,h\in G}s_{g}t_{h}(g\cdot h).

(1)

Furthermore, if
\mathbb{S}
is a ring, then
\mathbb{S}G
is a ring.

Proof.

First note that the formula in (
1
) determines well-defined function
\mathbb{S}G\times\mathbb{S}G\rightarrow\mathbb{S}G
: by definition
g\cdot h
is a formal sum with coefficients in
\mathbb{S}
and finite support; since
\mathbb{S}
acts on the left of
\mathbb{S}G
it follows that for all
g,h\in G
and all
a,b\in\mathbb{S}
we have
ab(g\cdot h)\in\mathbb{S}G
; then the fact that only finitely many of the coefficients
s_{g}
and
t_{h}
are non-zero yields that the above element has finite support. It is also easy to see that the empty sum
0
is a left and right zero element with respect to this multiplication. Condition 1 together with the fact that multiplication in
\mathbb{S}
is commutative clearly implies that this product is commutative, whilst the existence of the element
e
from condition 2 ensures that
e\in\mathbb{S}G
is an identity element for the multiplication. Next note that for all
g,h,i\in G
we have

\displaystyle(g\cdot h)\cdot i

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j\in G}(g\cdot h)_{j}j\right)\cdot i=\sum_{j\in G}((g\cdot h)_{j}(j\cdot i))=\sum_{j\in G}\left((g\cdot h)_{j}\sum_{k\in G}(j\cdot i)_{k}k\right)=\sum_{k\in G}\sum_{j\in G}(g\cdot h)_{j}(j\cdot i)_{k}k

\displaystyle g\cdot(h\cdot i)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g\cdot\left(\sum_{j\in G}(h\cdot i)_{j}j\right)=\sum_{j\in G}(h\cdot i)_{j}(g\cdot j)=\sum_{j\in G}\left((h\cdot i)_{j}\sum_{k\in G}(g\cdot j)_{k}k\right)=\sum_{k\in G}\sum_{j\in G}(h\cdot i)_{j}(g\cdot j)_{k}k,

so we have
(g\cdot h)\cdot i=g\cdot(h\cdot i)
if and only if condition 3 holds. From this it is then routine to check that the multiplication defined on
\mathbb{S}G
is associative, and moreover that the multiplication distributes over the addition. Thus
\mathbb{S}G
is a semiring.
∎

Example 2.2
.

If
G
is a commutative monoid and
\mathbb{S}
a semiring, by taking
g\cdot h=gh\in\mathbb{S}G
it is easy to see that each of the conditions above are satisfied. Indeed, associativity of the monoid product in
G
ensures that the third condition is satisfied, since
(ab)_{c}=\delta(ab,c)
for all
a,b,c\in G
and hence

\sum_{j}(gh)_{j}(ji)_{k}=\delta((gh)i,k)=\delta(g(hi),k)=\sum_{j}(hi)_{j}(gj)_{k}.

In this case
\mathbb{S}G
with respect to the given operations is the monoid algebra of
G
over
\mathbb{S}
(typically studied in the case where
\mathbb{S}
is taken to be a field).

Remark 2.3
.

Suppose we are given an operation
G\times G\rightarrow\mathbb{N}G
satisfying the conditions of Theorem
2.1
. Then, for any semiring
\mathbb{S}
we may define a multiplication on
\mathbb{S}G
using the operation
\cdot:G\times G\rightarrow\mathbb{N}G
. Specifically if
\phi
is the unique semiring homomorphism from
\mathbb{N}
to
\mathbb{S}
we may define
\cdot_{\mathbb{S}}:G\times G\to\mathbb{S}G
as
g\cdot_{\mathbb{S}}h=\sum_{j}\phi((g\cdot h)_{j})j
, and it is easy to check that this map also satisfies the conditions of Theorem
2.1
.

.

Taking

to be the set of all finite directed graphs with maximum out-degree at most one, regarded up to isomorphism, the semiring of discrete dynamical systems is then the semiring
\mathbb{N}G
where the product of two graphs is determined by a function

as described in the introduction. For example, with

as in Figure

we find that

is the component (occurring twice up to isomorphism) with three vertices and

is the component (also occurring twice up to isomorphism) consisting of a single vertex. For

the field of two elements, we may also consider the ring
\mathbb{F}_{2}G
, which corresponds to considering all coefficients in

modulo

. For instance, in the example above, we have

2.2
Formal sums over finite semilattices with
\mathbb{F}_{2}

We now focus on the particular case where
G
is a finite semilattice and
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{F}_{2}
, which will be useful for Sections
3
and
4
. In this case, the structure of
\mathbb{S}G
is that of a Boolean algebra.

Let us review some basic notation and terminology for lattices; see the book by Gräzer
[
14
]
for an authoritative review of lattices. Let
\mathcal{L}=(L,\lor,\land,\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1})
be a bounded lattice with top element denoted by
\mathbf{1}
and bottom element denoted by
\mathbf{0}
. We naturally denote
l\leq m
for
l=l\land m
, or equivalently
m=l\lor m
. For any
l\in L
, we denote
l^{\uparrow}=\{m\in L:m\geq l\}
,
l^{+}=\{m:m>l\}
, and
l^{\downarrow}=\{m\in L:m\leq l\}
. Note that
l^{\downarrow}
is also a bounded lattice with top
l
and bottom
0
. For any
l\in L
, let
l^{c}
be the set of elements of
L

covered
by
l
, i.e.
l^{c}=\{m\in L:m<l,\not\exists n:m<n<l\}
. An element
a\in L
is an
atom
of
\mathcal{L}
if
a^{c}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
; we denote the set of atoms of
\mathcal{L}
as
\mathrm{A}(\mathcal{L})
. Recall that for all
l,m\in L
, the
interval

[l,m]
is given by
[l,m]=\{x\in L:l\leq x\leq m\}
; this interval is empty unless
l\leq m
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=(L,\lor,\land,\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1})
be a finite lattice on
n
elements. Then

\mathbb{F}_{2}L=\left\{\sum_{l\in L}a_{l}l:a_{l}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}\ \forall l\in L\right\},

endowed with the addition and product from Example
2.2
, treating
L
as a monoid with respect to meet, forms a Boolean ring. In order to emphasise the distinction between the element
l\in L
and the formal sum
1\cdot l
, we shall refer to the latter as the unit vector
e_{l}
. In turn, the Boolean ring on
\mathbb{F}_{2}L
induces the Boolean algebra
V(\mathcal{L})=(\mathbb{F}_{2}L,\lor_{V(\mathcal{L})},\land_{V(\mathcal{L})},\mathbf{0}_{V(\mathcal{L})},\mathbf{1}_{V(\mathcal{L})})
, where:

•

the meet is given by
a\land_{V(\mathcal{L})}b=ab
,

•

the join is given by
a\lor_{V(\mathcal{L})}b=a+b+ab
,

•

the lower bound is the all-zero vector (empty sum)
\mathbf{0}_{V(\mathcal{L})}=0
,

•

the upper bound is the unit vector
\mathbf{1}_{V(\mathcal{L})}=e_{\mathbf{1}}
,

•

the complement is given by
\neg_{V(\mathcal{L})}a=a+e_{\mathbf{1}}
.

We shall omit the subscript
V(L)
from our notation when it is clear from the context that we are working in
V(\mathcal{L})
.

All finite lattices of the same order yield isomorphic Boolean algebras: if
|L|=n
, then
V(\mathcal{L})
is isomorphic to the power set lattice of
\{1,\dots,n\}
, which of course has
n
atoms
[
11
]
. However,
V(\mathcal{L})
does encapsulate the structure of
\mathcal{L}
, since
e_{l}\leq e_{m}
in
V(\mathcal{L})
if and only if
l\leq m
in
\mathcal{L}
.

Each element of
\mathbb{F}_{2}L
may be identified with its support, that is, the subset of elements of
L
arising with non-zero coefficient. As such, if
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2}L
, we write
l\in a
if
a_{l}=1
, and we denote
\bigvee a=\bigvee_{l\in a}l
; addition
a+b
in
V(\mathcal{L})
then corresponds to the symmetric difference of sets
a\Delta b
.

In Section
3.2
, we shall work in the Boolean algebras
\mathbb{F}_{2}L
for a specific kind of finite lattices arising naturally from odd cycles. It will be very useful to characterise the atoms of those algebras. More generally, in order to perform computations in
\mathbb{F}_{2}L
, determining its atoms is a basic requirement. As such, we construct in Theorem
2.6
below all the atoms of
\mathbb{F}_{2}L
. We begin with a construction.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{D}=(D,\vee,\land,\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{D}},\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{D}})
be a finite lattice. Then there is a unique subset
a^{*}(\mathcal{D})\subseteq D
such that
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{D}}\in a^{*}(\mathcal{D})
and for all
m\in D
with
m\neq\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{D}}
,
a^{*}(\mathcal{D})
contains an even number of elements greater than or equal to
m
.

Proof.

Label the elements of
D
as
d_{1},\dots,d_{n}
such that
d_{i}\geq d_{j}
only if
i\leq j
(in particular,
d_{1}=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{D}}
and
d_{n}=\mathbf{0}_{\mathcal{D}}
). We note that for all
i\geq 2
,
d_{i}^{+}\subseteq\{d_{1},\dots,d_{i-1}\}
. We construct
a^{*}(\mathcal{D})
recursively as follows. Firstly,
d_{1}=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{D}}\in a^{*}(\mathcal{D})
. Then, for all
2\leq i\leq n
,
d_{i}\in a^{*}(\mathcal{D})
if and only if
|d_{i}^{+}\cap a^{*}(\mathcal{D})|
is odd. We note that any other choice will violate either condition of the lemma.
∎

Now for any element
l
of a bounded lattice
L
let us write
a^{l}=a^{*}(l^{\downarrow})
. In particular,
a^{\mathbf{1}}=a^{*}(\mathcal{L})
. Note that (by construction)
l\in a^{l}
and moreover, all elements of
a^{l}
are less than or equal to
l
. Recall that we view subsets of
L
, and hence in particular
a^{l}
, as elements of
\mathbb{F}_{2}L
. Thus
\bigvee a^{l}=l
.

Theorem 2.6
.

The set of atoms of
V(\mathcal{L})
is given by

\mathrm{A}(V(\mathcal{L}))=\{a^{l}:l\in L\}.

We shall see in the proof that
b=a^{l}
is the unique element of
V(\mathcal{L})
satisfying
\bigvee b=l
.
We begin the proof by a simple but useful property of atoms of
V(\mathcal{L})
.

Lemma 2.7
.

A nonzero element
a\in V(\mathcal{L})
is an atom if and only if
e_{l}a\in\{a,0\}
for all
l\in L
.

Proof.

Since
a
is nonzero, it is an atom if and only if
xa\in\{a,0\}
for all
x\in V(\mathcal{L})
. If
a
is an atom, then in particular this holds for any unit vector
e_{l}
. Conversely, if
e_{l}a\in\{a,0\}
for all
l\in L
, then for any
x=\sum_{l}\alpha_{l}e_{l}
we have
xa=\sum_{l}\alpha_{l}(e_{l}a)\in\{a,0\}
.
∎

Corollary 2.8
.

If
a
is an atom of
V(\mathcal{L})
, then
e_{l}a=0
for all
l<\bigvee a
.

Proof.

If
l<\bigvee a
, then
l
cannot be greater than or equal to all elements of
a
, that is there exists
m\in a\setminus l^{\downarrow}
. Recalling that all elements of
e_{l}a
lie in
l^{\downarrow}
we then have
m\notin e_{l}a
and hence
e_{l}a\neq a
; we obtain
e_{l}=0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.6
.

We first prove that if
a
and

are distinct atoms then
\bigvee a\neq\bigvee b
. Arguing for contradiction, suppose
a
and
b
are atoms with

so that
a\Delta b\neq\emptyset
, but
s=\bigvee a=\bigvee b
. Let

be a maximal element of

, i.e.
l
lies in exactly one of
a
or
b
and for any

, either
m\in a\cap b
or
m\notin a\cup b
. We obtain
|l^{+}\cap a|=|l^{+}\cap b|
and hence
|l^{\uparrow}\cap a|=|l^{\uparrow}\cap b|\pm 1
. Thus either
e_{l}a\neq 0
(if
|l^{\uparrow}\cap a|
is odd) or
e_{l}b\neq 0
(if
|l^{\uparrow}\cap a|
is even). On the other hand, since
l<s
, we have
e_{l}a=e_{l}b=0
by Corollary
2.8
, which is the desired contradiction.

We have just proved that the mapping
\mathrm{A}(V(\mathcal{L}))\to L
,
a\mapsto\bigvee a
is injective. Recalling that
V(\mathcal{L})
is isomorphic to the power set lattice of

, we have
|\mathrm{A}(V(\mathcal{L}))|=|L|=n
, this mapping is also surjective, that is, for every
l\in L
, there is an atom of

, say

, such that
\bigvee b=l
. Note that (by assumption) all elements of

must be in

. We will now prove that

.

First, we prove that
l\in b
. For the sake of contradiction, suppose

. Since

, there are at least two distinct maximal elements of

, say

and

. Since

are maximal elements of

and

. Next,

, and so

. From this contradiction, we then conclude that

∎

3
The semiring generated by
C
with
\mathbb{F}_{2}

In this section, we consider “permutations computed modulo
2
.” A permutation is a sum of cycles, where
C_{d}=d\to d-1\to\dots\to 1\to d
is the
cycle
of length
d
for all
d\geq 1
. Therefore, let
C
be the set of cycles and consider
S=\mathbb{F}_{2}C
.

3.1
Basic properties

Clearly
S
is a ring, with multiplicative identity
C_{1}
. In
S
we have
[
5
, Proposition 3]

C_{m}C_{n}={\rm gcd}(m,n)C_{\mathrm{lcm}(m,n)}=\begin{cases}C_{\mathrm{lcm}(m,n)}&\mbox{ if at least one of }m\mbox{ and }n\mbox{ is odd,}\\
0&\mbox{else.}\end{cases}

We begin by gathering some basic facts concerning the structure of
S
. Notation: For a positive integer
n
we shall write
d(n)={\rm max}\{m:2^{m}\mid n\}
. If
d(n)=m
, then
C_{n}=C_{2^{m}}C_{n^{\prime}}
where
n=2^{m}n^{\prime}
and
n^{\prime}
is an odd positive integer.

Let

S_{0}=\left\{\sum_{k\text{ odd}}\alpha_{k}C_{k}\in S:\alpha_{k}\in\{0,1\},\text{ finite number of nonzero }\alpha_{k}\right\},

be the set of elements in
S
where all the cycle lengths are odd.

Then, any element
s\in S
can be uniquely written as

s=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}s_{i}C_{2^{i}},

where
s_{i}\in S_{0}
for all
i
(and there is a finite number of nonzero
s_{i}
’s). We also denote the elements of
S
that only have even cycles as

S_{+}=\left\{\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}s_{i}C_{2^{i}}\in S:s_{0}=0\right\}.

We remark that
S_{+}
is the multiplicative ideal generated by
C_{2},C_{4},C_{8},\dots
.
Then, any element
s\in S
can be uniquely written as
s=s_{0}+s_{+}
, with
s_{0}\in S_{0}
and
s_{+}\in S_{+}
. It is easily seen that for all
s,t\in S

s_{+}t_{+}=0,\quad s^{2}=s_{0}^{2}=s_{0},\quad s_{+}=s+s^{2}.

The next lemma records several facts resulting from these simple observations.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
x,y\in S
.

1.

x
is idempotent if and only if
x
is a sum of odd length cycles.

2.

If
x\neq 0
, then there exists
k\geq 1
such that
x
can be uniquely written as
x=\sum_{i=0}^{k}x_{i}C_{2^{i}}
where each
x_{i}
is idempotent and
x_{k}\neq 0
.

3.

For
x=\sum_{i=0}^{k}x_{i}C_{2^{i}}
, we have
x^{2}=x_{0}
. Consequently, for any
x\in S
we have
x^{4}=x^{2}
.

4.

For
x=\sum_{i}x_{i}C_{2^{i}}
and
y=\sum_{i}y_{i}C_{2^{i}}
, we have
xy=z=\sum_{i}z_{i}C_{2^{i}}
with
z_{0}=x_{0}y_{0}
and
z_{i}=x_{0}y_{i}+x_{i}y_{0}
for all
i\geq 1
.

5.

x
is a unit if and only if
x_{0}=C_{1}
(and in this case
x^{2}=C_{1}
).

6.

x_{0}\leq y_{0}
if and only if
x_{0}+1\geq y_{0}+1
.

Proof.

1.

First suppose that
x
is a sum of odd length cycles only, say
x=C_{i_{1}}+\cdots+C_{i_{k}}
where each
i_{j}
is odd. Then, since we are in characteristic
2
we have:

x^{2}=(C_{i_{1}}+\cdots+C_{i_{k}})^{2}=C_{i_{1}}^{2}+\cdots+C_{i_{k}}^{2}=C_{i_{1}}+\cdots+C_{i_{k}}.

Next, for an arbitrary element
x\in S
we may write
x=x_{0}+x_{+}
where
x_{0}
is a sum of odd cycles only and
x_{+}
is a sum of even cycles only. Since the product of any two even length cycles is 0 it follows that
(x_{+})^{2}=0
and hence that
x^{2}=(x_{0})^{2}+(x_{+})^{2}=x_{0}+0=x_{0}
. Thus
x
is idempotent if and only if
x
is a sum of odd length cycles only.

2.

By definition of
S
we have that
x
may be uniquely written as
x=C_{i_{1}}+C_{i_{2}}+\cdots+C_{i_{t}}
for some
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{t}
. Let
M=\{d(i_{1}),\ldots,d(i_{t})\}
where for each
1\leq j\leq t
we recall that
d(i_{j})
denotes the maximal power of
2
dividing
i_{j}
. For
m\in M
let
I_{m}=\{s:d(i_{s})=m\}
and note that for each
s\in I_{m}
write
i_{s}=2^{m}i^{\prime}_{s}
where
i^{\prime}_{s}
is odd. Then we may reorder the terms in this unique expression as follows:

x=\sum_{m\in M}\sum_{s\in I_{m}}C_{i_{s}}=\sum_{m\in M}\sum_{s\in I_{m}}C_{2^{m}}C_{i^{\prime}_{s}}=\sum_{m\in M}C_{2^{m}}\sum_{s\in I_{m}}C_{i^{\prime}_{s}}.

Setting
x_{m}=\sum_{s\in I_{m}}C_{i^{\prime}_{s}}
it is then clear that each
x_{m}
is a sum of cycles of odd length only, and hence by part (
1
), an idempotent. One can add in terms with
0
coefficients to obtain the required form.

3.

As noted in the proof of part
1
, if
x=x_{0}+x_{+}
where
x_{0}
is a sum of cycles of odd length only and
x_{+}
is a sum of cycles of even length only, then
x^{2}=x_{0}
. Thus
x^{4}=(x^{2})^{2}=(x_{0})^{2}=x_{0}=x^{2}
.

4.

We have
z=(x_{0}+\sum_{i\geq 1}x_{i}C_{2^{i}})(y_{0}+\sum_{j\geq 1}y_{j}C_{2^{j}})
. Since
C_{2^{i}}C_{2^{j}}=0
if
i,j>0
, we obtain
z=x_{0}y_{0}+\sum_{i\geq 1}x_{i}y_{0}C_{2^{i}}+\sum_{j\geq 1}x_{0}y_{j}C_{2^{j}}
.

5.

Suppose that
x
is a unit. Then
xy=C_{1}
for some
y
, and by part (
4
),
x_{0}y_{0}=C_{1}
. Thus,
x_{0}=x_{0}x_{0}y_{0}=x_{0}y_{0}=C_{1}
. Conversely, if
x_{0}=C_{1}
, then
x^{2}=x_{0}=C_{1}
, demonstrating that
x
is unit.

6.

For all
x_{0},y_{0}\in S_{0}
we have
x_{0}+1\leq y_{0}+1
if and only if
(x_{0}+1)(y_{0}+1)=x_{0}y_{0}+x_{0}+y_{0}+1=x_{0}+1
if and only if
x_{0}y_{0}+y_{0}=0
if and only if
x_{0}y_{0}=y_{0}
if and only if
x_{0}\geq y_{0}
.

∎

3.2
Boolean algebra of idempotents

We first note that the set
S_{0}
of idempotent elements forms an infinite Boolean algebra.

Lemma 3.2
.

The set
E(S)=S_{0}
of idempotent elements of
S
forms an atomless Boolean algebra, where for
e,f\in E(S)
we have
e\land f=ef
,
e\lor f=e+f+ef
and
\neg e=C_{1}+e
.

Proof.

Since
S
is commutative, it is clear that a product of idempotents is idempotent. Moreover, since
S
is of characteristic
2
, it is also easy to see that a sum of idempotents is idempotent. Thus
E(S)
is a subring of
S
, and clearly
E(S)
is a Boolean ring. It well known
[
11
, Chapter 2]
that any Boolean ring can be viewed as a Boolean algebra with respect to the given operations.

Let us show that it is atomless. Let
s\in E(S)
be a nonzero element, say
s=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}C_{i}
and let
p
be a prime number that does not divide any cycle length in
s
. Then
t=sC_{p}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}C_{ip}
satisfies
t\notin\{0,s\}
and
t\leq s
(i.e.
ts=t
), hence
s
is not an atom.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

There is a unique (up to isomorphism) countably infinite atomless Boolean algebra, e.g. the interval algebra of the rationals
[
11
, Chapter 16]
. Therefore,
(E(S),\land,\lor,\neg)
is isomorphic to that interval algebra.

Dividing in
S_{0}
.

By using the Boolean algebra structure of
S_{0}
, we can easily solve the equation
ax=b
when
a,b\in S_{0}
only have odd cycle lengths. Note that if
ax=b
with
a,b\in S_{0}
, then necessarily
x\in S_{0}
as well (unless
a=b=0
, in which case any
x\in S
is a solution).

We shall need the general division formula for any Boolean algebra
\mathcal{V}
(see Section
4
, notably Theorem
4.1
and Theorem
4.2
). We remark that in any Boolean algebra,
a\geq b
is equivalent to
b\leq a+b+\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{V}}
; as such, the interval
[b,a+b+\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{V}}]
is non-empty if and only if
ab=b
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a Boolean algebra and
a,b\in\mathcal{V}
. Then the solutions to
ax=b
are the interval
[b,a+b+\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{V}}]
in
\mathcal{V}
.

Proof.

There exists
x
such that
ax=b
if and only if
ab=b
(since
ax=b
implies
b=ax=aax=ab
). Assume then that
ab=b
. If
ax=b
, then by a similar argument as above, we have
xb=b
. Therefore,
x\geq b
and
x(b+a+\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{V}})=b+b+x=x
, hence
x\leq a+b+\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{V}}
. Conversely, if
b\leq x\leq a+b+\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{V}}
, then
(a+b+\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{V}})x=x
and
bx=b
, giving

ax=ax+bx+x+bx+x=(a+b+\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{V}})x+bx+x=b.

∎

Finite case.

We now focus on the finite lattice generated by odd cycles that divide a given length. Let
k
be an odd positive integer, and let
D(k)
denote the set of divisors of
k
. Hence we consider the lattice
\mathcal{C}_{k}=\{C_{i}:i\ |\ k\}
, with the meet operation
C_{i}C_{j}=C_{\mathrm{lcm}(i,j)}
. For instance, the lattice
\mathcal{C}_{15}
is displayed in Figure
3
. The elements of
S
whose cycle lengths divide

then form the lattice
V(\mathcal{C}_{k})
. Theorem
2.6
then gives an abstract expression for its atoms; however, we can be much more explicit, as seen below.

The lattice
\mathcal{C}_{k}

3.3

We now consider the division equation

in

First of all, it is worth noting that in general, there could be an infinite number of solutions, e.g.

For all

denote the set of cycle lengths that appear in

and

.

Now, for any

and

denote the element obtained from

by omitting any cycles

with lengths divisible by

and let

denote the element obtained from

by omitting any cycles

with lengths not divisible by

\displaystyle a_{-n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in Q_{a}:d(q)\leq n}C_{q}=\sum_{i\leq n}a_{i}C_{2^{i}},

\displaystyle a_{|\ k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in Q_{a}:q\ |\ k}C_{q}.

We note that
(a_{-n})_{|\ k}=(a_{|\ k})_{-n}
.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let

, and

be such that
\max(N(a),N(b))\leq n
and
\mathrm{lcm}(K(a),K(b))\ |\ k

a(x_{-n})_{|\ k}=b
.

Proof.

We first note that, by assumption,
a=(a_{-n})_{|\ k}
and
b=(b_{-n})_{|\ k}
. Now, let
x
be a solution to

. The maps

and

are semigroup homomorphisms on the multiplicative semigroup of

, that is: for all

we have

. In particular,

∎

Let

. For all

\displaystyle\upsilon_{i}

\displaystyle=a_{0}b_{i}+a_{i}b_{0}+a_{0}+1.

Lemma 3.10

For any

\displaystyle a_{0}x_{0}

\displaystyle=b_{0}

(4)

\displaystyle a_{i}x_{0}

\displaystyle=b_{i}+b_{i}a_{0}+a_{i}b_{0}\text{ for all }i\geq 1

(7)

3.4
Extended Green’s relations

We next examine the multiplicative structure of
S
. Since
(S,\cdot)
is a commutative semigroup, we have that all of Green’s relations
[
16
]
coincide, that is,
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{J}
. We shall refer to this relation as
\mathcal{R}
in all that follows, recalling that for
a,b\in S
we have
a\mathcal{R}b
if and only if the principal (right) ideal generated by
a
is equal to the principal (right) ideal generated by
b
. The next proposition gives a simple characterisation of this relation, and also the extended Green relations
\mathcal{R}^{*}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
.
Recall that for a monoid
M
, and elements
a,b\in M
we have
a\mathcal{R}^{*}b
if for all
x,y\in M

xa=ya
if and only if
xb=yb
, and
a\mathcal{\tilde{R}}b
if for all idempotents
e
,
ea=a
if and only if
eb=b
.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
a,b\in S
. Then in the multiplicative semigroup of
S
we have:

1.

if and only if
b=ag
for some unit
g
;

2.

a\mathcal{R}^{*}b
if and only if
a^{+}=b^{+}
and
a_{0}=b_{0}
;

3.

a\tilde{\mathcal{R}}b
if and only if
a^{+}=b^{+}
.

Proof.

1.

Let
G
denote the group of units of
S
. By part (4) of Lemma
3.1
,
G=\{g\in S:g_{0}=C_{1}\}
. Clearly if
a=bg
for some
a,b\in S
and
g\in G
we have that
a\mathcal{R}b
. Suppose then that
a\mathcal{R}b
, that is
ax=b
and
by=a
for some

.

We then have
a_{0}x_{0}=b_{0}
and
b_{0}y_{0}=a_{0}
, giving

and
a_{0}\leq b_{0}
and hence
a_{0}=b_{0}
. In particular,
a_{0}x_{0}=a_{0}y_{0}=a_{0}
. Also, since

, in the notation of subsection

a_{+}=(axy)_{+}=a_{0}x_{0}y_{+}+a_{0}x_{+}y_{0}+a_{+}x_{0}y_{0}=a_{0}y_{+}+a_{0}x_{+}+a_{+}x_{0}y_{0}.

In particular,

a_{+}x_{0}=a_{0}x_{0}y_{+}+a_{0}x_{0}x_{+}+a_{+}x_{0}y_{0}=a_{0}y_{+}+a_{0}x_{+}+a_{+}x_{0}y_{0}=a_{+},

and hence

.
Now consider

as required.

2.

Since

is a ring we have

in the multiplicative semigroup of

if and only if, for all

,

if and only if

It is easy to check that the following are equivalent for any idempotent

5
Conclusion and future work

In this paper, we introduced a general framework for semirings of formal sums. We then applied this framework to study
S=\mathbb{F}_{2}C
, where
C
is the set of cycles. We characterise many algebraic properties of
S
, and we solve the division problem efficiently in this ring. This is in stark contrast with the original problem of dividing sums of cycles, for which no polynomial-time algorithm is known. We then extend the study to
\mathbb{F}_{2}(L\cup C)
, i.e. considering sums of cycles and chains, which are the injective partial transformations.

The study of partial transformations, and semigroups of formal sums thereof, has many degrees of freedom.

•

Choice of partial transformations. The approach used in this paper could be extended to more general classes of partial transformations. A natural candidate is the class of transformations of bounded height (the maximum length of a path from a state to a periodic state, also referred to as the pre-period), which form a subsemiring.

•

Choice of semiring
\mathbb{S}
. Choosing the semiring
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{F}_{2}
yielded some strong algebraic properties and dramatic simplifications to some problems (notably the division problem of sums of cycles). Other choices for
\mathbb{S}
may shed different lights on the overall structure of the semiring of partial transformations. Natural examples include the Boolean semiring (which, for the product, is equivalent to working on the so-called power monoid) and other prime fields
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

•

Different problems. Even when restricting to the focus
S=\mathbb{F}_{2}C
of this paper, many interesting questions remain open. These include: the intersection of principal ideals, the polynomial identities, solving more general univariate polynomial equations, solving multivariate equations, etc.
