Fulminant Clostridioides difficile pseudomembranous colitis requiring subtotal colectomy after pancreaticoduodenectomy: A case report and mini-review
Authors: Beata Hemmelová, Ondřej Kráčmar, Jan Resler, Michal Reška, Michaela Bártová, Alena Siváková
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPseudomembranous colitis due to Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) is a serious complication of antibiotic therapy and hospitalization that can progress to toxic megacolon, necessitating emergency colectomy. Patients undergoing pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) are particularly at high risk due to biliary obstruction, recurrent cholangitis, broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure, and prolonged postoperative stays. We report the case of an elderly woman with multiple cardiovascular comorbidities and recent CDI who underwent PD for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). Prior to surgery, she had an endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with duodenobiliary (DB) stent placement due to obstructive jaundice, and biopsies confirmed PDAC. Intravenous cefotaxime was initiated because of signs of acute cholangitis. She subsequently developed CDI and completed treatment with oral vancomycin, which was discontinued only 11 days before the PD. Standard prophylaxis with cefazolin was administered prior to surgery. On postoperative day (POD) 1, empirical antibiotic therapy with cefotaxime was initiated. By POD 3, she developed profuse watery diarrhea, fever, and leukocytosis, with confirmed recurrent CDI. Despite prompt fidaxomicin therapy and intensive care, she rapidly deteriorated. Computed tomography (CT) revealed severe pancolitis and toxic megacolon. Emergency subtotal colectomy with end ileostomy was performed, and histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis. She eventually stabilized but remained frail, and the tumor board recommended best supportive care instead of adjuvant chemotherapy due to the disease stage and the severe course of CDI. This case underscores the need for early CDI testing in postoperative diarrhea following PD and for cautious perioperative antibiotic strategies in patients with recent CDI, as broad-spectrum antibiotics may precipitate fulminant recurrence.Keywords: Pancreaticoduodenectomy; Clostridioides difficile infection; Carcinoma, pancreatic ductal; Pseudomembranous colitis; Antibiotic prophylaxis

INTRODUCTION

Pseudomembranous colitis is a severe form of antibiotic-associated colitis, most commonly caused by Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI). It is characterized by profuse watery diarrhea, abdominal pain, fever, and marked leukocytosis, and may progress to toxic megacolon, perforation, septic shock, and death [1].

Well-established risk factors for CDI and pseudomembranous colitis include major abdominal surgery, exposure to healthcare facilities, use of proton pump inhibitors and H2 blockers, age, a history of CDI, a weakened immune system, malignancies and other systemic diseases, chemotherapeutic and immunosuppressive agents, and the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics. Patients undergoing pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) are particularly at high risk due to factors such as malignant biliary obstruction, preoperative biliary drainage, recurrent cholangitis, multiple courses of antibiotics, and frequent postoperative complications [2,3].

Despite this high-risk profile, pseudomembranous colitis following PD is rarely detailed in the surgical literature, and postoperative diarrhea is often initially attributed to pancreatic fistula, bile leak, delayed gastric emptying, intolerance to enteral nutrition, insufficient exocrine function of the pancreas, or sepsis from other causes [4]. Fulminant CDI with toxic megacolon requiring emergency colectomy in this context is exceptional [5].

We report a case of fulminant pseudomembranous colitis requiring subtotal colectomy in an elderly patient after PD for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) of the pancreatic head. Additionally, we provide a brief review focusing on risk factors, diagnostic pitfalls, and surgical decision-making, with particular emphasis on perioperative antibiotic strategies in a patient with a recent history of CDI. The patient had provided written consent at admission for the use of anonymized data for scientific and research purposes. The manuscript was prepared in full anonymized form.

CASE

An 81-year-old woman in otherwise good biological condition, with a history of arterial hypertension, ischemic heart disease with sick sinus syndrome, and chronic venous insufficiency, was admitted to the internal medicine department in January 2024 with painless obstructive jaundice. An abdominal contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a 2-cm solid, heterogeneous mass in the uncinate process of the pancreas, with irregular dilatation of the main pancreatic duct (up to 13 mm) and marked dilatation of the common bile duct (up to 24 mm). There was no radiological evidence of vascular invasion, lymphadenopathy, or distant metastases (Fig. 1). A chest contrast-enhanced CT scan also showed no lymphadenopathy, distant metastases, or any other lesions.

Contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography scan for tumor staging shows a solid heterogeneous mass in the uncinate process of the pancreatic head (black asterisk), a dilated gallbladder (white asterisk), a dilated common bile duct (black arrow), and a dilated pancreatic duct (white arrow).

The following day, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) was performed, during which a plastic duodenobiliary (DB) stent was placed to bridge the suprapapillary common bile duct stenosis, resulting in a subsequent decrease in bilirubin and cholestatic enzyme levels. Due to clinical and laboratory signs of cholangitis, intravenous cefotaxime was initiated at that time. Brush cytology and biopsy confirmed PDAC.

During her first hospitalization, she developed antibiotic-associated diarrhea; stool testing was positive for Clostridioides difficile antigen and toxins A and B. She completed a full course of treatment with oral vancomycin, resulting in complete clinical resolution. Vancomycin was stopped 11 days before surgery. Therefore, at the time of PD, she had a recent history of hospitalization in a healthcare facility for C. difficile colitis related to third-generation cephalosporin exposure, pancreatic head malignancy, ERCP with biliary stenting, and multiple cardiovascular comorbidities.

At the elective admission for PD, a preoperative physical examination found her to be a frail but hemodynamically stable woman, with no abdominal tenderness, no signs of ongoing infection, and no diarrhea. Laboratory tests showed mild anemia, normal renal function, and normalized inflammatory markers. After a multidisciplinary discussion, she was deemed fit for major surgery (American Society of Anesthesiologists class III).

Pancreaticoduodenectomy and early postoperative course

The patient underwent a standard Whipple PD for PDAC of the pancreatic head. Intraoperatively, the tumor was located in the pancreatic head, involving peripancreatic tissue but without macroscopic distant metastases. Resection and reconstruction were completed without major technical difficulties, and no intraoperative complications occurred. Standard peripancreatic and abdominal drains were placed. Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis consisted of intravenous cefazolin according to institutional protocol.

Histopathological examination of the resection specimen revealed a poorly differentiated (high-grade) ductal adenocarcinoma (G3) of pancreatobiliary type, infiltrating peripancreatic fibroadipose tissue, the duodenal wall, and the common bile duct wall. The resection margins of the pancreas, duodenum, stomach, and bile duct were microscopically free of tumor; however, the tumor invaded the ventral resection margin, corresponding to an R1 resection according to current consensus criteria. One of 21 regional lymph nodes contained metastatic adenocarcinoma (pT2 pN1 M0, stage IIB).

During the perioperative period, the patient remained hemodynamically stable and was extubated without respiratory distress. Postoperative drain output was serous, with no evidence of pancreatic fistula or bile leak. However, considering her age, frailty, comorbidities, and the risk of postoperative infectious complications following PD, she was closely monitored in the intensive care unit (ICU).

On postoperative day (POD) 1, in the context of rising inflammatory markers (Leukocytes 33.1 × 109/L) and concern for early infectious complications, empirical antibiotic therapy was initiated with intravenous cefotaxime at the discretion of the operating surgeon. At that time, cultures of intraoperatively collected bile had not yet been completed. The patient did not experience diarrhea.

Second episode of CDI and development of toxic megacolon

During POD 2 (the day after cefotaxime escalation), the patient developed new-onset watery diarrhea. On POD 3, low-grade fever and increasing leukocytosis (25.7 × 109/L) and C-reactive protein (CRP) (227.8 mg/L) were observed. Pancreatic fistula was ruled out due to a negative amylase level in the abdominal drain output. Intolerance to enteral feeding was not considered, as parenteral nutrition had been started early after the surgery. Empirical antibiotic therapy with oral fidaxomicin was initiated immediately due to concerns about a recurrence of Clostridioides difficile colitis. Stool testing again returned positive for C. difficile antigen and toxins A and B, indicating an early recurrent episode of CDI in the immediate postoperative period. The established antibiotic therapy with oral fidaxomicin was continued, along with bowel rest, meticulous fluid and electrolyte management, and close monitoring in the ICU.

On POD 4, watery diarrhea persisted, and the patient developed abdominal distension with tenderness. The subhepatic drain adjacent to the hepaticojejunal anastomosis yielded cloudy serous fluid, which was collected for microbiological examination. Due to a continued rise in inflammatory markers, with CRP peaking at 328.4 mg/L and suspected concomitant intra-abdominal postoperative complications, intravenous meropenem was empirically added to the ongoing oral fidaxomicin therapy. Due to persistent abdominal distension, colonoscopic decompression was indicated on POD 5. The scope could only be safely advanced to 30 cm, revealing severe pseudomembranous colitis. The procedure was terminated due to the high risk of colonic perforation.

On POD 7, microbiological culture from the abdominal drain was finalized, identifying vancomycin-resistant enterococcus. Intravenous tigecycline was added to the ongoing antibiotic regimen, which included oral fidaxomicin and intravenous meropenem.

Despite these interventions, the patient’s clinical condition deteriorated on POD 9: abdominal distension worsened, bowel sounds diminished, and she developed signs of systemic inflammatory response and incipient septic shock.

CRP was relatively low at 63.6 mg/L, however leucocyte count rose to 44.1 × 103/µL, suggestive of an evolving septic state. A contrast-enhanced abdominal CT was performed, revealing marked circumferential thickening of the colonic wall with submucosal edema and mucosal enhancement, most pronounced in the splenic flexure, suggestive of pancolitis. The colon was significantly dilated, particularly the cecum and ascending colon, measuring approximately 11 × 6.5 cm, with no evidence of free intraperitoneal air (Fig. 2). A moderate amount of free fluid was present in the peritoneal cavity, and there were no signs of mechanical obstruction. These findings were consistent with severe pseudomembranous colitis and toxic megacolon in the context of CDI. Given the rapidly progressive abdominal distension, systemic toxicity, and radiological evidence of toxic megacolon, the decision was made to proceed with emergency laparotomy and subtotal colectomy. This decision was reached in agreement with other specialists, despite the recent major pancreatic resection, as non-operative management was deemed unlikely to succeed, and delay could result in intestinal perforation and death.

Contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography scan, performed due to worsening clinical condition and signs of developing shock, demonstrates a markedly dilated colon, particularly the cecum, with submucosal edema and mucosal enhancement. These findings, visible at the hepatic flexure, are consistent with pancolitis. (A) The transverse image. (B) The coronal image.

Emergency colectomy and histopathology

At laparotomy, the colon was massively dilated and edematous from the cecum to the sigmoid colon, exhibiting a thinned, friable wall and diffuse serosal hyperemia, but without visible perforation. A moderate amount of turbid peritoneal fluid was present, but there were no localized abscesses or anastomotic leaks. The small intestine appeared viable without significant dilation. A subtotal colectomy, including resection of the terminal ileum, was performed, along with the construction of a terminal ileostomy and closure of the rectal stump. Abdominal drains were placed in standard positions, and a right-sided chest drain was inserted due to pleural effusion.

Histopathological examination of the resected colon and terminal ileum revealed thickening of the entire intestinal wall with severe transmural inflammation, extensive mucosal ulcerationwith necrosis, and characteristic pseudomembrane formation consisting of adherent layers of inflammatory cells, bacteria, and fibrin exudate. These findings were consistent with severe pseudomembranous colitis and toxic megacolon. No cellular dysplasia or malignancy was identified, and regional lymph nodes showed only reactive changes.

Post-colectomy course and oncological outcome

Following colectomy, the patient remained in the ICU, requiring vasopressor support and ongoing antibiotic therapy, which included intravenous tigecycline and vancomycin administered via rectal stump infusions. Over the subsequent days, the patient’s hemodynamic status gradually stabilized, inflammatory markers decreased, and lactate levels normalized. The ileostomy became functional with adequate output, and the patient tolerated a progressive reintroduction of enteral nutrition alongside pancreatic enzyme supplementation. No pancreatic leak or intra-abdominal abscess developed.

The subsequent hospital course was complicated by transient pleural effusions that required chest drainage, a urinary tract infection caused by Candida albicans and Klebsiella pneumoniae, and an episode of somnolence and bradypsychia due to a mild traumatic brain injury. A brain CT scan performed for this episode revealed only chronic atrophic and ischemic changes without any acute pathology. Neurological and cardiological evaluations did not identify any new focal deficits or cardiovascular issues requiring intervention.

With intensive physiotherapy and nutritional support, the patient gradually regained strength and mobility while using a walking frame and learned basic ileostomy care with assistance. A multidisciplinary tumor board reviewed the final histopathology, which revealed PDAC of the pancreatic head, classified as pT2 pN1 M0, poorly differentiated G3, with R1 resection. The board concluded that, under standard circumstances, adjuvant chemotherapy would have been indicated. However, considering the patient’s advanced age, comorbidities, and the severe postoperative course complicated by fulminant C. difficile colitis that required subtotal colectomy, the board recommended against initiating adjuvant chemotherapy. The patient was discharged to a rehabilitation facility for continued physiotherapy and stoma care education, with outpatient follow-up scheduled at the surgical and oncology clinics. Subsequent oncological follow-up focused on providing the best supportive care, with planned interval CT scans to reassess the need and feasibility of further systemic therapy.

Oncological follow-up and palliative chemotherapy

During the follow-up in 2025, chest CT scans showed no evidence of metastatic disease. However, abdominal and pelvic CT scans revealed progressive retroperitoneal and intra-abdominal lymphadenopathy, consistent with a diagnosis of locoregional recurrence of pancreatic cancer. In April 2025 (15 months from diagnosis) a multidisciplinary gastrointestinal tumor board recommended palliative systemic therapy. The patient then underwent a total of four cycles of gemcitabine monotherapy, administered via an implanted venous port. An incidental bilateral internal jugular vein thrombosis was detected during port placement and was managed with temporary anticoagulation until ultrasound confirmed recanalization. The first cycle was complicated by grade 2 neutropenia, and after the fourth cycle, she developed decompensated type 2 diabetes mellitus, leading to a treatment interruption. At the most recent oncology visit, considering her multiple complications, borderline performance status (Karnofsky 60%–70%), and stable disease on imaging, a pause in systemic therapy with best supportive care and close surveillance was recommended.

DISCUSSION

CDI is a leading cause of healthcare-associated diarrhea and is strongly linked to recent antibiotic exposure and hospitalization. Postoperative CDI following major abdominal surgery increases morbidity, mortality, length of stay, and overall hospitalization costs, particularly in elderly, polymorbid, and frail patients. Patients undergoing PD are additionally burdened by malignant disease, biliary obstruction, preoperative biliary instrumentation, recurrent cholangitis, repeated courses of broad-spectrum antibiotics, malnutrition, and frequent postoperative complications, all of which further increase the risk of CDI and other infectious events [6].

Over the past three years, our department has identified a total of 50 cases of CDI, with 9 (4.5%) diagnosed in patients with hepatopancreatobiliary (HPB) conditions. Four cases occurred in patients who experienced complicated cholecystectomy with fistula formation or bile duct injury, two cases followed PD, and three followed total pancreatectomy. In two instances of pancreatic resection, patients had a history of prior biliary tract instrumentation and an indwelling DB stent. Overall, among 75 pancreatic operations, CDI developed in 5 patients, with two infections occurring after prior DB stent placement. Only one case, that of the patient presented here, followed a severe course, resulting in pseudomembranous colitis that required subtotal colectomy. In selected patients with preoperative biliary drainage or colonized biliary stents, broader antimicrobial prophylaxis—such as piperacillin–tazobactam—may be justified based on local microbiological patterns and institutional protocols to reduce postoperative infectious complications and surgical site infections [7]. However, particularly in patients at high risk for CDI, especially those with a recent history of CDI, the potential benefit of broader-spectrum prophylaxis must be carefully weighed against the increased risk of recurrent CDI. Any extension beyond standard perioperative prophylaxis should be strictly avoided, as it may precipitate CDI recurrence in susceptible patients.

Our patient presented several classic and procedure-specific risk factors: advanced age, PDAC, biliary stent, recent acute cholangitis treated with cefotaxime, proton pump inhibitor use, and a notable recent history of C. difficile colitis. The first episode of CDI was treated with oral vancomycin, with therapy completed only 11 days prior to the planned procedure. The patient received a prophylactic dose of cefazolin before surgery, and empirical antibiotic therapy with cefotaxime was initiated on POD 1. This constituted a second exposure to a third-generation cephalosporin within a short interval, which may have contributed to recurrence in a patient already carrying multiple established CDI risk factors. This sequence illustrates how repeated exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics in a high-risk patient following HPB surgery may lead to early onset, severe, or even fulminant CDI [8].

CDI and pseudomembranous colitis after major abdominal HPB surgery

Major abdominal surgery is associated with an increased risk of CDI due to the disruption of gut microbiota, perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis, prolonged fasting, and the frequent use of proton pump inhibitors. Postoperative CDI has been reported across various abdominal procedures and is consistently linked to increased morbidity, longer hospital stays, higher overall hospitalization costs, and elevated mortality rates. Fulminant CDI with toxic megacolon is a rare but life-threatening condition that often necessitates emergency colectomy [9].

While CDI has been described following a wide range of abdominal surgical procedures, pancreatic surgery is considered a particularly high-risk setting [10]. In a large national cohort study involving over 76,000 patients undergoing hepatic and pancreatic resections, postoperative CDI occurred in approximately 1.1% of cases, with nearly three-quarters of infections arising after pancreatic resections. Notably, almost half of all CDI episodes developed within the first postoperative week and were associated with prolonged hospitalization and an increased risk of adverse outcomes, highlighting the early postoperative vulnerability of this population [11].

Recent evidence focusing specifically on PD further confirms the clinical significance of this complication. In a large national cohort study of patients undergoing PD for pancreatic cancer, postoperative CDI was linked to significantly worse perioperative outcomes, including increased morbidity and longer hospital stays. The study emphasized that CDI is an independent adverse prognostic factor in this surgical population, reinforcing its role as more than just a benign infectious complication [12].

Additionally, prior retrospective HPB studies have shown that pancreatic resection carries a disproportionate risk of infectious complications compared to other abdominal procedures, particularly when preoperative biliary drainage and biliary stents are involved. Bacteriobilia and multidrug-resistant biliary colonization have been identified as independent risk factors for postoperative infectious morbidity, including surgical site infections and clinically significant pancreatic fistula, reinforcing the idea that this patient population is particularly vulnerable to microbiota-related complications [6,7,13].

However, despite the increasing evidence regarding postoperative CDI following pancreatic surgery, detailed case-level accounts of fulminant recurrent CDI progressing to toxic megacolon—necessitating emergency colectomy shortly after PD—are exceptionally rare. This case, therefore, provides clinically relevant insights by demonstrating how recent CDI, early postoperative exposure to systemic antibiotics, and complex HPB surgical conditions can interact to trigger a rapidly progressive and life-threatening course.

Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis and recurrent CDI risk

Current guidelines for surgical antimicrobial prophylaxis classify PD as a gastroduodenal or upper gastrointestinal procedure. They recommend a single preoperative dose of a first-generation cephalosporin, such as cefazolin, as standard prophylaxis, with repeated administration only in prolonged operations or cases of major blood loss. Extending treatment beyond 24 hours or escalating to broader-spectrum antibiotics does not reduce the incidence of surgical site infections; instead, it is associated with an increased risk of CDI and the emergence of resistant organisms [14,15].

For selected very high-risk patients who have recently experienced CDI and require systemic antibiotic therapy, some authors have proposed secondary prophylaxis with oral vancomycin during systemic antibiotic exposure to reduce recurrences. However, the evidence remains limited, and recommendations are not uniform [13,16]. Our case suggests that patients undergoing PD/HPB surgery who have recently experienced an episode of CDI may belong to this high-risk group.

Recurrent and fulminant Clostridioides difficile infection, Czech Guidelines

Recurrent CDI refers to the return of diarrhea occurring within an eight-week period after the resolution of symptoms and the completion of therapy for a prior CDI episode. Diarrhea is defined as having at least three unformed stools within a 24-hour period [17]. According to the updated Czech Guidelines for the Treatment of CDI, the recommended therapy for a first episode in hospitalized patients is oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin. The first recurrence should be managed with either oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin, followed by fecal microbiota transplantation. In the case of a second or subsequent recurrence, oral vancomycin remains the treatment of choice and should again be followed by fecal microbiota transplantation. Fulminant CDI colitis should be treated with oral fidaxomicin or, when oral administration is not feasible, via alternative intraluminal routes, with the addition of intravenous tigecycline when indicated. In patients with sepsis, intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem should be used instead of tigecycline, in combination with fidaxomicin administered orally or via the same intraluminal routes. In cases refractory to medical management, a subtotal colectomy should be considered [18]. Antibiotic therapy followed the aforementioned updated Czech Guidelines for the treatment of CDI and was conducted in accordance with the recommendations of the local antibiotic center.

Diagnostic challenges after PD

Early postoperative diarrhea after PD is often attributed to causes such as pancreatic exocrine insufficiency, enteral feeding intolerance, pancreatic fistula, biliary leak, or sepsis, particularly in patients already receiving broad-spectrum antibiotics. This can delay the consideration of CDI. In our case, the recurrent nature of CDI, its close temporal relationship to re-exposure to cefotaxime, and the rapid progression to systemic toxicity and toxic megacolon highlight the importance of maintaining a low threshold for CDI testing in any early postoperative diarrhea following PD. This is especially crucial for patients with a recent history of CDI.

CT scans play a key role in differentiating between mechanical obstruction, anastomotic leak, diffuse colitis, and other complications. Typical findings in toxic megacolon include significant dilation and thickening of the colonic wall, accompanied by submucosal edema and mucosal enhancement, as observed in this patient. Although colonoscopy may reveal pseudomembranes, it is often unsafe for unstable patients with severe colitis and a risk of imminent perforation.

Indications for colectomy in fulminant CDI

Guidelines for fulminant CDI recommend early surgical consultation and subtotal colectomy with end ileostomy for patients with toxic megacolon who have perforation or refractory shock despite maximal medical therapy. Delays in operative management are associated with higher mortality rates. Several studies suggest that performing colectomy before the onset of multiorgan failure results in better outcomes [5]. In our patient, the decision to proceed with colectomy was made when CT confirmed toxic megacolon and clinical signs indicated impending septic shock, despite ongoing targeted antibiotic therapy. This timely, proactive, and aggressive intervention likely contributed to her survival [19].

This case underscores the challenging balance between the risks of re-operation in a patient who recently underwent PD and the high mortality associated with fulminant CDI if surgery is postponed. The favorable postoperative recovery after colectomy in this frail elderly woman supports the need for early and aggressive surgical management upon diagnosis of toxic megacolon.

Oncologic implications

From an oncologic perspective, the patient’s final tumor stage (pT2 pN1, R1 resection, G3, stage IIB) typically indicates the need for adjuvant chemotherapy. However, the severe postoperative infectious course, the necessity for subtotal colectomy, and the patient’s subsequent frailty prompted the multidisciplinary tumor board to recommend best supportive care instead of adjuvant therapy. During follow-up, the patient developed radiologic evidence of locoregional recurrence, characterized by progressive retroperitoneal and intra-abdominal lymphadenopathy, while a chest CT scan showed no distant metastases. The patient received four cycles of palliative gemcitabine chemotherapy, which were complicated by grade 2 neutropenia, decompensation of type 2 diabetes, and bilateral internal jugular vein thrombosis. Following these complications, systemic therapy was paused in favor of best supportive care and imaging surveillance. According to Tummers et al. [20], the overall median survival after R1 resection for PDAC is 15 months (95% confidence interval [CI], 13–17), while patients with N1 disease, primarily corresponding to stage IIB, have a median overall survival of 14 months (95% CI, 12–16). In patients who received neoadjuvant treatment, Klaiber et al. [21] reported a median overall survival of 15.3 months after R1 resection. Teske et al. [22] found that in patients with N1 disease and R1 resection, the median overall survival was 13.6 months (95% CI, 10.7–18). Our patient survived 23.6 months after resection, despite a complicated postoperative course that required subtotal colectomy, which is notably longer than the reported median survival of 14–15 months for patients with N1 disease and R1 resection. This case illustrates that severe CDI and its surgical consequences may effectively preclude optimal oncologic therapy in high-risk pancreatic cancer patients. It also underscores the importance of rigorous CDI prevention and antibiotic stewardship strategies in this context, as these measures are crucial for reducing the risk of complications that could limit cancer treatment options.

Practical implications for HPB surgeons

Based on this case and available literature, several practical considerations may be relevant for HPB surgeons managing patients with recent CDI history.

1) Postoperative diarrhea after PD should prompt early CDI testing, particularly after recent antibiotic exposure.

2) Unnecessary prolongation or escalation of postoperative antibiotics should be avoided whenever possible.

3) Patients with preoperative biliary stents or recent cholangitis require careful balancing between infectious risk and CDI recurrence risk.

4) Early multidisciplinary collaboration involving surgeons, intensivists, infectious disease specialists and microbiologists is essential in rapidly deteriorating patients.

5) Early surgical consultation should be considered in fulminant CDI because delayed colectomy may worsen outcomes.

In conclusion, although CDI after major abdominal surgery is well recognized, fulminant recurrent CDI that progresses to toxic megacolon requiring emergency colectomy shortly after PD has been described in detail only rarely. In elderly, comorbid patients with recent CDI, biliary tract instrumentation, and repeated exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics, any early postoperative diarrhea should prompt testing for C. difficile and careful reassessment of antibiotic therapy. Perioperative antibiotic strategies should prioritize a single dose of narrow-spectrum, guideline-concordant prophylaxis and minimize prolonged use and postoperative exposure to broad-spectrum antibioticsto reduce the risk of recurrent, potentially fulminant CDI. Early recognition of toxic megacolon and timely subtotal colectomy can be life-saving, but may complicate or constrain subsequent oncological management, as altered bowel anatomy and stoma formation can worsen functional outcomes and tolerance of multimodal treatment.
