Temporal dynamics of rhizosphere microbiome assembly and carbon-phosphorus coupling in poplar-medicinal plant intercropping systems
Authors: Chunlong Wang, Shitong Li, Yiwo Liu, Xinyue Zhao, Fusen Wang, Yimin You, Xiyang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntercropping can reshape the rhizosphere microbiome, but how specific companion plants influence nutrient cycling and host growth remains unclear. We proposed that intercropping poplar with medicinal plants creates distinct rhizosphere niches that select for microbial communities with distinct​ functional potential, thereby improving tree nutrition.ResultsIntercropping significantly promoted poplar growth, with increases in diameter at breast height (DBH) of 15.33%, 14.3%, and 15.23% in systems with Anemarrhena asphodeloides, Belamcanda chinensis, and Saposhnikovia divaricata, respectively. Intercropping did not change microbial alpha diversity but led to plant-specific shifts in beta diversity with clear seasonal dynamics. Metagenomic analyses revealed corresponding shifts in the functional potential of microbial communities related to carbon (C) and phosphorus (P) cycling, including genes such as frdC, aldB, ppk2, and phnH. Intercropping, particularly with S. divaricata, was associated with an increased genetic potential for microbial C metabolism and​ a heightened potential for P solubilization. These co-occurring shifts in genetic potential were correlated with greater P accumulation in poplar leaves. Network analysis showed distinct temporal microbial co-occurrence patterns across intercropping treatments, with A. asphodeloides supporting the most interconnected community linked to P mobilization. Three bacterial genera (Priestia, Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter) were strongly associated with key soil nutrient pools. Re-inoculation experiments confirmed their functional roles: Priestia sp. increased N and P retention in the rhizosphere; Pseudomonas sp. promoted plant growth, suggesting a role in​ stimulating plant secondary metabolism; and Acinetobacter sp. enhanced organic C mineralization.ConclusionsIntercropping with specific medicinal plants structures the rhizosphere microbiome through niche differentiation. This restructuring leads to distinct patterns of microbial functional potential, centered on C and P metabolism, which correlate with improved poplar nutrient acquisition and growth. Our findings, integrating metagenomic inference with experimental validation, provide a framework for selecting companion plants to steer the rhizosphere microbiome toward beneficial functional outcomes in agroforestry systems. Introduction

Sustainable agroforestry systems are increasingly important for improving land productivity while maintaining ecological stability [1]. Intercropping, the cultivation of two or more plant species on the same land, is a proven strategy for improving resource-use efficiency and enhancing ecosystem functions [2]. In China and elsewhere, poplar (Populus spp.) plantations intercropped with medicinal plants offer combined benefits, including timber production, medicinal yields, soil conservation, and C storage [3]. Commonly used medicinal species in these systems include Anemarrhena asphodeloides, Belamcanda chinensis, and Saposhnikovia divaricata. These species are selected not only for their economic value but also for their distinct functional traits—such as contrasting root architectures (e.g., deep vs. shallow rooting) and diverse root exudate profiles rich in specific secondary metabolites—that may create unique rhizosphere niches [4, 5]. Although the agronomic and ecological advantages of poplar–medicinal intercropping are recognized, the precise belowground mechanisms, particularly how the distinct functional traits of different companion plants differentially drive rhizosphere microbiome assembly and function over time, remain insufficiently understood.

Previous assessments of intercropping effects have focused primarily on aboveground interactions and interspecific root competition [6]. Some studies have reported altered soil chemical properties under poplar–medicinal intercropping, including increased organic C and available P [7]. Critically, the rhizosphere microbiome, a key driver of nutrient cycling, is shaped by a dynamic, co-evolutionary dialogue with plant roots, where seasonal shifts in plant physiology and exudation drive microbial succession [8–10]. While microbiome composition and function are known to be influenced by plant identity [11, 12], most studies in agroforestry contexts are snapshots in time, leaving a gap in understanding the temporal dynamics of microbiome assembly and its functional consequences in multi-species systems [10, 13, 14]. Specifically, for poplar–medicinal plant systems, it is unclear: (i) how different medicinal species, with their unique functional traits, steer the seasonal succession of rhizosphere microbial communities; (ii) whether these community shifts translate into specific and potential changes in microbial functional potential, particularly for coupled processes like C and P cycling; and (iii) how these temporally structured microbial enhance nutrient availability to benefit poplar growth [15]. Resolving these questions requires moving beyond correlative surveys to integrate temporal sampling with functional genomic and causal validation approaches.

We hypothesized that intercropping with medicinal plants would promote the assembly of distinct rhizosphere microbial communities that undergo plant-specific seasonal succession. These shifts were expected to differentially reshape the functional potential of the microbiome, particularly by altering the co-occurrence or association between microbial C metabolism and P solubilization genes [16, 17]. In turn, this temporally patterned and microbially mediated reprogramming of nutrient cycling should enhance rhizosphere nutrient bioavailability, thereby supporting greater nutrient acquisition and biomass accumulation in poplar. To test this hypothesis, we combined field-based seasonal sampling with controlled re-inoculation experiments. Our objectives were: (i) to characterize plant-specific and seasonal dynamics in rhizosphere microbial community composition across a poplar monoculture and three poplar–medicinal intercropping systems; (ii) to quantify the abundance of functional genes related to C, N, and P cycling in each system across seasons; (iii) to construct time-resolved microbial co-occurrence networks to identify intercropping-specific microbial modules; and (iv) to use regression analysis and microbiome re-inoculation to determine the contributions of key microbial taxa to soil nutrient pools and poplar nutrient uptake. By integrating temporal patterns, metagenomic functional potential, network structure, and causal validation, this study provides an integrative and time-explicit framework for understanding how medicinal plant identity shapes rhizosphere microbiomes to enhance nutrient cycling and promote poplar growth.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and design

This study was conducted at the Xinjiang Experimental Forest Farm in Qiqihar, Heilongjiang Province, China, located in the Songnen Plain (124°22′30″–124° 30′ 00″ E, 47°58′30″–48° 05′ 00″ N). The region has a continental monsoon climate, with a mean annual temperature of 0.7 °C, maximum and minimum recorded temperatures of 36.5 °C and − 39 °C, respectively. The average annual precipitation is 430 mm, mostly in July and August. Spring is typically dry and windy, autumn is characterized by early frosts, and winters are long and cold. The frost-free period lasts 126–130 days, with approximately 2,715 h of annual sunshine. The dominant soil type is sandy soil. The soil nutrient content of the study site was as follows: soil organic carbon (SOC) was 7.85 g/kg, soil total nitrogen (TN) was 0.11%, soil total phosphorus (TP) was 0.31 g/kg, and soil total potassium (TK) was 8 g/kg. An existing 8-year-old poplar (Populus × xiaohei) plantation was used for the intercropping experiment. Medicinal plants were established by seed sowing in spring 2022. The medicinal plants were sown in rows spaced 0.5 m apart, with an intra-row plant spacing of approximately 0.3 m after thinning. The rows of medicinal plants were established 1.5 m from the poplar tree rows to minimize direct root competition and allow for machinery access. No chemical fertilizers or pesticides were applied to either the poplar trees or the medicinal plants during the entire experimental period to assess the natural belowground interactions. Four treatments were included: (i) poplar monoculture (Control); (ii) poplar intercropped with B. chinensis; (iii) poplar intercropped with A. asphodeloides; and (iv) poplar intercropped with S. divaricata. To account for spatial heterogeneity, three blocks were arranged along a gradient across the plantation. Within each block, the four treatments were randomly assigned to four adjacent plots using a random number generator. To minimize spatial spillover effects (e.g., root or nutrient interaction) between treatments, a 5-m-wide buffer zone, planted with grassland vegetation, was maintained between adjacent plots. This resulted in one 30 m × 30 m plot for each treatment per block, three replicates (field replicates) per treatment.

Soil and plant sample collection

After three years of intercropping (sampling year: 2024), soil and plant samples were collected during the poplar growing season at three phenological stages: early (May, corresponding to the leaf expansion stage), mid (July, corresponding to the peak growth and canopy closure stage), and late season (August, corresponding to the late summer/early senescence stage). At each time point and for each treatment, we collected rhizosphere soil, bulk soil, fine roots, and leaves. Bulk soil was sampled from the top 0–20 cm using a 35-mm diameter soil auger. To avoid the direct influence of tree trunks and major roots, and to standardize sampling across all treatments, bulk soil cores were taken at locations more than 1 m from any poplar trunk within the central area of each plot. For rhizosphere soil and root sampling, fine, absorptive poplar roots (diameter < 2 mm) from the top 20 cm soil layer were targeted. Within each plot, five healthy poplar trees were randomly selected, avoiding the plot edges to minimize edge effects. From each selected tree, one representative fine root segment (approximately 10–15 cm in length) was excavated from the inter-row space adjacent to the medicinal plant rows (for intercropping treatments) or from a similar location (for monoculture). Loosely attached soil was removed by shaking, and soil adhering within approximately 2 mm of the root surface was collected by brushing. The cleaned roots were retained for analysis. Fully expanded leaves were sampled from healthy lower-canopy, south-facing branches.

Three replicate plots per treatment were sampled per time point. Within each plot, the five sub-samples (one per tree) of each sample type (rhizosphere soil, bulk soil, fine roots, leaves) were thoroughly homogenized to form one composite sample per plot per time point, which served as the independent experimental unit for statistical analysis. All samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid N and stored for subsequent analyses. Sample naming and grouping are summarized in Table 1. Metagenomics analysis​

DNA extraction and quality assessment

Total genomic DNA was extracted from 0.5 g of each soil or root sample using the FastDNA Spin Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA purity was assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and DNA concentration was quantified using a TBS-380 fluorometer (Turner BioSystems, USA). DNA integrity was verified by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel at 5 V/cm for 20 min.

Library preparation

Extracted DNA was sheared to approximately 400 bp using a Covaris M220 ultrasonicator (Covaris, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared with the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit (PerkinElmer, USA). Adapter ligation and magnetic bead-based purification were followed by PCR enrichment and a second bead purification step to obtain the final libraries.

Metagenomic sequencing and analysis

Sequencing libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA). An average of 6 Gb of raw data (approximately 67 million read pairs) was generated per sample to ensure sufficient depth for downstream microbial community and functional gene analysis. Following quality control, an average of 44.0 ± 3.8 million high-quality read pairs (6.6 ± 0.6 Gb) were retained per sample, representing 98.8% ± 0.3% of the raw data (detailed per-sample statistics are provided in Supplementary Table S1). The pooled high-quality reads from all samples were co-assembled, yielding 2.1 million contigs with an N50 of 2,154 bp. The average mapping efficiency of clean reads from each sample to the non-redundant gene catalog was 85.4% ± 3.2%. These metrics confirm the high quality, depth, and comparability of the metagenomic dataset across all treatments.​

Raw reads were assessed for quality with FastQC and subjected to standard quality filtering. Clean reads were assembled using MEGAHIT v1.2.9, and open reading frames were predicted with Prodigal v2.6.3. A non-redundant gene catalog was constructed using CD-HIT v4.8.1. Gene abundance was estimated by mapping clean reads to the gene catalog with Bowtie2 v2.4.5. Taxonomic annotation was performed against the NR database, and functional annotation was conducted using KEGG and eggNOG v5.0. Detailed parameter settings are provided in Supplementary Material 1. For details on the quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) quantification method and specific experimental procedures for functional genes in this study, please refer to Supplementary Material 1.

Determination of nutrients and enzyme activities

SOC was determined using the modified Walkley–Black method [18]. Briefly, 2 g of air-dried soil (100 mesh) was digested with potassium dichromate–sulfuric acid at 180 °C in an oil bath for 5 min, and the remaining dichromate was titrated with FeSO₄ to calculate SOC content. Available P (AP) was measured from 5 g of air-dried soil (10 mesh) extracted with 50 mL of 0.5 M NaHCO₃ (pH 8.5) [19]. ​TP was quantified by digesting 2 g of soil in a nickel crucible with 2.0 g NaOH and fusing the mixture at 750 °C. For both AP and TP, extracts were reacted with ammonium molybdate and potassium antimonyl tartrate to form phosphomolybdenum blue, and absorbance was measured at 880 nm. NH₄⁺ and NO₃⁻ were extracted with 2 M KCl and measured using a CleverChem ONE continuous flow spectrophotometer (DeChem Tech GmbH, Hamburg, Germany) [20]. TC and TN were determined using a Vario EL III elemental analyzer (Elementar, Germany) ​​[21]. Lignin content was determined following the Klason procedure, involving sequential hydrolysis with 72% and then 3% H₂SO₄, with the acid-insoluble residue quantified gravimetrically [22].

Microbial biomass C (MBC) and N (MBN) were quantified using the chloroform fumigation–extraction method. Samples were fumigated with ethanol-free CHCl₃ for 24 h, extracted with 0.5 M K₂SO₄, and analyzed on a TOC analyzer; biomass values were calculated as the difference between fumigated and unfumigated samples. Microbial biomass P (MBP) was determined by fumigation followed by extraction with 0.5 M NaHCO₃ (pH 8.5) and quantified colorimetrically based on the phosphomolybdenum blue reaction [23].

Activities of acid phosphatase (ACP), β-glucosidase (β-GC), dehydrogenase (DHA), sucrase (SC), leucine aminopeptidase (LAP), and phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) were measured using commercial assay kits (Beijing Boxbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Isolation and identification of rhizosphere soil microbial strains

Bacterial isolation and purification

Five grams of rhizosphere soil were suspended in 95 mL of sterile 10 mM MgCl₂ containing sterilized glass beads in a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask. The suspension was shaken at 180 rpm at 28 °C for 2 h and then allowed to settle for 30 min. Serial ten-fold dilutions (10⁻1 to 10⁻6) were prepared from the supernatant under aseptic conditions. Aliquots (100 μL) from each dilution were spread, in triplicate, onto LB agar (10 g/L NaCl, 10 g/L peptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, pH 7.0), one-tenth strength TSA (1.5 g/L tryptone, 0.5 g/L soytone, 0.5 g/L NaCl, 15 g/L agar, pH 7.0), and half-strength TSA (7.5 g/L tryptone, 2.5 g/L soytone, 2.5 g/L NaCl, 15 g/L agar, pH 7.0). Plates were incubated inverted at 28 °C for 1–3 days. Colonies displaying distinct morphology were repeatedly re-streaked on the same medium until axenic cultures were obtained.

Strain sequencing and identification

Single colonies were suspended in sterile ultrapure water under aseptic conditions and used as PCR templates. Amplification of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was carried out using Premix Taq™ and universal primers 27 F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-CTACGGCTACCTTGTTACGA-3′). Reaction mixtures were briefly centrifuged (30–60 s) before thermal cycling. PCR amplification was performed in a standard thermocycler (run time ~ 2 h). Amplification products were sequenced commercially (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), and resulting sequences were compared to the NCBI GenBank database using BLASTn to determine taxonomic identity. The PCR reaction system and thermal cycling parameters are provided in Tables S2 and S3.

Linkage to metagenomic data and selection criteria for functional strains

The primary objective of the cultivation approach was not to fully capture the in situ diversity but to obtain tractable, culturable representatives of key functional groups suggested by the culture-independent metagenomic data. To ensure ecological relevance, the taxonomic identity of all isolates was cross-referenced with the metagenomic dataset. The three strains selected for downstream inoculation experiments—Priestia sp. ASI7, Pseudomonas sp. JDBD4, and Acinetobacter sp. BDH12—were explicitly chosen because their corresponding genera (Priestia, Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter) were identified as significantly enriched in the rhizosphere of intercropping systems in our metagenomic differential abundance analysis (see Results, Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. S7). This confirms that these isolates originate from taxonomic groups that actively respond to the intercropping treatment. The selection criteria were: (1) taxonomic affiliation matching a metagenomically enriched genus; (2) robust growth and ease of maintenance in pure culture; and (3) for Pseudomonas sp. and Acinetobacter sp., their classification within genera widely reported in literature as harboring PGPR with phosphate-solubilizing potential. While 16S rRNA gene sequencing provides reliable genus-level identification, we acknowledge its limitation for precise species delineation in complex genera. Therefore, taxonomy is conservatively reported at the genus level, with functional inferences based on well-documented traits of their respective genera.

Pot experiment

The pot experiment was conducted in a greenhouse at Jilin Agricultural University, Jilin Province, China (43°82′N, 125°41′E). Cuttings of Populus × xiaohei with uniform stem diameter were selected and planted in nutrient pots. The growth substrate consisted of black soil, perlite, and vermiculite mixed at a ratio of 6:1:2 (v/v/v) and was sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 1 h. Approximately 1.5–2 kg of the sterilized substrate was placed into each pot, after which the poplar cuttings were planted and maintained under greenhouse conditions. The use of sterilized substrate was intended to eliminate the confounding effects of the native soil microbiome, thereby allowing for a clearer attribution of any observed effects to the introduced bacterial strains. We acknowledge that this approach reduces ecological realism and that interactions with a complex indigenous microbial community in the field may modulate the observed effects. However, this controlled setup provides essential proof-of-concept data on the intrinsic plant growth-promoting potential of the selected strains.

To minimize the impact of environmental gradients within the greenhouse (e.g., light, temperature, humidity), a completely randomized block design was employed. Pots representing all four treatments were grouped into blocks, with each block positioned to account for potential spatial variation. The entire set of pots was re-randomized within the greenhouse every week throughout the experiment. The greenhouse maintained an average daytime temperature of 25 ± 3 °C, a nighttime temperature of 18 ± 2 °C, a relative humidity of 60–70%, and a natural light cycle supplemented with artificial lighting to ensure a minimum 14-h photoperiod.

Three bacterial strains previously isolated and identified (Priestia sp. ASI7, Pseudomonas sp. JDBD4, and Acinetobacter sp. BDH12) were used for growth promotion assays. Four treatments were established: control (CK, no bacterial inoculation), inoculation with Priestia sp. ASI7, inoculation with Pseudomonas sp. JDBD4, and inoculation with Acinetobacter sp. BDH12. Each treatment included 20 biological replicates. After the cuttings were established, bacterial inoculations were applied on the 4th, 19th, 34th, and 49th days after planting. This inoculation schedule was designed based on common practices in similar PGPR studies and aimed to establish and maintain a sufficient population of introduced bacteria in the rhizosphere during the early and active growth phases of the poplar cuttings. It was hypothesized that repeated inoculations would enhance colonization and persistence. Each strain was cultivated in liquid LB medium on a rotary shaker at 28 °C and 150 rpm for 24 h. Viable cell concentrations met the Chinese national standard for agricultural microbial inoculants (GB 20287–2006), requiring at least 2.0 × 108 CFU/mL. Specific viable counts and inoculation volumes are listed in Table S5.

For each inoculation event, the bacterial suspensions were diluted 50 mL in sterile ultrapure water. Prior to application, the substrate surface moisture was standardized by pre-watering all pots to approximately 60% of the substrate’s water-holding capacity 24 h earlier. Then, 50 mL of the diluted bacterial suspension (or sterile ultrapure water for the control) was applied evenly to the soil surface in the immediate vicinity of the plant stem, ensuring direct delivery to the rhizosphere zone. Care was taken to avoid runoff. Control plants received an equal volume of sterile ultrapure water. Sampling was performed 15 days after the final inoculation. To capture developed plant growth responses after bacterial establishment while avoiding interference from recent inoculation disturbance, this time point was selected. This aligns with preliminary observations and common timeframes in short‑term PGPR bioassays.

Determination of poplar growth parameters

Branch diameter was measured 1 cm above the soil surface using a vernier caliper. The length and width of the third, fourth, and fifth fully expanded leaves from the shoot apex were recorded, and leaf area was calculated using ImageJ software (v1.53q). Irrigation was stopped three days prior to sampling. Plants were carefully removed from the pots. Soil adhering to the roots was collected as rhizosphere soil, while soil detached by gentle shaking was collected as bulk soil. Both rhizosphere and bulk soil samples were immediately sealed in zip-lock bags and stored at − 80 °C. Roots were then rinsed three times with sterile ultrapure water to remove any remaining soil.

Plant height was measured using a steel ruler (0.1 cm precision), recording the vertical distance from the soil surface to the shoot apex. Fresh biomass of aboveground and belowground tissues was measured separately using an analytical balance (0.0001 g precision), and total biomass was calculated as their sum.

Soil NH₄⁺, NO₃⁻, AP, SOC, MBP, MBC, MBN, and the activities of ACP, β-GC, and PAL were determined as described in Section “Determination of nutrients and enzyme activities”.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel 2010, IBM SPSS Statistics v26.0, and R v4.3.2. Data organization and calculation of descriptive statistics (mean and standard error) were performed in Microsoft Excel. For the field experiment, the effects of intercropping treatment and phenological stage on soil and plant traits were analyzed in SPSS. Two-way ANOVA was used where appropriate. When significant main effects or interactions were detected (p < 0.05), Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test was applied for post hoc comparisons. Data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test. For the pot experiment, treatment effects were assessed using one-way ANOVA in SPSS. When data did not meet parametric assumptions after transformation, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was used, followed by Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

Microbial community analyses were performed in R. Alpha diversity indices were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test (vegan package). Beta diversity was evaluated by principal component analysis (PCA) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity. To account for the non-Euclidean nature of Bray–Curtis dissimilarities, the community composition data were first subjected to Hellinger transformation, which makes the data suitable for Euclidean-based ordination methods such as PCA.​ Differences in community composition among treatments were tested using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with the adonis2 function in vegan (999 permutations).

Microbial co-occurrence networks were constructed separately for each major treatment group. For each network, pairwise Spearman correlations between ASVs (amplicon sequence variants) were calculated. Only robust correlations with a Spearman’s |ρ|> 0.6 and a p-value < 0.01 were retained for network construction. To account for multiple testing, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction; edges with an FDR-adjusted p-value (q-value) > 0.05 were removed. To reduce sparsity and focus on strong associations, ASVs with a prevalence < 10% across samples were filtered out prior to correlation analysis. The resulting correlation matrices were imported into Gephi (version 0.10) for visualization and calculation of global network properties (e.g., average degree, modularity). The significance of the observed network topology was assessed by comparison to 100 random networks of identical size and edge distribution generated using the Erdős–Rényi null model.

Spearman correlation matrices were generated and visualized using the corrplot package. Redundancy analysis (RDA) relating community composition to environmental variables was also conducted in vegan. Figures and visualizations were produced using ggplot2. All data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE), and statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Temporal dynamics of the rhizosphere microbiome in intercropping systems

Alpha diversity indices (Chao1, Sobs, Shannon, Simpson) did not differ significantly among intercropping treatments for either poplar or its companion medicinal plants (Fig. 1A; Figure S1). In contrast, beta diversity analysis showed clear treatment-related shifts in microbial community composition. PCA indicated that the rhizosphere communities in the S. divaricata intercropping system differed significantly from the corresponding poplar rhizosphere (P = 0.003). This structural distinction coincides with the identification of SOC as the environmental variable most strongly correlated with the abundance of carbon and phosphorus cycling genes across all systems (Fig. 3D). This correlation suggests a potential link between the unique community assembly in this system and the soil carbon dynamics highlighted in Fig. 3D. Seasonal comparisons revealed distinct temporal dynamics. In May, medicinal plant rhizosphere communities differed significantly among intercropping treatments (P = 0.045). By contrast, divergence among poplar rhizosphere communities became more pronounced later in the growing season, with significant differentiation observed in August (P = 0.049), indicating a temporal shift in community structure that coincided with the later growth stages (Fig. 1B).

At the phylum level, poplar and medicinal plant rhizospheres shared dominant bacterial groups including Actinomycetota, Pseudomonadota, and Acidobacteriota (Figs. S2–S5). However, genus-level composition varied substantially across treatments and sampling periods. In the S. divaricata rhizosphere, Mycobacterium and Mycolicibacterium were enriched in May, Ktedonobacter remained consistently enriched from May to July, and Pseudonocardia, Jatrophihabitans, Arthrobacter, and Priestia became dominant in August (Fig. S7). In the B. chinensis rhizosphere, Pedosphaera and Pseudomonas were enriched during the later growth stages (July–August) (Fig. S7). Poplar rhizosphere communities also showed treatment- and time-specific shifts. Intercropping with B. chinensis enriched Solirubrobacter, Acinetobacter, and Klebsiella in August, while intercropping with S. divaricata enriched Nitrospira in May and Friedmanniella in July (Fig. 2 and Fig. S6). Both B. chinensis and S. divaricata intercropping systems were associated with Rubrobacter enrichment in July (Fig. S6). Mediation of soil nutrient cycling by intercropping

Intercropping was associated with changes in the abundance of microbial functional genes associated with C, N, and P cycling. These changes corresponded to distinct, time-dependent nutrient cycling pathways across the different medicinal plant rhizospheres (Figure S8; Tables S6–S10). In the B. chinensis system, the concurrent increase of the C fixation gene tfrB and the C oxidation gene aspB from May to August (Fig. 3A) was observed. This trend co-occurred with elevated organic C and nitrate levels in the poplar rhizosphere (Table S7). In the A. asphodeloides rhizosphere, increased gene abundance of frmB and CHS1 in August indicated a seasonal shift in C cycling genes (Fig. 3A). This coincided with increased available P in the bulk soil (Table S6). The S. divaricata system showed a distinct seasonal pattern in gene abundance, with early-season enrichment of the C fixation gene frdC in May followed by increased abundance of the C oxidation gene aldB in August (Fig. 3A). Differences among systems extended to N and P transformation. In the S. divaricata rhizosphere, increasing gene abundance of the N assimilation gene nasB was associated with markedly low nitrate levels (Fig. 3B; Table S7). Although the P mineralization gene pgnH peaked in August, soil TP remained stable while poplar root TP increased (Fig. 3C; Table S8). In contrast, the B. chinensis system showed declining gene abundance of the phosphate degradation gene ppk2, despite increasing C fixation capacity. This gene pattern corresponded with a pronounced increase in DHA activity relative to a more modest rise in ACP activity (Tables S7 and S9). In the A. asphodeloides system, low LAP activity was associated with relatively low poplar leaf N content (Tables S8–S10). However, enhanced P mobilization in the poplar rhizosphere co-occurred with increased leaf total P. Under A. asphodeloides intercropping, the organic C oxidation gene ABAT peaked in July, while the C degradation gene NEG1 declined consistently across systems (Fig. S9). Enzymatic activity patterns were likewise system specific: SC activity peaked under S. divaricata intercropping (Table S10), while the B. chinensis system enhanced both ACP and DHA activities, alongside higher MBP and NO₃⁻ accumulation (Tables S7 and S9). Poplar rhizosphere responses mirrored these system-specific pathways. The qPCR quantification showed that the relative abundance trends of three functional genes (phnH, frdC, and nasB) in different medicinal plant rhizosphere soils were largely consistent with metagenomic sequencing results. Moreover, the inter‑group differences revealed by qPCR were more pronounced, providing additional support for the metagenomic trends (Fig. S10).

Mantel tests identified SOC as the strongest correlate​ of nutrient cycling gene abundance across all intercropping systems (Fig. 3D and Fig. S9). The S. divaricata system exhibited the highest SOC content (Table S6), which aligned with the co-variation of C and P metrics described above. The A. asphodeloides system showed increased lignin accumulation in poplar roots linked to enhanced rhizosphere P activation (Table S8). In contrast, the B. chinensis system exhibited high available P but low poplar root P accumulation, along with reduced ppk2 gene abundance, showing a contrasting pattern between soil AP, a P-cycling gene, and plant P content (Fig. 3C; Table S8).

Intercropping-specific co-occurrence network structure and modularity

Microbial co-occurrence network structure in medicinal plant rhizospheres

Microbial interaction networks in the medicinal plant rhizospheres showed distinct system- and time-dependent structural patterns (Figs. 4 and S11). In the A. asphodeloides rhizosphere (ARS), network complexity was highest in May, with both the total number of edges and the average node degree exceeding those of the B. chinensis (BRS) and S. divaricata (SRS) systems during the same period (Fig. 4A; Table S12). By August (ARS8), the proportion of positive correlations increased to 63.05%, the highest among all samples. In the B. chinensis system, network density remained relatively stable across the season. However, modularity decreased to 0.402 in August (BRS8). The S. divaricata system exhibited the most pronounced structural transitions. Intercropping effects on microbial interaction networks in the poplar rhizosphere

Microbial co-occurrence networks in the poplar rhizosphere showed distinct responses to companion plant identity and seasonal progression (Fig. S12). In the S. divaricata intercropping system, network complexity was highest in July, reflected by the peak total number of links, the highest average degree, and the greatest graph density among all treatments. During this same period, modularity reached its lowest value from May to August (Fig. S12B; Table S13). The B. chinensis system exhibited strong niche partitioning in May, with the highest modularity value and a negative correlation proportion of 49.48%. This pattern weakened over time, with modularity decreasing to 0.578 in August. The A. asphodeloides system maintained relatively stable cooperative network characteristics throughout the season. The proportion of positive correlations remained within a narrow range (52.02%–52.87%), graph density remained between 0.34 and 0.42, and modularity decreased steadily from 0.614 in May to 0.401 in August. The control (poplar monoculture) displayed comparatively conservative network dynamics, with smaller fluctuations in both link number and modularity relative to the intercropping systems (Fig. S12B; Table S13). Across all treatments, networks showed an average clustering coefficient of 1 and an average path distance of 1. Seasonal changes further differentiated intercropping effects. In the S. divaricata system, the total number of links increased by 28.5% from May to July, whereas in the B. chinensis system the number of links declined by 17.2% over the same period (Fig. S12B; Table S13). These were contrasting seasonal patterns in link number between the two systems.

Association between the microbial co‑occurrence module and nutrient cycling functional genes

This study constructed rhizosphere microbial co‑occurrence networks for pure poplar stands and poplar‑medicinal plant intercropping systems, identifying three core interaction modules (Modules 1–3). Spearman correlation analysis showed that the association patterns between these modules and functional genes involved in C, N, and P cycling differed markedly between the two rhizospheres (Figs. 4B and S12B). In the medicinal plant rhizosphere, Module 1 was positively correlated with multiple C- and P‑metabolism genes; Module 2 was negatively correlated with the N‑metabolism gene nasB; and Module 3 showed mixed correlations with C‑metabolism genes (positive for some, negative for others). In the poplar rhizosphere, Module 2 was primarily positively correlated with C‑cycle genes (BAT, NEG1), whereas Module 3 was mainly negatively correlated with them. These correlation patterns differed between the medicinal plant and poplar rhizospheres.

Microbial genera linked to soil fertility and poplar nutrition

Integrated community and functional gene analyses showed that intercropping reshaped the poplar rhizosphere microbiota which was associated with changes in the abundance of C- and P-cycling genes, as well as related soil enzyme activities and nutrient pools. These microbial shifts were associated with enhanced poplar growth (P < 0.001). In the A. asphodeloides intercropping system, poplar DBH increased by 15.33% and tree height by 6.28% relative to the control. In the B. chinensis system, DBH increased by 21.17 ± 1.98 cm (14.3% higher than the control). The S. divaricata system increased DBH by 15.23% and tree height by 6.5% (Table S14). Redundancy analysis showed that MBP was a significant correlate of variation in the poplar rhizosphere microbiota (RDA1 explained 30.73% of variation). Other environmental variables collectively contributed to the remaining variation along subsequent RDA axes. Samples from the B. chinensis system (e.g., BPRS8) grouped in the region associated with higher MBP, whereas the control clustered on the opposite axis (Fig. S13A). Linear regression confirmed a significant association between MBP and microbial community structure (R2 = 0.19, P = 0.0076), with B. chinensis samples consistently positioned above the fitted line, showing a consistent positive deviation from the regression line (Fig. S13B). SOC showed a positive trend with community structure (P = 0.0552; Fig. S13B), and the abundance of C cycling genes in the medicinal plant rhizospheres was strongly positively correlated with SOC (P < 0.01; Fig. 3D). In turn, gene abundance of P metabolism genes in the poplar rhizosphere was significantly positively correlated with soil TC (P < 0.01; Fig. S9B). These correlations indicated co-variation​ C and P metrics in both the medicinal plant and poplar rhizospheres.

Poplar nutrient status reflected this microbial C–P linkage. Root TP was strongly correlated with microbial community structure (R2 = 0.31, P = 0.0003; Fig. S14). Leaf TN was also significantly correlated with community variation (R2 = 0.28, P = 0.008; Fig. S15), indicating a potential link between rhizosphere P activation potential and aboveground N assimilation. Overall, compared to the monoculture control, intercropping was associated with changes in the poplar rhizosphere microbial community, root P enrichment, leaf N content, and improved growth.

Discussion

Ecological significance of intercropping systems in shaping spatiotemporal microbial differentiation

The asynchronous succession of rhizosphere microbial communities in poplar and medicinal plants indicates that the companion plant is consistent with the companion plant’s role as a primary correlate​ of community assembly, with its effects coinciding with​ both temporal succession and spatial differentiation [24]. In this study, microbial divergence appeared earlier in the medicinal plant rhizosphere (May; P = 0.045), while the poplar rhizosphere showed a delayed response (August; P = 0.049). This pattern is consistent with plant-mediated effects on microbial recruitment [25].

The observed differences in community structure can be interpreted through the framework of ecological processes governing microbiome assembly, which distinguishes between deterministic selection (e.g., imposed by the host plant) and stochastic drift [26]. The pronounced differentiation observed in the S. divaricata intercropping system (P = 0.003) was accompanied by enrichment of degradation-associated genera such as Mycobacterium and a reduction in network modularity (0.206), which could be interpreted as​ a consequence of strong deterministic selection pressure, potentially shaping a distinct and less modular community. A similar recruitment pattern has been reported in maize rhizospheres, where phosphate-solubilizing bacteria are selectively enriched to enhance nutrient availability [4]. In contrast, the B. chinensis system maintained greater microbial compositional homogeneity and network stability, which may indicate a weaker or more homogeneous selective environment, potentially allowing for a greater relative influence of stochastic processes or supporting functionally redundant groups. This pattern parallels the role of specific root metabolites in shaping stable microbial consortia [27]. These contrasting outcomes reflect species-specific plant–microbe association principles and likely differences in the relative influence of deterministic versus stochastic assembly processes [28].

In the A. asphodeloides intercropping system, the proportion of positive microbial interactions increased to 63.05% during plant development, a pattern that has been associated with deterministic habitat filtering or mutualistic interactions [29]. Intercropped plants may influence microbial zonation through gradients in root exudate profiles, enriching oligotrophic bacteria near root tips and copiotrophic taxa in more mature zones, as observed in the wheat rhizosphere [30]. Such feedbacks are consistent with the concept of plants influencing microbial composition through root exudation, a key mechanism of host selection. For example, organic acids such as citric and malic acid can activate phosphate-solubilizing Pseudomonas through metal chelation processes [31, 32]. While our data are consistent with this mechanism, we acknowledge that direct causal evidence requires further experimental validation. These dynamics highlight that intercropping systems may be designed strategically by pairing species with complementary belowground functional traits (e.g., root depth, exudate chemistry) to enhance nutrient acquisition efficiency [24]. In this framework, regulatory control is exerted through specific biochemical interactions between exudates and functional microbial groups which may represent a mechanism that modifies​ the ecological assembly processes in the shared rhizosphere.

Functional differentiation and energy efficiency trade-offs in microbial C and P metabolism coupling

This study shows clear differences in how intercropping systems regulate microbial C and P metabolism. In the S. divaricata system, a temporal cascade from C fixation (frdC) to C oxidation (aldB), together with the activation of organic P mineralization genes (pgnH), supported a potential C-driven P mobilization pathway. This pattern aligns with observations of coordinated C-P processes in other systems [33]. In contrast, the metagenomic evidence showed a distinct C–P interaction pattern in the B. chinensis system, where enhanced microbial C fixation (e.g., tfrB) was not accompanied by a proportional increase in the gene abundance of key P-solubilizing genes (e.g., ppk2). This corresponded with​ greater C accumulation relative to P mobilization activity, analogous to microbial functional shifts under environmental stress [34]. The A. asphodeloides system was associated with effective P transfer from the rhizosphere to the plant canopy, indicating potential link microbial–plant regulation of P allocation rather than simple rhizosphere retention​ [35]. Together, these patterns suggest differences in how C and P metabolic potentials are associated across systems.

Traditional models assume that C mineralization directly drives P transformation [36]. However, in the B. chinensis system, increased C fixation paired with reduced P metabolism was accompanied by an accumulation of MBP. This observation can be interpreted through the lens of ecological stoichiometry [37]. The significant accumulation of MBP alongside high MBC in the B. chinensis system suggests a shift in the microbial biomass C:P ratio, which may reflect a microbial community strategy to immobilize P in response to stoichiometric imbalances. MBP could thus serve as a temporary storage pool, potentially stabilizing excess C inputs and gradually releasing P back into the cycle [38]. This offers a plausible explanation for​ the observed increase in available P may increase in the B. chinensis rhizosphere without corresponding increases in plant P uptake: microbial P retention might offset the apparent decoupling​ of C and P metabolism, potentially limiting​ immediate plant access. Thus, MBP may represent the key functional adjustment mechanism in systems where C and P metabolism are not tightly aligned.

The finding that 30.73% of the variation in the poplar rhizosphere microbial community was explained by MBP, while N-related indicators had limited explanatory power, suggests a pronounced role of P dynamics in this system. While this does not preclude N limitation in other contexts, our data indicate that P metabolism (as represented by MBP) was a primary correlate​ of microbial community structure. The co-variation​ of SOC, TC, and MBP suggests that​ C and P dynamics were correlated​ in this system, whereas inorganic N forms showed less consistent variation across systems. This highlights the need to consider context-dependent nutrient limitations and the integrated C–N–P stoichiometric interactions that govern nutrient coupling efficiency [39, 40].

Cascading effects of functional bacterial genera

The re-inoculation experiments provided functional validation under controlled conditions, linking the genus-level nutrient associations to specific microbial outcomes, and identified Priestia, Pseudomonas, and Acinetobacter as distinct functional contributors to rhizosphere processes and poplar growth.

In accordance with the correlation analysis, inoculation with Priestia sp. ASI7 enhanced rhizosphere NH₄⁺, MBN, and MBP cycling, although its mechanism of P mobilization appears non-canonical [41]. Despite a reduction in ACP activity, MBP increased significantly, suggesting that the Priestia sp. ASI7 strain may activate organic P through phosphatase-independent mechanisms, such as organic acid secretion [42]. This aligns with reports that some phosphate-solubilizing microorganisms simultaneously influence N and P turnover through diverse biochemical pathways [43]. Our findings refine this understanding by indicating that P activation by Priestia may be partially decoupled from ACP activity [44].

The Pseudomonas sp. JDBD4 strain induced the strongest growth response, despite not increasing soil AP. Instead, it substantially increased PAL activity in poplar roots. PAL is a key plant enzyme at the entry point of the phenylpropanoid pathway. The increase in PAL activity could indicate an upregulation of the phenylpropanoid pathway, which in plants is known to promote​ the synthesis of lignin and related secondary metabolites that support structural development and biomass accumulation [45, 46]. Similar bacterial modulation​ of plant phenylpropanoid metabolism, often via microbial-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) or specific metabolites, has been observed in other tree species [47]. For instance, bacterial volatile organic compounds or specific lipopeptides can trigger systemic responses that upregulate PAL activity and downstream pathways [48, 49]. The PAL induction observed in our study is consistent with such a signaling-mediated interaction [4, 50].

For Acinetobacter sp. BDH12, the re-inoculation results highlighted its role in C–N turnover. The strain increased β-GC activity and MBC, indicating strong C mineralization capacity, but also reduced rhizosphere NO₃⁻ content [51]. This reduction, coupled with its more modest plant growth effects, suggests that Acinetobacter sp. BDH12 competes for nitrate during C processing, limiting its contribution to plant nutrient supply [52, 53]. This dual functional role, driving nutrient turnover while simultaneously competing for inorganic N, is consistent with broader perspectives on microbial participation in N cycling [54, 55]. Thus, Acinetobacter sp. BDH12 functions primarily as a soil nutrient transformation agent, rather than as a direct plant growth promoter. It is important to note that these re-inoculation assays, while offering mechanistic insights, were conducted under simplified greenhouse conditions. The absence of full microbial community complexity limits direct extrapolation to field-scale dynamics, where interspecific interactions and environmental variability would modulate these functional outcomes.

From empirical observation to microbial design

This study elucidates the differentiated belowground patterns and potential mechanisms​ by which intercropping influences poplar growth through rhizosphere microbiome restructuring. B. chinensis intercropping shaped the poplar rhizosphere into system with enhanced P activation, S. divaricata was associated with promoted C–N transformation, and A. asphodeloides was linked to P redistribution from the rhizosphere to the canopy through a cooperative microbial network. These targeted functional shifts partially resemble the directed behavior of synthetic microbial consortia [56], but here they arise through plant–microbe interactions within naturally assembled communities. The associated growth outcomes were consistent with established plant architecture–nutrient relationships: B. chinensis intercropping enhanced radial (DBH) growth, while S. divaricata intercropping promoted height development [57], aligning with the plant architecture–nutrient matching hypothesis [58]. Our results are consistent with the possibility that microbial functional differentiation contributes to architectural divergence, rather than occurring as a secondary consequence of growth differences.

Furthermore, this study links community-level effects to specific microbial taxa and proposes their mechanistic roles [59]. This supports the practical relevance of selectively isolating functional strains from complex microbiomes [60] and provides a nuanced view that specific functions, rather than simply greater diversity, may be key in certain contexts [61]. Building on our findings, we propose a conceptual framework for the future design and optimization of intercropping systems based on microbial function. This framework involves three iterative steps: (i) selecting intercropping plant partners based on complementary functional traits (e.g., root exudate profiles, nutrient demand patterns) to create a desired rhizosphere biochemical niche; (ii) identifying and applying microbial inoculants, guided by functional gene modules (e.g., C–P coupling genes, signaling pathway activators) that match the target nutrient cycling process; and (iii) optimizing the structure of introduced or resident microbial communities based on network topology principles (e.g., fostering positive interactions, reducing competitive exclusion) to enhance stability and function [62]. Moving forward, research should examine how these functional hub strains interact with plant root exudates to refine this framework. The long-term goal is to shift from general microbial inoculation toward a more precise, knowledge-based regulation of rhizosphere processes. Ultimately, incorporating principles from synthetic ecology may enable the design or reinforcement of microbial functions in systems where nutrient cycling is inefficient, potentially advancing intercropping systems from empirical practice toward targeted functional optimization.

It is important to note that our functional inferences are primarily based on the abundance of functional genes derived from metagenomic data. While this reflects the genetic potential of the microbial community for processes such as carbon metabolism and phosphorus cycling, it does not directly confirm active gene expression, enzyme production, or in situ metabolic fluxes. Future studies incorporating metatranscriptomics, metaproteomics, or isotope tracing would be valuable to validate the actual activity and cooperation suggested by the genetic potential observed here. Furthermore, this study was conducted at a single experimental site. While this design allows for a rigorous, controlled comparison of the treatments under the specific soil and climatic conditions described, the generalizability of the findings to other geographical regions or soil types may be limited and warrants validation in future multi-site studies.

Conclusion

The forest–medicinal intercropping system was associated with improved poplar growth, which coincided with the restructuring of the rhizosphere microbial community and changes in C and P dynamics. S. divaricata intercropping enriched functional genera such as Priestia, which was linked to coordinated C and P processes​ as suggested by the co-occurrence of C fixation (frdC) and P mineralization (pgnH) genes. In the B. chinensis system, reduced association between C and P metabolism correlated with rhizosphere P retention rather than plant uptake. A. asphodeloides intercropping was associated with strengthened cooperative microbial associations that correlated with the translocation of P from roots to leaves. Re-inoculation experiments under controlled conditions confirmed that Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter, and Priestia strains directly influenced nutrient availability and plant growth, demonstrating potential to play key functional roles within these systems. These findings highlight how microbial functional differentiation is linked to nutrient flow and poplar growth responses under intercropping. Building on this, future work should integrate in situ root exudate metabolomics with controlled synthetic microbial community experiments to clarify the molecular basis of plant–microbe interactions, supporting the development of strategies for managing rhizosphere microbial functions to optimize intercropping outcomes.
