Nonlocalised damping estimates for hyperbolic relaxation systems in one space dimensions
Johannes Bärlin

Abstract
In this paper, we present a new approach to obtain so-called damping estimates for self-similar solutions to general hyperbolic relaxation systems applying the method of characteristics. Such damping estimates are an important part of the stability theory of shock profiles, where they enable the closure of nonlinear stability arguments [ 7 ] . We extend the damping estimate obtained in [ 7 ] from the L 2 L^{2} -case to the L ∞ L^{\infty} -case and, at the same time, generalize the L 2 L^{2} -estimates of [ 7 ] to the non-symmetric setting. Our estimates open the door to a general stability theory of shock profiles of hyperbolic relaxation systems [ 6 ] under nonlocalised perturbations in one space dimension.



Let
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be open with
0\in\mathcal{U}
.
We consider a nonlinear hyperbolic equation with source term

(1)

\displaystyle U_{t}+A(U)U_{x}=q(U)

where
(t,x)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R},U\in\mathcal{U}
, and the functions

A:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},q:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
are smooth. For
U\in\mathcal{U}
set for the differential
dq
of
q

\displaystyle Q(U):=dq(U).

We make the following set of assumptions:

Assumption 1
.

Existence of a stationary solution:
There exists a smooth solution

\bar{U}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathcal{U}

of

\displaystyle A(\bar{U})\bar{U}_{x}=q(\bar{U})

decaying at
\pm\infty
to the end-states
U^{\pm}\in\mathcal{U}
at
exponential rate

(2)

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{x}^{k}\left(\bar{U}-U^{\pm}\right)(x)\right|\leq c_{k}e^{-\theta_{k}|x|},\quad x\gtrless 0,\quad k=0,1,\ldots.

We will refer to
\bar{U}
as a profile. It naturally defines a
stationary solution of (
1
) via

(t,x)\mapsto\bar{U}(x).

Assumption 2
.

Strict hyperbolicity and non-characteristicity:

Along the profile
\bar{U}
the coefficient matrix
A(U)
is strictly
hyperbolic and non-characteristic. This means that for

U\in\mathcal{C}:=\overline{\bar{U}(\mathbb{R})}

there are
n
distinct and real eigenvalues

(3)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(U)<\lambda_{2}(U)<\ldots<\lambda_{n}(U)

with

(4)

\displaystyle|\lambda_{j}(U)|\geq c>0,\quad j=0,\ldots,n

where
c
is a constant independent of
U
.

Assumption 3
.

Stability at high frequencies:
At the endstates
U^{\pm}
the matrices

\displaystyle Q^{\pm}:=Q(U^{\pm})=dq(U^{\pm})\quad\text{and}\quad A^{\pm}=A(U^{\pm})

satisfy the high-frequency stability condition

(5)

\displaystyle\textnormal{Re}\,\sigma(i\xi A^{\pm}+Q^{\pm})\leq-c<0,\quad|\xi|\geq C,

for some positive constants
C,c>0
.

Remark 1
.

Assumption
3
means that, after linearisation at the endstates,
the Fourier symbol of the operator in
(
1
)
has a uniform spectral gap for large frequencies.
It is the high-frequency part of Shizuta and Kawashima’s
“strict dissipativity”
[
8
]
without further symmetry assumptions.

Since
\mathcal{C}
is compact and one-dimensional, Assumption
2
implies
the existence of a neighborhood
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{U}
of
\mathcal{C}
in which
A(U)

possesses a set of smooth eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}(U)
with smooth left and right eigenvectors

l_{j}(U)\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times N},r_{j}(U)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}

such that the order
of eigenvalues (
3
) and the lower bound
(
4
) on their absolute values continue
to hold. Further, we may assume that

\displaystyle l_{j}\cdot r_{k}=\delta_{jk}.

Let us arrange the left and right eigenvectors in two real
N\times N
matrices

\displaystyle L(U)=\begin{pmatrix}l_{1}\\
l_{2}\\
\vdots\\
l_{N}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad R(U)=\begin{pmatrix}r_{1}r_{2}\cdots r_{N}\end{pmatrix}.

Using
L(U)
and
R(U)
we can diagonalize
A(U)
to obtain a diagonal matrix
\Lambda(U)

that has the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}(U)
as its diagonal entries:

\displaystyle L(U)A(U)R(U)=\Lambda(U)=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1}(U),\lambda_{2}(U),\ldots\lambda_{N}(U)).

We transform
Q(U)
in the same way and decompose the resulting matrix into a diagonal
matrix
E(U)
and an off-diagonal matrix
F(U)
(both of course smooth
in
U
):

\displaystyle L(U)Q(U)R(U)=E(U)+F(U).

At
U=U^{\pm}
, the dissipativity Assumption
3
implies
that

l_{j}(U^{\pm})Q^{\pm}r_{j}(U^{\pm})<0.

Thus, the diagonal matrix
E^{\pm}=E(U^{\pm})
has only negative entries:

(6)

\displaystyle E_{jj}^{\pm}<0.

Remark 2
.

Inequality (
6
) can be deduced from the high-frequency stability condition
(
5
) by applying matrix perturbation theory
[
4
]
to the matrix-valued function
\xi\mapsto i\xi A^{\pm}+Q^{\pm}
for large
|\xi|
. We find that the
eigenvalues expand as

(7)

\displaystyle i\lambda_{j}^{\pm}\xi+E_{jj}^{\pm}+\mathcal{O}(|\xi|^{-1})\quad\text{for}\quad|\xi|\gg 1

where
\lambda_{j}^{\pm}=\lambda_{j}(U^{\pm})\in\mathbb{R}
. Considering their real parts and using
(
3
) shows (
6
).

On the other hand, the expansion (
7
)
shows that if all
E^{\pm}_{jj}
are negative then an estimate like in (
3
)
holds.

For a
K
-times differentiable function
U=U(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{M},M\geq 1,
define the norm

||U||_{C^{K}_{b}}:=||U||_{C^{K}_{b}(\mathbb{R})}:=\max\limits_{0\leq j\leq K}\sup\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\partial_{x}^{j}U(x)|.

The main result of this paper is the following

Theorem 1
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
,
2
,
and
3
hold, and let
K\geq 0
be a non-negative integer. Then there exist constants

C,\varepsilon,\theta>0
such that for all times

if a smooth function

\tilde{U}\in C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R})

solves (
1
), and for
a continuously differentiable phase shift
\delta:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

with

|\dot{\delta}(t)|\leq\varepsilon,\quad t\in[0,T],

the shifted perturbation variable

satisfies

\displaystyle||U(t)||_{C^{K}_{b}(\mathbb{R})}\leq

\displaystyle Ce^{-\theta t}||U(0)||_{C^{K}_{b}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle+C\int\limits_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta(t-s)}\left(||U(s)||_{C^{0}_{b}(\mathbb{R})}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|ds\right),\quad t\in[0,T],.

holds.

Theorem
1
makes a statement on a nonlinear damping estimate
for nonlocalised perturbations of
\bar{U}
with a possibly dynamic shift
\delta=\delta(t)
.
Such damping estimates are an important
part in closing nonlinear stability arguments when
\bar{U}
is a shock profile, see, e.g.,
[
7
]
(also
[
10
]
for
shock profiles with a subshock, and
[

]

for the case of detonations).
In this setting, nonlinear stability
of shock profiles actually means orbital stability

]
:
In the rest frame of the shock and with respect to an appropriate norm, perturbations of
\bar{U}
do not decay to a standing travelling wave
solution
(t,x)\mapsto\bar{U}(x)
, but to a
dynamically shifted profile

(t,x)\mapsto\bar{U}(x-\delta(t))
where
\delta=\delta(t)
is
the so-called instantaneous shock location with
\delta(0)=0
.

Remark 3
.

Using an approximation argument, Theorem
1
continues to
hold for
K\geq 1
and comparison solutions
\tilde{U}
of
(

.

1
Proof of Theorem
1

Theorem
1
is proved by an application of the method
of characteristics and a commutator argument inspired by stability considerations
involving Fourier symbols (e.g.
[
8
]
).

For some time
T>0
, let
\tilde{U}\in C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R})
be a solution of
the nonlinear hyperbolic system with source term
(
1
), and let
\delta:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

be a continuously differentiable phase shift. Let
U
be the shifted perturbation
variable

U(t,x)=\tilde{U}(t,x+\delta(t))-\bar{U}(x).

For a small constant
\varepsilon>0
suppose that

|\dot{\delta}(t)|\leq\varepsilon,\quad t\in[0,T],

and

||U(t)||_{C^{1}_{b}}\leq\varepsilon,\quad t\in[0,T].

As a first assumption of smallness, suppose
\varepsilon>0
is small enough such
that for all
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
one finds
\tilde{U}(t,x)\in\mathcal{V}
.

Denote the derivative of the perturbation variable
U
as

\displaystyle W(t,x):=\partial_{x}U(t,x).

Then
U
and
W
satisfy the perturbation equations

(8)

\displaystyle U_{t}+\left(A(\tilde{U})-\dot{\delta}\right)U_{x}=

\displaystyle Q(\tilde{U})U+\dot{\delta}\bar{U}_{x}

\displaystyle+\left(\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|)+\mathcal{O}(|U|)\right)U

and

(9)

\displaystyle W_{t}+\left(A(\tilde{U})-\dot{\delta}\right)W_{x}=

\displaystyle Q(\tilde{U})W+\dot{\delta}\bar{U}_{xx}

\displaystyle+\left(\mathcal{O}(|W|)+\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|)\right)W

\displaystyle+\left(\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{xx}|)+\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|)\right)U

where we abused notation in setting

A(\tilde{U})(t,x)=A(\tilde{U}(t,x+\delta(t))).

In order to apply the method of characteristics, the differential operator on
the left-hand sides of (
8
) and (
9
) needs
to be diagonalized. Thus, set

\displaystyle\Phi(t,x):=L(\tilde{U}(t,x+\delta(t)))U(t,x)\text{ and }\Psi(t,x):=L(\tilde{U}(t,x+\delta(t)))W(t,x).

Performing this change of variables, the equation satisfied by
\Phi
is

(10)

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}+(\Lambda(\tilde{U})-\dot{\delta})\Phi_{x}=

\displaystyle\{E(\tilde{U})+F(\tilde{U})\}\Phi+\dot{\delta}L(\tilde{U})\bar{U}_{x}

\displaystyle+\big[\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|)+\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)+\mathcal{O}(|\Psi|)

\displaystyle\quad\;\;+\dot{\delta}\left(\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|)+\mathcal{O}(|\Psi|)\right)\big]\Phi.

1.1
The method of characteristics

Before treating
\Psi
let us derive an estimate for
\Phi
demonstrating
the method of characteristics when exponentially decaying terms, like
the contributions in (
10
) with
\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|)
,
are involved. Equation (
10
) can be rewritten as

(11)

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}+(\Lambda(\tilde{U})-\dot{\delta})\Phi_{x}=

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{E}(t,x)+\mathcal{F}(t,x)\}\Phi+\dot{\delta}L(\tilde{U})\bar{U}_{x}

where
\mathcal{E}(t,x)
is a diagonal matrix with

\mathcal{E}=E(\tilde{U})+\mathcal{O}(\bar{U}_{x})

and
\mathcal{F}(t,x)
is viewed
as a forcing term, not necessarily of a specific shape. While we will use a crude bound on
\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}(t,x)|\leq C=C(\varepsilon),

we estimate the diagonal matrix
\mathcal{E}
for
j=1,\ldots,N
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{jj}(t,x)\leq

\displaystyle E_{jj}(\bar{U}(x))+(E_{jj}(\tilde{U}(t,x+\delta(t)))-E_{jj}(\bar{U}(x)))+Ce^{-\theta_{1}|x|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle E_{jj}(\bar{U}(x))+C\varepsilon+Ce^{-\theta_{1}|x|}

where
\varepsilon
is small depending on the bound on
U
, and the exponentially decaying term comes from the estimate
(
2
) of the derivative
\bar{U}_{x}
of the profile

\bar{U}
.
Define

(12)

\displaystyle\theta_{E}:=\frac{\max_{j}\{{E_{jj}^{\pm}}\}}{2}.

By dissipativity
3
, which has negativity of
E^{\pm}_{jj}
(
6
)
as a consequence, it holds

\theta_{E}<0.

Using the decay of
\bar{U}
to
U^{\pm}
, there are a constant

\tilde{\theta}>0
depending on the decay rates (
2
) of the profile
and a large constant
C
depending on
\varepsilon

and the size of the profile
\bar{U}
and its first derivative, such that

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{jj}(t,x)\leq-2\theta_{E}+C\varepsilon+Ce^{-\tilde{\theta}|x|}

holds. For
\varepsilon
small enough, inequality (
13
) implies the existence of a constant
R>0
such that

(14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{jj}(t,x)\leq-\theta_{E},\quad|x|\geq R.

Consider the
j
-th component
\Phi_{j}
of
\Phi
. Then, from
(
11
), with

\displaystyle D(t,x)=\Lambda(\tilde{U}(t,x+\delta(t)))-\dot{\delta}(t)

it holds

(15)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Phi_{j}+D_{jj}(t,x)\partial_{x}\Phi_{j}=\mathcal{E}_{jj}(t,x)\Phi_{j}+(\mathcal{F}(t,x)\Phi+\dot{\delta}L(\tilde{U})\bar{U}_{x})_{j}.

For
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, define the
j
-th characteristic
X_{j}=X_{j}(s)=X_{j}(s;x_{0})

as the unique solution of the ordinary differential equation

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \dot{X}_{j}(s)&=D_{jj}(s,X_{j}(s))=\lambda_{j}(\tilde{U}(s,x+\delta(s)))-\dot{\delta}(s),s\in[0,T],\\
X_{j}(0)&=x_{0},\end{aligned}

and denote by

\displaystyle\dot{\Phi}_{j}(s)=\frac{d}{ds}\Phi_{j}(s,X_{j}(s))

the derivative of
\Phi_{j}
along a characteristic
X_{j}
.

When applying the method of characteristics, one integrates each
j
-th equation (
15
) along the
j
-th
characteristics
X_{j}
passing through
x
at time
t
for all pairs
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
. Then estimates are
made on the resulting path integrals.

Let
(t,x)\in(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
, and consider
j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. Then there is a unique
x_{0}=x_{0}(t,x;j)
such that the
j
-th
characteristic
X_{j}
passes through
x
at time
t
:

X_{j}(t;x_{0})=x.

Along the
j
-th characteristic the
j
-th equation (
15
) reads

\displaystyle\dot{\Phi}_{j}(s)=\mathcal{E}_{jj}(s)\Phi_{j}(s)+G_{j}(s)

where we have abused notation with

\Phi_{j}(s)=\Phi_{j}(s,X_{j}(s)),\mathcal{E}_{jj}(s)=\mathcal{E}_{jj}(s,X_{j}(s)),

and collected forcing terms in

G_{j}(s)=\mathcal{O}(|\Phi(s)|)+\mathcal{O}(|\dot{\delta}(s)|).

Using Duhamel’s formula, one obtains

(16)

\displaystyle\Phi_{j}(t,x)=\Phi_{j}(0,x_{0})e^{H_{j}(t)}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{H_{j}(t)-H_{j}(s)}G_{j}(s)ds

where

\displaystyle H_{j}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{E}_{jj}(s)ds.

For
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
let us estimate
H_{j}(t)-H_{j}(s)
: The map

s\mapsto X_{j}(s)
is strictly monotonic on
[0,T]
since

\displaystyle|\dot{X}_{j}(s)|\geq|\lambda_{j}(\tilde{U}(s,X_{j}(s)+\delta(s)))|-\varepsilon\geq c>0

for all
s\in[0,T]
by the assumption of non-characteristicity
2

and smallness of
|\dot{\delta}|
. Thus, we can change variables in

H_{j}(t)-H_{j}(s)=\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{E}_{jj}(r)dr=-\theta_{E}(t-s)+\int_{s}^{t}\theta_{E}+\mathcal{E}_{jj}(r)dr

via

y=X_{j}(r),\quad dy=\dot{X}_{j}dr

turning the integration over time into an integration over space:

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{E}_{jj}(r)dr=

\displaystyle-\theta_{E}(t-s)+\int_{y\in X_{j}([s,t])}\frac{\theta_{E}+\mathcal{E}_{jj}(r(y),y)}{|\dot{X}_{j}(r(y))|}dr.

Using the general bound (
13
) for
\mathcal{E}_{jj}

on
[0,T]\times[-R,R]
and the “large-
x
” bound (
14
)
stating that

\theta_{E}+\mathcal{E}_{jj}<0\quad\text{on}\quad[0,T]\times(\mathbb{R}\backslash[-R,R])

one obtains

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{E}_{jj}(r)dr\leq

\displaystyle-\theta_{E}(t-s)+\int_{-R}^{R}\frac{Ce^{-\tilde{\theta}|y|}}{c}dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\theta_{E}(t-s)+\frac{2C}{c\tilde{\theta}},

and thus

(17)

\displaystyle H_{j}(t)-H_{j}(s)\leq-\theta_{E}(t-s)+C.

Applying (
17
) to
(
16
) yields

(18)

\displaystyle|\Phi_{j}(t,x)|\leq Ce^{-\theta_{E}t}||\Phi(0)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta_{E}(t-s)}\left(||\Phi(s)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|\right)ds

with a large constant
C>0
depending on the
C^{1}_{b}
-norm of
U
, the size
of
|\dot{\delta}|
as well as the size of
\bar{U}
and
\bar{U}_{x}
and their
exponential decay rates. Taking the supremum over
x
in (
18
)
and collecting all components of
\Phi
gives

(19)

\displaystyle||\Phi(t)||_{C^{0}_{b}}\leq Ce^{-\theta_{E}t}||\Phi(0)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta_{E}(t-s)}\left(||\Phi(s)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|\right)ds.

Remark 4
.

Non-characteristicity is used in an essential way to derive the damping
estimate (
19
). The idea is that each characteristic
stays in a region with no damping, here quantified as
[0,T]\times[-R,R]
,
only for a finite time which results in a contribution to the constants
C
in
(
19
) but leaves enough time for “overall” damping due to the exponential
decay of the profile to the endstates
U^{\pm}
where damping is present.

1.2
The damping estimate

The aim of this subsection is to show a damping estimate on
\Psi
that
“binds” the
C^{0}_{b}
-norm of
\Psi
to the initial values
\Phi(0),\Psi(0)
and
a Duhamel term involving only
U
and
\dot{\delta}
but not
\Psi
itself.
The estimate will be of the form

(20)

\displaystyle||\Psi(t)||_{C^{0}_{b}}\leq Ce^{-\theta t}||(\Phi(0),\Psi(0))||_{C^{0}_{b}}+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta(t-s)}\left(||\Phi(s)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|\right)ds

where
\theta>0
is comparable to (
12
), but possibly slightly smaller, and
C
is a constant
with a similar dependence as the corresponding quantity in the
\Phi
-estimate
(
19
).

To achieve an estimate like (
20
) we
need to be more careful than in the previous deduction of an estimate of

\Phi
where we accepted
\Phi
itself as part of the linear source term. We
will in fact eliminate all off-diagonal terms in the (linear)
\Psi
-source term by
a Fourier analysis inspired variable transform involving
\Psi
and
\Phi

at the cost of increasing the
\Phi
-source term. But since
we treat a term of order
\mathcal{O}(\Phi)
as a forcing, in
accordance to the damping estimate (
20
),
the resulting larger
\Phi
-source term does not pose a problem.

To this end, let us first state an equation for
\Psi
. Replacing
W
by

R(\tilde{U})\Psi
in equation (
9
) and multiplying both sides
by
L(\tilde{U})
from the left yields

(21)

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}+D(t,x)\Psi_{x}=

\displaystyle\{E(\tilde{U})+F(\tilde{U})\}\Psi+\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|)\Psi+\dot{\delta}L(\tilde{U})\bar{U}_{xx}+\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)

\displaystyle+\left[\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)+\mathcal{O}(|\Psi|)+\mathcal{O}(|\dot{\delta}|)\right]\Psi.

We point out that no derivatives of
\Psi
appear on the right-hand side of
(
21
), thus the variable change does not introduce higher
derivatives of
U
other than
U_{x}=W
into the equation for
\Psi
. The term

\dot{\delta}L(\tilde{U})\bar{U}_{xx}+\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)

will be treated as a forcing, while

\left[\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)+\mathcal{O}(|\Psi|)+\mathcal{O}(|\dot{\delta}|)\right]\Psi

is viewed as a higher order term that can be bounded uniformly by smallness of
|\Phi|
,

|\Psi|
and
|\dot{\delta}|
later on. We could have included the
\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)\mathcal{O}(|\Psi|)

term in the
\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)
term due to the smallness of
\Psi
but when dealing with higher derivatives
later on the absorption of analogous mixed terms into
\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)
is
not possible anymore at this stage and are treated in a different way.

To make sure that only the forcing term
and the higher order term appear in the slaving of
\Psi
,
we need to diagonalise the source term

\{E(\tilde{U})+F(\tilde{U})\}\Psi+\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|)\Psi.

To this end, decompose the
\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|)
term into diagonal and
off-diagonal parts, too, and collect to find

\{E(\tilde{U})+F(\tilde{U})\}\Psi+\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|)\Psi=\{\tilde{E}(t,x)+\tilde{F}(t,x)\}\Psi

where

\displaystyle\tilde{E}=E(\tilde{U})+\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|)\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{F}=F(\tilde{U})+\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|)

do not contain terms that involve
\Psi
and
\dot{\delta}
. By choosing a suitable combination of
\Psi
and
\Phi
we will eliminate the
off-diagonal terms
\tilde{F}\Psi
in the equation (
21
) while
preserving its structure.

For a matrix
\Theta=\Theta(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
that is to be determined, set

(22)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Psi}=\Psi+\Theta\Phi.

As we shall see later, we may assume boundedness of
\Theta
:

\Theta(t,x)=\mathcal{O}(1).

For the
x
-derivative of
\tilde{\Psi}
we have

(23)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Psi}_{x}

\displaystyle=\Psi_{x}+\Theta\Phi_{x}+\Theta_{x}\Phi

\displaystyle=\Psi_{x}+\Theta\Psi+\left[\Theta\mathcal{O}(|\Psi|+|\bar{U}_{x}|)+\Theta_{x}\right]\Phi

Since we want to treat any term that involves a multiple of (a component of)
\Phi

as a large, but bounded forcing,
\Theta
can only be constructed from terms
involving
\Phi
and
\bar{U}
but not
\Psi
, otherwise we lose control of the derivatives
\Theta_{x}

and
\Theta_{t}
(we handle time derivatives of
\Phi
using the equation (
10
)
satisfied by
\Phi_{t}
.)

For the time derivative of
\tilde{\Psi}
we find

\displaystyle\tilde{\Psi}_{t}=

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}+\Theta\Phi_{t}+\Theta_{t}\Phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-D(t,x)\Psi_{x}+\{\tilde{E}(t,x)+\tilde{F}(t,x)\}\Psi+\left[\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)+\mathcal{O}(|\Psi|)+\mathcal{O}(|\dot{\delta}|)\right]\Psi

\displaystyle-\Theta(t,x)D(t,x)\Phi_{x}+\dot{\delta}\mathcal{O}(1)+\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)+\Theta_{t}\Phi.

Using (
23
) and

\Phi_{x}=\Psi+\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)

we find

(24)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Psi}_{t}=

\displaystyle-D(t,x)\tilde{\Psi}_{x}+\tilde{E}(t,x)\tilde{\Psi}+\left[\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)+\mathcal{O}(|\Psi|)+\mathcal{O}(|\dot{\delta}|)\right]\tilde{\Psi}

\displaystyle+\big[D(t,x)\Theta(t,x)+\tilde{F}(t,x)-\Theta(t,x)D(t,x)\big]\Psi

\displaystyle+\dot{\delta}\mathcal{O}(1)+\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)+\Theta_{t}\Phi+\mathcal{O}(1)\Theta_{x}\Phi.

Hence, if
\Theta
solves

(25)

\displaystyle\Theta D-D\Theta=[\Theta,\Lambda]=\tilde{F}

equation (

) will be free of linear off-diagonal
\tilde{\Psi}

terms (see Remark
5
for a discussion of the origins
of a similar commutator argument in Fourier analysis). Inspecting each component, we find that the solution of equation (
25
) is

(26)

\displaystyle\Theta(t,x)_{jk}=\begin{cases}\frac{\tilde{F}(t,x)_{jk}}{\lambda_{k}(\tilde{U})-\lambda_{j}(\tilde{U})}&j\neq k,\\
0&j=k.\end{cases}

Since
\tilde{F}
is free of contributions from
\Psi
and
\dot{\delta}
, so is

\Theta
, and we find

\Theta(t,x)=\mathcal{O}(1),\quad\Theta_{t}\Phi=\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|),\quad\Theta_{x}\Phi=\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|).

Thus, with this choice of
\Theta
the equation for
\tilde{\Psi}
reads

(27)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Psi}_{t}+D(t,x)\tilde{\Psi}_{x}=

\displaystyle\tilde{E}(t,x)\tilde{\Psi}+\left[\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)+\mathcal{O}(|\Psi|)+\mathcal{O}(|\dot{\delta}|)\right]\tilde{\Psi}

\displaystyle+\dot{\delta}\mathcal{O}(1)+\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|).

Notice that the diagonal matrix
\tilde{E}
in (
27
) and
the diagonal matrix
\mathcal{E}
in equation (
11
)
are of the form

E(\tilde{U})+\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|).

Therefore, we can proceed as in the derivation of the estimate (
19
)
on
\Phi
to find for all
t\in[0,T]

(28)

\displaystyle||\tilde{\Psi}(t)||_{C^{0}_{b}}\leq

\displaystyle Ce^{-\theta t}||\tilde{\Psi}(0)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta(t-s)}\left(||\Phi(s)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|\right)ds

\displaystyle+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta(t-s)}\left(||\Phi(s)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+||\Psi(s)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|\right)||\tilde{\Psi}(s)||_{C^{0}_{b}}ds

with a constant
\theta>0
and a large constant
C>0
. By choosing
\varepsilon
small enough, thus
making
|\Phi|
,
|\Psi|
and
|\dot{\delta}|
as small as needed, we can apply
Gronwall’s Lemma to eliminate the higher order term in
\tilde{\Psi}
in (
28
)
at the cost of enlarging
C
and shrinking
\theta
, but keeping the latter’s positive sign. In this way, the desired damping estimate

\displaystyle||\tilde{\Psi}(t)||_{C^{0}_{b}}\leq Ce^{-\theta t}||\tilde{\Psi}(0)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta(t-s)}\left(||\Phi(s)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|\right)ds

is obtained. The definition of
\tilde{\Psi}=\Psi+\Theta\Phi
with bounded

\Theta
and the
\Phi
-estimate (
19
) imply the damping
estimate (
20
) on
\Psi
.

Remark 5
.

The application of the variable transform
(
31
) to eliminate lower order off-diagonal
terms by solving a commutator equation like (
25
)
is inspired by stability considerations regarding the linearisation of
(
1
) at a (constant)
equilibrium solution. In
[
8
]
the analog of (
25
)
in Fourier space is used to prove various characterisations of dissipativity
for systems like (
1
) resulting
in theorems regarding the global existence of solutions to
(
1
) for data near equilibria.
These insights and results have been generalised in the following years, e.g.
[
11
,
5
,
2
,
1
,
9
]
.
In particular, in
[
9
]
many
structural assumptions were successfully removed, among them a symmetry assumption
on the Jacobian of the source term, something that is paralleled here.

1.3
Damping estimates for higher derivatives

The derivation of a damping estimate for
higher derivatives
\partial_{x}^{K}U,K\geq 2,
does not require further smallness
assumptions. We demonstrate the extension of the damping estimate to the
second derivative

Y(t,x):=U_{xx}.

Then
Y
solves

(29)

\displaystyle Y_{t}+(A(\tilde{U})-\dot{\delta})Y_{x}=

\displaystyle Q(\tilde{U})Y+\left(\mathcal{O}(|W|)+\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|)\right)Y+\dot{\delta}\bar{U}_{xxx}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(|W|)\left(\mathcal{O}(|W|)+\mathcal{O}(|W|^{2})\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\left.+\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|)+\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{xx}|)+\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|^{2})\right)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(|U|)\left(\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{xx}|)+\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|^{2})+\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}||\bar{U}_{xx}|)\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\left.+\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|^{3})\right)

Remark 6
.

The form of equation (
29
) is typical for derivatives of
U
of order
two or higher: No higher powers of components of
Y
appear,
that is, there are no
\mathcal{O}(|Y|^{2})
-terms. In fact, by the product rule, the relevant part
for the damping estimate will always have the form

Y_{t}+(A(\tilde{U})-\dot{\delta})Y_{x}=Q(\tilde{U})Y+\left(\mathcal{O}(|W|)+\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|)\right)Y+\dot{\delta}\bar{U}_{xxx}+\ldots

where
Y
does not appear in any of the trailing terms indicated by “
\ldots
”.
While lower derivatives might mix, a term of the form
\mathcal{O}(|U|)

only pairs with derivatives of the profile
\bar{U}
which are all bounded.
To handle products of lower derivatives note that a damping estimate for
these lower derivatives, like the one obtained for
W=U_{x}
(
20
),
implies a bound on such derivatives that depends on the sizes of the initial
data, of
|U|
and
|U_{x}|
and of
|\dot{\delta}|
, but not on time
T
!

As before, the change of variables

\displaystyle\Upsilon(t,x)=L(\tilde{U}(t,x+\delta(t)))Y(t,x)

is applied, and the equation for
\Upsilon
is derived:

(30)

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{t}+D(t,x)\Upsilon_{x}=

\displaystyle\{E(\tilde{U})+F(\tilde{U})\}\Upsilon+\mathcal{O}(|\bar{U}_{x}|)\Upsilon+\dot{\delta}L(\tilde{U})\bar{U}_{xxx}

\displaystyle+\left[\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)+\mathcal{O}(|\Psi|)+\mathcal{O}(|\dot{\delta}|)\right]\Upsilon+\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)+\mathcal{O}(|\Psi|).

Notice that compared to (
21
) now
\Psi
appears as a forcing, too.
Proceeding as in the previous section, we consider

(31)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Upsilon}=\Upsilon+\Theta\Psi

where
\Theta
is exactly as in (
26
) (this is because

\Upsilon
has replaced
\Psi
as the highest derivative of
U
, and the
deductions of (
30
) and (
21
) follow
the same lines). Definition (
31
) again eliminates
problematic off-diagonal terms in (
30
), and we find the
damping estimate

(32)

\displaystyle||\Upsilon(t)||_{C^{0}_{b}}\leq

\displaystyle Ce^{-\theta t}||(\Phi(0),\Psi(0),\Upsilon(0))||_{C^{0}_{b}}

\displaystyle+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta(t-s)}\left(||\Phi(s)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+||\Psi(s)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|\right)ds

for positive constants
\theta,C>0
.

It remains to remove the additional integral of
\Psi
in (
32
).
This is a standard Gronwall-type estimate using the damping estimate on

\Psi
. That is, by setting

\mathbb{V}_{0}:=||(\Phi(0),\Psi(0),\Upsilon(0))||_{C^{0}_{b}},\quad\mathbb{G}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}e^{\theta s}\left(||\Phi(s)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|\right)ds,

and combining (
32
)
and (
20
) we find for any positive integer
N>0

(33)

\displaystyle||\Upsilon(t)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+N||\Psi(t)||_{C^{0}_{b}}\leq

\displaystyle C(1+N)e^{-\theta t}(\mathbb{V}_{0}+\mathbb{G}(t))

\displaystyle+\frac{C}{N}e^{-\theta t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\theta s}\left(||\Upsilon(t)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+N||\Psi(t)||_{C^{0}_{b}}\right)ds.

With

\mathbb{V}_{N}(t):=e^{\theta t}\left(||\Upsilon(t)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+N||\Psi(t)||_{C^{0}_{b}}\right)\quad(N>0)

we see from (
33
)
that

(34)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{N}(t)\leq C(1+N)\left(\mathbb{V}_{0}+\mathbb{G}(t)\right)+\frac{C}{N}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{V}_{N}(t).

Applying Gronwall’s Lemma to (
34
) gives

(35)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{N}(t)\leq C(1+N)\Big[

\displaystyle\Big(1+e^{\frac{C}{N}t}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\frac{C}{N}e^{-\frac{C}{N}s}ds\Big)\mathbb{V}_{0}

\displaystyle+\mathbb{G}(t)+e^{\frac{C}{N}t}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\frac{C}{N}e^{-\frac{C}{N}s}\mathbb{G}(s)ds\Big].

Integrating the first integral in (
35
) and using partial integration to
treat the second integral in (
35
) yields

(36)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{N}(t)\leq C(1+N)\Big[e^{\frac{C}{N}t}\mathbb{V}_{0}+\int\limits_{0}^{t}e^{\frac{C}{N}(t-s)}e^{\theta s}\left(||\Phi(s)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|\right)ds\Big].

Multiply (
36
) by
e^{-\theta t}
and unravel the definitions
of
\mathbb{V}_{N},\mathbb{V}_{0}
and
G
to find

\displaystyle||\Upsilon(t)||_{C^{0}_{b}}\leq

\displaystyle||\Upsilon(t)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+N||\Psi(t)||_{C^{0}_{b}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1+N)e^{(C/N-\theta)t}||(\Phi(0),\Psi(0),\Upsilon(0))||_{C^{0}_{b}}

\displaystyle+C(1+N)\int\limits_{0}^{t}e^{(C/N-\theta)(t-s)}\left(||\Phi(s)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|\right)ds.

Choosing
N
large enough so that

C/N<\theta/2

gives the desired damping estimate

(37)

\displaystyle||\Upsilon(t)||_{C^{0}_{b}}\leq

\displaystyle Ce^{-\frac{\theta}{2}t}||(\Phi(0),\Psi(0),\Upsilon(0))||_{C^{0}_{b}}

\displaystyle+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\theta}{2}(t-s)}\left(||\Phi(s)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|\right)ds.

Since the change of variables

(t,x)\mapsto L(\tilde{U}(t,x+\delta(t))),\quad(t,x)\mapsto R(\tilde{U}(t,x+\delta(t)))

is uniformly bounded by

||\tilde{U}(t)-\bar{U}||_{C^{0}_{b}}\leq\varepsilon,\quad t\in[0,T]

we can summarize our estimates (
19
), (
20
), and (
37
),
by taking the largest of the constants
C
and the smallest of the constants
\theta
, in the inequality

\displaystyle||U(t)||_{C^{2}_{b}}\leq Ce^{-\theta t}||U(0)||_{C^{2}_{b}}+C\int\limits_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta(t-s)}\left(||U(s)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|\right)ds.

Remark 7
.

Higher-order damping estimates

\displaystyle||U(t)||_{C^{K}_{b}}\leq Ce^{-\theta t}||U(0)||_{C^{K}_{b}}+C\int\limits_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta(t-s)}\left(||U(s)||_{C^{0}_{b}}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|\right)ds.

can be obtained in the same way as demonstrated for the case
K=2
(see also Remark
6
).

2
L²-damping estimates

In this section we demonstrate how one can recover the
L^{2}
-damping estimates of

[
7
]
without imposing any symmetry assumptions on the coefficients of
(
1
).

Let
\tilde{U}=\tilde{U}(t,x)
be the solution of (
1
)
with initial data

\tilde{U}(0)=\tilde{U}_{0}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

For a small constant
\varepsilon>0
, suppose
\tilde{U}
and a continuously differentiable shift
\delta=\delta(t)
exist on
a time interval
[0,T]
with

(38)

\displaystyle||U(t)||_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad|\dot{\delta}(t)|\leq\varepsilon,\quad t\in[0,T],

where
U
is the shifted perturbation variable

U(t,x)=\tilde{U}(t,x+\delta(t))-\bar{U}(x).

In this section we prove

Proposition 1
.

There exist constants
C,\theta>0
such that if
\varepsilon>0
in (
38
)
is small enough, then for all
t\in[0,T]
the damping estimate

(39)

\displaystyle||U(t)||_{H^{2}}^{2}\leq Ce^{-\theta t}||U(0)||_{H^{2}}^{2}+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta(t-s)}\left(||U(s)||_{L^{2}}^{2}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|^{2}\right)ds

holds.

Proof.

Define
\Phi,\Psi
and
\Upsilon
together with the conjugate variables
\tilde{\Psi},\tilde{\Upsilon}

as before. They continue to satisfy the equations (
10
),
(
21
), (
30
), and (
27
) and as well as an appropriate equation for
\tilde{\Upsilon}
(not written out).

First, the deduction of the
L^{2}
damping estimate for
\Phi
is shown.
The estimate for derivatives of
U
follow in a similar fashion.
Rearrange equation (
10
) as

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}+\Lambda(\bar{U})\Phi_{x}=

\displaystyle E(\bar{U})\Phi+\dot{\delta}\Phi_{x}+\dot{\delta}L(\tilde{U})\bar{U}_{x}+\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)

where

\Lambda(\tilde{U})=\Lambda(\bar{U})+\mathcal{O}(\Phi)\quad\text{and}\quad E(\tilde{U})=E(\bar{U})+\mathcal{O}(\Phi)

was used. Note that the
\mathcal{O}(\Phi)
term also includes terms that come from products of (components of)
\Phi
and
\Psi
. For
j=1,\ldots,N
let the weights
\alpha_{j}
be solutions to the ordinary
differential equations

(40)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\alpha_{j}=-\frac{C_{\alpha,j}e^{-c_{\alpha,j}|x|}}{\lambda_{j}(\bar{U}(x))}\alpha_{j}(x),

where the constants
c_{\alpha,j},C_{\alpha,j}>0
are to be determined.
No matter the choice of these constants, one can normalize each
\alpha_{j}
such that

(41)

\displaystyle 0<\min\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\alpha_{j}(x)<\max\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\alpha_{j}(x)=1.

Taking the time derivative of the
\alpha_{j}
weighted
L^{2}
inner product
\left\langle\Phi_{j},\alpha_{j}\Phi_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

yields

(42)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}\left\langle\Phi_{j},\alpha_{j}\Phi_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=

\displaystyle-\left\langle\Phi_{j},\alpha_{j}\lambda_{j}(\bar{U})\partial_{x}\Phi_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}+\left\langle\Phi_{j},\alpha_{j}E_{jj}(\bar{U})\Phi_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\dot{\delta}\left\langle\Phi_{j},\alpha_{j}\partial_{x}\Phi_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}+\dot{\delta}\left\langle\Phi_{j},\alpha_{j}\mathcal{O}(\bar{U}_{x})_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\left\langle\Phi_{j},\alpha_{j}\mathcal{O}(\Phi)_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\Phi_{j},(\partial_{x}\alpha_{j})\lambda_{j}\Phi_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\Phi_{j},\alpha_{j}(\partial_{x}\lambda_{j}(\bar{U}))\Phi_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\left\langle\Phi_{j},\alpha_{j}E_{jj}(\bar{U})\Phi_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}-\frac{\dot{\delta}}{2}\left\langle\Phi_{j},(\partial_{x}\alpha_{j})\Phi_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\dot{\delta}\left\langle\Phi_{j},\mathcal{O}(\bar{U}_{x})_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}+\left\langle\Phi_{j},\alpha_{j}\mathcal{O}(\Phi)_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\left\langle\Phi_{j},\alpha_{j}C_{\alpha,j}e^{-c_{\alpha_{j}}|x|}\Phi_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}-2\theta_{E}\left\langle\Phi_{j},\alpha_{j}\Phi_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\left\langle\Phi_{j},\alpha_{j}Ce^{-c|x|}\Phi_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}+|\dot{\delta}|\left\langle\Phi_{j},\alpha_{j}C_{\alpha,j}e^{-c_{\alpha,j}|x|}|\lambda_{j}|^{-1}\Phi_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+C|\dot{\delta}|||\Phi||_{L^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(||\Phi||_{L^{2}}^{2})

where partial integration, the definition of
\alpha_{j}
(
40
),
the decay properties of the profile
\bar{U}
(
2
), definition of
\theta_{E}
(
12
), and
Hölder’s inequality as well as boundedness of
U(t)
in
W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
(
38
) were used. Choosing
C_{\alpha,j}>2C
and
0<c_{\alpha,j}<c
as well as

|\dot{\delta}|\leq\frac{\min\limits_{k,x}|\lambda_{k}(\bar{U}(x))|}{2}

one finds

Ce^{-c|x|}-\frac{1}{2}C_{\alpha_{j}}e^{-c_{\alpha,j}|x|}=e^{-c_{\alpha,j}|x|}\left(e^{-(c-c_{\alpha,j})|x|}C-\frac{1}{2}C_{\alpha,j}\right)<0,\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

and thus (
42
) implies

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left\langle\Phi_{j},\alpha_{j}\Phi_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\leq

\displaystyle-2\theta_{E}\left\langle\Phi_{j},\alpha_{j}\Phi_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(|\dot{\delta}|)+\mathcal{O}(||\Phi||_{L^{2}}^{2})

having the damping estimate, by the bounds on
\alpha_{j}
(
41
),

(43)

\displaystyle||\Phi(t)||_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq Ce^{-2\theta_{E}t}||\Phi(0)||_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2\theta_{E}(t-s)}\left(||\Phi(s)||_{L^{2}}^{2}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|^{2}\right)ds

as a consequence. The term
|\dot{\delta}|||\Phi||_{L^{2}}
is treated by Young’s inequality.

For an
L^{2}
-estimate of
\Psi
we again use the variable transform

\tilde{\Psi}=\Psi+\Theta\Phi.

Then
\tilde{\Psi}
satisfies

(44)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Psi}_{t}+\Lambda(\bar{U})\tilde{\Psi}_{x}=

\displaystyle\bar{E}(t,x)\tilde{\Psi}+\dot{\delta}\tilde{\Psi}_{x}-(\Lambda(\tilde{U})-\Lambda(\bar{U}))\tilde{\Psi}_{x}

\displaystyle+\left[\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)+\mathcal{O}(|\Psi|)+\mathcal{O}(|\dot{\delta}|)\right]\tilde{\Psi}+\dot{\delta}\mathcal{O}(1)+\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)

with a diagonal matrix
\bar{E}
of the form

\bar{E}(t,x)=E(\bar{U})+\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|).

Using the weighted inner product

\left\langle\tilde{\Psi}_{j},\alpha_{j}\tilde{\Psi}_{j}\right\rangle

where
\alpha_{j}
is as in (
40
) with possibly larger constant
C_{\alpha,j}>0
and smaller exponent
c_{\alpha,j}>0
one deduces similar to the treatment of
\Phi
that

(45)

\displaystyle||\tilde{\Psi}(t)||_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle Ce^{-\theta t}||\tilde{\Psi}(0)||_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta(t-s)}\left(||\Phi(s)||_{L^{2}}^{2}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|^{2}\right)ds

\displaystyle+C\varepsilon\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta(t-s)}||\tilde{\Psi}(s)||_{L^{2}}^{2}ds.

The term

(46)

\displaystyle\left\langle\tilde{\Psi}_{j},(\lambda_{j}(\tilde{U})-\lambda_{j}(\bar{U}))\alpha_{j}\partial_{x}\tilde{\Psi}_{j}\right\rangle,

which arises when taking the weighted inner product of
\tilde{\Psi}
with equation (
44
) and whose analog in the
\Phi
estimates
was handled simply by smallness of
\Psi
, needs to be taken care of by partial
integration using the Taylor expansion

\lambda_{j}(\tilde{U})-\lambda_{j}(\bar{U})=\int_{0}^{1}d\lambda_{j}(\bar{U}+sU)ds\cdot U.

Doing so yields

\left\langle\tilde{\Psi}_{j},(\lambda_{j}(\tilde{U})-\lambda_{j}(\bar{U}))\alpha_{j}\partial_{x}\tilde{\Psi}_{j}\right\rangle\leq C\varepsilon\left\langle\tilde{\Psi}_{j},\alpha_{j}\tilde{\Psi}_{j}\right\rangle

where
C
still depends on the choice of constants in the defining equation (
40
) of the weights
\alpha_{j}
since
the derivative
\partial_{x}\alpha_{j}
appears when carrying out the partial integration.
C
becomes a fixed constant as soon as the constants have been chosen
to eliminate the terms with exponentially decaying weights. Thus, (
46
)
feeds into the
\varepsilon
term in the second line of (
45
).

Gronwall’s Lemma and using the definition of
\tilde{\Psi}
together with
the
L^{2}
damping estimate (
43
) for
\Phi
gives

(47)

\displaystyle||\Psi(t)||_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle Ce^{-\theta t}||(\Phi,\Psi)(0)||_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta(t-s)}\left(||\Phi(s)||_{L^{2}}^{2}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|^{2}\right)ds

with possibly larger
C
and smaller
\theta
than in (
45
).

Finally,

\tilde{\Upsilon}=\Upsilon+\Theta\Psi

satisfies an equation of the form

\displaystyle\tilde{\Upsilon}_{t}+\Lambda(\bar{U})\tilde{\Upsilon}_{x}=

\displaystyle\bar{E}(t,x)\tilde{\Upsilon}+\dot{\delta}\tilde{\Upsilon}_{x}-(\Lambda(\tilde{U})-\Lambda(\bar{U}))\tilde{\Upsilon}_{x}

\displaystyle+\left[\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)+\mathcal{O}(|\Psi|)+\mathcal{O}(|\dot{\delta}|)\right]\tilde{\Upsilon}+\dot{\delta}\mathcal{O}(1)+\mathcal{O}(|\Phi|)+\mathcal{O}(|\Psi|)

which gives with the techniques described above an estimate of the form

(48)

\displaystyle||\tilde{\Upsilon}(t)||_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle Ce^{-\theta t}||\tilde{\Upsilon}(0)||_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta(t-s)}\left(||\Psi(s)||_{L^{2}}^{2}+||\Phi(s)||_{L^{2}}^{2}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|^{2}\right)ds

\displaystyle+C\varepsilon\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta(t-s)}||\tilde{\Upsilon}(s)||_{L^{2}}^{2}ds.

Similar to the
C^{0}_{b}
-case, use a large multiple of the
\Psi

L^{2}
-estimate (
47
)
to remove the
\Psi
forcing in (
48
), and
unwrap definitions to find the damping estimate

\displaystyle||\Upsilon(t)||_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle Ce^{-\theta t}||(\Phi(0),\Psi(0),\Upsilon(0))||_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\theta(t-s)}\left(||\Phi(s)||_{L^{2}}^{2}+|\dot{\delta}(s)|^{2}\right)ds

which together with the estimates (
43
), (
47
)
implies (
39
) since
L(\tilde{U})
and
R(\tilde{U})
are bounded.

∎
