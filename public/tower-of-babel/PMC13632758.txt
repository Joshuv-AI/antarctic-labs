Ultrahigh discharge voltage plateau of 3.2 V achieved in Li-CO2 batteries using iron polyphthalocyanine

Ning Zhao, Yi Zhang, Li-Min Liu, Han-Qi Zhang, Zheng Meng, Shu-Jiang Ding, Qiang Wang, Xiao-Fei Hu

Abstract

Rechargeable lithium-carbon dioxide (Li-CO2) batteries offer great potential for greenhouse gas use and high-energy-density storage. However, their practical application is hindered by the formation of a thermodynamically stable Li2CO3 (lithium carbonate) product that is hard to decompose during charging. Highly crystalline and conductive reticular material iron polyphthalocyanine (pFeIIPc) as a solid-state redox mediator into Li-CO2 batteries tailors the cathode reaction pathway, enabling stable Li2C2O4 (lithium oxalate) generation and an extraordinary discharge plateau of 3.2 V beyond the theoretical limit. Beyond delivering an ultrahigh discharge capacity of 28,790 milliampere-hours per gram and reversible cyclability over 1400 hours (cutoff capacity of 1000 milliampere-hours per gram) at 100 milliamperes per gram, the Li-CO2 batteries exhibit excellent temperature adaptability (−40 to 80°C). The Li-CO2 pouch cells using pFeIIPc as the solid-state redox mediator achieve a high energy density of 678 watt-hours per kilogram on the basis of total device mass and maintain reversible cycling for 251 hours. Our work pioneers a promising pathway toward practical high-efficiency Li-CO2 batteries.

INTRODUCTION

The excessive consumption on fossil fuels has resulted in an energy crisis and substantial carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions, exerting profound adverse impacts on environmental integrity and sustainable economic development (–). Consequently, developing an innovative electrochemical energy storage device with dual energy-saving and emission-reduction capabilities has become an urgent imperative. Recently, the reversible lithium-carbon dioxide (Li-CO2) batteries have emerged as promising candidates for next-generation energy storage systems owing to the superb theoretical energy density of 1876 W·hour kg−1 and CO2 utilization efficiency (–). Furthermore, Li-CO2 batteries exhibit unparalleled application potential in CO2-rich environments, particularly for deep-sea missions and Mars exploration where atmospheric CO2 concentrations exceed 95% (–).

However, Li2CO3 pathway Li-CO2 batteries typically exhibit limited discharge voltages of 2.0 to 2.5 V because of strong CO2 bond interactions and sluggish reaction kinetics, notably lower than the theoretical value of 2.8 V (4Li+ + 3CO2 + 4e− → 2Li2CO3 + C, E0 = 2.80 V versus Li+/Li) (–). Although the introduction of cobalt phthalocyanine has increased the discharge voltage of the Li-CO2 battery via the Li2CO3-product pathway to 2.98 V, this additive suffers risks of contaminating lithium metal anodes, and the high thermodynamic stability of Li2CO3 [∆fG0(Li2CO3) = −1132.1 kJ mol−1] results in a large overpotential, necessitating a charging voltage as high as 4.38 V for decomposition (, –). Notably, the theoretical decomposition voltage of side reaction in the Li-CO2 battery (Li2CO3 − 2e− → 2Li+ + CO2 + 1/21O2, E0 = 4.1 V versus Li/Li+) is 4.1 V, indicating a high probability of parasitic reactions occurring (). The radical oxygen (1O2) produced by parasitic reactions may can aggressively degrade both electrodes and the electrolyte, ultimately leading to abrupt death of Li-CO2 batteries.

In contrast to the Li2CO3 pathway (2.8 V), the Li2C2O4-product route (3.01 V) exhibits superior theoretical discharge voltage and faster reaction kinetics in the Li-CO2 battery (, ). Also, Li2C2O4 exhibits both lower thermodynamic stability (∆fG0 = −286 kJ mol−1) and diminished decomposition energy barriers (Ea = 0.75 eV) relative to Li2CO3 (Ea = 2.1 eV). As a result, Li-CO2 batteries following the Li2C2O4-product route deliver high energy efficiency and extended cycle life (, , ). Attractively, recent studies have confirmed that heterogeneous catalysts (e.g., Mo- and Ti-based materials) and homogeneous catalysts (e.g., Cu- and Ru-based complexes) can ensure the stable formation of Li2C2O4 in the Li-CO2 battery through a two-electron reaction (2Li+ + 2CO2 + 2e− ↔ Li2C2O4, E0 = 3.01 V versus Li/Li+) (–, , ). On the one hand, the use of heterogeneous catalysts enables stable formation of Li2C2O4 in Li-CO2 batteries, but the practical discharge voltage (2.8 V) of the Li2C2O4-product route Li-CO2 battery remains below the theoretical value (3.01 V), and further improvements in cycle life are still required (). On the other hand, homogeneous catalysts (soluble redox mediators) demonstrably elevate the discharge voltage plateau to ∼3.0 V, yet the underlying mechanism for this high-voltage platform remains unclear. Homogeneous catalysts inherently suffer from inevitable critical limitations, including poor solubility, sluggish kinetics, severe corrosion of lithium anodes, and pronounced self-decomposition, which severely restrict the practical application of homogeneous catalysts in Li-CO2 batteries (, , ). Accordingly, the rational design of high-efficiency solid-state redox mediators represents a promising strategy for elevating the discharge voltage plateau (to >3.01 V) in the Li2C2O4-product route Li-CO2 battery.

Herein, a highly crystalline and conductive reticular material iron polyphthalocyanine (pFeIIPc) was introduced as a solid-state redox mediator for Li-CO2 batteries (). As shown in , using pFeIIPc as the solid-state redox mediator in Li-CO2 batteries not only achieves stable generation of Li2C2O4 but also boosts the discharge voltage to 3.2 V, exceeding theoretical limit. The solid-state redox mediator pFeIIPc features a conductive and stable network structure, along with precisely distributed single-active sites and multiactive centers. Owing to pFeIIPc’s ultrahigh electron affinity and excellent stabilizing effect on Li2C2O4, pFeIIPc preferentially accepts electrons instead of CO2 and then transfers the captured electrons to CO2 to form CO2− (activation) while simultaneously adsorbing Li+ ions (). Ultimately, the monomers undergo coupled catalysis to form the final product Li2C2O4, which is stabilized by pFeIIPc. Therefore, the Li-CO2 battery with pFeIIPc as the solid-state redox mediator exhibits an ultrahigh discharge voltage plateau of 3.2 V, an exceptional discharge capacity of 28,790 mA·hour g−1 with a reversible charge capacity of 26,338 mA·hour·g−1, a low voltage gap of 0.58 V, a long cycling life exceeding 1480 hours, and ultrawide temperature range operation (−40° to 80°C). As a proof of concept, the Li-CO2 pouch cells achieve an energy density of 678 W·hour kg−1 on the basis of the total device mass and demonstrate stable cycling performance. This work is expected to accelerate the practical application of Li-CO2 batteries.

RESULTS

Electrochemical performance of the Li-CO2 battery catalyzed by pFeIIPc

Highly crystalline and conductive pFeIIPc has been successfully synthesized through the annulation of tetracyanobenzene in the presence of transition metals (fig. S1). A typical three-electrode cell with a controlled atmosphere was used to investigate the catalytic activity of the pFeIIPc cathode toward the CO2 reduction reaction. As shown in and fig. S2, under an Ar atmosphere, the solid-state redox mediator pFeIIPc exhibits a pair of redox peaks at 3.23 V (Ed1) and 3.45 V (Ec1), which suggests the possible occurrence of a reversible redox reaction between Fe(II) and Fe(I) in the solid-state redox mediator pFeIIPc. Similarly, a reduction peak observed at the same position (3.23 V, Ed2) on the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curve indicates that the same reduction process occurs in the solid-state redox mediator pFeIIPc under a CO2 atmosphere. This can be ascribed to the reduction reaction of pFeIIPc and pFeIPc, as traced in . In addition, compared to the oxidation peak observed in Ar, the oxidation peak in CO2 appears at 3.75 V (Ec2), which is attributed to the decomposition of the discharge product Li2C2O4, facilitated by the electron-transfer cascade from pFeIPc to CO2 during Li2C2O4 formation. Both in situ online gas chromatography–mass spectrometry and experimental results demonstrate that pFeIIPc, after gaining electrons and being reduced to pFeIPc, can promote the CO2 reduction reaction (figs. S3 to S5). As shown in fig. S6, ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy shows no notable shift in the spectral features after discharge in CO2, while a blue shift in the characteristic peaks is observed after discharge in Ar, which aligns with the expected reduction of pFeIIPc to pFeIPc (). By comparing the above results, it can be concluded that in the presence of the solid-state redox mediator pFeIIPc, the reaction pathway of the Li-CO2 battery is altered by the transfer of electrons from pFeIPc to CO2.pFeIIPc+e−↔pFeIPc(1)

pFeIIPc, cobalt polyphthalocyanine, nickel polyphthalocyanine, and copper polyphthalocyanine were used as a catalytic cathode in Li-CO2 batteries. The Li-CO2 battery using solely pFeIIPc as the solid-state redox mediator displays a discharge voltage plateau of 3.2 V (fig. S7). Li-CO2 batteries were assembled with and without pFeIIPc to further investigate the electrochemical performance of the solid-state redox mediator pFeIIPc. The full charge-discharge process was conducted within a voltage range of 2.0 to 4.5 V at 100 mA g−1, as provided in . shows that the solid pFeIIPc cathode in the CO2 atmosphere exhibits outstanding stability, with an initial discharge capacity as high as 28,790 mA·hour g−1 and a reversible capacity of 26,338 mA·hour g−1. Also, the solid pFeIIPc cathode shows severely limited performance in the Ar atmosphere, with discharge and charge capacities of only 1560 and 1364 mA·hour g−1, respectively. In the control group, the Li-CO2 battery without pFeIIPc demonstrates limited performance under CO2, achieving merely 9468 mA·hour g−1 discharge capacity and 7531 mA·hour g−1 recharge capacity. As illustrated in , the Li-CO2 battery containing pFeIIPc demonstrates not only a high discharge voltage plateau of 3.2 V (considerably exceeding the theoretical discharge voltage of 3.01 V) but also achieves a reduced charge voltage of 3.7 V compared to its pFeIIPc-free counterpart. It exhibits a substantially lower overpotential of merely 0.58 V, representing just one-third of that observed in the control group (1.74 V). Meanwhile, rate capability is a critical parameter for evaluating reaction kinetics, which was assessed at multiple current densities under a fixed cutoff capacity of 1000 mA·hour g−1. As shown in , with the current density increased progressively from 100 to 1000 mA g−1, the Li-CO2 battery with pFeIIPc demonstrated a controlled voltage gap expansion (0.59 → 1.32 V), markedly lower than the pronounced polarization increase (1.66 → 2.08 V) observed in the pFeIIPc-free counterpart cell. Notably, upon recovering the current density to 100 mA g−1, the potential gap of the Li-CO2 battery with pFeIIPc returned to 0.73 V. The Li-CO2 battery with pFeIIPc maintains a discharge voltage plateau exceeding 3.01 V even at 600 mA g−1, with a high median discharge voltage of 2.8 V persisting at ultrahigh 1000 mA g−1. To systematically evaluate the cycling stability, Li-CO2 batteries with and without pFeIIPc were subjected to extended cycling tests at varying current densities with a cutoff capacity of 1000 mA·hour g−1. demonstrates the stable cycling stability of the pFeIIPc cathode, maintaining stable operation for 40 cycles with a 3.01-V discharge termination potential and <4.1-V charge voltage plateau, and the energy efficiency stays above 75%. Nevertheless, the accumulation of discharge products and partial deactivation of the catalyst during prolonged cycling bring about a decay in the discharge plateau of the Li-CO2 battery after 45 cycles (fig. S8). Moreover, the Li-CO2 battery with pFeIIPc also displays prolonged cycling stability, maintaining continuous operation for 1482 hours at 100 mA g−1 ( and fig. S9). This is due to the extremely low solubility of pFeIIPc in the electrolyte, as well as the fact that during cycling in the Li-CO2 battery, the crystal structure of pFeIIPc remains largely intact without significant evolution or degradation, and the valence state of its catalytic active sites remains unchanged (figs. S10 and S11). To evaluate high-rate cycling stability, Li-CO2 batteries with pFeIIPc were tested at elevated current densities under a cutoff capacity of 1000 mA·hour g−1. The cells exhibited sustained operation for 191 and 139 hours at 600 and 1000 mA g−1 (fig. S12). This special electrochemical performance stems directly from pFeIIPc’s superior conductive and catalytic activity, which mitigates polarization and enhances reaction kinetics.

The Li-CO2 battery with pFeIIPc maintains excellent performance across an ultrawide operating temperature range beyond room temperature. The electrochemical performance of the Li-CO2 battery with pFeIIPc under a cut-off capacity of 500 mA·hour g−1 was evaluated at temperatures ranging from −40° to 80°C (−40°, −30°, −20°, 0°, 20°, 40°, 60°, and 80°C), as presented in . Despite the fact that the voltage gap is slightly larger at −30° and −20°C, the pFeIIPc-based Li-CO2 battery retains a stable 2.9-V discharge voltage plateau and consistently operates above 3.01 V from 0° to 80°C (fig. S13). The Li-CO2 battery with pFeIIPc demonstrates excellent electrochemical performance over a wide temperature range. However, the discharge plateau inevitably declines with decreasing temperature—a phenomenon rooted in the temperature-dependent electrical conductivity of pFeIIPc, which diminishes as the temperature falls. Furthermore, theoretical calculations also reveal that the Gibbs free energy barrier of the rate-determining step during the discharge process of Li-CO2 batteries rises with decreasing temperature (figs. S14 and S15). This further corroborates that kinetic limitation is another critical factor underlying the voltage decline. The battery maintains normal operation even at −40°C, which is attributed to the extraordinary catalytic properties of pFeIIPc across an ultrawide operating temperature range. At the same time, the porous structure in the separator also helps to lower the freezing point of the electrolyte to some extent, further enhancing the performance of the Li-CO2 battery at low temperatures (fig. S16). In situ temperature-dependent Raman spectroscopy further confirms this observation (, and fig. S17), with pFeIIPc maintaining structural integrity over −60° to 80°C, confirming superior wide-temperature stability. In addition, the Li-CO2 battery using pFeIIPc as the solid-state redox mediator exhibits stable cycling performance at −20° and 80°C (fig. S18).

Product characterization of Li-CO2 batteries catalyzed by pFeIIPc

To investigate the discharge products and the reversibility of the Li-CO2 battery, a series of in situ and ex situ characterizations were conducted during the charge-discharge process. As evident in , products deposited on the carbon fibers were clearly visible after discharging but disappeared following complete recharge. Ex situ x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) were performed to further identify the species of the discharge products. The XPS spectra of C 1s and Li 1s were comparatively analyzed in the pristine state, after discharge, and after recharge ( and fig. S19). The XPS spectra of C 1s from the discharged electrode clearly exhibits characteristic peaks at 288.90 eV (O─C═O), which unambiguously confirm the formation of Li2C2O4 as the discharge product.

Two prominent signals observed after discharge at mass/charge ratios (m/z) of 101.98 and 88 in and fig. S20 exhibit exact correspondence with Li2C2O4 and disappear after charging, providing direct evidence for Li2C2O4 formation during discharge. In addition, the TOF-SIMS results show that no prominent signals appear at m/z 23 and 60, confirming that Li2CO3 is absent in the products (fig. S20). The ex situ x-ray diffraction (XRD) and Raman analyses of the deep discharge products also confirm that Li2C2O4 is the final discharge product (fig. S21). The dynamic evolution of discharge/charge processes was monitored in real time using specially designed in situ XRD and Raman electrochemical cells ( and fig. S22). presents the galvanostatic charge-discharge profiles of the in situ Li-CO2 XRD battery using the pFeIIPc cathode, demonstrating a reversible capacity of 1600 mA·hour g−1 at a current density of 200 mA g−1. The XRD diffraction peak intensities of Li2C2O4 at 2θ = 36.44° and 40.64° progressively intensify with increasing discharge depth and systematically diminish during the charging process, exhibiting a reversible evolution pattern. Consistent with the in situ XRD findings, complementary in situ Raman spectroscopy corroborated these observations. As documents, the Raman peak intensities associated with Li2C2O4 at 865 cm−1 (C─C) and 1486 cm−1 (C═O) exhibited a discharge depth–dependent enhancement. These spectral features exhibited progressive attenuation during charging and ultimately disappeared upon reaching the full state of charge. Furthermore, the discharge product of the Li-CO2 battery without pFeIIPc as the solid-state redox mediator is Li2CO3 (fig. Mechanistic analysis of the ultrahigh discharge plateau

Quantitative differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) was performed to analyze the electrons transferred on the pFeIIPc surface as well as the consumption and evolution of CO2. Dual-gas-path in situ DEMS testing during the charge-discharge process is illustrated in and fig. S24. The transferred charge-to-CO2 consumption ratio (e−/CO2) for the reaction 2Li+ + 2CO2 + 2e− → Li2C2O4 is determined to be 1 during the charge-discharge process. The near-unity e−/CO2 ratio (0.97) observed during discharge suggests highly efficient CO2 utilization and effective Li2C2O4 formation on the pFeIIPc electrode (). Moreover, analyses of the evolved gas species and the CO2 evolution mechanism were conducted during the charge process. As illustrated in and fig. S25, only CO2 is generated during charging, with no detectable generation of O2 and CO, indicating the reversibility of CO2 evolution. DEMS measurements show a 1.04 e−/CO2 ratio during charging, consistent with the theoretical value of 1 e−/CO2 predicted by the reversible CO2 evolution reaction (Li2C2O4 → 2Li+ + 2CO2 + 2e−). Variations in charge/discharge currents and carrier gas flow rates result in differing CO2 fluxes, yet the experimental-to-theoretical value ratio is close to 1 for both processes. Therefore, these findings confirm that the Li-CO2 battery using pFeIIPc as the solid-state redox mediator undergoes a two-electron transfer process, with Li2C2O4 as the main discharge product.

To elucidate the formation mechanism of Li2C2O4 and ultrahigh discharge platform mechanism on the solid pFeIIPc cathode, the cathode surface composition at various electrochemical stages was systematically analyzed by ex situ XPS and density functional theory (DFT). First, XPS analysis reveals that the Fe 2p spectrum of the pristine pFeIIPc cathode under the Ar atmosphere (, top) exhibits a single characteristic peak at ∼707.2 eV, confirming the exclusive presence of Fe(II) in the pristine pFeIIPc cathode. As the discharge progresses (, middle), a new peak emerges at ∼705.4 eV under Ar, attributed to the increased Fe(I) state. Concurrently, the Fe(I) signal diminishes substantially, indicating near-complete reduction to Fe(I). Notably, the Fe(I) valence state shows no change after CO2-assisted discharge, in sharp contrast to the Ar case (, bottom). This provides direct evidence for the rapid electron shuttle mechanism: Reduced Fe(I) promptly donates its electrons to CO2 and is therefore oxidized back to Fe(II). This conclusion is fully consistent with the CV results, corroborating the proposed reaction mechanism and ultrahigh discharge platform mechanism. Subsequently, the formation of Li2C2O4 in the CO2 atmosphere is confirmed by the appearance of two characteristic peaks: C 1s (O─C═O) at 288.90 eV and Li 1s at 55.34 eV (). This phenomenon was not observed in the Ar atmosphere, confirming the CO2-dependent nature of Li2C2O4 formation and pFeIIPc solely as a redox mediator. An unexpected Li signal appeared on the electrode after discharge in Ar, which is attributed to the precipitation of lithium salts in the electrolyte and the contribution of Li from the cathode electrolyte interstate. Furthermore, DFT calculations reveal that pFeIIPc exhibits an electron affinity of 4.91 eV, accompanied by electron cloud redistribution upon CO2 adsorption on pFeIPc. (figs. S26 and S27). This thermodynamic preference ensures pFeIIPc as the preferential electron acceptor: pFeIIPc is preferentially reduced (pFeIIPc + e− → pFeIPc) and subsequently mediates electron donation to CO2 for the ensuing electrochemical reactions. In addition, a control system with Fe(III) active sites was constructed, demonstrating that uncoordinated Fe(III) lacking phthalocyanine ligands fails to catalyze the reduction reaction efficiently, whereas the present system realizes efficient electrocatalysis via the Fe(II)/Fe(I) redox cycle (fig. S28).

Reaction pathway for Li2C2O4 formation from CO2 catalyzed by pFeIIPc

The DFT calculation was performed to systematically investigate the stability of Li2C2O4 on the pFeIIPc surface. and figs. S29 to S31 present the DFT-optimized adsorption configurations and corresponding adsorption energies of Li2C2O4 on C32H16N8Fe, C58H26N16Fe2, and carbon nanotubes (CNTs). The calculated adsorption energy of Li2C2O4 on C32H16N8Fe (−1.21 eV) is markedly higher than those on C58H26N16Fe2 (−0.68 eV) and CNTs (−0.61 eV), indicating preferential stabilization of Li2C2O4 as discharge products on the C32H16N8Fe surface during the discharge process. To clarify the reaction mechanism of Li2C2O4 on the redox mediator surface during discharge, two distinct reaction pathways using monomers C32H16N8Fe and C58H26N16Fe2 as catalytic units were computationally investigated. presents the free energy profiles for the most probable 2e−/2CO2 nucleation pathways of Li2C2O4 formation on surfaces C32H16N8Fe and C58H26N16Fe2. The two dominant reaction pathways proceed as follows:

Path I∗+(Li++e−)→∗Li++CO2(g)→∗LiCO2+(Li++e−)→∗Li2CO2+CO2(g)→∗Li2C2O4→∗+Li2C2O4

Path II#+2(Li++e−)→#2Li++CO2(g)→#LiCO2Li++CO2(g)→#2(LiCO2)→#Li2C2O4→#+Li2C2O4

In both pathways, all Li+-involving steps with electron transfer exhibit exothermic downhill profiles in the free energy diagrams, confirming their spontaneous nature. However, both CO2 adsorption on *Li2CO2 to form *Li2C2O4 in pathway I and that on #2(LiCO2) to form #Li2C2O4 in pathway II exhibit endothermic characteristics. These endothermic uphill steps serve as the rate-determining steps for Li2C2O4 formation in both pathways I and II. The activation energy barrier for the rate-determining step in pathway I (0.49 eV) is substantially lower than that in pathway II (0.71 eV). The overall Gibbs free energy change (∆G) for Li2C2O4 decomposition in pathway I (2.57 eV) is considerably more negative than that in pathway II (4.68 eV). These findings unambiguously demonstrate that the steady Li2C2O4 electrochemistry via the 2e−/2CO2 reaction preferentially follows pathway I in pFeIIPc-based Li-CO2 batteries (). On the basis of the optimal reaction pathway determined through dynamic simulations via Li2C2O4 formation, we have captured the dynamic evolution of characteristic vibrational peaks of Li-O and C2O42− intermediates during discharge in in situ Raman spectroscopy (). Li2C2O4 can serve as the final discharge product in the Li-CO2 battery because of its stronger adsorption energy with pFeIIPc. In addition, projected density of states analysis shows that the interaction between Li2C2O4 and pFeIIPc primarily occurs near the Fermi level (−2 to 0 eV). In comparison to Li2CO3 (−4 to 0 eV), the O-p orbital distribution in *Li2C2O4 is narrower, indicating that the p-electrons of the oxygen atoms in *Li2C2O4 are more concentrated near the Fermi level, resulting in a lower and more stable energy state ( and figs. S32 and S33).

Proof-of-concept Li-CO2 pouch cells were demonstrated using pFeIIPc as the solid-state redox mediator paired with lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide/tetraglyme (LiTFSI/TEGDME) electrolytes. As shown in , the Li-CO2 pouch cell (5.5 cm by 4 cm, 2.19 g) exhibits a discharge voltage plateau of 3.1 V with a high discharge capacity of 479 mA·hour at room temperature under 100 mA g−1, delivering an impressive energy density of 678 W·hour kg−1 based on the total pouch cell mass. In addition, the Li-CO2 pouch cell using pFeIIPc as solid-state redox mediator cathodes with a cutoff capacity of 500 mA·hour g−1 at 100 mA g−1 maintains a discharge voltage plateau above 3.01 V for 19 reversible cycles, with a total cycle duration of up to 251 hours (). As displayed in , the pouch cell successfully powered light-emitting diodes (LEDs) under simulated Martian conditions (95% CO2), demonstrating that the pFeIIPc-based Li-CO2 battery technology holds significant potential for practical applications.

DISCUSSION

In summary, we proposed a novel strategy that uses pFeIIPc with ultrahigh electron affinity as the solid-state redox mediator to achieve the Li2C2O4-product pathway while elevating the discharge voltage plateau of Li-CO2 batteries to 3.2 V, enabling long-term stable reversible cycling on the basis of the Li2C2O4 pathway. The reaction mechanism of Li-CO2 batteries using pFeIIPc as the cathode material is collectively elucidated by electrochemical studies, in situ spectroscopic characterization, and DFT calculation. During discharge, pFeIIPc preferentially accepts electrons from the anode instead of CO2, being reduced to pFeIPc. Subsequently, pFeIPc rapidly transfers these electrons to CO2 (thereby being oxidized back to pFeIIPc), and ultimately, the reduced CO2 reacts with Li+ to form Li2C2O4. Notably, pFeIIPc exhibits strong adsorption energy toward Li2C2O4, thereby stabilizing the Li2C2O4 product. Given that the reduction potential of pFeIIPc accepting electrons to form pFeIPc exceeds 3.01 V, this process results in the battery demonstrating a discharge voltage plateau higher than its theoretical value. As a proof-of-concept reliability verification candidate, pFeIIPc demonstrated exceptional catalytic kinetics in the Li-CO2 battery, delivering an ultrahigh discharge capacity of 28,790 mA·hour g−1 and reversible cyclability exceeding 1400 hours at 100 mA g−1. The introduction of pFeIIPc not only enables the Li2C2O4 pathway in Li-CO2 batteries but also achieves a discharge voltage plateau of 3.2 V. The designed Li-CO2 battery system enables superb wide-temperature operability (−40° to 80°C). Ultimately, our engineered pouch cell achieves an energy density of 678 W·hour kg−1 while maintaining stable cycling performance over 251 hours. This research provides a universally effective strategy for the practical application of Li-CO2 batteries.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Super P, poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) [PVDF-HFP; average Mn (number-average molecular weight) of ∼130,000], and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP; 99.5%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The LiTFSI/TEGDME electrolyte (1.0 M) was purchased from Fosai. Carboxylated multiwalled CNTs (>95%) were purchased from MREDA. 1,2,4,5-Tetracyanobenzene was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Iron powder (Fe; 99.9%, 50 nm, 7439-89-6) was purchased from Macklin.

Synthesis of pFeIIPc

1,2,4,5-Tetracyanobenzene (50 mg, 0.28 mmol) was mixed with iron powder (31.5 mg, 0.56 mmol). The mixture was thoroughly ground and transferred into a Pyrex tube measuring outer diameter × inside diameter = 10 mm by 8 mm. The Pyrex tube was then evacuated and flame sealed with the total length kept at ∼5.0 cm. The glass tube was heated to 400°C at 5°C/min and kept for 4 hours in a tube furnace under vacuum conditions. After cooling to room temperature, the product was washed several times with ethanol, iron chloride solution, and H2O and then dried at 60°C in a vacuum oven to afford a purple-black powder.

Li-CO2 battery assembly

In the preparation of the porous cathode, for the experimental group, 45 wt % Super P, 45 wt % pFeIIPc, and 10 wt % PVDF were mixed in appropriate NMP. For the control group, 45 wt % Super P, 45 wt % CNTs, and 10 wt % PVDF were mixed in appropriate NMP. The resulting slurry was then pasted on a carbon paper, and the loading mass of active materials on the carbon cloth was ∼0.2 mg. The LiTFSI/TEGDME electrolyte (1.0 M) was used as the electrolyte. CR2032 coin batteries with holes for CO2 permeation were assembled with the as-prepared cathodes, glass fiber separator, Li metal anode, and 80 μl of as-prepared electrolyte in a glovebox filled with ultrapure argon. The assembled Li-CO2 batteries were then placed in a sealed glass bottle filled with CO2 and rested for 8 hours before testing with a Land battery tester at different temperatures.

Characterizations and electrochemical measurements

CV testing was conducted in a specialized three-electrode cell, with a scan rate of 10 mV/s and a voltage testing range of 2.0 to 4.0 V during the process. The morphologies and structures of the discharge product were characterized by using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Gemini SEM 500), and XRD patterns were collected with a Bruker D8 ADVANC diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation at a scan rate of 2° min−1, which was used to acquire the in situ XRD results. The Renishaw Raman spectrometer (InVia Qontor) with a 532-nm wavelength incident laser was used to obtain the in situ Raman spectra. XPS measurements were performed on a Thermo Fisher Scientific K-Alpha instrument. DEMS measurements were performed by a quadrupole mass spectrometer (PrismaPro) with a turbomolecular pump (Pfeiffer Vacuum). The mechanism analysis was obtained through TOF-SIMS (M6) and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (Agilent 8890-5977C). A Land cycler (Wuhan Land Electronic Co., Ltd.) was used for galvanostatic charge-discharge tests of Li-CO2 batteries. The calculation of specific capacities and current densities were all based on the mass loading of pFePc and CNTs. The electrochemical stability of pFePc and CNTs were performed by CV conducted on an electrochemical workstation (CHI 660E) at the sweep rate of 1.0 mV s−1.

Computational methods

Electronic calculations were performed to determine the adsorption energy and free energy profiles using spin-polarized DFT in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) with a plane-wave basis set and the VASP method (, ). The exchange-correlation potential was treated by using generalized gradient approximation with Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof parametrization (). A vacuum region of about 15 Å was applied to avoid the interaction between adjacent images. The energy cutoff was set to be 450 eV. A Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh of 1 × 1 × 1 was used for structural optimizations. The structures were fully relaxed until the maximum force on each atom was less than 0.02 eV/Å, and the energy convergent standard was 10−5 eV.

i: C32H16N8FeII + Li+ + e− → C32H16N8FeILi+

ii: C32H16N8FeILi+ + CO2(g) → C32H16N8FeIILiCO2

iii: C32H16N8FeIILiCO2 + Li+ + e− → C32H16N8FeILi2CO2

iv: C32H16N8FeILi2CO2 + CO2(g) → C32H16N8FeIILi2C2O4

v: C32H16N8FeIILi2C2O4 → C32H16N8FeII + Li2C2O4

Pathway II:

i: C58H26N16Fe2II + 2Li+ + 2e− → C58H26N16Fe2I2Li+

ii: C58H26N16Fe2I2Li+ + CO2(g) → C58H26N16FeIFeIILiCO2Li+

iii: C58H26N16FeIFeIILiCO2Li+ + CO2(g) → C58H26N16Fe2IILiCO2CO2Li

iv: C58H26N16Fe2IILiCO2CO2Li → C58H26N16Fe2IILi2C2O4

v: C58H26N16Fe2IILi2C2O4 → C58H26N16Fe2II + Li2C2O4
