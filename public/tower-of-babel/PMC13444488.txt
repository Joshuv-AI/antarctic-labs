Lumbosacral aggressive osteoblastoma with extensive flare phenomenon: A diagnostic pitfall
Authors: Demamu Agegn, Hilaludin Mohammed, Kifle Tadesse, Helen Tesfay Tadesse, Zewditu Nigatu, Bethelhem Belachew, Biniyam Beyene Tabor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While osteoblastoma is a rare benign bone-forming tumor, its aggressive variant can mimic malignant bone tumors. This resemblance is especially pronounced when accompanied by a flare phenomenon. We report a 12-year-old female with progressive neurological deficits and a lumbosacral mass. The initial MRI suggested a malignant bone tumor due to an ill-defined, avidly enhancing lesion at L4-S1 with severe canal stenosis. However, CT imaging revealed a more benign appearance, showing a well-defined lytic-sclerotic lesion with a narrow zone of transition. Histopathology confirmed the diagnosis of an aggressive osteoblastoma. This case highlights the importance of recognizing the flare phenomenon as a potential imaging pitfall in aggressive osteoblastoma, emphasizing the necessity of multimodal imaging-histopathology correlation for correct diagnosis and management.

Introduction

Osteoblastoma is a rare benign bone tumor accounting for 1% of bone tumors that tends to affect the axial skeleton, commonly the posterior element. Despite being a histologically benign bone tumor, its aggressive variant can be larger (>4 cm) and locally aggressive and has a potential for local recurrence resembling a malignant bone tumor [1]. In addition to the locally aggressive nature of the aggressive osteoblastoma, another important source of diagnostic error is the flare phenomenon, characterized by a severe inflammatory response involving the marrow of adjacent normal bone and soft tissue. This unusual phenomenon is likely due to the interleukin-mediated immune response mounted by the patient to the tumor. These findings may closely simulate infiltrative malignant bone tumors. While MRI is better in the characterization of the central canal, neural foramina, and paraspinal soft tissue extension, it is prone to overestimation of the extent of the lesion. CT is vital for accurately assessing lesion size, cortical involvement, and internal mineralization, thereby playing a central role in preoperative planning, biopsy guidance, and surgical decision-making. Accurate recognition of the aggressive nature of aggressive osteoblastoma and flare-related changes is essential, as misinterpretation can result in erroneous diagnosis and overtreatment [2,3].

We report an unusual case of multivertebral lumbosacral aggressive osteoblastoma with an extensive flare phenomenon, presented with back pain and urinary incontinence mimicking a malignant bone tumor.

Case presentation

A 12-year-old girl presented with a 3-year history of progressive back pain and limping, followed by the development of a gradually enlarging lower back swelling and urinary incontinence. She had completed a full course of anti-tuberculous therapy for presumed spinal tuberculous without clinical improvement. On examination, the patient appeared chronically ill and malnourished. Vital signs were stable. A nontender, 7 × 4 cm soft tissue swelling was present over the left posterior lower back. Neurologic examination revealed saddle anesthesia with preserved anal sphincter tone. At admission, WBC was 9.93. Neutrophils: 64.3%; hematocrit: 38.4%. Urine analysis was normal.

With clinical consideration of compressive myelopathy caused by a lumbosacral mass, a multisequence postcontrast lumbosacral MRI was done, which revealed a large, lobulated, ill-defined lesion centered at the left L4 and L5 laminae and pedicles and partially involving the S1 vertebral body with extension into the spinal canal and left-side neural foramina. It was causing significant spinal canal and neural foraminal stenosis. It demonstrated T1 and T2 hypointense signals with avid postcontrast enhancement and a central T2 hyperintense focus having no postcontrast enhancement, suggesting cystic necrosis. Subsequent contrast-enhanced CT of the thorax, abdomen, and pelvis demonstrated a large, expansile, lobulated, well-defined, mixed lytic sclerotic lesion centered over the left L4 and L5 laminae and pedicles and partially involving the S1 vertebral body. The mass had a central canal and neural foraminal extension, resulting in severe central canal and left-side neural foraminal stenosis. It had a narrow zone of transition and focal area of cortical breach with surrounding soft tissue stranding. In contrast to the aggressive appearance on MRI, the adjacent uninvolved vertebral structures and left iliac bone demonstrated diffuse sclerosis without focal destructive lesions, favoring a reactive osseous response rather than multifocal tumor involvement. Together with the enhancing paravertebral and iliopsoas soft-tissue abnormalities seen on MRI, these findings were subsequently interpreted as a flare phenomenon rather than true tumor extension. The urinary bladder was distended, having wall thickening with trabeculation and multiple diverticula, suggesting neurogenic bladder (Fig. 2). With the above clinical presentation and CT and MRI findings, aggressive osteoblastoma with an associated flare phenomenon was considered the likely diagnosis; however, the presence of an expansile mass and soft tissue involvement kept osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma as close differentials. Incisional biopsy and histopathology analysis revealed anastomosing woven bone trabeculae lined by osteoblasts within edematous fibrovascular stroma, containing mildly atypical spindled to ovoid cells and scattered osteoclast-type giant cells with an area of extravasated RBCs, confirming a final diagnosis of aggressive osteoblastoma (Fig. 3). Discussion

Osteoblastoma is a benign, bone-forming neoplasm, constituting less than 1% of all benign bone tumors. Although cases have been reported across a broad age spectrum (3-70 years), approximately 90% of diagnoses occur before the age of 30 years [4]. The spine, particularly the posterior element, is a common site of involvement and poses diagnostic challenges due to complex anatomy and proximity to the neural structures. Although osteoblastoma is typically a benign bone tumor [2,5]. A clinically and radiologically aggressive subtype is well described and is characterized by larger size (>4 cm), local expansile behavior, cortical disruption, soft tissue extension, and a higher risk of local recurrence (10%-21%) compared with conventional osteoblastoma. Despite its locally invasive nature, metastatic spread is absent unless malignant transformation to osteosarcoma occurs, which remains extremely rare [1,6,7].

A major diagnostic pitfall in osteoblastoma is the flare phenomenon, a marked reactive inflammatory response involving adjacent marrow and soft tissues. This unusual phenomenon is likely due to the immune response mounted by the patient to the tumor rather than a true tumor infiltrate. This reaction can produce extensive edema-like signal abnormality and enhancement on MRI, making the lesion appear much more infiltrative than the true osseous tumor extent. As a result, MRI may simulate a primary malignant bone tumor and may overestimate the required surgical margins if interpreted in isolation. This pitfall is particularly important in the spine, where imaging findings directly influence surgical planning and biopsy approach [3,8]. CT is best for defining the true bony lesion and is key for pre-op planning, biopsy guidance, and surgery, but it can underestimate the true extent of reactive changes surrounding the lesion. Osteoblastoma may also show increased 18F-FDG uptake because of its high osteogenic activity. Although PET is generally not useful for distinguishing benign from aggressive osteoblastoma, it may help differentiate reactive inflammatory change from true tumor infiltration in selected cases [3].

The principal imaging pitfall in our patient was the marked discrepancy between the MRI and CT appearance. MRI demonstrated a large, ill-defined, avidly enhancing lumbosacral lesion with extensive surrounding marrow and soft tissue abnormality, raising concern for malignancy such as osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma. CT, however, more clearly defined the primary osseous lesion margins, internal mineralization, and the relatively narrow zone of transition. In addition, the diffuse sclerosis of adjacent lumbosacral vertebrae (L4, L5, and sacral vertebral body and posterior element) and left iliac bone with no focal lesion on the CT supports a reactive change due to a flare phenomenon rather than a malignant infiltration. The MRI, specifically postcontrast and T2/STIR sequences, has exaggerated the inflammatory response, mimicking a malignant infiltration. This CT-MRI mismatch supported osteoblastoma with a flare phenomenon. These findings are consistent with the flare phenomenon described in the literature for osteoblastoma [2,3,9]. Batra et al. [2] described a locally aggressive spinal osteoblastoma exhibiting these phenomena in a young patient. However, the flare phenomenon in our case was more pronounced, involving the left iliopsoas muscle, iliac bone, and anterior longitudinal ligament.

Differentiating aggressive osteoblastoma from malignant bone tumors like osteosarcoma is vital but can be difficult, as both can appear locally aggressive. Imaging findings in a large case series of primary vertebral osteosarcoma by Ilaslan et al. [10] closely resemble the findings in our case. Therefore, imaging alone is insufficient to reliably differentiate the two entities. Finally, the gold standard for definitive diagnosis is pathologic examination. Histopathology in our case demonstrated anastomosing woven bone trabeculae lined by osteoblasts within edematous fibrovascular stroma, containing mildly atypical spindle-to-ovoid cells and scattered osteoclast-type giant cells. These findings mirror the characteristic features of osteoblastoma described in the literature [11,12]. Notably, the presence of osteoclast-type giant cells, a feature noted in all cases of aggressive osteoblastoma reported by these authors, further supports our diagnosis. The benign histopathology result correlates with the nondestructive sclerosis seen on the CT scan of the lumbosacral vertebrae and left iliac bone. Meanwhile, the extensive muscle and anterior longitudinal ligament enhancement on MRI is suggestive of reactive changes rather than malignancy.

Multiple studies have established that wide surgical resection offers the best overall outcomes for osteoblastoma and is the strongest predictor of preventing local recurrence. However, complete resection is not possible in certain locations, such as the spine, because of anatomic constraints, tumor volume, and an unacceptable risk of morbidity and loss of function. To address the surgical challenge posed by the high vascularity of osteoblastoma, preoperative embolization is commonly employed to facilitate safer and more complete tumor resection [11,13]. Despite the clinical significance of the initial findings, our case is limited by a lack of longitudinal follow-up. Such attrition is a documented challenge in oncology patients, specifically in resource-limited settings, and might be attributed to socio-economic barriers or the pursuit of alternative care.

In summary, this case highlights that aggressive osteoblastomas with an extensive flare phenomenon can closely mimic malignant bone tumors, such as osteosarcoma, leading imaging findings to falsely suggest malignancy. Awareness of this entity, careful correlation of CT and MRI, and integration with histopathologic findings are essential to avoid misdiagnosis and ensure appropriate surgical management. This case also highlights the role of CT as a biopsy planning tool to avoid inappropriate sampling of the reactive flare changes.
