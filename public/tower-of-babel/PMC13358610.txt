The relationship between physical activity and metabolic syndrome risk: differential effects by exercise intensity and decision tree-based prediction model
Authors: Woo Hyeon Son, Min Seong Ha, Yu mi Jang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
[Purpose]Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease and diabetes. However, ecological evidence regarding the differential effects of exercise intensity remains limited. In this study, we examined the associations between three types of exercise (high-intensity, moderate-intensity, and strength training) and five MetS components at the population level and developed a decision tree-based model to identify high-risk groups.[Methods]Age group-level aggregate data (N = 150) from the National Health Screening Statistical Yearbook (2014-2023) were analyzed. Pearson’s and age-adjusted partial correlations were used to assess associations between exercise frequency and the prevalence of metabolic conditions. A CART-based decision tree model was developed and evaluated using five-fold stratified cross-validation.[Results]In simple correlation analyses, high-intensity exercise was negatively correlated with hypertension and hyperglycemia. After adjusting for age, moderate-intensity exercise was positively correlated with abdominal obesity, hypertension, and hyperglycemia, whereas strength training was positively correlated with hypertriglyceridemia and low HDL cholesterol (all p < 0.001). The decision tree model achieved an accuracy of 78.7% using high- and moderate-intensity exercise variables, which improved to 82.2% when all three exercise types were included. Under the MetS criterion (≥ 3 risk factors), accuracy reached 85.6% with a precision of 0.94.[Conclusion]Partial correlations differed in direction from findings at the individual level, indicating a potential ecological fallacy. Nevertheless, the decision tree model demonstrated acceptable predictive performance, suggesting that exercise frequency may be a useful population-level screening indicator.

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a complex condition characterized by the co-occurrence of three or more of the following: abdominal obesity, hypertension, hyperglycemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and low high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol [1]. MetS is a well-established risk factor for cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and stroke, and its global prevalence is steadily increasing [2].

In South Korea, the prevalence of MetS has risen continuously, largely due to westernized dietary patterns and sedentary lifestyles. According to the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES), the prevalence of MetS among Korean adults ranges from approximately 20% to 30% and increases sharply with age [3]. This trend underscores the urgent need to develop effective strategies for the prevention and management of MetS.

Physical activity is widely recognized as a cornerstone of MetS prevention and treatment. Beyond general adult guidelines, clinical and intervention studies in MetS indicate that aerobic training (moderate to vigorous intensity), resistance training, or their combination can improve waist circumference, blood pressure, glycemic control, and lipid profiles, with combined programs often demonstrating broader benefits across multiple MetS components [4,5]. High-intensity interval training (HIIT) has also been reported as an efficient approach to improving cardiometabolic health in adults with MetS, with effects comparable to or, for some outcomes, greater than those of continuous moderate-intensity training in some outcomes [6,7]. Accordingly, current public health recommendations emphasize accumulating 150-300 minutes per week of moderate-intensity or 75-150 minutes per week of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity, along with muscle-strengthening activities on ≥ 2 days per week [8].

Despite strong evidence at the individual level, population-based surveillance research that distinguishes exercise by intensity and type remains limited. Most studies rely on individual clinical cohorts, whereas national screening statistics offer a complementary, policy-relevant perspective by linking population-level behavioral patterns to disease burden over time. An ecological approach is useful for (i) monitoring macro-level covariation between health behaviors and disease prevalence, (ii) generating hypotheses for individual-level studies, and (iii) informing public health targeting when only aggregate data are available [9,10]. However, ecological associations cannot be interpreted causally at the individual level and are susceptible to aggregation bias and ecological fallacy; therefore, explicit methodological caution is required [9,10].

In addition, translating association patterns into actionable screening rules is increasingly emphasized in public health analytics. Decision tree models are well suited for this purpose because they can capture nonlinear relationships and threshold effects while producing transparent if-then rules that are easy to communicate and implement in practice. Compared with purely linear models, an interpretable tree can support risk stratification using routinely collected variables and facilitate model-informed policy discussions, provided that overfitting is controlled and out-of-sample performance is reported [11].

Within this framework, the study objectives were defined as follows: (1) to quantify age-adjusted ecological associations between three exercise types and five MetS components in order to describe population-level patterns; (2) to translate these patterns into an interpretable classification model that identifies high-risk MetS groups using exercise frequency indicators; and (3) to compare variable sets to determine whether more parsimonious combinations achieve comparable predictive performance, thereby informing practical population-level screening scenarios.

METHODS

Study Design and Data

This ecological study used data from the National Health Screening Statistical Yearbook, published by the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) of South Korea, covering the period from 2014 to 2023. The yearbook provides aggregate statistics on screening results and health behavior questionnaire responses, stratified by age and sex, for adults who undergo national health screening each year.

The unit of analysis consisted of 150 group-level observations, defined by combinations of 15 age groups and 10 years. The 15 age groups were ≤ 19, 20-24, 25-29, 30-34, 35-39, 40-44, 45-49, 50-54, 55-59, 60-64, 65-69, 70-74, 75-79, 80-84, and ≥ 85 years. Sex-combined aggregate data were used for all analyses. The annual number of screenings ranged from approximately 5 million to 17.5 million, representing a cumulative total of approximately 143.7 million screening records over the study period.

This study was exempt from review by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Korea Maritime and Ocean University (KMOU-IRB 2026-01).

Variable Definitions

Dependent variables: metabolic syndrome components

The five MetS components used as dependent variables were defined according to the harmonized criteria of the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) and the American Heart Association (AHA) [1]. Abdominal obesity was defined as a waist circumference ≥ 90 cm for men or ≥ 85 cm for women. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 85 mmHg. Hyperglycemia was defined as fasting blood glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL. Hypertriglyceridemia was defined as triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL, and low HDL cholesterol was defined as HDL cholesterol < 40 mg/dL for men or < 50 mg/dL for women. Each component was measured as the proportion (%) of individuals meeting the respective criterion within each age group-year combination.

Independent variables: physical activity

Physical activity variables were derived from health screening questionnaire responses. High-intensity exercise was defined as vigorous activities such as running, aerobics, and fast swimming. Moderate-intensity exercise included activities such as brisk walking, light swimming, and doubles tennis. Strength training referred to muscle-strengthening activities such as weight training and push-ups.

The mean weekly exercise frequency (days/week) for each type was calculated as a weighted average based on the distribution of respondents across exercise frequencies (0-7 days/week). Specifically, the number of respondents at each frequency level was multiplied by the corresponding number of days, summed, and then divided by the total number of respondents within each age group-year combination. The strength training item was introduced into the screening questionnaire in 2018; therefore, analyses involving strength training were restricted to data from 2018-2023 (N = 90).

Analytical Methods

Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis

Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, and range) were computed for all variables. Because the unit of analysis was age group × year aggregate data, confounding by age was a concern. Age showed extremely strong positive correlations with metabolic disease prevalence (r = 0.87-0.99) and was also correlated with exercise frequency, potentially distorting bivariate relationships. Therefore, both simple Pearson correlation analysis and partial correlation analysis controlling for age were performed, with the latter used as the primary analysis. Age was operationalized as an ordinal categorical variable coded from 1 to 15 corresponding to the 15 age groups (≤ 19 to ≥ 85 years). Partial correlation coefficients represented the net association between exercise frequency and metabolic disease prevalence after linearly adjusting for age. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. As a robustness check, additional analyses excluding the extreme age groups (≤ 19 and ≥ 85 years) were conducted, yielding consistent directional patterns.

Decision tree analysis

Decision tree analysis was conducted to develop a prediction model for high-risk MetS groups based on exercise frequency data. For classification purposes, each age group-year observation was evaluated to determine whether the prevalence of each of the five MetS components exceeded its overall median. An observation was classified as having a ‘risk factor present’ for each component that exceeded its median, and two binary classification variables were created based on the number of risk factors: ≥ 2 (high-risk group) and ≥ 3 (MetS criterion). The median threshold was adopted to create a balanced binary classification framework in the absence of clinically defined group-level cutoffs, thereby ensuring a comparable class distribution for model training and cross-validation.

The Classification and Regression Trees (CART) algorithm was used. To prevent overfitting, the maximum depth was set to four and the minimum number of samples per leaf node was set to five. Model generalization performance was evaluated using five-fold stratified cross-validation. Model performance was reported using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score.

Seven variable combination models (single-variable, two-variable, and three-variable) were systematically compared based on cross-validated performance. All analyses were performed using Python 3.12 (scikit-learn 1.6, SciPy 1.14) and SPSS 28.0.

RESULTS

Descriptive Statistics

The analysis included 150 observations comprising 15 age groups across 10 years (2014-2023). Strength training data were available from 2018 onward, yielding 90 observations (15 age groups × 6 years) for analyses involving this variable.

As shown in Table 1, the mean weekly exercise frequencies were 1.23 days for high-intensity exercise, 1.94 days for moderate-intensity exercise, and 1.28 days for strength training. Among metabolic disease prevalence rates, hypertension was the highest (M = 48.00%), followed by hyperglycemia (39.19%), abdominal obesity (25.42%), hypertriglyceridemia (23.60%), and low HDL cholesterol (20.71%).

Correlations between exercise frequency and metabolic disease prevalence

Pearson correlation analysis

As presented in Table 2, simple correlation analysis showed that high-intensity exercise was significantly negatively correlated with hypertension (r = −0.23, p < 0.01) and hyperglycemia (r = −0.19, p < 0.05). Moderate-intensity exercise was significantly and positively correlated with abdominal obesity (r = 0.29, p < 0.001), hyperglycemia (r = 0.22, p < 0.01), and hypertension (r = 0.16, p < 0.05). Strength training was positively correlated with hypertriglyceridemia (r = 0.28, p < 0.01).

However, because the unit of analysis was group-level data, confounding factors such as age must be considered. Age showed very strong positive correlations with all five metabolic disease prevalence rates (r = 0.72-0.99, all p < 0.001) and a significant negative correlation with high-intensity exercise frequency (r = −0.26, p < 0.01). Older age groups exhibited higher disease prevalence and lower exercise frequency, indicating that age confounded the bivariate relationship. Accordingly, partial correlation analysis controlling for age was conducted as the primary analysis.

Partial correlation analysis controlling for age

As shown in Table 3, partial correlation analysis controlling for age produced markedly different results compared with the simple correlations. Moderate-intensity exercise showed significant positive partial correlations with abdominal obesity (r = 0.40), hypertension (r = 0.38), and hyperglycemia (r = 0.40; all p < 0.001). High-intensity exercise showed significant positive partial correlations with hypertriglyceridemia (r = 0.35, p < 0.001) and low HDL cholesterol (r = 0.22, p < 0.01), as well as weaker positive correlations with abdominal obesity (r = 0.17, p < 0.05) and hyperglycemia (r = 0.18, p < 0.05). Analyses including strength training were based on data from 2018 to 2023 (N = 90).

Strength training exhibited strong positive partial correlations with hypertriglyceridemia (r = 0.63, p < 0.001) and low HDL cholesterol (r = 0.63, p < 0.001). This indicates that, within the same age group, populations with higher strength training frequency tended to have higher prevalence of hypertriglyceridemia and low HDL cholesterol.

The predominantly positive direction of the partial correlation contrasts with findings from prior individual-level research. The interpretation of these findings is addressed in detail in the Discussion section.

Decision tree prediction of metabolic syndrome high-risk groups

For the decision tree analysis, each observation (age group × year) was classified based on the number of metabolic disease prevalence rates exceeding the overall median. High-risk was defined as having ≥ 2 risk factors, and MetS was defined as having ≥ 3 risk factors. Of the 150 observations, 84 (56.0%) were classified as high-risk (≥ 2) and 70 (46.7%) met the MetS criterion (≥ 3).

High-risk criterion: ≥ 2 risk factors

As shown in Table 4, the decision tree model using high- and moderate-intensity exercise as predictors achieved a training accuracy of 90.0% and a five-fold cross-validated accuracy of 78.7%. The cross-validated recall for the high-risk group was 0.87, indicating that 87% of high-risk observations were correctly identified. As illustrated in Figure 1, the primary split criterion was high-intensity exercise frequency at 1.00 days/week, with observations below this threshold classified as high-risk. Feature importance values were 0.718 for high-intensity exercise and 0.282 for moderate-intensity exercise.

As presented in Table 5 and Figure 2, incorporating strength training as a third predictor improved the cross-validated accuracy to 82.2%, with a high-risk group precision of 0.87, resulting in a more balanced predictive performance.

Metabolic syndrome criterion: ≥ 3 risk factors

Table 6 and Figure 3 present the results of the model applying the MetS criterion (≥ 3 risk factors). The cross-validated accuracy was 85.6%. The precision of MetS prediction was 0.94, indicating that 94% of the observations classified as MetS by the model were correctly identified.

Comparison of predictive performance by exercise variable combination

As shown in Table 7, among the single-variable models, moderate-intensity exercise yielded the highest predictive accuracy (80.7%). Among the two-variable combinations, high-intensity + strength training achieved 83.3%, while high-intensity + moderate-intensity exercise achieved 78.7%. Under the MetS criterion (≥ 3 risk factors), the high-intensity + moderate-intensity combination yielded the highest cross-validated accuracy at 86.0%, and the moderate-intensity + strength training combination showed 87.8% accuracy with a precision of 0.97. Overall, combining two or more exercise variables improved predictive performance, with the most balanced performance achieved when aerobic exercise (high- or moderate-intensity) and strength training were included together.

DISCUSSION

This study examined population-level associations between physical activity patterns and MetS risk using age group-level aggregate data, and evaluated the predictive utility of a CART decision tree model. MetS is defined as a cluster of abdominal obesity, hypertension, hyperglycemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and low HDL cholesterol levels [1,2]. The strong age-related gradients in MetS prevalence documented in South Korea and elsewhere provide an important context for the substantial age confounding observed in this ecological dataset [3,9].

In simple Pearson correlations, higher high-intensity exercise frequency was inversely associated with hypertension and hyperglycemia. After controlling for age, most partial correlations became positive. This directional reversal should not be interpreted as evidence that exercise increases metabolic risk. Rather, it is consistent with structural confounding and aggregation-related distortions that can arise when health behaviors and disease burden co-vary across population strata (e.g., age groups), as well as potential post-diagnosis behavioral changes at the group level (reverse causation) [9,10].

When contrasted with individual-level evidence, the ecological partial correlations highlight the limits of causal interpretation. Randomized trials and meta-analyses have generally reported that aerobic, resistance, and combined exercise improve cardiometabolic risk factors relevant to MetS, including blood pressure, glycemic control, and lipid profiles [4,5,12]. Recent evidence syntheses also support HIIT as an efficient strategy for improving cardiometabolic health in adults with MetS [6,7]. Therefore, the present positive ecological associations are more plausibly explained by (i) residual confounding within age strata, (ii) medical advice-driven increases in reported exercise among higher-risk groups, and (iii) measurement limitations inherent to questionnaire-based frequency indicators aggregated at the population level.

The strength training results showed strong positive partial correlations with hypertriglyceridemia and low HDL cholesterol levels. Given the broader clinical literature indicating beneficial or neutral effects of resistance training and combined programs on lipid profiles [5,13], these findings likely reflect the same ecological mechanisms noted above (e.g., reverse causation and aggregation bias) rather than adverse physiological effects.

The decision tree models demonstrated that exercise frequency indicators can classify high-risk metabolic groups with acceptable cross-validated performance. Importantly, decision trees provide explicit threshold rules that are readily interpretable, which is advantageous for public health communication and screening support compared with less transparent algorithms [11]. The higher precision observed under the ≥ 3 risk-factor criterion suggests that the model may be particularly useful when the goal is to minimize false-positive identification of MetS at the population-stratum level.

The finding that combining aerobic and strength-related exercise variables tended to improve predictive performance aligns with contemporary physical activity guidelines recommending both aerobic and muscle-strengthening activities for cardiometabolic health [8,13]. Conceptually, this supports the idea that multi-domain activity profiles may better capture population-level risk heterogeneity than any single exercise indicator.

This study has several strengths: (i) use of nationwide health screening statistics covering approximately 143 million cumulative examinations from 2014 to 2023; (ii) explicit handling of age confounding via partial correlations; and (iii) integration of traditional epidemiologic analysis with interpretable machine learning to generate practical screening rules.

This study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the ecological design precludes individual-level causal inference and is susceptible to ecological fallacy and aggregation bias [9,10]. Second, the cross-sectional structure of age group × year aggregates limits inferences about temporal directionality. Third, important confounders such as diet, body mass index (BMI), smoking, alcohol use, medication use, and socioeconomic status were not available in the aggregated dataset. Fourth, sex differences could not be examined because the analyses used sex-combined aggregates; given known sex-specific patterns in MetS prevalence and physical activity, future work should use sex-stratified and individual-level data. Finally, strength training data were only available from 2018 onward, reducing the sample size for models that included this variable.

In conclusion, although age-adjusted ecological correlations between exercise frequency and MetS components may differ from individual-level effects due to structural confounding and aggregation bias [9,10], the decision tree model demonstrated meaningful predictive utility at the population-stratum level. These findings support the potential use of simple exercise frequency indicators for exploratory surveillance and screening support applications, while emphasizing the need for external validation using individual-level longitudinal data.
