Diurnal vasopressor variation in critically ill adults
Authors: Michael R. Kahn, Chanidapa Klinhom, Amila Adili, Melissa L. Wilson, Dong W. Chang, Luis E. Huerta
Published: 2026

Shock, circulatory failure resulting in tissue hypoperfusion, is often treated with vasopressors titrated to mean arterial pressure (MAP) of 65–70 mmHg [1]. Recent trials suggest nuanced resuscitation targets may be useful in the intensive care unit (ICU), one of which may be consideration of circadian rhythms, the intrinsic 24-h cycles of homeostasis associated with diurnal variation of vital signs including blood pressure [2]. While circadian rhythms in ambulatory and non-ICU hospitalized patients are well described, to our knowledge, no studies have examined the presence of diurnal variation of blood pressure or other markers of circadian rhythm in critically ill adults [3, 4]. In patients requiring vasopressors, vasopressor dose rate (VDR) is a variable affected by clinical management (for example, titration of vasopressors), but also a patient’s underlying physiology, including circadian rhythm, and thus may serve as a representation of intrinsic blood pressure variation [5].

We conducted a retrospective analysis of the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III (MIMIC-III), a well-established and validated dataset of 46,520 admissions to the ICU at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2001 and 2012, to characterize the relationship between time of day and VDR.

Spontaneously awake adults in the ICU requiring continuous vasopressor infusion for over 24 h were included during the initial admission. Intubated/sedated patients and patients whose intubation/sedation status was unclear were excluded as sedating medications may alter circadian rhythm and clinician variation in sedative administration by time of day may confound the relationship between time of day and VDR. There were 468 patients in the analysis with median age 72.7 years; 51.50% were female and 65.60% received care in a medical ICU.

VDR and MAP were assessed during daytime hours of 0700–1900 and nighttime hours of 0000–0600, correlating with neurohormonal/blood pressure peaks and troughs [3]. A sensitivity analysis with nursing-shift-adjusted time intervals, defined as daytime 0700–1859 and nighttime 1900–0659, was also conducted, including the period between 1900 and 0000. Continuous norepinephrine, vasopressin, phenylephrine, dopamine, and epinephrine were converted into norepinephrine equivalents expressed in µg/kg/min. A longitudinal linear mixed-effects model with a subject-specific random intercept was used to assess repeated measures of VDR between time intervals over the ICU stay, accounting for within-patient correlation, with time modeled as a continuous variable. The outcome was log-transformed to better satisfy model assumptions. An interaction between day/night and time was included to evaluate changes over time. Least-squares means were estimated with Tukey adjustment for multiple comparisons. Model estimates were exponentiated and presented as geometric means.

Average VDR differed significantly between daytime and nighttime across the ICU stay (median difference = −0.012 µg/kg/min; 95% Confidence Interval [CI] = −0.014, −0.009; p-value = < 0.001; Table 1). Sensitivity analysis with nursing-shift-adjusted time intervals also showed statistically significant decreases in VDR during the daytime period (median difference = −0.010 µg/kg/min; CI = −0.014, −0.007; p-value = < 0.001; Table 1). Longitudinal analysis of VDR over the ICU admission period showed a significantly lower VDR during daytime versus nighttime on ICU days two through five, and days seven and eight (Fig. 1). Similar longitudinal analysis of MAP between daytime versus nighttime showed a p-value of 0.058 with CI −0.07, 4.42 mmHg. These findings suggest that VDR is higher at night in critically ill adults. Unlike MAP, VDR can be affected not only by patient physiology but also by differing blood pressure targets, which may vary by institution, provider, or time of day. In this database, without a clear difference between patients’ daytime and nighttime MAP, VDR may be a surrogate for patients’ unsupported blood pressure. Written alternatively, “diurnal variation” in vasopressor-dependent patients can be represented by a change in VDR over the course of the day-night cycle, suggesting the presence of diurnal variation of blood pressure in critically ill adults. Given the observational nature of this analysis, these findings can certainly be affected by clinician/institutional practice, limiting clinical application.

Our analysis does have significant limitations. The findings, while statistically significant, are of unclear clinical significance given the small absolute variation in VDR. Our analysis was not designed to examine clinical outcomes; as such, we aimed to capture the hemodynamics of all critically ill adults and did not consider other confounders such as age, sex, outcome, and disease severity, acknowledging that disease severity may substantially affect VDR, circadian rhythm, and ICU length of stay. To the extent possible given the retrospective observational nature of the study, we did evaluate additional important confounders in our design: we attempted to address the effects of sedation and nursing shifts by excluding intubated/sedated patients and performing a sensitivity analysis matching our definitions of daytime/nighttime to nursing shift changes, respectively.

Our findings, while not clinically applicable, do generate hypotheses regarding optimal MAP targets in the ICU, considering that “non-dippers,” people whose blood pressure does not physiologically decrease at night, have increased rates of cardiovascular disease [3]. For example, could promotion of “non-dipper” physiology, maintaining the same MAP over a 24-h period, cause harm or lengthen ICU stay by masking restoration of homeostasis? Additionally, could decreasing vasopressor exposure decrease harms or perhaps confer benefit?

We pose these unanswered questions to be tested in future prospective studies examining the clinical significance of lower nocturnal MAP goals. Alternate nocturnal vasopressor strategies may have the benefit of limiting vasopressor administration, central venous catheter use, and resultant complications such as cardiac events, procedural complications, and organ ischemia from vasoconstriction. Continued study of nocturnal MAP goals and diurnal variation in critically ill patients is an important frontier in assessing resuscitation in the ICU.
