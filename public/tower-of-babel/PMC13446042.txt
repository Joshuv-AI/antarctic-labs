Measuring the efficiency of a solids-based wastewater extraction using BCoV and validation of a BCoV and PMMoV RT-qPCR duplex assay
Authors: Codey E Dueck, Nataliya Santiago, Nikho A Hizon, Ravinder Lidder, Dave Spreitzer, Umar P Mohammed, MacKenzie Dyck, Chand S Mangat, Edgard M Mejia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBovine coronavirus (BCoV) is commonly used to evaluate the efficiency of nucleic acid extraction protocols used in wastewater-based surveillance (WBS). Here we outline a method for measuring the efficiency of a solids-based extraction protocol using RT-qPCR detections of BCoV spiked into wastewater samples. Validation steps included: determination of a suitable quantification method for BCoV starting material, measures of endogenous BCoV in wastewater, assessment of the effects of BCoV incubation times on wastewater extraction efficiencies, and implementation of a BCoV and Pepper Mild Mottle Virus (PMMoV) RT-qPCR duplex assay. Results show that an accurate baseline measure of BCoV stock concentrations is best achieved using heat lysis extraction compared to an automated method. We also show that endogenous BCoV can be present in wastewater, albeit at low viral loads. Additionally, a BCoV incubation time of 16 h in wastewater resulted in the highest percent recovery. Lastly, the use of the BCoV and PMMoV RT-qPCR duplex assay demonstrates comparable performance to their singleplex counterparts.• A method for measuring the extraction efficiency of a solids-based wastewater extraction process using BCoV.• Insights into the in situ prevalence of BCoV in wastewater samples.• Assessment of a multiplex assay for simultaneous detection of BCoV and PMMoV.Keywords: Nucleic acid extraction, Wastewater-based surveillance, Surrogate recovery control, Quality control, Endogenous control

Background

Wastewater-based surveillance (WBS) allows for the monitoring of a myriad of biological and chemical targets at a community level. However, data interpretability can be hindered by technical constraints associated with a lack of standardized methods [1]. The inclusion of a process control in wastewater extractions is highly recommended to help capture variabilities from any given sample and individual processing runs [2,3]. Here, we provide a method describing the use of bovine coronavirus (BCoV) for measuring the wastewater extraction efficiency of a solids-based protocol isolating viral nucleic acids. Previous studies have shown the use of different surrogate viruses as process controls to measure extraction efficiency, including: BCoV [4], bovine respiratory syncytial virus (BRSV) [5], and murine hepatitis virus (MHV) [6]. Limitations of viral process controls include morphological differences between surrogate viruses, type of wastewater extraction method, and the fraction of the wastewater used for extraction (e.g., sludge, liquid, or solid). The Public Health Agency of Canada’s National Microbiology Laboratory makes use of a solids-based extraction process for its WBS program [7,8] as this method has afforded greater sensitivity compared to others [6,[9], [10], [11]]. We show the incorporation of BCoV into this extraction process and describe key considerations for its successful implementation.

BCoV is a single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus with a genome size of approximately 31 kbp. It was first identified as the causative agent of severe diarrhea in neonatal calves [12]. Like SARS-CoV-2, BCoV belongs to the coronaviridae family of enveloped RNA viruses, and has a similarly-sized genome. Given the morphological similarities to SARS-CoV-2 [13,14], many WBS studies have identified BCoV as an ideal candidate for evaluating the efficiency of wastewater extraction methods [4,15,16]. In addition, BCoV is routinely labelled as an “exogenous” control not present in municipal wastewater [17], allowing for controlled and measurable spike-ins. As such, BCoV is one of the most widely used process controls in WBS [16,18], which is why it was selected for this method.

Pepper mild mottle virus (PMMoV) is highly abundant in wastewater samples [19] and is commonly used in WBS as a human fecal indicator as well as to indirectly measure PCR inhibition [20,21]. Similar to BCoV, PMMoV is a single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus, but with a smaller genome of approximately 6.3 kbp and is non-enveloped [22,23]. Together, BCoV and PMMoV are important components of a WBS’ QA/QC program, with the former required for efficiency measures and the latter used as a fecal indicator to ensure samples are representative of the site’s population. Both BCoV and PMMoV are regularly measured using PCR, with many laboratories incorporating the use of multiplex reactions to maximize resources. The use of BCoV and PMMoV in a multiplex RT-qPCR reaction is beneficial given that both can be highly abundant at similar magnitudes and therefore less likely to interfere with the other’s signal. Previous WBS work has shown the use of process controls in multiplex reactions [[24], [25], [26]], however, the majority employs the use of dPCR [18,27]. Although dPCR is reported to increase sensitivity [28,29] and minimize the effects of inhibition [30], its use can be accompanied by higher operating costs, limited reagent flexibility, and lower throughput compared to qPCR. Therefore, the BCoV and PMMoV duplex RT-qPCR method outlined here can provide a viable alternative along with validation information.

Safety considerations and precautions.

1.We suggest that biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) controls be used when handling wastewater samples as they may contain potentially hazardous pathogens and chemicals. Personal protective equipment (PPE) should be used to meet BSL-2 requirements, including working in a biosafety cabinet (BSC), wearing gloves, lab coat, gown, and eye/face splash protection.

Collection of primary influent wastewater

1.Collect 500 mL of 24-hour composite primary influent wastewater. This volume provides a more stable and representative view of the site being sampled compared to smaller volumes which may introduce variability. In addition, 500 mL of wastewater is a large enough volume to allow for multiple tests for various targets being monitored, while also small enough to facilitate shipping from wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) to WBS laboratories. Alternatively, grab samples can be used, however, they may not be entirely representative of variations in wastewater composition over the course of a day.

Data analysis and visualisation

All analyses were performed using R Statistical Software v4.5.1 with rstatix v0.7.2 [37] and dunn.test v1.3.6 [38] packages. The graphical abstract was prepared in Biorender (https://BioRender.com/twc7e3x).

Extraction, dilution and quantification of BOVILIS® CORONAVIRUS

To ensure an accurate baseline measure of the nucleic acid extracted from the reconstituted BOVILIS® CORONAVIRUS (from here on out referred to as the BCoV vaccine), a comparison of heat lysis [31] and an automated extraction method (using the Roche MP96) was conducted as described in the methods section. For each extraction method, an additional comparison of neat versus a 1:2500 dilution of the BCoV vaccine was performed to assess inhibition. Lastly, incremental volumes of 2 and 20 µL for each dilution condition were also tested for both extraction methods with an additional volume of 200 µL for the MP96 extraction.

Our results show that BCoV vaccine extracted via heat lysis and diluted 1:2500 yields a significantly higher concentration compared to all other conditions (p < 0.001, Tukey post-hoc test; Fig. 2). When using a 2 µL volume, the heat-lysed and diluted vaccine yields concentrations that are 62% higher than its neat counterpart (Cdiluted = 4.96×106 cp/µL, Cneat = 3.07×106 cp/µL; p < 0.001, Tukey post-hoc test), and 411% higher than any MP96 extraction (Average CMP96 = 9.71×105 cp/µL; p < 0.001, Tukey post-hoc test). Interestingly, when 20 µL are used, a 324% decrease is observed in the neat heat-lysed BCoV vaccine compared to 2 µL (C20µL, neat = 9.46×105 cp/µL, C2µL, neat = 4.96×106 cp/µL; p < 0.001, Tukey post-hoc test). This drop in concentration is likely due to reagents present in the BCoV vaccine formulation that may act as PCR inhibitors which elicit a stronger effect at higher concentrations [39]. Due to the volume limitations of a PCR reaction, the use of 200 µL of BCoV vaccine was not attempted for the heat lysis extractions. However, no increase in yield was observed when 200 µL of BCoV vaccine was extracted using the MP96. We chose the highest quantifying extraction and quantification method (heat lysis and 1:2500 dilution, 3.01×106 cp/µL) for the baseline quantification of the BCoV vaccine as it should be the closest to the true concentration of the vaccine.

BCoV background in wastewater

The use of BCoV as an extraction efficiency control is a widely adopted practice in WBS, yet studies demonstrating its wastewater exogeny are limited. We assessed the in situ levels of BCoV in wastewater samples from seven different Canadian wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) (Table 11). Additionally, wastewater samples were also pooled to further assess if this resulted in the dilution and cross reaction of the potentially endogenous BCoV signal.

Results show that out of a total of ten samples (seven unique WWTPs plus three pooled samples), five were positive for in situ BCoV (Table 11). Of the five positive samples, three came from the unique WWTPs, while two came from the pooled samples. These results show in situ BCoV signals in wastewater samples, regardless of sample pooling. However, Decaro et al. [33] reported that their RT-qPCR assay was also able to detect BCoV-like viruses, including canine respiratory coronavirus and bubaline coronavirus. Even though humans are the predominant contributors to wastewater, studies have shown that other organisms including: dogs, birds, cows, and pigs, also contribute to a lesser extent [40,41]. To further investigate the origin/source of in situ detections, the BCoV primers underwent a BLAST query and were assessed using Geneious Prime 2025.1.3 software. Although the resulting output revealed several candidate organisms (Table S1) that theoretically could cause the cross-reactivity, in practice this seems unlikely given that many of them are not native to the collection site. Additionally, many of these potential genetic targets were not a 100% match to primer and probe sequences (Table S1; Figure S1), minimizing the chances of cross-reactivity in wastewater due to reduced sensitivity. In addition, all the BCoV signals obtained from this experiment fell below the reported limit of detection of 20 copies per reaction [33] and would likely have a negligible effect on extraction efficiency measures. With consideration of these observations, we believe that BCoV is still an appropriate process control for use in wastewater extraction efficiency measures, though laboratories should test for in situ BCoV before employing its use.

Lastly, we assessed the probability of the BCoV vaccine inducing false-positives in other qPCR assays used to detect commonly-monitored pathogens in wastewater. As our standard protocol uses 5 µL of BCoV vaccine per wastewater spike, we tested concentrations beyond our standard extraction to assess the degree of potential cross-reaction. The BCoV vaccine was directly extracted using the MP96 at 4X (20 µL BCoV and 180 µL 1X TE buffer) and 40X (200 µL BCoV) volumes. No cross-reaction was observed in any of our in-use assays in either condition (Table S2). Therefore, there is negligible risk of introducing false-positive signal with any of our existing assays.

Determination of extraction efficiencies and the effects of BCoV incubation times

Nucleic acids were extracted using a solids-based wastewater extraction method (as described in the method details section titled “Nucleic acid extraction from solids-fraction of wastewater”). To facilitate this process and optimize laboratory resources, 30 mL wastewater aliquots are prepared a day prior to extraction. For this purpose, an assessment of not only the extraction efficiency, but also the impacts of BCoV incubation times in wastewater, was necessary. Also, other studies have shown that viral partitioning is an important consideration when conducting WBS [6,42,43]. Given that this method explores the extraction efficiency of the solids partition of wastewater, an investigation into the effects of incubation on spiked BCoV was pertinent. This began by preparing 700 mL pools of wastewater each from WWTP/site 1, 2, and 3. These pools were further split into six treatment streams that were spiked with 5 µL of BCoV at various points in the extraction process (Fig. 3). The spike was added to four 30 mL wastewater aliquots and divided into incubation groups at 4 °C, which included: 30 min, 16 h, 40 h, and 64 h. Additionally, the BCoV vaccine was added at the bead-beating step and spiked into the bead-beating tube with the wastewater pellet. Finally, the BCoV vaccine was added at the purification step and directly spiked into the wastewater lysate in the MP96 extraction cartridge. BCoV concentration was measured in all of the wastewater extracts and recovery was assessed by comparing to the seeded baseline concentration of the BCoV vaccine.

Illustration of experiment design for measuring the effects of bovine coronavirus (BCoV) incubation times on wastewater extraction efficiencies. Created in BioRender. Mejia, E. (2026)https://BioRender.com/bihtftm.

Within the aggregated sites data, our results show that the 30 min incubation has significantly lower recovery compared to 16 h (Fig. 4A), with mean BCoV percent recoveries of 1.64% and 3.57%, respectively. In addition, across all three sites, the incubation data shows a non-significant decreasing trend in BCoV percent recovery from 16 to 64 h, providing some insight into the stability of the BCoV vaccine in wastewater (Fig. 4B). No consistent significant differences were observed between 30 min and 64 h. It is possible that the 30 min of incubation is not sufficient for the BCoV to bind to the solid material present in wastewater. On the other end, incubations longer than 64 h may result in greater viral degradation compared to shorter times. Studies describing decay kinetics of BCoV in wastewater are limited, and this study did not perform measurements beyond 64 h of incubation. Most protocols are not prescriptive regarding BCoV incubation times [3] resulting in wide variations in existing literature ranging from immediate processing to seven days [11,44,45]. Likewise, BCoV extraction efficiencies reported by other laboratories range from as low as 0.08% to as high as 30% [31,46,47]. Differences in extraction protocols will undoubtedly impact surrogate virus recovery, which is why obtained extraction efficiencies and incubation times should be carefully evaluated by laboratories conducting WBS.

Despite an observed decreasing trend in BCoV percent recovery from 16 to 64 h from both the site-specific and aggregated results, the data did not reach significance. Only the 30 min incubation time showed significantly lower percent recovery compared to other time points (p < 0.001 vs 16 h, p = 0.008 vs 40 h; Dunn post-hoc test with Holm correction). Therefore, incubation times shorter than 16 h are not recommend for BCoV in wastewater.

We highly recommend extraction efficiency trends are monitored longitudinally for all in-scope sites to detect any deviations from the expected recovery. To establish a percent recovery baseline, we recommend that 20 samples be tested for each site to stabilize variability and minimize the calculated standard deviation. Provided that a stable baseline is established when this protocol is on-boarded by laboratories, the focus should not solely lie on the level of the percent recovery, but also on its stability over time. This could be accomplished using statistical QC methods like the Levey-Jennings analysis and monitoring for percent extraction efficiency changes that exceed two standard deviations, for example. This longitudinal measurement of BCoV could also help with any potential BCoV signals detected as a result of its endogenous presence, as discussed in the “BCoV background in wastewater” section. Outlier thresholds will need to be determined empirically by laboratories, but it is recommended that at a minimum, once an outlier is identified based on Levey-Jennings set parameters, that sample should be re-tested to confirm the results. If results are confirmed, the endogenous BCoV viral load should be subtracted from the spiked amount to more accurately assess extraction efficiency.

This validation also included a comparison of the extraction efficiencies between positive controls and wastewater samples (Fig. 5). To this end, 5 µL of BCoV vaccine was spiked into the following test groups: MP96 cartridge containing 1X TE buffer, bead-beating tube containing 1X TE buffer, MP96 cartridge containing wastewater sample, and bead-beating tube containing wastewater sample. The results of these test conditions were compared to the aggregated wastewater samples spiked with BCoV and incubated for 16–64 h at 4 °C. Our results show the recovery of BCoV is significantly higher for the MP96 cartridge containing wastewater sample and bead-beating tube containing wastewater sample positive controls compared to the aggregated wastewater samples (p < 0.001; Dunn post-hoc test with Holm correction). No significant differences were observed between the MP96 cartridge containing 1X TE buffer and bead-beating tube containing 1X TE buffer positive controls and the aggregated wastewater samples. These findings are indicative of the protective effects of solid material on viral particles in wastewater compared to free and suspended virus as well as the demonstrate that signal may be lost due to the incubation step. Studies have previously shown that viral particles and RNA in wastewater, bound to solids, decay slower and are found in greater quantities compared to free and suspended virus [48,49]. However, this effect may only be partially effective for the spiked BCoV, which is a limitation of its use. These findings also highlight key differences that can be found in extraction efficiency measures depending on where in the extraction process BCoV is spiked, and should be considered by laboratories performing WBS. In fact, these spike-in sites can be used as a guide to help identify if any changes to this protocol improve extraction efficiencies.

PMMoV and BCoV qPCR duplex assay validation

The BCoV qPCR test from Decaro et al. [33] was incorporated into a duplex RT-qPCR assay with PMMoV [32] to allow for efficient use of both samples and reagents. To assess assay performance following conversion to multiplex, five-point standard curves were prepared from both BCoV and PMMoV gBlocks as described in Table 8, and generated using both the singleplex and duplex assays and pooled and unpooled controls at equimolar concentrations (Fig. 6). Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was employed to measure statistical slope and intercept differences arising from duplexing (Fig. 6A) and/or template pooling (Fig. 6B). Results show minimal insignificant differences in slopes for all conditions (slope differences ≤ 0.05, p > 0.1), with the exception of BCoV standard curves using unpooled gBlocks (slope difference = 0.05, p = 0.02). Although statistically significant, the 0.05 difference in slope is biologically negligible and passes MIQE guidelines [35]. The observed differences are likely driven by the greater precision of the PCR reaction with unpooled template. Similarly, statistically significant differences were also observed between BCoV and PMMoV y-intercepts when comparing singleplex and duplex reactions (BCoV y-intercept differences = 0.38 and 0.28, p < 0.01; PMMoV y-intercept differences = −0.70 and −0.42, p < 0.01) that would have negligible effects on quantification since there is no loss in sensitivity and the change is consistent. Interestingly, no significant differences were detected when comparing the effects of pooling templates.

The effect of (A) multiplexing and (B) positive control pooling on the linear standard curves of bovine coronavirus (BCoV) and pepper mild mottle virus (PMMoV) qPCR assays. Full standard curve parameters can be found in Table S3. Significant differences are indicated by *p<0.05,**p<0.01,***p<0.001,****p<0.0001,n.s.=notsignificant (ANCOVA).

Next, we sought to validate the performance of this duplex assay using wastewater by comparing the detections of the duplex and singleplex versions in BCoV-spiked samples from six WWTPs. For PMMoV, 20 BCoV-spiked samples from each of the six sites were used to compare the duplex and singleplex versions (Fig. 7A, B). Using a linear correlation analysis, our results show excellent positive correlation (R2 = 0.9116) between duplex and singleplex versions of the test. These results also show a moderate tendency for the singleplex version of the PMMoV assay to quantify higher compared to the duplex (as shown by the slope = 0.6), however, this effect is largely driven by site 2A which typically exhibits higher PMMoV concentrations than the rest of the sites. When conducting the linear correlation analysis using the 1:10 diluted nucleic acid/template, excellent positive correlation is still observed (R2 = 0.8913) and is coupled with an increase in the slope to 0.8, which indicates greater similarity between duplex and singleplex quantifications. The greater PMMoV levels routinely detected in site 2A and the outlier detections from sites 1A and 3B are indicative of wastewater samples with greater fecal loads, which may contain greater accompanying inhibitors [50,51]. For this reason, it is recommended that when using the PMMoV and BCoV duplex assay, the 1:10 PMMoV concentration is longitudinally monitored for QA/QC purposes while still using the neat concentration for inhibition calculation (ΔCt).

For the validation of the BCoV portion of the duplex assay, 13 samples from six WWTPs were used to compare duplex to singleplex performance. This was done as a preliminary assessment of the assay to capture any potential differences between duplex and singleplex versions, hence the use of fewer samples compared to the PMMoV validation. Our results (Fig. 7C, D) show that excellent positive correlation exists between the BCoV duplex and singleplex versions for both neat and the 1:10 dilution (R2 = 0.8353 and 0.9595, respectively). Next, we used 19–21 BCoV-spiked wastewater samples (variability is a result of sample availability) from each of the six WWTPs and measured the BCoV extraction efficiency over a ten week period (Fig. 8). Also, nucleic acid sample extracts were compared at both neat and a 1:10 dilution for each site during this same time period. Lastly, all of these samples were additionally tested for in situ BCoV (Table S4). Results show that extraction efficiencies are largely consistent within each site, with only a few exceptions in which spikes were detected (Fig. 8). Interestingly, out of a total of 120 samples tested, only two were positive for in situ BCoV (Table S4). The two in situ BCoV signals detected are 10,000X and 1,000X (respectively) smaller compared to both the spike-in concentration and lower range recovered concentration (i.e. maximum in situ BCoV = 3 cp/mL WW; expected spiked BCoV = 3 × 105 cp/mL equivalent; minimum recovered BCoV = 3 × 105 cp/mL equivalent). In addition, both of those positive samples originated from site 3B and do not align with any observable spikes in extraction efficiencies. These findings show that although in situ BCoV is rare and lowly abundant, samples should be carefully monitored to ensure that any changes in extraction efficiency are properly interpreted. Our results also show that when comparing the extraction efficiencies of neat and 1:10 diluted template (Fig. 8), five out of six sites show a significantly greater value with the 1:10 dilution (paired t-tests). However, though statistically significant, the difference in median extraction efficiency between neat and 1:10 diluted template for sites 3A and 3B is 0.4% and 0.3%, respectively. Additionally, there was no significant difference between neat and 1:10 extraction efficiencies in site 9. These findings show that site-to-site variations may occur that could impact the performance of the BCoV and PMMoV duplex assay. Therefore, it is highly recommended that BCoV extraction efficiency is longitudinally monitored using both the neat and 1:10 diluted template to better capture existing WWTP diversity.

Overall, our results show that combining BCoV and PMMoV RT-qPCR assays into a duplex reaction does not meaningfully impact the performance of the tests and allows for the efficient measure of both targets.

Limitations

The method outlined here, though promising and in alignment with previous studies, has limitations that must be considered. First, this method describes the extraction of nucleic acids from wastewater using a solids-based protocol. A wide variety of wastewater extraction methods have been developed and optimized for the monitoring of SARS-CoV-2 [52], and it is possible that the findings reported here may differ if applied to alternative protocols. Also, only BCoV was used in the validation protocol, and it is possible that other surrogates may yield different results. In addition, although efforts were made to account for BCoV incubation times when samples arrived at the laboratory, its on-site inclusion at time of wastewater collection was not examined. However, despite these limitations, the findings of this work provide novel insights into the use of BCoV to measure extraction efficiencies and its use in a WBS QA/QC system.
