Melatonin Prolonged-Release May Improve Insomnia and Depression in Epilepsy Patients
Authors: Dong Won Kwack, Dong Wook Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and PurposeInsomnia is a common sleep disorder that is highly prevalent among patients with epilepsy. Despite the known benefits of melatonin for insomnia and its potential anticonvulsive effects, clinical evidence regarding its efficacy in epilepsy patients with comorbid insomnia remains limited. This pilot study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of prolonged-release (PR) formulation of melatonin in epilepsy patients with insomnia.MethodsWe prospectively enrolled 34 adult patients with epilepsy from March 2024 to February 2025. The insomnia severity index (ISI) and the Korean version of the Neurological Disorders of Depression Inventory for Epilepsy (K-NDDI-E) were assessed before and after 12 weeks of melatonin PR intake. Data were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Changes in seizure status before and after treatment were analyzed using McNemar’s test.ResultsA total of 25 patients (nine males, mean age 53.5±11.6 years) completed the study. Of these, 23 had focal-onset seizures and two had generalized-onset seizures. After 12 weeks of treatment, ISI scores significantly improved from 16.0±4.4 to 12.2±5.2 (p=0.001). Similarly, K-NDDI-E scores showed a significant reduction from 11.3±4.2 to 9.8±3.2 (p=0.041). Seizure status remained unchanged following treatment, with no statistically significant difference observed before and after melatonin PR administration (p=1.000). No patients discontinued the medication due to seizure aggravation.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that melatonin PR effectively improves both sleep quality and depression in epilepsy patients with insomnia. These results suggest that melatonin PR could be a potential therapeutic option for managing comorbid insomnia and depression in patients with epilepsy.

Introduction

Sleep and epilepsy have a reciprocal relationship. Sleep plays a crucial role in influencing seizures, with non-rapid eye movement sleep promoting epileptic activity, while rapid eye movement sleep serves to suppress it.1 Sleep deprivation is one of the most common seizure precipitants 2,3 and increases the likelihood of specific epileptiform discharges in the electroencephalography. On the other hand, seizures and interictal epileptic activity during sleep may fragment sleep, worsening seizures in a vicious circle, and interfere with its restorative and neuroplastic function.1 Moreover, nocturnal seizures, epileptiform discharges and anti-seizure medications (ASMs) can interfere with sleep architecture and sleep quality.4

Melatonin, N-acetyl-5-methoxytryptamine, is a neurohormone synthesized and secreted by the pineal gland.5 It has an important role in the regulation of circadian rhythms, as well as proven neuroprotective, antioxidant, and anticytotoxic effects on the central nervous system.6 Many experimental models with animals showed neuroprotective or anticonvulsive effects of melatonin and clinical studies also suggested anticonvulsive effect of melatonin in pediatric and adult patients with epilepsy.7,8 The anticonvulsive effect of melatonin can be associated with direct biologic effect of melatonin and indirect effect on the sleep architecture of melatonin. Therefore, melatonin and its analogues were suggested to have potential as possible medications mainly due to their neuroprotective and antioxidant properties.9

Insomnia is one of the most common sleep problems in general population and is highly prevalent among patients with epilepsy.10 Despite well-documented effects of melatonin on insomnia and potential anticonvulsive effect, there is only limited information on the effect of melatonin in epilepsy patients with the comorbid insomnia. We performed this pilot study to evaluate the effect and safety of prolonged-release (PR) formulation of melatonin in epilepsy patients with insomnia.

Methods

This was a prospective pilot study conducted at a single center from March 2024 to February 2025. A total of 34 adult epilepsy patients were enrolled. The inclusion criteria were 1) at least 2 years after the initial diagnosis of epilepsy; 2) co-morbid insomnia measured by insomnia severity index (ISI) >10; and 3) obtaining written informed consent. Patients were excluded from the study if 1) they had clear seizure or insomnia precipitants except sleep deprivation such as alcohol abuse and exposure to certain medications that could affect the seizure threshold; 2) poor adherence to ASMs has been suspected as a cause of seizure exacerbation by history taking or measurement of blood levels of ASMs; and 3) change or dose adjustment of ASMs during the treatment with melatonin PR. Although emotional stress is a well-documented seizure precipitant, it is difficult to quantify, so we included cases where emotional stress was the only seizure precipitant. All participants received melatonin PR 2 mg orally, administered 30 minutes before bedtime each night for 12 weeks.

Clinical or demographic characteristics of the patients were collected, including age, gender, types of epilepsy, and number of concomitant ASMs. The severity of insomnia was assessed using the ISI. Depressive symptoms were evaluated using the Korean version of the Neurological Disorders of Depression Inventory for Epilepsy (K-NDDI-E). Both instruments were administered at baseline and at 12 weeks post-treatment initiation.

Pre- and post-treatment score were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test SPSS 25 version (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), given the non-parametric nature of the data. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Changes in seizure status before and after melatonin PR treatment were analyzed using McNemar’s test, which is appropriate for comparing paired categorical data in repeated-measures designs. Seizure status was classified as seizure-free or persistent seizure at baseline and at 12-week follow-up. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed on the per-protocol population of patients who completed the 12-week follow-up.

Results

Of the 34 adult epilepsy patients initially enrolled, nine patients were excluded from the analysis due to incomplete outcome assessments during the follow-up period or poor medication adherence. Twenty-five patients (nine males) aged between 35 years and 77 years (mean 53.5±11.6) were analyzed in the study. Regarding seizure classification, the majority of patients (n=23) had focal onset seizures. Detailed demographic information of the study participants is summarized in Table 1.

After 12 weeks of melatonin PR treatment, ISI scores demonstrated a statistically significant improvement compared to baseline (16.0±4.4 vs. 12.2±5.2; p=0.001), indicating a meaningful reduction in insomnia severity across the study cohort. K-NDDI-E scores also showed a significant reduction following treatment, decreasing from 11.3±4.2 to 9.8±3.2 (p=0.041), suggesting that melatonin PR was associated with improvement in depressive symptoms as well. Spaghetti plots illustrating the changes in individual scores for each measure are presented in Figs. 1, 2. With regard to the safety profile, no significant adverse events were observed, with the exception of one patient who reported dizziness and another who complained of alopecia. No patients discontinued the medication due to seizure aggravation during the 12-week treatment period, indicating an acceptable safety profile with respect to seizure control.

Prior to melatonin PR treatment, 13 of 25 patients (52.0%) were seizure-free and 12 (48.0%) had persistent seizures. After 12 weeks, 14 patients (56.0%) were seizure-free and 11 (44.0%) had persistent seizures. Notably, no patient experienced seizure aggravation during the treatment period, and one patient who had previously had persistent seizures achieved seizure freedom. McNemar’s test showed no statistically significant change in seizure status following treatment (p=1.000), indicating that melatonin PR did not adversely affect seizure control.

Discussion

In this prospective pilot study, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of melatonin PR in adult patients with epilepsy and comorbid insomnia over a 12-week treatment period. Our study demonstrates that melatonin PR significantly improved both sleep quality and depressive symptoms, as evidenced by significant reductions in ISI and K-NDDI-E scores following treatment, without significant adverse reactions.

Sleep and epilepsy share a bidirectional relationship; sleep deprivation exacerbates seizures, while epilepsy can contribute to the development of sleep disorders. Moreover, it is also well recognized that ASMs can affect sleep architecture and sleep quality.11 Sleep/wake disorders, such as insomnia, restless legs syndrome, and sleep disordered breathing, are much more common in adult patients with epilepsy.12 Among these, insomnia has been reported as the most prevalent sleep disorder in this population, and has been associated with poor seizure outcomes and impaired quality of life.12,13 Several medical approaches were tried to alleviate insomnia as well as seizure controls in epilepsy patients.

Melatonin is an endogenous neurohormone produced mainly nocturnally in the pineal gland, responsible for regulating the circadian rhythm.9,14 It is involved in various mechanisms associated with alleviating the comorbidities of epilepsy. It demonstrates neuroprotective effects via the gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)ergic pathway and through upregulation of glutathione peroxidase and glutathione reductase activity, anxiolytic effects via the GABA/benzodiazepine receptor pathway, antidepressant effects through serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine) regulation and kynurenic pathway, and anti-oxidant effects through a reduction in lipid peroxidation (Thiobarbituric acid-reactive substances).9 In addition to the effect of regulating circadian rhythm, attempts are increasing to use melatonin, which has various anticonvulsive effects, as an adjunctive therapy in addition to ASMs.14 Because melatonin PR more closely resembles the human physiologic release profile than immediate-release (IR) melatonin and possesses sustained nighttime-release characteristics (mean t1/2 of 5.10 hours),15 we compared insomnia and depressive symptoms following melatonin treatment in adult patient with epilepsy using melatonin PR rather than the IR form.

The close relationship between insomnia severity and seizure control has been well known. In a previous cross-sectional study of 207 epilepsy patients, patients with more severe insomnia (higher ISI scores) were significantly associated with a lack of seizure freedom and worse quality of life, even after adjusting for multiple covariates.16 Although our study was not designed to evaluate causal relationships, melatonin PR treatment was not associated with seizure aggravation-with seizure remaining stable before and after 12-week treatment (McNemar’s test, p=1.000)-suggests that managing insomnia effectively does not carry inherent risk of worsening seizure control.

The association between insomnia and depression in patients with epilepsy is another well-acknowledged feature. In a previous multicenter cross-sectional study, depressive symptoms are one of the strongest predictors of insomnia in patients with epilepsy, with over 56% of patients with depressive symptoms meeting criteria for clinically significant insomnia compared to only 9.3% of those without.17 Additionally, patients with epilepsy with poorer sleep quality showed higher NDDI-E scores in large European survey studies.11 Though anxiety and depression are more prevalent among patients with epilepsy than healthy people, sleep disturbances may worsen the seizure threshold and impairs quality of life, which may aggravate anxiety and depressive symptoms.13 In our study, both ISI and K-NDDI-E scores improved significantly following melatonin PR treatment, suggesting that melatonin PR may address insomnia and depressive symptoms, consistent with the notion that these two conditions are closely intertwined and may share common neurobiological pathways.

This study had several limitations. First, we only included a small number of patients in a single tertiary referral hospital. Therefore, as all patients were recruited from a single tertiary referral center, the study population may not be representative of the broader epilepsy population seen in primary or secondary care settings. The lack of a multicenter design further limits the generalizability of our findings, as patients’ characteristics, clinical practices, and treatment responses may vary considerably across different institutions and geographic regions. Further multicenter studies enrolling larger and diverse patient populations are warranted to validate these preliminary results. Furthermore, the absence of a placebo control group is a significant methodological limitation of the present study. Given that insomnia and depressive symptoms are both highly susceptible to placebo effects, we cannot exclude the possibility that the observed improvements in ISI and K-NDDI-E scores were at least partially attributable to non-pharmacological factors. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled design would be necessary to definitively establish the pharmacological efficacy of melatonin PR in this population. Finally, 12-week follow-up period may be insufficient to assess long-term efficacy and safety. Future randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes, multicenter studies and longer follow-up periods are warranted to confirm these preliminary findings.

In summary, our study suggests that melatonin PR appears to be an effective and well-tolerated therapeutic option for managing comorbid insomnia and depression in patients with epilepsy, without adversely affecting seizure control. These results support its potential role as an adjunctive treatment strategy in this population.
