The effect of asymmetrical vs. symmetrical high flow nasal cannula on inspiratory effort
Authors: Effrosyni Gerovasileiou, Georgios Mavrovounis, Apostolos–Alkiviadis Menis, Konstantina Karadimou, Demosthenes Makris, Ioannis Pantazopoulos
Published: 2026

Nasal high-flow (NHF) reduces inspiratory effort through washout of upper-airway dead space, generation of low positive airway pressure, and improved ventilatory efficiency [1]. A novel asymmetrical cannula may augment these mechanisms by creating unequal nasal resistance, favoring expiratory flow through the less occluded nostril, and promoting unidirectional flow within the nasal cavities. Clinical data, however, remain limited and somewhat inconsistent across settings [2–4]. We therefore compared asymmetrical and symmetrical NHF interfaces in clinically stable adults evaluated within 24 h after discharge from a respiratory ward, considering that this population would allow physiologic assessment without the confounding influence of ongoing respiratory instability, acute escalation of treatment, or prior adaptation to NHF.

Adults aged ≥ 18 years with clinical stability, SpO₂ ≥95% on room air, and no NHF exposure during the preceding year were eligible. Patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, neuromuscular disease, pregnancy, recent noninvasive ventilation, or contraindications to esophageal pressure monitoring were excluded. Participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to one of two crossover sequences: asymmetrical NHF interface first followed by symmetrical NHF interface, or symmetrical NHF interface first followed by asymmetrical NHF interface, with a 3-minute washout period between the two 15-minute application phases. NHF was delivered at 50 L/min, FiO₂ 0.21, and 37 °C. Esophageal pressure was measured with a balloon catheter and tidal volume with validated bioimpedance monitoring (ExSpiron, Respiratory Motion Inc., Waltham, MA), with values averaged over the final minute of each phase. Dynamic end-expiratory and end-inspiratory transpulmonary pressures were estimated by subtracting Pes from a fixed Paw value of 3.5 cmH₂O; driving transpulmonary pressure (ΔPL) was then calculated as the difference between estimated end-inspiratory and end-expiratory transpulmonary pressures. We focused on pressure-time product (PTPes) of esophageal pressure per minute, inspiratory esophageal pressure swing (ΔPes), dynamic lung compliance, tidal volume, respiratory rate, minute ventilation (MV), and basic hemodynamic variables. This approach was intended to capture the immediate mechanical consequences of interface design while keeping device settings, inspired oxygen, and exposure duration constant across both conditions.

Of 306 screened patients, 118 met eligibility criteria, 98 declined participation, 20 consented, and all completed both phases. Mean age was 59.9 ± 19.0 years, 70% were male, and the admission diagnoses reflected a mixed respiratory ward population, including bronchitis, pneumonia, pleural disease, pulmonary embolism, and asthma exacerbation. Baseline oxygenation was preserved on room air, consistent with recovery at the time of assessment. Key physiologic findings are summarized in Table 1. Compared with the symmetrical interface, the asymmetrical interface was associated with significantly lower PTPes per minute, with an estimated mean difference of 30.66 cmH₂O·s/min (p = 0.006). ΔPes was also lower during the asymmetrical interface, with an estimated mean difference of 1.36 cmH₂O (p = 0.038). In contrast, VT was significantly higher during the asymmetrical interface, with an estimated mean difference of 33.00 mL (p = 0.005). A numerical reduction in ΔPL was also seen although it did not reach statistical significance. Respiratory rate and MV did not differ significantly between interfaces. Thus, participants achieved similar overall ventilation while breathing with less pressure generation and slightly larger tidal breaths. Hemodynamic differences were small but statistically significant, with lower heart rate and mean arterial pressure during asymmetrical NHF, a pattern compatible with modest unloading of respiratory drive and sympathetic activation rather than any clinically important cardiovascular effect.

These observations support the possibility that interface geometry matters even when the NHF device settings are unchanged. The combination of lower PTPes, lower pressure swing, preserved MV, and slightly higher tidal volume is physiologically consistent and suggests more efficient breathing with the asymmetrical cannula. Our findings are compatible with bench and physiologic work suggesting that asymmetrical prongs improve CO₂ washout and pressure behavior by generating unequal resistance and facilitating directional flow across the nasopharynx [5]. Our findings are directionally consistent with the reduction in respiratory effort reported by Slobod et al. in acute hypoxemic respiratory failure [2], although the breathing-pattern response differed, particularly for VT. In Slobod et al., the asymmetrical interface reduced VT and MV while PaCO₂ remained unchanged, suggesting improved ventilatory efficiency through enhanced upper-airway CO₂ clearance. In our clinically stable post-discharge cohort, MV remained unchanged, whereas VT increased modestly, alongside lower PTPes and ΔPes. This may reflect a different physiological adaptation in patients with lower baseline respiratory drive, with slightly larger tidal breaths achieved at lower inspiratory pressure generation rather than reduced total ventilatory output. On the other hand, Boscolo et al. observed similar effects between interfaces after extubation [3]. Differences in population, timing, disease severity, baseline respiratory drive, prior NHF exposure, flow and FiO₂ settings, mouth-breathing conditions, and VT measurement technique may explain these discrepant findings.

Our observations should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously. This was a single-center physiologic comparison with a small and heterogeneous sample, brief exposure periods, no carbon dioxide measurements, and no gastric pressure monitoring. The present data therefore cannot be extrapolated to clinical efficacy, patient comfort, or outcome benefit. ΔPL was estimated using a fixed Paw value rather than directly measured. Therefore, ΔPL should be interpreted cautiously. Even so, the consistency of the signal across PTPes, esophageal pressure swing and tidal volume argues against a chance finding alone. In patients recovering from respiratory illness, subtle reductions in inspiratory effort may still be relevant because they could lessen respiratory muscle load while preserving effective ventilation. Larger studies should determine whether these short-term physiologic advantages persist over time, whether they translate into better tolerance or dyspnea relief, and whether they identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit from asymmetrical NHF, particularly those with persistent increased respiratory effort despite apparent clinical stability.
