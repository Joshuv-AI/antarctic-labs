Effects of incremental mandibular advancement on upper airways morphology and anatomical structures: a preliminary CT scan study on body donors
Authors: Elisa Lodolo, Elia Evangelisti, Tommaso Gorini, Giulio Vara, Laura Forni, Simone Lodi, Stefano Leto, Serena Incerti Parenti, Giulio Alessandri Bonetti, Lucia Manzoli, Alessandra Ruggeri, Stefano Ratti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obstructive Sleep Apnoea (OSA) is a sleep-related chronic disorder affecting over one billion people worldwide. Mild to moderate OSA can be treated with a Mandibular Advancement Device (MAD), but its effects on airway anatomy remain unclear. The present study aims to standardize upper airway and soft tissue CT measurements and to evaluate three-dimensional changes following progressive mandibular protrusion. Eight body donors underwent CT acquisitions at baseline and at 2 mm mandibular advancement steps up to 12 mm using a MAD-like device. Upper Airway Space (AS), Tongue (T) and Floor of the Oral Cavity (CF) measurements were obtained using a standardized protocol. Inter- and intra-observer reliability were assessed using ICC. Superior Airway Space (SPAS) increased significantly starting from 4 mm, while Inferior Airway Space at Gonion (G-IAS) changed from 6 mm. Inferior portions of T and CF increased from 2 mm, whereas differences in the superior CF occurred only at 12 mm. SPAS and G-IAS appear the most responsive airway regions, while early soft tissue changes are detected in their inferior anatomical sections. High intra- and inter-observer reliability has been observed. Although limited by the model, the proposed protocol provides a reproducible and reliable framework for analysing structural effects of mandibular advancement and may support treatment optimization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58060-6.

Introduction

Obstructive Sleep Apnoea (OSA) is a sleep-related chronic disorder characterized by repeated obstructions of the upper airways that cause apnoea (total cessation of airflow) or hypopnea (reduction of airflow by more than 30%)1. These episodes of obstruction undermine regular sleep and trigger a cascade of consequences, including an increased risk of cardiovascular and neurocognitive disorders, excessive daytime sleepiness, and an overall reduced quality of life2. It has been recently estimated that over 1 billion people worldwide suffer from OSA3, but most of them remain untreated and undiagnosed3, resulting in a substantial socio-economic burden. Laboratory-based polysomnography is currently the gold standard for OSA diagnosis4, and it evaluates the severity of the disease through the Apnoea–Hypopnea Index (AHI), which represents the number of obstructions per hour of sleep5. Considering the complex multifactorial aetiology of OSA, a number of therapeutic approaches have been proposed over the years, including behavioural measures, medical devices, and surgery6. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is currently considered the first line treatment for OSA, and while it is characterized by high clinical efficacy, it is often limited by low patient tolerability7. A possible alternative is a mandibular advancement device (MAD), recommended for individuals with mild to moderate OSA or primary snoring, but also for patients who do not respond to CPAP or are unable or unwilling to tolerate it8. MADs can feature several designs and characteristics. The most widely used are custom-made, duobloc and titratable devices, comprising two trays that anchor the maxillary and mandibular dental arches and enable the clinician to precisely adjust the amount of advancement9. MAD’s mechanism of action is the anterior displacement of the mandible and tongue base, increasing the cross-sectional area of the oro-pharynx and improving upper airway patency. MAD effects are mainly anatomical, affecting the passive closure critical pressure of the pharynx in a dose-dependent manner10. However, no consensus exists regarding the optimal amount of mandibular advancement; current clinical practice aims to identify the minimum effective advancement, balancing efficacy and tolerability. In this context, anatomical investigation and standardized research on donors represent an essential tool, long employed in both medical and dental fields11. These approaches provide precise morphometric information, detailed structural insights, and reliable control measurements that can be a valuable help for understanding the anatomical modification associated with the use of MAD12. Past studies have evaluated the morphological changes through a 2D cephalometric analysis, which is an affordable method with low radiation doses13. On the other hand, 2D imaging fails to show latero-lateral airways changes, and authors underline to interpret their results with caution14. More recently, interest in 3D imaging has surged, both in clinical practice and research, showing optimal accuracy and reliability15. In this context, radiology has progressively assumed a complementary role in OSA, not as a substitute for polysomnography, but as an adjunctive tool for anatomical phenotyping, identification of the likely site of collapse, treatment planning, and post-treatment assessment. Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have shown that adults with OSA frequently present recognizable dentofacial and upper-airway features on cephalometric and three-dimensional imaging, including maxillomandibular retrusion, inferior hyoid position, soft palate enlargement, and reduced pharyngeal airway dimensions16. Moreover, CT, CBCT, and MRI allow a more comprehensive evaluation of airway calibre, cross-sectional morphology, and surrounding soft tissues than conventional two-dimensional techniques. Dynamic sleep MRI can further characterize the level and pattern of collapse. However, current evidence indicates that static upper-airway measurements alone are weak predictors of disease severity, remaining strongly influenced by acquisition protocol, patient positioning, and segmentation criteria. This emphasizes the need for standardized radiological methodologies in OSA research and in the assessment of treatment-related anatomical changes17–20. Following this pathway, studies analysing airways modifications with 3D imaging have gradually developed. Donor-based anatomical studies allow the use of CT and other advanced imaging technologies without constraints related to radiation exposure or patient safety. This enables three-dimensional investigations and detailed CT analysis of anatomical structures, including soft tissues, which cannot be adequately assessed using conventional radiographic techniques. A recent study investigated three-dimensional upper airways changes after an incremental maxillomandibular advancement surgery performed on donors. Taking advantage of donor-based CBCT analysis, authors described specific morphological differences every 2 mm of surgical advancement. Building on the aforementioned study, the aim of the present work is to standardize the measurement of the upper airway and surrounding soft tissues (tongue and floor of the oral cavity), and to evaluate detailed three-dimensional morphological changes of the upper airway and related anatomical structures in fresh donors using an intraoral device to simulate MAD-like incremental mandibular advancement.

Results

The results of the study are presented as quantitative comparisons between baseline mandibular position (0 mm) and successive incremental advancement levels. Morphometric changes of the upper airway, tongue, and floor of the oral cavity were assessed across progressive mandibular protrusion steps using computed tomography. Statistical differences between baseline and each advancement condition were evaluated to characterise the pattern and timing of anatomical modifications associated with mandibular advancement. Descriptive statistics are reported in Supplementary Tables 1, 2 and 3.

Upper airway space

Descriptive statistics were first calculated to summarise upper airway morphometric parameters at baseline and across progressive mandibular advancement levels. Measures of central tendency and variability were used to characterise the distribution of each parameter (Supplementary Table 1).

For the airway perimeter, statistically significant differences compared with baseline were observed in the Superior Airway Space (SPAS) starting from 4 mm of mandibular protrusion (median changes: 42.55 mm, IQR: 58.69, p-value: 0.0156, CI 95%: [− 49.05, − 30.15], r: 0.891) (Fig. 1a), whereas in the Inferior Airway Space at Gonion (G-IAS) statistically significant changes were detected from 6 mm (median change: 7.11 mm, p-value: 0.0469, CI 95%: [− 20.660, − 5.080], r: 0.792) (Fig. 1d). Regarding the anteroposterior airway length, statistically significant differences were observed in the SPAS from 8 mm of mandibular protrusion (median change: 10.82 mm, IQR: 19.86, p-value: 0,0078, CI 95%: [− 12,970, − 8,890], r: 0,891)(Fig. 1b), while in the G-IAS significant changes were detected at 6 mm and 10 mm (median change: 0.89 mm and 5.10 mm, IQR: 3.68 and 7.34, p-value: 0.0391, CI 95%: [− 2.814, − 0.830], r: 0.743) (Fig. 1e). In the latero-lateral airway dimension, statistically significant differences compared with baseline were observed in the SPAS starting from 8 mm of mandibular protrusion (median change: 9.99, IQR:12.73 mm, p-value: 0,0078, CI 95%: [− 10,825, − 16,100], r: 0,891) (Fig. 1c), whereas in the G-IAS statistically significant changes were detected from 10 mm (median change: 22.06 mm, IQR: 43.70, p-value: 0,0391, CI 95%: [− 30,555, − 16,845], r: 0,743)(Fig. 1f). Although statistically significant changes were observed in the G-IAS perimeter from 8 mm of mandibular protrusion, a high degree of inter-subject variability was noted, as reflected by the wide interquartile range.

No statistically significant differences compared with baseline were observed in either the Medial Airway Space (MAS) or the Inferior Airway Space at Hyoid (H-IAS) across any mandibular advancement level, for any of the three measured dimensions (perimeter, anteroposterior length, and latero-lateral length). Notably, the MAS was found to be completely closed in all donors at baseline, a finding that was consistently observed across advancement levels and is further documented in Supplementary Table 1.

Morphology

Only samples in which the upper airway was patent at both 0 mm and 12 mm were included in the analysis, to ensure reliable assessment of protrusion-related morphological changes. Visual inspection of the normalized airway shape representations (Fig. 2) reveals distinct protrusion-related morphological changes characterized by alterations in the relative antero–posterior (AP) and latero–lateral (LL) dimensions of the airway lumen. At SPAS, protrusion induces the most pronounced and heterogeneous shape changes, with a wide range of AP/LL ratios at 12 mm, reflecting substantial inter-individual variability in antero–posterior airway elongation. At G-IAS, shape changes are more limited, with modest increases in AP/LL ratio and reduced dispersion among patients, suggesting greater anatomical constraint on airway remodelling at this level. At H-IAS, a more uniform pattern is observed, with most subjects demonstrating a consistent increase in AP predominance, resulting in a relatively homogeneous shift toward elongated airway shapes. Morphological shape analysis was not performed for the MAS region, as the airway was completely closed in all 8 donors at baseline and remained occluded in the majority of subjects throughout the advancement protocol (6/8 at 2 mm and 4 mm, 5/8 at 6 mm, 4/8 at 8 mm and 10 mm, 3/8 at 12 mm), precluding reliable assessment of cross-sectional geometry.

Inter- and intra-operator reliability

Inter-observer reliability analysis demonstrated good to excellent agreement for most skeletal and airway-related measurements, particularly for SPAS and G-IAS. In contrast, lower agreement was observed for tongue-related measurements, likely reflecting the inherent variability in identifying soft tissue landmarks. ICC could not be calculated for MAS due to the high presence of closed space airway at 0 mm. Intra-operator reliability analysis demonstrated good to excellent agreement for all the measurement. Discussion

The present study provides a three-dimensional donor-based evaluation of upper airway and adjacent soft tissue modifications induced by incremental mandibular advancement using a MAD-like titratable intraoral device. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first investigation specifically designed to assess progressive, stepwise mandibular protrusion through repeated CT acquisitions under standardised and controlled conditions. In contrast to earlier research protocols that were based on two-dimensional imaging or were limited to pre- and post-intervention comparisons13,14, the primary contribution of the present study lies in its detailed morphometric analysis of airway changes across multiple advancement levels. This is because two-dimensional techniques inherently fail to capture latero-lateral modifications and volumetric adaptations. Furthermore, the utilisation of CT in a donor-based setting enabled repeated acquisitions without radiation constraints, thereby ensuring consistent head positioning and acquisition parameters at each advancement step. This methodological consistency enhances the internal validity of the morphometric comparisons and strengthens the reproducibility of the protocol. Furthermore, standardised segmentation procedures permitted site-specific assessment of airway regions, thus enabling detection of localised responses that would likely remain undetected in global volumetric analyses alone. Only one previous donor-based CT investigation has examined progressive skeletal advancement in a comparable manner. The study reported that incremental maxillomandibular advancement produced the greatest volumetric airway increase between 6 and 8 mm, suggesting a non-linear dose–response relationship12. Despite the differences in intervention, both studies converge in indicating that airway enlargement does not progress linearly with skeletal displacement but rather demonstrates threshold-dependent behaviour. In the current analysis, SPAS and G-IAS were identified as the anatomical regions exhibiting statistically significant modifications during progressive protrusion, while MAS and H-IAS regions were not as influenced by the MAD-like device.

Notably, the airway perimeter at SPAS increased significantly after 4 mm of advancement, whereas G-IAS demonstrated significant changes at 6 mm. As for airway morphology, mandibular advancement produced an expansion of the airway predominantly in the lateral dimension. These findings suggest a region-specific response of the upper airway complex and are consistent with earlier research that highlighted MAD ability to improve the airway calibre predominantly at the velopharyngeal21 level by increasing the latero-lateral dimension22,23. Moreover, the previously mentioned donor-based study also demonstrated that surgical mandibular advancement produced greater changes in the lateral dimension of the upper airway than in the anteroposterior dimension12,14. Our results are also consistent with two-dimensional cephalometric studies, which reported significant airway sagittal increase at both the tongue base and uvular tip levels following mandibular advancement14. These anatomical modifications support the current understanding that mandibular advancement stabilizes the upper airway not only through anterior displacement of the tongue and associated soft tissues, but also by promoting lateral stabilization of the pharyngeal walls22,24.

With regard to adjacent soft tissues, the tongue is affected by mandibular advancement only in its inferior portion and starting from 2 mm of protrusion. Likewise, floor of oral cavity muscles shows significant differences predominantly in the inferior border, also from 2 mm of advancement. These results indicate that pharynx bordering muscles are specifically influenced by MAD in their inferior anatomical boundary. From a clinical standpoint, these observations are relevant to the titration strategies employed in MAD therapy for OSA. Current evidence indicates that mandibular advancement generally does not produce dose-dependent reductions in the AHI, although with substantial interindividual variability25,26. In the absence of clear quantitative clinical recommendations, treatment titration is frequently based on the clinician’s experience and on the patient’s symptomatic improvement rather than on a standardized evidence-based threshold27. Recent literature suggests that devices are frequently titrated to approximately 50–75% of maximal protrusion; however, some patients require advancements exceeding 75–80% to achieve meaningful AHI improvement28, often at the expense of increased adverse effects such as temporomandibular discomfort, dental sensitivity, and occlusal alterations29. Conversely, other individuals exhibit significant clinical improvement at relatively modest protrusion levels, suggesting the existence of patient-specific anatomical or functional thresholds. Considering not only that the aforementioned side effects can potentially undermine MAD treatment adherence30, but also that MAD is a lifelong therapy requiring continuous titration and adjustment, it becomes crucial for clinicians to achieve the lowest mandibular advancement that is clinically effective. Although a direct correlation between static CT morphometry and dynamic AHI reduction cannot be established in a donor model, the threshold-dependent structural changes observed in this study may offer an anatomical framework that partially explains the heterogeneous clinical response to MAD therapy. Importantly, the present work also introduces a standardized and reproducible CT-based protocol for investigating incremental mandibular advancement. The combination of a titratable intraoral device, predefined advancement increments, consistent acquisition parameters, and structured segmentation criteria establishes a methodological platform that can be replicated and expanded in future studies. Such a framework enables the integration of additional variables within a controlled imaging environment. In this regard, the study contributes not only new morphometric data but also a reproducible laboratory and radiological model for further multi-variable investigation.

Limitations

It is important to consider the limitations of this study: the absence of muscle tone, the lack of airflow and negative inspiratory pressure, and post-mortem tissue alterations further reduce the ability to model in vivo airway behaviour. Additional constraints include limited donor availability as well as lack of diversity, since all the donors are Italian and showed a relatively high mean age. Although this age profile may be representative of the OSA population, given the age-related increase in disease prevalence, aging is also associated with reduced soft tissue elasticity. Consequently, the mechanical response of the upper airway may have differed from that of a younger population, potentially affecting the study results31,32. Nevertheless, research on body donors remains essential for assessing structural features, spatial relationships, and morphometric changes during incremental mandibular advancement, while providing detailed anatomical data and reliable internal controls not obtainable in living subjects. Future integration of advanced simulation platforms such as the SimLife® technology (Simedys), capable of artificial perfusion and ventilation of donated bodies, may help bridge the gap between ex vivo anatomical studies and in vivo airway behaviour, opening new perspectives for both biomedical research and medical training33.

Clinical implications and future research

In conclusion, this donor-based CT investigation demonstrates that incremental mandibular advancement produces region-specific and threshold-dependent morphological changes of the upper airway and adjacent soft tissues. The identification of distinct response patterns at SPAS and G-IAS supports the concept that airway remodelling during mandibular protrusion is spatially heterogeneous rather than uniformly linear. Although clinical extrapolation must be approached with caution, the standardized laboratory and radiological protocol developed in this study establishes a reproducible framework for future research aimed at establishing a link between three-dimensional morphometry and functional and clinical outcomes.

This integration may contribute to a deeper understanding of the structural interactions underlying airway remodelling during mandibular advancement and could provide valuable insights towards the development of more personalised and evidence-based titration methodologies in MAD therapy. Further investigations, incorporating additional measurements of the floor of the oral cavity, in conjunction with the evaluation of other relevant soft tissue components, such as the soft palate, may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the structural interactions underlying airway remodelling during mandibular advancement. In addition, baseline measurements at 0 mm protrusion without the MAD will be incorporated into the analysis, alongside an expanded sample, to enhance the robustness and generalizability of the results.

Methods

A total of eight donated bodies were included in this study. Specimens were selected based on specific anatomical and functional requirements. Inclusion criteria comprised the presence of at least five dental crowns, either natural teeth or implant-supported, to ensure adequate stability of the intraoral device. Only donors exhibiting sufficient mandibular mobility to allow both mouth opening and mandibular protrusion were considered eligible, as these movements were essential for the experimental procedures. Furthermore, bodies had to be sectioned at least up to the level of the fourth cervical vertebra (C4) to guarantee full access to and visualisation of the anatomical regions of interest. Specimens were excluded in cases of complete edentulism, a documented history of maxillofacial surgery, or previous oropharyngeal tumours, as these conditions could compromise anatomical integrity or alter the structures relevant to the study. The study, that received approval from the Bioethics Committee of the University of Bologna (Prot. 0,102,300—10/04/2024), has been conducted on bodies donated to science, that provided their consent for study, training, and scientific research purposes and are part of the National Program for Body Donation to Science34, for which the University of Bologna serves as a reference centre for the preservation and utilisation of donated bodies35,36. The donors were treated with respect in accordance with International Federation of Associations of Anatomists (IFAA) recommendations37.

CT acquisitions of the body donors were performed using the Philips Incisive CT 128 scanner was acquired within the framework of the “HEAL Italia” project (PE6–Spoke 6, CUP J33C22002920006), supported by the European Union’s Next Generation EU initiative as part of the Italian National Recovery and Resilience Plan (PNRR), Mission 4, Component 2, Investment 1.3 (MUR Decree No. 341, 15 March 2022). Installed at the Anatomy Center, the system represents the first CT scanner in Italy expressly dedicated to precision medicine and advanced anatomical investigations. For the present study, tailored acquisition protocols were implemented for both soft-tissue and mineralised (calcified) structures, with the integration of OMAR (Orthopaedic Metal Artifact Reduction) to minimise artefactual noise and optimise image quality. CT acquisitions were performed using the following parameters: matrix 512 × 512, tube voltage 120 kV, slice thickness 0.8 mm with an overlap of 0.4 mm, rotation time 1.50 s, and pitch 0.60. Demographic data, collected to assess the presence of potential risk factors, are reported in Table 2. The sample consisted of 4 males and 4 females with a mean age of 72.6 ± 9.5 years (range: 60–90 years).

To ensure methodological standardization, head positioning was also standardized by placing the specimen within the CT head holder in maximum intercuspal position and with Frankfort Plane perpendicular to the floor. Foam supports were used to maintain head stability without altering anatomical relationships. To simulate MAD use on donors and to allow precise and reproducible titration of mandibular advancement, the AB-slide system (Uniontech s.r.l., Via Rocco Bormioli 5/A, 43,122 Parma, Italy) was employed. This device comprises an upper and a lower impression tray, each individually anchored to the dental arches using Elite HD + Putty Soft impression material (Zhermack SpA – Via Bovazecchino, 100 – 45,021 Badia Polesine (RO), Italy) to guarantee a stable and retentive positioning on the teeth. The two trays are mechanically connected by a sliding system incorporating an adjustment screw and a graduated scale. Rotation of the screw allows the trays to glide relative to each other, producing controlled anterior displacement of the mandible. The presence of the calibrated scale enables accurate, incremental advancement of the mandible in a standardized and reproducible manner, closely mimicking the titration process of a clinical MAD. For each specimen, computed tomography (CT) scans were acquired at baseline position, using the AB-slide gauge, and at progressive mandibular advancements of 2 mm, up to a maximum of 12 mm (P0 → P12). The baseline position consisted of 0 mm of mandibular protrusion with the intraoral device in situ. This choice was motivated by the inherent characteristics of the donor model: in the absence of active muscle tone, a reproducible maximum intercuspation position cannot be reliably achieved, whereas the intraoral device ensures a stable and consistent mandibular position across acquisitions. In the absence of any additional anatomical landmarks, the baseline position was defined through CT imaging with the mandibular condyle observed at the centre of the glenoid fossa, as this represents the most common condylar position at rest38,39. This criterion was adopted to ensure a reproducible and anatomically sound reference position across specimens. The mandibular advancement protocol was performed in 2 mm increments to reproduce the gradual titration strategy commonly adopted in clinical practice for MAD therapy, where progressive adjustments are used to balance therapeutic efficacy and patient comfort while minimizing side effects40. Since the study was conducted on donors and individualized assessment of the protrusive mandibular range was not feasible, fixed 2 mm advancements were selected to approximate the incremental titration approach typically performed in vivo. Moreover, this approach is coherent with a previously conducted study about mandibular advancement on donors12. At every advancement step, a set of standardised measurements, derived from previously published studies was obtained to quantify modifications of the upper airway and surrounding soft tissues using 3D Slicer (version 5.8)41. The datasets generated and analysed during the current study are available in Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19606933). The dataset includes anonymized measurements derived from CT scans of body donors, collected at incremental steps of mandibular protrusion. No personally identifiable information is included. To develop a standardized acquisition protocol, easily identifiable landmarks on CT images or readily measurable reference points were used (Fig. 5). Supplementary Table 3 reports all the measurements together with their corresponding descriptions.

The maximum antero–posterior (AP) and latero–lateral (LL) dimensions were measured to characterize airway shape. To describe morphological changes independently of absolute airway size, airway shape was expressed using the AP/LL ratio, calculated as the ratio between the AP and LL dimensions at each level. Higher AP/LL values indicate a predominance of antero–posterior elongation, whereas lower values reflect a relatively wider latero–lateral configuration. This approach allowed direct comparison of airway shape changes across patients and between protrusion steps, minimizing the influence of inter-individual differences in airway size. The airway was considered closed when the anterior and posterior margins were in contact, resulting in an AP dimension of zero; such cases were excluded from shape analysis. Statistical analyses and descriptive statistics were performed using R software (version 4.5.2)42. Quantitative data are reported as Mean, Standard Deviation (SD), Median, Interquartile Range (IQR) and first/third quartile (Q1-3). Differences between baseline (0 mm) and each mandibular advancement step were analysed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired data. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied. Given the exploratory nature of this study, the primary aim was to identify potential patterns of change across increasing levels of mandibular protrusion rather than to confirm specific hypotheses. In this context, applying a correction such as Bonferroni or false discovery rate would increase the risk of Type II errors (false negatives), potentially obscuring clinically relevant trends. Results should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution, and findings warrant confirmation in larger, confirmatory studies43.

Inter- and intra-operator reliability

In order to evaluate the standardisation of the protocol, both inter- and intra-observer reliability were assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC). Prior to the analysis, all observers underwent a standardised training session to ensure consistent identification of anatomical landmarks and measurement procedures. To ensure the integrity of data acquisition, the three observers collected measurements independently at three separate time points, recording their data in three distinct files. The datasets were merged exclusively at the stage of statistical analysis, thereby preventing any potential influence of one observer’s measurements on those of the others. The inter-observer reliability of the measurements was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC1,2), based on a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement. The measurement precision was then subjected to further evaluation using the standard error of measurement (SEM). The intra-observer reliability of the measurements was assessed by repeating all measurements after a one-week interval by the same observer, who was blinded to the previous results. The temporal span was selected to mitigate the impact of recall bias while ensuring the uniform identification of landmarks. The extent to which the two measurement sessions were consistent with each other was measured using a statistical technique known as the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC1,3), which is a type of statistical model that is specifically designed for situations where there are two or more groups that are being measured.
