Abstract
We investigate the spatial profile of the curvature perturbation generated in multi-waterfall hybrid inflation models, which are known to produce various topological defects. Using the lattice simulation code STOLAS , based on the stochastic formalism of inflation, we analyse six cases by varying the number of waterfall fields n n and the functional form of the inflaton potential (“Quadratic” and “Cubic” cases). Our statistical analysis shows that the PDF and power spectra are broadly consistent with the so-called stochastic- δ ​ 𝒩 \delta\mathcal{N} algorithm. The “Cubic” case also exhibits a characteristic upper bound in the PDF , as discovered in our previous work, that suppresses PBH formation while potentially affecting halo formation. Furthermore, we employ the Euler characteristic as a topological diagnostic tool to identify the structures of the waterfall fields as well as the curvature perturbation. We find that the topological defects, such as domain walls ( n = 1 n=1 ), cosmic strings ( n = 2 n=2 ), and monopoles ( n = 3 n=3 ), are reconnected during inflation into finer structures by the stochastic noise, making their correlation lengths much smaller than the Hubble scale at the critical point of the waterfall phase transition counterintuitively. The Euler characteristic also implies global structures of the curvature perturbation for n = 1 n=1 , though we do not conclude if they are due to the domain wall, because neither the strings ( n = 2 n=2 ) nor monopoles ( n = 3 n=3 ) leave such structures. The global structures of the curvature perturbation will provide a novel probe for the physics of the early universe.

Since the dynamics of the waterfall phase depend sensitively on the field fluctuations at the critical point, the system is intrinsically non-perturbative, often leading to a significant enhancement of curvature perturbations.

Discrete stochastic formalism of inflation

While the original

[

Here,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{ij}^{1/2}(N,\mathbf{x})\simeq\frac{H(N,\mathbf{x})}{2\pi}\delta_{ij}.

These stochastic equations can be implemented by a numerical lattice simulation with a certain appropriate discretisation.
In the temporal direction, the

is discretised in the Euler–Maruyama way as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\phi_{i}(N+\Delta N,\mathbf{x})-\phi_{i}(N,\mathbf{x})=\Delta_{\mathrm{D}}\phi_{i}(N,\mathbf{x})+\frac{H(N,\mathbf{x})}{2\pi}\Delta W_{i}(N,\mathbf{x}),\\

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{D}}\phi_{i}(N,\mathbf{x})=\frac{\pi_{i}(N,\mathbf{x})}{H(N,\mathbf{x})}\Delta N,\quad\Delta_{\mathrm{D}}\pi_{i}(N,\mathbf{x})=-\pqty{3\pi_i(N,\bfx)+\frac{V_i\qty(\bm{\phi}(N,\bfx))}{H(N,\bfx)}}\Delta N,

(2.8)

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\Delta W_i(N,\bfx)}=0,\quad\expectationvalue{\Delta W_i(N,\bfx)\Delta W_j(N',\bfx')}=\delta_{ij}\delta_{NN^{\prime}}\frac{\sin k_{\sigma}(N)\absolutevalue{\bfx-\bfx'}}{k_{\sigma}(N)\absolutevalue{\bfx-\bfx'}}\Delta N.

For stability, we adopt the fourth-order Runge–Kutta method to evaluate the deterministic parts

2.2
Average
\delta\mathcal{N}

According to the
\delta N
formalism
[
59
,
52
,
53
,
54
,
66
,
36
,
37
]
, the conserved curvature perturbation
\zeta
is equivalent to the fluctuation in the e-folding number from an initial flat hypersurface to a final uniform-density hypersurface.
In the stochastic formalism, it can be calculated as the stochastic fluctuation in the first passage time
\mathcal{N}
from the initial field values to the final uniform-density condition, known as the stochastic-
\delta\mathcal{N}
approach (see, e.g., Refs.
[
21
,
22
,
65
]
).
In other words, the curvature perturbation at the spatial point
\mathbf{x}
is given by

\displaystyle\zeta(\mathbf{x})=\delta\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x})-\overline{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x})},

(2.11)

where
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x})
is the first passage time realised at
\mathbf{x}
, and the simulation box average
\overline{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x})}
is subtracted so that
\overline{\zeta(\mathbf{x})}=0
.

The final hypersurface is well approximated by the end of inflation, as often done in the literature.
In the original paper
[
41
]
, the first passage time
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x})
is calculated by numerically solving the
EoM
(
2.7
) from the field values at the end of the lattice simulation to the end of inflation without the noise term to focus on the fluctuations during the lattice simulation, supposing that the noise effects after the lattice simulation are negligible.
However, in the mild-waterfall hybrid inflation, as our interest, stochastic effects remain significant until near the end of inflation.
We therefore continue to include the noise term even after the lattice simulation, but neglect its spatial correlations, since the lattice sites are separated beyond the coarse-graining scale.
Specifically, the independent noise is included until
\eta_{\mathrm{r}}=-1.2
while the final hypersurface is defined by
\eta_{\mathrm{r}}=-2
, where
\eta_{\mathrm{r}}
is the slow-roll parameter defined by Eq. (
3.3
) below.

The first passage time calculated in this way is related to the curvature perturbation coarse-grained over
k_{\sigma}^{-1}
at
\eta_{\mathrm{r}}=-1.2
, which is much smaller than the lattice grid size.
Such small-scale fluctuations unresolved by the lattice cause numerical artefacts in the discrete Fourier transform.
We would rather obtain the curvature perturbation coarse-grained over the lattice scale.
To this end, at each grid point, we repeat the above uncorrelated-noise stage multiple times with different realisations of the noise, and estimate the mean value of
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x})
over these realisations.
Namely, the coarse-grained curvature perturbation
\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbf{x})
is calculated as

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbf{x})=\expectationvalue{\calN\pqty{\bm{\phi}(N_\uf,\bfx),\bm{\pi}(N_\uf,\bfx)}}-\overline{\expectationvalue{\calN\pqty{\bm{\phi}(N_\uf,\bfx),\bm{\pi}(N_\uf,\bfx)}}},

(2.12)

where
\bm{\phi}(N_{\mathrm{f}},\mathbf{x})
and
\bm{\pi}(N_{\mathrm{f}},\mathbf{x})
are the field and momentum values at the end of the lattice simulation
N_{\mathrm{f}}
at the spatial point
\mathbf{x}
, and
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\phi})
is the first passage time from these configuration to the final hypersurface.
In this paper, we evaluate the stochastic average
\expectationvalue{\cdot}
by
20
realisations of the uncorrelated noise at each grid point.
We hereafter drop the subscript ‘
\mathrm{c}
’ for conciseness.

3
Hybrid inflation

In this section, we review the aspects of the mild-waterfall hybrid inflation.
Hybrid inflation, originally proposed by Linde
[
35
]
, is driven by two types of scalar fields: the inflaton and the waterfall fields.
In this paper, we employ a single inflaton
\phi
(
=\phi_{0}
in the previous notation) and multiple waterfall fields
\bm{\psi}=\pqty{\psi_1,\psi_2,\cdots,\psi_n}
(
=(\phi_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n}

n
.
The potential respects
\mathrm{O}(n)
(or
\mathrm{Z}_{2}
for
n=1
) symmetry as given by

\displaystyle V(\phi,\bm{\psi})=\Lambda^{4}\bqty{\pqty{1-\frac{\psi_\ur^2}{M^2}}^2+2\frac{\phi^2\psi_\ur^2}{\phi_\uc^2M^2}+v(\phi)},

(3.1)

where
\psi_{\mathrm{r}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}^{2}}
is the radial direction of waterfall fields, and
\Lambda
,
M
, and
\phi_{\mathrm{c}}
are dimensionful model parameters.
The unstable origin of the waterfall fields,
\bm{\psi}=\bm{0}
, can be stabilised by the coupling to the inflaton
\phi
when
\phi
is larger than the critical point
\phi_{\mathrm{c}}
.
Hence, hybrid inflation consists of two phases.
The inflaton first rolls down its potential
v(\phi)
along the valley
\bm{\psi}\sim\bm{0}
during
\phi>\phi_{\mathrm{c}}
(we suppose
\phi>0
throughout this work without loss of generality).
After reaching the critical point
\phi_{\mathrm{c}}
, the waterfall fields are destabilised and roll down toward the true minimum
\psi_{\mathrm{r}}=M
, ending inflation.
Depending on the parameter choice, the waterfall direction can be sufficiently flat so that inflation continues for several e-folds even after the critical point.
In particular, the model where the waterfall phase lasts for more than a few e-folds but less than
\sim 50
e-folds corresponding to the current horizon scale, is called the mild-waterfall hybrid inflation
[
23
,
38
,
6
,
7
,
39
,
24
,
26
,
14
,
29
,
61
,
62
,
63
,
44
]
.
In this model, the waterfall dynamics, such as its duration and the direction of the final
VEV
(
VEV
),
\bm{\psi}/\absolutevalue{\bm{\psi}}
, are highly sensitive to the field fluctuations around the critical point, indicating its non-perturbative nature.
We hereby series-expand the inflaton potential
v(\phi)
around the critical point for a general analysis as

\displaystyle v(\phi)=\frac{\phi-\phi_{\mathrm{c}}}{\mu_{1}}-\frac{(\phi-\phi_{\mathrm{c}})^{2}}{\mu_{2}^{2}}+\frac{(\phi-\phi_{\mathrm{c}})^{3}}{\mu_{3}^{3}}+\cdots,

(3.2)

with model parameters

,

We define the end surface both for inflation and the
\delta N

\eta_{\mathrm{r}}=-2
with

\displaystyle\eta_{\mathrm{r}}=M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}\frac{V_{\psi_{\mathrm{r}}\psi_{\mathrm{r}}}}{V},

(3.3)

for simplicity as done in the literature
[
30
,
14
,
44
]
.
Throughout this paper, we neglect the stochastic noise for
\phi
, which is almost irrelevant to the waterfall dynamics, compared to the noise for

Some of the above parameters are constrained by the

Suppose that the corresponding perturbations crossed the horizon during the valley phase.
The waterfall fields are irrelevant to them, and the amplitude

\displaystyle

\displaystyle A_{\mathrm{s}}=\frac{1}{24\pi^{2}M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{4}}\frac{V}{\epsilon_{\phi}}\simeq\frac{\Lambda^{4}\mu_{1}^{2}}{12\pi^{2}M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{6}},

(3.4)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\phi}=\frac{M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}}{2}\pqty{\frac{v'(\phi)}{v(\phi)}}^{2},\quad\eta_{\phi}=M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}\frac{v^{\prime\prime}(\phi)}{v(\phi)}.

(3.5)

While we only kept the leading term in the first line in Eq. (

The

3.1
Fitting formula of power spectrum

The standard linear perturbation theory for the curvature perturbation breaks down around the critical point because of its non-perturbative nature that the field fluctuations determine the background dynamics during the waterfall phase.
The semi-perturbative analysis developed in Refs.

around the critical point

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\zeta}(k)=\mathcal{P}_{\zeta}^{(\rm peak)}\exp[-2\frac{\chi_{2}}{\Delta N_{1}^{2}}\quantity((N_{\rm water} - \calN_{k})^2 + \frac{2 \Mpl^2}{3\mu_{2}^2} (N_{\rm water} - \calN_{k})^3)],

(3.6)

with the fitting parameters

and
\mathcal{P}_{\zeta}^{(\text{peak})}

Here,
\mathcal{N}_{k}
is the backward e-folds at

from the end of inflation,
\chi_{2}
is defined by

\displaystyle\chi_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\ln\quantity[\frac{12 \sqrt{2\pi^3} \Mpl^6 \Pi}{n\Lambda^{4} \mu_{1}^{2}}],

(3.7)

with the characteristic parameter combination

\displaystyle\Pi=\frac{M\sqrt{\phi_{\mathrm{c}}\mu_{1}}}{M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}},

(3.8)

and

is determined from the solution of the following equation:

\displaystyle\chi_{2}=\frac{4}{\Pi^{2}}\quantity(\Delta N_{1}^2 + \frac{2\Mpl^2}{3\mu_{2}^2} \Delta N_{1}^3).

This formula has also been confirmed in the stochastic-

This algorithm, proposed by Refs.

5
Topological defect

Hybrid inflation is known to be accompanied by topological defects in general
[
35
]
, as the waterfall dynamics spontaneously break the global
\mathrm{O}(n)
(
\mathrm{Z}_{2}
for
n=1
) symmetry.
The models with
n=1
,
n=2
, and
n=3
lead to the domain wall, cosmic string, and monopole, respectively.
5
5

5

It is also known that any non-Abelian compact global symmetry is accompanied by the texture if it is completely broken
[
17
,
16
,
64
]
, though the
\mathrm{O}(n-1)
symmetry remains for
n\geq 3
even after the end of inflation in our models.

These defects correspond to the
\psi_{\mathrm{r}}\sim 0
regions, which are not allowed to roll down toward the potential minimum due to topology.
On the other hand, a small
\psi_{\mathrm{r}}
is naïvely expected to result in a large
\delta\mathcal{N}
. In this section, we investigate whether the topological defect could also leave an imprint on the large-scale structure of the curvature perturbation.

5.1
Snapshots of the waterfall fields

In this subsection, we illustrate the region where the radial direction of the waterfall fields is below the threshold values at each time in Fig.
4
.
The threshold values are chosen as

\displaystyle\psi_{\rm r,th}=\begin{cases}0.05\sigma_{\psi}&\text{($n=1$; both for ``Quadratic" and ``Cubic"),}\\
0.1\sigma_{\psi}&\text{($n=2$; both for ``Quadratic" and ``Cubic"),}\\
0.5\sigma_{\psi}&\text{($n=3$),}\\
2.5\sigma_{\psi}&\text{($n=15$),}\end{cases}

(5.1)

where
\sigma_{\psi}
is the typical amplitude of the waterfall fields around the critical point (see Refs.
[
14
,
29
]
for its derivation),

\displaystyle\sigma_{\psi}\coloneqq\frac{\Lambda^{2}\sqrt{\Pi}}{4\sqrt{3}(2\pi^{3})^{1/4}M_{\mathrm{Pl}}}.

(5.2)

In particular, for
n=1
(domain wall),
n=2
(cosmic string), and
n=3
(monopole), at around time
N\sim 3\text{--}5
right after the inflaton passed the critical point
\phi_{\mathrm{c}}
, structures emerge that resemble topological defects, which then keep reconnecting due to the stochastic noise into very fine structures.
It has been naïvely expected that a single or a few defects are formed per the comoving Hubble patch at around the critical point
\phi_{\mathrm{c}}
in the literature (see, e.g., Ref.
[
63
]
). However, our result suggests that the stochastic reconnection makes their correlation lengths smaller, and a few defects are expected per the comoving Hubble patch at the end of inflation eventually.
The number density and also the spatial configuration of the topological defect in the mild-waterfall models should be investigated in more detail by continuing the simulation up to the end of inflation, which we leave for future work.

The monopole-like objects are formed at
N\sim 3
also for
n=15
, but they disappeared by the end of inflation.
It is because they are not topologically stable for
n\geq 4
.
One also finds for
n=1
and
2
that the “Quadratic” or “Cubic” hardly affects the structure because the topology is determined only by the symmetry of the waterfall fields.

5.2
Euler characteristic of waterfall fields

We also present the Euler characteristic corresponding to the snapshots exemplified in Fig.
4
to quantify the topological features of the waterfall field.
There, the grid points with
\psi_{\mathrm{r}}<\psi_{\mathrm{r},\mathrm{th}}
are understood as cubes with the grid size whose centres are put at the corresponding grids.
In Mathematica, which we used, these cubes are considered a single connected object if they are touching at a vertex, edge, or face.
We referred to the total number of connected objects in the simulation box
\mathscr{N}
.
The Euler characteristic
\chi_{I}
of the
I
-th object is defined by

\displaystyle\chi_{I}=V_{I}-E_{I}+F_{I}\quad(I=1,\cdots\mathscr{N}),

(5.3)

where
V_{I}
,
E_{I}
, and
F_{I}
are the number of vertices, edges, and faces of the
I
-th object, respectively.
We compute the total Euler characteristic for each object in the simulation box:

\displaystyle\chi=\sum_{I=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\chi_{I}.

(5.4)

The Euler characteristic of any polyhedron with the surface of a topological sphere (i.e., a single cube or cubes connected only by their faces without a hole in our case) is two.
A single hole reduces it by two, and a connection by edges or vertices increases it by one (see Appendix
A
).
Qualitatively, the Euler characteristic smaller than two on average implies the existence of certain global structures, while two or larger than two suggests more isolated configurations.

In Fig.
5
, we plot the time-evolution of the Euler characteristic of the waterfall field for each model.
For
n=1
or
2
, it becomes negative around
N\sim 4\text{--}5
, implying the emergence of holed domain walls or looped cosmic strings. It then turns into a large positive value because the stochastic noise reconnects the defects into finer structures and makes them unresolved, seen as isolated objects.
Its non-vanishment ensures the topologically stable structures.
Again, one finds that the topological structure does not significantly depend on the inflaton potential, i.e., “Quadratic” or “Cubic”.

For
n=3
, the monopoles and their reconnections monotonically increase the Euler characteristic.
One can again see that the monopoles are stable as the Euler characteristic approaches a constant.
In the case of
n=15
, it first increases but then disappears because the structures are not topologically stable.

In Fig.

\zeta_{\mathrm{th}}

\mathscr{N}

64^{3}

Only the case of
n=1

\zeta_{\mathrm{th}}\sim\sigma_{\zeta}

\sigma_{\zeta}=\sqrt{\overline{\delta\mathcal{N}^{2}}}
is the standard deviation in the simulation box, implying global structures of the curvature perturbation.
We do not conclude whether it is because of the domain wall or the characteristic
PDF

2

Indeed, the
n=2

n=3

5

For sufficiently high thresholds, the Euler characteristic approaches two, indicating that high peaks are configured isolatedly.
They finally disappeared for a too high threshold, and we define
\chi/\mathscr{N}=0
if there is no corresponding object in the box.
In the “Cubic” cases, as there are upper bounds on the curvature perturbation, the high-
\zeta

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we investigated the spatial profile of the curvature perturbation generated by a mild-waterfall-type hybrid inflation.
Given that this model is known to produce topological defects, we performed simulations using
STOLAS
(
STOLAS
), a lattice simulation code based on the stochastic formalism of inflation, to determine the topological configuration of the curvature perturbation that would be imprinted by defects.
We examined six cases (“Quadratic”
n=1
,
2
,
3
, and
15
, and “Cubic”
n=1
and
2
) by varying the number of waterfall fields
n
and the functional form of the inflaton potential.

In Sec.
4
, we analysed the statistical properties of the curvature perturbation.
Our results for the
PDF
(
PDF
) at
\delta\mathcal{N}\gtrsim 1
appeared to contradict previous research
[
44
]
.
This discrepancy arises because our results are averaged at each lattice point and thus coarse-grained at the lattice scale, while our previous computation corresponds to the minimal scale, i.e., the Hubble scale at the end of inflation.
We expect that increasing the resolution will allow
STOLAS
to reproduce the findings of
[
44
]
, which leaves for future work.
For the “Cubic” case, Ref.
[
44
]
previously pointed out the existence of the upper bound in the
PDF
.
We observed the same upper bound also in
STOLAS
, and it results in a bunch of regions with
\zeta
around the corresponding maximum value.
In such configurations, regions exceeding
\delta\mathcal{N}>1
do not exist; therefore,
PBH
(
PBH
) are seldom formed.
These structures may affect halo formation, making future
N
-body simulations an intriguing direction for research.
It would also be interesting to investigate the scalar-induced gravitational wave (see, e.g., Refs.
[
4
,
5
,
51
,
20
,
31
,
18
]
) from such configurations of the curvature perturbation.

The power spectrum was derived through a direct Fourier transform of the curvature perturbation.
Our results are well fitted using the analytical formula derived in Ref.
[
61
]
based on the stochastic-
\delta\mathcal{N}
algorithm.
Except for deviations caused by slow-roll effects, the algorithm is consistent with the
STOLAS
results.
While the peak of the power spectrum is roughly proportional to
1/n
in the “Quadratic” case as known in the literature
[
26
,
62
,
44
]
, we observed that the “Cubic” case does not follow this relation because the amplitude of the power spectrum is mainly determined by the upper bound which does not depend on the waterfall number
n
.

In Sec.
5
, we employed the Euler characteristic to investigate the topological properties of the waterfall fields and curvature perturbation.
We found it consistent with the interpretation that topological defects emerge according to their respective symmetries at around the critical point, and then reconnect into finer and finer structures due to the stochastic noise, as can be seen directly in snapshots (Fig.
4
) as well.
We leave a detailed study about the correlation length of the defects in the mild-waterfall models for future work.
Despite the formation of the topological defects, it is suggested that only the case of
n=1
leaves a non-trivial topological structure in the curvature perturbation.
The
n=1
model suffers from the domain wall problem, but it could be solved if the two potential minima are not completely degenerate (see, e.g., Ref.
[
50
]
for a recent review).
Then, such a topological structure of the curvature perturbation may leave a unique signature in, e.g., the cosmological large-scale structure.

Since the curvature perturbation map generated by
STOLAS
includes all information about the spatial correlation in principle, it can also calculate not only the power spectrum but also the bispectrum or higher-order correlators, for which no rigorous stochastic-
\delta\mathcal{N}
algorithm has been proposed.
It would also be important to investigate the practical
PBH
scenario for
n\sim 15
with an appropriate
PDF
.
We leave them for future work.

Appendix A
Computation of Euler characteristic in Mathematica

We here summarise the specification in calculation of the Euler characteristic with Mathematica.
Each point in the lattice is given as a cube, and the Euler characteristic is calculated by counting the number of vertices
V
, edges
E
, and faces
F
with
\chi=V-E+F
.
A single cube (case (a) in Fig.
7
) returns an Euler characteristic of
\chi=8-12-6=2
.
An object containing one hole yields
\chi=0
, while an object with two holes yields
\chi=-2
.
This corresponds to the general relation
\chi=2-2g
, where
g
is the genus (number of independent holes).
When two cubes share one full edge, Mathematica returns
\chi=3
, due to the resulting counts:
V=14,\ E=23,\ F=12
, which satisfy
\chi=V-E+F=3
.
Even when the cubes share only one vertex, the Euler characteristic computed is again
\chi=3
.

is summarised in Table
