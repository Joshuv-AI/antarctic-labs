Using biological control in free-range laying hens to prevent infections by digestive endoparasites. Effects on productivity, welfare and health
Authors: M.S. Arias, J.A. León, J. Cuervo, A.M. Palomero, G. Pérez, J.Á. Hernández, R. Bonilla, J. Guerrero, A. Paz-Silva, C. Cazapal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Controlling endoparasites in free-range laying hens remains a challenge, as the existence of infective stages in the soil poses a continuing risk of infection. To reduce the presence of oocysts and nematode eggs in the soil, the saprophytic filamentous parasitophagous fungus Mucor circinelloides was distributed in the shelters and pens of two small-scale poultry farms following a two-phase protocol. In the first trial, the conditioning phase involved spraying fungal spores in two pens every three days for eighteen or nine days before 16-week-old Lohmann Brown-Classic pullets were housed; subsequently, the maintenance phase consisted of spraying the spores every two weeks for one month; in another plot, water was sprayed (untreated controls). Oocysts of Eimeria spp., and eggs of Ascaridia galli and Capillaria spp. were identified in the bird feces, which reduced by 65-95% (χ2= 25.471, P = 0.001), 84-100% (χ2= 159.992, P = 0.001), and 75-89% (χ2= 206.074, P = 0.001), respectively, in the plots sprayed with fungal spores. Trial 2 was performed into two plots and comprised a conditioning phase (with spores) of nine days followed by a maintenance phase of 10 months, and another control plot (water-sprayed). Oocysts of Eimeria spp. and eggs of A. galli were detected in the feces, and their values decreased by 71-95% (χ2= 165.212, P = 0.001) and 67-100% (χ2= 137.684, P = 0.001), respectively. In both trials, similar results were obtained for body weight, laying rate (% laying / bird / day), and the numbers of eggs per housed bird, as well as for egg characteristics (weight, freshness, shell thickness, and yolk color). It is concluded that spraying of M. circinelloides in shelters and pens of small-scale poultry farms following a biphasic protocol provides a useful tool for applying this biocontrol agent to reduce the risk of infection by digestive endoparasites in free-range laying hens, without affecting their health, welfare, egg production or quality. Further studies on a larger number of birds are needed to confirm these results.

Introduction

In recent decades, egg production from free-range laying hens (Gallus gallus domesticus) has been increasing in response to consumer demand, supported by the belief that conventional cages are welfare-compromising (Shields et al., 2017; Jeni et al., 2021). Free-range farming has shown marketable benefits that customers clearly appreciate, such as antibiotic-free poultry products, eggs with elevated content of polyunsaturated fatty acids, and meat with a highly prized flavor, which have resulted in overall commercial appeal (Pettersson et al., 2016; Bray and Ankeny, 2017; Rodríguez-Mengod et al., 2024).

The management of farms focused on the production of eggs from free-range hens is characterized by the mandatory availability of an outdoor area that allows the hens to dust-bathe, forage for food and sunbathe (Commission Regulation (EC) No 589/2008 of 23 June 2008 laying down detailed rules for the implementing of Council Regulation (EC) No 1234/2007 as regards marketing standards for eggs, 2008). In addition to space and fresh air, birds can also forage, run and/or fly, while the incidence of pecking is reduced due to lower stocking densities (Bestman et al., 2018).

Grazing or pasturing has also been associated with health benefits, such as improved plumage condition and a reduced incidence of footpad dermatitis (Rodriguez-Aurrekoetxea and Estevez, 2016). However, free-range hens are at higher risk than caged hens of certain problems, such as heat stress, smothering or predation (Campbell et al., 2021). Another problem consists of some infections caused by endo- and ectoparasites that develop part of their life cycle in the ground until the infective stages are attained (Bestman et al., 2023). Previous studies have demonstrated that high prevalences of parasitic diseases can be reached among individuals accessing ground or vegetation, especially regarding helminths such as cestodes (Raillietina spp.) or nematodes (Ascaridia galli, Heterakis spp., Capillaria spp.) (Thapa et al., 2015; Wuthijaree et al., 2017). The prevalence of hens infected with one or more helminth species was 99-100% in organic flocks from Germany and Italy (Kaufmann et al., 2011; Wuthijaree et al., 2017). Most endoparasites have direct cycles, and though earthworms can act as intermediate hosts, it has been suggested that contact with feces of domestic birds previously reared in the same paddocks, or with feces of wild birds, is the cause of higher levels of parasite infection in free-range or organic hens. Also, the fact that helminths eggs can survive and remain infective in the soil for months or years (Thapa et al., 2015; Feyera et al., 2022; Bestman et al., 2023). Furthermore, prior investigations have pointed out that Ascaridia and Heterakis are associated with reduced productivity and welfare in hens (Permin et al., 2006).

Control of parasites is usually addressed through the administration of dewormers, although the use of anthelmintics appears to be responsible of adverse effects directly in poultry, and the presence of residues ending up in products intended for human consumption or accumulation in the environment (Bestman et al., 2023). In this way, certain species of saprophytic filamentous fungi have been successfully tested as biocontrol agents of different helminths (strongyles, roundworms or threadworms) affecting livestock (cattle, sheep, goats, pigs and horses) (Mendoza-de Gives et al., 2018; Alves do Carmo et al., 2023). The best known species of parasitophagous fungi are antagonists of the immobile stages (oocysts/eggs) (Mucor circinelloides, Pochonia chlamydosporia, Trichoderma spp., Purpureocillium lilacinum), or the mobile phases (larvae) (Duddingtonia flagrans, Monacrosporium thaumasium, Arthrobotrys spp.) (Fernández et al., 2023; Salmo et al., 2024; Fonseca et al., 2025). Recently, it has been reported that the viability of eggs of ascarids and capillarids in birds feces was reduced by 64–79% and 79–82%, respectively, in the presence of M. circinelloides (León et al., 2025).

Based on the demonstration of some parasitophagous fungi can survive the passage through the gastrointestinal tract of domestic chickens, their oral administration has been successfully assayed, resulting in a significant reduction on the viability of eggs of A. galli and H. gallinarum in feces of birds feeding on P. chlamydosporia (Valadão et al., 2020); likewise,coccidial infection (caused by Eimeria spp.) was decreased in free-range peacocks living in a public park receiving M. circinelloides (Lozano et al., 2024a). Oral administration of these biological control agents for parasites interferes with their survival and development in feces, thus decreasing the risk of infection. Nevertheless, some parasitic stages can remain viable for months and even years (Sander and Neumann, 2024).

With the aim of preventing endoparasite infection in free-range laying hens and, thereby, reducing the incidence of damage and the resulting need for frequent chemical deworming, a pilot study was conducted that focused on targeting parasitic stages found in the soil to reduce their survival and ability to develop into the infective stage. This entailed spraying shelters and pastures with M. circinelloides spores on two small-scale poultry farms, following a biphasic protocol.

Materials and methods

The present investigation was carried out in Galicia (NW Spain), at two poultry houses keeping small flocks of between 60 - 90 birds raised at a high outdoor stocking density (≈ 4-5 hens/m2) (Bonnefous et al., 2022) (Fig. 1). Saprophytic Fungus with Parasiticide Activity

The saprophytic filamentous fungus Mucor circinelloides (Spanish Type Collection Culture, CECT 28024), with demonstrated antagonism on oocysts/eggs of some endoparasites, was applied by spraying in the poultry houses. According to previous trials, the fungus was grown in a submerged medium until concentrations ≈ 106 chlamydospores / mL were reached (Arias et al., 2013), then sprayed at 0.25 L/m2, and no problems related to excessive moisture were observed when using this concentration (León et al., 2025). Prior to distribution, the viability of the spores was assessed by taking five 25-μl aliquots/l of medium and analyzing their morphology under an optical microscope (40X). A viability rate of ≥98% was obtained.

Study design

The current study consisted of the distribution of chlamydospores of M. circinelloides inside the shelters (on the floor), and outside on the grass. For these purposes, two trials were conducted:

*Trial 1 involved the assessment of the appropriate period of applications of fungal spores, nine or eighteen days before the birds arrived at the farm, to reduce the risk of infection.

*Trial 2 aimed to establish the effect of spraying the fungal chlamydospores to maintain a low level of infection that would not affect the health or productivity of the birds.

A total of 230 Lohmann Brown-Classic pullets (16 weeks old) were used in the two trials, all of which were vaccinated (Evant®, Hipra, Spain) and dewormed with amprolium (Coccivex® 200 mg amprolium/ml; S.P. Veterinaria, Spain; 30 mg/kg body weight/day for 5 days) and fenbendazole (Gallifen® 200 mg fenbendazole/ml; Huvepharma NV, Bulgaria) before entering the respective poultry farms. The number of birds on each farm was determined taking into account that, as mentioned earlier, both farms observe a high outdoor stocking density.

Trial 1. Conditioning Phase

At the PRODEME facilities, three plots (100-125 m2 each) used for the regular rearing of hens to produce eggs for self-consumption. These plots are divided by wooden and wire fences, with their respective shelters (with a total surface area of approximately 7-9 m2) equipped with feeders, drinkers, perches and nesting boxes. A shade net (10×10 m) was placed alongside the shelters to promote access by the hens to the range area.

The conditioning protocol consisted of spraying (plot and shelter) Mucor at doses of 106 spores/m2 (0.5 L medium/m2) every three days, starting nine days before the hens entered plot GC1, and from 18 days before entry into plot GC2 (Fig. 3). Water (as a placebo) was sprayed on the plot and the shelter (control group) 18 days before the birds were brought in. Trial 2. Maintenance phase

The usefulness of spraying chlamydospores of Mucor to maintain a low risk of infection in laying hens was evaluated at a private poultry farm. A 200 m2 paddock was divided into two similar sections (≈100 m2) fenced with bricks and wire mesh to prevent the birds and their droppings could pass from one section to another. In addition, each section had a shelter completely independent of the other and equipped with feeders, drinkers and laying troughs, with a total area of approximately 5-6.5 m2 each. A shade net (10×10 m) was installed at the front of the shelters.

The shelter in plot GM was conditioned before the hens arrived at the farm, by spraying a dose of 106 spores/m2 on days −9, −6, −3 and 0 (entry). Twenty 16-week-old Lohmann Brown-Classic pullets were introduced into each plot (stocking density= 5 birds/m2). During a period of 10 months, every two weeks, 10 fresh fecal samples were taken directly from the soil of each plot, then analyzed by coprological tests (in duplicate).

Four months after the trial was completed, it was repeated with two new groups of 20 hens each, as shown in Fig. 3.

Coprological tests

The fecal samples collected during the trials were tested by using two different probes. The presence of Eimeria oocysts and eggs of nematodes was ascertained by means of the McMaster flotation test using saturated saline solution (sensitivity= 50 eggs or oocysts per gram of feces) (Lozano et al., 2024a). Briefly, three grams of each sample were weighed, added 42 mL distilled water and stirred until completely homogenized, then filtered through a 150 μm mesh. The filtered liquid was placed in two 15 ml glass tubes and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 minutes. The supernatant was discarded, saturated saline solution (specific gravity: 1.2) was added to the tubes and the mixture was homogenized again, and finally the two cells of a McMaster chamber were filled. Under a light microscope at 10-20x magnification, the parasites were initially classified as non-viable (alterations in the shell and/or cytoplasm) and viable. Finally, viable parasite counts were expressed as the number of oocysts or eggs per gram of feces (OPG or EPG, respectively) (León et al., 2025).

The possibility of parasite larvae being excreted in bird feces was investigated using a Baermann probe, which consists of placing 10 g of fecal sample in a funnel, closing the end and adding water until the feces are completely covered. After 12 hours, one mL was collected and analyzed in a Favati chamber, and the results are expressed as number of larvae per gram of feces (LPG).

All tests were conducted by technicians who did not know which group the samples belonged to. Two different persons analyzed each sample.

Every week, the laying rate (% Hen-Daily Egg Production, HDEP), and the numbers of eggs per housed bird (Hen-Housed Egg Production, HHEP) were estimated according to the formulae:HDEP (%) = (Total number of eggs laid / Total number of live laying hens) x 100HHEP = Total eggs produced / Total hens housed at start of laying period

Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, which showed that the kinetics of endoparasite-output were not normally distributed, and for this reason, data were subsequently analyzed for differences by means of the non-parametric test Friedman in Trial 1, and the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank in Trial 2. Besides this, the Kendall’s W and the Spearman’s tests were applied for assessing the effect size. Significant differences were considered when P < 0.05. All tests were carried out by using the statistical SPSS package, version 30 (IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Parasites detection

Trial 1

Since no statistically significant differences were observed between the two replicates, their data were pooled and treated and drawn as a single combined experiment. The analysis of feces showed coccidian oocysts (Eimeria necatrix, E. tenella, E. maxima) from the second week in the controls, and from the third week in the presence of fungus (Fig. 5). In the feces of hens kept in the plots GC1 and GC2 (both exposed to the spores of M. circinelloides), Eimeria OPG counts remained below 300 throughout the trial, which means a 65-95% reduction compared to the untreated controls. Health, welfare and production

No mortality was observed in any of the groups of hens throughout the study. The percentage of hens with dermatitis on their footpads was less than 2%, with no differences between the two groups. There were also no differences in the levels of damage or cleanliness of the plumage, and the body condition score (BCS) ranged from 3 to 3.5 in all birds.

The analysis of production parameters in Trial 2 showed the absence of any negative effect on the hens exposed to M. circinelloides spores, and differences with control hens were not significant (P > 0.05). Egg laying began three weeks after the birds entered the two plots. No differences were observed in the percentage of birds laying eggs each day or in the number of eggs per housed bird in relation to the spraying of M. circinelloides (Fig. 7). Discussion

The effect of spraying spores of the fungus M. circinelloides for preventing the infection by digestive endoparasites among free-range laying hens was ascertained. A significant reduction in fecal counts of oocysts of coccidia and eggs of gastrointestinal nematodes was observed compared to untreated controls not exposed to the spores of the parasitophagous fungus. These results are partly in agreement with those of Lozano et al. (2022), who demonstrated a notable decrease in the numbers of Eimeria oocysts passed in the feces of grazing peacocks administered orally chlamydospores of M. circinelloides. In a study performed on Petri dishes, Valadão et al. (2020) reported that the ovicide fungus P. chlamydosporia was able of destroying 59.9% of A. galli eggs and 43.2% of H. gallinarum.

Cage-free systems enhance the exposure of birds to certain parasites that spend part of their life cycle in the soil, and the risk of ingestion of infective oocysts, eggs, and/or larvae increases significantly (Wuthijaree et al., 2017; Shifaw et al., 2021). In this study, protozoan oocysts (Eimeria spp.) and nematodes eggs (Ascaridia, Heterakis and Capillaria spp.) were found in the feces of laying hens 2-3 weeks after they were first introduced to a plot with vegetation, partly in agreement with Bonnefous et al. (2022), and the numbers increased especially during the first weeks,. Jansson et al. (2010) reported that 4.3% of caged birds were infected with endoparasites, compared to 77% of free-range/organic birds. In an observational cross-sectional study carried out in eight European countries (Austria, Belgium, Denmark, Germany, Italy, The Netherlands, Sweden and the United Kingdom) it was pointed out that 69.5% of free-range organic layers were infected with Ascaridia galli, 29% with Heterakis spp. and 14% with Raillietina spp. (Thapa et al., 2015). A higher prevalence of infection with cestodes and the nematode A. galli was demonstrated in “rangers” (hens which move freely) than in “stayers” (which rarely or never go outside) in Australia, and the frequency and extent of individual use of an outdoor free-range area did not influence the numbers of A. galli nematodes (Bari et al., 2020; Sibanda et al., 2020).

Control of parasites is still based on deworming, and the limited number of antiparasitic drugs registered for poultry has been associated with high frequencies of administration, resulting in an elevated selective pressure conducive to anthelmintic resistance (Knapp-Lawitzke et al., 2015; McDougald, 2020; Feyera et al., 2022; Collins et al., 2025). Nevertheless, three treatments instead of one (the conventional strategy being one treatment) has been recommended in Sweden, as a way to reduce the level of infection in the soil (Tarbiat et al., 2016). Another problem arising from the administration of anthelmintics to laying hens is that caused by chemical residues in eggs for human consumption (Lahr et al., 2018). In view of these situations, an interesting safe and efficient proposal to reduce the use of anthelmintics could rely on treating hens only when the fecal egg counts exceed a given threshold. By considering a threshold of 200 eggs/g of feces, deworming every 4-6 weeks was dropped to three treatments per flock a year in the Netherlands (Bestman and Wagenaar, 2014). Considering all of the above, the persistence of the infectious stages of endoparasites in the soil causes birds to become reinfected quickly, since deworming only acts on the hosts. .

The oral administration of different saprophytic filamentous fungi has been successfully tested in order to assist in the control of certain parasites that affect livestock and captive wild species (Mendoza-de Gives et al., 2018; Voinot et al., 2021; Fernández et al., 2023; Alves do Carmo et al., 2023; Salmo et al., 2024; Fonseca et al., 2025). Regarding poultry, only a few field trials have been performed, and when enriching peacocks feeding with spores of M. circinelloides, a significant reduction in the fecal oocysts counts of Eimeria spp. was highlighted (Lozano et al., 2024a). By adding a blend of spores of P. chlamydosporia and D. flagrans to the feed, low reductions in the counts of A. galli eggs were reported (Ferreira et al., 2025). In a previous investigation, chicken feces containing eggs of ascarids and capillarids were placed on a pastureland, and four weeks after spraying them with M. circinelloides spores, egg-viability was reduced by 64% to 82% (León et al., 2025). Accordingly, and in order to gain information under field conditions a two-phase protocol was tested in the current investigation. The conditioning phase consisted of spraying spores of M. circinelloides every three days, beginning nine or 18 days before the hens were brought into the shelters. These treatments resulted in a significant decrease in the numbers of Eimeria occysts and of eggs of ascarids and Capillaria passed in the feces. Considering the possibility of hens might become infected when feeding on grass, a maintenance phase was observed, spraying fungal spores every two weeks in the shelter and paddock. The fecal analyses showed significantly lower counts of fecal parasitic stages in comparison to the absence of fungus (untreated controls).It is also interesting to note that, although all the birds were dewormed before being placed in their respective pens, they became reinfected quickly in the absence of the parasitophagous fungus (control groups), and high fecal levels of Eimeria oocysts were reached, along with a threshold of 200 roundworm eggs per gram of feces, in the control groups starting in the fourth week, but not in the plots where M. circinelloides was sprayed, which can easily be attributed to its antagonistic effect on the infectious stages of the endoparasites. Although the current research involved only two small-scale egg-laying poultry farms and should therefore be considered a pilot study, the results regarding the control of endoparasites are very promising, though further trials encompassing a larger number of farms and larger farms and of greater size appear needed to definitive confirmation. Production of eggs in many developed countries has experienced significant changes over the past two decades, many of them in response to consumer demands (Kaufmann et al., 2011; Ruhnke et al., 2015). Accordingly, conventional cages have been replaced by enriched cages and free-range systems involving barn, free-range and organic ones (Terčič et al., 2012; Philippe et al., 2020; Pavlović et al., 2020). Unlike cage systems, access to deep littered floors or even grassed outdoor areas is provided to laying hens under barn or free-range production systems, respectively, for the purpose of improving their welfare, and behavior manifestations such as sunbathing, dust bathing, running, playing, resting, wing stretching (Commission Regulation (EC) No 589/2008 of 23 June 2008 laying down detailed rules for implementing Council Regulation (EC) No 1234/2007 as regards marketing standards for eggs, 2008). The changes implemented in this sector of the poultry industry have focused, in part, on increasing the space available to birds, mainly suggesting cage-free systems to improve their behavioral expressions and, therefore, their welfare. In this way, hens in barn systems are reared (without cages) on deep littered floors in a confined poultry house with controlled ambient conditions; free-range production systems are similar to barn systems, but birds also have access to an outdoor free-range area. Finally, organic production is conducted like a free-range system with several specific and restrictive production standards, including the provision of organic feed that must be free of synthetic additives and genetically modified organisms (Samiullah et al., 2017; Lordelo et al., 2017; Dalle Zotte et al., 2021). A positive relationship between housing systems and egg-quality has been established, with a higher proportion of albumin in organic eggs than in those produced by caged hens, and the same was found for moisture and polyunsaturated fatty acids (n-6 and n-3 series) contents (Dalle Zotte et al., 2021). In the current trial, spraying the fungus in the henhouse and throughout the pasture did not influence the weight of the hens or production parameters, such as the percentage of birds laying eggs each day and the number of eggs per housed bird. Examination of egg characteristics (weight, freshness, shell thickness, and yolk color) also showed no differences in relation to the distribution of the fungus. Furthermore, the low prevalence of dermatitis on the footpads, together with a BCS of 3-3.5, rules out the existence of side effects or negative productivity effects, confirming the safety of this preventive measure. The innocuousness and efficacy of M. circinelloides as a biological control agent have already been demonstrated in animal species such as horses, heifers, lambs, and poultry (Hernández et al., 2018; Voinot et al., 2020, 2021; Lozano et al., 2024b). Therefore, although no studies have been conducted on the fungus’s persistence in the soil, it is important to note that this saprophytic species acts as a decomposer of excrement, which is essential for the nutrient cycle and the decomposition of organic matter, and also serves as a food source for other soil microorganisms (Acheampong et al., 2025), and to promote the biosynthesis and accumulation of tanshinones in plant roots (Chen et al., 2021). The absence of differences in plant development in the presence of this fungus led to the conclusion that it is safe for vegetation (Magos Amado et al., 2026).

Parasites affecting the digestive tract in free-range laying hens are frequently detected and attributed to their encountering feces of infected domestic or wild avian individuals passing oocysts, eggs and/or larvae of some endoparasites (Jansson et al., 2010; Bestman et al., 2023). The finding in the present study of significant reductions in the numbers of oocysts of Eimeria spp. and eggs of ascarids and Capillaria in hens maintained in environments where spores of M. circinelloides had been previously sprayed confirms the remarkable antagonistic and destructive effect of this fungus reported in previous investigations (Lozano et al., 2022), and on the possibilities of reinfection.

Conclusion

To the best of our understanding, this is the first field trial involving the use of parasitophagous fungi to prevent the infection by digestive endoparasites in laying hens reared under a free-range regime. A strategy entailing two phases, spraying with spores of M. circinelloides in the shelters and pens for at least nine days before the birds entered the poultry farms (conditioning phase), and then every two weeks while they remained there (maintenance phase), resulted in a significant reduction of fecal counts of coccidia oocysts and gastrointestinal nematode eggs. No adverse effects on health, production or welfare were detected. Therefore, it is concluded that this biphasic protocol of treatment provides a practical biological control tool for decreasing the risk of infection by endoparasites in free-range laying hens, without compromising their health or egg production or quality. Further research is being conducted on other farms with larger flocks to confirm these results regarding the control of endoparasites, as well as the safety in the soil.
