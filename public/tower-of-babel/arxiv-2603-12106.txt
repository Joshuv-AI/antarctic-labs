Space-Efficient Approximate Spherical Range Counting in High Dimensions
Andreas Kalavas, Ioannis Psarros

Abstract
We study the following range searching problem in high-dimensional Euclidean spaces: given a finite set P ⊂ ℝ d P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} , where each p ∈ P p\in P is assigned a weight w p w_{p} , and radius r > 0 r>0 , we need to preprocess P P into a data structure such that when a new query point q ∈ ℝ d q\in\mathbb{R}^{d} arrives, the data structure reports the cumulative weight of points of P P within Euclidean distance r r from q q . Solving the problem exactly seems to require space usage that is exponential to the dimension, a phenomenon known as the curse of dimensionality. Thus, we focus on approximate solutions where points up to ( 1 + ε ) ​ r (1+\varepsilon)r away from q q may be taken into account, where ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 is an input parameter known during preprocessing. We build a data structure with near-linear space usage, and query time in n 1 − Θ ⁡ ( ε 4 / log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) ) + t q ϱ ⋅ n 1 − ϱ n^{1-\Theta(\varepsilon^{4}/\log(1/\varepsilon))}+t_{q}^{\varrho}\cdot n^{1-\varrho} , for some ϱ = Θ ⁡ ( ε 2 ) \varrho=\Theta(\varepsilon^{2}) , where t q t_{q} is the number of points of P P in the ambiguity zone, i.e., at distance between r r and ( 1 + ε ) ​ r (1+\varepsilon)r from the query q q . To the best of our knowledge, this is the first data structure with efficient space usage (subquadratic or near-linear for any ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 ) and query time that remains sublinear for any sublinear t q t_{q} . We supplement our worst-case bounds with a query-driven preprocessing algorithm to build data structures that are well-adapted to the query distribution.

Introduction

Range searching is a fundamental problem
with
wide applications in areas such as computational geometry
[
13
]
, databases
[
36
]
, geographic information systems (GIS)
[
25
]
, and computer graphics
[
4
]
. One fundamental variant of range searching is that of
range counting
, where given a dataset
P
, we need to preprocess it into a data structure so that for a query range
R_{q}
, we can efficiently estimate
|R_{q}\cap P|
. Primary examples of types of query ranges include rectangles, balls, halfspaces, and simplices.

In this paper, we study the special case of range counting queries for Euclidean balls of fixed radius. Solving the problem exactly seems to require space usage that is exponential to the dimension, a phenomenon known as the curse of dimensionality. Therefore, aiming for solutions that scale well with the dimension, we focus on approximate solutions where points up to
(1+\varepsilon)r
away from
q
may be considered, where
\varepsilon>0
is an input parameter known during preprocessing.
Formally, given as input a dataset
P\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, such that each point
p\in P
is assigned a weight
w_{p}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, search radius
r>0
, and approximation parameter
\varepsilon>0
, the
approximate spherical range counting
problem is to preprocess
P
into a data structure that given any query point
q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, it reports

w_{S}=\sum_{p\in S}w_{p}
, where
S\subseteq P
is
a subset such that

B(q,r)\cap P\subseteq S\subseteq B(q,(1+\varepsilon)r)\cap P
, where, for any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
r>0
,
B(x,r)
denotes the Euclidean ball of radius
r
centered at
x
.
Since we can uniformly rescale the dataset, we assume throughout the paper that the search radius is
1
.

Related work

We juxtapose the above problem definition with a different notion of approximate counting, adapted for example by Aronov and Har-Peled
[
10
]
, where one is interested in approximating the number of points inside a query range. Given a query range
R_{q}
, the data structure shall return a value
\nu
such that
(1-\varepsilon)|R_{q}\cap P|\leq\nu\leq(1+\varepsilon)|R_{q}\cap P|
. Aronov and Har-Peled
[
10
]
show that one can reduce such a counting
query to a sequence of “emptiness” queries, determining if the query range is empty or not, of length polynomial in
\log|P|
and
\varepsilon^{-1}
. In contrast, our definition asks for an
exact
aggregate over a set whose geometry is allowed to vary within the annulus
B(q,1+\varepsilon)\setminus B(q,1)
. This definition has been used, e.g., by Arya and Mount
[
11
]
, although they study solutions in the low-dimensional regime.

In terms of known solutions, approximate spherical range counting in high dimensions is
not clearly distinguished from other variants of approximate range searching. Perhaps the most promising approach for related problems in high dimensions is locality sensitive hashing (LSH) (see
[
26
,
20
,
6
,
8
]
), which maps the points to buckets with the promise that near (resp. far) points are more (resp. less) likely to be mapped together.
It is not clear how one could use an LSH-based approach to solve the counting variant without paying at least the cost of exploring all points in
B(q,1)
. This suggests that, within LSH-based frameworks, counting is unlikely to admit a fundamentally more efficient solution than reporting.
In this work, we focus on designing solutions better suited to the counting variant.

Our Results

Our main contribution is to design a data structure for the approximate range counting problem in high-dimensional Euclidean spaces. For any constant approximation parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, our data structure uses near-linear space, polynomial preprocessing time, and query time that is sublinear for any sublinear number of points in the ambiguity zone, i.e., for any sublinear
t_{q}=|\left(B(q,1+\varepsilon)\setminus B(q,1)\right)\cap P|
the query time is sublinear.

Our approach is reminiscent of classic results for the
exact
range searching problem in low-dimensional Euclidean spaces. In particular, a central notion of our approach is that of partition trees
[
32
,
14
]
. We adapt well-studied relations between spanning trees with low stabbing
1
1

1

which also appears as “crossing” in previous works
numbers and partition trees (see, e.g.,
[
15
]
) to our approximate setting. A key difference in our setting is that we use a stronger notion of stabbing; we say that a point
q

\varepsilon
-stabs a set
S
if
\exists x\in S:\|x-q\|\leq 1
and
\exists y\in S:\|y-q\|\geq 1+\varepsilon
(classic stabbing would correspond to the case where
\varepsilon=0
).
An important step in our preprocessing algorithm is finding a “light” edge, i.e., a pair of input points that is
\varepsilon
-stabbed by a
polynomial in
d
ratio of the relevant queries.
Then, using the multiplicative weight update (MWU) method in a similar manner as
[
38
,
15
,
39
]
, we are able to show that we can compute a spanning tree with low
\varepsilon
-stabbing number, implying that we can build a partition tree such that for any query, we only need to visit a bounded number of nodes. To traverse the partition tree, we need to be able to decide for each internal node if the corresponding subset is
\varepsilon
-stabbed by the query. We can only do this approximately, meaning that we may also explore nodes that are almost
\varepsilon
-stabbed, but the overall cost of this exploration is proportional to
t_{q}
.

Theorem 1

(Simplified version of
\Cref

.

Given a set of
n

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we can build a randomized data structure for the approximate range counting problem, with space usage in
\tilde{{O}}(n)
, preprocessing time in

and sublinear query time if
t_{q}
is also sublinear.

Our data structure is the first to achieve space usage that is near-linear in
n
and polynomial in
d
, and query time that is sublinear in
n
and polynomial in
d
for any sublinear
t_{q}
. In
\Cref
table, we juxtapose our result with previous known results that even implicitly solve the same problem. While LSH is originally proposed for the approximate near neighbor problem, where the data structure is only required to report one witness point within distance
1+\varepsilon
, Ahle et al.
[
2
]
proposed an LSH-based solution for the problem of reporting all points. Their data structure is adaptive; while building time and space can be that of an LSH table prepared for worst-case inputs (parameter
\rho
in
\Cref
table), query time is that of the best LSH parameters for the query/instance (parameter
b
).
Moreover, LSH tuned for near-linear space
[
8
]
can solve the reporting problem with only a logarithmic overhead in space and query time, since we can amplify the probability that every near neighbor will eventually be encountered. Since both LSH-based methods can solve the reporting variant, they can also solve the counting problem. However, it is not clear whether one can modify such an approach to avoid looking into all near points.
Although our result does not guarantee sublinear query time in the worst case, it advances prior space-efficient work in high dimensions by making the complexity depend on the number of points in the annulus rather than on all points inside the entire ball.
Trivially, the bucketing method, when combined with a Johnson-Lindenstrauss dimension reduction (as in
[
20
]
), can solve the counting problem; after reducing the dimension, one can store the relevant information in grid cells near the dataset. Such methods that discretize the query space, while providing efficient query times, unavoidably have huge space requirements. Finally, when the dimension is constant, partition trees
[
14
]
solve the problem exactly, while BBD-trees
[
11
]
leverage improved query times for approximate solutions (although still exponential in
d
).

In addition to our worst-case guarantees, we present an algorithm inspired by learning theory. Specifically, this algorithm is query-driven, i.e., it receives a number of query samples and aims to construct the data structure that yields the best expected query runtime for the distribution of the queries. This way, we design a much faster preprocessing algorithm (from
n^{\mathsf{poly}(1/\varepsilon)}
, the complexity falls to
n^{O(1)}
), which gives us similar bounds for the average case.

Our approach is motivated by the viewpoint of analyzing algorithms through the lens of statistical learning (e.g.,
[
19
,
12
]
). While this has been influential in optimization and mechanism design, to the best of our knowledge, a similar treatment for data structures is absent. Although related ideas appear in learned index structures
[
31
]
, LSH trees whose cutting rules are optimized with respect to the given dataset
[
7
]
, learning-augmented binary trees with optimized search assuming access to advice from a frequency estimation oracle
[
30
]
, these works do not directly cast the data structure construction itself as a PAC learning problem.
We also remark that the related notions of self-improving
[
3
]
and instance-optimal algorithms
[
1
]
address different guarantees.
A simplified version of our result follows:

Theorem 2

(Simplified version of
\Cref

.

Given a set of
n
points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and a sample of
O(nd\log n)

, we can compute a partition tree

We believe this is a neat way to use tools from other areas to design efficient algorithms for difficult problems or problems with only existence guarantees, and it would be interesting to see what other data-structure problems might benefit from this viewpoint.

(\varepsilon,r)

A

{\mathcal{T}}

, and for each node
v

Range spaces

Fix a countably infinite domain set

A

2.3
Model

We assume that our algorithms operate in the Real-RAM model with the additional assumption that the floor function can be computed in constant time.
While it is known that this model is strictly more powerful than the classical word‑RAM
[
16
]
, it is often used in the Computational Geometry literature
[
23
]
. We stress that we do not use encodings that pack superpolynomial information into a single machine word or real number, and our use of
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
is purely for convenience (e.g., discretization and bucketing).

One building block of our algorithms is a data structure known as the dictionary.
A
dictionary
is a data structure which stores a set of (key, value) pairs
and when presented with a key, either returns the corresponding value, or returns that the key is
not stored in the dictionary. In our case, keys are typically vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and values are pointers to lists.
In the Real-RAM model, we can use a balanced binary tree to build a dictionary with
n
values, using
\tilde{O}(dn)
preprocessing time and storage and
\tilde{O}(d)
query time.

3
Approximate Stabbing Queries

In this section, we design a solution for our main subproblem, that is, the problem of preprocessing a set of points to support queries that ask to determine if a query
\varepsilon
-stabs the given pointset. In particular, the data structure is required to determine if the query
\varepsilon
-stabs the pointset, if all points are near the query, or if all points are far from the query. The resulting data structure returns approximate answers, meaning that it allows for wrong decisions when the pointset is “nearly”
\varepsilon
-stabbed, as formalized in
\Cref
thminternalnode. This is sufficient for our purposes, which are building these data structures in internal nodes of a partition tree to assist in traversing the tree to answer a query.

Our main technical ingredient is a randomized embedding to the Hamming metric, which is implemented using LSH to partition the space into buckets and then applying random binarization of the buckets. A similar argument has been used in Alman et al.
[
5
]
, where they first embed the points to
\ell_{1}
and then use an LSH function to partition points into buckets; each bucket is then assigned to a random bit. Repeating the above sufficiently many times gives the desired embedding. We obtain a slightly more direct construction that does not need to embed the points in
\ell_{1}
, but instead uses the LSH family of Datar et al.
[
18
]
. The final embedding succeeds in preserving distances with respect to a single scale; distances considered small with respect to a given threshold remain small, and large distances remain large. This is formally stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 9
.

For any
\delta,\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists a randomized embedding
f:~{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to\{0,1\}^{d^{\prime}}
, and specific
\theta\in[d^{\prime}]
, such that, for any
p,q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

•

if
\|p-q\|\leq 1
then
{\mathrm{P}r}\left[\|f(p)-f(q)\|_{1}>\theta\right]\leq e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}d^{\prime}}{19200}}

•

if
\|p-q\|\geq(1+\varepsilon)
then
{\mathrm{P}r}\left[f\|(p)-f(q)\|_{1}<\theta+\varepsilon d^{\prime}\right]\leq e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}d^{\prime}}{19200}}
.

Moreover, for any
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
f(p)
can be computed in
{O}(d)
running time.

Proof.

We employ the LSH family of
[
18
]
. For
p\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
, consider the random function
h(p)=\left\lfloor\frac{\langle p,g\rangle+s}{w}\right\rfloor
,
where
g
is a
d
-dimensional vector with i.i.d. random variables following
N(0,1)
,
s
is a shift chosen uniformly at random from
[0,w]
, and
w
is a fixed parameter defining the side-length of the one-dimensional grid on the line. We set
w=1+\varepsilon
. Then, the probability of collision for two points
p,q
is

\int_{s=0}^{1+\varepsilon}\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}\|p-q\|}e^{\left(-\frac{s^{2}}{2{\|p-q\|}^{2}}\right)}\left(1-\frac{s}{1+\varepsilon}\right)dt.

Let
p_{1}:={\mathrm{P}r}[h(p)=h(q)\mid\|p-q\|\leq 1]
and
p_{2}:={\mathrm{P}r}[h(p)=h(q)\mid\|p-q\|\geq 1+\varepsilon]
.
Using straightforward calculus, we obtain the following:

p_{1}-p_{2}\geq\varepsilon/5\text{ and }p_{2}\geq 1/4.

(1)

We now sample
d^{\prime}
independent hash functions from the above family.
For each
i\in[d^{\prime}]
, we denote by
h_{i}(P)
the image of
P
under
h_{i}
, i.e., a set of nonempty buckets.
Now each point in each nonempty bucket
b\in h_{i}(P)
is mapped to
\{0,1\}
: with probability
1/2
, for each
x\in b
, set
f_{i}(x)=0
, otherwise set
f_{i}(x)=1
.
We define
f(p)=(f_{1}(h_{1}(p)),\ldots,f_{d^{\prime}}(h_{d^{\prime}}(p))).

Now, observe that

\displaystyle\|p-q\|\leq 1\implies\mathbb{E}\left[\|f(p)-f(q)\|_{1}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{d^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[|f_{i}(h_{i}(p))-f_{i}(h_{i}(q))|\right]\leq 0.5d^{\prime}\left(1-p_{1}\right)=:\mu_{1},

and

\displaystyle\|p-q\|\geq 1+\varepsilon\implies\mathbb{E}\left[\|f(p)-f(q)\|_{1}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{d^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[|f_{i}(h_{i}(p))-f_{i}(h_{i}(q))|\right]\geq 0.5d^{\prime}\left(1-p_{2}\right)=:\mu_{2}.

Now, let
\theta=\mu_{1}(1+\varepsilon/80)
, we conclude the statement. By (
1
), for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\theta=\mu_{1}(1+\varepsilon/80)<\mu_{2}(1-\varepsilon/80),

and by Chernoff bounds, if
\|p-q\|\leq 1
, then

{\mathrm{P}r}\left[\|f(p)-f(q)\|_{1}\geq(1+\varepsilon/80)\mu_{1}\right]\leq exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}d^{\prime}}{19200}\right)

and, if
\|p-q\|\geq 1+\varepsilon
, then

Pr\left[\|f(p)-f(q)\|_{1}<(1+\varepsilon/80)\mu_{1}\right]\leq{\mathrm{P}r}\left[[\|f(p)-f(q)\|_{1}\leq(1-\varepsilon/80)\mu_{2}\right]\leq exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}d^{\prime}}{19200}\right)

∎

We aim to build a data structure that supports approximate “stabbing queries”. Given a query point
q
we need to identify if there exists a pair of points in the input pointset that are (approximately)
\varepsilon
-stabbed by
q
.
Using the embedding provided by
\Cref
lemma:embedtohamming, we map points to keys in
\{0,1\}^{d^{\prime}}
, and we store them in a dictionary, where each bucket is assigned the list of points mapped to it. We set
d^{\prime}
slightly below
\log n
to balance the number of buckets
2^{d^{\prime}}
with the number of arbitrarily distorted distances from
q
, in a way that both remain sublinear. To answer a query, we search the dictionary for an approximate near neighbor, i.e., a point within distance
1+\varepsilon
, and an approximate far neighbor, i.e., a point at a distance of at least
1
. The following lemma indicates that a simple search over the buckets of a single dictionary is sufficient to guarantee that if there is a point within distance
1
, we will find a point within distance
1+\varepsilon
with constant probability, and if there is a point at distance at least
1+\varepsilon
, we will find a point at distance at least
1
with constant probability. We remark that for finding an approximate near neighbor, one could instead use the data structure of Andoni et al.
[
8
]
that provides near-linear space with comparable query time. However, we are not aware of a comparable result for computing an approximate far neighbor (which we also need and do). The complete pseudocode is diverted to
\Cref
app:pseudocode.

Lemma 10
.

Let
P=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
We can build a randomized data structure with preprocessing time and space in
\tilde{O}(dn)
and query time in
\tilde{O}(dn^{1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{19200(1+\varepsilon^{2})}})
, such that for any query
q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, it reports
\mathcal{I}
,
|\mathcal{I}|\leq 2
, as follows:

•

if
\exists x\in P:~\|x-q\|\leq 1
then
{\mathrm{P}r}[\exists i\in\mathcal{I}\text{ such that }\|q-p_{i}\|\leq 1+\varepsilon]\geq 0.95
,

•

if
\exists y\in P:~\|y-q\|\geq 1+\varepsilon
then
{\mathrm{P}r}[\exists j\in\mathcal{I}\text{ such that }\|q-p_{j}\|\geq 1]\geq 0.95
,

where the probabilities are taken over the randomness during preprocessing.

Proof.

Data Structure.

We sample an embedding
f
, as in
\Cref
lemma:embedtohamming, to map points of
P
to the Hamming metric of dimension
d^{\prime}=\frac{\log n}{1+\varepsilon^{2}}
. We store points of
P
in a dictionary
{\mathcal{D}}
; each point
p\in P
is associated with a key
f(p)\in\{0,1\}^{d^{\prime}}
. For each non-empty bucket in the dictionary, we store a linked list with all points falling in that bucket. The complete pseudocode of the preprocessing algorithm can be found in
\Cref
alg:preprocessing, in
\Cref
app:pseudocode. Using balanced binary trees to implement
{\mathcal{D}}
, the preprocessing algorithm runs in
\tilde{O}(dn)
time, and the data structure uses
\tilde{O}(dn)
space, as discussed in
\Cref
sec:model.

Query Algorithm.
When a query point
q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
arrives, we first compute
f(q)
, and we start looking at points in buckets of
{\mathcal{D}}
whose key is of Hamming distance at most
\theta
(as in
\Cref
lemma:embedtohamming) from
f(p)
. We stop when either we find a point whose pre-image is within distance
1+\varepsilon
from
q
, and we include it in
\mathcal{I}
, or when we have seen
100n^{1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{19200(1+\varepsilon^{2})}}
(irrelevant) points of
P
. Then, we start looking at points in buckets of
{\mathcal{D}}
whose key is of Hamming distance at least
\theta+\varepsilon d^{\prime}
(as in
\Cref
lemma:embedtohamming) from
f(p)
. We stop when either we find a point whose pre-image is at a distance of at least
1
from
q
, and we include it in
\mathcal{I}
, or when we have seen
100n^{1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{19200(1+\varepsilon^{2})}}
(irrelevant) points of
P
. The complete description of the algorithm can be found in
\Cref
alg:query, in
\Cref
app:pseudocode. The query time is upper bounded by the number of available buckets, i.e.,
O(n^{1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{(1+\varepsilon^{2})}})
, plus the number of irrelevant candidates checked through the above iteration, i.e.,
O(n^{1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{19200(1+\varepsilon^{2})}})
.

Correctness.

Let
N_{far}=|\{p\in P\mid\|p-q\|\geq 1+\varepsilon\text{ and }\|f(p)-f(q)\|_{1}\leq\theta+\varepsilon d^{\prime}\}|
be the number of points of
P
which are at a distance of at least
1+\varepsilon
from
q
but are embedded to within a distance
\theta+\varepsilon d^{\prime}
from
f(q)
. By
\Cref
lemma:embedtohamming,
{\mathbb{E}}[N_{far}]=n\cdot{\mathrm{P}r}[\|f(p)-f(q)\|<\theta+\varepsilon d^{\prime}\ |\ \|p-q\|\geq 1+\varepsilon]\leq n\cdot e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}d^{\prime}}{19200}}=n^{1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{19200(1+\varepsilon^{2})}}
, as
d^{\prime}=\frac{\log n}{1+\varepsilon^{2}}
. By Markov’s inequality
{\mathrm{P}r}[N_{far}\geq 99n^{1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{19200(1+\varepsilon^{2})}}]\leq 1/99
. Moreover, the probability that a point within distance
1
from
q
is embedded to a distance larger than
\theta
from
f(q)
is at most
n^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{19200(1+\varepsilon^{2})}}
. By a union bound, any of the above two events happens with probability at most
1/99+n^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{19200(1+\varepsilon^{2})}}\leq 2/99<0.05
.
Hence, if there is a point of
P
within distance
1
from
q
, the preprocessing will be successful for
q
, with probability at least
0.95
, in that the query algorithm will find a point within distance
1+\varepsilon
from
q
.
Similarly, the expected number
{\mathbb{E}}[N_{near}]
of points of
P
which are within distance
1
from
q
but are embedded to a distance of at least
\theta
from
f(q)
is at most
n^{1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{19200(1+\varepsilon^{2})}}
. By a union bound and Markov’s inequality, the probability that a far neighbor of
q
(a point at distance at least
1+\varepsilon
) is mapped correctly under
f
, and that
N_{near}\leq n^{1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{19200(1+\varepsilon^{2})}}
is at least
0.95
. ∎

Now using
\Cref
thm:stabds, we can prove the main result of this section, namely, a data structure that determines, with high probability, if a query point
\varepsilon
-stabs the preprocessed pointset, if it is near the entire pointset (the pointset is
covered
by
q
), or if it is far from the entire pointset (the pointset is
disjoint
from
q
). The data structure gives approximate answers in the following sense: if all points are within distance
1+\varepsilon
, and there is at least one point at a distance at most
1
, it can falsely report that the pointset is
\varepsilon
-stabbed. Similarly, if all points are at a distance larger than
1
, and there is at least one point at a distance of at least
1+\varepsilon
, it can falsely report that the pointset is
\varepsilon
-stabbed. If all points lie in the annulus
\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\mid\|x-q\|\in(1,1+\varepsilon)\}
then any answer is possible.
Additionally, the success probability of the preprocessing is for each query individually, i.e., it is not for all queries simultaneously. Copies of this structure will be used as parts of the final data structure for the approximate spherical range counting problem.

Theorem 11
.

Let
P\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a set of
n
points and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
We can build a randomized data structure such that for any query
q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, it reports as follows:

•

if
\exists x\in P:~\|x-q\|\leq 1
and
\exists y\in P:~\|y-q\|\geq 1+\varepsilon
then it returns “stabbed”,

•

if
\forall y\in P:~\|y-q\|\leq 1
then it returns “covered”

•

if
\forall x\in P:~\|x-q\|\geq 1+\varepsilon
then it returns “disjoint”,

•

if
\exists x\in P:~\|x-q\|\leq 1
and
\forall y\in P:~\|y-q\|<1+\varepsilon
then it can return “stabbed”, or “covered”, and

•

if
\exists x\in P:~\|x-q\|\geq 1+\varepsilon
and
\forall y\in P:~\|y-q\|>1
then it can return “stabbed”, or “disjoint”.

•

if
\forall x\in P:~\|x-q\|\in(1,1+\varepsilon)
then it can return “stabbed”, or “disjoint”, or “covered”.

For each query and for any constant
c>0
, the preprocessing of the data structure succeeds with probability
1-n^{-c}
,
has
\tilde{O}(dn)
preprocessing time and space and the query time is
\tilde{O}(dn^{1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{19200(1+\varepsilon^{2})}})
.

Proof.

We build
L=c\log n
independent data structures as in
\Cref
thm:stabds. For any query point
q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we query the data structures, and we report as follows. If we find a point
x
such that
\|x-q\|\leq 1+\varepsilon
and a point
y
such that
\|y-q\|\geq 1
we return “stabbed”. Otherwise, if all returned points are within distance
1+\varepsilon
, we return “covered”. Else, we return “disjoint”.

If there is a pair
x,y
in
P
such that
\|x-q\|\leq 1
and
\|y-q\|\geq 1+\varepsilon
then the probability that all
L
data structures fail to return a point
x^{\prime}
such that
\|x^{\prime}-q\|\leq 1+\varepsilon
or a point
y^{\prime}
such that
\|y^{\prime}-q\|\geq 1
is by union bound at most
2\cdot 0.05^{L}\leq n^{-c}
. Moreover, if all points of
P
are within distance
1
from
q
(resp. at a distance of at least
1+\varepsilon)
, then all returned points are within distance
1+\varepsilon
from
q
(resp. at a distance of at least
1
), implying that the query algorithm correctly identifies “covered” (resp. “disjoint”).
∎

4
Spanning Trees with low
\varepsilon
-stabbing number

The
\varepsilon
-stabbing number of a spanning tree (or of a set of edges in general) is the maximum number of edges of that tree (or that set) that are
\varepsilon
-stabbed by any query range. Chazelle and Welzl showed in
[
15
]
a close relationship between the stabbing number of a spanning tree (the stabbing number is the special case of
\varepsilon
-stabbing number where
\varepsilon=0
) and the maximum number of nodes visited, during a worst-case query, in a corresponding partition tree. Motivated by this, we wish to extend this notion to the
\varepsilon
-stabbing number and to achieve similar results, but without exponential dependency on
d
as we deal with the approximation scheme. In this section, we show that a spanning tree with sublinear
\varepsilon
-stabbing number exists, and can be computed in polynomial time.
Following
[
15
]
, we use MWU to find a spanning tree with small
\varepsilon
-stabbing number. We remark that there are other alternatives
[
17
,
22
]
in the literature for computing spanning trees with low stabbing numbers. While it is plausible that they can be modified to support our notion of
\varepsilon
-stabbing, they all share the same bottleneck, i.e., they depend at least linearly on the number of ranges.

Similarly, to
[
15
]
, we start by showing that for any set of ball queries, we can always find a pair of points that is not
\varepsilon
-stabbed many times.

Lemma 12
.

Let
P\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a set of
n
points and let
Q
be a
multiset consisting of
m
points from a universe set
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
There exists a pair of points in
P
which is not
\varepsilon
-stabbed
by more than
O\left(m\cdot\frac{d}{n^{\rho}}\right)

points of
Q
, where
\rho=\Theta(\varepsilon^{2}/\log(1/\varepsilon))
.

If
Q
is stored in a multiset sampling data structure as in
\Cref
lemma:multisampling, then for any constant
c
, we can find such a pair of points in
{\mathrm{poly}}(n,d,\frac{1}{\varepsilon})\cdot O(\log|U|)
time with probability
1-n^{-c}
.

Proof.

If
d\geq n^{\rho}
, then the statement trivially holds, since
|Q|=m
. Thus, we assume
d<n^{\rho}
.
For any two points
x,y
, let
S_{x,y}
be the set of points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
that
\varepsilon
-stab
x,y
. We define a range space
\mathcal{R}=({\mathbb{R}}^{d},\{{S}_{x,y}\mid x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},x\neq y\})
. Notice that for any point
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, determining if
p\in S_{x,y}
reduces to evaluating the signs of the following four polynomials of degree
2
in
d
variables:
\|p-x\|^{2}-1
,
\|p-x\|^{2}-(1+\varepsilon)^{2}
,
\|p-y\|^{2}-1
,
\|p-y\|^{2}-(1+\varepsilon)^{2}
. This implies (see, e.g.,
[
9
]
[Theorem 8.3]) that the VC dimension of
\mathcal{R}
is
O(d)
. Hence, by
\Cref
thmepsnet, for any
\delta<1
and constant
c>0
,
a random sample
N_{\delta}
of
O\left(\frac{d}{\delta}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)+\log n\right)\right)
points of
Q
is a
\delta
-net for
\mathcal{R}
with probability
1-n^{-c-1}
. Moreover, we can find such a
\delta
-net by randomly sampling from
Q
, using the assumed multiset sampling data structure of
\cref
lemma:multisampling, in
\tilde{O}(\frac{d}{\delta}\log n)\cdot O(\log|U|)
time.

By
\Cref
thm:terminals, there exists a terminal embedding
f
to dimension
d^{\prime}=O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log|N_{\delta}|)
such that
\forall p\in N_{\delta},q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
\|f(p)-f(q)\|\in(1\pm\varepsilon/20)\|p-q\|
. The time needed to compute
f(P)
is in
O(n)\cdot{\mathrm{poly}}(|N_{\delta}|)
for the probability of success to be at least
1-n^{-c-1}
.

Now let
x,y\in P
be
\varepsilon
-stabbed by some
q\in Q
, and further assume wlog that
\|x-q\|\leq 1
and
\|y-q\|\geq 1+\varepsilon
. We obtain
\|f(x)-f(q)\|\leq 1+\varepsilon/20
and

\|f(y)-f(q)\|\geq(1+\varepsilon)(1-\varepsilon/20)\geq 1+3\varepsilon/4
.
Hence,
f(x),f(y)
are
(\varepsilon/2,1+\varepsilon/20)
-stabbed by
f(q)
.
Moreover, by the triangle inequality,
\|f(x)-f(y)\|\geq\varepsilon/2
. Now consider the grid of side-length
\varepsilon/(4\sqrt{d^{\prime}})
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d^{\prime}}
and let
U_{B}
be the set of all grid cells that intersect with at least one ball of radius
1+\varepsilon
centered at a point of
f(N_{\delta})
. There are

n_{\varepsilon,d^{\prime}}=|N_{\delta}|\cdot O(1/\varepsilon)^{d^{\prime}}=|N_{\delta}|\cdot O(1/\varepsilon)^{O(\varepsilon^{-2}\cdot\log|N_{\delta}|)}=|N_{\delta}|^{O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log(1/\varepsilon))}

such cells, where
|N_{\delta}|=O\left(\frac{d}{\delta}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)+\log n\right)\right)
. Now let
\delta=\frac{d}{n^{\rho}}
where
\rho=\Theta(\varepsilon^{2}/\log(1/\varepsilon))
is chosen sufficiently small (in terms of the hidden constants) so that
|N_{\delta}|^{O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log(1/\varepsilon))}\leq\left(n^{\rho}\log n\right)^{O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log(1/\varepsilon))}<n-2
.
By the pigeonhole principle, there is either a pair of points of
P
mapped to the same cell of
U_{B}
, under
f
, or there is a pair of points of
P
that are not mapped to
U_{B}
. In either case, the two points are not
(\varepsilon/2,1+\varepsilon/20)
-stabbed by any point of
f(N_{\delta})
. To see this, notice that if the two points are in the same cell, then they are within distance
\varepsilon/4
from each other, contradicting the fact that they are
(\varepsilon/2,1+\varepsilon/20)
-stabbed (which would mean they are separated by a distance of at least
\varepsilon/2
). If they are not mapped in
U_{B}
then neither of them is within distance
1+\varepsilon/20
from any point of
f(N_{\delta})
, also implying that they cannot be
(\varepsilon/2,1+\varepsilon/20)
-stabbed by any point of
f(N_{\delta})
.
Since the two points are not
(\varepsilon/2,1+\varepsilon/20)
-stabbed by any point of
f(N_{\delta})
in the mapped space, their pre-images (under
f
) are not
\varepsilon
-stabbed by any point of
N_{\delta}
. Since
N_{\delta}
is a
\delta
-net, it must be that at most
\delta m
points of
Q

\varepsilon
-stab those two points.

We now focus on the running time needed to find a pair of points as above. By
\Cref
thm:terminals, we can map points under
f
in
{\mathrm{poly}}(n)
time.
To compute the two points, we essentially use the bucketing method (see, e.g.,
[
20
]
) for balls centered at
f(N_{\delta})
.
We first store all cells of
U_{B}
in a dictionary data structure. Then, for each point
x\in f(P)
, we query the data structure to see if its cell
C_{x}
is stored, i.e.,
C_{x}\in U_{B}
. If
C_{x}\in U_{B}
then we store
x
in a linked list in the bucket of
C_{x}
. We can stop and output any two points stored in the same linked list, or any two points whose cells do not belong to
U_{B}
. Iterating over all points of
f(P)
costs
\tilde{O}(dn)
assuming that the dictionary is implemented using balanced binary trees. The overall probability of success follows from a union bound over failure events of the random sampling for computing
N_{\delta}
and the failure event of computing the terminal embedding
f
.
∎

Knowing that a good pair always exists, and that we can find it, we repeat the process
n/2
times, in order to build a good forest, in the sense that it has a small
\varepsilon
-stabbing number. To do that, we use the MWU technique introduced in the context of trees with low stabbing number in
[
15
]
. The proof is very similar to
[
15
]
.

Lemma 13
.

Let
P\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a set of
n
points and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
There is a forest of trees on
P
with at least
n/2

edges, such that every point of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\gamma}^{d}

\varepsilon
-stabs
\tilde{O}(dn^{1-\rho})
edges, where
\gamma=\Theta(\varepsilon/\sqrt{d})
,
\rho=\Theta(\varepsilon^{2}/\log(1/\varepsilon))
.
For any
c>0
, we can compute such a forest in
{\mathrm{poly}}(n,d,\frac{1}{\varepsilon}))+\tilde{O}(n)\cdot O(1/\varepsilon)^{d}
time, with probability of success
1-n^{-c}
.

Proof.

We initiate our discussion with a high-level description of our algorithm, which is sufficient to show that a forest with the desired properties exists. We then discuss implementation details and bound the running time of the algorithm.

Let
Q_{0}\leftarrow\bigcup_{p\in P}B(p,(1+\varepsilon))\cap{\mathcal{G}}_{\gamma}^{d}
. Notice that points of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\gamma}^{d}
not in
\bigcup_{p\in P}B(p,(1+\varepsilon))
do not
\varepsilon
-stab any pair of points of
P
. Using
\Cref
lemma:lightedge, we pick as first edge the edge realized by a pair of points
x,y
of

To implement the above scheme, we first store

Now for each

5
Spherical Range Counting

In this section, we show how to construct a partition tree for the approximate spherical range counting problem with sublinear query time. We first show how to get a partition tree with a sublinear visiting number given a spanning tree with a sublinear
\varepsilon
-stabbing number, by extending the procedure in
[
15
]
to accommodate the approximation scheme. Subsequently, we incorporate the data structures of
\Cref
section:stabbing in the inner nodes of the partition tree to get sublinear worst-case guarantees.

We start by proving that having a spanning tree with low
\varepsilon
-stabbing number can give us a spanning path with low
\varepsilon
-stabbing number, which we then use to construct a partition tree with low visiting number.
\Cref
lem:treepath,lem:pathtree are adapted from
[
15
]
for
\varepsilon
-stabbing numbers, and the bounds become dependent on the ambiguity zone of the query.

Lemma 15
.

Given a spanning tree
T
on a pointset
P\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
we can build a spanning path
{\mathcal{P}}
on
P
, in
{O}(dn)
time, such that for any point
q
,
\sigma(q,{\mathcal{P}})\leq 2\sigma(q,T)+2t_{q}
, where
t_{q}=|\left(B(q,1+\varepsilon)\setminus B(q,1)\right)\cap P|
.

Proof.

We get the spanning path by running a depth-first traversal of
T
. Let an edge
e
of the traversal be
\varepsilon
-stabbed by a query
q
. If
e\in T
we are done, else
e
creates a cycle with edges of
T
. We have 2 cases regarding the
\varepsilon
-stabbing number of the cycle:

•

The number is at least 2 (
q

\varepsilon
-stabs at least one edge of the tree), in which case we can charge an edge of the tree which is
\varepsilon
-stabbed.

•

The number is 1 (
q
only
\varepsilon
-stabs the edge that closes the cycle). In order for this to be true, there must be a number of points of the cycle that are on the annulus centered at
q
, i.e., be at distance between 1 and
1+\varepsilon
from
q
.

It follows from the first case that because of the depth-first traversal, we can charge each edge at most twice. Additionally, by the same argument, a node in the annulus of
q
is charged at most twice. This is because in a depth-first order, every time the tree crosses the annulus, the path constructed from the order crosses the annulus at most twice (once going down the branch, and once when it retracts). We conclude that
\sigma(q,{\mathcal{P}})\leq 2\sigma(q,T)+2t_{q}
. The running time is
O(dn)
since we only run a depth-first search on a tree.
∎

Lemma 16
.

Given a spanning path
{\mathcal{P}}

on a pointset
P\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
we can build a binary partition tree
{\mathcal{T}}
on
P
, in
(dn\log n)
time, such that for any point
q
,
we have

\zeta(q,{\mathcal{T}})\leq 2[\sigma(q,{\mathcal{P}})+t_{q}]\cdot\log n+1
, where
t_{q}=|\left(B(q,1+\varepsilon)\setminus B(q,1)\right)\cap P|
.

Proof.

We build a complete binary tree, and assign the points along the path to the leaves from left to right sequentially. This takes
O(dn\log n)
time.

Let
v
be an inner node.
For the two children nodes of
v
to be visited by
q
, one of the following must be true:

•

the subset of points
P_{v}
assigned to the leaves of the subtree rooted at
v
is
\varepsilon
-stabbed by
q

•

there exists a
p\in P_{v}
such that
p\in B(q,1+\varepsilon)\setminus B(q,1)
.

As there are at most
\sigma(q,{\mathcal{P}})
edges that are
\varepsilon
-stabbed by
q
and at most
t_{q}
points that can be on the annulus of
q
, then there are at most
[\sigma(q,{\mathcal{P}})+t_{q}]\cdot\log n
inner nodes that are visited by
q
. When an inner node is
\varepsilon
-visited, we continue our search to both children. These are all the nodes that can be visited, plus the root. Therefore, the number of nodes visited for a query point
q
can be at most
2[\sigma(q,{\mathcal{P}})+t_{q}]\cdot\log n+1
.
∎

We combine these results to get a bound on the visiting number of a partition tree.

Corollary 17
.

Let
P\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a set of
n
points.
For any
c>0
, we can build a binary partition tree
{\mathcal{T}}
on
P
, in
{\mathrm{poly}}(n,d,\frac{1}{\varepsilon})+\tilde{O}(n)\cdot O(1/\varepsilon)^{d}
time such that with probability
1-1/n^{c}
every
q\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\gamma}^{d}
, with
\gamma=\varepsilon/\sqrt{d}
, visits
\zeta(q,{\mathcal{T}})=\tilde{O}(dn^{1-\rho}+t_{q})
nodes, where
t_{q}=|\left(B(q,1+\varepsilon)\setminus B(q,1)\right)\cap P|
and
\rho=\Theta(\varepsilon^{2}/\log(1/\varepsilon))
.

Proof.

Using
\Cref
thm:treeexists,
for any
c>0
, we can compute a spanning tree
T
in
{\mathrm{poly}}(n,d,\frac{1}{\varepsilon})+\tilde{O}(n)\cdot O(1/\varepsilon)^{d}
time, such that with probability of success

∎

Finally, we present the main contribution of our paper, which states that for the approximate spherical range counting problem, we can build a data structure in polynomial time that supports sublinear queries (if

on the input with error parameter

6
Data-driven Range Searching

As we saw in the previous section, we can construct the partition tree in polynomial time. However, the exponent depends on
1/\varepsilon
, which we would like to avoid. In this section, we present an algorithm inspired by learning theory that constructs a partition tree with near-optimal visiting number in expectation, over the distribution of queries. Specifically, the algorithm will have access to a number
m
of sample queries, and will construct a partition tree that is optimal for that sample - thus the algorithm is data-driven. Showing that uniform convergence is achieved, we prove that this partition tree is nearly optimal, in that its expected visiting number is nearly minimal.

In the next lemma, we show that using only a near-linear number of queries we can achieve uniform convergence for the expected
\varepsilon
-stabbing number, i.e., for any spanning tree, the estimation of the
\varepsilon
-stabbing number using only these queries is close to its real expected
\varepsilon
-stabbing number.

Lemma 19
.

Let
P
be a set of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}
be a ball query distribution having as support a countably infinite set
{\mathcal{R}}
.
It suffices to sample
m\in O(n\cdot(d\log n+\log(1/\delta)))
queries
S
independently from
{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}
, so that with probability at least
1-\delta
,
for any spanning tree
{T}
of
P
,
m^{-1}\cdot\sum_{q\in S}\sigma(q,{T})
is in

\left[\frac{5}{8}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{q\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}}\left[\sigma(q,{T})\right]-\frac{3}{8},\frac{11}{8}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{q\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}}\left[\sigma(q,{T})\right]+\frac{3}{8}\right]
.

Proof.

We apply
\Cref
theo:etaepsilonapproxfunctionsrelative with
\eta=1/n
on the set of functions
{\mathcal{F}}
defined by the pairs of points
P\times P
and the domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}
corresponding to the set of ball queries
{\mathcal{R}}
. For each

, we have a function
f_{ab}\in{\mathcal{F}}
such that for any query centered at
q
:
f_{ab}(q)=1\iff

q

\varepsilon
-stabs
\{a,b\}
. It remains to bound the VC dimension of the range space
({\mathcal{R}},{\mathcal{F}})

{\mathcal{F}}^{-}=\{f_{a}^{-}\mid a\in P\}
(
{\mathcal{F}}^{+}=\{f_{a}^{+}\mid a\in P\}

f_{a}^{-}(q)=1\iff\|a-q\|\leq 1-\varepsilon
(
f_{a}^{+}(q)=1\iff\|a-q\|\leq 1+\varepsilon
).
Notice that
f_{ab}(q)=1\iff(f_{a}^{-}(q)=1\land f_{b}^{+}(q)=0)\lor(f_{a}^{+}(q)=0\land f_{b}^{-}(q)=1)
.
The dual VC dimension of
(P,{\mathcal{R}})
is
d+1
(
[
9
]
), meaning that the VC dimension of
({\mathcal{R}},\{f_{a}^{-}\mid a\in P\})
and
({\mathcal{R}},\{f_{a}^{+}\mid a\in P\})
is also
d+1
, implying that the VC dimension of
({\mathcal{R}},{\mathcal{F}})
is
O(d)
(since each function of
{\mathcal{F}}
is defined as
O(1)
and/or operations on functions of
{\mathcal{F}}^{-}
and
{\mathcal{F}}^{+}
). Let
S=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
be as in
\Cref
theo:etaepsilonapproxfunctionsrelative, i.e., each

is a query sampled independently at random from
{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}
, and

By
\Cref
theo:etaepsilonapproxfunctionsrelative, with probability at least
1-\delta
over the choice of
S
, for all “light” pairs of points, i.e., pairs that are
\varepsilon
-stabbed with probability at most
1/n

with an additive error of

whereas for all “heavy” pairs of points, i.e., those that are
\varepsilon
-stabbed with probability more than
1/n
, we can estimate the probability of getting
\varepsilon
-stabbed with a multiplicative error of
1\pm 3/8
.
Now let
{T}
be any spanning tree of
P
and let
E({T})
be the set of its edges. Let

be the set of light edges of
{T}
, i.e., edges

for which the probability that
\{a,b\}
is
\varepsilon
-stabbed is at most
1/n
and let
E_{h}\subseteq E({T})
be the set of heavy edges of
{T}
, i.e., edges
(a,b)\in E({T})
for which the probability that
\{a,b\}
is
\varepsilon
-stabbed is more than
1/n
. Let

\tilde{\sigma}(S,{T})=m^{-1}\cdot{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma(x_{i},{T})}
.
We have,

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}(S,{T})=\frac{1}{m}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma(x_{i},{T})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{e\in E({T})}\sigma(x_{i},e)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{e\in E_{\ell}}\sigma(x_{i},e)+\sum_{e\in E_{h}}\sigma(e,x_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(a,b)\in E_{\ell}}\mu_{f_{ab}}(S)+\sum_{(a,b)\in E_{h}}\mu_{f_{ab}}(S)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{5}{8}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{q}\left[\sigma(q,{T})\right]-\frac{3}{8}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}(S,{T})=\frac{1}{m}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{e\in E({T})}\sigma(x_{i},e)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{(a,b)\in E_{\ell}}\left({\mathbb{E}}_{q}[f_{ab}(q)]+\frac{3}{8n}\right)+\sum_{(a,b)\in E_{h}}\frac{11}{8}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{q}[f_{ab}(q)]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{11}{8}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{q\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}}\left[\sigma(q,{T})\right]+\frac{3}{8}.

Hence, for any spanning tree

of
P
,

is in

\left[\frac{5}{8}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{q\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}}\left[\sigma(q,{T})\right]-\frac{3}{8},\frac{11}{8}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{q\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}}\left[\sigma(q,{T})\right]+\frac{3}{8}\right].

∎

\tilde{{\mathcal{T}}}
is in

O(n^{3}(d\log n+\log(1/\delta))
.

Proof.

By
\Cref
lemma:spanningtreespreserved, with probability at least
1-\delta
,
for any spanning tree
{T}
of
P
,
m^{-1}\cdot\sum_{q\in S}\sigma(q,{T})
is in

\left[\frac{5}{8}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{q\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}}\left[\sigma(q,{T})\right]-\frac{3}{8},\frac{11}{8}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{q\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}}\left[\sigma(q,{T})\right]+\frac{3}{8}\right].

Now, let
E({T})
denote the edges of spanning tree
{T}
and
\tilde{{T}}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{T}\{m^{-1}\cdot\sum_{q\in S}\sigma(q,{T})\}
. Notice that

\sum_{q\in S}\sigma(q,{T})=\sum_{q\in S}\sum_{e\in E({T})}\sigma(q,\{e\})=\sum_{e\in E({T})}\sum_{q\in S}\sigma(q,\{e\})
for any spanning tree
{T}
.
Hence, we can compute
\tilde{{T}}
, by first constructing a complete weighted graph on
P
where we assign weight
\sum_{q\in S}\sigma(q,\{e\})
to each edge
e\in{{P}\choose{2}}
, and then computing the minimum spanning tree on that graph, using Kruskal’s algorithm. The running time of this step is
O(n^{2}m)
, which corresponds to the step of building the graph.

Let
{T}^{\ast}
be the spanning tree that minimizes
{\mathbb{E}}_{q\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}}[\sigma(q,{T})]

over all spanning trees
{T}
of
P
.
By
\Cref
lem:ubstabbing we get
{\mathbb{E}}_{q\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}}[\sigma(q,{T}^{\ast})]\leq\min_{{\mathcal{T}}}{\mathbb{E}}_{q\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}}[\zeta(q,{\mathcal{T}})]
and by
\Cref
lemma:spanningtreespreserved we get:

\Omega(1)\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{q\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}}[\sigma(q,\tilde{{T}})]\leq m^{-1}\sum_{q\in S}\sigma(q,\tilde{{T}})\leq m^{-1}\sum_{q\in S}\sigma(q,{{T}}^{\ast})\leq O(1)\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{q\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}}[\sigma(q,{{T}}^{\ast})]

Hence
{\mathbb{E}}_{q\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}}[\sigma(q,\tilde{{T}})]\subseteq O\left(\min_{{\mathcal{T}}}{\mathbb{E}}_{q\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}}[\zeta(q,{\mathcal{T}})]\right)
.
Given
\tilde{{T}}
, by
\Cref
lem:treepath,lem:pathtree we can construct a binary partition tree
\tilde{{\mathcal{T}}}
of
P
such that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{q\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}}[\zeta(q,\tilde{{\mathcal{T}}})]

\displaystyle=O\left(\left[{\mathbb{E}}_{q\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}}[\sigma(q,\tilde{{T}})]+t_{q}\right]\cdot\log n\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\left[\min_{{\mathcal{T}}}{\mathbb{E}}_{q\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{Q}}[\zeta(q,{\mathcal{T}})]+t_{q}\right]\cdot\log n\right)

in
O(n)
time. Thus, the overall running time is
O(n^{2}m)\subseteq O(n^{3}(d\log n+\log(1/\delta))
.
∎

A.2
Proof of
\Cref
lemma:multisampling

See
6

Proof.

We preprocess a balanced binary tree on values
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}
where each internal node
v
also stores the sum
s_{v}
of all leaves of the subtree rooted at
v
. The time needed to compute the tree is
O(n\log n)
, which also bounds its space usage.

To sample a point from
P
, we sample uniformly at random an integer
z\in[\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}]
and we search the binary tree for the left-most leaf with value, say,
\gamma_{j}
such that
\sum_{i\leq j}\gamma_{i}\geq z
. We return
p_{j}
. The time needed to traverse the tree is
O(\log n)
.

To update the weight of a point
p_{i}\in P
, it suffices to update the sums stored in the ancestors of the leaf storing
\gamma_{i}
. Assuming that
\gamma_{i}
is being replaced by
\gamma_{i}^{\prime}
, we traverse the
O(\log n)
ancestors, and we add
\gamma_{i}^{\prime}-\gamma_{i}
in each one of them. This requires
O(\log n)
time in total.
∎

Proof of

Proof.

We assign an arbitrary left-to-right order among the children of every internal node of
{\mathcal{T}}
, and let
l_{1},\dots,l_{n}
denote the leaves of
{\mathcal{T}}
from left to right. Next, form the spanning path
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
of
P
, where
\{x_{i}\}
is the node-set of
l_{i}
. If the edge
\{x_{i},x_{i+1}\}
is
\varepsilon
-stabbed by a query
q
, we charge this event to the unique child of the nearest common ancestor
v
of
l_{i}
, and
l_{i+1}
that is also an ancestor of
l_{i}
(or
l_{i}
itself). Since the pointset corresponding to
v
is necessarily
\varepsilon
-stabbed by
q
, the child which takes the charge is visited. Furthermore, such a node cannot be charged twice.
∎
