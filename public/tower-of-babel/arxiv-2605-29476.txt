Comparative Evaluation of Machine Translation Systems on Images with Text
Blai Puchol, Sergio Gómez González, Miguel Domingo, Francisco Casacuberta

Abstract
This work presents a comparative evaluation of machine translation systems applied to images containing textual information, a task that lies at the intersection of computer vision and natural language processing. The study compares three main paradigms: modular pipelines that separate text detection, recognition, and translation; multi-modal large language models (MLLMs) capable of processing both image and text jointly; and an end-to-end model, Translatotron-V, which directly generates translated images. The modular systems employ state-of-the-art OCR (docTR) combined with multilingual LLMs such as Llama and EuroLLM, while the evaluated MLLMs include different configurations of Gemini 2.5. Experiments were conducted on parallel multilingual datasets covering multiple language pairs, with evaluation based on BLEU, chrF, and TER metrics. The results show that modular pipelines outperform the end-to-end approach, while MLLMs achieve the best overall performance, demonstrating superior flexibility and contextual understanding. These findings underscore the effectiveness of multi-modal reasoning for image-to-text translation and provide a solid foundation for future research on integrating visual understanding and language generation in multilingual settings.



1
Introduction

Machine translation was born as an automated way to translate text from one language to another, allowing more readers to understand the information contained in the original text. However, most of the information that humans produce and receive is visual; yet it contains text in some language. As a result, people who do not speak the language cannot understand most of the content in these images. In order to ensure the accessibility of the visually codified text, text-image machine translation
18
has emerged. This research field includes solutions dedicated to obtaining textual translations of images containing embedded text.

Since text-image machine translation models receive an image and produce text, the task is at the intersection between computer vision and natural language processing. Specifically, text-image machine translation can be expressed as a combination of optical character recognition and machine translation. From an optical character recognition point of view, text-image machine translation solutions should locate and extract the text embedded in the images. However, from an machine translation perspective, they must adequately translate the recognized text into the target language. Sometimes, this division is more evident when different models are used in a cascade-model
33
or end-to-end
25
fashion.

In this work, we propose a modular pipeline that allows the use of different models to perform sequentially optical character recognition and machine translation. Furthermore, we have selected a dataset
17
that was previously used in other text-image machine translation works. Thus, we tested our pipeline with different models to find the best combination. Moreover, we compared it with an end-to-end model
17
and some multimodal large language model to assure its potential. We found that our pipeline is competitive with selected multimodal large language models, and surpasses the performance of the end-to-end model.

The rest of this paper is structured as follows: first, we present an overview of the state of the art with current tendencies in the text-image machine translation field, and the best performing models. After that, we describe the methodology that we have followed in our experiments in Section
3
. There, we introduce our modular pipeline (Section
3.2
), an end-to-end system, and some multimodal large language models (Section
3.3
). Then, in Section
4
, we present the evaluation framework that we have followed. Afterward, in Section
5
, we review and study the results of our experimental session. Finally, the article ends with our conclusions and future steps in Section
7
.

2
Related Work

Image-based machine translation is considered a sub-field of multimodal machine translation dedicated to the translation of textual content embedded in the media
8
;
11
. Additionally, image-based machine translation can be divided into two sub-tasks: text-image machine translation and in-image machine translation. The objective of the first is to translate the textual content of an image, obtaining a pure textual output
19
;
24
. Meanwhile, in-image machine translation aims to substitute the text in the image for its translation into another language
41
;
40
. Thus, the result is a new image with the same visual components but a rendered translation of the text instead of the original.

To address the task of text-image machine translation, multiple proposals have been made, ranging from cascade-based modular pipelines
33
to end-to-end models
39
;
18
;
22
;
17
. The former often divides the task into optical character recognition, followed by machine translation on the recognized text. They tend to suffer from error propagation
39
, but can exploit highly optimized models trained on vast amounts of data. In contrast, end-to-end models face the task as a whole solid challenge to avoid error propagation. They are highly specialized models that are trained only for text-image machine translation with the available data.

The common approach to cascade pipelines starts with detecting and recognizing text in images. While vanilla optical character recognition models focus primarily on scanned documents,
21
;
5
text-image machine translation images usually do not follow this pattern. Real world applications of text-image machine translation require detecting text in natural scenes. This is a more challenging task, since images usually contain complex backgrounds, variable lighting, distortions, arbitrary orientations, and diverse fonts. To face this type of challenge, the availability of data is vital; therefore, datasets were created incorporating these special conditions
12
;
43
;
44
. Furthermore, challenges such as the one organized at
ICDAR 2015

15
promote performance improvement in natural-scene optical character recognition models.

After recognizing text in images, cascade approaches tend to use an machine translation model to obtain the final output
33
. Machine translation has a long history with a large availability of models, both direction-specific and multilingual
13
. In addition, conferences such as
WMT

3
;
10
;
45
promote research on several open challenges of machine translation, such as quality estimation
38
. Many datasets have been created to address various specific machine translation tasks
16
;
42
, with increasing difficulty. Lately, the use of large language model has also been introduced in machine translation, even with the support of institutions such as the European Union
26
.

The other common approach to text-image machine translation is to train an end-to-end model. This branch is closely related to in-image machine translation since text-image machine translation is usually used as an auxiliary task to obtain an in-image machine translation model
17
. Attempting to directly attack the problem at the pixel level
39
has proven to be a poor approach
18
. Therefore, other approaches have tried to involve a codebook in their models architecture
18
;
17
. Other models produce a formatted output to render the image in case of need
22
.

Usually, given the scarcity of large-scale high-quality parallel datasets
20
, end-to-end models are trained with synthetic data
17
. To our knowledge, the only manually curated datasets that exist to date are
OCRMT30k

18
,
DoTA

22
and
ECOIT

48
. These datasets are valuable; however, they are small in scale and restricted in language coverage.

The revolution of large language models has also affected text-image machine translation tasks, since they are often used as part of pipelines. Tools such as
Anytrans

33
include these models as translators. They can help mitigate optical character recognition errors through contextual understanding
46
.

3
Methodology

This section describes the experimental framework used to evaluate image-based machine translation systems. We compare modular pipelines that separate optical character recognition from text translation with end-to-end systems. All models are evaluated on a multilingual dataset of synthetic images that contain rendered text, ensuring consistent conditions across architectures. The methodology covers the dataset, the modular pipeline, the evaluated models, and the prompting strategies used for each large language model and multimodal large language model.

512\times 512

3.2
Modular pipeline

The modular pipeline processes input images by first extracting text through optical character recognition and then translating it into the target language.

3.2.1
OCR Stage

Two open-source optical character recognition systems were considered for the text extraction stage:
EasyOCR
1
1

1

https://github.com/JaidedAI/EasyOCR
and
docTR
2
2

2

https://github.com/mindee/doctr

27
.

EasyOCR
employs a deep learning pipeline combining a Character Region Awareness for Text Detection
1
model for detection with a convolutional recurrent neural network
35
for recognition. Although it supports more than 80 languages and offers high inference speed, its robustness can be degraded under significant text distortion.

docTR
provides a highly flexible framework built on TensorFlow and PyTorch, offering a choice of different detection and recognition models. It is particularly known for its high accuracy and strong performance in handling rotated or multi-line text.

After a preliminary accuracy comparison
(Section
5.1
)
,
docTR
was selected for all the main experiments due to its superior performance. Leveraging its modularity, we set up a specific two-stage pipeline: for the text detection stage, we employed the Faster Arbitrarily-Shaped Text Detector
4
, and for the text recognition stage, we chose a convolutional neural network. This recognition model uses a VGG16-BN backbone, consisting of a VGG16
36
that adds batch normalization layers, which act as a powerful visual feature extractor for cropped text images. These features are then sequentially fed into the recurrent neural network
14
, which decodes them into the final character sequence.

3.2.2
Translation Stage

At this stage, the text extracted in the previous phase is fed into the selected machine translation models. Our methodology distinguishes between two fundamentally different approaches to this task, based on the model’s architecture.

For standard multilingual neural machine translation systems, such as
M2M100-1.2B

9
, the process is direct. These models are specialized exclusively for translation, and the task is conditioned by providing predefined source and target language tags to the input
9
.

In contrast, large language models are general-purpose instruction-following models. They are not specialized for a single task and must be guided to perform the translation. This is achieved by formatting the input via natural-language instructions, i.e., a
prompt
. These prompts, further detailed in Section
3.4
, explicitly request only the translated text to ensure a clean, comparable output.

This modular setup allows separate evaluation of optical character recognition and translation quality using dedicated metrics.

3.3
Models

We considered four main categories of models, selected to provide a clear contrast in architecture, specialization and capability.

As a traditional NMT
28
;
2
, we selected
M2M100-1.2B

9
. This model employs a standard Transformer encoder-decoder architecture and is known for its strong cross-lingual translation performance.

For the
LLM-based modular pipeline
, we employed general-purpose, decoder-only models. This included instruction-tuned models from Meta’s Llama family

Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct
,
Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct
, and
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
.We also included
EuroLLM-1.7B
and
EuroLLM-9B

For the

Gemini-2.5

6

flash-lite
,

. These models are native multi-modal and utilize a sparse Mixture-of-Experts architecture for efficient scaling. Thus, this architecture functions as an end-to-end system, processing the raw image and text prompt in a single step, thus avoiding the separate optical character recognition stage entirely.

Finally, we compared our results with an
end-to-end image-to-image model
,
Translatotron-V

17
, using accuracy values obtained through private communication with the authors. This provides a second, more specialized comparison for end-to-end in-image machine translation performance, although the results were limited to two language pairs due to the inaccessibility of a public checkpoint.

3.4
Prompting Strategies

Prompt design was a critical step in obtaining consistent and clean translations of large language models and multimodal large language models. As demonstrated in previous work
23
, well-structured prompts significantly improve model behavior in translation tasks by reducing irrelevant or reformulated outputs. Our primary goal was to engineer prompts that enforced a minimal response, thereby minimizing the need for post-processing and ensuring a fair, metric-based comparison across model families.

The required prompt structure varied depending on each model’s architecture. For EuroLLM, a simple format that specifies the source language and target language tags was sufficient to obtain clean translations. In contrast, LLaMA models required more explicit instructions to suppress unnecessary commentary. We experimented with both direct text commands (e.g., “Respond only with the translation”) and structured JSON outputs to facilitate automated parsing. For multi-modal Gemini models, prompts were designed to integrate both optical character recognition and translation into a single query, instructing the model to extract the source text from the image and then to return only the final translation.

4
Experimental Session

This section describes the technical framework used to perform our evaluation. We first detail the implementation, including the software libraries and hardware used to run the different model families. We then define the specific evaluation metrics used to assess the performance of both optical character recognition and translation stages.

4.1
Implementation Details

Our experiments were conducted using two primary methods, depending on model accessibility. All open-weight models (M2M100, Llama, and EuroLLM) were run locally using the Hugging Face
transformers
library
47
. These tests were performed on servers equipped with
NVIDIA RTX 4090
and
NVIDIA Quadro RTX 8000
GPUs.

For the closed-source Gemini family, the models were evaluated using the official Google GenAI API, powered by Google Cloud.

4.2
Evaluation Metrics

We assessed both the OCR and translation stages using complementary metrics.
For OCR, we report Character Error Rate and Word Error Rate
29
, which quantify transcription errors at the character and word levels, respectively. Lower values indicate better recognition accuracy.

For translation quality, we used three standard metrics widely adopted in machine translation research:

•

Bilingual evaluation understudy
30
: Measures
n
-gram overlap between model output and reference translations.

•

Character n-gram F-score
31
: Computes an F-score based on character
n
-gram precision and recall, capturing fine-grained similarity.

•

Translation error rate
37
: Calculates the normalized number of edits required to match the reference translation.

For consistency, machine translation metrics were computed using
sacreBLEU

32
. Additionally, we applied approximate randomization testing
34
—with
10,000
repetitions and using a
p
-value of
0.05
—to determine whether two systems presented statistically significance.

In summary, comparative analysis across all four translation directions reveals a consistent performance hierarchy: traditional neural machine translation systems establish reliable baselines; large-scale LLMs, especially EuroLLM, provide significant improvements; and multi-modal models, particularly Gemini-2.5-pro, deliver the highest translation quality overall. These findings underline the growing advantage of multi-modal architectures that jointly process visual and linguistic information. Nevertheless, the lack of complete results for Translatotron-V highlights ongoing challenges and the need for more accessible end-to-end image-to-image translation benchmarks.

6
Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the evaluation is based on a synthetic dataset where text is rendered under controlled conditions. Although this setup enables clean comparisons across systems, it can introduce biases and may overestimate performance relative to real-world scenarios. In practice, text in natural images is often affected by motion blur, occlusion, perspective distortion, complex backgrounds, and uncontrolled lighting. Therefore, evaluating with in-the-wild images would provide a more realistic estimate of system robustness.

Second, language coverage remains limited to a small set of relatively high-resource European language pairs. The conclusions may not transfer directly to typologically distant languages, non-Latin scripts, or genuinely low-resource settings where OCR and MT errors can compound differently.

Third, the analysis relies primarily on automatic metrics (BLEU, chrF, and TER). Although standard in MT evaluation, these metrics do not fully capture adequacy, faithfulness to visual context, or user-perceived quality. We could also use other automated human-based metrics to complement the current results and provide a more complete assessment.

Finally, some comparisons are constrained by model availability and reproducibility factors. For example, Translatotron-V results were only partially available, and closed-source API-based models may change over time due to backend updates. As a result, exact replication of all reported scores may be difficult.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

Experimental results demonstrate that large language models and multimodal large language models are highly effective in translating images containing text. Among the approaches assessed, modular pipelines that combine OCR and LLM-based translation achieved superior performance compared to the end-to-end system, Translatotron-V. This suggests that decomposing the process into specialized stages for detection, recognition, and translation leads to more accurate and reliable outputs.

Meanwhile, multimodal large language models showed strong potential by jointly processing visual and textual information, achieving performance that rivals or exceeds traditional OCR-based pipelines. Their ability to integrate semantic understanding and contextual reasoning enables more flexible and coherent translations, particularly in visually complex scenarios.

Future work will focus on expanding the evaluation to a broader range of models and languages, including low-resource pairs, and exploring hybrid architectures that integrate the strengths of modular and end-to-end approaches. Additionally, the development of larger and more diverse multilingual image datasets would allow for more comprehensive analysis of multi-modal translation performance across domains.
