Evaluation of Salter and Pemberton osteotomies in developmental dysplasia of the hip: the role of the congruity index as a prognostic factor
Authors: Jiahao Gao, Yang Ma, Menglong Li, Fuwen Zheng, Chenyu Wang, Jinshuo Tang, Jinrui Zhang, Jianlin Zuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Salter osteotomy (SO) and the Pemberton osteotomy (PO) are common surgical methods for treating paediatric developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH). Although they each have their own advantages, complications resulting from open surgeries are still relatively common. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical outcomes of SO and PO in the treatment of DDH and to identify prognostic factors associated with postoperative outcomes.MethodsBetween April 2011 and May 2018, we retrospectively reviewed 72 DDH patients who underwent open reduction combined with pelvic osteotomy (SO/PO) at our hospital, and divided them into two groups based on the different surgical procedures: SO and PO. The minimum follow-up duration was 5 years. Subsequently, general patient information, along with clinical and radiographic results, were collected for comparative analysis. We introduced the congruity index, defined as the percentage of femoral head coverage by the acetabulum, to evaluate the prognosis of DDH. The Pearson’s chi-square test and Student t tests were used for comparisons of outcomes between the groups. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to analyze factors associated with avascular necrosis (AVN) and with the poorer McKay classification.ResultsA total of 65 patients completed the final follow-up. Laterality, duration of follow-up, and preoperative CEA differed significantly between the groups. Patients in the SO group had higher postoperative congruity index than those in the PO group (P < 0.001). According to the Severin and McKay classifications, satisfactory radiographic outcomes (Severin class I or II) were achieved in 88.9% of patients in the SO group and 76.9% of patients in the PO group. Satisfactory clinical outcomes (McKay class I or II) were achieved in 92.6% of patients in the SO group and 84.6% of patients in the PO group. Congruity index was risk factor for AVN (P = 0.007), and the lower congruity index was associated with a poorer McKay classification (P = 0.007).ConclusionBoth SO and PO are effective in treating paediatric DDH, with favourable radiographic performance and clinical outcomes. Introduction

Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip (DDH) is a common hip joint abnormality in infants [1, 2], characterised by insufficient acetabular coverage on the anterolateral side [3]. Early identification of developmental dysplasia of the hip allows effective management with minimally invasive modalities, mainly including the Pavlik harness (< 6 months) [4] and closed reduction (6–18 months) [5], and prevents complications such as avascular necrosis (AVN) and the need for further surgery [6, 7]. Conversely, diagnosis beyond this early developmental period or delays management often necessitate open hip reduction, with or without pelvic osteotomy.

Salter osteotomy (SO) [8] and Pemberton osteotomies (PO) [9] are the most common surgical methods used currently, both are widely recognized for their effectiveness in enhancing anterolateral acetabular coverage [10, 11]. SO, a redirectional procedure indicated for children over 18 months old, changes the direction of the acetabulum using the pubic symphysis as the fulcrum, often requiring subsequent surgeries for hardware removal [11–13]. PO, a reshaping procedure recommended for children over 12 months old, uses the Y-type cartilage around the acetabulum as the rotation fulcrum, offering better anterior coverage [12]. However, it is reported that PO may increase the risk of impingement and AVN [11]. Therefore, follow-up is essential to monitor outcomes and complications in patients with DDH [14]. Moreover, according to the EFORT CRC Paediatrics syllabus [15], the upper age limits for these procedures are approximately 8 years for SO and 6–7 years for PO.

Currently, radiography remains an important adjunct for objective postoperative evaluation in patients with DDH. When combined with physical examination and other assessments, it can provide more informative guidance for both orthopedic surgeons and patients. Therefore, assessment of postoperative radiographic and clinical outcomes is of particular importance.

AVN is the major complication following open reduction. Multiple factors may contribute to its development; one recognized risk factor is forced abduction during bracing treatment [16]. However, the association between AVN and objective patient-related factors remains controversial [16, 17].

This study aimed to compare the effectiveness of these procedures, including AVN incidence, functional results, and radiographic parameters, and mainly, to identify prognostic risk factors. We hypothesized that both SO and PO could lead to favorable outcomes in patients with DDH, and that our newly proposed radiographic parameter, the congruity index, could provide an objective measure for postoperative assessment in these patients.

Methods

Participants and radiography examination

The current study adhered to the ethical principles detailed in the Declaration of Helsinki and obtained ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of our institution (No. 2024041607). We retrospectively analyzed hip joint data from patients diagnosed with DDH who underwent surgical treatment between April 2011 and May 2018 at osteoarthritis specialized disease database of China-Japan Union Hospital of Jilin University. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of DDH; (2) children aged 1–7 years who had previously undergone conservative treatments, such as the Pavlik harness, closed reduction, and others, which had failed; (3) all patients with irreducible frank dislocation or subluxation that required open reduction with pelvic osteotomy (SO/PO). These patients were excluded if they had incomplete clinical data; inadequate radiographs, including poor image quality, incomplete visualization of the pelvis, or obvious pelvic tilt or rotation; neuromuscular or muscular disorders; syndromic presentation; or residual deformity resulting from prior hip infections. All surgeries were performed using a one-stage open reduction, and patients were divided into two groups according to the pelvic osteotomy technique (SO or PO). Finally, 41 (89.1%) of the 46 patients with SO and 24 (92.3%) of 26 patients with PO had a minimum of five years of clinical and radiographic follow-up and were included in this study. For the included DDH patients, preoperative radiographs and radiographs from the latest postoperative follow-up were selected for analysis. All radiographic measurements were performed on standardized anteroposterior pelvic radiographs. Standardized radiographs were defined as those with a neutral pelvic position, symmetrical obturator foramina, and clearly identifiable anatomical landmarks.

Surgical procedure and postoperative care

The choice between SO and PO was non-randomized and based on patient age, acetabular morphology, dysplasia severity, post-reduction hip stability, and surgeon preference. SO was generally used for hips requiring acetabular reorientation with acceptable congruity after reduction, whereas PO was favored for more severe acetabular dysplasia or insufficient femoral head coverage, especially when additional acetabular roof coverage was needed with open triradiate cartilage. Femoral shortening osteotomy was performed when excessive pressure on the reduced femoral head was observed intraoperatively.

Surgeries were performed according to the original descriptions of the SO or PO procedures. Both approaches used Smith’s anterior approach, retracted to protect the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve, incised the joint capsule, removed the ligamentum teres, severed the transverse acetabular ligament, and released the iliopsoas tendon to facilitate full reduction of the acetabulum and femoral head. In SO procedures, the osteotomy extends from the anterior inferior iliac spine to the great ischial notch. The acetabulum was rotated forward and outwards, and the wedge-shaped bone removed from the ilium was inserted into the fracture line. This was secured with a Kirschner wire to enhance acetabular stability post-osteotomy (Fig. 1A, B). In the PO procedures, an arched osteotomy was performed, beginning at the anterior inferior iliac spine and extending to the posterior wing of the Y-type cartilage. Subsequently, the acetabulum is repositioned forward, downward, or outwards. The wedge-shaped bone was then placed on the fracture line (Fig. 1C, D).

To ensure hip joint stability, patients with bilateral DDH underwent contralateral surgery three months apart. After surgery, all patients wore an abduction orthosis for six weeks, then rehabilitation training was initiated under the guidance of an orthopedic rehabilitation physician.

Definition of the congruity index and radiographic measurements

Post-osteotomy acetabular morphology plays a critical role in long-term prognosis [6]. Therefore, we introduced the congruity index to assess acetabular–femoral head alignment, defining it as the percentage of the femoral head covered by the acetabulum. As previously described by Cooperman et al. [18] and Delaunay et al. [19], the coverage was assessed using three vertical reference lines: (1) through the teardrop, (2) through the lateral acetabular margin, and (3) through the lateral edge of the femoral head. The distance between the first and second lines (a) was divided by the distance between the first and third lines (b), and the a/b ratio was converted to a percentage ([a/b] × 100) (Fig. 2).

To objectively assess postoperative hip joint recovery in patients with DDH, we also measured the Tönnis grade [20], Acetabular index (AI), and centre-edge angle (CEA). As some patients with DDH had closed Y-type cartilage at the latest follow-up, the Sharp angle was also included. All the radiographic parameters were measured by two experienced orthopaedic surgeons (J.R.Z. and J.L.Z.) performed 2 measurements for patients with SO/PO procedures, with a 1-week interval between each session.

Clinical and radiographic evaluation

Among the various evaluation methods, the Severin and McKay classifications are widely used for radiographic and clinical assessments. The Severin classification, first described by Severin et al. [21], divides hips into six grades based on the radiographic severity of femoral head displacement (Supporting Information, Table S1). The McKay classification, proposed by McKay [22] and widely used to assess clinical outcomes, grades postoperative hip function as Excellent, Good, Fair, or Poor (Supporting Information, Table S2). Kalamchi et al. [17] devised a four-grade classification based on the extent of damage to the ossific nucleus and the physis after dislocation treatment (Supporting Information, Table S3).

At the latest follow-up, Severin classification was used for radiographic evaluation, and the satisfaction rate (I or II) was calculated. The last clinical outcome was categorised using the McKay classification and the excellent rate (excellent or good) was calculated. Furthermore, AVN grade was assessed using the Kalamchi-MacEwen classification, and the occurrence rate of AVN was also determined with class II or higher. (Class I regarded as a minor sequela.)

Statistical analysis

The independent-samples t test was applied to continuous variables (age at surgery, duration of follow-up, AI, CEA, the Sharp angle, and congruity index), whereas the differences of sex, laterality, Tönnis grade, Severin classification, McKay classification, and AVN grade between two groups, Pearson’s chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate) was applied instead.

Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify risk factors associated with clinically significant AVN (defined as Kalamchi classes II–IV) and poorer McKay classification (defined as fair or poor). Variables with P < 0.25 in univariate analysis or clinical relevance were considered candidates for multivariable analysis, and a parsimonious final model was constructed to reduce the risk of overfitting. Multicollinearity was examined using tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF). Model fit and discrimination were assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test and Nagelkerke R². Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were reported.

To further validate the predictive value of the congruity index, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed for poorer McKay classification and clinically significant AVN. The optimal congruity index cutoff was determined using the Youden index. The area under the curve (AUC), 95% CI, sensitivity, and specificity were calculated.

Intraclass and interclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated to evaluate the intra- and inter-observer reliability of radiographic parameter measurements using a 2-way, single-measures ICC agreement model. Generally, all measurements were independently performed by 2 experienced orthopaedic surgeons, each repeating the assessment twice. When within-observer reliability was good to excellent, the average of the two readings per surgeon was used to evaluate inter-observer agreement. Statistical analyses were conducted in SPSS Statistics (v26.0; IBM). Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

General information and radiographic parameters

As previously described, 41 patients (54 hips) with SO were compared with 24 patients (26 hips) with PO. Baseline characteristics were compared between the SO and PO groups to evaluate potential selection bias (Table 1). Variables that differed between groups or were considered clinically relevant, including age at surgery, sex, laterality, Tönnis grade, and during of follow-up, were considered in the adjusted analyses. However, the distribution of laterality differed between the groups (P = 0.009), and the SO group had a longer mean follow-up duration than the PO group (P = 0.016). To compare preoperative and postoperative radiographic parameters between the two groups, we measured AI, CEA, Sharp’s angle, and the congruity index. No significant differences were found preoperatively between the two groups but for CEA (P = 0.024, Table 2). However, the SO group demonstrated a significantly higher congruity index postoperatively (P < 0.001, Table 2). Analysis of ICCs demonstrated excellent reliability both within and between observers (Table 3).

Radiographic and clinical outcomes with AVN incidence

At the latest follow-up, satisfactory radiographic outcomes (Severin class I or II) were observed in 48 hips (88.9%) in the SO group and 20 hips (76.9%) in the PO group, with no significant difference in the distribution of Severin classification between groups (P = 0.053, Table 4). Similarly, satisfactory clinical outcomes, defined as excellent or good according to the McKay classification, were achieved in 50 hips (92.6%) and 22 hips (84.6%) in the PO group, with no significant difference between the groups (P = 0.171, Table 4). However, as one of the major postoperative complications, AVN was observed in 4 hips (7.4%) in the SO group and 4 hips (15.4%) in the PO group (P = 0.009, Table 4).

Risk factors for McKay classification and AVN grade

Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with poorer McKay classification and clinically significant AVN. No significant multicollinearity was observed in either model, with all VIF values < 5. The models showed no evidence of poor fit according to the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, with P values of 0.833 for poorer McKay classification and 1.000 for clinically significant AVN. The corresponding Nagelkerke R² values were 0.743 and 0.722, respectively, indicating good discriminative performance. Lower congruity index (P = 0.007) was significantly associated with poorer McKay classification at the latest follow-up, whereas sex and duration of follow-up were not associated with the clinical outcome (Table 5). Similarly, lower congruity index (P = 0.007) was significantly associated with clinically significant AVN, while group and sex showed no significant associations (Table 6).

ROC analysis was performed to assess the predictive value of CI for poorer McKay classification and clinically significant AVN. For poorer McKay classification, CI showed an AUC of 0.974 (95% CI, 0.934–1.000). The optimal cutoff value was 76.9%, sensitivity of 98.6%, specificity of 87.5% (Supporting Information, Figure S1A). For clinically significant AVN, CI yielded an AUC of 0.971 (95% CI, 0.924–1.000, with an optimal cutoff of 75.4%, sensitivity of 98.6%, specificity of 85.7% (Supporting Information, Figure S1B).

Discussion

We included multiple radiographic parameters, including AI, CEA, the Sharp angle, and the congruity index, to assess surgical outcomes in patients with DDH [23]. The results showed that both SO and PO were effective corrective procedures. According to the Severin and McKay classifications, both groups achieved satisfactory radiographic and clinical outcomes after surgery. Although Kalamchi class I of AVN is generally considered a minor sequela [20], a small number of patients developed class II or higher of AVN at the latest follow-up, warranting particular attention. Multivariable logistic regression analyses showed that the congruity index may be an independent risk factor for poorer McKay classification and clinically significant AVN. These findings may help guide the future management of postoperative patients with DDH.

AI and the Sharp angle were used to assess the severity of preoperative acetabular dysplasia and postoperative acetabular remodeling [23, 24]. Similarly, the CEA can be used to objectively assess residual acetabular dysplasia during follow-up and may help identify patients who require further intervention [25]. In our study, both groups showed significant postoperative improvement for AI, CEA, and the Sharp angle. Despite a significant between-group difference in preoperative CEA (P = 0.024), the SO group showed greater CEA improvement than the PO group (P = 0.005). In previous clinical studies, the Reimers migration index has been widely used as a quantitative measure of femoral head displacement relative to the ossified acetabular roof [26]. However, this index cannot be measured when the femoral head is completely contained within the acetabulum. Therefore, we chose the congruity index as a new evaluation indicator of acetabular femoral head coverage. In our DDH patients, the preoperative mean congruity index for the SO and PO groups were 50.80% and 54.78%, respectively, both indicative of incomplete coverage, a pathological condition. However, compared to the PO group, the SO group exhibited a greater improvement in postoperative congruity index (P < 0.001), suggesting more favorable postoperative femoral head–acetabulum congruity after SO.

As described above, SO and PO are the most commonly used corrective procedures, with both showing favorable outcomes at mid-term follow-up [27]. At the latest follow-up, 80 hips were evaluated using the Severin, McKay, and Kalamchi-MacEven classifications in our study, and the findings showed favorable radiographic and clinical outcomes in both groups, which contrasts with those of Merkcaet et al. [28], who favoured PO. In their meta-analysis, the Severin and McKay classification was evaluated for 1372 hips with SO and 374 hips with PO. The results indicated that compared to the SO group, postoperative outcomes in terms of clinical and radiographic measures were statistically better in the PO group. However, Bhatti et al. [11] reported that most patients who underwent PO exhibited an irregular acetabulum, unclear joint lines, and unclear cartilage spaces, whereas these did not appear in the SO group. Consistent with these findings, our comparative analysis showed that the PO group had a significantly higher incidence of AVN than the SO group (P = 0.009). Therefore, a larger sample size is required for further validation in future studies to compare differences between the two groups.

Regarding AVN, 4 of 54 hips (7.4%) in the SO group and 4 of 26 hips (15.4%) in the PO group were classified as Kalamchi class II. Wang et al. [20] has found older age at surgery, higher Tönnis grades, and longer follow-up times were associated with a higher risk of AVN. However, Bian et al. [16] demonstrated that patients with DDH with incomplete ossification nucleus development and severe dislocation were prone to AVN, irrespective of age. Given the controversy surrounding these factors and to explore potential predictors of poorer McKay classification and clinically significant AVN, variables with P < 0.25 in univariate logistic regression were considered for multivariable analysis. Follow-up duration, congruity index, and sex were included in the model for poorer McKay classification, while group, congruity index, and sex were included for clinically significant AVN. After adjustment for these factors, the congruity index remained significantly associated with both outcomes, suggesting its potential value as an indicator of postoperative prognosis. ROC analysis further demonstrated the predictive value of the congruity index, with AUC values of 0.974 for poorer McKay classification and 0.971 for clinically significant AVN. The optimal cutoff values were 76.9% and 75.4%, respectively, both showing high sensitivity and acceptable specificity. These findings suggest that a lower CI may help identify patients at increased risk of unfavorable outcomes and support its potential use as a practical radiographic marker for postoperative risk stratification. However, as these thresholds were derived from the present cohort, external validation is required before broader clinical application.

Limitation

The retrospective design of this study is its most conspicuous limitation. Further limitations of this study are as follows: (1) Congruity index measurements were based on anteroposterior pelvic radiographs and may be influenced by pelvic tilt, pelvic rotation, and radiograph quality. Although radiographs were obtained according to the standardized protocol at our institution and images with poor visualization of key anatomical landmarks or obvious pelvic malposition were carefully reviewed before measurement, minor variations in pelvic positioning could not be completely avoided because of the retrospective design. (2) The sample size was relatively small (n = 65), limiting our ability to conduct further subgroup analyses, including an assessment of the potential effect of femoral shortening osteotomy on AVN. (3) Selection of SO or PO may have been influenced by patient age, acetabular morphology, dysplasia severity, intraoperative hip stability, and surgeon preference, thereby introducing potential selection bias. Although adjusted analyses were performed, residual confounding may persist because of the limited sample size. (4) The age range of the population studied varied from 1 year to 6 years, significant differences in skeletal maturation across different ages still exist [29, 30].

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that both SO and PO yielded favourable radiographic performance and clinical outcomes in the treatment of DDH in children. Compared with patients with PO, those with SO demonstrated more pronounced improvements in congruity index. In patients with DDH, the congruity index may serve as an independent risk factor for AVN, and a lower congruity index may be associated with a poorer McKay classification.
