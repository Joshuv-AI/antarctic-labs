Breaking the activity-stability trade-off via dual-mechanism induced by rare earths for lasting seawater electrolysis
Authors: Le-le Ma, Huihui Cao, Zhen Yan, Yi-ru Hao, Jiawen Sun, Yaqin Chen, Chunhao Li, Jia Liu, Hui Xue, Jing Sun, Yali Zhang, Yaowen Li, Limin Wu, Qin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTriggering the adsorbate evolution and lattice oxygen mechanisms coupled path holds promise for overcoming the activity-stability trade-off of oxygen evolution reaction (OER). However, achieving precise regulation and activation of multiple OER pathways via 3d-metal modification strategy remains challenging due to the external effects of crystal fields and coordination environments. Herein, we propose an atomic-dispersed rare earth substitution strategy to construct a spatially partitioned reaction mechanism, and achieve dual activation between adsorbate evolution and lattice oxygen mechanisms. The strong shielding effect of 5 s/5p electrons in RE significantly reduces the influence of the external environment on the 4 f orbitals, thereby enhancing the controllability of the reaction pathway. The optimized Sm-NiMoO4 exhibits favorable OER performance. At an industrial temperature of 70 °C, the OER in simulated seawater exhibits an overpotential of only 214 mV at 0.5 A cm−2, while maintaining stability for 1500 h. Preliminary techno-economic analysis reveals that the cost of hydrogen produced from the Sm-NiMoO4 is US$2.56 kg−1. This synthetic strategy and mechanism analysis represent a valuable contribution towards achieving industrial-scale seawater hydrogen production.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis, Hydrogen energy, Electrocatalysis

Introduction

Anion exchange membrane water electrolysis (AEMWE) for hydrogen production is recognized as a pivotal approach to advance green energy technology and realize carbon neutrality1. Water electrolysis has garnered considerable interest as a clean and sustainable approach to hydrogen production2–4. In particular, driven by the Earth’s abundant seawater resources, the direct electrolysis of seawater has become a cutting-edge research direction5,6. However, the water-splitting efficiency for hydrogen production is constrained by the sluggish kinetics of the OER7,8. Additionally, a competitive relationship exists between the anodic OER and the chloride oxidation reaction (ClOR), while chloride ions can attack the active sites of the catalyst, leading to rapid catalyst deactivation9–11. Particularly under industrial-grade current densities, the structural stability and corrosion resistance of the catalyst deteriorated significantly12–14. Consequently, it remains a grand challenge to obtain a large current at a low voltage and resist Cl- corrosion. Therefore, designing an electrocatalyst that can simultaneously obtain high activity and long-term durability has become the key to optimizing seawater electrolysis technology.

Transition metal molybdates have attracted considerable attention owing to their favorable catalytic activity, structural stability, and electrical conductivity15–17. The crystal structure of NiMoO4 features Ni2+ and Mo6+ occupying the centers of octahedral sites, forming edge-sharing chains. NiMoO4 has been regarded as a promising catalyst due to its high structural tunability and the synergistic effect between Ni and Mo18,19. However, achieving both high reactivity and stability becomes challenging due to the dissolution of metals and the structural collapse in seawater. The rational regulation of the complex mechanisms of OER has been recognized as a pivotal strategy to enhance catalytic activity and stability17,20,21. In general, OER involves two distinct reaction pathways: one is the adsorbate evolution mechanisms (AEM) with metal species as the active sites, and the other is the lattice oxygen mechanisms (LOM) featuring oxygen sites as the active centers22,23. Compared with AEM, the LOM bypasses the highest energy barrier step corresponding to *OOH formation in the AEM, resulting in a lower theoretical overpotential requirement24,25. However, the LOM still suffers from stability problems induced by the generation of oxygen vacancies, which leads to destructive reconstruction. Therefore, catalysts relying on either AEM or LOM face a compromise between activity and stability. Precisely controlling the reaction pathway, or ideally enabling dual activation of AEM and LOM, is crucial for improving activity without sacrificing stability.

In accordance with the classical catalysis theory, the metal-oxygen (M-O) bonds can exert an influence on the energy band structure and the associated chemical adsorption of intermediates26–28. Adjusting the optimal covalent nature of the M-O bond and weakening the lattice oxygen confinement are beneficial to break of scaling relations, thereby synergistically activating both the AEM and the LOM29,30. Recently, introducing transition metals featuring d-valence electrons has been recognized as a viable strategy to modulate the local microenvironment of M-O bonds16,17,30. However, the d orbitals of transition metals are situated on the outermost electron shell, rendering them susceptible to external effects exerted by the crystal field and coordination environment. These external influences make it difficult to precisely control the reaction pathway. In contrast to d-block elements, the RE elements usually exhibit strong chemical inertness due to the shielding effect of the 5 s/5p electrons and the lanthanide contraction effect20,31. This effectively mitigates the impact of the coordination environment on the 4 f orbitals, thereby enhancing the controllability of electronic structure regulation and reaction pathway manipulation29,32. Similarly, these special valence electron configurations can create oxygen non-bonding states (ONB) and trigger the lattice oxygen activation33. However, the energy barrier hindering direct covalent bonding of f-block RE elements with transition metal oxide (TMOs) via f-d orbital coupling. This necessitates an effective strategy for the controlled integration of RE into TMO.

Herein, we report the rational incorporation of atomic Sm into NiMoO4 oxide, which can effectively activate and stabilize both the AEM and LOM. Sm tends to substitute for the octahedral Ni site, forming an Sm-O-M bridging structure. The asymmetric Sm-O-M bridging endows the catalyst with a rapid f-p-d electron gradient structure, and ultimately results in the emergence of localized LOM. Specifically, the traditional AEM pathway occurs at Ni/Mo sites, while the LOM pathway is activated at the Sm-O-M sites. Importantly, by controlling the substitution amount of Sm, the conversion process from AEM to LOM can be precisely regulated. The synthesized Sm-NiMoO4 demonstrates favorable OER activity, with a low overpotential of 200 mV at 10 mA cm−2 in 1.0 M KOH. The homemade AEMWE utilizing Sm-NiMoO4 as the anode requires only 2.1 V for the overall seawater splitting to attain 1.0 A cm−2. Moreover, the AEM electrolyzer maintains stable operation for up to 250 hours. The anchoring of atomic Sm enhanced the catalytic stability relative to the original NiMoO4. As revealed by in-situ Raman spectroscopy, the Sm facilitates the reconstruction of NiMoO4, leading to the formation of a NiOOH layer at low voltages. These in-situ generated hydroxides can inhibit the adsorption of chloride ions on the catalyst surface, thereby reducing the competition between anodic OER and CLOR. This synthetic strategy and mechanism analysis represent a valuable contribution towards achieving industrial-scale seawater hydrogen production.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and structural characterization

As illustrated in Fig. 1a, the NiMoO4 precursor and Sm-modified NiMoO4 (Sm-NiMoO4) catalysts were obtained via a facile one-step hydrothermal method. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) reveals that the mass fraction of Sm in Sm-NiMoO4 is 1.55% (Suppl. Table 1). X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis confirmed the crystal structure of the Sm-NiMoO4. The peaks located at 14.3°, 24.0°, 28.8°, 41.2°, and 43.9° are assigned to the (110), (−121), (220), (040), and (330) planes of NiMoO4 (JCPDS #33-0948 and JCPDS #13-0128), respectively (Suppl. Fig. 1). The similar peaks are observed in Sm-NiMoO4 and NiMoO4, implying that Sm has successfully incorporated into the NiMoO4 lattice. Microstructural investigations were investigated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The morphology of NiMoO4 consist of nanorods with a smooth surface (Fig. 1b and Suppl. Fig. 2). No obvious change in its morphology is observed after Sm introduction (Fig. 1c and Suppl. Fig. 3). High-resolution TEM (HR-TEM) images and the associated fast Fourier transform (FFT) pattern of Sm-NiMoO4 indicated a lattice spacing of 0.310 nm, which is indexed to the (220) plane of NiMoO4 (Fig. 1d and Suppl. Fig. 4). Meanwhile, high-angle annular dark-field STEM (HAADF-STEM) mapping revealed that the bright spots marked by circles and rectangles represent atomically dispersed Sm atom (Fig. 1e–g). Furthermore, the measured interatomic distance of 1.0 nm between Sm sites in the HAADF-STEM confirms the successful anchoring and uniform atomic dispersion of Sm within the NiMoO4 lattice (Fig. 1h, i). Additionally, the uniform distribution of Ni, Mo, O, and Sm throughout the region is evidenced by HAADF-STEM and corresponding elemental mapping (Fig. 1j and Suppl. Fig. 5). The surface chemical states and compositions of the electrocatalysts were systematically characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). In the Ni 2p spectrum of NiMoO4 and Sm-NiMoO4, the peaks observed at 855.9, 873.7, 857.3, and 875.5 eV are assigned to 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 of Ni2+ and Ni3+, respectively34. In addition, the signals observed at 862.4 and 880.5 eV originate from a satellite peak (Fig. 1k). In Fig. 1l, the peaks at 232.2 and 235.4 eV correspond to Mo6+ (3d5/2 and 3d3/2). Deconvolution of the O 1 s spectrum yields three components at 530.4, 531.9, and 533.3 eV, which are attributed to lattice oxygen (O1), oxygen vacancy (O2), and adsorbed OH- (O3), respectively (Fig. 1m)12,31. The high-resolution Sm 3 d spectrum exhibits the characteristic doublet of Sm3+, with the 3d5/2 and 3d3/2 peaks positioned at binding energies of 1082.9 and 1110.2 eV, respectively (Suppl. Fig. 6)1. Notably, compared with NiMoO4, the peak position of Ni2+ in Sm-NiMoO4 shows a positive shift. However, the binding energy of Mo 3 d remains basically unchanged, indicating that the introduction of Sm selectively modulates the electronic structure of Ni and induces a strong electronic coupling between Sm and Ni.

Electronic structure and coordination analysis

X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra were employed to probe the electronic structure and coordination environment of the electrocatalysts. The Ni K-edge XANES spectra reveals that the absorption edge of Sm-NiMoO4 is closer to NiO and Ni2O3, indicating the coexistence of Ni2+ and Ni3+ (Fig. 2a). This is in agreement with the XPS result. To further elucidate the role of Sm dopants, Fourier transform (FT) EXAFS k3 curves and the corresponding fitting parameters were analyzed. As observed in Ni K-edge FT-EXAFS (Fig. 2b and Suppl. Figs. 7–10), the first shell at 1.60 Å corresponds to the Ni-O, and the second shell at 2.58 Å is Ni-O-M1. After the introduction of Sm, a second shell peak split into two peaks at 2.73 and 3.34 Å, suggesting the co-existence of Ni-O-M1 and Ni-O-M2 coordination in Sm-NiMoO435. Moreover, the bond lengths of Ni-O (1.59 Å) have become shorter, indicating that Sm is incorporated into the Ni octahedral (Oh) sites and the local coordination environment of Ni sites is also changed. The fitting results show that the coordination number (CN) of Ni-O in Sm-NiMoO4 decreases from 5 to 4 compared with NiMoO4, indicating the formation of O vacancies (Suppl. Table 2)36. For Sm L3-edge XANES spectra, the profile of Sm-NiMoO4 is essentially consistent with Sm2O3, confirming that the valence state of Sm is +3 (Fig. 2c). From FT-EXAFS k3 curves of Sm, the bond length of 3.34 Å corresponds to the Sm-O-M1 site (Fig. 2d). The Mo K-edge XANES spectra of Sm-NiMoO4 display that its valence state is close to +6 (Fig. 2e)35. Moreover, for Mo K-edge FT-EXAFS, the coordination number of the Mo-O bond is 6, indicating that Mo occupies the Oh site (Fig. 2f and Suppl. Figs. 11–14)17. Upon the introduction of Sm, the coordination environment of Mo remains basically unchanged. The wavelet transformation is employed to resolve the scattering path signals for Ni-O, Sm-O, Ni-Ni, Mo-O, and Sm-O-M1 bonds (Fig. 2g and Suppl. Figs. 15, 16). The above results indicate that Sm regulates the electronic environment of Ni Oh sites and forms asymmetric Sm-O-M units. Furthermore, Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements were conducted to confirm the existence of spin-orbit coupling and vacancies. Compared with NiMoO4, Sm-NiMoO4 exhibited a broad peak at g = 1.94, indicating that the 4 f orbitals of Sm indirectly regulated the effective electron filling number at the Ni/Mo sites through coupling with the O 2p orbitals (Fig. 2h, i)32,37. Meanwhile, the enhanced peak intensity at g = 2.003 suggests an increase in oxygen vacancies, which is consistent with the O XPS result.

Electrochemical alkaline OER performance

The OER performances were estimated in 1.0 M KOH. The different Sm doping contents on the performance of the catalyst were systematically studied. It is concluded that the Sm element with a concentration of 0.005 M exhibits the optimal performance (Suppl. Fig. 17). Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was assessed to evaluate the OER activities of NiMoO4, Sm-NiMoO4, and RuO2/NF (90% iR compensation) (Suppl. Fig. 18). Sm-NiMoO4 achieves overpotentials of 190 mV at 10 mA cm−2 and 350 mV at 500 mA cm−2, outperforming NiMoO4 (239 mV @ 10 mA cm−2, 479 mV @ 500 mA cm−2) and commercial RuO₂/NF (309 mV @ 10 mA cm−2, 597 mV @ 500 mA cm−2) (Fig. 3a). Furthermore, Sm-NiMoO4 exhibits a low Tafel slope of 36.8 mV dec−1 (Fig. 3b), lower than those of NiMoO4 (51.4 mV dec−1) and RuO2/NF (75.1 mV dec−1). Moreover, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) exhibits that the Rct of Sm-NiMoO4 (3.0 ohms) outperforms NiMoO4 (5.1 ohms) and RuO2/NF (9.3 ohms) (Fig. 3c and Suppl. Fig. 19). The above Tafel slope and EIS results indicate an accelerated reaction kinetic rate for Sm-NiMoO4. The double-layer capacitance (Cdl) was employed to estimate the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) (Suppl. Fig. 20). Sm-NiMoO4 exhibits a Cdl of 2.60 mF cm−2, which exceeds NiMoO4 (2.48 mF cm-2) and RuO2/NF (2.22 mF cm−2), indicating increased intrinsic active sites and enhanced OER capability of Sm-NiMoO4 (Fig. 3d). Furthermore, Sm-NiMoO4 demonstrates nearly 100% Faraday efficiency at 200, 500, and 1000 mA cm−2 for OER (Fig. 3e and Suppl. Fig. 21). For overall water splitting in 1.0 M KOH, the potential of Pt-C//Sm-NiMoO4 at 200 mA cm−2 is 1.58 V, outperforming commercial electrodes (1.79 V) (Fig. 3f). In addition, a prolonged durability evaluation for OER was carried out. At 500 mA cm−2, the Sm-NiMoO4 retains 91% activity after 600 h testing (Fig. 3g). Notably, a comparison with recently reported Mo/Ni-based OER catalysts demonstrates that Sm-NiMoO4 has competitive catalytic activity (Suppl. Fig. 22 and Table 3).

Electrochemical performance in seawater

To explore the potential for industrial application, the OER performances were studied in simulated seawater and alkaline seawater using a three-electrode system within the temperature range of 25–70 °C38. At 25 °C, compared with NiMoO4, Sm-NiMoO4 exhibits competitive electrolytic performance in seawater (Suppl. Fig. 23). From the LSV curve in Fig. 4a, at 70 °C, the overpotentials requires for simulated seawater electrolysis are 214 and 270 mV to reach 500 and 1000 mA cm−2, respectively. And for alkaline seawater, overpotentials of 282 and 338 mV are needed to reach the same current densities (Fig. 4b). To elucidate the electron transfer mechanism during the OER in simulated seawater, we performed open-circuit potential (Vocp) decay analysis at varying temperatures. Following a 100 s chronopotentiometric measurement at 100 mA cm−2, the power supply was disconnected, and Vocp decay was recorded immediately (Fig. 4c)39. Compared to laboratory conditions (25 °C), Sm-NiMoO4 exhibits accelerated Vocp decay at elevated temperatures, suggesting a reduction in electron transfer resistance that enhances seawater electrolysis efficiency. The catalyst exhibits favorable performance at the industrial temperature of 70 °C compared to the laboratory temperature of 25 °C. Long-term durability tests were conducted for NiMoO4 at 500 mA cm-2 in simulated seawater (Suppl. Fig. 24). After 140 h of continuous operation, its current density decreased by 21%. In contrast, Sm-NiMoO4 showed only a 9% current density loss over 1500 h, demonstrating its promising potential for industrial applications (Fig. 4d). Moreover, Sm-NiMoO4 displays competitive electrocatalytic performance for seawater electrolysis relative to recently reported Mo/Ni-based catalysts (Suppl. Fig. 25 and Tables 4, 5). Furthermore, it exhibits an OER Faraday efficiency of nearly 99% in seawater (Suppl. Fig. 26).

To assess its industrial applicability, an anion exchange membrane (AEM) water electrolyzer was constructed employing Sm-NiMoO4 and Pt/C as the anode and cathode, respectively (Fig. 4e). LSV testing reveals that in simulated seawater and alkaline seawater, only 2.1 and 2.2 V are required to drive 1000 mA cm−2, respectively (Fig. 4f and Suppl. Fig. 27). The durability of the AEM electrolyzer was evaluated in simulated seawater and alkaline seawater. At 700 mA cm−2, the cell maintains stable operation for 250 and 150 h, respectively (Suppl. Fig. 28), with negligible decrease in current density throughout the testing period. To further evaluate the practical application potential of Sm‑NiMoO4 for industrial‑scale green hydrogen production, we performed a systematic techno‑economic analysis (TEA) (Suppl. Tables 6–8). As shown in Fig. 4g, Sm‑NiMoO4 exhibits a levelized cost of hydrogen (LCOH) of approximately 2.56 $ kg−1 H2, which is lower than the $ kg-1 H2 for NiMoO4, and approaches the 2026 green hydrogen cost target (2.0 $ kg-1 H2) set by the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE). A contour plot of LCOH as a function of cell voltage and electricity price is displayed in Fig. 4h, clearly indicating that the operating conditions achieved in this work are located within a highly cost-effective region. The sensitivity analysis further quantifies the influence of key operational and economic parameters on hydrogen production cost, verifying that cell voltage and electricity cost are the most dominant factors governing LCOH (Fig. 4i). Collectively, these results demonstrate that Sm doping represents an effective strategy for reducing the cost of green hydrogen production, providing solid economic feasibility support for Sm‑NiMoO4 as a high‑performance electrocatalyst toward large‑scale industrial seawater electrolysis.

Characterization of resistance to Cl−

A further investigation was conducted to elucidate the reasons and mechanisms behind the catalyst’s resistance to Cl- erosion in seawater. Firstly, OER tests were conducted in simulated seawater with different concentrations of Cl-. The experiment demonstrates that as Cl- concentration increases, the performance of Sm-NiMoO4 remains basically unchanged, while the performance of NiMoO4 decays obviously (Fig. 5a, b). Furthermore, analysis of the XPS peaks for Cl− was performed after the OER (200 CV cycles) in simulated seawater. The XPS results show that a large amount of Cl− is enriched on NiMoO4, and very few Cl− are observed on the Sm-NiMoO4 (Fig. 5c). This further indicates that the doping of Sm reduces the adsorption of Cl-. According to the Bode plot of Sm-NiMoO4 and NiMoO4, electrochemical reconstruction of the catalyst is observed at high frequencies, while the OER occurs at low frequencies (Fig. 5d and Suppl. Fig. 29). As shown in Fig. 5d, upon application of potential of 1.4 V to Sm-NiMoO4, the vanishing of the phase angle in the high-frequency region evidences the transition of low-conductive Ni (II) to high-conductive Ni (III), implying the formation of active hydroxides40. The HRTEM of Sm-NiMoO4 after OER shows that the 0.14 and 0.22 nm lattice distance corresponds to the (1 1 1) and (1 0 -1) face of NiOOH, respectively (Fig. 5e and Suppl. Fig. 30). This indicates that the catalyst has undergone reconstruction, and the result is consistent with the Bode plot. From in situ-Raman spectroscopy, the two new bands located at 473 and 551 cm−1 correspond to the bending (Ni-O) and stretching (Ni-O) vibrations of Ni3+ in NiOOH, respectively14. At 1.2 V, NiOOH in Sm-NiMoO4 can be detected during the OER process (Fig. 5f, g). However, NiOOH in NiMoO4 is only observed above 1.4 V (Fig. 5h, i), indicating that Sm doping facilitates the formation of a NiOOH shell. This result is consistent with the above-mentioned Bode plot and TEM results. Meanwhile, the Cl- peak can be observed at 1650 cm−1. Interestingly, with the appearance of NiOOH, the Cl− peak gradually disappears41. Compared with NiMoO4, the Cl- peak of Sm-NiMoO4 disappears at a lower voltage (1.4 V). The in-situ generated local alkalinity (NiOOH) preferentially enriches OH-, which effectively prevents Cl- access to the catalyst surface (Fig. 5j).

AEM-LOM dual-path mechanistic analysis

To gain comprehensive insights into the impact of Sm incorporation on OER activity and stability, further investigation was carried out to explore the OER reaction pathways of different catalysts. Specifically, the traditional AEM occurs at a single-metal site via a four-step OER pathway (HO* → O* → OOH* → O2), which is fundamentally limited by a theoretical minimum overpotential of 370 mV (Fig. 6a)17. In contrast, LOM refers to the direct coupling of lattice oxygen through O-O bonds to facilitate O2 formation, which overcomes the overpotential limitation inherent in AEM (Fig. 6b)18. Nevertheless, the LOM pathway remains plagued by stability issues, stemming from the generation of substantial oxygen vacancies. Therefore, catalysts with either AEM or LOM involve a trade-off between activity and stability. To explore the reaction mechanisms of the catalysts, the OER performance of Sm-NiMoO4 and NiMoO4 samples was initially assessed under different pH conditions. The reduction of pH from 14.0 to 12.5 leads to a decline in OER activity of Sm-NiMoO4, indicating a strong reliance on the pH of the electrolyte (Fig. 6c), whereas NiMoO4 displays weak pH dependence (Fig. 6d, e). The strong pH sensitivity of Sm-NiMoO4 suggests that Sm doping may promote the LOM mechanism42. According to prior investigations, peroxide species (*O22−) have been established as key reaction intermediates in the LOM pathway. And the interaction between *O22− and tetramethylammonium cations (TMA⁺) leads to a deceleration in the OER kinetics of the LOM pathway42,43.

To further verify the LOM mechanism, LSV measurements were performed on samples with and without TMA⁺. The results demonstrate that upon the introduction of TMAOH, the OER activity of Sm-NiMoO4 substantially diminishes, whereas the activity of undoped NiMoO4 exhibits only a marginal decrease (Fig. 6f). This discrepancy indicates that the OER process of Sm-NiMoO4 exists LOM pathway, while the undoped NiMoO4 follows an AEM. In other words, Sm doping triggers the transformation of the OER mechanism from AEM to LOM. We further determined the LOM pathway using in situ differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) tests measured by isotope labeling (Suppl. Fig. 31). Firstly, the Sm-NiMoO4 catalyst was labeled by CV cycling in 1.0 M KOH with H218O as the water source, and then the DEMS test was carried out in the KOH solution with H216O as the water source. Signals with mass-to-charge ratios (m/z) of 32, 34, and 36 for Sm-NiMoO4 were detected, corresponding to 32O2 (16O16O), 34O2 (16O18O), and 36O2 (18O18O), respectively (Fig. 6g)17,33. This confirmed the role of lattice oxygen in OER. Based on the above analysis, it can be inferred that the LOM mechanism represents the energy-favorable path of Sm-NiMoO4 after the introduction of Sm. Subsequently, in situ attenuated total reflection surface-enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (ATR-SEIRAS) was employed to capture real-time signals of OER reaction intermediates (Suppl. Fig. 32a). As the potential was raised to 1.22 V, an absorption band emerged at 1026 cm−1 in the operando ATR-SEIRAS spectra of NiMoO4, which is attributed to the stretching vibration of *OOH. This confirms that only the AEM pathway was operative on the surface (Fig. 6h). Conversely, the spectrum of Sm-NiMoO4 displays not only the *OOH stretching vibration but also a distinct *O-O stretching vibration at 1090 cm−1 (Fig. 6i), signifying the involvement of LOM in the AEM pathway. Integrating the above analysis validated the concurrent occurrence of AEM and LOM pathways in Sm-NiMoO4. Interestingly, further increasing the Sm doping content causes the *O-O characteristic peak to emerge at a lower potential with enhanced intensity, indicating a progressively dominant LOM pathway (Suppl. Fig. 32b). Therefore, precise control over the Sm doping level enables effective modulation and optimization of the OER reaction mechanism. The AEM pathway proceeds at Ni sites, whereas the LOM pathway occurs at Sm-O-M bridged oxygen sites (Fig. 6j).

Theoretical analysis

To elucidate the impact of Sm substitution on OER activity, Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were conducted on structural models of NiMoO4, Sm-NiMoO4, and Sm-NiMoO4/NiOOH (Fig. 7a and Suppl. Data 1). Three energy intervals are exhibited by the Sm f orbitals in the projected density of states (PDOS) (Suppl. Fig. 33)44,45. It can be observed that all f orbitals with downward spin are empty states. Near the Fermi level at a position 1 eV above, there is a distribution of empty states with upward spin, indicating that the 7 f orbitals of Sm are not in a fully electron-filled state. Ni exhibits the d-orbital distribution characteristics of Ni (II) under octahedral field splitting. The O p orbitals are characterized by a relatively continuous distribution, with degenerate px and py and a higher-lying pz orbital. Since the p orbitals are mainly distributed near the Fermi level, they can contribute more O 2p non-bonding orbitals (O 2p NB). In addition, the crystal orbital Hamiltonian population (COHP) analysis was utilized to explore the interactions between different orbitals of the catalyst. The d-p hybridization between Ni-3dz2 and O-2pz forms σ orbitals, the dyz and dxz orbitals form π bonds with 2px and 2py orbitals of O. Meanwhile, the Sm-O bond forms a localized f-p interaction (Fig. 7b, c). A strong orbital interaction among the three components is evidenced (Fig. 7d). At the Sm-O-Ni bridging oxygen site, the p orbitals of O are mainly located near the Fermi level. Meanwhile, the coupling effect among Sm 4 f, O 2p, and Ni 3 d enables them to contribute more O 2p NB, thereby promoting the occurrence of the LOM mechanism. Furthermore, multiple OER reaction pathways including the AEM and LOM were systematically explored, and all plausible adsorption sites on the catalysts were identified to determine the optimal reaction pathway and favorable active adsorption sites (Suppl. Figs. 34–48 and Suppl. Tables 9–16). For NiMoO4, the OER follows the AEM pathway, consisting of four steps at Ni active sites (H2O → HO* → O* → OOH* → O2). The formation of *O serves as the rate-determining step (RDS), featuring a notable free energy barrier of 1.08 eV (Fig. 7e and Suppl. Tables 9, 10). Conversely, Sm-NiMoO4 enables OER to proceed concurrently via both the AEM and LOM pathways at distinct active sites. Given the relatively low electron depletion at oxygen defects, these sites can readily adsorb OH-/H2O. This allows them to function as “lattice oxygen” participants, enabling subsequent reversible OER cycles. Compared with the free energy barrier of the AEM pathway in NiMoO4 (1.08 eV), the formation of the *OvO intermediate becomes the rate-determining step (Fig. 7f and Suppl. Tables 11, 12, 15, 16), with a lower barrier (0.97 eV). This indicates that the bridging Ov can effectively activate the lattice oxygen in Sm-NiMoO4-Ov. Besides, the AEM pathway persists on the Ni sites, and the formation of *O is still serving as the RDS. The energy barrier for this step is 1.27 eV, which is close to that in NiMoO4, confirming the existence of the AEM pathway. Thus, the incorporation of Sm not only initiates the LOM pathway characterized by a lower energy barrier for the RDS and favorable reaction kinetics, but also maintains an appropriate AEM energy barrier at the Ni site. A synergistic enhancement in both activity and stability is achieved through the concurrent activation of AEM and LOM pathways. To gain a deeper understanding of the corrosion resistance during the OER in alkaline seawater for Sm-NiMoO4/NiOOH, a theoretical calculation was conducted. A comparative analysis of the Gibbs free energy profiles demonstrates that the CLOR pathway on Sm-NiMoO4 is lower in energy than the OER pathway, thereby confirming the priority of the CLOR (Fig. 7g and Suppl. Tables 13, 14, 17). Moreover, we constructed the Sm-NiMoO4/NiOOH model and conducted calculations on Cl− adsorption and exchange. The results show that the exchange of Cl- with H2O has an energy barrier of 1.5 eV, which is higher than OH− (−1.88 eV) (Suppl. Fig. 49). This indicates that Cl- can only theoretically adsorb onto unsaturated coordination atoms on the surface, and cannot stably adsorb on the surface through exchange. In addition, the adsorption energies of OH− and Cl− on the surfaces of Sm-NiMoO4 and Sm-NiMoO4/NiOOH were also calculated (Fig. 7h and Suppl. Figs. 50, 51). Thermodynamically, the adsorption energies on Sm-NiMoO4/NiOOH are −0.88 eV for Cl- and −3.83 eV for OH−, compared to −1.88 and −1.3 eV on Sm-NiMoO4, respectively. The more negative adsorption energy for OH− reveals its pronounced preference for adsorption on the Sm-NiMoO4/NiOOH surface. This finding indicates that the in situ formed NiOOH, creating a localized alkaline environment, inhibiting the adsorption of chlorine through the preferential enrichment of OH-.

In summary, we introduced Sm atoms into the Ni octahedral sites of NiMoO4 to form Sm-O-M bridges. The asymmetric Sm-O-M unit with a well-defined f-p-d electron ladder enables regulation of reaction pathways, facilitating the concurrent activation and stabilization of both AEM and LOM. The optimized Sm-NiMoO4 shows an OER overpotential of 200 mV at 10 mA cm−2. At an industrial temperature of 70 °C in simulated seawater, an OER overpotential of only 270 mV is required to achieve 1.0 A cm−2, stable operation is maintained for 1500 h. Moreover, it exhibits favorable seawater performance in anion exchange membrane water electrolyzers, delivering a large current of 1.0 A cm−2 at a voltage of 2.1 V. Preliminary techno-economic analysis reveals that the cost of hydrogen produced from the Sm-NiMoO4-based electrolyzer is US$2.56 kg−1, which is lower than the $ kg−1 H2 for NiMoO4, and approaches the 2026 green hydrogen cost target (2.0 $ kg−1 H2) set by the U.S. DOE. This synthetic strategy and mechanism analysis represent a valuable contribution towards achieving industrial-scale seawater hydrogen production.

Methods

Chemicals

Potassium hydroxide (KOH), ammonium molybdate (H24Mo7N6O24·4H2O, ≥ 99.9%), nickel nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, ≥ 99.9%), tetramethylammonium hydroxide (TMAOH), ruthenium oxide (RuO2), and Nafion (5 wt.%) were purchased from Aladdin corporation. Additionally, Ethanol (C₂H₅OH) was obtained from Beijing Chemical Corp., and nickel foam (NF, 0.5 mm thick) was purchased from Qingyuan Metal Materials Co., Ltd. (Xingtai, China). Seawater samples were collected from Huangdao District, Qingdao, China, in the summer. Prior to use, the surface oxide layer of the NF was removed by pretreatment, whereas all other reagents were used as received without any additional purification46.

Synthesis of NiMoO4/NF

A clean NF was obtained through sequential 30 min ultrasonic treatments in 3.0 M HCl, deionized water, and ethanol. 0.174 g Ni(NO3)2·6H2O and 0.185 g (NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O were dissolved in 15 mL deionized water. The pretreated NF (2 cm × 3 cm) was placed in a 50 mL Teflon-lined autoclave containing the solution mentioned above, followed by a constant-temperature treatment at 150 °C for 6 h. After 6 h at room temperature, the NiMoO4 was taken out, then rinsed repeatedly with deionized water and ethanol and dried at 50 °C.

Synthesis of Sm-NiMoO4/NF

0.174 g Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, 0.185 g (NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O and 0.033 g of Sm(NO3)3·6H2O were dissolved in 15 mL deionized water. The pretreated NF (2 cm × 3 cm) was placed in a 50 mL Teflon-lined autoclave containing the solution mentioned above, followed by a constant-temperature treatment at 150 °C for 6 h. After 6 h at room temperature, the NiMoO4 was taken out, then rinsed repeatedly with deionized water and ethanol and dried at 50 °C.

Material characterization

A SEM (JEOL-JSM 6700-F) was employed to characterize the morphology and microstructure of the samples. The morphology, crystal faces, and lattice fringing were characterized by TEM, FEI Tecnai F20. XRD measurements were conducted in the PuXi XD3 diffractometer. The catalyst surface element valence, composition, and relative content of each element were characterized using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, XSAM800). EPR measurements were conducted utilizing a Bruker A300-9.5/12 spectrometer. The content of Ni and Sm in the catalyst was determined using ICP-OES, Thermo ICAP 6300. XAFS spectra of Ni K-edge, Mo K-edge, and Sm L-edge were collected at station 1W1B of the Beijing. XAS data were collected in transmission mode for Ni/Mo foil, NiMoO4-Ni/Mo, and Sm-NiMoO4-Ni/Mo using an ionization chamber, and in fluorescence mode for Sm-NiMoO4-Sm and Sm2O3-Sm-flu with a Lytle detector, all under ambient conditions. In situ ATR-SEIRAS measurements were performed on a Bruker Vertex 80.

Electrochemical measurements

The electrochemical testing process utilized a CHI 760E electrochemical workstation. Within the three-electrode configuration, the counter electrode is graphite rod, and the reference electrode is Hg/HgO. Electrolytes were prepared via volumetric dilution, including 1.0 M KOH (pH = 14.00 ± 0.02), 1.0 M KOH + 0.5 M NaCl (pH = 13.95 ± 0.02), and 1.0 M KOH + seawater (pH = 13.93 ± 0.02). The temperature of the electrochemical experiment was conducted at around 25 °C. The working electrode is a piece of NF loaded with a catalyst (0.5 × 0.5 cm2 in OER and OWS). LSV with 90% iR compensation is measured at 5 mV s−1. EIS tests were measured from 10−1 Hz to 105 Hz at 1.48 V vs. RHE. The ECSA was evaluated by determining the double-layer capacitance (Cdl) from CV measurements performed in the non-Faradaic region between 1.1 and 1.2 V versus RHE. AEMWE tests

In this configuration, bipolar plates with serpentine flow channels (active area: 1 × 1 cm2) serve as current collectors, and the anion exchange membrane (AEM, Fumasep FAB-PK-130) is fixed using gaskets with a thickness of 0.8 mm. Commercial Ni foam (thickness: 2 mm) functions as both the gas diffusion layer (GDL) and the catalyst support for cathode and anode. The AEM is pre-treated by soaking in a 1.0 M KOH electrolyte for 24 h. And the Sm-NiMoO4 with the size of 1 × 1 cm2 was used as the anode catalytic layer, and commercial 20% Pt/C-coated NF (1 × 1 cm2) was used as the cathode catalytic layer. Within the AEMWE system, the 1.0 M KOH + 0.5 M NaCl and 1.0 M KOH +seawater electrolyte is provided to both the cathode and anode at a flow rate of 25 mL min−1. The electrochemical measurements of the AEM electrode device were studied by using the Donghua electrochemical workstation (DH7002A, Jiangsu). The LSV curves at various temperatures were measured at a scanning rate of 5 mV s−1 within a range of 1.2 to 3.0 V, and 90% iR compensation was performed. For the durability test, the AEMWE was continuously operated with a peristaltic pump flow rate of 25 mL min−1 to circulate the electrolyte.

Operando ATR-FTIR spectroscopy

Operando ATR-SEIRAS measurements were conducted using a Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectrometer equipped with a mercury-cadmium-telluride (MCT) detector. The working electrode consists of catalyst-supported nickel foam pressed into thin slices and affixed to the surface of the single-crystal silicon prism. This assembly was installed in a custom-built spectroelectrochemical cell coupled to the ATR accessory. An Ag/AgCl electrode (saturated KCl) served as the reference electrode and a Pt wire as the counter electrode. The Ar-saturated 1.0 M KOH was used as the electrolyte for the OER test. The cyclic voltammetry method was used in this experiment at different potentials (1.22–1.57 V vs. RHE).

Operando DEMS with isotope labeling

Operando DEMS characterizations were implemented using a Linglu QAS100 differential electrochemical mass spectrometer. A water-resistant and gas-permeable PTFE membrane supplied by LingLu was equipped in the testing system. This membrane possesses a porosity above 50% and a pore size no larger than 20 nm. All measurements were conducted in a standard three-electrode configuration with 1.0 M KOH, following three steps: (1) Catalysts were labeled with 18O via CV in 18O-tagged 1.0 M KOH, scanning at 100 mV s-1 between 1.0 and 2.0 V vs. RHE; (2) The labeled electrode was rinsed repeatedly with H216O to remove physically adsorbed H218O from the catalyst surface, while retaining 18O-containing chemically bound species; (3) Next, three LSV cycles were run in 1.0 M K16OH, within 1.1 V to 2.0 V vs. RHE. The mass spectrometer identified gases including 32O2, 34O2, and 36O2, with the signal intensity of gaseous products defined as the integrated area under the intensity curve.

DFT calculations

All DFT computations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) with a plane-wave basis set and the projector augmented-wave (PAW) method, and the exchange-correlation effects were described by the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional. Given the inherent limitations of GGA, the GGA + U approach was additionally implemented. Moreover, the DFT calculations incorporated Grimme’s DFT-D3 model for van der Waals interactions and a 15 Å vacuum layer to isolate periodic images. A 450 eV plane-wave cutoff and a 2 × 3 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh were used, with convergence criteria of 0.05 eV/Å for atomic forces and 10−5 eV for total energy.
