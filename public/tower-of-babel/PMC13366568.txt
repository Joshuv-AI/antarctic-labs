Minimum Inoculum of Resistance Assay for Evaluating Antitoxoplasmosis Compounds That Target Phenylalanine tRNA Synthetase
Authors: Taher Uddin, Payal Mittal, Han Xie, Bruno Melillo, Amit Sharma, Arnab K. Chatterjee, L. David Sibley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Toxoplasma gondii is a globally important intracellular parasite, and treatment regimens are limited by the failure of drugs to target latent tissue cysts. Developing new candidates for treatment also needs to address the potential for resistance to arise. Herein, we developed a minimum inoculum for resistance assay as a semiquantitative metric for evaluating inhibitors of T. gondii. The resistance assay, adapted from malaria, measures the frequency of pre-existing resistance alleles by exposing different-sized parasite populations to drug pressure. We profiled a series of bicyclic pyrrolidone analogues that inhibit phenylalanine tRNA synthetase. We demonstrate that these inhibitors require higher inocula to lead to parasite resistance (up to >108 parasites) in comparison with an inhibitor of DNA synthesis and that resistance values vary across inhibitors with closely related chemical structures. Clonal analysis of resistant parasites emerging from resistance assays revealed both new and previously identified resistance-conferring mutations in T. gondii phenylalanine tRNA synthetase, and structural modeling revealed their potential impact on the enzyme active site. The minimum inoculum for resistance assay provides a functional benchmark to compare new and existing inhibitors, allowing for rational prioritization of lead compounds with a high genetic barrier to resistance.

Introduction

Toxoplasma gondii is an obligate intracellular protozoan parasite responsible for toxoplasmosis, a globally prevalent infection with significant human and veterinary health impacts. − T. gondii infection in humans usually results in mild symptoms, consisting of fever, aches, and swollen glands, that resolve in weeks in healthy individuals. However, in immunocompromised people such as those with HIV/AIDS, infection can cause severe issues affecting the brain, eyes, and lungs. − Additionally, in the case of pregnancy, congenital infection can affect the development of the fetus and result in defects that range from serious CNS deformations to mild cognitive impairment and ocular complications later in life. Persistent infection depends on transition from the rapidly replicating tachyzoite to the slow-growing, semidormant bradyzoite within tissue cysts that reside in muscle and brain tissues and can reactivate, leading to serious illness. The chronic stages predispose individuals to the potential to reactivate, while the actively replicating tachyzoite form is responsible for clinical disease.

Standard therapies such as pyrimethamine and sulfadiazine are effective at stopping T. gondii tachyzoite proliferation, but they fail to eliminate the chronic bradyzoite in tissue cysts because bradyzoites are metabolically semidormant and well protected within the cyst wall. − Additionally, long-term, high-dose therapies can result in severe side effects. Novel strategies, including combination therapies, cyst wall-targeting drugs, and immunotherapies, are under investigation but require extensive testing for efficacy and safety before they could advance to the clinic. Antimicrobial resistance is a universal challenge for anti-infective drugs and often reflects heritable, genetically encoded changes in microbes that reduce a drug’s efficacy. Although clinical resistance is not commonly observed in toxoplasmosis, assessing the potential for new compounds to develop resistance provides an important criteria for evaluating early leads. The minimum inoculum for resistance (MIR) assay, originally established in malaria research, provides a quantitative framework for evaluating resistance at the preclinical stage. − T. gondii has a haploid genome that allows mutations to be easily expressed without being masked by a second allele. By determining the smallest population of parasites from which pre-existing resistance can emerge under selective drug pressure, the MIR assay provides a filter for prioritizing clinical candidates. Historical examples in antimalarial drug development have shown that drugs with low MIR values frequently fail in clinical trials due to rapid resistance emergence, while compounds with high MIR are more likely to show durable efficacy and become standard therapies. ,− Thus, MIR data can also influence lead advancement by allowing for deprioritization of molecules with unacceptable resistance risk.

In this study, we established an MIR protocol for T. gondii that defines the inoculum thresholds for resistance. The MIR assay in Toxoplasma research will facilitate prioritization of compounds with high genetic barriers to resistance and thus speed discovery of durable therapies for toxoplasmosis.

Results and Discussion

To establish an MIR assay for T. gondii, we systematically exposed cultures infected with tachyzoites to high-level, single-step drug pressure. For these assays, we used human foreskin fibroblast (HFF) cultures seeded with different parasite inocula (106–108 TgMe49-Fluc tachyzoites) and treated them with 3XEC90 concentrations of candidate compounds in triplicate populations (defined as F1, F2, F3) for 30 days (Figures A and A–E). We employed a low-passage, clonal TgMe49-Fluc parasite line B6 that was isolated by limited dilution using standard procedures. This line is considered genetically stable and although we did not measure the neutral mutation rate here, estimates for related type I strains predict a relatively low mutation rate of <10–9 per site per generation. Cultures were monitored for parasite growth by microscopy and clones were derived from resistant populations by limiting dilution.

We compared the resistance profile of previously characterized potent and selective inhibitors of T. gondii phenylalanyl-tRNA synthetase (PheRS), including one 4,8-bicyclic azetidine compound (referred to as BRD7929) and six 5,7-bicyclic pyrrolidine analogues − (Figure C and Table S1). As a positive control, we used 5-fluoro-2′-deoxyuridine (FUDR), which is a substrate analogue incorporated into fluorodeoxy-UMP by the salvage enzyme uracil phosphoribosyl transferase (UPRT), thus blocking the activity of thymidylate synthetase (Figure C). The primary mechanism for FUDR resistance in T. gondii is the loss of UPRT activity; when it is inactivated, the parasite cannot convert FUDR into toxic metabolites, thus allowing survival. , FUDR resistance can arise through any disruption in the non-essential UPRT gene and as anticipated, robust resistance to FUDR emerged rapidly within 2 weeks at the lowest inoculum tested (<106) (Figures B and A). In contrast, resistance to test compounds was observed only under higher inoculum pressures. For example, selection with compound 1 (previously referred to as BRD2108) and compound 10 resulted in resistant lines at an inoculum of 107 in all three populations (F1, F2, and F3), while compound 21 required an inoculum of 108 to generate a resistant population in all three populations (F1, F2, and F3). In contrast, compound 15 selection resulted in resistance in one (F3) out of three flasks/population at an inoculum of 108 (Figures B and B–E). No resistance to compounds 12, 19, or BRD7929 was observed up to an inoculum of 108, which was the maximum level tested here (Figure B). Despite the close structural similarity of these compounds that share the same PheRS target, − they varied substantially in the different inoculum sizes required to achieve resistance.

In cases where resistant parasites emerged in the culture, we evaluated the resistance profile using in vitro luciferase-based growth to estimate EC50. Parasites resistant to FUDR were observed at an inoculum of 106 in a similar timeline in all three populations; however, separate populations (F1, F2, and F3) showed different fold resistances (FRs) calculated by the difference in EC50 between resistant lines and the parental Me49Fluc line (Figure A). Compound 1-resistant populations also showed different levels of fold resistance in an inoculum of 107 (Figure B). In contrast, three compound 10-resistant populations showed lower-level fold resistance at an inoculum of 107 (Figure C). The sole compound 15-resistant population (F3) that developed at an inoculum of 108 had an FR of 3 (Figure D). Like compound 1, compound 21-resistant populations developed at an inoculum of 108 also showed diverse fold resistance (Figure E).

To evaluate the clonal populations further, single-cell clones were generated by limiting dilution from all parasite populations resistant to the compounds 1, 10, 15, and 21. Two to three clones (named as C1, C2, and C3) per population were cultured to evaluate fold resistance in the individual lines (Table ). With the exceptions of compound 10 F2C2 and compound 15 F3C3 clonal lines, the fold resistance in clonal parasite lines mirrored that seen in the population (Table ). We also tested the sensitivity of clonal parasite lines to the compounds FUDR, atovaquone, and pyrimethamine to evaluate the potential for cross-resistance. None of the resistant parasite lines showed increased resistance to these other compounds (Table ).

To determine the molecular basis of resistance, genomic DNA from the parasite clonal lines were used to amplify the presumed target of the compounds, the cytosolic α subunit of TgPheRS (TGME49_234505, toxodb.org) followed by next-generation sequencing to define potential mutations (Table ). Two of the clones (C1, C2) from compound 1 selected F1 population carried an L425 V mutation, whereas the third clone (C3) carried an additional mutation L521P in addition to L425V (Table ). Two clones (C1, C2) from other two compound 1 selected F2, F3 population carried an L497V mutation (Table ). Two clonal lines (C1, C2) from each compound 10-resistant population (F1, F2, and F3) carried an M260I mutation. Only one of the clones (compound 10 F2C2) carried an additional W495C mutation (Table ). For compound 15, the resistant clones C1 and C2 carried L497V mutations, whereas C3 carried an additional W495L mutation in addition to L497V (Table ). For compound 21-resistant parasites, two clones from F1C1 and F1C2 showed a mutation V262A, and four clones, F2C1, F2C3, F3C1, and F3C2, exhibited an L497V mutation (Table ). Next, we tested all clonal parasite lines for cross-resistance to the same panel of six compounds (Figure ). Clonal lines that showed high resistance to their selecting compound also showed high resistance to other related compounds, whereas lines with low resistance to their parental compound showed correspondingly low cross-resistance (Figure ).

Prior work reported selection of BRD7929-resistant T. gondii Me49 parasites by cultivation in gradually increasing concentrations over 5 months, resulting in 10- to 100-fold EC50 elevations. These studies identified mutations in the TgPheRS α-subunit (L497V/I, V262A, M484I) as principal resistance determinants. Introduction of the L497I mutation alone into a wild-type background conferred strong resistance (40–60-fold EC50 shift), while V262A and M484I mutations contributed lower levels of resistance (2–4-fold) validating TgPheRS as the primary resistance locus. Our study expanded on this prior work by testing the propensity for resistance to arise within a defined starting inoculum selected at a single high concentration. Like prior studies, our findings indicate that most clones that carry a mutation at L497V show high resistance, while mutations at V262A lead to low resistance. Somewhat surprisingly, two clones carrying L497V (compound 15 F3, C1, and C2) showed only low-level resistance, despite the fact that previous studies have shown that introduction of this single change is sufficient to confer high-level resistance. We retested the sequence of the TgPheRS α-subunit and the resistance profile of these clones and confirmed the original findings. In addition, we also checked the TgPheRS β-subunit (TGME49_306960), however did not identify any mutation in this subunit. Although we have not defined the basis for the low-level resistance of clones harboring L497V here, it suggests an unknown extragenic change that masked the normal resistance profile of this mutation. Additionally, we identified four new mutations (M260I, L425V, W495L, and L521P) (Table ). Clones with the L425V mutation showed moderate resistance, while those harboring M260I showed low resistance, with the exception of compound 10 clone F1, C2, which also harbored an M495C mutation leading to high resistance (Table ).

To gain more insight into the molecular basis of resistance, we generated a structural model of TgPheRS using AlphaFold3, as no crystallographic structure for the inhibitor-bound enzyme is currently available (Figure A). The model was of high quality, with a pLDDT score exceeding 90 in the catalytic domain and consistent interdomain confidence (iPTM = 0.68), allowing reliable interpretation of mutation impacts. After energy minimization and hydrogen bond optimization, the aminoacylation active site and substrate-binding pockets were annotated using homology with known FRS structures. Docking studies were then performed with representative bicyclic pyrrolidine analogues using the Glide XP protocol, with the best-ranked poses retained for analysis. We mapped resistance-associated mutations (V262A, M260I, L425V, M484I, L497I/V, W495L, L521P) onto the TgPheRS–ligand complexes using UCSF Chimera, differentiating those newly identified in this study from previously reported sites (Figure B). This approach revealed that novel low-resistance mutations, such as L425V, M260I, and L521P, localize outside the ligand-binding region, whereas high-resistant mutation W495L is located near the ligand-binding region, suggesting potential mechanisms for altered inhibitor affinity or substrate competition. The model also supports the functional importance of residues such as V262 and L497, which correspond to resistance hot spots in related apicomplexan PheRS enzymes. These findings provide a structural and mechanistic framework for understanding the diverse mutation profiles observed in resistant clones.

Conclusion

The MIR assay described here evaluates the potential for resistance to be either pre-existing or rapidly emergent under strong selection. Under these conditions, we observed a similar pattern of mutations that give rise to high-level resistance when compared with previous studies that used stepwise selection with increasing concentrations of the compound. In addition, we identified other potential sites of low-level resistance. Mapping these onto the structure of TgPheRS reveals a general pattern of high-level resistance being due to changes in the active site, while changes at more distant sites lead to lower-level resistance. Surprisingly, we observed widely different propensities for the rapid emergence of resistance using structurally related compounds that target the TgPheRS enzyme. Notably, spontaneous mutation rates in type I strains of T. gondii are <10–9 per site per generation, making the emergence of resistance at the inoculum sizes tested here surprising. The different frequencies of resistance were not obviously correlated with the relative potency, selectivity, hydrophobicity, or other physical-chemical properties of the chosen compounds (Table S1). Because we added compounds at intervals of 3–7 days, but did not monitor their stability, differences in compound degradation could have resulted in variable drug pressure and hence different likelihoods of resistance emerging. Finally, we cannot rule out the possibility that some compounds are protected from resistance by unknown secondary targets.

In summary, this study establishes the MIR assay as a quantitative resistance-risk framework for T. gondii and demonstrates its utility for distinguishing compounds with varying propensities for resistance emergence. Adoption of MIR-based resistance profiling will strengthen preclinical prioritization strategies and accelerate the development of next-generation antitoxoplasmosis therapies with improved long-term efficacy.

Experimental Methods

Compound Source and In Vitro Luciferase-Based Potency Evaluation

Compounds were obtained from Calibr (Scripps Research, La Jolla, CA 92037, USA) either as lyophilized powder or 10 mM stocks in 100% DMSO and stored at −80 °C. Human foreskin fibroblast (HFF) cell line and parasites were cultured in a medium consisting of 10% DMEM (Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium, D10), supplemented with 10 mM glutamine, 10 μg/mL gentamycin, and 10% fetal bovine serum. The cultures were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and routinely confirmed negative for mycoplasma contamination using the e-Myco Plus kit from Intron Biotechnology. Luciferase-based growth assays were conducted using the previously described TgMe49-Fluc strain. To reduce variability, only the inner 60 wells of white 96-well plates (Costar) were used to generate a 3-fold series of compounds. Each well received 10 × 103 freshly harvested TgMe49-Fluc or resistant lines tachyzoites cultured in confluent HFF in 100 μL volumes, achieving a final compound concentration of 200 μL/well with 0.1% DMSO in 10% DMEM. Plates were incubated for 72 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2 before analysis, following the Promega Luciferase Assay System protocol. After incubation, culture media were replaced with 50 μL of 1× Cell Culture Lysis Reagent (Promega). After 10 min at room temperature, 100 μL of Luciferase Assay reagent (Promega) was added using an injection system, and luciferase activity was measured using a BioTek Cytation 3 multimode imager with Gen5 software.

Generation of Compound-Resistant Parasite Lines and MIR Assay

To evaluate the MIR, 8 compounds, including 7 previously reported − and 5-fluorodeoxyuridine (FUDR), were used in this study. Potency, selectivity, and fold resistance to previously reported BRD7929-specific point-mutated TgRH-PheRS[L497I]-FLuc parasite line − are summarized in Table S1, modified from previously published data. − Triplicate HFF confluent T25 flasks were prepared in D10 media for each compound. Freshly harvested 106 TgMe49-Fluc tachyzoites (inoculum 106) were added to each flask, with the D10 media supplemented to a 3XEC90 concentration of the compound (Figure A). The flasks were monitored using a phase contrast inverted microscope two to three times per week for 30 days, with media replaced by fresh compound-supplemented D10 media every 3–7 days. If parasite growth was observed under a microscope, parasites were subcultured to expand the culture sufficiently for further assays, and TgEC50 values were assessed using the luciferase assay protocol described above. If resistance did not emerge at lower inoculum, additional triplicate HFF confluent T75 flasks with 107 TgMe49-Fluc tachyzoites (inoculum 107), and ultimately, triplicate HFF confluent T175 flasks with 108 TgMe49-Fluc tachyzoites (inoculum 108), were set up (Figure A). This sequential approach systematically evaluated the development of compound-resistant parasite lines to establish the lowest dose at which resistance arose. Resistance detected at any stage prompted cloning-resistant parasites via minimum dilution in 96-well plates. TgEC50 and fold differences between at least two clonal-resistant lines and the parental TgMe49-Fluc line (WT) were evaluated as described above, and gDNA from each clonal line was extracted for PCR amplification of TgPheRS α-subunit (Toxodb.org, TGME49_234505) and β-subunit (TGME49_306960), and sequence for genetic mutations.

gDNA Extraction, PCR, Sequencing, Mutation Mapping

Infected HFF cells were scraped and syringe-lysed using 25 gauge needles to release the parasites. The parasites were harvested by passage through a 3 μm filter and centrifugation at 400g for 10 min. gDNA was isolated using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN). The TgPheRS sequence was amplified using PrimeSTAR GXL Premix (Takara Bio), and the PCR primer sequences are provided in Table S2. After gel conformation and cleaning the PCR product using PCR cleanup and gel extraction kits (Takara Bio), the plasmidsaurus sequence amplicons service that utilizes the ONT Rapid Barcoding Kit V14 and is sequenced on a PromethION flow cell to get full-length coverage of PCR products, with no primers. Sequence data was analyzed using SnapGen 8.2.0.

Structure Models of the Resistance Mutations

As the crystallographic structure of T. gondii phenylalanyl-tRNA synthetase (TgcPheRS) in complex with inhibitors is not yet deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB), the TgcPheRS model was generated using AlphaFold3 , based on the full-length amino acid sequence of the α-subunit (Toxodb.org, TGME49_234505). The interdomain predicted TM (iPTM) is 0.68, which indicates high overall model confidence and a very high pLDDT score (>90) for most of the residues in the catalytic domain. The model was energy-minimized in Schrödinger’s Protein Preparation Wizard (Schrödinger Release Maestro version 14.2.121, MMshare Version 6.8.121, Release 2024–4, Platform Darwin-x86_64) with default parameters, ensuring correct protonation states at pH 7.4 and optimization of the hydrogen-bonding network. The binding site was defined based on the aminoacylation active site and phenylalanine/ATP binding pockets inferred from homologous FRS structures. Compounds were prepared using LigPrep with the OPLS4 force field and all of the possible states were determined at target pH 7.5 ± 1.00, and specific chirality was retained. Molecular docking was performed using Glide XP mode. The best-ranked poses for each compound were retained for visualization. Resistance-associated mutations (L425V, W495L, V262A, M260I, L497I/V, M484I) were mapped onto the TgcPheRS–ligand complexes in UCSF Chimera v1.16, with residues color-coded to distinguish newly identified mutations from those previously reported.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, Inc.). Dose–response inhibition curves for parasite and host cell toxicity screens (EC50 and CC50 values) were generated using the “Log­(inhibitor) vs normalized responseVariable slope” function.
