Daily stressor control buffers stressor-related negative and positive affect associations
Authors: Dakota D. Witzel, Zachary E. Taylor, Eric S. Cerino, David M. Almeida, Robert S. Stawski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Appraisals of daily stressors (e.g., daily stressor control) have been theorized to be associated with better short-term and long-term health and well-being. Although daily stressor control has been identified as a resource for buffering same-day and next-day affective responses to daily stressors, little work has explored associations across type of daily stressor. As such, the current study aimed to understand the potential protective utility of perceived stressor control for daily stressor-affect associations. Using the National Study of Daily Experiences (NSDEIII; n = 1,263, Mage = 62.62, 57.20% women), two-level multilevel models revealed a protective pattern where stressor control was associated with dampened affective response to daily stressors, particularly for interpersonal stressors (ps < .05). On days when people perceived more control over their arguments or avoided arguments participants exhibited less negative and positive affective reactivity compared to days with less perceived control. Perceived control over daily interpersonal and home stressors were associated with dampened negative and positive affective reactivity, respectively. Results underscore the importance of perceived stressor control for interpersonal and home stressors and the utility of perceived control as a protective factor for the more immediate/same day effects of daily stress. Individuals may aim to leverage resources (e.g., social support, active coping) to feel more in control of their own daily interpersonal stress.

Introduction

Exposure and affective reactions to daily stressors (i.e., minor inconveniences that occur throughout the day1) are associated with increased risk for chronic physical health2 and depression (Charles et al., 20133), poorer cognitive health4, and increased mortality5,6. Affective responses to daily stressors, or stressor-related affect, describes differences in affect on the same day as a stressor or the following day, relative to days without stressors4,7,8. This stressor-related affect is a potentially modifiable mechanism through which daily stressors inform health and well-being9,10. Determining what characteristics of daily stressors may mitigate stressor-related affect is crucial for informing daily and long-term health. The current study examines how one characteristic—how much control people perceive over their stressors (i.e., daily stressor control11,12)—informs daily stressor and stressor-related affect associations across domains.

Examining unique daily stressor domains

Daily stressors are diverse; this diversity is reflected in how individuals appraise and react to a stressor (Almeida et al. 2002). For over a decade, research has called to disambiguate daily stress research into daily stressor domains (Hay and Diehl 2010)8. The daily stress process (DSP) model1 highlights several domains, or types, of stressors that have previously been a focus in research: interpersonal (arguments and avoided arguments), work, home, and network daily stressors. These daily stressor types have theoretically and empirically been shown to have differential effects on well-being (i.e., stressor-related affect, Hay and Diehl 2010;13–15). Research suggests that daily interpersonal stressors are the most common and emotionally evocative13, possibly resulting from appraisals (e.g., lower likelihood of resolving daily interpersonal stressors than other daily stressors7), or a higher level of social threat associated with daily interpersonal stressors. Most notably, interpersonal stressors may be of particular impact as a result of the heightened emotional significance attributed to arguments or avoided arguments with friends or family, especially in the context of adulthood (e.g., socioemotional selectivity theory16). Similar albeit weaker associations between affect and other daily stressors have been evidenced (Hay and Diehl 2010). Given previous associations between daily stressor type and affect, the current study aims to extend previous research14,15 by testing associations between daily stressors, negative and positive stressor-related affect, and daily stressor control across daily stressor contexts (contexts: argument, avoided argument, work, home, and network).

Affective responses to daily stressors

A blossoming area of research has underscored the importance of stressor-related affect as a mechanism linking daily stress and long-term health17. Notably the DSP model highlights stressor-related affect as a critical psychosocial mechanism through which daily stress informs long-term health and well-being1,17. Stressor-related affect is operationalized via two temporally distinct phenomena: affective reactivity and affective residue. Although most research focuses on negative stressor-related affect (e.g.,4), it is important to recognize that negative and positive affect are interrelated, but distinct, exhibiting unique associations with health and well-being18–20. As such, the current study examines both negative and positive stressor-related affect.

Affective reactivity

Affective reactivity is reflected in the same-day increases in negative affect or decreases in positive affect associated with a daily stressor7. Most research examining daily stressor-affect links focus on negative affective reactivity as a product of daily stressor exposure (e.g.,7,18) or as a correlate for health-related outcomes (e.g.,3,4). Although researchers have noted the potential implications of positive affective reactivity on depression21, inflammation22, gene expression23, and mortality5, only recently have there been examinations of correlates and modifiers of positive affective reactivity to daily stressors (i.e.,7,24,25). As such, one aim of the current study is to test daily stressor control as a modifier of both negative and positive affective reactivity.

Affective residue

Affective residue is reflected in the increases in negative affect or decreases in positive affect associated with a daily stressor that occurred in the previous day7. Affective residue may be modulated by daily stressor characteristics such as resolution (i.e.,7), but not others (e.g., family involvement,8), and such modulation depends on the daily stressor domain. Therefore, an additional goal for the current research is to determine whether daily stressor control modifies negative and positive affective residue across daily stressor domains.

Control beliefs and daily stressors

Control beliefs—particularly one situational component, daily stressor control—may modify both negative and positive affective reactivity and residue across domains. Control beliefs refer to the belief that an individual’s resources or behaviors can result in desired outcomes26. Notably, research suggests that control beliefs can be global/general (e.g.,15), domain specific (e.g.,14,27), and situation specific (e.g.,12). Global control typically refers to general aspects of perceived control, such as mastery or constraints, while situational control highlights time-varying, state-like perceived control over specific experiences11,12. Indeed, past work using the NSDE identified small, positive associations between trait-like global control and state-like daily stressor control, further demonstrating the correlated, but distinct types of control11. Within situational control, daily control beliefs refer to daily feelings of having the resources and ability to adapt to situations in everyday life28,29.

Only a handful of studies have examined global control15 and domain specific control14,27 in the context of daily stressor affective reactivity. Neupert and colleagues15 found that individual differences in global control—operationalized as personal mastery over events—were associated with decreased negative affective reactivity specifically to daily interpersonal stressors (arguments and avoided arguments) across all ages but did not find significant associations with other domains of daily stress (work, home, network). Diehl and Hay27 extended these findings from general control to daily personal control,although researchers examined the number of daily stressors rather than domain specific stressors, they found that people with low feelings of daily control (e.g., feeling they could control life in the past 24 h) exhibited more negative affective associated with daily stressors, compared to people with high feelings of daily control.

Only one study to date has examined whether situational control beliefs are associated with positive affective reactivity14. Koffer and colleagues14 found that higher daily control beliefs—defined as feelings of control over daily events—was related to smaller increases in negative or decreases in positive affective reactivity, respectively in an adult sample across nine weeks. Daily control beliefs refers to more general state-like aspect of control beliefs, and although previous research has highlighted important areas for how situational control indices (e.g., personal control over specific daily experiences) relate to affective reactivity, there has yet to be a comprehensive examination of associations between daily stressor domains, daily stressor control, and both negative and positive stressor-related affect.

Aligned with transactional models of stress, both daily control beliefs and daily stressor control may reduce affective responses to stress in similar ways—namely through increased perceptions of coping resources and decreased perceived threat30. Critically, although both daily stressor control and daily control beliefs are similar in that they refer to perceived control over more micro-level processes unfolding in day-to-day life (i.e., stressful situations or daily events), they remain distinct phenomena. As such, two important considerations remain. First, it is unclear whether and how perceived daily stressor control is associated with (1) positive stressor-related affect and (2) negative affective residue. Second, no studies to date have tested how perceived daily stressor control, control linked to a particular stressor, may modify stressor-affect associations across daily stressor domains and whether associations align with previous research using other situation-level control beliefs.

The current study

Using data from the third wave of the National Study of Daily Experiences (NSDE), we aimed to determine how daily stressor control modified associations between daily stressors and affect. We examine: (1) associations for arguments, avoided arguments, home, work, and network daily stressors and both negative and positive affective reactivity (same-day stressor-affect associations) and affective residue (lagged stressor-affect associations) and (2) tested whether stressor control mitigated affective reactivity and residue, evidenced by stressor-related changes in both negative and positive affect. Although NSDE is the premier dataset to examine daily experiences, no studies to our knowledge have examined daily stressor-affective reactivity or daily stressor-affective residue associations in the third wave of data. We anticipate that days when participants encounter daily stressors, they will exhibit more stressor-related affect (evidenced by increased negative affect and decreased positive affect), with the strongest associations being for interpersonal daily stressors (i.e., arguments and avoided arguments;13,15, Hypothesis 1). Additionally, in line with previous research on daily control beliefs14,15, we anticipate the within-person buffering effect of daily stressor control to be strongest for daily interpersonal stressors (arguments and avoided arguments) and negative affective reactivity (Hypothesis 2).

Method

Participants and procedure

This study utilized data from the NSDE III, the eight-day daily diary project of the Midlife in the United States Study (MIDUS; see www.wisc.edu for more information). The NSDE III is the third wave of the NSDE project that began in 1995 with data collection occurring approximately every ten years. The sample was originally recruited with random digit dialing and were asked to participate in the larger MIDUS study. Half of the sample was recruited for the daily diary subset of MIDUS. Ten years later (NSDE II), an additional sample of participants were recruited. Notably, although associations between daily stressors and other health-related outcomes in the NSDE III (e.g.,31), no studies to our knowledge have examined daily stressor-affect associations in NSDE III. Participants (n = 1263) completed approximately 20-min telephone interviews every day at the end of the day for eight consecutive days and responded to questions pertaining to phenomena such as daily life, affect, and physical health.

Measures

Negative and positive affect

Negative and positive affect were measured using an adapted measure of the positive and negative affect schedule20. Participants reported on 14-items for negative affect (e.g., angry, nervous, upset, frustrated) and 13-items for positive affect (e.g., happy, cheerful, proud, enthusiastic) using the question, “How much of the time today did you feel [emotion]?” from 0 (none of the time) to 4 (all of the time). Items were averaged so higher scores represent higher negative or positive affect. Reliabilities were calculated following methods detailed in Scott et al. (2020). Within-person reliabilities for NA and PA were 0.77 and 0.86, respectively, while between-person reliabilities for NA and PA were 0.97 and 0.99, respectively.

Daily inventory of stressful experiences (DISE)

The DISE32,33 was utilized to determine both the type of stressor reported and daily stressor control. Participants were asked if a specific type of negative event occurred in the past 24-h and were reported as 0 (no stressor occurred) or 1 (yes, a stressor occurred). For arguments, participants were asked, “Did an argument or disagreement occur in the past 24-h?” For avoided arguments, participants were asked, “Did anything happen that you could have argued about, but you decided to let pass in order to avoid a disagreement?” For work and home stressors, participants were asked, “did anything happen at [work or school]/[home] that most people would consider stressful?” For network stressors, participants were asked, “did anything happen to a close friend or relative that turned out to be stressful for you?” Examples of the types of stressors found in previous literature (e.g.,33) are as follows: (1) an argument may refer to a disagreement with a partner, (2) an avoided argument may be a disagreement a person could have had but they chose to let pass, (3) a work stressor may refer to an impending deadline or work demand, (4) a home stressor may refer to a financial problem or a pet event, and (5) a network stressor refers to an event that happens to another person in your social network.

Daily stressor control

If participants reported a stressor within that domain, they were asked about characteristics and appraisals of that daily stressor. Regarding the specific daily stressor, participants were asked, “How much control did you have over the situation?” and were asked to report from a scale of 0 (none at all) to 3 (a lot). Higher values indicated greater perceived control over that stressor. As such, this design created a conditional association between stressor exposure and daily stressor control similar to that of previous research7.

Covariates

Age34, gender (0 = women; 1 = men35), education (0 = less than high school, 1 = some college, 2 = Bachelor’s or more36), employment status37, marital status37, and depressive and anxiety symptomology (e.g.,38) were considered as covariates, given their potential relationships to daily stressor-affect associations and/or perceived control. Between-person stressor effects and day of study were also included to account for differential exposure39.

Data availability

Data and codebooks are publicly available on the Inter-university Consortium for Political and Social Research (ICPSR). Analytic code can be made available for appropriate use upon emailed request to the corresponding author.

Analytic plan

Two level multi-level models using SAS PROC MIXED v.9.4 (Sas40) were utilized given the nested structure of the data (days nested within persons) and to allow for time-varying associations between daily stressors and affect39. Models utilized maximum likelihood estimation to leverage all available data and an unstructured covariance structure given the small number of repeated days. Moreover, as models failed to converge with random slopes for all daily stressors (i.e., 5 random slopes and a random intercept) models were run with a random intercept only.

We ran two models for each aim, one for affective reactivity and one for affective residue. Affective reactivity refers to the time-varying slopes between current-day stressors and negative or positive affect, respectively. Given missingness on covariates, the analytic sample for models examining only affective reactivity was 1,005. Affective residue is operationalized as the time-varying slopes between previous-day stressors and negative or positive affect, after accounting for current-day stressors. For affective residue, the first day of the study was coded as missing. As affective residue models lose one day of data, analytic sample for models examining only affective reactivity was lower (n = 982). The intercept, then, is the level of negative or positive affect on days when no stressors were reported in the same-day and/or previous-day.

Hypothesis 1

To test Hypothesis 1, we regressed each daily stressor type at day d, on negative and positive affect, respectively. All daily stressors were included in the models simultaneously to account for the possible same day associations by each type of daily stressor and facilitate examination of stressor-specific control. Examining all daily stressors within the same model allowed for the examination of the effect of each daily stressor domain for negative and positive affect after accounting other daily stressors—thus contributing to the examination of unique associations between domains of daily stressors affect. When examining affective residue, we additionally regressed each daily stressor type at day d-1.

Hypothesis 2

To examine the moderating effect of daily stressor control on affective reactivity and residue, we included within-person centered stressor control (i.e., within-person deviation scores from person means) for each type of daily stressor at day d for affective reactivity models, and at day d-1 for affective residue models. Notably, the NSDE protocol imposes a contingency on stressor control whereby the prompt is only provided if a participant reports that type of stressor—data for stressor control on days when no stressor occurs is missing on all cases (missing not at random). As such, when stressor control is included in the model with stressor exposure, the main effect is representative of a change in stressor control on days when a stressor occurred (e.g., design-based moderation). When within-person daily stressor control is included within the models, the main effect of the daily stressor represents the effect of the daily stressor when stressor control is at each person’s average for that daily stressor and the increase in stressor control represents the incremental difference associated with increases or decreases in stressor control. We defined statistical significance at p < 0.05.

Discussion

The current study demonstrates that perceived stressor control modifies associations between daily stressors and affect in a sample of middle-aged to older adults (ages 43 to 91), but the moderating effect of control is selective. In support of hypothesis one, we extended previous research on daily stressors and stressor-related affect14,15 finding that most daily stressors have significant associations with same-day negative and positive affective reactions, indicative of affective reactivity, even after controlling for perceived control over daily stressors, with the exception of daily network stressors and positive affective reactivity. Only previous-day arguments showed significant associations with negative affect, indicative of negative affective residue. In line with hypothesis two, increased stressor control was related to dampened affective reactivity for arguments (negative and positive affect), avoided arguments (negative and positive affect), work-related stressors (negative affect only), and home related stressors (negative and positive affect). Contrary to hypothesis two, stressor control did not modify associations between previous-day daily stressors and negative or positive affect (i.e., affective residue) across any stressor domain. These results highlight the possible utility of perceived stressor control as a modifiable mechanism for interventions pertaining to daily stress and/or same-day affect, particularly for interpersonal and home related stressors.

Affective reactivity and daily stressor domains

Affective reactivity is associated with poorer health (e.g.,2–5). The current study extends the literature by disaggregating these associations by stressor type, as well as testing how associations operate for positive affect in analyses examining positive affective reactivity. Similar to previous research, and in line with previous our previous hypothesis, arguments were characterized by the largest increases in negative affect1,13.

Unique to the current study, however, we found significant associations for home related stressors—for both negative and positive affective reactivity. This is consistent with previous work suggesting that positive and negative affect, while related, are distinct from one another18,19. Although associations were similar in strength for both negative and positive affect, it is important to note that patterns of reactivity and residue across stressor types were asymmetric by affect valence (positive or negative), evidenced by the lack of associations for positive affective reactivity for home stressors. As such, results from the current study provide further evidence for examining both negative and positive affect as outcomes where possible. Indeed, in addition to differential correlates, changes in negative and positive affect may confer different risk for health and well-being and thus, both should be considered in future research.

Affective residue

Although affective residue was present for daily arguments and negative affect, no other associations between daily stressors and affective residue emerged, contrary to hypothesized associations and some previous associations7,8. Differences may be attributed to the inclusion of multiple types of daily stressors. Indeed, Witzel and Stawski7 focused on interpersonal daily stressors and did not include other domains of daily stress, and as such, other domains of daily stress may have accounted for associations. Additionally, differences in results from previous studies7,8 and the current study may be related to the use of a different wave of the NSDE (e.g., history effects,Wave 2: ~ 2005; Wave 3: ~ 2015. Importantly, it is unclear, and beyond the scope of the current study to determine, whether the daily arguments that occurred are qualitatively distinct across waves.

Interpersonal stressor control

We found that daily stressor control dampened the effect of interpersonal and home daily stressors on affective reactivity. These findings are in line with previous research on global and daily control15,27. Notably, both Diehl and Hay27 and Neupert and colleagues (2007) found that daily and global control beliefs dampened the association between interpersonal stressors and emotional distress. Our results echo these findings for interpersonal daily stressors and negative affective reactivity and extend these findings to situation-specific control for arguments. Indeed, on days when an argument or avoided argument occurred, there was a larger increase in negative affect when daily stressor control was lower, compared to higher. The current evidence suggests that control over interpersonal daily stressors may be a mechanism that can be utilized to promote short-term well-being. For example, an individual may report low feelings of daily control (e.g., I cannot control my day) but find that they can control one type of daily stressor (i.e., interpersonal).

The result that interpersonal daily stressors showed higher negative affect when daily stressor control was lower is additionally unsurprising given interpersonal stressors are the most common and distressing type of daily stressors7,13, and people tend to prioritize their control resources for interpersonal tensions as they grow older12. Moreover, the controllability of the interpersonal stressor may reflect other relationship characteristics (e.g., risk of dissolution), that could be considered mechanisms for these associations. Indeed, it may be that who is involved8 additionally modifies the effect of perceived stressor control. As such, future work may aim to disentangle who is involved in these interpersonal stressors and the associations between who is involved (spouses, children) and perceived control.

Home stressor control

Contrary to some previous literature (i.e.,15), but in line with others (i.e.,27), affective reactivity to home stressors were significantly dampened when daily stressor control was higher compared to lower. This may result from the comparison of studies with trait-level global control (e.g., mastery and constraint in15) compared to a daily locus of control (e.g.,27). Indeed, our results align with the daily indicators of non-specific control and allow us to make broader generalizations across types of control (e.g., broad vs. stressor specific). Moreover, home stressors refer to home demands or family responsibilities that are stressful. The control of these home stressors may signify the specificity of the stressor that falls under multiple domains,for example, having to pay a gas bill but not being paid until the following week may be considered more uncontrollable compared to a scheduling stressor between a ballet recital for a son at the same time as a karate event for a daughter.

Daily stressor control and affective residue

Despite hypothesized pathways, daily stressor control was not significantly related to affective residue. This is in line with other aspects of daily stress processes (e.g., who is involved8), but not all aspects (e.g., resolution status7) and highlights the complexity of characteristics within daily stress processes. It could be that affective residue reflects qualitatively and quantitatively different dimensions of stressors, and the benefits of control may be negated by factors that make the affective response of minor daily stressors persist. More work is needed to understand affective residue associated with daily stressors, and the appraisals of the daily stress process that serve as psychosocial resources against its impact in everyday life.

Limitations and future directions

Although the current study is a national sample of adults, the sample was middle and older-aged adults, as well as White and highly educated, limiting our ability to generalize to the entire U.S. adult population. Future work may need to replicate these associations in younger adults, racialized minority populations, and across socioeconomic backgrounds to confirm how daily stressor control may be related to affective reactivity and residue in samples that often report more and qualitatively different daily stressors13,41,42. Of note, as the sample was primarily middle-aged and older, findings may be contextualized to the specific cohort, or developmental period as research has identified changes in emotion regulation and appraisal strategies with age43. Further, individual differences in self-efficacy may have resulted in differential results,unfortunately, the NSDE does not include measures of self-efficacy, resulting in the inability to test these associations. Future work will need to disentangle how additional psychosocial resources may inform daily stressor control—affect associations. Finally, we only examined how one daily stressor characteristic may be related to affective reactivity and residue across daily stressors. Indeed, other characteristics of daily stress—who is involved8, resolution7—may work in tandem with daily stressor control to inform associations. Future work will need to disentangle the unique and interactive effects of different daily stressor characteristics to better understand variation in affective reactivity and residue.

The current study focuses on end-of-day assessments and, as such, causality between perceived feelings of control and affect (i.e., affective reactivity) cannot be established. Indeed, it is unclear, based on the study procedure, whether participants had higher negative affect in the day and this, in turn, was related to lower feelings of control when a stressor did occur. Additionally, although we have chosen to interpret findings based on a more resilience-style framework (e.g., more control is related to better affective responses), an equally plausible interpretation for the current findings given the study design is that lower daily stressor control is related to exacerbated affective reactivity and residue. As such, the lack of daily stressor control may have important implications for downstream health consequences.

Future work may aim to utilize event-contingent ecological momentary assessment (EMA) designs to disentangle directionality of associations. In addition to the inability to test causal patterning of associations, the current study only utilizes the third wave of the NSDE, limiting the ability to test longitudinal pathways and the potential of maturation impacting results. Although previous literature has tested how perceived stressor control may change over time over two waves11,12 and how affective reactions to daily stressors may change over time44, it will be pertinent for future work to additionally examine associations between daily stressor control and stressor-related affect in a longitudinal space to parse out differences between history, cohort, and maturation effects.

Implications and conclusions

The current study extends existing literature on control and daily stressor reactions by examining how daily stressor control modified associations between five types of daily stressors and both negative and positive stressor-related affect. Although there was limited evidence for daily stressor control modifying affective residue to daily stressors, higher daily stressor control contributed to dampened affective reactivity to three types of daily stressors—in particular interpersonal daily stressors and home stressors. Findings suggest that daily stressor control may be an avenue for intervention to decrease daily affective reactivity for interpersonal and home-related daily stressors. Although more research is needed to generalize findings in a diverse sample of adults across time, these findings may be particularly useful for people who report chronic interpersonal or home stressors to decrease daily stressor pile-up9. Adults may work to promote control over their daily stressors when they can, and clinicians may utilize this research to promote strategies related to improving feelings of control across situations. In sum, identifying ways to facilitate control across days may have value for promoting same-day emotional wellbeing.
