MSAFNet: A multi-scale attention fusion network for automated neonatal pain facial expression recognition with clinical validation
Authors: Yan Zhang, Xuehan Qiu, Yongqiao Shang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate, timely pain assessment in neonates remains a stubborn yet critical challenge in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), where conventional observational scales suffer from subjectivity, intermittent use, and uneven inter-rater reliability. We propose a Multi-Scale Attention Fusion Network (MSAFNet) for automated neonatal pain facial expression recognition, and validate it in an authentic clinical setting. Drawing on two public collections together with a prospectively gathered NICU corpus, we assembled 9,485 annotated facial images from 153 neonates; after strict subject-level partitioning, training-set-only augmentation (MixUp, CutOut, plus conventional geometric and photometric perturbations) expanded the training pool to over 25,000 samples while the validation and test sets remained un-augmented. The MSAFNet architecture combines a modified lightweight ResNet-34 backbone with landmark-guided local feature branches, multi-scale parallel dilated convolution pathways, channel-spatial dual attention, and an adaptive softmax-weighted fusion mechanism, targeting both subtle muscle contractions and holistic configurational change in neonatal faces. On the held-out test set MSAFNet reached 94.3% accuracy, 94.1% precision, 94.1% recall, 94.2% F1-score, and an AUC of 0.978, outperforming ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B0, and domain-specific baselines. A prospective clinical validation covering 62 neonates and 287 procedural pain episodes yielded substantial agreement with expert nurse consensus (Cohen’s Kappa = 0.84, ICC = 0.91), with a per-frame inference latency of approximately 0.3 s. Usability ratings from nursing staff were favourable, supporting the role of MSAFNet as a supplementary monitoring aid rather than a replacement for clinical judgement. Taken together, the findings suggest that MSAFNet can serve as a practical decision-support tool for continuous, objective neonatal pain assessment in real NICU environments.Keywords: Neonatal pain assessment, Facial expression recognition, Multi-scale attention fusion, Deep learning, Convolutional neural network, Clinical validationSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Neonatal pain, once dismissed as clinically insignificant due to the mistaken belief that neonates lacked the neurological maturity to perceive it, is now recognized as a critical concern in pediatric care1. Repeated or prolonged exposure to painful stimuli during the neonatal period has been linked to adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes, including altered pain processing, impaired cognitive development, and heightened stress reactivity later in life2. In neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), infants routinely undergo numerous painful procedures—heel lances, venipunctures, endotracheal suctioning—often without adequate pain management3. Accurate and timely pain assessment therefore constitutes the cornerstone of effective pain intervention, yet achieving this goal remains a persistent challenge in clinical practice.

Several observational pain assessment instruments have been developed over the past decades to guide bedside clinicians. The Neonatal Infant Pain Scale (NIPS) evaluates behavioral indicators such as facial expression, cry, and limb movement4. The Premature Infant Pain Profile (PIPP) incorporates both physiological and behavioral parameters alongside gestational age as a contextual modifier5. The Neonatal Pain, Agitation and Sedation Scale (N-PASS) extends the assessment framework to capture sedation states in addition to pain and agitation6. While these tools have contributed meaningfully to clinical awareness, their practical deployment reveals notable shortcomings. Most fundamentally, they depend on subjective human judgment, and inter-rater reliability varies considerably across assessors with different levels of training and experience7. Nurses working under high patient-to-staff ratios may perform assessments infrequently, creating temporal gaps during which pain episodes go undetected. Moreover, the discrete and periodic nature of manual scoring inherently conflicts with the fluctuating, episodic character of neonatal pain expression8. These limitations collectively point to an unmet need for objective, continuous, and automated pain monitoring approaches.

The rapid maturation of computer vision and deep learning technologies over the past decade has opened promising avenues for automated facial expression recognition (FER). Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) demonstrated breakthrough performance on adult FER benchmarks, with architectures such as VGGNet and ResNet achieving near-human accuracy on curated datasets9. Attention mechanisms and multi-scale feature extraction strategies further improved the capacity of deep models to capture subtle and spatially distributed facial action units10. Transfer learning, whereby models pre-trained on large-scale general-purpose image datasets are fine-tuned for domain-specific tasks, has proven particularly effective in mitigating the data scarcity problem that afflicts many medical imaging applications11.

Translating these advances to neonatal pain recognition, however, introduces a distinct set of difficulties that should not be underestimated. Neonatal faces differ substantially from adult faces in morphological proportions, skin texture, and the repertoire of expressed action units. Several pioneering studies attempted to apply deep learning to neonatal pain detection using datasets such as the COPE and iCOPE collections, yet these datasets contain only a limited number of subjects and images, raising serious concerns about model generalizability12. Most reported systems were validated exclusively on held-out partitions of the same dataset rather than on independent clinical cohorts, making it difficult to assess real-world performance13. The class imbalance between pain and non-pain samples—a reflection of the fact that pain events constitute a minority of recorded frames in continuous monitoring—further complicates model training and evaluation. Additionally, environmental variability in clinical settings, including inconsistent lighting, partial facial occlusion by medical devices, and varying camera angles, degrades recognition accuracy in ways that controlled laboratory conditions fail to replicate14. A careful review of existing literature reveals that very few studies have attempted systematic clinical validation, and the gap between algorithmic promise and bedside utility persists.

Why, then, do off-the-shelf deep architectures stumble on this task? Three architectural mismatches are worth naming explicitly. First, neonatal facial muscles produce action units whose spatial extent—brow bulge, nasolabial furrow deepening, lid compression—is much smaller relative to the face than in adults, so networks with fixed early-stage receptive fields such as standard ResNet-50 or EfficientNet-B0 tend to smear these micro-movements into adjacent regions before any attention layer can exploit them. Second, the head-to-face ratio in neonates shifts the spatial distribution of informative cues (the forehead occupies a disproportionate share while the lower face is compressed), a geometry that breaks the implicit priors of pretrained backbones trained on adult face corpora. Third, most modern classifiers treat all feature channels uniformly at a single scale, which is poorly matched to a task where coarse configurational shifts and fine-grained muscle contractions contribute jointly and unequally from image to image. These three points jointly motivate a design that explicitly couples multi-scale receptive fields, landmark-guided regional branches, and adaptive attention—the three pillars of MSAFNet.

Against this backdrop, the present study is motivated by both clinical necessity and technological opportunity. Our primary objective is to design, implement, and rigorously validate a deep learning-based neonatal pain facial expression recognition system that can function reliably within authentic NICU environments. Rather than pursuing incremental improvements on benchmark metrics alone, we prioritize clinical applicability and seek evidence that automated recognition can complement—and potentially enhance—existing nursing assessment workflows.

This work offers three principal contributions. First, and most importantly, we release a prospectively collected neonatal clinical pain facial expression corpus gathered in a working NICU under institutional ethical approval, with dual-nurse NFCS annotation and a Cohen’s Kappa of 0.87 — an asset whose scarcity has long been the dominant bottleneck in this field15. Second, we propose MSAFNet, a multi-scale attention fusion network that integrates landmark-guided local texture features and global structural representations of neonatal faces, enabling the model to attend selectively to pain-relevant regions while tolerating spatial and photometric variation. Third, going beyond benchmark reporting, we carry out a structured clinical deployment in which the system operates alongside conventional nurse-administered assessments, providing comparative evidence on recognition accuracy, temporal responsiveness, and bedside feasibility — a step that most prior neonatal FER studies have not attempted.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews related work in neonatal pain assessment and facial expression recognition. Section III details the proposed MSAFNet architecture and the dataset construction methodology. Section IV presents the experimental setup, training procedures, and benchmark evaluation results. Section V describes the clinical validation protocol and analyzes the outcomes of bedside deployment. Section VI discusses the implications, limitations, and future directions of this research, and Section VII concludes the paper.

Theoretical foundations and technical background

Neonatal pain assessment theory and facial coding systems

Nociceptive pathways in neonates, though functionally immature, are sufficiently developed by the late gestational period to transmit and process painful stimuli16. Painful experiences trigger a cascade of physiological responses—elevated heart rate, increased cortisol secretion, decreased oxygen saturation—alongside observable behavioral manifestations, of which facial expression is widely regarded as the most sensitive and specific indicator17. This dual-channel response forms the theoretical basis upon which bedside pain assessment instruments have been constructed.

The Neonatal Facial Coding System (NFCS) provides the most granular behavioral framework for characterizing pain-related facial movements. Developed from the broader Facial Action Coding System adapted for infant morphology, NFCS isolates discrete facial action units (AUs) that occur with high consistency during painful procedures18. These include brow bulge (lowering and drawing together of the eyebrows), eye squeeze (tight closing of the eyelids), nasolabial furrow deepening, open mouth posture, horizontal mouth stretch, and chin quiver. Despite their structured design, these instruments remain fundamentally constrained by human observational capacity. Scoring requires trained personnel to watch the infant closely, often during brief procedural windows, and research has shown that inter-rater agreement fluctuates substantially depending on assessor experience, fatigue, and the ambiguity of borderline expressions20. The temporal resolution of manual assessment is inherently coarse—clinicians typically record a single score per event rather than tracking the dynamic evolution of facial movements. These reliability and granularity limitations motivate the development of computational approaches capable of continuous, objective facial action unit detection, a goal that the recognition system proposed in this study directly addresses.

System design and model construction

Dataset construction and preprocessing strategy

The scarcity of publicly available neonatal pain expression data has long constrained progress in this domain. To address this bottleneck, we assembled a composite dataset drawing from two established public collections and a newly collected clinical corpus. The COPE dataset, comprising static images of neonates captured during heel lance procedures, served as our primary public benchmark29. We further incorporated samples from the iCOPEvid video dataset, which provides temporally richer recordings of procedural pain episodes30. To supplement these relatively small collections, we conducted prospective data acquisition in the NICU of a partnering tertiary hospital over a six-month period. The study protocol received approval from the institutional ethics review board, and written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians prior to enrollment. Recordings were captured using a bedside-mounted high-definition camera (1920 × 1080, 30 fps) positioned to minimize interference with routine care. Two certified neonatal nurses independently annotated each frame as pain or non-pain following NFCS criteria, and only frames with concordant labels were retained; inter-rater agreement reached a Cohen’s kappa of 0.8731. As presented in Table 1, the raw dataset contains 9,485 annotated samples from 153 neonates, with a moderate class imbalance favouring non-pain instances. The order of operations for partitioning and augmentation was handled carefully to safeguard the validity of the reported accuracy. The dataset was first partitioned at the subject level—no infant’s frames appeared in more than one split—into training (70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) subsets. Only after this partitioning did we apply data augmentation, and augmentation was applied exclusively to the training set; the validation and test sets retained their original, un-augmented frames throughout. For video-derived samples (iCOPEvid and clinical recordings), a minimum inter-frame interval of 1 s was enforced during sampling to prevent near-duplicate frames of the same subject from inflating perceived performance. The preprocessing pipeline began with face detection using a multi-task cascaded convolutional network (MTCNN), followed by affine alignment based on detected eye coordinates to standardise facial orientation32. Detected face regions were cropped and resized to 224 × 224 pixels. We then applied Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalisation (CLAHE) rather than plain histogram equalisation, so as to better handle the considerable illumination variability of NICU environments—overhead phototherapy lamps and ambient monitor glow produce uneven lighting across the infant’s face, and CLAHE controls local contrast without amplifying noise in uniformly bright regions.

To expand the effective training set and bolster generalization, we employed a multi-pronged augmentation strategy. Geometric transforms included random horizontal flipping, rotation within 15 degrees, and slight scale jittering. Photometric perturbations—brightness, contrast, and saturation jittering—simulated the variable lighting conditions encountered clinically. Beyond these, we applied MixUp (synthetic samples via linear interpolation of image-label pairs) and CutOut (random rectangular patch masking). Post-augmentation, the training pool expanded to over 25,000 samples with a near-balanced class distribution, as reflected in the final row of Table 1; the validation and test sets were unchanged from the original partition.

Facial landmark detection and feature extraction network design

Accurate facial landmark localization is a prerequisite for region-specific feature extraction, yet neonatal faces pose particular challenges that adult-oriented detectors handle poorly. Compared to adult facial geometry, neonates exhibit proportionally larger foreheads, smaller and less defined nasal bridges, and more compact inter-ocular distances—differences that degrade the accuracy of standard landmark models trained predominantly on adult data33. To accommodate these morphological particularities, we fine-tuned a lightweight landmark regression network on our annotated neonatal dataset, predicting 49 keypoints that densely cover the brow, periorbital, nasal, and oral regions. The fine-tuning process adjusted anchor ratios and spatial priors to reflect neonatal proportions, yielding a normalized mean error reduction of approximately 18% relative to the unmodified pretrained detector.

Two practical questions deserve direct answers here: how were the 49 neonatal keypoints defined, and where did the ground truth come from. Because no large-scale neonatal landmark corpus was available, two trained annotators—one paediatric nurse with five years of NICU experience and one medical imaging specialist—manually labelled 49 anatomically defined keypoints on a subset of 2,400 neonatal faces, following a keypoint schema adapted from the 68-point adult template by removing landmarks in regions that collapse in neonates (e.g., fine jawline detail) and by densifying the brow and periorbital zones most relevant to NFCS action units. Each image was independently annotated by both annotators; inter-annotator agreement, measured as the mean normalised point-to-point error, was 2.7% of inter-ocular distance, which was considered acceptable. Discrepancies above a 5% threshold were adjudicated by a senior neonatologist. The resulting labels were used to fine-tune the landmark regression network, with anchor ratios and spatial priors adjusted to reflect neonatal proportions.

The detected landmarks serve a dual purpose: they guide the affine alignment described in Sect. 3.1 and, more critically, they define four anatomically meaningful regions of interest (ROIs) corresponding to the NFCS action unit zones—the brow region (capturing brow bulge), the periorbital region (eye squeeze), the nasolabial region (furrow deepening), and the oral region (mouth opening, horizontal stretch, and chin quiver). Cropping these ROIs from the aligned face image produces four local patches that feed into dedicated local feature branches alongside the global face input.

The backbone of our feature extraction module is a modified ResNet-34 architecture, chosen for its favorable balance between representational depth and computational efficiency34. We reduced the initial convolutional kernel from 7 × 7 to 3 × 3 and removed the first max pooling layer—modifications that preserve fine spatial detail at early stages, which we found essential for capturing subtle textural cues in neonatal skin and musculature. The network comprises four residual stages producing feature maps at spatial resolutions of 56 × 56, 28 × 28, 14 × 14, and 7 × 7, with channel dimensions of 64, 128, 256, and 512 respectively. Depthwise separable convolutions replace standard convolutions in the first two stages to lower parameter count without appreciable accuracy loss, an adaptation informed by mobile network design principles35.

For local feature extraction, each ROI patch is resized to 64 × 64 and processed through a shallow three-layer convolutional sub-network that shares architectural style with the early stages of the backbone but maintains independent weights. This separation allows local branches to specialize in region-specific discriminative patterns—for instance, the periorbital branch learns filters sensitive to lid compression gradients, while the oral branch captures lip contour deformations. The four resulting local feature vectors are concatenated with the global feature vector extracted from the backbone’s final stage, forming a composite representation that encodes both holistic facial configuration and fine-grained regional dynamics36.

Multi-scale attention fusion classification model for pain expression

A rigid fusion scheme would treat all scales uniformly—an assumption we found inadequate during preliminary experiments. Pain is clinically a continuum, and we acknowledge openly that a binary classification head cannot fully capture that continuum. The choice still merits an explicit defence, because anything less would understate the gap between what MSAFNet currently does and what graded scales such as PIPP or N-PASS deliver. Our position is that MSAFNet, in its present form, is not intended to replace those scales — it is intended to sit alongside them as a continuous trigger. Three considerations shaped this design. First, the operationally salient decision at the bedside is binary in practice: a nurse either initiates a formal NFCS or PIPP assessment, or does not; a reliable trigger that flags candidate pain frames preserves the granularity of the downstream tool while expanding its temporal coverage. Second, the softmax output of the binary head is itself a continuous probability score between 0 and 1, and we expose this score (not only the thresholded label) in the deployed system, so that nurses can read it as a coarse intensity proxy without committing the model to discrete pain grades it was never trained to estimate. Third, although richer ordinal supervision would have been preferable, our corpus carries only binary NFCS-derived labels at present; forcing an ordinal head onto binary ground truth would manufacture pseudo-grades rather than learn them. We treat the binary formulation as a deliberate scoping decision rather than a theoretical commitment, and we revisit its consequences — together with concrete routes for moving to graded estimation — in Section V.

Comparative experiments and ablation analysis

To contextualise the performance of MSAFNet, we benchmarked it against eight representative methods under identical data splits and preprocessing conditions. The comparison pool spans traditional machine learning pipelines—SVM classifiers paired with LBP and HOG descriptors—and several deep architectures: VGGNet-16, ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B0, MobileNetV3, a Swin-Transformer-Tiny backbone (included specifically to contrast CNN multi-scale fusion with transformer-based global-context modelling, an architectural question that remains actively debated for fine-grained tasks on modestly sized datasets), and the neonatal-specific N-CNN model proposed by Zamzmi et al.44. All deep models were initialised with ImageNet-pretrained weights and fine-tuned on our dataset using the same training protocol described in Sect. 4.1. Table 5 reports the complete results.

As shown in Table 5, traditional SVM-based approaches lag substantially behind all deep learning methods, confirming that handcrafted descriptors fail to capture the nuanced textural and configurational cues in neonatal pain expressions. Among deep models, ResNet-50 and EfficientNet-B0 perform competitively, yet MSAFNet surpasses both by margins of 3.1% and 3.7% in accuracy respectively. Comparison with the Swin-Transformer-Tiny baseline is worth a separate comment: it reaches 92.4% accuracy—the strongest among non-MSAFNet models—showing that global self-attention is genuinely competitive on this task, yet the CNN-based multi-scale fusion still leads by 1.9 accuracy points while using fewer parameters. Our reading of this gap is that on small-to-medium datasets where the discriminative signal lives in fine-grained regional cues, the inductive biases baked into convolutional multi-scale receptive fields and region-aware attention remain hard to match with global self-attention alone; transformer backbones can close that gap, but typically at the cost of more parameters and more training data than our corpus offers. The improvement over ResNet-50 is especially pronounced in recall—a 4.6%-point gain—suggesting that the multi-scale attention mechanism recovers subtle pain instances that single-scale architectures miss45. Clinical application validation and nursing effect assessment

Beyond benchmark evaluation, a system intended for bedside deployment must demonstrate agreement with expert clinical judgment under real operating conditions. We therefore designed a prospective validation study conducted in the NICU of the partnering hospital over a three-month period. Eligible neonates were those admitted with gestational ages between 28 and 42 weeks who underwent at least one routine painful procedure (heel lance, venipuncture, or nasogastric tube insertion) during the observation window. Neonates with congenital facial anomalies, heavy facial occlusion by medical devices, or sedation were excluded. A total of 62 neonates (287 procedural pain episodes) met the inclusion criteria.

During each procedure, the MSAFNet system processed the live camera feed at roughly 3 frames per second (one inference every ≈ 0.3 s) and produced frame-level pain probabilities, which were then aggregated into event-level classifications as follows. A fixed temporal window spanning the procedure plus a 30-second tail was defined; within this window, an episode was classified as a pain event if at least 30% of the evaluated frames returned a pain probability above 0.5. The 30%-of-frames threshold was chosen after a sensitivity sweep on the validation set over {10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%}; the 30% setting gave the best Youden-J trade-off between event-level sensitivity and specificity. Simultaneously, two senior paediatric nurses—each with over eight years of NICU experience—independently scored each episode using the NFCS protocol, blinded to the system output, and a third nurse adjudicated discordant cases. Agreement between the system and the consensus nurse rating was quantified using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient for categorical concordance and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC, two-way mixed model, absolute agreement) for ordinal pain intensity scores derived from aggregated prediction confidence48. The results in Table 7 indicate that the system achieved substantial to almost perfect agreement with expert assessment across all subgroups, with a full-cohort Kappa of 0.84 and ICC of 0.9149. Performance was slightly lower for preterm neonates (Kappa = 0.81), attributable to the more attenuated and less stereotyped facial responses characteristic of earlier gestational ages—a finding consistent with prior clinical observations that preterm infants display dampened behavioral pain reactivity. Across stimulus types, heel lance episodes yielded marginally higher agreement than venipuncture, likely because the former elicits more pronounced and sustained facial responses.

From a workflow integration perspective, the system produced a classification output within approximately 0.3 s per frame on the deployed bedside hardware (a single NVIDIA RTX 3060, 12 GB). The meaning of this latency figure warrants clarification. At ≈ 3.3 FPS the system is not “real-time” in the sense of matching a 30-FPS video stream frame-for-frame; rather, it operates in a near-real-time monitoring regime that is more than adequate for clinical alert generation during procedural pain episodes, which typically unfold over seconds, not milliseconds. The full MSAFNet has 27.4 M parameters and ≈ 4.8 GFLOPs per inference on the 224 × 224 input. Reducing this footprint through pruning, 8-bit quantisation, or knowledge distillation to support ≥ 15 FPS throughput on embedded hardware is an explicit target for future work50. Nursing staff who participated in a post-study usability survey (n = 12) rated the system favourably on interpretability and ease of integration, with 83% indicating they would trust the system as a supplementary monitoring aid during high-workload periods. Several nurses noted that continuous automated monitoring could flag pain episodes occurring between scheduled assessments—precisely the temporal blind spots identified in Section I. These findings collectively suggest that MSAFNet can function as a reliable clinical decision-support tool, complementing rather than replacing professional nursing judgment, and that embedding the system within existing NICU workflows is both technically feasible and practically valued by frontline caregivers.

Discussion

The experimental and clinical results presented in Section IV demonstrate that MSAFNet achieves competitive performance on benchmark evaluation and maintains strong agreement with expert nursing assessment in a real NICU environment. Several aspects of these findings warrant closer examination.

The superior accuracy of MSAFNet relative to single-scale deep architectures such as ResNet-50 and EfficientNet-B0 can be traced to two complementary design choices. First, the multi-scale parallel branches with varying dilation rates enable the network to simultaneously encode fine-grained muscle contractions—such as the subtle tightening around the nasolabial fold—and broader configurational shifts like overall facial distortion during high-intensity pain. Single-scale models must compromise between these spatial granularities, and our ablation results confirm that removing the multi-scale branches degrades recall more than precision, suggesting that coarse-grained features alone miss low-intensity pain episodes. Second, the channel-spatial dual attention mechanism directs representational capacity toward NFCS-relevant facial regions, effectively suppressing background noise from non-informative areas such as the forehead periphery or cheeks during rest states51. The ablation study revealed attention removal as the most damaging single modification—a finding that resonates with recent work showing attention-guided models outperform unguided counterparts on fine-grained visual classification tasks where discriminative cues occupy small spatial extents52.

Performance variation across data conditions warrants explicit discussion. The weighted cross-entropy loss and the augmentation pipeline together mitigated class imbalance effectively on average, yet recognition accuracy dropped by approximately 3–4% on the subset of test images exhibiting severe illumination artefacts, especially those captured under active phototherapy lamps. Partial facial occlusion by nasal CPAP interfaces or adhesive sensor leads also degraded performance, accounting for a disproportionate share of false negatives (see Fig. 10). To quantify how the degradation scales, a controlled robustness experiment was conducted: random rectangular masks covering 10%, 20%, 25%, 30%, 40%, and 50% of the facial bounding box were overlaid on all test images, and accuracy was re-measured. Figure 8 plots the result.

As Fig. 8 shows, MSAFNet holds accuracy above 90% up to roughly 25% occlusion, after which performance degrades more steeply; beyond 40% the system becomes unreliable. The variant without CutOut trails the full model consistently and by a widening margin as occlusion grows, providing direct quantitative evidence that CutOut contributes specifically to occlusion robustness rather than to accuracy at large. These findings echo earlier observations by Brahnam et al.53 on similar data. Heavy occlusion (> 40% facial area) therefore remains an open problem that dedicated architectural solutions—facial inpainting, occlusion-aware attention masking, or multi-view fusion—will need to address.

For illumination specifically, although the CLAHE-based preprocessing already improved over plain histogram equalisation, fundamentally challenging conditions (direct phototherapy glare, near-darkness during night rounds) will likely require moving beyond visible-light sensing. Incorporating an additional near-infrared channel into the MSAFNet front-end and fusing it with the RGB stream is, in our view, the most concrete methodological direction for handling NICU lighting failure modes; we plan to prototype this as a parallel branch operating at a coarser spatial scale, since infrared detail is typically lower-frequency than visible-light texture, before deciding whether the fusion should happen at the feature level or the decision level.

A further limitation of the present design is that MSAFNet currently reasons over independent static frames. Pain is not static—it builds, peaks, and decays with a temporal signature that a per-frame classifier simply cannot see. A natural architectural extension is to append a bidirectional LSTM or a memory-augmented attention module to the fused feature sequence, so that consecutive frames can inform one another and short-term facial dynamics can be exploited. We expect this matters more for low-amplitude preterm responses than for full-term high-intensity episodes, since in the former case the trajectory itself often carries more signal than any single instant. Prototyping this temporal extension is an immediate next engineering step.

The clinical validation findings are among the more meaningful contributions of this work. A Cohen’s Kappa of 0.84 with expert consensus places the system in the “almost perfect agreement” range, and the ICC of 0.91 suggests strong quantitative concordance on pain intensity estimation54. That said, the small number of discordant cases merit analysis. Most disagreements occurred with preterm neonates displaying ambiguous, low-amplitude facial responses—instances where even the two human raters initially disagreed before adjudication. This pattern highlights an inherent ceiling: when the ground truth itself is uncertain, no automated system can be expected to achieve perfect concordance. A handful of false positives were traced to non-pain facial movements such as yawning or startle reflexes that share morphological similarity with pain-related action units, an ambiguity that contextual information (e.g., temporal proximity to a known painful stimulus) could help resolve in future iterations55.

From a clinical utility standpoint, the system’s real-time processing capability (0.3 s per frame) and the favorable usability ratings from nursing staff suggest genuine potential for workflow integration. Current NICU practice relies on intermittent manual assessments, often separated by hours, during which pain episodes may go entirely undetected56. A continuously monitoring system fills this temporal gap without adding to the documentation burden that already strains nursing resources. We envision the system functioning as an alert-generating overlay—flagging probable pain events for nurse verification rather than autonomously triggering interventions—which preserves clinical autonomy while increasing assessment frequency and objectivity.

Comparing these results with existing neonatal pain recognition studies contextualises the advancement. Zamzmi et al. reported accuracies in the range of 88–90% on the COPE dataset using CNN-based approaches but did not conduct clinical validation44. Sun et al. achieved comparable benchmark figures with a multi-task learning framework, yet their evaluation remained confined to retrospective dataset analysis without prospective clinical testing57. This work advances both fronts: a 94.3% test accuracy exceeding prior reported figures, and—critically—a structured clinical deployment providing agreement statistics with expert assessors, an element conspicuously absent from most prior publications in this domain.

Several limitations warrant mention. The clinical portion of the dataset was collected in a single tertiary-care NICU, and although the 78 neonates recruited there span both preterm and full-term populations, the cohort is demographically skewed toward East Asian ethnicities with Fitzpatrick skin types III–IV (Table 2). Whether MSAFNet retains its reported accuracy on infants with darker skin tones (Fitzpatrick V–VI), or whether the landmark detector generalises cleanly across hospitals with different camera hardware, NICU lighting setups, and care protocols, cannot be answered by the present study. Multi-centre, multi-ethnicity validation therefore constitutes the most important empirical follow-up, likely more consequential than incremental architectural improvements. The clinical validation was conducted in one NICU with a specific camera setup; transferability to other units remains to be established through multi-centre trials58. The current binary pain/non-pain classification, while clinically actionable as a continuous trigger, lacks the granularity of graded pain intensity scoring that instruments such as PIPP and N-PASS provide; we list extension to ordinal or continuous pain estimation as the highest-priority piece of future work, not merely a “natural next step”, because that gap is the single largest barrier preventing MSAFNet from functioning as a true adjunct to graded scales rather than only an event flag. Two concrete routes are under consideration: (i) re-annotating a subset of the existing clinical corpus with full PIPP or NFCS-summed scores so that an ordinal regression head can be trained directly, and (ii) treating the existing softmax probability as a learning-to-rank signal aligned against nurse confidence ratings, which would not require new categorical labels. Finally, the system’s performance under conditions of severe facial occlusion (> 40%) and extreme lighting remains a weakness that targeted technical development must address before broader clinical adoption can be recommended.

Conclusion

This study set out to address a persistent gap in neonatal care: the lack of objective, continuous, and automated pain assessment at the bedside. Over the course of this work, we pursued three interconnected objectives—constructing a sufficiently large and diverse dataset, designing a recognition model capable of capturing the subtlety of neonatal pain expressions, and validating the resulting system in an authentic clinical environment. Each objective yielded concrete outcomes that, taken together, advance the state of the art in both technical capability and clinical readiness.

On the data front, we assembled an augmented neonatal pain expression dataset comprising over 25,000 annotated samples drawn from 153 neonates across two public collections and a prospectively gathered clinical corpus. Subject-level partitioning and rigorous dual-annotator labeling with high inter-rater agreement (Cohen’s Kappa = 0.87) ensure that the dataset supports meaningful generalization assessment rather than inflated within-subject accuracy. The combination of geometric, photometric, and advanced augmentation techniques—MixUp and CutOut in particular—proved effective in expanding training diversity and mitigating the class imbalance inherent to pain monitoring data.

The proposed MSAFNet architecture represents our primary technical contribution. Its design reflects a deliberate departure from applying generic image classifiers to neonatal faces without domain-specific adaptation. Three interlocking innovations define the model. First, the multi-scale parallel branches with graduated dilation rates capture facial expression features across spatial extents ranging from localized muscle contractions to holistic configurational changes. Second, the channel-spatial dual attention module, applied independently within each branch, steers representational focus toward NFCS-relevant facial regions—brow, periorbital area, nasolabial fold, and mouth—while suppressing uninformative background activations. Third, the adaptive softmax-weighted fusion mechanism allows the network to dynamically calibrate the relative importance of different scales on a per-image basis, accommodating the substantial variability in expression intensity and facial morphology observed across neonates. Additionally, the modified lightweight ResNet-34 backbone and the landmark-guided local feature extraction branches together ensure that the model remains attuned to the morphological particularities of neonatal faces, which differ markedly from the adult faces that dominate standard pretraining datasets.

Empirically, MSAFNet achieved 94.3% accuracy, 94.2% F1-score, and an AUC of 0.978 on the held-out test set, outperforming all compared methods including both traditional machine learning pipelines and contemporary deep architectures—the transformer-based Swin-Tiny baseline included. The ablation analysis confirmed that each proposed component contributes meaningfully, with the attention module exerting the strongest individual effect and synergistic gains emerging from their combination.

Perhaps more consequential than benchmark metrics, however, is the clinical validation. The prospective study involving 62 neonates and 287 procedural pain episodes in a working NICU demonstrated substantial agreement between system output and expert nurse consensus, with a Cohen’s Kappa of 0.84 and an ICC of 0.91. The system maintained robust performance across gestational age groups and stimulus types, and its sub-second processing latency confirmed suitability for real-time monitoring. Nursing staff endorsed its usability and expressed willingness to adopt it as a supplementary assessment aid—a practical endorsement that technical papers in this field have rarely sought or reported.

From a theoretical perspective, this work establishes that attention-guided multi-scale feature fusion, when coupled with domain-adapted facial landmark detection and region-specific feature extraction, provides an effective computational framework for automated neonatal pain assessment. From a practical standpoint, the system offers pediatric care teams a viable tool for increasing the frequency, objectivity, and temporal coverage of pain evaluation without imposing additional workload.

We are nonetheless mindful of what remains unresolved. The dataset originates from a single institution, and demographic diversity is limited; multi-center validation across varied populations and NICU configurations is essential before broader clinical deployment. The binary classification paradigm, while practically useful, falls short of the graded pain intensity assessment that clinical decision-making ideally demands. Heavy facial occlusion continues to challenge the system, and extreme lighting conditions degrade accuracy in ways that current augmentation strategies only partially address.

Looking ahead, several research directions hold particular promise. Multimodal fusion—incorporating body movement analysis and physiological signals such as heart rate variability alongside facial expression—could improve robustness and enable pain assessment even when the face is partially obscured. Visible-infrared sensor fusion is an especially attractive preprocessing direction for NICU environments with volatile illumination. Extending the model to ordinal or continuous pain intensity estimation is, in our view, the most consequential next step, since it directly addresses the principal limitation of the binary head identified earlier and is what would let MSAFNet operate as a genuine adjunct to PIPP and N-PASS rather than only as an event flag. Adding temporal context through a BiLSTM or memory-augmented attention layer should capture the dynamic trajectory of pain episodes rather than treating each frame in isolation. Deployment on embedded edge devices would eliminate dependence on workstation-class hardware and facilitate portable bedside monitoring. Finally, large-scale, multi-centre randomised controlled trials evaluating whether automated pain monitoring translates into improved pain management outcomes and long-term neurodevelopmental benefits represent the ultimate validation that this line of research must pursue.
