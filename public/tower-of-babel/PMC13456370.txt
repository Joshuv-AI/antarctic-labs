Postsurgical Opioid Prescribing Among Injured Workers in Washington State: 2017–2020 Trends and Policy Effects
Authors: John R. Haight, Deborah Fulton‐Kehoe, Anjum Hajat, Joel D. Kaufman, Gary M. Franklin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe widespread adoption of longer duration opioid prescribing for acute pain management following surgeries has coincided with concerning increases in misuse, dependence, and overdose. We evaluated the impact of voluntary opioid prescribing guidelines and concurrent state pharmacy rules on prescribing trends following surgeries for work‐related injuries.MethodsWe analyzed claims data for surgical and postoperative care in the Washington State workers' compensation system, linked with prescription drug monitoring program data, from 2017 to 2020. We evaluated whether rates of long‐duration opioid prescribing (> 14 opioid days) or persistent opioid prescribing (3–6 months post‐surgery) decreased after the implementation of Washington State's postsurgical opioid prescribing guidelines and pharmacy rules (2018–2019), using an interrupted time‐series analysis, stratified by surgical severity.ResultsPrior to policy implementation, longer duration opioid prescribing was declining at a rate of 0.52 percentage points per month, on average, among all surgeries. Following the promulgation of guidelines and rules, this decline slowed significantly to 0.14 percentage points monthly (slope change: +0.38 percentage points; 95% CI: 0.11, 0.65). Similar decelerations were observed across surgical severity types. Persistent opioid use following surgery remained steady, around 6.8%.ConclusionsOverall declines in longer duration opioid prescribing continued steadily after policy implementation, however, Washington State's postsurgical opioid prescribing guidelines and rules were associated with a modest slowing of pre‐existing downward trends in longer duration opioid prescribing, counter to expectations. These findings suggest that voluntary guidelines may have had limited impact. More robust implementation strategies may be needed to further reduce longer duration postsurgical opioid prescribing patterns in a workers' compensation setting.

Introduction

Postsurgical opioid prescribing, while intended to manage acute surgical pain, is associated with considerable risk for prolonged opioid use beyond the expected recovery period, and approximately 6% of previously opioid‐naïve patients are prescribed opioids beyond 90 days after surgery [1, 2, 3, 4]. Given the risks associated with long‐term opioid prescribing, including dependence, overdose, and reduced functional recovery, policymakers have implemented prescribing regulations to mitigate opioid‐related harms [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13].

Federally, the 2016 CDC guidelines for opioid prescribing represented a landmark effort to curb opioid misuse and overdose deaths [14]. The guidelines recommend using the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration necessary, typically not more than 3–7 days for acute pain [14]. They emphasize the importance of nonopioid therapies and caution against the use of opioids for chronic pain, except in specific cases such as cancer treatment, palliative care, and end‐of‐life care [8, 14]. Studies have found that the CDC guidelines led to a decrease in opioid prescribing [15].

Within Washington State, several policy interventions were implemented to address opioid prescribing practices over the past decades [6, 9, 16]. More recently, the Washington State Department of Health convened a multidisciplinary task force with representatives from various medical authorities to develop standardized postoperative opioid prescribing rules [17]. These state rules established a consistent 14‐day maximum supply for postoperative opioid prescriptions [18, 19, 20, 21]. While prescribers could exceed this limit when clinically necessary, they were required to document their justification in the patient's medical record, balancing standardization with clinical judgment. These state rules were partly published in October 2018 and became effective for all providers in January 2019 [18, 19, 20, 21].

Alongside these state rules, the Dr. Robert Bree Collaborative, working with the Washington Agency Medical Directors' Group (AMDG), developed a supplemental addendum to Washington's 2015 opioid prescribing guidelines, published in late July 2018, which specifically focused on postsurgical prescribing [12]. The Bree Collaborative, a healthcare quality improvement initiative housed within the Washington State Health Care Authority, brings together stakeholders to improve healthcare quality and reduce costs across the state. The collaborative convened a multidisciplinary expert panel, including academic leaders, pain specialists, and surgical experts, to supplement the existing guidelines, and received endorsement from state health organizations and major healthcare institutions [12]. The collaborative stratified procedures based on expected recovery durations, suggesting ≤ 3, ≤ 7, and ≤ 14 opioid days depending on severity. For the national context, about half of the states have rules limiting initial opioid prescriptions to ≤ 7 days for acute pain [8].

Our study aims to evaluate the impact of Washington State's recent opioid prescribing guidelines and policies on reducing longer‐duration postsurgical opioid prescribing.

Methods

Study Design and Population

We conducted an interrupted time series analysis (ITSA) using workers' compensation data from 2017 through 2020 to assess the effectiveness of Washington State's postoperative opioid prescribing rules and the supplemental guidance on postsurgical prescribing published by the Bree Collaborative [22, 23, 24]. ITSA allows us to evaluate whether an intervention (in this case, the prescribing rules and guidance) led to changes in outcomes by comparing trends before and after implementation while accounting for pre‐existing temporal prescribing patterns.

The study population included Washington State Fund injured workers who underwent surgery between 2017 and 2020 because of their work‐related injury. The Washington State Fund is a state‐operated insurance program that provides workers' compensation (WC) coverage for Washington employees, ensuring they receive medical benefits and wage replacement if injured on the job. The State Fund covers approximately 75% of nonfederal workers in Washington State, with the remaining 25% covered by self‐insured employers [25]. Study participants were aged 18 years or older at the time of surgery.

Data Sources and Linkage

Our study used Washington State Department of Labor and Industries (L&I) administrative records, which include claims data alongside medical, hospital, and pharmacy billing information. All surgeries were identified using L&I records. Where possible, we incorporated data from the Washington State Prescription Drug Monitoring Program (PDMP), which documents all Schedule II–V prescriptions outside of hospital and correctional settings. Records were probabilistically linked using patient name, address, and date of birth.

Surgery Identification

Common surgeries in workers' compensation injury claims were identified using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes using hospital and medical billing data. The Bree Collaborative categorized procedures into three types based on expected recovery duration: (1) Type 1 procedures (e.g., minor laparoscopic interventions) with expected rapid recovery warrant opioid use for ≤ 3 days when needed; (2) Type 2 procedures (e.g., ACL repair, rotator cuff repair) with medium‐term recovery warrant opioid use for ≤ 7 days when needed; and (3) Type 3 procedures (e.g., lumbar fusion, knee or hip replacement) with longer‐term recovery warrant opioid use for ≤ 14 days when needed. Our analysis treated each claimant's surgical procedure as a distinct observation. For claimants who underwent multiple procedures on the same day, only the procedure with the highest severity rating was included to avoid double‐counting outcomes. Claimants who had procedures performed on multiple separate days within a 6‐week window were excluded from the analysis, as their cases likely involve more complex surgical situations that may require more aggressive approaches to postoperative pain management. CPT codes are identified in Supporting Information S1: Table 1.

Policy Implementation

We defined three study periods based on the guidelines' implementation timeline. The Pre‐period (January 2017 through July 2018) represents the time before any guidelines were introduced. The Transition period (August 2018 through December 2018) covers the phase during which the state rules were gradually going into effect and Bree guidance documents were published. The Post‐period (January 2019 through December 2020) represents the time after all postsurgical rules were in effect and all guidance was published.

Opioid Prescribing Measurements

Opioid prescriptions were identified using NDC codes and therapeutic class codes (H30, H3A, H3N, H3U, H3X, and H3Z) as defined by L&I and CDC opioid definitions. Prescription durations in the first 6 weeks after surgery were determined using the fill dates and days' supply reported in pharmacy records from either L&I or PDMP within that timeframe. Opioid‐naïve patients were defined as those that had no opioids prescribed in the 90 days preceding their surgery.

Outcomes

To evaluate the effectiveness of the state rules that recommended limiting postsurgical opioid prescriptions to 14 days or fewer, we calculated the monthly proportion of surgeries that exceeded this limit. To evaluate adherence to the Bree guidelines, we established secondary outcomes that measured excessive opioid prescribing relative to recommended limits of the corresponding surgical procedure.

As presurgical opioid prescribing predicts increased opioid prescribing post‐surgery, our outcomes were analyzed in two populations: the full sample (all eligible surgical procedures) and an opioid‐naïve subset (no opioid prescriptions in the 90 days prior to surgery) [26].

Additionally, we investigated patterns of new persistent opioid use among previously opioid‐naïve injured workers after surgical procedures. Persistent opioid use was defined as those: (1) that did not have opioids in the 90 days prior to surgery, (2) had opioids prescribed within the first 6 weeks after surgery, and (3) continued to receive opioids 3–6 months postsurgically [1].

Statistical Analysis

We implemented an ITSA using ordinary least squares regression with Newey–West standard errors to account for heteroscedasticity and possible autocorrelation [22, 27]. Monthly proportions were calculated separately for the full sample and opioid‐naïve subset, with denominators being all eligible procedures of that severity level occurring in that month, and numerators being those exceeding the corresponding prescribing duration threshold. Models included quarterly indicators to adjust for seasonality, as surgeries may vary in both frequency and severity across quarters [22, 28]. The Cumby–Huizinga general specification test was used to identify appropriate lag times for autocorrelation adjustment [29]. Separate stratified analyses were conducted for each surgical severity category (based upon expected recovery time) and opioid status prior to surgery. An analysis testing persistent opioid use among the opioid naïve following surgery was also performed [1]. Finally, given that our study period overlapped with the COVID‐19 pandemic emergence, we performed a sensitivity analysis to test the robustness of our findings. Surgical volumes decreased substantially in April 2020; therefore, we excluded that month's data to assess whether this period influenced our overall results.

As the Bree guidelines and the state rules were published and effective within about 5 months of one another, this transition period was not included in the time series regressions [23, 30, 31, 32, 33]. The 5‐month span would not have provided sufficient data points to properly test the effects, resulting in an underpowered analysis [23, 24, 34].

Analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.2 and Stata version 18 [35, 36]. The study was approved by the Washington State and University of Washington Institutional Review Boards.

The overall cohort was mostly male (72.5%) with the largest age group being 55–64 years (34.6%), followed by 45–54 years (30.2%). Notably, the proportion of patients with evidence of opioid prescriptions in the 90 days prior to surgery varied by surgical severity: 26.4% for Type 1 (expected rapid recovery), 39.7% for Type 2 (expected medium recovery), and 44.3% for Type 3 (expected longer‐term recovery) procedures. In total, 13.4% of injured workers were not prescribed opioids in the 6 weeks following surgery. A breakdown of agreement between the PDMP and L&I pharmacy data sources can be found in Supporting Information S2: Table 2.

State Rule Thresholds

At baseline, approximately 36% of all surgeries exceeded the state prescribed duration threshold. Prior to the policy intervention, this proportion of all surgeries exceeding 14 opioid days of opioid prescribing was decreasing significantly by 0.52 percentage points monthly (−6.24 percentage points annually; 95% confidence interval [CI]: −0.76, −0.28 monthly), indicating an already existing downward trend in opioid prescribing practices (Figure 1).

Opioid prescribing trends among injured workers (2017–2020) stratified by surgery types and persistent opioid use.

We observed a modest immediate decrease in level in the post‐period (−1.58 percentage points); however, this level change was not statistically significant (95% CI: −5.4, 2.3). More notably, the policy implementation was associated with a significant flattening in the trend by 0.38 percentage points (95% CI: 0.11, 0.65). This resulted in a flattening of the previously steeper downward trajectory, with the post‐policy monthly trend slowing to approximately −0.14 percentage points per month (from the pre‐policy trend of −0.52 percentage points monthly).

Stratified Analyses by Procedural Severity Thresholds

At baseline (2017), 56.0% of Type 1 surgeries, 61.8% of Type 2 surgeries, and 70.7% of Type 3 surgeries exceeded their respective prescribing thresholds. All three categories demonstrated significant downward trends prior to policy implementation, with monthly declines of −0.77 percentage points for Type 1 (95% CI: −1.09, ‐0.45), −0.54 for Type 2 (95% CI: −0.83, −0.25), and −1.05 for Type 3 (95% CI: −1.31, −0.80), indicating ongoing reductions in prescribing that exceeds guideline recommendations.

Following both state rules and guideline implementation, an immediate decrease in level was observed for Type 1 (−3.18 percentage points) and Type 2 (−4.35), though neither change reached statistical significance; meanwhile, Type 3 showed a small, nonsignificant increase (+2.77). Consistent with the “All surgeries” findings, the most notable change after policy implementation was a flattening of these existing downward trends, reflected by slope changes of +0.34 percentage points for Type 1 (95% CI: 0.03, 0.65), +0.31 for Type 2 (95% CI: −0.05, 0.66), and +0.86 for Type 3 (95% CI: 0.54, 1.19). In practical terms, these trend changes reduced the steeper pre‐policy declines to approximately −0.43, −0.23, and −0.19 percentage points per month for Type 1, Type 2, and Type 3 surgeries, respectively, compared to their faster downward trajectories prior to the policy (Figure 1 and Table 2). By December 2020, the percentages of workers undergoing surgeries that were being prescribed opioids which exceeded the recommended guidelines were 30.6% for Type 1, 44.6% for Type 2, and 52.2% for Type 3.

Opioid‐Naïve Subset

A separate set of analyses were conducted for the opioid‐naïve group (n = 10,851), which was restricted to injured workers who had no opioid prescriptions in the 90 days preceding surgery, as recorded by L&I or PDMP. Overall, opioid prescribing that exceeded guidelines/rules was lower in the opioid‐naïve group (23.7% vs. 36.0% in the main cohort which included those prescribed opioids prior to surgery) yet both populations exhibited similar downward trends prior to policy implementation (Figure 2A). The policy's effect on change in level followed a similar pattern, with small and mostly nonsignificant level decreases (and one increase) alongside a statistically significant flattening of the downward prescribing trends. Type 2 surgeries among opioid‐naïve patients exhibited a clearer immediate drop than in the main analysis (Figure 2C). Contrary to our other findings, the declining trend for Type 3 surgeries in the pre‐period was offset by a slope increase of the same magnitude post‐policy. However, for both the full sample and opioid naïve subset, the overall takeaway is that while prescribing continued to decline post‐policy for most surgical groups, the rate of the decline slowed, reflecting a flattening of pre‐existing downward trajectories (Table 2).

Opioid prescribing trends after surgery among injured workers that did not have opioids prescribed to them in the 90 days prior to surgery (2017–2020), stratified by surgery types.

Persistent Opioid Use

At baseline, 6.7% of all injured workers undergoing surgery (95% CI: 5.48%, 7.92%) exhibited persistent opioid use. Prior to the guideline and rules' implementation, this outcome showed no significant upward or downward monthly trend (0.03 percentage points; 95% CI: −0.06, 0.12). Following guideline and rules' implementation, we observed a very small but statistically significant decrease in level (−0.98 percentage points; 95% CI: −1.87, −0.09). However, there was no statistically significant change in the post‐policy trend (−0.004 percentage points; 95% CI: −0.10, 0.10), suggesting the underlying trajectory of persistent opioid use did not shift appreciably (Figure 1E and Table 2).

Sensitivity Analysis

To address potential confounding effects from the COVID‐19 pandemic, we conducted sensitivity analyses that excluded April 2020 data, when surgery counts were most severely impacted [39]. Results remained consistent with our primary findings, suggesting that pandemic‐related disruptions did not substantially affect the observed policy impact on prescribing behaviors (Table 3).

Discussion

Our ITSA of postsurgical opioid prescribing guidelines and rules in Washington State found that while longer‐duration opioid prescribing had been declining prior to policy implementation, this downward trend continued at a somewhat slower pace following the introduction of the new policies. While we had anticipated a steeper decrease in longer‐duration opioid prescribing post‐policy, the guidelines and rules were, instead, associated with a significant flattening of the downward trend in longer‐duration opioid prescribing post‐policy.

It is important to note that these state‐level guidelines and rules followed the 2016 CDC Guideline for Prescribing Opioids for Chronic Pain which had a large national impact on prescribing practices [7]. The pre‐implementation declines seen in our analysis may partially reflect the broader shift in clinical practice spurred by the 2016 CDC guidelines. Although it is unclear why we observed a flattening of the pre‐existing downward prescribing trend, federal guidelines may have had a more profound impact initially and then may have diminished over time.

The Washington guidelines were of a voluntary nature, and the rules did not necessarily have mandatory requirements for implementation. Prescription records would typically only be reviewed if a complaint was filed against a prescriber. Additionally, the Bree Collaborative relied upon providers and leaders to disseminate the proposed guidelines. Without stringent enforcement mechanisms, adherence to the new guidelines and rules hinged on voluntary compliance among both providers and the Washington State Department of Labor and Industries workers' compensation system. As a result, although prescribing continued to decline overall, a substantial proportion of injured workers still received opioids for durations exceeding recommended levels (e.g., 52.2% among Type 3 surgeries in December 2020), indicating there are opportunities for additional provider, healthcare, and policy interventions. While financial incentives to reach safe prescribing thresholds do exist, more rigid strategies could include mandatory prior authorization requirements for opioids exceeding recommended limits following surgeries within the workers' compensation system.

Aside from the role of Federal guidelines, state, local, and institutional guidelines have proven effective in separate settings. Recent national workers' compensation data have seen general declines in overall opioid prescribing in recent years [40]. More recent state mandatory opioid‐limit laws in Massachusetts, for example, have been associated with reductions in postoperative prescribing [41]. In Michigan, a state‐wide quality collaborative was associated with a decrease in new persistent opioid use, and in overall quantity of oral morphine equivalents [42]. Research from Dartmouth showed that local, institutional, operation‐specific guidelines for surgeons reduced opioid prescribing by more than half [37]. It is likely in these circumstances that peer guidance has greater impact at the institutional level than the types of voluntary guidance exemplified by the Washington State and Bree Collaborative efforts.

Our study has several strengths. The interrupted time series design accounts for pre‐existing secular trends and allows for the examination of opioid prescribing following policy introduction. Our analysis uses comprehensive administrative data from a large sample of injured workers linked with PDMP data, improving measurement and generalizability. Stratification by surgical severity and opioid exposure allowed us to discern possible differential impacts of the guidelines across varying surgical severities.

The study has important limitations. The analysis did not address potential unintended or negative outcomes resulting from opioid prescribing policies, such as suboptimal pain control from healthcare providers' reluctance to prescribe opioids and accept patients requiring pain management [13]. Additionally, given that other local, state, and national initiatives were also ongoing during the study period, we cannot definitively attribute all prescribing changes to these specific state guidelines. We also note that the higher prevalence of presurgical opioid prescribing for more severe procedure types likely reflects a greater pain burden associated with more severe workplace injuries, therefore requiring more complex surgeries. Our definition of opioid‐naïve status was based on the 90 days preceding surgery rather than the date of injury; future work could explore whether redefining this window relative to injury date affects the composition of the opioid‐naïve subgroup.

Further research is needed to better understand the observed deceleration in prescribing reductions and to develop more effective implementation strategies. Multistate comparative analyses could help determine whether our findings represent a Washington‐specific effect or reflect broader national patterns. Studies incorporating healthcare provider interviews or surveys, which have been conducted for previous policy implementations in Washington State, would offer valuable insights into prescribing decision‐making and barriers to guideline adherence that quantitative analyses alone cannot capture [38]. Finally, expanding our analyses beyond workers' compensation to include private insurance and other payers would help provide a more comprehensive picture of postsurgical opioid prescribing patterns and guideline effects across Washington State.

Our findings indicate that while longer‐duration opioid prescribing has declined following Washington State's most recent guidelines and rules, the rate of decline has slowed, and a substantial proportion of injured workers continue to receive longer postsurgical opioid prescriptions. This is particularly concerning given the documented risks of long‐term opioid prescribing, including dependence, overdose, and impaired functional recovery [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Consequently, reliance on voluntary recommendations alone may not be sufficient, and more robust implementation strategies may be necessary to drive reductions in longer‐duration opioid prescribing among injured workers undergoing surgeries.
