Waves within a network of slowly time-modulated interfaces: time-dependent effective properties, reciprocity and high-order dispersion
Michaël Darche, Raphael Assier, Sébastien Guenneau, Bruno Lombard, Marie Touboul

Abstract
We consider wave propagation through a 1D periodic network of slowly time-modulated interfaces. Each interface is modelled by time-dependent spring-mass jump conditions, where mass and rigidity interface parameters are modulated in time. Low-frequency homogenisation yields a leading-order model described by an effective time-dependent wave equation, i.e. a wave equation with effective mass density and Young’s modulus which are homogeneous in space but depend on time. This means that time-dependent bulk effective properties can be created by an array where only interfaces are modulated in time. The occurrence of k-gaps in case of a periodic modulation is also analysed. Second-order homogenisation is then performed and leads to an effective model which is reciprocal but encapsulates higher-order dispersive effects. These findings and the limitations of the models are illustrated through time-domain simulations.

We consider the propagation of transient waves in a 1D

\rho_{h}(X)=\rho(X/h)

the periodic cell considered. Without loss of generality we may assume that

Given a source term

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rho_{h}(X)\,\partial^{2}_{TT}U_{h}(X,T)=\partial_{X}S_{h}(X,T)+F(X,T),

(1a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle S_{h}(X,T)=E_{h}(X)\,\partial_{X}U_{h}(X,T),

(1b)

with

the displacement field,

the stress field, and

the dimensional time variable. The system (

) is completed by initial conditions. Both

and

are continuous on the open intervals
(X_{n,1},X_{n+1,1})
, except at inner interfaces
X_{n,\ell}
.

For any function
g(X)
, we define the jump and mean operators across the interface
X_{n,\ell}
:

\left\llbracket g\right\rrbracket_{X_{n,\ell}}=g^{+}(X_{n,\ell})-g^{-}(X_{n,\ell}),\qquad\left\llangle g\right\rrangle_{X_{n,\ell}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(g^{+}(X_{n,\ell})+g^{-}(X_{n,\ell})\right),

(2)

where

g^{\pm}(X_{n,\ell})=\lim_{{\chi}\to 0^{+}}g(X_{n,\ell}\pm{\chi}).

(3)

For the
\ell
-th interface within a periodic cell, we introduce its rigidity
{K}_{\ell}>0
, compliance
{{C}_{\ell}=1/{K}_{\ell}}
, mass
{M}_{\ell}\geq 0
, and dissipation
{Q}_{C,\ell}\geq 0
and
{Q}_{M,\ell}\geq 0
. Across these interfaces, the fields satisfy the following time-dependent jump conditions:

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\left\llbracket V_{h}(\cdot,T)\right\rrbracket_{X_{n,\ell}}=\partial_{T}\left({C}_{\ell}(T)\,\left\llangle S_{h}(\cdot,T)\right\rrangle_{X_{n,\ell}}\right)+{Q}_{C,{\ell}}\left\llangle S_{h}(\cdot,T)\right\rrangle_{X_{n,\ell}},\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle\left\llbracket S_{h}(\cdot,T)\right\rrbracket_{X_{n,\ell}}=\partial_{T}\left({M}_{\ell}(T)\,\left\llangle V_{h}(\cdot,T)\right\rrangle_{X_{n,\ell}}\right)+{Q}_{M,{\ell}}\left\llangle V_{h}(\cdot,T)\right\rrangle_{X_{n,\ell}},\end{array}

(4)

where we have introduced the velocity field
V_{h}(X,T)=\partial_{T}U_{h}(X,T)
.

3.1
Two-scale analysis

Non-dimensionalisation.

We consider a reference wavelength
\lambda^{\star}
and we define

k^{\star}=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda^{\star}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\eta=k^{\star}\,h.

(5)

Arbitrary reference material properties are introduced:
E^{\star}
,
\rho^{\star}
that define the wave speed
c^{\star}=\sqrt{\frac{E^{\star}}{\rho^{\star}}}
. We define the non-dimensionalised space and time variables
x=k^{\star}X
and
t=k^{\star}c^{\star}T
, respectively, as well as the non-dimensionalised fields and forcing

u_{\eta}(x,t)=k^{\star}\,U_{h}(X,T),\qquad\sigma_{\eta}(x,t)=\frac{1}{E^{\star}}\,S_{h}(X,T),\qquad f(x,t)=\frac{1}{k^{\star}\,E^{\star}}\,F(X,T).

(6)

Upon defining

\alpha=\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\star}},\quad\beta=\frac{E}{E^{\star}},

(7)

the non-dimensionalised momentum equation and constitutive law become

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\alpha\left(\frac{x}{\eta}\right)\partial_{t}u_{\eta}(x,t)\right)=\partial_{x}\sigma_{\eta}(x,t)+f(x,t),\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\sigma_{\eta}(x,t)=\beta\left(\frac{x}{\eta}\right)\partial_{x}u_{\eta}(x,t).\end{array}\right.

(8)

Then the time-dependent jump conditions are recast as

\displaystyle\left\llbracket u_{\eta}(\cdot,t)\right\rrbracket_{x_{n,\ell}}=\eta\,{c}_{\ell}(t)\,\left\llangle\sigma_{\eta}(\cdot,t)\right\rrangle_{x_{n,\ell}},\qquad\displaystyle\left\llbracket\sigma_{\eta}(\cdot,t)\right\rrbracket_{x_{n,\ell}}=\eta\,\partial_{t}\left({m}_{\ell}(t)\,\left\llangle\partial_{t}u_{\eta}(\cdot,t)\right\rrangle_{x_{n,\ell}}\right),

(9)

with the non-dimensionalised interface parameters

{k}_{\ell}(t)=\frac{{K}_{\ell}(T)\,h}{E^{\star}},\quad{m}_{\ell}(t)=\frac{{M}_{\ell}(T)}{\rho^{\star}\,h},\quad{c}_{\ell}(t)=1/{k}_{\ell}(t).

(10)

No scaling is considered in the constitutive parameters, so that
\alpha
,
\beta
,
{c}_{\ell}
,
{m}_{\ell}
,
{c}^{\prime}_{\ell}
and
{m}^{\prime}_{\ell}
are all assumed to be
{\cal O}(1)
coefficients in the forthcoming asymptotic expansions, with prime notation denoting time derivative. It means that
{M}_{\ell}
and
{K}_{\ell}
must be of the same order of magnitude as
\rho^{\star}h
and
E^{\star}/h
, respectively and, importantly for the present work, should not vary too quickly.

Asymptotic expansion.

We assume that
0<\eta\ll 1
meaning that we are in the long-wavelength, or low-frequency, regime

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\llbracket u_{\eta}\right\rrbracket_{\ell}=\eta\,{c}_{\ell}(t)\,\left\llangle\beta(y)\,\left(\partial_{x}+\frac{1}{\eta}\partial_{y}\right)u_{\eta}\right\rrangle_{\ell},

Lemma 1

For any two 1-periodic functions

\left\langle\frac{dp}{dy}\,q\right\rangle=-\left\langle p\,\frac{dq}{dy}\right\rangle-\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\left\llbracket p\,q\right\rrbracket_{\ell}=-\left\langle p\,\frac{dq}{dy}\right\rangle-\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\left(\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket_{\ell}\left\llangle q\right\rrangle_{\ell}+\left\llangle p\right\rrangle_{\ell}\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket_{\ell}\right).

The first equality of the Lemma comes from a standard integration by parts, while the second comes from the useful property

Remark 1

Taking

\left\langle\frac{dp}{dy}\right\rangle=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket_{\ell}.

(19)

Leading-order effective model

Model derivation.

As is classical in multiple scale homogenisation, one can input the ansatz (

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial_{y}\left(\beta\partial_{y}u_{0}\right)=0,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\llbracket u_{0}\right\rrbracket_{\ell}={c}_{\ell}(t)\,\left\llangle\beta\partial_{y}u_{0}\right\rrangle_{\ell},

(20b)

\displaystyle\left\llbracket\beta\,\partial_{y}u_{0}\right\rrbracket_{\ell}=0,

for

Since

Consequently

u_{0}(x,y,t)=\overline{u}_{0}(x,t).

(23)

The overline indicates that the fields depend only on the slow space variable.

Then, the
{\cal O}(\eta^{-1})
terms in (

terms in (

) and

terms in (

) yield

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial_{y}\left(\beta\partial_{y}u_{1}\right)+\partial_{y}(\beta)\partial_{x}\overline{u}_{0}(x,t)=0,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\llbracket u_{1}\right\rrbracket_{\ell}={c}_{\ell}(t)\,\left\llangle\beta(y)\left(\partial_{x}u_{0}+\partial_{y}u_{1}\right)\right\rrangle_{\ell},

(24b)

\displaystyle\left\llbracket\beta(y)\,\left(\partial_{x}u_{0}+\partial_{y}u_{1}\right)\right\rrbracket_{\ell}=0,

(24c)

for
\ell=1,\cdots,N
. The governing equation (

) is an ordinary differential equation in

can be written as

u_{1}(x,y,t)=\overline{u}_{1}(x,t)+P_{1}(y,t)\,\partial_{x}\overline{u}_{0}(x,t),

(25)

where the

is solution of the following

({P}_{1}):\left|\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\partial_{y}(\beta(\partial_{y}P_{1}+1))=0,&\text{ on }\bigcup\limits_{\ell=1}^{N}(y_{\ell},y_{\ell+1})\\[-2.0pt]

One notes that

The
{\cal O}(\eta^{0})
terms in (

{\cal O}(\eta^{2})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\alpha(y)\,\partial_{t}u_{0})=\partial_{y}(\beta\partial_{y}u_{2})+\partial_{y}(\beta\partial_{x}u_{1})+\beta\partial^{2}_{xy}u_{1}+\beta\partial^{2}_{xx}u_{0}+f(x,t),

) together with (

\partial_{t}\left(\alpha_{0}(t)\,\partial_{t}\overline{u}_{0}\right)-\beta_{0}(t)\,\partial^{2}_{xx}\overline{u}_{0}=f,

\alpha_{0}(t)=\left\langle\alpha\right\rangle+\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}{m}_{\ell}(t),\qquad\beta_{0}(t)=\left\langle\beta(1+\partial_{y}P_{1}(\cdot,t))\right\rangle.

The effective mass density
\alpha_{0}(t)
is defined explicitly in terms of the physical parameters. One can look for similar expressions for

Integration of the PDE in (

) on

for some unknown functions

. The continuity of flux in (

It follows that

Averaging (

Finally, the jump condition satisfied by

\beta_{0}(t)=\left(\left\langle\frac{1}{\beta}\right\rangle+\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}{c}_{\ell}(t)\right)^{-1}>0.

3.3
Properties

In this section, we analyse some properties of the obtained leading-order effective model. The first important property is that, by solely modulating
interface properties
one gets an effective model whose effective
bulk properties
are time-dependent. This network of modulated interfaces can therefore be seen as a way to create time-varying metamaterials which are known to exhibit unusual phenomena such as time reflection and time refraction, unusual topology
[
30
]
, frequency conversion
[
36
]
, as well as unidirectional and parametric amplification
[
27
,
26
,
44
]
. We focus here on some of these unusual phenomena.

3.3.1
k
-gaps for a modulation periodic in time

Let us consider the case of a periodic time modulation of the interface parameters with period
\tau
. Consequently, the leading-order effective parameters (
36
) in the leading-order effective model (
35
) are also
\tau
-periodic in time. Using the space-domain Fourier transform, (
35
) then becomes:

\partial_{T}\left(\rho_{0}(T)\,\partial_{T}\hat{U}(T)\right)=-k^{2}E_{0}(T)\,\hat{U}(T),

(37)

where
\hat{U}
refers to the Fourier transform in space of
\overline{U}_{0}
, and
k
is the wavenumber. The Floquet theorem ensures that the displacement field is the product of a
\tau
-periodic function and a phase shift. Then one can write

\hat{U}(T)=U^{\sharp}(T)\,e^{i\omega T},

(38)

with
U^{\sharp}(T+\tau)=U^{\sharp}(T)
and
\omega
is the Bloch angular frequency evolving in the first Brillouin zone,
i.e

\omega\in[0,\frac{\pi}{\tau}]
.
For piecewise constant parameters
\rho_{0}
and
E_{0}
, the dispersion relation is easily found using (
37
) and (
38
) using e.g. the transfer matrix method. For smooth modulations, the derivation of the dispersion relation relies on plane wave expansions and is detailed in Appendix
B
.

In both modulations, gaps in wavenumber, i.e.
k
-gaps may occur. They are the time-counterpart of the usual gaps in frequency observed for materials periodic in space and constant in time. It is non-usual that this kind of behaviour, associated to a high dispersion, is encapsulated by a leading-order model. In our case, it is due to the fact that we performed homogenisation assuming a small parameter in space, but without any further assumption on the time. Moreover, contrary to a gap in frequency where the fields are evanescent, in a
k
-gap one gets amplification of the solution. We will see later on that this amplification is well described by the leading-order model.

3.3.2
Reciprocity

Regardless of the choice of modulation, (
35
) is a wave equation with time-dependent parameters. It remains invariant by changing
x
into

4.3.2
Effective equation in dimensionalised coordinates and energy analysis

From (

(62)

The wave equation is thus enriched by a fourth-order mixed derivative in space and time. We take the product of (

\partial_{T}U
and integrate over

Proposition 2
.

Let

{\cal E}(T)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho\left(\partial_{T}U\right)^{2}dX+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(E_{0}(T)+h^{2}\rho\,\mathcal{B}_{1}(T)\right)\left(\partial_{X}U\right)^{2}dX+\frac{h^{2}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho\,\mathcal{B}_{2}(T)\left(\partial^{2}_{XT}U\right)^{2}dX.

(63)

Introducing the power of external forces

{\cal P}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F\,\partial_{T}U\,dX,

(64)

then one has

\frac{d}{dT}{\cal E}(T)={\cal P}(T)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(E_{0}^{\prime}(T)+h^{2}\rho\,\mathcal{B}_{1}^{\prime}(T)\right)\left(\partial_{X}U\right)^{2}dX-\frac{h^{2}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho\,\mathcal{B}_{2}^{\prime}(T)\left(\partial^{2}_{XT}U\right)^{2}dX.

(65)

Three remarks arise from Proposition

•

the parameter
{\cal B}_{2}
in (

can be negative so that

•

if there are no body force

•

if

due to the power of external forces required to modify the interface stiffness; the sign of the right hand side in (

5
Numerical simulations

5.1
Numerical methods

Different types of time-domain numerical methods are used, depending on the model studied. In each case, one introduces a uniform mesh size
\Delta X=L/N_{X}
(where
L
is the length of the domain and
N_{X}
is the number of grid nodes) and a time step
\Delta T
. The latter depends on a Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) condition of stability. Lastly, given
Z(X,T)
, one denotes
Z_{m}^{n}
an approximation of
Z(X_{m}=m\,\Delta X,T_{n}=n\,\Delta T)
.

Microstructured model.

The momentum equation with Hooke’s law (
1b
) is written as a first-order velocity-stress system of PDEs. It is solved using a fourth-order finite difference scheme (ADER-4). This scheme is explicit in time and stable under the usual CFL condition
\max(c_{h})\,\Delta T/\Delta X\leq 1
where
c_{h}=\sqrt{E_{h}/\rho_{h}}
. The modulated jump conditions are discretised using an immersed interface method, as described in
[
21
]
. After each time step, a numerical integration is performed to obtain the displacement field

Leading-order effective model.

A second-order explicit finite-difference scheme is used to solve the scalar wave equation (
35
) and determine an approximation
U_{m}^{n}
of
\overline{U}_{0}(X_{m},T_{n})
. Second-order centered approximations of the operators lead to the scheme

\displaystyle U_{m}^{n+1}=U_{m}^{n}

(66)

\displaystyle+\frac{\rho_{0}(T_{n-1/2})\left(U_{m}^{n}-U_{m}^{n-1}\right)+\left(\frac{\Delta T}{\Delta X}\right)^{2}E_{0}(T_{n})\left(U_{m+1}^{n}-2\,U_{m}^{n}+U_{m-1}^{n}\right)+F(X_{{m}},T_{n})}{\rho_{0}(T_{n+1/2})}.

This scheme is explicit in time and second-order accurate in both space and time. It is stable under the usual CFL condition
\max(c_{0})\Delta T/\Delta X\leq 1
, where
\max(c_{0})=\displaystyle\max_{T}\sqrt{E_{0}(T)/\rho_{0}(T)}
.

Remark 3
.

With dissipation, the leading-order model involves a velocity-stress formulation given in Appendix
A
. The numerical modelling is described in Appendix
A.3
.

Second-order homogenised medium.

The PDE (
61
) can be recast as:

\partial^{2}_{TT}U-\left(\frac{E_{0}(T)}{\rho}+h^{2}\mathcal{B}_{1}(T)\right)\partial^{2}_{XX}U-h^{2}\mathcal{B}_{2}(T)\partial^{4}_{XXTT}U-h^{2}\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{2}(T)\partial^{3}_{XXT}U=\frac{1}{\rho}F(X,T).

(67)

To solve (
67
), the following quantities are introduced:

A(T)=\sqrt{\frac{E_{0}(T)}{\rho}+h^{2}\mathcal{B}_{1}(T)}\frac{\Delta T}{\Delta X},\qquad B(T)=\frac{h^{2}\mathcal{B}_{2}(T)}{\Delta X^{2}},\qquad C(T)=\frac{h^{2}\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{2}(T)\Delta T}{\Delta X^{2}}.

(68)

Then one obtains the following discretised equation :

\displaystyle\left(1+2B(T_{n})+C(T_{n})\right)\,U^{n+1}_{m}-\left(B(T_{n})+\frac{C(T_{n})}{2}\right)\,\left(U^{n+1}_{m+1}+U^{n+1}_{m-1}\right)

(69)

\displaystyle-2\,\left(1-(A(T_{n}))^{2}+2B(T_{n})\right)\,U^{n}_{m}-\left((A(T_{n}))^{2}-2B(T_{n})\right)\left(U^{n}_{m+1}+U^{n}_{m-1}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(1+2B(T_{n})-C(T_{n})\right)\,U^{n-1}_{m}-\left(B(T_{n})-\frac{C(T_{n})}{2}\right)\,\left(U^{n-1}_{m+1}+U^{n-1}_{m-1}\right)=\frac{\Delta T^{2}}{\rho}F(X_{{m}},T_{n}).

Setting
P(T)=(1+2B(T)+C(T))
and
Q(T)=-(B(T)+\frac{C(T)}{2})
, and considering Dirichlet boundary conditions (
U_{0}^{n}=U_{N_{X}}^{n}=0
), the implicit equations (
69
) can be rewritten as a tridiagonal system:

\text{tridiag}(Q(T_{n}),P(T_{n}),Q(T_{n}))\,\textbf{U}^{n+1}=\textbf{D}(\textbf{U}^{n},\textbf{U}^{n-1})+\frac{\Delta T^{2}}{\rho}\,\textbf{F}(T_{n}).

(70)

In (
70
),
\textbf{U}^{n}=(U_{1}^{n},\cdots,U_{N_{X}-1}^{n})^{\top}
,
D
combines the effects of previous time steps, and
\textbf{F}(T_{n})
is the vector of source terms. The system (
70
) can be solved efficiently using Thomas’ algorithm
[
39
]
. A Von-Neumann analysis with static coefficients yields a CFL condition of stability, which is extended to dynamic coefficients:
\max_{T}A(T)\,{\Delta T}/{\Delta X}\leq 1
.

Remark 4

6
Conclusion

We have studied the properties of a one-dimensional array of time-modulated interfaces using time-dependent spring-mass jump conditions. We have focused on the long-wavelength (low-frequency) homogenisation regime, wherein time-modulation is slow, making use of two-scale asymptotic expansions.

At leading order, we have obtained a time-dependent wave equation
with effective mass density and Young’s modulus which are homogeneous in space but depend on time. The occurrence of
k
-gaps in case of a periodic modulation has been analysed. Second-order homogenisation has allowed us to describe higher-order dispersive effects and local fluctuations. These findings have been illustrated through time-domain simulations, showing excellent agreement between full-field solutions and solutions of the effective models for moderate modulation frequencies, even for quite high central frequencies.

Neither the leading-order (
35
) nor the second-order (
54
) effective wave equations exhibit odd-order spatial derivatives. Consequently, spatial inversion symmetry is preserved, and the effective models obtained are reciprocal.
However, as we illustrated through direct time-domain numerical simulations, when considering very fast time modulation of the interfaces, the microstructured media clearly exhibits non-reciprocity. This regime lies beyond the validity of our current approach, but it clearly motivates further developments.

Appendix A
Leading-order homogenisation in the case with dissipation

In this appendix, we consider the more general case (
4
) with dissipation, i.e.
{Q}_{C,\ell}\neq 0
and
{Q}_{M,\ell}\neq 0
, which requires a velocity-stress formulation. The strategy being very similar, we underline only the differences with the case treated in Section
3
.

A.1
Two-scale analysis

Non-dimensionalisation.

With dissipation, it is necessary to introduce the non-dimensionalised velocity
v_{\eta}=\partial_{t}u_{\eta}
, and the non-dimensionalised dissipation interface parameters

{q}_{c,\ell}=\frac{1}{\eta}\sqrt{E^{\star}\rho^{\star}}{Q}_{C,\ell},\quad{q}_{m,\ell}=\frac{1}{\eta}\frac{1}{\sqrt{E^{\star}\rho^{\star}}}{Q}_{M,\ell}.

(78)

The scaling in
1/\eta
for
{q}_{c,\ell}
and
{q}_{m,\ell}
is chosen so that the dissipation terms have a contribution of same order of magnitude than the mass and rigidity terms in the following non-dimensionalised jump conditions:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\llbracket v_{\eta}\right\rrbracket_{\ell}=\eta\,\partial_{t}\left({c}_{\ell}(t)\,\left\llangle\sigma_{\eta}\right\rrangle_{\ell}\right)+\eta\,{q}_{c,\ell}\left\llangle\sigma_{\eta}\right\rrangle_{\ell},

(79a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\llbracket\sigma_{\eta}\right\rrbracket_{\ell}=\eta\,\partial_{t}\left({m}_{\ell}(t)\,\left\llangle v_{\eta}\right\rrangle_{\ell}\right)+\eta\,{q}_{m,\ell}\left\llangle v_{\eta}\right\rrangle_{\ell}.

(79b)

Asymptotic expansion.

The velocity-stress formulation being necessary to perform homogenisation in the presence of dissipation, the fields
v_{\eta}
and
\sigma_{\eta}
are expanded using the usual ansatz:

v_{\eta}(x,t)=\sum_{j\geq 0}\eta^{j}v_{j}(x,y,t)\text{ and }\sigma_{\eta}(x,t)=\sum_{j\geq 0}\eta^{j}\sigma_{j}(x,y,t).

(80)

For all
j\geq 1
, the fields
v_{j}
and
\sigma_{j}
are assumed to be continuous with respect to the first variable and
1
-periodic with respect to the second variable, i.e.
v_{j}(x,y,t)=v_{j}(x,y+1,t)
for all
y
, and similarly for
\sigma_{j}
.
Injecting the ansatz into (
8
) yields the followingPDEs

\displaystyle\alpha(y)\,\partial_{t}v_{\eta}(x,y,t)=\left(\partial_{x}+\frac{1}{\eta}\,\partial_{y}\right)\sigma_{\eta}(x,y,t)+f(x,t),

(81a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma_{\eta}(x,y,t)=\beta(y)\left(\partial_{x}+\frac{1}{\eta}\,\partial_{y}\right)v_{\eta}(x,y,t),

(81b)

together with time-dependent jump conditions (
79b
).

A.2
Model derivation

The
{\cal O}(\eta^{-1})
terms in (
81b
) yield

\partial_{y}v_{0}=0,\qquad\partial_{y}\sigma_{0}=0,

(82)

and the
{\cal O}(\eta^{0})
terms in the jump conditions (
79a
) and (
79b
) lead to

\left\llbracket v_{0}\right\rrbracket_{\ell}=0,\qquad\left\llbracket\sigma_{0}\right\rrbracket_{\ell}=0.

(83)

Consequently one gets

v_{0}(x,y,t)=\overline{v}_{0}(x,t)\text{ and }\sigma_{0}(x,y,t)=\overline{\sigma}_{0}(x,t).

(84)

The
{\cal O}(\eta^{0})
term in (
81b
) reads

\partial_{t}\sigma_{0}=\beta\partial_{x}v_{0}+\beta\partial_{y}v_{1}.

(85)

Differentiation of this equation with respect to
y
, together with (
84
), yields

\partial_{y}(\beta\partial_{y}v_{1})=-\beta^{\prime}(y)\partial_{x}v_{0}.

(86)

This equation on
v_{1}
comes with the following jump conditions

{}\displaystyle\left\llbracket v_{1}\right\rrbracket_{\ell}=({c}_{\ell}^{\prime}+{q}_{c,\ell})\sigma_{0}+{c}_{\ell}\beta(\partial_{x}v_{0}+\partial_{y}v_{1}),\qquad\displaystyle\left\llbracket\beta(\partial_{x}v_{0}+\partial_{y}v_{1})\right\rrbracket_{\ell}=0.

(87)

The first one is obtained by identifying the
{\cal O}(\eta)
term in (
79a
) and using (
85
), and the second one by taking the jump of (
85
) while remembering that
\left\llbracket\sigma_{0}\right\rrbracket_{\ell}=0
.
Consequently
v_{1}
can be written as

v_{1}(x,y,t)=\overline{v}_{1}(x,t)+P_{1}(y,t)\partial_{x}v_{0}(x,t)+Q(y,t)\sigma_{0}(x,t),

(88)

where
P_{1}
has already been introduced without dissipation (
26
) and
Q
is solution of the following cell problem:

({Q}):\left|\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\partial_{y}(\beta\partial_{y}Q)=0,&\text{ on }\bigcup\limits_{\ell=1}^{N}(y_{\ell},y_{\ell+1})\\[-2.0pt]
\displaystyle\left\llbracket\beta\partial_{y}Q\right\rrbracket_{\ell}=0,&\text{ for }\ell=1,\cdots,N\\[-2.0pt]
\displaystyle\left\llbracket Q\right\rrbracket_{\ell}={c}_{\ell}^{\prime}(t)+{q}_{c,\ell}+{c}_{\ell}(t)\,\beta\partial_{y}Q&\text{ for }\ell=1,\cdots,N\\[-2.0pt]
\displaystyle\left\langle Q\right\rangle=0,\quad Q\,\mbox{ 1-periodic in }y.&\end{array}\right.

(89)

As
P_{1}
,
Q
also depends on
t
because of the time-modulation of the interface compliance.

To obtain the leading-order model, one averages the
{\cal O}(\eta^{0})
terms in (
81a
) while using (
19
) and the terms of order
{\cal O}(\eta^{1})
in (

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}(\alpha_{0}(t)\overline{v}_{0}(x,t))+\gamma_{m,0}(t)\overline{v}_{0}(t)=\partial_{x}\overline{\sigma}_{0}(x,t)+f(x,t),\\[-2.0pt]
&\partial_{t}\overline{\sigma}_{0}(x,t)=\beta_{0}(t)\partial_{x}\overline{v}_{0}(x,t)+\gamma_{c,0}(t)\overline{\sigma}_{0}(t),\end{aligned}\right.

(90)

where
\alpha_{0}
and
\beta_{0}
are unchanged (

\gamma_{m,0}(t)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}{q}_{m,\ell}(t),\qquad\gamma_{c,0}(t)=\left\langle\beta\partial_{y}Q(\cdot,t)\right\rangle.

(91)

One can look for an explicit expression for

as we did for
\beta_{0}
(

\gamma_{c,0}(t)=\beta\partial_{y}Q=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}[{c}^{\prime}_{\ell}(t)+{q}_{c,\ell}]\beta_{0}(t).

(92)

Limit case without dissipation.

If there is no dissipation, i.e.
{q}_{c,\ell}=0
and
{q}_{m,\ell}=0
for
\ell=1,\cdots,N
, then (

\partial_{t}(\alpha_{0}(t)\overline{v}_{0}(x,t))=\partial_{x}\overline{\sigma}_{0}(x,t)+f(x,t),\qquad\partial_{t}\overline{\sigma}_{0}(x,t)=\beta_{0}(t)\partial_{x}\overline{v}_{0}(x,t)+\gamma_{c,0}(t)\overline{\sigma}_{0}(t),

(93)

where
\gamma_{c,0}(t)

\gamma_{c,0}(t)=\frac{\beta_{0}^{\prime}(t)}{\beta_{0}(t)}.
The second equation integrated in time reads
\overline{\sigma}_{0}(x,t)=\beta_{0}(t)\partial_{x}\overline{u}_{0}(x,t)

Dimensionalised coordinates.

From (

(95)

A.3
Numerical modelling

The velocity-stress formulation (
95
) can be recast as:

\partial_{T}\textbf{U}+\textbf{A}(T)\,\partial_{X}\textbf{U}=\textbf{S}(T)\,\textbf{U}+\textbf{F}(X,T),

(96)

where the material properties depend on time but are homogeneous in space. The vectors and matrices are expressed as:

\begin{array}[]{ll}{\bf U}(X,T)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\overline{V}_{0}(X,T)\\
\overline{S}_{0}(X,T)\end{array}\right),&{\bf F}(X,T)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\frac{F(X,T)}{\rho_{0}(T)}\\
0\end{array}\right),\\[12.0pt]
{\bf A}(T)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\rho_{0}(T)}\\
-E_{0}(T)&0\end{array}\right),&{\bf S}(T)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho_{0}(T)}\Gamma_{M,0}(T)&0\\
0&E_{0}(T)\Gamma_{C,0}(T)\end{array}\right).\end{array}

(97)

The system (
96
) can be solved by a splitting method
[
8
]
, where (
96
) is divided into a propagative part:

\partial_{T}{\bf U}+{\bf A}(T)\,\partial_{X}{\bf U}={\bf 0},

(98)

and a diffusive/relaxation part:

\partial_{T}{\bf U}={\bf S}(T){\bf U}+{\bf F}(X,T).

(99)

The solutions to (
98
) and (
99
) are given by the time-dependent discrete operators
\textbf{H}_{p}
and
\textbf{H}_{d}
, respectively. Second-order Strang’s splitting is then used, leading to the time-marching

\textbf{U}^{(1)}_{m}=\mathcal{\textbf{H}}_{d}\left(T_{n},\frac{\Delta T}{2}\right)\textbf{U}^{n}_{m},\quad\textbf{U}^{(2)}_{m}=\mathcal{\textbf{H}}_{p}\left(\Delta T\right)\textbf{U}^{(1)}_{m},\quad\textbf{U}^{n+1}_{m}=\mathcal{\textbf{H}}_{d}\left(T_{n+1},\frac{\Delta T}{2}\right)\textbf{U}^{(2)}_{m}.

(100)

The operator
\mathcal{\textbf{H}}_{p}
relies on the same ADER-4 scheme as for the microstructured medium (Section
5.1
). For the operator
\mathcal{\textbf{H}}_{d}
, an analytical solution yields:

\mathcal{\textbf{H}}_{d}\left(T,\frac{\Delta T}{2}\right){\bf U}_{m}=\exp\left(\int_{T}^{T+\Delta T/2}{\bf S(\tau)}d\tau\right)\,{\bf U}_{m}.

(101)

During one time step, we consider that the parameters do not vary, so that

\mathcal{\textbf{H}}_{d}\left(T_{n},\frac{\Delta T}{2}\right){\bf U}_{m}=\exp\left({\bf S}(T_{n})\frac{\Delta T}{2}\right)\,{\bf U}_{m}.

(102)

Appendix B
Dispersion relation for the time-periodic leading-order model

We detail here the Plane Wave Expansion (PWE) methods used to derive the dispersion relation associated with the leading-order effective equation (
35
) in case of a time-periodic modulation of period
\tau
. The unit cell in time is denoted by
\mathcal{T}=[0,\tau]
.

Fourier series expansion.

As the material properties and the function
U^{\sharp}
are
\tau
-periodic, they can be expanded in Fourier series:

\rho_{0}(T)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\rho_{n}e^{\text{i}\frac{2\pi}{\tau}nT},\qquad E_{0}(T)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}E_{n}e^{\text{i}\frac{2\pi}{\tau}nT},\qquad U^{\sharp}(T)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}U^{\sharp}_{n}e^{\text{i}\frac{2\pi}{\tau}nT},

(103)

where the coefficient
\rho_{n}
and
E_{n}
are :

\rho_{n}=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\rho_{0}(T)e^{-i\frac{2\pi}{\tau}nT}dT,\qquad E_{n}=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{\mathcal{T}}E_{0}(T)e^{-i\frac{2\pi}{\tau}nT}dT.

(104)

Using the expression of
U^{\sharp}
into (
38
), the leading-order homogenised displacement field can be written:

\hat{U}(T)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}U^{\sharp}_{n}e^{i(\frac{2\pi}{\tau}n+\omega^{\sharp})\,T}.

(105)

Injecting the Fourier series expansion of the displacement field and of the material parameters into the wave equation (
37
), one obtains

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\rho_{m}\left(\frac{2\pi}{\tau}n+\omega^{\sharp}\right)\left(\frac{2\pi}{\tau}(n+m)+\omega^{\sharp}\right)U^{\sharp}_{n}-k^{2}E_{m}U^{\sharp}_{n}\right)e^{i\frac{2\pi}{\tau}(n+m)\,T}=0.

(106)

Scalar product.

The hermitian scalar product is defined for
f
and
g
two
\tau
-periodic functions:

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{-\frac{\tau}{2}}^{\frac{\tau}{2}}f(T)\,\overline{g}(T)\,dT.

(107)

where
\overline{g}(T)
denotes the complex conjugate of
g
. Taking
f(T)=e^{i\frac{2\pi}{\tau}(n+m)T}
and
g(T)=e^{i\frac{2\pi}{\tau}pT}
yields:

\left\langle e^{i\frac{2\pi}{\tau}(n+m)T},e^{i\frac{2\pi}{\tau}pT}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{-\frac{\tau}{2}}^{\frac{\tau}{2}}e^{-i\frac{2\pi}{\tau}(n+m-p)\,T}dT=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}0\text{ if }n+m\neq p,\\
1\text{ if }n+m=p.\end{array}\right.

(108)

By taking the scalar product of the (
106
) and the
\tau
-periodic function
g(T)
previously defined, the following relation is obtained for each value of
p\in\mathbb{Z}
:

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\frac{2\pi}{\tau}n+\omega^{\sharp}\right)\left(\frac{2\pi}{\tau}p+\omega^{\sharp}\right)\rho_{p-n}U^{\sharp}_{n}-k^{2}E_{p-n}U^{\sharp}_{n}=0,

(109)

where relation
m=p-n
is used. This equation is truncated to
n=-N_{f},\cdots,N_{f}
, where
N_{f}
is the number of Fourier modes, and we choose
p=-N_{f},\cdots,N_{f}
. It follows that the vector
{\bf U}=\left(U^{\sharp}_{-N_{f}},...,U^{\sharp}_{N_{f}}\right)^{T}
satisfies the generalised eigenvalue problem

{\bm{P}(\omega^{\sharp})\,{\bf U}}=k^{2}\,{\bm{Q}\,{\bf U}},

(110)

with the components:

P_{(N+n)(N+p)}=\left(\frac{2\pi}{\tau}n+\omega^{\sharp}\right)\left(\frac{2\pi}{\tau}p+\omega^{\sharp}\right)\rho_{p-n},\qquad Q_{(N+n)(N+p)}=E_{p-n}.

(111)

Remark 5
.

The matrices
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
in the PWE are hermitian. Consequently, the Generalised Eigenvalue Problem (
110
) has real eigenvalues and its eigenvectors form a basis. One can easily show it by using the fact that the effective parameters are real.
