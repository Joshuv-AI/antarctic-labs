Cross-kingdom microbial associations characterize responsiveness to fecal microbiota transplantation in patients with irritable bowel syndrome
Authors: Qiulong Yan, Min Li, Guangyang Wang, Aiqin Zhang, Yuming Li, Ruochun Guo, Yue Zhang, Wei Yang, Yidi Zhang, Xiaohua Liu, Xin Li, Ning Zheng, Leyi Wang, Shao Fan, Renzhen Ma, Tong Lu, Shan Zhou, Tianyu Guan, Guorui Xing, Shenghui Li, Liang Wang, Yanxia Li

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrecise outcome prediction for fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) in irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) remains a clinical challenge. The roles of the gut virome and its interplay with bacteria in FMT efficacy are particularly underexplored. This secondary analysis aimed to conduct an exploratory, hypothesis-generating investigation into these cross-kingdom dynamics.MethodsWe conducted a secondary, integrative analysis of a published cohort, performing longitudinal, cross-kingdom metagenomic profiling on 83 samples from 22 IBS patients and healthy donors. We integrative approach combined microbial diversity, species-specific biomarker identification, bacterial-viral associated networks, and exploratory random forest modeling to identify microbial features associated with FMT outcomes.ResultsIBS patients showed higher bacterial and viral alpha diversity than donors. Cross-kingdom profiling identified 223 bacterial and 724 viral biomarkers. Donor-enriched biomarkers were predominantly health-associated Bacteroidetes (e.g., B. ovatus, B. faecis), whereas pre-FMT–enriched biomarkers were largely Firmicutes (e.g., B. obeum) with potential pathobiont roles. The Effect and No effect groups displayed different microbial trajectories. Although both groups shifted toward a donor-like composition initially, only responders maintained a stable donor-like ecology throughout the 12-month follow-up, supported by more resilient bacterial-viral association networks. Exploratory random forest modeling highlighted microbial features, such as R. pickettii, with high relative importance for outcome discrimination. However, permutation testing (p = 0.548–0.616) confirmed that model performance on this small cohort did not exceed chance level, underscoring the risk of overfitting and the exploratory nature of these computational findings.ConclusionsThis integrative re-analysis provides preliminary evidence that cross-kingdom gut microbiome profiles are strongly associated with FMT outcomes in IBS. Successful outcomes appear linked to sustained donor-like remodeling and stable bacterial-viral networks. Our findings are primarily hypothesis-generating and offer a framework of candidate biomarkers for future validation in larger cohorts. This work underscores the necessity of external validation to develop robust, microbiome-based tools for personalized FMT therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08269-w.Keywords: Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), Gut microbiome, Gut virome, Treatment outcomes

Introduction

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a prevalent chronic gastrointestinal disorder that affects approximately 10–20% of the adult population worldwide, contributing to substantial morbidity and significant socioeconomic burdens [1]. Current clinical management primarily focuses on symptomatic relief. The microbial pathophysiology of IBS remains poorly understood, as alterations in the gut microbiota may represent either a cause of the disorder or a consequence of IBS-induced changes in intestinal secretion and motility [2]. A prevailing hypothesis is that fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), the transfer of gut microbiota from a healthy donor to a recipient, can restore IBS-associated dysbiosis, thereby leading to symptom amelioration or reversal [3]. FMT refers to the process of transferring the gut microbiota from a healthy donor into a recipient [4, 5]. FMT has been established as an effective treatment for recurrent Clostridium difficile infection [6], and its potential application in IBS is of growing interest. Compared to healthy individuals, the gut microbiota in IBS is characterized by reduced bacterial diversity and aberrant proportions of specific bacteria [7]. In patients with IBS and other disorders, FMT leads to an increase in bacterial diversity [4, 8] and enables the durable coexistence of donor and recipient microbial strains for up to one year post-treatment [9–11].

The gut microbiota is considered to be one of the key factors playing a critical role in the etiology of IBS [12]. Given that the gut microbiota constitutes a complex community of bacteria, fungi, and viruses, a notable gap in previous research is the limited exploration of the distinct roles played by each constituent. While current research has predominantly focused on gut bacteria, the importance of the gut virome should not be overlooked. Global virome databases reveal that the human gut virome is predominantly composed of bacteriophages (97.7%), with eukaryotic viruses and archaeal viruses constituting 2.1% and 0.1%, respectively; a substantial proportion of these viral entities remain uncultured and/or unclassified [13]. The gut virome interacts closely with the microbiota, and emerging evidence has highlighted its associations with various diseases, particularly inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), obesity, diabetes, stress-related disorders, autoimmune conditions, and liver diseases [14–19]. Understanding the diversity and dynamics of the gut virome in disease states is therefore crucial for elucidating underlying mechanisms.

Using the metagenomic dataset from Goll et al. [20], we integrated gut bacterial and viral profiles with clinical symptom data from patients with IBS. This study aimed to characterize the longitudinal dynamics and interactions between gut bacteria and viruses following FMT in individuals with IBS.

Materials and methods

Data collection

This study is a secondary analysis of data from the REFIT trial, a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled, single-center study (Goll et al. [20]). Eligible participants met ROME III criteria for IBS-D or IBS‑M, were aged 18–75 years, and had moderate‑to‑severe symptoms (IBS‑SSS score ≥ 175). Participants were randomized (2:1) to receive either active FMT or an autologous fecal placebo. The FMT material was prepared from 50 to 80 g of donor feces homogenized in 200 mL of isotonic saline and 50 mL of 85% glycerol and administered into the cecum via colonoscopy; all participants received 8 mg loperamide orally 2 h before the procedure to aid retention. The primary endpoint of the original trial was a reduction in IBS‑SSS of > 75 points at 3 months post‑FMT.

We obtained the metagenomic dataset (BioProject: PRJEB36140) from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive, which comprised 83 fecal samples: 17 baseline samples from 2 donors, and 66 longitudinal samples (baseline, 6 months, and 12 months post-FMT) from 22 patients who received active FMT [20]. In the original trial, the active FMT material was derived from a pool of samples provided by two healthy donors and was randomly allocated to participants in the active FMT arm. Consistent with the original trial endpoint, we defined treatment response using the same threshold: subjects with an IBS-SSS reduction > 75 points were classified as responders (Effect group, n = 14), and those with a reduction ≤ 75 points as non-responders (No effect group, n = 8). This clinically established cutoff ensures comparability with prior work. Longitudinal samples were available for each patient at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months post-FMT (Supplementary Table S1).

Data preprocessing

Raw reads were quality-controlled via fastp v0.23.4 [21] with the options ‘-q 20 -u 30 -n 5 -y -Y 30 –trim_poly_g –trim_poly_x -j’ and bbduk [22] with the parameters ‘entropy = 0.6 entropywindow = 50 entropyk = 5’. Furthermore, reads from human and Escherichia phage phiX174 were removed by matching quality-filtered reads against the CHM13v2.0 and phiX174 (NCBI accession NC_001422.1) genomes with bowtie2 v2.4.4 [23].

Taxonomic profiling and diversity

Construction of the integrated Bacterial-Fungal-Viral (iBFV) database. We constructed a custom integrated database to enable comprehensive, cross-kingdom taxonomic profiling. The iBFV database comprises:4,744 prokaryotic reference genomes from the Unified Human Gastrointestinal Genome (UHGG) v2.0 [24]. 145,764 viral species-level reference genomes. These were derived from 622,643 medium- or high-quality viral genomes (assessed by CheckV) sourced from five public databases (cnGVC [25], CHVD-gut [26], GPD [27], GVD [28], MGV [29]) and an additional 21,912 genomes from our FMT cohort. Viral genomes were clustered into species-level clades at 95% average nucleotide identity and 85% genome coverage, with the longest genome in each clade selected as the reference. 317 fungal reference genomes from the Cultivated Gut Fungi (CGF) catalogue [30]. Quality-controlled clean reads were taxonomically profiled using our custom database. Clean reads were aligned against iBFV using Kraken2 v2.1.3 with the optimized parameter ‘–confidence 0.1’ [31]. Species-level relative abundance estimates were derived through Bayesian re-estimation using Bracken v2.8 [32]. Key parameters included: read length specification (150 bp matching sequencing fragments), minimum read threshold (5 reads), and taxonomic level restriction to species rank (−l S).

To classify viral contigs as either lytic or temperate phages, we performed lifestyle prediction using Bacphlip [33]. To infer potential ecological interactions between viruses and bacteria, we performed host prediction for viral contigs. This was achieved by aligning viral contigs against the prokaryotic genome subset of our custom database. Contigs with significant matches were assigned to putative bacterial hosts at the genus and species levels. This analysis linked viral dynamics to specific bacterial clades but does not constitute functional annotation of viral genes.

Identification of the biomarkers

To identify potential microbial biomarkers, we first filtered bacterial and viral species with a relative abundance exceeding 0.01% that were present in at least 20% of all samples, resulting in 629 bacterial and 1,841 viral species for downstream analysis. Using Multivariate Association with Linear Models (MaAsLin2) [34], we compared species abundances between donor controls and pre-FMT baseline patient samples, which identified 370 differentially abundant bacterial species and 1,131 differentially abundant viral species. Subsequently, we applied MaAsLin2 to evaluate longitudinal changes in species abundance across time points (baseline, 6 months, and 12 months post-FMT) relative to donor samples. From the resulting longitudinal models, species were further filtered based on divergent abundance trends (coefficient change trajectories) between the Effect and No effect groups. This step yielded 223 bacterial and 724 viral species exhibiting divergent trends between the two groups, which were selected as candidate biomarkers. To systematically interpret these trends, the candidate biomarkers were categorized as “donor-enriched” or “pre-FMT-enriched” based on the direction and significance (FDR < 0.05) of coefficients from the longitudinal MaAsLin2 models. A “donor-enriched” species was defined as one that showed a significant positive association (coefficient > 0) with donor samples relative to the pre-FMT patient baseline. A “pre-FMT-enriched” species was defined as one that showed a significant positive association (coefficient > 0) with the pre-FMT patient baseline relative to donors.

Correlation network analysis

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to identify significant co-occurrence patterns (statistical associations) between bacterial and viral species, and adjust the p-value using the function p.adjust with the option “method = BH” in the R platform. Pairs with an absolute Spearman’s ρ >0.60 and a q-value < 0.05 were defined as significantly associated. Finally, use Cytoscape to visualize the relationship between IBS-related preferences and viruses [35].

Random forest analysis

Random forest analysis was performed using the randomForest package in R. The analysis was conducted on a cohort of 22 IBS patients (14 Effect, 8 No effect) with longitudinal sampling. The input feature space was high-dimensional, comprising 3,441 bacterial species and 15,980 viral species (contigs) per sample, creating a scenario where the number of features vastly exceeded the number of samples (p ≫ n). Given this context, the analysis was framed as an exploratory feature ranking exercise to identify outcome-associated taxa, rather than to build a definitive predictive model. Models were built separately using the relative abundance data from different timepoints (pre-FMT, 6-month, and 12-month post-FMT). For model training and evaluation, we employed a 50-times repeated threefold cross-validation scheme. In each iteration, to mitigate the extreme p ≫ n problem and overfitting, feature selection was performed exclusively within the training fold by selecting the top N features based on the mean decrease in Gini importance from a preliminary random forest. The value of N was optimized per iteration (range: 5 to 20 features) with the aim of balancing model stability and relevance. The model’s performance within this specific cohort was summarized by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) on the held-out test folds, with the reported AUC representing the mean across all cross-validation repeats. Crucially, to assess whether any observed performance exceeded chance level, a primary concern given the small sample size and high feature dimensionality, we conducted rigorous permutation testing. This involved randomly shuffling the outcome labels 1000 times and repeating the entire cross-validation and feature selection process for each permutation to generate a null distribution of AUC values. The 95% confidence intervals for the reported AUCs were estimated using bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations).

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.3.1). Alpha diversity was assessed by calculating observed richness and the Shannon diversity index from species-level relative abundance profiles. Between-group differences in alpha diversity indexes were quantified as Hedges’ g standardized mean differences using the metafor:escalc and metafor:rma functions. In addition, Bray-Curtis distances between samples were computed based on the species-level relative abundance profile using the vegan:vegdist function. Using this distance matrix, we conducted a permutational multivariate analysis (PERMANOVA) using the vegan:adonis function. The obtained R2 values were further adjusted using the RsquareAdj function. Similarly, based on the same distance matrix, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was carried out using the ape:pcoa function. The PCoA plot was generated using the ggplot function.

Result

Sequencing data and cohort characteristics

We profiled the gut microbiota of a published IBS cohort via whole-genome shotgun sequencing [20]. Our analysis encompassed 83 samples comprising donor materials and serial collections from 22 patients at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months post-FMT. Based on clinical outcomes assessed at three months, patients were categorized as responders (the Effect group, n = 14) or non-/transient responders (the No effect group, n = 8). Approximately 614.21 Gbp of clean sequencing data were generated (Supplementary Table S1).

Altered gut microbiome diversity in IBS

To comprehensively characterize the gut microbiota, quality-controlled reads were classified against our in-house reference database, identifying 3,816 bacterial species and 25,220 viral species across all samples. We then performed a longitudinal comparison of bacterial and viral diversity between donor samples and patient groups (Effect and No effect) across three time points.

The results revealed that both bacterial and viral alpha diversity were elevated in IBS patients compared to donor individuals (Fig. 1A–B). Detailed pairwise statistical comparisons were provided in Supplementary Table S2. After FMT, the bacterial diversity in the No effect group remained consistently higher than in the Effect group across all three time points, whereas viral diversity did not differ significantly between the two patient groups (Fig. 1A–B). Correlation analysis revealed a strong positive association between bacterial and viral diversity in donors (Fig. S1; R2 > 0.8, p < 0.001). Notably, the Effect group exhibited higher bacterium-virus diversity correlations at multiple time points compared to the No effect group.

The adjusted R2 value (point size) of (D) bacterial and (F) viral species, represents the proportion of change explained by each group

Consistent with these findings, PCoA analysis based on Bray-Curtis distances at the species level revealed distinct clustering of donor and patient samples in both bacterial and viral community structures (Fig. 1C, E). Before FMT, the microbial profiles of the Effect group were compositionally closer to donors than those of the No effect group. After FMT, both Effect and No effect groups showed compositional shifts toward donor-like states. Furthermore, we did not observe significant clustering of results strictly based on donors in PCoA. PERMANOVA revealed significant differences in bacterial and viral community structures between the Donor group and all post-FMT time points (Fig. 1D, F; p < 0.001). The Donor group accounted for up to 16% of the total variation (adjusted R2 = 0.16).

Identification of IBS-associated bacterial and viral biomarkers

To identify robust microbial biomarkers, we first excluded taxa with low abundance (relative abundance < 0.01%) or low prevalence (occurring in <20% of samples in any key group: donor or pre-FMT). This process yielded 629 bacterial and 1,841 viral species for downstream analysis. Using MaAsLin2, we first identified 370 bacterial and 1,131 viral species with differential abundance (q-value < 0.05) in pre-FMT patients compared to donors (Figs. 2–3; Supplementary Tables S3-4). Subsequently, a longitudinal MaAsLin2 model was applied to evaluate species abundance changes relative to the donor baseline at each post-FMT time point (6 and 12 months). Based on divergent abundance trajectories (coefficient trends) between Effect and No effect, the biomarker set was further refined to 223 bacterial and 724 viral species. The final collection consisted of 76 donor-enriched and 147 pre-FMT-enriched bacteria, as well as 494 donor-enriched and 230 pre-FMT-enriched viruses.

We first characterized the phylogenetic distribution of the 223 bacterial biomarkers by mapping them onto a reference phylogeny to visualize their enrichment patterns and relative abundances (Fig. 2). Among the 76 donor-enriched bacterial biomarkers, the majority belonged to the phylum Bacteroidetes (Supplementary Table S3). Key genera within this phylum included Bacteroides (B. ovatus, B. faecis), Alistipes (A. shahii), and Phocaeicola (P. dorei, P. massiliensis). In contrast, the 147 biomarkers enriched in pre-FMT samples were predominantly affiliated with the phylum Firmicutes_A (Supplementary Table S3). Representative species from this group comprised Blautia_A spp. (B. obeum), Coprococcus spp. (C. eutactus_A), Gemmiger spp. (G. qucibialis) and Pseudoruminococcus (P. massiliensis).

We next performed a parallel analysis of the viral biomarkers, focusing on their life strategies and host associations. Of the 494 donor-enriched viral biomarkers, 65% were temperate phages and 35% were lytic (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S4). In contrast, among the 230 pre-FMT-enriched viral biomarkers, the predicted lifestyle composition was 78% temperate and 22% lytic. These proportions are broadly consistent with the overall shifts in temperate and lytic phage proportions observed across sample groups (Fig. S2A-C), suggesting an association between viral lifestyle structure and patient groups. We observed that the proportion of temperate phages increased in IBS patients and was further modulated by FMT, with a markedly greater increase in the No effect group compared to the Effect group. Putative host prediction, based on sequence alignment, revealed that the majority of donor-enriched viruses were associated with hosts within the phylum Bacteroidota (Supplementary Table S5), with prominent associations to species such as Phocaeicola spp. (P. dorei) and Alistipes spp. (A. shahii). Conversely, pre-FMT-enriched viruses also primarily infected Bacteroidota hosts, with higher abundance species infecting Tidjanibacter spp. (T. inops_A), Alistipes spp. (A. putredinis) and Alistipes spp. (A. shahii).

Longitudinal tracking of these biomarkers over one year revealed that at baseline, the No effect group exhibited more severe microbial dysbiosis than the Effect group (Figs. 2–3; Fig. S3A-D). At baseline, the No effect group exhibited more severe microbial dysbiosis than Effect group. Among the 76 donor-enriched bacterial biomarkers, all showed reduced abundance in pre-FMT patient samples compared to donors (Fig. 2). The Effect group had a significantly higher proportion of donor-enriched bacteria (90.8%) than No effect group (72.4%). Similarly, for the 494 donor-enriched viral biomarkers (Fig. 3), the proportion in Effect group (96.4%) was greater than that in No effect group (72.5%). A significantly higher proportion of the 147 pre-FMT-enriched bacterial and the 230 pre-FMT-enriched viral biomarkers was maintained in Effect group (81.6% and 71.3%, respectively) than in No effect group (60.0% and 55.2%).

Although donor-enriched bacteria did not expand substantially in either group, their abundance remained significantly lower than in donors throughout the intervention (Fig. S3A-D). By 12 months, the Effect group sustained suppression of 68.4% of these bacterial taxa, compared to only 48.7% in No effect group. A parallel trend was observed for viruses: donor-enriched viruses remained significantly suppressed in 65.4% of Effect group, versus 49.4% in No effect group at 12 months. Meanwhile, pre-FMT-enriched (IBS-associated) bacteria and viruses remained declined in both groups, but the extent of significant enrichment declined more sharply in No effect group, indicating a weaker sustained capacity to control disease-associated microbial communities in No effect group.

In summary, these results underscore that the No effect group displayed more severe baseline ecological dysbiosis, whereas the Effect group achieved more durable microbial remodeling, characterized by sustained suppression of IBS-associated bacteria and viruses and stable maintenance of a donor-like microbial ecosystem.

Distinct bacterial-viral co-occurrence networks

Having established the divergent temporal abundance patterns of bacterial and viral biomarkers, we next investigated differences in their co-occurrence networks between Effect and No effect groups. These networks are based on statistical correlations and reflect co-abundance patterns, which may arise from direct interactions, shared ecological niches, or responses to common host factors. Applying a threshold of |ρ| >0.6 and q < 0.05, we identified a total of 104 persistent co-occurring bacterial-virus pairs that were consistently present at all three time points in the Effect group (Fig. 4A) and 29 bacterial-virus pairs that were consistently present at all three time points in the No effect group (Fig. 4B). Our analysis revealed that the Effect group exhibited a higher number of strong, long-term (1 year) bacterial-virus associations compared to the No effect group. Notably, the bacteria most strongly correlated with viruses were primarily from Bacteroidota, specifically Bacteroides spp., Phocaeicola spp., and P. sartorii, as well as N. massiliensis from Firmicutes. P. sartorii and N. massiliensis showed increasing correlations with multiple viruses throughout the intervention. Conversely, the No effect group exhibited fewer stable bacterial-virus associations, with most pairs lacking a consistent upward or downward trend. We further identified bacterial-virus pairs present in all samples and calculated the differences in correlations between bacteria and viruses at the same time points in the Effect and No effect groups (Fig. 4C). Before FMT, the differences in correlations between these shared bacterial-virus pairs were minimal between the Effect and No effect groups, with most pairs showing higher correlations in the Effect group. Specifically, B. ovatus and the viruses V143644 and V144166 exhibited significantly higher correlations in the Effect group compared to the No effect group before FMT. However, this pattern reversed 12 months post-FMT, with most shared bacterial-virus pairs showing higher correlations in the No effect group after FMT.

Predicting FMT outcomes using microbial profiles

Having identified distinct microbial patterns, we next employed random forest (RF) models as an exploratory tool to rank microbial features by their association with clinical outcomes. Given the high dimensionality of the data relative to the cohort size (n = 22), the primary goal was to identify and prioritize candidate biomarkers for future validation, rather than to establish a clinically predictive model. To this end, we first trained RF models using all available bacterial features from pre-FMT samples. The mean decrease in Gini impurity was used to rank species by their relative importance. A post hoc analysis showed that the cross-validated AUC calculated on subsets of the top-ranked features reached a high value (e.g., >0.99 with the top 15 species) (Fig. 5A). However, permutation testing (1000 iterations) on the model using the top 15 species confirmed that this performance was not statistically significant and did not exceed the level expected by chance (AUC = 0.473; p = 0.548) (Supplementary Table S5). This underscores the exploratory nature of the analysis and the high risk of overfitting in this small sample. Notably, the feature importance ranking consistently highlighted species such as Ralstonia pickettii, Streptococcus constellatus, and Phocaeicola spp. as top-associated features. A similar exploratory analysis of the pre-FMT virome also yielded a ranked list of viral features, the apparent discriminative capacity of which was likewise not significant upon permutation testing (AUC = 0.438; p = 0.616) (Supplementary Table S5).

We extended this exploratory approach to post-FMT samples to investigate features associated with sustained outcomes. Consistent with the baseline analysis, permutation tests indicated that the apparent performance of models using top-ranked features at 6 and 12 months did not surpass chance expectations. Therefore, we focus on the biological relevance of the persistently top-ranked features across timepoints. For instance, species such as Alistipes putredinis and Odoribacter massiliensis were highly ranked at 6 months, while Blautia spp. emerged as notable at 12 months (Fig. 5B–C; Supplementary Table S6). Their consistent prominence suggests they may mark ecological states relevant to treatment response, forming a testable hypothesis for future studies.

In summary, our internal, cross-validated analysis suggests that specific pre- and post-FMT microbial profiles show suggestive potential as biomarkers for predicting FMT success in IBS and warrant further investigation in larger cohorts.

Discussion

Our integrated cross-kingdom analysis suggests that the gut microbiome is strongly associated with FMT outcomes in IBS, providing an ecological framework to guide future hypothesis-driven research.

Altered community structure and its longitudinal dynamics

Our analysis revealed elevated alpha diversity in IBS patients relative to healthy donors (Fig. 1A–B). This observation contributes to the heterogeneous landscape of IBS microbiome findings, where both increased and decreased diversity have been reported, likely reflecting the clinical and etiological diversity of the syndrome itself [20, 36, 37]. In the context of our study, we interpret this increased diversity not as a marker of health, but as a potential indicator of a disrupted or unstable ecosystem. Such an increase can arise from a loss of dominant, stable commensal taxa and a concomitant expansion of low-abundance or transient organisms, leading to a community structure that is more stochastic and less resilient—a state consistent with the concept of dysbiosis [38]. Beyond differences in alpha diversity, we also observed pronounced shifts in overall microbial community structure. PCoA analysis at the species level revealed clear separation between donor and patient samples in terms of bacterial and viral community composition (Fig. 1C, E). Before FMT, the microbial profile of the Effect group was closer to that of the donor. Post-intervention, both the Effect and No effect groups shifted toward a donor-like state, suggesting that FMT induced significant changes in the microbial community structure, consistent with FMT trials for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection [39]. At 12 months post-FMT, the No Effect group exhibited significantly distinct microbial communities compared to all other groups (p < 0.05; Fig. 1D, F), indicating a greater divergence from both the donor and responder profiles. Notably, the magnitude of dissimilarity between the No Effect group and other groups was greater than that observed between the Effect group and other groups, suggesting a more pronounced failure in microbiome restoration in non-responders. In contrast, the Effect group sustained a more stable, donor-like microbial composition. We observed that the No effect group exhibited more severe baseline microbial dysbiosis compared to Effect group, characterized by a significant loss of health-associated taxa (e.g., F. prausnitzii) and an increase in IBS-related taxa (e.g., Proteobacteria), a pattern corroborated by multiple recent studies linking dysbiosis severity to FMT responsiveness [40–42]. These findings align with emerging evidence that pre-treatment microbial configuration may serve as a predictive biomarker for FMT success.

Cross-kingdom biomarkers and co-occurrence networks underlie FMT outcomes

To move beyond community-level analysis and identify specific taxa underlying these structural shifts and clinical outcomes, we employed an integrative, cross-kingdom approach. This approach identified a robust set of bacterial and viral biomarkers that distinguish donor-like from IBS-associated microbial states and track dynamic responses to FMT throughout the intervention. We identified 223 bacterial and 724 viral species exhibiting divergent abundance trajectories between Effect and No effect groups, culminating in a final biomarker panel comprising 76 donor-enriched and 147 IBS-enriched bacterial taxa, alongside 494 donor-enriched and 230 IBS-enriched viral taxa. Notably, donor-enriched bacteria were predominantly affiliated with the phylum Bacteroidota (formerly Bacteroidetes), including key species such as B. ovatus, B. faecis, A. shahii, and P. dorei—taxa widely recognized as core components of a healthy gut microbiota [43–45]. Critically, the longitudinal trajectories of these identified biomarkers revealed a clear microbial basis for divergent therapeutic outcomes. Although both patient groups shifted toward a donor-like state (Fig. 2), the Effect group achieved more persistent and stable microbial remodeling. This was evidenced by a sustained suppression of IBS-associated bacteria and viruses and better maintenance of a donor-like ecosystem. In contrast, the No effect group showed significant differences in their bacterial and viral communities compared to other groups at 12 months after FMT (Fig. 3), indicating that their microbial communities failed to establish a stable, donor-like new steady state, but instead experienced early ecological relapse or regression. This result was consistent with previous studies, further confirming that the long-term stable reconstruction of the microbiota is a key factor in the sustained outcomes of FMT [46]. Notably, while the primary clinical response was defined at 3 months, our analysis of microbial trajectories up to 12 months aimed to explore the long-term ecological dynamics following FMT. This approach allows us to examine whether early post-FMT microbial changes are sustained and to identify potential predictors of long-term ecological stability, even though sustained symptom improvement beyond 3 months was not formally assessed in the original trial. Future studies incorporating serial symptom assessments will be crucial to directly link durable clinical response to sustained microbial remodeling.

We next investigated whether differences in ecological stability were reflected in the association networks between bacteria and viruses. We identified 104 persistent bacterial-virus pairs across all time points in the Effect group (Fig. 4A), compared to only 29 in the No effect group (Fig. 4B). Notably, the bacteria most strongly correlated with viruses were primarily from Bacteroidota (Bacteroides spp., Phocaeicola spp., P. sartorii) and Firmicutes (N. massiliensis). Bacteroides spp. were considered the main participants in maintaining the ecological balance of the human gut [47]. Most studies consistently report Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes as the dominant bacterial phyla in IBS patients, both of which are understood to play pivotal roles in human metabolic processes [48, 49]. These species showed strengthening correlations with multiple viruses throughout the intervention, indicating the establishment of increasingly coordinated cross-kingdom association networks. It is important to note that these correlation-based co-occurrence patterns may arise not only from direct biological interactions but also from shared responses to the host environment, sequencing depth, or community-wide restructuring after FMT. In contrast, the No effect group exhibited fewer stable associations, with most pairs lacking a consistent upward or downward trend. This suggests that the No effect group may have a less stable microbial community, which could hinder treatment outcomes.

To pinpoint specific associations that might differentiate the groups, we analyzed bacterial-virus pairs that were ubiquitous across all samples. We compared the correlation strength of these shared pairs between the Effect and No effect groups at each time point (Fig. 4C). Before FMT, correlation differences for most shared pairs were small, though slightly higher in the Effect group. Notably, B. ovatus exhibited significantly stronger correlations with viruses V143644 and V144166 in the Effect group before FMT. This suggests that these specific microbial co-occurrence patterns may be predictive of treatment success. Previous studies have established B. ovatus as a pivotal, health-associated member of the gut microbiota, highlighting its significant promise for developing next-generation probiotics [50, 51]. However, this trend reversed post-FMT, with most shared bacterial-virus pairs showing higher correlations in the No effect group 12 months after FMT. This reversal indicates a potential shift in microbial dynamics throughout the intervention, which may have implications for long-term treatment outcomes. The No effect group may experience a transient improvement in microbial interactions post-FMT, but these changes may not be sustained, leading to a lack of long-term outcomes.

Microbiota-based exploratory models for identifying outcome-associated features

Our study aimed to explore the association between gut microbiota and FMT outcomes in patients with IBS. We employed random forest modeling as an exploratory tool to rank microbial taxa by their relative importance for discriminating between responders and non-responders. A model based on the top fifteen most important bacterial species identified key taxa including R. pickettii, S. constellatus, and Phocaeicola spp. (Fig. 5A). However, to rigorously assess the significance of this model, we performed permutation testing (1000 iterations). The observed model performance (AUC = 0.473) was not significantly better than the null distribution obtained from permuted labels (mean permuted AUC = 0.493, p = 0.548) (Supplementary Table S6). This result confirms that in this limited cohort size, the model’s discriminative power does not exceed chance level, underscoring the high risk of overfitting and the exploratory nature of this analysis. Therefore, we interpret the value of this modeling not in its predictive accuracy but in its ability to highlight microbial taxa, such as R. pickettii, that are strongly associated with outcome and warrant investigation as potential ecological markers in larger studies. The consistent top ranking of R. pickettii suggests it may serve as a candidate microbial indicator of a gut ecosystem state less amenable to successful FMT.

A parallel exploratory analysis of the baseline gut virome similarly identified a distinct set of viral species that were ranked as most important (Fig. 5A). Permutation testing yielded a comparable result for the viral model (observed AUC = 0.438, p = 0.616), reinforcing the need for cautious interpretation (Supplementary Table S6). The fact that both bacterial and viral kingdoms yielded stable sets of top-ranked features strengthens the premise that cross-kingdom ecology is associated with treatment outcomes.

We extended this exploratory approach to post-FMT samples to investigate which microbial features were associated with outcomes at later stages (Fig. 5B–C; Supplementary Table S6). Consistent with the baseline analysis, permutation tests indicated that model performance at 6 and 12 months did not surpass chance expectations. Therefore, we focus on the longitudinal persistence of certain features in the importance rankings. For instance, species such as Alistipes putredinis and Odoribacter massiliensis were among the top-ranked features at 6 months, while Blautia spp. appeared prominently at 12 months. The recurrence of these taxa across timepoints suggests they may mark ecological states relevant to sustained treatment response, a hypothesis that requires future testing.

Our study has several limitations. First, due to the small cohort size (n = 22), our machine learning models showed evidence of overfitting (permutation test p = 0.548–0.616) and must be viewed strictly as hypothesis-generating tools. Second, the limited cohort and donor number constrain generalizability, requiring validation in larger independent studies. Third, the large set of candidate biomarkers identified should be interpreted cautiously, as their stability was not assessed across resampling schemes. Fourth, the 3-month binary clinical endpoint may not fully capture symptom changes, and its temporal mismatch with 12-month microbiome data limits direct symptom-microbe linkage. Fifth, while providing taxonomic and ecological insights, our virome analysis lacks functional gene annotation, precluding mechanistic conclusions. Finally, the observational design establishes association only; causal roles require experimental validation.

Despite these limitations, our findings provide a hypothesis-generating framework. The cross-kingdom ecological dynamics and candidate biomarkers identified could inform donor screening, patient stratification, and the design of precision-FMT trials. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts are essential to validate these feature rankings, confirm causality, and develop clinically actionable tools.

Conclusion

Our integrative analysis provides evidence that cross-kingdom gut microbiome dynamics are associated with FMT outcomes in IBS. We found that successful outcomes were linked to a distinct ecological trajectory, characterized by sustained, donor-like microbial remodeling and the development of a more resilient bacterial-viral association network. In contrast, the microbial ecology in non-responders reverted towards a pre-FMT state.

Exploratory machine learning was used to rank microbial features, identifying specific bacteria and viruses as candidate biomarkers associated with treatment response. Although the predictive performance of these models on our small cohort was limited, the consistent ranking of features like R. pickettii provides a hypothesis for future testing. Collectively, our findings advocate for an ecology-driven perspective to understand FMT and offer a framework of testable hypotheses and candidate biomarkers. Future validation in larger cohorts is essential to translate these exploratory insights into robust tools for personalizing FMT therapy in IBS.
