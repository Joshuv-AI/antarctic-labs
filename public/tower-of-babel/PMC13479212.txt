Transcending geographic and disciplinary borders: leadership lessons from a century of the Harkness Fellowship
Authors: Molly Fitzgerald, Mathew Kelley, Reginald Williams
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFounded in 1925 as a ‘reverse Rhodes Scholarship’, the Harkness Fellowship was designed to foster international collaboration through transatlantic academic exchange. Over a century, it has evolved into a leadership programme uniting mid-career professionals from nine countries and a range of professional backgrounds to improve health systems through a year of immersion in the USA. As the fellowship marks its centennial, we reflect on what its history reveals about leading and learning across borders.MethodsThis centennial reflection draws upon archival research, programme documentation, alumni interviews and individual case studies. The analysis synthesises historical shifts, strategic pivots and leadership development outcomes over the programme’s 100-year history.ResultsThree core lessons emerged: (1) challenge assumptions—fellows consistently innovated by questioning entrenched practices and applying cross-cultural insights; (2) harness the power of fellowship—sustained relationships and a shared mission drive collaborative outcomes across borders and (3) embrace a multidisciplinary mindset—fellows from diverse fields have advanced more holistic approaches to healthcare reform. Real-world examples illustrate the fellowship’s tangible impact and provide practical lessons for healthcare leaders across countries and sectors.ConclusionsThe Harkness Fellowship demonstrates that leadership in healthcare demands curiosity, humility and sustained collaboration. The fellowship’s story is a reminder that the ability to learn across differences remains one of the most powerful tools for change. As the programme enters its second century, its relevance will rest on staying true to its core values: shared purpose, openness to difference and collaboration across borders to drive systemic change.Keywords: career development, followership, health policy, health system, multi-disciplinary

What do a software entrepreneur from Singapore, an economist from Norway and a family medicine doctor from the UK have in common? All are spending the year in the USA as Harkness Fellows in Health Care Policy & Practice, travelling, learning and collaborating with each other and their US colleagues on projects to improve healthcare systems at home and abroad.

As leaders of the Harkness Fellowship—launched 100 years ago in 1925 and administered by the New York-based Commonwealth Fund—we take immense pride in the fellowship’s century-long legacy of achievement and the vital contributions that Harkness Fellows continue to make today. We also remain hopeful for the future of international exchange.

The centennial milestone has spurred not just celebration, but also reflection on the Harkness story and the leadership insights it offers. We have delved deep into our archives and talked with many people who have been a part of the experience over the years. Revisiting this history has no doubt surfaced moments of tension: times when the fellowship had to interrogate its own direction, redefine success and make difficult choices. We believe the Harkness Fellowship’s century of experience—its inflection points as much as its achievements—can be distilled into three central leadership lessons with value for today’s international health leaders: (1) challenge assumptions, (2) harness the power of fellowship and (3) embrace a multidisciplinary mindset.

Lesson 1: challenge assumptions

From the very beginning, the Harkness Fellowship was about dispelling misconceptions. The programme began in 1925 as the passion project of Edward Harkness, son of philanthropist and Commonwealth Fund founder Anna Harkness. At the time, Edward envisioned a reverse Rhodes Scholarship for British postgraduate students to study in the USA. He hoped the fellowship would cultivate a transatlantic network of emerging leaders, with distrust and stereotypes dissipating in favour of new insights and collaborative innovation. Despite the early focus on Anglo-American ties, the programme was global from the start—a function of the British reach—with early fellows hailing from Australia, New Zealand, South Africa and Malaysia. By the 1950s, this had expanded to include continental European fellows.

But even in this era of expansion, members of the community grew worried that the fellowship’s unique edge was blurring. A 1955 alumni survey revealed that the programme had begun to favour future academics over the broader civic and professional leaders it originally sought to cultivate. The critique prompted a major course correction for the first time in the fellowship’s history: reaffirming its mission to nurture leaders across government, labour, business and the arts. The lesson then, as now, was that self-evaluation and humility are essential tools of leadership. In hindsight, that moment of reckoning captured the essence of what the Harkness Fellowship would come to teach: leadership begins with the courage to challenge one’s own assumptions.

What strikes us most, when delving into the stories from this era, is how fellows’ exposure to new ways of being and doing stayed with them, taking their careers in unforeseen directions with unexpected impact. For instance, Ikahara Puketapu of New Zealand (1967–1968) adapted previously-unknown-to-him US models of urban community development to drive a reform agenda as Secretary of Māori Affairs, focused on local self-empowerment. Italy’s Silvana Sciarra (1974–1976) pioneered the judicial application of European Union law as president of the Italian Constitutional Court, drawing on the comparative approaches she’d honed at UCLA and Harvard. And the UK’s Aminatta Forna (1996–1997) turned her Harkness research project—interviewing women across the USA about their perceptions of motherhood—into her first book, spurring a previously unplanned transition from journalism to creative writing.

Since 1997, the Harkness Fellowship has focused on healthcare, and now more than ever, we note how new and different contexts spark unexpected innovation. Witnessing how our fresh-eyed fellows can examine, improve and export these innovations, even in areas they weren’t expecting to find them, only underscores the value of this mindset. For instance, 2018–2019 French fellow and policy researcher Angele Lansac Malatre learnt from US colleagues at the University of Washington who had deep practical experience with behavioural health integration. She went on to launch a ‘collaborative care’ programme in France, integrating mental healthcare into primary care so that patients’ psychiatric needs could be addressed more proactively. The pilot was a success, and the French government plans to roll the project out nationwide by the end of 2026.

The lesson for leaders? Be willing to be surprised by what works—and seek to understand the why and how it can be replicated and honed. Likewise, seek out or create the kinds of international opportunities (for yourself and colleagues) where your assumptions can be challenged in this way.

Lesson 2: harness the power of fellowship

The more recent history of the Harkness Fellowship is a lesson in making the most of fellowship, in all senses.

The decades leading up to the 1990s were not without strain. Global economic shifts and internal budget pressures forced the fellowship to scale back some of its European partnerships—decisions that tested certain relationships and raised difficult questions about what ‘fellowship’” truly meant when continuity could not be guaranteed. Those experiences underscored the importance of intentional design, revealing that the fellowship could not rely on resources alone; it had to be grounded in shared purpose and mutual respect, especially when circumstances change.

By 1997, the Commonwealth Fund recognised that the programme’s contribution would be greater—and the fellows’ experience more valuable—if it aligned directly with the Fund’s mission: ‘improving the health and productivity of Americans’. Accordingly, in 1997, the fellowship pivoted. We still welcomed applicants from a wide range of backgrounds, including academia, government, journalism, policy research and of course medical and health professions. But their projects and long-term career goals now had to impact healthcare policy and practice more directly.

The difference was striking—a lesson in what fellowship can truly mean. Much remained the same about the programme’s structure: mentors at affiliating institutions, an emphasis on intra-US travel, research projects culminating in year-end presentations. But the new, more targeted focus made possible a more comprehensive, supportive learning experience. Fellows were no longer just a cohort in name but shared in a slate of activities and experiences throughout the year: Washington, DC policy briefings, site visits and seminars across the USA and Canada, leadership development trainings and an annual symposium with senior international healthcare leaders. This focus on networking around a shared mission, in turn, meant that US colleagues and mentors took on a more active role, one that frequently blossomed into longstanding research collaborations and co-authored publications with fellows.

Above all, the focus shifted to collaborative impact, in the USA as well as in fellows’ home countries. For instance, 2015–2016 UK fellow and economist Irene Papanicolas collaborated with her mentor, physician and public health expert Ashish Jha to write a seminal paper debunking myths about American overutilisation of healthcare. Her research transformed US policymakers’ views on healthcare spending, which Jha credited to Papanicolas’s “totally fresh perspective to the problems that America has, that American policymakers can’t because they get stuck in their own debates”.

It’s experiences like these that have taught us, as Harkness programme leaders, to think about ‘fellowship’ in multiple senses. Even moments of contraction proved clarifying. They reminded us that while fellowship might be practically impacted by funding, it is ultimately not defined by size or scope, but by the values that connect us. The fellowship is more than funding and travel; at its heart, it is about community. We see the power of that connection in the ongoing international friendships and collaborations our fellows build, both during their time in the USA and for years to come, like the partnership between Papanicolas and Jha. Beyond that, we’ve found that the global network of Harkness alumni functions as an instant ‘phone a friend’ when considering new policies and practices. Data and literature review are important, but there’s nothing quite like calling up someone whose expertise and experience can help socialise new ideas to drive positive change.

The lesson? As a leader, cultivate this kind of network however you can—through travel, conferences or ideally immersing yourself in international exchanges of your own.

Lesson 3: embrace a multidisciplinary mindset

The Harkness Fellowship continues to evolve. The programme recently expanded to include Singapore and has increasingly embraced new methods and mindsets. A multidisciplinary approach has been a hallmark of the Harkness Fellowship since its earliest days, and it remains so today, with fellows tackling healthcare improvement from various disciplines. For instance, today’s programme has increasingly focused on health equity, and on the application of new technologies—such as artificial intelligence—to address health needs, especially those of marginalised communities. We’ve cultivated a more explicitly comparative lens, encouraging fellows to focus on innovations that work in different contexts and examine why they succeed. And we’ve continued to integrate the fellowship with the Commonwealth Fund’s other work, particularly its international health policy research, which provides our network of fellows with additional data and insights to drive change.

Holding continuity and change in balance continues to be part of our journey. But this evolution has not always been easy or straightforward. The 1997 shift towards health policy and practice, for example, while strengthening focus and coherence, narrowed the programme’s scope in ways that left some alumni from earlier decades wondering where their disciplines might fit in. Rebuilding those bridges—through dialogue, alumni engagement and renewed openness to diverse approaches—has become a critical part of our leadership work today. As we reconnect with fellows from all eras of the programme, the fellowship’s centennial inspires us to pursue an even broader approach to improving healthcare policy and practice. For instance, Aminatta Forna, the 1996 UK fellow who has gone on to an illustrious career as a writer, shared her experience teaching creative writing to medical students—helping them cultivate imagination and empathy to better understand the patient experience.

Looking to the next 100 years of the Harkness Fellowship, we can envision welcoming more fellows in this vein, bringing an ever-wider range of perspectives and tools to advance healthcare policy and practice.

As the Harkness Fellowship enters its next century, its story points to enduring values applicable far beyond any single organisation or programme: curiosity, adaptability, collaboration, reflection and fluency across disciplines. These are vital to effective leadership in health and healthcare, as well as to general human well-being. In a world marked by complexity and fragmentation, the Fellowship can model a new kind of leadership that is focused on connection over certainty and synthesis over narrow expertise in addressing one of the most complex challenges of our time, health.

The programme’s impact will only grow if it continues to evolve while staying true to its core values. Tomorrow’s leaders need courage beyond credentials. Fellowship and community must develop as a practice of shared purpose, not as a static bond. In a fragmented world, lasting fellowship means staying engaged, creating solutions together and respecting differences. The last century has taught us that change is constant; those who adapt, listen deeply, accept discomfort and build unseen bridges can drive positive change in the most complex environments.

Looking ahead to 2125, leadership and exchange may take new forms, but their essence will remain anchored in timeless values. The most meaningful exchanges may occur not in boardrooms or lecture halls, but across disciplines and cultures where artists, healers, ethicists, coders, scientists and designers collaborate to support healthier societies. The Harkness Fellowship can serve as a living laboratory for multidisciplinary dialogue, where ideas are shared and combined, and leadership is defined by the ability to navigate uncertainty with grace to help people live healthier lives. If the last century taught us to challenge assumptions, harness fellowship and embrace multidisciplinary thought, the next century will require us to commit to fellowship not as a moment but a mindset.
