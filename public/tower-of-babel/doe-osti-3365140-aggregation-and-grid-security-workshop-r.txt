Executive Summary
The Aggregation and Grid Security Workshop—held June 17–18, 2025, at the National
Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) in Golden, Colorado—brought together approximately 40 external
stakeholders from the energy sector, including virtual power plant (VPP) owner/operators, aggregators,
original equipment manufacturers (OEMs), utilities, testing and certification labs, trade associations, and
cybersecurity vendors. Led by key facilitators, the workshop focused on addressing cybersecurity
challenges and enhancing grid resilience for distributed energy resources (DERs) and VPPs. The
workshop was prompted by the realization that technological innovation in DERs, and aggregations
thereof, are rapidly outpacing current cybersecurity best practices and regulations in energy. Given the
increasing scale and complexity of distributed technologies within the energy sector with unclear
ownership of cybersecurity responsibilities and requirements, there is a lack of thorough understanding
and increased uncertainty surrounding cybersecurity risk.

The following are the major priorities identified during the workshop:

 Define and Implement Shared Cyber Responsibilities:
 Workshop attendees identified the need for a roadmap that (1) highlights the priority cyber
 risks and benefits associated with VPPs and other aggregated DER systems, (2) provides a
 detailed understanding of the mechanisms, processes, and types of stakeholders involved in
 VPPs, and (3) outlines a robust framework for distributing responsibilities based on identified
 shared cyber risks and stakeholder roles.

 Develop an Impact Assessment Capability:
 Industry can’t define responsibilities and prioritize mitigations if cybersecurity risks are not
 fully understood. Developing a comprehensive capability to evaluate, visualize, and understand
 the potential consequences of successful cyberattacks targeting aggregated DERs at the
 system-of-systems level, including the bulk electric system level, was also identified as one of
 the major priorities. Workshop attendees suggested including improved interdependent
 telecommunication modeling, technology validation, and mechanisms for data sharing between
 OEMs, utilities, third-party aggregators, and VPP owner/operators.

 Prepare for High-Impact Scenarios:
 To enhance clarity and preparedness, workshop attendees suggested developing targeted
 educational trainings and tabletop exercises focused on low-frequency, high-impact events
 such as cyberattacks during periods of high grid stress involving VPP owner/operators, OEMs,
 aggregators, and utilities. Attendees suggested prioritizing extreme, multistakeholder, all-
 hazards scenarios over routine training to clarify roles, responsibilities, and data sharing needs.
 Additionally, such training will support workforce development for operators, engineers, and
 managers, fostering a unified understanding of cybersecurity roles across all levels.

 Standardize VPPs and Data Sharing:
 Enhancing secure and standardized data sharing mechanisms between OEMs, aggregators, and
 utilities to improve situational awareness and enable proactive risk management was also
 identified as a major priority. Attendees suggested a secure and common data sharing
 mechanism would help alleviate some of the risks that may be associated with cloud platforms.

 Establish a Cross-Stakeholder Group or Consortium:
 Lastly, workshop attendees identified forming a team of utilities, OEMs, VPP owner/operators,
 and aggregators to align security controls, train workforces, and understand the economic,
 social, and operational impacts of cyber events on the grid, shifting from static compliance to a
 dynamic, forward-looking, and risk-based approach.

The workshop underscored that no single entity can address these challenges alone; a sector-wide
approach that includes the broader DER community is imperative to ensure the reliable and secure
operation of the electric grid. The priorities identified above provide a roadmap for collaborative action,

Workshop Overview
Rapid technological changes and increased reliance on DERs and VPPs managed by cloud-
native platforms are transforming the grid into a digitally connected, software-defined system
that could introduce new complexities. For example, these changes can create an expanded
attack surface, supply chain security risks, and zero-day vulnerabilities resulting from
inconsistent regulations and data silos. As utilities increasingly rely on VPPs, especially during
peak demand, and cybersecurity policies vary across regions, proactive cybersecurity and clear
stakeholder roles and responsibilities are needed (Industry Research 2025).

The goal of the Aggregation and Grid Security Workshop was to identify and address the critical
cybersecurity challenges of a rapidly changing electric grid and to drive cybersecurity priorities
that foster shared responsibility among all energy sector stakeholders, including aggregators,
DER manufacturers, VPP owners/operators, and utilities, among others. Since no standardized
cyber ownership and participation model currently exists for VPPs or DER aggregators, the
workshop aimed to:

• Characterize the priority cyber risks and benefits associated with aggregated DER systems or
 VPPs
• Evaluate the distribution of cyber risk among stakeholders within the DER ecosystem, such
 as VPP owners/operators, DER aggregators, utilities, and vendors, etc.
• Prioritize shared responsibilities for managing cyber risks across all stakeholders
• Understand the impact of compromised DER devices or systems within VPP networks on the
 bulk electric systems’ security
• Accelerate the adoption of priority cybersecurity controls among VPPs, DER aggregators,
 and utility aggregators.

The Need for This Workshop
As the integration of grid-connected DERs increases, new markets and technology innovation
are driving operational changes on the grid, making it more digitally connected and software
defined. This modernization drives new utility business models with a growing number of non-
utility stakeholders—such as VPP owners/operators and aggregators—playing an active role in
energy markets and providing grid services that affect grid reliability. These new non-utility
stakeholders are shifting from selling kilowatts to selling flexibility by transforming batteries and
smart inverters into dispatchable assets that utilities value. While the degree of DER adoption
and participation varies around the country, there is a wide variation in (1) cybersecurity policies
and processes for managing and maintaining aggregated DER systems, (2) tools and
technologies that enhance coordination across multiple stakeholders without sacrificing data
confidentiality, and (3) standards and regulations for defining roles and responsibilities. This
variability is a key challenge for applying consistent and interoperable cybersecurity controls to
ensure collective defense of the grid at the national level.

During the workshop, attendees identified the following themes to address:

• What are the key cybersecurity challenges in aggregated DER systems, including VPPs?
• What are the major trends that are expected to have the greatest impact to cybersecurity?
• What are the greatest challenges in understanding the distribution of risk across stakeholders,
 including VPP owners/operators, DER aggregators, utilities, and vendors?
• How can we promote shared responsibilities for managing risks across all stakeholders?
• What is the potential impact of compromised devices or systems within a VPP network or an
 aggregation of DERs on bulk electric system security?
• As the attack surface increases, what will be the mix of utility versus aggregator-controlled
 systems?
• What new tools are needed to secure aggregated DER systems?
• What type of training and education is needed and on what topics?

Audience
The participants encompassed a broad spectrum of stakeholders, reflecting the diverse landscape
of DER and VPP operations. Their expertise and knowledge in aggregated DER systems, VPPs,
technology, grid management, energy markets, regulation, and cybersecurity played a crucial
role in steering the workshop. They contributed valuable perspectives to pinpoint major risks,
analyze risk distribution, and foster collective accountability for addressing these critical
cybersecurity challenges.

Session Summaries and Key Takeaways
Session 1: Cybersecurity Dependencies for Resilient DERs and VPPs

The regulatory landscape for DERs is shaped by the involvement of numerous stakeholders,
which has led to the establishment of various regulations. However, as the number of DER
owners continues to grow, ensuring safety becomes increasingly complex and highlights the
need for more flexible technological solutions. In this context, the concept of sovereign power
has emerged, with discussions focusing on encouraging individuals to own their own
cryptographic keys for their equipment. As more people install their own energy systems, like
solar panels and batteries, keeping the grid safe becomes harder. To solve this, the panel
discussed sovereign power. The idea is to give individuals more control by having them manage
their own digital security keys for their equipment. This approach is envisioned to start within
campuses or smaller communities before potentially scaling to individual users.

Liability and risk are significant concerns, particularly in scenarios involving black sky
operations, such as the leasing of conductors after major events like hurricanes. When things go
wrong during a disaster, it’s often unclear who is legally responsible or who pays for the damage.
This is a major worry during black sky operations when emergency work is required to fix the
power grid after a massive catastrophe.

For example, if a utility company needs to lease or borrow power lines and equipment to get the
lights back on after a major hurricane, the legal risks regarding who owns, maintains, or is liable
for that equipment become very complicated.

The risks extend to utilities as well, especially if a major distributed energy resource
management system provider or aggregator is compromised, which could have cascading effects
across multiple utilities within a region. A foundational problem for VPPs is the lack of clear
identification and management, as their composition can change rapidly and make oversight
challenging. A major hurdle for VPPs is that they are hard to track and manage. Because the
components that make up a VPP can change in an instant, it is difficult for regulators and grid
operators to keep a clear eye on what they are actually doing. It helps to remember what a VPP
is: it’s not a single facility, but a cloud-based power plant. It’s a collection of hundreds or
thousands of small energy sources—like home batteries, rooftop solar panels, and electric
vehicle chargers—all connected by software to act as one big power source.

Data silos present another major issue, as OEMs and meter providers often do not share data,
resulting in blind spots for utilities. For example, some utilities may not ingest telemetry from
certain OEMs, limiting their visibility and operational awareness. This fragmentation raises
questions about how to collectively distribute risk when data is siloed and ownership is
ambiguous.

Cybersecurity remains a critical focus in DER and inverter-based system integration, with key
challenges including determining responsibility for implementing and overseeing controls (i.e.,
“who controls the controls?”) and avoiding overreliance on any single entity, such as an OEM,

which is widely seen as unsustainable. There is growing consensus that current incentives often
misalign costs and benefits, prompting calls to rebalance them so that those who gain from
secure grid connectivity—utilities and customers—share the investment burden. As a result, an
emerging perspective is that customers (i.e., DER owners or aggregators) may need to contribute
to cybersecurity costs as a condition of grid interconnection to ensure more equitable and
sustainable risk management across the ecosystem.

Session 2: Assessing the Impact of Compromised DER Devices or
Systems Within VPPs on U.S. Bulk Energy Systems

Utilities are increasingly recognizing the need to model the potential impacts of compromised
DER aggregations, yet there remains a notable lack of comprehensive discussion and
preparedness to address these risks in depth. While some progress has been made in resource
adequacy modeling—particularly with inverter-based resources—the integration of cyber
considerations, such as the implications of inverter-based resources tripping, restoration, or
permanent disabling (bricking), is still in its early stages. This gap highlights the broader
challenge of validating and certifying cyber standards for DER equipment, a topic that has
generated significant discussion but requires more robust implementation and oversight.

 6

A persistent obstacle is the difficulty in accessing and leveraging data from behind-the-meter
DERs. For example, utilities often cannot access critical solar or battery data from many
residential solar systems through standard metering infrastructure, such as existing smart meters,
which limits their operational visibility. This issue is compounded by the risk of single-
threadiness, where single points of failure—such as Remote Terminal Units—can leave utilities
reliant on manual controls if automated systems fail.

The sector also suffers from a lack of cohesive strategy, with no centralized leadership to unify
cybersecurity efforts across diverse stakeholders. This fragmentation is exacerbated by
regulatory lag, as technological advancements in DER products frequently outpace the
development of corresponding regulatory guidelines. The division of responsibility between
OEMs and utilities further complicates matters; grid operators often bear the risk but must
depend on OEMs for the implementation of cybersecurity measures.

Session 3: Ensuring Continuity in the Face of Cyberattacks

The evolving cybersecurity threat landscape presents significant challenges for DERs and VPPs,
as best practices often struggle to keep pace with rapidly changing attack vectors. A critical issue
is the shortage of professionals with expertise in supervisory control and data acquisition systems
within the aggregator space, compounded by a lack of incentives for DER owners to maintain
robust cybersecurity measures after initial interconnection. This workforce gap is particularly
concerning given the substantial visibility and control gaps for residential and small commercial
VPPs, which leaves them highly vulnerable to sophisticated attacks, such as those exemplified
by Volt Typhoon-type manipulations.

Standard response protocols in the event of a cyber incident typically involve a sequence of
disconnection, identification, isolation, eradication, and reconnection, with operators frequently
resorting to manual controls when automated systems are compromised. Maintaining system
stability during such disruptions is a key concern, requiring careful balancing of generation drops
with controlled load shedding to prevent broader grid instability.

The sector also faces misaligned incentives: aggregators are primarily motivated to fulfill
contracts cost-effectively, while regulators emphasize security, leading to conflicting investment

shared investment from utilities a necessity. This fuels ongoing debates about risk allocation—
whether utilities, VPPs, or OEMs should bear the primary responsibility, especially as cloud-
based DER platforms become more prevalent.

Effective cybersecurity prioritization must consider the criticality of each system, including
factors such as size, the number of customers affected, and potential outage duration. There is a
growing need to quantify cyber risk and related investments in practical, budget-aligned terms to
facilitate informed decision-making. Recommendations for cyber-informed engineering
emphasize the importance of design-level resilience and the inclusion of analog redundancies to
mitigate digital vulnerabilities.

Session 4: Role of Aggregators in Securing VPPs

Questions have emerged regarding the precise definition of an aggregator within the DER and
VPP ecosystem, reflecting the complexity and evolving nature of these roles. There is a strong
consensus that customers and asset owners should bear minimal responsibility for cybersecurity;
if forced to deal with complex security rules or high technical costs, they will likely get
frustrated and quit, which would hurt overall participation in energy programs. As one
participant noted, "Customers/asset owners should be minimally responsible for cybersecurity. If
they have to do something, they lose."

The debate continues over the extent of vendor responsibility, with some arguing that vendors
should play a supporting role, while others believe they should assume full responsibility for
secure protocols and system design. This uncertainty is particularly pronounced in VPP leasing
models where ownership of security responsibilities remains unclear.

VPPs operating on thin margins often focus on minimizing risk just enough to meet regulatory
requirements frequently passing additional costs to customers or vendors. This approach can
result in a bare-minimum compliance culture where reputation is currently the only motive for
implementing cybersecurity fixes. A clear distinction is also drawn between general DER
security and the more specialized cybersecurity needs of VPPs.

Session 5: Can You Say That in English? Simplifying Grid Reliability
and Security Standards With Common Language

The complexity of standards in the VPP ecosystem is driven by the involvement of numerous
stakeholders, a proliferation of standards, and the absence of clear, unified rules specifically
tailored for VPPs. This environment creates both opportunities and risks: while VPPs are
recognized as entrepreneurial ventures that can unlock new value streams, they also introduce
novel vulnerabilities and operational uncertainties. One proposed solution to this complexity is
the formal registration of entities and their roles—such as with Independent System Operators,
Regional Transmission Organizations, or Distribution System Operators—to establish clearer
lines of responsibility and oversight.

 Graphic from Uri Sadot, SolarEdge Technologies

A key distinction has emerged between traditional VPPs and cloud-capable aggregation entities,
raising questions about which bodies are best positioned to regulate the associated risks. Many
existing standards, often based on National Institute of Standards and Technology guidance,
emphasize perimeter security; however, this approach may fall short in addressing the unique
challenges of distributed, cloud-centric architectures. This has led to ongoing debates about who
should be responsible for validating the cybersecurity requirements that utilities impose on
aggregators, and how to design effective cyber defense exercises that meaningfully involve these
entities.

 16

There is a growing recognition of the need to refine learning objectives for cybersecurity, such as
distinguishing between interoperability and interdependencies, and shifting from basic to
essential requirements. The increasing reliance on Software as a Service in the VPP sector has
exposed a training gap, particularly regarding the delineation of roles and responsibilities in a
cloud-based environment.

Allowing states to set their own standards could foster localized innovation, but it also
underscores the tension between the need for flexibility and the imperative for high-level
alignment across the industry. Discussions have considered whether National Association of
Regulatory Utility Commissioners baselines could serve as a foundational framework for this
evolving paradigm. Another challenge lies in applying concepts from physical system security to
virtual products and platforms, such as those offered by aggregation and DERMS platform
providers, which may require fundamentally different approaches.

The industry is also witnessing a shift from recommended practices (should) to mandatory
requirements (shall), reflecting a broader move toward enforceable standards. There is potential
for the Department of Energy to develop tailored roadmaps for different stakeholder groups and
support more coordinated progress.

Quantifying the cost of cybersecurity and articulating the consumer benefits remain ongoing
challenges, as does planning for critical operations—such as black start procedures—during

Session 6: The Role of Mission Rehearsal in U.S. Grid Resilience

The current landscape of cybersecurity exercises and training in the energy sector features a
range of established programs and competitions, such as Cyber Fire, GridEx, CyManII,
CyberForce, CyberPatriot, and cyber-informed engineering-based challenges. While these
initiatives provide valuable experience, a significant gap remains in their coverage of VPPs and
DERs. Most existing grid exercises are heavily focused on transmission systems, leaving VPPs
and DERs as notable blind spots in preparedness efforts.

GridEx, hosted by the Electricity Information Sharing and Analysis Center, exemplifies this
limitation by concentrating primarily on transmission and systemic risk. The exercise often relies
on assumptions that may not hold in real-world extreme scenarios, such as the loss of critical
infrastructure like airports. This highlights the need for the development of tabletop exercises
specifically tailored to the unique challenges posed by VPPs and DER aggregators.

A persistent challenge is the lack of clear definitions for aggregators and VPPs, as well as their
overlapping roles with utilities. This ambiguity complicates the design of effective exercises and
the assignment of responsibilities. Data sharing between aggregators, OEMs, and utilities is
another area of concern; current practices are often bespoke and limited, hindering the ability of
utilities to access real-time data from DERs—such as through smart meters—that is essential for
effective preparation and response.

Stakeholders have expressed particular concern about threat scenarios in which attackers target
VPPs during periods of high demand, such as hot days when utilities rely on these resources for
load management. Reference was made to various tabletop exercises that underscored the
nation’s unpreparedness for cyber warfare and emphasized the need for a civil defense mindset.
There is growing interest in exercising extreme, low-frequency, high-impact scenarios, rather
than focusing solely on day-to-day cyberattacks.

Education is also a priority, with a recognized need to better inform engineering teams about the
potential impacts of cyberattacks and the critical role of VPPs in grid stability. Understanding the
scale at which utilities can implement load shedding—whether at the device, neighborhood, or
community level—is essential for effective incident response planning.

Additional recommendations include requiring VPPs to register if they are connected to the
energy market to improve oversight and coordination. There is also concern about cascading
failures of compliance due to third-party risk dependencies, as well as the lack of clear
delineation of ownership for risk management and incident response. These challenges
underscore the urgent need for more comprehensive VPP and DER focused cybersecurity
exercises, clearer definitions and roles, improved data sharing, and enhanced education and
preparedness across the sector.

Appendix B. Definitions
• Virtual Power Plants (VPP): VPPs are aggregations of DERs that can balance electrical
 loads and provide utility-scale and utility-grade grid services like a traditional power plant 1.
 They enable smaller energy resources to participate in energy markets and provide grid
 services as aggregated entities, which would otherwise not be feasible.
• Inverter-Based Resources (IBRs): Generation or storage systems using power electronic
 converters connected to the grid (transmission, sub-transmission, or distribution) and
 managed as a single resource at a common interconnection point.
• Distributed Energy Resources (DERs): DERs are grid edge devices, including generation
 resources (wind, solar), storage (battery, hydrogen), and energy management systems
 (demand response, building load controllers) that are typically connected to the distribution
 grid. DERs also form the core components of VPPs.
• DER Aggregators: DER aggregators are entities that combine multiple DERs into a single,
 manageable unit to participate in energy markets and provide grid services. Aggregators
 optimize the collective output of these resources, enabling smaller DERs to access markets
 and services they couldn't individually.
• Cloud Service Provider (CSP): CSPs offer cloud-based platforms and services for VPP
 operations, including data storage, processing, and analytics. They may also provide the
 technological infrastructure that enables remote monitoring, control, and optimization of
 DERs within a VPP.
• Consumer: Consumers own or operate DERs and participate in a VPP by allowing their
 resources to be aggregated and controlled as part of the larger system.
• Utility: Utilities are responsible for electricity distribution and grid management that can
 leverage VPPs to enhance grid reliability and resilience. Utilities play a crucial role in VPP
 deployment by partnering with technology providers and customers to create VPPs that may
 benefit both the grid and consumers.
• Vendor: Vendors supply hardware, software, and services essential to the operation of a
 VPP. This may include manufacturers of control systems or DER equipment as well as
 providers of VPP or energy management software.
• VPP Owner: VPP owners are responsible for the overall management and operation of
 VPPs, including integration with other stakeholders and market participation. VPP owners
 may be a utility, a third-party operator, or a cloud service provider.
• VPP Operator: VPP operators are tasked with executing VPP services, managing day-to-
 day operations, and ensuring seamless integration with other stakeholders. This role may be
 performed by the VPP owner or a separate entity contracted for this purpose.
• VPP Network: VPP networks are the communication infrastructure that interconnects VPP
 devices, users, utilities, Independent System Operators or Regional Transmission
 Organizations, and energy markets. This network enables real-time data exchange, control
 commands, and market participation.
• VPP Devices: VPP devices are any hardware connected to the VPP network, including
 power generating units, energy storage systems, smart loads, and control systems. These
 devices may be owned by the VPP owner or third parties.
