Covariant phase space approach to noncommutativity in tensile and tensionless open strings
and Sourav Maji

Abstract
We study noncommutativity in open strings using the covariant phase space formalism. For tensile open strings in a constant Kalb-Ramond background, we show that the (pre)-symplectic current splits into a bulk kinetic term plus an exact boundary term, recovering the Seiberg-Witten noncommutativity parameter. We then extend the analysis to intrinsically tensionless strings. In the absence of background fields, the reduced phase space is degenerate and carries no intrinsic Poisson structure. In the presence of a constant Kalb-Ramond field, the symplectic current localises entirely on the boundary, so that the physical phase space becomes purely boundary-supported and the endpoint coordinates acquire a noncommutative Poisson algebra. Including a boundary gauge-field coupling similarly leads to a boundary symplectic form governed by the effective Born-Infeld combination on the D-brane. Our results provide a unified description of noncommutativity in both tensile and tensionless open strings.

At the technical level, the standard derivation of this result is crucially dependent on structures that are special to the tensile string. The operator approach relies on the existence of a non-degenerate worldsheet metric, a well-defined two-dimensional conformal field theory (CFT), and the corresponding logarithmic propagator for the embedding fields. These ingredients enable separating the holomorphic and antiholomorphic sectors, explicitly solving the mixed boundary conditions, and extracting the endpoint commutator from the antisymmetric part of the boundary correlator. The tensionless limit

In this limit, the worldsheet geometry degenerates into Carrollian geometry, the conventional left- and right-moving decompositions cease to exist in their usual forms, and the theory becomes ultralocal in the spatial direction. Consequently, the standard oscillator expansion and the familiar logarithmic propagator are no longer available, so the usual route to derive endpoint noncommutativity breaks down. For this reason, tensionless theory calls for a framework that does not presuppose an ordinary conformal worldsheet description; see Section

The covariant phase space (CPS) formalism provides precisely such a framework. Rather than deriving the phase space structure indirectly from operator algebras or propagators, the CPS method constructs it directly from the variational principle. Starting from the classical action, one identifies the symplectic potential from its first variation and the associated pre-symplectic current from a second antisymmetrised variation, thereby obtaining the symplectic structure on the space of classical solutions in a manifestly covariant manner. 1.1
Motivation for the covariant phase space formalism

For the ordinary tensile open string, the target space noncommutativity is usually derived from the worldsheet operator formalism. In the presence of a constant Kalb-Ramond field, the open string boundary conditions become mixed, and the boundary two-point function acquires an antisymmetric part proportional to the Seiberg–Witten parameter
\Theta^{\mu\nu}
. The equal-time endpoint commutator is then read off from this antisymmetric part of the boundary propagator
[
69
]
. We briefly review this standard derivation in Appendix
A
. That derivation, however, relies crucially on structures special to the tensile theory: a nondegenerate worldsheet metric, the existence of an ordinary
2
-dim CFT, the holomorphic/antiholomorphic decomposition of the embedding fields, the usual oscillator expansion, and the logarithmic form of the propagator. In the tensionless limit
T\to 0
, these ingredients cease to be available in their standard form. The worldsheet geometry degenerates to a Carrollian geometry, the left- and right-moving sectors no longer admit the usual separation, and the theory becomes ultralocal along the spatial direction of the worldsheet. As a result, the standard boundary-propagator argument for endpoint noncommutativity no longer works.

The CPS viewpoint is particularly useful because it treats the tensile and tensionless theories within a single geometric language. In the tensile theory, it reproduces the usual Seiberg–Witten noncommutativity parameter from the boundary symplectic form. In the tensionless theory, where the free bulk symplectic structure becomes degenerate, it reveals that a constant antisymmetric background generates a symplectic form supported entirely on the boundary. The resulting endpoint noncommutativity is therefore not merely a deformation of the standard tensile result but the surviving remnant of the phase space itself in the Carrollian regime. When a boundary
U(1)
gauge field is present, the corresponding boundary contribution is incorporated most naturally using the CPS formalism with boundaries (CPSB).

1.2
Summary of main results

In this paper, we develop a CPS treatment of noncommutativity for open bosonic strings in constant antisymmetric backgrounds, with the aim of placing the usual tensile theory and the intrinsically tensionless theory within a single geometric framework. The central idea is that the noncommutative structure of the open string endpoints can be derived directly from the symplectic data on the space of classical solutions, without relying on the standard operator-based analysis of boundary correlators.

For the tensile open string in a constant Kalb-Ramond background, we show that the pre-symplectic current naturally splits into two qualitatively distinct pieces: a genuine bulk contribution coming from the kinetic term, and an exact contribution generated by the antisymmetric background field. After imposing the mixed open-string boundary conditions, the full symplectic structure reduces at the endpoints to a boundary form whose inverse reproduces the familiar Seiberg–Witten noncommutativity parameter,

\Theta^{\mu\nu}=2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{g+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B}}\right)^{\mu\nu}_{A}.

(1.1)

or equivalently,

\mathbf{\Theta}=-(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}(\boldsymbol{g}+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathscr{B}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathscr{B}}(\boldsymbol{g}-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mathscr{B}})^{-1}.

(1.2)

Thus, in the tensile theory, the noncommutative geometry of the D-brane worldvolume emerges directly from the boundary symplectic structure of the CPS.
qWe then study the tensionless reduction of this result by taking the limit

T=\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\to 0,\qquad\alpha^{\prime}\to\infty.

(1.3)

In this limit, the Seiberg–Witten parameter remains finite and approaches

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\text{tensionless}}=\lim_{\alpha^{\prime}\to\infty}\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\boldsymbol{\mathscr{B}}^{-1},

(1.4)

provided the antisymmetric background is invertible on the relevant brane directions. This provides an important bridge between the ordinary tensile description and the intrinsically tensionless theory.

The most significant result of the paper concerns the CPS of the intrinsically tensionless string itself. Starting from the ILST action, we show that in the absence of background antisymmetric fields the bulk pre-symplectic current vanishes identically, and therefore the corresponding symplectic form is degenerate

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{0}=0.

(1.5)

This implies that the free tensionless open string carries no nontrivial intrinsic bulk Poisson structure. In this sense, the ordinary propagating phase space collapses in the Carrollian regime.

Once a constant Kalb-Ramond background is included, however, the situation changes qualitatively. Although the free tensionless sector continues to contribute no nontrivial bulk symplectic form, the antisymmetric background generates an exact (pre)symplectic current, so that the full symplectic structure localises entirely on the boundary. The reduced boundary symplectic form takes the form

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{bdry}}=\dfrac{1}{2}\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\,\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu},

(1.6)

and therefore the endpoint coordinates satisfy the noncommutative Poisson algebra

\{X^{\mu},X^{\nu}\}=\mathscr{B}^{\mu\nu}.

(1.7)

Equivalently, the intrinsic noncommutativity parameter of the tensionless theory is

\Theta^{\mu\nu}=\mathscr{B}^{\mu\nu}.

(1.8)

This shows that, in the tensionless regime, noncommutativity is not merely a deformation of an otherwise nondegenerate bulk phase space. Rather, the boundary noncommutative algebra is the surviving physical remnant of the phase space itself.

We further extend the analysis by including a boundary
U(1)
gauge field coupling for strings ending on a D
p
-brane. In this case, the appropriate framework is the CPSB. The resulting pre-symplectic current remains exact, and the boundary symplectic structure is controlled by the effective antisymmetric combination on the brane

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\text{bdry}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathscr{F}_{\mu\nu}\,\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu},

(1.9)

where
\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}
denotes the gauge invariant Born-Infeld type field strength induced on the boundary. The endpoint Poisson brackets are then governed by

\{X^{\mu},X^{\nu}\}=\mathscr{F}^{\mu\nu},\qquad\Theta^{\mu\nu}=\mathscr{F}^{\mu\nu}.

(1.10)

Thus, in the tensionless theory, the noncommutative geometry is determined more generally by the antisymmetric boundary data seen by the open-string endpoints.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section

Gist of the two formalisms.

Iyer-Wald formalism.

Standard Hamiltonian mechanics picks a preferred time and breaks covariance. The Iyer-Wald (IW) formalism keeps covariance by taking the
space of all solutions to the equations of motion
as the phase space. It gives a geometric derivation of conserved charges and Hamiltonians directly from the Lagrangian.

•

From fields to phase space.
Start with the space of fields
\mathcal{F}
and impose the equations of motion
\mathbf{E}_{\phi}=0
to obtain the CPS
\mathcal{P}
.

•

The symplectic structure.
Varying the Lagrangian defines the symplectic potential
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{IW}}
and hence the symplectic current
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{IW}}
, whose integral over a Cauchy surface
\mathcal{C}
gives the symplectic form
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\text{IW}}
, the basic geometric structure on phase space.

•

Symmetry and charges.
In diffeomorphism-invariant theories, every generator
\xi
determines a Noether current
\boldsymbol{J}_{\xi}
and Noether charge
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\xi}
, allowing the Hamiltonian variation to be written entirely in terms of these.

CPS with boundaries (CPSB).

In systems with boundaries, especially in holography, the original IW formalism becomes ambiguous. Harlow and Wu (HW) extended it by adding a boundary term
\boldsymbol{\ell}
to the action to ensure a well-posed variational principle.

•

Stationarity with boundaries.
The full action has a boundary term
\int_{\partial\mathcal{M}}\boldsymbol{\ell}
. A well-posed variational principle requires a boundary consistency condition relating the symplectic potential to a
(d-2)
-form
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}
.

•

The refined Hamiltonian.
The Hamiltonian is adjusted to include boundary contributions from
\boldsymbol{\ell}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{C}}
, so that it still correctly generates symmetries in the presence of edges.

•

Gravity as a boundary term.
For diffeomorphism covariant theories like GR, the bulk Noether current is exact (
\boldsymbol{J}_{\xi}=\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{Q}_{\xi}
), so the Hamiltonian becomes an integral over the boundary of the Cauchy surface
\partial\mathcal{C}
, supporting the holographic principle.

3
CPS for tensile strings in the presence of a uniform Kalb–Ramond field

In this section, we apply the CPS machinery to study the specific physical system: a tensile string propagating through a target spacetime with a constant Kalb-Ramond background. To this end, our analysis naturally begins with the standard Polyakov action

\begin{split}S_{\text{tensile}}&=-\frac{T}{2}\int_{\Sigma}d^{2}\sigma\left(\sqrt{-g}\,g^{ab}\,\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\,g_{\mu\nu}+\varepsilon^{ab}B_{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\right).\end{split}

In the conformal gauge, the action simplifies to

S_{\text{conf}}=-\frac{T}{2}\int_{\Sigma}d^{2}\sigma\left(\eta^{ab}\,\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\,g_{\mu\nu}+\varepsilon^{ab}B_{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\right),

(3.1)

where
\eta^{ab}
represents the inverse of the flat worldsheet metric characteristic of the conformal gauge. From this simplified action, we can easily read off the corresponding Lagrangian
2
-form

\boldsymbol{L}=-\frac{T}{2}\left(\eta^{ab}\,\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\,g_{\mu\nu}+\varepsilon^{ab}B_{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\right).

(3.2)

The core of the CPS method involves taking the first variation of the Lagrangian form. This variation naturally splits into two distinct parts: a bulk term that dictates the equations of motion (e.o.m.), and an exact boundary term that defines the symplectic potential. Mathematically, this is expressed as

\delta\boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{E}_{\mu}\,\delta X^{\mu}+\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}(X,\delta X).

By isolating the bulk piece, we recover the standard wave equation for a free string, as the constant
B
-field topological term does not contribute to the bulk dynamics

\boldsymbol{E}_{\mu}=T\eta^{ab}g_{\mu\nu}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}=0.

(3.3)

Meanwhile, the boundary piece yields the symplectic potential
\theta^{b}
, which encodes the foundational phase space structure before taking the second variation

\theta^{b}=-T\left(\eta^{ab}g_{\mu\nu}+\varepsilon^{ab}B_{\mu\nu}\right)\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\delta X^{\nu}.

(3.4)

To construct the symplectic current
\omega^{b}
, we apply an exterior derivative in the configuration space of the fields. In practice, this means taking two independent, anti-symmetrised variations (
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
) of the symplectic potential. We calculate

\displaystyle\omega^{b}(X;\delta_{1}X,\delta_{2}X)

\displaystyle=\delta_{1}\theta^{b}(X,\delta_{2}X)-\delta_{2}\theta^{b}(X,\delta_{1}X)

\displaystyle=-T\left(\eta^{ab}g_{\mu\nu}+\varepsilon^{ab}B_{\mu\nu}\right)\big(\partial_{a}\delta_{1}X^{\mu}\delta_{2}X^{\nu}-\partial_{a}\delta_{2}X^{\mu}\delta_{1}X^{\nu}\big)

\displaystyle=T\left(\eta^{ab}g_{\mu\nu}+\varepsilon^{ab}B_{\mu\nu}\right)\big(\partial_{a}\delta_{2}X^{\mu}\delta_{1}X^{\nu}-\partial_{a}\delta_{1}X^{\mu}\delta_{2}X^{\nu}\big)

\displaystyle=T\left(\eta^{ab}g_{\mu\nu}+\varepsilon^{ab}B_{\mu\nu}\right)\partial_{a}\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}.

At this stage, it is highly insightful to dissect the symplectic current into contributions arising from the spacetime metric
g
and the antisymmetric
B
-field. Let’s look closely at the
B
-field contribution,
\omega^{b}_{B}
. Because of the antisymmetry of the field, we can cleverly rewrite it as an exact total worldsheet derivative (extracting a factor of
1/2
)

\omega^{b}_{B}=T\varepsilon^{ab}B_{\mu\nu}\partial_{a}\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}=\frac{T}{2}\varepsilon^{ab}B_{\mu\nu}\partial_{a}(\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}).

(3.5)

Notice that this
B
-field component exactly gives the boundary-only result. The tensile string gives a non-vanishing bulk contribution generated by the spacetime metric

\omega^{b}_{g}=T\eta^{ab}\eta_{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{a}\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}.

(3.6)

The total symplectic current
\omega^{b}
can be cleanly organised as the sum of a bulk current and the derivative of a boundary term

\omega^{b}=\omega^{b}_{g}+\partial_{a}k_{B}^{ab}

(3.7a)

with

k_{B}^{ab}=\frac{T}{2}\varepsilon^{ab}B_{\mu\nu}\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}.

(3.7b)

In literature, the quantity
\boldsymbol{k}
is called the
surface charge
form.
Integrating the symplectic current over a Cauchy slice
\mathcal{C}
on the worldsheet yields the fundamental symplectic form
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
of the theory. The tensile string’s symplectic form possesses both bulk and boundary characteristics

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\Omega}&=\int_{\mathcal{C}}\omega_{g}+\int_{\mathcal{C}}\omega_{B}\\
&=\int_{\mathcal{C}}\omega_{g}+\Big[k_{B}\Big]_{\partial C}\\
&=\int_{\mathcal{C}}T\eta^{ab}\eta_{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{a}\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}+\Bigg[\frac{T}{2}B_{\mu\nu}\,\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}\Bigg]_{\partial C}.\end{split}

(3.8)

3.1
Noncommutativity from the symplectic structure of open strings

In this section, we transition our focus to open strings. A fascinating consequence of turning on a constant background
B
-field is the emergence of noncommutative spacetime geometry localised at the endpoints of an open string. We will now rigorously derive this noncommutative parameter,
\Theta^{\mu\nu}

Action and boundary conditions.

We resume with the Polyakov action in the conformal gauge, explicitly parameterised with the inverse string tension
2\pi\alpha^{\prime}

S_{\text{tensile}}=-\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\int d\tau d\sigma\left(-g_{\mu\nu}(\dot{X}^{\mu}\dot{X}^{\nu}-X^{\prime\mu}X^{\prime\nu})+\varepsilon^{ab}B_{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\right).

(3.9)

As we are going to compare the result with

69

\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}=-\dfrac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}B_{\mu\nu},

and rewriting the action in terms of the rescaled field as

S_{\text{tensile}}=\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\int d\tau d\sigma\left(g_{\mu\nu}(\dot{X}^{\mu}\dot{X}^{\nu}-X^{\prime\mu}X^{\prime\nu})+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\varepsilon^{ab}\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\right).

(3.11)

By varying the action with respect to the worldsheet time derivative (
\dot{X}^{\mu}
), we obtain the generalised canonical momentum density
\Pi_{\mu}
. The presence of the
B

\Pi_{\mu}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{X}^{\mu}}=\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}g_{\mu\nu}\dot{X}^{\nu}+\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}X^{\prime\nu}.

(3.12)

When ensuring the overall variation of the action is zero, the boundary terms evaluated at the string endpoints (
\sigma=0,\pi
) must vanish. For freely moving endpoints, this forces the following constraint

11

g_{\mu\nu}X^{\prime\nu}+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\dot{X}^{\nu}=0,\qquad\sigma=0,\pi.

This boundary condition intimately links the temporal and spatial variations of the string at its endpoints. We can algebraically invert this relationship to express the temporal velocity
\dot{X}^{\mu}
strictly in terms of the spatial gradient
X^{\prime\nu}

\dot{X}^{\mu}=-\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}(\mathscr{B}^{-1}g)^{\mu}{}_{\nu}X^{\prime\nu}.

(3.14)

Conversely, we can isolate the spatial derivative
X^{\prime\mu}
, describing the string’s local orientation at the boundary as a function of its velocity

X^{\prime\mu}=-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}(g^{-1}\mathscr{B})^{\mu}{}_{\nu}{\dot{X^{\nu}}}.

(3.15)

By substituting this spatial derivative expression back into our earlier formula for the canonical momentum (
3.12
), we establish a new relationship defining an

driven solely by the velocity. This naturally leads to the identification of the

\Pi_{\mu}=\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\left[g_{\mu\nu}-(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}(\mathscr{B}g^{-1}\mathscr{B})_{\mu\nu}\right]\dot{X}^{\nu}=\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}G_{\mu\nu}\dot{X}^{\nu},

(3.16)

where the explicit matrix form of this effective metric
G_{\mu\nu}
is given by

G_{\mu\nu}=\left[g_{\mu\nu}-(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}(\mathscr{B}g^{-1}\mathscr{B})_{\mu\nu}\right],

(3.17)

and its inverse,
G^{\mu\nu}
, is expressed as

G^{\mu\nu}=\left(\frac{1}{g+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B}}\right)^{\mu\nu}_{S}=\left(\frac{1}{g+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B}}\;g\;\frac{1}{g-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B}}\right)^{\mu\nu},

(3.18)

where
(~)_{S}
denotes the symmetric part of the matrix.

Symplectic structure.

Returning to the phase space formulation, the symplectic potential
\theta^{b}
(now tracking factors of
\alpha^{\prime}
) takes the form

\theta^{b}=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\eta^{ab}g_{\mu\nu}+\varepsilon^{ab}\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\right)\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\delta X^{\nu}.

(3.19)

Taking two antisymmetrised exterior variations provides the symplectic current given by

\displaystyle\omega^{b}(X;\delta_{1}X,\delta_{2}X)

\displaystyle=\delta_{1}\theta^{b}(X,\delta_{2}X)-\delta_{2}\theta^{b}(X,\delta_{1}X)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\eta^{ab}g_{\mu\nu}+\varepsilon^{ab}\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\right)\big(\partial_{a}\delta_{1}X^{\mu}\delta_{2}X^{\nu}-\partial_{a}\delta_{2}X^{\mu}\delta_{1}X^{\nu}\big)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\eta^{ab}g_{\mu\nu}+\varepsilon^{ab}\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\right)\partial_{a}\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}.

To find the global pre-symplectic form
\Omega
, we integrate the temporal component of this current (
\omega^{\tau}
) across a constant worldsheet time slice (from
\sigma=0
to
\sigma=\pi
). Notice how the canonical momentum
\Pi_{\mu}
naturally factors into the final simplified step

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}d\sigma\,\omega^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}d\sigma\,\left(\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\eta^{\tau\tau}g_{\mu\nu}\,\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\partial_{\tau}\delta X^{\nu}+\varepsilon^{\sigma\tau}\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\,\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\partial_{\sigma}\delta X^{\nu}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}d\sigma\,\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta\Pi_{\mu}.

We can parse this integral by isolating the distinct contributions stemming from the symmetric metric
g
and the antisymmetric
B
-field into
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{g}
and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{B}
, respectively

\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\int d\sigma\left(\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\,g_{\mu\nu}\,\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta\dot{X}^{\nu}+\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\,\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\prime\nu}\right)\equiv\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{g}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{B}.

(3.20)

The
B
-field piece requires a bit of manipulation via integration by parts. Using the product rule for the spatial derivative of the wedge product

\partial_{\sigma}(\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu})=\delta X^{\prime\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}+\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\prime\nu},

(3.21)

and combining this with the innate antisymmetry of
B_{\mu\nu}
, the bulk integral collapses entirely. This localises the
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{B}
contribution strictly to the boundary endpoints of the string

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{B}=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}\Big]_{0}^{\pi}.

(3.22)

Next, we examine the metric contribution,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{g}
. At the boundaries, we can substitute our previously derived relation linking
\dot{X}^{\mu}
to
X^{\prime\nu}

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{g}=-\frac{1}{(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}}\int g_{\mu\nu}(\mathscr{B}^{-1}g)^{\nu}{}_{\rho}\,\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\prime\rho}.

(3.23)

Similarly to the
B

(g\mathscr{B}^{-1}g)_{\mu\nu}
ensures that this integral also evaluates to a pure boundary term

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{g}=-\frac{1}{2(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}}\left[(g\mathscr{B}^{-1}g)_{\mu\nu}\,\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}\right]_{\partial}.

(3.24)

Boundary symplectic form.

By superimposing these two evaluated terms, we construct the total symplectic form. This equation explicitly highlights how the background fields couple together to warp the phase space exactly at the string endpoints

\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}}(g\mathscr{B}^{-1}g)_{\mu\nu}\right]\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}.

(3.25)

The inverse of the symplectic matrix components determines the Poisson bracket of the coordinates. Writing
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
generically as

\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\frac{1}{2}\Omega_{\mu\nu}\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}.

(3.26)

The Poisson bracket is given by

\{X^{\mu},X^{\nu}\}=\Omega^{\mu\nu}.

(3.27)

To elegantly compute this inverse, it is mathematically convenient to define an auxiliary matrix
\mathcal{E}
that bundles the metric and
B
-field together

\mathcal{E}=g+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B},

(3.28)

Through straightforward matrix algebra, the operator in the total symplectic form can be factored using this newly defined matrix
\mathcal{E}

\mathscr{B}-\frac{1}{(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}}\,g\mathscr{B}^{-1}g=-(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{-2}(g+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B})\mathscr{B}^{-1}(g-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B}).

(3.29)

Substituting this factored form back into our expression for
\Omega
gives

\boldsymbol{\Omega}=-(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{-2}\mathcal{E}\,\mathscr{B}^{-1}\,\mathcal{E}^{T}.

(3.30)

Finally, calculating the inverse of this symplectic matrix provides the fundamental noncommutative parameter
\Theta^{\mu\nu}

\Theta^{\mu\nu}=2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{g+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B}}\right)^{\mu\nu}_{A}.

(3.31)

where,
(~)_{A}
denotes the antisymmetric part of the matrix
G^{\mu\nu}

In matrix form,

\mathbf{\Theta}=-(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}(g+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B})^{-1}\mathscr{B}(g-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B})^{-1}.

(3.32)

Therefore, the classical Poisson brackets (and upon quantisation, the commutators) for the coordinates restricted to the open-string endpoints do not vanish. Instead, they satisfy a non-trivial commutation relation governed by
\Theta^{\mu\nu}

\{X^{\mu},X^{\nu}\}=\Theta^{\mu\nu},

(3.33)

This profound result is exactly the famous Seiberg-Witten noncommutativity parameter.

3.2
Tensionless reduction of the noncommutativity parameter

We now examine how the Seiberg–Witten noncommutativity parameter behaves in the tensionless regime. Physically, this limit corresponds to
\alpha^{\prime}\to\infty
, or equivalently
T\to 0
, one expects the antisymmetric background to dominate over the metric contribution. It is therefore natural to study the large-
\alpha^{\prime}
limit of
\Theta^{\mu\nu}
and check whether it reproduces the intrinsic noncommutative structure of the tensionless theory.

The
tensionless
(or Carrollian) string corresponds to

T=\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\longrightarrow 0,\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\alpha^{\prime}\longrightarrow\infty.

(3.34)

We now take this limit in the tensile string noncommutativity parameter

\mathbf{\Theta}=-(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}\,(g+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B})^{-1}\,\mathscr{B}\,(g-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B})^{-1}.

(3.35)

For notational convenience, define

\chi\equiv 2\pi\alpha^{\prime}.

(3.36)

Then

\mathbf{\Theta}=-\chi^{2}\,(g+\chi\mathscr{B})^{-1}\,\mathscr{B}\,(g-\chi\mathscr{B})^{-1}.

(3.37)

To extract the large-
\chi
behaviour, factor out
\chi
from each matrix appearing inside the inverses. First,

g+\chi\mathscr{B}=\chi\left(\mathscr{B}+\frac{1}{\chi}g\right)\implies(g+\chi\mathscr{B})^{-1}=\frac{1}{\chi}\left(\mathscr{B}+\frac{1}{\chi}g\right)^{-1}.

Similarly,

g-\chi\mathscr{B}=-\chi\left(\mathscr{B}-\frac{1}{\chi}g\right)\implies(g-\chi\mathscr{B})^{-1}=-\frac{1}{\chi}\left(\mathscr{B}-\frac{1}{\chi}g\right)^{-1}.

Substituting these two expressions into
\mathbf{\Theta}
, we obtain

\begin{split}\mathbf{\Theta}&=-\chi^{2}\left[\frac{1}{\chi}\left(\mathscr{B}+\frac{1}{\chi}g\right)^{-1}\right]\mathscr{B}\left[-\frac{1}{\chi}\left(\mathscr{B}-\frac{1}{\chi}g\right)^{-1}\right]\\[4.0pt]
&=\left(\mathscr{B}+\frac{1}{\chi}g\right)^{-1}\mathscr{B}\left(\mathscr{B}-\frac{1}{\chi}g\right)^{-1}.\end{split}

(3.38)

Now take the limit
\chi\to\infty
. Since
\chi^{-1}\to 0
, we have

\left(\mathscr{B}+\frac{1}{\chi}g\right)^{-1}\longrightarrow\mathscr{B}^{-1},\qquad\left(\mathscr{B}-\frac{1}{\chi}g\right)^{-1}\longrightarrow\mathscr{B}^{-1},

(3.39)

provided
\mathscr{B}
is invertible on the relevant brane directions. Therefore,

\mathbf{\Theta}\longrightarrow\mathscr{B}^{-1}\,\mathscr{B}\,\mathscr{B}^{-1}=\mathscr{B}^{-1}.

(3.40)

Thus the large-
\alpha^{\prime}
limit of the Seiberg–Witten noncommutativity parameter is

\mathbf{\Theta}_{\rm tensionless}=\lim_{\alpha^{\prime}\to\infty}\mathbf{\Theta}=\mathscr{B}^{-1}.

(3.41)

Equivalently, one may write the same result as an asymptotic expansion. Using

\left(\mathscr{B}\pm\frac{1}{\chi}g\right)^{-1}=\mathscr{B}^{-1}\mp\frac{1}{\chi}\mathscr{B}^{-1}g\mathscr{B}^{-1}+O(\chi^{-2}),

(3.42)

one finds

\begin{split}\mathbf{\Theta}&=\left[\mathscr{B}^{-1}-\frac{1}{\chi}\mathscr{B}^{-1}g\mathscr{B}^{-1}+O(\chi^{-2})\right]\mathscr{B}\left[\mathscr{B}^{-1}+\frac{1}{\chi}\mathscr{B}^{-1}g\mathscr{B}^{-1}+O(\chi^{-2})\right]\\[4.0pt]
&=\mathscr{B}^{-1}+O(\chi^{-1})=\mathscr{B}^{-1}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{\prime}}\right).\end{split}

(3.43)

Hence, the noncommutativity parameter has a smooth and finite tensionless limit.

In the next section, we derive this same noncommutativity parameter intrinsically from the CPS of the tensionless open string itself. The resulting boundary Poisson structure agrees precisely with the tensionless reduction above, thereby showing that the intrinsic tensionless noncommutativity parameter coincides with the
\alpha^{\prime}\to\infty
limit of the usual tensile-string expression.

4.1
Presence of a uniform Kalb–Ramond field

We begin with the Polyakov-type action for a bosonic string coupled to a Kalb–Ramond field,

\begin{split}S_{\text{tensile}}&=-\frac{T}{2}\int_{\Sigma}d^{2}\sigma\left(\sqrt{-g}\,g^{ab}\,\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\,\eta_{\mu\nu}+\varepsilon^{ab}B_{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\right).\end{split}

(4.14)

A useful way to formulate the tensionless limit is to express it in a dimensionless manner. Since
\alpha^{\prime}
is dimensionful, one should not simply say
\alpha^{\prime}\to\infty
; rather, one introduces a fixed reference scale
c^{\prime}
of dimension
(\text{length})^{2}
and writes

\alpha^{\prime}=\frac{c^{\prime}}{\epsilon},\qquad\epsilon\to 0.

(4.15)

Then

T=\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}=\frac{\epsilon}{2\pi c^{\prime}}\to 0,

(4.16)

so the tensionless limit is governed by the dimensionless parameter
\epsilon
. Equivalently, the intrinsic string length grows as
\ell_{s}\sim\sqrt{\alpha^{\prime}}\sim\sqrt{c^{\prime}}\epsilon^{-1/2}
, showing that the limit corresponds to a long-string, vanishing-tension regime. Written this way, the scaling makes transparent that one is really taking the ratio
\alpha^{\prime}/c^{\prime}
to be large, while keeping
c^{\prime}
fixed. A nontrivial limit then requires the background couplings to be rescaled simultaneously (see eq.(
4.19
)) so that the action remains finite.

To obtain a well-defined tensionless theory, we consider the limit in which the string tension vanishes. To retain nontrivial dynamics, the worldsheet fields must be redefined simultaneously. Specifically, we perform the following replacements

\begin{split}-\dfrac{T}{2}\sqrt{-g}\,g^{ab}&\longrightarrow V^{a}V^{b},\\
B_{\mu\nu}&\longrightarrow-\frac{B_{\mu\nu}}{\epsilon},\end{split}\qquad\text{with}\qquad V^{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}(1,-\rho).

(4.17)

Here,
V^{a}
is interpreted as a timelike vector density (or vielbein-like object) on the worldsheet, encoding the degenerate Carrollian geometry characteristic of tensionless strings as done in the free background case. Notice that we have an extra minus sign in the
B
-field as opposed to
[
11
,
39
]
, because our Polyakov action differs by a minus from those.
To keep the antisymmetric background field finite in this limit, we introduce a rescaled field
\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}
via
13
13

13

Notice that our rescaling is different from the existing literature
[
11
,
39
]
.

\begin{split}\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}&=\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}B_{\mu\nu}.\\
\end{split}

(4.18)

Under this redefinition, the
B
-field contribution remains finite as
T\to 0
.
The physical reason for the scaling of the Kalb–Ramond field is that, in the tensile action, the antisymmetric background does not appear as an independent coupling, but only through the combination multiplied by the string tension. Hence, if one takes the tensionless limit
T\to 0
while keeping
B_{\mu\nu}
fixed, the entire antisymmetric coupling vanishes, and the background decouples from the worldsheet dynamics.
Here,

\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{1}{2\pi c^{\prime}}B_{\mu\nu}.

(4.19)

remains finite as
T\to 0
or equivalently
\alpha^{\prime}\to\infty
.

Substituting these scalings into the original action and taking the strict tensionless limit yields the action for an intrinsically tensionless string in a constant Kalb–Ramond background

\begin{split}S_{\text{tensionless}}&=\int_{\Sigma}d^{2}\sigma\left(V^{a}V^{b}\,\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\,\eta_{\mu\nu}+\dfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{ab}\,\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\right).\end{split}

(4.20)

This action generalises the original ILST formulation, which describes free tensionless strings in flat spacetime by consistently incorporating coupling to a constant antisymmetric tensor field. It will serve as the starting point for the CPS analysis that follows.

Now, we will compute the symplectic form for the tensionless string in a constant Kalb–Ramond background, using the CPS formalism.
From the total action (
4.20
), the variation yields

\delta S_{\text{tensionless}}=\delta S_{0}+\delta S_{B}\,,

where
\delta S_{0}
is given in (
4
) and
\delta S_{B}
is given by,

\delta S_{B}=\int_{\Sigma}d^{2}\sigma\left[-\varepsilon^{ab}\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\delta X^{\mu}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}+\partial_{a}\left(\varepsilon^{ab}\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\delta X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X^{\nu}\right)\right].

(4.21)

Combining it with the ILST action and rewriting in the form language, we get

\delta\boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{E}_{\mu}\,\delta X^{\mu}+\boldsymbol{E}_{b}\,\delta V^{b}+\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}(X^{\mu},\delta X^{\mu}),

(4.22)

where the e.o.m.s are given by

\boldsymbol{E}_{\mu}=0\implies\partial_{a}\left(V^{a}V^{b}\eta_{\mu\nu}\partial_{b}X^{\mu}\right)=0;

(4.23a)

and for the vector density
V^{a}
,

\boldsymbol{E}_{b}=0\implies V^{a}{\gamma}_{ab}=0.

The symplectic potential is given by

\theta^{a}(X,\delta X)=\theta^{a}_{0}+\theta^{a}_{B}=\left(2\eta_{\mu\nu}V^{a}V^{b}+\varepsilon^{ab}\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\right)\,\delta X^{\mu}\,\partial_{b}X^{\nu}.

(4.24)

The (pre)symplectic current is defined by

\omega^{a}(X;\delta_{1}X,\delta_{2}X)=\delta_{1}\theta^{a}(X,\delta_{2}X)-\delta_{2}\theta^{a}(X,\delta_{1}X).

However, the pre-symplectic current vanishes identically for the ILST action, as we saw in the previous section. Here, we will compute the same for the Kalb-Ramond part of the action as follows

\begin{split}\omega^{a}_{B}&=\,\varepsilon^{ab}\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\big(\delta_{1}X^{\mu}\,\partial_{b}\delta_{2}X^{\nu}-\delta_{2}X^{\mu}\,\partial_{b}\delta_{1}X^{\nu}\big)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{ab}\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\partial_{b}\big(\delta_{1}X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta_{2}X^{\nu}\big)\\
&=\partial_{b}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{ab}\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\,\delta_{1}X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta_{2}X^{\nu}\right),\end{split}

where we used the fact that
\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}
and
\varepsilon^{ab}
are constant. Thus, the
\mathscr{B}
-contribution to the (pre)symplectic current is exact

\omega^{b}_{B}=\partial_{b}k^{ab}_{B},\qquad k_{B}^{ab}=\dfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{ab}\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\,\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}.

(4.25)

The total (pre)symplectic current is

\omega^{a}=\omega^{a}_{0}+\omega^{a}_{B}=\partial_{b}k^{ab}.

(4.26)

The (pre)symplectic form on a Cauchy slice
\mathcal{C}
(e.g., at fixed
\tau
) is obtained by integrating
\omega^{\tau}
over
\sigma
:

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\Omega}&=\int_{\mathcal{C}}d\sigma\,\omega^{\tau}=\int_{\mathcal{C}}d\sigma\,\partial_{\sigma}k^{\tau\sigma}\\
&=k^{\tau\sigma}\big|_{\sigma=\pi}-k^{\tau\sigma}\big|_{\sigma=0}=\Big[k^{\tau\sigma}\Big]_{\partial\mathcal{C}}.\end{split}

(4.27)

Using
\varepsilon^{\tau\sigma}=+1
, we have

k^{\tau\sigma}=\dfrac{1}{2}\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\,\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}=\dfrac{1}{2}\mathscr{B}_{\nu\mu}\,\delta X^{\nu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\mu}.

(4.28)

Therefore, the total (pre)symplectic form is purely boundary-supported

\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\left[\dfrac{1}{2}\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\,\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}\right]_{\partial\mathcal{C}}.

(4.29)

For an open-string with endpoints at
\sigma=0
and
\sigma=\pi
, this becomes

\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\dfrac{1}{2}\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\Big[\delta X^{\mu}(\tau,\pi)\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}(\tau,\pi)-\delta X^{\mu}(\tau,0)\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}(\tau,0)\Big].

(4.30)

The reduced boundary symplectic form is

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{bdry}}=\dfrac{1}{2}\mathscr{B}_{\mu\nu}\,\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}.

(4.31)

Emergence of noncommutativity

As we have a symplectic form in hand, it can be used to define the
Poisson bracket
as

\{f,h\}\equiv\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}(\delta f,\delta h)=\Omega^{\mu\nu}\,\hat{\partial}_{\mu}f\hat{\partial}_{\nu}h\,,

(4.32)

where
\{,\}
denotes the Poisson bracket of two smooth functions
f
and
h
on the symplectic manifold, ‘
\hat{\partial}
’ denotes the partial derivative on it, and
\Omega^{\mu\nu}
is the inverse of the symplectic form (for more details see Appendix
B
). Applying this definition to
X^{\mu}
’s yields the following Poisson bracket

\{X^{\mu},X^{\nu}\}=\Omega^{\mu\nu}.

(4.33)

The r.h.s. is non-zero given a non-vanishing symplectic form. In that case, the background geometry becomes noncommutative and the r.h.s. is related to the noncommutative parameter
\Theta^{\mu\nu}
. For the tensionless string with a uniform background Kalb-Ramond field, the symplectic form is given by (
4.31
). So, the Poisson bracket of the target space becomes

\{X^{\mu},X^{\nu}\}=\mathscr{B}^{\mu\nu}.

(4.34)

The boundary symplectic structure makes the geometry noncommutative. It implies that the tensionless strings in the presence of a uniform Kalb-Ramond field have an intrinsically noncommutative geometry with the noncommutative parameter

\Theta^{\mu\nu}=\mathscr{B}^{\mu\nu}.

(4.35)

This derivation of the noncommutative structure of the boundary phase space of tensionless strings is inherently covariant and bypasses the first-order Lagrangian method of
[
39
]
.

5
Conclusions and outlook

In this paper, we have developed a CPS analysis of open bosonic strings in constant antisymmetric backgrounds, treating the ordinary tensile theory and its intrinsically tensionless counterpart within a single geometric framework. The main result is that the origin and role of noncommutativity can be understood directly from the symplectic structure, without relying on the standard operator derivation from worldsheet propagators.

For the tensile open-string in a constant Kalb-Ramond background, we showed that the pre-symplectic current decomposes into a nontrivial bulk contribution coming from the kinetic term and an exact boundary contribution generated by the
B
-field. Upon imposing the mixed open-string boundary conditions, the full symplectic form reduces at the endpoints to the inverse structure whose antisymmetric part reproduces the Seiberg–Witten noncommutativity parameter. In this sense, the familiar noncommutative geometry on the D-brane worldvolume emerges as a direct consequence of the boundary symplectic data of the CPS.

The tensionless theory exhibits a qualitatively different structure. In the absence of background fields, the bulk pre-symplectic form is degenerate, reflecting the collapse of the ordinary propagating phase space in the Carrollian regime. Once a constant Kalb–Ramond field is turned on, however, the symplectic current becomes exact and localises completely on the boundary. The resulting reduced phase space is therefore supported entirely at the string endpoints, and the corresponding Poisson brackets imply an intrinsic noncommutative geometry,

\{X^{\mu},X^{\nu}\}=\mathscr{B}^{\mu\nu}.

This sharply distinguishes the tensionless regime from the tensile one: in the former, noncommutativity is not merely a boundary deformation of an otherwise nondegenerate bulk phase space, but rather the defining remnant of the physical phase space itself.

We further incorporated a boundary
U(1)
gauge-field coupling using the CPS formalism with boundaries. The Harlow–Wu framework makes it possible to treat the boundary term systematically. The resulting pre-symplectic current is again exact, and the effective field-strength combination on the brane controls the boundary symplectic form. Thus, the endpoint noncommutativity in the tensionless theory is governed not only by the Kalb-Ramond field, but more generally by the gauge-invariant antisymmetric data induced on the boundary.

Our analysis opens several avenues for future research.

Non-constant antisymmetric backgrounds.

A natural extension of the present work is to go beyond constant antisymmetric backgrounds and study the covariant phase space in the presence of a non-constant Kalb–Ramond field with non-vanishing field strength
H_{\mu\nu\rho}=\partial_{[\mu}B_{\nu\rho]}

[
46
]
. See cf.
[
69
, section 4]
for a slowly varying field. In such situations, the pre-symplectic current need no longer remain exact, and genuine bulk contributions to the symplectic structure may survive even in the tensionless limit. A concrete question is whether the resulting reduced phase space continues to be purely boundary-supported or instead acquires a mixed bulk-boundary character.

Deformation quantisation from the boundary phase space.

An important direction is to quantise the boundary symplectic structure obtained in the present analysis and relate it directly to deformation quantisation
[
67
]
. In the standard open string description, the antisymmetric part of the boundary propagator gives rise to the Moyal-Weyl star product and the Seiberg-Witten noncommutativity parameter. By contrast, in the CPS approach, the same structure emerges from the inverse of the boundary symplectic form. It would therefore be very interesting to develop a direct quantisation of the CPS boundary phase space and show how the corresponding noncommutative star product arises purely from the symplectic data, both in the tensile theory and in the intrinsically tensionless regime.

Higher structures and nonassociativity.

A closely related question is whether the covariant phase space approach can be generalised so as to capture not only ordinary Poisson brackets but also genuinely higher structures. The standard open string story leads to a noncommutative but associative product, whereas more general flux backgrounds in string theory suggest quasi-Poisson, tri-product, and even nonassociative structures
[
19
,
33
,
47
]
. It is therefore natural to ask whether a suitable extension of CPS or CPSB can detect the geometric origin of such higher brackets, for instance, through a controlled failure of exactness of the pre-symplectic current or through a higher-corner structure in the reduced phase space.

More general D-brane boundary conditions.

Another natural extension is to analyse more general D-brane boundary conditions. In the present work, the endpoint noncommutativity is governed either by a constant background
B
-field or by the effective boundary field strength
F_{\mu\nu}
. One may generalise this discussion to configurations with different fluxes at the two endpoints, intersecting branes, or spatially varying worldvolume gauge fields. Such systems may lead to inequivalent endpoint symplectic structures and could provide a CPS perspective on more general noncommutative D-brane geometries.

Extension to superstrings.

It would also be very worthwhile to extend the analysis to superstrings. Since the present paper shows that the tensionless bosonic theory admits a purely boundary supported noncommutative phase space in suitable antisymmetric backgrounds, a supersymmetric generalisation may reveal how fermionic degrees of freedom, supersymmetry, and boundary gauge couplings modify this structure. This could lead to supersymmetric extensions of the endpoint algebra and may clarify whether similar boundary-supported remnants of phase space persist in tensionless superstring theories.

Corner symmetries and endpoint observables.

The use of the Harlow-Wu framework suggests a broader conceptual direction: one may ask whether the open string endpoints support a nontrivial corner symmetry algebra (may be in the line of
[
29
,
30
,
31
]
). Since the Hamiltonian in the boundary-sensitive CPS formalism receives explicit contributions from corner data, it would be interesting to understand whether the endpoint noncommutativity derived here can be reinterpreted in terms of boundary charges, corner observables, or an emergent endpoint symmetry principle. Such a perspective may provide a deeper geometric understanding of why, in the tensionless regime, the surviving physical phase space is entirely localised at the boundary.

Overall, the CPS approach provides a unified and conceptually transparent description of noncommutativity in open string theory across both the tensile and tensionless regimes. Its central lesson is that the singular tensionless limit does not erase the symplectic structure; rather, it strips it down to its most elemental form, namely a purely boundary-supported noncommutative phase space.

Appendix A
Open string propagator and tensionless limit

In this appendix, we briefly review the worldsheet derivation of the noncommutativity parameter for open strings in a constant
B
-field background
[
69
]
, and contrast it with the tensionless limit.

Open string on the upper half plane

We consider an open string propagating in the presence of an antisymmetric field
B_{ij}
. The worldsheet is mapped to the upper half plane (UHP), with boundary at
\mathrm{Im}(z)=0
, where

z=\tau+i\sigma,\qquad\bar{z}=\tau-i\sigma.

(A.1)

The embedding coordinates admit the decomposition

x^{i}(z,\bar{z})=X^{i}(z)+\widetilde{X}^{i}(\bar{z}).

(A.2)

The presence of the
B
-field modifies the boundary conditions, leading to
[
69
]

g_{ij}(\partial-\bar{\partial})x^{j}+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B}_{ij}(\partial+\bar{\partial})x^{j}\Big|_{z=\bar{z}}=0.

(A.3)

Here we are using the rescaled Kalb-Ramond field (
3.10
). Restricting to the boundary, this relates the holomorphic and antiholomorphic sectors as

(g+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B})_{ij}\,\partial X^{j}=(g-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B})_{ij}\,\bar{\partial}\widetilde{X}^{j}.

(A.4)

Introducing

M=(g+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B})^{-1}(g-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B}),

(A.5)

one obtains the reflection relation

\widetilde{X}^{i}(\bar{z})=(M^{-1})^{i}{}_{\,j}\,X^{j}(z^{*}),\qquad z^{*}=\bar{z},

(A.6)

which allows all correlators on the UHP to be expressed in terms of holomorphic fields.

Worldsheet propagator

The bulk correlators are given by

\langle X^{i}(z)X^{j}(w)\rangle=-\alpha^{\prime}g^{ij}\ln(z-w),

(A.7)

and similarly for
\widetilde{X}^{i}
. Using the reflection relation, the full propagator can be written as a sum over image contributions. A straightforward computation yields

\displaystyle\langle x^{i}(z)x^{j}(z^{\prime})\rangle=-\alpha^{\prime}\Big[

\displaystyle g^{ij}\ln(z-z')+g^{ik}R^{j}{}_{\,k}\ln(z-\bar z')

(A.8)

\displaystyle+R^{i}{}_{\,k}g^{kj}\ln(\bar z-z')+R^{i}{}_{\,k}g^{kl}R^{j}{}_{\,l}\ln(\bar z-\bar z')\Big],

where

R=(g-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B})^{-1}(g+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B})=M^{-1}.

(A.9)

It is convenient to separate the symmetric and antisymmetric parts by defining

G^{ij}=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(g^{ik}R^{j}{}_{\,k}+R^{i}{}_{\,k}g^{kj}\big),\qquad\Theta^{ij}=2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\big(g^{ik}R^{j}{}_{\,k}-R^{i}{}_{\,k}g^{kj}\big).

(A.10)

The propagator then takes the form

\displaystyle\langle x^{i}(z)x^{j}(z^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=-\alpha^{\prime}\Big[g^{ij}\ln|z-z^{\prime}|-g^{ij}\ln|z-\bar{z}^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\quad+G^{ij}\ln|z-\bar{z}^{\prime}|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\,\Theta^{ij}\ln\!\frac{z-\bar{z}^{\prime}}{\bar{z}-z^{\prime}}\Big]+D^{ij},

(A.11)

Equivalently, one finds

G_{ij}=g_{ij}-(2\pi\alpha^{\prime})^{2}(\mathscr{B}g^{-1}\mathscr{B})_{ij},\qquad\Theta^{ij}=2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\Big(\frac{1}{g+2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\mathscr{B}}\Big)^{ij}_{A}.

(A.12)

Here
G_{ij}
is the effective open-string metric, while
\Theta^{ij}
encodes the noncommutativity induced by the
B
-field.

Boundary limit and noncommutativity

Restricting the propagator to the boundary
z=\tau
,
z^{\prime}=\tau^{\prime}
, one uses

\ln\frac{z-\bar{z}^{\prime}}{\bar{z}-z^{\prime}}=i\pi\,\epsilon(\tau-\tau^{\prime}),

(A.13)

to obtain

\langle x^{i}(\tau)x^{j}(\tau^{\prime})\rangle=-\alpha^{\prime}G^{ij}\ln(\tau-\tau')^{2}+\frac{i}{2}\Theta^{ij}\epsilon(\tau-\tau^{\prime}).

(A.14)

The antisymmetric part determines the equal-time commutator

[x^{i},x^{j}]=i\,\Theta^{ij},

(A.15)

which exhibits noncommutativity on the D-brane worldvolume.

Tensionless limit

For the tensile string, the propagator is logarithmic,

\langle X^{i}(z,\bar{z})X^{j}(w,\bar{w})\rangle=-\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{2}g^{ij}\ln|z-w|^{2},

(A.16)

reflecting the underlying two-dimensional conformal symmetry. In the limit
\alpha^{\prime}\to\infty
, the worldsheet geometry degenerates to a Carrollian structure and the dynamics become ultralocal in the spatial direction.

Choosing the gauge
V^{a}=(1,0)
, the action reduces to

S=\frac{1}{2}\int d\tau d\sigma\,g_{ij}\,\partial_{\tau}x^{i}\partial_{\tau}x^{j},

(A.17)

which contains no spatial derivatives. The corresponding Green’s function satisfies

-\partial_{\tau}^{2}\mathcal{G}_{\tau}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})=\delta(\tau-\tau^{\prime}),

(A.18)

with solution

\mathcal{G}_{\tau}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{2}|\tau-\tau^{\prime}|.

(A.19)

The full propagator is therefore

\langle x^{i}(\tau,\sigma)x^{j}(\tau^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}g^{ij}|\tau-\tau^{\prime}|\delta(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime}).

(A.20)

For periodic
\sigma

\delta(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{in(\sigma-\sigma^{\prime})}.

(A.21)

Thus, while the tensile theory exhibits a logarithmic propagator governed by conformal symmetry, the tensionless limit leads to a linear, ultralocal propagator characteristic of a Carrollian worldsheet.

Appendix B
Symplectic mechanics

A symplectic manifold is a pair
(\mathcal{Q},\boldsymbol{\Omega})
, where
\mathcal{Q}
is a smooth manifold and

\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\frac{1}{2}\,\Omega_{\mu\nu}(q)\,\hat{\mathrm{d}}q^{\mu}\curlywedge\hat{\mathrm{d}}q^{\nu}

(B.1)

is a two-form. Here,
\hat{\mathrm{d}}
denotes the exterior derivative defined on the manifold
\mathcal{Q}
, and
\curlywedge
is the wedge product. The
2
-form
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
satisfies

\hat{\mathrm{d}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}=0,\qquad\det(\Omega_{\mu\nu})\neq 0.

(B.2)

The first condition states that
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is closed, while the second one states that it is non-degenerate. Non-degeneracy implies that
\Omega_{\mu\nu}
is invertible, so there exists a bivector
\Omega^{\mu\nu}
such that

\Omega^{\mu\alpha}\Omega_{\alpha\nu}=\delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu}.

(B.3)

Since
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is closed, it is locally exact. Hence, at least locally, one may write

\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\hat{\mathrm{d}}\boldsymbol{\alpha},\qquad\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\alpha_{\mu}(q)\,\hat{\mathrm{d}}q^{\mu},

(B.4)

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is called the symplectic potential or
tautological
1
-form
15
15

15

It is also known as the canonical one-form, the Liouville one-form, or the Poincaré one-form depending on the context.
defined on the cotangent bundle
\mathcal{T}^{*}\mathcal{Q}
. The potential is not unique: it is defined up to the shift

\boldsymbol{\alpha}\to\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\hat{\mathrm{d}}Z,

(B.5)

with
Z\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{Q})
. This transformation leaves
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
unchanged.

Canonical transformations and Hamiltonian vector fields

Let

\hat{\xi}=\hat{\xi}^{\mu}(q)\,\hat{\partial}_{\mu}

(B.6)

be a vector field on
\mathcal{Q}
with
\{\hat{\partial}_{\mu}\}
being the basis on
\mathcal{T}\mathcal{Q}
. Its action on the symplectic form is determined by Cartan’s formula,

\mathscr{L}_{\hat{\xi}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\mathsf{I}_{\hat{\xi}}\hat{\mathrm{d}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}+\hat{\mathrm{d}}(\mathsf{I}_{\hat{\xi}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}),

(B.7)

where
\mathscr{L}_{\hat{\xi}}
is the Lie derivative defined on
\mathcal{Q}
, and
\mathsf{I}_{\hat{\xi}}
is the interior derivative (or product) defined as the mapping
\mathsf{I}_{\hat{\xi}}:\Lambda^{p}\rightarrow\Lambda^{p-1}
with
\Lambda^{p}
being the space of all
p
-forms.
A transformation generated by
\hat{\xi}
is canonical if it preserves the symplectic form,

\mathscr{L}_{\hat{\xi}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}=0.

(B.8)

Using
\hat{\mathrm{d}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}=0
, this becomes

\hat{\mathrm{d}}(\mathsf{I}_{\hat{\xi}}\boldsymbol{\Omega})=0.

(B.9)

Thus,
\mathsf{I}_{\hat{\xi}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is a closed one-form. If the first homology class is trivial, i.e.,
\mathtt{H}^{1}(\mathcal{Q})=0
, every closed one-form is exact, and one may write

\mathsf{I}_{\hat{\xi}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}=-\hat{\mathrm{d}}f

(B.10)

for some smooth function
f
on
\mathcal{Q}
. The function
f
is the Hamiltonian generator of the transformation, and
\hat{\xi}
is the corresponding Hamiltonian vector field. In components,

\hat{\xi}^{\mu}=\Omega^{\mu\nu}\,\hat{\partial}_{\nu}f.

(B.11)

Therefore, the action of the flow generated by
f
on another function
g
is

\hat{\xi}(g)=\Omega^{\mu\nu}\,\hat{\partial}_{\mu}f\,\hat{\partial}_{\nu}g.

(B.12)

This expression leads directly to the definition of the Poisson bracket. In particular, every smooth function on phase space defines an infinitesimal canonical transformation, and the Hamiltonian function generates time evolution.

Poisson brackets

For two smooth functions
f,g\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{Q})
, the Poisson bracket is defined by

\{f,g\}=\Omega^{\mu\nu}\,\hat{\partial}_{\mu}f\,\hat{\partial}_{\nu}g.

(B.13)

Equivalently, it gives the variation of
g
under the Hamiltonian flow generated by
f
. For any observable
F
,

\delta F=\{F,f\}.

(B.14)

Because
\Omega^{\mu\nu}
is antisymmetric, the Poisson bracket is antisymmetric,

\{f,g\}=-\{g,f\}.

(B.15)

Moreover, the closure of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
implies the Jacobi identity,

\{f,\{g,h\}\}+\{g,\{h,f\}\}+\{h,\{f,g\}\}=0.

(B.16)

Hence
C^{\infty}(\mathcal{Q})
forms a Lie algebra under the Poisson bracket. The bracket structure becomes more prominent when we look at the phase space of a theory with canonical coordinates
(x^{i},p_{i})
. By using Darboux’s theorem, one can always find a local basis for the symplectic form such that it can be expressed as
[
61
]

\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\frac{1}{2}\,\Omega_{\mu\nu}(q)\,\hat{\mathrm{d}}q^{\mu}\curlywedge\hat{\mathrm{d}}q^{\nu}=\hat{\mathrm{d}}p_{i}\curlywedge\hat{\mathrm{d}}x^{i},

(B.17)

where
q^{\mu}=(x^{i},p_{i})
. In these canonical bases, the basic Poisson brackets reduce to

\{x^{i},x^{j}\}=0,\qquad\{x^{i},p_{j}\}=\delta^{i}{}_{j},\qquad\{p_{i},p_{j}\}=0.

(B.18)

More generally, applying the definition to the coordinates
q^{\mu}
themselves gives

\{q^{\mu},q^{\nu}\}=\Omega^{\mu\nu}.

(B.19)

Thus, the basic Poisson brackets are simply the components of the inverse symplectic form. This provides the direct link between the geometric data
\Omega_{\mu\nu}
and the algebraic structure of observables.

The Moyal-Weyl product

A physically relevant example of noncommutative spaces arises via deformation quantisation of a symplectic manifold, typically applied to the phase space of quantum mechanics or the worldvolume of D-branes
[
54
,
33
]
. The standard commutative pointwise product of smooth functions
f,g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
is deformed into the Moyal-Weyl star product (
\star

f\star g=f\exp\left(\frac{i}{2}\,\overleftarrow{\partial_{\mu}}\,\Theta^{\mu\nu}\,\overrightarrow{\partial_{\nu}}\right)g=f\cdot g+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{i}{2}\right)^{n}\frac{1}{n!}\Theta^{\mu_{1}\nu_{1}}\cdots\Theta^{\mu_{n}\nu_{n}}(\partial_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{n}}f)(\partial_{\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{n}}g)

where
\Theta^{\mu\nu}
is a constant real antisymmetric Poisson bivector. This

(i)

associativity:

f\star(g\star h)=(f\star g)\star h\,;

(ii)

noncommutativity:

f\star g\neq g\star f\,;

(iii)

cyclicity or trace:

\int(f\star g)=\int(g\star f)=\int(f\cdot g)\,.

It is evident that the star product is a deformation of the commutative product with leading order correction proportional to the Poisson bracket

\{f,g\}_{\Theta}=\Theta^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_{\mu}f\,\partial_{\nu}g\,.

(B.20)

In particular, the star commutator of the coordinate functions produces the algebraic structure of the spacetime quantisation,

\left[x^{\mu}\,\overset{\star}{,}\,x^{\nu}\right]\coloneqq x^{\mu}\star x^{\nu}-x^{\nu}\star x^{\mu}=i\Theta^{\mu\nu}.

(B.21)

Kontsevich showed that any Poisson manifold (i.e., a smooth manifold
\mathcal{Q}
endowed with a Poisson structure
\Theta^{\mu\nu}
) admits a deformation quantisation
[

\varepsilon^{\tau\sigma}=+1
and write the symplectic form as

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\rm{bdry}}=\dfrac{1}{2}\mathscr{F}_{\mu\nu}\,\delta X^{\mu}\curlywedge\delta X^{\nu}.

(D.12)

It matches with the symplectic form in section
4.2
, where we have used the CPSB method. Thus, when both ends of the string end on the same D
p
-brane, the IW and CPSB methods give us the same symplectic form and hence the same symplectic structure of the boundary phase space.
