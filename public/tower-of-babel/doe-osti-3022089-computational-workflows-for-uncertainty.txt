1 Introduction
Reactions on unstable nuclei, particularly those on the neutron-rich side of stability, are important for both
fundamental and applied physics. For fundamental science, the most prevalent use case is astrophysi-
cal nucleosynthesis by rapid neutron capture—the r-process—by which heavy nuclei are formed in extreme
astrophysical environments, such as in supernovae and neutron star mergers; see, e.g., Refs. [1–3]. For ap-
plications, these processes are relevant for the interpretation of radiochemical data from historic nuclear
tests, which contribute to our ability to certify the enduring stockpile in the absence of nuclear testing [4];
see Ref. [5] for a broader discussion of applications. However, reaction cross sections involving unsta-
ble species are generally poorly understood, for the simple reason that useful data become scarce as one
moves away from stability. While there are avenues for improving the amount and quality of data for these
species [6], one is fundamentally reliant on nuclear theory to make progress on these ﬁelds of study.
However, in the absence of data, theoretical predictions should be regarded with healthy suspicion. The
development of nuclear cross sections from ﬁrst principles remains, for now, infeasible for heavy systems,
such as those involved in r-process nucleosynthesis. Therefore, theoretical models used for these calcu-
lations are often phenomenological, or else rely on some measure of (conceptual or quantitative) approx-
imation, in order to provide a prediction at all. Ultimately, these models have some number of parameters
that must be constrained using relevant data, with the hope that they generalize acceptably into the region
in which data do not exist. This scheme is not intrinsically problematic, but it carries with it the burden of
developing robust uncertainties for the associated predictions. For reactions on unstable, neutron-rich tar-
gets, this uncertainty quantiﬁcation (UQ) has historically been lacking, though not for a lack of interest or
competence. Cross section calculations rely on collections of models for a variety of physical inputs; con-
ditioning these models on data is generally computationally intensive and may not have been feasible until
recently.
Progress in both nuclear theory and computing are enabling precisely this sort of work. The objective of
this project (LDRD 24-ERD-023) is to synthesize these developments into state-of-the-art predictions for
neutron-capture rates on unstable targets, for use in astrophysical r-process nucleosynthesis calculations.
More speciﬁcally, we focus on species relevant for the weak r-process, which populates up to atomic mass
numbers around A ≈ 120−140; this is in contrast to the main r-process, which populates super-heavy species
that may undergo nuclear ﬁssion in an astrophysical environment. In addition to providing cutting-edge
theoretical descriptions of the necessary physics inputs, this project will endow these with robust, defensible
uncertainties, so that the descendent predictions and results obtained therewith are well measured. At the
highest level, the approach we adopt is as follows:
 1. Produce ensembles of nuclear theory inputs that capture the underlying modeling uncertainties.
 2. Using these inputs, calculate ensembles of neutron-capture cross sections.
 3. Process these cross sections into a form that is amenable to astrophysics calculations.
 4. Calculate ensembles of astrophysical observables in the form of elemental and isotopic abundances,
 thereby furnishing nuclear-theory-based uncertainties for these quantities.
This UQ pipeline from nuclear theory inputs to astrophysical outputs is simple to articulate but difﬁcult to
realize. On one hand, producing high-quality models for the physical inputs and training them on data can
be a yearslong process; the ﬁrst item on this list encompasses signiﬁcant effort. On the other hand, even
if one had these inputs ready for use, calculating ensembles of cross sections for each of O(100 − 1000)
nuclei is a signiﬁcant organizational undertaking—this simply cannot be done by hand.
This report describes our efforts at addressing the second of these issues. Speciﬁcally, we describe the
computational workﬂows we have developed to facilitate cross section calculations for a large set of target
nuclei and for a large number of input-parameter variations. The primary intention of this report is to describe
how we have used existing, LLNL-developed software to structure, execute and analyze these studies. We
assume the audience will consist primarily of (1) nuclear physicists who are interested in the details of
how we have structured and performed our calculations, and (2) HPC enthusiasts who may be interested
to understand how workﬂow-management tools have been applied to our particular set of computational

2 Nuclear Reactions in Astrophysics
In this section, we review the aspects of nuclear reaction theory that are relevant for astrophysical nucleosyn-
thesis and brieﬂy describe how these are implemented computationally. We comment on how the produced
cross section are prepared for use in calculations of r-process abundances, and discuss opportunities for
the inclusion of improved physics in these calculations. We aim to provide enough detail to keep this report
self-contained for the interested reader, so that it is clear what we are calculating and how we are calculating
it, but relegate deeper discussion to the dedicated scientiﬁc literature.

2.1 Compound Nuclear Reactions
The conceptual starting point for the modeling of nuclear reactions considered here is Bohr’s compound
nucleus hypothesis [9]. This mechanism assumes that the projectile is completely consumed by the target,
its energy distributed among the constituent nucleons over comparatively long timescales (relative to those
of the strong interaction) until thermodynamic equilibrium is attained. The resulting object—the compound
nucleus (CN)—is in an excited state and will thus de-excite by particle (neutrons, protons, alphas, etc.) or
photon emission. This decay process is iterative: the initial CN will likely decay to some intermediate nucleus
with nonzero excitation energy, resulting in a cascade of decays contributing to the overall reaction.
This process is represented pictorially in Fig. 1. Neutrons impinge on the target A Z to form the CN A+1 Z.
The diagram represents the three different incident neutron energies, represented by the black arrows, which
lead to three different exictation energies in the CN; these excitation energies correspond to the vertical
dimension. Each CN can then de-excite in any number of ways, represented by gray arrows in the ﬁgure; the
solid arrows denote neutron emission, and the dotted arrows denote photon emission. (Not shown is the
possibility of emitting protons, alphas, etc.—we ignore these for this discussion.) At the lowest energies, the
possibilities are limited: the CN may either re-emit a neutron and return to the target’s ground state, leading
to an elastic-scattering reaction, or it may emit a cascade of photons to reach its own ground state. This
latter process is precisely the neutron capture with which we are concerned, which is denoted (n, γ). As the
energy of the incident neutron increases, so, too, does the set of possible outcomes. For instance, the initial
CN may emit a neutron and produce a new CN that is also in an excited state; if this new CN is a long-lived
discrete state of the target, then this constitutes an inelastic-scattering reaction. At still higher energies, this
secondary CN may also emit a neutron, leading to an (n, 2n) reaction—and so on. It remains to determine
the relative probabilities of these outcomes.
To make this scheme quantitative, we apply the statistical model of Hauser and Feshbach [10]. This frame-
work assumes that the CN is formed at a high enough excitation energy that it is appropriately described in
terms of average quantities (level densities, mean decay widths, etc.) without needing to account for spe-
ciﬁc nuclear states and their properties. This allows us to mathematically describe the two main steps in
the reaction: the formation of the CN, and its subsequent decay.

2.2 Yet Another Hauser-Feshbach Code
To evaluate these cross sections, we Yet Another Hauser-Feshbach Code, or YAHFC [15], a Fortran-based
nuclear reaction code developed at LLNL. We will not detail this code here; instead, we broadly survey how
YAHFC sets up and performs its computations, focusing on how the necessary physical inputs enter the
calculation.
Each YAHFC calculation begins by deﬁning the nuclei for the problem: an internal data structure is cre-
ated for each possible nucleus (the target, the initial CN and any nuclei kinematically accessible from this
CN), simply called nucleus(i). In addition to high-level metadata (charge, mass, spin, etc.), this data struc-
ture contains information about every state available in that nucleus. On one hand, there exists informa-
tion about the discrete states for that nucleus; YAHFC uses existing RIPL-3 evaluated discrete level [16] for
these deﬁnitions, or else the user may deﬁne their own data. On the other hand, YAHFC also discretizes
the continuous-level-density portion of the excitation spectrum, and treats the resulting bins as if they were

 7

discrete states. Properties of discrete and binned states are encoded as substructures within the larger
data structure, respectively nucleus(i)%states(n) (labeled by integer n) and nucleus(i)%bins(J,ip,n)
(labeled by angular momentum J, parity ip and integer n). The last major, relevant step is that YAHFC deter-
mines the probabilities for every computational state in the problem to decay to every possible ﬁnal state by
one-particle emission, through Eq. 2. These probabilities are stored within their respective substructures.
Understandably, this setup represents a signﬁcant portion of computation time in a YAHFC run, but seldom
takes much longer than one or two minutes, depending on the scope of the calculation.
At its core, YAHFC is a Monte Carlo (MC) code. While the formation cross section for the CN is computed
analytically via Eq. 1 for a given projectile energy, the decay of the compound nucleus is treated by iterative
MC. YAHFC begins by sampling the relative probabilities to populate given J Π states in the CN, given by the
ratios of their formation cross sections. Once a state is chosen, YAHFC randomly selects a valid ejectile
type (photon, neutron, proton, etc.) and queries the relative probabilities for each possible ﬁnal state given
the emission of that ejectile;3 YAHFC then chooses one of these ﬁnal states via MC. If this ﬁnal state is not a
discrete state, then YAHFC iterates this procedure, with the previous ﬁnal state as the new initial state, until a
discrete state is reached. YAHFC assumes that all discrete states are below all particle-separation energies
for the daughter nuclei and thus considers it safe to stop the computation at this point, in that the reaction
channel is ﬁxed. From here, the various decay histories are weighted and combined into an MC estimate of
the cross section. The procedure repeats until cross sections have been calculated at every incident energy
speciﬁed in the calculation.
In addition to basic nuclear physics information such as masses and discrete-state properties, YAHFC must
procure the following nuclear physics inputs for each nucleus and for each transition in the problem:
 1. The nuclear level densities (NLDs), ρ(E), for each species in the problem. This contributes to the
 relative decay probabilities for different excited states, captured in Eq. 2.
 2. The various sets of TCs, Tc,ℓj (Ec ). We emphasize that every possible ejectile c has its own set of TCs.
In Sec. 3, we will provide more details on how YAHFC models these quantities and, as appropriate, how we
provide these quantities to YAHFC.

conditions and on nuclear-reaction rates enters through Rp (t). We will not discuss outcomes of any PRISM
calculations in this report; results will be addressed in future work.

2.4 Future Opportunities
The HF formalism has a ﬁnite domain of applicabilty (as all models do) beyond which it is not an appropriate
description of scattering. We brieﬂy comment on the deﬁciencies of this computational scheme and how
one could attempt to do better.

At low energies, describing scattering in terms of average quantities is not viable: one is sensitive to the de-
tails of resonances in the CN. These resonances generally change ⟨σn v⟩ relative to the HF result, particulrly
for keV-scale temperatures; this is relevant for r-process nucleosynthesis. If the details of these resonances
were known, then one could form a composite picture where scattering is described by resonance contri-
butions below some energy and the HF form above it (with some scheme for matching the two). However,
resonance data are nonexistent in the neutron-rich portions of the nuclear chart. There has been discussion
in the literature about developing statistical resolved resonances in these regions on the basis of averaged
quantities and some assumptions about their underlying statistical distributions [21–23]. This is an active
area of research within our collaboration, and we expect to describe our efforts in a technical report during
FY 2026.

More generally, there are short-timescale contributions to the cross section—called (semi-)direct reactions—
that become the dominant mechanism at higher energies. There is also a mounting body of evidence that
direct reactions contribute at low energies and are thus relevant for astrophysical capture [24, 25]. These
contributions are beyond YAHFC’s capabilities; to calculate them would require additional computational
machinery that we do not possess. To develop this machinery is outside of the scope of this project, but
would be an important component of Livermore’s nuclear data evaluation arsenal.4

That said, YAHFC (and other HF codes, such as TALYS [26]) can approximate these (semi-)direct contribu-
tions through pre-equilibrium contributions to scattering. This mechanism hypothesizes that the CN does
not fully thermalize before particle emission occurs, modifying the spectra and probabilities for this emis-
sion. The standard description of this phenomenon is the two-component exciton model of Ref. [27], which
is implemented in YAHFC. However, we have encountered a signiﬁcant bug when trying to compute these
contributions for neutron-rich species—the cross sections contain unphysical, order-of-magnitude disconti-
nuities. The cause of this bug is unknown; disabling the pre-equilibrium model allows us to sidestep the issue
while it is studied. Because the pre-equilibrium model is most relevant in the many-MeV energy domain, we
expect its effects on astrophysical neutron capture to be small. However, reactions involving many-MeV
incident neutrons are relevant for programmatic interests; any push for more complete UQ in this energy
regime may have to remedy YAHFC’s implementation of this model.

Note added: During the later stages of this work, we became aware of Ref. [28], which presents neutron-
capture rates calculated both with and without pre-equilibrium corrections using TALYS. The authors demon-
strate that the pre-equilibrium mechanism can be relevant for neutron-rich targets for O(100) keV-scale en-
ergies; this manifests as reductions in ⟨σn v⟩ by as much as an order of magnitude for O(1) GK temperatures.
We are therefore motivated to try to include these in future calculations; this will involve probing the causes
of YAHFC’s shortcomings.

the r-process region; developing a consistent framwork for characterizing these uncertainties is one of the
key objectives of this project.
We brieﬂy overview our plans for realizing this objective. First, we will develop NLDs from an uncertainty-
quantiﬁed nuclear shell model (SM). Early results have recently been reported in Ref. [31]; there, extensions
of the USDB SM [32] have been developed to account for the uncertainties in the underlying interaction,
trained on known binding energies and low-lying excitation energies. This interaction applies to species in
the sd shell, which is a comparatively small SM space relative to the r-process-relevant species we intend
to consider. Diagonalizing the associated Hamiltonian for a single nucleus for a ﬁxed set of SM parameters
takes a couple minutes on a standard Macbook; while manageable as a one-off, this is not sufﬁciently fast
to perform a complete UQ analysis in a reasonable amount of time. To address this, the authors implement
eigenvector continuation (EC) as a means to accelerate solving the Schrödinger equation; after developing
an EC-based emulator and validating the outputs for some test cases, they perform a Markov Chain Monte
Carlo (MCMC) study to optimize the SM parameters and infer their posterior distributions. Work is ongoing
to generalize these methods and develop similar emulators for model spaces directly relevant for our tar-
gets of interest, at which point we will be equipped to provide uncertainty-quantiﬁed NLDs. Once these are
developed, the second step will be to compute GSFs by directly calculating transitions between states in
the CN using wavefunctions provided by the uncertainty-quantiﬁed SMs. These components will eventually
need to interface with YAHFC; fortunately, YAHFC’s internal modeling of either the NLD or GSF of a given
species (and multipolarity, for the GSF) can be easily overridden if supplemental ﬁles are provided. This
is the mechanism by which we will implement this physics, and we will construct our computational work-
ﬂows with this approach in mind. We anticipate that these developments will be completed and reported
upon during FY 2026.

3.2 Nucleon-Nucleus Interactions and Optical Model Potentials
A ﬁrst-principles description of neutron-nucleus interactions is infeasible, so these are modeled by analogy
to optics: the nucleus behaves like a clouded lens, which either absorbs the incident nucleon or else causes
it to scatter elastically. Owing to this analogy, these effective interactions are described by optical model
potentials (OMPs). Once the OMP has been articulated, we solve for the elements of the scattering S-matrix
using the machinery of the calculable R-matrix [33], from which one derives the TCs as

                                              Tℓj = 1 − |Sℓj |2 .                                         (18)

We defer on all details and instead point the reader to the open literature. For our purposes, we emphasize
that the TCs encode the probability of forming a CN given the projectile, target, scattering energy and pro-
jectile angular momentum. The task, then, is to determine a suitable OMP for our calculations—ideally one
with UQ.
While there are a number of options, our starting point is the phenomenological OMP developed by Koning
and Delaroche [34], hereafter KD, which is both widely used and well regarded. We do not reproduce its
full form here; we note, however, that it contains 46 free parameters that, because it is phenomenological
in nature, have been calibrated to a diverse set of neutron- and proton-induced scattering data. However,
Koning and Delaroche did not provide UQ for their OMP; this was addressed by Pruitt, Escher & Rahman in
Ref. [35]. In that work, the authors recompiled the corpus of data used in Ref. [34] and reﬁt them using more
modern statistical techniques. In particular, they performed an MCMC study of these data, allowing them to
obtain not just optimal values of the parameters of the KD OMP, but their posterior distributions, including
the correlations between them. The resulting ensembles of potentials, collectively called KDUQ (“Koning-
Delaroche with UQ”), come in two different ﬂavors, related to different statistical treatments applied to the

 Computational Workﬂows for Uncertainty-Quantiﬁed Nuclear Reactions 12

data: KDUQ Federal and KDUQ Democratic.5 These ensembles each consist of 416 parameter vectors. This
is then the basis of our UQ treatment of nucleon-nucleus interactions: our ensembles of phenomenological
potentials are converted into ensembles of TCs, which YAHFC uses to calculate ensembles of cross sections
for a set of nuclear targets, thereby allowing us to develop ensembles of ⟨σn v⟩ for use in nucleosynthesis
calculations.
YAHFC contains a working implementation of the KD OMP, which is the default OMP for species with Z < 90
and Z > 96; more specialized OMPs are available for actinides [36–38]. When YAHFC is run, it looks in
the run directory for existing TC ﬁles that it needs for a calculation; if it does not see all of the TC ﬁles
that it needs, then it invokes the FRESCOX code [39] to compute them and write them to a ﬁle that it then
reads. YAHFC currently does not let the user redeﬁne the parameters of the KD OMP; one must instead
provide alternative TC ﬁles. As a part of their work, the authors of Ref. [35] developed a codebase called
The Optical Model Framework Out of Livermore (TOMFOOL) [40], that enables OMP-based nuclear physics
calculations, including the calculation of TCs given a projectile-target system and an OMP. TOMFOOL is
packaged with a script, createTransCoeffFile.py, that executes the calculation and formats the output
in a YAHFC-readable form. Moreover, TOMFOOL contains the full set of KDUQ parameter sets, which are
available for use in all calculations. This is the mechanism by which we generate TC ﬁles for use with
YAHFC in this work.
Aside from its parametric uncertainty, one may also consider the model-form uncertainty of the KD OMP.
This is more difﬁcult to thoroughly quantify—one would need a framework for comparing and contrasting
KD against an inﬁnity of phenomenological alternatives—but we may consider the effect of changing one
particular modeling choice as a proxy for this broader consideration. The KD form models the proton and
neutron Fermi energies (EFn,p ) of its targets—essentially a measure of boundedness of the system—as simple
functions of the atomic mass A:

                                            EFn = (−11.2814 + 0.02646A) MeV                                                       (19)
                                             EFp = (−8.4075 + 0.01378A)           MeV                                             (20)

This may be acceptable near stability, but is unsuitable off of stability: because, say, 60 Ca is much less bound
than 60 Ni, it would be inappropriate to assume they have the same Fermi energies. The most recent versions
of TOMFOOL allow for modiﬁcations to the KD form such that the (proton and neutron) Fermi energies are
target-speciﬁc, and may be provided by the user to be consistent with the set of nuclear masses:

                                    EFn = 21 [M (N + 1, Z) − M (N − 1, Z)] − M (1, 0)                                             (21)
                                    EFp = 21 [M (N, Z + 1) − M (N, Z − 1)] − M (0, 1)                                             (22)

Here, M (N, Z) is the mass of the species with N neutrons and Z protons. The resuling OMP is called KDEF
(“Koning-Delaroche with EF ”). We explore this form assuming that the calibration of the KD parameters is
unaffected by this modeling choice. We expect this to (mostly) hold because the data underpinning this
calibration are from stable targets, where the difference between the two forms for EF should be small.
We apply the same assumption to the two ﬂavors of KDUQ, to provide potentials we call KDEF Federal and
KDEF Democratic. We emphasize that this aspect of our work is exploratory; the potential parameters for-
mally need to be reﬁt, but we expect to learn qualitative lessons about model-form uncertainty through this
exercise.
We stress that OMP development is an active area of research, both in the nuclear theory community at
large [41, 42] and within our collaboration. Speciﬁcally, there is an ongoing effort to develop an uncertainty-
quantiﬁed dispersive OMP for use in these calculations. This class of OMPs imposes additional physical
requirements on the form of the OMP, particularly focusing on causality. This imposes a consistency condi-
tion on the real and imaginary parts of the potential, involving an integral over all energies, both positive and
negative. The effect is that, in addition to scattering cross sections, bound-state data of target nuclei are
relevant to understanding the full potential, in essence unifying aspects of nuclear structure and scattering
in an uncertainty-quantiﬁed way. Early efforts are described in Ref. [43]; these developments will be ﬁnalized
and reported during FY 2026.

3.3 A Comment on Nuclear Masses
Before concluding, we discuss the nuclear masses that we use for this work. The masses of the most
neutron-rich species have not been determined experimentally, but we must adopt some set of masses to
calculate their associated cross sections Developing a robust UQ scheme for these masses would be a
complicated endeavor. One could imagine articulating a (phenomenological or microscopic) model for nu-
clear masses and conditions the associated parameters on existing data, analogous to what has been done
with KDUQ. However, given that there are O(103 ) known nuclear masses, large-scale studies involving many
variations of a microscopic model would be computationally intensive. Phenomenological calculations are
perhaps more manageable, but we are unaware of any work in this vein. Alternative approaches that rely
on Bayesian statistical techniques have been explored in the literature: the frameworks of Bayesian model
averaging and Bayesian model mixing may be used to combine sets of predictions based on their relative
levels of competence in different parts of the nuclear chart [48–51]. We also note that advances in machine
learning have enabled new approaches to this question [52–58]. Modeling unknown nuclear masses is an
interesting vein of research, but it (a) is decidedly beyond the scope of this LDRD, and (b) would require input
from subject matter experts to properly implement. We relegate such a study to future work.
In the absence of a UQ approach, we are forced to choose ﬁxed, well-deﬁned nuclear masses. We will use
experimental masses where possible; for these, we refer to the AME 2020 evaluation [59, 60]. Where these
masses are not available, we must appeal to models. We arbitrarily choose FRDM(2012) [61] as our bench-
mark; these masses are commonly used for nuclear astrophysics applications. These choices present a
consistency problem: FRDM(2012) is not guaranteed to match AME 2020 masses. One approach to reme-
dying this inconsitency would be to simply use AME 2020 masses where available and FRDM(2012) masses
otherwise, but this is not a satisfying solution. To wit, this approach will give unphysical behavior in the par-
ticle separation energies at the boundaries between the AME 2020 and FRDM(2012) masses—neutron (Sn )
and proton (Sp ) separation energies, in particular, differ from local trends by several MeV in this approach.
We instead adopt the FRDM(2012) neutron separation energies instead of the masses, and use these sepa-
ration energies to reconstruct the full table of masses. There will still be some unrealistic shifts in Sn , but
we expect these to be largely mitigated; discontinuities will persist in Sp , but proton removal is less relevant
in the neutron-rich reaches of the nuclear chart, and so these jumps will have little bearing on our results.

4 Computational Workﬂows
We turn now to the computational tools and techniques that we apply to derive reaction cross sections for
wide swaths of nuclear species accounting for uncertainties descended from the KDUQ OMPs. After deﬁn-
ing the computational task, we offer a general discussion of the workﬂow-management utilities Maestro
and Merlin, and then describe the workﬂows that we have developed with this software.

4.1 Our Computational Scope
It is helpful to begin by clearly articulating our computational task: we must deﬁne the space of nuclei whose
cross sections we must calculate, determine the conditions under which we must run YAHFC and deﬁne the
set of parametric variations we need to cover. The scope of this project is to provide rates for weak r-process
nuclei, covering species from stabilty to the neutron drip line, i.e., the most neutron-rich species that are
bound. Our nominal range is species for which 20 ≤ Z ≤ 45. As a stretch goal, we would like to extend the

reach of our calculations to higher values of Z; we choose Z ≤ 60 as an arbitrary upper limit. Going beyond
this point would be technically feasible, but accurate reaction rates in this region would require extended
models for, e.g., nuclear level densities and particle-nucleus interactions, and is thus well beyond the scope
of this work.
We need also establish a range in N for each Z. Nuclear masses are not well known for the most neutron-
rich nuclei, and the exact location of the neutron drip line is not well understood. As noted above, we use
evaluated nuclear masses from AME 2020 where available, and we supplement these with neutron sepa-
ration energies from FRDM(2012). If we assume the resulting composite set of masses is gospel, then the
position of the drip line is prescribed. This allows us to concretely deﬁne the highest-N species to consider
in a given isotopic chain:
 1. We identify the lowest-N species in a chain that is not neutron-bound, i.e., for which the one- and two-
 neutron separation energies are not both positive. This and all heavier species in the isotopic chain
 are excluded (even if they are neutron-bound).
  2. Of the remaining set, the heaviest species is also excluded: the compound nucleus it would form would
 be neutron-unbound, which could not lead to a neutron-capture reaction.
  3. Therefore, the heaviest species in an isotopic chain is taken to be that with two fewer neutrons than
 the lightest neutron-unbound species.
On the proton-rich side, there is, in principle, no technical limitation in computing out to the proton drip line,
but there is little incentive to do so if one is focusing on neutron-rich environments. Therefore, we deﬁne the
lower bound of an isotopic chain to be the species with two fewer neutrons than the lightest (quasi-)stable
species.6

Under these run conditions, most YAHFC calculations take no more than a couple minutes. Curiously, an

making it possible to precompute and recycle them, reducing compuation time by as much as a factor of
100.7 This reﬁnement is present in the most recent versions of YAHFC (from v3.85 onward).

4.2 Maestro and Merlin
Maestro [7] and Merlin [8] are related workﬂow-management codebases developed at LLNL for the execution
of computational studies. Simply put, computational studies consist of sets of steps whose outcomes may
or may not inﬂuence later steps; they are encoded as study speciﬁcations, which provide Maestro and Merlin
with concrete implementations of these steps and of the relationships between them. Both codes take these
study speciﬁcations as inputs and convert them into speciﬁc tasks to be performed by a HPC system. They
begin by parsing the study speciﬁcation (which we will often abbreviate to “spec”) into a directed acyclic
graph (DAG) that maps out the dependencies between steps. The codes differ in what happens next.

Maestro

Maestro adopts a fairly direct approach to executing these studies. When the user runs Maestro on their
study, speciﬁed by a ﬁle named SPEC.yaml, via
  maestro run SPEC.yaml [OPTIONS]
the study spec is parsed into a DAG and each step becomes a distinct job to be submitted to the HPC
system’s scheduler, accounting for all possible dependencies. To coordinate these submissions, Maestro
creates as daemon—aptly named the Conductor—that monitors all active jobs for that study. As jobs com-
plete, the Conductor will replenish the queue with new jobs, including those that depend on previous steps,
until the study has been fully executed. The beneﬁts are clear: once the user has created the study spec,
its execution is completely painless, being completely managed by the Conductor. Moreover, the Conduc-
tor continually records the status of existing jobs in a ﬁle aptly named status.csv in the top layer of the
study’s directory structure, allowing the user to monitor their study with essentially no effort. This scheme
is demonstrated in the left part of Fig. 3.
While this is an incredibly powerful approach, it is not without its drawbacks. Maestro is well suited for
studies with not too many individual steps—of the order several hundreds or perhaps thousands. Studies

 17

much larger than this are feasible, but it can be time-consuming to get such a large number of jobs through
the HPC scheduler’s queue. While this approach works well when jobs need to be run across multiple cores
(perhaps on multiple nodes), as is often the case for, e.g., hydrocodes, Maestro does not seem immediately
amenable to running multiple jobs on the same node.8 This is particularly inauspicious for our study: while
YAHFC can be run in parallel—MC de-excitation of an ensemble CN is amenable to this—it is not embarass-
ingly parallel, with signiﬁcant computational overhead going into establishing its various data structures at
the beginning of a run. It therefore behooves us to try to run as many YAHFC realizations on a node as
possible, to maximize the wall-time efﬁciency of a given allocation, but Maestro does not seem conducive
to this desire. To realize this would require an alternative approach to study execution.

Merlin

Merlin is an extension of Maestro that makes it suitable for large-scale machine learning studies. Where
Maestro assumes that the user will be conducting relatively few simulations that are relatively resource-
hungry, Merlin exists for studies with many smaller-scale computational tasks. This different focus is cou-
pled with a different approach to organizing and executing studies: Merlin adopts a producer-consumer
model of task execution. When the user runs Merlin on their study via
  merlin run SPEC.yaml [OPTIONS]
the same underlying machinery as Maestro parses the spec ﬁle into a series of computational jobs, but this
is the point at which Maestro and Merlin diverge. Instead of creating jobs to be submitted to the machine’s
queue, Merlin invokes third-party message-distribution software called Celery that converts these jobs into
tasks and shuttles them off to an external server, called the broker server, and queues them for execution. At
this point, nothing else happens; the user must separately obtain an allocation of HPC resources and create
workers that will ﬁnd and execute these tasks. The user does so via the command
  merlin run-workers SPEC.yaml [OPTIONS]
At this point, the workers will continually consume and execute tasks that are produced by the broker—
hence, producer-consumer model. When these tasks are completed, the workers pass the results to another
external server, aptly called the results server, before they are passed back into the standard ﬁle system. This
scheme is shown in the right part of Fig. 3, to contrast it with Maestro.
This concept is immediately more complicated than its Maestro equivalent: launching a study requires two
command line inputs instead of one (though neither command is particularly difﬁcult to conjure), and we
are now suddenly reliant on external infrastructure—the broker and results servers, as well as third-party
software—to perform the study. Fortunately, the steps for setting up these servers have been well docu-
mented by the Merlin team [8] and are not terribly laborious. Furthermore, while the user can, in principle,
use external-to-Livermore resources, LLNL users can create and conﬁgure their broker and results servers
using cloud-based persistent data services through LaunchIT; this is recommended, from the perspective
of security.
Any additional hassle is, however, well justiﬁed by the beneﬁts, particularly for our work. The most signiﬁcant
beneﬁt is that once an allocation is obtained, the worker processes will simply grind through tasks until either
(a) the study is concluded, or (b) something goes wrong (more on this below). This is facilitated by the fact
that Merlin workers are scheduled at the per-core level, in contrast to Maestro. This allows us to exploit full
concurrency9 on a node, i.e., to run with a separate realization of YAHFC on every core on a node, affording
massive wall-time acceleration. Additionally, instead of waiting to get many thousands (or millions) of few-
minute-long jobs through the machine’s queue, one need only get a handful of hour-long jobs through; this
process can be efﬁcient during off-peak hours. Lastly, we note one more useful property: a Merlin study
can launch another Merlin study, while a Maestro study cannot. This is possible because Merlin allows for
global variables (for a given study) to be deﬁned at the command line; among other uses, this allows the
 8 We vaguely recall that LLNL machines scheduled with SLURM are necessarily scheduled at the per-node level when using Mae-

user to step through a series of study indices until some stop criterion is reached. These are just a few of
the beneﬁts afforded by using Merlin instead of Maestro; we refer the reader to the documentation [8] for a
more complete accounting.

Merlin is also not without some drawbacks, apart from the added complexity. The reliance on infrastructure
external to the HPC system on which the study is executed can be a headache: there have been times when
the external servers were unable to maintain a consistent connection with no apparent cause, resulting in
downtime that is outside of the user’s control. Additionally, we ﬁnd that some small fraction of tasks (no
more than ≈ 0.1%) fail to execute properly with no apparent cause—it is unclear if this is an issue with
Merlin, Celery, the servers, YAHFC, or some combination of these. Generally, Merlin provides less useful
status information that Maestro does; the user must explicitly query the status of their study, but jobs that
are running provide less useful information about, e.g., timing. Fortunately, if something does go wrong with
a study, Merlin is equipped with an explicit restart feature:

  merlin restart MY_STUDY

This is in stark contrast to Maestro; if something goes wrong with a Maestro study, the user must manually
relaunch the offending jobs, or else start the study from scratch.10 Contrariwise, Merlin studies can be
restarted from a single, simple command; completed steps are ignored, and dependencies are properly
accounted for. While we have had to work around Merlin’s deﬁciencies, we cannot imagine how this work
would have been done without it.

We emphasize that while Merlin is the superior utility for our workﬂow, this is not the case globally. Even for
this project, we have encountered scenarios in which it is more efﬁcient to use Maestro instead of Merlin:
this is the case for executing astrophysical reaction network calculations with PRISM, which we will not
discuss in any detail here. We include both Maestro and Merlin in our discussion, however, because it is
extremely valuable to have these two utilities with complementary strengths; there may be workﬂows to
which neither is particularly well suited, but we expect that these will play an increasingly important role in
computational work done at LLNL in the coming years.

Parametric Variation

We conclude this section by brieﬂy discussing how Maestro and Merlin incorporate parameteric variations
within a study. For either tool, parameters to be varied are assigned some study-speciﬁc variable names,
which are used in the deﬁnition of computational steps. From here, the user has several options for how to
deﬁne the concrete values that these variables will take.

 1. Parameter values can be explicitly deﬁned within the study spec ﬁle.

  2. The user can also provide pre-deﬁned tables of parameters at the command line.

  3. There may be instances in which the user would rather provide procedures for generating parameters
 instead of the parameter values themselves. This may be because (a) the parameter space is high
 dimensional, (b) the desired number of variations is large, or (c) the user would rather not pre-compute
 a list of draws (or some combination of these factors). If so, then the user can provide Maestro/Merlin
 with a parameter generator (“pgen,” for short) ﬁle. These relatively simple Python scripts allow the user
 to procedurally generate parametric variations using Maestro’s ParameterGenerator class. These
 pgen ﬁles can also accept arguments from the command line, if desired.

  4. Merlin can also dynamically generate parametric variations using external scripts. This capability is
 particularly useful for machine learning studies, where sampling for step i + 1 may depend on the
 outcomes of step i.

4.3 Our Workﬂows
Here, we summarize how we have used Merlin, in particular, to coordinate and execute the computational
study described above. We present a high-level overview of the study speciﬁcation; while we will mention
some peculiarities of integrating YAHFC into this workﬂow, we intend to be more descriptive than detailed.
The authors are happy to provide more in-depth discussion upon request.

Study Structure

We begin by deciding that each nucleus will constitute its own Merlin study, within which we will vary the
OMP (and, in the future, other nuclear theory inputs). In principle, we could create a separate spec ﬁle for
each species, but manually launching each of these would be tedious; we would instead like to automate
the iteration through targets. This pushes us to use Merlin’s ability to launch a study from another study:
by indexing every species in the study, we can iterate through this index until every unique study has been
created and queued. In practice, folding all species into one uber-spec ends up being cumbersome: simulta-
neously queueing hundreds of tasks for hundreds of nuclei proves to be too strenuous for the broker server,
in particular, causing sudden and unexpected failures.11 We strike something of a middle ground: we create
separate study specs for each isotopic chain, and iterate only through the species in that chain. This leads to
fewer concurrent tasks in the broker server queue; we still encounter issues with tasks failing for no obvious
reason, but these are much less frequent and are easily resolved by restarting the offending studies.
The iteration through the OMPs with UQ is handled with a parameter generator ﬁle, as described above. This
ﬁle provides for the deﬁnition of a single study parameter named RUN; this parameter takes values between
0 and N − 1, where N is some predetermined number of samples we wish to consider. Early tests of the
workﬂow used N = 4 to ensure that everything worked; production runs either took N = 52 or N = 416, as
we will discuss later. We elect to provide Merlin with a pgen ﬁle instead of explicitly deﬁning values in the
study spec ﬁles; the maximum value N can be speciﬁed in the pgen ﬁle, allowing it to be varied more easily
over the course of testing.
Figure 4 provides a graphical representation of the steps in each of our studies, including the dependencies
between the individual steps. Some of the dependencies are logically necessary; these are shown as solid
black arrows. Some of the dependencies, however, are imposed out of convenience; we have found that
adding too many tasks to the broker queue can induce unexplained failures in some steps. Therefore, we
include dependencies as a means to throttle the study; these dependencies are shown as dashed black
lines. We include a brief description of each step, but will gloss over many of the details.
 1. Launch the next study. We elect to make the execution of the next study to be the ﬁrst step, to avoid
 having to wait for any YAHFC runs to ﬁnish to progress through the isotopic chain. Operationally, this is
 handled by augmenting the A value of the target under consideration, starting from a given minimum
 value up to some maximum. When this maximum is reached, the iteration step ends without launching
 another study.
 2. Run YAHFC using its default implementation of the KD OMP. There are two reasons for this step. Firstly,
 this is a consequence of a peculiarity of YAHFC: if it can’t ﬁnd all of the TC ﬁles that it needs at the start
 of a calculation, then it will recompute all of these, overwriting any ﬁles that may already exist. This
 is relevant because, while TOMFOOL provides proton and neutron TCs, it does not provide alpha TCs,
 which we require for our calculations. Therefore, we must run YAHFC once before invoking TOMFOOL
 in order to get these alpha TCs, or else YAHFC will overwrite any ﬁles furnished by TOMFOOL. Future
 versions of YAHFC may remedy this (so that only the missing TCs are computed), but we are forced
 to live with this for the time being. Secondly, this provides an opportunity to compare cross sections
 derived from TOMFOOL’s implementation of KD against YAHFC’s. The results of this step are termed
 KD-YAHFC; all other steps in the study depend on the completion of this step.

Postprocessing

While one can, in principle, include a postprocessing step (or steps) within a Merlin study, we elect to es-
tablish our postprocessing workﬂows externally, so that we can collectively study the outcomes of a set of
Merlin studies (i.e., all targets with a given isotopic chain) all at once. We will not wade into the details of
every postprocessing script we have developed; instead, we will provide an overview of the capabilities that
we have established to be able to interact with the outcomes of our studies.
 1. We have developed machinery that parses Merlin status ﬁles for each study we have run to assess
 which have failed. If a study contains one or more steps that failed, then we relaunch that study. If
 particular steps continually fail, due to issues with either YAHFC or Merlin, then these steps are deleted
 and relaunched. This procedure has been sufﬁcient to address any failures that we have encountered.
 2. To assess resource utilization, we have written simple scripts that aggregate the computational time
 spent on every steps within each study for each isotopic chain, based on the same Merlin status ﬁles.
 3. Once all studies in an isotopic chain are complete, we can convert the (n, γ) cross sections into the
 requisite MACS and ⟨σn v⟩. At this stage, we occassionally encounter YAHFC runs that completed but
 that were not properly written to disk (for unknown reasons). We identify these failures, delete the
 (unusable) results and relaunch these steps. We also calculate the partition functions for each target
 via Eq. 10, consistent with YAHFC’s internal modeling of discrete states and level densities.
 • This step could easily have been included as a postprocessing step within our Merlin studies, but
 we separated it out while these capabilities we in development. There would be no barrier to us
 including this as an explicit step in future Merlin workﬂows.
 4. The ⟨σn v⟩ for all isotopic chains and all OMPs are aggregated into a single ﬁle. This ﬁle then undergoes
 additional processing to provide a unique output ﬁle for each OMP, containing ⟨σn v⟩ as a function of
 temperature for every target, in a form that is ready to be used in nucleosynthesis calculations.
While the postprocessing scripts are serialized when applied to a given study, we often make use of Python’s
MPI capabilities (through mpi4py) to accelerate the iteration through studies within a given isotopic chain.

5.1 Objectives and Computational Performance
We begin this section by precisely deﬁning the computational studies that we have run and presenting some
ﬁgures about resource utilization. Our ﬁrst computational study covered our nominal target region shown
in blue in Fig. 2. Our objectives were twofold:
 1. TOMFOOL has been tested, but we use this opportunity to systematically compare TOMFOOL results
 with FRESCOX results over a wide swath of the nuclear chart. Speciﬁcally, because YAHFC only calls
 FRESCOX with the default KD parameters, this serves as our testbed. Results we calculate using
 YAHFC’s calls to FRESCOX will be called “KD-YAHFC,” whereas those calculated using TOMFOOL’s TCs
 will be called “KD-TOMFOOL.”
 2. We would like to compare and contrast the two ﬂavors of KDUQ, KDUQ Federal and KDUQ Democratic,
 with each other and with the nominal KD OMP. Moreover, we investigated the effect of using the KDEF
 form (including KDEF Federal and KDEF Democratic) instead of the normal KD form, whose effects
 are expected to be largest off of stability. At the same time, we wanted to be conservative with the

 number of YAHFC evaluations to run for this ﬁrst study, so we considered only the ﬁrst 52 parameters
 sets for each uncertainty-quantiﬁed OMP, instead of the full 416.
In total, we performed 211 YAHFC calculations for each of 816 species, requiring a total of 172,176 calcu-
lations. We will discuss the outcomes of this study in the next subsection, but we note here that these
objectives were attained. The total computer time used for this study amounted to around 44,000 CPU-

should be regarded as a lower bound on the true computation time. There are several considerations that
would extend this runtime; we have detailed some of the potential failure modes of Merlin studies above.
The most prevalent issue, however, is the computational slowdown we had encountered as a result of the
WFCF calculation, described previously. This only affected a handful of species, but these species had an
outsized impact on the total resource utilization. The largest challenge was that the YAHFC runs would per-
sist for so long that the Merlin workers allocation would elapse before completion; because YAHFC does
not have a restart feature, this would result in hours of progress simply disappearing. We do not have an
estimate of exactly how much compute effort was lost this way, but sufﬁce it to say that we were deeply
pleased that the WFCF subroutine was improved for our next round of calculations.
For the next round of calculations, we decided to investigate both the blue and red regions in Fig. 2, but-

without this conceptually simple algorithmic improvement to YAHFC.

these correspond to the lower-left and upper-right regions of the ﬁgure for 75 As and 85 As, respectively. We
can see that the MACS at astrophysically relevant temperatures are largely (though not entirely) correlated
for 75 As; the picture is a bit richer for 85 As, where there are regions of both strong and weak correlations
across temperatures, and even some regions of modestly strong anticorrelations. On the other hand, we can
also extract the cross-isotope, cross-temperature correlations from either the lower-right or upper-left regions

reaching the level of +0.75 in some regions. We underscore the novelty of this ﬁnding: we are unaware of
any work that has characterized these kinds of correlations for species that are separated by ten units on
the nuclear chart.

YAHFC’s Performance
During the course of this work, we encountered a number of issues with YAHFC, as a natural consequence of
applying it to regions where the underlying modeling is less robust. It is unclear if these have been recorded
in a public forum; we present them for posterity.
 1. As noted in a previous section, there was a computational inefﬁciency in the calculation of the WFCF
 that has since been improved. While this was not an error, per se, this greatly accelerates YAHFC
 calculations in the barely-proton-rich part of the nuclear chart. Moreover, it obviates the need for a
 computational shortcut: YAHFC had  P been approximating

 where the sum over i only runs over photon transitions, and c, j run over every transition for every ejec-

 guarantee that it is reliable, in particular, for species far from stability. With the computational speed-
 up implemented, there is no longer any need to make this approximation; it is now treated exactly in
 recent versions of YAHFC.
 2. As also mentioned in a previous section, we have disabled pre-equilibrium contributions, but these may
 contribute at astrophysically relevant temperatures for species close to the neutron drip line. In our
 early testing of YAHFC, we observed that, for neutron-rich species, including pre-equilibrium modeling
 introduced numerical artifacts in the cross section, in the form of order-of-magnitude jumps in the cross
 section around a few MeV. Turning off the pre-equilibrium contributions was sufﬁcient to quell this
 behavior; this has, in truth, partly motivated us to ignore these contributions altogether. However, this
 may not be sufﬁcient going forward; therefore, it remains to determine the cause of this misbehavior.
 3. Early tests revealed an inconsistency in YAHFC’s modeling of the E1 GSF, fE1 : the TCs that it had been
 writing to disk were often inconsistent with the radiative width it was claiming to have ﬁt. The culprit
 was determined to be the subroutine that determines the strength of the second GSF contribution.
 This subroutine was inadvertently double-counting the contributions of high-energy discrete states to
 the s-wave radiative width; these contributions were, however, included properly in the subroutine that
 determines TCs for decay calculations. This issue has since been ﬁxed, but we make note of it because
 it is unclear if the existence of this correction has propagated into the community of YAHFC users. If
 this bug still exists in the wild, then it is crucial that it be remedied.
 4. We have also periodically encountered another oddity related to fE1 . If phenomenological models
 overshoot the experimental inference, then YAHFC will include a contribution with negative strength to
 correct for this. This can lead to situations in which the total GSF is negative for some excitation ener-
 gies. This is not immediately obviously a bug; it is more likely a consequence of YAHFC’s approach to
 incorporating these corrections. Given that we are expecting more sophisticated GSFs to be produced
 for this project, there is little incentive for us to spend much time on this issue. For now, we elect to
 take our lumps on this matter and move on.
 5. Lastly, we note that, for neutron-rich species, YAHFC will give low-energy cross sections that are suspi-
 ciously high. An example of this is shown in Fig. 14, where we show our calculated cross sections for
 93
 As(n, γ)94 As; we speciﬁcally point to the bump-like feature in the keV-scale energy domain. Simply
 put, this feature is odd—we do not expect cross sections to behave like this. After some investigation,
 we observe that this behavior is correlated with whether or not a target has any discrete states in the
 calculation. We therefore suspect that this is connected to modeling the NLD down to zero excitation
 energy; we hypothesize that YAHFC is underestimating the transition probability to low-energy states
 of the target, possibly because the small level densities lead to small neutron TCs and thus small (func-
 tionally) inelastic scattering cross sections. We do not have a more concrete explanation than this; it
 will be investigated in future work. However, this may be rendered moot if an SM emulator is able to
 provide a few low-lying excited states for targets off of stability, where they currently do not exist.15
We emphasize that most of the issues above (a) have either been addressed, or else (b) arise from using
YAHFC outside of the domain where its modeling assumptions can reasonably be expected to hold. We will
ultimately be improving upon the physical inputs that YAHFC needs to function; aside from the minor bugs
addressed above, we have every expectation that YAHFC is well positioned to provide robust results.

The focus of this report has been on the tools and techniques needed to establish these workﬂows; we
have discussed the physics, but this has primarily been in service of deﬁning the computational task(s) we
needed to execute—though it is, of course, interesting in its own right. Relatedly, we have tried to be speciﬁc
about how we have interacted with the various codes that we have used, even if we have not been speciﬁc
about how these codes function. However, it would be a missed opportunity not to reﬂect on the physical
insights we have gained through the used of these tools.
 • We have been able to systematially compare KD-YAHFC and KD-TOMFOOL cross sections for a wide
 swatch of species: we have studied the range 20 ≤ Z ≤ 60, from just on the proton-rich side of stability
 out to the neutron drip line. This sort of study may have been doable by hand, but it surely would have
 been time intensive; automation has been a boon.
 • Executing O(100 − 1000) YAHFC calculations for each species would simply not be doable manually—
 large-scale UQ studies would not be possible without software to organize them, for the OMP or for
 any of the other inputs that we will incorporate.
 • Moreover, because Maestro and Merlin studies are structured clearly, in terms of accepting inputs and
 organizing outputs, determining correlations between cross sections for different species is nearly
 trivial, after postprocessing. Because of this, we have learned that the OMP induces fairly strong
 correlations—stronger than we may have thought, a priori—between reaction rates for species many
 units apart on the nuclear chart. Maestro and Merlin have facilitated insights into the matter of correla-
 tions that would otherwise have been much more difﬁcult—if they were possible at all.
 • We will soon have uncertainty-quantiﬁed nuclear level densities and gamma strength functions, and

 Computational Workﬂows for Uncertainty-Quantiﬁed Nuclear Reactions 32

 we will want to assess their impact on our uncertainties. The precise mechanism by which these
 will be provided has yet to be worked out, but there is a clear path to hooking them into our existing
 workﬂows—there will be some rewriting of some computational steps, but we anticipate that these will
 constitute relatively minor perturbations on our existing studies, and they will not require additional
 infrastructure.
We emphasize that the OMP is only one of many sources of uncertainty that will ﬁlter into our complete UQ
pipeline. On one hand, there are the physical uncertainties that descend from the NLDs and GSFs, which will
ultimately be injected into these computational workﬂows, as we have said. Our prior expectation is that the
OMP will constitute the least important contribution to the total (physical) uncertainty budget, particularly
off stability. On the other hand, YAHFC is a MC code, so there will always be noise in its cross sections. This
can be improved, in principle, by increasing the number of MC histories for each incident neutron energy;
this will, of course, increase the computation time. Going forward, we intend to run with as many as 106
histories at each energy, but there is little incentive to go beyond this.
This report has consciously stopped short of describing and interpretting the results of astrophysical reac-
tion network calculations—those calculations will be presented in the open scientiﬁc literature when they
are ﬁnalized. Rather, our purpose here is to document the computational workﬂows that support this anal-
ysis. There is little here in the way of novel contributions to our understanding of nuclear reactions or of
astrophysical nucleosynthesis, but simply coordinating the large number of YAHFC calculations needed for
this work, and demonstrating the insights that these tools facilitate, required sufﬁcient effort as to warrant
a dedicated report.
We hope that this work serves as a proof of concept for other large-scale nuclear physics UQ studies that
would not be possible without workﬂow-management tools. One particular application that can beneﬁt from
these techniques is ﬁssion modeling: prompt ﬁssion fragments de-excite in precisely the same manner as
a compound nucleus formed in the Hauser-Feshbach framework, and thus rely on many of the same in-
puts [62]. This process is modeled in codes such as FREYA [63] and CGMF [64], but we currently cannot
interface our inputs with these codes. Recently, Ref. [65] integrated TCs from KDUQ into CGMF and de-
rived uncertainty-quantiﬁed ﬁssion observables (see also Ref. [66]). There is an ongoing effort at LLNL to
develop a YAHFC-based utility that performs the same functions; this work is still in its early stages, but
we have performed some preliminary calculations to demonstrate the integration of this utility with Merlin,
accounting for the KDUQ ensemble of OMPs. Early results have been encouraging, and we hope to mature
these capabilities in the coming years. There is also a signiﬁcant interest in reactions on unstable species
for programmatic applications beyond neutron capture; scattering data are scarce for these species, if they
exist at all, so one is reliant on theory to ﬁll in the gaps. Any predictions that one makes, however, are fun-
damentally ill conditioned without associated uncertainties; Maestro and Merlin provide a route by which to
robustly develop these. We expect that these will be important for future evaluations of nuclear data.
Workﬂow-management utilities are becoming increasingly important for the sort of computational work
performed at LLNL. This work demonstrates that nuclear theory, too, can beneﬁt immensely from these
tools.
