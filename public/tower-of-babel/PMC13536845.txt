Dual-transcriptomic analysis of human nasal transcriptome and microbiome reveals host-bacteria associations in symptomatic respiratory infection
Authors: Xiangyu Ye, Molin Yue, Sojin Lee, Andrew Li, Anna F. Wang-Erickson, Erick Forno, Taylor Eddens, Nader Shaikh, Wei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe human nasopharynx is colonized by a diverse community of commensal microbiota linked to many respiratory diseases, yet their associations with the host remain unclear.ResultsIn this study, we introduced a dual-transcriptomics analysis strategy, which can characterize the host transcriptome and microbiome from nasal samples simultaneously. We applied this workflow to a local SARS-CoV-2 cohort with 76 asymptomatic infected patients, among whom 52 (68.42%) developed symptomatic infection during a 1-week follow-up period. Nasal swabs were collected from all 76 patients at enrollment and from 73 patients at one-week later follow-up. We detected a median of 8.94% reads that did not map to the human genome across all 149 samples, among which around half (median 49.68%) were successfully mapped to microbiome genome. Meta-transcriptomic analysis detected significantly higher SARS-related coronavirus loads in samples from the symptomatic group at enrollment (P = 0.004), and both groups showed decreased loads one week later (symptomatic, P = 0.001; asymptomatic, P = 0.035). Compared with benchmarking 16 S rRNA sequencing on 53 samples, our computational strategy showed high correlation of relative abundance in all top 20 genera (median Rho = 0.90, Pmax < 0.001). A total of 670 bacteria species were identified to show a relative abundance ≥ 0.01% in at least 10% samples. Differential abundance analysis identified 76 species (DASs) from six phyla with significantly decreased abundance in samples from the symptomatic group (log2(fold change or FC) < -1 and adjusted P < 0.05) compared to the asymptomatic group at enrollment. Integrating these symptom-associated DASs with host’s gene expression using an expression quantitative trait bacteria (eQTB) model, we found 45 symptom-associated DASs identified at enrollment were significantly associated with one to 14 genes (adjusted P < 0.05). GSEA showed a series of symptom-associated DASs were significantly correlated with pathways related to olfactory function, keratinocyte differentiation, and DNA methylation.ConclusionsIn summary, our dual-transcriptomic analysis strategy effectively characterized host-microbiome associations, offering insights into microbial contributions to respiratory diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13054-6.

Background

Microbiomes inhabit many tissue sites of the human body, living in a symbiotic relationship with the host and forming complex communities [1]. Relationships between commensal microbiota and various health and disease states have been well established, but most studies have focused on intestinal microbiota [2]. In recent years, microbial ecosystems in respiratory tract, including the nasopharynx, have received increasing attention for their role in human health and disease [3, 4]. Compared to the intestinal tract, the nasopharynx represents one of the initial sites for respiratory pathogen invasion [5, 6]. Owing to the convenience of swab-based sample collection, the nasal cavity has long been recognized as a critical site for the rapid and non-invasive diagnosis of respiratory infections [3, 7]. Beyond the role in immediate infection diagnosis, the human nasopharynx is also physiologically colonized by a distinct and abundant microbial community, which potentially serves as a “gatekeeper” for respiratory health [8–10].

The nasal microbiota is shaped by factors such as climate, air pollution, infection, and the host’s immune status (Fig. 1) [11, 12]. Previous studies have demonstrated associations between the nasal microbiota and susceptibility to various respiratory diseases [13–15]. Multi-omic studies have further investigated the underlying mechanisms of these associations [16–18]. However, most studies to date have assessed the impacts of microbiota on systematic immune response rather than the local immune response in the nasopharynx, which is often distinct from the systemic response [19]. The potential of studying local immune response in identification of biomarkers for respiratory disease status has recently been demonstrated in several studies [20, 21]. Therefore, it is of great interest to better understand how nasopharyngeal microbiome may impact the host at early infection.

Dual-transcriptomics strategy offers such an opportunity to identify nasal microbiome-host interaction events associated with respiratory diseases by enabling the characterization of host transcriptome and microbiome from a single sample [22, 23]. However, the validity of such an approach has not been evaluated. In this study, we constructed a refined dual-transcriptomics analysis workflow (Fig. 2) and applied it to nasal samples collected from a local severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection cohort. To confirm the validity of microbiome abundance derived from this workflow, we compared it to 16S rRNA sequencing, performed on a separate aliquot of the same sample.

Results

Dual-transcriptome analysis and reliability validation

We included a total of 76 patients infected with SARS-CoV-2 aged 0.6 months to 75 years (median 13.06 years old, 6.88 ~ 35.26), among which 37 (48.68%) were female (Supplementary Table 1). All 76 patients were asymptomatic at enrollment and had their nasal swabs collected. 52 patients (68.42%) developed symptomatic infection by 1-week post-enrollment and were classified into the symptomatic group. In addition, 21 patients remained asymptomatic and 3 patients had indeterminate symptom status. Follow-up nasal swabs at 1-week post-enrollment were collected from 73 patients (96.05%).

A median of 8.94% (5.62 ~ 17.36%) of reads failed to map to the human genome, among which a median of 49.68% (26.52 ~ 66.17%) were successfully further mapped to microbiome genome (Supplementary Fig. 1). As expected, SARS-related coronavirus was identified as the most abundant virus species across all groups (Fig. 3A). Samples from the symptomatic group showed higher levels of SARS-related coronaviruses (median 13.33, 6.50 ~ 17.74 log2(per million microbiota abundance)) compared with asymptomatic group (median 6.98, 3.74 ~ 10.26 log2(per million microbiota abundance)) at enrollment (Pbaseline = 0.004), while both groups showed decreased load one week later (Psymptomatic = 0.001, Pasymptomatic = 0.035) (Fig. 3B).

For bacteria, meta-transcriptomic analysis detected 13,061 species from 2,366 genera and 53 phyla. To validate the reliability of the abundance retrieved by meta-transcriptomic analysis, we compared it with those from 16 S analysis. At the phylum level, both 16 S and meta-transcriptomic analysis identified Pseudomonadota (synonym Proteobacteria in SILVA), Actinomycetota, Bacillota (synonym Firmicutes in SILVA), Bacteroidota, and Fusobacteriota as the most abundant taxa (Fig. 4A). At the genus level, 16 S analysis detected a total of 809 taxa, of which 432 (53.40%) overlapped with those identified by meta-transcriptomic analysis. Importantly, among these overlapping genera, we found that the relative abundance of all top 20 abundant ones estimated by meta-transcriptomic and 16 S analysis showed high correlation (median Rho = 0.90, 0.86 ~ 0.91, Pmax < 0.001, Fig. 4B), which remain robust after excluding samples with near-zero abundance (median Rho = 0.90, 0.87 ~ 0.92, Pmax < 0.001, Supplementary Table 2), confirming the reliability of our dual-transcriptomic computational strategy in estimating microbiota abundance.

Differentially abundant species analysis

We then performed differential analysis at the species level using abundance retrieved by meta-transcriptomic analysis to identify taxa associated with symptomatic disease; 670 species with a relative abundance ≥ 0.01% in at least 10% samples were included for downstream analysis. Differential abundance analysis identified 76 species from six phyla that had significantly lower abundance values in the symptomatic group compared to the asymptomatic group (log2FC < -1 and BH-adjusted P value < 0.05) at enrollment, while another 13 species from three phyla showed significantly higher abundance values in the symptomatic group (log2FC > 1 and BH-adjusted P value < 0.05) (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Table 3). Of the 76 species with decreased abundance in the symptomatic group at enrollment, four showed a significant within-subject increase during the one-week follow-up, including Mycobacterium sp. SMC-16, Shewanella xiamenensis, Moraxella canis, and Providencia sp. PROV222 (Supplementary Table 4) and consequently showed no significance between symptomatic and asymptomatic groups one week later.

When exploring the differences in longitudinal changes during the one-week follow-up between symptomatic and asymptomatic groups by including a group-by-visit interaction term, no species reached significance for the interaction term after Benjamini–Hochberg multiple-testing correction (BH-adjusted Pmin = 0.402; Supplementary Table 5). Instead, 11 species remained significantly lower in the symptomatic group at the follow-up visit (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 6). In addition, 37 other species from four phyla showed significant increased abundance during the one-week follow-up, among which several Providencia and Proteus species from Pseudomonadota phylum showed extreme expansion (log2FC > 10 and BH-adjusted P value < 0.05) (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Table 4).

Of note, several widely known conditional pathogenic species were also identified as DASs at one of these two time points. In specific, compared with asymptomatic group, Moraxella catarrhalis (log2FC = 1.27, BH-adjusted P < 0.001) showed increased abundance in the symptomatic group at enrollment while several Anaerococcus sp. showed decreased abundance in symptomatic group at enrollment (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Table 3). At the follow-up one week later, Haemophilus parainfluenzae (log2FC = 1.70, BH-adjusted P = 0.019) showed increased abundance in symptomatic group. In addition, although Pseudomonas aeruginosa showed no significant difference between symptomatic and asymptomatic groups at enrollment, we observed its abundance increased during the one-week follow-up (log2FC = 2.01, BH-adjusted P < 0.001) (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Table 4). At one week later, Pseudomonas aeruginosa showed nominally lower abundance in symptomatics in comparison to asymptomatics (log2FC = -1.03, unadjusted P = 0.011) (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Table 6). Although we did not identify Streptococcus pneumoniae as a DAS at either time point, it showed slightly increased abundance in symptomatic group at the follow-up one week later (log2FC = 0.51, unadjusted P = 0.018), and several other Streptococcus species, including Streptococcus sp. group B (log2FC = 2.13, BH-adjusted P < 0.001), Streptococcus sp. oral taxon 064 (log2FC = 1.73, BH-adjusted P < 0.001), Streptococcus sp. wuxiensis (log2FC = 1.18, BH-adjusted P < 0.001), and Streptococcus sp. FDAARGOS_522 (log2FC = 1.50, BH-adjusted P < 0.001), expanded in symptomatic group at the follow-up one week later.

In the sensitivity analysis, we further included viral load (log2(per million microbiota abundance)) as additional covariate. The results were highly consistent with the main results (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Nasal microbiome-host transcriptome association analysis

To explore the potential impact of identified DASs on host immune and defense function, we employed an expression quantitative trait bacteria (eQTB) framework to integrate bacteria abundance with host’s gene expression to identify DAS-associated genes. The results showed that 45 symptom-associated DASs identified at enrollment were significantly associated with one to 14 genes, among which Lactococcus sp. and Kocuria palustris were significantly associated with ten or more genes (BH-adjusted P value < 0.05) (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Table 7). Of interest, GSEA showed that Lactococcus sp. was significantly associated with the activation of olfactory-related pathways, while Psychrobacter sp. AntiMn-1, Shewanella xiamenensis, Staphylococcus pettenkoferi, and Lactobacillus iners, showed significant association with the suppression of multiple olfactory-related pathways, indicating that they may potentially be associated with the host’s sense of smell (Fig. 6B). Several species also showed significant correlation with DNA methylation, interferon signaling, and keratinocyte differentiation-related pathways (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. 4). In addition, almost all species showed significant correlation with post-transcriptional gene silencing (PTGS), one of the established natural mechanisms to defend against SARS-CoV-2 (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. 4).

Discussion

While a companion paper from our group used the same cohort to identify host transcriptomic signatures predictive of symptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection [24], the current manuscript complements that work by characterizing the microbial side of host-microbiome associations and by validating the dual-transcriptomic analysis strategy. Specifically, in this study, we identified a series of nasal microbiota associated with symptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection. Some differences remained for at least one week. Further integrative analysis with host transcriptome highlighted candidate biological pathways correlated with these associations.

Although controversies exist, some previous studies have associated the abundance of one or more nasal microbiota with COVID-19 infection and development, among which Corynebacterium is one of the most studied genera [25]. For example, one study reported a decreased abundance of some Corynebacteria strains in SARS-CoV-2 infected patients compared with the uninfected group [26]. Szabo et al. reported similar results and demonstrated Corynebacteria strains may serve as gatekeepers against SARS-CoV-2 infection [27]. Shilts et al. found that Corynebacterium decreased in abundance with increased COVID-19 severity [28]. Consistently, we also found that multiple Corynebacterium species showed significantly decreased abundance in symptomatic COVID-19 patients, and the depletion of Corynebacterium segmentosum, Corynebacterium sp. LaCa97, Corynebacterium sp. LaCa101, and Corynebacterium sp. LaCa222 could be detected even one week later.

However, for Streptococcus, another genus commonly involved in the formation of the normal nasopharyngeal microbiota, more controversial results were reported by previous studies. For example, Chen et al. found that lower abundance of Streptococcus in nasal swab was associated with severe disease, while another study from the USA reported almost opposite results [29, 30]. Of note, the nasal samples were collected at enrollment and four days after diagnosis in these two studies, respectively. Our study also found that two Streptococcus species depleted in the symptomatic group at enrollment, while other six species showed increased abundance in symptomatic group at the follow-up one week later. Considering our samples were collected based on household transmission, the interval between time points of infection and sampling should be easier to determine. Thus, differences in sampling time points may partly explain these controversial findings. In addition, although we did not identify a significant association between Streptococcus pneumoniae and symptomatic COVID-19, we found that Moraxella catarrhalis and Haemophilus parainfluenzae, two other common conditional pathogenic species that cause COVID-19 co-infection [31, 32], showed increased abundances in patients with symptomatic infection at enrollment and the follow-up one week later, respectively.

The current limited understanding of the specific mechanisms by which commensal microbiota impact host health status represents a major barrier for clinical translation. As the initial infection sites for many respiratory pathogens, the nasopharynx is one of the most suitable sampling sources for dual-transcriptomics analysis. Here, integrating analysis under the eQTB framework showed that some identified DASs were associated with alterations in host olfactory pathway expression. Olfactory dysfunction represents one of the most frequently reported symptoms of SARS-CoV2 infection [33]. Koskinen et al. found that Faecalibacterium spp. and Porphyromonas spp. were associated with the decline of olfactory function, while Corynebacterium members were involved in reduced odor discrimination and threshold [34]. Biswas et al. reported a reduction of genus Corynebacterium in post-viral olfactory dysfunction patients [35]. Recently, another study also reported reduced abundance of Corynebacterium in COVID-19 patients, which was associated with anosmia [36]. Consistent with these findings, our results showed that at enrollment, several Corynebacterium species decreased in nasal samples from symptomatic COVID-19 patients were significantly correlated with olfactory-related pathways. It has been reported that nasal microbiota may play essential role in the normal olfactory epithelium development [37]. Of interest, we also found several DASs, including multiple Corynebacterium species, significantly correlated with keratinocyte differentiation and cilium function, suggesting they are associated with nasal epithelial barrier function. In addition, we found that almost all symptomatic infection-associated species were significantly correlated with PTGS-related pathways, and some also showed correlation with DNA methylation. This indicated that pre- and post-transcriptional regulation may also represent additional layers of association between nasal microbiota and symptomatic infection that warrant mechanistic investigation.

Beyond these, we also identified some DASs that were rarely reported in previous studies on nasal samples and respiratory infection. For example, three Micrococcus species showed decreased abundance in the symptomatic group at the one-week follow-up, one of which was already depleted at enrollment. Micrococcus is commonly regarded a normal commensal taxon, but several studies also reported that it may cause severe respiratory infections in certain populations, particularly those with undeveloped or impaired immunity [38, 39]. In addition, Lactobacillus iners showed decreased abundance in symptomatic group at enrollment. Integrative analysis also showed it was significantly correlated with viral response and interferon signaling. Previous studies on Lactobacillus were mainly limited to vagina samples [40]. Several recent studies reported the associations between nasal Lactobacillus iners and rhinitis and e-cigarette use [41, 42], indicating their roles in respiratory health are also worth exploring.

Although several recent studies have tried to use similar dual-transcriptomics strategy to explore the associations between nasal microbiome and respiratory diseases like asthma [22, 23], the reliability of microbiota abundance derived is uncertain. In this study, we demonstrated the overall reliability of microbiota abundance derived from our dual-transcriptome analysis via several sensitivity analysis. First, from the perspective of virus, we identified SARS-related coronavirus as the most abundant species across all groups and found that its load was higher in symptomatic group compared to asymptomatic group and decreased one week later in both groups. Second, from the perspective of bacteria, we collected samples from a subset of participants for 16 S rRNA sequencing and compared the resulting microbiota abundances with those from the dual-transcriptomics strategy for validation. As demonstrated by the results, at both phylum and genus levels, top microbiota detected by dual-transcriptomics were largely consistent in abundance with those identified by 16 S rRNA sequencing. In addition, our major DASs results were comparable with those identified by many previous studies, and results from the integrative analysis with the host’s transcriptome were also highly interpretable.

Our study still has some limitations. First, regarding study design, the cohort included only SARS-CoV-2-positive participants without an uninfected control group, limiting our ability to formally distinguish infection-specific signatures from symptom-specific signatures. Considering the anatomic characteristics of the nasal cavity and advantages of nasal swab sampling, it would be reasonable to extend this dual-transcriptomics strategy to many other respiratory infectious diseases and chronic non-infectious diseases in our future study. Second, regarding data collection, symptom status was recorded as binary presence/absence without a graded severity score, limiting dose-response analyses; quantitative Ct values from clinical SARS-CoV-2 testing were not returned to the study team, precluding direct concordance evaluation of our meta-transcriptomic-derived viral load estimates against an established clinical measurement; and the self-administered single-swab sampling design exhausted available material for the dual-transcriptomic and 16 S workflows, preventing orthogonal qPCR validation of the observed bacterial abundance shifts from the enrollment to the one-week follow-up. Fortunately, some past studies have also supported a high sensitivity of meta-transcriptomics-based respiratory virus detection compared with qPCR [43, 44]. Third, regarding methodology, the integrative analysis here was restricted to host transcriptome. As indicated in the GSEA results, some microbiota may affect DNA methylation and PTGS-related processes and pathways, and it would be of interest and potential to explore their interactions with epigenome and proteome. Finally, although we have demonstrated the overall reliability of microbiota abundance estimates derived from our dual-transcriptome strategy, it remains difficult to accurately assess whether, and to what extent, host transcripts interfere with these estimations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, using a refined dual-transcriptomics analysis strategy, we defined a series of nasal microbiota whose early-infection abundance was associated with subsequent development of symptomatic COVID-19. Integrative analysis indicated they may be associated with the development and function of olfactory sensors and nasal epithelial barrier. With growing interest in omics profiling of nasopharyngeal tissues, a large volume of data has been accumulated, and additional datasets are continually emerging, which may serve as valuable resources for more comprehensive evaluation and further extending these findings to more traits and omics. An in-depth understanding of nasal microbiota-host associations may potentially contribute to the development of microbiota-based preventive and therapeutic strategies, as well as advancing clinical translation and precision medicine.

Methods

Participants enrollment and sample collection

Participants in this study were household SARS-CoV-2 contacts enrolled from 4/20/2021 to 7/19/2023 [24]. Specifically, index cases were COVID-19 patients identified through pediatric outpatient sites, urgent care centers emergency departments and via an online research recruitment program (Pitt + Me). The infection was diagnosed based on rapid antigen or PCR test. Household contacts of index cases were then enrolled as participants while index cases themselves were not enrolled. Valid household contacts were defined as those that had been exposed for at least 24 h to a confirmed index case. Household contacts were required to be asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic both on the day before and the day of enrollment. And those who emerged two or more of the following symptoms during the 1-week study period were defined as symptomatic infection: (1) fever plus cough or difficulty breathing; or (2) fever or cough plus loss of taste or smell. Note that these households were enrolled an average of 5 days after exposure to the index case. Considering that the incubation period of SARS-CoV-2 is usually 2–4 days, it should be reasonable to infer that, for most participants, the first sample was collected within the initial 1 to 3 days following infection. Written informed consent was obtained from all household contacts or their guardians.

Participants were mailed individually wrapped, sterile, flocked swabs and a preservation tube containing 3 mL of buffer (DNA/RNA shield, Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) for self- or parental swabbing. Those who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 at enrollment were mailed a second swab kit for use (7–12 days after enrollment). RNA was extracted from swab samples and sequencing was performed using NovaSeq 6000.

Transcriptome data quality assessment and processing

A dual-transcriptome workflow was employed to split the sequencing reads into two parts and align them to human and microbiota genome to get host transcriptomic expression and taxonomic classification abundance matrix, respectively (Fig. 2). In specific, for host transcriptomic quantification, fastp (v.1.3.2) was first used to assess the quality of raw reads, and adapter sequences and low-quality bases were trimmed off or filtered out [45]. The cleaned reads were then aligned to human hg38 reference genome by STAR (v.2.7.11a). Mapped reads from STAR were quantified using HTSeq (v.2.0.5) according to the GTF file from GENCODE hg38 annotations (release 49) (13, 14), while unmapped reads were retained for meta-transcriptomics analysis. Reads with mapping quality lower than 10 were dropped, and then the expression of 78,724 genes were counted across all samples. We further removed genes outside 22 autosomes, genes without formal symbol names, unclassified non-coding genes (misc_RNA), and those showed no expression in all samples. Finally, a total of 27,347 genes across 149 samples were included in the downstream analysis.

To quantify microbiota abundance, unmapped reads from STAR were first imported into KneadData (v.0.12.4) for additional quality control (QC) to further filter residual host reads related to the human genome and human transcriptome references [46]. Of note, we built both KneadData human genome and human transcriptome reference indices using the same GENCODE GRCh38 primary assembly (release 49) and the corresponding GENCODE v49 transcript in STAR and HTSeq. We set --sequencer-source to none to disable a second adapter removal procedure. Retained reads were subjected to taxonomic classification using Kraken2 (v.2.17.1) against the standard Kraken2 database (k2_standard_20260226, released on 2026-02-26) [47]. Specifically, we set the confidence threshold to 0.05 (the minimum proportion of k-mers mapped to a taxon) after benchmarking four candidate thresholds (0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.50) to improve the classification accuracy (Supplementary Tables 2 and Supplementary Fig. 6), consistent with the recommendation of Pereira-Marques et al. [48]. The relative abundance of species within a sample was then estimated using Bracken (v.3.0.1) [49]. Specifically, to ensure the robustness of our analysis, we mainly focused on species with relative abundance ≥ 0.01% in at least 10% samples while set other rare species as “Others” [50].

Differential abundant species analysis

DESeq2 (v.1.48.1) was used to identify differentially abundant species (DAS) with absolute species abundances as input [51]. Quasi-likelihood framework in glmGamPoi (v.1.20.0) was used for dispersion estimation [52], and likelihood ratio test was used to detect differences. For comparison between two visits, we used a pair-wise design with donor ID included as the block variable. For comparison between symptomatic and asymptomatic groups, age, sex, race, and sequencing batch were included as covariates while COVID vaccination history and virus strain types were not included due to their collinearity with other covariates (Supplementary Fig. 7). DASs were defined as species with | log2(fold change or FC) | > 1 and Benjamini & Hochberg (BH)-adjusted P value < 0.05.

16 S rRNA amplicon sequencing and analysis

Part nasal swabs were selected for 16 S rRNA amplicon sequencing based on residual nucleic acid availability after the meta-transcriptomic workflow (Supplementary Table 9). Collected swab samples were in Zymo DNA/RNA shield. Nucleic acid was extracted using Quick DNA/RNA Microprep Plus kit (Zymo Research, Cat No D7005). The extracted RNA was then utilized for RNA sequencing, while the extracted DNA was used for 16 S microbiome test. Library was prepared using Quick-16 S Plus NGS Library Prep kit (Zymo Research, Cat No D6430) targeting V4 by running 1-step PCR and sequenced on Illumina MiSeq with v3 reagent kit, 600cycles.

DADA2 pipeline (v.2023.7.0, python v.3.8.15) in QIIME2 was employed for QC, denoise, and amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) identification on 16 S sequencing data [53, 54]. Specifically, raw fastq files were first imported in Phred33 V2 format, and cutadapt (v.2023.7.0) was used to remove adapters and merge PE reads with a minimum 10 bp overlap length and a maximum 0.2 mismatch ratio allowed in the overlap area [55]. Then FASTQC (v.0.12.1) and MultiQC (v.1.15) were used to assess the quality of merged reads and determine the truncation position and pruning length for DADA2 denoise [54]. Finally, scikit-learn (v.2023.7.0), together with a pre-trained classifier based on SILVA (https://data.qiime2.org/2023.7/common/silva-138-99-nb-classifier.qza), was used for taxonomic annotation on DADA2 denoised ASVs [56–59]. ASVs annotated as mitochondria, chloroplast or eukaryotic organism were filtered out and those with confidence score ≤ 0.7 were defined as “Unassigned”. Then ASVs with the same annotation were merged to get the final abundance table.

Significant microbiome-host gene expression associations and enriched pathways were defined as ones with BH-adjusted P value < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

Continuous baseline variable with non-normal distribution was presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and Wilcoxon test was used for comparison between symptomatic and asymptomatic groups. Categorical variables are presented as number (percentage), and χ2 test was used for differential tests. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Partial Spearman’s correlation was used to measure the correlation between the abundance of taxa from 16 S and meta-transcriptomics. All analyses were performed using statistical software R 4.4.0 (Lucent Technologies, Jasmine Mountain, USA).
