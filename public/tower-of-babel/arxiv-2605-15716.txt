Nonlocal Optical Response and Surface Susceptibilities: A Systematic Derivation via Spatial Moment Expansion
F. Zolla

Abstract
We present a systematic theory connecting the nonlocal response kernel of a homogeneous medium to its effective surface susceptibilities for an arbitrary curved interface. Starting from the most general tensorial nonlocal constitutive relation and combining a spatial moment expansion with a distributional thin-layer limit, we show that the full complexity of the interfacial response condenses, at leading order, into a single scalar: the surface susceptibility χ s \chi^{s} , equal for the tangential and normal components of the electric field. These quantities provide a constructive generalization of the Feibelman d d -parameters to interfaces of arbitrary curvature, and the curvature corrections, proportional to the geometric invariants H H (mean curvature) and K K (Gaussian curvature), are derived explicitly. The formalism is illustrated on a comprehensive set of analytically tractable cases (planar, spherical, cylindrical, and ellipsoidal interfaces) for several kernel choices (Gaussian, Yukawa, tensorial Lorentz). Generalized Maxwell boundary conditions are established and compared with the classical Fresnel results.



1
Introduction

The interaction between light and matter is one of the oldest subjects in
physics. Despite the abundance of work devoted to this question over more than
a century, it would be an overstatement to claim that the field is fully
understood today. In many practical situations, theoretical descriptions still
rely on partially empirical models whose microscopic justification remains
incomplete.

One of the deep reasons for this state of affairs lies in the coexistence of
several vastly different spatial scales in optical phenomena. In a typical
problem of electromagnetism applied to optics, one can distinguish at least
three characteristic scales.

•

The first is the atomic scale, of the order of the angström. At
this scale, electromagnetic fields vary over distances comparable to the
dimensions of electronic orbitals. Quantum mechanics reigns supreme.

•

The second is the scale of optical wavelengths, typically of the
order of a micrometre in the visible range.

•

The third is the scale of the macroscopic objects being illuminated,
which can reach several millimetres or centimetres.

A complete description of the light–matter interaction should in principle
allow these three levels of description to be connected. In particular, the
passage from the atomic scale to the optical scale should be obtained through
a procedure of statistical and quantum averaging, making it possible to define
the macroscopic quantities used by the optical physicist, such as the
permittivity or the susceptibility of the medium
[
1
,
2
]
.

In practice, this programme remains largely unfulfilled for the systems of
interest to modern optics.
Ab initio
approaches exist, but they remain
difficult to apply in realistic situations involving surfaces, interfaces, or
complex structures
[
3
]
.

One is therefore led to adopt a different approach, often described as
phenomenological, which consists in postulating certain constitutive relations
between the macroscopic electromagnetic quantities. More precisely, one seeks
to establish relations connecting the electric and magnetic fields

(\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B})
to the displacement and induction fields

(\mathbf{D},\mathbf{H})
.

These relations must satisfy several fundamental requirements. In particular,
they must:

•

be independent of the choice of coordinate system;

•

respect the symmetries of the physical system under consideration;

•

be compatible with the general principles of physics, in particular
the causality principle.

In this framework, the electric polarisation
\mathbf{P}
plays a central
role, since it connects the fields
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{D}
through the
relation

\mathbf{D}=\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}.

The main difficulty then consists in determining the functional relation
between
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{E}
.
The optical response of material media is traditionally formulated within
the local framework, in which the polarization vector at a point
\mathbf{r}
depends
only on the electric field at that same point
[
1
,
2
]
.
This approximation, which is remarkably successful for describing wave
propagation in macroscopic homogeneous media, breaks down as soon as the
relevant length scales become comparable to the microscopic characteristic
lengths of the material, typically a few Ångströms to a few nanometres,
for metals and semiconductors.

Spatial non-locality
is a considerably more subtle issue. In this case, the
polarisation vector at a point in space depends on the electric field in a spatial
neighbourhood.
Unlike the temporal case, there is no principle of spatial causality that
could constrain the form of this relation. Furthermore, the three-dimensional
structure of space allows for a wide variety of geometric behaviours that have
no counterpart in the temporal problem.

Spatial dispersion
which translates the dependence of the dielectric
susceptibility not only on frequency
\omega
but also on the wavevector

\mathbf{k}
was introduced theoretically by Pekar
[
4
]

and developed systematically by Agranovich and Ginzburg

[
5
]
. It expresses the fact that the polarization at

\mathbf{r}
results from a weighted integral of the field over a neighbourhood
of
\mathbf{r}
, whose spatial extent is set by the range of the response kernel.
This nonlocal framework has proved indispensable for describing excitonic
polaritons in semiconductors
[
6
,
7
]
, and more
recently, nonlocal effects in plasmonic nanostructures

[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.

The rapid growth of nanophotonics has renewed interest in the description
of interfaces in this nonlocal context. Even in a centrosymmetric medium,
the symmetry breaking induced by a surface generates a response
concentrated in an atomic-scale transition layer, qualitatively different
from the bulk. Feibelman
[
11
]
showed that this surface
response can be characterized, at leading order, by two scalar parameters

d_{\perp}
and
d_{\parallel}
, the so called
Feibelman
d
-parameters
,
encoding the response of the transition layer to the normal and tangential
components of the electric field. These parameters have since been
computed
ab initio
for a wide range of metal surfaces

[
12
,
13
]
, measured experimentally via ellipsometry
and differential reflectance spectroscopy
[
14
,
15
]
,
and incorporated into extended Maxwell boundary conditions for various
geometries
[
16
,
17
]
.

Despite this progress, the connection between the bulk nonlocal response
kernel and the effective surface susceptibilities, and more generally
the generalized boundary conditions for an arbitrarily curved interface, has remained elusive. Most existing approaches rely either on
phenomenological models such as the nonlocal hydrodynamic model

[
7
,
8
,
18
]
, or on material-specific
microscopic calculations
[
12
,
19
]
, without
providing a constructive link between the bulk kernel and the surface
response for an arbitrary geometry. In particular, the influence of
interface curvature which is central for understanding the optical properties
of nanoparticles
[
10
,
16
]
and the nonlocal Mie
corrections has not been derived systematically from the bulk kernel
alone.

The present paper fills this gap by providing a systematic and rigorous
derivation of the linear surface susceptibilities from the bulk nonlocal
response kernel. Our approach rests on two ingredients:

1.

A spatial moment expansion
of the tensorial kernel

\Delta^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
, which extends the classical Taylor
expansion of the field to the three-dimensional vector case and
naturally decomposes the response into a bulk contribution and a
boundary contribution;

2.

A distributional thin-layer limit
, which converts
functions concentrated near the interface into singular distributions
supported on
\partial\Omega
, i.e. Dirac deltas and their normal derivatives,
whose coefficients are moment integrals of the kernel over the
exterior half-space.

This framework yields, for any regular surface geometry: (i) the surface
susceptibility
\chi^{s}
at leading order, providing a constructive
generalization of the Feibelman parameters; (ii) curvature
corrections proportional to
H
and
K
; and (iii) generalized Maxwell
boundary conditions in explicit form, relating field jumps to kernel
moments. All microscopic complexity is thereby condensed into a finite set
of effective parameters, measurable experimentally without knowledge of
the underlying microscopic model.

The present paper restricts attention to the case where the nonlocal kernel
is
centrosymmetric
, i.e., invariant under parity

\mathbf{R}\mapsto-\mathbf{R}
. This symmetry, which holds for all standard
plasmonic metals and dielectrics, forces the rank-one moment to vanish
identically and makes the surface susceptibility
\chi^{s}
the
leading-order descriptor of the interfacial response.
When the kernel is
not
centrosymmetric, as occurs, for instance,
in a medium composed of identically handed chiral inclusions , a
rank-three moment survives, governed by a single pseudoscalar parameter

\beta
that encodes the chirality of the microscopic response. This
complementary regime, which leads to optical activity and the
Drude–Born–Fedorov constitutive relation, is treated in the companion
paper
[
20
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
treats the
one-dimensional scalar case: with trivial geometry, all distributional
calculations can be carried out explicitly, and the essential mechanisms
i.e. bulk/boundary decomposition, thin-layer limit, expansion in powers of
the kernel range emerge without tensorial complications, together with
a set of explicit numerical examples. Section
3
extends the
formalism to the three-dimensional tensorial case, treating successively
the planar interface (reference case), the sphere, the cylinder and the
ellipsoid, with fully explicit calculations for Gaussian, Yukawa and
tensorial Lorentz kernels. Section
4
derives the generalized
Maxwell boundary conditions and establishes their connection with the
Feibelman
d
-parameters. Section
6
concludes and outlines
the extension to the nonlinear case, which is the subject of a companion
paper
[
21
]
.

2
The one-dimensional scalar case

2.1
Physical motivation and setup

Before tackling the full three-dimensional tensorial problem, we develop
the essential ideas in the simplest possible setting: a one-dimensional
scalar model. This is not merely a pedagogical device. It corresponds
precisely to the physically relevant situation of TE-polarized light
incident on a planar interface between vacuum and a nonlocal medium. In
this geometry the electric field has a single nonzero component parallel
to the interface, and the constitutive relation reduces to a scalar
integral operator.

We consider two half-spaces: vacuum for
z<0
and a nonlocal medium
occupying
\Omega=\{z>0\}
, with the interface at
z=0
.
The nonlocal constitutive relation takes the form

P(z)=\varepsilon_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta(z,z^{\prime})\,E(z^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}.

(1)

2.2
Spatial moment expansion

We expand the field
E(z^{\prime})
in a Taylor series about the observation
point
z
:

E(z^{\prime})\approx E(z)+(z^{\prime}-z)\,E^{\prime}(z)+\tfrac{1}{2}(z^{\prime}-z)^{2}\,E^{\prime\prime}(z)+\cdots

(2)

Inserting this into (
1
) yields the

spatial moment expansion
:

P(z)\approx\varepsilon_{0}\bigl[\chi_{0}(z)\,E(z)+\chi_{1}(z)\,E^{\prime}(z)+\chi_{2}(z)\,E^{\prime\prime}(z)+\cdots\bigr],

(3)

where the
spatial moments
of the kernel are defined by

\chi_{j}(z):=\frac{1}{j!}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(z^{\prime}-z)^{j}\,\Delta(z,z^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime},\qquad j=0,1,2,\ldots

(4)

Remark 1

(Validity of the expansion)
.

For a plane-wave component
E(z)=e^{ikz}
, the expansion
(
2
) is accurate provided
k|z^{\prime}-z|\ll 1
, i.e., the
nonlocality range
\ell
satisfies
\ell\ll\lambda
. This condition
holds in all optical situations of interest, since
\ell
is of the
order of a few Ångströms while optical wavelengths are at least
several nanometres.

2.3
Hypotheses on the kernel

We impose the following conditions on
\Delta(z,z^{\prime})
, in increasing order
of specificity.

1.

Rapid decay.

For fixed
z
, the function
\Delta(z,z^{\prime})
decays rapidly:
all moments with respect to
(z^{\prime}-z)
exist.

2.

Support in
\Omega\times\Omega
.

The polarization vanishes in vacuum, and the vacuum region does not
drive the nonlocal response:

\Delta(z,z^{\prime})=H(z)\,H(z^{\prime})\,\Delta(z,z^{\prime}),

(5)

where
H
denotes the Heaviside function.

3.

Bulk homogeneity.

Far from the interface the medium is translationally invariant:

\Delta(z,z^{\prime})=H(z)\,H(z^{\prime})\,\tilde{\Delta}(z-z^{\prime}).

(6)

This guarantees that the medium becomes homogeneous away from
z=0
.

4.

Centro-symmetry.

The bulk kernel
\tilde{\Delta}
is even:

\tilde{\Delta}(-Z)=\tilde{\Delta}(Z)
.

Under these hypotheses and the change of variable
Z=z^{\prime}-z
:

\chi_{j}(z)=\frac{H(z)}{j!}\int_{-z}^{+\infty}Z^{j}\,\tilde{\Delta}(Z)\,\mathrm{d}Z.

(7)

Remark 2
.

The constraint
z^{\prime}>0
translates into

Z=z^{\prime}-z>-z
, hence the lower limit
-z
in (
7
).
It is this lower limit, dependent on the distance to the interface, that
carries all the surface physics.

2.4
Bulk and boundary decomposition

We split the integration range in (
7
) by writing

\int_{-z}^{+\infty}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}-\int_{-\infty}^{-z}
:

\chi_{j}(z)=\chi_{j}^{\Omega}\,H(z)\;-\;\chi_{j}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}(z),

(8)

where the
bulk moment
is the constant

\chi_{j}^{\Omega}:=\frac{1}{j!}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Z^{j}\,\tilde{\Delta}(Z)\,\mathrm{d}Z,

(9)

and the
boundary term
is

\chi_{j}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}(z):=\frac{H(z)}{j!}\int_{-\infty}^{-z}Z^{j}\,\tilde{\Delta}(Z)\,\mathrm{d}Z.

(10)

Remark 3
.

This dichotomy is crucial: it is clear that
\chi_{j}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}(z)
tends to zero
as
z
tends to infinity (i.e. towards the non-local material at play), and
that consequently this susceptibility is attached to the boundary, hence the
presence of the superscript
\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)
(vicinity of
\partial\Omega
) , while
the other part
\chi_{j}^{\Omega}
can only be attached to the “bulk” itself.

Vanishing of odd bulk moments.

By centro-symmetry, the integrand
Z^{j}\tilde{\Delta}(Z)
is odd for odd
j
,
so
\chi_{j}^{\Omega}=0
for
j
odd. In particular,
\chi_{1}^{\Omega}=0
,
and the leading nonlocal correction to the bulk polarization is of second
order in the field gradient:

P^{\Omega}(z)=\varepsilon_{0}\bigl(\chi_{0}^{\Omega}\,E(z)+\chi_{2}^{\Omega}\,E^{\prime\prime}(z)+\cdots\bigr),

(11)

with

\chi_{0}^{\Omega}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{\Delta}(Z)\,\mathrm{d}Z,\qquad\chi_{2}^{\Omega}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Z^{2}\,\tilde{\Delta}(Z)\,\mathrm{d}Z.

(12)

Equivalently, in the language of distributional convolutions:

P^{\Omega}(z)=\varepsilon_{0}\bigl(\chi_{0}^{\Omega}\,\delta+\chi_{2}^{\Omega}\,\delta^{\prime\prime}\bigr)\star E\,(z).

(13)

Localization of boundary terms.

The boundary term
\chi_{j}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}(z)
is concentrated in a layer of
thickness
\sim\ell
near
z=0
, and vanishes as
z\to+\infty
:

\lim_{z\to 0^{+}}\chi_{j}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}(z)=\chi_{j}^{\Omega}\;\text{(for even }j\text{)},\qquad\lim_{z\to+\infty}\chi_{j}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}(z)=0.

(14)

These boundary terms are the fundamental objects that, after a
distributional thin-layer limit, give rise to the surface susceptibilities.

2.5
Explicit example: the exponential kernel

To fix ideas, we carry out all computations for the Yukawa-type kernel:

\tilde{\Delta}(Z)=\frac{A}{\ell}\,e^{-|Z|/\ell},

(15)

where
\ell>0
is the nonlocality length and
A
is a dimensionless
amplitude. This kernel is even, integrable, and admits finite moments of
all orders;
\tilde{\Delta}
has dimension of an inverse length.

2.5.1
Bulk moments

\displaystyle\chi_{0}^{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\frac{A}{\ell}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-|Z|/\ell}\,\mathrm{d}Z=2A,

(16)

\displaystyle\chi_{1}^{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{(by parity)},

(17)

\displaystyle\chi_{2}^{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\frac{A}{2\ell}\int_{\mathbb{R}}Z^{2}\,e^{-|Z|/\ell}\,\mathrm{d}Z=A\ell^{2}.

(18)

2.5.2
Boundary terms

By using the equality (
10
), we obtain :

\displaystyle\chi_{0}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}(z)

\displaystyle=A\,e^{-z/\ell}\,H(z),

(19)

\displaystyle\chi_{1}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}(z)

\displaystyle=-A\ell\,e^{-z/\ell}\!\left(1+\frac{z}{\ell}\right)H(z),

(20)

\displaystyle\chi_{2}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}(z)

\displaystyle=A\ell^{2}\,e^{-z/\ell}\!\left(1+\frac{z}{\ell}+\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\frac{z}{\ell}\right)^{\!2}\right)H(z).

(21)

These functions are plotted in Fig.
1
for
A=1
,
\ell=10
.

Figure 1:
Boundary functions
\chi_{0}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}
,

\chi_{1}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}/\ell
, and
\chi_{2}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}/\ell^{2}

for the exponential kernel (
15
)
with
A=1
and
\ell=10
. Each function starts
at
\pm A
at
z=0
and decays to zero on the
scale
\ell
(dotted vertical line).

2.6
Distributional thin-layer limit

The boundary terms (
10
) are functions localized in a
layer of thickness
\ell
near
z=0
. Since
\ell\ll\lambda
in all
optical situations of interest, it is natural to collapse this layer onto
a mathematical surface and represent the boundary contribution as a
distribution supported at
z=0
.

2.6.1
General principle

We recall the standard result in distribution theory: if

f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, then

\frac{1}{\eta}\,f\!\left(\frac{z}{\eta}\right)\;\xrightarrow[\eta\to 0]{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\;\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\right)\delta(z).

(22)

We apply this to the rescaled boundary term

\chi_{\eta}(z):=\frac{1}{\eta}\,\chi_{0}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}(z/\eta)
.

2.6.2
Distributional convergence

Testing against a smooth compactly supported function
\varphi
:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{\eta}(z)\,\varphi(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=\frac{1}{\eta}\int_{0}^{+\infty}F\!\left(\frac{z}{\eta}\right)\varphi(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=\int_{0}^{+\infty}F(u)\,\varphi(\eta u)\,\mathrm{d}u,

(23)

where we set
u=z/\eta
and introduced

F(u):=\int_{-\infty}^{-u}\tilde{\Delta}(Z)\,\mathrm{d}Z\;\geq 0,\qquad u\geq 0.

(24)

By dominated convergence, as
\eta\to 0
:

\frac{1}{\eta}\,\chi_{0}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}\!\left(\frac{z}{\eta}\right)\;\xrightarrow[\eta\to 0]{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\;A_{0}\,\delta(z),

(25)

with

A_{0}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}F(u)\,\mathrm{d}u.

(26)

2.6.3
Expression in terms of
\tilde{\Delta}

Inverting the order of integration (domain:
Z\leq-u
,
u\geq 0

\Leftrightarrow

Z\leq 0
,
0\leq u\leq-Z
):

\int_{0}^{\infty}F(u)\,\mathrm{d}u=\int_{Z=-\infty}^{0}\left(\int_{u=0}^{-Z}\mathrm{d}u\right)\tilde{\Delta}(Z)\,\mathrm{d}Z=-\int_{-\infty}^{0}Z\,\tilde{\Delta}(Z)\,\mathrm{d}Z=\int^{+\infty}_{0}Z\,\tilde{\Delta}(-Z)\,\mathrm{d}Z,

(27)

where the last equality uses centro-symmetry. Hence the

leading surface coefficient
is the first moment of
\tilde{\Delta}
:

A_{0}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}Z\,\tilde{\Delta}(Z)\,\mathrm{d}Z.

(28)

In short

\chi_{0}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}\left(\frac{z}{\eta}\right)=\eta\,A_{0}\,\delta(z)+o(\eta)\,,

(29)

where
A_{0}
is given by (
28
). The singular part is the denoted
\chi_{0}^{\partial\Omega}(z):=A_{0}\,\delta(z)
.

2.6.4
Verification for the exponential kernel

For
\tilde{\Delta}(Z)=(A/\ell)\,e^{-|Z|/\ell}
:

A_{0}=\frac{A}{\ell}\int_{0}^{+\infty}Z\,e^{-Z/\ell}\,\mathrm{d}Z=\frac{A}{\ell}\times\ell^{2}=A\,\ell.

One checks directly:
\chi_{0}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}(z)=Ae^{-z/\ell}H(z)
, so

\frac{1}{\eta}\chi_{0}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}(z/\eta)=\frac{A}{\eta}\,e^{-z/(\eta\ell)}H(z)
,
which is a classical approximation of
A\,\ell\,\delta(z)
. ∎

2.7
Full distributional expansion

We now extend the leading-order result (
25
) to a
complete asymptotic expansion in powers of the layer thickness.

2.7.1
Expansion theorem

Assuming all moments of
\tilde{\Delta}
are finite, the distributional
expansion of
\chi_{\eta}(z)=\frac{1}{\eta}\,\chi_{0}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}(z/\eta)

in the limit
\eta\to 0
reads:

\chi_{\eta}(z)=A_{0}\,\delta(z)+\eta\,A_{1}\,\delta^{\prime}(z)+\eta^{2}\,A_{2}\,\delta^{\prime\prime}(z)+o(\eta^{2})

(30)

with

A_{k}=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\int_{0}^{+\infty}u^{k}\,F(u)\,\mathrm{d}u,

(31)

and
F
defined in (
24
).

2.7.2
Explicit coefficients

Converting to moments of
\tilde{\Delta}
via Fubini
(domain:
Z\leq-u
,
0\leq u\leq-Z
) and using centro-symmetry:

A_{k}=\frac{1}{(k+1)!}\int_{0}^{+\infty}Z^{k+1}\,\tilde{\Delta}(Z)\,\mathrm{d}Z.

(32)

The first three coefficients are therefore:

\displaystyle A_{0}

\displaystyle=\phantom{-}\int_{0}^{+\infty}Z\,\tilde{\Delta}(Z)\,\mathrm{d}Z\;>0,

(33)

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}Z^{2}\,\tilde{\Delta}(Z)\,\mathrm{d}Z\;<0,

(34)

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=\phantom{-}\frac{1}{6}\int_{0}^{+\infty}Z^{3}\,\tilde{\Delta}(Z)\,\mathrm{d}Z\;>0.

(35)

Remark 4

(Sign of
A_{1}
)
.

The coefficient
A_{1}
is
negative
: this is a direct consequence
of the
(-1)^{k}
factor in (
31
) and is crucial for the
correct sign of the dipolar surface correction. The signs alternate:

A_{0}>0
,
A_{1}<0
,
A_{2}>0
, etc.

2.7.3
Distributional limit for higher-order boundary terms

The same argument applied to each
\chi_{n}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}
defined
in (
10
) gives:

\frac{1}{\eta}\,\chi_{n}^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}\!\left(\frac{z}{\eta}\right)\;\xrightarrow[\eta\to 0]{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\;\left(\frac{1}{n!}\int_{0}^{+\infty}Z^{n+1}\,\tilde{\Delta}(Z)\,\mathrm{d}Z\right)\delta(z).

(36)

The surface coefficient associated with the
n
-th boundary term is
therefore the
(n+1)
-th half-line moment of
\tilde{\Delta}
, up to
a factorial.

2.7.4
Physical interpretation of the hierarchy

The expansion (
30
) encodes the full hierarchy of
surface effects:

•

A_{0}\,\delta(z)
: the leading
surface susceptibility
,
set by the first moment
\int_{0}^{\infty}Z\,\tilde{\Delta}\,\mathrm{d}Z
;

•

A_{1}\,\delta^{\prime}(z)
: a
dipole-layer correction
,
set by the second moment;

•

A_{2}\,\delta^{\prime\prime}(z)
: a
quadrupole-layer correction
,
set by the third moment.

All microscopic complexity of the boundary layer is thereby encoded in
the sequence of half-line moments of
\tilde{\Delta}
, which constitute the
sole input for the surface electromagnetic problem in this geometry.

2.7.5
Systematic construction of the kernel

Conversely, if one wishes to prescribe a given set of surface
coefficients
(A_{0},A_{1},A_{2},\ldots)
, it suffices to choose a kernel

\tilde{\Delta}
with the corresponding prescribed half-line moments.
The most tractable choice is a superposition of exponentials,

\tilde{\Delta}(Z)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}\,e^{-|Z|/\ell_{k}},

(37)

for which all moments are analytically computable: the lengths
\ell_{k}

control the nonlocality scales, and the amplitudes
a_{k}
fix the moment
conditions. This representation is the one-dimensional analogue of the
tensor decomposition used in the three-dimensional case
(Section
3
).

3
The three-dimensional tensorial case

3.1
General framework and moment expansion

Figure 2:
Illustration of non-locality for an isotropic material (left) and an anisotropic material (right).

We now extend the analysis to the physically relevant three-dimensional
setting (See Fig.
2
). The most general tensorial nonlocal constitutive relation reads

P_{i}(\mathbf{r},\omega)=\varepsilon_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Delta_{ij}^{(1)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)\,E_{j}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime},

(38)

where Einstein’s summation convention is used throughout and the frequency
dependence
\omega
is henceforth suppressed. Setting
\mathbf{R}:=\mathbf{r}^{\prime}-\mathbf{r}

and
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}=\mathrm{d}\mathbf{R}
:

P_{i}(\mathbf{r})=\varepsilon_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Delta_{ij}^{(1)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{R})\,E_{j}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{R})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{R}.

(39)

Expanding the field about
\mathbf{r}
,

E_{j}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{R})=E_{j}(\mathbf{r})+R_{k}\,\partial_{k}E_{j}(\mathbf{r})+\tfrac{1}{2}R_{k}R_{l}\,\partial_{k}\partial_{l}E_{j}(\mathbf{r})+\cdots,

(40)

and inserting into (
39
) yields the

spatial moment expansion
:

P_{i}(\mathbf{r})=\varepsilon_{0}\bigl[\chi^{(1,0)}_{ij}(\mathbf{r})\,E_{j}(\mathbf{r})+\chi^{(1,1)}_{ijk}(\mathbf{r})\,\partial_{k}E_{j}(\mathbf{r})+\chi^{(1,2)}_{ijkl}(\mathbf{r})\,\partial_{k}\partial_{l}E_{j}(\mathbf{r})+\cdots\bigr],

(41)

with the spatial moment tensors

\displaystyle\chi^{(1,0)}_{ij}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Delta_{ij}^{(1)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{R})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{R},

(42)

\displaystyle\chi^{(1,1)}_{ijk}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}R_{k}\,\Delta_{ij}^{(1)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{R})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{R},

(43)

\displaystyle\chi^{(1,2)}_{ijkl}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}R_{k}R_{l}\,\Delta_{ij}^{(1)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{R})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{R}.

(44)

3.2
Hypotheses on the kernel

We impose the following conditions, in direct analogy with the
one-dimensional case.

1.

Rapid decay.

For every
\mathbf{r}
, the function
\Delta^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{R})

is rapidly decaying: all tensor moments exist.

2.

Support in
\Omega\times\Omega
.

\Delta^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\mathds{1}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})\,\mathds{1}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\,\Delta^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}).

(45)

The indicator
\mathds{1}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})
ensures zero polarization vector outside

\Omega
;
\mathds{1}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
ensures that the exterior region
does not drive the nonlocal response.

3.

Bulk homogeneity.

Far from
\partial\Omega
the medium is homogeneous:

\Delta^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\mathds{1}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})\,\mathds{1}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\,\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}).

(46)

4.

Bulk isotropy.

The bulk kernel is isotropic, i.e., it has the form

\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{R})=\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}(R)\,\delta_{ij}+\bigl[\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}(R)-\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}(R)\bigr]\frac{R_{i}R_{j}}{R^{2}},\quad R=|\mathbf{R}|,

(47)

where
\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}
and
\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}
are two scalar
radial functions. This is the most general form of a rank-2
tensor-valued function of
\mathbf{R}
that is covariant under all
rotations; see
[
5
,
2
]
and
Appendix
A
for a self-contained proof.

5.

Centro-symmetry.

\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}(-\mathbf{R})=\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{R})
,
which is automatically satisfied by (
47
).

Remark 5
.

The assumption
\mathds{1}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
in Hypothesis 2 implies that
the nonlocal interaction is cut off sharply at the interface. While
quantum mechanics tells us that the actual transition is smooth over an
atomic scale, this hypothesis does not simplify the calculations
significantly and can be relaxed at the cost of additional terms in the
moment expansion. We retain it here for clarity.

Remark 6
.

The last two assumptions are not strictly necessary and could be lifted
if one were to study materials that are either anisotropic or
non-centrosymmetric. The non-centrosymmetric case, in which the
rank-one moment
\chi^{(1,1)}_{ijk}
no longer vanishes, is the
subject of the companion paper
[
20
]
, where it is shown
to lead to optical activity and the Drude–Born–Fedorov constitutive
relation.

3.3
Bulk and boundary decomposition

In the sequel, the set
\Omega
is supposed to be a simply connected and sufficiently smooth domain (See Fig.
3
) and
\Omega^{c}
denotes the complement of
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Figure 3:
The set
\Omega

\Omega^{c}=\mathbb{R}^{3}-\Omega

For
\mathbf{r}\in\Omega

\mathbb{R}^{3}=(\Omega-\mathbf{r})\cup(\Omega^{c}-\mathbf{r})

\Omega-\mathbf{r}:=\{\mathbf{R};\,\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{R}\in\Omega\}

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{R})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{R}=\int_{\Omega-\mathbf{r}}\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{R})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{R}+\int_{\Omega^{c}-\mathbf{r}}\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{R})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{R}.

(48)

Subtracting and adding the full-space integral, the polarization

P_{i}(\mathbf{r})=P_{i}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})+P^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}_{i}(\mathbf{r}),

(50)

is the
bulk polarization
(that of an infinite homogeneous medium),
and

P^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega)}_{i}(\mathbf{r}):=-\varepsilon_{0}\,\mathds{1}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})\int_{\Omega^{c}}\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\,E_{j}^{+}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}

(51)

is the
interfacial contribution
, concentrated in a layer of
thickness
\ell
near
\partial\Omega
. Here

denotes the field value
on the exterior side
\Omega^{c}

Accordingly, each spatial moment tensor decomposes as

\chi^{(1,n)}_{ij\,k_{1}\cdots k_{n}}(\mathbf{r})=\chi^{\Omega,(n)}_{ij\,k_{1}\cdots k_{n}}-\chi^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega),(n)}_{ij\,k_{1}\cdots k_{n}}(\mathbf{r}),

(52)

with the constant
bulk moment

\chi^{\Omega,(n)}_{ij\,k_{1}\cdots k_{n}}:=\frac{1}{n!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}R_{k_{1}}\cdots R_{k_{n}}\,\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{R})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{R},

(53)

and the
boundary term

\chi^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega),(n)}_{ij\,k_{1}\cdots k_{n}}(\mathbf{r}):=\frac{\mathds{1}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})}{n!}\int_{\Omega^{c}-\mathbf{r}}R_{k_{1}}\cdots R_{k_{n}}\,\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{R})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{R}.

(54)

Vanishing of odd bulk moments.

By bulk isotropy (
47
) and centro-symmetry,

\mathbf{R}\mapsto R_{k_{1}}\cdots R_{k_{n}}\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{R})
is odd
for odd

, so
\chi^{\Omega,(n)}=0
for all odd

.
In particular
\chi^{\Omega,(1)}_{ijk}=0
, and the first nonlocal bulk
correction appears at order

.

Remark 7

(

vs.
E^{-}
)
.

The tangential component of
\mathbf{E}
does not suffer a jump when crossing
\partial\Omega

(
\mathbf{E}^{+}_{\parallel}=\mathbf{E}^{-}_{\parallel}
), while the normal component is
generally discontinuous. Since the integral (
51
) is
taken over
\Omega^{c}
, it is
E^{+}_{j}
that appears naturally; the
interior limit
E^{-}_{j}
does not enter this term. This asymmetry
is structural, not imposed by hand.

3.5
Distributional thin-layer limit and surface susceptibilities

The boundary terms (
54
) are concentrated in a layer of
thickness
\ell
around
\partial\Omega
. We apply the distributional
thin-layer procedure of Section
2.6
, now in three
dimensions with the Jacobian (
57
).

3.5.1
Leading-order moment (
M_{0}
)

At leading order in
\ell/\lambda
, the thin-layer limit of

\chi^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega),(n)}_{ij\,k_{1}\cdots k_{n}}
yields a distribution supported
on
\partial\Omega
:

\chi^{\mathscr{V}(\partial\Omega),(n)}_{ij\,k_{1}\cdots k_{n}}(\mathbf{r})\;\xrightarrow{\ell\to 0}\;\mathcal{M}^{(n)}_{ij\,k_{1}\cdots k_{n}}\,\delta_{\partial\Omega},

(59)

with the
surface moment

\mathcal{M}^{(n)}_{ij\,k_{1}\cdots k_{n}}(\mathbf{R}_{0}):=\frac{1}{n!}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}R_{k_{1}}\cdots R_{k_{n}}\,\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{R})\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{R}_{\parallel}\,\mathrm{d}R_{\perp},

(60)

where the half-space
R_{\perp}>0
corresponds to the exterior
\Omega^{c}

near
\partial\Omega
.

3.5.2
Vanishing of the first-order correction (
M_{1}=0
)

The first-order correction in
\ell
arises from the linear term
sH

in the Jacobian expansion (
57
). Concretely, it
contributes an integral of the form

\mathcal{M}^{(1)}_{ij}\;\propto\;H\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}s\,\mathrm{d}s\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{R}_{\parallel},s)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{R}_{\parallel},

(61)

where the integration runs over the thin layer
|s|\lesssim\ell
and
over the tangential displacement
\mathbf{R}_{\parallel}
. For a bulk-isotropic
kernel (
47
), the integrand is
even
in
s
(since

\tilde{\Delta}_{ij}
depends only on
R=|\mathbf{R}|=\sqrt{R_{\parallel}^{2}+s^{2}}
),
while the prefactor
s
is
odd
. The full integrand is therefore
odd under
s\to-s
, and integrates to zero on the symmetric domain

s\in(-\ell,+\ell)
:

\mathcal{M}^{(1)}_{ij\,k_{1}\cdots k_{n}}=0.

(62)

This is a fundamental result: for an isotropic bulk kernel, the curvature
corrections
\partial_{n}\delta_{\partial\Omega}
and
H\,\delta_{\partial\Omega}

at order
\ell
both vanish simultaneously. Curvature effects first
appear at order
\ell^{2}
.

3.5.3
Full distributional expansion

Combining the above, the interfacial polarization (
51
)
admits the distributional expansion:

P^{\partial\Omega}_{i}(\mathbf{r})=-\varepsilon_{0}\Bigl[\mathcal{S}^{(0)}_{ij}\,\delta_{\partial\Omega}+\mathcal{S}^{(1)}_{ijk}\,\partial_{k}\delta_{\partial\Omega}+\ell^{2}\,\mathcal{S}^{(2)}_{ij}\,H\,\delta_{\partial\Omega}+\ell^{2}\,\mathcal{S}^{(3)}_{ij}\,K\,\delta_{\partial\Omega}+\cdots\Bigr]E_{j}^{+}+O(\ell^{3}/\lambda^{3}),

(63)

where
\mathcal{S}^{(1)}_{ijk}=0
by isotropy, and the remaining
tensors
\mathcal{S}^{(p)}
are given by moments of

\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}
over the exterior half-space.

Remark 8
.

It is isotropy and centrosymmetry
together
that lead to the vanishing of the
term
\mathcal{S}^{(1)}_{ijk}
, for if the centrosymmetry constraint is
relaxed, isotropic tensors of order 3 do exist. They are then proportional to
the Levi-Civita pseudo-tensor
\epsilon_{ijk}
, which is completely
antisymmetric, and the tensor takes the form

\mathcal{S}^{(1)}_{ijk}=S_{0}\,\epsilon_{ijk}
.
The scalar
S_{0}
is a
pseudo-scalar
: it changes sign under
spatial inversion, and its non-vanishing signals the chirality of the
medium. Since
\epsilon_{ijk}\,\partial_{j}E_{k}\propto(\nabla\times\mathbf{E})_{i}\propto B_{i}
, this term introduces a coupling between the electric
polarization and the magnetic field, which is precisely the
Drude–Born–Fedorov constitutive relation characteristic of optically
active media. This non-centrosymmetric regime (isotropic but chiral !)
is the subject of the companion paper
[
20
]
.

3.5.4
Surface susceptibility

By bulk isotropy (
47
) and centrosymmetry, the half-space
integral
\mathcal{S}^{(0)}_{ij}
is proportional to
\delta_{ij}

(proved in Appendix
B
):

\mathcal{S}^{(0)}_{ij}=\chi^{s}\,\delta_{ij},

(64)

with the single
surface susceptibility

\chi^{s}:=\frac{2\mu_{0}^{\parallel}+\mu_{0}^{\perp}}{6},

(65)

and the scalar half-line moments

\mu_{n}^{\alpha}:=4\pi\int_{0}^{+\infty}r^{n+2}\,\tilde{\Delta}_{\alpha}(r)\,\mathrm{d}r,\qquad\alpha\in\{\parallel,\perp\},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(66)

The scalar
\chi^{s}
is the
surface susceptibility
: it encodes the
isotropic response of the interfacial layer and constitutes a
constructive generalization of the Feibelman
d
-parameters
(see section
4
).

Remark 9
.

For a centrosymmetric isotropic kernel the tangential and normal
responses are
equal
:
\chi^{s}_{\parallel}=\chi^{s}_{\perp}=\chi^{s}
. The
notation
\chi^{s}_{\parallel}\neq\chi^{s}_{\perp}
only becomes meaningful when either
isotropy or centrosymmetry is broken (cf. the chiral
companion paper
[
20
]
).

3.5.5
Curvature corrections

At order
\ell^{2}
, the coefficients of
H\,\delta_{\partial\Omega}
and

K\,\delta_{\partial\Omega}
in (
63
) are given by the
second moments of the kernel:

\nu^{\parallel}:=\frac{2\pi}{3}\,\mu_{2}^{\parallel},\qquad\nu^{\perp}:=\frac{2\pi}{3}\,\mu_{2}^{\perp}.

(67)

These corrections are responsible for the size-dependent optical
properties of curved nanostructures (Mie corrections) and will be
evaluated explicitly for each geometry below.

3.7.1
Planar interface (

For

3.7.2
Spherical interface

For the sphere of radius

(Induced surface anisotropy)
.

3.8.1
Gaussian scalar kernel on a planar interface

Kernel.

\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{R})=\Delta_{0}\,e^{-R^{2}/\ell^{2}}\,\delta_{ij}
,

\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}=\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}=\Delta_{0}\,e^{-R^{2}/\ell^{2}}
.

Scalar moments.

Setting

\mu_{n}=4\pi\Delta_{0}\ell^{3}\int_{0}^{+\infty}u^{n+2}\,e^{-u^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}u=4\pi\Delta_{0}\ell^{3}\times\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{n+3}{2}\right).

(72)

In particular:

\mu_{0}=\Delta_{0}\,\pi^{3/2}\ell^{3},\qquad\mu_{2}=\tfrac{3}{2}\,\mu_{0}\,\ell^{2}.

(73)

Surface susceptibility (plane,
H=K=0
).

Since
\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}=\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}
,
equation (
65
) gives:

\chi^{s}=\frac{3\mu_{0}}{6}=\frac{\mu_{0}}{2}=\frac{\Delta_{0}\,\pi^{3/2}\ell^{3}}{2}.

(74)

The factor
1/2
arises because the half-space integral covers only

R_{\perp}>0
.

Interfacial polarization.

P^{\partial\Omega}_{i}=-\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\Delta_{0}\pi^{3/2}\ell^{3}}{2}\,\delta_{ij}\,E_{j}^{+}\,\delta(z)+O(\ell^{2}/\lambda^{2}).

(75)

3.8.2
Yukawa scalar kernel on a spherical interface

Kernel.

\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{R})=\Delta_{0}\ell\,e^{-R/\ell}/R\,\delta_{ij}
,
i.e.,
\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}=\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}=\Delta_{0}\ell\,e^{-R/\ell}/R
.

Scalar moments.

\mu_{0}=4\pi\Delta_{0}\ell^{3},\qquad\mu_{2}=6\ell^{2}\,\mu_{0}=24\pi\Delta_{0}\ell^{5}.

(76)

Surface susceptibilities.

\chi^{s}_{\parallel}=\chi^{s}_{\perp}=\mu_{0}/2=2\pi\Delta_{0}\ell^{3}
.

Curvature correction on the sphere.

From (
69
) with
H=2/R_{0}
and

\nu^{\parallel,\perp}=\frac{2\pi}{3}\mu_{2}
:

\frac{|\delta P^{\text{curv}}|}{|\delta P^{(0)}|}=\frac{(4\pi/3)\times 24\pi\Delta_{0}\ell^{5}/R_{0}}{2\pi\Delta_{0}\ell^{3}}=\frac{16\pi\ell^{2}}{R_{0}}.

(77)

For
R_{0}=10\,\mathrm{nm}
and
\ell=0.3\,\mathrm{nm}
, this gives

\approx 1.5\times 10^{-2}
: the Mie correction is of order
1\%
,
observable in nanophotonic experiments.

3.8.3
Tensorial Lorentz kernel on a planar interface

Kernel.

\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{R})=\frac{\Delta_{0}}{(1+R^{2}/\ell^{2})^{2}}\left[\delta_{ij}+\frac{\beta\ell^{2}}{R^{2}+\ell^{2}}\,\frac{R_{i}R_{j}}{R^{2}}\right],

(78)

with
\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}=\Delta_{0}/(1+R^{2}/\ell^{2})^{2}
and

\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}=\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}(1+\beta\ell^{2}/(R^{2}+\ell^{2}))
.
The parameter
\beta
controls the normal/tangential anisotropy of the bulk
kernel;
\beta=0
recovers the isotropic case.

Remark 12

(Illustrative model)
.

Equation (
78
) is not derived from a specific microscopic
model but is chosen for its analytical tractability: the moments

\mu_{0}^{\parallel}
,
\mu_{0}^{\perp}
, and the surface susceptibility

\chi^{s}
all follow in closed form. The scalar envelope

(1+R^{2}/\ell^{2})^{-2}
is the three-dimensional Cauchy–Lorentz profile,
which decays as
R^{-4}
and is square-integrable. The tensorial factor

\beta\ell^{2}/(R^{2}+\ell^{2})
is the simplest choice that (i) respects
SO(3) invariance, (ii) introduces a finite longitudinal/transverse
anisotropy at
R=0
, and (iii) vanishes at large
R
, so that the kernel
is asymptotically isotropic. The parameter
\beta
is not fixed by
symmetry alone; its value is determined by the underlying microscopic
model.

Scalar moments (setting
u=R/\ell
and using

\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{2}/(1+u^{2})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u=\pi/4
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{2}/(1+u^{2})^{3}\,\mathrm{d}u=\pi/8
):

\mu_{0}^{\parallel}=\pi^{2}\Delta_{0}\ell^{3},\qquad\mu_{0}^{\perp}=\pi^{2}\Delta_{0}\ell^{3}\!\left(1+\frac{\beta}{2}\right).

(79)

Surface susceptibility (plane).

Inserting into (
65
):

\chi^{s}=\frac{2\mu_{0}^{\parallel}+\mu_{0}^{\perp}}{6}=\frac{\pi^{2}\Delta_{0}\ell^{3}(6+\beta)}{12}.

(80)

Remark 13

(No surface anisotropy for SO(3)-invariant kernels)
.

Although
\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}\neq\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}
when
\beta\neq 0
,
the half-space integral still gives
\mathcal{S}^{(0)}_{ij}\propto\delta_{ij}

(see Appendix
B
). The reason is that the

\varphi
integration yields equal angular factors for the tangential
(
\pi\cdot\frac{4}{3}
) and normal (
2\pi\cdot\frac{2}{3}
) components,
cancelling the apparent asymmetry between
\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}
and

\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}
. Surface anisotropy (
\chi^{s}_{\parallel}\neq\chi^{s}_{\perp}
) can
only arise from kernels that break SO(3) invariance, e.g., through
interface-induced anisotropy not captured by the present bulk model.

3.8.4
Local limit: recovery of Fresnel conditions

Kernel.

\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{R})=\chi_{0}\,\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{R})\,\delta_{ij}
. We are then in the case of a local material.
All moments of order
n\geq 1
vanish:
\mu_{n}=0
for
n\geq 1
.

For any surface
\partial\Omega

: the interface is a simple susceptibility

4
Generalized Maxwell boundary conditions

We now derive the boundary conditions associated with the
Maxwell equations in the presence of the surface distributions
established in Section
3
. We work in the harmonic
regime at frequency
\omega
with the
e^{-i\omega t}
convention,
and set
\mathbf{M}=0
(no magnetic polarization).

Remark 14
.

Throughout this section we retain the notation
\chi^{s}_{\parallel}
and
\chi^{s}_{\perp}

for the tangential and normal surface susceptibilities in order to
facilitate comparison with the Feibelman literature, where

d_{\parallel}\neq d_{\perp}
in general. Within the present model,
however, Section
3.5
and Appendix
B

establish that
\chi^{s}_{\parallel}=\chi^{s}_{\perp}=\chi^{s}
for any centrosymmetric
isotropic bulk kernel. All results below specialise to this case by
setting
\chi^{s}_{\parallel}=\chi^{s}_{\perp}
.

4.1
Maxwell equations in the distributional sense

The macroscopic Maxwell equations in matter read:

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle=i\omega\mu_{0}\mathbf{H},

(82)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{H}

\displaystyle=-i\omega\mathbf{D},

(83)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D}

\displaystyle=0,

(84)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{H}

\displaystyle=0,

(85)

with
\mathbf{D}=\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}
. When the fields and the
polarization contain singular distributions supported on
\partial\Omega
,
these equations must be interpreted in the distributional sense.
We recall the standard distributional differentiation rules for a
field
\mathbf{F}
discontinuous across
\partial\Omega

[
23
]
:

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}

\displaystyle=\left\{\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\right\}+\left[\!\left[\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\right]\!\right]\,\delta_{\partial\Omega},

(86)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\mathbf{F}

\displaystyle=\left\{\nabla\times\mathbf{F}\right\}+\mathbf{n}\times\left[\!\left[\mathbf{F}\right]\!\right]\,\delta_{\partial\Omega},

(87)

\displaystyle\nabla f

\displaystyle=\left\{\nabla f\right\}+\left[\!\left[f\right]\!\right]\,\mathbf{n}\,\delta_{\partial\Omega},

(88)

where
\left\{\,\cdot\,\right\}
denotes the regular part of the
distribution (identified with a locally integrable function away from

\partial\Omega
) and
\left[\!\left[\mathbf{F}\right]\!\right]:=\mathbf{F}^{+}-\mathbf{F}^{-}

denotes the jump of
\mathbf{F}
across
\partial\Omega
, with
\mathbf{F}^{+}

the limit from
\Omega^{c}
and
\mathbf{F}^{-}
the limit from
\Omega
.

For a surface distribution
\mathbf{Q}^{s}\delta_{\partial\Omega}
:

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{Q}^{s}\delta_{\partial\Omega})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{s}\cdot\mathbf{Q}^{s}_{\parallel}\,\delta_{\partial\Omega}-Q^{s}_{\perp}\,\partial_{n}\delta_{\partial\Omega},

(89)

\displaystyle\nabla\times(\mathbf{Q}^{s}\delta_{\partial\Omega})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{s}\times\mathbf{Q}^{s}_{\parallel}\,\delta_{\partial\Omega}+\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{Q}^{s}\,\partial_{n}\delta_{\partial\Omega},

(90)

where
\nabla_{s}
is the surface gradient operator (Laplace–Beltrami),

\mathbf{Q}^{s}_{\parallel}
the tangential component, and

Q^{s}_{\perp}=\mathbf{Q}^{s}\cdot\mathbf{n}
the normal component.

4.2
Structure of the total polarization

From the distributional expansion established in
Section
3.5
, the total polarization splits into two terms :

\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{P}^{\Omega}(\mathbf{r})+\mathbf{P}^{\partial\Omega}(\mathbf{r}),

(91)

where
\mathbf{P}^{\Omega}
is the regular bulk polarization and
\mathbf{P}^{\partial\Omega}

the interfacial contribution. At the order retained here
(leading surface susceptibilities plus curvature corrections):

\mathbf{P}^{\partial\Omega}=-\varepsilon_{0}\Bigl[\mathcal{S}^{(0)}_{ij}\,E_{j}^{+}\,\delta_{\partial\Omega}+\bigl(\nu^{\parallel}H\,P^{\parallel}_{ij}+\nu^{\perp}H\,P^{\perp}_{ij}\bigr)E_{j}^{+}\,\delta_{\partial\Omega}-\bigl(\nu^{\parallel}P^{\parallel}_{ij}+\nu^{\perp}P^{\perp}_{ij}\bigr)E_{j}^{+}\,\partial_{n}\delta_{\partial\Omega}\Bigr]+O(\ell^{3}),

(92)

where we recall
\mathcal{S}^{(0)}_{ij}=\chi^{s}_{\parallel}P^{\parallel}_{ij}+\chi^{s}_{\perp}P^{\perp}_{ij}
.

We separate (
92
) into tangential and normal parts:

\displaystyle P^{\parallel,\partial\Omega}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon_{0}\bigl[\chi^{s}_{\parallel}+\nu^{\parallel}H\bigr]E^{+}_{i,\parallel}\,\delta_{\partial\Omega}+\varepsilon_{0}\,\nu^{\parallel}\,E^{+}_{i,\parallel}\,\partial_{n}\delta_{\partial\Omega}+O(\ell^{3}),

(93)

\displaystyle P^{\perp,\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon_{0}\bigl[\chi^{s}_{\perp}+\nu^{\perp}H\bigr]E^{+}_{\perp}\,\delta_{\partial\Omega}+\varepsilon_{0}\,\nu^{\perp}\,E^{+}_{\perp}\,\partial_{n}\delta_{\partial\Omega}+O(\ell^{3}).

(94)

4.3
Identification of boundary conditions

We insert (
92
) into the Maxwell
equations (
82
)–(
85
) and identify the
coefficients of
\delta_{\partial\Omega}
and
\partial_{n}\delta_{\partial\Omega}
.

4.3.1
Faraday’s law (See (
82
))

In the absence of magnetic polarization,
\nabla\times\mathbf{E}
contains no
singularity contributed by
\delta\mathbf{P}
. Collecting the coefficient of

\delta_{\partial\Omega}
in (
82
) via (
87
)
yields the classical tangential continuity condition:

\mathbf{n}\times\left[\!\left[\mathbf{E}\right]\!\right]=\mathbf{0},

(95)

i.e.,
\mathbf{E}^{+}_{\parallel}=\mathbf{E}^{-}_{\parallel}
. This condition is not
modified by the surface susceptibilities.

4.3.2
Ampère’s law (See (
83
))

The surface part of
\mathbf{D}=\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}^{\Omega}+\mathbf{P}^{\partial\Omega}

contributes to
\nabla\times\mathbf{H}=-i\omega\mathbf{D}
via (
90
).
Collecting the coefficient of
\delta_{\partial\Omega}
gives:

\mathbf{n}\times\left[\!\left[\mathbf{H}\right]\!\right]=-i\omega\varepsilon_{0}\Bigl[\bigl(\chi^{s}_{\parallel}+\nu^{\parallel}H\bigr)\mathbf{E}^{+}_{\parallel}+\nu^{\parallel}\,\nabla_{s}\times\mathbf{E}^{+}_{\parallel}\Bigr]+O(\ell^{3}).

(96)

Here
\nabla_{s}\times\mathbf{E}^{+}_{\parallel}
is the surface curl of the
tangential field, which is a vector normal to
\partial\Omega
. Collecting the coefficient of

\partial_{n}\delta_{\partial\Omega}
yields an additional condition on the
mean of the normal derivatives:

\mathbf{n}\times\left\langle\partial_{n}\mathbf{H}\right\rangle=i\omega\varepsilon_{0}\,\nu^{\parallel}\,\mathbf{E}^{+}_{\parallel}+O(\ell^{3}),

(97)

where
\left\langle\partial_{n}\mathbf{H}\right\rangle:=(\partial_{n}\mathbf{H}^{+}+\partial_{n}\mathbf{H}^{-})/2
.

4.3.3
Electric Gauss law (See (
84
))

Applying (
86
) and (
89
) to

\mathbf{D}=\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}^{\Omega}+\delta\mathbf{P}
, and collecting the
coefficient of
\delta_{\partial\Omega}
:

\left[\!\left[D_{\perp}\right]\!\right]=-\varepsilon_{0}\Bigl[\bigl(\chi^{s}_{\perp}+\nu^{\perp}H\bigr)E^{+}_{\perp}+\nabla_{s}\cdot\bigl(\chi^{s}_{\parallel}\mathbf{E}^{+}_{\parallel}\bigr)\Bigr]+O(\ell^{3}).

(98)

The coefficient of
\partial_{n}\delta_{\partial\Omega}
gives:

\left\langle D_{\perp}\right\rangle=\varepsilon_{0}\,\nu^{\perp}\,E^{+}_{\perp}+O(\ell^{3}).

(99)

4.3.4
Magnetic Gauss law (See (
85
))

In the absence of magnetic polarization, the condition is classical:

\left[\!\left[H_{\perp}\right]\!\right]=0.

(100)

Condition (

Remark 15

(Physical meaning of the renormalization)
.

The surface susceptibility
\chi^{s}_{\perp}
renormalizes the effective
permittivity on the exterior side: the transition layer behaves as
if the exterior medium had permittivity

\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{ext}}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ext}}+\varepsilon_{0}\chi^{s}_{\perp}
. For a denser-than-vacuum interfacial layer
(
\chi^{s}_{\perp}>0
),
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{ext}}>\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ext}}
: the layer enhances the effective
permittivity seen by the exterior field.

4.5
Explicit boundary conditions

Substituting (
103
) into the conditions of
Section
4.3
and using
\mathbf{E}^{+}_{\parallel}=\mathbf{E}^{-}_{\parallel}
(Faraday), we obtain conditions involving only the
interior field
\mathbf{E}^{-}
and the exterior tangential field, both
well-defined quantities.

Jump of tangential
\mathbf{H}
.

\mathbf{n}\times\left[\!\left[\mathbf{H}\right]\!\right]=-i\omega\varepsilon_{0}\,\chi^{s}_{\parallel}\,\mathbf{E}^{-}_{\parallel}-i\omega\varepsilon_{0}\,\nu^{\parallel}\Bigl(H\,\mathbf{E}^{-}_{\parallel}+\nabla_{s}\times\mathbf{E}^{-}_{\parallel}\Bigr)+O(\ell^{3}).

(104)

Jump of normal
D
.

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ext}}\,E^{+}_{\perp}-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{int}}\,E^{-}_{\perp}=-\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{int}}\,\chi^{s}_{\perp}}{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{ext}}}\,E^{-}_{\perp}-\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{ext}}}\bigl(1+\nu^{\perp}H\bigr)\nabla_{s}\cdot\!\bigl(\chi^{s}_{\parallel}\mathbf{E}^{-}_{\parallel}\bigr)+O(\ell^{3}).

(105)

Remark 16

(Universality and experimental access)
.

The two conditions (
104
)–(
105
)
are universal: the entire microscopic complexity of the nonlocal kernel
is encoded in just four effective scalars,
\chi^{s}_{\parallel}
,
\chi^{s}_{\perp}
,

\nu^{\parallel}
,
\nu^{\perp}
. As shown in
Section
4.6
below,
\chi^{s}_{\parallel}
and
\chi^{s}_{\perp}
are
proportional to the Feibelman
d
-parameters, which are routinely
extracted from
flat
interfaces by spectroscopic ellipsometry
(angular and frequency dependence of the Fresnel coefficients
r_{p}
,

r_{s}
) or from the dispersion of surface plasmons measured by
attenuated total reflection or electron energy-loss spectroscopy.
The curvature parameters
\nu^{\parallel}
and
\nu^{\perp}
appear
multiplied by the mean curvature
H
and are accessible by comparing
interfaces of different curvatures: for spherical nanoparticles the
plasmonic Mie resonances are shifted from their classical positions by
an amount proportional to
d_{\perp}/R
, and measuring this shift as a
function of radius
R
in single-particle dark-field spectroscopy
isolates
\nu^{\perp}
(and similarly
\nu^{\parallel}
from non-spherical
geometries). All four parameters can therefore be determined
experimentally without any knowledge of the underlying nonlocal kernel.

4.6
Connection with the Feibelman
d
-parameters

The Feibelman
d
-parameters are defined as
[
11
]
:

d_{\perp}(\omega):=\frac{\int z\,\delta\varepsilon(z,\omega)\,E_{\perp}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z}{\left[\!\left[\varepsilon E_{\perp}\right]\!\right]},\qquad d_{\parallel}(\omega):=\frac{\int z\,\partial_{z}E_{\parallel}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z}{\left[\!\left[E_{\parallel}\right]\!\right]},

(106)

where
\delta\varepsilon(z)
is the excess permittivity in the
transition layer. In the present formalism these parameters emerge as:

d_{\perp}\sim\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\,\chi^{s}_{\perp}}{\left[\!\left[\varepsilon\right]\!\right]},\qquad d_{\parallel}\sim\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\,\chi^{s}_{\parallel}}{\left[\!\left[\varepsilon\right]\!\right]},

(107)

where
\left[\!\left[\varepsilon\right]\!\right]=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ext}}-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{int}}
. The curvature parameters
\nu^{\parallel}

and
\nu^{\perp}
constitute the natural extension of the Feibelman

d
-parameters to curved interfaces:

d_{\perp}^{\mathrm{curv}}\sim\frac{\varepsilon_{0}(\chi^{s}_{\perp}+\nu^{\perp}H)}{\left[\!\left[\varepsilon\right]\!\right]},\qquad d_{\parallel}^{\mathrm{curv}}\sim\frac{\varepsilon_{0}(\chi^{s}_{\parallel}+\nu^{\parallel}H)}{\left[\!\left[\varepsilon\right]\!\right]}.

(108)

The flat-interface Feibelman parameters are thus the zeroth-order
members of a hierarchy of surface coefficients, each controlled by a
successive moment of the nonlocal kernel.

4.7.1
Planar interface

For
\partial\Omega=\{z=0\}
one has

and, by Faraday’s law in the

\nabla_{s}\times\mathbf{E}^{-}_{\parallel}=0
.
Conditions (
104
)–(

)
reduce to:

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}\times\left[\!\left[\mathbf{H}\right]\!\right]

\displaystyle=-i\omega\varepsilon_{0}\,\chi^{s}_{\parallel}\,\mathbf{E}^{-}_{\parallel},

(109)

\displaystyle\left[\!\left[D_{\perp}\right]\!\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{int}}\,\chi^{s}_{\perp}}{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{ext}}}\,E^{-}_{\perp}-\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{ext}}}\nabla_{s}\cdot\!\bigl(\chi^{s}_{\parallel}\mathbf{E}^{-}_{\parallel}\bigr).

(110)

For

and

4.7.2
Spherical interface

For the sphere of radius

(

,
\mathbf{n}=\hat{\mathbf{r}}
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}\times\left[\!\left[\mathbf{H}\right]\!\right]

(111)

(112)

The terms proportional to

are the nonlocal Mie corrections:
they shift the scattering resonances of a plasmonic nanosphere with
respect to the planar case, and become significant for

.

Remark 17

(Limiting cases)
.

Setting

)–(

).

5.2.2
Modified Mie coefficients

TM polarization.

The incident plane wave
E_{z}^{\mathrm{inc}}=e^{ik_{0}x}
is decomposed
in cylindrical harmonics as
\sum_{m}i^{m}J_{m}(k_{0}r)\,e^{im\varphi}
.
The scattered and interior fields have the same angular structure, with
coefficients
b_{m}
(scattered,
H_{m}^{(1)}
outside) and
c_{m}
(interior,

J_{m}
inside). The two boundary conditions at
r=R_{0}
are:

\displaystyle\text{(Faraday)}\quad[\![E_{z}]\!]=0,

(116)

\displaystyle\text{(modified Amp\`{e}re)}\quad[\![\partial_{r}E_{z}]\!]=-k_{0}^{2}\,\chi^{s}_{\parallel}\,E_{z}\big|_{R_{0}}.

(117)

Inserting the Bessel expansions and solving the
2\times 2
system,
one obtains:

b_{m}^{\mathrm{TM}}=-i^{m}\,\frac{k_{0}J_{m}^{\prime}(\rho_{1})J_{m}(\rho_{2})-k_{2}J_{m}(\rho_{1})J_{m}^{\prime}(\rho_{2})+k_{0}^{2}\chi^{s}_{\parallel}\,J_{m}(\rho_{1})J_{m}(\rho_{2})}{k_{0}H_{m}^{(1)^{\prime}}(\rho_{1})J_{m}(\rho_{2})-k_{2}H_{m}^{(1)}(\rho_{1})J_{m}^{\prime}(\rho_{2})+k_{0}^{2}\chi^{s}_{\parallel}\,H_{m}^{(1)}(\rho_{1})J_{m}(\rho_{2})},

(118)

where
\rho_{1}=k_{0}R_{0}
,
\rho_{2}=k_{2}R_{0}
, and primes denote
derivatives with respect to the argument. For
\chi^{s}_{\parallel}=0
this
reduces to the standard 2D TM Mie coefficient.

Remark 18
.

The surface susceptibility
\chi^{s}_{\parallel}
adds a term proportional to

J_{m}(\rho_{1})J_{m}(\rho_{2})
in both numerator and denominator of

b_{m}
. For
k_{0}\chi^{s}_{\parallel}\ll 1
, the correction is perturbative and
shifts the Mie resonances (zeros of the denominator) by a complex
amount of order
k_{0}\chi^{s}_{\parallel}
.

TE polarization.

For the TE case the field
H_{z}
satisfies the same Helmholtz equation,
and the physical components
E_{r}=(m/\omega\varepsilon r)\,H_{z}
and

E_{\varphi}=(1/i\omega\varepsilon)\,\partial_{r}H_{z}
are derived from it.
The three modified boundary conditions reduce, after elimination of the
interior coefficient
d_{m}
via the continuity of

, to a
single scalar equation per mode
m
:

\displaystyle\text{(Faraday)}\quad n_{2}\bigl(i^{m}J_{m}^{\prime}(\rho_{1})+a_{m}H_{m}^{(1)^{\prime}}(\rho_{1})\bigr)=d_{m}J_{m}^{\prime}(\rho_{2}),

(119)

\displaystyle\text{(modified Gauss + Amp\`{e}re)}\quad(1+\chi^{s}_{\perp})\,A_{m}-B_{m}=-ik_{0}\chi^{s}_{\parallel}\,\bigl(i^{m}J_{m}^{\prime}(\rho_{1})+a_{m}H_{m}^{(1)^{\prime}}(\rho_{1})\bigr),

(120)

where
A_{m}=i^{m}J_{m}(\rho_{1})+a_{m}H_{m}^{(1)}(\rho_{1})
and

B_{m}=d_{m}J_{m}(\rho_{2})
. The second condition is derived from the
distributional divergence identity applied to the modified
Gauss law (
98

charge-continuity equation; the term
-ik_{0}\chi^{s}_{\parallel}
arises from the
surface divergence of the tangential polarization current.

Substituting (
119

120

for

a_{m}^{\mathrm{TE}}=-i^{m}\,\frac{(1+\chi^{s}_{\perp})\,J_{m}(\rho_{1})-\tilde{\eta}_{m}\,J_{m}^{\prime}(\rho_{1})}{(1+\chi^{s}_{\perp})\,H_{m}^{(1)}(\rho_{1})-\tilde{\eta}_{m}\,H_{m}^{(1)^{\prime}}(\rho_{1})},\qquad\tilde{\eta}_{m}=\frac{n_{2}J_{m}(\rho_{2})}{J_{m}^{\prime}(\rho_{2})}-ik_{0}\chi^{s}_{\parallel}.

(121)

The standard 2D TE Mie coefficient is recovered for
\chi^{s}_{\parallel}=\chi^{s}_{\perp}=0
.

Remark 19

(Physical interpretation of the two susceptibilities)
.

The two parameters enter (
121

ways. The susceptibility
\chi^{s}_{\parallel}
shifts the effective interior
impedance
\eta_{m}\to\tilde{\eta}_{m}
by a purely imaginary term

-ik_{0}\chi^{s}_{\parallel}

condition, entirely consistent with the energy conservation result
of Appendix
E

\chi^{s}_{\perp}

contrast, renormalizes the exterior permittivity in the boundary
condition:
(1+\chi^{s}_{\perp})
multiplies both
J_{m}
and
H_{m}^{(1)}

in exact correspondence with the renormalized permittivity

\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{ext}}=\varepsilon_{0}(1+\chi^{s}_{\perp})

The 2D scattering efficiency is

Q=\frac{C_{\mathrm{sca}}}{2R_{0}}=\frac{2}{k_{0}\times 2R_{0}}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}|b_{m}|^{2}\quad\text{(TM),}

6
Conclusion

We have presented a systematic and self-contained framework for deriving
the surface electromagnetic response of a nonlocal medium from its bulk
response kernel, without any phenomenological assumption on the structure
of the transition layer.

Summary of results.

Starting from the most general tensorial nonlocal constitutive relation,
we introduced a
spatial moment expansion
that naturally splits the
polarization into a bulk contribution which is identical to that of an infinite
homogeneous medium and a boundary contribution concentrated within a
layer of thickness
\ell
near the interface
\partial\Omega
. Applying a

distributional thin-layer limit
to the boundary contribution then
converts it into a hierarchy of singular distributions supported on

\partial\Omega
, whose coefficients are successive moments of the bulk kernel

\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}
over the exterior half-space.

For a bulk-isotropic kernel, the hierarchy simplifies considerably: the
first-order correction (
M_{1}
) vanishes by a parity argument, so that
curvature effects first appear at order
\ell^{2}
. At leading order, the
entire interfacial physics is encoded in a single scalar, the surface
susceptibility
\chi^{s}
, given by the explicit
formula (
65
) in terms of the zeroth-order radial moments

\mu_{0}^{\parallel,\perp}
of the kernel. These quantities generalize the Feibelman
d
-parameters
constructively: rather than fitting parameters, they are derived
quantities determined by the bulk kernel alone.

The curvature corrections, at order
\ell^{2}
, are proportional to the
mean curvature
H
and Gaussian curvature
K
of
\partial\Omega
, with
coefficients
\nu^{\parallel,\perp}
given by the second-order moments

\mu_{2}^{\parallel,\perp}
. For a plasmonic nanosphere of radius

R_{0}\sim 10\,\mathrm{nm}
with a nonlocality range
\ell\sim 0.3\,\mathrm{nm}
, these corrections are of order
1\%
, consistent with
recent experimental observations in nanophotonics

[
14
,
16
]
.

The formalism has been illustrated on four canonical geometries
(planar, spherical, cylindrical, and ellipsoidal interfaces) and four
kernel models (Gaussian scalar, Yukawa scalar, tensorial Lorentz), and
local limit. In every case the calculations are fully explicit, and the
local (Fresnel) limit is recovered as a special case. Finally, the
generalized Maxwell boundary conditions derived in Section
4

provide a practical form of the results, directly usable in scattering
and reflection calculations.

Key structural observations.

Three points deserve emphasis.

1.

The kernel encodes everything.
All surface properties :
susceptibilities, curvature corrections, boundary conditions
follow from a small number of scalar moments of
\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}
.
The microscopic complexity is fully absorbed into these moments, which
play the role of effective parameters characterising the interface.

2.

Surface anisotropy is a bulk property.
For a
scalar kernel (
\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}=\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}
),
the surface response is always isotropic (
\chi^{s}_{\parallel}=\chi^{s}_{\perp}
),
regardless of the interface geometry. Anisotropy (
\chi^{s}_{\parallel}\neq\chi^{s}_{\perp}
) is a signature of the tensorial structure of the bulk
kernel, not of the curvature of the surface.

3.

The hierarchy is universal.
The same distributional
structure, leading
\delta_{\partial\Omega}
, vanishing first-order
correction, curvature terms at order
\ell^{2}
holds for every
geometry and every isotropic kernel. It reflects the interplay
between the rapid decay of nonlocal interactions and the geometric
invariants of the surface.

Outlook.

Several natural extensions present themselves.

First, the present analysis assumed bulk centro-symmetry, which causes
the odd bulk moments to vanish. Relaxing this assumption (e.g., for
interfaces between two different noncentrosymmetric media) introduces
additional terms in the moment expansion and modifies the boundary
conditions accordingly; the distributional framework developed here
applies without structural change.

Second, the hypothesis that the nonlocal interaction is cut off sharply
at the interface (
\mathds{1}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
in the kernel support) can
be replaced by a smooth transition profile. This amounts to replacing
the sharp half-space integrals by weighted integrals over a transition
zone, and does not alter the leading-order results.

Third, and most importantly, the entire formalism extends to the

nonlinear
case. As shown in the companion
paper
[
21
]
, the second-order surface susceptibility

\chi^{(2),s}_{ijk}
is derived from the bulk nonlinear kernel

\tilde{\Delta}^{(2)}_{ijk}
by exactly the same distributional procedure,
with the nonlinear kernel replaced by its linear effective kernel
obtained by integrating out one of the two field arguments. The
hierarchy of nonlinear surface coefficients is thereby closed
recursively:
\chi^{(n)}_{s}
always reduces to a
\chi^{(1)}_{s}

effective problem by successive marginal integrations. This result
provides, for the first time, a systematic derivation of the nonlinear
surface susceptibilities including
\chi^{(2)}_{s}
for
second-harmonic generation at curved interfaces directly from the
bulk nonlinear kernel, without any
ad hoc
surface model.

Appendix A
Isotropic rank-2 tensor functions

We give a self-contained proof that every rank-2 tensor-valued function

F_{ij}(\mathbf{R})
satisfying the isotropy condition

F_{ij}(Q\mathbf{R})=Q_{ik}\,Q_{jl}\,F_{kl}(\mathbf{R})\qquad\forall\,Q\in SO(3),\quad\forall\,\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\},

(123)

has the form (
47
).

Step 1 — basis of available tensors.

At a fixed
\mathbf{R}\neq 0
, one seeks the symmetric rank-2 tensors that
can be formed from
\delta_{ij}
(the only
SO(3)
-invariant rank-2
tensor) and
\mathbf{R}
. The only such tensors are
\delta_{ij}
itself and

R_{i}R_{j}
. Any antisymmetric combination would require the
Levi-Civita symbol
\epsilon_{ijk}
, which is of rank 3. Hence the
most general
ansatz
consistent with isotropy is

F_{ij}(\mathbf{R})=a(R)\,\delta_{ij}+b(R)\,R_{i}R_{j},

(124)

where
a
and
b
can depend only on
R=|\mathbf{R}|
by the residual

SO(2)
symmetry around the axis
\mathbf{R}
.

Step 2 — physical identification.

Introduce the longitudinal projector

P^{\parallel}_{ij}:=\hat{R}_{i}\hat{R}_{j}
and the transverse projector

P^{\perp}_{ij}:=\delta_{ij}-\hat{R}_{i}\hat{R}_{j}
, where

\hat{\mathbf{R}}:=\mathbf{R}/R
. Contracting (
124
) with each:

\displaystyle F_{ij}\,\hat{R}_{i}\hat{R}_{j}

\displaystyle=a+b\,R^{2}=:\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}(R),

\displaystyle F_{ij}\,e^{\perp}_{i}e^{\perp}_{j}

\displaystyle=a\phantom{{}+b\,R^{2}}=:\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}(R),

for any unit vector
\mathbf{e}^{\perp}\perp\mathbf{R}
. Solving for
a
and
b

and substituting back into (
124
) yields exactly (
47
).
∎

Remark 20
.

In Fourier space,
\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}(\omega,k)
and

\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}(\omega,k)
are the familiar longitudinal and
transverse dielectric functions of an isotropic
medium
[
5
,
2
]
. For a
centrosymmetric kernel both are even functions of
R
.

Appendix B
Derivation of the surface susceptibilities

We compute the zeroth-order surface moment

\mathcal{S}^{(0)}_{ij}=\int_{R_{\perp}>0}\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{R})\,\mathrm{d}^{3}R,

(125)

where
R_{\perp}=\mathbf{R}\cdot\mathbf{n}>0
selects the exterior half-space.

Step 1 — insert the isotropic decomposition.

Equation (
47
) rewrites the integrand as

\tilde{\Delta}^{(1)}_{ij}(\mathbf{R})=\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}(R)\,\delta_{ij}+\bigl[\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}(R)-\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}(R)\bigr]\hat{R}_{i}\hat{R}_{j}.

(126)

Step 2 — spherical coordinates.

Set
\mathbf{n}=\hat{\mathbf{z}}
and

\mathbf{R}=R(\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\cos\theta)
with

R>0
,
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
,
\varphi\in[0,2\pi)
.
The relevant angular integrals over the hemisphere are:

\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\!\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta=1,\quad\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\!\sin^{3}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{2}{3},\quad\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\!\cos^{2}\theta\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{1}{3}.

(127)

Step 3 — tangential component (
i=j=x
).

Since
\hat{R}_{x}=\sin\theta\cos\varphi
, the
xx
component of (
126
) is

\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}+(\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}-\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel})\sin^{2}\theta\cos^{2}\varphi
.
Using
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\varphi=2\pi
and
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos^{2}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\varphi=\pi
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{(0)}_{xx}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!r^{2}\mathrm{d}r\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\!\!\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\bigl[2\pi\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}+\pi(\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}-\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel})\sin^{2}\theta\bigr]

\displaystyle=\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}\mathrm{d}r\,\Bigl[\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\pi/2}(2-\sin^{2}\theta)\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta}_{=\,4/3}+\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin^{3}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta}_{=\,2/3}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}\bigl(2\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}(r)+\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}(r)\bigr)\mathrm{d}r.

(128)

Step 4 — normal component (
i=j=z
).

Since
\hat{R}_{z}=\cos\theta
, the
zz
component of (
126
) is

\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}\sin^{2}\theta+\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}\cos^{2}\theta

(no
\varphi
dependence;
\varphi
integral gives
2\pi

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{(0)}_{zz}

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!r^{2}\mathrm{d}r\Bigl[\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin^{3}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta}_{=\,2/3}+\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\cos^{2}\theta\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta}_{=\,1/3}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}\bigl(2\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}(r)+\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}(r)\bigr)\mathrm{d}r.

(129)

Step 5 — off-diagonal components.

The
\varphi
integral of
\hat{R}_{i}\hat{R}_{j}
for
i\neq j
involves

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos\varphi\sin\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\varphi=0
or

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\varphi=0
, so all off-diagonal
components vanish.

Result.

Comparing (
128
) and (
129
), we find

\mathcal{S}^{(0)}_{xx}=\mathcal{S}^{(0)}_{yy}=\mathcal{S}^{(0)}_{zz}

and all off-diagonal terms are zero. Therefore:

\mathcal{S}^{(0)}_{ij}=\frac{2\pi}{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}\bigl(2\tilde{\Delta}_{\parallel}(r)+\tilde{\Delta}_{\perp}(r)\bigr)\mathrm{d}r\,\delta_{ij}.

(130)

In terms of the moments
\mu_{0}^{\alpha}=4\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}\tilde{\Delta}_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}r

(i.e.,
\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}\tilde{\Delta}_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}r=\mu_{0}^{\alpha}/(4\pi)
):

\mathcal{S}^{(0)}_{ij}=\frac{2\mu_{0}^{\parallel}+\mu_{0}^{\perp}}{6}\;\delta_{ij}.

(131)

Remark 21

(Proportionality to
\delta_{ij}
)
.

The fact that
\mathcal{S}^{(0)}_{ij}\propto\delta_{ij}
is a theorem,
not a coincidence: integrating an SO(3)-invariant kernel over a half-space
preserves SO(2) symmetry about
\mathbf{n}
, but the
\varphi
average
additionally forces the result to be proportional to
\delta_{ij}

(rather than
aP^{\parallel}_{ij}+bP^{\perp}_{ij}
with
a\neq b
),
because the angular factors for the tangential and normal components
happen to be equal:

\pi\times\tfrac{4}{3}=2\pi\times\tfrac{2}{3}
(tangential, Step 3) and

\pi\times\tfrac{2}{3}=2\pi\times\tfrac{1}{3}
(normal, Step 4).
Consequently:

\chi^{s}_{\parallel}=\chi^{s}_{\perp}=\frac{2\mu_{0}^{\parallel}+\mu_{0}^{\perp}}{6}.

(132)

For a centrosymmetric isotropic medium, the two surface susceptibilities
are therefore
equal
.

Appendix C
Volume element in normal coordinates

Let
\partial\Omega
be a smooth, closed surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and let

\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{u})
denote the outward unit normal at the point

\mathbf{R}_{0}(\mathbf{u})\in\partial\Omega
, where
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},u_{2})
are local
surface coordinates. Any point
\mathbf{r}
in a sufficiently thin
tubular neighbourhood of
\partial\Omega
can be written uniquely as

\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{R}_{0}(\mathbf{u})+s\,\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{u}),

(133)

where
s\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
is the
signed
distance
to
\partial\Omega
, positive in
\Omega^{c}
and negative
in
\Omega
.

Shape operator.

The
Weingarten map
(shape operator)

W:T_{\mathbf{R}_{0}}\partial\Omega\to T_{\mathbf{R}_{0}}\partial\Omega
is defined by

W:=-\mathrm{d}\mathbf{n}
.
Its eigenvalues
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}
are the

principal curvatures
of
\partial\Omega
at
\mathbf{R}_{0}
, counted
positive when the surface bends toward
\Omega^{c}
. The two
fundamental geometric invariants are

H:=\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2}\quad\text{(mean curvature)},\qquad K:=\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}\quad\text{(Gaussian curvature)}.

(134)

Jacobian computation.

The Jacobian of the map
(\mathbf{u},s)\mapsto\mathbf{r}
is

J(s)=\det\bigl(\mathrm{Id}+sW\bigr)=(1+s\kappa_{1})(1+s\kappa_{2})=1+s(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2})+s^{2}\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}+O(s^{3}),

(135)

so that the volume element reads

\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}=J(s)\,\mathrm{d}S\,\mathrm{d}s=\bigl(1+sH+s^{2}K+O(s^{3})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}S\,\mathrm{d}s,

(136)

where
\mathrm{d}S
is the area element of
\partial\Omega
.
Equation (
136
) is exact for the sphere and
the cylinder (for which
J(s)
is a polynomial in
s
),
and is an asymptotic expansion in general.

Verification on canonical geometries.

•

Plane
(
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}=0
):
J(s)=1
.

•

Sphere of radius
R_{0}

(
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}=1/R_{0}
):

J(s)=(1+s/R_{0})^{2}=1+2s/R_{0}+s^{2}/R_{0}^{2}
,
consistent with
H=2/R_{0}
,
K=1/R_{0}^{2}
.

•

Cylinder of radius
R_{0}

(
\kappa_{1}=1/R_{0}
,
\kappa_{2}=0
):

J(s)=1+s/R_{0}
,
consistent with
H=1/R_{0}
,
K=0
.

For a proof and a complete treatment of tubular
neighbourhoods, see Gray
[
22
]
.

Appendix D
Derivation of the generalized Fresnel coefficients

We derive the reflection and transmission coefficients
for TE polarization at a planar interface
z=0
between a
non-dispersive medium of index
n_{1}
(
z<0
) and a nonlocal
medium of bulk index
n_{2}
(
z>0
), in the presence of a
surface susceptibility
\chi^{s}
.

Field ansatz.

For TE polarization (electric field along
\hat{\mathbf{y}}
),
with the in-plane wavevector
k_{x}=n_{1}(\omega/c)\sin\theta_{i}=n_{2}(\omega/c)\sin\theta_{t}
, we write

E_{y}(x,z)=e^{ik_{x}x}\begin{cases}e^{ik_{1z}z}+r\,e^{-ik_{1z}z},&z<0,\\
t\,e^{ik_{2z}z},&z>0,\end{cases}\qquad k_{jz}:=n_{j}\frac{\omega}{c}\cos\theta_{j}.

(137)

Magnetic field.

From Faraday’s law
\nabla\times\mathbf{E}=i\omega\mu_{0}\mathbf{H}

(with the
e^{-i\omega t}
convention), the relevant component is

H_{x}=-\frac{1}{i\omega\mu_{0}}\,\frac{\partial E_{y}}{\partial z}.

(138)

Evaluating at
z=0
:

\displaystyle H_{x}(0^{-})

\displaystyle=-\frac{n_{1}\cos\theta_{i}}{\mu_{0}c}(1-r),

(139)

\displaystyle H_{x}(0^{+})

\displaystyle=-\frac{n_{2}\cos\theta_{t}}{\mu_{0}c}\,t.

(140)

Boundary conditions.

Two conditions apply at
z=0
.

(i) Continuity of
E_{y}
(Faraday):

1+r=t.

(141)

(ii) Jump of
H_{x}
(generalized Ampère,
equation (
109
)):

H_{x}(0^{+})-H_{x}(0^{-})=-i\omega\varepsilon_{0}\,\chi^{s}\,E_{y}(0)=-i\frac{\omega}{c}\,\frac{\chi^{s}}{\mu_{0}c}\,(1+r).

(142)

Using
1/(\mu_{0}c)=\varepsilon_{0}c
and substituting the
expressions for
H_{x}
, condition (
142
) becomes

n_{1}\cos\theta_{i}(1-r)-n_{2}\cos\theta_{t}\,t=-i\frac{\omega}{c}\,\chi^{s}(1+r).

(143)

Resolution.

Substituting
t=1+r
from (
141
)
into (
143
):

n_{1}\cos\theta_{i}(1-r)-n_{2}\cos\theta_{t}(1+r)=-i\frac{\omega}{c}\,\chi^{s}(1+r).

(144)

Collecting terms in
r
:

n_{1}\cos\theta_{i}-n_{2}\cos\theta_{t}+i\frac{\omega}{c}\,\chi^{s}=r\!\left[n_{1}\cos\theta_{i}+n_{2}\cos\theta_{t}-i\frac{\omega}{c}\,\chi^{s}\right],

(145)

which gives immediately

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{TE}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{1}\cos\theta_{i}-n_{2}\cos\theta_{t}+i(\omega/c)\,\chi^{s}}{n_{1}\cos\theta_{i}+n_{2}\cos\theta_{t}-i(\omega/c)\,\chi^{s}},

(146)

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{TE}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2n_{1}\cos\theta_{i}}{n_{1}\cos\theta_{i}+n_{2}\cos\theta_{t}-i(\omega/c)\,\chi^{s}}.

(147)

For
\chi^{s}=0
one recovers the standard Fresnel
coefficients. At normal incidence (
\cos\theta_{i}=\cos\theta_{t}=1
), the formula reduces to

r_{\mathrm{TE}}\big|_{\theta=0}=\frac{n_{1}-n_{2}+i(\omega/c)\chi^{s}}{n_{1}+n_{2}-i(\omega/c)\chi^{s}},

(148)

which is the relevant case for the numerical
examples of Section
5
.

Appendix E
Energy balance and the role of spatial non-locality

We examine the energy balance at a planar interface in the presence
of a surface susceptibility
\chi^{s}
, and address the question of
whether spatial non-locality can give rise to dissipation in analogy
with temporal non-locality.

E.1
Temporal vs. spatial non-locality: a structural
comparison

In the temporal case, the constitutive relation reads

P(t)=\varepsilon_{0}\int_{-\infty}^{t}\chi(t-t^{\prime})\,E(t^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime},

(149)

where causality imposes
\chi(\tau)=0
for
\tau<0
. In Fourier
space this becomes
\hat{P}(\omega)=\varepsilon_{0}\hat{\chi}(\omega)\hat{E}(\omega)
, with
\hat{\chi}(\omega)
complex in general. The
Kramers–Kronig relations, which are a direct consequence of causality,
force
\mathrm{Im}(\hat{\chi}(\omega))\neq 0
whenever

\mathrm{Re}(\hat{\chi}(\omega))
is non-trivial: temporal dispersion
and absorption are structurally linked.

In the spatial case, the constitutive relation reads

P(z)=\varepsilon_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{\Delta}(z-z^{\prime})\,E(z^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime},

(150)

and there is no principle of spatial causality to constrain

\tilde{\Delta}
. If the kernel is real and even,

\tilde{\Delta}(-Z)=\tilde{\Delta}(Z)
, its Fourier transform

\widehat{\tilde{\Delta}}(k)
is real: spatial non-locality introduces
no dissipation at the level of the bulk constitutive relation. The
analogy with the temporal case therefore breaks down at a fundamental
level.

E.2
Energy balance at the interface

We now carry out the energy balance explicitly for the generalized
Fresnel problem of Appendix
D
. We introduce the
shorthand

\beta_{1}:=n_{1}\cos\theta_{i},\qquad\beta_{2}:=n_{2}\cos\theta_{t},\qquad\gamma:=\frac{\omega}{c}\,\chi^{s}\in\mathbb{R},

(151)

so that

r_{\mathrm{TE}}=\frac{(\beta_{1}-\beta_{2})+i\gamma}{(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})-i\gamma},\qquad t_{\mathrm{TE}}=\frac{2\beta_{1}}{(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})-i\gamma}.

(152)

Reflectance and transmittance.

The reflectance and transmittance are defined as

R:=|r_{\mathrm{TE}}|^{2},\qquad T:=\frac{\beta_{2}}{\beta_{1}}\,|t_{\mathrm{TE}}|^{2},

(153)

where the factor
\beta_{2}/\beta_{1}
accounts for the ratio of normal
energy fluxes (Poynting vectors) on both sides of the interface.
A direct computation gives:

R=\frac{(\beta_{1}-\beta_{2})^{2}+\gamma^{2}}{(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})^{2}+\gamma^{2}},\qquad T=\frac{4\beta_{1}\beta_{2}}{(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})^{2}+\gamma^{2}}.

(154)

Exact conservation.

R+T=\frac{(\beta_{1}-\beta_{2})^{2}+\gamma^{2}+4\beta_{1}\beta_{2}}{(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})^{2}+\gamma^{2}}=\frac{(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})^{2}+\gamma^{2}}{(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})^{2}+\gamma^{2}}=1.

(155)

Energy is
exactly
conserved for any real value of
\chi^{s}
,
regardless of the angle of incidence or the indices. The surface
susceptibility, however large, does not introduce any absorption.

E.3
Physical interpretation: reactive vs. dissipative
response

The appearance of
i\gamma
in the numerator and denominator of

r_{\mathrm{TE}}
might suggest dissipation, since it shifts the
pole structure of the Fresnel coefficient. The energy balance above
shows that this is not the case: the surface term acts as a purely

reactive
element, analogous to a lossless capacitor or
inductor in circuit theory.

This can be understood directly from the boundary
condition (
109
):

\mathbf{n}\times\left[\!\left[\mathbf{H}\right]\!\right]=-i\omega\varepsilon_{0}\,\chi^{s}\,\mathbf{E}^{-}_{\parallel}.

(156)

For real
\chi^{s}
and harmonic fields (
e^{-i\omega t}
), the surface
current
\mathbf{J}^{s}:=\mathbf{n}\times\left[\!\left[\mathbf{H}\right]\!\right]
is in quadrature with
the field
\mathbf{E}^{-}_{\parallel}
: the time-averaged power dissipated in
the surface,

\mathcal{P}^{s}=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Re}\bigl(\mathbf{J}^{s}\cdot\mathbf{E}^{-*}_{\parallel}\bigr)=\frac{\omega\varepsilon_{0}\chi^{s}}{2}\,\mathrm{Re}\bigl(i|\mathbf{E}^{-}_{\parallel}|^{2}\bigr)=0,

(157)

is identically zero. The surface term redistributes energy between
the reflected and transmitted waves without absorbing any.

E.4
Effect on the phase of the reflection coefficient

Although
|r_{\mathrm{TE}}|^{2}
is modified at order
\gamma^{2}
only
(since the first-order term in
\gamma
is purely imaginary), the

phase
of
r_{\mathrm{TE}}
is sensitive to
\gamma
at first
order. Writing
r_{\mathrm{TE}}=|r_{\mathrm{TE}}|\,e^{i\phi}
:

\phi=\arg\!\bigl[(\beta_{1}-\beta_{2})+i\gamma\bigr]-\arg\!\bigl[(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})-i\gamma\bigr]=\arctan\!\frac{\gamma}{\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}}+\arctan\!\frac{\gamma}{\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}},

(158)

and for
\gamma\ll\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
:

\phi\approx\phi_{0}+\gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}}+\frac{1}{\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}}\right)+O(\gamma^{2})=\phi_{0}+\frac{2\beta_{1}\gamma}{\beta_{1}^{2}-\beta_{2}^{2}}+O(\gamma^{2}),

(159)

where
\phi_{0}=\arg(r_{0})\in\{0,\pi\}
for real indices.
The phase shift is linear in
\gamma\propto\chi^{s}\ell/\lambda

and is the primary observable signature of the surface
susceptibility in the transparent case. This is why ellipsometry,
which measures the ratio
r_{p}/r_{s}
and is directly sensitive to
phase differences, is the technique of choice for detecting
non-local surface effects at transparent interfaces

[
14
]
.

E.5
When does spatial non-locality produce dissipation?

The above analysis assumed
\chi^{s}\in\mathbb{R}
. We now identify the
physical situations in which
\chi^{s}
becomes complex and genuine
dissipation arises.

(i) Frequency-dependent kernel.

At finite frequency
\omega
, the bulk kernel
\tilde{\Delta}
depends
on
\omega
through the electronic response of the medium.
Integrating out the electronic degrees of freedom generically yields
a complex
\tilde{\Delta}(\cdot,\omega)
, and hence a complex

\chi^{s}(\omega)
. In this case,

\mathrm{Im}(\chi^{s}(\omega))\neq 0,

(160)

and the Kramers–Kronig relations now apply to
\chi^{s}(\omega)
,
linking spatial dispersion and surface absorption. This is precisely
the mechanism responsible for Landau damping at metal surfaces
[
24
]
.

(ii) Non-centrosymmetric kernel.

If the bulk kernel is real but not even,

\tilde{\Delta}(-Z)\neq\tilde{\Delta}(Z)
, its Fourier transform
acquires a non-zero imaginary part:

\widehat{\tilde{\Delta}}(k)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{\Delta}(Z)\,e^{ikZ}\,\mathrm{d}Z=\widehat{\tilde{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{even}}(k)+i\,\widehat{\tilde{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{odd}}(k).

(161)

The surface susceptibility
\chi^{s}
then inherits an imaginary part
from the odd component of the kernel. The energy balance becomes:

R+T=1-\mathcal{A},\qquad\mathcal{A}=\frac{4\beta_{1}\,(\omega/c)\,\mathrm{Im}(\chi^{s})}{(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})^{2}+|(\omega/c)\chi^{s}|^{2}}\geq 0,

(162)

where
\mathcal{A}\geq 0
is the fraction of incident energy
absorbed in the surface layer. The inequality
\mathcal{A}\geq 0

requires
\mathrm{Im}(\chi^{s})\geq 0
, which is the surface analogue
of the bulk condition
\mathrm{Im}(\hat{\chi}(\omega))\geq 0

(passivity of the medium).

(iii) Propagating surface modes.

Even for a real
\chi^{s}
, the modified Fresnel denominator

(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})-i\gamma
can have a zero for complex angles of
incidence, corresponding to a surface polariton whose radiation
damping is modified by the non-local surface term. This is not
dissipation in the thermodynamic sense, but a redistribution of
energy into a guided surface mode.

Remark 22

(Summary)
.

Spatial non-locality, for a real and even kernel, is a purely
reactive phenomenon: it shifts the phase of the reflected wave and
modifies the balance between
R
and
T
only at order

(\ell/\lambda)^{2}
, but conserves energy exactly. Dissipation
arises only when
\mathrm{Im}(\chi^{s})\neq 0
, which requires
either a frequency-dependent (absorbing) bulk kernel or a
non-centrosymmetric kernel. In both cases the condition

\mathrm{Im}(\chi^{s})\geq 0
is the surface expression of the
passivity of the medium, in direct analogy with the bulk condition

\mathrm{Im}(\hat{\varepsilon}(\omega))\geq 0
.

Thin-layer limit

Effective boundary conditions

Applying the transfer matrix

\displaystyle E_{y}^{+}

F.5
Physical content of the limit

The thin-layer limit
L\to 0

\chi_{\mathrm{lay}}L=\chi^{s}

fixed has a precise physical meaning. Three remarks are in order.

Consistency with the moment expansion.

In the nonlocal framework,
\chi^{s}
is given by the first moment
of the kernel (equation (

\chi^{s}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}Z\,\tilde{\Delta}(Z)\,\mathrm{d}Z.

(170)

For the exponential kernel
\tilde{\Delta}(Z)=(A/\ell)\,e^{-Z/\ell}
,
this gives
\chi^{s}=A\ell
. The thin-layer model reproduces
this result with the identification

\chi_{\mathrm{lay}}L=A\ell,

(171)

provided the layer thickness
L
is identified with the nonlocality
range
\ell
and the layer susceptibility with the kernel amplitude

\chi_{\mathrm{lay}}=A
.

Energy conservation.

Since the matrix
\mathbf{I}(\chi^{s})
is unimodular
(
\det\mathbf{I}=1
) for real
\chi^{s}
, the thin-layer limit
preserves the energy flux:
R+T=1
exactly, consistently
with Appendix
E
.

Breakdown of the limit.

The expansion (

k_{\mathrm{lay}}L\ll 1
,
i.e.,
L\ll\lambda/n_{\mathrm{lay}}
. For a metallic layer with

n_{\mathrm{lay}}\sim 10
at optical frequencies, this restricts the
validity of the thin-layer approximation to
L\lesssim 5
nm,
precisely the range in which the surface susceptibility description
is expected to be adequate, and beyond which higher-order terms in
the moment expansion (proportional to
\delta^{\prime}_{\partial\Omega}
,

\delta^{\prime\prime}_{\partial\Omega}
, etc.) become necessary.

Remark 23

(Connection with the Drude–Smith model)
.

For a Drude metal layer

, the layer susceptibility at

\chi_{\mathrm{lay}}(\omega)\approx-\omega_{p}^{2}/\omega^{2}
. The thin-layer limit then gives

\chi^{s}(\omega)=-\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}L}{\omega^{2}},
