Acceleration of multi-component multiple-precision arithmetic with branch-free algorithms and SIMD vectorization
Tomonori Kouya

Abstract
Multiple-precision floating-point branch-free algorithms can significantly accelerate multi-component arithmetic implemented by combining hardware-based binary64 and binary32, particularly for triple- and quadruple-precision computations. In this study, we achieved benchmark results on x86 and ARM CPU platforms to quantify the accelerations achieved in linear computations and polynomial evaluation by integrating these algorithms.



1
Introduction

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), underpinned by deep learning technologies, have led to the widespread adoption of large language models and generative AI systems, permeating diverse industrial sectors and everyday human activities, including software development and coding. This trend has accelerated the shortening of mantissa length of floating-point formats employed in AI computation in pursuit of higher performance. However, in scientific and engineering computing, double-precision arithmetic represents the minimum standard, and ill-conditioned problems frequently demand even higher precision. In such cases, the effective exploitation of single instruction multiple data (SIMD) instructions and OpenMP-based parallelization techniques is crucial for harnessing the full capabilities of modern hardware. Among existing multiple-precision arithmetic frameworks, arbitrary-precision arithmetic is dominated by MPFR
[
15
]
, which relies on the multiple-precision natural number arithmetic (MPN) kernel of GMP
[
5
]
, and MPC
[
3
]
—an arbitrary-precision complex arithmetic library built on MPFR that achieves acceleration through the Karatsuba 3M multiplication algorithm—is widely used.

By contrast, fixed-precision multi-component arithmetic based on error-free transformation (EFT) techniques, which employs combinations of multiple IEEE binary32 (24-bit mantissa and 32-bit total length) and binary64 values supported by existing hardware, is represented by quadruple-double (QD)
[
1
]
for CPUs and GQD
[
12
]
—a CUDA port of QD, though dated—for GPUs. When a relatively short mantissa length suffices, such as in double-double (DD, 106-bit), triple-double (TD, 159-bit), and quadruple-double (QD, 212-bit) arithmetic implemented by combining two to four binary64 values, computations can be executed faster than with MPFR. It has further been demonstrated by Kotakemori et al.’s Lis
[
7
]
and Hishinuma et al.
[
6
]
that basic linear computations can be accelerated by leveraging AVX, AVX2, and AVX-512, which are x86-based SIMD instruction sets. Building on an existing AVX2 implementation, we have demonstrated that basic linear computations in DD, TD, and QD precision can be accelerated on Raspberry Pi and Snapdragon platforms by exploiting Neon instructions
[
18
]
.

Nevertheless, DD, TD, and QD arithmetic invariably require a renormalization process at the conclusion of each operation. Particularly, for TD and QD arithmetic, this step has been identified as a bottleneck impeding performance gains through SIMD vectorization. Double-precision pair arithmetic that bypasses renormalization has been proposed as a faithfully rounded pair arithmetic scheme
[
11
]
, and practical sparse matrix solver implementations that exploit its efficiency have been reported
[
14
]
. The branch-free algorithms for multiple-precision floating-point arithmetic addressed in this work
[
17
]
eliminate all conditional branches from every algorithmic step, including renormalization, and additionally reduce the operation count for TD and QD arithmetic relative to conventional algorithms. Consequently, this methodology can substantially accelerate triple- and quadruple-precision multi-component arithmetic implemented by combining hardware-based binary64 and binary32 values, and is immediately applicable to existing fixed-precision multiple-precision arithmetic implementations. As the benchmarks presented in the original paper provided insufficient comparison against existing SIMD implementations, we first quantified the speedups achievable in basic linear computations by incorporating these algorithms on x86 and ARM CPU platforms. Furthermore, as a practical application, we analyzed the effectiveness of the proposed approach when applied to a multiple-precision algebraic equation solver.

As these EFT operations are constructed entirely from existing floating-point operations and contain no conditional branches, they can be directly implemented using SIMD instructions. With AVX2, which employs 256-bit registers on x86 architectures, four binary64 or eight binary32 values can be processed simultaneously by EFT operations; with Neon on AArch64, which employs 128-bit registers, two binary64 or four binary32 values can be processed simultaneously. Computations that contain no conditional branches and can be executed without overflowing SIMD registers in this manner are termed branch-free (BF).

By combining EFT operations, multiple-precision arithmetic with an extended mantissa can be implemented by composing multiple floating-point numbers; this approach is referred to as the multi-component (or multiterm) method. Because subtraction reduces to sign negation and division can be implemented via Newton’s method combining addition and multiplication, these two operations are crucial from a performance perspective. The correct handling of rounding errors is critical in this implementation and requires a carefully derived, rigorous error analysis. As the mantissa length increases, the operation count grows, and error management becomes increasingly complex. Zhang & Aiken proposed an abstract model for automated reasoning that simplifies floating-point arithmetic
[
16
]
and introduced branch-free multi-component multiple-precision addition and multiplication algorithms. In the following, we present and discuss these BF algorithms.

2.3
Remarks on QW Arithmetic

Quadruple-precision arithmetic (Quadruple-Word, QW) is based on the implementation provided in QD; as noted previously, TD arithmetic is an abbreviated form of QD arithmetic with the fourth component zeroed. Both QD addition and QD multiplication conclude with a call to the QW renormalization function (QWRenormalize), which contains conditional branches and requires up to seven QuickTwoSum function calls.

In contrast, the BF algorithms are presented by Algorithms
13
and
14

Consequently, the benefit of SIMD vectorization is expected to be even more pronounced for QW arithmetic than for TW arithmetic.

Benchmark Tests for Complex Matrix Multiplication

Owing to the superiority of BF arithmetic for TD and QD computations demonstrated previously, the complex matrix multiplication benchmarks employed BF-based implementations for comparison. The complex square matrix multiplication is defined using a uniform random variable
r_{u}\in[0,1]
and a standard normal random variable
r_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
as

\begin{split}C&:=AB,\\
A&=[a_{ij}]_{i,j=1,2,...,n},B=[b_{ij}]_{i,j=1,2,...,n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\\
a_{ij}&:=\exp(r_{n})(r_{u}-1/2)+\exp(r_{n})(r_{u}-1/2)\sqrt{-1}\\
b_{ij}&:=\exp(r_{n})(r_{u}-1/2)+\exp(r_{n})(r_{u}-1/2)\sqrt{-1}\end{split}

(2)

Our complex matrix multiplication is implemented using three real matrix multiplications via the 3M method, a variant of the Karatsuba algorithm
[
10
]
. Therefore, both the Strassen and block-based algorithms perform three real matrix multiplications. The numerical accuracy, measured as the minimum of the real and imaginary parts, falls within the ranges of 23.2–24.4 significant decimal digits for DD, 39.2–40.8 for TD, and 55.8–56.5 for QD precision.

4
Benchmark Tests for Polynomial Evaluation and Algebraic Equation Solvers

As demonstrated in the matrix multiplication benchmarks, BF arithmetic offers no speedup for DD precision when the conventional sloppy addition and multiplication are employed. In this section, we present benchmark results for polynomial evaluation and an algebraic equation solver based on the Durand–Kerner (DK) method, both of which utilize TD and QD precision arithmetic, for which performance improvements are anticipated.

4.2
Algebraic Equation Solver Based on the DK Method

The method under comparison is the quadratically convergent DK method, a classical simultaneous iterative solver. Denoting the approximate roots after
k
iterations as

\mathbf{z}_{k}=[z^{(k)}_{1}\ z^{(k)}_{2}\ ...\ z^{(k)}_{n}]^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{n},

the recurrence relation, expressed using the monic polynomial
q_{n}(x)=p_{n}(x)/a_{n}
, is given by

z^{(k+1)}_{i}:=z^{(k)}_{i}-\frac{q_{n}(z^{(k)}_{i})}{\prod^{n}_{j=1,j\not=i}(z^{(k)}_{i}-z^{(k)}_{j})}

(3)

as the quadratically convergent DK method.

The Aberth initial values are expressed by

z^{(0)}_{i}:=-\frac{c_{n-1}}{n}+r\exp\left\{\left(\frac{2(i-1)\pi}{n}+\frac{3}{2n}\right)\mathrm{i}\right\}

(4)

In the experiments, the radius
r
was defined using the number of nonzero coefficients
n_{\rm nz}\leq n
as

r:=\max_{0\leq i\leq(n-1)}|n_{\rm nz}\,c_{i}|^{1/(n-i)}.

The test problem was the Chebyshev integration problem, in which the polynomial coefficients, with
a_{n}=1

\begin{cases}a_{n-(2k-1)}:=0&\\
a_{n-2k}:=-\sum^{k}_{2j+1}a_{n-2(k-j)}&\\
\end{cases}

(5)

where
k=1,2,\ldots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor
.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

In this study, we applied BF algorithms, which suppress conditional branching, to multi-component multiple-precision arithmetic (DD/TD/QD) and evaluated their performance on CPU SIMD (AVX2 on EPYC) and Arm Neon. Benchmark tests were conducted for real and complex square matrix multiplication, real- and complex-valued evaluation of real-coefficient polynomials, and the Chebyshev integration problem. The results confirm that, for high precision (particularly TD and QD), the combined use of BF conversion and SIMD vectorization is effective and reduces computation time compared with the standard implementation (NoBF). In addition, the results validate that BF conversion offers no benefit for DD precision arithmetic. These findings indicate that achieving performance improvements in multi-component arithmetic requires (i) branch elimination (BF conversion) and (ii) exploitation of instruction-level parallelism (SIMD vectorization) to be combined, with the implementation strategy selected according to the target problem, precision level, and architecture.

The following directions are identified for future work.

1.

Adaptive implementation selection (autotuning)
:
As the optimal execution path (NoBF/BF, SIMD width, and loop partitioning) varies with the precision level (DD/TD/QD) and problem size, we will introduce a mechanism for selecting the optimal implementation either at runtime or at build time.

2.

Support for wider SIMD extensions and instruction sets
:
Extensions to AVX-512 and Arm SVE/SVE2 will be pursued, along with establishing design guidelines for data layout and reduction operations as register width increases.

3.

Memory access optimization and enhanced parallelization
:
In addition to improving cache efficiency in matrix operations and polynomial evaluation, we will optimize the coordination of thread-level parallelism via OpenMP with SIMD vectorization, including NUMA placement and scheduling.

4.

Extension to application problems
:
The scope of application will be expanded to include high-precision linear algebra problems (QR decomposition, eigenvalue problems, and iterative methods), special function evaluation, numerical integration, and optimization, and the effectiveness in practical settings (convergence behavior, total runtime, and energy efficiency) will be evaluated.

Through these efforts, we aim to realize the accelerated multi-component high-precision arithmetic in a more general and portable form, establishing a high-precision numerical computing infrastructure applicable to a wide range of computational environments from mobile devices to supercomputers.
