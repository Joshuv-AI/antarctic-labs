Handicap reduction for linear complementarity problems
Marianna E.-Nagy, László A. Végh

Abstract
Linear Complementarity Problems (LCPs) with sufficient matrices form an important subclass of LCPs, and it remains a significant open question whether problems in this class can be solved in polynomial time. Kojima, Megiddo, Noma, and Yoshise gave an Interior Point Algorithm (IPA) in 1991, that can solve LCPs with sufficient matrices in time bounded polynomially in the input size and the so-called handicap number κ ^ ​ ( 𝐌 ) \hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M}) of the coefficient matrix 𝐌 \mathbf{M} . However, this value can be exponentially large in the bit encoding length. In fact, no upper bounds were previously known on κ ^ ​ ( 𝐌 ) \hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M}) . Settling an open question raised in de Klerk and E.-Nagy (Math Programming, 2011), we give an exponential upper bound on κ ^ ​ ( 𝐌 ) \hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M}) in the bit-complexity of 𝐌 \mathbf{M} . This is based on a new characterization of sufficient matrices. The new characterization also leads to a simple new proof of Väliaho’s theorem on the equivalence of sufficient and 𝒫 ∗ \mathcal{P}^{*} -matrices (Linear Algebra and its Applications, 1996). Noting that one can obtain an equivalent LCP by rescaling the rows and columns by a positive diagonal matrix, we define κ ^ ⋆ ​ ( 𝐌 ) \hat{\kappa}^{\star}(\mathbf{M}) as the best possible handicap number achievable under such rescalings. Our second main result is an algorithm for LCPs with sufficient matrices, where the running time is polynomially bounded in the input size and in the optimized value κ ^ ⋆ ​ ( 𝐌 ) \hat{\kappa}^{\star}(\mathbf{M}) . This algorithm is based on the observation that the set of near-optimal row-rescalings forms a convex set. Our algorithm combines the Ellipsoid Method over the set of row rescalings, and an IPA with running time dependent on the handicap number of the matrix. If the IPA fails to solve the LCP in the desired running time, it provides a separation oracle to the Ellipsoid Method to find a better rescaling.

Introduction

Consider a Linear Complementarity Problem (LCP) for a matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the form

-\mathbf{M}x+s=q\,,\quad{x}^{\top}{s}=0\,,\quad x,s\geq\mathbf{0}\,.

(LCP
(\mathbf{M},q)
)

The general problem encompasses several well-known special cases. We can formulate Linear Programming (LP) in a primal-dual embedding with a skew-symmetric matrix
\mathbf{M}
, and convex quadratic programs with a positive semidefinite matrix. LCPs also capture problems such as Nash equilibria in two-player bimatrix games, stopping problems, market equilibrium problems, and mean payoff games. We refer the reader to the classical monograph by Cottle, Pang, and Stone
[
2
]
on the theory and applications of LCP.

The general problem, however, is strongly NP-complete
[
1
]
. The exact boundary between polynomially solvable and hard cases of LCP remains a fundamental question.
Kojima, Megiddo, Noma, and Yoshise
[
11
]
gave an important general result in this context, by showing that LCPs where
\mathbf{M}
has a bounded
handicap number
are solvable in polynomial time.

The handicap number and
\mathcal{P}_{*}
-matrices

The handicap number
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})
can be seen as a relaxation of positive semidefiniteness. Recall that
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is positive semidefinite (PSD) if and only if
x^{\top}\mathbf{M}x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}(\mathbf{M}x)_{i}\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Note that we do not assume the symmetry of the matrix
\mathbf{M}
.
For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let us consider the following partition of the index set according to the sign of
x_{i}(\mathbf{M}x)_{i}=x_{i}\mathbf{M}_{i}x
, where
\mathbf{M}_{i}
denotes the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{M}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{M}}^{+}(x)\coloneqq\{i\,|\,x_{i}(\mathbf{M}x)_{i}>0\}\,,\quad\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{M}}^{0}(x)\coloneqq\{i\,|\,x_{i}(\mathbf{M}x)_{i}=0\}\,,\quad\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{M}}^{-}(x)\coloneqq\{i\,|\,x_{i}(\mathbf{M}x)_{i}<0\}\,.

The
handicap number
of the matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is defined as

\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})\coloneqq\min\left\{\kappa\geq 0\;|\;x^{\top}\mathbf{M}x+4\kappa\!\!\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{M}}^{+}(x)}\!x_{i}(\mathbf{M}x)_{i}\geq 0\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}\,.

Thus,
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})=0
if and only if
\mathbf{M}
is PSD.
We let
\mathcal{P}_{*}(\kappa)
denote the set of
n\times n
matrices with
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})\leq\kappa
, and
\mathcal{P}_{*}\coloneqq\cup_{\kappa\geq 0}\mathcal{P}_{*}(\kappa)
. A matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{P}_{*}
is called a
\mathcal{P}_{*}
-matrix
. Kojima et al.
[
11
]
gave a
unified interior point method
that can solve
\mathrm{LCP}(\mathbf{M},q)
in
O((1+\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M}))\sqrt{n}L)
iterations, where
L
is the total encoding length of the rational input
(\mathbf{M},q)
.

Nevertheless,
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})
may not be polynomially bounded in
L
, and therefore this does not yield a polynomial-time algorithm for
\mathcal{P}_{*}
-matrices. The first such example was given by de Klerk and E.-Nagy
[
4
]
; they showed that the following simple matrix with
0,\pm 1
entries, called the
Csizmadia matrix
, has exponential handicap numberÉ

\mathbf{C}_{n}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
-1&1&0&\cdots&0\\
-1&-1&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-1&-1&-1&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}\,.

(1)

This matrix has
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{C}_{n})=2^{2n}-\tfrac{1}{4}
(see
[
4
,
5
]
). The paper
[
4
]
raised as an open question to bound the handicap number of a rational matrix with encoding length
L
as
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})\leq 2^{\mathrm{poly}(L)}
. The first contribution of this paper is resolving this in the affirmative, see Theorem
1.2
below.

Optimizing the handicap number

Even if
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})
is large, a suitable preconditioning could turn the LCP into a tractable one.
Let
\mathcal{D}_{>0}^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denote the set of
n\times n
positive diagonal matrices.
Row and column rescalings of
\mathbf{M}
turn the LCP to an equivalent problem: for positive diagonal matrices
\mathbf{D}^{\prime},\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}_{>0}^{n}
,
(x,s)
is a solution to
LCP

(
M
,
q
)

if and only if
(\mathbf{D}^{-1}x,\mathbf{D}^{\prime}s)
is a solution to

\mathrm{LCP}(\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{D},\mathbf{D}^{\prime}q)
. This motivates the following definition:

Definition 1.1
.

For a matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, the
optimized handicap number

\hat{\kappa}^{\star}(\mathbf{M})
is defined as

\hat{\kappa}^{\star}(\mathbf{M})\coloneqq\inf\{\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M})\mid\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}_{>0}^{n}\}\,.

The definition only uses row rescalings, but row scalings can achieve the same handicap values as allowing both row- and column scalings, by noting that
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{D}^{\prime})=\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})
for
\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{>0}^{n}
(see Lemma
2.3
below); this is immediate from the definition of
\hat{\kappa}
.

For the matrix
\mathbf{C}_{n}
defined in (
1
), it turns out that
\hat{\kappa}^{\star}(C_{n})=0
. Let

\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1/2&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1/2^{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1/2^{n-1}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\mathbf{D}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&2&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&2^{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&2^{n-1}\end{pmatrix}.

These scalings yield

\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\mathbf{C}_{n}\mathbf{D}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
-1/2&1&0&\cdots&0\\
-1/2^{2}&-1/2&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-1/2^{n-1}&-1/2^{n-2}&-1/2^{n-3}&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}\,.

The rescaled matrix is diagonally dominant and therefore positive semidefinite. Consequently,
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\mathbf{C}_{n}\mathbf{D})=0
.
One can similarly show that
\hat{\kappa}^{\star}(\mathbf{M})=0
for every triangular matrix
\mathbf{M}
with positive diagonal entries.

The question of whether
\hat{\kappa}^{\star}(\mathbf{M})=0
is equivalent to deciding whether a matrix can be turned PSD by a suitable rescaling. This latter class of matrices is already appeared in
[
4
]
and was denoted by
\mathcal{H}
. De Klerk and E.-Nagy proved that the membership problem for set

can be formulated as a semidefinite feasibility problem (see Lemma 5 in

), therefore it can be solved by an interior point algorithm (IPA) in polynomial time with

Let us state here a simplified SDP feasibility problem (a linear matrix inequality) for the sake of completeness:

\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{H}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\exists\;y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{\geq 0}:\quad\mathbf{T}_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\mathbf{T}_{i}\succeq 0\,,

where
\mathbf{T}_{0}=\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{M}^{\top}
and
\mathbf{T}_{i}=\operatorname{diag}(e^{i})\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{M}^{\top}\operatorname{diag}(e^{i}),i=1\dots,n
.

1

1

\operatorname{diag}(d)\in\mathcal{D}_{>0}^{n}
denotes the diagonal matrix with entries
d
.
For any feasible

\mathbf{D}
with the diagonal elements
\mathbf{D}_{ii}=y_{i}+1
,
i=1,\dots,n
defines a suitable rescaling for matrix
\mathbf{M}
, that is,
DM

However, the complexity of computing
\hat{\kappa}^{\star}(\mathbf{M})
is not clear; even the complexity of computing of
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})
, or even deciding finiteness of
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})
is open, although these problems are likely hard. Despite this, our second contribution, Theorem

1.1
Our contributions

Our first result resolves an open question in
[
4
]
. The binary encoding length is formally defined in Section
2
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For a rational matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{P}_{*}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}
with
L=\operatorname{length}(\mathbf{M})
,
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})\leq 2^{O(nL)}
.

Showing a
2^{\mathrm{poly}(n)L}
bound is easy for many matrix condition numbers. Such bounds are known for condition numbers such as
the maximum ratio between singular values, maximum subdeterminants, the Dikin–Stewart–Todd measure
\bar{\chi}(\mathbf{M})

[
15
]
or the circuit imbalance measure
[
3
]
. These arguments ultimately reduce to relating the condition numbers to the entries of the inverse of nonsingular square submatrices of
\mathbf{M}
.
Analogous arguments suffice to show Theorem
1.2
for the special case of
\mathcal{P}
-matrices, i.e., matrices where all principal minors are positive. This argument is given as Proposition
3.2
and we now sketch the proof. We consider
\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
that is optimal to the program defining
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})
; we may normalize it as
\|\hat{x}\|_{1}=1
. Let us define a polytope
\mathcal{Q}
containing all points
y
where
y_{i}
and
(\mathbf{M}y)_{i}
have the same signs as
\hat{x}_{i}
and
(\mathbf{M}\hat{x})_{i}
respectively, for each index
i
, and
\|y\|_{1}=1
. We can write
\hat{x}
as a convex combination of at most
n+1
extreme points of
\mathcal{Q}
; let
v^{(1)}
be the point with the largest coefficient. The
\mathcal{P}
-matrix property guarantees that
v^{(1)}_{i}(\mathbf{M}v^{(1)})_{i}>0
for at least one index (see Lemma
2.1
). This positive entry will be
2^{-O(nL)}
due to standard bit-complexity arguments on basic solutions. This already suffices to derive the bound on

\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})
.

The proof becomes significantly more involved for matrices
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{P}_{*}\setminus\mathcal{P}
. For
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
u\circ v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the Hadamard product. We start the proof as above, but it is possible that

v^{(1)}\circ(\mathbf{M}v^{(1)})=\mathbf{0}_{n}
, i.e.,

v^{(1)}_{i}(\mathbf{M}v^{(1)})_{i}=0
for all
i
. In fact,
v^{(k)}\circ(\mathbf{M}v^{(k)})=\mathbf{0}_{n}
may happen for all extreme points
v^{(k)}
with large coefficients in the combination.
Theorem
1.2
follows by showing that there are two basic solutions
u=v^{(k)}
and
v=v^{(\ell)}
, and
\lambda>0
, such that the handicap value corresponding to
u+\lambda v
is within a factor
n^{2}
from the optimum value in the problem defining
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})
(see Lemma
3.3
). However, these critical basic solutions
u=v^{(k)}
and
v=v^{(\ell)}
may have arbitrarily low coefficients in the combination giving
\hat{x}
, necessitating a more careful argument.

The same Lemma
3.3
also leads to a new equivalent characterization of
\mathcal{P}_{*}
-matrices, and in turn to a simple new proof of Väliaho’s classical result asserting that the class
\mathcal{P}_{*}
is the same as the class of sufficient matrices. Let us now define this matrix class.

Definition 1.3
.

A matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is
column
sufficient if
x\circ(\mathbf{M}x)\leq\mathbf{0}_{n}
implies
x\circ(\mathbf{M}x)=\mathbf{0}_{n}
, and
row sufficient
if
\mathbf{M}^{\top}
is column sufficient. A matrix is
sufficient
if it is both row and column sufficient.

In the equivalence of sufficient and
\mathcal{P}_{*}
-matrices, the only trivial direction is that every
\mathcal{P}_{*}
-matrix is column sufficient. The row sufficiency of
\mathcal{P}_{*}
-matrices was shown by Guu and Cottle in
[
8
]
, and the converse direction that all sufficient matrices are
\mathcal{P}_{*}
was shown by
Väliaho in
[
13
]
. We give a simple direct proof of the first direction (Lemma
4.1
). The new proof of the converse direction is via a third, equivalent characterization as stated next.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Then, the following are equivalent:

(A)

\mathbf{M}
is
\mathcal{P}_{*}
.

(B)

\mathbf{M}
is sufficient.

(C)

For any vectors
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
v\circ(\mathbf{M}v)\leq\mathbf{0}_{n}
,
u\circ(\mathbf{M}v)+v\circ(\mathbf{M}u)\leq\mathbf{0}_{n}
, we must have
v\circ(\mathbf{M}v)+u\circ(\mathbf{M}v)+v\circ(\mathbf{M}u)=\mathbf{0}_{n}
.

To get an intuition of part (
C
), take vectors
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
v\circ(\mathbf{M}v)\leq\mathbf{0}_{n}
,
u\circ(\mathbf{M}v)+v\circ(\mathbf{M}u)\leq\mathbf{0}_{n}
.
If
v\circ(\mathbf{M}v)\lneq\mathbf{0}_{n}
, then
v
is a certificate that
\mathbf{M}
is not column sufficient. Assume
v\circ(\mathbf{M}v)=\mathbf{0}_{n}
and
u\circ(\mathbf{M}v)+v\circ(\mathbf{M}u)\lneq\mathbf{0}_{n}
; let
\gamma\coloneqq-(u^{\top}\mathbf{M}v+v^{\top}\mathbf{M}u)>0
. Then, for
z\coloneqq v+\varepsilon u
, we have
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{M}}^{+}(z)}z_{i}(\mathbf{M}z)_{i}\leq\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{M}}^{+}(u)}u_{i}(\mathbf{M}u)_{i}
, while
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{M}}^{-}(z)}z_{i}(\mathbf{M}z)_{i}\leq\varepsilon(u^{\top}\mathbf{M}v+v^{\top}\mathbf{M}u)+\varepsilon^{2}u\circ\mathbf{M}u=\varepsilon(-\gamma+\varepsilon u\circ\mathbf{M}u)
. Thus,
|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{M}}^{-}(z)}z_{i}(\mathbf{M}z)_{i}|/\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{M}}^{+}(z)}z_{i}(\mathbf{M}z)_{i}\geq(\gamma-\varepsilon|u\circ\mathbf{M}u|)/(\varepsilon\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{M}}^{+}(u)}u_{i}(\mathbf{M}u)_{i})\to\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, showing that
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})=\infty
, that is,
\mathbf{M}
is not in
\mathcal{P}_{*}
. Our proof reveals that whenever a matrix is not in
\mathcal{P}_{*}
, then one can identify either a certificate that it is not column sufficient of the form
v\circ(\mathbf{M}v)\lneq\mathbf{0}_{n}
, or a direction witnessing the unboundedness of
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})
.

We now turn to the main algorithmic contribution of the paper.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}
and
q\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
. Then, there exists an
algorithm whose running time can be bounded polynomially in
\operatorname{length}(\mathbf{M},q)
and linearly in
\hat{\kappa}^{\star}(\mathbf{M})
, and returns one of the following outputs:

(i)

A feasible solution to
LCP

(
M
,
q
)

.

(ii)

A dual certificate of infeasibility.

(iii)

The conclusion that
\mathbf{M}\notin\mathcal{P}_{*}
.

As already noted, we do not have a preconditioning algorithm to find
\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}_{>0}^{n}
such that
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M})
is approximately the optimal value
\hat{\kappa}^{\star}(\mathbf{M})
. Instead, we use a combination of the ellipsoid method and an IPA subroutine to gradually find a suitable column scaling. We note that the algorithm does not need to know the value of
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M})
.

The crucial observation is that for any
\tau\geq 0
, the set of positive diagonal matrices
\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}_{>0}^{n}
such that
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M})\leq\tau
is convex (Lemma
5.2
). This follows by observing that for any
\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}_{>0}^{n}
,

\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}}^{-}(x)=\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{M}}^{-}(x)
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}}^{+}(x)=\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{M}}^{+}(x)
hold. Moreover, we show that—assuming there exists a rescaling with
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M})\leq\tau
—there exists a ball
\mathcal{B}^{*}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
of radius
r=1/4
, contained in a ball of radius
R=\tau^{2n^{2}}2^{O(n^{2}L)}
, such that for any
d\in\mathcal{B}^{*}
,
\hat{\kappa}(\operatorname{diag}(d)\mathbf{M})=\rho
for
\rho=O(n^{2}\hat{\kappa}^{\star}(\mathbf{M}))
(Theorem
5.4
and Lemma
5.3
). Thus, the ellipsoid method (see
[
7
]
) would be a natural approach to find a rescaling in
\mathcal{B}^{*}
, assuming we can find a separation oracle.

Algorithm
1
in Section
6

maintains a guess
\tau
on
\hat{\kappa}^{\star}(\mathbf{M})
, initialized as
\tau=1
. We maintain an ellipsoid centered at a point
d\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
, initialized as
d=\mathbf{1}_{n}
, and we consider the rescaled LCP instance
(\operatorname{diag}(d)\mathbf{M},\operatorname{diag}(d)q)
. We set the parameter
\rho=O(n^{2}\tau)
, and
use an IPA for
LCP

(
M
,
q
)

for the rescaled system. As noted by Illés, E.-Nagy, and Terlaky
[
10
]
, the unified IPA from
[
11
]
terminates with a solution to the LCP in
O((1+\rho)\sqrt{n}L)
steps as long as all step directions used in the algorithm are consistent with the hypothesis
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{P}_{*}(\rho)
. That is, either the IPA makes the desired progress in each step, or a vector
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is found that testifies
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M})>\rho
.

In the latter case, we terminate the IPA, and change our ellipsoid. We use the vector
z
to define a separating hyperplane between the current centre
d
and the set of rescalings
d^{\prime}
for which
\hat{\kappa}(\operatorname{diag}(d^{\prime})\mathbf{M})\leq\rho
. If the IPA does not succeed, then in a polynomial number of times the volume of the ellipsoid drops below the volume of a ball of radius
1/4
, at which point we conclude that there is no rescaling such that
\hat{\kappa}(\operatorname{diag}(d^{\prime})\mathbf{M})\leq\tau
. If this happens, we double our guess
\tau
and restart.

The algorithm described here is not practical as it in effect multiplies the time bounds of the ellipsoid and IPAs. Still, it conceptually improves the running time dependence from
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})
to
\hat{\kappa}^{\star}(\mathbf{M})
.
Our approach is loosely inspired by the result of Dadush, Huiberts, Natura, and Végh
[
3
]
that improves the running time of IPAs for exact LP from the logarithm of the circuit imbalance measure of the constraint matrix to the optimized version of this measure. However, in contrast to our approach,
[
3
]
is able to efficiently find a suitable rescaling as a preconditioning, as well as develop a scaling invariant IPA. In contrast, we resort to the less efficient approach via the ellipsoid method.

As already noted, the optimized handicap
\hat{\kappa}^{\star}(\mathbf{M})
can be arbitrarily better than
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})
. Our algorithm is polynomial whenever
\hat{\kappa}^{\star}(\mathbf{M})
is polynomially bounded. Our next example shows that this is not always the case.

Proposition 1.6
.

For any
\alpha\geq 3
, the matrix

\mathbf{M}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha&-1\\
-1&1&\alpha\\
\alpha&-1&1\end{pmatrix}

(2)

is sufficient, with
\hat{\kappa}^{\star}(\mathbf{M})\geq\frac{\alpha-3}{8}
.

row sufficient
if
\mathbf{M}^{\top}
is column sufficient. A matrix is
sufficient
if it is both row and column sufficient.
We will use the following lemma, see
[
2
, Theorem 3.3.4]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

A matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a
\mathcal{P}
-matrix if and only if
x\circ(\mathbf{M}x)\leq\mathbf{0}_{n}
implies
x=\mathbf{0}_{n}
.

Clearly,
\mathbf{M}
is a
\mathcal{P}
-matrix if and only if
\mathbf{M}^{\top}
is a
\mathcal{P}
-matrix. Hence, this lemma implies that all
\mathcal{P}
-matrices are sufficient.

For a particular vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the
handicap number of
\mathbf{M}
with respect to
x
is

\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\infty\,&\mbox{if }x^{\top}\mathbf{M}x<0\mbox{ and }\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{M}}^{+}(x)=\emptyset\,,\\
-\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{M}}^{-}(x)}x_{i}(\mathbf{M}x)_{i}}{4\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{M}}^{+}(x)}x_{i}(\mathbf{M}x)_{i}}-\frac{1}{4}\,,&\mbox{if }x^{\top}\mathbf{M}x<0\mbox{ and }\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{M}}^{+}(x)\neq\emptyset\,,\\
0\,,&\mbox{if }x^{\top}\mathbf{M}x\geq 0\,.\end{cases}

With this notation, we can equivalently write

\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},x)=\sup_{\|x\|\leq 1}\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},x)=\sup_{\|x\|=1}\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},x)\,.

In general, the supremum of
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},x)
is not attained at any
x
, but for special matrices an optimal point exists:

Lemma 2.2

(
[
14
, Theorem 2.3]
)
.

If
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, then there exists a vector
\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})=\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},\hat{x})
.

We also observe the following simple statement. Note that here we allow
\mathbf{D}
to be any nonsingular (not necessarily positive) diagonal matrix.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}^{n}
, and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then,
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},x)=\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{D},\mathbf{D}^{-1}x)
. Consequently,
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})=\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{D})
.

Encoding length of rational numbers.

For an integer
p\in\mathbb{Z}
,
p\neq 0
, we let
\operatorname{length}(p)=1+\lceil\log_{2}|p|\rceil
denote its binary encoding length. For a rational number represented as
p/q
, we let
\operatorname{length}(p/q)=\operatorname{length}(p)+\operatorname{length}(q)
. For a vector
v
and matrix
\mathbf{M}
, we define the encoding length
\operatorname{length}(v)
and
\operatorname{length}(\mathbf{M})
as the sum of the encoding length of all entries. We use the following simple bounds, see
[
7
, Section 1.3]

Lemma 2.4
.

(a)

For
a,b\in\mathbb{Q}
,
\operatorname{length}(a+b)\leq 2\operatorname{length}(a)+\operatorname{length}(b)
and

\operatorname{length}(ab)\leq\operatorname{length}(a)+\operatorname{length}(b)
.

(b)

For vectors
u,v\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
,
\operatorname{length}(\left\langle u,v\right\rangle)\leq\operatorname{length}(u)+\operatorname{length}(v)
.

(c)

For a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}
,
\operatorname{length}(\det(\mathbf{A}))\leq 2\operatorname{length}(\mathbf{A})
.

The principal pivotal transformation

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\mathcal{J}\subseteq[n]
be an index set such that
\mathbf{A}_{\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}}
is a nonsingular principal submatrix of
\mathbf{A}
.
Let
\bar{\mathcal{J}}=[n]\setminus\mathcal{J}
denote the complement of
\mathcal{J}
. The
principal pivotal transformation
of matrix
\mathbf{A}
for
\mathbf{A}_{\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}}
is the matrix

\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{J}}(\mathbf{A})\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}}^{-1}&-\mathbf{A}_{\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\,\mathbf{A}_{\mathcal{J}\bar{\mathcal{J}}}\\
\mathbf{A}_{\bar{\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{J}}\,\mathbf{A}_{\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}}^{-1}&\;\;\mathbf{A}_{\bar{\mathcal{J}}\bar{\mathcal{J}}}-\mathbf{A}_{\bar{\mathcal{J}}\bar{\mathcal{J}}}\,\mathbf{A}_{\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\,\mathbf{A}_{\mathcal{J}\bar{\mathcal{J}}}\end{bmatrix}.

For a singleton
\mathcal{J}=\{j\}
, we use
\mathcal{T}_{j}
to denote
\mathcal{T}_{\{j\}}
.
The main significance of this operation is that if
y=\mathbf{A}x
for the vectors
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
(x_{\mathcal{J}},y_{\bar{\mathcal{J}}})=\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{J}}(\mathbf{A})(y_{\mathcal{J}},x_{\bar{\mathcal{J}}})
. That is, it corresponds to exchanging variables
(\mathbf{A}x)_{\mathcal{J}}
and
x_{\mathcal{J}}
;
see
[
2
, Section 2.3]
.
It is also easy to see that principal pivoting defines an equivalence relation of
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
: if
\mathbf{B}=\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{J}}(\mathbf{A})
and
\mathbf{C}=\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}}(\mathbf{B})
, then
\mathbf{C}=\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{J}\Delta\mathcal{K}}(\mathbf{A})
.
Noting that any principal pivot on a matrix
\mathbf{M}
leaves the vector
x\circ(\mathbf{M}x)
unchanged, we immediately obtain the following.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
8
]
)
.

The matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is sufficient if and only if every principal pivotal transform of
\mathbf{M}
is sufficient.

Sign patterns of sufficient matrices

We state some well-known properties of sufficient matrices; for completeness, we include their proofs.

Lemma 2.6

(
[
14
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

(i)

If
\mathbf{M}
is either row sufficient or column sufficient, then
\mathbf{M}_{ii}\geq 0
for all
i\in[n]
.

(ii)

Assume
\mathbf{M}
is column sufficient.
Let
i\in[n]
such that
\mathbf{M}_{ii}=0
. Then,
\mathbf{M}_{ij}\mathbf{M}_{ji}\leq 0
for all
j\in[n]
, and if
\mathbf{M}_{ji}\neq 0
then
\mathbf{M}_{ij}\neq 0
.

(iii)

Assume
\mathbf{M}
is row sufficient.
Let
i\in[n]
such that
\mathbf{M}_{ii}=0
. Then,
\mathbf{M}_{ij}\mathbf{M}_{ji}\leq 0
for all
j\in[n]
, and if
\mathbf{M}_{ij}\neq 0
then
\mathbf{M}_{ji}\neq 0
.

Proof.

(a)
If
\mathbf{M}_{ii}<0
, then
(e^{i})\circ(\mathbf{M}e^{i})\lneq\mathbf{0}_{n}
and
(e^{i})\circ(\mathbf{M}^{\top}e^{i})\lneq\mathbf{0}_{n}
, showing that the matrix is neither row nor column sufficient.

(b)
For a contradiction, assume
\mathbf{M}_{ii}=0
, and

\mathbf{M}_{ij}\mathbf{M}_{ji}>0
. For some
\varepsilon>0
,
consider
x=e^{i}-\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{sgn}(\mathbf{M}_{ij})e^{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus,
x_{i}(\mathbf{M}x)_{i}=-|\mathbf{M}_{ij}|\varepsilon
and
x_{j}(\mathbf{M}x)_{j}=-|\mathbf{M}_{ji}|\varepsilon+\mathbf{M}_{jj}\varepsilon^{2}
. We have
x_{k}(\mathbf{M}x)_{k}=0
for
k\notin\{i,j\}
.
For sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
,
x\circ(\mathbf{M}x)\lneq\mathbf{0}_{n}
holds. This proves
\mathbf{M}_{ij}\mathbf{M}_{ji}\leq 0
. If
\mathbf{M}_{ji}\neq 0
but
\mathbf{M}_{ij}=0
, we get a contradiction using the same
x
as above. Part
(c)
follows by applying part
(b)
for
\mathbf{M}^{\top}
.
∎

3
Bounding the handicap number

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.2
. The key is to
analyze combinations of two basic solutions to the following polytope.
For a matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, and two disjoint index sets
\mathcal{I}^{+},\mathcal{I}^{-}\subseteq[n]
,
\mathcal{I}^{+}\cap\mathcal{I}^{-}=\emptyset
, we define the polytope

\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{M},\mathcal{I}^{+},\mathcal{I}^{-}):=\left\{x\in\sigma_{n}\,|\,\mathbf{M}_{i}x\geq 0\;\,\forall i\in\mathcal{I}^{+},\;\;\mathbf{M}_{i}x\leq 0\;\,\forall i\in\mathcal{I}^{-},\;\;\mathbf{M}_{i}x=0\;\,\forall i\in[n]\setminus(\mathcal{I}^{+}\cup\mathcal{I}^{-})\right\}\,,

where
\sigma_{n}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,x\geq 0\,,\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=1\}
is the unit simplex.
Note that for any
x\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{M},\mathcal{I}^{+},\mathcal{I}^{-})
we have
\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\mathbf{M}}(x)\subseteq{\mathcal{I}^{+}}\subseteq\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\mathbf{M}}(x)\cup{\mathcal{I}^{0}_{\mathbf{M}}(x)}^{\phantom{1}}
and
\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\mathbf{M}}(x)\subseteq\mathcal{I}^{-}\subseteq\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\mathbf{M}}(x)\cup\mathcal{I}^{0}_{\mathbf{M}}(x)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}
be a rational matrix of binary encoding length
L
.
There exists
\delta\geq 2^{-O(nL)}
and
\Delta\leq 2^{O(nL)}
, such that for any
\mathcal{I}^{+},\mathcal{I}^{-}\subseteq[n]
,
\mathcal{I}^{+}\cap\mathcal{I}^{-}=\emptyset
,
and any diagonal matrix
\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}^{n}
with
\mathbf{D}_{ii}\in\{\pm 1\}
,
and every basic feasible solution
v
to
\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{D},\mathcal{I}^{+},\mathcal{I}^{-})
, and
i\in[n]
, either
v_{i}=0
or
\delta\leq|v_{i}|\leq 1
, and either
\mathbf{M}_{i}v=0
or
\delta\leq|\mathbf{M}_{i}v|\leq\Delta
.
Moreover, for every basic feasible solution
v
, both
v
and
\mathbf{M}v
have encoding length
O(n^{2}L)
.

Proof.

We assume w.l.o.g.
\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
, noting that the encoding length of
\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{D}
is also
L
.
For every basic solution
v
, the vector of nonzero entries
v_{B}
arises as the solution to a linear system of the form
\mathbf{A}v_{B}=e^{k}
, where
\mathbf{A}
is a nonsingular matrix comprising of a submatrix of
\mathbf{M}
and a row of all ones in the
k
-th row.
By Cramer’s rule, every entry
v_{i}>0
is the ratio of the determinants of two matrices of encoding length
\leq L
, thus Lemma
2.4
(
c
) yields
\operatorname{length}(v_{i})=O(L)
, and consequently,
\operatorname{length}(v)=O(nL)
. Hence, by Lemma
2.4
(
b
),
\operatorname{length}(\mathbf{M}_{i}v)=O(nL)
for all
i\in[n]
, yielding the bounds
\delta
and
\Delta
.
This also implies
\operatorname{length}(\mathbf{M}v)=O(n^{2}L)
, proving the second part.
∎

We start by proving Theorem
1.2
for the special case of
\mathcal{P}
-matrices, where the proof is considerably simpler.

Proposition 3.2
.

For a rational matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}
with
L=\operatorname{length}(\mathbf{M})
,
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})\leq 2^{O(nL)}
.

Proof.

Recall that for a
\mathcal{P}
-matrix the handicap is attained (Lemma
2.2
), namely there exists a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})=\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},x)
. Let us pick such a vector
x
and
define
\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}^{n}
with
\mathbf{D}_{ii}=-1
if
x_{i}<0
and
\mathbf{D}_{ii}=1
otherwise; note that
\mathbf{D}^{-1}=\mathbf{D}
. By Lemma
2.3
,
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{D},\mathbf{D}x)=\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},x)
; we also have
\mathbf{D}x\geq 0
. We can thus replace
\mathbf{M}
by
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{D}\in\mathcal{P}
. For simplicity of notation, we can henceforth assume that
x\geq 0
.
The statement is straightforward for
x=\mathbf{0}_{n}
. If
x\neq\mathbf{0}_{n}
, then, noting that
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},x)=\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},\alpha x)
for any real number
\alpha\neq 0
, we can normalize such that
\|x\|_{1}=1
, i.e.,
x\in\sigma_{n}
.

Let us fix
\mathcal{I}^{+}\coloneqq\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\mathbf{M}}(x)
and
\mathcal{I}^{-}:=\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\mathbf{M}}(x)
, and define
\mathcal{Q}\coloneqq\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{M},\mathcal{I}^{+},\mathcal{I}^{-})
. If
\mathcal{I}^{-}=\emptyset
then
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},x)=0
. For the rest of the proof, we assume
\mathcal{I}^{-}\neq\emptyset
.
By the above normalization,
x\in\mathcal{Q}
.
By Carathéodory’s theorem, we can write
x=\sum_{j=1}^{h}\lambda_{j}v^{(j)}
, where
h\leq n+1
, each
v^{(j)}
is a basic feasible solution in
\mathcal{Q}
, and
\lambda\geq 0
,
\sum_{j=1}^{h}\lambda_{j}=1
. We order the indices such that
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{h}
.

By Lemma
2.1
, there exists an index
i\in[n]
such that
v^{(1)}_{i}(\mathbf{M}v^{(1)})_{i}>0
, namely
i\in\mathcal{I}^{+}
. By Lemma
3.1
, it follows that
v^{(1)}_{i}\geq\delta
and
(\mathbf{M}v^{(1)})_{i}\geq\delta
for
\delta\geq 2^{-O(nL)}
.
Note that
\lambda_{1}\geq 1/h\geq 1/(n+1)
as it is the largest coefficient. Hence,
x_{i}\geq\lambda_{1}v^{(1)}_{i}\geq\delta/(n+1)
and
(\mathbf{M}x)_{i}\geq\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{M}v^{(1)})_{i}\geq\delta/(n+1)
, yielding
x_{i}(\mathbf{M}x_{i})>\delta^{2}/(n+1)^{2}
.
Further,
x^{\top}\mathbf{M}x\geq-\|\mathbf{M}\|_{2}\geq-O(2^{L})
by the normalization
\|x\|_{2}\leq\|x\|_{1}=1
. Thus,
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})=\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},x)\leq 2n^{3}\|\mathbf{M}\|_{2}/\delta^{2}\leq 2^{O(nL)}
.
∎

The following lemma will be needed in the proofs of Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.4
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
x\geq 0
. Then, there exist basic feasible solutions
u
and
v
to

\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{M},\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\mathbf{M}}(x),\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\mathbf{M}}(x))
, and
\lambda\in[0,1]
, such that

\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},u+\lambda v)\geq\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},x)/(n+1)^{2}-1
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{I}^{-}\coloneqq\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\mathbf{M}}(x)
,
\mathcal{I}^{+}\coloneqq\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\mathbf{M}}(x)
, and
\mathcal{Q}\coloneqq\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{M},\mathcal{I}^{+},\mathcal{I}^{-})
.
If
x=\mathbf{0}_{n}
or
\mathcal{I}^{-}(x)=\emptyset
, then
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},x)=0
and therefore the statement holds for any
u,v
, and
\lambda
. For the rest, let us assume
x\neq\mathbf{0}_{n}
and
\mathcal{I}^{-}\neq\emptyset
.
Noting that
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},x)=\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},\alpha x)
for any
\alpha\neq 0
, we can normalize such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=1
.
Hence,
x\in\mathcal{Q}
.
By Carathéodory’s theorem, we can write
x=\sum_{j=1}^{h}\lambda_{j}v^{(j)}
, where
h\leq n+1
, each
v^{(j)}
is a basic feasible solution in
\mathcal{Q}
, and
\lambda\geq 0
,
\sum_{j=1}^{h}\lambda_{j}=1
. We order the indices such that
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{h}>0
.
Let us define

A_{jk}\coloneqq-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{-}}v^{(j)}_{i}(\mathbf{M}v^{(k)})_{i}\quad\mbox{and}\quad B_{jk}\coloneqq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{+}}v^{(j)}_{i}(\mathbf{M}v^{(k)})_{i}\,.

(3)

Thus, we can write

1+4\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},x)=\frac{\sum_{j,k\in[h]}\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k}A_{jk}}{\sum_{j,k\in[h]}\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k}B_{jk}}\,.

(4)

Let us pick
j,k\in[h]
,
j\leq k
such that
\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k}A_{jk}
is maximal (
j=k
is possible). For
z\coloneqq\lambda_{j}v^{({j})}+\lambda_{k}v^{({k})}
, we see that

\displaystyle 1+4\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},x)

\displaystyle\leq h^{2}\cdot\frac{\lambda_{{j}}\lambda_{k}A_{{j}{k}}}{\sum_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}\in[h]}\lambda_{j^{\prime}}\lambda_{k^{\prime}}B_{j^{\prime}k^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq h^{2}\cdot\frac{-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{-}}\left(\lambda_{j}v_{i}^{(j)}+\lambda_{k}v_{i}^{(k)}\right)\mathbf{M}_{i}\left(\lambda_{j}v^{(j)}+\lambda_{k}v^{(k)}\right)}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{+}}\left(\lambda_{j}v_{i}^{(j)}+\lambda_{k}v_{i}^{(k)}\right)\mathbf{M}_{i}\left(\lambda_{j}v^{(j)}+\lambda_{k}v^{(k)}\right)}

\displaystyle=h^{2}(1+4\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},z))\,,

and consequently,
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},z)\geq\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},x)/(n+1)^{2}-1
. Let us now set

u\coloneqq v^{(j)}\,,\quad v\coloneqq v^{(k)}\,,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lambda\coloneqq\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{j}}\,.

By the choice
j\leq k
, we have
\lambda_{j}\geq\lambda_{k}
, implying
\lambda\in[0,1]
.
Then,
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},u+\lambda v)=\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},z)
, and the statement follows.
∎

We next derive a bound on
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},z)
where
z=u+\lambda v
for basic feasible solutions to a polytope of the form
\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{M},\mathcal{I}^{+},\mathcal{I}^{-})
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Then, there is a finite value
C(\mathbf{M})\in\mathbb{R}
such that
for any
\mathcal{I}^{+},\mathcal{I}^{-}\subseteq[n]
such that
\mathcal{I}^{+}\cap\mathcal{I}^{-}=\emptyset
, any
two basic feasible solutions
u
and
v
to
\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{M},\mathcal{I}^{+},\mathcal{I}^{-})
, and
\lambda\in[0,1]
, at least one of the following hold:

(a)

\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},u+\lambda v)\leq C(\mathbf{M})
,

(b)

u\circ\mathbf{M}u=\mathbf{0}_{n}
and
u\circ(\mathbf{M}v)+v\circ(\mathbf{M}u)\lneq\mathbf{0}_{n}
,

(c)

u\circ\mathbf{M}u\lneq\mathbf{0}_{n}
,

(d)

v\circ\mathbf{M}v\lneq\mathbf{0}_{n}
.

If
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}
is a rational matrix, then this holds for
C(\mathbf{M})=2^{O(n\operatorname{length}(\mathbf{M}))}
.

Proof.

Let
0<\delta\leq\Delta<\infty
be chosen such that for any basic feasible solution
x
to
\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{M},\mathcal{I}^{+},\mathcal{I}^{-})
for any choice of
\mathcal{I}^{+}
,
\mathcal{I}^{-}
, and
i\in[n]
, either
x_{i}=0
or
\delta\leq x_{i}\leq\Delta
, and either
(\mathbf{M}x)_{i}=0
or
\delta\leq(\mathbf{M}x)_{i}\leq\Delta
. Since there are finitely many choices of
\mathcal{I}^{+}
,
\mathcal{I}^{-}
, and finitely many basic feasible solutions in each case, we can choose
\delta
positive and
\Delta
finite. Let us define
C(\mathbf{M})\coloneqq 4n\Delta^{2}/\delta^{2}
; we show that this is suitable choice for (
a
).

Let
z\coloneqq u+\lambda v
. If
z\circ(\mathbf{M}z)\geq\mathbf{0}_{n}
, then we get (
a
) by
0\leq C(\mathbf{M})
.
For the rest of the proof, we assume that
\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\mathbf{M}}(z)\neq 0
.
Since
\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\mathbf{M}}(z)\subseteq\mathcal{I}^{+}\subseteq\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\mathbf{M}}(z)\cup\mathcal{I}^{0}_{\mathbf{M}}(z)
and
\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\mathbf{M}}(z)\subseteq\mathcal{I}^{-}\subseteq\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\mathbf{M}}(z)\cup\mathcal{I}^{0}_{\mathbf{M}}(z)
, we can write

1+4\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},z)=\frac{-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{-}}u_{i}(\mathbf{M}u)_{i}+\lambda\left(u_{i}(\mathbf{M}v)_{i}+v_{i}(\mathbf{M}u)_{i}\right)+\lambda^{2}v_{i}(\mathbf{M}v)_{i}}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{+}}u_{i}(\mathbf{M}u)_{i}+\lambda\left(u_{i}(\mathbf{M}v)_{i}+v_{i}(\mathbf{M}u)_{i}\right)+\lambda^{2}v_{i}(\mathbf{M}v)_{i}}\,.

(5)

By the above choices, the numerator is at most
4n\Delta^{2}
. If
\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\mathbf{M}}(u)\neq\emptyset
, then the denominator is at least
\delta^{2}
. Thus, the above expression is at most
4n\Delta^{2}/\delta^{2}=C(\mathbf{M})
, showing the bound
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},z)\leq C(\mathbf{M})
of case (
a
).

Let us assume henceforth
\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\mathbf{M}}(u)=\emptyset
, i.e.,
u\circ\mathbf{M}u\leq\mathbf{0}_{n}
. If
u\circ\mathbf{M}u\lneq\mathbf{0}_{n}
, then we obtain case (
c
). Thus, let us assume
u\circ\mathbf{M}u=\mathbf{0}_{n}
. From (
5
), we obtain

1+4\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M},z)=\frac{-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{-}}u_{i}(\mathbf{M}v)_{i}+v_{i}(\mathbf{M}u)_{i}+\lambda v_{i}(\mathbf{M}v)_{i}}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{+}}u_{i}(\mathbf{M}v)_{i}+v_{i}(\mathbf{M}u)_{i}+\lambda v_{i}(\mathbf{M}v)_{i}}\,.

(6)

If
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{+}}u_{i}(\mathbf{M}v)_{i}+v_{i}(\mathbf{M}u)_{i}>0
, then again the denominator is at least
\delta^{2}
, while the numerator is at most
3n\Delta^{2}
, leading to case (
a
). Assume now
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{+}}u_{i}(\mathbf{M}v)_{i}+v_{i}(\mathbf{M}u)_{i}=0
, i.e.,
u\circ(\mathbf{M}v)+v\circ(\mathbf{M}u)\leq\mathbf{0}_{n}
. If equality does not hold, then we obtain case (
b
). Otherwise, if
u\circ(\mathbf{M}v)+v\circ(\mathbf{M}u)=\mathbf{0}_{n}
, then (
6
) further becomes

Lemma 5.1

and

have the same signs for all

(\mathbf{M}x)_{i}=\mathbf{M}_{i}x

Proof.

Convexity follows since

\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{diag}(d)

\frac{1}{n}\bar{d}_{h}x_{h}(\mathbf{M}x)_{h}\geq\lambda^{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\ell}}\bar{d}_{j}|v_{j}(\mathbf{M}v)_{j}|\,,

Next, we show

(1+4\tau)\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\ell}}\bar{d}_{i}\left(u_{i}(\mathbf{M}v)_{i}+v_{i}(\mathbf{M}u)_{i}\right)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{+}_{>\ell}}\bar{d}_{i}\left(u_{i}(\mathbf{M}v)_{i}+v_{i}(\mathbf{M}u)_{i}\right)\geq-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\ell}}\bar{d}_{j}\left(u_{j}(\mathbf{M}v)_{j}+v_{j}(\mathbf{M}u)_{j}\right)\,.

To verify this, note that
if we were able to choose

, then for any

\frac{1}{n^{2}}\bar{d}_{h}(v_{h}(\mathbf{M}u)_{h}+u_{h}(\mathbf{M}v)_{h})>\frac{K\bar{d}_{j}}{n^{2}}\delta^{2}>2\bar{d}_{j}\Delta\geq\bar{d}_{j}\left(|u_{j}(\mathbf{M}v)_{j}|+|v_{j}(\mathbf{M}u)_{j}|\right)\,,

Summing up over
j\in\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\ell}
, we get

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{+}_{>\ell}}\bar{d}_{i}\left(u_{i}(\mathbf{M}v)_{i}+v_{i}(\mathbf{M}u)_{i}\right)>\frac{1}{n}\bar{d}_{h}(v_{h}(\mathbf{M}u)_{h}+u_{h}(\mathbf{M}v)_{h})\geq-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\ell}}\bar{d}_{j}\left(u_{j}(\mathbf{M}v)_{j}+v_{j}(\mathbf{M}u)_{j}\right)\,,

and therefore (

) holds.
We chose
h\in\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\ell}
only when

for all

In this case, Lemma

for

,

and

,
and the fact that

(1+4\tau)\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\ell}}\bar{d}_{i}\left(u_{i}(\mathbf{M}v)_{i}+v_{i}(\mathbf{M}u)_{i}\right)\geq-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\ell}}\bar{d}_{i}\left(u_{i}(\mathbf{M}v)_{i}+v_{i}(\mathbf{M}u)_{i}\right)\,,

completing the proof of (

This now implies

(1+4\tau)\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\ell}}\bar{d}_{i}x_{i}(\mathbf{M}x)_{i}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{+}_{>\ell}}\bar{d}_{i}x_{i}(\mathbf{M}x)_{i}\geq-\lambda\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\ell}}\bar{d}_{j}\left(u_{j}(\mathbf{M}v)_{j}+v_{j}(\mathbf{M}u)_{j}\right)\,.

(18)

Adding up with (

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{1}{n}+4\tau\right)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\ell}}\bar{d}_{i}x_{i}(\mathbf{M}x)_{i}+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{+}_{>\ell}}\bar{d}_{i}x_{i}(\mathbf{M}x)_{i}

\displaystyle\geq-\lambda\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\ell}}\bar{d}_{j}\left(u_{j}(\mathbf{M}v)_{j}+v_{j}(\mathbf{M}u)_{j}\right)-\lambda^{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\ell}}\bar{d}_{j}v_{j}(\mathbf{M}v)_{j}=-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\ell}}x_{j}(\mathbf{M}x)_{j}\,,

where the last equality uses that

in the current case.

Let us restate the linear complementarity problem
LCP

(

and

, the goal is to find vectors

such that

-\mathbf{M}x+s=q\,,\quad x^{\top}s=0\,,\quad x,s\geq\mathbf{0}\,.

(LCP
(\mathbf{M},q)

In this section, we prove the following.

See

First of all, we would like to check the feasibility of the LCP problem. Fukuda and Terlaky
[

proved an alternative theorem for the LCP, which they called LCP Duality Theorem. Before recalling it, let us consider the dual LCP problem (DLCP)

[

: find vectors

, that
satisfy the constraints

{u}+\mathbf{M}^{\top}{z}={0},\quad{q}^{\top}{z}=-1,\quad{u}^{\top}{z}={0},\quad{u},\,{z}\geq{0}.

(DLCP
(\mathbf{M},q)
)

We consider the linear relaxation
DLCP

(
M
,
q
)

as

\mathcal{F}_{D}=\{({u},{z})\geq{0}\;:\;{u}+\mathbf{M}^{\top}{z}={0},\;{q}^{\top}{z}=-1\}.

The above mentioned theorem by Fukuda and Terlaky
[
6
]
asserts duality between these systems:

Theorem 6.1
.

Let a sufficient matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and a vector
{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

be given. Then exactly one of
LCP

(
M
,
q
)

and
DLCP

(
M
,
q
)

has a feasible solution.

Later, Illés, E.-Nagy, and Terlaky
[
9
]
observed the following; the proof is immediate by the definition of row sufficiency.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be a row sufficient matrix. Then,
({u},{z})\in\mathcal{F}_{D}
if and only if
({u},{z})
is a solution to
DLCP

(
M
,
q
)

.

Combining these two statements, it follows that the solvability of a sufficient LCP reduces to solving a linear feasibility problem, and thus can be checked in polynomial time. Therefore, from now on, we assume that the LCP has a solution.
Our first main tool is the following result from
[
10
]
.

Theorem 6.3

(
[
10
, Theorem 20]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}
and
q\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
, and
\rho\geq 0
, and assume
LCP

(
M
,
q
)

is feasible. There exists an algorithm whose running time can be bounded polynomially in
\operatorname{length}(\mathbf{M},q)
and linearly in

(i)

A feasible solution
(x,s)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2n}
to
LCP

(
M
,
q
)

\operatorname{length}(\mathbf{M},q)
.

A vector

(19)

certifying that

Thus, the algorithm either solves the LCP in the running time bound applicable under the assumption

We denote the algorithm asserted in the theorem as

The Ellipsoid Method

We now state the result on our main other tool, the Ellipsoid Method, as described in
[
7
]
. For a convex body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a
strong separation oracle for
K
takes a vector
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as input, and either correctly concludes
y\in K
, or finds a vector
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\left\langle c,y\right\rangle<\min\{\left\langle c,x\right\rangle\mid x\in K\}
. In an
oracle-polynomial algorithm
,
the number of oracle calls and the number of arithmetic operations is polynomial in the input encoding length.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
0<r<R
, and let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex body such that
\|x\|_{2}\leq R
for all
x\in K
, and assume a strong separation oracle is available for
K
. There exists an
oracle-polynomial time algorithm that in
O(n^{2}\log(R/r))
oracle calls and additional polynomial time, either finds a feasible solution
x\in K
, or concludes that
K
does not contain a ball of radius
r
.

In more detail, the ellipsoid method maintains in each iteration an ellipsoid of the form

E(\mathbf{B},d)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,(x-d)^{\top}\mathbf{B}(x-d)\leq 1\}\,,

where
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a positive definite matrix, and
d
the centre of the ellipsoid.

The key subroutine
\mathsf{EllipsoidUpdate}(\mathbf{B},d,c)
takes the current ellipsoid and a direction vector
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and finds a new ellipsoid
E(\mathbf{B}^{\prime},d^{\prime})
that contains the intersection of
E(\mathbf{B},d)
with the halfspace defined by
\left\langle c,x\right\rangle\geq\left\langle c,d\right\rangle
, i.e.,

E(\mathbf{B}^{\prime},d^{\prime})\supseteq E(\mathbf{B},d)\cap\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,\left\langle c,x\right\rangle\geq\left\langle c,d\right\rangle\}\,.

(20)

The key volumetric progress is guaranteed by

\mathrm{vol}(E(\mathbf{B}^{\prime},d^{\prime}))\leq\mathrm{e}^{-1/(2n)}\mathrm{vol}(E(\mathbf{B},d))\,,

(21)

see Chapter 3, in particular Lemma 3.1.34 in
[
7
]
. Recall that the volume of
E(\mathbf{B},d)
is
\mathrm{vol}(E(\mathbf{B},d))=V_{n}/\sqrt{\det(\mathbf{B})}
, where
V_{n}
is the volume of the
n

7
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we analyzed the handicap number
\hat{\kappa}{(\mathbf{M})}
of sufficient matrices, and showed that it can be bounded polynomially in the input encoding length. Further, we introduced the notion of the optimized handicap number
\hat{\kappa}^{\star}{(\mathbf{M})}
that can be achieved by diagonal rescalings, and showed that the set of near-optimal rescalings form a convex set. This enables us to improve the running time dependence of LCP algorithms from
\hat{\kappa}{(\mathbf{M})}
to
\hat{\kappa}^{\star}{(\mathbf{M})}
; the running time improvement may be of exponential magnitude for certain matrices.

However, as shown in Section
5.1
,
\hat{\kappa}^{\star}{(\mathbf{M})}
can still be arbitrarily large. Thus, the question of solving LCPs with sufficient matrices in polynomial time remains open in general. Still, one may ask the following question: given an integer matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
, can
\hat{\kappa}^{\star}{(\mathbf{M})}
be bounded polynomially in
n
and
\|\mathbf{M}\|_{\infty}
, the maximum absolute value of an entry? Together with our result, this would imply an LCP algorithm that is polynomial in the input encoding length and the largest absolute value of the entries of the integer matrix.
Note that no such bound can be given for
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})
, as illustrated by the Csizmadia matrix (
1
). On the other hand, the bound holds for
\hat{\kappa}^{\star}(\mathbf{M})
for the family of matrices in Proposition
1.6
.

One can also ask for the complexity status of computing or approximating
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})
and
\hat{\kappa}^{\star}(\mathbf{M})
. While even approximating these quantities seems very difficult, we are not aware of any hardness proofs. Tseng
[
12
]
showed that deciding column (or row) sufficiency is co-NP complete. However, these do not immediately imply co-NP completeness of deciding sufficiency. Note that deciding sufficiency is equivalent to deciding whether a finite
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})
value exists.

The algorithm in Section
6
is a black-box combination of the ellipsoid and interior point methods and as such is highly non-practical. It would be desirable to develop more direct and efficient IPAs for LCPs where the running time depends on
\hat{\kappa}^{\star}(\mathbf{M})
instead of
\hat{\kappa}(\mathbf{M})
.
