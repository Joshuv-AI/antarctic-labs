Deep Adaptive Dimension Reduction for Bayesian Inference in Inverse Problems
Yueyang Wang, Xili Wang, Kejun Tang, Xiaoliang Wan, Tao Zhou, Chao Yang

Abstract
Solving high-dimensional PDE-governed inverse problems is often challenging due to complex non-Gaussian posterior distributions, expensive forward model evaluations, and misspecified prior information. To address these issues, we propose a deep adaptive dimension-reduction Bayesian inference framework based on the Variational Flow (VF) model. Since standard normalizing flows are restricted by bijective mappings and cannot directly reduce dimensions, VF overcomes this limitation by integrating VAE-based nonlinear dimension reduction with dual normalizing flows for the latent prior and encoder. This design provides a strictly higher evidence lower bound than VAE and allows more flexible approximation of complex posterior distributions. We further introduce an iterative prior updating strategy that gradually moves the prior mean toward high-probability posterior regions, avoiding manual prior tuning. These components form a closed adaptive loop together with an adaptively fine-tuned Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) surrogate: VF generates posterior-concentrated samples to refine the surrogate, while the updated surrogate further improves posterior inference. Numerical experiments on a 100-dimensional Rosenbrock problem and three standard PDE-governed inverse problems show that our method delivers competitive or superior accuracy compared with MCMC, UKI, and SVGD baselines across all tested configurations, with the most pronounced advantages emerging in challenging scenarios such as high-noise observations and high-dimensional parameter spaces.

Introduction

Bayesian inverse problems governed by partial differential equations (PDEs) arise widely in science and engineering, including subsurface flow modeling, medical imaging, and climate science
[
30
,
34
]
. The primary task is to recover unknown parameters from noisy, incomplete observations by characterizing the posterior distribution. In practice, the parameters are often high-dimensional, while the posterior is induced by a nonlinear and expensive PDE forward map and is typically non-Gaussian. This makes accurate and efficient posterior approximation a central challenge. Existing methods face clear limitations: MCMC methods
[
29
,
11
,
8
]
are computationally expensive, Kalman-based methods such as EKI
[
13
]
and UKI
[
12
]
rely on Gaussian approximations, and particle-based variational inference (VI) methods such as SVGD
[
25
]
often degrade in high-dimensional spaces.

Forward evaluation is often the dominant computational bottleneck in Bayesian inverse problems. Neural operators such as FNO
[
23
]
and DeepONet
[
26
]
provide efficient surrogates, but models trained on prior samples can incur large out-of-distribution (OOD) errors in posterior regions. A related and often overlooked issue is the use of a static prior: in high-dimensional spaces lacking explicit domain knowledge, a static prior can rigidly confine inference to its initial subspace
[
15
,
7
]
. A narrow prior restricts the search space and may miss the true posterior, whereas an overly broad prior provides insufficient regularization, exacerbates ill-posedness, and can push PDE solvers into unphysical regimes. This motivates a posterior-informed view of prior construction: a useful prior should be compatible with the structure of the posterior induced by the data and the forward model. Recent adaptive surrogate methods
[
9
]
partially address distribution shift, but they typically rely on Gaussian posterior approximations (via UKI) and expensive sample-selection procedures.

To address these challenges, we propose a
deep adaptive dimension-reduction Bayesian inference framework
with three components. First, we introduce
Variational Flow
(VF), a latent-variable model that combines nonlinear dimension reduction with flow-based density modeling to approximate complex non-Gaussian posteriors. Second, we develop an
iterative prior updating
strategy that progressively shifts the prior mean toward the posterior, improving stability and reducing prior misspecification. Third, we use
adaptive surrogate fine-tuning
based on local perturbations around posterior-concentrated VF samples, avoiding expensive sample-selection heuristics. These components form a mutually reinforcing loop: VF guides the surrogate toward the posterior region, while the refined surrogate improves posterior inference.

Related Work

Bayesian inference for PDE-governed inverse problems

Classical MCMC methods, such as preconditioned Crank–Nicolson (pCN)
[
29
]
and affine-invariant ensemble samplers

[

]
and the Unscented Kalman Inversion (UKI)
[
12
]
, are more efficient but rely on Gaussian posterior structure, limiting accuracy for multimodal or strongly nonlinear posteriors. Stein variational gradient descent (SVGD)
[
24
,
25
]
offers a flexible particle-based alternative, yet its reliance on gradient information and diminishing particle efficiency in high-dimensional spaces limit its applicability.

Neural operator surrogates and adaptive training

Neural operators, including FNO
[
23
]
, DeepONet
[
26
]
, and related architectures
[
36
,
21

, provide efficient surrogates for PDE forward evaluations. However, surrogates trained only on prior samples often suffer from distribution shift and generalize poorly in posterior regions. Adaptive retraining or fine-tuning of the surrogate can mitigate this issue

,
37
,
2
,

,

. A closely related work
[
9
]
initializes the surrogate on prior samples, then alternates between UKI-based posterior estimation and surrogate refinement by selecting an observation-matching anchor from posterior samples and greedily adding nearby yet prediction-diverse samples for retraining.

Deep generative models

Deep generative models have been widely applied to complex problems such as image generation
[
4
,
1
]
, protein conformation
[
14
,
39
,
38
]
, statistical physics
[
40
,
32
]
, and probability approximation
[
31
,
27
]
. Variational autoencoders (VAEs)
[

]
are scalable but can be limited by Gaussian assumptions. Consequently, many dimension-reduction approaches for inverse problems

,

perform inference in a latent space following the VAE framework. Normalizing flows

Preliminaries

2.1
Bayesian Inverse Problems

Let
\Omega_{s}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded spatial domain and
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\Omega_{p}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the unknown parameter vector. We consider physical systems governed by parametric partial differential equations:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u(\boldsymbol{x};m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\boldsymbol{x}));m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\boldsymbol{x}))

\displaystyle=s(\boldsymbol{x}),

\displaystyle\forall(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})\in\Omega_{s}\times\Omega_{p},

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(u(\boldsymbol{x};m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\boldsymbol{x}));m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\boldsymbol{x}))

\displaystyle=g(\boldsymbol{x}),

\displaystyle\forall(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\xi})\in\partial\Omega_{s}\times\Omega_{p},

(2)

where
m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the coefficient field characterized by
\boldsymbol{\xi}
. Here,
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{B}
denote the differential and boundary operators respectively, while
s
and
g
specify the source and boundary conditions. For any
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\Omega_{p}
, we assume the system (
1
)-(
2
) is well-posed, admitting a unique solution
u
in a state space
\mathcal{U}
.

The goal of inverse problems is to infer the unknown parameters
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\Omega_{p}
from noisy measurements
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Let
\mathcal{S}:\Omega_{p}\to\mathcal{U}
be the forward parameter-to-state map, and
\mathcal{O}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be the observation operator. The composite parameter-to-observation map is defined as
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{O}\circ\mathcal{S}
. We consider the additive Gaussian noise model:

\boldsymbol{y}=\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{\xi})+\boldsymbol{\eta},\qquad\boldsymbol{\eta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\eta}).

(3)

The negative log-likelihood function is defined as:

\Phi(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{y}):=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\eta}^{-1/2}\left(\boldsymbol{y}-\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\right)\right\|^{2}.

(4)

Since typically
m\ll d
, the inverse problem is ill-posed. The Bayesian framework regularizes this by modeling parameters as random variables and introducing a prior distribution, assumed here to be a Gaussian
\pi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0})
supported on
\Omega_{p}
. According to Bayes’ theorem, the posterior distribution is:

\begin{split}\pi(\boldsymbol{\xi}|\boldsymbol{y})&=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left(-\Phi(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{y})\right)\pi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),\\
&\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\eta}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{y}-\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{\xi}))\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0})\right\|^{2}\right),\end{split}

(5)

where
Z=\int_{\Omega_{p}}\exp(-\Phi(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{y}))\pi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\xi})d\boldsymbol{\xi}
is the intractable normalization constant (model evidence).

To achieve numerical tractability, we parameterize the spatially dependent Gaussian random field
m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\boldsymbol{x})
using a truncated Karhunen-Loève (KL) expansion, which maps a finite-dimensional parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{d})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to the continuous field. The detailed formulation of the covariance operator and the KL expansion is provided in Appendix
A
.

Variational Flow

3.1
Background: VAEs and Normalizing Flows

3.1.1
Variational Autoencoder (VAE)

To approximate the true distribution
p_{\boldsymbol{x}}
of data
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, VAE
[
17
]
introduces a latent variable
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
(
k<d
for dimension reduction) and optimizes the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO):

L_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x})=-D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}}\,\big\|\,p_{\boldsymbol{z}}\right)+\int q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}}\log p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,d\boldsymbol{z},

(6)

where the decoder, encoder, and prior of VAE are respectively defined as:

\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{de},\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}),\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathrm{de},\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}))\right),

(7)

\displaystyle q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{en},\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x}),\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathrm{en},\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right),

(8)

\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{z}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I}),

(9)

with mean and variance
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{de},\boldsymbol{\theta}}
,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathrm{de},\boldsymbol{\theta}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{en},\boldsymbol{\phi}}
,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathrm{en},\boldsymbol{\phi}}
parameterized by neural networks.

While these structural choices enable tractable optimization, they fundamentally limit the model’s expressiveness: the standard Gaussian prior distribution assigned to the latent variable
\boldsymbol{z}
cannot capture complex structures in the latent space, and the diagonal Gaussian encoder cannot represent multimodal or highly correlated distributions, both common in high-dimensional Bayesian inference.

3.1.2
Normalizing Flow

A normalizing flow
[
28
,
27
]
constructs a complex probability distribution through an invertible, differentiable transformation
f:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
that maps variable
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
to a simple base variable
\boldsymbol{v}=f(\boldsymbol{z})
with known density
p_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{v})
(typically
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})
). By the change-of-variables formula, the density of
\boldsymbol{z}
is:

p_{\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{z})=p_{\boldsymbol{v}}\!\left(f(\boldsymbol{z})\right)\left|\det\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}f(\boldsymbol{z})\right|.

(10)

In practice,
f
is composed of
K
invertible layers
f=f_{K}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}
, each designed so that the Jacobian determinant is efficiently computable. This yields:

p_{\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{z})=p_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{K})\prod_{k=1}^{K}\left|\det\nabla_{\boldsymbol{v}_{k-1}}f_{k}(\boldsymbol{v}_{k-1})\right|,

(11)

where
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}=\boldsymbol{z}
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{k}=f_{k}(\boldsymbol{v}_{k-1})
.

Affine coupling layers

A key building block of normalizing flows is the affine coupling layer
[
5
,
6
]
. Given an input
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, it is split into two disjoint partitions
\boldsymbol{v}=[\boldsymbol{v}^{A},\boldsymbol{v}^{B}]
, and the transformation is defined as:

\boldsymbol{v}^{A}_{\mathrm{out}}=\boldsymbol{v}^{A},\quad\boldsymbol{v}^{B}_{\mathrm{out}}=\boldsymbol{v}^{B}\odot\exp\!\left(\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{v}^{A})\right)+\boldsymbol{t}(\boldsymbol{v}^{A}),

(12)

where
\boldsymbol{s}(\cdot)
and
\boldsymbol{t}(\cdot)
are scale and translation functions parameterized by neural networks, and
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication. Since
\boldsymbol{v}^{A}
is unchanged, the Jacobian of this transformation is lower-triangular, and its log-determinant reduces to
\sum_{j}s_{j}(\boldsymbol{v}^{A})
. More expressive variants, such as Glow
[
18
]
, further incorporate invertible
1\times 1
convolutions and actnorm layers to improve flexibility.

Universal approximation

Crucially, normalizing flows have been shown to be
universal probability approximators
: given sufficient depth and width, coupling-based invertible networks can approximate any diffeomorphism and thus any continuous target distribution to arbitrary precision
[
35
,
20
]
. This universal approximation property is the theoretical foundation underlying the expressiveness guarantees of the Variational Flow model.

3.1.3
Conditional Normalizing Flow

A Conditional Normalizing Flow (CNF)
[
43
]
extends the standard normalizing flow by conditioning each invertible transformation on an external variable, here the data
\boldsymbol{x}
.

Architecture

The CNF is constructed by composing
K
invertible conditional layers. Starting from
\boldsymbol{z}_{0}=\boldsymbol{z}
, each layer applies

\boldsymbol{z}_{k}=f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k-1};\boldsymbol{x}),\quad k=1,\dots,K.

(13)

Each
f^{(k)}(\cdot\,;\boldsymbol{x})
is invertible with respect to
\boldsymbol{z}_{k-1}
for any fixed
\boldsymbol{x}
. Typical implementations use conditional affine coupling layers, in which the scale and translation functions
\boldsymbol{s}(\cdot\,;\boldsymbol{x})
and
\boldsymbol{t}(\cdot\,;\boldsymbol{x})
take both the passthrough partition and
\boldsymbol{x}
as inputs.

Base distribution

The final output
\boldsymbol{z}_{K}=\boldsymbol{u}
follows a conditional Gaussian base distribution:

p_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{x})=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}),\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right),

(14)

where the mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})
and variance
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})
are parameterized by neural networks. This data-dependent base distribution provides additional flexibility beyond a fixed standard Gaussian.

Density computation

By iteratively applying the change-of-variables formula, the conditional density is:

q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})=p_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{x})\prod_{k=1}^{K}\left|\det\frac{\partial f^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k-1};\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial\boldsymbol{z}_{k-1}}\right|.

(15)

Since each conditional layer preserves the triangular Jacobian structure of affine coupling, the log-determinant remains computable.

3.2
Variational Flow Model

Normalizing flows provide expressive density models with exact likelihood evaluation, but their bijective structure requires equal input and output dimensions and thus does not support intrinsic dimensionality reduction. VAEs naturally address this issue through latent-variable compression, but standard Gaussian priors and encoders can be restrictive. We therefore propose Variational Flow (VF), which enriches both the latent prior and the encoder using normalizing flows.

Generalizing the prior

We replace the fixed Gaussian prior
p_{\boldsymbol{z}}=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})
with a normalizing flow prior. Specifically, we define a normalizing flow
f_{\mathrm{pr},\boldsymbol{\beta}}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
with trainable parameters
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, which maps the latent variable
\boldsymbol{z}
to a base variable
\boldsymbol{v}=f_{\mathrm{pr},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{z})
with
\boldsymbol{v}\sim p_{\boldsymbol{v}}=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})
. By the change-of-variables formula, the induced prior density on
\boldsymbol{z}
is:

p_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{z})=p_{\boldsymbol{v}}\!\big(f_{\mathrm{pr},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{z})\big)\left|\det\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}f_{\mathrm{pr},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{z})\right|.

(16)

Generalizing the encoder

We replace the diagonal Gaussian encoder
q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}}
with a CNF. Specifically, we define a CNF
f_{\mathrm{en},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
with trainable parameters
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, which maps the latent variable
\boldsymbol{z}
to a base variable
\boldsymbol{u}=f_{\mathrm{en},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{x})
with
\boldsymbol{u}\sim p_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{x})
. By the change-of-variables formula, the encoder density becomes:

q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})=p_{\boldsymbol{u}}\!\left(f_{\mathrm{en},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{x});\boldsymbol{x}\right)\left|\det\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}f_{\mathrm{en},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{x})\right|.

(17)

VF definition

Combining the above generalizations, the Variational Flow is defined by:

\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=p_{\boldsymbol{v}}\!\big(f_{\mathrm{pr},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{z})\big)\left|\det\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}f_{\mathrm{pr},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{z})\right|,

(18)

\displaystyle q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=p_{\boldsymbol{u}}\!\left(f_{\mathrm{en},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{x});\,\boldsymbol{x}\right)\left|\det\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}f_{\mathrm{en},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{x})\right|,

(19)

\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{de},\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}),\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathrm{de},\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}))\right).

(20)

This “dual-flow” architecture addresses the main limitation of standard normalizing flows – the bijectivity constraint that prevents dimensionality reduction. It achieves this by using the encoder-decoder structure of VAE for latent compression and applying flows to model the density in both the prior and encoder.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose the decoder
p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}}
in VAE is parameterized by nonlinear neural networks. If

(a)

the family of flow priors
\{p_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
induced by the bijection
f_{\mathrm{pr},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
can approximate any
k
-dimensional probability density function to arbitrary precision;

(b)

the family of conditional normalizing flow encoders
\{q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
can approximate any conditional distribution to arbitrary precision;

suppose
(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})
are the optimal parameters for standard VAE:

(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})=\mathop{\arg\max}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}}\;\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[L_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x})],

(21)

then the Variational Flow (VF) model achieves a strictly higher Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) than standard VAE if either of the following conditions holds:

(i)

the aggregated posterior of the optimal encoder in the standard VAE does not equal the prior, i.e.,
q_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}=\int q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}p_{\boldsymbol{x}}d\boldsymbol{x}\neq p_{\boldsymbol{z}}
;

(ii)

there exists a set of positive measure with respect to
p_{\boldsymbol{x}}
such that the model posterior
p_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}=p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}p_{\boldsymbol{z}}/\int p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}p_{\boldsymbol{z}}d\boldsymbol{z}
of VAE is not a diagonal Gaussian distribution.

Furthermore, when conditions (i) and (ii) hold simultaneously, the flow prior and the conditional normalizing flow encoder each provide a strictly positive improvement to the ELBO.

Proof.

Let

E_{0}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[L_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{x})]=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{z}})]+\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}\,q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}}[\log p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}].

(22)

Generalize the prior

Fixing the encoder
q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}
and decoder
p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}
and replacing the prior
p_{\boldsymbol{z}}
with the normalizing flow prior
p_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, the ELBO becomes

\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[L_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{x})]=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\beta}})]+\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}\,q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}}[\log p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}].

(23)

Subtracting
E_{0}
yields

\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[L_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{x})]-E_{0}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{z}})]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\beta}})].

(24)

Simplifying the right-hand side of the above equation, we note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\|p)]

\displaystyle=\int q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})\,p_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{x})\log\frac{q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})}{p(\boldsymbol{z})}\,d\boldsymbol{z}\,d\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=\int q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\,p_{\boldsymbol{x}}\log q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\,d\boldsymbol{z}\,d\boldsymbol{x}-\int q_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{z})\log p(\boldsymbol{z})\,d\boldsymbol{z},

(25)

where
q_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{z})=\int q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})\,p_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{x})\,d\boldsymbol{x}
is the aggregated posterior. Thus, (
24
) simplifies to

\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[L_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{x})]-E_{0}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{z}})-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\beta}}).

(26)

By assumption (a), the flow prior
p_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
can approximate
q_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}
to arbitrary precision. If condition (i) holds, i.e.,
q_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\neq p_{\boldsymbol{z}}
, then
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{z}})>0
. By choosing
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
such that
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{z},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})<D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{z}})
, we have

E_{1}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[L_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(\boldsymbol{x})]>E_{0}.

(27)

Generalize the encoder

Fixing the decoder
p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}
and the prior
p_{\boldsymbol{z},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
, we extend the encoder from a diagonal Gaussian to a conditional normalizing flow
q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
. Utilizing the following decomposition of the ELBO:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[L_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(\boldsymbol{x})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\left[\int q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\left[\log\frac{p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}p_{\boldsymbol{z},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}{q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\right]d\boldsymbol{z}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\left[\int q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\left[\log\frac{p_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}p_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}{q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\right]d\boldsymbol{z}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[\log p_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(\boldsymbol{x})]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})],

(28)

where

p_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\int p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z})\,p_{\boldsymbol{z},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(\boldsymbol{z})\,d\boldsymbol{z}

(29)

is the model evidence (independent of the encoder parameters), and

p_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z})\,p_{\boldsymbol{z},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(\boldsymbol{z})}{p_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(\boldsymbol{x})}

(30)

is the true model posterior under the decoder
p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}
and the prior
p_{\boldsymbol{z},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
.
From the decomposition (
28
), maximizing the ELBO with respect to the encoder is equivalent to minimizing
\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})]
. In prior generalization, the encoder remains a diagonal Gaussian
q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}
, and the corresponding ELBO is

E_{1}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[\log p_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(\boldsymbol{x})]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})].

(31)

Since the decoder mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{de},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{z})
is a nonlinear function of
\boldsymbol{z}
, the log model posterior

\log p_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})=\log p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z})+\log p_{\boldsymbol{z},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(\boldsymbol{z})-\log p_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(\boldsymbol{x})

is generally not a quadratic function of
\boldsymbol{z}
; thus,
p_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
is generally not a diagonal Gaussian distribution. If condition (ii) holds, the diagonal Gaussian encoder cannot accurately match the model posterior, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})]>0.

(32)

By assumption (b), the conditional normalizing flow encoder
q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
can approximate any conditional distribution to arbitrary precision. Therefore, there exists
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
such that

\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})]<\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})].

(33)

From (
28
) and (
33
), we have

E_{2}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[L_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(\boldsymbol{x})]>E_{1}.

(34)

Combining the two steps

From (
27
) and (
34
), when both conditions (i) and (ii) hold,

E_{2}>E_{1}>E_{0}.

(35)

Since the optimal ELBO of the VF model is no lower than the ELBO value at any specific parameters, we have

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[L_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{x})]\geq E_{2}>E_{0}=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[L_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x})].

(36)

When only condition (ii) holds, one can directly choose
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
in encoder generalization step to correspond to the identity mapping (i.e., the prior remains
p_{\boldsymbol{z}}
), in which case the encoder improvement still provides a strict improvement. In summary, if either condition (i) or (ii) holds, a strict improvement in the ELBO is guaranteed.

Remark 2
.

Condition (i) is expected to hold for most non-degenerate VAEs in practice: if
q_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}=p_{\boldsymbol{z}}=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})
, it implies that the marginal distribution of the latent variables across all training samples is exactly the standard Gaussian, which is almost impossible to achieve precisely in practice.
Condition (ii) typically holds when the nonlinear decoder induces a non-quadratic log-likelihood in the latent variable: since the model posterior satisfies
p_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})\propto p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z})\,p_{\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{z})
, and
\log p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}
is generally non-quadratic with respect to
\boldsymbol{z}
(as the decoder mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{de},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{z})
is a nonlinear function), the model posterior is generally non-Gaussian.

Remark 3
.

The proof reveals that ELBO improvement stems from two complementary mechanisms:

•

Prior matching
: The flow prior eliminates the mismatch between the aggregated posterior
q_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}
and the prior
p_{\boldsymbol{z}}
, with a contribution of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{z}})-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{z},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})>0
;

•

Posterior approximation
: The CNF encoder narrows the variational gap, i.e., the difference in KL divergence between the encoder and the true model posterior:
\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\cdot})]-\mathbb{E}_{p_{\boldsymbol{x}}}[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\|p_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\cdot})]>0
.

The two work synergistically: the flow prior absorbs the burden of matching the aggregated posterior, freeing the CNF encoder to capture complex model posteriors (multimodality, asymmetry, etc.).

3.3
Approximating Unnormalized Densities via Variational Flow

Beyond standard generative modeling where VF is trained on observed data by maximizing the ELBO (see Appendix
B
), VF can also approximate a target density
\hat{p}(\boldsymbol{x})
that is known only up to a normalization constant
C=\int\hat{p}(\boldsymbol{x})\,d\boldsymbol{x}
. We minimize the KL divergence between the VF joint and a target joint constructed with the encoder:

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,p_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\,\Big\|\,q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\,\frac{\hat{p}(\boldsymbol{x})}{C}\right)=

\displaystyle\int p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,p_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\log\!\left(\frac{p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,p_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\beta}}}{q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\,\hat{p}(\boldsymbol{x})}\right)d\boldsymbol{z}\,d\boldsymbol{x}

(37)

\displaystyle+\log C.

Since
\log C
is independent of the model parameters
(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\theta})
, we minimize only the first term via Monte Carlo:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VF}}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\theta})\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\log\!\left(\frac{p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}|\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\,p_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})}{q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}|\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)})\,\hat{p}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)})}\right),

(38)

where
\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}\sim p_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\sim p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. In this work, we set
\hat{p}(\boldsymbol{x})=\exp\!\left(-\Phi(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{y})\right)\pi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
as the unnormalized posterior from Eq. (
5
) by identifying the VF data variable
\boldsymbol{x}
with the unknown parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\xi}
. Once trained, VF efficiently generates posterior samples
\boldsymbol{\xi}\sim p_{\mathrm{VF}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
via

\boldsymbol{z}=f^{-1}_{\mathrm{pr},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{v}),\;\boldsymbol{v}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})\quad\Rightarrow\quad\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{de},\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z})+\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\mathrm{de},\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z})\odot\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\;\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I}),

(39)

which are used to adaptively update the surrogate model as described in the next section.

4
Deep Adaptive Bayesian Inference

4.1
Overall Framework

The deep adaptive framework addresses two coupled challenges: the out-of-distribution (OOD) inaccuracy of the surrogate and the difficulty of specifying an appropriate prior mean. We tackle both through an alternating optimization loop between the VF posterior approximation and the surrogate model fine-tuning, alongside a dynamic update of the prior mean.

At each stage
k
, the framework executes three sequential steps:

•

VF training
: approximate the current unnormalized posterior

\hat{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\propto\exp(-\Phi(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{y}))\,\pi_{0}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\xi})

via (
38
) and update the prior mean iteratively (see Section
4.2
).

•

Data replacement
: sample
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathrm{post}}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{M}\sim p_{\mathrm{VF}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
, query the exact PDE solver for these samples, and construct a newly generated local dataset
\mathcal{D}_{k}
as demonstrated in Subsection
4.3

•

Surrogate fine-tuning
: fine-tune the surrogate model
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
exclusively on the new dataset
\mathcal{D}_{k}
to align its accuracy with the current posterior region.

The loop terminates when the relative change in data misfit evaluated at the prior mean,

\bigl|\Phi(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{prior}}^{(k-1)},\boldsymbol{y})-\Phi(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{prior}}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{y})\bigr|/\Phi(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{prior}}^{(k-1)},\boldsymbol{y})
falls below a threshold
\epsilon

4.2
Iterative Prior Updating

To overcome the limitation of a narrow initial guess and prevent the surrogate from querying unphysical regimes caused by overly broad priors, we seamlessly embed a dynamic prior updating strategy directly into the Variational Flow (VF) training loop. We dynamically recalibrate the mean of Gaussian prior
\pi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
appearing in the posterior distribution (
5
) before the
i
-th epoch of stage
k
via a momentum-based moving average:

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{prior}}^{(k,i)}=\alpha\,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{post}}^{(k,i)}+(1-\alpha)\,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{prior}}^{(k-1)}.

(40)

This scheme balances adaptability and stability across two time scales.
At the epoch level
, the posterior mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{post}}^{(k,i)}
is estimated by averaging
M
samples drawn from the current VF model
p_{\mathrm{VF}}^{(k,i-1)}
(i.e., the VF obtained after epoch
i-1
of stage
k
):

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{post}}^{(k,i)}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(j)},\quad\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(j)}\sim p_{\mathrm{VF}}^{(k,i-1)}(\boldsymbol{\xi}).

(41)

At the stage level
, the anchor
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{prior}}^{(k-1)}
remains fixed throughout stage
k
and is updated only at the end of the stage via
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{prior}}^{(k)}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{prior}}^{(k,N_{e})}
. Accordingly, the effective prior is
\pi_{0}^{(k,i)}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{prior}}^{(k,i)},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0})
, where
\alpha\in(0,1]
controls the update strength. As noted in
[
12
]
, smaller
\alpha
yields stronger regularization, whereas
\alpha=1
removes this effect; that study further found that
\alpha\approx 0.5
is close to optimal in general. Keeping the covariance fixed at
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}
further prevents mode collapse and promotes stable exploration. We initialize with a standard isotropic Gaussian, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}=\boldsymbol{I}
, and then use Eq. (
40
) to gradually shift the prior toward posterior region.

4.3
Adaptive Surrogate Fine-tuning

Following each VF training stage, the FNO surrogate must be rectified to match the newly located posterior region. We draw a set of high-fidelity parameter samples
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathrm{post}}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{M}
from the updated VF model
p_{\mathrm{VF}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
. Because these samples tightly concentrate around the inferred posterior, we inject a Gaussian perturbation with controlled strength to maintain local support coverage and prevent the surrogate from overfitting to a degenerate region:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(j)}=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathrm{post}}^{(j)}+\gamma\,\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(j)},\quad\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(j)}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I}),

(42)

where
\gamma>1
controls the perturbation strength. This perturbation is applied directly in the Karhunen-Loève coefficient space, and its scale is defined relative to the prior standard deviation, since the prior is standard normal in this space.

Instead of accumulating a growing historical dataset or relying on expensive greedy filtering, we adopt an aggressive replacement strategy. We generate new PDE solutions strictly for these perturbed samples and fine-tune the surrogate model exclusively on this newly generated data. This dynamic replacement directly rectifies the OOD problem by continuously shifting the surrogate’s effective training distribution from the initial broad prior to the highly localized posterior support, ensuring local surrogate accuracy with minimal computational overhead.

5
Numerical Experiments

5.1
Classes of Problem Studied

The performance of the proposed framework is evaluated on two types of problems. First, to specifically assess the posterior approximation capability of the VF model, we consider a challenging high-dimensional synthetic distribution. Second, to validate the complete framework, which incorporates both the adaptive surrogate fine-tuning and iterative prior updating, we test it on three PDE-governed inverse problems of increasing complexity.

1.

A 100-dimensional Rosenbrock inverse problem:
In Subsection
5.2
, we utilize this problem to evaluate the capability of various methods in approximating complex, non-Gaussian posterior distributions. To isolate the generative performance of the models, the iterative prior updating module is not employed in this example. The numerical results demonstrate that our approach achieves superior posterior approximation accuracy compared to the baseline methods. Notably, it significantly outperforms the vanilla VAE, which empirically corroborates our theoretical result.

2.

PDE-governed inverse problem:
In Subsections
5.3
,
5.4
, and
5.5
, we evaluate our framework on three PDE-governed inverse problems, where observations are corrupted by relative Gaussian noise:

\boldsymbol{y}=\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathrm{ref}})+\delta\max\bigl\{|\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathrm{ref}})|\bigr\}\,\boldsymbol{\eta},\quad\boldsymbol{\eta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I}),

6
Conclusions

We present a deep adaptive dimension-reduction Bayesian inference framework for high-dimensional PDE-governed inverse problems, addressing non-Gaussian posteriors, surrogate out-of-distribution (OOD) errors, and prior misspecification. Our core Variational Flow (VF) model integrates VAE-based dimensionality reduction with dual normalizing flows, achieving a strictly higher ELBO than VAE and capturing multimodal posteriors. Coupled with an iterative prior updating scheme and an adaptively fine-tuned FNO surrogate, the framework establishes a mutually reinforcing loop for accurate posterior approximation. Empirically, our method achieves state-of-the-art accuracy on standard PDE benchmarks, particularly excelling under high noise and large dimensions. Future work will address the current lack of theoretical convergence guarantees and extend to multi-physics systems.

Appendix A
Karhunen-Loève Expansion with Covariance Operator

The Karhunen-Loève (KL) expansion provides a systematic way to represent a spatially dependent Gaussian random field (GRF) as a spectral series. After truncation, it yields a finite-dimensional parameterization of the random field, forming the basis for representing the coefficient field in our PDE-governed inverse problems.

Covariance operator

Let
m(\boldsymbol{x})
be a GRF with mean
\bar{m}(\boldsymbol{x})
and covariance kernel
k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})
. We define the corresponding covariance operator
\mathcal{C}:L^{2}(\Omega_{s})\to L^{2}(\Omega_{s})
as

(\mathcal{C}\,\phi)(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{\Omega_{s}}k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,\phi(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}.

(62)

In many PDE-governed inverse problems, we parameterize the covariance via an elliptic operator, e.g.

\mathcal{C}=\sigma^{2}\,(-\Delta+\tau^{2})^{-l},

(63)

where
\Delta
is the Laplacian on
\Omega_{s}
with appropriate boundary conditions,
\sigma>0
controls the overall variance (amplitude) of the Gaussian random field,
\tau>0
controls the inverse correlation length, and
l>0
controls the regularity of the field. This operator is symmetric, positive definite, and compact, ensuring the existence of a countable set of eigenpairs.

Solving the eigenvalue problem
\mathcal{C}\,\phi_{j}=\lambda_{j}\,\phi_{j}
yields eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq 0
and orthonormal eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
in
L^{2}(\Omega_{s})
. The GRF can then be represented as an infinite series
m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\infty}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\bar{m}(\boldsymbol{x})+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\,\xi_{j}\,\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})
, where
\xi_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. For numerical tractability, we truncate the series to the first
d
leading modes:

m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\bar{m}(\boldsymbol{x})+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\,\xi_{j}\,\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{d})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(64)

This finite-dimensional parameterization defines the exact inference space
\Omega_{p}
over which our Variational Flow (VF) posterior approximation is constructed.

Appendix B
VF for Standard Generative Modeling

Section
3.3
addresses the setting where the target density
\hat{p}(\boldsymbol{x})
is known up to a normalization constant. Here we describe the complementary setting of standard generative modeling, where only i.i.d. samples
\{\boldsymbol{x}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}\sim p_{\boldsymbol{x}}
are available but
p_{\boldsymbol{x}}
cannot be evaluated.

In this case, we train VF by maximizing the ELBO of
\log p_{\boldsymbol{x}}
:

L_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\!\left[\log\frac{p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}}\,p_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\beta}}}{q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\right]\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\log\frac{p_{\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\,p_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})}{q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}|\boldsymbol{x})},

(65)

where
\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{M}
are drawn from
q_{\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})
. The total training objective is

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}L_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(n)}).

(66)

Appendix C
Pre-training of the Fourier Neural Operator Surrogate

We adopt the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO)
[
23
]
as the surrogate. The FNO learns mappings between infinite-dimensional function spaces in the spectral domain, achieving discretization-invariant representations that generalize across different grids.

Dataset construction

Training samples
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are drawn from the prior
\pi_{0}=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0})
, and the corresponding coefficient fields are generated via the truncated KL expansion (
64
) (see Appendix
A
for details). For each sample, the PDE system (
1
)-(
2
) is solved numerically to obtain the state field
u(\cdot;m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)}})
, yielding the training dataset:

\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{\,m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)}}(\boldsymbol{x}),\;u\bigl(\boldsymbol{x};m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)}}\bigr)\,\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N}.

(67)

Training objective

Let
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
denote the trainable parameters of the FNO, denoted
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}:m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\mapsto u(\cdot;m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
. The model is trained by minimizing the relative
L^{2}
loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FNO}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta};\mathcal{D})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\bigl\|\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}(m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)}})-u(\cdot;m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)}})\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{s})}}{\bigl\|u(\cdot;m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)}})\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{s})}}.

(68)

Once trained, the composite map
\boldsymbol{\xi}\mapsto\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}(m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
serves as the surrogate for the forward map throughout Bayesian inference. Specific hyperparameter settings (dataset size, learning rate schedule, training epochs) for each experiment are reported in Appendix
D
.

Appendix D
Implementation Details

Computational Resources.
All experiments were conducted on a single workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU (24GB VRAM). The total computational time for a single run of each PDE-governed inverse problem (1D Darcy flow, 2D Darcy flow, and 2D Navier-Stokes) is approximately 20 minutes. The Rosenbrock inverse problem takes approximately 2 hours to complete.

D.1
PDE-governed Inverse Problems

The stopping threshold of the overall framework is set to a tolerance of
\epsilon=0.01
. For training the FNO as a surrogate model, the initial dataset size is set to 2,000,
and the model is trained for 1,000 epochs with an initial learning rate of 0.001, using a decay rate of 0.5 applied every 50 epochs. During the adaptive fine-tuning of the FNO, 500 samples are drawn at each stage with a perturbation strength of
\gamma=3
(as defined in Eq. (
42
)), and the model is then
fine‑tuned for 100 epochs using a batch size of 25. The learning rate is initialized at 0.001 and decreased by a factor of 0.5 every 25 epochs, using the Adam optimizer
[
16
]
.

For Variational Flow (VF) model, the data dimension is set to
d
and the latent dimension is 16 throughout all experiments. The flow-based prior employs 6 affine coupling layers, while the conditional normalizing flow encoder uses 2 affine coupling
layers. Each flow subnetwork has a hidden width of 32, and the multiscale squeezing step is fixed at 4. The accompanying VAE-style decoder contains 5 hidden layers of width 64. In terms of VF training, we run at most 20 adaptive stages. At each stage the VF model is trained on 1024 samples with a batch size of 32 and a learning rate of 0.001 for 10 epochs.

D.2
Rosenbrock Problem

In this problem, we compare our proposed model with a vanilla VAE, MCMC, SVGD, and UKI. Our method and UKI do not employ iterative priors in this problem. The MCMC method is implemented using the emcee Python package with 1,000 chains, a burn-in period of 50,000 steps, and 10,000 sampling steps. The SVGD method utilizes 20,000 particles and is run for 10,000 iterations. Our model and VAE model are trained with a learning rate of 0.001 on a dataset of 100,000 samples, using a batch size of 10,000, for 20,000 epochs. The UKI method is simulated for 500 steps. For all methods, the results are presented based on 20,000 samples.

D.3
Preconditioned Crank-Nicolson (pCN) MCMC

We implement the preconditioned Crank-Nicolson (pCN) MCMC method, a standard sampler whose efficiency does not degrade with parameter dimension. Assuming a Gaussian prior
\boldsymbol{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{m}_{0},\boldsymbol{C}_{0})
, the proposal rule is

\boldsymbol{m}^{*}=\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}\,(\boldsymbol{m}_{n}-\boldsymbol{m}_{0})+\boldsymbol{m}_{0}+\beta\boldsymbol{\eta},\quad\boldsymbol{\eta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{C}_{0}),

(69)

with step size
\beta\in(0,1]
. Since the proposal preserves the prior measure, the acceptance probability depends only on the likelihood:

a(\boldsymbol{m}_{n},\boldsymbol{m}^{*})=\min\bigl(1,\;\exp\bigl(\Phi(\boldsymbol{m}_{n})-\Phi(\boldsymbol{m}^{*})\bigr)\bigr),

(70)

where
\Phi
is the negative log-likelihood. We set
\beta=0.1
and run 5,000 iterations of the high-fidelity FDM solver, discarding the first
20\%
as burn-in and thinning by a factor of 10.

We implement the UKI algorithm of

9
]

Evolution:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{m}_{n+1}=\boldsymbol{r}_{0}+\alpha(\boldsymbol{m}_{n}-\boldsymbol{r}_{0})+\boldsymbol{\omega}_{n+1},\quad

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}_{n+1}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma_{\omega}}),

Observation:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{n+1}=\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{m}_{n+1})+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n+1},\quad

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n+1}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma_{\eta}}),

(71)

where
\boldsymbol{m}_{n+1}
is the unknown parameter vector,
\boldsymbol{y}_{n+1}
the observation,
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{n+1}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n+1}
are mutually independent zero-mean Gaussian errors,
\boldsymbol{r}_{0}

\alpha\in(0,1]
is the regularization parameter, set to
\alpha=0.5
.

D.5
SVGD with Adaptive Surrogate Fine-tuning

We implement Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD)
[
24
,
25
]
with the radial basis function (Gaussian) kernel

k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\exp\!\left(-\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\|^{2}/h\right),

(72)

whose bandwidth
h
is set via the median heuristic
h=\mathrm{med}^{2}/\log n
, with
\mathrm{med}
the median of pairwise particle distances and
n
the number of particles. We run 20 adaptive stages, alternating 50 SVGD updates with FNO fine-tuning at each stage; FNO fine-tuning uses perturbation strength
\gamma=3
(Eq. (
42
)) for adequate local coverage.
