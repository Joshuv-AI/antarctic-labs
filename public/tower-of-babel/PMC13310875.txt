Green synthesis and characterization of silver nanoparticles from the aerial parts of Leucas lanata with enhanced antioxidant and antimicrobial activities
Authors: Ruchika Sharma, Sheetal Tyagi, Indra Rautela, Rakesh Kumar Bachheti, Archana Bachheti, Limenew Abate Worku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In the present study, a green, Phyto-assisted approach was employed to synthesize silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) using the aqueous extract of the aerial parts of Leucas lanata (L. lanata). The successful formation of AgNPs was evidenced by a characteristic surface plasmon resonance (SPR) band at 384 nm in the UV–VIS absorption spectrum. Elemental mapping and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis further confirmed the presence of silver, showing a prominent peak at 2.7 keV. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns revealed distinct crystalline planes, consistent with the results of selected-area electron diffraction (SAED). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) determined an average particle size of 25.37 nm. The nanoparticles exhibited a high zeta potential value, suggesting strong stability attributed to phytochemical-mediated capping, as corroborated by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR). The biosynthesized L-AgNPs displayed potent antioxidant activity with an IC50 value of 49 µg/mL in the DPPH (2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) assay. L-AgNPs exhibited a higher proportion of ABTS (2,2′-Azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) scavenging activity (12.26%) than the L. lanata extract at a lower concentration of 100 µg/mL, indicating enhanced antioxidant potential. Similarly, the FRAP (Fluorescence Recovery After Photobleaching) value of L-AgNPs (21.56 ± 0.17 µM at 100 µg/mL) was significantly greater than that of the aqueous plant extract, further confirming their superior reducing capacity. Moreover, the silver nanoparticles demonstrated significant antimicrobial activity against Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Salmonella abony, and Bacillus subtilis. The MIC (Minimum Inhibitory Concentration) value for L. lanata aqueous extract showed effects between 0.45 and 0.84 mg/mL, whereas L-AgNPs showed effects between 0.09 and 0.34 mg/mL across bacteria. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report on the silver nanoparticle-synthesizing potential of L. lanata, highlighting its promising applications in biomedical and pharmaceutical fields.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Bacterial contamination is a significant cause of various issues in the food industry, medical devices, and water treatment. Both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria are to blame for contamination and many human illnesses. Although many antibacterial agents are available on the market, they harm society due to their side effects, high cost, and toxicity [1, 2]. All these obstacles have been overcome primarily through the synthesis of metallic nanoparticles, which are useful in various fields, including electronics, environmental remediation, medicine, and agriculture [3–5]. Metallic nanoparticles can be made from top to bottom and bottom to top. In the top-down method, metallic particles are converted into nanoparticles using various methods, including physical, chemical, and mechanical processes. In contrast, researchers employ physical or chemical vapour deposition, sol-gel, chemical reduction, hydrothermal, solvothermal, spray pyrolysis, laser ablation, and biomimetic techniques to create metallic nanoparticles utilising the bottom-up strategy [6–9]. The most effective method among these is biomimetic, as it utilizes various microorganisms, enzymes, and plant extracts. In this method, readily available, non-toxic, and inexpensive materials are used to make metallic nanoparticles. Hence, this method is safe for both society and the environment [10–13]. Many silver nanoparticles have been made using the biomimetic method in the biomedical field. Most of these are prepared from plant extracts, viz., silver nanoparticles synthesized from leaf extracts of Leucas aspera, leaf extracts of Solanum sisymbriifolium, root and leaf extracts of Rhus chinensis Mill., and flower extracts of Aerva lanata [14–17]. Phytochemicals are present in the extracts of various plant parts, including root, stem, flower, fruit, seed, and leaf. Ag metal nanoparticles of different sizes are made by reducing silver ions [18–23].

In addition to its traditional medicinal uses, L. lanata extract offers notable advantages over conventional antibiotics, particularly in wound management. The extract exhibited stronger antimicrobial activity against bacterial pathogens than against fungal strains, which is highly relevant, as bacterial infections are the primary cause of delayed wound healing. The 10% (w/w) ointment formulated with a 50% ethanolic extract significantly accelerated wound healing by markedly reducing the epithelization period and enhancing wound contraction in both infected and uninfected models. Unlike many synthetic antibiotics that target only the microbial load, L. lanata extract demonstrated a dual therapeutic effect by simultaneously controlling infection and improving the host’s antioxidant defence system, as evidenced by increased levels of superoxide dismutase, catalase, and reduced glutathione. HPLC analysis confirmed the presence of bioactive phenolic acids, including gallic, protocatechuic, chlorogenic, caffeic, and ferulic acids, which are known to contribute synergistically to antimicrobial, antioxidant, and tissue-regenerative properties. Furthermore, the presence of bioactive fatty acids and ascorbic acid derivatives enhances its therapeutic potential, offering a multi-targeted, biocompatible, and resistance-free alternative to conventional antibiotics. These findings highlight the novelty of L. lanata-mediated AgNPs as a promising natural therapeutic platform with improved efficacy, reduced side effects, and lower risk of antimicrobial resistance. (Fig. 1) [24–27].

Green synthesis has emerged as an attractive strategy for nanoparticle fabrication due to the environmental concerns and biological limitations associated with conventional chemical and physical approaches. Among metal nanoparticles, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) have received considerable attention because their nanoscale dimensions confer high surface reactivity and broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, leading to their extensive use in wound management, antimicrobial coatings, drug delivery systems, and other biomedical applications. In plant-mediated synthesis, naturally occurring phytochemicals serve dual roles as reducing and stabilising agents, thereby improving nanoparticle compatibility and functional performance while avoiding the use of toxic chemical additives. Although numerous plant species have been explored for AgNP synthesis, earlier studies often report nanoparticle formation primarily through physicochemical characterization, with limited emphasis on reproducibility, mechanistic interpretation, or direct correlation between nanoparticle properties and biological activity. Moreover, medicinal plants from the Himalayan region remain relatively underexplored despite their rich ethnopharmacological significance [18, 19]. L. lanata, a traditionally used medicinal herb, is known for its antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties [28–30]. Despite its ethnopharmacological significance and rich phytochemical profile, L. lanata remains underexplored in nanobiotechnology. To date, no comprehensive scientific report is available on the use of the aerial parts of L. lanata for the green synthesis of AgNPs and their evaluation for antioxidant and antimicrobial activities. This study aims to bridge this knowledge gap by investigating the potential of L. lanata for AgNPs biosynthesis and assessing their biomedical relevance, thereby opening new perspectives in the field of plant-based nanomaterials.

The objective of the current study was to produce, describe, and evaluate the biological activity of silver nanoparticles derived from the aerial extract for medical applications, given the importance of L. lanata.

Materials and methods

Collection and identification of plant material

The plant material was collected from Karnaprayag, Uttarakhand, India (30.2575° N, 79.2466° E) in October 2022. The collection of plant material was carried out in compliance with applicable local and national regulations, and no specific permits were required. The plant material was collected from private land and not from any protected or Forest land. A voucher specimen (No. 1203) was deposited at the Botanical Survey of India, Dehradun, India. The scientific name of the plant material used in this report is Leucas lanata Benth. According to the International Plant Names Index (IPNI), the authoritative international database for plant names, the name was validly published as Leucas lanata Benth., Pl. Asiat. Rar. (Wallich) 1: 61 (1830) (IPNI record: https://www.ipni.org/n/449404-1; LSID: urn: lsid: ipni.org: names:449404-1).

Preparation of Leucas lanata plant extract

Leucas lanata fresh aerial parts were cleaned thrice with tap water before being rinsed with distilled water to remove any dust or other visible particles from the leaves’ surface. It took ten to twenty days in May for this aerial portion to dry at room temperature in the shade. After that, the dried plant was finely ground into a powder. 20 g of plant powder and 500 mL of water were heated to 60 °C for 20 min to produce an aqueous leaf extract. After filtering the extract using Whatman filter paper No. 1, it was stored at 4 °C for subsequent use [31].

Green synthesis of AgNPs

To synthesize AgNPs, 1 mL of L. lanata extract was added individually to 7 mL, 8 mL, 9 mL, 10 mL, and 11 mL of aqueous solutions containing 1 mM (millimolar), 2 mM, 3 mM, 4 mM, and 5 mM AgNO3 (99.0% pure), respectively. The reaction mixture was centrifuged for 20 min at 4000 rpm and then dried at 60 °C to produce the AgNPs, which exhibited a dark brown colour at 5 mM and 1:10 AgNO₃ concentrations.

Characterization of AgNPs nanoparticle

Using a UV-VIS spectrophotometer (JASCO V-650), the absorbance of AgNPs in ultrapure water was measured. Spectral analysis was performed between 300 and 600 nm. The essential information regarding the synthesis of AgNPs is provided by the absorption peak at 400–500 nm [32]. AgNPs were analyzed by FT-IR (Fourier transform infrared) spectroscopy between 4000 and 400 cm−1 (NICOLET 6700, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany). AgNPs’ purity and percentage composition were examined using EDX (Energy Dispersive X-ray). The phase composition and structural analysis of the generated product were ascertained by utilizing X-ray beam diffraction (XRD, Bruker, D8 Advance, Germany) at room temperature with CuKα (λ = 1.5406 Å and step size = 0.02°) radiation in the scattering angle of 10–80°. The secondary electron mode of field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, Carl Zeiss, Ultra Plus) was used to examine the surface microstructure and grain size of the produced nanoparticles. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), integrated with the FE-SEM, was used to determine the chemical composition of the representative samples. The diameter of the produced AgNPs was measured, and their shape was determined using HETEM analysis. Ethanol was used to dissolve the material. A thin dispersion drop was put on a “staining mat.” A copper grid coated with Lacey carbon was placed within the drop and coated side up. After approximately 30 min of sonication, the grid was removed, air-dried for an additional 30 min, and then examined using a JEOL 2100 Transmission Electron Microscope.

Antioxidant properties

Using the DPPH (97% pure) free radical scavenging assay technique, the antioxidant activity of the produced AgNPs was evaluated. In UV-Vis (Ultraviolet-Visible) spectroscopy, the deep-violet DPPH solution in methanol exhibits an absorption peak at 517 nm. An electron from an antioxidant causes it to lighten and turn yellow [33, 34]. 1 mL of DPPH alcoholic solution was combined with an equivalent volume of L. lanata extract or L-AgNPs at varying concentrations, and the mixture was incubated for 30 min at room temperature. One common antioxidant is ascorbic acid. 1 mL of methanol was added to the reaction mixture in place of the extract or sample to obtain a control absorption.

The modified FRAP method described by Benzie and Strain [35] was used to perform the FRAP assay. The stock solution consisted of a 20 mM FeCl3·6H2O (99% pure) solution in distilled. water, 300 mM acetate buffer at pH 3.6, and a 10 mM TPTZ (2,4,6-tripyridyl-striazine,99% pure) solution in 40 mM HCl. Next, FeCl3·6H2O (2.5 mL) was combined with acetate buffer (25 mL) and TPTZ (99% pure, 2.5 mL). Prior to use, the solution’s temperature was increased to 37 °C. Under dark conditions, 150 µL of plant extract and 2.85 mL of FRAP solution were incubated for 30 min. At 593 nm, the absorbance was measured. The standard curve, between 100 and 500 µg/ mL FeSO4, was linear. The results were compared with ascorbic acid standards and expressed as µM Fe(II)/g dry mass.

According to Ferreira-Santos et al. [36], the extracts’ capacity to scavenge ABTS radicals was evaluated. In 100 milliliters of distilled water, 0.360 g of 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) diammonium salt were dissolved to create a 7 mM ABTS ) stock solution. 0.066 g of the salt was dissolved in 100 mL of distilled water to create a 2.45 mM potassium persulfate (98% pure, K2S2O8) solution separately. The 2.45 mM K2S2O8 and 7 mM ABTS solutions were mixed in equal volumes (10 mL each) to create the radical cation. The reaction mixture was allowed to sit at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) in the dark for 12 h to achieve full radical production and absorbance stability.

A UV-Vis spectrophotometer was used to confirm that the working solution had an absorbance of 0.700 ± 0.020 at 734 nm when the resultant concentrated ABTS⁺• solution was diluted 1:1 (v/v) with absolute ethanol. To assess antioxidant capacity, L. lanata extract or L-AgNPs were used at concentrations of 100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 µg/mL. For two hours, the reaction mixtures were allowed to sit at room temperature in the dark. Using spectrophotometry, a drop in absorbance at 734 nm was observed. Appropriate blanks and controls were utilized.

Antibacterial activity

Initially, a suspension culture of selected bacteria was prepared in Nutrient Broth and incubated at 37 °C for 24–36 h, after which adequate growth was observed in the liquid broth. 20 µL of culture was taken and spread on a petri plate for further processing. The antibacterial susceptibility of L-AgNPs against Salmonella abony, Bacillus subtilis, Staphylococcus aureus, and Escherichia coli was evaluated using the agar well diffusion method. A sterile glass spreader dipped in the bacterial culture’s inoculum suspension was applied to the hardened nutrient agar plates, and the agar surface was then thoroughly cleaned. After loading the different concentrations of the produced L-AgNPs solution into the bored wells, the Petri plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C to examine the inhibition zone. The assay’s standard was streptomycin at 10 µg/mL. The diameter of the inhibitory zone was measured in mL and compared to the control [37].

MIC (minimum inhibitor concentration) assay

A tube dilution approach was used to determine the MIC. The investigated bacterial species were cultured for 24 h and then diluted to 108 CFU mL 1 using the 0.5 McFarland standard in 10 mL of tryptic soy broth (TSB) to produce an inoculum. Plant extracts at a range of strengths, from 5000 g/mL to 10.0 g/mL, were made in a series of culture tubes using DMSO. Each tube received 0.1 mL of the bacterial cell suspension, which was then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The development of the inoculum was ascertained by measuring the turbidity of the broth. The lowest extract concentration that inhibited the development of the test organism was known as the minimum inhibitory concentration, or MIC [38, 39].

Results and discussion

UV–VIS (ultraviolet–visible) spectroscopy

The reaction mixture’s colour shift from light brown to dark brown, due to surface plasma resonances, was the leading indicator of AgNP production. UV–VIS spectrum of the colloidal solution of AgNPs was then taken, in which a peak at 384 nm was observed for L-AgNPs, as shown in Fig. 1. This is similar to the AgNPs mediated by the Fusarium exquisite, Fusarium solani, and Ficus carica extracts. Several biomolecules and functional groups (Fig. 2) found in L. lanata extract facilitate the bio-reduction and capping of synthetic L-AgNPs [40, 41].

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

X-ray diffraction was employed to ascertain the crystalline structure of the bio-synthesized L-AgNPs. Five peaks were found at 38.09°, 44.18°, 64.41°, 77.33°, and 81.44° in the XRD pattern, as shown in Fig. 3, which is also represented by the SAED pattern. These peaks correspond to the [1 1 1], [2 0 0], [2 2 0], [3 1 1], and [2 2 2] planes, respectively. This result confirmed the cubic crystalline structure of L-AgNPs, as supported by Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards file numbers 040783 and 021098, as reported previously. According to XRD, the crystallite size of the synthesized L-AgNPs is estimated using the Debye-Scherrer formula and ranges from 10 nm to 75 nm. In comparison, the average crystallite size is approximately 25.37 nm, which is also vindicated by HRTEM result [42–46].

EDAX (energy dispersive analysis of X-rays)

EDAX was used to determine the chemical composition of the biomimetic synthesized L-AgNPs. The absorption peak for silver nanoparticles is generally believed to occur at 2.7–3 keV. In Fig. 4, a peak at 2.7 keV was observed, indicating the presence of silver nanoparticles. Apart from this, signals for C and Cl were also obtained in EDAX, indicating that the phytochemical present in the extract of L. lanata is responsible for the reduction of Ag+ ions and the stability of AgNPs. The C-Cl group in the L. lanata biomolecule is responsible for the significantly lower Cl level, as confirmed by FTIR. The other reason is that the LR-grade AgNO3 used in the synthesis of silver nanoparticles contained 0.001% chlorine as an impurity. That could be a possible source of chlorine. These findings are in good agreement with the published research [47–51].

Fourier transform infrared analysis

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) analysis helps determine which functional groups are present in the biomolecules in the L. lanata extract, which is essential for Ag+ ion reduction, AgNP capping, and AgNP stability. Various sharp bands were obtained in the FTIR of L-AgNPs between 3900 cm−1 and 532 cm−1 (Fig. 7). The band near 3900 cm−1 is due to N-H stretching, while the band at 3492 cm−1 is due to O–H stretching vibration, indicating the presence of alcoholic and phenolic groups. The minor band observed at 2917 cm−1 is due to C–H stretching in an alkane. The band observed at 2376 cm−1 corresponds to the ≡ C–C stretching vibration. The shared band at 1638 cm−1 indicates the presence of a C=O or C=C stretching vibration in the aromatic ring. The bands found at 1382 cm−1 and 1113 cm−1 are due to the C–O, N–H stretching vibration of the amide linkage, while the bands found at 844 cm−1 are due to the C-H banding of alkenes. The band at 532 cm−1 is attributed to C-Cl or C-Br stretching vibrations, as confirmed by EDAX. These bands indicate the presence of phytochemicals in the plant extract, which play a vital role in the formation process and act as capping and stabilizing agents [55–63]. As shown in Fig. 7, the reduced intensity of the L-AgNPs spectrum compared to the plant extract may be attributed to the involvement of phytochemicals in the reduction and stabilisation of Ag+ ions. The binding of these biomolecules to the nanoparticle surface reduces spectral intensity.

Figure 7 showed the FT-IR spectra of Lantana camara-mediated silver nanoparticles (L-AgNPs) and the plant extract. The spectra reveal key differences between the two peaks: the plant extract shows broad peaks at approximately 3492 cm−1 (O–H stretching from phenols/alcohols), 2917 cm−1 (C–H stretching), and 1646 cm−1 (C=O stretching from carbonyls), while the L-AgNPs spectrum exhibits reduced intensity in the peaks. There is shifted the intensity peaks from 1646 to 1652, 1392 to1389, in L-AgNPs there is no any peaks between 3000 and 2000 cm−1, but in Lantana camara there is peaks between 3000 and 2000 cm−1. the reduced intensity in L-AgLNPs indicating the involvement of plant phytochemicals (phenolics, flavonoids, proteins) as reducing and capping agents during nanoparticle synthesis.

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy analysis

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) plays a crucial role in determining the morphology of AgNPs, including their size and shape. Figure 8 shows the particles of L-AgNPs at different magnifications. L-AgNPs have a spherical shape, but due to agglomeration, they also have rod-like morphology, which is also confirmed by the FESEM image (Fig. 8a). For the average particle size, a total of 44 L-AgNPs were sampled to get the average particle size, for which the Image J software was used. The particle size of L-AgNPs ranges from 10 nm to 112 nm. However, particles with more than 50 nm are very small, and the average particle size is 28.59 nm, which is also verified by XRD (Fig. 8e). In the SEAD pattern of synthesized L-AgNPs, eight spots are found that represent specific crystal planes. Dynamic light scattering (DLS)

The size distribution and zeta potential of L-AgNPs were ascertained by DLS analysis. The zeta potential can be used to assess the stability of AgNPs. The zeta potential gives information about the charge of the phytochemicals acting as capping reagents. The higher the charge on the surface, the greater the repulsion force existing between the synthesized L-AgNPs, due to which the aggregation of L-AgNPs will decrease and the dispersity of nanoparticles will increase in the medium. The zeta potential value of synthesized L-AgNPs was − 25.4mV, as shown in Fig. 9a. According to previous research, a voltage of 30 mV is considered the most stable range for nanosuspensions. It also confirms that synthesized L-AgNPs are relatively stable and exhibit good colloidal stability. In contrast, Fig. 9b shows the particle size distribution of L-AgNPs, which shows that particles are of a higher particle size of more than 100 nm as compared to the particle size suggested by XRD and HRTEM. The difference is due to the sample preparation procedure. In the DLS method, the size of L-AgNPs is reported as the hydrodynamic diameter, which is why the DLS-measured particle size is higher than that predicted by XRD and HRTEM [63–68].

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant activity was determined using the DPPH assay. In this case, ascorbic acid was taken as a control. The antioxidant activity of ascorbic acid, L. lanata plant extract, and silver nanoparticles made from L. lanata is completely dose-dependent towards free radicals like DPPH, as shown in Fig. 10a. The scavenging activity of DPPH of plant extract was lower than that of synthesized L-AgNPs and almost equal to that of ascorbic acid at a high concentration of 500 µg/mL. The %RAS values for the plant extract and L-AgNPs at 100 µg/mL were 38% and 45%, respectively. For a maximum concentration of 500 µg/mL, the %RAS values were 81% and 93%, respectively. The IC50 value for the plant extract of L. lanata was 154 µg/mL, whereas for L-AgNPs, it was found to be 49 µg/mL, as shown in Fig. 10b, which is significantly lower compared to the L. lanata plant extract.

This result is also confirmed by results obtained for silver nanoparticles mediated by Cucumis prophetarum [69–71]. The aqueous extract of L. lanata and L-AgNPs demonstrated their ABTS radical scavenging activity in Fig. 10c. When compared to aqueous plant extract, L-AgNPs exhibit a larger proportion of ABTS scavenging activity. At lower concentrations of 100 µg/mL, L-AgNPs exhibit 12.26% inhibition, whereas at higher concentrations of 500 µg/mL, they exhibit 59.92% inhibition. Nonetheless, the plant’s aqueous extract exhibits 8.75% at 100 µg/mL and 53.17% at 500 µg/Ll. Figure 10d showed the FRAP assay of L-AgNPs and the aqueous extract of L. lanata. L-AgNPs have a higher FRAP than aqueous plant extract. L-AgNPs show 21.56 ± 0.17 µM at a lower concentration of 100 µg/mL and 83.31 ± 0.15 µM at higher values of 500 µg/mL. However, at a lower concentration of 100 µg/mL, the aqueous solution of the plant extract shows 14.29 ± 0.11, whereas at a higher concentration of 500 µg/mL, it shows 69.54 ± 0.21. The collective findings show that silver nanoparticles exhibit better radical scavenging activity than the plant extract. This suggests that metallic nanoparticles enhance the capacity to neutralise reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby mitigating intracellular oxidative stress. This suppression of oxidative stress reduces damage to biomolecules and the progression of pathologies associated with oxidative stress. These results are in good agreement with previous studies [72–74].

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity was tested using two gram-positive and two gram-negative pathogens: S. ebony, B. subtilis, E. coli, and S. aureus as positive and negative controls, respectively. Streptomycin and all bacterial cultures were diluted to 108 CFU/ml and spread on agar plates for incubation at 37 °C for 24 h to measure the inhibition zones. DMSO were employed as observed in Figs. 11 and 12. The activity of the produced L-AgNPs against gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria was nearly identical. Silver nanoparticles derived from the peels of Citrus sinensis and Acacia leucophloea also showed results comparable to those of the other methods [75, 76]. The table displays the inhibition zone at various concentrations. Compared to L-AgNPs, the inhibitory zone of the plant extract is smaller. At the lowest concentration of L-AgNPs, B. subtilis exhibited the highest inhibitory zone, measuring 10 mm (Table 1). When bacteria and silver ions interact, it inhibits essential processes such as DNA replication, respiratory enzyme function, and ATP synthesis, ultimately leading to cell death [77, 78].

MIC (minimum inhibitory concentration) against bacteria

Figure 13 shows that the aqueous extract of L. lanata exhibited effects between 0.45 and 0.84 mg/mL, whereas L-AgNPs showed effects between 0.09 and 0.34 mg/mL across the bacteria. With a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 0.84 mg/mL, the plant extract in aqueous solution showed the most potent effects against S. abony. B. subtilis was inhibited with a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 0.76 mg/mL, whereas E. coli showed the strongest action with the lowest MIC value of 0.45 mg/mL. MIC 0.68 mg/mL, is moderate for S. aureus. With a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 0.09 mg/mL, L-AgNPs exhibited the most notable efficacy against E. coli because the cell membrane of gram-negative bacteria has a thick lipopolysaccharide (LPS) layer in their cell wall with a thin peptidoglycan layer whereas, gram-positive bacterial cell wall composition is vice versa, and presence of thick peptidoglycan layer reduces the penetration of silver nanoparticles. MIC and inhibition zone are strongly correlated with the generation of reactive oxygen species induced by silver nanoparticles. Free silver ions are taken up by cells, leading to increased oxidative stress and damage to bacterial membranes, proteins, lipids, and DNA, ultimately killing bacteria [79, 80]. The MIC value for the second activity against S. aureus was 0.16 mg/mL. B. subtilis and S. abony, on the other hand, exhibit 0.34 and 0.24 mg/mL, respectively. Consequently, L-AgNPs exhibit a MIC value that is noticeably higher than that of the plant’s aqueous extract.

Limitations and future scope of the study

The study has some limitations that warrant further investigation. The exact mechanism by which phytochemicals interact to stabilize and cap the silver nanoparticles was not fully elucidated, leaving a gap in understanding the underlying processes. The antimicrobial activity was evaluated against a limited number of bacterial strains; expanding the investigation to include a broader range of microorganisms, such as fungi and drug-resistant strains, would provide more comprehensive insights into the nanoparticles’ efficacy. Additionally, the environmental impact of L. lanata-based silver nanoparticles, including their biodegradability and long-term stability within ecological systems, remains unexplored. The absence of in vivo studies to validate the in vitro antioxidant and antimicrobial efficacy represents another limitation, as such studies are critical for practical and clinical applications. Addressing these limitations in future research would significantly enhance the relevance and applicability of the study.

Conclusion

This study establishes L. lanata extract as an efficient biological system for the green synthesis of silver nanoparticles, yielding predominantly spherical AgNPs with an average particle size below 29 nm, a feature known to enhance surface reactivity and biological performance. The characteristic UV–VIS absorption peak at 384 nm and the crystalline nature confirmed by XRD and EDX analyses validate the successful formation and purity of L-AgNPs. The nanoparticles exhibited superior antioxidant activity across DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP assays, indicating an improved free-radical scavenging efficiency compared to the plant extract alone. Furthermore, the lower MIC values and consistent zones of inhibition observed against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial strains suggest enhanced antibacterial potency, particularly against B. subtilis. These findings imply that phytochemical-mediated surface functionalization contributes synergistically to the bioactivity of L-AgNPs, rather than solely through size effects. Nevertheless, the study is limited to in vitro biological assessments, and the exact molecular interactions responsible for the enhanced activity remain unexplored. Future work should therefore include mechanistic studies, cytotoxicity evaluation, and in vivo validation to assess the translational potential of L-AgNPs in biomedical applications.
