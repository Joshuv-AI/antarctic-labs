Is the Cognitive Ability of Fawn‐Footed Mosaic‐Tailed Rat Melomys cervinipes Offspring Related to Mothers’ Care or Cognitive Abilities?
Authors: Misha K Rowell, Tasmin L Rymer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe cognitive abilities of many non‐human animals have been investigated in recent years; however, outside of laboratory rodent strains, it is largely unknown how direct and indirect genetic factors influence the development of cognition. In a previous study, we found no impact of maternal genetic or non‐genetic effects on problem solving in the fawn‐footed mosaic‐tailed rat Melomys cervinipes. However, cognition is multifaceted, and different forms of cognition may not all be influenced by the same factors. Therefore, we investigated whether maternal genetic and non‐genetic effects affected the development of multiple, different measures of cognition in the fawn‐footed mosaic‐tailed rat. We first measured the amount of maternal care mothers provided to their offspring and then tested mothers and offspring as adults in three tests: an associative memory test, a novel object recognition test, and a food extraction task (to assess learning). We assessed whether the maternal care provided by mothers influenced cognition and used parent‐offspring regressions to determine whether the cognitive abilities of individuals had a heritable component. Offspring that received more indirect care were significantly more likely to learn the lever task than offspring that received less indirect care, possibly because this task required more neurological processing for successful completion than the other tests. No other measure of maternal care significantly influenced offspring cognition, and offspring cognition did not appear to have a heritable component. This suggests that cognitive development in this species is likely quite flexible, and that maternal direct and indirect genetic effects may not play a significant role in the development of cognition.Keywords: learning, memory, problem solving, recognition, rodent

Introduction

Cognition, how animals gather, store, access, and use information, in all its forms, has important effects on fitness and survival (Rowe and Healy 2014; Shettleworth 2010), and several studies have focused on investigating its evolution and fitness benefits (Thornton and Truskanov 2022). However, for cognitive traits to evolve via natural selection, there needs to be sufficient variation between individuals and these traits should be heritable (Rowe and Healy 2014). Thus, investigating the heritability of cognition is important for fully understanding its fitness value and flexibility in response to changing environments (Croston et al. 2015).

Despite the importance of understanding the heritability of cognitive traits, it is often difficult to establish pedigrees of wild‐living species, or animals not maintained in captive breeding programmes. Thus, the heritability of cognition remains poorly studied in wild animals and non‐model species (Thornton and Lukas 2012). Furthermore, in species where the heritability of cognition has been studied (e.g., intelligence in chimpanzees Pan troglodytes, Hopkins et al. 2014), there is considerable variation in the strength of heritability estimates (e.g., narrow sense heritability h 2 , Hoffmann and Merilä 1999). For example, a meta‐analysis of a range of taxa (insects, birds, fish, mammals) found that the heritability estimates ranged from 0.08 to 0.91 (Croston et al. 2015). Heritability estimates can also vary across different domains of cognition and even within species, as seen in red junglefowl Gallus gallus (reversal learning: h 2 = 0.26, discriminative learning: h 2 = 0.00, cognitive judgment bias: h 2 = 0.09, Sorato et al. 2018). It is therefore likely that multiple factors affect an individual's cognitive ability (Thornton and Wilson 2015), including maternal effects.

Maternal effects (a form of indirect genetic effect, IGE, Wolf et al. 1998), in particular, may be important drivers of cognition as mothers often buffer offspring against external environmental conditions (e.g., huddling to protect from cold temperatures) and control what environmental stimuli offspring are exposed to (e.g., what food to eat, Bjorklund 2006). Consequently, the effects of maternal care on offspring neural development and cognitive ability have been well studied in laboratory rodents (Čater and Majdič 2022; Champagne 2008). For example, in Long‐Evans hooded rats Rattus norvegicus, mothers that frequently licked and groomed pups and exhibited arched‐back nursing raised offspring with increased N‐methyl‐d‐aspartate (NMDA) receptor binding and increased survival of hippocampal neurons, which increased hippocampal‐dependent spatial learning (Bredy et al. 2003) and memory (Liu et al. 2000). However, the influence of maternal care on offspring cognitive ability has not been well studied outside of laboratory rats and mice, and the few studies that have been conducted have shown mixed results. For example, California mouse Peromyscus californicus pups that received higher amounts of licking and grooming made fewer errors on the first day of reversal training in the Barnes maze, but other measures of cognition were not always affected by the amount of care received (Bredy et al. 2004). There was also no significant effect of maternal care on offspring innovation (i.e., solving a problem in one session without previous exposure) in the fawn‐footed mosaic‐tailed rat Melomys cervinipes (Rowell and Rymer 2022a). This limited understanding of the genetic factors and maternal effects that influence cognition currently makes it difficult to fully determine how cognition in many species evolves.

Therefore, we investigated the influence of maternal genetic and non‐genetic effects on different cognitive measures in a native Australian rodent, the fawn‐footed mosaic‐tailed rat. Mosaic‐tailed rats are commonly found in forests along the eastern coast of Australia (Moore et al. 2008), where they can cope with habitat disturbance (Laurance 1994) and solve novel problems (Rowell and Rymer 2023). They have a prolonged period of maternal care (Rowell and Rymer 2020a). We measured maternal care and then tested mothers and offspring (as adults) in three cognitive tests: an associative memory test, a novel object recognition (NOR) test, and a food extraction test. To assess whether cognition in offspring is heritable (i.e., direct genetic effect), we used parent‐offspring regressions. Due to the variation in heritability of cognition observed across other species (Croston et al. 2015) and even forms of cognition (Sorato et al. 2018), we made no a priori predictions on the heritability of cognition in mosaic‐tailed rats, despite previous results showing that problem solving is not heritable in this species (Rowell and Rymer 2022a). As maternal care, particularly huddling and grooming pups, affects neural development and subsequent cognitive ability in other rodent species (Bredy et al. 2004; Liu et al. 2000), we hypothesized that it would also affect mosaic‐tailed rat cognition. We predicted that offspring of mothers that showed higher levels of maternal care would perform better in the cognitive tasks (e.g., learn a scent cue faster) than offspring of mothers that showed lower levels of care.

Methods

Husbandry

During the maternal care component of this study (see below), we housed adult female mosaic‐tailed rats (n = 10) in separate glass tanks (44 × 35 x 32 cm) with their offspring (n = 22). Barricades (i.e., black plastic container lids) were placed between cages to reduce visual stimuli and provide simulated cover. The tanks were set up to allow mosaic‐tailed rats to exhibit natural behaviors and contained a base layer of approximately 5 cm of wood shavings, a cylindrical plastic nest box (11 ×11 x 20 cm) filled with nesting material (pasture hay and paper towel strips), sticks for climbing (mosaic‐tailed rats are scansorial; Callaway et al. 2018), and a cardboard roll for chewing. Environmental conditions were partially controlled (22°C–26°C, 50%–65% relative humidity), and a window provided natural lighting. We adjusted the food quantities given to animals while females were housed with their pups (based on pup size and age), with mosaic‐tailed rats receiving up to 10 g of mixed seeds and rodent chow (Vetafarm Origins) and up to 15 g of fruit/vegetable daily. Water was available ad libitum.

Pups were briefly removed from their mothers to collect behavioral and physical development data every second day from 3 days old (see Rowell and Rymer 2020a). Pups were immediately returned after measurements had been taken, with no adverse incidents occurring during these handling sessions. Tanks were not cleaned during the recording period (Day 1–12 of pups’ life) and were then only spot cleaned until weaning (i.e., removal of soiled patches of wood shavings or nesting material) to minimize stress. Mosaic‐tailed rats generally give birth to two offspring (max. 3 in our study). Females have four inguinal nipples, and pups nipple cling tenaciously from birth, thus there was likely little nutritional competition between pups. We weaned pups from their mothers at 21 days old (as per Yom‐Tov 1985) by removing mothers from the glass tanks and housing them individually in standard adult caging (see below). Tanks were cleaned at this time. Siblings were kept together until testing was completed at approximately 2 months old, as sexual maturation only occurs at approximately 5 months old (Rowell and Rymer 2020a).

We then used these individuals (n = 32) plus an additional six females and their adult offspring (n = 13) for the heritability component of this study (see below). Previous work has shown that individuals handled during early development do not perform differently from individuals not handled during this critical period (Rowell and Rymer 2022b). As all pups and mothers were treated/handled in the same manner, any differences in cognitive abilities were unlikely to be a consequence of differential treatment of mothers and pups. During the testing period, adult mosaic‐tailed rats were individually housed in standard housing, which consisted of wireframe cages (34.5 × 28 × 38 cm) attached to deep plastic bases (34.5 × 28 × 11 cm) that were filled with approximately 10 cm of wood shavings. Each cage contained the nesting and enrichment items described above for mothers in glass tanks, as well as a wire shelf to further facilitate climbing. We gave each individual 5 g of mixed seed and rodent chow, and 5 g vegetable/fruit daily, and water was provided ad libitum.

General Testing Procedures

Testing occurred between June 2018 and March 2021. All tests were conducted between 18 and 22 h (as this is the peak activity period of mosaic‐tailed rats; Wood 1971), and under red light, which does not interfere with mosaic‐tailed rat behaviors (Paulling et al. 2019; Rowell and Rymer 2020b). All tests were filmed using a Sony HDR‐CX405 Camcorder. Cognitive testing was conducted in a random order (see below), and all testing equipment was wiped with a 70% ethanol dilution after each individual. The video analysis software BORIS (http://www.boris.unito.it) was used to collect behavioral data (Friard and Gamba 2016).

Maternal Care

The maternal care data used here have also been used in Rowell and Rymer (2022a) and Rowell and Rymer (2024), which each had a different focus (problem solving and personality, respectively). Briefly, we recorded maternal care behavior over 6 sessions every second night as pups developed (from 2 days old to 12 days old). To do so, we briefly moved the mother and pups out from the nest box into a plastic container (less than 1 min) while we removed most of the material from the nest box (leaving only the plastic cylinder and some strips of paper towel). This allowed us to film maternal care behavior more clearly. The nest box remained in the home tank, and the mother and pups were then gently placed back in the front portion of the nest box, and we recorded the mother's behavior for 20 min (direct care: time spent huddling over the pups, and time spent licking and grooming pups; indirect care: time spent moving the nesting material (paper towel strips) around). Each of these behaviors were individually summed over all sessions, and all behaviors were also summed to generate a total measure of maternal care. We also calculated the average amount of care given to each pup by dividing each care measure by the number of pups in each litter (as we could not be certain which specific pup was receiving care at any time).

Cognitive Measures

Mosaic‐tailed rat cognition was measured in several ways as described in Rowell and Rymer (2022b), and part of the data used in that study is also used here, as described below. All individuals were tested as adults (more than 5 months old, Rowell and Rymer 2020a).

Associative Memory Test

This test assessed whether mosaic‐tailed rats could form and subsequently remember an association between a scent cue (vanilla, lemon, or peppermint oil) and a food reward (chocolate‐flavored breakfast cereal; Tronel and Sara 2002), and is fully described in Rowell and Rymer (2022a). Briefly, we first conducted two nights of pre‐training to allow mosaic‐tailed rats to habituate to the testing arena (Night 1) and sponges (Night 2). Training then occurred on Night 3 over five 5‐min trials (Figure 1), where small sponges were scented with the scented oil on each corner. The sponges had a small hole in the center where the food reward could be placed. During these sessions, we placed three sponges each scented with one of the different scents in random corners of the testing arena. One scent was randomly selected to be the association scent for each individual mosaic‐tailed rat, and the food reward was placed in the center of this sponge. We then placed a mosaic‐tailed rat in the empty corner of the arena and left it to explore until the food had been consumed or 5 min had elapsed. We then removed the individual from the arena, rebaited and reset the sponges to new positions, and repeated the trials. On Night 4, we tested each mosaic‐tailed rat once with the same scented sponges (un‐baited) from Night 3 to assess memory retention. We measured the number of errors made (nose pokes to the hole inside an incorrect sponge) and the latency to locate the correct sponge. An individual was considered to have learnt and remembered the association if it moved to the correct sponge quickly and made few errors. Individuals that did not interact with the correct sponge (n = 1) were given the maximum latency (300 s).

Set up of the associative learning task during the training sessions of fawn‐footed mosaic‐tailed rats Melomys cervinipes. Placement of scented sponges was random, and the association scent was randomized for each individual.

Recognition Test

We used the NOR test to assess the visual recognition and discrimination ability of the mosaic‐tailed rats (Figure 2), which is again fully described in Rowell and Rymer (2022b). Briefly, we first familiarized each individual to a novel object (i.e., sample object exposure phase) by placing it in the testing arena with two identical novel objects. The individual was left to explore for 10 min and then returned to its home cage to rest for 1 h. We then placed the mosaic‐tailed rat back into the arena with one familiar object and a new object for 2 min. We measured the proportion of time that individuals spent interacting with the novel object compared to the familiar object (i.e., the discrimination ratio). An individual was considered to have recognized that the novel object was different if it spent more time interacting with the novel object than the familiar object (i.e., a discrimination ratio closer to 1; Bevins and Besheer 2006).

Test configuration for the novel object recognition (NOR) test showing (a) sample object exposure and (b) novel object test, conducted using fawn‐footed mosaic‐tailed rats Melomys cervinipes. Choice of objects was randomized for each individual.

Learning in a Food‐Extraction Test

We presented a food‐baited puzzle board to the mosaic‐tailed rats for five consecutive nights to assess learning ability (Figure 3) as described in Rowell and Rymer (2022b). Briefly, the Trixie Dog Activity Board (level 2) had three types of puzzles: pillars (could be knocked over), tiles (could be slid to the side) and levers (individual had to move a lever to lift a flap). Sunflower seeds as rewards were placed under each puzzle type, and individuals had 30 min each night over five nights to try and solve the puzzles. We recorded how problem solving (i.e., how long it took the mosaic‐tailed rats to solve each puzzle type) changed across testing, with a decrease in solving time from Night 1 to Night 5 indicating learning. Due to the complexity of the tile and lever tasks, some individuals did not solve these problems (tile: n = 3; lever: n = 8) and were removed from the dataset as we could not determine if this was due to cognition or another factor (e.g., stress, lack of motivation).

The Trixie dog activity board with pillar, tile, and lever tasks presented to fawn‐footed mosaic‐tailed rats Melomys cervinipes (from Rowell and Rymer 2021).

Statistical Analyzes

Statistical analyzes were performed using R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team 2025) and RStudio version 2025.09.0 + 387 (Posit Team 2025). The model‐level significance was set at α = 0.05, and model residuals were tested for normality (Shapiro–Wilk test). In each model, we calculated Cohen's D effect size and power for every variable.

Maternal Care and Cognition Measures

We used separate general linear models or linear models to investigate how maternal care (direct and indirect), litter identity, and offspring sex (independent variables) influenced cognitive ability across multiple tests (associative memory: the latency to reach the trained sponge, recognition: proportional time spent investigating the novel object, and learning across multiple food‐extraction tests: Night 5 latency to solve puzzle divided by Night 1 latency to solve puzzle, with a value below less than 1 indicating learning) for the 10 litters where maternal care was measured. These behaviors were the dependent variables in the models. The latency to reach the trained sponge was square root transformed to better fit the assumptions of a linear model. We used a quasibinomial distribution in the model testing the proportional time spent investigating the novel object. We used general linear models for learning in the food‐extraction test models, and we removed the individuals that never solved the puzzles from the datasets (cylinder: n = 0, tile: n = 3, lever: n = 8).

Heritability of Cognition Measures

For all offspring used in this study, we calculated the heritability of cognition as for Rowell and Rymer (2022a) using parent‐offspring regressions to estimate the narrow‐sense heritability (h 2 ; Haldane 1996). The heritability of learning in the lever foraging extraction task could not be calculated due to the lower solving success of this task. For the other cognitive tests, we calculated the narrow‐sense heritability of the cognition measures by regressing the scaled averaged value for every litter (the mid‐offspring value) on the scaled value of the mothers (Keller et al. 2001) using the lmtest package (Zeileis and Hothorn 2002). We used the scale function to standardize each factor based on its mean and standard deviation, thereby allowing the beta coefficient to be calculated for each regression model (Cheng and Wu 1994). In each model, the average pup values were the dependent variables, and the mother values were the independent variables.

We used the beta coefficient of the parent‐offspring regressions to estimate the maternal genetic contribution for each cognition measure. Most females had already fallen pregnant before being transported to the laboratory, so we were not able to assess the problem‐solving abilities of fathers. As the genetic contribution from parents to offspring may not necessarily be equal (Changxin 1993), we could not assume that the maternal genetic contribution represented only ½ h 2 . Thus, we simply present the maternal h 2 (i.e., the beta coefficient) as we could not estimate total heritability values of cognition. We assumed that negative heritability estimates were zero (Robinson et al. 1955), but we still present them here, as suggested by Dudley and Moll (1969).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated narrow‐sense heritability (direct) and maternal care (indirect) effects on multiple aspects of cognition (learning, memory, and recognition) in the mosaic‐tailed rat. Similar to our previous findings with problem solving in this species (Rowell and Rymer 2022a), there were no significant relationships between the cognition of mothers and their offspring, suggesting that learning, memory, and recognition are not strongly heritable in mosaic‐tailed rats. This is not surprising as various types of cognition are often used across many different contexts (e.g., animals learn when foraging, navigating, and forming social relationships) and rarely in isolation of other cognitive processes (e.g., animals must remember what they learn to use this information), and it may therefore be difficult for selection to act on a single type of cognition (Rowe and Healy 2014). Instead, these forms of cognition are likely flexible (Croston et al. 2015), changing in response to environmental conditions and individual experiences (Tello‐Ramos et al. 2018). If cognition is indeed flexible, this could help mosaic‐tailed rats cope with environmental variation (Croston et al. 2015) as seen in other species. For example, species of birds capable of innovatively foraging were able to successfully invade new environments (Sol et al. 2002). However, given the small sample size (supported by the effect sizes calculated), it would be beneficial to specifically test for cognitive flexibility in the future.

We found a significant relationship between the amount of indirect care received and learning performance in the lever task, with a greater amount of indirect care being correlated with a larger change in the time taken to solve the lever task (i.e., values close to 0, indicating learning). Interestingly, we also noted that there was more variation in learning performance when indirect care was lower. The lever task was the most complex cognitive test we presented to mosaic‐tailed rats, as it required animals to move a different part (i.e., the lever) of the puzzle to what was blocking the food reward (i.e., the flap). As such, performance in this task may be more reliant on neurological structures and processes (e.g., hippocampal neurons required for learning complex associations, Nokia et al. 2012) than performance in the more basic tests that may have simply been completed using trial‐and‐error (Thornton and Samson 2012). The smaller amount of variation in solving performance at higher levels of care also suggest this possibility. Maternal care can impact offspring development (e.g., nest building improves offspring thermoregulation and growth, Weber and Olsson 2008), including hippocampal development and subsequent cognition (Bredy et al. 2003; Liu et al. 2000) in other species. Thus, it is not unexpected that maternal care may impact performance in more complex tasks, like the lever task, but not on simpler tasks, which has also been seen in other species (e.g., no effect on object recognition of female California mice offspring, Bredy et al. 2004). Due to the complexity of the task, however, the lever had the lowest solving rate/participation rate of all tests presented to the mosaic‐tailed rats (Rowell and Rymer 2020b, 2022b). Thus, again, the smaller sample size should be considered when interpreting the results, and further testing is recommended.

Contrary to our predictions, we found no significant influence of direct maternal care on any other measures of offspring cognition. This could possibly be due to the reproductive strategy of mosaic‐tailed rats. Unlike laboratory rodents that generally produce large litters (rats: 1–13 pups, Chahoud and Paumgartten 2009; house mice: 4–17 pups, Tanaka 2004), mosaic‐tailed rats produce small litters of 1–3 pups that nipple‐cling to the mother (Callaway et al. 2018). Because all mosaic‐tailed rat pups have access to a nipple without fighting for access, this likely results in less variation in the level of care each pup receives (both within and across litters) compared to laboratory rodent pups. Similar results for many measures of cognition have also been found in California mice (Bredy et al. 2004), which only have 2–3 pups in a litter and also display tenacious nipple clinging (Merritt 1978), further suggesting that the breeding strategy may be an important consideration when investigating maternal effects on the development of cognition. Another possibility is the environmental enrichment that all mosaic‐tailed rats received during development (e.g., sticks for climbing, nesting material, wheels for running), which could have moderated any potential detrimental neurological effects caused by variations in maternal care received (Bredy et al. 2004; Zhang et al. 2023), leading to no discernible effects in adult offspring. Further investigation into the effects of environmental enrichment on cognition would be beneficial in this species.

Conclusion

Taken together, these results suggest that learning, memory, and recognition in mosaic‐tailed rats are not likely to be significantly constrained by genetics or early life conditions (i.e., maternal care). These forms of cognition may therefore be flexible and may change in response to internal (e.g., hunger, Rowe and Healy 2014) and external (e.g., environmental, Leggio et al. 2005) conditions encountered later in life. If cognition is indeed flexible, this could be beneficial for mosaic‐tailed rats in their complex and variable rainforest environments (Griffin et al. 2017). However, it is important to note that a small sample size was used in the maternal care component of this study, and further work is required to better understand the effects of maternal care on cognition in this species. Future work investigating what other factors influence cognition in mosaic‐tailed rats (e.g., environmental quality, Leggio et al. 2005; hippocampal volume, Smulders et al. 1995), and whether cognitive performance is stable or variable over time (Dingemanse and Réale 2005), is also needed to properly understand the extent of this suggested flexibility.
