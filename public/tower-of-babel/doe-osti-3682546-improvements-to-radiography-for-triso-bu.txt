 ABSTRACT

Tristructural-isotropic (TRISO) fuel is an advanced nuclear fuel form that consists of a uranium-bearing
kernel surrounded by various coating layers to ensure fission product retention even in high-temperature
irradiation environments. To ensure proper fuel performance, it is critical to measure the key material
properties of each layer (e.g., thickness and density) to confirm that they meet fuel specifications. The
pyrolytic carbon buffer layer is intentionally manufactured to be porous so that it can accommodate
gaseous fission products while preventing stress buildup in nearby layers from kernel swelling under
irradiation. Unfortunately, conventional methods of measuring buffer density, such as mercury
porosimetry, have some critical shortcomings. These include the inability to measure buffer densities on a
per particle basis, the generation of hazardous enriched uranium/mercury waste, and dependence on
interrupted coating runs. Radiography has been proposed as an alternative method of determining buffer
density, but many challenges have been identified in initial efforts to develop the technique, the most
notable of which is the overestimation of buffer layer density. These challenges present clear directions
for future work. In this work, the primary focus was modifying previous radiography approaches to
measure buffer layer density on a per particle basis. Although significant improvements were made, the
overestimation of the buffer density remains a challenge due to limitations associated with sample
preparation.

 1. IMPORTANCE OF BUFFER DENSITY MEASUREMENT

Tristructural-isotropic (TRISO) fuel particles are an advanced nuclear fuel developed specifically for
advanced reactor types: high-temperature gas-cooled reactors and small modular reactors. TRISO was
developed in response to a pressing need for a fuel form capable of withstanding higher in-core operating
temperatures and ensuring local fission product retention. TRISO typically consists of five layers: kernel,
buffer, inner pyrolytic carbon, SiC, and outer pyrolytic carbon. Each layer has a specific function.

The buffer layer provides space for gaseous fission products and accommodates kernel swelling under
irradiation, both of which are necessary to prevent the accumulation of stress within nearby layers. To
accomplish these tasks, the buffer is intentionally synthesized to be approximately 50% porous, which
corresponds to a density of approximately 1 g/cm3 [1]. Buffer densities for TRISO are conventionally
determined using mercury porosimetry, by which pressure (up to 50 psi) is applied to a container filled
with buffer-coated TRISO particles. The density is then calculated using the measured volume of these
particles, which is based on the mass and volume of the cell and the known density of mercury at a
specific temperature [2]. The extraction of buffer density requires the subtraction of kernel mass and
volume. This creates a large associated uncertainty. Although this technique can provide useful insight
into the pore size and distribution within buffer layers, it has several critical shortcomings: It generates
mixed high-assay low-enriched uranium/mercury waste, it provides only a bulk buffer density
measurement, and it relies on interrupted TRISO coating runs [3]. Radiography has been proposed as an
alternative method to determine TRISO buffer density because it provides interparticle statistics and
avoids the shortcomings associated with mercury porosimetry [1]. Radiography utilizes the fact that
x-rays attenuate differently through materials with varying atomic numbers and thicknesses, enabling
both qualitative and quantitative analysis of the buffer layer. Improving upon previous radiography
methods to extract key material properties (e.g., density) from the buffer layer is critical for providing
feedback to fabricators to enable a better understanding of the effects of buffer density distribution on
TRISO performance.

2.1 THEORY OF RADIOGRAPHY DENSITY MEASUREMENT

Previous work was conducted at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) to explore the viability of using
radiography to determine buffer layer density. It was hypothesized that a calibration curve could be
generated by analyzing the transmitted intensities of x-rays through a material of known density at
various thicknesses, such as grafoil. This curve would enable the extraction of information from materials
with the same chemical makeup—in this case, the carbon buffer layer in TRISO [1]. This theory hinged
on the Beer–Lambert law, which states that the transmitted intensities of x-rays through a material depend
on the initial x-ray intensity (I0) and the material thickness (t) and linear attenuation coefficient (µ), as

2.2 RADIOGRAPHY RESULTS

Prior radiography measurements were performed on thin spherical segments through the midplane of
TRISO particles. A single TRISO particle from either Zr-X05-33T or ZrX05-31T surrogate batches,
which were produced for the Advanced Gas Reactor Fuel Development and Qualification (AGR)
Program at ORNL, was mounted in Crystalbond Clear 509 and was then ground and polished down to a
desired thickness of 100–200 μm to maximize the unoccluded area of the buffer layer for imaging. The
extracted buffer densities ranged from 0.741 to 2.322 g/cm3 between the two batches, and the average
buffer density measurement was 1.782 g/cm3 [1]. It was noted that this range in densities and significantly
higher average density could be due to many factors: uncertainty in sample thickness due to sample tilt

during imaging, encapsulation of pucks in Kapton tape potentially altering x-ray attenuation through the
particle, instability of the x-ray source during imaging, and, most notably, the infiltration of epoxy into
pores within the buffer layer due to insufficient final acetone cleaning of the samples prior to imaging [1].

3.1 GRAFOIL CALIBRATION

Grafoil sheets of a single thickness (0.005 in.) with a purity of > 99.8% metals basis were purchased from
ThermoScientific and used for generation of the calibration curve. Calibration standards ranging from
0.005 to 0.015 in. thick were produced by cutting small rectangular areas from a 0.0005 in. thick grafoil
sheet and stacking them on top of each other. To ensure that rectangular standards were in contact
throughout imaging (because nonparallel grafoil standards would alter the transmitted intensities), each
corner of the standards was folded inward and pressed together tightly.

Due to slight differences in the way grafoil sheets are manufactured and the importance of an accurate
standard density for buffer layer analysis, the density of the grafoil sheet used to make the standards was
independently determined. A single large grafoil sheet was carefully folded, and the mass was weighed on
a balance accurate to ± 2 × 10−4 g. To determine grafoil volume, a caliper was used to measure the

 3

thickness, and a ruler accurate to 1/8 in. was used to measure the side lengths. Three small rectangular
sections of grafoil were cut to weigh approximately 0.15 g and were imaged to determine the transmitted
intensity of x-rays through grafoil sheets 127, 254, and 381 μm thick, which encapsulated the full range of
TRISO particle thicknesses imaged, each with a measured density of 1.09 g/cm3. Two grafoil calibration
curves were generated, each using different source-to-sample and sample-to-detector distances, to
improve radiograph quality and explore the effect of imaging parameters on buffer densities. For both
calibration curves, a source accelerating voltage of 40 kV was selected to maximize the attenuation by the
low-Z carbon buffer layer with a maximum power of 8 W and an imaging time of 15 s. The first set of
imaging conditions tested was a source-to-sample distance of 96.14 mm, a sample-to-detector distance of
27.82 mm, and a 20X objective, resulting in a pixel size of 0.8822 µm. The second set of imaging
conditions tested was a source-to-sample distance of 97.67 mm, a sample-to-detector distance of
49.7 mm, and a 20X objective, resulting in a pixel size of 1.0417 µm. Each calibration image was
referenced to the preceding reference image once the source became stable. Based on Eq. (3), the linear fit
through these points had a slope equal to µ/p, which was found to be 1.8983 cm2/g and 1.613 cm2/g for

3.2 EPOXY THICKNESS CALIBRATION

To accurately determine TRISO buffer density using Eq. (4), it is critical to know the thickness of each
TRISO particle analyzed within a single slice because x-ray attenuation depends heavily on thickness.
Due to an approximate 100 µm thickness variation within each slice—a result of an uneven application of
pressure during the grinding and polishing process required for sample preparation—a single caliper
measurement could not accurately represent the thickness of each TRISO particle analyzed. Additionally,
because multiple particles were mounted on a single slice, a side-view radiograph of the slice would not
accurately represent the thickness of all TRISO particles. Therefore, a new calibration method was
explored to determine the thickness of each TRISO particle within the slice. Specifically, fiducial marks
approximately 200, 300, and 400 µm thick were etched near the outer circumference of three blank slices
that did not contain particles. Caliper measurements were taken directly above each fiducial mark, and
radiograph images were taken at the same locations using a 20X objective lens to generate a relationship

The calibration curve generated using the 20X objective yielded a slope of 17.74 and a y-intercept of 0
with a much-improved R2 value of 0.9957. This significant improvement compared with the initial

more heterogenous than the epoxy farther away from particles. Therefore, fiducial material near particles
should be implemented to calibrate particle thicknesses. For example, small particles of a material of
known density and low Z, such as alumina, could be placed near TRISO particles to extract particle
thicknesses without significantly affecting the x-ray attenuation. It is important to note that although the
initial calibration encapsulated particle thicknesses ranging from 180 to 480 µm, the re-performed and
improved calibration was developed using a single slice so that a smaller range of TRISO thicknesses
(~260 to ~410 µm) was encapsulated. However, because the slice was broken in half, it is expected that
thicker TRISO particles near the slice edge and thinner particles near the slice center were properly
accounted for using the fiducial marks.

 4. CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

Although several challenges were identified with this modified approach used for radiography
measurement of the buffer density in TRISO particles, a few improvements were made. The imaging
system used for this buffer radiography study, the Micro-XCT 400, was significantly more stable than the
Versa 620 system used in ORNL’s previous study. This improved the reliability in transmitted intensities
through the buffer layer. The use of epoxy was avoided for the TRISO mounting process, and
significantly more TRISO particles were successfully introduced into spherical slices, improving particle
statistics and enabling faster radiograph analysis. Additionally, an epoxy calibration was introduced to
determine TRISO thickness. This approach does not depend on side-view radiographs of TRISO spherical
sections, in which slight tilting affected the transmitted intensities, reducing the repeatability and
reliability of visual identification of the unoccluded region within the buffer layer. The epoxy thickness
calibration provided a reasonable estimate of TRISO thickness if the measured transmitted intensities
were taken from a single slice that also contained the TRISO particles to be analyzed; however, using
multiple blank standards was not sufficient.

If determination of buffer density through radiography analysis of TRISO cross sections is to be pursued
further, several shortcomings should be addressed. Due to challenges associated with the generation of
calibration curves using epoxy standards of different thicknesses, a reference standard of known density
should be placed near each TRISO particle within the slice, which would allow for the confirmation of
TRISO thickness through analysis of transmitted intensity through a homogenous reference standard
instead of a heterogenous one like the epoxy that contained dispersed silicon glass fibers. It could also

 11

prove beneficial to confirm the usefulness in radiography analysis of layer densities using a known
reference material with less heterogeneity, such as SiC. This would confirm that nearby layers of different
atomic numbers do not significantly affect the way in which x-rays attenuate through the layers of
interest.

Overall, even after extensive sample cleaning, the generation of an epoxy thickness calibration, the use of
a stable CT source, and the implementation of various imaging parameters, the modified method for
buffer density measurements still encountered challenges that were not resolved in the second evaluation.
Due to difficulties with buffer heterogeneity and porosity, which result in significant artifact uptake
during the polishing process, an alternative sample preparation process should be explored. It is evident
that mechanically polishing and grinding TRISO slices to the desired thickness results in significant high-
density fragment infiltration into the buffer region; therefore, milling techniques using either a xenon or
gallium plasma focused ion beam should be explored. This sample preparation method has the potential to
reduce the infiltration of uranium and other epoxy minerals into the buffer region because it does not
depend on the mechanical grinding of TRISO. Additionally, the deposition of material into the buffer
region due to sputtering from the ion beam could be minimized by meticulous control of the sample and
plasma beam orientation.
