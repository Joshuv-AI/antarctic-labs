The Same Homozygous Pathogenic Variant in CHAT Underlies Lethal Fetal Akinesia Syndrome in Three Xhosa South African Fetuses
Authors: Jade Ramini, Cumine van Tonder, Yolandi Swart, Kerry Rademan, Samantha Budhram, Lut Geerts, Shahida Moosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Summary What is already known about this topic?◦Biallelic pathogenic variants in CHAT cause congenital myasthenic syndrome (CMS), typically presenting postnatally with episodic apnea and neuromuscular weakness. CHAT encodes choline acetyltransferase, essential for acetylcholine synthesis at the neuromuscular junction, and previously has not been associated with uniformly lethal prenatal phenotypes.What does this study add?◦We demonstrate that biallelic CHAT variants can cause severe fetal akinesia resulting in lethal multiple pterygium syndrome, expanding the phenotypic spectrum to include presentation, lethality and supporting inclusion of CHAT in fetal akinesia gene panels. Keywords: CHAT, exome sequencing, fetal akinesia, lethal multiple pterygium syndrome, South Africa

Fetal Phenotype

All three fetuses were of Xhosa ancestry and pregnancies were characterized by decreased fetal movement. Fetuses 1 and 2 were evaluated at the Fetal Medicine Unit, where antenatal ultrasounds demonstrated a recognizable pattern consistent with fetal akinesia and athrogryposis multiplex congenita: micrognathia (2/2), nuchal edema (2/2), narrow chest (2/2), and multiple fixed flexion contractures at all joints (2/2) (Table 1).

Diagnostic Method

Comprehensive clinical examinations were undertaken following delivery (Images of Fetus 2 ‐Figure 1A‐D) and post‐natal findings were correlated with antenatal ultrasound imaging, in the two prenatally detected cases. DNA was extracted from post‐mortem fetal skin biopsies. Singleton exome sequencing was performed as described previously [1].

Diagnostic Results and Interpretation

A homozygous pathogenic variant in CHAT, NM_020549.5: c.605 T > G (p.Met202Arg) (see VariantValidatorReport) [2], was identified in the three unrelated Xhosa fetuses (Table 2). For Fetus 1 and 2, the variant was located in a run of homozygosity (ROH) of 9 and 2kb, respectively. This missense variant is rare in population databases (gnomAD 86/1613622 heterozygotes, 0 homozygotes) with an allele frequency of 0.00005330. Notably, the African/African American allele frequency is 0.001053. There are 5 other Xhosa carriers in our in‐house database. In silico prediction tools predict a deleterious impact on the protein (e.g., REVEL = 0.94). It has been reported in ClinVar (ID: 430062) underlying familial infantile myasthenia (OMIM 254210). It affects a highly conserved residue within the active site tunnel of CHAT. Functional studies have demonstrated a severe reduction in catalytic activity, with < 10% residual enzymatic function [3].

Pregnancy Outcome

Two fetuses were observed within our multidisciplinary prenatal genetic service. Parents were counseled regarding the ultrasound findings and poor prognosis associated with severe fetal akinesia, informed by prior clinical experience of in utero demise in similar cases. Following counseling, both pregnancies were medically terminated, and postmortem genetic testing was requested. Fetus 3 presented following reduced fetal movement around 28 weeks of gestation and was diagnosed with intrauterine fetal demise. The mother consented to postmortem genetic testing after counseling by the medical genetics team.

Discussion

Fetal akinesia deformation sequence (FADS) encompasses a heterogeneous group of severe disorders characterized by reduced or absent fetal movement, leading to secondary anomalies including arthrogryposis, pterygia, pulmonary hypoplasia, craniofacial abnormalities, and fetal or neonatal demise. Lethal multiple pterygium syndrome (LMPS) represents the most severe end of this spectrum. The underlying mechanism typically involves disruption of neuromuscular transmission during critical developmental periods [4].

To date, pathogenic variants in several genes involved in neuromuscular transmission, including CHRNG, CHRNA1, CHRND, MUSK, RAPSN, and DOK7, have been implicated in both lethal fetal phenotypes and non‐lethal congenital myasthenic syndromes (CMS), highlighting a continuum of disease severity depending on the timing and extent of neuromuscular impairment. In contrast, biallelic pathogenic variants in CHAT have classically been associated with non‐lethal CMS, characterized by episodic aponia, respiratory distress, swallowing difficulty, hypotonia, and muscle weakness presenting in the neonatal or infantile period rather than with a uniformly lethal prenatal disorder [5].

Over the last decade, we have encountered dozens of similar Xhosa fetuses, none of whom survived. We report on three unrelated South African fetuses with antenatal features consistent with severe FADS and postmortem findings indicative of LMPS who share a pathogenic missense variant in CHAT. The severe phenotype associated with this CHAT variant is consistent with the critical role of residue p.Met202 in catalysis, and prior functional studies showing very low residual enzyme activity for the p.Met202Arg variant. This observation expands the phenotypic spectrum associated with variants in CHAT to include a prenatal lethal neuromuscular phenotype, paralleling other neuromuscular junction genes, particularly CHRNA1, CHRND, and CHRNG (OMIM 253290).

This case series provides novel evidence that CHAT‐related disease exists on a severity continuum extending from lethal fetal akinesia to survivable postnatal CMS, with important implications for prenatal diagnosis, genetic counseling, and recurrence risk assessment. Furthermore, the value of comprehensive genomic investigation in African populations, where recurrent pathogenic variants may reveal previously unrecognized genotype–phenotype correlations, is illustrated.
