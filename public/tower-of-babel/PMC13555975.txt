Caries experience and associated factors among Iranian and Afghan immigrant adolescents aged 13–15 years in Tehran: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sayed Shoaib Ghafari, Hossein Hessari, Mohammad Hasan Fekrat, Ahmad Reza Shamshiri, Fatemeh Seyedzadeh, Monire Heidari, Anoosheh Ghasemian, Mohammad Reza Khami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDental caries can be significantly associated with many sociodemographic and behavioral factors. The present study aimed to evaluate and compare caries experience and associated determinants among Iranian and Afghan adolescents in Tehran in 2024.MethodsThis cross-sectional study conducted on 761 students aged 13 to 15 years old who were selected through multistage random sampling from 18 secondary schools. Data on sociodemographic characteristics and oral health behaviors were collected via a self-reported questionnaire. Decayed, missing, and filled teeth (DMFT), and dental plaque were measured through a clinical examination form. Both univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were applied to examine the associations of sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical factors with caries experience. All statistical analyses were done using SPSS Version 26.ResultsOf total students, 46.6% were Afghan. Dental caries prevalence was approximately 72.5% in both nationalities. Being Iranian male was associated with a decrease of dental caries (OR = 0.54; 95% CI = 0.33–0.90, p = 0.02). The more the Iranian adolescents reported dental flossing the more caries experience they had (OR = 1.89; 95% CI = 1.04–3.43, p = 0.04). Negative association was found between mother education and caries experience among Afghan school children (OR = 0.28; 95% CI = 0.09–0.91, p = 0.04). Lower family size was associated with a decrease of dental caries among Afghan students (OR = 0.56; 95% CI = 0.32–0.98, p = 0.04). Having dental plaque was positively associated with caries experience among Iranian (OR = 1.96; 95% CI = 1.13–3.39, p = 0.02) and Afghan adolescents (OR = 1.60; 95% CI = 1.05–2.30, p = 0.03).ConclusionsBoth Iranian and Afghan immigrant students experienced high dental caries. Policies should be prioritized on culturally tailored school-based flossing and plaque control programs, maternal oral health literacy interventions, and family-size-sensitive service delivery. Immediate action under the World Health Organization(WHO) agenda for universal oral health coverage is warranted to reduce the prevalence of dental caries among immigrant adolescents.

Background

Dental caries in permanent teeth is the most common health problem in the world according to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 [1]. Although it is greatly preventable, the disease continues to increase worldwide with developing urbanization and changing in living situations [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the prevalence of dental caries was 60–90% among school children in 2021 [2]. Given the importance of oral health in adolescents, investigations on the prevalence of dental caries and its associated factors among school children have been conducted in various regions of Iran’s neighboring countries [3–9]. In this regard, different prevalence of dental caries has been reported approximately from 50% to 80% among adolescents across adjacent countries of Iran, for instance, 70.1% in Afghanistan [3], 61% in Iraq [4], 79.7% in Pakistan [5], 69.4% in Turkish [6], 74% in Kazakhstan [7], 61.7% in Saudi Arabia [8], and 53.7% in China [9], underscoring the necessity of preventive interventions for improving oral health in this population.

Adolescence is a transitional phase marked by rapid biological development, considerable physical changes, and shifts in social roles, serving as the bridge between childhood and adulthood [9]. Adolescence remains a high-risk period for dental caries, with significant regional variations in prevalence and severity [10, 11]. For instance, in Palestine, 54.4% of adolescents (aged 12–15 years) have dental caries in permanent teeth, peaking at 75.8% among 15-year-olds, with a high mean of decayed, missing, and filled teeth (DMFT) (5.39), indicating severe decay and limited restorative care [12]. Similar prevalence was reported in China (53.7%); however, mean of DMFT was lower (1.64), suggesting less severe cases due to better preventive measures [9].

Dental caries is a multifactorial disease associated with the main etiological factors such as bacteria, carbohydrate diet, time, and susceptible host. Additionally, demographic, socioeconomic, and nutritional factors have substantial role on its development [13]. In this regard, age has a great influence on dental caries. It is recognized that the annual increase in caries prevalence was 3% with increasing age, and the DMFT score was 0.15. Moreover, being female, living in a rural area with lack of oral health care, poor oral health, bad dental experience, and high sugar intake are of utmost importance for increasing risk of dental caries [14].

Immigration is also known as a significant determinant of dental caries, as immigrant adolescents are at higher risk for dental caries [15, 16]. Particularly, according to the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), Iran hosts one of the largest immigrants worldwide, where 3.5 million Afghans living in the country in 2023 and 2024. This figure includes approximately 1 million registered, 500,000 documented, and over 2 million undocumented Afghan immigrants [17]. Immigrants often face barriers and challenges for utilizing dental healthcare services in their host countries. These barriers arise from linguistic, cultural, and transportation difficulties, limitations of using public dental insurance, fear of pursuing dental care, and lack of culture-based preventive programs, leading to lower use of dental services and higher prevalence of untreated dental caries among immigrant population in comparison with their non-immigrant counterparts [15, 16].

Although health care system in Iran tries to cover basic health needs of Afghan immigrants, it is well-known that Afghan immigrants in Iran face major access barriers including absence of legal citizenship, financial difficulties, and lack of dental insurance [18]. While some epidemiological studies approve that Afghan immigrant populations have high risk of dental caries [18], evidence on the specific oral health-related associated factors among Afghan immigrant adolescents in Iran remains scarce. There is a lack of research on the dental caries status and the related factors among Afghan immigrant adolescents in Iran. Furthermore, the role of immigration and its barriers on oral health status of Afghan immigrant students remains greatly unknown in the Iranian setting.

A number of epidemiological studies have evaluated the caries experience status and the related factors among adolescents [19–22]. In a study conducted in Rwanda, dental caries experience was associated with being female, rural residents, low-income families, using non-fluoridated toothpaste, and having irregular dental visits among secondary school students [19]. Another cross-sectional study in China on adolescents aged 12–15 years found that dental caries experience was associated with inadequate tooth brushing frequency, frequent sugary drinks consumption, and insufficient use of fluoride [20]. In a study conducted among Turkish school children, higher DMFT was not associated with inadequate tooth brushing frequency, irregular dental check‑ups, and poor oral hygiene [21]. A cross-sectional study in Iran on 14-18-year-old adolescents showed that higher DMFT was associated with increasing age, being male, having low parental educational level, low-income families, inadequate tooth brushing and dental flossing frequency, and having no history of dental visits [22].

However, the previous research has no consistent results with regard to the associated factors of dental caries. Additionally, to our knowledge, no research has been yet performed to evaluate and compare dental caries status and its related factors among Afghan immigrants and Iranian students in a vulnerable age group of 13 to 15 years old who defy parental advice, neglect their personal oral hygiene, and overeat unhealthy food. Given this gap in research, there is an urgent need to achieve the current status of caries experience and significant associated factors among Iranian and Afghan immigrant school children and to compare their oral health situation in order to identify oral health inequalities, design targeted interventions, and inform policies and advocacies for the prevention, control, and treatment of dental caries. The results will guide the development of targeted oral health promotion programs to reduce caries prevalence in these vulnerable adolescent populations.

Therefore, the aim of the present study was to assess and compare caries experience and its associated factors among Iranian and Afghan immigrant adolescents aged 13–15 years in the south of Tehran. It is noteworthy that the present study is the first study in Iran that compares caries experience and associated factors between Iranian and Afghan Immigrant adolescents. This novelty makes the current study interesting in that caries experience and the related factors were measured considering challenges of immigration in Afghans that can affect the results. A confirmation of nationality disparities in oral health status of school children is important, as this would inspire further studies for the actual cause of this assumption to be addressed. Null hypothesis was that there is no difference between Iranian and Afghan immigrant students with regard to caries experience and associated factors such as sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical variables. The primary and secondary research objectives were whether the prevalence of dental caries and its associated factors were different between the both nationalities, respectively.

Methods

Study design and participants

This analytical cross-sectional study was performed among 761 (406 Iranian and 355 Afghan) school children aged 13 to 15 years in the southern part of Tehran from April to May in 2024. Participants were selected via multistage random sampling to reflect socioeconomic and geographic diversity. At the first stage, two deprived areas in the southern part of Tehran (Eslamshahr and Shahr-e-Rey) were selected, locating the most Afghan residents. At the second stage, secondary schools were stratified by gender, and 18 schools (9 girls’ and 9 boys’) were chosen via random cluster sampling from the total list of secondary schools in the deprived areas (districts 15 to 20) under the coverage of Tehran University of Medical Sciences. At the third stage, students in 7th to 9th grades were randomly selected from the total list of students in each secondary school.

The inclusion criteria were Iranian and Afghan immigrant students in the age range of 13 to 15 years and grade of 7 to 9, had permanent teeth, lived and studied in the two deprived areas in the south of Tehran, and whose parents signed the informed consent, and desired to take part in the study. Participants who were non-Iranian citizens or Afghan immigrants, suffered from systemic disease and physical or mental illness, and had fluorosis and enamel hypoplasia were excluded.

Sample size

Sample size was calculated according to the following parameters: a type I error (α) = 0.05, power = 0.8, and design effect = 2. Additionally, the required information about DMFT was derived from Pakpour et al., study [22] reporting a DMFT of 2.61 and standard deviation (SD) of 1.89 for Iranian adolescents, and from Falk Schwendicke et al., study [23] reporting a DMFT of 4.04 and SD of 3.03 for Afghan adolescents.

The minimum sample size required to estimate DMFT in the study group with an accuracy of 15% of the SD was equal to 342 for each nationality (half girls and half boys).

The study population size was determined using the following formula:

n = [(Z 1 - α/2) ^2 * SD ^2] / d ^2

Based on this estimation, the required sample size for 13–15 year-old in this study was at least 700. To account for potential non-response and ensure robust comparisons, the final sample included 761 students (Iranians and Afghans immigrants), exceeding the minimum requirement. An additional 61 students were included to strengthen statistical power, and data were weighted during analysis to adjust for oversampling and ensure equal representation.

Weighting was performed as the distributions of age, sex, and class were heterogeneous between Afghan immigrant and Iranian participants. The disproportion of data makes us to apply weighting according to the mentioned variables in order to correct the proportion. This adjustment ensured that both groups were comparably represenative in the analysis. Afterwards, we selected the complex samples option on the weighted data to conduct the data analyses.

Calibration of dentists

Four dentists, all PhD students in Community Oral Health at Tehran University of Medical Sciences, participated in two calibration sessions first at a private dental office and second in the field. The sessions focused on the study’s objective, the diagnostic criteria for oral health status, and data collection processes. Subsequently, to establish inter-examiner reliability, each dentist examined 10 patients. The inter-examiner and intra-examiner Kappa coefficients for oral health status (DMFT) were 0.90 and 0.97, respectively.

Data collection

Instrument and measurement

Data was collected through a self-reported validated oral health questionnaire and oral health assessment form designed by WHO [24]. The questionnaire consisted of two parts. The first part comprised sociodemographic characteristics including gender (girl, boy), age (13, 14, 15 years old), parental education level (illiterate, under high school diploma, high school diploma, higher than high school diploma), place of residence (urban or rural), home status (with parents means living with parents in personal and rental house, or without parents means living in dormitory and other places), and family size (1–4, more than 4). The second part comprised oral health behaviors including frequency of tooth brushing (twice or once a day, once or more a week, or once a month and do not know), frequency of dental flossing (once a day and more, sometimes, or never), frequency of use of fluoride tooth paste (always or sometimes/never), and dietary habits such as frequency of sweet consumption (3 or more per day, once or twice per day, or sometimes/never), and frequency of sweetened beverages consumption in the last week (several times a day, once a day, or rarely/never).

The oral examination form consisted of caries experience measured by DMFT (0 or more than 0), and existence of dental plaque (yes or no). It should be noted that as our objective was to assess caries experience and the data did not have normal distribution, we dichotomized DMF to 0 or more than 0. Dental plaque was assessed by direct visual inspection supplemented by the WHO CPI probe to detect the presence of a soft, adherent plaque layer on the buccal surfaces of the explored teeth [25]. No disclosing solution was used in this regard.

Oral examinations were conducted following the WHO guidelines [24] for evaluating tooth and gingiva, on Iranian and Afghan adolescents simultaneously by the four calibrated dentists via head light, disposable instruments such as gloves, face masks, dental mirrors, and sterilized Williams probes. The total period for completing the questionnaire and doing clinical examination last more than two months from April to June 2024.

Ethics

The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (Approval Code: IR.TUMS.DENTISTRY.REC.1402.018). Written informed consent forms were obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants aged 13 to 15 years old before their enrollment in the study. Informed assent was also acquired directly from the students during the data collection time. The clinical examinations were performed with permission of authorities in emigrational and educational sectors. Individuals were assured that their private information would remain confidential.

Data analysis

Categorical variables were presented by numbers and percentages (%). Both univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were performed to investigate the associations between caries experience and related sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical factors. We aimed to evaluate the relationships of all independent variables with caries experience by nationality to compare the results between both nationalities. So, we used the “select case” option to analyze the data for Iranian and afghan immigrant students separately. In univariate logistic regression models, the variables that had a p-value < 0.20 were considered statistically significant and entered the multivariate logistic regression models. Using a significance level of 0.20 at the univariate screening stage helps avoid inadvertently excluding variables that might demonstrate significant effects when considered together in a multivariable context due to the presence of confounding or synergistic relationships among covariates [26]. The results were shown as odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). In multivariate logistic regression models, the significance was set at p-value < 0.05. All statistical analyses were implemented via SPSS Version 26.

Results

Tables 1 and 2 present frequencies of basic characteristics of Iranian and Afghan school children by gender in south of Tehran. Among Iranian students, girls (55.9%) were more than boys (50.4%), while among Afghan students, boys (49.6%) were more than girls (44.1%). The highest percentages of age in both Iranian boys and girls were 14 years old (approximately 40.0%), however, among both Afghan boys and girls most of the students were 13 (40.9%) and 15 (38.0%) years old, respectively.

Prevalence of dental caries was similar among both nationalities (about 72.5%). In both Iranian and Afghan adolescents, girls experienced more caries than boys. Twice- or once-daily tooth brushing was the highest percentage of frequency of tooth brushing in Iranians (53.4%) and Afghan immigrants (47.5%). Both Iranian and Afghan girls brushed their teeth more frequently (two or one time a day) compared to boys. Sometimes-dental flossing was the most frequency of dental flossing among Iranians (43.9%) and Afghan immigrants (50.3%). In both Iranian and Afghan school children, girls who flossed their teeth from time to time were more compared to boys. Usual use of fluoride tooth paste was the highest percentage of frequency of use of fluoride tooth paste in Iranians (84.7%) and Afghan immigrants (88.7%). Both Iranian and Afghan girls used fluoride tooth paste more frequently (always) compared to boys.

Sometimes- or never-sweet consumption was the most frequency of sweet consumption among Iranians (44.5%) and Afghan immigrants (54.9%). In both Iranian and Afghan students, boys who consumed sweet sometimes or never were more compared to girls. Rarely- or never- sweetened beverages consumption in the last week was the highest percentage of frequency of sweetened beverages consumption in Iranians (41.8%) and Afghan immigrants (44.7%). Both Iranian and Afghan girls consumed sweetened beverages less frequently (rarely or never) compared to boys. Prevalence of dental plaque was similar among both nationalities (about 67.0%). In both Iranian and Afghan adolescents, boys had more plaque than girls.

The associations between caries experience and nationality, sociodemographic characteristics, oral health behaviors, and dental plaque among Iranian and Afghan students in south of Tehran are shown in Table 3. Being male was associated with a decrease in dental caries (OR = 0.73; 95% CI = 0.52–1.02, p = 0.09). Caries experience was positively associated with increasing age (OR = 1.49; 95% CI = 0.96–2.33, p = 0.08).

Negative association was found between father educational level of high school diploma and caries experience (OR = 0.58; 95% CI = 0.37–0.89, p = 0.02), as well as between mother educational level of under high school diploma (OR = 0.62; 95% CI = 0.38–1.03, p = 0.06), high school diploma (OR = 0.51; 95% CI = 0.31–0.84, p = 0.01), and higher than high school diploma (OR = 0.52; 95% CI = 0.27–1.03, p = 0.06) with caries experience.

Lower family size (OR = 0.68; 95% CI = 0.49–0.95, p = 0.02) and using fluoride tooth paste (OR = 0.68; 95% CI = 0.41–1.11, p = 0.12) were associated with a decrease of dental caries. Caries experience was positively associated with dental plaque (OR = 1.64; 95% CI = 1.21–2.23, p = 0.02).

Discussion

The present study aimed to assess and compare the caries experience and its association with sociodemographic characteristics and oral health behaviors among Iranian and Afghan immigrant adolescents aged 13–15 years in south of Tehran.

According to the results of the current study, Iranian and Afghan students had the similar caries experience (about 72.5%). Our finding is consistent with the result of a meta-analysis in Iran which showed that the prevalence of dental caries among Iranian children and adolescents was 72.8% [27]. Additionally, another meta-analysis conducted in Saudi Arabia reported approximately the same dental caries prevalence of 72% in school children [28].

However, our result is in contrast to a nationwide study on Arab school children aged 12 and 15 years [8], as well as a cross-sectional study among Indian adolescents aged 12 to 15 years [29], that both found lower prevalence of dental caries which was 61.7% and 61.4%, respectively. Moreover, two studies from China among 12-15-year-old adolescents, the first performed in Shanxi province [11], and the second in Xide county [20], reported a much lower caries prevalence of 44.6% and 42.5%, respectively. The inconsistency in the results could be attributed to some individual factors associating with dental caries such as oral hygiene practices, dietary habits, use of fluoride, regular dental visit, and oral health knowledge, as well as some governmental factors such as accessibility to oral health services, water fluoridation, and oral health educational initiatives in schools [30] which are dissimilar among different countries.

The results of the present study indicated that being Iranian male was significantly associated with a decrease of dental caries. Our result aligns with a cross-sectional study conducted on 12- to 16-years old Palestinian school children which reported that being female was associated with higher caries experience [12]. Furthermore, in another cross-sectional study from the capital city of Rwanda, Kigali among secondary students, girls were 50% more likely to have dental caries in comparison with boys [31]. Similarly, according to the results of a study on adolescents aged 12 to 16 years in Ahvaz, Iran, the prevalence of dental caries was higher in girls than boys [10]. Potential explanations could be due to the gender difference in growth patterns explaining by biological factors including earlier permanent teeth eruption in girls, and, consequently, their more exposure time to the cariogenic foods. Moreover, behavioral disparities such as dietary preferences among both genders might be another potential reason for this discrepancy [32].

We found that regular dental flossing among Iranian school children was positively associated with caries experience. Consistent with our result, two studies among 14- and 18-year-old Norwegian adolescents showed that those who flossed their teeth more frequently experienced higher approximal dental caries [33, 34]. However, our finding stand in contrast to some studies which found no or reverse association of dental flossing with caries experience [35, 36]. In this regard, in a Cochrane systematic review no association was reported between dental flossing and interproximal caries among adolescents [35]. Nevertheless, a study conducted in Syria on 8 to 12 years old students confirmed the protective effect of dental flossing in controlling dental caries progression [36]. Our contradictory results regarding the positive association could be interpreted with caution. This dental flossing paradox needs stronger emphasis on reverse causation, social desirability bias, measurement bias or residual confounding. In other words, reverse causality should be considered as a plausible explanation for the observed association which argue that adolescents with a history of caries are more likely to have visited a dentist for treatment, and, consequently, to have received professional oral hygiene instructions, including recommendations to use dental floss. In this context, flossing behavior may reflect prior disease experience, greater perceived need for interdental cleaning, or increased contact with dental services [37].

Additionally, the potential influence of social desirability bias should be acknowledged, as dental flossing was self-reported. In this regard, adolescents may overreport recommended oral hygiene behaviors in order to align with socially accepted norms or perceived expectations from caregivers and health professionals. As such, reported dental flossing frequency may not accurately reflect actual or effective interdental cleaning practices. Furthermore, it would be important to contextualize the current evidence regarding the effectiveness of flossing in preventing caries among adolescents. While interdental cleaning contributes to gingival health, the evidence supporting its isolated effectiveness in preventing dental caries—especially in younger populations and in the absence of professional supervision—is limited and inconsistent. Thus, regular exposure to fluoride through tooth brushing with fluoridated dentifrice remains the primary preventive factor for caries in this age group. Another plausible reason for the observed association might be related to residual confounding refers to some unmeasured factors such as dietary or genetic not considering in the data analysis. It is also important to note that although dental flossing is recognized as a protective measure which decreases dental caries prevalence, scientific research reported that improper use of dental flossing technique without proper instruction can paradoxically increase the risk. Furthermore, it has been recognized that typical self-flossing could be failed to affect dental caries, while professional flossing is greatly effective to reduce the risk of interproximal caries [33].

In our study significant reverse association was found between mother education and caries experience among Afghan adolescents. Our finding is consistent with the results of previous studies among children and adolescents conducted in Syria [36], China [38], and India [29] which reported that higher maternal education was significantly associated with lower dental caries. Nevertheless, our finding is in contrast to a study conducted in northeast China among 12-15-year-old adolescents in which father education showed as a significant protective influence of dental caries [9]. This contrary result could be explained by the fact that higher mother education is strongly associated with greater oral health literacy and knowledge, which could be directly translated into healthier daily practices of their children. In this case, educated mothers enable to better understand and implement healthier oral-health related daily practices for their children such as supervising effective tooth brushing, establishing a diet with lower consumption of cariogenic foods and sugary drinks, and consistently utilizing dental care services for preventive visits rather than problem-based treatments [29, 39].

We found a significant positive association between family size and caries experience among Afghan school children. This finding has been supported by a systematic review showing that family size was associated with an increase in dental caries among children and adolescents [40]. Our result could be due to the fact that students who were belonged to large families received insufficient attention from their parents which made them take less care of their oral hygiene, and use less dental health services, exhibiting the higher prevalence of dental caries [41]. Unlike, students who lived in small families received more care from their parents encouraging them to have better oral health behaviors and regular dental visits. Additionally, another potential reason might be because of the confirmed relationship of family with dental caries status among other studied demographic factors. Dental caries status of children could be associated with several factors in their family including parents’ educational and occupational level, psychological status, oral hygiene behavior, dietary pattern, dental service utilization, and oral health-related knowledge [40].

The results of the present study showed that having dental plaque among Iranian and Afghan students was significantly associated with an increase of dental caries. Our results are similar to the findings of two studies in developing countries, the first among 9 to 15 years old school children in India [42], and the second among 8 to 12 years old school children in Syria [36], in which higher plaque accumulation was significantly associated with higher dental caries prevalence. Additionally, in two other studies among adolescents in developed countries including Brazil and Spain, the presence of dental plaque had a significant detrimental effect on development of dental caries [43, 44]. This result has been also supported by the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD) guidelines emphasizing dental plaque is related to an increased risk of dental caries in adolescents [45]. The consistent results could be related to the acid produced by bacteria in dental plaque from sugary diet which lead to dissolve tooth enamel, and, consequently would be the main factor in the development of dental caries among adolescents [46]. Dental caries is caused by the conversion of fermentable carbohydrates by plaque bacteria into acids on the tooth surface. Thus, it is important to focus on sugar reduction and plaque control [47].

The present study had some certain strengths. First, the study population was a substantial sample of both boy and girl school children selected from considerable numbers of schools. Second, to our knowledge, it was the first time that we evaluated caries experience status and its determinants of Afghan immigrants living in Tehran, Iran and compared them with Iranian counterparts. Third, the findings could contribute to establish an oral healthcare strategy in Iran via requesting assistance from UNHCR to provide healthcare services for Afghan immigrant students.

The present study also had some certain limitations. First, the cross-sectional study design cannot derive causal relationship. Second, a self-reported questionnaire was used to collect data on sociodemographic and oral health behaviors, which could result in self-report bias. Additionally, as we used a self-reported questionnaire that might lead to social desirability bias, the results were the optimistic estimations of the real conditions and should be interpreted with caution. Third, we limited the study population to adolescents living in urban and rural areas in the south of Tehran, which could potentially limit the generalizability of the findings to the deprived areas and the generalizability to the whole population should be performed with caution. Fourth, despite the duration of residence of Afghan immigrants are heterogeneous, their immigration duration was not measured which can potentially affect the results. The study was conducted on Afghan immigrants who had lived in Iran for many years, therefore, the detrimental effects of recent immigration might not be entirely reflected through the results of the study. Fifth, some related factors to dental caries such as clinical caries severity, use of water fluoridation, dental service utilization, and coverage of dental insurance remained unmeasured.

The findings of the present study hold direct policy relevance, particularly for advancing oral health status among Afghan immigrant students in Iran. The identification of distinct associated factors—including maternal education, family size, and dental plaque—highlights the need for resource allocation to be urgently directed toward these vulnerable groups. In line with global models, best practices for oral health promotion include culturally sensitive school-based programs that integrate dental education, preventive services such as fluoride varnish application, and assistance in navigating the healthcare system.

Conclusions

We concluded that high prevalence of dental caries was found among Iranian and Afghan immigrant school children along with inequities between both nationalities with regard to the associated factors. The findings highlight the urgent need to implement national community-based oral health programs in schools to control dental caries, as well as to develop culturally adapted prevention strategies in order to promote oral health status among Iranian and Afghan immigrant school children. Considering the multidimensional and long-term adverse impact of dental caries, effective preventive and treatment strategies such as oral health education, early intervention, and available restorative care should be prioritized to reduce the disease burden. Further research is required to focus more on oral health status of Afghan immigrants and to design efficient plans in order to ideally promote their oral health. Future studies should also emphasize on new immigrants to fully address the major causal factors of dental caries in Afghan immigrant populations.
