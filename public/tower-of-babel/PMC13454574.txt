Intranasal boosting induces variant-specific responses in SARS-CoV-2 vaccine-imprinted mice
Authors: Chieh-Yu Liang, Suzanne M. Scheaffer, James Brett Case, Igor P. Dmitriev, Yuhao Li, Sameer Kumar Malladi, Xinyi Liu, Baoling Ying, Pritesh Desai, Sean P. J. Whelan, David T. Curiel, Ali H. Ellebedy, Michael S. Diamond
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The continued evolution of SARS-CoV-2 variants that evade immunity highlights a need to develop vaccines that elicit variant-specific antibodies and neutralize emerging strains. However, immune imprinting from antecedent SARS-CoV-2 exposure can limit the generation of such antibodies. Here, we evaluate strategies to enhance variant-specific antibody responses in female mice primed with Wuhan-1 spike-based mRNA or chimpanzee adenoviral-vectored (ChAd) vaccines and boosted with Omicron variant-matched vaccines. Altering the intramuscular injection site did not substantially affect variant-specific serum antibody responses. However, increasing booster antigen doses, performing repeated boosters, and administering booster vaccines intranasally enhanced variant-specific responses against the vaccine-matched Omicron strain. Boosting intranasally with a ChAd vaccine encoding the spike protein of Omicron XBB.1.5 elicited stronger XBB.1.5-specific responses in serum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, and draining lymph nodes than intramuscular boosting with the same vaccine. Regardless of booster regimen, neutralizing activity against XBB.1.5 was predominantly mediated by antibodies that were non-reactive to Wuhan-1 spike. These findings establish that in mice, intranasal or repeated variant-matched boosting can overcome the effects of imprinting and enhance immunity against SARS-CoV-2 strains.

Introduction

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) vaccines based on the spike protein of the ancestral Wuhan-1 strain of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) show reduced efficacy against variant strains1–4. This occurrence led to the development of updated vaccines, encoding the spike protein of more recent variants. Although immunization with updated vaccines augments neutralizing activity against vaccine-matched strains5–8, back-boosting of responses to the ancestral strain was observed in those with prior Wuhan-1 exposure8. This phenomenon, known as immune imprinting, refers to the effects of prior antigen exposure on the response to subsequent, related antigens. The process is thought to be mediated by the recall of cross-reactive memory B cells (MBCs) and can be associated with limited induction of de novo B cell responses. Imprinting can be beneficial when cross-reactive responses confer protection8; however, it can also hinder the induction of protective variant-specific immunity. In individuals vaccinated with the prototype Wuhan-1 vaccine, boosting with Omicron variant-matched vaccines primarily expanded cross-reactive antibodies and B cells8,9. Despite the limited induction of Omicron-specific responses, cross-reactive antibodies inhibited infection of the vaccine-encoded Omicron variant, other Omicron subvariants, and distantly related sarbecoviruses. However, as SARS‑CoV‑2 has continued to evolve under immune pressure, neutralizing activity induced by prototype vaccines is substantially decreased against more recent variants1–4,10, suggesting that neutralization of emerging strains may increasingly depend on variant-specific responses.

Several approaches have been explored to augment responses that target variant strains. These include strategies to enhance the immunogenicity of variant-matched booster vaccines, which potentially lead to stronger responses against the variant antigen. Some groups showed that extending the interval between immunizations increased antibody binding titers, enhanced neutralizing activity, and induced stronger germinal center (GC) B cell and antibody-secreting long-lived plasma cell responses against the homologous virus strain11–14. Boosting with higher antigen doses resulted in stronger neutralizing activity against Omicron B.1.1.529 in clinical trial participants receiving the Wuhan-1/B.1.351 bivalent mRNA vaccine, although the variant-specificity of the response was not explored15. In individuals imprinted by immunization with inactivated Wuhan-1 viral vaccines, two breakthrough infections with Omicron variants alleviated imprinting and induced Omicron spike-specific MBCs, suggesting that repeated vaccination with variant-matched vaccines could induce variant-specific responses16. Since MBCs residing within GCs in the draining lymph nodes (DLN)17–20 may mediate imprinting, boosting at distal (i.e., contralateral) sites might allow for induction of booster-specific responses. While some groups detected stronger GC B cell responses against the second immunogen in mice boosted on the contralateral side relative to the priming dose of vaccine21,22, others showed inconsistent results regarding the outcomes of ipsilateral and contralateral immunizations and did not assess the antigen specificity of the response22–28. Although these findings suggest that extending the dosing interval, increasing the antigen dose, or altering the administration site might enhance the immunogenicity of variant-matched vaccines, it is unclear whether these responses entail expansion of cross-reactive or variant-specific responses.

Intramuscular immunization with vaccines against respiratory viruses elicits immunity that protects against severe infection and disease29–32. However, the inability of intramuscular vaccination to induce mucosal IgA results in limited protection against upper respiratory tract infection or transmission31,32. In comparison, intranasal immunization induces both mucosal and systemic immunity, conferring protection in both the upper and lower airways33,34 and potentially reducing virus transmission capacity35. Intramuscular priming followed by intranasal boosting induces stronger systemic and mucosal responses than intramuscular priming and boosting regimens32,36–41. In these settings, intranasal boosters recall immunity generated by primary antigen exposure and establish resident memory B cells (BRM), resident memory T cells (TRM), and plasmablasts in the lung38,42. Although such “prime-and-pull” vaccination regimens induce multi-compartment immunity, the antigen specificity of such responses remains poorly understood, especially in the context of heterologous variant boosting. Given that the induction of variant-specific antibodies may be increasingly important for neutralization of newer SARS-CoV-2 variants1–4,10, identifying optimal ways to generate de novo B cell responses against variants in the setting of heavily imprinted cross-reactive immunity is an important goal.

Here, we address whether mucosal boosting and other immunization approaches can overcome the imprinting effects of prior SARS-CoV-2 antigen exposure and result in greater variant-specific responses. We characterized the spike-specificity of serum antibodies in mice immunized with different vaccination intervals, antigen doses, injection sites, administration routes, and numbers of boosters. Stronger variant-specific antibody responses were observed in animals that received higher or multiple booster doses or were given boosters via the intranasal route. Intranasal boosting also resulted in higher variant-specific IgA levels in respiratory tract fluid than intramuscular boosting, regardless of the priming scheme. Additionally, stronger variant-specific MBC responses were detected in the DLN of mice boosted intranasally compared to intramuscularly. Together, these findings suggest ways to mitigate imprinting, allowing for the induction of variant-specific antibodies that neutralize emerging variant strains.

Results

Boosting interval effects

We first examined the effect of the boosting interval on imprinting responses. Groups of 6-week-old female C57BL/6J mice were intramuscularly primed with two doses of mRNA-1273 (encoding Wuhan-1 spike protein) 1 month apart and then boosted with mRNA-1273.529 (encoding Omicron BA.1 spike protein) at 3- or 6-month intervals (herein 1273/1273/529, Supplementary Fig. 1a). Mice vaccinated with three doses of mRNA-1273 (1273/1273/1273) or mRNA-1273.529 (529/529/529) were included as controls. Serum was collected 3 weeks after the third immunization, and Wuhan-1 spike-reactive, BA.1 spike-reactive, and BA.1 spike-specific IgG titers were measured. Binding responses to Wuhan-1 and BA.1 spike proteins were boosted in all groups (Supplementary Fig. 1b). BA.1 spike-specific antibody titers were assayed by first depleting samples with Wuhan-1 spike-loaded magnetic beads, followed by measurement of residual binding reactivity to BA.1 spike-loaded detection beads. Removal of Wuhan-1 spike-reactive antibodies abolished binding to Wuhan-1 detection beads, establishing a system for depleting Wuhan-1 spike-specific and cross-reactive antibodies (Supplementary Fig. 1c, left, light gray circles). Regardless of the boosting interval, 1273/1273/1273 mice lacked detectable BA.1-specific IgG, whereas 529/529/529 mice had the highest fraction of BA.1-specific IgG (median of 19%, Supplementary Fig. 1c, d, yellow circles). In mice primed with two doses of mRNA-1273 and boosted with mRNA-1273.529 at 3- or 6-month intervals, statistically significant differences were not observed in the fraction of BA.1-specific IgG (median of 6% and 2%, respectively; Supplementary Fig. 1e). Thus, at 6 months after the primary mRNA-1273 vaccinations, serum responses in mice were still strongly imprinted such that low BA.1-specific responses were generated following a BA.1-matched mRNA booster.

Intramuscular boosting site effects

We next tested whether the site of intramuscular boosting affects the variant-specific antibody response by comparing serum antibody levels in mice boosted with an Omicron-matched vaccine in the same (ipsilateral) or opposite (contralateral) leg relative to the priming doses (Supplementary Fig. 2a). More contemporary SARS-CoV-2 strains, including XBB.1.5, further evade neutralization by antibodies elicited by the original Wuhan-1 vaccines3,4, highlighting the importance of generating variant-specific responses. Thus, we analyzed XBB.1.5 spike-specific responses in mice primed with two doses of mRNA-1273 and boosted in the ipsilateral or contralateral leg with mRNA-1273.815 (encoding XBB.1.5 spike protein). Serum collected 1 month after boosting showed XBB.1.5 spike-specific IgG in 8 of 15 (53%) and 6 of 10 (60%) mice boosted on the ipsilateral and contralateral sides, respectively, with no differences in magnitude between groups (ipsilateral: range 0–3%, median 0.2%; contralateral: range 0–1%, median 0.4%; Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). These data suggest that the site of intramuscular boosting with XBB.1.5-matched vaccines does not significantly affect variant-specific serum antibody responses in mice.

Antigen dose effects

We assessed whether higher doses of the heterologous booster could enhance variant-specific responses. In addition to animals that received three equal 0.25-µg doses of mRNA vaccines in the ipsilateral leg (Supplementary Fig. 2a), separate groups of mice were boosted heterologously in the same leg with increasing doses (0.125, 1.25, or 5 µg) of mRNA-1273.815. Analysis of serum collected 1 month later showed that binding titers to Wuhan-1 and XBB.1.5 spike proteins trended higher in the 1.25 and 5 µg-boosted animals than in the lower dose-boosted groups (Supplementary Fig. 2d). The fraction of XBB.1.5 spike-specific serum IgG increased with the dose of the mRNA-1273.815 boosting vaccine: 5 µg dose (range 3–8%, median 5%), 1.25 µg dose (range 1–7%, median 3%), 0.25 µg dose (range 0–3%, median 0.2%), and 0.125 µg dose (range 0–2%, median 0%) (Supplementary Fig. 2e). Thus, boosting with a higher dose of mRNA-1273.815 vaccine after priming with two doses of mRNA-1273 can increase the magnitude of XBB.1.5 spike-specific serum IgG responses in mice.

Repeated vaccination effects

Because humans who experienced multiple Omicron breakthrough infections developed Omicron spike-specific MBCs16, we evaluated whether repeated immunization could enhance variant-specific antibody responses in serum. Mice primed twice with mRNA-1273 and then boosted with 0.25 µg of mRNA-1273.815 were boosted 3 months later with a second dose of mRNA-1273.815 (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Serum collected one and 3 months later had comparable Wuhan-1-reactive but higher XBB.1.5-reactive IgG levels than that collected after the third dose, consistent with boosting of XBB.1.5 spike-specific antibodies (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The fraction of XBB.1.5 spike-specific serum IgG increased after the second mRNA-1273.815 immunization and persisted at 3 months post-boost (post-3rd dose: range 0–1%, median 0%; 1 month post-4th dose: range 4–63%, median 18%; 3 months post-4th dose: range 3–75%, median 20%; Supplementary Fig. 3c). The second XBB.1.5-matched booster also increased neutralization of a chimeric vesicular stomatitis virus displaying the XBB.1.5 spike protein (VSV-XBB.1.5, Supplementary Fig. 3d). Depletion of antibodies that bound to Wuhan-1 spike protein minimally impacted the neutralizing activity against VSV-XBB.1.5, suggesting that neutralization was mediately principally by XBB.1.5 spike-specific antibodies. Thus, in mice primed with two doses of mRNA-1273, boosting with a second dose of heterologous mRNA-1273.815 vaccine can increase the magnitude of XBB.1.5 spike-specific serum neutralizing antibody responses.

To better model human vaccination series, we characterized serum antibody responses in mice immunized with the Wuhan-1 monovalent primary series, followed by a Wuhan-1/BA.5 bivalent booster, and then given XBB.1.5 boosters. Mice were primed intramuscularly with mRNA-1273 3 weeks apart, boosted with mRNA-1273.222 (encoding Wuhan-1 and BA.4/5 spike proteins) 1 year later, and then boosted 1 month later with two doses of mRNA-1273.815 1 month apart (Supplementary Fig. 3e). XBB.1.5-specific antibody responses were undetectable prior to administration of XBB.1.5 boosters (Supplementary Fig. 3f, g). Following the first XBB.1.5 booster, depletion of serum with Wuhan-1 spike-loaded beads revealed the presence of Wuhan-1-non-reactive, XBB.1.5-reactive serum IgG. However, co-depletion with both Wuhan-1 and BA.5 spike-loaded beads abolished XBB.1.5 spike binding, indicating that these antibodies were cross-reactive to BA.5 spike. XBB.1.5-specific IgG was detected only after the second XBB.1.5 booster (range 0–9%, median 4%). Neutralizing activity against VSV-XBB.1.5 increased after the second mRNA-1273.815 dose and was mediated principally by Wuhan-1-non-reactive antibodies (Supplementary Fig. 3h). Thus, a second dose of mRNA-1273.815 booster enhances variant-specific serum antibody responses, even in the context of an intermediate bivalent vaccination encoding an Omicron variant spike.

Similar results were observed in a separate cohort receiving higher primary doses (1.25 µg of mRNA-1273) and lower booster doses (0.5 µg of mRNA-1273.222 and mRNA-1273.815), more closely reflecting the human immunization regimen. Mice were primed with mRNA-1273 3 weeks apart, boosted with mRNA-1273.222 3 months later, and subsequently boosted 1 month later with two doses of mRNA-1273.815 1 month apart (Supplementary Fig. 3i). In this cohort, XBB.1.5-specific IgG was again detected after the second XBB.1.5-matched booster (Supplementary Fig. 3j, k; range 0–11%, median 0.7%). Neutralization of VSV-XBB.1.5 was mediated by Wuhan-1-non-reactive antibodies, the majority of which were cross-reactive to BA.5 spike (Supplementary Fig. 3l). Together, repeated boosting with the same variant-matched vaccine expands serum antibody responses that are not imprinted by the primary Wuhan-1 mRNA-1273 series, regardless of whether an intermediate bivalent vaccine is administered.

Mucosal versus intramuscular boosting after intramuscular priming

We next evaluated how boosting with a variant-matched vaccine at a mucosal site affects variant-specific immunity. Because lipid-encapsulated mRNA vaccines were designed for intramuscular injection and show poor immunogenicity after mucosal delivery39, we boosted mice with a chimpanzee adenoviral-vectored vaccine (ChAd) encoding the XBB.1.5 spike protein, ChAd-XBB.1.5; this vaccine platform elicits robust systemic immune responses when administered intranasally or intramuscularly29. Groups of mice were primed intramuscularly with a two-dose series of mRNA-1273 1 month apart and then boosted 3 months later with mRNA-1273.815 intramuscularly, or with ChAd-XBB.1.5 either intranasally or intramuscularly (Fig. 1a). Serum collected 28 days after boosting showed enhanced binding to Wuhan-1 and XBB.1.5 spike proteins by all vaccines (Fig. 1b). Depleting serum of Wuhan-1 spike-binding antibodies reduced binding to XBB.1.5 spike protein, indicating that a large fraction of XBB.1.5 spike-binding IgG was cross-reactive to Wuhan-1 spike (Fig. 1c). Nonetheless, XBB.1.5 spike-specific IgG was detected in all boosting groups except the 1273/1273/1273 control mice, as expected. A strong immune imprinting effect of mRNA-1273 priming was observed, as the fraction of XBB.1.5 spike-specific IgG was higher in 815/815/815 mice (range 15–43%, median 26%) than in 1273/1273/815 mice (range 0–1%, median 0.2%, Fig. 1d). Imprinting by mRNA-1273 also was observed in mice boosted with the ChAd-XBB.1.5 vaccine; animals that received a single intramuscular or intranasal dose of ChAd-XBB.1.5 had higher fractions of XBB.1.5 spike-specific IgG (range 0–74%, median 43%, and range 31–76%, median 50%, respectively) than those also primed with mRNA-1273 (intramuscular: range 0–3%, median 0%; intranasal: range 1–16%, median 5%; Fig. 1d). Animals that were boosted with ChAd-XBB.1.5 intranasally had higher fractions (8.6-fold, p < 0.001) of XBB.1.5 spike-specific IgG than those receiving the same vaccine booster intramuscularly. Mice boosted with ChAd-XBB.1.5 by an intranasal route also had higher neutralizing activity (5.2-fold, p < 0.001) against VSV-XBB.1.5 (Fig. 1e). Again, neutralization of XBB.1.5 was mediated largely by XBB.1.5 spike-specific antibodies, as such activity was minimally affected by depletion of Wuhan-1-reactive antibodies.

In contrast to the neutralization results with XBB.1.5, depletion of Wuhan-1 cross-reactive antibodies abolished neutralizing activity against chimeric viruses expressing the spike proteins of more distantly related sarbecoviruses, Pangolin/Guangdong (Pang/GD) and SARS-CoV-1; in this case, inhibition was mediated principally by Wuhan-1 cross-reactive antibodies (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). The higher level of XBB.1.5 spike-specific antibodies in mice boosted intranasally did not positively or negatively impact the neutralizing activity against Pang/GD and SARS-CoV-1. However, little to no cross-neutralization of the more closely related JN.1 strain of SARS-CoV-2 was observed (Supplementary Fig. 4d), consistent with the immune escape profile of Omicron variant strains against cross-reactive antibodies induced by legacy vaccines or earlier Omicron boosters8,43.

Because the spike-binding antibody titers and fraction of XBB.1.5 spike-specific responses trended higher in mice receiving ChAd-XBB.1.5 via intranasal compared to intramuscular routes (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 5a), we were concerned that the inherent immunogenicity of the vaccine might vary with route of administration and confound interpretation. Indeed, the intrinsic immunogenicity of a vaccine is a determinant of variant-specific responses to Omicron strains44. To address this issue, we analyzed serum responses after intranasal boosting at a lower 108 viral particle dose (Supplementary Fig. 5b–e), which elicited more comparable levels of spike-binding antibodies as intramuscular vaccination at a 109 viral particle dose (Supplementary Fig. 5a). The fraction of XBB.1.5 spike-specific serum IgG still was higher in mRNA-1273-primed mice receiving low-dose intranasal ChAd-XBB.1.5 (range 0–4.6%, median 2.7%) than those receiving high-dose intramuscular ChAd-XBB.1.5 (range 0–2.5%, median 0%; Supplementary Fig. 5d). Serum neutralizing activity against XBB.1.5 pseudoviruses also trended higher in mice receiving low-dose intranasal versus high-dose intramuscular ChAd-XBB.1.5 (Supplementary Fig. 5e). Collectively, these data show that boosting with a variant-matched XBB.1.5 vaccine at a mucosal site after intramuscular priming with a Wuhan-1-specific mRNA vaccine results in enhanced XBB.1.5-specific serum antibody titers and neutralizing activity compared to intramuscular delivery of the same booster vaccine.

We also measured variant-specific responses in the mucosal fluid lining the respiratory tract. From the same cohort of mice that were boosted intranasally or intramuscularly with ChAd-XBB.1.5 after a primary mRNA-1273 series (Fig. 1), bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected 28 days after boosting, and IgG and IgA reactivity to Wuhan-1 and XBB.1.5 spike proteins was determined (Fig. 2a–c). IgG against both spike proteins was present in the BALF of all animals except those that received a single intramuscular injection of ChAd-XBB.1.5 (Fig. 2b). XBB.1.5 spike-specific IgG was detected in the BALF of mice that received 815/815/815, PBS/PBS/ChAd-XBB.1.5 [intranasal], and 1273/1273/ChAd-XBB.1.5 [intranasal] immunizations but not 1273/1273/815 or 1273/1273/ChAd-XBB.1.5 [intramuscular] immunizations. The strong correlation between levels of XBB.1.5 spike-specific IgG in BALF and serum (Supplementary Fig. 6a) suggests that circulating IgG might gain access to the BALF of these mice through permeability of the alveolar-capillary interface or neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn)-mediated transport45–49. In contrast, spike-binding IgA was detected in the BALF only in animals that received an intranasal booster (Fig. 2c). Mice primed with mRNA-1273 and boosted intranasally with ChAd-XBB.1.5 had measurable Wuhan-1 spike-binding (9 of 9), XBB.1.5 spike-binding (9 of 9), and XBB.1.5 spike-specific (8 of 9) IgA in the BALF, indicating activation of both variant-specific naïve B cells and cross-reactive MBCs. However, spike-binding IgA was detected in only 1 of 10 (to Wuhan-1 spike) and 2 of 10 (to XBB.1.5 spike) mice that received two PBS injections before a single intranasal ChAd-XBB.1.5 immunization, suggesting adjuvant effects from prior SARS-CoV-2 antigen exposures, potentially from memory CD4+ T cells38, which are absent in mice immunized with a single vaccine dose. As a higher fraction of XBB.1.5 spike-specific IgA compared to IgG was observed in the BALF of 1273/1273/ChAd-XBB.1.5 [intranasal] mice, de novo B cell response elicited by intranasal XBB.1.5-matched vaccines in the respiratory tract likely differentiated more into IgA- than IgG-secreting cells (Fig. 2e). Functional analysis of the antibodies in the BALF showed neutralization of XBB.1.5 in mice immunized with 815/815/815 and those receiving a ChAd-XBB.1.5 booster by an intranasal route (Fig. 2f). We next analyzed the spike specificity of MBCs in the DLN of mice boosted intranasally (mediastinal LN) or intramuscularly (inguinal LN) with ChAd-XBB.1.5. Animals were primed with two doses of mRNA-1273 1 month apart and then boosted 3 months later with ChAd-XBB.1.5 via intramuscular or intranasal routes (Fig. 3a). Approximately 6 months later, the DLN proximal to the booster site was analyzed. Naïve mice were included as negative controls for spike staining. To determine spike-specific binding by B cell receptors (BCR) on the surface of MBCs (defined as IgDlowCD138-CD19+CD38highGL7low cells; Fig. 3b), samples were stained concurrently with fluorescently labeled Wuhan-1 and XBB.1.5 spike protein probes and analyzed by flow cytometry (Fig. 3c). Given that repeated intramuscular boosting with mRNA-1273.815 enhances XBB.1.5-specific immunity (Supplementary Fig. 3), we next determined whether stronger variant-specific responses also could be achieved through repeated intranasal ChAd-XBB.1.5 vaccinations. Mice primed intramuscularly with a two-dose series of mRNA-1273 were boosted intranasally 3 months later with two doses of ChAd-XBB.1.5 at a 1-month interval (Supplementary Fig. 8a). As expected, serum collected prior to XBB.1.5 boosting did not show XBB.1.5 spike-specific binding or neutralizing activity (Supplementary Fig. 8b–d). Serum harvested after the second ChAd-XBB.1.5 booster exhibited stronger XBB.1.5-specific IgG responses than those measured after the first dose (post-1st booster: range 7–21%, median 17%; post-2nd booster: range 15–56%, median 26%). The increase in variant-specific binding titers was accompanied by higher XBB.1.5 neutralizing activity after the second booster dose. Thus, repeated intranasal boosting with ChAd-XBB.1.5 augments XBB.1.5-specific neutralizing serum antibody responses. Although we observed greater XBB.1.5-specific responses in mice given two intranasal boosters with ChAd-XBB.1.5 than two intramuscular doses of mRNA-1273.815 (Supplementary Fig. 3), additional studies are needed to determine whether this effect is due to differences in vaccine platform or boosting site.

Similar findings were observed in a separate cohort that received an additional intermediate Wuhan-1/BA.5 bivalent mRNA booster. Mice were primed with two doses of mRNA-1273, boosted intramuscularly with mRNA-1273.222 1 month later, and then administered two doses of intranasal ChAd-XBB.1.5 1 month apart (Supplementary Fig. 8e). Serum antibodies that were not cross-reactive to Wuhan-1 or BA.5 spike and neutralized XBB.1.5 infection developed after one intranasal ChAd-XBB.1.5 booster and expanded after a second ChAd-XBB.1.5 dose (Supplementary Fig. 8f–h). Together, these results show that intranasal ChAd-XBB.1.5 boosting induces XBB.1.5-specific antibodies in mice imprinted by two doses of intramuscular Wuhan-1 mRNA vaccine with or without an additional Wuhan-1/BA.5 bivalent booster, and that the fraction of variant-specific responses is enhanced by repeated intranasal boosting with variant-matched vaccines.

Discussion

We investigated multiple approaches to mitigate imprinting effects from prior SARS-CoV-2 vaccinations and elicit stronger variant-specific responses, which are increasingly important for protection against emerging SARS-CoV-2 strains. Whereas the vaccination interval and site of intramuscular boosting did not appreciably affect the fraction of Omicron-specific serum antibodies elicited by variant-matched booster vaccines, enhanced variant-specific responses were observed in mice boosted with higher antigen doses, those boosted twice with the same variant-matched vaccine, and animals boosted intranasally after receiving intramuscular vaccines. In mice primed with intranasal vaccines, XBB.1.5 spike-specific IgA responses were also stronger in intranasally boosted than in intramuscularly boosted mice. Our results suggest that intranasal boosting may mitigate imprinting effects and induce booster-specific serum and mucosal responses that neutralize variant viruses.

We focused on approaches to strengthen variant-specific responses in the context of a single booster dose that targets a specified variant spike protein. Other strategies to alleviate imprinting effects have been investigated, including repeated exposures to the variant strain (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 8)16,50, inclusion of dendritic cell-activating adjuvants in the booster vaccine50, and immune focusing on unique epitopes either by epitope masking51 or removal of cross-reactive epitopes from the vaccine antigen. Boosting with variant strains that are more antigenically distant from the priming strain also may increase induction of variant-specific responses52.

We observed comparable Omicron spike-specific serum antibody responses in mice boosted intramuscularly in different legs. Although multiple studies have investigated how this variable affects immune responses, the results are conflicting21–28. Whereas most studies measured overall antibody binding titers and neutralizing activity after vaccination, few investigated how these factors affect de novo variant-specific responses after boosting with variant antigens. One study examined the specificity of GC B cells isolated from mice primed with the influenza virus H1 hemagglutinin (HA) protein and then boosted ipsilaterally or contralaterally with the H3 HA protein21. Whereas most cells reacted with H3 HA in both groups, a higher fraction of H3 HA-specific cells was observed in mice boosted in the contralateral leg. Another study characterizing GC B cells from mice primed with SARS-CoV-2 Wuhan-1 spike and then boosted with BA.1 spike reported a higher fraction of BA.1-biased cross-reactive cells in mice boosted in the contralateral leg at day 19 post-boost, although animals boosted in the ipsilateral leg achieved the same fraction at a later time point22. These studies, however, did not interrogate the specificity of serum antibodies. In our model, we show that the intramuscular injection site did not affect variant spike-specific serum antibody responses, consistent with our prior analysis showing comparable serum binding titers to Omicron BA.1 in mice boosted ipsilaterally or contralaterally with a Wuhan-1/BA.1 bivalent vaccine24.

We characterized variant spike-specific responses elicited by intranasal and intramuscular XBB.1.5-matched ChAd boosters following intramuscular mRNA or intranasal ChAd vaccine priming (results summarized and modeled in Fig. 6). Intramuscular Wuhan-1 mRNA vaccination imprinted both systemic and mucosal antibody responses, but intranasal boosters more efficiently bypassed the imprinting effects and elicited stronger variant-specific immunity. In the context of intramuscular boosting, we speculate that circulating and LN-resident cross-reactive, high-affinity MBCs may outcompete variant-specific, low-affinity naïve B cells for spike antigen. During mucosal boosting, although peripherally-primed CXCR3+ MBCs can be recruited into the lung38, their migration to the mucosa or bronchial-associated and nasal-associated lymphoid tissues (BALTs and NALTs) may not be efficient. We hypothesize that a reduced or absent BRM in mucosal-associated lymphoid tissues following intramuscular priming could create a niche for variant-specific B cells to expand and differentiate following heterologous antigen boosting. In the reverse sequence, intranasal ChAd-Wuhan-1 priming elicited both systemic and mucosal responses, likely imprinting both compartments. Presumably due to the presence of pre-existing mucosal BRM, XBB.1.5-specific IgA was detected at lower levels in intranasally primed-boosted mice than in intramuscularly primed animals that received a subsequent intranasal booster. Nevertheless, the induction of these antibodies, despite lower levels, suggests that the mucosal niche may not have been fully populated by a single intranasal dose of ChAd-Wuhan-1. Indeed, comparable fractions of XBB.1.5-specific antibody responses were achieved even after two intranasal immunizations with ChAd-Wuhan-1. We hypothesize that some portion of the memory response established at mucosal sites may emigrate or is shorter-lived than those in circulation. Thus, an unfilled mucosal niche may persist even after repeated intranasal vaccinations, allowing for induction of variant-specific responses. That said, the relative benefit of multiple intranasal versus intramuscular boosters in overcoming imprinting was less pronounced, suggesting that the advantages of intranasal boosting may depend on the site of antecedent antigen exposure. While both IgG and IgA are present in the BALF after intranasal vaccine boosting, their origin remains unclear. Recent studies have suggested that following intramuscular priming, the majority of mucosal IgA induced by intranasal boosting arises from pre-existing IgG+ MBCs in DLNs through T cell-mediated class switching37,38. Such findings are consistent with our observations, in which cross-reactive antibodies comprised most of the XBB.1.5 spike-reactive IgA pool. The remaining XBB.1.5-specific IgA likely originates from de novo stimulated B cells in respiratory tract lymphoid tissues. Among mice vaccinated intranasally with ChAd-XBB.1.5, animals primed with mRNA-1273 had higher XBB.1.5 spike-specific IgA titers in the BALF than those receiving PBS. This finding may be explained by the induction of CXCL9 and CXCL10 by memory CD4+ T cells, which attract CXCR3-expressing MBCs and mucosal-activated B cells38,53,54. In mice primed intramuscularly and boosted intranasally, the higher fraction of variant-specific antibodies in the IgA than IgG compartment supports the hypothesis that de novo responses after intranasal boosting predominantly produce IgA. IgG in the BALF likely comes from leakage across the alveolar-capillary interface, FcRn-mediated transport across epithelium, and, to a lesser extent, local production45–49,53. In animals vaccinated with three intramuscular doses of XBB.1.5 mRNA vaccines, IgG was detected in the BALF at 0.03% of serum levels (Supplementary Fig. 6b), which is consistent with reported rates of IgG accumulation (~0.1%)47. By contrast, in mice primed with mRNA-1273 and then boosted intranasally with ChAd-XBB.1.5, BALF IgG reached 0.3% of serum levels (Supplementary Fig. 6b). This higher fraction of BALF/serum IgG compared to 815/815/815 mice might reflect more efficient IgG transport driven by local inflammatory cues or greater mucosal production of IgG.

We observed a striking dependence of XBB.1.5 neutralizing activity on antibodies that did not react to the Wuhan-1 spike protein. In mice primed and boosted with Wuhan-1-based vaccines, serum neutralizing activity against XBB.1.5 was undetectable across vaccine platforms and administration routes, consistent with reports showing little or no neutralization of XBB lineage variants by Wuhan-1 spike vaccine-elicited antibodies2–4. While neutralization of earlier Omicron strains including BA.1 and BA.5 was mediated predominantly by cross-reactive antibodies in individuals who had been immunized with Wuhan-1 vaccines and then later boosted or infected with Omicron variants8,55,56, the contribution of cross-reactive and variant-specific antibodies to neutralization of more recent XBB variants has not been clearly defined. Although cross-reactive antibodies dominated neutralizing activity against XBB.1.5 in one study, it remains unclear how the complex exposure history to Omicron strains or vaccines in those individuals affected imprinting57. Using antibody depletion assays, we show that in mice primed with Wuhan-1 spike-based vaccines and boosted heterologously with XBB.1.5-matched vaccines, XBB.1.5 spike-specific antibodies in serum mediated much of the neutralization of XBB.1.5 virus, despite comprising a smaller fraction than the cross-reactive antibodies (Supplementary Fig. 10). In mice that also received an intermediate Wuhan-1/BA.5 bivalent vaccine, the exposure to an antecedent Omicron strain enabled induction of Wuhan-1-non-reactive, BA.5/XBB.1.5 cross-neutralizing antibodies after a single XBB.1.5 booster; this was presumably mediated by a selection of B cells with receptors having higher affinity to sites shared by Omicron variants. These findings highlight the critical functional role of antibodies not imprinted by the prototype Wuhan-1 vaccine and the need to identify approaches that elicit such responses.

We acknowledge several limitations in our study: (1) We showed greater induction of variant-specific antibody responses by intranasal than intramuscular boosting with ChAd vaccines but did not test other vaccine platforms; (2) We evaluated responses in mice exposed only to the spike proteins of Wuhan-1, Omicron BA.5, and XBB.1.5. The translational relevance of findings to humans warrants further study, as species-differences and the complex exposure history of the human population could affect responses; (3) Our boosting interval analysis was conducted at relatively short times (3, 6, and 12 months) due to the life span of laboratory mice. Longer intervals and waning memory before final mRNA-1273.529 or mRNA-1273.815 boosting could enable greater induction of BA.1 or XBB.1.5 variant-specific B cell responses; (4) We did not assess variant-specific responses in the context of hybrid infection- and vaccine-induced immunity. Although our study showed a greater benefit of intranasal over intramuscular boosting in overcoming imprinting in mice primed with intramuscular vaccines, its translational relevance in humans with a complex history of infections remains to be determined; (5) While we report the potential of intranasal boosting to overcome the effects of immune imprinting, the precise cellular mechanisms for how this occurs warrant further studies; and (6) Our studies evaluate variant-specific responses at the polyclonal level in serum. Single B cell repertoire and somatic hypermutation analyses of MBCs can provide additional insight as to how vaccination route impacts imprinted responses.

In summary, our study demonstrates that intranasal boosting with variant-matched vaccines mitigates imprinting and elicits stronger variant-specific responses in serum, BALF, and DLNs than intramuscular boosting. We observed a striking dependence on XBB.1.5 spike-reactive, Wuhan-1-non-reactive antibodies for effective neutralization of the XBB.1.5 variant, even though neutralization of other sarbecoviruses was mediated by cross-reactive antibodies. Thus, while intranasal delivery of variant-matched vaccines can enhance variant-specific antibody induction in mice, the utility of this strategy as well as others (e.g., repetitive variant, higher dose, and potentially, longer interval boosting) warrants further study in non-human primates or humans in the context of continuously evolving strains.

Methods

Ethics

All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the recommendations in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the NIH. Animals were monitored regularly, and those reaching predefined humane endpoints were sacrificed, including for significant body weight loss (>20%), impaired mobility or inability to access food and water, or other signs of pain or distress. The protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Washington University School of Medicine (assurance number A3381–01, protocol 24-0151).

Cells and viruses

The Vero-TMPRSS2 cell line was previously generated in our laboratory58. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 10 mM HEPES pH 7.3, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 1X nonessential amino acids, 100 U/mL of penicillin–streptomycin, and 5 μg/mL of blasticidin.

Chimeric VSV pseudoviruses expressing spike proteins corresponding to the SARS-CoV-2 Wuhan-1 virus, XBB.1.5, JN.1, and other coronaviruses (Pang/GD and SARS-CoV-1) were produced as described previously59. Briefly, the spike genes of different coronaviruses (SARS-CoV-2 Wuhan-1, GenBank MN908947.3; XBB.1.5, GenBank OP790748.1; and JN.1, GenBank OR950792), Pang/GD (GenBank MT799524.1), and SARS-CoV-1 (GenBank JN854286.1) were synthesized (Integrated DNA Technologies) and inserted into an infectious cDNA clone of VSV60,61 as described previously59. Modifications to the cytoplasmic tail (21 amino acid deletion) of the spike genes were introduced to promote secretion of viral particles. Other VSV N, P, L and G expression plasmids have been described61. Recovery of recombinant VSV was performed as described61, and aliquots were stored at −80 °C.

Chimpanzee adenovirus vectors

The ChAd-Wuhan-1 and ChAd-XBB.1.5 vaccine vectors were derived from a simian Ad36 backbone as described29,62,63. The replication-incompetent ChAd vectors were constructed to express the mouse codon-optimized sequence of Wuhan-1 or XBB.1.5 prefusion-stabilized spike protein with six proline residues (F817P, A892P, A899P, A942P, K986P and V987P) and furin cleavage site substitutions (residues 682–686). The vectors were rescued by transfection in HEK-293 cells and purified by CsCl density-gradient ultracentrifugation. Viral particle concentration was determined by spectrophotometry at 260 nm as described64.

Mouse experiments

Six-week-old female C57BL/6J or K18-hACE2 mice purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Cat. #000664 and #034860, respectively) were used. Animals were housed in groups of five and fed standard chow diets. Photoperiod was 12 h on:12 h off dark/light cycle. The ambient animal room temperature was 70 °F, controlled within ±2 °F. The room humidity was 50%, controlled within ±5%. Mice were immunized, and serum and tissue samples were obtained at specified time points as described in the Figure legends. All mRNA vaccines used are preclinical materials and were administered intramuscularly at a 0.25 µg per mouse dose, unless otherwise specified (Supplementary Fig. 2d, e and Supplementary Fig. 3i–l). ChAd vaccinations were performed intramuscularly in unanesthetized mice or intranasally under anesthesia with 108 or 109 viral particles (depending on the experiment) in 50 µL per mouse. In vivo studies were not blinded, and mice were randomly assigned to vaccination groups. No sample-size calculations were performed to power each animal study. Instead, sample sizes were determined based on prior in vivo immunization experiments.

Viral antigens

Recombinant soluble spike proteins from Wuhan-1, BA.1, BA.5, and XBB.1.5 SARS-CoV-2 strains were expressed as described65–67. Recombinant proteins were produced in Expi293F cells (Thermo Fisher, Cat. #A14635) by transfection of DNA using the ExpiFectamine 293 Transfection Kit (Thermo Fisher, Cat. #A14524). For biotinylation of Avi-tagged recombinant spike proteins, Avi-tagged spike expression constructs were co-transfected with BirA-Flag expression plasmid (Addgene, Cat. #64395) at a molar ratio of 10:1 (spike:BirA-Flag). Sixteen to 18 h post-transfection, cells were supplemented with 1 mM D-Biotin (Sigma, Cat. #2031) containing enhancer cocktail (Thermo Fisher, Cat. #A14524). Supernatants were harvested at 3 to 5 days post-transfection. Recombinant proteins were then purified using Strep-Tactin XT resin (IBA Lifesciences, Cat. #2-5010), buffer exchanged into PBS, and concentrated using Amicon Ultracel centrifugal filters (EMD Millipore, Cat. #UFC905096).

Untagged proteins for use in antibody depletion and spike-binding assays were biotinylated with EZ-Link™ NHS-PEG4-Biotin (Thermo Fisher, Cat. # A39259) for 2 h at 4 °C and processed through Zeba spin desalting columns (Thermo Fisher) to remove excess unbound biotin. Aliquots were flash-frozen and stored at −80 °C.

Biotinylated Avi-tagged proteins used for flow cytometry staining were incubated with a 1.04-fold molar excess of APC-, BV421-, BV570-, or BV711-conjugated streptavidin (BioLegend, Cat. #405207, 405225, 405227, and 405241) for 1 h at 4 °C. Free biotin (Avidity, Cat. #BIO200) was added at 5 µM and incubated for 15 min at 4 °C to block unoccupied biotin binding sites on streptavidin. The complexed probes were mixed with an equal volume of glycerol and stored at −20 °C.

Spike-binding assay

Antibody binding to spike proteins was measured as previously described8. Briefly, streptavidin beads of different fluorescent intensity (Spherotech, Cat. #SVF3-2552) were incubated with recombinant spike proteins from Wuhan-1, BA.1, or XBB.1.5 SARS-CoV-2 strains and then blocked with free biotin (Avidity, Cat. #BIO200). Beads loaded with different spike proteins were then pooled and incubated with serially diluted depleted serum samples. For detection of spike-binding IgG titers, beads were washed twice with FACS buffer (PBS supplemented with 2% FBS, 2 mM EDTA, and 0.1% NaN3) and stained with anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor™ 647 (Thermo Fisher, Cat. #A-21235, 1:3000) or anti-human IgG Alexa Fluor™ 647 (Thermo Fisher, Cat. #A-21445, 1:3000) for 10 min at room temperature in dark. For detection of spike-binding IgA titers, beads were stained with goat anti-mouse IgA-Alexa Fluor™ 647 (SouthernBiotech, Cat. #1040-31, 1:1500) for 1 h at room temperature. After two washes with FACS buffer, beads were resuspended in FACS buffer and analyzed on an iQue®3 flow cytometer (Sartorius). Population gating and analysis of fluorescence intensity were performed using iQue Forecyt 9.0 software. Quantification of IgG titers was performed by interpolation with an anti-spike CV3-25 monoclonal antibody standard curve. Interpolated values were multiplied by the sample dilution factor, and the final binding titers were shown as ng/mL equivalents of CV3-25. Spike-binding IgA antibodies were presented as endpoint titers, determined using a four-parameter logistic curve fit in GraphPad Prism version 11.

Antibody depletion assay

Depletion of spike-reactive antibodies was performed as described8. Streptavidin magnetic beads (BioLabs, Cat. #S1420S) were incubated with recombinant biotinylated spike proteins from SARS-CoV-2 Wuhan-1 or BA.5 strains. Diluted serum samples were incubated with Wuhan-1 spike-loaded beads or a 1:1 mixture of Wuhan-1 spike-loaded beads and BA.5 spike-loaded beads to allow for binding of antibodies. Depleted sera were collected on the KingFisher Flex extraction robot (Thermo Fisher) and stored at 4 °C.

Pseudovirus neutralization assay

Vero-TMPRSS2 cells were seeded into 96-well plates in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. The next day, samples or PBS were serially diluted with DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS and mixed with VSV pseudoviruses and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. BALF samples assayed for neutralization were concentrated ~10-fold using Amicon ultra centrifugal filters (Millipore Sigma, Cat. #UFC8030). Antibody-virus complexes were added to Vero-TMPRSS2 cell monolayers and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, cells were overlaid with 1% (w/v) methylcellulose in minimum essential medium (MEM). Plates were harvested 20 h (Wuhan-1, XBB.1.5, and JN.1), 26 h (SARS-CoV-1), or 30 h (Pang/GD) later by removing overlays and fixing with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 20 min at room temperature. Plates were washed and sequentially incubated with anti-VSV matrix protein antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. #MABF2347, 1:5,000) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. #A5278, 1:750) in PBS supplemented with 0.1% saponin and 0.1% bovine serum albumin. Pseudovirus-infected cell foci were visualized using TrueBlue peroxidase substrate (KPL, Cat. #5510-0050) and quantitated on an ImmunoSpot analyzer (Cellular Technology).

Spike-specific B cell staining

DLN were processed through 70 µm nylon mesh into single cell suspensions and incubated with purified anti-mouse CD16/32 (BioLegend, clone 93, 1:100) for 15 min at 4 °C to block Fcγ receptor binding. Cells were then stained with a cocktail of labeled antibodies and complexed spike probes for 30 min at 4 °C in dark: fixable viability dye eFluor 506 (Invitrogen, Cat. #65-0866-18, 1:1000), TCRβ-Alexa Fluor 700 (BioLegend, Cat. #109224, clone H57-597, 1:400), CD19-APC/Fire 750 (BioLegend, Cat. #115558, clone 6D5, 1:200), IgD-PerCP/Cy5.5 (BioLegend, Cat. #405710, clone 11-26 c.2a, 1:400), TACI-PE (BioLegend, Cat. #133404, clone 8F10, 1:200), CD138-BV605 (BioLegend, Cat. #142516, clone 281-2, 1:400), CD38-PE/Cy7 (BioLegend, Cat. #102718, clone 90, 1:800), GL7-Alexa Fluor 488 (BioLegend, Cat. #144612, clone GL7, 1:2000), streptavidin-APC-labeled Wuhan-1 spike (1:50), streptavidin-BV421-labeled Wuhan-1 spike (1:50), streptavidin-BV570-labeled XBB.1.5 spike (1:50), and streptavidin-BV711-labeled XBB.1.5 spike (1:50). Cells were washed twice and resuspended with FACS buffer for data acquisition on an Aurora (Cytek) spectral flow cytometer and analyzed with FlowJo software version 10.10.0.

Statistical analysis

P values were determined using GraphPad Prism version 11. Tests (Mann-Whitney test with Bonferroni correction, Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA with Dunn’s post-test, Friedman test with Dunn’s post-test, Wilcoxon sign-ranked test, and lognormal ordinary one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-test), number of mice, and statistical comparison groups are indicated in the Figure legends.
