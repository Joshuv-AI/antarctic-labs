Commentary: Drain Amylase Trend as a Noninvasive Gatekeeper for Oral Intake after Esophagectomy
Authors: Woo Sik Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSee Article page 165.

Anastomotic leak at the cervical esophagogastric anastomosis—defined by consensus as a full-thickness defect of the anastomosis or conduit [1]—remains one of the most consequential complications after esophagectomy. It can prolong hospitalization, predispose patients to stricture, and occasionally delay adjuvant therapy [2]. A long-standing clinical question is when oral intake can be safely resumed. Contrast esophagraphy has traditionally been used for this purpose: the study is typically performed approximately 1 week after surgery, when the anastomosis is expected to have healed, and oral feeding is started if no leak is detected [2,3].

Contrast esophagraphy, however, has consistently shown low sensitivity for detecting anastomotic leaks [4,5], and the present study was no exception, reporting a sensitivity of only 28.6% [2]. Consequently, a normal esophagram is not fully reassuring. A leak may still become apparent after feeding has begun, by which point the wound bed may be contaminated and the clinical course may have worsened [5]. These limitations have prompted some centers to evaluate alternative strategies, including routine endoscopy [6] and drain fluid amylase measurement [3,7,8]. Drain fluid amylase is particularly attractive because it can be measured noninvasively, and several groups have examined its use for leak detection [3,7,8].

Ting and colleagues contribute to this effort by proposing a trend-based drain amylase criterion [2]. In a single-center retrospective study of patients who underwent McKeown esophagectomy, they measured drain fluid amylase on postoperative day (POD) 3 and POD 7. A positive result required both a higher amylase level on POD 7 than on POD 3 and a POD 7 value greater than 80 IU/L. Among the 65 patients with amylase data in the combined cohort, this criterion achieved a sensitivity of 75%, a specificity of 96.5%, a negative predictive value of 96.5%, and an overall accuracy of 93.9% [2]. The strength of this approach lies less in the cutoff itself than in the combination of an absolute value with the direction of change: a single time point performed insufficiently, whereas incorporating the trend improved diagnostic accuracy [2]. The high negative predictive value is especially useful in practice because it suggests that feeding can be initiated with reasonable confidence after a negative result.

These results should be interpreted in light of how the cohort was assembled. Eight patients whose leaks were diagnosed before POD 7 were excluded; these early leaks are usually more clinically overt and severe [2], and their amylase trends were therefore not represented. This exclusion is important because a leak does not always manifest as a rise in drain amylase from POD 3 to POD 7. In our own series of 99 patients with daily amylase measurement, which included early leaks, the leak group showed peak values on POD 3, followed by a subsequent decline [7]. Viewed from this perspective, the criterion proposed by Ting and colleagues may apply best to patients whose leaks are not clinically overt by POD 7. The unusually low cutoff of 80 IU/L, which is well below the 150–1,800 IU/L range reported elsewhere [3,7,8], may reflect the same selection effect: after early leaks were removed, the analyzed cohort was weighted toward subtler leaks. These considerations do not diminish the study’s value, but they suggest that the criterion should be validated in a larger prospective cohort before it is widely applied.

Even with these caveats, drain fluid amylase has clear value as a noninvasive gatekeeper for the safe resumption of oral intake. In practice, the amylase trend can help guide the timing of feeding. When the trend, clinical signs, or inflammatory markers raise concern, delaying oral feeding or pursuing further evaluation with esophagram, computed tomography, or endoscopy is reasonable [9]. Cervical leaks often present late, sometimes after an initially reassuring evaluation. A single negative test should therefore not eliminate clinical suspicion, and the patient’s clinical course should still be monitored closely after feeding has begun [5].

Ting and colleagues deserve credit for translating a simple laboratory measurement into a practical, trend-based protocol [2]. The main value of this approach lies not in the exact cutoff, but in the role of drain fluid amylase as a first-line gatekeeper for timing oral intake. It is most useful not as a stand-alone test, but as an initial screening step, with additional evaluation performed when the patient’s condition raises concern. Used in this way, drain fluid amylase can help guide clinical decision-making, but it should support— not replace—clinical judgment.
