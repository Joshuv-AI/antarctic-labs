USP Gene Network Modulation and Osmoprotection Define Salt Resilience in Chenopodium quinoa Genotypes
Authors: Hajira Imran, Alvina Gul, Rabia Amir, Faiza Munir, Rehan Zafar Paracha, Muhammad Faraz Bhatti, Mustafeez Mujtaba Babar, Muhammad Tahir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoil salinity poses a severe threat to global food security, making the halophytic crop Chenopodium quinoa a vital model for sustainable agriculture. While quinoa is known for its extreme salt tolerance, the molecular mechanisms integrating stress perception with metabolic adaptation remain poorly defined. This study employs a multi-level approach, integrating morphological, physiological, biochemical, transcriptomic, and metabolomic analyses, to dissect the complex adaptation strategies across eight advanced quinoa lines. Morphological and physiological screening identified three distinct survival strategies: transient high-productivity, stress-induced optimal performance, and conservative stability. We demonstrated that salt resilience is not merely a survival response but a highly coordinated regulatory program driven by one of the key regulators such as the Universal Stress Protein (USP) gene network. Key physiological findings revealed a progressive decline in photosynthetic efficiency (ΦPSII) and rising oxidative damage (MDA), yet tolerant lines maintained superior photoprotection through highly regulated heat dissipation (ΦNPQ). The cellular defense involved the mobilization of enzymatic scavengers (superoxide dismutase and peroxidase), peaking at the highest stress level. Transcriptomic analysis of the USP gene family showed dynamic, tissue-specific regulation at moderate stress and activation of ion exclusion genes in the roots at 300 mM salinity. Metabolomic profiling identified one variety Q-33 as highly resilient, characterized by coordinated metabolic reprogramming, specifically strong induction of osmolytes and volatile defense compounds. Introduction

Soil salinity is expected to affect more than 50% of agricultural land by 2050. Recently, research on soil salinity and its effects on the environment has been of major scientific interest because it strongly affects crop production worldwide. Sustainable crop production is one of the aspects of cash crop halophytes, like quinoa plants. These tolerant crops can be used to minimize the negative effects of soil salinity. Halophyte species can be used in salt barren zones, alluvial flat lands, and desert steppes, where they help in sand fixing and conservation of soil and water1. The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) has selected Chenopodium quinoa as one of the few crops that are destined for biosaline agriculture, which will have a significant role in food security in the future. Similar to many other halophytes, quinoa can grow and reproduce in soils with salinity levels as high as seawater. According to a report published by FAO in 2017, the world population is expected to reach 10 billion by 2050. It is also expected that the demand for staple crops as well as livestock will increase by as much as 60% till then2. Hence, it needs time to grow crops that can survive abiotic stresses, including high temperatures, floods, low temperatures, snow and frost, drought, and salinity in the soil. It is also expected that by 2050, many of the agricultural lands on which cultivation is performed will be turned into marginal lands3. To keep such lands productive while maintaining sustainable food security, we need highly resistant and tolerant crops that can replace current staple foods4. Belonging to the family Chenopodiaceae, Quinoa is an herbaceous plant. The family also includes other plants with high nutritional value, like spinach and beets. There are around 250 known species of Chenopodium in the world. However, this herb is native to South America.

The main edible part of the plant is its seeds, having various colors, including white, yellow, red, and black5. In recognition of its importance and nutritive qualities, the United Nations declared 2013 as the year of Quinoa, which also increased awareness about quinoa, leading to many countries actively participating in the growth of quinoa6. This crop has a remarkable ability to grow in different agro-ecological conditions. The crop is estimated to resist temperatures from -4 to 38 degrees centigrade. Most of the cultivars of quinoa are seen to withstand saline environments, cold, high humidities, and desert climates. The crop produces a satisfactory yield with rainfall of 100–200 mm, but that does not mean that it cannot grow under drought. It is a highly tolerant and water-efficient plant and hence provides considerable yield in low soil moisture. It can be an exceptional source for sustainable agriculture systems7. It is estimated that almost 20% of all of the irrigated land in the world has been affected by salinity. It is also expected that this problem will increase in the future instead of decreasing. Usually, under saline conditions, the plant decreases its transpiration by reducing the stomatal density and increasing the water use efficiency. One of the activities with which plants mitigate salinity stress is through potassium consumption. Plants absorb potassium from the soil and maintain the potassium ion/ sodium ion ratio, resulting in proper osmotic regulation in the cells8,9.

The physiological processes that are affected by soil salinity include reduction in turgor pressure in the leaf, closure of stomata in the leaves, and decline in the photosynthetic rate of the plants. All these processes lead to the stopping of plant growth10. Soil salinity is increasing at a rate of 1–2 million hectares per year. This will result in a significant portion of agricultural land becoming unsuitable for cultivation. Mostly in the dryland regions, soil evaporation takes place, which results in precipitation and enhances the chances of soil salinity11. One of the most studied abiotic stresses in quinoa plants is salinity. The reduction of freshwater resources due to global warming has led to an increase in soil salinization. Quinoa is a facultative halophyte. Halophytes are plants that are known to grow and provide proper yield in soil with high salt conditions. Halophytes can produce viable seeds in soil with salt concentrations of more than 200mM. Soils with this much salt content are lethal for most of plants12.

In response to salinity in the soil, plants exhibit conditions of hyperosmotic stress in different parts. They shut down their functions as they are unable to absorb water efficiently. All of this results in a reduction of metabolism and photosynthesis. However, plants like quinoa have gained the ability to deal with hyperosmotic stress efficiently. With the increase in salinity content in the soil, quinoa plants are observed to reduce water loss and optimize water absorption. Besides this, it has also been seen that plants can reduce the toxicity of salt by compartmentalizing sodium ions in vacuoles13. A study has been conducted in which it is revealed that quinoa varieties are most tolerant to salinity stress at the concentration of 100–200 mM sodium chloride. After that, the yield starts decreasing14. Moreover, it has also been observed that salt concentrations higher than 400 mM result in significant inhibition of seed germination. At 250 mM NaCl, research has been conducted on 182 accessions of quinoa plants. The results prove that around 25% of accessions show a 60% germination rate at such high salt concentrations.15,16 Salinity tolerance in quinoa plants can also be enhanced by the treatment of soil with phosphorus fertilizer. It has been observed that phosphorus fertilization helps improve essential nutrient content as well as nutrient uptake by quinoa plants. It can be said that phosphorus fertilization can be used as a coping mechanism for plants under saline conditions17.

Salt-tolerant plants have two mechanisms to enhance salinity resistance and defense mechanisms. First, these plants avoid the consumption of sodium and chloride ions through their leaves. The process is known as ion exclusion, in which these plants rely on osmotic solutes to maintain their electrolyte and ionic balance18. Secondly, the tissue tolerance in which plants absorb these ions from the soil sequesters them into multiple vacuoles and uses them as osmotic shuttles. Under higher saline conditions, plants reduce their transpiration rate to maintain turgor pressure19. The quinoa plants compartmentalize sodium and chloride ions into vacuoles and produce species-compatible solutes out of these. These include mannitol, glycine betaine, sorbitol, proline, pinitol, and inositol. The concentrated sodium and chloride ions within the vacuole not only reduce the chances of salt toxicity in the cytoplasm but also adjust the cytosolic diffusion pattern, which leads to the enhancement of the diffusion potential of the cells20. Tolerance to salt in the soil is a very complex trait that requires a coordinated response of plants through physiological processes that involve the maintenance of osmotic and ionic cycles that are affected by salinity. One of the very first responses to salt stress is the closing of stomata to maintain the turgor pressure. Osmolyte concentration in root tissue is increased which further helps in maintaining turgor pressure. The major response is an osmotic adjustment, which is done by the synthesis of organic compounds or by sequestering sodium and chloride ions in vacuoles. Plants must deal with several issues during the salt stress. One of the major challenges is to transport water under salt stress. Quinoa plants have several strategies to keep the sodium and chloride ion levels lower in the cytosol, especially in the mesophyll cells of the leaves. Quinoa plants secrete the ions from root tissues back to the root medium. They can also be retrieved from xylem parenchyma cells by ion transporters21. Salinity affects the plants through a complex procedure. It modifies the osmotic balance, ion homeostasis, and regulation of reactive oxygen species. In an experiment, six different quinoa cultivars obtained from Chile were grown under conditions where they were applied with three different salinity levels. It was observed that as the salinity conditions increase, the yield of the crop is negatively impacted. Two genotypes of quinoa, Q21, and AMES13761, showed higher yield and stability under saline conditions than other genotypes. The experiment suggests that quinoa biodiversity should be protected to grow more sustainable crops and better seedling establishment in drought and saline conditions (Iftikhar22). Studies have shown that 100mM sodium chloride solution has minimum to no effect on biomass production and chloroplast ultrastructure in quinoa plants. Under 300 mM sodium chloride concentrations, it has been observed that biomass production is reduced, but there are no symptoms of toxicity. Prolonged exposure to salt stress leads to damage in the chloroplast, which is identified by swelling in thylakoids, deformation of the envelope, collection of starch grains, and plastoglobuli23. Two contrasting cultivars of quinoa, one salt-resistant, and one salt-sensitive, were treated with salt. It was observed that salinity treatment decreases the photosynthetic assimilation of carbon dioxide by 67% in salt-sensitive varieties and only 25% in salt-resistant varieties24,25. Compost and humic acid were used to enhance the fertility of saline irrigational water to be applied to quinoa plants. It was observed that 3% compost and 3% humic acid have a significant effect in mitigating the detrimental effects of saline water on quinoa plants. It not only enhances the bioavailability of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium in the soil but is also associated with an increase in plant height, fresh weight, and plant dry matter26. Quinoa exhibits a genotype-specific molecular response to salinity stress, characterized by the differential regulation of ion transporters, osmoprotectant synthesis, and antioxidant defense mechanisms. We hypothesize that salt-tolerant quinoa genotypes achieve superior stress adaptation by maintaining ionic homeostasis through Na+ exclusion and K+ retention, enhancing ROS scavenging via upregulated antioxidant enzymes, and activating stress-responsive genes involved in stress-induced metabolic pathways . These molecular signatures collectively contribute to sustained photosynthetic efficiency, improved water use efficiency, and better growth under saline conditions. Identifying these key molecular markers will provide valuable targets for breeding salt-resilient quinoa varieties. This study aims to discover molecular signatures of advanced quinoa lines under saline conditions. This may lead to a better understanding of the processes involved in adapting to soil salinization.

Materials and methods

Growth conditions

The experiment was conducted at the Atta-ur-Rahman School of Applied Biosciences (ASAB). Eight advanced C. quinoa lines (Q-7, Q-18, Q-33, Q-45, Q-49, Q-128, Q-184, and Q-192) were obtained from the University of Agriculture, Faisalabad, Pakistan, to evaluate genotype-specific responses to salinity stress. Seeds were surface-sterilized and sown in a standard potting mix. Following a 4–5 day germination period, 5-day-old seedlings were transplanted from the soil into a hydroponic cultivation system for further treatment and analysis27–29.

Experimental design

Five-day-old seedlings were transplanted into 3.5 L black hydroponic containers (15 × 30 × 15 cm) containing Hoagland’s solution. The experiment was conducted using a half-strength (0.5 ×) Hoagland nutrient solution to ensure adequate nutrient availability while maintaining a lower initial ionic background. The basal Electrical Conductivity (EC) of the control solution was approximately 1.2 dS/m. Following the induction of salt stress, the total EC values for the 100, 200, and 300 mM NaCl treatments were approximately 11.9, 21.5, and 30.6 dS/m, respectively. The nutrient solution was maintained at a constant 3 L volume through daily replenishment and was completely replaced weekly to ensure nutrient stability. Continuous aeration was provided by an air pump (PA:100 Jebao®) with an output capacity of 100 L/min (220–240 V). The experiment comprised four treatment groups, including an untreated control. Seedlings were cultivated under controlled environmental conditions: a 16 h photoperiod with a Photosynthetically Active Radiation (PAR) of 220 mol m–2 s–1, 55–65% relative humidity, and a 21 °C/16 °C day/night temperature cycle. Upon reaching the 8-leaf stage, plants were subjected to varying NaCl concentrations for seven days to evaluate the effects of prolonged salinity stress28–30. After 14 days of growth in the control medium, three plants per treatment were harvested for morphological assessment. Photosynthetic activity was quantified using the PhotosynQ MultispeQ v2.031. Harvested shoot and root tissues were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis. All procedures involving plant materials complied with relevant international and institutional guidelines.

Biochemical analysis and malondialdehyde quantification

Frozen plant samples were thawed on ice, and 0.5 g of tissue was homogenized in pre-cooled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) using a chilled, autoclaved mortar and pestle. The resulting homogenate was transferred to 15 mL Falcon tubes and subsequently aliquoted into microcentrifuge tubes. To isolate the enzyme extract, samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 60 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected and stored at 4°C for immediate biochemical analysis. Antioxidant enzyme activities, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), and glutathione reductase (GR), were quantified following the protocols described by Senthilkumar et al.32,33. Additionally, lipid peroxidation was assessed through malondialdehyde (MDA) quantification, a critical biochemical marker for evaluating cellular damage induced by abiotic stressors, such as drought33,33

Whole metabolome profile using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry

Three C. quinoa varieties (SV1/Q-192, SV2/Q-33, and SV3/Q-128) were selected from an initial pool of eight based on superior morphological and physiological performance under abiotic stress. For metabolite extraction, 1 g of coarsely ground dried leaf material was processed using a 1:10 (w/v) ratio with 10 mL of HPLC-grade methanol and macerated for 12 h. Following filtration through Whatman No. 1 paper, the extracts were analyzed using a GC–MS system equipped with an SH-Rxi-5Sil MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 µm film thickness). Helium served as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 5 mL/min, utilizing a 10:1 split injection mode.

To ensure high-quality data for publication, peak area percentages were normalized to the total column area for each condition, accounting for relative abundance variations across the 2,492 total MS records. Data quality was verified by a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) > 10:1 and a retention time (RT) relative standard deviation (RSD) of < 0.3%. A stringent filtering process was applied to remove 200 contaminant records (8.0%), such as siloxanes and phthalates, identified by characteristic mass fragments like m/z 149. Metabolite identification reached multiple confidence levels: primary markers were cross-validated against the NIST, PubChem, and MassBank databases, combined with Kovats retention index (RI) predictions, yielding an error margin of < 0.08 min. Statistical testing of differential metabolites involved ranking by relative peak area to identify the “Top 80” compounds, representing 78.9% of total abundance, for pathway mapping. Genetic divergence was quantified using Jaccard similarity indices, while salt stress intensity was statistically evaluated through a derived Osmolyte Index and an Oxidative Stress Index to distinguish varietal resilience (Table 1).

Primer synthesis, RNA extraction, quantitative real-time reverse transcription PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from C. quinoa shoot and root tissues using TRIzol reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 500 μL of TRIzol was added to 200 mg of homogenized tissue. The quality and concentration of the extracted RNA were verified before cDNA synthesis was performed using the ABClonal cDNA synthesis kit. To analyze the expression profiles of selected Universal Stress Protein (USP) genes under salinity stress, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted using ABClonal 2X Universal SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (RK21203) on both 96-well and 384-well plates. Primers were designed using Primer3Plus (version 2.4.2)34 and validated via Primer Stat35,specific sequences are provided in Table 2. Actin served as the internal reference gene for normalization (McDowell et al., 1996). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the Pfaffl method to account for individual primer efficiencies. Statistical significance was evaluated through a two-way repeated measures ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

Experiments were performed in triplicate, with data expressed as mean ± SEM. Data distribution was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homoscedasticity using the Brown-Forsythe test. Differences between genotypes and salinity levels were analyzed via two-way ANOVA (Genotype Salinity). To account for multiple comparisons, -values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedure, particularly for gene expression and metabolic data. Post-hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey’s HSD test. Statistical significance was defined at an FDR-adjusted. Pearson’s correlation was tested for morphological, physiological and gene expression datasets to check system level interactions.. All analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism. (Prism—GraphPad)36.

Results

Differential morphological adaptations of advanced quinoa genotypes to increasing salinity stress

The morphological response of eight advanced quinoa lines was evaluated under varying saline conditions, including control (0 mM), 100 mM, 200 mM, and 300 mM NaCl treatments. Significant genotypic variation was observed in shoot growth performance, allowing the classification of the lines into highly tolerant, moderately tolerant, and susceptible groups.

The two-way ANOVA results for total plant length across different salinity levels (0 mM, 100 mM, 200 mM, 300 mM) revealed significant effects of salinity on quinoa growth. At higher salinity levels, particularly 200 mM and 300 mM, plant length significantly decreased in several quinoa lines. The study analyzing the effect of increasing salinity on the Total Plant Length of eight advanced quinoa lines reveals a differential response to salt stress. Varieties Q-128 and Q-184 demonstrated the strongest growth, with Q-128 achieving the highest overall length under control conditions (≈46cm) and maintaining a substantial length even at the highest salinity (≈31cm), despite a statistically significant reduction at 200mM and 300mM. Conversely, varieties Q-7 and Q-49 were highly salt-sensitive, exhibiting a significant decline in plant length compared to their controls, especially Q-7 at 200mM and 300mM (** and *). A third group of varieties, Q-18, Q-33, and Q-192, showed remarkable stability, with plant length remaining constant across all four salinity levels with no statistically significant differences (ns) detected between any conditions, although their overall length was low (≈14cm to ≈17cm). This differential response suggests that quinoa uses diverse strategies to cope with salinity, ranging from the high growth potential of Q-128 to the stress-insensitive stability of Q-192 (Fig. 1A).

In contrast, the significant drop in Q-7 and Q-192 at suggests that while they may tolerate mild stress, the high concentration severely compromises their ability to maintain turgor and accumulate biomass (Fig. 1B).

Root length is critical for water and nutrient uptake. The maximum root length (RL) is seen in Q-128 under control conditions (≈25 cm), significantly higher than its 100 mM and 200 mM treatments (*** and **). The severe reduction in RL for Q-128 confirms its root growth is highly compromised. The stability of RL in Q-184 and Q-45 suggests these varieties protect root meristem activity, maintaining growth despite stress (Fig. 1C).

Shoot length reflects growth and expansion. Shoot length (SL) is highest in Q-128 at 100 mM (≈17 cm), though not significantly different from control or 200 mM. Q-184 and Q-33 show significant increases at moderate salinity, while susceptible lines demonstrated significant sensitivity to saline conditions. Q-7 exhibited a drastic reduction in shoot growth at 300 mM, with complete growth inhibition, indicating severe susceptibility to salt stress. Q-33 showed consistently low shoot growth with a pronounced decline at the highest salinity level. Similarly, Q-95 exhibited substantial growth reductions at higher salt concentrations, particularly at 300 mM, while Q-18 maintained relatively low growth throughout, with moderate declines as salinity increased. The sharp decrease in shoot development among these genotypes suggests limited capacity for osmotic adjustment and ion homeostasis under saline stress Q-184 and Q-33 show a mild-stress hormetic response, suggesting adaptive growth under moderate salinity (Fig. 1D).

Root Fresh Weight reflects water uptake and storage. The maximum root fresh weight (RFW) is observed in Q-45 under control (≈0.45 mg), significantly higher than its 100 mM and 200 mM treatments (* and **). Q-128 and Q-184 show reductions at higher salinity. SFW reflects water content in photosynthetic tissue. Shoot fresh weight (SFW) peaks in Q-128 at 100mM (≈1.9mg), significantly higher than at 200mM (***). Q-184 and Q-7 also show increases under moderate stress. Q-128, Q-184, and Q-7 maintain effective water transport and storage at moderate salinity, key to maintaining photosynthesis (Fig. 1F).

Shoot Dry Weight is a primary indicator of accumulated biomass and photosynthetic efficiency. The maximum shoot dry weight (SDW) is observed in Q-128 under control conditions (≈0.19mg), which is significantly higher than its 100mM and 200mM treatments (** and ***). For Q-45, the 100mM condition (≈0.08mg) is significantly higher than the control and 300mM conditions (** and *). Q-33 shows a significant increase in SDW at 200mM (≈0.08mg) compared to the control (*). Conversely, Q-18 experiences a significant reduction in SDW at 200mM compared to 100mM (*). Overall, most varieties show a low SDW (≈0.05mg or less) under 300mM stress, with Q-128 having the most pronounced decline from control. The sharp decline in SDW for Q-128 from control to saline conditions suggests that, while it has high potential biomass, its physiological processes are highly sensitive to osmotic stress and ion toxicity. Conversely, the ability of Q-33 and Q-45 to increase SDW at moderate salinity indicates a potential for osmotic adjustment, suggesting moderate tolerance mechanisms in this trait. Root Dry Weight (RDW) represents long-term carbon allocation (Fig. 1G). Highest RDW is in Q-33 at 200 mM (≈0.09 mg), significantly higher than control and 100 mM (* and ***). The data in Table 3 clearly demonstrate that the moderate salinity range (100–200 mM) acts as the critical turning point, where quinoa’s physiological responses are most varied and intense. It is in this range that high-potential lines begin to crash (e.g., Q-128 SDW/RL drop), while acclimating lines deploy active coping mechanisms to survive the impending high stress.

Photosynthetic parameters

The photosynthetic parameters (PSII OC, Φ PSII, LEF, PS1ORC, PSI OC, NPQt, ΦNPQ, ΦNO, gH+, vh+) are all related to Chlorophyll Fluorescence and the Electron Transport Chain (ETC), giving a high-resolution view of how energy is utilized and dissipated.

Photosystem II (PSII) efficiency and damage (PSII OC, ΦPSII)

PSII OC (Photosystem II Operating Center) represents the proportion of active, functional PSII reaction centers that are open (oxidized). All varieties show a drastic, significant reduction in PSII OC as salinity increases from Control to 300 mM. The most significant drop often occurs between Control and 100 mM or 200 mM. Lines Q-45, Q-128, and Q-192 show slightly higher PSII OC values than others (like Q-49 and Q-7) at 200 mM and 300 mM, suggesting better structural integrity or faster repair of PSII under severe stress. ΦPSII (Φ2) (Effective Quantum Yield of PSII) represents the actual efficiency of light utilization in PSII photochemistry. It mirrors PSII OC, with a sharp decline in all varieties, particularly at 300 mM. This indicates major impairment in utilizing light energy for CO2 fixation.Q-128 maintains the highest ΦPSII values at 200 mM and 300 mM (around 0.70 and 0.68, respectively), indicating superior performance in actual photosynthetic electron flow compared to others (e.g., Q-18 and Q-33 drop to 0.35 and 0.17) (Fig. 2A).

Photosystem I (PSI) performance and cyclic electron flow (PS1ORC, PSI OC)

PS1ORC (PSI Operating Center) measures the oxidation rate of P700, reflecting the overall activity of PSI under light. PS1ORC shows a decline under salinity, but the pattern is more complex than PSII. The oxidation capacity drops, which is often tied to a lack of electron acceptors (NADP+) due to impaired CO2 fixation (biochemical stress). PSI OC (PSI Oxidized Centers): shows the proportion of open PSI reaction centers (i.e., P700 is oxidized), ready to accept an electron. PSI OC shows a sharp drop, particularly from Control to 100 mM and 200 mM across most varieties. Low PSI OC suggests a reduction of P700 due to electron accumulation (over-reduction) at the acceptor side of PSI (lack of NADP+ reduction) or limited P700 oxidation by the ETC. Q-18 and Q-45 maintain relatively higher PSI OC at 200 mM and 300 mM, compared to highly affected lines like Q-49 and Q-192 at 300 mM (Fig. 2B,C).

Energy dissipation mechanisms (NPQt, ΦNPQ, ΦNO)

These parameters define how absorbed light energy is partitioned: ΦPSII (Photochemistry), Φ NPQ(Regulated Heat Dissipation), and ΦNO(Unregulated Heat/Non-Photochemical Loss), PQt (Total Non-Photochemical Quenching), and ΦNPQ (Regulated Energy Dissipation). In most varieties, NPQt and ΦNPQ increase as salinity increases, peaking around 200 mM or 300 mM. This is a crucial photoprotective response. Q-184, Q-128, and Q-192 show the highest ΦNPQ (and NPQt) at 300 mM (around 0.30 − 0.35 for ΦNPQ). This superior ability to safely dissipate excess energy prevents photo-oxidative damage, which directly correlates with the relative maintenance of ΦPSII. ΦNO(Unregulated Energy Dissipation/Non-Photochemical Loss): Energy lost through heat that is not photoprotective; often a sign of damage ΦNO generally increases with stress across all lines, with the highest values seen at 300 mM. Sensitive Varieties: Q-18 and Q-192 show a dramatic jump in ΦNO at 300 mM (up to 0.50 and 0.55), suggesting significant structural damage or irreversible photo-inhibition at severe stress levels, despite good ΦNPQ (Fig. 2D–F).

Proton gradient components (gH+ and vh+)

These parameters relate to the pH gradient (ΔpH) across the thylakoid membrane, essential for ATP synthesis. gH+ (Proton Conductivity) and vh+ (Rate of Proton Efflux/ATP Synthesis) both show a major reduction under salinity, especially vh+ .The significant drop indicates a failure in ATP synthesis capacity, which is a major constraint on the CO2 fixation cycle. Q-45 and Q-128 maintain the highest gH+ at 300 mM (around 150), suggesting a relatively functional ATP synthase and a less compromised thylakoid membrane, supporting their higher ΦPSII. Q-33 shows an unusually high Control value for gH+ and vh+, which plummets at 100 mM, indicating high sensitivity (Fig. 2G,H).

Malondialdehyde activity and differential activities of antioxidant enzymes in response to varying salinity levels

Malondialdehyde (MDA) is the end-product of polyunsaturated fatty acid peroxidation and is a direct, quantifiable marker of oxidative damage. The rising MDA indicates that the production of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) like O2∙−and H2O2 is overwhelming the plants’ defensive capacity, leading to compromised cell membranes. The MDA chart shows a clear, progressive increase in the mean specific activity of MDA across all quinoa lines as salinity rises, peaking at the 300 mM treatment. The mean MDA level consistently trends upward: Control < 100 mM < 200 mM < 300 mM. This visually confirms that salinity stress is causing increasing levels of lipid peroxidation (damage to cell membranes) in the quinoa leaves.

The highest damage according to the antioxidant activity is shown by Q-18 at 300 mM, suggesting it is most sensitive to severe salt stress in terms of membrane integrity. Quinoa lines Q-49 and Q-192 show the lowest MDA levels at 300 mM, indicating they are the most effective at minimizing cell membrane damage, a hallmark of greater salt tolerance. Lines with lower MDA (Q-49, Q-192) possess superior mechanisms, either better ROS scavenging or enhanced membrane stabilization, that confer greater tolerance (Fig. 3A).

Primary antioxidant defense: superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT)

The SOD chart shows a biphasic response with a clear surge at the highest concentration. Mean SOD activity is relatively stable or slightly drops at 100 mM and 200 mM compared to the control. A sharp increase in mean SOD activity occurs at 300 mM. This suggests that the initial salt stress is manageable, but severe stress requires a significant and rapid upregulation of the primary ROS-scavenging enzyme. Lines Q-192 and Q-49 show the highest SOD activity, correlating with their low MDA levels, suggesting strong detoxification of O2∙− radicals. Lines Q-184 and Q-33 show minimal SOD induction, suggesting a potential failure or exhaustion of the initial defense mechanism in these lines, which might contribute to their stress susceptibility (Fig. 3B).

The POD chart shows a pattern similar to SOD, indicating its crucial role in managing the downstream product of SOD. Mean POD activity is slightly lower or stable up to 200 mM (Fig. 3C).

Like SOD, mean POD activity shows a significant jump at 300 mM, indicating a mass mobilization of H2O2 scavenging mechanisms under severe stress. Q-33 and Q-192 exhibit the highest POD activity, suggesting they are very efficient at detoxifying H2O2 via the guaiacol peroxidase pathway. Q-49 shows the lowest POD activity, which is surprising given its high SOD and low MDA, possibly indicating it relies more heavily on the CAT or APX pathways for H2O2 clearance (Fig. 3A-C).

The combined trend of SOD and POD peaking at 300 mM reflects the plants’ emergency response to rapidly escalating ROS levels under severe osmotic and ionic stress. SOD converts toxic superoxide. The CAT chart shows a relatively flat trend until 300 mM. CAT activity remains largely unchanged at 100 mM and 200 mM. There is a moderate increase in the mean activity only at the highest stress, 300 mM. Q-33 and Q-18 show the highest CAT activity. Q-192 shows very low CAT activity (Fig. 3D) (O2∙−) is converted into the less toxic hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). POD and CAT then dismantle the H2O2. The fact that POD induction is much stronger than CAT induction suggests that POD, which works primarily in the cytosol and cell wall, is the main enzyme responsible for H2O2​ clearance under high salinity stress in these quinoa lines. Lines that effectively induce both SOD and POD (like Q-192) are better adapted to prevent the severe oxidative burst that leads to membrane damage (low MDA).

The ascorbate–glutathione cycle: APX and GSH

APX and GSH are central components of the Ascorbate–Glutathione Cycle, which detoxifies H2O2 in the chloroplasts and cytosol using antioxidants (ascorbate and glutathione). The observed constitutive (non-inducible) stability of APX and GSH activity suggests two possibilities: These enzymes are not rate-limiting for the H2O2 scavenging process under increasing salt stress. The H2O2 produced in the cell is primarily cleared by the massively induced POD, making the small changes in the APX/GSH cycle irrelevant to the overall oxidative status. The collective enzyme profile suggests that these quinoa lines primarily employ a "last-resort, mass induction" strategy for severe salt stress. The priority is the immediate and robust scavenging of O2∙−and H2O2 via the SOD and POD pathways, which show significant induction only at the most stressful level (300 mM). The APX chart shows remarkable stability across all salinity levels. Mean APX activity barely changes from Control to 300 mM (Fig. 3E).

The GSH chart also displays high stability across the treatments. There is a minor, non-linear increase at 200 mM followed by a return to near-control levels at 300 mM. Q-49 and Q-192 are the most promising lines for tolerance. Their combination of high SOD/POD activity and low MDA at 300 mM demonstrates effective detoxification leading to minimal cellular damage. Q-18 is likely the most susceptible line, indicated by its highest MDA level at 300 mM, despite having moderate enzyme activity, suggesting its defense response is inadequate to prevent serious membrane damage (Fig. 3F).

Differential expression of universal stress protein gene family (USP) under varying level of saline conditions

In our previous study, we identified and characterized 41 members of the USP gene family in C. quinoa. Among these, 19 genes were selected for further analysis based on transcriptomic data, which revealed their significant expression under various abiotic stress conditions. These 19 candidate genes were subsequently investigated under variable saline conditions in a hydroponic system to examine their molecular responses and to elucidate how their transcriptional regulation is modulated by salinity stress. The gene expression analysis of 19 genes across three quinoa lines (Q-192, Q-33, Q-128) under increasing NaCl stress (0 mM, 100 mM, 200 mM, and 300 mM) revealed highly complex, tissue-specific, and genotype-dependent regulatory patterns. The overall transcriptional response was characterized by three primary regulatory groups: genes displaying massive induction at moderate stress, genes exhibiting late-stage induction following initial suppression (Last-Resort Genes), and genes showing consistent suppression. AUR62027874 and AUR62025147 exhibited this classic "all-or-nothing" profile in the root. Both genes were significantly suppressed at 100 mM across most lines but showed massive, coordinated upregulation (over 100-fold for AUR62027874) at 300 mM, suggesting their function is critical for the root’s ultimate survival against lethal ion toxicity (e.g., ion exclusion). AUR62011820 was severely and significantly suppressed to near-zero levels (as low as 0.00006-fold) across all lines and in both shoot and root tissues at initial/moderate stress, indicating it is a strong negative regulator or is associated with a metabolic pathway that must be shut down for survival. Variety-specific differences and tissue specialization

Significant genotypic variation was evident in the timing, magnitude, and tissue priority of the transcriptional response. Q-33 frequently showed the highest magnitude of gene expression (e.g., AUR62011306, AUR62000674, AUR62020060, AUR62029290) peaking at 100mM or 200mM in the shoot. Its defense strategy appears to involve early, high-output acclimation. However, for AUR62041164, Q-33 was characterized by consistent, strong suppression in the root, in stark contrast to the massive upregulation seen in Q-192 and Q-128 roots, suggesting Q-33 utilizes an alternative, unmeasured pathway. Q-128 often exhibited the most pronounced regulatory switches. For many genes (e.g., AUR62027874, AUR62037593, AUR62033121), Q-128 displayed an extreme suppression at a moderate level (e.g., AUR62000674 suppressed to 0.001-fold in the shoot at 300mM) followed by abrupt activation or, conversely, rapid shutdown when stress became lethal, indicating a strategy based on threshold-dependent resource commitment. Q-192 often showed high, sustained induction across tissues for genes like AUR62027886 (root, sustained approx 40-fold) and AUR62019037 (root, sustained > 300-fold), suggesting a strategy of robust, sustained defense across the entire salinity gradient. AUR62027874 and AUR62029290 provided the clearest evidence of tissue specialization, with massive root induction at 300mM coinciding with the active suppression of the same genes in the shoot, illustrating a mechanism that prioritizes root-based ion exclusion over general shoot metabolism when facing lethal external stress Figs. 4, 5, 6, 7

Multivariate correlation analysis demonstrates tight coupling among growth, antioxidant, photosynthetic, and gene expression responses

Correlation analysis revealed a highly integrated response across morphological, antioxidant, photosynthetic, and transcriptomic datasets in quinoa under salinity stress. Of the 903 pairwise comparisons examined, 120 correlations were significant at p < 0.05, including 62 at p < 0.01 and 30 at p < 0.001, indicating substantial coordination among traits and molecular markers. The strongest associations were observed within the transcriptomic dataset, where multiple AUR genes exhibited highly significant intercorrelations, consistent with coordinated transcriptional regulation under stress. Notably, antioxidant variables, particularly glutathione content, catalase, and ascorbate peroxidase, showed extensive associations with gene expression, suggesting close coupling between redox homeostasis and molecular responses. Morphological traits, including root dry weight, shoot dry weight, root length, and total plant length, were also significantly associated with key photosynthetic parameters such as PHI2, PS1OC, and PS1ORC, underscoring the dependence of growth performance on photosynthetic efficiency. Collectively, these patterns indicate that salinity tolerance in quinoa is underpinned by a coordinated physiological and molecular network rather than isolated trait-specific responses. Comprehensive GC–MS metabolomic analysis reveals coordinated osmoprotection and defense signaling in Chenopodium quinoa salt stress response

The GC–MS metabolomics analysis of Chenopodium quinoa leaf extracts yielded 2,492 mass spectrometry records, with 1,883 unique metabolite features identified across six experimental conditions. To ensure data integrity, peak area percentages were normalized to the column total, reflecting relative abundance patterns across replicates. The dataset demonstrated high chromatographic reproducibility, evidenced by a retention time (RT) variance of < 0.05 min and a Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) exceeding 10:1. Structural annotations were conducted with high confidence; 90.4% of detected compounds were non-derivatized, reducing data ambiguity. For the “Top 80” high-abundance metabolites, identification was cross-validated against at least two chemical databases (e.g., NIST, PubChem) with a retention time prediction error of < 0.08 min. However, it is noted that 24% of secondary metabolites remain structurally uncharacterized, representing a limitation in current plant metabolite catalogs (Table 4).

Varietal metabolomic differentiation

The three quinoa varieties exhibited profound biochemical divergence, characterized by extremely low Jaccard similarity indices (0.064–0.072), indicating that over 90% of detected compounds were variety-specific. SV2 (Q-33) displayed the highest metabolite richness (735 unique compounds) and the most robust secondary metabolite diversity (68.4%). In contrast, SV3 (Q-128) maintained a more conservative profile with higher levels of unsaturated fatty acids but lower polyol accumulation. SV1 (Q-192) demonstrated a balanced primary-to-secondary metabolic ratio. These distinct fingerprints suggest divergent evolutionary adaptation strategies among the cultivars. Figure 10A-C. Metabolic response to salt stress

Saline exposure triggered a comprehensive metabolic reorganization rather than a uniform accumulation: approximately 48% of metabolites increased (stress-induced), while 48% decreased (resource reallocation). The highest relative inductions were observed in Volatile Organic Compounds (+ 58–78%) and Polyols (+ 42–65%), whereas structural components like hydrocarbons remained stable (-5% to + 8%). To quantify these shifts, two indices were utilized: the Osmolyte Index, which rose from 2.1–2.8% to 3.8–4.6% under stress, and the Oxidative Stress Index, which doubled from ~ 1.0 to 2.0. SV2 exhibited the highest Osmolyte Index (4.6%), suggesting superior osmotic adjustment capacity.

Pathway-level and network correlations

Metabolite Set Enrichment Analysis (MSEA) identified significant coverage in Glycolysis (34.4%) and Fatty Acid Biosynthesis (42.1%), with Phenylpropanoid and Terpenoid pathways showing the strongest saline induction. Co-metabolite correlation analysis identified four functional modules, most notably an "Osmolyte Production Cluster" (correlation + 0.75 to + 0.95) and a "Volatile Defense Cluster". These findings provide a targeted roadmap for transcriptomic integration, specifically prioritizing the analysis of genes encoding aldose reductase, terpene synthases, and lipoxygenases to validate the observed metabolic flux. The GC–MS metabolomics analysis of C. quinoa reveals a highly sophisticated and variety-specific response to saline stress, characterized by the coordinated upregulation of protective osmolytes and signaling volatiles. With a dataset showing 96.7% consistency and rigorous normalization, the findings highlight SV2 (Q-33) as the most metabolically plastic variety, achieving a 4.6% Osmolyte Index and significant volatile induction (+ 78%). The extreme biochemical divergence between varieties, demonstrated by Jaccard indices below 0.075, underscores the potential for using these unique metabolic fingerprints as markers in salt-tolerant breeding programs. While lipid membranes remained largely stable, the marked shift in secondary metabolism (+ 25–60%) confirms that quinoa employs an aggressive defense strategy to maintain physiological homeostasis under high salinity. Discussion

Salinity stress appears to significantly influence the morphological and physiological traits of C. quinoa, as evidenced by changes in total plant length, biomass accumulation, and root/shoot development. The observed progressive decline in plant length under increasing salinity is consistent with the effects of osmotic stress and ion toxicity, which are hypothesized to inhibit cell division and elongation in meristematic tissues37,38. While root length may initially show slight tolerance under low salinity, potentially reflecting an adaptive mechanism to explore water sources, higher concentrations lead to marked suppression. This inhibition is likely associated with reduced mitotic activity and potential membrane damage resulting from sodium and chloride ion accumulation. Shoot length appears more sensitive to salt stress, exhibiting a substantial decline even under moderate salinity; this may be attributed to impaired cell expansion, hormonal imbalances, or reduced photosynthetic efficiency39. Consequently, the significant decline in root and shoot fresh weights suggests that water uptake is hampered and metabolic activities are disrupted. While mild salinity might exert a negligible or slightly stimulatory effect on biomass production, moderate to severe stress leads to pronounced reductions in fresh and dry biomass, likely driven by inhibited growth, reduced carbon assimilation, and altered resource allocation. In quinoa, the tendency for shoot dry weight to be more severely affected than root dry weight may reflect the shoot’s higher sensitivity to ionic and osmotic components of stress. Overall, these results suggest that increasing salinity imposes a cumulative detrimental impact on quinoa growth by limiting water and nutrient uptake and impairing broader physiological processes40,41.

The response of the photosynthetic apparatus in C. quinoa to increasing salinity suggests a complex interplay between protective acclimation and eventual photo-oxidative damage, varying significantly across genotypes. The substantial reduction in ΦPSII and PSIIOC at severe stress levels (200–300 mM NaCl) indicates that a primary limitation to carbon assimilation may be rooted in the operational efficiency of Photosystem II42. This decline is characteristic of stress responses in many halophytes, where initial osmotic shock is thought to trigger stomatal closure, potentially leading to a CO2 deficit and a subsequent electron backlog in the Electron Transport Chain (ETC)43. This impairment appears to transition from a stomatal limitation at moderate stress to more severe biochemical inhibition at 300 mM NaCl. This interpretation is supported by the corresponding drop in PSI OC and PS1ORC, which suggests an over-reduction of P700 (particularly visible in highly affected lines like Q-49 and Q-192). Such over-reduction may occur because the electron sink capacity, primarily NADP + reduction for the Calvin Cycle, is potentially compromised by ion toxicity or damage to key enzymes such as Rubisco44. Crucially, the regulation of excess light energy appears to serve as a key differentiator between tolerant and sensitive lines. The observed increase in NPQt and ΦNPQ is interpreted as an essential adaptive photoprotective strategy. Genotypes Q-128 and Q-45, which maintained higher ΦPSII values under severe stress, also exhibited a superior capacity for regulated dissipation, suggesting that the flexible conversion of excess energy into heat may help prevent the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and preserve the structural integrity of reaction centers45,46. Conversely, the significant jump in ΦNO (Unregulated Energy Dissipation) in sensitive lines (Q-18 and Q-192) serves as a strong indicator of possible irreversible photo-inhibition and structural damage. While Q-192 also showed high ΦNPQ, the simultaneous rise in ΦNO sat the highest stress level suggests that protective mechanisms may eventually have been overwhelmed, leading to destructive non-photochemical losses47.

Furthermore, a major physiological constraint observed across all varieties is the significant reduction in gH + (Proton Conductivity) and vH + (Rate of Proton Efflux), particularly vH + . This finding is suggestive of a potential decline in the capacity for ATP synthesis, possibly stemming from compromised ΔpH gradient maintenance or damage to the ATPase complex itself48. Given that both ATP and NADPH are required for CO2 fixation, the observed reduction in ATP synthesis capacity may place a severe secondary limitation on the entire photosynthetic process.The ability of Q-45 and Q-128 to maintain relatively higher gH + levels at 300 mM (around 150) points toward a more resilient thylakoid membrane structure and a less compromised ATP synthase complex. This reinforces their status as tolerant lines by potentially ensuring better overall energy transduction under high saline conditions. In summary, the data collectively suggest that superior salt tolerance in quinoa, as exemplified by lines Q-128 and Q-45, may be conferred by a multi-faceted photosynthetic strategy. This strategy appears to combine structural integrity, functional maintenance of electron flow (ΦPSII), superior regulated photoprotection (ΦNPQ), and resilience in bioenergetic coupling (gH +). These adaptive physiological responses likely allow tolerant genotypes to sustain photosynthetic energy conversion more effectively than sensitive varieties, such as Q-18 and Q-33, whose sharp drop in ΦPSII and high ΦNO indicate a greater vulnerability to salinity-induced photo-inhibition46,49–51.

The initial analysis of photochemical parameters indicates that the drastic reduction in ΦPSII (Effective Quantum Yield of PSII) and PSII OC (PSII Operating Center) at severe stress levels (200–300 mM NaCl) points toward a primary limitation to carbon assimilation rooted in the operational efficiency of Photosystem II (PSII). This decline is characteristic of stress responses in many halophytes, where an initial osmotic shock is hypothesized to trigger stomatal closure, potentially leading to a CO2 deficit and a subsequent electron backlog in the Electron Transport Chain (ETC)52,53. This impairment appears to transition from a stomatal limitation at moderate stress to more severe biochemical inhibition at 300 mM NaCl, a conclusion supported by the drop in PSI OC and PS1ORC, which suggests an over-reduction of P700 due to a compromised electron sink capacity. The observed impairment in photochemical efficiency is associated with escalating levels of potential cellular damage and the subsequent mobilization of enzymatic defense systems. Quantification of Malondialdehyde (MDA), an end-product of polyunsaturated fatty acid peroxidation, revealed a progressive increase in oxidative damage across all lines, peaking at 300 mM NaCl. This upward trend (Control < 100 mM < 200 mM < 300 mM) suggests that osmotic and ionic stress may overwhelm homeostatic mechanisms, potentially leading to compromised cell membrane integrity. Notably, genotypes Q-49 and Q-192 demonstrated the lowest MDA levels at 300 mM, suggesting superior membrane stabilization or more effective ROS detoxification compared to the more susceptible line, Q-1854.

The enzymatic defense strategy against this rising oxidative load was characterized by a rapid, biphasic induction of primary scavengers. Mean Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) and Peroxidase (POD) activities showed a sharp surge at 300 mM NaCl, a pattern consistent with an “emergency mobilization” in response to an oxidative burst under severe stress55. The high SOD activity in Q-192 and Q-49 correlates with their low MDA, suggesting an effective defense pathway for converting toxic O2·– into H2O2. The subsequent clearance of H2O2 appears to be predominantly managed by POD, given its strong induction compared to the more moderate increase in Catalase (CAT). Conversely, the constitutive stability of APX and GSH suggests that H2O2 scavenging in these quinoa lines may not be primarily rate-limited by the Ascorbate–Glutathione Cycle, but rather by the induction of POD in the cytosol and cell wall. The integrated analysis suggests a coordinated mechanism of tolerance in the most resilient lines56,57. Superior maintenance of ΦPSII and high ΦNPQ in lines like Q-128 and Q-45 may act as a primary defense by minimizing ROS generation at the source. This photoprotection appears to be complemented by an efficient enzymatic scavenging mechanism (high SOD/POD, low MDA), prominently seen in Q-192 and Q-49. Furthermore, the significant reduction in gH + and vH + is indicative of a major physiological constraint, potentially reflecting a decline in the capacity for ATP synthesis due to a compromised ΔpH gradient. The ability of Q-45 and Q-128 to maintain relatively higher gH + levels reinforces their status as tolerant lines by potentially ensuring better energy transduction. This duality, mitigation of energy input via NPQ and the scavenging of residual damage via enzymatic pathways, is proposed as a crucial strategy for survival under extreme saline conditions. Finally, the transcriptional behavior of the 19 USP genes provides preliminary molecular evidence of a coordinated response that likely underliesquinoa’s remarkable regulatory plasticity and its function as a facultative halophyte.

The transcriptional profiles of the 19 USP genes analyzed in this study suggest a genetically diversified response that may contribute to C. quinoa’s classification as a facultative halophyte. When interpreted alongside our previously established USP-interactome58, these expression patterns provide insight into the potential functional roles of these genes during salinity stress. A prominent trend was the significant induction of AUR62011306, AUR62000674, AUR62020060, AUR62019037, AUR62039292,and AUR62016751 at moderate salinity (100–200 mM NaCl). This induction aligns with the reported optimal growth window for salt-tolerant quinoa, suggesting these genes may function as primary acclimation effectors. In this capacity, they are hypothesized to act as molecular chaperones that stabilize proteins and maintain cellular osmotic balance59,60. Specifically, the high expression of AUR62011306 in Q-33 shoots (> 1000-fold) is consistent with a role in protecting the photosynthetic apparatus, possibly through ROS scavenging or the stabilization of enzymatic complexes61. Conversely, the marked downregulation of several USPs at 300 mM NaCl suggests a metabolic reprogramming phase. This shift likely reflects a transition where non-essential pathways are suppressed to conserve energy and redirect metabolic flux toward survival-critical processes62. In the resilient genotype Q-33, the upregulation of AUR62011306 coincides with the maintenance of PSII efficiency and regulated heat dissipation (NPQ). Given that this USP is predicted to interact with SnRK2 kinases, we propose a mechanistic model where AUR62011306 facilitates ABA-mediated stomatal control and protein stabilization. This interaction is hypothesized to mitigate the “electron backlog” in the transport chain, potentially limiting oxidative damage as indicated by the lower MDA levels observed in tolerant lines. Further functional validation is required to confirm these specific protein–protein interactions under saline conditions.

The tissue-specific modulation of USP expression suggests a potential division of physiological labor between shoots and roots under salinity stress. Genes exhibiting strong shoot-biased induction are hypothesized to be associated with mechanisms such as vacuolar Na + sequestration or the regulation of epidermal bladder cells, both of which may facilitate ion detoxification and the maintenance of photosynthetic activity. Conversely, the significant activation of genes such as AUR62000674, AUR62027874, AUR62029290, and AUR62025147in the roots, particularly at 300 mM NaCl, points toward a root-centric defense phase. The simultaneous repression of these genes in shoots alongside their induction in roots exemplifies a pattern of inverse tissue regulation. This trend likely reflects a strategic reallocation of metabolic energy toward Na + exclusion and compartmentation at the primary site of ion entry. The abrupt activation of these specific genes only at high salinity levels is consistent with their potential involvement in late-stage signaling cascades or the recruitment of key Na + transport systems, such as SOS1 or NHX. These systems are essential for maintaining ionic equilibrium under extreme conditions63. However, further functional studies are necessary to confirm whether these USPs directly regulate these transporters or serve as broader markers of cellular stress at the root interface.

The genotypic divergence among the three quinoa lines, Q-33, Q-128, and Q-192, points toward ecotypic specialization in USP regulation within C. quinoa, suggesting that salinity tolerance is governed by a spectrum of regulatory strategies rather than a uniform genetic program64. For instance, Q-33 exhibited a pattern of early acclimatization characterized by the rapid, high-amplitude activation of multiple USPs. This proactive defense model, which relies on early transcriptional commitment, is further distinguished by the consistent suppression of AUR62041164 in the roots, potentially representing an alternative and more energy-efficient mechanism for ion homeostasis under moderate salinity65. In contrast, Q-128 follows a threshold-dependent response where genes such as AUR62008647remain repressed until salinity reaches severe levels, a strategy that may minimize metabolic costs under sublethal stress while preserving the capacity for a rapid response under extreme conditions66. Q-192 represents a third strategy of sustained expression, maintaining consistently elevated levels of genes like AUR62027886 and AUR62019037 across all salinity levels, suggesting a conservative defense mode that ensures continuous readiness during prolonged stress exposure. Furthermore, the universal downregulation of AUR62011820 suggests that certain USP members may function as negative regulators of stress adaptation. This marked repression may constitute a form of adaptive silencing, where specific metabolic pathways are actively inhibited to reallocate energy toward protective processes, thereby enhancing overall system efficiency under resource-limited conditions67.

The application of gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) profiling revealed patterns consistent with a coordinated metabolic reprogramming in C. quinoa under saline stress. Our analysis demonstrated high data reproducibility, providing a rigorous baseline for quantitative comparison. The observed metabolic response was characterized by a balanced transition, with roughly equal proportions of detected metabolites increasing and decreasing, suggesting a model of regulated remodeling rather than passive stress accumulation68,69. A central observation was the pronounced upregulation of metabolites associated with osmotic adjustment. The strong induction of alcohols/polyols and carbohydrates/glycosides (+ 42–65%) likely contributed to the sharp increase in the calculated Osmolyte Index70. While the accumulation of compatible solutes like myo-inositol and glucose is a known hallmark of halophytic adaptation, these data point toward their role in maintaining turgor and protecting enzyme function in this context. Furthermore, the predominance of fatty acids and esters, coupled with minimal shifts in overall lipid levels, may reflect a metabolic priority for maintaining membrane homeostasis during salt exposure71.

Salt exposure was also accompanied by a broad activation of secondary metabolism (overall + 25–60%). The dramatic increases in volatile organics (+ 58–78%) and phenolics (+ 18–28%) suggest a potential dual-defense strategy; volatiles, such as limonene, are hypothesized to act as stress signals or protectants, while the simultaneous rise in phenolic antioxidants alongside aldehydes/ketones (ROS byproducts) is indicative of a coordinated effort toward redox homeostasis, as inferred by the shift in the Oxidative Stress Index from ~ 1.0 to 2.070,72. The exceptionally low Jaccard similarity indices (0.064 to 0.072) suggest pronounced metabolomic divergence among the three C. quinoa varieties, implying the existence of biochemically distinct survival strategies73. Specifically, Q-33 exhibited the highest metabolic plasticity, showing the strongest induction of osmolytes and volatiles, which correlates with the superior salt tolerance observed in previous physiological studies. The identification of a 49-metabolite conserved core may represent the essential metabolic machinery required for basic cell integrity across varieties. Collectively, these findings support a model where C. quinoa adaptation is achieved through the apparent co-regulation of four key metabolic modules: (i) osmolyte biosynthesis, (ii) volatile defense, (iii) lipid maintenance, and (iv) antioxidant metabolism. The adaptive capacity of Q-33 suggests that specific osmolyte and defense compound concentrations may serve as candidate metabolomic traits for future screening and genetic improvement efforts74,75.

Altogether, the complex interplay of induction, repression, tissue specialization, and genotype-specific expression delineates a highly dynamic regulatory framework within quinoa’s USP network. Rather than functioning as passive responders, USPs appear to serve as central integrators of stress signaling, linking redox control, protein stability, and ion transport into a coherent survival strategy. This network-level regulation explains how C. quinoa achieves its extraordinary salinity resilience and provides a foundation for the targeted manipulation of USP pathways to enhance salt tolerance in other crop species.

Conclusion

This comprehensive investigation establishes that the extraordinary salt tolerance of C. quinoa is achieved through a multifaceted and highly coordinated regulatory framework. At the physiological level, superior tolerance is conferred by a strategic combination of maintaining photosynthetic efficiency through flexible, regulated heat dissipation and deploying a rapid, high-magnitude enzymatic defense against oxidative damage only when stress is severe. The molecular foundation for this resilience lies in the dynamic regulation of the USP gene network, which effectively switches between energy-intensive acclimation at moderate stress and conservative, root-centric ion exclusion strategies under lethal conditions. Metabolomic evidence strongly supports this by showing that the most resilient genotypes achieve metabolic plasticity via the strong accumulation of compatible solutes (osmolytes) and volatile defense compounds, ensuring both turgor and redox homeostasis. These integrated findings not only validate quinoa’s status as a facultative halophyte but also provide a powerful set of molecular and metabolic signatures that can serve as valuable targets for breeding programs aimed at enhancing salt resilience in other crop species.
