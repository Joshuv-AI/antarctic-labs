This memo documents completion of Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory’s milestone #9193: Update
on laser-plasma interaction study of higher yield designs driven at higher laser energy.

Completion criteria for #9193:

 1. Execute shot series probing LPI risks in a scale 1.09 N221204-like design delivering up to 2 MJ laser
 energy and provide mitigation/design recommendations as appropriate. Compare measurements
 to simulations and/or existing data extrapolations.
 2. Present peer-reviewed findings at an ICF seminar and to external community.

Evidence of Completion:

Future laser facilities, such as the National Ignition Facility (NIF) Enhanced Yield Capability (EYC) upgrade,
or entirely new facilities (often referred to as a “Next Generation” laser facilities), will drive inertial
confinement fusion (ICF) targets with higher laser energies and total laser drive powers than those
currently accessible at the NIF. One of the primary concerns is the loss of driver energy or symmetry control
due to unwanted laser-plasma interactions (LPIs) that can backscatter, divert, or otherwise redistribute
laser drive in a deleterious manner, or pre-heat the capsule ablator or fuel through the generation of hot
electrons.

Milestone 9193 addresses LPI risk and understanding in multiple ways. First, a hohlraum target was fielded
that is 9% larger (scale 1.09) than our current ignition platform. This represents the largest cylindrical
hohlraum ever shot at the NIF. Such a target is motivated in part by the targets planned for NIF EYC
operations but also represents an opportunity to examine our predictive capability and physics
understanding of LPI in hohlraum targets. The laser energy available at the current NIF laser (1.9-2.2 MJ) is
below the ~2.6 MJ that would be required to drive such a target to high-performance ignition. In FY24, laser
pulses truncated in time were used to examine LPI early in peak power (up to ~8 ns), and LPI was found to
be similar to current NIF ignition designs at scale 1.0. In FY25, the laser pulse was extended to 11 ns in a
three-shot series, all of which were executed successfully and collected good data from key diagnostics.
To work around the shortfall in laser drive energy, the laser pulse was “dipped” during the peak power
portion of the pulse and then restored to full power later in the pulse, with the goal of examining LPI late in
the pulse. The three shots were carried out by “walking up” sequentially in energy and duration. This walk

up was needed due to the threat of laser damage caused by stimulated Brillouin scattering (SBS). The key
result is that backscatter in a scale 1.09 target up to 11 ns was not found to be significantly different in
energy to scale 1.0 targets, in agreement with simulations. Some differences in the late-peak power
behavior of stimulated Raman scattering were observed relative to scale 1.0 targets, but these are likely
due to a cooling of the hohlraum caused by the “dipped” pulse and were energetically modest.

Second, experiments directly imaging the inner cones of laser beams after undergoing crossed-beam
energy transfer were conducted. The target used was a truncated hohlraum (one third of the usual length),
with an imaging plate that captured the inner cone beams after undergoing crossed-beam energy transfer
(CBET). These experiments show for the first time, and in exceptional detail, the intensities of each inner
cone beam post CBET. The results of these experiments are being used to update our modeling approach,
enhancing our predictive capability for future, as-yet unshot, targets.

A potential mitigation of unwanted LPI is the increase of the smoothing by spectral dispersion (SSD) that is
applied to the laser. The SSD was increased from the nominal bandwidth of 45 GHz to 118 GHz in a three-
shot series, completed successfully and with good data from all key diagnostics. The underlying physics is
that by increasing the bandwidth, the high-intensity laser speckles that drive LPI are scrambled on a faster
timescale, reducing the time-averaged intensity that drives LPI. The key result is that SSD at 118 GHz was
not found to produce a reduction in backscatter that would justify the tradeoffs of laser efficiency and
damage caused by operating at 118 GHz. These results were understood using simulations (using the LPI
code pF3D) that reproduced the measurements.

A substantial effort was made to increase our physics understanding of inner cone SBS, a primary LPI
concern in targets where CBET is significant (true of current ignition designs). By using a multi-species
radiation hydrodynamics model in LASNEX and fully accounting for CBET, pF3D simulations are found to
reproduce key experimental trends and levels of SBS, including the observed differences in SBS on the 23.5
degree and 30 degree inner cones. This effort bolsters our predictive capability and LPI physics
understanding for targets at future laser facilities.
