Structural mechanism of SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2 synthesis by the type III-D2 CRISPR effector complex
Authors: Yoshihisa Mitsuda, Maki Sugaya, Junichiro Ishikawa, Naoto Nagahata, Sae Okazaki, Masahiro Hiraizumi, Kazuki Kato, Jonathan S Gootenberg, Omar O Abudayyeh, Tsuyoshi Osawa, Keitaro Yamashita, Hiroshi Nishimasu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe type III-D2 CRISPR-Cas system comprises multiple Cas subunits and a CRISPR RNA, and is likely an evolutionary intermediate between the well-studied type III-A and III-E systems. Here we show that the type III-D2 complex synthesizes two distinct second messengers, SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2, from S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) and ATP in response to target RNA recognition. We determined cryo-electron microscopy structures of the type III-D2 effector complex in different functional states, providing mechanistic insights into target RNA cleavage and second messenger synthesis. The structures reveal how SAM and ATP are recognized by the Cas10 subunit within the effector complex. Furthermore, our biological data suggest that both SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2 act on the CorA ancillary effector, inducing growth arrest of infected bacterial cells and thereby conferring immunity. Thus, our study establishes the type III-D2 system as a unique anti-phage defense mechanism that employs both SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2 as second messengers, expanding the repertoire of second messenger strategies in bacterial defense systems and highlighting the remarkable functional diversity of CRISPR-Cas systems.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, Bacteria, Bacteriophages, Bacterial structural biology

Introduction

CRISPR-Cas systems in prokaryotes provide adaptive immunity against foreign nucleic acids via programmed nucleic-acid cleavage by Cas ribonucleoprotein effectors, and are divided into two classes (Class 1 and Class 2) based on effector composition1–4. In Class 1 systems, encompassing types I, III, IV, and VII, invading nucleic acids are degraded by multi-protein effector complexes programmed by a CRISPR RNA (crRNA). In Class 2 systems, such as types II, V, and VI, the effector complexes are formed by a single multi-domain Cas protein and a guide RNA.

The type III CRISPR-Cas systems are divided into nine subtypes (III-A–III-I) and include diverse effector complexes comprising multiple Cas subunits, such as Cas5, Cas7, Cas10, and Cas11 family proteins4,5. Type III-A Csm and type III-B Cmr complexes discriminate non-self RNA from self RNA through base complementarity between the 5′ tag of the crRNA and the 3′ anti-tag region of the target RNA6,7. Binding of a cognate (non-self) target RNA (CTR), with a 3′ anti-tag non-complementary to the 5′ tag in the crRNA, to the effector complex leads to the activation of the Cas10 subunit, whereas a non-cognate (self) target RNA (NTR) with a complementary 3′ anti-tag does not8,9. The activated Cas10 (Csm1/Cmr2) subunit catalyzes two enzymatic reactions: non-specific DNA cleavage10 and cyclic oligoadenylate (cOA) production11,12. In typical type III-A/B systems, the cOA molecules act as second messengers to activate downstream ancillary effectors. Subsequently, the activated ancillary effectors induce cellular responses, such as programmed cell death, thereby preventing phage propagation and protecting the cell population. Diverse ancillary effectors have been identified in the type III-A/B systems, including RNases13–15, DNases16, proteases17, and transmembrane proteins18,19. Moreover, a recent study revealed that SAM-AMP, synthesized from ATP and S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), functions as a second messenger in the type III-B Cmr system from Bacteroides fragilis20.

Phylogenetic analyses suggest that the type III-D and III-E systems likely evolved from the type III-A system4. The type III-E system encodes a unique multi-domain effector protein, Cas7-11, which is a fusion of four Cas7 domains and a Cas11 domain21–23. Cas7-11 associates with the crRNA and the caspase-like protease Csx29 to form the effector complex, which exhibits RNA-guided target RNA cleavage activity and RNA-activated Csx30 protein cleavage activity24–26. The type III-D systems are further divided into the III-D1, III-Dv, and III-D2 subtypes21,27,28. The type III-D1 system encodes multiple Cas7 subunits, whereas the type III-Dv and type III-D2 systems encode Cas7-2x and Cas7x3, respectively, which comprise multiple Cas7 domains. The number of subunits within the effector complexes suggests that the type III-E system evolved from the type III-D1 system, via the III-Dv and III-D2 systems.

The type III-D2 system typically encodes Cas7x3 (a fusion of three Cas7 subunits), Cas7-5 (a fusion of Cas7 and Cas5), and Cas10, along with CorA and a toxin–antitoxin (TA) module21 (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1a). CRISPR-associated CorA proteins are found in certain type III CRISPR systems, such as the B. fragilis type III-B system and some type III-D2 systems29–31. They comprise an N-terminal soluble domain with a unique fold and a C-terminal transmembrane domain similar to that of the CorA family of divalent cation transporters. In the B. fragilis type III-B system, CorA acts as an ancillary effector and binds the Cas10-produced SAM-AMP second messenger, presumably resulting in pore opening and membrane depolarization, which leads to the growth arrest of infected host cells20. The TA systems play a role in prokaryotic anti-phage defense and stress response32, and are sometimes associated with CRISPR-Cas systems2. The TA modules found in type III-D2 loci resemble the VapBC subfamily of the type II TA systems33,34. These observations suggest that the type III-D2 effector complex, consisting of Cas7x3, Cas7-5, Cas10, and the crRNA, recognizes a target RNA and produces second messengers, such as SAM-AMP, to activate the CorA transmembrane effector. However, the biological and biochemical functions of the type III-D2 CRISPR-Cas system remain unknown.

To elucidate the function of the type III-D2 system, we performed biological, biochemical, and structural analyses of the type III-D2 complex. Biological experiments demonstrated that the type III-D2 system exhibits anti-phage defense activity, which requires the type III-D2 complex, CorA, and the TA module. Notably, biochemical analyses revealed that, unlike canonical type III complexes, the type III-D2 complex cleaves its target RNA at a single site. Surprisingly, we found that the type III-D2 complex synthesizes two distinct second messengers, SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2, making type III-D2 the first known CRISPR system with dual second messengers. Furthermore, the cryo-EM structure of the type III-D2 complex provided mechanistic insights into both target RNA cleavage and second messenger synthesis. Collectively, our findings indicate that the type III-D2 complex detects phage-derived RNA and produces SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2, which potentially activate the CorA membrane protein, thereby providing anti-phage defense.

Results

Anti-phage defense by the type III-D2 system

To investigate the function of type III-D2 systems, we focused on the type III-D2 system from Gammaproteobacteria bacterium HyVt-522 (Fig. 1a). We reconstituted the heterologous system in Escherichia coli to express both the G. bacterium type III-D2 locus, encoding Cas6, the TA module, CorA, Cas10, Cas7-5, and Cas7x3, and a crRNA targeting the T7 phage capsid protein Gp10A. We then infected the bacterial cells with T7 phage and assessed phage resistance using spot assays (Fig. 1b). E. coli cells expressing the wild-type (WT) type III-D2 system exhibited resistance under high phage titer conditions compared to the control strain containing an empty vector (Fig. 1b), demonstrating that the type III-D2 system protects host cells against invading phages. The deletion of any of the individual genes abolished the defense activity (Fig. 1b), indicating that all genes in the type III-D2 locus are essential for anti-phage defense. E. coli cells expressing a non-targeting crRNA showed no detectable defense (Fig. 1b), confirming that the type III-D2 system detects invading phage-derived RNA via base complementarity with the crRNA guide. Furthermore, a double mutation (D347A/E348A) in the conserved catalytic motif of Cas10 eliminated the defense activity (Fig. 1b), suggesting that the type III-D2 complex synthesizes second messengers via its Cas10 subunit.

To explore the anti-phage defense mechanism of the type III-D2 system, we monitored the growth of E. coli cells expressing the G. bacterium type III-D2 system after infection with T7 phage (Fig. 1c). The control strain exhibited growth defects at all tested multiplicities of infection (MOI). In contrast, E. coli cells expressing the WT type III-D2 system exhibited normal growth under low MOI conditions, but their growth was inhibited under high MOI conditions (Fig. 1c), indicating that the type III-D2 system protects host cells against phage infection by inducing cell growth arrest. However, E. coli cells expressing the type III-D2 system eventually exhibited growth defects under low MOI conditions, suggesting that the system is insufficient to completely prevent the rapid cell lysis and propagation induced by the T7 phage.

To investigate the mechanism of bacterial growth arrest by the type III-D2 system, we transformed E. coli cells with plasmids encoding either the WT type III-D2 system, a system with the Cas10 D347A/E348A mutation, or systems lacking either CorA or the TA module, with or without a plasmid encoding a target RNA complementary to the crRNA guide. We then examined colony formation by bacterial cells expressing the III-D2 systems in the presence or absence of the target RNA. E. coli cells with the WT system yielded colonies in the absence, but not the presence, of the target RNA (Fig. 1d), indicating that the III-D2 system induces bacterial growth arrest upon target RNA recognition. In contrast, E. coli cells with the system containing the Cas10 mutation yielded colonies even with the target RNA (Fig. 1d), indicating that Cas10-produced second messengers mediate the growth arrest. Notably, the deletion of CorA allowed colony formation even with the target RNA (Fig. 1d), suggesting that the second messengers act on CorA to induce growth arrest. In contrast, colony formation was still inhibited even when the TA module was deleted (Fig. 1d), although our phage spot assay showed that the TA module is required for anti-phage defense (Fig. 1b), suggesting that the TA module does not contribute to growth arrest but plays a distinct role in this process. Collectively, these results indicate that the type III-D2 complex detects phage infection and produces second messengers that act on the CorA membrane protein, thereby providing anti-phage defense by halting the growth of infected bacterial cells.

Target RNA cleavage by the type III-D2 complex

To investigate the enzymatic activity of the type III-D2 complex, we individually purified the Cas7x3 subunit, the Cas7-5–Cas10 subcomplex, and the Cas6 nuclease, which processes precursor crRNAs (pre-crRNAs) into mature crRNAs in some CRISPR-Cas systems35 (Supplementary Fig. 1b). To examine pre-crRNA processing by Cas6, we incubated the purified Cas6 with a 72-nt FAM-labeled pre-crRNA, consisting of a 37-nt direct repeat and a 35-nt spacer, and analyzed the reaction products by denaturing urea-PAGE. The pre-crRNA was processed by Cas6 into a 43-nt mature crRNA (Supplementary Fig. 1c), suggesting that the mature crRNA in the type III-D2 system contains an 8-nt repeat-derived 5′ tag. To assess the target RNA cleavage activity of the type III-D2 complex, we next incubated the type III-D2 complex, consisting of Cas7x3, Cas10–Cas7-5, and the mature crRNA, with a 60-nt Cy5-labeled target RNA (Fig. 2a). We found a single 35-nt product, indicating that the type III-D2 complex cleaves the target RNA at a single site between positions 30 and 31, counting from the 5′ end of the spacer-complementary sequence. These results revealed that, unlike the type III-A/B and III-E complexes, which cleave the target RNA at multiple sites, the III-D2 complex produces a single cut in the target RNA. The target RNA was not cleaved in the absence of Mg2+ (Supplementary Fig. 1d), indicating that a divalent metal ion is required for target RNA cleavage. Furthermore, the type III-D2 complex cleaved both the CTR and NTR (Supplementary Fig. 1d), confirming that RNA cleavage is independent of the 3′ anti-tag, as observed in other type III-A/B complexes9.

SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2 synthesis by the type III-D2 complex

To investigate the Cas10 activity, we incubated the type III-D2 complex with CTR and ATP, and then analyzed the reaction mixture by thin-layer chromatography. However, no product was detected (Fig. 2b), indicating that, unlike canonical type III-A/B complexes, the type III-D2 complex does not synthesize cOAs. A previous study reported that the B. fragilis type III-B complex synthesizes SAM-AMP, serving as a second messenger for a CorA family membrane protein20. As type III-D2 systems also encode CorA homologs, we hypothesized that the type III-D2 complex might synthesize SAM-AMP. To test this possibility, we incubated the type III-D2 complex with the target RNA (CTR or NTR), ATP, and SAM, and then analyzed the reaction products. Notably, two distinct products (products 1 and 2) were generated in the presence of CTR, but not NTR (Fig. 2b), indicating that CTR binds to the complex and activates Cas10 to facilitate the synthesis of these molecules from ATP and SAM. We analyzed the reaction mixture using capillary electrophoresis–mass spectrometry, and identified product 1 (m/z = 728.1978) as SAM-AMP, and product 2 (m/z = 1057.2491) as SAM-AMP conjugated with AMP (Fig. 2c–e and Supplementary Fig. 1e–g). Tandem mass spectrometry analysis of product 2 identified fragment ions consistent with SAM-AMP20, along with a fragment with m/z = 677.1237, corresponding to pApA, a dinucleotide formed by the conjugation of two AMP molecules (Supplementary Fig. 1f, g). These data suggest that product 2 is a molecule composed of SAM and two AMP molecules, and it is hereafter referred to as SAM-AMP2 (Fig. 2f). In addition, both SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2 were detected in lysates of E. coli cells heterologously expressing the type III-D2 complex along with the target RNA, but they were not detected in lysates from cells expressing the complex with a non-targeting crRNA (Fig. 2g).

To gain mechanistic insights into Cas10-mediated second messenger production, we examined the in vitro synthesis of SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2 under varying ratios of ATP and SAM (1:1, 1:2, 1:5, 2:1, and 5:1) (Fig. 2h). SAM-AMP was the major product under conditions of 1 mM ATP and 1 mM SAM, while SAM-AMP2 production was enhanced when an excess amount of ATP (2 or 5 mM) was present compared to SAM (1 mM) (Fig. 2h). Notably, we observed a decrease in SAM-AMP and a concomitant increase in SAM-AMP2 during the reaction, indicating that SAM-AMP2 is synthesized from ATP and SAM-AMP. SAM-AMP, but not SAM-AMP2, was produced at 1 mM ATP and 2 mM SAM (Fig. 2h), suggesting that excess SAM impedes SAM-AMP binding to the Cas10 active site, thereby inhibiting SAM-AMP2 synthesis. Neither SAM-AMP nor SAM-AMP2 was formed at 1 mM ATP and 5 mM SAM (Fig. 2h), suggesting that excess SAM may occupy the ATP-binding site in the Cas10 subunit, thereby inhibiting SAM-AMP synthesis. These results further support the notion that SAM-AMP2 is synthesized from ATP and SAM-AMP. In canonical type III effector complexes, cOA synthesis is enhanced by prolonged target RNA binding8. To test this possibility, we compared the in vitro synthesis of SAM-AMP/SAM-AMP2 between the WT type III-D2 complex and a complex containing the Cas7-5 D29A mutant, which lacks target RNA cleavage activity. The Cas7-5 D29A mutation did not affect the production of either SAM-AMP or SAM-AMP2 (Fig. 2h), likely because the target RNA cleavage by the Cas7-5 subunit is relatively slower than the second messenger synthesis by the Cas10 subunit. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that, unlike other known CRISPR effector complexes, the type III-D2 system utilizes two second messengers, SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2. To our knowledge, SAM-AMP2 has not been previously described, suggesting that it is a new class of signaling molecule.

Cryo-EM analysis of the type III-D2 complex

To explore the molecular mechanisms of target RNA cleavage and second messenger synthesis by the type III-D2 complex, we determined the cryo-EM structure of the type III-D2 complex bound to a target RNA (CTR or NTR), SAM, and AMPPNP (a less-reactive ATP analog), which substantially reduced second messenger production (Supplementary Fig. 1h). We reconstituted the complex by mixing Cas7x3, Cas7-5–Cas10, a 43-nt mature crRNA, and a 47-nt target RNA (CTR or NTR), and then added SAM and AMPPNP, and performed the cryo-EM analysis (Supplementary Figs. 2a–j and 3a–i). Our cryo-EM analysis of the CTR-bound complex revealed two distinct states (states 1 and 2) (Supplementary Fig. 2b–j). In state 1, nucleotides −6 to 31 in CTR are well resolved in the density map (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Fig. 2i). In contrast, in state 2, no density was observed for nucleotides −6 to 7 in CTR (Supplementary Figs. 2j and 4a, b), suggesting that CTR was partially cleaved and dissociated during sample preparation. Since states 1 and 2 represent pre- and post-cleavage structures, respectively, we refer to them as CTR-pre and CTR-post. In the NTR-bound structure, nucleotides −6 to 31 in NTR are resolved in the density map (Supplementary Figs. 3i and 4c, d), thus representing the pre-cleavage state, which we refer to as NTR-pre.

The type III-A/B effector complexes possess two ATP-binding sites in their Cas10 active sites, where the 3′-OH group of one ATP molecule (the acceptor) attacks the α-phosphate of another ATP molecule (the donor) during cOA synthesis36–39. In the CTR-pre state, we observed distinct densities corresponding to AMPPNP and two Mg2+ ions at the donor ATP-binding site in the Cas10 active site (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Video 1). Additionally, we identified a nucleobase-shaped density at a position equivalent to the acceptor ATP-binding sites of the type III-A/B complexes (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Video 1), suggesting that this density corresponds to the acceptor SAM molecule for SAM-AMP synthesis. Although the amino-acid moiety of SAM is located near Cas10 residues such as E8 and Q186, it is less well resolved in the density map, indicating that this moiety does not form stable interactions with the Cas10 protein. Furthermore, we detected an additional nucleobase-shaped density adjacent to the Cas7-5 residue W351 (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Video 1), suggesting that this density represents a second SAM molecule bound to the active site. While the densities for these two SAM molecules, aside from their adenine moieties, are relatively weak, our mutational analysis indicated that E8/Q186 and W351 are essential for the production of SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2, respectively (as described later), supporting our assignment of the two SAM molecules to these densities. Due to the overlap of the amino-acid moieties of the SAM molecules, we modeled a SAM molecule with two alternative conformations (referred to as SAM1 and SAM2) in the CTR-pre state. In the CTR-post and NTR-pre states, we observed similar densities for AMPPNP and SAM2, while no density was detected for SAM1, possibly due to conformational differences in the Cas10 active site (as described later) (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). Thus, we modeled AMPPNP and SAM2 in the CTR-post and NTR-pre states.

Overall, we determined the cryo-EM structures of the type III-D2 complex in three distinct states—CTR-pre (AMPPNP and SAM1/SAM2), CTR-post (AMPPNP and SAM2), and NTR-pre (AMPPNP and SAM2)—at 2.6-, 2.8-, and 2.7-Å resolutions, respectively (Fig. 3a–d and Supplementary Fig. 4a–d). Since their architectures are essentially identical, except for the conformational differences around the Cas10 active site, we first describe the overall structure of the CTR-pre state, and then focus on the conformational changes in the Cas10 subunit, which are important for second messenger synthesis.

Overall structure of the type III-D2 complex

The structures reveal that the type III-D2 complex includes the Cas10, Cas7-5, and Cas7x3 subunits (Fig. 3a–d, Supplementary Fig. 6a–e, and Supplementary Video 2). Cas10 comprises four domains: Palm1, Linker, Palm2, and the C-terminal domain (CTD) (Fig. 3c, d and Supplementary Fig. 6c). Cas7-5 contains the Cas7.1 and Cas5 domains, separated by an interdomain linker (L1) (Fig. 3c, d and Supplementary Fig. 6d), while Cas7x3 consists of three Cas7 domains (Cas7.2–Cas7.4), separated by two interdomain linkers (L2 and L3), with the Cas7.4 domain harboring a large insertion (INS) domain and a C-terminal extension (CTE) domain (Fig. 3c, d and Supplementary Fig. 6e). Cas5 and Cas7.1–Cas7.4 stack together to form a right-handed helical filament, as observed in other type III complex structures24,28,36,40–42 (Fig. 3c, d). Cas10 interacts with Cas5/Cas7.1 in Cas7-5 and Cas7.4 in Cas7x3. The 5′ tag of the crRNA is anchored by Cas7-5, while the spacer region base pairs with the target RNA to form the guide–target duplex, which is recognized by Cas7.1 in Cas7-5 and Cas7.2–Cas7.4/INS in Cas7x3 (Fig. 3c, d).

Structures of Cas7-5, Cas7x3, and Cas10

Cas7.1–Cas7.3 adopt a modified RRM (RNA recognition motif) fold, with the thumb-like β-hairpins characteristic of Cas7 domains, as commonly observed in other type III Cas7 proteins23,28,36,40–42 (Supplementary Fig. 6f–i). Cas7.1 contains a catalytic loop with a conserved aspartate residue (D29) located adjacent to its thumb-like β-hairpin, whereas Cas7.2 and Cas7.3 have elongated β-hairpins at equivalent positions but lack this aspartate residue (Supplementary Fig. 6f–h). Unlike the other three Cas7 domains, Cas7.3 contains a zinc-finger motif, with a zinc ion coordinated by C338, C344, C351, and C354 (Supplementary Fig. 6h). Cas7.4 adopts a highly divergent RRM fold and contains the INS and CTE domains, instead of the thumb-like β-hairpin (Supplementary Fig. 6i). Cas5 consists of N- and C-terminal modified RRM folds, with the β-strand-rich Lid domain between the two RRM modules (Supplementary Fig. 6d). The L2 linker between Cas7.2 and Cas7.3 is disordered, while the L3 linker between Cas7.3 and Cas7.4 extensively interacts with the Cas7.4 and CTE domains (Supplementary Fig. 6e).

The two Palm domains (Palm1 and Palm2) contain a modified RRM fold typical of nucleic-acid polymerases and nucleotide cyclases, and form a pseudosymmetric catalytic pocket in which SAM, AMPPNP, and two Mg2+ ions are bound (Supplementary Fig. 6c). The Palm1 domain interacts with the Lid domain in Cas7-5, while the Palm2 domain contains the catalytic GGDE motif (residues 345–348) responsible for the synthesis activity (Supplementary Fig. 6c). The CTD adopts a Cas11-like fold comprising a five-helix bundle and interacts with the Palm2 domain of Cas10, the Cas7.1 domain of Cas7-5, the Cas7.4 domain of Cas7x3, and the target RNA (Fig. 3c, d and Supplementary Fig. 6c). The Linker domain interacts with the Palm/CTD in Cas10, Cas5 in Cas7-5, and the 3′ anti-tag region of the target RNA (Fig. 3c, d and Supplementary Fig. 6c).

RNA recognition mechanism

The 5′ tag region (A( − 8)–C( − 1)) of the crRNA adopts a single-stranded conformation and is extensively recognized by Cas7-5 (Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Fig. 7a). The thumb-like β-hairpin of Cas5 intercalates between U1 and A( − 2), resulting in the base flipping of C( − 1) (Fig. 4b). The nucleobase of C( − 1) forms stacking interactions with K50 and I374 and a hydrogen bond with Q53 (Fig. 4b). The nucleobases of A( − 2)–G( − 5) are sandwiched between F387 and Y522, with A( − 4) and G( − 5) forming hydrogen bonds with S86 and S86/R231, respectively, thereby leaving the Watson-Crick faces in A( − 2)–G( − 5) solvent-exposed and available for base pairing (Fig. 4b). The nucleobase of U( − 6) stacks with L523, while that of U( − 7) forms a hydrogen bond with T255 (Fig. 4b). A( − 8) adopts the syn conformation, with its nucleobase sandwiched between F281 and W589. Notably, A( − 8) contains a hydroxy group rather than a phosphate at its 5′ position, and is buried within Cas7-5 (Fig. 4b), consistent with Cas6-catalyzed pre-crRNA processing between positions −9 and −8. In addition, the 5′ tag region is recognized by Cas7.1 and Cas5 through sequence-independent backbone interactions (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 7a).

The 32-nt spacer region of the crRNA (U1–A32) base pairs with the target RNA (A1*–U32*) to form the guide–target duplex, which is recognized by Cas7.1–Cas7.4, INS, and CTE (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 7a). The duplex can be divided into two distinct segments (the helical and ladder segments), based on their structural features (Supplementary Fig. 7a). The helical segment (A19-U19*–A32-U32*) adopts a distorted helical conformation and is recognized by Cas7.4, INS, and CTE (Fig. 4a). In contrast, the ladder segment (U1-A1*–A18-U18*) adopts an underwound ribbon-like conformation resembling a ladder and is recognized by Cas7.1–Cas7.3 (Fig. 4a). The thumb-like β-hairpins in Cas7.1–Cas7.3 intercalate within the guide–target duplex, flipping every sixth nucleotide at the 6th, 12th, and 18th positions, respectively, as in other type III complexes36,40 (Fig. 4c–e).

Target RNA cleavage mechanism

The cryo-EM structure explains how the type III-D2 complex cleaves a target RNA at a single, specific site. The C5-G5* and U7-A7* base pairs stack with R119/V135 and I120/I134 in the thumb-like β-hairpin of Cas7.1, respectively, facilitating the base flipping of A6* (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). The catalytic loop of Cas7.1 is located adjacent to the β-hairpin, where the conserved residue D29 coordinates a Mg2+ ion near the target phosphodiester bond between G5* and A6* (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 7b). Furthermore, Y491 of Cas10 stacks with the flipped-out A6*, while E436 coordinates a second Mg2+ ion that contacts the phosphate group between G5* and A6* (Fig. 4c). The D29A mutation abolished target RNA cleavage in vitro (Fig. 4f), confirming that D29 is essential for catalysis. The E436A mutation moderately reduced the cleavage activity (Fig. 4f), suggesting the involvement of the second Mg2+ ion in target RNA recognition. Similarly, U12* and U18* in the target RNA are flipped out from the duplex (Fig. 4d, e). However, D29 of Cas7.1 corresponds to Q52 of Cas7.2 and A299 of Cas7.3, explaining why Cas7.2 and Cas7.3 lack target RNA cleavage activity. Together, these observations demonstrate that the type III-D2 complex cleaves the target RNA at a single site between positions 5* and 6*.

Structural changes in Cas10 upon 3′ anti-tag binding

In the CTR-pre and NTR-pre states, the 3′ anti-tag regions of the target RNA bind to the type III-D2 complex in distinct manners (Fig. 5a–d and Supplementary Fig. 7a). In the NTR-pre state, the thumb-like β-hairpin of Cas5 intercalates between A1* and U( − 2)*, resulting in the base flipping of G( − 1)*, which is recognized by D439 in Cas10 (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 7b). U( − 2)*–C( − 5)* form base pairs with A( − 2)–G( − 5) of the crRNA, with U( − 2)* and A( − 6)* interacting with F387 and E525 in Cas5, respectively. Thus, the 3′ anti-tag of NTR is bound to the interface between Cas7-5 and Cas10 (Fig. 5c). In contrast, in the CTR-pre state, A( − 2)*–G( − 5)* cannot base pair with A( − 2)–G( − 5) of the crRNA and instead bind to a channel in the Cas10 Linker domain (Fig. 5d). In the channel, A( − 2)*, A( − 3)*, A( − 4)*, and G( − 5)* form base-non-specific interactions with H400/N401, L264/H405, P217, and D215/R240, respectively (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 7a). In the CTR-post state, the 3′ anti-tag region dissociates from the complex and no longer interacts with the Cas10 subunit (Supplementary Fig. 7c).

A structural comparison of the CTR-pre and NTR-pre states reveals that 3′ anti-tag binding to the Cas10 Linker domain induces a conformational change in the Cas10 active site (Fig. 5e, f). Specifically, 3′ anti-tag binding causes a slight shift (~3 Å) of the Palm2 domain relative to Palm1, resulting in a conformational rearrangement in the interface between the Palm1 and Palm2 domains, where AMPPNP and SAM are bound in the current structures (Fig. 5g). A superimposition of the CTR-pre and NTR-pre structures indicates that AMPPNP and SAM bound to the CTR-pre structure sterically clash with the Cas10 active site in the NTR-pre structure (Fig. 5h), suggesting that ATP and SAM can bind to the Cas10 active site in a productive conformation in the CTR-bound state, but not in the NTR-bound state (as described later). Together, these structural findings establish that the 3′ anti-tag of CTR contacts the Cas10 subunit to allosterically induce a conformational change in the Cas10 active site, explaining why the type III-D2 complex generates the second messengers in the presence of CTR, but not NTR.

SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2 synthesis mechanism

The structures reveal that AMPPNP and SAM bind to the Cas10 active site in distinct manners in the three states, as mentioned above (Fig. 6a–e, Supplementary Fig. 8a–c, and Supplementary Video 3). In the CTR-pre state, the adenine base of AMPPNP is accommodated within a pocket formed by G121/A122/W124 in the Palm1 domain and F290/F306/G346 in the Palm2 domain (Fig. 6a, c, d and Supplementary Fig. 8a–c). D307 in the Palm2 domain is located 3.6-Å from the amino group of the adenine base, contributing to ATP specificity. In addition, the phosphate groups of AMPPNP interact with the main-chain amide groups of K289, F290, and G291, as well as two Mg2+ ions, which are coordinated by D286/G287/D347 and D286/D347/E348, respectively (Fig. 6d). Alanine mutations of D286, D307, D347, and E348, but not W124A, abolished SAM-AMP synthesis in vitro (Fig. 6f), confirming their functional importance.

Cryo-EM density maps indicate that, while SAM binds to the Cas10 active site in two distinct conformations (SAM1 and SAM2) in the CTR-pre state, it adopts a single conformation (SAM2) in the CTR-post and NTR-pre states (Fig. 6a–e and Supplementary Fig. 8a–c). The adenine base of SAM1 is accommodated within a pocket between the Palm1 and Palm2 domains, forming hydrophobic interactions with L12/L33 and hydrogen bonds with S29/S123 (Fig. 6d). The carboxylate and amino groups of SAM1 are located near N11/L12/Q186 and E8 in the Cas10 Palm1 domain, respectively (Fig. 6d). While the E8A and Q186A mutations did not substantially affect SAM-AMP synthesis, the E8A/Q186A double mutation abolished this activity (Fig. 6f), indicating that E8 and Q186 are involved in SAM recognition. The D29A mutation in Cas7-5 had no effect on SAM-AMP synthesis (Fig. 6f), reflecting that SAM-AMP synthesis does not depend on target RNA cleavage. While the amino-acid moieties of SAM1 and SAM2 reside at similar positions, the adenine base of SAM2 interacts with W351 on the surface of the Cas7-5 Lid domain (Fig. 6d, e and Supplementary Fig. 8a–c). In addition, the sulfonium group of SAM2 is located near the main-chain carbonyl group of L337 in the Lid domain (Fig. 6e). Notably, the 3′-OH group of SAM1 is adjacent to the α-phosphate of AMPPNP, whereas that of SAM2 is far from AMPPNP (Fig. 6d, e and Supplementary Fig. 8c). These observations indicate that SAM1 adopts a productive conformation, enabling the nucleophilic attack on the α-phosphate of ATP during SAM-AMP synthesis, while SAM2 assumes a non-productive conformation.

In the CTR-post and NTR-pre states, SAM and AMPPNP also bind to the Cas10 active site (Fig. 6b, e and Supplementary Fig. 8a–c). However, AMPPNP binds at the interface between the Palm1 and Palm2 domains in a manner distinct from that in the CTR-pre state, due to conformational differences in their Cas10 active sites (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 8a–c). The adenine base of AMPPNP forms hydrophobic interactions with W124, Q294, and K295 (Fig. 6e). The β- and γ-phosphate groups of AMPPNP are stabilized by a Mg2+ ion, which is coordinated by D286, G287, and D347, and form hydrogen bonds with the side chains of K420 and K425 and the main-chain amides of G291 and Q292 (Fig. 6e). SAM is located at a position similar to SAM2 in the CTR-pre state, with its 3′-OH group distant from the α-phosphate of AMPPNP (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 8c). These observations indicate that, in the NTR-pre state, SAM and ATP bind to the Cas10 active site in non-productive conformations, explaining why the type III-D2 complex does not produce SAM-AMP in the presence of NTR.

Mutations of residues essential for SAM-AMP synthesis abolished SAM-AMP2 formation (Fig. 6f), indicating that SAM-AMP2 is produced from ATP and SAM-AMP, consistent with our biochemical data (Fig. 2h). Notably, the W351A mutation enhanced SAM-AMP synthesis but abolished SAM-AMP2 production (Fig. 6f), suggesting that the SAM2-binding site is critical for SAM-AMP binding during SAM-AMP2 synthesis. Modeling of SAM-AMP and ATP into the CTR-pre structure suggests that the SAM and AMP moieties of the SAM-AMP molecule can bind to the SAM2- and SAM1-binding sites, respectively (Fig. 6g). In this model, the 3′-OH group of the AMP moiety is positioned close to the donor ATP molecule, with the adenine base in the SAM moiety stacking with W351 (Fig. 6g). These findings imply that the synthesized SAM-AMP molecule translocates to serve as the acceptor for SAM-AMP2 synthesis, similar to the successive adenylation mechanism observed in cOA synthesis by the type III-A complexes37–39. Overall, the cryo-EM structures provide mechanistic insights into SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2 synthesis by the type III-D2 complex.

Biological significance of SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2 second messengers

To assess the biological significance of the enzymatic activities of the type III-D2 complex, we examined the effects of mutations in the Cas7.1 and Cas10 active sites on anti-phage defense, using spot assays (Fig. 6h). The D29A mutation in Cas7-5, which abolished target RNA cleavage, had no effect (Fig. 6h), indicating that RNA cleavage is not necessary for anti-phage defense in our T7 infection system. In contrast, the D347A and E8A/Q186A mutants, which are incapable of second messenger synthesis, exhibited no defense activity (Fig. 6h), revealing that second messenger synthesis is essential for anti-phage defense. The W124A and Q186A mutations, which lacked substantial effects on second messenger synthesis, still reduced anti-phage defense (Fig. 6h), and thus anti-phage defense is attenuated by even partial reductions in second messenger synthesis. Interestingly, the W351A mutation, which enhances SAM-AMP synthesis but abolishes SAM-AMP2 production, reduced the defense activity (Fig. 6h), suggesting that SAM-AMP alone is insufficient for anti-phage defense by the type III-D2 system, with SAM-AMP2 playing a supportive role. Collectively, these results demonstrate that both SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2 participate in anti-phage defense by the type III-D2 system.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the function and structure of the type III-D2 CRISPR effector complex, providing insights into the molecular evolution of the type III systems and highlighting the mechanistic diversity of the CRISPR effector complexes. This diversity is evident from a structural comparison of the type III-D2 complex with the other type III complexes24,28,36 (Fig. 7a, b). While type III complexes commonly contain a central filament formed by multiple Cas7 family subunits, which generally mediate target RNA cleavage, the components at both ends of the Cas7 filament are structurally divergent. In the Streptococcus thermophilus type III-A complex, the Cas7 (Csm3) filament is sandwiched between the Cas5/Cas10 (Csm4/Csm1) subunits and the Cas7 family subunit Csm536. In the Desulfonema ishimotonii type III-E complex, the Cas7.1 domain and the Csx29 protease subunit are located at one end of the Cas7 filament, while the INS domain, inserted in the Cas7.4 domain, interacts with the guide–target RNA duplex at the other end24 (Fig. 7a). The type III-Dv28 and III-D2 complexes adopt hybrid architectures comprising the Cas5/Cas10 domains (like type III-A) and the INS domain (like type III-E) (Fig. 7a). These structural observations suggest that, during evolution, the type III-D and III-E complexes acquired the INS domain within a Cas7 subunit, followed by the fusion of Cas subunits to generate multidomain proteins such as Cas7-5, Cas7x3, and Cas7-11. The type III-E complex further diverged by the replacement of Cas5/Cas10 with Csx29. The type III-A, III-B, and III-D systems encode Cas6 and Cas10, which mediate pre-crRNA processing and second messenger synthesis, respectively. In contrast, the type III-E system lacks Cas6 and Cas5/Cas10, yet it possesses the diverged Cas7.1 domain and the Csx29 protease subunit, which mediate pre-crRNA processing and target RNA-dependent signal transduction, respectively23–26 (Fig. 7a, b). Together, these structural and functional comparisons illuminate the gradual structural transitions accompanying the functional diversification during the molecular evolution of the type III effector complexes.

Unlike the other type III complexes, the type III-D2 complex produces both SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2. A structural comparison of the type III-D2 complex with the type III-A complex explains their abilities to synthesize different second messengers (Fig. 7c and Supplementary Fig. 8d). In the type III-A and III-D2 complex structures, donor ATP molecules (pppA3 in the Lactococcus lactis type III-A complex) and acceptor molecules (ATP in type III-A and SAM in type III-D2) occupy similar pockets at the Cas10 active sites36–39 (Fig. 7c), indicating the conserved catalytic mechanisms for cOA and SAM-AMP synthesis. However, the 5′ moieties of their acceptor molecules (ATP and SAM) are recognized in distinct manners. In the L. lactis type III-A complex, the triphosphate group of the acceptor ATP molecule interacts with the side chains of S255 and K379 and the main-chain amides of G256, V257, and Q258, along with a Mg2+ ion coordinated by D253 and M25438 (Fig. 7c). In contrast, in the type III-D2 complex, the amino and carboxylate groups of the acceptor SAM molecule are likely recognized by E8 and Q186, located at positions equivalent to D253 and K379 in the type III-A complex (Fig. 7c). These structural differences in their Cas10 subunits explain why the type III-A and III-D2 complexes produce cOA and SAM-AMP, respectively, as second messengers. In the type III-A complex, the guide RNA–target RNA base-pairing in the Cas10-proximal region is more important for cOA production than that in the Cas10-distal region43, suggesting that the guide–target base-pairing in the Cas10-proximal region is also crucial for second messenger synthesis by the type III-D2 complex.

The B. fragilis type III-B complex also produces SAM-AMP, using the Cas10 subunit20, and its cryo-EM structure bound to ATP and SAM was reported while our manuscript was under review44. A structural comparison of the type III-D2 complex with the B. fragilis type III-B complex illuminates the shared and distinct mechanisms of SAM-AMP synthesis (Fig. 7c). In both structures, ATP and SAM occupy the donor- and acceptor-binding pockets of the Cas10 active site, respectively (Fig. 7c and Supplementary Fig. 8d). In the type III-D2 Cas10 structure, the amino-acid moiety of SAM is positioned near the main-chain amides of N11 and L12 and the side chains of E8 and Q186. Consistently, the E8A/Q186A double mutation abolished SAM-AMP synthesis, underscoring the importance of these residues for SAM recognition, despite the weak density for the amino-acid group. In the B. fragilis type III-B Cas10 structure, the amino-acid moiety of SAM is located near D70 and E15144, and the D70N/E151R double mutation similarly abolished SAM-AMP synthesis20. These structural observations highlight the similar SAM-AMP synthesis mechanisms in the type III-D2 and B. fragilis type III-B complexes. In addition, almost no density was observed for the amino-acid moiety of SAM in the type III-B complex structure44, suggesting that SAM may commonly bind to the Cas10 active sites in a transient manner. Notably, the B. fragilis type III-B complex lacks a tryptophan residue corresponding to Cas7-5 W351 in the type III-D2 complex, which is important for SAM-AMP2 synthesis (Fig. 7c and Supplementary Fig. 8d). This may explain why the B. fragilis type III-B complex produces SAM-AMP, but not SAM-AMP2. Collectively, these structural insights enhance our understanding of non-canonical SAM-AMP second messenger synthesis by the type III effector complexes.

Our biological data demonstrate that (1) both CorA and the TA module are essential for anti-phage defense in the G. bacterium type III-D2 system, (2) CorA, but not the TA module, is involved in growth arrest mediated by the second messengers, and (3) SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2 play primary and supportive roles, respectively, in the defense mechanism. In the B. fragilis type III-B system, the SAM-AMP second messenger binds to the cytoplasmic domain of CorA, inducing the opening of its transmembrane pore and thereby leading to cell dormancy or death through the disruption of membrane integrity20. Thus, it is likely that, in the type III-D2 system, CorA also binds the second messengers and induces growth arrest through a similar mechanism. Notably, the CorA proteins in the type III-B and type III-D2 systems share only limited sequence identity (15% for the full-length proteins and, in particular, 12% for their N-terminal soluble domains) (Supplementary Fig. 9), suggesting that these differences underlie their distinct abilities to bind different second messengers (i.e., type III-B CorA specifically binds SAM-AMP, whereas type III-D2 CorA binds both SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2).

The TA module in the G. bacterium type III-D2 system resembles the VapBC family of TA systems33,45. In canonical VapBC systems, VapC, a PIN-domain ribonuclease, cleaves specific RNA substrates, thereby inhibiting bacterial growth46,47. However, E. coli cells harboring the type III-D2 system without the TA module still exhibited growth arrest, indicating that the TA module does not function as a toxin, consistent with the critical role of CorA in growth inhibition. Notably, previous studies reported that genes encoding phosphodiesterases, such as the NrN phosphodiesterase, SAM-AMP lyases, and DEDD-family nucleases, are located adjacent to the corA gene in CorA-containing type III CRISPR loci20,31. Furthermore, previous biochemical studies showed that the NrN phosphodiesterase and the SAM-AMP lyase from the type III-B systems degrade SAM-AMP and are indispensable for anti-phage defense20,48. Thus, Chi et al. proposed that these SAM-AMP degrading enzymes function as an “off switch” and deactivate the SAM-AMP signaling molecule to reset the immune response once phages have been eliminated from infected bacterial cells20, analogous to the ring nucleases that degrade cOA second messengers in canonical type III CRISPR-Cas systems49. Together, these observations suggest that the putative VapC-like enzyme in the type III-D2 TA module acts as an “off switch” by degrading SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2, thereby helping to regulate the immune response. In addition, our cryo-EM analysis revealed that, after target RNA cleavage, the Cas10 subunit adopts an inactive conformation and can no longer produce second messengers, due to the dissociation of the 3′ anti-tag-containing RNA fragment from the type III-D2 complex. This suggests that target RNA cleavage by the effector complex also contributes to immune response regulation.

Our biochemical experiments showed that SAM-AMP2 is produced in greater amounts than SAM-AMP when ATP is present in excess over SAM. Given that the intracellular ATP concentrations in bacteria are in the millimolar range50 and are likely higher than those of SAM (~0.4 mM)51, these observations suggest that SAM-AMP2 is the major product in vivo. However, our phage spot assays indicated that SAM-AMP2 contributes only partially to anti-phage defense, suggesting that SAM-AMP plays a primary role in this process. Together, these observations indicate that most SAM-AMP molecules produced in bacterial cells act on the CorA ancillary effector, with the remaining portion serving as a substrate for SAM-AMP2 synthesis.

Based on our functional and structural data, we propose a model for anti-phage defense in the type III-D2 CRISPR-Cas system (Fig. 7d). Upon phage infection, the type III-D2 complex recognizes and cleaves phage-derived RNA via Cas7-5, although this cleavage is dispensable for phage defense. Instead, target RNA recognition enables Cas10 to produce SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2, which potentially activate the CorA membrane protein, leading to host cell growth arrest and consequent anti-phage defense. After phage clearance, the TA module degrades the second messengers to reset the immune system. However, further studies are needed to elucidate how SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2 activate CorA and are potentially degraded by the TA module to facilitate anti-phage defense in the type III-D2 CRISPR-Cas system.

Methods

Bacterial strains and phages

Escherichia coli DH10B (Thermo Fisher Scientific), Rosetta 2(DE3) (Novagen), and BL21(DE3) (Novagen) cells were cultured at 37 °C in LB medium supplemented with 30 mg/L kanamycin, 100 mg/L ampicillin, 20 mg/L chloramphenicol, or 50 mg/L streptomycin. The T7 phage used in this study is listed in Supplementary Data 1.

Plasmid construction

The type III-D2 CRISPR-Cas locus from Gammaproteobacteria bacterium HyVt-522 (DRNY01000543.1) was cloned into the pLG001 plasmid (Addgene ID: 157879) to construct pGabCas (Supplementary Data 1). For crRNA expression, a direct repeat followed by a spacer targeting either the T7 phage capsid gene gp10A or the streptomycin resistance gene strR was inserted into a modified pUC vector to construct pGabCRISPR_Gp10A and pGabCRISPR_StrR (Supplementary Data 1). For the plasmid challenge assay, a target sequence derived from part of the gp10A gene was inserted downstream of the T7 promoter in a modified pCDFDuet-1 vector to construct pCDFDuet-1_gp10A (Supplementary Data 1). For in vivo second messenger synthesis, the genes encoding Cas10, Cas7-5, and Cas7x3 were amplified by PCR from pGabCas and cloned into the pET28a vector to construct pET-Cas10–Cas7-5–Cas7x3 (Supplementary Data 1). For purification of the Cas7-5–Cas10 subcomplex, the corresponding genes were amplified by PCR from pGabCas and cloned into the pET28a vector (Novagen) to construct pET-Cas10–Cas7-5 (Supplementary Data 1). For purification of the Cas7x3 and Cas6 proteins, each gene was amplified by PCR from pGabCas and cloned into a modified pE-SUMO vector with an N-terminal His6-SUMO tag (LifeSensors) to construct pESUMO-Cas7x3 and pESUMO-Cas6 (Supplementary Data 1). The genes encoding Cas6 and the crRNA were amplified and cloned into the MCS-1 and MCS-2 sites of the pACYCDuet-1 vector (Novagen), respectively, to construct pACYC-Cas6-crRNA_StrR and pACYC-Cas6-crRNA_Gp10A, which express the mature crRNA (Supplementary Data 1). A modified pCDFDuet-1 vector was used as the strR target plasmid (Supplementary Data 1).

Phage spot assay

An overnight culture (500 μL) of E. coli DH10B cells harboring the indicated plasmids (Supplementary Data 1) was mixed with 12.5 mL of LB top agar (LB medium, 0.5% agarose, and 1 mM CaCl2) and poured onto an LB agar plate. The phage solution was serially diluted 10-fold in Sodium-Magnesium (SM) buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgSO4, and 0.01% (w/v) gelatin solution). Then, 2 µL of each phage dilution was spotted onto the bacterial lawn. Plates were incubated at room temperature until the spots dried and then at 37 °C. Lysis zones (plaques) were recorded 5 hours later.

Growth assay

E. coli DH10B cells harboring the indicated plasmids (Supplementary Data 1) were cultured at 37 °C until the OD600 reached 0.2, then supplemented with 1 mM CaCl2, and infected with the T7 phage at the indicated multiplicity of infection (MOI). A 100 µL aliquot of each diluted culture was transferred to a 96-well plate, which was sealed with a Breathe-Easy sealing membrane (Sigma Aldrich). Growth was monitored at 37 °C for 5 h with shaking, and OD600 values were monitored every 5 min using a SpectraMax iD5 plate reader (Molecular Devices). Raw data were analyzed and plotted using GraphPad Prism 10.

Plasmid challenge assay

E. coli BL21(DE3) cells were transformed with 50 ng of the indicated plasmids (Supplementary Data 1) and cultured in 1 mL SOC medium at 37 °C for 1 h. The cells were collected by centrifugation and resuspended in 30 μL of SOC medium, and then 25 μL of each 10-fold serial dilution was plated on LB agar supplemented with 100 mg/L ampicillin, 20 mg/L chloramphenicol, 50 mg/L streptomycin, and 0.1 mM IPTG. Colony formation was recorded 20 hours later.

Sample preparation

For the expression of His6-SUMO-tagged Cas7x3 and Cas6, E. coli Rosetta 2(DE3) cells individually transformed with pE-SUMO-Cas7x3 and pE-SUMO-Cas6 were cultured at 37 °C in LB medium until the OD600 reached 0.8, and then induced with 0.5 mM IPTG and cultured overnight at 20 °C. The cells were harvested, resuspended in Buffer A (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 20 mM imidazole, 1 M NaCl, 3 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, and 1 mM PMSF), and lysed by sonication. The lysates were centrifuged, and the supernatant was applied to Ni-NTA Superflow resin (QIAGEN) and eluted with buffer B (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 300 mM imidazole, 300 mM NaCl, and 3 mM 2-mercaptoethanol). After treatment with Ulp1 (prepared in-house), the protein was equilibrated with buffer C (20 mM HEPES-NaOH, pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT), loaded onto a HiTrap Heparin column (Cytiva), and then eluted with a linear gradient of 300–2,000 mM NaCl. The purified protein was stored at −80 °C until use.

For the expression of the Cas7-5–Cas10 subcomplex, E. coli Rosetta 2(DE3) cells transformed with pET-Cas10–Cas7-5 were cultured at 37 °C in LB medium until the OD600 reached 0.8, and then induced with 0.5 mM IPTG and cultured overnight at 20 °C. The cells were harvested, resuspended in buffer D (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 20 mM imidazole, 300 mM NaCl, 3 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, and 1 mM PMSF), and lysed by sonication. The lysates were centrifuged, and the supernatant was applied to Ni-NTA Superflow resin and eluted with buffer B (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 300 mM imidazole, 300 mM NaCl, and 3 mM 2-mercaptoethanol). The eluted protein was equilibrated with buffer E (20 mM HEPES-NaOH, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT), loaded onto a HiTrap Heparin column, and then eluted with a linear gradient of 150–2,000 mM NaCl. The purified protein was stored at −80 °C until use.

The unlabeled ssRNA targets (45 nucleotides plus 5′ GG) were transcribed in vitro with T7 RNA polymerase (Supplementary Data 1), and purified by 8% denaturing (7 M urea) polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. The 43-nt mature crRNA was purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (Supplementary Data 1). The 60-nt 5′-Cy5-labeled ssRNA targets and the 72-nt 3′-FAM-labeled pre-crRNA were purchased from Ajinomoto Bio-Pharma (Supplementary Data 1).

The type III-D2 complex was reconstituted by mixing the purified His6-Cas10–Cas7-5 subcomplex, the purified Cas7x3 protein, and the 43-nt mature crRNA at molar ratios of 1:1:1 for in vitro assays and 1:1:1.5 for cryo-EM analysis, respectively.

In vitro target RNA cleavage experiments

In vitro cleavage assays were performed using the reconstituted type III-D2 complex (1 μM) and 5′-Cy5-labeled ssRNA target (500 nM) in buffer F (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 145 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM DTT). Reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h (unless otherwise indicated) and then quenched by the addition of proteinase K, EDTA, and urea (final concentrations of 1 mg/ml proteinase K, 6 mM EDTA, and 400 μM urea) at 50 °C for 15 min. The samples were treated with 4.5 M urea denaturing buffer at 95 °C for 5 min, and then loaded onto a 20% Novex PAGE Tris–borate–EDTA (TBE)–urea gel (Invitrogen), which was run at 250 V at 60 °C. Fluorescent signals on the gels were imaged using the FUSION Solo S system (Vilber Bio Imaging).

In vitro pre-crRNA processing experiments

In vitro processing assays were performed using the purified Cas6 (1 μM) and the 3′-FAM-labeled pre-crRNA (500 nM) in buffer G (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT). Reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 0, 1, 5, and 15 min, and then quenched by the addition of proteinase K, EDTA, and urea (final concentrations of 1 mg/ml proteinase K, 6 mM EDTA, and 400 μM urea) at 50 °C for 15 min. The samples were treated with 4.5 M urea denaturing buffer at 95 °C for 5 min, and then loaded onto a 20% Novex PAGE Tris–borate–EDTA (TBE)–urea gel, which was run at 200 V at 60 °C. The gels were imaged with the FUSION Solo S system.

In vitro synthesis of SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2

In vitro second messenger synthesis assays were performed by incubating the reconstituted type III-D2 complex (1 μM) with the unlabeled ssRNA target (1 μM), 1 mM ATP, and 1 mM SAM at 37 °C for 1 h (unless otherwise indicated) in 100 μL of buffer H (20 mM HEPES-NaOH, pH 7.0, 145 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM DTT). For the thin-layer chromatography (TLC) analysis, the reactions were terminated by the addition of proteinase K, EDTA, and urea (final concentrations of 1 mg/ml proteinase K, 6 mM EDTA, and 400 μM urea) at 50 °C for 15 min. For the capillary electrophoresis and mass spectrometry (CE-MS) analysis, the reactions were terminated by heating at 95 °C for 1 min. The reaction mixtures were analyzed by TLC and CE-MS, as described below.

In vivo synthesis of SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2

E. coli BL21(DE3) cells harboring the indicated plasmids (Supplementary Data 1) were cultured at 37 °C in 20 mL of LB medium until the OD600 reached 0.4–0.6, and then induced with 0.2 mM IPTG and cultured overnight at 20 °C. The cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and then resuspended in cold extraction solvent (acetonitrile:methanol:water, 2:2:1 by volume). The samples were analyzed by CE-MS, as described below.

Thin-layer chromatography (TLC)

SAM-AMP and SAM-AMP2 were separated by TLC. The reaction mixtures (1 µL) were analyzed on 20 × 20 cm Silica gel coated with fluorescence indicator (F254) TLC aluminum plates (Merck) with 0.5 cm of TLC buffer (0.2 M ammonium bicarbonate, pH 9.0, 67% ethanol, and 33% water) at 37 °C. TLC plates were left in the TLC chamber for 90 min until the solvent front was approximately 3 cm from the top of the TLC plate. Product images were acquired by an iPhone (Apple Inc.), while TLC plates were exposed to 260 nm UV light.

Capillary electrophoresis and mass spectrometry (CE-MS)

The samples from either in vitro synthesis or E. coli cells were analyzed by CE-MS (Agilent Technologies). A fused silica capillary (50 µm i.d. × 100 cm) was used with 1 M formic acid as the electrolyte for the measurement of cationic metabolites. Methanol/water (50% v/v) containing 0.1 mM hexakis (2,2-difluoroethoxy) phosphazene was added at 10 µL/min as the sheath solution. Electrospray ionization (ESI)-TOFMS was performed in the positive ion mode and the capillary voltage was set to 4 kV. The acquired spectrum was re-calibrated by two reference standards ([13C isotopic ion of a protonated methanol dimer (2 MeOH + H)]+, m/z 66.0632) and ([hexakis(2,2- difluoroethoxy)phosphazene + H]+, m/z 622.0290). Relative migration times of all peaks were measured for metabolite identifications with normalization to the reference compound 3-aminopyrrolidine, by comparing their m/z values and relative migration times to the standards. Quantification of metabolites was performed by comparing the peak area of each metabolite to calibration curves generated using internal standardization with methionine sulfone. For anionic metabolite measurements, a COSMO(+) (chemically coated with cationic polymer) capillary (50 µm i.d. × 105 cm) was used with a 50 mM ammonium acetate solution (pH 8.5) as the electrolyte (Nacalai Tesque). Methanol/5 mM ammonium acetate (50% v/v) containing 0.1 mM hexakis (2,2-difluoroethoxy) phosphazene was applied at 10 µL/min as the sheath liquid. ESI-TOFMS was performed in the negative ion mode, and the capillary voltage was set to 3.5 kV. Trimesate and CAS were used for the references of the internal standards. Capillary electrophoresis time-of-flight mass spectrometry (CE-TOFMS) raw data were analyzed with the Master Hands software (Ver. 2.17.0.10). Relative peak areas were calculated by the ratio of the peak area divided by the internal standards. Metabolite concentrations were calculated based on the relative peak area between the sample and the standard, as previously described52.

Cryo-EM analysis

The type III-D2 complex (Cas7x3–Cas7-5–Cas10–crRNA) was mixed with the unlabeled ssRNA target (CTR or NTR) at a molar ratio of 1:2, and then incubated with 4 mM PEG8-NHS at 4 °C for 1 h to enhance the stability of the complex on cryo-EM grids. The PEGylated complex was purified by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superose 6 Increase 3.2/300 column (GE Healthcare), equilibrated with buffer I (20 mM HEPES-NaOH, pH 7.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT). The purified Cas7x3–Cas7-5–Cas10–crRNA–target RNA (CTR or NTR) complex solution (0.15 mg/mL) was mixed with 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM SAM, and 1 mM AMPPNP, and the mixture (3 μL) was immediately applied to two types of freshly glow-discharged Quantifoil Holey Carbon Grids (R1.2/1.3, Au, 300 mesh; R1.2/1.3, Cu, 300 mesh) (Quantifoil) using a Vitrobot Mark IV system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 4 °C, with a waiting time of 10 s and a blotting time of 6 s under 100% humidity conditions. The grids were plunge-frozen in liquid ethane cooled at liquid nitrogen temperature. Cryo-EM data were collected using a Titan Krios G3i electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific), running at 300 kV and equipped with a Gatan Quantum-LS Energy Filter (GIF) and a Gatan K3 Summit direct electron detector. Imaging was performed at a nominal magnification of 105,000×, corresponding to a calibrated pixel size of 0.83 Å. For the CTR-bound complex, each movie was dose-fractionated to 50 frames and recorded using the correlated double sampling mode at a dose rate of 7.6 e−/px/s, resulting in a total accumulated exposure of 49.8 e−/Å2. For the NTR-bound complex, each movie was similarly dose-fractionated to 50 frames and recorded using the correlated double sampling mode at a dose rate of 8.5 e−/px/s, resulting in a total accumulated exposure of 50.6 e−/Å2. The data were automatically acquired by the image shift method using the EPU software (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with a defocus range of −0.8 to −2.0 μm. In total, 10,669 and 4,760 movies were acquired for the CTR- and NTR-bound complexes, respectively.

For the CTR-bound complex, the data were processed using the cryoSPARC v4.6.2 software package53. The dose-fractionated movies were aligned using Patch motion correction, and the contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated using Patch-Based CTF estimation. Particles were automatically picked using Blob picker and template picker. The particles were curated by Heterogeneous Refinement, using the map derived from cryoSPARC Ab initio Reconstruction as the template. To further classify the conformational heterogeneity, the curated particles were divided into five particle sets using 3D classification without alignment, applying a focused mask on Cas7-5/Cas10. The two selected particle sets for the CTR-pre and -post states were refined using Non-uniform refinement54 with CTF value optimization and reference-based motion correction, yielding maps at 2.6- and 2.8-Å resolutions, respectively, based on the Fourier shell correlation (FSC) = 0.143 criterion55. To further improve the map quality in the Cas10 subunit, focused masks were generated for Cas7-5/Cas10. Particle sets of CTR-pre and CTR-post were refined using Local refinement with the Cas7-5/Cas10 masks, yielding local maps at 2.7- and 2.9-Å resolutions, respectively. The local resolutions were estimated by BlocRes in cryoSPARC56. For the NTR-bound complex, the data were processed using the same procedure. After 3D classification without alignment, the selected particle set of NTR-pre was refined by Non-uniform refinement with CTF value optimization and reference-based motion correction, yielding a map at 2.7-Å resolution. To further improve the map quality in the Cas10 subunit, the particle set of NTR-pre was refined using Local refinement with the Cas7-5/Cas10 mask, yielding a local map at 2.8-Å resolution. The local resolution was estimated by BlocRes in cryoSPARC.

Model building and validation

The models of the target RNA-bound type III-D2 complexes were manually built using COOT57, starting from a model predicted by ColabFold58. The global refinement maps and the local-refined maps were used in the model building. The models were refined using Servalcat59 against unsharpened half maps, with structural restraints from Boltz-260 predictions processed by ProSMART61. The models were validated using MolProbity62. The statistics of the 3D reconstruction and model refinement are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The molecular graphics and cryo-EM density map figures were prepared with CueMol (http://www.cuemol.org) or UCSF ChimeraX63.
