Reply to: Letter “The invisible “cuneus vein”…does it even exist?” on “The cuneus vein: initial anatomical description and preservation technique for posterior interhemispheric approaches”
Authors: Muhammet Enes Gurses, Abuzer Gungor, Uğur Türe
Published: 2026

Response to the Letter “The invisible ‘cuneus vein’…does it even exist?”

We thank Dr. Benndorf for his thoughtful analysis of our study and for highlighting the important role of modern venous imaging in the investigation of cerebral venous anatomy [1]. We are particularly appreciative of his expertise in neuroradiology and his constructive discussion regarding current and emerging imaging techniques.

The series referred to by the author as a “small case series” actually represents one of the largest reported series worldwide, including 303 posterior interhemispheric approaches. We believe that direct intraoperative observation by a surgeon represents a valid clinical finding and does not necessarily require additional validation through cadaveric or radiological studies. There are several possible reasons why this vein may not be demonstrated in cadaveric studies. First, obtaining access to very large cadaveric series is challenging, even in the largest anatomical laboratories worldwide. We believe that a minimum of 150 cadaveric specimens would be required to adequately evaluate this structure; however, obtaining such a large number of specimens is not practically feasible. Furthermore, available cadaveric specimens may not always be adequately injected or optimally preserved, which can significantly limit the visualization and dissection of small venous structures.

We believe that careful attention during surgical procedures is essential. The fact that this vein has remained largely unrecognized to date may be attributed to insufficient attention being paid to this anatomical structure during surgical exploration. We believe that it may also have been overlooked during routine surgical procedures due to the lack of specific awareness regarding its presence.

We agree that the principal limitation of our study is the inability to reliably identify the cuneus vein on preoperative magnetic resonance venography [3]. Indeed, we explicitly acknowledged this limitation in our manuscript and emphasized that the observation originated from intraoperative findings rather than radiological detection. Our primary objective was to report a recurrent venous structure encountered during posterior interhemispheric approaches and to describe practical techniques for its preservation. The central question raised by Dr. Benndorf is whether a structure that is not visualized on routine imaging can be considered a true anatomical entity. Throughout the history of microneurosurgery, numerous anatomical observations were initially identified through cadaveric dissection or operative experience before becoming consistently demonstrable with contemporary imaging techniques. The inability to visualize a structure with a particular imaging protocol should not be interpreted as evidence of its absence. We believe that this is a concept that has been misunderstood. We did not suggest that the reason this vein is not visualized on MRI is simply due to its small size. Rather, we believe that its anatomical course makes it difficult to identify on preoperative imaging. Because the vein runs parallel to the falx, we hypothesize that it may remain unrecognized on MRI, similar to the tentorial bridging veins. We agree that the retrospective nature of our imaging analysis represents a significant limitation. The surgical series spans two decades, during which substantial advances occurred in MRI hardware, acquisition protocols, and post-processing methods. Consequently, imaging studies were not homogeneous with respect to scanner generation, sequence selection, or venographic reconstruction techniques. Because the study was conceived as an anatomical and surgical report rather than an imaging investigation, systematic radiological measurements such as parieto-occipital vein diameter, drainage pattern classification, and comparative venographic analysis were not performed. In our previously published study on tentorial veins, these venous structures were also not adequately visualized on preoperative MRI [2]. We believe that the clear intraoperative identification of this vein becomes possible after brain relaxation, achieved through gravity-assisted retraction and cerebrospinal fluid drainage, which allows the venous structure to become exposed and accessible during surgery. As stated in our original manuscript, all MRI examinations were performed using a 3-T magnetic resonance scanner (GE Healthcare, SIGNA Architect, USA). Patients underwent pre- and postoperative MRI evaluations, including contrast-enhanced MR venography with a slice thickness of 0.625 mm. We have been using 3-T MRI technology for more than 20 years. We do not routinely employ time-resolved (dynamic) MR venography (4D MRV), as we do not consider it likely to provide clinically or scientifically meaningful additional information in the context of the present study. Consequently, this modality was not included in our imaging protocol. Nevertheless, all other advanced MRI techniques and imaging modalities referenced in your commentary are routinely utilized at our institution. Although 7-T MRI has not yet become widely adopted worldwide due to several technical and practical limitations, we believe that many of these challenges will be addressed over time. For this reason, we have not yet implemented 7-T MRI in our clinical practice. However, similar concerns existed during the early period of 3-T MRI implementation, and these limitations were gradually overcome with technological advancements. We anticipate that 7-T MRI will likewise become more accessible and clinically applicable as the technology continues to mature.

We do not believe that the inclusion of Figs. 1a and 1b provides relevant information regarding the identification or demonstration of the cuneus vein. The venous structure illustrated in Fig. 2, as mentioned in the comment, does not correspond to the cuneus vein described in our manuscript. Rather, the vein demonstrated in that figure appears to represent a tributary of the parieto-occipital vein and should be distinguished from the cuneus vein. The anatomical variation we aimed to describe is precisely the deviation from the commonly observed venous anatomy. Our purpose was to highlight this uncommon venous structure and its potential clinical significance during the posterior interhemispheric approach. As mentioned in our original manuscript, obtaining DSI in every patient undergoing a posterior interhemispheric approach is not feasible in routine clinical practice. Such an approach would also expose patients to additional procedures and unnecessary risks. Therefore, intraoperative identification and characterization of these venous variations remain particularly important for surgical planning and preservation of critical venous structures.

Dr. Benndorf correctly notes that the statement regarding a thinner-than-expected parieto-occipital vein should not be regarded as a validated radiological marker. Our intention was merely to share an intraoperative observation that may warrant future investigation. We agree that this hypothesis requires objective validation in larger radiological studies before any clinical application can be recommended.

Ultimately, the principal message of our study remains surgical rather than radiological. During posterior interhemispheric approaches, surgeons may occasionally encounter a cortical vein extending from the medial occipital region toward the falx cerebri that narrows the operative corridor and requires preservation. Whether future anatomical and imaging studies ultimately refine its nomenclature, redefine its anatomical classification, or demonstrate that it represents a specific variant of an already recognized venous pathway, awareness of its potential presence remains relevant for operative safety. Our intention was to provide insight based on intraoperative observations for more expert radiologists, rather than to present a radiological study. The primary aim of our work is to identify this anatomical variation and to preserve it during surgery. We are pleased that our work has stimulated interest and may pave the way for further research in this area. However, we do not believe that the intraoperative video and imaging data presented in our study require additional external validation, as they directly reflect real surgical observations under operative conditions. We encourage further investigation of this entity by radiologists and anatomists. In this context, well-perfused cadaveric head studies (more than 150 cadaveric head) involving a substantial number of specimens could serve as useful control studies.

Finally, we would like to emphasize that the cuneus vein exists as a distinct anatomical venous structure. Its presence has been demonstrated through direct intraoperative observation, supported by detailed surgical video documentation and anatomical illustrations. Although it may have been underrecognized in previous anatomical and radiological evaluations, this should not be interpreted as evidence of absence. We believe that increased awareness of this venous structure will facilitate its identification in future anatomical, radiological, and clinical studies.
