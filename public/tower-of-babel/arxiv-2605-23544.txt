Explicit Construction of Polytopes whose Ehrhart Polynomials Realize any Given Sign Pattern
Feihu Liu, Sihao Tao, Guoce Xin, [2mm] School of Mathematical Sciences, [-0.8ex] Capital Normal University, Beijing, 100048, P.R. China, Email address: liufeihu7476@163.com, Email address: sihao_tao@cnu.edu.cn, Email address: guoce_xin@163.com

Abstract
In Ehrhart theory, the well-known sign pattern problem asks: given a positive integer d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 and integers 1 ≤ i 1 < ⋯ < i k ≤ d − 2 1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq d-2 , does there exist a d d -dimensional integral polytope 𝒫 \mathcal{P} such that in its Ehrhart polynomial i ⁡ ( 𝒫 , t ) i(\mathcal{P},t) the coefficients of t i 1 , … , t i k t^{i_{1}},\ldots,t^{i_{k}} are negative, while all remaining coefficients are positive? This problem was proposed by Hibi, Higashitani, Tsuchiya, and Yoshida. In this paper, we first construct a class of simplices 𝒮 d ​ ( m ) \mathcal{S}_{d}(m) whose Ehrhart polynomial has leading coefficient m m and all other coefficients fixed positive constants. Then, using the Cartesian product of 𝒮 d ​ ( m ) \mathcal{S}_{d}(m) and the Reeve tetrahedron, we obtain the first complete solution to the sign pattern problem. Finally, while attacking the sign pattern problem, we discovered a fast algorithm for computing the h ∗ h^{*} -polynomial of a class of simplices Δ ⁡ ( 0 , q ) \Delta(0,q) . This algorithm is crucial for constructing the simplices 𝒮 d ​ ( m ) \mathcal{S}_{d}(m) .

Introduction

1.1
Basic concepts

An
integral convex polytope
(also called a
lattice polytope
) is the convex hull of finitely many integer points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For a finite set
\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

\mathcal{P}=\mathrm{conv}\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}v_{i}\mid\lambda_{i}\geq 0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=1\right\}.

Its
dimension

\dim\mathcal{P}
is the dimension of its affine hull. Its
Ehrhart function
, defined as
i(\mathcal{P},t)=|t\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|
for positive integers
t
, counts the number of integer points in the
t
-th dilation
t\mathcal{P}=\{t\alpha\mid\alpha\in\mathcal{P}\}
. We always assume
\mathcal{P}
is full-dimensional (i.e.,
\dim\mathcal{P}=d
).

Ehrhart’s famous theorem
[
18
]
says that
i(\mathcal{P},t)
is a polynomial in
t
of degree
d
, now known as the
Ehrhart polynomial
of
\mathcal{P}
.
It is well-known
[
6
, Corollary 3.20; Theorem 5.6]
that the leading coefficient of
i(\mathcal{P},t)
equals the volume of
\mathcal{P}
, the second highest coefficient of
i(\mathcal{P},t)
equals half of the boundary volume of
\mathcal{P}
, and the constant term is
1
.
Therefore, these three coefficients are always positive.

The remaining coefficients, however, are more intricate and lack a straightforward geometric interpretation
[
38
]
.
We refer to the coefficients of
t,t^{2},\ldots,t^{d-2}
in
i(\mathcal{P},t)
as the
middle Ehrhart coefficients
of
\mathcal{P}
.

The generating function

\mathrm{Ehr}(\mathcal{P},x)=1+\sum_{t\geq 1}i(\mathcal{P},t)x^{t}

is called the
Ehrhart series
of
\mathcal{P}
.
It can be expressed in the form

\mathrm{Ehr}(\mathcal{P},x)=\frac{h_{\mathcal{P}}^{*}(x)}{(1-x)^{d+1}}=\frac{h_{d}^{*}x^{d}+h_{d-1}^{*}x^{d-1}+\cdots+h_{1}^{*}x+h_{0}^{*}}{(1-x)^{d+1}},

where
\dim\mathcal{P}=d
and
h^{*}(x)
is a polynomial in
x
of degree at most
d
(see
[
6
, Chapter 3.5]
).
The polynomial
h_{\mathcal{P}}^{*}(x)
is commonly known as the
h^{*}
-polynomial
of
\mathcal{P}
.

Stanley
[
41
]
(or
[
6
, Theorem 3.12]
) shows that
h_{d}^{*},h_{d-1}^{*},\ldots,h_{0}^{*}
are nonnegative integers.
Furthermore, the polynomial
h^{*}(x)
satisfies
[
6
, Lemma 3.13; Corollary 3.16; Exercise 4.9]

\displaystyle h_{0}^{*}=1,\quad h_{1}^{*}=\#(\mathcal{P}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d})-(d+1),\quad\text{ and }\quad h_{d}^{*}=\#(\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{P})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}),

(1)

where
\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{P})
denotes the interior of
\mathcal{P}
.
Clearly the Ehrhart polynomial is given by

\displaystyle i(\mathcal{P},t)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h^{*}_{i}\binom{t+d-i}{d}.

(2)

An integral convex polytope
\mathcal{P}
is said to be
Ehrhart positive
(or have
Ehrhart positivity
) if all the coefficients of
i(\mathcal{P},t)
are non-negative.
A well-known non-Ehrhart positive example is Reeve’s tetrahedron.

Definition 1.1
.

[
6
, Example 3.22]

Let
\mathcal{T}_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
be the tetrahedron with vertices
(0,0,0)
,
(1,0,0)
,
(0,1,0)
, and
(1,1,m)
, where
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. Its Ehrhart polynomial is given by

\displaystyle i(\mathcal{T}_{m},t)=\frac{m}{6}t^{3}+t^{2}+\frac{12-m}{6}t+1.

(3)

This tetrahedron is known as the
Reeve tetrahedron
.

Conversely, many polytopes have been shown to be non-Ehrhart positive, including order polytopes

.

[

1.2
Main results

To solve this open problem, we need to construct a polytope with any given sign patterns as stated in Theorem
1.3
.
These cases seem hard to attack, but we solved them by adding a new block, called Eulerian simplices of dimension
d\geq 1
,
because their
h^{*}
-polynomials are similar to the Eulerian polynomials
A_{d}(x)
(see Proposition
6.1
).

Definition 1.4
.

For every positive integer
m
(i.e.,
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
),
a parameterized family
\mathcal{R}_{d}(m)
of
d
-dimensional integral simplices in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called an
Eulerian simplex family of dimension
d
if the
h^{*}
-polynomial of
\mathcal{R}_{d}(m)
admits a decomposition of the form

h^{*}_{\mathcal{R}_{d}(m)}(x)=mA_{d}(x)+T(x),

where
T(x)
is a polynomial independent of
m
with
T(1)=0
,
\deg T(x)\leq d
.

As the main tool of this paper, we construct a class of simplices, denoted by
\mathcal{S}_{d}(m)
, which belongs to the Eulerian simplex family.

Theorem 1.5
.

For every positive integer
m
and
d\geq 1
, the following family
\mathcal{S}_{d}(m)
of polytopes fall into the Eulerian simplex family
\mathcal{R}_{d}(m)
of dimension
d
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{d}(m)=\begin{cases}\ell_{m}=[0,m]=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\mid 0\leq\alpha\leq m\}&\text{ if }\quad d=1,\\
\mathrm{conv}\{\mathbf{0}^{d},\mathbf{e}_{1}^{d},\mathbf{e}_{2}^{d},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{d-1}^{d},(q_{1}(d),q_{2}(d),\ldots,q_{d-1}(d),d!\cdot m)\}&\text{ if }\quad d\geq 2,\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{d}
denote the
i
-th unit coordinate vector of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
;
\mathbf{0}^{d}
denote the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; and

q_{i}(d)=\frac{-d!}{i!+(i-1)!}\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq i\leq d-1.

Moreover, we compute the closed formula of the Ehrhart polynomial of
\mathcal{S}_{d}(m)
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
m\geq 1
. For every positive integer
d
, the Ehrhart polynomials of
\mathcal{S}_{d}(m)
are given by

\displaystyle i(\mathcal{S}_{d}(m),t)=mt^{d}+\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\binom{d}{i}t^{i}.

(4)

Observe that the middle Ehrhart coefficients and the second highest coefficient of a polytope are sufficiently small compared to the leading coefficient. This greatly facilitates the construction of polytopes for certain sign patterns.

Our main result is a resolution of Open Problem
1.2
by combining our constructed simplex family
\mathcal{S}_{d}(m)
, the Reeve tetrahedron, and Cartesian products of polytopes.

Theorem 1.7
.

For any dimension
d\geq 3
and every possible sign pattern of the coefficients in the Ehrhart polynomial,
we can construct an integral polytope that realizes it.

The construction of the above Eulerian simplices

is a little unexpected. We begin with a general family of simplices (see Section

\Delta(0,q):=\mathrm{conv}\left\{\mathbf{0}^{d},\mathbf{e}_{1}^{d},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{d-1}^{d},n\mathbf{e}_{d}^{d}+\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}q_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}\right\}.

These simplices can be treated as a generalization of the Reeve tetrahedron. Such simplices have been considered in

]
, where the

We develop a fast algorithm for computing the

-polynomial of

As a

These are simplices whose volume tends to infinity but with constant boundary volume as

increases. See Section

.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section

, we prove Theorem
1.7

,

Section

In Section

In Section

Section

.

Preliminaries

\displaystyle i(\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{Q},t)=i(\mathcal{P},t)\cdot i(\mathcal{Q},t).

2.2
Proof of main result

Given an arbitrary sequence of integers
d_{i}\geq 2
, we construct positive rational parameters
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}
such that the coefficients of the expanded polynomial product strictly follow a prescribed sign pattern as the scaling parameter

Theorem 2.2

For any

The polynomial

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=\epsilon,

(5)

\displaystyle\beta_{i}=1+\epsilon-\alpha_{i},\quad(\text{i.e., }\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}=1+\epsilon),

Lemma 2.3

The sequence

\displaystyle 1+\epsilon-\alpha_{k}=(1+\epsilon-\alpha_{1})\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{d_{j}}\right).

(\beta_{i})
is strictly monotonically decreasing, and the maximum weight for the
i
-th component satisfies

w_{i}(d_{i})=d_{i}\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}=d_{i}\alpha_{i+1}.

We define a decomposition
S^{*}=(\ell_{1}^{*},\dots,\ell_{k}^{*})
for a degree
x
to be
greedy
if there exists an
m\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
such that

\ell_{i}^{*}=d_{i}\quad\text{for}\quad i>m,\quad 0\leq\ell_{m}^{*}<d_{m},\quad\ell_{i}^{*}=0\quad\text{for}\quad i<m,\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{k}\ell_{i}^{*}=x.

Let
S=(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k})
be an arbitrary valid decomposition satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\ell_{i}=x
.
The total weight of a decomposition
S
is given by
W(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}(\ell_{i})
. The global maximum weight for a fixed sum
x
is denoted as
W(x)=\max_{S}W(S)
.

Lemma 2.4
.

For any integer
x
such that
0\leq x\leq D
, the greedy decomposition
S^{*}
realizes the maximum weight, that is,
W(S^{*})=W(x)\geq W(S)
for any valid decomposition
S
.

Proof.

We now define

\displaystyle V=\sum_{i=m+1}^{k}(d_{i}-\ell_{i})\geq 0.

Since both decompositions sum to the same total
x
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=m+1}^{k}d_{i}+\ell_{m}^{*}+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}0=\sum_{i=m+1}^{k}\ell_{i}+\ell_{m}+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\ell_{i}.

Rearranging the terms yields

\displaystyle V=\sum_{i=m+1}^{k}(d_{i}-\ell_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\ell_{i}+(\ell_{m}-\ell_{m}^{*}).

(8)

We consider the three cases as follows.

Case I: (
i>m
)

For each
i>m
, let
\delta_{i}=d_{i}-\ell_{i}\geq 0
.
We have

\displaystyle w_{i}(d_{i})-w_{i}(\ell_{i})=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if}\quad\delta_{i}=0\\
(d_{i}\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i})-\ell_{i}\alpha_{i}=\delta_{i}\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i},&\text{if}\quad\delta_{i}\geq 1.\end{cases}

Since
i\geq m+1
, the monotonic increasing property guarantees
\alpha_{i}\geq\alpha_{m+1}>\alpha_{m}
.
Recall that
\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}=1+\epsilon
for all
i\geq 1
.
Then we obtain

\displaystyle\delta_{i}\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}

\displaystyle=\delta_{i}\alpha_{m}+\delta_{i}(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{m})+\beta_{i}

\displaystyle\geq\delta_{i}\alpha_{m}+1\cdot(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{m})+\beta_{i}

\displaystyle=\delta_{i}\alpha_{m}+1+\epsilon-\alpha_{m}=\delta_{i}\alpha_{m}+\beta_{m}.

Thus, for any
\delta_{i}\geq 0
, we have
w_{i}(d_{i})-w_{i}(\ell_{i})\geq\delta_{i}\alpha_{m}
. Furthermore, if
V>0
, there exists at least one
j>m
such that
\delta_{j}\geq 1
, which strictly contributes an additional
\beta_{m}
.
We now employ the characteristic function
\chi
:
\chi(\text{true})=1
and
\chi(\text{false})=0
.
Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i=m+1}^{k}(w_{i}(d_{i})-w_{i}(\ell_{i}))\geq V\alpha_{m}+\chi(V>0)\beta_{m}.

(9)

Case II: (
i<m
)

In this case,
\ell_{i}^{*}=0
, so we seek to upper bound
w_{i}(\ell_{i})
.
If
\ell_{i}<d_{i}
, then
w_{i}(\ell_{i})=\ell_{i}\alpha_{i}
. Since
i\leq m-1
, we have
\alpha_{i}\leq\alpha_{m-1}<\alpha_{m}
. Then we have
w_{i}(\ell_{i})\leq\ell_{i}\alpha_{m}
.
If
\ell_{i}=d_{i}
, then the weight is
w_{i}(d_{i})=d_{i}\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}=d_{i}\alpha_{i+1}
. Because
i<m
(i.e.,
i+1\leq m
), it follows that
\alpha_{i+1}\leq\alpha_{m}
. Hence,
w_{i}(d_{i})\leq d_{i}\alpha_{m}
.
Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}(w_{i}(\ell_{i}^{*})-w_{i}(\ell_{i}))=-\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}w_{i}(\ell_{i})\geq-\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\ell_{i}\alpha_{m}.

(10)

Case III: (
i=m
)
The condition
\ell_{m}^{*}<d_{m}
imply
w_{m}(\ell_{m}^{*})=\ell_{m}^{*}\alpha_{m}
.
For the arbitrary
\ell_{m}
, we have

w_{m}(\ell_{m})=\ell_{m}\alpha_{m}+\chi(\ell_{m}=d_{m})\beta_{m}.

Therefore, we get

\displaystyle w_{m}(\ell_{m}^{*})-w_{m}(\ell_{m})=(\ell_{m}^{*}-\ell_{m})\alpha_{m}-\chi(\ell_{m}=d_{m})\beta_{m}.

(11)

Equations (
9
), (
10
), and (
11
) establish a lower bound as follow

\displaystyle W(S^{*})-W(S)

\displaystyle\geq(V\alpha_{m}+\chi(V>0)\beta_{m})+((\ell_{m}^{*}-\ell_{m})\alpha_{m}-\chi(\ell_{m}=d_{m})\beta_{m})-\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\ell_{i}\alpha_{m}

\displaystyle=\left(V-(\ell_{m}-\ell_{m}^{*})-\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\ell_{i}\right)\alpha_{m}+(\chi(V>0)-\chi(\ell_{m}=d_{m}))\beta_{m}.

By Equation (
8
), the inequality become

\displaystyle W(S^{*})-W(S)\geq(\chi(V>0)-\chi(\ell_{m}=d_{m}))\beta_{m}.

It remains to prove that
\chi(V>0)-\chi(\ell_{m}=d_{m})\geq 0
. It suffices to show the case
\chi(\ell_{m}=d_{m})=1
and
\chi(V>0)=0
cannot happen.
If not, then
V=0
and
\ell_{m}=d_{m}
. Substituting
\ell_{m}=d_{m}
into Equation (
8
) yields the contradiction:

V=\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\ell_{i}+(d_{m}-\ell_{m}^{*})>0.

Furthermore, we obtain
W(S^{*})\geq W(S)
.
This completes the proof that the greedy strategy guarantees the maximum weight, i.e., Lemma
2.4
.
∎

Define the marginal increment
\Delta(x)=W(x)-W(x-1)
.

Lemma 2.5
.

For
x=E_{j}
with
1\leq j\leq k
, we have
\Delta(E_{j})=1+\epsilon
.
For
E_{j+1}<x<E_{j}
, we have
\Delta(x)=\alpha_{j}\leq\alpha_{k}<1
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.4
, when
x=E_{j}
, we have
\Delta(E_{j})=\alpha_{j}+\beta_{j}=1+\epsilon
.
For
E_{j+1}<x<E_{j}
, the greedy decomposition has
0<\ell_{j}^{*}<d_{j}
.
Decreasing the degree by 1 removes one unit from
\ell_{j}^{*}
, reducing the weight strictly by
\alpha_{j}
. Lemma
2.3
guarantees
\alpha_{j}\leq\alpha_{k}<1
.
∎

We define the weights combining
P_{0}(t)
and
\prod P_{i}(t)
as

, where

.

as

as

.

according to the following four cases.

.

we have

as

.

Case 2:

•

with

. The coefficient

.

•

When

is always

.

For

, we must prove

strictly dominates all legal positive components

,
W_{2}(h)
,

.

Case 3:

•

\ell_{0}=1

3
Fundamental parallelepiped

In solving the above sign pattern problem, an important tool is the discovery of the simplex family
\mathcal{S}_{d}(m)
. Before describing this discovery, we first introduce some necessary concepts concerning simplices and polytopes.

A
d
-dimensional convex polytope with exactly
d+1
vertices is called a
d
-dimensional
simplex
.
For example, the
3
-dimensional simplices are the tetrahedra.
The
cone over
\mathcal{P}
is defined as

\mathrm{cone}(\mathcal{P})=\{(\lambda,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}:x\in\lambda\mathcal{P}\}.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a
d
-dimensional integral simplex with vertices
v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}
. The
fundamental parallelepiped
of
\mathrm{cone}(\mathcal{P})
is

\Pi(\mathrm{cone}(\mathcal{P})):=\{\lambda_{0}(1,v_{0})+\lambda_{1}(1,v_{1})+\cdots+\lambda_{d}(1,v_{d}):\lambda_{i}\in[0,1)\},

and its Ehrhart series
[
6
, Lemma 3.10]
is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ehr}(\mathcal{P},x)=\frac{\sum_{\omega\in\Pi(\mathrm{cone}(\mathcal{P}))\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}}x^{\omega_{0}}}{(1-x)^{d+1}},

(12)

where
\omega_{0}
is the first coordinate of
\omega=(\omega_{0},\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{d})
.

The concept of a fundamental parallelepiped can be viewed within a broader algebraic framework.
For a nonsingular matrix
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{d\times d}
, the
lattice generated by
A
is defined as

\mathcal{L}(A)=\{Ax:x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
.
The
fundamental parallelepiped
of the lattice
\mathcal{L}(A)
is defined as

\Pi(A)=\{A\xi:\xi\in[0,1)^{d}\}.

Proposition 3.1
.

[
3
, Theorem 2.5]

Let
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{d\times d}
be a nonsingular matrix and
\mathcal{L}(A)
the lattice generated by
A
. Then we have

1.

The quotient group
\mathbb{Z}^{d}/\mathcal{L}(A)
is finite.

2.

The number of elements in the quotient group
\mathbb{Z}^{d}/\mathcal{L}(A)
equals
|\det(A)|
.

3.

The set
\Pi(A)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
forms a complete set of representatives for the quotient group
\mathbb{Z}^{d}/\mathcal{L}(A)
.

The following lemma appears in
[
2
]
, but without a detailed proof. Here we provide one.

Lemma 3.2
.

[
2
]

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{Z}^{d\times d}
be two nonsingular matrices such that
B=UAV
for some unimodular matrices
U,V\in\mathbb{Z}^{d\times d}
.
Let

\displaystyle\Pi(A)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=\{A\xi\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\xi\in[0,1)^{d}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\Pi(B)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=\{B\xi\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\xi\in[0,1)^{d}\}.

Then

\displaystyle\Pi(A)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=\{A(VB^{-1}b-\lfloor VB^{-1}b\rfloor):b\in\Pi(B)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}.

Proof.

Let
G_{1}=\mathbb{Z}^{d}/\mathcal{L}(B)
and
G_{2}=\mathbb{Z}^{d}/\mathcal{L}(A)
. By Proposition
3.1
,
\Pi(B)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
\Pi(A)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
are complete sets of representatives for
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, respectively. Moreover,
|G_{1}|=|\det(B)|=|\det(A)|=|G_{2}|
.

Define a map
\phi:G_{1}\to G_{2}
by

\phi(b+\mathcal{L}(B))=AVB^{-1}b+\mathcal{L}(A).

1.

\phi
is a well-defined group homomorphism.
If
b_{1}-b_{2}\in\mathcal{L}(B)
, then
b_{1}-b_{2}=Bz
for some
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Thus

AVB^{-1}(b_{1}-b_{2})=AVz\in\mathcal{L}(A),

so
\phi(b_{1}+\mathcal{L}(B))=\phi(b_{2}+\mathcal{L}(B))
. The homomorphism property is immediate from linearity.

2.

\phi
is a group isomorphism.
Define
\psi:G_{2}\to G_{1}
by

\psi(a+\mathcal{L}(A))=BV^{-1}A^{-1}a+\mathcal{L}(B).

It is straightforward to verify that
\psi\circ\phi=\text{id}_{G_{1}}
and
\phi\circ\psi=\text{id}_{G_{2}}
.

Finally, for any
b\in\Pi(B)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, write

VB^{-1}b=\left\lfloor VB^{-1}b\right\rfloor+\xi,\quad\xi\in[0,1)^{d}.

Then

\phi(b+\mathcal{L}(B))=A\xi+\mathcal{L}(A),

which means
A\xi\in\Pi(A)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. This completes the proof.
∎

4
Simplex family:
\Delta(0,q)

4.1
Formula for the
h^{*}
-Polynomials

We now introduce a special class of simplices, denoted by
\Delta(0,q)
, which exhibits numerous elegant properties. Most notably, this class of simplices is central to the study of Ehrhart sign pattern problems.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
q=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{d-1},n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
n\neq 0
, we define the simplex

\displaystyle\Delta(0,q):=\mathrm{conv}\left\{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{d-1},n\mathbf{e}_{d}+\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}q_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}\right\},

(13)

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
denote the
i
-th unit coordinate vector of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
1\leq i\leq d
; and
\mathbf{0}
denote the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Similar families of simplices have been studied in
[
1
]
and
[
11
]
.
However, the simplices here are slightly different from theirs.

Theorem 4.2
.

The
h^{*}
-polynomial of
\Delta(0,q)
is given by

\displaystyle h_{\Delta(0,q)}^{*}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}x^{\left\lceil{\frac{q_{1}j}{n}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil{\frac{q_{d}j}{n}}\right\rceil}\quad\text{ with }\quad q_{d}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}q_{i}.

Proof.

We construct a nonsingular matrix
A
and a unimodular matrix
V
as follows:

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1&1\\
0&1&\cdots&0&q_{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1&q_{d-1}\\
0&0&\cdots&0&n\end{pmatrix},\quad V=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&\cdots&-1&-q_{d}\\
0&1&\cdots&0&-q_{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1&-q_{d-1}\\
0&0&\cdots&0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Direct computation yields
AV=\mathrm{diag}\{1,\ldots,1,n\}
. By Lemma
3.2
(here,
B=AV
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Pi(\mathrm{cone}(\Delta(0,q)))\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}=\Pi(A)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{A(\xi_{j}-\lfloor\xi_{j}\rfloor):\xi_{j}=V\mathrm{diag}\left\{0,\ldots,0,\frac{j}{n}\right\}=\frac{j}{n}\begin{pmatrix}-q_{d}\\
-q_{1}\\
\vdots\\
-q_{d-1}\\
1\end{pmatrix},\ j=0,\ldots,n-1\right\}.

We observe that the first coordinates of the vectors
(A\xi_{j})
and
(-A\lfloor\xi_{j}\rfloor)
, denoted by
(A\xi_{j})_{0}
and
(-A\lfloor\xi_{j}\rfloor)_{0}
respectively, satisfy
(A\xi_{j})_{0}=0
and

\displaystyle(-A\lfloor\xi_{j}\rfloor)_{0}

\displaystyle=-\left(\left\lfloor\frac{-q_{d}j}{n}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{-q_{1}j}{n}\right\rfloor+\cdots+\left\lfloor\frac{-q_{d-1}j}{n}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{j}{n}\right\rfloor\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(\left\lfloor\frac{j}{n}\right\rfloor+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\lfloor\frac{-q_{i}j}{n}\right\rfloor\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\lceil\frac{q_{i}j}{n}\right\rceil.

The conclusion now follows from Equation (
12
).
∎

We now consider the
h^{*}
-polynomials for three special cases of
\Delta(0,q)
.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
a,s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Let
d=2k-1
for
k\geq 2
, and consider the
d
-dimensional simplex defined as

\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{odd}}_{s,k,a}:=\mathrm{conv}\{0,\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{d-1},(\underbrace{a,\ldots,a}_{k-1},\underbrace{-a,\ldots,-a}_{k-1},s+1)\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},

The
h^{*}
-polynomial of this simplex is given by

\displaystyle h^{*}_{\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{odd}}_{s,k,a}}(x)=(s+1-b)x^{k}+(b-1)x+1,\quad\text{where}\quad b=\gcd(a,s+1).

Proof.

By Theorem
4.2
, we have

\displaystyle h^{*}_{\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{odd}}_{s,k,a}}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{s}x^{\left\lceil{\frac{ai}{s+1}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil{\frac{ai}{s+1}}\right\rceil+\left\lceil{\frac{-ai}{s+1}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil{\frac{-ai}{s+1}}\right\rceil+\left\lceil{\frac{(1-(k-1)a+(k-1)a)i}{s+1}}\right\rceil}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{i=1}^{s}{x^{(k-1)\left(\left\lceil{\frac{ai}{s+1}}\right\rceil-\left\lfloor{\frac{ai}{s+1}}\right\rfloor\right)}x^{\left\lceil{\frac{i}{s+1}}\right\rceil}}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{i=1\atop(s+1)\mid ai}^{s}x^{(k-1)\left(\left\lceil{\frac{ai}{s+1}}\right\rceil-\left\lfloor{\frac{ai}{s+1}}\right\rfloor\right)}\cdot x+\sum_{i=1\atop(s+1)\nmid ai}^{s}x^{(k-1)\left(\left\lceil{\frac{ai}{s+1}}\right\rceil-\left\lfloor{\frac{ai}{s+1}}\right\rfloor\right)}\cdot x.

Let
a=bp_{1}
and
s+1=bp_{2}
, where
\gcd(p_{1},p_{2})=1
. Then
s+1\mid ai
if and only if
p_{2}\mid i
. This implies that the possible values for
i
are exactly
p_{2},2p_{2},\dots,(b-1)p_{2}
.
we obtain

\displaystyle h^{*}_{\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{odd}}_{s,k,a}}(x)=1+(b-1)x+(s+1-b)x^{k}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
a,s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Let

for

, and consider the

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{even}}_{s,k,a}:=\mathrm{conv}\{0,\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{d-1},(1,\underbrace{a,\ldots,a}_{k-1},\underbrace{-a,\ldots,-a}_{k-1},s+1)\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle h^{*}_{\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{even}}_{s,k,a}}(x)=h^{*}_{\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{odd}}_{s,k,a}}(x).

, we have

\displaystyle h^{*}_{\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{even}}_{s,k,a}}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{s}x^{\left\lceil{\frac{i}{s+1}}\right\rceil+\left\lceil{\frac{ai}{s+1}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil{\frac{ai}{s+1}}\right\rceil+\left\lceil{\frac{-ai}{s+1}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil{\frac{-ai}{s+1}}\right\rceil+\left\lceil{\frac{(1-1-(k-1)a+(k-1)a)i}{s+1}}\right\rceil}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{i=1}^{s}{x^{(k-1)\left(\left\lceil{\frac{ai}{s+1}}\right\rceil-\left\lfloor{\frac{ai}{s+1}}\right\rfloor\right)}x^{\left\lceil{\frac{i}{s+1}}\right\rceil}}

This completes the proof.

Using an analogous argument, we derive the following result.

Corollary 4.5
.

(Extended Reeve simplices)
Let
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Let
d\geq 3
be an integer, and consider the
d
-dimensional simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{s,d}:=\mathrm{conv}\{0,\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{d-1},\omega\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d},

where

\displaystyle\omega=\begin{cases}(\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{k-1},\underbrace{s,\ldots,s}_{k-1},s+1),&\text{if $d=2k-1$},\\
(\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{k},\underbrace{s,\ldots,s}_{k-1},s+1),&\text{if $d=2k$}.\end{cases}

Then we have

h^{*}_{\mathcal{M}_{s,d}}(x)=sx^{k}+1.

Proof.

When
d
is odd, by Theorem
4.2
, we have

\displaystyle h^{*}_{\mathcal{M}_{s,d}}(x)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{i=1}^{s}x^{(k-1)\left(\left\lceil{\frac{i}{s+1}}\right\rceil+\left\lceil{\frac{si}{s+1}}\right\rceil\right)}x^{\left\lceil{\frac{(1-(k-1)-(k-1)s)i}{s+1}}\right\rceil}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{i=1}^{s}x^{k-1}x^{(k-1)\left\lceil{\frac{(s+1)i-i}{s+1}}\right\rceil}x^{\left\lceil{\frac{(-k+1)(s+1)i+i}{s+1}}\right\rceil}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{i=1}^{s}x^{k-1}x^{(k-1)i}x^{(-k+1)i+1}

\displaystyle=1+sx^{k}.

For the case when
d
is even, a similar argument applies.
This completes the proof.
∎

When
d
is odd, the result of Corollary
4.5
reduces to
[
24
, Example 2.5]
.
Such simplices
\mathcal{M}_{s,d}
are referred to as
generalized Reeve simplices
and denoted by
\mathcal{R}_{s,k}
.
When
d=3
, these coincide with the Reeve tetrahedra.
The
h^{*}
-polynomial of
\mathcal{R}_{s,k}
in
[
24
, Example 2.5]
was derived using a result from
[
4
]
.

An
empty simplex
is a simplex which contains no lattice point except for its vertices.
A
d
-dimensional simplex having
h^{*}
-polynomial
h_{d}x^{d}+\cdots+h_{1}x+h_{0}
is empty if and only if
h_{1}=h_{d}=0
.
Batyrev and Hofscheier
[
4
]
proved that for an odd-dimensional empty simplex of dimension
d
, the
h^{*}
-polynomials look like
1+qx^{\frac{d+1}{2}}
.
Both simplices
\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{even}}_{s,k,a}
and
\mathcal{M}_{s,d}
are empty.

4.2
Fast algorithm for the
h^{*}
-polynomials of
\Delta(0,q)

Given
q=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{d-1},n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
n\neq 0
, by Theorem
4.2
, the
h^{*}
-polynomial of
\Delta(0,q)
is

\displaystyle h_{\Delta(0,q)}^{*}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}x^{\left\lceil{\frac{q_{1}j}{n}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil{\frac{q_{d}j}{n}}\right\rceil},\quad\text{ where }\quad q_{d}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}q_{i}.

This formula is hard to use when
n
is large.

For further exploration, we develop a fast algorithm for computing the
h^{*}
-polynomial of
\Delta(0,q)
.
Our algorithm only depend on the numerical values of
q_{1},\dots,q_{d-1}
, and is independent of
n
(no less than
|q_{i}|
).

Let us introduce several functions.
Let

\displaystyle a_{i}=\frac{n}{q_{i}}\in\mathbb{Q}\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi_{a_{i}}(j)=\left\lceil\frac{j}{a_{i}}\right\rceil\in\mathbb{Z}\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq i\leq d.

Note that by assumption,
|a_{i}|\geq 1
. It is evident that for a fixed
a_{i}
,
\varphi_{a_{i}}(j)
is a piecewise constant function in
j\in\mathbb{R}
.
Its difference function is defined by

\displaystyle\nabla\varphi_{a_{i}}(j)=\varphi_{a_{i}}(j)-\varphi_{a_{i}}(j-1)=\left\lceil\frac{j}{a_{i}}\right\rceil-\left\lceil\frac{j-1}{a_{i}}\right\rceil\in\mathbb{Z}\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq i\leq d.

Observe that the difference function is almost
0
everywhere. In particular, we have

Lemma 4.6
.

For
1\leq i\leq d
and
m\geq 1
, let

\displaystyle c_{i,m}=\begin{cases}\lfloor(m-1)a_{i}+1\rfloor&\text{\emph{if}}\quad a_{i}\geq 1;\\
\lceil-ma_{i}\rceil&\text{\emph{if}}\quad a_{i}\leq-1.\end{cases}

For
a_{i}\geq 1
, we have
\nabla\varphi_{a_{i}}(c_{i,m})=1
for
m\geq 1
, and
\nabla\varphi_{a_{i}}(j)=0
for all positive integers
j\neq c_{i,m}
.
For
a_{i}\leq-1
, we have
\nabla\varphi_{a_{i}}(c_{i,m})=-1
for
m\geq 1
, and
\nabla\varphi_{a_{i}}(j)=0
for all positive integers
j\neq c_{i,m}
.

Proof.

This conclusion follows from the fact that:
when
a_{i}\geq 1
and
j
is a positive real number, the function
\nabla\varphi_{a_{i}}(j)
takes value
1
on the non-overlapping intervals
((m-1)a_{i},(m-1)a_{i}+1]
and
0
elsewhere;
when
a_{i}\leq-1
and
j
is a positive real number, the function
\nabla\varphi_{a_{i}}(j)
takes value
-1
on the non-overlapping intervals
[-ma_{i},-ma_{i}+1)
and
0
elsewhere. The proof is completed by taking
j
to be a positive integer.
∎

By Lemma
4.6
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\nabla\varphi_{a_{i}}(j)x^{j}=\begin{cases}x^{c_{i,1}}+x^{c_{i,2}}+\cdots+x^{c_{i,q_{i}-1}}&\text{if}\quad a_{i}=1;\\
x^{c_{i,1}}+x^{c_{i,2}}+\cdots+x^{c_{i,q_{i}}}&\text{if}\quad a_{i}>1;\\
-x^{c_{i,1}}-x^{c_{i,2}}-\cdots-x^{c_{i,-q_{i}-1}}&\text{if}\quad a_{i}\leq-1.\end{cases}

(14)

Let

\Phi(j)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varphi_{a_{i}}(j),\quad j\in\mathbb{N}.

Note that
\Phi(0)=0
.
We consider the following two generating functions:

\displaystyle F(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\nabla\varphi_{a_{i}}(j)x^{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\nabla\varphi_{a_{i}}(j)x^{j}\quad\text{and}\quad G(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\Phi(j)x^{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varphi_{a_{i}}(j)x^{j}.

Lemma 4.7
.

The polynomial
G(x)
can be reconstructed from the polynomial
F(x)
. In other words,
G(x)
is determined uniquely by the polynomial
F(x)
.

Proof.

By
\Phi(0)=0
, we know that the constant term of
G(x)
is
0
.
We only need to obtain the coefficient of
x^{j}
(
1\leq j\leq n-1
) in
G(x)
, i.e.,
\Phi(j)
.
Let
[x^{j}]F(x)
denote the coefficient of
x^{j}
in the polynomial
F(x)
.
According to

\displaystyle\Phi(j)-\Phi(j-1)

\displaystyle=[x^{j}]F(x),

\displaystyle\Phi(j-1)-\Phi(j-2)

\displaystyle=[x^{j-1}]F(x),

\displaystyle\cdots

\displaystyle\Phi(1)-\Phi(0)

\displaystyle=[x^{1}]F(x),

we obtain
\Phi(j)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}[x^{\ell}]F(x)
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

The polynomial
h_{\Delta(0,q)}^{*}(x)
can be reconstructed from the polynomial
G(x)
. In other words,
h_{\Delta(0,q)}^{*}(x)
is determined uniquely by the polynomial
G(x)
.

Proof.

It is clear that

\displaystyle h_{\Delta(0,q)}^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}x^{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varphi_{a_{i}}(j)}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{d}\left|\left\{j:\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varphi_{a_{i}}(j)=\ell,\quad 0\leq j\leq n-1\right\}\right|x^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{d}\left|\left\{j:\Phi(j)=\ell,\quad 0\leq j\leq n-1\right\}\right|x^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{d}\left|\left\{j:[x^{j}]G(x)=\ell,\quad 0\leq j\leq n-1\right\}\right|x^{\ell}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Example 4.9
.

. Thus we get
q_{7}=1-(1+5+6+8-3-7)=-9
.

\displaystyle G(x)

\displaystyle=0x^{0}+4x^{1}+4x^{2}+3x^{3}+4x^{4}+4x^{5}+4x^{6}+3x^{7}+4x^{8}+3x^{9}+3x^{10}+5x^{11}

However, if we explicitly construct
G(x)
, we still have
n
terms to deal with. Our point is that
we can construct the

-polynomial directly from

, which has at most

terms.

Indeed, if
F(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{s-1}c_{j}x^{i_{j}}
, then

can be computed by the following facts:

, and the coefficient remains
0
until
[x^{i_{1}}]G(x)=c_{1}

then the coefficients remains

until
[x^{i_{2}}]G(x)=c_{1}+c_{2}

\displaystyle h_{\Delta(0,q)}^{*}(x)=(i_{1}-i_{0})x^{0}+(i_{2}-i_{1})x^{c_{1}}+\cdots+(i_{s}-i_{s-1})x^{c_{1}+\cdots+c_{s-1}},

(15)

where we treat
i_{0}=0,\ i_{s}=n
.

q=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{d-1},n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
q_{d}=1-(q_{1}+q_{2}+\cdots+q_{d-1})
and

.

Output:

The
h^{*}
-polynomial of
\Delta(0,q)
.

1

Computing
a_{i}=\frac{n}{q_{i}}
for
1\leq i\leq d
.

2

Computing the polynomial
F(x)
by Equation (

).

3

Return the
h^{*}
-polynomial of
\Delta(0,q)
by Equation

.

4

.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
q=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{d-1},n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with

and

.

-polynomial of
\Delta(0,q)

with

It follows from Equation (

), together with Lemmas
4.7

, that Algorithm

correctly computes the

, is bounded by

.

5
Simplex family:
\Delta(0,q^{(m)})

We now introduce a family of parametric
\Delta(0,q)
-simplices, namely
\Delta(0,q^{(m)})
. This family has nicer
h^{*}
-polynomials.

5.1
Characteristic polynomials

Consider
q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{d-1},n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, where
n
is a positive integer. We further require

q_{i}\mid n
for all
i=1,\dots,d
, with the convention
q_{d}=1-\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}q_{j}
.
For a positive integer
m
, define
q^{(m)}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{d-1},mn)
. We study the family of
\Delta(0,q^{(m)})
-simplices.
We will often use the notation
a^{+}=\max(0,a)
for any integer
a
.

Theorem 5.1
.

There exist unique polynomials
L_{1}(x)
and
L_{2}(x)
such that

\displaystyle h^{*}_{\Delta(0,q^{(m)})}(x)=mx\cdot L_{1}(x)+L_{2}(x),

where

\displaystyle L_{1}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}x^{\left\lceil{\frac{q_{1}j+q_{1}^{+}}{n}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil{\frac{q_{d}j+q_{d}^{+}}{n}}\right\rceil-1}

and

\displaystyle L_{2}(x)=h^{*}_{\Delta(0,q)}(x)-\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}x^{\left\lceil{\frac{q_{1}j+q_{1}^{+}}{n}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil{\frac{q_{d}j+q_{d}^{+}}{n}}\right\rceil}.

Proof.

Since
q_{k}\mid n
, let
a_{k}=n/q_{k}
(which is an integer) with
1\leq k\leq d
. By Theorem
4.2
, we have

\displaystyle h^{*}_{\Delta(0,q^{(m)})}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{mn-1}x^{\left\lceil{\frac{q_{1}i}{mn}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil{\frac{q_{d}i}{mn}}\right\rceil}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\sum_{i=mj+1}^{m(j+1)-1}x^{\left\lceil{\frac{q_{1}i}{mn}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil{\frac{q_{d}i}{mn}}\right\rceil}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}x^{\left\lceil{\frac{q_{1}mj}{mn}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil{\frac{q_{d}mj}{mn}}\right\rceil}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\sum_{i=mj+1}^{mj+m-1}x^{\left\lceil{\frac{i}{ma_{1}}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil{\frac{i}{ma_{d}}}\right\rceil}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}x^{\left\lceil{\frac{q_{1}j}{n}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil{\frac{q_{d}j}{n}}\right\rceil}.

It is clear that for every
1\leq k\leq d
and
0\leq j\leq n-1
, we get

x^{\left\lceil{\frac{mj+1}{ma_{k}}}\right\rceil}=x^{\left\lceil{\frac{mj+2}{ma_{k}}}\right\rceil}=\cdots=x^{\left\lceil{\frac{mj+m-1}{ma_{k}}}\right\rceil}.

Moreover, when
a_{k}>0
, (now
q_{k}^{+}=q_{k}
), we have

x^{\left\lceil{\frac{mj+m-1}{ma_{k}}}\right\rceil}=x^{\left\lceil{\frac{mj+m}{ma_{k}}}\right\rceil}=x^{\left\lceil{\frac{q_{k}mj+mq_{k}^{+}}{mn}}\right\rceil},

while for
a_{k}<0
, (now
q_{k}^{+}=0
), we have

x^{\left\lceil{\frac{mj+1}{ma_{k}}}\right\rceil}=x^{\left\lceil{\frac{mj}{ma_{k}}}\right\rceil}=x^{\left\lceil{\frac{q_{k}mj+mq_{k}^{+}}{mn}}\right\rceil}.

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle h^{*}_{\Delta(0,q^{(m)})}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(m-1)\left(x^{\left\lceil{\frac{q_{1}mj+mq_{1}^{+}}{mn}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil{\frac{q_{d}mj+mq_{d}^{+}}{mn}}\right\rceil}\right)+h^{*}_{\Delta(0,q)}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}x^{\left\lceil{\frac{q_{1}j+q_{1}^{+}}{n}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil{\frac{q_{d}j+q_{d}^{+}}{n}}\right\rceil}+\left(h^{*}_{\Delta(0,q)}(x)-\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}x^{\left\lceil{\frac{q_{1}j+q_{1}^{+}}{n}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil{\frac{q_{d}j+q_{d}^{+}}{n}}\right\rceil}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle mx\cdot L_{1}(x)+L_{2}(x).

Since
m
is arbitrary and
h^{*}_{\Delta(0,q^{(m)})}(x)
is a polynomial with constant term
1
, it follows that
L_{1}(x)
and
L_{2}(x)
are uniquely determined polynomials.
∎

We call
L_{1}(x)
and
L_{2}(x)
the
characteristic polynomials
of the
\Delta(0,q^{(m)})
simplex family.
An efficient algorithm similar to Algorithm
1
also exists for computing the polynomial
L_{1}(x)
. It can be obtained through a minor adaptation of Algorithm
1
, the details of which we omit here. We have implemented this algorithm in
Maple
, which is available at the same address:

https://github.com/TygerLiu/TygerLiu.github.io/tree/main/Procedure/Sign-pattern-problem
.

Let
L(x)=xL_{1}(x)
. We now provide an alternative proof that
L(x)
is a polynomial of degree at most
d
and at least
1
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
n
be a positive integer.
For any given vector
q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{d-1},n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, assume that
q_{i}\mid n
for every
i=1,\dots,d
,
where we set

q_{d}=1-\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}q_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad q_{d+1}=-1.

Then

L(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}x^{\left\lceil{\frac{q_{1}j+q_{1}^{+}}{n}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil{\frac{q_{d}j+q_{d}^{+}}{n}}\right\rceil}

is a polynomial of degree at most
d
and at least
1
.

Proof.

Since
q_{d+1}=-1
, we have
q_{1}+\cdots+q_{d}+q_{d+1}=0
and

\left\lceil\frac{q_{d+1}j+q_{d+1}^{+}}{n}\right\rceil=0\quad\text{for all }0\leq j\leq n-1.

Let
\{u_{1},u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}
and
\{-v_{k+1},\ldots,-v_{d+1}\}
be the sets of positive and negative integers in
\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{d},q_{d+1}\}
, respectively.
Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\lceil{\frac{q_{i}j+q_{i}^{+}}{n}}\right\rceil=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left\lceil{\frac{u_{i}(j+1)}{n}}\right\rceil+\sum_{i=k+1}^{d+1}\left\lceil{\frac{-v_{i}j}{n}}\right\rceil\quad\text{for all }\quad 0\leq j\leq n-1.

For any
a>0
and
j\geq 0
, it is known that the floor function satisfies

\left\lceil\frac{j+1}{a}\right\rceil=1+\left\lfloor\frac{j}{a}\right\rfloor
.
By
q_{i}\mid n
for all
i
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left\lceil{\frac{u_{i}(j+1)}{n}}\right\rceil+\sum_{i=k+1}^{d+1}\left\lceil{\frac{-v_{i}j}{n}}\right\rceil=k+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left\lfloor{\frac{u_{i}j}{n}}\right\rfloor-\sum_{i=k+1}^{d+1}\left\lfloor{\frac{v_{i}j}{n}}\right\rfloor\quad\text{for all }\quad 0\leq j\leq n-1.

For any
a\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\{a\}=a-\lfloor a\rfloor
.
Since
u_{1}+\cdots+u_{k}-v_{k+1}-\cdots-v_{d+1}=0
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left\lceil{\frac{u_{i}(j+1)}{n}}\right\rceil+\sum_{i=k+1}^{d+1}\left\lceil{\frac{-v_{i}j}{n}}\right\rceil

\displaystyle=k+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{u_{i}j}{n}-\left\{\frac{u_{i}j}{n}\right\}\right)-\sum_{i=k+1}^{d+1}\left(\frac{v_{i}j}{n}-\left\{\frac{v_{i}j}{n}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\left\{\frac{u_{i}j}{n}\right\}\right)+\sum_{i=k+1}^{d+1}\left\{\frac{v_{i}j}{n}\right\}.

According to

0<1-\left\{\frac{u_{i}j}{n}\right\}\leq 1,\quad 0\leq\left\{\frac{v_{i}j}{n}\right\}<1,\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}-\sum_{i=k+1}^{d+1}v_{i}=0,

for
0\leq j\leq n-1
, we obtain

\displaystyle 1\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\left\{\frac{u_{i}j}{n}\right\}\right)+\sum_{i=k+1}^{d+1}\left\{\frac{v_{i}j}{n}\right\}<k+(d+1-k-1+1)=d+1.

This completes the proof.
∎

5.2
Associated properties

Follow the notation in the previous subsection.

Proposition 5.3
.

The polynomials
L_{1}(x)
and
L_{2}(x)
satisfy

\displaystyle L_{1}(1)=n,\quad L_{2}(0)=1,\quad\text{and}\quad L_{2}(1)=0.

Proof.

By Equation (
1
), we have
h^{*}_{\Delta(0,q^{(m)})}(0)=1
, this imples
L_{2}(0)=1
.
By Equation (
12
) and
[
6
, Lemma 9.2]
, we know that

h^{*}_{\Delta(0,q^{(m)})}(1)=\#(\Pi(\mathrm{cone}(\Delta(0,q^{(m)})))\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d+1})=mn.

Since
h^{*}_{\Delta(0,q^{(m)})}(1)=m\cdot L_{1}(1)+L_{2}(1)=mn
for any
m
, we have
L_{1}(1)=n
and
L_{2}(1)=0
.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

The characteristic polynomial
L_{1}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}c_{i}x^{i}
is a symmetric polynomial, i.e.,
c_{i}=c_{d-1-i}
for all
i=0,\ldots,d-1
.

Proof.

Let
L(x)=xL_{1}(x)
. To prove that the polynomial
L_{1}(x)
is symmetric, we need to show that
L(x)=x^{d+1}L(1/x)
.
Recall that

\displaystyle L(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}x^{A(j)},\quad\text{where}\quad A(j)=\left\lceil{\frac{q_{1}j+q_{1}^{+}}{n}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil{\frac{q_{d}j+q_{d}^{+}}{n}}\right\rceil.

Since
q_{i}\mid n
for all
i
and
q_{d}=1-(q_{1}+\cdots+q_{d-1})
, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}=1
.
Let
\{q_{k_{1}},\ldots,q_{k_{s}}\}
and
\{q_{k_{s+1}},\ldots,q_{k_{d}}\}
be the sets of positive and negative integers in
\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{d}\}
, respectively.
Then we have

\displaystyle A(n-1-j)

\displaystyle=\left\lceil{\frac{q_{1}(n-1-j)+q_{1}^{+}}{n}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil{\frac{q_{d}(n-1-j)+q_{d}^{+}}{n}}\right\rceil

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left\lceil-\frac{q_{k_{i}}j}{n}\right\rceil+\sum_{i=s+1}^{d}\left\lceil-\frac{q_{k_{i}}(j+1)}{n}\right\rceil.

Using the identity
\lceil-r\rceil=-\lfloor r\rfloor
and the fact that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}=1
, we obtain

A(n-1-j)=1-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left\lfloor\frac{q_{k_{i}}j}{n}\right\rfloor+\sum_{i=s+1}^{d}\left\lfloor\frac{q_{k_{i}}(j+1)}{n}\right\rfloor\right).

Let

B(j)=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left\lfloor\frac{q_{k_{i}}j}{n}\right\rfloor+\sum_{i=s+1}^{d}\left\lfloor\frac{q_{k_{i}}(j+1)}{n}\right\rfloor.

Since
q_{i}\mid n
, we set
a_{i}=n/q_{i}
(which is an integer).
Now consider the difference
A(j)-B(j)
:

A(j)-B(j)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left\lceil\frac{q_{k_{i}}(j+1)}{n}\right\rceil+\sum_{i=s+1}^{d}\left\lceil\frac{q_{k_{i}}j}{n}\right\rceil\right)-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left\lfloor\frac{q_{k_{i}}j}{n}\right\rfloor+\sum_{i=s+1}^{d}\left\lfloor\frac{q_{k_{i}}(j+1)}{n}\right\rfloor\right)=d,

since for
i=1,\dots,s
, (
a_{k_{i}}>0
), we have

\left\lceil\frac{q_{k_{i}}(j+1)}{n}\right\rceil-\left\lfloor\frac{q_{k_{i}}j}{n}\right\rfloor=\left\lceil\frac{j+1}{a_{k_{i}}}\right\rceil-\left\lfloor\frac{j}{a_{k_{i}}}\right\rfloor=1,

and for
i=s+1,\dots,d
, (
a_{k_{i}}<0
), we have

\left\lceil\frac{q_{k_{i}}j}{n}\right\rceil-\left\lfloor\frac{q_{k_{i}}(j+1)}{n}\right\rfloor=\left\lceil\frac{j}{a_{k_{i}}}\right\rceil-\left\lfloor\frac{j+1}{a_{k_{i}}}\right\rfloor=1.

It follows that

A(j)+A(n-1-j)=A(j)+(1-B(j))=1+(A(j)-B(j))=1+d.

We now obtain

\displaystyle L(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}x^{A(j)}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}x^{A(n-1-j)}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}x^{d+1-A(j)}=x^{d+1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}x^{-A(j)}=x^{d+1}L(1/x).

Therefore,
L(x)
(i.e.,
L_{1}(x)
) is a symmetric polynomial. The conclusion holds.
∎

Note that the polynomial
L_{1}(x)
need not be unimodal. For example, when

(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3},q_{4},q_{5},q_{6},q_{7})=(1,2,3,3,4,-5,-7)

and
n=420
, we have
L_{1}(x)=159x^{4}+102x^{3}+159x^{2}
.

Proposition 5.5
.

The characteristic polynomial
L_{1}(x)
is unimodal for
d=3,4
.

Proof.

Let
h^{*}_{\Delta(0,q^{(m)})}(x)=mx\cdot L_{1}(x)+L_{2}(x)=1+h_{1}^{*}x+\cdots+h_{d}^{*}x^{d}
.
By Hibi’s lower bound theorem
[
29
]
, we have
h_{1}^{*}\leq h_{i}^{*}\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq d-1
.
Let
mxL_{1}(x)=m(c_{1}x+c_{2}x^{2}+\cdots+c_{d}x^{d})
.
For
d=3
, if
L_{1}(x)
is not unimodal, then it must admit
c_{1}>c_{2}<c_{3}
. For sufficiently large
m
, this yields
h_{1}^{*}>h_{2}^{*}
, leading to a contradiction.
For
d=4
, if
L_{1}(x)
is not unimodal, then it must admit
c_{1}>c_{2}=c_{3}<c_{4}
(by Proposition
5.4
). For sufficiently large
m
, this yields
h_{1}^{*}>h_{2}^{*}
, leading to a contradiction.
Then the conclusion follows.
∎

We now present two interesting families
\Delta(0,q^{(m)})
with closed formula.

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
q=(-1,-1,\dots,-1,d)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Then we have

h^{*}_{\Delta(0,q^{(m)})}(x)=m\sum_{i=1}^{d}x^{i}+(1-x^{d}).

Proof.

Since
n=d
and
q_{d}=d
, we have

\displaystyle xL_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}x^{\lceil\frac{-j}{d}\rceil(d-1)+\lceil j+1\rceil}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x^{i}

\displaystyle L_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}x^{\lceil\frac{-j}{d}\rceil(d-1)+\lceil j\rceil}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}x^{i}=1-x^{d}.

The conclusion follows.
∎

A simplex whose
h^{*}
-polynomial is
\sum_{i=0}^{d}x^{i}
is constructed in
[
24
, Example 2.7]
.

Corollary 5.7
.

Let
q=(-2^{0},-2^{1},\dots,-2^{d-2},2^{d-1})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Then we have

h^{*}_{\Delta(0,q^{(m)})}(x)=((m-1)x+1)(1+x)^{d-1}.

Proof.

Since
n=2^{d-1}
and
q_{d}=2^{d-1}
, we have

\displaystyle xL_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{d}-1}x^{\left\lceil\frac{-j}{2^{d-1}}\right\rceil+\left\lceil\frac{-2j}{2^{d-1}}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil\frac{-2^{d-2}j}{2^{d-1}}\right\rceil+\left\lceil\frac{2^{d-1}j+2^{d-1}}{2^{d-1}}\right\rceil}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{d}-1}x^{-\left(\left\lfloor\frac{j}{2^{d-1}}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{j}{2^{d-2}}\right\rfloor+\cdots+\left\lfloor\frac{j}{2}\right\rfloor\right)+(j+1)}.

Let
j=\sum_{k=0}^{d-2}b_{k}2^{k}
be the binary representation of
j
(
0\leq j\leq 2^{d-1}-1
), where
b_{k}\in\{0,1\}
.
Therefore, we get

\sum_{m=1}^{d-1}\left\lfloor\frac{j}{2^{m}}\right\rfloor=\sum_{m=1}^{d-1}\sum_{k=m}^{d-2}b_{k}2^{k-m}=\sum_{k=1}^{d-2}b_{k}\sum_{m=1}^{k}2^{k-m}=\sum_{k=1}^{d-2}b_{k}(2^{k}-1).

It follows that

xL_{1}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{d}-1}x^{-\sum_{k=1}^{d-2}b_{k}(2^{k}-1)+\sum_{k=0}^{d-2}b_{k}2^{k}+1}=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{d}-1}x^{1+\sum_{k=0}^{d-2}b_{k}}.

Because
\sum_{k=0}^{d-2}b_{k}
is the number of 1’s in the binary representation of
j
, we have

xL_{1}(x)=x\sum_{j=0}^{2^{d}-1}x^{\sum_{k=0}^{d-2}b_{k}}=x\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\binom{d-1}{i}x^{i}=x(1+x)^{d-1}.

On the other hand, the polynomial
L_{2}(x)
is given by

\displaystyle L_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{d}-1}x^{-(\lfloor\frac{j}{2^{d-1}}\rfloor+\lfloor\frac{j}{2^{d-2}}\rfloor+\cdots+\lfloor\frac{j}{2}\rfloor)+j}-x(1+x)^{d-1}

\displaystyle=(1+x)^{d-1}-x(1+x)^{d-1}

\displaystyle=(1-x)(1+x)^{d-1}.

Thus, the
h^{*}
-polynomial of
\Delta(0,q^{(m)})
is given by

h^{*}_{\Delta(0,q^{(m)})}(x)=mxL_{1}(x)+L_{2}(x)=((m-1)x+1)(1+x)^{d-1}.

This completes the proof.
∎

6
Eulerian simplex family:
\mathcal{R}_{d}(m)

6.1
A new formula for Eulerian polynomials

Let
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
be the set of all permutations on
d
elements. For a permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}
, a
descent
is an index
j\in\{1,\dots,d-1\}
such that
\pi_{j}>\pi_{j+1}
, and we write
\mathrm{des}(\pi)
for the number of descents of
\pi
. The
Eulerian number

A(d,i)
counts permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
with exactly
i-1
descents. The
Eulerian polynomial
is then

A_{d}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}A(d,i)x^{i}=\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}}x^{1+\mathrm{des}(\pi)}.

It is well known
[
17
, P292, Exercise 3]
that all roots of
A_{d}(x)/x
are simple, real, and strictly negative.

Proposition 6.1
.

[
44
, Proposition 1.1.4]

For every
d\geq 0
, the Eulerian polynomials satisfy the following generating function identity

\sum_{t\geq 0}t^{d}x^{t}=\frac{A_{d}(x)}{(1-x)^{d+1}}.

Let
[0,d]_{\mathbb{Z}}=\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
. There exists a one-to-one correspondence between the
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
and
[0,d!-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
A pair
(\pi_{i},\pi_{j})
is called an
inversion
of
\pi
if
i<j
and
\pi_{i}>\pi_{j}
.
Let
c_{i}=\#\{i<j\mid\pi_{i}>\pi_{j}\}
for
1\leq i\leq d
.
The sequence
I(\pi)=(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{d})
is called the
inversion table
(or
Lehmer code
) of
\pi
.
Therefore, for a given
\pi
, the number of descents of
\pi
equals the number of pairs with
c_{i}>c_{i+1}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

[
44
, Proposition 1.3.12]

Let

\mathcal{I}_{d}=\{(c_{1},\ldots,c_{d})\mid 0\leq c_{i}\leq d-i\}=[0,d-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}\times[0,d-2]_{\mathbb{Z}}\times\cdots\times[0,0]_{\mathbb{Z}}.

The map
I:\mathfrak{S}_{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}_{d}
that sends each permutation to its inversion table is a bijection.

For a given
(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{d})
, we briefly describe how to return
\pi=I^{-1}((c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{d}))\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}
.
To construct this permutation, begin with
\pi=()
and the initial available set
\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
.
We construct
\pi
by successively choosing the
(c_{i}+1)
-th smallest remaining element as
\pi_{i}
and removing it from the set.
For example, given
(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4},c_{5})=(2,0,0,1,0)
, we return
\pi=31254
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Given a permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}
, suppose the inversion table of
\pi
is
I(\pi)=(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{d})
.
Let

\aleph(\pi)=c_{1}\cdot(d-1)!+c_{2}\cdot(d-2)!+\cdots+c_{d-1}\cdot 1!+c_{d}\cdot 0!.

The map
\aleph:\mathfrak{S}_{d}\rightarrow[0,d!-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
that sends each permutation to
[0,d!-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
is a bijection.

Proof.

It is clear that
\aleph(\pi)\in[0,d!-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
For any integer
j\in[0,d!-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
, by repeatedly applying the Euclidean division algorithm with respect to
(d-1)!,(d-2)!,\dots,1!
, we obtain the factorial base coefficients:
c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{d-1}
.
The proof is now complete by Lemma
6.2
.
∎

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
0\leq N<d!
with
d\geq 2
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{des}(\aleph^{-1}(N))=N-\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}\left\lfloor\frac{N}{i!(i+2)}\right\rfloor.

(16)

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
d
.
When
d=2,3
, the equality is easily verified.
Assuming the formula holds for
d-1
, we prove it for
d
.
For convenience, we denote the left-hand side of Equation (
16
) by
T_{d}(N)
.

Let
m=\left\lfloor\frac{N}{(d-1)!}\right\rfloor
and
r=N-m\cdot(d-1)!
. We know that
0\leq m<d
and
0\leq r<(d-1)!
.
Assume
\aleph^{-1}(N)=\pi
and
I(\pi)=(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{d})
, given that
c_{1}=m
and
c_{2}=\left\lfloor\frac{r}{(d-2)!}\right\rfloor
.
Since the number of descents of
\pi
equals the number of pairs with
c_{i}>c_{i+1}
,
we obtain

T_{d}(N)=\chi(c_{1}>c_{2})+T_{d-1}(r).

The inequality
c_{1}>c_{2}
is equivalent to
r<m\cdot(d-2)!
, which means

N<m\cdot(d-1)!+m\cdot(d-2)!=md\cdot(d-2)!.

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle T_{d}(N)

\displaystyle=\chi(N<md\cdot(d-2)!)+T_{d-1}(r)

\displaystyle=\chi(N<md\cdot(d-2)!)+r-\sum_{i=0}^{d-3}\left\lfloor\frac{r}{i!(i+2)}\right\rfloor.

To prove

T_{d}(N)=m\cdot(d-1)!+r-\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}\left\lfloor\frac{N}{i!(i+2)}\right\rfloor,

it suffices to show that

m\cdot(d-1)!-\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}\left\lfloor\frac{m\cdot(d-1)!+r}{i!(i+2)}\right\rfloor+\sum_{i=0}^{d-3}\left\lfloor\frac{r}{i!(i+2)}\right\rfloor=\chi(N<md\cdot(d-2)!).

We denote the left-hand side of the above equation by
\mathrm{LHS}
.
Since for
0\leq i\leq d-3
,
\frac{m\cdot(d-1)!}{i!(i+2)}
is an integer, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{LHS}

\displaystyle=m\cdot(d-1)!-\left\lfloor\frac{m\cdot(d-1)!+r}{(d-2)!d}\right\rfloor-\sum_{i=0}^{d-3}\frac{m\cdot(d-1)!}{i!(i+2)}

\displaystyle=m\cdot(d-1)!-\left\lfloor\frac{m\cdot(d-1)!+r}{(d-2)!d}\right\rfloor-m\cdot(d-1)!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d-3}\left(\frac{1}{(i+1)!}-\frac{1}{(i+2)!}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=m\cdot(d-1)!-\left\lfloor\frac{N}{(d-2)!d}\right\rfloor-m\cdot(d-1)!\left(1-\frac{1}{(d-1)!}\right)

\displaystyle=m-\left\lfloor\frac{N}{(d-2)!d}\right\rfloor.

By
N=m\cdot(d-1)!+r
, we set
\frac{N}{(d-1)!}=m+\delta
. Thus
0\leq\delta=\frac{r}{(d-1)!}<1
.
We consider the following two cases:

1.) When
m=0
, we have
\mathrm{LHS}=0=\chi(N<md\cdot(d-2)!)
.

2.) When
m\geq 1
, we consider the value of

\left\lfloor\frac{N}{(d-2)!d}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{N}{(d-1)!}\cdot\frac{d-1}{d}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{m(d-1)}{d}+\frac{r}{(d-1)!}\cdot\frac{d-1}{d}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{m(d-1)}{d}+\delta\cdot\frac{d-1}{d}\right\rfloor.

It is clear that the integer part of
\frac{m(d-1)}{d}
is
m-1
, and its fractional part is
1-\frac{m}{d}
.
Therefore, we have

\left\lfloor\frac{N}{(d-2)!d}\right\rfloor=m-1+\left\lfloor 1-\frac{m}{d}+\delta\cdot\frac{d-1}{d}\right\rfloor.

Obviously,
\left\lfloor 1-\frac{m}{d}+\delta\cdot\frac{d-1}{d}\right\rfloor\in\{0,1\}
.
Observe that
1-\frac{m}{d}+\delta\cdot\frac{d-1}{d}\geq 1
is equivalent to
\delta\geq\frac{m}{d-1}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\left\lfloor\frac{N}{(d-2)!d}\right\rfloor=\begin{cases}m-1&\text{ if }\delta<\frac{m}{d-1},\\
m&\text{ if }\delta\geq\frac{m}{d-1}.\end{cases}

Since
\delta<\frac{m}{d-1}
is equivalent to
r<m\cdot(d-2)!
(i.e.,
N<md\cdot(d-2)!
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{LHS}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }N<md\cdot(d-2)!,\\
0&\text{ if }N\geq md\cdot(d-2)!\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\chi(N<md\cdot(d-2)!).

We have thus finished the proof.
∎

A direct consequence of the above result is an explicit formula for the Eulerian polynomials.

Corollary 6.5
.

The Eulerian polynomial
A_{d}(x)
is given by

A_{d}(x)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}}x^{1+\mathrm{des}(\pi)}=\sum_{j=0}^{d!-1}x^{j+1-\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}\left\lfloor\frac{j}{i!(i+2)}\right\rfloor}.

6.2
Eulerian simplex family

Based on our previously implemented algorithm for computing polynomial
L_{1}(x)
, we can construct some Eulerian simplex families for small
d
through a bounded search. This also inspires us to construct a family of Eulerian simplex family in any dimension.

Theorem 6.6
.

For every positive integer
m
and
d\geq 1
, the following family
\mathcal{S}_{d}(m)
of polytopes fall into the Eulerian simplex family
\mathcal{R}_{d}(m)
of dimension
d
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{d}(m)=\begin{cases}\ell_{m}=[0,m]=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\mid 0\leq\alpha\leq m\}&\text{ if }\quad d=1,\\
\mathrm{conv}\{\mathbf{0}^{d},\mathbf{e}_{1}^{d},\mathbf{e}_{2}^{d},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{d-1}^{d},(q_{1}(d),q_{2}(d),\ldots,q_{d-1}(d),d!\cdot m)\}&\text{ if }\quad d\geq 2,\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{d}
denote the
i
-th unit coordinate vector of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
;
\mathbf{0}^{d}
denote the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; and

q_{i}(d)=\frac{-d!}{i!+(i-1)!}\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq i\leq d-1.

Proof.

Let
q_{d}(d)=1-\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}q_{j}(d)
. Then we have

\displaystyle q_{d}(d)

\displaystyle=1-d!\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\frac{-1}{j!+(j-1)!}=1+d!\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\frac{1}{(j-1)!(j+1)}

\displaystyle=1+d!\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{d-2}\frac{j+1}{(j+2)!}=1+d!\cdot\sum_{i=2}^{d}\left(\frac{1}{(i-1)!}-\frac{1}{i!}\right)

\displaystyle=d!.

By Theorem
5.1
, it suffices to prove that
x\cdot L_{1}(x)=A_{d}(x)
.
We have

\displaystyle x\cdot L_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{d!-1}x^{\left\lceil\frac{q_{1}(d)\cdot j}{d!}\right\rceil+\cdots+\left\lceil\frac{q_{d-1}(d)\cdot j}{d!}\right\rceil+\left\lceil\frac{q_{d}(d)\cdot j+q_{d}(d)}{d!}\right\rceil}=\sum_{j=0}^{d!-1}x^{j+1+\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\left\lceil\frac{-j}{i!+(i-1)!}\right\rceil}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{d!-1}x^{j+1-\sum_{i=0}^{d-2}\left\lfloor\frac{j}{i!(i+2)}\right\rfloor}.

By Corollary
6.5
, this completes the proof.
∎

Note that
\mathcal{S}_{d}(m)
is a special case of
\Delta(0,q^{(m)})
-simplices and is itself a member of the Eulerian simplex families.
There exist other Eulerian simplex families.
For example,

\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{5}(m)=\mathrm{conv}\{\mathbf{0}^{5},\mathbf{e}_{1}^{5},\mathbf{e}_{2}^{5},\mathbf{e}_{3}^{5},\mathbf{e}_{4}^{5},(-20,-10,3,4,5!\cdot m)\}.

Its Ehrhart series is given by

\mathrm{Ehr}(\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{5}(m),x)=\frac{m(x^{5}+26x^{4}+66x^{3}+26x^{2}+x)+(-x^{5}-10x^{4}-3x^{3}+10x^{2}+3x+1)}{(1-x)^{6}}.

Proposition 6.7
.

Given a positive integer
d
, if the polytope
\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}_{d}(m)
fall into the Eulerian simplex family
\mathcal{R}_{d}(m)
of dimension

, then we have

i(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}_{d}(m),t)=mt^{d}+c_{d-1}t^{d-1}+\cdots+c_{1}t+1,

where

are constants independent of

.

Proof.

We assume that the
h^{*}
-polynomial of
\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}_{d}(m)
can be expressed as

h^{*}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}_{d}(m)}(x)=mA_{d}(x)+T(x)\quad\text{with}\quad T(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}r_{i}x^{i}.

By Equation (
2
), Proposition
6.1
, we have

i(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}_{d}(m),t)=mt^{d}+\sum_{i=0}^{d}r_{i}\binom{t+d-i}{d}.

Since
T(1)=0
(from Proposition

), the coefficient of
t^{d}
in the polynomial
\sum_{i=0}^{d}r_{i}\binom{t+d-i}{d}
is
0
. As
T(x)
is independent of
m
, it follows that each
r_{i}
is also independent of
m
.
∎

Corollary 6.8
.

Follow the notation above, and the following equation holds:

\#(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}_{d}(m)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d})=[t]T(x)+m+d+1\quad\text{and}\quad\#(\mathrm{int}(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}_{d}(m))\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d})=[t^{d}]T(x)+m.

).

.

Corollary 6.9
.

Let

.

Corollary 6.10

\displaystyle=\frac{m(x^{3}+4x^{2}+x)+(-x^{3}-3x^{2}+3x+1)}{(1-x)^{4}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ehr}(\mathcal{S}_{4}(m),x)

\displaystyle=\frac{m(x^{4}+11x^{3}+11x^{2}+x)+(-x^{4}-10x^{3}+10x+1)}{(1-x)^{5}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ehr}(\mathcal{S}_{5}(m),x)

\displaystyle=\frac{m(x^{5}+26x^{4}+66x^{3}+26x^{2}+x)+(-x^{5}-25x^{4}-40x^{3}+40x^{2}+25x+1)}{(1-x)^{6}}.

6.3
Reverse isoperimetric phenomenon

Given a geometric object
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, its
volume
is defined by the integral
|E|:=\int_{E}d\mathbf{x}
.

The isoperimetric problem stands as one of the most classical and renowned problems in the history of geometry.
In two dimensions, the isoperimetric inequality asserts that a disk has the smallest boundary length among all domains in the plane with a given area.

The isoperimetric problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is to minimize the surface area among all domains having given volume, or equivalently, maximize the volume among all domains whose boundary surfaces have fixed
(d-1)
-dimensional area.
The solution in both cases is that the unique extremal is the domain bounded by a sphere.

Proposition 6.12
.

[
12
, Isoperimetric inrequality]

Let
E
be a compact domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with smooth boundary. Then

|\partial E|^{d}\geq d^{d}|B_{1}^{d}|\cdot|E|^{d-1},

where
|E|
denotes the volume of
E
and
|\partial E|
denotes the
(d-1)
-dimensional measure of the boundary
\partial E
. Moreover,
B_{1}^{d}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x|<1\}
denotes the open unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
|B_{1}^{d}|
denotes its volume.

The isoperimetric inequality is sharp on balls. Proposition
6.12
can be understood intuitively as the volume of
E
being bounded by the volume of its boundary.
We refer the reader to the many review books and papers
[
14
,
26
,
27
,
39
]

Note that the isoperimetric problem above is treated in the “continuous” context.
The discrete isoperimetric inequality has also been extensively studied; see, e.g,
[
28
,
16
,
9
,
10
]
.

For the lattice polytopes in our study, the isoperimetric inequality mentioned above fails to hold for their volume and boundary volume. The Reeve tetrahedron (Example
1.1
) provides a counterexample in dimension three, with volume
\frac{m}{6}
and boundary volume
2
. As
m
tends to infinity, the volume of the Reeve tetrahedron is much larger than its boundary volume.

In fact, for each dimension, by Corollary
6.10
, the isoperimetric inequality fails to hold.

Corollary 6.13
.

Let
m\geq 1
.
For every positive integer
d
, there exists a family of
d
-dimensional integral polytopes
\mathcal{P}_{d}(m)
such that, as
m
increases, their volume tends to infinity while their boundary volume remains constant.

Proof.

For an integral polytope
\mathcal{P}
, it is well-known
[
6
, Corollary 3.20; Theorem 5.6]
that the leading coefficient of
i(\mathcal{P},t)
equals the volume of
\mathcal{P}
, the second highest coefficient of
i(\mathcal{P},t)
equals half of the boundary volume of
\mathcal{P}
.
This result now follows directly from Theorem
6.6
and Proposition
6.7
.
∎

Inequalities among the coefficients of Ehrhart polynomials have also been extensively studied; see, e.g.,
[
5
,
8
,
30
,
41
,
43
]
.
By Corollary
6.10
, the following result follows immediately.

Corollary 6.14
.

Consider any
d
-dimensional integral polytope
\mathcal{P}
with Ehrhart polynomial:

i(\mathcal{P},t)=c_{\mathcal{P},d}t^{d}+c_{\mathcal{P},d-1}t^{d-1}+\cdots+c_{\mathcal{P},1}t+1.

There does not exist a function
F(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{d-1}):\mathbb{Q}^{d-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that for every integral polytope
\mathcal{P}
, the following inequality holds:

c_{\mathcal{P},d}<F(c_{\mathcal{P},1},c_{\mathcal{P},2},\ldots,c_{\mathcal{P},d-1}).

We now end this subsection with a property of the
h^{*}
-polynomial of
\mathcal{S}_{d}(m)
.

A sequence of positive integers
(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})
is
strictly log concave
if
a_{i}^{2}>a_{i-1}a_{i+1}
for
1\leq i\leq d-1
and is
strictly unimodal
if
a_{0}<a_{1}<\cdots<a_{i}
and
a_{i+1}>a_{i+2}>\cdots>a_{d}
for some
0\leq i\leq d
.
It is well known that if
(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})
is strictly log concave, then it is strictly unimodal.
If the polynomial
a_{0}+a_{1}t+\cdots+a_{d}t^{d}
has negative real roots, then the sequence
(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})
is strictly log concave and hence strictly unimodal
[
7
]
.
An introduction to log concave and unimodal sequences can be found in
[
42
]
.

The following conclusion was obtained by Beck and Stapledon in their study of properties related to the Hecke operator
U_{n}
.

Proposition 6.15
.

[
7
, Corollary 1.3]

Fix a positive integer
d
and let
\rho_{1}<\rho_{2}<\cdots<\rho_{d}=0
denote the roots of the Eulerian polynomial
A_{d}(x)
.
There exist positive integers
m_{d}
and
n_{d}
such that, if
\mathcal{P}
is a
d
-dimensional lattice polytope and
n>n_{d}
, then

h^{*}_{n\mathcal{P}}(x)=h_{0}^{*}(n)+h_{1}^{*}(n)x+\cdots+h_{d}^{*}(n)x^{d}

has negative real roots
\beta_{1}(n)<\beta_{2}(n)<\cdots<\beta_{d-1}(n)<\beta_{d}(n)<0
with
\beta_{i}(n)\to\rho_{i}
as
n\to\infty
, and the coefficients of
h^{*}_{n\mathcal{P}}(x)
are positive, strictly log concave, and satisfy

1=h^{*}_{0}(n)<h^{*}_{d}(n)<h^{*}_{1}(n)<\cdots<h^{*}_{i}(n)<h^{*}_{d-i}(n)<h^{*}_{i+1}(n)<\cdots<h^{*}_{\lfloor\frac{d+1}{2}\rfloor}(n)<m_{d}h^{*}_{d}(n).

Polytopes
\mathcal{S}_{d}(m)
lead to a similar but distinct result, as follows.

Proposition 6.16
.

There exist positive integers
m_{d}
and
n_{d}
such that, if
n>n_{d}
, then

h^{*}_{\mathcal{S}_{d}(n)}(x)=h_{0}^{*}(n)+h_{1}^{*}(n)x+\cdots+h_{d}^{*}(n)x^{d}

has negative real roots
\beta_{1}(n)=\rho_{1}<\beta_{2}(n)=\rho_{2}<\cdots<\beta_{d-1}(n)=\rho_{d-1}<\beta_{d}(n)=\frac{-1}{n-1}<0
with
\beta_{d}(n)\to\rho_{d}=0
as
n\to\infty
, and the coefficients of
h^{*}_{\mathcal{S}_{d}(n)}(x)
are positive, strictly log concave, and satisfy

1=h^{*}_{0}(n)<h^{*}_{d}(n)<h^{*}_{1}(n)<\cdots<h^{*}_{i}(n)<h^{*}_{d-i}(n)<h^{*}_{i+1}(n)<\cdots<h^{*}_{\lfloor\frac{d+1}{2}\rfloor}(n)<m_{d}h^{*}_{d}(n).

Proof.

By Corollary
6.9
, the
h^{*}
-polynomial of
\mathcal{S}_{d}(n)
is given by

h^{*}_{\mathcal{S}_{d}(n)}(x)=\frac{A_{d}(x)\cdot(nx-x+1)}{x}=1+(n-1)x^{d}+\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}((n-1)A(d,i)+A(d,i+1))x^{i}.

Therefore, we only need to verify the last inequality chain in the above proposition.
It is well known that the Eulerian polynomials are symmetric (i.e.,
A(d,i)=A(d,d+1-i)
) and strictly unimodal.
When
d
is even, for
0\leq i<\left\lfloor\frac{d+1}{2}\right\rfloor
, we have

\displaystyle h^{*}_{d-i}(n)-h^{*}_{i}(n)

\displaystyle=(n-1)A(d,d-i)+A(d,d-i+1)-(n-1)A(d,i)-A(d,i+1)

\displaystyle=(n-2)(A(d,i+1)-A(d,i))>0

and

\displaystyle h^{*}_{i+1}(n)-h^{*}_{d-i}(n)

\displaystyle=(n-1)A(d,i+1)+A(d,i+2)-(n-1)A(d,d-i)-A(d,d-i+1)

\displaystyle=A(d,i+2)-A(d,i)>0.

An analogous result holds when
d
is odd.
This completes the proof.
∎

7
Concluding Remarks

One of the important contributions of this paper is the resolution of the well-known sign pattern problem of coefficients in Ehrhart polynomials. This work is based on the authors’s previous paper
[
36
]
, particularly on the restated Theorem
1.3
,
which allows us to only consider certain specific sign patterns.

In
[
36
]
, we resolve the Ehrhart coefficient sign pattern problem for dimensions
d\leq 9
. After constructing several simplices
\mathcal{S}_{d}(m)
for small
d
, we are able to construct the integral polytopes corresponding to all sign patterns of dimension
10\leq d\leq 17
.
The relevant program is available at the following address:

https://github.com/TygerLiu/TygerLiu.github.io/tree/main/Procedure/Sign-pattern-problem
.

After constructing all
\mathcal{S}_{d}(m)
, it is still unclear how to settle the open problem: there are too many choices
r_{i}
and
m_{i}
, and the pattern seems chaotic. The philosophy is “the greater the chaos, the greater the opportunity”. Our proof seems natural by hindsight: the greedy decomposition seems to be the
most controllable choice.

For the reader’s convenience, we outline the explicit construction of a polytope with any given sign pattern:

\operatorname{\mathbf{Sgn}}=(s_{d-2},s_{d-3},\ldots,s_{1}),\quad\text{where}\quad s_{i}\in\{+1,-1\}\quad\text{for all}\quad i,\quad d\geq 6.

We now construct an integral polytope
\mathcal{P}
of dimension
d
such that the Ehrhart polynomial
i(\mathcal{P},t)=1+\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}t^{i}
satisfies
\operatorname{sgn}(c_{i})=s_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq d-2
.

Case 1: If
s_{d-2}=+1
, then we construct an integral polytope
\mathcal{Q}
of dimension
d-1
satisfying

\operatorname{Sgn}(\mathcal{Q})=(s_{d-3},\ldots,s_{1})
. Then by
[
36
, Embedding Theorem I]
,
\mathcal{P}=r\mathcal{Q}\times[0,1]
is the desired polytope
when
r
is sufficiently large.

Case 2: If
s_{1}=+1
, then we construct an integral polytope
\mathcal{Q}
of dimension
d-1
satisfying

\operatorname{Sgn}(\mathcal{Q})=(s_{d-2},\ldots,s_{2})
. Then by
[
36
, Embedding Theorem I]
,
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{Q}\times[0,m]

is the desired polytope
when
m
is sufficiently large.

Case 3: If
s_{d-2}=s_{d-3}=-1
and
s_{1}=-1
, then we construct an integral polytope
\mathcal{Q}
of dimension
d-3
satisfying

\operatorname{Sgn}(\mathcal{Q})=(-s_{d-4},\ldots,-s_{2})
. Then by
[
36
, Embedding Theorem IV]
,
\mathcal{P}=r\mathcal{Q}\times\mathcal{T}_{m}

is the desired polytope when
m\gg r\gg 0
.

Case 4: If
s_{1}=-1
,
s_{2}=+1
, and
s_{3}=-1
, then we construct an integral polytope
\mathcal{Q}
of dimension
d-2
satisfying

\operatorname{Sgn}(\mathcal{Q})=(s_{d-2},\ldots,s_{3})
. Then by
[
36
, Embedding Theorem III]
,
\mathcal{P}=r\mathcal{Q}\times\mathrm{conv}\{(0,0),(1,0),(1,a),(2,a)\}
is the desired polytope when
a\gg r\gg 0
.

Case 5: If
\operatorname{\mathbf{Sgn}}
contains two consecutive
+1
, then we treat the following two cases separately.
Suppose
d=d_{1}+d_{2}
with
d_{1}\geq d_{2}\geq 2
.

1.) When
s_{d_{1}}=s_{d_{1}-1}=+1
, we construct two integral polytopes
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
(of dimension
d_{1}
) and
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
(of dimension
d_{2}
) satisfying
\operatorname{Sgn}(\mathcal{Q}_{1})=(s_{d_{1}-2},\ldots,s_{1})
and
\operatorname{Sgn}(\mathcal{Q}_{2})=(s_{d-2},\ldots,s_{d_{1}+1})
, respectively. Then by
[
36
, Embedding Theorem II]
,
\mathcal{P}=r\mathcal{Q}_{1}\times\mathcal{Q}_{2}
is the desired polytope when
r
is sufficiently large.

2.) When
s_{d_{2}}=s_{d_{2}-1}=+1
, we construct two integral polytopes
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
(of dimension
d_{1}
) and
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
(of dimension
d_{2}
) satisfying
\operatorname{Sgn}(\mathcal{Q}_{1})=(s_{d-2},\ldots,s_{d_{2}+1})
and
\operatorname{Sgn}(\mathcal{Q}_{2})=(s_{d_{2}-2},\ldots,s_{1})
, respectively. Then by
[
36
, Embedding Theorem II]
,
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{Q}_{1}\times r\mathcal{Q}_{2}
is the desired polytope when
r
is sufficiently large.

Case 6: If
\operatorname{\mathbf{Sgn}}
does not satisfy all of Cases 1–5, then the sign pattern reduces to the case described in Theorem
1.3
. Thus, we set

\mathcal{P}=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}\mathcal{S}_{d_{i}}(m_{i})\right)\times r_{0}\mathcal{T}_{m_{0}}.

This is the main result obtained in this paper; see Theorem
2.2
.

In future work, we will further explore the applications of the family of simplices
\mathcal{S}_{d}(m)
in Ehrhart theory.
