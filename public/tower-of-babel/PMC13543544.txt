Self-perceived burden and quality of life in patients with radiation-induced brain injury after radiotherapy for nasopharyngeal carcinoma: a cross-sectional study on the mediating role of stigma and the moderating role of sleep quality
Authors: Yifan Ye, Rui Song, Fengjiao Zhao, Liangying Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough self-perceived burden (SPB), stigma and sleep quality are associated with quality of life (QoL) in patients with radiation-induced brain injury (RBI) after radiotherapy for nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), little is known about the mechanism underlying the relationship among these variables. The aim of this study was to examine the relationship among SPB, stigma, and QoL, and further explore how sleep quality may moderate the effect of stigma.MethodsThis cross-sectional study investigated 150 NPC patients with RBI who were recruited from an affiliated hospital of the university in Guangzhou, China. The participants completed several questionnaires, including a socio-demographic characteristics questionnaire, the Chinese version of the Self-Perceived Burden Scale (SPBS), the Social Impact Scale (SIS), the World Health Organization Quality of Life Instrument Short Form (WHOQOL-BREF), and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). The mediation analysis and moderated mediation analysis were conducted using PROCESS macro Model 4 and Model 58 in SPSS 28, respectively.ResultsThe results of mediation analysis showed that stigma had a significant indirect effect between self-perceived burden and quality of life (effect = -0.152, 95% CI = -0.223 to -0.032), and the direct effect of self-perceived burden on quality of life was not statistically significant (effect = -0.083, 95% CI = -0.300 to 0.134). In the moderated mediation analysis, the indirect effect of self-perceived burden on quality of life as mediated by stigma was apparent only among patients with poorer sleep quality (higher PSQI scores; effect = -0.115, 95% CI = -0.246 to -0.021).ConclusionsThe findings suggest that stigma may function as a mechanism through which self-perceived burden is associated with quality of life in NPC patients with RBI, especially when patients suffer low sleep quality. Healthcare providers should pay attention to sleep quality and consider developing a supportive care intervention to improve patients’ quality of life by incorporating effective strategies to reduce self-perceived burden and stigma for this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16375-7.Keywords: Radiation-induced brain injury, Radiotherapy, Nasopharyngeal carcinoma, Self-perceived burden, Stigma, Quality of life, Sleep quality

Background

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a form of epithelial cancer that originates from the mucosal tissue in the nasopharynx, which is the upper part of the throat behind the nose [1]. In China, the age-standardized rate of NPC is 3.0 per 100,000 [1]. Radiotherapy is still the cornerstone of treatment for patients with non-metastatic NPC, primarily because of the tumor’s specific anatomical location and the heightened sensitivity of the cancer cells to radiation therapy [1, 2]. After radiotherapy, the 5-year locoregional failure rates for newly diagnosed non-metastatic NPC have dropped to 7.4%, alongside an increase in the overall survival rate [3, 4].

Though radiotherapy has proven to be effective in treating NPC, patients may still experience unavoidable complications following the completion of their treatment [5]. Radiation-induced brain injury (RBI) is a serious side effect of radiotherapy in nasopharyngeal carcinoma patients, diagnosed through brain imaging techniques like computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [6]. Some areas of the brain, such as the temporal lobe, are situated in close proximity to the nasopharyngeal region due to their anatomical positioning [7]. Due to the proximity of these brain regions to the nasopharyngeal cavity, they are often within the radiotherapy field, which may result in potential damage as part of the treatment process [7]. RBI, with the high incidence rate, from 22.5% to 47%, can manifest anywhere from a few months to several years following the completion of radiotherapy [8, 9]. It can also lead to significant clinical manifestations, such as dizziness, headaches, memory deterioration, muscle weakness, and seizures, all of which can adversely affect the quality of life (QoL) for patients [8, 9].

A previous study showed that QoL of NPC patients with RBI was significantly lower, with the domains of physical health, psychological health, and social relationships being worse than those of NPC patients without RBI [5]. However, the knowledge about the influencing factors and mechanisms among predictors of QoL in these patients is still unknown, especially concerning psychological factors. Only anxiety, depression and cognitive impairment have been identified to influence QoL [5]. Some other factors, such as self-perceived burden (SPB) and stigma, have been shown to affect QoL in patients with lung cancer [10], advanced cancer [11], and head and neck cancer [12]. Nevertheless, their relationships and mechanisms remain unclear in NPC patients with RBI.

Self-perceived burden (SPB) is defined as the “empathic concern engendered from the impact on others of one’s illness and care needs, resulting in guilt, distress, feelings of responsibility and diminished sense of self” [13, 14]. It was identified to have association with QoL among cancer patients [15]. One study interviewed 429 patients with urologic cancers and found that 73.2% of them experiencing SPB [15]. In a study conducted across multiple centers, involving 352 patients with advanced cancer, it was observed that SPB was negatively correlated with QoL [11]. Moreover, existential distress and anxiety were found to mediate the connection between SPB and QoL [11]. Given the serious effects that SPB may have on QoL among cancer patients, it is important to clarify the level of SPB and explore the relationship and potential mechanisms between SPB and QoL in NPC patients with RBI.

Stigma is defined as a characteristic or trait that diminishes an individual’s reputation, causing them to feel set apart from others and lowering their social standing to that of someone marked or tainted [16]. It also has close connections with QoL. Stigma was shown to negatively impact QoL through the mediating effect of distress among primary lung cancer patients [17]. It was also verified in patients with prostate cancer, with direct effects on QoL [18]. In Kenya, a cross-sectional study found that cancer survivors reported low levels of stigma in multiple areas, such as social awkwardness, perceived severity of their condition, financial bias, personal responsibility, avoidance behaviors, and stigmatization within policies [19]. Because NPC patients with RBI may experience clinical symptoms such as stuttering and changes in appearance, which can cause them to feel stigma and affect their normal social participation, it is significant to notice the stigma these patients are facing.

A significant proportion of cancer survivors, ranging from 45% to 80%, report having sleep problems and experiencing suboptimal sleep quality [20–22]. A systematic review identified the negative relationship between stigma and sleep quality [23]. Most of the current studies have investigated the relationship between stigma and sleep quality in individuals with breast cancer, revealing that the presence of stigma is associated with lower sleep quality. The process underlying sleep disorders is intricate and often occurs alongside various psychological factors, which can be interpreted through the interactive effects between sleep quality and mental health [24, 25]. Nevertheless, it is still uncertain how sleep quality plays a role in moderating stigma and influencing the QoL in NPC patients with RBI.

Based on the evidence mentioned above, RBI in NPC patients may create a unique psychosocial vulnerability nexus. Neurological deficits (e.g., dysarthria, cognitive decline) heighten caregiver dependency, fostering intense SPB which is a catalyst for withdrawal and internalized shame [26]. This directly cultivates stigma through visible disability and perceived “burdensomeness”, triggering social alienation. Critically, RBI’s neuropathology and psychological distress may disrupt sleep architecture. Poor sleep quality then exacerbates stigma’s impact by impairing emotional regulation and amplifying negative self-appraisals, thereby intensifying SPB’s downstream erosion of QoL. SPB reflects patients’ internal evaluation that they impose a burden on caregivers, stigma captures perceived social devaluation and internalized shame, prior evidence suggests that feeling burdensome can foster stigma and adverse emotional reactions, which then undermine QoL [27]. Therefore, we conceptualized stigma as a psychological mechanism (mediator) through which SPB is associated with poorer QoL. This model addresses a critical gap: while SPB, stigma, and sleep individually correlate with QoL in cancer populations, their synergistic trajectory, particularly RBI’s role in initiating this cascade, remains unexamined. Understanding this integrated pathway is important for developing targeted interventions that concurrently address dependency-related distress, social discredit, and sleep health to improve holistic outcomes in this underserved population.

Concerning the severe effects of SPB and stigma on QoL, as well as the possible moderating role of sleep quality in NPC patients with RBI, the purpose of this study was to examine the relationship among SPB, stigma, and QoL, and further explore how sleep quality may moderate the effect of stigma.

Methods

Design

This research was conducted as a cross-sectional study and adheres to the guidelines set forth by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) for cross-sectional research (refer to Appendix).

Settings and participants

This study involved patients who had experienced radiation-induced brain injury (RBI) because of undergoing radiotherapy for nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC). They were recruited by convenience sampling from Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital Department of Neurology in Guangzhou, China between November 2023 and July 2024. PASS 15 was used to estimate the sample size for cross-sectional study using effect size. Based on Cohen’s recommendations for a medium effect size, we set the effect size at f2 = 0.20 in PASS to estimate the required sample size [28].To achieve a power of 0.95 with a two-tailed test, and assuming an effect size (f2) of 0.20 and α of 0.05, the required sample size for NPC patients experiencing radiation-induced brain injury (RBI) was calculated to be 116. Finally, we recruited 150 patients in total.

Eligible participants were included if they satisfied the following criteria: (1) a documented history of NPC treated with radiotherapy; (2) a clinical diagnosis of RBI by professional doctors, compatible MRI or CT findings (e.g., necrosis, white matter changes, or enhancing lesions in temporal lobe or adjacent regions), after excluding tumor recurrence, brain metastases, or other central nervous system diseases based on both clinical manifestations and MRI or CT scans that meet the criteria for RBI in Merritt’s Neurology (10th edition); (3) no evidence of symptomatic recurrent tumors, brain metastasis, or other central nervous system disorders; (4) no signs of consciousness disturbances or unstable vital signs; (5) need to receive care by normal/ informal caregivers for more than 6 months in order to decrease the bias of SPB assessment. The following exclusion criteria applied to NPC patients with RBI: (1) receiving ongoing radiotherapy for NPC; (2) inability to comprehend the purpose of the questionnaires, even after a thorough explanation; (3) a history of mental health issues, including obsessive-compulsive disorder, bipolar disorder, other related conditions.

Measures

Self-perceived burden

Self-perceived burden (SPB) was assessed by Chinese version of Self-Perceived Burden Scale (SPBS). The SPBS includes 10 items, containing patient’s self-perceived physical, emotional, and economic burdens to their caregivers [29, 30]. The total score, which ranges between 10 and 50, is calculated by adding up the 10 individual items. A higher score reflects a greater sense of self-perceived burden. A total score exceeding 20 suggests a significant degree of distress associated with SPB [14, 31]. Exploratory factor analysis of Chinese version of the SPBS extracted two common factors, which together accounted for 61.00% of the total variance [32]. The Cronbach’s alpha of the SPB in this study was 0.874.

Stigma

Stigma is defined as a characteristic that diminishes an individual’s social standing, causing them to feel separated from others and lowering their status to that of someone marked or flawed [16]. The level of stigma was assessed by Social Impact Scale (SIS). The SIS was first created by Fife and Wright [33] in 2000, and was translated into Chinese by Pan [34]. The SIS measures four dimensions, including social rejection, financial insecurity, internalized shame, and social isolation, which together capture both self-stigma (internalized negative beliefs and shame) and perceived social stigma (anticipated or experienced rejection). NPC patients with RBI often have visible speech, cognitive, or functional impairments, and may be particularly vulnerable to both internalized and enacted stigma in social and occupational contexts. Therefore, for these reasons, the SIS was considered suitable for comprehensively capturing the stigma experiences of this population. This is a 24 items’ 4-point Likert scale. The higher score of SIS indicates a greater level of perceived stigmatization. The item-level content validity indices of the scale ranged from 0.875 to 1.000, with an average content validity index of 0.978. Exploratory factor analysis extracted four common factors, which together accounted for 64.04% of the total variance [35]. The Cronbach’s alpha of the SIS in this study was 0.821.

Quality of life

Quality of life was assessed by World Health Organization Quality of Life Instrument Short Form (WHOQOL-BREF). Developed by the WHO, the WHOQOL-BREF aims to measure four major aspects of quality of life [36]. It contains 26 items and uses a 5-point Likert scale. The total score, ranging from 0 to 100, with higher score indicates a better level of quality of life. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) supported construct validity; Rasch models revealed that almost all items were embedded in their expected domains; all correlation coefficients between Rasch scores and original domain scores were above 0.9 [37]. The Cronbach’s alpha of the WHOQOL-BREF in this study was 0.813.

Sleep quality

Sleep quality was measured using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), which was created by Buysse [38] and colleagues in 1989. It has become a widely accepted instrument for evaluating subjective sleep quality over the past month. The PSQI contains 19 self-rated items and 5 additional items rated by a bed partner or roommate. Only the 19 self-rated items are used to compute seven component scores, which are then summed to yield a global score (0–21), with higher scores reflecting poorer sleep quality. If the total score is more than 5, it indicates that participant has sleep problems. In Chinese patients with breast cancer, exploratory or confirmatory factor analyses supporting a two- to three- factor structure. Significant moderate correlations (r ≈ 0.30–0.60) with measures of depression, anxiety, fatigue and quality of life further support its convergent and criterion-related validity [39]. The Cronbach’s alpha of the PSQI in this study was 0.745.

Procedures

A professional nurse (SR) and an experienced therapist (ZFJ) screened and recruited participants in the Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital Department of Neurology. If patients showed interest in joining the study and met with the inclusion criteria, they would be asked to complete the assessments after signing the written informed consent forms.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 28.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois) and the PROCESS macro for data processing [40]. PROCESS is a framework based on linear regression that calculates indirect effects (IE) and provides bias-corrected confidence intervals [41]. Mediation enables researchers to explore “how” certain effects occur, whereas moderation focuses on “when” these effects are likely to take place [41]. By using moderated mediation analysis, researchers can explore both the “how” and “why” behind certain phenomena at the same time [41]. No missing data was found in analysis. Descriptive statistics were applied to summarize the socio-demographic and disease-related characteristics of the participants. The mediation analysis was performed using Model 4 of the PROCESS macro [40]. This process of creating datasets, taking bootstrap samples, and conducting significance tests was carried out 5000 times. An indirect effect was considered statistically significant if its 95% bootstrap confidence intervals did not contain zero [40]. Mediation analysis was performed using one independent variable (Self-perceived Burden), one mediator (Stigma), and one dependent variable (Quality of Life), with controlling four covariates (Age, Number of radiotherapy sessions, Interval time, Stage of NPC) which are associated with disease severity, neurocognitive outcomes, and QoL in NPC and RBI populations to decrease the bias [5–9, 35, 36].

After confirming the mediating role of stigma, we added moderation analysis to analyze the moderating role of sleep quality. The moderated mediation analysis was conducted using Model 58 from the PROCESS macro [40], which enables the examination of whether the mediating effect of stigma in the relationship between SPB and QoL varies based on sleep quality (Fig. 1). To examine the indirect effects, sleep quality was considered at three different points: the mean, one standard deviation above the mean (high), and one standard deviation below the mean (low). The analyses were based on 5000 bootstrap samples, with 95% confidence intervals calculated for each.

Ethics considerations

Approval for the study was granted by the Medical Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital (SYSKY-2023-1106-01). Participants were asked to sign a written consent form, with assurances that they could withdraw from the study at any time if they wished.

The characteristics of the participants are displayed in Table 1. The mean age of 150 participants was 50.75 years (SD = 10.10). Most of them were male (75.3%), employed (70.7%), and married (91.3%). More than 70% of the participants were diagnosed with nasopharyngeal carcinoma at a later stage (levels 3/4). On average, the time from the last radiotherapy to the diagnosis of RBI in the participants was 66.15 months (SD = 52.71). The mean number of radiotherapy sessions that participants received was 34.08 (SD = 11.78). The percentages of participants with coexisting hypertension and diabetes were 24% and 21.3%, respectively.

Mediation analysis

The mediation model and corresponding tests, shown in Tables 2 and 3, examined how stigma directly and indirectly influenced the relationship between SPB and QoL, adjusting for four covariates (Age, Number of radiotherapy sessions, Interval time, and Stage of NPC). The results showed that stigma had a significant indirect effect between SPB and QoL (effect = -0.152, 95% CI = -0.223 to -0.032). However, the direct effect of self-perceived burden on QoL was not statistically significant (effect = -0.083, 95% CI = -0.300 to 0.134). Therefore, the relationship between SPB and QoL was completely mediated by stigma. The paths of the relationship among these three variables were shown in Fig. 1.

After confirming the mediating role of stigma, we further did the moderated mediation analysis to analyze the moderating function of sleep quality. Table 4 showed the results of the regression model of moderated mediation analysis, and Table 5 showed the results of conditional indirect effect of the moderation of sleep quality. The indirect effect of SPB on QoL as mediated by stigma was apparent only among patients who experienced poorer sleep quality (higher PSQI scores, + 1 SD) (effect = -0.115; 95% CI = -0.246 to -0.021). Discussion

The study aimed to examine the role of stigma and sleep quality in the relationship between SPB and QoL in NPC patients with RBI. The key finding identified in this study was that stigma mediated the relationship between SPB and QoL, and sleep quality had a moderating role in this relationship, addressing the gap in QoL management research in NPC patients with RBI.

In this study, NPC patients with RBI showed high levels of SPB and stigma, poor sleep quality, and reduced QoL, indicating that clinical professionals should notice the management of mental health and stress to improve patients’ QoL. The poor QoL observed in this study was consistent with previous findings in NPC patients with RBI [5]. This underscored the importance of enhancing the QoL in this population. Most existing studies have explored the basic medicine and drug mechanisms of RBI among NPC patients, including predictive factors for side effects such as cerebrovascular disease and the progression of cognitive dysfunction [42, 43]. It is worth noting that very few studies have investigated mental health and personalized supportive care for these patients.

The study first discovered and indicated that stigma was a mediator of the association between SPB and QoL. This result contrasted with findings from breast cancer survivors, where SPB was found to mediate the relationship between stigma and QoL [44]. We interpreted that the reasons behind these two different paths might be the strong interaction correlation between stigma and SPB, both of which significantly affect QoL, and that the disease characteristics of breast cancer were unlike those of NPC patients with RBI. The typical process that most breast cancer survivors experienced includes changes in appearance and challenges related to returning to work and normal life [45]. However, in NPC patients with RBI, the brain injury following radiotherapy represents secondary trauma for the patients, even more so than the effects of minor changes to their appearance, as they need to undergo additional therapies and are uncertain about their recovery prospects. The second experience would make them feel stressed again, even more so than before, because their average age was 50 years and most of them were the main source of income for their families, bearing a lot of responsibilities for the household. When they were unable to contribute to the family and took on responsibilities as usual, they felt guilty. Similar to the findings of another study, this result indicates that when individuals feel responsible for their disease, they are more likely to encounter stigma and adverse emotional reactions [46].

A significant observation in this study was that the mediating role of stigma in the relationship between SPB and QoL was influenced by sleep quality, especially when sleep quality was poor. The relationship between sleep quality and stigma was identified in previous studies, which found that cancer stigma was positively associated with sleep disorders and negatively associated with sleep quality [47, 48]. Most existing studies focused on the effects of stigma on sleep quality instead of the opposite pathway in cancer patients [48, 49]. Our study emphasized the strong relationship between these two factors and indicated the significant role that sleep quality had in moderating the psychological level. Due to the strong association between sleep quality and mood as well as mental state, patients with poor sleep quality are likely to experience more negative emotions, which may further lead to psychological distress, such as stigma. The interaction mechanism between stigma and sleep quality among NPC patients with RBI could be interpreted that they might receive misperceptions of sleep disorders by others, which would perpetuate and intensify the stigma, and even be labeled as antisocial, lazy, or faking [50, 51], especially most of them were still employed. Poor sleep is associated with emotional dysregulation, heightened threat perception, and reduced coping resources. In patients with high SPB, poor sleep may amplify the internalization of stigma and negative self-appraisals, thereby exerting a stronger detrimental effect on QoL; this may explain why the indirect effect of SPB on QoL via stigma was only significant among those with poorer sleep quality. The discovery of this moderate pathway revealed the significance of focusing on sleep quality because of its effects on mental health and QoL among patients with this condition. Clinically, we highlight that routine assessment of sleep quality, along with SPB and stigma, may help identify patients at higher psychosocial risk; integrated interventions that address both sleep problems and stigma/SPB (e.g., psychoeducation, cognitive-behavioral therapy, and sleep hygiene or CBT-I) may be particularly beneficial for improving QoL in this population.

Study limitations

A few limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Firstly, due to the cross-sectional design of the study, it was not possible to confirm the directionality of the causal links between the variables. To confirm the associations found, future research should employ longitudinal studies. Secondly, we used convenience sampling from a single tertiary hospital in Guangzhou, this may limit the generalizability of our findings to other regions and healthcare settings, and this approach may introduce selection bias, as patients with milder symptoms or those not attending regular follow-up visits may be under-represented. Thirdly, the extent and range of adjustments for the variables are currently unclear, and it remains to be determined at what specific level sleep quality becomes a moderating factor for stigma. Fourthly, stigma fully mediated the relationship between SPB and QoL, meaning that SPB did not directly affect QoL. We need to interpret this outcome carefully because we cannot dismiss the direct effects that SPB may have on QoL. There may be some unavoidable factors and biases behind this outcome, including the lack of diverse backgrounds among participants and a limited sample size. Therefore, future research needs to address these limitations and clarify these issues.

Clinical implications

The outcomes of this study hold valuable relevance for clinical practice. The confirmed mediating effect of stigma on the relationship between SPB and QoL, moderated by sleep quality, offers valuable insights for healthcare professionals in creating and assessing effective interventions for NPC patients suffering from RBI. It is significant to note the significance of mental health management in this population. From the perspective of SPB, this approach seeks suitable intervention strategies from a mental health standpoint. The process evaluation will focus on reducing stigma associated with illness, ultimately aiming to enhance QoL. Additionally, regular assessments of sleep quality will be conducted to monitor its impact on stigma. This framework can assist clinical professionals in improving patients’ overall QoL.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study of NPC patients with radiation-induced brain injury, higher self-perceived burden and stigma, poorer sleep quality, and lower quality of life were observed. Stigma was associated with the link between self-perceived burden and quality of life, particularly among patients with poorer sleep quality. These results highlight the need for clinicians to assess self-perceived burden, stigma, and sleep quality of patients, and to consider integrated interventions targeting these factors to improve quality of life. Longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to further clarify these relationships and test targeted management strategies.
