Management of aneurysmal bone cysts of the spine and sacrum: A narrative review
Authors: Justin Hyde, Mark Ehioghae, Brock Adams, Robert Henshaw, Seyed Kalantar, Jonathan P Japa, Kevin Yoon, Aaron Phung, Sean Bae, Ala Alshomali, Jamie Lee, Addisu Mesfin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis narrative review summarizes contemporary diagnosis and management of aneurysmal bone cysts (ABCs) of the spine and sacrum, emphasizing axial-specific risks, decision thresholds, and adjunctive therapies. A PubMed/MEDLINE review was performed focusing on the last 10 years, with selective inclusion of earlier foundational studies. Search terms combined “aneurysmal bone cyst” with anatomy-specific modifiers (“spine,” “vertebral,” and “sacrum”) and domains relevant to axial care, including molecular diagnostics such as ubiquitin-specific peptidase 6, imaging, biopsy, differential diagnosis, operative strategies, embolization, percutaneous therapies, and recurrence. ABCs are benign but locally aggressive lesions that frequently involve posterior elements, extend toward the canal, and threaten neurologic function and stability. Imaging may demonstrate expansile osteolysis and fluid–fluid levels but lacks specificity in axial disease; biopsy remains essential to exclude malignant mimics and secondary ABC-like change. Management is individualized based on neurologic compromise, instability, deformity, and lesion extent. Surgical options range from intralesional curettage to more extensive excision with stabilization, while adjuncts such as selective arterial embolization, doxycycline sclerotherapy, and denosumab may support hemorrhage control and local control in selected cases. Recurrence remains most strongly associated with incomplete resection and younger age. Prospective, spine-specific comparative studies are needed to standardize treatment selection, define the sequencing of adjuncts, and clarify long-term outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Aneurysmal bone cysts (ABCs) are rare, benign but locally aggressive osteolytic lesions characterized by expansile destruction and blood-filled cystic spaces separated by fibrous septa.[12] The World Health Organization defines ABCs as expansile osteolytic lesions composed of blood-filled spaces partitioned by connective tissue septa containing osteoid and osteoclast-like giant cells.[34]

ABCs were historically considered reactive vascular malformations; most primary lesions are now recognized as clonal neoplasms driven by recurrent ubiquitin-specific peptidase 6 (USP6) rearrangements.[5] Secondary ABCs occur in association with other benign or malignant tumors, a distinction that is particularly relevant in axial disease where underlying pathology can alter evaluation and operative planning.[67]

Although most ABCs arise in long bones, vertebral involvement is clinically distinct.[18] Spinal lesions often originate in the posterior elements and may extend into the vertebral body or epidural space, where they can threaten neurologic function and stability.[36] Variant forms (solid, multifocal, extraosseous, and surface) may mimic malignancy radiographically and histologically, and biopsy is required when imaging suggests aggressive behavior.[59]

Spinal and sacral ABCs, therefore, differ from appendicular disease: mobile-segment expansion may cause collapse, deformity, or acute cord compression, while sacral lesions may produce bowel/bladder dysfunction, limit margin attainment, and require lumbopelvic stabilization.[38] Because the literature often aggregates axial and appendicular ABCs, spine-specific guidance remains limited.[58] This review focuses on the ABCs of the spine and sacrum, emphasizing neurologic risk, instability, and axial-specific management considerations.

METHODS

Using PubMed/MEDLINE, studies addressing the ABCs of the spine and sacrum were reviewed. Emphasis was placed on literature published within the last 10 years, reflecting advances in molecular diagnostics, imaging, minimally invasive therapies, and modern spinal instrumentation. Earlier studies were included selectively when foundational to classification systems or historical context.

Search terms included “Aneurysmal Bone Cysts” combined with anatomy-specific modifiers such as “Spine,” “Vertebral Column,” and “Sacrum.” Additional terms related to epidemiology, USP6, imaging, biopsy, differential diagnosis, operative management, embolization, treatment outcomes, and recurrence were incorporated as needed. English-language human studies – including case reports, case series, cohort studies, and reviews – were eligible.

Epidemiology

ABCs are rare benign tumors, accounting for 1%–6% of primary osseous neoplasms, with an annual incidence of approximately 0.14 per 100,000 individuals.[310] They predominantly affect children and adolescents, with peak incidence in the first two decades; in spine-specific cohorts, the mean age at diagnosis is 26 years (range: 6–64).[611] Sex distribution is variable, with near-equal representation or slight female predominance reported.[9]

Approximately 8%–30% of ABCs occur in the spine, representing up to 15% of primary spinal tumors.[36] The lumbar spine is most commonly affected (40%–45%), followed by the cervical (30%) and thoracic regions (25%–30%).[3] Lesions typically arise in the posterior elements with extension toward the vertebral body or canal, increasing the risk of neurologic compromise and instability.[61112]

Sacral lesions are less frequent but often present at a larger size and with greater cortical destruction and soft-tissue extension, complicating resection and increasing recurrence risk.[1314] Recurrence is more common in pediatric patients and after intralesional procedures.[611] Most recurrences occur within 2 years, and incomplete resection as well as secondary lesions are associated with increased likelihood of recurrence.[11]

Etiology

Osteolysis in ABC reflects neoplastic stromal activity with activation of the RANK–RANKL pathway, promoting osteoclast differentiation and progressive bone resorption.[5] The resulting effect is expansile osseous destruction, often rapid in progression. Prominent vascularity further drives lesion growth and contributes to intraoperative hemorrhage, a consideration of particular importance in spinal surgery.[3]

These biologic features arise from recurrent rearrangements of the USP6 gene on chromosome 17p13, establishing ABCs as clonal neoplasms.[1516] Promoter-swap translocations increase USP6 transcription while preserving the coding sequence.[1718] CDH11 is the most frequent fusion partner, although others – including USP9X and AHNAK – have been described, reflecting molecular heterogeneity across classic and solid.[1719] Recognition of these alterations shifted understanding away from reactive vascular models.[5]

Primary lesions arise de novo and harbor USP6 rearrangements; secondary ABCs develop in association with other bone tumors and typically lack these changes.[16] Osteoblastoma, giant cell tumor (GCT), and telangiectatic osteosarcoma (TOS) may show ABC-like features yet differ substantially in biologic behavior and treatment approach.[3] Accurate molecular or histopathologic differentiation, therefore, informs operative planning. Confined axial anatomy amplifies the consequences of osteolysis and vascular proliferation. Expansion through posterior elements or the vertebral body produces cortical thinning and instability, and neural compression may follow more readily than in appendicular sites.[36]

Clinical presentation and prognostic factors

Spinal ABCs typically present with progressive focal axial pain.[20] In a 42-patient series, 88.1% (37/42) reported segmental pain with a mean symptom duration of 5.6 months.[11] Mechanical compromise is common: 76.2% had Spinal Instability Neoplastic Score (SINS) 7–12 and 23.9% had SINS 13–18, indicating universal instability risk. Sacral ABCs are frequently multisegmental and large. A representative lesion extending L5–S4 measured 680 cm3 with sacral canal and pelvic extension.[21] In pooled sacral cohorts, mean tumor volumes reached 145.5 cm3, reinforcing the high structural burden of sacral disease.[21]

Neurologic involvement is common in spinal ABCs. In Zhao et al., 100% (42/42) were symptomatic, with sensory deficits (40.4%), motor weakness (16.7%), and sphincter dysfunction (9.6%).[11] Although most were Frankel D (83.3%), 15% were Frankel A–C, and postoperative Frankel A–C status predicted worse survival (P = 0.003). Sacral lesions more commonly produce radiculopathy, paresis, and bladder/bowel dysfunction due to canal and plexus involvement, particularly in multisegmental disease.[21]

Pathologic fracture occurred in 40.5% (17/42) of spinal ABCs and was associated with worse disease-free survival (DFS) on univariate analysis (P = 0.021).[11] Five-year DFS was 54.1%. En bloc resection (P = 0.004) and primary (vs. secondary) ABC status (P = 0.008) independently improved DFS. Incomplete cyst wall removal increased recurrence risk (P = 0.012). In a broader ABC cohort, recurrence occurred in 16.3% (14/86), and a preoperative neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio ≥ 2.054 conferred a 4.561-fold increased recurrence risk (odds ratio = 4.561; P < 0.001).[22] Sacral series report recurrence in up to 4/10 cases, reflecting margin limitations in multisegmental lesions.[21]

Imaging

Spinal ABCs account for approximately 10%–30% of all ABCs and most commonly originate in the posterior elements with possible vertebral body extension.[2324] On radiographs, lesions appear as expansile osteolytic masses with cortical thinning and thin sclerotic margins, often centered in the lamina or pedicle.[2425] X-rays, however, frequently underestimate lesion extent in axial disease, particularly when canal or sacral foraminal involvement is present.[2326]

Cross-sectional imaging provides diagnostic detail beyond that achievable with conventional radiography.[2728] Computed tomography (CT) better delineates cortical breach, pedicle involvement, vertebral body destruction, and multi-column extension, directly informing mechanical stability and operative planning.[232429] Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), in contrast, defines epidural extension and neural compression, typically demonstrating multiloculated cystic architecture with fluid–fluid levels.[242930] When cord edema develops, intramedullary T2/STIR hyperintensity may be present. In cases of vertebral height loss or collapse, imaging findings may prompt earlier stabilization because of cord compromise risk.[29] Postembolization studies can demonstrate cortical ossification and reduction of intracanal components, supporting structural follow-up.[24]

Fluid–fluid levels reflect hematocrit layering within blood-filled cystic spaces. This is characteristic but not pathognomonic.[25] Similar appearances may occur in TOS and other hemorrhagic lesions. Additionally, 23%–32% of ABCs arise secondary to other primary tumors, further limiting imaging specificity.[29] In spinal lesions, aggressive features such as soft-tissue extension or rapid progression cannot exclude malignancy on imaging alone.[2429] Therefore, histopathologic confirmation remains necessary.

Biopsy

Histologic confirmation is mandatory in spinal and sacral ABCs because imaging findings – cortical destruction, epidural extension, and canal compromise – may mimic malignant tumors such as TOS.[2425] Even when MRI demonstrates classic fluid–fluid levels, imaging alone cannot reliably exclude malignancy in axial lesions.[2529]

In reported thoracic spinal cases with cord compression, CT-guided biopsy was performed prior to embolization or definitive treatment, underscoring the necessity of tissue diagnosis despite characteristic imaging features.[24] Multidisciplinary spinal series similarly emphasize pretreatment histologic confirmation before selecting embolization, resection, or stabilization strategies.[29]

Biopsy of spinal ABCs is technically challenging due to hypervascularity and multiloculated blood-filled cavities that may yield nondiagnostic samples.[25] Lesions involving pedicles, posterior elements, or sacral foramina further increase procedural complexity and risk to adjacent neural structures.[2429]

For these reasons, image-guided core biopsy is preferred in spinal and sacral ABCs, and tissue diagnosis should precede definitive intervention in all cases with aggressive radiographic features or neurologic involvement.[2429]

Differential diagnosis

Telangiectatic osteosarcoma

TOS is the most important malignant mimic of spinal ABC. Both lesions may present as expansile osteolytic processes with cortical destruction and cystic architecture, limiting the diagnostic reliability of imaging alone.[31] Aggressive radiographic features, including extraosseous extension, may be seen in both benign and malignant entities and therefore cannot independently distinguish pathology.[32] Histologic evaluation remains essential, as ABC demonstrates benign fibrovascular septa and blood-filled spaces, whereas malignant tumors exhibit atypical cellular features and tumor-producing stroma.[31]

Giant cell tumor

GCT is a key diagnostic consideration in axial lesions suspected to represent ABC. GCTs are locally aggressive spinal tumors that may demonstrate imaging characteristics similar to ABC, including expansile bone destruction and soft-tissue extension.[32] Importantly, ABC may arise secondary to an underlying lesion, most commonly GCT, complicating radiographic interpretation.[33] Because biological behavior and recurrence risk are driven by the primary tumor, accurate histologic classification is critical.[33]

Osteoblastoma and secondary aneurysmal bone cyst changes

Osteoblastoma may coexist with or mimic ABC due to overlapping clinical and imaging findings, particularly in lesions involving posterior spinal elements.[34] Additionally, secondary ABC-like transformation may occur within benign or malignant tumors, producing multiloculated lesions indistinguishable from primary ABC on imaging.[5] Consequently, biopsy and comprehensive histopathologic assessment are required prior to definitive treatment planning in spinal and sacral disease.[3135]

Management principles for spinal and sacral aneurysmal bone cysts

Indications for intervention versus observation

Spinal ABCs commonly present with structural or neurologic compromise.[36] In a pediatric cohort (n = 24), 88% reported radicular pain, 50% had neurologic deficits, posterior element involvement occurred in 83%, and neural foraminal obstruction occurred in 58%.[37] Similar series confirm frequent axial structural instability at diagnosis.[29]

Overall recurrence was 21% (median 8 months), predominantly following subtotal resection.[37] Given the high rate of neurologic involvement and early recurrence, observation is generally reserved for asymptomatic, radiographically stable lesions without canal compromise.[3738]

Role of embolization and minimally invasive therapies

Selective arterial embolization (SAE) was performed in 33% of pediatric cases, primarily to reduce intraoperative blood loss in hypervascular lesions.[37] Additional spine-specific series support embolization as an adjunct within multimodal management.[2939]

Percutaneous and adjuvant therapies have demonstrated radiographic stabilization in selected cases; however, durability in axial disease remains variable.[3740] These strategies are therefore best considered adjunctive in lesions causing structural compromise.

When surgery is indicated

Surgical intervention is indicated for neurologic compromise, mechanical instability, or progressive deformity. In a pediatric series, 96% underwent surgery; gross total resection was achieved in 61%, and fusion was required in 70%.[37] Recurrence occurred exclusively after subtotal resection, reinforcing the importance of maximal safe resection when feasible.[3738] In sacral disease, multilevel involvement and neural compression further support decompression with stabilization.[3941]

Operative management

Surgical management in spinal and sacral ABCs must balance tumor control with preservation of neurologic function and mechanical stability. Indications include neurologic compromise, progressive deformity, structural instability, pathologic fracture, or failure of minimally invasive therapies. Posterior element destruction and canal compromise frequently necessitate decompression with stabilization.[3942]

While intralesional curettage remains widely used, incomplete removal of the cyst lining is associated with recurrence rates near 19% in spinal series.[43] Total excision lowers recurrence risk but may create instability requiring instrumented fusion.[4344] Operative strategy should therefore be individualized based on column involvement and sacral extension.

Surgical techniques

Intralesional curettage

Curettage with cavity filling (cement, ceramics, or graft) remains common.[43] Hypervascularity may limit visualization and increase the risk of incomplete resection. Cement augmentation can provide hemostasis and structural reinforcement in thoracolumbar lesions.[45]

Minimally invasive and endoscopic techniques

Endoscopic approaches, including percutaneous endoscopic lumbar techniques, permit intralesional curettage through small incisions with irrigation and limited blood loss (<10 mL reported).[43] These methods may preserve posterior elements and avoid fusion in select lumbar cases, though incomplete resection remains a concern.[4346]

En bloc resection and stabilization

For aggressive or recurrent multi-column lesions, en bloc resection offers the lowest recurrence risk but carries higher morbidity.[4344] Instrumented stabilization is frequently required when posterior elements or vertebral bodies are resected.[42]

Sacral lesions add complexity due to neural root preservation and pelvic biomechanics, sometimes necessitating combined anterior–posterior approaches in extensive disease.[39]

Intraoperative adjuncts

Adjunctive therapies in axial ABCs aim to reduce excessive blood loss, improve local control, and preserve neurologic and structural integrity.

Preoperative embolization

SAE is frequently used to decrease intraoperative blood loss and, in selected sacral lesions, as staged therapy. Sacral ABCs supplied by internal iliac branches have demonstrated progressive ossification and durable symptom control following embolization.[21] In extensive sacral disease, embolization may reduce surgical morbidity or limit the need for complex reconstruction.[2141]

Doxycycline sclerotherapy

Percutaneous doxycycline sclerotherapy has demonstrated stabilization or symptom improvement in more than half of treated mobile spine cases, with recurrence rates below 12% in selected cohorts.[2947] Doxycycline suppresses osteoclast-mediated resorption and promotes gradual ossification.[47] CT-guided injection in cervical and sacral lesions has shown radiographic consolidation, particularly when combined with embolization.[48] However, sclerotherapy does not replace decompression in the presence of neurologic compromise or instability.[29]

Denosumab

Targeting RANKL-mediated osteoclast activity, denosumab has been used in large sacral lesions not amenable to resection. Reported cases demonstrate early symptom improvement, cortical reconstitution, and reduction in giant cells.[49] It serves as a biologic adjunct in anatomically constrained lesions.

Radiation therapy

Radiation therapy has been described for unresectable or recurrent spinal ABCs but is limited by risks of secondary malignancy and radiation-induced myelopathy.[5051] It remains a salvage option when other strategies fail.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS AND RESEARCH GAPS

Despite the growing body of literature on the management of ABCs of the spine and sacrum, significant gaps remain in standardizing treatment strategies. Many reports base treatment choice on factors such as neurologic involvement, instability, lesion characteristics, and technical feasibility; however, no consensus or standardized criteria exist to guide the selection between surgical and nonsurgical approaches or to stratify patients by recurrence risk and complication rates.

Future research should aim to establish objective criteria to guide patient selection and treatment modality, particularly in predicting recurrence risk and determining candidacy for surgical versus conservative management. Additionally, further studies are needed to evaluate the optimal sequencing or combination of newer agents, including bisphosphonates, denosumab, and doxycycline.[23] The long-term efficacy, functional outcomes, and neurological status following minimally invasive treatments remain inadequately characterized, largely due to variable follow-up durations and underreporting of late complications.[52] Moreover, standardized follow-up protocols and long-term outcome data for newer therapeutic agents are lacking and warrant prospective investigation.

CONCLUSION

ABCs of the spine and sacrum differ fundamentally from appendicular lesions. Although histologically benign, axial ABCs frequently involve posterior elements, extend into the spinal canal, and threaten neurologic function and structural stability. Their management therefore requires not only tumor control but also preservation of neural elements and maintenance of spinal biomechanics.

Molecular advances, including the identification of USP6 rearrangements, have improved diagnostic precision and differentiation from malignant mimics. However, treatment strategy in spinal and sacral disease must be individualized. Options now include embolization, percutaneous doxycycline sclerotherapy, biologic therapies such as denosumab, and operative approaches ranging from intralesional curettage to en bloc resection with instrumentation. Incomplete resection carries recurrence risk, whereas aggressive resection may necessitate fusion and complex reconstruction.
