Do Thinking Tokens Help with Safety?
Narutatsu Ri, Abhishek Panigrahi, Sanjeev Arora

Abstract
Today’s reasoning models use thinking tokens to attain stronger performance on benchmarks than their instruction-tuned counterparts. It is also generally believed that this more “deliberative” mode should improve alignment and safety, by providing the model a safe space to consider whether its planned answer to a request violates its safety principles. We present evidence that this intuition is not always correct. Across frontier open-weight reasoning models spanning GPT-OSS, Qwen, Olmo, and Phi families, we find that the eventual refusal/compliance outcome is already strongly predictable via a trained head on the first token’s hidden representation ( 0.84 0.84 – 0.95 0.95 AUROC and ∼ 88 % \sim\!88\% balanced accuracy for predicting refusal/compliance) before any visible thinking. The thinking process turns out to be more akin to prefix completion than to deliberative revision, with the final outcome rarely changing after the first ∼ 20 % \sim\!20\% of thinking, despite giving the appearance of deliberation at the text level ( ∼ 74 % \sim\!74\% of text-level deliberations occur when the response distribution is already locked to one refusal/compliance side). We also find that existing inference-time and training-based safety interventions, despite being motivated by the goal of inducing deliberation, largely shift model behavior toward over-refusal while suppressing already-scarce deliberation signals. Our results suggest that safety behavior in current reasoning models is much less deliberative than commonly assumed, and highlight the need for methods that induce real safety deliberation. 1 1 1 Code is available at github.com/narutatsuri/lrm_safety_deliberation .

Introduction

Large reasoning models (LRMs) are trained to “think” before answering by generating a long reasoning trace before producing a final response.
This use of inference-time compute has led to substantial capability gains on many verifiable tasks, such as math and coding
(
51
;
26
;
63
;
43
;
12
)
.
Excitingly, the broader appeal of reasoning is that these capability gains appear to extend beyond verifiable tasks, with growing evidence of improvements in domains without directly verifiable rewards
(
44
;
56
;
42
;
16
)
.

An important line of research asks whether this reasoning capability can improve safety in the same way that it improves performance
(
47
;
64
)
.
Safety is commonly evaluated with two metrics that are in tension:
Attack Success Rate
(ASR), the rate at which a model complies with harmful prompts, and
Over-Refusal Rate
(ORR), the rate at which a model incorrectly refuses benign prompts.
Importantly, a safe and helpful model should simultaneously attain low ASR and ORR
(
24
)
.
A natural belief is that allowing the model to think longer should improve this tradeoff, as models may use the reasoning process to recognize harmful requests while preserving helpfulness on benign inputs.
This intuition is appealing, and indeed underlies several prominent rule- and specification-based safety defenses
(
5
;
14
)
.

Yet, there are strong reasons to question this hypothesis.
Recent work
(
41
;
32
;
62
)
shows that safety alignment in instruction-tuned models is often shallow, with behavior largely determined by a small number of initial tokens and thus easily circumvented through simple early token manipulations.
Even so, it would be natural to expect reasoning models to behave differently for the better; after all, extended thinking often changes and improves answers on many tasks, so the thinking trace should seem likely to contribute directly to better safety decisions.
This motivates the following natural question:

Does thinking truly improve safety decision making in reasoning models?

Given that many defense methods try to improve safety by leveraging thinking processes
(
18
;
23
)
,
a related question naturally arises:

How much do existing defenses meaningfully improve both the ASR–ORR tradeoff and safety deliberation capability of reasoning models?

Our Contributions.

We provide evidence that safety behavior in current LRMs may be far less deliberative than the reasoning paradigm suggests.
First, we present a surprising finding that a model’s eventual refusal/compliance outcome is already strongly decodable with a trained head even before any visible thinking content is generated (§
2.2
).
Across four frontier open-weight models, we find that a linear probe on the first token’s representation in the thinking trace separates final refusals from compliances with AUROC 0.84–0.95 and up to
88\%
balanced accuracy.
Notably, the identity of the generated token itself is far less predictive, highlighting that this signal is encoded in the hidden representation rather than the surface token.

Second, we investigate the role of thinking in the safety decision process and find that its impact is limited.
Namely, thinking does not meaningfully contribute to improved safety decisions; instead, once a short thinking prefix is fixed, later continuations tend to preserve the refusal/compliance trajectory established early in the thinking trace (§
3.1
).
Further, while thinking traces do exhibit sentence-level patterns that resemble safety deliberation at the text level, these segments turn out to have minimal influence on the final response (§
3.2
).

Third, we study how existing defenses change the reasoning behavior of LRMs by evaluating several inference-time
(
19
;
40
;
23
)
and training-based
(
49
;
18
;
65
;
30
;
58
;
52
)
methods.
We find that inference-time methods either have limited impact or reduce ASR at the cost of higher ORR, while also suppressing the already weak deliberation signals (§
4.1
).
This pattern is consistent with the prefix-continuation behavior observed in §
3.1
, where later thinking rarely steers the model away from early refusal/compliance trajectories.
In contrast, training-based methods induce larger behavioral shifts, but do so by pushing models toward the same over-refusal pattern and do not meaningfully increase deliberation in the thinking trace (§
4.2
).
Our findings highlight the need for new training objectives that reward true deliberation rather than merely encouraging models to generate safety-flavored reasoning.

2.2
Safety Decisions are Decodable Before Visible Reasoning

We first show that the representation of the first token in the thinking trace encodes a refusal/compliance signal that corresponds surprisingly well to the final decision.
We do so by probing the directional geometry of last-layer hidden states at each token on refusal and compliance thinking traces separately.
For each model, we generate one completion per prompt in the full ASR pool and expanded ORR pool of §
2.1
, label each completion’s final response using the four-guardrail vote, and separate instances into refusal and compliance groups within each pool.

We consider two measures at each position
t
between refusal and compliance groups.
First, we use a trace-normalized Fisher discriminant ratio to compute the distance between the two groups’ mean representations, which measures group distinguishability modulo within-class variability:

J(t)\;=\;\big(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{R}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C}(t)\big)^{\!\top}\,\big(\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}(t)\big)^{-1}\boldsymbol{I}\,\big(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{R}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C}(t)\big),

where
R
and
C
respectively denote the refusal and compliance groups,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{R}(t),\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C}(t)
are class-conditional means of
\mathbf{h}_{t}
, and
\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}(t)
is the trace of the within-class covariance
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}(t)
, i.e., the within-class variance.
More concretely,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{R}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{R}\mathbf{h}_{t};\quad\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{C}\mathbf{h}_{t};\quad

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{W}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{C}(\mathbf{h}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C}(t))(\mathbf{h}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{C}(t))^{\top}+\mathbb{E}_{R}(\mathbf{h}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{R}(t))(\mathbf{h}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{R}(t))^{\top}.

Second, we train an
\ell_{2}
-regularized logistic regression probe on the first thinking-token representation
\mathbf{h}_{0}
across both harmful and benign prompts to predict the guardrail-majority-vote’s label on the model’s final response.
We standardize each feature dimension and project onto the top
100
principal components of
\mathbf{h}_{0}
, fitting both within each training fold of a 5-fold stratified cross-validation that yields one out-of-fold refusal probability for every example.
We report two metrics on these held-out probabilities: AUROC, a threshold-free rank-order measure of class separability, and balanced accuracy (BAcc), a thresholded measure of probe accuracy.
For BAcc, each held-out fold is binarized using a Youden-
J
threshold chosen from out-of-fold predictions on the other four folds, ensuring the threshold is selected without train-test contamination and accounts for class imbalance.
See §
B
for additional details.

Importantly, the early readout result in §

In general, however, the thinking process should be able to affect the model’s final behavior, rather than simply rationalize a decision already evident from the early representations.
Here, we investigate the extent to which this is possible in current reasoning models.

3.1
How Does Thinking Contribute to the Safety Decision?

A follow-up question from §
2.2
is what role the thinking trace plays in the decision.
Here, we provide evidence that for current models, it largely serves as post-hoc rationalization.
For each prompt, we sample
M{=}8
independent thinking traces and form prefixes at budgets
B\in\{20\%,40\%,60\%,80\%,100\%\}
of the natural trace length by truncating each trace at the corresponding fraction.
We sample
N{=}8
final responses conditioned on each prefix and label the responses accordingly with guardrails.
Given a fixed prompt and budget
B
, let
z_{m,B,n}\in\{0,1\}
denote whether the
n
-th final response from sampled thinking prefix
m
with budget
B
is labeled as a refusal, and let

q_{m,B}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}z_{m,B,n}
be the refusal rate after conditioning on that prefix.
We measure the following form of variance, which captures how frequently different continuations from the same prefix lead to different outcomes:

\displaystyle\mathrm{ContinuationVariance}(B)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{m}\!\left[4q_{m,B}(1-q_{m,B})\right],

where the factor of
4
normalizes Bernoulli variance so that the maximum possible variance is
1
.
We pool results across both ASR and ORR pools and examine per-model results for each budget
B
.

3.2
Do Individual Sentences Steer Thinking Meaningfully?

§
3.1
suggests that the thinking trace often has limited effect on an outcome already decodable early on in the generation.
Then, what function does the text in the thinking trace serve?

To quantify this, we analyze safety deliberation at the sentence level. For 250 randomly sampled prompts each from the ASR and ORR pool (500 prompts in total), we sample
M{=}4
independent thinking rollouts and segment each into sentences
c_{1:T}
. We use three frontier models (GPT-5.4, Gemini 3 Pro, and Sonnet 4.6) to assign each sentence a stance label in
\{-1,0,+1\}
, corresponding to refusal-leaning, neutral, and compliance-leaning text, respectively.
6
6

6

See §
C.3
for an evaluation of inter-annotator agreement among frontier models.

We first measure the fraction of rollouts that exhibit at least one
oscillation
, i.e., a change in stance from refusal-leaning to compliance-leaning or vice versa, taken over all
M
rollouts of all prompts.
On rollouts where we find oscillations, we then measure whether each oscillation has a statistically significant effect on the final response.
For each oscillation, let
i_{\mathrm{pre}}
denote the index of the last sentence with the previous stance, and let
i_{\mathrm{post}}
denote the index of the last sentence with the new stance.
We define the corresponding prefixes of the thinking trace as

x^{\mathrm{pre}}=c_{1:i_{\mathrm{pre}}}

and

x^{\mathrm{post}}=c_{1:i_{\mathrm{post}}}.

We estimate the refusal rates at
q_{x^{\mathrm{pre}}}
and
q_{x^{\mathrm{post}}}
by truncating the thinking trace at each and using
K{=}100
final-response generations.
The effect of the segment between
i_{\mathrm{pre}}
and
i_{\mathrm{post}}
is then quantified using its
stance-aligned shift
:

\Delta\hat{q}_{i_{\mathrm{pre}}:i_{\mathrm{post}}}=s_{\mathrm{post}}\left(q_{x^{\mathrm{post}}}-q_{x^{\mathrm{pre}}}\right),

where
s_{\mathrm{post}}=+1
for compliance
\rightarrow
refusal transitions and
s_{\mathrm{post}}=-1
for refusal
\rightarrow
compliance transitions.
By construction,
\Delta\hat{q}\in[-1,1]
, and positive values indicate that the intervening segment shifts the final response distribution in the direction implied by the new stance.

Depending on the values of
q_{x^{\mathrm{pre}}}
and
q_{x^{\mathrm{post}}}
, we characterize each oscillation along two axes.
First, the oscillation is “locked” if
q_{x^{\mathrm{pre}}}
is saturated (
\leq 0.05
or
\geq 0.95
), i.e., the majority of
K
rollouts already collapse to one label.
Second, for each oscillation we measure whether it imposes a statistically significant change in the final response distribution between
x^{\mathrm{pre}}
and
x^{\mathrm{post}}
. Statistically significant oscillations are termed
meaningful
, while others are denoted as
performative
.
See §
B
for additional evaluation details.

4
What Do Existing Defenses Do?

The results in §
2
suggest that safety outcomes are often strongly decodable from the first token’s representation, and that additional thinking does not reliably improve both ASR and ORR.
We now ask whether existing safety defenses change this picture along two axes: whether they improve the ASR–ORR tradeoff, and whether they increase the influence of thinking traces as measured by sentence-level oscillation analysis.
We examine two classes of practical defenses: inference-time interventions and training-based defenses based on supervised fine-tuning (SFT) or reinforcement learning (RL).

4.1
Inference-Based Defenses

Inference-time defenses provide a direct test to whether LRM safety can be improved by simply intervening on the thinking trace itself.
Most existing defenses are of this form, and do so by inserting (off-policy) safety-relevant text at selected positions during generation:
SafePath ZS

(
19
)
prepends a short safety primer at the start of the reasoning block;
PSR

(
40
)
interleaves generation with periodic self-reflection checkpoints;
SafeRemind

(
23
)
dynamically inserts safety-reminding phrases during thinking when entropy indicates a decision-locking point.
7
7

7

See Appendix
B.2
for further details on implementation.

The common premise is that injecting safety considerations into the trace should “activate” the model’s safety deliberation capabilities and steer subsequent reasoning
(
23
)
.

However, §
3
suggests a more pessimistic view: If LRMs do not effectively use thinking for safety deliberation, such interventions should either have little effect (§
3.2
) or, at best, push models toward over-refusal by forcing them to complete a thinking block that cautions against compliance (§
3.1
).
We thus evaluate ASR and ORR for all defenses and report them in Table
2
.
The results are consistent with this hypothesis: inference-time defenses either lower ASR at the cost of higher ORR or have limited effect.
The tradeoff is most pronounced for Olmo-3-7B-Think; for the remaining models, the effects are weaker but similarly trade harmful-prompt safety against benign-prompt helpfulness.

We further examine what happens at the sentence-level compared to the base model; do non-performative segments increase under inference-time defenses?
We apply the analysis from §
3.2
(
M{=}4
traces, 250 instances from ASR/ORR pools) to inference-time defenses and find that they do not; see Table
3
.
Notably, both the average number of total oscillations per trace and meaningful oscillations decrease sharply, with drops up to
95\%
for Phi-4-Reasoning and over
60\%
on both metrics for Olmo-3-7B-Think under all three defenses.
For these two models, meaningful oscillation counts decrease at least as fast as total oscillation counts.
Overall, this suggests that inference-time defenses may be a less promising lever for safety deliberation in reasoning models, as they improve ASR by suppressing already sparse deliberation rather than making the trace more deliberative.

4.2
Training-Based Defenses

Training-based defenses are a more direct approach to modifying the model’s safety behavior than inference-time interventions.
We consider defenses mainly from two classes of methods.
The first are supervised fine-tuning (SFT) methods that center around training the model on well-curated gold reasoning traces:

•

STAR-1

(
49
)
constructs a 1K-example policy-grounded safety-reasoning dataset.

•

R1-ACT

(
18
)
fine-tunes on traces that explicitly insert a harmfulness-assessment step to activate latent safety knowledge.

•

SafeKey

(
65
)
augments safety-trace SFT with auxiliary objectives targeting the emergence of a “safety aha” key sentence.

•

ThinkSafe

(
30
)
uses refusal steering to elicit in-distribution safety traces from the model itself before fine-tuning on those self-generated responses.

The second are reinforcement learning (RL)-based or preference-optimization methods that further optimize the model using preference or process-level safety signals:

•

STAIR

(
58
)
combines structured safety reasoning with iterative preference optimization and process-reward-guided search.

•

RAPO

(
52
)
constructs preference pairs that favor reasoning traces whose amount and granularity of safety analysis match the prompt’s risk level, and optimizes the model to produce more risk-appropriate safety reasoning.

We include further implementation details for each method in Appendix
B.3
.

Training-Based Defenses Do Not Improve Deliberativeness in Reasoning Traces.

Beyond the ASR–ORR tradeoff, we also examine how training reshapes the model’s safety deliberation mechanism.
We do so by looking at how the first-token probe readability of §

On-policy methods (ThinkSafe, STAIR) turn out to preserve first-token readability but reduce meaningful and performative oscillations alike, further weakening deliberation.
Off-policy methods (STAR-1, SafeKey) instead suppress the early refusal/compliance signal (AUROC

Overall, training-based defenses can substantially alter model behavior, but neither family consistently improves the ASR–ORR tradeoff or make the visible thinking trace more deliberative.

5
Discussion

We provide evidence that current LRMs do not effectively use thinking for safety deliberation, and that existing defenses may not induce such deliberation reliably.
We show that the hidden representation of the first thinking token already strongly encodes a refusal/compliance decision and that this prediction closely matches the model’s final response.
We also find that thinking does not consistently improve safety decisions, and seemingly deliberative thinking segments exert limited influence on the final outcome.
Moreover, many existing defenses primarily steer models toward more refusal-leaning behavior, often increasing over-refusal.
Below, we discuss related work, limitations, and future directions.

Related Works.

More broadly, our work contributes to the growing literature on the safety properties of large reasoning models and reasoning-based alignment strategies
(
47
;
64
;
14
)
.
Our findings connect to several recent lines of work on the safety and interpretability of instruction-tuned and reasoning language models, which we discuss below.
We further defer additional discussions to Appendix §
A
.

First, several prior works have shown that safety alignment in instruction-tuned language models can be surprisingly shallow, with refusal behavior often determined by a small number of early tokens and vulnerable to simple prefix manipulations
(
41
;
32
;
62
;
54
)
.
Our work extends these observations to reasoning models.
In particular, we show that refusal/compliance behavior is already strongly decodable at the beginning of the thinking trace, and current reasoning models make only limited use of their thinking capabilities for safety deliberation.

Second, our work is closely related to studies on the faithfulness of chain-of-thought reasoning.
In reasoning tasks,
7
and
9
show that, for easier questions, the final answer can often be decoded from hidden representations of the initial tokens, suggesting that later reasoning tokens may partially rationalize an answer already decodable early on.
However, these effects become substantially weaker on harder questions.
Our work provides complementary evidence in the context of safety: although reasoning traces may contain apparent safety deliberation, these intermediate deliberative segments exert only limited influence on the final response.

Limitations.

Our study has two limitations. First, we exclusively focus on open-weight LRMs of moderate sizes, which calls into question whether significantly larger reasoning models exhibit the same behavior.
However, prior work suggests that scale alone should not be assumed to resolve safety failures as they find that larger models can remain unreliable under safety-relevant conditions, and that refusal robustness under adversarial pressure is not guaranteed by model size
(
66
;
3
;
48
)
.
Second, our evaluation is also limited to refusal/compliance behavior on harmful and benign prompt sets.
This captures the central ASR–ORR trade-off, but does not cover all dimensions of safety, such as factuality, deception, privacy, or multi-turn behavior.

Future Work.

While §
2
shows that refusal/compliance behavior is strongly decodable at the first token, it is not immediately clear where in the training process this originates.
Understanding whether it arises during pretraining, instruction tuning, or post-training is an important avenue for investigation.
From §
4
, we observe that simply supervising on gold traces, mixing in both harmful and benign data, or optimizing with respect to final labels may be insufficient to induce true safety deliberation.
More optimistically, §
3.2
suggests that the desired behavior is rare but present, opening the possibility of training objectives that reward real deliberation.
We leave both as promising directions for future work.

Broader Impacts and Ethics Statement

This work aims to improve the reliability of safety mechanisms in large reasoning models by clarifying how refusal and compliance decisions emerge during thinking. A potential positive impact is that these findings may help guide the development of models that better distinguish harmful from benign requests, reducing harmful compliance without unnecessarily refusing safe user requests. However, the analysis could also have negative impacts if misused: showing that safety decisions are often encoded early in the reasoning process, and that current defenses may primarily shift models toward refusal rather than improve safety discrimination, could inform attempts to design stronger jailbreaks or evade existing safeguards. We mitigate these risks by focusing on aggregate empirical measurements over existing models and standard benchmarks, rather than releasing new attack datasets, jailbreak procedures, or model checkpoints. We also frame the results around defensive goals, namely the need for training methods that make thinking traces genuinely useful for safety deliberation rather than post-hoc rationalization.

Related Works

In this section, we provide additional discussion on works that are closely connected to our work.

Reasoning and Deliberative Alignment.

Recent advances in large reasoning models (LRMs) have shown that allocating inference-time compute through chain-of-thought or thinking traces can substantially improve performance on difficult reasoning tasks
[
51
,
26
,
63
,
43
,
12
]
.
These developments have motivated the broader hypothesis that reasoning may also improve alignment and safety by enabling models to deliberate before producing a final response.
Several recent works explicitly build on this intuition. Prominent examples include Constitutional AI
[
5
]
and deliberative alignment methods
[
14
]
, which train models to reason over explicit safety principles before making safety-critical decisions.
Following these developments, an important question in the community has been whether safety-trained models can perform robust safety deliberation across diverse settings, and more broadly, how we can make reasoning robust enough to handle diverse and adversarial scenarios.

Jailbreaks and Robustness of Safety Alignment.

Although our work does not directly study jailbreak attacks on language models, such attacks are closely related to the question of whether reasoning models can robustly deliberate about safety. A large body of work has shown that safety alignment can be bypassed through carefully designed prompting strategies. Early works demonstrated that instruction-tuned models are vulnerable to jailbreak attacks based on role-play prompting, contextual reframing (e.g., hypothetical or past-tense requests), and prompt injection attacks
[
50
,
39
]
. Subsequent works developed many-shot in-context jailbreaks, multi-turn conversational attacks, and optimization-based jailbreak strategies
[
3
,
17
,
31
,
34
,
67
,
33
,
55
,
35
]
. An important open direction is to understand how such attacks interact with large reasoning models that can think. In particular, it remains unclear whether longer thinking enables models to better deliberate about harmful requests and resist adversarial manipulations, or whether these attacks simply steer the model toward harmful behavior early in the thinking process itself. Our work takes a first step toward this question by studying how refusal/compliance decisions emerge within the internal reasoning traces of large reasoning models.

Shallow Safety Alignment.

Closely related to our work, several recent studies suggest that safety alignment in instruction-tuned language models can be surprisingly shallow.

41
show that refusal behavior may depend heavily on a small number of early tokens and can often be bypassed through simple prefix manipulations.
Similarly,
62
demonstrate that weakly aligned models can be leveraged to jailbreak stronger models, while
32
show that adversarial steering of early generations can substantially alter the model’s safety behavior.
More recently,
54
study the representation geometry of reasoning models and show that many poorly aligned reasoning models correctly identify harmful prompts and maintain strong refusal intentions throughout most of the thinking process, but experience a sharp drop in refusal scores near the final tokens before response generation.
Our work complements these findings by showing that refusal/compliance behavior is already strongly encoded at the beginning of the thinking process itself, and that subsequent thinking often has limited influence on the final decision.

Mechanistic Studies of Chain-of-Thought Reasoning.

One closely related line of work seeks to mechanistically understand the role of chain-of-thought reasoning in language models and how reasoning traces connect to the model’s final response
[
6
,
27
,
13
]
.
Closely related to our findings are the recent works of
7
and
9
, which show that for many easier reasoning problems, the final answer can often be decoded from hidden representations of the initial tokens, suggesting that later reasoning tokens may partially rationalize an already-determined answer.
However, these effects become substantially weaker on harder reasoning tasks.
Our work provides complementary evidence in the safety setting, where we show that refusal/compliance behavior is already strongly encoded at the beginning of the thinking trace and that later thinking often has limited influence on the final safety decision.

Tangentially related to our work is a growing literature questioning the faithfulness of chain-of-thought reasoning, showing that generated reasoning traces need not faithfully reflect the internal computation responsible for the model’s final answer
[
46
,
29
,
4
,
7
]
.
Consistent with these observations, our findings in §
3.1
show that even when individual segments of the thinking trace appear deliberative, the model often remains strongly biased toward its initial decision.

Youden’s
J
Threshold.

AUROC evaluates whether final-refusal examples receive higher probe scores than final-compliance examples, but it does not choose a binary decision threshold. To report balanced accuracy, we must convert the probe’s refusal probability into a refusal/compliance prediction. A fixed cutoff such as
0.5
is arbitrary and can be misleading under class imbalance, since the probe may be well-ranked but poorly calibrated. We therefore choose the cutoff using Youden’s
J
, defined for a cutoff
c
as

J(c)=\mathrm{sensitivity}(c)+\mathrm{specificity}(c)-1,

where sensitivity is the refusal-match rate and specificity is the compliance-match rate. Maximizing
J
is equivalent to maximizing balanced accuracy on the threshold-selection data.

In our setting, the logistic probe is evaluated with 5-fold stratified cross-validation, producing one out-of-fold refusal probability for every example. For each held-out fold
k
, we select the Youden-
J
cutoff using only the out-of-fold probabilities from the other four folds, and then apply that cutoff to fold
k
. This ensures that each example is evaluated with both a probe score and a cutoff selected without using that example. We then pool the resulting binary predictions across folds and report refusal-match, compliance-match, and their average as balanced accuracy.

follows a Student’s

We report a change as significant at level

C.3
Effect of Thinking

More Permissive No-Think-to-Think Change Measures.

The majority-label flip measure in §
3.1
captures whether thinking changes the dominant input-level decision, but does not credit small shifts in the desired direction unless they cross the majority threshold.
We therefore consider two more permissive measures of the no-think to think change.
For each input, let
q_{\mathrm{NT}}
and
q_{\mathrm{T}}
respectively denote the estimated refusal rates under the no-think and think conditions.
We orient the difference so that positive values always indicate movement toward the desired behavior:

\Delta_{\mathrm{desired}}=\begin{cases}q_{\mathrm{T}}-q_{\mathrm{NT}},&\text{ASR inputs},\\
q_{\mathrm{NT}}-q_{\mathrm{T}},&\text{ORR inputs}.\end{cases}

The first measure binarizes this signed shift and counts an input as moving in the desired direction whenever
\Delta_{\mathrm{desired}}>0
and in the undesired direction whenever
\Delta_{\mathrm{desired}}<0
; this is the most lenient directional criterion, as any nonzero improvement is counted.
The second measure examines the magnitude of the shift and reports the mean and standard deviation of
\Delta_{\mathrm{desired}}
over inputs.

See Figure
8
for the results.
Phi-4-Reasoning shows a mild exception, but both measures largely support the same qualitative conclusion as the majority-label flip analysis and corroborate the main takeaway.

Extended Thinking and ASR/ORR.

In §
3.1
, we found that continuation variance given a fixed prefix is very low and that enabling thinking does not improve model behavior overall.
Here, we ask whether the ASR–ORR tradeoff changes when we control the amount of thinking more directly.
We evaluate both limited thinking budgets and extended thinking beyond the model’s natural stopping point.
For the latter, we mask the end-of-thinking token and continue sampling from the remaining distribution until the extra budget is exhausted.

See Figure
9
for the results.
Although GPT-OSS-20B improves ASR with additional thinking, this tends to come at the cost of increasing ORR.
While the remaining models show the opposite failure mode, we see a trend where more thinking increases compliance, consistent with the no-think versus think results in Figure
4
.
Furthermore, forcing models to think past their natural stopping point does not meaningfully change behavior.
Overall, we find weak evidence that more thinking improves the ASR–ORR tradeoff.

Observe that no defended cell improves on its base model in both metrics at once across all nine defenses applied to the four base models; every cell that lowers ASR does so by raising ORR, a smaller number instead trade higher ASR for lower ORR, and a handful are worse than the base on both axes.
