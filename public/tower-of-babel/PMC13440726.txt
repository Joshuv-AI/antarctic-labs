Effectiveness of Interventions for Interpersonal Functioning Among Adolescents With Posttraumatic Stress Symptoms: A Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Sabrina Ee‐Ying Ong, Sayedhabibollah Ahmadi Forooshani, Zahra Izadikhah, Govind Krishnamoorthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionAdolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms often experience interpersonal difficulties; however, the effectiveness of existing interventions in improving interpersonal functioning remains unclear. This meta‐analysis aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of current interventions on interpersonal functioning among adolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms.MethodsA systematic search identified 17 studies comprising 35 therapeutic effect sizes that met the inclusion criteria. Effect sizes were synthesised using meta‐analytic procedures, and potential moderators, including intervention type, mode, and number of sessions, were examined.ResultsThe aggregated post‐intervention effect size was small but statistically significant (g = 0.39, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.28, 0.49]). Intervention type and mode significantly moderated outcomes. Trauma‐Focused Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (TF‐CBT) demonstrated the largest effect size, while mixed intervention formats involving both individual and conjoint or group sessions showed greater efficacy than group‐only approaches. At the 3‐month follow‐up, based on six effect measurements, the aggregated effect size was medium (g = 0.54, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.28, 0.81]). Although the number of sessions did not significantly moderate post‐intervention outcomes, interventions with more sessions were associated with stronger follow‐up effects.ConclusionsCurrent interventions demonstrate small post‐intervention and medium follow‐up effects on interpersonal functioning. However, the findings highlight a lack of robust and consistent evidence regarding the adequacy and long‐term effectiveness of existing interventions. Further high‐quality research is needed to examine interventions that directly target trauma‐related mechanisms influencing interpersonal functioning and to rigorously evaluate their long‐term effectiveness among adolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms.Keywords: adolescents, interpersonal functioning, meta‐analysis, posttraumatic stress symptoms, psychological interventions

Introduction

Adolescence serves as a crucial bridge between childhood and adulthood, characterized by significant cognitive, emotional, behavioral, and social changes (Crocetti and Rubini 2017; Spear 2000). These substantial changes influence emotional regulation, behavioral control, identity development, abstract thinking, and social functioning (Crone and Dahl 2012; Keating 2004). Since adolescence represents a sensitive developmental period, mental health disruptions such as traumatic experiences during this stage may interfere with developmental processes that are important for long‐term adjustment and well‐being (Blakemore and Mills 2014; Sisk and Gee 2022).

Exposure to trauma during adolescence can significantly disrupt the capacities to regulate emotions, manage behaviors, develop a stable sense of self, and navigate social environments effectively (American Psychiatric Association [APA] 2022; Benjet et al. 2016; Herman 2015; Knipscheer et al. 2020). Trauma occurs when individuals experience life‐threatening events or serious threats to their well‐being, often beyond their control, and perceive these as threats to their survival or sense of integrity (Benjet et al. 2016; Herman 2015; Knipscheer et al. 2020; Lassemo and Sandanger 2018). Following trauma, various symptoms may emerge, including intrusive memories, persistent avoidance of reminders of the events, negative shifts in mood and thoughts, and notable changes in arousal and reactivity (APA 2022). Repeated and prolonged exposure to traumatic events can lead to chronic difficulties in regulating emotions, negative self‐concept, and challenges in relationships (Cloitre et al. 2013; World Health Organization 2019).

Although trauma exposure may affect multiple areas of adolescent development, interpersonal functioning assumes particular importance during adolescence. Adolescents develop increasingly complex understandings of themselves, others, and social relationships through ongoing interactions with family members, peers, and broader social environments (Crocetti and Rubini 2017; Pfeifer and Berkman 2018; Spear 2000; Steinberg and Morris 2001). During adolescence, the focus on interpersonal relationships shifts from family bonds to peer and romantic connections, accompanied by increased sensitivities in social interactions (Burnett et al. 2011; Lam et al. 2014; Sebastian et al. 2010). Within this developmental context, trauma exposure and posttraumatic stress symptoms are associated with increased interpersonal difficulties among adolescents and may function as transdiagnostic risk factors for broader psychosocial dysfunction (Elmi and Clapp 2021; Habib and Labruna 2011; McLaughlin et al. 2020). Such interpersonal difficulties can shape adolescents' emotional well‐being, social adjustment, and academic functioning, with implications for long‐term psychosocial adjustment (Morosan et al. 2022; Shah et al. 2024; Wilson et al. 2024).

When these interpersonal relationships are navigated successfully, they foster companionship, support, and a sense of belonging, encourage healthy behaviors, and enhance social skills such as communication, cooperation, conflict resolution, and resistance to negative peer pressure (Bukowski et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2018). These interpersonal experiences and skills lay the foundation for successful adult relationships (Allen et al. 2020). Conversely, challenges in interpersonal relationships can lead to the development of various forms of psychopathology, including anxiety, depression, and personality disorders (Dawood et al. 2018; Hopwood et al. 2013; McEvoy et al. 2013). They also contribute to issues such as poor academic performance and a higher risk of self‐harm and suicidality (Adrian et al. 2011; Inglés et al. 2005; King and Merchant 2008).

The impact of posttraumatic stress symptoms on interpersonal functioning warrants interventions aimed at enhancing interpersonal functioning for adolescents experiencing these symptoms. A review of existing meta‐analyses on interventions targeting adolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms, including those diagnosed with posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and complex posttraumatic stress disorder (CPTSD), revealed that only one recent study (Phillips et al. 2024) evaluated social skills and functioning in this population. Other meta‐analyses primarily focused on reducing PTSD and related symptoms (e.g., Gutermann et al. 2016) and comparing different interventions (e.g., Morina et al. 2016).

Past meta‐analytical studies have identified Trauma‐Focused Cognitive Behavior Therapy (TF‐CBT) as the most effective intervention for reducing posttraumatic stress and associated symptoms (e.g., Gutermann et al. 2016; Lenz and Hollenbaugh 2015; Thielemann et al. 2022). Hoogsteder et al. (2022) also reported that EMDR therapy and TF‐CBT have a large and significant overall effect on trauma symptoms, while Lewey et al. (2018) suggested that TF‐CBT might be marginally more effective than EMDR therapy. When comparing treatment options, Morina et al. (2016) found that psychological therapies generally outperform the limited evidence available for psychopharmacological treatments for trauma symptoms. Although multiple meta‐analyses confirm the effectiveness of these interventions in reducing PTSD symptoms among adolescents, there remains a limited understanding of their impact on interpersonal functioning within this population.

Phillips et al. (2024) conducted the only meta‐analysis on interpersonal functioning in adolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms, noting limited research on social and interpersonal outcomes with mixed findings. They examined trauma‐focused interventions of TF‐CBT and EMDR therapy on social skills and functioning in children and young people aged 5−25 years through randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Of the 13 studies analysed, they found a small, nonsignificant effect favouring these treatments over five active and eight passive control groups.

The narrow focus on TF‐CBT and EMDR therapy in Phillips et al. (2024) limited a comprehensive evaluation of potentially effective interventions for adolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms. The small, nonsignificant effect size they reported also raises questions about whether including a broader range of interventions could improve interpersonal functioning outcomes. There are several established evidence‐based interventions for trauma‐related mental health difficulties in adolescents, including approaches that effectively target emotional regulation and peer relationship skills. Examples include Dialectical Behavior Therapy for Adolescents (DBT‐A; Rathus and Miller 2015), Skills Training in Affective and Interpersonal Regulation (STAIR; Cloitre et al. 2002), and Trauma Affect Regulation: Guide for Education and Therapy (TARGET; Ford and Russo 2006). However, a gap remains in the availability of evidence‐based interventions that specifically target trauma‐related difficulties in interpersonal functioning among adolescents. It also remains unclear whether interventions primarily focused on other trauma‐related mechanisms, such as emotion regulation or PTSD symptoms, are robustly and consistently effective for improving interpersonal functioning in adolescents with trauma histories, or which types or modes of intervention may yield stronger and more stable effects in this population.

Therefore, the current study aims to conduct a meta‐analytical review of a diverse range of interventions for adolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms to evaluate their effectiveness in improving interpersonal functioning. This focus is important because of the significance of interpersonal functioning during adolescence, the negative impact of posttraumatic stress symptoms on it, and the current lack of interventions specifically targeting interpersonal functioning. Compared to the previous meta‐analysis (i.e., Phillips et al. 2024), this analysis can offer additional insights by including a larger number of studies (not limited to RCTs), a broader array of interventions, and a focus that has been previously overlooked (interpersonal functioning in adolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms).

Methods

Literature Search

The meta‐analysis adhered to the PRISMA guidelines (Liberati et al. 2009; Page et al. 2021). Multiple databases and search engines were searched to find relevant English articles for this meta‐analysis. These databases included PsycINFO, PubMed, Scopus, CINAHL, and Google Scholar. The software program Publish or Perish (Harzing 2007) was used to retrieve articles from Google Scholar. The search was conducted in 2024, with a 10‐year publication date limit applied across the databases to capture interventions most relevant to contemporary adolescents, as older studies may have reduced applicability due to generational changes (Al‐Lawati 2019; Grelle et al. 2023). This criterion was specified a priori in the pre‐registered protocol (PROSPERO: CRD42023480411 December 2023) and adhered to ensure methodological transparency and integrity. All relevant keywords were considered in the search, including adolescent, trauma, posttraumatic stress disorder, intervention, psychotherapy, interpersonal problems, social adjustment, and related terms associated with these key concepts. To reduce the chances of missing relevant studies, the reference lists and citation histories of included articles were reviewed for related peer‐reviewed papers. As this study involved the analysis of previously published, de‐identified data, additional ethical approval and participant consent were not required. All included studies were expected to have obtained appropriate ethical approval and informed consent in accordance with their respective institutional and national guidelines.

The inclusion criteria for the meta‐analysis were as follows: (a) the interventions involved psychological therapeutic or education‐based methods, (b) the participants included adolescents aged 13−18 years, (c) the participants had posttraumatic stress symptoms, including PTSD or CPTSD symptoms, (d) the participants did not report intellectual disabilities, (e) the participants did not have a brain or neurological disorder, (f) a measure of interpersonal functioning or an aspect of interpersonal functioning was included before and after the intervention, (g) quantitative analyses were used to report intervention results, (h) sufficient statistics information (e.g., means, standard deviations, or t or F statistics) to calculate Hedges' g was provided to enable quantitative synthesis and comparability across studies, and (i) the original studies were published as peer‐reviewed articles within the past 10 years. Studies using qualitative or non‐empirical methodologies, including case studies, were excluded.

Figure 1 illustrates the study screening and selection process. The software JBI SUMMARI (Munn et al. 2019) was used for the screening process. Two independent coders screened all titles, abstracts, and full‐text articles against the inclusion and exclusion criteria using JBI SUMMARI. Any discrepancies between coders were discussed during review meetings and resolved through consensus. The initial search identified 900 articles. After reviewing titles and abstracts, 877 studies were excluded because they did not use quantitative analyses, include psychological interventions (e.g., systematic reviews, meta‐analyses, review papers), include outcomes (e.g., study protocols), or have adolescents as their target population, leaving 23 studies. Of these, eight were removed because they either did not report suitable statistics for calculating effect sizes or did not include interpersonal functioning or related features in their outcome measures. Together with two articles identified through review of the reference list of included articles and citation searches, 17 studies with 35 effect sizes were included in the meta‐analysis (N = 1394 individuals).

Overview of Included Interventions

This study incorporated a range of specific interventions (14 approaches) primarily aimed at reducing PTSD symptoms, with improvements in interpersonal functioning as a secondary outcome. Most studies were conducted in Germany (seven studies) and the United States (three studies), while others took place in Spain, Australia, Canada, South Africa, Switzerland, South Korea, and India. Participants were mainly recruited from specialized settings such as child and adolescent mental health clinics, specialist schools, and inpatient psychiatric units. Given the complex profiles of participants who experienced at least one traumatic event and exhibited PTSD symptoms, most studies involved more than 10 treatment sessions that were regular and intensive (i.e., daily, weekly, or twice weekly). They also used relatively small treatment sample sizes (< 50 participants), likely to enable adequate monitoring of the participants. Conversely, some studies involved brief interventions (lasting less than 5 weeks, e.g., EPIPAC in Kramer and Landolt 2014, and 3CAP in Lee and Jeon 2021), mainly focusing on psychoeducation rather than skill development and practice. The 14 therapeutic approaches included in the meta‐analyses are briefly reviewed in this section.

A few features of the interventions were identified. First, guided exposure to traumatic events and the processing of thoughts and feelings related to these events are important aspects, as demonstrated in D‐CPT, TF‐CBT, EPIPAC, FORNET, STSA‐A, and MBT‐G. Some interventions (i.e., D‐CPT, CPT‐A +, TF‐CBT) identify unhelpful negative thoughts associated with the trauma experience, challenge, and modify the interpretation of the traumatic event, which lessens the impact of trauma on daily life. Another feature is the enhancement of a sense of safety and trust through developing a more secure attachment, as seen in AAP (in Balluerka et al. 2015), the ReLATE intervention model (in Diggins 2021), and Capoeira Angola (in Mom et al. 2019). Skill‐building in emotional and interpersonal regulation is another important feature in some interventions, including STAIR‐A (e.g., Gudiño et al. 2016), the START program (in Dixius and Möhler 2021), and Yoga with counseling (in Mukherjee 2020). Lastly, external support proved beneficial, as observed in AAP (therapy animals), the ReLATE intervention (teachers), TF‐CBT (non‐offending caregivers), and Capoeira Angola (mentor).

Coding of Studies

Two independent coders coded each study included in this meta‐analysis, and any discrepancies between the two coders were resolved by consensus. Studies were coded based on (a) type of intervention, (b) mode (e.g., group vs. individual sessions) of intervention, (c) number of sessions, and (d) the outcome instruments used. Since none of the included studies used a questionnaire specifically measuring interpersonal functioning (e.g., Inventory of Interpersonal Problems‐64; Horowitz et al. 2000), components of interpersonal functioning and related features were assessed through subscales of other relevant questionnaires. As a result, a heterogeneity analysis (Borenstein et al. 2009) was conducted to examine variability across assessments of interpersonal functioning.

Measures Used by the Studies

All studies incorporated reliable and valid measures. One‐third of the studies used both the Clinician‐Administered PTSD Scale for Children and Adolescents for DSM‐IV (CAPS‐CA; Nader et al. 1996) and the University of California Los Angeles PTSD Reaction Index for DSM‐IV (UCLA‐PTSD‐RI; Steinberg et al. 2004) to assess posttraumatic stress symptoms. Meanwhile, one study exclusively employed the CAPS‐CA (Kramer and Landolt 2014), while another used only the UCLA‐PTSD‐RI (Gudiño et al. 2014). Other measures included the Child PTSD Symptom Scale (CPSS; Foa et al. 2001, in Goodman et al. 2023 and Gudiño et al. 2016), the Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist, Parent Report (Weathers and Ford 1996, in Diggins 2021), the Child and Adolescent Trauma Screening (CATS; Sachser Berliner et al. 2017, in Dixius and Möhler 2021), and the Children's Impact of Trauma Events Scale II (CITES II; Wolfe 2002) PTSD subscale (in Hébert and Amédée 2020).

In terms of measurements related to interpersonal functioning, the subscales of the Child and Behavior Checklist (CBCL; Achenbach and Rescorla 2001, in Goldbeck et al. 2016; Hébert and Amédée 2020 and Kramer and Landolt 2014), the Youth Self‐Report (YSR; Achenbach 1991, in Lee and Jeon 2021; Rosner et al. 2019 and Vogel et al. 2020), the Behavior Assessment System for Children (BASC; Reynolds and Kamphaus 1992, in Balluerka et al. 2015; Gudiño et al. 2016), the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ; R. Goodman 2001, in Diggins 2021 and Mom et al. 2019), and the FEEL‐KJ (Goldschmidt and Berth 2006, in Dixius and Möhler 2021) were used to assess various aspects of interpersonal functioning. Other measures included the Experiences in Close Relationships‐Revised Questionnaire (ECR‐R; Ehrenthal et al. 2009, in Rimane et al. 2022), the Adolescent Attachment Questionnaire (AAQ; Westen et al. 2006, in Goodman et al. 2023), KidCOPE (Spirito et al. 1988, in Gudiño et al. 2014), and the Emotional Maturity Scale (Singh and Bhargava 1990, in Mukherjee 2020).

Methodological Quality Assessment

The Effective Public Health Practice Project Quality Assessment Tool for Quantitative Studies (EPHPP; McMaster University 2008) was used to assess the quality of studies in this study. The EPHPP evaluates the quality of quantitative studies to facilitate the synthesis of knowledge from these studies (Tanner 2019). Two researchers from this study independently evaluated each study included in the meta‐analysis using the six domains of the EPHPP: selection bias, study design, control of confounders, blinding, data collection methods, and participant withdrawals and dropouts (McMaster University 2008). Based on scores from each domain, the quality of each study was classified as strong (no elements rated as weak), moderate (one element rated as weak), or weak (two or more elements rated as weak). The findings are shown in Table 1. The blinding domain posed challenges for most studies and influenced their quality ratings. Despite this, most studies were deemed of moderate quality, with one rated as weak and three as strong. It is important to note that this scale is utilized in this study to provide an overview of the quality of the studies included in the meta‐analysis. It is not used as an inclusion criterion for the meta‐analysis, nor is it generally considered a necessary criterion for study selection in meta‐analytical assessments (Borenstein et al. 2009).

Statistical Analysis

This study's meta‐analysis has been computed using Hedge's g statistic. This statistic is calculated based on mean differences and pooled standard deviations. Mean differences in effect sizes estimated through a random‐effects model were used to compute aggregated effect sizes and identify potential moderators. Random (or mixed) effects analysis is recommended when the results of several random samples need to be generalized to a larger population (Hedges and Vevea 1998; Konstantopoulos 2006).

The type and mode of intervention, as well as the number of sessions, were examined as potential moderators. Meta‐regression was used to evaluate the only continuous moderator: the number of sessions. Due to limited studies for each specific intervention type (i.e., 11 interventions with only one study each), separate subgroup analyses were not feasible. Therefore, interventions were categorized into the following broader categories to increase the number of studies in each group: Cognitive Behavior Therapy (CBT), TF‐CBT, interventions other than TF‐CBT involving exposure (Exposure Interventions), interventions involving body movements (Movement Interventions), and interventions focusing on self‐regulation strategies (Self‐regulation Interventions). The mode of intervention was classified based on whether it involved individual sessions (Individual), group sessions (Group), or a combination of individual and conjoint or group sessions (Mixed).

Comprehensive meta‐analysis (CMA; Borenstein et al. 2022), Version 4, was utilized for the analyses. The heterogeneity of effect sizes was evaluated using Q statistics, and publication bias was investigated with the Classic Fail‐safe N to estimate the number of studies with nonsignificant effects needed to bring the computed aggregated effect size to a nonsignificant level (p < 0.05; Rosenthal 1979, 1991).

Results

Most studies in the meta‐analysis included multiple subscales related to interpersonal functioning. Each subscale was therefore considered a separate effect size. Some of these subscales reflected positive aspects of interpersonal functioning, such as prosocial behaviors and social support, indicating the ability to engage effectively in social interactions (Erozkan 2013). Others represented problems in interpersonal functioning, like aggressive behaviors and social stress, highlighting difficulties in social relationships (Horowitz et al. 1988, 1993). Consequently, the first group of subscales was categorized as interpersonal competence, while the second was classified as interpersonal problems. The test of heterogeneity following categorization was not significant, suggesting that the two categories (interpersonal competence and interpersonal problems) were not heterogeneous (Q = 0.027, p > 0.05) and are related to the same dependent variable, interpersonal functioning. Based on all studies included in the meta‐analysis, the post‐intervention aggregated effect size was computed as g = 0.39, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.28, 0.49] (see Figure 2 for a forest plot showing all the studies). According to Cohen's guidelines (Cohen 1992), this represents a small but statistically significant improvement in interpersonal functioning. Using the classic fail‐safe N analysis (Rosenthal 1979, 1991), 1300 studies with nonsignificant effect sizes would be needed to bring this effect size to a nonsignificant level (p > 0.05). These results indicate a small but statistically significant aggregated efficacy of the included interventions in improving the interpersonal functioning of adolescents with post‐traumatic stress symptoms.

Forest plot for all studies grouped by outcome for post‐intervention.

The results of the meta‐regression analysis showed no significant moderating effect for the number of therapeutic sessions across all studies (b = 0.0006, SE = 0.0053, p = 0.91), implying that an increase in the number of sessions may not be associated with stronger immediate post‐intervention improvements in interpersonal functioning. The results of subgroup analyses showed a significant moderating effect for the type (Q = 19.38, p = 0.001; see Figure 3 for a forest plot of all the studies grouped by type) and mode (Q = 7.00, p = 0.030; see Figure 4 for a forest plot of all the studies grouped by mode) of intervention on improving interpersonal functioning post‐intervention.

Forest plot for all studies grouped by type of intervention for post‐intervention.

Forest plot for all studies grouped by mode of intervention for post‐intervention.

Accordingly, TF‐CBT demonstrated the largest aggregated effect (g = 0.605, p < 0.001), and the smallest effect belonged to CBT for the type of intervention. Mixed mode (g = 0.551, p < 0.001) had the largest effect for the mode of intervention, and group mode had the smallest effect.

Follow‐Up Assessments

Four of the 17 studies included data on a 3‐month follow‐up. The heterogeneity test for outcome variables was not significant for follow‐up assessments (Q = 7.71, p = 0.173). This suggests that all assessments within the studies relate to the same dependent variable of interpersonal functioning, both post‐intervention and at follow‐up. Furthermore, this suggests relative consistency in the measurement of interpersonal functioning across the included studies.

Considering all included studies with follow‐up data in the meta‐analysis, the aggregated effect size was g = 0.34, p = 0.019, 95% CI [0.055, 0.620] for post‐intervention and g = 0.54, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.28, 0.81] for follow‐up. The aggregated effect size is regarded as small for post‐intervention and medium for follow‐up, based on Cohen's interpretation (Cohen 1992), suggesting that the improvements in interpersonal functioning may become more evident over time. Figure 5 displays the forest plot for follow‐up assessments. Based on classic fail‐safe N analysis (Rosenthal 1979, 1991), 33 studies with nonsignificant results would be needed to reduce the effect sizes to a nonsignificant level.

Forest plot for studies with follow‐up assessments.

The results of the meta‐regression analysis, based on follow‐up assessments of interpersonal functioning, showed a small but significant moderating effect for the number of sessions (b = 0.018, SE = 0.0086, p = 0.04), indicating that a higher number of sessions may be linked to greater long‐term efficacy. Therefore, more sessions are associated with larger effect sizes in improving interpersonal functioning. However, because of the limited number of studies (N < 5) for each type of intervention, subgroup analyses for studies with follow‐up assessments could not be performed.

Discussion

This meta‐analysis aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of current interventions on interpersonal functioning in adolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms. The findings showed a small but statistically significant post‐intervention effect, with the type and mode of intervention moderating the outcome. Furthermore, at a 3‐month follow‐up, a medium‐sized effect was observed, and the number of sessions acted as a moderating factor.

Although the small‐to‐medium aggregated effect sizes identified in this meta‐analysis do not provide robust or consistent evidence for gold‐standard interventions in this context, the effects observed are still statistically significant. This suggests that the therapeutic elements embedded in current interventions can support basic improvements in interpersonal functioning among adolescents with trauma histories. However, further intervention mapping research is needed to refine existing protocols, either by including more targeted therapeutic elements or by adjusting intervention delivery characteristics to achieve larger and more stable short‐ and long‐term effects. The only other study known to us that examined interpersonal functioning in adolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms is Phillips et al. (2024), who investigated how TF‐CBT and EMDR affect social and interpersonal functioning in children and adolescents with PTSD. Similar to Phillips et al. (2024), measures of interpersonal functioning in the included studies mainly consisted of subscales derived from broader assessments, highlighting the lack of specific focus on interpersonal functioning. The finding of a small yet significant post‐intervention effect in this meta‐analysis, however, contrasts with the small, nonsignificant effects reported by Phillips et al. (2024). This difference could potentially be due to a more flexible inclusion approach regarding interventions (not limited to TF‐CBT and EMDR therapy) and study design (not limited to RCTs). Another explanation may relate to the narrower age range included in the current study (i.e., 13−18 years), which recognizes developmental differences between children and adolescents. This emphasizes the need to explore treatments beyond symptom‐focused trauma interventions when addressing interpersonal issues in adolescents, or to adapt trauma‐focused approaches to more directly target interpersonal functioning, as reductions in PTSD symptoms may not necessarily translate into improvements in interpersonal functioning. The importance of age‐appropriate interventions is also underscored.

The current meta‐analysis found that combining individual and group or conjoint sessions is the most effective way to improve interpersonal functioning, while group intervention alone is the least effective. This highlights that group sessions, which are useful for skill development and practice, are not sufficient on their own for the population of adolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms. Individual sessions are essential for enhancing interpersonal functioning, providing a safe space to explore and address interpersonal issues, work through intrapersonal concerns, and offer personalized feedback and insights (Bernal‐Manrique et al. 2020; Goodman et al. 2023; O'Shea et al. 2015). This aligns with Gutermann et al. (2016)'s finding that individual treatment is effective for adolescents with PTSD symptoms, and parental involvement further increases the effectiveness of the interventions. In clinical settings, these findings may highlight the potential benefits of combining individual and group‐based components to address both personalized therapeutic needs and opportunities for interpersonal skill practice. Such approaches may be feasible within schools and mental health services where group programs are already established, although implementing individualized intervention components may require additional clinician time, coordination, and resources. Future research is needed to examine how combined individual and group‐based intervention approaches can be optimized to improve interpersonal functioning in adolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms across school and mental health settings.

The findings of the current meta‐analysis demonstrated a medium follow‐up effect size, indicating that treatment effects may strengthen over time. This is in line with Gutermann et al. (2017)'s finding of a medium to large follow‐up effect size for PTSD symptoms in their investigation of the long‐term effects of psychological interventions on children and adolescents with these symptoms. Together, these findings suggest that the benefits of psychological interventions for adolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms may continue beyond the immediate post‐intervention period, highlighting the importance of follow‐up assessments when evaluating treatment outcomes, particularly for interpersonal functioning.

Among the 17 studies, only four included follow‐up assessments 3 months after treatment, highlighting a gap in research on the sustained efficacy of interventions for interpersonal functioning. An analysis of these four studies using six effect sizes to compare posttreatment and follow‐up effects revealed that the follow‐up effect size was nearly twice as large as the immediate posttreatment effect size. The stronger follow‐up effects may indicate that improvements in interpersonal functioning continue to develop after treatment has ended, as interpersonal skills need to be practiced, reinforced, and applied over time before meaningful improvements can become apparent in adolescents' everyday relationships. This pattern may reflect the gradual generalization of interpersonal skills across family, peer, and social contexts following the completion of treatment.

Another finding was that the number of sessions was not a significant moderator for the post‐intervention assessment, but it showed a small yet significant moderating impact on the follow‐up, despite only four studies being included in that analysis. This suggests that while increasing the number of sessions may not improve immediate post‐intervention outcomes, incorporating more sessions may contribute to stronger long‐term improvements in interpersonal functioning. This likely reflects the gradual nature of interpersonal change following trauma exposure, whereby interventions with a greater number of sessions may provide additional opportunities for the consolidation and maintenance of therapeutic gains over time. Clinically, these findings suggest that interventions targeting interpersonal functioning in adolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms may require sufficient treatment duration and ongoing opportunities for skill application, as meaningful interpersonal improvements may continue to strengthen beyond the immediate post‐intervention period.

A key distinction between this study and many previous meta‐analyses on adolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms is that the interventions in the studies included in this meta‐analysis did not directly target interpersonal functioning. This contrasts with earlier meta‐analyses in which intervention content (e.g., TF‐CBT) was more closely aligned with the primary outcomes being targeted, such as PTSD and mood disorders. For instance, those studies focused on reducing PTSD symptoms through empirically supported PTSD interventions. While interpersonal functioning is vital during adolescence, most interventions mainly target posttraumatic stress or other mental health issues rather than specifically aiming to improve interpersonal functioning within this age group.

While most studies in this meta‐analysis did not focus on the interpersonal functioning of adolescents with PTSD symptoms, two studies (Goodman et al. 2023, and Mom et al. 2019) did consider the complex effects of trauma on interpersonal functioning within their intervention design. Goodman et al. (2023) achieved significant medium effect sizes (g = 0.409−0.576), while Mom et al. (2019) reported an effect size nearly classified as large (g = 0.747). The two interventions used in Goodman et al. (2023), Storytelling/Story‐Acting for Adolescents (STSA‐A) and Mentalization‐Based Treatment Group Intervention (MBT‐G), involved weekly sessions over 8 months, allowing time to gradually open up and build trust in a group setting, which is important for PTSD treatment outcomes (e.g., Castillo et al. 2016 and Sloan et al. 2013). Similarly, Capoeira Angola, the movement and connection approach described in Mom et al. (2019), involves physical movement and social connection, which are shown to greatly benefit young people with complex trauma histories (e.g., Perry and Szalavitc 2006 and Van der Kolk 2014). Therefore, interventions targeting interpersonal functioning in this population may be more effective when they directly address the specific interpersonal challenges associated with trauma exposure.

The importance of addressing specific interpersonal challenges faced by adolescents who have experienced trauma is affirmed by another finding in this meta‐analysis. The current meta‐analysis shows that when TF‐CBT is analyzed separately from CBT, the latter has the smallest effect on improving interpersonal functioning. This contrasts with Gutermann et al. (2016), who concluded that CBT effectively treats PTSD symptoms; however, this conclusion pertains to the inclusion of TF‐CBT within the broader category of CBT. This suggests that CBT without a trauma focus may not be the most suitable intervention for adolescents dealing with both posttraumatic stress symptoms and interpersonal challenges. However, it is important to recognize that only two effect sizes were included for CBT, which could have influenced the results.

A limitation of this study was the relatively small number of studies meeting the inclusion criteria for the meta‐analysis. However, it is worth noting that this number is comparable to other meta‐analyses focusing on PTSD and mood disorders (e.g., Gillies et al. 2013; Lenz and Hollenbaugh 2015; Silverman et al. 2008). Nonetheless, the lack of high‐quality RCTs remains a general limitation in this field, and potential publication bias should also be acknowledged, as studies reporting significant intervention effects may have been more likely to be published. Only English‐language publications were included in the meta‐analysis, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to non‐English‐speaking populations and cultural contexts. In addition, the included studies varied in intervention type, treatment duration, delivery mode, and measures of interpersonal functioning, which may have contributed to heterogeneity in the findings and influenced the comparability of effect sizes across studies. The use of subscales from different assessments to measure features of interpersonal functioning may also not have accurately reflected individuals' overall interpersonal functioning. Finally, the potential moderating effects of gender and age on interpersonal functioning could not be examined, as these variables were not sufficiently investigated or consistently reported across the included studies. Future research would benefit from exploring the impact of gender and age on interpersonal functioning in adolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms.

Overall, the results of this study highlight the importance of reconsidering current interventions for adolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms. Although previous research has shown that psychological interventions have generally been effective for PTSD and mood disorders, the current study indicates that most of the evidence‐based interventions have limited impact on improving the interpersonal functioning of adolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms. The medium follow‐up effect identified in this study also suggests that improvements in interpersonal functioning may strengthen over time, highlighting the importance of follow‐up assessments when evaluating interpersonal functioning outcomes in adolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms. Evidence regarding the sustained efficacy of these interventions for improving interpersonal functioning over time nevertheless remains limited. Therefore, future research should prioritize RCTs with follow‐up assessments that examine interventions directly targeting interpersonal functioning, including trauma‐focused approaches that incorporate social and interpersonal components to better address the interpersonal impacts of trauma in adolescents with posttraumatic stress symptoms. Moreover, additional high‐quality empirical investigations are needed for newer trauma‐focused approaches (e.g., trauma‐focused yoga, animal‐assisted therapy), and all studies in this context should incorporate comprehensive assessments and report detailed information on trauma types (e.g., relational trauma vs. event‐based trauma). Such considerations would enable more nuanced meta‐analytical investigations, including moderator and subgroup analyses comparing the moderating effects of factors such as intervention type and trauma type.
