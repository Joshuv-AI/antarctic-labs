Emotion Entanglement and Bayesian Inference for Multi-Dimensional Emotion Understanding
Hemanth Kotaprolu, Kishan Maharaj Raey Zhao, Abhijit Mishra, Pushpak Bhattacharyya

Abstract
Understanding emotions in natural language is inherently a multi-dimensional reasoning problem, where multiple affective signals interact through context, interpersonal relations, and situational cues. However, most existing emotion understanding benchmarks rely on short texts and predefined emotion labels, reducing this process to independent label prediction and ignoring the structured dependencies among emotions. To address this limitation, we introduce Emo tional Scen arios ( EmoScene ), a theory-grounded benchmark of 4,731 context-rich scenarios annotated with an 8-dimensional emotion vector derived from Plutchik’s basic emotions . We evaluate six instruction-tuned large language models in a zero-shot setting and observe modest performance, with the best model achieving a Macro F1 of 0.501 , highlighting the difficulty of context-aware multi-label emotion prediction. Motivated by the observation that emotions rarely occur independently, we further propose an entanglement-aware Bayesian inference framework that incorporates emotion co-occurrence statistics to perform joint posterior inference over the emotion vector. This lightweight post-processing improves structural consistency of predictions and yields notable gains for weaker models (e.g., +0.051 Macro F1 for Qwen2.5-7B). EmoScene therefore provides a challenging benchmark for studying multi-dimensional emotion understanding and the limitations of current language models.

Related Work

Emotion theories and psychological grounding.

Emotions have long been studied in psychology as adaptive mechanisms that influence cognition, behavior, and social interaction
Damasio 1998
;
Gross and Barrett 2011
. Emotional intelligence refers to the capacity to recognize, interpret, and manage emotional information in oneself and others
Mayer et al. 2004
. A large body of work has explored how emotional experiences are structured and expressed in human cognition. For example, emotional granularity describes the ability to distinguish subtle affective states and has been linked to improved emotional regulation and social functioning
Hoemann et al. 2021
. Psychological theories such as Plutchik’s psychoevolutionary theory further suggest that complex emotional states arise from combinations of a small set of fundamental emotions
Plutchik 1982
;
Plutchik 1980
. These theoretical perspectives highlight that emotions are inherently structured and often co-occur rather than appearing in isolation.

Emotion understanding in NLP.

Emotion analysis has been widely studied within natural language processing as an extension of sentiment analysis. Early work focused on detecting affective states in text through lexicons and classification models
Mohammad and Turney 2013
. Subsequent research expanded this task to emotion recognition in conversations and context-dependent settings, emphasizing the importance of discourse structure and speaker interactions
Fu et al. 2023
. Recent work has also explored multimodal approaches that combine textual, acoustic, and visual signals to improve emotion recognition and generation in dialogue systems
Liu et al. 2024
;
Chandraumakantham et al. 2024
. These studies demonstrate that emotional signals are often distributed across multiple modalities and contextual cues.

Emotion datasets and benchmarks.

The development of emotion understanding systems has been driven by the availability of annotated datasets. Early benchmarks such as the SemEval affective text tasks focused on identifying emotions in short texts such as tweets
Strapparava and Mihalcea 2007
;
Mohammad et al. 2018
. More recent datasets, including GoEmotions
Demszky et al. 2020
, EmotionLines
Chen et al. 2018
, and crowdsourced corpora derived from ISEAR
Troiano et al. 2019
;
Scherer and Wallbott 1990
, have introduced larger taxonomies of emotions and conversational contexts. Despite these advances, most datasets rely on short or decontextualized inputs and predefined emotion categories, which may limit their ability to capture complex emotional states. In addition, annotation practices vary widely across datasets, ranging from crowdsourcing to expert labeling, which can affect label consistency and reliability.

Emotion understanding in large language models.

With the rise of large language models (LLMs), recent research has begun to examine their capacity for emotional intelligence. Studies have evaluated LLMs on emotion recognition, empathy, and affect-aware reasoning tasks
Sabour et al. 2024
;
Sorin et al. 2024
. Other work has investigated how LLM architectures represent emotional information internally or handle ambiguous emotional cues
Hong et al. 2025
;
Reichman et al. 2026
. These findings suggest that while LLMs demonstrate promising capabilities in affective language tasks, their performance remains sensitive to context and evaluation design.

Limitations of existing benchmarks and our approach.

Despite substantial progress, existing emotion benchmarks exhibit several limitations. Many datasets rely on short or decontextualized texts that lack sufficient contextual information to disambiguate emotional states. In addition, most evaluation protocols treat emotion prediction as a collection of independent classification decisions, ignoring the structured dependencies among emotions suggested by psychological theories. As a result, it remains unclear whether models genuinely reason about emotional states or merely detect surface-level affective cues. To address these limitations, we introduce
EmoScene
, a benchmark of context-rich emotional scenarios grounded in Plutchik’s theory of emotions. Each scenario is annotated with an 8-dimensional emotion vector representing combinations of fundamental emotions. We further model dependencies among emotions using a Bayesian inference framework that incorporates statistical co-occurrence patterns observed in the dataset, enabling emotion understanding to be studied as a structured multi-label inference problem.

Methodology

Traditional multi-label classification frameworks typically model each emotion as an independent Bernoulli variable, implicitly assuming that emotional states occur in isolation. However, this assumption is fundamentally misaligned with the nature of human affect, where emotions are rarely independent and often co-occur in structured, interdependent ways. For instance, emotions such as joy and surprise may reinforce each other, while others, like anger and sadness, may exhibit complex conditional relationships depending on context.

To address this limitation, we introduce the concept of Emotion Entanglement, which captures the inherent dependencies and interactions among emotions as a coupled system rather than a set of disjoint labels. Building on this perspective, we propose an Entanglement-Aware Bayesian Inference framework that explicitly models the joint distribution over emotions. By incorporating these dependencies into the inference process, our approach enables more coherent, context-sensitive predictions that better reflect the underlying structure of human emotional expression.

4.1
Modeling Emotion Entanglement

We define
Emotion Entanglement
as the phenomenon where the presence of one emotion in a text significantly alters the likelihood of another co-occurring. Formally, we hypothesize that for an input sentence
x
and an emotion vector
E=(E_{1},\dots,E_{8})
, the joint distribution does not factorize:

P(E|x)\neq\prod_{i=1}^{8}P(E_{i}|x)

(1)

To quantify this, we analyze the structural non-independence of emotions using information-theoretic measures. Specifically, we compute the Mutual Information
I(E_{i};E_{j})
between emotion pairs to establish that emotional expression is structurally dependent. For instance, in the EmoScene dataset, the probability of
Anger
is significantly higher given the presence of
Sadness
than its marginal probability, i.e.,
P(\text{Anger}=1|\text{Sadness}=1)\gg P(\text{Anger}=1)
.

4.2
Entanglement-Aware Bayesian Inference

To correct the independence assumption made by vanilla LLMs, we propose a post-processing step based on Bayesian inference. We treat the LLM output as a noisy likelihood and combine it with a data-derived prior that captures label entanglement.

Let
x
be the input text and
E=(E_{1},\dots,E_{8})
be the binary emotion vector where
E_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. Our goal is to find the Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) estimate
\hat{E}
:

\hat{E}=\arg\max_{E}P(E|x)\propto\arg\max_{E}P(x|E)\cdot P(E)

(2)

4.2.1
Estimating the Entanglement Prior

We model the label-only prior
P(E)
using an
Ising Model
(a type of Markov Random Field) to find the least-committed distribution that matches observed marginals and pairwise correlations in the training data. The prior is defined as:

P(E)=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left(\sum_{i}\theta_{i}E_{i}+\sum_{i<j}\theta_{ij}E_{i}E_{j}\right)

(3)

where
\theta_{i}
represents the bias for individual emotions and
\theta_{ij}
represents the coupling strength between emotion pairs. These parameters are estimated from the training set via maximum-entropy solutions:

\displaystyle\theta_{i}

\displaystyle=\log\frac{\hat{P}(E_{i}=1)}{1-\hat{P}(E_{i}=1)},

(4)

\displaystyle\theta_{ij}

\displaystyle=\log\frac{\hat{P}(E_{i}=1,E_{j}=1)}{\hat{P}(E_{i}=1)\hat{P}(E_{j}=1)}

4.2.2
Likelihood and MAP Inference

The likelihood
P(x|E)
is derived from the LLM’s log-probabilities for each emotion category. Assuming a Bernoulli likelihood for the text given an emotion configuration:

\log P(x|E)=\sum_{i}\left[E_{i}\log p_{i}^{(1)}+(1-E_{i})\log p_{i}^{(0)}\right]

(5)

Substituting the prior and likelihood back into the MAP objective, our final a-posteriori log-objective becomes:

\displaystyle\log P(E|x)={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\left[E_{i}\log\frac{p_{i}^{(1)}}{p_{i}^{(0)}}\right]

(6)

\displaystyle+\alpha\left[\sum_{i}\theta_{i}E_{i}+\sum_{i<j}\theta_{ij}E_{i}E_{j}\right]

Since we utilize a fixed space of 8 labels, the exact argmax is found by evaluating all
2^{8}
combinations, ensuring a parameter-efficient post-processing step that explicitly models emotion co-occurrence fidelity.

Results & Analysis

In this section, we discuss the evaluation of multiple instruction-tuned open-source LLMs on the EmoScene benchmark for multi-label emotion prediction using Scenario and Subject. We compare zero-shot performance with our proposed Bayesian Inference (BI) approach and report the results in Table
1
. Performance is assessed using Lexical Accuracy, Vector Accuracy, Hamming Loss, and Macro F1-score, capturing label-wise correctness, exact set match, label errors, and balanced performance across emotions. The prompt used for evaluation is included in the Appendix
8
.

Our Bayesian Inference (BI) method consistently outperforms the zero-shot baseline across all models and metrics on the EmoScene dataset. Gemma-2-9b-it achieves the highest F1-Macro of 0.501 with lexical accuracy 83.48%, while Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct shows the largest gains (F1-Macro +5%, Hamming Loss, -6.275); improvements are most evident in smaller models such as Gemma-2-2b-it (lexical accuracy +7.858, Hamming –7.858), confirming BI boosts reliability and reduces per-emotion errors without affecting strong performers like Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct.

Vector accuracy remains modest (15–25%) despite high lexical scores because BI performs joint MAP inference over the full emotion vector
\mathbf{E}
, enforcing an entanglement prior via an MRF/Ising graph that models pairwise correlations (e.g., sadness co-occurs more with anger than joy). This corrects the independence assumption of zero-shot LLMs by re-weighting predictions to better match co-occurrence patterns and reduce Hamming Loss. As a result, the final vector may deviate from the original marginals, lowering exact-match accuracy. However, this trade-off consistently improves Hamming Loss and Macro F1, demonstrating BI’s effectiveness for robust, scene-aware emotion classification.

6.1
Predicitive Analysis

Per-emotion precision & recall heatmaps can be found in Figure
4
. Joy and Anticipation emerge as the strongest categories overall, where Joy achieves high precision and recall across all models consistently, while Anticipation shows moderate precision and recall, reflecting their frequent occurrence in the dataset and the clear lexical cues that LLMs readily capture. In contrast, Disgust displays critically low recall (near-zero in Llama and Mistral variants), and Surprise similarly suffers low recall in larger models, indicating models might be adopting conservative strategies, predicting them only when evidence is overwhelming.

In this multi-label setting, precision reflects the reliability of positive predictions (avoiding false cues like incorrectly detecting Anger), while recall ensures coverage of the full emotional spectrum. Smaller model like Gemma-2-2b-it show high recall for Anticipation, Fear, Joy, Sadness, but low precision on labels like Anger and Surprise, indicating over-prediction on rare classes (e.g., Disgust, Surprise). Larger models such as Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct are more balanced but remain conservative on these emotions. This motivates our Bayesian post-processing: the MRF/Ising-based entanglement prior, which enforces realistic co-occurrence patterns, improves Macro F1 and reduces Hamming Loss even for rare emotions without retraining. Below, we discuss a general case where our posterior formulation corrected the label from the baseline.

Scenario [Anticipation, Sadness]

Sonia scrolled through her Instagram feed, her eyes narrowing as she paused on Frank’s latest post about his trip to Bali. Her stomach felt unsettled, and she noticed her muscles tensing as she read about his adventures. She couldn’t help but compare her own routine life to his glamorous lifestyle. Trying to shake off the feeling, she decided to focus on her own upcoming weekend plans and the things she has achieved recently.

In the above scenario, the baseline incorrectly predicts Anger along with Anticipation and Sadness. In general, Sadness is a low-arousal negative emotion, whereas Anger is a high-arousal negative emotion. The description of her stomach being "unsettled" and muscles "tensing" injects a high level of physical arousal into the text. The baseline model struggled to map this high-arousal discomfort to Sadness, defaulting instead to Anger, which is the most common high-arousal negative emotion in text classification. Our formulation removes this spurious label of Anger driven by the learned prior, where
\theta_{ij}
favours the co-occurrence of Sadness and Anticipation but penalises unlikely associations with Anger.

Specifically, we focused on the Gemma-2-9b-it model’s outputs, which achieved the best Macro-F1 score among all the evaluated models.

The emotional signal arises from the relational interaction, particularly the phrase “Kim nodded slowly, grateful for the offer,” which indicates reliance on another person’s support. However, the model likely focuses on earlier anxiety cues such as “hands were damp” and “heart racing,” which describe stress rather than interpersonal trust. As a result, the relational aspect of the interaction is overlooked.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

We introduced
EmoScene
, a context-rich benchmark for evaluating multi-dimensional emotion understanding in language models. Grounded in Plutchik’s theory, EmoScene represents emotional states using an 8-dimensional emotion vector and contains 4,731 scenarios that require reasoning over contextual and relational cues. Our evaluation of six instruction-tuned LLMs in a zero-shot setting shows that even strong models achieve modest performance, highlighting the difficulty of context-aware multi-label emotion prediction. To address the independence assumption commonly made in emotion classification, we proposed an entanglement-aware Bayesian inference framework that incorporates emotion co-occurrence statistics to perform joint posterior inference, improving prediction consistency and yielding notable gains for weaker models. These results suggest that modelling dependencies among emotions is important for robust emotion understanding. In future work, we plan to extend EmoScene with additional modalities and languages and investigate architectures and reasoning strategies that explicitly model emotional structure in context. Further, we would also like to explore the utility of preference tuning strategies in improving the emotional awareness of large language models.

Limitations

While EmoScene provides a context-rich benchmark for multi-dimensional emotion understanding, several limitations remain. First, the scenarios are generated using a language model and may not fully capture the diversity and complexity of naturally occurring emotional narratives. Second, the dataset focuses solely on textual descriptions and does not incorporate multimodal signals such as facial expressions, speech, or physiological cues that often accompany emotional expression. Finally, although our Bayesian inference framework models pairwise dependencies among emotions, it assumes relatively simple statistical relationships and may not capture higher-order emotional interactions present in real-world settings.

Ethics Statement

All snippets and examples used in this work were collected or generated following ethical practices and, where applicable, under permissive licenses. The dataset does not contain personally identifiable information or sensitive data. All human annotators involved in constructing the EmoScene dataset participated voluntarily and provided informed consent prior to annotation. Annotators were compensated on an hourly basis in accordance with the labor regulations of their respective country, and the task was designed to minimize cognitive burden during labeling. The scenarios were generated using a language model and subsequently reviewed and annotated by human experts, with duplicates and inconsistent outputs removed during dataset curation. Additionally, generative AI tools were used only to improve the clarity and grammar of the manuscript during writing; they were not used for literature search, research design, or idea generation. All scientific contributions, analyses, and conclusions presented in this work are the result of the authors’ own research.
