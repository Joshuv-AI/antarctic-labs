 INTRODUCTION
The purpose of this letter report is to document the mitigation of adverse effects of a proposed
undertaking that would remove hazardous materials from the Engine Installation Vehicle (EIV) in Area
25 of the Nevada National Security Site (NNNSS) in compliance with Section 106 of the National
Historic Preservation Act (NHPA) and the terms of the 2024 Programmatic Agreement among the U.S.
Department of Energy and the Nevada State Historic Preservation Officer and the Advisory Council on
Historic Preservation Concerning the Protection of Historic Properties on the Nevada National Security
Site, Nye County, Nevada, hereafter referred to as the NNSS PA. The EIV (State Historic Preservation
Office [SHPO] Resource No. S3057) has been determined individually eligible for listing in the National
Register of Historic Places (NRHP) under Criteria A and C and as a contributing element to the Nuclear
Rocket Development Station (NRDS) Historic District, which is eligible for the NRHP under all four
Significance Criteria (Reno et al. 2023; Reed 2024).
The hazardous materials proposed for removal are a stack of lead counterweights and the concrete
transfer shield assembly.
 MITIGATION FOR THE ENGINE INTALLATION VEHICLE
The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), National Nuclear Security Administration Nevada Field Office
(NNSA/NFO), in consultation with the SHPO and the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation
(ACHP), defined standard mitigation for undertakings adversely affecting contributing elements in
historic districts in Appendix D of the NNSS PA. NNSA/NFO notified the SHPO of its intention to utilize
the standard mitigation via letter dated August 5, 2025 (Huck) and the ACHP via their e106 filing system.
The SHPO was not able to issue a timely response and advised NNSA/NFO to proceed to the next step of
the PA (standard mitigation) in an email dated September 29, 2025 (De Antoni).
Appendix D.I of the NNSS PA outlines standard mitigation for contributing elements in historic districts,
such as the EIV. The mitigation consists of an updated Architectural Resource Assessment (ARA) form to
document current conditions (Appendix D.I.A), including high-quality digital images (Appendix D.I.A.1)
and plans, drawings, or annotated sketch plans (Appendix D.I.A.2), and a brief letter report describing
mitigation contents and summarizing the historic significance (Appendix D.I.B).
This mitigation report documents the completion of mitigation for the EIV in accordance with the NNSS
PA Appendix D. An ARA Update form was prepared to satisfy NNSS PA Appendix D.I.A (see
Attachment A). It includes a description of the historic property, high-quality digital photographs, and
current annotated sketch plans with emphasis on the elements proposed for removal.
 DESCRIPTION OF THE ENGINE INSTALLATION VEHICLE
The EIV was a critical component of the Railroad Transportation System (RTS) in the NRDS, a
specialized group of three railcars designed and constructed to handle nuclear rocketry test objects
(Drollinger 1999). A Manned Control Car (MCC) housed a crew of two personnel with controls tied
through umbilicals allowing remote operation of adjoining cars. The EIV was designed to emplace and
remove the nuclear engine or reactor from the test stands. The third element, the prime mover, provided
the primary tractive force for the RTS. The prime mover (also known as the L1 locomotive) could be
manned or unmanned and remotely operated from inside the MCC. After a test, the RTS retrieved the
highly radioactive reactors and engines for disassembly and study. All three RTS specialty vehicles
included radiation shielding and special safety and operating systems for these functions.

The EIV is about 50 feet long, 19 feet wide, and 30 feet tall. It weighs 70 tons and was able to carry 20
tons. The chassis is a flatcar with an added superstructure to hold the various test objects. It had the
capabilities to move by inches the entire car back and forth in the test stand, level the reactor or engine by
moving it back and forth and side to side, and precisely position the object in the test stand with various
vertical and lateral carriages. Other equipment included a programmed and remote manipulator system,
television cameras for viewing the operations, a load readout system, radiating monitor system, dust
cover, and a nozzle closure actuator to install or remove the nozzle on the engine (O’Neill and Wedding
2022:23-24).
The NRDS shuttered in 1973, but in 1977, the EIV received a new purpose when it was modified for use
during spent fuel experiments on the NNSS. For these experiments, the EIV transported spent fuel casks
and emplaced them in dry storage wells (O’Neill and Wedding 2022:23). The modifications to the EIV
proposed in 2025 include removal of two elements: a stack of lead counterweights and the concrete
transfer shield assembly which was added as part of the 1977 modifications (Figure 1).

The counterweights consist of seven weights stacked on top of one another near the rear of the EIV. Each
is a 67 × 67 × 12-inch lead box filled with concrete. The stack is not parallel with the sides of the EIV but
instead rotated 45 degrees to form a rough diamond shape in between two electrical cabinets. The weights
are black with yellow stenciling on each side reading “5,000 LBS”. There is a large hook welded to the
top of each corner of each of the counterweights to facilitate hoisting with a crane. The hooks are made
using 1-inch diameter rods curved into a hook shape and angled out from the counterweight. The total
length of each hook is 6 inches with the welded area totaling 3.5 inches and the exposed part of the hook
measuring 2.5 inches from each corner. The bottom weight is welded to the deck of the EIV with angle
brackets and further held stationary by the total weight above it. None of the other weights are currently
welded together, although residual welding markings match locations in an engineering drawing showing

As described in O’Neill and Wedding, the EIV transfer shield was added in 1977 to minimize personnel
radiation exposure during transfer of canisters from the hot bay to dry wells in a storage area as part of the
spent fuel experiments at the Engine Maintenance Assembly and Disassembly (E-MAD) facility. The

to be picked up simultaneously for transfer to the dry well; together they were called the canister/shield
plug assembly (Figure 2).

The transfer shield was controlled by the MCC. The controls allow the drive system to move the transfer
shield vertically, longitudinally, and laterally (Unterzuber et al. 1982:A-23). Other features include a
winch to raise and lower the canister/shield plug assembly, and a foot valve to open and close the bottom
of the shield during pickup and drop-off of the canister/shield assemblies (Unterzuber et al. 1982:A-23).
The transfer shield assembly is made of two nesting carbon steel cylinders approximately 25 feet tall and
3 feet wide at maximum. The annular space between the cylinders is 6.5 inches and is filled with lead shot
that was vibrated into place and surrounded with neutron-absorbing shielding oil (Unterzuber et al.

strength greater than 12 tons (Unterzuber et al. 1982:A-24).

 SIGNIFICANCE SUMMARY
The EIV, as a main component of the RTS, has previously been determined eligible for the NRHP under
Significance Criteria A and C by the DOE in consultation with the SHPO (James 1999 and Reed 2022).
The RTS was exceptionally important to the development and research of nuclear-propelled rockets. The
EIV, in particular, was highly specialized to suit the needs of the tests. Its direct association with nuclear
rocket development under Criterion A is obvious, as is its unique and significant design under Criterion C
(O’Neill and Wedding 2022).
The EIV is also significant under Criteria A and C for its association with spent fuel tests in the context of
nuclear waste storage and handling. The spent fuel experiments completed at E-MAD utilizing the EIV
were important within the engineering context of nuclear waste management and served as base models

for the massive Yucca Mountain project. The reasons for the EIV’s significance within this context are
essentially the same as under the nuclear rocket development context: it was integral to the operation of
the spent fuel experiments, and its design was modified to meet the programs’ specific requirements. The
EIV retains all seven aspects of integrity from both of its periods of significance (O’Neill and Wedding
2022). In addition, the EIV is a contributing element to the NRDS Historic District (Reno et al. 2023).

 6. Narrative Eligibility Justification Update
Provide a detailed explanation of the resource’s eligibility for the National Register, including supporting historic
information, methods for evaluation under the four criteria, discussion of the seven aspects of integrity, and conclusions
about eligibility.

 The EIV (S3057) was previously determined to be individually eligible for listing in the NRHP under Criteria A and C
 with SHPO concurrence (September 23, 1999) and unevaluated as a contributing element of the E-MAD district
 (Reed 2022). See the original ARA form for that narrative eligibility justification and description.
 For the purposes of the present survey, resources within the boundary of the Nuclear Rocket Development Station
 (NRDS) Historic District were evaluated to determine if they contribute to the significance of the district. The NRDS
 Historic District is recommended eligible for listing to the NRHP under all four criteria as the testing center for the
 Rover/NERVA (Nuclear Engine for Rocket Vehicle Application) program from the initial boundary survey in 1956 to
 the end of nuclear rocket development on January 5, 1975.
 The EIV was a main component of the Railroad Transport System (RTS), which operated on the Jackass & Western
 Railroad and served the NRDS from around 1964 to 1973. The RTS consisted of three main cars: the EIV, Manned
 Control Car (MCC), and L1 locomotive. The EIV was used to emplace and remove nuclear reactors and engines and
 was important to NRDS development and research. The EIV retains all seven aspects of integrity and conveys its

 6. Narrative Eligibility Justification
Provide a detailed explanation of the resource’s eligibility for the National Register, including supporting historic
information, methods for evaluation under the four criteria, discussion of the seven aspects of integrity, and conclusions
about eligibility.
The Engine Installation Vehicle (EIV) is situated in the fenced yard of the Engine Maintenance Assembly and Disassembly
Facility (E-MAD; SHPO Resource #B4845). The E-MAD facility was determined eligible in 2019 as an individual historic
property with 28 accessory resources (ARs). That determination did not include the various rolling stock (including
locomotives, flatcars, and unique specialized cars) sitting in open storage on the siding tracks at E-MAD, either as
potential ARs or as individual resources.
A new survey of E-MAD and its vicinity is in process as of the date of this form as part of mitigation for a separate
undertaking. That survey has identified a potential E-MAD historic district that includes the E-MAD building and its 28
ARs, along with the rolling stock and additional elements in the vicinity. A final determination about the EIV’s contributing
or non-contributing status will be made as part of that survey. For the purposes of the current undertaking, the EIV is
assumed eligible as a contributor to the potential district. An ARA Update form will be provided when the district survey is
complete.
The EIV was a main component of the Railroad Transport System (RTS) in Area 25 of the Nevada National Security Site
(NNSS). The RTS operated on the Jackass & Western Railroad and served the Nuclear Rocket Development Station
(NRDS) from around 1964 to 1973. It later served several spent fuel experiment programs between 1977 and 1986.
During the second period of use, it operated within the E-MAD compound, one of the primary facilities within the NRDS.
The RTS consisted of three main cars: the EIV, Manned Control Car (MCC), and L1 locomotive, also known as the prime
mover. Other utility cars on the Jackass & Western Railroad included two diesel-electric locomotives (L2, and L3), a
specialized spacer flatcar, and at least ten common flatcars. The EIV, MCC, and L1 were determined eligible for listing in
the NRHP as the RTS under Significance Criteria A and C by the DOE in consultation with the SHPO in 1999 (James). In
researching the determination of eligibility, only the evaluation report (Drollinger 1999) and concurrence letter (James
1999) were found. No resource number, Historic Resource Inventory Forms (HRIF), or ARA forms appear to exist. Due to
the lack of a resource number and documentation forms, DRI requested a current resource number and prepared new
ARA forms for this known historic property utilizing information from the 1999 report and research conducted for the
current undertaking.
The L1 was relocated to the Radioactive Materials Storage Facility (RMSF) in another part of the NRDS at an unknown
date, and it is not within the Area of Potential Effect (APE) for the current undertaking. As a result, documenting the RTS
on a single form was not practicable. Instead, the EIV and MCC were each assigned an individual resource number and
documented on separate ARA forms. Doing so will ensure that each car has its own historical record moving forward.

A historical overview of the EIV’s use, a summary of significance based on the previous determination of eligibility for the
RTS, and an update to the reasons for the EIV’s significance are provided below. Refer to the associated Section 106
report (DRI Report SR112221-1) for full narrative contexts on the nuclear rocket development and spent fuel
experimentation programs, as well as transportation elements related to them.
Historical Overview
Rail transportation was a key component of the NRDS from its beginning. The Jackass & Western Railroad supported the
railcars that traveled among NRDS facilities, transporting nuclear rocket reactors and engines. Railcars used during the
nuclear rocket development programs included three locomotives (L1, L2, and L3), the MCC, the EIV, a specialized
spacer flatcar, and at least ten common flatcars. The main three cars, the MCC, EIV, and L1 became known as the
Railroad Transport System (RTS).
During the nuclear rocket development program, the EIV was used to emplace and remove nuclear engines and reactors
from the test stands in Area 25. It also served to support and protect the engine or reactor during transport. It was
propelled by the L1 and/or MCC, depending on the operation. A highly specialized vehicle, the EIV had the capabilities to
move by inches the entire car back and forth in the test stand, level the reactor or engine by moving it fore and aft and
side to side, and precisely position the object in the test stand with various vertical and lateral carriages (NERVA Program
1968).
After the closure of the NRDS, the E-MAD facility was repurposed as part of spent fuel experiments aimed at solving
problems related to temporary and permanent storage and handling of spent fuel casks from commercial nuclear power
reactors. The EIV was retrofitted correspondingly for use in the experiments. Its superstructure was partially modified to
be able to grasp, support, and emplace the casks (Patrick 1986).
As cars operating on the Jackass & Western reached the end of their use in the NRDS and at E-MAD, they were typically
moved to the RMSF. Several flatcars and the L1 locomotive are located there to the present day. The EIV and MCC both
remain in the E-MAD yard with flatcars F5, F6, and F9. In 2010 and 2006, the L2 and L3 locomotives, respectively, were
transferred to the Nevada State Railroad Museum in Boulder City.
Significance Summary and Update
The significance statement for the RTS, including the EIV, MCC, and L1/prime mover, in Drollinger 1999 is as follows:
 The Railroad Transportation System is recommended to be considered eligible to the NRHP under criteria a and
 c, consideration g for its exceptional importance in the development and research of nuclear-propelled rockets for
 the space program of the United States from the 1950s to the 1970s. Technology developed for nuclear-propelled
 rockets is still part of the space program today and its role will eventually become greater and more important as
 larger quantities of dependable and long-lived fuel sources are required for future exploratory and other similar
 far-ranging types of missions in the solar system and into deep space (Angelo and Buden 1985:ix; Porta 1995).
 The transport system consisted of specially-modified train engines and a flatcar for conveying and emplacing
 nuclear reactors and engines between assembly, disassembly, and testing facilities at the NRDS complex on the
 NTS. Modification of the vehicles involved radiation shielding and special safety and operating systems in order to
 handle the objects, particularly after testing when they were highly radioactive (8).
The basic rationale for the significance of the RTS and each of its associated cars under Criteria A and C remains valid.
The system was exceptionally important to the development and research of nuclear-propelled rockets. The EIV, MCC,
and L1 were integral to the operation and success of reactor and engine tests. The EIV, in particular, was highly
specialized to suit the needs of the tests. Its direct association with nuclear rocket development under Criterion A is
obvious, as is its unique and significant design under Criterion C.
The Drollinger report does not discuss in detail the EIV’s later context and period of use related to spent fuel handling and
storage experiments at E-MAD; however, the EIV appears to also possess significance under Criteria A and C within this
context and period based on current research. The spent fuel experiments completed at E-MAD utilizing the EIV were
important within the engineering context of nuclear waste management and served as base models for the massive (still
unfinished) Yucca Mountain project. As in the nuclear rocket development context, the EIV was critical and integral to the
operation of the spent fuel tests. It was modified specifically to meet program needs, and its existence at E-MAD was one
of the key reasons the facility was selected for the experiments. The design modifications remain intact and convey the
unique engineering requirements of the spent fuel tests. Therefore, it is recommended that the EIV is eligible for listing in
the NRHP under Criteria A and C for both its associations with nuclear rocket development and nuclear waste storage
and handling experimentation.

Integrity
The EIV retains all seven aspects of integrity. Though it was modified to handle spent fuel casks in 1977, the
modifications reflect its second period of significance within the context of nuclear waste storage and handling

 7. Narrative Architectural Description
Provide a detailed description of the resource, including all character defining features, potential construction methods,
potential alterations (both historic and non-historic), and any accessory resources.

The EIV is a specialty railcar. It is roughly 50 ft long, 19 ft wide, 30 ft in height. The EIV weighs 70 tons and was able to
carry 20 tons. The chassis is a flatcar with an added superstructure to hold the various test objects. An expanding
umbilical system was attached between the EIV and the MCC for remote control of these functions. Other equipment
included a programmed and remote manipulator system, television cameras for viewing the operations, a load readout
system, radiating monitor system, dust cover, and a nozzle closure actuator to install or remove the nozzle on the engine
(NERVA Program 1968).

The EIV was retrofitted with a transfer shield to minimize personnel radiation exposure during transfer of canisters from
the hot bay to dry wells in a storage area as part of the spent fuel experiments at E-MAD. It received other minor
modifications and changes to equipment, as well (Patrick 1986). It retains the modifications from the spent fuel era at
present. The EIV is currently openly stored on Spur B outside the Cold Bay on the north side of E-MAD.

The EIV’s exterior is in good condition with some paint wear and surface rust due to exposure to the elements while in
open storage. The operational condition of the EIV’s mounting and grappling systems, controls and equipment gauges,
trucks and couplers was not assessed as part of the current undertaking.

 INTRODUCTION
The purpose of this letter report is to document the mitigation of adverse effects of a proposed
undertaking that would remove hazardous materials from the Manned Control Car (MCC) in Area 25 of
the Nevada National Security Site (NNSS) in compliance with Section 106 of the National Historic
Preservation Act (NHPA) and the terms of the 2024 Programmatic Agreement among the U.S.
Department of Energy and the Nevada State Historic Preservation Officer and the Advisory Council on
Historic Preservation Concerning the Protection of Historic Properties on the Nevada National Security
Site, Nye County, Nevada, hereafter referred to as the NNSS PA. The MCC (State Historic Preservation
Office [SHPO] Resource No. S3058) has been determined individually eligible for listing in the National
Register of Historic Places (NRHP) under Criteria A and C and as a contributing element to the Nuclear
Rocket Development Station (NRDS) Historic District, which is eligible for the NRHP under all four
Significance Criteria (Reno et al. 2023; Reed 2024).
Original plans for the undertaking were to remove the MCC from its current location, either by disposing
of it, dismantling it, or transferring it to an offsite location; these plans were revised to leave it in place in
2025. However, to meet the requirements of the Federal Facility Agreement and Consent Order (FFACO)
for the NNSS, hazardous materials must be removed from the railcar. The hazardous materials proposed
for removal are a series of universal waste light fixtures.
 MITIGATION FOR THE MANNED CONTROL CAR
The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), National Nuclear Security Administration Nevada Field Office
(NNSA/NFO), in consultation with the SHPO and the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation
(ACHP), defined standard mitigation for undertakings adversely affecting contributing elements in
historic districts in Appendix D of the NNSS PA. NNSA/NFO notified the SHPO of its intention to utilize
the standard mitigation via letter dated August 5, 2025 (Huck) and the ACHP via their e106 filing system.
The SHPO was not able to issue a timely response and advised NNSA/NFO to proceed to the next step of

Appendix D.I of the NNSS PA outlines standard mitigation for contributing elements in historic districts,
such as the MCC. The mitigation consists of an updated Architectural Resource Assessment (ARA) form
to document current conditions (Appendix D.I.A), including high-quality digital images (Appendix
D.I.A.1) and plans, drawings, or annotated sketch plans (Appendix D.I.A.2), and a brief letter report
describing mitigation contents and summarizing the historic significance (Appendix D.I.B).
This mitigation report documents the completion of mitigation for the MCC in accordance with the NNSS
PA Appendix D. An ARA Update form was prepared to satisfy NNSS PA Appendix D.I.A (see
Attachment A). It includes a description of the historic property, high-quality digital photographs, and
current annotated sketch plans with emphasis on the elements proposed for removal.
 DESCRIPTION OF THE MANNED CONTROL CAR
The MCC was a critical component of the Railroad Transportation System (RTS) in the NRDS, a
specialized group of three railcars designed and constructed to handle nuclear rocketry test objects
(Drollinger 1999). The MCC housed a crew of two personnel with controls tied through umbilicals allowing
remote operation of adjoining cars. The Engine Installation Vehicle (EIV) was designed to emplace and
remove the nuclear engine or reactor from the test stands. The third element, the prime mover, provided the
primary tractive force for the RTS. The prime mover (also known as the L1 locomotive) could be manned or
unmanned and remotely operated from inside the MCC. After a test the RTS retrieved the highly radioactive

Manned Control Car Mitigation Page 1 of 4 LR070225-2-MIT

reactors and engines for disassembly and study. All three RTS specialty vehicles included radiation
shielding and special safety and operating systems for these functions.
The MCC is 32 feet long, 13 feet wide, and 18 feet tall. Weighing 107 tons, the MCC had a maximum
allowable speed of 6 mph (10 kph). The fully enclosed car is equipped with special radioactive shielding, a
radiation monitoring system, a fire control system, an emergency escape hatch, an emergency air breathing
system, an air conditioning system, electrical power, emergency tractive power, viewing aids, and
compressed air. The emergency air system was able to support its two-person crew for four hours. It also
has emergency tractive power in case of failure with the prime mover (O’Neill and Wedding 2022:22).
The NRDS shuttered in 1973, but in 1977, the MCC was repurposed for use in spent fuel experiments on
the NNSS. For these experiments, the MCC controlled the EIV, which transported spent fuel casks and
emplaced them in dry storage wells at the Engine Maintenance Assembly and Disassembly (E-MAD)
facility (O’Neill and Wedding 2022:23).
The modifications to the MCC required to fulfill FFACO obligations include removal of 25 light fixtures
(Figure 1). The exterior of the viewing window from the MCC Control Room is surrounded on all four
sides by a series of these light fixtures wired together through electrical conduit pipe. The yellow metal
pipe also acts as the mounting frame around the square window. Facing away from the window toward
the front of the car, there are seven lights along the top, five across the bottom, and six on each side for a
total of 24 lights. There is one additional light fixture of the same type mounted on the rear of the MCC.
They are all Quartzlite G-6000-1 series with 500-watt quartz-iodine lamps produced by Appleton Electric
Co. out of Chicago, Illinois. The quartz-iodine lamps (also called halogen lamps, tungsten halogen lamps,
or quartz-halogen lamps) were deemed efficient and economical due to the fact that they lasted longer and
produced a brighter light compared with other light bulbs of the time; their drawback was that that they
needed to be placed within four degrees of horizontal to maintain their efficacy long term (Appleton
Electric Co., nd).

 SIGNIFICANCE SUMMARY
The MCC, as a main component of the RTS, has previously been determined eligible for the NRHP under
Significance Criteria A and C by the DOE in consultation with the SHPO (James 1999 and Reed 2022). The
RTS was exceptionally important to the development and research of nuclear-propelled rockets. The MCC,
in particular, was highly specialized to suit the needs of the tests. Its direct association with nuclear rocket
development under Criterion A is obvious, as is its unique and significant design under Criterion C.
The MCC is also significant under Criteria A and C for its association with spent fuel tests in the context
of nuclear waste storage and handling. The spent fuel experiments completed at E-MAD utilizing the
MCC were important within the engineering context of nuclear waste management and served as base
models for the massive Yucca Mountain project. The reasons for the MCC’s significance within this
context are essentially the same as under the nuclear rocket development context: the MCC was integral
to the operation of the spent fuel experiments and its design was modified to meet the programs’ specific
requirements. The MCC retains all seven aspects of integrity from both of its periods of significance
(O’Neill and Wedding 2022). In addition, the MCC is a contributing element to the NRDS Historic
District (Reno et al. 2023).

window. Facing away from the window toward the front of the car, there are seven lights along the top, five across the
bottom, and six on each side for a total of 24 lights. There is one additional light fixture of the same type mounted on the
rear of the MCC. They are all Quartzlite G-6000-1 series with 500-watt quartz-iodine lamps produced by Appleton Electric
Co. out of Chicago, Illinois. The quartz-iodine lamps (also called halogen lamps, tungsten halogen lamps, or quartz-
halogen lamps) were deemed efficient and economical due to the fact that they lasted longer and produced a brighter light
compared with other light bulbs of the time; their drawback was that they needed to be placed within four degrees of
horizontal to maintain their efficacy long term (Appleton Electric Co., nd).

  6. Narrative Eligibility Justification
Provide a detailed explanation of the resource’s eligibility for the National Register, including supporting historic
information, methods for evaluation under the four criteria, discussion of the seven aspects of integrity, and conclusions
about eligibility.
The Manned Control Car (MCC) is situated in the fenced yard of the Engine Maintenance Assembly and Disassembly
Facility (E-MAD; SHPO Resource #B4845). The E-MAD facility was determined eligible in 2019 as an individual historic
property with 28 accessory resources (ARs). That determination did not include the various rolling stock (including
locomotives, flatcars, and unique specialized cars) sitting in open storage on the siding tracks at E-MAD, either as
potential ARs or as individual resources.
A new survey of E-MAD and its vicinity is in process as of the date of this form as part of mitigation for a separate
undertaking. That survey has identified a potential E-MAD historic district that includes the E-MAD building and its 28
ARs, along with the rolling stock and additional elements in the vicinity. A final determination about the MCC’s contributing
or non-contributing status will be made as part of that survey. For the purposes of the current undertaking, the MCC is
assumed eligible as a contributor to the potential district. An ARA Update form will be provided when the district survey is
complete.
The MCC was a main component of the Railroad Transport System (RTS) in Area 25 of the Nevada National Security Site
(NNSS). The RTS operated on the Jackass & Western Railroad and served the Nuclear Rocket Development Station
(NRDS) from around 1964 to 1973. It later served several spent fuel experiment programs between 1977 and 1986.
During the second period of use, it operated within the E-MAD compound, one of the primary facilities within the NRDS.
The RTS consisted of three main cars: the Manned Control Car (MCC), the Engine Installation Vehicle (EIV), and L1
locomotive, also known as the prime mover. Other utility cars on the Jackass & Western Railroad included two diesel-
electric locomotives (L2, and L3), a specialized spacer flatcar, and at least ten common flatcars. The EIV, MCC, and L1
were determined eligible for listing in the NRHP as the RTS under Significance Criteria A and C by the DOE in
consultation with the SHPO in 1999 (James). In researching the determination of eligibility, only the evaluation report
(Drollinger 1999) and concurrence letter (James 1999) were found. No resource number, Historic Resource Inventory
Forms (HRIF), or ARA forms appear to exist. Due to the lack of a resource number and documentation forms, DRI
requested a current resource number and prepared new ARA forms for this known historic property utilizing information
from the 1999 report and research conducted for the current undertaking.
The L1 was relocated to the Radioactive Materials Storage Facility (RMSF) in another part of the NRDS at an unknown
date, and it is not within the Area of Potential Effect (APE) for the current undertaking. As a result, documenting the RTS
on a single form was not practicable. Instead, the EIV and MCC were each assigned an individual resource number and
documented on separate ARA forms. Doing so will ensure that each car has its own historical record moving forward.

A historical overview of the MCC’s use, a summary of significance based on the previous determination of eligibility for the
RTS, and an update to the reasons for the MCC’s significance are provided below. Refer to the associated Section 106
report (DRI Report SR112221-1) for full narrative contexts on the nuclear rocket development and spent fuel
experimentation programs, as well as transportation elements related to them.
Historical Overview
Rail transportation was a key component of the NRDS from its beginning. The Jackass & Western Railroad supported the
railcars that traveled among NRDS facilities, transporting nuclear rocket reactors and engines. Railcars used during the
nuclear rocket development programs included three locomotives (L1, L2, and L3), the MCC, the EIV, a specialized
spacer flatcar, and at least ten common flatcars. The main three cars, the MCC, EIV, and L1 became known as the
Railroad Transport System (RTS).
During the nuclear rocket development program, the MCC was used to control the EIV, which emplaced and removed
nuclear engines and reactors from the test stands in Area 25, and the L1 locomotive in a radioactive environment. It
provided safe working space for two operators. A highly specialized vehicle, the MCC had a lead-shielded cab with leaded
windows to protect the operators, while allowing for full visibility. It hosted all of the controls for itself, the EIV, and L1
(NERVA Program 1968).
After the closure of the NRDS, the E-MAD facility was repurposed as part of spent fuel experiments aimed at solving
problems related to temporary and permanent storage and handling of spent fuel casks from commercial nuclear power
reactors. Cameras, emergency bypass components, and other minor modifications were made to the MCC for use in the
experiments. The MCC would continue to propel the retrofitted EIV until the spent fuel programs ended in 1986.
As cars operating on the Jackass & Western reached the end of their use in the NRDS and at E-MAD, they were typically
moved to the RMSF. Several flatcars and the L1 locomotive are located there to the present day. The EIV and MCC both
remain in the E-MAD yard with flatcars F5, F6, and F9. In 2010 and 2006, the L2 and L3 locomotives, respectively, were
transferred to the Nevada State Railroad Museum in Boulder City.
Significance Summary and Update
The significance statement for the RTS, including the EIV, MCC, and L1/prime mover, in Drollinger 1999 is as follows:
 The Railroad Transportation System is recommended to be considered eligible to the NRHP under criteria a and
 c, consideration g for its exceptional importance in the development and research of nuclear-propelled rockets for
 the space program of the United States from the 1950s to the 1970s. Technology developed for nuclear-propelled
 rockets is still part of the space program today and its role will eventually become greater and more important as
 larger quantities of dependable and long-lived fuel sources are required for future exploratory and other similar
 far-ranging types of missions in the solar system and into deep space (Angelo and Buden 1985:ix; Porta 1995).
 The transport system consisted of specially-modified train engines and a flatcar for conveying and emplacing
 nuclear reactors and engines between assembly, disassembly, and testing facilities at the NRDS complex on the
 NTS. Modification of the vehicles involved radiation shielding and special safety and operating systems in order to
 handle the objects, particularly after testing when they were highly radioactive (8).
The basic rationale for the significance of the RTS and each of its associated cars under Criteria A and C remains valid.
The system was exceptionally important to the development and research of nuclear-propelled rockets. The EIV, MCC,
and L1 were integral to the operation and success of reactor and engine tests. The MCC, in particular, was highly
specialized to suit the needs of the tests. Its direct association with nuclear rocket development under Criterion A is
obvious, as is its unique and significant design under Criterion C.
The Drollinger report does not discuss in detail the MCC’s later context and period of use related to spent fuel handling
and storage experiments at E-MAD; however, the MCC appears to also possess significance under Criteria A and C
within this context and period based on current research. The spent fuel experiments completed at E-MAD utilizing the
MCC were important within the engineering context of nuclear waste management and served as base models for the
massive (still unfinished) Yucca Mountain project. As in the nuclear rocket development context, the MCC was critical and
integral to the operation of the spent fuel tests. It was modified to meet program needs, and its existence at E-MAD was
one of the key reasons the facility was selected for the experiments. The design modifications remain intact and convey
the unique engineering requirements of the spent fuel tests. Therefore, it is recommended that the MCC is eligible for
listing in the NRHP under Criteria A and C for both its associations with nuclear rocket development and nuclear waste
storage and handling experimentation.

Integrity
The MCC retains all seven aspects of integrity. Though it was modified for spent fuel experimentation in 1977, the
modifications were minor and generally related to small equipment alterations, rather than large engineering changes.
Furthermore, the changes occurred during the MCC’s second period of significance and, therefore, have gained
significance in their own right. Thus, the MCC retains sufficient integrity to reflect both of its periods of significance and
engineering contexts.

  7. Narrative Architectural Description
Provide a detailed description of the resource, including all character defining features, potential construction methods,
potential alterations (both historic and non-historic), and any accessory resources.

The MCC is approximately 32 ft long, 13 ft wide, and 18 ft in height. The shielded cab is roughly 8 ft × 8 ft × 8 ft, with a few
additional inches in length and width. Overall, the MCC weighs 107 tons; the cab alone weighs 50 tons. The diesel-electric
vehicle had a maximum allowable speed of 6 mph (10 kph). The fully enclosed car is equipped with special radioactive
shielding, a radiation monitoring system, a fire control system, an emergency escape hatch, an emergency air breathing
system, an air conditioning system, electrical power, emergency tractive power, closed circuit television, viewing aids, and
compressed air. The emergency air system was able to support its two-person crew for four hours. It also has emergency
tractive power in case of failure with the prime mover (NERVA 1968). The MCC is currently openly stored on Spur B
outside the Cold Bay on the north side of E-MAD.
