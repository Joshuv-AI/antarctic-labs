Pure Red Cell Aplasia as an Isolated Paraneoplastic Manifestation of Thymoma: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Heba Amer, Modar Alajlani, Mhd Rashad Jamle, Hareth Almanfe, Mohammad Shbat, Mohammad Abd Alrahman Saif, Anas Abdulrazzak, Ahmed Nabhan, Bassam Darwish
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThymomas are epithelial tumors of the anterior mediastinum and are frequently associated with paraneoplastic autoimmune disorders. Pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) is a rare but clinically significant complication, caused by immune‐mediated suppression of erythropoiesis. We report a case of a 52‐year‐old male with a previously diagnosed but untreated thymoma, who presented after 9 years with severe normocytic, normochromic anemia and reticulocytopenia. Bone marrow examination revealed selective erythroid suppression with preservation of myeloid and megakaryocytic lineages, consistent with PRCA. Imaging demonstrated a large anterior mediastinal mass measuring 16 cm. The patient received supportive blood transfusions followed by surgical thymectomy. Histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis confirmed a mixed spindle cell thymoma (Type A/AB). Postoperatively, the patient achieved complete hematological recovery without additional immunosuppressive therapy. This case highlights that thymoma‐associated PRCA may present as an isolated paraneoplastic manifestation without neuromuscular symptoms. It underscores the crucial role of thymectomy in restoring erythropoiesis and achieving transfusion independence. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of thymoma‐associated PRCA in Syria, providing valuable insight for clinicians managing similar rare presentations.

Introduction

Thymomas are epithelial tumors and represent the most common neoplasms of the anterior mediastinum [1]. They are frequently associated with paraneoplastic autoimmune syndromes, with pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) being a rare but clinically significant manifestation [1]. First described by Kaznelson in 1922 [2], PRCA is characterized by a selective failure of erythropoiesis [3]. In acquired cases, thymic epithelial neoplasms are implicated in approximately 5% of diagnoses [3]. These autoimmune reactions are thought to result from structural disruption of the thymus, impairing T‐lymphocyte maturation and allowing autoreactive T cells to escape [1]. Management of thymoma‐associated PRCA is challenging, often requiring both surgical and pharmacological interventions. Extended thymectomy, with or without adjunctive immunosuppressive therapy, can achieve long‐term remission of anemia [3]. Here we present a 52‐year‐old Arab male who suffered from thymoma 9 years before developing pure red cell aplasia and was treated successfully with thymectomy. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first documented case of thymoma‐associated PRCA in Syria, highlighting its clinical and educational significance.

Case History/Examination

A 52‐year‐old male smoker was initially diagnosed with thymoma 9 years previously after presenting with cough productive of frothy sputum and exertional dyspnea (New York Heart Association [NYHA] class I). The diagnosis was established by CT‐guided biopsy; however, the patient did not pursue further evaluation or treatment at that time.

2 months prior to the current admission, he presented with chest pain, progressive fatigue, generalized weakness, and worsening exertional dyspnea (NYHA class II). He denied weight loss, night sweats, or hemoptysis. His past medical history was otherwise unremarkable. His medications included prednisolone 30 mg daily, calcium, and vitamin D supplementation. He had no prior surgeries and no known drug allergies.

On examination, his vital signs were stable: blood pressure 110/80 mmHg, heart rate 90 beats per minute, respiratory rate 18 breaths per minute, temperature 36.5°C, and oxygen saturation 99% on room air.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

Differential Diagnosis

In view of the severe normocytic anemia with reticulocytopenia, differential diagnoses included iron deficiency anemia, anemia of chronic disease, hemolytic anemia, aplastic anemia, and bone marrow infiltration. Hemolysis was excluded due to normal bilirubin levels and absence of hemolytic features on peripheral smear. Iron deficiency was unlikely given elevated serum iron and ferritin levels with normal total iron‐binding capacity. Bone marrow findings demonstrated selective erythroid suppression with preserved myeloid and megakaryocytic lineages, supporting the diagnosis of pure red cell aplasia.

Investigations

Laboratory evaluation revealed severe anemia with hemoglobin 4.9 g/dL, hematocrit 13%, mean corpuscular volume 81 fL, and red cell distribution width 50%. White blood cell count was 18.9 × 109/L. Reticulocyte count was low at 1.1%. Serum iron was elevated at 246 μg/dL, total iron‐binding capacity was normal (230 μg/dL), and ferritin was elevated (441 ng/mL). Folate and vitamin B12 levels were within normal limits. Total and direct bilirubin levels were normal. Peripheral blood smear demonstrated normocytic, normochromic erythrocytes.

Bone marrow aspiration revealed marked erythroid suppression with preserved myeloid activity. Biopsy demonstrated a hypercellular marrow (~70% cellularity) with an increased myeloid‐to‐erythroid ratio secondary to myeloid hyperplasia with left shift, markedly decreased erythroid precursors, adequate megakaryocytes, normal plasma cells, and no fibrosis, consistent with pure red cell aplasia (Figures 1a–c).

Pulmonary function tests and transthoracic echocardiography were within normal limits. Chest radiography demonstrated a mediastinal mass (Figure 2). Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography revealed a right‐sided anterior mediastinal mass measuring approximately 16 cm in greatest diameter, without lymphadenopathy or pleural effusion (Figures 3 and 4).

Treatment

The patient received 6 units of packed red blood cells, resulting in improvement of hemoglobin to 11.3 g/dL and hematocrit to 34%. Occult gastrointestinal bleeding was excluded. He subsequently underwent surgical thymectomy via right thoracotomy through the fourth intercostal space (Figure 5).

Histopathological examination revealed a well‐demarcated 16‐cm spindle cell neoplasm composed of spindle‐shaped epithelial cells with an associated lymphoid component, without significant mitotic activity or necrosis, and with clear surgical margins (Figure 6). Immunohistochemistry demonstrated cytokeratin positivity confirming epithelial origin, while CD34 and S100 were negative (Figure 7), consistent with spindle cell thymoma (Type A/AB).

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Postoperatively, the patient experienced sustained hematological recovery, with postoperative hemoglobin stabilizing at approximately 15.5 g/dL during follow‐up. No additional transfusions or immunosuppressive therapy were required. Follow‐up chest radiography was unremarkable (Figure 8).

At follow‐up, the patient remained in good general condition with complete remission of pure red cell aplasia.

Discussion and Literature Review

Thymoma is a rare malignancy, accounting for 0.2%–1.5% of all cancers [4], and is frequently associated with autoimmune complications, most commonly myasthenia gravis [5]. Pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) is a rare type of anemia characterized by profound normocytic, normochromic anemia, marked reticulocytopenia, and near‐complete absence of erythroblasts in the bone marrow, while granulocyte and megakaryocyte production remains intact [6]. Approximately 10% of PRCA cases are linked to thymoma; however, among patients already diagnosed with thymoma, PRCA remains uncommon [7]. Acquired PRCA can be classified as primary or secondary, with thymoma being a recognized cause of secondary acquired PRCA [8].

The pathogenesis of thymoma‐associated PRCA is not fully understood. Proposed mechanisms include production of serum thymic factor by the tumor, which activates suppressor T cells that inhibit erythroid precursors, and thymic production of IgG, which may release cytotoxic or suppressive cytokines that impair erythropoiesis [9]. Clinically, PRCA should be suspected in patients presenting with severe anemia and an inappropriately low reticulocyte count. After excluding common causes of bone marrow failure, rare etiologies such as thymoma‐associated PRCA should be considered in the differential diagnosis.

Small thymomas are often asymptomatic. As tumors enlarge, patients may develop symptoms from mediastinal compression, including chest pain and dyspnea [10]. Anemia commonly presents with fatigue, pallor, and exertional dyspnea. In our patient, chest pain, fatigue, generalized weakness, and dyspnea were the main presenting symptoms, without systemic features such as weight loss or night sweats. Given the absence of alternative causes, surgical thymectomy was pursued, resulting in complete hematological recovery without immunosuppressive therapy.

Computed tomography (CT) is the imaging modality of choice for thymoma, providing essential information for differentiating it from other anterior mediastinal masses [11]. Histopathological confirmation is typically obtained via core needle biopsy [12]. In this case, initial chest radiography revealed mediastinal widening, and subsequent CT localized a large anterior mediastinal mass measuring 16 cm. Surgical excision confirmed a spindle cell thymoma (Type A/AB) with negative margins. Immunohistochemistry was positive for cytokeratin, confirming the epithelial nature of the tumor.

Bone marrow examination remains essential for confirming PRCA, demonstrating selective reduction of erythroid precursors with preserved granulopoiesis and megakaryopoiesis [13]. In our patient, aspiration and biopsy revealed marked erythroid suppression with a hypercellular marrow and elevated myeloid‐to‐erythroid ratio, consistent with PRCA.

Thymectomy has demonstrated efficacy in managing thymoma‐associated paraneoplastic syndromes; however, most required adjuvant therapy. Reported remission rates for PRCA following thymectomy are approximately 30%, often requiring adjunctive immunosuppressive therapy if erythropoiesis does not recover within 6 months [14]. In our case, postoperative recovery was rapid and sustained, with no further transfusions or immunosuppressive treatment required. The favorable hematologic response in our patient may be attributed to complete surgical resection with negative margins, which likely eliminated the primary source of immune dysregulation driving erythroid suppression. In addition, the tumor histology (Type A/AB spindle cell thymoma), which is generally associated with a more indolent biological behavior, may have contributed to a better immunological and clinical response. The absence of residual disease likely played a key role in the sustained recovery of erythropoiesis without the need for immunosuppressive therapy.

Mortality in thymoma‐associated PRCA is primarily due to infection, heart failure, or tumor progression, with poor prognosis associated with resistance to immunosuppressive therapy and recurrence of anemia [15]. Fortunately, our patient experienced an uncomplicated postoperative course with no recurrence or treatment‐related complications.

We performed a concise literature review examining the clinical characteristics, temporal relationship, management strategies, and outcomes of patients diagnosed with thymoma‐associated pure red cell aplasia (PRCA). Our final analysis incorporated 20 publications from 2000 to 2025, comprising a total of 27 reported patients. The cohort included 14 females (51.9%) and 13 males (48.1%), with ages ranging from 25 to 90 years. PRCA most frequently developed after thymoma diagnosis or thymectomy (13/27, 48.1%), followed by concurrent presentation with thymoma (12/27, 44.4%), while in a minority of cases PRCA preceded thymoma detection (2/27, 7.4%). Among patients who developed PRCA after thymoma, the reported interval ranged from 1 week to 13 years; notably, more than half of cases with available data (5/9, 55.6%) occurred within the first year, whereas very late presentations beyond 10 years were uncommon. Treatment approaches varied considerably and included thymectomy alone, immunosuppressive therapy (most commonly cyclosporine A or corticosteroids), or combination regimens. Hematologic improvement or complete remission was achieved in the majority of patients, particularly those receiving immunosuppressive therapy. In contrast, remission following thymectomy alone was documented in only 2 of 27 cases (7.4%), underscoring the rarity of surgical monotherapy success. Compared with these findings, our patient demonstrated complete and sustained hematologic recovery following thymectomy alone, without the need for adjunctive immunosuppressive therapy. This places our case among the very small subset of patients achieving remission with surgical treatment alone and highlights the potential for thymectomy to be curative in carefully selected cases. Conclusion

In conclusion, this case highlights the importance of timely clinical intervention and patient awareness to prevent neglect of diagnosed conditions such as thymoma. Although thymoma is classically associated with myasthenia gravis, clinicians should recognize that severe isolated normocytic anemia with reticulocytopenia may represent the primary paraneoplastic manifestation, even in the absence of neuromuscular symptoms. This report describes the first documented case in Syria of thymoma‐associated pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) presenting as an isolated complication. The complete hematological recovery observed following thymectomy emphasizes the pivotal role of surgical resection in restoring erythropoiesis and achieving transfusion independence in selected patients.
