Abstract
. The simplest genuinely multidimensional monopolist’s problem involves minimizing a linearly perturbed Dirichlet energy among nonnegative convex functions u u on an open domain X ⊂ [ 0 , ∞ ) 2 X\subset[0,\infty)^{2} . The geometry of the region of strict convexity Ω ⊂ X \Omega\subset X for the unique minimizer u u is of central interest. A relatively closed portion X 1 0 ⊂ X X_{1}^{0}\subset X of the domain is comprised of line segments starting and ending on ∂ X \partial X along which u u is affine. For convex polygons and potentially all domains X ⊂ 𝐑 2 X\subset{\mathbf{R}}^{2} , we build on results with Zhang to show that outside X 1 0 ∪ { u = 0 } X_{1}^{0}\cup\{u=0\} , the free boundary of Ω \Omega is a continuous curve of Hausdorff dimension one, and that Ω \Omega has density 1 / 2 1/2 along it (and is C loc α C^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}} for all 0 < α < 1 0<\alpha<1 ), except perhaps at a discrete set of singular points. We do this by showing that much of the free boundary solves an obstacle problem whose endogenous obstacle is C 2 C^{2} . From a slightly stronger conclusion, we deduce the free boundary becomes locally C ∞ C^{\infty} outside a closed set whose relative interior is empty. In response to the circulation of the present manuscript, we received a concurrent but independent work of Chen, Figalli and Zhang who verify a strengthening sufficient for this partial regularity result; (they show in particular that α = 1 \alpha=1 and the discrete set mentioned above is empty).

Lemma 2.3

(Reduction to the obstacle problem)

Let

.

2.2.
Regularity of the obstacle

We now show that the obstacle

gains two degrees of regularity compared to the continuous

Remark 2.4

(The Heine-Cantor uniform continuity theorem)
.

Recall that any function which is continuous on a compact set is uniformly continuous, and thus possesses a uniform modulus of continuity, by the Heine-Cantor theorem
[
16
]
*Theorem 4.3.32.

Proposition 2.5

(Obstacle gains two degrees of regularity compared to
R(t)
)
.

Suppose that
X\subset\mathbf{R}^{2}
is
a convex set satisfying hypotheses (a) and (c) of Theorem
1.1
. Let
x\in\Gamma
be a tame free boundary point.
Let
u_{1}
be the
minimal convex extension (
2.9
)
of
u|_{\mathcal{N}}
to
U
, where
\mathcal{N}
and
U
are defined in (
2.2
) and (
2.8
) respectively. Suppose that

has modulus of continuity
\sigma
. Then,
D^{2}u_{1}
has modulus of continuity
\rho(t)=C\max\{\sigma(t),t\}
for some constant
C
. In particular,
u_{1}\in C^{2}(\overline{U})
.

Proof.

Recall the Laplacian of
u_{1}
is given (
2.10
) by

\Delta u_{1}(x(r,t))=3-\frac{3r-2R(t)}{r+\frac{\xi(t)\times\dot{\gamma}(t)}{|\dot{\xi}(t)|}}.

By
[
23
]
*Lemma 6.5,
\xi(t)
is Lipschitz. Moreover, from (
2.11
),
we have that

|\dot{\xi}(t)|=\frac{2|\dot{\gamma}(t)|(\nabla u(\gamma(t))-\gamma(t))\cdot\mathbf{n}}{R(t)^{2}}.

Since
u\in C^{1,1}(\overline{U})
by Lemma
2.3
, the fact that
R(t)\geq r_{0}>0
implies that
|\dot{\xi}|
has modulus of continuity bounded by
C\max\{\sigma(t),t\}
for some constant
C
; moreover, since
(\nabla u(\gamma(t))-\gamma(t))\cdot\mathbf{n}\geq\eta_{0}>0
on
(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
, we have
|\dot{\xi}(t)|
bounded above and below as well. So,
\Delta u_{1}
has modulus of continuity bounded by
C\max\{\sigma(t),t\}
in
(r,t)
coordinates, and so since the coordinate change between
(r,t)
coordinates and Cartesian coordinates is bi-Lipschitz by
[
23
]
*Corollary 6.6, we conclude that
\Delta u_{1}
has modulus of continuity bounded by
C\max\{\sigma(t),t\}
in Cartesian coordinates, for some possibly different constant
C
.

By the expression (
2.9
), it is clear that
\partial_{rr}^{2}u_{1}=0
, since
u_{1}
is affine in the

direction. So, the Hessian of
u_{1}
in Cartesian coordinates has a null eigenvalue, whose corresponding eigenvector is the ray with direction
\xi
. Since the eigenvectors of
D^{2}u_{1}
are orthogonal, we see that
\frac{\dot{\xi}}{|\dot{\xi}|}
is also an eigenvector of
D^{2}u_{1}
, on account of it being orthogonal to
\xi
(since
|\xi(t)|=1
for each
t
). Therefore, we can write the Hessian of
u_{1}
as

(2.13)

D^{2}u_{1}=P^{-1}\mathrm{diag}(\Delta u_{1},0)P,

where
P
is the orthogonal matrix whose rows are the eigenvectors
\xi
and
\frac{\dot{\xi}}{|\dot{\xi}|}
of
D^{2}u_{1}
.

We claim that
\xi
and
\frac{\dot{\xi}}{|\dot{\xi}|}
are Lipschitz in Cartesian coordinates. Indeed,
\xi(t)
is Lipschitz
[
23
]
*Lemma 6.5, and so since the change of coordinates between
(r,t)
coordinates and Cartesian coordinates is bi-Lipschitz
[
23
]
,
\xi
is Lipschitz in Cartesian coordinates as well. Moreover, we claim
\frac{\dot{\xi}(t)}{|\dot{\xi}(t)|}
is Lipschitz. Since
\xi(t)
is Lipschitz, let
\zeta
denote the (Lipschitz) normal vector to
\xi
satisfying
\xi\times\zeta>0
. Since
|\xi(t)|=1
for each
t
, we see that
\frac{\dot{\xi}(t)}{|\dot{\xi}(t)|}=\pm\zeta(t)
at every

. But from
[
23
]
*Remark 6.4, we know that
\xi\times\dot{\xi}>0
, therefore
\frac{\dot{\xi}(t)}{|\dot{\xi}(t)|}=\zeta(t)
is Lipschitz in
(r,t)
coordinates, and hence in Cartesian coordinates as well.

Therefore, since
\xi
,
\frac{\dot{\xi}}{|\dot{\xi}|}
, and
\Delta u_{1}
have modulus of continuity bounded by
C\max\{\sigma(t),t\}
in Cartesian coordinates, we conclude by (

) that

has modulus of continuity bounded by
C\max\{\sigma(t),t\}
for some constant
C
on
\overline{U}
. In particular,

R(t)
is continuous
by Remark
2.2
, and thus uniformly continuous on the compact set

by the Heine-Cantor Theorem of Remark

. So,

.
∎

3.
The low-regularity obstacle problem

Having reduced the problem of studying the tame free boundary to an obstacle problem, we now wish to study the obstacle problem

(3.1)

\Delta v=f1_{\{v>0\}}

in the case that
f
is only continuous. Recall that given a domain
U\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
and a nonnegative function
v\in C^{1,1}(U)
, we define its
contact set

\Lambda(v)=v^{-1}\{0\}
,

noncontact set

\Omega(v)=v^{-1}((0,\infty))

and

F(v):=\Lambda(v)\cap\overline{\Omega(v)}

as in (
2.4
)–(

).

In his pioneering work on the obstacle problem (see
[
6
]
), Caffarelli showed that when

, the free boundary of a solution to (

) can be divided into a set of

, near which the free boundary is

, and a set of
singular points
. Moreover, when
f\in C^{1,\alpha}
, he showed that the singular points are in fact contained in a
C^{1}
submanifold. Blank
[
4
]
later showed that if
f
is Dini continuous, then the free boundary is
C^{1}
-smooth near regular points. Moreover, Blank’s initial regularity result is sharp; Blank gives an example
[
4
]
*Theorem 7.3 in which
f
is continuous, but the free boundary spirals infinitely upon itself and is not even locally the graph of a function. Nonetheless, Blank still shows that there exists a dichotomy between regular and singular points on the free boundary, which will be the starting point for our investigation into the monopolist’s free boundary:

Theorem 3.1

(Caffarelli alternative for
C^{2}
obstacles
[
4
]
*Theorem 4.3)
.

Let
U\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
be a domain and
c_{0}>0
.
Let
v\in C^{1,1}(\overline{U})
be a nonnegative function such that
\Delta v=f1_{\{v>0\}}
for some
f\in C(\overline{U})
satisfying
f\geq c_{0}>0
on
\overline{U}
. Then, for any point
x_{0}\in F(v)\cap U
, either:

(a)

is a singular point of the free boundary, and

\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{|\Lambda(v)\cap B_{r}(x_{0})|}{|B_{r}|}=0,\ \mbox{\rm or}

(b)

x_{0}
is a regular point of the free boundary, meaning

\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{|\Lambda(v)\cap B_{r}(x_{0})|}{|B_{r}|}=\frac{1}{2}.

3.1.

As is typical of the obstacle problem, a crucial tool for our study of the free boundary will be blow-ups of

U\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}

For any

The following lemma follows from

Lemma 3.2

(Blank’s measure stability for blow-ups

]

Let

Proposition 3.3

(Classification of blow-ups

v_{\infty}(x-x_{0})=\frac{f(x_{0})}{2}(x\cdot\tau)_{+}^{\ 2},

where

.

Proof.

Proposition

(b) is simply a restatement of
[

]
*Theorem 2.10(c).

Now, suppose

is a singular point, and choose coordinates so that

. By
[

*Theorem 2.10(b),

is a global solution to the obstacle problem
v_{\infty}=f(0)1_{\{v_{\infty}>0\}}
. In particular,

is convex by

]
*Corollary 7. Moreover, the definition of a singular point in Theorem

combined with Lemma

implies that
\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Lambda(v_{\infty}))=0

is convex, this implies that
\Lambda(v_{\infty})
is contained in a hyperplane
\Gamma:=\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}\ |\ x\cdot\tau=0\}
for some

. Since

is

and satisfies

except on a line,

is a weak solution to
\Delta v_{\infty}=f(0)
, and therefore, by the Weyl lemma, is a classical solution to
\Delta v_{\infty}(x)=f(0)
for each for each

. Moreover, since

∎

Thanks to the ray structure of the monopolist’s problem, we can in fact show that there is a unique blow-up at a singular point of the free boundary.

3.3.
Singular points are discrete

The main theorem of this section shows that set of singular points of the monopolist’s free boundary is a discrete subset of the tame free boundary, i.e. there is no tame free boundary point which is the limit of singular tame free boundary points. Before proceeding, we need to show that every singular point is a local maximum for
R
—
and not just the accumulation point of such maxima as implied by

[
23
, Theorem 7.8]
.

Lemma 3.6

(Singular points are local maxima)
.

Let
v
,
U
, and
u_{1}
be defined as in Lemma
2.3
, and suppose
x_{0}=x(R(0),0)\in F(v)
is a singular point. Then,
0
is a strict local maximum of
R
.

Proof.

For the sake of contradiction, suppose
x_{\infty}=x(R(0),0)\in F(v)
is a singular point that is not a strict local maximum of
R
, and let
\{t_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
be a sequence of points with
t_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
and
R(t_{k})\geq R(0)
for every
k
. Choose coordinates so that
x_{\infty}=0
and

\widetilde{x_{\infty}}=\{te_{2}\ |\ -R(0)\leq t\leq 0\},

and write

x_{k}:=x(R(t_{k}),t_{k})

in the
(r,t)
-coordinates of Remark
2.2
. Define

y_{k}:=x(R(0),t_{k}).

Since
R(0)\leq R(t_{k})
for each
k
, we know that
y_{k}\in\widetilde{x_{k}}
for each
k
, and consequently

(3.5)

v(y_{k})=0\qquad\text{for every $k$.}

Now setting

r_{k}:=|y_{k}|
,
we have for large
k
that

(\frac{y_{k}}{r_{k}}\cdot e_{1})^{2}\geq c>0

for some
c
depending only on the Lipschitz constant for the
(r,t)
-coordinate change.
Moreover,
\frac{y_{k}}{r_{k}}\in\mathbf{S}^{1}
, and so possibly passing to a subsequence we may assume that
\frac{y_{k}}{r_{k}}\to y_{\infty}
for some
y_{\infty}\in\mathbf{S}^{1}
. Clearly,

(y_{\infty}\cdot e_{1})^{2}\geq c>0.

By Corollary
3.4
, let

v_{\infty}(x):=\frac{3-\Delta u_{1}(0)}{2}(x\cdot e_{1})^{2}

be the unique blow-up of
v
at
x_{\infty}
. Then, since
v_{r_{k}}\to v_{\infty}
uniformly on compact sets, we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{k}^{2}}v(y_{k})

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}v_{r_{k}}(\frac{y_{k}}{r_{k}})

\displaystyle=v_{\infty}(y_{\infty})

\displaystyle=\frac{3-\Delta u_{1}(0)}{2}(y_{\infty}\cdot e_{1})^{2}

\displaystyle>0,

a contradiction.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove that the set of tame singular points is discrete;
for Example
1.3
— the square of Rochet and Choné
[
35
,
23
]
— this means any singular points can only accumulate at two points of the fixed boundary and/or one or two points on
\Omega_{0}\cup\Omega_{1}^{0}
.

Theorem 3.7

(Singular set is discrete)
.

Let
u
solve the monopolist’s problem on a convex domain
X\subset\mathbf{R}^{2}
satisfying hypotheses (a)–(c) of Theorem
1.1
. Let
\mathcal{T}\subset\Gamma
be the set of tame free boundary points,
and let
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{T}
be the subset of singular tame free boundary points,
as in (
1.5
)–(
1.7
). Then,
\mathcal{S}
is a discrete subset of
\mathcal{T}
in the subspace topology.

Proof.

For the sake of contradiction, suppose
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal{S}
is a sequence of singular tame free boundary points converging to the tame free boundary point
x_{\infty}\in\mathcal{T}
. Since the set of singular points is relatively closed by Proposition
3.5
,
x_{\infty}
is a singular point as well. Let
U
,
v
, and
u_{1}
be defined as in Lemma
2.3
, and without loss of generality assume
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset U
. For each
k
, write

x_{k}=x(R(t_{k}),t_{k})

in the
(r,t)
-coordinates of Remark
2.2
, and let
x_{\infty}=x(R(t_{\infty}),t_{\infty})
. By choosing coordinates and parametrization we may assume that
t_{\infty}=0
,
x_{\infty}=0
, and

\widetilde{x_{\infty}}=\{te_{2}\ |\ -R(0)\leq t\leq 0\}.

By Lemma
3.6
,
0
is a local maximum of
R
, so by passing to a subsequence we may assume that
R(t_{k})\leq R(0)
for all
k
. Define

y_{k}:=x(R(t_{k}),0),

then since
R(t_{k})\leq R(0)
we have
y_{k}\in\widetilde{x_{\infty}}
for all
k
. Set

r_{k}:=|y_{k}-x_{k}|,

and consider

v_{k}(x):=\frac{1}{r_{k}^{2}}v(x_{k}+r_{k}x).

Since
v\in C^{1,1}(\overline{U})
, the Arzela-Ascoli theorem implies that there is some globally defined
v_{\infty}\in C^{1,1}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
and a subsequence (not relabelled) such that

v_{k}\to v_{\infty}

and

\nabla v_{k}\to\nabla v_{\infty}

uniformly on compact sets. Recall from
[
4
]
*Theorem 2.10(a) that
v_{\infty}
is a global solution to the obstacle problem

\Delta v_{\infty}=(3-\Delta u_{1}(0))1_{\{v_{\infty}>0\}},

so in particular
v_{\infty}
is convex by
[
6
]
*Corollary 7.

We claim

Claim 1:

v_{\infty}\equiv 0
on
\{(0,s)\ |\ s<0\}
.

Claim 2:

There is some
\gamma=(\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2})\in\mathbf{S}^{1}
with
\gamma^{1}\neq 0
such that
v_{\infty}(\gamma)=0
.

Claim 3:

|\Lambda(v_{\infty})|=0
.

Of course, Claims 1 and 2 combined with the fact that
\Lambda(v_{\infty})
is convex contradict Claim 3. It remains to establish Claims 1–3.

Recall from Remark
2.2
that
\xi(t_{k})
is the unit vector parallel to the ray
\widetilde{x_{k}}
such that
x_{k}+\delta\xi(t_{k})\in\Omega(v)
for all sufficiently small
\delta>0
. The fact that
\xi
is Lipschitz
[
23
]
*Lemma 6.5 implies that
\xi(t_{k})\to\xi(0)=e_{2}
as
k\to\infty
.

Claim 1.
Let
s<0
. By the uniform convergence of
v_{k}\rightarrow v_{\infty}
we have

\displaystyle v_{\infty}(0,s)

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}v_{k}(0,s)

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}v_{k}(s\xi(t_{k}))\quad\quad\text{ since $s\xi(t_{k})\rightarrow(0,s)$}

\displaystyle=0,

where the final equality is because
x_{k}+r_{k}s\xi(t_{k})\in\widetilde{x_{k}}
for
s<0
.

Claim 2.
For each
k
, define

\gamma_{k}:=\frac{y_{k}-x_{k}}{r_{k}}\in\mathbf{S}^{1},

then since the coordinate change to
(r,t)
-coordinates is bi-Lipschitz, there is some
c>0
depending only on this Lipschitz constant so that for large enough

,

(\gamma_{k}\cdot e_{1})^{2}\geq c>0.

Possibly passing to a subsequence, there is some
\gamma_{\infty}\in\mathbf{S}^{1}
such that

\gamma_{k}\to\gamma_{\infty}.

Then, we have

(\gamma_{\infty}\cdot e_{1})^{2}\geq c>0,

so if we write
\gamma_{\infty}=(\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2})
, we have
\gamma^{1}\neq 0
. Moreover, since
y_{k}\in\widetilde{x_{\infty}}
for each

, we have

\displaystyle v_{\infty}(\gamma_{\infty})

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}v_{r_{k}}(\gamma_{k})

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{k}^{2}}v(x_{k}+r_{k}\gamma_{k})

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{k}^{2}}v(y_{k})

\displaystyle=0,

as desired.

Claim 3.

Finally, we wish to show that
|\Lambda(v_{\infty})|=0
. By Proposition
3.5
, for any
R>0
we have

\displaystyle\frac{|\Lambda(v_{k})\cap B_{R}|}{|B_{R}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\Lambda(v)\cap B_{Rr_{k}}(x_{k})|}{|B_{Rr_{k}}(x_{k})|}

\displaystyle\leq\varpi(Rr_{k}).

Applying Lemma
3.2
, we see that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\big||\Lambda(v_{k})\cap B_{R}|-|\Lambda(v_{\infty})\cap B_{R}|\big|

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{k\to\infty}|\big(\Lambda(v_{k})\Delta\Lambda(v_{\infty})\big)\cap B_{R}|

\displaystyle=0,

and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{|\Lambda(v_{\infty})\cap B_{R}|}{|B_{R}|}

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{|\Lambda(v_{k})\cap B_{R}|}{|B_{R}|}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{k\to\infty}\varpi(Rr_{k})

\displaystyle=0.

Since
R
was arbitrary, we conclude that
|\Lambda(v_{\infty})|=0
.
∎

4.
Regular points on rough free boundaries

In this section, we study the regular points of the free boundary for solutions to the obstacle problem with
C^{2}
obstacle. Thanks to Blank’s example
[
4
]
*Theorem 7.3, we know that in this scenario, the free boundary need not be locally the graph of a function. Nonetheless, using the results of
[
4
,
3
]
, we prove that the regular free boundary is a
C^{\alpha}
-submanifold for any

, which combined with Theorem

yields the sharp Hausdorff dimension bound for the free boundary.

4.1.
Hölder regularity at regular points

As a simple application of the work of Blank
[
4
]
, we get Hölder regularity of the free boundary at regular points. Our main input will be the classical Reifenberg theorem
[
34
]
.

Denote by

the Hausdorff distance, so if

d_{\mathcal{H}}(A,B):=\max\{\sup_{a\in A}\mathrm{dist}(a,B),\sup_{b\in B}\mathrm{dist}(b,A)\}.

Denote by
\mathbf{Gr}(n-1,n)
the space of hyperplanes through the origin in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.
Given a set

and a compact set

, we will define the Reifenberg modulus of flatness by

(4.1)

\vartheta_{V}(r;K):=r^{-1}\sup_{x\in V\cap K}\left(\inf_{\Gamma\in\mathbf{Gr}(n-1,n)}d_{\mathcal{H}}\big(V\cap B_{r}(x),(\Gamma+x)\cap B_{r}(x)\big)\right);

c.f.
[
4
]
*(6.3),
[
3
]
*(5.3),
[
19
]
. The set

will be called

with
\vartheta_{V}(r;K)\leq 2\delta
for all
r<r_{\delta}
. The set
V
will be called

if
\vartheta_{V}(r;K)\to 0
as

for any compact
K
.

The main input we will need from
[
4
,
3
]
is the following result, which is proven in
[

]
*Theorem 7.1,
[

Theorem 4.1

(Reifenberg vanishing at regular points
[

,

)
.

Let

be a bounded domain, and suppose

, where

satisfies

Recalling the definitions of

(

) Suppose some relatively open
