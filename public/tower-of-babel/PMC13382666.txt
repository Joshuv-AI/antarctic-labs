Carboxylic Ester Amidation under Mild Conditions and Its Application in Spectral Sensing
Authors: Chuan Chen, Qing-Yun Zhong, Shuai-Bing Li, Zhen Chen, Jiang-Shan Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this work, a series of carboxylic esters bearing strong electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., –NO2) on the aromatic ring were designed. These activated esters undergo amidation with organic amines in aprotic solvents (e.g., acetonitrile) under mild conditions (room temperature, ambient pressure, and without any exogenous catalysts), a combination rarely achieved in conventional ester amidation protocols. By incorporating chromophores (e.g., nitrophenol) or fluorophores (e.g., 2-naphthol) onto the ester structures, the amidation processes can be conveniently monitored by UV–vis absorption or fluorescence spectroscopy. Notably, these esters, such as ester 1 and ester 6, exhibited remarkably high selectivity in their spectral responses toward tris­(2-aminoethyl) amine (TAEA), which is attributed to the unique molecular structure of TAEA (possessing three –NH2 groups), enabling synergistic interactions of these –NH2 groups with the carbonyl group of the esters. Therefore, using UV–vis absorption spectra-based ester 1 and fluorescence spectra-based ester 6, the limits of detection (LOD) for TAEA were determined to be 1.10 and 0.19 μM, respectively. This work provides a proof-of-concept molecular spectral sensing system based on ester amidation, operating under mild, exogenous catalyst-free, homogeneous solution conditions with high selectivity and sensitivity.

Introduction

Molecular spectral probes are broadly divided into reaction-based probes and nonreactive probes. , Developing reaction-based probes is of paramount importance, , as they can be designed by exploiting a wide range of reactions, including inorganic and organic reactions, and the intrinsic selectivity of these chemical transformations can be translated into spectral signal readouts with high specificity. However, developing such probes from common reactions remains challenging, as the reaction must satisfy two essential requirements: (i) proceeding under mild conditions (ambient temperature, no exogenous catalysts, and homogeneous solution to avoid spectral interference) and (ii) generating a readily detectable spectral signal (UV–vis absorption or fluorescence).

Amide bond formation is a fundamental transformation in organic and medicinal chemistry. Strategies for amide bond formation can be broadly divided into three categories: (i) amidation via direct condensations between carboxylic acids and primary or secondary amines; (ii) transamidation, which involves the conversion of one amide into another amide compound; , and (iii) ester amidation. , In addition to these strategies, there are also other, less mainstream methods. − Among these strategies, ester amidation is particularly attractive because carboxylic ester motifs are ubiquitous in organic and medicinal chemistry, rendering this transformation both important and versatile for subsequent applications.

Yet, owing to the low electrophilicity of esters resulting from resonance effect, three general strategies are typically employed to facilitate ester amidation (Scheme ): (i) using harsh conditions (high temperature/pressure, etc.); (ii) strongly activating the ester electrophilicity or amine nucleophilicity; , and (iii) employing additional catalysts, particularly metal-based catalysts. For example, Liu et al. recently reported cobalt-catalyzed ester amidation under high-temperature conditions, while Mukhopadhyay et al. demonstrated a sustainable ball-milling approach for ester amidation. In 2024, Haraguchi et al. reported catalyst-free ester amidations at exceptionally low reaction temperatures (e.g., −50 °C) by tethering cationic heterocycles (imidazolium, triazolium, or pyridinium) to the carbonyl carbon of esters. The positive charge on a heteroaromatic ring endows these esters with high reactivity toward nucleophiles, enabling amidations even under cryogenic conditions. In addition, other approaches for the amidation of activated esters have also emerged in recent years, including the use of pentafluorophenyl esters, succinimidyl esters, and photoswitchable DTE-PNP systems. In the case of pentafluorophenyl esters, the strong organic base 1,8-diazabicyclo[5.4.0]­undec-7-ene (DBU) is typically required. However, as noted above, such harsh conditions are not conducive to the development of spectral sensing systems. Succinimidyl esters, on the other hand, are overly reactive and their reactivity is difficult to modulate, an equally unfavorable trait for spectral sensing applications. Photoswitchable DTE-PNP systems require external light stimulation to activate amidation, introducing additional operational complexity that is also disadvantageous for spectral sensing system design. Despite substantial progress in ester amidation chemistry, to the best of our knowledge, no existing method simultaneously meets the prerequisites of reaction-based molecular spectral sensing systems (mild temperature/pressure, exogenous catalyst-free, homogeneous solution conditions, and in situ spectral signal generation) to develop highly selective and sensitive spectral sensing systems. Therefore, the application of ester amidation in molecular spectral sensing remains virtually unexplored.

Previous Studies on Ester Amidations (i) and Amidations of Activated Carboxylic Esters Operated under Mild Conditions and Their Applications on Spectral Sensing (ii) Proposed in This Work

Herein, a series of activated carboxylic esters (esters 2–7) was synthesized by introducing strong electron-withdrawing group(s) (e.g., –NO2) on the aromatic ring of carboxylic acid or phenol moieties (Scheme i; ester 1 is commercially available). It was found that these esters may undergo amidation with organic amines in an aprotic solvent such as acetonitrile, featuring high selectivity at room temperature without any additionally introduced catalysts and high pressure. When a strong electron-withdrawing group(s) such as −NO2 is introduced into aromatic alcohol (phenol) moieties, the products (e.g., p-NO2-phenolate anion generated after amidation) could act as signaling reporters detectable by UV–vis absorption spectroscopy. This is due to the formation of a strong ground-state electron donor–acceptor (D–A) pair, giving rise to a pronounced charge-transfer (CT) absorption band centered around 400 nm. In addition to chromogenic reactions, fluorogenic reactions can provide another means to monitor amidation. This approach involves introducing strong electron-withdrawing group(s) into the carboxylic acid moiety and employing a fluorophore such as naphthol as the alcohol component to synthesize nonfluorescent activated esters (Scheme ). Tris­(2-aminoethyl) amine (TAEA), which contains three primary amine groups (Scheme e), exhibits exceptionally high selectivity in spectral response toward acetonitrile solutions of esters 1–3, 6, and 7. This selectivity arises from the synergistic action of two or three amine groups on the carbonyl group of the esters: one acts as the nucleophile, while the other(s) serves as a hydrogen-bond donor(s) to the O atom of the carbonyl group to further activate the esters. We should note that owing to TAEA’s unique molecular structure, it is commonly employed as a synthetic monomer or chelating agent. , However, TAEA exhibits corrosive, toxic, and flammable properties, which can cause severe skin burns and eye damage upon vapor exposure. Based on this design, novel spectral sensing systems for the important organic amine TAEA with high selectivity and sensitivity were developed. To the best of our knowledge, this is a novel example of an ester amidation-based molecular spectral sensing platform operating under truly mild, homogeneous, catalyst-free conditions.

Results and Discussion

For esters 1–3, one or two strong electron-withdrawing groups, −NO2, were introduced into the phenol moiety. Given the strong ground-state CT character expected for the resulting nitrophenolate anions, the reactions between esters 1–3 and the amines (Scheme ii) were monitored by UV–vis absorption spectroscopy. To avoid transesterfication, the reactions were conducted in acetonitrile, an aprotic solvent, rather than protic solvents such as methanol or ethanol, at 25 °C. In these spectral experiments, the molar ratio of amines to ester 1 was maintained at 50:1. The absorbance at 428 nm of acetonitrile solution containing only ester 1 remained negligible throughout the incubation period at 25 °C (black line in Figure a), even after extending the time to 20 min (black line in Figure b). In contrast, upon addition of 500 μM of various organic amines to a 10 μM ester 1 acetonitrile solution, TAEA exhibited markedly higher selectivity in UV–vis absorption spectral response with a maximum wavelength at 428 nm (red line in Figure ) compared to other tested amines, including ethylenediamine (EDA), n-propylamine (PA), diethylamine (DEA), triethylamine (TEA), phenylpropylamine (1-PPA), and dipropylamine (DPA). Although EDA yielded a slight response, the pronounced spectral signal enhancement observed with TAEA underscores its critical role in generating a detectable UV–vis absorption spectral response in acetonitrile solution under ambient conditions. High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) coupled with electron-spray ionization (ESI) was employed to investigate the reaction of ester 1 acetonitrile solution in the presence of TAEA. The HRMS-ESI analysis of the acetonitrile solution containing ester 1 and 50 equiv of TAEA revealed the observed signals corresponding to both 4-nitrophenol and amide 1 since the found m/z of 138.0198 could match the deprotonated form of 4-nitrophenol ([M – H]−, calc. 138.0197), while a peak at m/z of 189.1715 was consistent with that of the protonated amide 1 ([M + H]+, calc. 189.1710) (Figure c,d). These results support the occurrence of an amidation reaction between ester 1 and TAEA, releasing spectrally detectable 4-nitrophenol. Under the alkaline conditions provided by TAEA, 4-nitrophenol is likely deprotonated to form its anion (4-NP–), which exhibits a ground-state CT absorption band around 428 nm. This accounts for the UV–vis spectral response observed for the ester 1 acetonitrile solution upon the addition of TAEA. Further confirmation was obtained by comparing the UV–vis absorption spectra of the reaction mixture with that of authentic 4-nitrophenol in the presence of TAEA; the near-identical spectral profiles (Figure S1) additionally corroborate the release of 4-nitrophenol via the amidation reaction in ester 1 acetonitrile solution containing TAEA.

1H NMR spectroscopy was further employed to validate the amidation between ester 1 and TAEA in acetonitrile solution (Figure ). Initially, 3.7 mg of ester 1 was dissolved in 500 μL of CDCl3 and analyzed by the 1H NMR experiment (Figure b). Subsequently, 30 μL of TAEA was further introduced into the same NMR tube, affording a molar ratio of ester 1 to TAEA of 1:10 (final TAEA concentration was 400 mM). After 20 min of reaction, a second 1H NMR spectrum was acquired. A new proton signal emerged at 7.5 ppm in the spectrum of the mixed solution (Figure c), which can be attributed to the –C­(O)–N­(H)– proton of amide 1. This result provides additional support for the occurrence of the amidation between ester 1 and TAEA in acetonitrile solution.

To elucidate the influence of structural variation, two ester derivatives bearing two −NO2 groups on the phenolic moiety, ester 2 and ester 3, were synthesized (Scheme i). Structurally, ester 2 features an additional −NO2 group compared to ester 1, whereas in ester 3, the acetic acid moiety of ester 2 is replaced by the benzoic acid moiety. The reactivity of these esters was then investigated through UV–vis absorption spectra upon treatment with each of the seven tested organic amines in acetonitrile at 25 °C (Figure ). Both the spectral comparisons (Figure S2) and the accompanying HRMS-ESI data support the finding that the maximum absorption at ∼425 nm originates from the 2,4-dinitrophenolate anion (2,4-NP–) released upon the reaction of ester 2 or ester 3 with the amines. Furthermore, the HRMS-ESI analysis of the reaction mixing solution of ester 2 or ester 3 with TAEA in acetonitrile not only showed the appearance of 2,4-dinitrophenol but also revealed the corresponding amide products, amide 2 (from ester 2 and TAEA mixed system) and amide 3 (from ester 3 and TAEA mixed system) (Figures S3 and S4).

Time-dependent Abs@425 nm change curves of 10 μM ester 2 (a), ester 3 (b) acetonitrile solution containing 0.5 mM amines ((1) TAEA; (2) EDA; (3) PA; (4) DEA; (5) DPA; (6) 1-PPA; (7) TEA; (8) ester 2 or 3 only) at 25 °C.

Therefore, to quantitatively assess the reaction activity, the amidation of esters was modeled as a pseudo-first-order reaction. A comparison of ester 1–3 acetonitrile systems, along with their corresponding UV–vis absorption spectral data, revealed distinct selectivity among different tested organic amines. Furthermore, the reaction rate constants followed a consistent trend (Table S1), generally in the order of TAEA > EDA > PA > DEA > DPA > 1-PPA > TEA. As indicated by the results, different esters exhibit distinct UV–vis absorption spectral responses to the same amine, following the order: ester 2 > ester 3 > ester 1. A comparison of the structural differences between ester 2, which contains two −NO2 groups, and ester 1, which contains one −NO2 group, suggests that the ease of activating carboxylic ester is strongly dependent on the electron-withdrawing character of the substituents, with more such groups enhancing the electrophilicity of the carbonyl carbon. Furthermore, a comparison of ester 3 and ester 2 suggests that steric hindrance also influences the ester amidations.

Motivated by these findings, fluorescence spectroscopy was tried to replace UV–vis absorption spectroscopy to monitor the ester amidations, thereby establishing a more sensitive system for spectral sensing purposes. Accordingly, a series of carboxylic esters containing a fluorescent moiety, 2-naphthol (2-NA), was designed and synthesized (esters 4–6, Scheme i). These esters were then reacted separately with each of the seven tested amines in acetonitrile at 25 °C (Figures S5–S7). Among all tested amines, TAEA exhibited the most pronounced fluorescence spectral response toward the esters (Figure ), featuring the originally nonfluorescent solution system gradually exhibiting fluorescence and intensifying as time progressed, reaching the maximum fluorescence peak at 360 nm. Moreover, when the number of strong electron-withdrawing groups was increased from one in ester 4 to two in ester 5 and ester 6 (i.e., one –NO2 and one −CF3 in ester 5, or two –NO2 groups in ester 6), the fluorescence response of TAEA toward the corresponding ester became even more prominent (Figure ).

Reaction time-dependent fluorescence spectra of 10 μM ester 4 (a), ester 5 (b), and ester 6 (c) acetonitrile solution containing 500 μM TAEA for 20 min at 25 °C.

In addition, the HRMS-ESI analysis revealed that the detected MS peaks could be attributed to the amide products (amides 4–6) and 2-NA (Figures S8–S10). These assignments are supported by the following observations: in Figure S8, the observed peak at m/z of 296.1718 well matches the deprotonated form of amide 4 ([M + H]+, calc. 296.1718), while a peak at m/z of 143.0504 corresponds to deprotonated 2-NA ([M – H]−, calc. 143.0502); in Figure S9, the signal at m/z of 364.1599 is consistent with deprotonated amide 5 ([M + H]+, calc. 364.1596), alongside a peak at m/z of 143.0496 attributable to deprotonated 2-NA; in Figure S10, the observed m/z of 341.1570 aligns with deprotonated amide 6 ([M – H]−, calc. 341.1568), and a peak at m/z of 143.0503 is again in agreement with deprotonated 2-NA. The agreement between the fluorescence spectral profile of 2-NA and the HRMS-ESI data indicates that the fluorescence emission at 358 nm originates from the released 2-NA during the reaction of the esters with TAEA (Figure S11). Collectively, these results provide further support for the occurrence of the amidations in acetonitrile solutions containing esters 4–6 and TAEA.

Kinetic analysis revealed that the rate constants for the reaction of TAEA with the three fluorescence-based esters followed a clear trend: ester 6 > ester 5 > ester 4 (Table S2). As the number of strong electron-withdrawing groups on the esters increased from one to two, the fluorescence response of TAEA toward the corresponding esters became more pronounced, indicating that a greater number of strong electron-withdrawing groups enhances the reaction rate. These results suggest that the reactivity of the carboxylic ester is influenced by the number and nature of electron-withdrawing substituents. To evaluate the generality of this fluorescence approach, ester 7, featuring a single electron-withdrawing group (−NO2), was designed and synthesized from fluorescent 7-hydroxycoumarin (7HC) and 4-nitrobenzoic acid (Scheme i). The reactivity of ester 7 toward tested organic amines under mild conditions was subsequently investigated using fluorescence spectroscopy, HRMS-MS, and 1H NMR spectroscopy. As demonstrated in Figure , following the reaction of 10 μM ester 7 with 500 μM amine in acetonitrile for 20 min, a pronounced fluorescence spectral response to TAEA was observed at the maximum wavelength of 464 nm, consistent with the behavior observed in prior systems such as ester 5 and ester 6 systems. We hypothesized that the fluorescence response at 464 nm originated from 7HC released during the reaction. To verify this, the fluorescence spectra of acetonitrile solution containing 7HC mixed with amines at a 1:50 molar ratio were also examined (Figure S12). At 464 nm, 7HC showed varying degrees of fluorescence response toward different amines, with TAEA eliciting the most prominent spectral response. A comparison of spectral experiments (Figure S13) revealed that both the reaction of ester 7 with TAEA and that of 7HC with TAEA produced fluorescence peaks at 464 nm, indicating the release of the same signaling molecule.

To determine whether the reaction between ester 7 and TAEA yields the corresponding amide and releases 7HC, the mixing solution system was in situ analyzed by 1H NMR and HRMS-ESI. As shown in Figure a, upon the addition of TAEA, a new singlet emerged at approximately 8.85 ppm. This proton signal is tentatively assigned to the amide formed from the reaction of ester 7 with TAEA, i.e., amide 7. HRMS-ESI analysis shown in Figure b,c revealed both deprotonated 7HC at m/z of 161.0247 (calcd. 161.0244 for [M – H]−) and protonated amide 7 at m/z of 296.1712 (calcd. 296.1717 for [M + H]+). These results provide substantial support for the occurrence of ester amidation to release 7HC and form amide 7. This transformation can be conveniently monitored by fluorescence spectral means. The released 7HC anion (in a solution of TAEA in acetonitrile) is responsible for the fluorescence response observed at 464 nm.

Given the aforementioned pronounced spectral response of TAEA toward these esters (as the summary of the results of ester structural optimization in Table S3), ester 1 (chosen over other esters like ester 2 due to its higher spectral selectivity toward TAEA, see Figures a,b and ) and ester 6 were selected as UV–vis absorption spectra-based probe and fluorescence spectra-based probe, respectively, with acetonitrile as the solvent to study their spectral sensing performance toward TAEA. As shown in Figure a, for the ester 1 acetonitrile solution, the relationship between the absorbance at 428 nm and TAEA concentration revealed that Abs@428 nm correlated linearly with TAEA concentration, fitting the equation Abs@428 nm = 10,571­[TAEA] – 0.0019 (R 2 = 0.99). The limit of detection (LOD) was determined to be 1.10 μM, calculated through LOD = 3σ/k, where σ is the standard deviation of 11 blank measurements of the 10 μM ester 1 acetonitrile solution in the absence of TAEA and k is the slope of the linear fitting equation. Similar to ester 1, acetonitrile solution containing ester 6 and TAEA showed a fluorescence spectral response that intensified with higher TAEA concentrations (Figure b). The fluorescence intensity at 358 nm showed a linear correlation with TAEA concentration, well fitting to afford the equation PL@358 nm = 3.0383 × 107 [TAEA] + 6.4396 (R 2 = 0.99). LOD was calculated as 0.19 μM. Compared to the ester 1 system used in UV–vis absorption spectroscopy for detection, this lower LOD (lower by 1 order of magnitude) supports the fact that fluorescence spectroscopy is more sensitive to spectral means. It should be noted that gas chromatography (GC) is the most common method for the determination of TAEA and is well-suited for industrial production. , In addition to GC serving as the mainstream approach for assessing the purity of TAEA and ensuring its quality, real-time monitoring of the reaction progress of TAEA is sometimes required during production. For example, the o-phthalaldehyde (OPA) method can be employed to monitor the extent of the amino group reaction. While GC is a classic and standard analytical technique, it suffers from the problem of high instrumentation costs; the OPA method is operationally simple yet suffers from a lack of selectivity.

In a previous work, our group observed that activated carboxylic esters can undergo transesterification in protic solvents (e.g., methanol) when organic amines are present not only as a promoter for enhancing methanol’s nucleophilicity toward the carbonyl group of esters via hydrogen bond to facilitate transesterification reaction but also as a base role to deprotonate −OH released from 4-nitrophenol to generate 4-nitrophenolate anions to afford the UV–vis absorption spectral signaling way. Therefore, to ensure that amidation predominates over transesterification in the carboxylic ester and organic amine mixing systems, the experiments described above were performed in the aprotic solvent acetonitrile. This could prevent protic solvents such as methanol from competing with organic amines as nucleophiles. Other aprotic solvents, including N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and hexane (Hex), were also used to check whether the amidation of carboxylic esters could proceed in different aprotic media, using ester 1 and ester 6 as examples. The results are presented in Figure . Both the absorbance and fluorescence intensity at maximum wavelengths increased significantly after the addition of TAEA, indicating that ester amidations in the presence of organic amine can also take place in other nonacetonitrile aprotic solvents.

We further investigated the mechanism of the amidation reaction in these ester systems. Given that the activated esters used in this work exhibit high selectivity in spectral response toward TAEA, this high selectivity should be attributed to the unique molecular structure of TAEA. The core of TAEA is a nitrogen atom connected via ethyl groups to three –NH2 groups, forming a unique structure with a C3 symmetry (Scheme ii). If the −NH2 moieties in TAEA functioned solely as nucleophiles, the spectral responses should be identical between two activated ester (ester 1 or ester 6) acetonitrile solutions containing 1 equiv of TAEA (containing three −NH2 groups) and 3 equiv of PA (containing one −NH2 group). However, the results revealed that the spectral response of TAEA toward both ester 1 (UV–vis absorption spectral response) and ester 6 (fluorescence spectral response) remained far higher than that of PA (Figure ). That is, in the ester 1 system, the spectral response of TAEA is ca. 66.7-fold greater than that of PA, whereas in the ester 6 system, the spectral response of TAEA is ca. 131 times that of PA at the same time (20 min). These findings indicate that the high selectivity of TAEA to amidation is clearly not attributable to a simple −NH2 concentration effect. It is preferable to consider that the unique molecular structure of TAEA enables a synergistic effect among the −NH2 groups to promote ester amidations.

In general, it is believed that the –NH2 group of the organic amine, as a nucleophilic group, attacks the carbonyl carbon (CO) in the carboxylic ester, leading to the carbonyl oxygen atom accepting a proton from the –NH2 group to form a −OH group, and an addition reaction occurs to create a tetrahedral intermediate. Subsequently, the H atom of the −OH group is transferred to the O atom of the phenyl ether to form nitrophenol through an elimination reaction to create a new amide. Therefore, in this work, the ester amidation mechanism mediated by TAEA was proposed, as in Figure . Upon the addition of TAEA to an acetonitrile solution of carboxylic esters, H atom(s) of one or two –NH2 from TAEA form an intermolecular hydrogen bond with the O atom of the carbonyl group in the ester. This hydrogen bond can further electrophilically activate the ester. Another −NH2 group from the same TAEA molecule undergoes nucleophilic addition to the carbonyl carbon, forming a tetrahedral intermediate. This intermediate subsequently undergoes proton transfer and elimination steps, ultimately affording the corresponding new amide product and releasing spectral signal reporters such as nitrophenol. Therefore, building on these findings regarding the amidation of these activated esters, a sensitive spectral sensing system for organic amines was established in this work. We should point out that, although the activation of carboxylic esters via the introduction of electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., –NO2) onto the aromatic ring has long been reported, , a systematic investigation aimed at modulating the reactivity of carboxylic esters by such substituents to achieve a highly selective and sensitive spectroscopic sensing system has, to the best of our knowledge, never been achieved.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed a series of activated carboxylic esters that undergo amidation with organic amines under exceptionally mild conditions: room temperature, ambient pressure, and in the abscence of exogenous catalysts. By incorporating a chromophore (nitrophenol) or a fluorophore (2-naphthol, or 7-hydroxycoumarin) into the ester structure, the amidation process can be monitored directly by UV–vis absorption or fluorescence spectroscopy. Systematic spectral screening against seven amines revealed that the introduction of stronger or more numerous electron-withdrawing groups accelerates the amidation rate. Among all tested amines, TAEA exhibits outstanding selectivity due to the synergistic cooperation of its three −NH2 groups, a unique feature that enables both nucleophilic attack and hydrogen-bond-mediated electrophilic activation of the ester carbonyl: specifically, one or two –NH2 can form intermolecular hydrogen bond(s) with the O atom of the carbonyl group in the carboxylic ester, further achieving electrophilic activation and facilitating nucleophilic attack, while another –NH2 group acts as a nucleophile, participating in the nucleophilic addition reaction with the carbonyl group. Using ester 1 (UV–vis absorption spectroscopy) and ester 6 (fluorescence spectroscopy), LODs for TAEA reach 1.10 and 0.19 μM, respectively. This work provides a proof-of-concept molecular spectral sensing system based on ester amidation, opening a new avenue for designing reaction-based spectral probes. Ongoing work in our laboratory is extending this strategy to chiral sensing systems by introducing chiral elements into the ester scaffold.

Materials

Dipropylamine (DPA, GC ≥ 99%) was purchased from TCI. Propylamine (PA, GC ≥ 99.5%) was obtained from Macklin. Other chemicals and solvents for synthesis were commercially available with AR grade. All solvents for spectral measurements were HPLC grade.

Other Experimental Procedures

Mass Spectrometry Experiments Using the Reaction between Ester 1 and TAEA as an Example

(1) Mass spectrometry for detecting 4-nitrophenol: Ester 1 and TAEA were mixed in acetonitrile at a molar ratio of 1:50 ([ester 1] = 10 μM) and kept at 25 °C for 20 min. The mixing solution was then analyzed by high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) using electrospray ionization (ESI) under the following conditions: negative-ion mode, injection volume of 5 μL, and a mobile phase consisting of methanol to acetonitrile (2:8, v/v).

(2) Mass spectrometry for detecting the new forming amide product (amide 1): Ester 1 and TAEA were mixed in acetonitrile at a molar ratio of 1:50 ([ester 1] = 10 μM) and kept at 25 °C for 20 min. The mixing solution was then analyzed by HRMS-ESI under the following conditions: positive-ion mode, injection volume of 5 μL, and a mobile phase consisting of methanol to acetonitrile (2:8, v/v).

1H NMR Experiments Using the Reaction between Ester 1 and TAEA as an Example

A sample of 3.7 mg of ester 1 was dissolved in 500 μL of deuterated chloroform (CDCl3) and analyzed by 1H NMR spectroscopy, resulting in the final concentration of ester 1 of approximately 40 mM.

After the first 1H NMR measurement, 30 μL of TAEA was added to the same NMR tube, corresponding to a final TAEA concentration of about 400 mM in the mixing solution. This yielded a molar ratio of [ester 1]/[TAEA] = 1:10. The reaction mixture was allowed to stand for 10 min at ambient temperature, after which the second 1H NMR spectrum was acquired.

Fluorescence and UV–Vis Absorption Spectroscopy Probing the Amidation Reactions of Carboxylic Esters

Stock solutions (2 mM each) of esters were prepared in acetonitrile. Stock solutions of the organic amines (100 mM) were also prepared in acetonitrile.

For each measurement, 15 μL of the ester stock solution was added to 3 mL of the tested solvent (acetonitrile, n-hexane, or DMF), followed by the addition of 15 μL of the corresponding amine stock solution.

The mixing solution was maintained at 25 °C, and its UV–vis absorption or fluorescence spectra were recorded at 1 min intervals. Fluorescence spectra were acquired with an excitation wavelength of 280 nm and slit widths of 2.5 nm for both excitation and emission. Esters 1–3 were analyzed by UV–vis absorption spectroscopy, while esters 4–7 were analyzed by fluorescence spectroscopy.

Determination of Molar Absorption Coefficients

Stock solutions of 2 mM 4-nitrophenol and 100 mM TAEA were prepared in acetonitrile.

First, 15 μL of the TAEA stock solution was added to 3 mL of acetonitrile, yielding a final TAEA concentration of 0.5 mM. Different volumes of the 2 mM 4-nitrophenol stock solution were then introduced into this solution to prepare a series of 4-nitrophenol solutions of varying concentrations (0, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5 mM). The UV–vis absorption spectra of each solution were measured, and the absorbance at 428 nm (Abs@428 nm) was plotted against the concentration of 4-nitrophenol. Linear fitting of the data allowed the calculation of the molar absorption coefficient of the 4-nitrophenolate anion.

The molar absorption coefficient of the 2,4-dinitrophenolate anion was determined via an analogous procedure.

Method for Calculating the Reaction Rate Constants of the Amidation Reaction of Carboxylic Esters

(1) Example for ester 1 analyzed by UV–vis absorption spectroscopy.

Amidation reaction equation: 1 ester1+amine→amide+4‐NP Since the amine concentration is approximately constant, eq can be treated as pseudo-first-order.

For a pseudo-first-order reaction: 2 ln⁡Ct=−kt+a where Ct is the ester 1 concentration at a given time, k is the observed rate constant, and a is a constant.

Equation can be further changed as 3 ln(C0−C4‐NP)=−kt+a where C 0 is the initial concentration of ester 1 and C 4‑NP is the concentration of the 4-nitrophenolate product. According to Lambert–Beer’s law, A = εbc, we can give: c=Aεb where A is the absorbance at 428 nm, ε is the molar absorption coefficient of 4-nitrophenlate ion, that is, 28,654 L/(mol·cm), and b is 1 cm. Therefore, we can further get eq : 4 C4‐NP=A28654

C 4‑NP of eq is substituted into eq , and the initial concentration of ester 1 is known to be 0.00001 mol/L. Thus, we can get eq : 5 ln(0.00001−A28654)=−kt+a

Therefore, the relationship between A and t can be fitted according to eq to afford the rate constant k.

(2) Example for ester 4 analyzed by fluorescence spectroscopy.

Amidation reaction equation: 1a ester4+amine→amide+2‐NA According to eq and the experimental procedure, the concentration of amine can be considered approximately constant. Therefore, the reaction described by eq can be treated as a pseudo-first-order reaction. According to the pseudo-first-order reaction, we can get eq : 2a ln⁡Ct=−kt+a In this equation, Ct represents the concentration of ester 4 at a given time, k is the rate constant, and a is a constant. Therefore, eq can be further transformed into eq : 3a ln(C0−C2‐NA)=−kt+a In the equation, C 0 represents the initial concentration of ester 4 and C 2‑NA denotes the concentration of the product 2-naphthol. This study measured the fluorescence spectra of a series of acetonitrile solutions containing varying concentrations of 2-NA; thereby, the relationship between the fluorescence intensity at 358 nm (F PL) and C 2‑NA is further established, as in eq : 4a FPL=1.43×108C2‐NA+15.23 We can further change eq to eq : 5a C2‐NA=FPL−15.231.43×108 C 2‑NA from eq is substituted into eq and the initial concentration of ester 4 (C 0) is set at 0.00001 mol/L. Thus, it can further give eq : 6 ln(0.00001−FPL−15.231.43×108)=−kt+a Therefore, according to eq , the rate constant k is determined by fitting the relationship between F PL and t.
