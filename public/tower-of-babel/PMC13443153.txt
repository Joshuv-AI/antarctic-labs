Ligand-mediated suppression of Ostwald ripening enables low-temperature sol-gel ZnO for efficient inverted flexible organic photovoltaics
Authors: Jiaqiang Zhang, Chi Xu, Yongqi Bai, Ran Su, Jiahan Xie, Quan Liu, Ziyi Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInverted flexible organic solar cells (F-OSCs) require low-temperature processing, but sol-gel ZnO as widely used electron transport layers (ETLs) demand high-temperature annealing above 150 oC, limiting incompatibility with plastic substrates. Here we introduce a ligand-directed strategy using N,N-dimethylethylenediamine (DMEN) that stabilizes the precursor as discrete Zn–O–Zn clusters, suppresses Ostwald ripening, and lowers the crystallization barrier, thus enabling high-quality ZnO ETLs at remarkably low temperatures (<90 oC). DMEN-ZnO achieves >17.3% efficiency with PM6:BTP-eC9, matching the performance of ethanolamine-ZnO processed at 200 oC. With an ultrathin LiF passivation layer, champion efficiencies of 19.13% (rigid) and 18.01% (flexible) are obtained in the D18:BTP-eC9 system. Flexible devices retain 96% of initial performance after 3000 bending cycles at 4.5 mm radius. This work provides insight into precursor chemistry and crystallization control, establishes a low-temperature, scalable route for ZnO ETLs that bridges the gap between efficiency-flexibility in OSCs.Subject terms: Solar cells, Solar cells

Introduction

Flexible organic solar cells (F-OSCs) present compelling advantages such as light weight design, low-cost solution processability, and high power-to-weight ratio, positioning them with strong market potential for wearable electronics, portable power systems, and building- or agriculture-integrated photovoltaics1–9. Their inherent mechanical flexibility and tolerance to cyclic bending effectively address the rigidity limitations of conventional inorganic solar technologies1–4,10–16. However, replacing rigid glass substrates with flexible polymer films (e.g., PET, PEN) introduces significant fabrication challenges, including higher surface roughness and lower thermal tolerance17–21. Such substrate constraints hinder the formation of high-quality charge transport interlayers and the photoactive layer, often leading to more morphological defects and reduced structural integrity, thereby degrading overall device performance. Thus, developing compatible low-temperature processes, optimizing charge transport layers over a wide thickness range, and precisely controlling interfacial properties are essential pathways toward realizing efficient, durable, and high-performance F-OSCs22,23.

Over the past decades, OSCs in an inverted configuration have attracted widespread attention due to their significantly better stability compared to their commericial PEDOT:PSS or self-assembled monolayer (SAM) based direct ones24–30. However, the efficiencies of inverted cells still significantly lag behind direct architectures, the latter of which have recently exceeded 20% barrier on rigid31–33 and over 19% on flexible substrates34,35. Moreover, the traditional sol-gel (SG) method for fabricating highly crystalline ZnO ETLs, typically requires annealing temperatures above 200 °C24,36–40, exceeding the thermal limits of flexible plastic substrates, thus forming a critical temperature barrier for widespread implementation. To overcome this bottleneck, researchers have explored various low-temperature fabrication routines. Among them, synthesizing ZnO nanoparticle inks from alcoholic solvent systems has been widely studied. Janssen, et al. firstly introduced this approach in the field of organic electronics40 and a recent modification incorporating with a radical scavenger has achieved a record inverted OSC efficiency of 19.47%41. However, nanoparticles often present challenges related to complex synthesis and purification, poor batch-to-batch reproducibility, and a tendency to aggregate in solution, resulting in limited shelf-life42–46. In contrast, molecular-level design of the sol-gel precursor is regarded as an intrinsically stable and scalable approach. In earlier work, Sun, et al.47 developed a relatively low temperature route for fabricating ZnO-SG ETLs using an ethanolamine (EA) ligand, which requires annealing at temperatures of at least 150 °C in air. This method remains widely used for flexible cells even when employing modified ligands or dopants (e.g., PEI-Zn48, In-doped ZnO49,Al-doped ZnO50). Yet, annealing at such temperatures risks deforming flexible substrates, especially in large-area modules. Very recently, Hou, et al. employed a new propyl-amine ligand with a lower pKb to replace EA in preparing ZnO sol-gel precursor, reducing the fabrication temperature to ~130 °C51. Despite this progress, low-temperature processed ZnO films often suffer from high surface defect densities, leading to low efficiency and instability47–52. Crucially, how ligand design controls precursor nucleation kinetics and the correlation between cluster state and its crystallization energy barrier, remain unclear. Therefore, elucidating the underlying mechanism and developing low-temperature, scalable methods to fabricate high-quality ZnO ETLs remains a critical and unmet challenge in advancing flexible inverted organic solar cells.

In this work, we demonstrated an novel ligand N,N-dimethylethylenediamine (DMEN) to prepare ZnO sol-gel, in which DMEN stabilizes the precursor as discrete, small Zn-O-Zn clusters. This process involves three possible mechanisms: strong zinc chelation that inhibits further hydrolysis and condensation, competitive disruption of acetate bridges, and suppression of hydrogen-bonding following amine protonation. As a result, DMEN suppresses precursor Ostwald ripening and reduces the crystallization energy barrier, enabling the preparation of high-quality ZnO films at low temperatures of <90 °C. Implementing such ZnO ETLs with the highly efficient bulk-heterojunction (BHJ) blend of PM6:BTP-eC9, we achieved devices with an average PCE over 17.3%, comparable to that of conventional ethanolamine-ZnO processed at 200 °C. Notably, at an even lower annealing temperature of 70 °C, such DMEN-ZnO-based devices still work well with only ~1% absolute efficiency reduction. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the lowest reported processing temperature for sol-gel ZnO films that still retain high device performance. By further incorporating an ultrathin Lithium fluoride (LiF) passivation layer, champion efficiencies of 19.13% (rigid) and 18.01% (flexible PET/ITO) are attained in the D18:BTP-eC9 system. The resulting flexible cells demonstrate excellent mechanical durability, retaining 96% of their initial performance after 3000 bending cycles at an extremely small radius of 4.5 mm (substrate is 20 × 20 mm2). This work not only reveals how the ligand structure governs the low-temperature transformation pathway of ZnO precursors through the regulation of Ostwald ripening, but also provide a new a ligand-directed design strategy for the green, low-temperature and manufacturing of high-performance flexible optoelectronic devices.

Results and discussion

Ligand-directed evolution and ripening pathways of ZnO sol-gel precursors

The sol-gel process is accompanied by Ostwald ripening, a mechanism in which small clusters dissolve and feed larger ones, hereby broadening the size distribution and elevating the energy barrier for crystallization53–58. In the traditional method, a ZnO sol-gel precursor is prepared in 2-methoxyethanol using EA as the hydrolysis ligand (upper panel in Fig. 1a). This process drives extensive Zn-O-Zn cross-linking via Ostwald ripening, typically requiring thermal annealing above 150 °C in air to form high-quality ZnO films suitable as ETLs in inverted organic solar cells47. Thus, a key challenge lies in designing novel ligands capable of suppressing ripening, directing nucleation, and ultimately yielding highly crystalline, low-defect ZnO films at low temperatures, a requirement essential for compatibility with flexible substrates. In this work, we employ a novel DMEN ligand to replace EA, aiming to suppress Ostwald ripening via the steric bulk of its N-(CH₃)₂ end-group and enhanced chelation sites for Zn²⁺ ions. As shown in the bottom panel of Fig. 1a, the DMEN ligand stabilizes the ZnO sol-gel precursor as discrete, small Zn-O-Zn clusters. This effectively suppresses precursor ripening and lowers the crystallization energy barrier, enabling the preparation of high-quality ZnO films at low temperatures (70–90 °C). To investigate the influence of ligand structure on the nucleation pathway of ZnO precursors, we conducted systematic spectroscopic and structural characterizations. In-situ UV-Vis absorption spectroscopy monitoring of the precursor solutions (Fig. 1b) revealed that the EA-ZnO precursor exhibits a broad absorption peak at 265 nm, which intensified over time, signifying continuous growth of a dynamic oligomeric Zn-O-Zn network. In contrast, the strong absorption of DMEN-ZnO in the 250–270 nm range rapidly saturates early and remains stable, indicating the formation of inert, small zinc-oxo clusters. Notably, both systems showed a characteristic peak near 320 nm, assigned to hydroxyl-rich zinc-oxo clusters. However, this peak is blue-shifted by approximately 10 nm in DMEN-ZnO relative to EA-ZnO. This directly demonstrates that the strong bidentate chelation of DMEN effectively suppress cluster aggregation and growth, thereby hindering the evolution of precursors into extended highly cross-linked network structures. Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) (Fig. S1) showed that DMEN-ZnO formed homogeneous nano-colloids of 80-100 nm, while EA-ZnO formed inhomogeneous aggregates on the micrometer scale, providing further evidence at the solution level. Electrospray Ionization Mass Spectrometry (ESI-MS) (Fig. S2) also indicated that EA-ZnO exhibited broad, continuous mass spectral signals, representing a poly-disperse complex system, whereas DMEN-ZnO showed several sharp, discrete peaks corresponding to more defined discrete species.Ab initio molecular dynamics simulations (Fig. S3) further support these findings and reveal that DMEN-ZnO forms discrete clusters (1-3 Zn nuclei), whereas EA-ZnO forms larger clusters (~5 Zn nuclei) that cross-link into a network. To provide more direct evidence for discrete clusters in the DMEN precursor, we performed small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) measurements and Guinier analysis (Fig. S4). The DMEN-ZnO precursor exhibits a clear scattering signal in the low-q region, whereas almost no signal is detected from the EA-ZnO precursor. Fitting ln (I) versus q²plot in the low q·Rg range of 1.1–1.9 according to the Guinier approximation (I(q)=I0 exp (-q2 Rg2/3)59, the DMEN-ZnO precursor yields excellent linearity and a radius of gyration Rg = 2.03 nm, directly confirming the formation of discrete clusters in DMEN system.

To elucidate this inhibition mechanism at the molecular level, the coordination structures were further analyzed using high resolution X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) with ZnO xerogel films. The Zn 2p XPS spectra shown in Fig. 1c revealed a significant 0.5 eV increase in the Zn 2p3/2 binding energy for DMEN-ZnO relative to EA-ZnO, confirming a stronger electronic interaction between DMEN and Zn²⁺ ions. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations further quantify such stronger coordination (Fig. S5). The calculated binding energy of DMEN-Zn²⁺ is −3.61 eV, which is significantly higher than that of EA-Zn²⁺ (-2.21 eV), confirming the stronger bidentate chelation of DMEN with Zn²⁺. Moreover, O 1 s analysis in Fig. 1d reveals that the Zn-O-Zn bridging oxygen component accounts for 8.2% in EA-ZnO but is negligible (~0.7%) in DMEN-ZnO, directly demonstrating that DMEN greatly suppresses the condensation and extension of the zinc-oxo network at the chemical bond level. N 1 s XPS spectra (Fig. 1e) further revealed that, in EA-ZnO, the nitrogen signal comprises 24% coordinated nitrogen (398.6 eV) and 76% hydrogen-bonded nitrogen (400.0 eV). The latter corresponded to the strong, broad absorption peak in the Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) at 3000–3500 cm⁻¹ (Fig. S6), together confirming the formation of a continuous network structure supported by hydrogen bonds in the precursor. While in DMEN-ZnO, nitrogen was almost equally divided into 48% neutral amine nitrogen (399.9 eV) and 52% protonated amine nitrogen (401.3 eV), and its FTIR hydrogen-bonding signal was significantly weaker. This suggests that DMEN also employs a protonation strategy to weaken the ability of the precursor to extend three-dimensionally via hydrogen bonding. Note that while the degree of DMEN protonation can be regulated by the triggering conditions such as temperature and solvent environment, it shows a negligible impact on the resulting ZnO crystallization and device performance (see Figs. S7–S8, Table S1–S2, and discussion in Supplementary Note 1).

Furthermore, FTIR analysis (Fig. 1f) revealed that acetate groups in EA-ZnO xerogel primarily adopt a bridging coordination mode (Δν = 179.3 cm⁻¹). In contrast, in DMEN-ZnO they shift to monodentate and non-coordinated states (Δν = 196.7 and 227.6 cm⁻¹, respectively), using zinc acetate dihydrate (Δν = 115.7 cm⁻¹, bidentate coordination) as a reference. This result aligns well with the positive shift (+0.58 eV) in the binding energy of the carbonyl carbon in the acetate group, as observed in the C 1 s XPS spectra (Fig. S9). These shifts indicate that DMEN effectively competes for and weakens the bridging function of acetates, thereby fundamentally reducing the number of available sites for inter-cluster cross-linking.

For ZnO xerogels obtained by solvent evaporation, the two kinetic pathways yielded distinct nanostructures and macroscopic morphologies. High-Resolution Transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) measurements, as shown in Fig. 1g, exhibited that while both systems formed sponge-like porous structures, DMEN-ZnO contained more numerous and larger nano-scale cavities, consistent with its structural feature of smaller and more discrete clusters. AFM phase images (Fig. S10) also revealed that the DMEN-ZnO surface exhibited high mechanical uniformity, suggesting it is composed of discrete precursor clusters with uniform composition and size. In contrast, the EA-ZnO surface showed significant mechanical heterogeneity, stemming from local density and cross-linking degree variations within the network structure caused by Ostwald ripening during the traditional sol-gel process. The differences at the molecular and nanoscale ultimately determined the macroscopic state of the products. Following rotary evaporation, EA-ZnO formed a viscous gel-like solid, visually confirming the presence of an extended 3D zinc-oxo gel network (Fig. S11). DMEN-ZnO, however, precipitated directly as a yellowish-white powder. This indicates that the material consists solely of discrete low-nuclearity clusters that stack together without forming a continuous Zn-O-Zn network, providing macroscopic evidence for the suppression of network condensation by DMEN.

In short, DMEN ligand inhibits Ostwald ripening in the classic sol-gel process through a synergistic, multi-faceted mechanism: (i) strong bidentate chelation locks that locks zinc ions and suppress hydrolysis-condensation, (ii) steric hindrance and competitive coordination that weaken acetate bridging and physically block inter-cluster aggregation, and (iii) amine protonation that disrupts long-range hydrogen-bonding networks. Together, these mechanisms stabilize the precursor in a discrete, low-nuclearity zinc-oxo cluster state, thereby laying the structural foundation for low-temperature, low-energy-barrier crystallization. This structural difference dictates the distinct crystallization pathways. In EA-ZnO, the highly cross-linked network must be dismantled before crystallization can occur, requiring simultaneous disruption of multiple bonds with high energy input (high annealing temperature). In contrast, DMEN-ZnO consists of the discrete clusters without cross-links, allowing crystallization to proceed directly via short-range rearrangement after ligand detachment at lower temperatures. This will be discussed in next section.

Mechanism of the DMEN-Mediated low-temperature transformation of ZnO ETLs

To investigate how precursor structure influences solution-to-solid conversion behavior, we employed in-situ variable-temperature Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (VT-FTIR) to analyzed the crystallization pathways of both precursors (EA-ZnO and DMEN-ZnO). As shown in Fig. 2a, the EA-ZnO precursor required isothermal holding at 130 °C at least for 10 min before the characteristic Zn-O absorption peak began to appear, accompanied by a gradual detachment of the organic amine ligands. In contrast, the DMEN-ZnO precursor exhibited a distinct Zn-O peak after only 5 min at 70 °C, reaching signal saturation within 15 min. The >60 °C reduction in crystallization onset temperature, along with significantly accelerated kinetics, indicates that a significantly lower thermal transformation barrier for the DMEN-ZnO system. Note that the acetate groups in DMEN-ZnO can be completely removed at low temperatures during thermal annealing process, exhibited in Fig. S12 (supporting information). This observation aligns with previous FT-IR and XPS results in Fig. 1f and Fig. S9,which indicate that the acetate groups exist in a non-coordinated/weakly bound state in the precursor. Such low-energy-barrier transformation pathway ensures effective low-temperature crystallization. To quantitatively determine the crystallization energy barrier, non-isothermal DSC measurements were performed on xerogels of DMEN-ZnO and reference EA-ZnO. In the meantime, to clarify the specific role of the primary amine (-NH₂) in DMEN, we also included N,N,N’-trimethylethylenediamine (TMEDA), N, N, N’,N’-tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED) for comparison. Such xerogels were obtained by drying the precursor solutions at 50 °C under N₂ atmosphere for 12 h. Using the Kissinger method60, the crystallization activation energies derived from DSC data follow a clear trend: EA (129.8 kJ/mol) > DMEN (85.7 kJ/mol)>TMEDA (72.6 kJ/mol)>TEMED (40.1 kJ/mol), as shown in Supplementary Figs. S13–14. Higher tertiary amine content lowers crystallization barriers, directly confirming that the steric hindrance from tertiary amines facilitates low-temperature crystallization. All the N-(CH3)2- based ZnO precursors can be converted into high quality ZnO films at low temperature of 90 °C, yielding similar film properties and corresponding device performance (see supplementary Note 2 and Figs. S15–17, Table S3).

As shown in Fig. 2b, DMEN-ZnO films display the typical absorption band edge of a ZnO semiconductor across a broad annealing temperature range of 70–200 °C, whereas EA-ZnO films require annealing above 130 °C to achieve a similar transition. HR-TEM measurement provides more direct phase evidence. As shown in Fig. 2c–g, DMEN-ZnO annealed at 70 °C already exhibits distinct lattice fringes of the Zinc-blende structure and sharp polycrystalline diffraction rings. In contrast, EA-ZnO under the same conditions displays only sparse lattice fringes, with well-defined crystal ordering emerging after annealing at 130 °C. Notably, atomic force microscopy (AFM) reveals that both sol-gel-derived ZnO films maintain similar microscopic morphology across different annealing temperatures, with root-mean-square (RMS) roughness consistently below 2 nm (Fig. S18). A key distinction is that DMEN-ZnO exhibits a homogeneous distribution of smaller clusters, unlike EA-ZnO. We note that, compared to EA-ZnO (130 °C), the local SAED features of DMEN-ZnO (130 °C) show peak splitting and reduced intensities, suggesting more random grain orientation within individual DMEN-ZnO aggregates during low-temperature crystallization, possibly accompanied by minor lattice distortion. Nevertheless, GIWAXS measurements on intact films (Fig. S19) reveal that DMEN-ZnO and EA-ZnO (annealed at 130 °C) exhibit comparable crystallite sizes, FWHM, and integrated peak areas, indicating similar macroscopic crystallinity.

To further investigate the surface defect characteristics of the ZnO prepared from different amine ligands, high-resolution XPS was performed on ITO/ZnO samples annealed at various temperatures. As shown in Fig. 3a and 3b, the O 1 s peak position and shape for DMEN-ZnO films remain stable across the 70–200 °C range. The content of lattice oxygen stays constant at ~60%, and the Zn 2p3/2 binding energy shows no shift (Fig. S20a), suggesting that as- deposited DMEN-ZnO film has been almost completely converted into a stable ZnO phase at only 70 °C. Conversely, the EA-ZnO system exhibits strong temperature dependence: its lattice oxygen content rises gradually with increasing temperature, reaching only ~53% at 130 °C, while the Zn 2p₃/₂ binding energy shifts by as much as 0.2 eV (Fig. S20b). This indicates that EA-ZnO must overcome a substantial energy barrier to undergo continuous Zn-O network reconstruction. This aligns with the residual organic amine analysis (Fig. S20c and S20d), which confirms that the DMEN ligands are completely removed by 90 °C, whereas EA-ligand signals persist across a broad temperature range of 70–130 °C, and do not significantly diminish until 160 °C. Moreover, Ar⁺ ion etching XPS depth profiling (Fig. S21) shows no detectable N 1 s signal in DMEN-ZnO films, confirming that N does not dope into the ZnO lattice and that DMEN ligands are completely removed after annealing at 90 °C. Given the reduced surface defect density in DMEN-ZnO films, we further measured their photoluminescence (PL) spectra to examine emission characteristics. As shown in Fig. 3c, DMEN-ZnO exhibited stronger band-edge emission than reference EA-ZnO, without no defect-related emission peak near 600 nm, whereas EA-ZnO, particularly at low temperatures, shows a pronounced defect emission band.

The influence of different amine ligands on the electronic structure of ZnO thin films was examined using ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS). As shown in Fig. 3d, the secondary electron cutoffs of EA-ZnO and DMEN-ZnO are located at 17.6 eV and 17.5 eV, respectively, indicating a shift of 0.1 eV. Using the photon energy of the He Iα source (21.22 eV) as a reference, the work functions (WF) were determined to be 3.62 eV for EA-ZnO (200 °C) and 3.72 eV for DMEN-ZnO (90 °C). Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) measurements (Figs. S22 and S23) further support this conclusion: a lower surface potential reading for DMEN-ZnO corresponds to its higher WF, which is consistent with the UPS observations. To evaluate the electrical performance of the DMEN-ZnO films, we performed conductive atomic force microscopy (c-AFM) and conductivity measurements. As illustrated in Fig. 3e, under the same bias voltage, the current response of the DMEN-ZnO film is approximately 2.3 times that of the EA-ZnO film, further corroborating that DMEN-ZnO possesses superior charge transport capability. Despite AFM topography (Fig. S18) shows that the grain size of DMEN-ZnO is smaller than that of EA-ZnO, GIWAXS analysis (Fig. S24 and Table S5) reveals that DMEN-ZnO (90 °C) and EA-ZnO (200 °C) exhibit comparable full widths at half maximum, indicating that both films possess similar crystallite sizes. Such superiority would originate from a lower oxygen vacancy defect density, as indicated in previous XPS analysis in Fig. 3a. These results are in good agreement with J-V characteristics under dark conditions based on ITO/ZnO (100 nm)/Ag (100 nm) diode structure (Fig. 3f), exhibiting that the conductivity of the DMEN-ZnO film is as high as 1.41 × 10-4 S/cm, which is approximately 80% higher than that of the EA-ZnO film (7.97 × 10-5 S/cm).

Comparing photovoltaic performance of devices with EA-ZnO versus DMEN-ZnO ETLs

To evaluate the photovoltaic performance of ETLs prepared from EA-ZnO and DMEN-ZnO precursors at different annealing temperatures, we initially fabricated OSCs with an inverted device configuration of ITO/ZnO/PM6: BTP-eC9/MoO₃/Ag (Fig. 4a). Bulk-heterojunction blends of PM6: BTP-eC9 were prepared from a Chloroform (CF) solution containing TCB as a solid additive. The current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics of these devices were subsequently measured under AM 1.5 G illumination at 100 mW cm⁻². As shown in Fig. 4b, devices based on the traditional EA-ZnO sol-gel exhibited optimal photovoltaic performance at an annealing temperature of 200 °C, achieving an average PCE of 17.39%. A similar efficiency was maintained when the temperature was reduced to 160 °C. However, significant efficiency degradation was observed when the annealing temperature fell below the threshold temperature of 130 °C, with performance dropping sharply to only 2.6% for devices fabricated at 120 °C. This severe performance decline can be attributed an unusually high density of surface trap states in the ZnO ETL. Such trend is in accordance with previous work49–52, confirming that 130 °C is the minimum annealing temperature required for reaching good performance. In stark contrast, DMEN-ZnO consistently achieved a high statistical PCEs exceeding 17.3% across a broad annealing temperature window of 90–200 °C, with device-to-device variation remaining below 5%. Notably, even at a low annealing temperature of 70 °C, such DMEN-ZnO-based devices still attained an average PCE exceeding 16.5%, representing only a ~ 1% absolute reduction compared to the performance achieved at higher temperatures. Nevertheless, when the annealing temperature was further reduced to 60 °C, DMEN-ZnO-based devices also exhibited markedly reduced performance. In this case, the devices failed to function properly, showing behavior comparable to the severe degradation observed in EA-ZnO devices annealed at 120 °C (see Fig. S25). It is worth noting that the device based on DMEN-ZnO (annealed at 90 °C) significantly outperforms the propylamine-based ZnO sol-gel system (PA-ZnO, annealed at 130 °C) reported by Hou et al.51. This performance advantage can be attributed to lower defect density, as evidenced by higher lattice oxygen (59.25% vs. 43.87%), complete ligand removal, and absence of defect emission (Figs. S26–S27). The evolution of the other three key photovoltaic parameters, including open-circuit voltage (Voc), fill factor (FF), and short-circuit current density (Jsc), under these conditions is further summarized in Fig. S28 and Table S3. The corresponding J-V curves and measured external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra are provided in Fig. S29. We note that the JSC calculated from the EQE aligns well with the value obtained from the J–V measurement, with a deviation of less than 5%, confirming the reliability of the photovoltaic performance reported here. Furthermore, both ZnO interfaces preserve the nano-morphology and charge extraction dynamics of the active layer. This is supported by AFM (Fig. S30) and grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) measurements (Figs. S31 and S32), as well as light-dependent Voc analysis (Fig. S33), all of which show negligible differences between the two systems.

In addition, benefiting from the enhanced conductivity of the DMEN-ZnO layer exhibited in Fig. 3f, we fabricated solar cells with varying ETL thicknesses. As summarized in Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. S34 and Table S4 (detailed PV parameters), DMEN-ZnO-based devices exhibit greater tolerance to variations in ETL thickness, maintaining their efficiency without significant loss even at film thicknesses up to 80 nm. Such exceptional process window suggests that low-temperature processed DMEN-ZnO is highly advantageous for large-scale manufacturing. Furthermore, both EA- and DMEN-ZnO ETLs exhibited comparable operational stability, retaining over 90% of their initial efficiency after 500 hours of thermal stress at 85 °C (Fig. 4d) and under continuous 1-sun-equivalent white LED light soaking (Fig. 4e). This stability is markedly superior to that of conventional devices employing PEDOT: PSS or SAM-based HTLs and PDINN-based ETLs, underscoring the strong commercial potential of this architecture. Note that both ZnO films exhibit excellent structural stability upon thermal aging. AFM morphology analysis (Fig. S35) shows that after aging at 85 °C for 200 h, the surface roughness changes negligibly, with no signs of degradation such as pinholes or cracks. For further stability improvement, future efforts could focus on freeze-blending morphologies, increasing domain crystallinity, and mitigating metal diffusion, such as suppressing MoO₃/Ag penetration into the active layer as a p-type dopant61.

Furthermore, it is commonly believed that ZnO ETLs prepared via sol-gel methods, in particular annealed at low-temperatures, often result in multiple surface defects such as oxygen vacancies, dangling bonds, and residual organic ligands52,62–67. These defects act as charge traps and recombination centers, degrading charge transport and collection, and ultimately limit the device performance. Herein, to further improve the DMEN-ZnO based device, we employed a surface passivation strategy utilizing an ultra-thin LiF layer deposited via vacuum processing, which is compatible with the low-temperature requirements of flexible substrates62. The passivation of ZnO surface defects (e.g., oxygen vacancies) with fluoride ions significantly increased the photocurrent by nearly 1 mA/cm², as shown in Fig. 4f. Such enhancement can be visualized in a broadband enhancement in external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra (Fig. 4g), indicating suppressed non-radiative recombination at BHJ/ZnO interface and thus improved charge collection, given no change in the optical electric field within the active layer when only ~1 nm LiF was introduced. These results are accordance with earlier work employing in situ-derived SiOxNy, multi-arm aromatic ammonium salt (PhTMABr), N-methylacetamide (NMA), or polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) passivation interlayers63–67, which similarly prevent detrimental reactions between ZnO and the BHJ layer through intentional n-type doping or interfacial recombination. In addition, defect passivation with LiF slightly improved the fill factor (FF) to a high value of ~77%, yielding an overall efficiency exceeding 18.5% for the champion PM6:BTP-eC9 binary blend in an inverted configuration. To further investigation of the interaction mechanism between the LiF passivation layer and DMEN-ZnO, XPS and UPS measurements were performed. XPS analysis (Fig. S36) shows that the lattice oxygen content increased from 59.24% for DMEN-ZnO to 63% for LiF-DMEN-ZnO, indicating effective passivation of surface defects. In addition, UPS measurements (Fig. S37) reveal that the work function decreased from −3.72 eV to −3.35 eV after LiF deposition, suggesting improved charge extraction during device operation. To study the generality of the LiF passivated DMEN-ZnO interlayer, the other three different blend systems were used. In detail, PM6:L8-BO, D18:BTP-ec9, and PCE10:eC9-2Cl using such device configuration demonstrated average PCEs of 18.4%, 18.7% and 14%, respectively, higher than those of DMEN-ZnO counterparts. Note that devices based on DMEN-ZnO exhibit photovoltaic performance comparable to that of EA-ZnO across the above-mentioned blend systems, as shown in Figs. S38 and Table S6. This confirms that DMEN-ZnO (90 °C) itself achieves electron transport/collection capability equivalent to that of high-temperature processed EA-ZnO (200 °C). The detailed PV parameters are shown in Table 1 and Fig. S39 in Supporting information. The optimized rigid D18:BTP-eC9 device reached a champion efficiency of over 19.1%, with a Voc of 0.888 V, a Jsc of 27.2 mA/cm2, and a remarkable high FF of 78.8%, placing it among the highest efficiencies reported for the state-of-the-art inverted organic cells.

Fabrication and performance of flexible cells on PET/IZO substrates

After completing the systematic optimization of low-temperature deposited interlayers and BHJ blend, we then translated the process to flexible substrates. To ensure better mechanical stability under bending cycles, we selected polyethylene terephthalate (PET)/indium zinc oxide (IZO) flexible electrode, as it is less brittle than conventional indium tin oxide (ITO)17,68. The detailed flexible cell stack is demonstrated in Fig. 5a. Before fabricating flexible cells, PET/IZO substrates with a protective release film on the backside were temporarily laminated onto a glass carrier using 3 M double-sided tape. All subsequent deposition and processing steps followed the optimized protocol for best-performing rigid devices. Upon completion, the flexible cells were carefully peeled from the carrier for performance characterization.

Due to the planar structure of flexible electrode, the nanoscale morphology of both the ZnO layer and the overlying active layer were preserved, thereby maintaining high device performance. Figure 5b shows the J-V characteristics of the best-performing flexible cell under AM 1.5 G 1-sun illumination, which achieved a high PCE of 18.1%, with a Voc of 0.89 V, a Jsc of 26.4 mA/cm2 and an FF of 76.5%. We believe the results reported here for low-temperature processed rigid and flexible cells represent state-of-the-art performance for inverted OSCs, as illustrated by the direct comparison with the current state-of-the-art literature reported data in Fig. 5c, Table S3 and S4 (supplementary information). The decrease in photocurrent compared to rigid ones is further confirmed by the EQE spectra (Fig. 5d), particularly in the UV and NIR regions, which aligns well with the optical transmission profiles of the PET/IZO and rigid glass/ITO substrates (Fig. S40). Note that the slight decline in FF can be ascribed to the increased sheet resistance of the flexible electrode: PET/IZO shows 25 Ω sq⁻¹, compared to 10.8 Ω sq⁻¹ for rigid ITO, a more than twofold difference that likely contributes to higher series resistance in the flexible cell. Owing to the high thickness tolerance of the DMEN-ZnO ETL, both rigid and flexible cells exhibited high reproducibility and a narrower PCE distribution, as shown in Fig. 5e, a beneficial trait for scalable manufacturing.

The mechanical robustness of the low-temperature processed flexible cells was evaluated by cyclic bending tests under a radii of 4.5 mm (Fig. S41) following the proposed bending test protocol69. Remarkably, as shown in Fig. 5f, the flexible cells retained over 96% of their initial PCE even after 3000 bending cycles at such an extreme curvature radius, demonstrating an excellent mechanical robustness. This outstanding stability stems from a synergistic combination of robust functional layers, including the high crack resistance of the IZO electrode and a crack-free, well-adhered microstructure on the PET/IZO substrate, which together prevent delamination under repeated mechanical stress.

Discussion

In conclusion, this work presents a novel ligand-directed strategy to overcome the critical “thermal-processing barrier” in fabricating high-quality ZnO interlayer for high-performance flexible organic solar cells. By replacing conventional EA with the rationally designed DMEN ligand, we fundamentally suppress Ostwald ripening in the sol-gel precursor. Through strong chelation, acetate competition, and disrupted hydrogen bonding, DMEN keeps the precursor as small, separate zinc-oxo clusters. This reduces the energy needed for crystallization, allowing high-quality ZnO films to form at just 70 ~ 90 °C—over 60 °C lower than conventional methods. The resulting DMEN-ZnO ETLs exhibit excellent electronic properties and exceptional thickness tolerance, enabling champion power conversion efficiencies of 19.13% (rigid) and 18.01% (flexible) in the D18:BTP-eC9 system. Crucially, flexible devices retain >96% of their initial efficiency after 3000 bending cycles at a 4.5 mm radius, demonstrating outstanding mechanical robustness essential for wearable applications.

Moreover, beyond providing a low-temperature fabrication route, this study elucidates the microscopic mechanism by which ligand structure governs precursor ripening and crystallization kinetics. The established “ligand-directed” design paradigm offers a generalizable strategy for the molecular engineering of solution-processable metal-oxide interfaces. This work thus bridges the gap between high efficiency and scalable, low-temperature manufacturing, paving a practical pathway toward the commercialization of high-performance, durable flexible photovoltaics and related optoelectronic fields such as LED, sensors and transistors.

Methods

Materials and ZnO precursors

ethanolamine, dimethoxyethanol, LiF(99.99%), MoO3(99.99%), Zinc acetate dehydrate, N,N-dimethylethylenediamine and 1,3,5-trichlorobenzene (TCB) solid additive were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The photovoltaic materials PM6, PCE10, BTP-eC9, eC9-2Cl and L8BO were obtained from Beijing Chenhua Guangyuan Technology Co., Ltd., and D18 was sourced from Jiatian Technology Co., Ltd. Their molecular structures are provided in Fig. S42. Toluene, chloroform, and carbon disulfide were supplied by Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Flexible polyethylene terephthalate/indium zinc oxide (PET/IZO) substrates (sheet resistance≈25 Ω/sq, and optical transmittance shown in Fig. S40) were purchased from Ningbo Najin Thin-Film Technology Co., Ltd. Glass/indium tin oxide (ITO) substrates were purchased from Suzhou Gaichuang Technology Co., Ltd. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) characterization (Fig. S43) determined its glass transition temperature (Tg) and melting temperature (Tm) to be 78.0 °C and 235.2 °C, respectively. For the EA-ZnO precursor, zinc acetate dihydrate (100 mg) was dissolved in 2-methoxyethanol (1.5 mL), followed by the addition of ethanolamine (28 µL). The mixture was stirred at 60 °C in ambient air for 12 h. The DMEN-ZnO precursor was prepared similarly, using N,N-dimethylethylenediamine (42 µL) as the ligand.

Device fabrication

The as-prepared ZnO precursor was spin-coated onto Glass/ITO substrates that had been pre-treated with UV-ozone for 20 min, followed by annealing at temperatures ranging from 70 °C to 200 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, a ≈ 1 nm thick LiF layer was thermally evaporated at a deposition rate of 0.2 Å/s under a vacuum of 10-6 Pa. PM6 and BTP-eC9 (1:1.2, wt/wt) were co-dissolved in a mixed solvent of CF containing 10 mg/mL TCB to achieve a total concentration of 15.4 mg/mL. The solution was stirred at 55 °C for 3 h and then spin-coated at a substrate temperature of 40 °C. PM6 and L8BO (1:1.2, w/w) were co-dissolved in a mixed solvent of CF containing 10 mg/ml TCB to achieve a total concentration of 15.4 mg/ml. The solution was stirred at 55 °C for 3 h and then spin-coated at 40 °C. PCE10 and eC9-2Cl (1:1.3, wt/wt) were co-dissolved in a mixed solvent of CF containing 10 mg/ml 1,4-diiodobenzene (DIB) to achieve a total concentration of 15.4 mg/mL. The solution was stirred at 55 °C for 5 h and then spin-coated at 40 °C. D18 and BTP-eC9 (1:1.2, wt/wt) were co-dissolved in a mixed solvent of toluene and CS2 (1:1, vol/vol) containing 8 mg/ml TCB to achieve a total concentration of 11 mg/ml. The solution was stirred at 60 °C for 2 h and then spin-coated at 60 °C. An 8 nm thick MoO3 layer was thermally evaporated at a rate of 0.5 Å/s under a vacuum of 10-6 Pa, followed by the thermal evaporation of a 100 nm thick Ag electrode at the same rate. The overlap of both electrodes defines the area of single cells is 0.09 cm2.

Characterization

The photovoltaic performance of the device was evaluated by current-voltage measurements using a Keithley 2400 source meter under simulated AM 1.5 G irradiation from a xenonlamp solar simulator (SSX50, Enlitech), with the light intensity calibrated using a certified KG2-filtered silicon solar cell. EQE was measured with a system (PD-QE system, Enlitech) calibrated by a certified silicon detector. UV-Vis-NIR absorption spectra of the samples were recorded on a Lambda 950 spectrophotometer. The morphology and structure of the materials were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Tecnai F20) and two-dimensional grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (2D GIWAXS, Xeuss 3.0 UHR system). Surface chemical states and elemental composition were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, AXIS SUPRA spectrometer, with spectra referenced to the C 1 s peak at 284.8 eV) and micro-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (micro-FTIR, Cary 660 + 620 system). Furthermore, the surface electronic structure and work function of the films were investigated using ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS, Axis Ultra DLD spectrometer) and Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM, Dimension ICON system). The hydrodynamic diameter of the precursor was assessed by dynamic light scattering (DLS, Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument). The structural composition of the precursor was analyzed by liquid chromatography quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (LC-Q-TOF) on a 4600 system.
