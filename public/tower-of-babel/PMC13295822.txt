Feasibility and acceptability of lay counsellor-delivered single-session therapy: a pragmatic mixed-methods pilot cohort study protocol
Authors: Beatrice Kudzaishe Shava, Jermaine Dambi, Rufaro H Mushonga, Tapiwa Takaona, Lloyd Dzapasi, Thandiwe Mashunye, Cloudius Mugumba Mukoki, Esther Tumbare, Ruth Verhey, Dixon Chibanda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCommon mental disorders (CMDs) are endemic globally. The availability of evidence-based mental health interventions has grown rapidly, with many services focused on psychotherapy. Most psychotherapies require multiple sessions, resulting in lengthy waiting times due to limited provider availability. These delays can lead to higher morbidity, poorer adherence and less favourable outcomes.Recent research indicates that single-session therapy (SST) can be as effective as multi-session approaches and offers cost savings. This pragmatic, prospective mixed-methods pilot cohort study assesses the uptake, feasibility, acceptability, appropriateness and preliminary clinical effectiveness of SST in individuals receiving the Friendship Bench intervention. The Friendship Bench is a task-shifting mental health intervention for individuals with low to moderate symptoms of CMDs.Methods and analysisWe will conduct a pragmatic, prospective, mixed-methods pilot interventional cohort study involving 350 first-time clients of the Friendship Bench intervention in Harare and surrounding periurban areas. We will primarily explore the intervention’s feasibility, uptake, appropriateness and acceptability including the feasibility of a larger follow-up clinical trial. Secondary outcomes include changes in CMDs severity, depression and anxiety symptoms, social connectedness, functional status, hope, resilience, health-related quality of life and therapeutic alliance. Participants will be followed for 6 weeks, with data collected at baseline and at 2, 4 and 6 weeks postintervention time points. A qualitative component will explore participants’ perspectives and experiences with SST. Quantitative data will be analysed using descriptive statistics, bivariate analysis, Cox proportional hazards models and generalised mixed models with maximum likelihood estimation. Qualitative data will be analysed thematically.Dissemination and ethicsEthical approval was granted by the Medical Research Council of Zimbabwe. Findings from this study will contribute to the evidence base for SST in the management of CMDs and may inform mental health service delivery in low-resource settings. Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations, social media platforms and policy briefs.Clinical trial registrationPACTR202605687485459

Introduction

Common mental disorders (CMDs) are a global epidemic and are the leading cause of disability. A systematic review indicates that the global prevalence of CMDs has risen substantially, increasing from 654.8 million (95% CI 603.6 to 708.1) in 1990 to 970.1 million (95% CI 900.9 to 1044.4) in 2019.1 With the increased burden of CMDs, there has been a significant increase in the availability and coverage of evidence-based mental health interventions, with psychotherapies comprising most of these interventions.2 3 A salient feature of psychotherapies is the multisession format for the realisation of optimal treatment outcomes.4 For example, cognitive behavioural therapies (CBTs), the most common psychotherapy for CMDs, require a minimum of six consecutive sessions.4 The length of CBT treatment varies according to each person’s unique circumstances and the severity of their condition; in severe cases, some individuals may need 50 sessions or more.4 Also, most psychotherapies are associated with lengthy waiting times, which is regrettably associated with increased morbidity, reduced adherence with scheduled sessions and poor prognosis.58 Also, the multisession structure of most psychotherapies may exacerbate the treatment gap, as the requirement for repeated sessions means that only a limited number of individuals can simultaneously receive care from the available mental healthcare professionals.9 Fortunately, the growing body of evidence suggests that single-session therapies (SSTs) could be as effective as multisession therapies and could also be a cost-saving resource.1012 For instance, systematic reviews have demonstrated that SSTs are equally effective in improving non-severe depressive symptoms and mitigating anxiety in adults and children, with the proportion of self-reported improvement of symptoms ranging from 67.5% to 78%.11 13

SST can be defined as a programme intended to be provided in a single session.14 Each session is viewed as self-contained by both the client and counsellor, with no obligatory follow-up sessions.15 Thus, SST tend to be pragmatic, focusing on the current difficulty rather than its underlying causes.16 SST is a standalone intervention that requires only one session for clients, making it a promising option for low-resource settings where care gaps are especially widespread.15 Furthermore, the brevity of its format could shorten the waiting time, allowing individuals needing treatment to receive timely intervention and potentially attenuate the high dropout rates associated with multisession therapies.13 However, most of the evolving evidence on the utility of SST is from high-income countries, and its applicability in low-income countries is unknown.13 17 We thus propose a pragmatic cohort study to explore the utility of SST for recipients of the Friendship Bench (FB), a task-shifting intervention provided by trained lay health workers (LHWs). The FB is a CBT intervention, primarily based on problem-solving therapy, with elements of behavioural activation and activity scheduling.18 Originating from Zimbabwe, the FB is anchored on firm evidence from a cluster randomised clinical trial, and is being replicated globally, including scaling up in all Zimbabwe administrative provinces.18 19 Beyond the clinical trial, evidence from real-world implementation suggests that most recipients received between one and three sessions, a paradigm departure from the hypothesised six sessions in the original clinical trial. Fewer sessions may increase treatment coverage and be sufficient for managing mild-to-moderate mental health challenges. This proposition is informed by the Problem-Solving Therapy–Behavioural Activation framework.20 SST may foster psychological skills through psychoeducation, collaborative goal setting, practical problem-solving and actionable strategies that can be implemented after a single session. Further, in accordance with the Common Factors/Contextual Model of psychotherapy,21 we hypothesise that SST may improve mental health outcomes by strengthening hope, therapeutic alliance, resilience, perceived problem-solving capacity and engagement with meaningful activities, ultimately improving mental health outcomes. However, the practicality (feasibility, acceptability, uptake and appropriateness) and effectiveness of the SST within the context of the FB intervention remain unknown, creating an evidence gap that this study aims to address. Importantly, there is a paucity of robust clinical trials exploring the utility of SST in low-income settings. Therefore, this pilot study aims to determine:

Feasibility, uptake, acceptability and appropriateness of SST (primary aim).

Feasibility, uptake, acceptability and appropriateness of SST (primary aim).

Feasibility of conducting a future fully powered clinical trial to test the effectiveness of SST (coprimary aim).

Feasibility of conducting a future fully powered clinical trial to test the effectiveness of SST (coprimary aim).

Methods and analysis

Study design

This pragmatic, prospective and mixed-methods pilot interventional cohort study (see figure 1) is designed and reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement guidelines18 (online supplemental additinal file 1) and the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for pragmatic trials22 (online supplemental additional file 2). We will use a convergent mixed-methods pilot design with an explanatory sequential component as outlined by Aschbrenner et al.23 Quantitative implementation and clinical outcomes will be collected prospectively from the full cohort at 2, 4 and 6 weeks follow-up points. Afterwards, qualitative interviews will then be conducted with a purposively selected subsample to explain patterns in session completion, acceptability, feasibility, appropriateness and clinical response (figure 1). This pilot study will also evaluate the feasibility of a larger clinical trial and collect preliminary data on effectiveness to inform future sample size calculations. Although randomised clinical trials are considered the gold standard for generating evidence, this study is embedded in the real-world implementation of the FB in Zimbabwe to assess the potential utility of SST. We will recruit a cohort of new patients initiating FB sessions and monitor them over a defined period. Newly recruited participants will be offered the SST intervention. However, as this is a real-world pilot study, we will not prescribe a maximum number of sessions; rather, we will pragmatically record the number of sessions completed over a 6-week period, from which we will analyse the study outcomes in relation to the number of sessions completed. The qualitative outcome evaluation is designed per COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research (COREQ) checklist guidelines24 (online supplemental additional file 3).

The study processes from enrolment, intervention allocation, participants’ follow-up and analysis.

Setting

Participants will be recruited from 10 randomly selected primary healthcare (PHC) facilities in urban Harare and the surrounding periurban settlements. FB services are available in all primary healthcare facilities (N=31) in Harare, the capital city of Zimbabwe and are being scaled up in rural districts in the 10 provinces of Zimbabwe. Participants will be recruited from January 2026 and followed up for 3 months. Participants will be recruited by the study site research assistant (RA). Initial awareness of the study will be created by the LHW who provided counselling services; however, the RA will be responsible for obtaining written informed consent and completing full participant enrolment. Recruitment will be conducted in person at the study site, which will be the participants’ nearest PHC facility. To mitigate the potential risk of lay counsellors coercing their clients into participating in the study, the lay counsellors will be informed to present the study as a separate opportunity. The formal informed consent process will be conducted by the RA to ensure the client does not feel obligated to participate in the study. For participants with limited literacy, the RA will read the participant information sheet and consent form aloud (in the participant’s preferred language) in a private setting, allow time for questions, and confirm understanding using a brief teach-back approach. Where the participant is unable to provide a written signature, consent will be documented using a thumbprint (or mark) in the presence of an independent witness (not a member of the research team and not involved in delivering FB counselling), who will attest that the information was accurately conveyed and apparently understood and will co-sign the consent form. We anticipate a recruitment rate of 30 clients/month; thus, the study is likely to run till September 2026.

Intervention

This pragmatic, prospective, interventional cohort will recruit participants who are receiving mental healthcare from the FB. The FB is lay-delivered therapy aimed at decreasing symptoms in mild to moderate depression and anxiety. Ideally, clients undergo six individual therapy sessions that are based on problem-solving therapy, behavioural activation and activity scheduling.19 However, our process evaluation data indicate substantial variability in the number of completed sessions, with most clients completing one to three sessions. The variability from the six sessions of the original clinical trial is partially attributable to variance in the severity of presenting symptoms. For instance, the clinical trial recruited participants with more severe symptoms and who met the diagnostic criteria of CMDs, which presumably resulted in a greater number of sessions.19 During the scaling-up phase, participants receive therapy regardless of baseline severity, provided they wish to attend a session. This suggests that clients presenting with less severe symptoms may require fewer than the proposed minimum of six sessions. The first session primarily focuses on psychoeducation, that is, it introduces the client to the concept of Problem Solving Therapy. It outlines treatment goals and expectations, including an orientation to the probable therapeutic processes. We envisage the SST intervention including elements of psychoeducation, counselling, and referrals to additional services as needed.

Lay counsellor training

FB intervention is delivered by trained lay counsellors, who are usually community health workers employed by local city or rural district councils. No formal mental health qualification is required; however, counsellors must be able to read and write. Before certification, lay counsellors complete the standard FB training, which includes 8 days of theory and role plays, followed by a 1-month supervised internship with real clients. Competency is assessed through a written quiz at the end of training and a fidelity assessment of an audio-recorded counselling session during internship. Those who meet the required standards are certified as FB lay counsellors and receive periodic supervision and refresher training. For this pragmatic SST cohort study, lay counsellors will not receive separate SST-specific training. Instead, SST will be delivered by certified FB lay counsellors using the standard FB counselling framework, which includes psychoeducation, problem-solving, goal-setting, behavioural activation and referral when needed. This approach reflects the study’s pragmatic objective of assessing the feasibility, acceptability and potential utility of SST delivery within routine FB implementation.

Follow-up methods

We will identify and recruit consenting new clients at clinical sites. As part of standard FB implementation procedures, clients are assigned unique identification codes based on site, LHW who counselled the client and the cumulative number of the client for the LHW. We will review on-site clinical records to identify clients on the treatment waiting list who are scheduled for a future session but have not yet attended one. We will contact clients by telephone or in person through the FB lay counsellors who establish initial contact and then hand over the client to the RA for consenting and recruitment. On contact, we will explain the study rationale and procedures. Clients will be allowed to ask any pertinent questions before making an informed decision. In the case where a participant presents with limited literacy, during the formal informed consent process, a witness will be able to represent the participant. Participation in this study is voluntary and will not affect the client’s ability to access FB services. On consent, baseline and follow-up data will be collected and stored in a dedicated REDCap database for all participating clients. This study database will operate as a standalone system and will not be linked to the main FB database. Each participant will be assigned a unique study identifier, and no directly identifying information (eg, names, contact details, national identification numbers) will be captured within the study dataset. We will document the number of sessions completed, as this will determine the administration of subsequent follow-up measures (see table 1). Should a participant present at follow-up with escalating distress, psychotic symptoms or suicidal ideation, the RA will alert the lay counsellor and the lay counsellor will either offer a follow-up session or take the client through the usual FB implementation referral pathways, which include referral to the onsite mental health nurse who, if need be, will refer the client for stepped-up care.

Instrumentation

Acceptability of intervention measure, intervention appropriateness measure and feasibility of intervention measure

The Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM), Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM) and Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM) are four-item tools commonly used as early indicators of implementation success.20 21 These measures can assess how acceptable, appropriate and feasible participants find an intervention or implementation strategy. Cut-off scores for interpretation are not yet available; however, higher scores indicate greater acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility.25 The AIM, IAM and FIM demonstrated strong psychometric properties with a Cronbach’s α=0.85, 0.91 and 0.89, respectively.25

Patient Health Questionnaire-9

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) is a multipurpose instrument for screening, diagnosing, monitoring and measuring the severity of depression. The PHQ-9, which derives its scoring system from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders IV criteria for depressive disorders, asks about symptoms over the past 2 weeks.26 Each of the nine items is scored from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day).27 The total score for the questionnaire ranges from 0 to 27 and can be further classified into minor depression (cut-off: 0–9), moderate/moderately severe depression (cut-off: 15–19), and severe depression (cut-off: 20–27).26 27 The PHQ-9 has been validated in the Zimbabwean adult population, and has excellent diagnostic accuracy with a sensitivity of 85%, specificity of 69% for the set cut point (≥11), and reliability with a Cronbach’s α=0.86.27 28

The Generalised Anxiety Disorder Screen-7

The Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) is a seven-item Likert scale, which measures the severity of anxiety symptoms according to reported responses, with a maximum possible score of 21.29 The questionnaire inquires about symptoms experienced in the last 2 weeks, and responses range from ‘not at all’ (0) to ‘several days’ (1), ‘more than half the days’ (2) and ‘nearly every day’ (3). Scores on the GAD-7 are categorised as follows: 0–4 minimal anxiety, 5–9 mild anxiety, 10–14 moderate anxiety and greater than 15 as severe anxiety. The GAD-7 has been validated in the Zimbabwean adult population, and has excellent diagnostic accuracy, a sensitivity of 89%, specificity of 73% and reliability with a Cronbach’s α=0.87 at a cut-off of ≥10.27 29 30

Shona Symptom Questionnaire-14

The Shona Symptoms Questionnaire-14 (SSQ-14) was developed and validated in Zimbabwe.31 This instrument is a screening tool for individuals at risk of developing CMDs, used in both research and clinical settings.31 The questionnaire has two responses, yes or no, for each question. Scoring ranges from 0 (best/not at risk of having CMDs) to 14 (at risk of having CMDs), with a cut-point of ≥9.27 The tool has been validated in the local setting and found to have good reliability (Cronbach’s α=0.74) with a sensitivity of 84% and specificity of 73%.27

EQ-5D-5L

The Euro Qol 5 Dimensions (EQ-5D) is a generic health-related quality of life outcome measure that includes mobility, personal care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression domains. Respondents indicate that they either experience no problems, slight problems, moderate problems, severe problems or extreme problems. The instrument also includes a 100-point vertical Visual Analogue Scale where 0 (0 cm) and 100 (20 cm) are represented by the worst and best imaginable health states, respectively.32 Responses from the five domains can be converted to a single summary score (known as multiattribute utility) where death and full health are represented by 0.00 and 1.00, respectively. Utility scores for Zimbabwean adults are available.32 The EQ-5D-5L exhibits excellent psychometric properties across a broad range of populations, conditions and settings33

WHO Disability Assessment Schedule 2.0

The WHO Disability Assessment Schedule (WHODAS 2.0) is a practical, generic assessment instrument that can measure health and disability at the population level or in clinical practice. WHODAS 2.0 captures the level of functioning in six domains of life: cognition, mobility, self-care, getting along, life activities and participation.34 Scores for the WHODAS can be calculated using ‘simple scoring’, where the scores assigned to each of the items—‘none’ (1), ‘mild’ (2) ‘, ‘moderate’ (3), ‘severe’ (4) and ‘extreme’ (5). Scores can be summed, tha is, total scores range from 12 to 60 (where 12=no disability; 60=full disability). Cronbach’s alpha levels were generally very high (0.94–0.96 for domains and 0.97 for the total score).34 35

Herth Hope Index

The Herth Hope Index (HHI) is a 12-item Likert scale, arranged with scores from 1 to 4, where 1 is ‘strongly disagree’ and 4 is ‘strongly agree’.36 Negative items should be reversed, and the scores may range from 12 to 48; the higher the score, the higher the level of hope.36 37 The HHI has undergone prior validation in Rwanda, a context comparable to Zimbabwe, demonstrating satisfactory validity and reliability, as evidenced by a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.85.38 39 We have translated and validated the Hope Index into Shona, a Zimbabwean native language (article in writing phase).

Brief Resilience Scale

The Brief Resilience Scale (BRS) is a six-item measure created to assess the ability to bounce back or recover from stress.40 It is a self-report measure of resilience where respondents rate items on a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).40 The possible score ranges from 6 (minimum resilience) to 30 (maximum resilience), with a cut-off point of 10 points for low resilience and 21 points for high resilience.41 The BRS has been validated and found to be valid and reliable.40 42

Working Alliance Inventory-Short Revised

Consisting of 12 items, the Working Alliance Inventory-Short Revised (WAI-SR) is a brief and widely applied therapeutic measure. It measures therapeutic alliance in three domains, that is, agreement of tasks, agreement on therapeutic goals and bond creation.43 The WAI-SR is a patient-rated questionnaire, and patients rate items on a 5-point Likert scale anchored at each end with ‘rarely or never’ (1) and ‘always’ (5), with each of the three domains’ scores ranging from 5 to 20.44 It is a widely used and psychometrically robust therapeutic alliance measure.45 46

Social Connectedness Scale

The Social Connectedness Scale–Revised (SCS-R) measures the extent to which individuals feel connected to others in their social environment.47 This self-report scale comprises 8 items, each rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly agree) to 4 (strongly disagree).48 Scores are summed, with higher total scores indicating a greater sense of social connectedness.48 The SCS-R has been validated and demonstrates strong psychometric properties, including good reliability and construct validity.47 48

Sample size calculation

Based on our implementation data, up to 48% of the participants only complete one (1) therapy session (p0=0.48). We assume that about 53% of the treatment-naïve participants recruited into this cohort will complete a single session (p1=0.53). This 5-percentage-point difference was selected as a conservative but operationally meaningful increase, as even modest improvements in single-session completion may improve treatment coverage as it increases counsellor availability and reduce waiting times, ultimately increasing the utility of task-shifted mental health services. The assumption is also plausible because treatment-naïve clients in the routine implementation of the FB intervention are offered therapy sessions regardless of baseline severity of mental health symptoms, with a majority not meeting the diagnostic criteria for CMD. Consequently, a single session may be sufficient for clients with mild symptoms, as it includes psychoeducation, problem-solving, goal setting and referral when needed. Therefore, the minimum sample size required is 350 at α=0.05, 1−β=0.80, and an anticipated 10% loss to follow-up. The sample size was calculated using STATISTICA (V.14.0).

Qualitative methods

We will use qualitative methods to explore clients’ perspectives on SST, including acceptability, feasibility, appropriateness and satisfaction, to explain quantitative results. Approximately 30 clients will be purposively sampled from the larger cohort (N=350) after the 6-week follow-up (T3), stratified by (1) completion pattern (single-session vs multiple-session) and (2) clinical response (≥50% SSQ-14 reduction vs minimal/no improvement). Written informed consent for audio-recording and interviews will be obtained separately. The semistructured interview guide will be developed based on the implementation outcomes framework, reviewed by SST experts and pilot-tested with 3–4 clients for clarity and cultural appropriateness. Each participant will complete one 45–60 min face-to-face semistructured interview. Trained RAs not involved in SST delivery or quantitative data collection will conduct interviews. All sessions will be audio-recorded, with field notes taken. Audio will be transcribed verbatim; Shona transcripts will be translated into English, with 20% independent backchecking. Identifying information will be replaced with pseudonyms. Data will be managed in NVivo on encrypted, password-protected servers. Finally, rigour will be ensured through member checking, audit trail, reflexivity statements and researcher triangulation (≥2 analysts).

Statistical analysis

General analysis plan

Descriptive statistics such as means, frequencies and proportions will be used to describe participants’ characteristics and clinical outcomes. Missing data will be imputed. The Shapiro-Wilk test will be used to assess the normality of all data before determining whether to use parametric (eg, t-tests) or non-parametric (eg, Mann-Whitney U) tests. We will use the χ² test for binary outcomes and the t-test for continuous outcomes.

Clinical and implementation outcomes analysis

We anticipate that some participants will complete one session, whereas others will complete more than one. Participants will be mainly grouped as either single-session or multiple-session completers, since this classification directly addresses the study’s central question: can clients’ needs be feasibly and acceptably met in just one Friendship Bench session? We acknowledge that dichotomisation may reduce variability in session exposure; therefore, the number of sessions completed will also be explored as a continuous or ordinal variable in sensitivity analyses. We will also assess potential selection bias by comparing baseline characteristics between single-session and multiple-session completers, including demographic variables and baseline clinical scores such as SSQ-14. Continuous variables will be compared using t-tests or non-parametric equivalents, while categorical variables will be compared using χ² tests and ORs, with corresponding 95% CIs. Mean and proportional differences in clinical and implementation outcomes will be compared between baseline and follow-up time points. Repeated-measures analysis of variance or appropriate nonparametric alternatives will be used to assess changes in continuous outcomes over time. ORs will be used to evaluate differences in binary data (see table 3). Cox proportional hazards models and generalised mixed-effects models using maximum likelihood estimation will be used to estimate treatment differences and associated 95% CIs, adjusting for baseline scores and key potential confounders, including baseline symptom severity, age, sex, and other relevant demographic or clinical characteristics. Sensitivity analyses will compare unadjusted, minimally adjusted, and fully adjusted models. Because participants will be recruited from multiple clinics and treated by different lay counsellors, potential clustering by clinic and/or counsellor will be addressed using random effects where feasible, or cluster-robust SEs where model convergence is limited.

Where data are plausibly missing at random, multiple imputation by chained equations will be used, incorporating baseline demographic and clinical variables, session completion status, and available outcome data. Complete-case analyses will be conducted as sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of findings. Exploratory subgroup analyses may be conducted by sex, age group, and baseline symptom severity; however, these will be considered hypothesis-generating only. For example, exploratory mediation analyses may examine whether changes in therapeutic alliance, hope, resilience, social connectedness, or functioning help explain changes in CMD symptoms. Given the pilot nature of the study, mediation analyses will be interpreted cautiously and used primarily to inform future trial design. All tests will be performed at α=0.05 using STATA V.17.

Qualitative analysis

Thematic analysis, combining both deductive and inductive coding will be employed. We will begin with a deductive coding framework informed by targeted implementation science constructs (feasibility, accessibility, appropriateness and satisfaction), while also applying inductive coding to capture emergent themes that reflect unanticipated experiences or contextual factors. This dual approach will ensure that both theory-driven and data-driven insights are captured to inform future implementation of SST. Qualitative data will be analysed using NVivo. Saturation will be assessed iteratively.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative analysis (Meta-inferences)

Following separate quantitative and qualitative analyses, findings will be integrated using joint displays organised by the main feasibility domains: uptake, adherence/session completion, acceptability, appropriateness, perceived feasibility, retention, and preliminary clinical response. The joint displays will juxtapose quantitative indicators with qualitative themes to identify convergence, complementarity, and divergence across the two strands. Integrated meta-inferences will then be developed to inform whether and how SST should be evaluated in a future fully powered trial.

Patient involvement and engagement

This study was co-created with lay counsellors and expert clients. For instance, the research questions for the present study were formulated through a priority-setting exercise that involved several interest holders. The study advisory board comprises lay workers and representatives from lived experience experts. The representatives will also be involved in interpreting the qualitative findings and disseminating the study findings.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval was granted by the Medical Research Council of Zimbabwe (Ref: MRCZ/A/3376). Participants who present with escalating distress during the session or at follow-up—including suicidal ideation (as measured with SSQ-14 question 11) or suspected psychotic symptoms (as assessed with SSQ-14 question 5)—will be managed using routine FB clinical pathways. Specifically, the lay counsellor will immediately alert the on-site/nearest PHC mental health nurse for same-day assessment. The mental health nurse will determine the appropriate level of care based on clinical assessment, which may include (1) management within PHC by the mental health nurse, (2) review by the PHC medical officer and/or (3) referral to specialist services (psychiatrist/psychologist) where indicated. Where additional counselling sessions are clinically indicated, these will be provided as part of routine care (outside the SST dose) and will be documented as part of the pragmatic evaluation. The findings will inform the utility of SSTs in managing CMDs and the feasibility of conducting a fully-powered clinical trial. The dissemination of the study’s outcomes is expected through publications in peer-reviewed journals, presentations at conferences, social media and policy briefs.

Discussion

This study will generate important evidence on the feasibility, acceptability and preliminary outcomes of a peer-delivered, task-shifted single-session intervention. We will apply the study’s findings to the development methodology for a future fully powered trial, for instance, a sample size calculation for a hybrid clinical trial. Generating such evidence is particularly important in resource settings where access to mental health services remains limited and the treatment gap is substantial. In addition, the findings may contribute to broader discussions on scalable and contextually relevant mental health interventions.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It assesses both negative and positive mental health outcomes, allowing for a more comprehensive evaluation of well-being. The use of mixed methods will provide preliminary insights into possible changes in clinical and implementation outcomes after exposure to the SST intervention. In addition, the study contributes to the limited evidence on task-shared single-session interventions in Zimbabwe and was developed collaboratively with lay counsellors and service users to enhance contextual relevance. However, several limitations should be considered. Loss to follow-up may introduce bias despite measures to support participant retention. The absence of a comparison group limits causal interpretation of findings; this pilot study was designed to generate preliminary evidence for a future definitive trial. Furthermore, because assessors are aware of participants’ intervention completion status, there is potential for detection bias. This risk will be minimised using validated self-report measures and standardised data-collection procedures conducted by trained RAs, independent of intervention delivery.
