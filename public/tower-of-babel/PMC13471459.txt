Application of synthetic patient samples in biochemistry laboratory case-based learning course to improve biosafety
Authors: Shanshan Sha, Hanyu Yang, Danni He, Hengchao Liu, Xinyu Shao, Yanli Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Case-based learning (CBL) requires students to fully engage in class activities, motivates active thinking, and therefore effectively improves the teaching and learning quality. However, CBL method is not commonly used in laboratory courses in medical schools due to the biosafety and the ethical issues of patient samples. Here, we developed a novel method to prepare synthetic sera and doped urine without the need for a patient donor. These materials are safe for both students and teachers and were used in a 3-week biochemistry laboratory CBL course. Fifty-eight second-year undergraduates from Dalian Medical University (China) received the CBL course, while another 56 students in the control group received traditional teaching. In this mode, each biochemical assay was explained and conducted separately without clinical case scenarios. The learning outcomes were assessed via written examinations for all participants, and questionnaires were additionally distributed to students in the CBL group. The results revealed that the CBL course significantly increased their test scores, improved their learning interest, and fostered their self-learning habits and critical thinking with teamwork. As for different genders, more female students endorsed the benefits of the CBL model for skill development, while more male students gave positive feedback on CBL model in terms of stimulating their motivation to study and stated that they preferred CBL model in the future. Notably, this study adopted a quasi-experimental design with grouping based on intact classes and voluntary student participation, which may lead to self-selection bias when interpreting the results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09798-0.

Introduction

A biochemistry laboratory courses is routinely offered for pre-medical students in their junior years in China. In a traditional biochemistry lab course, classes are arranged to introduce various biochemical techniques across 8–10 sessions through the semester. In each 3-hour session, instructors explained the principles and protocols of one biochemical technique in the first 30 min, and students conducted the experiments for the remaining 2.5 h. This kind of laboratory course is clearly designed, under control and usually dominated by scatter, conventional and classic content [1]. However, such lab course doesn’t clearly connect to the medical cases. All the students obtained are lab techniques which lacks clinical case scenarios and practical clinical connections, and as a result, the underlying importance of laboratory techniques is often underestimated. A new teaching model, which can bridge the gap between fundamental sciences and clinical applications, is urgently needed in biochemistry laboratory courses, as it stimulates students’ interest in learning.

Case-based learning (CBL), an active teaching-learning tool that can efficiently motivate students to study on their own, was first introduced at Harvard Law School in the 1870s [2], and later appeared to be a success in medical schools worldwide [3–7]. Students were provided with a clinical case and encouraged to acquire related knowledge through self-directed learning and group discussions. Compared to other teaching models, CBL has demonstrated its effectiveness in improving students’ interest, problem-solving ability and knowledge retention [8, 9]. More recent studies have shown that CBL has been applied not only in individual medical courses [10–13], but also in the integration of teaching methods and curricula [14–17]. This CBL approach has also been applied to biochemistry education. It improves students’ recognition of the clinical significance of biochemistry in clinical medicine and facilitates in-depth understanding by increasing students’ engagement and learning interests [18–21]. However, considerable scope remains for the broader application of CBL in biochemistry laboratory courses, mainly because patients’ serum or urine samples, the critical experimental materials for laboratory CBL class, are often difficult to obtain from hospitals due to the ethical and biosafety issues. All the serum and urine samples are tightly managed by the hospital. Even if they can be taken out of hospital, it cannot be guaranteed that the samples maintain stability during the transportation and storage. The complex biochemical changes in clinical samples also increase the difficulty of laboratory CBL course design. Even though the patient samples are obtained from the hospitals with authorities’ approval, biosafety risks posed by infectious microorganisms remain for students and teachers when these samples are used in laboratory classes [22]. It does not comply with the guidance for biosafety in teaching laboratories [23, 24] and puts teachers and students in a dangerous situation.

Here, we developed a simple method to prepare synthetic patient sera and doped urine. These samples can reflect the typical changes of three diseases: diabetic nephropathy, nephrotic syndrome, and liver cirrhosis. Based on these synthetic samples, we designed a 3-week biochemistry laboratory CBL course for the second-year undergraduate students majoring in medicine. For each assigned clinical case, the students first conducted self-directed studying and group discussion, and then performed experiments and presented case analyses in class. The learning outcomes were finally assessed not only through paper tests, but also through student questionnaires. Students who attended the CBL course were surveyed in these questionnaires to evaluate the CBL model in enhancing their comprehensive abilities and learning motivation.

Methods

Preparation of synthetic serum and doped urine samples

To characterize patient sera and urine samples from authentic clinical cases, medical records of 25 with diabetic nephropathy, 20 with nephrotic syndrome, and 25 with liver cirrhosis were collected from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University (Dalian, China). Overly complex clinical cases were excluded. Reference ranges for biochemical markers in serum and urine were summarized based on the remaining patient cases (Supplementary file 1). According to the summarized data, synthetic samples were prepared by dissolving corresponding additives in 1% (w/v) yeast extract solution for serum mimics, or in pooled urine from healthy subjects for doped urine samples. It was expected that the synthetic serum and doped urine would recapitulate the typical biochemical alterations of each disease, with other indicators remaining within normal ranges.

A novel method for preparing synthetic sera and doped urine without the need for a patient donor was developed. As shown in Table 1, the synthetic sera of diabetic nephropathy, nephrotic syndrome, and liver cirrhosis were prepared by supplementing a sterilized 1% yeast extract solution with varying doses of additives, including glucose (Glu), glycerol, albumin (Alb), urea (Ure), creatinine (Cre), and liver homogenate. Liver homogenate containing alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate transaminase (AST) was prepared by homogenizing 0.7 g mouse liver tissue in 2 mL of 0.9% cold NaCl solution, followed by centrifugation at 3,000 ×g for 10 min. It was noted that triglyceride (TG) was substituted with glycerol, a water-soluble compound that is detectable by TG detection reagent. At the same time, doped urine samples for diabetic nephropathy and nephrotic syndrome were prepared by supplementing normal urine with Glu and Alb, as detailed in Table 2.

Here, the chemical reagents involved in the sample preparation were bought from Aladdin Inc. and their corresponding Safety Data Sheets (SDS/MSDS) contain no hazard statements. No prohibited agents such as chloral hydrate and chloroform were used. The liver homogenate was derived from healthy male C57BL/6J mice aged 6–8 weeks, which were purchased from Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Liaoning, China). The mice were sacrificed via cervical dislocation without anesthesia, and liver tissues were immediately isolated by abdominal incision after euthanasia. The animal work was approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of Dalian Medical University. Permit number: SYXK (Liao) 2020-0001. The reason we used normal urine as solvent is to mimic real clinic samples to our best effort, and the collection of human urine was strictly followed the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by Research Ethics Committee of Affiliated Zhongshan Hospital of Dalian University (Approval No. 2022109) and conducted with the informed consent of all volunteers. Therefore, the synthetic serum and doped urine were safe for the users and could be used in the CBL course.

Validation of the synthetic serum and doped urine

Considering that the detection of individual compounds or enzymes may be interfered by other substances, all synthetic serum were validated using commercial detection reagents (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) prior to their application in the CBL course. The detailed detection principles are summarized in Supplementary file 2. Doped urine samples were validated via dry chemistry method using commercial urinalysis reagent strips (Yantai bioway Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). In addition, sample stability was assessed immediately after preparation and after 30 days of storage at -20 °C.

Course design

A biochemistry laboratory CBL course was offered using the synthetic samples, and Fig. 1 illustrates the framework and timeline. Prior to class, students were divided into groups of four, with each group randomly assigned a short clinical case. The available case options are diabetic nephropathy, nephrotic syndrome and liver cirrhosis as shown in Table 3. The laboratory examination results and final diagnoses were removed from the clinical cases before being distributed to students. Students were also provided with corresponding learning issues (Table 3), experimental principles and procedures, supporting references, and the overall framework and timeline of the CBL course, together with the assigned clinical case. After that, students worked in groups through brainstorming, literature research, and groups discussion; while teachers provided timely appropriate guidance and supervision to ensure full participation of every student. Then, students themselves decided which biochemical indicators were required for case diagnosis, including serum Glu, TG, Alb, Ure, Cre, ALT and AST, as well as urine Glu and total proteins. On day 7 after receiving the assigned clinical cases, students submitted a pre-lab report containing a tentative diagnosis, a list of selected biochemical indicators, and a detailed explanation of the detection rationale and methods. Teachers reviewed the submitted pre-lab reports and provided essential correction and guidance if group discussions deviated substantially from the topic. During this process, instructors avoided revealing the final case diagnosis.

On day 11 after cases distribution, students attended a 3-hour experimental session. Instructors spent 20 min illustrating key points of experiments, after which students completed their self-selected biochemical detections using the prepared synthetic serum and urine samples. During the experiments, all students were asked to wear gloves and masks. After the experiment, group members compiled and discussed their experimental data. On day 14, each student submitted a formal report containing the experiment records, final diagnosis and comprehensive case analysis. In the final week of the course, each group prepared PowerPoint slides and their collaborative case analysis in class. Subsequently, instructors presented the complete medical records of each case and provided a systematic theoretical analysis based on relavant biochemistry knowledge.

In the control group, the students received two 3-hour sessions under traditional teaching model, matching the total class hours of the CBL group. In the first session, the teacher spent 30 min explaining the principles, experimental procedures and clinical significance of detecting serum Glu, TG, Alb, Ure and Cre. Then the students carried out the measurements using synthetic patient samples and were required to submit their laboratory reports within one week. In the second session, identical teaching arrangements were followed for assays of serum ALT, AST as well as urinary Glu and total proteins. Preview materials covering the principles and experimental protocols for each biochemical indicator were distributed to students one week prior to the session. Group size and experimental equipment for the control group were identical to those of the CBL group.

Learning assessment

To assess the outcomes of the CBL course, the second-year undergraduate students majoring in clinical medicine in Dalian Medical University (China) were asked if willing to participate the teaching model assessment. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All student participation in the study and the following questionnaire survey was completely voluntary. Prior to the implementation of the teaching intervention, all enrolled students were fully informed of the research purpose, content, procedures, anonymous data management rules, confidentiality protection and the right to decline or withdraw from the study at any time without any academic penalty including score reduction or course disadvantage. In Dalian Medical University, the students were grouped into administrative different classes based on their majors upon enrollment started. Therefore, the allocation in this study was based on existing fixed classes, rather than random assignment of individual students. The study was implemented in classes where all students agree. Six classes majoring in clinical medicine comprising 172 students were surveyed, among whom 153 (89.0%) consented to participate in the study. The remaining 19 students from two classes declined participation, and these two classes were therefore excluded from the research. Among the four participating classes, two administrative classes (58 students) voluntarily chose the CBL course, whereas the other two administrative classes (56 students) voluntarily opted for traditional course as the control group. Written informed consent was obtained from each student. To minimize the confounding influence brought by quasi-experimental design, the final scores of the prerequisite fundamental chemistry course completed by the identical student cohorts in the preceding semester were analyzed by unpaired t-test, and the results revealed no significant between-group difference in scores (p > 0.05) (Supplementary file 3 A). In addition, a pre-course test was given to all students and the unpaired t-test showed no significant difference in test scores between the CBL group and control group (p > 0.05) (Supplementary file 3B). All participants initially received eight routine biochemistry laboratory experiments delivered via traditional teaching approaches. Then, the CBL group students were introduced to the CBL course, while the control group students continued to be instructed in the traditional laboratory course. All teaching sessions for both the CBL group and the traditional control group were delivered by the same teacher. The total class hours and assessment standards were strictly unified across two groups.

At the end of the semester, a written examination was conducted to evaluate the learning outcomes of students in the biochemical laboratory course. The examination consisted of 30 multiple-choice questions, 5 short-answer questions and 5 essay questions. The multiple-choice test was compiled strictly in accordance with the teaching syllabus and core knowledge points of biochemistry laboratory courses. All test items were reviewed, revised and screened by three experienced professional teachers to eliminate ambiguous, repetitive or off-topic questions, to guarantee satisfactory content validity. The test scores, together with the score increases after taking CBL or traditional course, were statistically analyzed by unpaired t-test between the control group and the CBL group. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Meanwhile, pre‑ and post‑course questionnaires were distributed to and completed by students in the CBL group. Students’ opinion towards the effects of CBL course on improving their learning interests and various abilities, including practical skills, critical thinking, self-learning habits and teamwork spirit, were asked in the questionnaire. All students in the CBL group returned the questionnaires. Gender-based differences in student attitudes toward the CBL course were analyzed to explore the influence of gender in the acceptance of this new teaching model.

Results

Validation of the synthetic sera and doped urine

The values and reasonable reference ranges of biochemical markers in the sera and urine of diabetic nephropathy, nephrotic syndrome or liver cirrhosis patients were summarized (Supplementary file 1). Briefly, patients with diabetic nephropathy exhibited elevated serum Glu, TG, Ure and Cre, reduced serum Alb, as well as elevated urine Glu and proteins, resulting from metabolic disturbance and kidney damage. Patients with nephrotic syndrome showed elevated serum Ure and Cre, reduced serum Alb, and increased urine protein due to the renal injury. Elevated serum TG was also observed in majority of patients with nephrotic syndrome. Patients with liver cirrhosis presented elevated serum ALT and AST and decreased serum Alb owing to hepatic injury. Based on above findings, synthetic patient sera were prepared by supplementing 1% yeast extract solution with standard compound solutions or tissue homogenates. The validation results demonstrated that all indicators in synthetic sera exhibited disease-specific characteristic changes, though several markers slightly deviated from the expected values (Table 4). For example, the Glu concentration in synthetic serum simulating diabetic nephropathy was 28.86 ± 0.25 mM, which was slightly below the expected concentration of 30 mM, but still significantly higher than the normal level. A plausible explanation is that the detection of a single biochemical marker or enzyme may be interfered by other coexisting indicators or enzymes. All synthetic serum samples were proved stable for at least 30 days under storage at -20 °C (Table 4).

Validation of the synthetic patient serum samples

The doped urine was prepared by supplementing normal urines with additives; normal urine can be collected via non-invasive sampling. The validation tests using dry chemistry test strips showed the anticipated abnormal signals, including positive glucose or positive protein results (Fig. 2). It is noteworthy that the doped urine samples need to be freshly prepared, as repeated freezing and thawing commonly results in precipitation.

Students’ scores

Using the synthetic serum and doped urine samples, the learning effects of CBL course were assessed within 114 second-year undergraduates from 4 administrative classes by assigning them into CBL group and control group. The results showed that 58 students from CBL group and 56 students from control group had the similar knowledge level before intervention with a p value of 0.443. However, after CBL course, the CBL group obtained significantly higher test scores (88.02 ± 6.77) compared to the control group (78.25 ± 8.82) (p < 0.0001) (Table 5). It should be noted that our quasi-experimental grouping relies on natural intact classes and voluntary student enrollment, a factor that may cause self-selection bias when interpreting the outcomes.

Students’ achievement of the learning objectives

To further understand the students’ gains from CBL course, we collected and compared the information of the students’ performances in the post-course examination on some specific questions which were designed to evaluate the learning objectives listed in the course design. As shown in Supplementary file 4, question 1 “Which is not true about nephropathy syndrome patients?” was designed to examine students’ understanding of metabolic disorders in various diseases. For this question, 91.4% of students in the CBL group selected the correct answer, while only 62.5% of students in the control group made the right choice. The question 2 “What reagents are needed for serum glucose detection with glucose oxidase?”, question 3 “Which reagent can be used for detecting serum creatinine?”, question 4 “Which detection is required to remove serum proteins?”, question 5 “Which wavelength is used for detecting urea with the reagents of urease, hypochlorite and phenol?” and question 6 “The enzymatic activity is defined as:” were to test students’ knowledge in the detection of biochemical indicators. The results of question 2, 4 and 6 showed that 87.9%, 84.5% and 86.2% students in the CBL group mastered the relevant experimental details, exceeding the control group by 16.5%, 15.4% and 11.7% respectively. Nevertheless, there were no significant differences between the CBL group and the control group for question 3 and 5. A plausible explanation is that question 3 was comparatively easy, supported by the high correct-answer rates in both groups (91.4% for the CBL group, 91.1% for the control group); question 5 was relatively difficult, with low accuracy rates observed in both groups (79.3% for the CBL group, 72.7% for the control group). Even so, the CBL group still held a minor advantage over the control group for both questions. The essay questions such as “Describe the function of ALT and AST and their applications in disease diagnoses” and “Describe the major changes in the sera and urine of diabetes patients and explain the reasons” were designed to assess improvements in students’ critical thinking and self-directed learning abilities. Students’ performances demonstrated that most students in the CBL group fulfilled these two learning objectives and attained higher scores than those in the control group. For example, the average score was 5.00 in the CBL group but only 2.98 in the control group on the question about the biochemical changes in diabetic nephropathy (total score 6).

Students’ perceptions to the CBL course design

To get the direct feedback of students on CBL course design, a questionnaire was distributed to all students in the CBL group after the CBL course. The results (Supplementary file 5) showed that all the students were satisfied with the CBL course design, and 93.1% of students felt the difficulty of the cases was appropriate. As for the experimental content, although different students had different responses, 72.4% of students thought the content was appropriate. Notably, 10.3% of students reported that the course content was somewhat excessive and brought a heavy study burden. Such feedback indicates that the content arrangements need to be optimized and improved in subsequent iterations of this CBL course.

Students’ perceptions of the CBL course in ability development

The questionnaires about whether CBL could improve participants’ abilities were delivered to the CBL group students both pre- and post-CBL course. The results showed that (Supplementary file 6), before the CBL course was offered, 63.8%, 70.7%, 67.2%, and 60.3% of students believed the CBL course would be more helpful for improving their abilities of practice, critical thinking, self-learning and teamwork spirit, respectively. After the CBL course, these percentages increased further by 19.0%, 20.7%, 22.5% and 24.2% respectively. These data indicated that the CBL course was widely acknowledged by students for its capacity to boost their comprehensive learning abilities, especially after the implementation of this teaching module. Of all learning abilities assessed, critical thinking was rated as the most significantly improved skill after the CBL course, with 91.4% of students in agreement. The students’ acceptance to CBL in developing teamwork spirit was mostly changed, with 60.3% before taking the CBL course and 84.5% after taking the CBL course, demonstrating that interpersonal collaboration within teams improved in line with expectations. In contrast, the students reported the lowest improvement in practical skills (from 63.8% to 82.8%) compared with other competencies, suggesting that the course is slightly insufficient in cultivating students’ hands-on operational proficiency.

Different genders showed different attitudes to CBL model in ability development. In general, female students reported greater gains from the CBL model than male students after course completion, especially in self-learning ability (females 93.8%, males 84.6%) and teamwork spirit (females 93.8%, males 73.1%) (Q3 and Q4 from Supplementary file 7). A plausible explanation is that female students tend to be introverted and reserved. The CBL model provided them with increased opportunities to communicate with group members and make decisions based on judgment, which may account for their more positive evaluations. Moreover, female students exhibited a dramatic improvement in their evaluations regarding teamwork spirit development after CBL participation, with positive response rate rising from 59.4% pre-course to 93.8% post-course (Q4 from Supplementary file 7).

Students’ perceptions of the CBL course in study interest improvement

A questionnaire about whether the CBL course could increase students’ learning motivation was also completed by students in the CBL group. The survey results (Supplementary file 8) showed that only 63.8% of the participants thought the CBL model would be beneficial prior to course participation, and only 48.3% expressed willingness to take the CBL course. Potential reasons are the that students did not believe that they had adequate clinical knowledge to enroll such case-based classes, or they were simply skeptical of this novel teaching and learning model. Encouragingly, both proportions rose sharply to above 85.0% after the CBL course, demonstrating that the CBL course effectively boosted students’ learning motivation and gained wide acceptance after actual learning practice.

As for different genders, male and female students gave different feedbacks to the effects of this new model on learning interest after completing the course (Supplementary file 7). Compared to males, more females thought the CBL course gave them stronger motivation to study (81.3%) (Q5 from Supplementary file 7) and stated they benefited from the CBL course (96.9%) (Q6 from Supplementary file 7). In comparison, merely 34.6% of male students preferred the CBL course before the course, but quite a few male students changed their attitudes after participation, as 88.5% indicated they hoped to adopt the CBL approach in future studies (Q7 from Supplementary file 7). The female students also favored this new model, with a slightly lower approval rate of 87.5%, though they acknowledged improvements in multiple abilities and gained more from the CBL course.

Discussion

To encourage undergraduate students to be independent thinkers and self-learners, a shift in basic science education from traditional lecture-based models to inquiry- or experience-based approaches had been advocated for several years [25]. Moreover, some educators combined biochemistry laboratory course with scientific research in recent years. A “real” research environment was provided to undergraduate students and some good learning outcomes were yielded correspondingly [26–31]. Even so, for students majoring in medicine or students who would enter medical schools, an exposure to clinical cases and samples may be more attractive than scientific research. However, clinical specimens including blood, body fluids, tissues, secretions, etc., may carry pathogenic microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites. Improper experimental operation may raise risks of staff infection and laboratory environmental contamination. In recent years, virtual simulations were introduced to address such biosafety concerns in some medical laboratory courses [32, 33]. However, virtual simulation cannot truly replace hands-on practice due to its limitation in developing students’ on-the-spot adaptability and problem-solving capabilities [34].

Therefore, in this study, using the synthetic patient serum and doped urine, the CBL educational tool was introduced into the biochemistry laboratory course. The implementation of the CBL curriculum achieved favorable outcomes. The students showed great enthusiasm both in class (experiments and presentation) and out of class (self-learning and group discussions). The learning assessment achieved satisfactory results from both objective measurements like test scores (Table 5) and subjective questionnaire responses (Supplementary file 6–8). More than 80% of students believed that the CBL course greatly enhanced their learning interest and various abilities. Although the CBL model shows great promise, several critical considerations should keep in mind by educators when implementing CBL curricula at universities or in other basic science subjects.

First, clinical cases should be set at an appropriate level of difficulty and complexity, given the limited clinical knowledge base of junior undergraduates. It is not advisable to provide students with overlong cases containing tangential or irrelevant information [18]. Concise cases opening with specific guidance information are more effective at capturing students’ attention and promoting in-depth discussions. For instance, the description of “extreme edema of the whole body” for nephrotic syndrome, or the description of “polydipsia, polyphagia, polyuria, weight loss” for diabetes can serve as ideal opening clues [35]. Meanwhile, overly simple cases with obvious answers ought to be excluded, as such materials fail to cultivate students’ independent thinking and self-learning abilities. It is recommended to use standardized professional terminology when writing case descriptions, as a simulated clinical environment is always attractive for medical students [20].

Second, teachers played a critical role in a CBL course although this teaching-learning model was student-centered. Teachers acted as designers, supervisors, and participators during the entire course. It had been identified that poor instructional planning was the main cause of failure in CBL course design [36]. Therefore, teachers were required to provide students with a detailed framework and timetable of the CBL course and keep close interaction with students in each step (Fig. 1). The discussion with students and the correction of pre-lab reports before laboratory class gave teachers an opportunity to understand students’ progress and redirected misguided ideas back on track. Moreover, the synthetic serum and doped urine samples provided to students for the CBL course should look like genuine patient samples. Synthetic samples with biochemical markers far beyond the expected reference range, for example, extremely elevated or reduced single biochemical indicators, are not recommended, as they may bias students’ analytical judgments and raise students’ doubts regarding sample authenticity. Therefore, it is critical to clarify the normal reference range of each disease-related indicators before preparing synthetic samples.

Third, it is necessary to monitor students’ initial attitudes toward new teaching methods. Pre-course surveys revealed that around half of students, especially male students, had doubts about CBL before implementation. A complete course plan and clear schedule can assist students in adapting to new learning formats. Proper guidance and supporting resources also play a positive role in this process. With increased familiarity, students become more engaged in learning. Regular teacher-student communication during CBL classes benefits both knowledge acquisition and the development of students’ communicative skills.

Besides the inspiring discoveries that most of our learning objectives were met, it is critical to address our limitations in this study. First, although our current pathological urine samples are prepared by spiking additives into donated normal urine, fully chemically synthetic urine [37] will be prioritized in future teaching trials to eliminate biosafety concerns. Second, this study adopted a quasi-experimental design with intact classes as research groups rather than individual random allocation. Differences in students’ learning characteristics between cohorts may interfere with the interpretation of intervention effects, which slightly reduces the internal validity of the study. Additionally, student participation was voluntary, which might result in self-selection bias and affect the generalizability of the results. Third, the designed questionnaire questions possess inherent limitations. For example, apart from the “do you feel” type of question that only allows a binary choice, more questions should provide the choices as a one-to-five or one-to-ten scale so that responses from students can be analyzed and compared quantitatively. Although choosing a position on a scale can be highly arbitrary depending on each individual, some stratification or grouping strategies can be adopted to reduce such bias. In addition, the questionnaires were only distributed to the CBL group before and after the course, as most survey items focused on the perceptions of the CBL course, which only students receiving CBL instruction were qualified to complete. Finally, the synthetic patient serum and doped urine only contained a subset of biochemical indicators; therefore, students can only design their detection experiments among the provided indicators. Also, CBL course must be arranged in the latter stages of the biochemistry laboratory course when students had mastered the fundamental knowledge of body metabolism and routine experimental techniques. Otherwise, the students could not analyze the case themselves out of class and complete all the experiments in class within 3 h. In conclusion, the CBL course can serve only as a supplement rather than a full substitute for traditional biochemistry laboratory course.
