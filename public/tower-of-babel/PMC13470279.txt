Whole genome sequence data of thermotolerant Pseudobacillus badius strain WCB-1 isolated from Kawah Putih Indonesia
Authors: Alam Ahmad Hidayat, Digdo Sudigyo, Rudi Nirwantono, Joko Pebrianto Trinugroho, Fina Amreta Laksmi, Isa Nuryana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe whole genome sequence data of a thermotolerant bacterium, Pseudobacillus badius strain WCB-1, isolated from acidic hot spring sediment at Kawah Putih, West Java, Indonesia. The isolate was obtained using culture-based methods and initially identified by 16S rRNA gene sequencing, followed by a whole genome-based taxonomic analysis. Whole-genome sequencing was performed using a hybrid approach combining Illumina NextSeq 500 and Oxford Nanopore GridION platforms. The assembled genome has a total size of 4,093,976 bp with a GC content of 44.20%, comprising 4,106 protein-coding sequences, 102 tRNAs, and 33 rRNAs. Comparative analysis using Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) and digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) confirmed the taxonomic assignment of the new isolate as Pseudobacillus badius. Genome annotation provided a set of predicted genes and functional assignments, and genome mining identified several putative biosynthetic gene clusters. The dataset provides genomic information for a thermotolerant bacterium isolated from acidic hot-spring sediment and can be used for comparative genomics and further functional analyses.Keywords: Pseudobacillus badius, Thermotolerant bacterium, Whole genome sequencing, Acidic hot spring, Kawah Putih, West Java, Indonesia

Background

Geothermal environments provide a unique ecological niche for a diverse variety of thermophilic and thermotolerant microorganisms that can adapt to high temperatures and high acidity (low pH). The territory of Indonesia archipelago is known to sit in the infamous “Pacific Ring of Fire”, harboring a large number of volcanic hotspots of such ideal environments for thermophilic and thermotolerant microorganisms, but an extensive database of thermophilic isolates still remains limited so far. For example, the Kawah Putihin the West Java province is one of the most popular volcano sites for geothermal explorations and tourism in Indonesia, but only a few research publications or records of thermophilic microorganisms inhabiting the area are barely available [1,2].

Therefore, due to the scarcity of such valuable microbial data, this work aims to describe a genomic dataset of a newly found thermotolerant bacterial isolate, Pseudobacillus badius strain WCB-1, obtained from sediment samples collected at the Kawah Putih. The bacterium was isolated based on its ability to grow at high temperatures and was subjected to whole-genome sequencing using a hybrid approach that combines short-read and long-read sequencing technologies.

The dataset includes raw sequencing reads, an assembled genome, and functional annotations. The taxonomic description is detailed to provide its utility for future genomic comparison of thermotolerant bacteria found across known volcano craters and hot springs in Indonesia. Moreover, the data of functional genomic annotation is also described to map predicted genes and relevant biological functions. This data article complements ongoing research on extremophilic microorganisms by providing publicly accessible genomic data for future comparative and functional genomics studies.

Data Description

The dataset presented in this study describes the whole genome sequence and genomic features of a thermotolerant bacterium, Pseudobacillus badius strain WCB-1, isolated from the acidic hot spring environment of Kawah Putih, West Java, Indonesia. The sampling site is characterized by extreme environmental conditions, including high temperature (53.3°C) and very low pH (0.96) (Table 1).

Initial identification of the isolate was performed using 16S rRNA gene sequencing, followed by genome-based taxonomic analysis. Phylogenetic analysis based on the 16S rRNA gene sequence further supported the taxonomic placement of WCB-1 within the genus Pseudobacillus. The phylogenetic tree showed that WCB-1 clustered with Pseudobacillus badius B4, consistent with the results obtained from ANI and dDDH analyses (Fig 1). Comparative analysis using Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) and digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) demonstrated that WCB-1 belongs to Pseudobacillus badius, showing high similarity to reference strains P. badius DSM-28241 (ANI: 99.54%; dDDH: 96.80%) and P. badius NBPM-293 (ANI: 99.49%; dDDH: 95.80%) (Table 2). These values exceed the accepted species delineation thresholds for prokaryotes (ANI ≥ 95–96% and dDDH ≥ 70%) [3,4]. The species Pseudobacillus badius was reclassified from the genus Bacillus based on phylogenetic and comparative genomic analyses [5], which has been validated by the International Code of Nomenclature of Prokaryotes (ICNP) [6].

The assembled genome of WCB-1 has a total length of 4,093,976 bp, consisting of one chromosome and eight plasmids, with a GC content of 44.20%. The genome assembly is available under accession GCF_057197545.1. Genome annotation predicted 4,106 protein-coding sequences (CDS), 102 tRNAs, 33 rRNAs, 1 tmRNA, and 13 non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs). Functional annotation assigned 1,465 CDS to Gene Ontology (GO) categories and 2,276 CDS to KEGG Orthology (KO) pathways. Both assembly and annotation statistics are displayed in Table 3.

In addition to structural genome features, functional annotation was also conducted to categorize predicted genes based on orthology and biological function. The distribution of genes across COG categories, KEGG Orthology, and Gene Ontology terms is presented in Fig. 2, Fig. 3. Functional annotation based on Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) classification showed that the majority of predicted genes were assigned to category S (function unknown), followed by categories E (amino acid transport and metabolism), K (transcription), and P (inorganic ion transport and metabolism). A portion of genes was not assigned to any COG category (Fig 2).

KEGG Orthology (KO) annotation (Fig 3a) revealed that a substantial number of predicted genes were not assigned to any KO entry. Among the annotated genes, the most frequently detected KO identifiers included K03406, K01990, K01992, and K02003, indicating the presence of conserved functional elements within the genome. Gene Ontology (GO) annotation (Fig 3b) indicated that most annotated genes were associated with general functional categories, including biological process (GO:0008150), molecular function (GO:0003674), and cellular component (GO:0005575).

Genome mining for secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) using antiSMASH identified multiple putative clusters [7], including terpene, terpene-precursor, ranthipeptide, betalactone, type III polyketide synthase (T3PKS), nonribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS), siderophore-related, and opine-like metallophore clusters. Some clusters showed low similarity to known biosynthetic pathways such as fengycin, pseudomonine, staphyloferrin B, and bacillopaline (Table 4), suggesting potential for novel metabolite production.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Sample collection and bacterial isolation

Sediment and water samples were collected from Kawah Putih, Ciwidey, West Java, Indonesia. Detailed sampling location and environmental metadata are presented in Table 1. Water samples were collected using a sterile one-litre bottle, while sediment samples were directly used for bacterial isolation.

For isolation, sediment samples were inoculated onto Nutrient Agar (NA) plates and incubated at 50°C for 3–5 days. Distinct colonies were selected and purified through repeated streaking to obtain pure cultures. A thermotolerant isolate, designated as WCB-1, was selected for further genomic analysis. No experimental evaluation of acid tolerance was performed in this study; therefore, the environmental pH represents only the sampling condition.

Genomic DNA extraction, sequencing, and genome assembly

DNA extraction, library preparation, and high-throughput sequencing were performed by Genetika Science (Jakarta, Indonesia). Prior to whole genome sequencing (WGS), preliminary identification of the isolate was conducted via Sanger sequencing that targets the 16S rRNA gene.

A hybrid whole-genome sequencing approach was employed using both short-read and long-read sequencing platforms. Illumina NextSeq 500 (paired-end 150 bp) was used for short-read sequencing, while Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) GridION platform was used for long-read sequencing.

The raw Illumina reads were subjected to a standard quality control using FastQC v0.12.1 [8] and subsequently filtered using fastp v0.23.4 [9]. Long-read data generated from ONT were evaluated using NanoPlot v1.42.0 [10] and filtered using Filtlong v0.2.1. All high-quality reads were then co-assembled using the hybrid de novo assembler Unicycler v0.5.1 [11]. The quality of the assembled contigs was assessed using QUAST v5.2.0 and CheckM2 v1.1.0 [12,13]. In addition, genome completeness was assessed using BUSCO v5.7.1 with the bacillales_odb10 lineage dataset [14].

Taxonomic identification

Initial taxonomic identification of the isolate was performed based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Further genome-based identification was conducted using Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) and digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) analyses.

ANI values were calculated using the OrthoANIu algorithm implemented in the EZBioCloud web server [15]. Meanwhile, dDDH values were estimated using the Genome-to-Genome Distance Calculator (GGDC) web server [16]. Genome sequences of representative Bacillota species were retrieved from the NCBI GenBank database for comparative analysis.

Genome annotation and functional characterization

Structural genome annotation of Pseudobacillus badius WCB-1 was performed using Bakta v1.9.4 [17]. Functional annotation was conducted using standard tools, including TRAPID v2.0 for Gene Ontology (GO), BlastKOALA and KEGG Mapper for KEGG Orthology (KO), and eggNOG-mapper for COG and CAZy prediction. Genome visualization was performed using Proksee [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. The assembled genome of Pseudobacillus badius WCB-1 has a total size of 4,093,976 bp distributed across 9 contigs, with a GC content of 44.20%. Genome annotation predicted 4,106 protein-coding sequences (CDS), along with 102 tRNAs, 33 rRNAs, 1 tmRNA, and 13 non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs). A subset of CDS was assigned to functional categories, including 1,465 genes annotated with Gene Ontology (GO) terms and 2,276 genes mapped to KEGG Orthology (KO) pathways (Table 3).

In silico evaluation of secondary metabolite biosynthetic potential

The genomic potential of Pseudobacillus badius WCB-1 for secondary metabolite production was analyzed using the antiSMASH web server v8.0.1 [7]. Assembled genome contigs were used as input, applying a relaxed detection strictness to identify both highly conserved and putative or truncated biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs).

To enhance the detection and characterization of BGCs, additional analytical features were enabled, including ClusterBlast, KnownClusterBlast, and SubClusterBlast for comparative genomics, as well as comparison with the Minimum Information about a Biosynthetic Gene cluster (MIBiG) database. Functional predictions were further supported by ActiveSiteFinder, RREFinder, Cluster Pfam analysis, TIGRFam analysis, and Pfam-based Gene Ontology annotation.

Limitations

The dataset is derived from a single bacterial isolate from a specific sampling location, which may not represent the complete microbial diversity in the Kawah Putih geothermal environment. Further, environmental parameters were recorded at the time of sampling and may not reflect temporal variations in the site conditions. The acid tolerance of Pseudobacillus badius WCB-1 was not experimentally evaluated; therefore, adaptation to extremely acidic conditions cannot be inferred solely from the sampling environment. In addition, functional annotations are based on computational predictions and available reference databases, which may be subject to database limitations and annotation biases.
