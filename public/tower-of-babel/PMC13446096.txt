From COPD and asthma to lung cancer: potential candidate genes and their associated biological pathways for the incidence risk of lung cancer
Authors: Masoud Arabfard, Sadegh Azimzadeh Jamalkandi, BFatemeh Nobakht M Gh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung cancer (LC) is the second most prevalent cancer globally. Both genetic and environmental factors significantly contribute to the onset and progression of various respiratory diseases. Notably, conditions such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) have been reported to increase the risk of LC. This study aims to identify the shared genes and biological pathways in patients with LC/COPD and LC/asthma to enhance our understanding of the progression of LC from other shared pulmonary disorders.In this study, nine microarray datasets (three for asthma, four for LC, and two for COPD) were selected from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. These datasets were analyzed to explore differentially and shared expressed genes (LC/asthma/COPD), as well as unique and shared biological pathways involved in LC/asthma and LC/COPD patients.The analysis included nine datasets comprising 547 individuals. Our findings, serving as hypothesis-generating insights, revealed 46 potential candidate genes capable of predicting the incidence risk of LC from asthma, 23 from COPD, and five shared genes among LC, asthma, and COPD. Additionally, we identified 28 shared biological pathways between LC and asthma and 22 in LC/COPD patients.This study suggests that it may be possible to predict the incidence risk of LC from asthma and COPD by examining specific and shared essential genes and biological pathways between LC/COPD and LC/asthma. Further investigation into the significance of these key genes in predicting LC is warranted.Keywords: Lung cancer, Asthma, COPD, Prognostic genes, Microarray analysis, Lung cancer risk prediction

Introduction

Lung cancer (LC) is the second most prevalent cancer globally, following prostate cancer in men and breast cancer in women.1 The incidence of LC is increasing worldwide, posing a significant public health challenge.2 LC is often diagnosed at an advanced stage, resulting in a poor prognosis with a 5-year survival rate of only 5%.1 The prevalence of LC varies globally due to differences in smoking habits, air quality, and genetic factors.2

Several studies have reported that respiratory diseases such as asthma and COPD can increase the risk of developing LC.3, 4, 5, 6 A meta-analysis of 18 studies found a strong association between asthma and an increased risk of LC (Odds Ratio typically ranging from 1.38 to 1.45 for males and females, respectively). Notably, even non-smoking asthma patients had an elevated risk of LC.7 Another meta-analysis indicated that asthma significantly increases the risk of squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and small cell LC (SCLC).8 Recent Mendelian Randomization (MR) studies, which use genetic variants to infer causal relationships and minimize confounding biases, have supported a causal relationship between genetic susceptibility to asthma and the risk of lung cancer, particularly for certain subtypes like lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC).9

Similarly, emphysema and COPD have been associated with a significantly elevated risk of LC, as demonstrated in another meta-analysis of 18 studies. The risk estimates were consistent for both never-smokers and ever-smokers, highlighting the importance of early diagnosis of COPD in LC monitoring and prevention.5 Chronic inflammation, a known contributor to cancer development, is hypothesized to underlie this association.3, 4 Crucially, the persistent inflammatory milieu in asthma and COPD acts as a direct driver of oncogenic transformation in the respiratory epithelium. Activated immune cells continuously generate high levels of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species, creating profound oxidative stress. This stress induces severe DNA damage, including double-strand breaks and the formation of mutagenic lesions like 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), which can inactivate key tumor suppressor genes (e.g., TP53) and initiate genomic instability.10 Simultaneously, chronic tissue injury triggers aberrant airway remodeling. Driven by inflammatory growth factors such as TGF-β, this remodeling forces epithelial cells to undergo Epithelial-to-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT), a process that dismantles normal cellular architecture and endows cells with migratory properties.11 Within this permissive microenvironment, certain cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors involved in the asthmatic inflammatory milieu (e.g., IL-6, TNF-alpha) are also known to promote tumor cell survival, proliferation, and angiogenesis.12, 13 Inflammatory markers such as IL-6 are strongly associated with the risk of LC.14

Various computational studies have been conducted to identify biomarkers for LC. For instance, a bioinformatics pipeline was used to identify dysregulated tumor genes and transcription factors (TFs) related to the progression of lung, breast, and other cancers. Analysis of 20 microarray datasets revealed shared and unique Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) and TFs as potential biomarkers.15 Another study utilized blood serum samples from 31 lung cancer patients and 29 healthy volunteers to perform Metabolomics analysis using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry. Pathway-based analysis identified eight enriched metabolic pathways, five of which were statistically significant.16 Additionally, a computational framework was developed to identify potential prognostic biomarkers for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). Gene expression profiles from three independent studies were analyzed to identify DEGs in LUAD. A disease-specific protein-protein interaction network was then constructed from the shared DEGs, leading to the identification of hub proteins as potential biomarkers.17 Despite these advancements, a critical gap exists in the literature regarding the shared gene and protein expression profiles of asthma, COPD, and LC. While the clinical and epidemiological links between these chronic respiratory conditions and lung malignancy are well-documented, the exact shared transcriptomic architectures and regulatory networks remain poorly characterized. A purely mechanistic hypothesis is insufficient without a system-level validation; therefore, a comparative, multi-disease bioinformatics approach18 is required to decode the common genetic signatures that drive the transition from chronic inflammation to oncogenesis.

This study aims to address this gap by microarray datasets to detect DEGs across healthy controls and patients with asthma, COPD, and LC (SCC and SCLC). To explicitly disentangle the distinct molecular contributions of each inflammatory condition to LC, Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks and pathway enrichment analysis (PEA) were employed to systematically isolate shared genes and biological pathways in the LC/COPD and LC/asthma patient groups separately, rather than pooling them into a single heterogeneous analysis. Identifying these disease-specific and overlapping gene expression panels could enhance our understanding of disease causation, clarify the progression mechanisms from chronic airway diseases to malignancy, and provide robust targets for early evaluation and risk stratification.

Method

Gene expression data collection

This study utilized the NCBI-GEO public database to collect pertinent microarray datasets. Specific keywords such as “Lung Cancer,” “COPD,” “Asthma,” and “Homo sapiens” were employed to locate datasets related to these conditions. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were rigorously defined to ensure the selection of suitable datasets for transcriptome analysis and to minimize population biases. Datasets were eligible if they: (i) contained gene expression profiles from human lung tissue or airway samples; (ii) included both patients with clinically confirmed disease (lung cancer, asthma, or COPD) and appropriate non-diseased controls; (iii) reported detailed clinical characteristics, including age distribution, sex, smoking status, and, for lung cancer datasets, histological subtypes. Only datasets involving human subjects were considered. For lung cancer studies, control samples were obtained from healthy tissue of the same patients who had cancer. For asthma and COPD studies, control samples were sourced from individuals unaffected by the respective respiratory conditions. Datasets were excluded if they lacked a control group, involved only cell lines, did not provide sufficient sample-level annotation to distinguish cases from controls, or failed to report essential demographic and clinical variables necessary for assessing potential population biases. The selected datasets contained transcriptomics data, enabling standardized preprocessing and analysis.

Gene expression data analysis

Nine microarray datasets were further analyzed to detect DEGs across healthy controls, asthma, COPD, and LC subtypes (SCC and SCLC).

The raw gene expression data from selected datasets underwent processing using the GEO2R package, a web-based tool available in the GEO database. The GEO2R package performed quality control to identify and eliminate low-quality samples, ensuring that only high-quality data were included in the analysis. Subsequently, quantitative normalization methods, including the quantile normalization approach, were employed to make gene expression values comparable across all samples. Subsequently, the package utilized the ‘limma’ package and ‘eBayes’ method to calculate DEGs between groups. For DEG identification, we applied a raw p-value threshold of <0.05 with no additional log2 fold change cutoff, prioritizing sensitivity to detect subtle expression changes. No multiple testing correction (e.g., FDR) was applied, as this analysis was intended to be exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Consequently, the identified DEG list may include false positives, and all findings require independent validation.

To identify the most significant genes, we focused on those with the lowest p-values, ensuring that only genes meeting our significance criteria were included in the final list. In our analysis, we employed solely a p-value threshold of <0.05 to determine the DEG list. This criterion was selected to ensure that only genes with significant differences in expression were included in the final DEG list. Finally, functional annotation of the identified DEGs was performed using the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases using an adjusted p-value (Adj p-value) < 0.05 to elucidate the biological processes and pathways associated with the differentially expressed genes. After identifying DEGs for each dataset, gene enrichment analysis was used to find shared biological pathways by the Enrichr tool.19

Data integration

Among the various data analysis methods, including integration and fusion, the integration method was chosen, which involves analyzing each dataset separately. The analysis of each dataset is done without any specific dependencies, and then the results are integrated at the decision level.20 We analyzed each GEO dataset independently rather than merging expression matrices across studies. For each dataset we performed dataset-specific quality control and normalization. Differential expression analysis was performed separately within each dataset comparing cases versus matched controls while accounting for any available sample-level covariates (for example, age or sex) when reported. Results from the individual analyses combined at the decision level: we defined an integrated DEG as any gene that was differentially expressed in at least two independent datasets with a consistent direction of change. No across-study data merging or cross-platform batch correction was performed prior to DEG calling and Because we did not merge expression matrices across studies, we did not apply batch-effect removal methods such as ComBat to pooled data. Finally, the shared terms were extracted using the Intervene tool (https://asntech.shinyapps.io/intervene/),21 and their relationship was examined.

Functional and enrichment analysis

Enrichr tool available at https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr was employed to identify the biological pathways and evaluate the functional similarities of the gene sets.22 Enrichr is a comprehensive gene set enrichment analysis web-based tool that allows users to analyze the enrichment of gene sets in their input gene lists. It provides access to a large collection of gene set libraries, enabling researchers to identify the biological pathways, functions, and mechanisms associated with their genes of interest.23 This method was applied separately to each dataset's list of DEGs. Using an overrepresentation analysis, we identified a set of enriched pathway keywords, highlighting the biological significance of the DEGs. In our Enrichr analysis, we employed KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) to identify enriched pathways. We evaluated only the significant pathways with an Adj p-value <0.05, after removing any duplicates.

Calculation of shared and unique genes and pathways

To identify the most important genes, we calculated the common genes across the datasets for each disease and compiled a list of their interactions. This resulted in the construction of a PPI network, from which the most significant nodes were extracted using network analysis techniques in Cytoscape using the degree centrality parameter. The lists of DEGs obtained from each comparison were then overlapped to identify genes that were consistently differentially expressed across the multiple datasets. This overlapping analysis was performed using bioinformatics tools of Venn diagrams (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/) to visualize and characterize the shared DEGs. Shared genes were found in at least two datasets for asthma, COPD, and lung cancer. Following this, shared genes that displayed consistent directional expression in both diseases were identified as potential genetic biomarkers connecting asthma/COPD to lung cancer. Additionally, gene ontology and pathway enrichment analysis were conducted to gain insights into the biological functions and pathways associated with the identified DEGs. This comprehensive approach allowed for the robust identification and characterization of DEGs that are consistently dysregulated across different experimental conditions or disease states in the microarray study. A similar approach was utilized to identify shared and unique pathways among different conditions.24

Survival analysis and statistical methods

RNA-seq expression profiles and corresponding clinical data for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) patients were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) using the UCSCXenaShiny R package.25 Gene expression levels (mRNA profiles) were extracted for the candidate genes of interest. Clinical data included disease-specific survival (DSS) time and DSS event status, which were used as the primary survival endpoints. Patients with missing expression data or incomplete survival information were excluded from the analysis. For each gene, patients were stratified into “High” and “Low” expression groups based on the median expression value as the cutoff threshold. Overall survival differences between high and low expression groups were assessed using Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves, and statistical significance was determined by the two-sided log-rank test.26 Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to estimate the hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for each gene, treating gene expression as a continuous variable. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software with the survival and survminer packages. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant, while P-values between 0.05 and 0.10 were classified as borderline significance. Genes that failed to converge or had insufficient sample size (n < 10) were excluded from the final analysis.

Physical protein-protein interaction network

A physical protein-protein interaction network was established to analyze the shared genes between lung cancer and asthma, as well as between lung cancer and COPD. The shared genes were analyzed with STRING tool (https://string-db.org/).27, 28 The data source for this analysis included text mining, experimental evidence, protein-protein interaction databases, gene fusion data, gene neighborhood analysis, and co-occurrence patterns. Specific parameters were configured for building the biological network, designating it as a “full STRING network” and setting a minimum interaction score of 0.4. The study focused on physical and experimental protein-protein interactions to create the interaction network, which was then visualized and analyzed with Cytoscape software (https://cytoscape.org/).29 Hub genes with a high degree were identified with the Network Analyzer plugin in Cytoscape. Degree centrality served as the primary topological metric for ranking and selecting hub genes. Genes with a degree ≥10 were defined as hub genes in the present study. Betweenness and closeness centralities were also calculated but were not used as selection criteria.

Data collection

The search strategy using the keywords “Lung Cancer”, “COPD”, “Asthma”, and “Homo sapiens” yielded nine microarray datasets from the GEO database. These datasets, based on Affymetrix and Agilent platforms, were chosen for their relevance to the study's focus on respiratory conditions in humans.

In total, the selected gene expression datasets (GSE64913, GSE67472, GSE130499, GSE103174, GSE106986, GSE40275, GSE67061, GSE126533, and GSE149507) met the inclusion criteria, which required the presence of healthy samples for comparison with disease conditions. Control samples for lung cancer studies were non-cancerous (or adjacent normal) tissues from the same cancer patients, while control samples for asthma and COPD studies were obtained from individuals without respiratory conditions.

Discussion

Although COPD, asthma, and lung cancer share phenotypic similarities, their underlying biological mechanisms and etiologies differ significantly. Environmental and genetic factors play important roles in the development and exacerbation of respiratory diseases. Notably, asthma and COPD have been associated with an increased risk of LC.3, 5, 7 This study used a PPI network and PEA to identify shared genes and biological pathways in LC, COPD, and asthma. This approach was intended to provide insights into disease mechanisms, progression, and treatment responses. By focusing on key genes shared across these respiratory conditions, we sought to identify underlying molecular connections.

The interplay between oncogenic and tumor suppressor proteins highlights the complex mechanisms underlying cancer development and progression. MDM4, an oncogenic protein, exerts its effects by interacting with tumor suppressor p53, leading to increased cellular growth and reduced apoptotic activity. Notably, amplification and overexpression of MDM4 in cancer cells have been implicated in the disruption of p53 downstream targets, hthereby promoting tumorigenesis. Targeting the MDM4–p53 interaction has emerged as a promising strategy to restore p53 tumor-suppressive function and may offer potential avenues for cancer treatment. Moreover, specific MDM4 variants lacking the p53 binding region have been identified, suggesting a distinct mechanism through which MDM4 may drive cancer progression independently of p53 and positioning MDM4 as a potential therapeutic target in oncology.30 In parallel, the regulatory role of long non-coding RNAs such as MALAT1 in cancer pathogenesis has attracted conciderable attention. MALAT1 has been shown to modulate the growth, invasiveness, and survival of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells through the miR-185-5p/MDM4 pathway. Additionally, the impact of tumor suppressor proteins such as p53 on pulmonary conditions, inclulding emphysema, underscores the intricate genetic and molecular landscape underlying respiratory diseases.31, 32

Furthermore, the involvement of key signaling pathways, such as the Sonic hedgehog (SHH) pathway, in embryonic development and adult tissue maintenance highlights their critical role in physiological homeostasis.33 Disruptions in SHH signaling have been implicated in various malignancies because of their regulatory effects on cell growth, differentiation, and programmed cell death.34 Notably, the interplay between proteins such as Dispatched-1 (DISP1) and lipid-modified SHH underscores the mechanisms governing cellular export and protein degradation, highlighting potential therapeutic targets within these pathways.35 Additionally, the SHH pathway plays a important role in adult lung pathologies, including asthma, COPD, and lung cancer, where aberrant signaling and chronic inflammation, contribute to tissue remodeling.36 In conditions such as COPD and asthma, fibroblast proliferation and extracellular matrix accumulation contribute to lung fibrosis, and activation of the mesenchymal HH signaling pathway has been observed in fibrotic lungs and other organs.37, 38 Experimental inhibition of the HH pathway has shown anti-fibrotic effects, suggesting a broader mechanism underlying its activation in these pathologies.36

The emerging role of zinc finger proteins such as ZMAT1 in regulating tumor suppressor pathways, particularly involving p53, suggests a broader impact on cancer progression. Although ZMAT1 has been extensively studied in various cancer types, its potential implications in respiratory diseases such as COPD remain underexplored, warranting further investigation into its role in oxidative stress and inflammatory responses within the pulmonary microenvironment.39

Similarly, the ABCA subfamily, notably ABCA13, has been implicated in lipid transport processes and innate immune functions within the bronchial epithelium,40, 41, 42 particularly in the context of smoking-related alterations.42 The dysregulation of ABCA13 expression may contribute to inflammatory responses and compromised lung function, emphasizing the intricate interplay between lipid metabolism and respiratory health and cancer.43, 44, 45 Studies have demonstrated that the expression levels of ABCA13 in epithelial cells are influenced by factors such as asthma and smoking.42 Furthermore, a reduction in ABCA13 expression has been observed to correlate with a tendency toward elevated asthma severity.42 Furthermore, a genome-wide association study (GWAS) identified a genetic variant in the ABCA13 gene that is reproducibly associated with a increased risk of asthma exacerbations. This finding identifies the ABCA13 locus as a novel genetic factor influencing the clinical severity and prognosis of asthma.46

Lastly, the prognostic relevance of the spectrin repeat-containing nuclear envelope family member SYNE2 in malignancies underscores its potential as a therapeutic target in tumor invasiveness and metastasis. The implications of SYNE2 expression for the tumor microenvironment, nucleus stability, and apoptosis in cancer cells underscore its multifaceted role in cancer biology and highlight its potential utility in future cancer therapies.47 A pooled GWAS followed by individual genotyping in French Canadian adult women with allergic asthma identified several additional susceptibility genes. SYNE2 was one of the genes implicated by an in silico functional analysis of the asthma-associated SNPs.48 Another study utilized Transcriptomics data from bronchial epithelial cells of children with asthma and identified a diagnostic model including SYNE2. This study suggests SYNE2 expression may be linked to the hypoxia that can occur during severe asthma and its related inflammatory pathways.49

The identification of shared pathways across lung cancer, COPD, and asthma sheds light on shared mechanisms that underlie these respiratory diseases. The research on Th17 cell differentiation has revealed an imbalance between Th17 and T regulatory (Treg) cells in lung conditions, indicating a potential impact on the pathophysiology of these diseases. Th17 cells, along with their associated cytokine IL-17, have been implicated in both the chronic progression of COPD and acute exacerbations triggered by bacterial infections.50 Recent findings have also linked Th17 cells to asthma, where their presence in the airways corresponds to enhanced neutrophil levels and resistance to conventional treatments.51 Treg cells, known for their role in mitigating inflammatory responses, have been shown to play a crucial role in preventing the onset and advancement of asthma and COPD.52 Moreover, Th17 cells have been identified in various human malignancies, including lung cancer, highlighting their potential impact on cancer development through mechanisms like angiogenesis stimulation.

The neurotrophin signaling pathway, primarily recognized for its role in the nervous system, has been found to involve growth factors in various lung components. Studies have shown that neurotrophins (NTs) play crucial roles in normal lung development, allergic reactions, inflammation, and pulmonary cancer.53 In asthma, allergen exposure has been linked to increased NT levels, influencing sensitization to allergens and airway inflammation.54 Additionally, NTs may impact airway responsiveness, airway remodeling, and epithelial inflammation. NTs have also been implicated in cancer development, particularly in pulmonary tumors, where they induce angiogenesis and promote tumor growth and proliferation.53, 55, 56 Targeting NTs could potentially contribute to tumor regression by inhibiting their expression.

The p53 signaling pathway is crucial in maintaining lung homeostasis, particularly in response to DNA damage, hypoxia, and oxidative stress, which are key features of COPD.57 Studies have shown that dysregulation of p53, such as through miR-675-mediated downregulation or altered interaction with MDM2, may play a role in the pathogenesis of COPD and asthma.32 Elevated levels of miR-675 have been linked to p53 downregulation in NSCLC patients who also have COPD, while in asthma, excessive accumulation of IL-1β and neutrophils has been associated with p53 expression.58, 59 Understanding the role of p53 in lung diseases could offer insights into potential therapeutic strategies targeting this pathway to improve disease outcomes.

Pathways like autophagy and mitophagy regulate cellular homeostasis by removing damaged cellular components; altered autophagy has been linked to chronic lung inflammation and may contribute to carcinogenesis via cellular stress responses.60 Also, Infectious disease pathways (viral myocarditis, Epstein-Barr virus infection, Human T-cell leukemia virus 1 infection) may seem unrelated superficially but reflect host-pathogen interactions and immune modulation processes that can exacerbate chronic respiratory disease and influence tumor microenvironments.61

The results of the shared biological pathways are necessarily derived from computational analysis of integrated gene expression data through functional enrichment methods. While these bioinformatics approaches provide powerful insights into potential common mechanisms across asthma, COPD, and lung cancer, they represent a computational model that requires cautious interpretation.62 The identification of statistically enriched pathways highlights candidates for further mechanistic investigation but does not constitute direct experimental validation.63, 64 Limitations inherent to such analyses include potential biases from sample heterogeneity, data integration challenges, and the complexity of biological systems that may not be fully captured computationally. Therefore, these findings serve as a hypothesis-generating step, emphasizing the need for targeted experimental studies to validate the functional relevance and causal roles of the shared pathways in these respiratory diseases. This approach aligns with current best practices in computational biology, where in silico results guide but do not replace empirical research.

To comprehensively evaluate the specific dysregulated molecular cascades, we integrated the shared top-enriched KEGG pathways identified in our study with the 5 overlapping genes (MDM4, DISP1, ZMAT1, ABCA13, and SYNE2). This integrated cascade explicitly demonstrates how these shared targets bridge the gap between chronic pulmonary inflammation and oncogenesis by orchestrating cell cycle progression and immune evasion.

Destruction of cellular homeostasis, apoptosis, and p53 pathways

As illustrated in the functional enrichment profile, several pathways tightly governing cellular survival and cycle regulation are highly enriched, most notably the p53 signaling pathway, Apoptosis, Autophagy, and Mitophagy. In a healthy respiratory epithelium, chronic inflammatory stress from asthma or COPD normally activates p53 to induce cell cycle arrest,65 autophagy, or apoptotic clearance of heavily damaged cells. However, the upregulation of MDM4—a potent negative regulator within the p53 signaling network—antagonizes p53-mediated transcriptional control, preventing the initiation of apoptosis in damaged epithelial cells and driving them toward survival and unchecked cell cycle progression.66 This cell cycle checkpoint failure is further compounded by the significant down-regulation of ZMAT1 observed across the datasets. ZMAT1 has been established as a crucial tumor suppressor that critically restricts cell proliferation by inducing p21 expression, which subsequently halts cellular progression through the S/G2 phases of the cell cycle.67, 68 Consequently, the marked down-regulation of ZMAT1 within the chronically inflamed pulmonary microenvironment of asthma and COPD leads to a loss of p21-mediated checkpoint control. This molecular deficiency facilitates the oncogenic transformation toward cancer. Furthermore, the convergence on Autophagy and Mitophagy pathways, alongside SYNE2, allows cells to metabolically re-adapt, recycle damaged organelles under hypoxic stress, and escape the mechanical mitotic restrictions typically encountered during chronic airway remodeling.49, 69, 70

Immune response

Concurrently, the enrichment analysis highlighted critical immune-regulatory architecture, including Endocytosis, Phagosome kinetics, and SNARE interactions in vesicular transport.71, 72 These pathways are pivotal for antigen processing, presentation, and membrane receptor recycling. In this context, the dysregulation of ABCA13 compromises transmembrane lipid transport and lipid raft stability, and ABCA1 is also involved in immune responses.73 Simultaneously, DISP1 (Dispatched RND Transporter Family Member 1), which functions through vesicular transport mechanisms, aberrantly drives the extracellular release of Hedgehog signaling ligands. It is important for development of T-cells and immune response.74

Furthermore, the identification of the five shared genes underscores a common genomic denominator across all three pulmonary pathologies. However, to decipher the broader mechanistic network and its clinical relevance, the 21 hub genes validated through the TCGA-LUAD cohort offer a more comprehensive view. These validated genes transition our findings from a shared hypothesis to distinct functional pathways—such as tissue remodeling, metabolic reprogramming, and immune evasion—directly influencing patient prognosis:

a.Tissue Remodeling and EMT: Genes such as EFNB2 (Ephrin B2), HPSE (Heparanase), and NMU (Neuromedin U) represent a critical bridge. The extracellular matrix degradation by HPSE75 and vascular remodeling mediated by EFNB276—which characterize structural airway changes in COPD and severe asthma—directly facilitate tumor invasion, angiogenesis, and cell motility in lung adenocarcinoma.

Collectively, our validated candidate genes provide a molecular framework for understanding the biological continuum from COPD/Asthma to lung cancer and may inform future risk stratification and therapeutic strategies for high-risk populations.

This study has identified specific potential candidate biomarkers and biological pathways that may contribute to the increased risk of lung cancer in patients with asthma or COPD. While further experimental validation is essential, these findings have potential practical significance in several areas. Firstly, the identified gene signatures could serve as the basis for developing novel diagnostic or prognostic tools to improve risk stratification in these patient populations. Secondly, the identified biological pathways may reveal potential therapeutic targets for intervention. Finally, this study generates specific hypotheses for future in vitro and in vivo studies aimed at elucidating the molecular mechanisms linking these respiratory diseases to lung cancer.

Limitation

This study is subject to several limitations. First, All findings reported in this study are derived from in silico analyses using publicly available transcriptomic datasets. While our bioinformatics approach identifies potentially relevant genes and pathways, these results are strictly hypothesis-generating. Experimental validation including qPCR, western blotting, or immunohistochemistry in independent clinical cohorts is required before any of the proposed candidates can be considered clinically useful biomarkers. Second, the heterogeneity arising from the integration of datasets across various studies, characterized by differences in patient demographics and experimental methodologies, may affect the generalizability of our results. Third, the absence of clinical data and the focus on gene expression, rather than protein levels or epigenetic modifications, limit our ability to establish causal relationships or direct clinical implications. A critical limitation in this study is the confounding influence of tobacco smoking, a primary shared driver for chronic airway diseases and lung cancer. Evidence shows that active smoking extensively alters transcriptomic profiles related to xenobiotic metabolism, carcinogen activation, and the dysregulation of genetic pathways governing inflammation and oxidative stress.82, 83 In public repositories like NCBI GEO, the lack of granular patient-level smoking metadata precludes the direct computational filtering of this variable. Therefore, some of our identified candidate genes likely reflect these broad, smoke-induced inflammatory alterations. Future prospective cohorts with strictly matched smoking histories are required to precisely decouple independent disease pathways from smoke-exposure artifacts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study elucidates the shared transcriptomic landscapes linking chronic inflammatory airway diseases, specifically asthma and COPD, to an increased susceptibility to lung cancer (LC). Rather than merely identifying overlapping profiles, our findings map out distinct molecular bridges that may drive malignant transformation in chronic pulmonary environments. However, we acknowledge that these insights are derived from retrospective computational analyses of microarray data, which inherently lack direct clinical history and longitudinal follow-up. To transition these hypothesis-generating insights into clinical utility, extensive preclinical validation is now required. Future in vitro and in vivo studies should focus on mechanistically dissecting how these specific essential genes alter cellular pathways to initiate oncogenesis. Ultimately, validating this robust panel of biomarkers offers a promising horizon for early diagnostic screening; incorporating these genetic targets into routine monitoring could significantly refine risk stratification and improve early intervention strategies for high-risk, non-cancerous pulmonary patients.
