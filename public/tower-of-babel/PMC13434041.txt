A functional map of phosphoprotein phosphatase regulation identifies an evolutionarily conserved reductase for the catalytic metal ions
Authors: Bob Meeusen, Sara M. Ambjørn, Jiri Veis, Rachel C. Riley, Gianmatteo Vit, Brooke L. Brauer, Mads H. Møller, Elora C. Greiner, Camilla B. Chan, Anna M. Schmoker, Blanca Lopez Mendez, Melanie B. Weisser, Dimitriya H. Garvanska, Hao Zhu, Norman E. Davey, Arminja N. Kettenbach, Egon Ogris, Jakob Nilsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Serine/threonine phosphoprotein phosphatases (PPPs) are conserved metalloenzymes and key regulators of intracellular signaling. Here, we systematically map the components and residues required for PP2A-like phosphatase (PP2A/PP4/PP6) function using genome-wide CRISPR knockout and focused base editing screens and provide this as a comprehensive resource. We uncover the reductase CYB5R4 as an evolutionarily conserved activator of PP4 and PP6, but not PP2A. We demonstrate that PP4 and PP6 are redox sensitive and require CYB5R4 to reduce the active site metal ions for activation. Mechanistically, this involves the selective binding of PP4 and PP6 through binding elements in the N-terminal tail of CYB5R4 and CYB5R4-bound heme serving as an electron donor for the active site. We further show that this activation is critical for regulating the response to DNA damage. Our work hints that active site metal ion redox regulation mediates crosstalk between the oxidative state of the cell and specific phosphatase activities.

Introduction

Reversible protein phosphorylation is an essential regulatory mechanism allowing cells to respond to extracellular and intracellular cues. The interplay between protein kinases and protein phosphatases controls the phosphorylation state of tyrosine, serine, and threonine residues on their target proteins, collectively dictating the outputs from signaling pathways1. Phosphoprotein phosphatases (PPPs, PP1–PP7) constitute a highly conserved family of serine/threonine protein phosphatases, accounting for the majority of dephosphorylation activity in eukaryotic cells2. The PPP catalytic subunits incorporate two metal ions to activate a water molecule that acts as the nucleophile in the dephosphorylation reaction3. Despite a highly similar catalytic core structure, the PPP family members achieve specificity by incorporating their catalytic subunits into family-specific holoenzymes. The PP2A, PP4, and PP6 catalytic subunits (referred to as PP2A C, PP4 C, and PP6 C) uniquely form stable trimeric holoenzymes with a structural subunit and a substrate-specifying regulatory subunit4,5. Another hallmark of PP2A, PP4, and PP6 is their similar nanomolar affinity for the PPP inhibitor okadaic acid (OA)3. These so-called PP2A-like phosphatases are widely involved in fundamental biological processes such as mitogenic signaling, cell cycle regulation, and the DNA damage response, and consistent with this, dysregulation of these proteins is linked to human diseases6–9. Central to the regulation of the PP2A-like phosphatase holoenzymes is the tightly orchestrated assembly and activation, which involves the action of at least five regulatory proteins (alpha4, PTPA, LCMT1, PME-1, and TIPRL) that engage the catalytic site, hereby governing catalytic metal ion coordination and trimeric holoenzyme composition (Fig. 1a)4,5,10–16. To address this, we performed genome-wide knockout and focused base editing screens under the selective pressure of the phosphatase inhibitor OA and established a high-resolution functional map of PP2A-like phosphatase regulation. We uncover several candidate regulators, including CYB5R4, which we demonstrate is a dedicated active site reductase for PP4 and PP6, but not PP2A, conserved from yeast to man.

Results

A genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screen identifies CYB5R4 as an evolutionarily conserved activator of PP4 and PP6 protein phosphatases

To expand our understanding of PP2A-like phosphatase regulation, we performed a genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 chemogenetic interaction screen to potentially identify additional regulatory mechanisms (Fig. 1b)17. We reasoned that in the presence of a low dose of the PPP inhibitor OA, where phosphatase activity is lowered but still permissive for proliferation (lethal dose 20, LD20), further lowering of phosphatase activity through knockout of genes required for phosphatase activity would cause sensitization to OA. At LD20 (2 nM), OA primarily inhibits PP2A-like phosphatases, and the screen therefore probes PP2A-like phosphatase biology. RPE-1 TP53-/- hTERT Cas9 cells were transduced at a low multiplicity of infection (~0.3) and high guide RNA (gRNA) representation (>300 fold) with the genome-wide knockout lentiviral library TKOv3::pLCKO218. Transduced cells were selected and expanded for another 6 days after selection (day 6) and then divided into 2 nM OA or untreated conditions and passaged for another 12 days in duplicate (to day 18). At day 18, cells were collected and processed for next-generation sequencing (NGS) to quantify gRNA abundances (Fig. 1b). Gene-level depletion scores were calculated using DrugZ19 to identify genes whose knockout sensitized to OA (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Data S1). Our screening approach was validated by the fact that the top depleted genes included PP2A-like holoenzyme components (PPP2CA, PPP2R1A, PPP2R5E, PPP6C, INTS6/10/13/15), and importantly, four out of five regulators of holoenzyme assembly (PTPA, LCMT1, PPME1, TIPRL) (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Data S1). The selectivity towards PP2A-like phosphatases at this dose of OA was supported by the lack of PP1 components scoring in our screen (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Data S1). Among the top 20 depleted genes, we identified eight candidates with no or poorly understood roles in PP2A-like phosphatase regulation20–23, which we here refer to as candidate phosphatase regulators. These eight candidates covered several distinct biological functions, including reductase activity (CYB5R4)24–26, ceramide synthesis (SPTCL1 and SPTLC2)27,28, and extracellular signal transduction (GTPase complex RIC8A and GNA12)29,30 (Fig. 1c).

To confirm that our screen indeed identified bonafide phosphatase regulators, we focused our attention on the top-scoring candidate, the cytochrome b5 oxidoreductase CYB5R4. CYB5R4 is a multidomain protein consisting of a Cytochrome b5 (Cytb5) heme-binding domain linked with a CS domain to its Cytochrome b5 reductase domain (Cytb5-R) (Fig. 1d), which can reduce various substrates in vitro24–26. Although previously linked to diabetes and oxidative stress in mouse models31,32 and genetically correlated with the PP2A-like phosphatase regulators PTPA and TIPRL20,33, its cellular function remains largely unknown.

First, to validate our screen results, we generated U2OS CYB5R4 knockout (KO) cells and confirmed that they are hypersensitive to OA, and this effect could be rescued by reintroduction of CYB5R4 (Fig. S1a–c). Since OA can target multiple PPPs in addition to PP2A, PP4, and PP6 (although at higher IC50’s), we next sought to get an unbiased view of which PPPs were affected by loss of CYB5R4. To this end, we used the phosphatase inhibitor beads and mass spectrometry (PIB-MS) approach, where all PPP catalytic subunits and their associated proteins are captured on microcystin-LR beads (MC-beads) followed by quantitative MS analysis, allowing for unbiased PPP profiling34. Capturing PPPs on MC-beads from lysates of U2OS parental and CYB5R4 KO cells revealed the specific loss of PP4 and PP6 but not PP2A holoenzyme components in CYB5R4 KO cells, which was rescued by reintroduction of CYB5R4 (Figs. 1e, S1d, and Supplementary Data S2). The specific loss of PP4 and PP6 components was reproducible in RPE-1 cells, MEFs from CYB5R4 KO mice31, observed at physiological oxygen levels, and not due to changes at the proteome level (Fig. S1e–g and Supplementary Data S2–6). As microcystin-LR binds to the catalytic site of PPPs, this suggests that CYB5R4 has a selective effect on PP4 and PP6 catalytic subunits, potentially by affecting the catalytic site. To test if PP4 and PP6 activity was affected, we generated stable cell lines expressing affinity-tagged catalytic subunits of PP2A, PP4, and PP6 in U2OS parental and CYB5R4 KO cells. We subsequently performed affinity-tag purification to isolate the respective holoenzymes and tested their activity in dephosphorylation assays using a standard model phosphopeptide (WRRApTVA). This showed that PP4 and PP6 catalytic activity was impeded in the absence of CYB5R4, while PP2A activity was unaffected (Fig. 1f). The reduction of PP6 activity in the absence of CYB5R4 was confirmed in cells as we observed a strong increase in the PP6-specific target pT35 MOB1 in CYB5R4 KO cells (Fig. S1h)35. The loss of activity was not an effect of aberrant holoenzyme formation, since quantitative MS indicated that most holoenzyme subunits co-purified with the catalytic subunits with no major differences between parental and CYB5R4 KO cells (Fig. S1i–j and Supplementary Data S7-9).

In line with the evolutionary conservation of PPPs and their regulation, deletion of the CYB5R4 orthologue irc21 in S. cerevisiae resulted in depletion of Pph3 (PP4 C) and Sit4 (PP6 C) in PIB-MS (Fig. S2a and Supplementary Data S10) and reduced catalytic activity of Pph3 and Sit4 but not Pph21 (PP2A C) in vitro (Fig. 1g). Additionally, the activity of Sit4 towards its substrate Sap185 was reduced in vivo without affecting holoenzyme composition (Fig. S2b). Although also reported to be a regulator of Pph2136, our data demonstrate that Irc21 is a positive regulator of Sit4 and Pph3, not Pph21, which is consistent with existing genetic data36,37.

Intriguingly, Irc21 contains only a Cytb5 domain and not the CS or Cytb5-R domains (Fig. 1d), prompting us to ask whether the Cytb5 domain of CYB5R4 would be sufficient for its function in PP4/PP6 activation. Indeed, by re-introducing the domains separately in U2OS CYB5R4 KO cells, we found that the Cytb5 domain was necessary and sufficient for rescuing the sensitivity to OA (Fig. S2c, d), arguing that at least in this cell culture assay, the CS and Cytb5-R domains are not required. Consistent with this, affinity-tag purifications from HeLa cells analyzed by MS showed that CYB5R41–153 was sufficient for binding to PP4 and PP6, but not PP2A components (Fig. S2e and Supplementary Data S11). Gratifyingly, the human Cytb5 domain could complement the irc21 deletion in yeast, rescuing the activity of Sit4 towards its substrate Sap185 in vivo (Fig. S2f).

Altogether, our genome-wide OA chemogenetic interaction screen uncovered regulatory mechanisms for PP2A-like phosphatases and identified CYB5R4 as an evolutionarily conserved activator of PP4 and PP6.

Base editing tiling screens provide a high-resolution map of phosphatase function and regulation

To get deeper molecular insights into the regulation of PP2A-like phosphatases, we subjected established and candidate regulators and holoenzyme components scoring in the genome-wide OA chemogenetic interaction screen to base editing tiling screening. We selected a total of 19 targets, choosing to include all PP2A-like catalytic subunits and known regulators (Fig. 2a, b). Briefly, base editing relies on a Cas9 protein (often a Cas9 nickase) fused to a base editor (a deaminase) being directed by a gRNA to a specific genomic locus where mutations are precisely introduced (Fig. 2a). The base editor makes mutations in a defined editing window at a specific distance from the protospacer adjacent motif (PAM), where the mutational outcome is determined by the underlying codons38. We decided to use the efficient adenine base editor (ABE) ABE8e, which makes A- > G conversions in a 5-nucleotide editing window, fused to an engineered SpG Cas9 nickase, which has a relaxed PAM requirement (NGN) that allows high mutational coverage of the protein-encoding sequence39–43. We generated a custom 7928 gRNA ABE ‘tiling’ library targeting the 19 genes, hereby mutating 5471 residues, covering on average 50.8% of the coding sequence with mutations (Fig. 2a, b). RPE-1 TP53-/- hTERT cells were transduced with the base editing tiling library in duplicate at a low multiplicity of infection (~0.3) and high gRNA representation (>500 fold). Transduced cells were selected, expanded, and treated with a low dose of OA (LD20, 1.7 nM) as in the genome-wide OA chemogenetic interaction screen (Fig. 1b). At day 0 and day 18, cells were collected and processed for NGS to quantify gRNA abundances at the different timepoints and treatments. Changes in gRNA abundances were used as a measure of mutational effects on cell fitness and interpreted in light of the predicted amino acid mutations introduced by the base editor, which we have previously shown to correlate well with actual editing44. By comparing gRNA abundance at the endpoint of the screen (day 18) to the starting point (day 0), as well as OA-treated (day 18) to untreated (day 18), we mapped residues whose mutation caused proliferation defects and residues whose mutation affected OA sensitivity. This allowed us to generate a functionally inferred residue map for each protein (Figs. 2b, S3a, b, and Supplementary Data S12). In total, we identified 585 and 378 gRNAs that introduced mutations causing proliferation defects and sensitization to OA, respectively (using a z-score cut-off of <−5). Importantly, stability modeling of the predicted mutations using RaSP45 showed they would largely have a minor impact (pΔΔG < 3) on protein stability, except for the introduction of prolines (Fig. S3c and Supplementary Data S12). The proliferation defect and OA-sensitization effect data were further integrated with curated datasets on residue conservation, post-translational modification, annotated function, and disease mutations, as well as structural models of proteins, and are available to explore in an interactive web portal (https://slim-tools.org/base_editing/base_editing_tiling) to facilitate future research on the regulation of PP2A-like phosphatases.Fig. 2Base editing screens map functional residues of PP2A-like phosphatase components and regulators.a Schematic of adenine base editing with ABE8e-Cas9 SpG and details on the base editing library. The base editing approach was validated by the fact that mutation of residues known to be involved in activity, substrate specificity, and regulation of PP2A-like phosphatases resulted in proliferation defects and sensitization to OA. For example, we identified residues coordinating the active site metal ions in the catalytic subunits3 and residues in B56epsilon involved in substrate binding46,47 (Fig. S4a–d). For LCMT1, a key regulator of holoenzyme assembly that methylates the C-terminal tail of the catalytic subunit, several residues in the indispensable alpha1 helix, S-adenosyl-methionine (co-factor) binding residues (e.g., D122), and PP2A C-tail binding residues (e.g., C199, Y315)12, as well as residues without an assigned function (e.g., D156 and C258) scored in the screen (Fig. 2c, d). Importantly, expression of affinity-tagged LCMT1 in HeLa cells showed that introduction of the predicted base editing mutations in these residues did not affect protein stability, as witnessed by similar expression levels of the mutant proteins as compared to wildtype (Fig. 2e). Four out of five of the tested base editing mutations affected LCMT1 function through loss of binding (C199R, C258R, Y315H) or trapping (D122G) of PP2A C, while D156G did not affect binding (Fig. 2e). In addition to supporting known molecular mechanisms, the base editing functional maps also revealed possible additional mechanisms of regulation. Importantly, mutation of several residues amongst the candidate regulators sensitized to OA (Fig. 2b, Fig. S3c, and Supplementary Data S12), providing additional support for their function in PP2A-like phosphatase biology. Altogether, we have generated a powerful resource that can be used as a starting point to further dissect PP2A-like phosphatase regulation.

Heme binding is required for CYB5R4 function and binding to PP4 and PP6

We were intrigued by the observation that heme-coordinating residues (H89/H112) in CYB5R4, as well as a proximal tryptophan residue (W114), scored strongly in our base editing screen (Fig. 3a, b), suggesting a key role of the heme group in activating PP4 and PP6. To pursue these observations further, we first confirmed that the gRNAs introduced the predicted mutations. We transduced RPE-1 TP53-/- hTERT cells with the single gRNAs targeting these residues in CYB5R4, PCR amplified and deep sequenced the target genomic loci, and analyzed the mutational outcome with CRISPResso250. Indeed, the analysis showed efficient introduction of the predicted mutations targeting H89 (Y88 was also frequently targeted), H112, and W114 (Figs. 3c and S5a). Next, to validate the screen results, we reintroduced CYB5R4 carrying the single base editing mutations in U2OS CYB5R4 KO cells and confirmed that these mutations conferred hypersensitivity to OA (Figs. 3d and S5b, c).

Since the heme-coordinating histidines and the proximal tryptophan are important for CYB5R4 function, we asked whether they would be required for the observed binding to PP4 and PP6. Indeed, when either H89/H112 or W114 was mutated to alanine, binding of PP4 and PP6 components to CYB5R41–153 was lost (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Data S11). Given that the five key PP2A-like phosphatase regulators engage the active site of the catalytic subunit in a mutually exclusive fashion16, we reasoned that CYB5R4 could possess a similar mode of interaction. We therefore generated AlphaFold 3 (AF3) models of CYB5R41–153 and the catalytic subunits of PP4 and PP6 to obtain further insights into their mode of interaction (Figs. 4b and S6a, b)52. The AF3 models confidently predicted the interaction of the Cytb5 domain with the phosphatase catalytic subunits. More specifically, the Cytb5 domain was positioned facing the catalytic site, in such a way that the heme group and W114 closely contacted residues on PP4 C and PP6 C whose mutations sensitized to OA (Y125 and C263 mutated in PP4 C; R85 mutated in PP6 C) (Figs. S6c and 4c). This AF3 model explains the requirement for heme coordination by H89/H112 and W114 for binding to the catalytic subunit and suggests that CYB5R4 competes with other regulators, such as PTPA, for binding to the catalytic subunit11. Indeed, in the absence of CYB5R4/Irc21, we observe increased binding of PTPA/Rrd1 to the catalytic subunits (Figs. S1i and S6d). This potentially reflects competition for the active site, or that the active site is partially unfolded, causing retention by PTPA11.

CYB5R4 activates PP4 and PP6 by reducing the metal ions in the catalytic site

The positioning of the CYB5R4 heme group in close proximity to the catalytic site of PP4 and PP6 in the AF3 models led us to hypothesize that CYB5R4 activates these phosphatases by donating electrons from the heme group to their catalytic site. This could be facilitated by a relay of electrons from the heme group through W11424,25. To test this directly, we established an in vitro reconstituted system with purified components. To this end, we incubated purified reconstituted PP4 (PP4 C, PP4R2, PP4R3A) and PP6 (PP6 C, PP6R3, ANKRD28) holoenzymes (Supplementary Data S13), expressed with the Multi-Mam system in HEK293, with purified recombinant CYB5R41–153 wild-type or H89A/H112A, and tested their activity. While reconstituted PP4 and PP6 holoenzymes both displayed modest baseline activities, CYB5R41–153 wild-type stimulated their activities over 3-fold, whereas the H89A/H112A mutant was unable to do so (Figs. 4d and S7a). These findings were fully recapitulated in yeast, where inactive Sit4 (PP6) isolated from the irc21 deletion strain could be rescued by human CYB5R41–153 wildtype but not H89A/H112A in vitro (Fig. S7b–d). Importantly, in each of these assays, only CYB5R41–153 pre-reduced by L-ascorbate could induce activation, corroborating our hypothesis that CYB5R4 acts by donating electrons to the phosphatase (Fig. S7e). The most likely targets for reduction by CYB5R4 would be either the catalytic metal ions or cysteine residues, since both are prone to oxidation53,54. However, in our in vitro assays, PP4 and PP6 activity was not stimulated by the cysteine reducing agents DTT or TCEP, while L-ascorbate, which can reduce metal ions, stimulated phosphatase activity (Figs. 4e and S7f). This argues that in our in vitro assays, CYB5R4 stimulation is through metal ion reduction. To support this hypothesis, we converted PP6 into an oxidation-resistant phosphatase by removing the active site metal ions by incubation with EDTA and reactivated the phosphatase by adding back bivalent metal ions in the form of Mn2+. The activity of this PP6-Mn2+ complex was not further stimulated by CYB5R4, supporting that CYB5R4 activates phosphatases by safeguarding the bivalency of the catalytic metal ions (Figs. 4f and S7g). Given the role of CYB5R4 as a reductase and the specific requirement of PP4 and PP6 activity for CYB5R4, we reasoned that PP4 and PP6 might be more susceptible to the redox environment than PP2A. To test this, we isolated PP2A C, PP4 C, and PP6 C, and Pph21, Pph3, and Sit4, by affinity tag purification from HeLa cells and yeast, respectively, and subsequently assessed the effect of L-ascorbate on their activity in a peptide dephosphorylation assay. While PP2A and Pph21 activity was not further increased by L-ascorbate, PP4/Pph3 and PP6/Sit4 activity were potently stimulated (Figs. 4g and S7h). Altogether, our observations support the role of CYB5R4 as a reductase for the active site metal ions and explain why PP4 and PP6, and not PP2A, require reduction by CYB5R4.

The N-terminal tail of CYB5R4 confers phosphatase selectivity

Given the high sequence homology between the catalytic subunits of PP2A, PP4, and PP63, we were intrigued by the selective binding of CYB5R4 to CaN, PP4, and PP6, and not PP2A. Indeed, AF3 also models CYB5R4 confidently to the catalytic subunit of PP2A, suggesting that additional contacts between CYB5R4 and PP4 and PP6 are required for specificity (Fig. S8a). We noticed that the N-terminal unstructured tail of CYB5R4 contains putative binding motifs for CaN (LNVP2–6)55 and PP4 regulatory subunit PPP4R3 (FPAP9–12)56, and co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP)-MS analysis of systematic tail truncations from HeLa cells confirmed that these motifs are required for binding (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Data S14). CYB5R4 binding to PP6 also depends on residues 1–25, suggesting the presence of PP6 binding elements in this region. Interestingly, we noticed two consensus phosphorylation sites for the DNA damage response (DDR) kinases (SQ sites) flanking the FxxP motif (6-SQSFPAPRSQ−15) (Fig. S8b). Co-IP-MS experiments in HeLa cells with phospho-dead (S6A/S14A) and phospho-mimetic (S6D/S14D) CYB5R4 variants and ITC experiments with phosphorylated peptides and the EVH1 domain of PP4R3A support that the phosphorylation of these SQ sites negatively regulates the binding to PP4 and to a lesser extent PP6 (Fig. S8b, c and Supplementary Data S15). Altogether, our observations explain the selectivity of CYB5R4 towards PP4, PP6, and CaN, and hint at a potential regulatory mechanism.

CYB5R4 activation of phosphatases is central to the response to DNA damage

Loss of CYB5R4 and Irc21 has previously been shown to induce hypersensitivity to the DNA-damaging agents cisplatin and Illudin S20,57, potentially reflecting a biological setting where CYB5R4 activation of phosphatases is critical for proper response to DNA damage. Consistent with this idea, loss of the core PP2A-like phosphatase regulators PTPA and TIPRL also causes increased sensitivity to cisplatin and Illudin S20. Cisplatin-induced DNA crosslinks are mainly repaired by nucleotide excision repair (NER), the Fanconi anemia (FA) and homologous recombination (HR) pathways, while Illudin S–induced DNA adducts are resolved by transcription-coupled NER58,59. To get mechanistic insights into how these DNA repair processes are affected by phosphatase-activating mechanisms, we expanded our base editing screening approach to cisplatin and Illudin S. In these screens, we identified 89 and 98 gRNAs (z-score < −5), which caused sensitization to cisplatin and Illudin S, respectively, pinpointing residues that are functionally important in the response to these DNA-damaging agents (Fig. S9a-b and Supplementary Data S12). We first analyzed the data at a high level, by profiling the functional similarities between proteins based on correlations of their residue-drug interactions across treatments (OA, cisplatin, Illudin S). Genes with similar correlation scores across treatment pairs cluster together, suggesting they are functionally linked. This revealed a close correlation of residue-drug interactions for CYB5R4, PTPA, TIPRL, PP4, and PP6, but not PP2A, across all three treatments (Fig. 5a). Fully in line with this, the top-scoring mutations in CYB5R4 from the OA screen, H89R, H112R, and W114R, similarly sensitized cells to cisplatin and Illudin S, and we could validate the hypersensitivity to cisplatin by reintroduction of CYB5R4 carrying these mutations in CYB5R4 KO U2OS cells (Figs. 5b and S9c). Together, this suggests that the sensitivity of CYB5R4 KO cells to cisplatin and Illudin S is associated with their inability to activate PP4 and PP6. In line with loss of phosphatase activity, we observed increased levels of DDR kinase site (SQ) phosphorylation after treatment with cisplatin in CYB5R4 KO cells compared to parental cells (Fig. S9d). To further investigate this in an unbiased fashion, we compared the phopshoproteomes (normalized to the total proteomes) of U2OS parental and CYB5R4 KO cells. At baseline, we observed only moderate changes in the phosphorylation profile, but upon treatment with 1 µM cisplatin, we observed a massive deregulation of cellular phosphorylation (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Data S16). Pathway enrichment analysis suggested a strong increase in phosphorylation of RNA metabolism and processing factors (Fig. S9e). Focusing on phospho-sites in DNA repair proteins, categorized by DNA repair process (as in ref. 20), revealed that phosphorylation was significantly decreased for proteins involved in HR and increased for proteins involved in NER and Ribonucleotide Excision Repair (RER) (Fig. 5c, d and Supplementary Data S16 and 17). Discussion

Here, we integrate CRISPR knockout and base editing screens to uncover molecular mechanisms of PP2A-like protein phosphatase regulation—a general approach that can be applied to other signaling enzymes where specific inhibitors are available. We provide a readily accessible web resource (https://slim-tools.org/base_editing/base_editing_tiling) for consulting our near amino acid-resolution functional map of PP2A-like phosphatase regulators and holoenzyme components, facilitating future research on mechanisms controlling their activity. Our map can be used to broaden our understanding of cross-talk between PP2A-like phosphatases and intracellular signaling mechanisms, as exemplified by CYB5R4, which we pursued in depth. Like other technologies, base editing screens have certain limitations, including the possibility of gRNA off-target effects or bystander mutations, indirect effects on drug sensitivity relating to drug uptake and/or metabolism, and protein stability effects. Therefore, thorough validation, as done for CYB5R4, is necessary for hit validation.

We identified CYB5R4 as an evolutionarily conserved reductase for the active site metal ions of PP4 and PP6 but not PP2A. We propose a model where CYB5R4 selectively acts on PP4 and PP6 holoenzymes through a binding interface comprised of binding elements in its N-terminal tail and the Cyt-b5 domain, positioning the heme group in close proximity to the catalytic site. Electrons are then transferred from the Cytb5-heme group—potentially shuttled through CYB5R4 W11425—to the PP4/6 catalytic site, hereby inducing reduction of the catalytic metal ion(s) and phosphatase activation (Fig. 5e). Given the strong conservation of a functional CaN binding motif in CYB5R4, we favor that CaN is regulated in a similar way, and future studies will be aimed at investigating this. The presence of a dedicated active site reductase indicates crosstalk between the redox state of the cell and PP4 and PP6 activity. This expands the regulatory mechanisms of PPPs and suggests that these phosphatases likely share regulatory principles with protein tyrosine phosphatases, where dedicated enzymes controlling active site oxidation and reduction are a central and well-established mechanism60–62. The presence of such a mechanism fits with the fact that PPPs have been shown to be inactivated through catalytic metal ion oxidation53,63. Whether a dedicated oxidase for the active site of PP4 and PP6 exists, as reported for PP164, or whether the active site metal ions are highly sensitive to oxidation is presently unclear. While the exact nature of the catalytic metal ions is debated, Fe2+ has been proposed, and importantly, this ion is readily oxidized3,53,65. We show that CYB5R4 reduction of PP4 and PP6 is important for controlling the response to DNA damage, providing a biological example where active site redox state needs to be regulated. Whether additional biological pathways are regulated by PP4 and PP6 oxidation outlines an important future research direction. Surprisingly, under our cell culture conditions, the CytB5 reductase domain of CYB5R4 is not required for its function. This is likely due to intracellular ascorbate providing a reducing source for the heme of the CytB5 domain, as in our in vitro assays. However, we speculate that under conditions of elevated oxidative stress, the CytB5 reductase domain will be required to maintain activation of PP4 and PP6. An intriguing observation is that PP2A activity is not dependent on CYB5R4 function, despite a high level of sequence identity with PP4 and PP6 catalytic subunits. Consistent with this, we show that PP4 and PP6 highly depend on a reducing environment for activity, while PP2A does not. Whether this reflects a difference in the nature of the active site metal ions or subtle differences in the active site architecture that make the metals more sensitive to oxidation in PP4 and PP6 requires further investigation. Collectively, we uncover a previously unknown regulatory mechanism for PP4 and PP6, which is rooted in a higher redox susceptibility of their catalytic sites compared to PP2A.

Method

Cell culture

Cell lines, listed in Supplementary Data S18, were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium with GlutaMAX (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco) and 100 U/ml penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Low oxygen experiments with U2OS cells were performed in an oxygen control chamber (InvivO2 400 Hypoxia workstation, Baker Ruskinn) set at 37 °C, 5% CO2, and 6% O2.

CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screens

Virus production

Lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfection of the gRNA plasmid library TKOv3::pLCKO2, with lentiviral packaging plasmids pMD2.G and psPAX2 in HEK293T/17 cells (ATCC, CRL-11268) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) in Opti-MEM (Gibco). 6 h after transfection, the medium was exchanged for DMEM GlutaMax + 10% FBS + 100 U/ml penicillin–streptomycin + 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA). 48 h after transfection, viral particles were harvested and filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter before freezing at −80 °C. The TKOv3::pLCKO2 library (Addgene plasmid #125517) was a gift from Jason Moffat. pMD2.G (Addgene plasmid # 12259; http://n2t.net/addgene:12259; RRID:Addgene_12259) and psPAX2 (Addgene plasmid # 12260; http://n2t.net/addgene:12260; RRID:Addgene_12260) were gifts from Didier Trono.

Transduction and cell culture

RPE-1;hTERT;TP53−/−;Cas9 cells (a kind gift from D. Durocher) were cultured in DMEM GlutaMax supplemented with 10% FBS and 100 U/ml penicillin–streptomycin and passaged every three days. The screen was performed as a technical duplicate (1 single transduction split up into two replicate drug treatments) at a coverage of above 300-fold gRNA representation, which was maintained throughout the screen. Cells were transduced with the lentiviral library at a low multiplicity of infection (0.2–0.3) by treating cells with 8 μg/ml polybrene and lentiviral supernatants for 24 h. Transduced cells were selected by treatment with 20 μg/ml puromycin for 24 h, followed by trypsinization and reseeding in the same plates with 20 μg/ml puromycin for another 24 h. After selection, cells were passaged for 6 days before splitting into untreated or OA-treated fractions. Cells were passaged for an additional 12 days with passaging every 3 days in medium with or without a low dose of OA (2 nM), which corresponds to predetermined LD20 concentrations. Cell pellets were harvested after completion of selection, which we consider the start of the screen (day 0) and at the final timepoint (day 18).

NGS and analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from the cells, and the genomic DNA regions containing the integrated gRNAs were amplified by PCR using NEBNext Ultra II Q5 Master mix (New England BioLabs) with the pLCKO2_forward and pLCKO2_reverse primers (Supplementary Data S19). A second PCR reaction introduced i5 and i7 multiplexing barcodes (Supplementary Data S19), and gel-purified PCR products were sequenced on Illumina NextSeq500. Data was analyzed as in ref. 66. Briefly, fastq files were generated using bcl2fastq v2.20.1, reads were trimmed to 20 bp using cutadapt 1.1867, and trimmed reads were assigned to gRNAs in the TKOv3 library by MAGeCK 0.5.9.568 to create a count matrix, from which gene scores (NormZ) were calculated with DrugZ19. Data quality was assessed by BAGEL.py “pr” function69 with core essential and non-essential gene lists (https://github.com/hart-lab/bagel), comparing day 0 to day 18 of untreated cells.

Generation of U2OS Flp-In T-REx CYB5R4 knockout cells

A gRNA targeting CYB5R4 (AATTGACCCAACGATGAAC20) was synthesized as DNA oligonucleotides with overhangs for BbsI cloning: Forward CACCGAATTGACCCAACGATGAACC; Reverse: AAACGGTTCATCGTTGGGTCAATTC (cloning sites in italic). The oligos were annealed and cloned into pSpCas9(BB)−2A-Puro (pX459) using BbsI cloning70. pSpCas9(BB)−2A-Puro (pX459) was a gift from Feng Zhang (Addgene plasmid # 48139; http://n2t.net/addgene:48139; RRID:Addgene_48139). U2OS Flp-In T-REx cells (a kind gift from H. Piwnica-Worms; referred to as U2OS for brevity) were transfected with the gRNA encoding plasmid using JETOptimus (Polyplus) and selected for 48 h in 1 μg/ml puromycin, followed by growth until colonies formed. Single colonies were picked, expanded in medium supplemented with 5 μg/ml blasticidin S HCl (Sigma) and 100 μg/ml Zeocin (Invitrogen), and CYB5R4 knockout was verified by Western blot analysis.

Cloning of plasmids

The human CYB5R4 cDNA was obtained from Promega and cloned into pcDNA5/FRT/TO/ plasmids with N- or C-terminal venus- or myc-tagging using restriction enzyme cloning. Mutations were introduced by mutagenic PCR with the primers indicated in Supplementary Data S19. Fragments of CYB5R4 for domain analysis were cloned by PCR with restriction site overhangs and subsequent restriction enzyme cloning. PP2A, PP4, and PP6 catalytic subunits were cloned into pcDNA5/FRT/TO with 3xFLAG-tagging using restriction enzyme cloning. Primers and plasmids used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data S19 and S20, respectively.

Generation of stable U2OS Flp-In T-REx cells

U2OS Flp-In T-REx cells or U2OS Flp-In T-REx CYB5R4 knockout (KO) cells were grown in medium supplemented with 5 μg/ml blasticidin S HCl (Sigma) and 100 μg/ml Zeocin (Invitrogen). To generate stable cell lines in the Flp-In system, cells were co-transfected with pOG44 and a pcDNA5 plasmid encoding the indicated construct (Supplementary Data S20) in a 10:1 ratio using the JETOptimus transfection reagent. After transfection, stable Flp-In T-REx cells were selected in medium supplemented with 5 μg/ml blasticidin S HCl and 200 μg/ml Hygromycin B. Expression from the Tet-ON inducible promoter in Flp-In T-REx cells was induced with the doxycycline doses as indicated in Supplementary Data S18. In U2OS Flp-In T-REx CYB5R4 KO cells, the doxycycline doses required to achieve expression levels of exogenous CYB5R4 constructs equal to or higher than endogenous CYB5R4 were estimated by Western blot analysis.

WB analysis of cell extracts

Cells were harvested by trypsinization and lysed on ice in RIPA buffer (10 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) supplemented with 1 mM DTT and Complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). The lysate was cleared by centrifugation at 20,000 g at 4 °C for 30 min, and the BCA assay (Pierce) was used to even out protein concentrations between samples. Samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and Western blotting using the antibodies indicated in Supplementary Data S21. For WB analysis with phospho-antibodies, RIPA buffer was additionally supplemented with PhosSTOP phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche), and lysates were sonicated with Bioruptor Plus (Diagenode) prior to clearing.

PIB-MS

Cells (n = 4 technical replicates) were harvested by trypsinization, pelleted, snap frozen, and processed as previously described34. Proteins were enriched from eluates using the SP3 method71 and digested overnight in 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate with trypsin for mass spectrometric analysis. Digests were analyzed using either a Q-Exactive Plus quadrupole Orbitrap mass spectrometer (ThermoScientific) equipped with an Easy-nLC 1000 (ThermoScientific) or an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (ThermoScientific) equipped with an Easy-nLC 1200 (ThermoScientific) and a nanospray source (ThermoScientific). COMET (release version 2014.01) in high-resolution mode was used to search raw data72 against a target-decoy (reversed)73 version of the human proteome sequence database (UniProt; downloaded 8/2020), mouse proteome sequence database (UniProt; downloaded 8/2020), or Saccharomyces cerevisiae proteome sequence database (UniProt; downloaded 8/2020) with a precursor mass tolerance of ±1Da and a fragment ion mass tolerance of 0.02 D requiring fully tryptic peptides (K, R; not preceding P) and up to three mis-cleavages. Static modifications included carbamidomethylcysteine, and variable modifications included oxidized methionine. Searches were filtered to a <1% FDR at the peptide level. Quantification of LC-MS/MS spectra was performed using MassChroQ74 and the iBAQ method75. Missing PPP subunit abundances were imputed and normalized across all samples by quantile normalization in Perseus76. Statistical analysis was carried out by a two-tailed Student’s t-test, and heatmaps were generated in Perseus.

Proteome analysis of RPE-1 wild-type and CYB5R4 KO cells

Cells (n = 4 technical replicates) were harvested by trypsinization, pelleted, snap frozen, and processed as previously described34. Proteins were reduced and alkylated using 5 mM DTT and 15 mM iodoacetamide, respectively. Samples were incubated overnight at 37 ˚C with 1:100 (w/w) trypsin. The next day, the trypsin digest was stopped by the addition of 0.25% TFA (final v/v). Precipitated lipids were removed by centrifugation (3500× for 15 min), and the peptides in the supernatant were desalted over an Oasis HLB plate (Waters). Peptides were labeled with Tandem-Mass-Tag (TMT) reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Once labeling efficiency was confirmed to be at least 95%, each reaction was quenched by the addition of hydroxylamine to a final concentration of 0.25% for 10 min, mixed, acidified with TFA to a pH of about 2, and desalted over an Oasis HLB plate (Waters).

The desalted multiplex was dried by vacuum centrifugation and separated by offline Pentafluorophenyl (PFP)-based reversed-phase HPLC fractionation as previously described77. TMT-labeled peptides were analyzed on an Orbitrap Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) equipped with an Easy-nLC 1200 (Thermo Scientific) and a nanospray source (Thermo Scientific). Raw data were searched and processed as previously described78. Peptide intensities were adjusted based on total TMT reporter ion intensity in each channel, and log2 transformed. p-values were calculated using a two-tailed Student’s t-test.

Phosphoproteome analysis of U2OS parental and CYB5R4 KO cells

U2OS parental and CYB5R4 KO cells were treated with 1 µM cisplatin for 24 h before harvest and snap freezing. Cell pellets were resuspended in ice-cold lysis buffer (8 M urea, 50 mM HEPES, pH 8.5, 150 mM NaCl, containing phosphatase inhibitors and protease inhibitors (Roche Life Sciences)) and were lysed by sonication. Lysates were subjected to centrifugation (21,000×g for 30 min at 4˚C), and supernatants were transferred to a new tube. The protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Pierce/ThermoFisher Scientific). DTT and iodoacetamide were added to reduce and alkylate, respectively. Proteins were precipitated with methanol and chloroform and resuspended in 8 M urea, 50 mM HEPES, pH 8.5. Urea was diluted by 6× with 50 mM HEPES pH 8.5 prior to an overnight incubation at 37 ˚C with 1:100 (w/w) trypsin. The next day, the trypsin digest was stopped by the addition of 0.25% TFA (final v/v). Peptides were desalted over an Oasis HLB plate (Waters). A peptide aliquot was removed for proteomic analysis. Phosphopeptide enrichment was achieved using an Fe-NTA phosphopeptide enrichment kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to instructions provided by the manufacturer and desalted over an Oasis HLB 10 mg plate (Waters).

Peptides and phosphopeptides were labeled with proTMT reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific). Once labeling efficiency was confirmed to be at least 95%, each reaction was quenched by the addition of hydroxylamine to a final concentration of 0.25% for 10 min, mixed, acidified with TFA to a pH of about 2, and desalted over an Oasis HLB plate (Waters).

The desalted multiplex was dried by vacuum centrifugation and separated by offline PFP-based reversed-phase HPLC fractionation as previously described in ref. 77. TMT-labeled peptides were analyzed on an Orbitrap Lumos mass spectrometer (ThermoScientific) equipped with a Vanquish Neo liquid chromatography system (ThermoScientific).

In brief, samples were analyzed on the Orbitrap Lumos operating in data-dependent, SPS-MS3 quantification mode wherein an Orbitrap MS1 scan was taken (scan range = 350–1250 m/z, R = 120 K, AGC target = 2.5e5, max ion injection time = 50 ms), followed by data-dependent ion trap (proteomics) or Orbitrap (phosphoproteomics) MS2 scans on the most abundant precursors for 1.8 s. Ion trap MS2 parameters: ion selection charge state = 2: minimum intensity 2e5 and precursor selection range 600-1400, ion selection charge state = 3–5: minimum intensity 3e5; quadrupole isolation = 0.8 m/z, CID collision energy = 35%, CID activation time = 10 ms, activation Q = 0.25, scan range mode = auto, AGC target = 4e3, max ion injection time = 40 ms. Orbitrap MS2 parameters: ion selection charge state = 2: minimum intensity 2e5, precursor selection range 600–1250 m/z, ion selection charge state = 3: minimum intensity 3e5 and precursor selection range 525–1250, ion selection charge state 4-5: minimum intensity 5e5, quadrupole isolation = 0.9 m/z, CID collision energy = 35%, CID activation time = 10 ms, activation Q = 0.25, resolution = 30k, scan range mode = auto, AGC target = 2.5e4, max ion injection time = 50 ms. Orbitrap MS3 scans for quantification: resolutio = 50k, AGC target = 5e4, max ion injection time = 100 ms, HCD collision energy = 45%, scan range = 100–500 m/z, synchronous precursors selected = 679.

The raw data files were searched using COMET with a static mass of 229.162932 Da on peptide N-termini and lysines and 57.02146 Da on cysteines and a variable mass of 15.99491 Da on methionines and 79.96633 Da on serines, threonines and tyrosine against the target-decoy version of the human proteome sequence database (UniProt; downloaded 2/2013, 40,482 entries of forward and reverse protein sequences), maximum of three missed cleavages allowed, precursor ion mass tolerance 1 Da, fragment ion mass tolerance ±8 ppm, and filtered to a <1% FDR at the peptide level. Quantification of LC-MS/MS spectra was performed using in-house developed software. Peptide intensities were adjusted based on total TMT reporter ion intensity in each channel and log2 transformed. p-values were calculated using a two-tailed Student’s t-test, assuming unequal variance.

Activity assays with phosphatases immunopurified from cells

Immunoprecipitation of PP2A C, PP4 C, and PP6 C was performed as described above for IP-MS, but differing at lysis and washing steps. Specifically for lysis, samples were left on ice for 20 min with vortexing every 5 min and washes were performed 2 times with low salt lysis buffer (50 mM NaCl2, 50 mM Tris.HCl, pH 7.4) supplemented with 0.1% NP-40 and one time in activity assay buffer (50 mM NaCl2, 150 mM Tris.HCl, pH 7.4). Subsequently, dry beads were resuspended in activity assay buffer and divided across conditions. For ascorbate stimulation experiments, buffer was supplemented to a final concentration of 5 mM L-ascorbate (Sigma), whereupon resuspended beads were left on ice for 15 min. Phosphopeptide WRRA(pT)VA (Peptide2.0) dissolved at 1 mM in 100 μM Tris.HCl pH 8 was added to a final concentration of 230 μM, and dephosphorylation reactions were incubated at 37 ˚C. Reactions were stopped at indicated timepoints by adding malachite green (PicolorLock, Abcam), and activity was determined by means of absorbance measurements at 620 or 630 nm in a multi-well plate reader (Fluostar Omega, BMG Labtech or SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices). Input levels of immunopurified phosphatases were determined by Western blot. Activities were corrected for background using absorbance measured in 3xFLAG control conditions. Experiments were performed in triplicate. Data were analyzed in PRISM 10 (Graphpad).

Colony formation assays

The cells were trypsinized, resuspended in medium, and counted. 200 cells were seeded per well in 6-well plates in a minimum of two wells per condition, with the doxycycline concentration indicated in Supplementary Data S18. Twenty-four hour after seeding, cells were treated with the indicated compounds (2 nM OA or 1 μM cisplatin) or left untreated. After 7 additional days of growth, formed colonies were fixed and stained in a methyl violet solution (0.5% methylviolet, 25% methanol), and the number of colonies was quantified on a GelCount (Oxford Optronix). The survival after treatment with a given compound is calculated as the number of colonies after treatment normalized to the number of colonies of the untreated condition (in %). All experiments were performed as biologically independent triplicates and analyzed in PRISM10 (Graphpad). One-way ANOVA analysis with Tukey’s multiple comparisons tests were performed to test for statistical significance.

Protein purification

CYB5R4 cloning, expression, and purification

CYB5R41–153 was cloned into pCPR0063 using LIC cloning, allowing expression of a His-GST-TEV fusion protein. The protein was expressed overnight in BL21(DE3) at 18 °C and harvested by centrifugation. Cell pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM NaP, pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, 0.5 mM EDTA) supplemented with Benzonase and protease inhibitor cocktail. The cells were lysed by sonication and cleared by centrifugation, and the supernatant was applied to a 5 ml HiTrap Nickel column. After washing with running buffer (50 mM NaP, pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, 0.5 mM TCEP), the protein was eluted with a 10-500 mM imidazole gradient (100 CVs). The protein was dialyzed and cleaved with TEV and applied to a 5 ml HiTrap Nickel column to remove His-GST. The flowthrough was concentrated and applied to a Superdex 75 16/60 column equilibrated with 50 mM NaP, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 0.5 mM TCEP, and peak fractions were pooled and stored at −80 °C.

PP4 and PP6 holoenzyme cloning, expression, and purification

The PP4 and PP6 holoenzyme expression constructs were generated following the ACEMBL Multimam guidelines. Briefly, PPP4R3A and PPP6R3 were cloned into pACEMam1, and the PPP4R2/FLAG-PPP4C and ANKRD28/FLAG-PPP6C into the donor vectors pMDC and pMDK. A 2-step recombineering protocol was followed to obtain the final pACEMam1 constructs with all 3 cDNAs. Suspension HEK293 cells were grown in FreeStyle F17 complete medium and transfected at a cell density of 1x10E6. Cells were transfected using OPTIMEM and PEI using 1 μg DNA per 1 ml of culture and following standard transfection procedures. Cells were harvested after 48 h and processed for purification of complexes. Pellets were lysed in lysis buffer (75 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10% glycerol, 2 mM MgCl2, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% w/v Tween 20, 0.008% w/v NP-40) supplemented with complete protease inhibitor cocktails (Roche) and 8 U/mL Benzonase nuclease (Millipore) using pressure homogenizer EmulsiFlex-C3 (Avestin). The lysates were cleared at 21,000×g at 4 °C for 1 h and again for another 30 min. Pre-equilibrated anti-FLAG M2 affinity gel (Sigma Aldrich) was added to the supernatant, followed by incubation for 1 h at 4 °C with rotation. The beads were spun down and washed first in FLAG buffer (75 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10% glycerol, 2 mM MgCl2, 150 mM NaCl), then in FLAG buffer supplemented with 5 mM ATP, Mg, and then again in FLAG buffer. The protein was eluted in FLAG buffer supplemented with 0.4 mg/mL 3xFLAG peptide (Sigma Aldrich). Protein complexes were analyzed by analytical size exclusion chromatography and mass spectrometry.

Mass spectrometry sample preparation, data acquisition, and analysis

Twenty-five micrgram (in ~20 µl) of the PP4 and PP6 complexes were diluted to 100 µl of room-temperature (25 °C) 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (09830, Sigma). The samples were reduced with 5 mM DTT (43815, Sigma) by incubation for 20 min at 56 °C and alkylated with 10 mM iodoacetamide (I1149, Sigma) by incubation for 15 min in the dark at room temperature. Subsequently, 0.9 µg of sequencing-grade trypsin (V5111, Promega) was added, and the samples were incubated for 3 h at 37 °C with gentle mixing (Thermomixer, Eppendorf). The digests were then acidified with formic acid (1% final concentration), incubated at room temperature for 5 min and centrifuged at 14,000×g for 10 min to remove particulate material. Supernatants were stored at −20 °C until LC-MS/MS analysis.

The protein digests (80 ng in 20 µl) were loaded onto Evotip Pure disposable trap columns (Evosep) following the standard protocol. The Evotips were placed on an Evosep One (Evosep), which eluted the peptides into the LC-MS/MS system. The 60 SPD (samples per day) method (21 min per sample) was used, and peptides were separated on an 8 cm × 100 μm ReproSil-Pur reversed-phase column (EV1064 – endurance column, Evosep) coupled to a NanoSpray Flex NG ion source (Thermo Scientific). The spray voltage was set at 2 kV in positive ion mode, the ion transfer tube temperature to 275 °C, and the acquisition mode to peptide. Data-dependent acquisition (DDA) runs were performed on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 (Thermo Scientific) mass spectrometer. The full scan range was set to 350–1600 m/z, MS1 Orbitrap resolution to 120,000, normalized MS1 AGC target to 300, and MS1 maximum injection time to “Custom”. Precursors with charge states 2–6 were selected for fragmentation using an isolation width of 1.3 m/z and fragmented by higher-energy collision dissociation (HCD) with a normalized collision energy of 30%. Monoisotopic Precursor Selection (MIPS) was enabled in “Peptide” mode. Precursors were prevented from being repeatedly sequenced by setting the dynamic exclusion duration to 30 s, with an exclusion mass tolerance of 10 ppm and exclusion of isotopes. MS/MS resolution was set to 30,000, normalized MS/MS AGC target to 200, MS/MS intensity threshold to 200,000 charges/second, MS/MS maximum injection time to “Custom”, and the number of dependent scans to 12.

Activity assays with recombinant proteins

Activity assays were performed in activity assay buffer (150 mM NaCl, 75 mM Tris.HCl pH 7.4) by mixing purified PP4 and PP6 holoenzymes (in FLAG buffer, 150 mM NaCl, 75 mM Tris.HCl pH 7.4, 2 mM MnCl2, 10% glycerol; final concentration of approx. 421 and 257 nM for PP4 and PP6, respectively) with reaction components (below), transferring the mix to 96-well plates, and adding 6,8-Difluoro-4-Methylumbelliferyl Phosphate (DiFMUP, Invitrogen; final concentration of 100 μM). Baseline activity was determined immediately after adding DiFMUP by measuring fluorescence at 350 nm excitation/450 nm emission in a microplate reader (Fluostar Omega, BMG Labtech), followed by incubation at 30 °C with fluorescence sampling at 355 nm excitation/460 nm emission performed at indicated times. For each timepoint the baseline activity was subtracted. For CYB5R4 reactivation assays, CYB5R41–153 WT, H89A/H112A (final concentration approx. 100 μg/μl, 6.2 µM), or FLAG buffer control was pre-reduced with 1 mM L-ascorbate for 15 min at room temperature before addition to the reaction. For phosphatase activation with reducing reagents, phosphatases were incubated with 1 mM DTT, TCEP, and L-ascorbate for 15 min at room temperature before DiFMUP addition. For EDTA treatments, PP6 holoenzyme purified in FLAG buffer without MnCl2 was incubated with 15 μM EDTA at 30 °C overnight in activity buffer supplemented with 1 mM DTT to prevent cysteine oxidation. This was followed by the addition of metal ion (MnCl2) to a final concentration of 50 μM in activity buffer and incubation for 10 min at room temperature. Finally, pre-reduced CYB5R41–153 WT or FLAG buffer control was added before DiFMUP addition.

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC)

Peptides were purchased from Peptide 2.0 Inc (Chantilly, VA, USA). The purity obtained in the synthesis was 95–98 % as determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and subsequent analysis by mass spectrometry. Prior to ITC experiments, both the protein and the peptides were extensively dialyzed against 50 mM sodium phosphate, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM TCEP, pH 7.5. All ITC experiments were performed on an Auto-iTC200 instrument (Microcal, Malvern Instruments Ltd.) at 25 °C. Both peptide and PPP4R3α2–117 concentrations were determined using a spectrophotometer by measuring the absorbance at 280 nm and applying values for the extinction coefficients computed from the corresponding sequences by the ProtParam program (http://web.expasy.org/protparam/). The CYB5R44–17 peptides at approximately 1250 μM concentration were loaded into the syringe and titrated into the calorimetric cell containing PPP4R3α2–117 at ~ 25 μM. The reference cell was filled with distilled water. In all assays, the titration sequence consisted of a single 0.4 μl injection followed by 19 injections, 2 μl each, with 150 s spacing between injections to ensure that the thermal power returns to the baseline before the next injection. The stirring speed was 750 rpm. Control experiments with the peptides injected in the sample cell filled with buffer were carried out under the same experimental conditions. These control experiments showed heats of dilution to be negligible in all cases. The heats per injection normalized per mole of injectant vs the molar ratio [CYB5R4 peptide]/[PPP4R3α2–117] were fitted to a single-site model with the n-value (stoichiometry) fixed to 1.0. Data were analyzed with MicroCal PEAQ-ITC (version 1.1.0.1262) analysis software (Malvern Instruments Ltd.). The ITC experiments for each CYB5R44–17 peptide were performed three times.

Animal experiments

Animal husbandry

Global Ncb5or-null (KO) mouse was generated through genetic ablation of exon 431 and backcrossed into C57BL/6 J for >12 generations prior to this study. The KO and wild-type (WT) embryos (littermates, sex not taken into consideration) were produced from heterozygous crossing in a pathogen-free facility at 24 °C and 33–56% humidity under a 12-h light cycle with unlimited access to water and a standard rodent chow. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the University of Kansas Medical Center Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Harvest and culture of mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs)

MEFs (E14.5) were collected from 14-day post-coitum pregnant dams (https://app.jove.com/v/3854/preparation-mouse-embryonic-fibroblast-cells-suitable-for-culturing). Immediately after each dam was anesthetized and cervical dislocated, the uterine horns were removed and placed in a clean 10-cm petri dish and washed three times with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, no Ca2+/Mg2+). Embryos were released into sterile PBS and processed individually under sterile conditions. Upon the removal of tail for genotyping and visceral (red or dark) tissues for disposal, each embryo was washed with 10 ml PBS three times, minced thoroughly with a curved iris scissor in a total of 7 ml Trypsin/EDTA digestion solution, and incubated at 37 °C for ~20 min with repeated pipetting until few chunks remained. After an additional 10-min incubation, each digestion mixture was neutralized with ~20 ml culture medium (see below for composition) and transferred to a 50 ml conical tube for thorough mixing. The content was then evenly added to a T25 culture flask containing 5 ml prewarmed culture medium, placed in a 37 °C incubator overnight, and changed to fresh medium the next day to remove cell debris. When each flask became 80-90% confluent, cells were transferred to new T75 flasks for further expansion (1:5 to 1:3 for each passage). After passage 1 or 2, cell stocks were prepared from each MEF line in cryopreservation medium (each vial equivalent to one T25 flask) for long-term storage in liquid nitrogen or expedited shipment on dry ice for additional analyses. Culture medium: DMEM with 4.5 g/L glucose, L-glutamine, sodium pyruvate, plus 10% FBS (heat-inactivated), 1% non-essential amino acids, and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin. Cryopreservation medium: 90% FBS, 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO).

Genotyping

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed on the genomic DNA of all embryos or adult breeders. Tissues were digested at 55 °C overnight in lysis buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.8, 5 mM EDTA, 0.2% SDS, 200 mM NaCl, 0.1 mg/ml proteinase K added immediately before use). After a 5-min high-speed centrifugation, the clear supernatant was diluted 10x with sterile water and directly subjected to genotyping PCR as previously described in ref. 31. Primer sequences and PCR conditions available upon request.

CRISPR base editing tiling screen

Library design and cloning

A set of gRNAs was designed to tile missense mutations across a selected set of 19 proteins (listed in Fig. 2B). The original design included both gRNAs predicted to give rise to mutations with adenine base editing (ABE) and cytosine base editing (CBE). However, the screens were exclusively conducted using the well-performing ABE SpG Cas9 ABE8e41, and as a consequence, only gRNAs predicted to give rise to a mutation with ABE were analyzed. To predict the mutations resulting from ABE edits, we assumed full editing efficiency within the “editing window” of nucleotides 4–8 of the gRNA-targeted DNA sequence. Only gRNAs predicted to introduce amino acid substitutions were retained for further analysis. Positive (essential splice sites) and negative (non-targeting and intergenic) control gRNAs described in ref. 41 were also included to benchmark the screen. The gRNA library was synthesized (GenScript) as an oligonucleotide pool that followed a previously published design for amplification and Esp3I cloning41: 5’-[Forward primer (20 nt)]CGTCTCACACCG[gRNA (20 nt)]GTTTCGAGACG[Reverse Primer (20 nt)]. The gRNA oligonucleotide pool was amplified using NEBNext Ultra II Q5 Master Mix (New England BioLabs) and the primers: Forward: GTGTAACCCGTAGGGCACCT; Reverse: GTCGAGAGCAGTCCTTCGAC, and then cloned into the Abe8e-Cas9-SpG lentiviral vector pRDA_47941 using Golden gate cloning with Esp3I and T7 ligase. pRDA_479 was a gift from John Doench & David Root (Addgene plasmid # 179099; http://n2t.net/addgene:179099; RRID:Addgene_179099). The plasmid library was purified first by PCR purification (NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean‑up, Macherey-Nagel) and then by isopropanol precipitation. Purified plasmid library was electroporated into Endura Electrocompetent cells (Lucigen), which were grown at 30 °C for 16 h on agar with 100 μg/ml carbenicillin. The colonies were scraped of the plates and plasmid DNA prepared (NucleoBond Xtra Maxi, Macherey-Nagel). The library was sequenced to confirm gRNA representation. This was done by PCR amplification from the library using NEBNext Ultra II Q5 Master mix and primers from Supplementary Data S19, gel purification of amplicons, and finally sequencing on a NextSeq2000 (Illumina).

Virus production

Lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfection of the gRNA plasmid library with lentiviral packaging plasmids pMD2.G and psPAX2 in HEK293T/17 cells (ATCC, CRL-11268) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) in Opti-MEM (Gibco). pMD2.G (Addgene plasmid # 12259; http://n2t.net/addgene:12259; RRID:Addgene_12259) and psPAX2 (Addgene plasmid # 12260; http://n2t.net/addgene:12260; RRID:Addgene_12260) were gifts from Didier Trono. Six hours after transfection, the medium was exchanged for DMEM GlutaMax + 10% FBS + 100 U/ml penicillin–streptomycin + 1% bovine serum albumin. Forty-eight hours after transfection, viral particles were harvested and filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter before freezing at −80 °C.

Transduction and cell culture

RPE-1;hTERT;TP53−/− cells (a kind gift from D. Durocher) were cultured in DMEM GlutaMax supplemented with 10% FBS and 100 U/ml penicillin–streptomycin and passaged every three days. The screen was performed as a duplicate (two separate transductions) at a coverage of above 500-fold gRNA representation, which was maintained throughout the screen. Cells were transduced with the lentiviral library at a low multiplicity of infection (0.3–0.4) by treating cells with 8 μg/ml polybrene and lentiviral supernatants for 24 h. Transduced cells were selected by treatment with 20 μg/ml puromycin for 24 h, followed by trypsinization and reseeding in the same plates with 20 μg/ml puromycin for another 24 h. After selection, cells were passaged for 6 days before splitting into untreated, OA, cisplatin, or Illudin S-treated fractions. Cells were passaged for an additional 12 days with passaging every 3 days in medium with low doses of OA (1.7 nM), cisplatin (1 μM), and Illudin S (1.4 ng/ml), which corresponds to predetermined LD20 concentrations in uninfected cells. Cell pellets were harvested after completion of selection, which we consider the start of the screen (day 0), and at the final timepoint (day 18).

NGS and data analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from treated and untreated cells at day 0 and day 18, and the genomic DNA regions containing the integrated gRNAs were amplified by PCR using NEBNext Ultra II Q5 Master mix with the LCV2_forward and LCV2_reverse primers (Supplementary Data S19). A second PCR reaction introduced i5 and i7 multiplexing barcodes (Supplementary Data S19), and gel-purified PCR products were sequenced on Illumina NextSeq2000. Fastq files were generated using bcl2fastq v2.20.1, reads were trimmed to 20 bp using cutadapt 3.767, and trimmed reads were assigned to the gRNA library by MAGeCK 0.5.9.568 to create a count matrix. To quantify relative gRNA abundance, first, low-abundance gRNAs (counts < 30) were excluded from downstream analysis to reduce noise and improve data reliability. Raw sequencing counts were then normalized to log2 transcripts per million (log2TPM) within each replicate to account for differences in sequencing depth. The log2TPM values were processed using the Linear Models for Microarray and Omics Data (limma) library80 to define log2 fold changes in gRNA abundance and subsequently converted to z-scores by comparing target gRNAs against negative control (intergenic and non-targeting) gRNAs. Four key comparisons were performed: day 0 vs day 18, to assess temporal changes in gRNA abundance over the course of the experiment; and untreated day 18 vs treatment (OA, cisplatin, or Illudin S) at day 18, to evaluate the behavior of gRNAs in the three treatment conditions. A z-score cut-off of <−5 was set to identify gRNAs causing proliferation defects and sensitization to OA, cisplatin, or Illudin S. Log2 fold changes of gRNAs can be consulted through an interactive web portal at https://slim.icr.ac.uk/projects/base_editing_tiling.

Protein stability predictions

Precalculated protein-stability variant effect predictions were obtained from RaSP45, which were generated using structural models from the AlphaFoldDB UP000005640_9606_HUMAN_v4 human proteome81. For each gRNA, the corresponding pΔΔG values were extracted by mapping predicted amino-acid substitution to its position on the UniProt reference sequence82 and are reported in Supplementary Data S12.

Visualization of base editing data

Linear residue maps were generated in Python (3.12.2) using pandas83, numpy 2.1.384, and matplotlib 3.9.285. Each plot shows the mean log2 fold change of gRNAs targeting a given residue. Domain and feature level annotations were gathered from UniProt and InterPro and mapped to the protein sequence.

Clustering of gene essentiality across treatments was performed in Python (3.12.2) using pandas83, numpy 2.1.384, and matplotlib 3.9.285. In summary, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated for a pair of treatments for each gene, using log2 fold changes (treatment/untreated) of individual gRNAs at day 18. Treatment comparisons were OA vs cisplatin, OA vs illudin S, and cisplatin vs illudin S. Only gRNAs significant in at least one treatment condition (non-adjusted p-value < 0.05) were included. Using these, correlation matrices for all genes across treatments were calculated and used for hierarchical clustering of the genes using the Ward method86 and Euclidean distance metrics.

The S. cerevisiae strains used in this study were haploid and congenic to the S288C strain (BY4741). A list of yeast plasmids and yeast strains used in this study is provided in Supplementary Data S20 and Supplementary Data S22. Deletion of genes (irc21 Δ, rrd1 Δ, and sit4 Δ) and the C-terminal tagging of Sap185 were carried out by transformation of PCR-amplified cassettes as described previously90 (list of primers in Supplementary Data S19). N-terminal tagging of Sit4 and Pph3 was achieved by the genomic integration of a URA3 plasmid containing the native promoter and an N-terminally tagged version of the gene (plasmids have been constructed either by fusion PCR or ordered at GENESCRIPT and subcloned using restriction enzyme cloning; plasmid maps and plasmids are available upon request). Insertion of the construct at the respective endogenous locus was promoted by cutting the ORF in the plasmid with restriction enzymes. After insertion, selection on 5-Fluorooretic acid (FOA) for ura3Δ cells forced the URA3-containing plasmid DNA removal from the genome. Candidate cells containing solely the tagged gene version were identified by PCR and western blot, and proper locus reconstitution was verified by DNA sequencing. This approach resulted in single N-terminally tagged genes expressed under the control of their native promoters at their endogenous loci. In the case of HA-PPH21 (YJV 905), an integrative LEU2 plasmid was used instead, as an already available strain harboring the URA3 marker at the locus of PPH21 was available. In this case, selection on FOA led to the loss of both auxotrophic markers and a reconstitution of the locus. Before harvest, all strains were grown into the exponential phase on YPD medium (1% w/v yeast extract, 2% w/v bacto-peptone and 2% w/v glucose). For in vivo complementation, wildtype and mutant alleles of myc-Irc21 (purchased from GENESCRIPT) and myc-CYB5R4 (see above) were cloned into a centromeric HIS3 plasmid, both under the control of the native yeast promoter (plasmids are described in Supplementary Data S20) and transformed into a SAP185-HA irc21Δ strain (YJV 1241). Before harvest, cells were grown into the exponential phase (2 × 107 cells/ml) on glucose-containing synthetic dropout medium (SD, -HIS) for selection of plasmid markers.

Preparation of yeast pellets for PIB MS

One liter of wildtype (wt) (BY4741) or irc21Δ (YJV 1206) cells was grown in YPD to 3 × 107/ml, harvested by filtration (0.4 µM pore filters), washed once with PBS, and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen. Frozen cell pellets were broken up using the SPEX 6770 cryogenic grinder (settings: 7 cycles; 2’ grinding at power level 14 and 3’ of cooling). The frozen cell powder contained about 40 mg of total protein and was kept at −80 °C until further analysis.

Immunoprecipitation and phosphatase activity assays with malachite green

25 microliter of yeast cultures expressing either HA-Pph21, myc-Pph3 or HA-Sit4 in the wildtype (YJV 905, YJV 1225, YJV 1275) or in the irc21 Δ background (YJV 1198, YJV1228, YJV1277), as well as the BY4741 control strain was grown in YPD to a density of 2 × 107cells/ml, harvested by centrifugation at 1000×g, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and taken up in 600 µl Lysis buffer (1% Triton, 50 mM MES pH 6,5, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 2 mM DTT, cOmpleteTM protease inhibitor cocktail Roche). Cells were lysed with 400 µl glass beads in a FastPrepTM-24 5 G (MP Biomedicals) bead beating homogenizer (1 cycle 45” PL 6,5). For immunoprecipitation 500 µl of the lysate was incubated for 1 h at 4 °C together with 20 µl of Protein-A Sepharose beads CL-4B (Cytiva #17-0780-01), crosslinked either to HA-tag antibody 16B12 (mouse, Biolegend #901515, for HA-Pph21 and HA-Sit4 IP) or myc-tag antibody 4A6 (for myc-Pph3 IP). Immunoprecipitates (IPs) were washed 1× with 1 ml Lysis buffer and 3x with 1 ml Wash buffer (50 mM MES pH 6,5, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 2 mM DTT); 1’ 400×g at 4 °C. After the last wash, IPs were incubated with 200 µl wash buffer containing 5 mM L-ascorbate on ice for 20’, centrifuged again, and taken up in 400 µl assay buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl) supplemented with 5 mM L-ascorbate. 40 µl of the beads/assay buffer suspension was mixed with 10 µl of 4 mM KRpTIRR phospho-peptide (dissolved in assay buffer) and incubated at 30 °C, shaking. The reaction was stopped at specific time points by the addition of 50 µl malachite green mastermix (20 µl of Malachite Green Phosphate Kit #MAK307 Sigma-Aldrich + 30 µl assay buffer) 80 µl of the final reaction (100 µl) was transferred into a 96-well plate, and the absorption of the malachite green molybdate phosphate complex was measured at 600 nm in a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader. The absorption measured for the immunoprecipitated phosphatases, the “Total IP activity” was background corrected based on the absorption measured for the immunoprecipitate from the control strain lysate. To calculate specific activities (activity/immunoprecipitated HA-tagged phosphatase) of immunoprecipitates, 25% of the immunoprecipitated HA-tagged phosphatases were boiled in 1× protein loading dye (Laemmli buffer), analyzed by western blot, and the levels of immunoprecipitated HA-tagged phosphatases were quantified as described below. Datasets from three independent experiments were normalized to the arithmetic sum of each individual experiment91, and means and standard deviations for each time point were calculated thereafter.

In vitro rescue with CYB5R4 wildtype and mutant

25 ml of yeast cultures expressing HA-Sit4 in the wt (YJV 1225) or irc21D background (YJV 1228), as well as the BY4741 control strain, were harvested by centrifugation at 1000×g, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, and taken up in 600 µl lysis buffer II (1% Triton, 50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, cOmpleteTM protease inhibitor cocktail Roche). Cells were lysed with 400 µl glass beads in a FastPrepTM-24 5 G (MP Biomedicals) bead beating homogenizer (1 cycle 45” PL 6,5). For immunoprecipitation 500 µl of lysate was incubated for 1 h at 4 °C together with 20 µl of Protein-A Sepharose beads CL-4B (Cytiva #17-0780-01) crosslinked to HA-tag antibody 16B12 (mouse, Biolegend #901515). Sepharose Beads were washed 1x with 1 ml lysis buffer and 3× with 1 ml assay buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl) by centrifugation for 1’ 400×g at 4 °C. After the last wash, beads were incubated with 200 µl assay buffer containing 5mM L-ascorbate on ice for 20’. In parallel, 0.13 ng of purified bacterially expressed mammalian CYB5R4 1–153 (wt or H89A/H112A mutant) was incubated for 15 ‘at RT in 60 µl of CYB5R4 incubation buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0,2% Triton X-100, 1mM L-ascorbate), giving a final concentration of ~125 pM CYB5R4 in the incubation buffer (respectively ~ 50 pM in the final assay). 20 µl of each IP suspension was mixed with 20 µl of the L-ascorbate-activated CYB5R4 (wildtype or mutant) or with mock buffer and incubated for 5’ at RT in 96-well plates. Phosphatase reaction was started by the addition of 10 µl of freshly prepared 2 mM DiFMUP (in 50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 5% DMSO; Invitrogen #D6567, Lot # 2729822), and enzyme kinetics were determined by measuring the emission at 445 nm (358 nm excitation) at 37 °C in a BioTek Synergy H1 plate reader. For each time point, the DiFMUP emission of the phosphatase reaction, was background corrected based on the emission measured for the immunoprecipitate from the control strain lysate. To calculate specific activities (activity/immunoprecipitated HA-tagged phosphatase) of immunoprecipitates, 25% of the immunoprecipitated HA-tagged phosphatases was boiled in 1× protein loading dye (Laemmli buffer), analyzed by western blot and the levels of immunoprecipitated HA-tagged phosphatases were quantified as described below. Datasets from three independent experiments were normalized to the arithmetic sum of each individual experiment91, and means and standard deviations for each time point were calculated thereafter.

Western blot analysis and quantification

Whole cell protein lysates or IPs were boiled for 5 min at 95 °C in protein sample buffer (Laemmli buffer). Samples were separated by SDS-PAGE (8% for SAP185-HA, 12% for Irc21, and 10% for all other proteins; Sigma Aldrich #01708, Tris/glycin buffered) and blotted on nitrocellulose membrane (GE Healthcare, 0.2 μm). Membranes were stained with PonceauS, and blocked with 3% non-fat dry milk (NFDM) in PBS-Tween-20 (0.05%) for 1 h at RT. The membranes were incubated with primary antibody (for antibodies & dilutions see Supplementary Data S21) in 0.5% NFDM/PBS-Tween-20 o/n at 4 °C. Incubation with secondary peroxidase-conjugated antibody (1:10,000 in 0.5% NFDM/PBS-Tween-20) was performed for 2 h at RT, followed by incubation with western blotting detection reagents (GE Healthcare or Bio-Rad) as suggested by the manufacturer. Signal acquisition was performed using the ChemiDocTM (Bio-Rad) system. When western blot quantification was required, serial dilutions of the sample with the strongest signal were loaded, and linear or logarithmic regression of the resulting signals was used to calculate relative ratios between samples.

Statistics and reproducibility

The statistical tests are mentioned in the figure legends. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Blinding and randomization were not performed. No data were excluded from analyses, except for the cisplatin phospho-MS data, where post hoc clustering analysis indicated switching of 2 replicates from different conditions. Uncropped blots are provided in the Source Data file.
