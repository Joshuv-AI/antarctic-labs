Tumor count and risk of lymph node metastasis in multiple rectal neuroendocrine tumors: an individual-level pooled analysis

Background

Rectal neuroendocrine tumors (R-NETs) are rare, well-differentiated neoplasms arising from neuroendocrine cells in the rectal mucosa [1]. They account for approximately 27% of all gastrointestinal neuroendocrine neoplasms, and their increasing incidence has been largely attributed to the widespread use of colonoscopic screening and improved diagnostic recognition [2, 3]. Most R-NETs present as small, solitary, asymptomatic lesions confined to the submucosa and are generally considered low risk, with local endoscopic resection regarded as the standard treatment approach [4, 5]. In contrast, multiple rectal neuroendocrine tumors (RM-NETs), defined as two or more discrete rectal lesions, represent a rare and underrecognized subtype, accounting for only 2%–5.7% of R-NETs [6]. These multifocal tumors are usually ≤ 10 mm and well differentiated, which may lead to underestimation of their metastatic potential when risk stratification is based on tumor size alone [7]. However, accumulating case reports and small case series suggest that RM-NETs may carry a higher risk of lymph node metastasis (LNM), challenging the conventional view that metastatic risk is determined primarily by tumor size [8, 9]. Despite these observations, current clinical guidelines do not provide specific recommendations for RM-NETs, and treatment strategies are generally extrapolated from solitary tumors [2, 10, 11]. This lack of tailored guidance may lead to overtreatment in some patients and undertreatment in others, particularly when reliable preoperative nodal assessment is unavailable.

To address these uncertainties, we performed an individual-level pooled analysis of published case reports and case series, supplemented by one institutional case. The aims of this study were to describe the clinicopathological characteristics of RM-NETs, examine patterns of lymph node involvement, and assess whether tumor count is associated with LNM risk. By assembling the largest individual-level dataset of RM-NETs to date, this study provides preliminary evidence to inform risk stratification and future research in this rare and understudied condition.

Methods

Literature search

This individual-level pooled analysis was conducted in accordance with the general principles of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement, with adaptations appropriate for pooled analyses of published case reports and case series. A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus from database inception to September 30, 2025. The search strategy combined terms related to neuroendocrine tumors, multiplicity, and rectal location, including “neuroendocrine tumor”, “carcinoid”, “multiple”, “multifocal”, “rectal”, and “rectum”. The detailed search strategy for each database is provided in Supplementary Table 1. No restrictions were applied with respect to language, publication year, geographic region, or study design. Reference lists of all eligible articles were also screened manually to identify additional relevant studies. Duplicate records were removed using EndNote and subsequently verified by manual review.

Study selection and data extraction

All retrieved records were screened independently by two reviewers in two stages, consisting of title and abstract screening followed by full-text assessment. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion. The overall study selection process is summarized in Fig. 1 in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 framework. Extracted data were recorded in a customized Microsoft Excel 2021 spreadsheet (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) and subsequently analyzed using SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data extraction was performed using a standardized form that captured study information, demographic characteristics (age and sex), presenting symptoms, reported tumor marker findings, reported diagnostic information, tumor count, maximum tumor diameter, depth of invasion, histologic grade, lymphovascular invasion, nodal status, treatment type, and follow-up data. Tumor marker findings were recorded descriptively as reported in the source articles. Because tumor marker panels were not uniform across studies, the reported markers included some or all of the following: CA-50, CA72-4, CA24-2, 5-HIAA, CEA, AFP, CA125, and CA19-9. If the source article stated that the assessed tumor markers were within the normal range, the finding was recorded as “Normal” in Supplementary Table 2. If one or more abnormal tumor markers were explicitly reported, the specific abnormal marker(s) were recorded. If tumor marker information was not documented, it was recorded as “ND”.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklists for case reports and case series [12–14]. The checklist for case reports includes 8 items, whereas that for case series includes 10 items. Two reviewers independently evaluated each study after pilot calibration, and disagreements were resolved by consensus [14]. Each item was rated as Yes, No, Unclear, or Not applicable. Based on the proportion of “Yes” responses among applicable items, studies were categorized as high quality (≥ 70%), moderate quality (50%–69%), or low quality (< 50%). The wording of individual items followed the original JBI tools.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized descriptively. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. For variables with missing data rates below 5%, missing values were imputed using the mode. Tumor count was analyzed as a continuous variable to explore its association with lymph node metastasis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess its discriminative ability, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. The optimal cutoff value was determined using the Youden index. Because tumor count is an integer variable, the statistically derived cutoff of 6.5 was rounded to 7 for subsequent exploratory stratification, and patients were categorized into < 7 and ≥ 7 tumor groups. Univariable comparisons were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Variables with a p value < 0.05 in univariable analysis were entered into an exploratory multivariable logistic regression model to examine factors associated with LNM. Collinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with a VIF < 5 considered acceptable. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Given the retrospective and descriptive nature of the pooled case-level data, all analyses were considered exploratory and intended to generate hypotheses for future validation.

Results

Study selection

A total of 1638 records were identified through database searching, including 436 from PubMed, 507 from Embase, 140 from Web of Science, and 555 from Scopus. After removal of duplicates, 990 unique records remained. Following title and abstract screening, 886 records were excluded because they were irrelevant or lacked sufficient information. The remaining 104 articles underwent full-text review. Of these, 68 were excluded because they did not involve RM-NETs, and 19 were excluded because of insufficient clinical information. Ultimately, 17 published reports comprising 45 patients were included in the pooled analysis, including 11 case reports and 6 case series. One additional unpublished case from our institution was included, yielding a final cohort of 46 patients. The study selection process is shown in Fig. 1. All 11 case reports were assessed as high quality. Among the 6 case series, 3 were rated as high quality and 3 as moderate quality. Detailed item-level assessments are presented in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4.

Patient characteristics

The overall clinical and pathological characteristics of the 46 patients are summarized in Table 2. Most patients were male (80.4%), and the median age was 57 years. Overall, 73.9% of patients were aged 50 years or older. In 91.3% of patients, the maximum tumor size was ≤ 10 mm, and submucosal invasion was present in 89.1%. Tumor grade was reported as G1 in 35 patients (76.1%) and G2 in 3 patients (6.5%), whereas grade was unavailable in 8 patients (17.4%). Local treatment, including endoscopic mucosal resection and transanal excision, was performed in 67.4% of patients, while 28.3% underwent radical surgery with lymph node dissection. Lymph node metastasis was identified in 8 patients (17.4%), and no distant metastases were reported. Follow-up data were available for 33 patients (71.7%), and no recurrence was documented during the reported follow-up period. Additional case-level details on reported symptoms and tumor marker findings are provided in Supplementary Table 2. Symptoms were reported variably across studies. Among cases with explicitly reported symptoms, there were 3 cases of positive fecal occult blood; 2 cases each of hematochezia, abdominal pain, abdominal discomfort, diarrhea, anal heaviness, abdominal distension, and changes in bowel habits; and 1 case each of constipation and recurrent alternating constipation and diarrhea. Tumor marker findings were also reported variably. Among cases with explicitly reported tumor marker abnormalities, there was 1 case each with elevated CEA (case 3), CA125 (case 31), CA19-9 (case 36), AFP (case 38), and CA72-4 (case 39). Diagnostic work-up was inconsistently reported across studies. All patients were initially identified by colonoscopy and subsequently confirmed histopathologically as having RM-NETs. When described, the reported diagnostic modalities included endoscopic ultrasonography, chest and abdominal CT, and MRI in selected cases, which were used to assess tumor extent and possible nodal or distant metastasis.

Tumor number and lymph node metastasis

Tumor count was analyzed as a continuous variable to explore its association with lymph node metastasis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.91 (95% CI 0.83–0.99) (Fig. 2). Based on the Youden index, the optimal statistical cutoff was 6.5 tumors. Because tumor count is an integer variable, this value was rounded to 7 for subsequent exploratory stratification, rather than as a clinically validated threshold. At this cutoff, the sensitivity and specificity for distinguishing patients with and without lymph node metastasis were 87.5% and 78.9%, respectively. Patients were subsequently stratified into two groups according to tumor count, namely < 7 and ≥ 7 tumors. Lymph node metastasis was identified in 7 of 15 patients (46.7%) with ≥ 7 tumors and in 1 of 31 patients (3.2%) with < 7 tumors, indicating a statistically significant difference (p = 0.001; Table 3). Given the small number of lymph node metastasis events in this cohort, these ROC-derived results should be interpreted as dataset-specific exploratory observations rather than evidence of reproducible predictive performance.

Exploratory risk factor analysis

In univariable analysis, tumor count ≥ 7 and the presence of clinical symptoms were associated with lymph node metastasis (p = 0.001 and p = 0.024, respectively), whereas age, sex, tumor size, histologic grade, and depth of invasion were not (Table 3). An exploratory multivariable logistic regression model was then fitted with tumor count and symptom status as covariates. In this small-event model, tumor count ≥ 7 remained associated with lymph node metastasis after adjustment for symptom status (odds ratio 13.15, 95% confidence interval 1.30–133.24; p = 0.029), whereas symptom status was not statistically significant (Table 4). Because only a limited number of lymph node metastasis events were available, the model may be vulnerable to overfitting, and the wide confidence interval suggests substantial imprecision. This result should therefore be interpreted as exploratory rather than as evidence of a stable predictive model.

Discussion

Clinical features and management of R-NETs

R-NETs typically present as solitary, well-differentiated lesions (NET G1 or G2) and are associated with a 5-year survival rate of approximately 75% [4]. Although these tumors typically exhibit indolent growth, the risk of LNM varies widely (3%–60%) and is influenced by several pathological features including tumor size, depth of invasion, histological grade, and lymphovascular invasion [31]. Among these, tumor size has been consistently recognized as the most important prognostic determinant. As outlined in the 2023 European Neuroendocrine Tumor Society (ENETS) guidelines for colorectal neuroendocrine neoplasms, endoscopic removal is recommended for rectal NETs measuring ≤ 10 mm because of their limited metastatic potential, whereas lesions larger than 20 mm, which carry a high risk of nodal involvement, generally require radical oncologic resection [10]. For tumors measuring 10–20 mm, preoperative staging with endoscopic ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is recommended. In this group, treatment decisions are guided by nodal status, with radical surgery indicated in the presence of metastases and local resection when nodes are uninvolved [10, 32].

Surgical options for R-NETs

Surgical treatment options for R-NETs include local excision techniques such as endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR), endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), and transanal endoscopic microsurgery, as well as radical procedures such as total mesorectal excision (TME), anterior resection, and abdominoperineal excision [33–35]. EMR and ESD are widely used owing to their technical feasibility and favorable safety profiles; however, they carry relatively high rates of positive vertical margins, reported in up to 50% of patients [4, 36]. To address this limitation, several modified EMR techniques, including cap-assisted EMR, band ligation-assisted EMR, and pre-cut EMR, have been developed to improve complete resection rates while maintaining procedural safety [37]. Nonetheless, these strategies are primarily suitable for solitary R-NETs and may not be appropriate for multifocal lesions. Current clinical decision-making for R-NETs primarily relies on tumor size and evidence of metastasis [10]. In the context of RM-NETs, the present findings suggest that tumor multiplicity may be considered an additional clinical feature during preoperative assessment. However, given the limited evidence available, tumor count should not be used to define a treatment algorithm or a definitive surgical threshold. Instead, extensive multifocality may warrant closer nodal assessment and multidisciplinary discussion, even when the maximum lesion size is ≤ 10 mm.

Distinct biology and metastatic potential of RM-NETs

RM-NETs, defined as the synchronous presence of two or more lesions in the rectum, may exhibit biological behaviors distinct from those of solitary tumors. As early as 1987, Kanter et al. proposed that tumor multiplicity could influence the likelihood of LNM [24]. Subsequently, Maeda et al. reported that even small, low-grade RM-NETs were capable of developing nodal metastases, suggesting that size-based risk stratification alone may underestimate the metastatic potential in this subgroup [7]. More recently, a single-center study observed an LNM rate of 14.3% among patients with RM-NETs, despite all lesions measuring < 10 mm in diameter [9].

These observations are consistent with our pooled analysis (Table 2), which showed an overall LNM rate of 17.4%. The rate increased to 46.7% among patients with ≥ 7 lesions, suggesting that extensive tumor multiplicity may reflect greater cumulative tumor burden and broader biological risk rather than simply a higher lesion count. In this context, multifocality may be viewed as a composite clinical feature that reflects tumor number, cumulative mucosal involvement, and possible field-related neuroendocrine proliferation [9, 38, 39]. This may partly explain why RM-NETs with extensive multiplicity could carry a higher risk of nodal involvement even when individual lesions are small.

Tumor number and lymph node metastasis in RM-NETs

In the present dataset, exploratory ROC analysis suggested that a tumor count ≥ 7 may represent a potential statistical threshold for distinguishing patients with and without LNM, with an AUC of 0.91. This observation is broadly consistent with previous clinical reports indicating that RM-NETs with greater tumor multiplicity may have a higher risk of nodal involvement, and it may help guide future validation in larger multicenter cohorts [6, 9, 25]. However, given the small sample size and limited number of metastatic events in this pooled cohort, this threshold should not be regarded as a clinically established cutoff. The relatively high AUC may also reflect dataset-specific separation rather than reproducible predictive performance.

In exploratory multivariable logistic regression, tumor count ≥ 7 remained associated with LNM after adjustment for symptom status (Table 4), further supporting a possible relationship between extensive tumor multiplicity and nodal involvement in RM-NETs. However, the wide confidence interval around the effect estimate indicates substantial imprecision, and the model may be vulnerable to overfitting because only eight LNM events were available. This finding should also be viewed in the context of potential residual confounding. Maximum tumor size remains a well-recognized determinant of nodal metastasis in rectal neuroendocrine tumors, and the lack of statistical significance for tumor size in this small cohort should not be taken as evidence that tumor size is unimportant [10, 40]. Patients with more numerous lesions may be more likely to harbor at least one larger or biologically aggressive tumor, which could partly account for the observed association between tumor count and LNM. In addition, multifocality may not necessarily represent an independent metastatic driver. Rather, it may serve as a surrogate marker of diffuse neuroendocrine field change, increased cumulative tumor burden, or more extensive mucosal neuroendocrine proliferation [9, 38, 39]. From this perspective, tumor count may be clinically relevant not simply as a numerical variable, but as one component of broader cumulative biological risk. Symptom status was significant in the univariable analysis (Table 3) but did not remain associated with LNM in the multivariable model, suggesting that symptoms may reflect overall tumor burden rather than directly indicate metastatic spread.

Clinical implications of tumor count in RM-NET management

From a therapeutic perspective, the present findings highlight the potential importance of tumor count in clinical decision-making. Current guidelines recommend local excision for small, well-differentiated solitary R-NETs; however, this approach may overlook the metastatic potential of RM-NETs with a high tumor burden [10, 41]. Although the present findings suggest an association between higher tumor count and increased risk of LNM, they do not justify immediate changes to existing clinical protocols. Current recommendations for rectal NETs remain based primarily on maximum tumor size, depth of invasion, grade, and radiologic or pathologic evidence of nodal disease. Within that framework, the findings of this study suggest that extensive multifocality may warrant closer nodal assessment and multidisciplinary discussion, even when the maximum lesion size is small. Among the 46 patients with RM-NETs included in this cohort (Supplementary Table 2), marked variability in therapeutic approaches was observed, reflecting the lack of uniform treatment consensus in clinical practice. Although most lesions were ≤ 10 mm and classified as G1 or G2, 13 patients (28.3%) underwent more extensive surgical procedures, including TME and laparoscopic low anterior resection. When treatment rationale was described in the source reports, decisions regarding more extensive procedures appeared to be influenced by tumor multiplicity, maximum lesion size, depth of invasion, suspected nodal disease, and individual clinical judgment; however, these indications were not documented consistently across studies. This pattern likely reflects clinicians’ concerns about the occult metastatic risk associated with multifocality and the reliance on individualized judgment in the absence of standardized risk assessment tools. This interpretation is further supported by the recent single-center retrospective study by Hiyoshi et al., in which all 14 patients with multiple rectal NETs were staged as cN0 before surgery, whereas lymph node metastasis was pathologically confirmed after radical resection in 9 patients [6]. Taken together, these observations suggest that tumor multiplicity may be a useful consideration in clinical judgment, particularly when determining whether apparently low-risk multifocal disease requires more careful staging rather than routine local management alone. When radical surgery is considered for RM-NETs, its potential oncologic benefit should be balanced against individualized perioperative risk. Patient-level factors, including physiological reserve, frailty, comorbidity burden, and functional status, may influence tolerance to radical surgery and postoperative recovery. These considerations are particularly relevant because current evidence is insufficient to define a uniform surgical threshold based on tumor count alone. Therefore, treatment decisions for patients with extensive multifocal disease should incorporate tumor-related risk factors together with an individualized assessment of operative vulnerability and patient fitness [42]. Nevertheless, the present findings should not be interpreted as sufficient to establish a treatment algorithm or a definitive surgical threshold.

Limitations of preoperative imaging and future directions

The limitations of preoperative imaging warrant careful consideration. Ushigome et al. reported that in R-NETs, metastatic lymph nodes are frequently too small to be reliably detected on preoperative computed tomography (CT), complicating the accurate prediction of LNM [43]. Similarly, a single-center retrospective study of 14 patients with RM-NETs found no lymphadenopathy on preoperative CT or MRI; however, postoperative pathology confirmed LNM in 64% of patients, highlighting the limited sensitivity of conventional imaging for detecting micrometastases [6]. In our pooled dataset, pathological LNM was also identified in patients with small, well-differentiated tumors despite negative imaging findings, further reinforcing the limited diagnostic accuracy of conventional radiological approaches. In this context, tumor multiplicity may serve as a complementary clinical indicator when radiological findings are inconclusive. More advanced diagnostic strategies are required to overcome the limitations of conventional imaging. For example, multiple studies have demonstrated that 68Ga-SSR-PET/CT provides superior sensitivity for detecting low-burden disease, particularly for the early identification of LNMs [44, 45]. Its role in the assessment of RM-NETs warrants systematic evaluation. Ingenerf et al. compared non-contrast PET/MRI with contrast-enhanced PET/CT and found that sequences incorporating T2-weighted and diffusion-weighted imaging yielded detection rates for hepatic and skeletal metastases comparable to, or exceeding, those of the contrast-enhanced modality [46]. These findings suggest that PET/MRI may serve as a safe and effective alternative for patients in whom intravenous contrast is contraindicated, such as those with renal impairment [46, 47]. Moreover, radiomics-based predictive models that incorporate imaging texture features along with clinical variables such as tumor size, multiplicity, and histological grade have demonstrated promising potential for the preoperative assessment of LNM [48, 49]. Artificial intelligence-assisted diagnostic algorithms may further enhance risk stratification and lesion detection in patients with R-NETs, with potential future applications in identifying micrometastatic disease and supporting more precise clinical decision-making [50, 51].

Possible molecular origins and biological heterogeneity of RM-NETs

The mechanisms underlying R-NET tumorigenesis remain poorly understood. Moyana et al. proposed that these tumors may originate from localized hyperplasia of crypt cells rather than diffuse proliferation of enteroendocrine cells [52]. Similarly, Sasou et al. observed morphological continuity between tumor cells and adjacent intestinal glands, further supporting a crypt-derived origin [16]. Additionally, scattered endocrine micronests, which are frequently observed in patients with RM-NETs, may represent preneoplastic changes. Their density, structure, and proliferative activity are thought to reflect the early stages of tumor development [21]. From a pathological perspective, these observations suggest that at least some RM-NETs may develop against a background of localized or field-related neuroendocrine proliferation, rather than simply reflecting the incidental coexistence of multiple small synchronous lesions.

Recent molecular studies further suggest that synchronous lesions in RM-NETs may develop through heterogeneous mechanisms rather than from a single common origin, with some lesions showing evidence of independent clonal evolution [53–57]. From this perspective, multifocality may reflect diffuse endocrine cell hyperplasia, field cancerization, clonal heterogeneity, possible genetic predisposition, or a combination of these processes, although direct evidence in rectal lesions remains limited. These considerations may help explain why tumor multiplicity could be linked to metastatic potential even when individual lesions are small. However, they do not establish tumor count itself as an independent driver of metastasis. A hereditary background or local field effect has also been proposed in selected RM-NET cases. For example, Doi et al. reported monozygotic twins with highly similar rectal tumor distributions, supporting a possible genetic contribution [19]. In addition, several studies have described familial aggregation and field-effect-related mechanisms [39, 58–61]. These observations remain preliminary and should be interpreted with caution. Overall, RM-NETs may represent a biologically distinct and heterogeneous phenotype rather than simply a numerical extension of solitary tumors. Further molecular and multicenter studies are needed to clarify their origin, clonal relationships, and possible relevance to metastatic behavior and future risk stratification. Limitations

This study has several limitations. RM-NETs represent a rare clinical entity, and high-quality evidence remains limited. No randomized controlled trials or large observational studies are currently available. Most data therefore come from published case reports and small case series, which are vulnerable to publication bias, selective reporting of unusual presentations, incomplete clinical documentation, and heterogeneity in diagnostic work-up, treatment selection, and follow-up strategy. The included reports also spanned several decades, during which endoscopic techniques, radiological staging, pathological classification, and surgical strategies evolved substantially. This temporal heterogeneity may have influenced the detection of multifocal lesions, the ascertainment of nodal disease, and the choice of treatment.

Lymph node status was not assessed using a uniform standard across all patients. Some patients did not undergo radical resection with lymph node dissection, which may have led to underdetection of nodal involvement in individual cases. Patients with more extensive multifocal disease may also have been more likely to undergo radical surgery, thereby increasing the opportunity for pathological confirmation of nodal metastasis and introducing potential verification bias. This asymmetry in nodal ascertainment should be regarded as a structural limitation of the available literature rather than an isolated methodological limitation of the present analysis.

Although tumor count ≥ 7 emerged as an exploratory threshold in the present dataset, this finding was based on a small cohort with a limited number of metastatic events and should not be regarded as a definitive clinical cutoff. The ROC-derived threshold may be unstable, and the relatively high AUC may reflect dataset-specific separation rather than reproducible predictive performance. The multivariable analysis was also based on only eight LNM events; accordingly, the wide confidence interval around the effect estimate suggests substantial imprecision and possible model instability. Residual confounding cannot be excluded, particularly because maximum tumor size remains an established determinant of nodal metastasis in rectal NETs. In addition, although mode imputation was applied only to variables with missing data rates below 5%, even limited imputation may influence categorical subgroup comparisons in a cohort of 46 patients.

Finally, follow-up data were incomplete and heterogeneous in duration, precluding a robust evaluation of long-term outcomes. No recurrence was documented among patients with available follow-up; however, the absence of reported recurrence should not be interpreted as evidence of oncologic safety. Given the indolent biology of many neuroendocrine tumors, the long-term oncologic trajectory of RM-NETs remains uncertain without systematic surveillance data.

Future directions

Future research should focus on validating the prognostic significance of tumor multiplicity through prospective multicenter cohort studies incorporating standardized imaging protocols, histopathological evaluations, and comprehensive long-term follow-up. Building on the variables examined in the present study, future research should evaluate tumor count within a broader framework of cumulative tumor burden and biological risk. This framework should integrate lesion number with maximum tumor size, depth of invasion, lymphovascular invasion when available, and radiological assessment of nodal disease. Such an approach may help clarify whether extensive multifocality contributes independently to metastatic risk or primarily reflects broader biological alterations, such as field-related neuroendocrine proliferation or increased cumulative mucosal involvement. Molecular profiling of synchronous RM-NET lesions may further clarify whether multiplicity arises from clonal expansion of a single progenitor cell, independent tumorigenic events, or a combination of both. These findings may contribute to the identification of biologically meaningful markers and help refine future risk stratification. In addition, integrating tumor burden with next-generation imaging features, including radiomics and PET/CT-derived parameters, may facilitate the development of composite models for preoperative assessment. The establishment of a dedicated RM-NET registry that captures detailed clinical, pathological, molecular, treatment-related, and longitudinal outcome data would further support these research efforts and aid in the development of evidence-based management strategies.

Conclusion

RM-NETs are a rare subtype of R-NETs and may have clinical and biological features distinct from those of solitary tumors. In this pooled analysis, greater tumor multiplicity was associated with a higher reported risk of LNM. The exploratory threshold of approximately seven lesions identified in this dataset should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than as a definitive clinical cutoff. For patients with extensive multifocal disease, more careful nodal staging and multidisciplinary discussion may be appropriate, even when the largest lesion is small. Further prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate the prognostic value of tumor count, clarify how clinical presentation and imaging findings should be incorporated into risk stratification, and support the development of standardized management strategies for RM-NETs.
