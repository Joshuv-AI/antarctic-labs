Designing a Visualization Atlas: Lessons & Reflections from The UK Co-Benefits Atlas for Climate Mitigation
\authororcid Jinrui Wang0009-0007-9313-5180, \authororcid Alexis Pister0000-0002-2817-020X, \authororcid Sian Phillips0009-0001-4006-5525, \authororcid Sarah Bissett, \authororcid Ruaidhri Higgins-Lavery0009-0001-0117-0541, \authororcid Clare Wharmby, \authororcid Andrew Sudmant0000-0001-8650-8419, \authororcid Uta Hinrichs0000-0001-7494-0941, \authororcid Benjamin Bach0000-0002-9201-7744

Abstract
This paper reports on the process of designing the UK Co-Benefits Atlas, which communicates and publicizes data for climate mitigation. Visualization atlases—an emerging type of platform to make data about complex topics comprehensive through interactive visualizations and explanatory content—pose challenges beyond traditional visualization projects. Atlases must address diverse and often uncertain audiences and use cases, support both explanatory and guided exploration, and accommodate complex, evolving data. Over 10 months, our team of visualization and domain experts conducted 8 design workshops, iterative prototyping, 15 stakeholder onboarding sessions, and continuous reflection. These intertwined processes informed the development of the Atlas, comprising over 400 pages of visualizations and explanations. They also enabled a deeper understanding of how stakeholders may critically engage with the atlas in practice, in terms of interests, potential frictions when navigating huge amounts of data, and envisioned usage scenarios. Reflecting on our design process, we identify five driving forces in atlas design—data, people, stories, context, and the atlas itself—whose shifting dynamics influence different stages of visualization atlas design in different ways. Grounded in our case study, we discuss using these forces as a conceptual starting point for structuring and reflecting on future atlas design processes.

Introduction

Visualization atlases are an emerging genre of open data platforms designed to make complex datasets about societal challenges available, transparent, and comprehensive
[
44
]
.
Prominent examples include the Atlas of Economic Complexity
[
39
]
, the Atlas of UN Sustainable Development Goals
[
30
]
, and the IPCC’s WGI Interactive Atlas of possible climate futures
[
13
]
. While all of these examples provide comprehensive multidimensional data from public sources and institutions, they also go beyond traditional open-data platforms that merely grant access to data files: these atlases leverage interactive visualizations and rich contextual explanations to curate cohesive collections of visualizations, dashboards, reports, and stories. They also typically offer onboarding, navigation, and methodological explanations to support their readers.

Providing multiple means of engaging with data is complex, and visualizations for both exploration and explanation introduce challenges that are not captured by existing visualization design methodologies (e.g.,
[
37
,
28
,
25
]
). Yet, the few limited written accounts on atlas design
[
8
,
41
,
27
]
provide some early, yet largely incomplete evidence of the challenges inherent to larger atlas projects. These challenges to atlas design include typically large and multidimensional datasets and that they require extensive resources for design, development, and communication. Visualization atlases are also designed for diverse audiences and different use cases: they generally aim to inform, educate, and support both in-depth analysis and decision making. Atlases need to be directed simultaneously towards domain experts, data-analysts, researchers, communicators, businesses, policy makers, as well as the general public. Each of these user groups have different objectives and knowledge about the topic in question, the visualizations provided, and the ways in which the data has been collected and analyzed, requiring careful explanations and onboarding. Existing visualization design guidelines and frameworks are generally directed towards specific forms of visualization (e.g., dashboards
[
2
]
, data-stories
[
18
]
, news articles
[
10
]
) with rather specific tasks, user groups, and design spaces.
Ultimately, atlases need to dynamically adapt to changes in data and topics they present
[
12
,
42
,
43
]
.

To better understand these design challenges and provide guidance on future visualization atlas projects, we present a case study on designing an atlas platform to communicate complex data in the context of climate mitigation. The atlas is built on a dataset consisting of modeled projections of future monetary gains (benefits) arising from climate interventions to reduce CO
2
emissions. For example, such interventions may lead to reduced healthcare costs due to improved air quality or lower energy bills through better home insulation, etc.
[
38
]
. These co-benefits data are intended to support policy makers in planning and advocating for climate mitigation projects and provide data to researchers doing refined climate mitigation and economic analyses.
It is the first dataset of its kind, covering 46,426 local communities, 11 co-benefits, 25 years of projected data, and 17 socio-economical factors (including education, income, household size, etc). Over a period of 10 months, our team of visualization designers and domain experts in policy and climate mitigation led the co-design and implementation of the resulting
The UK Co-Benefits Atlas
(
https://ukcobenefitsatlas.net
).

Based on experiences, findings and reflections from 8 co-design workshops, 15 sessions with external stakeholders, and prototype iterations, we provide the first comprehensive account of visualization atlas design. This includes: (1) a documented design process featuring interdisciplinary collaboration and design workshops (

); (2) a set of bespoke design solutions comprising multiple page templates and structural components (

We reflect on our design process through the metaphor of five distinct forces we identified, which influence each other and the design process:
data
,

,

,

We discuss how these five forces create a spatial, rather than linear approach to atlas design, implying no single starting or end point and instead suggesting a dynamic non-linear, and potentially never-ending design process. This forces metaphor also allows for linking existing design and evaluation methods into an atlas project, without prescribing one singular methodology. We conclude by outlining future avenues (

) to explore in the context of visualization atlas design.

2
Background

Our work builds on recent research on visualization atlases as a relatively new form of visualization. It is further grounded in visualization design methodology on the one hand and the specifics of our case study—climate co-benefits modeling—on the other, as outlined below.

2.1
Visualization Atlases

Wang et al.
[
44
]
define the concept of a visualization atlas as
“a compendium of (web) pages aimed at explaining and supporting the exploration of data about a dedicated topic through data, visualizations and narration”.
This definition highlights three main characteristics of visualization atlases: Firstly, their focus on a
topic of public interest
but of considerable complexity (e.g., economic inequality and development
[
39
]
, sustainability
[
30
,
13
]
or public health
[
8
]
), and their aim of providing data-driven views that support a broad understanding of this topic. Secondly, visualization atlases leverage
interactive visualization and narrative capabilities
to let users explore the data while providing sufficient explanatory power to guide and inform. They can incorporate all forms of visualization; interactive charts, dashboards
[
2
]
, data videos
[
1
]
, data-articles
[
10
]
, and more.
Unlike traditional exploratory or explanatory visualizations, atlases do not assume explicit audiences, goals, or tasks. Instead, they are designed to support users who are already familiar with the topic and seeking access to data, those wanting to learn about it, and those interested in scrutinizing or extending it.
Third and finally, atlases are highly
curated sets of bespoke pages
that consist of visualizations, explanations, and guidance, navigable through a clear structure. Curation is essential to increasing communication and accessibility, while also framing the dataset, its purpose, and potential use.
To that end, Wang et al. identified
design patterns
for specific design decisions such as entry page design, atlas structure, or visualization/interaction designs, and atlas
genres
as distinct approaches to presentation and engagement—for example, Storybanks provide sets of written stories and in-depth analyses; Observatories track and monitor multiple datasets; Exploratoria emphasize interactive exploration. While these patterns, genres, and examples demonstrate the sophistication and diversity of atlas designs, they give little actionable indication of the actual design process and how to navigate specific problems of data complexity and user engagement in practice. Our goal is to better understand the dynamics of atlas design processes and decision-making as part of this in order to scaffold the design of future atlases.

2.2
Approaches to Visualization Design

A rich body of work exists on visualization methodology, focused in particular, on scaffolding visualization design processes.
Some of the most prominent examples include the four key visualization design activities of understanding, ideating, making, and deploying
[
23
]
; the classic nine-step process of visualization design studies
[
37
]
; and the extension towards data-first design studies
[
28
]
. Across these models, task abstraction plays a central role, supported by frameworks such as the nested model
[
25
]
and the multi-level typology
[
4
]
. While these models are widely applicable, most of these frameworks are built on specific assumptions such as a well-defined problem and data, a set of constraint stakeholders that can be analyzed, as well as a clear linear or iterative
progression
towards a visualization outcome. Moreover, these models assume that this process is
deterministic
with a measure for when to stop.

However, a growing body of work suggests more reflective and non-linear methodological perspectives. These considerations include design-by-immersion that promote transdisciplinary engagement
[
9
]
, applying action design research in visualization studies
[
22
]
, and applying an interpretivist perspective toward design studies
[
24
,
34
]
. More recent perspectives suggest that both problem and visualization co-evolve through the design process
[
29
]

with goals, constraints, and evaluation criteria remaining fluid. Similarly, many (co-)design processes involve a “fuzzy front end” and are highly non-linear and perhaps non-deterministic
[
35
,
45
]
.
Further emerging work has begun to address specific aspects of such challenges, for example, structured visualization workshops to surface design opportunities
[
15
]
, and accounts of evolving data and handover between visualization designers and engineers
[
43
]
.
Building on these perspectives, we aim to explore a more flexible interpretation of existing design guidelines to better navigate the complexity of visualization atlas design.

2.3
Case Study Context: Climate Co-Benefits Modeling

We situate our inquiry into atlas design based on a case study of climate co-benefits research in the UK.
Co-benefits refer to the wider socio-economic effects of reducing greenhouse gas emissions
[
14
]
. For example, switching from fossil-fuel burning cars to electric vehicles results in reduced noise pollution as well as improved air quality.
Co-benefits can be expressed as monetary values representing money saved
[
5
]
. They, therefore, can play a crucial role in closing the gap between the investments required for climate action and the perceived limited immediate payoffs.
The dataset used in our case study is informed by previous research by Sudmant et al. in 2024
[
38
]
.
In total, our datasets contains
11
projected
co-benefits
such as air quality improvements, physical activity increase, and reduced dampness.
These projected co-benefits are based on a range of national climate interventions proposed by the UK Climate Change Committee (CCC)’s Seventh Carbon Budget,
1
1

1

https://www.theccc.org.uk/publication/the-seventh-carbon-budget
including improving building insulation, shifting to low-carbon modes of transport, and reducing meat consumption in favor of healthier diets, etc.
They are modeled projections on a yearly basis from 2025 to 2050, which is the UK Net Zero target year, highlighting the timeliness of designing a visualization atlas for this context.
Besides such
temporal aspects
, the co-benefits data contains
17 source indicators
collected from census records and national surveys, encompassing
continuous data
(e.g., median income),
discrete data
(e.g., number of cars), and
categorical data
(e.g., energy efficiency bands). All co-benefits values are modeled on a
hyper-local
level, totaling
46,426 small geographical units
(Data Zone) across the entire UK.

3
Co-Benefits Atlas: Research & Design Methods

Our case study is motivated by the goal of creating a visualization atlas to make co-benefits data public and usable for diverse audiences.
The novelty and multidimensionality of the data, combined with the
wickedness

[
33
,
19
]
of the topic—climate change and mitigation—and its broad societal relevance, makes co-benefits an ideal example to study visualization atlas design. Our goal was to visualize the entire data set, encompassing over 46,000 local communities, multiple data dimensions, and projected changes over time. In doing so, we aimed to learn from existing atlas projects, design patterns for exploration and explanation
[
44
]
, and sought to better understand the challenges in designing open data platforms. In this section, we describe the people involved in the UK Co-benefits atlas design as well as the design activities and related research methodologies involved.

[
44

We call them

•

•

The authors of this paper comprise all the Atlas designers and hosts, along with one key stakeholder with long-term involvement in co-benefits policy work. All authors were consistently involved throughout the entire project and are therefore referred to as the

3.3
Design workshop series

The 8 co-design workshops were structured into three stages—
Understand
,
Ideate & Make
, and
Reflect
. Each stage addressed specific design questions and featured tailored activities. Each workshop lasted two hours, with a minimum one-week interval between consecutive workshops to allow time for reflection and preparation. The workshops were conducted in a hybrid format to accommodate remote participation. Brainstorming and reflection were facilitated through an online Miro whiteboard,
2
2

2

https://miro.com
while color pens and paper were provided for those attending in-person.
In total, eleven participants took part in the design workshops (
Appendix

A
): nine core team members who attended consistently across sessions (including one key stakeholder), and two additional external stakeholders who each joined a single workshop: one in W1 for their interest in atlas concepts, and one in W4 for their familiarity with local communities.
The first author led the documentation of the workshops, including recordings, workshop materials, and participant attendance.
After each workshop, the first author organized the materials and produced a structured summary of emerging ideas and design directions.
These summaries were then presented and discussed in team reflection meetings, where they informed both prototype development and the preparation of subsequent workshops.
We outline the three-stage workshop setup as follows, with detailed documentation provided on our project website.

Stage 1: Understand [W1&2].
The first workshops aimed to create a shared understanding among core team members of the domain context and data, as well as potential audience groups and usage scenarios.
In the first workshop
[W1]
, hosts shared familiar tools to establish domain context for the visualization experts and the external stakeholder, and discussed existing atlas examples taken from online atlas collections
[
44
]
. Collectively, we discussed our project goals and why we would need an atlas, i.e., whether an atlas was the appropriate tool for this project. The visualization experts also asked many questions about the dataset and its methodology, which informed the structuring of the Atlas.
In the second workshop
[W2]
, we collectively created
speculative
stakeholder persona cards, including potential professions, painpoints, and anticipated goals in using the Atlas. These mainly originated from discussions with the team of hosts, using their experience working on climate mitigation. From the persona cards, we speculated on stakeholders’ potential data roles when using the Atlas, e.g., analyst, policy translator, defender of unpopular decisions.

Stage 2: Ideate & Make [W3-6].
The following four ideation workshops focused on different Atlas design dimensions, also described by Wang et al.
[
44
]
. These dimensions included

atlas content page design

[W3]
,

visualizations

[W4]
,

transparency and methodology

[W5]
, and

entry points and visual consistency

[W6]
. These workshops aimed to establish the visual, structural, and narrative framework of the atlas, i.e., what pages to include, what information these would feature, and how they were to be structured.
W3 asked
what types of atlas pages are needed for different audiences?
based on the different page types identified in Wang et al.’s design space
[
44
]
, such as articles, dashboards, and reports. To that end, each participant picked one persona and scenario card from W2 and sketched atlas pages for these profiles, including visualizations, structure, and required explanations. In W4, we spend more time on the different visualizations to be used, aiming to find a tradeoff between expressiveness in visualizing multidimensional data and simplicity. For example, some data could be integrated into the same visualization or it could be placed into separate, juxtaposed visualizations.
We did so through dedicated sketching sessions inspired by Kerzner et al’s visualization opportunities workshops
[
15
]
.
W5 involved sketching graphical labels
[
6
]
alongside visualizations and page features to signpost contextual information such as different data types, methodological assumptions, and key insights. This exercise clarified what the Atlas would need to disclose, and in what form: directly alongside visualizations, within summary texts, or on a dedicated methodology page.
In W6, we discussed how people could be onboarded and guided when using the atlas. This workshop explored UI components such as navigation bars, search bars, and data overviews as prompts to brainstorm what entry points should be prioritized for the landing page’s limited screen-space. Eventually, W6 focused on the visual identity and consistency of the Atlas.
Here, Atlas hosts assigned color schemes and icons (e.g., for co-benefits and socio-economic factors) from an extensive color palette provided by visualization designers.

Stage 3: Reflect [W7&8].

During month 7 of the Atlas development, we conducted two additional workshops to reflect on implemented prototypes and identify emerging challenges.
In
[W7]
, we reviewed early versions of page prototypes that contained real data. Domain experts were prompted to annotate page designs, verify patterns in visualizations and check that they corresponded to the main messages they had in mind, and to refine explanatory texts.
The last workshop
[W8]
focused on resolving outstanding design decisions. While page layouts and codebases had largely stabilized, several analytical and representational choices remained open, such as the default data measurements for co-benefits (per capita vs. total), and prioritization for certain socio-economic factors on the co-benefits page based on correlation strength. Visualization experts discussed these decisions with atlas hosts in order to draw on the hosts’ domain knowledge and inform solutions.

4
Atlas Design Decisions

The design process outlined above informed the UK Co-Benefits Atlas.
We outline its features alongside related design decisions—highlighting the rationales and un-realized directions behind a seemingly ‘simple-looking’ surface.
These features evolved over a set of intermediary versions of the atlas, alongside 8 design workshops and reflection meetings.
Consequently, our discussion captures not only what has been resolved, but also the uncertainties they included.

A central concept in a visualization atlas is the
page
which (e.g., in the form of a web page) includes information about a specific data element or feature of the atlas
[
44
]
. The UK Co-Benefits Atlas has the following pages: 11 Co-benefits report pages (
Fig.

3
-A), each providing all the data available for the respective co-benefit, 382 local authority report pages showing data about city-sizes communities (e.g., City of Edinburgh) (
Fig.

3
-B); 17 socio-economic factor report pages (e.g,. unemployment rate, number of cars per household). The Atlas also features a landing page, an interactive map with all 46,426 Data Zones, a data story page, and a methodology page. Each report page features between 5-20 individual visualizations with titles and brief explanations. Pages are linked where necessary, e.g., to socio-economic factor and co-benefits pages through clickable text and visual marks, and to other local authorities and Data Zones via an interactive map (
Fig.

3
-C).

We also discussed additional page types that would support more specific tasks, such as temporal pages focusing on individual years and comparison pages between Data Zones and co-benefits
[W2]
. We did not pursue these ideas due to the additional complexity they would introduce for navigation and the landing page (see below). However, these concepts highlight the potential of an atlas as a collection of pages dedicated to specific tasks (e.g., overview, analytics, explanation). Below, we detail the page types ultimately included in the atlas.

Landing pages
are the pages that users first encounter when visiting a visualization atlas. They aim to present the overarching ideas of the atlas while providing possible entry points into its content. They must provide context and explanations, while also communicating what users can learn, what the data are, and how to navigate into the details of the atlas. The challenge of designing landing pages is to include all this information within one page (limited screen space) in an inviting way. We decided to feature a ‘cover visualization’ in the form of a waffle chart on the Co-Benefits Atlas landing page, showing the total amounts of individual co-benefits (see Fig.

). The visualization aims to communicate
a)
the data-driven approach of this platform, and
b)
the main data findings
[W1]
. As we use the same waffle chart design across several pages, it also acts as a distinctive visual landmark of the atlas.
Explanatory text introduces co-benefits and informs potential stakeholders about what the atlas is and how to use it. We also featured a prominent search bar (to jump to specific local authorities), links to data stories, and all report page types with preview thumbnails.
These entry points were shaped together during workshops
[W6]
and were developed for two professional audiences
[W1]
+
[W2]
: those interested in specific climate interventions (e.g., housing retrofits) and those interested in specific geographic areas (e.g. city councils).

A major challenge in
content page
design was how to slice and group information into individual visualizations within and across different report pages. In early discussions about the dataset
[W1]
and during the sketching activity
[W4]
, we found different ways of presenting co-benefits data: geographical distribution at different levels of aggregation, total benefits vs. per capita benefits, temporal distribution in relation to socio-economic factors, etc.
Early workshops
[W2]
also led to a range of speculative designs, including geographical comparison analysis, policy impact stories, customizable dashboards, a geographical data report, policy simulation, etc. However, without direct evidence of which stories different stakeholders would find most valuable, or how they would engage with the data in practice, it was difficult to commit to specific narratives or page workflows. We therefore decided that each report should emphasize one primary data element
[D2]
, structured consistently from overview to detail. To scale this across the Atlas, we designed three bespoke

corresponding to the main data dimensions: co-benefits, local authorities, and socio-economic factors (see Fig.

. For example, a co-benefit page focuses on a single co-benefit and shows its national distribution (Fig.

Workshop insights
[W4]

While the report pages present information in a structured and communicative form, some stakeholders might need more analytical angles (e.g., community stakeholders focusing on data zone level interventions, or policy makers in charge of larger geographical regions
[W2]
). An
interactive map
of the UK shows co-benefits for all 46k local authority Data Zones to maximize open-ended exploration, particularly for more advanced users ( Fig.

Complementing data exploration, the atlas provides
written articles
, currently comprising a policy brief with main insights and a methodology page (

).
The need for explanatory content was raised repeatedly
[W1]

[W5]
, driven by the complexity of the data and the novelty of quantified co-benefits, underlying domain concepts, and the data modelling process.
We initially had planned workshops on creating more data stories

, focusing on storytelling, chart annotations, infographics, and fully narrative articles

. However, we decided to table these stories as extendable future avenues. At the time of design, the data were simply too new and lacked in-depth analysis.
Instead an example data story was co-crafted with a close stakeholder during later stages of the Atlas development and centered around a streamgraph from a local authority report (

5
Observations from stakeholder discussions

The emerging Co-Benefits Atlas prototype described above allowed more engagement with external stakeholders. We presented the Atlas to 25 external stakeholders as part of dedicated onboarding sessions. We began these sessions as soon as the Atlas included a landing page and report pages for local authorities and co-benefits implemented with real data (Month 7). At this stage, any study would not qualify as an evaluation of the Atlas but as a means to assess some of the designs described in Section 4 and whether an atlas was a promising direction for trying to make the data in question accessible. Consequently, we were mostly interested in the following questions:

a) What are people’s
first impression
when engaging with the atlas and do they find it too complex?

5.1
Participants and session setup

We organized four public outreach events to promote the Atlas and meet potential stakeholders. The events were advertised through organizational mailing lists and networks and attracted over 200 participants, including
\sim
20 colleagues within the core team’s institution,
\sim
50 local climate policy professionals,
\sim
100 officers from local governments across the UK, and
\sim
50 members of community-led climate networks with practitioners from diverse backgrounds. Following the second outreach event, the core team started to organize onboarding sessions to help potential users get familiar with the atlas and let them ask questions, discuss use cases, etc.
The onboarding sessions were promoted on the Atlas contact page, on LinkedIn, and announced during the outreach events.

25 participants attended one of our onboarding sessions: 11 university staff providing professional services to local business development and government policy (P1-11); 2 staff from social enterprises (P11-12); 5 from local climate community hubs (P14-18), 2 from public libraries (P19-20), 2 from public consultancies (P21-22), and 3 holding different positions in government organizations (P23-25). For full demographic and professional details, refer to
Appendix

B
. None of the participants indicated proficiency in data analysis or interpreting visualizations, despite their substantial professional expertise.

5.2
Data Collection & Analysis

We ran a total of 15 onboarding sessions (11 individual and 4 group sessions), each attended by 2-5 participants.
Participation and the data collection process complied with the university’s ethics committee guidelines and approval.
The sessions were conducted online via Microsoft Teams and lasted around one hour each. The first author facilitated the sessions, which were also attended by one of the Atlas hosts.
During the sessions, participants were first asked to introduce their professional background and familiarity with co-benefits. This was followed by a walkthrough of the Atlas structure and page features. Participants were then invited to freely explore the Atlas and ask questions.
We asked participants to think aloud to record their thoughts. Facilitators replied to questions, clarified information, and asked follow-up questions.

All onboarding sessions were recorded and automatically transcribed. The lead facilitator conducted a two-stage thematic analysis
[
3
]
.
First, the transcripts were reviewed to identify information relevant to: participants’ professional profiles, how they explored the Atlas report pages, and the usage scenarios they proposed. During this stage, ad-hoc thematic tags were created and applied to conversation segments to capture both the topic discussed (e.g., data credibility) and where it arose in the Atlas (e.g., local authority report).
In the second round, these tags were reviewed, refined, and merged into broader categories. The thematic analysis was mainly conducted by the first author; emerging themes were discussed within the core team
[M22]
, who collectively reflected on and helped refine the categories to identify key barriers and usage scenarios. The final themes reported below reflect this shared review.

5.3
Observations

All participants expressed enthusiasm for the Atlas and its features. We also observed engagement with individual Atlas pages, i.e., participants reading and trying to interpret individual visualizations.

First Impressions—What are people’s first impression engaging with the Atlas?

For many participants, the Atlas
looked impressive yet overwhelming
at first glance. While participants quickly grasped the richness of content in the Atlas, the represented data was experienced as complex, even overwhelming:
“it looks like there’s a lot of helpful information—would be my initial reaction. But then, I wouldn’t know where to start”
(P16).
Participants also asked for similar onboarding sessions in the future to
“gain my confidence and navigate the Atlas”
(P20).

Engagement—How do people explore the atlas? What information do they look for?

Participants had
different approaches to exploring the Atlas
, often guided by their personal experience or professional background. We observed explorations of the low-level Data Zones that led to ideas around community-level applications:
“If an [interest] group wants to apply for [a specific intervention], they can go straight into that hyper-local area and see if we get money to install this”
(P15).
Other participants instead started with specific economic factors, such as housing energy bands (P12) or the inhabitants’ age profile (P20).
This supports the idea that atlases accommodate a range of tasks and exploration approaches and suggests the importance of providing various exploratory features and diverse points of views on the data. Participants did not necessarily follow the ‘overview-then-detail’ journey
[
21
]
, and often began their exploration from a feature they were personally interested in or involved with.

Participants also
asked specific questions related to the data and methodologies
, for example, concerning the measures used in the modeling process:
“does it know in a Data Zone which properties are suitable for certain types of retrofit measures?”
(P12). They also highlighted the importance of methodology:
“you don’t have to get the great details, but just give me a sense of what that is, the parameters, the assumptions and the methodology”
(P14).
The Atlas triggered more in-depth methodology questions as sessions progressed, especially by those with an academic background. For example, P5 asked about the interconnection between societal impact data
“how do you set the boundary of the co-benefits?”
. We also frequently observed instances of participants interpreting but also questioning the presented information based on their work experience. For example, P15 mentioned certain interventions
“are not going to land greatly in the city where I work.”

Strongly related, we found participants
curious about data not included in the Atlas
. For example, participants asked for clarifications on why certain co-benefits and socio-economic factors were not included in the Atlas:
“so are you only using data sets that apply to the whole of the UK or do you use regional data?”
(P9). This suggests a keen interest and critical reflection on methodological limitations and trying to interpret the atlas in its wider context, i.e., atlases as platforms that
could
include more data. Some participants were particularly skeptical—in a positive sense—about the data, e.g., when local data contradicted their day-to-day experience:
“[For local travel times,] you might want to have a little asterisk and say this is not the case necessarily for everyone”
(P6).
Some participants suggested possible approaches to address their concerns, for example via the introduction of external datasets. For example:
“Did you use past history data and compared it to see like how accurate the future data would be?”
(P22).

Frictions—Which content and design features present challenges in exploration and interpretation?

The methodological questions outlined above represent critical reflections that can be considered as frictions between participants’ understanding (and belief) in the data presented. Moreover, we also found that some of the data representations presented challenges for interpretation. For example, participants found it challenging to
understand the quantification of abstract co-benefits
: i.e., how social benefits can be compared,
“I’m not sure how you compare that value of my [travel] time relating to the value of breathing cleaner air”
(P6). While this is related to methodology, P9 acknowledged the complexity of
“the [multi-faceted] data that you have to put in[to the atlas].”
We believe that this challenge of explaining analytical values and measures to describe complex real-world topics is inherent in the comprehensive approach of visualization atlases in general (cf.
[
44
]
).

The interpretation of patterns in the visualizations and understanding their real-world meaning also presented challenges. While participants found the standard visualizations like bar charts easy to read, some found it challenging to
translate observed patterns to practical policy implications
. For example, P6 asked about the disproportionate contributions across the 11 co-benefits categories (
Figure 3
-B),
“Why will physical activity go up that much?”

This lack of clarity may negatively impact participants’ willingness to take action:
“So if I talk to a policy maker, improved diets sounds great, but how? What specifically do you need me in order to do that?”
(P5).
Related to this are frictions imposed by participants questioning the
reliability of the data
. While participants understood that projection data does not promise certainty, they still sought some indication of the data’s reliability to represent reality:
“What can one do to avoid overstating what is possible? Obviously you can’t have a detailed survey of every single property!”
(P12).

5 participants found it challenging to
maintain data context when switching pages
in the Atlas navigating between pages and visualizations. For example, when switching to a local authority page, P17 asked about the time range shown in the co-benefits distribution:
“So what is the year that it starts in your graph? What is the year model 2025?”
Similarly, P19 noted
“the landing page figure (waffle, is that national as well or can you change that by district or region?”
. We believe this to be a general challenge in visualization atlas design, especially if many pages contain similar (looking) visualizations and information.

Last but not least, participants asked for
proofs of integrity
from some form of authority, e.g., by displaying logos from the core team’s institutions alongside a clear statement that the methodology aligns with (UK) government guidance on policy evaluation. The lack of such links challenged the trust that participants have in the information provided and actions they may take. For example, one participant noted that they needed to know
“where the funding comes from and what the future is for the Atlas. This will help me decide how comfortable I feel putting resources into it.”
(P18). Some participants further expressed a strong interest in quoting a credible statement about whether the Atlas had been recognized or officially adopted by local governments. Establishing trust is key for visualization atlases to have an impact.

Usage scenarios—How do people envision using an atlas in the long run?

For our participants, the onboarding sessions were their first time exploring the UK Co-Benefits Atlas in-depth, however, these guided explorations cannot provide insight how the Atlas will be used in the long run. Nevertheless, participants highlighted the Atlas as a novel and potentially valuable source of information and expressed enthusiasm for sharing the Co-Benefits Atlas with peers within their professional and community networks,
“I’d love to show our librarians and they can demonstrate this to their library communities”
(P19). Participants working with local communities acknowledged the general value of publicly accessible research resources:
“We wouldn’t be able to do this—we don’t have the capacity or the required skills with it being at a national level and linked to the institution, which gives it credibility that we could use in funding applications or in conversations with the council.”
(P16). Participants mentioned four potential usage scenarios involving the Co-Benefit Atlas, ranging from lightweight engagements, such as learning, to stronger impacts through decision making.

Scenario 1: Learning about co-benefits and co-benefits data
—As co-benefits still remain an emerging concept, learning about the scope of the Atlas and its core data dimensions represents an early-stage use, where the Atlas would function not primarily for accessing data but for learning. Six participants signed up for training sessions out of curiosity rather than with a concrete application in mind, for example:
“I was keen to understand in a bit more detail exactly how it’s built and how it works, for future reference”
(P7).

Scenario 2: Raise awareness and foster wider engagement
—The Atlas was found to have the potential to reach wide audiences in different ways.
P19, who works in a library, emphasized promoting information about the Atlas in a non-intrusive way, stating that
“we tend not to try and force things on to people, we like people to find their own feet, especially when it can be classed as a sensitive issue, such as climate change.”

Several participants viewed the Atlas as a valuable resource for policy makers, for example P6 shared
“it’s quite important that we can bring resources like this to national government, make them aware of things that are available.”
.
Finally, participants raised the question of how to best shape the story about the Atlas. For example,
“It’s how we communicate the importance and the uncertainties around those assumptions”
(P9), as well as crafting the overall take-home message:
“what we’re saying [now] is the investment and the costs, but these co-benefits in 5 or 10 years time will be much more tangible.”
(P9).

Scenario 3: Justify & plan decision making
—Participants envisioned the Atlas to be useful in different ways to support decision making.
Seven participants wanted to use the Atlas for seeking confirmation and evidence for predefined agendas, such as justifying the positive social impact of prior work. For example:
“So we want to try and convince the government to fund this long term to 2045 […] this Atlas could be invaluable to convince them”
(P14). However, the Atlas also challenged participants’ ideas. For example, P1 was shocked at the negative outcome of road conditions and expressed concerns about the related transport policy in place, raising the critical question of how public data influence decision-making.
Finally, participants also underscored the Atlas’ power guide future actions and priorities. As P14 pointing to promising co-benefits in physical activity increase that closely linked to active transport policies, saying the Atlas changed their perspective.
P25 further highlighted the Atlas can support the allocation of limited resources:
“We want to pick actions that […] maximize return of co-benefits on investment”
.

6
Informing Atlas Design

Reflecting on our design process, we now ask:
what can we learn from this for future atlas design?
To address this question, we lean onto the metaphor of
forces
, i.e., major influences or agencies that shape the design process at different moments. The forces we observed in our process are
Data
,
People
,
Stories
,
Atlas
, and
Context
(
Fig.

4
).
These forces and their respective interactions frame atlas design as a dynamic process, continuously shaped by shifting priorities and constraints. In the following, we first introduce the forces as experienced in our design process, then reflect on their relation to established visualization design approaches,
and eventually discuss the implications for future atlas design.

6.1
Forces in the atlas design process

Figure 4
:
Navigating shifting forces driving atlas design: data, stories, people, atlas, and context. We illustrate how these forces changed in our case study from A) early stage, B) workshop & prototype, to C) stakeholder engagement.

The
Data
force
is made up from the available data, their size, complexity and comprehensiveness, uncertainties, and analysis processes. It also captures the data origin, their construction and technologies involved.
In our case, the co-benefits data were provided prior to the design process by the Atlas hosts, resulting from a supercomputer model and were heavily informed and influence by government guidelines.
In our process,
Data
became a relatively dominant force from the beginning, where its dimensions influenced sketching activities (
Section

3.3
,
[W4]
), and where the complexity in the data implicated the amount of resources required to construct visual representations and explanations.

The
People
force
encompasses all actors involved in or engaging with the visualization atlas. This includes atlas hosts who commission the atlas (associated scientists, analysts, and domain experts) and visualization designers. It also includes external stakeholders that the atlas is aimed at, such as the participants in our design workshops and onboarding sessions.

People
are a force shaped by their knowledge of the topic, data, and visualizations; their interests and tasks; and their time and willingness to engage with, trust, promote, and contribute to the atlas (
Fig.

4
-A).
During the workshop and development stages, the
People
force was influenced by the vision of the Atlas hosts and the designers’ ability to translate this vision into concrete representations. Only a fraction of possible external stakeholders could be involved, as the Atlas remained an unstable, work-in-progress system with limited and uneven access to
Data
; therefore, we deliberately used workshops to observe and amplify diverse ways of engaging with the Atlas as design opportunities.

Through the continued interaction between
People
and
Data
, the
Atlas
gradually emerged as its own force (
Fig.

4
-B), taking shape through its structure, visualizations, interactivity, navigation- and explanatory elements.
This Atlas is now a dense yet highly structured platform with a landing page, over 400 content pages for different data elements (co-benefits, local areas, socio-economic factors), an interactive map, and stories. This design reflects existing design patterns
[
44
]
, and resembles the ‘Exploratoria’ atlas genre.
Once established, this Atlas became an active force feeding into interactions between
People
and
Data
by shaping how data can be accessed and interpreted. In particular, it enabled engagement with external stakeholders by providing a concrete medium through which to explore and interpret the data (
Section

5
).
While the Atlas was often perceived as overwhelming at first, the breadth of data was regarded as a valuable resource across use cases such as learning, awareness, decision-making, and future planning. At the same time, stakeholders sought specific information, critically interrogated data methodologies and omissions, and expressed difficulty interpreting complex and abstract phenomena, such as translating co-benefits into monetary terms.

This led us to define
Stories
as their own force as they are further able to define how people experience the data through the atlas (
Fig.

4
-C).
Stories
can include anything from specific articles, annotations, titles and explanations in the atlas, to methodology explanations, policy briefs
3
3

3

https://ukcobenefitsatlas.net/stories/story.pdf
and reports
[
36
]
and eventually high-level narratives about climate mitigation.
Many of these stories emerged from
People
’s analysis of the data, and can highlight the relevance of the atlas, its topic and its wider context to stakeholders.
In our case, stories are intended to originate more strongly from external stakeholders than from the hosts, positioning
Stories
as a force that becomes more prominent in later stages of development.
Since reaching a prototype stage and involving more stakeholders, the development of stories has the potential to constitute important entry points to the Atlas data, helping connect with wider audiences who resonate with them (
Section

5
).

A final force we noticed was the
Context
, including everything surrounding the other forces and the design process. It includes the atlas’ topic (e.g., co-benefits for climate mitigation) and the public discourse around the topic. This discourse influences relevance to
People
and
Data
as well as frameworks and sources for data collection or creation (e.g., government guidelines, explicit rigorous practices, quality control). The
Context
also includes more pragmatic aspects such as funding, timelines, and external frameworks and guidelines for design. As a ‘background’ force, shifts in context can have a direct impact on data, stakeholders, stories and the atlas (design). We experienced such a shift in the form of revised government directives which triggered regular updates to data and consequently the Atlas design. For example, in one such update, the UK government decided to remove a particular data dimension (comparison between different futures) from its agenda. We consequently had to remove an entire data dimension from the Atlas, including the
Stories
and some potential stakeholders (
People
).

6.2
Relation to Other Design Processes

As mentioned in
Section

2.2
, traditional visualization design frameworks explain design as a linear, albeit iterative process, but in any case deterministic process (e.g.,
[
37
,
25
,
23
]
). These processes typically assume domain experts and their data and imply a step-by-step progress from specification to ideation and prototyping all the way to evaluation.
We do not suggest visualization atlases not following such processes: however, our experience highlights that understanding atlas design, and to some extent visualization design more generally, as a dynamic, non-linear process can help plan for different forces and their interactions at different design stages. This experience is more akin to fuzzy front ends
[
35
]
, team-based visualization projects
[
43
]
and negotiating design tradeoffs (e.g.,
[
2
]
) where changes to one design aspect sets constraints while opening up possibilities elsewhere in the process.

In our case study, a traditional requirement analysis was not feasible due to the novelty of the data and uncertain use cases. People would most likely not have understood what atlases were either. Also, both hosts and external stakeholders consistently favored simple, standard chart types, shifting the challenge away from novel visualization techniques toward organizing relevant information across a wide range of atlas design dimensions
[
44
]
(e.g., navigation, page structure, etc.).
This implies that atlas prototyping draws on a broader set of design methods beyond visualization, including higher-level practices such as persona construction
[
31
]
(WS2) that involve speculation, visualization sketching
[
15
]
(WS3--4), and more focused design sessions (WS4--8) that can be guided by design patterns
4
4

4

https://vis-atlas.github.io/#/collection
, helping translate abstract atlas design dimensions into concrete and actionable activities for a more focused exploration of design directions (Fig.
4
Prototyping & Iteration).

Once we had an atlas prototype, discussions of potential frictions and usage scenarios with more external stakeholders could be mediated through this prototype
[
12
]
. As an
aesthetic provocation

[
12
]
, the atlas prototype also raised new questions in terms of the data construction methodologies or uncertainties, potentially leading to an interrogation of data-related methods, structures, or missing entities. This prototype allowed us to illustrate to stakeholders the co-benefits data, and their facets. In turn, this enabled stakeholders to envision potential usage scenarios and highlight potential frictions, for example, the need for onboarding mechanisms and scaffolding in-situ interpretation.

It is likely that other atlas projects require different design processes entirely. For example, Wang et al. highlighted a range visualization atlas projects
[
44
]
that would suggest different force constellations and a demand for different design methods. For example, the World Bank Atlas of Sustainable Development Goals
[
30
]
has been be characterized as
story-driven

[
44
]
, where data has already been analyzed and structured into narratives prior to design. In such cases, the design process focuses on communicating these stories effectively through visualization design. This design process is also highly influenced by previous versions of the same atlas. Conversely, in the Rare Diseases Observatory
[
40
]
, stakeholders’ requirements were the most dominant force, driving not only the design process but also the selection of data sources and the curation of stories, suggesting a highly participatory and task-oriented design process, where the stakeholder strongly influenced the data force.

6.3
Implications for Design: Anticipate Change

From this perspective, we believe that the core idea of interacting forces—while possibly incomplete—can be a productive lens to inform visualization atlas design processes. It provides a “spatial”, rather than temporal approach to navigating design, while no single starting or end point is suggested. In other words, rather than prescribing a methodology, this perspective describes the problem space in which design happens. This can inform different design and evaluation methods at different stages.
Early on in the design process, reflecting on existing and anticipated forces can help take stock of what aspects are available to drive the design process, what aspects we strive to develop further, and what methods might be appropriate to get there. Regular reflection on force constellations and how they have changed can (1) help recognize evolving insights as part of the process and (2) ground intentional shifts to different methods if required.

More than anything, though, approaching visualization atlas design through the lens of dynamic forces anticipates changes to the design process at anytime. This is important because visualization atlas projects are typically too complex to foresee concrete outcomes from the beginning, and insights throughout the design process may change ideas, visions, and design approaches. Framing visualization atlas design as a dynamic, speculative, and long-term process where the atlas artefacts produced are considered more as “immutable mobiles”
[
17
]
or “sandcastles”
[
12
]
that act as
“aesthetic provocations”
and
“mediators of ideas”
softens the characteristics suggested by the term “atlas” (e.g., stability, impact, even authority) but in a productive way. Productive in a sense that it frames visualization atlases as artefacts that open up active discussions of complex, publicly relevant topics across a wide range of audiences. Frictions or blindspots introduced by an atlas artefact can be addressed in a new version.
An atlas design that can anticipate changes of certain forces (e.g., data updates, insights and stories, new stakeholder groups and/or usage scenarios) may prove more sustainable. This, we think, is the main challenge in atlas design:
how to design with change in mind and to make an atlas a sustainable source for critical engagement and information
.

7
Avenues for Future Work

With our atlas case study we hope to start a discussion of methodologies that can inform and facilitate the design of visualization atlases. However, this discussion also needs to also include questions around people’s use and engagement with information presented in the form of atlases. Our own reflections on utilizing the metaphor of forces as a lens to inform atlas design while anticipating changes present a starting point to this discussion, and raise interesting questions, in particular, regarding the interactions between the
People
and
Atlas
forces.

How to strive for inclusive visualization atlas design?

The
People
force is highly heterogeneous and future research needs to unpack its interactions with other forces more carefully to ensure the
inclusion of different groups of people in atlas design processes
. Public decision making should consider “the public’s” perspectives, i.e., be transparent, inclusive, and embrace different views. To fully realize the potential of visualization atlases to make complex, publicly relevant topics and related data accessible, means creating conditions that invite for discourse and knowledge exchange. This calls for a wide range of people to be involved early on in atlas design, at the very least, as soon as a prototype is formed that can mediate a critical discourse.

Since our Atlas launched in July 2025, we have delivered four public outreach events and 15 stakeholder engagement sessions. Google Analytics reports over 4,200 active Atlas users as of March 2026. The Atlas has been used to support the UK government’s Department for Energy Security and Net Zero as well as by analysts involved in Scotland’s Climate Plan
[
7
]
. In Glasgow it is being used to develop the city’s climate action plan, and in Manchester it informed a work program on financing climate action. This suggests that the Atlas supports different stakeholders in their respective agendas; policy makers in devising policy, governments in making and justifying decisions, businesses in planning investments, advocacy groups in planning campaigns, and citizens in informing voting behaviors and personal projects.

However, this engagement predominantly focuses on stakeholders with particular educational backgrounds, approaching the Atlas from a professional context. Opening visualization atlas design decidedly to those who are often excluded in the public discourse but are directly affected by certain policies (e.g., people from deprived areas, without access to higher education, people with migration backgrounds, or simply young people) raises important and challenging questions for future research, in terms of onboarding strategies, and
designing atlases with data and visualization literacy in mind
. For example, the Atlas recently become the subject of a public data visualization competition inviting people to design (more) visualizations for the data.
5
5

5

https://thedatalab.com/data-visualisation-competition-2025

How do people use visualization atlases?

Another important area for future research is
studying the (long-term) use and impact of visualization atlases
, further interrogating interactions between the
People
,
Atlas
, and also
Stories
forces. While we have observed people engaging with our atlas as part of training and onboarding sessions, only the long-term use of atlases will reveal if they can live up to the promise of supporting open and transparent access to data and inform data-driven decision making. While we hear reports of the latter on an anecdotal basis, more detailed studies are required to understand what aspects and features of visualization atlases are productive in certain scenarios and how. As the complexity of visualization atlases requires long-term engagement, lab-based evaluation methods are not enough, but methodologies need to be explored that allow studying the engagement with atlases “in-the-wild”.

How can visualization atlases influence preconceptions?

Closely linked to the above question of real-world engagement with visualization atlases and their impact is the question of if and how visualization atlases can address and potentially widen existing frames of references. Engagement with (new) information is always filtered through prior knowledge, beliefs, and intent (
confirmation bias

[
26
,
16
]
) which can lead individuals to focus on atlas findings that align with their expectations while downplaying or rationalizing away conflicting data
[
26
,
20
]
, even in well-meaning cases
[
11
]
. There is furthermore a wide range of statistical fallacies such as the
multiple-comparison problem

[
46
]
in which statistical differences and patterns may emerge by chance when examining many relationships within large datasets. Future work may examine in particular the role of the
Stories
force to navigate such issues, and also address the potential of misinterpretation which can also have a negative impact on atlas authors and hosting institutions.
