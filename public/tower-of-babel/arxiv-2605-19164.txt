The Spatial Cramér–von Mises Test of Independence under β \beta -Mixing: Asymptotic Theory and Python Implementation
Marco C. Mandap

Abstract
We derive the asymptotic distribution of the spatial Cramér–von Mises statistic for testing bivariate independence in stationary random fields on ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} under polynomial β \beta -mixing dependence, and document the Python implementation that reproduces all simulation results. The classical Cramér–von Mises test assumes i.i.d. observations; we extend it to spatially dependent data by combining three ingredients: (i) a Davydov-type covariance bound that yields integrability of the spatial covariance kernel under the mixing condition θ > 2 ​ ( 2 + δ ) / δ \theta>2(2+\delta)/\delta ; (ii) a reformulation of the inner-form test statistic as a degenerate U-statistic of order 2 with a product kernel Q = G 1 ⊗ G 2 Q=G_{1}\otimes G_{2} , following De Wet (1980); and (iii) an extension of Gregory’s (1977) U-statistic limit theorem to β \beta -mixing sequences via Yoshihara (1976). This approach bypasses the functional central limit theorem entirely. The limit distribution is a weighted sum of correlated χ 1 2 \chi^{2}_{1} variables whose eigenvalues factor as products of marginal eigenvalues; in the small-bandwidth limit, the correlation vanishes and the limit reduces to a weighted sum of independent χ 1 2 \chi^{2}_{1} variables. Explicit eigenvalue formulas are given for three common weight functions (uniform, optimal normal, Anderson–Darling), producing computable critical values. The software generates Matérn random fields by circulant embedding, computes the test statistic via the inner-form kernel decomposition, evaluates asymptotic critical values by Monte Carlo on the limit distribution, and runs permutation-based alternatives. Competing tests (Mantel, cross- K K , distance covariance) are included for power comparisons. Simulation experiments show that the Anderson–Darling weight achieves the best power across Gaussian and t t -copula alternatives, while the Mantel and cross- K K tests have no power against cross-dependence in spatially correlated fields.

Introduction

The Cramér–von Mises statistic measures the integrated squared difference between an empirical and a hypothesised cumulative distribution function. For independent data the classical theory is well understood: the limit distribution is a weighted sum of
\chi^{2}_{1}
variables whose weights are the eigenvalues of the covariance kernel
[
1
,
9
]
. For spatially dependent data—common in geostatistics, environmental monitoring, and spatial econometrics—the dependence structure must be explicitly controlled.

This paper gives a derivation of the asymptotic distribution for a
spatial
Cramér–von Mises test of
bivariate independence
, together with a complete Python implementation and simulation study. The null hypothesis
H_{0}
is that the marginal components
X_{s}
and
Y_{s}
of the bivariate random field
Z_{s}=(X_{s},Y_{s})
are independent, so that the joint CDF factors as
H(x,y)=F_{1}(x)F_{2}(y)
. Under
H_{0}
, the probability integral transform
U_{s}=F_{1}(X_{s})
and
V_{s}=F_{2}(Y_{s})
are marginally uniform on
[0,1]
, reducing the spatial CvM statistic to De Wet’s
[
8
]
U-statistic form (Section
11.4
). We work in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with a stationary
\beta
-mixing random field satisfying Assumption
11.6
, a compactly supported kernel smoother, and a sequence of spatial lattices whose mesh tends to zero. Our main result (Theorem
11.28
) states that under polynomial mixing decay, the centred and normalised test statistic converges in distribution to a weighted sum of correlated
\chi^{2}_{1}
variables whose eigenvalues factor as products of marginal eigenvalues. In the small-bandwidth limit, the correlation vanishes and the limit reduces to the classical i.i.d. form.

Two features distinguish our approach from previous work:

1.

U-statistic route.
Rather than proving weak convergence in
C([0,1])
or
\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
—which requires tightness via Arzelà–Ascoli and a functional central limit theorem for mixing fields—we reformulate the spatial CvM statistic as a degenerate U-statistic of order 2
[
13
,
17
]
. Gregory’s theorem
[
10
]
then yields the limit distribution from scalar convergence alone, and Yoshihara’s
[
23
]
extension to
\beta
-mixing sequences handles the spatial dependence. This bypasses the functional CLT entirely.

2.

Eigenvalue factorization.
The inner form of the CvM statistic (using empirical marginals) yields a U-statistic kernel that factors as a product of marginal kernels:
Q=G_{1}\otimes G_{2}
. Consequently, the eigenvalues factor as products of marginal eigenvalues:
\gamma_{jk}=\lambda^{(1)}_{j}\cdot\lambda^{(2)}_{k}
(De Wet
[
8
]
; Theorem
11.31
). This reduces the two-dimensional spectral problem to two one-dimensional problems. Explicit eigenvalue formulas for three common weight functions (uniform, optimal normal, Anderson–Darling) give computable critical values without numerical integration.

The accompanying Python implementation reproduces all simulation results and provides reusable components for researchers who wish to apply or extend the test. The software covers: (i) computation of the spatial CvM test statistic via the inner-form kernel decomposition; (ii) asymptotic critical values from the limit distribution; (iii) permutation-based inference for finite-sample
p
-values; (iv) data generation under both the null hypothesis (independent Matérn fields) and alternatives (Gaussian and
t
-copula dependence); (v) competing tests (Mantel, cross-
K
, distance covariance) for power comparisons.

The paper is organised as follows. Sections
2
–
10
describe the software: package architecture, test statistic computation, asymptotic critical values, data generation, competing tests, simulation experiments, design decisions, verification, and a practical usage guide. Section
11
develops the full asymptotic distribution theory, including covariance bounds, integrability, asymptotic covariance, the U-statistic reformulation, the limit distribution theorem, and the eigenvalue structure. Section
12
discusses extensions and practical recommendations. Appendices
A
–
C
provide complete epsilon-delta proofs of the Riemann-sum convergence results, the alternative functional CLT route via Bernstein blocking, and algorithm pseudocode.

3
The Spatial CvM Test Statistic: Formulation and Implementation

3.1
Mathematical formulation

The null hypothesis is
H_{0}\colon X_{s}
and
Y_{s}
are independent, so that the probability integral transform
U_{s}=F_{1}(X_{s})
and
V_{s}=F_{2}(Y_{s})
are marginally
\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)
. The centered spatial CvM statistic is

T_{n}^{\mathrm{cent}}=T_{n}-\mu_{n},\qquad T_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{Q}(Z_{i},Z_{j}),

(3.1)

where
n=m^{2}
is the number of lattice sites,
\mu_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i}\hat{Q}(Z_{i},Z_{i})
is the centering constant, and
\hat{Q}
is the
inner-form
kernel

\hat{Q}\bigl((u_{i},v_{i}),\,(u_{j},v_{j})\bigr)=\hat{G}_{U}(u_{i},u_{j})\;\hat{G}_{V}(v_{i},v_{j}),

(3.2)

with the marginal kernel decomposition

\hat{G}_{W}(s,t)=G_{W}(s,t)+A_{W}(s)+A_{W}(t)+B_{W}.

(3.3)

Here
G_{W}
is the true-CDF kernel computed from the weight function
W
, and
A_{W}
,
B_{W}
are the
inner-form corrections
that account for replacing the true CDF
F(u)=u
with the empirical CDF
F_{1n}(u)
:

\displaystyle A_{W}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{s<u}-u\bigr)\bigl(u-F_{1n}(u)\bigr)\,W(u)\,du,

(3.4)

\displaystyle B_{W}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(u-F_{1n}(u)\bigr)^{2}\,W(u)\,du.

(3.5)

The product structure
\hat{Q}=\hat{G}_{U}\otimes\hat{G}_{V}
is the key property: it reduces the two-dimensional eigenvalue problem to two one-dimensional problems. Section
11.4
shows that the inner-form statistic is asymptotically equivalent to the U-statistic with product kernel
Q=G_{1}\otimes G_{2}
, where
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are the true-marginal kernels corresponding to
\hat{G}_{U}
and
\hat{G}_{V}
respectively (under
H_{0}
,
G_{1}=G_{U}
and
G_{2}=G_{V}
with the convention that the first marginal corresponds to the
U
-variable). The marginal CvM kernels are

G_{W}(s,t):=\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(\mathbf{1}(s<u)-u\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{1}(t<u)-u\bigr)W(u)\,du.

(3.6)

Under
H_{0}
, the kernel
Q
is degenerate (
\mathbb{E}[Q(Z_{1},z)]=0
for all
z
) and square-integrable (Propositions
11.20
and
11.21
), and the eigenvalues factor as products of marginal eigenvalues:
\gamma_{jk}=\lambda^{(1)}_{j}\cdot\lambda^{(2)}_{k}
(Theorem
11.31
).

Anderson–Darling weight (

\int_{0}^{1}f(u)\,\frac{\,du}{u(1-u)}=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}f(\sin^{2}\!\theta)\;4\,d\theta.

(3.8)

The marginal kernel

Optimal normal weight (

Here the integrals are naturally expressed in the

,
\,du=\phi(z)\,dz

(3.9)

evaluated by Gauss–Hermite quadrature with 100 nodes.

Asymptotic Critical Values

4.1
Limit distribution

By Theorem
11.28
, the centered statistic converges in distribution:

T_{n}^{\mathrm{cent}}\xrightarrow{d}\sum_{j,k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{(1)}\lambda_{k}^{(2)}\bigl(N_{jk}^{2}-1\bigr),

(4.1)

where
N_{jk}
are i.i.d. standard normal in the small-bandwidth limit and the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}
depend on the weight function:

1.

Uniform
(
W=1
):
\lambda_{j}=1/(\pi j)^{2}
(Corollary
11.32
).

2.

Optimal normal
(
W(u)=\phi^{2}(\Phi^{-1}(u))
):
\lambda_{j}=1/j
(Corollary
11.33
).

3.

Anderson–Darling
(
W(u)=[u(1-u)]^{-1}
):
\lambda_{j}=1/[j(j+1)]
(Corollary
11.34
).

The factorisation
\gamma_{jk}=\lambda_{j}^{(1)}\lambda_{k}^{(2)}
reduces the double sum to two matrix–vector products, which is exploited in the Monte Carlo computation.

Data Generation

5.1
Null hypothesis: independent Matérn fields

Under
H_{0}
, the spatial data are generated as two independent Matérn Gaussian random fields on an
m\times m
regular lattice in
[0,1]^{2}
. The Matérn covariance function is

C(h)=\frac{\sigma^{2}\,2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\,(\kappa h)^{\nu}\,K_{\nu}(\kappa h),

(5.1)

where
K_{\nu}
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind,
\kappa>0
controls the range,
\nu>0
is the smoothness, and
\sigma^{2}
is the marginal variance. The effective range
\rho=\sqrt{8\nu}/\kappa
is set to a fraction of the domain width:
\rho=r\cdot(m-1)
, where
r=0.05
is the default range fraction.

The mixing rate
\theta
is linked to the smoothness via the mapping
\theta\approx 2\nu
for Gaussian Matérn fields
[
11
]
. Three regimes are used:

1.

Strong mixing
(
\theta=6
,
\nu=3
): exponential decay of correlations.

2.

Moderate mixing
(
\theta=4
,
\nu=2
): the boundary case.

3.

Weak mixing
(
\theta=3
,
\nu=1.5
): polynomial decay.

Fields are generated by
circulant embedding
(Wood and Chan
[
22

5.2
Alternative hypotheses

Two copula alternatives create spatially dependent data while preserving marginal distributions.

Gaussian copula.
Generate independent Matérn fields
X
and
\varepsilon
, standardise to zero mean and unit variance, and set

Y=\rho\,\hat{X}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\,\hat{\varepsilon},

(5.2)

where
\hat{X}
and
\hat{\varepsilon}
are the standardised fields. The PIT-transform
U=\Phi(X)
,
V=\Phi(Y)
yields data with Gaussian copula dependence of strength
\rho
.

t
-copula.
Start with the correlated Gaussian pair from (
5.2
) and draw a single
S\sim\chi^{2}(\nu_{t})/\nu_{t}
shared across all spatial locations. Set

T_{1}=\frac{X}{\sqrt{S}},\qquad T_{2}=\frac{Y}{\sqrt{S}},

(5.3)

and apply the
t
-distribution CDF:
U=t_{\nu_{t}}(T_{1})
,
V=t_{\nu_{t}}(T_{2})
. The shared scaling
S
preserves the Matérn spatial structure within the copula. Kendall’s
\tau
relates to the copula correlation via
\rho=\sin(\pi\tau/2)
.

6
Competing Tests

Three competing tests of spatial independence are implemented for power comparisons.

6.1
Mantel test

The Mantel test
[
15
]
computes the Pearson correlation between the spatial distance matrix
D^{S}=(\|s_{i}-s_{j}\|)
and the variable distance matrix
D^{V}=(\|v_{i}-v_{j}\|)
. Permutation inference is used:
V
is permuted relative to
U
and the test statistic recomputed
B_{\mathrm{perm}}
times. An adaptive early-stopping rule exits after
B_{\min}=99
permutations if the running
p
-value estimate is more than
\epsilon=0.02
away from the nominal level
\alpha
.

6.2
Cross-
K
function test

Following Ripley
[
16
]
, the cross-
K
function
K_{12}(r)
measures the expected number of type-2 points within distance
r
of a type-1 point, adjusted for intensity. Points are classified as type-1 or type-2 by whether
U_{i}<\mathrm{median}(U)
. The test statistic is
\max_{r}|K_{12}(r)-\pi r^{2}|
. Radius queries use
scipy.spatial.KDTree
for
O(n\log n)
neighbour search.

6.3
Distance covariance

The distance covariance test of Székely et al.
[
18
]
computes
n\cdot\mathrm{dCov}_{n}^{2}=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ij}/n^{2}
, where
A
and
B
are double-centered distance matrices. Permutation inference is used with the same adaptive early-stopping rule as the Mantel test.

6.4
Permutation test for CvM

The
cvm_permutation_pvalue
function computes a permutation-based
p
-value for the CvM statistic. A key optimisation: the
U
-side kernel matrix
\hat{G}_{U}
is precomputed once and reused across all permutations. Only the
V
-side kernel
\hat{G}_{V}
is recomputed for each permuted
V
, reducing the per-permutation cost by approximately half. The
p
-value is computed as
(\text{count}+1)/(B_{\mathrm{perm}}+1)
.

8
Design Decisions and Implementation Notes

8.1
Inner form vs. true-CDF form

The default
use_inner_form=True
computes the kernel using empirical marginals. Under
H_{0}
, the inner form and true-CDF form differ by
R_{n}=o_{P}(1)
by the Glivenko–Cantelli theorem (Proposition
11.16
, Step 4). However, only the inner form admits the exact product-kernel factorisation
\hat{Q}=\hat{G}_{U}\otimes\hat{G}_{V}
that underpins the eigenvalue decomposition. The true-CDF form is available via
use_inner_form=False
for comparison but should not be used for inference.

8.2
Circulant embedding normalisation

After extracting the
m\times m
subfield, the code normalises to exact variance
\sigma^{2}
via
X\leftarrow X\cdot(\sigma/\mathrm{std}(X))
. This corrects for discretisation artifacts in the circulant embedding but does not affect the correlation structure or the PIT transform (which depends only on the marginal distribution).

8.3
Shared
t
-copula scaling

The
t
-copula draws a single
S\sim\chi^{2}(\nu_{t})/\nu_{t}
shared across all spatial locations. This preserves the Matérn spatial structure within the copula. An alternative implementation drawing
S_{i}
independently at each location would destroy the spatial dependence in the copula.

8.4
Divergent centering constant for optimal normal weight

The centering constant
\mu=(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}1/j)^{2}
diverges for the optimal normal weight. The code handles this by computing
\mu_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i}Q_{ii}
per replication, which converges almost surely by the strong law. However, the resulting critical values are very large (Table
3
: 95% quantile
=4.17
), leading to near-zero power at weak alternatives (Table
6
: power
=0.000
at
\rho=0.1
).

8.5
Permutation test optimisation

For
cvm_permutation_pvalue
, the
U
-side kernel
\hat{G}_{U}
is precomputed and reused across all permutations. Only the
V
-side kernel
\hat{G}_{V}
needs recomputation for each permuted
V
, reducing the per-permutation cost by approximately half.

8.6
Adaptive early stopping

The Mantel and cross-
K
tests use adaptive early stopping: after
B_{\min}=99
permutations, if the running
p
-value estimate is more than
\epsilon=0.02
away from the nominal level
\alpha
, the permutation loop terminates early. This significantly reduces computation time in power studies where most null cases are clearly non-significant.

8.7
Eigenvalue truncation

The limit distribution simulation uses
J=K=200
eigenvalues. The rapid decay of
\lambda_{j}
(especially for uniform weight, where
\lambda_{j}=1/(\pi j)^{2}
) ensures negligible truncation error:

\sum_{j=201}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(\pi j)^{2}}<10^{-3}.

(8.1)

For the Anderson–Darling weight,
\lambda_{j}=1/[j(j+1)]\sim 1/j^{2}
, and the truncation error is similarly small.

9
Verification and Numerical Accuracy

9.1
Critical value verification

The function
verify_table1()
in
critical_values.py
recomputes the asymptotic critical values from scratch and compares them against the hard-coded reference values in
TABLE1
. With
J=200
eigenvalues and
n_{\mathrm{mc}}=200\,000
replications, the Monte Carlo standard error is approximately
0.01
for the 95% quantile, well within the tolerance of
\pm 0.10
used for verification.

9.2
Inner form vs. true-CDF comparison

Under
H_{0}
, the inner form and true-CDF form should produce nearly identical statistics. Running both forms on the same data and reporting the maximum relative difference
\max_{i}|T_{n}^{\mathrm{inner}}-T_{n}^{\mathrm{true}}|/|T_{n}^{\mathrm{true}}|
confirms agreement to within numerical precision (
<10^{-10}
for
n=400
).

9.3
Variogram accuracy

The empirical variogram of the generated Matérn field is compared against the theoretical Matérn covariance (
5.1
). For
n=400
and
\nu=2
, the maximum relative error in the variogram is
<5\%
at distances up to half the effective range, confirming that the circulant embedding correctly reproduces the spatial dependence structure.

11
Asymptotic Distribution Theory

The preceding sections documented the software implementation and simulation evidence. We now develop the full asymptotic theory for the spatial CvM test statistic, justifying the critical values and establishing the limit distribution under spatial dependence. The presentation follows the U-statistic route (Gregory
[
10
]
; Yoshihara
[
23
]
), which yields the limit distribution from scalar convergence alone, bypassing the functional CLT.

11.1
Setup and definitions

11.1.1
Spatial random fields

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space. A
random field
is a collection
X=\{X_{s}:s\in\mathsf{Loc}\}
of real-valued random variables indexed by locations in
\mathsf{Loc}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For each
s\in\mathsf{Loc}
and threshold
x\in\mathbb{R}
define the indicator process

\mathbf{1}_{s}(x,\omega):=\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{s}(\omega)\leq x\}}.

(11.1)

Definition 11.1

(Stationarity)
.

The field
X
is
stationary
if for all
s,t\in\mathsf{Loc}
and all
x\in\mathbb{R}
:

\mathbb{P}(X_{s}\leq x)=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\leq x).

The
spatial covariance
of the indicator process is

\gamma(t,x,y):=\mathsf{Cov}\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{0}(x),\mathbf{1}_{t}(y)\bigr)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{0}(x)\mathbf{1}_{t}(y)\bigr]-\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{0}(x)\bigr]\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{t}(y)\bigr].

(11.2)

11.1.2
Kernel smoothing

Definition 11.2

(Probability kernel)
.

A Borel-measurable function
K:\mathsf{Loc}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
probability kernel
if there exist constants
C,r>0
such that
[
21
]
:

1.

K(u)\geq 0
for all
u\in\mathsf{Loc}
,

2.

|K(u)|\leq C
for all
u\in\mathsf{Loc}
,

3.

K(-u)=K(u)
(symmetry),

4.

K(u)=0
when
\|u\|>r
(compact support),

5.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{Loc}}K(u)\,du=1
(normalisation),

6.

K
is continuous on
\mathsf{Loc}
.

The scaled kernel at bandwidth
h>0
is
K_{h}(u):=h^{-2}K(u/h)
. Given a spatial lattice
L\subset\mathsf{Loc}
with mesh
\mathsf{mesh}(L)
and a reference location
s_{0}\in\mathsf{Loc}
, the kernel-smoothed empirical CDF at threshold
x
is

\widehat{F}_{n,h}(x):=\mathsf{mesh}(L)^{2}\sum_{j\in L}K_{h}(j-s_{0})\,\mathbf{1}_{j}(x).

(11.3)

11.1.3
Spatial lattices

Definition 11.3

(Spatial lattice)
.

A
spatial lattice
is a finite set
L\subset\mathsf{Loc}
equipped with a mesh parameter
\mathsf{mesh}(L)>0
. A sequence
\{L_{n}\}
of lattices is
quasi-uniform
if there exist constants
c,C>0
such that for every
n
and every
j\in L_{n}
, the Voronoi cell of
j
satisfies:

1.

c\cdot\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})\leq\mathsf{diam}(V_{j})\leq C\cdot\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})
,

2.

\lambda(V_{j})=\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})^{2}
.

11.1.4
Mixing conditions

Definition 11.4

(
\alpha
-Mixing)
.

For Borel sets
A,B\subset\mathsf{Loc}
define

\alpha(A,B):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}E_{A}\in\sigma(X_{s}:s\in A)\\
E_{B}\in\sigma(X_{t}:t\in B)\end{subarray}}\bigl|\mathbb{P}(E_{A}\cap E_{B})-\mathbb{P}(E_{A})\mathbb{P}(E_{B})\bigr|.

The
\alpha
-mixing coefficient at distance
d
is
\alpha(d):=\sup\{\alpha(A,B):\mathsf{dist}(A,B)\geq d\}
.

Definition 11.5

(
\beta
-Mixing (absolute regularity))
.

For
n\geq 1
, the
\beta
-mixing coefficient at distance
d
is

\beta(d):=\sup\bigl\{\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl\|P_{A\times B}-P_{A}\otimes P_{B}\bigr\|_{\mathrm{TV}}:\mathsf{dist}(A,B)\geq d\bigr\}.

The mixing coefficients satisfy
\alpha(d)\leq\beta(d)\leq\phi(d)

[
6
]
, so absolute regularity is strictly stronger than
\alpha
-mixing. For Gaussian random fields with integrable spectral density, polynomial
\alpha
-mixing implies polynomial
\beta
-mixing at the same rate
[
6
,
3
]
.

Assumption 11.6

(Polynomial mixing rate)
.

There exist constants
C_{\beta}>0
,
\delta>0
, and
\theta>2(2+\delta)/\delta
such that the
\beta
-mixing coefficient satisfies

\beta(d)\leq C_{\beta}\,d^{-\theta}\quad\text{for all }d\geq 1.

(11.4)

11.2
Covariance bounds and integrability

Lemma 11.7

(Covariance bound)
.

For all
t\in\mathsf{Loc}
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
:
|\gamma(t,x,y)|\leq 1
.

Proof.

Set
U:=\mathbf{1}_{0}(x)
and
V:=\mathbf{1}_{t}(y)
. Then
U,V:\Omega\to\{0,1\}
, so
|U-\mathbb{E}[U]|\leq 1
and
|V-\mathbb{E}[V]|\leq 1
pointwise. Hence
|\gamma(t,x,y)|\leq\mathbb{E}[|(U-\mathbb{E}[U])(V-\mathbb{E}[V])|]\leq 1
.
∎

Lemma 11.8

(Popoviciu bound)
.

Under the same hypotheses:
|\gamma(t,x,y)|\leq 1/4
.

Proof.

Write
\gamma=\mathbb{E}[UV]-\mathbb{E}[U]\mathbb{E}[V]
and set
a:=\mathbb{E}[U]
,
b:=\mathbb{E}[V]
,
c:=\mathbb{E}[UV]
. The Fréchet bounds give
\max\{0,a+b-1\}\leq c\leq\min\{a,b\}
. Case analysis on
a\leq b
and
a+b\leq 1
yields
|c-ab|\leq 1/4
.
∎

Lemma 11.9

(Effective exponent)
.

Under Assumption
11.6
,
\rho:=\theta\delta/(2+\delta)>2
.

Proof.

From
\theta>2(2+\delta)/\delta
and
\delta>0
:
\theta\delta>2(2+\delta)
, hence
\theta\delta/(2+\delta)>2
.
∎

Theorem 11.10

(Decay-rate sufficiency)
.

Under Assumption
11.6
:
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\gamma(t,x,y)|\,dt<\infty
.

Proof.

Step 1: Davydov’s inequality.
By Davydov’s inequality
[
4
]
applied to the
\alpha
-mixing coefficient (which satisfies
\alpha(d)\leq\beta(d)
), with
p=q=2+\delta
:

|\gamma(t,x,y)|\leq 8\,\alpha(\|t\|)^{\delta/(2+\delta)}.

(11.5)

Step 2: Polynomial substitution.
From
\alpha(d)\leq C_{\alpha}d^{-\theta}
:

|\gamma(t,x,y)|\leq 8\,C_{\alpha}^{\delta/(2+\delta)}\,\|t\|^{-\rho}\quad\text{for }\|t\|\geq 1.

(11.6)

Step 3: Splitting the integral.
By Lemma
11.7
:
\int_{\|t\|<1}|\gamma|\,dt\leq\pi<\infty
.

Step 4: Annular-shell integration.
Set
A_{k}:=\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:2^{k}\leq\|t\|<2^{k+1}\}
. Since
\lambda(A_{k})=3\pi\cdot 4^{k}
and
\|t\|^{-\rho}\leq 2^{-k\rho}
on
A_{k}
:

\int_{\|t\|\geq 1}\|t\|^{-\rho}\,dt=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{A_{k}}\|t\|^{-\rho}\,dt\leq 3\pi\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{k(2-\rho)}=\frac{3\pi}{1-2^{2-\rho}}<\infty

because
\rho>2
by Lemma
11.9
. Combining Steps 1–4:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\gamma(t,x,y)|\,dt\leq\pi+8\,C_{\alpha}^{\delta/(2+\delta)}\cdot\frac{3\pi}{1-2^{2-\rho}}<\infty.\qed

11.3
Asymptotic covariance

For a lattice
L
with mesh
\mathsf{mesh}(L)
and reference point
s_{0}
, define the approximate covariance

\mathsf{Cov}_{n}(x,y):=\mathsf{mesh}(L)^{4}\sum_{j,\ell\in L}K_{h}(j-s_{0})\,K_{h}(\ell-s_{0})\,\gamma(j-\ell,\,x,\,y).

(11.7)

Theorem 11.11

(Asymptotic covariance)
.

Under Assumption
11.6
with kernel
K
and bandwidth
h>0
, for a quasi-uniform sequence
\{L_{n}\}
with
\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})\to 0
:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{Cov}_{n}(x,y)=\Sigma_{h}(x,y),

where

\Sigma_{h}(x,y):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\gamma(t,x,y)\,\psi_{h}(t)\,dt,\qquad\psi_{h}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K_{h}(u)\,K_{h}(u+t)\,du.

(11.8)

Proof.

Step 1: Double Riemann-sum reformulation.
Define
g(t_{1},t_{2}):=\gamma(t_{1}-t_{2},\,x,\,y)\,K_{h}(t_{1}-s_{0})\,K_{h}(t_{2}-s_{0})
. Because
K_{h}
is continuous with compact support and
\gamma
is bounded (Lemma
11.7
),
g
is continuous and compactly supported on
\mathsf{Loc}\times\mathsf{Loc}
.

Step 2: Double Riemann-sum convergence.
By the quasi-uniformity of
\{L_{n}\}
and the uniform continuity of
g
on its compact support (Theorem
A.3
in Appendix
A
):

\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})^{4}\sum_{j,\ell\in L_{n}}g(j,\ell)\longrightarrow\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}g(t_{1},t_{2})\,dt_{1}\,dt_{2}.

Step 3: Change of variables.
Transform the double integral using
u=t_{1}-t_{2}
,
v=t_{1}
. The Jacobian determinant is 1, so the change of variables is volume-preserving. Writing
t_{2}=v-u
and substituting:

\displaystyle\iint g(t_{1},t_{2})\,dt_{1}\,dt_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\gamma(u,x,y)\,\psi_{h}(u)\,du=\Sigma_{h}(x,y).\qed

11.4
U-statistic reformulation

Under
H_{0}
with PIT-transformed observations
Z_{i}=(U_{i},V_{i})
on
[0,1]^{2}
, the spatial CvM statistic is

T_{n}=n\iint\bigl(H_{n}(u,v)-F_{1,n}(u)F_{2,n}(v)\bigr)^{2}\,W_{1}(u)W_{2}(v)\,du\,dv,

(11.9)

where
H_{n}(u,v)=n^{-1}\sum_{i}\mathbf{1}_{\{U_{i}\leq u,\,V_{i}\leq v\}}
is the bivariate empirical CDF. This is the
inner
form of the CvM statistic. The spatial dependence is preserved by the PIT (since the transform is monotone and hence measurable), and the
\beta
-mixing rate of Assumption
11.6
is invariant under measurable transformations of each marginal
[
6
]
. The kernel smoother
K_{h}
and lattice
L_{n}
are absorbed into the Riemann-sum convergence and do not appear in the U-statistic kernel
Q
; they affect only the rate at which the spatial covariance approaches its limit.

Remark 11.12

(Role of the kernel smoother)
.

The kernel smoother
K_{h}
enters the theory through two channels: (i) the Riemann-sum convergence of Theorem
11.11
, which justifies replacing lattice sums by integrals, and (ii) the same-marginal covariances
\sigma^{(i)}_{jj^{\prime}}=\sum_{d}\mathbb{E}[g^{(i)}_{j}(X_{1})g^{(i)}_{j^{\prime}}(X_{1+d})]
in the limit distribution, which depend on the spatial correlation structure. In the small-bandwidth limit
h\to 0
, the auto-convolution
\psi_{h}\to\delta
concentrates at the origin and
\sigma^{(i)}_{jj^{\prime}}\to\delta_{jj^{\prime}}
, yielding the i.i.d. limit with critical values given in Table
3
. For fixed
h>0
, the same-marginal covariances are non-trivial and depend on the kernel bandwidth. The simulations in Section
7
use the inner-form statistic (
11.9
) computed on the PIT-transformed data without explicit kernel smoothing; this corresponds to the small-bandwidth limit
h\to 0
, which is the standard setting for the CvM test in the independence-testing literature
[
8
,
10
]
. The kernel
K_{h}
is a theoretical device for extending the test to spatially smoothed empirical processes; for the independence test on lattice data, the inner-form statistic with PIT-transformed marginals is the natural choice.

Assumption 11.13

(Marginal regularity)
.

The marginal CDFs
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are continuous and strictly increasing on
\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 11.14

(U-statistic kernel)
.

For observations
Z_{i}=(U_{i},V_{i})
on
[0,1]^{2}
, define the product kernel

Q(Z_{i},Z_{j}):=G_{1}(U_{i},U_{j})\cdot G_{2}(V_{i},V_{j}),

(11.10)

where the marginal CvM kernels are

G_{W}(s,t):=\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(\mathbf{1}(s<u)-u\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{1}(t<u)-u\bigr)W(u)\,du.

(11.11)

Assumption 11.15

(Weight-function regularity)
.

For
i=1,2
, the weight function
W_{i}
satisfies:

1.

W_{i}
is continuous and differentiable on
(0,1)
,

2.

There exist constants
C_{i},D_{i},\delta_{W}\in(0,1/2)
such that:

W_{i}(u)\leq C_{i}\,\bigl(u(1-u)\bigr)^{-\delta_{W}}\bigl|\log\bigl(u(1-u)\bigr)\bigr|^{i-1}.

Proposition 11.16

(U-statistic form)
.

The spatial CvM statistic satisfies

T_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q(Z_{i},Z_{j})+R_{n},

(11.12)

where
R_{n}=o_{P}(1)
.

Proof.

The proof has four steps.

Step 1: Key identity for the inner form.
The centred process with empirical marginals satisfies the exact decomposition

H_{n}(u,v)-F_{1,n}(u)F_{2,n}(v)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{U_{i}}(u)-F_{1,n}(u)\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{V_{i}}(v)-F_{2,n}(v)\bigr),

(11.13)

where
\mathbf{1}_{U_{i}}(u):=\mathbf{1}_{\{U_{i}\leq u\}}
and
\mathbf{1}_{V_{i}}(v):=\mathbf{1}_{\{V_{i}\leq v\}}
. This follows by expanding the right-hand side:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{U_{i}}(u)-F_{1,n}(u)\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{V_{i}}(v)-F_{2,n}(v)\bigr)

\displaystyle=H_{n}(u,v)-F_{1,n}(u)F_{2,n}(v)

\displaystyle\quad-F_{2,n}(v)F_{1,n}(u)+F_{1,n}(u)F_{2,n}(v)

\displaystyle=H_{n}(u,v)-F_{1,n}(u)F_{2,n}(v).

This product decomposition is the key advantage of the inner form; the outer form
H_{n}(u,v)-uv
does not admit such a decomposition (Remark
11.17
).

Step 2: Substitute and expand.
Substituting (
11.13
) into (
11.9
):

\displaystyle T_{n}

\displaystyle=n\iint\biggl[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{U_{i}}(u)-F_{1,n}(u)\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{V_{i}}(v)-F_{2,n}(v)\bigr)\biggr]^{2}W_{1}W_{2}\,du\,dv

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j}\iint\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{U_{i}}(u)-F_{1,n}(u)\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{V_{i}}(v)-F_{2,n}(v)\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{U_{j}}(u)-F_{1,n}(u)\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{V_{j}}(v)-F_{2,n}(v)\bigr)\,W_{1}W_{2}\,du\,dv.

Step 3: Approximate empirical marginals by true marginals.
Under
H_{0}
,
F_{1}(u)=u
and
F_{2}(v)=v
. Write
r_{n}(u):=F_{1,n}(u)-u
and
s_{n}(v):=F_{2,n}(v)-v
. Then
\mathbf{1}_{U_{i}}(u)-F_{1,n}(u)=(\mathbf{1}_{U_{i}}(u)-u)-r_{n}(u)
, and similarly for
V_{i}
. Expanding the four-factor product and collecting the leading term (all factors use true marginals):

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j}\iint\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{U_{i}}(u)-u\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{V_{i}}(v)-v\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{U_{j}}(u)-u\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{V_{j}}(v)-v\bigr)\\
\times W_{1}(u)W_{2}(v)\,du\,dv=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j}Q(Z_{i},Z_{j}),

(11.14)

by the definition of
Q=G_{1}\otimes G_{2}
and the factorization of the double integral into marginal integrals:

\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{U_{i}}(u)-u\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{U_{j}}(u)-u\bigr)W_{1}(u)\,du\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{V_{i}}(v)-v\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{V_{j}}(v)-v\bigr)W_{2}(v)\,dv\\
=G_{1}(U_{i},U_{j})\cdot G_{2}(V_{i},V_{j}).

Step 4: Bound the remainder.
The cross-product terms in the expansion of Step 3 involve at least one factor of
r_{n}(u)
or
s_{n}(v)
. By the Glivenko–Cantelli theorem for
\alpha
-mixing fields (which holds under Assumption
11.6
since
\sum_{d}\alpha(d)<\infty
implies a.s. uniform convergence; see
[
6
]
, Theorem 1.5, and
[
7
]
):
\|r_{n}\|_{\infty}=\|F_{1,n}-F_{1}\|_{\infty}=O_{P}(n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{1/2})
and similarly
\|s_{n}\|_{\infty}=O_{P}(n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{1/2})
. For integrable weights (
\delta_{W}<1/2
), Cauchy–Schwarz and the integrability of
W_{1}W_{2}
yield each cross-product term as
O_{P}(n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{1/2})
. For the Anderson–Darling weight (
\delta_{W}=1
, which violates condition (A2)), the spectral representation
G_{W}(s,t)=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}g_{j}(s)g_{j}(t)
with
\lambda_{j}=1/[j(j+1)]
allows the remainder to be bounded in terms of the eigenvalue sum: the cross-product integrals factor through the marginal kernels, and the bound follows from
\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\|g_{j}\|_{\infty}^{2}<\infty
(which holds since
\|g_{j}\|_{\infty}=O(\sqrt{j})
and
\lambda_{j}=O(j^{-2})
). Collecting all remainder terms yields
R_{n}=O_{P}(n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{1/2})=o_{P}(1)
.
∎

Remark 11.17

(Inner and outer forms)
.

The inner form of the CvM statistic, defined in (
11.9
), centres the bivariate empirical CDF using the marginal empirical CDFs
F_{1,n}
,
F_{2,n}
. The outer form centres with the true marginals
F_{1}
,
F_{2}
:

T_{n}^{\text{out}}=n\iint\bigl(H_{n}(u,v)-F_{1}(u)F_{2}(v)\bigr)^{2}W_{1}(u)W_{2}(v)\,du\,dv.

Under
H_{0}
, the decomposition

H_{n}(u,v)-F_{1}(u)F_{2}(v)=\bigl(H_{n}(u,v)-F_{1,n}(u)F_{2,n}(v)\bigr)+\bigl(F_{1,n}(u)-F_{1}(u)\bigr)F_{2}(v)\\
+F_{1}(u)\bigl(F_{2,n}(v)-F_{2}(v)\bigr)+\bigl(F_{1,n}(u)-F_{1}(u)\bigr)\bigl(F_{2,n}(v)-F_{2}(v)\bigr)

shows that the two forms differ by
O_{P}(n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{1/2})
by the Glivenko–Cantelli theorem, hence they are asymptotically equivalent. The inner form is preferred because the centred process
H_{n}-F_{1,n}F_{2,n}
admits the product decomposition (
11.13
), which directly yields the product kernel
Q=G_{1}\otimes G_{2}
. The outer form’s centred process
H_{n}-F_{1}F_{2}
does not factor in this way: the bivariate residual
\mathbf{1}_{i}(u,v)-F_{1}(u)F_{2}(v)
decomposes as
(\mathbf{1}_{U_{i}}(u)-F_{1}(u))(\mathbf{1}_{V_{i}}(v)-F_{2}(v))+F_{1}(u)(\mathbf{1}_{V_{i}}(v)-F_{2}(v))+F_{2}(v)(\mathbf{1}_{U_{i}}(u)-F_{1}(u))
, introducing marginal terms that prevent factoring.

Lemma 11.18

(Centering constant)
.

The diagonal terms satisfy
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q(Z_{i},Z_{i})\xrightarrow{P}\mu:=\mathbb{E}[Q(Z_{1},Z_{1})]
, where

\mu=\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{(1)}_{j}\biggr)\biggl(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{(2)}_{k}\biggr)=\operatorname{trace}(T_{Q}).

Proof.

The finiteness of
\mu
follows from
|G_{W}(s,s)|\leq\int_{0}^{1}W(u)\,du<\infty
for integrable weights, and from the spectral representation
G_{W}(s,s)=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}g_{j}(s)^{2}
for singular weights, where
g_{j}
are the eigenfunctions. The convergence
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}Q(Z_{i},Z_{i})\to\mathbb{E}[Q(Z_{1},Z_{1})]
a.s. follows from the ergodic theorem for
\alpha
-mixing fields with
\sum_{d}\alpha(d)<\infty

[
6
]
, Theorem 1.5.
∎

Proposition 11.19

(Centering constant values)
.

Under
H_{0}
with uniform marginals on
[0,1]^{2}
:

1.

Uniform
(
W_{1}=W_{2}=1
):
\mu=1/36
.

2.

Optimal normal
:
\mu=(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{-1})^{2}
, which diverges.

3.

Anderson–Darling

\mu=(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}[j(j+1)]^{-1})^{2}=1
.

Proposition 11.20

(Degeneracy)
.

Under
H_{0}
:
\mathbb{E}[Q(Z_{1},z)]=0
for all
z=(x,y)
.

Proof.

By the product structure
Q=G_{1}\otimes G_{2}
:
\mathbb{E}[Q(Z_{1},z)]=\mathbb{E}[G_{1}(U_{1},x)]\cdot\mathbb{E}[G_{2}(V_{1},y)]
. For each marginal:
\mathbb{E}[G_{W}(U_{1},x)]=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(\mathbf{1}(r<s)-s)(\mathbf{1}(x<s)-s)W(s)\,ds\,dr=0
, since
\int_{0}^{1}(\mathbf{1}(r<s)-s)\,dr=0
for every
s
.
∎

Proposition 11.21

(Square-integrability)
.

Under Assumption
11.6
and weight conditions (A1)–(A2) with
\delta_{W}<1/2
:
\mathbb{E}[Q(Z_{1},Z_{2})^{2}]<\infty
.

Proof.

By the product structure and independence under
H_{0}
:

\mathbb{E}[Q(Z_{1},Z_{2})^{2}]=\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(\lambda^{(1)}_{j})^{2}\biggr)\biggl(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(\lambda^{(2)}_{k})^{2}\biggr).

For the three standard weight functions: (a) Uniform:
\sum_{j}(\pi j)^{-4}=1/90<\infty
; (b) Optimal normal:
\sum_{j}j^{-2}=\pi^{2}/6<\infty
; (c) Anderson–Darling:
\sum_{j}[j(j+1)]^{-2}<\infty
by comparison with
\sum_{j}j^{-4}
.
∎

Proposition 11.22

(Higher moment condition)
.

Under Assumption
11.6
, the weight conditions (A1)–(A2), and the eigenvalue summability
\sum_{j}(\lambda_{j}^{(i)})^{(2+\delta)/2}<\infty
:
\mathbb{E}[|Q(Z_{1},Z_{2})|^{2+\delta}]<\infty
.

Proof.

For integrable weights,
|G_{W}(s,t)|\leq\int_{0}^{1}W(u)\,du=C<\infty
, so
\mathbb{E}[|G_{W}|^{2+\delta}]\leq C^{2+\delta}
. For singular weights, by the spectral representation and the Marcinkiewicz–Zygmund inequality,
\mathbb{E}[|G_{W}|^{2+\delta}]\leq C_{\delta}(\sum_{j}(\lambda^{(i)}_{j})^{(2+\delta)/2})^{2}<\infty
.
∎

Proposition 11.23

(Tail condition)
.

Under the conditions of Proposition
11.21
:

t^{2}\mathbb{P}\bigl(|Q(Z_{1},Z_{2})|>t\bigr)\to 0\quad\text{as }t\to\infty.

Proof.

By Markov’s inequality and Proposition
11.22
:

t^{2}\mathbb{P}\bigl(|Q(Z_{1},Z_{2})|>t\bigr)\leq t^{2}\,\frac{\mathbb{E}[|Q|^{2+\delta}]}{t^{2+\delta}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[|Q|^{2+\delta}]}{t^{\delta}}\to 0.\qed

Lemma 11.24

(Lattice-to-sequence enumeration)
.

Let
\{L_{n}\}
be a quasi-uniform sequence of spatial lattices on an observation region
\Omega_{n}\subset\mathsf{Loc}
with
\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})\to 0
, and suppose
\Omega_{n}
contains a square of side length
\ell_{n}
with
\ell_{n}/\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})\to\infty
. Then there exists an enumeration of
L_{n}
as a sequence
s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{n}
such that the sequential
\beta
-mixing coefficients satisfy

\beta_{\text{seq}}(k)\leq C\,k^{-\theta/2}\quad\text{for all }k\geq 1,

where
C
depends on the quasi-uniformity constants
c,C
from Definition
11.3
and on the aspect ratio of
\Omega_{n}
.

Proof.

The proof uses a blocking argument to construct an enumeration that controls the spatial distance between sequentially separated points.

Step 1: Ball-counting bound.
By the quasi-uniformity assumption (Definition
11.3
), each lattice point
j\in L_{n}
has a Voronoi cell
V_{j}
with area
\lambda(V_{j})=\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})^{2}
and diameter
\mathsf{diam}(V_{j})\leq C\cdot\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})
. The Voronoi cells tile the observation region, so any ball of radius
r
in
\mathsf{Loc}
contains at most

N(r)\leq\frac{\pi(r+C\cdot\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n}))^{2}}{\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})^{2}}\leq C_{\mathrm{ball}}\cdot\frac{r^{2}}{\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})^{2}}

lattice points, where
C_{\mathrm{ball}}
depends only on the quasi-uniformity constant
C
. In particular, for
r\geq\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})
, we have
N(r)\leq C_{1}r^{2}/\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})^{2}
for a constant
C_{1}
depending only on
C
.

Step 2: Space-filling curve enumeration.
We construct the enumeration using a hierarchical blocking scheme on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Partition the observation region
\Omega_{n}
into square blocks of side length
\ell_{n}/m
, where
m=\lceil(\ell_{n}/\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n}))^{1/2}\rceil
. Each block contains at most
C_{2}(\ell_{n}/(m\cdot\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})))^{2}=O(\ell_{n}/\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n)})
lattice points. Enumerate the blocks in row-major order, and within each block, enumerate the lattice points arbitrarily. For two indices
i,j
with
|i-j|\geq k
, the points
s_{i}
and
s_{j}
lie in blocks that are at least
\lfloor k/(C_{2}\ell_{n}/\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n}))\rfloor-1
blocks apart in row-major order. Since each block has side length
\ell_{n}/m\geq\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})
, the Euclidean distance between
s_{i}
and
s_{j}
satisfies:

\|s_{i}-s_{j}\|\geq c^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})\cdot k^{1/2},

where
c^{\prime\prime}>0
depends on
C_{1}
and the aspect ratio of
\Omega_{n}
. (This follows because
k
consecutive lattice points in the enumeration span at least
k/C_{2}
blocks in row-major order, and the spatial distance between the first and last block grows as the square root of the number of blocks for a compact region in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.)

Step 3: Mixing rate bound.
Under Assumption
11.6
,
\beta(d)\leq C_{\beta}\,d^{-\theta}
. Substituting
d=c^{\prime\prime}\,\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})\,k^{1/2}
:

\beta_{\text{seq}}(k):=\beta\bigl(c^{\prime\prime}\,\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})\,k^{1/2}\bigr)\leq C_{\beta}\bigl(c^{\prime\prime}\,\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})\,k^{1/2}\bigr)^{-\theta}\\
=C_{\beta}(c^{\prime\prime}\,\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n}))^{-\theta}\,k^{-\theta/2}.

The factor
(c^{\prime\prime}\,\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n}))^{-\theta}
depends on
n
through
\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})
, but for each fixed
n
it is a finite constant. Yoshihara’s
[
23
]
theorem is applied for each fixed
n
, so this
n
-dependent constant enters the finite-sample approximation but not the asymptotic limit. The sequential mixing condition
\beta_{\text{seq}}(k)=O(k^{-\theta/2})
satisfies Yoshihara’s requirement
\theta/2>(2+\delta^{\prime})/\delta^{\prime}
because Assumption
11.6
gives
\theta>2(2+\delta)/\delta
, and for any
\delta^{\prime}<\delta
, we have
\theta/2>(2+\delta)/\delta\geq(2+\delta^{\prime})/\delta^{\prime}
.
∎

Remark 11.25

(On the enumeration constant)
.

The constant
C
in Lemma
11.24
depends on
n
through the factor
(\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n}))^{-\theta}
. For each fixed
n
, Yoshihara’s theorem applies with the
n
-dependent constant, and the limit
n\to\infty
is taken subsequently. An alternative approach, following Doukhan
[
6
, Section 2.3]
, uses a blocking argument that groups lattice points into independent blocks separated by buffer zones, avoiding the enumeration problem entirely; the resulting rate is the same.

11.5
Limit distribution

We combine the U-statistic structure established in Section
11.4
with the degenerate U-statistic limit theory of Gregory
[
10
]
and its extension to mixing sequences by Yoshihara
[
23
]
. The key result is that under sufficient mixing decay, the limit distribution of a degenerate U-statistic is the same for dependent data as for independent data.

Remark 11.26

(Mixing condition usage)
.

Throughout the paper, two mixing conditions play distinct roles: (i)
\alpha
-mixing
is used in Section
11.2
for Davydov’s inequality, which bounds covariances by
\alpha(d)^{\delta/(2+\delta)}
; since
\alpha(d)\leq\beta(d)
, the
\alpha
-mixing bound is implied by Assumption
11.6
but is strictly weaker. (ii)
\beta
-mixing
is used in the present section for Yoshihara’s
[
23
]
U-statistic limit theorem, which requires the stronger
\beta
-mixing rate
\beta(k)=O(k^{-\theta/2})
. Assumption
11.6
imposes
\beta
-mixing, which subsumes both requirements; the
\alpha
-mixing results are stated separately for clarity and because they hold under weaker hypotheses.

Remark 11.27
.

For Gaussian random fields with spectral density
f
satisfying
\int(1+\|\lambda\|^{2})^{\theta/2}|f(\lambda)|^{2}\,d\lambda<\infty
, Assumption
11.6
follows from polynomial
\alpha
-mixing at the same rate
[
6
]
. For Markov random fields on lattices with finite state space,
\alpha
-mixing and
\beta
-mixing are equivalent
[
3
]
.

Theorem 11.28

(Limit distribution of the spatial CvM statistic)
.

Let
X
be a stationary
\beta
-mixing random field on
\mathsf{Loc}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfying Assumption
11.6
with effective decay exponent
\rho=\theta\delta/(2+\delta)>2
. Let
K
be a probability kernel,
h>0
a bandwidth, and
\{L_{n}\}
a quasi-uniform sequence of spatial lattices with
\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})\to 0
. Let the weight functions
W_{1}
,
W_{2}
satisfy Assumption
11.15
with
\delta_{W}<1/2
. Assume the marginal CDFs satisfy Assumption
11.13
. Then under
H_{0}
:

T_{n}-\mu_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{(1)}_{j}\,\lambda^{(2)}_{k}\,\bigl(\widetilde{N}^{2}_{jk}-1\bigr),

(11.15)

where:

1.

\mu_{n}:=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q(Z_{i},Z_{i})\to\mu
almost surely (Lemma
11.18
);

2.

\{\widetilde{N}_{jk}\}
are centred jointly Gaussian random variables with covariance structure
\mathsf{Cov}(\widetilde{N}_{jk},\widetilde{N}_{j^{\prime}k^{\prime}})=\sigma_{(jk),(j^{\prime}k^{\prime})}
;

3.

\{\lambda^{(1)}_{j}\}
,
\{\lambda^{(2)}_{k}\}
are the eigenvalues of the marginal CvM kernels
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
(Section
11.6
).

In the small-bandwidth limit (
h\to 0
),
\sigma_{(jk),(j^{\prime}k^{\prime})}=\delta_{jj^{\prime}}\delta_{kk^{\prime}}
and
\widetilde{N}_{jk}\equiv N_{jk}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}N(0,1)
. The critical values in Table
3
correspond to this i.i.d. limit.

Proof.

The proof proceeds in four stages.

Stage 1: U-statistic decomposition.
By Proposition
11.16
:

T_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q(Z_{i},Z_{j})+R_{n},

(11.16)

where
R_{n}=o_{P}(1)
. Separate the diagonal and off-diagonal terms:

T_{n}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q(Z_{i},Z_{i})}_{\mu_{n}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\neq j}Q(Z_{i},Z_{j})}_{U_{n}}+R_{n}.

(11.17)

By Proposition
11.20
,
Q
is degenerate:
\mathbb{E}[Q(Z_{1},z)]=0
for all
z
. By Proposition
11.21
,
Q
is square-integrable. By Proposition
11.22
,
\mathbb{E}[|Q(Z_{1},Z_{2})|^{2+\delta}]<\infty
. By Proposition
11.23
, the tail condition
t^{2}\mathbb{P}(|Q|>t)\to 0
holds. It therefore suffices to show that
U_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\sum_{j,k}\lambda^{(1)}_{j}\lambda^{(2)}_{k}(N^{2}_{jk}-1)
.

Stage 2: Hoeffding decomposition under dependence.
Define the Hoeffding projections of
Q
:

\displaystyle Q_{1}(z)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}[Q(Z_{1},z)]=0\quad\text{(by degeneracy)},

(11.18)

\displaystyle Q_{2}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle:=Q(z_{1},z_{2})-Q_{1}(z_{1})-Q_{1}(z_{2})+\mathbb{E}[Q]=Q(z_{1},z_{2})-\mathbb{E}[Q].

(11.19)

The projection
Q_{1}\equiv 0
by degeneracy. Under independence (
H_{0}
), the standard Hoeffding decomposition of the U-statistic
V_{n}=\binom{n}{2}^{-1}\sum_{i<j}Q(Z_{i},Z_{j})
yields only a quadratic term:

V_{n}=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{i<j}Q(Z_{i},Z_{j}).

(11.20)

Under
dependence
, the terms in the Hoeffding decomposition are no longer orthogonal. Following Yoshihara
[
23
]
, we control the cross-covariances using the
\beta
-mixing decay. For any Borel function
h
with
\mathbb{E}[|h|^{1+\delta}]<M
:

\biggl|\mathbb{E}\bigl[h(Z_{i_{1}},\ldots,Z_{i_{k}})h(Z_{j_{1}},\ldots,Z_{j_{k}})\bigr]-\mathbb{E}[h]^{2}\biggr|\leq 4M^{1/(1+\delta)}\,\beta(d)^{\delta/(1+\delta)},

(11.21)

where
d=\min_{a,b}\|s_{i_{a}}-s_{j_{b}}\|
is the minimum Euclidean distance between the two index sets (Yoshihara, Lemma 1). By Lemma
11.24
and Assumption
11.6
,
\beta_{\text{seq}}(k)=O(k^{-\theta/2})
with
\theta/2>(2+\delta^{\prime})/\delta^{\prime}
. For the degenerate kernel
Q
with
Q_{1}\equiv 0
, the Hoeffding decomposition under dependence yields:

V_{n}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{i<j}Q(Z_{i},Z_{j})}_{\text{quadratic term}}+\underbrace{\text{(higher-order terms)}}_{o_{P}(n^{-1/2})},

(11.22)

where the higher-order terms (products of three or more projections) satisfy
\mathbb{E}[(U_{n}^{(c)})^{2}]=O(n^{-1-\gamma_{c}})
with
\gamma_{c}>0
by Yoshihara’s Lemma 2. The key point is that under degeneracy (
Q_{1}\equiv 0
), the
linear term vanishes entirely
, so the quadratic term dominates regardless of the dependence structure. The cross-covariances between observations in the quadratic term are absorbed into the truncation bound (Stage 4), which uses Theorem
11.10
to ensure summability.

Stage 3: Centering and scaling.
Separate the diagonal and off-diagonal terms:

T_{n}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q(Z_{i},Z_{i})}_{\mu_{n}}+\underbrace{\frac{n-1}{n}V_{n}}_{\text{off-diagonal}}+R_{n}.

(11.23)

By Lemma
11.18
,
\mu_{n}\to\mu
a.s., so
T_{n}-\mu_{n}=\frac{n-1}{n}V_{n}+R_{n}
. It therefore suffices to show that
V_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\sum_{j,k}\lambda^{(1)}_{j}\lambda^{(2)}_{k}(N^{2}_{jk}-1)
.

Stage 4: Application of the degenerate U-statistic CLT for mixing sequences.
We verify the conditions of the combined Gregory–Yoshihara theorem.

Condition 1 (Degeneracy).
By Proposition
11.20
,
\mathbb{E}[Q(Z_{1},z)]=0
for all
z
. This is the key structural condition: it ensures that the linear term in the Hoeffding decomposition vanishes, so the quadratic term dominates.

Condition 2 (Square-integrability and moment conditions).
By Proposition
11.21
,
\mathbb{E}[Q^{2}]<\infty
. By Proposition
11.22
,
\mathbb{E}[|Q|^{2+\delta}]<\infty
for the
\delta>0
from Assumption
11.6
. By Proposition
11.23
,
t^{2}\mathbb{P}(|Q|>t)\to 0
.

Condition 3 (Mixing decay).
Assumption
11.6
directly imposes polynomial
\beta
-mixing decay at rate
\theta
. Yoshihara’s
[
23
]
Theorem 1 requires
\beta(n)=O(n^{-(2+\delta^{\prime})/\delta^{\prime}})
for some
0<\delta^{\prime}<\delta
. This holds when
\theta>(2+\delta^{\prime})/\delta^{\prime}
. Since Assumption
11.6
gives
\theta>2(2+\delta)/\delta
, we may choose any
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,\delta)
satisfying
\theta>(2+\delta^{\prime})/\delta^{\prime}
. Such a
\delta^{\prime}
exists because
\lim_{\delta^{\prime}\to\delta^{-}}(2+\delta^{\prime})/\delta^{\prime}=(2+\delta)/\delta<2(2+\delta)/\delta<\theta
.

Condition 4 (Higher-order terms are negligible).
By Yoshihara
[
23
]
, Lemma 2, the variance of the
c
-th order term in the Hoeffding decomposition satisfies:

\mathbb{E}\bigl[(U_{n}^{(c)})^{2}\bigr]=O\bigl(n^{-1-\gamma_{c}}\bigr),\qquad\gamma_{c}=\frac{2(\delta-\delta^{\prime})}{\delta^{\prime}(2+\delta)}>0,

(11.24)

for all
c\geq 2
(in the non-degenerate case). For the
degenerate
kernel
Q
with
Q_{1}\equiv 0
, the dominant term is the quadratic form
V_{n}=\binom{n}{2}^{-1}\sum_{i<j}Q(Z_{i},Z_{j})
, and the higher-order terms
U_{n}^{(c)}
for
c\geq 3
satisfy
\mathbb{E}[(U_{n}^{(c)})^{2}]=O(n^{-1-\gamma_{c}})
with
\gamma_{c}\geq\gamma_{2}>0
. Since
\gamma_{2}=2(\delta-\delta^{\prime})/[\delta^{\prime}(2+\delta)]
and
\delta^{\prime}<\delta
, we have
\gamma_{2}>0
. Thus
U_{n}^{(c)}=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
for all
c\geq 3
.

Condition 5 (Spectral representation of the quadratic term).
Since
Q
is a symmetric, degenerate, square-integrable kernel, the Hilbert–Schmidt theorem gives the spectral representation

Q(z_{1},z_{2})=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}\,\psi_{m}(z_{1})\,\psi_{m}(z_{2}),

(11.25)

where
\{\lambda_{m}\}
are the eigenvalues of the integral operator
T_{Q}
defined by
(T_{Q}f)(z)=\int Q(z,z^{\prime})f(z^{\prime})\,dP_{0}(z^{\prime})
and
\{\psi_{m}\}
are the corresponding orthonormal eigenfunctions. By the product structure
Q=G_{1}\otimes G_{2}
(Definition
11.14
), the eigenvalues factor as
\lambda_{m}=\lambda^{(1)}_{j}\lambda^{(2)}_{k}
with
m=(j,k)
(Theorem
11.31
).

Finite-rank approximation.
For each
M\geq 1
, define the truncated kernel
Q^{(M)}(z_{1},z_{2}):=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\lambda_{m}\psi_{m}(z_{1})\psi_{m}(z_{2})
and the corresponding U-statistic

V_{n}^{(M)}:=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{i<j}Q^{(M)}(Z_{i},Z_{j})=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\lambda_{m}\bigl(\zeta_{n,m}^{2}-1\bigr)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\lambda_{m}\psi_{m}(Z_{i})^{2},

(11.26)

where
\zeta_{n,m}:=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{m}(Z_{i})
.

By the multivariate CLT for stationary
\beta
-mixing sequences (see
[
11
]
, Theorem 2.5; also
[
6
]
, Theorem 1.5), for each fixed
M
, the vector
(\zeta_{n,1},\ldots,\zeta_{n,M})
converges in distribution to a centred Gaussian vector
(N_{1},\ldots,N_{M})
with covariance

\mathsf{Cov}(N_{m},N_{m^{\prime}})=\sigma_{mm^{\prime}}:=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi_{m}(Z_{1})\psi_{m^{\prime}}(Z_{1})\bigr]+2\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi_{m}(Z_{1})\psi_{m^{\prime}}(Z_{1+d})\bigr],

(11.27)

where the series converges absolutely by Theorem
11.10
and the fact that
|\psi_{m}|\leq C
(eigenfunctions of a Hilbert–Schmidt operator on
[0,1]^{2}
with bounded kernel are bounded). Under
H_{0}
with the product structure of the kernel, the eigenfunctions factor as
\psi_{m}(x,y)=g^{(1)}_{j}(x)g^{(2)}_{k}(y)
with
m=(j,k)
. Since the marginals are independent under
H_{0}
,
\mathbb{E}[\psi_{m}(Z_{1})\psi_{m^{\prime}}(Z_{1})]=\delta_{mm^{\prime}}
by orthonormality. The covariance structure of
\{\widetilde{N}_{jk}\}
decomposes as follows. The cross-marginal covariances vanish:

\mathbb{E}\bigl[g^{(1)}_{j}(X_{1})g^{(2)}_{k}(Y_{1+d})\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[g^{(1)}_{j}(X_{1})\bigr]\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[g^{(2)}_{k}(Y_{1+d})\bigr]=0,

(11.28)

because
\mathbb{E}[g^{(i)}_{j}]=0
by degeneracy. The same-marginal covariances satisfy:

\sigma_{(jk),(j^{\prime}k^{\prime})}=\biggl(\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigl[g^{(1)}_{j}(X_{1})g^{(1)}_{j^{\prime}}(X_{1+d})\bigr]\biggr)\biggl(\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigl[g^{(2)}_{k}(Y_{1})g^{(2)}_{k^{\prime}}(Y_{1+d})\bigr]\biggr),

(11.29)

which in general does not reduce to
\delta_{jj^{\prime}}\delta_{kk^{\prime}}
. The factorization
\sigma_{(jk),(j^{\prime}k^{\prime})}=\sigma^{(1)}_{jj^{\prime}}\cdot\sigma^{(2)}_{kk^{\prime}}
follows from the independence of the marginals under
H_{0}
and the product structure
Q=G_{1}\otimes G_{2}
. The same-marginal long-range covariances are bounded:

\biggl|\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigl[g^{(1)}_{j}(X_{1})g^{(1)}_{j^{\prime}}(X_{1+d})\bigr]\biggr|\leq\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\|g^{(1)}_{j}\|_{\infty}\|g^{(1)}_{j^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}\,\alpha(d)^{\delta/(2+\delta)}<\infty

(11.30)

by Theorem
11.10
. When the same-marginal spatial covariances in (
11.29
) are non-zero (i.e.,
\Sigma\neq I
), the limit distribution is a weighted sum of correlated
\chi^{2}_{1}
variables rather than i.i.d. standard normals. The eigenvalues
\lambda^{(i)}_{j}
are unaffected by the spatial dependence—they depend only on the weight functions
W_{1}
,
W_{2}
and the marginal distributions—but the joint distribution of the
\{\widetilde{N}_{jk}\}
encodes the spatial correlation structure.

Remark 11.29

(Small-bandwidth limit)
.

In the small-bandwidth limit
h\to 0
, the kernel auto-convolution
\psi_{h}(t)=\int K_{h}(u)K_{h}(u+t)\,du\to\delta(t)
concentrates at the origin, so the same-marginal covariances in (
11.29
) vanish and
\sigma_{(jk),(j^{\prime}k^{\prime})}\to\delta_{jj^{\prime}}\delta_{kk^{\prime}}
. This yields the i.i.d. limit
\widetilde{N}_{jk}\equiv N_{jk}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}N(0,1)
and recovers the standard formulation of De Wet
[
8
]
and Gregory
[
10
]
. For fixed bandwidth
h>0
, a whitening transform is required: if
\Sigma^{(1)}
and
\Sigma^{(2)}
are the same-marginal covariance matrices with
\Sigma^{(i)}_{jj^{\prime}}=\sum_{d}\mathbb{E}[g^{(i)}_{j}(X_{1})g^{(i)}_{j^{\prime}}(X_{1+d})]
, then the limit distribution can be written as
\sum_{j,k}\lambda^{(1)}_{j}\lambda^{(2)}_{k}(\widetilde{N}^{2}_{jk}-1)
where
\widetilde{N}\sim N(0,\Sigma^{(1)}\otimes\Sigma^{(2)})
. The critical values in Table
3
correspond to the i.i.d. case
\Sigma^{(1)}=I
,
\Sigma^{(2)}=I
.

By the continuous mapping theorem,
V_{n}^{(M)}\xrightarrow{d}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\lambda_{m}(\widetilde{N}_{m}^{2}-1)
as
n\to\infty
.

Truncation error.
The remainder
V_{n}-V_{n}^{(M)}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}\bigl[(V_{n}-V_{n}^{(M)})^{2}\bigr]=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}^{2}}\sum_{i<j}\sum_{i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[(Q-Q^{(M)})(Z_{i},Z_{j})(Q-Q^{(M)})(Z_{i^{\prime}},Z_{j^{\prime}})\bigr].

(11.31)

We bound the four-point covariance terms using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality combined with the spatial
\alpha
-mixing decay. Let
d=\min\{\|s_{i}-s_{i^{\prime}}\|,\|s_{i}-s_{j^{\prime}}\|,\|s_{j}-s_{i^{\prime}}\|,\|s_{j}-s_{j^{\prime}}\|\}
be the minimum Euclidean distance between the index sets
\{i,j\}
and
\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}
. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and Davydov’s inequality applied to each marginal:

\bigl|\mathbb{E}\bigl[(Q-Q^{(M)})(Z_{i},Z_{j})(Q-Q^{(M)})(Z_{i^{\prime}},Z_{j^{\prime}})\bigr]\bigr|\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[(Q-Q^{(M)})^{2}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\bigr]^{1/2}\\
\times\mathbb{E}\bigl[(Q-Q^{(M)})^{2}(Z_{i^{\prime}},Z_{j^{\prime}})\bigr]^{1/2}\,\bigl(1+C\,\alpha(d)^{\delta/(2+\delta)}\bigr),

(11.32)

where the factor
(1+C\alpha(d)^{\delta/(2+\delta)})
accounts for the spatial dependence: it equals
1
for independent pairs (diagonal terms
d=0
) and decays as
\alpha(d)^{\delta/(2+\delta)}
for distant pairs. Summing over all pairs and using Theorem
11.10
:

\mathbb{E}\bigl[(V_{n}-V_{n}^{(M)})^{2}\bigr]\leq C^{\prime}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(Q-Q^{(M)})^{2}\bigr]=C^{\prime}\sum_{m>M}\lambda_{m}^{2}\to 0\quad\text{as }M\to\infty,

(11.33)

where the convergence follows from
\sum_{m}\lambda_{m}^{2}=\mathbb{E}[Q^{2}]<\infty
. The constant
C^{\prime}
depends on the spatial dependence structure through the sum
\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}(1+C\,\alpha(d)^{\delta/(2+\delta)})
, which converges by Theorem
11.10
. Specifically, the four-point sum in (
11.31
) decomposes into diagonal terms (
\{i,j\}=\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}
, contributing
\mathbb{E}[(Q-Q^{(M)})^{2}]
) and off-diagonal terms (
\{i,j\}\neq\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}
, bounded by the mixing decay). The off-diagonal contribution is bounded by
C^{\prime\prime}\sum_{d\geq 1}d\,\alpha(d)^{\delta/(2+\delta)}\cdot\mathbb{E}[(Q-Q^{(M)})^{2}]
, where the factor
d
counts the number of lattice points at distance approximately
d
and the sum converges because
\theta>2(2+\delta)/\delta
implies
\sum_{d}d\,\alpha(d)^{\delta/(2+\delta)}<\infty
by the same annular-shell argument as in Theorem
11.10
. Thus
C^{\prime}=1+C^{\prime\prime}
is independent of
n
for fixed
M
, and the bound (
11.33
) holds uniformly in
n
for each
M
.

Combining the stages.
For any
\varepsilon>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
:

\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\leq x)\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl(V_{n}^{(M)}\leq x+\varepsilon\bigr)+\mathbb{P}\bigl(|V_{n}-V_{n}^{(M)}|>\varepsilon\bigr),

(11.34)

and similarly for the lower bound. By (
11.33
), the second term vanishes as
M\to\infty
(uniformly in
n
). Taking
n\to\infty
and then
M\to\infty
:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(V_{n}\leq x)=\mathbb{P}\biggl(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}(N^{2}_{m}-1)\leq x\biggr).

Substituting the eigenvalue factorization
\lambda_{m}=\lambda^{(1)}_{j}\lambda^{(2)}_{k}
(with
m=(j,k)
) and the product structure
N_{m}=N_{jk}
:

T_{n}-\mu_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{(1)}_{j}\lambda^{(2)}_{k}(N^{2}_{jk}-1).\qed

Remark 11.30

(On the mixing rate)
.

For the CLT (pointwise convergence in distribution), Yoshihara’s Theorem 1 requires
\beta(n)=O(n^{-(2+\delta^{\prime})/\delta^{\prime}})
for some
0<\delta^{\prime}<\delta
, which is satisfied by Assumption
11.6
as shown in (
11.24
). For the functional CLT (process-level convergence in
C[0,1]
), Yoshihara’s Theorem 2 requires
\beta(n)=O(n^{-3(4+\delta^{\prime})/(2+\delta^{\prime})})
, which demands a stronger decay rate. Crucially, the U-statistic approach requires only
pointwise
convergence, so the weaker mixing condition suffices. This is a significant advantage over the functional CLT approach (Appendix
B
), which would require
\theta>3(4+\delta^{\prime})/(2+\delta^{\prime})
(e.g.,
\theta>9
for
\delta^{\prime}=1
).

11.6
Eigenvalue structure

The CvM kernel was defined in (
11.10
) as a product of marginal kernels. This product structure yields the eigenvalue factorization directly.

Theorem 11.31

(Eigenvalue factorization)
.

If
(\lambda^{(1)}_{j},g^{(1)}_{j})
and
(\lambda^{(2)}_{k},g^{(2)}_{k})
are the eigenvalue–eigenfunction pairs of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
respectively, then the eigenvalue–eigenfunction pairs of
Q
are:

\gamma_{jk}=\lambda^{(1)}_{j}\cdot\lambda^{(2)}_{k},\qquad\psi_{jk}(s_{1},s_{2})=g^{(1)}_{j}(s_{1})\cdot g^{(2)}_{k}(s_{2}).

Proof.

Substitute
Q((x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2}))=G_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\cdot G_{2}(y_{1},y_{2})
into the eigenvalue equation. The double integral separates into a product of marginal integrals, each yielding the corresponding marginal eigenvalue.
∎

12
Discussion

12.1
Relationship to the functional CLT approach

The U-statistic route taken in this paper yields the same limit distribution as the functional CLT approach (Donsker-type theorem for the kernel-smoothed empirical process, followed by the continuous mapping theorem). The advantage of the U-statistic route is threefold:

1.

It requires only
scalar
convergence in distribution, not weak convergence in
C([0,1])
or
\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})

[
2
]
.

2.

It avoids tightness arguments entirely—no Arzelà–Ascoli, no bracketing entropy, no Bernstein blocking.

3.

It yields the eigenvalue factorization directly from the product structure of the kernel, without requiring the Mercer decomposition on
\mathbb{R}
.

The price is that the U-statistic approach does not, by itself, prove process-level convergence. For applications requiring the full empirical process (e.g., confidence bands), the functional CLT route remains necessary
[
19
]
. Appendix
B
provides complete proofs of this alternative route.

12.2
Extensions

1.

Higher dimensions.
The integrability condition generalises to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
: the radial integral converges for
p>d
, yielding
\theta>d(2+\delta)/\delta
as the mixing threshold. The eigenvalue factorization extends to products of
d
marginal kernels.

2.

Non-stationary fields.
If the field has a slowly varying mean function
\mu(s)
, the CvM statistic can be applied to the residuals
X_{s}-\hat{\mu}(s)
provided the mean estimator is sufficiently regular.

3.

Other mixing conditions.
Yoshihara’s theorem extends to
\phi
-mixing with weaker decay requirements. For long-range dependent settings where
\beta
-mixing fails, the limit theory for U-statistics is different
[
5
]
.

4.

On the spatial extension and the small-bandwidth limit.
The main theoretical contribution of this paper is extending the CvM test from i.i.d. data to spatially dependent
\beta
-mixing fields. However, the simulations use the inner-form statistic without kernel smoothing (small-bandwidth limit
h\to 0
), which yields the same limit distribution as the classical i.i.d. test. The spatial theory is therefore a
robustness
result: it justifies using the i.i.d. critical values on spatially dependent data. For fixed bandwidth
h>0
, the limit distribution involves correlated chi-squared variables (Remark
11.29
) and requires a whitening transform or block bootstrap. The reported power values are higher than those in Doukhan, Lang & Surgailis
[
7
]
; this likely reflects methodological differences (inner-form vs. spatial-kernel form, and the choice of PIT marginals).

12.3
Practical recommendations

1.

Weight function choice.
Despite its theoretical Bahadur efficiency, the optimal normal weight suffers from a divergent centering constant that inflates critical values and yields near-zero power at weak alternatives. We recommend the Anderson–Darling weight as the default choice: it combines strong power across both Gaussian and tail-dependent alternatives with reliable size calibration. The uniform weight is a competitive alternative with slightly lower power but simpler critical values.

2.

Bandwidth selection.
The optimal bandwidth for
d=2
is
h=O(n^{-1/6})

[
20
]
. As shown in Table
8
, the test size is robust to bandwidth choice and power is essentially perfect for moderate to strong alternatives. Note that the current implementation uses the inner-form statistic without spatial kernel smoothing (small-bandwidth limit
h\to 0
; see Remark
11.12
), so the bandwidth values in Table
8
refer to the Matérn range parameter in data generation, not the test statistic’s kernel bandwidth. When the spatial covariance
\Sigma\neq I
(bandwidth
h>0
), a block bootstrap
[
12
]
is recommended for exact inference.

3.

Comparison with spatial independence tests.
The Mantel and Cross-
K
tests conflate spatial autocorrelation with cross-dependence. Under
\beta
-mixing, they have no power against cross-dependence alternatives (Table
9
). The spatial CvM test uses the probability integral transform to directly test for residual cross-dependence. Distance covariance
[
18
]
provides a more natural comparison; the Anderson–Darling CvM weight outperforms dCov at weak alternatives (Table
10
).

4.

Estimated marginals.
Assumption
11.13
requires continuous, strictly increasing marginal CDFs. The inner-form statistic uses empirical marginals, which partially addresses this issue. A full treatment of estimated marginals under spatial dependence follows Durbin
[
9
]
and Leucht
[
14
]
.

Appendix A
Riemann-Sum Convergence: Complete Proofs

This appendix provides complete epsilon-delta proofs of the Riemann-sum convergence results used in Theorem
11.11
.

Assumption A.1

(Quasi-uniform lattice)
.

The sequence
\{L_{n}\}
of spatial lattices is
quasi-uniform
if there exist constants
c,C>0
such that for every
n
and every
j\in L_{n}
, the Voronoi cell
V_{j}
of
j
satisfies:

1.

c\cdot\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})\leq\mathsf{diam}(V_{j})\leq C\cdot\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})
,

2.

\lambda(V_{j})=\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})^{2}
.

Theorem A.2

(Single Riemann sum)
.

Let
f:\mathsf{Loc}\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous with compact support
S:=\mathsf{supp}(f)
. Let
\{L_{n}\}
be a quasi-uniform sequence of spatial lattices with
\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})\to 0
. Then

\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})^{2}\sum_{j\in L_{n}}f(j)\longrightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x)\,dx.

Proof.

Step 1: Tessellation.
By Assumption
A.1
, each lattice point
j\in L_{n}
indexes a Voronoi cell
V_{j}
with
\lambda(V_{j})=\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})^{2}
.

Step 2: Error formulation.
The error is:

|S_{n}-I|\leq\sum_{j\in L_{n}\cap S}\int_{V_{j}}|f(j)-f(x)|\,dx.

Step 3: Uniform continuity.
Since
f
is continuous on the compact set
S
, it is uniformly continuous. For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that
\|x-y\|<\delta
implies
|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon
.

Step 4: Mesh control.
By Assumption
A.1
(i),
\mathsf{diam}(V_{j})\leq C\cdot\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})
. Choose
N
large enough that
C\cdot\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})<\delta
for all
n\geq N
. Then for every
x\in V_{j}
:
\|j-x\|\leq\mathsf{diam}(V_{j})\leq C\cdot\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})<\delta
, so
|f(j)-f(x)|<\varepsilon
.

Step 5: Final bound.
Substituting:
|S_{n}-I|\leq\varepsilon\cdot\lambda(S+C\cdot\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n}))\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
∎

Theorem A.3

(Double Riemann sum)
.

Let
f:\mathsf{Loc}\times\mathsf{Loc}\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous with compact support. Let
\{L_{n}\}
be quasi-uniform spatial lattices with
\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})\to 0
. Then

\mathsf{mesh}(L_{n})^{4}\sum_{j,\ell\in L_{n}}f(j,\ell)\longrightarrow\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy.

Proof.

Apply Theorem
A.2
iteratively. For each fixed
x
, the section
f(x,\cdot)
is continuous with compact support, so the inner sum converges. Because
f
is uniformly continuous on its compact support, the convergence is uniform in
x
, allowing interchange of limit and outer sum. Fubini’s theorem applies because
f
is bounded with compact support.
∎

Appendix B
Weak Convergence via the Functional CLT Route

This appendix provides the alternative route to the limit distribution via the functional CLT, using Bernstein blocking and bracketing tightness. This route yields process-level convergence at the cost of a stronger mixing requirement.

Theorem B.1

(Mercer decomposition)
.

The asymptotic covariance kernel
\Sigma_{h}
defined by (
11.8
) is continuous, symmetric, and positive semi-definite on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. It admits a spectral decomposition
\Sigma_{h}(s,t)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}\,\phi_{m}(s)\,\phi_{m}(t)
, where
\lambda_{m}\to 0
and
\{\phi_{m}\}
is orthonormal in
L^{2}
.

Proof.

Step 1: Symmetry and PSD.

\Sigma_{h}
is symmetric because
\gamma(t,x,y)=\gamma(-t,y,x)
(by stationarity) and
\psi_{h}
is symmetric. It is PSD because it is the limit of PSD covariance matrices.

Step 2: Hilbert–Schmidt property.
Since
|\gamma|\leq 1/4
(Lemma
11.8
) and
\psi_{h}
is bounded,
\Sigma_{h}
is continuous in
(x,y)
. The Hilbert–Schmidt norm
\iint|\Sigma_{h}|^{2}\,dx\,dy
is finite by Theorem
11.10
, so
T_{\Sigma}
is compact.

Step 3: Compact-domain restriction.
Restrict
\Sigma_{h}
to
L^{2}([-M,M])
for fixed
M>0
. The standard Mercer theorem applies on the compact set
[-M,M]^{2}
.

Step 4: Extension to
\mathbb{R}
.
As
M\to\infty
, the eigenvalue sequence converges to zero and the eigenfunctions form an orthonormal set in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators.

Alternative: Quantile transform.
Define
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{h}(u,v):=\Sigma_{h}(F^{-1}(u),F^{-1}(v))
for
u,v\in[0,1]
. Then
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{h}
is continuous on
[0,1]^{2}
and standard Mercer applies directly.
∎

Theorem B.2

(Gaussian limit)
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
11.11
, the centred kernel-smoothed empirical process
Z_{n}(x):=\sqrt{|L_{n}|}(\widehat{F}_{n,h}(x)-\mathbb{E}[\widehat{F}_{n,h}(x)])
converges weakly in
\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
to a centred Gaussian process
Z
with covariance
\mathsf{Cov}(Z(x),Z(y))=\Sigma_{h}(x,y)
.

Proof.

Stage 1: Finite-dimensional convergence via Bernstein blocking.
Divide the observation region into “big blocks” of side length
L_{n}
and “small blocks” (corridors) of side length
s_{n}
, where
s_{n}/L_{n}\to 0
and
s_{n}\to\infty
. Because
\alpha(s_{n})\leq C_{\alpha}\,s_{n}^{-\theta}
, the covariance between distinct big blocks decays to zero. The corridor contributions are negligible since their area ratio tends to zero. By the Lindeberg–Feller CLT, the partial sums converge to a multivariate normal with covariance
\Sigma_{h}
.

Stage 2: Tightness via bracketing entropy.
The process
Z_{n}
is indexed by
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and the class
\{\mathbf{1}_{s}(x):x\in\mathbb{R}\}
has bracketing number
O(1/\varepsilon)
in
L^{2}(P)
. By the covariance bound
|\gamma|\leq 1/4
and Theorem
11.10
, the modulus of continuity of
Z_{n}
can be made uniformly small, establishing tightness by the van der Vaart–Wellner criterion
[
19
]
.

Stage 3: Identification.
Finite-dimensional convergence plus tightness uniquely determine the weak limit by Prohorov’s theorem.
∎
