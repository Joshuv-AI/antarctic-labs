Introduction

A simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
is
flag
provided every minimal non-face of
\mathcal{K}
has two vertices. The condition of flagness often imposes strong combinatorial restrictions on the structure of simplicial spheres. For instance, the boundary of the
d
-simplex has
\binom{d+1}{i+1}
faces of dimension
i
, the smallest among all
(d-1)
-spheres. In contrast, Meshulam
[
11
]
proved that every flag simplicial
(d-1)
-sphere must satisfy
f_{i}\geq 2^{i+1}\binom{d}{i+1}
for all
i
, with equality achieved by the boundary of the
d
-dimensional cross-polytope.

Another interesting comparison concerns the graphs of these spheres. A connected graph is said to be
k
-connected
if it has more than
k
vertices and remains connected whenever fewer than
k
vertices are removed. The
connectivity
of a graph is the largest
k
for which the graph is
k
-connected. A classical result of Balinski
[
4
]
, later extended by Barnette
[
5
]
, states that graphs of
(d-1)
-spheres are
d
-connected, while Athanasiadis
[
2
]
proved that the graph of every flag
(d-1)
-sphere has connectivity at least
2d-2
.

In this paper, we study the independence numbers of these graphs. The
independence number

\alpha(G)
of a graph
G
is the size of its largest independent set. Chudnovsky and Nevo
[
6
, Conjecture 4.1]
conjectured that if
\mathcal{K}
is a flag
(d-1)
-sphere, then its graph
G(\mathcal{K})
satisfies

\alpha(G(\mathcal{K}))\leq\left\lfloor\frac{f_{0}(\mathcal{K})-2(d-2)}{2}\right\rfloor.

(1.1)

The conjecture is immediate when
d=2
and was established by the authors of
[
6
]
for
d=3
; however, their arguments rely essentially on planarity. They also note that equality in (
1.1
) is attained by the
(d-1)
-sphere obtained by suspending an
(n-2(d-2))
-gon
d-2
times.

If the spheres are not required to be flag, then the situation is seemingly different. The number of facets
m
of a neighborly simplicial
(d-1)
-sphere with
n
vertices (such as the boundary of the cyclic polytope
C(n,d)
) is on the order of
n^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}
; when
d\geq 4
,
n/m
approaches
0
as
n\to\infty
. Performing stellar subdivisions at each facet produces a simplicial sphere with
n+m\sim m
vertices, while the
m
added vertices form an independent set. This shows that there exist simplicial spheres with independent sets of size asymptotically equal to the number of vertices. Since stellar subdivisions do not preserve flagness, however, this construction does not apply in the flag setting.

In this paper, we disprove the conjecture of Chudnovsky and Nevo for all
d\geq 4
by establishing the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists a constant
C=C(d)>0
and a family of
(d-1)
-dimensional flag spheres
\mathcal{K}_{n}
satisfying
f_{0}(\mathcal{K}_{n})\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, and

\alpha(G(\mathcal{K}_{n}))\geq f_{0}(\mathcal{K}_{n})-\frac{Cf_{0}(\mathcal{K}_{n})}{(\log f_{0}(\mathcal{K}_{n}))^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor-1}},

for all sufficiently large
n
.

When
d\geq 4
, taking
n\to\infty
gives

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\alpha(G(\mathcal{K}_{n}))}{f_{0}(\mathcal{K}_{n})}=1.

If true, (
1.1
) would imply that
\alpha(G(\mathcal{K}))\leq\left\lfloor\frac{f_{0}(\mathcal{K})}{2}\right\rfloor
for all flag
(d-1)
-spheres
\mathcal{K}
, and consequently the above limit could not exceed
1/2
. So Theorem
1.2
provides a counterexample to (
1.1
) for every
d\geq 4
.

Let us briefly describe our construction. Our starting point is the construction of (not necessarily flag) spheres with large independence number discussed above. We replace the simplicial spheres in that construction by cubical spheres with
n
vertices and
m
facets, where
n/m\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. We then perform an operation resembling a stellar subdivision: each cubical facet is triangulated after adding a new vertex in its interior. This produces a flag sphere with
m+n\sim m
vertices, while the
m
new vertices form an independent set.

Organization of the paper.
In Section
2
, we introduce necessary background on simplicial and cubical complexes, which are the main objects we use in this paper. In Section
3
, we describe a triangulation of cubes, which is then used in Section
4
to prove Theorem
1.2
.

2.1
Simplicial Complexes

A
simplicial complex

\mathcal{K}
on a vertex set

is a collection of subsets of
V

\{v\}\in\mathcal{K}

F\subseteq G\in\mathcal{K}

. Elements of
\mathcal{K}
are called
faces
, and maximal faces (with respect to inclusion) are called
facets
. The
dimension
of a face

is
|F|-1
, and the dimension of
\mathcal{K}
is the largest dimension of any face of
\mathcal{K}
. If every facet of
\mathcal{K}
has the same dimension, then
\mathcal{K}
is said to be
pure
. Moreover,
0
-dimensional faces are called
vertices
and
1
-dimensional faces are called
edges
. For all

, we denote by

the number of
k
-dimensional faces of
\mathcal{K}
.

We often omit the usual set notation for faces when no ambiguity can arise. For example, if
\{x\}
is a vertex of
\mathcal{K}
, we denote it by
x
, and if
\{x,y,z\}
is a face of
\mathcal{K}
, we denote it by
xyz
.

Let

. The
k
-skeleton

\mathcal{K}
is the simplicial complex consisting of all faces of
\mathcal{K}
of dimension at most
k
. We refer to the
1
-skeleton of
\mathcal{K}
as the
graph
of
\mathcal{K}
and denote it by
G(\mathcal{K})
.

Every simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
has a

|\mathcal{K}|
, obtained as a union of simplices in Euclidean space whose intersections reflect the intersections among the faces of
\mathcal{K}
. This space is well-defined up to homeomorphism. Throughout the paper, we will therefore blur the distinction between a face of
\mathcal{K}
and the corresponding simplex of
|\mathcal{K}|
, viewing
\mathcal{K}
as a triangulation of
|\mathcal{K}|
.

A
simplicial
(d-1)
-sphere
is a simplicial complex whose geometric realization is homeomorphic to the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. When no confusion is likely, we will simply call such a complex a
(d-1)
-sphere
.

We say that a simplicial complex
\mathcal{L}
is a

is a face of
\mathcal{K}
. If

\mathcal{K}
, then the

with vertex set

. The

2.1.1
Flag Complexes

A simplicial complex is
flag
if all of its minimal non-faces have two vertices; see Figure
2
for an example. A set of pairwise adjacent vertices in a graph is a
clique
. An equivalent characterization of the flag property is that every clique in
G(\mathcal{K})
is a face of
\mathcal{K}
.

The boundary of a cross-polytope is flag, whereas the boundary of a triangular bipyramid is not: the blue triangle is a clique but not a face.

An important feature of flagness is that it is preserved under coning.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a flag complex with vertex set
V
. The cone of
\mathcal{K}
over any subset
U\subseteq V
is also flag.

Proof.

Let
v
be the cone point, and let
A\subseteq V\cup v
be a clique in the graph of the cone. If
v\notin A
, then
A
is a clique in
G(\mathcal{K})
, and since
\mathcal{K}
is flag,
A
is a face of
\mathcal{K}
. Hence
A
is also a face of the cone.

Now suppose that
v\in A
. Then
A\setminus v
is a clique in
G(\mathcal{K})
, and therefore a face of
\mathcal{K}
. Moreover,
A\setminus v\subseteq U
, since
v
is adjacent only to vertices of
U
. Thus
A\setminus v
is a face of
\mathcal{K}[U]
, so
A
is a face of the cone by definition.
∎

2.2
Cubes

The
n
-cube
is the polytope
[0,1]^{n}
. We refer the reader to
[
13
]
for background on polytopes and the combinatorics of cubes. We will only need the following basic fact: every
k
-dimensional face of the
n
-cube is obtained by fixing
n-k
coordinates to be either
0
or
1
and allowing the remaining
k
coordinates to vary freely. Consequently, every
k
-face is itself a
k
-cube and has
2^{k}
vertices.

The graph
Q_{n}
of
[0,1]^{n}
has vertex set
\{0,1\}^{n}
, and two vertices
u
and
v
are adjacent if they differ in exactly one coordinate. In general, the
distance

d(u,v)
between vertices
u,v
is the number of coordinates in which they differ. Since every
k
-dimensional face of
[0,1]^{n}
is itself a
k
-cube, its vertex set induces a subgraph of
Q_{n}
isomorphic to
Q_{k}
. The converse, which appears for example in
[
8
, Lemma 12]
, will be useful in Section
4
. We include a proof for completeness.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
H
is a subgraph of
Q_{n}
isomorphic to
Q_{k}
, then
V(H)
is the set of vertices of some
k
-dimensional face of
[0,1]^{n}
.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
k
. The cases
k=0
and
k=1
are immediate, so assume
k\geq 2
. The vertex set of
H
can be partitioned into two sets
A
and
B
, each of size
2^{k-1}
, such that the induced subgraphs on
A
and
B
are both isomorphic to
Q_{k-1}
. By the induction hypothesis,
A
and
B
are the vertex sets of
(k-1)
-dimensional faces of
[0,1]^{n}
.

Let
I
be the set of coordinates that are fixed on
A
. We claim that among the coordinates in
I
, at most one is not fixed in
B
to the same value as on
A
. Suppose otherwise. Then there exist two coordinates
i,j\in I
such that
B
is either free in those coordinates or fixed to the opposite value. Hence we may choose
b\in B
that differs from the fixed values on
A
in both coordinates
i
and
j
.

Since
A
is the vertex set of a
(k-1)
-dimensional face, the
k-1
coordinates not in
I
vary freely in
A
. Choosing
a\in A
so that it disagrees with
b
in each of these varying coordinates, we obtain

d(a,b)\geq(k-1)+2=k+1,

contradicting the fact that every two vertices of
H
are at distance at most
k
in
Q_{n}
. Indeed, since
H
is isomorphic to
Q_{k}
, any pair of vertices in
H
is connected by a path of length at most
k
, and each edge of
Q_{n}
changes exactly one coordinate.

It follows that the vertices of
H
vary freely in at most
k
coordinates, and hence
V(H)
is contained in the vertex set of a
k
-dimensional face
G
of
[0,1]^{n}
. Since both
V(H)
and
V(G)
have cardinality
2^{k}
,
V(H)
must equal
V(G)
.
∎

2.3
Cubical Complexes

A
cubical complex

\mathcal{B}
on a vertex set
V
is a collection of subsets of
V
, partially ordered by inclusion, satisfying the following properties:

1.

\varnothing\in\mathcal{B}
and
{v}\in\mathcal{B}
for every
v\in V
.

2.

For every nonempty face
F\in\mathcal{B}
, the interval

[\varnothing,F]=\{G\in\mathcal{B}:\varnothing\subseteq G\subseteq F\}

is isomorphic to the face poset of a cube of some dimension.

3.

If
F,G\in\mathcal{B}
, then
F\cap G\in\mathcal{B}
.

In particular, the collection of vertex sets of faces of an
n
-cube forms a cubical complex in this sense.

The elements of
\mathcal{B}
are called
faces
. If
F\in\mathcal{B}
is nonempty, the
dimension
of
F
is the dimension of the cube whose face poset is isomorphic to
[\varnothing,F]
. The dimension of
\mathcal{B}
is the largest dimension of any face of
\mathcal{B}
. As in the simplicial setting, maximal faces are called
facets
,
0
-dimensional faces are called
vertices
, and
1
-dimensional faces are called
edges
. We denote by
f_{k}(\mathcal{B})
the number of
k
-dimensional faces of
\mathcal{B}
.

Many constructions for simplicial complexes have cubical analogues. In particular, the
graph
of
\mathcal{B}
, denoted
G(\mathcal{B})
, is the graph whose vertices are the vertices of
\mathcal{B}
and whose edges are the
1
-dimensional faces of
\mathcal{B}
.

Every cubical complex admits a geometric realization as a topological space, unique up to homeomorphism. A
cubical
(d-1)
-sphere
is a cubical complex whose geometric realization is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

2.3.1
Neighborly Cubical Spheres

A cubical
(d-1)
-sphere with

vertices is said to be

-skeleton is that of the
n
-cube. For every

, Babson, Billera, and Chan

constructed neighborly cubical

-spheres with

vertices in 1997. Subsequently, Joswig and Ziegler
[

Let

, and let
\mathcal{P}
be a neighborly cubical
(d-1)
-sphere having

vertices. Label the vertices of
\mathcal{P}
by elements of
\{0,1\}^{n}
so that the graph of
\mathcal{P}
coincides with

. Any

Proposition 2.3
.

A neighborly cubical

-sphere with

Define a partial order on

The faces of

In this case we say that the chain has

consists of the vertices of
F
together with a single additional coning vertex, and since
x,y\in{0,1}^{n}
are not coning vertices, it follows that
x,y\in V(F)
. This proves
(i)
.

For
(ii)
, observe that every face of
\mathcal{T}(F)
whose vertices are all contained in
\{0,1\}^{n}
is a chain in
\{0,1\}^{n}
. Since
xy
is such a face,
\{x,y\}
is a chain, and hence either
x\leq y
or
y\leq x
.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

The simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}_{n}
is a flag
(d-1)
-sphere.

Proof.

Let
A
be a clique in the graph of
\mathcal{K}_{n}
. We show that
A
is a face of
\mathcal{K}_{n}
.

We first prove that
A\subseteq V(\mathcal{T}(F))
for some facet
F
of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
. The vertex set of
\mathcal{K}_{n}
consists of the vertices of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
together with one cone vertex for each facet of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
. If
A
contains a cone vertex
v_{F}
of some
\mathcal{T}(F)
, then by construction
v_{F}
is adjacent only to vertices of
F
. Hence
A\subseteq V(\mathcal{T}(F))
.

Now suppose that
A
consists entirely of original vertices of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
. If
x,y
are adjacent in
G(\mathcal{K}_{n})
, then by Lemma
4.1
, item
(ii)
, they are comparable. Hence the elements of
A
are pairwise comparable, so
A
is a chain. Write

A=\{x^{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq x^{(r)}\}.

Since
A
is a clique,
x^{(1)}
and
x^{(r)}
are adjacent in
G(\mathcal{K}_{n})
. By Lemma
4.1
, item
(i)
, there exists a facet
F
of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
containing both
x^{(1)}
and
x^{(r)}
. Since
F
is an interval in the Boolean lattice, it contains the entire interval
\left[x^{(1)},x^{(r)}\right]
, and hence
A\subseteq F
.

In either case, we obtain
A\subseteq V(\mathcal{T}(F))
for some facet
F
of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
. Since each
\mathcal{T}(F)
is flag, it follows that
A
is a face of
\mathcal{T}(F)
, and hence also of
\mathcal{K}_{n}
. Therefore
\mathcal{K}_{n}
is flag.

Moreover,
\mathcal{K}_{n}
triangulates the
(d-1)
-sphere
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, which implies that
\mathcal{K}_{n}
is a flag
(d-1)
-sphere.
∎

Now we can prove our main theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

By Proposition
4.2
, the complex
\mathcal{K}_{n}
is a flag
(d-1)
-sphere for every
n\geq 1
. Its vertex set consists of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
together with a cone vertex for each facet of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, and hence

f_{0}(\mathcal{K}_{n})=f_{0}(\mathcal{P}_{n})+f_{d-1}(\mathcal{P}_{n}).

Each cone vertex is adjacent only to original vertices. Hence the set of cone vertices is independent, and
\alpha(G(\mathcal{K}_{n}))\geq f_{d-1}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
. Therefore

\frac{\alpha(G(\mathcal{K}_{n}))}{f_{0}(\mathcal{K}_{n})}\geq\frac{f_{d-1}(\mathcal{P}_{n})}{f_{0}(\mathcal{P}_{n})+f_{d-1}(\mathcal{P}_{n})}=1-\frac{f_{0}(\mathcal{P}_{n})}{f_{0}(\mathcal{P}_{n})+f_{d-1}(\mathcal{P}_{n})}\geq 1-\frac{f_{0}(\mathcal{P}_{n})}{f_{d-1}(\mathcal{P}_{n})}.

(4.3)

Recall that
f_{0}(\mathcal{P}_{n})=2^{n}
, and so, by (
2.4
), there exist constants
M_{1},M_{2}>0
such that

M_{1}n^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor-1}2^{n}\leq f_{d-1}(\mathcal{P}_{n})\leq M_{2}n^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor-1}2^{n}.

The upper bound implies
f_{0}(\mathcal{K}_{n})\leq 2^{n}(1+M_{2}n^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor-1})\leq e^{n}
for sufficiently large
n
, and hence
\log f_{0}(\mathcal{K}_{n})\leq n
. Combining this with the lower bound on
f_{d-1}(\mathcal{P}_{n})
gives

\frac{f_{0}(\mathcal{P}_{n})}{f_{d-1}(\mathcal{P}_{n})}\leq\frac{1}{M_{1}n^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor-1}}\leq\frac{1}{M_{1}(\log f_{0}(\mathcal{K}_{n}))^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor-1}}.

Substituting into (
4.3
) and multiplying through by
f_{0}(\mathcal{K}_{n})
gives

\alpha(G(\mathcal{K}_{n}))\geq f_{0}(\mathcal{K}_{n})-\frac{Cf_{0}(\mathcal{K}_{n})}{(\log f_{0}(\mathcal{K}_{n}))^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor-1}},

where
C=1/M_{1}
.
∎
