Deep learning analysis of breast cancer histology predicts ATM pathogenic variant carrier status
Authors: Nicolas M Viart, Lucie Thibault, Tristan Lazard, Séverine Eon-Marchais, Yue Jiao, Laetitia Fuhrmann, Dorothée Le Gal, Eve Cavaciuti, Marie-Gabrielle Dondon, Juana Beauvallet, Marina De Brot, Joanne Ngeow, Soo-Hwang Teo, Maria Isabel Achatz, Elizabeth Santana Dos Santos, Fergus J Couch, Dominique Stoppa-Lyonnet, Melissa C Southey, Anne Vincent-Salomon, Thomas Walter, Nadine Andrieu, Fabienne Lesueur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe performed deep learning analysis of histopathological whole-slide (full-face) images (WSI) to predict ATM pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant (PV/LPV) status of women with breast cancer and identify specific histological patterns of their tumor.In the discovery set composed of tumors from PV/LPV carriers (58 WSI) and noncarriers (129 WSI), our deep learning model predicted ATM status of patients with an area under the curve of 0.90 [95%CI: 0.85–0.95] and a balanced accuracy of 0.80 [95%CI: 0.72–0.88]. In the replication set (29 WSI from carriers and 22 WSI from noncarriers), corresponding results were 0.85 [95%CI: 0.70–1.00] and 0.67 [95%CI: 0.51–0.83]. We found that tumors developed by ATM PV/LPV carriers often displayed discohesive neoplastic cells as observed in invasive lobular carcinomas, and dense lymphocytic infiltrate reflecting an immune-enriched microenvironment.Recognizing these tumors at the time of diagnosis is a critical first step toward precision medicine in affected women and precision prevention in family members.Subject terms: Cancer, Oncology

Introduction

Germline biallelic inactivation of the ATM gene causes ataxia-telangiectasia (A-T), a rare hereditary syndrome affecting about 1/100,000 children in Europe and characterized by progressive cerebellar ataxia, ocular telangiectasia, immunodeficiency, high sensitivity to ionizing radiation and predisposition to developing cancers1–3. A-T cells show chromosomal instability, telomere shortening, and defects in response to ionizing radiation and radiomimetic drugs4. Epidemiological studies on A-T relatives demonstrated a 5- to 9-fold increased risk of developing breast cancer in women heterozygous for an ATM pathogenic variant (PV) compared to women from the general population3,5–9. A-T is rare with 0.5 to 1% of the general population expected to be heterozygous for an ATM PV. An ATM pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant (PV/LPV) is identified in approximately 5% of index cases of Hereditary Breast and Ovarian Cancer (HBOC) families undergoing genetic counseling and testing, independently of an A-T familial context10–15.

To date, few studies have investigated the histological characteristics of breast tumors developed by ATM PV/LPV carriers. They showed that most of these tumors express the estrogen receptor (ER+), and more particularly belong to the luminal B molecular subtype16–18. We and others showed that these tumors do not have the homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) signature identified in tumors in which BRCA1 or BRCA2 is inactivated16,17,19, but that they exhibit a distinctive genome-wide DNA methylation profile20. These studies suggest that analytical approaches that integrate multiple omics datasets could further characterize these tumors, more precisely predict ATM PV/LPV carrier status and identify potential therapeutic targets that would benefit these carriers.

The recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), and particularly in deep learning (DL) analysis of histopathological whole-slide images (WSI), represent a cost-effective approach to predict tumor grade21, hormone receptor status and response to treatment22. DL analysis of WSI has been shown to predict genetic aberrations such as microsatellite instability23–25 and the presence of some somatically acquired gene signatures26–32. Notably, the DL approach developed by Lazard et al. (2022) predicts the HRD status of luminal breast tumors with high accuracy (78%) and area under the curve (AUC) (86%), and discovered previously unknown histological patterns associated to HRD30.

Here we assessed whether a DL analysis strategy could predict ATM PV/LPV carrier status of breast tumors and identify histopathological patterns specific to the ATM deficiency.

Results

Discovery set

The discovery set was composed of WSI images from breast tumors of patients enrolled in the French epidemiological studies CoF-AT216,33,34 and GENESIS35 (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1). Tumor slides were stained with hematoxylin, eosin and saffron (HES), which is the routine staining protocol of slides for diagnosis in France. Since about 97% of breast tumors developed by ATM PV/LPV carriers are ER +16, we trained the model on WSI of ER+ tumors from PV/LPV carriers (58 WSI from 50 tumors of 46 patients) and noncarriers (129 WSI from 56 tumors of 51 patients).

Choice of the histopathological slides encoding method

We first compared the classification performances of the model to predict the ATM status of patients when using the three encoding methods ImageNet, MoCo (pre-trained on TCGA-BRCA dataset from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)) and CTransPath (Table 2 and Supplementary Fig 1). Use of strategic sampling to control for the color intensity bias

In the discovery set, HES staining of tumor slides was performed at different times (between 1988 and 2018 for ATM PV/LPV tumors and between 1999 and 2021 for non-ATM PV/LPV carrying tumors). Given that saffron tends to fade away over time, we wondered whether this heterogeneity in the color intensity could affect the prediction of ATM status. To check this, we categorized WSI into two groups using k-means clustering on the coloration (R, G, B channels of the two most present colors) and color intensity of the tiles within the area showing the highest number of tumor cells. Doing so, we found that tumors were grouped according to their color intensity and that 55% of ATM WSI and 17% of non-ATM WSI belong to a group with an “intense coloration”. This coloration intensity category is highly predictable by the model (AUC = 0.98 [95%CI: 0.96–1.00] and BA = 0.88 [95%CI: 0.81–0.95]) and therefore represents a potential confounding variable in the prediction of ATM status. Indeed, when predicting the ATM status of WSI, we found that this bias was amplified by 20% in the test sets as compared to the training sets. To mitigate the coloring bias, we used the strategic sampling procedure implemented by Lazard et al. 30. Doing so improved the ATM status prediction performance (AUC = 0.90 [95%CI: 0.85–0.95] and BA = 0.80 [95%CI: 0.72–0.88]), and the amplification bias decreased by 9% in the test sets as compared to the training sets.

We also performed a Fisher’s exact test to assess whether our model predicts well the ATM status of the patients, and calculated the Cohen’s Kappa coefficient to assess the agreement between the predicted and the true labels. The Fisher’s exact test p value was 2.2 × 10−16, which confirmed the good prediction performance, and the Cohen’s Kappa coefficient was 0.61, in favor of a substantial agreement between the predicted and the true status of the patients.

Sensitivity analyses

To verify that predictions considering bias intensity were not the result of a random effect or of an underlying effect that was not considered, we trained the model 10 times after the random attribution of ATM status of WSI, while considering the proportion of ATM tumors in the dataset. When keeping the previous strategic sampling, the classification performances with these reassigned labels dramatically dropped (AUC = 0.48 [95%CI: 0.37–0.59] and BA = 0.49 [95%CI: 0.44–0.54]), confirming that after controlling for the color intensity bias, ATM status assigned to WSI is indeed not random.

In the main analysis, all WSIs were considered independent from each other. However, for some patients, several WSIs were available, and they could have been assigned to different test sets of the cross-validation, which could result in data leakage across cross-validation folds. We thus re-ran the analysis, assigning all WSI from a given patient to the same test set. This led to similar classification performances (AUC = 0.88 [95%CI: 0.82–0.94] and BA = 0.75 [95%CI: 0.68–0.82]; Supplementary Table 2), suggesting that the slide-level splitting is not artificially inflating the model’s performance.

Identification of histological patterns predictive of ATM tumors

We used the attention and decision modules of the model trained on WSI to identify the 300 most predictive tiles of ATM tumors and the 300 most predictive tiles of non-ATM tumors. Figure 1 shows a Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) clustering of these 600 tiles' embeddings, and the histological patterns within each cluster are predictive of ATM (clusters D1 to D8) and non-ATM (cluster D9) statuses. Supplementary Fig 2 and Supplementary Fig 3 display the most predictive tiles of the ATM and the non-ATM tumors, respectively. Thirty-seven WSIs contributed to ATM clusters, and 59 WSIs contributed to non-ATM clusters. Clusters D1 and D2 demonstrate similar features, with discohesive tumor cells arranged within a thin fibrotic network, accompanied by lymphocytic infiltrate. These features are consistent with the classic invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) morphology. Cluster D3 shows trabeculae of irregular and discohesive tumor cells within a dense fibrotic stroma. Cluster D4 is defined by a dense inflammatory infiltrate embedded within fibrotic stroma, with rare and scattered discohesive tumor cells. In all four clusters, the discohesive nature of the tumor cells may indicate lobular differentiation36. Conversely, cluster D7 shows large, cohesive nests of atypical tumor cells with dense lymphocytic infiltration. This pattern is consistent with infiltrating carcinoma of no special type. Clusters D5, D6 and D8 show morphologic similarities with small or rounded cohesive tumor cell nests, but cluster D5 and D6 are also notable for their dense lymphocytic infiltrate, suggesting an active tumor-immune interaction, while cluster D8 exhibits minimal stromal or immune response. In cluster D9, tiles predictive of the non-ATM status mainly show dense fibrosis with some tumor cells.

Replication sets

We applied the same DL analysis pipeline with CTransPath encodings on the ENIGMA replication set composed of WSI of ER+ tumors from ATM PV/LPV carriers (29 WSI from 29 patients) and noncarriers (22 WSI from 22 patients) (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1). All slides were stained during the same timeframe using a standard hematoxylin and eosin (HE) protocol between 1993 and 2017 for ATM tumors, and between 1994 and 2016 for non-ATM tumors. Therefore, no strategic sampling was applied here. The performance of the model to predict ATM status was good (AUC = 0.85 [95%CI: 0.70–1.00] and BA = 0.67 [95%CI: 0.51–0.83]) with an amplification bias of only 4%, confirming that no strategic sampling was needed (Table 2). Histological patterns predictive of ATM inactivation in this replication set confirmed results obtained in the discovery set (clusters R1 to R6 in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig 5). Notably, cluster R1 displays short trabeculae or small clusters of cohesive cells within abundant fibrosis and no lymphocytes, as in cluster D6. Cluster R5 displays clusters of tumor cells with glandular differentiation dispersed in a rather dense fibrosis, as observed in cluster D3. Cluster R6 also displays dense clusters of rounded discohesive cells, resembling solid sheets of neoplastic cells of lobular tumors. This pattern is also present in cluster D8 and D7, although tumor cells are more cohesive in cluster D7. Intriguingly, cluster R2 and R3 do not exhibit notable characteristics: cluster R2 shows in situ carcinoma or non-tumoral glands and cluster R3 displays mostly non tumor cells. Cluster R4 shows micropapillary-like patterns with small tumor cell clusters surrounded by empty spaces delimitated by delicate stromal framework. As in the discovery set, the cluster containing tiles predictive of non-ATM tumors (cluster R7 in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig 6) displays mostly normal glands with some cancerous cells.

As a second replication set, we investigated WSI from ER+ breast tumors from the TCGA-BRCA dataset, considering WSI developed by 10 ATM PV/LPV carriers and 20 noncarriers (frequency matched on age at diagnosis, pathology stage and morphology, and with no PV/LPV in another cancer susceptibility gene). In this dataset, the model was much less performant with an AUC of 0.69 [95%CI: 0.49–0.89] and BA of 0.50 [95%CI: 0.50–0.50] (Table 2).

ATM status prediction on tumors developed by carriers of a variant of uncertain clinical significance

Using models trained on tumors of PV/LPV carriers and noncarriers in each dataset, we next interrogated how the tumors from patients carrying an ATM variant of uncertain clinical significance (VUS) were classified. The predicted classification of the 44 WSI from tumors of VUS carriers included in the discovery and replication sets is shown in Supplementary Table 3. While we do not have ground truth for these variants, we checked whether the same predictions were obtained for WSI from the same tumors or from tumors from different patients carrying the same variant. The variants c.1229T>C identified in three patients (3 WSI), and c.8071C>T identified in two patients (2 WSI) were predicted as PV in all instances. The variants c.2021A>G identified in two patients (2 WSI), c.4853G>A identified in one patient (3 WSI) and c.6086C>T identified in one patient (2 WSI), were predicted as benign in all instances. However, predictions on variants c.7187C>G (2 WSI) and c.4709T>C (3 WSI) were conflicting (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

Our DL model applied on WSI ER+ tumors can predict ATM status with a mean AUC > 0.80 and mean BA > 0.65 in the discovery and ENIGMA replication sets and identified histological patterns specific to breast tumors developed by ATM PV/LPV carriers. In part, the highest performance of the model in the discovery set could be explained by the fact that all HES tumor slides from ATM PV/LPV carriers and noncarriers were prepared in the same laboratory and with the same protocol, while slides of the replication sets were prepared in different laboratories.

After classification, the most predictive tiles of ATM status identified several tumor microenvironment patterns characteristic of ATM PV/LPV carrying tumors. These include dense lymphocytic infiltrates, either surrounding cohesive tumor nests or dispersed within fibrotic stroma, highlighting immune-active phenotypes that may correlate with defective DNA repair mechanisms37. Tiles contained also discohesive tumor cells within fibrotic matrices, suggestive of ILC or lobular-like morphologies.

Clusters in the replication set with discohesive cells isolated in trabeculae or in a dense fibrosis indicative of lobular differentiation were also highlighted along with the presence of small clusters of cohesive cells. However, the rich immune environment seen in some predictive tiles of the discovery cohort was not present in the most predictive tiles of the replication series. This may be due to the higher proportion of patients of Asian ancestry in the replication set than in the discovery set, where most patients were of European ancestry. Indeed, it was shown that overall, tumors developed by Asian breast cancer patients are enriched in tumor-infiltrating immune cells as compared to those developed by European patients38. Consequently, ATM tumors could be less distinguishable from non-ATM tumors in Asian patients.

After a posteriori blind review of the slides by the pathologist, the lobular-like features seen in clusters D1 and D2 were indeed more present in tumor slides from ATM PV/LPV carriers (9%) than in tumor slides from noncarriers (2%). Furthermore, 77.4% of the tiles in these two clusters were from slides showing absence of e-cadherin expression, which is also a characteristic feature of invasive lobular breast cancer. The immune-enriched environment seen in clusters D4, D5, D6 and D7 was also more present in tumors from ATM PV/LPV carriers (7%) than in tumors from noncarriers (3%), confirming that the presence of these two features could be indicative of ATM PV status of the patients. The fact that the pathologist’s eye could only detect these features in a small proportion of the tumors highlights how DL analysis of WSI could provide crucial support to pathologists for more accurate patient care.

Although results in the ENIGMA replication set were in line with results in the discovery set, our attempt to replicate findings in the TCGA-BRCA dataset was unsuccessful. The poor performance of the model can be explained by the low power of the analyzed sample which includes only 10 ER+ tumors from women carrying an ATM PV/LPV. Since ATM PV/LPV carrying tumors display diffuse ILC-like characteristics where tumors cells tend to invade breast tissue by spreading out rather than forming a firm lump, it is possible that the analyzed WSI correspond to the central part of the tumors away from the invasive edge that do not contain the ATM specific features seen in the two other series.

To conclude, we showed that the presence of a germline ATM PV/LPV in breast cancer may be predicted from a WSI using DL strategies. A broader multicenter validation is needed to further strengthen confidence in clinical generalizability. Still, we found that histological features of ATM PV/LPV carrying tumors include discohesive neoplastic cells indicative of a lobular differentiation and an immune-enriched microenvironment. Knowledge of patients’ ATM PV/LPV status at the time of diagnosis may aid treatment choices and precipitate cascade testing in relatives who can benefit from precision prevention strategies and manage issues related to family planning. Moreover, identifying ATM PV/LPV carriers even outside of familial breast cancer context may be useful to personalize the screening methods of unaffected relatives carrying also the variant.

Information about a woman’s ATM PV/LPV carrier status and possible lobular histology is extremely informative at the time of breast cancer screening as lobular tumors are more difficult to detect using conventional imaging techniques such as digital mammography39.

Finally, the identification of the histological features of ATM-deficient tumors could provide additional evidence for or against pathogenicity for ATM VUS in ACMG/AMP variant classification rules that already include evidence from segregation analyses, in silico prediction tools and functional assays.

Methods

Tumor material

In the discovery set, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded breast tumor samples were collected from patients enrolled in the French epidemiological studies CoF-AT2 (French prospective cohort on families segregating an ATM variant)16,33,34 and GENESIS (GENE SISters study)35. These studies are described in Renault et al. 16 and Viart et al. 20. The French series included tumor samples from 3 A-T patients who were either homozygous or compound heterozygous ATM PV carriers (3 WSI), 43 heterozygous ATM PV/LPV carriers (55 WSI), 34 ATM VUS carriers (38 WSI) and 51 noncarriers (129 WSI). None of the patients included in the discovery set carried a PV/LPV in the cancer susceptibility genes BRCA1, BRCA2, BARD1, BRIP1, CDH1, CHEK2, PALB2, PTEN, RAD51C, RAD51D, STK11 and TP53.

Characteristics of the analyzed tumors are summarized in Table 1 and detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

As a first replication set, we used WSI from ER+ breast tumors provided by collaborators from the ENIGMA consortium (https://enigmaconsortium.org/). This set included tumors from patients enrolled in the Australian Breast Cancer Family Registry (ABCFR)40, the Malaysian Breast Cancer Genetic Study (MyBrCa)41 and tumors from ATM variant carriers identified through routine HBOC multigene panel testing at the Mayo Clinic, USA and at the AC Camargo and Sirio-Libanês Hospitals in Brazil. In total, the replication set was composed of tumors from 29 carriers of an ATM PV/LPV, 6 carriers of an ATM VUS and 22 noncarriers (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1). None of these patients carried a known PV/LPV in another cancer susceptibility gene.

As a second replication set, we investigated WSI from 805 ER+ breast tumors with paired whole-exome sequencing data from TCGA42. Analysis of whole-exome sequencing (WES) data obtained from blood DNA or normal breast tissue DNA of the 805 patients allowed us to identify 10 ATM PV/LPV carriers with no PV/LPV in other above-mentioned cancer susceptibility genes. In the analysis, we included WSI from these 10 carriers and WSI from 20 noncarriers (frequency-matched on age at diagnosis, pathology stage and morphology) to have the same proportion of carriers and noncarriers as in the discovery set. The bioinformatics pipeline used to analyze the germline WES data is available at https://github.com/u900-epidemio/genesis.

ATM variants classification

ATM tumors were from patients carrying a germline ATM variant classified as pathogenic for A-T disorder or classified as PV or LPV in ClinVar43. We also considered tumors from carriers of a missense VUS with a minor allele frequency < 0.0005 in all GnomAD populations and a Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) Phred score > 20 according to CADD v1.6, therefore likely to have a deleterious effect on the protein function44.

Histopathological slide preparation, staining and scanning

Slide preparation (block cutting, embedding, fixation, permeation and staining) was done in the pathology department of the coordinating study center of each study.

Slides from CoF-AT2 and GENESIS were stained with HES, which is the routine staining protocol of slides for diagnosis in France, while slides from other countries were stained using a standard HE protocol. Hematoxylin stains cell nuclei in purplish blue and eosin stains the extracellular matrix and cytoplasm in pink, which allows to visualize different cellular and extracellular structures. In French laboratories, saffron is commonly added to the HE staining to highlight collagen fibers in the connective tissue of the extracellular matrix with an orange hue.

Tissue slides were scanned on Aperio scanners. WSI from CoF-AT2, GENESIS, SBCC, Mayo clinic and Brazil were generated at Institut Curie as .ndpi files, and tiles were extracted at magnification 10 (0.92 µm/pixel). Slides from ABCFR and MyBrCa were generated in the reference department of pathology of the respective coordinating study center as .svs files and tiles were extracted at magnification 10 (1 µm/pixel).

Deep learning analysis pipeline

We used the wsi_mil pipeline developed by Lazard et al. (2022)30 which is available at: https://github.com/trislaz/wsi_mil. This pipeline is composed of the four following steps (Fig. 3):

Image tiling and encoding: WSI are divided into tiles of 224 × 224 pixels and tiles with enough tissues are selected. Tiles are then embedded into a low-dimensional space using a neural network under the form of a value vector. Mapping each tile to an embedding representing the visual signal in the tile is generic and specifically trained for the downstream classification task.

Pipeline used to predict ATM status from the histopathological WSI.

The pipeline is composed of four main steps: 1) tiling and encoding of the slides, 2) scoring of the tiles by an attention module which is a multilayer perceptrons, 3) aggregation of the tiles by a weighted sum of the tile representations, with weights corresponding to scores learned in step 2, and 4) prediction of ATM status by a decision module which is a multilayer perceptrons.

Tile embedding

We tested several strategies for tile embedding with respect to their impact on ATM status prediction, evaluated in terms of BA and AUC. Specifically, we tested 1) a ResNet 18 model pretrained on the ImageNet database45, a database of natural images, i.e. images of very different nature as compared to pathology images, 2) the MoCo self-supervision scheme for representation learning46 pretrained on the TCGA-BRCA dataset, and 3) the CTransPath foundation model, pretrained with self-supervision on the whole TCGA dataset (https://github.com/Xiyue-Wang/TransPath).

Classification performances

Using the generated embeddings, we performed classification with a five-fold cross-validation procedure. If not specified, each experiment was repeated four times, and the classification performances were reported with the mean and the standard deviation of these four repetitions. Models were trained and validated with this five-fold cross-validation procedure on each dataset independently.

The large networks for tile encoding were trained on external datasets and not specifically fine-tuned for our classification problem. Specifically, we compared three tile-encoding networks: ImageNet, MoCo (pre-trained on TCGA-BRCA) and CTransPath. ImageNet and CTransPath trainings were not done by us. For MoCo training on TCGA-BRCA, we used the transformations Gaussian blur, crop and resize, color jitter, grayscale, horizontal and vertical symmetries, and a color augmentation in the Hematoxylin and Eosin specific space. The use of these pre-trained models allows to drastically reduce the number of parameters specifically optimized to the ATM-prediction problem.

To train the models for the ATM classification task, tile encodings were frozen and no additional augmentation was used at this stage. Models were trained using the following parameters: a batch size of 8 and for a maximum of 10 epochs. Early stopping based on the validation loss was used with a patience of 10 epochs. A dropout rate of 0.4 and a learning rate of 0.003 were employed. Feature embedding depth was set to 512 for ImageNet pre-trained models and 256 for MoCo and CTransPath models. While the number of parameters is larger than the number of samples, these regularization techniques allow to alleviate overfitting, which has been estimated using the cross-validation method.

Quantification of the coloration bias

The color intensity bias was assessed by calculating the intense coloration bias toward ATM tumors using the method by Zhao et al. 47 as followed: bias(ATM, bi) = cbi, ATMcbi, ATM+ c(bi, non−ATM), with c() denoting a co-occurrence between the intense coloration (bi) and the respective ATM status (ATM or non-ATM). To determine if this bias is amplified by the model, we subtracted the training biases, calculated in the training sets with the true ATM labels, from the prediction biases, calculated in the tests sets with the predicted ATM labels. Biases of each cross-validation were then averaged.

Identification of histological features specific to breast tumors developed by ATM pathogenic variant carriers

The best ensemble method over the four repetitions was used to identify the most predictive tiles of ATM and non-ATM PV/LPV carrying tumors. To achieve this, we applied the method by Lazard et al. (2022), where the importance of each tile is assessed in two ways. First, each tile is associated with an attention score that represents how much this tile contributes to the slide representation. Very low scores indicate that the tile has not been used for classification, while high scores indicate a strong influence of the tile on the slide representation and thus the downstream task. For each WSI, the n most attended tiles are selected as candidate tiles. These tiles are then presented to the decision module that is trained to predict ATM probability from slide representations (step 4 above). The k tiles having the highest probability to belong to each class are then selected. To set these numbers, n x N should be highly superior to k, N being the number of slides in the dataset. In the discovery set, N was 187 and we chose to set n to 20 and k to 300. We kept these parameters for the two replication sets.

These tiles were next projected using a UMAP dimension reduction technique to cluster them according to their histological patterns. The UMAP function was used with the following parameters: n_components = 2, n_neighbors = 15, min_dict = 0.5 and random_state = 42; the HDBSCAN function was then used to identify clusters with the following parameters: min_sample = 3, min_cluster_size = 15. The identified clusters were then reviewed by the reference pathologists for this study (L.T. and A.V.S.) who were blinded to carrier status.
