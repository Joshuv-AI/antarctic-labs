Dynamic evaluation of global sustainability: longitudinal ranking and spatial profiling across countries
Authors: Yin-Yin Huang, Xiangyan Wang, Lei Jiang, Tsai-Sung Lin, Ruey-Chyn Tsaur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study addresses the limitations of static evaluation models in global sustainability assessment by tracing longitudinal sustainability trajectories across 103 countries. We develop an integrated analytical framework that combines Dynamic TOPSIS with time-decayed entropy weighting to capture the temporal evolution of sustainability performance from 2014 to 2024. To enhance robustness, linear ranking results are cross-validated through non-linear spatial classification using K-means clustering with k-means + + initialization. Seven core indicators spanning economic, social, and environmental dimensions are employed to characterize the structural profiles of sustainability tiers. The results reveal a pronounced staircase effect in global sustainability, with countries converging into three stable tiers. The three-cluster solution is supported by a reduction in the Sum of Squared Errors from 26.34 to 8.06, with diminishing marginal improvements thereafter. Robustness tests show stable rankings, with no rank reversal among the top 20 countries and consistent positions within the top five. A notable gap is found in the Lagging Tier: despite relatively high environmental efficiency driven by low industrialization, sustainability progress is held back by weak human capital and institutional quality. The Leading Tier, by contrast, maintains balanced performance through sound governance and environmental management. By giving greater weight to recent observations through time-decayed weighting and cross-checking results through both linear and spatial methods, this study offers a dynamic alternative to static sustainability rankings. The findings point to education and governance gaps as key barriers in lower-tier nations, with implications for international interventions that go beyond purely economic assistance.Keywords: Dynamic TOPSIS, K-means clustering, Time-decayed weights, Sustainability tiers, Structural bottlenecksSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Geography, Geography, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The implementation of the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development requires high-precision evaluation frameworks to address regional disparities1–3. While GDP per capita has traditionally served as a primary developmental metric, its inability to reflect ecological and social dimensions has led to the exploration of multidimensional indexes4–6. Recent global instability underscores the need to integrate climate risk and governance quality into national performance assessments7–9. Furthermore, identifying institutional drivers such as Rule of Law and Human Capital are increasingly seen as prerequisites for nations to escape developmental stagnation10–12.

Despite these trends, existing evaluation frameworks remain limited by three critical issues: (1) a continued reliance on static GDP-centric models that mask ecological trade-offs4,6; (2) a dilution effect where the aggregation of longitudinal data without temporal decay obscures recent policy progress13,14; and (3) a lack of structural validation to identify institutional bottlenecks hindering the transition between development tiers11,15. While TOPSIS is a standard tool for national ranking, current models often overlook the evolving utility of temporal information and fail to provide spatial validation for identified rankings16–19.

In response to these gaps, this study develops an integrated evaluation framework leveraging six methodological scenarios (M1-M6) to cross-verify national sustainability performance. As detailed in the subsequent comparative analysis (Sect. "Comparative analysis of experimental scenarios and global rankings"), we establish static baselines (M1, M2) to contrast with our primary Dynamic Main Model (M3), while incorporating consistency (M4) and stability (M5) validations (utilizing Grey Relational Analysis and Monte Carlo simulations) alongside tier-based clustering (M6)16,20–22. This research seeks to solve: (1) how global trajectories evolve when prioritizing contemporary information utility; (2) the stability of rankings under stochastic perturbations; and (3) the structural indicators constituting the primary bottlenecks for lagging nations.

This work provides three original contributions spanning both theoretical and applied dimensions of sustainability evaluation. First, we develop a framework integrating six methodological scenarios (M1-M6) as a theoretically grounded cross-verification protocol that extends beyond conventional single-scenario assessments23. Second, this research introduces a dynamic weighting mechanism within the M3 model that captures the evolving utility of information, providing a more responsive trajectory analysis than traditional models14. Finally, our findings leverage the integration of dynamic ranking and spatial clustering to pinpoint the exact institutional thresholds required for tier transition, offering a level of policy granularity missing from aggregate ranking systems7,24.

Literature review

Multidimensional indicators for sustainable development

Following the thematic framework established by the United Nations3, this study selects seven core proxy indicators to capture the multifaceted nature of global sustainability. Given the multifaceted nature of sustainable development, these metrics serve as representative proxies for three main pillars: Economic (GDP per capita and Adjusted Net Savings), Social (Education Index, Life Expectancy, and Rule of Law), and Environmental (CO2 Emissions and the Environmental Performance Index). This selection aligns with the multidimensional evaluation approaches validated in recent literature4–6,25,26, so that the selected proxies cover both human development and ecological efficiency1,27.

Economic and social dimensions: beyond income metrics

The transition from traditional GDP per capita centric evaluations to multidimensional sustainability frameworks represents a fundamental shift in development economics4,6. Scholars argue that while GDP per capita reflects average economic output (SDG 8 & 9), it fails to account for the depreciation of natural capital and the long-term social costs of rapid industrialization5,11,28. In this context, Adjusted Net Savings (ANS) has emerged as a useful metric for evaluating genuine wealth, as it integrates environmental depletion and human capital investments into a nation’s savings profile1,2. These metrics together reflect the interplay between human development and institutional strength10–12. Furthermore, the Rule of Law (SDG 16) and institutional quality are identified as essential prerequisites for stable progress, as environmental regulations and democratic governance significantly moderate the impact of economic activities on sustainability7,10–12.

Environmental dimension: carbon intensity and ecological efficiency

The inclusion of CO2 Emissions and the Environmental Performance Index (EPI) is essential for tracking long-term sustainability trajectories11,26,27. As countries transition toward higher income levels, the challenge of decoupling economic growth from carbon intensity becomes paramount6,29–32. Furthermore, environmental performance is significantly moderated by institutional regulations and sectoral sustainability targets10,33,34. Consequently, robust policy frameworks are required to maintain ecosystem health and ensure that national trajectories remain statistically robust15,16,24.

Methodological evolution in sustainability assessment

Multi-criteria decision making (MCDM) in SDGs

The complexity of evaluating Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), characterized by conflicting economic and environmental objectives, necessitates the use of Multi-Criteria Decision-Making (MCDM) methodologies to synthesize heterogeneous data17,23,35–37. Among various MCDM techniques, the TOPSIS method(originally developed by38 is widely used to identify the shortest distance to ideal development targets13,18,19,38,39. To reduce subjectivity, researchers have used the Entropy Weight Method (EWM) to determine weights based on data dispersion14,28,39. These frameworks provide the foundation for assessing institutional commitment and ecological efficiency through a unified, evidence-based lens23,40,41.

The shift toward dynamic and longitudinal models

A significant evolution in recent literature is the movement beyond static averages toward dynamic longitudinal frameworks. Wang et al.14, have identified a dilution effect in traditional studies, arguing that equal weighting of longitudinal data obscures recent policy gains13,14. To address this issue, current research increasingly adopts dynamic weight allocation mechanisms that adjust indicator importance across multiple time points to capture fluctuating development trajectories14. These scholars emphasize the concept of Information Utility, where contemporary observations are assigned higher coefficients through time-decayed algorithms so that the evaluation reflects recent national trajectories14. This theoretical shift establishes Information Utility as a primary criterion for longitudinal assessments, emphasizing that the temporal relevance of data is as critical as its statistical weight in capturing evolving sustainability trends21,42.

Robustness, stability, and spatial validation: ensuring scientific rigor

Uncertainty and stochastic stability testing

The reliability of national rankings in complex MCDM models is frequently challenged by the sensitivity of weight assignments and inherent data noise16,21,40,41. To address these challenges, Monte Carlo simulation has emerged as a key technique for assessing the stochastic stability of multi-criteria assessments16,43. Baudry et al.43, combined Monte Carlo simulation with multi-actor multi-criteria analysis to account for parametric uncertainty, showing that probabilistic outputs improve decision confidence. Building on this, Cui et al.44, have extended this framework to hybrid MCDM models, where Monte Carlo iterations are coupled with weighted data structures to improve both ranking stability and decision reliability. By introducing random perturbations to indicator weights (typically 1,000 to 10,000 iterations) this method allows researchers to observe the probabilistic distribution of rankings rather than a single deterministic result16,21,42–44. Furthermore, such stochastic modeling ensures that performance benchmarks are statistically robust across diverse weighting configurations, significantly enhancing the credibility of policy recommendations40,41.

Consistency analysis in hierarchical rankings

In addition to stochastic stability, existing literature emphasizes that ensuring ordinal consistency between different methodological configurations is a prerequisite for scientific cross-verification13,23. Studies have shown that traditional correlation coefficients often fail to capture the non-linear nuances of ranking shifts in longitudinal data45. To address this methodological gap, Grey Relational Analysis (GRA) offers an alternative by evaluating the geometric similarity and trajectory overlaps between various ranking outputs13,41. This helps determine whether ranking changes reflect genuine performance shifts or are artifacts of weighting sensitivity. Consequently, the application of GRA has become a standard protocol for validating the transition between static baselines and more complex dynamic evaluation models19,23.

Unsupervised machine learning for performance tiering

Complementing ordinal assessments, scholars have increasingly integrated unsupervised machine learning to add a spatial and structural dimension to sustainability analysis20,22,46. Recent studies suggest that the K-means + + clustering algorithm offers greater stability in identifying performance tiers compared to traditional grouping methods20,22. By categorizing nations based on multidimensional structural similarities, this approach allows examination of how institutional variables differentiate high-performing from lower-performing clusters11,12,20.

Methodology

This study proposes a comprehensive dynamic multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) framework to evaluate the sustainable development performance of 103 countries. Unlike conventional static assessments that rely on single-period snapshots, our approach captures both the magnitude and the temporal trajectory of sustainability across an 11-year observation window from 2014 to 2024.

The systematic research framework, illustrated in Fig. 1, begins with data preprocessing of seven core SDG indicators. Logarithmic transformation is applied to reduce skewness and variance in economic and environmental metrics4–6. The core analysis contrasts static baselines (M1 and M2) with the dynamic main model (M3), which integrates a Dynamic Entropy Weight Method (DEWM) with time-decayed information utility to give greater weight to recent development trends14,28.

Robustness is assessed through Grey Relational Analysis (M4) for ordinal consistency13,41 and Monte Carlo simulations (M5) for stochastic stability testing under weight perturbations16,21. Data collection and preprocessing

The research dataset centers on 103 representative countries, categorized according to the World Bank’s income classification. In order to uphold scientific rigor and the validity of the longitudinal analysis, the initial data pool from 195 nations reported by the World Bank was subjected to a strict screening process. Nations with missing values in any of the seven indicators across the entire 2014–2024 observation window were excluded, resulting in a balanced panel of 103 countries (1,133 observations). This eliminates imputation bias and supports the reliability of subsequent entropy weight calculations. Despite the exclusion of certain regions due to data incompleteness, the final sample spans all major continents and development stages, covering a broad cross-section of global sustainability conditions.

Seven core indicators aligned with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) were selected to provide a holistic view of national performance:

The theoretical justification for each indicator is detailed in the section "Multidimensional indicators for sustainable development". The selection was further guided by the five criteria proposed by Keeney and Raiffa47: completeness, operationality, decomposability, non-redundancy, and minimum size. Each indicator addresses a distinct dimension of sustainable development without conceptual overlap, remains consistently measurable across the 103-country panel over the full 2014–2024 window, and has established precedent in validated sustainability assessment frameworks4,6,25. The number of indicators also falls within the 7 ± 2 range recommended by Saaty48, who drew on Miller’s (1956) cognitive manageability principle to suggest that decision matrices with fewer than nine criteria maintain greater judgemental consistency in MCDM applications.

Dominance of Institutional and Environmental Stability: Under the log-transformed DEWM framework, European nations consistently occupy the top 10 positions (e.g., Switzerland, Sweden). This dominance is attributed not only to economic output but primarily to their superior performance in the Rule of Law (RUL) and CO2 Intensity metrics. Notably, after mitigating extreme skewness through logarithmic scaling, the DEWM model identifies institutional quality as a more significant differentiator of sustainability than raw economic volume.

Robustness and comparative analysis

The M3 model represents an Objective Dynamic Framework based on the Dynamic Entropy Weight Method (DEWM). It is characterized as ‘objective’ because it eliminates human bias through entropy-based weight allocation, and ‘dynamic’ because it incorporates temporal variances across the 11-year study period.

Structural consistency and tier validation: K-means clustering (M6)

For the purpose of further validate the objectivity of the sustainability tiers, a K-means Clustering analysis (M6) was performed. While the Dynamic TOPSIS model (M3) provides a linear hierarchy, the K-means algorithm groups the 103 nations based on their multi-dimensional Euclidean proximity across the seven indicators. The convergence between these two independent methods ensures that the classification is statistically robust and not a byproduct of arbitrary thresholds.

Cluster centroid characteristics

Determining the optimal number of clusters, the Elbow Method was employed by calculating the Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS) for K ranging from 1 to 6. This “elbow” at K = 3 justifies the three-tier classification as the most stable structural representation of global sustainability disparities in this dataset.

Tier Stability under Dynamic Weighting: The integration of time-decayed entropy weights into the dynamic evaluation model provides a more precise reflection of national transitions along recent development trajectories compared to traditional static rankings. Results indicate a significant staircase effect in global sustainability structures, with tier distributions showing resilience across GRA and Monte Carlo stability tests.

Theoretical and policy implications

The empirical findings of this study carry several theoretical implications for the sustainability evaluation literature. The identification of a persistent staircase effect across 103 nations over an 11-year period provides empirical support for development trap theory, suggesting that structural inequalities between tiers are self-reinforcing rather than transitory11. The Lagging Tier’s paradox of high environmental scores driven by low industrialization rather than active policy intervention offers new empirical evidence for the environmental Kuznets curve debate, demonstrating that favorable environmental indicators in low-income economies should not be conflated with genuine ecological progress6,29. Furthermore, the consistent role of Rule of Law as the primary differentiator between the Leading and Lagging Tiers reinforces the theoretical centrality of SDG 16 as a cross-cutting enabler of sustainable development, extending prior arguments on institutional prerequisites for international sustainability transitions10,12.

Based on the refined centroid characteristics, this study proposes the following targeted governance strategies to bypass developmental bottlenecks and optimize global sustainability pathways:

Leading Tier (Cluster 1): Deepening Green Finance and Institutional Export Nations in this tier should leverage their institutional capital to further climate resilience. Beyond maintaining superior environmental efficiency (0.852), policy focus should shift toward pioneering next-generation carbon-neutral technologies and exporting successful governance models through multilateral frameworks to assist lower-tier transitions, thereby fostering global institutional convergence11,12.
