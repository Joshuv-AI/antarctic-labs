Non-vacuum gravitational effective action
Andrei O. Barvinsky, Farahmand Hasanov, Nikita Kolganov

Abstract
Curvature expansion for the heat kernel trace and the one-loop effective action is built for the wave operator of the theory in the quasi-thermal setup of a nonvacuum quantum state. This setup implies a non-static and non-stationary Euclidean gravitational background with periodic boundary conditions of the period β = 1 / T \beta=1/T , where T T plays the role of effective global temperature to be locally rescaled by the metric gravitational potential. The results are obtained in the approximation quadratic in metric perturbations on top of flat Euclidean space and covariantized in terms of spacetime curvature. Covariantization includes a special vector field ξ μ ​ ( x ) \xi^{\mu}(x) which generalizes the Killing vector of static geometries with time translation isometry to the case of a generic arbitrarily inhomogeneous metric subject to timelike periodicity condition. This vector field is obtained as a covariant metric functional to quadratic order in metric perturbations and gives rise to the local function T / ξ 2 ​ ( x ) T/\sqrt{\xi^{2}(x)} , ξ 2 ​ ( x ) = g μ ​ ν ​ ( x ) ​ ξ μ ​ ( x ) ​ ξ ν ​ ( x ) \xi^{2}(x)=g_{\mu\nu}(x)\xi^{\mu}(x)\xi^{\nu}(x) , reducing to Tolman temperature T / g 00 ​ ( x ) T/\sqrt{g_{00}(x)} on stationary manifolds with Killing symmetry. High “temperature” asymptotic behavior of the nonlocal formfactors—operator coefficients of the curvature tensor structures in the heat kernel and effective action—are obtained and possible cosmological applications of these results are discussed.

Introduction

The purpose of this work is to extend the results of
Barvinsky and Vilkovisky 1987
;
Barvinsky and Vilkovisky 1990a
on the covariant expansion of gravitational effective action to non-vacuum states potentially applicable in a wide range of nonequilibrium and nonlocal phenomena. The underlying curvature expansion was developed in the form of local Schwinger-DeWitt or Gilkey-Seeley heat kernel technique
Schwinger 1951
;
DeWitt 1964
;
Barvinsky and Vilkovisky 1985
;
Seeley 1967
;
Gilkey 1975
and its nonlocal version pioneered in
Deser et al. 1976
and later promoted to a universal method for generic field models in curved spacetime
Barvinsky and Vilkovisky 1987
;
Barvinsky and Vilkovisky 1990a
;
Barvinsky et al. 1993
. It has proved to be very efficient in calculation of divergences, anomalies and nonlocal effects in massless theories, and also underlies computations in such challenging problems, like renormalization of Hořava gravity models
Barvinsky et al. 2022
, where computational attempts based on conventional Feynman diagrammatic methods fail or the endeavors to proceed simply fear to tread.

Serious limitation of this nonlocal expansion for the ultraviolet (UV) finite part of the effective action, which is sensitive to the global spacetime geometry, is that in
Barvinsky and Vilkovisky 1987
;
Barvinsky and Vilkovisky 1990a
it was developed only for vacuum quantum states in asymptotically flat spacetime. Except several sound applications in cosmology and quantum field theory like
Donoghue and El-Menoufi 2014
;
Donoghue and El-Menoufi 2015a
;
Donoghue et al. 2017
, it was very often inappropriately extrapolated to black hole systems with a horizon, spatially closed models or the models with (A)dS-type boundary conditions, etc. (see some references in
Donoghue et al. 2017
). Moreover, it does not include modifications due to the choice of a nontrivial quantum state encoded either as quantum initial data (in-in problem
Barvinsky and Vilkovisky 1987
;
Adshead et al. 2009
) or as in-out states of the physical scattering theory setup. Here we try to fill up this omission for one-loop effective action within covariant expansion up to the second order in spacetime curvature and quantum matter field strengths.

Modelling a generic quantum state of the physical system is, of course, subject to a vast functional ambiguity in the choice of its (pure or mixed) density matrix. As is well known, one of the simplest cases of specification of this ambiguity is the situation with equilibrium canonical ensemble in static external fields, characterized by a single temperature parameter
T=1/\beta
and canonical density matrix whose partition function is represented by the Euclidean path integral over periodic fields with the period
\beta
. In fact, this setup can be generalized to non-equilibrium nonstationary states with their density matrix also prescribed by the Euclidean path integral, while the relevant generating functional for evolving quantum correlators is formulated in terms of quantum fields periodic on the Schwinger-Keldysh closed contour in the complex plane of time
Schwinger 1961
;
Keldysh 1965
;
Jordan 1986
;
Calzetta and Hu 1987
;
Ford and Woodard 2005
;
Onemli and Woodard 2002
;
Adshead et al. 2009
. Within this approach the generating functional can be obtained by analytic continuation from the real Euclidean time segment of length
\beta

Barvinsky and Kolganov 2024
, while the systems with a generic density matrix would follow from it by the variation of its sources. Thus, within this line of reasoning, specification of a rather generic quantum state consists in the calculation of effective action for quantum fields periodic on the complex Schwinger-Keldysh contour. The first step of this program is the effective action calculation on real Euclidean time segment of
\beta
length, which can be called a non-stationary quasi-thermal setup.

Local curvature expansion of the one-loop effective action at finite temperature for
static
geometries of spacetime metric with vanishing shift functions
g_{0i}=0
was built in
Dowker and Schofield 1988
. Non-local expansion for static geometries was developed in
Gusev and Zelnikov 1999
and subsequently extended to the stationary (non-zero
g_{0i}
) case in
Elías et al. 2017
;
Valle and Vazquez-Mozo 2025
. It leads to the thermal generalization of the Schwinger-DeWitt expansion, featuring the high temperature limit in terms of the local Tolman temperature parameter
T_{\rm Tolman}(x)
related to the Euclidean interval
\beta=1/T
by the relation
T_{\rm Tolman}(x)=T/\sqrt{g_{00}(x)}
. Here we extend this result to geometries without timelike Killing symmetry for the main building block of quantum effective action—the relevant heat kernel of the field theory wave operator—and the effective action itself in the approximation quadratic in spacetime curvature and matter field strengths.

The result turns out to be significantly more complicated due to non-stationary nature of the metric background. Moreover, it reveals a challenging problem, namely the covariantization of the final result. This involves converting the expression into a form that is manifestly covariant with respect to local diffeomorphisms and local gauge transformations in the matter sector, specifically, a form constructed solely in terms of spacetime and fibre-bundle curvature tensors and their covariant derivatives. This problem (called later in
Donoghue and El-Menoufi 2015b
nonlinear completion) was encountered in
Barvinsky and Vilkovisky 1987
;
Barvinsky and Vilkovisky 1990a
in the course of derivations for the vacuum case, where it was solved by the following procedure.

First, the spacetime metric gets decomposed into the sum of flat space metric
\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}
and the perturbation
h_{\mu\nu}
,
g_{\mu\nu}=\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu}
, but this split is performed in a generic coordinate system, rather than the one chosen by the Cartesian coordinate gauge
\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}
. In a generic coordinate system the definition of
\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}
is determined solely by the condition of its vanishing Riemann curvature
R^{\alpha}_{\;\;\beta\mu\nu}(\tilde{g})=0
which can be rewritten as the equation for the metric perturbation
h=h_{\mu\nu}
,
R^{\alpha}_{\;\;\beta\mu\nu}(g-h)=0
. This equation can iteratively be solved for
h
in some preferably Lorentz-invariant gauge imposed on the unknown
h
, which yields this perturbation as a nonlocal covariant series in the Riemann (or Ricci) curvature. For example, in the DeWitt background covariant gauge,
\nabla^{\nu}h_{\mu\nu}-\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla_{\mu}h=0
, this covariant series begins with the nonlocal term

h_{\mu\nu}=\tfrac{2}{\Box}R_{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{O}(R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}^{2})
,
where
\tfrac{1}{\Box}
denotes the Green’s function of the covariant d’Alembertian acting on second rank Ricci tensor
R_{\mu\nu}=R^{\alpha}_{\;\;\mu\alpha\nu}
.

Second, the perturbation theory in
h_{\mu\nu}
is developed for the quantity in question (that is the heat kernel of the wave operator or the corresponding effective action—the functional determinant of the wave operator). On top of the flat space background metric this step does not differ much from a conventional Feynman diagrammatic method whether it is performed in the coordinate or momentum space representation (see, for example,
Codello and Zanusso 2013
). The resulting Lorentz-invariant series in
h_{\mu\nu}
then was converted into the series in the original curvature tensor by substituting the expression for
h_{\mu\nu}
in terms of
R^{\alpha}_{\;\;\beta\mu\nu}(g)
and by rewriting all partial derivatives and flat space metric coefficients, occurring in the operator coefficients of
h_{\mu\nu}
, in terms of covariant derivatives
\nabla_{\mu}
and the full metric tensor
g_{\mu\nu}
. The same steps can be undertaken in the matter sector of the theory, where the role of
h_{\mu\nu}
and
R^{\alpha}_{\;\;\beta\mu\nu}
play the connection and fibre bundle curvature.

Ultimately, this procedure made order by order in powers of the curvature yields manifestly Lorentz-covariant and manifestly diffeomorphism invariant answer for the heat kernel, effective action and other objects in question. For the theory with the wave operator
F
,

F=-\Box+\hat{P},

(1.1)

this expansion procedure looks as follows. Here the covariant d’Alembertian

are acting on generic fibre bundle fields in the Euclidean

,

Consider the heat kernel of this operator—the kernel of its operator exponent with the proper time parameter

(1.2)

Covariant curvature expansion derived for such a setup in

\Re=(R^{\alpha}_{\;\;\beta\mu\nu},\hat{\cal R}_{\mu\nu},\hat{P})

\Tr K(\tau)=\frac{1}{(4\pi\tau)^{D/2}}\int d^{D}x\,\sqrt{g}\,\tr\biggl[\,\hat{1}+\tau\Bigl(\frac{1}{6}R\,\hat{1}-\hat{P}\Bigr)+\frac{1}{2}\tau^{2}\Bigl(R_{\mu\nu}\,f_{1}(-\tau\Box)\,R^{\mu\nu}\hat{1}\\

Obviously, this expression is fully Lorentz and diffeomorphism invariant, its nonlocal operator coefficients—functions of the full curved space d’Alembertians—are symmetric in the sense that their operator arguments can be integrated by parts without extra surface terms, which is guaranteed by the asymptotic behavior of curvatures at spacetime infinity.

For the generalization of (
1.5

h_{\mu\nu}
and its covariantization. As a result, the underlying periodicity in one spacetime coordinate, which will now play a distinguished role as compared to the rest of coordinates, will break manifest Lorentz invariance and indicate the presence of structures additional to metric and curvature tensors. Technically this means, along with the obvious replacement
\int_{R^{D}}d^{D}x\to\int_{0}^{\beta}dx^{0}\int_{R^{d}}d^{d}\boldsymbol{x}

h_{\mu\nu}

;

\bar{\xi}^{\mu}
in terms of which the results acquire a manifestly diffeomorphism invariant form. Covariantization in this case proceeds by replacing any noncovariant zeroth component of any tensor by its contraction with this Killing vector field, which has a trivial form
\bar{\xi}^{\mu}=\delta^{\mu}_{0}

In our setup such vector is a priori absent because spacetime does not possess infinitesimal Killing symmetries—in the covering space it has only finite shift “symmetry” associated with the displacement by the period
\beta

Thus, one of the major goals of this work will be the construction of a special vector field
\xi^{\mu}

This vector field will be obtained as a nonlocal covariant functional of metric expanded to second order in metric perturbations. As we will see, this functional has a two-parameter ambiguity which can be used to simplify the formalism, but thus far we do not have good principles to fix this ambiguity.

is introduced to keep the right dimension.

4.1
Vacuum contribution separation

It turns out that the answer for the heat kernel trace can be effectively separated into the vacuum part, corresponding to the zero temperature (or infinite
\beta
), and the pure thermal part. To show this, we use the Poisson summation formula to the sum in the definition (
2.7
) of the form-factor
f(\tau,\partial)
, that gives

\frac{\sqrt{4\pi\tau}}{\beta}\sum_{n}e^{-\tau(\omega_{n}+i\alpha\partial_{0})^{2}}=1+2\sum_{n\geq 1}e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}n^{2}}{4\tau}}\cosh(n\alpha\beta\partial_{0}).

(4.3)

Hence, the form-factor naturally splits into a sum

\displaystyle f(\tau,\partial)=f_{v}(-\tau\partial^{2})+f_{\beta}(\tau,\partial)

(4.4)

where the individual terms correspond to the first and second terms in the r.h.s. of (
4.3
). The explicit form of the vacuum form-factor
f_{v}
was previously defined in (
3.6
), whereas the expression for
f_{\beta}
reads

\displaystyle f_{\beta}(\tau,\partial)=2\int_{0}^{1}dx\,e^{\alpha(1-\alpha)\tau\partial^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}n^{2}}{4\tau}}\cosh(n\alpha\beta\partial_{0}),

(4.5)

The form factors
f_{v}
and
f_{\beta}
are referred to as the vacuum and thermal ones, respectively. We note that the vacuum form factor is the function of the combination
-\tau\partial^{2}
, whereas the thermal one is the function of the separate variables
\tau
and
\partial_{\mu}=(\partial_{0},\boldsymbol{\partial})
.
Similarly, setting
\partial_{0}=0
in (
4.3
), we have the splitting for the function
r(\tau)
, defined in (
2.5
)

r(\tau)=1+r_{\beta}(\tau),\qquad r_{\beta}(\tau)=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}n^{2}}{4\tau}}.

(4.6)

The splittings (
4.4
) and (
4.6
) induce the corresponding decomposition of the heat trace into vacuum and thermal components
\Tr K(\tau)=\Tr K_{v}(\tau)+\Tr K_{\beta}(\tau)
. Significant simplification takes place in the vacuum part of (
3.26
). In
\tau^{0}
and
\tau^{1}
parts of the heat trace
\tau\partial_{\tau}
acts on the vacuum part of the function
r(\tau)
, which equals to 1, according to (
4.6
). Hence, all
\tau\partial_{\tau}
-derivatives vanish in these parts.

To handle
\tau^{2}
-part we rewrite the form-factors
f_{I}(\tau,\partial)
, which correspond to the curvature
-
square part of the effective action (
3.26
), in the following general form

f_{I}(\tau,\partial)=\sum_{p=0}^{2}c_{I}^{[p]}(\partial,\tau\partial_{\tau})f^{[p]}(\tau,\partial).

(4.7)

where the coefficients
c^{[p]}_{I}
can be extracted from the definition (
3.23
).
Defining thermal part of the form-factors
f^{[p]}_{\beta}
via the decomposition
f^{[p]}=f^{[p]}_{v}+f^{[p]}_{\beta}
, we conclude that the same representation (
4.7
) holds separately for vacuum and thermal parts of the form-factors
f_{vI}
and
f_{\beta I}
. The vacuum part can be further simplified. As follows directly from the definition (
4.9
) of the vacuum form factor
f_{v}
, it satisfies the first
-
order differential equation

\tau\partial_{\tau}f_{v}(-\tau\partial^{2})=-\frac{1}{4}(-\tau\partial^{2}+2)f_{v}+\frac{1}{2}.

(4.8)

This equation allows us to express the
\tau\partial_{\tau}
-derivatives of
f_{v}
solely in terms of
f_{v}
. Upon applying (
4.8
), the vacuum part of the form factors
f_{5},\ldots f_{8}
vanishes, while that of
f_{1},\ldots f_{4}
simplifies to

f_{v1}=2f_{v}^{[2]},\qquad f_{v2}=-\frac{1}{4}f_{v}^{[2]}+\frac{1}{4}f_{v}^{[1]}+\frac{1}{16}f_{v},\qquad f_{v3}=-f_{v}^{[1]}-\frac{1}{2}f_{v},\qquad f_{v4}=f_{v},

(4.9)

so that we arrive to the well-known
Barvinsky and Vilkovisky 1990a
answer

\displaystyle\Tr K_{v}(\tau)={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(4\pi\tau)^{D/2}}\int d^{D}x\sqrt{g}\Bigl[1+\tau\bigl(\tfrac{1}{6}R-P\bigr)

(4.10)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\tau^{2}\Bigl(R_{\mu\nu}f_{v1}(-\tau\Box)R_{\mu\nu}+R\,f_{v2}(-\tau\Box)\,R+P\,f_{v3}(-\tau\Box)\,R+P\,f_{v4}(-\tau\Box)\,P\Bigr)\Bigr]

for the vacuum part of the heat trace (cf. (
1.5
)). Note that all
SO(D)
non-invariant quantities disappear from the answer for the vacuum part.
In contrast to the vacuum form-factors
f_{vI}
, the thermal form-factors
f_{\beta I}
do not admit any additional identities like (
4.8
), so they cannot be further simplified. However, simplifications arise when these factors are used in effective action calculations, as described below.

4.2
Effective action

Now, let us calculate the effective action from known expressions for the heat kernel trace.
Let us begin with the vacuum part. Substitution of (
4.10
) to the expression (
4.2
) for zeta-regularization of the effective action, gives the vanishing answer for its
\tau^{0}
and
\tau^{1}
parts have power-law dependence in
\tau
, since we define that the integrals of such functions over positive real line vanish in zeta regularization, i.e.
\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\,\tau^{q}\equiv 0
for any
q
. Next, substituting the quadratic
\tau^{2}
-part of the heat trace, defined by the corresponding form factors (
4.9
), to (
4.2
), we find

W_{v}(\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{D/2}}\int d^{D}x\sqrt{g}\,\Bigl(R_{\mu\nu}\varphi_{v1}(-\Box)R_{\mu\nu}+R\,\varphi_{v2}(-\Box)\,R+P\,\varphi_{v3}(-\Box)\,R+P\,\varphi_{v4}(-\Box)\,P\Bigr)

(4.11)

where the vacuum form factors are defined as

\varphi_{vI}(-\partial^{2})=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\biggl[\frac{\mu^{2s}}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\,\tau^{s-1}\frac{1}{\tau^{D/2}}\tau^{2}f_{vI}(-\tau\partial^{2})\biggr]\biggr|_{s=0}.

(4.12)

Next, it is useful to represent the form-factors
f_{vI}
, defined in (
4.9
) as

f_{vI}(-\tau\partial^{2})=\sum_{p=0}^{2}c_{vI}^{[p]}\,f_{v}^{[p]}(-\tau\partial^{2}).

(4.13)

where
c^{[p]}_{vI}
denote the constant coefficients that can be extracted from (
4.9
). Substituting the definition (
3.7
) of
f_{v}^{[p]}(\tau k^{2})
, we can rewrite the form-factors (
4.12
) as

\varphi_{vI}(-\partial^{2})=\sum_{p=0}^{2}c_{vI}^{[2-p]}\,(-\partial^{2})^{p-2}\,\varphi_{v}^{D/2-p}(\partial^{2}),

(4.14)

where we employ the property
\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\,\tau^{q}\equiv 0
once again and introduce the following basic form factors for the effective action

\displaystyle\varphi_{v}^{q}(-\partial^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\biggl[\frac{\mu^{2s}}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\,\tau^{s-1}\frac{1}{\tau^{q}}f_{v}(-\tau\partial^{2})\biggr]\biggr|_{s=0}

\displaystyle=(-)^{q+1}\frac{q!}{(2q+1)!}(k^{2})^{q}\Bigl(\log\bigl(-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\partial^2}{\mu^2}$}}\bigr)+H_{q}-2H_{2q+1}\Bigr),

(4.15)

Here
H_{l}=\sum_{n=1}^{l}1/n
denotes the so-called harmonic number.

Now let us proceed to the thermal part of the effective action. The latter is finite, i.e. the integral over
\tau
in (
4.2
) is convergent in
s\to 0
limit. Hence zeta-regularization can be lifted, so that the thermal part of the effective action reads

W_{\beta}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\tau}{\tau}\Tr K_{\beta}(\tau).

(4.16)

The
\tau^{0}
and
\tau^{1}
terms of the thermal part of the effective action consist of the expressions of the form

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\tau}{\tau}\frac{1}{\tau^{D/2}}\,\tau^{p}\,c(\tau\partial_{\tau})\,r_{\beta}(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\tau}{\tau}\frac{1}{\tau^{D/2}}\,\tau^{p}c(\tfrac{D}{2}-p)\,r_{\beta}(\tau)=c(\tfrac{D}{2}-p)\,\rho^{D/2-p}_{\beta}

(4.17)

with
p=0,1
and
r_{\beta}(\tau)
is defined defined in (
4.6
). Here in the first equality we integrate by parts and no boundary terms arise since
r_{\beta}
vanishes in the
\tau\to 0
limit along with all its
\tau
-derivatives, and introduce the following basic integral

\displaystyle\rho_{\beta}^{q}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\tau}{\tau}\frac{1}{\tau^{q}}r_{\beta}(\tau)=2\Bigl(\frac{2}{\beta}\Bigr)^{\!2q}\Gamma(q)\zeta(2q).

(4.18)

In the second equality, we exchange the order of summation (as it appears in the definition of
r_{\beta}
) and integration, evaluate the integral over
\tau
and use the definition of the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n^{s}
.

In its turn, substitution of the
\tau^{2}
-terms to the thermal part of the heat kernel to (
4.16
) leads to the following form of the effective action form-factors

\varphi_{\beta I}(\partial)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\tau}{\tau}\frac{1}{\tau^{D/2}}\tau^{2}f_{\beta I}(\tau,\partial)

(4.19)

where the explicit form of
f_{\beta I}
is the thermal part of the total form-factors
f_{I}
. The latter can be represented in the form (
4.7
), which can be rewritten using the definition of subtraction procedure (
3.17
) as

\tau^{2}f_{I}(\tau,\partial)=\sum_{p=0}^{2}\tau^{p}c^{p}_{I}(k,\tau\partial_{\tau})f(\tau,\partial)+\sum_{p=0,1}\tau^{p}d^{p}_{I}(\partial,\tau\partial_{\tau})r(\tau)

(4.20)

Substituting (
4.20
) to (
4.19
) we can integrate by parts with no appearance of the boundary terms as it was in (
4.17
), i.e.

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\tau}{\tau}\frac{1}{\tau^{D/2}}\tau^{p}\,c(\tau\partial_{\tau})f_{\beta}(\tau,\partial)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\tau}{\tau}\frac{1}{\tau^{D/2}}\,c\bigl(\tfrac{D}{2}-p\bigr)\tau^{p}f_{\beta}(\tau,\partial)

(4.21)

As a result, we conclude that all
\tau
-integrals involve the following basic one

\varphi_{\beta}^{p}(\partial)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\tau}{\tau}\frac{1}{\tau^{D/2}}\,\tau^{p}\,f_{\beta}(\tau,\partial)

(4.22)

in terms of which the form-factors (
4.19
) are expressed as

\varphi_{\beta I}(\partial)=\sum_{p=0}^{2}c^{p}_{I}(\partial,\tfrac{D}{2}-p)\,\varphi_{\beta}^{D/2-p}(\partial)+\sum_{p=0,1}d^{p}_{I}(\partial,\tfrac{D}{2}-p)\,\rho_{\beta}^{D/2-p}.

(4.23)

After substitution of the coefficients, the form-factors acquire the following explicit form

\displaystyle\varphi_{\beta 1}=-\frac{2D}{\partial^{4}}\varphi_{\beta}^{D/2}+\frac{1}{\partial^{2}}\varphi_{\beta}^{D/2-1}+\frac{2D}{\partial^{4}}\rho_{\beta}^{D/2}+\frac{D-3}{3\partial^{2}}\rho_{\beta}^{D/2-1}

(4.24)

\displaystyle\varphi_{\beta 2}=-\frac{D(D+2)}{4\partial^{2}}\varphi_{\beta}^{D/2}-\frac{1}{2\partial^{2}}\varphi_{\beta}^{D/2-1}-\frac{D(D+2)}{4\partial^{4}}\rho_{\beta}^{D/2}-\frac{D^{2}+2D-12}{24\partial^{2}}\rho_{\beta}^{D/2-1}

\displaystyle\varphi_{\beta 3}=-(D-2)\frac{1}{\partial^{2}}\varphi_{\beta}^{D/2-1}+\frac{D-2}{\partial^{2}}\rho_{\beta}^{D/2-1}

\displaystyle\varphi_{\beta 4}=\varphi_{\beta}^{D/2-2}

\displaystyle\varphi_{\beta 5}=-\frac{D(D+1)}{2\partial^{2}\boldsymbol{\partial}^{2}}\varphi_{\beta}^{D/2}+\frac{D}{4\boldsymbol{\partial}^{2}}\varphi_{\beta}^{D/2-1}+\frac{D(D+1)}{2\partial^{2}\boldsymbol{\partial}^{2}}\rho_{\beta}^{D/2}+\frac{D(D-2)}{12\boldsymbol{\partial}^{2}}\rho_{\beta}^{D/2-1}

\displaystyle\varphi_{\beta 6}=\frac{D-1}{\boldsymbol{\partial}^{2}}\varphi_{\beta}^{D/2-1}-\frac{\partial^{2}}{2\boldsymbol{\partial}^{2}}\varphi_{\beta}^{D/2-2}-\frac{D-1}{\boldsymbol{\partial}^{2}}\rho_{\beta}^{D/2-1}

\displaystyle\varphi_{\beta 7}=\frac{2(D+1)}{\boldsymbol{\partial}^{4}}\varphi_{\beta}^{D/2}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\boldsymbol{\partial}^{2}}\varphi_{\beta}^{D/2-1}-\frac{2(D+1)}{\boldsymbol{\partial}^{4}}\rho_{\beta}^{D/2}-(D-2)\frac{\partial^{2}}{\boldsymbol{\partial}^{4}}\rho_{\beta}^{D/2-1}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\varphi_{\beta 8}=\frac{D^{2}-1}{4\boldsymbol{\partial}^{4}}\varphi_{\beta}^{D/2}-\frac{1}{4}(D-1)\frac{\partial^{2}}{\boldsymbol{\partial}^{4}}\varphi_{\beta}^{D/2-1}+\frac{1}{16}\frac{\partial^{4}}{\boldsymbol{\partial}^{4}}\varphi_{\beta}^{D/2-2}\\
&\hskip 50.00008pt+\frac{D^{2}-1}{4\boldsymbol{\partial}^{2}}\rho_{\beta}^{D/2}-(D-1)(D-5)\frac{\partial^{2}}{24\boldsymbol{\partial}^{4}}\rho_{\beta}^{D/2-1}\end{aligned}

Collecting all terms of the thermal part of the effective action, we obtain

\displaystyle W_{\beta}=\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{D/2}}\int d^{D}x\sqrt{g}\biggl[\rho_{\beta}^{D/2}\frac{1}{(g_{\xi\xi})^{D/2}}

(4.25)

\displaystyle+\rho_{\beta}^{D/2-1}\frac{1}{(g_{\xi\xi})^{D/2-1}}\biggl(\frac{1}{6}R-P

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{6}(D-2)\biggl[\frac{R_{\xi\xi}}{g_{\xi\xi}}+\bigl(\nabla_{(\mu}\xi_{\nu)}\bigr)^{2}-\bigl(\nabla_{\mu}\xi^{\mu}\bigr)^{2}-\xi^{\mu}\,\nabla_{\xi}\xi^{\nu}\nabla_{(\mu}\xi_{\nu)}+\frac{3}{2}\xi^{\mu}\,\nabla^{\nu}\xi^{2}\,\nabla_{(\mu}\xi_{\nu)}\biggr]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{12}D(D-2)\Bigl[(\nabla_{\mu}\omega)^{2}-2\xi^{\mu}\,\nabla^{\nu}\xi^{2}\,\nabla_{(\mu}\xi_{\nu)}+2\xi^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}\nabla_{(\mu}\xi_{\nu)}\,\nabla_{\rho}\xi^{\rho}\Bigr]\biggr)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\biggl(R_{\mu\nu}\,\varphi_{\beta 1}\,R_{\mu\nu}+R\,\varphi_{\beta 2}\,R+P\,\varphi_{\beta 3}\,R+P\,\varphi_{\beta 4}\,P

\displaystyle+R\,\varphi_{\beta 5}\,{}^{(d)\!}R+P\,\varphi_{\beta 6}\,{}^{(d)\!}R+{}^{(d)\!}R_{ij}\,\varphi_{\beta 7}\,{}^{(d)\!}R_{ij}+{}^{(d)\!}R\,\varphi_{\beta 8}\,{}^{(d)\!}R\biggr)\biggr].

The calculation of the basic form-factor
\varphi_{\beta}^{p}(\partial)
defining, and appearing in the explicit expression of
\varphi_{\beta I}
, is rather involved and is done in
Barvinsky and Kolganov
. Here we reproduce answer in the following form (cf.
ik_{\mu}\leftrightarrow\partial_{\mu}
and
k_{\mu}=(\omega_{m},\boldsymbol{k})
)

\varphi_{\beta}^{p}(ik)=2\Bigl(\frac{2}{\beta}\Bigr)^{2p}\,k^{2p}\,\partial_{k^{2}}^{p}\bigl[\varOmega\,\varPhi_{p}(k)\bigr].

(4.26)

Here
\varOmega
is translation-like operator, acting on the test function as

\varOmega\varPhi(k)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl[\varPhi(k_{+})+\varPhi(k_{-})\bigr]+\frac{1}{2}\frac{k_{+}-k_{-}}{k_{+}+k_{-}}\bigl[\varPhi(k_{+})-\varPhi(k_{-})\bigr],\quad k_{\pm}=\sqrt{k^{2}-\omega_{m}^{2}}\pm i\omega_{m}.

(4.27)

Note that
k_{\pm}
are two different “square roots” of
k^{2}
in the sense that
|k_{\pm}|=k
, and
k_{+}k_{-}=k^{2}
. The function
\varPhi_{p}
has the following form

\varPhi_{p}(k)=(-)^{p}\frac{(2\pi)^{2p+1}}{(2p)!}\frac{1}{i\beta k}\bigl(\zeta^{\prime}(-2p,-\tfrac{i\beta}{4\pi}k)-\zeta^{\prime}(-2p,\tfrac{i\beta}{4\pi}k)\bigr)\\
-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2(2p)!}\Bigr(\frac{\beta k}{2}\Bigr)^{\!2p-1}+\sum_{l=0}^{p}\frac{2}{(2l+1)!}\zeta(2p-2l)\bigl(\log(\tfrac{\beta}{4\pi}k)+H_{2p}-H_{2l+1}\bigr)\Bigr(\frac{\beta k}{2}\Bigr)^{\!2l},

(4.28)

where
\zeta(s,a)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}1/(n+a)^{s}
is the Hurwitz zeta function, and prime denotes the derivative with respect to the first argument
s
.
The answer (
4.26
) after substitution of (
4.27
) and (
4.28
) is rather cumbersome. While it may be suitable for numerical calculations, it is of limited use for further analysis. Therefore, in the next section we will derive high-temperature expansions of the quantity (
4.26
), and use it in the corresponding expansions of the effective action.

4.3
High temperature expansion

Using the series expansion of Hurwitz zeta function, we develop the high-temperature expansion of the form factors (
4.26
), which is valid in
\beta\to 0
limit. The answer reads

\displaystyle\varphi_{\beta}^{q}(ik)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{l=0}^{q-1}(-)^{l}\frac{l!(q-l-1)!}{(2l+1)!}\zeta(2q-2l)\,{}_{2}F_{1}(q-l,l+1;\tfrac{1}{2};\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\omega_m^2}{k^2}$}})\,k^{2l}\Bigl(\frac{\beta}{2}\Bigr)^{\!2l-2q}+2(-)^{q}\biggl[\frac{\pi^{2}}{2^{2q}q!}\frac{(k^{2})^{2q}}{(\boldsymbol{k}^{2})^{q+\frac{1}{2}}}\beta^{-1}

\displaystyle\;\;+\frac{q!}{(2q+1)!}\bigl(\log\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\beta k}{4\pi}$}}+\tfrac{1}{2}H_{q}-H_{2q+1}+\gamma\bigr)k^{2q}-\frac{(q+1)!}{(2q+1)!}\,\frac{\omega_{m}^{2}}{k^{2}}\,{}_{3}F_{2}(1,1,q+2;\tfrac{3}{2},2;\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\omega_m^2}{k^2}$}})k^{2q}

\displaystyle\;\;+q!\sum_{l=q+1}^{\infty}\frac{(2l-2q)!}{(2l+1)!}\zeta(2l-2q+1)\,C_{2l-2q}^{(q+1)}(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\omega_m}{k}$}})\,k^{2l}\Bigl(\frac{\beta}{4\pi}\Bigr)^{\!2l-2q}\biggr].

(4.29)

Here the hypergeometric functions
{}_{2}F_{1}
and
{}_{3}F_{2}
reduce to elementary functions for the specific values of the parameters involved,
\gamma
is the Euler’s constant and
C_{l}^{(p)}
denotes the Gegenbauer polynomial. The first line contains negative powers of
\beta
, i.e. terms exhibiting power
-
law divergence in the high
-
temperature limit.
The second line consists of terms involving logarithms and constants in
\beta
.
Finally, the third line represents the subleading terms, which vanish at high temperatures.

Although we have obtained a closed
-
form expression for the effective action (
4.25
) and derived the high
-
temperature expansion of the basic form
-
factors, we argue that the result for the effective action obtained from (
3.26
) is not convenient for analysis in the high
-
temperature limit.
First, we show that its stationary counterpart (
3.15
) has a clear structure, allowing to decouple terms which are the most singular in the high-temperature limit. Specifically, the
\tau^{0}
and
\tau^{1}
-parts of the heat trace in the stationary case (
3.15
), correspond to the leading and subleading terms of the effective action in the
\beta\to 0
limit, respectively. Consequently, the
\tau^{2}
-part contains all remaining sub
-
subleading high
-
temperature contributions.
Next, we demonstrate that in the non
-
stationary case, the result (
3.26
) does not exhibit this structure, namely the
\tau^{2}
part now contribute to the leading and subleading orders, complicating the further analysis. Finally, we derive an alternative form of the heat trace that preserves the desirable property observed in the stationary case.

In the stationary case the basic form-factor simplifies a lot. Particularly,
\varOmega
, defined in (
4.27
), becomes the identity operator, hence (
4.26
) simplifies to

\bar{\varphi}_{\beta}^{p}(ik)=2\Bigl(\frac{2}{\beta}\Bigr)^{2p}\,k^{2p}\,\partial_{k^{2}}^{p}\varPhi_{p}(k)

(4.30)

and the high-temperature expansion (
4.3
) takes simpler form

\bar{\varphi}_{\beta}^{q}(ik)=2\sum_{l=0}^{q-1}(-)^{l}\frac{l!(q-l-1)!}{(2l+1)!}\zeta(2q-2l)\,k^{2l}\Bigl(\frac{\beta}{2}\Bigr)^{\!2l-2q}\\
+2(-)^{q}\biggl[\frac{\pi^{2}}{2^{2q}q!}(k^{2})^{q-\frac{1}{2}}\beta^{-1}+\frac{q!}{(2q+1)!}\bigl(\log\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\beta k}{4\pi}$}}+\tfrac{1}{2}H_{q}-H_{2q+1}+\gamma\bigr)k^{2q}\\
+\frac{q!}{(2q+1)!}\sum_{l=q+1}^{\infty}\zeta(2l-2q+1)\,k^{2l}\Bigl(\frac{\beta}{4\pi}\Bigr)^{\!2l-2q}\biggr].

(4.31)

Note that in contrast to non-stationary situation, non-locality only appears in
\beta^{-1}
-term.

Now, we are ready to substitute the thermal part of the answer (
3.11
) for heat trace into the expression (
4.16
) for the thermal part of the heat trace. According to (
4.18
), its
\tau^{0}
part is proportional to
\rho_{\beta}{}^{\!\!\!\!D/2}\propto\beta^{-D}
, and define the leading-order term in the high-temperature limit
\beta\to 0
. Similarly,
\tau^{1}
-part after substitution to (
4.16
) is proportional to
\rho_{\beta}{}^{\!\!\!D/2-1}\propto\beta^{-D+2}
, which is subleading in the high-temperature limit. Now, our aim to demonstrate that the remaining
\tau^{2}
-part is sub-subleading, i.e. less singular than
\beta^{-D+2}
in the high-temperature limit. Substituting this part to (
4.16
) we obtain the stationary version of the form-factors (
4.19
). Now, in contrast to the non-stationary case, we do not expand the
\bar{f}_{\beta}^{[p]}(\tau,\partial)
in terms of
\bar{f}_{\beta}(\tau,\partial)
, but rather use the representation (
4.7
) for
\bar{f}_{\beta I}(\tau,\partial)
. Substituting the latter to (
4.19
) we find that the result is naturally expressed in terms of subtracted version of the effective action thermal form-factors

\bar{\varphi}_{\beta}^{q[p]}(\partial)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\tau}{\tau^{1+q}}\bar{f}_{\beta}^{[p]}(\tau,\partial)

(4.32)

we obtain

\displaystyle\bar{\varphi}_{\beta I}(\partial)=\sum_{p=0}^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\tau}{\tau}\frac{1}{\tau^{D/2}}\tau^{2}c_{I}^{p}(\partial,\tau\partial_{\tau})\bar{f}_{\beta}^{[p]}(\tau,\partial)=\sum_{p=0}^{2}c_{I}^{p}(\partial,\tfrac{D}{2}-2)\bar{\varphi}_{\beta}^{D/2-2[p]}(\partial).

(4.33)

It can be shown, that the form-factors
\bar{\varphi}_{\beta}^{q[p]}(\partial)
coincide with
\bar{\varphi}_{\beta}^{q+p}(\partial)
, up to overall factor and subtraction of
p
leading order in
\beta\to 0
terms (cf. the high-temperature expansion (
4.31
)), specifically

\bar{\varphi}_{\beta}^{q[p]}(\partial)=(-\partial^{2})^{-p}\,\bar{\varphi}_{\beta}^{\,q+p}(\partial)\bigr|_{\text{no $\beta^{-l}$, $l>2q$}}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{O}(\beta^{-2q}),&&\quad q>0\\
&\mathcal{O}(\beta^{-1}),&&\quad q=0\end{aligned}\right.

(4.34)

Thus, from (
4.33
) we conclude that in the stationary case the
\tau^{2}
part of the heat trace correspond to the part of the effective action whose most singular part behaves as
\beta^{-1}
in
D=4
and as
\beta^{-(D-2)}
in
D\geq 6
.

It turns out that a direct (naive) generalization of the form-factors (
4.32
) to the non-stationary case, namely

\varphi_{\beta}^{q[p]}(\partial)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\tau}{\tau^{1+q}}f_{\beta}^{[p]}(\tau,\partial)

(4.35)

spoils the property (
4.34
) of the high-temperature behavior. Specifically, instead of the latter we have the following high-temperature behavior
\varphi_{\beta}^{q[p]}(\partial)=\mathcal{O}(\beta^{-2(p+q)})
for
p>0
. This particularly implies that
\tau^{2}
-part of heat trace, written in the form (
3.26
), contribute to leading and subleading terms of the thermal part of the effective action, in contrast to the stationary situation. The reason is that in the stationary case the subtraction procedure for
\bar{f}_{\beta}^{[p]}(\tau,\partial)
leads to cancellation of the first
p
leading-order terms in
\beta\to 0
limit in its effective action counterpart
\bar{\varphi}^{q[p]}_{\beta}(\partial)
. In the non-stationary case, the subtraction procedure
f_{\beta}^{[p]}(\tau,\partial)
, does not yield the same result for the effective action form-factor
\varphi_{\beta}^{q[p]}(\partial)
.

5
Discussion

Main results of this work comprise the curvature expansion for the heat kernel trace (
3.26
) and the one-loop effective action (
4.25
) with the explicit expressions for their gravitational formfactors (
3.23
) and (
4.24
) along with their high and low “temperature” asymptotic behaviors in the quasithermal setup of a nonvacuum quantum state. This setup implies a non-static and non-stationary Euclidean background spacetime with periodic boundary conditions of the period
\beta=1/T
where
T
plays the role of effective global temperature to be locally modified by the metric gravitational potential. The results are obtained in the approximation quadratic in metric perturbations on top of flat Euclidean space and covariantized, or in the language of
Donoghue and El-Menoufi 2015b
nonlinearly completed, in terms of spacetime curvature. Covariantization includes a special vector field
\xi^{\mu}(x)
which generalizes the Killing vector of static geometries with time translation isometry to the case of a generic arbitrarily inhomogeneous metric subject to a single timelike periodicity condition. This vector field is obtained as a covariant metric functional to quadratic order in metric perturbations and gives rise to the local function
T/\xi^{2}(x)
,
\xi^{2}(x)=g_{\mu\nu}(x)\xi^{\mu}(x)\xi^{\nu}(x)
, reducing to Tolman temperature
T/g_{00}(x)
on stationary manifolds with Killing symmetry when
\xi^{\mu}(x)=\delta^{\mu}_{0}
.

Obtained algoritms look frustratingly complicated—addition of a single dimensional parameter
\beta
with the requirement of periodic boundary conditions immediately gives rise to numerous new tensor structures and significantly extends the set of nonlocal formfactors and their complexity. At the same time, this is only a small piece of the total effective action, quadratic in curvatures, not to mention that this is just one-loop approximation. Moreover, it is restricted to the case of a very simple topology of a spatial slice of spacetime, which is supposed to be asymptotically flat in spatial directions. Though potentially the above results in view of their universality can be meaningful in various applications, it is worth pointing out a concrete physical setup in which all this formalism can be directly useful despite all its perturbation theory limitations.

Another objection, that can be raised against the usefulness of this universal algorithm and its covariantization, is the complexity of nonlocal formfactor functions of the
exact
curved-space d’Alembertian or Laplacian. Their feasibility can be called in question as long as their implementation is not available in exact form unlike their flat-space counterparts. For the perturbation scheme to be feasible and practicable, all ingredients of its leading order should be exactly available in analytical form—the problem raised in
Donoghue and El-Menoufi 2015b
. This problem is not at all compelling for local differential operator structures, but may be very stringent for nonlocal formfactors whose exact kernels are not known. Various approaches to this difficulty are conceivable, ranging from the search for special representations of these formfactors, like spectral representations of
Barvinsky and Vilkovisky 1990b
;
Vilkovisky 2008
, to a possible retreat back to their analytical flat space form.

With regard to the above mentioned limitation of the small curvature and one-loop approximation we can point out to a concrete model that served as a main motivation for this work. This is a model of initial conditions for inflationary cosmology in the form of the microcanonical density matrix
Barvinsky 2007
represented by the Euclidean path integral over periodic configurations. When the model is dominated by numerous conformally invariant fields it incorporates the quasi-thermal stage preceding inflation
Barvinsky and Kamenshchik 2006
and provides the origin of the Higgs-type or Starobinsky
R^{2}
-type inflationary scenario
Starobinsky 1980
;
Barvinsky et al. 2015
. This scenario turns out to be a subplanckian phenomenon subject to semiclassical
1/N
-perturbation theory in the number of higher spin conformal fields
Barvinsky 2016
, which justifies the use of curvature expansion.

Thus, the above results on the effective action for nonvacuum states become critically important for the studies of this model, especially for its CMBR spectrum, in particular, because the quadratic order in curvatures is responsible for the propagator of cosmological perturbations. It should be emphasized that the flat space background of our nonvacuum perturbation theory does not contradict the cosmological Friedmann metric background, because the conformal mode of the Friedmann metric is treated in
Barvinsky and Kamenshchik 2006
;
Barvinsky et al. 2015
by the nonperturbative method of conformal transformation from the Friedmann universe to asymptotically flat spacetime. The contribution of this transformation for conformal fields is exactly calculable via their one-loop trace anomaly, and for higher spin conformal fields, due to their higher-derivative superrenormalizable nature, Weyl anomaly is restricted to the one-loop order of the semiclassical expansion. Similar mechanisms justifying the validity of one-loop results work also within the scope of recently suggested models of infinite-derivative quantum gravity with nonlocal formfactors based on entire functions
Buoninfante et al. 2018
;
Koshelev et al. 2018
;
Koshelev et al. 2025
—UV divergences in these models can also be restricted to the one-loop order approximation.

Direct physical predictions following from the structure of the effective action go beyond the material of this paper. However, already at this stage one can infer some info on the form of its quasi-thermal corrections to gravitational equations. In particular, zero order curvature corrections represent a kind of a radiation component, because the metric variation of the first term of (
3.26
) is traceless. Terms linear in the curvature are expected to be anisotropic in spacetime because of the presence of the vector
\xi^{\mu}
, which means that the thermally corrected effective gravitational coupling “constant” becomes anisotropic and, moreover, nonlocal tensor, etc. Details of these modifications will be considered in future publications.

To accomplish this paper let us briefly dwell on the list of problems subject to further studies. One of them is unclarified geometrical meaning of the vector field
\xi^{\mu}
generalizing the notion of the Killing isometry to generic inhomogeneous metrics. This includes the task of clarifying the two-parameter ambiguity in its construction, the behavior of its integral curves and the relation to the geometrical meaning of the period parameter
\beta
.

The second most important task is the construction of the Schwinger-Keldysh generating functional from the obtained Euclidean effective action. This corresponds to the deformation of the Euclidean closed contour into the complex plane of time, where the Lorentzian segments of this contour are associated with the unitary back and forth evolution in physical time—bras and kets of the path integral
Witten 2026
;
Fumagalli et al. 2025
. From
Barvinsky and Kolganov 2024
it is known that various Green’s functions of Schwinger-Keldysh diagrammatic technique for the density matrix of the system prescribed by the Euclidean path integral can be obtained by analytical continuation from the purely Euclidean contour and, moreover, satisfy famous KMS condition (contrary to observer’s no-boundary states of
Blommaert et al. 2025
. However, the resulting physical field correlators on these Lorentzian segments hardly obey the same analytical continuation rule for a generic inhomogeneous geometry, in contrast with the homogeneous de Sitter space where this continuation seems possible
Higuchi et al. 2011
. Probably, the needed analytical rule is more complicated and includes some extra causality retardation prescription like the one observed for vacuum setup in asymptotically flat spacetime
Barvinsky and Vilkovisky 1987
.
