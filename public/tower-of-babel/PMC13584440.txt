The moderating effect of depression on social media use and online social support: differential effects on psychological well-being
Authors: Ariel Pollock Star, Yaacov G. Bachner, Nofar Tsur, Moshe Shmueli, Norm O’Rourke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSocial support is integral to the psychological well-being of most people but is not equally available to all. For instance, traditional in-person social support may be less accessible to those who experience depression, despite the well-established role of social support in fostering recovery and forestalling relapse. Today, social support occurs both in person and online; it remains unclear, however, whether online social support functions similarly to, or distinctly from, in-person support. We contend that depression fundamentally affects social media use (SMU) and perceived online social support. Accordingly, depression status was examined as a moderator of associations between social media use and perceived online social support in relation to psychological well-being.MethodsFor this study, online social support was measured by the Brief Online Social Support Scale (BrOS-12), and psychological well-being was operationalized as (+) life satisfaction and (-) loneliness. We recruited a sample of 340 adults with no reported mental health diagnoses; participants were then divided into two groups on the basis of their responses to a depression screening measure (i.e., euthymic & dysphoric). Separate multivariate path models were estimated for each group, and moderation was tested using invariance analyses to compare groups. Bootstrapping procedures were used to estimate the statistical significance of indirect effects.ResultsOur findings suggest that the influence of social media and perceived online social support on well-being is primarily indirect, and differs between those with and without depression. Active SMU was positively associated with online social support which in turn predicted lower loneliness and higher life satisfaction. Evidence of moderation emerged in the stronger inverse association between online social support and loneliness for dysphoric participants.ConclusionsThese results are consistent with social compensation theory which contends that online social support plays a particularly important role for adults with depression who report lower levels of in-person social support. More broadly, results highlight the importance of distinguishing between types of social media engagement and individual psychological contexts when examining how digital environments function as risks or resources for mental health.

Introduction

Social media are ubiquitous to daily life, used by more than 92% of the global adult population [1]. As a result, many interactions have moved online including social interaction and social support that can today take place both in-person and online [2, 3]. Whereas some aspects of in-person social support are easy to access online such as information and emotional support [4, 5], tangible assistance is more difficult or impossible to give or receive online (e.g., walking a dog or watering plants).

Whether online social support fosters psychological well-being in ways similar to in-person social support, however, may depend on individual characteristics and differ across contexts [6]. For instance, individuals with lower levels of traditional social support may benefit more from online interactions than those with adequate or good support. By creating new social circles and contacts distinct from their “offline” lives [7], persons with depression can compensate for limited in-person support [8]. Known as social compensation theory, this suggests that online social support is a substitute for, not an adjunct to, in-person support [9].

Most research examining associations between social media use (SMU) and depression assumes that depression is a consequence of SMU [10, 11]. Yet evidence for a causal relationship, particularly at the population level, is unsupported [12]. Instead, depression may not be a consequence of SMU, but rather a variable which moderates how social media and social support affect well-being. Depression is not simply a clinical condition but a cognitive phenomenon that alters perception and behavior [13].

A defining feature of depression is the presence of cognitive distortions that influence how individuals perceive themselves, others, and their social environments. Cognitive theories of depression suggest that negative cognitive schema lead individuals to selectively attend to negative information, interpret ambiguous social cues pessimistically, and discount positive social feedback [14, 15]. These biases shape how social interactions are perceived and remembered, often reinforcing expectations of rejection or lack of support. According to Everaert and colleagues [16], adults experiencing depression may interpret the same social interaction differently than those free of depression.

Depression is also associated with behavioral changes that alter the structure of social relationships. Adults experiencing depression commonly withdraw from in-person social interaction, report smaller social networks, and perceive lower levels of available support [8, 17]. Reduced participation in offline social networks limits opportunities for traditional social support, though such support is known to buffer against stress and distress. In this context, digital environments may provide alternative opportunities for interaction and support that are more accessible to adults with depression.

Taken together, these cognitive and behavioral features suggest that depression fundamentally alters how online environments are experienced and function. Rather than social media exerting uniform effects across users, adults with depression experience a different online reality, interpreting social signals, support exchanges, and social comparison processes from a skewed, negative perspective [16]. That is, depression may not be an outcome of social media use but a contextual factor that shapes how online interactions affect psychological well-being. As depression alters both the availability of in-person support and the interpretation of social interactions, associations between social media engagement, perceived online support, and psychological well-being may differ between individuals with and free of depression.

The present study treats depression not as an outcome of social media use but a moderator that significantly affects social media use and online social support in relation to psychological well-being. Moderation was tested by multi-group path analyses computed independently for persons with and free of depression. Invariance or comparative analyses were next performed to identify model elements that significantly differ between groups. This approach frames depression not as a clinical outcome of digital environments, but as a contextual factor that fundamentally affects how adults experience and derive support from online social interaction.

Literature review

In-person versus online social support

In-person social support is a multidimensional construct known to foster mental health, in part, by buffering against stress [18, 19]. Broadly, social support refers to the function and quality of relationships [5]. However, the construct of social support is complex, commonly divided into types of support, sources of support, and perceived versus received support. Types of support include emotional support (e.g., intimacy, caring, concern), instrumental assistance, tangible resources, informational guidance, and social companionship or networking (e.g., togetherness, shared experience) [20–22]. Sources of support include friends, parents, other relatives, coworkers, and various forms of community (e.g., religious, geographic, interest-based) [22, 23].

In recent years, online social support has emerged as a distinct mode of social support [24]. Social relationships conducted online may be supplementary, complementary, or completely distinct from traditional in-person social connections [25]; they may be conducted entirely online or as an extension of existing in-person relationships. Yet the nature of online social support differs from in-person social support. Research on online social support has primarily focused on support provided via special-interest communities or online support groups, leading to a presumption that social support provided online is primarily informational or instrumental [26–28]. However, online social support differs depending on the context, person, and target. It may be a more limited form of support than in-person (e.g., less tangible than help when ill, in need of transportation, food support) or it may create new opportunities for support (e.g., more access to informational or emotional support, and ease in making connections across geographical and conventional/social constraints) [4].

Social media platforms allow people to interact with others, most of whom they will never meet, and function as an additional or alternative means of receiving certain forms of social support. In one study, online social support was found to provide an independent and distinct network of contacts for young adults with limited in-person support, whereas for those reporting strong in-person social support networks, online networks were more likely to simply reflect in-person networks [25]. Another study similarly found that the positive association between frequency of social interaction online and perceived social support was stronger for those who reported fewer friends [29].

Online interactions which build or bolster new relationships distinct from those offline seem to support the social compensation theory, whereby individuals with weaker in-person social networks form distinct and compensatory social networks online [7]. Existing research on online interaction supports the social compensation theory, at least for those with low levels of social interaction or in-person social support [25, 29–32]. However, online social support is not well defined and may have considerable, little, or no overlap with in-person social support both in terms of function and relationship to mental health. In part, this is due to difficulties in defining and measuring the construct of online social support. Online social support is typically measured using adapted scales initially designed to measure in-person support, rather than instruments specifically developed to measure the unique facets and characteristics of online support [25, 29, 30, 33]. The parameters for quality, type, and source of online social support, as well as its role in contributing to well-being, remain unclear.

Depression and social support

Research indicates that social support serves a protective function by fostering recovery from major depressive episodes and forestalling relapse [17]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, higher social support reported by healthcare workers was associated with lower depression and anxiety symptom levels [2, 3]; social support also appears to mediate the association between gratitude and depression [34]. By corollary, lower social support is associated with greater anxiety and depression including self-harm [35, 36].

Unfortunately, depression is also often associated with social isolation, impeding the protective benefits of in-person social support which may not be feasible for those who experience chronic depression [8]. In response, online social support may provide a compensatory opportunity for adults with depression and low levels of in-person social support to benefit from online social support.

The online environment of social media

Research conducted with general adult samples underscores that social media use is not a uniform behavior [37]; instead, social media are used in various ways with differential effects on mental health and well-being [38]. The type of platform and mode of SMU may play a role [12]. Additionally, active versus passive SMU patterns appear to have opposing associations with social comparison and, in turn, depressive symptoms [37].

Active SMU entails direct engagement with social media, such as posting comments or commenting on posts. Active use can be further divided into active social use, which involves direct interaction with other users, and active nonsocial use, which involves producing or sharing content without direct interpersonal exchange. In contrast, passive SMU involves consuming online information without posting or commenting (e.g., scrolling news feeds, viewing posts). This behavior allows the user to observe other people while maintaining relative anonymity.

In the context of social media use, well-being and depressive affect have repeatedly been identified as public health concerns. A growing body of research reports associations between excessive social media use and depression among adolescents and young adults [10, 11, 39]. Research specific to Instagram, for example, reports that 13% of girls in the UK attributed their suicidal ideation to the photo-sharing platform [40]. Some studies suggest that excessive social media use is associated with greater symptom levels among those with clinical depression [41], but most research has been performed with convenience samples of college-age or other young adults, limiting generalizability to other populations.

Additionally, depression is almost reflexively considered an outcome of SMU. Yet an advisory report issued by the US Surgeon General [42] suggests that there is a need for research that examines the role of individual differences and contexts that may impact the way that social media affect well-being. However, social media may not cause depression; depression may instead fundamentally change how SMU and online social support are experienced with different effects on psychological well-being.

This study uses path analyses to examine how depression alters the relationship between social media use and online social support in relation to psychological well-being. Specifically, the following questions are addressed:

Methods

For this study, 340 participants were recruited from March to October 2024 using three methods: prior research participants who agreed to contact [43]; social media advertising on Facebook and Instagram; and the Positly recruitment platform.

Positly is increasingly used as a recruitment vehicle in social science and health research [44]. The system excludes participant responses if descriptive information contradicts that previously provided or if questionnaire responses are mostly missing or determined to be unreliable (i.e., rapid responding without reading questions). Positly is designed to work on the Qualtrics questionnaire platform. Anonymized sociodemographic information is also provided (e.g., age, household income) along with questionnaire responses.

Social media advertising has repeatedly and effectively been used as a recruitment tool in mental health research [45]. It enables recruitment of individuals who may be depressed and/or be socially isolated, meeting them ‘where they are’ in their homes and on their phones. Social media advertising is generally more cost-effective than traditional mass advertising and can be used to recruit invisible and other hard to reach populations [45, 46].

Participants were initially screened by Positly to include only adults with no mental health history (i.e., no psychiatric diagnoses). We later divided those responding in clinical range to the Center for Epidemiological Studies–Depression (CES-D) Scale [47] (n = 112 dysphoric; CES-D M = 27.58, SD = 10.30) from participants responding below threshold (n = 228 euthymic; CES-D M = 5.71, SD = 4.52, t [338] = 26.85, p < .01). That is, dysphoric participants are probably depressed but have no formal diagnosis. The existence of the former is likely as approximately two-thirds of persons with depression have no formal diagnosis [48]. Designating participants as either eutythymic or dysphoric allowed us to compute and compare path models, and test if depression moderates associations between social media use, social support and psychological well-being.

To facilitate and hasten data collection, participants recruited from previous studies as well as those recruited via social media advertising were offered the chance to win a lottery prize of $500 USD after completing study questionnaires. Those recruited via Positly were also paid according to time required to fully respond to questionnaires. (Clinical trial number: not applicable.)

Instruments

Center for Epidemiological Studies–Depression Scale (CES-D) [47] is a 20-item measure of depressive symptoms. Responses describe the frequency of various symptoms over the past week on a Likert scale ranging from none of the time (0) to most of the time (3). CES-D scores 16 or higher are suggestive of clinical symptomatology [49].

Meta-analytic research and validation studies indicate that the CES-D typically achieves high sensitivity (true positives) in the range of 85% to 90% yet lower specificity between 65% and 75% (true negatives). This has been consistently reported in large-scale epidemiological analyses and cross-cultural research with diverse populations [49]. Overall, the existing literature concludes that the CES-D is highly effective for identifying depressed adults in community. The CES-D is not a diagnostic tool, yet responses are commonly corroborated by structured diagnostic interviews (i.e., effective screening measure).

The Passive and Active Facebook Use Measure (PAUM) [50] was developed to study the differential effects of Facebook use. Exploratory factor analyses suggest three distinct patterns: active social, active nonsocial, and passive use. Internal consistency across subscales ranges from adequate to good (0.70 < α < 0.81). For this study, we replaced the word “Facebook” with “social media” to extend measurement across platforms.

UCLA Loneliness Scale (ULS-8) [51] is an abbreviated measure developed to identify associations between loneliness and health-related behaviors [52]. ULS-8 responses are reported along a Likert scale ranging from I never feel this way (0) to I often feel this way (3). Internal consistency of ULS-8 responses is high (α = 0.84) [53].

The Social Support Scale (BS6) [54] measures perceived support, composed of both emotional-informational support and tangible support. Reliability of BS6 responses is high (α = 0.90 & α = 0.92) [54].

The Brief Online Social Support Scale (BrOS-12) [55] is an abridged version of the Online Social Support Scale [56], developed to measure subjective social support received online. Responses are reported on a Likert scale ranging from never (0) to a lot (4). BrOS-12 factors measure: instrumental support, informational support, esteem/emotional support, and social companionship. Internal consistency of BrOS-12 responses was high for the full scale (α = 0.94 & α = 0.93) and within ideal parameters for each factor (0.82 < α < 0.91).

The Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) measures perceived quality of life based on person-specific criteria [57]. The SWLS is composed of five questions (e.g., “The conditions of my life are excellent”) with response alternatives ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). Higher totals are suggestive of greater life satisfaction [58]. Test–retest reliability over 1 month has been reported as r = .84. Concurrent validity of SWLS responses has been demonstrated relative to the Fordyce Global Scale (r = .82). Internal consistency has been reported as α = 0.89 [58].

A socio-demographic questionnaire was constructed asking participants to report date of birth, gender, country of residence, relationship status, education, ethnicity, and occupation. All participants were asked to list any mental health diagnoses to corroborate that they had no mental health history.

Path analyses

Path analysis was performed for this study as a three-step process as described by O’Rourke and Hatcher [59]. A hypothesized model was first tested; nonsignificant paths were deleted, and statistically significant paths not initially hypothesized were added if supported by existing research or theory. With 7 independent variables this hypothesized model requires a minimum of 102 participants to identify large to medium effect sizes, where α = 0.05, β = 0.20 [59]. See Fig. 1.

Hypothesized path model of social media use types, social support, and psychological well-being

Path analysis is an extension of linear regression with three significant advantages. Path analysis allows us to simultaneously predict one or more dependent variables (touched by an arrowhead in path models). Prediction in this context refers to statistical prediction enabled by path analysis (multiple regression), not causal prediction. Arrows pointing from independent to dependent variables represent significant prediction, critical ratio (CR) values >|1.96|, p < .05. Path analysis is a multivariate statistical procedure, meaning that all significant paths emerge concurrently (i.e., over and above other statistically significant results). Path models allow us to identify both direct and indirect predictors of well-being [59]. Indirect prediction occurs via other variables (i.e., 2 + pathways between variables). In more complex models, variables can have direct and indirect effects on dependent variables, and indirect effects can be of equal or greater magnitude than direct effects (total effects = direct + indirect effects).

Path analysis was applied for this study to test the hypothesized model in which different aspects of social media use indirectly predicted life satisfaction and loneliness, through online social support and in-person social support. This path model was first tested with euthymic participants then replicated with participants with no mental health history but CES-D responses suggestive of clinical depression. Invariance analyses were then performed comparing path models following the procedures described by Byrne [60].

Depression as a moderating condition was tested using multi-group path and invariance analyses. That is, models were computed separately and independently to identify hypothesized differences between dysphoric and euthymic participants. Invariance or comparative analyses were next performed to identify differences in magnitude between shared paths between models.

In accord with convention, we reported three goodness-of-fit indices: the Comparative Fit Index (CFI; an incremental fit measure), the Standardized Root Mean Residual (SRMR; an absolute fit measure), and the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA; a parsimonious fit index). Ideal SRMR and RMSEA values are less than 0.055 [59] whereas CFI values greater than 0.95 indicate excellent fit [60]. Good model fit is required to interpret path models.

Bootstrapping was also performed as a simulation-based approach to estimate population parameters [61]. Bootstrapping treats the sample as a pseudo-population, drawing multiple random samples with replacement to generate new parameter estimates. The distribution of these estimates provides bias-corrected confidence intervals for path coefficients; intervals not crossing zero are statistically significant [61]. Bootstrapping was also used to estimate indirect effects and to compute the Bollen-Stine statistic as an additional model-fit index.

Ethical considerations

Ethics approval was obtained from the institutional review board at Ben-Gurion University of the Negev, Be’er Sheva, Israel (499-7; August 2023). Respondents provided informed consent to participate by clicking to proceed as stated on the study splash page. Participants were anonymous; they were not required to provide identifying information aside from an email address if they wished to receive a summary of the findings or take part in the participant lottery (US $500). All aspects of this study were performed in accord with the Declaration of Helsinki [62].

Univariate analyses were conducted to compare sociodemographic characteristics and primary study variables between participants scoring above and below the CES-D threshold. Groups significantly differed in age, income, loneliness, life satisfaction, depressive symptoms, and levels of in-person social support. The mean CES-D response among dysphoric participants was nearly five times higher and five standard deviations greater than euthymic participants (euthymic: M = 5.71, SD = 4.52; dysphoric: M = 27.58, SD = 10.30), indicating two distinct populations. Dysphoric participants reported higher loneliness and lower life satisfaction than euthymic participants.

Euthymic participants

The hypothesized path model was first tested among euthymic participants. Active social, active nonsocial, and passive SMU were each statistically correlated. Hypothesis 1 was partially supported among euthymic participants: active social SMU significantly predicted online social support; passive and active nonsocial SMU, however, were not significantly associated with online social support. These paths were removed from the model. No additional paths were added. Otherwise the hypothesized model was supported largely as predicted.

The resulting model demonstrated excellent goodness of fit, χ² (12) = 19.81, p = .07, CFI = 0.98, SRMR = 0.041, RMSEA = 0.054. This path model explained 33% variance in loneliness (R² = 0.33, p < .01) and 20% variance in life satisfaction, R² = 0.20, p < .01. See Fig. 2.

Consistent with Hypotheses 2 and 3, both online and in-person social support were associated with psychological well-being. Again, online social support did not directly predict life satisfaction; instead, the association with life satisfaction was indirect, operating through both loneliness and in-person social support. In-person social support significantly predicted both loneliness and life satisfaction, and loneliness emerged as a strong inverse predictor of life satisfaction. These findings suggest that life satisfaction is predicted directly via in-person social support and indirectly via online support.

Bootstrapping was also performed to estimate bias-corrected confidence intervals for parameter estimates, statistical significance for indirect effects, and the Bollen-Stine (BS) statistic. The BS chi-square value was estimated and averaged across 500 bootstrap samples (BS χ2 = 12.02, [SE=0.24]; p = .09). similar to the original sample, χ2 (df = 16) = 19.81, p = .07. Nonsignificant chi-square values suggest generalizability of findings to the population.

Dysphoric participants

A similar path model emerged with dysphoric participants. Again, the three forms of SMU were significantly correlated; yet with dysphoric participants, both active social SMU and active nonsocial SMU significantly predicted online social support, providing partial support for Hypothesis 1. With euthymic participants, nonsocial SMU did not predict online social support.

Model statistics indicated good to ideal fit to the data, χ² (11) = 9.23, p = .60. The Comparative Fit Index (CFI = 0.99) and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA = 0.001) are both in ideal parameters; the Standardized Root Mean Residual is in adequate parameters (SRMR = 0.057). The Bollen-Stine statistic (BS χ2 = 10.86, [SE=0.22]; p = .58) is similar to the original sample, again suggesting generalizability to the population, χ2 (11) = 9.23, p = .60.

As in the euthymic group, Hypothesis 2 was supported, as in-person social support directly predicted life satisfaction. Hypothesis 3 was also supported: online social support did not directly predict life satisfaction; instead, its effects on life satisfaction were indirect, operating through loneliness and in-person social support. These results suggest that the general structure linking social media use, social support, and well-being were broadly similar across groups (Table 3).

Between group comparisons

To test Hypothesis 4, invariance analyses were conducted to determine whether path coefficients significantly differed between groups. Results indicated that most structural relationships in the model were equivalent between euthymic and dysphoric participants. For instance, relative reliance on the three facets of SMU do not significantly differ. Neither active nor passive SMU differ between groups. Yet active nonsocial SMU predicts online social support among dysphoric participants but not the euthymic as earlier noted.

One other significant difference between models also emerged: The inverse association between online social support and loneliness was significantly stronger for dysphoric participants (β = −0.33, p < .01) compared to the euthymic group (β = −0.14, p < .01). This finding suggests that online social support has a stronger protective effect on loneliness for dysphoric adults, and supports our assertion that depression moderates the association between online social support and well-being.

For neither group does online social support directly predict life satisfaction. In contrast, the direct effects of in-person support and loneliness on life satisfaction are statistically indistinguishable between groups (Table 4).

Discussion

A central contribution of this study is the finding that depression moderated the relationship between online social support and loneliness. Although the overall structure of the model was similar across groups, the inverse association between online social support and loneliness was significantly stronger among dysphoric participants.

A central contribution of this study is the finding that depression moderated the relationship between online social support and loneliness. Although the overall structure of the model was similar across groups, the inverse association between online social support and loneliness was significantly stronger among dysphoric participants.

This finding suggests that online support may play a compensatory role for individuals with lower levels of in-person support, consistent with social compensation theory. For those experiencing depression, who commonly report reduced access to traditional social support, online environments may provide alternative avenues for connection and emotional support.

The difference in variance of life satisfaction explained between groups may be the strongest indicator of moderation. As noted above social media use, social support and loneliness explained 39% of variance among dysphoric participants compared to just 20% for euthymic participants. These independent variables, and associations among them, account for almost twice as much variance in life satisfaction. In other words, social media use and online support are demonstrably more central to the well-being of those with possible depression to a greater extent than euthymic adults.

The absence of a direct association between online social support and life satisfaction is theoretically informative. Online support may be particularly effective at redressing immediate social needs (e.g., feeling less alone) without building new support networks or changing broader evaluations of life circumstances. Life satisfaction is a global judgment that likely reflects multiple domains, including health, financial security, work, family relationships, and future expectations. In contrast, online support may operate more narrowly by increasing perceived connection, validation, or companionship. Thus, the present findings suggest that online support may contribute to well-being indirectly by reducing loneliness and strengthening perceived support, rather than building new support networks or directly altering overall life satisfaction.

This pattern also highlights an important limit of online support. For dysphoric participants, online support was more strongly associated with lower loneliness, suggesting that digital environments may provide meaningful social relief. However, this relief did not translate into a direct increase in life satisfaction. This distinction matters clinically and theoretically: feeling less lonely is not equivalent to evaluating one’s life more positively. Online support may help alleviate a specific social deficit, but broader life satisfaction may depend more heavily on stable offline relationships, material conditions, physical and mental health, and perceived agency. Thus, online support should not be framed as a substitute for comprehensive support or treatment, but as one pathway through which loneliness may be reduced.

The finding that active nonsocial use predicted online social support only for dysphoric participants may reflect differences in how online behavior is experienced and interpreted [16]. Active nonsocial use does not necessarily involve direct interpersonal exchange; however, it may still include behaviors such as posting, sharing, reacting, or contributing content in ways that create a sense of visibility or participation. For dysphoric participants, whose in-person support was lower and who may experience heightened sensitivity to social exclusion or rejection, even less explicitly interpersonal forms of activity may function as meaningful bids for connection. This interpretation is consistent with cognitive models of depression, which suggest that depression shapes the perception and meaning of social cues. Among euthymic participants, active nonsocial use may remain relatively instrumental or recreational; among dysphoric participants, the same behavior may be experienced as more socially salient.

These findings support previous research indicating that social media may play a compensatory role in social support and in well-being for those with lower levels of in-person support [25]. The dysphoric group reported significantly lower levels of in-person support and higher levels of depressive symptoms than the euthymic group.

Social media use explained a substantial proportion of variance in perceived online social support (30% among euthymic participants and 35% among dysphoric participants), supporting the construct validity of the BrOS-12 scale. In the dysphoric group, both active social and active nonsocial social media use predicted online social support, whereas among euthymic participants only active social use was associated with online support.

The magnitude of these effects should be interpreted cautiously. The standardized paths were generally modest, indicating that online support is not a dominant determinant of well-being. However, modest effects may still be meaningful in applied terms when they involve accessible, low-barrier forms of support that can reach individuals who are less connected offline. The practical implication here is not that online support replaces in-person support or clinical care; instead, it may serve as a modest but protective resource, particularly for lonely dysphoric adults.

This study was limited by several factors. First, the cross-sectional nature of data prevents causal inference. However, the unique nature of path modeling does provide some information suggestive of directionality, which creates a foundation for future longitudinal research.

The study sample was recruited online, and as such may not reflect the broader population of English-speaking adults with and without depression. All constructs were assessed through self-report in a single survey. This raises the possibility of common-method variance and response biases, including shared affective tone, recall bias, or impression management. These biases may be particularly relevant in studies of depression, social support, and loneliness, because participants’ current mood may influence how they evaluate both their online experiences and their well-being. Additionally, depression was estimated based on CES-D scores rather than clinical diagnosis. The CES-D is a widely used screening measure, but it is not a diagnostic tool. In addition, dichotomizing CES-D scores reduced depression to a dichotomous construct and may have obscured meaningful variation in depressive symptom severity. However, CES-D response levels between groups show a considerable difference between euthymic (M = 5.71, SD = 4.52) and dysphoric participants (M = 27.58, SD = 10.30). This difference between goups is not simply statistical; the CES-D mean for dysphyoric participants was almost five times higher and five standard deviations greater than euthymic participants. These dysphoric participants presented as a remarkably unhappy subpopulation; their CES-D responses were akin to psychiatric inpatients [63] (Fig. 3).

Additionally, the Passive and Active Facebook Use Measure was adapted by replacing “Facebook” with “social media” in order to capture use across platforms. This adaptation increases ecological relevance, but the modified wording was not independently re-validated.

Finally, online social support was measured using the BrOS-12, a new brief measure of online support. Although internal consistency was high in the present sample, and the measure was designed to capture multiple dimensions of online support, its validation paper remains under review. Therefore, interpretation of the online support construct should remain cautious until further validation studies are available.

Future directions for research may address the limitations of cross-sectional research design by utilizing ecological momentary assessment or similar longitudinal techniques to capture social media use patterns and aspects of well-being in or adjacent to the moment of experience [45]. Clinical diagnosis screening may provide additional information about the experience of those with clinical depression. Future research should also test depressive symptoms as a mediating variable and, where possible, include structured clinical interviews or clinical corroboration to determine if the protective effect of online social support on loneliness in dysphoric participants extends to those with a diagnosis of clinical depression, or whether the phenomenon is unique to those with undiagnosed depression, who may receive less social support than those with a clinical diagnosis.

As social media use is an evolving research domain, certain scales used in this study have been more widely than others. Further research is needed to validate our adaptation to the PAUM for social media use research, not Facebook only. And though the original Online Social Support scale has been used repeatedly across distinct samples, the brief version used in this study requires further validation. This research is underway.

Finally, future research may also extend this work using structural equation modeling with multiple indicators for each construct. Such models would allow psychological well-being to be tested as a latent outcome rather than represented by separate observed variables, and would permit more rigorous tests of measurement and structural invariance across depression-status groups. These findings contribute to the growing literature examining digital environments and mental health by suggesting that individual psychological characteristics may shape how online social interactions influence well-being. Rather than exerting uniform effects, social media environments may function differently depending on users’ psychological and social contexts (Fig. 3).
