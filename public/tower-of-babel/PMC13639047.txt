SORBS2 regulates diastolic function through cytoskeletal networks and calcium handling
Authors: Zexuan Wu, Camille Blandin, Jiehui Chen, Jiajin Li, Li He, Qing Ma, Michael M Peters, Anna Ponek, Dogacan Yucel, Kevin Kit Parker, Fujian Lu, William T Pu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiastolic dysfunction, defined by impaired relaxation and increased ventricular stiffness, is central to heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, yet cardiomyocyte-intrinsic mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Here, we show that SORBS2 is a vital component of murine cardiomyocyte adhesion complexes. Its genetic ablation in mice causes progressive diastolic dysfunction with preserved systolic function, accompanied by atrial enlargement and reduced survival. Postnatal cardiomyocyte-directed re-expression of SORBS2 restores diastolic indices and significantly improves longevity. Mechanistically, SORBS2 functions as an integrative scaffold linking adhesome integrity, cytoskeletal remodeling, and calcium homeostasis. SORBS2 deficiency increases microtubule detyrosination, reduces SERCA2 abundance, disrupts dyad-associated organization, which collectively impair active cardiomyocyte relaxation. Concurrently, this deficiency promotes extracellular matrix remodeling and myocardial fibrosis, driving passive ventricular stiffness. Pharmacological inhibition of microtubule detyrosination partially rescues relaxation defects. These findings establish SORBS2 as a key regulator of diastolic function and define a structural axis governing myocardial mechanics, offering potential therapeutic targets. Subject terms: Heart failure, Cardiomyopathies

Introduction

Diastolic dysfunction, characterized by impaired left ventricular relaxation and increased myocardial stiffness, is a fundamental physiological impairment that underlies diverse forms of heart failure, including heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF)1,2. Although HFpEF constitutes nearly half of all heart failure cases3, the cardiomyocyte-intrinsic mechanisms by which adhesion-associated cytoskeletal architecture governs diastolic function remain poorly defined.

The cytoskeletal adaptor protein SORBS2 (Sorbin and SH3 domain-containing protein 2, also known as Arg-binding protein 2 (ArgBP2)) plays a crucial role in cell adhesion, migration, and signaling as a component of the focal adhesion complex4–6. Within the heart, SORBS2 is predominantly expressed in cardiomyocytes7. By light microscopy, SORBS2 protein localization overlaps with the sarcomere Z-discs and the intercalated discs, and protein-protein interaction studies indicate that SORBS2 interacts with key components of focal adhesion complexes, including α-actinin, vinculin, and focal adhesion kinase4,7.

Previous studies of human heart tissue and experimental models have shown that SORBS2 is upregulated under pathological conditions such as dilated cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, pressure overload, and myocardial infarction7,8. At baseline, Sorbs2 knockout caused increased fibrosis, cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, and increased susceptibility to systolic dysfunction in the context of pressure overload7,8. Progressive systolic dysfunction was noted after 30 weeks of age, leading to these mice being described as having dilated cardiomyopathy8. Another group reported that SORBS2 localized to cardiomyocyte intercalated discs, and that Sorbs2 knockout disrupted intercalated disc structure and manifested features of arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy9.

In this study, we investigated the role of SORBS2 in cardiac homeostasis using a constitutive murine knockout model. We found that over the first 6 months of life, Sorbs2 inactivation caused premature death and severe diastolic dysfunction with preserved ejection function. Our mechanistic investigations of Sorbs2 suggest that its ablation causes dysregulation of cytoskeletal networks and cardiomyocyte Ca2+ handling, linking SORBS2 and the adhesome complex to maintenance of cardiac diastolic function.

Results

SORBS2 deficiency causes severe diastolic dysfunction with preserved ejection fraction

We interrogated public single-nucleus sequencing data of the human heart10 to determine the cell types that express SORBS2. SORBS2 was robustly and selectively expressed in ventricular and atrial cardiomyocytes (Supplementary Fig. 1A). SORBS2, RYR2, and JPH2 immunostaining revealed SORBS2 localized at intercalated discs, in a striated pattern consistent with cardiac dyads, which are immediately adjacent to sarcomere Z-discs, and at the cell periphery (Supplementary Fig. 1B, C and Supplementary Data 1). Re-analysis of published transcriptomic datasets showed that SORBS2 is consistently upregulated across multiple human and murine cardiac stress and remodeling models (Fig. 1A).

To investigate the functional role of SORBS2 in cardiac homeostasis, we studied mice globally lacking Sorbs2 exon 8 (Fig. 1B). These Sorbs2Δex8/Δex8 (KO) mice did not detectably express SORBS2 protein (Fig. 1C). KO mice survived normally to weaning but displayed poorer long-term survival compared with wild type (WT) and Sorbs2Δex8/+ (Het) mice (Fig. 1D). Premature death started at 3 months of age, and 50% died by 5.4 months. Male and female survival were comparably decreased in KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 1D).

At 6 months of age, KO mice had lower body weight compared to WT (Supplementary Fig. 1E), while their tibia lengths were comparable (Supplementary Fig. 1F). KO hearts had dramatically abnormal morphology, with an enormous left atrium (arrow, Fig. 1E) and a concave left ventricular silhouette (arrowhead, Fig. 1E). There was no increase in heart weight normalized to tibia length (Fig. 1F). The KO right atrium was not significantly enlarged, but the KO left atrium was 4.6-fold larger than WT as a percentage of heart weight (Fig. 1G, H). Thrombi were frequently observed in the distended left atria. The lung weight of KO mice was also significantly elevated (Fig. 1I), suggestive of pulmonary edema and decompensated left ventricular heart failure.

We assessed cardiac size and function by echocardiography in 6-month-old mice (Fig. 2A–F and Supplementary Table 1). KO mice had preserved systolic function, as assessed by the left ventricular ejection fraction (Fig. 2A, B). Their left atria were dilated, consistent with the post-mortem observations (Fig. 2C). The ratio of early to late diastolic mitral inflow velocity (E/A ratio) was significantly elevated in KO mice (Fig. 2D, E), consistent with diastolic dysfunction. Another measure of diastolic dysfunction, the ratio of the early mitral inflow velocity to the early diastolic mitral annular velocity (E/e’), was likewise significantly elevated (Fig. 2F).

While echocardiography allows non-invasive assessment of diastolic function, cardiac catheterization is the gold standard method. We obtained intracardiac pressure and volume recordings on 6-month-old KO and WT mice (Fig. 2G). Systolic function parameters (the maximum rate of change of pressure over time ([dP/dt]max), and the end-systolic pressure-volume relationship) did not differ between groups (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B). In contrast, the left ventricular end diastolic pressure, [dP/dt]min, and the left ventricular end diastolic pressure volume relationship, measures of diastolic function, all indicated impaired diastolic function in KO mice (Fig. 2H–J).

To assess the temporal development of these abnormalities, a cohort of mice was analyzed at 3 months of age. At this age, body weight, heart weight, and tibia length were comparable between genotypes (Supplementary Fig. 2C–E). The right atrium was enlarged (Supplementary Fig. 2F), and the left atrium tended to be enlarged, but this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.06; Supplementary Fig. 2G). Lung weight was comparable between groups (Supplementary Fig. 2H). By echocardiography, KO mice had normal systolic function (Supplementary Fig. 2I; Supplementary Table 2) and the left atrium was not yet enlarged (Supplementary Fig. 2J). The E/A ratio was not significantly different, but E/e’ was significantly elevated in KO (Supplementary Fig. 2K, L). These data suggest that at 3 months of age, KO mice have early diastolic dysfunction indicated by elevated E/e’, preserved E/A and LA size, and normal lung weight. Consistent with this sequence, gross morphology remained largely unremarkable at 6 weeks and 3 months, but atrial remodeling became prominent with age—manifesting as marked LA dilation by 6 months and progressing to biatrial enlargement with RV dilation and frequent LA thrombi by 9 months (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Together, these findings demonstrate that through 6 months of age, Sorbs2 inactivation causes diastolic dysfunction with preserved ejection fraction.

Cardiomyocyte SORBS2 is required to maintain diastolic function

Since Sorbs2Δex8 is a constitutive null allele, KO mice globally lack SORBS2, and SORBS2 deficiency extends from development into adulthood. To assess whether postnatal expression of SORBS2 in cardiomyocytes is sufficient to rescue cardiac function and survival, we treated neonatal KO mice with 5.5 × 1010 vg/g AAV9-Tnnt2-SORBS2, in which the cardiomyocyte-selective Tnnt2 promoter drives expression of Sorbs2 (Fig. 3A). We used a Sorbs2 isoform that includes the sorbin homology (SoHo) domain, a protein-protein interaction domain that binds to the lipid raft protein flotillin, and three SH3 protein-protein interaction domains, but lacks a neuron-specific exon (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table 3). AAV-mediated postnatal expression of Sorbs2 increased the survival of KO mice by over 10 months (Fig. 3C), indicating that the early death of Sorbs2 KO mice at 5-6 months was largely due to the absence of Sorbs2 in postnatal cardiomyocytes rather than a developmental disorder11 or the lack of Sorbs2 in other cell types.

Based on the dramatically enhanced survival of KO mice treated with AAV-Tnnt2-SORBS2, we developed a complementation assay to investigate the regions of SORBS2 that are required for its cardiac activity. We constructed AAV vectors that expressed full-length mouse Sorbs2 cDNA, or mutant Sorbs2 cDNA encoding SORBS2 proteins with deletions of the N-terminal region (ΔΝΤ) or the SoHo, SH3-1, SH3-2, or SH3-3 domains (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table 3). KO mice were treated with AAV at postnatal day 2 (P2). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that full-length SORBS2 and all truncation mutants exhibited a preserved striated distribution aligned with ACTN2-positive Z-discs (Supplementary Fig. 4A). These findings indicate that the differential rescue capacity of SORBS2 mutants is unlikely to result from its gross mislocalization. Western blotting (Supplementary Fig. 4B) showed that the AAV-expressed SORBS2 protein was more highly expressed than endogenous SORBS2. SORBS2-ΔNT, SORBS2-ΔSoHo, SORBS2-ΔSH3-2, and SORBS2-ΔSH3-3 were also expressed at greater levels than endogenous, whereas SORBS2-ΔSH3-1 was expressed at a lower level.

To assess the ability of SORBS2 or its deletion mutants to rescue KO heart function, we performed echocardiography and assessed gross morphology and tissue weights at 6 months of age (Fig. 3D–H, Supplementary Fig. 4C, and Supplementary Tables 4-5). Expression of SORBS2 or SORBS2 deletion mutants did not affect ventricular systolic function (Fig. 3E). KO mice treated with AAV-Tnnt2-SORBS2 had normal gross heart morphology (Fig. 3D) and normal lung weight to tibial length ratio (Fig. 3F), E/e’ ratio (Fig. 3G), and left atrial size (Fig. 3H, Supplementary Fig. 4C, and Supplementary Table 4-5). These results, combined with AAV-Tnnt2-SORBS2 enhancement of KO survival, indicate that postnatal cardiomyocyte expression of SORBS2 is sufficient to rescue the diastolic dysfunction of KO mice. Thus, diastolic dysfunction in KO mice is due to a lack of SORBS2 in postnatal cardiomyocytes.

In KO mice treated with AAV-SORBS2-ΔNT and AAV-SORBS2-ΔSoHo, lung weight normalized to tibia length (LW/TL) was comparable to WT (Fig. 3F), as with AAV-SORBS2. However, E/e’ and LA size were incompletely normalized, suggesting that these mutants have partial activity in maintaining heart diastolic function. Deletion of each SH3 domain did not alter these parameters compared to untreated KO mice (Fig. 3F–H and Supplementary Fig. 4C), suggesting that each SH3 domain is essential for SORBS2 function in cardiac relaxation.

Together, our data demonstrate that SORBS2 is required postnatally in cardiomyocytes to maintain heart diastolic function. The NT and SoHo regions may be partially dispensable for this function, whereas the SH3-1, SH3-2, and SH3-3 domains are essential.

SORBS2 interacts with focal adhesion complex proteins

SORBS2 contains multiple protein-protein interaction domains. To further delineate the SORBS2 interactome, we performed proximity proteomics by using AAV to express SORBS2 fused to BirA*, an engineered biotin ligase that releases biotin free radicals, resulting in biotinylation of proteins within ~10 nm12 (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. 5A). AAV expressing GFP was used as a negative control. Biotinylated proteins, purified by binding to immobilized streptavidin, were identified by mass spectrometry (Fig. 4B, C, Supplementary Table 6 and Supplementary Data 2). Peptides derived from SORBS2 were among the most enriched. Consistent with prior studies of the integrin adhesome4–6 and of proteins that co-immunoprecipitate with SORBS27, other highly enriched peptides were derived from components of focal adhesion complexes, which link the cardiomyocyte cytoskeleton to the extracellular matrix. Among the proteins enriched by BirA*-SORBS2 were talin (TLN1 and TLN2), vinculin (VCL), SORBS1, Plectin (PLEC), and CRK. TLN1 and TLN2 are concentrated at focal adhesions and link integrins to the actin cytoskeleton directly or indirectly by interacting with vinculin and α-actinin13. Vinculin is a focal adhesion protein that anchors the cytoskeleton to the plasma membrane by binding both talin and actin14. Like SORBS2, SORBS1 is an adaptor protein localized at focal adhesions that is involved in cell adhesion and cytoskeletal regulation15. Plectin, essential in cardiomyocytes for sarcomere and intercalated disc assembly, links the actin cytoskeleton to microtubules and intermediate filaments16. CRK is an oncogene that bridges tyrosine kinase signaling to downstream pathways to regulate cell adhesion and cytoskeletal organization17. We performed co-immunoprecipitation assays to confirm the interaction of SORBS2 with VCL and ACTN2. In cardiomyocytes transduced with AAV9-Tnnt2-HA-SORBS2, we detected VCL and ACTN2 in the HA-SORBS2 immunoprecipitate (Fig. 4D). Confocal imaging showed that VCL at focal adhesions at the cell periphery co-localized with SORBS2, while these proteins did not co-localize within the cell interior (Supplementary Fig. 5B).

We next investigated the effect of SORBS2 deficiency on the function of focal adhesions and cytoskeletal organization. We plated Sorbs2 KO and WT neonatal cardiomyocytes on laminin-coated glass coverslips and stained for filamentous actin (F-actin; Fig. 4E). KO neonatal cardiomyocytes were smaller than WT controls, consistent with reduced cell spreading and weakened cell adhesion (Fig. 4F). F-actin staining in KO cardiomyocytes was more intense than WT (Fig. 4G), suggesting actin aggregation at the cell periphery at sites of detachment. Additionally, F-actin filaments became shorter (Fig. 4H), and their organization was disrupted, as evidenced by an increased number of filament branches and junctions (Fig. 4I, J). These data indicate that SORBS2 is a component of cardiomyocyte focal adhesion complexes and is essential for their normal function to regulate cytoskeletal organization.

BirA*-SORBS2 biotinylated proteins also included dyadic proteins SPEG and RYR2. Proximity proteomics of the RYR2 complex18 and Cav1.219 reciprocally identified SORBS2 (and SPEG), suggesting that a subset of SORBS2 localizes to cardiac dyads and may influence Ca2+ handling. We used the proximity ligation assay (PLA), which yields signal when two antibodies bind to targets that are within ~50 nm, to validate that SORBS2 was located within the vicinity of dyadic proteins junctophilin (JPH2) and Cav1.2 (CACNA1C). We detected PLA signals with SORBS2 + JPH2 and SORBS2 + CACNA1C antibody pairs, but not SORBS2, JPH2, or CACNA1C antibody alone (Fig. 4K). Immunoblotting revealed a significant reduction in JPH2 protein abundance in Sorbs2 KO hearts (Supplementary Fig. 5C), accompanied by disrupted JPH2 spatial organization with loss of the normal striated dyadic pattern in heart sections and isolated cardiomyocytes (Supplementary Fig. 5D, E). Consistent with dyadic destabilization, in situ confocal imaging of T-tubules and RYR2-GFP20 demonstrated reduced regularity of both transverse and longitudinal T-tubules in KO hearts (Fig. 4L), indicating that SORBS2 is required to maintain dyad architecture. Together, these data indicate that cardiomyocyte SORBS2 is enriched at focal adhesions and within cardiac dyads, where it plays an essential role in organizing dyads.

Mechanisms of diastolic dysfunction in Sorbs2 KO hearts

Active and passive factors can impair myocardial relaxation. We assessed passive factors by measuring the storage modulus of KO and WT hearts using dynamic mechanical analysis. This assay revealed that KO hearts had a 5.5-fold higher storage modulus (P < 0.001; Fig. 5A), indicating greater total passive myocardial stiffness.

Extracellular and cellular sources contribute to myocardial stiffness. We assessed the KO myocardium for fibrotic changes in the extracellular matrix by performing picrosirius red-fast green staining of 6-month-old myocardial sections (Fig. 5B, C). KO myocardium exhibited greater fibrosis (red staining, Fig. 5C). Quantification of fibrotic area demonstrated that it was significantly higher in KO than WT (Fig. 5D). Cardiac fibrotic markers Col1a1 and Col3a1 were upregulated in KO compared to WT, as were cardiac stress markers Nppa and Nppb (Fig. 5E).

The splicing of the sarcomeric protein titin is an important determinant of the passive stiffness of sarcomeres21. RT-PCR showed that the ratio of compliant (N2BA) to stiff (N2B) titin isoforms did not significantly differ between KO and WT (Supplementary Fig. 6), suggesting that altered titin splicing is not a major contributor to diastolic dysfunction in Sorbs2-deficient hearts.

We also assessed cellular changes that could contribute to myocardial stiffness. In addition to the aforementioned abnormalities in focal adhesion function (Fig. 4E-J), we found that loss of SORBS2 altered the cardiomyocyte microtubule network, an important determinant of cardiomyocyte stiffness16. Immunoblotting demonstrated greater α-tubulin and detyrosinated α-tubulin in KO myocardium, with an elevated ratio of detyrosinated to total tubulin (Fig. 5F, G). Consistent with greater tubulin detyrosination, tubulin tyrosine ligase (TTL), which catalyzes the post-translational addition of tyrosine to the C-terminal end of α-tubulin, was downregulated in KO myocardium (Fig. 5F, G).

These changes in α-tubulin level and tyrosination would stabilize microtubules and increase myocardial stiffness. To further test this hypothesis, we isolated cardiomyocytes from 6-month-old WT and KO mice and stained for α-tubulin (Fig. 5H) or detyrosinated α-tubulin (Fig. 5I). In keeping with the immunoblot data, vehicle-treated KO cardiomyocytes had stronger α-tubulin and detyrosinated α-tubulin staining than WT (Fig. 5H, I). As a positive control for detyrosinated α-tubulin staining, we treated cells with taxol, which stabilizes microtubules and increases detyrosinated α-tubulin. Cardiomyocyte treatment for 2 h with parthenolide (PTL) or EpoY, inhibitors of the carboxypeptidase that detyrosinates α-tubulin, reduced KO α-tubulin staining to levels comparable to WT (Fig. 5H). PTL similarly reduced detyrosinated α-tubulin signal in KO cardiomyocytes to WT levels (Fig. 5I). Together, these data indicate that Sorbs2 ablation increases microtubules and microtubule detyrosination.

We investigated whether microtubule dysregulation contributes to diastolic dysfunction in KO cardiomyocytes by measuring sarcomere and cytosolic Ca2+ dynamics in dissociated cardiomyocytes treated with PTL, EpoY, or vehicle (DMSO). Vehicle-treated KO sarcomeres displayed impaired relaxation, reflected in reduced resting length (Fig. 6A), longer relaxation time, and slower relaxation velocity (Fig. 6B, C). In contrast, systolic parameters were largely similar between KO and WT, with sarcomere shortening, shortening time, and shortening velocity being comparable between genotypes (Fig. 6D–F). PTL and EpoY, inhibitors of α-tubulin detyrosination, did not significantly affect these parameters in WT cardiomyocytes (Fig. 6A–F). In contrast, PTL or EpoY enhanced the diastolic function of KO cardiomyocytes, as reflected by increased sarcomere length, sarcomere relaxation time, and sarcomere relaxation velocity (Fig. 6A–C). PTL and EpoY also increased systolic parameters of KO cardiomyocytes, enhancing sarcomere shortening, sarcomere shortening time, sarcomere contraction velocity, and Ca2+ release velocity (Fig. 6D–F). Together, these data support a mechanistic role for microtubule detyrosination in impaired cardiomyocyte relaxation.

Cardiomyocyte Ca2+ handling affects active cellular contributions to myocardial stiffness. To measure the effect of Sorbs2 ablation on cardiomyocyte Ca2+ handling, we dissociated cardiomyocytes from 6-month-old WT and KO mice, loaded them with Fura-2, a ratiometric Ca2+-sensitive dye, and recorded Ca2+ transients under field pacing at 0.5 Hz. Compared to WT, KO cardiomyocytes had impaired clearance of cytoplasmic Ca2+ during diastole, as shown by elevated diastolic Ca2+ concentration (Fig. 6G), increased Ca2+ transient time to 50% baseline, and decreased Ca2+ decay velocity (Supplementary Fig. 7A, B). PTL and EpoY improved diastolic Ca2+ clearance, reducing the Ca2+ transient time to 50% baseline and enhancing Ca2+ decay velocity (Supplementary Fig. 7A, B). However, these agents did not normalize diastolic Ca2+ concentration (Fig. 6G). In systole, KO cardiomyocytes had elevated peak Ca2+ (Fig. 6H) without significantly altered Ca2+ release kinetics. PTL and EpoY did not significantly normalize peak Ca2+ (Fig. 6H) or the time to 50% Ca2+ release (Supplementary Fig. 7C), but they significantly increased Ca2+ release velocity (Supplementary Fig. 7D).

Ca2+ is cleared from the cytosol into the sarcoplasmic reticulum by SERCA2, which is inhibited by phospholamban (PLN). PLN phosphorylation alleviates this SERCA2 inhibition. SERCA2 levels were significantly lower in KO (Fig. 6I–J), while total PLN and its phosphorylation at Ser16 were comparable between KO and WT (Supplementary Fig. 7E, F). The change in SERCA2 would reduce the cytosolic Ca2+ clearance rate, consistent with the Ca2+ transient data. Elevated cardiomyocyte diastolic Ca2+ and lower rate of Ca2+ clearance would increase sarcomere cross-bridge activity, thereby impairing cardiomyocyte relaxation.

Sorbs2 knockout has been reported to disrupt intercalated disk organization9, although this finding was not reproduced by McLendon et al.8. We examined intercalated disks in KO hearts and did not observe aberrant N-cadherin or Cx43 localization (Supplementary Fig. 7G, H), suggesting that altered organization of intercalated disks does not substantially contribute to the diastolic dysfunction of Sorbs2 KO mice.

Together, our data indicate that loss of SORBS2 causes diastolic dysfunction through a combination of increased fibrosis and passive myocardial stiffness, increased microtubule stability and detyrosination, and impaired diastolic Ca2+ clearance.

Altered gene expression downstream of SORBS2 deficiency

To gain additional insights into the mechanisms underlying altered cardiac function in Sorbs2 deficiency, we performed bulk RNA sequencing on 6-month-old ventricular myocardium from WT, KO, and KO mice treated with AAV9-Tnnt2-SORBS2 (KO + SORBS2). PCA analysis showed that independent biological replicates grouped together and were well-separated by between-group differences (Fig. 7A; Supplementary Data 3). A majority of differentially expressed genes (DEGs; FDR ≤ 0.05 and |fold-change | ≥ 2) between WT and KO were rescued by AAV-SORBS2 treatment (Fig. 7B, C).

We performed gene set enrichment analysis to identify pathways or cell components that were significantly impacted by SORBS2 deficiency. Pathways enriched in KO compared to WT included those related to collagen formation, extracellular matrix organization, cell motility, and integrin cell surface interactions, consistent with SORBS2’s function in the integrin-focal adhesion complex and our observation of increased fibrosis in KO hearts (Fig. 7D). Many of these differentially expressed genes were normalized by AAV-SORBS2 treatment (Fig. 7B and Supplementary Fig. 8A). Cell components enriched in KO included collagen fibrils, extracellular matrix, and microtubules (Fig. 7E and Supplementary Fig. 8B). Transcript levels of Tuba1a (encoding α-tubulin), Ttl, and Vash1 (encoding the primary tubulin detyrosinase) were not significantly altered in Sorbs2 KO, suggesting that the protein changes that we detected occurred post-transcriptionally.

Pathways enriched in WT (i.e., downregulated in KO) were predominantly related to the TCA cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, and mitochondria (Fig. 7F), and many of these DEGs were corrected by AAV-Tnnt2-SORBS2 (Supplementary Fig. 8C). Similarly, cell components significantly enriched in WT and restored by AAV-Tnnt2-SORBS2 were related to mitochondria and the respirasome (Fig. 7G and Supplementary Fig. 8D).

To identify gene expression changes that occur early in the disease process, we performed bulk RNA sequencing on 6-week-old ventricular WT and KO myocardium. PCA analysis confirmed high intergroup and low intragroup variation (Fig. 7H). We identified 28 and 53 genes more highly expressed in WT or KO, respectively (Padj< 0.05 and | Log2FC | ≥ 1; Fig. 7I; Supplementary Data 3). Canonical pathways enriched in KO continued to include collagen biosynthesis, extracellular matrix organization, and integrin cell surface interactions, and canonical pathways enriched in WT included mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation, respiratory electron transport, and oxidative phosphorylation.

These data identify integrin signaling, collagen formation, and extracellular matrix organization as processes enriched in KO, and mitochondria and respiratory electron transport as processes enriched in WT. Downregulation of gene programs related to mitochondria and oxidative phosphorylation by SORBS2 inactivation is consistent with prior studies linking focal adhesion kinase signaling to mitochondrial function22,23.

Discussion

Our study identifies SORBS2 as an essential regulator of diastolic function. Using a global Sorbs2 knockout mouse model, we demonstrate that, in the first 6 months of life, loss of SORBS2 leads to progressive diastolic dysfunction with preserved systolic performance, accompanied by atrial remodeling and reduced survival. Mechanistically, SORBS2 deficiency disrupts the integrin-adhesome complex, drives ECM remodeling, alters microtubule dynamics, and impairs calcium handling. Importantly, cardiomyocyte-directed re-expression of SORBS2 was sufficient to substantially rescue these phenotypes, establishing SORBS2 as a cardiomyocyte-intrinsic regulator of diastolic homeostasis.

In cardiomyocytes, SORBS2 is enriched at intercalated discs, cardiac dyads, and focal adhesions, functioning as a cytoskeletal adaptor that links mechanical cues to signaling pathways24. Furthermore, our study highlights the function of SORBS2 and the adhesome in regulating cardiac relaxation. Although several groups have studied Sorbs2 global or cardiac-specific knockout mice, and one previously noted diastolic dysfunction8, the finding that impaired relaxation with preserved systolic function is the predominant cardiac phenotype of these mice through 6 months of age was not previously emphasized. Rather, prior studies focused on impaired systolic function, which becomes severe by 1 year of age8. Although one group reported intercalated disc disorganization and severe downregulation of Cx439, this was not reproduced by another group8 or in this study.

ECM remodeling is a hallmark of diastolic dysfunction and contributes to increased myocardial stiffness and impaired relaxation25. In Sorbs2-deficient hearts, we observed substantial interstitial fibrosis, upregulation of ECM-associated genes, and activation of integrin signaling pathways, consistent with prior studies of SORBS2 deficient hearts7,9. Proximity proteomics, co-immunoprecipitation, and immunostaining further confirmed SORBS2’s enrichment at focal adhesion complexes, supporting its role in maintaining adhesome integrity.

Transcriptomic profiling of Sorbs2-ablated hearts revealed concerted upregulation of integrin and ECM-related transcripts, potentially to compensate for impaired signaling caused by loss of SORBS2. These data support a model in which cardiomyocyte-intrinsic loss of SORBS2 destabilizes focal adhesion signaling, leading to increased cytoskeletal stiffness and impaired mechanotransduction. These stress responses are well-established triggers of pro-fibrotic paracrine signaling from cardiomyocytes26, thereby activating cardiac fibroblasts and driving maladaptive ECM deposition. Thus, Sorbs2 ablation disrupts ECM–integrin signaling through focal adhesions and promotes secondary matrix remodeling, consistent with recent work identifying SORBS2 as a GATA4-regulated, stress-inducible adaptor that integrates integrin-mediated mechanotransduction with cytoskeletal and ECM homeostasis7.

Diastolic relaxation requires rapid cytosolic Ca2+ clearance, primarily via sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) reuptake by SERCA2. In Sorbs2 KO cardiomyocytes, we observed elevated diastolic Ca2+, reduced transient amplitude, and delayed decay kinetics—hallmarks of impaired SR calcium cycling. These changes were accompanied by reduced SERCA2 expression in the absence of alterations in total or Ser16-phosphorylated PLN, suggesting a PLN-independent mechanism. SERCA2 downregulation in Sorbs2 KO hearts is consistent with prior reports in a Sorbs2 cardiac-specific knockout model8, although our study reveals earlier onset and greater functional impairment.

Notably, SERCA2 mRNA levels were preserved, suggesting post-transcriptional mechanisms underlying reduced SERCA2 protein abundance. We observed increased detyrosinated α-tubulin, a modification that stiffens microtubules27,28 and may impairs SR vesicle trafficking29, suggesting a potential mechanism underlying reduced protein level. Increased detyrosinated α-tubulin and destabilization of dyadic architecture, including reduced JPH2 abundance and impaired T-tubule regularity, provide a mechanistic link between cytoskeletal remodeling and disrupted calcium microdomain organization. These findings support an indirect role for SORBS2 in diastolic calcium homeostasis via maintenance of cytoskeletal and dyadic integrity.

Cytoskeletal remodeling, particularly microtubule dynamics, has emerged as a key contributor to myocardial stiffness and impaired diastolic relaxation16,27,30. In Sorbs2 KO hearts, we observed upregulation of both α-tubulin and detyrosinated α-tubulin, coupled with downregulation of TTL, consistent with excessive microtubule stabilization.

Functionally, detyrosinated microtubules are more resistant to depolymerization and promote mechanical coupling between sarcomeres and the sarcolemma, increasing passive stiffness and delaying relaxation27,30. Pharmacological inhibition of tubulin detyrosination using PTL or EpoY restored sarcomere relaxation dynamics in Sorbs2 KO cardiomyocytes, directly linking microtubule dysregulation to diastolic dysfunction. Together, our results suggest that SORBS2 antagonizes microtubule detyrosination, likely indirectly through impaired mechanotransduction signaling at focal adhesions31,32.

ECM remodeling and destabilization of the adhesome have been implicated in mitochondrial dysfunction and diastolic impairment. Focal adhesion kinase (FAK), a core adhesome component, mediates integrin-dependent mitochondrial biogenesis and bioenergetics in cardiomyocytes22. Activation of integrin–FAK signaling enhances mitochondrial respiration via STAT3, increasing ATP production and reducing reactive oxygen species33. Conversely, ECM stiffening or altered integrin engagement disrupts microtubule-dependent mitochondrial positioning and dynamics, impairing ATP delivery and calcium responsiveness34. Furthermore, focal adhesions and associated protein complexes stabilize microtubules31,32, and stable, long-lived microtubules preferentially accumulate tubulin detyrosination28, thereby increasing cardiomyocyte stiffness27,30.

In Sorbs2 KO hearts, transcriptomic downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle genes was accompanied by excessive detyrosinated microtubules. This combination likely disrupts mitochondrial trafficking and calcium integration at dyadic–mitochondrial junctions, compromising energy supply and promoting diastolic dysfunction. Restoration of SORBS2 expression normalized both mitochondrial gene expression and diastolic performance, supporting a model wherein adhesome–cytoskeleton–mitochondria interplay is essential for efficient myocardial relaxation.

Collectively, our findings define a previously unrecognized role for SORBS2 as a cardiomyocyte-intrinsic determinant of myocardial diastolic function and reveal the adhesome as a critical regulator of cardiomyocyte relaxation. While prior studies have implicated SORBS2 in diverse aspects of cardiomyocyte structure and stress responses, our work provides integrative evidence that SORBS2 governs myocardial relaxation by coupling focal adhesion integrity to cytoskeletal dynamics and calcium microdomain organization through an adhesome–cytoskeleton–calcium signaling axis.

From a translational perspective, although SORBS2 itself is unlikely to represent a direct therapeutic target due to its foundational structural role, its loss exposes downstream, more selectively targetable mechanisms—particularly microtubule post-translational modifications and cytoskeletal–calcium coupling—that directly regulate myocardial stiffness and relaxation. Disease-associated upregulation of SORBS2 in human cardiomyopathy likely reflects a compensatory response to cytoskeletal and microdomain stress; defining when and why this adaptive response fails may reveal new strategies to preserve diastolic homeostasis across diverse cardiac diseases.

These findings should be interpreted in the context of several experimental considerations. This study employed a global Sorbs2 knockout model. Cardiomyocyte-directed AAV re-expression substantially rescued diastolic dysfunction and remodeling, supporting its requirement in postnatal cardiomyocytes to maintain cardiomyocyte relaxation, but more refined conclusions about SORBS2’s cell-autonomous function will benefit from conditional gene inactivation strategies. The late death of AAV-Tnnt2-SORBS2-treated KO mice might be due to insufficient AAV-directed SORBS2 expression, or to SORBS2 requirement in development or in other cell types. While ventricular diastolic dysfunction precedes atrial enlargement in the Sorbs2 KO model, suggesting that atrial remodeling is secondary to diastolic dysfunction, a direct atrial cardiomyocyte role of SORBS2 cannot be fully excluded. While our study clearly demonstrates the essential role of SORBS2 in maintaining cardiomyocyte diastolic function in mice and SORBS2 dysregulation in human heart disease, further studies are required to elucidate SORBS2’s contribution to the pathophysiology of human heart disease, such as HFpEF.

Methods

Animals

All animal experiments were performed under protocols approved by the Boston Children’s Hospital Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. Mice with a constitutive Sorbs2 knockout allele (Sorbs2e8; also known as C57BL/6N-Atm1Brd Sorbs2tm1.1(KOMP)Mbp/JMmucd) were obtained from the Mutant Mouse Resource & Research Center: UC DAVIS (stock number: 049805-UCD). Mice were maintained in a specific pathogen-free facility under a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle at 22 ± 2 °C and 45–65% humidity. Genotyping was performed using PCR primers listed in Supplementary Table 7. Mice were euthanized in an uncharged chamber by gradual-fill CO2 asphyxiation as specified by the American Veterinary Medical Association’s Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals.

Echocardiography

Mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane and lack of hind paw withdrawal response was confirmed. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed using a VisualSonics Vevo 3100 system with MX550D transducer (Visual Sonics Inc) under 1.0–1.5% isoflurane anesthesia, titrated to maintain heart rate at 400–450 bpm. Body temperature was controlled throughout.

Left ventricular (LV) systolic function was assessed by M-mode in the mid-ventricular short axis view at the level of the papillary muscles. Measurements included LV end-diastolic diameter (LVID, d), LV end-systolic diameter (LVID, s), LV fractional shortening (LVFS), LV ejection fraction (LVEF), end-diastolic interventricular septal wall thickness (IVS, d), and LV posterior wall thickness (LVPW, d). Diastolic function was evaluated in the apical 4-chamber view using pulsed-wave and tissue Doppler at the mitral valve. Parameters included peak early (E) and late (A) diastolic mitral inflow velocities, and early diastolic mitral annular velocity (E’). All measurements represent the average of ≥3 cardiac cycles. Mice recovered uneventfully from anesthesia.

Pressure-volume analysis

Pressure-volume loop analysis was performed as previously described35. Mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane, then maintained at 1.0–1.5% isoflurane. A 1.4-F catheter with pressure and conductance sensors (Transonic ADV500 Scisense Pressure-Volume Measurement System) was advanced into the LV via an apical approach. To obtain load-independent indices, the inferior vena cava was transiently occluded during a pause in mechanical ventilation. Data were analyzed using PV Lab Chart software (version 8).

AAV production and injection

The full-length Sorbs2 cDNA was previously described36. We excluded the neuron-specific exon (NSE), which is predominantly expressed in the nervous system. Truncated versions of the Sorbs2 cDNA were cloned into a plasmid with an N-terminal 3× haemagglutinin (HA) epitope tag. The complete sequence is provided in Supplementary Table 3.

For in vivo overexpression experiments, genes of interest were subcloned into an AAV9-cardiac troponin T promoter vector (Addgene, 69915)37. AAV was produced by co-transfecting HEK293 T cells with the AAV plasmids and helper plasmids AAV9-Rep/Cap (Addgene, 112865) and pAd-ΔF6 (Addgene, 112867) using polyethylenimine (Polysciences, 23966-2). Viruses were purified by iodixanol density gradient ultracentrifugation as described previously37. AAV was titered by qPCR and injected subcutaneously into postnatal day 2 (P2) pups at a dose of 5.5 × 1010 vg/g in a total volume of less than 30 μl.

RNA extraction and quantitation

Mouse tissues were homogenized by adding 5 mm Stainless Steel Beads (Qiagen, 69989) to tissues and processing in a TissueLyser (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, using 25 Hz for 2 min for complete tissue rupture. Total RNA was isolated using Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Plus kits (Zymo Research, R2071). DNase I-pretreated RNA (1 µg) was reverse transcribed using SuperScript III First-strand Synthesis SuperMix (Thermo Fisher, 18080400). Gene expression was analyzed by qPCR using Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, 4367659) and a Bio-Rad CFX96 or CFX384 touch thermocycler. qPCR primers are listed in Supplementary Table 7.

Titin isoform analysis

Titin isoforms were analyzed using a semi-quantitative PCR approach as previously described38. Briefly, PCR was performed using Taqman polymerase (Takara) with primer sequences provided in Supplementary Table 7. The resulting PCR products were separated and quantified using a Bioanalyzer 2100 Expert instrument (Agilent) with the DNA1000 kit.

RNA sequencing

For RNAseq, total RNA was isolated using Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Plus kits (Zymo Research, R2071). RNA integrity and concentration were assessed using an Agilent TapeStation 4200 (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA), with all samples having RIN values > 9. Poly(A) RNA purification, stranded RNA-seq library preparation (Illumina Stranded mRNA kit), and 150 nt paired-end sequencing were performed by MedGenome on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S4 sequencer. Raw reads were trimmed using Trim Galore (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore) and aligned to the mouse reference genome GRCm38/mm10 using STAR (version 2.7.10)39. A minimum of 40 million paired-end reads were obtained per sample, with at least 40 million mapped paired-end reads per sample. Gene expression values were calculated as fragments per kilobase million (FPKM) using Cufflinks (version 2.1.1)40. Principal component analysis was performed using the “prcomp” function in R. Differential expression analysis was performed using the nf-core/differential abundance pipeline (v1.5.0), which employs DESeq2 (Wald test, parametric fit)41. Normalization was carried out using the ratio-based size factor estimation, and log fold changes were shrinkage-adjusted. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Genes were considered significantly differentially expressed if they exhibited an absolute log2 fold change ≥1 (fold change ≥2) and an adjusted p value (FDR) ≤ 0.05. The RNA-seq results are summarized in Supplementary Data 3.

BioID and mass spectrometry

BioID proximity proteomics was performed as previously reported18. AAV-BirA*-Sorbs2 was constructed by fusing BirA* to the 5’ end of the Sorbs2 open reading frame. In total, 0.5 mg of protein extract was immunoprecipitated using streptavidin Dynabeads (Invitrogen, M280). Beads were washed five times with RIPA lysis buffer (Santa Cruz, #sc-24948) and stored in PBS for on-bead digestion. Streptavidin pull-downs were performed using hearts from four control mice injected with AAV-GFP and six mice injected with AAV-BirA*-SORBS2. Beads were subsequently washed at least five times with 100 µL of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate. Digestion was initiated by adding 5 µL of modified sequencing-grade trypsin (200 ng/µL; Promega, Madison, WI), followed by overnight incubation at 37 °C. Beads were then removed magnetically, and the supernatant was dried using a speed-vac. Samples were resuspended in 50 µL of HPLC solvent A (2.5% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) and desalted using STAGE tips. On the day of analysis, samples were reconstituted in 10 µL of solvent A.

Nano-scale reverse-phase HPLC columns were prepared by packing 2.6 µm C18 silica beads into a fused silica capillary (100 µm inner diameter × ~30 cm length) with a flame-drawn tip. After equilibration, samples were loaded using a Famos autosampler (LC Packings, San Francisco, CA), and peptides were eluted via a gradient of increasing concentrations of solvent B (97.5% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid). Eluted peptides underwent electrospray ionization and were analyzed using an LTQ Orbitrap Velos Elite ion-trap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Peptides were detected, isolated, and fragmented to generate tandem mass spectra, which were matched to protein databases using Sequest (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Databases included reversed sequences for false discovery rate (FDR) estimation, and results were filtered to achieve a peptide FDR of 1–2%.

Western blotting

Protein samples (50 µg) were separated by 4–12% SDS–PAGE (Invitrogen, NW04120BOX) and transferred to Immobilon-P PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore, IPVH00010). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk, incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 8) overnight at 4 °C, and then with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using a Fujifilm LAS-3000 imager and quantified with Fiji software. Uncropped gel images are shown in Supplementary Data 4.

Capillary-based immunoassay was performed using the Wes-Simple Western method with the anti-rabbit detection module (ProteinSimple). Protein expression was measured by chemiluminescence and quantified as area under the curve using the Compass for Simple Western program (ProteinSimple).

Co-immunoprecipitation assays

For each co-immunoprecipitation, 50 µL protein G beads (Invitrogen, 10003D) and 5 µL primary antibody (1 mg/mL) or IgG control were incubated with 500 µg tissue lysate overnight at 4 °C. Beads were washed ten times with immunoprecipitation buffer (Thermo Fisher, 87787) containing protease inhibitor (Thermo Fisher, A32965). Bound proteins were eluted by boiling beads at 100 °C for 10 min.

Histology

To measure fibrosis, mouse hearts were excised and fixed in 4% PFA overnight, dehydrated through an ethanol gradient, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 7 µm. Sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin, or Fast green/ Picrosirius red. Sections were imaged by a widefield microscope (Keyence) at ×10 and ×20 magnification.

Immunofluorescence assays

Mouse hearts were fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, and embedded in tissue freezing medium. Cryosections (10 µm) were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min and blocked with 10% normal goat serum for 1 h. Isolated cells were fixed in 4% PFA for 15 min, washed with PBS, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 1% BSA for 1 h.

Sections or cells were incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 8) overnight at 4°C, followed by Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, 1:200) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (1 µg/mL) for 10 min. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope with the following excitation/emission settings: DAPI (405/420-470 nm), Alexa Fluor 488 (488/490-520 nm), Alexa Fluor 555 (543/560-620 nm), and Alexa Fluor 647 (647/ > 650 nm).

Adult cardiomyocyte isolation and treatment

Adult mouse ventricular myocytes (AMVMs) were isolated enzymatically from mouse hearts37. Briefly, hearts were excised from isoflurane-anesthetized mice, rinsed in cold perfusion buffer (in mM: 137 NaCl, 15 glucose, 20 HEPES, 4.9 KCl, 1.2 MgCl2, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 5 taurine, and 10 BDM; pH 7.4), and mounted on a Langendorff perfusion system. Hearts were perfused at 37 °C with oxygenated Ca2+-free perfusion buffer until blood was cleared, followed by digestion with collagenase type II (0.7 mg/mL; Worthington, LS004176), protease type XIV (0.1 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, P5147), BSA (1 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, A3912), and 50 µM Ca2+. Softened ventricles were minced, agitated, and filtered through a 100 µm cell strainer. Cells were centrifuged at 20 g for 1 min and subjected to a Ca2+ gradient recovery (0.25, 0.35, 0.52, and 1 mM). Viable myocytes were enriched by gravity sedimentation for 5–7 min, and the supernatant containing dead cells was aspirated. Cells were resuspended in normal Tyrode’s (NT) solution containing 1 mM Ca²⁺ and maintained at 37 °C in NT solution for up to 6 h.

For pharmacological manipulation, AMVMs were incubated in NT solution with 10 μM Taxol (Cytoskeleton, TXD01), 10 μM parthenolide (Sigma-Aldrich, P0667), or 20 μM epothilone-Y (EpoY; Sigma-Aldrich, SML2301), each dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), at 37 °C for 2 h27,42. DMSO alone served as the vehicle control. WT and KO myocytes from each condition were subsequently used for functional assays.

Sarcomere shortening and relaxation were measured in freshly isolated AMVMs using the IonOptix Calcium and Contractility System. Briefly, AMVMs were placed in a C-Stim CMC microscope chamber. The cells were electrically stimulated at 0.5 Hz using a field stimulator, and changes in sarcomeric length were recorded. Basal and peak sarcomere length, maximum departure/return velocities, time to peak 50%, and time to baseline 50% were measured. All measurements were performed at room temperature. All parameters were quantified using IonWizard software.

For calcium transient analysis, AMVMs were incubated with 1 µM Fura-2 AM (ThermoFisher, F14185) at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 20 min, followed by washout and stimulation at 0.5 Hz. Intracellular calcium dynamics were recorded using ratiometric photometry (340/380 nm excitation) via a photomultiplier tube, and parameters including diastolic Ca²⁺, peak Ca2+, transient amplitude, and decay kinetics were analyzed.

A subset of AMVMs was fixed for immunofluorescence analysis. AMVMs were plated on laminin-coated 96-well plates (5 µg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich, L2020) for 30 min and cultured in MEM (Sigma-Aldrich, M2279) supplemented with 10 mM BDM. Cells were then fixed in pre-chilled 100% methanol at –20 °C for 8 min. Following fixation, wells were rinsed with PBS and blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were incubated at 4 °C for 48 h with primary antibodies: anti-α-tubulin (1:100), anti-detyrosinated α-tubulin (1:100) and anti-desmin (1:200). After PBS washes, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500) for 1 h at room temperature, counterstained with DAPI. Imaging was performed using Olympus confocal microscope under identical acquisition settings for all groups.

Neonatal cardiomyocytes isolation and treatment

Neonatal mouse ventricular myocytes (NMVMs) were isolated from 1-to 2-day-old Sorbs2 WT or KO pups using Neonatal Cardiomyocyte Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-100-825) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell preparations typically contained >95% cardiomyocytes. F-actin was stained using phalloidin staining. Images were acquired using the 60×objective on the Olympus microscope. All cells were measured as a unitary unit. Cell area and F-actin intensity were measured using ImageJ software. F-actin average length (in µm), the branches number and junctions were quantified using ImageJ Skeleton plugin.

Myocardial mechanical analysis

To evaluate the comparative stiffness of WT and KO hearts, the tissues underwent dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA). The testing setup measures the tissues’ viscoelastic response in a dynamic environment, allowing for the differentiation between the elastic behavior (storage modulus) and the viscous behavior (loss modulus). When stressed, soft biological tissues both store elastic energy and dissipate energy43. This creates a time dependent stress response as a function of testing frequency. Because of this, dynamic testing can elucidate more consistent assessment of mechanical properties than traditional static tests44. Typical heart rates of mice are on the scale of ~600 beats/min (10 Hz)45, making the frequency dependent responses an important aspect when evaluating relative cardiac tissue stiffnesses under relevant mechanical stimuli.

The testing setup was designed and executed in accordance with ASTM D502446. Samples were biopsied (4 mm diameter) from the left ventricular wall. Tissue thickness was measured with digital calipers (Mitutoyo, Kawasaki, Japan) prior to testing. Each tissue specimen was rehydrated with 37 °C phosphate buffered saline (PBS) prior to loading into the DMA (Mettler Toledo DMA 1, Columbus, OH, USA). The samples were compressed between two flat parallel plates (5-10 µm displacement) across a logarithmic frequency sweep covering 1–300 Hz. Storage moduli (E’) was recorded for each specimen across the full sweep. In all specimens, E’ plateaued in frequency domains below ~50 Hz. For each sample, an E’ value was established as the average across ~1–10 Hz in order to encapsulate elastic performance across low to physiological tissue properties. The plots provided indicate the average values with the error bars representing standard error of the mean.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis was performed using Prism 9 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed data, group comparisons were conducted using an unpaired Student’s t test for two groups or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s or Sidak’s post hoc tests for multiple groups. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed with the Mann–Whitney U test for two-group comparisons or the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test for multiple groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates. Experimental animals were randomly assigned to each experimental/control group. Investigators were blinded to the genotypes of the individual animals during the experiments and outcome assessments.
