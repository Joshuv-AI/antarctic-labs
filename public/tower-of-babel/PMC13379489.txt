Real-World Systemic Treatment Patterns and Outcomes of Children With Severe Alopecia Areata: A French Nationwide Study (ESTAPED Cohort)
Authors: Ninon Le Goaziou, Christine Bodemer, Christine Labrèze, Emmanuel Mahé, Annabel Maruani, Helene Aubert, Sébastien Barbarot, Claire Abasq, Nina Sigg, Audrey Lasek, Stéphanie Mallet, Christine Chiaverini, Juliette Miquel, Eve Puzenat, Florence Poizeau, Jasmine Flament, Bertille Bonniaud, Justine Pasteur, Catherine Droitcourt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/ObjectiveSevere forms of alopecia areata (AA) often require off-label systemic treatments (ST) for which data on effectiveness and safety are limited. This study aimed to provide a comprehensive real-world overview of ST patterns in children with severe AA.MethodWe conducted a retrospective, longitudinal, multicenter study of children with AA (aged <18 years) receiving at least one ST (2010–2023), identified using a keyword search in Ouest Data Hub Warehouse (a hospital data network in France with a critical mass of 5.1 million patients) or by members of the Société Française de Dermatologie Pédiatrique. The primary outcome was drug survival (DS), defined as time on ST, and the secondary outcomes included reasons for treatment discontinuation and long-term observations.ResultsAmong the 262 included children (median age at AA onset: 9 years; 58.4% girls, atopic dermatitis in 32.4%), the first-line ST most frequently initiated (median age: 12 years, alopecia totalis in 28.6%) was intravenous corticosteroids (35.9%, median duration: 3 months), followed by methotrexate + intravenous corticosteroids (24.0%, 12.5 months), oral corticosteroids (17.9%, 1 month), methotrexate (10.7%, 9.5 months), methotrexate + oral corticosteroids (8.8%, 9 months), and lastly baricitinib (2.7%, 2.5 months). Specific maintenance treatments were not significantly associated with DS. Psychological impact, atopic dermatitis, later childhood onset and female sex were associated with longer first-line systemic treatment drug survival. Treatment failure was the main reason for discontinuation (51.9%) and no serious adverse events were reported. Five years after starting the first ST (n = 62), 14.5% of patients had achieved remission.ConclusionsIn this national, retrospective real-world study, drug survival analyses highlighted two treatment profiles: pulse therapy (corticosteroids) for flare-ups and maintenance treatments (methotrexate, baricitinib) for long-term management. Overall, the effectiveness of therapies appears limited with frequent relapse and a likely need for prolonged treatment. However, data on long-term treatment outcomes of JAKi in children are lacking. This study provides real-life data which could help improve management strategies in children with severe AA.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40272-026-00757-2.

Introduction

Alopecia areata (AA) is a disorder causing non-scarring hair loss, with a prevalence of 1.92% among children and adolescents [1]. It is one of the most common reasons for consultations in pediatric dermatology. It presents in various forms, from limited, patchy hair loss to extensive or total loss affecting the entire scalp (alopecia totalis) or the whole body (alopecia universalis). The severity of AA has multiple determinants, including the extent of scalp involvement (assessed using the Severity of Alopecia Tool [SALT]), disease duration and visibility, relapses, quality of life and psychosocial burden [2], and as such no standardized definition of severe AA can be given.

While the diagnosis of AA is generally straightforward, its management remains challenging and frustrating for both dermatologists and patients, especially in severe and refractory cases. Children and adolescents with AA may experience stigma, impaired quality of life, and psychiatric disorders [3], leading to high therapeutic demand. Topical corticosteroids (TCS) and topical immunotherapy often constitute first-line therapies. However, severe and extensive forms frequently require off-label systemic treatments (ST), for which effectiveness and safety data are limited. The Janus kinase inhibitor (JAKi) ritlecitinib was approved in 2023 by both the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) for use in children with AA aged 12 years and above [4].

Various off-label ST are used, including methotrexate (MTX) [5–8], oral and intravenous (IV) corticosteroids (CS) [9–12], dupilumab [13–16] and more recently, JAKi [17–20]. Data on the patterns of ST use in pediatric AA are lacking. The objective of this study was to provide a comprehensive overview of real-world prescribed ST patterns in children and adolescents with severe AA.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

We conducted a retrospective, longitudinal national cohort study in France, including children and adolescents with AA who received at least one ST between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2023, across 16 pediatric dermatology centers.

Data Sources and Participants

The study included patients aged 0–17 years, diagnosed with AA by a dermatologist and treated with at least one ST during the study period. Exclusion criteria were missing treatment dates, initiation of first-line ST after 18 years of age or treatment for another medical condition. Severe AA was in this study defined as cases in which ST was administered for the condition.

Data were obtained from two sources. The first was the French ‘Ouest Data Hub Warehouse [21]’ database, including the de-identified data from over five million individuals treated in university hospitals within four French cities (Brest, Nantes, Rennes and Tours). Data encompass results from additional investigations, medical reports from outpatient consultations and hospital stays. A comprehensive keyword search was applied, and documents were reviewed based on inclusion criteria (see electronic supplementary material [ESM], Text 1). A single investigator then reviewed and extracted data from medical reports using a standardized data collection form. The second source was the network of pediatric dermatology centers affiliated with the Société Française de Dermatologie Pédiatrique working group. All members were invited to contribute data from eligible patients. Together, the two sources provided a nationwide data collection.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the duration a patient remained on a first-line ST (i.e. drug survival [DS]) used as a proxy for treatment effectiveness, safety and both patient and physician preferences [22].

Secondary outcomes were the reasons for ST discontinuation and the long-term effectiveness and safety assessed 5 years after starting first-line ST.

Variables

Data were retrospectively extracted from medical records using a standardized questionnaire. Collected variables included age at AA onset and at first-line ST initiation; sex; AA characteristics (subtype, nail and ophthalmological involvement [23]); comorbidities (atopic dermatitis [AD], vitiligo, psoriasis, thyroid dysfunction, inflammatory rheumatism, Down syndrome); first-degree family history of AA or other autoimmune disease; psychological triggers of AA and reported psychological impact; use of topical anti-inflammatory treatments; disease surface area severity (SALT score); ST characteristics (drug name and maximum dose, start and end date of each ST line) and reasons for treatment discontinuation (effectiveness, failure, adverse events or other reasons).

A treatment line was defined as a continuous period on one specific drug. A new line started if treatment was stopped for ≥3 months.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive Analysis

A descriptive analysis of patient characteristics and ST lines was performed. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages, and quantitative data using means, medians, quartiles and ranges. The Chi-squared test was used to compare reasons for first-line treatment discontinuation.

Drug Survival Analysis

First-line systemic treatments were categorized as acute therapy for flare-ups (oral or IV CS alone) and maintenance therapy for long-term control of the chronic disease (MTX, dupilumab and JAKi) [24, 25]. All children were included in the DS analysis.

The event was defined as ST discontinuation. Data were censored for patients lost to follow-up or still on treatment at study end (January 1, 2024). Drug survival was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves to estimate the probability of continuing first-line ST. Two curves were drawn, one for all STs and another for maintenance therapies. Differences in DS were assessed using log-rank tests.

As baseline characteristics at treatment initiation were unbalanced across groups, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using overlap weighting based on propensity score to create balanced pseudo-populations. This method aims to mimic randomization by balancing baseline characteristics between treatment groups at the analysis stage. By weighting patients according to their propensity score—which represents the probability of receiving a given treatment based on the set of baseline characteristics—the method creates a pseudo-population in which the distribution of observed baseline covariates is similar between treatment groups. This weighted pseudo-population allows for a less biased comparison of treatment effects by reducing confounding due to measured covariates. This analysis was not performed for pulse therapies or treatment with baricitinib due to the limited number of patients (n = 7). Kaplan Meier curves and weighted log-rank tests were performed in the pseudo-populations. Given the limited number of observations and multiple STs, pairwise propensity score matching was performed using MTX as the reference.

Analyses were performed using the survival package in R software (version 3.5-5).

Cox Regression Analysis

A Cox regression model was used to calculate hazard ratios to identify predictive factors of DS for first-line maintenance treatments. The potential determinants of DS among those studied included age at treatment initiation, sex, age at AA onset, AA subtype, presence of AD, and type of systemic drug. Results were considered statistically significant if the 95% confidence interval did not cross 1.0. Variables with a p-value ≤0.2 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model.

Authorizations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Rennes University Hospital (IRB approval n°24.16).

Results

A total of 262 children (58.4% girls) with a median age at AA onset of 9 years (Q1–Q3: 5–12) received at least one ST. Across this population, 414 treatment lines were identified.

At first-line ST initiation, the median age was 12 years (Q1–Q3: 9–14). AA was patchy in 31.3% of patients, totalis in 28.6%, universalis in 26% and ophiasis in 12.2%. SALT scores were available for 98 patients, with a median score of 70 (IQR: 50–98.75).

Nail and ophthalmologic involvement were observed in 46.3% (n = 94/203) and 25.6% (n = 22/86), respectively. Atopic dermatitis was present in 32.4% (n = 79/244) and autoimmune thyroiditis in 7.9% (n = 20/253). Eighteen percent had a first-degree familial medical history of AA (n = 44/245). A psychiatric trigger was reported in 31.5% (n = 73/232) and psychological impact in 63.1% (n = 147/233). The most frequent first-line ST was pulse IV CS (n = 94, 35.9%), followed by MTX combined with IV CS (n = 63, 24.0%), oral CS (n = 47, 17.9%), MTX alone (n = 28, 10.7%), MTX combined with oral CS (n = 23, 8.8%), and baricitinib (n = 7, 2.7%). The dosages for the first-line treatments are provided in the ESM, Text 2. Before first-line ST, most children had received topical treatments (n = 223/262, 85.1%), including TCS (n = 153/223), a combination of TCS and minoxidil (n = 48/ 223) or minoxidil alone (n = 10/223). Twelve patients had received topical immunomodulators (tacrolimus or topical JAKi).

While 185 (71%) of the 262 children initiated on first-line STs experienced no adverse events, 73 (28%) reported at least one (data unavailable for 4 patients). The highest adverse event rates were observed with IV CS combined with MTX (36%), followed by IV CS alone (33%), then MTX alone (19%), oral CS combined with MTX (6.8%), and finally baricitinib (2.7%) or oral CS alone (2.7%). The reported first-line adverse events are detailed in the ESM, Text 3.

Of the 104 children receiving second-line ST, 24 % (n = 25) received MTX combined with IV CS, 20.2% (n = 21) MTX alone, 17.3% (n = 18) IV CS, 14.4% (n = 15) oral CS, 9.6% (n = 10) baricitinib, 7.6% (n = 8) MTX combined with oral CS, 4.8% (n = 5) upadacitinib and 1.9% (n = 2) dupilumab.

Among the children who received oral CS as first-line ST, 55.3% (26/47) then received second-line oral CS (45.8%, n = 11), IV CS (12.5%, n = 3), MTX combined with IV or oral CS (29.2%, n = 7 and 4.2%, n = 1, respectively), MTX alone (4.2%, n = 1), or upadacitinib (4.2%, n = 1). Similarly, 43.6% (41/94) of the children who received pulse IV CS as first-line ST then received second-line ST MTX alone (31.7%, n = 13), MTX combined with IV or oral CS (29.3%, n = 12 and 2.4%, n = 1, respectively), second-line IV CS (19.5%, n = 8), baricitinib (9.8%, n = 4) or oral CS (7.3%, n = 3). The main reasons for discontinuing second-line treatments are detailed in the ESM, Text 4.

Long-term outcomes data at 5 years after starting first-line ST were available for 62 patients. Among them, 53.2% demonstrated disease stability, maintaining their condition, 22.6% showed improvement and 14.5% had achieved complete remission. However, 9.7% experienced disease progression despite ST.

Discussion

This French, national, retrospective, longitudinal study of children and adolescents with severe AA treated with first-line ST highlighted two treatment profiles: use of systemic CS for acute therapy, and MTX alone or combined with CS for maintenance treatment or long-term control. Maintenance duration was longer in girls, and/or in cases of concomitant AD, psychological impact, or late AA onset. While boys tend to present with more severe AA [26–28], the female predominance and longer therapeutic persistence observed in our study are consistent with literature, suggesting increased feelings of stigmatization, anxiety and depression in this population [3, 29]. These findings may reflect greater treatment adherence among girls, potentially due to a higher psychological burden. Similarly, late-onset AA was associated with a longer treatment duration, possibly due to the higher frequency of bullying among adolescents and the vulnerable period of puberty [30]. The psychological impact of AA can be significant, leading to impaired quality of life and a higher prevalence of psychiatric disorders [31, 32], which may encourage patients, families and practitioners to pursue treatment. Additionally, longer treatment duration observed in patients with concomitant AD may be explained by the dual benefit of ST on AA and eczema [33], two conditions sharing common inflammatory pathways, including Th2 activation [34] and JAK-STAT signaling [35]. Lack of effectiveness was the main reason for discontinuation. It is important to note that only a few patients discontinued due to adverse events, none of which were severe.

No pediatrics studies have directly compared STs in AA and only two randomized controlled trials have assessed their effectiveness and safety in this population (ritlecitinib [JAKi] versus placebo [4, 20] and low-dose mini-pulse oral dexamethasone versus diphenylcyclopropenone [36]). Ongoing clinical trials (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05866562) aim to assess the effectiveness and safety of dupilumab and JAKi in pediatric AA. Several retrospective case studies in children taking ST have reported real-world clinical experience with CS, MTX [5–8], dupilumab [13, 15, 16] and JAKi [17–20], with varying outcomes. For MTX, reported failure rates ranged from 58.3 [7] to 61.5%, with up to 60% of initial responders experiencing relapse [6]. In our cohort, the failure rate was 54%. For MTX + CS, a poor response was observed in 57.1% of patients [40], comparable with the failure rates in our study combining MTX with IV CS (54%) or oral CS (67%). JAK inhibitors showed lower failure rates: 20.6% for baricitinib [18] (vs 25% in our cohort) and 18% for tofacitinib [17]. IV CS are the most studied treatment in the literature and also the most frequently used first-line therapy in our cohort (35.9%) [37–39]. Regarding IV CS, a 33% complete failure rate was reported with high-dose pulse CS, and 29% of patients showed a partial response; among those with an initial response, 81% experienced relapse [12]. In our study, the overall failure rate was 48%. For oral CS, failure rates of 40% [41] and 32.3% [11] have been reported in separate studies, with 16.9% of patients relapsing during treatment [11] (vs a rate of 51% observed in our study). These data should be interpreted with caution, as definitions of treatment failure vary across studies.

Interestingly, recent guidelines recommended oral CS use in pediatric cases of acute AA [24]. This is consistent with our findings showing the lowest frequency of adverse events with this treatment. In our study, nearly half of the treatments were discontinued due to their inefficacy, in line with previous findings—except for JAKi. Results for JAK inhibitors (e.g., baricitinib) should be interpreted with caution due to the limited sample size.

Strengths of our study include its homogeneity and multicentric design, including a large cohort of 262 children with AA treated with at least one ST. The characteristics of our population are consistent with the literature [1, 28, 42–46] and the cohort was homogeneous in terms of disease severity. Secondly, comprehensive data were collected from the Ouest Data Hub database, which encompasses 5 million inhabitants in western France, by a single investigator, thus helping to reduce selection bias. Finally, the study focused on first-line and second-line treatments, using pseudo-populations to minimize confounding factors.

Certain limitations should be discussed. Firstly, this was a retrospective study, limited by missing data and a potential classification bias regarding the reasons for treatment discontinuation. However, a single investigator reviewed all medical records using a standardized questionnaire, to ensure uniform data collection and help minimize these limitations. Data were derived from two distinct sources, which may have introduced variability. Furthermore, the limited availability of severity scores (SALT), which in clinical practice, unlike clinical trials, are not systematically used to guide treatment decisions, prevented a quantitative definition of severity. A multidimensional approach, combining clinical criteria and quality of life impact, is recommended for a comprehensive and personalized evaluation of patients [47]. Secondly, the number of patients treated with JAKi was small, as ritlecitinib was only recently and partially reimbursed in France for adolescents aged over 12 years with severe AA. Baricitinib is currently under investigation in a phase III clinical trial (BRAVE-AA-PEDS) in children aged 6–18 years with severe AA but has not yet received approval. To date, no specific safety signals have been identified in pediatric patients or in adults with AA [48]. Reported risks, including infectious, oncologic, and cardiovascular events, have mainly been described in adults over 65 years of age. However, long-term safety data in children remain limited, and potential long-term toxicities require further investigation. Lipid abnormalities have been reported with potential cardiovascular implications [49], highlighting the need for cautious use while awaiting long-term data.

‘Drug survival’ is a useful measure in clinical research, reflecting effectiveness, safety and factors related to patients, families and physicians influencing treatment choices. It is particularly appropriate for retrospective data on chronic conditions (initially developed for rheumatology diseases, psoriasis or AD). It helps identify predictive factors for long-term treatments [22]. The optimal duration of maintenance therapy for AA remains undefined. Studies like BRAVE on JAKi (baricitinib) showed promising short-term effectiveness but highlighted the risk of relapse after treatment discontinuation [50]. Similarly, a long-term follow-up study on MTX reported frequent relapses, with successful retreatment [8]. This is consistent with our findings, which show limited long-term effectiveness 5 years after stopping first-line ST, with only 14.5% achieving complete hair regrowth. This highlights the importance of therapeutic persistence in AA, a relapsing condition for which long-term maintenance therapy appears necessary [51]. Furthermore, recent guidelines help physicians decide upon the appropriate initiation of an immunosuppressive treatment, with systemic CS for active AA (flare-up or recent hair loss) and maintenance therapy to minimize the risk of relapse or flare-up after complete hair regrowth [24]. Nevertheless, these recent guidelines make no distinction between adults and children. The advent of JAKi for AA in adults and the recent European approval of ritlecitinib for adolescents over 12 years [52] is reshaping ST prescriptions. However, prescriptions remain physician-dependent and are influenced by experience, drug availability and concerns regarding use of each ST in children, especially given the lack of pediatric guidelines. Experts also highlight the high cost and limited long-term safety data for JAKi in children, reinforcing the need for alternative treatments [51].

Conclusions

This national, retrospective real-world study assessed and compared first-line ST for severe pediatric AA, using DS as a proxy for treatment effectiveness and tolerability. It identified two distinct ST patterns consistent with recent expert consensus guidelines: acute therapy (e.g. systemic CS), primarily used to manage flare-ups and not intended for long-term use; and maintenance treatments, preferred for chronic and relapsing disease forms, including MTX (with or without CS), JAKi and dupilumab. Overall, the effectiveness of therapies appears limited with frequent relapse and a likely need for prolonged treatment. However, data on long-term treatment outcomes of JAKi in children are lacking. In this context, physicians can choose to prescribe older systemic treatments, which may represent valuable alternatives in the pediatric population. More studies on the newer STs in the pediatric population are needed in order to develop guidelines. In this direction, our study has provided real-life data which could help develop future management strategies in children with severe AA.
