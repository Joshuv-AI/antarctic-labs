Research note: Untargeted metabolomics to understand the impact of controlled atmosphere stunning and electrical stunning on metabolite profiles of early postmortem breast fillet☆
Authors: Rishav Kumar, Melissa Boersma, Sunoh Che, Zahidul Tushar, Rabin Raut, Dianna V. Bourassa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pre-slaughter stunning influences postmortem muscle metabolism and may affect broiler breast meat quality and processing consistency. Although electrical stunning (ES) and controlled atmosphere stunning (CAS) are commonly used in poultry production, comparative evidence regarding their biochemical impacts during the early postmortem period remains limited. In this study, we utilized an untargeted metabolomics approach to identify metabolite profile alterations in broiler breast muscle following CAS and ES under pilot plant processing conditions. Early postmortem breast fillet samples from Ross 708 broilers stunned using CAS or ES were analyzed using ultra-performance liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS). Breast fillets (n = 6 per treatment) were collected approximately 30 min postmortem, with early pH measured at 15 min and instrumental color recorded before snap freezing for metabolomics. Meat quality was analyzed using GLM in SAS 9.4, with stunning method as the main effect (P < 0.05). CAS fillets exhibited lower muscle pH than ES fillets (P < 0.001), while color traits were non-significant. Metabolite normalization, statistical analysis, and pathway enrichment were conducted using MetaboAnalyst 6.0. We identified 508 metabolites, of which five differed between CAS and ES at P < 0.05 (uncorrected): methylglyoxal, gluconic acid, (R)-malate, aspartyl-leucine, and 1D-myo-inositol-3-phosphate. All were downregulated in CAS fillets. Pathway analysis showed effects on pyruvate metabolism and the pentose phosphate pathway, with involvement in the TCA cycle, amino acid metabolism, and inositol phosphate signaling. Lower carbonyl stress metabolites and pentose phosphate intermediates in CAS fillets suggest reduced metabolic and oxidative stress, while TCA cycle and proteolysis differences indicate altered postmortem energy use. These findings show stunning methods affect early postmortem metabolism, with CAS inducing fewer stress- and energy-related metabolic changes than ES. Findings are preliminary, given the lack of false discovery rate correction, absence of targeted validation, and potential confounding by sex and body weight. As a practical application, the metabolomic profile of breast tissue helps understand the physiological effects of different stunning methods to enhance meat quality and consumer acceptability and supports optimized stunning protocols.

Introduction

The global poultry industry has expanded rapidly in recent decades. According to the USDA (2025), broiler chicken production is projected to increase from 21.591 million metric tons in 2025 to 21.864 million metric tons in 2026. As production continues to rise, consumer expectations regarding both meat quality and animal welfare have become increasingly important. Consequently, greater attention has been directed toward poultry slaughter practices, which can significantly influence animal welfare outcomes and the quality characteristics of poultry meat. Various slaughter and stunning technologies have been developed and implemented worldwide to address these concerns, with electrical stunning (ES) and controlled atmosphere stunning (CAS) being the two most widely adopted commercial methods (Ham et al., 2025).

Among the factors influencing poultry meat quality, the stunning method applied before slaughter plays a critical role because it affects both animal welfare and postmortem muscle metabolism (Petracci et al., 2010). Electrical stunning, the most widely used commercial method, induces unconsciousness through disruption of neural activity. However, the passage of electrical current through muscle tissue can cause intense muscle contractions, increase the risk of carcass defects and influence postmortem biochemical processes related to pH decline, color stability, and water-holding capacity (Adeyemi and Sazili, 2014). In contrast, CAS reduces pre-stun handling and generally promotes a more uniform progression of postmortem metabolism, potentially resulting in more consistent meat quality. Nevertheless, exposure to stunning gases during the induction phase may elicit aversive behaviors and muscle activity that can affect muscle structure and water-holding capacity (Riggs et al., 2023). Although electrical stunning and CAS have been widely compared in previous studies, most investigations have focused on conventional meat quality and stress indicators, while the underlying physiological and metabolic responses remain incompletely understood. Therefore, evaluating the effects of these stunning methods on early postmortem metabolism is important for advancing our understanding of how slaughter practices influence poultry metabolic responses.

While traditional meat quality measurements provide important phenotypic outcomes, they offer limited insight into the complex biochemical events occurring during the early postmortem period. Untargeted metabolomics enables a comprehensive and unbiased characterization of low-molecular-weight metabolites, and it focuses on measuring metabolic responses of living organisms to different stimuli or physiological conditions. Previous studies have demonstrated the utility of untargeted metabolomics in poultry meat to elucidate metabolic mechanisms underlying meat quality as well as muscle myopathies (Zhou et al., 2025). This approach is particularly beneficial to postmortem meat, where rapid transitions in glycolysis, mitochondrial activity, oxidative stress, and cellular defense mechanisms occur within hours after slaughter and are highly sensitive to pre-slaughter handling and stunning methods. By integrating untargeted metabolomic profiling, this study aims to elucidate how CAS and ES differentially influence early postmortem metabolism in chicken breast meat. Such metabolomic differentiation is particularly important for poultry processing. The ability to detect subtle metabolic differences using sensitive, unbiased analytical approaches strengthens our understanding of stunning processes while also advancing our mechanistic understanding of how slaughter practices impact postmortem muscle metabolism and poultry meat quality. In our study we hypothesized that electrical stunning and controlled atmosphere stunning induces distinct early postmortem metabolic responses in chicken breast muscle due to differences in physiological stress and muscle activity during stunning. Therefore, the objective of this study was to compare early postmortem metabolite profiles between electrical stunning and CAS in broiler breast meat using an untargeted metabolomics approach.

Material and methods

Sample collection and broiler processing

The experiment was conducted at the Charles C. Miller Jr. Poultry Research and Education Center at Auburn University. All samples were collected postmortem from a study with approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Auburn University (Approval No. 2025-5575). A total of 30 birds (15 per treatment) were subjected to either CAS or ES, followed by standard commercial post-stunning and processing procedures. All birds were Ross 708 YPM, a mixed population of males and females, and were processed at comparable market weights (2.5 to 3.0 kg) under typical industry conditions, with a market age of 35 days. Feed withdrawal was performed overnight prior to processing. For ES, birds were removed from transport crates, shackled, and stunned in an electrical water bath at 20 mA per bird for 12 s, followed by mechanical neck cutting and exsanguination for 240 s. Carcasses were then hard scalded at 54°C for 160 s and defeathered for 35 s. For CAS, birds remained in their transport crates and were exposed to progressively increasing concentrations of CO₂ across five phases, ranging from 20% to 85% over a 6-min period, with oxygen maintained at 21% during the initial 90 s. The ES and CAS conditions were selected based on standard commercial poultry processing practices and previously established industry-relevant protocols to ensure practical applicability and consistency with typical industrial stunning operations. Following CAS, carcasses were processed using the same post-stunning procedures as those applied to ES birds. Following defeathering and evisceration, each carcass was assigned to its respective treatment and used for the collection of raw breast (Pectoralis major) muscle samples. Breast fillets (n = 6 per treatment) were collected approximately 30 min postmortem for metabolomic analysis. Early pH and instrumental color measurements were recorded at 15 min postmortem prior to metabolite sampling. These time points were selected to capture early postmortem physiological changes while ensuring appropriate sample handling for metabolomic stability. A representative portion of muscle tissue (∼50 to 60 g) was excised from the central region of each fillet using sterile instruments to minimize contamination and spatial variability. Samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, placed in pre-labeled aluminum foil, and stored at −80°C until further analysis to preserve metabolic integrity.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Raw breast meat samples (n = 6 per treatment) were collected and used for untargeted metabolomics; a time point at which metabolic profiles predominantly reflect stunning-induced physiological responses. Approximately 100 mg of liquid nitrogen snap-frozen tissue was extracted with 1 mL of methanol and homogenized using a BeadBug bead beating at speed 5 for 2 min. Homogenates were centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 5 min, and the clarified supernatants were collected for downstream metabolomic analyses.

UPLC-MS analysis

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis was performed on a Vanquish Flex Binary UPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to a quadrupole orbitrap mass spectrometer (Orbitrap Exploris 120, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a heated electrospray ionization (H-ESI) source. Data were acquired in both positive and negative modes using Xcalibur software (V4.4.16.14). Samples (1 μL injection volume) were separated on a Xbridge-Amide column (Waters 3.5 µm, 2.1 × 50 mm) at a 400 μL/min flow rate of mobile phase of solution A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solution B (100% acetonitrile). The gradient began at 98% B for the first 2 min, decreased linearly to 60% B at 10 min, then to 40% B at 12 min, and held for 0.8 min. The system was returned to 98% B in 0.2 min, followed by 5 min re-equilibration, resulting in a total run time of 18 min. The column was maintained at 35°C, and the samples were kept at 10°C in the autosampler. To minimize contamination of the mass spectrometer, the LC flow was diverted to waste for the first 0.8 min and after 13 min of each run. A blank was injected to create a targeted mass exclusion list, and a pooled quality-control sample was injected repeatedly to assess analytical reproducibility. Mass spectra were acquired over an m/z range of 50-500 m/z with a resolution of 60,000, 70% RF lens, and auto maximum injection time with EASY-IC run-start on. The positive spray voltage was 3200 V and the negative was 2200 V. The ion transfer tube temperature was 320°C, and the vaporizer temperature was 275°C. Data-dependent MS/MS fragmentation was performed on singly charged precursor ions with intensities greater than 20,000 counts and with auto dynamic exclusion. Fragmentation was carried out with an isolation window of 1.7 m/z, normalized collision energy of 15, 30, and 17, and Orbitrap resolution of 15,000. Compound identification was performed using Compound Discoverer (v3.2) with ChemSpider, an endogenous metabolite database, and the mzCloud spectral library. Metabolite annotation was based on accurate mass, isotope pattern, retention behavior, and MS/MS spectral matching against mzCloud database entries. Putative metabolite identifications were assigned at Level 2 confidence (putatively annotated compounds) according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative, unless confirmed using authentic reference standards.

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

Meat quality measurements were analyzed using a completely randomized design with the stunning method as the main effect. pH, and color parameters were evaluated using the GLM procedure of SAS (Version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All statistical analysis of metabolomics data was analyzed using MetaboAnalyst (v. 6.0). All peak intensities were median-normalized, log10-transformed, and auto-scaled before analysis. A pairwise comparison between CAS and ES breast samples was performed using Student’s t-tests (P < 0.05). Because this study was exploratory in nature, a strict false discovery rate (FDR) correction was not applied at this stage, as it may remove potentially relevant metabolites. Therefore, results and discussion were reported based on p-value significance (P < 0.05) to highlight metabolites of interest for further investigation. PLS-DA, VIP score analysis, and heatmap visualization were also performed using the built-in tools in MetaboAnalyst. Model performance was assessed using R² and Q² statistics. Permutation testing and 5-fold cross-validation were performed to evaluate model robustness and potential overfitting. The functional roles of metabolites were identified using MetaboAnalyst and pathway databases such as KEGG and HMDB. Metabolite structures were represented using SMILES notation obtained from PubChem, and chemical classification was performed using the ClassyFire system to assign hierarchical taxonomy (kingdom to direct parent) for metabolite annotation.

Results and discussion

Breast fillet pH differed significantly between treatments (P = 0.0007), with CAS samples showing a lower pH (6.23) compared with ES samples (6.28). Lightness (L*) did not differ between treatments (53.01 vs. 52.81; P = 0.60). Similarly, redness (a*) (2.75 vs. 2.66; P = 0.42) and yellowness (b*) (9.49 vs. 9.26; P = 0.42) showed no significant effects of stunning methods. However, hue angle differed significantly (74.76 vs. 75.02; P = 0.005), while chroma values were non-significant for ES (10.35) compared with CAS (10.98). The lower pH observed in CAS samples may be attributed to increased glycolytic activity and lactic acid production during the early postmortem period, resulting in a faster decline in muscle pH. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting lower pH values in broilers subjected to CAS compared with ES (Riggs et al., 2023; Salwani et al., 2016). In contrast, the effects of stunning method on color traits have been less consistent across studies. Riggs et al. (2023) reported significant differences in L* and a* values but not b*, while Pinto et al. (2012) found that fillets from gas-stunned broilers were lighter and less red than those from electrically stunned birds. However, the magnitude of these color differences was relatively small. Similarly, although no significant differences in L*, a*, or b* values were observed in the present study, hue angle differed between treatments. Since hue angle is derived from both a* and b* values, minor shifts in these color components may have contributed to the observed difference, even when the individual color measurements were not significantly affected.

In total, 508 features were identified from the metabolite library across both treatments and classified into eight major categories based on structural and biochemical characteristics. A feature refers to a unique combination of m/z and retention time that appears in the processed LC-MS/MS data after peak detection and alignment. A total of 508 detected features were classified into different metabolite classes, including 327 others (unclassified or structurally diverse compounds), 66 organic acids and their derivatives, 48 amino acids and their derivatives, 23 glycerophospholipids, 19 fatty acyls, 18 nucleotides and their derivatives, 5 carbohydrates and their metabolites, and 2 cholines. Among the annotated metabolites, organic acids and their derivatives represented the largest proportion (12.99%), followed by amino acids and their derivatives (9.45%), glycerophospholipids (4.53%), fatty acyls (3.74%), and nucleotides (3.54%). Minor contributions were observed from carbohydrates (0.98%) and cholines (0.39%), while the remaining features were classified as others (64.37%).

Based on t-test analysis, five metabolites had a p-value less than 0.05 between CAS and ES treatments. These included (R)-malate (TCA cycle intermediate), methylglyoxal (glycolysis-derived carbonyl stress marker), gluconic acid (associated with the pentose phosphate pathway and sugar oxidation), Asp–Leu (a dipeptide reflecting proteolysis), and 1D-myo-inositol-3-phosphate (linked to inositol phosphate signaling). All five metabolites were significantly lower in CAS compared with ES, indicating greater abundance of these compounds in ES-stunned fillets. Differences in metabolite profiles between treatments were visualized using PLS-DA score plots, highlighting group separation. PLS-DA identified key discriminating metabolites based on VIP scores (>1), indicating greater importance, while heatmap analysis further confirmed distinct clustering patterns between CAS and ES groups. Subsequently, permutation testing (n = 1000) and 5-fold cross-validation were performed to evaluate the robustness and predictive ability of the PLS-DA model and to assess potential overfitting. The cumulative R² values increased from 0.76496 to 0.99984 with increasing numbers of components, indicating a strong fit to the training data. The regression line of Q² intersected the ordinate below zero, suggesting limited structure in the permuted models. However, permutation testing yielded a non-significant result (P = 0.097), indicating that the model was not statistically better than randomly generated models. In addition, Q² values obtained during cross-validation indicated limited predictive performance under validation conditions, suggesting potential overfitting and that the model has stronger descriptive than predictive capability. Overall, the PLS-DA model shows apparent separation in the supervised analysis; however, this separation is not supported by permutation testing or cross-validation, and therefore the results should be interpreted as exploratory. Volcano plot analysis integrates fold-change analysis and t-tests, allowing identification of metabolites based on biological significance, statistical significance, or both. Notably, no metabolites were found to be significantly upregulated in CAS compared with ES. All interpretations presented in this discussion are exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Metabolomic comparisons were based on unadjusted p-values (P < 0.05) without false discovery rate (FDR) correction; therefore, the findings are preliminary and should be interpreted with caution. Only five metabolites differed significantly between controlled atmosphere stunning (CAS) and electrical stunning (ES), and no direct measurements of glycolytic flux, glycogen depletion, lactate accumulation, oxidative stress, advanced glycation end-product (AGE) formation, or protein degradation were performed. To our knowledge, this is the first untargeted metabolomics study comparing early postmortem metabolite profiles between CAS and ES in broiler breast muscle. The differential abundance of methylglyoxal, gluconic acid, 1D-myo-inositol 3-phosphate, R-malate, and aspartyl-leucine may represent a metabolic signature distinguishing these stunning methods. Metabolites are the end products of gene expression and protein activity. Therefore, metabolite profiling provides valuable insight into how these small molecules influence and reflect phenotypic changes in biological systems.

The five differential metabolites suggest that CAS and ES induce distinct early postmortem metabolic trajectories, tentatively grouped into themes such as glycolytic flux, redox handling, and energy metabolism with proteolysis. Methylglyoxal (MG) is a reactive by-product of glycolysis formed mainly from the intermediates dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (GAP), with minor contributions from lipid and protein metabolism. Although it represents only a small fraction of glycolytic flux, MG is highly reactive and readily forms advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), which contribute to oxidative modification of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids (Allaman and BÃ, 2015; Ouali et al., 2013; Przybylski et al., 2022). Elevated MG levels have been associated with increased oxidative stress and impaired protein functionality in postmortem muscle, potentially affecting meat quality attributes such as water-holding capacity (Sundekilde et al., 2017). In response to MG accumulation, cells may activate the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) to generate NADPH for detoxification and redox balance, linking glycolytic metabolism with antioxidant defense mechanisms (Stincone2 et al., 2015). Therefore, MG serves as a key metabolic intermediate connecting glycolysis, oxidative stress response, and PPP activity, and its abundance may reflect differences in early postmortem energy metabolism between treatments. In the present study, CAS samples showed a faster early postmortem pH decline, indicating differences in the progression of glycolysis. Despite this, MG levels were lower in CAS than in ES. This pattern may reflect altered timing and handling of glycolytic intermediates during early postmortem metabolism rather than differences in overall glycolytic rate. In ES samples, a slower postmortem pH decline may allow greater accumulation of MG precursors, resulting in higher MG levels. These observations suggest that differences in early postmortem metabolic dynamics between CAS and ES may influence MG abundance; however, glycolytic activity and AGE formation were not directly measured, and therefore any relationship between pH decline, glycolysis, and MG abundance remains speculative.

Gluconic acid, a metabolite associated with glucose oxidation and redox-related metabolism (Kornecki et al., 2020), was also lower in CAS samples. Therefore, differences in gluconic acid abundance may be associated with variation in glucose utilization and redox-related metabolic processes between treatments. Similar reductions in gluconic acid have been reported in dark-cutting beef and have been linked to altered postmortem glucose metabolism characterized by impaired glycolytic processing (Harr et al., 2025). Therefore, the lower abundance of gluconic acid in CAS may reflect differences in glucose utilization and redox-related metabolic processes between treatments. According to (Carrillo et al., 2016) the pentose phosphate pathway operates alongside glycolysis and generates NADPH for cellular metabolism and redox balance. Consequently, variation in PPP-associated metabolites may reflect differences in glucose utilization and metabolic responses to physiological stress. However, PPP activity and oxidative stress markers were not directly measured in the present study; therefore, this interpretation should be considered tentative.

Additional metabolite differences were observed for 1D-myo-inositol 3-phosphate, R-malate, and aspartyl-leucine. 1D-myo-inositol 3-phosphate was also lower in CAS samples. Myo-inositol derivatives are synthesized from glucose-derived intermediates and are involved in phosphoinositide metabolism and cellular signaling processes (Gonzalez-Uarquin et al., 2020). Previous studies have demonstrated a close relationship between myo-inositol and glucose metabolism, as myo-inositol is derived from glucose-6-phosphate (Dinicola et al., 2017). R-malate is a recognized intermediate of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and is associated with mitochondrial energy metabolism (Zhang et al., 2020). Recent study has reported interactions between malate and glycolytic metabolites, suggesting coordinated involvement of glycolytic and mitochondrial metabolism in postmortem muscle (King, et al., 2026). Aspartyl-leucine is a dipeptide associated with peptide turnover and amino acid metabolism (Lee et al., 2022), and it may reflect early postmortem proteolytic activity as an intermediate product of protein degradation in muscle. The lower abundance of these metabolites in CAS samples may reflect differences in early postmortem metabolic processes between stunning methods. However, direct measurements of pathway activity were not conducted; therefore, these metabolites should be interpreted as indicators of potential metabolic variation rather than evidence of pathway activation or suppression.

When considered collectively, the metabolite profile of CAS samples differed from that of ES samples, with CAS showing relatively lower abundances of metabolites associated with glycolysis by-products, redox-related metabolism, energy metabolism, cellular signaling, and peptide turnover. These observations suggest that the stunning method may influence early postmortem metabolic responses in broiler breast muscle, although the interpretation is based on a limited number of significant metabolites and should be considered exploratory.

Importantly, all mechanistic interpretations remain hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, as the study was conducted at a single postmortem time point (30 min) with a limited sample size and did not include direct measurements of glycolytic flux, glycogen depletion, lactate accumulation, oxidative stress, AGE formation, mitochondrial activity, or proteolysis. Phenotypic measurements (pH and color at 15 min postmortem) and metabolomic sampling (30 min postmortem) were conducted at different time points. Given the rapid metabolic changes that occur during the early postmortem period, this temporal difference should be considered when interpreting associations between metabolite abundance and meat quality traits.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides preliminary evidence that stunning methods may influence early postmortem muscle metabolism. Future studies incorporating larger sample sizes, multiple postmortem sampling times, targeted metabolomics, and direct biochemical measurements are needed to validate these observations and determine whether the metabolic differences observed between CAS and ES translate into meaningful effects on poultry meat quality.
