Cellular senescence: the “hidden driver” in chronic inflammatory skin disorders
Authors: Huifang Liang, Junqin Li, Kaiming Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic inflammatory skin disorders are characterized by persistent skin inflammation, impaired barrier function, and frequent recurrence, all of which substantially reduce patients’ quality of life. Traditional studies attribute the pathogenesis of these diseases to immune dysregulation, whereas new evidence indicates that cellular senescence is a key risk factor and a mechanism underlying disease progression. Cellular senescence, defined by irreversible cell cycle arrest, is accompanied by a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), mitochondrial dysfunction, and DNA damage. This review systematically outlines the hallmarks, regulatory mechanisms, and inducers of cellular senescence, elucidates its pathological role in representative inflammatory skin disorders, and evaluates emerging therapeutic strategies that target senescent cells. By elucidating the dynamic crosstalk between cellular senescence and inflammatory skin diseases, this review aims to identify novel therapeutic targets and provide new insights into their diagnosis, treatment, and prevention.

Introduction

Inflammatory dermatology encompasses a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by cutaneous inflammation and impaired skin barrier function, with multifactorial etiologies that involve complex crosstalk between genetic predisposition and environmental triggers [1]. The traditional understanding of inflammatory dermatosis pathogenesis centers on the dysregulation of innate and adaptive immune responses, encompassing an imbalance in Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells, as well as the aberrant activation of keratinocytes and excessive secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. However, emerging research in aging biology highlights cellular senescence as a substantial risk factor for the development and progression of these disorders [1]. The heightened prevalence and persistence of inflammatory dermatoses in the elderly further underscore the pivotal role of cellular senescence in their pathophysiological mechanisms.

Cellular senescence is defined as an irreversible state of cell cycle arrest, characterized by the secretion of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), a distinct repertoire of inflammatory factors, and impaired mitochondrial function [2]. Senescent cells secrete inflammatory mediators that markedly disrupt local tissue homeostasis [3], and the resultant pro-inflammatory microenvironment further accelerates cellular senescence [4], establishing a self-amplifying feedback loop. Accumulating evidence confirms that SASP secretion by senescent cells impairs cellular function and exacerbates inflammatory responses and tissue damage. Notably, the central role of cellular senescence in the pathogenesis of inflammatory dermatoses has been increasingly recognized [1].

In this review, we provide an overview of the characteristics, hallmarks, and pathophysiological mechanisms of cellular senescence, with a specific focus on its functional implications and therapeutic strategies for inflammatory dermatoses. By dissecting the interplay between cellular senescence and these disorders, we aim to identify potential therapeutic targets and provide valuable insights into the diagnosis, treatment strategies, and preventive interventions for inflammatory dermatoses.

Cellular senescence

The characteristics of cellular senescence

Cellular senescence refers to a phylogenetically conserved biological process characterized by permanent, irreversible cell cycle arrest induced by diverse endogenous and exogenous stimuli, including DNA damage, telomere attrition, and oxidative stress (Fig. 1). This process is closely linked to organismal aging as well as the initiation and progression of multiple age-related diseases (Table 1).

Notably, cellular senescence lacks a single specific biomarker and is instead defined by a constellation of complementary morphological, functional, and molecular hallmarks: (i) Morphological remodeling: senescent cells exhibit distinct structural alterations, accumulation of the autofluorescent pigment lipofuscin [5], and mitochondrial abnormalities [6]; (ii) irreversible cell cycle arrest; (iii) elevated senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity; (iv) DNA damage accumulation and epigenetic dysregulation; and (v) SASP: a key functional hallmark, senescent cells secrete a complex repertoire of bioactive molecules, which collectively modulate the local microenvironment [2].

The mechanism of cellular senescence

Cellular senescence is a tightly regulated, multi-layered biological process driven by interconnected cellular and molecular pathways that act synergistically to induce irreversible cell cycle arrest and modulate age-related phenotypes [7]. These pathways converge on core regulatory networks, integrating intrinsic stress signals and extrinsic microenvironmental cues to orchestrate the senescence program.

Telomere attrition-DNA Damage Response (DDR) pathway

Telomeres are specialized nucleoprotein complexes at the chromosomal termini, composed of repetitive non-coding DNA sequences (e.g., TTAGGG in humans) and shelterin proteins, which safeguard chromosome integrity and prevent aberrant DNA repair [8]. Once telomere length declines to a critical threshold, a persistent DNA damage response (DDR) is activated [9]. Persistent DDR ultimately locks cells into an irreversible G1/G0 phase arrest, initiating the senescent phenotype.

p53-p21 pathway

As a central stress-responsive pathway, the p53-p21 axis is activated by diverse senescence-inducing factors, including DNA damage, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and telomere dysfunction. Upon stimulation, kinases—including ataxia telangiectasia mutated (ATM), ataxia telangiectasia and Rad3 related (ATR), and checkpoint kinase 1/2 (CHK1/2)—are activated [10]. These kinases phosphorylate the tumor suppressor p53, which upregulates the expression of the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor p21 (CDKI) [11]. Elevated p21 levels inhibit the activity of cyclin-dependent kinase-cyclin complexes, thereby blocking cell cycle progression.

p16-Retinoblastoma (Rb) pathway

Encoded by the CDKN2A locus via alternative splicing, p16INK4a is a key CDK inhibitor that functions independently of p53 to drive senescence, particularly in response to oncogenic stress and chronological aging [12]. In younger tissues, p16 expression is tightly repressed; however, its levels accumulate dramatically in aged and stressed cells. The p16 specifically binds to CDK4 and CDK6, preventing the dephosphorylation of the retinoblastoma (Rb) protein. When Rb is dephosphorylated, it remains active and binds to E2F transcription factors, blocking cell cycle progression [13]. This pathway plays a vital role in stress-induced cellular senescence.

SASP pathway

Beyond intrinsic cell cycle arrest, senescent cells exert paracrine effects through the secretion of SASP factors. SASP induction is driven by signaling cascades, including nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), CCAAT/enhancer binding protein β (C/EBPβ), mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), GATA binding protein 4 (GATA4), and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38MAPK), which are activated downstream of DDR or inflammatory cues [14]. These signaling networks regulate the transcription and secretion of SASP components, such as IL-1α, IL-6, TNF-α, and metalloproteinases (MMPs) [15]. The SASP reinforces the senescent state and induces paracrine senescence in adjacent cells.

The factors that trigger cellular senescence

Cellular senescence is a context-dependent biological response induced by the integration of intrinsic genetic determinants and extrinsic environmental cues, with the core pathogenic basis being cumulative cellular damage that exceeds the capacity of homeostatic repair systems.

Telomere dysfunction is a classic intrinsic trigger of cellular senescence and is closely associated with replicative exhaustion. When telomere length falls below a critical threshold, telomeres lose their protective function and become dysfunctional [16]. Dysfunctional telomeres activate the DNA damage response (DDR) cascade. This impairs DNA repair efficiency and stabilizes tumor suppressor p53. The combined effects of telomere attrition, persistent DDR activation, and p53-mediated transcription initiate cellular senescence.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated during oxidative stress represent a major extrinsic driver of cellular senescence [17]. ROS imposes irreversible inhibition of cell proliferation and causes oxidative damage to cellular macromolecules, including DNA, proteins, and lipids [18]. This multifaceted cellular damage activates the p53-p21 signaling pathway, which inhibits cyclin-CDK complexes, thereby initiating the senescence program [19].

Chronic inflammation and immune system dysregulation also serve as critical extrinsic triggers of cellular senescence. Inflammatory signals can directly promote the senescence of neighboring cells, while the SASP secreted by senescent cells further amplifies local inflammatory responses [20]. This positive feedback loop sustains chronic inflammation, induces persistent cellular stress, and impairs the immune system’s ability to clear senescent cells. The accumulation of uncleared senescent cells and prolonged inflammatory stimulation collectively exacerbates tissue dysfunction and accelerates the progression of senescence.

Inflammatory skin disorders

psoriasis

Psoriasis is an immune-mediated chronic recurrent inflammatory skin disorder with a relatively high prevalence in adults. Clinically, psoriasis is characterized by erythematous plaques and papules covered with silvery-white scales, often accompanied by varying degrees of pruritus [21]. The pathogenesis of psoriasis is complex and involves the interplay of genetic predisposition, environmental triggers, and immune dysfunction [22].

Disease pathogenesis is predominantly orchestrated by Th17 cell-driven immune dysregulation. Elevated secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (including IL-17 A, IL-22, and IL-23) promotes the abnormal proliferation and disrupted differentiation of keratinocytes (KC), while facilitating the recruitment of inflammatory cells (neutrophils and macrophages) into dermal tissue. This establishes a self-sustaining pathogenic cycle of “inflammatory factor storm-aberrant keratinocyte activation-inflammatory progression”. Notably, recent studies have confirmed that cellular senescence is markedly enhanced in psoriatic lesions. Senescent cells secrete a cocktail of factors (pro-inflammatory factors, chemokines, growth factors, and proteases), which collectively contribute to the maintenance of chronic inflammation in psoriasis [23].

The insulin-like growth factor binding protein (IGFBP) family plays a key role in regulating cellular senescence. Beyond their interaction with insulin-like growth factors (IGFs), these proteins also exert independent biological functions, including the regulation of cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis, and participate in various physiological and pathological processes [24]. Specifically, in psoriatic keratinocytes, aberrant expression and secretion of IGFBP2 inhibit excessive keratinocyte proliferation and promote cellular senescence by stabilizing cytoplasmic p21 [25].

The AKT signaling pathway and its associated molecular networks are also involved in regulating cellular senescence in psoriasis. Accumulating evidence indicates that AKT drives cellular senescence through multiple mechanisms. In human endothelial cells, AKT promotes senescence via the p53/p21-dependent pathway [26]; in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (FB), it mediates RAS-induced cellular senescence [27]; and in human fibroblasts, AKT enhances p53 translation, interferes with its ubiquitination and degradation, thereby facilitating the onset of senescence [28]. As a key downstream effector of RAS (a small GTPase that participates in multiple signaling cascades) [29], the PI3K/AKT (Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/Protein kinase B) pathway is abnormally activated in psoriatic skin. Notably, both RAS and its downstream effectors are upregulated in human psoriatic skin, and their overexpression maintains cellular senescence through activating the PI3K/AKT signaling axis [30].

Further studies have clarified the roles of specific senescence-related genes in psoriasis pathogenesis. Deng et al. [23] identified four key genes that regulate psoriasis development by modulating cellular senescence and immune responses. Downregulation of the transcriptional regulator ID4 and cell cycle regulator CCND1 inhibits cell proliferation and impairs skin barrier function. Conversely, the upregulation of the chemokine CXCL1 and interferon regulator IRF7 promotes cellular senescence and sustains the chronic inflammatory state of psoriasis.

Atopic dermatitis

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a common chronic recurrent inflammatory skin disease that predominantly occurs in infancy [31]. Clinically, it is characterized by dry skin, eczematous rashes, intense pruritus, erythema, edema, and desquamation [32]. The etiology of atopic dermatitis is multifactorial, involving genetic predisposition, environmental exposure, immune dysfunction, and allergic responses [33].

A typical feature of AD is type 2 inflammation dominated by cytokines IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, which contribute to keratinocyte dysfunction, persistent pruritus, and impaired skin barrier integrity [34]. Notably, Patients with AD often exhibit increased numbers of macrophages and fibroblasts, along with upregulated expression of SASP [35]. Currently, clinical therapies such as topical corticosteroids and calcineurin inhibitors are effective in controlling acute AD episodes but fail to prevent recurrence and may cause adverse effects [33], highlighting the urgent need for safe and effective long-term therapies to reduce AD relapse rates.

Nuclear factor-erythroid 1-related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a transcription factor that promotes the expression of genes involved in the detoxification of various compounds and reactive oxygen species (ROS). Thus, Nrf2 protects cells from oxidative stress, DNA damage, and ultraviolet radiation-induced apoptosis [36]. Recent experimental studies on AD mouse models have shown that Nrf2 is downregulated in AD lesions, accompanied by increased activity of the senescence marker β-galactosidase and upregulation of another senescence-associated marker CDKN2D (p19) [36]. Furthermore, keratinocytes in these mice exhibited prominent DNA damage and accelerated cellular senescence, a phenotype that worsened with age.

Systemic lupus erythematosus

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a systemic autoimmune inflammatory connective tissue disease characterized by multi-system and multi-organ involvement, recurrent flares, and chronic inflammation [37]. Clinically, it presents with typical cutaneous manifestations, such as malar rash and discoid erythema, and the production of a broad spectrum of autoantibodies is a hallmark feature of the disease. The pathogenesis of SLE is multifactorial, involving complex interactions among genetic, environmental, and endocrine disorders [38].

With a deepening understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms of SLE, accumulating evidence has revealed that cellular senescence and chronic inflammation are prevalent and critical features throughout the disease course, providing a novel perspective for elucidating the driving forces of SLE progression. Specifically, multiple cell types in SLE patients, including mesenchymal stem cells [39], T cells [40], and neutrophils [41], exhibit distinct senescence-associated phenotypes. These phenotypes are characterized by elevated p16 expression, accumulation of reactive oxygen species, and aberrant activation of the p53/p21 pathway, which further exacerbates immune dysregulation and inflammatory responses, thereby contributing to disease advancement.

Sex-determining region Y-related high-mobility group box 4 (SOX4), a key member of the SOX transcription factor family containing a high-mobility group (HMG) domain, plays a regulatory role in various cellular processes [42]. For example, SOX4 can activate the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway to modulate cell proliferation and differentiation [43]. Downregulation of SOX4 has been shown to suppress cell proliferation and migration, promote p53 expression, and induce cellular senescence [44], and SOX4-deficient mice also display accelerated cellular senescence [45]. Notably, in the context of SLE, upregulation of SOX4 attenuates cellular senescence [46], indicating that SOX4 exerts a notable regulatory effect on cellular senescence in SLE.

p16INK4A, another critical regulator of cellular senescence, is not only a causative gene in familial melanoma but also an inhibitor of cyclin-dependent kinases CDK4 and CDK6. It is widely recognized as a principal mediator of premature cellular senescence. Importantly, under SLE pathological conditions, suppression of p16INK4A expression can reverse senescence-related phenotypes by activating the extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling pathway [47], providing valuable insights into the regulatory network of cellular senescence in SLE.

The link between inflammatory skin disorders and cellular senescence

Senescent cells-derived inflammatory secretions

A defining feature of senescent cells is their capacity to release a diverse array of bioactive molecules, collectively termed the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [47]. This secretory profile includes numerous pro-inflammatory agents, among which interleukin-6 (IL-6) is particularly crucial in initiating and sustaining the inflammatory response by activating immune cells [48]. Tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), another key component of the SASP, can induce cell apoptosis, amplify inflammatory signaling, and promote the release of other inflammatory cytokines [49]. Interleukin-1β (IL-1β) also contributes to the inflammatory cascade by stimulating the activation of T and B cells, further intensifying the immune response. In addition to these cytokines, the SASP includes chemokines such as monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), which recruits immune cells (including macrophages and neutrophils) to the inflammatory site and enhances inflammatory infiltration [50]. Growth factors such as transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) are also present in the SASP. Although TGF-β exerts context-dependent functions, it can promote the expression of senescence-associated factors in inflammatory environments, thereby exacerbating inflammatory damage [51].

The secretion of SASP exerts multifaceted effects on the body, triggering local inflammatory reactions and potentially contributing to systemic inflammation (Fig. 2).

Inflammatory-driven induction of cellular senescence

The inflammatory microenvironment comprises various interacting factors that collectively promote the initiation and progression of cellular senescence (Fig. 2). Inflammatory mediators play critical roles in this process: for example, TNF-α activates the intracellular NF-κB signaling pathway, and upon translocation to the nucleus, NF-κB regulates the expression of multiple genes, including those associated with cellular senescence [52]. Additionally, TNF-α induces cellular oxidative stress, leading to DNA damage and accelerating senescence. IL-6 impairs cell proliferation and promotes senescence by activating the STAT3 signaling pathway. Once activated, STAT3 modulates the expression of cell cycle-related proteins, resulting in suppressed proliferation and the induction of a senescent phenotype [53].

Oxidative stress is another key mechanism by which the inflammatory microenvironment drives cellular senescence. During inflammation, immune cells generate substantial amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can damage critical intracellular macromolecules such as DNA, proteins, and lipids. If unrepaired, persistent oxidative DNA damage triggers cellular senescence. Moreover, ROS-mediated protein damage leads to misfolding and aggregation, disrupting normal metabolic functions, lipid peroxidation, impairing membrane integrity, and exacerbating cellular dysfunction [54].

Additional components of the inflammatory microenvironment—such as inflammation-associated proteases and adhesion molecules—also regulate cellular senescence [55, 56]. Proteases degrade the extracellular matrix, altering the cellular niche and impairing cell growth and proliferation. Dysregulated expression of cell adhesion molecules disrupts cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions, thereby interfering with signal transduction and cellular homeostasis. For instance, in arthritis patients, inflammation-induced extracellular matrix degradation and altered adhesion molecule expression predispose chondrocytes to senescence, accelerating joint degeneration.

Importantly, a robust bidirectional interplay exists between cellular senescence and chronic inflammatory diseases. The proinflammatory secretory profile of senescent cells, combined with the senescence-promoting effects of the inflammatory microenvironment, forms a self-amplifying feedback loop that perpetuates tissue dysfunction and disease progression.

The self-amplifying senescence-inflammation loop in dermatosis pathogenesis

Cellular senescence and inflammation are not independent biological processes; rather, they engage in tightly coordinated, bidirectional crosstalk that constitutes a self-reinforcing pathological feedback loop. This loop is a hallmark feature of chronic inflammatory skin disorders and critically contributes to disease persistence, recurrent flare-ups, and progressive tissue remodeling.

On the one hand, senescent cells residing in the skin, including keratinocytes, dermal fibroblasts, and vascular endothelial cells, continuously secrete abundant SASP factors. These bioactive mediators perpetuate local inflammation, promote the recruitment of circulating immune cells to lesion sites, and actively reshape the cutaneous tissue microenvironment. In contrast, persistent inflammatory stimuli, including pro-inflammatory cytokines, ROS, and matrix-degrading enzymes, induce de novo senescence in neighboring skin cells [57, 58]. The resulting accumulation of additional senescent cells further amplifies the SASP output, thereby reinforcing and sustaining the cycle.

Critically, this reciprocal senescence-inflammation axis exerts acute and long-term pathogenic effects. In established inflammatory dermatoses, chronic activation of this axis can drive epidermal hyperplasia, dermal fibrosis, dysregulated angiogenesis, and compromised epidermal barrier dysfunction [59, 60]. Unlike transient, self-resolving acute inflammation, this coupled state exhibits intrinsic resistance to resolution, offering a mechanistic explanation for the high rates of relapse and treatment resistance observed in many chronic inflammatory skin diseases. Consequently, therapeutic strategies targeting pivotal nodes within this bidirectional loop represent a promising avenue for disease-modifying intervention.

Although senescence and inflammation are spatially and functionally intertwined in lesional skin, they are molecularly and cellularly distinct states that can be rigorously distinguished in vivo. Integrated approaches, including multiplex immunohistochemical co-staining, cell-type-specific molecular biomarker profiling, and high-resolution transcriptomic analyses, enable robust differentiation between senescent and infiltrating inflammatory cells. Indeed, recent single-cell RNA sequencing and multiparameter imaging studies have confirmed that these two populations constitute non-overlapping cellular identities within diseased tissue [58, 61]. Transcriptomic and proteomic profiling robustly corroborates this functional dichotomy: senescent cells exhibit hallmarks of irreversible cell cycle arrest, DNA damage response activation, and p53/p16 pathway upregulation, whereas inflammatory cells display dominant enrichment in immune effector functions, cytokine signaling cascades, and antigen presentation pathways [61].

Nevertheless, the accurate detection and quantification of senescent cells in human and murine skin remain technically challenging due to three principal limitations: (i) the absence of a universally specific, single-senescence marker [58]; (ii) confounding interference from the complex inflammatory and stromal microenvironment; and (iii) the inherently low frequency and heterogeneous spatial distribution of senescent cells. Accordingly, multimodal, multi-marker detection strategies currently represent the methodological gold standard for in vivo discrimination of senescence from inflammation, and overcoming the aforementioned technical constraints remains essential to advance both mechanistic understanding and translational applications in dermatology.

Therapeutic strategies targeting cellular senescence

Chronic inflammatory skin diseases are pathologically characterized by progressive accumulation of senescent cells and dysregulated hyperactivation of the SASP. In recent years, interventions targeting cellular senescence have emerged as central in the treatment of inflammatory diseases. Current therapeutic modalities encompass three principal approaches: (i) senolytics, agents that selectively eliminate senescent cells; (ii) SASP modulators, compounds that suppress the production or activity of SASP factors without eliminating senescent cells; and (iii) next-generation biologics integrating anti-inflammatory and anti-senescent mechanisms through targeted molecular or cellular pathways.

Senolytics in inflammatory skin diseases: from preclinical validation to clinical translation

Senolytics are pharmacologically targeted agents designed to induce apoptosis in senescent cells. Senolytics selectively inhibit these dysregulated survival cascades, triggering caspase-dependent apoptosis in senescent populations while preserving the viability and function of non-senescent dermal and epidermal cells. This selective ablation interrupts the SASP-driven feed-forward cycle of chronic inflammation and tissue dysfunction in the affected skin.

Currently, senolytic candidates are broadly classified into monotherapies and rationally designed combination regimens. The dasatinib–quercetin (D + Q) combination remains the most extensively validated senolytic regimen to date. Dasatinib inhibits tyrosine kinase–dependent survival signaling in senescent immune cells and dermal fibroblasts, whereas quercetin preferentially targets senescent keratinocytes and epithelial-derived cells by modulating p53/p21 expression [62]. Additional well-characterized agents include fisetin, a naturally occurring flavonoid with demonstrated efficacy against multiple senescent cell types, and navitoclax, a potent, orally bioavailable Bcl-2/Bcl-xL inhibitor with selective activity against senescent stromal and mesenchymal cells [63, 64].

Preclinical evidence from murine models of psoriasis, AD, and SLE consistently demonstrates robust anti-senescent and anti-inflammatory effects of senolytic treatment. Senolytic treatment can effectively reduce the abundance of senescent keratinocytes and dermal fibroblasts in lesional skin, downregulate the expression of canonical senescence biomarkers, and attenuate the secretion of key SASP mediators [65, 66]. Moreover, senolytic therapy ameliorates epidermal hyperplasia, restores expression of barrier-associated structural proteins, and diminishes dermal infiltration of T cells and neutrophils [63, 64].

These compelling preclinical outcomes have catalyzed their clinical translation. Several phase I and II trials are currently underway to evaluate the safety, pharmacokinetics, and preliminary efficacy of senolytics in patients with chronic inflammatory dermatoses. Early phase clinical data indicate that intermittent oral D + Q administration markedly reduces disease activity scores, concomitant with measurable declines in circulating SASP factors and peripheral blood markers of cellular senescence [62]. However, existing clinical trials are limited in terms of scale and duration.

Senomorphics in inflammatory skin diseases: from preclinical validation to clinical translation

Senomorphics, also referred to as SASP modulators, are pharmacological agents that suppress the synthesis, secretion, or biological activity of SASP factors, either directly or indirectly by inhibiting downstream signaling pathways activated by SASP ligands [67]. In contrast to senolytics, which induce selective apoptosis of senescent cells, senomorphics preserve cellular viability while functionally neutralizing the proinflammatory and tissue-destructive effects of SASP. This mechanistic distinction positions senomorphics as a complementary therapeutic strategy, thereby expanding the therapeutic repertoire of senescence-driven chronic inflammatory dermatoses.

The Nrf2 pathway is a well-validated endogenous regulator of redox homeostasis and cytoprotection. Recent studies have demonstrated that Nrf2 paradoxically contributes to pathological senescence, with elevated Nrf2 activity driving aberrant SASP amplification [36]. These findings identify Nrf2 not only as a protective factor but also as a context-dependent therapeutic target in AD-associated senescence. Consistently, preclinical models have confirmed that pharmacological activation of Nrf2 attenuates cutaneous inflammation, reduces epidermal oxidative DNA damage, and delays keratinocyte senescence in mice with AD [68]. Notably, both systemic administration and topical application of Nrf2 agonists restore expression of barrier integrity proteins and improve transepidermal water loss metrics, indicating functional recovery of epidermal barrier competence [69]. Collectively, these data support Nrf2 modulation as a dual-purpose intervention that mitigates the initiation of senescence and reinforces structural and functional barrier resilience.

The mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), a serine/threonine kinase within the phosphatidylinositol kinase-related kinase (PIKK) family, is a central regulator of in vivo cellular senescence [70]. In murine models of SLE, rapamycin reverses the senescent phenotype of bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) by suppressing hyperactivation of mTOR signaling. Critically, the adoptive transfer of rapamycin-pretreated MSCs ameliorated glomerulonephritis, skin inflammation, and autoantibody production in SLE mice [71].

A growing body of evidence implicates the JAK–STAT pathway as the dominant effector axis mediating SASP-induced paracrine crosstalk between senescent skin-resident cells. Consequently, JAK inhibitors constitute the most clinically advanced class of senomorphics to date. Multiple preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated that JAK blockade suppresses SASP factor expression, inhibits STAT1/3 phosphorylation in lesional tissues, and reduces SA-β-gal activity in epidermal and dermal compartments [72–74]. Aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin and p53/p21 axes has been consistently observed in MSCs from patients with SLE. Targeted inhibition, either pharmacological Dickkopf-1 [DKK1] or genetic β-catenin–specific siRNA, downregulates p53 and p21 protein levels, restores proliferative capacity, and rescues mitochondrial function in senescent MSCs, thereby reversing key hallmarks of cellular senescence [75].

To date, clinical evaluation of senomorphics in chronic inflammatory skin diseases has predominantly focused on canonical anti-inflammatory and symptom endpoints. Dedicated trials designed to quantify senescence-associated outcomes are notably scarce. Few studies have specifically examined how these factors affect cellular senescence. Nevertheless, phase II and III clinical trials of JAK inhibitors in psoriasis, AD, and SLE have consistently yielded evidence of disease-modifying effects. Beyond rapid improvement in cutaneous inflammation, pruritus, and epidermal hyperplasia, these agents greatly reduce the risk of disease relapse and the incidence of infections [72–74]. These agents substantially decreased expression of senescence-associated transcripts in peripheral blood and lesional tissues, suggesting a potential reduction in senescence-associated pro-inflammatory activity. These correlative findings provide indirect yet robust clinical validation of the capacity of JAK inhibitors to remodel the senescent microenvironment and interrupt pathogenic SASP feedback loops in human inflammatory dermatoses.

Biological agents in inflammatory skin diseases: from preclinical validation to clinical translation

In contrast to senolytics and senomorphics, which directly target cellular senescence, conventional biologics primarily exert therapeutic effects through the selective neutralization of key pro-inflammatory cytokines or inhibition of pathological immune cell activation. Accumulating preclinical and clinical evidence indicates that these immunomodulatory agents can indirectly modulate the senescent cell burden and attenuate SASP by reshaping the inflammatory microenvironment.

Biologics achieve their core therapeutic efficacy by precisely intercepting pivotal inflammatory mediators that are central to the pathogenesis of chronic inflammatory skin disorders. In psoriasis, anti-IL-17 and anti-IL-23 monoclonal antibodies selectively inhibit the IL-23/Th17 axis, which is the principal driver of epidermal hyperplasia and dermal inflammatory infiltration [76, 77]. In AD, dupilumab, a first-in-class biologic that antagonizes IL-4 and IL-13, suppresses dysregulated Th2 immunity and promotes restoration of epidermal barrier integrity [78]. In SLE, belimumab, an inhibitor of B-cell activating factor (BAFF), effectively controls autoimmune-driven inflammation; by mitigating chronic autoinflammatory stress, it also ameliorates persistent senescence in lesional keratinocytes [79].

Mechanistically, biologics interrupt upstream inflammatory stimuli and abrogate stress signals that initiate and propagate cellular senescence. Such targeted interventions effectively reduce SASP factor expression in lesional skin, solidifying the evidence that biologics indirectly regulate the cutaneous senescent microenvironment. In essence, they ameliorate senescence-associated pathologies via systemic reprogramming of the inflammatory landscape rather than through direct modulation of the senescence machinery.

A broad spectrum of biologics has received regulatory approval for the treatment of chronic inflammatory skin diseases, supported by decades of robust clinical experience. Large-scale randomized controlled trials (Phase III), long-term extension studies, and real-world evidence consistently demonstrate favorable efficacy and an acceptable safety profile, characterized by sustained lesion clearance, improved patient-reported outcomes, and enhanced health-related quality of life. Emerging clinical research highlights the regenerative capacity of stem cell–derived exosomes in cutaneous repair, positioning them as promising candidates for both diagnostic biomarkers and next-generation therapeutics in dermatology [80].

Challenges in the clinical translation of senescence-targeted therapeutics

Despite the compelling efficacy of senescence-targeted interventions, including senolytics, senomorphics, and biologics, in preclinical models and early-phase clinical trials, their broader clinical translation remains constrained by several interrelated challenges.

Limited tissue specificity

Systemically administered senolytics do not preferentially accumulate in lesional skin and may inadvertently eliminate physiologically beneficial senescent cells in healthy tissues, compromising tissue homeostasis and adaptive immunity.

Suboptimal cutaneous drug delivery

Orally delivered senolytics exhibit low bioavailability in skin compartments due to poor dermal penetration and first-pass metabolism [81]. Topical formulations, although intuitively appealing, are hindered by stratum corneum barrier resistance, enzymatic degradation in the epidermis, and rapid cutaneous clearance, collectively resulting in insufficient local drug exposure and subtherapeutic intracellular concentrations.

Cell and molecular heterogeneity of senescence

Senescent cells in inflammatory skin lesions display marked heterogeneity across lineages and contexts, as reflected in the divergent expression profiles of senescence effectors, SASP composition, and pro-survival dependencies [82]. This variability undermines the universality of single-agent senolytic strategies.

Uncertainties in the dosing regimen and treatment scheduling

Chronic inflammatory dermatoses are characterized by relapsing-remitting courses. However, evidence-based consensus on the optimal senolytic dosing frequency, cumulative exposure thresholds, intervention windows, and comparative efficacy of intermittent pulsed regimens versus sustained low-dose administration remains lacking.

Uncharacterized long-term biological consequences

The systemic and cutaneous implications of repeated long-term senescent cell depletion are not yet fully elucidated [82]. Prospective longitudinal clinical studies with extended follow-up periods are required to rigorously evaluate the safety profile, durability of response, and overall risk–benefit ratio across heterogeneous patient populations.

Summary and future perspectives

Cellular senescence is an important regulatory factor in the initiation and progression of inflammatory skin diseases. By secreting SASP, senescent cells contribute to the establishment of a chronic inflammatory microenvironment, which interacts with key pathological mechanisms such as impaired skin barrier function and immune imbalance to form a self-perpetuating cycle that exacerbates disease development.

In recent years, substantial progress has been made in understanding the role of cell senescence in common inflammatory dermatoses, including psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, and lupus erythematosus, supporting the therapeutic potential of targeting these senescent cells. Preclinical studies from animal models demonstrate robust efficacy of senotherapeutic interventions [83]; however, several challenges remain for clinical translation, including drug selectivity, long-term safety, optimal administration routes, and reliable methods for detecting and quantifying senescent cells in cutaneous tissues.

Future research in this field should focus on the following directions: First, an in-depth investigation into the heterogeneity of senescent cells across different inflammatory skin diseases is warranted, along with elucidation of cell type-specific senescence mechanisms, particularly in keratinocytes, fibroblasts, and immune cells, to establish a foundation for precision-targeted therapies. Second, the development of novel therapeutics with enhanced selectivity and improved safety profiles is essential, accompanied by optimization of delivery strategies (topical formulations) to minimize systemic exposure and adverse effects. Third, identification and validation of robust cellular senescence-associated biomarkers could facilitate early diagnosis, objective disease monitoring, and prognostic assessment of inflammatory skin diseases. Fourth, well-designed, multicenter, large-scale clinical trials are needed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of anti-senescence therapies in human patients.

With continued advancements in this area, targeting cellular senescence holds great promise as a transformative strategy for treating inflammatory skin diseases, potentially offering more effective and personalized therapeutic options for affected individuals.

Conclusion

Cellular senescence is a central pathogenic driver of chronic inflammatory skin disorders. It contributes to persistent inflammation, barrier dysfunction, and high recurrence through irreversible cell cycle arrest and disruption of skin homeostasis. Core senescence features, including SASP, mitochondrial dysfunction, and DNA damage, form an interconnected cascade that amplifies and sustains cutaneous inflammation. The complex bidirectional crosstalk between senescence pathways and disease mechanisms reveals multiple druggable targets for novel therapeutics. Targeting senescence offers transformative potential for diagnosis, prevention, and treatment by breaking this self-perpetuating inflammatory cycle.
