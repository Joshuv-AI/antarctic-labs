Cultivar and tree age jointly shape rhizosphere bacterial diversity and community assembly in Macadamia integrifolia
Authors: Xingze Li, Linan Kang, Chengmeng Zhang, Huiping Zeng, Guixiang Yu, Jiangmin Yang, Furong Li, Wenliang Cai, Yanhuang Feng, Haibo Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractThis study investigates the regulatory effects of cultivar and tree age on the rhizosphere bacterial community of Macadamia integrifolia, aiming to provide theoretical support for targeted regulation of the rhizosphere microecosystem and high-quality, high-yield production in the industry. Using rhizosphere soil from 5-year-old and 8-year-old plants of five major cultivated cultivars as materials, the bacterial community characteristics were revealed through 16S rRNA gene high-throughput sequencing combined with R-based data analysis and visualization. The results show that the community is dominated by two phyla, Actinobacteria and Chloroflexi, with the core dominant genus being Rhodoplanes. Cultivar is the leading factor driving community differentiation, and the effect of tree age is cultivar-specific. Functionally, the community is dominated by amino acid, carbohydrate, and lipid metabolic pathways, which are well-adapted to the host plant’s growth demands; the co-occurrence network exhibits predominantly strong positive correlations and high modularity, suggesting synergistic adaptation and metabolic complementarity among dominant bacterial taxa. This study clarified that the rhizosphere bacterial community of Macadamia integrifolia is primarily regulated by cultivar, and has identified core functional taxa such as Rhodoplanes, laying a scientific foundation for the development of specialized microbial inoculants and the coordinated regulation of cultivar-microbe interactions.Key points• Cultivar is the dominant driver of macadamia rhizosphere bacterial community, with tree age exerting cultivar-specific effects.• Rhodoplanes is the core dominant genus, and the bacterial community functions are dominated by metabolic pathways.• Macadamia rhizosphere bacterial co-occurrence network shows strong positive correlations and modularity, guiding microbial inoculant development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13909-8.Keywords: Macadamia integrifolia, Rhizosphere bacterial community, Cultivar effect, Tree age

Introduction

Macadamia nuts (Macadamia integrifolia) are an evergreen tree of the family Proteaceae and genus Macadamia, the rainforests of southeastern Queensland and northeastern New South Wales, Australia (Nagao et al 1992; Mai et al 2021). Its kernels contain 70~80% oil, which can be used to produce high-quality salad oil and various foods (Aquino-Bolaños et al., 2017). Macadamia nuts are rich in unsaturated fatty acids, which can prevent and improve atherosclerosis, cardiovascular diseases, and enhance the skin’s anti-aging abilities (Kaseke et al. 2022; Jardim et al 2024). China introduced macadamia in the early twentieth century and now has a planting area of 336,400 hectares, accounting for 60% of the global total, making it the country with the largest planting area and the fastest industry growth in the world (Pan et al 2025; Lu et al 2025a, b). Among these, Yunnan Province has a planting area of 275,733 hectares, accounting for nearly 50% of the world and 80% of China, making it the largest production area in the country (Ning et al 2025).

Soil is the most abundant microbial gene pool on Earth and has immense development potential (Luo et al. 2022). In this ecosystem, microorganisms play a central role; they are not only key decomposers driving soil nutrient cycling and improving soil structure but also profoundly influence the growth of terrestrial plants. Their changes are closely related to the entire soil ecological environment (Phillips and Fahey 2006). The rhizosphere is a soil microregion strongly influenced by root exudates, with physicochemical and biological properties different from the original soil. Microorganisms that inhabit this environment are referred to as rhizosphere microorganisms (Cao et al. 2023; Gao et al. 2024). These include bacteria, fungi, viruses, and other groups, among which bacteria account for the highest proportion (Fang et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2022). They directly participate in nutrient cycles such as carbon and nitrogen, as well as organic matter decomposition in the rhizosphere, acting as an important medium connecting soil and plants. Known as the “second genome” of plants, the community structure and interaction mechanisms between these microorganisms and plants have become research hotspots in the field of microbiology in recent years (Song et al. 2025). Studies have shown that there are significant differences in the diversity and functions of microorganisms in rhizosphere and non-rhizosphere soils. These differences are mainly regulated by plant root exudates and rhizosphere environmental factors and are also closely related to intrinsic plant traits such as species and developmental stages (Gabriele et al., 2009; Ma et al. 2019; He et al. 2022). Currently, high-throughput sequencing technology has become an important cornerstone in the study of microbial community diversity. With this technology, the rhizosphere microorganisms of various nut crops such as walnut (Wang et al. 2023), peanut (Li et al. 2020), and almond (Bouabidi et al. 2025) have been detected and identified, providing strong support for the development of related research. Current research on macadamia mainly focuses on fresh fruit quality (Lu et al 2025a, b; Zhang et al 2025a, b), constituent components (Shuai et al 2022a, b; Zhu et al 2025), cultivation technology (Kang et al 2025), and processing technology (Shuai et al 2022a, b; Aruwajoye et al 2023). Although the significance of macadamia rhizosphere microorganisms in promoting plant growth and stress resistance has been preliminarily recognized (Yue et al 2020; Ning et al 2025), the response patterns of the rhizosphere bacterial community to varietal specificity and the dynamic changes associated with tree age remain unclear. Critical scientific questions, such as how the interaction between cultivar and tree age regulates the diversity of core functional rhizosphere bacteria and whether dominant bacterial groups that are adapted to macadamia growth exist, still lack systematic interpretation.

This study employs 16S rRNA gene high-throughput sequencing technology to analyze the characteristics of rhizosphere soil bacterial communities across 10 treatment groups, consisting of two tree ages of five macadamia varieties. The aim is to reveal the regulatory effects of cultivar and tree age on the diversity, structural composition, and function of rhizosphere bacterial communities, and to identify core functional bacterial communities related to macadamia growth and development. This will provide scientific evidence for subsequent targeted regulation of the rhizosphere micro-ecological environment, development of specialized microbial inoculants, and enhancement of the industry's sustainable development capacity.

Materials and methods

Collection of rhizosphere soil samples

On May 22, 2023, rhizosphere soil samples of 5-year-old and 8-year-old trees from five varieties of macadamia were collected at the Macadamia Germplasm Resource Base of the Yunnan Tropical Crops Science Research Institute in Jinghong City, Xishuangbanna Dai Autonomous Prefecture, Yunnan Province, China (20°01'N, 100°77'E, elevation 585.6 m) (Table 1). The five varieties of macadamia nuts were named 'Hidden Valley A16', 'Hidden Valley A4', 'HAES 816', 'Own Choice', and 'TZ', which were abbreviated as 'A16', 'A4', '816', 'OC', and 'TZ' in the text, respectively. The planting pattern at this base was 5 × 7 m row spacing, with five varieties, each planted in three rows (30 plants per row). For each cultivar, 5-year-old and 8-year-old trees were planted in adjacent plots with identical soil background and field management practices, minimizing potential spatial confounding effects. The middle row was selected as the sampling object, and the five-point sampling method was used for each individual tree: five sampling points were evenly distributed around the fibrous root zone of a single tree at different directions, and soil samples from these five points were thoroughly mixed to form one biological replicate. The sampling steps were as follows: removed surface coverings and debris from the soil, gently excavated the soil with a small hoe until the roots were exposed, removed the loose soil around the roots, collected the rhizosphere soil adhering to fine roots with a diameter ≤ 2 mm, and divided the samples into sterile sampling bags, sealed and labeled them. Three independent trees were randomly selected for each treatment as biological replicates, and a total of 30 soil samples were collected from ten treatments. Notably, although we minimized spatial heterogeneity through adjacent planting and uniform management, minor residual confounding effects from micro-environmental variations cannot be completely excluded.

Extraction of metagenomic DNA

After the soil samples were collected and pre-treated (removal of impurities and homogenization), total microbial DNA was extracted using the MagBeads FastDNA Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, CA, USA). The extracted DNA was detected by 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop 2000 micro-spectrophotometer; DNA with A260/A280 ratios of 1.8–2.0 and A260/A230 ratios > 1.7 was used for subsequent experiments.

Library construction and high-throughput sequencing

Using qualified total soil DNA as the template, the universal bacterial primers 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) were used to amplify the V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene (Sun et al. 2013). PCR amplifications were performed using TransStart FastPfu DNA Polymerase (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) in a 50 μL reaction system. The PCR program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles at 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. After detecting PCR products using 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis, they were purified using the Qiagen QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit.. The purified products were used to construct sequencing libraries with the Illumina TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Prep Kit. Libraries were quantified using a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (concentration ≥ 10 ng/μL) and quality controlled with Agilent 2100 (fragments 500 ~ 600 bp). Qualified libraries were then sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform for paired-end high-throughput sequencing. The agarose gel electrophoresis results of 16S rRNA gene V3-V4 region amplification are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Table S1).

16S rRNA gene sequencing data processing and ASV inference

Raw sequencing reads were processed using QIIME 2 (Version 2020.2.0) (Caporaso et al., 2010) following the DADA2 pipeline. First, raw reads were demultiplexed based on sample-specific barcodes, and barcode and primer sequences were trimmed. Quality filtering was performed to remove reads with Phred quality scores < 20, ambiguous bases, or length < 200 bp. Paired-end reads were merged using the qiime dada2 denoise-paired command with a minimum overlap length of 20 bp. Denoising was conducted simultaneously to correct sequencing errors, and chimeric sequences were identified and removed using the de novo method implemented in DADA2. Chloroplast, mitochondrial, and unassigned sequences (sequences not classified at the domain level) were filtered out from the ASV table. The original ASV abundance table, normalized (rarefied) ASV table, and the comprehensive summary file of tag counts and ASV statistics are deposited in the Supplementary Materials (Tables S2–S4).

Data analysis

For each ASV, representative sequences were extracted and taxonomically annotated against the Silva database (Version 138.1) (Quast et al 2013). MUSCLE (Version 3.8.3) (Edgar 2004) was used for multiple sequence alignment, and a phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the alignment results.

Alpha diversity indices (Shannon, Simpson, Chao1, Pielou’s evenness, Observed species, Faith’s PD, and Good’s coverage) were calculated to analyze differences in bacterial community richness and diversity among samples by Mothur (Version 1.30.1)(Chappidi et al 2019). The Simpson index was calculated as Simpson’s diversity index, expressed as 1 − ∑Pi2, where Pi is the proportional abundance of the i-th ASV; a higher value indicates greater diversity.

QIIME 2 (Version 2020.2.0) (Caporaso et al. 2010) was used to calculate unweighted and weighted UniFrac distance matrices, incorporating both the ASV abundance matrix and phylogenetic tree. Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) was used to assess beta diversity differences among groups. Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) and Principal component analysis (PCA) were performed using the vegan package in R software (Version 4.1.2) (Gao et al. 2024) to visualize differences in bacterial community composition. Functional predictions were conducted using PICRUSt software (Version 2.4.2) (Donovan et al. 2014) to identify differential KEGG metabolic pathways among samples.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R software to evaluate differences between samples; the Mann–Whitney U was used for comparison between two groups, and the Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction was used for comparison among multiple groups; P < 0.05 indicated statistically significant differences between groups.

Results

Quality control and processing of sequencing data

Through 16S rRNA gene high-throughput sequencing, the rhizosphere soil bacteria of five macadamia varieties and two tree ages (a total of ten treatment groups) were analyzed. Supplementary Figure S1 shows that the rarefaction curves of rhizosphere bacterial ASVs in different samples gradually increase in observed species number as the number of sequencing reads rises. When the sequencing reads exceed 20,000, the curve tends to flatten, indicating that the sequencing samples used can accurately reflect the diversity of most bacterial communities and encompass information on most microbial species.

Species composition analysis

The relative abundance distribution of rhizosphere soil bacteria at the phylum and genus levels (Top 20) in 10 treatment groups of different tree ages for five macadamia varieties was visualized using QIIME2 (2020.2.0) for statistical analysis of reads (Fig. 1). At the phylum level (Fig. 1A), the bacterial phylum with the highest abundance in all treatments was Actinobacteria, followed by Chloroflexi and Acidobacteria, among other groups. From the perspective of the interaction effect between cultivar and tree age, differences exist in the phylum-level composition of the same cultivar under different tree ages. For example, in the ‘A16’ cultivar, the five-year-old (A1) and eight-year-old (A2) treatments show obvious fluctuations in the abundance proportions of Actinobacteria and Chloroflexi; in the ‘OC’ cultivar, the difference in Chloroflexi abundance between the five-year-old (O1) and eight-year-old (O2) treatments is relatively significant. At the genus level (Fig. 1B), the bacterial genera with relatively high abundance in all treatments were Rhodoplanes, Candidatus Solibacter, and Burkholderia. Rhodoplanes was detected in both two tree ages of all five varieties with a detection frequency of 100%. Among them, the average relative abundance of Rhodoplanes in three varieties including ‘A16’, ‘816’ and ‘OC’ exceeded 2.0%. Combined with cultivar and tree age analysis, the genus-level composition differences are more pronounced in the same cultivar with different tree age treatments: for example, in the ‘A16’ cultivar, A1 and A2 treatments show significant changes in the abundance proportion of multiple dominant genera; in the ‘OC’ cultivar, O1 and O2 treatments also exhibit some differentiation in the structure of dominant genera.

Alpha diversity

The community species diversity of the 10 grouped samples was analyzed using Alpha diversity indices such as Chao1 index, Simpson index, Shannon index, Pielou’s index, Observed species, Faith’s PD, and Good’s coverage (Fig. 2). The Chao1 (P = 0.094) index shows fluctuations between groups, and the effect of tree age on species richness within the same cultivar exhibits varietal specificity. Specifically, the 8-year-old groups of varieties ’A16’, ’A4’, and ’816’ have higher Chao1 indices than those of the 5-year-old groups, while the 5-year-old groups of varieties ’OC’ and ’TZ’ are higher than the 8-year-old groups. This indicates that both cultivar and tree age can influence community species richness to varying degrees. There were no significant differences in Simpson index (P = 0.51), Shannon index (P = 0.24), and Pielou index (P = 0.45) among all groups, and the Simpson values for each treatment were close to 1, while the Shannon values ranged from 9.5 to 11.0, indicating that the overall diversity of the rhizosphere microbial communities in all treatments was at a relatively high level. The intergroup fluctuation trend of the Observed species (P = 0.14) is consistent with that of the Chao1 index, showing an interaction effect between cultivar and tree age; the Faith’s PD index (P = 0.08) shows a nearly significant difference, with the 8-year-old trees of varieties ‘A16’, ‘A4’, and ‘816’ being higher than the 5-year-old ones, while for varieties ‘OC’ and ‘TZ’ the 5-year-old trees are higher than the 8-year-old ones; the Good’s coverage index (P = 0.0056) shows significant differences between groups, with most cultivar and tree age combinations having a coverage close to 0.995, except for B2 and B1, which have relatively low coverage, indicating a higher proportion of rare species in these sample groups and relatively poor sequencing coverage completeness.

Beta diversity

Based on the weighted UniFrac phylogenetic distance matrix, Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) were used to compare community differences among samples and to assess the effects of macadamia varieties and tree age on the structure of associated communities (Fig. 4). The PCoA results (Fig. 4A) showed that Axis1 and Axis2 cumulatively explained 43.7% of the community variation. The samples of the same cultivar at ages 5 and 8 were relatively clustered in coordinate space, indicating small differences in community composition; whereas samples of different varieties exhibited significant spatial separation and marked differences in community structure (such as 'A16', 'OC', and 'TZ'). The fitted stress value (Stress) of NMDS was 0.151 (Fig. 4B), indicating a good fit for the analysis. Samples from different tree age groups of the same cultivar were more closely clustered, and the differentiation of community structure among different varieties was more pronounced. Among them, the separation between samples of the 'OC' and 'TZ' varieties and those of other varieties was particularly notable, further confirming the conclusions of the PCoA analysis. Figure 4C shows the ANOSIM inter-group distance box plot (with group A1 as the reference). From the distance distribution in the box plot, the distances between different cultivar treatment groups and group A1 are significantly greater than those between groups of different tree ages within the same cultivar, further proving the dominant role of cultivar in community structure differentiation.

Differential species analysis

A total of 767 ASVs were shared across all 10 treatment groups, and each group contained unique ASVs. The number of unique ASVs was the highest in group A2 (4435) and the lowest in group O2 (2470) (Fig. 5A). Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that PC1 and PC2 explained 86.1% and 5.3% of the total community variation, respectively, with a cumulative contribution rate of 91.4%. Samples from different tree ages of the same variety clustered relatively closely in the ordination space, while samples from different varieties were clearly separated (Fig. 5B). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis revealed multiple significantly different taxa among treatment groups (Fig. 5C). Among them, taxa such as Actinobacteria in group T1 had the highest LDA score (close to 5), and taxa including Pseudonocardiaceae and Burkholderia in group A1 also exhibited high LDA scores. Overall, groups T1, A1, and B2 harbored more differential taxa, whereas groups A2, A3, and O2 had fewer.

Predicted functional pathway analysis

Based on the KEGG database, predicted functional pathways of bacterial communities in the rhizosphere soil of macadamia nuts were annotated, and the relative abundance distribution characteristics of their secondary inferred functional pathways were analyzed (Fig. 7). The predicted functional pathways of this community can be divided into six major categories: Cellular Processes, Environmental Information Processing, Genetic Information Processing, Human Diseases, Metabolism, and Organismal Systems. Among these, the Metabolism category has the highest overall relative abundance of secondary pathways and is the core component of the community’s functionality. In the Metabolism category, the relative abundances of Amino acid metabolism, Carbohydrate metabolism, Lipid metabolism, and Metabolism of cofactors and vitamins are particularly prominent; in addition, secondary pathways such as Biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites and Metabolism of terpenoids and polyketides also exhibit higher abundance, indicating that the rhizosphere bacterial community of macadamia possesses strong putative functional potential in material metabolism and biosynthesis. In the Genetic Information Processing category, secondary pathways such as folding, sorting and degradation, and replication and repair show certain relative abundance, reflecting the basic functions of gene information processing and maintenance within the community. The relative abundance of secondary pathways in the Cellular Processes and Environmental Information Processing categories is relatively low; whereas the relative abundance of most secondary pathways in Human Diseases and Organismal Systems categories is close to 0, suggesting that the predicted functional characteristics of this rhizosphere bacterial community are highly adapted to the rhizosphere microenvironment.

Dominant microbiota identification and co-occurrence network characteristics analysis of bacterial communities

An analysis of the top 50 ASVs in terms of average abundance per sample (Fig. 8A) revealed that strong positive correlations predominate among features, suggesting potential synergistic adaptation and metabolic complementarity of dominant groups to the rhizosphere microenvironment. Combined with the pie charts within the nodes, it further suggests cultivars exert a stronger influence on group abundance and associations than tree age. The fluctuations in group abundance among different tree ages within the same cultivar are relatively weak, whereas the proportional differences in abundance among different cultivars are significant. This pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that cultivars may select for dominant groups through differences in the rhizosphere microenvironment and shape specific co-occurrence patterns. The modular association network diagram (Fig. 8B) revealed that ASVs within the community form distinct modular aggregation characteristics. Among these, the module_55 module represented by pink nodes accounts for a relatively high proportion of the total nodes and may represent one of the core functional clusters in the co-occurrence network. The edge connection density between nodes within each module is significantly higher than that between different modules, indicating that bacterial taxa within the same module have a closer co-occurrence relationship. This suggests that these taxa may have similar ecological niches.

Discussion

Analysis of rhizosphere soil bacterial diversity

As the core area for the exchange of matter and energy among plants, soil, and microorganisms, the structure of the rhizosphere microbial community is significantly regulated by plant characteristics (Prashar et al 2014; Ma et al 2019). In this study, Actinobacteria and Chloroflexi were identified as the dominant phyla of rhizosphere soil bacteria in macadamia, which is consistent with the characteristics of dominant microbial groups in the rhizosphere of other perennial fruit trees such as Citrus sinensis (Zhang et al 2017), Juglans regia (Wang et al 2023), and Malus domestica (Wang et al 2022a, b), reflecting the commonality in the composition of rhizosphere microbial communities in woody plants. In terms of function, the phyla Actinobacteria and Chloroflexi, as typical multifunctional bacterial phyla, not only participate in the decomposition of soil organic matter and the transformation of nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus (Liu et al. 2020), but also produce antibiotic secondary metabolites to enhance plant stress resistance (Zhou et al., 2022). The enrichment of these two phyla in the 5-year-old 'TZ' cultivar treatment may be related to the cultivar’s need for efficient nutrient transformation and stress protection. At the genus level, Rhodoplanes is a core advantage genus shared by all five macadamia varieties. Most bacteria in this genus are photoheterotrophic; although they do not perform oxygenic photosynthesis, they can use light energy to drive organic matter metabolism, giving them a competitive advantage in low-nutrient or microaerobic environments, and participate in nitrogen cycling processes (Srinivas et al. 2014). The relatively high abundance of Rhodoplanes in the rhizosphere of different varieties indicates a universal and stable demand for efficient energy utilization and nitrogen transformation functions in the macadamia rhizosphere microenvironment, which is consistent with the long-term nutrient acquisition strategies of perennial woody plants (Tao et al., 2022). Further research found that the associated microbial communities of different varieties showed significant differentiation. The relative abundance of Bradyrhizobium, a slow-growing rhizobial genus, in the rhizosphere of the 'A4' cultivar was significantly higher than in other varieties, while 'OC' was enriched with Streptomyces, a genus known for antibiotic synthesis. This function-oriented community differentiation may stem from differences in the chemical composition of root exudates among different varieties. Existing studies have shown that plants can actively recruit specific functional microorganisms by regulating the composition of exudates such as phenolic acids, organic acids, and sugars (Jjemba and Alexander 1999; Korenblum et al., 2020). For example, in Camellia oleifera, high-yield varieties secrete more citric acid to enrich phosphate-solubilizing bacteria, while disease-resistant varieties release specific flavonoids to induce the colonization of antagonistic bacteria (Dong et al 2024). We speculate that 'A4' may promote the growth of Bradyrhizobium by secreting specific carbon sources to supplement nitrogen, while 'OC' may rely on antimicrobial metabolites produced by Streptomyces to enhance rhizosphere defense.

The diversity of rhizosphere bacterial communities in macadamia is influenced by both cultivar and tree age, but the regulatory effect of cultivar is more significant. Alpha diversity analysis showed an interaction effect between cultivar and age on species richness, with the 8-year-old treatment of cultivar ‘816’ exhibiting significantly higher richness than the 5-year-old ‘A16’ cultivar and the 8-year-old ‘OC’ cultivar. However, there were no significant differences among groups in species diversity (Simpson, Shannon) or evenness (Pielou e), indicating that cultivar and age mainly affect species abundance and phylogenetic relationships, but have a weaker impact on the evenness of abundance distribution. This is consistent with the findings of Zhang et al. (2025a, b), who reported that plant root exudates can increase rhizosphere microbial complexity and are significantly related to alpha diversity, and also aligns with the characteristic selective regulation of rhizosphere microbial diversity by cultivars in most crops (Liu et al. 2024). PCoA and NMDS results of beta diversity showed that the community structures of samples from the same cultivar but different ages were relatively clustered, while those from different varieties were clearly separated. ANOSIM analysis (R = 0.621, P = 0.003) confirmed that the differences between groups (varieties) were much greater than those within groups (same cultivar, different ages), indicating that even among cultivated varieties with similar genetic backgrounds, the rhizosphere microbiome exhibited significant differentiation. It is noteworthy that the weak effect of tree age observed in this study contradicts the widespread notion that microbial communities undergo dynamic succession with plant development (Bever et al., 2012; Marín et al., 2021), while aligning more closely with the theory that the rhizosphere microenvironment of perennial woody plants tends to stabilize over time (Gao et al. 2024). This suggests that in mature macadamia plants, root architecture, carbon allocation patterns, and the profile of root exudates may have reached a steady state, thereby maintaining a relatively constant environment for microbial selection. This finding indicates that, during the mature stage of macadamia, soil microbial management strategies can be focused on varietal characteristics rather than requiring frequent adjustments to accommodate changes in tree age, thus providing a streamlined approach for precision agriculture.

Functional network analysis

In the differential species analysis, there were 767 shared taxonomic units among the 10 treatment groups, along with many unique taxa. This result is consistent with the findings of Dong et al. (2024), who reported that different Camellia oleifera varieties dominated specific microbial taxa in the rhizosphere. LEfSe analysis showed that the phylum Actinobacteria and its subordinate taxa in group T1 had the highest LDA score (close to 5), while Pseudonocardiaceae and Burkholderia were enriched in group A1. Combined with the predicted functional characteristics, Actinobacteria are closely related to functions such as tolerance to nutrient-poor conditions, antibiotic production, and degradation of complex organic matter (Liu et al. 2020). The enrichment of Actinobacteria in the TZ cultivar may be due to stronger environmental stress faced during the seedling stage. As a typical plant-growth-promoting bacterium, the high abundance of Burkholderia in the rhizosphere of the A16 cultivar may be related to this cultivar’s efficient demand for nitrogen and phosphorus. Such bacteria can increase the available nutrient content in soil through nitrogen fixation and phosphorus solubilization (Suárez-Moreno et al., 2012).

Putative KEGG functional annotation shows that the inferred functional pathways of the rhizosphere bacterial community are mainly related to metabolism, with the predicted highest relative abundances in amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, lipid metabolism, and cofactor and vitamin metabolism, which is highly consistent with the putative functional characteristics of rhizosphere microorganisms in perennial woody plants (Zhang et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2023). The high predicted abundance of amino acid metabolic pathways reflects the potential that rhizosphere microorganisms may participate in nitrogen transformation by degrading proteins and polypeptides in plant residues to produce amino acids, or by providing essential amino acids to plants through biosynthetic pathways (Tashkandi et al., 2022). This finding echoes the inferred nitrogen metabolism functions of the dominant bacterial phyla mentioned earlier. It is noteworthy that the predicted abundance of pathways categorized as Human Diseases and Organismal Systems is close to zero, a characteristic that highlights the strong environmental adaptability of the rhizosphere microbial community and the reduction of plant-unrelated functions. This is consistent with the pattern found by Xiao et al. (2024) in their study of functional pathways in plant rhizosphere microbes in kaolin mining areas, where specific pathways are enriched under stressful environmental conditions.

The co-occurrence network of rhizosphere bacteria in macadamia is primarily characterized by strong positive correlations and exhibits distinct modular aggregation features. This result is consistent with the conclusions of most soil microbial network studies (Wang et al. 2023; Pechlivanis et al., 2024). The predominance of positive correlations reflects the synergistic adaptation and metabolic complementarity of dominant groups to the rhizosphere microenvironment. For example, the positive correlation between Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria may result from Actinobacteria degrading complex organic matter to produce small molecules (such as organic acids) that serve as carbon sources for Proteobacteria, while the nitrogen-fixing activity of Proteobacteria supplements nitrogen for Actinobacteria. This forms a synergistic cycle (Liu et al., 2023). In this study, the module_55 module serves as the core functional cluster, with the internal node connectivity density significantly higher than that between modules, suggesting that the bacterial taxa within this module may possess similar ecological niches. This modular structure is an important guarantee for community stability; when environmental fluctuations occur, only specific modules are affected, while other modules can maintain normal functions, thus ensuring the ecological service capacity of the entire community (Zhou et al., 2022). In addition, the node abundance pie charts show that the regulatory effect of varieties on taxa associations is stronger than that of tree age, which is consistent with the conclusion from the Beta diversity analysis that cultivar is the main driver of community differentiation. Additionally, bulk soil samples were not collected as a systematic control in this study, which limits our ability to quantify the magnitude of the rhizosphere filtering effect of macadamia and distinguish rhizosphere-enriched taxa from ubiquitous soil-background taxa. In our ongoing follow-up studies conducted at the same experimental site, we will systematically include bulk soil samples collected 50 cm away from the tree canopy as controls to further elucidate the specific recruitment mechanism of core functional taxa in the macadamia rhizosphere.

Conclusion

The rhizosphere soil of different Macadamia varieties exhibits rich bacterial diversity, with cultivar being the dominant factor shaping the rhizosphere bacterial community, while the effect of tree age is relatively weak. Actinobacteria and Chloroflexi are among the core dominant phyla, with community functions mainly centered on metabolic pathways. The co-occurrence network demonstrates strong positive correlations and modular characteristics. Future research should focus on functional validation of dominant microbiota and systematically elucidate the plant-soil-microbe interaction mechanism by integrating soil physicochemical properties and root exudate characteristics.
