Digital twin optimization of water supply pump stations considering energy use, water hammer, and lifespan
Authors: Yongzeng Liu, Li Cheng, Kai Liu, Tan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Water supply pump stations operate under multiple and often conflicting requirements related to energy efficiency, hydraulic safety, and equipment durability. To address this problem, this study develops a digital-twin-assisted multi-objective optimisation framework for pump station operation, in which energy consumption, water hammer peak pressure, and equipment lifespan are considered simultaneously. Using the first-stage pumping station of the South Main Line of the Yellow River Diversion Project in Shanxi Province as a case study, a digital twin model is constructed from historical records, equipment parameters, and real-time monitoring data, and an improved Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II) is introduced to generate feasible Pareto solutions under operational constraints. The model validation results show that the mean simulation errors of energy consumption, water hammer peak pressure, and lifespan prediction are 3.13%, 2.14%, and 7.29%, respectively, while the response time remains within 0.4 s. Compared with the conventional operating scheme, the selected compromise solution reduces energy consumption by 12.0%, lowers water hammer peak pressure by 15.0%, and improves equipment lifespan by 9.0%, with full constraint satisfaction. The improved NSGA-II also shows better convergence, diversity, and feasible-solution performance than the traditional algorithm. Field trial results further indicate that the proposed framework can support safer, more efficient, and more stable pump station operation. This study provides a practical basis for multi-objective operational optimisation of water supply pump stations under dynamic operating conditions.

Introduction

Water supply pump stations are key hydraulic facilities in urban and regional water-transfer systems. Their operating conditions directly affect water-delivery reliability, energy expenditure, pressure safety, and equipment maintenance. In many large-scale pumping projects, operators must adjust pump frequency, unit combination, and hydraulic control measures under changing flow demand and pressure constraints. Therefore, pump station operation is not only an energy-saving problem, but also a coordinated engineering problem involving hydraulic transients and long-term asset condition. Previous studies have shown that digitalized management can improve the observability of drinking water systems when monitoring data are linked with virtual models1. Real-time pump optimisation has also been used to reduce energy consumption under dynamic operating conditions2. These studies provide useful methodological foundations, but they do not fully address the coupled relationship among energy use, water hammer peak pressure, and equipment service life in pump station operation.

Water hammer is one of the major hydraulic safety concerns in pump station systems. Sudden pump start-up, pump stoppage, valve regulation, or rapid flow variation may generate transient pressure waves in the pipeline. If the transient pressure exceeds the allowable limit, it may increase the risk of pipeline vibration, joint leakage, valve damage, and local structural fatigue. For long-distance water-transfer projects, the influence of water hammer is more difficult to ignore because pressure waves can propagate over long pipeline sections and interact with boundary conditions. At the same time, equipment service life is closely related to repeated hydraulic loading, vibration response, and operating stress cycles. If optimisation only focuses on energy consumption, the selected scheme may reduce short-term power cost but increase transient pressure fluctuation or accelerate fatigue accumulation. Studies on water infrastructure during abnormal operating conditions have indicated that digital models can help analyse system impacts and support operational adjustment3. Probabilistic state estimation has also improved the representation of hydraulic uncertainty in water networks4. However, further work is still needed to connect hydraulic safety and equipment degradation with pump scheduling decisions.

In recent years, data-driven methods have been increasingly used for state perception and operational decision support in water systems. Graph neural networks have been applied to state estimation in water distribution systems, showing their potential for capturing complex hydraulic relationships5. Energy-market-oriented studies have further suggested that water utilities can benefit from more flexible pump operation when hydraulic constraints are properly considered6. In addition, burst and anomaly detection methods based on graph learning have improved the identification of local risks in water distribution systems7. These studies have expanded the technical basis for intelligent water-system operation. Nevertheless, many existing works remain focused on a single task, such as state estimation, energy scheduling, or fault detection. For pump stations, there is still a need for an integrated framework that can transform real-time state information into multi-objective operational decisions.

Digital twin technology provides a promising way to bridge this gap. A digital twin can establish a continuously updated virtual representation of the physical system and provide state mapping, hydraulic prediction, and operational feedback. Recent studies have discussed digital twins for water distribution systems and highlighted their value in combining sensing, modelling, prediction, and control8. Related research has also argued that digital twins may support a paradigm shift in water distribution system management by linking virtual models with physical operation9. In addition, digital water services have been developed to extend the digital twin concept from model representation to operational decision support10. These studies show that digital twins are no longer limited to monitoring or visualisation. However, in pump station operation, the connection between digital twin state updating and optimisation algorithms is still not sufficiently clear. In particular, how the updated operating state is transferred to the optimisation module, and how the optimised decision is fed back to the physical system, needs to be described more explicitly.

Multi-objective optimisation is also essential for pump station scheduling because energy efficiency, water hammer control, and equipment lifespan may conflict with each other. A review of operational control strategies in water supply systems has shown that future operation should pay more attention to both energy and cost efficiency under practical constraints11. Sustainable pump configuration and water-network partitioning studies have further demonstrated that pump operation should be evaluated together with hydraulic performance and long-term system feasibility12. Booster optimisation research has provided useful evidence that pressure regulation and pump control can be coordinated under uncertain demand conditions13. Probabilistic multi-objective optimisation has also been used to improve water distribution network design by considering solution quality and uncertainty14. These studies confirm the necessity of multi-objective thinking. However, most optimisation targets still focus on energy use, cost, reliability, or pressure regulation, while water hammer peak pressure and equipment service life are rarely incorporated into one operational optimisation structure.

Recent research has further extended water distribution optimisation by considering distributed energy conditions and hydraulic power-based search-space reduction15. Digital twin studies on urban water systems have also emphasised the relationship between system efficiency and sustainable development goals16. These developments indicate that pump station optimisation should move toward a more dynamic, coupled, and engineering-oriented framework. Based on this background, this study develops a digital-twin-driven multi-objective economic operation optimisation framework for water supply pump stations. The proposed framework integrates real-time state mapping, hydraulic transient prediction, equipment lifespan estimation, and an improved NSGA-II algorithm. Energy consumption, water hammer peak pressure, and equipment service life are considered simultaneously. The first-stage pumping station of the South Main Line of the Yellow River Diversion Project in Shanxi Province is used as the engineering case.

The main contributions of this study are as follows. First, the study formulates pump station operation as a coupled multi-objective optimisation problem rather than a single energy-saving task. Second, a digital twin model is constructed to provide updated hydraulic, energy, and equipment-condition information for optimisation. Third, an improved NSGA-II algorithm is introduced to generate feasible Pareto solutions under flow, pressure, and operating-range constraints. Finally, the proposed framework is evaluated using an engineering pump station case, with attention to energy reduction, water hammer peak pressure mitigation, equipment service-life improvement, and operational feasibility. This study aims to provide a practical decision-support basis for safer and more economical pump station operation under dynamic working conditions.

Research progress

Research on multi-objective economic operation of water supply pump stations

The economic operation of water supply pump stations has increasingly moved from single energy-saving control to coordinated optimisation under multiple engineering constraints. In practical operation, pump scheduling must satisfy flow demand, pressure limits, unit efficiency, and safe operating ranges at the same time. Recent studies have shown that water distribution optimisation can be improved when hydraulic constraints and distributed energy conditions are incorporated into the decision process17. Variable-speed pump scheduling has also been studied through mixed-integer optimisation, which is useful for representing both pump start-stop states and speed regulation in one framework18. In addition, sensitivity-based optimisation can help identify the key variables affecting operating cost and hydraulic response, thereby improving the interpretability of pump operation decisions19.

Recent work has further linked pump operation with demand-side management, electricity price, and time-dependent scheduling. Cost-efficient pump operation can be improved when demand variation and operating conditions are considered jointly20. Coordinated scheduling research also indicates that pump operation should be evaluated together with hydraulic conditions and water quality rather than energy indicators alone21. More recently, digital twin-based dynamic scheduling has provided a real-time basis for energy-saving optimisation in pump stations22, while automated data transfer has been recognised as an important prerequisite for reliable digital twin applications23. However, existing studies still focus mainly on energy cost or scheduling efficiency. The simultaneous consideration of energy consumption, water hammer peak pressure, and equipment service life remains insufficient.

Application of digital twins in hydraulic engineering

Digital twin technology provides a useful way to connect physical pump station operation with virtual simulation and decision support. In hydraulic engineering, a digital twin is not only a visual model of the physical system. It should also support real-time data acquisition, model updating, state correction, and operational feedback. Recent real-time pump scheduling studies have shown that optimisation performance can be improved when scheduling models receive updated system information and respond to dynamic operating conditions24. This is especially important for pump stations because pump efficiency, transient pressure, and equipment condition may change with unit combination, frequency regulation, and flow demand.

Pressure estimation is another important basis for digital twin applications in water systems. Graph neural network-based pressure estimation has shown that complex hydraulic relationships can be learned from monitoring data and network structure25. Although the present study does not adopt graph neural networks as the main model, this research reflects the broader trend of using data-driven or hybrid methods to improve hydraulic state perception. For pump station optimisation, accurate state perception is closely related to water hammer peak pressure prediction and constraint evaluation. Therefore, digital twin modelling should not remain at the level of monitoring or visualisation. It needs to provide updated hydraulic, energy, and equipment-condition information to the optimisation algorithm and support a clear closed-loop process from state update to optimisation and feedback.

Application of NSGA-II algorithm in multi-objective optimization

NSGA-II has been widely used in engineering optimisation because it can generate Pareto solution sets for problems with conflicting objectives. This feature is suitable for pump station operation, where energy consumption, water hammer peak pressure, and equipment service life cannot usually be optimised to their best values at the same time. Recent experimental research has shown that pump operations can induce measurable hydraulic transients in water distribution systems26. Studies on long-distance pumping station water-transmission engineering have also confirmed the practical importance of water hammer protection during pump stoppage and other transient conditions27. These findings suggest that water hammer peak pressure should be included in the optimisation process rather than discussed only after scheduling results are obtained.

Equipment service life is another key factor in pump station optimisation. Pump units, bearings, and related components are affected by vibration, hydraulic loading, start-stop cycles, and fatigue accumulation. Digital twin-based remaining useful life prediction for centrifugal pump rolling bearings provides a useful reference for incorporating equipment condition into operational decision-making28. In addition, digital twin-based resilience evaluation of smart urban water distribution networks shows that virtual models and intelligent strategies can support emergency management and operational robustness29. However, existing studies often treat energy scheduling, transient protection, and asset management as separate tasks. Based on this gap, the present study introduces an improved NSGA-II algorithm into a digital twin framework, so that energy consumption, water hammer peak pressure, and equipment service life can be evaluated within one constrained multi-objective optimisation structure.

Study area and data collection

Yellow River Diversion Project in Shanxi Province as the engineering case. The station is a large-scale water-transfer facility with strict hydraulic safety requirements and frequent operational adjustments. It has a designed flow rate of 15 m3/s, a rated head of 120 m, six vertical centrifugal pump units, and a variable-frequency regulation system. These characteristics make the station suitable for evaluating multi-objective operation under practical constraints. In this study, the optimization problem is not limited to energy reduction. It also considers water hammer peak pressure and equipment service life, which are closely related to pressure safety and long-term maintenance. Recent studies on smart water systems have emphasized that reliable sensing, structured operational data, and transparent data processing are necessary for data-driven decision support in water infrastructure30.

The data used in this study include three parts: historical operation data, equipment parameter data, and real-time monitoring data. The historical operation data cover the period from 2023 to 2024. They include time-series records of flow rate, head, and power consumption, with a sampling interval of 5 minutes. After screening, approximately 175,000 valid observations were retained. The equipment parameter data were obtained from manufacturer manuals, technical records, and pump station management documents. They include pump characteristic curves, pipeline diameter, pressure limits, motor configuration, and relevant operating ranges. The real-time monitoring data were collected from pressure sensors at key hydraulic nodes, vibration sensors around pump bearings and stators, and flow sensors at the inlet and outlet sections. The transmission frequency of these sensors was 1 Hz. These data sources provide the basis for hydraulic state updating, water hammer peak pressure estimation, and equipment condition tracking.

Before being used for digital twin construction and optimization analysis, the collected data were processed through a unified quality-control procedure. Missing values in continuous monitoring series were filled by linear interpolation when the missing interval was short. Abnormal values were identified using the three-sigma rule. Observations outside the interval of μ ± 3σ were checked against equipment logs before removal or correction, so as to avoid deleting valid values caused by real operational changes. After preprocessing, the cleaned data were stored in a time-series database for model calibration, state updating, and optimization calls. This procedure improves the consistency of model inputs and establishes a clearer connection among physical monitoring, virtual model updating, and multi-objective optimization. It also provides a practical data foundation for the digital twin framework developed in the following sections.

Construction of digital twin model for water supply pump station

The digital twin model developed in this study is designed as a dynamic state representation and predictive support layer for pump station operation. It is not treated as a static simulation module. The model integrates historical operating records, real-time sensor data, equipment parameters, and operational feedback to update the virtual state of the physical pump station. In this study, the digital twin modelling process was implemented in a Python-based computing environment. The historical and real-time data were stored and called through a time-series database, while hydraulic-state correction, water hammer peak pressure estimation, and equipment-condition calculation were completed through modular calculation scripts. This setting allows the digital twin model to update key variables such as flow rate, head, pump frequency, transient pressure response, vibration state, and remaining equipment-life indicators. Recent research has shown that smart water systems increasingly rely on machine-learning-assisted state perception and real-time data interaction to support operational decision-making31.

As shown in Fig. 2, the proposed digital twin framework contains three connected layers: physical entity sensing, virtual model updating, and optimisation-oriented feedback. First, the physical layer collects flow, pressure, vibration, power, and unit operating data from the pump station. Second, the virtual layer uses these data to update the hydraulic state, estimate water hammer peak pressure, and track equipment-condition variation. Third, the updated state variables and constraint information are transmitted to the improved NSGA-II optimisation module. The optimisation module then generates feasible operating schemes, including pump unit combination, frequency adjustment, and auxiliary control parameters. These schemes are returned to the operation layer as decision-support outputs and are used by operators to adjust the physical pump station within safe operating limits. Therefore, the coupling mechanism follows a closed-loop process of “state update → optimisation calculation → decision feedback”. This design clarifies how the digital twin provides real-time inputs for optimisation and how the optimisation results are fed back to pump station operation.Fig. 2Digital twin model of water supply pump station.

The digital twin model was constructed using a Python-based computing environment, and the main calculation scripts were implemented for data preprocessing, hydraulic-state updating, water hammer peak pressure estimation, equipment-condition evaluation, and optimization calls. Historical operation data and real-time monitoring data were stored in a time-series database and were called by the virtual model according to the sampling time and equipment identification number. The Kalman-filter-based state correction, transient-pressure calculation, and equipment-life estimation were implemented as modular calculation procedures. The improved NSGA-II algorithm was programmed in the same environment, so that the updated state variables and constraint information from the digital twin could be directly transferred to the optimization module. This implementation setting supports the closed-loop process of data acquisition, virtual model updating, optimization calculation, and decision feedback.

Multi-objective optimization model coupling energy consumption, water hammer, and lifespan

The optimisation problem in this study is defined as a constrained multi-objective decision task for pump station operation. Three objectives are considered simultaneously: reducing energy consumption, controlling water hammer peak pressure, and improving equipment service life. These objectives are closely related in practical operation. For example, reducing pump frequency or decreasing the number of operating units may lower power consumption, but it may also change flow variation and transient pressure response. Frequent switching or inappropriate frequency adjustment may further increase vibration and fatigue accumulation. Therefore, the optimisation model should not treat the three objectives as independent indicators. It needs to describe how one operating decision affects economy, hydraulic safety, and equipment durability at the same time. Recent studies on booster optimisation in water distribution networks have also shown that pump operation should be analysed under pressure regulation, hydraulic constraints, and practical operating feasibility rather than through a single objective32.

As shown in Fig. 3, the coupled model in this study includes three groups of decision variables: pump operating status, variable-frequency settings, and auxiliary regulation parameters. The auxiliary parameters include blade angle and air-tank-related hydraulic control variables. These variables influence the three objectives through different mechanisms. Energy consumption is mainly affected by flow rate, head, unit efficiency, and operating duration. Water hammer peak pressure is mainly related to flow change, pressure-wave propagation, and surge protection conditions. Equipment service life is associated with vibration response, hydraulic loading, start-stop cycles, and fatigue accumulation. The optimisation process is also restricted by flow demand, pressure safety limits, pump operating ranges, and feasible control-parameter boundaries. Therefore, the coupled model provides a structured basis for the improved NSGA-II algorithm. It allows the algorithm to search for feasible trade-off solutions rather than only pursuing the minimum energy consumption.Fig. 3Coupled model of energy consumption, water hammer, and service life

Improved NSGA-II algorithm design

The improved NSGA-II algorithm is used as the optimization module of the proposed digital twin framework. It is not designed as an independent search procedure. Its input comes from the updated state information provided by the digital twin, including pump operating status, flow rate, head, water hammer peak pressure, vibration indicators, and equipment-condition parameters. Based on these inputs, the algorithm evaluates the three objectives of energy consumption, water hammer peak pressure, and equipment service life under flow, pressure, and operating-range constraints. The output of the algorithm is a set of feasible Pareto solutions for pump station scheduling. These solutions provide candidate operating schemes for unit combination, frequency adjustment, and auxiliary regulation. Recent studies on real-time pump scheduling have shown that optimization performance can be improved when scheduling strategies are connected with updated operating-state information33.

As shown in Fig. 4, the improved NSGA-II algorithm contains three main components: adaptive crossover control, penalty-based constraint handling, and dynamic iteration adjustment. Adaptive crossover is used to maintain population diversity while avoiding excessive random search in later generations. Constraint handling is incorporated into the fitness evaluation process, so that individuals violating flow demand, pressure safety, or equipment operating limits are penalized rather than directly removed. This design helps preserve near-feasible solutions that may still contain useful search information. The dynamic iteration-adjustment parameter is updated according to the error feedback from the digital twin. When the operating state is stable, the search process becomes more conservative. When the state changes clearly, the algorithm increases its response to updated constraints and state variables. Therefore, the coupling process can be described as “state update → objective evaluation → Pareto optimization → decision feedback”. Similar water-system optimization studies also indicate that high-quality Pareto solutions require both search efficiency and constraint feasibility under practical operating conditions34.Fig. 4Design of improved NSGA-II algorithm.

The main methodological feature of this study is the coupling of digital twin state updating with multi-objective optimization. The digital twin provides updated information on flow rate, head, water hammer peak pressure, and equipment condition. The improved NSGA-II algorithm then evaluates candidate operating schemes under energy, pressure, lifespan, and feasibility constraints. This process forms a practical decision-support loop from state update to optimization and feedback. Compared with static optimization, this framework is more suitable for pump station operation under changing hydraulic conditions. However, the results should be interpreted within the scope of the case study. The validation and field trial are based on one pump station, and the lifespan indicator depends on maintenance records and fatigue-based estimation. Future work should extend the validation period, include more pump stations, and further test the framework under seasonal demand changes and abnormal transient conditions.

Conclusion

Core conclusion

This study developed a digital-twin-assisted multi-objective optimization framework for water supply pump station operation. The framework jointly considers energy consumption, water hammer peak pressure, and equipment service life. In the case study of the first-stage pumping station of the South Main Line of the Yellow River Diversion Project in Shanxi Province, the digital twin model provided state updating, hydraulic response estimation, and equipment-condition information for the optimization module. The improved NSGA-II algorithm then generated feasible Pareto solutions under flow, pressure, and operating-range constraints.

The results indicate that the proposed framework can support pump station scheduling with acceptable simulation accuracy and real-time response. Compared with the conventional operation scheme, the selected compromise solution reduced energy consumption and water hammer peak pressure, while improving the equipment service-life indicator. The constraint satisfaction rate also increased to 100%. Compared with the traditional NSGA-II scheme, the improved algorithm showed better convergence, solution diversity, computation time, and feasible-solution ratio. These findings suggest that the coupling of digital twin state updating and multi-objective optimization can provide practical decision support for safer and more economical pump station operation.

Research limitations and future directions

This study still has several limitations. First, the analysis is based on one pump station, so the general applicability of the framework should be verified in more water-transfer and urban water-supply systems. Second, although the validation period was extended in the revised analysis, longer field trials are still needed to cover seasonal demand changes, extreme operating conditions, and abnormal hydraulic events. Third, the equipment service-life indicator was estimated from maintenance records, vibration information, and fatigue-related assumptions. It does not yet fully include corrosion, seal degradation, bearing wear, and other long-term failure mechanisms.

Future research should focus on three aspects. First, the framework should be tested in multiple pump stations with different hydraulic layouts and operating rules. Second, uncertainty-aware optimization should be introduced to consider demand fluctuation, sensor error, electricity price variation, and emergency control conditions. Third, the equipment health model should be refined by integrating more condition-monitoring signals and maintenance records. In addition, the proposed method should be compared with other multi-objective optimization algorithms and real-time control strategies, so that its applicability and limitations can be evaluated more comprehensively in broader water-system applications.
