1.0 ACCOMPLISHMENTS

 a. What are the major goals and objectives of this project?
 The research hypothesis of this proposal was that mesoporous chromatography materials that
 utilize physically absorbed ligands can be replaced by macroporous, selective membrane
 adsorbers that contain covalently bound polymeric ligands, in order to (1) improve adsorbent
 stability and (2) eliminate diffusion limitations in the system. Eliminating diffusion limitations in
 chromatography has the benefits of (1) reducing processing from hours to minutes, (2) reducing
 band broadening thereby increasing resolution and (3) reducing elution volumes. Doing so will
 contribute to a more selective and efficient separation—alleviating a bottleneck associated with
 medical isotope purification and progressing more radiotherapies to market. This project
 emphasized membrane characterization and performance in both batch and dynamic (flow-
 through) modes. The following goals were designed to test the research hypothesis:

 Goal 1: Synthesize a new class of radiochromatography membranes by electrospinning and
 covalently grafting ligands to the membrane surface and pores. Here, we will introduce a new
 class of membrane adsorbers for radiochemical separations. The physical properties of the
 membranes (porosity, pore volume, surface area) will be characterized to provide insights to
 the effect of adsorbent structure on performance. Functional groups will be introduced through
 covalently bound single or polymeric ligands.

 Goal 2: Evaluate the impact of membrane architecture and ligand chemistry on the
 performance of the membrane adsorber in both batch and continuous operation. Here, we
 perform equilibrium and kinetic binding experiments to characterize the binding properties
 (capacity, binding rate, selectivity). Dynamic (flow-through) column experiments will give insight
 to the effect of membrane architecture on chromatographic resolution and degree of
 separation. This will result in structure-property relationships between membrane design
 (chemistry and architecture) and performance. Multiple load/elution cycles will be performed to
 assess the stability of the materials.

 Goal 3: Benchmark performance against existing resin protocols for 225 Ac. Synthesized
 membranes will be used in dynamic binding experiments to benchmark them against resins
 used in the literature. This portion of the work will assess purification time and product purity in
 the context of purifying 225Ac, a promising TAT isotope.

 b. What was accomplished under these goals?
 In pursuit of goal 1, our team investigated two methods for creating functional membranes with
 tailorable surface chemistry. In the first part of the work, we explored UV-induced free radical
 polymerization from commercially available polyethersulfone membranes. While pursuing this
 approach, we discovered that obtaining direct evidence of covalent bond formation between the
 grafted polymer and the base membrane was challenging. Furthermore, direct evidence of the
 proposed mechanism for UV-induced free radical polymerization had never been shown in the
 literature. Our 2025 publication in the Journal of Membrane Science1 details the potential
 pitfalls of using this technique and provides, for the first time, the direct evidence required to
 show covalent bond formation in UV-induced grafting using 2D NMR techniques. This
 technique was deemed not suitable for this project due to the ambiguity surrounding covalent
 bond formation. Concurrently, our team designed and constructed an electrospinning apparatus
 to synthesize electrospun poly(vinylbenezyl chloride) polymer fibers. After electrospinning,
 membranes were crosslinked to increase their chemical stability in organic solvents and nitric
 acid. The synthesis and characterization of these materials (surface area, porosity, fiber
 diameter) are detailed in our 2023 publication in Membranes.2 The main accomplishment from

 2

this portion of the work was developing techniques and protocols to synthesize acid-stable
membranes with tailorable surface chemistry in large quantities.

The electrospun membranes were later functionalized using an amine-terminated tetrahexyl
diglycolamide (THDGA) ligand. Initially, two types of ligands were synthesized (linear and
branched); however, only one was covalently tethered to the electrospun membranes. Ligand
synthesis, membrane functionalization, and chemical characterization are detailed in our 2025
manuscript which is under review with ACS Applied Materials & Interfaces. The main
accomplishment that pertains to goal 1 is the successful synthesis of a THDGA functionalized
membrane.

In pursuit of goal 2, batch equilibrium and kinetic adsorption experiments were performed using
lanthanum (a non-radioactive surrogate of Ac-225) and Ac-225. The maximum capacity,
distribution coefficient, and selectivity were benchmarked against the state-of-the-art DGA
normal (linear) resins purchase from Eichrom technologies in 4 M, 6 M, and 10 M nitric acid.
Additionally, elution experiments were performed to strip Ac-225 from THDGA membrane
adsorbers using 10 M nitric acid. The results of this work are summarized in our manuscript
which is under review with ACS Applied Materials & Interfaces. The main conclusions of that
work include: 1) THDGA membrane adsorbers have lower capacity than the commercial
extractive resins, 2) THDGA membrane adsorbers follow the same trends in affinity as the
extractive resins, 3) THDGA membrane adsorbers have higher predicted selectivity for La/Ac-
225 than the commercial extractive resins in 10 M nitric acid, and 4) Ac-225 can be stripped
from the THDGA membranes 5x faster than the extractive resins. Faster elution times leads to
smaller elution volumes and can decrease processing steps downstream.

the column at different time intervals until the outlet concentration matched the feed

Additionally, a model was constructed for commercial DGA resins. Since the mass transport in
a resin column like DGA resins have additional diffusion resistance due to its meso-scale
particle pore sizes, modeling the breakthrough profiles is more complex as compared to
membrane adsorbers.6–8 Mass transfer in resins follows this process: 1) bulk diffusion or
convection from solution, 2) external diffusion through the liquid film around the resin particle or
boundary layer diffusion (film diffusion, kf) 3) intraparticle diffusion (surface diffusion, Ds, pore
diffusion, Dp) and 4) adsorbate-ligand complexation (Langmuir kinetics). The kinetics of resins
at 4, 6 and 10 M nitric acid were first obtained by performing time-resolved batch experiments
using La. To obtain rate constants, the kinetic Langmuir equation was applied to the
experimental data and the output parameters obtained through the curve fitting were the
adsorption rate constant (kads) and the desorption rate constant (kdes). Since these rate
constants are “lumped” rate constants, i.e., they depend on the transport resistances. Several
articles have explored deriving these resistances through phenomenological derivations9,10.
Therefore, batch and kinetic experimental data were used to obtain these transport resistances
by solving them systematically. Chromatography Analysis and Design Toolkit (CADET) is an
open-source chromatography modeling and simulation framework that was used to capture
resin breakthrough curves.11 The appropriate rate model chosen for the simulation was the
Generic Rate Model (GRM) with Langmuir kinetics as the binding model. Inputs to the model
include: inlet (feed) concentration, interstitial velocity, column length, column diameter, particle
radius, bed porosity, particle porosity, bed density, axial dispersion coefficient (obtained

 4

through empirical formula using interstitial velocity and particle physical properties), processing
time for loading and eluted, maximum adsorption capacity of the resin, adsorption rate (fitted
parameter obtained through Langmuir kinetics on MATLAB), desorption rate (fitted parameter
obtained through Langmuir kinetics on MATLAB), film diffusion coefficient, surface diffusion
coefficient, and pore diffusion coefficient.

The outputs obtained for the resin model are like the outputs obtained from the membrane
adsorber model where the normalized concentration (Ceffluent/Co) is obtained versus time as
shown in Figure 2.

Figure 2. Predicting breakthrough behavior of TODGA resins where the red line represents the
CADET model and the blue data points represent the dynamic experimental breakthrough data
for La adsorption on TODGA resin at 4 M nitric acid at a flow rate of 5 mL/min.

The prediction of the breakthrough time is accurate, however, the model diverges from the data
above between 10 – 100 % breakthrough of the column. One explanation for this could be the
error associated with calculating the diffusion resistances of the TODGA resins. Diffusion
resistances are calculated from experimentally collected kinetic adsorption data. TODGA resins
have fast kinetics and may result in some experimental error which propogates through to the
mass transfer coefficients. Although the prediction of breakthrough curve is satisfactory,
sensitivity analysis is required to accurately capture the kinetics and get accurate physical
properties of the column. Future work will focus on performing sensitivity analysis of the
experimental data and obtaining correct information of the physical properties of the resins as
some of the parameters (for example, the particle porosity) are assumed.

In pursuit of goal 3, dynamic adsorption experiments at Brookhaven National Laboratory were
performed using Ac-225. Membranes were prepared in the method described below where the
coupons were placed on custom 3D-printed membrane housings. The dynamic flow-through set
up consists of a feed solution containing Ac-225 in 4 M nitric acid. The feed solution was
passed through the membrane housing using a peristaltic pump and the effluent fractions were
collected. For desorption experiments, 10 M nitric acid was used as the eluent. An additional 4
M nitric acid with no activity was performed to check residual activities in housings and tubes as
a washing step. The effluent samples were diluted with DI water and analyzed using gamma
spectroscopy (HPGe) to quantify the Ac-225 activity in effluent fractions.

 5

A custom-built 3D printed membrane housing was prepared using Form4 3D printing equipment
(Formlabs, Somerville, MA) using Rigid 10K 3D-printing resin. The membrane housings were
designed through PreForm 3D printing software. Here, electrospun PVBC fibers and THDGA
membranes were die-cut using a 24 mm die-punch and the filter paper was removed carefully.
The freshly cut membrane coupons were carefully weighed using a weigh balance and the
mass of the membranes were recorded prior to the dynamic experiments. Three different trials
were conducted for the PVBC control experiments to test if there is any adsorption of Ac-225
and all three trials indicated no adsorption.

PVBC fibers were tested for Ac-225 dynamic adsorption as a control data to see if Ac-225 will
bind to the fibers. This experiment was performed at 0.5 mL/min, and three replicates were
studied. The fibers were subjected to a 5 μCi/mL Ac-225 in 4 M nitric acid feed, and the effluent
fractions were collected (10 mL fraction for each replicate). In all the 3 replicates, the activities
did not change from the loading solution and remained around 5 μCi/mL in all the fractions,
confirming that Ac-225 has negligible adsorption on PVBC membranes.

For THDGA membranes, a first trial experiment was conducted to check the breakthrough and
elution behavior, which is summarized in Figure 3A. We noticed an instant breakthrough of Ac-
225 and very minimal Ac-225 adsorption for the THDGA membranes. An additional 4 M nitric
acid washing step was included and residual activities in the membrane housing were detected.
However, Ac-225 was still desorbing from the membrane housing set-up upon switching to 10
M nitric acid desorption step. These results indicate that residual activities are indeed present in
the current membrane housing design and that dynamic Ac-225 adsorption is very minimal.

Figure 3. A) Dynamic adsorption and desorption of Ac-225 on THDGA membranes at a
flowrate of 0.5 mL/min. The load solution was 1 μCi/mL of Ac-225 in 4 M nitric acid. The
washing step was 4 M nitric acid. The elution step was 10 M nitric acid. B) Dynamic adsorption
and desorption of Ac-225 on THDGA membranes at a flowrate of 0.2 mL/min. The load solution
was ~4.5 μCi/mL of Ac-225 in 4M nitric acid. The washing step was 4 M nitric acid. The elution
step was 10 M nitric acid. In this test, the loading curve was not collected to ensure feed
equilibration with THDGA membrane to ensure adequate Ac-225 adsorption.

According to the approved experimental safety review (ESR), a complete batch adsorption of
Ac-225 on THDGA membranes were not prescribed and therefore could not be performed at

 6

the time. Instead, the final modified experiment (Figure 3B) included a slower flow rate of 0.2
mL/min where the feed of Ac-225 activity was increased to ~4.5 μCi/mL and introduced to the
membrane housing. After a few droplets were collected on the effluent side, the pump was
stopped for about 3 hours to ensure equilibration. Following this process, the feed solution was
flushed through at the same flow rate and 4 M nitric acid washing step was included as well as
the 10 M nitric acid desorption step. Indeed, the 4 M nitric acid washing step indicated residual
activity, but there was a sudden increase in Ac-225 activity in the first effluent fraction was
observed when switching to 10 M elution step. These results indicate that a proper equilibration
of the Ac-225 feed solution is needed to ensure adequate adsorption to occur. Future studies
will focus on determining the kinetics of Ac-225 adsorption on THDGA membranes that can
give insights on time required for complete saturation of the membrane. At present, La(III)
surrogates will be used to study kinetics at CWRU.

5. IMPACT

What is the impact of the project? How has it contributed?

  a. What was the impact on the development of the principal discipline(s) of the project?
  This project developed one of the first membrane adsorbers for use in isotope production. This
  first proof-of-concept suggests that membrane adsorbers have the potential to reduce
  purification times by a factor of 5 while minimizing elution volumes. Overall, this approach can
  help streamline isotope purification. Furthermore, the THDGA chemistry developed can be
  leveraged to purify other radiolanthanides that are emerging in pre-clinical trials. Additionally,
  this project highlights the importance of designing ligands with rapid kinetics which can further
  improve future separation processes by enabling rapid purification on solid-phase supports.

  b. What was the impact on other disciplines?
  The materials, ligand chemistries, and insights generated in this project are highly transferrable
  to other separation processes. In particular, the adsorptive separation of metals from acidic
  solutions is the leading technology for lithium purification from brines and plays an important
  role in purifying rare earth elements.

9. PROJECT OUTCOMES

Ac-225 is a promising isotope for targeted alpha therapy (TAT, a personalized cancer treatment)
that has shown excellent patient outcomes by avoiding whole body doses of radiation while
treating otherwise pervasive cancers. Until recently, all 225Ac used in domestic clinical trials was
harvested from legacy nuclear waste at Oak Ridge National Laboratory. This legacy supply
of 225Ac is so limited that only 1 in 5,500 people who qualified for the treatment were able to
receive it. Producing clinically relevant quantities of 225Ac requires 1) new production routes in
accelerators or cyclotrons and 2) streamlined purification processes. Resin-based extractive
chromatography has long been the workhorse for medical isotope purification. Regardless of the
source of Ac-225, multistep purification processes are required to isolate the isotope and prepare
it for conjugation with the targeting agent. While these separation materials perform well in bench-
scale purification schemes—they present challenges when scaling up for clinically relevant
production in hot cells. The overarching goal of this research was to design, synthesize, and test
new membrane adsorbers that can replace extractive resins in isotope purification schemes.
Membrane adsorbers are highly porous separation materials in which product capture is
independent of flowrate. Unlike their resin counterparts, this unique property enables them to be
used at higher flowrates—reducing separation time and volumes which increases process
efficiency.

In this work, we synthesized chemically stable membrane adsorbers with covalently attached
amine-terminated diglycolamide ligands. These robust materials were benchmarked against the
state-of-the-art extractive resins using both stable lanthanum and radioactive Ac-225 in process
relevant conditions. Key outcomes of this work include: 1) membrane adsorbers have 10x lower
capacity than the resins, 2) membrane adsorbers show the same trends in metal-ion affinity as the
resins, 3) the membrane-adsorbers may be kinetically limited during adsorption, 4) membrane
adsorbers have fast desorption/elution times in 10 M nitric acid, and 5) the membrane adsorbers
have higher selectivity than their resin-based counterparts. This research project was important to
identify the potential and limitations of this new approach for isotope purification. Future work will
focus on improving surface engineering to increase the adsorption kinetics and increase material
stability. Doing so will unleash the full potential of membrane adsorbers in this application area.
Additionally, the computational models that were constructed for both membrane adsorbers and
extractive resins can be used to identify new pathways for improvement.
