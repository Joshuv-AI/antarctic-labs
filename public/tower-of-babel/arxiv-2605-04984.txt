Self-Induced Outcome Potential: Turn-Level Credit Assignment for Agents without Verifiers
Senkang Hu, Yong Dai, Xudong Han, Zhengru Fang, Yuzhi Zhao, Sam Tak Wu Kwong, Yuguang Fang

Abstract
Long-horizon LLM agents succeed or fail through intermediate information-gathering turns, yet training signals are usually observed only at the final answer, because process-level rewards require extensive high-quality human annotation. Existing turn-level shaping methods address this supervision mismatch by rewarding turns that increase the likelihood of a gold answer, but they require answer supervision or stable task-specific verifiers that are often unavailable in agentic settings. Conversely, recent label-free reinforcement learning (RL) methods extract useful self-signals from output distributions, but mostly at the answer or trajectory level and therefore cannot attribute value to intermediate turns. To address these issues, we propose Self-Induced Outcome Potential (SIOP), a framework that treats semantic clusters of final answers as latent future outcome states for potential-based turn-level credit assignment. For each query, we sample multiple rollouts, cluster their final answers into semantic outcome modes, and construct a reliability-aware target distribution over these latent states. We first formulate a distributional potential that rewards turns for increasing posterior support for reliable future outcome states, and then instantiate it with a tractable cluster-level approximation. Terminal outcome mass is added only at the final answer turn and propagated backward through turn-level advantages. The resulting objective generalizes information-potential shaping from gold-answer supervision to settings without task-specific gold verifiers while avoiding the broadcasted rollout-level advantages used by standard GRPO. We formalize the framework, establish its reduction to supervised turn-level shaping in the gold-answer limit, and show that SIOP consistently improves over verifier-free outcome-level baselines on seven search-augmented agentic reasoning benchmarks while approaching the average performance of a gold-supervised outcome baseline. Code is available at https://github.com/dl-m9/SIOP.git .



1
Introduction

Recent reasoning models such as OpenAI o1
[
29
]
and DeepSeek-R1
[
10
]
have made a broader shift visible: large language models (LLMs) are increasingly trained to solve problems through extended reasoning rather than immediate answer generation.
Agentic settings extend this paradigm from internal reasoning traces to external interaction. Instead of answering from a fixed prompt, an agent reasons over a partial state, retrieves evidence, calls tools, observes returned information, and decides whether further interaction is needed. This turns model behavior from static generation into sequential decision making over reasoning, tool use, and external observations.

The shift is visible across recent systems and training recipes. Agentic retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems surveyed by Li
et al.

[
23
]
frame retrieval as adaptive interaction rather than static preprocessing. Kimi K2
[
19
]
uses large-scale agentic data synthesis and joint reinforcement learning through real and synthetic environments. ReSearch
[
4
]
and Search-R1
[
16
]
train search-augmented models to interleave reasoning and retrieval. ToolRL
[
32
]
and SimpleTIR
[
42
]
study tool-use RL and tool-integrated reasoning under multi-turn interaction. These works show that agentic RL has become a practical path for improving interactive reasoning.

However, many of these training recipes still rely on outcome-level feedback: the policy receives rewards mainly from final-answer correctness, task success, or terminal score after the full trajectory is complete. Such outcome-only reinforcement learning is not well suited to interactive agents. In long trajectories, a single terminal reward cannot reliably identify whether a specific thought, tool call, or observation improved the agent’s future prospects. For example, ReasonRAG
[
51
]
and InForage
[
33
]
report that outcome-supervised agentic RL suffers from low exploration efficiency, gradient conflict, and sparse rewards. These studies indicate that terminal outcome supervision is not enough to guide the agent’s long-horizon behavior.

To address this problem, one line of work proposes turn-level reward shaping. For example, methods such as IGPO
[
37
]
,
\Delta
Belief-RL
[
2
]
, TIPS
[
39
]
, and InfoPO
[
20
]
measure whether an interaction turn increases the model’s support for a desired target, such as the gold answer or the correct next action. These methods show that information-potential shaping can substantially improve multi-turn optimization.
However, these methods inherit a strong assumption: a rewardable target must already be available. In many agentic tasks, there is no unique gold answer or stable task-specific verifier against which a turn-level potential can be computed.

Another line of work removes this requirement by extracting training signals directly from model outputs. EMPO
[
50
]
clusters semantically equivalent responses and optimizes semantic entropy without external supervision, while TTRL
[
56
]
performs test-time reinforcement learning by selecting the majority answer from repeated samples as a self-generated pseudo-target. Related verifier-free RL methods, including RLPR
[
46
]
, NOVER
[
27
]
, and DRO
[
41
]
, similarly show that model-internal confidence or answer distributions can support learning beyond tasks with explicit verifiers. Yet these methods remain mostly outcome-level: they tell the model which final responses appear more reliable, but not which intermediate turns actually moved the trajectory toward those outcomes.

Building on these gaps, this paper studies how to lift unlabeled outcome-level reliability signals into turn-level credit assignment. Our key abstraction is to cluster sampled final answers into
semantic outcome modes
, where each mode is a query-specific group of answers that express the same underlying meaning, and then treat these modes as
latent future states
. Even without gold answers or stable task-specific verifiers, a batch of rollouts for the same query induces a structured distribution over these latent states. Some modes are frequent, consistent, evidence-supported, or otherwise more trustworthy than others, while others reflect low-consensus noise and should be down-weighted. We then define a potential function over this self-induced latent state space and reward each turn by how much it increases the model’s support for reliable future outcome states. The resulting
self-induced outcome potential
generalizes information-potential shaping from gold-answer supervision to verifier-free settings without task-specific gold verifiers.

Concretely, we sample multiple full rollouts for each query, cluster their final answers into semantic outcome modes, and assign soft reliability weights to these modes. This yields a distributional target over reliable latent futures rather than a hard pseudo-label. Because estimating the full mode distribution at every turn is expensive, we instantiate this principle in practice with a multi-reference cluster-level approximation that conditions each rollout on its realized semantic outcome mode while scoring a small set of reference answers rather than a single prototype. Terminal outcome mass is added only at the final answer turn and propagated backward through turn-level advantages, so different turns of the same rollout receive different credit. This design preserves the key intuition behind potential-based shaping, namely rewarding the intermediate interactions that move the trajectory toward reliable future states, while avoiding the assumption that a gold answer is known during training.
Our contributions are summarized as follows.

1.

We formulate
verifier-free turn-level credit assignment
as potential-based learning over self-induced semantic outcome modes, where clustered final answers serve as latent future states rather than fixed gold targets or trajectory-level pseudo-labels.

2.

We define a
self-induced outcome potential
(SIOP) over these latent states, calibrating empirical outcome modes by reliability signals and converting prefix-to-prefix changes in cluster support into turn-level process rewards.

3.

We construct turn-specific advantages for long-horizon agents, assigning credit only to trainable tokens within each turn rather than broadcasting a trajectory-level score.

4.

We provide theoretical characterizations showing how the proposed reward reduces to gold-answer shaping in the supervised limit and why it measures step-wise marginal support gain rather than broadcasting a trajectory score. We also present an empirical study on seven search-QA benchmarks showing that our method outperforms outcome-only verifier-free baselines and approaches a gold-supervised outcome RL baseline.

2
Problem Setup

We study an agentic setting where an agent receives a query
q
and interacts with an environment over multiple turns before producing a final answer. At turn
t
, the agent emits reasoning tokens, optionally issues a tool call, and receives an observation. We treat this as a single interaction segment

s_{t}=(\text{thought}_{t},\text{tool}_{t},\text{obs}_{t}),

(1)

so that a complete rollout can be written directly as

\tau=(q,s_{1},\dots,s_{T},y).

(2)

For convenience, we write
\tau_{\leq t}=(q,s_{1},\dots,s_{t})
for the rollout truncated after turn
t
.
For each query, we sample
K
rollouts from the current policy
\pi_{\theta}
:

\tau^{(k)}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q),\qquad k=1,\dots,K,

(3)

where the
k
-th rollout can be written as

\tau^{(k)}=(q,s_{1}^{(k)},\dots,s_{T_{k}}^{(k)},y^{(k)}).

(4)

Supervision mismatch.

Supervised turn-level shaping methods such as
\Delta
Belief-RL
[
2
]
, IGPO
[
37
]
, and TIPS
[
39
]
assume access to a gold answer
y^{\star}
or an equivalent target representation. In our setting, training does not observe a unique correct answer, a stable answer set, or a task-specific correctness verifier. The task-specific information available during learning consists of sampled rollouts from the current policy.

Verifier-free terminology.

We use
verifier-free
in the task-specific sense: training requires no gold answers, task-specific correctness verifiers, or hand-designed rewards. An off-the-shelf natural language inference (NLI) model is used only as a task-agnostic semantic primitive for clustering and evidence scoring, without access to benchmark labels or task-specific answer checkers.

Semantic outcome modes.

Let
\mathcal{Y}_{q}=\{y^{(1)},\dots,y^{(K)}\}
denote the set of answers sampled for query
q
. Following semantic entropy by Kuhn et al.
[
21
]
, we cluster these answers by bidirectional semantic entailment under the query context. Writing
y\Rightarrow y^{\prime}
for “
y
semantically entails
y^{\prime}
given
q
,” as judged by an NLI model applied to the query-contextualized pair, we assign
y
and
y^{\prime}
to the same cluster iff
y\Rightarrow y^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}\Rightarrow y
. Applying this criterion to
\mathcal{Y}_{q}
yields a partition

\mathcal{C}(q)=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{M}\},

(5)

where each cluster
c_{m}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{q}
collects answers with the same meaning. We call each cluster a
semantic outcome mode
. We write
c(k)\in\mathcal{C}(q)
for the cluster containing the final answer
y^{(k)}
of rollout
k
.

Cluster statistics.

For each cluster
c\in\mathcal{C}(q)
, let

S_{c}=\{k\in\{1,\dots,K\}\mid c(k)=c\}

(6)

denote the set of sampled rollouts whose final answers fall into that semantic outcome mode. The corresponding empirical cluster mass is

m(c\mid q)=\frac{|S_{c}|}{K}.

(7)

We use
q_{\theta}(c\mid q)
to denote the training target distribution over semantic outcome modes. Section
3
instantiates this distribution by calibrating the empirical cluster mass with reliability signals.

Goal.

Our objective is to construct a turn-level reward
r^{(k)}_{t,\mathrm{proc}}
for each segment
s_{t}^{(k)}
and convert these rewards into turn-specific learning signals for policy optimization. The next section defines the self-induced target distribution, outcome potential, and policy update used to compute these quantities.

3
Methodology

SIOP starts from a credit-assignment question: without a gold answer or task-specific verifier, what future state should an intermediate turn be rewarded for moving toward? We answer this by using semantic outcome modes as the future-state space for potential-based shaping. Figure
1
shows the resulting pipeline. Appendix
B
summarizes the notation used throughout this section.

Figure 1
:
Overview of Self-Induced Outcome Potential (SIOP). For each query, the policy samples multiple agent rollouts, clusters their final answers into semantic outcome modes, and calibrates these modes into a reliability-weighted target distribution. SIOP then evaluates each prefix by its outcome potential, assigns turn rewards from potential differences, adds terminal outcome mass at the final answer turn, and updates the policy with turn-level advantages.

3.1
Self-Induced Outcome Distribution

Semantic outcome modes.

Multiple rollouts for the same query often produce answers that differ in surface form but express the same semantic hypothesis. Treating each string as a separate target makes the reward sensitive to wording, while selecting only the majority answer collapses uncertainty too early. We therefore group final answers into semantic outcome modes and treat each mode as a latent future state.

Let
S_{c}=\{k\mid c(k)=c\}
denote the rollout subset assigned to cluster
c
. We begin with the empirical semantic outcome distribution

m(c\mid q)=\frac{|S_{c}|}{K},

(8)

and let
R(c)\subseteq c
denote a small reference set selected from cluster
c
, with nonnegative weights
\{\alpha_{y}^{(c)}\}_{y\in R(c)}
satisfying

\sum_{y\in R(c)}\alpha_{y}^{(c)}=1.

(9)

These reference answers provide a tractable semantic proxy for the full cluster while avoiding commitment to a single canonical wording.

Reliability calibration.

Empirical mode frequency is useful, but it is not a correctness guarantee. A wrong answer can become the majority when the base policy has a consistent bias or when retrieval returns misleading evidence. SIOP therefore does not directly optimize raw cluster frequency. It reweights the rollout-induced distribution using reliability evidence, so a frequent mode receives strong target mass only when it is also supported by the available semantic or evidential signal.

We then calibrate the empirical distribution with a reliability correction

u_{\theta}(c,q)=\eta\,r(c,q),

(10)

where
\eta\geq 0
controls the correction strength and
r(c,q)
is a domain-appropriate reliability signal. The framework only requires that larger
r(c,q)
indicate more reliable outcome modes.

For tool-using agents, we instantiate
r
as
evidence support
, namely the average degree to which tool observations along trajectories support the cluster’s reference set:

r(c,q)=\frac{1}{|S_{c}|}\sum_{k\in S_{c}}\frac{1}{T_{k}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{k}}\sum_{y\in R(c)}\alpha_{y}^{(c)}s_{\mathrm{NLI}}\!\bigl(\mathrm{obs}_{t}^{(k)},\,y\bigr),

(11)

where
s_{\mathrm{NLI}}(\mathrm{obs},y)
is the entailment confidence from an NLI model.
In reasoning-only settings without tool observations,
r(c,q)
can instead be instantiated with cluster-level self-consistency or mean log-confidence over the same reference set.

The resulting self-induced target distribution is

q_{\theta}(c\mid q)=\frac{m(c\mid q)\exp(u_{\theta}(c,q))}{\sum_{c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(q)}m(c^{\prime}\mid q)\exp(u_{\theta}(c^{\prime},q))}.

(12)

If
u_{\theta}(c,q)=0
for all clusters, this reduces to the empirical semantic outcome distribution induced by the sampled rollouts. Hence the self-induced target distribution can be viewed as the model’s own semantic answer distribution, reweighted by reliability signals before it is used for turn-level shaping.

3.2
Semantic Outcome Potential for Turn-Level Rewards

Multi-reference cluster support.

After forming reliable outcome modes, turn credit requires a prefix-conditioned support score for each mode. Because agentic answer spaces make exact mode probabilities impractical, we approximate mode support using a small reference set selected from the cluster.

The framework evaluates a turn against the distribution of reliable future outcome modes. For a truncated rollout
\tau_{\leq t}
, the true posterior mass on semantic outcome mode
c
is

p_{\theta}(c\mid\tau_{\leq t})=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(q)}p_{\theta}(y\mid\tau_{\leq t})\,\mathbf{1}[y\in c].

(13)

Because summing over the full answer space is intractable, we instantiate a tractable multi-reference surrogate for cluster support:

\tilde{p}_{\theta}(c\mid\tau_{\leq t})=\sum_{y\in R(c)}\alpha_{y}^{(c)}p_{\theta}(y\mid\tau_{\leq t}),

(14)

which scores a small set of reference answers rather than the full cluster. When
R(c)
contains a single answer, this recovers the earlier single-prototype approximation as a special case. With multiple references, however, the reward is less sensitive to any one wording choice and better reflects support for the semantic mode as a set.

Potential-based turn reward.

Once reliable semantic modes and prefix-conditioned support are defined, SIOP assigns turn-level credit by measuring changes in a potential over the rollout’s realized semantic outcome mode
c(k)
. A turn receives positive credit when it increases prefix support for this mode and negative credit when it decreases that support. The
outcome potential
at turn
t
is

\Phi_{\theta}(\tau_{\leq t}^{(k)},c(k))=q_{\theta}(c(k)\mid q)\,\log\bigl(\tilde{p}_{\theta}(c(k)\mid\tau_{\leq t}^{(k)})\bigr),

(15)

The potential combines a reliability-calibrated target mass with prefix-conditioned support. The target mass
q_{\theta}(c(k)\mid q)
reflects the rollout frequency of the realized cluster after calibration by evidence support, while the log-support term measures how strongly the current prefix predicts that cluster. Thus potential differences are largest when a turn increases support for a cluster with high calibrated target mass.
The step-level process reward is:

\displaystyle r^{(k)}_{t,\mathrm{proc}}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\theta}(\tau_{\leq t}^{(k)},c(k))-\Phi_{\theta}(\tau_{\leq t-1}^{(k)},c(k))

(16)

\displaystyle=q_{\theta}(c(k)\mid q)\Bigl[\log\bigl(\tilde{p}_{\theta}(c(k)\mid\tau_{\leq t}^{(k)})\bigr)-\log\bigl(\tilde{p}_{\theta}(c(k)\mid\tau_{\leq t-1}^{(k)})\bigr)\Bigr].

This reward is positive when turn
t
increases conditional support for the rollout’s semantic outcome mode, and negative when it moves the trajectory away from that mode. All rollouts assigned to the same cluster share the same semantic reference set
R(c)
, so the reward operates at the cluster level rather than the individual answer level.

Proposition 1

(Telescoping of the outcome potential)
.

For any rollout
\tau^{(k)}
, let
\tau_{\leq 0}^{(k)}
denote the initial query state before agent-generated turns. The cumulative process reward satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T_{k}}r^{(k)}_{t,\mathrm{proc}}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\theta}(\tau_{\leq T_{k}}^{(k)},c(k))-\Phi_{\theta}(\tau_{\leq 0}^{(k)},c(k))

(17)

\displaystyle=q_{\theta}(c(k)\mid q)\Bigl[\log\bigl(\tilde{p}_{\theta}(c(k)\mid\tau_{\leq T_{k}}^{(k)})\bigr)-\log\bigl(\tilde{p}_{\theta}(c(k)\mid q)\bigr)\Bigr].

Hence the process reward is potential-based: cumulative credit depends only on the change in outcome potential between the initial query state and the final trajectory state.

This telescoping form makes the process reward genuinely step-wise. The target distribution
q_{\theta}(c(k)\mid q)
determines which outcome modes receive calibrated target mass, while the potential difference determines which turns increased conditional support for the cluster reference set.

Proposition 2

(Factorization of outcome-potential shaping)
.

The per-turn SIOP reward induced by
\Phi_{\theta}
factors as

r^{(k)}_{t,\mathrm{proc}}=\underbrace{q_{\theta}(c(k)\mid q)}_{\text{calibrated target mass}}\cdot\underbrace{\Bigl[\log\!\bigl(\tilde{p}_{\theta}(c(k)\mid\tau_{\leq t}^{(k)})\bigr)-\log\!\bigl(\tilde{p}_{\theta}(c(k)\mid\tau_{\leq t-1}^{(k)})\bigr)\Bigr]}_{\text{cluster-level support gain}},

(18)

i.e., cluster-level support shaping weighted by the calibrated target mass of that cluster. In the supervised limit where
q_{\theta}(c^{\star}\mid q)=1
,
R(c^{\star})=\{y^{\star}\}
, and
\alpha_{y^{\star}}^{(c^{\star})}=1
, the reward recovers standard gold-answer support shaping:

r^{(k)}_{t,\mathrm{proc}}=\log p_{\theta}(y^{\star}\mid\tau_{\leq t}^{(k)})-\log p_{\theta}(y^{\star}\mid\tau_{\leq t-1}^{(k)}).

(19)

Proposition
2
reframes SIOP as calibrated target-mass weighting of support shaping:
q_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q)
is a query-specific target distribution over semantic modes, while the log-difference term measures turn-wise cluster support gain. Gold-answer shaping is recovered when the target distribution concentrates on the gold cluster. Diffuse or low-mass target distributions attenuate credit. Appendix
D.3
gives a Bayesian reading of the calibration step and proves when reliability evidence can reverse a spurious majority.

3.3
Turn-Level Advantage Construction

Process rewards identify which turns change semantic support, but global answer quality is still observed at the trajectory endpoint. We encode these two signals in an augmented immediate reward: process credit is assigned at every turn, while terminal outcome mass is added only at the final answer turn. For rollout
k
, this reward is

\bar{r}_{t}^{(k)}=\lambda r^{(k)}_{t,\mathrm{proc}}+\mathbf{1}[t=T_{k}](1-\lambda)r_{\mathrm{final}}^{(k)},

(20)

where
r_{\mathrm{final}}^{(k)}=q_{\theta}(c(k)\mid q)
is the target-distribution weight of the rollout’s semantic outcome mode. This design gives the final answer turn a direct outcome signal while allowing earlier turns to benefit only through return propagation.

For each query group (the
K
rollouts of one prompt), we pool all augmented rewards across its rollouts and turns and compute group-normalized immediate rewards using the group’s own statistics:

\tilde{r}_{t}^{(k)}=\frac{\bar{r}_{t}^{(k)}-\mu_{g}}{\sigma_{g}},\quad\text{where}\ \mu_{g}=\frac{1}{N_{g}}\sum_{k,t}\bar{r}_{t}^{(k)}\ \text{and}\ \sigma_{g}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{g}}\sum_{k,t}\bigl(\bar{r}_{t}^{(k)}-\mu_{g}\bigr)^{2}},

(21)

with
N_{g}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}T_{k}
, the total number of (rollout, turn) pairs in that query group. Normalization is computed per query group (GRPO-style) rather than batch-globally, so different queries do not interfere with each other’s turn scales. Pooling all turns within a group also means the
z
-score does not artificially rescale each turn index to the same variance: turns with systematically larger rewards retain their relative magnitudes within the group.

To propagate this terminal outcome signal back to earlier turns, we define the turn advantage as a discounted return over normalized immediate rewards:

A_{t}^{(k)}=\sum_{u=t}^{T_{k}}\gamma^{u-t}\tilde{r}_{u}^{(k)}.

(22)

This construction resolves the core mismatch with broadcasted rollout-level advantages: the final reward is not copied to all tokens, but only affects earlier turns through the discounted return of turn-local normalized rewards.

3.4
Turn-Conditioned Policy Optimization

To use turn-level advantages in a token-level clipped objective, we first identify the policy tokens generated by each interaction turn.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{t}^{(k)}
denote the set of trainable policy tokens generated within turn
t
of rollout
k
, excluding prompts, previous context replay, tool observations, and other non-policy text. We assign the same turn advantage
A_{t}^{(k)}
to all trainable tokens in
\mathcal{T}_{t}^{(k)}
, but not across different turns.

With token-level importance ratio
\rho_{t,j}^{(k)}
for token
j\in\mathcal{T}_{t}^{(k)}
, we minimize a GRPO-style clipped objective:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SIOP}}=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\!\Bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{k}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{T}_{t}^{(k)}}\min\!\Big(\rho_{t,j}^{(k)}A_{t}^{(k)},\mathrm{clip}(\rho_{t,j}^{(k)},1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}},1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}})A_{t}^{(k)}\Big)\Bigg]

(23)

\displaystyle+\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}).

We refer to this objective as the
SIOP objective
. It preserves the main appeal of group-based RL, namely critic-free optimization with normalized on-policy signals, while ensuring that the optimization signal remains turn-specific. Within a turn, tokens share the same local credit, but advantages differ across turns. Full training pseudocode is deferred to Appendix
C
.

4.2
Comparative Results

Table
1
reports EM and F1 on the seven-benchmark search-QA suite.
On the 4B backbone, SIOP is the best verifier-free method on average, improving over EMPO by +0.7% EM and +1.8% F1 and over TTRL by +1.6% EM and +1.8% F1. The gains are clearest on multi-hop benchmarks where intermediate retrieval matters: on HotpotQA and 2Wiki, SIOP beats both verifier-free baselines and also exceeds the gold-supervised GRPO outcome baseline on both metrics, with the 2Wiki EM gap to EMPO reaching +6.6%. SIOP also closes most of the supervision gap to the gold-supervised IGPO turn-level baseline (-0.9% EM, -0.2% F1 on average) without using any gold labels at training time.
On the 8B backbone, SIOP again achieves the strongest verifier-free average, reaching 0.388 EM and 0.480 F1. It matches the gold-supervised GRPO outcome baseline in average EM and is slightly higher in average F1, while still trailing on some individual tasks such as HotpotQA EM and MuSiQue EM. Against the gold-supervised IGPO turn-level baseline (0.401 EM / 0.493 F1), SIOP trails by 1.3% EM and 1.3% F1 on average, a small remaining supervision gap given that SIOP uses no gold labels at training time. The F1 advantage over GRPO plausibly stems from SIOP’s soft semantic-cluster supervision rewarding equivalent surface forms, while the dense per-turn signal compensates for GRPO’s sparse outcome reward at scale. These results suggest that the self-induced turn-level signal transfers to the larger backbone, although per-task variation remains.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we have introduced verifier-free turn-level credit assignment for agentic reinforcement learning and instantiated it with Self-Induced Outcome Potential (SIOP). SIOP treats clustered final answers as latent future states and rewards each turn by the prefix-conditioned support gain it produces for the rollout’s realized mode, weighted by a reliability-calibrated belief over modes. On seven question-answering benchmarks with Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B backbones, SIOP outperforms outcome-only verifier-free baselines and approaches the average performance of a gold-supervised outcome baseline without using gold labels during training.

Appendix A
Related Work

A.1
Outcome RL and Supervised Step-Level Credit Assignment

Modern reasoning-model training typically starts from outcome-level RL with verifiable rewards. DeepSeekMath
[
34
]
introduced GRPO as a critic-free optimizer that compares sampled responses within each prompt group, and DeepSeek-R1
[
10
]
showed that outcome rewards can elicit long chain-of-thought behavior at scale. DAPO
[
45
]
and GSPO
[
54
]
further improve this group-based paradigm by addressing sampling, clipping, loss normalization, and importance-weighting stability. This line clarifies how to optimize a policy when an outcome reward is already available. It does not by itself specify what the reward target should be without a task-specific verifier, or how that target should be assigned across interaction turns.

The complementary literature studies whether intermediate reasoning steps should receive their own supervision. Lightman et al.
[
24
]
showed that process supervision can outperform outcome supervision for mathematical reasoning. Zhang et al.
[
49
]
generalized process reward models through entropy-regularized MDPs, and rStar-Math
[
9
]
uses MCTS-based self-evolved process preference models. Other methods refine the temporal granularity of RL itself: VinePPO
[
18
]
uses Monte Carlo rollouts to replace learned value functions for unbiased credit assignment, SPO
[
11
]
operates at a segment level between tokens and trajectories, TEMPO
[
35
]
derives nonparametric branch-gated corrections from prefix trees, and Zeng et al.
[
38
]
estimate turn-level advantages for multi-turn tool-use agents modeled as MDPs. These approaches show that finer credit is useful, but they still assume access to supervised step labels, verifiable outcomes, or an externally supplied task reward.

For multi-turn agents, information-potential methods make the dependence on a target especially explicit. IGPO
[
37
]
rewards a turn by the marginal increase in the policy’s probability of the gold answer.
\Delta
Belief-RL
[
2
]
uses belief updates for long-horizon credit assignment. TIPS
[
39
]
defines an information-potential reward for search-augmented LLMs, InfoPO
[
20
]
adapts the idea to user-centric settings, and InfoReasoner
[
13
]
optimizes retrieval-augmented reasoning with a synthetic semantic information-gain reward over retrieval trajectories. These methods establish that turn-level shaping is important for long-horizon optimization, but their potentials are still anchored to a gold answer, supervised belief state, externally specified utility, or retrieval-specific information objective. SIOP changes the role of the target: it constructs a query-specific target space from sampled final answers, treats semantic outcome modes as latent future states, and computes turn rewards as potential changes over this self-induced state space. The supervised gold-answer case is therefore a limiting case rather than the starting assumption (Proposition
2
).

A.2
Label-Free Reinforcement Learning from Output Distributions

Label-free reasoning optimization tries to replace benchmark labels or task-specific verifiers with signals induced by the model’s own samples. One family relies on agreement structure across multiple outputs. Semantic entropy by Kuhn et al.
[
21
]
and Farquhar et al.
[
7
]
estimates uncertainty over meaning-equivalent answer clusters, while TTRL
[
56
]
uses majority behavior from repeated sampling as a self-generated label. EMPO
[
50
]
directly optimizes semantic clusters by minimizing semantic entropy, SEED-GRPO
[
3
]
uses semantic entropy to modulate policy updates on high-uncertainty prompts, and Agarwal et al.
[
1
]
show that entropy minimization alone can be surprisingly effective. Xu et al.
[
40
]
further study entropy scheduling from exploration to exploitation under noisy supervision. These methods establish that output distributions contain usable training signal, but they also expose a failure mode: high agreement is not always correctness. RESTRAIN
[
47
]
addresses this issue by penalizing spurious consensus rather than trusting majority votes unconditionally.

A second family uses internal model feedback, reference likelihood, or self-evaluation as the reward source. RLPR
[
46
]
uses token probabilities assigned to reference answers, NOVER
[
27
]
studies verifier-free incentive training from standard supervised data, DRO
[
41
]
uses reasoning-reflection signals, VeriFree
[
55
]
optimizes reference-answer likelihood without an external verifier, and Intuitor
[
53
]
replaces external rewards with self-certainty. Related self-improvement methods obtain training signal through self-rewarding or self-play: Self-Rewarding Language Models
[
48
]
use LLM-as-a-Judge rewards, SPIN
[
6
]
frames alignment as self-play, SPIRAL
[
25
]
and SPICE
[
26
]
extend self-play to multi-turn settings, and Absolute Zero
[
52
]
and R-Zero
[
14
]
study self-evolution from zero external data.

The common limitation for our setting is not the absence of useful unlabeled signals, but their granularity. TTRL
[
56
]
, EMPO
[
50
]
, SEED-GRPO
[
3
]
, RLPR
[
46
]
, NOVER
[
27
]
, VeriFree
[
55
]
, and Intuitor
[
53
]
primarily attach rewards to prompts, final answers, or complete trajectories. They help decide which sampled outcomes or prompts are more reliable, but they do not identify which intermediate interaction turns moved the trajectory toward those outcomes. SIOP uses the same kind of unlabeled distributional structure for a different object: semantic outcome modes define a query-specific latent future-state space, and process rewards are computed as potential changes over that space. This turns outcome-level reliability into turn-level credit assignment rather than only outcome selection.

A.3
Open-Ended Search and Tool-Using Agents

Search and tool-use agents make credit assignment harder than single-turn generation because the model must decide not only what to answer, but also when to retrieve, which evidence to trust, and when to stop interacting with the environment. The agentic RAG survey by Li et al.
[
23
]
summarizes this shift from static retrieval to iterative information seeking. ReSearch
[
4
]
and Scent of Knowledge
[
33
]
further show that search behavior itself can be optimized rather than treated as a fixed preprocessing step. Open-domain QA benchmarks such as PopQA
[
28
]
, HotpotQA
[
44
]
, and MuSiQue
[
36
]
expose the same issue empirically: final-answer quality depends on intermediate evidence acquisition, especially for long-tail and multi-hop questions.

Reward design has therefore become central in tool-use and search-augmented RL. ToolRL
[
32
]
studies how reward granularity, scale, and temporal dynamics affect tool-use learning. Process vs. Outcome Reward
[
51
]
shows that finer-grained feedback can improve long-horizon agentic RAG, while also emphasizing the cost of obtaining gold intermediate supervision. These results support the need for turn-level feedback, but they still leave open how to obtain such feedback when neither intermediate labels nor task-specific answer verifiers are available.

Recent agentic RL methods adapt group-based optimization to multi-step environments. Search-R1
[
16
]
and ReSearch
[
5
]
train models to interleave reasoning with search, while GiGPO
[
8
]
, Step-GRPO
[
30
]
, and Tree-GRPO
[
15
]
construct group comparisons, step-level advantages, or tree-structured credit over agent trajectories. These methods improve optimization for search agents, but their learning signal still typically comes from a task reward, answer verifier, or environment-defined success criterion.
SIOP addresses a complementary supervision gap. Instead of assuming that the environment can verify the final answer or score each tool call, it constructs a latent outcome space from unlabeled sampled answers and uses potential changes over that space to score intermediate turns. This lets the same output distribution that supports label-free outcome selection also guide which retrieval decisions and tool calls were informative.

Training Algorithm

Algorithm
2
summarizes the full training pipeline. Each iteration processes one batch of queries in four phases. The
rollout and clustering
phase samples
K

q_{\theta}(c\mid q)
over those modes. The
scoring

\log p_{\theta}(y\mid\tau_{\leq t})
for every
(y,\tau_{\leq t})
pair, aggregates them into the multi-reference cluster support
\tilde{p}_{\theta}(c\mid\tau_{\leq t})

The scoring pass is served by a GPU-side inference service sharing the rollout backend’s memory budget, while the NLI calls run through a CPU-side singleton. Thus the main additional GPU cost over outcome-only RL is the forward pass over pseudo-response pairs at each training step.

Appendix D
Theoretical Characterizations and Proofs

D.1
Proof of Proposition
1

By definition of the outcome potential and the process reward,

\displaystyle r_{t,\mathrm{proc}}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\theta}(\tau_{\leq t}^{(k)},c(k))-\Phi_{\theta}(\tau_{\leq t-1}^{(k)},c(k))

(24)

\displaystyle=q_{\theta}(c(k)\mid q)\Bigl[\log\!\bigl(\tilde{p}_{\theta}(c(k)\mid\tau_{\leq t}^{(k)})\bigr)-\log\!\bigl(\tilde{p}_{\theta}(c(k)\mid\tau_{\leq t-1}^{(k)})\bigr)\Bigr].

Summing over turns yields a telescoping series:

\sum_{t=1}^{T_{k}}r_{t,\mathrm{proc}}^{(k)}=\sum_{t=1}^{T_{k}}\Bigl[\Phi_{\theta}(\tau_{\leq t}^{(k)},c(k))-\Phi_{\theta}(\tau_{\leq t-1}^{(k)},c(k))\Bigr],

(25)

and all interior terms cancel. The remaining boundary terms are

\displaystyle\Phi_{\theta}(\tau_{\leq T_{k}}^{(k)},c(k))

\displaystyle-\Phi_{\theta}(\tau_{\leq 0}^{(k)},c(k))

(26)

\displaystyle=q_{\theta}(c(k)\mid q)\Bigl[\log\!\bigl(\tilde{p}_{\theta}(c(k)\mid\tau_{\leq T_{k}}^{(k)})\bigr)-\log\!\bigl(\tilde{p}_{\theta}(c(k)\mid q)\bigr)\Bigr].

Thus the cumulative reward depends on the improvement in final conditional support, while each turn’s local credit is determined only by its own marginal contribution to that improvement.

D.2
Proof of Proposition
2

Factorization.
The factored form follows by expanding the difference of two outcome potentials in Section
3
. The calibrated target-mass factor
q_{\theta}(c(k)\mid q)
is query-specific and constant over turns of the same rollout, so it can be grouped outside the log-difference of the multi-reference cluster support. The remaining factor is the turn-wise change in log cluster support for the realized cluster
c(k)
. Together they give “target mass times cluster-level support gain,” which is the stated factorization.

Supervised limit.
When a gold answer
y^{\star}
is available and the self-induced target distribution concentrates on the gold cluster
c^{\star}
, the assumptions
q_{\theta}(c^{\star}\mid q)=1
,
R(c^{\star})=\{y^{\star}\}
, and
\alpha_{y^{\star}}^{(c^{\star})}=1
imply
\tilde{p}_{\theta}(c^{\star}\mid\tau_{\leq t}^{(k)})=p_{\theta}(y^{\star}\mid\tau_{\leq t}^{(k)})
. Substituting into the factored reward collapses the target mass to
1
and the cluster support to the gold-answer likelihood, yielding

r_{t}^{(k)}=\log p_{\theta}(y^{\star}\mid\tau_{\leq t}^{(k)})-\log p_{\theta}(y^{\star}\mid\tau_{\leq t-1}^{(k)}),

(27)

which is the standard supervised log-support reward used by turn-level shaping methods.

D.3
Bayesian View and Reliability Calibration

The self-induced target distribution can be read as a calibrated belief over semantic outcome modes obtained by a Bayesian-style update. The empirical mass
m(c\mid q)
is the rollout-induced prior over latent future states, while the factor
\exp(u_{\theta}(c,q))
occupies the algebraic position of an evidence likelihood derived from generic reliability signals. Under this view,

q_{\theta}(c\mid q)\propto\underbrace{m(c\mid q)}_{\text{rollout-induced prior}}\underbrace{\exp(u_{\theta}(c,q))}_{\text{reliability likelihood proxy}},

(28)

where the reliability strength is already absorbed into
u_{\theta}(c,q)=\eta r(c,q)
. This interpretation is not a claim that the NLI score is a calibrated likelihood. It only states that SIOP uses reliability signals in the same algebraic position as an evidence term before normalizing over semantic outcome modes.

Proposition 3

(Reliability calibration can reverse a spurious majority)
.

Consider two semantic outcome modes
c_{s}
and
c_{r}
with empirical masses
m_{s}>m_{r}>0
, where
c_{s}
is the higher-frequency mode and
c_{r}
is the lower-frequency mode. Let
u_{\theta}(c,q)=\eta r(c,q)
with
\eta>0
. The calibrated target assigns larger mass to
c_{r}
than to
c_{s}
if and only if

r(c_{r},q)-r(c_{s},q)>\frac{1}{\eta}\log\frac{m_{s}}{m_{r}}.

(29)

Thus a lower-frequency mode can overtake a raw majority exactly when its reliability advantage exceeds the log frequency disadvantage scaled by
1/\eta
.

Proof.

The normalized denominator of
q_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q)
is shared by both modes, so
q_{\theta}(c_{r}\mid q)>q_{\theta}(c_{s}\mid q)
is equivalent to

m_{r}\exp(\eta r(c_{r},q))>m_{s}\exp(\eta r(c_{s},q)).

(30)

Taking logarithms and rearranging gives

\eta\bigl(r(c_{r},q)-r(c_{s},q)\bigr)>\log\frac{m_{s}}{m_{r}},

(31)

which is equivalent to the stated condition. If the inequality is reversed, the higher-frequency mode remains preferred. Equality gives tied calibrated mass.
∎

The right-hand side
(1/\eta)\log(m_{s}/m_{r})
is the frequency disadvantage of
c_{r}
measured in the units of the chosen reliability score. Larger
\eta
makes reliability evidence sharper and lowers the threshold a low-frequency mode must clear. This condition should not be read as a guarantee that every wrong majority is corrected. If
r(c,q)
is a bounded NLI confidence, then highly imbalanced majorities may be impossible to reverse under a fixed calibration strength. The proposition instead identifies the exact regime in which reliability evidence can overcome raw self-consistency.

Appendix F
Reward Hacking and Confident-but-Wrong Modes

In verifier-free optimization, self-generated targets can reward an answer mode simply because many rollouts converge to it. This creates a reward-hacking path for confident-but-wrong modes: semantic agreement may reflect repeated model bias rather than evidence-grounded correctness. SIOP addresses this by separating cluster frequency from reliability before either signal enters the reward. In the factorized structure of Proposition
2
, the target distribution
q_{\theta}(c\mid q)
is not a direct function of cluster frequency. It combines empirical mode mass
m(c\mid q)
with a reliability correction
u_{\theta}(c,q)
driven by evidence support. Proposition
3
makes this behavior explicit: a lower-frequency but evidence-consistent cluster can receive larger target mass whenever its reliability advantage is large enough to overcome the log frequency gap. Thus raw majority support alone is not enough to dominate the target distribution.

The same separation carries into turn-level credit assignment. The outcome potential weights prefix support by the calibrated target mass of the rollout’s semantic outcome mode, so turns that increase support for reliable modes receive stronger credit than turns that only make a high-frequency mode more predictable. Cluster-level averaging across multiple rollouts and references further prevents a single wording choice from dominating the target.

Appendix G
Limitations and Future Work

Limitations.
The experiments focus on search-augmented QA with Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B, a setting where semantic answer modes and retrieved evidence provide a controlled testbed for turn-level credit assignment. This scope keeps the evaluation focused, while leaving broader agentic environments to future study. Since SIOP uses generic semantic clustering and evidence-support models, its signal is most natural when final outcomes can be grouped by meaning and retrieved evidence can support or contrast those meanings. Settings with highly ambiguous outcomes, weak evidence, or fine-grained tool states may require adapting the outcome-state construction.

Future work.
Future work could extend semantic outcome potentials in several directions. First, the same abstraction may apply beyond search-QA to agentic settings where trajectories end in semantically diverse outcomes, such as tool use, web navigation, and interactive code repair. Second, outcome states need not be limited to text-answer clusters. They could be defined over structured tool traces, multi-modal observations, or hierarchical semantic modes, allowing the reward to track richer forms of future progress. Third, the potential-based view opens the door to adaptive credit granularity, where the training signal is assigned not only at the turn level, but also to retrieved evidence, tool actions, or finer action spans when they are the main source of progress.
