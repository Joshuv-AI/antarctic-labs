The Carnot Bound: Limits and Possibilities for Bandwidth-Efficient Consensus (WIP)
Andrew Lewis-Pye 1,2, Patrick O’Grady 2

Abstract
. In leader-based protocols for State Machine Replication (SMR), the leader’s outgoing bandwidth is a natural throughput bottleneck. Erasure coding can alleviate this by allowing the leader to send each processor a single fragment of each block, rather than a full copy. The data expansion rate , the ratio of total data sent to payload size, determines how close throughput can get to the underlying network bandwidth. We investigate the fundamental limits and possibilities for bandwidth-efficient leader-based consensus. On the negative side, we prove that protocols with 2-round finality (one round of voting) cannot achieve a data expansion rate below approximately 2.5 2.5 , a bound that is matched by existing protocols. On the positive side, we show that protocols with 3-round finality (two rounds of voting) can push the data expansion rate arbitrarily close to 1 1 . The key insight is that the second voting round provides a recovery mechanism: leaders can attempt aggressive erasure codes and safely fall back to more conservative ones when reconstruction fails, without compromising consistency. We present two protocols with 3-round finality realising this approach. Carnot 1 assumes n ≥ 4 ​ f + 1 n\geq 4f+1 processors (of which at most f f may be Byzantine) and achieves a clean design requiring no additional fragment dissemination beyond the initial protocol messages. Carnot 2 operates under the optimal resilience assumption n ≥ 3 ​ f + 1 n\geq 3f+1 , at the cost of additional fragment dissemination when Byzantine processors interfere. Both protocols can incorporate stable leaders and optimistic proposals to maximise throughput and minimise latency. Under favourable conditions, with correct leaders and few actual faults, both protocols allow leaders to use data expansion rates approaching 1 1 ; under adversarial conditions, leaders can revert to safe expansion rates of approximately 1.33 1.33 and 1.5 1.5 , respectively – both still well below the 2.5 lower bound for protocols with 2-round finality.



1.
Introduction

State Machine Replication (SMR)
(
Schneider 1990
)
is a fundamental primitive for distributed computing that allows a collection of processors to maintain a consistent, shared log of transactions despite the failure, or even malicious behaviour, of some participants. Originally developed for fault-tolerant systems, SMR has become the algorithmic backbone of modern blockchains and decentralised applications. The core challenge is to ensure that all correct processors agree on the same sequence of transactions (Consistency), while also guaranteeing that new transactions are eventually included (Liveness), even in the presence of
Byzantine
faults, where corrupted processors may behave arbitrarily.

Protocols for SMR typically operate in the
partially synchronous
model
(
Dwork et al. 1988
)
, in which messages may be delayed arbitrarily before an unknown time GST (the Global Stabilisation Time), but are delivered within a known bound
\Delta
thereafter. This model captures realistic network conditions, including temporary partitions, and underpins many widely deployed protocols, from PBFT
(
Castro et al. 1999
)
to the consensus layers of modern blockchains such as Ethereum
(
Buterin and Griffith 2017
)
.

The throughput challenge.
A central goal in recent protocol design is to maximise
throughput
: the rate at which transactions can be finalised. Transactions are typically grouped into
blocks
, and protocols proceed by having processors agree on a sequence of blocks. Two broad families of protocols have emerged. In
DAG-based
protocols
(
Keidar et al. 2021
;
Spiegelman et al. 2022
;
Shrestha et al. 2025b
;
Keidar et al. 2022
)
, multiple processors propose blocks concurrently, organising them into a directed acyclic graph from which a linear ordering is then extracted. In
leader-based
protocols
(
Castro et al. 1999
;
Yin et al. 2019
;
Shoup et al. 2025
;
Chou et al. 2025
)
, a single designated leader proposes each block, and the remaining processors vote to accept or reject it.

DAG-based protocols can achieve high throughput through their concurrency, but at the cost of higher
latency
, in the sense that finalisation requires more rounds of communication. Leader-based protocols offer lower latency, but the leader’s outgoing bandwidth is a natural bottleneck, which can be alleviated using
erasure coding
. Rather than sending a full copy of each block to every processor, the leader encodes the block’s payload and sends each processor a single fragment, from which the full payload can be reconstructed once sufficiently many fragments are collected. The efficiency of this encoding is captured by the
data expansion rate
: if the payload has size
\beta
, then the total data the leader must send is approximately
d\cdot\beta
, where
d
is the data expansion rate. As examined by Lewis-Pye, Nayak and Shrestha
(
Lewis-Pye et al. 2025
)
, this factor
d
directly governs the throughput bottleneck. For a network in which each processor has bandwidth
B
, i.e., processors can send
B
bits per unit time, payloads of size
\beta
require approximately
d\beta/B
time to disseminate. The maximum throughput—measured in payload bits finalised per unit time—is therefore approximately
B/d
. To approach throughputs matching the underlying network bandwidth, one must push the data expansion rate as close to
1
as possible.

Finality and voting rounds.
Before stating our results, we establish some basic terminology. We consider a set of
n
processors, of which at most
f
may display Byzantine (arbitrary) behaviour. In leader-based protocols, which are the focus of this paper, the protocol proceeds in sequential
views
, each with a designated
leader
who assembles transactions into a block and proposes it to the other processors, who then vote on whether to accept it. We use the term
r
-round finality
to describe a protocol in which finality requires
r
rounds of communication: one round for the leader’s proposal, followed by
r-1
rounds of voting. Protocols with
2-round finality
, consisting of one proposal round and one voting round, include Minimmit
(
Chou et al. 2025
)
, Hydrangea
(
Shrestha et al. 2025a
)
, and Kudzu
(
Shoup et al. 2025
)
, and typically assume
n\geq 5f+1
(
n\geq 5f-1
is necessary and sufficient
(
Kuznetsov et al. 2021
)
). Protocols with
3-round finality
, consisting of one proposal round and two voting rounds, include Simplex
(
Chan and Pass 2023
)
and the protocols we present in this paper, and typically operate under the (optimal) assumption that
n\geq 3f+1
.

Our contributions.
This paper investigates the fundamental limits and possibilities for bandwidth-efficient leader-based consensus using erasure coding. We establish a lower bound on the data expansion rate for protocols with 2-round finality, and show that protocols with 3-round finality can circumvent this bound, achieving data expansion rates approaching
1
.

An impossibility result for 2-round finality (Section
8
).
We prove that protocols with 2-round finality cannot achieve a data expansion rate below
2.5
. This bound is tight: E-Minimmit
(
Chou et al. 2025
)
and Kudzu
(
Shoup et al. 2025
)
achieve data expansion rates of approximately
2.5
.

Circumventing the bound with 3-round finality.
The additional voting round in 3-round finality protocols provides a crucial
recovery mechanism
. When the leader uses an aggressive erasure code (with a high reconstruction threshold
k
), it is possible that some correct processors reconstruct the payload, perhaps with the aid of fragments from Byzantine processors, while others cannot. In a 2-round finality protocol, this possibility leads to unrecoverable configurations: consistency and liveness cannot both be maintained. With two rounds of voting, however, the second round can detect whether data availability has been achieved and, if not, allow the protocol to nullify the view and retry. This means leaders can
attempt
low data expansion rates and safely fall back to higher rates when reconstruction fails.

Specifically, a correct leader can use an
(n,k)
-erasure code for any
k
up to
n-1
, meaning that
k
of
n
fragments suffice for reconstruction. This produces a data expansion rate of
n/k
. In the optimistic case, when the leader is correct, the network is synchronous, and at most
f_{a}
processors are actually faulty, the leader can set
k
as high as
n-f_{a}-1
(the leader does not need to disseminate its own fragment), giving a data expansion rate that approaches
1
as
f_{a}
becomes small relative to
n
. When conditions deteriorate, the protocol automatically reverts to a safe expansion rate without interrupting progress. We present two protocols realising this approach: Carnot 1, a simple protocol assuming
n\geq 4f+1
, and Carnot 2, which operates under the weaker (and optimal) assumption
n\geq 3f+1
. The safe rate depends on the resilience assumption: approximately
1.5
for
n\geq 3f+1
, or approximately
1.33
for
n\geq 4f+1
.

The name is inspired by the Carnot heat engine, which achieves the theoretical maximum efficiency for converting heat into work. Similarly, our protocols aim to approach the theoretical maximum efficiency for converting network bandwidth into throughput.
1: a clean design for
n\geq 4f+1
(Sections

).
Our first protocol assumes
n\geq 4f+1
and achieves a particularly clean design. A key concern when using aggressive erasure codes is ensuring
data availability
: if a correct processor advances to the next view on the basis of a block whose payload it cannot fully reconstruct, it must be guaranteed that the payload can eventually be recovered from the messages received by correct processors collectively. In many protocols, this guarantee requires correct processors to perform additional rounds of fragment dissemination when the leader is Byzantine, adding communication overhead precisely in the cases where resources are most constrained. Carnot 1 avoids this entirely: processors echo their certified fragment once upon receiving it from the leader and voting, but no further echoing is ever required. The protocol achieves this through a careful choice of quorum sizes that guarantees every view produces either a compact data-availability certificate or a mechanism for skipping the view, without additional communication.

Carnot 1 also incorporates
stable leaders
, where a single leader proposes blocks across multiple consecutive views (a
superview
), to eliminate the inter-proposal gap that arises with rotating leaders. Additionally, we describe how
optimistic proposals
, inspired by Moonshot
(
Doidge et al. 2024
)
, can be used to allow the next leader to begin proposing before the current superview has concluded, further reducing latency.

Carnot 2: a protocol for
n\geq 3f+1
(Appendices
B
–
D
).
Our second protocol operates under the standard optimal resilience assumption
n\geq 3f+1
, at the cost of requiring additional fragment dissemination when Byzantine processors interfere. When a processor reconstructs a payload but does not receive proof of data availability, it re-encodes the payload using a more conservative erasure code and disseminates the resulting fragments, ensuring that all correct processors can eventually reconstruct the payload as well. Under standard operation with correct leaders, no extra dissemination occurs.

Both protocols build on the Simplex consensus protocol
(
Chan and Pass 2023
)
, a simple and efficient protocol for partial synchrony with rotating leaders and two rounds of voting per view. Our protocols extend Simplex with erasure coding, stable leaders, and the mechanisms described above. To summarise the trade-off between the two: Carnot 1 is simpler and more communication-efficient under adversarial conditions, but requires a stronger resilience assumption; Carnot 2 tolerates the optimal number of faults, but may require additional communication when Byzantine processors interfere. Under standard operation with correct leaders, the two protocols behave almost identically.

The Setup

We consider a set
\Pi=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}
of
n
processors. At most
f
processors may become corrupted by the adversary during the course of the execution (i.e., the adversary is
adaptive
), and may then display
Byzantine
(arbitrary) behaviour.
Processors that never become corrupted by the adversary are referred to as
correct
. In Sections
4
-
6
, during the discussion of Carnot 1, we assume
n\geq 4f+1
. In Appendices
B
-

, during the discussion of Carnot 2, we assume
n\geq 3f+1

Cryptographic assumptions
. Our cryptographic assumptions are standard for papers on this topic. Processors communicate by point-to-point authenticated channels. We use a cryptographic signature scheme, a public key infrastructure (PKI) to validate signatures, and a collision resistant hash function
H
. As expanded upon later in this section, we also use threshold signatures and erasure codes.

We assume a computationally bounded adversary. Following a common standard in distributed computing and for simplicity of presentation (to avoid the analysis of negligible error probabilities), we assume these cryptographic schemes are perfect, i.e., we restrict attention to executions in which the adversary is unable to break these cryptographic schemes.

The partial synchrony model
. As noted above, processors communicate using point-to-point authenticated channels. We consider the standard partial synchrony model
(
Dwork et al. 1988
)
, whereby the execution is divided into discrete timeslots
t\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
and a message sent at time
t
must arrive at time
t^{\prime}>t
with
t^{\prime}\leq\max\{\text{GST},t\}+\Delta
. While
\Delta
is known, the value of GST is unknown to the protocol. We also write
\delta
to denote the (unknown) least upper bound on message delay after GST (noting that
\delta
may be significantly less than the known bound
\Delta
). The adversary chooses GST and also message delivery times, subject to the constraints already defined.

Correct processors begin the protocol execution before GST and are not assumed to have synchronised clocks. For simplicity, we do assume that
the clocks of correct processors all proceed in real time,
meaning that if

t^{\prime}>t
then the local clock of correct
p
at time
t^{\prime}
is
t^{\prime}-t
in
advance of its value at time
t
. Using standard arguments, our
protocol and analysis can
be extended in a straightforward way to the case
in which there is a known upper bound on the difference
between the clock speeds of correct processors.

Threshold signatures
. A
k

threshold signature scheme allows

from any
k
processors on a given message to be combined to form a

on that message. Forming a certificate on any message is infeasible given less than
k
signature shares. Such schemes can be implemented using BLS signatures
(
Boneh et al. 2001
)
.

.

Transactions
. Transactions are messages of a

. Each timeslot, each processor may receive some

Extractable SMR: informal discussion
. Many protocols for State Machine Replication (SMR)
(
Schneider 1990
)
do not
explicitly
specify the mechanism by which correct processors retrieve the full finalised log of transactions. Instead, they ensure
data availability
for the log. In Narwhal
(
Danezis et al. 2022
)

f+1
such acknowledgements acts as a
data availability certificate
for the block: it proves that at least one correct processor has received the block, and can therefore supply it to others upon request. Consensus can then be run on certificates rather than full blocks.
The notion of
Extractable SMR
was introduced in
(
Lewis-Pye and Shapiro 2025
)
to formalise the task being solved by such protocols. Roughly speaking, the requirement is that, while an individual processor may not be able to determine the full finalised log (without extra communication), the correct processors collectively receive sufficient messages to determine it.

Extractable SMR: definition
. Write
\sigma\preceq\tau
to denote that the string
\sigma
is a prefix of the string
\tau
.
If
\mathcal{P}
is a protocol for

be the set of all messages that are received by at least one (potentially Byzantine) processor during the execution. For any timeslot

be the set of all messages that are received by at least one correct processor at a timeslot

. We require the following conditions to hold:

Consistency
. For any
M_{1}\subseteq M_{2}\subseteq M^{*}
,
\mathcal{F}(M_{1})\preceq\mathcal{F}(M_{2})
.

Liveness
. If correct

Note that consistency suffices to ensure that, for arbitrary

are compatible. To see this, note that, by consistency,

. We specify a protocol that produces

Carnot: the intuition

In this section, we informally describe the intuition behind our positive results. In Section
8

n\geq 3f+1
. First, in Section
3.1
, we review the Simplex protocol
(

)
, upon which our protocols will be based. Then, in Section

-

3.1.
Recalling Simplex

Simplex
(
Chan and Pass 2023
)
is a protocol for partial synchrony assuming
n\geq 3f+1
, with rotating leaders. It proceeds in sequential
views

v=1,2,\ldots
, each with leader
\mathtt{lead}(v)
.

Two rounds of voting
. The protocol uses two rounds of voting per view. When a leader enters view
v
, it proposes a block extending a notarised chain. Upon receiving a valid proposal
b
, each processor disseminates (sends to all) a
stage-1
vote for
b
. A set of
n-f
stage-1 votes for
b
, each by a different processor, is called a
stage-1 notarisation
for
b
. Upon receiving a stage-1 notarisation for
b
, a processor moves to view
v+1
. If, upon entering
v+1
, the processor’s timer for view
v
has not yet fired, it disseminates a
stage-2
vote for
b
. A set of
n-f
stage-2 votes is called a
stage-2 notarisation
for
b
, and
b
is
finalised
when it receives such a notarisation.

Nullifications
. If the leader is faulty or slow, processors time out after
3\Delta
and disseminate a
nullify
(v)
message. A set of
n-f
nullify
(v)
messages is called a
nullification
for view
v
; processors also move to view
v+1
upon receiving a nullification. The critical design constraint is that each correct processor either sends a stage-2 vote or a nullify
(v)
message, but never both. It follows by the standard quorum intersection argument (
(n-f)+(n-f)-n\geq f+1
when
n\geq 3f+1
) that a stage-2 notarisation and a nullification for the same view cannot coexist. Similarly, two distinct blocks cannot both receive a stage-1 notarisation in the same view.

Progression through views
. Correct processors disseminate stage-1 notarisations and nullifications upon first receipt. If any correct processor leaves view
v
upon receiving a stage-1 notarisation, then all correct processors receive it and also leave the view. Otherwise, all correct processors eventually time out and disseminate nullify
(v)
messages, producing a nullification.

Consistency
. No correct processor votes for a view
v
block with parent
b^{\prime}
for view
v^{\prime}
unless it has received a stage-1 notarisation for
b^{\prime}
and nullifications for all views in the open interval
(v^{\prime},v)
. If a view
v
block
b
is finalised, then view
v
does not receive a nullification, so correct processors cannot vote for any block inconsistent with
b
in a subsequent view.

Liveness
. Recall that
\delta
denotes the (unknown) least upper bound on message delay after GST. The key lemma is that views are synchronised: if the first correct processor to enter view
v
does so at
t\geq\text{GST}
, then all correct processors enter view
v
by
t+\delta
, because processors disseminate stage-1 notarisations and nullifications upon first receipt.

Suppose the leader of view
v
is correct and proposes
b
with parent
b^{\prime}
for view
v^{\prime}
. The leader enters view
v
and proposes by
t+\delta
. Since the leader has entered view
v
, it must have received nullifications for all views in
(v^{\prime},v)
as well as a stage-1 notarisation for
b^{\prime}
; it disseminates all of these upon first receipt. All correct processors therefore receive these, together with the proposal
b
, by
t+2\delta
. They send stage-1 votes by
t+2\delta
and receive a stage-1 notarisation for
b
by
t+3\delta
. Since
\delta\leq\Delta
, this occurs before any timer fires (the earliest a timer can fire is
t+3\Delta\geq t+3\delta
), so all correct processors send stage-2 votes upon entering view
v+1
, and
b
is finalised by
t+4\delta
.

3.2.
The advantage of stable leaders

The cost of rotating leaders
. In Simplex, each view has a different leader. Before proposing, the new leader must receive a stage-1 notarisation or nullification from the previous view, and this creates a significant gap between consecutive proposals during which no block-data is being disseminated, limiting throughput and increasing latency. One can partially address this with
optimistic proposals
, whereby the leader of view
v+1
proposes a block upon receiving the proposal for view
v
, without waiting for a stage-1 notarisation. This reduces the gap, but does not eliminate it: the new leader must still receive the previous leader’s proposal before it can propose its own block.

Stable leaders and pipelining
. A more direct solution is to give the same leader multiple successive views. If a correct leader has
x
consecutive views, it can pipeline proposals: as soon as it sends the block for view
v
, it can immediately begin sending the block for view
v+1
, without waiting for any messages. This eliminates the inter-proposal gap entirely for all but the first view of each leader’s tenure, allowing throughput to approach the underlying network bandwidth as
x
grows. In our protocol, we formalise this using
superviews
: each superview is a sequence of
x
views with a single leader, and leaders rotate between superviews.

3.3.
Basic erasure coding

We now turn to the question of how each individual block is disseminated. In Simplex, the leader sends a copy of the full block to every other processor, so that the total data sent is approximately
n
times the block size — a data expansion rate of
n
. A natural approach to reducing this is to use erasure coding. In this section, we describe a straightforward approach producing a data expansion rate of 3, using methods first described in
(
Cachin and Tessaro 2005
)
. In Sections
3.4
and
3.5
, we consider how to achieve lower data expansion rates.

Erasure-coded proposals
. Rather than sending the entire block to each processor, the leader separates out the block’s
payload

C
(its sequence of transactions) and encodes it using an
(n,n-2f)
-erasure code, producing
n

a single

Voting and fragment dissemination
. Upon receiving and verifying its certified fragment, processor
p_{i}
disseminates both the fragment and a stage-1 vote for
b
. If
n-f
stage-1 votes are collected, a stage-1 notarisation is formed. To proceed safely to the next view, however, a processor must not only receive a stage-1 notarisation but also collect enough fragments to reconstruct the payload.

Why
n-2f
fragments must suffice
. Consider a correct processor
p_{i}
that receives a stage-1 notarisation for
b
. Since
p_{i}
disseminates the notarisation upon receipt, all correct processors will eventually receive it too. But can all correct processors also reconstruct the payload? The notarisation contains
n-f
votes, of which at least
n-2f
are from correct processors. These correct voters each disseminate their fragment, so every correct processor is guaranteed to receive at least
n-2f
distinct fragments. With an
(n,n-2f)
-erasure code, this suffices for reconstruction, ensuring that all correct processors can reconstruct the payload and proceed to the next view. (Note that
p_{i}
itself may have received more than
n-2f
fragments. For example, if the leader is correct,
p_{i}
will eventually receive fragments from all
n-f
correct processors. The point is that
n-2f
is the number we can guarantee for
every
correct processor.)

The data expansion rate is 3
. The data expansion rate is
n/(n-2f)
, which equals
(3f+1)/(f+1)
when
n=3f+1
, approaching 3 as
f
grows.

Stage-2 voting and finalisation
. Once a processor has received a stage-1 notarisation and reconstructed the payload, it proceeds to the next view and, if its timer has not fired, sends a stage-2 vote, exactly as in Simplex. The safety and liveness arguments carry over from Section
3.1
without essential modification: the key properties — that each correct processor sends at most one stage-1 vote and either a stage-2 vote or a nullification per view — are unchanged.

Threshold certificates
. In the description above, a stage-1 notarisation is a set of
n-f
individual votes, each of which must be disseminated. In practice, one can reduce the communication overhead by using threshold signatures: each processor’s vote includes a signature share, and
n-f
shares can be combined into a single compact

that any processor can verify. This is the approach we adopt in the formal specification (Section
4
).

3.4.
Achieving a data expansion rate of 1.5

The data expansion rate of 3 arose because only
n-2f
fragments were guaranteed to reach every correct processor, forcing the use of an
(n,n-2f)
-erasure code. To reduce the data expansion rate, we need to increase the number of fragments required for reconstruction. In this section, we describe how to achieve a data expansion rate of
n/(n-f)
, which equals
(3f+1)/(2f+1)
when
n=3f+1
, approaching
1.5
as
f
grows.

Using an
(n,n-f)
-erasure code
. Blocks are formed using erasure coding as before, but now with an
(n,n-f)
-erasure code:
n-f
fragments are required for reconstruction. A processor
p_{i}
proceeds to view
v+1
upon receiving both a stage-1 notarisation for a view
v
block
b
and fragments of
b
from
n-f
distinct processors. Under standard operation, i.e., correct leaders during synchrony, these
n-f
fragments arrive naturally as part of stage-1 voting, and the protocol behaves just as in Section
3.3
.

The problem: ensuring data availability
. The difficulty arises when the leader is faulty. In such cases, a correct processor
p_{i}
might collect
n-f
fragments and form a stage-1 notarisation, but some other correct processor
p_{j}
might not receive enough fragments to reconstruct the payload. Since we now require
n-f
fragments rather than
n-2f
, the guarantee from Section
3.3
— that the correct voters alone provide enough fragments — no longer suffices. We therefore need an additional mechanism to ensure that all correct processors eventually receive the fragments they need.

Fragment echoing
. The solution is as follows. Upon proceeding to view
v+1
, processor
p_{i}
continues with the instructions for the new view but also, in the background (without slowing the critical path), sets a timer to expire after
\Delta
. When the timer expires,
p_{i}
checks which processors have not yet sent their fragment of
b
, and sends each such processor
p_{j}
their fragment, which
p_{i}
can reconstruct from the decoded payload, along with the stage-1 notarisation for
b
. Additionally, any processor
p_{j}
that receives its own fragment of
b
together with a stage-1 notarisation (from any processor, not just the leader) is required to
echo
that fragment by disseminating it to all processors, even if
p_{j}
has since moved to a later view or has previously voted for a different fragment. The stage-1 notarisation requirement here is important: it limits the amount of echoing that Byzantine processors can provoke, since extra echoing is triggered only for a block that has actually been notarised.

Two types of extra communication
. Under standard operation, no extra fragment echoing occurs. When it does occur, there are two types: (a)
p_{i}
may have to send
p_{j}
their fragment (if
p_{i}
has not received that fragment from
p_{j}
); (b)
p_{j}
may subsequently have to echo their fragment to all processors upon receiving it together with the stage-1 notarisation.

M-certificates as a data availability optimisation
. An issue with the mechanism above is that even crashed processors trigger extra fragment echoing. To mitigate this, we introduce an optimisation. If
f+1
stage-2 votes for
b
are collected, at least one must be from a correct processor, and a correct processor only sends a stage-2 vote after successfully reconstructing the payload. The
f+1
votes can therefore be combined into a compact threshold certificate — which we call a stage-2
M-certificate
(where ‘M’ stands for ‘mini’) — that serves as a certificate of data availability. Since any block receiving an M-certificate must also have received a stage-1 notarisation (because each correct stage-2 voter must first have seen one), an M-certificate on its own suffices for view progression: a processor that receives an M-certificate for a view
v
block can proceed to view
v+1
without needing to collect
n-f
fragments directly. This eliminates the need for further fragment echoing upon receipt of an M-certificate.

3.5.
Pushing the data expansion rate lower than 1.5

While a data expansion rate of
1.5
may be necessary when
f
processors are actually faulty, when fewer processors are faulty it should be possible for leaders to use lower expansion rates. The idea is to allow leaders to
attempt
lower expansion rates and revert to rate
1.5
if reconstruction fails. We show in Section
8
that this is not possible for protocols with 2-round finality: even a single attempt at a lower data expansion rate can lead to a situation from which Consistency and Liveness cannot both be maintained. With 3-round finality (two rounds of voting), however, recovery is possible.

The difficulty
. When the leader uses an
(n,k)
-erasure code with
k>n-f
, the
n-f
fragments echoed by correct voters may not suffice for reconstruction. If
no
correct processor can reconstruct the payload, this is unproblematic: all correct processors eventually time out and nullify the view. The difficulty arises when
some
correct processors reconstruct the payload (perhaps with the aid of fragments from Byzantine processors) while others cannot. In this case, unlike in Section
3.4
, it does not suffice to have correct processors echo their fragments: even if all correct processors receive and echo their fragments, the
n-f
fragments from correct processors may not be enough for reconstruction when
k>n-f
.

Carnot 2: an approach for
n\geq 3f+1
. The most natural solution, which we present as Carnot 2 in Appendix
C
, is as follows. When a processor decodes the payload and moves to the next view, but does not receive an M-certificate (i.e., proof of data availability), it reverts to encoding the payload using an
(n,n-f)
-erasure code and sends out the relevant fragments; upon receiving their fragment together with the corresponding stage-1 certificate, other processors echo their fragment to all. In this way, if any correct processor leaves the view upon decoding the block, it can be sure all others will eventually recover it. While this approach works for
n\geq 3f+1
, it has the drawback that Byzantine action can trigger a significant amount of extra fragment echoing.

Carnot 1: avoiding extra echoing for
n\geq 4f+1
. In the main body, we present a cleaner approach that assumes
n\geq 4f+1

(a) A stage-2 notarisation and a nullification for the same view cannot coexist

Consequences for view progression
. Property (a) guarantees Consistency will still be satisfied. Property (b) means we can require either an M-certificate (the threshold certificate formed from an M-notarisation) or an N-certificate (the threshold certificate formed from a nullification) for view progression. Does requiring M-certificates for view progression limit throughput?
One might worry that waiting for an M-certificate rather than a stage-1 notarisation delays view progression and so limits throughput (for Carnot 2, this is not an issue). For Carnot 1, this is not an issue within a superview, because the leader pipelines block dissemination regardless of view progression. For transitions between superviews, the delay can be handled by optimistic proposals (see Section
6
), which allow the next leader to propose immediately upon receiving the previous leader’s block. One might still worry about latency; we discuss this further in Section

Carnot 1 vs Carnot 2
. To summarise, Carnot 2 works under the weaker assumption
n\geq 3f+1
but requires extra fragment echoing when Byzantine processors interfere. Carnot 1 assumes
n\geq 4f+1
but is simpler and requires no extra fragment echoing beyond the initial echo upon voting.

6.
Carnot 1: Optimistic proposals

In the protocol as specified in Section
4
, a correct leader
p_{i}
for superview
w
must enter the initial view
v
of superview
w
before proposing its first block. Since
p_{i}
enters view
v
only after adding a view
v-1
block to
\mathtt{blocks}
or receiving an N-certificate for view
v-1
, this creates a gap during which no leader is actively sending proposals. This gap increases transaction latency and reduces throughput, particularly when the number of views per superview is small.

In this section, we describe how Carnot 1 can integrate
optimistic proposals
, using an approach inspired by Moonshot
(
Doidge et al. 2024
)
, to allow
p_{i}
to propose a view
v
block
before
entering view
v
, while the previous superview is still in progress. Under standard operation—during synchrony and when leaders are correct—the optimistic proposal will be finalised, reducing the latency gap. If the optimistic proposal cannot be finalised, the protocol falls back to the standard mechanism:
p_{i}
makes a fresh proposal after entering view
v
. After GST and when
p_{i}
is correct, one of these proposals will be finalised.

Note that optimistic proposals are relevant only for the
first
block of each superview (the initial view block). For non-initial views within a superview, the same leader is already pipelining proposals, so no gap arises: with a stable leader there is not even the
\delta
time gap that arises during optimistic proposals due to message delay between successive leaders.

When should
p_{i}
propose optimistically?
Let
p_{j}=\mathtt{lead}(v-1)
be the leader of the previous superview, and suppose
p_{j}
proposes a view
v-1
block
b^{\prime}
. There are at least two options for when
p_{i}
should make its optimistic proposal:

(a)

Propose upon receiving a votable fragment for view
v-1
.
Upon receiving a certified fragment of
b^{\prime}
that is votable for view
v-1
, processor
p_{i}
immediately proposes a view
v
block with parent
b^{\prime}
.

(b)

Propose upon receiving the full block
b^{\prime}
.
Processor
p_{j}
sends fragments of
b^{\prime}
to all processors other than
p_{i}
, but sends
p_{i}
the full block
b^{\prime}
(i.e., the complete payload rather than a single fragment). Upon receiving
b^{\prime}
, processor
p_{i}
proposes a view
v
block with parent
b^{\prime}
, with knowledge of
b^{\prime}
’s payload.

Option (a) has the disadvantage that
p_{i}
proposes before knowing the payload of
b^{\prime}
, which may cause transaction repetition and other complexities. The advantage of Option (b) is that
p_{i}
can avoid including transactions already present in
b^{\prime}
. The disadvantage is that it roughly
doubles
the total information that
p_{j}
must send:
p_{j}
sends approximately
\beta
bits in fragments to other processors, and a further
\beta
bits for the full block to
p_{i}
. This increases latency and significantly impacts throughput when the number of views in each superview is small.

We treat Option (a) below and in Appendix
A
; modifying the approach for Option (b) is straightforward.

Coordinating proposals: the problem
. To ensure the proof of Consistency is unaffected, we still require correct processors to send at most one stage-1 vote and at most one stage-2 vote in each view. In a context where the leader
p_{i}
may make more than one proposal for an initial view
v
, this raises a question: how can we ensure that one of the proposals receives enough votes to become finalised?

If
no
correct processor is able to vote for the optimistic proposal, then making a second proposal is unproblematic: no correct processor regards the optimistic proposal as votable for view
v
, so when they receive a second (properly formed) proposal, they will all vote for it. The difficulty arises when
some
correct processors are able to vote for the optimistic proposal
b
(because its parent
b^{\prime}
is in their local value
\mathtt{blocks}
), while others are not.

Coordinating proposals: the solution
. To handle this, we implement a locking mechanism for initial views. When evaluating votability condition (i) from Section
4
for an initial view
v
, correct processors use their local value
\mathtt{blocks}

as defined at the timeslot at which they entered view
v
, rather than their current value. This restriction is lifted for a specific block when the leader
p_{i}
sends a
(\text{release},b)
message for that block: upon receiving
(\text{release},b)
, processors use their current value of
\mathtt{blocks}
when evaluating whether they have received a votable fragment of
b
.

When a second proposal is not needed
. Upon entering view
v
at timeslot
t
, the leader
p_{i}
waits
2\Delta
before deciding whether it needs to make a second proposal (note that, during synchrony and when leaders are correct, this wait does not impact the ‘critical path’). Suppose view
v
begins after GST, i.e., the first correct processor to enter view
v
does so after GST. By Lemma
5.7
, all correct processors enter view
v
by
t+\delta
. If any correct processor had
b^{\prime}
in its local value
\mathtt{blocks}
upon entering view
v
, then that processor had an M-certificate for
b^{\prime}
at that time, and disseminates it (line
4
). The leader
p_{i}
therefore receives this M-certificate by
t+2\delta\leq t+2\Delta
, and adds
b^{\prime}
to its own
\mathtt{blocks}
by this time. In this case,
p_{i}
disseminates
(\text{release},b)
without making a second proposal. Upon receiving this message, all correct processors evaluate votability for
b
using their current value of
\mathtt{blocks}
, which now contains
b^{\prime}
(since the M-certificate has been disseminated). All correct processors can therefore vote for
b
.

When a second proposal is needed
. If
p_{i}
has not added
b^{\prime}
to
\mathtt{blocks}
by
t+2\Delta
, then—assuming the view begins after GST—no correct processor had
b^{\prime}
in
\mathtt{blocks}
upon entering view
v
, and hence no correct processor has voted for
b
. In this case,
p_{i}
proposes a new block
b^{\prime\prime}
for view
v
(whose parent is determined by
p_{i}
’s current local variable
\mathtt{b}
) and disseminates
(\text{release},b^{\prime\prime})
. All correct processors can then vote for
b^{\prime\prime}
.

The formal specification and verification appear in Appendix
A
.

Further discussion.
This approach produces an optimistically responsive protocol, i.e., once things stabilise after GST, transactions are finalised in time
O(\delta)
so long as leaders are correct. If
p_{j}=\mathtt{lead}(v-1)
is faulty, this can cause
p_{i}
to wait
2\Delta
before making a second proposal. This does not significantly impact the overall latency, however, since a faulty
p_{j}
can already cause an
\Omega(\Delta)
delay in view
v-1
. Moonshot
(
Doidge et al. 2024
)
also describes an alternative approach in which
p_{i}
makes the second proposal without a fixed-length wait, instead proceeding upon receiving nullify messages from sufficiently many processors. This requires larger nullify messages; we do not pursue it here, though modifying the protocol accordingly would be straightforward.

8.
The data expansion bound for 2-round finality

In this section, we establish bounds on coding efficiency for protocols with 2-round finality. We first explain the connection between SMR protocols with 2-round finality and Byzantine Broadcast. We then define 2-round Byzantine Broadcast formally, state our theorem, and prove it using an indistinguishability argument.

From 2-round finality to Byzantine Broadcast
. An SMR protocol has
2-round finality
if, when the leader is correct, the network is synchronous, and sufficiently many processors are correct, a block proposed by the leader is finalised after two rounds of message exchange. Protocols with 2-round finality, such as E-Minimmit
(
Chou et al. 2025
)
, Hydrangea
(
Shrestha et al. 2025a
)
, and Kudzu
(
Shoup et al. 2025
)
, assume
n\geq 5f+1
(
n\geq 5f-1
is necessary and sufficient
(
Kuznetsov et al. 2021
)
).

We formalise the relevant notion of Byzantine Broadcast below, and then explain how any SMR protocol with 2-round finality yields a solution.

8.1.
2-round Byzantine Broadcast

We define 2-round Byzantine Broadcast (2-round BB) in the partially synchronous model with
\Delta=1
. Let
f^{*}\leq f
be a parameter.

•

We consider a set
\Pi
of
n
processors, of which at most
f
display Byzantine faults.

•

One processor is designated the
broadcaster
. All processors are told the identity of the broadcaster.

•

The broadcaster is given an input in some set
V
. The set
V
is known to all processors.

•

The protocol must satisfy the following conditions:

–

Termination
. All correct processors must eventually output a value in
V
.

–

Agreement
. No two correct processors output different values.

–

Validity
. If
\text{GST}=0
, the broadcaster is correct with input
v
, and at most
f^{*}
processors are faulty, then all correct processors output
v
by timeslot 2.

From SMR with 2-round finality to 2-round BB
. Any SMR protocol with 2-round finality yields a protocol for 2-round BB as follows. Given an input
v
, the broadcaster acts as the first leader and proposes a block containing
v
. Processors then execute the full SMR protocol and output the first finalised payload. Validity follows from the protocol’s liveness guarantee: if
\text{GST}=0
, the broadcaster is correct, and at most
f^{*}
processors are faulty (for these protocols,
f^{*}=f
), the block is finalised within two rounds. Agreement follows from Consistency. Termination follows from the protocol’s general liveness mechanism—processors eventually finalise some block, even if this requires additional rounds. Our lower bound therefore applies to any SMR protocol with 2-round finality.

Theorem 8.1.

Suppose
f\geq 2
,
n\geq 3f+1
and that
\mathcal{P}
is a polynomial-time protocol solving 2-round BB. Let
P\subseteq\Pi
be any set of size
n-2f-f^{*}+1
that does not contain the broadcaster. Then, in any execution of
\mathcal{P}
in which the broadcaster is correct, the broadcaster’s input can be computed in polynomial time from the messages sent by the broadcaster to processors in
P
at timeslot 0.

The requirement
f\geq 2
is used in the proof to ensure that a certain set of processors is non-empty for the indistinguishability argument (specifically, the set
P_{4}\setminus\{\text{broadcaster}\}
in Section
8.2
).

Our proof establishes a natural way to compute the broadcaster’s input from the messages sent to processors in
P
at timeslot 0. Let
P
be as in the statement of the theorem, and consider any execution
E
in which
\text{GST}=0
and:

•

The broadcaster has input
v
and correctly sends messages to processors in
P
at timeslot 0, but crashes immediately after doing so, without sending messages to any processor in
\Pi\setminus P
.

•

A set of
f-1
processors in
\Pi\setminus P
, other than the broadcaster, crash after correctly sending messages at timeslot 0.

•

All other processors act correctly.

We will show that all correct processors output
v
in
E
. The broadcaster’s input can therefore be computed from the messages sent by the broadcaster to processors in
P
at timeslot 0, simply by simulating
E
without knowledge of
v
.

Interpreting the theorem
. Protocols with 2-round finality, such as E-Minimmit, Hydrangea, and Kudzu, assume
n\geq 5f+1
and solve 2-round BB with
f^{*}=f
. When
n=5f+1
, Theorem
8.1
establishes that the broadcaster’s input can be recovered from the messages sent to any set of
n-2f-f^{*}+1=2f+2
processors, i.e., just over
2/5
ths of the total. If the broadcaster uses erasure coding and sends a single fragment to each processor, recovery from
2f+2
fragments requires a reconstruction parameter
k\leq 2f+2
, giving a data expansion rate of at least
n/k\geq(5f+1)/(2f+2)
, which approaches
2.5
as
f
grows. Note that E-Minimmit and Kudzu achieve data expansion rates of approximately
2.5
, showing that this bound is essentially tight.

10.
Related work

Classical Byzantine Consensus
. The study of
protocols for reaching consensus in the presence of Byzantine
faults was introduced by Lamport, Shostak, and
Pease
(
Lamport et al. 1982
)
, with a treatment of SMR given later by
Schneider
(
Schneider 1993
)
. Dwork, Lynch and
Stockmeyer
(
Dwork et al. 1988
)
showed that
n\geq 3f+1
is optimal for
partial synchrony. Standard protocols using this assumption, such
as PBFT
(
Castro et al. 1999
)
and
Tendermint
(
Buchman 2016
;
Buchman et al. 2018
)
, satisfy
3-round finality. As shown by
(
Abraham et al. 2021
)
, this is
optimal. More recently, Chan and
Pass
(
Chan and Pass 2023
)
introduced Simplex, a particularly
simple protocol for partial synchrony with rotating leaders and
two rounds of voting per view. Simplex achieves 3-round finality
with
n\geq 3f+1
and has a clean structure that lends itself
well to extension. Our protocols build directly on Simplex,
extending it with erasure coding, stable leaders, and the
mechanisms for pushing the data expansion rate towards
1

described in this paper.

Erasure coding in distributed protocols.

The use of erasure codes to reduce communication costs in
distributed protocols has a long history. Cachin and
Tessaro
(
Cachin and Tessaro 2005
)
introduced

asynchronous verifiable information dispersal (AVID)
,
combining erasure codes with Merkle trees to allow a broadcaster to
distribute data among
n
servers so that the data can be
recovered from any sufficiently large subset of fragments, with
each fragment verifiable against a commitment in the block
header. Our use of certified fragments follows this approach
directly. As detailed below, a number of other recent SMR protocols also make use of this approach.
The Pipes framework of Lewis-Pye, Nayak and
Shrestha
(
Lewis-Pye et al. 2025
)
analyses the throughput
implications of erasure coding in leader-based protocols, showing
that the data expansion rate directly governs the throughput
bottleneck. Our work builds on this line of research, establishing
that the
2.5
rate is optimal for 2-round finality and showing
how to circumvent this bound with 3-round finality.

Protocols with 2-round finality.

A long line of
work
(
Brasileiro et al. 2001
;
Friedman et al. 2005
;
Guerraoui and Vukolić 2007
;
Kursawe 2002
;
Martin and Alvisi 2006
;
Song and Van Renesse 2008
)

considers protocols with a ‘fast path’, allowing quick
finalisation under favourable conditions. FaB
(
Martin and Alvisi 2006
)

introduced a parameterised model with
n\geq 3f+2p+1

processors, achieving 2-round finality when at most
p
processors
are Byzantine. Kuznetsov et
al.
(
Kuznetsov et al. 2021
)
later showed that the optimal
bound is
n\geq 3f+2p-1
(see
also
(
Abraham et al. 2021
)
). Recent protocols in this family
include Minimmit and
E-Minimmit
(
Chou et al. 2025
)
,
Alpenglow
(
Kniep et al. 2025
)
,
Kudzu
(
Shoup et al. 2025
)
,
Hydrangea
(
Shrestha et al. 2025a
)
,
Banyan
(
Vonlanthen et al. 2024
)
, and
ChonkyBFT
(
França et al. 2025
)
. Among these,
E-Minimmit, Kudzu and Alpenglow incorporate erasure coding, achieving data
expansion rates of approximately
2.5
. Our impossibility result
(Section
8
) shows that this rate cannot be improved
for protocols with 2-round finality.

Optimistic proposals and stable leaders.

In rotating-leader protocols, a standard bottleneck is that the
leader of view
v+1
must wait for a certificate from view
v

before proposing. Moonshot
(
Doidge et al. 2024
)
introduced the
concept of
optimistic proposals
, in which the leader of view

v+1
proposes a block as soon as it receives the proposal for
view
v
, without waiting for the corresponding certificate.
Hydrangea++
(
Shrestha and Kate 2025
)
applies the same technique to
Hydrangea. In this paper, we combine optimistic proposals with
stable leaders
(where a single leader proposes
across multiple consecutive views) to allow the next leader to
begin proposing before the current superview has concluded,
eliminating the inter-superview gap.

DAG-based protocols.
It has often been observed
that low communication complexity does not necessarily translate
into high throughput in practice. Narwhal and
Tusk
(
Danezis et al. 2022
)
demonstrated that building on a
DAG-based dissemination layer, in which all processors share
responsibility for transmitting transactions, can yield
significantly higher throughput than leader-based approaches.
Narwhal further improves throughput at the systems level by
allowing each processor to employ multiple
worker nodes

for data transmission, effectively trading additional hardware
resources (CPUs and network bandwidth) for performance. While
increased bandwidth can be incorporated into our model, the use
of multiple CPUs per processor is not. Because data and protocol
messages are transmitted by many processors concurrently,
DAG-based protocols typically incur higher communication
complexity than their leader-based counterparts. Beyond serving
as a dissemination layer, a number of
works
(
Gągol and Świętek 2018
;
Keidar et al. 2021
;
Spiegelman et al. 2024
;
Shrestha et al. 2025b
;
Keidar et al. 2022
;
Babel et al. 2023
)

use the DAG structure itself to reach consensus. Early DAG-based
protocols suffered from high round-latency, and reducing this to
match leader-based protocols has been an active area of research.

11.
Discussion

We have shown that protocols with 2-round finality cannot
achieve a data expansion rate below
2.5
, and that protocols
with 3-round finality can push the data expansion rate
arbitrarily close to
1
. Two protocols realising this were
presented: Carnot 1, which assumes
n\geq 4f+1
and avoids any
extra fragment dissemination, and Carnot 2, which operates under
the optimal assumption
n\geq 3f+1
at the cost of additional
fragment dissemination when Byzantine processors interfere.

Several questions remain open. First, our impossibility result
applies to protocols with 2-round finality (one round of voting).
It would be interesting to establish tight bounds on the
achievable data expansion rate for protocols with 3-round finality
in the
worst case
. Carnot 1 achieves approximately
1.33
and
Carnot 2 achieves approximately
1.5
, but we do not know whether
these rates are optimal for their respective resilience
assumptions.

Second, while the Pipes model analysis of
Section
7
(to be added) provides formulas relating superview length,
erasure coding parameters, and throughput, an interesting
practical question is how to set these parameters dynamically in
realistic settings where network conditions and fault rates are in
flux. More broadly, one might wish to move between protocols with
2-round finality (which require fewer rounds of communication but a higher data
expansion rate) and 3-round finality (which can achieve lower data
expansion rates and thus higher throughput) depending on current
demand. Understanding how to smoothly transition between these
regimes is an appealing direction for future work.

Appendix B
Carnot 2: The intuition

In this appendix, we present Carnot 2, which operates under the optimal resilience assumption
n\geq 3f+1
. Much of the protocol is identical to Carnot 1. The key differences concern view progression, the use of threshold signatures for stage-1 votes, and the mechanism for ensuring data availability when the leader uses an aggressive erasure code.

Stage-1 certificates and view progression
. In Carnot 1, stage-1 votes do not use threshold signatures, and processors progress to the next view only upon receiving an M-certificate or an N-certificate. In Carnot 2, we use threshold signatures for stage-1 votes (formed from
n-f
signature shares, since
n\geq 3f+1
), allowing stage-1 notarisations to be compactly represented as stage-1
certificates
. These certificates are forwarded by processors upon receipt, just as M-certificates and N-certificates are.

View progression is then modified as follows. A processor
p_{i}
progresses to view
v+1
upon the earliest of the following events:

(a)

p_{i}
receives a stage-2 M-certificate for a view
v
block (as in Carnot 1), or;

(b)

p_{i}
receives a stage-1 certificate for a view
v
block
b
and successfully reconstructs the payload of
b
from certified fragments (or recovery fragments, described below), or;

(c)

p_{i}
receives an N-certificate for view
v
(as in Carnot 1).

In case (a), the M-certificate serves as proof of data availability, and no further action is needed. In case (c), the view is nullified and no data availability is required. Case (b) is the new case:
p_{i}
has reconstructed the payload but does not yet have proof that other correct processors can do so as well. In this case,
p_{i}
sets a timer and continues with other instructions (proposing and voting in subsequent views as normal). The timer does not impact the critical path during synchrony and when processors act correctly, and is only used to determine whether and when
p_{i}
should send recovery fragments. The choice of timing parameter is described in Appendix
C
.

If the timer expires and
p_{i}
has not yet received an M-certificate for
b
, then
p_{i}
sends
certified recovery fragments
of
b
to other processors. Upon receiving a recovery fragment together with the corresponding stage-1 certificate, a processor echoes its recovery fragment to all. This is analogous to the two types of extra fragment echoing described in Section
3.4
: (a)
p_{i}
may need to send a processor
p_{j}
their recovery fragment, and (b)
p_{j}
may subsequently echo their recovery fragment to all processors.

Recovery fragments when
k=n-f-1
. An important optimisation applies when the reconstruction parameter for the block is
k=n-f-1
. In this case, the primary
(n,n-f-1)
-encoding is already conservative enough for recovery when correct processors other than the leader echo their fragments. Certified recovery fragments are therefore
identical
to certified fragments. This means that
p_{i}
need not send a recovery fragment to any processor from whom it has already received a certified fragment, nor to any processor to whom it has already sent one, which may be every processor. In many cases, this results in no extra communication at all.

Recovery fragments when
k>n-f-1
: the verification problem
. When
k>n-f-1
, the recovery mechanism requires re-encoding the payload using the more conservative
(n,n-f-1)
-code. If done naively, this introduces a subtle issue concerning verifiability.

In the primary encoding, each fragment is accompanied by a Merkle proof against the Merkle root committed to in the block header, which is signed by the leader. This allows any processor to verify that a fragment is consistent with the block the leader proposed. Recovery fragments, however, belong to a
different
erasure coding of the same payload, with a different Merkle tree and a different root. A processor receiving a recovery fragment has no way to verify it against the block header, because the block header only contains the Merkle root for the primary
(n,k)
-encoding.

A naive approach therefore creates an opportunity for attack. A Byzantine processor could claim to have reconstructed the payload, produce recovery fragments for an arbitrary payload, and disseminate them. Correct processors, unable to verify these fragments, would echo them. While this does not violate consistency (a processor that reconstructs the payload can check it against the primary Merkle root, and no processor votes on the basis of recovery fragments when
k>n-f-1
), it allows Byzantine processors to cause correct processors to disseminate an arbitrary amount of data, which is undesirable.

Dual Merkle roots
. The solution is to have the leader commit to the recovery encoding at the time of proposal. Specifically, when proposing a block with reconstruction parameter
k>n-f-1
, the leader computes
two
erasure encodings of the payload: the primary
(n,k)
-encoding and a recovery
(n,n-f-1)
-encoding. The block header includes the Merkle roots of both encodings. Since the leader already has the full payload, this requires only additional computation (two erasure encodings, potentially computed in parallel, instead of one) and adds only a single hash to the block header, with no extra communication.

Recovery fragments can now be verified: a processor receiving a recovery fragment checks it against the recovery Merkle root in the block header, just as it would check a primary fragment against the primary Merkle root. Byzantine processors can no longer produce fake recovery fragments that will be accepted by correct processors.

Handling a Byzantine leader
. A Byzantine leader could commit to a bogus recovery Merkle root, one that does not correspond to the same payload as the primary encoding. To guard against this, a correct processor that reconstructs the payload from the primary encoding must verify the recovery Merkle root
before
adding the block to
\mathtt{blocks}
and progressing to the next view. It does so by re-encoding the payload using the
(n,n-f-1)
-code, computing the resulting Merkle root, and checking that it matches the one in the block header. If it does not match, the processor knows the leader is Byzantine: it does not add the block to
\mathtt{blocks}
, does not send a stage-2 vote, and instead nullifies the view.

Summary
. Carnot 2 extends Carnot 1 with stage-1 certificates and a recovery mechanism for ensuring data availability under the weaker assumption
n\geq 3f+1
. When a correct processor reconstructs the payload and progresses to the next view without an M-certificate, it sets a timer. If the timer expires without an M-certificate arriving, the processor produces certified recovery fragments and sends them to other processors, who echo them upon receipt. When
k=n-f-1
, recovery fragments coincide with primary fragments, limiting unnecessary communication. When
k>n-f-1
, a dual Merkle root mechanism ensures that recovery fragments are verifiable. Under standard operation with correct leaders, the M-certificate arrives before the timer expires, and no recovery fragments are produced.
