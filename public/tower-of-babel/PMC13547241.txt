Active-reactive power coordination for DC-interconnected microgrid clusters: a small-signal stability perspective
Authors: Leilei Chen, Yanyan Lin, Hongfei Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Unstable power exchange and voltage fluctuations at the PCC frequently compromise the operational stability of DC microgrid clusters. In response to these challenges, a coordinated control framework is developed wherein ICs actively cooperate with distributed sources within sub-microgrids. Distinct from conventional approaches, the proposed strategy enforces a global equilibrium for active power across the entire cluster, whereas reactive power regulation is confined to the local sub-microgrid level. To evaluate the system stability, a comprehensive small-signal model tailored to this specific control architecture is constructed. Simulation results under light-load, reactive heavy-load, and active heavy-load scenarios demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method. The findings underscore the capability of ICs to effectively track power flow targets without compromising the voltage stability at the PCC.

Introduction

With the depletion of fossil fuels and the increasing concerns regarding environmental degradation, DG technology, dominated by renewable energy sources, has received extensive attention. MG technology has become an effective approach for new energy utilization due to its ability to integrate distributed generation resources and local loads1–3. However, individual MGs face challenges such as limited capacity, weak regulation capability, and low redundancy4–6. The GMG formed by interconnecting multiple SMGs, can overcome these limitations and improve operational stability, reliability, and economy7–11.

When the GMG operates in grid-connected mode, energy exchange occurs simultaneously between the SMGs and the utility grid, as well as among the SMGs themselves. Conversely, in islanded mode, energy exchange is conducted solely among the SMGs to meet local power demand12. Energy interaction control is fundamental to the stability and efficiency of the GMG. It is highly complex, involving both short-term scheduling and real-time control13–16, and is closely related to the energy management strategy and interconnection topology.

From the perspective of topological structure, the GMG system is commonly composed of AC SMGs. The interconnection of AC GMGs can be classified into direct AC coupling or DC coupling. In the direct AC mode, SMGs with the same voltage level and frequency are directly connected to a common AC bus17,18. Alternatively, they can be connected through power electronic converters to form a controllable AC mode GMG19. Energy management in a direct AC mode GMG is relatively easier to implement20. However, the lack of ICs also makes tight electrical connections within the GMG, preventing effective physical isolation of electrical energy. Unbalanced power in one SMG affects other SMGs, causing fluctuations in frequency and voltage across all SMGs.

The DC-mode GMG connects its SMGs via AC-DC ICs, with DC tie-lines connecting the ICs21,22. The overall configuration is that AC power flows are confined within individual SMGs, whereas the tie-lines between the SMGs serve as DC links for power exchange. The coordinated control between ICs and SMGs is crucial for energy management in a GMG. Some studies employ droop control23,24 or constant power control25–27 to achieve this function.

Ref23 adopts V-I-f characteristic to couple the frequency deviation of the SMGs with the voltage variation of DC bus. Relying on the V-I-f characteristic, the active power is redistributed between SMGs. Though the adaptive droop control strategy enables the minimized frequency deviation of the AC system, the DC bus voltage undergoes unavoidable change during the process of frequency regulation. Similarly, Ref24 designs a P-F droop control scheme to determine the exchanged active power based on the frequency deviations across individual SMGs. The decentralized control for IC supports the primary frequency regulation, increasing the system reliability and reducing the required amount of spinning reserve. However, a centralized control for damping low-frequency oscillations is added by coordinating the injected reactive power, which results in widespread flow of reactive power across the GMGs.

Unlike droop control, constant power control aims to manage energy directly by regulating the exchanged power across the tie-lines. A multi-frequency controller for GMGs with different nominal frequencies is designed in Ref25. The frequency deviation of the SMGs is used to generate the exchanged power reference. By the proposed multi-frequency controller, the stability of the GMG is significantly improved. However, multi-frequency controller does not deal with the reactive power of the SMGs, causing the power through tie-line out of control when mutual power assistance is not required. Reference26 proposes a method for simultaneously detecting voltage deviations and frequency deviations in multiple independent microgrids. The detected voltage and frequency differences are used to determine the required active and reactive powers to be exchanged. This approach offers plug-and-play capability and good power sharing performance. However, it relies on logic control for detection to trigger the formation of a temporary interconnected microgrid, only suitable for emergency conditions. Moreover, it is unable to eliminate the voltage and frequency deviations within individual microgrids. In27, imbalanced power allocation across the global GMG is adopted to determine the exchanged power. This global control enables precise regulation of active and reactive power exchange, ensuring that voltage and frequency fluctuations within any SMG do not directly affect other SMGs. However, when a disturbance occurs on the grid side, the DC bus voltage deviation increases significantly, affecting the internal voltage of the connected SMGs.

Structure of the GMG

The topology of the studied GMG is illustrated in Fig. 1. The system is designed to operate in islanded mode. The GMG comprises multiple constituent microgrids, labeled as SMG A, SMG B, SMG C, etc., in the figure. In this paper, these interconnected microgrids are referred to as SMGs. Each SMG is an AC system containing DG units, such as wind turbines and photovoltaic panels, as well as energy storage units and local loads.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the islanded GMG topology.

Schematic diagram of the islanded GMG topology.

As shown in Fig. 1, adjacent SMGs are interconnected through AC/DC ICs. These converters link the internal AC bus of each SMG to a common DC network, facilitating flexible DC-based energy exchange among the sub-grids.

Since the islanded GMG is disconnected from the main utility grid, its voltage and frequency stability rely entirely on the autonomous regulation of local sources and the coordinated control of ICs. Therefore, establishing an effective power sharing mechanism through the DC links is a fundamental requirement for the resilient operation of the GMG.

Energy exchange control involves coordinated control both among SMGs and within each individual SMG. The scheduled output of DG units and the scheduled exchange power are determined during the day-ahead dispatching stage. These scheduled values are optimized considering load forecasting, power output forecasting, system security constraints, and power trading to ensure optimal economic operation and reliability. Therefore, the scheduled exchange power should be maintained stable and not fluctuate frequently during real-time operation.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the ports of the ICs are connected to the inter-SMG tie-lines and the common AC buses of the local SMGs. Consequently, the ICs possess the capability to participate in the reactive power regulation of the SMG to which they are connected. By adjusting the reactive power output of the ICs, the system can effectively assist the SMGs in voltage control.

In the following section, the specific control strategy for the IC, regarding its participation in both exchange power control and voltage support, will be elaborated.

Since the local DG reserve is insufficient, the ICs participate in reactive control as an auxiliary measure. In this state, all local DGs operate at unbalanced reactive power. The reactive power increment of the ICs is the difference between the unbalanced power and the DG reserve, which is added to the current value to form the reference:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}l} {Q_{{N,{\mathrm{G}}}} = \overline{Q}_{{N,{\mathrm{G}}}} } \hfill \\ {Q_{{N,{\mathrm{T}}}} = \sum\limits_{{i \in \widetilde{{\mathbf{I}}}_{N} }} {Q_{{N,{\mathrm{T}},0}}^{i} } - \left( {Q_{{N,{\mathrm{req}}}} - Q_{{N,{\mathrm{G}},{\mathrm{back}}}} } \right)} \hfill \\ \end{array} } \right.$$\end{document}

3) Overload: When the total reactive reserve of the SMG cannot meet the deficit, the SMG is in an overloaded state. To maintain voltage stability, load shedding is considered. In this case, all DGs and ICs in the SMG output their maximum reactive power:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{ \begin{gathered} Q_{{N,{\mathrm{G}}}} = \overline{Q}_{{N,{\mathrm{G}}}} \hfill \\ Q_{{N,{\mathrm{T}}}} = \overline{Q}_{{N,{\mathrm{T}}}} \hfill \\ \end{gathered} \right.$$\end{document}

The reactive-power feasibility conditions are governed by the apparent-power capacity limits of DGs and ICs, the available reactive reserves, and the equivalent reactive load-shedding bounds. The corresponding mathematical formulation is presented in Appendix F. With these constraints, the reactive overload state is explicitly associated with the shortage of feasible reactive-power support under the capacity limits.

Conclusion

In this paper, a coordinated control strategy for the DC-interconnected GMG is proposed to mitigate the issues of unstable exchange power among SMGs and voltage fluctuations at the PCC. The key conclusions are summarized as follows:The proposed strategy achieves mutual power support among SMGs through the coordinated control of DGs and ICs. Each SMG maintains its own frequency and voltage stability independently, ensuring that the power exchange via the DC tie-line is decoupled from local fluctuations.Under various operating conditions, such as load switching and generation tripping, the proposed strategy ensures that the DC tie-line power accurately tracks the specified value. This realizes the coordination between optimal dispatch and real-time operation, thereby contributing to the economic operation of the system.The strategy utilizes the reactive power capability of the ICs as a system-level reserve to participate in the voltage regulation of the SMGs. This coordination effectively reduces the demand for local reactive reserves within the SMGs and enhances overall voltage stability.A comprehensive small-signal state-space model of the DC-interconnected microgrid cluster is established. Eigenvalue analysis based on this model verifies that the proposed coordinated control strategy ensures sufficient damping and stability margins for the system, particularly under varying control parameters and operating conditions.
