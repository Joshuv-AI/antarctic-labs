Bone Marrow Stem Cell Exosomes Protect Lung Cells from Oxygen Damage by Regulating miR‐23a‐3p/Slc7a2 Pathway
Authors: Qifei He, Huaiwu Zheng, Junhao Xiong, Wei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveMesenchymal stem cell exosomes (MSC‐Exos) play crucial regulatory roles in the processes of alveolar epithelial cell development, proliferation, apoptosis and differentiation. The aim of this project was to explore the regulatory mechanism of the miR‐23a‐3p/Slc7a2 axis mediated by bone marrow MSC‐derived exosomes in hyperoxia‐induced injury to alveolar epithelial type II cells (AECIIs) in neonatal rats.MethodsThe miRBase and TargetScan databases were used to intersect and validate the differential expressions of microRNAs derived from BMSC exosomes in AECIIs under hyperoxic conditions and their target genes to construct in vitro and in vivo models of lung epithelial cell injury under hyperoxic conditions and to transfect BMSC‐Exos. Dual‐luciferase reporter assays were used to verify the binding relationship between miR‐23a‐3p and Slc7a2 and overexpression and siRNA plasmids for miR‐23a‐3p and Slc7a2 were constructed.ResultsmiR‐23a‐3p derived from BMSC exosomes directly targets Slc7a2 mRNA. miR‐23a‐3p antagonizes hyperoxia‐induced apoptosis of AECIIs and promotes cell proliferation in vitro by inhibiting Slc7a2 expression (p < 0.05). miR‐23a‐3p inhibited, while Slc7a2 promoted, the expression of ROS in AECIIs. miR‐23a‐3p significantly ameliorated hyperoxia‐induced lung tissue injury in neonatal rats and significantly reduced hyperoxia‐induced apoptosis in neonatal rat lung tissue (p < 0.05). The miR‐23a‐3p/Slc7a2 axis significantly regulates the expression of multiple biochemical markers in the supernatant of AECIIs and in the serum of neonatal rats under hyperoxia conditions.ConclusionmiR‐23a‐3p derived from BMSC exosomes participates in the repair of lung epithelial cell injury under hyperoxic conditions by regulating the expression of its target gene Slc7a2.Keywords: bronchopulmonary dysplasia, exosomes, hyperoxia, lung injury, mesenchymal stem cells, noncoding RNA

Introduction

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is among the most common and severe respiratory diseases in preterm infants, particularly those with very low birth weight. In recent years, with the improved success rate of rescuing very low birth weight and younger gestational age preterm infants, the incidence of BPD has been increasing and has become a long‐term risk factor affecting the quality of life of these infants [1]. In moderate to severe BPD, approximately 1/4 of infants develop pulmonary hypertension (PH) due to alveolar hypoxia, vascular cell dysfunction and inflammation.

Currently, there are no reliable therapeutic drugs for BPD and traditional treatment options are ineffective. However, evidence suggests that mesenchymal stem cell exosomes (MSC‐Exos) have advantages over mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) in treating BPD [2]. The composition of MSCs is complex and their safety and tumorigenicity are controversial. Moreover, multiple animal model studies have shown that the transplantation rate of MSCs is extremely low, limiting their therapeutic effects [3, 4]. Research has shown that MSCs function mainly through paracrine mechanisms and that mesenchymal stem cell exosomes (MSC‐Exos) may be the main carriers of their paracrine effects [5], with no safety concerns. Numerous studies have confirmed that the efficacy of MSC‐Exos in BPD models is comparable to or even better than that of MSCs [6, 7, 8]. Latest studies disclosed that the cell therapy holds potential to develop a new cure for diabetes mellitus based on the capability of bone marrow‐mesenchymal stem cells (BM‐MSCs) to generate β‐cell phenotype using specific protocol [9]. A study on lipoic acid improving wound healing through its immunomodulatory and anti‐inflammatory effects in a diabetic mouse model demonstrated that the beneficial role of lipoic acid in fine‐tuning the balance between anti‐inflammatory and pro‐inflammatory cytokines, influencing both their release and gene expression [10]. These suggest the superiority of exosome‐based paracrine therapy over direct stem cell transplantation.

Studies have shown that AECIIs play important roles in the pathogenesis of BPD. The depletion of AECIIs can lead to persistent lung injury in BPD [11, 12, 13]. Although literature shows that excessive or dysregulated AECII proliferation/transdifferentiation can also contribute to abnormal repair and fibrosis‐like changes in chronic BPD models [14, 15], in vitro experiments have shown that the simplification of the alveolar epithelium after high‐concentration oxygen treatment may be related to a decrease in AECII proliferation. MSC‐Exos contain abundant bioactive substances and play a key role in lung injury through the continuous transfer of miRNAs and related proteins [16, 17, 18]. miRNAs regulate mRNA translation or stability through gene cleavage or translation inhibition and have various biological functions. Numerous studies have confirmed that miRNAs play crucial regulatory roles in cell development, proliferation, apoptosis and differentiation during lung injury [19, 20]. However, there is currently no research on the mechanism by which microRNAs derived from MSC‐exosomes regulate hyperoxia‐induced AECII injury and BPD through the microRNA/target gene axis; only reports on miRNA mimics are available. A previous study has reported that MSC‐exo treatment decreased HYRX‐associated inflammation and changed the hyperoxic lung transcriptome, resulting in alleviation of HYRX‐induced BPD, improvement of lung function, decrease in fibrosis and pulmonary vascular remodeling and amelioration of pulmonary hypertension [8]. This suggests that the MSC‐exo mechanism of action is in relation to regulation of lung macrophage phenotype. Therefore, the aim of this project is to evaluate the differential expression of miRNAs derived from BMSC exosomes in AECIIs under hyperoxic conditions and their target genes and to elucidate the regulatory mechanism of the rno‐miR‐23a‐3p/Slc7a2 axis in BMSC exosomes under BPD conditions through in vitro and in vivo experiments.

Methods and Materials

Main Materials and Reagents

Rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (CP‐R131) and type II alveolar epithelial cells (AECIIs; CP‐R003) were purchased from Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology Co. Ltd. Complete medium for rat type II alveolar epithelial cells (CM‐R003) and medium for rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (MCM‐R131) were provided by Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology Co. Ltd. and Zhejiang Mingzhe Biotechnology Co. Ltd., respectively. Exosome‐free foetal bovine serum (EXO‐FBS‐50S‐1) was purchased from SERANA and foetal bovine serum (141215) was obtained from Hangzhou Tianhang Biotechnology Co. Ltd. GSTM Exosome Isolation Reagent (for cell culture media) (E3002) was purchased from Gisbio. The reactive oxygen species (ROS) probes DCFH‐DA (Beyotime, S0033) and Hoechst 33342 (Solarbio, B8040) were used in the experiments. A BCA protein concentration assay kit (AS1086, ASPEN) was used for protein quantification. The following ELISA kits were purchased from ELK Biotechnology Rat IL1b ELISA Kit (ELK1272), Rat MMP9 ELISA Kit (ELK2599), Rat TNF‐α ELISA Kit (ELK1396) and Rat VEGFA ELISA Kit (ELK2320). A malondialdehyde (MDA) test kit (A003‐1) and total superoxide dismutase (T‐SOD) assay kit (A001‐1) were obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute.

Cell Culture

Rat bone marrow‐mesenchymal stem cells and type II alveolar epithelial cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and maintained in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Animal Husbandry and Model Construction

Twenty‐six neonatal SD rats were randomly assigned to four experimental groups, with six animals per group. The groups included an air group, an air + miR‐23a‐3p agomir group, a hyperoxia group and a hyperoxia + miR‐23a‐3p agomir group. For subsequent sample collection, three rats were randomly chosen from each group at the end of the experiment. The first two groups were exposed to air and the latter two groups were exposed to 85% oxygen to construct the BPD model. In the hyperoxia and the hyperoxia + agomir groups, the SD neonatal rats were placed in an oxygen chamber with their mothers on the 4th day after birth and the oxygen and nitrogen ratio was adjusted through the oxygen valve to maintain an 85% oxygen concentration for 4 h per day for 7 days. The air group was exposed to a normoxic environment. The other experimental conditions were the same as those of the hyperoxia group. In the hyperoxia + miR‐23a‐3p agomir and air + miR‐23a‐3p agomir groups, SD neonatal rats were intraperitoneally injected with the agomir (5 nmol/rat/day) for seven consecutive days; the hyperoxia group was simultaneously subjected to hyperoxia injury. The sampling time was calculated from the 14th day after the end of hyperoxia induction, i.e., 4 days after birth + 7 days of hyperoxia induction + 14 days of normoxia. The approval number of the ethical approval for the animal study was (No. 2025838).

Animal Sampling and Detection

After anaesthesia with isoflurane, peripheral blood was collected from the neonatal rats, which were subsequently euthanized by cervical dislocation. Lung tissues were subsequently removed for the following analyses: HE staining to observe pathological changes, TUNEL fluorescence to detect apoptosis and immunohistochemistry to detect the protein expression levels of Ki‐67, Bcl‐2 and Bax. Moreover, Western blotting and PCR were used to detect the expression levels of miR‐23a‐3p and Slc7a2, respectively, in lung tissues. In addition, biochemical tests were performed on the peripheral blood of the neonatal rats to determine the levels of MDA and SOD and the expression levels of TNF‐α, IL‐1β, MMP9 and VEGF‐A were detected via ELISA.

Transcriptome Sequencing

Primary type II alveolar epithelial cells were divided into a blank group, exosome intervention group, hyperoxia group and hyperoxia + exosome group. The blank group was not treated. In the exosome intervention group, BMSC‐derived exosomes (20 μg/mL) were added to the cell culture medium for intervention. According to the model construction method of our previous research [21], the hyperoxia group was placed in a sealed oxygen chamber with an oxygen content of 85% for 6 h and then cultured in a normal oxygen content 5% CO2 incubator for 48 h. The hyperoxia + exosome group was treated the same as the hyperoxia group, with the addition of BMSC‐derived exosomes (20 μg/mL). After 48 h, cell samples from each group were collected for transcriptome sequencing. The exosomes were stained, reacted, terminated and extracted, after which RNA was extracted for miRNA sequencing.

Exosome Transfection

In our previous research, we conducted a thorough characterization of the BMSC‐derived exosomes including CD9, CD63 [21]. In accordance with previous methods [17], the extracted exosomes were transfected at a ratio of 5 μL of exosomes per 2 mL of cells. After 24 h of transfection, Hoechst was added and the cells were incubated for 4–6 h, after which the nuclear activity and exosome endocytosis were observed by fluorescence.

Cell Phalloidin‐AF488 Staining to detect Exosome Endocytosis

The cell supernatant was aspirated and the wells were subsequently washed three times with PBS and dried. Then, 4% paraformaldehyde was added to fix the cells for 20 min. After the fixative was removed, the cells were washed three times with PBS and dried. The residual water and cells were wiped off with a cotton swab. A histochemical pen was used to draw circles to reduce the amount of DAPI and phalloidin. The phalloidin stock solution (1 mg/mL BSA + PBS, 1:100) was diluted and stored in the dark at 4°C. Phalloidin‐AF488 was added to the centre of the circle and incubated at 37°C for 1 h, after which the cells were washed three times and dried. Afterwards, DAPI working solution was added and the samples were incubated at room temperature for 30 min, after which they were washed three times and dried. Finally, images were taken with a laser confocal microscope.

Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay

Wild‐type and mutant miR‐target gene sequence plasmids were constructed and the miR‐target gene sequence plasmids were cotransfected with mimics/NC into rat microglia. After transfection, the cells were cultured for an appropriate time to ensure full expression of the luciferase gene. A luciferase assay instrument was used to detect dual‐luciferase activity to determine the binding site and regulatory effect of the miRNA and the target gene. The following primer sequences were used: GAPDH: F‐AGACAGCCGCATCTTCTTGT, R‐TGATGGCAACAATGTCCACT; Slc7a2: F‐CAAGTGCCAGAGAACCACCT, R‐GCAGTCAAAGCCCACAAAGG.

Plasmid Construction and Transfection

A pcDNA‐Slc7a2 overexpression plasmid was constructed: the Slc7a2 gene was amplified by PCR, after which the PCR product and the pcDNA3.1(+) vector were double‐digested with restriction enzymes. After the Slc7a2 gene was ligated into the vector, the ligation product was transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α. Positive clones were screened by PCR verification and then large‐scale bacterial culture was performed to extract plasmid DNA to obtain sufficient plasmids for the transfection experiments.

siRNA plasmids were constructed: specific siRNA sequences were designed on the basis of the Slc7a2 gene sequence and an siRNA expression vector was constructed. The sense and antisense oligonucleotides of siRNA were synthesized and after annealing to form double‐stranded siRNA, they were cloned and inserted into the siRNA expression vector. The constructed siRNA plasmid was transformed into E. coli and large‐scale culture was performed to extract plasmid DNA. When the target cells reached the appropriate transfection density, Lipofectamine transfection reagent was selected and the plasmid DNA was mixed with the transfection reagent according to the instructions and added to the cell culture dish for transfection. After transfection, the cells were cultured to ensure full expression of the plasmid.

Flow Cytometry Detection of Apoptosis

After the AECIIs were digested with trypsin without EDTA, the cells were collected by centrifugation at 300 ×g for 5 min at 4°C. The cells were subsequently washed twice with precooled PBS, resuspended in 1 mL of PBS each time and centrifuged at 300 ×g for 5 min at 4°C. The cell pellet was resuspended in 100 μL of 1 × binding buffer. Next, 5 μL of Annexin V‐FITC and 5 μL of PI were added, mixed and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. The cells were subsequently washed twice with precooled 1 × binding buffer. Finally, the cells were resuspended in 500 μL of 1 × binding buffer, filtered into a flow tube and tested within 1 h.

DCFH‐DA Method for Detecting Intracellular ROS Expression

The cells to be tested were diluted with PBS and the DCFH‐DA working solution was prepared at a ratio of 1:1000 and preheated. The cells were incubated in a 37°C water bath in the dark for 30 min and then washed three times with PBS, after which Hoechst was added to stain the nuclei. The cells were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 20–30 min, rinsed with PBS and observed and photographed under a fluorescence microscope as soon as possible.

ELISA Detection

After the cell supernatant and neonatal rats lung blood samples were collected, they were centrifuged at 2000 ×g for 10 min at 4°C, after which the supernatant was collected. Then, according to the kit instructions, the expression levels of IL‐1β, MMP9, TNF‐α, VEGFA, MDA and SOD in the cell supernatant and neonatal rats lung tissue were detected.

Haematoxylin and Eosin (HE) Staining

Neonatal rats' lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for more than 24 h, followed by dehydration, paraffin embedding and sectioning at a thickness of 2–3 μm. After the paraffin sections were dewaxed in water, the nuclei were stained with haematoxylin for 1–5 min, washed with tap water, differentiated with 1% hydrochloric acid and blued with 1% ammonia water. The sections were then stained with eosin for 1–3 min, followed by dehydration, mounting and microscopic examination.

TUNEL Fluorescence Staining

Neonatal rats lung paraffin sections were dewaxed in water. Protease K from the kit was diluted 1:9 with PBS to prepare the working solution, incubated at 37°C for 20 min and then washed with PBS three times for 5 min each. TdT enzyme, CF488‐dUTP green reaction solution and EB equilibrium buffer from the kit were mixed at a ratio of 2:5:50 and incubated overnight at 4°C in the dark. After the cells were washed with PBS three times for 5 min each, DAPI was added to stain the nuclei and the cells were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 20–30 min and then washed with PBS. The sections were mounted with anti‐fade mounting medium and observed under a microscope.

Immunohistochemical Staining

Neonatal rats paraffin sections were dewaxed in water and placed in 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for antigen retrieval in a microwave. The sections were incubated with 3% H2O2 at room temperature for 15–30 min and then washed with PBS three times for 5 min each. Antibodies were diluted with 5% BSA and an appropriate amount of primary antibody working solution (Bax, Bcl‐2 and Ki67) was added and incubated overnight at 4°C. Secondary antibody working solution was added and the samples were incubated at 37°C for 30 min and then washed with PBS three times for 5 min each. DAB working solution was prepared and colour development was observed under a microscope, with positive results appearing brown–yellow. Nuclei were stained with haematoxylin for 1–5 min, differentiated with 1% hydrochloric acid, washed with tap water, blued with 1% ammonia water, washed with tap water, dehydrated, mounted and examined under a microscope.

Western Blotting

Total cellular proteins were extracted from treated cells using RIPA lysis buffer and protein concentrations were measured using a BCA protein assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. For each sample, equal amounts of total protein (40 μg per well) were mixed with 5× protein loading buffer, denatured by boiling at 95°C–100°C for 5 min and then cooled on ice immediately.

Subsequently, protein samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) at a constant voltage, followed by wet transfer onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. After transfer, the membranes were blocked with 5% non‐fat dry milk diluted in TBST buffer at room temperature for 1 h to block non‐specific binding sites. For target protein detection, membranes were incubated with corresponding primary antibodies overnight at 4°C with gentle shaking. After thorough washing with TBST three times, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 30 min.

Finally, protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection reagent and signal exposure was performed in a darkroom with X‐ray films. The exposed films were scanned and digitized and the relative optical density of target protein bands was quantitatively analysed using AlphaEaseFC software. The relative protein expression level was normalized to the internal reference protein (β‐actin) for statistical comparison.

Quantitative Real‐Time PCR

Total cellular RNA containing endogenous miRNA was isolated from experimental cells using TRIpure reagent combined with chloroform extraction. Briefly, cell samples were fully lysed with 1 mL TRIpure solution and 250 μL chloroform was added for stratified extraction. After vigorous mixing and standing, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 10 min at 4°C to separate the aqueous phase containing total RNA. The supernatant was collected and RNA precipitation was performed with pre‐cooled isopropanol, followed by washing with 75% pre‐cooled ethanol to remove impurities and residual salts. The purified total RNA was air‐dried and dissolved in 100 μL RNase‐free water.

Different from conventional mRNA reverse transcription, specific stem‐loop primer‐based reverse transcription was adopted for miRNA cDNA synthesis to ensure specific amplification of mature miRNA. First‐strand cDNA synthesis was performed using EntiLink 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Super Mix (ELK Biotechnology, EQ 031) following the standard protocol for miRNA reverse transcription. Statistical Analysis

All the data were processed using SPSS 26.0 software and are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Differences between two groups were compared using independent sample t‐tests and differences among multiple groups were compared using one‐way ANOVA, with multiple comparisons performed using the LSD method. A p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Exosome Uptake Experiment and miRNA Sequencing

Compared with AECIIs in the blank control group (Figure 1A), most of the BMSC‐derived exosomes were taken up by AECIIs 24 h after transfection, allowing for subsequent microRNA sequencing and target gene prediction.

Cytoskeleton was labelled with green fluorescent probe and exosomes were pre‐stained with PKH26 (red fluorescence). Scale bar = 20 μm. Through gene sequencing, we identified differentially expressed miRNAs mediated by BMSC‐derived exosomes, including rno‐miR‐23a‐3p, rno‐miR‐34a‐5p, rno‐miR‐22‐3p and rno‐miR‐152‐3p. Further intersection validation using the miRBase and TargetScan databases revealed that Slc7a2 might be a target gene for multiple miRNAs, such as rno‐miR‐23a‐3p and rno‐miR‐34a‐5p. On the basis of previous research findings (Table S1), this project primarily explored the rno‐miR‐23a‐3p/Slc7a2 mechanism axis. We found (Figure 1B) that after BMSC‐Exosome intervention, the expression of Slc7a2 in rat AECIIs was significantly lower than that in the nonintervention group (p < 0.0001).

Dual‐luciferase binding experiments verified the binding of rno‐miR‐23a‐3p to Slc7a2, revealing that the relative fluorescence value of the binding of the miR‐23a‐3p mimics to wild‐type Slc7a2 was the lowest (Figure 1C) and was significantly lower than that of the other groups (p < 0.0001), indicating a strong targeted binding effect between miR‐23a‐3p and Slc7a2 mRNA.

In Vitro Functional Validation of miR‐23a‐3p and Its Target Gene Slc7a2

We constructed an exosome‐derived miRNA in vitro expression system containing miR‐23a‐3p mimics and miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor plasmids, which were transfected into AECIIs. PCR detection of miR‐23a‐3p expression levels revealed that the miR‐23a‐3p mimic significantly promoted miR‐23a‐3p expression, whereas the miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor significantly downregulated its mRNA expression (Figure 2A), confirming successful plasmid transfection.

We subsequently established a hyperoxia‐induced AECII injury model and constructed Slc7a2 overexpression plasmids and shRNA knockdown vectors. After transfection into AECIIs, Slc7a2 expression was detected by PCR and WB. The results (Figure 2B and Figure 2D–E) revealed that Slc7a2 overexpression significantly increased Slc7a2 expression in hyperoxia‐induced AECII cells, whereas Slc7a2 siRNA significantly reduced its expression (p < 0.05).

After transfection of the miR‐23a‐3p mimics and the miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor into AECIIs, the miR‐23a‐3p mimics significantly reduced Slc7a2 expression and the miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor significantly promoted Slc7a2 expression compared with the control group (p < 0.05). Cotransfection of miR‐23a‐3p mimics and pcDNA‐Slc7a2 plasmids into AECIIs resulted in significantly higher Slc7a2 mRNA expression than that in cells transfected with miR‐23a‐3p mimics alone; this expression was still lower than that in the NC control group (Figure 2C). Additionally, after cells were cotransfected with the miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor and si‐Slc7a2 plasmids, Slc7a2 expression was significantly greater than that in the NC control group but significantly lower than that in the group treated with the miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor alone (p < 0.05). These results further confirm the regulatory role of miR‐23a‐3p on Slc7a2.

miR‐23a‐3p Antagonized Hyperoxia‐Induced AECII Cell Apoptosis and Promoted In Vitro Cell Proliferation by Inhibiting Slc7a2 Expression

As shown in Figure 2F, the results of the CCK‐8 assay indicated that after 72 h of treatment, compared with the miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor, the miR‐23a‐3p mimic significantly promoted the proliferation of hyperoxia‐induced AECIIs. Compared with si‐Slc7a2, pcDNA‐Slc7a2 significantly reduced the proliferation rate of hyperoxia‐induced AECIIs. The proliferation rate of cells in the miR‐23a‐3p mimic combined with pcDNA‐Slc7a2 group was lower than that in the miR‐23a‐3p mimic group but higher than that in the pcDNA‐Slc7a2 group. The proliferation rate of cells in the miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor combined with si‐Slc7a2 group was greater than that in both the miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor group and the si‐Slc7a2 group.

The apoptosis rate of the miR‐23a‐3p mimic‐treated cells was significantly lower than that of the miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor‐treated cells (p < 0.05), whereas the apoptosis rate of the pcDNA‐Slc7a2‐treated cells was significantly greater than that of the si‐Slc7a2‐treated cells (p < 0.05). The apoptosis rate of the miR‐23a‐3p mimics combined with pcDNA‐Slc7a2 treatment group was higher than that of the miR‐23a‐3p mimics group but lower than that of the pcDNA‐Slc7a2 group. The apoptosis rate of the miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor combined with si‐Slc7a2 treatment group was lower than that of the miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor group but higher than that of the si‐Slc7a2 group (see Figure 3A and Table S2a).

miR‐23a‐3p Expression Inhibited ROS Expression whereas Slc7a2 Promoted ROS Expression in AECIIs

The results of the DCFH‐DA assay (Figure 3B–C) revealed that the level of ROS in the miR‐23a‐3p mimic and si‐Slc7a2‐treated cells was significantly lower than that in the NC control cells (p < 0.05). Moreover, the ROS content in the si‐Slc7a2‐treated cells was even lower than that in the cells treated with the miR‐23a‐3p mimics (p < 0.05). Conversely, ROS expression was significantly higher in the pcDNA‐Slc7a2‐treated cells compared with the miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor‐treated cells (p < 0.05) and the cells in the NC control group (p < 0.05). The ROS content in the cells treated with the miR‐23a‐3p mimic combined with pcDNA‐Slc7a2 was higher than that in cells treated with the miR‐23a‐3p mimic alone (p < 0.05) but lower than that in the pcDNA‐Slc7a2 treatment group (p < 0.05). The level of ROS in cells treated with the miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor combined with si‐Slc7a2 was lower than that in cells treated with the miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor alone but higher than that in cells in the si‐Slc7a2 group (p < 0.05). Specific statistical data are shown in Table S2b.

The miR‐23a‐3p/Slc7a2 Axis Significantly Regulates the Expression of Multiple Biochemical Markers in Hyperoxia‐Induced AECIIs

As shown in Figure 4, compared with those in the NC control group, the expression levels of TNF‐α, IL‐1β, MMP‐9 and MDA in hyperoxia‐induced AECIIs treated with pcDNA‐Slc7a2 and the miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor significantly increased, whereas the expression levels of VEGFA and SOD significantly decreased (p < 0.05). This phenomenon could be reversed by treatment with si‐Slc7a2 or miR‐23a‐3p mimics (p < 0.05). However, the combined treatment of miR‐23a‐3p mimics with pcDNA‐Slc7a2 or the miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor with si‐Slc7a2 reversed the effects of the individual treatments (p < 0.05).

miR‐23a‐3p Significantly Ameliorated Hyperoxia‐Induced Lung Tissue Injury in Neonatal Rats

The body weight and mortality of neonatal rats were recorded at 4 days postbirth + 7 days of hyperoxia‐induced injury + 14 days of normoxia and the data were plotted. No deaths occurred during the experiment. Our results revealed that the body weight of hyperoxia‐treated neonatal rats was significantly lower than that of air‐treated rats. After the hyperoxia and air‐induced neonatal rats were treated with the miR‐23a‐3p agonist agomiR, we found that the miR‐23a‐3p agomiR significantly improved hyperoxia‐induced weight loss but did not significantly affect air‐induced weight loss, as shown in Figure 5A.

(A) Changes in the body weight of neonatal rats. (B) HE staining of rat lung tissue. (C) Comparison of the mean linear intercept (MLI) and radial alveolar count (RAC) in rat lung tissue. *p < 0.05.

HE staining of lung tissue from neonatal rats (Figure 5B) revealed that the lung tissue morphology in the air group was essentially normal, with a uniform alveolar size, occasional alveolar wall rupture, clear alveolar septal structure and a small amount of red blood cell and inflammatory cell infiltration. The lung tissue morphology in the miR‐23a‐3p agomiR group was also essentially normal, with a uniform alveolar size, clear alveolar septal structure and a small amount of red blood cell infiltration. In the hyperoxia group, the alveolar septa were significantly thinner and the alveoli were significantly enlarged (p < 0.05); i.e., the mean linear intercept (MLI) increased (Figure 5C) and the number of alveolar ruptures significantly increased (p < 0.05), i.e., the radial alveolar count (RAC) significantly decreased (Figure 5C), with a small amount of inflammatory cell infiltration and red blood cell accumulation in the lung tissue. Compared with the C group, the alveolar septa in the hyperoxia + miR‐23a‐3p group were thinner, the alveoli were enlarged, the MLI decreased and some alveoli ruptured (p < 0.05); moreover, compared with the control group, the RAC in the hyperoxia + miR‐23a‐3p group increased and there was a small amount of inflammatory cell and red blood cell infiltration in the lung tissue.

miR‐23a‐3p Significantly Reduced Hyperoxia‐Induced Apoptosis in Neonatal Rats Lung Tissue

As shown in Figure 6A–B, compared with the normoxia‐treated model, hyperoxia stimulation led to a significant increase in apoptosis in rat lung tissue cells (p < 0.05) and treatment with the miR‐23a‐3p agomiR significantly reduced hyperoxia‐induced apoptosis in rat lung tissue cells (p < 0.05). Additionally, the miR‐23a‐3p agomiR also significantly reduced apoptosis in rat lung tissue cells under normoxia, indicating that miR‐23a‐3p significantly protected lung tissue cells from death.

Discussion

Alveolar simplification and pulmonary microvascular dysplasia are the main pathological features of new BPD. Alveolar type II epithelial cells (AECIIs) are alveolar epithelial progenitor cells and AECIIs can proliferate into new AECIIs and transdifferentiate into AECI. Exposing neonatal rats to a hyperoxic environment can simulate the pathological characteristics of BPD [22]. These characteristics include AECII depletion and apoptosis [23], necrosis caused by high levels of reactive oxygen species [24], inhibition of AECII proliferation and DNA damage [25]. AECII depletion can lead to persistent lung injury in patients with BPD. Our results revealed that the body weight of neonatal rats exposed to hyperoxia was significantly lower than that of rats exposed to normoxia and pathological results revealed that hyperoxia exposure led to significantly thinner alveolar septa, an increased mean linear intercept (MLI), an increased number of alveolar ruptures and a decreased radial alveolar count (RAC). These findings further confirm that hyperoxia can cause significant lung tissue injury.

Studies have shown that MSC‐Exos contain a large number of bioactive substances and play a major role through the continuous transfer of miRNAs and related proteins. miRNAs can regulate mRNA translation or stability through gene cleavage or translation inhibition and play important roles in biological growth and development, cell proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation, tissue differentiation and disease occurrence. We previously isolated, extracted and identified BMSC‐derived exosomes [21]. In this study, we performed miRNA sequencing on exosomes and compared the sequencing results through bioinformatics analysis, screening out differentially expressed miR‐23a‐3p and its target gene Slc7a2 and explored the possible anti‐apoptotic, pro‐proliferative and immunomodulatory effects of miR‐23a‐3p/Slc7a2 on hyperoxia‐induced AECIIs, as well as the possible mechanisms underlying the promotion of lung epithelial cell development and tissue repair in a hyperoxia‐induced rat BPD model.

In our current study, BMSC‐derived exosomes were internalized by rat AECIIs under in vitro conditions (Figure S1). Following the endocytosis of BMSC‐derived exosomes by AECIIs, the expression of Slc7a2 in the cells was significantly reduced, indicating that miR‐23a‐3p in the cells could regulate the transcription of Slc7a2. We further verified the strong binding effect of miR‐23a‐3p with Slc7a2 mRNA through dual‐luciferase reporter assays. In the in vitro functional validation experiments, we established a hyperoxia‐induced AECII injury model. The results revealed that transfection of the miR‐23a‐3p mimic significantly reduced Slc7a2 expression, whereas transfection of the miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor significantly promoted Slc7a2 expression. After cotransfection of miR‐23a‐3p mimics with the pcDNA‐Slc7a2 plasmid into AECIIs, the mRNA expression of Slc7a2 significantly increased but remained below normal levels. Conversely, cotransfection of the miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor with the si‐Slc7a2 plasmid promoted an increase in Slc7a2 expression, but this increase was significantly lower than that in the miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor treatment group. These findings indicate that the expression of Slc7a2, a downstream target gene of miR‐23a‐3p, is regulated by miR‐23a‐3p.

In lung diseases such as ALI, it has been reported that miR‐214, miR‐16, miR‐377‐3p, miR‐30b‐3p and miR‐342‐5p have pro‐proliferative, anti‐apoptotic, immune factor regulatory and lung tissue repair effects on AECIIs and ALI, providing substantial evidence that miRNAs play a regulatory role in lung injury. However, the mechanism through which miR‐23a‐3p affects hyperoxia‐induced lung injury remains unclear. Analysis of published literature revealed that the role of miR‐23a‐3p in pathological changes such as acute lung injury is dualistic. Many studies suggest that miR‐23a‐3p can promote acute lung injury induced by various factors through a series of mechanisms [26]. In acute lung injury models, mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)‐derived exosomes can deliver miR‐182‐5p and miR‐23a‐3p to LPS‐treated MLE‐12 cells, with these two miRNAs targeting Ikbkb and Usp5, respectively. Usp5 interacts with IKKβ, hindering its ubiquitination. The coinhibition of miR‐182‐5p and miR‐23a‐3p alleviates the suppression of EMT processes in LPS‐treated MLE‐12 cells and LPS‐injured mouse lungs by MSCs [27]. Recent studies have shown that macrophage‐derived extracellular vesicles packaged with miR‐23a‐3p can impair the maintenance and angiogenic capacity of human endothelial progenitor cells in neonatal hyperoxia‐induced lung injury [28]. In fact, the apparent contradiction likely arises from differences in the extracellular vesicle cargo, the target cell type (endothelial progenitor cells vs. alveolar epithelial cells) and the microenvironmental context. Thus, we should clarify that the protective effect we observed in BMSC‐derived exosomes does not negate the detrimental effect of macrophage‐derived miR‐23a‐3p, but rather highlights the context‐dependent nature of miRNA functions.

Previous study reported that miR‐23a‐3p promoted M1 polarization of macrophages by regulating the PLK1/STAT1/STAT3 signaling pathway and aggravated lipopolysaccharide (LPS)‐induced acute lung injury [26]. However, some studies have indicated that miR‐23a‐3p alleviates cigarette smoke extract‐induced apoptosis in pulmonary vascular endothelial cells by targeting DnaJ homologue subfamily B member 1 (DNAJB1) [29]. Recent research has shown that increasing the number of alveolar macrophage‐derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) packaged with miRNA‐23a‐3p can target ACSL4, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis in mouse lung epithelial cells and alleviating lung injury [30]. These findings suggest that miR‐23a‐3p can also mitigate lung injury caused by various stimuli through the regulation of target genes.

We propose that the protective effect of BMSC‐exosomal miR‐23a‐3p likely arises from its cellular origin (mesenchymal stem cells vs. macrophages), the specific target gene (Slc7a2) we identified and the context‐dependent regulation of reactive oxygen species and apoptosis. We also emphasize that exosomal delivery may provide a more targeted and controlled modulation compared to endogenous or macrophage‐derived miR‐23a‐3p. This addition clarifies why miR‐23a‐3p from BMSC exosomes can be protective despite its pro‐injury potential in other settings.

In this study, our in vitro and in vivo functional validation of miR‐23a‐3p and its target gene Slc7a2 demonstrated that miR‐23a‐3p antagonizes hyperoxia‐induced AECII apoptosis and promotes in vitro cell proliferation by inhibiting Slc7a2 expression. Analysis of the effects of miR‐23a‐3p mimics combined with pcDNA‐Slc7a2 or a miR‐23a‐3p inhibitor combined with si‐Slc7a2 on cell proliferation or apoptosis revealed that miR‐23a‐3p acts as a protective factor, promoting hyperoxia‐induced alveolar epithelial cell proliferation and reducing apoptosis, likely through regulating the target gene Slc7a2. This finding was further confirmed by the effects of miR‐23a‐3p and Slc7a2 on intracellular ROS levels, which revealed that miR‐23a‐3p inhibits, while Slc7a2 promotes, ROS expression in AECIIs. However, multiple studies show miR‐23a‐3p can promote acute lung injury by driving macrophage M1 polarization and aggravating inflammation in LPS models, indicating context‐dependent (hyperoxia vs. LPS) or cell‐type‐specific (epithelial vs. macrophage) roles [26]. Broader literature has shown that Slc7a2 deficiency protects against hyperoxia‐induced lung injury by reducing nitric oxide‐related damage, supporting rather than contradicting the protective mechanism. Our previous research has shown that an appropriate amount of NO is beneficial to lung tissue, but hyperoxia can lead to excessive NO production. Excessive NO can cause damage to alveolar epithelial cells, etc. slc7a2 can play a role in adjusting the amount of NO, thereby exerting a protective effect on the lung epithelium [14].

Furthermore, the literature indeed suggests that uncontrolled or dysregulated AECII proliferation/transdifferentiation can contribute to fibrosis‐like remodeling in chronic BPD models [14, 15]. While our findings support the protective role of miR‐23a‐3p in acute hyperoxia‐induced injury, caution is warranted regarding long‐term outcomes and further studies are needed to assess the safety of sustained AECII proliferation.

Additionally, the miR‐23a‐3p/Slc7a2 axis regulates the expression of various biochemical markers (such as TNF‐α, IL‐1β, MMP‐9, MDA, VEGFA and SOD) in hyperoxia‐induced AECIIs. The inhibition of miR‐23a‐3p or the upregulation of Slc7a2 expression increased the levels of TNF‐α, IL‐1β, MMP‐9 and MDA in hyperoxia‐induced AECIIs but decreased VEGFA and SOD levels. Upregulation of miR‐23a‐3p expression or knockdown of Slc7a2 expression significantly reversed these effects and combined treatment with miR‐23a‐3p mimics and Slc7a2 overexpression or miR‐23a‐3p inhibition with Slc7a2 knockdown antagonized the effects of individual treatments.

The present study shows a regulatory link between miR‐23a‐3p and Slc7a2, but the downstream signaling mechanisms are still unclear. Prior research has validated the PI3K/Akt pathway as a master protective cascade against hyperoxic AECII apoptosis [31] and we speculate that the miR‐23a‐3p/Slc7a2 axis exerts its pro‐survival effects partially through activating PI3K/Akt phosphorylation. Slc7a2 overexpression may suppress Akt phosphorylation by enhancing oxidative stress‐mediated PTEN activation, whereas miR‐23a‐3p silencing Slc7a2 relieves PTEN inhibition and restores PI3K/Akt signaling to suppress intrinsic apoptosis. Emerging evidence ties hyperoxic lung injury to ferroptosis, a non‐apoptotic iron‐dependent cell death modality driven by ACSL4 and lipid peroxide buildup [32]. Although our current dataset lacks measurements of ferroptosis markers including ACSL4, GPX4 and labile iron pools, macrophage EV‐carried miR‐23a‐3p was reported to repress ACSL4 to block ferroptosis in lung epithelial cells [33], creating a plausible untested downstream branch for our axis. Elevated Slc7a2 may upregulate ACSL4 expression to sensitize AECIIs to ferroptosis under hyperoxia, while exosomal miR‐23a‐3p reverses this ferroptotic priming via Slc7a2 suppression. Future mechanistic extensions will address these gaps by detecting Nrf2/HO‐1 antioxidant signaling, mitochondrial membrane potential, phosphorylated PI3K/Akt and core ferroptosis regulators to fully unpack the multi‐layered transduction network downstream of miR‐23a‐3p/Slc7a2.

Previous studies have shown that hydrogen can protect AECIIs from hyperoxia‐induced apoptosis by inhibiting proapoptotic factors and promoting anti‐apoptotic factors [34]. This protective effect is achieved through the activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway, indicating that improving lung tissue injury can be accomplished by regulating the rate of apoptosis. Numerous studies have shown that MSC‐Exos enriched with miR‐30b‐3p effectively inhibit LPS‐induced apoptosis of AECIIs in acute lung injury (ALI) conditions by downregulating the expression of the miR‐30b‐3p target gene SAA3 and promoting their proliferation [35]. Research on LPS‐induced ALI models revealed that after MSC‐Exos intervention, the proliferation of AECIIs (MLE‐12 cells) gradually increased and the expression of AECII immune factors (TNF‐α, IL‐6 and IL‐10) decreased [36]. miR‐16 levels and SP‐A fluorescence were significantly suppressed by hyperoxia and the transfection of AECII cells with miR‐16 mimics increased SP‐A fluorescence in hyperoxia‐treated AECII cells, significantly reversing the hyperoxia‐induced reduction in cell proliferation and inhibiting hyperoxia‐induced apoptosis. The results of this in vivo study also revealed that after treatment with a miR‐23a‐3p agomiR in hyperoxia‐induced neonatal rats, compared with hyperoxic conditions, the miR‐23a‐3p agomiR significantly improved hyperoxia‐induced weight loss in rats, with thinning of the alveolar septa, enlarged alveoli, a reduced MLI and improved conditions. When the RAC increased, the proportion of apoptotic cells significantly decreased. Additionally, miR‐23a‐3p agomiR treatment significantly reversed the hyperoxia‐induced increase in Bax expression and the decrease in Bcl‐2 and Ki‐67 expression in rat lung tissues. These findings indicate that miR‐23a‐3p can significantly protect lung tissue cells from death by regulating the expression of apoptosis‐related proteins.

As mentioned above, miR‐23a‐3p exerts anti‐apoptotic and pro‐proliferative biological effects in vitro through the regulation of the target gene Slc7a2. Our in vivo study results in rat also revealed that the activation of miR‐23a‐3p expression significantly inhibited the hyperoxia‐induced high expression levels of TNF‐α, IL‐1β, MMP‐9 and MDA and the low expression levels of VEGFA and SOD in rat lung tissue. However, we found that the inhibitory effect of the miR‐23a‐3p agomiR was weaker in the hyperoxia model than in the normoxic (air) + miR‐23a‐3p agomiR‐treated model, possibly because the miR‐23a‐3p content in the lung tissues of hyperoxia + miR‐23a‐3p agomiR‐treated rat was lower than that in air + miR‐23a‐3p agomiR‐treated rat. These findings suggest that protection against hyperoxia‐induced lung tissue injury depends mainly on the expression level of miR‐23a‐3p in vivo, with higher expression levels leading to stronger protective effects.

Further experiments revealed that hyperoxia stimulation significantly promoted the expression of Slc7a2 mRNA and protein in rat lung tissue. Treatment with a miR‐23a‐3p agomiR significantly reduced the expression of Slc7a2. However, the expression of Slc7a2 in the lung tissue of rat treated with hyperoxia + a miR‐23a‐3p agomiR was still significantly greater than that in rat treated with air + a miR‐23a‐3p agomiR. These results further confirmed that miR‐23a‐3p can antagonize hyperoxia‐induced tissue damage caused by the dysregulation of multiple biochemical markers in rat lung tissue by inhibiting the expression of its target gene Slc7a2. Previous studies have shown that the percentage of TUNEL‐positive cells in the lungs of slc7a2−/− rat is significantly lower than that in wild‐type rat after hyperoxia exposure [14], which is consistent with our findings that the absence of slc7a2 can protect rat from acute lung injury. In fact, we now note that while our data show miR‐23a‐3p directly targets Slc7a2, the downstream effects may involve multiple pathways (e.g., arginine‐nitric oxide metabolism), which is consistent with the other results by Jin that support that Slc7a2 deficiency is protective in hyperoxia‐induced lung injury [14]. Besides, we consider that the protective effect observed with Slc7a2 deficiency may be context‐dependent and that the miR‐23a‐3p/Slc7a2 axis could interact with other signaling pathways. We also caution that the role of Slc7a2 may differ in other injury models or cell types and that future studies are needed to dissect these complex interactions. This addition does not change our core conclusion but provides a more balanced and accurate interpretation.

Some studies have combined novel drug delivery systems with natural anti‐inflammatory/polyphenol methods to improve long‐term tissue healing. For example, a study on tree thorns‐inspired hyaluronic acid‐based dissolving microneedles loaded with salvianolic acid B for effective hypertrophic scar treatment with enhanced long‐term stability indicates that tree‐inspired Sal‐B MN holds great potential as a highly effective tool for HS treatment with a long shelf‐life [37]. Previous studies have shown that green tea polyphenols (EGCG) nanoparticles can significantly promote apoptosis of breast cancer cells in vitro [38].

Finally, as noted, exosome‐based therapies, despite their favourable safety profile, do involve potential risks such as batch‐to‐batch variability, immune modulation heterogeneity and off‐target effects, especially in neonatal models [2]. Therefore, we should describe the safety profile of MSC‐Exos as generally favourable but requiring further investigation.

In conclusion, in both in vivo and in vitro models of hyperoxia‐triggered lung injury, exosomes derived from BMSCs carrying miR‐23a‐3p exert anti‐apoptotic effects against hyperoxia‐induced cell damage, boost cell proliferation and modulate the levels of numerous inflammatory biomarkers. Through gene sequencing and target gene screening, it was determined that miR‐23a‐3p promotes alveolar epithelial cell proliferation and tissue repair through the formation of the miR‐23a‐3p/Slc7a axis with its target gene Slc7a2. However, future studies using human‐derived cells or organoids are needed to confirm the clinical relevance of the miR‐23a‐3p/Slc7a2 axis.
