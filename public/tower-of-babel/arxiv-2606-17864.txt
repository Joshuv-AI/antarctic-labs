1.
Introduction

A real entire function is said to belong to the Laguerre–Pólya class, denoted by
\mathcal{LP}
, if it is the limit, uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}
, of real polynomials with only real zeros. Equivalently, it has the canonical product representation

(1.1)

f(z)=cz^{m}e^{-az^{2}+bz}\prod_{j=1}^{\omega}\left(1-\frac{z}{x_{j}}\right)e^{z/x_{j}},

where
c,b,x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
a\geq 0
,
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
,
x_{j}\neq 0
, and
\sum_{j}|x_{j}|^{-2}<\infty
; the product may be finite or empty. We shall use this standard form together with the approximation theorem for
\mathcal{LP}
; see, for example, Boas
[
1
, Chapter VII]
or Levin
[
7
, Chapter VIII]
.

The Laguerre–Pólya class of type I, denoted by
\mathcal{LP}^{I}
, consists of the functions in
\mathcal{LP}
which, after possibly replacing
z
by
-z
, are locally uniform limits of real polynomials whose zeros are all nonpositive. This is one of the classical zero-preserving subclasses considered already in the work of Laguerre and Pólya–Schur; see Laguerre’s collected works
[
5
]
, Pólya–Schur
[
10
]
, and Obreschkoff’s monograph
[
9
]
. Equivalently, up to the change
z\mapsto-z
, they have the form

(1.2)

f(z)=cz^{m}e^{bz}\prod_{j=1}^{\omega}\left(1+\frac{z}{x_{j}}\right),\qquad x_{j}>0,\qquad\sum_{j}x_{j}^{-1}<\infty,

with
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
b\geq 0
.

The classical Pólya–Schur theorem says that a real sequence
\{\gamma_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is a multiplier sequence, i.e. the diagonal operator

\sum c_{k}z^{k}\longmapsto\sum\gamma_{k}c_{k}z^{k}

preserves real-rootedness of polynomials, if and only if the exponential generating function

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}\frac{z^{k}}{k!}

belongs to
\mathcal{LP}^{I}
after possibly replacing
z
by
-z
; see
[
10
]
. We shall also use Jensen’s characterization: if

(1.3)

f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\frac{z^{k}}{k!}

is real entire, then
f\in\mathcal{LP}
if and only if all Jensen polynomials

(1.4)

J_{n}(f;z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}a_{k}z^{k},\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots,

have only real zeros.

Let
0<q<1
. We use the standard notation

(a;q)_{n}=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-aq^{j}),\qquad(a;q)_{0}=1,

from the theory of basic hypergeometric series; see, for example,
[
3
]
. We recall the big
q
-exponential

(1.5)

E_{q}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k(k-1)/2}}{(q;q)_{k}}z^{k}=(-z;q)_{\infty}=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}(1+q^{j}z).

Thus
E_{q}
has zeros
-q^{-j}
,
j=0,1,2,\ldots,
and belongs to
\mathcal{LP}^{I}
. This elementary fact is the main mechanism behind the coefficient-side part of the paper.

Given a real entire function
f
written as in (
1.3
), define its normalized
q
-Borel transform by

(1.6)

(\mathcal{B}_{q}f)(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\frac{q^{k(k-1)/2}(1-q)^{k}}{(q;q)_{k}}z^{k}.

The normalization is chosen so that

\frac{q^{k(k-1)/2}(1-q)^{k}}{(q;q)_{k}}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{k!}\qquad(q\to 1^{-})

for each fixed
k
; hence, under mild hypotheses which are automatic for entire functions,
\mathcal{B}_{q}f\to f
locally uniformly as
q\to 1^{-}
. This leads to the following coefficient-side class.

Definition 1.1
.

For fixed
q\in(0,1)
, the weak
q
-Laguerre–Pólya class is

\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{w}}_{q}:=\{f\text{ real entire}:\ \mathcal{B}_{q}f\in\mathcal{LP}\}.

Similarly,

\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{w}}_{q}:=\{f\text{ real entire}:\ \mathcal{B}_{q}f\in\mathcal{LP}^{I}\}

is the weak
q
-Laguerre–Pólya class of type I.

There is also a natural zero-side
q
-analog. A finite or infinite set of nonzero real numbers is logarithmically
q
-separated if, on each side of the origin, consecutive moduli differ by at least the factor
q^{-1}
. This condition is suggested by the zero set of
E_{q}
. A closely related zero-theoretic framework appears in Lamprecht’s work on Suffridge-type convolution theorems and
q
-extensions of Pólya–Schur theory
[
6
]
; finite-difference analogs of Pólya–Schur theory in an additive mesh setting were studied in
[
2
]
.

For later comparison we also mention that several classical
q
-special functions have been studied from the point of view of real zeros and membership in the Laguerre–Pólya class, for instance in
[
4
,
8
]
. The zero-side class considered here is the following.

Definition 1.2
.

A finite or infinite sequence of nonzero real numbers is called
q
-separated if, for every two distinct entries
x
and
y
of the same sign with
0<|x|\leq|y|
, one has

\frac{|x|}{|y|}\leq q.

Thus repeated nonzero entries are not allowed. Zeros at the origin are allowed with arbitrary multiplicity and are not counted in the separation condition.

A real polynomial is called
q
-hyperbolic if all its nonzero zeros are real and, counted with multiplicity, form a
q
-separated sequence. The strong
q
-Laguerre–Pólya class
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}
is the locally uniform closure, in the space of real entire functions, of
q
-hyperbolic polynomials. The class
\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{s}}_{q}
is defined similarly, with all nonzero zeros required to have one sign.

The main results of the paper can now be summarized as follows. First,
\mathcal{B}_{q}
preserves the classical Laguerre–Pólya class and its type-I subclass. Second, the weak class is strictly larger than the classical class. Third, the strong class has an exact genus-zero product representation, and this rules out any nonconstant exponential factor. These facts give the strict inclusions

\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}\subsetneq\mathcal{LP}\subsetneq\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{w}}_{q},\qquad\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{s}}_{q}\subsetneq\mathcal{LP}^{I}\subsetneq\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{w}}_{q}

for every
q\in(0,1)
.

Let us emphasize the distinction between the two constructions. The weak class is governed by the coefficient multiplier sequence coming from the big
q
-exponential; its basic properties are therefore consequences of the classical Pólya–Schur theorem together with Jensen’s criterion. The strong class is instead a closure of polynomials with a prescribed multiplicative mesh of zeros. Its product representation is not a formal consequence of the classical Laguerre–Pólya theorem: one must show that the mesh condition is closed under locally uniform limits and that it eliminates the exponential factors which are allowed in the classical canonical product.

2.
The coefficient-side class

We start with two elementary estimates for the normalized coefficients

(2.1)

\delta_{k}(q):=k!\frac{q^{k(k-1)/2}(1-q)^{k}}{(q;q)_{k}},\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots.

The exponential generating function of
\{\delta_{k}(q)\}
is

(2.2)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta_{k}(q)\frac{z^{k}}{k!}=E_{q}((1-q)z)=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(1+(1-q)q^{j}z\right).

Hence this generating function belongs to
\mathcal{LP}^{I}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
0<q<1
, the sequence
\{\delta_{k}(q)\}_{k\geq 0}
is a multiplier sequence of type I. Moreover

0<\delta_{k}(q)\leq 1,\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots.

Proof.

The first assertion follows immediately from (
2.2
) and the Pólya–Schur theorem, since all zeros of
E_{q}((1-q)z)
are real and negative.

For the estimate, write

\delta_{k}(q)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{jq^{j-1}}{1+q+\cdots+q^{j-1}}.

Since
1+q+\cdots+q^{j-1}\geq jq^{j-1}
for
0<q<1
, each factor is at most one.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
0<q<1
. If
f_{n}\to f
locally uniformly in
\mathbb{C}
, where the
f_{n}
are real entire functions, then
\mathcal{B}_{q}f_{n}\to\mathcal{B}_{q}f
locally uniformly in
\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

Write

f_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{n,k}z^{k},\qquad f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}z^{k}.

Then

\mathcal{B}_{q}f_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta_{k}(q)b_{n,k}z^{k},\qquad\mathcal{B}_{q}f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta_{k}(q)b_{k}z^{k}.

Fix
0<R<S
and put

M_{n}=\sup_{|z|\leq S}|f_{n}(z)-f(z)|.

By Cauchy’s estimate,
|b_{n,k}-b_{k}|\leq M_{n}S^{-k}
. Hence, using Lemma
2.1
, for
|z|\leq R
,

|\mathcal{B}_{q}(f_{n}-f)(z)|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta_{k}(q)|b_{n,k}-b_{k}|R^{k}\leq M_{n}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{R}{S}\right)^{k}.

Since
M_{n}\to 0
, the claim follows.
∎

Theorem 2.3
.

For every
0<q<1
,

\mathcal{B}_{q}(\mathcal{LP})\subseteq\mathcal{LP},\qquad\mathcal{B}_{q}(\mathcal{LP}^{I})\subseteq\mathcal{LP}^{I}.

Equivalently,

\mathcal{LP}\subseteq\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{w}}_{q},\qquad\mathcal{LP}^{I}\subseteq\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{w}}_{q}.

Proof.

Let

T_{q}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}z^{k}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\delta_{k}(q)c_{k}z^{k}.

By Lemma
2.1
and the Pólya–Schur theorem,
T_{q}
preserves real-rootedness of polynomials. More precisely, the generating function in (
2.2
) has only negative zeros and belongs to
\mathcal{LP}^{I}
; hence
\{\delta_{k}(q)\}
is a type-I multiplier sequence. Therefore
T_{q}
maps polynomials with all zeros in
(-\infty,0]
to polynomials with the same property. Since every
\delta_{k}(q)
is positive, applying the same statement to
p(-z)
shows that
T_{q}
also preserves the subclass of real-rooted polynomials whose zeros all lie in
[0,\infty)
.

If
f\in\mathcal{LP}
, choose real-rooted polynomials
p_{n}
converging to
f
locally uniformly. Then
T_{q}p_{n}
are real-rooted and, by Lemma
2.2
,
T_{q}p_{n}=\mathcal{B}_{q}p_{n}\to\mathcal{B}_{q}f
locally uniformly. Therefore
\mathcal{B}_{q}f\in\mathcal{LP}
. The type-I assertion is identical, using approximation by polynomials whose zeros have one sign, after one fixed change
z\mapsto-z
if necessary.
∎

Theorem 2.4

(A
q
-Jensen criterion)
.

Let
f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{k}/k!
be real entire and fix
q\in(0,1)
. Then
f\in\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{w}}_{q}
if and only if, for every
n\geq 0
, the polynomial

(2.3)

J_{n,q}(f;z):=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}a_{k}\,\delta_{k}(q)\,z^{k}

has only real zeros.

Similarly,
f\in\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{w}}_{q}
if and only if the polynomials
J_{n,q}(f;z)
have only real zeros of one sign, after one common change
z\mapsto-z
if necessary.

Proof.

The ordinary Maclaurin coefficients of
\mathcal{B}_{q}f
are
\delta_{k}(q)a_{k}/k!
. Thus the Jensen polynomials of
\mathcal{B}_{q}f
are precisely (
2.3
). Jensen’s characterization of
\mathcal{LP}
gives the first assertion.

For the type-I assertion, recall the standard type-I variant: a real entire function belongs to
\mathcal{LP}^{I}
if and only if, after possibly replacing
z
by
-z
once and for all, all its Jensen polynomials have only nonpositive zeros. This follows by applying the classical Jensen approximation theorem to
g(z)
or
g(-z)
and using the Pólya–Schur characterization of type-I multiplier sequences. Applying this statement to
g=\mathcal{B}_{q}f
gives the asserted criterion for
\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{w}}_{q}
.
∎

Theorem 2.5
.

For every real entire function
f
,

\mathcal{B}_{q}f\longrightarrow f\qquad\text{locally uniformly in }\mathbb{C}\text{ as }q\to 1^{-}.

Proof.

Write
f(z)=\sum a_{k}z^{k}/k!
. For fixed
k
,

\frac{q^{k(k-1)/2}(1-q)^{k}}{(q;q)_{k}}\to\frac{1}{k!}\qquad(q\to 1^{-}).

Moreover,

k!\frac{q^{k(k-1)/2}(1-q)^{k}}{(q;q)_{k}}=\delta_{k}(q)\leq 1

by Lemma
2.1
. Hence, on
|z|\leq R
,

\left|a_{k}\frac{q^{k(k-1)/2}(1-q)^{k}}{(q;q)_{k}}z^{k}\right|\leq|a_{k}|\frac{R^{k}}{k!}.

The majorant is summable because
f
is entire. Dominated convergence gives the result.
∎

Theorem 2.6
.

For every
q\in(0,1)
,

\mathcal{LP}\subsetneq\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{w}}_{q},\qquad\mathcal{LP}^{I}\subsetneq\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{w}}_{q}.

Proof.

Consider the quadratic polynomial

(2.4)

F_{q}(z)=1+2z+\frac{1+q}{2q}z^{2}.

In the notation
F_{q}(z)=\sum a_{k}z^{k}/k!
, one has

a_{0}=1,\qquad a_{1}=2,\qquad a_{2}=\frac{1+q}{q}.

Therefore

\mathcal{B}_{q}F_{q}(z)=1+2z+z^{2}=(1+z)^{2}.

Thus
F_{q}\in\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{w}}_{q}\subseteq\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{w}}_{q}
. On the other hand, the discriminant of
F_{q}
is

4-4\frac{1+q}{2q}=-\frac{2(1-q)}{q}<0.

Hence
F_{q}
has two nonreal zeros and does not belong to
\mathcal{LP}
. A fortiori it does not belong to
\mathcal{LP}^{I}
.
∎

3.
The zero-side class

We now prove the product representation for
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}
. The key point is that logarithmic
q
-separation prevents zeros escaping to infinity from producing a residual exponential factor. This is the only delicate point in the zero-side theory.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
p
be a
q
-hyperbolic polynomial. For
R>0
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x:p(x)=0\\
|x|\geq R\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|x|}\leq\frac{2}{(1-q)R},

where the sum is over nonzero zeros and counts multiplicities. More generally, if
s>0
, then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x:p(x)=0\\
|x|\geq R\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|x|^{s}}\leq\frac{2}{(1-q^{s})R^{s}}.

Proof.

On the positive half-axis, list the zeros not smaller than
R
as

R\leq x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots.

The
q
-separation condition gives
x_{j+1}\geq q^{-1}x_{j}
, hence
x_{j}\geq Rq^{-(j-1)}
. Therefore

\sum_{j}x_{j}^{-s}\leq R^{-s}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}q^{js}=\frac{1}{(1-q^{s})R^{s}}.

The negative half-axis gives the same bound.
∎

Lemma 3.2

(Closedness of the multiplicative mesh)
.

Let
0<q<1
, and let
p_{n}
be
q
-hyperbolic polynomials converging locally uniformly in
\mathbb{C}
to a real entire function
f\not\equiv 0
. Then all zeros of
f
are real. Moreover, every nonzero zero of
f
is simple, and the nonzero zeros of
f
form a
q
-separated set.

Proof.

The assertion that all zeros of
f
are real follows from Hurwitz’s theorem: a small disk centered at a nonreal zero and disjoint from the real axis would have to contain zeros of
p_{n}
for all sufficiently large
n
, which is impossible.

Let
x\neq 0
be a zero of
f
of multiplicity
r
. Choose
\varepsilon>0
so small that the interval
(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)
does not meet the origin and that all its points have the same sign. Hurwitz’s theorem, applied on a small circle around
x
, implies that for all sufficiently large
n
the polynomial
p_{n}
has exactly
r
zeros, counted with multiplicity, in this interval. If
r\geq 2
, two of these zeros have moduli whose quotient tends to
1
, contradicting the
q
-separation condition for all large
n
. Hence
r=1
.

It remains to check separation between distinct limiting zeros. Let
x
and
y
be two distinct zeros of
f
with the same sign and with
0<|x|\leq|y|
. If
|x|/|y|>q
, choose disjoint intervals
I_{x}
and
I_{y}
, centered at
x
and
y
respectively and contained in the same half-axis, such that

\frac{|u|}{|v|}>q\qquad(u\in I_{x},\,v\in I_{y},\ 0<|u|\leq|v|).

Hurwitz’s theorem gives zeros
x_{n}\in I_{x}
and
y_{n}\in I_{y}
of
p_{n}
for all large
n
, contradicting the
q
-separation of the zeros of
p_{n}
. Therefore
|x|/|y|\leq q
, as required.
∎

Theorem 3.3

(Product representation for the strong class)
.

Let
0<q<1
. A nonzero real entire function
f
belongs to
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}
if and only if it has the representation

(3.1)

f(z)=cz^{m}\prod_{j=1}^{\omega}\left(1-\frac{z}{x_{j}}\right),

where
c\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, the numbers
x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
are
q
-separated, and

\sum_{j}\frac{1}{|x_{j}|}<\infty.

The zero function belongs to
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}
by definition.

Proof.

First suppose that
f\in\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}
and
f\not\equiv 0
. Choose
q
-hyperbolic polynomials
p_{n}
such that
p_{n}\to f
locally uniformly. By Lemma
3.2
, all zeros of
f
are real, every nonzero zero is simple, and the nonzero zeros of
f
are
q
-separated. On the positive half-axis their moduli, if infinite in number, therefore grow at least geometrically; the same is true on the negative half-axis. Hence

\sum_{j}|x_{j}|^{-1}<\infty.

It remains to prove that no nonconstant exponential factor is left after passing to the limit. We first treat the case
f(0)\neq 0
. Then
p_{n}(0)\neq 0
for all sufficiently large
n
, and

p_{n}(z)=p_{n}(0)\prod_{x}\left(1-\frac{z}{x}\right),

where the product is over the nonzero zeros of
p_{n}
. Fix
\rho>0
. Choose
R>2\rho
such that
f
has no zero on
|z|=R
. Let
P_{n,R}
be the finite product formed from the zeros of
p_{n}
in
|z|<R
, and let
T_{n,R}
be the remaining tail product. Thus

p_{n}(z)=p_{n}(0)P_{n,R}(z)T_{n,R}(z).

By Hurwitz’s theorem, for this fixed
R
the zeros of
p_{n}
in
|z|<R
converge, as a multiset, to the zeros of
f
in
|z|<R
. Consequently

p_{n}(0)P_{n,R}(z)\longrightarrow f(0)P_{R}(z)

locally uniformly, where

P_{R}(z)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j:\ |x_{j}|<R\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{z}{x_{j}}\right).

In particular
p_{n}(0)P_{n,R}
is uniformly bounded on
|z|\leq\rho
for all large
n
.

For
|z|\leq\rho
and any tail zero satisfying
|x|\geq R
, we have
|z/x|<1/2
and hence, with the principal branch of the logarithm,

\left|\log\left(1-\frac{z}{x}\right)\right|\leq\frac{2\rho}{|x|}.

Lemma
3.1
gives the uniform estimate

\left|\log T_{n,R}(z)\right|\leq\frac{4\rho}{(1-q)R},\qquad|z|\leq\rho.

Therefore

\sup_{|z|\leq\rho}|T_{n,R}(z)-1|\leq\exp\left(\frac{4\rho}{(1-q)R}\right)-1,

uniformly in
n
. Passing first to the limit
n\to\infty
and then letting
R\to\infty
, we obtain

f(z)=f(0)\prod_{j}\left(1-\frac{z}{x_{j}}\right)

locally uniformly on
|z|\leq\rho
. Since
\rho
was arbitrary, this holds locally uniformly in
\mathbb{C}
.

Now suppose that
f
has a zero of order
m
at the origin. Choose
\varepsilon>0
so small that
f
has no zeros in
0<|z|\leq 2\varepsilon
. By Hurwitz’s theorem, for all sufficiently large
n
the polynomial
p_{n}
has exactly
m
zeros, counted with multiplicity, in
|z|<\varepsilon
and no zeros on
|z|=\varepsilon
. Let

A_{n}(z)=\prod_{\nu=1}^{m}(z-\alpha_{n,\nu})

be the monic factor formed from these zeros, and write
p_{n}=A_{n}g_{n}
. Then
\alpha_{n,\nu}\to 0
, so
A_{n}\to z^{m}
locally uniformly. On the circle
|z|=\varepsilon
the functions
g_{n}=p_{n}/A_{n}
converge uniformly to
f(z)/z^{m}
. Since the
g_{n}
are holomorphic in
|z|\leq\varepsilon
, the maximum principle gives the same convergence in the disk. On compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
the convergence follows directly from
p_{n}\to f
and
A_{n}\to z^{m}
. Hence
g_{n}\to f(z)/z^{m}
locally uniformly in
\mathbb{C}
.

The nonzero zeros of each
g_{n}
form a subsequence of the nonzero zeros of
p_{n}
; hence
g_{n}
is again
q
-hyperbolic. Since
f(z)/z^{m}
does not vanish at the origin, the already proved case applies to this limit and gives the required product representation for
f
.

Conversely, if
f
has the representation (
3.1
), then the partial products

cz^{m}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(1-\frac{z}{x_{j}}\right)

are
q
-hyperbolic polynomials and converge locally uniformly to
f
, because
\sum_{j}|x_{j}|^{-1}<\infty
. Hence
f\in\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}
.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

A nonzero real entire function belongs to
\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{s}}_{q}
if and only if, after possibly replacing
z
by
-z
, it has the representation

f(z)=cz^{m}\prod_{j=1}^{\omega}\left(1+\frac{z}{x_{j}}\right),\qquad x_{j}>0,

where the sequence
\{x_{j}\}
is
q
-separated and
\sum_{j}x_{j}^{-1}<\infty
.

Corollary 3.5
.

For every
q\in(0,1)
,

\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}\subseteq\mathcal{LP},\qquad\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{s}}_{q}\subseteq\mathcal{LP}^{I}.

More precisely, every nonzero function in
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}
has genus zero and no nonconstant exponential factor.

Proof.

This is immediate from Theorem
3.3
and the classical product representation of
\mathcal{LP}
.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

For every
q\in(0,1)
, the class
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}
is not closed under multiplication.

Proof.

Choose
y
with
1<y<q^{-1}
. The polynomials
1-z
and
1-z/y
each belong to
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}
, since each has only one nonzero zero. Their product has two positive zeros,
1
and
y
, which are not
q
-separated. By Theorem
3.3
, the product does not belong to
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}
.
∎

4.
Comparison of the two classes

The results above give the precise inclusion chain.

Theorem 4.1
.

For every
q\in(0,1)
,

\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}\subsetneq\mathcal{LP}\subsetneq\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{w}}_{q}.

Likewise,

\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{s}}_{q}\subsetneq\mathcal{LP}^{I}\subsetneq\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{w}}_{q}.

Proof.

The inclusions
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}\subseteq\mathcal{LP}
and
\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{s}}_{q}\subseteq\mathcal{LP}^{I}
follow from Corollary
3.5
. They are proper because
e^{-z^{2}}\in\mathcal{LP}
but, by Theorem
3.3
, a zero-free function in
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}
must be constant; and
e^{z}\in\mathcal{LP}^{I}
but a zero-free function in
\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{s}}_{q}
must again be constant.

The inclusions
\mathcal{LP}\subseteq\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{w}}_{q}
and
\mathcal{LP}^{I}\subseteq\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{w}}_{q}
are Theorem
2.3
. They are proper by Theorem
2.6
.
∎

Example 4.2
.

The function

E_{q}((1-q)z)=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(1+(1-q)q^{j}z\right)

belongs to
\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{s}}_{q}
, because its zeros are

-\frac{q^{-j}}{1-q},\qquad j=0,1,2,\ldots,

which form a
q
-geometric progression on the negative axis. It also belongs to
\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{w}}_{q}
by Theorem
2.3
, since it belongs to
\mathcal{LP}^{I}
classically.

Example 4.3
.

For every
q\in(0,1)
, the function
e^{-z^{2}}
belongs to
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{w}}_{q}
by Theorem
2.3
, but it does not belong to
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}
by Theorem
3.3
. Similarly,
e^{z}\in\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{w}}_{q}
but
e^{z}\notin\mathcal{LP}^{I,\mathrm{s}}_{q}
.

Example 4.4
.

The function
\sin z
belongs to
\mathcal{LP}
, hence to
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{w}}_{q}
, for every
q\in(0,1)
. It is not in
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}
, because its positive zeros
\pi,2\pi,3\pi,\ldots
are not logarithmically
q
-separated.

5.
Weak
q
-multiplier sequences

The coefficient-side definition suggests a cautious terminology for coefficient sequences.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
0<q<1
. A real sequence
\{a_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is called a weak
q
-multiplier sequence if the associated
q
-exponential generating function

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\frac{q^{k(k-1)/2}(1-q)^{k}}{(q;q)_{k}}z^{k}

belongs to
\mathcal{LP}
, whenever this series defines an entire function.

This definition is meant only as coefficient-side terminology; it is not, by itself, an intrinsic preservation theorem for an operator on a zero-side class. With this terminology, Theorem
2.3
says that every classical multiplier sequence gives a weak
q
-multiplier sequence after the normalization (
1.6
). A full Pólya–Schur-type classification of weak or strong
q
-multiplier sequences remains open. Lamprecht’s convolution theory
[
6
]
and the finite-difference mesh-preserving theory of
[
2
]
suggest that the zero-side version should be genuinely more rigid than the coefficient-side version.

6.
Questions

We close with some questions which, in the present formulation, are deliberately separated from the proved results.

Question 6.1
.

Find an intrinsic coefficient characterization of
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{w}}_{q}
which does not simply restate the condition
\mathcal{B}_{q}f\in\mathcal{LP}
. In particular, is there a useful analog of the classical Laguerre inequalities for the coefficients of functions in
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{w}}_{q}
?

Question 6.2
.

Is
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{w}}_{q}
closed under differentiation? Equivalently, if
\mathcal{B}_{q}f\in\mathcal{LP}
, must
\mathcal{B}_{q}(f^{\prime})\in\mathcal{LP}
?

Question 6.3
.

Characterize diagonal operators preserving
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}
or preserving the set of
q
-hyperbolic polynomials. Is there a satisfactory multiplicative-mesh analog of the Pólya–Schur theorem?

Question 6.4
.

Which classical
q
-special functions belong to
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{w}}_{q}
, to
\mathcal{LP}^{\mathrm{s}}_{q}
, or to both? In particular, can the known real-rootedness results for the Ramanujan function and related
q
-Bessel functions, such as those discussed in
[
4
,
8
]
, be reinterpreted naturally in this framework?

Question 6.5
.

Study the limiting behavior of the strong class as
q\to 1^{-}
and
q\to 0^{+}
. The weak class converges to the classical class in the operator sense of Theorem
2.5
; the corresponding zero-side limit is subtler because the
q
-separation condition degenerates as
q\to 1^{-}
.
