Graph Signal Diffusion Models for Wireless Resource Allocation
Yiğit Berkay Uslu, Samar Hadou, Shirin Saeedi Bidokhti, Alejandro Ribeiro

Abstract
We consider constrained ergodic resource optimization in wireless networks with graph-structured interference. We train a diffusion model policy to match expert conditional distributions over resource allocations. By leveraging a primal-dual (expert) algorithm, we generate primal iterates that serve as draws from the corresponding expert conditionals for each training network instance. We view the allocations as stochastic graph signals supported on known channel state graphs. We implement the diffusion model architecture as a U-Net hierarchy of graph neural network (GNN) blocks, conditioned on the channel states and additional node states. At inference, the learned generative model amortizes the iterative expert policy by directly sampling allocation vectors from the near-optimal conditional distributions. In a power-control case study, we show that time-sharing the generated power allocations achieves near-optimal ergodic sum-rate utility and near-feasible ergodic minimum-rates, with strong generalization and transferability across network states.

Introduction

Generative models enable approximating the solutions to stochastic optimization problems whose decision variable is itself a probability distribution over high-dimensional action spaces
[
2
]
. This is particularly relevant in physical decision-making setups, e.g., wireless systems, where optimizing quality-of-service (QoS) metrics under system requirements typically leads to constrained nonconvex problems. In such problems, whenever randomization is physically implementable, stochastic policies can attain utility-constraint trade-offs that are unattainable by any optimal deterministic policy because they induce convex combinations of deterministic operating points through time sharing
[
9
]
. This is especially appealing in fast-fading wireless channels, where QoS utilities and constraints are naturally evaluated in the ergodic sense
[
8
]
.

Much of the existing literature utilizes generative models either to generate domain-specific synthetic data or to perform data augmentation for learning-based wireless resource allocation

[
3
,
14
]
.
A separate line of work
[
7
,
5
,
4
,
1
]

proposes generative solvers for network optimization, aiming to learn solution distributions that place most of their probability mass near optimal deterministic solutions. The generative process maps random noise into high-quality solutions by denoising the samples corrupted in the forward process. In contrast to these prior works, we focus on ergodic wireless resource allocation, where the solutions are inherently stochastic.

We cast stochastic wireless resource allocation as a conditional generative modeling problem. For each network state, we learn a conditional distribution over allocation vectors matching the expert conditionals. Since the expert conditionals are unavailable in closed-form, we instead obtain approximate samples using a primal–dual expert algorithm and then train a conditional diffusion model to imitate the induced expert allocation distributions. Rather than solving a constrained optimization problem online for each new network realization, the learned diffusion model policy directly generates feasible and near-optimal allocations through conditional sampling and amortizes the iterative expert algorithm.

We exploit the graph structure of wireless networks in our approach. Interference relationships define a graph topology with the users as nodes, and the user states are represented as signals supported on that topology. This makes graph neural networks (GNNs), which exhibit scalability and transferability
[
6
]
, suitable parameterizations for our conditional generation task. We formulate optimal power control in ad-hoc wireless networks as a graph-signal denoising/generation problem and parameterize the diffusion policy with a U-graph neural network (U-GNN) architecture
[
12
]
.The U-GNN uses a U-shaped cascade of GNN blocks with skip connections and is conditioned jointly on channel states and user states that are represented as graphs and graph signals, respectively.

This paper extends our earlier work
[
11
]
, which first introduced a diffusion-based generative modeling framework for the imitation learning of stochastic wireless policies. This paper expands that framework in both algorithmic detail and empirical scope. First, we provide a more complete theoretical and implementation-level treatment of the primal–dual algorithm used to construct the expert stochastic policies. Second, we study a similar power control problem over a broader family of network configurations, including varying user densities and QoS requirements. Third, we employ the U-GNN architecture as the denoising backbone of the diffusion model. Finally, we demonstrate that the learned U-GNN policies, trained on moderately-sized networks, generalize well to unseen QoS requirements and transfer to networks at larger scale.

Optimal Wireless Resource Allocation

Consider a wireless system comprised of
N
users (nodes). We denote by
{\mathbf{H}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
the network state.
For a given
{\mathbf{H}}
, an allocation of network resources
{\mathbf{x}}({\mathbf{H}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
produces a quality-of-service (QoS) utility
f_{0}\big({\mathbf{x}}({\mathbf{H}}),{\mathbf{H}}\big)\in{\mathbb{R}}
that we want to maximize, along with
c
QoS requirements collected in a vector
{\mathbf{f}}\big({\mathbf{x}}({\mathbf{H}}),{\mathbf{H}}\big)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{c}
that we must satisfy. In this paper, we study stochastic wireless resource allocation policy design problems of the form

\displaystyle\text{P}({\mathbf{H}})\coloneqq\max_{\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}}~\E_{\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}}\!\Big[f_{0}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{H}})\Big],~\text{\,s.t.}~~~\E_{\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}}\!\Big[{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{H}})\Big]\geq{\mathbf{0}},

(1)

where
{\mathbf{x}}({\mathbf{H}})\sim\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}(\cdot{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}})
, which is the optimization variable, denotes a conditional distribution over resource allocation variables. This abstract formulation encompasses downlink power control in ad-hoc networks (see Section
3
), as well as analogous problems of interest in point-to-point, MIMO, and broadcast channels.

Assume that the network states are drawn from an underlying distribution
{\mathbf{H}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{H}}}
. We write
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}^{*}(\cdot{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}})
for an optimal
{\mathbf{H}}
-conditional allocation distribution solving (
1
), and

\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{H}}}^{*}

=

\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}^{*}(\cdot{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}})\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{H}}}

for the joint distribution of optimal allocations and network states. We fit a parametric family of
{\mathbf{H}}
-conditional resource allocation distributions
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}(\cdot{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta})
,
with parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, that minimizes the
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{H}}}
-average of conditional KL divergences,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\E_{{\mathbf{H}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{H}}}}\Big[\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}^{*}(\cdot{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}})\;\big\|\;\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}(\cdot{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta})\big)\Big].

(2)

In (
2
), we aim to learn a conditional generative policy
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}(\cdot{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})
that matches the expert conditional distributions
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}^{*}(\cdot{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}})
across (almost) all network states.

Solving (
2
) is intractable since the expert conditionals
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}^{*}(\cdot{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}})
are not available in closed-form. Instead, we assume access to samples
({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{H}})
drawn approximately from the joint expert distribution
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{H}}}^{*}
, generated by an expert algorithm described later in Section
2.2
. This recasts the problem as one of conditional generative modeling, where we learn a generative model
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}(\cdot{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})
whose samples are distributed similarly to the expert samples
{\mathbf{x}}{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}^{*}(\cdot{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}})
for any
{\mathbf{H}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{H}}}
given. In this paper, we instantiate
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}(\cdot{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta})
as a diffusion model parametrization.

2.1
Resource Allocation Generative Diffusion Models

Given clean expert samples
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}^{*}(\cdot{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}})
, a Gaussian noising process generates corrupted samples
{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
over
K
noising steps,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}_{k}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{k}}{\mathbf{x}}_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{k}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon},~~~\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{I}}),

(3)

where
\{\bar{\alpha}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
is a quantity related to the noise variance schedule. Diffusion model training learns a parametric denoiser
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\mathbf{x}}_{k},k;{\mathbf{H}})
that predicts the injected noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
from the corrupted sample
{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
, conditioned on the diffusion step
k
and the network state
{\mathbf{H}}
. This yields the standard DDPM training objective
[
2
]
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}=

\displaystyle~\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}~\E_{{\mathbf{x}}_{0},{\mathbf{H}},k,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\big\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big({\mathbf{x}}_{k}({\mathbf{x}}_{0},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}),k;{\mathbf{H}}\big)-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\big\|^{2}.

(4)

Here, the expectation is over the distribution
({\mathbf{x}}_{0},{\mathbf{H}})\sim\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}^{*}({\mathbf{H}})\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{H}}}
, diffusion steps
k\sim\mathrm{Unif}(1,K)
, and noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{I}})
.

The learned denoiser parametrizes a reverse diffusion process that maps noisy samples back into synthetic samples distributed similarly to the clean (expert) samples. To generate samples, we use a denoising diffusion implicit model (DDIM) reverse process
[
10
]
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1}\!=\!\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{k-1}}\,\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}\!+\!\sqrt{1\!-\!\bar{\alpha}_{k-1}\!-\!\sigma_{k}^{2}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}({\mathbf{x}}_{k},k;{\mathbf{H}})+\sigma_{k}{\mathbf{w}},

(5)

where
\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}=\big({\mathbf{x}}_{k}-\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{k}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}({\mathbf{x}}_{k},k;{\mathbf{H}})\big)/\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{k}}
are denoised estimates of the clean samples,
{\mathbf{w}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{I}})
, and
\sigma_{k}
controls the stochasticity of the sampler.

At inference, we initialize
{\mathbf{x}}_{K}\sim\mathcal{N}({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{I}})
and iterate (
5
) for
k=K,\ldots,1
. This sampling scheme induces a conditional generative distribution
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}(\cdot\,|\,{\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta})
for any given
{\mathbf{H}}
. Therefore, the generative diffusion modeling framework defined by (
3
)–(
5
) offers a practical, sample-based surrogate for matching
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}(\cdot\,|\,{\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})
to the expert conditionals
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}^{*}(\cdot\,|\,{\mathbf{H}})
in (
2
).

We particularize the denoiser
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
to a graph signal diffusion model that we discuss in Section
3
.
Next, we describe a primal-dual algorithm that generates expert policy samples for diffusion training.

2.2
A Primal-Dual Expert Algorithm

To construct the expert policies used for training, we begin with the deterministic resource-allocation problem for a fixed
{\mathbf{H}}
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\text{P}}({\mathbf{H}})\coloneqq\max_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}}~f_{0}\big({\mathbf{x}}({\mathbf{H}}),{\mathbf{H}}\big),~~\text{\,s.t.}~~{\mathbf{f}}\big({\mathbf{x}}({\mathbf{H}}),{\mathbf{H}}\big)\geq{\mathbf{0}}.

(6)

Here,
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}({\mathbf{H}})
denotes a deterministic allocation that maximizes the utility
f_{0}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{H}})
subject to the constraints
{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{H}})\geq{\mathbf{0}}
. For given
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\succcurlyeq{\mathbf{0}}
, we introduce the Lagrangian relaxation of (
6
),

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathcal{L}}}({\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=f_{0}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{H}})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{H}}).

(7)

Defining the dual function as
\widetilde{g}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\coloneqq\max_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\widetilde{{\mathcal{L}}}({\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
, the dual problem corresponding to (
6
) is given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}({\mathbf{H}})=\min_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\succcurlyeq{\mathbf{0}}}\widetilde{g}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\min_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\succcurlyeq{\mathbf{0}}}\max_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\widetilde{{\mathcal{L}}}({\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}).

(8)

Given a state
{\mathbf{H}}
, an initialization
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}\succcurlyeq{\mathbf{0}}
, and a dual step size
\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}>0
, a projected
dual (sub)gradient descent
algorithm for (
8
) generates the primal–dual iterates

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{x}}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k})\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\widetilde{{\mathcal{L}}}({\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}),

(9)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\Big[\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}-\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\,{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}}_{k+1},{\mathbf{H}})\Big]_{+},

(10)

where
[\cdot]_{+}\coloneqq\max(\cdot,0)
denotes a componentwise projection to the nonnegative orthant.

This algorithm alternates between a primal step that maximizes the Lagrangian for the current dual multiplier and a dual step that decreases the multipliers in the direction of positive constraint slack and increases them when constraints are violated. By parametrizing the primal variables
{\mathbf{x}}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\phi})
,
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\boldsymbol{\Phi}
, and approximating the exact maximization in (
9
) with multiple gradient-ascent steps on
\widetilde{{\mathcal{L}}}\big({\mathbf{x}}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\phi}),\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}\big)
for given
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}
, we obtain a
primal-dual learning algorithm
as an expert policy.

For general, nonconvex utilities
f_{0}
and
{\mathbf{f}}
, the primal iterates of (
9
)–(
10
) need not converge to a single optimizer of (
6
). Nevertheless, the algorithm provably generates a primal trajectory that is asymptotically near-optimal and almost surely feasible
[
8
]
1
1

1

The statement holds for the parametrized version, with the optimality gap additionally depending on the expressiveness of the parametrization
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
.
,

\displaystyle\liminf_{K\to\infty}\Bigg[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}f_{0}\big({\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{H}}\big)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\geq\text{P}({\mathbf{H}})-{\mathcal{O}}(\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),

(11)

\displaystyle\liminf_{K\to\infty}\Bigg[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}~{\mathbf{f}}\big({\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{H}}\big)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbf{0}},\qquad\text{a.s.}

(12)

Note that
\text{P}({\mathbf{H}})
denotes the optimum value of the stochastic problem in (
1
), and the guarantees in (
11
)–(
12
) do not hold pointwise in the primal iterates, but rather in the ergodic (average) sense. Hence, for sufficiently small
\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, large enough
K
, and after discarding an initial burn-in window of length
K_{0}
, the sequence of iterates
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\}_{k\geq K_{0}}^{K_{0}+K}
can be interpreted as samples from an induced stochastic policy

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathcal{D}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}({\mathbf{x}}{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}})\coloneqq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=K_{0}}^{K_{0}+K}\delta({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{k}),

(13)

where
\delta({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}_{k})
denotes the Dirac measure at
{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
.

We illustrate the generation of primal-dual expert policy datasets in Fig.
1
. Note that
\widehat{{\mathcal{D}}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}(\cdot{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}})
approximates an expert conditional distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbf{x}}}^{*}(\cdot{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}})
through a late-iterate window of the primal–dual trajectory and serves as an approximately optimal and feasible stochastic allocation policy for (
1
). It is also noteworthy that although the update rule (
9
)–(
10
) is derived from the deterministic problem (
6
), its limiting behavior is associated with the stochastic (convex) relaxation in (
1
).
The dual descent dynamics, rather than converging to a deterministic policy, generate a
stochastic time-sharing policy
whose ergodic averages attain near-optimal utility.

Remark 1

Despite the availability of an expert policy with such guarantees, its direct use at inference remains computationally prohibitive because sampling from the expert policy requires running the primal-dual updates online. Even the state-augmented variants
[
13
]
that move the Lagrangian maximization step in (
9
) to offline training, still require online dual updates and remain subject to the trade-off governed by
\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
between shorter transients and improved asymptotic performance. While this trade-off can be partially mitigated by improved initialization of the dual multipliers
[
13
]
, online dual updates remain necessary. By learning a generative model, we bypass online policy learning and eliminate the inference overhead, up to the inference cost of diffusion sampling in (
5
).

Optimal Power Control

We present an optimal power control case study that slightly modifies and extends the setups we worked with in prior works
[
11
,
13
]
.

We model the network state (configuration)

{\mathbf{H}}
as a realization drawn from a stochastic network geometry together with a fading-channel model
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{H}}}
. Specifically, we adopt a random geometric graph (RGG) model for the physical network layout and deploy
N=400
transmitter-receiver (tx-rx) pairs (users) uniformly over a square area of side length
R
meters. Extending
[
11
]
, we generate
32
networks for each
R\in\{5800,6300,7000,7800\}
, yielding a dataset
{\mathcal{T}}=\{{\mathbf{H}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\mathbf{H}}^{(|{\mathcal{T}}|)}\}
of
|{\mathcal{T}}|=128
distinct network configurations. The resulting average user densities vary as
\nu\in\{6.6,8.2,10.1,11.9\}
tx-rx pairs/
\text{\,}{\mathrm{km}}^{2}
.

The channel model includes both large-scale and small-scale Rayleigh fading. Let
h_{ij}
denote the large-scale gain from transmitter
i
to receiver
j
, and
h_{ij,t}
the instantaneous gain at time slot
t
, which fluctuates due to small-scale Rayleigh fading. We collect these gains in channel state matrices
[{\mathbf{H}}]_{ij}=h_{ij}
, and
[{\mathbf{H}}_{t}]_{ij}=h_{ij,t}
. The large-scale channel state is governed primarily by the path loss plus log-normal shadowing, which together capture the effects of stochastic geometry and large-scale fading. Each slot
t
spans
50
milliseconds. We set the channel bandwidth and noise power spectral density (PSD) to
W=40
MHz and
N_{0}=-174
dBm/Hz, respectively.

We optimize the transmit power levels
{\mathbf{x}}\in[0,P_{\max}]^{N}
, where
P_{\max}=10
mW is the maximum power budget. Given an allocation
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
and fading realization
{\mathbf{H}}_{t}
at time slot
t
, the rate of receiver
j
is

\displaystyle\big[\widetilde{{\mathbf{r}}}\big({\mathbf{x}}_{t},{\mathbf{H}}_{t}\big)\big]_{j}=\log_{2}\bigg(1+\frac{[{\mathbf{x}}_{t}]_{j}\cdot[{\mathbf{H}}_{t}]_{jj}}{WN_{0}+\sum_{i\neq j}[{\mathbf{x}}_{t}]_{i}\cdot[{\mathbf{H}}_{t}]_{ij}}\bigg).

(14)

We evaluate the policy performance by the ergodic receiver rates,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{r}}(\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}},{\mathbf{H}})=\E_{\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}},{\mathcal{D}}_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}|{\mathbf{H}}}}\Big[\widetilde{{\mathbf{r}}}(\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}},{\mathbf{x}})\Big]\approx\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widetilde{{\mathbf{r}}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t},{\mathbf{H}}_{t}),

(15)

where
\{{\mathbf{H}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
are drawn independently from the stationary fast-fading process
{\mathcal{D}}_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{H}}}|{\mathbf{H}}}
and a power allocation policy
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}(\cdot\,|\,{\mathbf{H}})
, respectively.
Given
{\mathbf{H}}
, the optimal power control design seeks to maximize the ergodic sum-rate utility
\mathbf{1}^{\top}_{N}{\mathbf{r}}\big(\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}({\mathbf{H}}),{\mathbf{H}}\big)
, subject to minimum-rate constraints
{\mathbf{r}}\big(\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}({\mathbf{H}}),{\mathbf{H}}\big)-\mathbf{1}_{N}f_{\min}
.

3.1
U-GNN Policies

We decompose the network state into a channel state component and a node state component. We identify the large-scale channel matrix
{\mathbf{H}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
with a weighted adjacency matrix of an interference graph, where each node
j
represents the tx-rx pair
j
, and the edge
i\to j
encodes the channel strength from transmitter
i
to receiver
j
. In particular, self-loops correspond to desired links, whereas off-diagonal edges correspond to interference links.

On this graph, we define a node signal
{\mathbf{u}}
with three features per node: the normalized direct-link strength, the normalized aggregate incoming interference under full-power transmission, and the QoS requirement
f_{\min}
. Thus, for each node
j=1,\ldots,N
, we set

[{\mathbf{u}}]_{j}=\big(h_{jj},\sum_{i\neq j}h_{ij},f_{\min}\big)^{\top}
.
The allocations
{\mathbf{x}}
and ergodic throughput vectors
{\mathbf{r}}
are likewise viewed as graph signals supported on the same graph. The U-GNN diffusion policy is then conditioned jointly on the channel matrix
{\mathbf{H}}
and the node-state signal
{\mathbf{u}}
. Accordingly, the denoiser is written as
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot,\cdot{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{u}})
, and the induced allocation policy takes the form
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}(\cdot{\,\big|\,}{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{u}};\boldsymbol{\theta})
.

We run separate primal–dual expert algorithms on all
|{\mathcal{T}}|=128
networks, grouped by the four density levels and five QoS levels
f_{\min}\in\{0.4,0.5,0.6,0.7,0.8\}
bits/s/Hz. This procedure results in 20 expert (sub)datasets spanning all density and QoS level combinations. Each expert policy is trained with early stopping, and the final
K=200
primal iterates are collected to form the expert datasets. The expert policy is parameterized by a shallow, 3-layer GNN that shares the same design as the U-GNN backbone.

Using these expert datasets, we train a U-GNN diffusion model, primarily based on the implementation described in
[
12
]
. The architecture has a total depth of
3
and stacks encoder-decoder levels in a U-shape with skip connections. The channel states
{\mathbf{H}}
are processed with
2
-layered,
64
-channel GNN blocks with
2
-hop aggregation at each encoder and decoder level, while the embeddings of diffusion step
k
and conditional (node) features
{\mathbf{u}}
both use
128
channels. We employ a linear schedule of
K=500
noise steps and accelerated DDIM sampling with
100
steps. Overall, the dataset contains
|{\mathcal{T}}|\times 5=640
network state instances and
200
expert samples per instance. We train U-GNN policies for a maximum of
5000
epochs with the AdamW optimizer and a learning rate of
10^{-4}
. Due to limited space, we defer most implementation and training details. We will make the full code publicly available upon publication.

We compare the U-GNN and expert policies with two deterministic baselines:

(i) Average-power transmission policy (AP)
: For each given
{\mathbf{H}}
, we fix the transmission policy to the conditional mean of the expert conditionals, i.e.,
{\mathbf{x}}^{\text{AP}}_{t}({\mathbf{H}})=\E_{\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{x}}}^{*}({\mathbf{H}})}[{\mathbf{x}}]\approx(1/K)\sum_{k=K_{0}}^{K+K_{0}}{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
at all slots [cf. (
13
)].

(ii) Full-power transmission policy (FP)
: All transmitters use all the transmission power available, i.e.,
{\mathbf{x}}^{\text{FP}}_{t}({\mathbf{H}})=P_{\max}\mathbf{1}_{N}
at all slots
t
.

3.2
Numerical Results

We perform a 5:1:2 split of the dataset
{\mathcal{T}}
across training, validation, and test sets, and draw
100
samples from the U-GNN policy for evaluating each test network. Fig.
2
shows the time-evolution of the cumulative ergodic p
1
-rates (1st percentile), p
5
-rates (5th percentile), and mean-rates (sum-rate utility
f_{0}
normalized by
N=400
) across receivers from all test networks for a reference

Overall, the U-GNN policy closely matches the primal–dual expert in the lower-tail percentiles while achieving comparable mean-rate utility. At the 5th percentile, both policies surpass the QoS threshold within 20 time-sharing slots and converge to
0.75
bits/s/Hz by 100 slots, with the U-GNN and expert curves nearly superimposed throughout. The two deterministic baselines, FP and AP, plateau at
0.20
and
0.40
bits/s/Hz, respectively, and fail to meet the QoS constraint. This trend becomes more nuanced at the 1st percentile, where the U-GNN reaches
0.45
bits/s/Hz, substantially outperforming AP at
0.15
bits/s/Hz and FP at
0.08
bits/s/Hz. In terms of mean-rate utility, the U-GNN achieves
3.14
bits/s/Hz, slightly better than the expert and AP, while FP attains a higher aggregate throughput of
3.3
bits/s/Hz at the expense of severe QoS unfairness.

We remark that the benchmark expert policy is sampled by running thousands of primal–dual iterations on these test networks, so the comparison is naturally favorable to the expert. Although a gap emerges at the p
1
tail as time progresses, the U-GNN remains close to the expert while replacing thousands of primal–dual iterations with a single DDIM sampling pass. We also note that the optimal power control policies are generally multi-modal and stochastic [cf. Fig.

4
Conclusion

We proposed a generative diffusion modeling framework for stochastic resource allocation and applied it to optimal power control in ad-hoc networks. Using a primal–dual algorithm to generate expert samples and parametrizing the diffusion policy with a U-GNN architecture tailored for graph signal diffusion, we learned to sample from optimal (expert) allocation distributions for given networks (graphs) and node states. The learned policies also generalized well across QoS levels and networks of varying sizes and densities. These results show that graph signal diffusion models are promising tools for learning stochastic policies in constrained wireless optimization.
