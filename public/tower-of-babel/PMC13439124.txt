Genome-wide network of antibiotic susceptibility in Pseudomonas aeruginosa
Authors: Océane Goncalves, Jean-Philippe Côté
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and Objective Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a ubiquitous Gram-negative pathogen notorious for causing infections with high mortality rates. Its large genome supports extensive metabolic diversity and promotes its adaptation to diverse environments. The survival and persistence of P. aeruginosa in clinical settings is further facilitated by a vast arsenal of strategies that contribute to its tolerance or resistance to antibiotics. Here, we aimed to systematically identify the genetic determinants that affect its susceptibility to antibiotics.MethodsThe ordered PA14 transposon mutant library was grown in the presence of sub-MIC concentrations of a panel of antibiotics of various classes, and the growth of each mutant was quantified to generate susceptibility scores.ResultsWe observed a dense network of genes affecting antibiotic susceptibility in P. aeruginosa, where a large portion of genes modulated susceptibility to various classes of antibiotics. Not surprisingly, efflux and outer membrane permeability were key contributors, but we also identified genes that are not typically associated with antibiotic susceptibility.ConclusionsThe data provide a genome-wide view of the antibiotic susceptibility network in P. aeruginosa. Overall, our approach deepens our understanding of antibiotic susceptibility and opens new avenues for developing strategies against multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is an opportunistic pathogen that causes acute and chronic infections in immunocompromised individuals.1 P. aeruginosa strains have large genomes providing diverse metabolic capacities and an extensive and diverse array of virulence factors that contribute to its ubiquity.2,3 In addition, P. aeruginosa possesses a remarkable ability to resist antibiotics because of intrinsic, adaptive, and acquired resistance.3,4

The extensive genome of P. aeruginosa contains numerous genes that affect its susceptibility and represent intrinsic antibiotic resistance factors.5 The primary factor contributing to the intrinsic resistance of P. aeruginosa is the low permeability of its outer membrane, which exhibits approximately 12 to 100 times lower permeability than that of Escherichia coli.6,7 Unlike E. coli, P. aeruginosa lacks non-specific porins like OmpF, which facilitate the diffusion of small molecules,8 but instead, possesses specific porins that mostly exclude antibiotics.9 In addition, P. aeruginosa possesses many efflux pumps to actively export antibiotics from the cell.10,11

Adaptive antibiotic resistance in P. aeruginosa is a transient phenomenon that enhances bacterial survival against antibiotics through reversible changes in gene expression in response to an environmental stimulus. The most well-characterized adaptive resistance mechanisms include biofilm formation and persisters, which both contribute to chronic infections and poor prognosis in cystic fibrosis (CF) patients.1,4

Acquired resistance occurs through genetic mutations or horizontal gene transfer. Mutations often result in the reduction of antibiotic uptake.3 For example, mutations in OprD conferring carbapenem resistance, or single-nucleotide polymorphisms causing the overexpression of efflux pumps such as MexAB-OprM and MexXY-OprM and a decrease in susceptibility to β-lactams, aminoglycosides, and fluoroquinolones, have been observed.4 Horizontal gene transfer, on the other hand, facilitates the acquisition of resistance genes through plasmids, transposons or prophages.4

This extensive resistance repertoire positions P. aeruginosa within the ESKAPE group of pathogens that are known to evade antibiotic treatments.12 Nevertheless, a significant proportion of gene functions in P. aeruginosa remains unidentified. In recent decades, significant strides have been made in sequencing the genomes of pathogens like P. aeruginosa.13 Today, their complete genomic sequences are fully accessible, and we now have genome-wide mutant collections for many of these pathogens. These are systematic libraries where each strain carries a specific genetic deletion or one transposon insertion, such as the non-redundant transposon mutant library of P. aeruginosa strain PA14.14 Such collections greatly facilitate the exploration of bacterial genomic networks and help researchers to systematically assess gene functions and interactions or key pathways that drive bacterial survival under antimicrobial pressure.15

Here, we exposed the PA14 mutant library to sub-inhibitory concentrations of a broad panel of antibiotics with different mechanisms of action. This allowed us to identify genetic determinants affecting the response to antibiotic stress and build a network of antibiotic susceptibility of P. aeruginosa PA14. Our results revealed a core set of genes that are key determinants influencing antibiotic susceptibility and identified novel chemical genomics interactions. Overall, our results lead to a better comprehension of the bacterial cell response in the presence of various antimicrobials and may reveal potential strategies to counteract antibiotic resistance in P. aeruginosa.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

The PA14 collection14 was grown from frozen stocks onto Luria–Bertani (LB) agar medium in 96-density. Colonies were grown overnight at 37°C, then upscaled to 384-density using a Singer Rotor HDA (Singer Instruments, Somerset, UK) and again grown overnight. From the 384-density plates, plates were upscaled to 1536-density, grown overnight, and duplicated in LB medium to make master plates. Master plates were maintained at this density at 4°C for 2–3 weeks. PA14 plates older than this were recultured using the above steps before screening, to limit the emergence of suppressor strains and/or passage effects. All plates used in this study contained 25 mL of agar medium and were dried on a completely level surface.

MIC determinations

MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of antibiotic at which no visible growth was detected after 16 h at 37 °C. The MICs were determined at least in duplicate. Overnight cultures were standardized to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 1 and diluted 1:1000 in LB broth before being added to a 96-well plate. Antibiotics (purchased from Sigma-Aldrich) were prepared at appropriate concentrations by serial 1:2 dilutions across the plate. Antibiotic stocks were prepared at 100× concentration in DMSO, H2O, or PBS, resulting in a final solvent concentration of 1% (v/v) in all susceptibility assays. Antibiotics were solubilized in DMSO (amoxicillin, azithromycin, chloramphenicol, delafloxacin, erythromycin, nalidixic acid, rifampicin, sulfamethoxazole, tetracycline, trimethoprim), H2O (ampicillin, ceftriaxone, cefuroxime, colistin, kanamycin, novobiocin, ruthenium red, spectinomycin, streptomycin, tobramycin, vancomycin), or PBS (apramycin, ceftazidime). MIC assays were performed using a BioTek Synergy HT plate reader. Liquid MICs were determined to provide reference values comparable to published literature and for use in individual mutant confirmation assays (Figures 3d and 4d).

Solid media MICs of drugs were determined using a unique protocol described by French et al.16 Briefly, an initial bed of 25 mL of 2% agarose was poured into an empty Singer PlusPlate and allowed to dry. Circular holes were cut out using a size 16 test tube, and each hole was filled with LB agar containing different drug concentrations. The concentrations ranged from 1× to 128× the liquid MIC. After drying, the agarose template was removed with a sterile scalpel, leaving plugs of media with a drug concentration gradient. P. aeruginosa PA14 wild-type was pinned in 1536-density from a master plate onto the agar plugs, using consistent Singer Rotor HDA settings throughout the study, and grown for 18 h at 37°C. This ensures that the inoculum for the MIC determination is equivalent to the inoculum during the chemical–genetic interaction screening. Because the solid MIC format replicates the physical conditions of the screen in terms of inoculum density, agar composition, and colony format, solid MICs were used to determine the sub-inhibitory concentration for all library screens.

PA14 library screen

Rectangular plates with 25 mL of LB 1.5% agar containing no antibiotic or 1/4 of the MIC of each antibiotic (Table 1) were poured and allowed to dry overnight. An ordered PA14 mutant library was pinned from source plates in 1536 colony density onto the rectangular agar plates using the ROTOR HDA (Singer Instruments). The screen was performed in duplicate using different source plates. Plates were incubated for 18 h at 37˚C, then imaged using a Phenobooth imaging system (Singer Instruments) using the transmissive light mode. Images were further processed in FIJI (ImageJ) using an ImageJ script available and detailed in the manuscript of French et al.16 Briefly, images were first split into 1536 regions of interest (with each colony on the plate) and the light absorbed by each colony was quantified as an integrated density value, which tracks with cell number in a linear manner. Integrated densities were then normalized for plate position effects, and the relative growth was determined by comparing to the untreated control. We then calculated a susceptibility score corresponding to the ratio of the normalized integrated density of each mutant in treated over untreated conditions, where values close to 1 indicate no significant growth change compared to untreated conditions. Mutants with susceptibility scores at least three standard deviations below the median (median—3×SD) were classified as significantly more susceptible to the treatment. This ratio-based score is specifically designed to distinguish antibiotic-specific growth effects from constitutive fitness defects: mutants with intrinsic growth defects display proportionally reduced growth in both treated and untreated conditions, yielding scores close to 1 and therefore not meeting the hypersusceptibility threshold. We used 3× SD, rather than a lower cutoff, to select only the mutants with the strongest phenotypes.

Results

Genetic responses of P. aeruginosa to antibiotic challenges

We first aimed to determine how P. aeruginosa was affected by various antibiotic treatments. To do so, we looked at the growth of a genome-wide mutant library of P. aeruginosa strain PA1414 in the presence of sub-minimal inhibitory concentrations (MIC) of antibiotics. We selected a panel of compounds from different antibiotic classes that target a wide range of cellular processes (Table S1, available as Supplementary data at JAC Online). This panel includes both clinically relevant compounds against P. aeruginosa and compounds with limited intrinsic activity against this organism. These antibiotics serve as chemical probes to interrogate specific susceptibility barriers, such as outer membrane integrity and efflux capacity, independently of target-based mechanisms.36–38 For each compound, the sub-inhibitory concentration used in the screen was set at one quarter of the solid medium MIC (¼ MIC), a concentration that allows growth of the wild-type strain while revealing fitness defects in susceptible mutants.16 To increase throughput, we conducted high-density growth assays on solid media (Figure S1A). Colony growth was assessed by imaging the high-density plates and isolating individual mutants from plate images to quantify their density as described previously.16 We calculated a susceptibility score for each mutant, defined as the ratio between the growth in treated and untreated conditions. A score of 1 indicates that growth was unaffected by the antibiotic (Figure S1B). Mutants with susceptibility scores at least 3 standard deviations below the median were classified as more susceptible to the treatment. For instance, hypersusceptible mutants to vancomycin were largely linked to genes involved in membrane structure and lipid organization, indicating that increased membrane permeability enhances vancomycin susceptibility as expected (Figure S1C-D and Table S2).39

We did observe mutants that grew better in the presence of antibiotics (susceptibility score > 1). For instance, the nfxB mutant displayed scores above 1 for several antibiotics, including delafloxacin, tetracycline, erythromycin, chloramphenicol, and azithromycin, consistent with the known role of NfxB as a repressor of MexCD-OprJ, whose derepression confers increased resistance to fluoroquinolones, as originally described by Hirai et al.40 The PA14_57180 mutant (putative cob(I)alamin adenosyltransferase) displayed elevated scores specifically for aminoglycosides. Beyond these examples, additional mutants including rluD, purF, topA and mutS, displayed elevated scores for trimethoprim, apramycin, and/or streptomycin. In general, however, hypersusceptible mutants were more informative on the pathways involved in the susceptibility to our panel of antibiotics. Therefore, we have focused our analysis on hypersusceptible genes.

620 mutants conferred increased susceptibility to at least one antibiotic in our panel (Figure 1 and Table S3). While approximately 40% of these genes increased susceptibility to a single antibiotic, a central core of genes broadly influenced antibiotic susceptibility. As expected, most antibiotics clustered according to their mechanism of action. For instance, aminoglycosides (shown in blue) clustered together on the network map, with many mutants being more susceptible to all aminoglycosides tested, indicating a shared response. This was also the case for β-lactams antibiotics (shown in green), macrolides (yellow), and quinolones (pink). Overall, our results highlight a complex genetic network underlying antibiotic susceptibility in P. aeruginosa, where many mutants influence antibiotic susceptibility across multiple classes.

Critical determinants for the susceptibility of P. aeruginosa to antibiotics

A subset of mutants from the PA14 mutant library displayed increased susceptibility to multiple antibiotics (Table 1). Notably, most of these genes have already been implicated in antibiotic resistance, including mexA and oprM, the most frequent hits in our screen. Mutant armZ (PA14_72210) showed increased susceptibility to eight antibiotics, including all aminoglycosides tested. The armZ gene encodes an anti-repressor of the mexXY operon, a major efflux system involved in aminoglycoside resistance.29 Mutations in armZ are frequently identified in clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa that are resistant to tobramycin.41 Not surprisingly, the mexY (PA14_38410) mutant (there is no mexX mutant in the collection) also potentiated all aminoglycosides tested, supporting the central role of the MexXY efflux system in mediating aminoglycoside resistance (Table 1). Similarly, mutant fis (PA14_64190) was more susceptible to nine antibiotics, including both tested fluoroquinolones. The fis gene encodes a global transcriptional regulator involved in modulating DNA topology and gene expression.42,43 Recent studies have shown that the Fis protein contributes to ciprofloxacin (another fluoroquinolone) resistance in P. aeruginosa through the regulation of pyocin production.27

In addition to well-characterized resistance determinants, Table 1 also includes genes with less defined roles in antibiotic susceptibility. For instance, mutant PA14_02410 showed increased susceptibility to seven antibiotics spanning multiple classes, including many antibiotics (erythromycin, rifampicin, vancomycin, and azithromycin, for instance) that depend on membrane permeability or membrane potential to penetrate inside the cell. Databases suggest that the PA14_02410 gene encodes a TonB-dependent receptor localized in the outer membrane.44 It is thus possible that the susceptibility of the PA14_02410 mutant to various antibiotics is explained by a disruption of the outer membrane integrity. More work is needed to understand the role of the PA14_02410 protein in antibiotic susceptibility. Other genes, such as cupD1, prfH, snr1, and PA14_58110, have also not been linked to antibiotic susceptibility before, but were more sensitive to multiple antibiotics in our screen.

Enrichment of resistance determinants among antibiotic-responsive genes

To further identify antimicrobial resistance determinants, we analyzed our dataset using the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD).45 Unsurprisingly, our dataset was enriched for efflux-related genes and regulators of efflux pumps (Figure 2 and Table S4). For instance, the mexA and oprM mutants showed increased susceptibility to most of the antibiotics tested (Figure 2). The MexAB-OprM efflux system is a key multidrug resistance determinant in P. aeruginosa.17,46 As stated previously, the mexY mutant and the mexXY regulator armZ mutant displayed increased susceptibility to many antibiotics tested. MexY is a component of the MexXY-OprM resistance-nodulation-division (RND) family multidrug efflux system and is widely recognized for its role in mediating aminoglycoside and macrolide resistance in P. aeruginosa.47,48 The nfxB (PA14_60860) mutant exhibited increased susceptibility to four antibiotics tested (apramycin, kanamycin, streptomycin, and tobramycin). NfxB influences outer membrane permeability, and the hypersusceptibility of the nfxB mutant to aminoglycosides is consistent with the original characterization of nfxB mutations in P. aeruginosa by Hirai et al.,40 who demonstrated that nfxB mutants display increased susceptibility to aminoglycosides and β-lactams attributed to alterations in outer membrane permeability. CARD identified a homology between PA14_23700 and adeL from Acinetobacter baumannii, which regulates the expression of the AdeFGH and AdeIJK RND efflux systems.49 In A. baumannii, adeL mutations lead to efflux pump overexpression and confer resistance to tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones, suggesting that PA14_23700 may act as a regulator of RND-type efflux pumps in P. aeruginosa.

CARD also highlighted other antibiotic resistance mechanisms, notably genes affecting envelope biogenesis. For instance, the yajC (PA14_14610) mutant exhibited increased susceptibility exclusively to aminoglycosides (tobramycin, kanamycin, streptomycin, and apramycin). yajC is located upstream of secD and secF, which encode essential components of the Sec translocon involved in protein translocation across the inner membrane. LptD is an essential component of the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) transport system in P. aeruginosa.50 This protein is part of the LptABCDEFG complex, which bridges the cell envelope and facilitates LPS assembly at the outer membrane. Given the role of LPS in regulating membrane permeability, it is hypothesized that loss of LptD increases membrane permeability.28 Increased susceptibility of the lptD mutant was observed for aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, and macrolides. Reduced aminoglycoside uptake due to altered membrane permeability is a well-established resistance mechanism in P. aeruginosa, particularly among clinical isolates from CF patients.25,51,52 In contrast, the mechanism underlying the increased susceptibility to delafloxacin and nalidixic acid in the absence of LptD remains unclear. In P. aeruginosa, EnvZ is part of a two-component regulatory system (EnvZ-OmpR) that senses environmental changes such as osmotic stress and modulates gene expression53. This system has been implicated in controlling outer membrane permeability and efflux pump regulation. In our screen, the envZ mutant was more susceptible to aminoglycoside antibiotics.

The kinB (PA14_72390) mutant exhibited increased susceptibility to nine of the 22 antibiotics tested (amoxicillin, azithromycin, chloramphenicol, ceftriaxone, erythromycin, nalidixic acid, novobiocin, rifampicin, and vancomycin) (Figure 2). According to the CARD database, kinB shows similarity to baeS, classified as a resistance-nodulation-cell division (RND) antibiotic efflux pump; however, the sequence similarity is only 26.75%, suggesting a distinct function. In P. aeruginosa, KinB is an inner membrane histidine kinase that regulates alginate production and multiple virulence traits through the KinB-AlgB system and interaction with RpoN.19 It is possible that the disruption of this gene affects the membrane homeostasis or efflux activity.

Among β-lactam-responsive mutants, ampC—encoding the chromosomally encoded cephalosporinase of P. aeruginosa—was identified as a significant hit for ampicillin and amoxicillin but was absent from the hit lists for third-generation cephalosporins ceftazidime and ceftriaxone.18 Consistently, ygbF—a regulator of AmpC-mediated β-lactam resistance—was also identified as a multidrug hit in our screen (Table 1), further supporting the contribution of the AmpC regulatory pathway to β-lactam susceptibility in P. aeruginosa.

Genes affecting the susceptibility of P. aeruginosa to tobramycin

Tobramycin is a frontline antibiotic used in the treatment of P. aeruginosa infections in individuals with CF.51,54 A total of sixty-five mutants showed reduced growth in the presence of a sub-inhibitory concentration of tobramycin (Figure 3).

Most of these mutants were associated with transport functions and encoded permeases or predicted transporters (Figure 3c-d and Table S2). Notably, components of major efflux systems, including mexA, oprM, mexZ, mexY, were identified as expected,17,47 in addition to several genes encoding transport or membrane-associated proteins. For instance, PA14_41190 (ppiD), an inner membrane-associated chaperone involved in Sec-dependent translocation, secD (PA14_14630), and yajC (PA14_14610), which encodes a small inner membrane protein that forms part of the Sec translocon operon, all exhibited reduced MICs to tobramycin. The co-occurrence of these genes suggests that the integrity or functionality of the Sec system may influence membrane homeostasis or protein assembly processes that indirectly affect aminoglycoside uptake. Other mutants in genes affecting envelope biogenesis or efflux were more susceptible to tobramycin as expected, such as yidC (PA14_73410), vacJ (PA14_27920), lptD (PA14_07770), envZ (PA14_68680) and armZ (PA14_72210) for instance. Another interesting hit was hflK (PA14_65280), which encodes a subunit of the HflK protease complex. HflK acts as a membrane-associated chaperone with FtsH, an essential ATP-dependent protease involved in maintaining inner membrane protein quality and integrity. Disruption of hflK may compromise the stability of membrane proteins and potentially alter envelope homeostasis, increasing aminoglycoside uptake.55

Together, these findings support the notion that drug permeation and intracellular accumulation represent key determinants of tobramycin susceptibility, consistent with previous reports.23,36 The addition of compounds permeabilizing the outer membrane of P. aeruginosa may therefore enhance its activity.37

Genes affecting the susceptibility of P. aeruginosa to delafloxacin

Delafloxacin is a recently approved fluoroquinolone that targets both DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV56 and represents a promising therapeutic option for the treatment of ciprofloxacin-resistant P. aeruginosa infections in patients with CF.57 Due to its recent clinical introduction, limited information is available regarding the genetic factors associated with resistance or reduced susceptibility to delafloxacin. Its enhanced activity in the CF lung environment is attributed to its distinct anionic chemical structure, which is particularly effective under the mildly acidic conditions characteristic of the CF airway (pH ∼6.8).58,59

In total, 49 mutants displayed increased susceptibility in the presence of sub-inhibitory concentrations of delafloxacin (Figure 4). As anticipated, a substantial number of the disrupted genes (13 out of 49) were associated with DNA-related processes (Figure 4c-d and Table S2), including DNA repair, DNA damage response, and homologous recombination. For instance, inactivation of radA, ruvAB, and xerCD all exhibited increased susceptibility to delafloxacin. Mutant ftsK (PA14_30290), an essential septum-associated DNA translocase that coordinates DNA segregation and cell division,60 also increased susceptibility to delafloxacin. The direct role of FtsK in antibiotic resistance is not clear, but its perturbation could compromise chromosome segregation and cell envelope integrity, leading to increased susceptibility to fluoroquinolone-induced DNA damage. Another mutant linked to DNA repair that increased susceptibility to delafloxacin was dinG (PA14_50840). This gene encodes an ATP-dependent DNA helicase crucial for DNA unwinding during replication. Contrary to other DNA-related genes identified, the effect of dinG inactivation was specific to delafloxacin.

Besides DNA-related processes, we also identified efflux genes (e.g. oprM, PA14_57870, PA14_18090) and outer membrane-related processes (e.g. lptCD, htrB1, oprF). Other genes identified in the screen were associated with metabolism or remain functionally uncharacterized in the context of antibiotic resistance. For example, the PA14_67920 (hisH) mutant exhibited increased susceptibility specifically to delafloxacin. HisH participates in L-histidine biosynthesis, suggesting a possible indirect link between amino acid metabolism and antibiotic susceptibility. Likewise, the PA14_67900 mutant (located within the histidine operon) also displayed increased susceptibility to delafloxacin. Although the precise role of this gene remains unclear, its genomic proximity to hisH may indicate a coordinated contribution to cellular processes influencing antibiotic response.

Overall, we identified many genes increasing the susceptibility to delafloxacin that are related to the function of fluoroquinolones. In addition, we also identified genes involved in LPS transport, efflux systems, and membrane permeability, providing insight into multiple mechanisms contributing to delafloxacin resistance. Finally, we also identified genes whose role in fluoroquinolone susceptibility is unclear and need further investigation.

Discussion

This comprehensive chemical-genetic analysis reveals the genetic landscape underlying the response of P. aeruginosa to a diverse panel of antibiotics. By systematically measuring the growth of all mutants of a genome-wide mutant library of P. aeruginosa strain PA14 in the presence of sub-inhibitory concentrations of antibiotics, we have identified genes that contribute to antibiotic susceptibility. Our list of genes includes well-characterized resistance determinants as well as genes that have not been linked to antibiotic susceptibility.

Previous studies have explored the resistome of P. aeruginosa using genome-wide mutant libraries to identify determinants of susceptibility to specific antibiotic classes.31,36,38,61 Our approach builds on these studies, but by including multiple antibiotics, also reveals cross-class connectivity and genes that have not been previously linked to antibiotic susceptibility. Our approach sometimes complements or overlaps with results obtained in these studies. For instance, while Schurek et al. looked for mutants of the PA14 library that decreased susceptibility to tobramycin, their results suggest important interactions between tobramycin and the bacterial cell and the importance of cell permeability, similar to our results.36 Similarly, several PA14 mutants found to increase susceptibility to ciprofloxacin38 also increased susceptibility to the fluoroquinolones tested here.

Not surprisingly, efflux systems, MexAB-OprM in particular, emerged as central players in multidrug resistance. These observations are consistent with their well-known prominent role in intrinsic resistance mechanisms.17,62 Additionally, our results highlight the contribution of genes involved in membrane structure, transport, and regulatory pathways—such as armZ, fis, envZ, kinB, and several TonB-dependent receptors—to the susceptibility landscape of P. aeruginosa. Integration with the CARD database validated many of our findings and confirmed the robustness of the methodology used. While CARD is a valuable resource for annotating known resistance genes, it can only identify well-established mechanisms based on online literature and experimental data. As a result, genes that contribute indirectly to resistance—through regulation, membrane permeability, metabolic adaptation, or stress response—may not be annotated as resistance determinants in this database. For example, hits such as cupD1, prfH, and snr1 were associated with susceptibility to multiple antibiotics but are not currently linked to resistance in CARD, suggesting unexplored roles in cell envelope homeostasis or stress adaptation. Functional validation of candidate genes identified in this screen—through genetic complementation, clean deletion construction, or cross-background reproducibility—represents a natural continuation of this work and a recommended next step for prioritized hits.

We further focused our analysis on two clinically relevant antibiotics: tobramycin and delafloxacin. Tobramycin is commonly used for chronic P. aeruginosa infections in CF patients by inhalation administration.63 On the other hand, delafloxacin is a recently approved fluoroquinolone with enhanced activity under acidic conditions typical of CF airways, with activity against ciprofloxacin-resistant strains.57

For tobramycin, our results revealed a dominant role for genes involved in transport and membrane permeability, including subunits of efflux pumps such as mexA, mexD, and oprM, as well as other transport-related genes such as trkA and aroP1. Similarly, pathways that modulate outer membrane composition, such as the OmpR/EnvZ two-component system (also referred to as AmgRS), also contribute to tobramycin susceptibility. Not surprisingly, genes affecting tobramycin susceptibility were also identified with other aminoglycosides. These findings highlight the importance of intracellular drug accumulation and efflux in driving aminoglycoside resistance, which has been observed in P. aeruginosa and other Gram-negative bacteria previously.17,47,52

For delafloxacin, our findings strongly implicate DNA repair and recombination pathways (radA, ruvA, ruvB, xerC, fis, ftsK, dinG) as critical determinants of susceptibility. These genes maintain chromosome integrity and resolve DNA damage, which is consistent with the mode of action of fluoroquinolones targeting DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV. Disruption of these pathways is consistent with a model in which compromised DNA repair capacity exacerbates fluoroquinolone-induced replication stress, though direct mechanistic validation remains to be established. These findings raise the hypothesis that inhibitors of bacterial DNA repair pathways may potentiate fluoroquinolone efficacy, a possibility that needs to be tested. Interestingly, fis—a global regulator—was linked to susceptibility across multiple classes, including fluoroquinolones and aminoglycosides. This observation is consistent with the possibility that nucleoid-associated proteins may broadly influence antibiotic response through transcriptional control and DNA topology in P. aeruginosa. Genes involved in membrane integrity (lptC, lptD, htrB1) and efflux (oprM, PA14_57870, PA14_18090) were also identified in the delafloxacin screen. The htrB1 gene encodes an acyltransferase involved in lipid A modification. Loss of lipid A acylation has been associated with increased membrane permeability and enhanced antibiotic uptake.33 Although no direct evidence links LPS transport systems to delafloxacin susceptibility, compromised membrane integrity may increase antibiotic penetration. Although delafloxacin has been described as less susceptible to efflux-mediated resistance than earlier fluoroquinolones, the identification of efflux-related hits in our screen suggests that MexAB-OprM nonetheless contributes to delafloxacin resistance in P. aeruginosa. Our data therefore suggest that efflux, alongside membrane permeability, modulates delafloxacin activity. A study has shown that in Escherichia coli, the AcrAB-TolC efflux pump contributes significantly to delafloxacin resistance.64 These observations open the possibility of combination therapies using membrane-permeabilizing agents to potentiate delafloxacin efficacy, particularly in CF lung conditions where acidic pH enhances its activity.

Overall, this study provides a systematic overview of the genetic landscape response of P. aeruginosa to different antibiotics. While efflux systems remain central to multidrug resistance, our data highlight additional layers of complexity involving membrane integrity, transport pathways, global regulators, and stress response mechanisms. The screen enables the identification of genes with unexplored roles in antibiotic resistance, suggesting that new antibiotic resistance mechanisms have not been discovered yet and might be used as potential avenues to treat P. aeruginosa infections. In general, the convergence of efflux, permeability, and DNA repair mechanisms across antibiotic classes opens opportunities for combination strategies, including efflux inhibitors, membrane-permeabilizing agents, and DNA repair blockers to potentiate existing drugs. Beyond P. aeruginosa, many of these mechanisms are shared among Gram-negative pathogens, reinforcing the translational potential of this work. In the future, it would be interesting to compare our results with similar experiments performed in infection-relevant conditions, such as artificial sputum media that mimic the environment encountered by P. aeruginosa in Cystic Fibrosis. Overall, our study expands the understanding of antibiotic susceptibility networks and provides a foundation for rational design of interventions targeting multidrug-resistant infections.
