Interaction of twisted light with free twisted atoms
I. Pavlov, A. Chaikovskaia, D. Karlovets

Abstract
We investigate absorption and scattering of structured light by atoms, treating the photon and the atomic center of mass as spatially localized wave packets. We show that vortex photons can transfer orbital angular momentum (OAM) to the atomic center of mass with near-perfect efficiency in head-on collisions when the impact parameter b b is smaller than the atomic transverse coherence length σ \sigma , which ranges from nanometers to sub-micrometer scales. Larger offsets result in a shifted mean OAM and a finite variance, both controlled by the ratio b / σ b/\sigma . The wave-packet nature of light enables electronic transitions that violate standard selection rules, albeit with a clear hierarchy where the dipole transition dominates. For femtosecond pulses, the finite spatial coherence of the photon leads to measurable shaping of the resonant absorption lines. We demonstrate a transverse recoil of the atom in a vicinity of the photonic vortex, dubbed “the superkick”, and its dual effect - “the selfkick” - when an initially twisted atomic packet experiences recoil upon absorbing a gaussian photon. These phenomena are within reach of experimental capabilities using structured light in combination with cold atomic beams and ions in Penning traps, providing a route to the controlled generation and manipulation of non-gaussian atomic packets.

Twisted beams of individual atoms have been experimentally realized using diffraction gratings

Here, we develop a theory of interactions between quantized radiation and localized atomic packets, including twisted states as a special case, using second-order perturbation theory and nonrelativistic velocities beyond the dipole and the infinitely heavy nucleus approximations. II
Interaction of an atom with external field

We start with a theoretical framework for the interaction of localized atoms with quantum light. Specifically, we use the quantum-mechanical perturbation theory for a nonrelativistic hydrogen-like ion interacting with a quantized electromagnetic field in free space. Next, we stay within first two orders of perturbation theory enabling the description of single-photon absorption (photoexcitation) and scattering. The key differences of our approach from the standard methods are the following:

1.

We treat both the photon and the atom as spatially localized and nonstationary packets. The “vortex” packets carrying OAM are of special interest to us, and we use the basis of the Hermite-Gaussian
\times
Laguerre-Gaussian (HG
\times
LG) modes to describe them (see details in Appendices
B
,
C
and
D
).

2.

We use neither a multipole expansion nor the infinitely heavy nucleus approximation.

Note that the Schrödinger equation for an electron and a nucleus is usually separated into two equations: one with Coulomb potential for the
relative motion
, which coincides with the electron in the limit of infinitely heavy nucleus, and one for the free center-of-mass (CM) dynamics (see Appendix
C
). In what follows, we refer to the relative motion component as “electron”, and sometimes refer to the CM component simply as “atom”.

II.3
Second-order processes: scattering

Analogously to absorption, we generalize the well-known resonant scattering amplitude

;
Sakurai 1967
, arising from the second-order perturbation theory, to our problem (the details are in Appendix
A
). The plane-wave amplitude reads

S_{fi}^{(2,PW)}=-i(2\pi)^{4}\delta\left(\varepsilon^{\text{(CM)}}_{i}({\bf P}_{i})+\varepsilon^{e}_{i}+\omega_{i}-\varepsilon^{\text{(CM)}}_{f}({\bf P}_{f})-\omega_{f}-\varepsilon^{e}_{f}\right)\delta({\bf P}_{i}+{\bf k}_{i}-{\bf P}_{f}-{\bf k}_{f})\frac{{\displaystyle e^{2}}}{{\displaystyle\sqrt{4\omega_{i}\omega_{f}}}}\\
\times\sum_{\tilde{\psi}^{e}}\Biggl[\frac{{\displaystyle\left(\bm{e}_{\lambda_{i}}({\bf k}_{i})\cdot\bm{J}_{i\tilde{\psi}^{e}}({\bf k}_{i},{\bf P}_{i})\right)\left(\bm{e}_{\lambda_{f}}({\bf k}_{f})\cdot{\bm{J}_{f\tilde{\psi}^{e}}}({\bf k}_{f},{\bf P}_{f})\right)^{*}}}{{\displaystyle\varepsilon_{i}^{e}-\tilde{\varepsilon}^{e}+\omega_{i}+\frac{{\displaystyle{\bf P}_{i}^{2}}}{{\displaystyle 2(m+M)}}-\frac{{\displaystyle({\bf P}_{i}+{\bf k}_{i})^{2}}}{{\displaystyle 2(m+M)}}}}+\frac{{\displaystyle\left(\bm{e}_{\lambda_{i}}({\bf k}_{i})\cdot\bm{J}_{\tilde{\psi}^{e}f}({\bf k}_{i},{\bf P}_{f})\right)\left(\bm{e}_{\lambda_{f}}({\bf k}_{f})\cdot{\bm{J}_{\tilde{\psi}^{e}i}}({\bf k}_{f},-{\bf P}_{i})\right)^{*}}}{{\displaystyle\varepsilon_{i}^{e}-\tilde{\varepsilon}^{e}+\frac{{\displaystyle{\bf P}_{i}^{2}}}{{\displaystyle 2(m+M)}}-\frac{{\displaystyle({\bf P}_{i}-{\bf k}_{f})^{2}}}{{\displaystyle 2(m+M)}}-\omega_{f}}}\Biggr].

(19)

Here
\tilde{\psi}^{e}
denotes the intermediate electronic states. As before, the momentum conservation includes only the CM momenta
{\bf P}_{i,f}
and the photon momenta
{\bf k}_{i,f}
, whereas the energy conservation law includes the electron energies
\varepsilon^{e}_{i,f}

\varepsilon^{\text{(CM)}}_{i,f}={\bf P}_{i_{f}}^{2}/2(m+M)

\omega_{i,f}=|{\bf k}_{i,f}|

There is, however, another contribution to the scattering, which arises from the Hamiltonian quadratic in the vector potential in the first order of perturbation theory (the “seagull” diagram

to the total amplitude is known to be suppressed w.r.t.
S_{fi}^{(1.5)}
by a factor of
\omega/m

Messiah 2014
. In the resonant regime, however, the situation becomes
completely reversed
, and we can safely neglect the “seagull” term

. A straightforward generalization for wave packets in the initial state reads

S_{fi}=\sum_{\lambda_{i}}\int\frac{{\displaystyle\differential^{3}P_{i}}}{{\displaystyle(2\pi)^{3}}}\frac{{\displaystyle\differential^{3}k_{i}}}{{\displaystyle(2\pi)^{3}}}S_{fi}^{(PW)}\psi^{\text{(CM)}}_{i}({{\bf P}}_{i})\psi^{\gamma}_{i}({{\bf k}}_{i},\lambda_{i}),

(20)

where the plane-wave scattering amplitude can be either (
19
), or (
78
), or their sum.
By analogy with the absorption amplitude (
5
), the S-matrix element (
20
) can be regarded as a two-particle momentum-space wave function describing an

of the photon and the atom after the scattering process ending at the time instance

S_{fi}e^{-it_{f}(\varepsilon^{\text{(CM)}}_{f}({\bf P}_{f})+\omega_{f})}=\langle{\bf P}_{f},{\bf k}_{f},|\psi^{\text{(CM+$\gamma$, ev)}}(t_{f})\rangle

For a head-on collision, this evolved state must be an eigenfunction of the
total
angular momentum (TAM) projection operator that is a sum of the TAM operators for each counterpart, the CM and the photon. The individual values of the photon TAM and the electron OAM, however, are not defined due to entanglement, which is why the possibility of the vortex photon/atom generation depends on the measurement schemes

;
Karlovets et al. 2022
;
Chaikovskaia et al. 2024

II.4
Probabilities and cross sections

In contrast to the plane-wave scattering theory
Sakurai 1967
;
Messiah 2014
, current more general wave-packet approach makes

of absorption or scattering a well-defined quantity. Indeed, the total probability of absorption can be evaluated by integrating the evolved state probability density over all possible final momenta of the CM
{\bf P}_{f}
,

P_{\text{abs}}=\int\frac{{\displaystyle\differential^{3}P_{f}}}{{\displaystyle(2\pi)^{3}}}|\langle{\bf P}_{f}|\psi^{\text{(CM, ev)}}\rangle|^{2}

(22)

For the scattering probability, an additional integration over the final momenta of the photon and summation over its polarizations is needed,

P_{\text{scatt}}=\sum_{\lambda_{f}}\int\frac{{\displaystyle\differential^{3}P_{f}}}{{\displaystyle(2\pi)^{3}}}\frac{{\displaystyle\differential^{3}k_{f}}}{{\displaystyle(2\pi)^{3}}}\Big|S_{fi}^{(2)}+S_{fi}^{(1.5)}\Big|^{2}.

(23)

We compute both probabilities (
22
) and (
23
) numerically, employing the Monte-Carlo method to evaluate the six-dimensional integral in Eq. (
23
).

Although the probability for collision of wave packets is a well defined quantity, it cannot be directly compared to a standard plane-wave model since the most common observable is a cross section. A general expression for a cross-section for an arbitrary scattering process with wave packets was derived in Ref.
Karlovets 2017
;
Karlovets and Serbo 2020
extending the earlier work
Kotkin et al. 1992
. It was used for some practical calculations, for instance, in

III.1
General features of cross sections

In Fig.

1s\to 2p
resonance (around
10.2\,\text{eV}
). These transitions connect the ground state
1s
to the first excited states with the principal quantum number
n=2
, which include the
2s
and
2p
levels. We neglect the fine-structure splitting within the
n=2
manifold, treating these levels as degenerate for simplicity. Throughout this section, we restrict ourselves to collisions with a vanishing impact parameter
b\to 0
. Fig.
2
also displays the total probabilities on the second vertical axis. This representation is valid because the luminosity depends only weakly on the average photon energy,
\left\langle\omega\right\rangle
.

First, although the natural linewidth is not taken into account in the absorption amplitude, the shape of the delta-like “absorption line” is regularized due to the photon’s lack of monochromaticity. For the chosen parameters, such
instrumental broadening is much more significant
than the radiative one: a femtosecond photon has a typical longitudinal coherence of
\sigma_{z}^{\gamma}\sim 10\;\mu m
corresponding to the spectral bandwidth of the order of
0.02
eV, whereas the natural linewidth of the hydrogen
2p
state is approximately
4\cdot 10^{-7}
eV.

Second, we observe the
violation of the conventional OAM selection rules
: transitions from the
1s
state to
all
the states with
n=2
are in principle possible, whereas in the plane-wave regime for helicity
\lambda
only the transition to
2p
state with
m_{f}=\lambda
would be allowed. Due to the OAM conservation, accounting for the CM is essential for all amplitudes to be nonvanishing. Transitions to
m\neq\lambda
levels, however, become possible primarily because of the momentum spread in the absorbed photon packet: each plane-wave component of the photon effectively couples to the “rotated” electronic wave function (
161
), which contains a contribution from the
m_{f}=\lambda
state. In contrast, the single-photon
1s-2s
transition is only possible thanks to the non-dipole corrections and the interaction with the CM, as the transition currents
j_{\lambda_{i}}^{(1,3)}
vanish in this case (see Table
1
). However, there is an obvious hierarchy of the probabilities. For instance, for a gaussian photon, each successive transition in the hierarchy is suppressed by some 10 orders of magnitude, and the dipole transition with
m_{f}=\lambda=\pm 1
remains the dominant one even for the absorbed photon carrying nonzero OAM.

Importantly, the scattering cross section coincides with the absorption cross section of the most probable transition in a small vicinity of the resonance, whereas outside this region they have different asymptotics - gaussian and polynomial decay for absorption and scattering, respectively. The qualitative explanation of this behavior is the following. Absorption occurs only to those plane waves that are strictly at the resonance, and since the photonic packet (and hence its spectrum) has gaussian tails, the probability decays accordingly. Scattering, in contrast, occurs at all frequencies: if the whole photonic packet is outside the resonance, then the scattering probability has the well-known Lorentz-like asymptotics
P_{\text{scatt}}\propto 1/(\left\langle\omega\right\rangle-\omega_{0})^{2}
, where
\omega_{0}
is the resonance frequency. However, if some of the plane waves turn out to be at the resonance, the scattering amplitude increases by many orders of magnitude, though stays finite because of the wave-packet averaging.

Finally, similarity of the scattering and absorption probabilities at the resonance indicates that the scattering can be interpreted as two sequential processes: photon absorption and spontaneous emission, where the latter occurs with the total probability of
1
as we consider the infinite timespan. This interpretation is justified if the collision time greatly exceeds the lifetime of the excited electronic state
Sakurai 1967
.

Focusing now on the motion of the atomic CM, we conclude that twisted photons are capable of transferring their quantized OAM to the CM,

Although the cross-section is a natural observable, it is the transition probability that measures the effectiveness of OAM transfer in terms of the number of “twisted” atoms produced per unit time. For a fixed photon flux, the rate of OAM transfer will then decrease exponentially, as shown in Fig.

Another factor that limits the rate of OAM transfer is the finite lifetime of the excited electronic state. The main decay channel is the electronic transition rather than the CM transition, since the coupling of the CM to the electromagnetic field is much weaker and
completely vanishes
in the dipole approximation for an infinitely heavy nucleus with

Recent studies have shown that any collision between a vortex particle and a non-vortex one gives rise to an intricate kinematical effect known as the superkick

;
Li et al. 2024
;

Although the momentum conservation law in this case seems to be violated, it holds for every plane-wave component comprising the colliding packets, so no paradox appears. As we address the same problem as analyzed in the original work by Barnett and Berry in Ref.
Barnett and Berry 2013
— namely, the absorption of a photon by a localized atomic packet — our model is expected to predict the same superkick effect. Importantly, our approach is fully quantum, whereas the optical vortex field was treated classically in Ref.
Barnett and Berry 2013

\sim 1/\sigma_{\perp}^{\gamma}\approx 0.2

\left\langle\ell^{\text{CM}}\right\rangle
and its standard deviation
\Delta\ell^{\text{CM}}
(see the caption). These values are perfectly consistent with the OAM distribution derived in Sec.
II.2
.

III.5
Selfkick effect

Since the superkick is a purely kinematical effect, one can anticipate
a dual phenomenon
in the opposite configuration — namely, in a collision between an initially twisted atom and a non-vortex (gaussian) photon packet. In our simulations, we indeed observe a comparable redistribution of momenta in the CM packet, shown in Fig.
10
. We refer to this as
“the selfkick” effect
. The relevant parameter regime, however, differs from that of the conventional superkick. The selfkick becomes significant only for impact parameters comparable to the width of the photon packet - that is, at least hundreds of nanometers - and it becomes more pronounced as the CM packet expands. Qualitatively, this can be understood as follows: only a portion of the extended vortex packet, where the azimuthal momentum density is large, effectively interacts with the tightly focused field. As a result, the transferred momentum is redistributed in an asymmetric manner across the packet. This situation contrasts with the standard superkick scenario, where the atom is assumed to be much smaller than the photon transverse coherence in order to probe approximately the same local momentum density of the photon coherently.

As highlighted in Refs.
Li et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025
, the superkick effect is universal to any collision process involving vortex particles and can therefore serve as a method for the unambiguous detection of a phase vortex. The same general kinematical mechanism underlies the selfkick effect. These two techniques can be particularly useful in experimental setups where conventional diagnostic tools — such as diffraction and interference — fail to work due to a short de Broglie wavelength or small coherence lengths of the colliding packets.

This also applies to photon–atom and photon-ion collisions: the selfkick effect can be used to probe vorticity in atomic and ionic wave packets. In this case, the atomic packet must expand to a transverse size of at
least a few micrometers
so that its internal transverse momentum distribution can be resolved. This requires long free-expansion times and correspondingly low densities to suppress collisional decoherence; for ions, space-charge effects must be negligible over this scale. In addition, the photon beam must be tightly focused, with a transverse coherence length
\lesssim 1\mu
m. These simultaneous requirements make the implementation challenging but, in principle, achievable in setups employing nonparaxial photon beams in combination with sufficiently coherent trapped ions or atomic/ionic beams.

IV
Discussion and conclusion

We have theoretically investigated the interaction of nonrelativistic, spatially localized atomic packets with twisted photons in free space. Our framework treats both atom and photon as localized packets, goes beyond the dipole approximation, and fully incorporates atomic center-of-mass (CM) motion, enabling a complete quantum-mechanical description of absorption and scattering with recoil and non–plane-wave effects. We predict line broadening due to finite photon coherence length, nonparaxial asymmetries, the superkick effect
Barnett and Berry 2013
, as well as it dual phenomenon - the selfkick, and splitting of resonances caused by the photon’s longitudinal structure.

The central result is the transfer of orbital angular momentum (OAM) from a twisted photon to the atomic CM during absorption, enabled by the violation of standard selection rules. For dominant dipole transitions in head-on collisions, the most probable transferred OAM matches that of the photon. The resonant cross section can be extremely large (up to
10^{9}
barn for hydrogen), suggesting a new route to generating twisted atoms complementary to diffraction
Luski et al. 2021
. A promising experimental realization is a collinear atom–laser configuration
Anton et al. 1978
;
König et al. 2021
;
Baturin and Volotka 2024
, for instance using beams produced by an Even–Lavie valve
Even et al. 2000
;
Luski et al. 2021
.

Although our analysis focuses on free atoms, it readily extends to trapped ions, used in spectroscopy
Schmiegelow et al. 2016
;
Stopp et al. 2022
. While recoil is continuous for free ions and discrete in traps, the trapping energies (
10^{-10}\ldots 10^{-8},\text{eV}
) are negligible compared with optical scales, so trapped ions effectively behave as free particles. This corresponds to replacing the continuous CM spectrum
\varepsilon^{\text{(CM)}}(\mathbf{P})
with discrete levels and the coherence length with the trap length scale. The basis used here is closely related to cylindrical harmonic oscillator eigenfunctions
Mukherjee et al. 2018
;
Mondal et al. 2014
;
Muthukrishnan and
Stroud Jr 2002
;
Peshkov et al. 2023
. More complex schemes can involve releasing trapped ions and illuminating them afterward
Fuhrmanek
et al. 2010a
;
Fuhrmanek
et al. 2010b
.

Twisted atoms are of growing interest because their intrinsic OAM introduces new degrees of freedom in light–matter and matter–matter interactions, modifying selection rules and coupling internal and motional angular momentum. In scattering, their chirality can reveal observables inaccessible to plane-wave probes, analogous to twisted electrons and neutrons
Bliokh et al. 2017
;
Afanasev et al. 2019
;
Afanasev
et al. 2021b
;
Madan et al. 2020
. The generation of vortex ions for accelerator experiments is anticipated at the Institute of Modern Physics
An et al. 2025
. In atom interferometry, CM OAM could enhance rotation sensitivity via
\ell
-dependent Sagnac-type phase shifts. While neutron interferometers have demonstrated related effects
Geerits et al. 2025
, atomic systems offer narrower momentum spreads and greater control
Barrett et al. 2014
;
Amico et al. 2021
.

Finally, vortex atoms can enable high-dimensional quantum information encoding. The quantized CM OAM provides an unbounded discrete degree of freedom suitable for qudits, extending concepts from photonic OAM encoding
Brandt et al. 2020
;
Kim et al. 2024
and their storage in cold atoms
Nicolas et al. 2014
;
Ye et al. 2022
. Motional states are already used as qubits in trapped ions
Bruzewicz et al. 2019
, and extending this to OAM offers an angular-momentum basis that can be entangled with internal states
Muthukrishnan and
Stroud Jr 2002
. Although not yet realized experimentally, these parallels suggest that vortex atoms could serve as flexible high-dimensional quantum resources.

(36)

and then we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle{\bf r}^{\prime},{\bf R}^{\prime}|\hat{H}^{\text{(int)}}(\hat{{\bf r}},\hat{{\bf R}},t^{\prime})|{\bf r},{\bf R}\rangle=\hat{H}^{\text{(int)}}({\bf r},{\bf R},t^{\prime})\,\delta({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})\delta({\bf R}-{\bf R}^{\prime}),

so we return to the Hamiltonian with the non-operator coordinates. The first-order matrix element now becomes

\displaystyle\displaystyle S_{fi}^{(1)}=\langle\Psi_{f}(t_{f})|\hat{S}^{(1)}|\Psi_{i}(t_{i})\rangle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-i\int dt^{\prime}d^{3}rd^{3}R\,\langle\psi_{f}^{(\text{at})}(t_{f})|{\bf r},{\bf R}\rangle\langle\gamma_{f}(t_{f})|\hat{H}^{\text{(int)}}({\bf r},{\bf R},t^{\prime})|\gamma_{i}(t_{i})\rangle\langle{\bf r},{\bf R}|\psi_{i}^{(\text{at})}(t_{i})\rangle.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\times\langle\gamma_{f}(t_{f})|e^{i(t^{\prime}-t_{f})\hat{H}_{f}}\hat{H}^{\text{(int)}}({\bf r},{\bf R},0)\,e^{-i(t^{\prime}-t_{i})\hat{H}_{f}}|\gamma_{i}(t_{i})\rangle\,e^{-i(t^{\prime}-t_{i})\hat{H}^{(at)}}\langle{\bf r},{\bf R}|\psi_{i}^{(\text{at})}(t_{i})\rangle,

Here

a photon absorption occurs and so the final photon state is vacuum,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle\gamma_{f}(t_{f})|e^{i(t^{\prime}-t_{f})\hat{H}_{f}}\hat{H}^{\text{(int)}}({\bf r},{\bf R},0)\,e^{-i(t^{\prime}-t_{i})\hat{H}_{f}}|\gamma_{i}(t_{i})\rangle=

Clearly

Referring again to absorption, when both the atom and the absorbed photon are described as wave packets we arrive at

Note that the energy conservation law includes the energies of the electron, of the CM, and of the photon packet, whereas the momentum conservation law only includes momenta of the CM and of the photon. This makes photon absorption without the change of the electronic state kinematically forbidden.

Let us now consider the second-order terms in

, which produce scattering. We will only discuss transitions between the plane-wave states of the CM and the photon because the generalization to wave packets is done similarly to the above procedure. The electronic state can, in principle, also be changed by scattering: we call such a process

A general second-order S-matrix term reads

\hat{S}^{(2)}=\left(-i\right)^{2}\frac{{\displaystyle 1}}{{\displaystyle 2}}\hat{T}\left[\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\hat{H}^{\text{(int)}}(t^{\prime})\differential t^{\prime}\right]^{2}=\left(-i\right)^{2}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\differential t^{\prime}\hat{H}^{\text{(int)}}(t^{\prime})\int_{t_{i}}^{t^{\prime}}\differential t^{\prime\prime}\hat{H}^{\text{(int)}}(t^{\prime\prime}).

Consider a transition from some initial state
\ket{i}
to
\ket{f}
through a set of intermediate states
\ket{y}
of the whole system (electron, CM, and field), which are eigenstates of
\hat{H}^{0}

and using Eq. (
41

(70)

Appendix B
Free packet of a massive particle

Consider a non-relativistic freely propagating wave packet of a charged particle with a mass
\mu
described with the Schrödinger equation – be it an electron, a proton, or an ion. We start with a one-dimensional (1D) case, for which there is an exact non-stationary solution, a generalization of the gaussian packet, called
a standard Hermite-gaussian (HG) packet
with an integer quantum number
k=0,1,2,...
,
Karlovets 2021
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\psi_{||}^{(k)}(z,t)=\frac{i^{k}}{\sqrt{2^{k}k!\sqrt{\pi}\sigma_{z}(t)}}\,H_{k}\left(\frac{z-\langle u\rangle t}{\sigma_{z}(t)}\right)\exp\{-it\frac{\langle p\rangle^{2}}{2\mu}+i\langle p\rangle z-

(79)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-i\left(k+\frac{{\displaystyle 1}}{{\displaystyle 2}}\right)\arctan\frac{t}{t_{d,z}}-\frac{(z-\langle u\rangle t)^{2}}{2\sigma_{z}^{2}(t)}\left(1-i\frac{t}{t_{d,z}}\right)\Big\},

(80)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dz\,|\psi_{||}^{(k)}(z,t)|^{2}=1,

(81)

where the term with
\arctan t/t_{d,z}
is called the Gouy phase,

\displaystyle\langle u\rangle=\frac{{\displaystyle\langle p\rangle}}{{\displaystyle\mu}}=\frac{1}{\mu}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dz\,\left(\psi_{||}^{(k)}(z,t)\right)^{*}\hat{p}\,\psi_{||}^{(k)}(z,t)

(82)

is the mean velocity,

t_{d,z}=\mu\,\sigma_{z}^{2}(0)\equiv\frac{\sigma_{z}^{2}(0)}{c\lambda_{c}}

is the diffraction time,
\lambda_{c}=1/\mu\equiv/\mu c
is the Compton wavelength. Here also

\sigma_{z}(t)=\sigma_{z}(0)\sqrt{1+t^{2}/t_{d,z}^{2}},

and one employs the orthogonality relation,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\,H_{n}(x)H_{m}(x)e^{-x^{2}}=2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi}\,\delta_{nm}.

(83)

This HG packet can be obtained starting from the momentum representation, in which

\displaystyle\displaystyle\psi_{||}^{(k)}(p,t)=\sqrt{\frac{{\displaystyle 2\pi\sigma_{z}}}{{\displaystyle 2^{k}k!\sqrt{\pi}}}}\,H_{k}\left(\sigma_{z}\left(p-\langle p\rangle\right)\right)\exp\{-it\frac{p^{2}}{2\mu}-\frac{{\displaystyle\sigma_{z}^{2}}}{{\displaystyle 2}}\left(p-\langle p\rangle\right)^{2}\Big\},

(84)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{{\displaystyle dp}}{{\displaystyle 2\pi}}\,|\psi_{||}^{(k)}(p,t)|^{2}=1.

(85)

One can estimate the diffraction time

\displaystyle t_{d,z}=\left(\frac{\sigma_{z}}{c}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{t_{c}},\ t_{c}=\lambda_{c}/c,

(86)

where
t_{c}\approx 1.29\times 10^{-21}
s for electron and
t_{c}\approx 0.7\times 10^{-24}
s for proton. The packets of atoms and ions in traps can nowadays be localized with the precision of
\sigma_{z}\sim 10
nm
Schmiegelow et al. 2016
, which yields

\displaystyle t_{d,z}^{(p)}\approx 1.6\,\text{ns}

(87)

for proton and some
16
ps for
\sigma_{z}\sim 1
nm. As the diffraction time grows with the particle mass,
t_{d,z}^{(p)}\propto\mu
, the former is even larger for heavy atoms.

Let us assume that the packet moves in a straight line in 3-dimensional (3D) space,

\langle z\rangle=\langle u\rangle t,

(88)

and that the longitudinal dynamics is decoupled from the transverse one. In the transverse plane, the packet can be described as
a standard Laguerre-gaussian beam
with a quantized angular momentum projection on the propagation direction
\ell=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,...
and the principal quantum number
n=0,1,2,...
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\psi_{\perp}^{(n,\ell)}({\bm{\rho}},t)=\sqrt{\frac{n!}{(n+|\ell|)!}}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\rho^{|\ell|}}{\sigma_{\perp}^{|\ell|+1}(t)}\,L_{n}^{|\ell|}\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{\sigma_{\perp}^{2}(t)}\right)\exp\{i\ell\phi-i(2n+|\ell|+1)\arctan\frac{{\displaystyle t}}{{\displaystyle t_{d,\perp}}}-

(89)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2\sigma_{\perp}^{2}(t)}\,\left(1-i\frac{{\displaystyle t}}{{\displaystyle t_{d,\perp}}}\right)\Big\},\ \int d^{2}{\bm{\rho}}\,|\psi_{\perp}^{(n,\ell)}({\bm{\rho}},t)|^{2}=1,

(90)

where
t_{d,\perp}=\mu\,\sigma_{\perp}^{2}(0)\equiv\sigma_{\perp}^{2}(0)/(c\lambda_{c}),\sigma_{\perp}(t)=\sigma_{\perp}(0)\sqrt{1+t^{2}/t_{d,\perp}^{2}}
. The wave function of the packet localized in 3D space looks as

\displaystyle\psi^{(k,n,\ell)}({\bf r},t)=\psi_{||}^{(k)}(z,t)\psi_{\perp}^{(n,\ell)}({\bm{\rho}},t),

(91)

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x\,|\psi^{(k,n,\ell)}({\bf r},t)|^{2}=1.

(92)

The set of these solutions, which we will call
an HG
\times
LG packet
, is parameterized with three quantum numbers
k,n,\ell
;
it is complete and orthogonal, and
\psi^{(k,n,\ell)}({\bf r},t)
exactly satisfies the free non-stationary Schrödinger equation.

The integers at the Gouy phases,

\displaystyle k+1/2\ \text{in 1D},

(93)

\displaystyle 2n+|\ell|+1\ \text{in 2D},

(94)

are called
the quality factors
of the packet and they are measures of its “quantumness” because we get a gaussian state for
n=\ell=k=0
. Non-classicality of the state could also be expressed through the concept of
quantum emittance
, defined as

\epsilon_{z}\equiv\sqrt{\langle z^{2}\rangle\langle{u}^{2}\rangle-\langle z{u}\rangle\langle{u}z\rangle},

(95)

\epsilon_{i}=\sqrt{\langle x_{i}^{2}\rangle\langle\hat{u}_{i}^{2}\rangle-\langle x_{i}\hat{u}_{i}\rangle\langle\hat{u}_{i}x_{i}\rangle}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\langle\bm{\rho}^{2}\rangle\langle\hat{\bm{u}}_{\perp}^{2}\rangle-\langle\bm{\rho}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}_{\perp}\rangle\langle\hat{\bm{u}}_{\perp}\cdot\bm{\rho}\rangle}\equiv\frac{\epsilon_{\bot}}{2},

(96)

where
i
enumerates the two transverse axes. For the above example we find

\displaystyle\epsilon_{z}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{c}\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right),

(97)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\bot}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{c}\sqrt{(2n+|l|+1)^{2}-1}.

(98)

Thus, the minimal transverse emittance is vanishing,
\epsilon_{\bot}(n=l=0)=0
, whereas the longitudinal one is not,
\epsilon_{z}(k=0)=\lambda_{c}/2
. The quality factors can be expressed via these emittances as

\displaystyle k+1/2=\epsilon_{z}/\lambda_{c},

(99)

\displaystyle 2n+|l|+1=\sqrt{\left(\epsilon_{\bot}/\lambda_{c}\right)^{2}+1}.

(100)

It is instructive to calculate the wave function in momentum representation,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\psi_{\perp}^{(n,\ell)}({\bf p}_{\perp},t)=\int d{\bm{\rho}}\,\psi_{\perp}^{(n,\ell)}({\bm{\rho}},t)\,e^{-i{\bf p}_{\perp}\cdot{\bm{\rho}}};

(101)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\psi_{\perp}^{(n,\ell)}({\bf p}_{\perp},0)=(-1)^{n}i^{-\ell}2\sqrt{\frac{\pi n!}{(n+|\ell|)!}}\sigma_{\perp}^{\ell+1}p_{\perp}^{\ell}\exp\left\{-\frac{p_{\perp}^{2}\sigma_{\perp}^{2}}{2}\right\}L_{n}^{\ell}\left({p_{\perp}^{2}\sigma_{\perp}^{2}}\right)\exp\left\{i\ell\phi_{p}\right\},

(102)

\displaystyle\int\frac{{\displaystyle d{\bf p}_{\perp}}}{{\displaystyle(2\pi)^{2}}}\,\left|\psi_{\perp}^{(n,\ell)}({\bf p}_{\perp},t)\right|^{2}=1.

(103)

For the azimuthal integral, let us substitute
{\bf p}_{\perp}\cdot{\bm{\rho}}=p_{\perp}\rho\cos(\phi-\phi_p)
,
d{\bm{\rho}}=\rho d\rho d\phi
. Calculating the azimuthal integral is straightforward with the use of Jacobi–Anger expansion

\int d\phi e^{i\ell\phi-ip_{\perp}\rho\cos(\phi-\phi_p)}=(-i)^{\ell}2\pi J_{\ell}(p_{\perp}\rho)e^{i\ell\phi_{p}}.

(104)

Appendix C
A wave packet of a free hydrogen atom

Consider now the time-dependent Schrödinger equation for a free Hydrogen atom:

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\Psi({\bf r}_{e},{\bf r}_{n},t)=\left(\frac{\hat{{\bf p}}^{2}_{e}}{2m}+\frac{\hat{{\bf p}}^{2}_{n}}{2M}+U(r)\right)\Psi({\bf r}_{e},{\bf r}_{n},t)

(105)

where
r=|{\bf r}_{e}-{\bf r}_{n}|
,
U(r)=-e^{2}/r
. Going to the following variables

\displaystyle{\bf r}={\bf r}_{e}-{\bf r}_{n},\ {\bf R}=\frac{m{\bf r}_{e}+M{\bf r}_{n}}{m+M},\ \mu=\frac{mM}{m+M},

(106)

we arrive at

\displaystyle\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\Psi({\bf r},{\bf R},t)=\underbrace{\left(\frac{\hat{{\bf p}}^{2}_{r}}{2\mu}+\frac{\hat{{\bf p}}^{2}_{R}}{2(m+M)}+U(r)\right)}_{\hat{H}^{(at)}}\Psi({\bf r},{\bf R},t).

(107)

Now we split the wave function as

\displaystyle\Psi({\bf r},{\bf R},t)=\psi({\bf r},t)\phi({\bf R},t),

(108)

both new functions being time dependent. For the electron component we come to the
stationary
Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle\psi({\bf r},t)=\psi({\bf r})\,\exp\left\{-i\varepsilon_{e}t\right\},

(109)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\varepsilon_{e}\,\psi({\bf r})=\left(\frac{\hat{{\bf p}}^{2}_{r}}{2\mu}+U(r)\right)\psi({\bf r}),

(110)

so the electronic degrees of freedom and the center-of-mass motion decouple.

For the center-of-mass wave function, we find the
non-stationary
free Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\phi({\bf R},t)=\frac{\hat{{\bf p}}^{2}_{R}}{2(m+M)}\,\phi({\bf R},t).

(111)

Along with a plane wave,
its exact solution
is a product (
91
) of the LG packet in the transverse plane (
90
) and the HG one (
81
) in the longitudinal plane with
\mu\to m+M
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\phi({\bf R},t)\equiv\phi_{k,n,\ell}({\bf R},t)=\phi_{||}^{(k)}(z,t)\phi_{\perp}^{(n,\ell)}({\bf R},t).

(112)

The packet of the center-of-mass moves on average in a straight line,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle z\rangle=\frac{\langle p\rangle}{m+M}\,t\equiv\langle u\rangle_{cm}t,

(113)

and it spreads in the transverse plane with time according to

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(t)=\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(0)+\frac{\partial\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(0)}{\partial t}\,t+\langle{\bm{u}}_{\perp}^{2}\rangle\,t^{2},

(114)

which follows from the Heisenberg equations for the
\rho^{2}
operator
Karlovets 2021
. If the focus of the packet is in the initial moment of time, i.e.
\partial\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(0)/\partial t=0
, then

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(t)=\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(0)+\langle{\bm{u}}_{\perp}^{2}\rangle\,t^{2}=\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(0)\left(1+\frac{t^{2}}{t_{d,\perp}^{2}}\right)=\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(0)\left(1+\frac{\langle z\rangle^{2}}{z_{R}^{2}}\right),

(115)

where
t_{d,\perp}=\sqrt{\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(0)/\langle{\bm{u}}_{\perp}^{2}\rangle}
is the diffraction time and
z_{R}=\langle u\rangle_{cm}t_{d,\perp}
is
the Reyleigh length
. When spreading is essential,
\langle z\rangle\gg z_{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rho(\langle z\rangle)\equiv\sqrt{\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(\langle z\rangle)}\approx\sqrt{\langle{\bm{u}}_{\perp}^{2}\rangle}\,t,

(116)

Within the model of the LG packet (
91
), one can calculate the averages and arrive at
1
1

: instead of

there must be
2n+|\ell|+1

Karlovets 2021

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sqrt{\langle{\bm{u}}_{\perp}^{2}\rangle}=\frac{1}{(m+M)\,\sigma_{\perp}(0)}\,\sqrt{2n+|\ell|+1},

(117)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sqrt{\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(0)}=\sigma_{\perp}(0)\,\sqrt{2n+|\ell|+1},

(118)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\text{and so}\quad t_{d,\perp}=\frac{{\displaystyle\sqrt{\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(0)}}}{{\displaystyle\sqrt{\langle{\bm{u}}_{\perp}^{2}\rangle}}}=(m+M)\sigma_{\perp}^{2}(0),

(119)

in accord with Eq. (

). If we introduce the rms width of the atomic packet in the initial moment of time,
\rho(0)\equiv\sqrt{\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(0)}
, and the de Broglie wavelength
\lambda_{\text{dB}}=2\pi/\langle p\rangle
, then

For the ground state with
n=\ell=0

Appendix D
Photonic wave packet

For the sake of consistency, we describe the photonic packets in the same HG
\times
LG basis that is used for atomic wave functions. In fact, one can in principle consider an
arbitrary
photonic wave function in momentum space
\psi^{\gamma}_{i}({\bf k}_{i},\lambda_{i})
, including HG
\times
LG wave function (Eqs. (
84
) and (
101
)), since it is basically a coefficient in the plane-wave expansion of the photonic state
Mandel and Wolf 1995
:

|\gamma(t)\rangle=\sum\limits_{\lambda_{i}=\pm 1}\int\frac{{\displaystyle d^{3}k}}{{\displaystyle(2\pi)^{3}}}\,|{\bf k},\lambda_{i}\rangle\psi^{\gamma}_{i}({\bf k},\lambda_{i})e^{-it\omega({\bf k})}.

(121)

Thus, we can write the photonic HG
\times
LG packet as

\psi^{\gamma}({\bf k},\lambda_{i})=\sqrt{\frac{{\displaystyle 2\pi\sigma_{z}^{\gamma}}}{{\displaystyle 2^{k_{\gamma}}k_{\gamma}!\sqrt{\pi}}}}\,H_{k_{\gamma}}\left(\sigma^{\gamma}_{z}\left(k_{z}-\langle k_{z}\rangle\right)\right)\exp\{-itk_{z}-\frac{{\displaystyle(\sigma_{z}^{\gamma})^{2}}}{{\displaystyle 2}}\left(k_{z}-\langle k_{z}\rangle\right)^{2}\Big\}\\
\times(-1)^{n_{\gamma}}i^{-\ell}2\sqrt{\frac{\pi{n_{\gamma}}!}{({n_{\gamma}}+|\ell_{\gamma}|)!}}({\sigma_{\perp}^{\gamma}})^{\ell_{\gamma}+1}k_{\perp}^{\ell_{\gamma}}\exp\left\{-\frac{p_{\perp}^{2}{\sigma_{\perp}^{\gamma}}^{2}}{2}\right\}L_{n_{\gamma}}^{\ell_{\gamma}}\left({k_{\perp}^{2}({\sigma_{\perp}^{\gamma}})^{2}}\right)\exp\left\{i(\ell_{\gamma}+\lambda_{i})\phi_{k}\right\}.

(122)

There are some peculiarities, however, in the definition of the position-dependent photon wave function. Indeed, there is a strongly formulated opinion that the photon wave function in position representation is ill-defined
Berestetskii et al. 1982
, although in quantum optics the following definition of the vectorial photon wave function is accepted rather widely
Mandel and Wolf 1995
;
Fedorov et al. 2005
:

\bm{\psi}^{\gamma}({\bf r},t)\equiv\frac{{\displaystyle 1}}{{\displaystyle\sqrt{\left\langle\omega\right\rangle}}}\sum\limits_{\lambda=\pm 1}\int\frac{{\displaystyle\differential^{3}k}}{{\displaystyle(2\pi)^{3}}}\bm{e}_{\lambda_{i}}({\bf k})\sqrt{\omega({\bf k})}\psi^{\gamma}_{i}({\bf k},\lambda_{i})\>e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}-i\omega({\bf k})t},

(123)

where
\left\langle\omega\right\rangle
is the mean photon frequency of the superposition (
121
). With this definition,

\left\langle\omega\right\rangle|\bm{\psi}^{\gamma}({\bf r},t)|^{2}

represents the energy density of the field. For a quasi-monochromatic packet, the factor
\sqrt{\omega({\bf k})}
can be approximated by
\sqrt{\left\langle\omega\right\rangle}
and taken out of the integral. Additionally, in the paraxial approximation the polarization vector
e_{\lambda_{i}}({\bf k})
can be substituted by
\bm{\chi}_{\lambda_{i}}
, thus Eq. (
123
) becomes a standard quantum-mechanical definition of a wave function up to the multiplication by the constant vector:

\bm{\psi}^{\gamma}({\bf r},t)\approx\bm{\chi}_{\lambda_{i}}\sum\limits_{\lambda_{i}=\pm 1}\int\frac{{\displaystyle\differential^{3}k}}{{\displaystyle(2\pi)^{3}}}\ \psi^{\gamma}_{i}({\bf k},\lambda_{i})\>e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}-i\omega({\bf k})t}.

(124)

When the photonic packet is circularly polarized, there is no sum over helicities and it is convenient to define a
scalar
position-space wavefunction as

\bm{\psi}^{\gamma}({\bf r},t)\equiv\bm{\chi}_{\lambda_{i}}\psi^{\gamma}({\bf r},t).

Whereas for quadratic dispersion of a nonrelativistic massive particle one can obtain a closed-form expression for spatial form of HG
\times
LG packet (Eqs. (
79
) and (
89
)), for linear dispersion of the photon
\omega=|{\bf k}|
it is not so straightforward. Although some cumbersome analytical expressions for symmetric packets can be obtained via the modified Bessel functions
Karlovets 2018
, we will not follow this approach here. Instead, we will limit ourselves to the paraxial approximation within which no additional calculations are needed: we can decouple transverse and longitudinal dynamics by expanding the photonic dispersion as

\omega({\bf k})=\sqrt{k_{\perp}^{2}+k_{z}^{2}}\approx k_{z}+\frac{{\displaystyle k_{\perp}^{2}}}{{\displaystyle 2\left\langle k_{z}\right\rangle}}.

(125)

Let us interpret it as a dispersion relation of a nonrelativistic particle with an effective mass
\left\langle k_{z}\right\rangle
(and thus with a diffraction time
\sigma_{\perp}^{2}(0)\left\langle k_{z}\right\rangle/c
) in transverse direction and with an infinite mass (and an infinite diffraction time) in
z
direction, which completely prevents the packet from longitudinal spreading. The linear dispersion along
z
axis enables the longitudinal propagation with the velocity
c=1
.

Appendix G
Delta function in cylindrical coordinates

One can separate the momentum delta function into transverse and longitudinal parts as

\delta^{(3)}({\bf P}_{i}+{\bf k}-{\bf P}_{f})=\delta^{(2)}({\bf P}_{i\perp}+{\bf k}_{\perp}-{\bf P}_{f\perp})\delta(P_{iz}+k_{z}-P_{fz}).

(169)

The transverse part can be then rewritten via azimuthal angles in cylindrical coordinates

\delta^{(2)}({\bf P}_{i\perp}+{\bf k}_{i\perp}-{\bf P}_{f\perp})=\\
\frac{\Theta(P_{i\perp},k_{i\perp},P_{f\perp})}{2\Delta}\Big(\delta\left(\phi_{i}-\phi_{f}+\alpha\right)\delta\left(\phi_{k}-\phi_{f}-\gamma\right)+\delta\left(\phi_{i}-\phi_{f}-\alpha\right)\delta\left(\phi_{k}-\phi_{f}+\gamma\right)\Big)=\\
=\frac{\Theta(P_{i\perp},k_{i\perp},P_{f\perp})}{2\Delta}\Big(\delta\left(\phi_{i}-\phi_{k}-(\beta-\pi)\right)\delta\left(\phi_{k}-\phi_{f}-\gamma\right)+\delta\left(\phi_{i}-\phi_{k}+(\beta-\pi)\right)\delta\left(\phi_{k}-\phi_{f}+\gamma\right)\Big),

(170)

where
\Delta
is an area of the triangle with the legs
P_{i\perp},P_{f\perp},k_{\perp}
and the angles
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\,(\alpha+\beta+\gamma=\pi)
,

\displaystyle\Delta=\frac{1}{2}P_{f\perp}P_{i\perp}\sin\alpha=\frac{1}{2}k_{\perp}P_{i\perp}\sin\beta=\frac{1}{2}P_{f\perp}k_{\perp}\sin\gamma

(171)

\displaystyle\alpha=\arccos\left\{\frac{P_{f\perp}^{2}+P_{i\perp}^{2}-k_{\perp}^{2}}{2P_{f\perp}P_{i\perp}}\right\},\,\beta=\arccos\left\{\frac{P_{i\perp}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2}-P_{f\perp}^{2}}{2k_{\perp}P_{i\perp}}\right\},\,\gamma=\arccos\left\{\frac{P_{f\perp}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2}-P_{i\perp}^{2}}{2k_{\perp}P_{f\perp}}\right\}.

(172)

The function
\Theta(p_{\perp},k_{\perp},p^{\prime}_{\perp})
equals 1 when the legs satisfy the triangle rules,

P_{f\perp}<P_{i\perp}+k_{\perp},\;P_{i\perp}<P_{f\perp}+k_{\perp},\;k_{\perp}<P_{i\perp}+P_{f\perp},

(173)

and vanishes otherwise. More details on this expansion can be found, for instance, in Ref.
Ivanov 2011
or in Supplementary of Ref.
Karlovets et al. 2025
.

Appendix H
Interference fringes due to double-twisted kinematics

The expansion (
170
) of the transverse delta function in cylindrical coordinates can be viewed as a mathematical trick that facilitates an evident identification of OAM conservation in a
2\to 1
or
1\to 2
process. However, it also has a deeper physical interpretation that has been studied extensively in Refs.
Ivanov 2011
;
Ivanov et al. 2016a
;
Ivanov 2022b
;
Ivanov et al. 2016b
;
Ivanov et al. 2020
. Specifically, the two terms in rhs of Eq.(
170
) correspond to two distinct plane-wave kinematic configurations that produce the same final momentum
{\bf P}_{f}
and therefore interfere. This effect, inherent to collisions involving two twisted particles, can be interpreted as a momentum-space analogue of the double-slit experiment
Ivanov et al. 2016a
. Although we do not explicitly employ this parametrization of the delta function when evaluating the integrals numerically, the interference remains present. The relevant questions are which observables are sensitive to this effect and which parameters must be varied in order to reveal the interference fringes.

The resonant absorption process considered here exhibits distinctive kinematical features. For nonrelativistic atoms interacting with optical photons, the allowed range of energy detuning (and hence of the transverse photon momentum
{\bf k}_{\perp}
) is extremely narrow due to the large mass of the nucleus. In contrast, the range of initial atomic momenta
{\bf P}_{i}
contributing to the process can be substantially broader. We therefore reveal the interference fringes by varying the transverse coherence length of the CM (
\sigma_{\perp}^{(\mathrm{CM})}
), which sets the characteristic transverse momentum scale
1/\sigma_{\perp}^{(\text{CM})}\times\sqrt{2n_{i}^{\text{CM}}+|\ell_{i}^{\text{CM}}|+1}
. Previous analyses of such interference effects focused on Bessel twisted states or on their gaussian-weighted superpositions
Ivanov 2011
;
Ivanov et al. 2016a
;
Ivanov 2022b
;
Ivanov et al. 2016b
;
Ivanov et al. 2020
. For consistency with those studies, we employ Laguerre–gaussian states with relatively large radial quantum numbers,
n_{i}=10
. These states closely approximate Bessel beams in that their transverse momentum distributions exhibit a single pronounced peak, while remaining square-integrable.

Let us consider an important limiting case when the initial CM state is a plane wave propagating along

\delta^{(3)}({\bf P}_{i}+{\bf k}-{\bf P}_{f})=\delta^{(2)}({\bf k}_{\perp}-{\bf P}_{f\perp})\delta(P_{iz}+k_{z}-P_{fz});

(5)

Appendix J
Parametrization of
\differential^{3}k
integrals

The expression
(8)
in the main text for the evolved state of the CM after the photon absorption is convenient in terms of analytics: it allows one to discriminate the azimuthal phase - a hallmark of a twisted state. However, it turns out to have very limited applicability for numerical calculations because of the resonant nature of the absorption process. For the numerical integration, which is essential for calculation of probabilities, cross sections, etc., we propose an alternative parametrization of the integral
(5)
.
First, let us use the delta function to integrate over
{\bf P}_{i}
, which yields
{\bf P}_{i}={\bf P}_{f}-{\bf k}
. The remaining delta function of energy can be rewritten as

\delta\left(\varepsilon^{\text{(CM)}}_{i}({\bf P}_{i})+\varepsilon^{e}_{i}+\omega-\varepsilon^{\text{(CM)}}_{f}({\bf P}_{f})-\varepsilon^{e}_{f}\right)\\
=\frac{\delta\Big(\omega+(m+M)-|{\bf P}_{f}|\cos\Theta-\sqrt{(m+M-|{\bf P}_{f}|\cos\Theta)^{2}+2(m+M)(\varepsilon_{f}-\varepsilon_{i})}\Big)}{\Big|\frac{{\displaystyle\omega}}{{\displaystyle m+M}}-\frac{{\displaystyle|{\bf P}_{f}|\cos\Theta}}{{\displaystyle m+M}}+1\Big|},

(180)

where
\Theta
is the angle between
{\bf P}_{f}
and
{\bf k}
. The second root of the quadratic equation can be omitted because it yields a negative value of
\omega
. Thus, we get a rather simple two-dimensional integral over the photon angles
\theta_{k}
and
\phi_{k}
. Most importantly, we have explicitly found a resonance condition for an arbitrary relative angle between the plane-wave components of photonic and atomic wave packets.

For scattering processes the kinematics becomes slightly more complex due to an additional photon in the final state. The parametrization, however, can be done is a similar way:

\delta\left(\varepsilon^{\text{(CM)}}_{i}({\bf P}_{i})+\varepsilon^{e}_{i}+\omega_{i}-\varepsilon^{\text{(CM)}}_{f}({\bf P}_{f})-\varepsilon^{e}_{f}-\omega_{f}\right)\\
=\delta\Bigg(\omega_{i}+(m+M)-|{\bf P}_{f}|\cos\Theta_{2}-|{\bf k}_{f}|\cos\Theta_{1}\\
-\sqrt{(m+M-|{\bf P}_{f}|\cos\Theta_{2}-|{\bf k}_{f}|\cos\Theta_{1})^{2}+2(m+M)(\varepsilon_{f}-\varepsilon_{i}+\omega_{f})-2\omega_{f}|{\bf P}_{f}|\cos\Theta_{3}-\omega_{f}^{2}}\Bigg)\\
/\Bigg|\frac{{\displaystyle\omega_{i}}}{{\displaystyle m+M}}-\frac{{\displaystyle|{\bf P}_{f}|\cos\Theta_{2}+|{\bf k}_{f}|\cos\Theta_{1}}}{{\displaystyle m+M}}+1\Bigg|

(181)

with
\Theta_{1}
,
\Theta_{2}
and
\Theta_{3}
being the angles between vectors
{\bf k}_{i}
and
{\bf k}_{f}
,
{\bf k}_{i}
and
{\bf P}_{f}
,
{\bf k}_{f}
and
{\bf P}_{f}
respectively and momentum conservation
