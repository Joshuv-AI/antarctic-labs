Descemet stripping endothelial keratoplasty following simple limbal epithelial transplantation for chemical injury-related endothelial dysfunction: two case reports
Authors: Mariya B. Doctor, Sayan Basu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Ocular chemical injury primarily results in corneal epithelial damage and limbal stem cell deficiency (LSCD), leading to delayed epithelial healing and recurrent epithelial breakdowns. Simple limbal epithelial transplantation (SLET) is the most promising surgical modality for the treatment of unilateral LSCD, helping restore and stabilize the ocular surface. However, in some instances, chemical injury can also result in corneal endothelial damage. This under-recognized entity can mimic SLET failure, leading to delayed or inappropriate management.Case presentation:We describe two patients with chemical injury-induced LSCD who were initially managed with SLET and subsequently developed persistent corneal edema and recurrent epithelial breakdowns. Despite the stable SLET transplants, the recurrent epithelial defects and non-resolving corneal edema raised the suspicion of an underlying endothelial dysfunction. Anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) revealed increased corneal thickness, Descemet membrane irregularity and undulations; and endothelial excrescences. Both patients underwent Descemet stripping automated endothelial keratoplasty (DSAEK). At the final follow-up, both patients demonstrated significant improvement in visual acuity, with a stable epithelial surface and compact cornea.Clinical discussion:Endothelial dysfunction can clinically resemble SLET failure due to overlapping features such as epithelial breakdown, corneal edema, scarring, and vascularization. Anterior segment Optical Coherence Tomography (AS-OCT) is a valuable diagnostic tool when specular microscopy is not feasible, as it can demonstrate features of endothelial dysfunction. Sequential management with initial ocular surface stabilization using SLET followed by endothelial keratoplasty, can achieve favorable outcome in selected cases with minimal underlying corneal scarring.Conclusion:Endothelial dysfunction following chemical injury is frequently overlooked due to compromised visualization of the anterior chamber from the overlying corneal pannus, stromal scarring, and ocular surface inflammation. Early recognition of endothelial involvement is essential. A staged surgical approach, with initial ocular surface reconstruction followed by endothelial keratoplasty, can optimize visual and anatomical outcomes.

Introduction

Ocular chemical burns are an important cause of visual morbidity[1]. The extent and severity of injury depend on the nature of the offending agent, the duration of exposure, and its ability to penetrate ocular tissues[2,3]. The acute phase is characterized by corneal epithelial defects, limbal ischemia, stromal edema, and ocular surface inflammation, whereas the chronic phase results in limbal stem cell deficiency (LSCD), leading to persistent epithelial defects, conjunctivalisation, corneal neovascularisation, and stromal scarring[3–5] Simple limbal epithelial transplantation (SLET) has emerged as an effective treatment for unilateral LSCD[6–8] Failure of SLET typically manifests as persistent epithelial instability, conjunctivalisation, and recurrent epithelial breakdowns despite stable SLET transplants[8,9].

Corneal endothelial dysfunction following chemical injury is an under recognized but clinically significant entity. Chemical agents can penetrate the corneal stroma and damage the endothelial cells, resulting in impaired endothelial pump function, stromal edema, epithelial bullae, and epithelial instability[10,11]. These manifestations, particularly recurrent epithelial breakdown, stromal haze, and corneal edema, may closely mimic LSCD and SLET failure. Furthermore, visualization of the posterior cornea is often difficult due to overlying pannus, stromal scarring, corneal edema, and inflammation, increasing the risk of delayed diagnosis and unnecessary repeated ocular surface procedures.

Evaluation of endothelial status is further hindered by the limited utility of specular microscopy in scarred or edematous corneas. Although penetrating keratoplasty (PK) has traditionally been performed for eyes with full-thickness corneal involvement, outcomes following chemical injury remain suboptimal due to ocular surface instability and increased risk of graft rejection.[12]. In contrast, selective endothelial keratoplasty, such as Descemet Stripping Automated Endothelial Keratoplasty (DSAEK) and Descemet Membrane Endothelial Keratoplasty (DMEK), preserves corneal integrity while replacing only the diseased endothelium, thereby reducing the risk of rejection[13–15].

There is limited literature addressing endothelial dysfunction masquerading as SLET failure or the optimal management of such eyes. We report two cases with ocular chemical injury induced LSCD who underwent successful SLET but developed persistent corneal edema and recurrent epithelial breakdowns attributed to endothelial dysfunction. We highlight the role of anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) in the diagnosis of such cases, and discuss the surgical considerations and outcomes of sequential endothelial keratoplasty. The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria[16].

Case 1

A male in his thirties presented with an accidental firecracker-related chemical injury to the right eye (RE). Visual acuity was 20/800 in the RE and 20/20 in the left eye (LE).

Initial presentation: Slit lamp examination revealed conjunctival congestion, 180-degree limbal ischemia, a total corneal epithelial defect, diffuse corneal edema and haze, and multiple pinpoint whitish deep stromal opacities suggestive of retained particles. B-scan ultrasonography was normal (Fig. 1 A, B). Thorough ocular irrigation was performed, and superficial particles were removed. Intensive medical therapy with topical corticosteroids, lubricants, antibiotics, and antiglaucoma medications was started. Due to a persistent epithelial defect after 1-week, an amniotic membrane graft with tarsorrhaphy was performed. He subsequently developed total LSCD and underwent autologous SLET 3 months after the injury. Postoperatively, complete corneal epithelization was achieved within 1 week. Diagnostic challenge: A central persistent epithelial defect with underlying corneal edema persisted at 6 weeks post-SLET, despite stable SLET transplants and the absence of conjunctivalization, raising suspicion of an alternative pathology (Fig. 1 C and D). Specular microscopy was not feasible due to corneal surface irregularity and stromal haze. AS-OCT demonstrated increased corneal thickness, diffuse stromal edema, sub-epithelial bullae, Descemet’s thickening and undulations, and hyper-reflective excrescences along the endothelial surface, consistent with endothelial dysfunction (Fig. 2). Surgical intervention: After stabilization of the ocular surface, the patient underwent DSAEK under peribulbar anesthesia. Intraoperative considerations included the instillation of topical brimonidine, avoidance of epithelial debridement, use of dispersive viscoelastic, full air tamponade, and watertight wound closure.

Outcome: At 6 months post-DSAEK, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) improved to 20/60p, with a compact cornea and a stable epithelial surface (Fig. 1 E and F).

Case 2

A male in his twenties presented 2 months after an accidental chemical injury to the RE caused by a car battery explosion. He had previously undergone amniotic membrane transplantation (AMT) at another facility. At presentation, he had a BCVA of 20/200 in the RE and 20/20 in the LE. Examination revealed partial LSCD with peripheral pannus extending from 12 to 8 o’clock, corneal neovascularization, stromal haze, corneal edema, and a superonasal symblepharon (Fig. 3 A, B, and C). The patient underwent symblepharon release and conjunctival autografting with SLET. One month later, the ocular surface was completely epithelialized with stable SLET transplants and no recurrence of conjunctivalization. Visual acuity improved to 20/125 in the RE (Fig. 3 D and E). Diagnostic challenge: The patient developed progressive stromal edema and bullae by 10 months despite a stable ocular surface. Specular microscopy was not feasible. AS-OCT demonstrated increased corneal thickness, stromal edema, Descemet’s membrane thickening, and undulations, consistent with endothelial decompensation (Fig. 3 F, G, H, and I).

Surgical intervention: The patient underwent DSAEK under peribulbar anesthesia with similar precautions as in Case 1. Additional considerations included careful handling of the conjunctiva and previously released symblepharon. A clear corneal incision was preferred. At 3 months postoperatively, RE BCVA was 20/200 with a stable surface and compact cornea. Subsequent RE cataract surgery improved his vision to 20/50 (Fig. 3 J, K, and L).

Discussion

Chemical injury can involve both the ocular surface and deeper corneal structures. While limbal stem cell deficiency (LSCD) is the most recognized cause of chronic visual impairment, endothelial dysfunction is an under-recognized contributor to poor visual recovery. The present report highlights the diagnostic challenge posed by endothelial dysfunction following chemical injury, particularly post-SLET, mimicking SLET failure.

The corneal endothelium may be damaged depending on the severity and penetrative ability of the chemical agent[2]. Previous studies have demonstrated endothelial cell loss, retrocorneal membrane formation and tractional changes leading to Descemet’s membrane irregularity or detachment[17]. These changes impair the endothelial pump function, resulting in persistent stromal edema. Because ocular surface abnormalities predominate clinically, endothelial dysfunction may be easily overlooked.

Differentiating endothelial dysfunction from true SLET failure is critical, since both conditions present with epithelial breakdown, stromal haze, and inflammation. In our cases, the SLET transplants remained stable without progression of conjunctivalization, suggesting adequate limbal stem cell restoration. Initial epithelialization after SLET further supported successful surface reconstruction. However, persistent edema, epithelial bullae, and recurrent epithelial breakdown raised suspicion of endothelial abnormality.

AS-OCT proved invaluable in diagnosis. Specular microscopy is often not possible in eyes with stromal scarring or irregular ocular surfaces[18]. AS-OCT enabled visualization of increased corneal thickness, epithelial bullae, Descemet’s membrane thickening, duplications, or undulations, and hyper-reflective excrescences suggestive of endothelial dysfunction. In addition, visualization of iris details on red-free images may help differentiate edema from dense stromal scarring and aid in deciding between endothelial keratoplasty and PK[17,19,20].

Alternative causes of persistent corneal edema were also considered such as recurrent LSCD, persisting inflammation and raised intraocular pressure. Recurrent LSCD was unlikely because the ocular surface remained stable without progressive conjunctivalization. Ocular inflammation as the cause of corneal edema was ruled out since the inflammation had subsided following the surface reconstruction procedure, despite which the corneal edema continued to progress. Intraocular pressure was monitored throughout the follow-up and was well controlled.

Management requires a staged approach with initial ocular surface stabilization, followed by endothelial rehabilitation when required[12]. In both cases, SLET successfully restored epithelial integrity, but persistent edema necessitated DSAEK. Compared with PK, DSAEK preserves corneal architecture and may reduce rejection risk and surface-related complications in eyes with relatively clear stroma[15]. The primary goal in such complex eyes is surface stabilization, ocular comfort, and the gain of functional vision improvement.

Endothelial keratoplasty in post-chemical injury eyes presents unique challenges due to compromised visualization, cicatrization, and epithelial fragility. Surgical modifications adopted in our cases included peribulbar anesthesia anticipating prolonged surgical duration and possibility of intraoperative difficulties; and avoiding excessive use of topical anesthetic and povidone-iodine to reduce epithelial toxicity[21,22]. The corneal surface was continuously coated with dispersive (2% hydroxypropyl methylcellulose) to protect the epithelium and improve clarity. Epithelial debridement must be avoided in eyes with LSCD or prior SLET, as it can precipitate persistent epithelial defects and stromal melt. Preoperative topical brimonidine tartrate 0.2% helps reduce conjunctival congestion and intraoperative bleeding[23–25]. Trypan blue can be used to stain the graft to assess the orientation of the tissue intraoperatively[26]. All wounds were sutured watertight, and adequate air tamponade was ensured. These modifications collectively helped achieve successful graft attachment and epithelial stability[27].

This report has a few limitations, including the heterogeneity in injury severity and follow-up duration, and the inability to obtain endothelial cell counts because of poor visualization. Long-term graft survival was not evaluated. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution and not generalized.

In conclusion, endothelial dysfunction following chemical injury is an under-recognized entity that can closely mimic SLET failure. Failure to identify this condition may result in delayed or inappropriate management. Incorporation of AS-OCT into routine evaluation and a high index of suspicion can facilitate timely diagnosis. A staged approach with ocular surface reconstruction followed by endothelial keratoplasty may provide favorable anatomical and functional outcomes in carefully selected cases. Further prospective studies with larger cohorts are required to validate these observations.
