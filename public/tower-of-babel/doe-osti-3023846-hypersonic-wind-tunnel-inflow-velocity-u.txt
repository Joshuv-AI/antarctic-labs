2. VELOCITY UNCERTAINTY METHODS
This work is the first known to characterize and quantify the uncertainty of the inflow velocity in the
HWT. The scope covers the systematic/epistemic uncertainty resulting from a lack of knowledge of
the inputs to measure/calculate this velocity. However, it does not capture the random/aleatory
sources of uncertainty that may come from inherent variation in the system (though using
measurements to determine the velocity associated with a model response is thought to offset this).
Therefore, the results should be considered the uncertainty for a measured inflow velocity, not the
range in which future runs may be found. It is expected that HWT run-to-run variation may be
meaningful. Future work may include this consideration.
Because the inflow velocity is not directly measured but determined through data-reduction
equations, a method to propagate the uncertainty from measured values to the velocity is required.
The Taylor Series Method (TSM) of uncertainty propagation is one way and has advantages in being
straightforward to calculate for algebraic data reduction equations such as found in this work but
suffers from first-order approximations [4].

As was shown, the velocity uncertainty is dominated by the static-temperature uncertainty term.
Furthermore, the static-temperature uncertainty is largely affected by the total-temperature
uncertainty. This uncertainty was estimated to be 12% by a group of subject-matter experts when
reviewing the behavior in the HWT. The total temperature is both measured and calculated based
on reservoir conditions. The measurement is done by direct contact with the working gas by a
thermocouple probe in the settling chamber. At Mach 5, this temperature measurement is used
directly for total temperature. However, at Mach 8 and 14, meaningful changes have been observed
when inserting to different depths and in time due to temperature stratification downstream of the

 6

heater. Therefore, in reporting tunnel conditions, a calculated temperature is used instead (see [2]
and [3]). This calculated temperature uses mass-balance equations to estimate total temperature. The
inputs to this iterative equation are the measured stagnation and control pressure and the control
temperature, as well as the diameter of the control orifice and nozzle throat. The result is an
‘average’ total temperature. However, this calculated value can different significantly from the
measured values observed when operating the HWT, and it does not account for retained heat
within the wind tunnel as multiple runs are executed during the course of a testing day. Deviations
are generally expected to be within the 12% uncertainty estimated.

Future work may include treatment of random uncertainty, removing the isentropic assumption on
the static temperature uncertainty calculation, and efforts to reduce the uncertainty on total
temperature to drive down velocity uncertainty efficiently. In addition, a study to reduce uncertainty
in the calculated total temperature can be undertaken. This would optimize placement and design of
a measured total-temperature probe, as well as mapping the calculated total temperature to the
measured total temperature using a rake installed in the test section.
