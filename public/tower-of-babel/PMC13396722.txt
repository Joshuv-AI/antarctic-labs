Altered Spontaneous Brain Activity in Adolescents and Adults with Comitant Strabismus and Strabismic Amblyopia
Authors: Yiru Huang, Zitian Liu, Yanyan Wu, Daming Deng, Minbin Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeNeuroimaging studies have established that strabismus and amblyopia are associated with structural and functional brain alterations. However, differences in spontaneous brain activity between strabismus patients with and without amblyopia remain unclear. This study employed fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation (fALFF), a normalized version of the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation that reduces physiological noise and improves detection of spontaneous brain activity, to examine spontaneous brain activity patterns in comitant strabismus (CS) and strabismic amblyopia (SA) patients in adolescents and adults.DesignCross-sectional study.ParticipantsSeventeen patients with CS (10 males and 7 females; mean age, 24.41 ± 6.70 years), 19 patients with SA (9 males and 10 females; mean age, 24.53 ± 6.12 years), and 22 normal controls (12 males and 10 females; mean age, 22.29 ± 3.93 years) were recruited.MethodsResting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging was performed.Main Outcome MeasuresSpontaneous brain activity was assessed using fALFF. Pearson correlation analysis was employed to examine the relationships between fALFF values in altered brain regions and clinical manifestations. Additionally, receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was conducted to descriptively evaluate the discriminatory capacity of fALFF signals between SA and CS patients in this exploratory sample.ResultsCompared with normal controls, both CS and SA patients showed widespread fALFF alterations in visual, sensorimotor, and higher-order cognitive regions. Relative to CS patients, SA patients showed reduced fALFF in the bilateral frontal lobes and elevated fALFF in the left precentral gyrus. An exploratory analysis revealed that fALFF values in the left middle frontal gyrus of SA patients were negatively correlated with the best-corrected visual acuity of the amblyopic eye (r = –0.49, nominal P = 0.033). Receiver operating characteristic analysis using left middle frontal gyrus fALFF yielded an apparent area under the curve of 0.81 for discriminating SA from CS in this sample.ConclusionsPatients with CS and SA demonstrate distinct patterns of abnormal spontaneous brain activity. Exploratory associations, particularly in the frontal lobe, were observed with visual acuity loss in amblyopia and may be associated with differing neuropathological states between the 2 conditions. These findings provide neuroimaging evidence that contributes to the understanding of strabismus and amblyopia pathophysiology and may inform future research into neural markers of disease progression.Financial DisclosuresProprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.Keywords: Comitant strabismus, Strabismic amblyopia, Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging, Amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations, Spontaneous brain activity.

Strabismus is an ocular alignment disorder clinically characterized by poor fusion control and impaired stereopsis, affecting 1% to 3% of children.1,2 Without timely intervention, strabismus may cause loss of depth perception and permanent vision impairment such as strabismic amblyopia (SA).3 Comitant strabismus (CS) is the most common type, typically developing in early childhood and presenting with abnormal eye position and poor stereopsis.4 Beyond functional limitations, strabismus and amblyopia patients may also suffer from significant psychosocial problems.5 Previous neuroimaging studies using voxel-based morphometry, diffusion tensor imaging, and amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation (ALFF) have demonstrated that patients with strabismus and amblyopia exhibit structural and functional brain alterations.6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 However, most studies have examined strabismus or amblyopia in isolation, with limited research systematically comparing cerebral functional alterations between CS and SA. Although CS and SA are clinically distinguishable, their underlying neural alterations, particularly in spontaneous brain activity, remain poorly differentiated. Understanding these distinctions in older individuals (beyond the peak of visual plasticity) is crucial for elucidating the pathophysiology of long-standing strabismus and the neurocompensatory mechanisms that protect against amblyopia development in some strabismic patients.

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) is a noninvasive technique ideal for detecting intrinsic neurological alterations without complex task paradigms.14 Fractional ALFF (fALFF) is an improved version of ALFF that normalizes the low-frequency power by the total power across all frequencies, thereby reducing physiological noise and increasing sensitivity to spontaneous brain activity.15 Therefore, this study aimed to (1) compare fALFF patterns between CS, SA, and normal controls (NCs); (2) identify brain activity differences specifically associated with amblyopia in the context of strabismus; and (3) explore correlations between aberrant brain activity and clinical visual function. We hypothesize that compared to CS patients, SA patients will show reduced spontaneous activity in frontoparietal attention networks involved in spatial attention and executive control and increased activity in sensorimotor and occipital regions. Furthermore, we hypothesize that the magnitude of these alterations will be associated with the severity of visual impairment in the amblyopic eye.

Methods

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center, Sun Yat-sen University and followed the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all adult subjects. For participants aged <18 years, written informed assent was obtained from the participant alongside written informed consent from their parent/legal guardian, after full explanation of the study’s purposes, procedures, risks, and benefits.

Subjects

Patients were recruited from the Strabismus and Amblyopia Department at Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center. All subjects underwent resting-state fMRI and comprehensive ocular examinations, including cycloplegic refraction, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), intraocular pressure, slit lamp examination, examinations of the anterior segment and the fundus, alternate prism cover tests at near and distance fixations, examination of eye movements, and the stereo acuity test (Vision Assessment Corporation Co) to assess the binocular vision. Snellen visual acuity was converted to the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR).

The inclusion criteria for the patient groups were as follows: (1) diagnosis of CS or SA by experienced ophthalmologists according to the diagnostic criteria of the Esotropia and Exotropia Preferred Practice Pattern or the Amblyopia Preferred Practice Pattern of the American Academy of Ophthalmology.3,4 Comitant strabismus was defined as a constant ocular deviation with equal deviation across gaze directions and BCVA ≤0.0 logMAR in both eyes. Strabismic amblyopia was defined as monocular BCVA reduction (interocular difference ≥0.2 logMAR) secondary to constant strabismus during early childhood, with BCVA in the fellow eye ≤0.1 logMAR. Patients with high ametropia (myopia < –6.00 D or hyperopia >5.00 D spherical equivalent in either eye) and significant anisometropia (interocular spherical equivalent difference >1.5 D) were excluded from the study to minimize confounding; (2) age ≥12 years (encompassing adolescents and adults); (3) early-onset strabismus (onset age ≤6 years) and first-time diagnosis; and (4) right-handed.

The exclusion criteria for the patient groups were as follows: (1) vertical, paralytic, or restrictive strabismus; acute concomitant esotropia; nystagmus; prior prism therapy or strabismus surgery; (2) complicated with other types of amblyopia (e.g., refractive amblyopia, anisometropic amblyopia, or deprivation amblyopia); or any prior amblyopia treatment; (3) complicated with other ocular diseases, such as glaucoma, corneal diseases, or fundus disorders; (4) previous history of ocular or neuropsychiatric trauma or surgery; and (5) contraindications to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (e.g., pregnancy, metal implants, or claustrophobia).

Age-matched and gender-matched NCs were recruited from the local community via advertisements and underwent comprehensive ophthalmic examinations to confirm the absence of strabismus, amblyopia, significant refractive errors, or any other ocular disorders. The inclusion criteria for NCs were as follows: (1) age ≥12 years; (2) BCVA was ≤0.0 logMAR in both eyes, with refractive errors between –3.00 D and +3.00 D and an interocular spherical equivalent difference of ≤1.5 D; and (3) right-handed. The exclusion criteria for NCs were as follows: (1) previous history of ocular or neuropsychiatric diseases, trauma, or surgery and (2) contraindications to MRI.

After excluding subjects with excessive head motion or motion artifacts in MRI, 17 patients with CS, 19 patients with SA, and 22 NCs were ultimately included in the subsequent data analysis.

MRI Data Acquisition

The MRI data were acquired using a 3T scanner (Magnetom Prisma; Siemens) with a 20-channel head and neck coil at the Sun Yat-Sen University Instrumental Analysis and Research Center. The subjects were placed in a supine position on the scanner bed, instructed to keep their eyes closed but remain awake. Their heads were stabilized using foam padding to minimize motion. Earplugs and headphones were provided to reduce scanner noise. The scanning room lights were turned off before acquisition. Horizontal T1-weighted and T2-weighted images were routinely scanned to exclude brain lesions. Then, 3-dimensional T1-weighted images were obtained using a magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo sequence with the following parameters: repetition time = 7.5 ms, echo time = 3.7 ms, echo spacing = 7.2 ms, field of view = 256 × 256 mm2, flip angle = 8°, slice thickness = 1 mm, and number of slices = 180 (axial orientation). Resting-state fMRI data were acquired using a gradient echo-echo planar imaging sequence with the following parameters: repetition time = 3000 ms,16,17 echo time = 30 ms, echo spacing = 0.7 ms, field of view = 240 × 240 mm2, flip angle = 90°, matrix size = 64 × 64, slice thickness = 4.0 mm, and number of slices = 36. The scanning time was 13 minutes and 25 seconds in total. All MRI scans were performed by the same experienced imaging technician.

fMRI Data Preprocessing and ALFF Analysis

Preprocessing of the fMRI data for each subject was performed using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12, version 7771, http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm) and Data Processing & Analysis for Brain Imaging (version 7.0, http://www.rfmri.org/dpabi) on the MATLAB 2018b platform with the following procedures that were described in previously published articles.10,18 First, raw data in the DICOM format were converted to the NIFTI format for analysis, and the first 10 volumes were discarded to remove the initial transient signal fluctuations. Second, slice timing correction and head motion correction were performed, and subjects were excluded from further analysis if their head movement was >3 mm translation in any axis and 3° rotation in any axis during scanning. We further calculated the mean framewise displacement, which measured the micro head motion for each subject, and included it as a covariate in the group comparisons. Global signal regression and scrubbing were not performed. The data were then spatially normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute template (resampling voxel size = 3 mm × 3 mm × 3 mm) and smoothed with a 6 mm full width at half maximum Gaussian kernel and detrended to remove linear trends. Finally, linear regression was performed to remove the nuisance covariates, including head motion parameters, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid signals.

After preprocessing, the linear trend subtraction and temporal filtering were performed on the time series of each voxel to reduce the effect of low-frequency drifts and high-frequency noise via Data Processing & Analysis for Brain Imaging software. The time courses were converted to the frequency domain using a Fast Fourier Transform. The square root of the power spectrum was computed and then averaged across 0.01 to 0.08 Hz at each voxel, taken as the raw ALFF. To reduce the global effects of variability across subjects, the raw ALFF of each voxel was then divided by the average raw ALFF value of the whole brain for each subject, resulting in the fALFF used in the following analysis.15 The BrainNet Viewer toolbox (version 1.53, https://www.nitrc.org/projects/bnv) was selected to show the statistical maps of the cortex.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis of clinical data was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 25 (IBM). χ2 tests were employed to analyze the categorical data. Shapiro-Wilk tests were used to assess data normality. Normally distributed continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using 1-way analysis of variance among the NC, CS, and SA groups, while non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using Kruskal-Wallis tests. Paired-sample t tests were used for within-group comparisons between left and right eyes. Clinical characteristics between the CS and SA groups were compared using 2-sample t tests for data with normal distribution and Mann-Whitney U tests for data with non-normal distribution. The statistical significance threshold was set at P < 0.05.

One-way analysis of variance followed by Bonferroni-corrected post hoc tests was conducted to compare the differences in fALFF values among the NC, CS, and SA groups using Data Processing & Analysis for Brain Imaging software in SPM12, with age, gender, head motion, and spherical equivalent as covariates. Gaussian random field correction was applied, with statistical significance set at voxel-level P < 0.005 and cluster-level P < 0.05. The Montreal Neurological Institute coordinates, cluster size, and corresponding t-values of statistically significant brain regions were recorded. The fALFF values in each significant cluster were extracted for each subject. To aid interpretation of the observed differences, sensitivity power analysis was performed using G∗Power software. Given the sample sizes (CS = 17, SA = 19, and NC = 22), a 2-tailed independent t test with α = 0.05 and power (1-β) = 0.80 was assumed.19 Effect sizes (Cohen d) for each significant cluster identified in the group comparisons were calculated and interpreted as small (0.2), medium (0.5), or large (0.8) according to conventional thresholds.20 To assess the robustness of our findings under a more stringent threshold, we repeated the whole-brain group comparisons using a stricter voxel-level threshold of P < 0.001 (cluster-level P < 0.05, Gaussian random field corrected).

Pearson correlation analyses were performed between the fALFF values of the identified regions and clinical parameters (e.g., BCVA, stereoacuity, disease duration, and the degree of ocular deviation) in the CS and SA groups, with a statistical significance threshold of P < 0.05. Due to the exploratory nature of this analysis, no correction for multiple comparisons was applied; therefore, results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve method was used to evaluate the ability of fALFF values in specific brain regions to discriminate SA patients from CS patients, and 10-fold cross-validation was conducted to mitigate overfitting.21 Specifically, the ROC analysis was performed on the left middle frontal gyrus fALFF, which was identified as a significant cluster from the same data set (CS vs. SA comparison). The 10-fold cross-validation was applied only to the extracted feature values, not to the feature selection step. Therefore, the results are descriptive of the current sample and do not imply generalizable diagnostic performance.

To examine whether the laterality of the amblyopic eye influenced the lateralization of fALFF findings, we performed a subgroup analysis within the SA group. Patients were divided into left-amblyopic (n = 11) and right-amblyopic (n = 8) subgroups based on the eye with lower BCVA. Given the small sample sizes, fALFF values from regions that differed between SA and NC or CS groups were extracted and compared between the 2 subgroups using Mann-Whitney U tests.

No significant differences in gender distribution (χ2 = 0.49, P = 0.783) or age (F2, 55 = 1.83, P = 0.173) were found among the 3 groups. The distribution of esotropia and exotropia did not differ significantly between the CS and SA groups (CS: esotropia n = 11, exotropia n = 6; SA: esotropia n = 13, exotropia n = 6; χ2 = 0.06; P = 0.813), indicating that subtype imbalance is unlikely to be a major confounder in the group comparisons. As expected, SA patients showed significant BCVA differences between amblyopic and fellow eyes (t = 5.46, P < 0.001), whereas CS patients did not (t = –1.71, P = 0.104). Intergroup comparisons demonstrated significant differences in BCVA of nondominant/amblyopic eyes (F2, 55 = 37.98, P < 0.001) but not in dominant/nonamblyopic eyes (F2, 55 = 1.20, P = 0.312). Significant stereopsis differences were also observed among groups (Kruskal-Wallis test, P < 0.001), with post hoc pairwise comparisons showing that both CS and SA patients had significantly worse stereopsis than NCs (both P < 0.001), but no difference between CS and SA patients (P = 0.672). No significant differences were detected between the CS and SA groups regarding angle of ocular deviation (t = –0.15, P = 0.885) or duration of strabismus (t = –0.34, P = 0.740).

Sensitivity power analysis indicated that our study was powered to detect a minimum Cohen d of approximately 0.85 for 2-group comparisons. Therefore, the study is powered to detect large effects. Besides, effect sizes for key group differences (CS vs. NC, SA vs. NC, CS vs. SA) were uniformly large, with absolute Cohen d values ranging from 1.14 to 1.93 (Supplementary Table S2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org), suggesting that the main results are unlikely to be spurious despite the modest sample size. To examine whether the 2 adolescent participants in the CS group (aged 13 and 15 years) affected the SA versus CS comparisons, we repeated the analysis after excluding these 2 individuals. The results remained substantially unchanged: the left inferior frontal gyrus (t = 3.75, Cohen d = 1.30), left middle frontal gyrus (t = 3.35, Cohen d = 1.18), right middle frontal gyrus (t = 3.65, Cohen d = 1.28), and left precentral gyrus (t = –4.55, Cohen d = –1.60), all remained significant (all P < 0.05, 2-tailed). The effect sizes were comparable to those reported in the main analysis (Supplementary Table S3, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org, provides details). Thus, the inclusion of the 2 adolescents did not drive the main findings, indicating that the age imbalance did not materially affect the main conclusions.

Sensitivity analysis using a stricter threshold (voxel-level P < 0.001, cluster-level P < 0.05, Gaussian random field corrected) confirmed that most of the regions reported in the main analysis remained significant, with the exception of the right middle frontal gyrus and the left middle frontal gyrus in the CS greater than SA comparison (Supplementary Table S4, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Therefore, the frontal reductions in SA versus CS are only partially robust and should be interpreted with caution.

Subgroup Analysis Based on Amblyopic Eye Laterality

Within the SA group, no significant differences were observed between left-amblyopic (n = 11) and right-amblyopic (n = 8) patients in fALFF values of the left middle frontal gyrus (median [interquartile range]: left-amblyopic 0.52 [0.49–0.60], right-amblyopic 0.53 [0.49–0.55]; Mann-Whitney U = 42.5; P = 0.90) or in any other regions that showed group differences (all P > 0.05; Supplementary Table S5, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). These results suggest that the laterality of the amblyopic eye does not significantly affect the fALFF patterns reported in the main analysis.

Correlations with BCVA and ROC Curve Analysis in Strabismic Amblyopia Patients

In patients with SA, the fALFF values in the left middle frontal gyrus showed a negative linear correlation with the BCVA of their amblyopic eyes (r = –0.49, P = 0.033; Fig 4A). However, after applying Bonferroni correction, this correlation did not reach statistical significance (corrected α = 0.003). Accordingly, this finding is considered exploratory and should be interpreted with caution. No significant correlations were observed between fALFF values in other identified brain regions and the BCVA of amblyopic eyes (all uncorrected P > 0.05). Furthermore, no significant correlations were found between the fALFF values of those identified regions and stereopsis, disease duration, or angle of ocular deviation for either the CS or SA groups using Pearson correlation (all uncorrected P > 0.05).

Building upon our findings that SA patients exhibited significantly decreased fALFF values in the left middle frontal gyrus compared to CS patients and the exploratory association between the fALFF values in the left middle frontal gyrus and the BCVA of amblyopic eyes, we next explored whether the fALFF values of the left middle frontal gyrus could discriminate SA from CS patients in this exploratory sample. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis yielded an apparent area under the curve (AUC) of 0.81 (P = 0.001, 95% confidence interval: 0.67–0.96) for discriminating SA from CS patients (Fig 4B). The 10-fold cross-validation on the extracted feature gave a mean AUC of 0.75 (standard deviation = 0.10; Supplementary Figure S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Because the feature was identified from the same data set and the 10-fold cross-validation was applied only to the extracted feature values (not to the feature selection step), this AUC is descriptive of the current sample and does not imply generalizable diagnostic performance.

Discussion

This study identified distinct patterns of altered spontaneous brain activity in adolescents and adults with CS and SA by measuring the fALFF values of resting-state fMRI signals. Key findings include: (1) shared reductions in the fALFF values of the cerebellum, insula, and superior temporal gyrus in both CS and SA groups versus NCs, which are consistent with the hypothesis of common neural substrates associated with strabismus; (2) SA-specific reductions in frontal lobe activity compared to CS; and (3) an exploratory association between reduced frontal lobe activity and worse visual acuity in SA patients.

On the one hand, the observed hypoactivity in the cerebellum and insula aligns with their roles in eye movement control and multisensory integration, respectively, and may be associated with reported deficits in gaze stability and sensorimotor integration in strabismic patients. The cerebellum is involved in motor balance and eye movement control, including sustained eye pursuit movements, fixation, and saccades.22 The insula is a core brain area, serving as a multisensory integration center for diverse cognitive, emotional, and homeostatic processes.23,24 Min et al18 revealed that adult patients with SA exhibited reduced ALFF values in the left posterior lobe of the cerebellum compared with normal individuals, which aligns with our research findings. Previous studies have also suggested that patients with strabismus and SA exhibit unstable fixation, decreased hand-eye coordination, and visual search and reading dysfunction.25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30 Our findings of reduced cerebellar and insular activity are consistent with previous reports of motor control disorders in strabismic and amblyopic patients, suggesting a possible association between these behavioral deficits and altered spontaneous brain activity. However, the results of Li et al31 showed significantly enhanced spontaneous activity in the bilateral cerebellum of children with deprivation amblyopia. We believe that the observed differences may be attributed to the varying types of amblyopia and different age groups of the subjects.

On the other hand, our results showed hyperactivity in the precentral and occipital gyri in both CS and SA patients compared with NCs. The precentral gyrus is located in the frontal lobe and is the primary motor cortex that controls the execution of human movements.32 The occipital lobe is the visual processing hub involved in gaze, movement, and body guidance.33 One possible interpretation is that the increased spontaneous activity in these areas may relate to increased demands on visuomotor coordination and visual processing under strabismic conditions. However, given the cross-sectional design, this interpretation remains speculative and requires validation in future studies.

In our results, the SA-specific reduction in the fALFF values of the frontal lobe, particularly in the middle frontal gyrus, and its exploratory correlation with the BCVA of amblyopic eyes are consistent with the hypothesis that this region may play a role in the additional visual processing deficit characteristic of amblyopia. The frontal lobe is regarded as not only a motor area for saccades and head movements but also a critical region for spatial attention34 and has been shown to exhibit strabismus-related structural and functional abnormalities.13,35 One possible interpretation is that the reduced frontal activity in SA patients may be related to reduced top-down attentional allocation to the amblyopic eye or long-term visual suppression. This interpretation aligns with previous reports of gaze instability, visual search, and reading dysfunction in amblyopia.25,27,30 The exploratory correlation between decreased frontal activity and amblyopic eye visual acuity, although not surviving correction for multiple comparisons, is consistent with the hypothesis that frontal regions may be involved in visual attention deficits in amblyopia. However, a sensitivity analysis with a stricter threshold (P < 0.001) showed that the middle frontal gyri differences did not survive the more stringent correction, suggesting that they are less robust. The large effect sizes observed at the primary threshold (e.g., Cohen d = 1.14 for the left middle frontal gyrus) suggest potential biological significance, but the lack of robustness under stricter correction indicates that these findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Replication in larger, well-powered cohorts with more rigorous statistical thresholds is essential before any definitive conclusions can be drawn.

Limitations of the Study

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, regarding statistical power and generalizability, our study employed a cross-sectional design with a modest sample size (n = 17–22 per group). Sensitivity power analysis indicated that the study was powered to detect large effects (Cohen d ≥ 0.85), and the observed effect sizes for the main findings were indeed large (|d| = 1.14–1.93), suggesting that the key results are unlikely to be spurious. However, null findings (e.g., correlations after correction) should be interpreted with caution, and the lack of external validation for the ROC analysis (cross-validated AUC = 0.75) calls for replication in larger independent cohorts. Second, concerning analytical choices and robustness, we used a relatively liberal voxel-level threshold (P < 0.005) and did not correct for multiple comparisons in correlation analyses, which increases the risk of type I error. Sensitivity analyses using a stricter threshold (voxel-level P < 0.001) confirmed most findings, except for the middle frontal gyri in the CS greater than SA comparison. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution and warrant replication in future studies. Third, with respect to sample heterogeneity and clinical measures, our sample included few adolescents (only 2 CS patients) and no adolescents were present in the SA or NC groups. Nevertheless, a sensitivity analysis excluding these 2 adolescents yielded essentially identical results (all frontal clusters remained significant), indicating that the age imbalance did not materially affect the main conclusions. Additionally, our sample contained mixed strabismus subtypes (esotropia/exotropia) and small subgroup sizes for amblyopic eye laterality (left-amblyopic n = 11, right-amblyopic n = 8), all of which limited meaningful subgroup analyses. Moreover, we assessed only BCVA and stereopsis, lacking other visual functions (e.g., contrast sensitivity, oculomotor control). Future studies with larger, more homogeneous samples and comprehensive functional assessments are needed to validate and extend our findings. Despite these limitations, this exploratory study provides novel neuroimaging evidence differentiating the neural signatures of CS and SA.

Conclusions

In adolescents and adults with early-onset CS, the presence of amblyopia is associated with distinct alterations in spontaneous brain activity, particularly within the frontal lobe. Exploratory analyses suggest that these alterations may be associated with the severity of visual acuity loss and show potential for group-level differentiation between SA and CS, although these findings require replication in larger, independent samples. This study provides preliminary novel neuroimaging evidence differentiating the neural signatures of CS and SA, contributing to a more nuanced understanding of their pathophysiology.
