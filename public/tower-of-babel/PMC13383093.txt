Maternal lifetime stress and placental mitochondrial DNA mutational load: Effect modification by maternal characteristics
Authors: Kecia N. Carroll, Corina Lesseur, Kelly J. Brunst, Elena Colicino, Yueh-Hsiu Mathilda Chiu, Terryl J. Hartman, Brent A. Coull, Rosalind J. Wright
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Maternal psychosocial stress is associated with perturbations in biological systems, including placental alterations with implications for fetal programming. Here, we examined associations between maternal lifetime stress and placental mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutational load, while considering modifying effects of maternal characteristics.Methods:In 496 participants from the PRISM cohort, we profiled placental mtDNA mutational load with next-generation sequencing. We used negative binomial regression models to investigate associations between maternal lifetime stress/trauma using the Life Stressor Checklist-Revised (LSC-R), and placental mtDNA mutational load and, assessed effect modification by obesity and race/ethnicity in stratified models.Results:We detected 1,357 distinct mutations, most were homoplasmies and transitions. There was moderate evidence of an association between an interquartile increase in LSC-R and mtDNA mutational load, with incident rate ratio (IRR) 1.04 (95% confidence intervals (CI) 0.99, 1.09). In stratified analyses, associations were stronger in the non-obese than the obese, although estimates were imprecise in both groups. In analyses stratified by race/ethnicity, higher stress/trauma was associated with higher mtDNA mutational load only in Black women (IRR 1.07, 95%CI, 1.01-1.14). In analyses stratified by obesity and race/ethnicity, higher stress was associated with increased mtDNA mutational load in Black women in the non-obese (IRR = 1.09, 95%CI, 1.02-1.17) subgroup, but not in the obese (IRR = 1.02, 95%CI, 0.91-1.13) subgroup.Conclusions:We found associations between maternal lifetime stress/trauma and placental mtDNA mutational load with differential association by obesity and race/ethnicity. Further work exploring the role of stress-related mitochondrial pathways in maternal and birth outcomes is warranted.

Introduction

Psychosocial stress is associated with physiological changes during pregnancy with potential consequences for the mother and developing child [1]. In the field of Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD), investigations have found associations between maternal psychosocial stress and adverse pregnancy outcomes [2,3] as well as fetal programming of later-life respiratory, neuropsychiatric, and cardiometabolic disease [4-8]. Furthermore, a woman's exposure to stressors over her lifetime may influence programming of disease in her offspring, even when the stressors are remote to pregnancy [9,10]. In addition, evidence from animal and epidemiological studies have demonstrated adverse impacts of maternal obesity, which is associated with chronic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction [11,12], on fetal programming [13,14]. Although mechanisms linking maternal exposures to offspring outcomes are not fully understood, the placenta plays an important role in regulating fetal development [15]. Conditions such as psychosocial stress and obesity may disrupt normal placental function through mechanisms such as oxidative stress, which is an understudied area [16]. Furthermore, women from historically marginalized racial/ethnic groups may experience traumatic events [17,18] and obesogenic factors [19] disproportionately over their lifetime and thus may be particularly at risk for adverse consequences of both exposures.

Placental molecular marks are increasingly studied as candidate mechanisms and biomarkers of fetal programming [20-22]. As a complex and pleiotropic organ, the placenta has high energetic and metabolic demands [23,24]. Mitochondria are key organelles that survey the cellular environment to regulate energy production, cellular metabolism, the redox state, and cell fate in coordination with the nucleus [25-27]. Placental mitochondria are important to support a healthy pregnancy, and mitochondrial alterations have been described in various pregnancy complications, including maternal obesity, gestational diabetes (GDM) and preeclampsia, all of which are linked to oxidative stress [21,28-30].

Most of the cell's reactive oxygen species (ROS) are produced in mitochondria, as part of physiological cell signaling. Yet, when redox mechanisms are overwhelmed, ROS can lead to oxidative stress and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) damage [31]. mtDNA is a maternally inherited double-stranded circular molecule spanning 16,5 Kb that encodes 37 genes. mtDNA repair is less efficient than nuclear DNA repair, making it more vulnerable to mutations [26]. Cells contain only two copies of nuclear DNA but many copies of mtDNA. Homoplasmic mutations affect all the mtDNA molecules, whereas heteroplasmic mutations affect only a proportion of the molecules [26].

Very few studies in humans have investigated the link between gestational exposures (i.e., maternal stress, obesity) and mitochondrial markers. Most epidemiologic studies evaluating these links have been focused on placenta mtDNA copy number (mtDNAcn), with some investigations assessing mitochondrial gene expression [32-35]. While we previously reported an association between maternal stress/trauma, and placental mitochondrial mutational load [36], the potential impact of maternal pre-pregnancy obesity remains unexplored. Herein, we examine the interplay between maternal lifetime stress/trauma, pre-pregnancy obesity and race/ethnicity on placental mtDNA mutational load in 496 participants of the PRogramming of Intergenerational Stress Mechanisms (PRISM) study.

Methods

Study population

The PRogramming of Intergenerational Stress Mechanisms (PRISM) study is an ongoing prospective pregnancy cohort initially designed to examine the influence of prenatal stress, maternal nutrition, and other environmental exposures on child development and health. In this study, we included 496 PRISM mother-infant pairs, with placenta mtDNA sequencing data and relevant study covariates. The date of the last dyad enrolled in the current study was in January 2022, when the cohort size was 1,527. Part of the mtDNA sequencing data was initially collected as part of a study that investigated maternal stress/trauma and mtDNA mutational load [36]. Herein, we extended the sample size, to examine the associations between maternal lifetime stress/trauma, obesity status and race/ethnicity on placental mtDNA mutational load. Pregnant women during their mid-pregnancy and receiving prenatal care from the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center and East Boston Neighborhood Health Center in Boston, Massachusetts (from 2011 to 2013) and the Mount Sinai Hospital in New York City (NYC), New York (from 2013 to 2022) were recruited. Women with a singleton pregnancy were considered eligible if they were English or Spanish speaking and ≥18 years. Exclusion criteria included intake of ≥7 alcoholic drinks/week before pregnancy, any alcohol intake after pregnancy awareness, HIV-positive status, or newborn with major congenital abnormalities. Trained research staff obtained written informed consent from participants and study protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Boards at the Brigham and Women's Hospital and the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai. Clinical and sociodemographic characteristics of women and their infants were ascertained by questionnaires administered during in-person study visits (at enrollment and/or following delivery) and medical chart review.

Maternal lifetime exposure to stressful and traumatic events

To assess exposure to lifetime stressful events, research staff administered the Life Stressor Checklist-Revised (LSC-R) [37,38] to women during their 2nd trimester of pregnancy. The LSC-R has documented test–retest reliability and has been validated in diverse populations [37,39,40]. The LSC-R assessed exposure to 30 stressful experiences, including natural disasters, accidents, financial strain, violence/crime, sexual harassment or abuse, physical or mental health conditions, incarceration, emotional or physical abuse or neglect, divorce or separation, or sudden or unexpected death of close family member [37]. If women endorsed an event, they were asked to rate the impact of the event using the question “how much has this affected your life in the past year” with response options ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (extremely). We used a weighted LSC-R score summed across endorsed events accounting for exposure to and the potential impact with possible score range of 0 (no events endorsed) to 150 (all 30 events endorsed and rated impact at 5).

Covariates

We identified covariates for the analyses using a Directed Acyclic Graph [41] (DAG, Fig. S1), depicting the relations between lifetime maternal stress/trauma and placental mtDNA mutational load based on prior knowledge [22,23]. The variables of interest included maternal age at delivery (years), self-reported race/ethnicity (Black [non-Hispanic Black and Black-Hispanic], Hispanic [non-Black Hispanic], and White [non-Hispanic White]), education (high school or less, some college or above), and pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) calculated from self-reported weight (in kilograms [kg]) and height [in meters squared]) at enrollment [42]. To assess the impact of obesity, maternal BMI was analyzed as a categorical variable including non-obese (<30 kg/m2) and obese (≥30 kg/m2). Given the importance of fetal sex for placental molecular markers, including mtDNA mutational load [43], we accounted for sex. We also considered mitochondrial haplogroup lineage as a covariate, yet for this analysis, self-identified race/ethnicity may capture nongenetic factors that are more pertinent to psychosocial stress such as discrimination and structural inequities and provide different information to genetic predictors. Based on the DAG, the minimal sufficient adjustment set of variables for the relationship between lifetime stress and mtDNA load included maternal age, education, and race/ethnicity. We also included precision variables that affect the outcome but not the exposure [44], specifically infant sex and mtDNA sequencing platform. We identified pregnancy complications, gestational age, and smoking as variables on the pathway between lifetime stress and mtDNA mutational load [45-47]; thus, these variables were excluded from the statistical models to avoid overadjustment bias [48]. Parity was also considered but excluded due to its strong association with maternal age and education [49,50].

Placenta sampling

Placenta samples were collected within 2 h after delivery. Approximately, one cubic centimeter (cm3) of placental villi tissue was sampled (fetal side) following established protocols [22,51]. Samples were taken approximately 4 cm from the umbilical cord insertion site in four quadrants, below the chorionic membranes avoiding large vessels. To avoid contamination, decidua and membranes were removed, and samples were rinsed in cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Additionally, in previous work from this cohort, placental tissue was confirmed to be of fetal origin by almost complete concordance between placenta and cord blood genotyping probes in the 450K DNA methylation array [22]. Each tissue sample was then cut in smaller pieces (~0.1 cm3), and placed into RNAlater™ Stabilization Reagent (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) and stored at −4°C for ≤24 h, when excess RNAlater was removed and samples were stored at −80°C until DNA isolation. Laboratory standard operating processing protocols and sample storage are standardized across the study sites.

Mitochondrial DNA sequencing

Placenta samples were shipped on dry ice to the Genomics, Epigenomics and Sequencing Core at the University of Cincinnati. DNA was isolated with the Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega, Madison, WI). To amplify the human mtDNA genome, we followed protocol Document No. 15037958 v01 (Illumina, San Diego, CA) with modifications. We used 2 ng of DNA in two long-range PCRs reactions (50-mL) covering the entire sequence, using the TaKaRa LA Taq (Takara Bio USA, Mountain View, CA), and the following pairs of primers (MTL-F1/MTL-R1, MTL-F2/MTL-R2). PCRs volumes were pooled to make approximately equal amounts, purified with the Wizard SV Gel and PCR Clean-Up Kit (Promega), and ~100 ng was used for library preparation using NEBNext Ultra II FS DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA). Libraries were evaluated with the Agilent bioanalyzer (Santa Clara, CA) and PCR (NEBNext Library Quant Kit, New England Biolabs), indexed and pair-end sequenced, on two Illumina platforms (HiSeq 1000 and NextSeq 2000).

Bioinformatics

Initial assessment of the mtDNA sequencing reads was conducted using the FastQC software [52]. We used BWA-MEM (v0.7.15) [53] for paired-end read alignment to the revised Cambridge Reference Sequence (rCRS), GenBank NC_012920.1, which is based on European top-level haplogroup H [54]. We called variants according to the methods outlined in Ref. [55] using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK v4.3.0.0) Mutect2 [56] variant caller “mitochondrial mode”. Variants in the control region (CR) (positions 16,024–16,569 and 1–576), were aligned with BWA-MEM to a ChrM reference sequence shifted by 8000 base pairs. Coordinates of the variants on the sifted reference were then converted back to their original positions using Picard (v2.2.4) liftOver and combined with variants from the non-control region into a merged VCF file. Variants marked with ‘fail,’ ‘base-quality,’ ‘position,’ ‘blacklisted,’ and ‘strand’ flags in the VCF file's filter column were excluded, as were deletions and insertions. The variant allele fraction (VAF) was calculated as the ratio of alternate allele reads to total reads for a specific variant in a sample. Variants with a VAF between 0.05 and 0.98 were classified as heteroplasmic, while those with a VAF between 0.98 and 1.00 were considered homoplasmic. To minimize false positives, variants with alternative allele fractions below 5% were filtered out [55,57]. We defined mutational load as the count of homoplasmic and heteroplasmic variants detected in an individual. Repeated sequencing was performed on 10% of the samples and concordance in variant calls ranged between 80 and 100%. mtDNA contamination was estimated using Haplocheck (v1.3.3) [58]. Haplogroups were determined using HaploGrep2 (v2.2.9) [59]. Mitochondrial haplogroups were arranged into lineages including African (L), European (H, I, J, K, T, U, V, W, and X) and Native American/Asian (A, B, C, D, F, G, N, P, Y, Z, M, E, R) [60]. Mitochondrial variants that passed quality control filters were annotated using existing MITOMAP [61] and genomAD3.1 [55]. We computed the mtDNA mutation to base pair ratio for genes and functional regions (i.e. genes, tRNAs, rRNA, oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) Complex I genes, etc.) by dividing the number of mutations by the length in base pairs of the gene/region.

Statistical analyses

We used summary statistics including mean, standard deviation (SD), median, interquartile range (IQR), and/or frequency tables to evaluate the distribution of continuous and categorical variables. We performed nonparametric bivariate tests to evaluate the relationship between clinical/sociodemographic variables, and placental DNA mitochondrial mutational load. To assess associations between lifetime stress (continuous predictor, weighted LSC-R) and mtDNA mutational load (count outcome), while controlling for potential confounders, we fitted negative binomial models adjusting for age, education, race/ethnicity (overall sample), child sex and sequencing platform (HiSeq 1000 or NextSeq 2000). We also fitted models stratified by obesity status (non-obese vs obese) and race/ethnicity to evaluate differential effects of lifetime stress associations by maternal key factors. Due to small sample size (n = 8), we did not evaluate associations in the non-Hispanic White obese group. Additionally, in pre-planned sub-analyses in the sample including Black and Hispanic women (n = 421), we fitted negative binomial regression models with interaction terms between LSC-R score, race/ethnicity, and obesity status. We calculated model effect sizes (incidence rate ratio [IRR]) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) using an interquartile range (IQR) increase in weighted LSC-R score. Analyses were performed in R statistical computing software version 4.3.3 [62].

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Table 1 shows the characteristics of the participants, overall (n = 496) and stratified by obesity status; 167 (34%) of the women were obese. The median age at delivery was 29.3 years. 54% of the newborns were male and 46% were female. From the overall sample, 59% of mothers had at least some college education. The distribution of participants across race/ethnicity included 257 Black (52%), 162 Hispanic (33%) and 77 White (16%) women. The overall weighted LSC-R score ranged from 0 to 96 with a median of 9 and an interquartile range (IQR) of 13. Maternal BMI differed by race/ethnicity (Fisher's exact test p < 0.001), with higher proportions of obesity in Black and Hispanic women. Education also differed by obesity status; in the non-obese group there was a larger proportion of women that had higher than a high school education (p = 0.002).

Placental mitochondrial DNA variants characterization

Across 496 individuals the dataset included 5,449 single nucleotide variants (SNVs) with median read depth of 4,614X. Most SNVs were homoplasmies (92.8%, 5,059/5,449) and 7.2% were heteroplasmic. In this dataset, we observed 1,357 unique variants (i.e. different DNA changes). Of the unique variants, 78.1% were detected only as homoplasmy, 133 (9.8%) only as heteroplasmy, and 164 (12.1%) observed as both homoplasmy and heteroplasmy (Fig. 1A). The majority of variants were transitions (92.9% 1,262/1,357) (Fig. 1B). The median number of total mutations called per sample was 9; the distribution of homoplasmies was similar to the total number of mutations. In contrast, we detected few heteroplasmies per sample; 66% of the participants in the study population (327/496) had no heteroplasmies (Fig. S2). Nonsynonymous mutations were more frequent in the heteroplasmies (24.2%) compared to homoplasmies (14.9%) (Fig. 1C). In the control region (CR), heteroplasmies were slightly more common than homoplasmies (13.3% vs 11.9%)(Table S1). Amongst the 13 mtDNA coding genes, MT-ATP8 and MT-CYB were the most frequently mutated genes after adjusting for gene length for both homoplasmic and heteroplasmic SNVs (Fig. 1D-Table S2), and expectedly the noncoding DLOOP region was also prominent harboring an important number of mutations. Table S3 displays the distribution of mtDNA mutations to base pair ratio for genes grouped by OXPHOS complex across the 496 study participants, we detected more heteroplasmic mutations in complex-I genes, followed by complex-III and complex-V. Unique variants were annotated using MITOMAP, 1,319/1,357 SNVs were reported in the genomAD3.1 [55]. From the MITOMAP annotations, 139/1,319 variants had a reported disease association, including some chronic diseases (i.e. hypertension, type 2 diabetes), pregnancy-specific conditions (gestational diabetes, miscarriage) and mitochondrial diseases like Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) (Table S4). Mitochondrial haplogroups spanned 18 haplogroups; the L haplogroup was the most frequent (53.6%) followed by the A (10.3%) and the H (9.2%) haplogroups (Table S5). Most of the participants that self-identified as Black race/ethnicity (79%) had matched maternal genetic ancestry African (L) haplogroup. Similarly, a high proportion of self-identified White participants were classified in the European haplogroup lineage (83%). The haplogroup distribution of the self-identified non-Black Hispanic participants was more dispersed across the major lineages (Native American/Asian 55%, African 33% and European 12%) (Table S6).

Bivariate analysis between mtDNA mutational load and covariates

Next, we assessed the association between clinical, sociodemographic, and technical characteristics and placental mtDNA mutational load in bivariate analyses. We detected a weak but statistically significant correlation between maternal pre-pregnancy BMI and placental mtDNA mutational load (Spearman's ρ = 0.15, p < 0.001, Fig. S3). We further explored this relationship by evaluating mtDNA mutation load in the overall sample by race/ethnicity (Fig. S4), and by obesity status in the overall sample (Fig. 2A), and among Blacks and Hispanics (Fig. 2B and C). Mutational load was higher in the obese group in the overall sample (p < 0.001) and in obese Black women (p < 0.01). The total number of mutations varied by race/ethnicity (p < 0.001, Fig. S4) with medians of 10, 9, and 7 in the Black, Hispanic, and White categories, respectively. In bivariate analysis, the sequencing platform was associated with the number of variants per sample (p < 0.001) and the median number of variants was higher in placentas from male newborns (p < 0.001) (Fig. S5).

Associations between maternal cumulative lifetime stress/trauma and mitochondrial DNA mutation load

We assessed the association of lifetime stress/trauma and placental mtDNA mutational load using negative binomial models in the overall study population and stratified by obesity and race/ethnicity in separate analyses (Table 2). Overall and obesity-stratified models were adjusted for maternal race/ethnicity, age, education, child sex, and sequencing platform, race-stratified models were adjusted by these same covariates, excluding race/ethnicity. Overall, an IQR increase in the weighted LSC-R was marginally associated with mtDNA mutational load (incidence rate ratio (IRR) 1.04, 95%CI, 0.99-1.09, p = 0.09). We first explored the effect of stress/trauma and obesity on placental mtDNA mutational load using statistical interaction. However, due to sample size limitation of the White obese group (n = 8), these analyses only included Black and Hispanic women (n = 421). We fitted negative binomial models with a two-way interaction effect between maternal stress/trauma and obesity status. In these analyses, we did not detect significant interaction between lifetime maternal stress/trauma (LSC-R score) and obesity status on placental mtDNA mutational load (Pinteraction LSC-R x Obesity = 0.47). Next, we fitted stratified analyses in the non-obese and obese women, which suggested that the association between maternal stress and mutational load was mainly in the non-obese (IRR = 1.06, 95%CI, 1.01-1.11, p = 0.04) subgroup, whereas no association was found in the obese subgroup (IRR = 1.00, 95%CI, 0.91-1.09, p = 0.99).

We next explored potential effect modification by obesity and race/ethnicity on the relation between lifetime stress/trauma and mtDNA mutational load using interactions and stratified analyses. Fig. 3 shows the divergent patterns between weighted LSC-R and total mutational load among the race/ethnic groups, Pinteraction LSC-R x race/ethn. = 0.20, showing weak evidence of interaction. In race/ethnicity stratified analyses, among Black women an IQR increase in LSC-R was associated with an increase of mutation load by a factor of 1.07 (95% CI, 1.01-1.14, p = 0.02). The effect estimate was in a similar direction in White women (IRR = 1.02, 95% CI, 0.99-1.05, p = 0.13) and in the opposite direction in the Hispanic group (IRR = 0.96, 95% CI, 0.88-1.05, p = 0.35).

Results from stratified models by obesity status and race/ethnicity group (Table 2) revealed that Black women in the non-obese group with higher lifetime stress/trauma had increased placental mutational load (IRR = 1.09, 95% CI, 1.02-1.17, p = 0.01). However, this was not found in Black women in the obese category (IRR = 1.02, 95% CI, 0.91-1.13, p = 0.76). In general, effect estimates for Hispanic women were in the opposite direction (non-obese IRR = 0.95, 95% CI, 0.86-1.96, p = 0.37 and obese IRR = 0.99, 95% CI, 0.83-1.20, p = 0.96), yet not significant. Models examining LSC-R score, obesity status and race/ethnicity (Pinteraction LSC-R x race/ethn x obesity = 0.19) showed weak evidence of interaction. Only Black and Hispanic women were included due to small sample size in the obese, non-Hispanic White subgroup.

In pre-planned sub-analyses, we evaluated associations between lifetime stress/trauma by placental mtDNA mutation type and mtDNA region in Blacks stratified by obesity status (Fig. S6, Table S7). For nonsynonymous mutations, results were comparable to those including all the mtDNA mutations but with stronger effect sizes in the non-obese group (IRR = 1.19, 95%CI, 1.06-1.33, p = 0.002). We did not detect associations when stratifying analyses by mutations in specific regions of the mtDNA genome.

Discussion

The association between maternal lifetime stress and placental mitochondrial mutations has been sparsely examined in the literature. We characterized placental mitochondrial DNA variation in the largest population study conducted to date including 496 mother-infant pairs from the multi-ethnic PRISM study. We investigated associations between lifetime stress/trauma, as characterized by the weighted LSC-R, and placental mtDNA mutational load and assessed effect modification by characteristics including obesity status and race/ethnicity. In overall analyses, there was moderate evidence of a positive association between mtDNA mutational load and maternal lifetime stress/trauma. In stratified analyses, associations were stronger in the non-obese subgroup of women than those in the obese subgroup, although estimates were imprecise with wide confidence intervals, and analyses may be underpowered in assessment of effect modification. We also report associations between increased lifetime maternal stress/trauma and mtDNA mutational load in Black women. Of note, we did not detect associations between maternal stress/trauma and mtDNA mutation count in any other race/ethnicity, albeit those analyses were less powered. In pre-planned sub-analyses by obesity status and race/ethnicity, a direct association was seen in women who were non-obese and Black however associations between LSC-R and mtDNA mutational load were not found in the subgroup of women who were obese and Black. Findings highlight the importance of conducting higher-order analyses that may identify subgroups, however noting that identifying and addressing psychosocial stressors regardless of obesity status is important.

In prior work, placental mtDNA mutational load has been associated with perinatal outcomes (e.g. gestational length and birthweight-for gestational-age) and infant negative affectivity, including evidence of sex or stress-specific effects. [43,63]. This current study allowed for examination of associations between maternal cumulative stress/trauma and placental mtDNA mutational load by maternal obesity status and race/ethnicity to identify potential biomarkers and subgroups that may be most affected by stress-related programming. Since both psychosocial stress and obesity have been shown to increase oxidative stress [21,64], and Black women may disproportionately experience both exposures, we hypothesized that the association of cumulative lifetime stress/trauma on placental mtDNA mutational load would be more pronounced in Black women in the obese group. However, as women who were obese had higher levels of mtDNA mutational load it is possible that higher levels of lifetime stress/trauma did not lead to further increases. Hence, the impact of stress was potentially detectable in the non-obese group. However, as this study is the first to examine the impact of both these gestational stressors on this specific mitochondrial biomarker, further work should attempt to replicate these associations.

As the cell's energy engine, mitochondria regulate vital cellular functions like division, growth, and death [65]. Mitochondria can survey information from the cellular environment produced intermediates/ROS that signal the nucleus to regulate gene expression to adapt cell responses accordingly [66]. Cumulative psychosocial stress impacts the immune and neuroendocrine system which in turn can affect subcellular mitochondrial processes [67]. Indeed, studies in laboratory animals have shown that acute and chronic stressors can impact multiple aspects of mitochondrial function [64], including in the placenta [68]. Similarly, mitochondria also play a crucial role in cellular metabolism, particularly in a metabolically dynamic organ like the placenta in charge of fetal growth and development [21]. Studies in both rodents and humans show that maternal metabolic stressors like pre-pregnancy obesity and GDM increase placental ROS [69,70] and can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction [71,72]. Moreover, altered placental mitochondrial function can link an adverse intrauterine environment to fetal programming of later disease. For example, mitochondrial dysfunction in the placenta programs pancreatic beta-cell dysfunction in newborn mice by altering mTOR signaling pathways [73]. Likewise, mitochondrial phenotype in rodents is associated with fetal growth, an important proxy for the in-utero environment [74], which may lead to later life disease programming. Yet, the epidemiological literature assessing the impact of gestational stressors in general on mtDNA markers is not extensive, particularly as it relates to the placenta. Most studies have assessed associations between cord blood mtDNA copy number and adverse environmental maternal exposures like smoking and air pollution [33,75].

Higher lifetime stress/trauma was associated with higher mtDNA mutational load only in Black women. Importantly, women in racial and minoritized groups and of lower socioeconomic status are at greater risk of obesity [76], as they are disproportionately exposed to psychosocial stress, traumatic experiences [17], and obesogenic factors [77,78]. It is possible that the different racial and ethnic groups may overall report experiencing different patterns of life stressors, that can be associated with factors such as racial discrimination, socioeconomic status, and country of origin [79-81]. Psychosocial and/or metabolic stress may both influence oxidative stress, impacting placental mitochondrial biology and mtDNA. Thus, stress-related changes in mtDNA mutational load may further influence health disparities, as has been reported in other conditions and tissues in Africa Americans [82,83].

Compared to the nuclear genome, the maternally inherited mitochondrial genome is more susceptible to mutations because it has reduced DNA repair capacity [84]. Next generation sequencing has made it possible to accurately identify mitochondrial variants, including those found only in proportion of the mtDNA molecules [55,57]. These low-level heteroplasmies have been shown to be shared by the mother and the fetal placenta [85]. In this study, to avoid false positives we adopted stringent criteria by including only high-quality mtDNA variants and setting the heteroplasmy level at 5% [55,57]. After adopting such criteria, the distribution of mtDNA mutations, including heteroplasmy levels, resemble those described in recent studies [55]. Overall, most of the mtDNA variants called were homoplasmic, and those detected as heteroplasmic were also observed in homoplasmy in another participant. Consistent with previous studies, most of the identified mutations were transitions and 97% have already been reported in MITOMAP [61]. Haplogroups were consistent with participant self-reported race/ethnicity, particularly in non-Hispanic Whites and Blacks. However, Hispanic participants were distributed across Haplogroups, consistent with previous reports and higher degree of genetic admixture in this population [60].

This study has multiple strengths. We leveraged samples and data from the PRISM cohort of ethnically and socioeconomically diverse dyads from two urban US cities. Our work is the largest comprehensive characterization of placental mitochondrial DNA mutational load, while also assessing associations between this biomarker and maternal psychosocial stress, obesity and race/ethnicity. We used a well-characterized tool to assess lifetime stress and were able to assess complex interactions with maternal characteristics [86]. We used next generation sequencing to profile mtDNA mutations as well as stringent bioinformatic methods for variant calling.

This work has several limitations. Due to sample size limitations, we did not evaluate associations with specific mtDNA variants. We did not account for cell-type heterogeneity or mitochondrial copy number, which can impact mutational load and/or heteroplasmy levels [55,87]. Additionally, trophoblast cell types differ in mitochondrial density [88]. We used a PCR-based mtDNA sequencing across two sequencing platforms. Although we used stringent quality-control and bioinformatic filtering to retain only high-quality variants, residual technical biases may remain. We cannot assess if mtDNA variants are inherited or acquired. Nevertheless, evidence indicates a low frequency of de novo mtDNA mutations in the placenta, suggesting that most of these variants are inherited from the mother [85]. Still, more research is needed to understand maternal-fetal mtDNA variant transmission. We assessed lifetime stress/trauma using the LSC-R, a self-report survey on cumulative lifetime stress/trauma. Our tool didn't assess specific timing or duration of the stressors, although events were weighted for their negative impact. We acknowledge that the LSC-R may not measure all traumatic experiences that an individual may experience, however it assesses several experienced or witnessed stressful life events including sexual harassment or abuse, domestic violence, physical or mental health conditions, child abuse and neglect, foster care, emotional or physical abuse, divorce or separation, incarceration, or death of someone close in addition to disasters, accidents, money problems, violence/robbery. Future research should confirm the observed associations, assess the clinical significance of findings, and potential long-term implications.

In summary, this work describes associations among maternal lifetime stress, pre-pregnancy obesity, race/ethnicity, and placental mitochondrial mutational load. Future epidemiological studies should replicate these findings in other populations, and laboratory studies can assess the mechanisms behind the cumulative effects of maternal stress alone or in combination with obesity, which can impact the placenta and have consequences for fetal programming.
