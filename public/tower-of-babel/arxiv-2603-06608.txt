Scaling Strategy, Not Compute: A Stand-Alone, Open-Source StarCraft II Benchmark for Accessible Reinforcement Learning Research
Sourav Panda, Shreyash Kale, Tanmay Ambadkar, Abhinav Verma, Jonathan Dodge

Abstract
The research community lacks a middle ground between StarCraft II’s full game and its mini-games. The full-game’s sprawling state-action space renders reward signals sparse and noisy, but in mini-games simple agents saturate performance. This complexity gap hinders steady curriculum design and prevents researchers from experimenting with modern Reinforcement Learning algorithms in RTS environments under realistic compute budgets. To fill this gap, we present the Two-Bridge Map Suite, the first entry in an open-source benchmark series we purposely engineered as an intermediate benchmark to sit between these extremes. By disabling economy mechanics such as resource collection, base building, and fog-of-war, the environment isolates two core tactical skills: long-range navigation and micro-combat. Preliminary experiments show that agents learn coherent maneuvering and engagement behaviors without imposing full-game computational costs. Two-Bridge is released as a lightweight, Gym-compatible wrapper on top of PySC2, with maps, wrappers, and reference scripts fully open-sourced to encourage broad adoption as a standard benchmark.

In this section, we describe the Two-Bridge Map Suite from an RL and game-theoretic perspective, using a system-level abstraction that emphasizes entities, interactions, and decision structure.
We designed the benchmark suite from scratch using the SC2 Editor, the primary tool for authoring custom maps in SC2.

2.1
Core Map Components

This subsection describes the static entities and structural elements that define the Two Bridge Map environment.

Map Layout (Terrain):
Figure
2
(Left) illustrates the overall terrain layout.
The playable area has a long cliff bisecting the map vertically, forming two bridges that serve as choke points between the left and right sides.
Units cannot traverse the cliff directly, so all movement must funnel through these narrow passages, creating a “breaching scenario” that is a classic tactical case study (e.g.,
(
5
)
).
While this separation is not a technical constraint, it is an intentional design choice that introduces a clear physical and spatial divide between the two sides.

Spawn Regions:

Figure
2
(Left) shows the six spawn regions on the map (indicated by blue overlays on the terrain): R1–R3 on the left side and R4–R6 on the right side.
These regions serve as the predefined spawning areas for both the beacon and units, which we describe in detail below.

Beacon:

Figure
2
(Middle) illustrates the beacon, a static, non-controllable entity in the environment.
It spawns in one of the predefined spawn regions that is not occupied by friendly or enemy units and remains stationary.

Units:

A unit is an individual controllable entity characterized by its position, health, movement capability, and attack range.
Friendly and enemy units spawn simultaneously as cohesive groups in distinct spawn regions, with no reinforcements or respawns.
Units are limited to basic movement and ranged attacks, and do not possess special abilities, upgrades, or resource-dependent mechanics.
To reduce confounding factors and isolate tactical decision-making, we employ unit homogeneity—using the same unit type (Marines) for both friendly and enemy forces.

Unit Control:

Figure
2
(Right) illustrates the centralized control structure used in the Two-Bridge Map Suite.
Friendly units are controlled by an RL agent, which acts as a centralized commander issuing actions for multiple friendly units simultaneously, allowing different RL algorithms and policy architectures to be evaluated within the same environment.
Enemy units are controlled by the built-in SC2 AI, which similarly operates under a centralized control abstraction.
Due to the custom nature of the map, the built-in AI is reactive and does not demonstrate higher-level strategic decision-making tailored to this setting.
Enemy units remain stationary unless provoked and attack opposing units that enter their attack range.

Visibility and Fog-of-War:

The environment is fully observable, with fog-of-war disabled, ensuring that all relevant terrain and unit positions are visible to the agent at all times.
This removes the need for scouting, a core strategic component of the full game, and simplifies the state representation by eliminating uncertainty arising from partial observability in hidden-information strategy games
(
23
;
17
)
.

2.2
Game Setting and Episode Semantics

This subsection describes the episode lifecycle of the Two Bridge Map, including episode initialization, objective-driven interaction, and termination conditions.

Episode Start (Randomized Spawning):

At the start of each episode, friendly units, enemy units, and the beacon spawn in predefined spawn regions (R1-R6) on the map.
A fixed number of friendly and enemy units spawn as cohesive groups, and the beacon spawns in a region not occupied by either group.
The spatial placement of both unit groups and the beacon varies across episodes, resulting in different initial configurations.
Figure
2
(Middle) shows that this process yields diverse starting layouts while preserving the same underlying map structure.

Episode Dynamics (Objectives):

The map focuses on two core
tactical skills:

navigation
and
combat
.
All other strategic components present in the full game are disabled, allowing the environment to concentrate agent learning on movement, positioning, and tactical engagement.
Figure
2
(Right) shows both objectives are simultaneously present within the same map configuration, framing the environment as a
multi-objective reinforcement learning
(MORL) problem.

Navigation (Beacon Capture)

involves an
unguarded
beacon that spawns in a region not occupied by any units.
Beacon capture occurs when a friendly unit enters a predefined capture radius around the beacon.
Accomplishing this objective requires the agent to (i) identify the beacon location and (ii) plan and execute a path to reach it.

Combat (Eliminate Enemies)
requires the agent to (i) navigate toward the enemy spawn region and (ii) engage in fine-grained micromanagement.
The two objectives are
mutually exclusive
, allowing the agent to achieve only one objective per episode.
These design choices simplify the episode structure and isolate decision-making around objective selection.

Episode End (Terminal Conditions):

Each episode terminates with one of five mutually exclusive outcomes.
A
navigation_victory
occurs when a friendly unit captures the beacon.
A
combat_victory
occurs when all enemy units perish, conversely a
combat_loss
occurs if all friendly units perish.
If both sides eliminate each other within the same timestep, the episode ends in a
tie
(rare).
Episodes have a fixed time limit of five minutes; if no terminal condition occurs within this limit, the episode ends in a
timeout_loss
.

Across episodes, randomized initialization results in varying initial states.
Consequently, agents must learn policies that generalize across multiple spatial arrangements when pursuing either navigation or combat objectives.
These basic episode semantics form the foundation upon which we introduce controlled map variants in the next subsection.

2.3
Map Variations and Strategic Diagnostics

To assess agent behavior across a range of tactical scenarios, we define a structured set of map variations.
These variations are constructed along two orthogonal axes that characterize the strategic conditions faced by the agent:
(i)
Unit Count Balance
: The relative number of friendly and enemy units.
(ii)
Objective Proximity
: The spatial relationship between friendly units, enemy units, and the beacon.

Unit Count Variants.

Since all units are of the same type (Marines), numerical advantage primarily governs combat dynamics.
Across all variants, we fix the number of friendly units at five and vary the number of enemy units.
We consider three unit balance configurations:
(i)
V1: Friendly Advantage (5F
>
3E):

Friendly units outnumber enemy forces.
(ii)
V2: Balanced Forces (5F = 5E).

Both sides begin with equal unit counts.
(iii)
V3: Enemy Advantage (5F
<
8E).

Enemy units outnumber friendly forces.

Objective Proximity Variants.

In addition to unit balance, we vary the relative spatial arrangement of friendly units, enemy units, and the beacon.
Across variants, the left–right assignment is fixed but arbitrary.
The defining factor is the relative proximity between friendly units, enemy units, and the beacon, which modulates the cost and risk associated with each objective.
(i)
Base Variant - Equidistant Objectives:

Friendly units spawn on the left, while both enemy units and the beacon spawn on the right, placing both objectives at comparable traversal distance.
(ii)
Combat-Proximal Variant:

Friendly and enemy units spawn on the right, while the beacon spawns on the left, meaning enemies are closer than the beacon.
(iii)
Navigation-Proximal Variant:

Friendly units and the beacon spawn on the right, while enemy units spawn on the left, meaning the beacon is closer than enemies.

Benchmark Suite.

Each unique pairing of unit count balance and objective proximity defines a distinct map configuration.
Together, these axes form a Cartesian product, yielding a suite of
nine configurations
that systematically probe agent behavior under different tactical conditions.
Figure
3
shows the resulting configurations and their associated strategic diagnostics, highlighting the decision-making pressures induced by each variant.
Across episodes, randomized spawning further introduces variability in initial states, requiring agents to adapt their strategies rather than overfit to a single configuration.

3
Experiments and Training Recipes

To validate the benchmark suite end-to-end, we evaluate it using readily available, off-the-shelf RL algorithms with minimal algorithmic customization.
Our goal is to test whether the environment itself exposes a learnable reward signal and elicits meaningful strategic trade-offs from agents.
Across all experiments, agent–environment interaction follows a standard pipeline in which the SC2 map is interfaced through PySC2, wrapped in a Gym-compatible API, and optimized by an RL agent.
We train agents from scratch without any scripting, curriculum learning, or imitation signals, ensuring that competent behavior emerges solely through direct interaction.
We run all experiments under modest consumer-grade compute constraints, without distributed training or clustering; hardware and implementation details in the Appendix
A.2
.
We next present the training recipes, experimental setups, and results across benchmark variants.

3.1
Experiment 1: Pilot Training Strategy

Given limited compute resources and multiple benchmark variants, we conduct a pilot study that tests a single preliminary training strategy on the
V2_Base
variant, chosen as a representative mid-difficulty configuration, before scaling evaluation across the full benchmark suite.
We train two standard on-policy RL algorithms using the Stable-Baselines3 (SB3) framework with default hyperparameters, running each agent for a total of 2M environment timesteps.
Across pilot runs, we observe an emergent control pattern in which learned policies predominantly issue commands to a single unit at a time, reassigning control only after unit loss.
We report detailed results and algorithm-specific behaviors in Appendix
C
and use the pilot findings solely to inform the experimental design of subsequent evaluations.

3.2
Experiment 2: Action Masking

Experiment 1 serves only as a pilot to guide design choices.
We now present Experiment 2, which is fully self-contained and specifies the training setup for evaluation across the benchmark suite; understanding this experiment does not require reading the pilot.
We next detail the observation space, action space, reward function, and training procedure.

Observation Space.

We use a combined vector–spatial observation representation derived from
PySC2
.
The observation consists of two components.

Vector features
provide a flat 55-dimensional representation encoding unit-level metadata, including the positions, health values, and weapon cooldowns of both friendly and enemy units, the beacon’s coordinates, the Euclidean distance between a unit and the beacon, elapsed game time, and the number of remaining enemy units.

Spatial features
consist of image-like observations, including a 17-channel
feature_screen
and a 7-channel
feature_minimap
, each at a resolution of
64\times 64
.
These channels capture unit distributions and terrain structure, providing explicit geometric context for reasoning about movement, engagement, and spatial coordination beyond what vector metadata alone can convey.
We fix this combined vector–spatial observation representation for all experiments in Experiment 2.

Action Space.

We employ a discrete action space defined at the unit level, while exposing a structured representation that supports centralized control.
At each timestep, the agent selects an action specified by:
(i) a high-level action verb (
no-op
,
move
, or
attack
),
(ii) a binary selection mask over friendly units indicating which units execute the action,
(iii) a movement direction for movement actions, and
(iv) an enemy index for attack actions.
Concretely, each friendly Marine executes one of
9+N_{E}
discrete commands: action 0 corresponds to a no-op, actions 1–8 represent movement in the eight compass directions, and actions 9–(8+
N_{E}
) correspond to attack commands that target enemy units, with one attack action per available enemy target, where
N_{E}
denotes the number of enemy units currently alive.
This formulation allows a single decision to coordinate actions across multiple units while retaining an interpretable, unit-level command structure.
To constrain exploration and avoid infeasible high-level decisions, we apply
explicit action masking
at the
verb level
.
Masking disables high-level actions that are not applicable in the current state, ensuring that
move
is selected only when at least one friendly unit is alive and that
attack
is selected only when at least one enemy unit is present.
Restricting the policy’s choice among high-level action types reduces the effective action space and guides exploration toward state-relevant decisions while preserving a simple, centralized control interface.

Reward Function:

We employ a reward structure that provides dense and informative learning signals while remaining aligned with the two core objectives:
navigation
and
combat
, together with an explicit terminal signal.
At each discrete environment timestep
t
, the total reward is defined as

r_{t}=r^{\text{nav}}_{t}+r^{\text{combat}}_{t}+r^{\text{terminal}}_{t}.

Navigation
progress is incentivized using a dense shaping reward based on changes in Euclidean distance to the beacon.
At each timestep, we compute the distance between each alive friendly unit and the beacon and use the change in their
average
distance as the navigation reward.
Averaging across units prevents the distance-based reward from scaling linearly with the number of units, which would otherwise dominate the overall reward signal as multiple units move simultaneously.
We apply this change in average distance bidirectionally: the agent receives positive reward when units approach the beacon and negative reward when units retreat.
This yields a symmetric progress signal that encourages sustained advancement while discouraging regressions.

Combat
effectiveness is incentivized through a combination of spatial, health-based, and event-based shaping signals.
First, analogous to the navigation objective, we include a bidirectional distance-based reward that encourages friendly units to reduce their average distance to the centroid of alive enemy units, while penalizing movement away from it.
Second, we apply continuous health-based shaping at every timestep, proportional to changes in total enemy and friendly hit points (with each Marine having 45 hit points), rewarding damage dealt while penalizing friendly losses.
This health-based signal is deliberately scaled to ensure that it doesn’t dominate the overall reward.
Finally, discrete bonuses are applied for enemy kills and penalties for friendly casualties.

Terminal Outcome.

Upon episode termination, we apply an outcome-dependent terminal reward, positive for victory and negative for loss.
We apply a larger bonus
+25
for successful navigation (beacon capture) to balance the richer, dense shaping signals present in the combat objective, while combat victory yields
+10
.
Combat loss incurs a penalty of
-10
, and time-limit termination incurs a larger penalty of
-15
.
Ties receive no additional terminal reward.

3.3
Experiment 3: Camera Lock

Building on Experiment 2, we further refine the training setup while keeping the observation representation and reward function unchanged.
This experiment isolates two additional design choices: (i) enforcing a camera-locked, on-screen viewpoint, and (ii) strengthening action masking

Camera Lock.

Figure
4
illustrates the camera-locked observation setting used in this experiment.
In SC2, camera movement is itself an explicit action.
Under a free-camera setup, unit movement does not automatically update the screen observation;
unless the agent issues a
move-camera
action, the screen displays empty or irrelevant terrain as units move out of view.
This introduces a misalignment between agent actions and visual input and requires the policy to reason jointly about
where to look
and
what action to take
.
To avoid this confound and prevent unnecessary expansion of the action space, we enforce a camera-locked setting in which the camera remains centered on the friendly unit group at every timestep.
As a result, the screen observation consistently contains the agent’s units, nearby terrain, and proximal enemies, while the minimap continues to provide a compact global context.
This design yields richer and more informative screen observations without requiring the agent to learn camera control, while maintaining the same action semantics as Experiment 2.

Action Space.

In Experiment 3, we extend the structured action interface from Experiment 2 by applying
branch-level action masking
across all components of the action representation.
Specifically, we provide separate availability masks for the high-level action
verb
, the friendly unit selection mask
who
, the movement
direction
, and the attack target
enemy_idx
.
The
who
mask restricts selection to alive friendly units, the
enemy_idx
mask enables only currently alive enemy targets while always permitting a null target, and the
direction
mask enables the eight compass directions only when movement is feasible.
In addition, the
verb
mask permits
move
and
attack
only when the corresponding entities are present in the state.
By enforcing feasibility at each branch of the action structure, this design substantially reduces the number of invalid joint action combinations and concentrates exploration on semantically valid, executable commands under the same centralized, unit-level control paradigm.

3.4
Training Protocol and Compute Budget

Both experiments
3.2
and
3.3
follow a shared training protocol.
We train a Maskable PPO agent
(
7
)
using the SB3 implementation with default hyperparameters, without algorithm-specific tuning.
Each environment variant is trained for 5M environment timesteps.
Across the benchmark suite, this results in a total of 18 independent training runs, corresponding to all combinations of map difficulty levels and task variants evaluated in Experiments 2 and 3.
Each training run requires approximately 5–6 days of wall-clock time on a single consumer-grade GPU.
TensorBoard training logs are provided in Appendix
D
.

Results and Qualitative Analysis

Figure
5
summarizes the terminal outcome distributions across variants and difficulty levels for Experiments 2 and 3.
While these aggregate outcomes provide a coarse measure of performance, they do not fully capture the diversity of behaviors exhibited by the agents.
We therefore present a qualitative analysis organized by
training recipe
, with behaviors discussed across difficulty levels.

4.1
Experiment 2: Qualitative Behavior

V1:

Across all layouts, the agent adopts a consistent combat-dominant strategy, issuing multi-unit commands that commit the entire friendly group to engagement.
Combat victories are frequent and primarily driven by numerical advantage, rather than tactical positioning or formation control.
Navigation success is incidental, occurring only when the beacon lies along the agent’s path toward combat.
Even in the Navigate variant, the agent consistently crosses sides to engage enemies and does not explicitly target the beacon.

V2:

Across all layouts, the agent exhibits a consistent pattern of initial full-force combat followed by a deterministic retreat after partial unit loss.
The retreat follows a fixed trajectory—toward the upper-right region.
Navigation wins occur only when the beacon lies along this retreat path;
otherwise, objectives placed elsewhere remain undiscovered, resulting in timeout losses.
Due to balanced firepower, combat outcomes are stochastic, with occasional wins arising from engagement variability rather than adaptive tactics.

V3:

Performance degrades sharply and behavior diverges by layout.
In the Base and Combat layouts, agent initiates combat but frequently fragments its forces, leading to unfavorable engagements against numerically superior enemies and consistent combat losses.
In the Navigate layout, agent avoids combat and performs a brief, region-specific initial exploration—typically in the middle-right—before moving to the bottom-right region and idling until timeout.
It avoids crossing bridges and does not re-engage after this initial exploration phase.
Navigation wins occur only rarely, when the beacon is encountered during this limited initial search.

4.2
Experiment 3: Qualitative Behavior

Under the camera-locked setting, we observe a
peculiar camera-centered behavior
that consistently shapes agent decisions.
The agent selects all friendly units and issues group-level movement or attack commands, with the camera moving jointly with the unit group.
Once all enemy units within the camera’s field of view are eliminated, any remaining enemy units outside the camera boundary are not engaged.
In such cases, agent repeatedly selects the
no-op
action, leading to episode termination via timeout loss.

V1:

Behavior largely mirrors Experiment 2, with the agent consistently committing all units to combat while ignoring the navigation beacon across all layouts.
No distinguishable combat tactics or coordinated positioning strategies are observed.
The camera-bound failure mode is prominent.

V2:

Behavior varies by layout.
In the Base layout, the agent often selects no-op for extended periods before eventually committing all units to combat, yielding stochastic outcomes.
In the Combat layout, the agent immediately sends all units to engage enemies, ignores the beacon, and again exhibits the camera-bound failure mode, with combat results remaining stochastic.
In the Navigate layout, the agent moves all units toward the bridge nearest the enemy and becomes stuck oscillating near this region, failing to commit to either objective.
This reflects a local optimum induced by competing distance-based rewards for combat and navigation, with navigation wins occurring only incidentally when the beacon lies along the combat trajectory.

V3:

Behaviors diverge further across layouts.
In the Base layout, the agent immediately sends all units into combat.
In the Combat layout, the agent does not actively engage enemies; instead, it moves all units to the extreme right side of the map and traverses upward toward the upper-right region.
If enemy units are present in the upper-right area, combat is triggered due to proximity.
Otherwise, when enemies are located in the middle region, the agent avoids engagement by remaining at the extreme right edge, effectively using map boundaries to avoid entering enemy attack range while continuing exploratory movement in the upper-right region.
In the Navigate layout, the agent commits to a single trajectory toward the upper-right region.
If the beacon is located in the lower-right region, it is ignored, and the agent remains stuck at the upper boundary.
If the beacon is placed at the top, navigation wins are achieved.
When the beacon is located in the middle and the agent spawns at the bottom, it bypasses the beacon by moving along the rightmost edge and again becomes stuck at the upper extreme.

5
Discussion, Limitations, and Future Work

Discussion:

Designed explicitly for modest compute budgets, the Two-Bridge Map Suite nonetheless elicits a diverse range of agent behaviors rather than a single notion of success or failure.
Across training recipes, we observe consistent qualitative phenomena that emerge without being explicitly encoded in the reward or action structure.
At the same time, many aspects of strategic decision-making in this environment remain unresolved, indicating clear headroom for methodological advances beyond incremental tuning.
By emphasizing usability and controlled complexity, the Two-Bridge Map Suite encourages exploration of new representations, learning signals, and coordination strategies, enabling broader participation in SC2-based RL research without reliance on resource-intensive training regimes.

Limitations:

The current benchmark’s reliable reproducibility is currently limited to Windows-based systems, as Linux support for SC2 and PySC2 remains less stable and inconsistently maintained.
As a result, environment execution is CPU-bound, which restricts flexible deployment on GPU-centric compute clusters and limits certain large-scale training workflows.
In addition, while the environment integrates cleanly with modern PyTorch-based RL pipelines, long-term viability depends on continued maintenance of external dependencies, including SC2 binaries and PySC2 version compatibility.
These limitations reflect broader infrastructure challenges in SC2-based research rather than properties specific to the Two-Bridge Map Suite.

Future Work:

The Two-Bridge Map Suite enables several natural extensions.
Future work may explore decentralized or multi-agent control to study coordination and credit assignment, as well as stronger opponents through curriculum learning or self-play.
Reintroducing partial observability would allow investigation of belief-state reasoning.
Additional directions include incorporating unit-type diversity, expanding the objective set, and studying hierarchical or temporally abstract action representations.

SC2 has served as a major benchmark for RL.
Existing work falls into four categories: full-game, mini-games, custom scenarios, and SMAC, each offering different trade-offs.

Full-Game SC2:

7
Conclusion

The Two-Bridge Benchmark Suite offers a structured, open-source platform for evaluating strategic RL under controlled yet challenging conditions.
By occupying a middle ground between oversimplified mini-games and resource-intensive full-game setups, it enables researchers to explore nuanced behaviors like navigation, combat, and trade-off reasoning—without requiring replays, scripting, or extensive compute.
We hope this benchmark encourages experimentation with novel RL methods and supports the community’s broader efforts toward generalizable, interpretable, and scalable AI in complex environments.

8
Impact Statement

This paper presents an open-source benchmark intended to advance reinforcement learning research by enabling reproducible and accessible experimentation in strategic decision-making environments.
While the benchmark supports progress in algorithm design, evaluation, and interpretability, it does not introduce new deployment-facing capabilities.

Appendix B
Trigger Construction Details

This section documents the trigger-level construction of the Two-Bridge Map Suite using the SC2 Editor.
All map files are provided in the supplementary material.
To inspect the trigger logic, users should first install SC2 and copy the provided map files into the appropriate
StarCraft II/Maps
directory.
Opening any of these maps launches the StarCraft II Editor.
Within the editor, clicking the Triggers icon (represented by a gears symbol in the editor toolbar) opens the trigger editor, where the trigger configurations are described.
The figures in this section illustrate the trigger logic for the
V2
variants, in which both players are initialized with equal unit counts.
The triggers specify (i) randomized spawn region selection, (ii) beacon placement logic, and (iii) unit creation and camera initialization.
Different unit-count variants can be generated by modifying the player unit variables (e.g.,
P1Units
,
P2Units
) within the same trigger framework, without altering the overall map structure.

Importantly, episode termination conditions and game-control logic are
not
implemented in the SC2 triggers.
All termination criteria (e.g., win, loss, timeout) are handled exclusively within the Gym-compatible environment wrappers.
This separation avoids trigger-level side effects and potential in-game control glitches, and ensures consistent episode semantics across layouts and algorithms.

Base Layout Trigger Logic:

Figure
6
shows the SC2 Editor trigger configuration for the Base layout.
At map initialization, the spawn region for Player 1 is first selected uniformly at random from regions
R1–R3
.
A fixed number of units (five in the V2 configuration) are then spawned for Player 1 at random points within the selected region, and the camera is panned to center on this spawn location.
Next, the beacon spawn region is selected uniformly at random from the regions
R4–R6
, and the beacon is instantiated at a random point within the chosen region.
The spawn region for Player 2 is then selected from the remaining regions not occupied by the beacon, ensuring that player and objective placements do not overlap.
Player 2 units are spawned analogously within the selected region.
Finally, fog-of-war is disabled to provide full observability.

Appendix C
Experiment 1: Pilot Training Strategy

Here, we define the setup of our pilot training protocol.
For pilot validation, we use the
V2_Base
variant as a representative mid-difficulty test case.

Observation Space:

We evaluate two observation modalities provided by PySC2:
Non-Spatial features (NSF)
and
Spatial features (SF)
.
In the Non-Spatial configuration, the agent receives a flat 55-dimensional vector encoding unit-level metadata, including positions, health values, and weapon cooldowns for both friendly and enemy units, the beacon’s coordinates, the Euclidean distance to the beacon, elapsed game time, and the number of remaining enemy units.
This reduces computational overhead at the expense of explicit spatial and geometric context.
In the spatial configuration the same 55-dimensional vector is augmented with spatial observations derived from
PySC2
, including a 17-channel
feature_screen
and a 7-channel
feature_minimap
, each at a resolution of
64\times 64
.
These spatial channels provide richer state information, capturing unit distributions, visibility, and terrain structure that are not directly available in the vector-only representation.

Action Space:

For both observation configurations, we employ a discrete action space defined at the unit level.
Each friendly Marine is assigned one of 14 discrete commands: action 0 corresponds to a no-op, actions 1–8 represent movement in the eight compass directions, and actions 9–13 correspond to attack commands targeting enemy units indexed from 0 to 4.

Reward Function:
We structure it around the two objectives: navigation and combat, with an additional terminal signal.
The total reward is given by

r_{t}=r^{\text{nav}}_{t}+r^{\text{combat}}_{t}+r^{\text{terminal}}
,
where
t
denotes the discrete environment timestep.

Navigation
progress is incentivized using a dense shaping reward based on the reduction in Euclidean distance between a designated
leading unit
and the beacon:

r^{\text{nav}}_{t}=d_{t-1}-d_{t}
,
where
d_{t}
denotes the distance between the beacon and the leading unit.
The leading unit is defined as the first friendly Marine under a fixed ordering by unit tag.
This navigation reward is applied once per timestep and shared across all controlled units.

Combat
performance is incentivized via a kill–loss delta reward that reflects changes in unit counts:

r^{\text{combat}}_{t}=(E_{t-1}-E_{t})-(F_{t-1}-F_{t})
,
where
E_{t}
and
F_{t}
denote the number of alive enemy and friendly units at time
t
, respectively. This formulation rewards eliminating enemies while penalizing friendly casualties.

Terminal:
Upon episode termination, we apply a terminal reward of
+10
for any win condition (beacon capture or elimination of all enemies), a penalty of
-10
for any loss condition (all friendly units eliminated or time limit exceeded), and
0
for ties.

Training Details:

We train two on-policy RL algorithms—Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
(
21
)
and Advantage Actor–Critic (A2C)
(
14
)
—using the Stable-Baselines3 (SB3) implementation
(
18
)
, with all experiments using default SB3 hyperparameters.
Both PPO and A2C agents are trained for a total of 2M environment timesteps.
For NSF observation recipes, we use the
MlpPolicy
, while for SF observation recipes we employ the
MultiInputPolicy
to accommodate dictionary-structured observations.

C.1
Experiment 1: Results and Analysis

We analyze both quantitative outcomes and qualitative policy behavior across observation recipes and algorithms.
Figure
10
reports terminal outcomes and TensorBoard training logs for both PPO and A2C under NSF and SF observation recipes.
Across all settings, we observe a consistent emergent control pattern in which the learned policy predominantly issues commands to a single unit at a time, reassigning control only after the active unit is lost.

PPO-NSF
converges to a degenerate policy that repeatedly routes a unit toward a fixed map direction (upper-right), independent of objective placement.
Outcomes are therefore incidental: encountering a beacon yields a navigation win, while encountering enemy units results in unfavorable combat and unit loss.
In the absence of any objective, the agent remains at the terminal position and the episode ends in a timeout.
Following unit loss, subsequent units are dispatched along the same path, leading to repeated failures.
In contrast,
PPO-SF
exhibits improved early-stage movement dynamics, replacing immediate directional commitment with a short exploratory sweep (rightward motion followed by an upward scan).
However, this behavior remains focused primarily on beacon discovery rather than adversary reasoning: beacon encounters produce navigation wins, while enemy encounters often lead to unit sacrifice, after which remaining units are routed toward the bottom-right and frequently idle until timeout.

A2C-NSF
fails to develop a consistent conditional strategy across episode initializations, frequently struggling to traverse the central terrain constraint and spending substantial time transitioning from left to right.
Upon reaching the right side of the map, behavior remains inconsistent: the policy often routes units toward the map center but shows no preference between navigation and combat objectives, leading to noticeable variation in terminal outcomes across evaluation episodes.
In several episodes, the first unit is lost in combat and the second unit re-engages rather than idling; however, time wasted during earlier terrain traversal frequently results in timeout losses.

A2C-SF
collapses to a more deterministic but degenerate parking behavior.
Across episodes, the agent consistently crosses the nearest bridge, moves straight toward the rightmost boundary at a fixed vertical offset, and remains stationary thereafter.
If a beacon is incidentally encountered along this trajectory, the episode terminates in a navigation win; if enemy units are encountered, the leading unit is lost and subsequent units repeat the same behavior, frequently resulting in timeout losses.
