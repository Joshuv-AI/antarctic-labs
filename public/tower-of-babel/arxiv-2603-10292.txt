Inverse Learning-Based Output Feedback Control of Nonlinear Systems with Verifiable Guarantees
Yeongjun Jang, Hamin Chang, Heein Park, Hyeonyeong Jang, Takashi Tanaka, Hyungbo Shim

Abstract
In this paper, we present a data-driven output feedback controller for nonlinear systems that achieves practical output regulation, using noise-free input/output measurement data. The proposed controller is based on (i) an inverse model of the system identified via kernel interpolation, which maps a desired output and the current state to the corresponding desired control input; and (ii) a data-driven reference selection framework that actively chooses a suitable desired output from the dataset which has been used for the identification. We establish a verifiable sufficient condition on the dataset under which the proposed controller guarantees practical output regulation. Numerical simulations demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed controller, with additional evaluations in the presence of output measurement noise to assess its robustness empirically.



I
Introduction

Data-driven control aims to design controllers directly from measured data, thereby avoiding the need to identify an explicit mathematical model of the underlying system.
This method has emerged as an effective alternative to model-based control, particularly when obtaining a model is too costly or requires substantial domain expertise
[
21
]
.
In the case of linear systems, for example, data-driven control has been extensively studied
[
11
,
8
,
47
,
3
,
46
]
, building on the behavioral approach and the fundamental lemma introduced in
[
49
]
.

Recently, there has been growing interest in data-driven control for nonlinear systems.
The main challenge in this direction is to establish theoretical closed-loop guarantees
[
30
]
, and existing works that provide such guarantees rely on conditions that are often difficult to verify in practice.
For example,
[
4
]
proposed a model predictive control (MPC) scheme that exploits local linearizations of the system, whose closed-loop guarantees require recursive feasibility of the MPC optimization problem.
The works of
[
9
]
and
[
18
]
formulated data-dependent optimization problems based on linear matrix inequality (LMI) and sum-of-squares (SOS) conditions, respectively, to synthesize stabilizing controllers.
In
[
42
]
, the Koopman operator is employed to lift a nonlinear system into a higher-dimensional linear system, enabling an LMI-based control design that ensures stability.
Although these results provide formal guarantees, verifying the feasibility of the associated MPC optimization problem, and the LMI and SOS conditions can be nontrivial and computationally intensive.

In this context, kernel methods have become a popular tool for data-driven control of nonlinear systems due to their ability to provide practical and reliable regression error bounds
[
41
,
7
,
15
,
37
,
39
]
.
Moreover, their nonparametric nature allows them to represent a broad class of functions
[
40
,
50
,
25
]
.
These methods have mainly been utilized to identify a
forward model
of the system, followed by the design of a controller that compensates for residual (learning) errors that may occur
[
27
]
.
However, the resulting models are often highly nonlinear with limited physical meaning, which complicates the controller design.
Consequently, most existing approaches have been restricted to MPC frameworks
[
20
,
28
,
34
,
23
,
10
]
, which require solving an optimization problem at each time step.
This incurs substantial computational burden, and theoretical guarantees are typically stated under the assumption that the online optimization problem is recursively feasible, which is often difficult to verify.
Other existing approaches have focused on designing robust linear controllers under certain simplifications, such as assuming bounded nonlinearities
[
14
]
or considering control-affine systems with constant input matrices
[
22
]
.

On the other hand, there have been approaches that utilize kernel methods to identify an
inverse model
of the system
[
51
,
38
,
26
,
43
,
24
]
, which maps a desired output and the current state to the corresponding desired control input.
This obviates the need for designing a controller for the identified model because it can directly act as a tracking controller.
Provided that the reference trajectory is feasible,
[
26
]
and
[
43
]
established closed-loop guarantees on tracking performance.
However, determining whether a reference trajectory is feasible without explicit knowledge of the system dynamics is generally unrealistic, which limits the practicality of such guarantees.

To address this limitation,
[
24
]
introduced a data-driven framework that actively selects suitable reference points from the input/state data used to identify the inverse model.
The key idea is to leverage interpolation/regression error bounds for kernel methods to construct, for each data point, a region of the state space where using that point as a reference ensures a desirable closed-loop behavior.
Building upon this framework,
[
24
]
established a verifiable sufficient condition on the dataset under which a data-driven state feedback controller renders the closed-loop system ultimately bounded.

In this paper, we propose a data-driven
output
feedback controller for nonlinear systems that achieves practical output regulation.
The controller is designed based on an inverse model of the system identified from noise-free input/output measurements via kernel interpolation, and thus, does not require full state measurements.
Based on the error bound of kernel interpolation, we derive a verifiable sufficient condition on the dataset under which the proposed controller guarantees practical output regulation.
The effectiveness of the proposed controller is demonstrated through numerical simulations, and further tests carried out in the presence of output measurement noise indicate that the proposed controller can remain effective under noisy environments.

Specifically, we focus on systems represented in the nonlinear autoregressive exogenous (NARX) form, a popular framework for modeling nonlinear dynamics
[
6
,
5
,
32
,
36
]
.
NARX models can be reformulated into an augmented state-space representation, where a one-step ahead output is fully determined by an augmented state comprising past inputs and outputs.
A direct application of the framework in
[
24
]
, however, would ultimately bound the entire augmented state, which unnecessarily drives both input and output sequences toward zero.
This is particularly problematic for systems that require time-varying or oscillatory control inputs to regulate the output.
In this regard, we adopt and generalize the reference selection framework of
[
24
]
to accommodate the augmented state formulation.

Section

Finally, Section

II
Problem Formulation

Consider a discrete-time system represented by a nonlinear autoregressive exogenous (NARX) model of the form

\displaystyle y(t+1)=f(y_{[t-n+1,t]},u_{[t-n+1,t]}),

(1)

where
u(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the input,
y(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the output, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
is the model order.
For the sake of simplicity, we focus on the case
m=p=1
and demonstrate that our result can be extended to the multi-input multi-output case in Remark
5
.
It is assumed that the function
f
is unknown, while
n
is known.

The goal of this paper is to design a data-driven output feedback controller using input/output measurement data of (
1
) that achieves practical output regulation with a desired accuracy
\delta>0
in finite time.
That is, for some
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
that depends on the accuracy
\delta
and the initial condition
(y_{[-n+1,0]},u_{[-n+1,0]})
at
t=0
, the controller should ensure

\|y(t)\|\leq\delta\quad\forall t\geq\kappa.

(2)

In addition, we aim to establish a verifiable sufficient condition on the dataset under which the proposed controller guarantees (
2
).

In line with
[
26
]
and
[
24
]
, we identify an inverse model of (
1
) and utilize it as a foundation of the controller.
In order to formally define an inverse model, the following definitions and assumption are introduced.
Let us rewrite system (
1
), with a slight abuse of notation, as

y(t+1)=f(\zeta(t),u(t)),

(3)

where

\zeta(t):=\begin{bmatrix}y_{[t-n+1,t]}\\
u_{[t-n+1,t-1]}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}

(4)

is referred to as the
augmented state
at time step
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, consisting of the most recent
n
outputs and
n-1
inputs.
The set of feasible augmented states is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}:=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}\mid\exists u,y:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}~\mbox{satisfying}~\eqref{eq:sys}~\mbox{for all}~t\in\mathbb{Z}~\mbox{and}~\zeta=\zeta(\tau)~\mbox{for some}~\tau\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

In other words,
\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}
collects all augmented states that are realizable by some input/output trajectory of system (
3
).
For any
\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}
, we define the one-step reachable set of outputs by

\mathcal{R}(\zeta):=\left\{y^{+}\in\mathbb{R}\mid\exists u\in\mathbb{R}\ \mbox{such that}\ y^{+}=f(\zeta,u)\right\},

(5)

and assume that the mapping
f(\zeta,\cdot)
is injective for all
\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}
as follows.

Assumption 1
.

For any
\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}
and
y^{+}\in\mathcal{R}(\zeta)
, there exists a unique
u\in\mathbb{R}
such that
y^{+}=f(\zeta,u)
.

\square

Assumption
1
implies that system (
3
) has a global relative degree one, since the current input
u(t)
directly influences the next output
y(t+1)
and is uniquely determined from
\zeta(t)
and
y(t+1)
.
In Section
IV-C
, we discuss how our framework can be extended to NARX models with input delays, which corresponds to the case with global relative degree greater than one.

Under Assumption
1
, we define the inverse model of (
3
) as follows.

Definition 1
.

The inverse model of system (
3
) is defined as a function
c:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

y^{+}=f(\zeta,c([y^{+};\zeta]))

(6)

for all
\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}
and
y^{+}\in\mathcal{R}(\zeta)
.

\square

The inverse model
c
can be interpreted as a function that maps a desired output
y^{+}
and an augmented state
\zeta
to the corresponding desired control input
c([y^{+};\zeta])
such that (
6
) holds.
Definition
1
is implicit, as it does not specify the value of
c([y^{+};\zeta])
when
\zeta\notin\mathcal{Z}
or
y^{+}\notin\mathcal{R}(\zeta)
.
However, we emphasize that any function
c
satisfying Definition
1
can serve as the inverse model in our approach, and its specific choice does not affect the theoretical formulation or results.
For example, one may choose the inverse model as the function
c
that renders
f(\zeta,c([y^{+};\zeta]))
to be the projection of
y^{+}
onto
\mathcal{R}(\zeta)
, as in
[
24
]
.

Remark 1
.

Suppose that system (
3
) is affine-in-control, written as

y(t+1)=h(\zeta(t))+g(\zeta(t))\cdot u(t)

(7)

with some functions
h:\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
g(\zeta)\neq 0
for all
\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}
.
Then, it follows that
\mathcal{R}(\zeta)=\mathbb{R}
for all
\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}
, and Assumption
1
holds since the input
u\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying
y^{+}=f(\zeta,u)
is uniquely determined by
u=(y^{+}-h(\zeta))/g(\zeta)
.

\square

The inverse model
c
can be directly utilized as a feedback controller that tracks a given reference trajectory;
given
y_{\mathrm{r}}(t+1)\in\mathcal{R}(\zeta(t))
, a controller of the form

u(t)=c([y_{\mathrm{r}}(t+1);\zeta(t)])

(8)

achieves

\begin{split}y(t+1)=f(\zeta(t),c([y_{\mathrm{r}}(t+1);\zeta(t)]))=y_{\mathrm{r}}(t+1)\end{split}

by Definition
1
.
However, the design of controller (
8
) requires both exact knowledge of the inverse model
c
and a reference point
y_{\mathrm{r}}(t+1)
that is one-step reachable from
\zeta(t)
, clearly necessitating knowledge of the function
f
.

In this context, we aim to propose an inverse learning-based controller of the form

u(t)=\hat{c}([y_{\mathrm{r}}(t+1);\zeta(t)])

(9)

that guarantees (
2
), where (i)
\hat{c}
is a data-driven identification of the inverse model
c
based on input/output measurement data of (
3
);
(ii) the reference point
y_{\mathrm{r}}(t+1)
is actively chosen from the dataset used for the identification.

Since
f
is assumed to be unknown, we cannot, in general, verify one-step reachability of a chosen reference point.
Thus, we make a technical assumption that possibly conservative Lipschitz constants of the function
f
and its inverse model
c
are known.
This will enable us to quantify the deviation of the actual output
y(t+1)
, obtained by applying (
9
), from the reference point
y_{\mathrm{r}}(t+1)
.

Assumption 2
.

There exist known constants
L_{f}>0
and
L_{c}>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\|f(\zeta,u)-f(\zeta^{\prime},u^{\prime})\right\|\leq L_{f}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\zeta-\zeta^{\prime}\\
u-u^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}\right\|,~~~~~~\left\|c(\xi)-c(\xi^{\prime})\right\|\leq L_{c}\|\xi-\xi^{\prime}\|

for all
\zeta,\zeta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}
,
u,u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

\square

Remark 2
.

We show that nonlinear systems of the form

\displaystyle x(t+1)

\displaystyle=F(x(t),u(t)),

(10)

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=h(x(t)),

can be equivalently reformulated into the NARX form (
3
) if it is uniformly

]
.
Here,
x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
is the state, and
u(t)\in\mathbb{R}
and
y(t)\in\mathbb{R}
are the input and output, respectively, as in (
3
).
Uniform
n
-observability is frequently assumed in nonlinear observer design
[
19
,
17
]
, as it enables the reconstruction of the initial state from a finite number of input/output measurements.
That is, it implies that there exists a mapping
H:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
such that

x(t-n+1)=H(y_{[t-n+1,t]},u_{[t-n+1,t]})

for any pair of input sequence
u_{[t-n+1,t]}
and output sequence
y_{[t-n+1,t]}
of system (
10
), and the corresponding state
x(t-n+1)
.

Now, let
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
denote the
n
-step state transition mapping that satisfies

x(t+1)=\Phi(x(t-n+1),u_{[t-n+1,t]}).

Substituting this into (
10
) yields

\displaystyle y(t+1)

\displaystyle=h(x(t+1))

\displaystyle=h(\Phi(x(t-n+1),u_{[t-n+1,t]}))

\displaystyle=h(\Phi(H(y_{[t-n+1,t]},u_{[t-n+1,t]}),u_{[t-n+1,t]}))=:f(\zeta(t),u(t)),

which is in the NARX form (
3
).

\square

III
Inverse Model Identification and
Error Bounds

III-A

Learning the inverse model

For the design of controller (
9
), we employ kernel interpolation (KI) to identify the inverse model
c
as
\hat{c}
.
To conduct the identification, we first collect and rearrange input/output data of system (
3
) into input/output data of the inverse model
c
.
Consider a
T
-length input/output trajectory
1
1

1

The superscript
\mathrm{d}
indicates sample data collected from offline experiments conducted prior to control design.
of system (
3
) denoted by
(u_{[0,T-1]}^{\mathrm{d}},y_{[0,T]}^{\mathrm{d}})
, where
T\geq n
.
Then,

y^{\mathrm{d}}(i+n-1)=f(\zeta^{\mathrm{d}}(i+n-2),u^{\mathrm{d}}(i+n-2))

for all
1\leq i\leq T-n+1
, where

\zeta^{\mathrm{d}}(i+n-2):=\begin{bmatrix}y_{[i-1,i+n-2]}^{\mathrm{d}}\\
u_{[i-1,i+n-3]}^{\mathrm{d}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}.

By the definition of the inverse model
c
,

u^{\mathrm{d}}(i+n-2)=c([y^{\mathrm{d}}(i+n-1);\zeta^{\mathrm{d}}(i+n-2)])

holds for all
1\leq i\leq T-n+1
, and this allows us to construct a training dataset
\cal{D}
that consists of input/output data of the inverse model
c
:

\mathcal{D}:=\{([y_{i}^{+};\zeta_{i}],u_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N},

(11)

where
N:=T-n+1
is the number of training data and

[y_{i}^{+};\zeta_{i}]:=[y^{\mathrm{d}}(i+n-1);\zeta^{\mathrm{d}}(i+n-2)],~~u_{i}:=u^{\mathrm{d}}(i+n-2),

are the training input and output, respectively.
For notational simplicity, we also define

\begin{split}\xi_{i}:=[y_{i}^{+};\zeta_{i}],~~~~\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{in}}:=\{\xi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},~~~~\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{to}}:=\{y_{i}^{+}\}_{i=1}^{N}.\end{split}

It is emphasized that the training dataset
\mathcal{D}
can alternatively be constructed from multiple short input/output trajectories of (
3
).
This aspect is especially advantageous when the system is unstable, as it allows the collection of a sufficiently large training dataset while avoiding numerical instability issues.

Given the dataset
\mathcal{D}
, KI obtains an estimate of
c
by appropriately interpolating the data points.
To understand KI, two key concepts are crucial: (strictly) positive definite kernels, which measure similarity between data points, and reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS), which provides a mathematical framework for deriving interpolation error bounds.

Definition 2
.

For a nonempty set
\mathcal{X}
, a symmetric function
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called a kernel.
The kernel
k
is positive definite if for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
and any
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{N}\in\mathcal{X}
, the matrix

\mathbf{K}:=\begin{bmatrix}k(\xi_{1},\xi_{1})&\cdots&k(\xi_{1},\xi_{N})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
k(\xi_{N},\xi_{1})&\cdots&k(\xi_{N},\xi_{N})\end{bmatrix}

(12)

is positive semidefinite.
The kernel
k
is strictly positive definite if
\mathbf{K}
is positive definite for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
and any pairwise distinct
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{N}\in\mathcal{X}
.

\square

Definition 3
.

For a positive definite kernel
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of real-valued functions
h:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
equipped with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
is said to be an RKHS with the reproducing kernel
k
if

k(\cdot,\xi)\in\mathcal{H}\ \,\mbox{and}\ \,h(\xi)=\langle h,k(\cdot,\xi)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

for all
\xi\in\mathcal{X}
and
h\in\mathcal{H}
.

\square

By the Moore-Aronszajn theorem
[
2
]
, there exists a one-to-one correspondence between RKHSs and positive definite kernels.
In this regard, we denote by
\mathcal{H}_{k}
the unique RKHS having a positive definite kernel
k
as the reproducing kernel.
Also, we denote the norm induced by the inner product of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
by
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}:=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
.

Remark 3
.

The abstract definition of RKHS given in Definition
3
is further explored.
For a positive definite kernel
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the function
k(\cdot,\xi)
is called the canonical feature map of
\xi\in\mathcal{X}
.
The RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{k}
can then be explicitly constructed as the closure of the set of finite linear combinations of canonical feature maps
[
25
, Section 2.3]
:

\mathcal{H}_{k}=\left\{h=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}k(\cdot,\xi_{i})\mid\ \alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots\in\mathbb{R},\ \xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots\in\mathcal{X},~\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})<\infty\right\}.

(13)

This construction shows that the elements of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
naturally inherit geometric properties of
k
, such as smoothness or periodicity.

\square

Let us fix
k:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as a strictly positive definite kernel for the remainder of this paper.
Given the dataset
\mathcal{D}
in (
11
), KI seeks the minimal norm interpolant in
\mathcal{H}_{k}
by solving the following optimization problem:

\hat{c}=\argmin_{\mathsf{c}\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}\left\|\mathsf{c}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}\ \ \mbox{s.t.}\ \ \mathsf{c}(\xi_{i})=u_{i}\ \ \forall i=1,\ldots,N.

(14)

Thanks to the celebrated representer theorem
[
48
, Theorem 16.1]
, the closed-form solution of the infinite-dimensional optimization problem (
14
) can be written as

\hat{c}(\xi)=\mathbf{k}^{\top}(\xi)\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{u},

(15)

where
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is the matrix in (
12
) constructed from
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{in}}
, and

\mathbf{k}:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
are defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}(\xi):=\begin{bmatrix}k(\xi_{1},\xi);\cdots;k(\xi_{N},\xi)\end{bmatrix},~~~~~\mathbf{u}:=\begin{bmatrix}u_{1};\cdots;u_{N}\end{bmatrix}.

By removing redundant data from the training dataset, we can readily ensure that the training inputs
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{N}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{in}}
are pairwise distinct.
Since the kernel
k
is strictly positive definite, this guarantees the invertibility of the matrix
\mathbf{K}
.

III-B

Error bounds

A notable feature of KI is that it provides an explicit upper bound on the error between the true inverse model
c
and the estimate
\hat{c}
defined by (
15
).
This feature will play a central role in establishing formal guarantees for the proposed controller.
In fact, other learning methods could be employed as long as they provide an explicit error bound as KI.

In order to derive a practical and reliable error bound, we impose a regularity condition on the complexity of the inverse model
c
.

Assumption 3
.

The inverse model
c
of system (
3
) belongs to
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, and
\|c\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq\Gamma
for some known constant
\Gamma\geq 0
.

\square

The RKHS norm quantifies the smoothness of a function with respect to the associated reproducing kernel; the larger the norm, the less smooth the function is
[
25
, Section 2.3]
.
Obtaining a reliable yet non-conservative upper bound on the RKHS norm of a target function remains an open problem
[
13
, Section 6.1]
.
Nevertheless, the RKHS norm has been a central tool in deriving error bounds for KI and Gaussian process regression
[
7
,
15
,
29
]
, and is often assumed to be available in related works due to its theoretical utility
[
14
,
34
,
22
]
.
In practice, an upper bound
\Gamma
can be obtained from data using a safe Bayesian optimization algorithm in
[
45
]
, or by scaling
\|\hat{c}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{u}
with a safety factor greater than
1
, since
\|\hat{c}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq\|c\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
always holds
[
48
, Section 16]
.

An intuition behind the derivation of an error bound for
\hat{c}
is that the estimate is expected to be more accurate at test points that are closer to a training point.
In this regard, we focus on the case in which the kernel
k
is isotropic and decreasing, which naturally provides stronger correlations to data points that are closer to each other.

Definition 4
.

A kernel
k:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}
is isotropic and decreasing if there exists a decreasing function
\bar{k}:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

k(\xi,\xi^{\prime})=\bar{k}(\|\xi-\xi^{\prime}\|)

(16)

for all
\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

\square

Considering a strictly positive definite, isotropic, and decreasing kernel is not restrictive, as it includes many commonly used kernels, such as squared-exponential, Laplacian, Matérn, and rational quadratic kernels.
The following lemma presents an error bound for

under this setting.

Lemma 1

(
[
24

)
.

Consider a strictly positive definite kernel

\square

Although tightening the upper bound function

IV
Inverse Learning-Based Control

In this section, we complete the design of controller (
9
) by presenting a data-driven framework that actively selects an appropriate reference point

from
\mathcal{D}
, based on the error bound of

stated in Lemma

We then establish a verifiable sufficient condition on the training dataset

under which the proposed controller guarantees (

).

We begin by introducing an abstract formulation of the proposed framework, followed by a detailed procedure for its explicit construction and implementation.
We then demonstrate how the proposed framework can be extended to NARX models with input delays.

IV-A

Abstract formulation

For a given
\delta>0
, let us define

\mathcal{S}_{\delta}:=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{2n-1}\mid\|\zeta_{n}\|\leq\delta\},

where
\zeta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the
n
-th component of the vector
\zeta

Since

is the
n

\zeta(t)

\zeta(t)\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}\quad\forall t\geq\kappa

for some
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}

Given the dataset
\mathcal{D}
, we define the

of an arbitrary set

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{-}(\mathcal{A}):=\left\{\zeta(t)\in\mathcal{Z}\mid\exists y_{i}^{+}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{to}}~\mbox{such that}~\eqref{eq:igp}~\mbox{with}~y_{\mathrm{r}}(t+1)=y_{i}^{+}~\mbox{guarantees}~\zeta(t+1)\in\mathcal{A}\right\}.

9

\zeta(t+1)\in\mathcal{A}

Building on these definitions, we recursively define a sequence of sets

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{0}\subset\mathcal{S}_{\delta},\quad\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{j+1}\subset\mathcal{R}^{-}(\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{j})\quad\forall j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.\end{split}

guarantees the output to be bounded by
\delta

\kappa

This observation is formalized in the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

If
\zeta(t)\in\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{\kappa}
for some

and
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}

which implies

\square

Note that if
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{0}\subset\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{1}

holds in addition to (

\zeta(t+\kappa+1)\in\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{0}
according to Lemma
2

Therefore, by applying the same argument repeatedly, we can constrain the output to remain bounded by
\delta
for an infinite time horizon, thus ensuring (
2
).
The following lemma captures this observation and establishes a sufficient condition under which controller (
9
) guarantees (
2
).

Lemma 3
.

If
\zeta(0)\in\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{\kappa}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{0}\subset\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{1}
holds for some
\delta>0
and
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
then controller (

) with appropriate reference points in

guarantees (

).

\square

Although selecting
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}^{0}=\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
satisfies the defining property given in (

This is because

only constrains the

Proof.

Let us define

\displaystyle\left\|u_{i}-\hat{c}([y_{i}^{+};\zeta(t)])\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|c([y_{i}^{+};\zeta_{i}])-\hat{c}([y_{i}^{+};\zeta(t)])\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|c([y_{i}^{+};\zeta_{i}])-c([y_{i}^{+};\zeta(t)])\right\|+\left\|c([y_{i}^{+};\zeta(t)])-\hat{c}([y_{i}^{+};\zeta(t)])\right\|

.

and reformulate Assumption

Assumption 4

For any
\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}
and

Assumption

Under this assumption, we define the inverse model of (

Proposition 5
.

Consider system (

in (

Having (

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\theta(tT_{s}+2T_{s})-2\theta(tT_{s}+T_{s})+\theta(tT_{s})}{T_{s}^{2}},\qquad\dot{\theta}(tT_{s})\approx\frac{\theta(tT_{s}+T_{s})-\theta(tT_{s})}{T_{s}}

VI
Conclusion

We have presented a data-driven output feedback controller for systems represented in NARX form, using input/output measurement data.
The controller is constructed by identifying an inverse model of the system via KI and by combining it with a data-driven reference selection framework, described in Section
IV
.
By leveraging the interpolation error bound of KI, we have established a verifiable sufficient condition on the dataset under which the proposed controller guarantees practical output regulation.
We have validated the practical utility of the proposed method through numerical simulations.
Future work will consider an explicit treatment of measurement noise arising from sensor degradation and extensions to vector-valued kernel methods to capture coupling effects across multiple inputs.
