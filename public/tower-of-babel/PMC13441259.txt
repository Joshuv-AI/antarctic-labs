Inflammatory Signaling and Emotional Symptoms in Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis beyond Thyroid Function Status
Authors: Simiao Yao, Xingyu Zhu, Xin’ai Li, Xinying Si, Qian Zhang, Zhiguo Ding, Junhui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatients with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT) frequently report persistent emotional and cognitive symptoms even when thyroid hormone levels are within the reference range. The mechanisms underlying these symptoms beyond thyroid function status remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to characterize the symptom profile of HT patients with thyroid hormone levels within the reference ranges and to investigate whether peripheral inflammatory signaling is associated with emotional symptoms independent of thyroid-related factors.MethodsClinical data were analyzed from 80 patients with HT whose free triiodothyronine and free thyroxine levels were within the reference ranges and who did not have suppressed thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH). Thyroid-related quality of life was assessed using the Thyroid Patient-Reported Outcome-39 (ThyPRO-39). Serum interleukin-6 (IL-6) and interleukin-17 (IL-17) levels were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Associations between symptom dimensions, thyroid-related factors, and inflammatory markers were evaluated using multivariable linear regression models with log-transformed cytokine levels, adjusting for age, sex, elevated TSH, current levothyroxine sodium use, and thyroid autoantibodies.ResultsSymptom profiling revealed that fatigue and emotional dimensions, including emotional susceptibility, anxiety, and depressivity, were the most prominently affected domains, whereas thyroid-related somatic and social functioning domains were relatively preserved. Neither elevated TSH, current levothyroxine sodium use, nor thyroid autoantibody levels showed significant independent associations with emotional symptom scores. In contrast, higher peripheral IL-6 levels were independently associated with increased emotional susceptibility, anxiety, and depressivity after adjustment for thyroid-related parameters. No consistent independent associations were observed between IL-17 levels and emotional symptom dimensions.ConclusionIn patients with HT whose thyroid hormone levels are within the reference ranges, emotional symptoms are common and cannot be fully explained by TSH status, levothyroxine sodium use, or thyroid autoantibody levels. Peripheral inflammatory signaling, particularly IL-6, may be associated with emotional symptom burden beyond classical thyroid-related mechanisms, highlighting a potential neuroimmune pathway underlying persistent symptoms in HT.

Introduction

Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT) is the most common autoimmune thyroid disease, characterized by chronic lymphocytic infiltration of thyroid tissue and the persistent presence of thyroid autoantibodies [1, 2]. Traditionally, symptoms associated with HT have been attributed primarily to abnormalities in thyroid hormone levels. However, an increasing number of clinical studies have shown that even when thyroid hormone levels are maintained within the reference range, a subset of patients with HT continue to experience persistent symptoms such as fatigue, anxiety, depression, emotional instability, and cognitive impairment [3, 4]. These symptoms are often difficult to explain using conventional thyroid function indices alone, suggesting that their underlying mechanisms may extend beyond the classical states of thyroid hormone deficiency or excess.

In recent years, growing attention has been directed toward the role of neuro-immune-endocrine interactions in autoimmune diseases. Inflammatory cytokines not only play key regulatory roles in peripheral immune responses but can also influence central nervous system function through multiple pathways, including modulation of blood-brain barrier permeability, activation of glial cells, and regulation of neurotransmitter metabolism [5–7]. In various chronic inflammatory conditions, peripheral inflammatory signaling has been implicated in the development of emotional disturbances and psychological symptoms [8, 9]. In the context of HT, neurological and neuropsychiatric manifestations have also been reported. At the severe end of this spectrum, HT-related encephalopathy has been described as an immune-mediated condition involving cognitive, behavioral, and neurological symptoms. Although this condition is distinct from the more common emotional symptoms evaluated in the present study, it supports the broader concept that thyroid autoimmunity may be associated with brain-related manifestations beyond thyroid hormone abnormalities.

Interleukin-6 (IL-6) and interleukin-17 (IL-17) are among the most extensively studied inflammatory cytokines in autoimmune thyroid diseases [10, 11]. As a pleiotropic cytokine, IL-6 is involved in B-cell differentiation, autoantibody production, and neuroimmune regulation. Experimental and clinical studies have linked IL-6 to neuroinflammation, sickness behavior, and depressive or anxiety-related states [12, 13]. IL-17, a signature effector cytokine of Th17 cells, plays a critical role in the immunopathology of HT and is considered a key mediator in the maintenance of local and systemic inflammatory responses [14]. However, evidence regarding whether these 2 cytokines show differential associations with emotional and psychological symptom burden in patients with HT whose thyroid hormone levels are within the reference ranges remains insufficient.

Therefore, this study focused on patients with HT whose thyroid hormone levels were within the reference range. We systematically characterized the distribution of ThyPRO-39 symptom domains and further examined the associations of thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), thyroid autoantibody levels, current levothyroxine sodium use, and peripheral inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and IL-17) with emotional and psychological symptoms.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This study was designed as a cross-sectional clinical investigation. Participants were consecutively recruited from the outpatient and inpatient departments of Beijing University of Chinese Medicine Sun Simiao Hospital and were clinically diagnosed with HT.

Inclusion Criteria

Participants were required to meet all of the following criteria:

Clinical Diagnosis of HT. The diagnosis of HT was established according to contemporary diagnostic descriptions of autoimmune thyroiditis [15, 16]. The diagnostic criteria primarily included thyroid autoantibody positivity, defined as thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb) and/or thyroglobulin antibody (TGAb) above the upper limit of the reference range, together with compatible thyroid ultrasonographic findings, such as diffuse hypoechogenicity, heterogeneous echotexture, or thyroid enlargement.

Age Range. Participants aged between 15 and 80 years were eligible for inclusion.

Thyroid Function Status. Serum free tri-iodothyronine (FT3) and free thyroxine (FT4) levels were required to be within the reference ranges. Patients with suppressed TSH below the lower limit of the reference range were excluded to reduce potential confounding by hyperthyroid or subclinical hyperthyroid status. Patients with elevated TSH were retained in the main analysis, and TSH status was further considered in statistical analyses.

Thyroid Hormone Therapy. Patients with previous or current thyroid hormone replacement therapy were eligible for inclusion, including those receiving levothyroxine sodium preparations, provided that FT3 and FT4 levels were within the reference ranges at the time of enrollment. Current levothyroxine sodium use was recorded and included in the statistical models as a potential confounding factor.

Medication Use. Participants had not used medications known to significantly affect thyroid hormone synthesis, metabolism, or regulation prior to enrollment, including but not limited to corticosteroids, vitamin- or trace element-containing preparations, amiodarone, interferon-α, lithium preparations, antidepressants, or antipsychotic drugs.

Study Procedures and Ethical Requirements. All participants completed thyroid function testing, thyroid autoantibody assessment, and the ThyPRO-39 questionnaire at the study center. Participation was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Exclusion Criteria

Participants were excluded if they had other clearly diagnosed autoimmune diseases, severe infectious diseases, or malignant tumors; a history of diagnosed psychiatric disorders or ongoing psychiatric medication use; pregnancy or lactation; or incomplete clinical or laboratory data that precluded study evaluation. A total of 88 patients with HT completed the initial assessments. After excluding patients with suppressed TSH and those with FT3 or FT4 outside the reference ranges, 80 patients with HT were ultimately included in the main analysis.

Clinical Data and Laboratory Measurements

Collection of Clinical Data

Clinical information was collected for all participants, including age, sex, smoking status, alcohol consumption, history of previous diseases, recent thyroid function results, and current use of thyroid hormone replacement therapy. Medication history related to drugs that may affect thyroid hormone metabolism or neuropsychiatric status was also reviewed.

Thyroid Function and Thyroid Autoantibody Assessment

Laboratory measurements included TSH, FT4, FT3, TPOAb, and TGAb. All laboratory analyses were performed in the hospital’s clinical laboratory using a cobas e 411 electrochemiluminescence immunoassay analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Germany) with manufacturer-provided reagents. All measurements were conducted in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols by qualified laboratory personnel.

Measurement of Peripheral Inflammatory Cytokines

Peripheral blood inflammatory cytokine levels were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, including IL-6 and IL-17. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits were purchased from Shanghai Mlbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China), and absorbance was measured using a SpectraMax iD3 Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices, USA). Standard curves and quality control samples were included in each assay. All samples were processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions and analyzed by the same investigator to minimize inter-assay and operator-related variability.

Symptom and Quality of Life Assessment

Thyroid-related symptoms and quality of life were assessed using the Thyroid Patient-Reported Outcome-39 questionnaire (ThyPRO-39) [17]. The questionnaire was administered with guidance and assistance from trained physicians with clinical qualifications during outpatient visits or hospitalization to ensure that participants fully understood each item and filled the questionnaire independently.

All questionnaires were completed during the same clinical visit to minimize recall bias and repeated measurements. Scoring was performed in accordance with the published ThyPRO-39 scoring algorithm. For each domain, item scores were first summed to obtain the raw domain score. The raw summed score was then converted to a final 0–100 rescaled score according to the published ThyPRO-39 summed score conversion table. Higher scores indicate greater symptom burden.

The ThyPRO-39 covers multiple thyroid-related symptom and quality-of-life domains, including somatic, emotional, cognitive, and functional dimensions. All domains were included in descriptive symptom profiling. Based on the study objective, subsequent association analyses focused mainly on tiredness, cognitive complaints, anxiety, depressivity, and emotional susceptibility, which reflect fatigue, cognitive, and emotional symptom burden.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using two-sided tests, with a significance level set at p <0.05. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), depending on data distribution, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Because IL-6 and IL-17 exhibited skewed distributions, logarithmic transformation was applied prior to model fitting. Multivariable linear regression models using ordinary least squares were employed to evaluate the independent associations between peripheral inflammatory cytokines and symptom burden, with ThyPRO-39 standardized domain scores as dependent variables. The models included log-transformed IL-6 and log-transformed IL-17 as inflammatory predictors and were adjusted for age, sex, elevated TSH, current levothyroxine sodium use, TPOAb, and TGAb. Regression results were reported as unstandardized regression coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals, and p values.

To further assess the potential influence of thyroid-related factors, ThyPRO-39 domain scores were compared according to TSH status and current levothyroxine sodium use. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test, and p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate method.

A sensitivity analysis was performed in patients whose TSH, FT3, and FT4 levels were all within the reference ranges and who were not receiving current levothyroxine sodium therapy. In this analysis, multivariable linear regression models were adjusted for age, sex, TPOAb, TGAb, and log-transformed IL-17.

Model robustness was assessed through diagnostic evaluation of residual distributions, homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity. Robust standard errors were applied when appropriate.

All statistical analyses were performed in a Python environment, with data management and variable recoding conducted using the pandas package and multivariable regression modeling and diagnostics performed using the statsmodels package.

Results

Baseline Characteristics and Symptom Distribution

A total of 88 patients with HT completed assessments of thyroid function, thyroid autoantibodies, inflammatory cytokines, and ThyPRO-39 scores. To reduce potential confounding by overt thyroid dysfunction or suppressed TSH, patients with TSH below the lower reference limit or FT3/FT4 outside the reference ranges were excluded. Finally, 80 patients with HT whose thyroid hormone levels were within the reference range and who did not have suppressed TSH were included in the main analysis.

At enrollment, all included participants had FT3 and FT4 levels within the reference ranges. TSH levels were either within the reference range or elevated. Twenty patients were receiving current levothyroxine sodium therapy. All patients exhibited thyroid autoantibody levels above the upper limit of the reference range. Associations of Thyroid-Related Factors with Symptom Domains

Associations between thyroid-related factors and ThyPRO-39 symptom domains were further examined. First, participants were categorized according to TSH status into a TSH within-reference-range group and an elevated TSH group. Descriptive analyses showed that the overall ThyPRO-39 symptom profiles were broadly similar between the 2 groups (Fig. 2a). Detailed domain scores are provided in online supplementary Table S1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553087). No statistically significant differences were observed across the 12 ThyPRO-39 domains after correction for multiple comparisons.

We further compared ThyPRO-39 domain scores between patients with and without current levothyroxine sodium therapy. The overall symptom profiles were also similar between the 2 groups (Fig. 2b), and detailed results are shown in online supplementary Table S2. Although patients receiving levothyroxine sodium showed slightly higher mean scores for tiredness, anxiety, depressivity, and emotional susceptibility, these differences did not reach statistical significance after correction for multiple comparisons.

In multivariable linear regression models, anxiety, depressivity, emotional susceptibility, tiredness, and cognitive complaints were analyzed as outcome variables. Elevated TSH, current levothyroxine sodium use, TPOAb, and TGAb were entered as thyroid-related predictors, with age and sex included as covariates. None of these thyroid-related parameters showed significant independent associations with the above symptom domains. These results suggest that, in this cohort of patients with HT and thyroid hormone levels within the reference range, elevated TSH, current levothyroxine sodium therapy, and thyroid autoantibody titers were insufficient to explain emotional and psychological symptom burden.

Associations between Peripheral Inflammatory Cytokines and ThyPRO-39 Symptom Domains

Associations between IL-6 and Symptom Domains

Peripheral inflammatory cytokines were log-transformed prior to analysis. Spearman correlation analysis revealed that log-transformed IL-6 levels showed relatively strong correlations with emotional symptom domains, particularly emotional susceptibility, anxiety, and depressivity. In multivariable linear regression analyses, each ThyPRO-39 domain was entered as a dependent variable, with log-transformed IL-6 included as the primary predictor. Age, sex, elevated TSH, current levothyroxine sodium use, TPOAb, and TGAb were included as covariates. Higher IL-6 levels remained independently and positively associated with emotional susceptibility, anxiety, and depressivity scores (all p < 0.05). No stable independent associations were observed between IL-6 levels and tiredness, cognitive complaints, or other somatic or social functioning domains (Table 3). These findings indicate that elevated peripheral IL-6 levels are primarily associated with emotional regulation-related symptom burden rather than exerting a broad influence across all thyroid-related or quality-of-life domains (Fig. 3).

Associations between IL-17 and Symptom Domains

In contrast, log-transformed IL-17 levels demonstrated only weak correlation trends with a limited number of symptom domains in correlation analyses. In multivariable regression models, IL-17 levels did not show significant independent associations with any ThyPRO-39 domains (Table 3). These results suggest that, in patients with HT included in the main analysis, IL-17 levels were not clearly associated with emotional or other symptom domains after adjustment for thyroid-related and demographic factors.

Sensitivity Analyses

To assess the robustness of the primary findings, a sensitivity analysis was performed in patients whose TSH, FT3, and FT4 levels were all within the reference ranges and who were not receiving current levothyroxine sodium therapy (n = 30). In this strictly defined subgroup, log-transformed IL-6 remained independently and positively associated with emotional susceptibility, anxiety, and depressivity, whereas associations with tiredness and cognitive complaints were not statistically significant (online suppl. Table S3). These findings indicate that the observed associations between IL-6 and emotional symptom burden were not primarily driven by elevated TSH or current levothyroxine sodium therapy, further supporting the reliability of the main results.

Discussion

Symptom Profile in HT Patients with Thyroid Hormone Levels within the Reference Range

In this cross-sectional study, we systematically characterized symptom burden in patients with HT who maintained thyroid hormone levels within the reference range. The results demonstrate a heterogeneous symptom profile across ThyPRO-39 dimensions. Fatigue and emotional symptoms, including emotional susceptibility, anxiety, and depressivity, were the most prominent, whereas classical thyroid-related somatic symptoms, appearance concerns, and impairments in social or daily functioning were relatively mild. These findings indicate that patients with HT may experience substantial symptom burden even in the absence of overt thyroid hormone abnormalities. Importantly, the distribution of symptoms suggests that emotional and psychological domains represent a major component of patient-reported outcomes, rather than being secondary to overt thyroid dysfunction.

Limited Explanatory Role of Thyroid Function, Autoantibodies, and Levothyroxine Sodium Use

Traditional clinical paradigms emphasize thyroid hormone abnormalities as the primary drivers of symptoms in HT [18, 19]. However, in the present cohort, elevated TSH, current levothyroxine sodium use, and thyroid autoantibody titers did not show significant independent associations with emotional or psychological symptom dimensions after adjustment for demographic factors.

These results suggest that conventional thyroid-related biochemical markers may have limited explanatory value for persistent emotional symptoms in patients with HT whose thyroid hormone levels are within the reference range. This observation aligns with previous reports indicating that normalization of thyroid hormone levels does not necessarily lead to symptom resolution in a subset of patients [20–22]. Together, these findings support the notion that mechanisms beyond classical endocrine dysregulation may contribute to ongoing symptom burden.

Association between Peripheral IL-6 and Emotional Symptoms

A key finding of this study is the independent association between peripheral IL-6 levels and several emotional symptom dimensions, including emotional susceptibility, anxiety, and depressivity. This relationship remained significant after adjustment for thyroid-related factors, including elevated TSH, current levothyroxine sodium use, and thyroid autoantibody levels. In addition, sensitivity analysis in patients with normal TSH, FT3, and FT4 and without current levothyroxine sodium therapy showed consistent results. These findings suggest that IL-6 may reflect a distinct biological process related to emotional symptom expression.

IL-6 is a pleiotropic cytokine involved in both immune regulation and neurobiological processes. Experimental and clinical studies have shown that IL-6 can influence central nervous system function through multiple pathways, such as modulation of blood-brain barrier permeability, activation of glial cells, and regulation of neurotransmitter systems [23, 24]. Within this framework, elevated peripheral IL-6 may contribute to emotional symptoms through neuroimmune signaling rather than through direct effects on thyroid hormone metabolism.

Notably, the association observed in this study was specific to emotional and psychological domains and was not consistently observed across somatic or functional dimensions of the ThyPRO-39. This pattern suggests that inflammatory signaling may selectively affect emotional regulation, rather than causing a generalized decline in quality of life.

Absence of Independent Associations with IL-17

In contrast to IL-6, IL-17 did not show significant independent associations with any ThyPRO-39 symptom dimension in the adjusted models. IL-17 is widely recognized as a key effector cytokine in the immunopathogenesis of HT, particularly in relation to local thyroid inflammation and tissue injury [11, 25, 26].

The lack of association between IL-17 and emotional symptoms may indicate that IL-17 predominantly reflects localized autoimmune activity rather than systemic inflammatory signaling relevant to neuropsychological outcomes. In this context, IL-6 may better capture systemic neuroimmune communication, whereas IL-17 may be more closely related to thyroid-specific immune activation. This distinction highlights potential functional differences among inflammatory mediators in HT and suggests that not all immune pathways contribute equally to symptom expression beyond the thyroid gland.

Clinical Implications and Future Directions

The findings of this study support the concept that emotional and psychological symptoms in HT patients with thyroid hormone levels within the reference range are not solely determined by thyroid hormone status, elevated TSH, levothyroxine sodium use, or thyroid autoantibody levels. Instead, peripheral inflammatory signals, particularly IL-6, may play a role in shaping symptom burden through mechanisms that extend beyond classical endocrine pathways.

From a clinical perspective, these results may help explain why some patients continue to report emotional distress despite the absence of overt thyroid hormone abnormalities or biochemically adequate thyroid hormone replacement. This interpretation is also supported by neurophysiological and neuroimaging studies in HT. Previous MRS studies have reported metabolic alterations within normal-appearing brain tissue in patients with HT, and ERP combined with MRS has shown evidence of brain dysfunction in patients with compensated HT, with potential relevance to cognitive function [27, 28]. These findings suggest that normal thyroid hormone levels may not fully reflect brain-related homeostasis in HT.

From a research standpoint, the observed association provides a rationale for future mechanistic studies using experimental models to investigate how peripheral inflammatory mediators influence emotional behavior. Longitudinal studies are also needed to determine whether low-grade inflammation precedes emotional symptom development or whether emotional stress may contribute to inflammatory activation. In addition, future studies may explore whether interventions targeting inflammatory pathways could improve symptom burden in selected patients with HT.

Study Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between inflammatory markers and emotional symptoms. Second, the sample size was relatively limited, particularly in sensitivity analyses, which may reduce the ability to detect weaker associations. Third, although elevated TSH and current levothyroxine sodium use were considered in the analyses, detailed information regarding duration, dosage, and longitudinal changes in thyroid hormone replacement therapy was not available.

Fourth, the inflammatory profile assessed in this study was limited to IL-6 and IL-17. Other relevant immune mediators were not evaluated. In addition, this study measured thyroid autoantibodies but did not assess neuronal surface antibodies, such as NMDAR, GABAAR, Caspr2, LGI1, or AMPAR1/2 antibodies, which may be relevant to neuropsychiatric manifestations in selected patients. Finally, although ThyPRO-39 provides a validated patient-reported assessment of thyroid-related symptoms and quality of life, it does not replace structured psychiatric evaluation.

Future studies with longitudinal designs, larger cohorts, broader inflammatory panels, and more detailed neuroimmune assessments are needed to further clarify the temporal and mechanistic relationships between immune activation and symptom persistence in HT.
