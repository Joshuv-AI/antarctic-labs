A Two-Phase Stability Study of LLM Judges and Bar Council Examiners on Thai Bar-Exam Free-Form Essays
Pawitsapak Akarajaradwong, Wuttikrai Lertprasertphakorn, Chompakorn Chaksangchaichot, Sarana Nutanong

Abstract
Free-form legal essay evaluation in NLP treats expert inter-rater stability as a single ceiling number, and treats LLM-judge agreement with that ceiling as evidence of judge stability. We test both assumptions on the Thai bar examination through an identical-inputs protocol: three Bar Council-trained examiners (A, B, C) and a 26-LLM judge panel score the same 15 cross-graded answers from the same four inputs (question, official Bar Council grading regulation, gold answer, candidate answer). The headline finding is asymmetric. On 10 of 15 cells where the rubric prescribes both axes, all 29 raters converge in a tight band: panel agreement is universal. On the remaining 5 cells where the rubric does not prescribe how to grade a correct final answer that omits a decisive statutory citation, the human panel splits between two coherent readings (B/C majority at the upper rubric band, score 6 6 – 8 8 ; A minority at the lower band, score 1 1 – 2 2 ). The LLM judge population does not split symmetrically: 22 of 26 LLMs score in or near B/C’s contested band, 3 sit in the regulation-silent middle gap, and only 1 (GPT-5.4 Nano) approaches A’s band without consistently scoring within it. Zero LLMs in our 26-judge panel reproduce the minority human reading on the contested cells. The B/C-direction cluster spans every model size, vendor, and price tier we tested. An instrumented three-LLM anchor sub-panel (Claude 4.6 Opus, Gemini 3.1 Pro, GPT-5.4 Pro) carries determinism probes, input ablations, and bootstrap CIs, and reaches anchor panel α = 0.77 \alpha=0.77 on the 15 cells against human-panel α = 0.36 \alpha=0.36 . The high LLM-panel α \alpha reflects systematic convergence on the majority reading rather than balanced reproduction of both readings; a benchmark that selects its LLM judge by maximising agreement with a human reference panel will inherit this asymmetry by construction.

Free-form essay rubrics in interpretation-intensive domains combine a deterministic correctness axis (grounded by the gold answer) with an interpretive quality axis (judged without one). Expert panels are stability-equivalent to LLM panels on items where both axes are rubric-determined. We call these regulation-silent items the rubric’s
interpretive bottleneck

2
Background: The Thai Bar Examination

The Thai bar examination is the national qualifying assessment for legal practice in Thailand and a prerequisite for appointment to the judicial bench. It is administered twice a year by the Institute of Legal Education of the Thai Bar Council under the Ministry of Justice. A candidate sits four examination sessions, one per substantive doctrinal area (Civil and Commercial Code; Criminal Code; Civil Procedure Code; Criminal Procedure Code); each session presents ten free-form long-form questions, each scored 0–10 by a Bar Council-certified examiner, and a session pass requires roughly half marks. The bar is the entry point to a regulated profession; questions are constructed by sitting examiners, often current or former judges and senior practitioners, and graded by examiner panels selected from the same pool. The grading instrument is a published regulation that ranks free-form essay answers on an 8-tier ordinal scale.

This section makes three points the rest of the paper depends on. First, the bar exam is a free-form long-form essay assessment, not a multiple-choice instrument; the entire panel-agreement question we study below is meaningless on closed-form items. Second, the grading regulation has a specific structural shape (two crossed axes with overlapping tier-to-score ranges) that admits one regulation-silent boundary where competing legitimate interpretations are possible; that boundary is where the empirical stability fault line lands. Third, examiners do not undergo per-session calibration before grading; they apply the regulation under their own training and prior bar-grading experience, which is the realistic measurement setting any legal-NLP benchmark inherits when it cites a single credentialed expert as ground truth.

2.2
The deterministic and interpretive axes

We label the two axes
deterministic correctness
(final-answer-matches-gold against the official gold answer issued by the examiner committee) and
interpretive quality
(the reasoning-quality scale, judged without a gold target). The deterministic axis is grounded by an external artefact: the gold answer fixes which side of the matches-or-differs split the candidate sits on. The interpretive axis is grounded by examiner judgement under the same regulation; the regulation gives the tier names and score ranges, but does not specify how to map a particular fact-by-fact reasoning chain into “unusable / some / acceptable / good / very good” on the correct-answer side.

For most fact patterns, the interpretive axis is rubric-determined in practice: the gold answer names the decisive Code sections, and examiners agree that an answer omitting a decisive section sits in the lower tiers and an answer naming all decisive sections sits in the upper tiers. For a smaller set of fact patterns, the interpretive axis is regulation-silent: a candidate reaches the correct final conclusion via a reasoning chain that names some but not all of the gold answer’s decisive sections, and the regulation does not specify whether the omission is a T4 (correct, unusable) or T7 (correct, good) phenomenon. Section
6
shows that the human stability ceiling fractures specifically on this regulation-silent boundary, and Section
7
traces how the LLM judge population responds on the same boundary. The structural framing is that free-form essay rubrics combine a deterministic axis and an interpretive axis, and panel agreement degrades where the interpretive axis is regulation-silent.

2.3
Examiner qualification and contamination control

The three examiners (Judge A, Judge B, Judge C) are practising Thai legal professionals certified to grade at the Bar Council level. Each is a sitting Bar Council-trained examiner with prior bar-grading experience under the same regulation. The three examiners authored the three Q1–Q3 questions and the corresponding gold answers
before
any participant answer was collected. The instrument is therefore not an item that any LLM could have encountered online before the 2026 session, which rules out training-data contamination on the questions and gold answers by construction. The Code itself and the grading regulation are public documents; we address residual contamination risk on doctrinal patterns in Section
12
and via a gold-withheld input ablation in Section
11
.

Bar Council scheduling and examiner availability impose constraints we accept and document rather than fight. Each examiner primary-graded all 50 answers for one question (A on Q1, B on Q2, C on Q3); the cross-grading subset (Section
6
) is fully covered by all three examiners on each of its 15 cells. We did not run a pre-calibration round between the three examiners on a pilot subset before the live grading. Pre-calibrating examiners on each other would erase the interpretive variance that is the paper’s main finding, because the realistic measurement setting any legal-NLP benchmark inherits when it cites a single credentialed expert as ground truth is the pre-calibration setting, not the post-calibration one.

2.4
Doctrinal scope and the three questions

The three questions are drawn from the Civil and Commercial Code session and span three doctrinal clusters that bar candidates routinely encounter: ordinary-partnership liability (Q1, CCC Sections 1012, 1025, 1033, 1050); company director authority and good-faith third parties (Q2, CCC Sections 1022, 1023,
1023/1
); and shareholder annulment of a procedurally irregular general-meeting resolution (Q3, CCC Sections 1178, 1193, 1195). Each question is worth 10 points and a conventional pass on a three-question session is 15. Q1 turns on distinguishing the partner-internal management rule (Section 1033) from the partner-external liability rule (Section 1050); Q2 turns on the good-faith exception under Section
1023/1
to the general no-bind rule of Section 1023; Q3 turns on the one-month limitation on annulment of unlawful resolutions under Section 1195 versus the unbounded standing to challenge fabricated minutes. Appendix
A

The regulation-silent boundary that drives the Phase 2 stability fault line lands on Q2. The three contested Q2 cells are those where the candidate reaches the correct final answer on sub-question (a) but argues from Section 1023 alone, without naming Section
1023/1

Expert-panel consistency on subjective decisions.

Large-scale reviewer- and grader-consistency studies repeatedly document that expert verdicts on subjective tasks carry substantial panel-identity variance. The 2014 NeurIPS experiment routed 166 papers to two independent program committees and found that roughly half of the papers accepted by one were rejected by the other; the 2021 replication reproduces the same disagreement on a five-times-larger submission pool, with
5

(
22

and the automated-essay-scoring literature
(
11

report similar patterns on high-stakes essay grading: trained rater panels do not converge to a single true score, and agreement is raised through training and adjudication rather than existing a priori. The structural mechanism we identify (panel agreement holds where the rubric is specified, diverges where it is silent) is consistent with these findings.

Inter-rater stability and the value of disagreement.

Krippendorff’s
\alpha

(
13
)
and Cohen’s weighted
\kappa
are the standard panel-level inter-rater stability metrics for ordinal scoring, with
3
the canonical computational-linguistics framing. Recent NLP annotation scholarship
(
21
)
argues that legitimate disagreement among qualified annotators on subjective tasks should be modelled as informative signal rather than treated as noise to average out. Our two-cluster diagnosis on the regulation-silent boundary sits in this tradition: the three certified examiners disagree not because any is mis-calibrated but because the regulation leaves interpretive latitude at one specific tier boundary (T4 vs. T7, “unusable” vs. “good” reasoning when a decisive statutory citation is omitted on a correct final answer). What we add is the identical-inputs LLM-judge counterpart on the same items, a 29-rater cluster analysis that recovers the same fault line at population level without using vendor or size labels, and a per-question decomposition that localises the disagreement to a specific rubric zone rather than treating the panel as uniformly subjective.

4
Study Design

The study was run as a bar-qualification-style mock examination in early 2026. It is a two-phase design under identical inputs at each phase: Phase 1 puts LLMs in the examinee seat under blinded examiner grading; Phase 2 puts LLMs in the judge seat reading the same essays from the same four inputs every other rater receives. The two phases share a corpus by construction: the same 150 answers are produced in Phase 1 and graded in Phase 2. The full 15-cell cross-graded score matrix used in the Phase-2 stability analysis appears as Table

in Section

4.1
Corpus and instrument

Fifty participants were enrolled: 42 Thai law students and legal practitioners preparing for the bar examination under informed consent, and 8 LLMs (Claude 4.6 Opus, Gemini 3.1 Pro, GPT-5.4 Pro, DeepSeek v3.2 Speciale, Qwen 3.5 Plus, GLM-5, Minimax M2.5, Grok 4.1 Fast) inserted into the participant roster as sequence identifiers indistinguishable from the human slots. Each participant produced one answer per question on the three Civil and Commercial Code questions of Section
2.4

6

The grading instrument is the Bar Council regulation summarised in Table

The three examiners authored the questions and the gold answers before any participant answer was collected; the instrument is therefore not on the open web at the time of the 2026 session, which removes question and gold-answer contamination on the LLM side by construction. The 2014 grading regulation and the Civil and Commercial Code itself are public.

4.2
Phase 1: examinee sitting and primary grading

Human participants sat the session proctored, with a three-hour budget, no electronic aids, writing longhand on the standard Bar Council answer booklet. Each LLM was queried once per question through its provider API at temperature
0
with a zero-shot system prompt instructing it to produce flowing Thai legal prose in the register of a handwritten student answer, with no retrieval, no tools, and no in-context examples. The LLM-system-prompt language is reproduced verbatim in Appendix
C

The three examiners were not informed that any participant was an LLM; the blinding was full, not partial.

Each examiner primary-graded one full question across all 50 participants under the regulation in Table

4.3
Phase 2: cross-grading and LLM-judge scoring

For each of the 15 cross-graded cells, the two examiners who had not primary-graded that question independently scored the answer given the question text, the official gold answer, and the grading regulation. The two cross-graders never saw the primary grader’s score, the other cross-grader’s score, or the participant’s identity beyond the sequence ID.

On the same 15 cells, an LLM-judge panel grades from identical four-tuple inputs (question text, regulation, gold answer, participant answer). The three-LLM anchor sub-panel comprises Claude 4.6 Opus, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and GPT-5.4 Pro; the extended 26-LLM panel in Section
7
adds 23 further models spanning closed frontier large and small, flagship open-weight, and small open-weight, sampled through OpenRouter at temperature
0
. Each LLM judge receives, in a single forward pass at temperature
0
, the question text, the grading regulation reproduced verbatim in the system prompt, the official gold answer, and the participant answer. The judge returns a strict JSON object with a tier number (
1
–
8
), a half-integer score within that tier’s range, and a Thai-language justification (Appendix
C
). No chain-of-thought scaffolding, no tools, no retrieval, and no few-shot examples. The judge prompt is identical across all
29
LLM judges. Eight of the
26
extended-panel judges are the same model that sat the exam in Phase 1; the dual-role analysis in Section
9
exploits this overlap.

Human cross-graders and LLM judges thus received the same four inputs and had no access to the primary grader’s score, to each other’s scores, to per-judge aggregates, or to the participant’s identity. The stability comparison in the rest of the paper is therefore a comparison of scoring functions on identical information.

We deliberately did not pre-calibrate the three examiners against each other on a pilot subset before grading. The stability ceiling we measure is the one any legal-NLP benchmark inherits when it cites a single credentialed expert as ground truth without prior cross-rater calibration; pre-tuning would erase the interpretive variance that is the paper’s main finding.

5.2
Per-question pattern

The aggregate near-parity masks a clean per-question structure. Q1 is the hardest item for the LLM cohort: the decisive move is to distinguish partner-internal management (CCC Sections 1025, 1033) from third-party-external liability (Sections 1012, 1050) and to apply the latter to the fact pattern. Models that cite only one side of the internal/external distinction lose most of their credit. Three of eight LLMs score zero or one on Q1; only Gemini 3.1 Pro reaches the upper band (
9

6

7
Asymmetric LLM Convergence on the Contested Boundary

The anchor sub-panel of Section
6
establishes that a small instrumented LLM-judge panel agrees more than the human panel, and that the gap is localised to one regulation-silent rubric boundary. Two questions remain at the population level. First, is the LLM-panel-above-human-panel direction a property of just those three anchor LLMs, or does it replicate across a wider LLM judge population? Second, does the wider LLM population split along the same fault line as the human panel, reproducing both the B/C-majority reading and the A-minority reading, or does it converge on one reading and miss the other? This section addresses both by extending the judge panel to 26 LLMs sampled across closed-frontier large and small, flagship open-weight, and small open-weight tiers, each scoring the same 15 cells from the same four inputs.

The headline empirical finding is asymmetric. The LLM-population-above-human-panel direction replicates: 22 of 26 LLMs (85%) score in or near the B/C reading band on the contested cells; 3 LLMs (12%) sit in the regulation-silent middle gap; only 1 LLM (GPT-5.4 Nano,
m_{c}=2.33
) approaches the A reading band, and even Nano does not consistently score within A’s contested-cell band of
[1,2]
.
No LLM in the 26-judge panel reproduces A’s reading of the regulation-silent boundary.
This is the structural finding of the section. We treat it as a population-level pattern of the LLM judge ensemble on this rubric, not as a single-model failure mode.

7.4
Capability rank against direction

Cluster membership does not track size, vendor, or price. The B/C-direction cluster covers the full range of the panel; the single A-direction model (Nano) is a closed-frontier-small OpenAI model that fails on multiple other axes (Section

9.4
Implication for evaluator selection

The dual-role result has a consequence for any benchmark that selects an LLM judge by single-pass capability rank or by agreement-with-human-panel maximisation. First, scaling capability does not move judges toward the minority reading: the strongest Phase-1 examinees become moderate-strength B/C-direction judges; none becomes a A-direction judge. Second, the panel mean against which agreement is maximised is B/C-skewed by construction (B and C are 2 of 3 humans), so a judge that takes A’s reading would have lower agreement with that mean and would be filtered out. The cluster composition of a selected LLM panel is therefore an empirical property of the candidate pool and the selection criterion together; further scaling alone does not change it.

The structural claim (panel agreement holds where the rubric prescribes both axes, degrades where the interpretive axis is regulation-silent) is panel-independent, and the specific

Limitations

Small samples throughout.

The core comparison uses 15 cross-graded cells with 6 raters per cell; the dual-role overlap (Section

n=8
LLM examinees; the human panel is 3 examiners. Specific

Single-jurisdiction, single-rubric scope.

Three essay questions from one doctrinal cluster (Thai partnership and corporate law) under one Bar Council rubric. Whether other legal regulations have analogous regulation-silent zones, and whether other free-form expert-graded NLP tasks (medical case reasoning, scientific writing review, code-review essays) exhibit similar asymmetric LLM convergence, is an open empirical question. The structural mechanism (panels agree where the rubric is specified, diverge where it is silent) is not Thai-specific, but the numeric gap will differ on English or common-law essays.

Within-cell panel SD is

Our LLM judges are temperature-

Future work.

Four near-term research directions follow. (i) Develop rubric-audit annotation protocols that identify interpretive cases in advance, before LLM-judge selection, so a benchmark can report panel stability per interpretive case and per LLM direction. (ii) Evaluate LLM judges against multiple legitimate human readings on interpretive cases instead of a single averaged target; report which readings the LLM panel covers and which it misses. (iii) Replicate the deterministic/interpretive decomposition and the asymmetric convergence on other free-form expert-graded NLP tasks. (iv) Test whether supervised fine-tuning, retrieval-augmented prompting, or in-context minority-reading exemplars reduce the asymmetric convergence on rubric-silent items.

Ethics Statement

All 42 human participants consented and were compensated at a fair-market rate. The three examiners consented to share their scores and grading comments under the Thai Bar Council’s customary grading-fee structure and were paid the Council’s standard examiner rate. Language-model queries were made under each provider’s terms of service. Participant labels in this paper are anonymous sequential identifiers (Items C1–C15 for cross-graded cells) not recoverable from the source data. Any findings and analyses are intended for research on legal-reasoning evaluation methodology and should not be used for individual assessment or certification decisions.

13
Conclusion

We ran a two-phase identical-inputs stability study on the Thai bar examination. Phase 1 sat eight LLMs alongside 42 Thai law students through three Civil and Commercial Code essay questions under blinded examiner grading. Phase 2 had three Bar Council-trained examiners and a 26-LLM judge panel grade the same 15 cross-graded answers from the same four inputs. The structural finding is asymmetric: on items where both rubric axes are prescribed, panel agreement is universal across all 29 raters at
\alpha=0.75
; on items where the interpretive axis is regulation-silent, the human panel splits between two coherent readings, but the LLM judge population overwhelmingly converges on the majority human reading. Twenty-two of
26
LLMs land in or near B/C’s reading band on the contested cells; only one LLM (GPT-5.4 Nano) approaches the minority (A) reading without consistently scoring within it.
Zero LLMs in our 26-judge panel reproduce A’s reading.
The asymmetry holds across model size, vendor, and price, and is not alleviated by capability: the dual-role overlap (Section
9
) shows that scaling Phase-1 examinee strength does not move Phase-2 judges toward the minority reading.

Two observations follow. First, the variance in expert agreement is not flat across items: it concentrates on the regulation-silent boundary where the rubric leaves the reasoning-quality axis to expert judgement. We call this boundary the rubric’s
interpretive bottleneck
. Second, on bottleneck items both human readings are internally consistent with a different parsing of the regulation (T7 if the missing decisive citation is a within-tier fact, T4 if it is a tier-deciding fact), but the LLM judge population commits to the B/C parsing. A benchmark that selects an LLM judge by maximising agreement with a human reference panel will inherit the B/C parsing by construction; whether a different selection criterion can recover the minority parsing is an open empirical question.

Full Question Details and Gold Answers

The three essay questions were drawn from the Thai Civil and Commercial Code (CCC); each carried 10 points. Below we provide a faithful English translation of (i) each question’s fact pattern and call and (ii) the corresponding gold answer issued by the examiners as the official model answer.

A.1
Q1: Ordinary Partnership Liability

Decisive provisions: CCC Sections 1012, 1025, 1033, 1050.

Question.

The Three Friends Grilled Chicken Partnership (
hang-hun-suan saam-sa-hai kha kai-yang
) was formed with the purpose of trading grilled chicken. Its partners are Mr. Wanmai, Mr. Duenmai, and Mr. Pimai, each contributing 100,000 baht as capital.
In the course of the partnership’s business, Mr. Wanmai wished to purchase chickens from Charoen Food Co., Ltd. for grilling and sale, but Mr. Duenmai and Mr. Pimai objected. Mr. Wanmai did not heed the other partners’ objections and, in violation of them, entered into a contract to purchase fresh chicken from Charoen Food Co., Ltd. for 100,000 baht. When the delivery date arrived, Charoen Food Co., Ltd. delivered the chicken, but Mr. Duenmai and Mr. Pimai refused to accept it on the ground that Mr. Wanmai had acted without authority. From whom, if anyone, may Charoen Food Co., Ltd. demand liability, and on what ground?

Reference answer.

The issue is from whom Charoen Food Co., Ltd. may demand liability. The relevant provisions of the Civil and Commercial Code are as follows. Section 1012 provides that a partnership or company contract is a contract whereby two or more persons agree to unite for a common undertaking with a view to sharing the profits that may be derived from it. Section 1025 provides that an ordinary partnership is a partnership in which all partners are jointly and severally liable without limit for all debts of the partnership. Section 1026 provides that every partner must contribute something to the partnership, whether money, other property, or labour. Section 1033 provides that if the partners have not agreed otherwise on the manner of management, every partner may manage the partnership, but a partner may not enter into any contract to which another partner has objected; in such case every partner is deemed a managing partner. Section 1050 provides that any act done by a partner in the ordinary course of the partnership’s business binds all partners, who are jointly and severally liable without limit for the debts so created.

Analysis.
Because the Three Friends Grilled Chicken Partnership was formed with the purpose of trading grilled chicken, with Mr. Wanmai, Mr. Duenmai, and Mr. Pimai as partners each contributing 100,000 baht, the case is one in which two or more persons have agreed to unite by contributing 100,000 baht each under Section 1026 to conduct the business of trading grilled chicken with a view to sharing profits. The arrangement is the formation of an ordinary partnership under Sections 1012 and 1025, in which all partners bear unlimited joint and several liability for all partnership debts. Because the facts do not show any agreement on the manner of management, all three are deemed managing partners and each may manage the partnership under Section 1033.

As for Mr. Wanmai’s entry into the purchase contract with Charoen Food Co., Ltd. over the objections of Mr. Duenmai and Mr. Pimai: although the final clause of Section 1033 paragraph one provides that a partner may not enter into a contract to which another partner has objected, that provision governs the
internal
relation among partners, not the
external
relation between partners and third parties (governed by Section 1050). For the external relation, the sole consideration is whether the transaction was in the ordinary course of the partnership’s business.

Since the partnership was formed to trade grilled chicken, the purchase of fresh chicken for 100,000 baht is within the ordinary course of its business. All partners (Mr. Wanmai, Mr. Duenmai, and Mr. Pimai) are therefore bound by this purchase and are jointly and severally liable without limit for the debt Mr. Wanmai created, each for the full 100,000 baht. However, because the Three Friends Grilled Chicken Partnership is not registered as a juristic person separate from its partners, Charoen Food Co., Ltd. cannot demand liability from the partnership itself.

Conclusion.
Charoen Food Co., Ltd. may demand that Mr. Wanmai, Mr. Duenmai, and Mr. Pimai be jointly and severally liable for 100,000 baht.

A.2
Q2: Company Director Authority

Decisive provisions: CCC Sections 1022, 1023, 1023/1.

Question.

Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd. was formed with the purpose of dealing in all types of electrical appliances and equipment. Its directors were Mr. Somchai, Mr. Somchoke, and Mr. Somchaiya. On 26 February 2012 a shareholders’ meeting was held and the shareholders resolved to remove Mr. Somchai, Mr. Somchoke, and Mr. Somchaiya from office and to appoint Mr. Praneet, Mr. Pranom, and Mr. Pranit as replacement directors. The change of directors was duly registered with the Ministry of Commerce on 28 February 2012, but the Ministry had not yet published the change in the Royal Gazette.

(a)
If on 1 March 2012 Mr. Somchai and Mr. Somchoke entered into a contract in the name of Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd. to purchase 20 refrigerators from Arctic Cooling Co., Ltd., received the refrigerators from Arctic Cooling Co., Ltd., and took them for themselves without delivering them to Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd., may Arctic Cooling Co., Ltd. demand that Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd. pay the price of the refrigerators? On what ground?

(b)
If on 1 March 2012 Mr. Praneet and Mr. Pranom entered into a contract to purchase 20 refrigerators from AI-Freeze Co., Ltd., and when the delivery date arrived AI-Freeze Co., Ltd. delivered the refrigerators, but Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd. refused to accept delivery on the ground that at the time of the contract Mr. Praneet and Mr. Pranom were not yet lawful directors because the registration had not been published in the Royal Gazette, may AI-Freeze Co., Ltd. demand damages from Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd.? On what ground?

Note: the facts in (a) and (b) are unrelated.

Reference answer.

The change of directors from Mr. Somchai and Mr. Somchoke to Mr. Praneet and Mr. Pranom by resolution of the shareholders’ general meeting, duly registered, is a valid change of the persons authorised to act for Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd., with the consequence that Mr. Somchai and Mr. Somchoke no longer have authority to act on the company’s behalf. Whether the company may assert this change against third parties in (a) and (b) is analysed as follows.

(a)
Although in general Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd. may invoke the registered change of directors against third parties under CCC Section 1023 paragraph one (by arguing that the 1 March 2012 contract signed by the former directors Somchai and Somchoke does not bind the company because they were no longer authorised), Section 1023/1 establishes an exception: a company may not raise Section 1023 as a defence against a third party acting in good faith in order to escape liability on the ground that the directors lacked authority. Here, there is no indication that Arctic Cooling Co., Ltd. knew that Somchai and Somchoke were no longer directors, nor does it appear that the change of directors had been published in the Royal Gazette so as to constitute constructive notice under CCC Section 1022. Arctic Cooling Co., Ltd. is therefore a third party acting in good faith. Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd. cannot raise the lack of authority of Somchai and Somchoke against Arctic Cooling Co., Ltd. under CCC Section 1023/1. Arctic Cooling Co., Ltd. may demand the refrigerator price from Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd. (5 points).

(b)
The contract entered into by the new directors Praneet and Pranom on 1 March 2012 binds Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd. because it was made by directors vested with authority by a shareholders’ general-meeting resolution that had already been followed by a registered change of directors. Publication in the Royal Gazette under Section 1022 serves only to constitute notice to all third parties; it is not a condition for the effectiveness of the change of directors to Praneet and Pranom. AI-Freeze Co., Ltd. is therefore entitled to demand damages from Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd. for breach of the contract that Praneet and Pranom concluded on the company’s behalf (5 points).

Conclusion.
(a) Arctic Cooling Co., Ltd. may demand the refrigerator price from Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd. (b) AI-Freeze Co., Ltd. may demand damages from Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd.

A.3
Q3: Shareholder-Meeting Annulment

Decisive provisions: CCC Sections 1178, 1193, 1195.

Question.

Sudthawin Tour Co., Ltd. was formed to provide overseas tour services. Its registered capital is 200,000 baht, divided into 100 shares of 2,000 baht each, held by 100 shareholders of 1 share each. Its directors are Mr. Ake, Mr. Toh, Mr. Tri, and Mr. Jattawa. The board resolved to convene the 2/2557 ordinary general meeting of shareholders, which was held on 4 April 2014.

At the meeting, 22 shareholders attended. The chair opened the meeting and the meeting considered the payment of an annual dividend, the company having a reasonable profit. On this agenda item, 12 shareholders abstained, 6 voted to approve the dividend, and 4 voted against. The chair declared that the meeting had approved the annual-dividend payment.

On 2 May 2014, Mr. Khao, a shareholder, considered that the 2/2557 general meeting had been convened and had passed a resolution in violation of law, and wished to sue to annul the dividend-approval resolution. He also wished to sue to annul the minutes of meeting 6/2556 dated 6 December 2013, which had approved the appointment of Mr. Tawan as auditor, on the ground that no meeting had actually been held, since Mr. Toh had fabricated those minutes. The other shareholders disagreed and objected to Mr. Khao’s proposed action, but Mr. Khao insisted on proceeding alone. Determine:

(a)
Can Mr. Khao petition the court to annul the 2/2557 general-meeting resolution and the 6/2556 meeting minutes? Give reasons.

(b)
If Mr. Somchai, a major creditor of the company, wishes to petition the court to annul the 2/2557 general-meeting resolution and the 6/2556 meeting minutes, may he do so, and how?

Reference answer.

(a)
The issue is whether Mr. Khao may petition the court to annul the 2/2557 general-meeting resolution and the 6/2556 meeting minutes.

As to the 2/2557 general-meeting resolution, CCC Section 1178 provides that a general meeting must be attended by shareholders holding in aggregate at least one quarter of the company’s capital in order to pass any resolution. On the facts, the company’s registered capital is 200,000 baht divided into 100 shares of 2,000 baht, and only 22 shareholders attended, each holding 1 share. Only 22 shares attended, representing 44,000 baht, which is less than one quarter of the company’s capital. The meeting therefore lacked a quorum and could not pass a resolution on the dividend; the dividend-approval resolution is unlawful, notwithstanding that a majority of those voting approved it.

As to the right to sue for annulment, CCC Section 1195 provides that, where a general meeting has been convened or has passed a resolution in violation of a provision of the law on companies or of the company’s articles, a director or any shareholder may petition the court to annul the resolution, but the petition must be filed within one month from the date of the resolution. Mr. Khao is a shareholder and may exercise the right to petition alone; the disagreement of other shareholders does not deprive him of that right. The 2/2557 general-meeting resolution was passed on 4 April 2014 and his intended filing on 2 May 2014 is within the one-month period.

As to the 6/2556 meeting minutes dated 6 December 2013, which approved Mr. Tawan as auditor: Mr. Khao wishes to sue for annulment on the ground that no meeting actually took place and the minutes were fabricated. This is not a case of annulling a general-meeting resolution that violated the law on companies or the company’s articles, to which the one-month limitation in CCC Section 1195 applies. Even after the one-month period from the resolution date has elapsed, Mr. Khao may still sue for annulment.

Conclusion.
Mr. Khao may petition the court to annul the 2/2557 general-meeting resolution because the meeting lacked a quorum, Mr. Khao is a shareholder with standing to act alone, and he is within the one-month period. He may also petition for annulment of the 6/2556 meeting minutes, even after the one-month period, because no meeting actually took place; this is not the annulment of an unlawful general-meeting resolution under Section 1195.

(b)
The issue is whether Mr. Somchai, a major creditor, may petition the court to annul the 2/2557 general-meeting resolution and the 6/2556 meeting minutes.

CCC Section 1195 provides that a director or any shareholder has the right to petition the court to annul a general-meeting resolution that was passed in violation of the law on companies or of the company’s articles. On the facts, Mr. Somchai is a major creditor of the company but does not appear to be a director or a shareholder. He therefore has no right to petition for annulment of the 2/2557 general-meeting resolution. As to the 6/2556 meeting minutes, even though this is not strictly a case falling under CCC Section 1195, Mr. Somchai is merely a creditor, and the law does not confer on creditors a power to sue directly in respect of a company’s resolutions or meeting minutes; he therefore cannot petition the court.

Conclusion.
Mr. Somchai has no right to petition the court to annul the 2/2557 general-meeting resolution or the 6/2556 meeting minutes, because he is merely a creditor, not a director or shareholder, and no law confers on a creditor standing to petition for such annulment.

The provisions referred to below are provisions of the Civil and Commercial Code.

(a) When the shareholders’ meeting was held and the shareholders resolved to remove Mr. Somchai, Mr. Somchoke, and Mr. Somchaiya from office as directors of the company on 26 February 2012, the three ceased to have management authority from that date. However, on 28 February 2012 the change of directors was registered with the Ministry of Commerce but had not yet been published in the Royal Gazette. It therefore cannot be held that third parties had notice of the change of directors of Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd. When Mr. Somchai and Mr. Somchoke, who had formerly been directors of the company, entered into a contract to purchase 20 refrigerators from Arctic Cooling Co., Ltd., this was an act within the scope of the company’s purpose of dealing in all types of electrical appliances and equipment under Section 66, so the third party is protected under the law: Arctic Cooling Co., Ltd. may demand that Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd. be liable for the price of the refrigerators.

However, Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd. has the right to demand that Mr. Somchai and Mr. Somchoke compensate the company for damages, because they acted unilaterally, without authority in the capacity of company directors, in making the contract in the company’s name on 1 March 2012, after their terms as directors had ended by the resolution of the meeting on 26 February 2012.

(b) When the shareholders’ meeting was held and the shareholders resolved to appoint Mr. Praneet, Mr. Pranom, and Mr. Pranit as replacement directors on 26 February 2012, all three acquired management authority over the company from that date. It is therefore unnecessary to consider 28 February 2012, the date of registration with the Ministry of Commerce; even though the change had not yet been published in the Royal Gazette, the authority of all three as directors existed from the date of the shareholders’ valid resolution. When on 1 March 2012 Mr. Praneet and Mr. Pranom entered into a contract to purchase 20 refrigerators from AI-Freeze Co., Ltd., this was an act within the scope of the purpose of Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd., whose object is to deal in all types of electrical appliances and equipment under Section 66.

Accordingly, the purchase of refrigerators by Mr. Praneet and Mr. Pranom is a conduct of business in their capacity as managing directors of Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd., so the contract binds the company and does not bind Mr. Praneet and Mr. Pranom personally. AI-Freeze Co., Ltd. may therefore demand that Jipatha Electricals Co., Ltd. be liable.

Across the 15 cross-graded answers we see three recurring patterns: cells where the three LLM judges sit above the majority-human pair (e.g. Items C4, C5), cells where they sit within the majority-human band (e.g. Items C2, C1), and cells where all six graders are within 1 point of each other (e.g. Items C6, C7, C3).
