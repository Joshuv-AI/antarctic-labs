Comment on “Specific heat of an ideal Bose gas above the Bose condensation temperature,” [Am. J. Phys. 72 (9), 1193–1194 (2004)]
Frank Wang

Abstract




In 2004, I presented a derivation for an approximate formula for the
specific heat of an ideal Bose gas above the Bose-Einstein
condensation temperature in this
Journal
.
fw2004
I was
trying to produce a graph of the specific heat resembling the Greek
letter
\Lambda
, but failed to find an explicit formula in textbooks
I consulted with back then. In 2025, Princeton University Press
published
The Essential Einstein
,
ee2025
a collection
of Albert Einstein’s works including an English translation of Albert
Einstein’s groundbreaking 1925 paper,
einstein1925
and from it I
learned that Einstein himself set a procedure for the formula I was
searching for. In this Comment I will guide the readers to execute
the calculations that Einstein outlined, correct some numerical errors
in the paper, and compare his formula with mine. The history of the
acceptance of Einstein’s theory will be summarized.

In 1924, Einstein published “Quantum Theory of the Monoatomic Ideal
Gas” (Ref.
ee2025
, Chapter 24) which laid the foundation for
quantum statistical mechanics. Einstein’s interest was triggered by
the request to translate and publish a paper (previously rejected by
the
Philosophical Magazine
) by the then unknown Indian
physicist Satyendranath Bose. Bose used an innovative way to count
photons and obtained the Planck distribution; Einstein generalized
Bose’s counting method for identical particles, and established what
is now known as the Bose-Einstein distribution. In 1925, Einstein
wrote a second paper with the same title (Ref.
ee2025
, Chapter
25) and predicted the phenomenon of “Das gesättigte ideale Gas”
(the saturated ideal gas) which is now referred to as a Bose-Einstein
condensate. He ended the paper with the equation of state of the
unsaturated ideal gas, see Figure
1
, which is the focus
of this note.

Einstein used
E
for the internal energy and the lower case
n
for
the total number of bosons, and he wrote (Eq. (22) in
Ref.
ee2025
on page 353)

\frac{E}{n}=\frac{3}{2}\kappa T\frac{\sum_{\tau}\tau^{-5/2}\lambda^{\tau}}{\sum_{\tau}\tau^{-3/2}\lambda^{\tau}},

(1)

where he used
\kappa
for the Boltzmann constant,
T
for
temperature, and
\lambda
is the fugacity
e^{\mu/\kappa T}
(where

\mu
is the chemical potential), which is a real number between 0 and
1. One can read Einstein’s original papers or Ref.
fw2004
for
this result; his internal energy is equivalent to Eq. (17) in
Ref.
fw2004
; see the Appendix. Einstein denoted the
denominator in Eq. (
1
) by
y(\lambda)
and the numerator by

z(\lambda)
. His
y
in his Eq. (18c) shown in
Figure
1
is essentially a temperature parameter and can
be written more compactly as

y=\zeta(3/2)\left(\frac{T_{c}}{T}\right)^{3/2},

(2)

where
\zeta(3/2)
is the Riemann zeta function evaluated at
3/2
and

T_{c}
is the critical temperature for Bose-Einstein condensation;
see Ref.
fw2004
Eq. (11). The value of
\zeta(3/2)
is

2.612375\ldots
but Einstein used a crude value of
2.615
for it
throughout his paper. (The value of
\zeta(5/2)
is
1.341487\ldots

but Einstein used
1.348
in his Eqs. (40) and (41), and in Eq. (40)
he or the typist missed a factor of
3/2
for the internal energy.)

Einstein sought to express
z

y

his assistant Jakob Grommer for the solution. For sufficiently small

y

y
, and the coefficients can
be obtained from repeated differentiation. In the present case the
differentiation
d^{\nu}z/dy^{\nu}
involves parametric equations

z(\lambda)
and
y(\lambda)
, but the procedure is a standard topic
in a modern-day calculus course. Einstein’s Eqs. (43) and (44) can be
implemented using a computer algebra system such as Maple or
Mathematica, which excels in this kind of repetitive and tedious
process.

z=y-\frac{1}{2!}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}y^{2}-\frac{1}{3!}\left(\frac{4}{3\sqrt{3}}-\frac{3}{4}\right)y^{3}-\frac{1}{4!}\left(\frac{9}{4}+\frac{15\sqrt{2}}{8}-2\sqrt{6}\right)y^{4}+\ldots,

(3)

z=y-0.1768\,y^{2}-0.0033\,y^{3}-\,0.00011y^{4}+\ldots.

Einstein’s quartic term in his expansion

z=y-0.1768\,y^{2}-0.0034\,y^{3}-0.0005\,y^{4}

he or his assistant committed an error while performing the laborious

He introduced the notation
F(y)=z/y
, and with it the internal energy
per particle is
3/2\,\kappa TF(y)
, see his Eq. (19c). The
specific heat is the derivative of the internal energy with respect to
temperature, and it involves the following derivative rules.

\frac{d}{dT}\left[TF(y)\right]=F(y)+T\frac{dF(y)}{dT}=F(y)+T\frac{dF(y)}{dy}\frac{dy}{dT}.

(4)

Because
y
is proportional to
T^{-3/2}
,
T\,dy/dT=-3/2\,y
.
The specific heat per mole above
T_{c}
based on Einstein’s approach
(see
c_{v}
after his Eq. (18c) in Figure
1
) after
correcting the numerical error in his
z
is

c_{v}=\frac{3}{2}R(F(y)-\frac{3}{2}yF^{\prime}(y))=\frac{3}{2}R\left[1+0.231\left(\frac{T_{c}}{T}\right)^{3/2}+0.045\left(\frac{T_{c}}{T}\right)^{3}+0.0069\left(\frac{T_{c}}{T}\right)^{9/2}\right],

(5)

where
R
is the ideal gas constant and we use Eq. (
2

for
y
after the differentiation. For a comparison, the specific

T_{c}
in Ref.
fw2004

c_{v}=R\left\{\left[\frac{9}{2}\frac{\zeta(5/2)}{\zeta(3/2)}-\frac{3}{8}\frac{\zeta(3/2)^{2}}{\pi}\right]+\left[\frac{9}{4}\frac{\zeta(5/2)}{\zeta(3/2)}-\frac{3}{8}\frac{\zeta(3/2)^{2}}{\pi}\right]\left(\frac{T_{c}}{T}\right)^{3/2}+\left[\frac{3}{4}\frac{\zeta(3/2)^{2}}{\pi}-\frac{3\zeta(5/2)}{\zeta(3/2)}\right]\left(\frac{T_{c}}{T}\right)^{3}\right\},

After Einstein’s 1925 publication, “the degeneracy of the
Bose-Einstein gas has rather got the reputation of having only a
purely imaginary existence”
london1938a
and scientists
overlooked this work for years. In 1938, P. Kapitza and independently
J. F. Allen and D. Misener published results of the superfluidity of
liquid helium. The same year Fritz London wrote a letter to

Nature
london1938a
with a plot of the specific heat
similar to Figure
2
. The resemblance between the

\Lambda
shape and the measured specific heat convinced most
physicists about a direct link between Einstein’s theoretic work and
experimental observables. In a 1939 essay written for

Harper’s Magazine
titled “The Usefulness of Useless
Knowledge”
flexner1939
by Abraham Flexner, the then director of
the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, he highlighted
Einstein’s quantum statistical research as an example that unexpected
applications might emerge from seemingly “useless” studies. He
quoted the following from
Science
: “The stature of Professor
Albert Einstein’s genius reached new heights when it was disclosed
that the learned mathematical physicist developed mathematics fifteen
years ago which are now helping to solve the mysteries of the amazing
fluidity of helium near the absolute zero of temperature scale.”

In London’s letter to
Nature
, he wrote

c_{v}=\frac{3}{2}R\left[1+0.231\left(\frac{T_{c}}{T}\right)^{3/2}+0.046\left(\frac{T_{c}}{T}\right)^{3}+\ldots\right]

(8)

for
T\geq T_{c}
. Comparing this with Eq. (
5
),
London seemed to have noticed the numerical error in Einstein’s 1925
paper and dropped the last term. London published a paper in

Physical Review
titled “On the Bose-Einstein
Condensation,”
london1938b
and he stated “for
T\geq T_{c}
,
Einstein has previously given the semi-convergent expansion.” The
specific heat in London’s paper is

c_{v}=\frac{3}{2}R\left[1+0.231\left(\frac{T_{c}}{T}\right)^{3/2}+0.045\left(\frac{T_{c}}{T}\right)^{3}+0.040\left(\frac{T_{c}}{T}\right)^{9/2}+\ldots\right].

(9)

The coefficient of the
T^{-9/2}
term was incorrect. Finally, in
London’s monograph
Superfluids
london1954
published in
1954, he wrote a formula almost identical to
Eq. (
5
), except he rounded
0.0069
to
0.007

in the
T^{-9/2}
term. Like Einstein, London made the expansion
around the limit
T\rightarrow\infty
, as I pointed out in
Ref.
fw2004
.

In his book
Einstein and the Quantum
,
stone
Douglas
Stone said “The generosity of the ‘Bose-Einstein’ designation is not
widely appreciated, as few physicists realize that Bose said not a
word about the quantum ideal gas in his seminal paper. The paper that
does predict quantum condensation belongs to Einstein alone.” Stone
was referring to Einstein’s 1925 paper.
einstein1925

“Bose-Einstein condensation” first appeared in the title of London’s

Appendix A
Properties of the Bose-Einstein Integral Function

In the main text I stated Einstein’s expression of the internal
energy, Eq. (
1
), is equivalent to Ref.
fw2004

Eq. (17), which reads

U=\frac{3}{2}NkT\frac{g_{5/2}(\alpha)}{g_{3/2}(\alpha)},

(10)

where
U
is the internal energy and the Bose-Einstein integral
function is defined as

g_{\sigma}(z)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\sigma)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\sigma-1}}{z^{-1}e^{x}-1}\,dx,

(11)

where
\Gamma(\sigma)
is the gamma function. Do not confuse
z
here
and Ref.
fw2004
as fugacity with Einstein’s definition of
z

in the main text. For small
z
, which represents high temperature
and low density, we can expand the integrand as a power series then
perform the improper integral term by term.

g_{\sigma}(z)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\sigma)}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\sigma-1}\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}(ze^{-x})^{l}\,dx=z+\frac{z^{2}}{2^{\sigma}}+\frac{z^{3}}{3^{\sigma}}+\ldots=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}l^{-\sigma}z^{l}.

(12)

This is the connection between the Bose-Einstein integral and the
summation that Einstein used for the internal energy in
Eq. (
1
) in the main text. But if one is interested in low
temperature domain, this series is not appropriate, because
z\equiv e^{\beta\mu}
is actually an exponential function. This is a
Dirichlet series, named after the mathematician Peter Gustav Dirichlet
(1805–1859), who married Rebecka Mendelssohn, younger sister of the
composers Fanny and Felix. In Ref.
fw2004
a different series
has been developed to approximate the specific heat near the critical
temperature of Bose-Einstein condensation.
