The Ising Model on a Two-Community Stochastic Block Model
Alessandra Bianchi ∗, Vanessa Jacquier ∗, Matteo Sfragara ∗

Abstract
. We study the Ising model on a two-community stochastic block model, where n n spins are split into two equal groups with inter-community interaction parameter α n ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \alpha_{n}\in[0,1] . We provide a complete characterization of the phase diagram and show that, almost surely with respect to the graph realization, the model undergoes a uniqueness/non-uniqueness phase transition of the Gibbs measure. In particular, in the supercritical regime, the law of the magnetization vector of the two communities converges to a mixture of Dirac measures that, depending on whether α n ≫ 1 / n \alpha_{n}\gg 1/n or α n ≲ 1 / n \alpha_{n}\lesssim 1/n , is supported on two or four points, with possibly different weights. In the uniqueness region, we further analyze the fluctuations of the magnetization vector: in the subcritical regime, we prove a quenched central limit theorem under the classical n \sqrt{n} scaling, while at criticality we establish non-Gaussian fluctuations on the smaller scale n 1 / 4 n^{1/4} . 1.
Introduction

Interacting spin systems constitute a fundamental class of models in statistical mechanics and probability theory, describing how local interactions at the microscopic level generate collective macroscopic behavior in large systems
[
12
,
14
]
. Among these, mean-field models have been extensively investigated due to their analytical tractability and their ability to capture key features such as the structure of equilibrium states, phase transitions, and fluctuation phenomena. A well-known example is the Curie–Weiss (CW) model, which has become a central model in the rigorous study of equilibrium statistical mechanics. Beyond their original physical motivation, mean-field spin systems have proved to be remarkably flexible modeling tools. Over the past decades, they have been successfully employed in diverse contexts, including social dynamics, opinion formation, decision-making processes, finance, ecology, and chemical systems. This broad range of applications highlights the versatility of these models: by appropriately modifying the structure of interactions or introducing disorder, one can analyze both physical, social, and biological phenomena in a mathematically rigorous setting.

A natural extension of the CW model arises when one considers several interacting populations, or communities, so that the system is partitioned into distinct groups with potentially different interaction strengths within and across groups. From the perspective of statistical mechanics, this generalization offers a richer landscape for equilibrium analysis: the phase diagram can exhibit multiple phase transitions, metastable states, and complex patterns of magnetization
[
3
,
13
,
21
]
.

This viewpoint connects closely to random graph theory, which provides a powerful framework for modeling large systems in which collective behavior is shaped not only by interaction strengths but also by the topology of the underlying network. Within this setting, a particularly relevant class of random graphs is the Stochastic Block Model (SBM), in which vertices are divided into communities, producing stronger interactions within groups and weaker interactions between them. The SBM has become a central model in contexts where modular organization is essential
[
1
,
15
,
25
]
. In network science, it serves as a reference model for community detection and for analyzing the spread of information, diseases, and behaviors across structured populations. In the life sciences, it is used to model metabolic and protein interaction networks, capturing how aggregation and modularity influence global behavior. More broadly, the SBM provides a theoretical
framework for investigating emergent phenomena driven by community structure.

In this paper, we study the Ising model on a two-community SBM (2-SBM) and investigate how the presence of interacting communities affects spontaneous magnetization and asymptotic fluctuations. We consider a mean-field spin system consisting of
n
spins partitioned into two equally sized communities, where the interaction network is random and reflects the underlying community structure.

Our first set of results concerns the thermodynamic limit and the phase diagram of the model.
We characterize the limiting free energy and determine the asymptotic law of the magnetization vector as the system size tends to infinity.
In particular, almost surely with respect to the graph realization, we show that a phase transition emerges as a function of the inverse temperature
\beta
and of the inter-community interaction parameter
\alpha_{n}
.
Explicitly, while at high temperature regimes the system exhibits uniqueness of the asymptotic Gibbs measure,
in the low-temperature regime, the measure loses uniqueness and concentrates on multiple Dirac masses: four in the case
\alpha_{n}\lesssim 1/n
, with weights possibly of different depth, and only two if
\alpha_{n}\gg 1/n
.

As a refinement of this analysis, we further investigate the fluctuations of the magnetization vector in the
subcritical/uniqueness regime. At high-temperature, we establish a quenched central limit theorem (CLT), showing that under the
diffusive scale
\sqrt{n}
, the magnetization converges to a Gaussian distribution whose covariance matrix captures both intra- and inter-community correlations.
At critical temperature, we prove that the magnetization exhibits non-Gaussian fluctuations on the smaller scale
n^{1/4}
,
converging to a distribution with quartic tails. The quenched nature of these results emphasizes the robustness of this asymptotic behavior under disorder.

These findings extend the classical results for the one-community CW and Erdös–Rényi CW models, both of which are well understood in terms of equilibrium properties
[
2
]
and fluctuations
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
.
Comparable results have also been obtained in the multi-community framework, but only under deterministic settings and assuming inter-community parameters that remain constant (i.e., independent of the system size)
[
3
,
23
,
21
,
20
]
, apart from the very recent work
[
22
]
, which also considers size-dependent and random inter-community interactions.
In this paper, we address this gap by first analyzing the deterministic two-community setting with the inter-community parameter

\alpha_{n}
, and then incorporating randomness through the two-community stochastic block model (2-SBM).

Outline of the paper.
In Section 2, we introduce the Ising model on the 2-SBM,
present the main results, and outline the key ideas of their proofs.
In Section 3, we analyze the bipartite CW model, establishing the corresponding phase diagram and
proving convergence results for its magnetization.
We then return to the Ising model on the 2-SBM. In Section 4, we show that the random Gibbs measure concentrates
around the corresponding bipartite CW Gibbs measure, and we establish the results on the asymptotic behavior of the magnetization.
Finally, Section 5 is devoted to the proof of the fluctuation results for the magnetization in the uniqueness regime.

2.
Model and results

2.1.
Mathematical model

For any
n\in 2\mathbb{N}
, consider a two-community Stochastic Block Model (2-SBM) on
n
vertices with two communities of equal size
n/2
. This is obtained by including every edge independently and according to Bernoulli laws with a parameter depending on whether the vertices belong to the same or different communities. Formally, let
\mathscr{G}=(V,E)
be a complete graph on
n
vertices, with
V=[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and consider a partition of
\mathscr{G}
into two equal-sized communities
\mathscr{G}_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1})
and
\mathscr{G}_{2}=(V_{2},E_{2})
, where
\mathscr{G}_{1}
and
\mathscr{G}_{2}
are complete graphs on
n/2
vertices. Without loss of generality we assume
V_{1}=\{1,\ldots,n/2\}
and
V_{2}=\{n/2+1,\ldots,n\}
. Let
\mathcal{E}_{I}
and
\mathcal{E}_{E}
denote respectively the set of
internal edges
and
external edges
, that are identified by ordered pairs of vertices as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{I}=\{(i,j):\,i,j\in V_{1}\,\text{ or }\,i,j\in V_{2},\,i<j\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{E}_{E}=\{(i,j):\,i\in V_{1},\,j\in V_{2}\}.

We then consider two sequences of parameters
(p_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in(0,1]
and
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in[0,1]
such that:

-

Every edge in
\mathcal{E}_{I}
is included in the graph with probability
p_{n}
;

-

Every edge in
\mathcal{E}_{E}
is included in the graph with probability
\alpha_{n}p_{n}
.

This procedure produces a 2-SBM, whose interaction matrix has intra- and inter-connection probabilities among vertices, given by

p_{n}
and
\alpha_{n}p_{n}
respectively.

We now define the Ising model on the above 2-SBM. To this end, it is convenient to map the random procedure
described above with two independent sequences of Bernoulli i.i.d. random variables,

(\xi_{ij})_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{I}}
and
(\zeta_{ij})_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{E}}
,
with parameters
p_{n}
and
p_{n}\alpha_{n}
, respectively. Let
\mathcal{X}_{n}=\{-1,+1\}^{n}
be the configuration space of a
n
-spin system,
with elements denoted by
\sigma=({\sigma}_{i})_{i\in V}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
, where
\sigma_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}

is the spin value at
i\in V
.
The Hamiltonian of the Ising model on the 2-SBM is defined as

(2.1)

\displaystyle H_{n}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{np_{n}}\left\{\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{I}}\xi_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{E}}\zeta_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\right\},

and the corresponding Gibbs probability measure is given by

\displaystyle\mu_{n,\beta}(\sigma)=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{n}(\sigma)}}{Z_{n,\beta}}\quad\mbox{with }\quad Z_{n,\beta}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}e^{-\beta H_{n}(\sigma)},

where
\beta>0
is the inverse temperature and
Z_{n,\beta}
is referred to as the partition function.

Notice that all these quantities are random as they depend on the realization of the Bernoulli random variables
\xi_{ij}
’s and
\zeta_{ij}
’s, and thus intrinsically also on the value of
p_{n}
and
\alpha_{n}
.
Throughout the paper, we will denote by
\Omega
the probability space where these sequences are defined, and we write
\mathbb{P}
(resp.
{\mathbb{E}}
) to denote their joint probability law (resp. expectation). We will usually hide the dependence from
\omega\in\Omega
, although the presence of a source of randomness should be kept in mind. In this framework, the Gibbs measure
\mu_{n,\beta}
is also referred to as a
quenched Gibbs measure
, in contrast to the
annealed Gibbs measure
, which is obtained by averaging
\mu_{n,\beta}
with respect to
\mathbb{P}
.

We assume that

(2.2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}np_{n}=+\infty\quad\mbox{and }\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha_{n}=\hat{\alpha}\in[0,1],

and for notational convenience, we will simply write
p\equiv p_{n}
, while keeping the symbol
\alpha_{n}

to avoid being confused with its limit
\hat{\alpha}
, and because we are interested in understanding the asymptotic behavior of the model depending on the different choice of
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.

An important immediate observation is that taking the
\mathbb{P}
-average of the Hamiltonian (
2.1
), one obtains the following Hamiltonian of a bipartite Curie-Weiss (CW) model

(2.3)

\displaystyle\bar{H}_{n}(\sigma)=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{I}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}-\frac{\alpha_{n}}{n}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{E}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j},

with corresponding Gibbs measure
\bar{\mu}_{n,\beta}(\sigma)=\frac{e^{-\beta\bar{H}_{n}(\sigma)}}{\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}}
and partition function
\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}
.
In this sense, the Ising model on the 2-SBM can be considered
as a randomized version of the bipartite CW model. This relationship will be indeed a key tool in the proofs, which basically rest on the concentration properties of the graph.

Before stating the main results and describing the phase diagram of the model, it is convenient to introduce some further notation. Let us consider the (random)
finite- and infinite-volume free energies
of the model

(2.4)

\displaystyle f_{n,\beta}=\frac{1}{n\beta}\log Z_{n,\beta}\quad\mbox{and }\quad f_{\beta}=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n,\beta},

and define analogously the finite- and infinite-volume free energies for the bipartite CW model

(2.5)

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{n,\beta}=\frac{1}{n\beta}\log\bar{Z}_{n,\beta},\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\bar{f}_{\beta}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\bar{f}_{n,\beta}.

Let us introduce the
local magnetization

of the community
\mathscr{G}_{i}
, for
i=1,2
,

\displaystyle m_{i}^{(n)}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{n/2}\sum_{j\in V_{i}}\sigma_{j},

which takes value in
\Gamma_{n/2}=\left\{-1,-1+\frac{4}{n},...,1-\frac{4}{n},1\right\}
,
and let
{\bf m}^{(n)}({\sigma})=(m_{1}^{(n)}({\sigma}),m_{2}^{(n)}({\sigma}))
be the global
magnetization vector
of
{\sigma}
,
which takes value on
\Gamma_{n/2}^{2}
.

Denoting by
\|\cdot\|
the Euclidean norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, for
\alpha\in[0,1]
and
{\bf m}=(m_{1},m_{2})\in[-1,1]^{2}
, define

(2.6)

\displaystyle E_{\alpha}({\bf m})=\frac{\|{\bf m}\|^{2}}{4}+\frac{\alpha}{2}m_{1}m_{2},

and note that
\bar{H}_{n}(\sigma)=-\frac{n}{2}E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{(n)}({\sigma}))+1\,
.
However, the additive constant
+1
in the Hamiltonian does not affect the associated Gibbs measure,
since it only produces a constant multiplicative factor in the Boltzmann weight, which cancels out with the normalization.
Therefore, in the following, we neglect this term and work with the Hamiltonian

(2.7)

\displaystyle\bar{H}_{n}(\sigma)=-\frac{n}{2}E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{(n)}({\sigma})).

We will show that the infinite-volume free energy of the bipartite CW model is equal to

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{\beta}=\frac{1}{2\beta}\sup_{{\bf m}\in[-1,1]^{2}}G_{\hat{\alpha},\beta}({\bf m}),

where, for
\alpha\in[0,1],\,\beta>0
and
{\bf m}\in[-1,1]^{2}
, the free energy functional is given by

(2.8)

\displaystyle G_{\alpha,\beta}({\bf m})=\beta E_{\alpha}({\bf m})-\mathcal{I}({\bf m}),

and the entropic term
\mathcal{I}({\bf{m}})=\mathcal{I}(m_{1})+\mathcal{I}(m_{2})

is defined by setting

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(m_{i})=\frac{1+m_{i}}{2}\log\left(\frac{1+m_{i}}{2}\right)+\frac{1-m_{i}}{2}\log\left(\frac{1-m_{i}}{2}\right),\quad\mbox{ for }\,i=1,2.

2.2.
Results

Our first result describes the convergence of the magnetization law and characterizes the phase diagram. Since the free energy functional
G_{\alpha,\beta}({\bf m})
may exhibit one, two, or four maximizers depending on the value of
\beta
and
\alpha
,
we introduce the following notation. For
m_{g}>0
and
m_{l}>0
defined, respectively,
in (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) as implicit functions of
\alpha
and
\beta
, we set

{\bf m}^{0}=(0,0),\quad{\bf m}^{1}=-{\bf m}^{2}=(m_{g},m_{g}),\quad{\bf m}^{3}=-{\bf m}^{4}=(m_{l},-m_{l})\,.

Here,
{\bf m}^{1},{\bf m}^{2}
are symmetric maximizers corresponding to the
global maximum
of
G_{\alpha,\beta}({\bf m})
,
while
{\bf m}^{3},{\bf m}^{4}
are anti-symmetric maximizers corresponding to the
local maximum
.
We will show that they are all nonzero when
\beta
is larger than some explicit critical value
\beta_{c}\equiv\beta_{c}(\alpha)
,
and that if
\alpha=0
, then
m_{g}=m_{l}=m^{*}
, where
m^{*}>0
is the unique positive solution of the classical mean-field equation
\mathrm{atanh}(m)=\frac{\beta}{2}m

(see Eq.(
3.13
)).

Let
\mathcal{L}({\bf m}^{(n)})
be the law of
{\bf m}^{(n)}
under the Gibbs measure
\mu_{n,\beta}
and let
\delta_{x}
be the Dirac measure at
x
.

Theorem 1

(Convergence of the Magnetization Law)
.

Under the assumptions in (
2.2
), the following statements hold almost surely with respect to
\mathbb{P}
:

(i)

For any
\beta>0
, the infinite-volume free energy exists and coincides with the free energy
\bar{f}_{\beta}
of the bipartite CW model defined in (
2.5
), i.e.

\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n,\beta}=\bar{f}_{\beta}.

(ii)

A phase transition in the uniqueness of the infinite-volume Gibbs measure occurs at

\beta_{c}=\frac{2}{1+\hat{\alpha}}
.
More precisely:

(a)

If
\beta\leq\beta_{c}
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{L}({\bf m}^{(n)})=\delta_{{\bf m}^{0}}
.

(b)

If
\beta>\beta_{c}
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{L}({\bf m}^{(n)})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta_{{\bf m}^{1}}+\delta_{{\bf m}^{2}}\right),&\text{if }\alpha_{n}\gg\frac{1}{n},\\[6.0pt]
\frac{1}{2(1+e^{-c})}\left(\delta_{{\bf m}^{1}}+\delta_{{\bf m}^{2}}\right)+\frac{e^{-c}}{2(1+e^{-c})}\left(\delta_{{\bf m}^{3}}+\delta_{{\bf m}^{4}}\right),&\text{if }\alpha_{n}\lesssim\frac{1}{n},\end{cases}\,

where
c=\lim_{n\to\infty}n\alpha_{n}\geq 0
.

1

1

Given two sequences
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we write
a_{n}\gg b_{n}
if
\displaystyle{\lim_{n\to\infty}b_{n}/a_{n}=0}
, and
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
, if
\displaystyle{\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}/b_{n}=c<\infty}
.

Remark 1

(The role of
\alpha_{n}
)
.

The phase diagram described in Theorem
1
is governed by the structure of the energy landscape,
and in particular by the interplay between global and local maxima of the free energy functional.
The parameter
\alpha_{n}
plays a crucial role in this picture, as it controls the interaction
between the two communities, and therefore the relative stability of these maxima.
To make this precise, we will show in Lemma
10
that,
as
n\to\infty
, the difference between the free energy functional at global and local maxima satisfies
the following expansion in
\alpha_{n}\to 0
,

\varepsilon(\alpha_{n})=G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m}^{1})-G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m}^{3})=(m^{*})^{2}\beta\,\alpha_{n}+o(\alpha_{n})\,.

In addition, in (
3.13
) we obtain the expansions

\displaystyle m_{g}(\alpha_{n})=m^{*}+\frac{\frac{\beta}{2}m^{*}}{\frac{1}{1-(m^{*})^{2}}-\frac{\beta}{2}}\alpha_{n}+o(\alpha_{n}),\qquad m_{l}(\alpha_{n})=m^{*}-\frac{\frac{\beta}{2}m^{*}}{\frac{1}{1-(m^{*})^{2}}-\frac{\beta}{2}}\alpha_{n}+o(\alpha_{n}).

As a consequence, when
\alpha_{n}\to 0
, both
m_{g}(\alpha_{n})
and
m_{l}(\alpha_{n})
converge to
m^{*}
, and asymptotically
they all correspond to the global maximum.
However, at finite
n
, the contribution to the Gibbs measure around each maximum is proportional to
e^{-n\varepsilon(\alpha_{n})}
,
so that the rate at which
\alpha_{n}
vanishes determines the concentration properties of the measure.
This mechanism explains the different regimes described in the theorem.

Remark 2

(Extremal cases)
.

In the extremal case
\alpha_{n}\equiv 0
, the model reduces to two independent Erdös–Rényi CW communities on
n/2
vertices, whose deterministic counterpart is described in
[
3
]
: for
\beta\leq 2
the unique maximizer is
(0,0)
,
while for
\beta>2
four global maximizers (both symmetric and anti-symmetric) emerge.
At the opposite extreme, when
\alpha_{n}\equiv 1
, the model coincides with the Erdös–Rényi CW model on
n
vertices, and the classical picture is again recovered: for
\beta\leq 1
the unique maximizer is
(0,0)
, while for
\beta>1
two symmetric global maximizers emerge (see
[
2
]
for further details).

Remark 3

(Comparison with
[
22
]
)
.

We note that the recent work
[
22
]
analyzes a similar setting,
deriving comparable results under the assumptions
\alpha_{n}\gg 1/n
and
\alpha_{n}\ll 1/n
.
However, it does not consider the regime in which

\displaystyle{\lim_{n\to\infty}}n\alpha_{n}=c>0
.
This scaling is of particular interest, as the difference between global
and local maxima vanishes as
\alpha_{n}\to 0
, while their contribution to the Gibbs weights remains non-negligible,
being of order
n\alpha_{n}
.
This behavior is reflected in the convergence in law of the magnetization,
where different maximizers appear with non-trivial and distinct weights, as described in case (ii-b).

In the uniqueness region of the phase diagram, the above quenched distributional convergences
can be straightened to the following quenched strong law of large numbers for
{\bf m}^{(n)}
.

Corollary 2

(SLLN for
{\bf m}^{(n)}
)
.

Let
\beta\leq\beta_{c}
. Under assumptions (
2.2
), almost surely with respect to
\mathbb{P}
,

{\bf m}^{(n)}\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}{\bf m}^{0}\qquad\mbox{w.r.t. }\mu_{n,\beta},\mbox{ as }n\to\infty.

The proof of this corollary relies on a classical argument of exponentially fast convergence and is postponed to Section
4.3
, together with the proof of Theorem
1
.

Our second main result concerns the fluctuations of the magnetization in the high temperature regime when
\beta<\beta_{c}
.

Theorem 3

(Quenched CLT for the magnetization)
.

Let
\beta<\beta_{c}
. Under assumptions (
2.2
),

\mathcal{L}(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)})\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{P}-\text{ probability},

where
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
is a two-dimensional normal random vector with covariance matrix

(2.9)

\Sigma=\frac{2-\beta}{(1-\beta)+\frac{\beta^{2}(1-\hat{\alpha}^{2})}{4}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{\beta\hat{\alpha}}{2-\beta}\\
\frac{\beta\hat{\alpha}}{2-\beta}&1\end{pmatrix}.

Theorem
3
generalizes to the two-community setting, the quenched central limit theorem for the magnetization of the Erdös–Rényi CW model first proved in
[
16
]
and later improved in
[
17
]
.
Note that the covariance matrix reduces to a diagonal matrix when
\hat{\alpha}=0
, namely when the inter-community interaction vanishes,
while when
\hat{\alpha}=1
, it matches the variance term obtained in
[
17
]
for the Erdös–Rényi CW model.

We emphasize that similar results have recently been obtained in
[
6
]
for a broad class of models that includes the present two-community setting. However, our results rely on weaker assumptions on the asymptotic behavior of
p_{n}
as
n\to\infty
, as specified in (
2.2
).

We conclude our analysis with a non-standard limit theorem for the fluctuations of the magnetization
at the critical point
\beta=\beta_{c}
.

Theorem 4

(Fluctuations at the critical point)
.

Let
\beta=\beta_{c}
. Under assumptions (
2.2
),

\mathcal{L}(\sqrt[4]{n}{\bf m}^{(n)})\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}X\quad\text{in }\mathbb{P}-\text{ probability},

where
X
is a two-dimensional random vector with density

(2.10)

f(\textbf{x})\propto e^{-2(x_{1}^{4}+x_{2}^{4})},\quad\forall\textbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\,.

Theorem
4
shows that, at the critical point, the fluctuations around the unique maximizer
{\bf m}^{0}
occur
on the smaller scale
n^{1/4}
rather than
n^{1/2}
. The limiting distribution is non-Gaussian and exhibits quartic exponential tails, reflecting the fact that the free energy functional
G_{\hat{\alpha},\beta}({\bf m})
, around
{\bf m}^{0}
,
has a leading-order behavior of degree four.
In particular, the covariance matrix
\Sigma
in (
2.9
) becomes singular, with flat directions in the associated quadratic form.

2.3.
Outlines of the proofs and main ingredients

The proofs of Theorem
1
and Corollary
2
are based on the idea that the random Gibbs measure
concentrates around the Gibbs measure of a bipartite CW model.
Consequently, the first part of the analysis is devoted to the study of the Gibbs measure of the bipartite CW model and of its associated free energy functional, including the characterization of its global and local maximizers (see Section
3
).
In particular, the asymptotic behavior of the magnetization vector is obtained via a Taylor expansion of the free energy functional around its maximizers, combined with an analysis of the induced Gibbs measure on the magnetization space.

The second step consists in proving the aforementioned concentration (see Section
4
).
This is achieved by comparing the original Hamiltonian
H_{n}
with the averaged Hamiltonian
\bar{H}_{n}
,
corresponding to the bipartite CW model. By controlling the difference
\Delta_{n}=H_{n}-\bar{H}_{n}
,
one derives suitable exponential bounds, which imply that the Gibbs measures of the two systems are exponentially close. As a consequence, the free energy of the original system converges
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely to that of the bipartite CW model,
and all limiting properties identified for the latter carry over to the original system.

Finally, to establish the results in Theorems
3
and
4
,
we study the expectation of test functions of the rescaled magnetization under the Gibbs measure, expressed as a ratio of generalized and standard partition functions (see Section
5
).
By introducing a suitably tilted partition function, this ratio can be decomposed into factors that can be analyzed separately: one factor captures the limiting behavior, while the others converge to one, allowing the identification of the limiting law.
In the subcritical regime, this procedure yields a two-dimensional Gaussian distribution, whereas at the critical point, the scaling changes and the limit becomes a non-Gaussian random vector with a quartic exponential density.

3.
Bipartite Curie-Weiss model

3.1.
Model and phase diagram

The bipartite CW model is defined by the probability measure

(3.1)

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}_{n,\beta}(\sigma)=\frac{e^{-\beta\bar{H}_{n}(\sigma)}}{\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}},

where
\bar{H}_{n}(\sigma)
is defined in (
2.3
).
Recall also the definition of free energy given in (
2.5
).

Let
\bar{\mathcal{L}}({\bf m})
be the law of
{\bf m}
under the Gibbs measure in (
3.1
) and let
\delta_{x}
be the Dirac measure at the point
x
. The following main result echoes Theorem
1
and provides the asymptotic distribution of the magnetization, hence characterizing the phase diagram of the model.

Theorem 5

(Convergence of the magnetization law for CW)
.

Let
\alpha_{n}>0
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha_{n}=\hat{\alpha}\in[0,1]
. For
\beta>0
, a phase transition in the uniqueness of the infinite-volume Gibbs measure occurs at

\beta_{c}=\frac{2}{1+\hat{\alpha}}
.
More precisely:

(a)

If
\beta\leq\beta_{c}
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{L}({\bf m}^{(n)})=\delta_{{\bf m}^{0}}
.

(b)

If
\beta>\beta_{c}
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{L}({\bf m}^{(n)})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta_{{\bf m}^{1}}+\delta_{{\bf m}^{2}}\right),&\text{if }\alpha_{n}\gg\frac{1}{n},\\[6.0pt]
\frac{1}{2(1+e^{-c})}\left(\delta_{{\bf m}^{1}}+\delta_{{\bf m}^{2}}\right)+\frac{e^{-c}}{2(1+e^{-c})}\left(\delta_{{\bf m}^{3}}+\delta_{{\bf m}^{4}}\right),&\text{if }\alpha_{n}\lesssim\frac{1}{n},\end{cases}

where
c=\lim_{n\to\infty}n\alpha_{n}\geq 0
.

It is worth emphasizing that closely related results were already established in
[
3
]
and subsequently extended in
[
21
,
23
]
for a bipartite CW model with interaction parameters independent of
n
.
In contrast, the explicit dependence on
n
considered here gives rise
to novel phenomena that do not appear in
[
3
,
21
,
23
]
, and constitute
the central focus of this work.

In the next section, we present a collection of preliminary results that play a key role in the proof of Theorem
5
, while also being of independent interest.

3.2.
Preliminary results

3.2.1.
The free energy

We provide a variational characterization of the infinite-volume free energy.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha_{n}=\hat{\alpha}\in[0,1]
and
G_{\alpha,\beta}
be the function defined in (
2.8
).
The infinite-volume free energy is equal to

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{\beta}=\frac{1}{2\beta}\sup_{{\bf m}\in[-1,1]^{2}}G_{\hat{\alpha},\beta}({\bf m}).

Proof.

From (
2.7
), we have

\displaystyle\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}e^{-\beta\bar{H}_{n}(\sigma)}=\sum_{{\bf m}=(m_{1},m_{2})}\binom{n/2}{\frac{1+m_{1}}{2}n/2}\binom{n/2}{\frac{1+m_{2}}{2}n/2}e^{\beta\frac{n}{2}E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m})}.

Notice that the binomial coefficients satisfy the general bounds, for some constants
c,C>0
,

(3.2)

\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}}e^{-\frac{n}{2}\mathcal{I}(m_{i})}\leq\binom{n/2}{\frac{1+m_{i}}{2}n/2}\leq Ce^{-\frac{n}{2}\mathcal{I}(m_{i})},\qquad\mbox{ for }i=1,2.

Since we are interested in the behavior of the partition function on the exponential scale and since it is a sum of only
|\Gamma_{n/2}^{2}|=(n+1)^{2}
positive terms, we can estimate
\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}
by keeping only its
dominant term, and get from the above estimates that

\displaystyle\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}}\exp\left\{\frac{n}{2}\sup_{{\bf m}\in[-1,1]^{2}}G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})\right\}\leq\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}

\displaystyle\leq C(n+1)^{2}\exp\left\{\frac{n}{2}\sup_{{\bf m}\in[-1,1]^{2}}G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})\right\},

where the lower bound is obtained by the continuity in
{\bf m}
of
G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})
through a standard argument.
By the definition of free energy in (
2.5
), taking the limit for
n\to\infty

and using that
\lim_{n\to\infty}G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}=G_{\hat{\alpha},\beta}
uniformly in
n
,
we finally get

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}=\frac{1}{2\beta}\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{{\bf m}\in[-1,1]^{2}}G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})=\frac{1}{2\beta}\sup_{{\bf m}\in[-1,1]^{2}}G_{\hat{\alpha},\beta}({\bf m}).

∎

3.2.2.
Maxima of the free energy functional

In view of Lemma
6
, the analysis of the maximal values of
G_{\alpha,\beta}({\bf m})
becomes central to providing a precise asymptotic formula for the partition function and then establishing proper limit theorems for magnetization.

Proposition 7
.

Let
\beta>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1]
. Define

(3.3)

\displaystyle\beta^{*}(\alpha)=\frac{2}{1-\alpha}\frac{\operatorname{arctanh}(t_{*})}{t_{*}},

where
t_{*}\in(0,1)
is such that

(3.4)

\displaystyle(1-t_{*}^{2})\frac{\operatorname{arctanh}(t_{*})}{t_{*}}=\frac{1-\alpha}{1+\alpha}.

Then the functional
G_{\alpha,\beta}({\bf m})
defined in (
2.8
) admits one, two, or four local maximizers. In particular:

(i)

for
\beta\leq\frac{2}{1+\alpha}
,
{\bf m}^{0}
is the unique maximizer;

(ii)

for
\beta\in\left(\frac{2}{1+\alpha},\beta^{*}(\alpha)\right]
, there are two (symmetric) global maximizers,
{\bf m}^{1}
and
{\bf m}^{2}
;

(iii)

for
\beta>\beta^{*}(\alpha)
, there are two (symmetric) global maximizers,
{\bf m}^{1}
and
{\bf m}^{2}
, and two (anti-symmetric) local maximizers,
{\bf m}^{3}
and
{\bf m}^{4}
.

To prove the above proposition, we need the following technical lemma.

Lemma 8
.

For
u\in(0,1)
, define

\phi(u)=\frac{\operatorname{arctanh}(u)}{u}
and

\psi(u)=(1-u^{2})\frac{\operatorname{arctanh}(u)}{u}
.
Then:

(i)

\phi(u)<\dfrac{1}{1-u^{2}}
, for every
u\in(0,1)
;

(ii)

\psi
is strictly decreasing on
(0,1)
, with
\lim_{u\to 0}\psi(u)=1
and

\lim_{u\to 1}\psi(u)=0
.

Proof.

For part (i), let

f(u)=\frac{u}{1-u^{2}}-\operatorname{arctanh}(u)
,
and observe that

f^{\prime}(u)=\frac{2u^{2}}{(1-u^{2})^{2}}>0
.
Hence
f(u)>0
for every
u\in(0,1)
, and (i) follows.

For part (ii), a direct computation gives

\psi^{\prime}(u)=\frac{u-(1+u^{2})\operatorname{arctanh}(u)}{u^{2}}
.
Since
\operatorname{arctanh}(u)>u
for every
u\in(0,1)
,

(1+u^{2})\operatorname{arctanh}(u)>u,

hence
\psi^{\prime}(u)<0
for every
u\in(0,1)
. The endpoint limits follow from the standard expansion

\operatorname{arctanh}(u)=u+O(u^{3})
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
7
.

We consider the stationary points of
G_{\alpha,\beta}({\bf m})
defined by
\nabla G_{\alpha,\beta}({\bf m})=0
, namely

(3.5)

\begin{split}\frac{\partial G_{\alpha,\beta}}{\partial m_{1}}({\bf m})&=\beta\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}m_{1}+\tfrac{\alpha}{2}m_{2}\Big)-\operatorname{arctanh}(m_{1})=0,\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{\partial G_{\alpha,\beta}}{\partial m_{2}}({\bf m})&=\beta\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}m_{2}+\tfrac{\alpha}{2}m_{1}\Big)-\operatorname{arctanh}(m_{2})=0.\end{split}

Equivalently,

(3.6)

\displaystyle m_{1}=\tanh\!\Big(\frac{\beta}{2}(m_{1}+\alpha m_{2})\Big),\qquad m_{2}=\tanh\!\Big(\frac{\beta}{2}(\alpha m_{1}+m_{2})\Big).

The Hessian matrix is

\displaystyle D^{2}G_{\alpha,\beta}({\bf m})=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\beta}{2}-\frac{1}{1-m_{1}^{2}}&\frac{\beta\alpha}{2}\\
\frac{\beta\alpha}{2}&\frac{\beta}{2}-\frac{1}{1-m_{2}^{2}}\end{pmatrix}\,.

First we show that
{\bf m}^{0}
is the unique maximizer if and only if
\beta\leq\frac{2}{1+\alpha}
. By computing the Hessian in
{\bf m}^{0}
we obtain

D^{2}G_{\alpha,\beta}(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\beta}{2}-1&\frac{\beta\alpha}{2}\\
\frac{\beta\alpha}{2}&\frac{\beta}{2}-1\end{pmatrix}\,,

and the claim follows immediately from the signs of the two eigenvalues
\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{\beta}{2}(1\pm\alpha)-1
.

Next, we establish the existence of two symmetric critical points,
{\bf m}^{1}
and
{\bf m}^{2}
, which are global maximizers whenever
\beta>\frac{2}{1+\alpha}
. Let
{\bf m}^{1}=(m_{g},m_{g})
, with
m_{g}>0
, be the symmetric solution of
\nabla G_{\alpha,\beta}({\bf m})=0
. Using (
3.6
), we derive the condition

(3.7)

\displaystyle m_{g}=\tanh\!\Big(\frac{\beta}{2}(1+\alpha)m_{g}\Big),

which has nonzero solution if and only if
\beta>\frac{2}{1+\alpha}
. When this holds, by computing the Hessian in
{\bf m}^{1}
, we obtain

D^{2}G_{\alpha,\beta}(m_{g},m_{g})=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\beta}{2}-\frac{1}{1-m_{g}^{2}}&\frac{\beta\alpha}{2}\\
\frac{\beta\alpha}{2}&\frac{\beta}{2}-\frac{1}{1-m_{g}^{2}}\end{pmatrix}\,,

hence the eigenvalues are equal to
\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{\beta}{2}(1\pm\alpha_{n})-\frac{1}{1-m_{g}^{2}}
.
By (
3.7
),

\frac{\beta}{2}(1+\alpha)=\frac{\operatorname{arctanh}(m_{g})}{m_{g}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\lambda_{+}=\frac{\operatorname{arctanh}(m_{g})}{m_{g}}-\frac{1}{1-m_{g}^{2}}.

By Lemma
8
(i), it follows that
\lambda_{-}<\lambda_{+}<0
.
Since both eigenvalues are negative,
{\bf m}^{1}
is a maximizer. By symmetry, the same holds for
{\bf m}^{2}=(-m_{g},-m_{g})
.

It remains to show that there exists a value
\beta^{*}(\alpha)
, defined as in (
3.3
), such that for
\beta>\beta^{*}(\alpha)
, two additional (antisymmetric) maximizers,
{\bf m}^{3}
and
{\bf m}^{4}
, appear.
Let
{\bf m}^{3}=(m_{l},-m_{l})
, with
m_{l}>0
, be the antisymmetric solution of
\nabla G_{\alpha,\beta}({\bf m})=0
. Using (
3.6
), we derive the condition

(3.8)

\displaystyle m_{l}=\tanh\!\Big(\frac{\beta}{2}(1-\alpha)m_{l}\Big),

which has nonzero solution if and only if
\beta>\frac{2}{1-\alpha}
. When this holds, by computing the Hessian in
{\bf m}^{3}
, we obtain

D^{2}G_{\alpha,\beta}(m_{l},-m_{l})=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\beta}{2}-\frac{1}{1-m_{l}^{2}}&\frac{\beta\alpha}{2}\\
\frac{\beta\alpha}{2}&\frac{\beta}{2}-\frac{1}{1-m_{l}^{2}}\end{pmatrix}\,,

which has eigenvalues
\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{\beta}{2}(1\pm\alpha)-\frac{1}{1-m_{l}^{2}}

By (
3.8
) and Lemma
8
(i), we get

\frac{\beta}{2}(1-\alpha)=\frac{\operatorname{arctanh}(m_{l})}{m_{l}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\lambda_{-}=\frac{\operatorname{arctanh}(m_{l})}{m_{l}}-\frac{1}{1-m_{l}^{2}}<0.

We finally show that
\lambda_{+}<0
whenever
\beta>\beta^{*}(\alpha)
.
By (
3.8
), we get

\lambda_{+}=\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{\operatorname{arctanh}(m_{l})}{m_{l}}-\frac{1}{1-m_{l}^{2}},

hence
\lambda_{+}<0
if and only if

(1-m_{l}^{2})\frac{\operatorname{arctanh}(m_{l})}{m_{l}}<\frac{1-\alpha}{1+\alpha}.

Note that the left-hand side corresponds to the function
\psi
defined in Lemma
8
, and the right-hand side is in
(0,1)
. By Lemma
8
(ii) there exists a unique
t_{*}\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\lambda_{+}>0,&\qquad\text{ if }\,\,m_{l}<t_{*},\\
\lambda_{+}=0,&\qquad\text{ if }\,\,m_{l}=t_{*},\\
\lambda_{+}<0,&\qquad\text{ if }\,\,m_{l}>t_{*}.\end{cases}

Since by (
3.8
) the solution
t_{*}
is strictly increasing in
\beta
, there exists a unique value
\beta^{*}(\alpha)
such that

(3.9)

\displaystyle\beta^{*}(\alpha)=\frac{2}{(1-\alpha)}\frac{\operatorname{arctanh}(t_{*})}{t_{*}}\,.

In conclusion,
\lambda_{+}<0
for any
\beta<\beta^{*}(\alpha)
.
∎

The extremal case when
\beta>0
and
\alpha=0
, already analyzed in
[
23
]
, can be recovered from Lemma
9
by letting
\alpha_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Indeed, in this limit, the intermediate region
\left(\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{n}},\beta^{*}(\alpha_{n})\right]
collapses, since
\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{n}}\to 2
and
\beta^{*}(\alpha_{n})\to 2
, as we show in the next lemma. As a consequence, the system exhibits a single maximizer at high temperatures and four maximizers at low temperatures.
Moreover, the value of the antisymmetric local maxima becomes comparable to that of the global ones,
up to a factor
\varepsilon(\alpha_{n})
which vanishes as
\alpha_{n}\to 0
(see (
3.11
) and Lemma
10
).
In conclusion, in the low temperature regime, there are asymptotically four global maximizers.

Lemma 9
.

Let
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in(0,1]
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha_{n}=0
. Then
\beta^{*}(\alpha_{n})
defined in (
3.3
) satisfies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\beta^{*}(\alpha_{n})=2.

Proof.

By Taylor expanding around
0
both terms of the identity (
3.4
), we readily obtain

1-\frac{2}{3}t^{2}+O(t^{4})=1-2\alpha_{n}+2\alpha_{n}^{2}+O(\alpha_{n}^{3}),

which implies

t_{*}^{2}=3\alpha_{n}(1-\alpha_{n})+O(\alpha_{n}).

Plugging this value in (
3.9
), and applying again a Taylor expansion of
\operatorname{arctanh}(t_{*})
around
0
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\beta^{*}(\alpha_{n})=\frac{2}{1-\alpha_{n}}\left(1+\alpha_{n}(1-\alpha_{n})+O(\alpha_{n})\right)\to 2\,,\quad\mbox{ as }\alpha_{n}\to 0\,.

∎

Let us introduce the following notation for the value of
G_{\alpha,\beta}
in its local or global maximizers:

(3.10)

\begin{split}&M_{0}(\alpha_{n})=G_{\alpha,\beta}({\bf m}^{0}),\\
&M_{1}(\alpha_{n})=G_{\alpha,\beta}({\bf m}^{1})\equiv G_{\alpha,\beta}({\bf m}^{2}),\\
&M_{2}(\alpha_{n})=G_{\alpha,\beta}({\bf m}^{3})\equiv G_{\alpha,\beta}({\bf m}^{4}).\end{split}

Then we define the energy gap

(3.11)

\varepsilon(\alpha)=M_{1}(\alpha)-M_{2}(\alpha).

The following lemma shows that if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha_{n}=0
, then
\varepsilon(\alpha_{n})
decreases linearly with
\alpha_{n}
.

Lemma 10
.

Let
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in(0,1]
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha_{n}=0
, and for
\beta>\beta^{*}(\alpha_{n})
,
let
m^{*}>0
be the unique positive solution of the fixed-point equation
\mathrm{atanh}(m)=\frac{\beta}{2}m
.
Then, for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\alpha_{n})=(m^{*})^{2}\beta\,\alpha_{n}+o(\alpha_{n}).

Proof.

The stationary points of the functional
G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})
are computed in the proof of Proposition
7
.
In particular, they are obtained by solving the system
\nabla G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})=0
, i.e., (
3.5
).
Equating them to zero yields the system

(3.12)

\displaystyle\operatorname{arctanh}(m_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{2}\,(m_{1}+\alpha_{n}m_{2}),\qquad\operatorname{arctanh}(m_{2})=\frac{\beta}{2}\,(m_{2}+\alpha_{n}m_{1}).

For the symmetric maximizers
{\bf m}^{1}
and
{\bf m}^{2}
, which have
m_{1}=m_{2}=\pm m_{g}
, (
3.12
) gives that

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathrm{sym}}(m_{g},\alpha_{n})=\operatorname{arctanh}(m_{g})-\frac{\beta}{2}(1+\alpha_{n})m_{g}=0.

For the antisymmetric maximizers
{\bf m}^{3}
and
{\bf m}^{4}
, which have
m_{1}=-m_{2}=\pm m_{l}
, (
3.12
) gives

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathrm{as}}(m_{l},\alpha_{n})=\operatorname{arctanh}(m_{l})-\frac{\beta}{2}(1-\alpha_{n})m_{l}=0.

By explicit computations, we get

\displaystyle\partial_{m}\Phi_{\mathrm{sym}}(m^{*},0)

\displaystyle=\partial_{m}\Phi_{\mathrm{as}}(m^{*},0)=\frac{1}{1-(m^{*})^{2}}-\frac{\beta}{2},

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha_{n}}\Phi_{\mathrm{sym}}(m^{*},0)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\beta}{2}m^{*},\qquad\partial_{\alpha_{n}}\Phi_{\mathrm{as}}(m^{*},0)=+\frac{\beta}{2}m^{*}.

Let us explicitly stress the dependence of the maximizers on
\alpha_{n}
by introducing the notation
m_{g}=m_{g}(\alpha_{n})
and
m_{l}=m_{l}(\alpha_{n})
. Since
\partial_{m}\Phi_{\mathrm{sym}}(m^{*},0)\neq 0
for
\beta>2
, the Implicit Function Theorem implies that there exists
\tilde{\alpha}>0
such that, for all
\alpha_{n}\in[0,\tilde{\alpha})
, the functions
m_{g}(\alpha_{n}),m_{l}(\alpha_{n})\in C^{1}([0,\tilde{\alpha}))
. Differentiating
\Phi_{\mathrm{sym}}(m_{g}(\alpha_{n}),\alpha_{n})=0
and
\Phi_{\mathrm{as}}(m_{l}(\alpha_{n}),\alpha_{n})=0
with respect to
\alpha_{n}
, yields

\displaystyle\frac{dm_{g}}{d\alpha_{n}}\Big|_{\alpha_{n}=0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial_{\alpha_{n}}\Phi_{\mathrm{sym}}(m^{*},0)}{\partial_{m}\Phi_{\mathrm{sym}}(m^{*},0)}=\frac{\frac{\beta}{2}m^{*}}{\frac{1}{1-(m^{*})^{2}}-\frac{\beta}{2}},

\displaystyle\frac{dm_{l}}{d\alpha_{n}}\Big|_{\alpha_{n}=0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial_{\alpha_{n}}\Phi_{\mathrm{as}}(m^{*},0)}{\partial_{m}\Phi_{\mathrm{as}}(m^{*},0)}=-\frac{\frac{\beta}{2}m^{*}}{\frac{1}{1-(m^{*})^{2}}-\frac{\beta}{2}}\,.

Hence the Taylor expansions near
0
are

(3.13)

\begin{split}m_{g}(\alpha_{n})&=m^{*}+s\alpha_{n}+o(\alpha_{n}),\\[5.69054pt]
m_{l}(\alpha_{n})&=m^{*}+a\alpha_{n}+o(\alpha_{n}),\end{split}

with
s=-a=\frac{\frac{\beta}{2}m^{*}}{\frac{1}{1-(m^{*})^{2}}-\frac{\beta}{2}}
.

We now proceed to analyze the energy gap
\varepsilon(\alpha_{n}).
We start by noticing first the identity

G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})=G_{0,\beta}({\bf m})+\frac{\beta\alpha_{n}}{2}m_{1}m_{2},

which follows from (
2.6
) and (
2.8
). Thus

\displaystyle M_{1}(\alpha_{n})=G_{0,\beta}(m_{g}(\alpha_{n}),m_{g}(\alpha_{n}))+\frac{\beta\alpha_{n}}{2}(m_{g}(\alpha_{n}))^{2},

\displaystyle M_{2}(\alpha_{n})=G_{0,\beta}(-m_{l}(\alpha_{n}),m_{l}(\alpha_{n}))-\frac{\beta\alpha_{n}}{2}(m_{l}(\alpha_{n}))^{2}.

Since
G_{0,\beta}
is even in each coordinate, when expanding the arguments around
m^{*}
we get

G_{0,\beta}(m_{g}(\alpha_{n}),m_{g}(\alpha_{n}))=G_{0,\beta}(-m_{l}(\alpha_{n}),m_{l}(\alpha_{n}))+o(\alpha_{n}).

Hence

\varepsilon(\alpha_{n})=M_{1}(\alpha_{n})-M_{2}(\alpha_{n})=\frac{\beta\alpha_{n}}{2}\Big[(m_{g}(\alpha_{n}))^{2}+(m_{l}(\alpha_{n}))^{2}\Big]+o(\alpha_{n}).

Using again the expansions, we obtain

(m_{l}(\alpha_{n}))^{2}+(m_{g}(\alpha_{n}))^{2}=(m^{*})^{2}+(m^{*})^{2}+o(1)=2(m^{*})^{2}+o(1),

hence

\varepsilon(\alpha_{n})=(m^{*})^{2}\beta\,\alpha_{n}+o(\alpha_{n}).

∎

3.2.3.
Precise asymptotic of the partition function

We now want to provide an asymptotic formula for the partition function
\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}
. For
\beta>0
, let
k
be the number
of maximizers of
G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle k=\begin{cases}1,\qquad&\text{ if }\beta\leq\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{n}},\\
2,\qquad&\text{ if }\beta\in\left(\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{n}},\beta^{*}(\alpha_{n})\right],\\
4,\qquad&\text{ if }\beta>\beta^{*}(\alpha_{n}).\end{cases}

We partition the magnetization space
\Gamma_{n/2}^{2}
depending on the region of
\beta
.
In particular, for
\delta\in(1/3,1/2)
and
\delta_{c}\in(1/5,1/4)
, we define the following:

(3.14)

\begin{split}\mbox{ - \,\, if }\beta<\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{n}},\quad&B_{0}=\left\{{\bf m}\in\Gamma_{n/2}^{2}:\,\|{\bf m}-{\bf m}^{0}\|\leq\frac{1}{n^{\delta}}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{C}_{0}=\Gamma_{n/2}^{2}\setminus B_{0};\\
\mbox{ -\,\, if }\beta=\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{n}},\quad&B_{c}=\left\{{\bf m}\in\Gamma_{n/2}^{2}:\,\|{\bf m}-{\bf m}^{0}\|\leq\frac{1}{n^{\delta_{c}}}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{\tilde{C}}=\Gamma_{n/2}^{2}\setminus B_{c};\\
\mbox{ - \,\, if }\beta>\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{n}},\quad&B_{i}=\left\{{\bf m}\in\Gamma_{n/2}^{2}:\,\|{\bf m}-{\bf m}^{i}\|\leq\frac{1}{n^{\delta}}\right\},\,i=1,\ldots,k\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{C}=\Gamma_{n/2}^{2}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}B_{i}.\end{split}

Moreover, for
i=0,...,4
,
we set

(3.15)

R_{i}^{(n)}=\sqrt{n}\cdot B_{i},\qquad D_{i}^{(n)}=\sum_{{\bf x}\in R_{i}^{(n)}}\frac{4}{n}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}{\bf x}^{T}Q_{i}^{(n)}{\bf x}}}{\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}+m_{1}^{i}\right)^{2}}\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{x_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}+m_{2}^{i}\right)^{2}}},

where

\displaystyle Q_{i}^{(n)}=-\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\beta}{2}-\frac{1}{1-(m^{i}_{1})^{2}}&\frac{\beta\alpha_{n}}{2}\\
\frac{\beta\alpha_{n}}{2}&\frac{\beta}{2}-\frac{1}{1-(m^{i}_{2})^{2}}\end{pmatrix}=-\frac{1}{2}D^{2}G_{\alpha,\beta}({\bf m}^{i})

are positive defined matrices, being
{\bf m}^{i}
a maximizer.
Similarly, at criticality, we define

(3.16)

R_{c}^{(n)}={n^{1/4}}\cdot B_{c},\qquad D_{c}^{(n)}=\sum_{{\bf x}\in R_{c}^{(n)}}\frac{4}{n}\frac{e^{-2(x_{1}^{4}+x_{2}^{4})}}{\sqrt{1-\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}}\sqrt{1-\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}}}.

With this notation, we obtain the following asymptotic expression of the partition function.

Proposition 11
.

For
n
large enough, the partition function is given by

\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}=\begin{cases}\frac{e^{\frac{n}{2}M_{0}(\alpha_{n})}}{\pi}D_{0}^{(n)}(1+o(1)),\qquad&\text{if }\beta<\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{n}},\\[5.0pt]
\frac{e^{\frac{n}{2}M_{0}(\alpha_{n})}}{\pi}D_{c}^{(n)}(1+o(1)),\qquad&\text{if }\beta=\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{n}},\\[5.0pt]
\frac{e^{\frac{n}{2}M_{1}(\alpha_{n})}}{\pi}\sum_{i=1}^{2}D_{i}^{(n)}(1+o(1)),\qquad&\text{if }\beta\in\big(\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{n}},\beta^{*}(\alpha_{n})\big],\\[5.0pt]
\frac{e^{\frac{n}{2}M_{1}(\alpha_{n})}}{\pi}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2}D_{i}^{(n)}+e^{-\frac{n}{2}\varepsilon(\alpha_{n})}\sum_{i=3}^{4}D_{i}^{(n)}\right)(1+o(1)),\qquad&\text{if }\beta>\beta^{*}(\alpha_{n}).\end{cases}

where
\varepsilon(\alpha_{n})
is defined in
(
3.11
)
.

Proof.

We begin by assuming
\beta>\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{n}}
, which corresponds to the case of multiple maximizers, with
k\in\{2,4\}
.
From (
2.7
), we have

\displaystyle\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf m}\in\Gamma_{n/2}^{2}}\binom{n/2}{\frac{1+m_{1}}{2}n/2}\binom{n/2}{\frac{1+m_{2}}{2}n/2}e^{\frac{\beta n}{2}E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m})}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}(B_{i})+\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}(\mathcal{C}),

where we used the partition of
\Gamma_{n/2}^{2}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}B_{i}\cup\mathcal{C}
and the notation

\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}(A)=\sum_{{\bf m}\in A}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\sigma}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}:\\
{\bf m}({\sigma})={\bf m}\end{subarray}}e^{-\beta\bar{H}_{n}({\sigma})}\qquad\forall A\subseteq\Gamma_{n/2}^{2}\,.

We first analyze the contributions from
{\bf m}\in B_{i}
, and without loss of generality we consider
i=1
.
In
B_{1}
, the Stirling formula can be strengthened to the following asymptotical expression

(3.17)

\displaystyle\binom{n/2}{\frac{1+m_{j}}{2}n/2}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi n(1-m_{j}^{2})}}e^{-\frac{n}{2}\mathcal{I}(m_{j})}(1+o(1)),\quad\mbox{for }j=1,2,

so that

(3.18)

\displaystyle\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}(B_{1})=\sum_{{\bf m}\in B_{1}}\frac{4}{\pi n\sqrt{1-m_{1}^{2}}\sqrt{1-m_{2}^{2}}}e^{\frac{n}{2}G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})}(1+o(1)).

By Taylor expanding
G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})
around the global maximizer
{\bf m}^{1}=(m_{g},m_{g})
, we can write

(3.19)

\displaystyle G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})=

\displaystyle G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m}^{1})+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\beta}{2}-\frac{1}{1-m_{g}^{2}}\right)\|{\bf m}-{\bf m}^{1}\|^{2}+\frac{\beta\alpha_{n}}{2}(m_{1}-m_{g})(m_{2}-m_{g})+O(\|{\bf m}-{\bf m}^{1}\|^{3}).

Substituting this term in (
3.18
), and by the change of variable
{\bf{x}}=\sqrt{n}({\bf{m}}-{\bf{m}}^{1})
, we then get

(3.20)

\begin{split}\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}(B_{1})&=\frac{e^{\frac{n}{2}G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m}^{1})}}{\pi}\sum_{{\bf x}\in R_{1}^{(n)}}\frac{4}{n\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}+m_{g}\right)^{2}}\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{x_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}+m_{g}\right)^{2}}}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\,\,\,\cdot e^{\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\beta}{2}-\frac{1}{1-m_{g}^{2}}\right)\|{\bf x}\|^{2}+\frac{\beta\alpha_{n}}{4}x_{1}x_{2}}(1+o(1))\\
&=\frac{e^{\frac{n}{2}M_{1}(\alpha_{n})}}{\pi}D_{1}^{(n)}(1+o(1)),\end{split}

where we also used that, by definition (
3.14
),
n\|{\bf m}-{\bf m}^{1}\|^{3}\leq n^{1-3\delta}=o(1)
if
\delta>1/3
.

In the same way, we can analogously estimate
Z_{n,\beta}(B_{i})
, for
i=2,...,k
.

We conclude by analyzing the contribution to
\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}
coming from the configurations in
\mathcal{C}
. Suppose
k=4
and divide
\mathcal{C}
into the following four disjoint subsets:

(3.21)

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{C}_{1}=\{{\bf m}\in\mathcal{C}\,|\,m_{1},m_{2}\geq 0\},&\mathcal{C}_{2}=\{{\bf m}\in\mathcal{C}\,|\,m_{1},m_{2}<0\},\\
\mathcal{C}_{3}=\{{\bf m}\in\mathcal{C}\,|\,m_{1}<0,m_{2}\geq 0\},&\mathcal{C}_{4}=\{{\bf m}\in\mathcal{C}\,|\,m_{1}\geq 0,m_{2}<0\}.\end{array}

We already stress that when
k=2
, the last two subsets are excluded from
\mathcal{C}
, but the remainder of the proof proceeds in the same way.

We introduce the following notation for the local maxima of
G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}
on the above subsets:

\begin{split}&g_{1}({\bf m})=\max_{{\bf m}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m}),\qquad g_{2}({\bf m})=\max_{{\bf m}\in\mathcal{C}_{2}}G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m}),\\
&g_{3}({\bf m})=\max_{{\bf m}\in\mathcal{C}_{3}}G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m}),\qquad g_{4}({\bf m})=\max_{{\bf m}\in\mathcal{C}_{4}}G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m}).\end{split}

By the Stirling bound (
3.2
), recalling the notation introduced in (
3.10
), we get

\displaystyle\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}(\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle\leq Cn^{2}\left(e^{\frac{n}{2}M_{1}(\alpha_{n})}\sum_{i=1,2}e^{-\frac{n}{2}(M_{1}(\alpha_{n})-g_{i}({\bf m}))}+e^{\frac{n}{2}M_{2}(\alpha_{n})}\sum_{i=3,4}e^{-\frac{n}{2}(M_{2}(\alpha_{n})-g_{i}({\bf m}))}\right).

Note that by construction, for all
i=1,\ldots,4
and for all
{\bf m}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}
,

n|M_{i}(\alpha_{n})-g_{i}({\bf m})|>n\|{\bf m}-{\bf m}^{i}\|^{2}>n^{1-2\delta}.

Hence we obtain

(3.22)

\displaystyle\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}(\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle\leq Cn^{2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}n^{1-2\delta}}(2e^{\frac{n}{2}M_{1}(\alpha_{n})}+2e^{\frac{n}{2}M_{2}(\alpha_{n})})<e^{\frac{n}{2}M_{1}(\alpha_{n})}o(1)+e^{\frac{n}{2}M_{2}(\alpha_{n})}o(1),

where in the last inequality, we use that
\delta<1/2
.
Together, (
3.20
) and (
3.22
) show that

\displaystyle\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}=\left(\frac{e^{\frac{n}{2}M_{1}(\alpha_{n})}}{\pi}\sum_{i=1}^{2}D_{i}^{(n)}+\textbf{1}_{\{k>2\}}\frac{e^{\frac{n}{2}M_{2}(\alpha_{n})}}{\pi}\sum_{i=3}^{4}D_{i}^{(n)}\right)(1+o(1)),

which concludes the analysis for
k=2,4
.

A similar argument applies for
k=1
, whenever
\beta<\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{n}}
.

In the critical regime
\beta=\frac{2}{1+\alpha_{n}}
, when we still have the unique maximizer
{\bf m}^{0}
,
we must instead perform a Taylor expansion around
{\bf m}^{0}
up to the fourth order,
since all derivatives of lower order vanish. Equation (
3.19
) then becomes

(3.23)

\displaystyle G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})=

\displaystyle G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m}^{0})-2(m_{1}^{4}+m_{2}^{4})+O(||{\bf m}||^{5}).

Recalling
{\bf m}^{0}=(0,0)
and applying the change of variable
{\bf{x}}=n^{1/4}{\bf{m}}
, we obtain

\begin{split}\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}(B_{c})&=\frac{e^{\frac{n}{2}G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m}^{0})}}{\pi}\sum_{{\bf x}\in R_{c}^{(n)}}\frac{4}{n\sqrt{1-\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}}\sqrt{1-\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}}}e^{-2||\textbf{x}||_{4}^{4}}(1+o(1))\\
&=\frac{e^{\frac{n}{2}M_{0}(\alpha_{n})}}{\pi}D_{c}^{(n)}(1+o(1)),\end{split}

where we used that, by definition (
3.14
),
n\|{\bf m}\|^{5}\leq n^{1-5\delta_{c}}=o(1)
with
\delta_{c}>\frac{1}{5}
. The remainder of the proof follows the arguments used for
k=2,4
.
∎

3.3.
Proof of of Theorem
5

Let
\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}
be the measure on
\Gamma_{n/2}^{2}
induced by
\bar{\mu}_{n,\beta}
under the map
\mathcal{X}_{n}\to\Gamma_{n/2}^{2}
. More precisely,

(3.24)

\displaystyle\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}({\bf m})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}:\\
{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)={\bf m}\end{subarray}}\bar{\mu}_{n,\beta}(\sigma).

We treat in detail only the case where
\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha_{n}=0
and
\beta>\beta_{c}=2
, the other being analogous.

To prove the convergence, it suffices to analyze, for any continuous bounded function
\varphi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{{\bf m}\in{\Gamma}_{n/2}^{2}}\varphi({\bf m})\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}({\bf m})\,.

By Proposition
7
and Lemma
9
,
the function
G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}
presents, for all
n
large enough and
\beta>2
, four maximizers.
With the same notation as introduced in (
3.14
), we can write

(3.25)

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf m}\in\Gamma_{n/2}^{2}}\varphi({\bf m})\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}({\bf m})=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\sum_{{\bf m}\in B_{i}}\varphi({\bf m})\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}({\bf m})+\sum_{{\bf m}\in\mathcal{C}}\varphi({\bf m})\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}({\bf m}).

We begin with analyzing the sum over
B_{1}
in (
3.25
). By Proposition
11
, it holds

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf m}\in B_{1}}\varphi({\bf m})\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}({\bf m})=\sum_{{\bf m}\in B_{1}}\varphi({\bf m})\frac{4}{n\sqrt{1-m_{1}^{2}}\sqrt{1-m_{2}^{2}}}\frac{e^{\frac{n}{2}(G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})-M_{1})}}{\sum_{i=1}^{2}D_{i}^{(n)}+e^{-\frac{n}{2}\varepsilon(\alpha_{n})}\sum_{i=3}^{4}D_{i}^{(n)}}(1+o(1)).

From the Taylor expansion of
G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})
around
{\bf m}^{1}
and the change of variable
{\bf x}=\sqrt{n}({\bf m}-{\bf m}^{1})
, we get

(3.26)

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf m}\in B_{1}}\varphi({\bf m})\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}({\bf m})=\sum_{{\bf m}\in B_{1}}\varphi\left(\frac{{\bf x}}{\sqrt{n}}+{\bf m}^{1}\right)\frac{D_{1}^{(n)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{2}D_{i}^{(n)}+e^{-\frac{n}{2}\varepsilon(\alpha_{n})}\sum_{i=3}^{4}D_{i}^{(n)}}(1+o(1)).

Note that, by definitions (
3.15
) and (
3.16
), the terms

D_{i}^{(n)}
converge, for
n\to\infty
, to the following constants

(3.27)

\displaystyle D_{i}=\lim_{n\to\infty}D_{i}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-(m_{1}^{i})^{2}}\sqrt{1-(m_{2}^{i})^{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}{\bf x}^{T}Q_{i}{\bf x}}d{\bf x}\,,

where

\displaystyle Q_{i}=\lim_{n\to\infty}Q_{i}^{(n)}=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\beta}{2}-\frac{1}{1-(m^{*})^{2}}&0\\
0&\frac{\beta}{2}-\frac{1}{1-(m^{*})^{2}}\end{pmatrix},&\qquad\text{if }\,\,1\leq i\leq 4,\\
-\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\beta}{2}-1&0\\
0&\frac{\beta}{2}-1\end{pmatrix},&\qquad\text{if }\,\,i=0.\end{cases}

As a consequence, together with Lemma
10
, we get that the denominator in (
3.26
) converges to

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2}D_{i}^{(n)}+e^{-\frac{n}{2}\varepsilon(\alpha_{n})}\sum_{i=3}^{4}D_{i}^{(n)}\right)=\begin{cases}2D_{1},\qquad&\text{ if }\alpha_{n}\gg 1/n,\\
2D_{1}(1+e^{-c}),\qquad&\text{ if }\alpha_{n}=c/n,\\
4D_{1},\qquad&\text{ if }\alpha_{n}\ll 1/n,\end{cases}

hence

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{{\bf m}\in B_{1}}\varphi({\bf m})\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}({\bf m})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\varphi({\bf m}^{1}),&\qquad\text{ if }\alpha_{n}\gg 1/n,\\
\frac{1}{2(1+e^{-c})}\varphi({\bf m}^{1}),&\qquad\text{ if }\alpha_{n}=c/n,\\
\frac{1}{4}\varphi({\bf m}^{1}),&\qquad\text{ if }\alpha_{n}\ll 1/n.\end{cases}

Arguing as above, we obtain the same result for the sum over
B_{2}
in (
3.25
).

Next, we analyze the sum over
B_{3}
in (
3.25
). With an argument similar to the previous one, and recalling
M_{1}(\alpha_{n})=M_{2}(\alpha_{n})+\varepsilon(\alpha_{n})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf m}\in B_{3}}\varphi({\bf m})\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}({\bf m})

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{n}{2}\varepsilon(\alpha_{n})}\sum_{{\bf m}\in B_{3}}\varphi({\bf m})e^{\frac{n}{2}(G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})-M_{2}(\alpha_{n}))}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\cdot\frac{4}{n\sqrt{1-m_{1}^{2}}\sqrt{1-m_{2}^{2}}}\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{2}D_{i}^{(n)}+e^{-\frac{n}{2}\varepsilon(\alpha_{n})}\sum_{i=3}^{4}D_{i}^{(n)}}(1+o(1)).

From the Taylor expansion of
G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})
around
{\bf m}^{3}
and the change of variable
{\bf x}=\sqrt{n}({\bf m}-{\bf m}^{3})
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf m}\in B_{3}}\varphi({\bf m})\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}({\bf m})=e^{-\frac{n}{2}\varepsilon(\alpha_{n})}\sum_{{\bf m}\in B_{3}}\varphi\left(\frac{{\bf x}}{\sqrt{n}}+{\bf m}^{3}\right)\frac{D_{3}^{(n)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{2}D_{i}^{(n)}+e^{-\frac{n}{2}\varepsilon(\alpha_{n})}\sum_{i=3}^{4}D_{i}^{(n)}}(1+o(1)),

which together with (
3.27
) and Lemma
10
implies that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{{\bf m}\in B_{3}}\varphi({\bf m})\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}({\bf m})=\begin{cases}0,&\qquad\text{ if }\alpha_{n}\gg 1/n,\\
\frac{e^{-c}}{2(1+e^{-c})}\varphi({\bf m}^{3}),&\qquad\text{ if }\alpha_{n}=c/n,\\
\frac{1}{4}\varphi({\bf m}^{3}),&\qquad\text{ if }\alpha_{n}\ll 1/n.\end{cases}

Arguing as above, we obtain the same result for the sum over
B_{4}
in (
3.25
).

Finally, we analyze the last sum in (
3.25
). Since
\varphi
is bounded, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf m}\in\mathcal{C}}\varphi({\bf m})\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}({\bf m})\leq||\varphi||_{\infty}\sum_{{\bf m}\in\mathcal{C}}\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}({\bf m}).

Using the partition of
\mathcal{C}
given in (
3.21
),
we obtain four analogous terms. Let us first consider
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and note that, from the Stirling bound (
3.2
) and Lemma
15
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf m}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}({\bf m})=\frac{C}{\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf m}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\end{subarray}}e^{\frac{n}{2}G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})}\leq\frac{Ce^{\frac{n}{2}M_{1}(\alpha_{n})}}{\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}}\sum_{{\bf m}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}e^{-\frac{n}{2}(M_{1}(\alpha_{n})-G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m}))},

for some constant
C>0
. Since
{\bf m}\in\mathcal{C}
and
\delta<1/2
, we have that,
for some
\tilde{C}>0
,

n|M_{1}(\alpha_{n})-G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})|\geq\tilde{C}n\|{\bf m}^{1}-{\bf m}\|^{2}\geq\tilde{C}n^{1-2\delta}=o(1)
. Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf m}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}({\bf m})\leq\frac{e^{\frac{n}{2}M_{1}(\alpha_{n})}}{\bar{Z}_{n,\beta}}o(1)\,.

The same holds when considering the remaining sums over
\mathcal{C}_{2}
,
\mathcal{C}_{3}
, and
\mathcal{C}_{4}
, concluding
the proof for the regime
\beta>2
, with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha_{n}=0
.

The argument extends in a standard way to the regime
\beta\leq 2
, yielding the claimed result.
In particular, it can be adapted to handle the critical regime as well, by observing that

\displaystyle D_{c}^{(n)}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{-2(x_{1}^{4}+x_{2}^{4})^{1/4}}\,dx_{1}dx_{2}=2^{-\frac{5}{2}}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{2}.

∎

4.
Typical configurations and concentration of the random Gibbs measure

In this section, we present four lemmas needed for the proof of Theorem
1
on the magnetization law of the Ising model on the 2-SBM, and then provide the proof of the theorem.

In particular, in Section
4.1
we introduce a suitable notion of typical configurations and establish concentration estimates for the random environment, showing that these properties hold with overwhelming probability uniformly over all configurations.

In Section
4
, we compare the Hamiltonian of the model with its mean-field counterpart and show that their difference is uniformly negligible. This allows us to transfer the asymptotic behavior of the magnetization measure
from the bipartite CW model to Ising model on the 2-SBM, and then complete the proof of Theorem
1
,
which is given in Section
4.3
.

4.1.
Typical configurations

We begin by introducing a convenient partition of the set of edges based on the alignment of spins. For
\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
, define

\begin{split}&\mathcal{E}_{I}^{\pm}(\sigma)=\{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{I}\mid\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\pm 1\},\\
&\mathcal{E}_{E}^{\pm}(\sigma)=\{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{E}\mid\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\pm 1\}.\end{split}

We compute the cardinalities of these sets in terms of magnetization. For
j=1,2
, let
r_{j}
and
s_{j}
denote the numbers of vertices in
V_{j}
with spins
+1
and
-1
, respectively, in a configuration
\sigma
. Setting
\ell=\frac{n}{2}
, and dropping for notational convenience
the dependence on
{\sigma}
and
n
, we have
r_{j}+s_{j}=\ell
and
r_{j}-s_{j}=\ell m_{j}
, so that

r_{j}=\ell\frac{1+m_{j}}{2},\qquad s_{j}=\ell\frac{1-m_{j}}{2}.

A direct computation then gives

(4.1)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{I}^{+}(\sigma)|=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\binom{r_{j}}{2}+\binom{s_{j}}{2}=\frac{\ell^{2}}{2}\left(1+\frac{\|{\bf m}\|^{2}}{2}\right)-\ell,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{E}^{+}(\sigma)|=r_{1}r_{2}+s_{1}s_{2}=\frac{\ell^{2}}{2}(1+m_{1}m_{2}).

We further denote by
\mathcal{E}^{+}(\sigma)=\mathcal{E}_{I}^{+}(\sigma)\,\cup\,\mathcal{E}_{E}^{+}(\sigma)
the set of aligned pairs, and by
\mathcal{E}^{-}(\sigma)
its complement. With this notation, the Hamiltonian can be rewritten as

(4.2)

\displaystyle H_{n}(\sigma)=-\frac{1}{np}\left\{\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}^{+}(\sigma)}(\xi_{ij}+\zeta_{ij})-\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}^{-}(\sigma)}(\xi_{ij}+\zeta_{ij})\right\}.

We now introduce a set of typical environments where the random sums appearing above are well concentrated around their expectations. Define

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\left\{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}:\left|\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}^{+}(\sigma)}\left(\xi_{ij}+\zeta_{ij}\right)-p\left(\left|\mathcal{E}_{I}^{+}({\sigma})\right|+\alpha_{n}\left|\mathcal{E}_{E}^{+}({\sigma})\right|\right)\right|\leq\rho_{n}\,p\left(\left|\mathcal{E}_{I}^{+}({\sigma})\right|+\alpha_{n}\left|\mathcal{E}_{E}^{+}({\sigma})\right|\right)\right\},

where
\rho_{n}>0
is such that
\rho_{n}\to 0
and
\rho_{n}\geq\frac{3}{\sqrt{pn}}
, and set

(4.4)

\displaystyle\bar{\Omega}_{n}=\bigcap_{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\,\sigma\in\mathcal{A}\right\}\subset\Omega.

Note that the definition of
\bar{\Omega}_{n}
only involves the contribution from
\mathcal{E}^{+}(\sigma)
. However, this is sufficient to control also the contribution from
\mathcal{E}^{-}(\sigma)
. Indeed, the total sum
\sum_{(i,j)\in E}(\xi_{ij}+\zeta_{ij})
is a sum of independent Bernoulli random variables and therefore concentrates around its expectation with overwhelming probability by Chernoff bound. Since this quantity does not depend on
\sigma
, such concentration holds uniformly over all configurations. Moreover,

(4.5)

\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}^{-}(\sigma)}(\xi_{ij}+\zeta_{ij})=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}(\xi_{ij}+\zeta_{ij})-\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}^{+}(\sigma)}(\xi_{ij}+\zeta_{ij}),

which implies that an analogous bound holds for
\mathcal{E}^{-}(\sigma)
uniformly in
\sigma
.

The next lemma shows that the sequence of events
(\bar{\Omega}_{n})_{n}
is asymptotically typical, in the sense that it eventually occurs with probability one.

Lemma 12
.

Under assumptions (
2.2
),

\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{\Omega}^{c}_{n}\right)\leq c_{0}n\left(e^{-nc_{1}/2}+e^{-nc_{2}/2}\right),

with
c_{0},c_{1},c_{2}>0
constants.

Proof.

By definitions (
4.3
) and (
4.4
), we can write
\bar{\Omega}^{c}_{n}
as

\displaystyle\bigcup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\left|\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}^{+}(\sigma)}(\xi_{ij}+\zeta_{ij})-p(|\mathcal{E}_{I}^{+}({\sigma})|+\alpha_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{E}^{+}({\sigma})|)\right|\geq\rho_{n}p(|\mathcal{E}_{I}^{+}({\sigma})|+\alpha_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{E}^{+}({\sigma})|)\right\}.

Hence,

(4.6)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{\Omega}^{c}_{n}\right)&\leq\sum_{\sigma}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}^{+}(\sigma)}(\xi_{ij}+\zeta_{ij})\geq p(1+\rho_{n})(|\mathcal{E}_{I}^{+}({\sigma})|+\alpha_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{E}^{+}({\sigma})|)\right)\\
&\quad+\sum_{\sigma}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}^{+}(\sigma)}(\xi_{ij}+\zeta_{ij})\leq p(1-\rho_{n})(|\mathcal{E}_{I}^{+}({\sigma})|+\alpha_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{E}^{+}({\sigma})|)\right).\end{split}

Each addend in the first sum in (
4.6
) can be bounded by the exponential Markov inequality as

(4.7)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}^{+}(\sigma)}(\xi_{ij}+\zeta_{ij})\geq(1+\rho_{n})p(|\mathcal{E}_{I}^{+}({\sigma})|+\alpha_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{E}^{+}({\sigma})|)\right)\\
&\leq e^{-\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\{(\left|\mathcal{E}_{I}^{+}({\sigma})\right|+\alpha_{n}\left|\mathcal{E}_{E}^{+}({\sigma})\right|)(1+\rho_{n})tp-\left|\mathcal{E}_{I}^{+}({\sigma})\right|\log\left(1-p+pe^{t}\right)-\left|\mathcal{E}_{E}^{+}({\sigma})\right|\log\left(1-\alpha_{n}p+\alpha_{n}pe^{t}\right)\right\}},\end{split}

since a Bernoulli random variable
X
with parameter
p
satisfies
\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tX}\right]=1-p+pe^{t}
. Moreover, for each
t\geq 0
and
\alpha_{n},p\in[0,1]
, by the concavity of the logarithm we have that

\log\left(1-p\alpha_{n}+p\alpha_{n}e^{t}\right)\geq\alpha_{n}\log\left(1-p+pe^{t}\right).

We then get that (
4.7
) can be bounded by

e^{-(\left|\mathcal{E}_{I}^{+}({\sigma})\right|+\alpha_{n}\left|\mathcal{E}_{E}^{+}({\sigma})\right|)\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\{(1+\rho_{n})tp-\log\left(1-p+pe^{t}\right)\right\}}\leq e^{-(\left|\mathcal{E}_{I}^{+}({\sigma})\right|+\alpha_{n}\left|\mathcal{E}_{E}^{+}({\sigma})\right|)\mathscr{I}_{p}(p(1+\rho_{n}))},

where

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}_{y}(x)=x\log\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)+(1-x)\log\left(\frac{1-x}{1-y}\right).

Analogously, each addend in the second sum in (
4.6
) can be bounded as

\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}^{+}(\sigma)}(\xi_{ij}+\zeta_{ij})\leq(1-\rho_{n})p\left(|\mathcal{E}_{I}^{+}({\sigma})|+\alpha_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{E}^{+}({\sigma})|)\right)\leq e^{-(\left|\mathcal{E}_{I}^{+}({\sigma})\right|+\alpha_{n}\left|\mathcal{E}_{E}^{+}({\sigma})\right|)\mathscr{I}_{p}(p(1-\rho_{n}))}.

Thus, using (
4.1
) and (
3.2
), there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{\Omega}^{c}_{n}\right)\leq&\sum_{{\bf m}}Ce^{-\frac{n}{2}\mathcal{I}({\bf m})}e^{-\frac{n^{2}}{4}\left(2E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m})+1+\alpha_{n}\right)\mathscr{I}_{p}(p(1+\rho_{n}))}\\
&+\sum_{{\bf m}}Ce^{-\frac{n}{2}\mathcal{I}({\bf m})}e^{-\frac{n^{2}}{4}\left(2E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m})+1+\alpha_{n}\right)\mathscr{I}_{p}(p(1-\rho_{n}))}.\end{split}

Since
E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m})
and
\mathscr{I}_{p}(p(1-\rho_{n}))
are positive convex functions, and
\mathcal{I}({\bf m})
is a convex function where the minimum is attained in
-2\log(2)
, we have

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{n}{2}(\mathcal{I}({\bf m})+nE_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m})\mathscr{I}_{p}(p(1-\rho_{n})))}\leq e^{n\log(2)}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{\Omega}^{c}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{{\bf m}}e^{-\frac{n}{2}(\frac{n}{2}(1+\alpha_{n})\mathscr{I}_{p}(p(1+\rho_{n}))-2\log(2))}+C\sum_{{\bf m}}e^{-\frac{n}{2}(\frac{n}{2}(1+\alpha_{n})\mathscr{I}_{p}(p(1-\rho_{n}))-2\log(2))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Cn^{2}}{4}\left(e^{-\frac{n}{2}(\frac{n}{2}\mathscr{I}_{p}(p(1+\rho_{n}))-2\log(2))}+e^{-\frac{n}{2}(\frac{n}{2}\mathscr{I}_{p}(p(1-\rho_{n}))-2\log(2))}\right),

since
\alpha_{n}>0
and
{\bf m}\in\Gamma_{n/2}^{2}
.
When analyzing
\mathscr{I}_{p}(p(1\pm\rho_{n}))
, by Taylor expansion, we have

\begin{split}\mathscr{I}_{p}(p(1+\rho_{n}))&\geq p\left(\rho_{n}+\frac{\rho_{n}^{2}}{2}-\frac{\rho_{n}^{3}}{6}\right)+(1-p)\left(-\frac{p}{1-p}\rho_{n}\right)\geq p\frac{\rho_{n}^{2}}{3},\\
\mathscr{I}_{p}(p(1-\rho_{n}))&\geq p\left(-\rho_{n}+\frac{\rho_{n}^{2}}{2}\right)+(1-p)\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\rho_{n}\right)=p\frac{\rho_{n}^{2}}{2}.\end{split}

Therefore, there exist two constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}\mathscr{I}_{p}(p(1+\rho_{n}))-2\log 2\geq\frac{n}{6}p\rho_{n}^{2}-2\log 2\geq c_{1},

\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}\mathscr{I}_{p}(p(1-\rho_{n}))-2\log 2\geq\frac{n}{4}p\rho_{n}^{2}-2\log 2\geq c_{2},

where the last inequalities follow by assuming that
\rho_{n}
decreases to zero more slowly than
3/\sqrt{pn}
. Hence, we can conclude

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{\Omega}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Cn^{2}}{4}\left(e^{-nc_{1}/2}+e^{-nc_{2}/2}\right).

∎

As an application of the above result, we obtain the following characterization of typical configurations.

Lemma 13
.

Let
\Omega^{*}=\left\{\omega\in\Omega\mid\exists\,n_{0}:\,\omega\in\bar{\Omega}_{n}\,\text{ for all }\,n\geq n_{0}\right\}
.
Under assumptions (
2.2
),
\mathbb{P}(\Omega^{*})=1
.

Proof.

Note that
\mathbb{P}\left(\Omega^{*}\right)=1-\mathbb{P}\left(\left(\Omega^{*}\right)^{c}\right)
with

\left(\Omega^{*}\right)^{c}=\left\{\omega\in\Omega\mid\forall\,n_{0}\,\exists\,n\geq n_{0}\text{ s.t. }\omega\in\bar{\Omega}^{c}_{n}\right\}
.
Then

0\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left(\Omega^{*}\right)^{c}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\limsup_{n\to\infty}\bar{\Omega}^{c}_{n}\right)=0
,
where the last equality follows from Borel-Cantelli Theorem and by Lemma (
12
).
∎

4.2.
Concentration of the random Gibbs measure

We now compare the Hamiltonian of the Ising model on the 2-SBM (
2.1
)
with the Hamiltonian of the bipartite CW model (
2.3
), its mean-field counterpart. For
\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
, let

(4.8)

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}(\sigma)=H_{n}(\sigma)-\bar{H}_{n}(\sigma).

The following lemma provides a bound on
\Delta_{n}(\sigma)

for typical configurations
{\sigma}
.

Lemma 14
.

Under assumptions (
2.2
), for every
\omega\in\Omega^{*}
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
,

\displaystyle|\Delta_{n}(\sigma)|\leq\frac{3}{2}n\rho_{n},

with
\rho_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
is defined in (
4.3
).

Proof.

By (
4.2
), (
4.5
) and (
4.8
), we have

\displaystyle|\Delta_{n}(\sigma)|

\displaystyle=|H_{n}(\sigma)-\bar{H}_{n}(\sigma)|

\displaystyle=\Bigg|-\frac{1}{np}\bigg(2\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}^{+}(\sigma)}(\xi_{ij}+\zeta_{ij})-\sum_{(i,j)\in E}(\xi_{ij}+\zeta_{ij})\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{n}\left(2\left(|\mathcal{E}_{I}^{+}({\sigma})|+\alpha_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{E}^{+}({\sigma})|\right)-\left(|\mathcal{E}_{I}|+\alpha_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{E}|\right)\right)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\rho_{n}}{n}(|\mathcal{E}_{I}({\sigma})|+\alpha_{n}|\mathcal{E}_{E}({\sigma})|),

where the inequality follows by the definition of
\Omega^{*}
, and in particular by the concentration property described
in (
4.3
). Since
|\mathcal{E}_{I}(\sigma)|=|\mathcal{E}_{E}(\sigma)|=n^{2}/4
, and
\alpha_{n}\leq 1
, we conclude

\displaystyle|\Delta_{n}(\sigma)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{4}(1+\alpha_{n})n\rho_{n}\leq\frac{3}{2}n\rho_{n}.

∎

Next, let
\pi_{n,\beta}
be the measure on
\Gamma_{n/2}^{2}
induced by
\mu_{n,\beta}
under the map
\sigma\to{\bf m}^{(n)}({\sigma})
, namely

(4.9)

\displaystyle\pi_{n,\beta}({\bf m})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}:\\
{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)={\bf m}\end{subarray}}\mu_{n,\beta}(\sigma).

Let
\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}
denote the analogous measure for the bipartite CW model (see (
3.24
)).

Lemma 15
.

Under assumptions (
2.2
), for all
\omega\in\Omega^{*}
and all
{\bf m}\in\Gamma_{n/2}^{2}
,

e^{-\frac{3}{2}n\rho_{n}}\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}({\bf m})\leq\pi_{n,\beta}({\bf m})\leq e^{\frac{3}{2}n\rho_{n}}\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}({\bf m}).

Proof.

By (
4.9
), we can write

\displaystyle\pi_{n,\beta}({\bf m})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}:\\
{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)={\bf m}\end{subarray}}\mu_{n,\beta}(\sigma)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}:\\
{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)={\bf m}\end{subarray}}\frac{e^{-\beta H_{n}(\sigma)}}{Z_{n,\beta}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}:\\
{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)={\bf m}\end{subarray}}\frac{e^{-\beta(\Delta_{n}(\sigma)+\bar{H}_{n}(\sigma))}}{Z_{n,\beta}}.

Moreover, by Lemma
14
, we have

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{3}{2}\beta n\rho_{n}}\leq e^{-\beta\Delta_{n}(\sigma)}\leq e^{\frac{3}{2}\beta n\rho_{n}},

which readily provides the result.
∎

4.3.
Asymptotic magnetization law

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1
and Corollary
2
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

We start with part (i). For every
\omega\in\Omega^{*}
and every
\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
, we can write

\displaystyle|f_{n,\beta}-\bar{f}_{n,\beta}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta n}\Bigg|\log\sum_{\sigma}e^{-\beta H_{n}(\sigma)}-\log\sum_{\sigma}e^{-\beta\bar{H}_{n}(\sigma)}\Bigg|=\frac{1}{\beta n}\Bigg|\log\frac{\sum_{\sigma}e^{-\beta\bar{H}_{n}(\sigma)}e^{-\beta\Delta_{n}(\sigma)}}{\sum_{\sigma}e^{-\beta\bar{H}_{n}(\sigma)}}\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\beta n}\log\max_{\sigma}e^{\beta|\Delta_{n}(\sigma)|}\leq\frac{1}{\beta n}\log e^{\frac{3}{2}\beta n\rho_{n}}=\frac{3}{2}\rho_{n},

where in the last inequality we used Lemma
14
. Since
\rho_{n}\to 0
, the claim follows by Lemma
13
.

Parts (ii) and (iii) then follow from Lemma
15
and the corresponding results for the bipartite CW model (Theorem
5
in Section
3
) by a standard transfer argument: the measures
\pi_{n,\beta}
and
\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}
are exponentially close, so any limiting property of
\bar{\pi}_{n,\beta}
transfers to
\pi_{n,\beta}
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
2
.

From Proposition
11
and the bounds developed in its proof, in particular the Taylor expansion of
G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})
around
{\bf m}^{0}
,
we derive the following exponential convergence, which holds under the bipartite CW measure in the whole uniqueness region:
\forall\,\varepsilon>0
,

\bar{{\mu}}_{n,\beta}({\sigma}\,:|{\bf m}^{(n)}({\sigma})-{\bf m}^{0}|>\varepsilon)\leq C\sum_{\|{\bf m}\|>\varepsilon}e^{\frac{n}{2}\left(G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})-G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m}^{0})\right)}\leq Cn^{2}e^{-\frac{n}{2}\varepsilon^{2}}\leq e^{-cn\varepsilon^{2}},

where
C,c>0
are constants. By Lemmas
13
and
15
, the same holds
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. under the measure

\mu_{n,\beta}
, for
n
large enough. The stated strong law of large numbers then follows from the above exponential convergence as a straightforward application of the Borel-Cantelli Lemma.
∎

5.
Fluctuations of the magnetization

5.1.
Sketch of the proofs

To prove the results in Theorems
3
and
4
, we use the fact that convergence of a sequence of probability measures
is naturally defined via bounded continuous test functions. For
\varphi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, define the generalized partition function

(5.1)

Z_{n,\beta}(\varphi)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}e^{-\beta H_{n}(\sigma)}\,\varphi\Big(n^{s}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)\Big),

where the scaling
s=\frac{1}{2}
for Theorem
3
and
s=\frac{1}{4}
for Theorem
4
, and note that
Z_{n,\beta}(1)=Z_{n,\beta}
is the standard partition function. Then

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{n,\beta}}[\varphi(n^{s}{\bf m}^{(n)})]=\frac{Z_{n,\beta}(\varphi)}{Z_{n,\beta}},

and the proof reduces to analyzing the limit of
Z_{n,\beta}(\varphi)/Z_{n,\beta}
for all
\varphi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
as
n\to\infty
. We show that this ratio converges in the limit to the expectation of
\varphi
under an appropriate random vector. In order to do so, we define the tilted partition function

\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(\varphi)=Z_{n,\beta}(\varphi)\,e^{-\log\cosh\left(\frac{\beta}{np}\right)\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{I}}\xi_{ij}+\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{E}}\zeta_{ij}\right)},

and notice that

\frac{Z_{n,\beta}(\varphi)}{Z_{n,\beta}}=\frac{\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(\varphi)}{\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(1)}=\frac{\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(\varphi)}{{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(\varphi)]}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(\varphi)]}{{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(1)]}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(1)]}{\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(1)}.

We will prove that the first and third ratios in the r.h.s. converge to 1, while
the second ratio converges to the desired limit (see Lemmas
17
and
18
below).

5.2.
Tilted partition function and preliminaries

It is convenient to provide an equivalent representation of
\widetilde{Z}_{n}(\varphi)
defined in (
5.1
).
In particular, for
s=\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{4}
, we can write

(5.2)

\widetilde{Z}_{n}(\varphi)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}\varphi\left(n^{s}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)\right)T(\sigma),

where

T(\sigma)=\exp\left(\gamma\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{I}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\xi_{ij}+\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{E}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\zeta_{ij}\right)-\log\cosh(\gamma)\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{I}}\xi_{ij}+\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{E}}\zeta_{ij}\right)\right),

with
\gamma=\frac{\beta}{np}
.

With this notation, the following asymptotic estimates hold true.

Lemma 16
.

For
np\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, the two following statements hold.

(i)

For all
\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
,

{\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]=\exp\left(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{8}(1+\alpha_{n}^{2})-\frac{\beta}{2}+\left(\frac{\beta}{2}n+O\left(\frac{1}{np^{2}}\right)\right)E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{(n)}({\sigma}))+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right),

where
E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m})
is defined in (
2.7
).

(ii)

For all
\sigma,\tau\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
,

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)T(\tau)]&=\exp\Bigg(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}(1+\alpha_{n}^{2})-\beta+\left(\frac{\beta}{2}n+O\left(\frac{1}{np^{2}}\right)\right)\Big(E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma))+E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{(n)}(\tau))\Big)\\
&\qquad\qquad+O\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma\tau))+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\Bigg),\end{split}

where
\sigma\tau
is the configuration obtained by multiplying the spins of
\sigma
and
\tau
component-wise, that is for each

i\in V
we have
(\sigma\tau)_{i}=\sigma_{i}\tau_{i}
.

Proof.

To prove the above lemma we need some technical preparation.
For an integer
m
and arbitrary complex variables
y
and
z
, we introduce the function

F_{m}(x,y,z)=\log\left(1-y+ye^{xz-m\log\cosh(z)}\right).

The power series expansions of some linear combinations of
F_{m}(x,y,z)
,
with
m
and
x
fixed, have been investigated in
[
17
]
.
In particular, by Lemma 2.1 in
[
17
]
, for
y
and
z
variables around
(0,0)
,
we have the following expansions:

(5.3)

\begin{split}&F_{1}(1,y,z)+F_{1}(1,y,-z)=-y^{2}z^{2}+O(y^{2}z^{4}),\\
&F_{1}(1,y,z)-F_{1}(1,y,-z)=2yz+O(yz^{3}),\\
&F_{2}(2,y,z)-F_{2}(2,y,-z)=4yz+O(yz^{3}),\\
&F_{2}(2,y,z)+F_{2}(2,y,-z)-2F_{2}(0,y,z)=4y(1-y)\tanh^{2}(z)+O(y^{3}z^{3}),\\
&F_{2}(2,y,z)+F_{2}(2,y,-z)+2F_{2}(0,y,z)=-4y^{2}z^{2}+O(y^{2}z^{4}).\end{split}

We prove the two statements separately.

(i)

We want to study

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}\left[T(\sigma)\right]&={\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{\gamma\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{I}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\xi_{ij}+\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{E}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\zeta_{ij}\right)-\log\cosh(\gamma)\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{I}}\xi_{ij}+\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{E}}\zeta_{ij}\right)}\right]\\
&=\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{I}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{\xi_{ij}\left(\gamma\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}-\log\cosh(\gamma)\right)}\right]\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{E}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{\zeta_{ij}\left(\gamma\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}-\log\cosh(\gamma)\right)}\right]\\
&=\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{I}}\left(1-p+pe^{\gamma\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}-\log\cosh(\gamma)}\right)\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{E}}\left(1-\alpha_{n}p+\alpha_{n}pe^{\gamma\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}-\log\cosh(\gamma)}\right).\end{split}

Start by defining

\displaystyle f(x)=\log\left(1-p+pe^{\gamma x-\log\cosh(\gamma)}\right)=F_{1}(x,p,\gamma),

\displaystyle f_{\alpha_{n}}(x)=\log\left(1-\alpha_{n}p+\alpha_{n}pe^{\gamma x-\log\cosh(\gamma)}\right)=F_{1}(x,\alpha_{n}p,\gamma),

so that we can write

{\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]=\exp\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{I}}f(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j})+\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{E}}f_{\alpha_{n}}(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j})\right).

Since
\sigma_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
for all
i
, we are only interested in
f(\pm 1)
and
f_{\alpha_{n}}(\pm 1)
, which can be calculated by linearizing

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}f(x)=a_{0}+a_{1}x,&x\in\{-1,+1\},\\
f_{\alpha_{n}}(x)=a_{0,\alpha_{n}}+a_{1,\alpha_{n}}x,&x\in\{-1,+1\}.\end{array}

Using the above linearization, the expansions in
5.3
, and substituting
\gamma=\frac{\beta}{np}
, we get

\displaystyle a_{0}=\frac{F_{1}(1,p,\gamma)+F_{1}(1,p,-\gamma)}{2}=-\frac{p^{2}\gamma^{2}}{2}+O(p^{2}\gamma^{4})=-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}p^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle a_{1}=\frac{F_{1}(1,p,\gamma)-F_{1}(1,p,-\gamma)}{2}=p\gamma+O(p\gamma^{3})=\frac{\beta}{n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}p^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle a_{0,\alpha_{n}}=\frac{F_{1}(1,\alpha_{n}p,\gamma)+F_{1}(1,\alpha_{n}p,-\gamma)}{2}=-\frac{\alpha_{n}^{2}p^{2}\gamma^{2}}{2}+O(p^{2}\gamma^{4})=-\frac{\alpha_{n}^{2}\beta^{2}}{2n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}p^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle a_{1,\alpha_{n}}=\frac{F_{1}(1,\alpha_{n}p,\gamma)-F_{1}(1,\alpha_{n}p,-\gamma)}{2}=\alpha_{n}p\gamma+O(p\gamma^{3})=\frac{\alpha_{n}\beta}{n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}p^{2}}\right).

Hence, recalling that
m_{i}^{(n)}({\sigma})=\frac{1}{n/2}\sum_{j\in V_{i}}\sigma_{j}
for
i=1,2
,

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]&=\exp\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{I}}f(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j})+\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{E}}f_{\alpha_{n}}(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j})\right)\\
&=\exp\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{I}}(a_{0}+a_{1}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j})+\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{E}}(a_{0,\alpha_{n}}+a_{1,\alpha_{n}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j})\right)\\
&=\exp\left(\frac{n^{2}}{4}\right(a_{0}+a_{0,\alpha_{n}}\left)+a_{1}\left(\frac{n^{2}}{8}\|{\bf m}^{(n)}({\sigma})\|^{2}\right)+a_{1,\alpha_{n}}\frac{n^{2}}{4}m_{1}^{(n)}({\sigma})m_{2}^{(n)}({\sigma})-\frac{n}{2}(a_{0}+a_{1})\right)\\
&=\exp\left(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{8}(1+\alpha_{n}^{2})-\frac{\beta}{2}+\left(\frac{\beta}{2}n+O\left(\frac{1}{np^{2}}\right)\right)E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{(n)}({\sigma}))+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right).\end{split}

(ii)

We now want to study

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}\left[T(\sigma)T(\tau)\right]&=\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{I}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{\xi_{ij}\left(\gamma(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+\tau_{i}\tau_{j})-2\log\cosh(\gamma)\right)}\right]\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{E}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{\zeta_{ij}\left(\gamma(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+\tau_{i}\tau_{j})-2\log\cosh(\gamma)\right)}\right]\\
&=\exp\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{I}}g(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+\tau_{i}\tau_{j})+\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{E}}g_{\alpha_{n}}(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+\tau_{i}\tau_{j})\right),\end{split}

where

\displaystyle g(x)=\log\left(1-p+pe^{\gamma x-2\log\cosh(\gamma)}\right)=F_{2}(x,p,\gamma),

\displaystyle g_{\alpha_{n}}(x)=\log\left(1-\alpha_{n}p+\alpha_{n}pe^{\gamma x-2\log\cosh(\gamma)}\right)=F_{2}(x,\alpha_{n}p,\gamma).

Since
\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+\tau_{i}\tau_{j}\in\{-2,0,2\}
for all
i,j
, we are only interested in
g(0),g(\pm 2),g_{\alpha_{n}}(0)
and
g_{\alpha_{n}}(\pm 2)
, which can be represented in the form

\displaystyle g(x_{1}+x_{2})=b_{0}+b_{1}x_{1}+b_{2}x_{2}+b_{12}x_{1}x_{2},\qquad x_{1},x_{2}\in\{\pm 1\},

\displaystyle g_{\alpha_{n}}(x_{1}+x_{2})=b_{0,\alpha_{n}}+b_{1,\alpha_{n}}x_{1}+b_{2,\alpha_{n}}x_{2}+b_{12,\alpha_{n}}x_{1}x_{2},\qquad x_{1},x_{2}\in\{\pm 1\}.

Solving two appropriate systems of four linear equations in four variables each, using the expansions in
5.3
, and substituting
\gamma=\frac{\beta}{np}
, we get

(5.4)

\displaystyle b_{0}=\frac{F_{2}(2,p,\gamma)+F_{2}(2,p,-\gamma)+2F_{2}(0,p,\gamma)}{4}=-p^{2}\gamma^{2}+O\left(\ p^{2}\gamma^{4}\right)=-\frac{\beta^{2}}{n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}p^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle b_{12}=\frac{F_{2}(2,p,\gamma)+F_{2}(2,p,-\gamma)-2F_{2}(0,p,\gamma)}{4}=O\left(p\gamma^{2}\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}p}\right),

\displaystyle b_{1}=b_{2}=\frac{F_{2}(2,p,\gamma)-F_{2}(2,p,-\gamma)}{4}=p\gamma+O\left(p\gamma^{3}\right)=\frac{\beta}{n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}p^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle b_{0,\alpha_{n}}=\frac{F_{2}(2,\alpha_{n}p,\gamma)+F_{2}(2,\alpha_{n}p,-\gamma)+2F_{2}(0,\alpha_{n}p,\gamma)}{4}=-\alpha_{n}^{2}p^{2}\gamma^{2}+O\left(\ p^{2}\gamma^{4}\right)=-\frac{\alpha_{n}^{2}\beta^{2}}{n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}p^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle b_{12,\alpha_{n}}=\frac{F_{2}(2,\alpha_{n}p,\gamma)+F_{2}(2,\alpha_{n}p,-\gamma)-2F_{2}(0,\alpha_{n}p,\gamma)}{4}=O\left(p\gamma^{2}\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}p}\right),

\displaystyle b_{1,\alpha_{n}}=b_{2,\alpha_{n}}=\frac{F_{2}(2,\alpha_{n}p,\gamma)-F_{2}(2,\alpha_{n}p,-\gamma)}{4}=\alpha_{n}p\gamma+O\left(p\gamma^{3}\right)=\frac{\alpha_{n}\beta}{n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}p^{2}}\right).

Hence we obtain

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)T(\tau)]&=\,\exp\Bigg(\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{I}}g(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+\tau_{i}\tau_{j})+\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{E}}g_{\alpha_{n}}(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+\tau_{i}\tau_{j})\Bigg)\\
&=\,\exp\Bigg(\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{I}}(b_{0}+b_{1}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+b_{2}\tau_{i}\tau_{j}+b_{12}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\tau_{i}\tau_{j})\\
&\qquad\qquad+\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{E}}(b_{0,\alpha_{n}}+b_{1,\alpha_{n}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+b_{2,\alpha_{n}}\tau_{i}\tau_{j}+b_{12,\alpha_{n}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\tau_{i}\tau_{j})\Bigg).\end{split}

Expanding the sums in the last display, and recalling the definition of
\sigma\tau
in Lemma
16
, we get

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)T(\tau)]&=\,\exp\Bigg(\frac{n^{2}}{4}(b_{0}+b_{0,\alpha_{n}})+b_{1}\frac{n^{2}}{8}\|{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)\|^{2}+b_{1,\alpha_{n}}\frac{n^{2}}{4}m_{1}^{(n)}(\sigma)m_{2}^{(n)}(\sigma)\\
&\qquad\qquad+b_{2}\frac{n^{2}}{8}\|{\bf m}^{(n)}(\tau)\|^{2}+b_{1,\alpha_{n}}\frac{n^{2}}{4}m_{1}^{(n)}(\tau)m_{2}^{(n)}(\tau)\\
&\qquad\qquad+b_{12}\frac{n^{2}}{8}\|{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma\tau)\|^{2}+b_{12,\alpha_{n}}\frac{n^{2}}{4}m_{1}^{(n)}(\sigma\tau)m_{2}^{(n)}(\sigma\tau)\\
&\qquad\qquad-\frac{n}{2}(b_{0}+b_{1}+b_{2}+b_{12})\Bigg).\end{split}

Finally, inserting the specific values in (
5.4
) and developing as for the computation of
{\mathbb{E}}(T(\sigma))
, we obtain

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)T(\tau)]&=\exp\Bigg(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}(1+\alpha_{n}^{2})-\beta+\left(\frac{\beta}{2}n+O\left(\frac{1}{np^{2}}\right)\right)\Big(E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma))+E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{(n)}(\tau))\Big)\\
&\qquad\qquad+O\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma\tau))+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\Bigg).\end{split}

∎

5.3.
Mean and variance of the tilted partition function

We are now ready to prove the following results.

Lemma 17

(Mean of the tilted partition function)
.

Let
\beta\leq\beta_{c}
.
Under assumptions (
2.2
), for all non-negative
\varphi\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
,
\varphi\not\equiv 0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(\varphi)]2^{-n}\propto e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{8}(1+\hat{\alpha}^{2})-\frac{\beta}{2}}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left[\varphi(X)\right],

where
{\mathbb{E}}_{X}
denotes the expectation over
X
and

(i)

if
\beta<\beta_{c}
then
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
and
\Sigma
is given in (
2.9
);

(ii)

if
\beta=\beta_{c}
then
X
is a two-dimensional random vector with density given in (
2.10
).

Proof.

We first consider part (i). We start by defining a set of typical configurations whose magnetization is close to the unique minimum
{\bf m}^{0}=(0,0)
of the free energy functional, i.e.,

(5.5)

S_{n}=\left\{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}:\|{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)\|\leq r_{n}\right\},\quad r_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{(np)^{\delta}}{n}},\quad\delta\in(0,1/3).

The atypical configurations are then

S_{n}^{c}=\mathcal{X}_{n}\setminus S_{n}=\left\{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}:\|{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)\|>r_{n}\right\}.

Recalling the definition of tilted partition function
\widetilde{Z}_{n}(\varphi)
in (
5.2
) with
s=\frac{1}{2}
, we can write

(5.6)

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{Z}_{n}(\varphi)]&=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)\right){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]\\
&=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)\right){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]+\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{c}}\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)\right){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)].\end{split}

Step 1: Contribution of typical configurations.
For
\sigma\in S_{n}
, Lemma
16
(i) gives

{\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]=\exp\left(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{8}(1+\alpha_{n}^{2})-\frac{\beta}{2}+\left(\frac{\beta}{2}n+O\left(\frac{1}{np^{2}}\right)\right)E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{(n)}({\sigma}))+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right),

Since
\|{\bf m}(\sigma)\|^{2}\leq r_{n}^{2}
for typical configurations, we get
that
E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma))\leq r_{n}^{2}
, and hence

O\left(\frac{1}{np^{2}}\right)E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}(\sigma))\leq\frac{1}{np^{2}}r_{n}^{2}=O\left(\frac{1}{(np)^{(2-\delta)}}\right)=o(1),

due to our choice of
r_{n}
and
\delta
in (
5.5
), and since
np\to\infty
.
Moreover, using (
3.17
), the number of configurations with magnetization

{\bf m}=(m_{1},m_{2})
, with
\|{\bf m}\|\leq r_{n}
, is given by

\binom{n/2}{\frac{1+m_{1}}{2}n/2}\binom{n/2}{\frac{1+m_{2}}{2}n/2}=\frac{4}{\pi n\sqrt{(1-m_{1}^{2})(1-m_{2}^{2})}}e^{-\frac{n}{2}\mathcal{I}({\bf m})}(1+o(1)),

where
\mathcal{I}({\bf m})=\mathcal{I}(m_{1})+\mathcal{I}(m_{2})
is the entropic term introduced in (
2.8
).
Summing over magnetizations rather than configurations, the contribution of typical configurations becomes

(5.7)

\begin{split}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\varphi(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]&=\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{8}(1+\alpha_{n}^{2})-\frac{\beta}{2}}}{\pi}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|{\bf m}\|\leq r_{n}\end{subarray}}\frac{4}{n}\varphi(\sqrt{n}\,{\bf m})\frac{e^{\Big(\frac{\beta}{2}nE_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m})-\frac{n}{2}\mathcal{I}({\bf m})\Big)}}{\sqrt{(1-m_{1}^{2})(1-m_{2}^{2})}}(1+o(1))\\
&=\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{8}(1+\alpha_{n}^{2})-\frac{\beta}{2}}}{\pi}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|{\bf m}\|\leq r_{n}\end{subarray}}\frac{4}{n}\varphi(\sqrt{n}\,{\bf m})\frac{e^{\Big(\frac{n}{2}G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})\Big)}}{\sqrt{(1-m_{1}^{2})(1-m_{2}^{2})}}(1+o(1)).\end{split}

Elaborating as in (
3.18
)-(
3.20
), we first perform a Taylor expansion of
G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})
around
G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m}^{0})=2\log 2
, getting

(5.8)

\displaystyle G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})=

\displaystyle 2\log 2-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\beta}{2}\right)\|{\bf m}\|^{2}+\frac{\beta\alpha_{n}}{2}m_{1}m_{2}+O(\|{\bf m}\|^{3}),

and then we apply the change of variables
{\bf x}=\sqrt{n}{\bf m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Altogether, using that for typical configurations
n\|{\bf m}\|^{3}=o(1)
and for
\Sigma
as in (
2.9
) we obtain

\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\varphi(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]=\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{8}(1+\alpha_{n}^{2})-\frac{\beta}{2}}}{\pi}\,e^{n\log 2}\sum_{{\bf x}\in\sqrt{n}\cdot S_{n}}\frac{4}{n}\varphi({\bf x})\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}({\bf x})^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}{\bf x}}}{\sqrt{1-\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{n}}\sqrt{1-\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{n}}}(1+o(1)).

We note that the Riemann sum in the last display can be approximated, for
n\gg 1
, by the integral

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\varphi({\bf x})e^{-\frac{1}{2}{\bf x}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}{\bf x}}d{\bf x}.

Under the assumption
\beta<\beta_{c}
, the matrix
\Sigma
is positive definite,
which guarantees that the exponential weight is integrable over
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
In particular, the integral can equivalently be expressed as a Gaussian expectation,
so that altogether, for
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
, we obtain

(5.9)

\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\varphi(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]=2\sqrt{\det{(\Sigma)}}\,e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{8}(1+\alpha_{n}^{2})-\frac{\beta}{2}}\,e^{n\log 2}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left[\varphi(X)\right](1+o(1)).

Step 2: Contribution of atypical configurations.

Proceeding as in (
5.7
), from Lemma
16
(i), we easily get the bound

\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{c}}\varphi(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]\leq 2\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf m}\in{\Gamma}_{n}\\
\|{\bf m}\|>r_{n}\end{subarray}}\binom{n/2}{\frac{1+m_{1}}{2}n/2}\binom{n/2}{\frac{1+m_{2}}{2}n/2}e^{\left(\frac{\beta}{2}n+O\left(\frac{1}{np^{2}}\right)\right)E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}(\sigma))},

where we used that
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}(1+\alpha_{n})}{8}-\frac{\beta}{2}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)}\leq 2\|\varphi\|_{\infty}
. For large
n
,
we then approximate the two binomials by using the local limit theorem

(5.10)

\displaystyle\binom{n/2}{\frac{1+m_{1}}{2}n/2}\binom{n/2}{\frac{1+m_{2}}{2}n/2}\leq\frac{C2^{n}}{n}e^{-\frac{n\|{\bf m}\|^{2}}{4}}=\frac{C}{n}e^{n\log 2}e^{-\frac{n\|{\bf m}\|^{2}}{4}},

where
C>0
is constant, and we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{c}}\varphi(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]

\displaystyle\leq C(\beta,\varphi)e^{n\log 2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf m}\in{\Gamma}_{n}\\
\|{\bf m}\|>r_{n}\end{subarray}}\frac{4}{n}e^{\frac{n}{2}\left(\beta E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m})-\frac{\|{\bf m}\|^{2}}{2}+O\left(\frac{\|{\bf m}\|^{2}}{(np)^{2}}\right)\right)}

\displaystyle=C(\beta,\varphi)e^{n\log 2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf m}\in{\Gamma}_{n}\\
\|{\bf m}\|>r_{n}\end{subarray}}\frac{4}{n}e^{\frac{n}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{\beta}{2})\|{\bf m}\|^{2}-\frac{\beta\alpha_{n}}{2}m_{1}m_{2}+O\left(\frac{\|{\bf m}\|^{2}}{(np)^{2}}\right)\right)},

for some constant
C(\beta,\varphi)>0
.
Note that the main term in the exponent above corresponds to the main term in (
5.8
), which is the Taylor expansion of

G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})
around
G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m}^{0})
.
As before, applying the change of variables
{\bf x}=\sqrt{n}{\bf m}

and letting
\Sigma
be the matrix defined in (
2.9
),
the sum in the last display can be expressed as the Riemann sum

\sum_{{\bf x}\in\sqrt{n}\cdot S_{n}^{c}}\frac{4}{n}e^{-\frac{1}{2}({\bf x})^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}{\bf x}+O\left(\frac{\|{\bf x}\|^{2}}{(np)^{2}}\right)}=\sum_{{\bf x}\in\sqrt{n}\cdot S_{n}^{c}}\frac{4}{n}e^{-\frac{1}{2}({\bf x})^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}{\bf x}}\cdot(1+o(1)).

For
n\gg 1
, the sum can be approximated by the integral

\int_{\|{\bf x}\|^{2}>(np)^{\delta}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}({\bf x})^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}{\bf x}}d{\bf x}\cdot(1+o(1))=o(1),

since
(np)^{\delta}\to\infty
and the Gaussian tail outside the ball of radius
\sqrt{(np)^{\delta}}
vanishes exponentially. Altogether, we get that

(5.11)

\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}^{c}}\varphi(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]=o\left(e^{n\log 2}\right).

Putting together (
5.6
), (
5.9
) and (
5.11
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}\varphi(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\sigma}\in S_{n}}\varphi(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)](1+o(1))

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\det{(\Sigma)}}\,e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{8}(1+\alpha_{n}^{2})-\frac{\beta}{2}}\,e^{n\log 2}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left[\varphi(X)\right](1+o(1)),

as wanted.

For part (ii) we take
s=\frac{1}{4}
and the proof is analogous except for the following changes. At the critical point, the Taylor expansion of
G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})
in (
5.8
) becomes (
3.23
). As a consequence, the change of variable
\textbf{x}=\sqrt[4]{n}{\bf m}
gives

\sum_{\sigma\in\tilde{S}_{n}}\varphi(\sqrt[4]{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]=\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{8}(1+\alpha_{n}^{2})-\frac{\beta}{2}}}{\pi}\,e^{n\log 2}\sum_{{\bf x}\in\sqrt[4]{n}\cdot\tilde{S}_{n}}\frac{4}{n}\varphi({\bf x})\frac{e^{-2(x_{1}^{4}+x_{2}^{4})^{1/4}}}{\sqrt{1-\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}}\sqrt{1-\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}}}(1+o(1)),

where
\tilde{S}_{n}
is now defined as in (
5.5
) with
\delta\in\left(0,\frac{3}{5}\right)
. The Riemann sum in the last display can be approximated, for
n\gg 1
, by the integral

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\varphi({\bf x})e^{-2(x_{1}^{4}+x_{2}^{4})^{1/4}}d{\bf x}.

∎

Lemma 18

(Variance of the tilted partition function)
.

Let
\beta\leq\beta_{c}
.
Under assumptions (
2.2
), for all non-negative
\varphi\in C_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
,
\varphi\not\equiv 0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{{\rm Var}(\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(\varphi))}{{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(\varphi)]^{2}}=0.

As a consequence,

\frac{\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(\varphi)}{\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(\varphi)]}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}1\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P}).

Proof.

We first consider the case
\beta<\beta_{c}
hence the scaling
s=\frac{1}{2}
. Similarly to the proof above, define a set of typical pairs of configurations as

S^{\prime}_{n}=\left\{(\sigma,\tau)\in\mathcal{X}_{n}^{2}:\|{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma\tau)\|\leq r_{n},k=1,2\right\},

with
r_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{(np)^{\delta}}{n}},\,\delta\in(0,1/3)
, and denote the set of atypical pairs by

(S_{n}^{\prime})^{c}
. Recalling the definition of
\widetilde{Z}_{n}(\varphi)
in (
5.2
) and decomposing into a sum over typical and atypical pairs, we can write

(5.12)

\begin{split}{\rm Var}\left(\widetilde{Z}_{n}(\varphi)\right)=&\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in S^{\prime}_{n}}\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)\right)\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\tau)\right){\rm Cov}(T(\sigma),T(\tau))\\
&+\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in(S^{\prime}_{n})^{c}}\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)\right)\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\tau)\right){\rm Cov}(T(\sigma),T(\tau)).\end{split}

Step 1: Contribution of typical configurations.
We start by observing that from the definition of the covariance and Lemma
16

\begin{split}\mathrm{Cov}(T(\sigma),T(\tau))&={\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)T(\tau)]-{\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]{\mathbb{E}}[T(\tau)]\\
&={\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]{\mathbb{E}}[T(\tau)]\left(e^{O\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma\tau))}-1\right).\end{split}

For typical pairs
(\sigma,\tau)\in S^{\prime}_{n}
the exponent becomes
o(1)
, hence

{\rm Cov}(T(\sigma),T(\tau))=o\left({\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]{\mathbb{E}}[T(\tau)]\right).

We can then write

(5.13)

\begin{split}&\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in S^{\prime}_{n}}\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)\right)\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\tau)\right){\rm Cov}(T(\sigma),T(\tau))\\
&\qquad=\,o\left(\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in S^{\prime}_{n}}\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)\right)\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\tau)\right){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]{\mathbb{E}}[T(\tau)]\right)\\
&\qquad=\,o\left(\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in\mathcal{X}_{n}^{2}}\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)\right)\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\tau)\right){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]{\mathbb{E}}[T(\tau)]\right)\\
&\qquad=\,o\left({\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{Z}_{n}(\varphi)]^{2}\right),\end{split}

where in the second equality we used that
\varphi\geq 0
.

Step 2: Contribution of atypical configurations.

We write

(5.14)

\begin{split}&\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in(S^{\prime}_{n})^{c}}\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)\right)\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\tau)\right){\rm Cov}(T(\sigma),T(\tau))\\
&\qquad\qquad\leq\,\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in(S^{\prime}_{n})^{c}}\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)\right)\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\tau)\right){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)T(\tau)]\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+\,\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in(S^{\prime}_{n})^{c}}\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)\right)\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\tau)\right){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]{\mathbb{E}}[T(\tau)].\end{split},

and we analyze the two sums in the rhs separately.

In order to characterize the configurations with fixed magnetization, we define the set

V({\bf m}^{\prime},{\bf m}^{\prime\prime},{\bf m}^{\prime\prime\prime})=\{(\sigma,\tau)\in\mathcal{X}_{n}^{2}\,:||{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)||={\bf m}^{\prime},\,||{\bf m}^{(n)}(\tau)||={\bf m}^{\prime\prime}\,\text{ and }\,||{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma\tau)||={\bf m}^{\prime\prime\prime}\}.

By the three-dimensional local central limit theorem, there exists a universal constant
C>0

such that

\displaystyle|V({\bf m}^{\prime},{\bf m}^{\prime\prime},{\bf m}^{\prime\prime})|\leq C2^{3n}n^{-3}e^{-\frac{n}{4}(||{\bf m}^{\prime}||^{2}+||{\bf m}^{\prime\prime}||^{2}+||{\bf m}^{\prime\prime\prime}||^{2})}.

The first sum in (
5.14
) then becomes

(5.15)

\begin{split}&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}({\bf m},{\bf m}^{\prime},{\bf m}^{\prime\prime}):\\
||{\bf m}^{\prime\prime\prime}||>r_{n}\end{subarray}}\,\,\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in V({\bf m}^{\prime},{\bf m}^{\prime\prime},{\bf m}^{\prime\prime\prime})}\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)\right)\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\tau)\right){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)T(\tau)]\\
&\leq C^{\prime}(\beta,\varphi)2^{3n}n^{-3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}({\bf m},{\bf m}^{\prime},{\bf m}^{\prime\prime}):\\
||{\bf m}^{\prime\prime\prime}||>r_{n}\end{subarray}}e^{-\frac{n}{4}(||{\bf m}^{\prime}||^{2}+||{\bf m}^{\prime\prime}||^{2}+||{\bf m}^{\prime\prime\prime}||^{2})}e^{\left(\frac{\beta}{2}n+O\left(\frac{1}{np^{2}}\right)\right)\Big(E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{\prime})+E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{\prime\prime})\Big)+O\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{\prime\prime\prime})}\\
&=C^{\prime}(\beta,\varphi)2^{3n}n^{-3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}({\bf m},{\bf m}^{\prime},{\bf m}^{\prime\prime}):\\
||{\bf m}^{\prime\prime\prime}||>r_{n}\end{subarray}}e^{-\frac{n}{4}(||{\bf m}^{\prime}||^{2}+||{\bf m}^{\prime\prime}||^{2})}e^{\left(\frac{\beta}{2}n+O\left(\frac{1}{np^{2}}\right)\right)\Big(E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{\prime})+E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{\prime\prime})\Big)}e^{-\frac{n}{4}||{\bf m}^{\prime\prime\prime}||^{2}+O\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{\prime\prime\prime})}\\
&=C^{\prime}(\beta,\varphi)2^{3n}n^{-3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}({\bf m},{\bf m}^{\prime},{\bf m}^{\prime\prime}):\\
||{\bf m}^{\prime\prime}||>r_{n}\end{subarray}}e^{\frac{n}{2}\left(\beta E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{\prime})-\frac{\|{\bf m}^{\prime}\|^{2}}{2}+O\left(\frac{\|{\bf m}^{\prime}\|^{2}}{(np)^{2}}\right)\right)}e^{\frac{n}{2}\left(\beta E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{\prime\prime})-\frac{\|{\bf m}^{\prime\prime}\|^{2}}{2}+O\left(\frac{\|{\bf m}^{\prime\prime}\|^{2}}{(np)^{2}}\right)\right)}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\cdot e^{n\left(-\frac{||{\bf m}^{\prime\prime\prime}||^{2}}{4}+O\left(\frac{1}{np}\right)E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{\prime\prime\prime})\right)},\end{split}

for some constant
C^{\prime}(\beta,\varphi)>0.
Note that the main terms in the first two exponents above correspond to the main terms in the Taylor expansions of
G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m}(\sigma))
and
G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m}(\tau))
around
G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m}^{0})
. Apply the change of variables
{\bf x}=\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{\prime}
,
{\bf y}=\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{\prime\prime}
, and
{\bf z}=\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{\prime\prime\prime}
. Let
\tilde{\Sigma}
be the
6\times 6
Hessian matrix of the quadratic form appearing in the exponent, which in block form can be written as

\tilde{\Sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma&0&0\\
0&\Sigma&0\\
0&0&\Sigma^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},

where
\Sigma
is the
2\times 2
covariance matrix defined in
2.9
, and
\Sigma^{\prime}
is the
2\times 2
block coming from the cross terms such that
(\Sigma^{\prime})^{-1}=-\frac{1}{4}\text{Id}_{2}
. Then the sum over atypical pairs can be expressed as the Riemann sum

\sum_{({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})\in D_{n}}\frac{1}{n^{3}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})^{\top}(\tilde{\Sigma})^{-1}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})}\cdot(1+o(1)),

where

D_{n}=\left\{{\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z}\in\sqrt{n}\cdot S_{n}^{c}:\|{\bf z}\|^{2}>(np)^{\delta}\right\}.

Hence, for
n\gg 1
, the sum can be approximated by the integral

(5.16)

\int_{({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})\in D_{n}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})^{\top}(\tilde{\Sigma})^{-1}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})}d({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})\cdot(1+o(1))=o(1),

since
(np)^{\delta}\to\infty
. Combining the above (
5.15
)-(
5.16
) and Lemma
17
, we obtain

(5.17)

\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in(S^{\prime}_{n})^{c}}\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}(\sigma)\right)\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}(\tau)\right){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)T(\tau)]=o\left(e^{2n\log 2}\right)=o\left({\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{Z}_{n}(\varphi)]^{2}\right).

Next, we analyze the second sum in the rhs of (
5.14
), which from Lemma
16
(i) and (
5.10
), can be written as

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}({\bf m}^{\prime},{\bf m}^{\prime\prime},{\bf m}^{\prime\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}:\\
||{\bf m}^{\prime\prime\prime}||>r_{n}\end{subarray}}\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in V_{n}}\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\sigma)\right)\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)}(\tau)\right){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]{\mathbb{E}}[T(\tau)]

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}(\beta,\varphi)2^{3n}n^{-3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}({\bf m}^{\prime},{\bf m}^{\prime\prime},{\bf m}^{\prime\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}:\\
||{\bf m}^{\prime\prime\prime}||>r_{n}\end{subarray}}e^{\frac{n}{2}\left(\beta E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{\prime})-\frac{\|{\bf m}^{\prime}\|^{2}}{2}+O\left(\frac{\|{\bf m}^{\prime}\|^{2}}{(np)^{2}}\right)\right)}e^{\frac{n}{2}\left(\beta E_{\alpha_{n}}({\bf m}^{\prime\prime})-\frac{\|{\bf m}^{\prime\prime}\|^{2}}{2}+O\left(\frac{\|{\bf m}^{\prime\prime}\|^{2}}{(np)^{2}}\right)\right)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot e^{-\frac{n}{4}||{\bf m}^{\prime\prime\prime}||^{2}}.

Arguing as before, we obtain

(5.18)

\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in(S^{\prime}_{n})^{c}}\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}(\sigma)\right)\varphi\left(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}(\tau)\right){\mathbb{E}}[T(\sigma)]{\mathbb{E}}[T(\tau)]=o\left(e^{2n\log 2}\right)=o\left({\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{Z}_{n}(\varphi)]^{2}\right).

By putting together (
5.12
), (
5.13
), (
5.14
), (
5.17
), and (
5.18
), we conclude that

{\rm Var}\left(\widetilde{Z}_{n}(\varphi)\right)=o\left({\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{Z}_{n}(\varphi)]^{2}\right).

As a consequence,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\widetilde{Z}_{n}(\varphi)}{\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Z}_{n}(\varphi)]}-1\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{{\rm Var}(\widetilde{Z}_{n}(\varphi))}{\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Z}_{n}(\varphi)]^{2}}\rightarrow 0,

which shows that
\frac{\widetilde{Z}_{n}(\varphi)}{\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{Z}_{n}(\varphi)]}
converges to 1 in
L^{2}
.

For the critical point
\beta=\beta_{c}
, the proof proceeds analogously but with scaling
\sqrt[4]{n}
.
The different scaling arises because the Taylor expansion of
G_{\alpha_{n},\beta}({\bf m})
is now given by (
3.23
) and has a quartic term as its leading nonzero contribution. Note that the covariance matrix
\Sigma
becomes singular, and consequently so does the Hessian
\tilde{\Sigma}
, reflecting the presence of flat directions in the quadratic form.
∎

5.4.
Proof of Theorems
3
and
4

We are now able to prove Theorems
3
and
4
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

We argued that proving the CLT reduces to analyzing the limit of
Z_{n,\beta}(\varphi)/Z_{n,\beta}
for all
\varphi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
as
n\to\infty
, where
Z_{n,\beta}(\varphi)
is the generalized partition function defined in (
5.1
). By the definition of tilted partition function
\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(\varphi)
in (
5.1
), we have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{Z_{n,\beta}(\varphi)}{Z_{n,\beta}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(\varphi)}{\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(1)}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(\varphi)}{{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(\varphi)]}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(\varphi)]}{{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(1)]}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(1)]}{\widetilde{Z}_{n,\beta}(1)}={\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left[\varphi(X)\right],

where the third equality holds in

In conclusion, we have that, for all

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{n,\beta}}[\varphi(\sqrt{n}\,{\bf m}^{(n)})]=\frac{Z_{n,\beta}(\varphi)}{Z_{n,\beta}}\;\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\;{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left[\varphi(X)\right].

This readily implies that the law
\mathcal{L}(\sqrt{n}{\bf m}^{(n)})
