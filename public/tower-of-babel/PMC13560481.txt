How Does SNAP Access Prior to Pregnancy Affect Maternal and Infant Health Outcomes?
Authors: SARAH HAMERSMA, MITCHELL MCFARLANE
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Policy Points Reimposing work requirements for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program—by removing temporary, location‐based waivers—causes a decline in food assistance participation among childless women. This loss of nutritional support prior to pregnancy is linked to tangible, negative health outcomes for their infants.Analyses show that, when work requirements are reimposed, there are statistically significant increases in very low birth weight and very preterm births.These adverse effects are found among first‐time mothers, the group most likely to have been subject to these work requirements as “able‐bodied adults without dependents” just prior to their pregnancy. ContextAble‐bodied adults without dependents (ABAWDs) are eligible for Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program benefits for only 3 months in any 36‐month period, after which they are subject to a work requirement to continue receiving benefits. Because ABAWDs may later have children, this work requirement for childless adults may affect the health of pregnant women and their infants through the mother's nutritional well‐being prior to pregnancy. The objective of this study was to examine whether temporary county‐level waivers of these work requirements improved health outcomes for mothers and their infants.MethodsThis study used restricted National Vital Statistics System natality data from 2004 to 2018, with virtually every birth in the United States in that period, linked at the county‐month level to a dataset of ABAWD work‐requirement waivers in the year prior to pregnancy. Several binary maternal and infant health outcomes were examined in intent‐to‐treat analyses using two‐way fixed‐effects regressions and event studies with application of an estimator robust to heterogeneous treatment effects. The analysis also controlled for economic conditions upon which waiver eligibility is determined.FindingsStatistically significant increases in adverse birth outcomes were found for first‐time mothers exposed to (re‐introduced) work requirements just before pregnancy, including a 14% increase in very low birth weight infants and a 10% increase in very preterm births relative to baseline rates. In contrast, the introduction of a waiver was associated with little change in birth outcomes. For maternal outcomes, the removal of a waiver was unexpectedly associated with a significant reduction in eclampsia and a decline in labor inductions. The study found little to no effect of waiver status on other maternal outcomes, such as diabetes or cesarean section rates.ConclusionsThis study found evidence that work requirements for food benefits may harm infant health, particularly when waivers are terminated. These results provide evidence that may inform policymakers weighing the costs and benefits of modifications to ABAWD work requirements.

Consistent access to healthy food is a key component of a healthy pregnancy for both mothers and their children. Recent work suggests that nutritional status even before pregnancy can play an important role in maternal and prenatal health when pregnancy does occur. 1 The Centers for Disease Control now offers guidance on “planning for pregnancy” that includes several recommendations to improve the quality of maternal and infant health, including nutrition‐ and weight‐related investments. 2 The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) provides food assistance to low‐income individuals, including those who do not yet have children. The research reported herein provides insight into the possible effects of women's SNAP access in the year prior to a pregnancy on health outcomes for their first birth.

Maternal and infant health outcomes are a major concern in the United States, as rates of maternal and infant morbidity (as well as mortality) are higher than in many developed peer countries. 3 Women experiencing a first pregnancy are particularly vulnerable to poor infant health outcomes. 4 Public programs such as Medicaid and the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children (WIC) aim to improve health during pregnancy, primarily by covering prenatal care appointments and providing nutritious food. However, particularly for first births, women may not be familiar with these resources, and their participation may be delayed. Before a person becomes pregnant, WIC is unavailable to them, and Medicaid generally has much stricter eligibility criteria than it does during a person's pregnancy (particularly in states that did not expand Medicaid under the Affordable Care Act). In contrast, SNAP is a broader assistance program that is available to childless women with low incomes. Access to SNAP prior to pregnancy could improve outcomes through at least two distinct mechanisms. First, it may generate a direct improvement in a potential mother's physical health at the initiation of pregnancy. It is known that access to SNAP during pregnancy has such effects. 5 , 6 , 7 Second, SNAP may provide an existing link to assistance that may facilitate connection with other assistance (such as Medicaid or WIC) at pregnancy initiation.

One other important difference between SNAP and Medicaid is the uniformity of the SNAP eligibility criteria and benefits. Whereas Medicaid income eligibility varies widely across states (particularly for childless adults), SNAP eligibility is set federally. This has traditionally made research on the effects of SNAP more challenging, as there is less variation to leverage to identify effects. 5 States do vary, however, in a number of implementation strategies; some strategies (such as electronic benefits transfer cards or the standard utility allowance) have been introduced over time until they are nearly universal. Others vary across states without converging; for example, broad‐based categorical eligibility, which allows for multiple pathways to eligibility. 8 Many of these implementation rules vary at the state level, and policies often change just once (i.e., “turn on”). However, one important rule varies both more frequently and at a more precise geographic level than most: childless women face potential work requirements that vary across counties and over time. In particular, states have the ability to apply for statewide or county‐level waivers of federal work requirements that otherwise apply to able‐bodied adults without dependents (ABAWDs). There has been substantial variation in the waiver coverage of counties over time, from modest to near universal, and many counties have seen both waiver introduction and waiver removal (sometimes more than once).

This study leverages the variation in SNAP work requirements during the period from 2004 to 2018 to investigate a possible link between pre‐pregnancy SNAP access and the health outcomes of mothers and their infants at the time of birth. Using rich, population‐wide National Vital Statistics System data combined with county‐by‐month variation in SNAP access (through ABAWD work‐requirement waivers), this study is able to tease out effects of SNAP that might otherwise be missed in conventional analyses that rely on less variation or must directly address selection into participation. The initial two‐way fixed‐effects estimates indicate modest intent‐to‐treat effects of work‐requirement waivers: maternal hypertension may be slightly lower, and preterm birth and low birth weight become more common (possibly reflecting survival improvement in the lower tail of the birth distribution). However, a more sophisticated event‐study analysis (that is robust to treatment effect heterogeneity) suggests that corrected estimates are more substantial, particularly when allowance is made for asymmetric effects of the introduction of work‐requirement waivers and the removal of those waivers. Although minimal evidence of improved maternal outcomes was observed, this analysis uncovered more evidence for infants. In particular, the introduction of waivers (i.e., the elimination of work requirements) during the year before pregnancy was not found to have large effects, but the removal of waivers (i.e., the re‐introduction of work requirements) was found to have measurable impacts on infant health. First‐time mothers exposed to (re‐introduced) work requirements just before pregnancy were found to experience an estimated 14% increase in very low birth weight births and a 10% increase in very preterm births relative to baseline rates. The potential for this link to reflect SNAP participation was investigated by examining the relationship between the ABAWD waivers and SNAP participation itself, which could not be done with the Vital Statistics data because these data lack SNAP indicators. Thus, a separate analysis using the American Community Survey was conducted that revealed consistent but small effects of work requirements on SNAP participation, suggesting that work requirements may be a relatively weak policy lever. These findings contribute to the evidence base on the overall cost‐benefit profile of SNAP and also provide insights into whether SNAP access is a useful preconception vehicle for women to improve their own birth experience and birth outcomes for their first children.

Health Policy Context

SNAP and its rules and effects have been studied extensively. The current project contributes to an understanding of the effects of SNAP on pregnancy‐related health, relying on variation in access to SNAP before pregnancy as a result of work requirements for childless adults. As such, it is important to situate this work in the literature on SNAP and birth outcomes, as well as the literature documenting the effects of SNAP work requirements on SNAP access, employment, and health. This section also introduces additional details about waivers of these work requirements.

SNAP, Pre‐Pregnancy Health, and Birth Outcomes

The important role of SNAP in the health of pregnant women and children has been well‐established. 5 , 6 However, the role that SNAP may play prior to pregnancy has received less attention. There are two clear channels by which one might expect pre‐pregnancy access to SNAP to matter for birth outcomes. First, this access may allow women to be in better physical (and perhaps mental) health at the time they become pregnant, because of access to better nutrition. Baseline conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and obesity all have the potential to be improved by SNAP, and improvements in these conditions, in turn, provide a healthier start to pregnancy and a lower risk of developing complications. 1 Second, this access may allow women to access SNAP earlier in pregnancy. Although pregnant women do not face work requirements for SNAP, the application process may take time, and even eligible women may not be enrolled immediately upon becoming pregnant if they were not eligible beforehand. Those who are already on SNAP will be able to maintain consistent access to nutrition even during the early days of pregnancy, when important fetal development occurs. Their connection to SNAP may also provide earlier, easier access to related support programs such as WIC and Medicaid that are known to improve maternal and infant health outcomes. 9 , 10

ABAWD Waiver Eligibility and Variation

Federal law requires SNAP recipients who are ABAWD to consistently work at least 20 hours per week. However, childless women actually face variation in these requirements over time and across geography, because states and counties have the ability to apply for waivers of these federal work requirements that otherwise apply to ABAWDs. In areas without a waiver, there is a 3‐month time limit over a period of 36 months for ABAWDs if they do not meet the work requirement; in areas with a waiver, eligibility is maintained regardless of working patterns. State or county eligibility for a waiver depends on that entity providing evidence of a weak labor market, so these waivers are far from randomly assigned. However, by employing an analysis that compares counties with similar labor markets (i.e., that holds labor market variables constant by carefully controlling for them), one can assess the role of the waivers themselves on maternal and infant outcomes.

The waiver coverage of counties has varied substantially over time. In addition, at times during the Great Recession and the COVID‐19 pandemic, there was a blanket federal call to waive ABAWD work requirements. Work Requirements and SNAP Participation, Employment, and Health

The literature documenting the effects of SNAP work requirements on ABAWDs provides important evidence on how restricting eligibility may impact SNAP participation and employment. Several studies have used age cutoffs and geographic variation in work‐requirement waivers to estimate the causal impact of these policies. The findings of these studies indicate that work requirements significantly reduce SNAP participation among ABAWDs, with estimated declines ranging from 9% to 52%. However, the effects on employment appear to be minimal. This suggests that tightened eligibility primarily results in the loss of nutrition benefits without substantially increasing self‐sufficiency.

Ganong and Liebman 11 employed a relatively simple approach to estimating the effects of SNAP ABAWD waivers introduced during the Great Recession on SNAP enrollment, assuming that, in the absence of ABAWD waiver expansion, the trend in ABAWD enrollment would have been parallel to the trend in enrollment for those groups that did not see a policy change. Using SNAP Quality Control files from 2007 to 2011, they estimated that an additional 1.87 million ABAWDs were enrolled in SNAP in 2011 because of the widespread introduction of ABAWD waivers (a 77% increase in ABAWD enrollment). This estimate, however, relied on an assumption of parallel trends that is unlikely to hold and ended with the Great Recession period.

Two key studies examined ABAWD waivers and SNAP enrollment in the period after the Great Recession when many waivers expired. Ku et al. 12 examined county‐level variation in ABAWD waivers between 2013 and 2017. They used semi‐annual SNAP enrollment data reported by states to the US Department of Agriculture's (USDA's) Food and Nutrition Service, which lacks information on which participants are ABAWDs. They found that the introduction of the ABAWD work requirement in a county reduced total SNAP participation by 3.0%, with a 95% confidence interval of 1.6% to 4.3%. Because ABAWDs comprised about 8% to 9% of the overall caseload during their study period, this effect was large in magnitude. Vericker et al. 13 used state administrative data from Colorado, Missouri, and Pennsylvania in the years 2014‐2017 to examine the effects one year after waiver expiration (relative to a two‐year pre‐expiration comparison time period). They found that reinstating time limits reduced SNAP participation by 7 to 32 percentage points among SNAP participants potentially subject to the time limit.

Finally, one study examined a wider time period similar to that used in this work. Hamersma and Kaur employed county‐year–level data from 2003 to 2018 to estimate the relationship between county ABAWD waivers and overall SNAP caseloads (unpublished results, 2025). They found that, controlling flexibly for labor market covariates and county fixed effects, ABAWD waivers were associated with a smaller estimate of about a 3% increase in total SNAP participation (among ABAWDs and other participants).

SNAP work requirements did not apply to adults over the age of 49 years prior to October 2023 (this cutoff has since been raised), and several studies have used this age threshold (along with geographic variation in ABAWD waivers) to estimate the effects of work requirements on labor supply and SNAP participation. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Using the American Community Survey and various administrative sources, these studies also found increased SNAP participation (ranging from modest to large) when the work requirement was removed. However, the results may not be generalizable to a population significantly younger than the age of 50 years.

Most relevant to preconception health is not SNAP enrollment per se but the total resources available for food purchase. If ABAWD work requirements caused an increase in employment, this effect may attenuate the deterioration of nutrition likely associated with reduced SNAP participation discussed earlier. However, previous research has found that SNAP work requirements for ABAWDs cause very little, if any, increase in employment. Neither Han 16 nor Gray et al. 17 found a statistically significant change in employment for ABAWDs caused by work requirements. Cuffey et al. 14 found a decrease in work when requirements were lifted in late middle age. Although this could suggest a net decrease in nutritional resources, note again that all of these estimates examined the margin of middle‐aged adults, and thus, more research is needed to understand younger adults. Harris 15 obtained results similar to those of Cuffey et al. 14 when using the middle‐age cutoff, but Harris also employed a broader difference‐in‐differences strategy over the years from 2010 to 2017, finding that work requirements generated a small, statistically significant 1.1 percentage point increase (or 1.5% increase) in employment.

Few studies have examined how SNAP benefits affect the health of ABAWDs. Feng 18 examined the effects of work‐requirement waivers on the health of ABAWDs with data from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System and a triple‐differences approach that used variation in ABAWD status, income eligibility, and work‐requirement waivers at the state level from 2015 to 2016. They found that loss of an ABAWD waiver was associated with a 14% increase (95% confidence interval: 2% to 26% increase) in reported physically unhealthy days and a statistically significant decrease in the proportion of individuals who reported that they exercised. They found no change in the number of reported mentally unhealthy days. Allen et al. 19 employed county‐level variation in work‐requirement waivers in West Virginia in an event study using linked SNAP and Medicaid data. They found that SNAP work requirements did increase the use of mental health care services among those that were enrolled in both SNAP and Medicaid before the work‐requirement waivers expired.

Hypotheses and Data

The objective of this study was to investigate the degree to which variation in pre‐pregnancy access to SNAP is reflected in the health outcomes of mothers and their infants at the time of birth, with special attention to first births. SNAP access differences were operationalized using the presence or absence of work requirements in the county of birth in the 12 months prior to conception. Vital Statistics microdata for natality from 2004 to 2018 (described in more detail later in this section) were used to test the following set of hypotheses: Hypothesis 1Mothers with more pre‐pregnancy access to SNAP will have fewer maternal complications during pregnancy and birth than those with less access.

Mothers with more pre‐pregnancy access to SNAP will have fewer maternal complications during pregnancy and birth than those with less access.

Specific factors to be tested included the rates of cesarean sections; labor inductions; and maternal hypertension, diabetes, and eclampsia. Hypothesis 2Mothers with more pre‐pregnancy access to SNAP will have babies with healthier birth outcomes than those with less access.

Mothers with more pre‐pregnancy access to SNAP will have babies with healthier birth outcomes than those with less access.

This hypothesis is very broad, as operationalizing healthy child outcomes is not always straightforward. In this case, two potential theoretical effects of SNAP access on child health must be taken into account: SNAP may not only affect the health outcomes of the babies being born, but may also affect the composition of those babies who survive to birth. For example, if SNAP access leads to health improvements that prevent some miscarriages, creates financial security that reduces the probability of abortion, or extends some pregnancies marginally through increased viability, this changes the sample of live births in an important way. Specifically, the children being born will likely have some negative birth outcomes (e.g., low birth weight) even though the effect of the program on them was actually positive. This suggests that a focus on means could miss important improvements in outcomes. In fact, SNAP could shift the distribution of gestational age to the right, but also move the left‐tail censoring point to a lower level (e.g., more survival), with ambiguous effects on mean gestational age.

In light of the potentially complex effects of SNAP across the distribution of gestational age and birth weight, these measures were approached carefully by assessing several subhypotheses. Because it is not possible to know which births may not have happened in the absence of SNAP, the goal of this study was simply to lay out estimated effects of SNAP across the distribution as fully as possible. Hypothesis 2aBecause SNAP may increase the survival of early births but also reduce poor health outcomes, the effect of more pre‐pregnancy access to SNAP on low birth weight and prematurity is ambiguous. The expected effect on high birth weight is negative.

Because SNAP may increase the survival of early births but also reduce poor health outcomes, the effect of more pre‐pregnancy access to SNAP on low birth weight and prematurity is ambiguous. The expected effect on high birth weight is negative.

The best‐case scenario in the left‐tail case is to estimate the net effect of these two potentially countervailing pressures on low birth weight and preterm indicators (not means), because no records were available for fetal deaths. Dichotomous variables based on clinical cutoffs for these outcomes were used to assess changes in important parts of the distribution (very low birth weight, low birth weight, high birth weight, very premature, and premature), as well as an indicator for infants identified as small for gestational age (SGA) in accordance with Talge et al. 20 It was expected that high birth weight—which is a risk to children, but a larger risk to mothers—would decrease if women had easier access to nutrition assistance that helped them afford healthier food and that may have facilitated Medicaid participation to monitor fetal growth and potential maternal diabetes (a cause of high birth weight). Hypothesis 2bSNAP is likely to have larger effects for populations that are more often at risk for unhealthy birth outcomes.

SNAP is likely to have larger effects for populations that are more often at risk for unhealthy birth outcomes.

Many populations are at particular nutritional risk, based on various demographic and circumstantial characteristics that make them vulnerable. Such populations may include racial minorities, immigrants, those with lower levels of education, those who are reliant on public assistance, and others. Correspondingly, the effects of SNAP waivers may have scope to make a large difference in these subgroups. After the main analysis, effects on these groups were investigated in more detail. The focus for the subgroup analysis was on first births, as these populations are both high‐risk groups and, more importantly, the groups most likely to have recently been ABAWDs.

This study used the universe of Vital Statistics natality birth data on women aged 15 to 44 years and their newborns, of all races and ethnicities and across all counties in the United States, for births in the years from 2004 to 2018. Within this group, those experiencing first births were of particular interest, as they were the most likely to be influenced by SNAP work requirements given the ABAWD criterion of being “without dependents.” However, births of higher parities were retained for some analyses, with the expectation that more may be learned about the general effects of these policy changes that may have higher administrative burdens for the larger SNAP population. Moynihan et al. 21 noted that policy designs can create burdens such as learning costs, compliance costs, and psychological costs; some of these burdens may spill over to recipients who are not directly subject to the work‐requirement policy changes. For example, SNAP participants (or potential participants) with children may have to learn more about the program to establish whether they are subject to work requirements; may have to provide extra documentation to distinguish themselves as non‐ABAWDs; or may bear added stigma when work requirements are imposed, all of which could affect SNAP participation by non‐ABAWDs. 21 Note as well the potential that some women could be ABAWDs even for non‐first births, for instance, if a child has been removed from the home or has died. For all of these reasons, the sample of later births was not strictly speaking a placebo group; however, first births were still expected to be much more affected by ABAWD waivers than later births.

It is not unusual to restrict analyses of SNAP to lower‐income populations, but no such attempt was made to restrict the population used in the main analysis. One simple reason for this is that the data did not contain income, and so it was not possible to restrict the sample to those at or near the eligibility level for SNAP. Researchers sometimes use education level as a proxy for being at‐risk, but that was not done in this case. First, the education level measures on birth certificates are not consistent over the whole sample period, and thus, it would have been necessary to construct a harmonized education variable that was somewhat coarse. It was not prudent to make sample cuts based on such a variable. Second, and perhaps surprisingly, a substantial fraction of SNAP recipients have postsecondary education; among female SNAP recipients with ages between 18 and 39 years in the 2005‐2018 American Community Surveys, more than 40% of recipients have at least some college education, and of those, nearly one‐third have degrees. 22 For completeness, a less‐educated sample (among others) was included in the heterogeneity analysis to check whether there was some higher degree of sensitivity to policy, and additional education‐restricted analyses are also provided in Appendix Tables A1, A2, A3, A4 and Figures A1 and A2.

The public‐use Vital Statistics data provide the outcomes of interest in this work, namely, birth weight, gestational age, and complications, as well as maternal outcomes such as method of delivery, risk factors, obstetric procedures, and other relevant outcomes. However, these data do not include the fine‐grained geographic identifiers that would allow a match to county‐level ABAWD waiver indicators. Because about half of the population lives within states where waiver access varies by county, a state‐level analysis would involve an unacceptable level of measurement error. As a result, the restricted‐use geocoded Vital Statistics—Natality All‐County file was used for this project instead. The outcomes were found to not vary substantially between the first births and later births. The key policy of interest was the presence of a county ABAWD waiver during the pre‐pregnancy period. This study used historical waiver data collected by the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities (CBPP) containing county‐fiscal year indicators for waiver coverage (defined as coverage for a majority of the federal fiscal year) and the start and end dates of waivers at the state‐fiscal year level from 1998 through 2018. Multiple waivers occasionally existed within a state during a given fiscal year (this occurred in 38 state‐fiscal years), resulting in multiple sets of start and end dates. In these cases, the first start and end date for each state‐fiscal year was assigned to all counties in that fiscal year, when specific start and end dates could not be reliably matched to specific counties. These dates were then used to create a complete county‐month panel.

To supplement the CBPP data, the analysis also incorporated ABAWD waiver approval documentation from 1997 to 2024 obtained from the USDA Food and Nutrition Service website. 23 These documents were converted to machine‐readable text and systematically processed using a large language model (LLM) application programming interface. The LLM was prompted to extract three pieces of information: the state requesting the waiver, the counties covered by the waiver, and the effective start and end dates. This extracted information was then standardized and merged with a comprehensive list of US counties to create a parallel county‐month panel.

Because of incomplete documentation availability on the USDA website, the coverage indicators derived from these documents substantially underestimated the actual waiver coverage. Therefore, the USDA and CBPP data were combined, creating a third indicator denoting whether either source indicated waiver coverage for a given county‐month. This combined measure identified approximately 323,000 county‐months of waiver coverage, exceeding the CBPP‐only measure by about 17,000 county‐months. This indicator serves as the preferred measure of waiver status. By this measure, just over half of the women in the study population were exposed to an ABAWD waiver in their county for the majority of the year before conception (see the bottom row of Table 2).

The data were supplemented with covariates reflecting local demographics and labor market conditions by county‐year. County‐level annual employment variables (such as the unemployment rate) were obtained from the Bureau of Labor Statistics Local Area Unemployment Statistics program. Population demographics were obtained from the National Center for Health Statistics, and poverty and income data at the county‐year level were gathered from the US Census Bureau's Small Area Income and Poverty Estimates program.

To the authors’ knowledge, this project is the first to explore the role that access to SNAP immediately prior to pregnancy may have in improving maternal and infant outcomes. It used substate variation in SNAP access to compare these outcomes across groups without having to identify which individuals received benefits. This research design provided the flexibility to use administrative data from millions of birth certificates, a novel approach to SNAP research given that the lack of direct SNAP participation indicators usually rules out such data.

Research Strategy

This study's approach to answering the research questions of interest was built around the policy variation that applies uniquely to SNAP access for childless adults. Because work requirements may be a barrier to access, variation in work‐requirement waivers was used as a measure of access to SNAP, while controlling for the labor market characteristics that underlie the assignment of waivers. Comparison of those with and without easier access allows for the identification of the causal effect of SNAP access on maternal and birth outcomes.

Although one could instead compare women who do and do not participate in SNAP, or who are and are not eligible for the program, such comparisons suffer from differential selection of women into each group; the factors that influence whether a woman participates or is eligible may be related to other factors that influence birth outcomes. The advantage of the ABAWD waivers is that they are not chosen by the individuals in the study population, and thus, they lend themselves to a quasi‐experimental approach comparing those who do and do not have exposure to the waivers but who are otherwise similar.

The research design was implemented using a treatment‐effects model of health outcomes (for mother i in county c in month t) of the form HealthOutcomeict=α+β·ABAWDwaiverct−x+γ·indiv_varsict+δ·geog_varsict+countyi+month-yeart+εict

In this model, “indiv_vars” represents person‐level variables in the data set (such as mother's race, mother's age, mother's country of origin, and mother's education). The variables in “geog_vars” describe the local demographics and labor market (such as the county poverty and unemployment rates). The “county” and “month‐year” variables are sets of fixed effects that allow for distinct baseline outcomes for each county and for each time period. The key independent variable is an indicator for a woman being exposed to an ABAWD waiver in her county prior to conception (this time period is denoted as (t − x) to represent a lookback period that may vary depending on the length of pregnancy). Finally, the health outcomes include both maternal and infant health outcomes before and at the time of birth. This model was estimated using standard two‐way fixed‐effects estimation on the full sample and a subsequent subgroup analysis of at‐risk groups. After this overall analysis, a targeted analysis of key outcomes was conducted using an event‐study framework that was more robust to the structure of the data and policy changes. 24

Main Results

First, the magnitude of the relationship between ABAWD waivers (i.e., increased SNAP access) and maternal outcomes was estimated. The outcomes of interest were diabetes (pre‐pregnancy or gestational, combined), hypertension (with separate categories for pre‐existing and gestational), eclampsia, and two delivery‐related variables: cesarean section and labor induction. According to hypothesis 1, increased SNAP access (i.e., exposure to ABAWD waivers) should reduce the prevalence of these outcomes. Exposure to ABAWD waivers was measured on a granular level, where the 12‐month period before conception (where conception month was imputed using gestational age at birth) was identified and coded as 1 for those with a waiver in their county for at least 6 months of that 12‐month period. (Because this 12‐month period did not usually align with a calendar or fiscal year, it was not unusual for waivers to be introduced or removed during this period; thus, the majority of months was selected as the decision rule for assigning treatment.) The number of months to look back for the conception month was assigned as follows: For gestational ages of 17 to 21 weeks, conception was imputed as 4 months prior to birth. For gestational ages of 22 to 25 weeks, conception was imputed as 5 months prior to birth. Similarly, gestational ages of 26 to 29 weeks, 30 to 33 weeks, 34 to 37 weeks, and 38 to 41 weeks were assigned conception lookback periods of 6, 7, 8, and 9 months, respectively. Gestational ages of 42 weeks or longer were assigned a 10‐month lookback to conception. About 76% of women were assigned lookback periods of 9 months; 17%, 8 months; and the remaining small percentage were primarily in the 7‐ and 10‐month categories. Because this is an intent‐to‐treat estimate, it should not be surprising if the relationship is very small; it reflects the predictive power of a county ABAWD waiver for individual‐level health outcomes on average, regardless of SNAP participation of the individual. (The intermediate step of SNAP participation is the presumed mechanism for this relationship, but it is not possible to provide an estimated direct effect of SNAP participation because SNAP participation is not included in the data. This issue is discussed further later in this article.)

Tables 3 through 6 provide estimates of the relationships between ABAWD waivers and a variety of maternal and child health outcomes.

The findings for mothers suggest a modest but meaningful reduction in pre‐pregnancy hypertension of 0.07 percentage points, a 5% improvement relative to the baseline prevalence of 1.5%. The estimate was slightly smaller (and less precisely estimated) for first births. The findings were similar for gestational hypertension, but they were not precisely estimated (and were a smaller percentage of baseline). For all other outcomes, the findings were near zero, and some outcomes had confidence intervals that were narrow enough to rule out any meaningful effects. (For example, the entire confidence interval for cesarean sections was extremely small relative to a mean of 0.30, and the confidence intervals for diabetes and labor inductions were similar.) The results were very similar in a parallel analysis restricted to women without a college degree (about two‐thirds of the sample), as shown in Appendix Table A1.

Table 4 also presents very small estimates. Although limited precision makes the findings tentative at best, the model consistently predicts worse outcomes in the bottom tail for first births (more low and very low birth weight infants and more preterm and very preterm deliveries), which would be consistent with improved survival on the margin. Note that the fact that “low birth weight” rather than “very low birth weight” sees most of the increase is fully consistent with survival increases on the margin, as the policy may increase very low birth weights from the nonsurvival margin while also successfully moving some births from very low to low birth weight, such that the net effect on very low birth weight could be small or even zero despite a uniform improvement in health. There is also a consistent but small suggestion of a reduction in high birth weights of about 1.4% of baseline, which is statistically significant at the 95% level for first births. When the analysis was restricted to mothers without a college degree, the results were somewhat similar, but the estimated effect on very preterm births became slightly larger and statistically significant for first births (4% increase from baseline), whereas the result for high birth weights became smaller and less precise (see Appendix Table A2). It is important to recall that these are intent‐to‐treat estimates; the effect of SNAP receipt rather than mere exposure could be much larger.

Although SNAP participation is not available in the birth records, Medicaid and WIC participation have been recorded from the year 2009 forward. To the extent these records serve as proxies for an individual being likely to participate in SNAP, perhaps even before pregnancy, they may be helpful controls in this analysis. However, an analysis using data from just these later years did not generate new insight (although the effects on pre‐pregnancy hypertension and diabetes were more precisely estimated for first births). In this limited‐year analysis, the presence or absence of the Medicaid and WIC variables did not affect the findings (results available upon request), so instead, the analysis was continued with the full range of years, omitting these proxies from the specification.

Even among first‐time mothers, access to SNAP may be expected to matter differently for women with different demographics or in different circumstances. Some traditionally higher‐risk groups, namely, older mothers, racial minorities, those without a high school degree, and those who are unmarried, were identified to investigate whether the study findings were magnified among these groups. Table 5 reports the results obtained using the same model as before for the six maternal outcomes, but estimated in subgroups based on the aforementioned characteristics.

The subgroup findings were similar to those for the overall sample of first‐time mothers. The estimates for pre‐pregnancy hypertension were similar to or slightly larger than the results for first births in Table 3, but they were less precisely estimated. There continues to be little evidence of other improvements; again, some confidence intervals only contain values very close to zero.

The subgroup point estimates in this case continue to suggest a pattern of increased low‐birth‐weight and preterm births, and possibly decreased high‐birth‐weight births, but only some of the results are statistically distinguishable from zero. Mothers with no more than a high school education were found to exhibit the largest and most precise estimates of increases in lower‐tail births (i.e., low or very low birth weight and/or preterm or very preterm births). Estimates from a two‐way fixed‐effects model such as the one described earlier have the potential to be biased when used in a context with staggered timing of treatment adoption, particularly when treatment can be both applied and removed, as with ABAWD waivers. To address the shortcomings of this standard model, the analysis was supplemented with event‐study estimates using the method developed by de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfœuille. 24 (This was implemented using de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfœuille's Stata package did_multiplegt_dyn, which the authors of this work gratefully acknowledge.) This method allows the introduction and removal of waivers to be examined separately and thus allows for dynamic, heterogeneous effects of those changes. Hamersma and Kaur suggested a significant distinction in the effects of pre–Great Recession waivers, the addition of which did not seem to affect caseloads greatly, compared to post–Great Recession waivers, the removal of which generated substantial caseload declines (unpublished results, 2025). Their analysis was at the county‐caseload level and was not restricted by age or the presence of children.

In this work, the event‐study analysis was repeated separately for the introduction and removal of waivers and for all group/outcome combinations, generating 52 figures. For brevity, first‐time mothers are plotted on the same graphs as other mothers, and the focus here is on the outcomes for which there is (a) at least suggestive evidence of effects from the earlier models or (b) new evidence of effects from the event‐study strategy. (Outcomes for which there is no conclusive evidence of an effect, because of small estimates, large standard errors, or questionable pre‐existing differences in trends [pre‐trends], are available in Appendix Figures A1 and A2.) Notes under each figure include average treatment effects for those with and without children. For tractability, quarterly (rather than monthly) units of time were used, with a window of 3 years before treatment and 3 years after. This window choice helps determine the sample to be used: In the waiver introduction analysis, treated units must have had 3 years without a waiver (before its introduction) to be included in the analysis. Similarly, in the waiver removal analysis, treated units must have had at least 3 years with a waiver (before its removal) to be included.

Figure 1 provides estimates of the effects of waiver introduction, and then waiver removal, on maternal pre‐pregnancy hypertension, gestational hypertension, eclampsia, and labor induction. (Appendix Figure A1 contains the results for diabetes and cesarean delivery.) The magnitudes of estimated treatment effects and their 95% confidence intervals are shown for both first births and later births on each graph. For each outcome, there is one graph for the waiver introduction analysis and one for waiver removal. The middle of each graph indicates the time of a waiver change (introduction or removal), and the time periods shown to the left are there to help verify that there are no apparent “treatment effects” prior to treatment; in other words, these parts of the graphs help verify common pre‐trends in that outcome between counties that did and did not introduce waivers. On the right side of each graph are the estimated effects of the policy change for each quarter after the change was made. As before, it was expected that waiver introduction would increase SNAP participation, along with its downstream outcomes, and that waiver removal would have the opposite effect.

As noted earlier, the results from the two‐way fixed‐effects model suggested increased incidence of very low birth weight and very preterm births when waivers are in place (especially for less‐educated women), possibly due to increased survival rates. However, upon accounting for the timing of treatment more flexibly, avoiding the biases of two‐way fixed‐effects models, and allowing distinct effect sizes for the introduction and removal of waivers, the event‐study design suggested that it is actually waiver removal—that is, the reinstatement of work requirements—that is associated with this increasing incidence of poor birth outcomes. The results of this main analysis can thus be summarized with the tentative conclusion that the effects of ABAWD waivers prior to pregnancy are likely small for mothers, but that the reinstatement of work requirements may have a nontrivial impact on babies’ birth weight and gestational age. In the next section, this analysis is extended for further insight.

Filling in the Story: Mechanisms and First Stage

To gain further insight into the results of the main analysis, two additional analyses were performed. First, the Vital Statistics data were used to investigate whether the mechanism for the effects that were found might involve the generation of exposure to other assistance programs. Then, American Community Survey (ACS) data were employed to directly examine the relationship between exposure to ABAWD waivers and the receipt of SNAP benefits.

Although the Vital Statistics data do not contain indicators for SNAP participation during pregnancy, they do, in some years, provide indicators for WIC and Medicaid participation. SNAP access prior to pregnancy could potentially provide informational advantages leading to higher enrollment in these programs during pregnancy (or prompter enrollment in them among participants, although the data do not provide enrollment lengths). Both WIC and Medicaid are known to improve birth outcomes (see, e.g., Figlio et al., 2009, 9 and Guldi and Hamersma, 202310). Given the small magnitude of the links between ABAWD waivers and birth outcomes identified in this work, this mechanism may be small or nonexistent, but an effort was made to glean as much insight as possible from these data.

Provided here is an analysis that is parallel to the earlier estimation, using WIC and Medicaid enrollment as outcomes for the years with available data (2009‐2018). WIC participation and Medicaid participation in these birth records are both slightly greater than 40%. Notably, the Medicaid indicators on birth certificates can reflect coverage obtained at the time of birth, so these records are not an ideal measure of Medicaid exposure during pregnancy. WIC may be expected to potentially be more responsive to SNAP exposure prior to pregnancy, both because the WIC variable likely does reflect program participation during pregnancy and because WIC is under the same federal agency of USDA Food and Nutrition Service programs. The estimates here are small and do not provide evidence that ABAWD waivers are associated with Medicaid or WIC participation. Although the estimates for WIC are slightly larger than those for Medicaid, as expected, they are still less than 1% of baseline and are not statistically different from zero. This result indicates that participation in other programs is not a likely mechanism for even the modest effects of SNAP identified earlier in this work.

To uncover a relationship between pre‐pregnancy SNAP access and birth outcomes using ABAWD waivers, it must be the case that ABAWD waivers do indeed increase SNAP participation. As noted earlier, there is evidence that this is the case at least in some places and populations. Unlike the previous literature, this study focused on a specific subpopulation: childless women who would soon become mothers. It is worth asking whether SNAP is even salient to this group of (mostly) young women.

To investigate this question, another dataset, namely, the ACS, was employed to gain some broad insight into SNAP participation among a group more like the subpopulation of interest and to consider the possible effects of ABAWD waivers. The ACS is a large, nationally representative survey dataset collected by the US Census Bureau. The survey design is a repeated cross section that provides geography down to the public use microdata area (PUMA). The combination of a large sample size and geographic precision makes this a strong dataset relative to alternatives, even though it allows for the identification of only motherhood status and not pregnancy. Consistent 2000‐2010 PUMA aggregations defined by IPUMS USA (originally, the Integrated Public Use Microdata Series) were used and matched to the ACS, with the county‐level waiver data aggregated in the same way to link it to the survey at the level of consistent‐PUMA and month. (Note that, because PUMA definitions are updated after each decennial census, IPUMS created the “consistent‐PUMA” to allow comparability across 2000 and 2010 PUMA definitions. Each consistent‐PUMA is an aggregation of one or more 2010 PUMAs that align closely with a corresponding aggregation of 2000 PUMAs.) Annual data from 2005‐2018 were used, and the sample was restricted to women aged 18 to 40 years, to focus on a population most likely to experience first pregnancies. Altogether, the extract contained 11.4 million observations, about 3 million of which represented women without children at the time of the survey.

Two analyses were conducted to help provide insight into the questions of (a) the salience of SNAP and (b) the relevance of ABAWD waivers to this subpopulation. First, note that this sample of young childless women had lower participation in SNAP than women with children. Applying population weights, SNAP participation was nearly 25% among women with children and only 10.8% among those without. One can conclude, then, that although young childless women do participate in SNAP, their lower levels of participation suggest less salience of the program for them than for other vulnerable groups (especially those who already have children).

Second, the analysis considered the basic question of whether ABAWD waivers are correlated with participation by these women in SNAP (i.e., the “first stage”). Consistent with the county‐level approach, the preferred measure was developed using the combined indicator, estimating the percentage of each consistent‐PUMA population covered by a waiver in a given year according to either the CBPP data or the supplemental USDA documentation analysis. A person was considered to be exposed to a waiver if more than half of their consistent‐PUMA was exposed during a given year. This analysis moved directly to the event‐study framework used earlier 24 because it provides improvements over the two‐way fixed‐effects approach. Figure 3 displays the main results, which are divided into a section for waiver introduction (top panel) and another section for waiver removal (bottom panel). For women without children, the introduction of waivers seems to have a small but positive impact on SNAP participation, particularly relative to those with children. As reported in the Figure 3 footnote, the average effect was estimated to be 0.0075 for women without children (7% of baseline) and to be smaller and statistically insignificant for mothers. However, the pre‐trends for those without children were also slightly positive, so this result is not necessarily robust. In contrast, the removal of waivers was found to show clear evidence of a decline in SNAP participation for women without children. With estimates for pretreatment effects close to zero and with posttreatment estimates that were usually statistically significantly negative, more confidence can be placed in this conclusion. Waiver removal is predicted to reduce participation in SNAP by 1.88 percentage points for women without children (17% of baseline), whereas the estimated effect for women with children is statistically insignificant (and less than 2% of baseline in that case).

This combination of results suggests that ABAWD waivers have their largest impact not when they are introduced (when work requirements are paused) but when they are removed (when work requirements are reimposed). The finding of larger impacts on SNAP participation from waiver removal aligns with the findings of more substantial health effects on newborns only when waiver removal is examined.

Discussion and Conclusion

A key strength of this study is its examination of a question that has not yet been explored in any depth: the relationship between pre‐pregnancy SNAP access and maternal and infant health outcomes. Although there is an emerging literature on the role of pre‐pregnancy health, this study advances the understanding of the role of pre‐pregnancy nutritional assistance. Alongside but distinct from programs available during pregnancy, preconception SNAP access may provide a mechanism for better maternal and infant health outcomes. Developing an improved understanding of policy levers for improving maternal and infant health outcomes is increasingly important because of recent policy changes around pregnancy; births among vulnerable populations are expected to increase in some geographic areas in the coming years as a result of limited abortion access (see early evidence in Dench et al., 202425). Survey data suggest that the most common reasons for abortion often include material hardship, suggesting an increasing need for effective public assistance. 26 This study helps identify the role that pre‐pregnancy SNAP might play in promoting the health of women and their children in this current landscape.

Another key strength of this study is the leveraging of county‐level variation in SNAP access to understand its effects. This was possible because of the combination of restricted‐use Vital Statistics data (with county identifiers) and substantial county‐level variation in one particular SNAP eligibility criterion for childless adults: work requirements. Most SNAP eligibility rules are at the federal level, with some variation in implementation at the state level, but there is rarely an opportunity to understand SNAP at such a fine level of geography while still using a national sample. This allows the effects of SNAP access to be distinguished from the variety of other factors that differentiate states and counties from one another in a way that can also be correlated with maternal and infant outcomes.

The findings of this study suggest some negative effects on child outcomes when work requirements are reimposed after an ABAWD waiver expires. More births were found in the extreme ends of the distribution when waivers were removed, with a worsening of very low birth weights and very premature deliveries. Little to no effect of ABAWD waivers was found on most other maternal and infant health outcomes, although the finding of a decrease in inductions could reflect an increase in early births. That said, one must bear in mind that the means for some rare outcomes were low and that the fraction of women with SNAP exposure (or near‐exposure) was still small as a fraction of all births, so not all nonfindings were precise zeros.

This analysis faced some limitations. By necessity, this study relied on an “intent‐to‐treat” analysis, because it is impossible to know which mothers actually received SNAP in the year prior to birth. In addition, the women who were eligible for SNAP benefits before giving birth could not be directly identified. However, examination of a complementary sample in the ACS of young childless women where SNAP was included in the survey reveals participation rates of approximately 11% and demonstrates a responsiveness to ABAWD waivers (especially their removal) in this population. Future work might further investigate other SNAP rules that vary across geography (although most of that variation is at the state rather than local level). Researchers could also look further into geographic issues, perhaps identifying counties with high poverty rates or high SNAP participation rates to create a high‐need (or high SNAP salience) subsample. The key interpretation issue will be that any relationship found between SNAP work requirements (or other SNAP rules that vary across geography) and maternal/child outcomes is likely to understate the effects of being eligible for or participating directly in SNAP, given that so many births in each county are likely to be untouched by SNAP and not near the margin of participation regardless of work requirements. More work is needed to concentrate attention on those women most affected by the policy to evaluate the ways that SNAP plays out in their pregnancy health outcomes and in the health outcomes of their children.
