Immunotherapy in pediatric bone sarcomas: Current progress and future directions
Authors: Rachel Weil, David M Loeb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPediatric bone sarcomas continue to present substantial therapeutic challenges in the metastatic, relapsed, or refractory setting, where clinical outcomes have remained largely static for decades despite advances in multimodal therapy. This review summarizes emerging immunotherapeutic strategies aimed at overcoming the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, low mutational burden and limited targetable antigens characteristic of these tumors. Current clinical investigations span multiple immunotherapeutic modalities, including adoptive natural killer cell therapies, cytokine-based immune priming, CAR T cell approaches and a growing array of RNA-based therapeutics. Checkpoint inhibitors are also under evaluation, particularly in combination with immunomodulatory or targeted agents. Collectively, these trials highlight both the promise and limitations of immunotherapy in pediatric sarcomas and underscore the need for deeper understanding of sarcoma immune biology to guide future, more effective therapeutic strategies.KEYWORDS: Osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, immunotherapy, clinical trial, natural killer cells, innate immunity, immune checkpoint inhibitors, mRNA vaccines

Introduction

Sarcomas comprise a diverse group of malignant tumors that arise from bone or soft tissues. Accounting for 15% of all pediatric cancers, sarcomas represent a major clinical challenge in children and adolescents. Among pediatric bone tumors, Ewing sarcoma (ES) and osteosarcoma (OS)1 are the most common and clinically significant subtypes and are the focus of this review. Patients with localized disease generally achieve favorable outcomes with standard treatment approaches that combine surgery and chemoradiotherapy. However, patients with metastatic, relapsed or refractory disease face a markedly worse prognosis, with survival rates showing little improvement over the past three decades.2 This lack of improvement highlights the need for innovative interventions.

Immunotherapy represents one promising therapeutic avenue. Although it is a well-established treatment modality in oncology, its efficacy in sarcomas has been limited. This limited success is largely due to the heterogeneity of these tumors, the paucity of well-defined targetable antigens, and the challenges associated with conducting adequately powered clinical trials for rare subtypes.1 Moreover, immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments (TME), low tumor mutational burdens (TMB), and weak immunogenicity of tumor‑associated antigens (TAAs) further restrict therapeutic effectiveness.1 Nevertheless, deeper characterization of the sarcoma TME, combined with the implementation of combinatorial treatment approaches, is driving the development of more precise and potentially effective immunotherapeutic strategies.

To systematically characterize the current state of immunotherapy clinical investigation in pediatric OS and ES, we conducted a structured search of clinical trials filtering for trials enrolling pediatric patients with osteosarcoma or Ewing sarcoma and incorporating immunotherapy-based interventions. Trials were included regardless of current recruitment status, capturing both actively recruiting, not yet recruiting, and completed/terminated studies order to provide a comprehensive picture of the investigational landscape. Studies focusing exclusively on rhabdomyosarcoma or soft tissue sarcomas without an ES or OS cohort were excluded. The identified trials were organized thematically based on the primary therapeutic modality under investigation, yielding the following categories: natural killer cell therapies, cytokine-based approaches, CAR T cell therapies, RNA therapeutics, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (Figure 1). This review aims to achieve two objectives. First, to provide a structured and comprehensive overview of current immunotherapy clinical trials in pediatric OS and ES, organized by therapeutic modality. Second, to identify emerging strategies and areas of unmet need that could inform future directions for clinical investigation. By synthesizing the current trial landscape in this way, we aim to highlight both the progress made and the gaps that remain in developing safe and effective immune-based treatments for patients with metastatic or recurrent pediatric bone sarcomas.

Natural killer cells

Natural killer cells are innate immune lymphocytes that recognize and eliminate transformed or virally infected cells through a mechanism that does not require prior antigen sensitization or MHC-restricted presentation.3 Whether an NK cell kills its target is determined by the integration of competing inhibitory and activating signals received through surface receptors. Inhibitory signaling is mediated primarily through killer-cell immunoglobulin receptors (KIR), which bind HLA class I molecules HLA-A, -B, and -C, and through the CD94/NK group 2 member A (NKG2A) heterodimer, which recognizes HLA-E.4 Activation is driven by receptors like natural cytotoxic receptors (NCRs) and NK group 2 member D (NKG2D), which detect stress‑induced ligands such as MICA/B on the surface of damaged cells5 with DNAM-1 serving as a co-stimulatory amplifier of this activating signal.6 Upon activation, NK cells kill target cells through degranulation of granzyme- and perforin-containing cytotoxic granules, upregulation of death‑receptor ligands, and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (tumor necrosis factor-α and interferon-γ), which further amplify the local immune response.7

NK cell-based approaches carry a compelling biological rationale in pediatric bone sarcomas. Osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma frequently downregulate MHC class I expression as an immune‑evasion strategy,8 a mechanism that renders the tumors more susceptible to NK‑cell killing as NK cells are activated when inhibitory KIR signals are absent.9 Additionally, sarcoma cells have been shown to upregulate NKG2D ligands including MICA and MICB under cellular stress, supporting NK‑cell activation.9 These features make sarcomas theoretically attractive targets for NK‑cell-based immunotherapy, and preclinical models have borne this out.

Pre-clinical studies in OS and ES models support the development of clinical approaches using NK cells. ES cells demonstrate the greatest sensitivity, maintaining susceptibility to NK‑cell killing even at low effector-to-target ratios.10 Functional studies blocking specific activating receptors have demonstrated that NKG2D and DNAM-1 are both required for full cytotoxic activity in ES,8 while NKG2D blockade alone was sufficient to impair killing of OS cells.11 NK cells with KIR-HLA mismatch show enhanced cytotoxicity against OS cell in vitro, an effect that was further amplified when HLA class I surface expression on tumor cells was reduced.9 In vivo modeling showed that NK‑cell infusion reduced pulmonary metastasis in ES xenograft models12 and in OS orthotopic mouse models, intratumoral NK‑cell injection combined with systemic IL-2 suppressed tumor growth and prolonged survival.9

Within the clinical arena, a phase I clinical trial enrolled 16 patients to study the effects of infusing activated NK cells following lymphodepleting chemotherapy, with or without accompanying IL-15 (NCT01875601). Eligibility included patients with refractory pediatric malignant solid tumors. The patients were divided into two cohorts. All patients received pre-NK lymphodepleting chemotherapy with cyclophosphamide. Cohort A received escalating doses of activated autologous NK cells to demonstrate the feasibility of generating cells, establish the tolerability of this treatment approach, and potentially identify an MTD. Patients enrolled in cohort B received activated autologous NK cells plus escalating doses of IL-15. While this study has completed, results have yet to be published. A current study, actively recruiting, is testing whether an infusion of NK cells added to the standard sarcoma chemotherapy regimen GEM/DOX (gemcitabine and docetaxel) can improve outcomes in patients with relapsed disease or who have not responded to prior therapies (NCT05634369). The goal of this study is to determine safety and efficacy, as well as to identify any toxicities related to the addition of TGF-β imprinted NK cells to the chemotherapy regimen, looking at the 6-month progression‑free survival achieved with this treatment in patients with relapsed or refractory OS or ES. To circumvent weak NK cells in cancer patients, another study (NCT02890758) looked at the effects of infusing patients with NK cells from non-HLA matched donors. Some patients also received ALT-803, an IL-15 “superagonist” that promotes NK cell survival and proliferation and supports their cancer-fighting characteristics. Another phase I trial (NCT03420963) is looking at the side effects and best dose of cord blood-derived, ex vivo expanded, allogeneic NK cells and how well they work when given together with cyclophosphamide and etoposide in treating pediatric patients with relapsed or refractory solid tumors. A recently completed trial (NCT02849366) enrolled patients with recurrent sarcoma to look at the safety and the short- and long-term efficacy of combined therapy using cryoablation and NK cells. Following cryosurgery to clear the tumor, patients received multiple NK cell infusions. While completed, this study has yet to post results. NK cells are also being studied in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors. One such study (NCT03941262) is evaluating the safety and preliminary efficacy of SNK01 (autologous natural killer cells) as a single agent and in combination with avelumab or pembrolizumab for treatment of patients with advanced and/or metastatic refractory sarcomas that have failed three or more prior lines of conventional standard of care therapy. SKN01 monotherapy has been safe and well tolerated in patients with dose escalation still ongoing. While anti-tumor activity has yet to be fully elucidated, initial results show SKN01 slows and potentially halts disease progression and improves quality of life for patients.13 Finally, ongoing studies are also investigating therapies that activate NK cells in vivo. NK cells express an inhibitory receptor, NKG2A, which, when bound to HLA-E on tumor cells, inhibits NK cell cytotoxicity. Monalizumab blocks the interaction between NKG2A and its ligand. The combination of monalizumab and durvalumab (anti‑PD-L1) may promote antitumor immunity by enhancing both innate and adaptive immunity (NCT02671435). The safety, antitumor activity and pharmacodynamics in patients with advanced solid tumors was assessed and the results support further exploration of combinatorial approaches that target both innate and adaptive immune responses.14

Despite this activity (Table 1), clinical translation of NK cell therapies in pediatric sarcomas has been limited by several converging factors. While the preclinical rationale is strong, the immunosuppressive sarcoma TME impairs NK cell function following adoptive transfer. For example, TGF-β secreted by tumor and stromal cells downregulates NKG2D expression on NK cells15 and the M2-skewed macrophage environment further dampens NK cell activation.16 Autologous NK cells derived from cancer patients are often functionally impaired at baseline, a challenge that allogeneic and cord blood‑derived approaches aim to address.17 The most promising direction for NK cell treatment are combination strategies, such as pairing NK cells with cytokines to sustain in vivo persistence, checkpoint inhibitors to release NK cells from NKG2A‑mediated suppression, and chemotherapy to reduce immunosuppressive myeloid burden that limits NK cell efficacy in the tumor. Advancing these combination approaches with biomarker strategies, particularly the assessment of NKG2D ligand expression, KIR-ligand mismatch, and TME composition, will be essential to identifying patients most likely to benefit.

Cytokines

A common theme in the immunotherapy field is the use of immunomodulatory drugs in conjunction with other treatments to prime the immune system to be more active, thereby making the immunotherapy more effective. Cytokines are commonly used to stimulate the immune system by modulating antigen presentation and T‑cell activation,2 aiming to combat immune deficits that characterize these tumors. One of the most widely used cytokines is interleukin‑2 (IL-2), which functions to stimulate T‑cell proliferation, induce generation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and facilitate the maintenance of NK cells.15 While effective, high-doses of IL-2 result in significant toxicity, especially capillary leak syndrome making it unlikely to be successful as a single‑agent therapy but advantageous when used at lower doses in combination with other drugs. Interleukin-7 (IL-7) has also been investigated for its role in immune reconstitution, particularly in lymphodepleted patients, where it promotes CD4+ T cell recovery and may improve the durability of vaccine-induced immune responses.18

One completed trial (NCT00001566) evaluated IL-2-augmented immunotherapy to maintain remission in patients with metastatic or recurrent ES. Fifty-two patients with translocation-positive recurrent or metastatic ES underwent prechemotherapy T-cell harvest. Following completion of standard multimodal therapy, 30 patients proceeded with immunotherapy treatment, divided into three groups. All cohorts received autologous T cells, influenza vaccinations (as an adjuvant), and dendritic cells pulsed with peptides derived from tumor‑specific translocation breakpoints and E7, a peptide that binds HLA-A2. Cohort 1 received moderate dose IL-2, cohort 2 received low dose IL-2 and cohort 3 did not receive IL-2. While patients receiving immunotherapy demonstrated high survival rates and low toxicity, they failed to trigger a sufficiently consistent immune response. Further research is needed to enhance the refinement of antigen selection and implement diverse techniques for dendritic‑cell (DC) maturation.19 Similarly, another study (NCT00923351) evaluated whether adjuvant immunotherapy following standard cytotoxic therapy could improve outcomes in patients with recurrent or metastatic pediatric sarcomas including ES. At enrollment, patients underwent apheresis for lymphocyte collection and percutaneous biopsy for tumor lysate acquisition. Following completion of standard antineoplastic therapy, patients received autologous lymphocyte infusions alongside dendritic cell vaccine pulsed with autologous tumor lysate and keyhole limpet hemocyanin, with a subset also receiving recombinant IL-7 to support immune reconstitution. Of the 43 patients enrolled, 29 proceeded to immunotherapy and had no adverse effects from the regimen. Five-year overall survival by intent-to-treat analysis was 51%, with outcomes differing substantially by histology and by depth of response to prior chemotherapy, with patients achieving no residual disease faring markedly better than those with residual tumor. Critically, measurable T‑cell responses to autologous tumor lysate were detected in 62% of immunotherapy recipients and correlated with improved survival, providing direct biological evidence that treatment was engaging the immune system in patients who benefited. Additionally, IL-7 administration was associated with significantly enhanced CD4 count recovery, suggesting a role for cytokine-supported immune reconstitution in sustaining antitumor immunity following lymphodepleting chemotherapy.20 A pilot trial (NCT00101309) is evaluating the safety and immunologic activity of a fusion‑cell vaccine combined with IL-2 in pediatric patients with relapsed or refractory ES. The vaccine is generated by fusing autologous tumor cells with EBV-transformed B-lymphoblastoid cells derived from the patient’s own blood, creating a hybrid construct designed to enhance antigen presentation and stimulate a tumor-directed immune response. Patients receive the vaccine subcutaneously on a defined schedule along with IL-2 administered twice daily during alternating weeks. Those who demonstrated evidence of antitumor immune activation following vaccination proceeded to leukapheresis for CTL collection and expansion, with a subset subsequently receiving CTL infusions along with continued IL-2 to assess whether adoptive transfer of tumor-reactive T cells can further amplify the antitumor response. Another ongoing clinical trial (NCT01590069) is studying the side effects and best dose of aerosolized IL-2 to see how well it works in treating osteosarcoma patients with lung metastasis. Future directions will include identifying endogenous cytokines with tumor-promoting properties and conducting clinical trials aimed at inhibiting those signals.

Taken together, the cytokine trial data in pediatric bone sarcomas reveal that cytokine augmentation can improve immune reconstitution and, in some cases, generate measurable anti-tumor T‑cell responses (Table 2). Despite this, these responses have not translated consistently into durable clinical benefit. The most informative finding across these trials is from the IL-7 trial (NCT00923351), where T‑cell reactivity to autologous tumor lysate in 62% of recipients and its association with improved outcomes provides direct evidence that immune engagement is clinically meaningful when it can be achieved. This suggests that the lack of success is not due to the capacity of the immune system to respond, but rather the difficulty in sustaining that response in the context of a tumor that presents few well-defined antigens and actively suppresses the T‑cell infiltration needed to promote tumor killing. Future cytokine strategies should prioritize agents that address the upstream barriers (DC maturation, antigen presentation, and sustained T‑cell survival) to immune engagement rather than boosting the proliferation and activity of T cells and NK cells that have not been adequately primed or recruited to begin with.

CAR T cells

Chimeric Antigen Receptor (CAR) T cell therapy involves the genetic modification of a patient’s T lymphocytes to express a synthetic chimeric antigen receptor capable of recognizing and eliminating tumor cells independently of MHC‑mediated antigen presentation,21 a phenomenon seen in sarcomas.8 The CAR construct comprises three functional components: an extracellular domain (the variable region of a B cell receptor specific for the target), a transmembrane domain, and an intracellular signaling domain (including various T cell activation domains from costimulatory molecules that associate with T cell receptors).22 While CAR T cell therapy has shown incredible success in treating hematological malignancies, its effectiveness in treating solid tumors remains more challenging due to barriers such as antigen heterogeneity, limiting the coverage of any single CAR construct, poor T cell trafficking into the tumor, and an immunosuppressive TME that exhausts or inactivates transferred cells upon arrival.21

Multiple tumor-associated antigens have been identified as CAR T targets in OS and ES, each with varying degrees of preclinical support. In OS, the targets for CAR T‑cell treatment investigated thus far are HER-2, GD2,23 B7-H3,24 IL-11 Rα,25 IGF-1 R, and EphA2.26 Although OS cells exhibit variable levels of HER-2, HER-2 directed CAR T cells generated an antigen‑specific immune response against OS cells in vitro.27 Mice bearing OS xenografts exhibited tumor and lung metastasis regression after CAR T cell infusion. HER-2-specific T cells demonstrate activity against the tumor-initiating cell (TIC) compartment within established bone tumors, impairing the capacity for OS cell self-renewal.28 GD2-directed CAR T cells constructed with CD28 and OX40 costimulatory domains successfully eliminated GD2‑positive OS cell lines in vitro,2 but lost efficacy in OS xenograft models due to an accumulation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). The addition of all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA), which depletes MDSCs, restored CAR T cell antitumor activity.29 B7-H3 CAR T cells built using a binding domain derived from MGA271, an antibody proven safe in human clinical trials that preferentially recognizes B7-H3 on tumor tissue over normal tissue, showed significant antitumor activity in vivo.25 However, efficacy depended on the level of B7-H3 expression at the tumor cell surface, with tumors expressing low levels of the antigen demonstrating minimal response, suggesting that patient selection based on B7-H3 expression will be important for clinical translation.24 In orthotopic OS models, both primary tumor response and metastatic progression showed dose-dependent responses to B7-H3 CAR T infusion.30 IL-11 Rα-targeted CAR T cells effectively eliminated OS cells in vitro, however as not all OS cells strongly express IL-11 Rα, CAR T therapy needs to be combined with other treatments to provide a more effective approach in vivo.31 CAR T cells directed against IGF-1 R and ROR1 showed efficacy against OS cells both in vivo and in vitro.28 These CAR T cells are highly effective at preventing tumor growth in localized and disseminated sarcoma xenograft models, leading to increased survival.28 EphA2-directed CAR T cells demonstrated potent anti-tumor activity across both localized and disseminated metastatic OS models, achieving marked tumor reduction and in some cases complete eradication. However, tumors that survived treatment showed enrichment of EphA2-negative cell populations, a resistance mechanism based on immune escape.32 In ES, tumor‑specific antigens proposed as targets include VEGFR2, IGF-1 R, ROR1, GD2, B7-H3 and EphA2.33 VEGFR2 mediates VEGF signaling in endothelial cells and drives VEGF-mediated angiogenesis.34 VEGFR2-specific CAR T cells efficiently and selectively targeted VEGFR2-expressing cells, exhibiting antigen-specific degranulation and cytokine responses upon interactions with their targets.21 While yet to be tried in ES cells, human ES tumors are highly vascularized and express VEGFR2 on tumor endothelial cells indicating they could be a promising candidate for VEGFR2-targeted CAR-T cell therapy.35 CAR T cells targeting IGF-1 R and ROR1 from healthy donors and sarcoma patients exhibit targeted cytotoxicity in vitro. EphA2 CAR T cells demonstrate potent anti-tumor activity in vitro and are capable of eradicating established ES tumors in vivo.28

In the clinical setting, many trials are focusing on using B7-H3 expressing CAR T cells to treat pediatric solid tumors. In a phase I, open-label, non-randomized study (NCT04483778) for pediatric patients with relapsed or refractory non-CNS solid tumors to evaluate the safety, feasibility and efficacy of administering T cell products derived from the research participant’s blood that have been genetically modified to express B7-H3-specific receptor to target and kill B7-H3 expressing tumor cells. Patients in cohort A will receive B7-H3-specific CAR T cells only, whereas patients in cohort B will receive CAR T cells targeting both B7-H3 and CD19. The rationale for this approach is that the CD19 CAR will engage the patient’s own B lymphocytes, which naturally function as antigen‑presenting cells, to stimulate the expansion and persistence of the infused CAR T cells. The CAR T cells will include the protein EGFRt or HER2tG that can be used to track and destroy the CAR T cells in case of toxicity. A very similar study (NCT03618381) is evaluating the safety, feasibility and efficacy of administering T‑cell products derived from the patient’s blood that have been genetically engineered to express an EGFR-specific receptor to target and kill tumor cells expressing EGFR and the selection-suicide marker EGFRt. EGFRt is a protein incorporated into the cell with the EGFR CAR which is used to identify the modified T cells and can be used as a tag to destroy the modified T cells via antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), if necessary. Like the previous study, patients in this study are divided into two cohorts: patients in cohort A will receive EGFR-specific CAR T cells only, while patients in cohort B will receive CAR T cells targeting both EGFR and CD19. The 3CAR study (NCT04897321) is a phase I clinical trial evaluating the use of autologous T cells genetically engineered to express B7-H3 CAR for pediatric patients with relapsed or refractory B7-H3+ solid tumors. The goal of this study is to evaluate the safety and maximum tolerated dose of B7-H3 CAR T cells and to evaluate the antitumor activity of B7-H3 CAR T cells.

Other targets for CAR T cells are also being studied. Another trial (NCT04539366) is investigating the efficacy and safety of GD2-specific CAR T cells for patients with relapsed or refractory OS. Aside from measuring the feasibility, safety and maximum tolerated dose, this study also aims to measure persistence of adoptive GD2 CAR T cells and correlate this with anti-tumor effects. Another study (NCT05312411) focusing on patients with relapsed or refractory OS is developing fluorescein-specific (FITC-Ew) CAR T cells that recognize the fluorescein tag from folate-fluorescein (UB-TT170). Following chemotherapy, patients receive CAR T infusions. A few days after CAR T infusion, patients will receive infusions of UB-TT170 with the dose slowly increasing until the maximum dose is reached. The UB-TT170 attaches to tumor cells, flagging them to attract the CAR T cells, ideally leading to tumor cell death.

Despite the multitude of targets and approaches under investigation (Table 3), CAR T cell therapy in pediatric bone sarcomas face persistent biological obstacles that preclinical models have only partially addressed. The two most fundamental are antigen heterogeneity and the immunosuppressive TME. B7-H3 is emerging as the most promising target across both OS and ES given its broad and relatively uniform expression across sarcoma subtypes, which mitigates the antigen escape risk that undermines single-antigen approaches targeting more heterogeneously expressed molecules. Two areas of focus that provide potentially promising clinical benefit are the ATRA combination as it provides a proof of concept that myeloid TME remodeling can rescue CAR T efficacy and the approach of combining multiple CAR constructs targeting different antigens simultaneously. These represent a strong mechanistically rational approach to overcoming crucial barriers that have limited CAR T success to date.

RNA therapeutics

RNA-based therapeutics represent a mechanistically diverse and rapidly expanding modality with unique advantages over conventional small-molecule and protein-based approaches.36 Because their activity is governed by nucleotide sequence complementarity rather than protein binding site occupancy, RNA therapeutics can be designed to address previously undruggable targets including non-coding genomic elements.36 Their sequence-dependent mechanism streamlines manufacturing as modifications require changes to the sequence rather than fundamental redesign of the production process, enabling faster and more cost-effective development cycles than traditional therapeutic platforms.37 Across the multiple RNA therapeutic modalities explored in OS and ES, preclinical activity is mechanistically compelling, yet clinical translation is limited. This gap is reflective of challenges related to delivery of RNA cargo to tumor cells in vivo with sufficient efficiency and stability, the immunosuppressive TME that limits immune engagement even when RNA-based immune activation is achieved, and the absence of well-validated molecular targets that are consistently expressed across sarcoma subtypes.36,37 Understanding where each modality sits relative to these barriers helps explain the current state of the field and identify which approaches are most realistically likely to reach clinical translation.

RNA interference (RNAi) is a biological process in which non-coding RNA molecules regulate gene expression by promoting the degradation of specifically targeted mRNAs.36,38 Two distinct RNA classes, siRNA and miRNA, activate RNAi pathway through related but mechanistically distinct mechanisms. siRNA molecules are 21–23 nucleotide double‑stranded RNA duplexes characterized by two 3’ nucleotide overhangs. Endogenous or exogenous double-stranded RNA is cleaved by the ribonuclease (RNase) III-like enzyme, Dicer, to generate siRNA duplexes, which are loaded into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) complex in the cytoplasm. Within RISC, the AGO2 protein cleaves and ejects the sense passenger strand, leaving the antisense guide strand to direct the active complex to complementary mRNA, resulting in mRNA fragmentation and decay.36,39 In the context of cancer, synthetic siRNA-based therapies are designed to suppress oncogenic genes and target pathways involved in tumorigenesis.36 In pediatric bone sarcomas, preclinical siRNA studies have focused primarily on three biological processes: angiogenesis, metastasis, and cell survival. Anti-angiogenic approaches targeting VEGF and Ape1 have demonstrated tumor‑growth suppression and reduced microvessel density in osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma animal models,40–42 while siRNA-mediated silencing of CD15143 and SALL444 has reduced metastatic burden in OS xenograft models through disruption of the GSK-3β/β-catenin43 and the Wnt/β-catenin44 pathways respectively. Despite this preclinical activity, RNAi-based strategies in sarcoma not yet been translated to the clinical setting, reflecting the challenge of achieving efficient and stable in vivo siRNA delivery without off-target effects or immune activation from the delivery vehicle itself.

In contrast to siRNA, miRNAs are endogenous single‑stranded non-coding RNAs of approximately 21–23 nucleotides that regulate gene expression through partial complementarity to the 3’ untranslated region (3’ UTR) of target mRNAs, leading to mRNA degradation and inhibition of translation.36,39 Therapeutic strategies include miRNA mimics, which restore the activity of downregulated tumor suppressor miRNAs, and anti-miRs, which inhibit oncogenic miRNAs.36 In pediatric bone sarcomas, a consistent pattern has emerged in which multiple tumor suppressor miRNAs are downregulated and share convergent roles in suppressing proliferation.36 The miR-34 family is among the most studied,45–47 with downregulation of miR-34a associated with adverse clinical outcomes in both ES48 and OS patients.47 miR-34a acts as a tumor suppressor activating members of the Notch pathway,36 cell cycle regulators,49 MAPK signaling proteins50 and autophagy proteins.51 Restoring miR-34a increased chemosensitivity and reduces invasiveness through suppression of Notch1 and EF1.52 Similarly, miR-16,53 miR-22,54 miR-124,55 and miR-3056 family members are recurrently downregulated in osteosarcoma, and their restoration consistently suppresses tumor growth,38,57–59 reduces metastasis,53,54 and in several cases sensitizes cells to standard chemotherapy agents including doxorubicin and cisplatin.55,60 These miRNAs act through distinct but overlapping oncogenic pathways. miR-16 targets the Raf1-MEK1/2-ERK1/2 axis and cell cycle regulators.53,61,62 miR-22 suppresses autophagy-mediated chemoresistance through HMGB1 and Wnt/β-catenin signaling.54,55 miR-124 promotes mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition and reduces MMP driven invasion.55,63 miR-30 family members suppress key transcription factors including MEFD2, Runx2, and Sox9.56,64 In ES, miR-30a-5p specifically targets CD99, a surface antigen induced by the EWS-FLI1 oncogene, with overexpression reducing proliferation and invasion.65 Not all dysregulated miRNAs in sarcomas are tumor suppressive. miR-25 and miR-221 are upregulated in osteosarcoma and function as oncomiRs, promoting tumor progression through distinct mechanisms.36 miR-25, located within the minichromosome maintenance protein 7 (MCM7) gene, targets the Hippo pathway regulator Merlin66,67 as well as the tumor suppressor DKK368 and the cell-cycle inhibitor p27,69 collectively enhancing proliferation, invasiveness, and drug resistance. miR-221 is associated with lung metastasis70 and chemoresistance through regulation of the PI3K-Akt pathway71 and Wnt signaling.72 The identification of both tumor suppressive and oncogenic miRNAs in pediatric bone sarcomas highlights the complexity of miRNA-mediated regulation in these tumors and the need for careful target selection in therapeutic development.

Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) are short single-stranded sequences that bind complementarily to target mRNA, thereby modulating protein expression.36 Traditionally, ASOs act through DNA-RNA hybridization, but advancements in the field have led to the development of RNA-based ASOs which have distinct mechanisms that enhance their pharmacological properties by promoting ASO-induced mRNA degradation.36 Although ASOs are not yet used clinically for sarcoma treatment, they have received FDA approval for managing infectious diseases, cardiovascular diseases, metabolic and endocrine diseases, and neurological and neuromuscular diseases.73 In ES cells, treatment with an ASO targeting EWS-FLI1 induced cell cycle arrest at the G0/G1 phase, accompanied by reduced levels of G1 cyclins and cyclin D1/E.74 Phosphorothioate ASOs targeting survivin inhibit OS cell proliferation and trigger Fas-mediated apoptosis in a dose-dependent manner.36,75 A vivo morpholino (DkkMo) was used to block the expression of Dkk-1 in OS cells and xenograft mouse models. Reduction in Dkk-1 and Aldh1a1 expression resulted in reduced tumor growth, preservation of bone volume and structure, and increased tumor necrosis76

RNA aptamers are single-stranded RNA oligonucleotides that adopt complex three-dimensional conformations determined by their sequence, allowing them to bind protein and small-molecule targets with high affinity and selectivity through shape complementarity and non-covalent interactions.36 Aptamers are chemically synthesized rather than biologically produced, conferring advantages in stability, manufacturing scalability, and the ability to penetrate tissues that larger antibody molecules cannot.36

Guide RNA (gRNA) for CRISPR/Cas9-directed knockout allows for programmable genome modification. While several delivery methods for Cas9 protein and sgRNA in vitro have been successful, applying such methods (like plasmid DNA, viral transfection or electroporation) in vivo and in a clinical setting has yet to prove successful.77 The most effective strategy so far has been ex vivo CRISPR-based treatments followed by reintroduction of the modified cells into the patient.36 A PEG-PEI-Cholesterol (PPC) lipopolymer conjugated with an OS cell-specific aptamer (LC09) was developed to deliver CRISPR/Cas9 plasmids encoding VEGFA gRNA and Cas9.78 Targeting OS cells, this system effectively reduced VEGFA expression and secretion, leading to decreased angiogenesis and lung metastasis without apparent toxicity in mouse models.79 CRISPR technology has also been used to silence EWS-FLI1 in ES cells,79 where permanent gene inactivation significantly inhibited cell proliferation and induced cellular senescence.80

mRNA vaccines represent the most clinically advanced RNA-based strategy in pediatric sarcomas and the modality with the most immediate translational promise. Their ability to synthetically encode tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), combined with flexible and rapid manufacturing and a demonstrated capacity to activate robust antigen‑specific T‑cell responses, addresses several of the key barriers that have limited adaptive immunotherapy in sarcomas.36,80,81 Cancer mRNA vaccines can be divided into two categories: direct administration of mRNA to drive antigen expression, or dendritic cell-based vaccines, in which DCs are transfected ex vivo with mRNA encoding tumor antigens before being reintroduced to the patient.82 After delivery, the mRNA is translated into protein, which is subsequently processed by the proteasome into peptide fragments. These peptides are presented on major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I molecules to activate antigen-specific CD8+ T cells, or on MHC class II molecules to stimulate CD4+ T cell responses.83 To optimize intracellular trafficking and antigen presentation, mRNA constructs can include signal peptides or transmembrane domains that direct proper subcellular localization of the encode protein. Production via in vitro transcription (IVT) offers significant advantages over conventional vaccine platforms, including rapid, high-yield manufacturing and reduced production costs.84 IVT also reduces the risk of immune rejection as it is pathogen free.81 Clinically, mRNA vaccines have demonstrated the ability to generate potent immune responses with relatively limited adverse effects, highlighting this approach as a useful co-therapy that successfully converts the tumor microenvironment from immunosuppressive to immunostimulatory, thereby increasing the effectiveness of the drug it is given in tandem with.85 Their adaptability makes mRNA vaccines particularly well suited for targeting tumor-associated antigens, tumor-specific antigens or neoantigens, addressing the challenges of tumor heterogeneity.86 Although promising preclinical and early clinical results have been reported for melanoma, renal cancers, and prostate cancers, the application of mRNA vaccines in pediatric sarcomas remains in the early stages of investigation.

There are several active clinical trials using RNA lipid nanoparticles (LPs), which activate antigen‑presenting cells (APCs) and induce antigen-specific T‑cell immunity,87 including a clinical trial (NCT05660408) that is actively recruiting patients to investigate the manufacturing feasibility, safety and immunologic activity of an RNA-LP vaccine in patients with recurrent pulmonary or unresectable OS and recurrent pHGG. In vivo and in vitro studies demonstrated that a miR30-scaffold-based bifunctional RNAi construct effectively silenced target gene expression through two simultaneous mechanisms, RISC cleavage-dependent mRNA degradation and RISC cleavage-independent translational repression and P-body sequestration.88 In another study, a liposomal bi-shRNA construct targeting the EWS/FLI1 type I fusion transcript demonstrated 85–92% target knockdown in ES cell lines, with selective growth inhibition of fusion-positive cells, and no effect on cells lacking the EWS/FLI1 fusion.87 In vivo studies in xenograft models demonstrated dose-dependent tumor growth inhibition and a survival advantage, with target gene knockdown confirmed in treated tumor tissue, resulting in justification to initiate clinical testing.87 This lipoplex is currently in a phase I clinical trial (NCT02736565), measuring the safety and maximum tolerated dose in patients with advanced ES. In vivo preclinical studies showed the safety of using a 1,2-Dioleoyl-sn-Glycero-3-Phosphatidylcholine (DOPC) nanoliposomal EphA2-targeted therapeutic.89 DOPC is a liposomal construct developed for nucleic acid delivery to cells that is neutral (resulting in reduced toxicity) and is tenfold more efficient than cationic liposomes.88 EphA2-targeting DOPC-encapsulated siRNA may slow the growth of tumor cells by shutting down the activity of a gene that causes tumor growth.88 To test its effectiveness and safety in humans, a phase I trial (NCT01591356) is using EphA2 siRNA to treat patients with metastatic or recurrent solid tumors. RNA sequencing and whole‑exome sequencing are being used to develop individualized, patient-specific cancer vaccines with sarcoma-specific peptides in fusion-driven sarcoma patients with metastatic disease (NCT06094101). This approach is applicable regardless of the expression of distinct tumor‑associated antigens and independent of their HLA-typing and is based on the feasibility and proof of concept can be directly applied to other tumor entities.

Among all RNA-based strategies reviewed (Table 4), mRNA vaccines and lipid nanoparticle delivery platforms represent the most promising near-term translational opportunities in pediatric OS and ES.36 Their ability to stimulate innate immune activation and antigen-specific adaptive responses simultaneously addresses the two most fundamental immune deficits in these tumors, the cold TME and absence of adequate immune priming.36 The individualized neoantigen vaccine approach is particularly noteworthy as it offers a potential solution to the antigen heterogeneity problem, an issue every other treatment modality discussed has not been able to circumvent. However, its current complexity and cost make it logistically challenging to implement in pediatric populations. Advancing RNA-based strategies in sarcoma will require progress in both delivery technology and a deeper understanding of how the TME influences immune responses to RNA‑based therapies.

Checkpoint inhibitors

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) release T cells and natural killer cells from tumor-mediated suppression to allow them to kill tumor cells.90 Checkpoints like programmed death-1 (PD-1) and its ligand programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) have been extensively investigated.90 ICIs have proven successful in tumors characterized by high mutational burden and robust immune infiltration, biological features absent in pediatric OS and ES tumors. Pediatric bone sarcomas are characterized by low tumor mutation burden with few neoantigens being presented to T cells. Additionally, low PD-L1 expression on tumor cells means the PD-1/PD-L1 axis is not the primary driver of immune suppression. These barriers explain why single agent ICI has shown limited activity in pediatric bone sarcomas and suggests that ICIs need to be used in conjunction with something that has immunomodulatory effects to improve their efficacy.

The use of ICIs to treat childhood sarcomas has been extensively evaluated in clinical trials. Multiple trials have studied the efficacy of pembrolizumab monotherapy in pediatric patients with advanced sarcomas (NCT03013127, NCT02301039, NCT02332668, NCT03012620), with results across these studies consistently demonstrating modest activity as a single agent, due to the immune excluded nature of the sarcoma TME. In addition to studying pembrolizumab as a monotherapy, other trials are looking at the efficacy of using pembrolizumab and various chemotherapies (NCT02888665, NCT02406781, NCT02636725, NCT03056001). Pembrolizumab plus talimogene laherparepvec (T-VEC) was tested in patients with locally advanced or metastatic sarcomas (NCT03069378). T-VEC has been shown to increase tumor‑specific immune activation via augmenting antigen presentation and T-cell priming.91 Treatment with pembrolizumab and T-VEC was associated with antitumor activity with a manageable safety profile. Another drug used in combination with pembrolizumab is epacadostat, an IDO1 inhibitor (NCT03414229). The goal is to identify any positive and negative effects of the combination therapy and determine how well it works in patients with sarcomas.

Other PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors have been studied in clinical trials as single or combination use agents including retifanlimab (NCT04577014), sintilimab (NCT04356872, NCT04356872), nivolumab (NCT02304458, NCT03277924, NCT03190174, NCT03282344, NCT03590210), atezolizumab (NCT02541604), durvalumab (NCT02815995, NCT03085225, NCT03323398). Ipilimumab, an anti-CTLA-4 inhibitor, has been studied as both a monotherapy and combination therapy option in treating pediatric sarcoma patients (NCT01445379, NCT02500797, NCT01643278,

Clinical trials have shown the combination of apatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor and camrelizumab, an ICI targeting PD-1, in patients with advanced OS prolongs progression free survival. The novel tyrosine kinase inhibitor Famitnib exhibits affinity for VEGFR-2, and -3, as well as FGFR-1, -2, -3 and -4 and in preclinical studies, it demonstrated antitumor efficacy across xenograft models.92 Currently, a clinical trial (NCT04044378) is investigating the use of famitnib in combination with camrelizumab and assessing the safety of single‑agent famitinib, famitinib and camrelizumab and famitinib and ifosfamide in patients with inoperable high-grade OS progressing after chemotherapy. Apatinib is a small‑molecule vascular endothelial growth receptor (VEGFR) tyrosine kinase inhibitor, like pazopanib, but with greater binding affinity. When used in advanced OS (NCT02711007), it was found to have an objective response rate of approximately 45% with median progression‑free survival around 5-months. The trial progressed to study apatinib in combination with camrelizumab (NCT03359018) in patients with high-grade OS after chemotherapy. While the combination therapy appeared to extend progression‑free survival (PFS) compared to apatinib monotherapy, it failed to meet the predetermined benchmark of 60% PFS at the 6-month mark.93 Notably, patients with isolated lung metastases or high PD-L1 expression demonstrated better survival outcomes.93

Despite extensive clinical investigation into immune checkpoint inhibitors (Table 5), a clear role for these therapies in the standard treatment of pediatric OS and ES has yet to be established. Across checkpoint types, ICIs alone appear insufficient for treating immune-excluded, low tumor mutational burden tumors. However, when combined with approaches such as T-VEC91 or VEGFR inhibitors92 that promote immune infiltration and antigen priming, ICIs have demonstrated more meaningful clinical activity. Patient stratification will be critical for future trial design, with enrollment focused on biologically defined subgroups most likely to benefit from immunotherapy. Moving forward, the most rational ICI strategies should focus on overcoming upstream barriers to anti-tumor immunity, including impaired innate immune activation, immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments, and deficient antigen presentation.

Conclusions

Despite decades of investigation across multiple immunotherapeutic modalities, the treatment landscape for pediatric osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma remains characterized by limited clinical success. The trials reviewed in this article, spanning NK cell therapy, cytokine augmentation, CAR T cell approaches, RNA therapeutics, and immune checkpoint inhibition, collectively reflect both the breadth of strategies being pursued and the persistence of the biological barriers preventing their efficacy. Regardless of treatment type, failure is most commonly due to the immunosuppressive and immune-excluded tumor microenvironment that characterizes pediatric bone sarcomas. Checkpoint inhibitors have little to release in the absence of T‑cell infiltration.90 NK cells are exhausted or inactivated upon arrival in a myeloid‑dominated, TGF-β-rich environment.15 CAR T cells lose activating receptor expression under TME-mediated suppression.21 Cytokine augmentation can improve immune reconstitution but cannot overcome antigen presentation deficits and T cell exclusion that prevent durable responses.2 RNA therapeutics face not only delivery challenges but also challenges in generating immune activation in a tumor microenvironment that is specifically organized to suppress it.36 Taken together, meaningful clinical benefit from these therapies will likely require combination strategies that remodel the innate immune landscape of the tumor microenvironment from the ground up.

The most instructive pattern to emerge from reviewing these trials is not which individual agent or target shows the most promise, but rather which combination of principles tends to produce the most meaningful signals. Across every modality reviewed, the approaches that have generated the most encouraging results address multiple points of immune deficiency simultaneously, rather than targeting a single mechanism in isolation. The T-VEC and pembrolizumab combination works better than pembrolizumab alone because oncolytic priming generates the antigen presentation that checkpoint release requires.91 VEGFR inhibition enhances ICI activity because it remodels the vascular and myeloid environment that physically excludes T cells from the tumor.92,93 ATRA rescues CAR T efficacy not by improving the CAR construct but by depleting the MDSCs that otherwise neutralize it.29 mRNA vaccines are most compelling not as standalone therapies but as agents that can generate the innate and adaptive priming that makes downstream effector strategies viable. The consistent theme is that immunomodulatory therapies produce limited benefit when adjacent barriers remain unaddressed, whereas approaches that remodel the tumor microenvironment upstream generate more durable and mechanistically coherent responses. This principle should guide the design of the next generation of clinical trials in pediatric bone sarcomas more than the identification of any single new target.

Further research into immunomodulatory agents could aid in enhancing the effectiveness of available drugs. Mifamurtide (L-MTP-PE) is a non-specific modulator of innate immunity and activates monocytes and macrophages leading to the activation of many immunomodulatory molecules.2 While not FDA approved, mifamurtide is approved for use in Europe, and provides support for the idea that agents that target the innate immune system can be used in conjunction with standard of care treatment or with novel immunotherapies to improve outcomes. Macrophages play a key role in driving metastasis, so drugs that activate macrophages or polarize pro-tumorigenic M2-like macrophages to pro-inflammatory M1-like macrophages, or developing treatments that target them, has the potential to halt tumor progression before it reaches the metastatic stage.

In summary, meaningful progress in immunotherapy for pediatric OS and ES will require a shift toward biologically informed, multimodal treatment strategies that address the complexity of the sarcoma tumor microenvironment. Future advances will depend on improved patient stratification, rational combination therapies that coordinate innate and adaptive immune activation, and continued development to enable RNA‑based and cell-based therapies to function in the solid tumor context. Although the immunosuppressive nature of the sarcoma TME presents a major therapeutic challenge, it also provides multiple opportunities for targeted intervention. Ultimately, progress in the field will depend not on a single breakthrough therapy, but on integrating mechanistic, translational, and clinical insights to overcome the therapeutic plateau that has persisted for decades.

Biography

David M. Loeb has an active translational research laboratory focused on understanding bone tumor metastasis. His laboratory developed a clinically relevant mouse model of sarcoma metastasis and has used this model to perform preclinical testing of novel agents that can interfere with this process. More basic scientific studies in the lab involve exploring the role of the Wnt signaling pathway in bone tumor migration, invasion, and metastasis. Dr Loeb is also studying the role of an enzyme called RNA helicase DDX3 in bone sarcoma biology, especially how this enzyme affects the repair of damaged DNA and modulates the response of the innate immune system to osteosarcoma. More recently, the laboratory has developed an interest in targeting the metabolic reprogramming associated with metastasis as a way to prevent the outgrowth of distant metastases from disseminated tumor cells.

Dr Loeb is also actively involved in clinical research, including the development of immunotherapeutic approaches for the treatment of bone metastases and the development of a small-molecule inhibitor of DDX3. He led an international study of immune checkpoint inhibitors and has performed translational research developing new ways to harness the innate immune system to treat refractory sarcomas. He serves as the local PI for a clinical trial of reduced intensity haploidentical bone marrow transplantation for children with high-risk solid tumors.
